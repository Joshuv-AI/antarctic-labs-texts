Lightweight convolutional neural network for chest X-ray images classification
Authors: Chih-Ta Yen, Chia-Yu Tsao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, we developed a lightweight and rapid convolutional neural network (CNN) architecture for chest X-ray images; it primarily consists of a redesigned feature extraction (FE) module and multiscale feature (MF) module and validated using publicly available COVID-19 datasets. Experiments were conducted on multiple updated versions of the COVID-19 Radiography Database, a publicly accessible dataset on Kaggle. The database contained images categorized into three classes: COVID-19 coronavirus, viral or bacterial pneumonia, and normal. The results revealed that the proposed method achieved a training accuracy of 99.85% and a validation accuracy of 96.28% when detecting the three classes. In the test set, the optimal results were 96.03% accuracy for COVID-19, 97.10% accuracy for viral or bacterial pneumonia, and 97.86% accuracy for normal individuals. By reducing the computational requirements and improving the speed of the model, the proposed method can achieve real-time, low-error performance to help medical professionals with rapid diagnosis of COVID-19.Keywords: COVID-19, Convolutional neural networks, Chest x-ray imaging, Lightweight architecture, Computer-aided diagnosisSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases

Introduction

The lungs are vital organs in the human respiratory system, and pneumonia, which attacks the lungs, is usually caused by bacteria or viruses. Symptoms of pneumonia include cough, fever, and dyspnea. Another virus that attacks the lungs, among other organs, is SARS-CoV-2, responsible for the still-ongoing global COVID-19 pandemic1and putting incredible pressure on health systems worldwide2. If not treated promptly, COVID-19 can escalate to a severe condition that results in respiratory failure and potentially death, but early detection and treatment can help most patients recover3,4. Chest X-rays (CXRs) can be used to detect lung abnormalities and assist doctors in assessing the severity of lung infections5. However, relying solely on doctors to make a COVID-19 diagnosis has the following problems: first, the patient-to-doctor ratio is not favorable in low-income countries, and hence, diagnoses are slow. Second, diagnostic accuracy is often influenced by the experience and attentiveness of the doctor. Last, the enormous volume of patients can become a burden on doctors, which may lead to fatigue and a decrease in diagnostic accuracy6. Over time, COVID-19 variants have become more challenging to control. As the pandemic is an ongoing problem, computer-aided diagnosis is necessary to help doctors improve diagnostic speed and accuracy.

In general, intelligent algorithms can be used to extract and classify viral features. Traditional machine learning methods perform poorly in extracting high-dimensional features from medical images, resulting in limited accuracy and a weak generalization capability7,8. In the field of deep learning algorithms, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), significant advancements have been made in computer vision. Compared with traditional image processing algorithms, CNNs require less preprocessing and can be trained end-to-end. CNNs have exhibited excellent performance in various fields such as classification9, object detection10, and semantic segmentation11. Deep learning models trained on medical image datasets have also been used for detecting lung diseases. For example, Lessmann et al. proposed a method for detecting lung diseases on computed tomography (CT) scans12; specifically, they first used a dilated convolution to identify the location of potential lung abnormalities and then employed convolutions to identify calcified regions of lungs. Aledhari et al. investigated three basic CNN models13, namely VGG16, Inceptionv3, and ResNet50, to study their accuracy in pneumonia diagnosis; the results indicated that fine-tuned VGG16 achieved the highest test accuracy of 75%.

However, the advancements in COVID-19 detection methods, significant limitations remain. High-accuracy approaches often involve computationally intensive processes, rendering them impractical for deployment in resource-limited settings. Although lightweight models offer improved computational efficiency, they tend to compromise on accuracy. Furthermore, many techniques rely heavily on pre-trained models or complex ensemble methods, which introduce additional complexity and challenges in terms of model deployment and scalability. Despite the fact that computer-aided diagnostic techniques have made some progress in distinguishing COVID-19, applying deep learning technology to clinical diagnosis remains challenging because of the complexity and variability of lung abnormalities. Additionally, CNN-based pneumonia detection methods are prone to overfitting, causing poor model generalization. Therefore, lightweight and appropriate residual design of the network is required to enhance its generalizability. In consideration of the aforementioned problems, we proposed a lightweight and fast CNN architecture for rapid diagnosis of COVID-19. The proposed method mainly involves the redesign of the feature extraction (FE) module and multiscale feature (MF) module. FE is a fundamental module in deep learning networks, and a satisfactory FE method enables a model to perform better. Model quality and computational efficiency are mainly determined by FE methods. The FE module was designed with reference to the inverted residual modules of MobileNetV2 and the Cross Stage Partial (CSP) structure14proposed by Wang et al. Specifically, the CSP structure is an improved dense block method, with the initial objective of reducing the amount of computation and enhancing gradient performance. This design reduces computational complexity while enhancing gradient flow, effectively preventing vanishing or exploding gradients. The CSP structure mainly involves splitting an input into two parts—one part goes through the module for computation, and the other directly passes through a shortcut to concatenate the calculated features. The advantages of the CSP structure include enhancing the learning ability of the CNN, reducing computational bottlenecks, and minimizing memory consumption. To redesign the MF module, this study referred to and utilized the spatial pyramid pooling (SPP) method15. The MF module utilizes multiple MaxPools of different sizes on the input feature map to extract features, fully leveraging and integrating the information in the existing feature map. After max-pooling, each branch further performs convolution with dilated convolutions of multiple convolution kernels to extract detailed features16. By combining multiple MaxPooling layers and dilated convolutions, we can maximize the use of multi-scale information from the input feature maps, thereby enhancing the model’s sensitivity to fine details. The resulting features are then concatenated. This design effectively mitigates overfitting and improves the model’s ability to learn finer details from the feature maps. Compared to traditional methods, the MF module not only enhances model performance but also increases its ability to perceive features at different scales, making it more suitable for highly variable medical image classification tasks like COVID-19. The obtained features are then concatenated to help suppress overfitting and enhance the ability to learn information from the feature map. This study integrated these two proposed modules into a single CNN network, which demonstrates a favorable generalization capability and effectively identifies abnormalities in CXR images. Additionally, this study proposes a lightweight Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) architecture that not only addresses issues of high computational complexity and insufficient generalization but also offers significant flexibility. Our network allows users to flexibly design the split-transform-merge process based on specific application scenarios. The network features redesigned FE and MF modules, enabling state-of-the-art detection performance while maintaining a small model size, making it particularly suitable for edge devices and resource-constrained clinical environments. Unlike previous works that rely on transfer learning or complex ensemble methods, our lightweight architecture can perform end-to-end training without pre-training, demonstrating high flexibility and practicality in clinical applications. It achieves comparable or even superior results while significantly reducing computational demands. This design is particularly suitable for edge devices and resource-constrained clinical environments, achieving advanced detection performance while keeping the model size small. This study proposed a novel feature extraction module to reduce the amount of network computation and enhance gradient performance. A convolution (Conv) as the input increased the dimensionality of the input image, which facilitated the extraction of image features. Because this increased the amount of computation, depthwise separable convolutions similar to MobileNetV2 were added to solve the problem. This study applied split–transform–merge17. After the first convolution, we set a split ratio of 0.2 to divide the feature map into two parts. The upper part was left unprocessed, and the lower part underwent a dense block, which is a complex and dense network layer used for further feature extraction. Afterward, the extracted features were concatenated with the unprocessed raw data. A residual module was included as the final component of the network, where input was partially added to the concatenated feature map. This reduced the resources required for FE.

Following the release of an increasing number of medical image datasets18–23, the application of deep learning in medical diagnosis continues to be promoted and expanded. Through large amounts of data training, CNNs can achieve the same diagnostic accuracy as radiologists, but with higher diagnostic efficiency24. Deep learning algorithms can make up for the shortage of radiologists in areas with limited medical resources. Akhil Kumar et al. utilized RYOLO v4-tiny, a lightweight and efficient deep learning model, for detecting COVID and non-COVID pneumonia in CT and X-ray images. This model integrates YOLO v4-tiny with residual networks, significantly enhancing detection accuracy, achieving an mAP of 88.18%, which is 7.91% higher than the baseline YOLO v4-tiny. Additionally, they created a richly annotated dataset containing 8,178 images and trained the model on this dataset, demonstrating the feasibility of achieving both high lightweight and high accuracy25. Apostolopoulos et al. used transfer learning to divide data into two datasets: one dataset comprised 504 X-rays of normal lungs, 700 of lungs infected with bacterial pneumonia, and 224 of lungs infected with COVID-19, and the other dataset comprised 504 X-rays of normal lungs, 714 of lungs infected with bacterial pneumonia, and 224 of lungs infected with COVID-19. The X-ray images were scaled to 200 × 266, and the classification layer of the pretrained model was fine-tuned. In the first dataset, VGG-19 displayed the best two-class and three-class classification performance (accuracies of 98.75% and 93.48%, respectively), and in the second dataset, MobileNetV2 displayed the best two-class and three-class classification performance (accuracies of 96.78% and 94.72%, respectively)26. Ucar et al. proposed the COVIDiagnosis-Net model and used a dataset comprising 4,290 images of lungs infected with pneumonia, 76 of lungs infected with COVID-19, and 1,583 of normal lungs. To resolve the problem of data scarcity, the raw data were augmented using techniques such as noise addition, rotation of viewing angle, brightness enhancement, brightness reduction, and mirroring. Additional images were removed to balance the data across the three classes. The model achieved an accuracy of 98.3%27. Minaee et al. employed transfer learning by fine-tuning the classification layers of four pretrained models: ResNet18, ResNet50, SqueezeNet, and DenseNet121. They conducted COVID-19 prediction by using 5,000 chest X-ray images, 2,000 of which were used for training and 3,000 of which were used for model evaluation. The experimental results showed that the majority of the four networks achieved a sensitivity of 98% (± 3%) and approximately 90% specificity28.

Ergen et al. used 295 CXR images of lungs infected with COVID-19, 65 normal images, and 98 images of lungs infected with pneumonia in their study. They employed the fuzzy color technique and stacking technique to eliminate image noise and enhance data quality. The deep learning models MobileNetV2 and SqueezeNet were used for FE. Social mimic optimization (SMO) was then applied to extract effective features out of 1,000 features. The obtained effective features were combined and classified using SVM, resulting in an accuracy of 99.27%28. Ozturk et al. referenced the Darknet-19 model and proposed the DarkCovidNet model for COVID-19 detection29. The DarkCovidNet model was constructed with relatively few network layers and convolutional kernels, and LeakyReLU was chosen as the activation function to prevent neuron death. Ozturk et al. trained the model using 500 normal images, 500 pneumonia images, and 125 COVID-19 images. Accuracies of 87.02% for three-class classification and 98.08% for two-class classification were achieved30. Chowdhury et al. employed pretrained deep learning model in transfer learning to detect COVID-19 on CXR images; specifically, they used 423 COVID-19, 1,579 normal, and 1,485 viral pneumonia CXR images to explore the use of rotation and translation for data augmentation. According to the results, accuracies of 99.41% for two-class classification and 97.74% for three-class classification were achieved without data augmentation. With data augmentation, accuracies of 99.7% for two-class classification and 97.94% for three-class classification were achieved31. Ohata et al. utilized a pretrained model in transfer learning as the feature extractor for CXR images. Ohata et al. combined the extracted features with a machine learning classifier. Two datasets (each comprising 194 CXR images of both COVID-19 and healthy classes) were compared. The CXR images of the healthy classes in the two datasets were different. MobileNet combined with SVM achieved an accuracy of 98.62%, whereas DenseNet201 combined with Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) achieved an accuracy of 95.64%32. Umri et al. applied contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalization (CLAHE), an image processing technique, to the data and used VGG16 for binary classification of normal and COVID-19 CXR images, with a total of 100 images in each class, achieving an accuracy of 97.50%33.

Gupta et al. applied transfer learning and proposed a stacked deep learning network called InstaCovNet-19, which was created by stacking the pretrained models ResNet101, Xception, InceptionV3, MobileNet, and NASNet. The fuzzy color technique and stacking technique were employed to enhance image representation of 361 COVID-19, 365 normal, and 362 pneumonia CXR images. InstaCovNet-19 achieved an accuracy of 99.08% on three-class (COVID-19, pneumonia, normal) classification while achieving an accuracy of 99.53% on two-class (COVID-19, non-COVID-19) classification34. Khan et al. proposed the CoroNet model by adding dropout and two fully connected layers to a pretrained Xception model in transfer learning. The model classified 310 normal, 330 bacterial pneumonia, 327 viral pneumonia, and 284 COVID-19 CXRs. The model achieved accuracies of 89.6%, 95.02%, and 99.00% for four-class classification, three-class classification, and binary classification, respectively35. Wang et al. designed COVID-Net with a residual network architecture to extract features from CXR images and classify them as pneumonia (5,538 images), COVID-19 (358 images), or normal (8,066 images). To resolve the problem of limited data, data augmentation techniques such as translation, rotation, horizontal flipping, scaling, and shifting were employed. An accuracy of 93.3% was achieved36. Bhadra et al. proposed a multilayered CNN model for CXR recognition. Four databases, totaling 1,330 COVID-19, 1,330 pneumonia, and 1,330 normal CXRs, were combined, and the proposed method achieved an accuracy of 99.1%37. Karim et al. presented an ensemble method, and the VGG-19 + DenseNet161 combination outperformed the other combinations. The model proposed by Karim et al. was used for CXR image recognition, classifying images into 8,066, 5,538, and 358 CXRs as normal, pneumonia, and COVID-19, respectively; a prediction accuracy of 94.6% was achieved38. Abbas et al. proposed the DeTraC deep CNN. Data augmentation techniques such as flipping, translation, rotation, and contrast modification were applied to enhance 80 normal, 105 COVID-19, and 11 SARS images. Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to map high-dimensional image data to a lower-dimensional space for dimensionality reduction, thereby reducing computational efficiency. Transfer learning was utilized to adjust the model’s classification layer, achieving an accuracy of 93.1%5.

Tang et al. proposed EDL-COVID, an ensemble deep learning model for detecting COVID-19 on CXR images. EDL-COVID was based on COVID-Net and evaluated using the COVIDx dataset. Initially, training was done on multiple snapshot deep learning models with a cosine annealing learning rate schedule, causing considerable fluctuations in the learning rate. The learning rate started high, quickly dropped to a minimum value approaching zero, and then rose back to the maximum value. Next, the ensemble deep learning model EDL-COVID was developed by combining these models with a proposed model ensemble method, namely weighted averaging ensembling (WAE). The method was based on two observations: (1) individual deep learning models of different classes differ in sensitivity, and (2) different deep learning models have different sensitivities to each class. WAE operates under the assumption that a model with higher sensitivity to a class should contribute more to the final ensemble result, and the estimated weights are proportional to the sensitivity. EDL-COVID achieved excellent performance in the detection of COVID-19 from CXR images, with an accuracy of 95.0%, sensitivity of 96.0%, and positive predictive value (PPV) of 94.1% for the COVID-19 class39. Yamac et al. proposed convolution support estimation network (CSEN) and created an X-ray dataset called QaTa-Cov19 comprising over 6,200 CXRs. The dataset consisted of 462 CXR images of patients with COVID-19, as well as images of three other categories: bacterial pneumonia, viral pneumonia, and normal. When calculating the average performance of 5-fold cross-validation for the QaTa-Cov19 dataset, the proposed CSEN classification method achieved a sensitivity of over 98% and specificity of over 95% in direct identification of COVID-19 from the original X-ray images40. Li et al. proposed MHA-CoroCapsule, a capsule network, and evaluated its performance in COVID-19 diagnosis. In contrast to networks in other studies, the capsule network involved the use of a multihead attention-routing algorithm to quantify the relationship between two capsule layers. The traditional iterative dynamic routing process was replaced with a noniterative and parameterized multihead attention-routing algorithm, which enabled capsules in the model to jointly attend to information from different representational subspaces at different positions. More generalized features were extracted from CXRs, thus improving the detection of COVID-1941.

Recently proposed network models have mostly employ transfer learning. These methods achieved satisfactory results in detecting COVID-19 from CXR images in small datasets. However, most of these studies were conducted with imbalanced datasets or with a certain extent of image augmentation for model training. Although data augmentation may potentially yield better experimental results, it may also lead to situations where the generated images deviate from reality, potentially adversely affecting a model’s generalization ability. Validation with large datasets is needed for data augmentation. Therefore, this study explored three different datasets and applied data augmentation techniques. The effect of datasets on model performance was assessed in an attempt to select the optimal dataset for application in the proposed model. We have developed an end-to-end lightweight model that achieves performance comparable to state-of-the-art methods without the need for pre-training or complex ensemble techniques, while significantly reducing computational requirements and model size.

Methodology

In this section, we introduce two kernel modules42, namely the FE module and the MF module, in Sect. 2.1 and Sect. 2.2, respectively. These two modules were combined to design a network for classifying COVID-19 and other categories of lung infections.

FE module

The FE module is the most basic module in deep learning. Therefore, a good FE ability enables a model to better complete tasks. In addition, the parameters of FE determine the weight value of an entire model. In this study, we designed an FE module, as shown in Fig. 1.

Architecture diagram of the FE module, which can expand features in the channel space to encode more hidden information.

First, the FE module utilized 1×1 convolution to map the input feature map to the target feature map. This changed the number of feature channels while preserving spatial information. After 1×1 convolution, the feature map was divided into two parts using split–transform–merge. We set the split ratio to 0.2, meaning that 20% of the channels in the feature map would not engage in any action, whereas the remaining 80% would undergo ghost convolution (Ghost Conv), which is an efficient convolution method43 to reduce model computation costs and memory usage while maintaining the performance of the CNN. To reduce computational costs, the output channels were divided into two parts. One part was completed by a regular convolution module, and the other part was completed by a cheap operation, which is a low-cost convolution method primarily implemented using depthwise convolution. Depthwise convolution is a variant of standard convolution. In a standard convolution layer, each output channel is connected to every input channel. In depthwise convolution, each input channel is convolved with its own set of filters, which differs from a standard convolution operation, where each input channel is convolved with all filters. Depthwise convolution can reduce the computational burden by separating the learning between channels, rendering it more cost-effective than traditional convolution. Finally, the two parts of the feature maps were re-concatenated to achieve a cross-level feature structure, which helped improve the model’s expressive power and generalization performance. Subsequently, another 1×1 convolution was used to map the output feature map back to the original number of channels, which could integrate the obtained features while ensuring that the feature map size remained consistent. Finally, the processed feature map was added to the original feature map, creating a residual connection, which helped preserve feature diversity and enhance model stability.

MF Module

To obtain more effective feature maps in X-ray images through multiple scales, we designed an MF module, which is a hierarchical structure for obtaining multiscale contextual information. The architecture of the MF module is shown in Fig. 2.

Architecture diagram of the MF module including several Maxpool and GDConv layers of various sizes in the overall hierarchical structure to identify potential multiscale features. The results obtained at each stage are concatenated and used for subsequent predictions.

For the MF module, the first step is to utilize SPP15, which performs max-pooling operations of multiple sizes on the input feature map. This stage maximizes the use of existing feature map information and effectively integrates them. Each branch of the SPP is connected with another further hierarchy, which is achieved through the improved dilated convolution proposed in this study. Dilated convolution can enhance the ability to extract underlying information by changing the receptive field16. However, dilated convolution is regarded as a variant of normal convolution, without any lightweight improvement. As the levels deepen, the high dimensional burden increases. Direct use of dilated convolution would require intensive computation, require much storage, and be memory intensive. To solve this problem, we employed Ghost-Dilated Convolution (GDConv), which achieves dilation based on Ghost Conv. Specifically, GDConv retains the original structure of Ghost Conv but operates depthwise convolution with dilated convolution. The internal structure of GDConv is shown in Fig. 3.

GDConv retains the structure of Ghost Conv but implements depthwise convolution with different dilation rates.

Implementation

In this study, all experiments were run on an Intel i7-8700 K processor and a Nvidia GeForce GTX3060 graphics card. All models were based on the framework of Pytroch in deep learning. In terms of model parameters, the Adam optimizer was selected, and the initial learning rate was set to 0.001. The total number of training iterations was set to 200 by using early stopping technology, and the training stopped when the loss function of the validation set did not decrease after 25 iterations. Per the learning rate decay strategy, if the loss function of the validation set did not decrease after five iterations, the current learning rate would decrease by 10 times. CrossEntropyLoss was used as the loss function. The input images were 224×224, and the batch size was 32.

Results

The observed experimental results are shown in Figs. 6 and 7, and 8. Figure 6 shows the v3 dataset, and convergence was achieved in the 43rd iteration with a training accuracy of 99.68% and a validation accuracy of 94.85%. Figure 7 shows the v4 dataset, and convergence was achieved in the 57th iteration with a training accuracy of 99.81% and a validation accuracy of 96.53%. Figure 8 shows the v4 augmented dataset, and convergence was achieved in the 85th iteration with a training accuracy of 99.82% and a validation accuracy of 79.44%. From the analysis of three datasets, several observations can be made: the v3 dataset converged at the 43rd iteration, whereas the v4 dataset converged at the 57th iteration. This discrepancy may be attributed to the higher quality and greater diversity of samples in the v4 dataset, necessitating more iterations for the model to learn these complex features. Analysis of the three datasets revealed that the v3 dataset converged faster, with a training set accuracy of 99.68%. However, compared with the v4 dataset, v3 dataset had a lower validation set accuracy and higher loss function. Accordingly, the v4 dataset achieved higher validation set accuracy and lower loss function with less training time. The v4 dataset was considered superior, attributable either to the higher quality of images or the larger volume of data. The augmented v4 dataset required more iterations to converge due to its larger data size—nearly 8 times that of the v4 dataset. Although the training accuracy of the augmented v4 dataset was higher (by 0.01%) than that of the v4 dataset, the validation accuracy was much lower possibly because of the data augmentation techniques (such as flipping) used to create the dataset. For example, the model may fail to recognize flipped images. Through experiments on three different versions of datasets, we found that the quality and characteristics of the datasets significantly impact model performance. The v4 dataset performed the best across various metrics, likely due to its more diverse samples. However, simply increasing the data volume (as in the v4 Augmented dataset) does not necessarily improve model performance and may lead to overfitting. This emphasizes that in medical image analysis, data quality and appropriate data processing strategies are more important than sheer data volume. Future research should focus on how to collect higher quality data and design more effective data augmentation strategies to improve model generalization without introducing bias.

The ROC curve is a graphical tool used to measure the performance of a classification model. The horizontal axis represents the FP rate (FPR), and the vertical axis is the TP rate (TPR). The FPR is the ratio of incorrectly predicted positive samples to the total number of actual negative samples. The TPR is the ratio of correctly predicted positive samples to the total number of actual positive samples. The ROC curve plots these two metrics on the same graph, allowing researchers to visualize the performance of a model at different classification thresholds. Usually, AUC is used to evaluate the performance of an overall model. AUC score ranges from 0.5 (indicating random guessing) to 1 (indicating perfect performance). The ROC curve can facilitate understanding of the model’s trade-offs between FP and FN, which is important for many practical applications.

K-fold cross-validation is a model evaluation method widely used in statistics and machine learning. This method involves dividing the original dataset into k subsets, training the model on k-1 subsets, and testing the model on the remaining subset. This process is repeated k times. A different subset is selected each time as the test set to evaluate the model’s performance on unseen data. Maximizing the use of limited data, k-fold cross-validation provides an important assessment of a model’s generalization ability to prevent overfitting or underfitting.

Lightweight model

We identified that training with the v4 dataset yielded the optimal results. Ablation analysis

To validate the effectiveness of the Feature Extraction (FE) module and the Multi-Scale Feature (MF) module, we conducted a series of ablation experiments. These experiments aimed to evaluate the contribution of each module to the overall performance of the model. We divided the experiments into two groups: in one group, the FE module was replaced with a standard 3 × 3 convolutional layer, keeping all other conditions the same; in the other group, the MF module was replaced under the same conditions. All experiments were conducted on the same COVID-19 Radiography Dataset v4, using the same training, validation, and test set splits, and all modifications were made to the ×1model.

We constructed a basic CNN model as the baseline and conducted ablation tests by separately integrating only the FE (Feature Extraction) module and the MF (Multiscale Feature) module. With other configurations unchanged, we replaced the MF module in the x1 network with a 3 × 3 CNN. The results showed a significant increase in the number of parameters compared to the MF module, along with a notable decline in model accuracy. Similarly, with the other configurations unchanged, we replaced the FE module in the x1 network with a 3 × 3 CNN. The parameter count was slightly higher, and there was a slight decrease in model accuracy. Table 7 shows the ablation performance analysis without MF/FE module in the proposed network.

Conclusions

In this study, we introduced a new deep learning network comprising two kernel modules: the FE module and the MF module. Unlike traditional modules, the FE module is a fundamental part of the proposed deep learning network. Effective FE can enhance model performance, rendering FE the key to model quality and computational efficiency. When designing the FE module, we drew inspiration from the inverted residual blocks of MobileNetV2 and the cross-stage partial structure proposed by Wang. CSP, an improvement over dense blocks, primarily aims to reduce computational load and improve gradient performance. CSP divides an input into two parts at the beginning—one part is processed through the module, and the other part bypasses the module through a shortcut to connect with the processed features. This CSP structure offers multiple advantages, including enhanced CNN learning capability, reduced computational bottlenecks, and lower memory consumption. The MF module, for its part, combines and utilizes SPP; specifically, multiple MaxPools of different sizes are applied to the input feature map to extract features, thereby fully leveraging and integrating the information in the existing feature map. After max-pooling, each branch further performs convolution with dilated convolutions of multiple convolution kernels to extract detailed features. The obtained features are then concatenated to help suppress overfitting and enhance the ability to learn information from the feature map. The FE and MF modules are complementary to each other. To confirm the model’s performance in identifying chest X-ray images, we used publicly available COVID-19 datasets for training and testing. We conducted extensive experiments on the COVID-19 Radiography Database dataset using various versions to validate the effectiveness of our proposed method, which achieved state-of-the-art results. There is still room for improvement despite the fact that the proposed method yielded satisfactory classification performance—the ×1 network achieved 97.03%, 97.03%, 97.05%, 97.03%, and 98.53% accuracy, recall, precision, F1 score, and specificity, respectively; the ×1 network achieved 97.77%, 97.77%, 97.80%, 97.77%, and 100% accuracy, recall, precision, F1 score, and specificity, respectively.

Although our method performs excellently in many aspects, we also recognize its limitations and potential areas for improvement. While our model is relatively lightweight, further optimization may be needed in some cases to improve computational efficiency while maintaining high accuracy. The current research is primarily based on specific public datasets, so future work should validate the model on more diverse datasets to ensure its generalization capability. Deploying AI models in clinical environments faces numerous challenges, including but not limited to regulatory approvals and training for healthcare professionals. Despite these challenges, our model has significant potential for clinical applications. It can serve as an auxiliary diagnostic tool, seamlessly integrating into current radiographic diagnostic workflows by providing immediate analysis post-imaging, supporting remote healthcare in rural areas, and offering a lightweight design that runs on standard computers without the need for hospital equipment upgrades. The model’s rapid analysis and ease of integration make it highly adaptable to various needs. In the future, research directions worth exploring include improving model accuracy, reducing parameter size, and exploring multimodal learning methods that combine other imaging modalities such as CT. This would not be limited to COVID-19, pneumonia, and normal conditions but could also include diseases like lung cancer and tuberculosis, developing a more comprehensive diagnostic system. Additionally, incorporating real-time learning capabilities into the equipment could continuously input new patient data, constantly improving its performance.
