Halfspaces are hard to test with relative error
Xi Chen Anindya De Yizhi Huang Columbia University University of Pennsylvania Columbia University xichen@cs.columbia.edu anindyad@cis.upenn.edu yizhi@cs.columbia.edu Shivam Nadimpalli Rocco A. Servedio Tianqi Yang MIT Columbia University Columbia University shivamn@mit.edu rocco@cs.columbia.edu tianqi@cs.columbia.edu

Abstract
Several recent works [ 16 , 20 , 21 , 2 ] have studied a model of property testing of Boolean functions under a relative-error criterion. In this model, the distance from a target function f : { 0 , 1 } n → { 0 , 1 } f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\} that is being tested to a function g g is defined relative to the number of inputs x x for which f ⁡ ( x ) = 1 f(x)=1 ; moreover, testing algorithms in this model have access both to a black-box oracle for f f and to independent uniform satisfying assignments of f f . The motivation for this model is that it provides a natural framework for testing sparse Boolean functions that have few satisfying assignments, analogous to well-studied models for property testing of sparse graphs. The main result of this paper is a lower bound for testing halfspaces (i.e., linear threshold functions) in the relative error model: we show that Ω ~ ​ ( log ⁡ n ) \tilde{\Omega}(\log n) oracle calls are required for any relative-error halfspace testing algorithm over the Boolean hypercube { 0 , 1 } n \{0,1\}^{n} . This stands in sharp contrast both with the constant-query testability (independent of n n ) of halfspaces in the standard model [ 41 ] , and with the positive results for relative-error testing of many other classes given in [ 16 , 20 , 21 , 2 ] . Our lower bound for halfspaces gives the first example of a well-studied class of functions for which relative-error testing is provably more difficult than standard-model testing.

Our main result: Halfspaces are hard to test in the relative-error model.

Our main result is a super-constant lower bound for relative-error halfspace testing over
\{0,1\}^{n}
:

Theorem 1

(Boolean LTF lower bound)
.

For some constant
\varepsilon_{0}>0
, any relative-error
\varepsilon_{0}
-testing algorithm for halfspaces over
\{0,1\}^{n}
must either draw at least
\frac{0.05\log n}{\log\log n}
samples from
\mathrm{SAMP}(f)
or make at least
n^{0.01}
non-adaptive queries to
\mathrm{MQ}(f)
.

Using the standard fact that any algorithm making
q
adaptive queries to
\mathrm{MQ}(f)
can be simulated by an algorithm making
2^{q}
non-adaptive queries,
Theorem

1
implies a lower bound against adaptive testers that draw fewer than
\tilde{\Omega}(\log n)
samples and make fewer than
0.01\log n
queries.

Theorem

1
reveals a dramatic difference between halfspaces and all of the function classes listed in
Table

1
: for each of those classes of functions, the number of oracle calls required for relative-error testing is at most some fixed polynomial in the number of oracle calls used by the best known standard-model testers. In contrast, the
\tilde{\Omega}(\log n)

Theorem

1
together with the
\mathrm{poly}(1/\varepsilon)
-query standard-model testing algorithm of
[
41

shows that there is an arbitrarily large gap between the complexities of standard-model versus relative-error testing for halfspaces.

Very roughly speaking,
Theorem

1
is established by constructing a suitable pair of distributions over yes-functions (halfspaces) and no-functions (functions that are relative-error far from every halfspace).
In our constructions, the yes-functions are Hamming balls centered at random and unknown uniform points
\bm{z}\sim\{0,1\}^{n}
; the no-functions are more complicated and we do not describe them here, but they can also be thought of as being centered at random and unknown uniform points
\bm{z}\sim\{0,1\}^{n}

At a very high intuitive level, the idea of our lower bound is that

\tilde{\Omega}(\log n)
random satisfying assignments are not enough to distinguish yes- functions from no- functions, and moreover are not enough to completely determine the center
\bm{z}

\bm{z}
in both the yes- case and the no- case; this uncertainty about
\bm{z}
is leveraged to show that even making
n^{0.01}
many non-adaptive queries, after receiving the initial samples, does not suffice to distinguish yes-functions from no-functions.

Technical overview of
Theorem

1

To motivate our construction, we make the easy observation (which is explicitly stated in
[
16
]
) that if the function
f
that is being tested has
|f^{-1}(1)|=p2^{n}
, then relative-error
\varepsilon
-testing of
f
can be performed simply by doing standard-model
(p\varepsilon)
-testing of
f
.
Since LTFs over
\{0,1\}^{n}
are
\varepsilon
-testable with
\mathrm{poly}(1/\varepsilon)
queries in the standard uniform-distribution testing model
[
41
]
, it follows that any
\omega_{n}(1)
lower bound for testing LTFs must use functions for which
p\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=|f^{-1}(1)|/2^{n}
is at most some
o_{n}(1)
quantity. It is natural to think of such LTFs as being similar to Hamming balls with a small radius, and indeed this intuition is the starting point of our construction.

2.1
High-level overview of the construction

We consider a distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{yes}}
of yes- functions which are halfspaces with the following structure. For a uniform random “center”
\bm{z}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and a carefully chosen fixed value of the “radius”
r=

n/\log^{2}n=o(n)
, a yes- function outputs 1 on all inputs whose Hamming distance to
\bm{z}
is at most
r
and 0 on all inputs at Hamming distance strictly more than
r
from
\bm{z}
. (So in other words, our yes-function LTFs are simply Hamming balls of a certain fixed radius but with an unknown center
\bm{z}
.) One useful property of these functions, which is an easy consequence of our choice of
r
(satisfying
rs=o(n)
), is that a
1-1/\omega(s)
fraction of satisfying assignments are at Hamming distance exactly
r
from the “center”
\bm{z}
;
we denote this set of points by
\mathrm{Sphere}_{r}(\bm{z})
.

The distribution of no- functions we consider is similar to the yes- functions in that there is a uniform random center
\bm{z}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, and again points at Hamming distance strictly less than
r
from
\bm{z}
are labeled 1 while points at Hamming distance strictly greater than
r
from
\bm{z}
are labeled 0. The difference is in the points in
\mathrm{Sphere}_{r}(\bm{z})
. For no- functions, points in
\mathrm{Sphere}_{r}(\bm{z})
are labeled, roughly speaking, by partitioning them using a collection of
O(\log s)
randomly chosen LTFs
into
\text{poly}(s)
many pieces and assigning the same (random)
\{0,1\}
-label to all points in a given piece of the partition.
Section

4.1
describes the construction in detail and shows that such functions are, with probability
\Omega(1)
,
\Omega(1)
-far in relative-error from all LTFs (
Lemma

2
). The argument employs a greedy procedure to find
\Omega(1)\cdot|f^{-1}(1)|
many disjoint “violating 4-tuples”; given the existence of this many violating 4-tuples, an easy argument gives the claimed relative-distance bound.

2.2
High-level sketch of the lower bound argument

Given the above-described yes- and no- functions, we proceed to a sketch of the lower bound argument, which goes in two stages. In the first stage we show (in
Section

4.2
) that for any “sample-based” algorithm that is only given
s=\Theta({\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}})
uniform random satisfying assignments
\bm{u}^{1},\ldots,\bm{u}^{s}
of
f
and cannot make any queries, a random yes- function is indistinguishable from a random no- function. The high-level intuition is that with high probability,

(i)

All of the received satisfying assignments (in either case) will belong to
\mathrm{Sphere}_{r}(\bm{z})
;

(ii)

In the yes- case, these points
\bm{u}^{1},\ldots,\bm{u}^{s}
will be indistinguishable from a uniform random sample of points that belong to
\mathrm{Sphere}_{r}(\bm{z})
; and

(iii)

Likewise in the no- case, these points will be indistinguishable from a uniform random sample of points that belong to
\mathrm{Sphere}_{r}(\bm{z})
.

Item (i) is a simple consequence of the fact that a
1-1/\omega(s)
fraction of satisfying assignments, in either case, belong to
\mathrm{Sphere}_{r}(\bm{z})
.
Item (ii) is immediate, since if all of the satisfying assignments that are received belong to
\mathrm{Sphere}_{r}(\bm{z})
, then their distribution is uniform random over
\mathrm{Sphere}_{r}(\bm{z})
.
Item (iii), on the other hand, requires a more careful argument.
We consider the slightly different process of (1) drawing
O(s)
samples from
\mathrm{Sphere}_{r}(\bm{z})
first (regardless of how the function labels points in
\mathrm{Sphere}_{r}(\bm{z})
); and then (2) drawing
the random partition to label points in
\mathrm{Sphere}_{r}(\bm{z})
, in particular those samples
received in (1); and (3) finally returning the
first
s
samples that are satisfying assignments.
This can be shown to be close to the actual distribution of
\bm{u}^{1},\ldots,\bm{u}^{s}

as well as the uniform distribution of
s
samples
from
\mathrm{Sphere}_{r}(\bm{z})
;
the latter follows from the fact that it is very unlikely for any two of the
O(s)
samples from
\mathrm{Sphere}_{r}(\bm{z})
drawn at the beginning to land in the same piece of the partition of
\mathrm{Sphere}_{r}(\bm{z})
(see
Lemma

5
for details).

The second and main stage of the argument (in
Section

4.3
) shows that any deterministic algorithm which can make
q
non-adaptive queries after receiving its
s
samples from
f^{-1}(1)
will with high probability receive the same
q
-bit string as a response to its queries in both the yes- and no- cases. Given the results of the previous paragraph, for this it suffices to show the following (see
Lemma

8
): Any deterministic algorithm which takes as input
s
strings
\bm{u}^{1},\dots,\bm{u}^{s}
drawn uniformly at random from
\mathrm{Sphere}_{r}(\bm{z})
with a uniform center
\bm{z}
and outputs a point
\bm{y}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, can only have an
o_{n}(1)
probability of outputting a point
\bm{y}
which is both

(a) at least Hamming distance
t=n^{0.4}
away from each
\bm{u}^{i}
, and
(b) in
\mathrm{Sphere}_{r}(\bm{z})
.

It is sufficient to show this because the construction of the random yes- functions and the random no- functions ensures that if the string
\bm{y}
is either Hamming-close to any
\bm{u}^{i}
or is not in
\mathrm{Sphere}_{r}(\bm{z})
, then with high probability the yes- function and no- function will label
\bm{y}
the same way. To see this, note that if
\bm{y}\notin\mathrm{Sphere}_{r}(\bm{z})
then it will be labeled the same way in the yes- case and the no- case (because in both cases the label is 1 if
\mathrm{ham}(\bm{y},\bm{z})<r
and is 0 if
\mathrm{ham}(\bm{y},\bm{z})>r
). On the other hand, if
\bm{y}\in\mathrm{Sphere}_{r}(\bm{z})
but
\bm{y}
is Hamming-close to some
\bm{u}^{i}
, then with very high probability in the no- case we will have
f(\bm{y})=f(\bm{u}^{i})=1
(given that most likely every
\bm{u}^{i}
is far from
those random hyperplanes used to build the partition), and in the yes- case we also have
f(\bm{y})=1
.

Thus, it remains only to argue that any deterministic algorithm, given as input
\bm{u}^{1},\dots,\bm{u}^{s}
as described above, can only have an
o_{n}(1)
probability of outputting a point
\bm{y}
that satisfies both (a) and (b). Establishing this is the main technical challenge of the lower bound proof, and the argument is rather intricate, see
Section

4.4
. However,
some intuition for why this is true is as follows: we can think of the task of a deterministic algorithm as being that it must choose some
\bm{u}^{i}
and choose
t^{\prime}\geq t
coordinates in
[n]
to flip in
\bm{u}^{i}
to obtain the desired string
\bm{y}
. Since
\bm{u}^{i}\in\mathrm{Sphere}_{r}(\bm{z})
, the goal is for exactly
t^{\prime}/2
of the bit-flips to “move away from”
\bm{z}
and exactly
t^{\prime}/2
of the bit-flips to “move towards”
\bm{z}
. Intuitively, since each
\bm{u}^{i}
is a random point at Hamming distance exactly
r=o(n)
from
\bm{z}
, the best way for the algorithm to select
t^{\prime}/2
coordinates whose flips will all “move away from”
\bm{z}
is to select
t^{\prime}/2
coordinates from the set
\smash{\mathsf{Consistent}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{j\in[n]\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\bm{u}^{1}_{j}=\cdots=\bm{u}^{s}_{j}\}}
, since those are naturally the coordinates for which it is most likely that they are set the same way in
\bm{u}^{i}
as in
\bm{z}
. But a simple calculation, using the precise values of
r
and

, shows that at least
\kappa=\Omega(1/n^{0.1})
-fraction of the coordinates in
\mathsf{Consistent}
are actually coordinates which are set
the opposite way
in all of
\bm{u}^{1},\dots,\bm{u}^{s}
from how they are set in
\bm{z}
. Thus, the intuitively optimal strategy of flipping
t^{\prime}/2
coordinates from
\mathsf{Consistent}
will only have a
\approx(1-\kappa)^{t^{\prime}/2}
probability of moving exactly
t^{\prime}/2
steps away from
\bm{z}
. Instead, it will move roughly
t^{\prime}/2-2\cdot\mathrm{Bin}(t^{\prime}/2,\kappa)
steps away from
\bm{z}
. Since
t^{\prime}=\Omega(n^{0.4})\gg 1/\kappa
, there is “a lot of uncertainty” in the distance from
\bm{z}
that results from flipping these
t^{\prime}/2
coordinates, and there is at most an
o_{n}(1)
chance that the final string
\bm{y}
has Hamming distance exactly
r
from
\bm{z}
.

We close this overview by noting that it is not clear how to formalize a rigorous argument along these lines of the above informal reasoning, since the algorithm can pick any
\bm{y}
as a function of
\bm{u}^{1},\ldots,\bm{u}^{s}
.
So the actual proof uses a rather different, and delicate, combinatorial and probabilistic argument.

We use boldfaced letters such as

We will write

We write

Relative-error testing.

We recall the relative-error testing model that was introduced in
[
16
]
.
A
relative-error
testing algorithm for a class of functions
{\cal C}
over
\{0,1\}^{n}
has oracle access to a black-box oracle
\mathrm{MQ}(f)
for
f
, and also has access to a
\mathrm{SAMP}(f)
oracle which, when called, returns an element
\bm{x}
drawn uniformly at random from
f^{-1}(1)
.
A relative-error testing algorithm for
{\cal C}
must output “yes” with high probability (say at least 9/10; this success probability can be easily amplified) if
f\in{\cal C}
, and must output “no” with high probability (again, say at least 9/10) if
\mathsf{rel}\text{-}\mathsf{dist}(f,{\cal C})\geq\varepsilon
, where

\mathsf{rel}\text{-}\mathsf{dist}(f,{\cal C})=\min_{g\in{\cal C}}\mathsf{rel}\text{-}\mathsf{dist}(f,g)
and
the relative distance between
f
and
g
is defined as

\mathsf{rel}\text{-}\mathsf{dist}(f,g)={\frac{|f^{-1}(1)\ \triangle\ g^{-1}(1)|}{|f^{-1}(1)|}}.

The yes- and no- distributions

We write

to denote the Hamming distance between

Given

\mathrm{Sphere}_{r}(z)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\big\{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\textsf{ham}(x,z)=r\big\}.

Let

Then

\mathrm{Sphere}_{r}(z)
as
the set of

We introduce the following parameters:

\delta\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{1}{\log^{2}n},\qquad r\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\delta n\qquad\text{and}\qquad s\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{0.05\log n}{\log\log n}

(1)

and

\delta s=o_{n}(1)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\delta^{s}=\frac{1}{n^{0.1}}.

For simplicity we assume below that

The yes- distribution.

A random yes- function is obtained by first choosing a random “center”
\bm{z}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, and then taking the function to be the indicator of a Hamming ball of radius
r
centered at
\bm{z}
.
More formally, a draw of a function
\bm{f}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{yes}}
is performed as follows:

1.

Draw a uniform
\bm{z}\sim\{0,1\}^{n}
.

2.

For
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
,

\bm{f}(x)=f_{\bm{z}}(x)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if~}\textsf{ham}(x,\bm{z})\leq r\\
0&\text{if~}\textsf{ham}(x,\bm{z})>r\end{cases}.

It is easy to see that every function

f
in the support of
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{yes}}
is an LTF.

The no- distribution.
In words, a random no- function is obtained by first choosing a random “center”
\bm{z}\sim\{0,1\}^{n}
of a Hamming ball of radius
r
. The function outputs 1 on points that lie in the interior of the Hamming ball and 0 on points that lie outside the Hamming ball. (Note that thus far, this is the same as a random yes- function.) For points that lie exactly on the surface of the Hamming ball (i.e. points in
\mathrm{Sphere}_{r}(\bm{z})
), the output is determined by a randomly chosen
partition
of the radius-
r
Hamming sphere into disjoint pieces (defined by
O(\log s)
many random hyperplanes); each piece is assigned a random bit from
\{0,1\}
, and all points in the piece have that bit as the output value.

More precisely,
a draw of a function
\bm{g}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{no}}
is performed as follows:

1.

Draw a uniform
\bm{z}\sim\{0,1\}^{n}
. For conciseness let
\bm{X}
denote

.

2.

Let

.
Let

We define a partition of

into

\bm{X}_{\bar{b}}=\bm{X}_{(b_{1},\dots,b_{t})}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\left\{x\in\bm{X}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbf{1}\big[\bm{\zeta}^{i}\cdot(x\oplus\bm{z})\geq 0\big]=b_{i}\text{~for all~}i\in[t]\right\}.

Note that

\bm{\zeta}^{i}\cdot(x\oplus\bm{z})\geq 0

r
many

3.

For each string

is drawn uniformly at random, in expectation,

\text{$\frac{n}{2}\left(\delta^{\lvert c\rvert}(1-\delta)^{s-\lvert c\rvert}\right)$ indices $i$ with $z_{i}=0$ and $\frac{n}{2}\left(\delta^{s-\lvert c\rvert}(1-\delta)^{\lvert c\rvert}\right)$ indices $i$ with $z_{i}=1$}.

We say a triple

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{2}\left(\delta^{|c|}(1-\delta)^{s-|c|}\right)\pm n^{0.51}\quad\text{and}

O(1/n^{0.19})
, which is
o(1/q)
given
q=n^{0.01}
).

To see all
Z_{\kappa_{0}+\Delta}
are close to
Z_{\kappa_{0}}
, we take any

\Delta
with
|\Delta|\leq n^{0.4}
and have

\displaystyle\frac{Z_{\kappa_{0}+\Delta+1}}{Z_{\kappa_{0}+\Delta}}

\displaystyle=\frac{|S_{0}|-\kappa_{0}-\Delta}{\kappa_{0}+\Delta+1}\cdot\frac{\kappa_{1}-\Delta}{|S_{1}|-\kappa_{1}+\Delta+1}\cdot\frac{\lambda_{0}-\Delta}{|T_{0}|-\lambda_{0}+\Delta+1}\cdot\frac{|T_{1}|-\lambda_{1}-\Delta}{\lambda_{1}+\Delta+1}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}\right)^{s-2|c|}\cdot\left(\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}\right)^{s-2|c|-2}\cdot\left(\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}\right)^{s-2|c^{\prime}|}\cdot\left(\frac{1-\delta}{\delta}\right)^{s-2|c^{\prime}|-2}\cdot\left(1\pm O\left(\frac{\delta}{n^{0.9}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=1\pm O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right),

using our previous estimates about
\kappa_{0},\kappa_{1},\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1}
and
|S_{0}|,|S_{1}|,|T_{0}|,|T_{1}|
.
It follows that

Z_{\kappa_{0}+\Delta}=(1\pm o_{n}(1))\cdot Z_{\kappa_{0}}

for all
\Delta
with
|\Delta|\leq n^{0.4}
and the claim follows.

The cases of
(0,0)
and
(1,0)
are similar.
The only difference is that for
(0,0)
, we have

\displaystyle\textsf{ham}(y^{*},\bm{z})

\displaystyle=\big|i\notin S\cup T\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}y_{i}^{*}\neq\bm{z}_{i}\big|+\beta_{1}+\bm{k}_{0}+(|S_{1}|-\bm{k}_{1})=\text{some fixed integer}+2\bm{k}_{0}

and for
(1,0)
, we have

\displaystyle\textsf{ham}(y^{*},\bm{z})

\displaystyle=\big|i\notin S\cup T\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}y_{i}^{*}\neq\bm{z}_{i}\big|+\bm{k}_{0}+(|S_{1}|-\bm{k}_{1})+(|T_{0}|-\bm{\ell}_{0})+\bm{\ell}_{1}

\displaystyle=\text{some fixed integer}+4\bm{k}_{0}.

In both cases,
\textsf{ham}(y^{*},\bm{z})
is uniquely determined by
\bm{k}_{0}
and
the rest of the proof is the same.
This finishes the proof of
Lemma

12
.
∎

5
Discussion and Open Problems

(Near-)Optimality of the analysis of our lower bound construction.

Our main theorem shows that no algorithm which is given
\frac{0.05\log n}{\log\log n}
samples from
\mathrm{SAMP}(\bm{f})
and can make
n^{0.01}
black-box oracle calls to
\bm{f}
can reliably determine whether
\bm{f}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{yes}}
or
\bm{f}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{no}}
.
We remark here that this result is close to optimal for the
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{yes}}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{no}}
distributions that we consider; more precisely, an algorithm that is given
100\log n
samples from
\mathrm{SAMP}(\bm{f})
and can make 100 black-box oracle calls to
\bm{f}
can determine, with high constant accuracy, whether
\bm{f}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{yes}}
or
\bm{f}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{no}}
.
At a high level, this is because such an algorithm can use the
100\log n
samples to exactly identify the “center”
\bm{z}
of the unknown yes- or no- function, and then with knowledge of
\bm{z}
it can use 100 queries to determine whether the function is a yes- function or a no- function.

In a bit more detail, this is done as follows:

•

The arguments of
Section

4.2
that are used to prove
Lemma

5
show that with
1-o(1)
probability, the distribution of
100\log n
samples drawn from either
\bm{f}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{yes}}
or
\bm{f}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{no}}
will be identical to the distribution of
100\log n
samples drawn from
\mathrm{Sphere}_{r}(\bm{z})
where
\bm{z}
is uniform random over
\{0,1\}^{n}
.

•

For any coordinate
i\in[n]
, a simple probabilistic argument using the Chernoff bound shows that with probability
1-o(1/n)
, the majority vote of the
i
-th coordinate of
100\log n
samples drawn from
\mathrm{Sphere}_{r}(\bm{z})
will equal
\bm{z}_{i}
. Given this, a union bound over all
n
coordinates shows that with probability
1-o(1)
, an algorithm that receives
100\log n
samples drawn from either
\bm{f}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{yes}}
or
\bm{f}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{no}}
can completely determine the string
\bm{z}
.

•

Given the string
\bm{z}
, the algorithm queries
100
uniform random points at Hamming distance exactly
r
from
\bm{z}
. In the yes- case, the black-box oracle will respond 1 to all of these queries. In the no- case, (a simplified version of) the arguments used to prove
Lemma

2
show that with probability at least
999/1000
over the draw of the random bits
\bm{a}_{\bar{b}}
in Step 3 of the description of the no- distribution from
Section

4.1
, the black-box oracle will respond 0 to at least one of the 100 queries.

A
\mathrm{poly}(n/\varepsilon)
upper bound.

In
[
26
]
, De et al. gave an algorithm that uses
\mathrm{poly}(n/\varepsilon)
independent uniform samples from
f^{-1}(1)
to
learn
any unknown halfspace
f
over
\{0,1\}^{n}
to
\varepsilon
-accuracy in relative error (see Theorem 1.2 of
[
26
]
).
As is well known (see Proposition 3.1 of
[
34
]
), uniform-distribution PAC learning for a class of functions implies standard-model property testing with essentially the same complexity, and it is not difficult to establish this implication in the relative error setting as well. More precisely, the relative-error learning algorithm of
[
26
]
yields a testing algorithm in our relative-error model, by simply running the learning algorithm to obtain a hypothesis halfspace
h
and then drawing
O(1/\varepsilon)
random samples from
f^{-1}(1)
(respectively
h^{-1}(1)
) and checking that they are also satisfying assignments of
h
(respectively
f
). Thus, the
[
26
]
learning result yields a relative-error
\varepsilon
-testing algorithm for halfspaces that makes
\mathrm{poly}(n/\varepsilon)
draws from
\mathrm{SAMP}(f)
and
O(1/\varepsilon)
calls to
\mathrm{MQ}(f)
.

Directions for future work.
A natural goal for future work is to give a more precise characterization of the difficulty of testing halfspaces with relative error. As sketched above, the analysis of our lower bound construction is essentially best possible, but is it possible to give an alternate construction which would lead to an improved lower bound?

Another appealing goal is to try to develop a non-trivial relative-error testing algorithm for halfspaces. While our main result shows that
o({\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}})
complexity is unachievable, perhaps it is possible to do better than the naive approach based on relative-error learning that is sketched above. Specifically, does there exist a relative-error halfspace tester with
sublinear
(i.e.
\mathrm{poly}(1/\varepsilon)\cdot o(n)
) sample and query complexity? Some initial progress towards this goal has been achieved in
[
1
]
, which gives various algorithms for relative-error testing of halfspaces under the standard
N(0,I_{n})
Gaussian distribution which, under mild conditions, have complexity
\mathrm{poly}(1/\varepsilon)\cdot o(n)
.
