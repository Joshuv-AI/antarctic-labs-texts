Improvement of the Simmons model for tunnel junctions Preprint: AIP/123-QED
I. M. W. Räisänen, I. J. Maasilta

Abstract
The Simmons model is a well-known and widely used model for the elastic tunneling current of a metallic tunnel junction, and fitting it to electrical measurements can be used to estimate thicknesses and heights of the tunnel barriers. We present here an improvement of the Simmons model, deriving new more accurate analytical formulas for the tunneling current density and conductance at finite voltage and temperature. We demonstrate that our conductance-voltage formulas are much closer to the Wentzel-Kramers-Brillouin approximation than the Simmons model and its commonly used simplified parabolic approximation. In addition, we demonstrate the practical use of our model, by fitting it to experimental tunnel junction conductance-voltage data and showing a sizeable difference from the Simmons model.



I
Introduction

Tunnel junctions
31
, i.e., two electrical conductors separated by a thin electrically insulating barrier allowing quantum mechanical electron tunneling, are versatile and highly useful devices in many application areas of physics. In particular, superconducting tunnel junctions have in recent years been widely applied as components of various advanced devices, such as building blocks of superconducting quantum bits
14
, superconducting quantum interference devices (SQUIDs)
5
, sensitive radiation detectors
7
, on-chip electronic coolers and thermometers
10
;
18
and metrological applications for the definition of Ampere
22
. This pull from applications has generated renewed interest from the materials science perspective to explore more advanced barrier materials and fabrication methods
20
;
25
, in addition to the most commonly used thermally grown amorphous aluminum oxide barriers
31
.

With such experimental advances, it is also important to critically review established theories, as they describe how various material parameters affect the tunneling current. We limit the discussion here to the case of metal-insulator-metal tunnel junctions, for the sake of simplicity. From the barrier perspective, junctions with one or two superconducting electrodes can always be measured as normal metal junctions above the superconducting critical temperature, to avoid the strong extra features in the tunneling current originating from superconductivity
31
. In most cases, it is enough to work with the Wentzel-Kramers-Brillouin (WKB) approximation
31
;
3
, which, however, requires numerical integration even for the simplest barrier shapes, and is therefore not very practical when trying to determine the barrier properties from experimental data. For that reason, approximate analytical formulas
describing the elastic tunneling current as a function of bias voltage and temperature were developed in the past, most notably by Simmons
28
;
27
;
26
;
29
. His formulas were derived for a general barrier shape, the main parameters being the barrier thickness and the average barrier height.

The results by Simmons demonstrate that the barrier parameters not only influence the value of the zero-bias tunneling conductance, but also the rate at which the junction conductance increases with bias voltage and temperature. In other words, the tunneling current is not a purely linear (Ohmic) function of the voltage. This allows fitting of the conductance-voltage
(G-V)
curves to determine both barrier height and thickness independently. For that reason, Simmons’ model and its simplified low-voltage parabolic conductance-voltage
(G-V)
expansion have been widely used to estimate thicknesses and heights of tunnel barriers
15
;
9
;
16
;
21
;
11
;
6
;
24
;
4
;
30
;
1
;
2
. Another widely used approximative analytical model for zero-temperature conductance was derived by Brinkman, Dynes and Rowell (BDR)
3
for the case of an asymmetric trapezoidal barrier, giving identical results to the Simmons model for the curvature and the value of the conductance minimum in the lowest order.

In this paper, we revisit the theory for metal-insulator-metal tunnel junctions within the WKB approximation and derive new, more accurate analytical expressions for tunneling current and conductance as a function of both bias voltage and temperature. We demonstrate that our expressions approximate the numerical WKB results much more closely than the old expressions in the literature, using realistic barrier parameters. We also derive expressions for the low-voltage parabolic approximation for the conductance-voltage
G(V)
curves within our model, including the temperature dependence, which can easily be used to fit experimental data. As an example, we fit a set of experimental
G-V
data, demonstrating a sizeable difference to the widely used parabolic approximation of the Simmons model, even for the most common barrier material of thermally grown aluminum oxide.

II
Tunneling in the WKB approximation

Let us consider a tunnel junction with a general tunnel barrier, illustrated in Fig.
1

\phi(x)

At zero temperature, Eq. (

\displaystyle J=B\Big[eV\int_{0}^{\mu-eV}D(E_{x})dE_{x}+\int_{\mu-eV}^{\mu}(\mu-E_{x})D(E_{x})dE_{x}\Big],

\displaystyle J(V,T)=Bk_{\textrm{B}}T\int_{0}^{E_{m}}D(E_{x})

\displaystyle\times\ln\Bigg\{\frac{1+\exp[(\mu-E_{x})/(k_{\textrm{B}}T)]}{1+\exp[(\mu-E_{x}-eV)/(k_{\textrm{B}}T)]}\Bigg\}dE_{x},

(4)

where

III
Foundations and main results of the Simmons model

Next, we critically review the results of Simmons’ work. This is important as our modeling uses the same foundations, but extends the range of applicability of the original model. To be able to derive an analytical result for a general barrier shape, Simmons makes the key approximation to estimate the barrier shape function
\phi(x,V)
by its average

\overline{\phi}=\frac{1}{\Delta s}\int_{s_{1}}^{s_{2}}\phi(x,V)dx,

where
\Delta s=s_{2}-s_{1}
is the electrical thickness of the barrier at
\mu
(Fig.
1
), and we have made the dependence of the barrier shape on the bias voltage
V
explicit. By expanding the integrand of Eq. (
1
) in the lowest order (See Appendix
A
for details), Simmons derives Eq. (10) of Ref.
27
, giving

D(E_{x})\simeq\exp\left[-A(\mu+\overline{\phi}-E_{x})^{\frac{1}{2}}\right],

(5)

with

A=\frac{4\pi\beta\Delta s\sqrt{2m^{*}}}{h},

(6)

where Simmons has defined
\beta
as the lowest-order correction factor (Eq. (A6) of Ref.
27
), which contains the dependence on the barrier shape:

\beta=1-\frac{1}{8(\mu+\overline{\phi}-E_{x})^{2}\Delta s}\int_{s_{1}}^{s_{2}}[\phi(x)-\overline{\phi}]^{2}dx.

As pointed out by Hartman
13
, this factor is still a function of
E_{x}
, which prevents the analytical integration of Eq. (
3
). Therefore, Simmons makes a further simplification that
\beta
is approximately constant, which allows the derivation, along with various approximations (see details in Appendix
A
), of a simple formula for current density as a function of voltage in the zero-temperature limit, Eq. (20) of Ref.
27
:

J(V,0)=\frac{4B}{A^{2}}\Big\{\overline{\phi}\exp(-A{\overline{\phi}}^{\frac{1}{2}})-(\overline{\phi}+eV)\exp[-A({\overline{\phi}}+eV)^{\frac{1}{2}}]\Big\}.

(7)

Note that the above general result also contains an implicit voltage dependence through the voltage-dependent average barrier height
\overline{\phi}=\overline{\phi}(V)
, so that the actual calculation of the current-voltage characteristics using Eq. (
7
) requires the knowledge of the structure of the barrier.

Considering nonzero temperatures, from Eq. (
4
) Simmons also derives an approximate formula for current density at finite voltage and temperature (see details in Appendix
B
), which in the lowest order gives Eq. (11) of Ref.
29
:

J(V,T)=J(V,0)\Big[1+\frac{(\pi Ak_{\text{B}}T)^{2}}{24\overline{\phi}(V)}\Big],

(8)

where we stress that the voltage dependence appears not only in the zero-temperature current density
J(V,0)
, but also in the temperature-dependent factor through the voltage-dependent average barrier height
\overline{\phi}(V)
, giving a second term for the finite-temperature conductance.

III.1
Trapezoidal barrier shape

As noted above, Simmons’ results for the general barrier do not allow for an explicit calculation of the tunneling current-voltage characteristics without the knowledge of the shape of the barrier and its voltage dependence. For that reason, Simmons develops his results further for simplified models of rectangular and trapezoidal barrier shapes, which is an assumption made in the analytical BDR model
3
, as well. Such barriers have well-defined electrically sharp electrode-insulator interfaces, as shown in Fig.
2
, making the approximation of a constant tunneling path length
\Delta s=s_{2}-s_{1}=d_{\textrm{phys}}
exact for voltages lower than the barrier height. Let us call the zero-bias barrier heights at the two electrode-insulator interfaces
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
with
\phi_{1}\leq\phi_{2}
. Assuming the barrier material has uniform dielectric properties, the applied voltage drops linearly across the barrier such that

\phi(x)=\phi_{1}+\frac{x}{d_{\textrm{phys}}}(\phi_{2}-eV-\phi_{1})

(9)

with
|V|\leq\phi_{1}/e
. Then
\beta=1
to a good approximation
27
;
26
, and the average barrier height and the electrical thickness
\Delta s
are given by

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\overline{\phi}=\phi_{0}-\frac{eV}{2}\\
\Delta s=d_{\textrm{phys}}\end{array}\right.

(10)

where
d_{\textrm{phys}}
is the physical thickness of the insulating film and
\phi_{0}=(\phi_{1}+\phi_{2})/2
is the average height of the unbiased barrier.

We should note here that Simmons uses in his equations the free-electron mass
m_{\textrm{e}}
instead of the barrier effective band mass
m^{*}
in the barrier penetration factor
A
defined in Eq. (
6
). From Eq. (
6
), we see that such a substitution corresponds to the barrier thickness in Simmons’ notation being an effective thickness
d_{\textrm{eff}}
, which is related to the physical thickness
d_{\textrm{phys}}
by the ratio of the two masses as

d_{\textrm{eff}}=d_{\textrm{phys}}\sqrt{\frac{m^{*}}{m_{\textrm{e}}}}.

(11)

For the sake of simplicity of notation and to allow direct comparison to Simmons’ equations, for the rest of this article we also write equations using only
m_{\textrm{e}}
, so that
d
always denotes the effective thickness
d\equiv d_{\textrm{eff}}
instead of the physical one.

Instead of current, experiments often measure the differential conductance
G=dI/dV
. By substituting Eq. (
10
) into Eq. (
7

A

\displaystyle\frac{G}{A_{\textrm{j}}}=\frac{e^{2}}{8\pi hd^{2}}\Bigg\{\exp\left[-A\left(\phi_{0}-\frac{eV}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]\left[A\left(\phi_{0}-\frac{eV}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}-2\right]

\displaystyle+\exp\left[-A\left(\phi_{0}+\frac{eV}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]\left[A\left(\phi_{0}+\frac{eV}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}-2\right]\Bigg\},

(12)

a constant whose inverse is proportional to the (positive) curvature around zero bias. We call Eq. (

We should point out that both Eqs. (

As mentioned above, Simmons also derived a quadratic correction term for

To conclude the discussion on Simmons’ models, we are left with two key questions: (i) Are all the approximations used to derive Simmons model Eqs. (

(ii) What is the lowest-order conductance model for trapezoidal barriers that takes simultaneously into account both the finite bias and finite temperature? We answer those questions below by deriving new analytical formulas that improve on the above results from the Simmons model.

VII
Conclusions

In conclusion, we have derived a new analytical formula for the current density through a tunnel barrier of arbitrary shape at finite voltage and temperature, Eq. (
18
), and formulas for the conductance of a trapezoidal barrier for bias voltages smaller than the lower of the two barrier heights
0\leq V\leq\phi_{1}/e
, including simultaneously the effect of a finite temperature, Eqs. (
19
) and (
20
).
Eq. (
20
) shows that the effect of finite temperature is twofold: It not only shifts the zero-bias value of the conductance, a previously known effect, but it also changes the curvature of the conductance-voltage curve, which has not been understood before.

We also demonstrated that the new conductance-voltage formulas are much more accurate approximations of the numerically evaluated Wentzel-Kramers-Brillouin approximation than the well-known Simmons model of Eq. (
13
), for realistic barrier parameters.

Such simple formulas can be used to fit experimental data. In particular, we demonstrated how to use the quadratic formula of Eq. (
20
) in linear least-squares fitting of experimental conductance-voltage data from several different types of metal-insulator-metal tunnel junctions. For the junctions studied, the new more accurate formula predicts a correction of approximately
\sim 10
% to the key parameters of the old Simmons model, showing that the improved formulas have practical relevance when determining the barrier properties of tunnel junctions.

At zero temperature, the elastic tunneling current density
J
as a function of voltage

\displaystyle J=B\Big[eV\int_{0}^{\mu-eV}D(E_{x})dE_{x}+\int_{\mu-eV}^{\mu}(\mu-E_{x})D(E_{x})dE_{x}\Big]

(26)

with

B=\frac{4\pi m_{\textrm{e}}e}{h^{3}}.

Simmons starts from the WKB approximation expression for the tunneling probability

(27)

with the barrier effective mass set to the free-electron mass
m^{*}=m{{}_{\textrm{e}}}

f(x)=\mu+\phi(x)-E_{x}
and defining its average as

\overline{f}=\frac{1}{\Delta s}\int_{s_{1}}^{s_{2}}\!\!\!f(x)dx=\mu+\overline{\phi}-E_{x},

where
\Delta s=s_{2}-s_{1}
and

\overline{\phi}=\frac{1}{\Delta s}\int_{s_{1}}^{s_{2}}\!\!\!\phi(x)dx

\displaystyle\int_{s_{1}}^{s_{2}}f^{\frac{1}{2}}(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{s_{1}}^{s_{2}}(\overline{f}+f(x)-\overline{f})^{\frac{1}{2}}dx=\overline{f}^{\frac{1}{2}}\int_{s_{1}}^{s_{2}}\left(1+\frac{f(x)-\overline{f}}{\overline{f}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}dx

\displaystyle\approx\overline{f}^{\frac{1}{2}}\int_{s_{1}}^{s_{2}}\left[1+\frac{f(x)-\overline{f}}{2\overline{f}}-\frac{(f(x)-\overline{f})^{2}}{8\overline{f}^{2}}+...\right]dx

(\mu-E_{x})/\overline{\phi}\ll 1

\left(1+\frac{\mu-E_{x}}{\overline{\phi}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\approx 1+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mu-E_{x}}{\overline{\phi}}.

(45)

This he justifies by stating in Ref.
29
that only electrons with energies
E_{x}
close to
\mu
contribute effectively to the tunneling current. However, this statement is not mathematically accurate for realistic barrier parameters and temperatures, which can be seen by studying the integrand of Eq. (
43
) numerically.

Nevertheless, by making the above rough approximation, Eq. (
44
) becomes Eq. (6) of Ref.
29
which is

D(E_{x})\approx\exp(-A\overline{\phi}^{\frac{1}{2}})\exp\left[-A(\mu-E_{x})/(2\overline{\phi}^{\frac{1}{2}})\right].

(46)

By substituting Eq. (
46
) into Eq. (
43
), extending the upper limit to infinity and the lower limit to minus infinity
and integrating as in Ref.
19
, Simmons gets Eq. (8) of Ref.
29
which is

\displaystyle J(V,T)=\frac{B}{\overline{B}^{2}}\frac{\pi\overline{B}k_{\textrm{B}}T}{\sin(\pi\overline{B}k_{\textrm{B}}T)}

\displaystyle\times\exp(-A\overline{\phi}^{\frac{1}{2}})[1-\exp(-\overline{B}eV)],

(47)

where
\overline{B}=A/(2{\overline{\phi}}^{\frac{1}{2}})
. At
T=0
, Eq. (
47
) becomes the first line in Eq. (9) of Ref.
29
, which is

\displaystyle J(V,0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(B/\overline{B}^{2})\exp(-A\overline{\phi}^{\frac{1}{2}})[1-\exp(-\overline{B}eV)]

(48)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4B}{A^{2}}\Bigg\{\overline{\phi}\exp(-A{\overline{\phi}}^{\frac{1}{2}})

\displaystyle-\overline{\phi}\exp\Bigg[-A\overline{\phi}^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(1+\frac{eV}{2\overline{\phi}}\right)\Bigg]\Bigg\}.

Simmons further assumes that
eV\ll\overline{\phi}
, which allows him to add all higher-order Taylor polynomial terms to the linear approximation such that

1+\frac{eV}{2\overline{\phi}}\approx\left(1+\frac{eV}{\overline{\phi}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

which allows Eq. (
48
) to be written as

J(V,0)\approx\frac{4B}{A^{2}}\Big\{\overline{\phi}\exp(-A{\overline{\phi}}^{\frac{1}{2}})-\overline{\phi}\exp[-A({\overline{\phi}}+eV)^{\frac{1}{2}}]\Big\}

(49)

corresponding to the second line of Eq. (9) of Ref.
29
.
Eq. (
49
) is otherwise identical to Simmons’ zero-temperature result Eq. (20) of Ref.
27
, presented in Eq. (
7
) of this article, i.e.,

J(V,0)=\frac{4B}{A^{2}}\Big\{\overline{\phi}\exp(-A{\overline{\phi}}^{\frac{1}{2}})-(\overline{\phi}+eV)\exp[-A({\overline{\phi}}+eV)^{\frac{1}{2}}]\Big\},

(50)

except that the factor multiplying the last exponential is
\overline{\phi}
in Eq. (
49
) instead of
(\overline{\phi}+eV)
in Eq. (
50
). Simmons himself calls this an error resulting from the approximation of Eq. (
45
), and, without mathematical rigor, simply asserts at this point that the correct
J(V,T)
expression to be used is

J(V,T)=J(V,0)\frac{\pi\overline{B}k_{\textrm{B}}T}{\sin(\pi\overline{B}k_{\textrm{B}}T)},

(51)

with
J(V,0)
given by Eq. (
50
) instead of Eq. (
49
).

The second-order Taylor polynomial of the temperature-dependent part of Eq. (
51
) around
T=0
is

\displaystyle\frac{\pi\overline{B}k_{\textrm{B}}T}{\sin(\pi\overline{B}k_{\textrm{B}}T)}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle 1+\frac{1}{6}(\pi\overline{B}k_{\textrm{B}}T)^{2}

(52)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\frac{(\pi Ak_{\text{B}}T)^{2}}{24\overline{\phi}},

which is a good approximation even at room temperature for typical barrier heights of the eV range. Using Eq. (
52
) in Eq. (
51
) with (
50
), Simmons gets Eq. (11) of Ref.
29
, which is Eq. (
8
) in the main part of this article, i.e.,

J(V,T)=J(V,0)\Big[1+\frac{(\pi Ak_{\text{B}}T)^{2}}{24\overline{\phi}(V)}\Big],

(53)

where
J(V,0)
is defined by Eq. (
50
) (Eq. (
7
)).

Considering trapezoidal barriers in the voltage range
eV<\phi_{0}
, i.e. by making the approximation
\beta=1
and substituting Eq. (
10
), i.e.,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\overline{\phi}=\phi_{0}-\frac{eV}{2}\\
\Delta s=d\end{array}\right.

(54)

into Eq. (
53
) and differentiating with respect to voltage, one gets the specific conductance

\displaystyle\frac{G(V,T)}{A_{\textrm{j}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{G(V,0)}{A_{\textrm{j}}}\left[1+\frac{\pi^{2}k_{\textrm{B}}^{2}m_{\textrm{e}}d^{2}}{3\hbar^{2}(\phi_{0}-\frac{eV}{2})}T^{2}\right]

(55)

\displaystyle+J(V,0)\left[\frac{\pi^{2}k_{\textrm{B}}^{2}m_{\textrm{e}}ed^{2}}{6\hbar^{2}(\phi_{0}-\frac{eV}{2})^{2}}T^{2}\right],

where
G(V,0)/A_{\textrm{j}}
is defined by Eq. (
12
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\frac{G}{A_{\textrm{j}}}=\frac{e^{2}}{8\pi hd^{2}}\Bigg\{\exp\left[-A\left(\phi_{0}-\frac{eV}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]\left[A\left(\phi_{0}-\frac{eV}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}-2\right]

\displaystyle+\exp\left[-A\left(\phi_{0}+\frac{eV}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]\left[A\left(\phi_{0}+\frac{eV}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}-2\right]\Bigg\}

(56)

with
A=2d\sqrt{2m_{\text{e}}}/\hbar
and
J(V,0)
defined by Eq. (
37
), i.e.,

\displaystyle J(V,0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{e}{2\pi hd^{2}}\Bigg\{\left(\phi_{0}-\frac{eV}{2}\right)\exp\left[-A\left(\phi_{0}-\frac{eV}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]

\displaystyle-\left(\phi_{0}+\frac{eV}{2}\right)\exp\left[-A\left(\phi_{0}+\frac{eV}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]\Bigg\}.

By setting
V=0
in Eq. (
55
), one gets

\frac{G(0,T)}{A_{\textrm{j}}}=\frac{G(0,0)}{A_{\textrm{j}}}\left(1+\frac{\pi^{2}k_{\textrm{B}}^{2}m_{\textrm{e}}d^{2}}{3\hbar^{2}\phi_{0}}T^{2}\right),

(57)

where

\frac{G(0,0)}{A_{\textrm{j}}}=\frac{e^{2}\exp(-A\phi_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}})}{4\pi hd^{2}}(A\phi_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}-2),

which, by neglecting the term -2, reduces to Eq. (

) in the main text

G(V=0,T)=G_{0,0}\left(1+\frac{T^{2}}{T_{0,0}^{2}}\right),

(58)

with

T_{0,0}^{2}=\frac{3\hbar^{2}\phi_{0}}{\pi^{2}k_{\textrm{B}}^{2}m_{\textrm{e}}d^{2}}

and

G_{0,0}=\frac{e^{2}A_{\textrm{j}}\sqrt{2m_{\textrm{e}}\phi_{0}}}{h^{2}d}\textrm{exp}\biggr[\frac{-2d\sqrt{2m_{\textrm{e}}\phi_{0}}}{\hbar}\biggr]

being the same as in the zero-temperature Simmons formula, Eq. (

), i.e.,

G(V,T=0)=G_{0,0}\left(1+\frac{V^{2}}{V_{0,0}^{2}}\right).

(59)

Appendix C
Derivation of the new
J(V,T)
and
G(V,T)
equations

At nonzero temperatures, the elastic tunneling current density
J
as a function of voltage
V
and temperature
T
is given
31
;
29
by Eq. (
4
), i.e.,

\displaystyle J(V,T)=Bk_{\textrm{B}}T\int_{0}^{E_{m}}D(E_{x})

\displaystyle\times\ln\Bigg\{\frac{1+\exp[(\mu-E_{x})/(k_{\textrm{B}}T)]}{1+\exp[(\mu-E_{x}-eV)/(k_{\textrm{B}}T)]}\Bigg\}dE_{x}

(61)

with

B=\frac{4\pi m_{\textrm{e}}e}{h^{3}}.

Using dimensionless variables
\alpha=A\overline{\phi}^{\frac{1}{2}}
,
b=\overline{\phi}/(k_{\textrm{B}}T)
,
\gamma=eV/\overline{\phi}
and
\zeta=(E_{x}-\mu)/\overline{\phi}
, and working with Simmons’ approximation for the tunneling probability, Eq. (
5
),

\displaystyle D(E_{x})

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\exp\left[-A(\mu+\overline{\phi}-E_{x})^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left[-\alpha(1-\zeta)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right],

Eq. (
61
) becomes

J=B\overline{\phi}k_{\textrm{B}}T\int_{-\mu/\overline{\phi}}^{1}e^{-\alpha(1-\zeta)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\ln\left[\frac{1+e^{-b\zeta}}{1+e^{-b(\zeta+\gamma)}}\right]d\zeta,

(62)

where the maximum energy of the tunneling electron
E_{m}=\mu+\overline{\phi}
gives the upper limit of the integral
\zeta_{m}=1
.

Looking at the lower limit of the integral, it is the exponential function that suppresses the integrand for negative values of
\zeta
. Therefore, there are parameter ranges for
\alpha
(barrier properties) where the integrand is negligible at
\zeta=-\mu/\overline{\phi}
so that the lower limit can be extended to minus infinity (i.e. when the conduction band edge does not impact the current). We estimate that for typical barriers and metals this is indeed the case. For example, for a barrier with thickness
d=1
nm and average height
\overline{\phi}=1
eV (which for a rectangular barrier with
\overline{\phi}=\phi_{0}-eV/2
can for example correspond to the zero-bias height
\phi_{0}=1
eV at
eV=0
, or
\phi_{0}=2
eV biased at
eV=\phi_{0}
) we get
\alpha=10.2
, which translates to the integrand being negligible at
\zeta\approx-2.5
and thus a condition for the Fermi energy
\mu>
2.5 eV, easily satisfied by most normal metals. Increasing
\overline{\phi}
to 2 eV leads to
\alpha=14.5
and
\zeta_{min}\approx-1.5
, giving still an easily satisfiable condition
\mu>
3 eV.

Setting the lower integration limit to minus infinity and noting that as for typical barriers
k_{B}T\ll\overline{\phi}
and thus
b\gg 1
even at room temperature, the integrand is also negligible at the upper limit
\zeta=1
, and we obtain by integrating by parts

\displaystyle J=-B\overline{\phi}k_{\textrm{B}}T\int_{-\infty}^{1}\frac{2}{\alpha^{2}}e^{-\alpha(1-\zeta)^{\frac{1}{2}}}[\alpha(1-\zeta)^{\frac{1}{2}}+1]

\displaystyle\times\frac{b(e^{-b(\zeta+\gamma)}-e^{-b\zeta})}{(1+e^{-b\zeta})(1+e^{-b(\zeta+\gamma)})}d\zeta.

(63)

Integrating by parts once more, we get

\displaystyle J=\frac{4b^{2}B\overline{\phi}k_{\textrm{B}}T}{\alpha^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{1}\underbrace{e^{-\alpha(1-\zeta)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left[\frac{3(1-\zeta)^{\frac{1}{2}}}{\alpha}+\frac{3}{\alpha^{2}}+1-\zeta\right]}_{=K(\zeta)}

\displaystyle\times\left[\underbrace{\frac{e^{b\zeta}}{(e^{b\zeta}+1)^{2}}}_{=L(\zeta)}-\underbrace{\frac{e^{b(\zeta+\gamma)}}{(e^{b(\zeta+\gamma)}+1)^{2}}}_{=M(\zeta)}\right]d\zeta,

(64)

which can be written as a difference of two terms
J=J_{1}-J_{2}
in the form

\displaystyle J=\underbrace{\frac{4b^{2}B\overline{\phi}k_{\textrm{B}}T}{\alpha^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{1}K(\zeta)L(\zeta)d\zeta}_{=J_{1}}

\displaystyle-\underbrace{\frac{4b^{2}B\overline{\phi}k_{\textrm{B}}T}{\alpha^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{1}K(\zeta)M(\zeta)d\zeta}_{=J_{2}},

(65)

with functions
K(\zeta)
,
L(\zeta)
and
M(\zeta)
defined in Eq. (
64
). The function
L
has a narrow peak (width
\sim 1/b
) at
\zeta=0
, and the function
M
similarly at
\zeta=-\gamma
, while
K
is a more slowly varying function. We therefore make a small error if we perform low-order Taylor expansions of
K
around the peak positions.

For
J_{1}
the expansion around
\zeta=0
is then

J_{1}=\frac{4b^{2}B\overline{\phi}k_{\textrm{B}}T}{\alpha^{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{K^{(n)}(0)}{n!}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\zeta^{n}\frac{e^{b\zeta}}{(e^{b\zeta}+1)^{2}}d\zeta,

(66)

where
K^{(n)}(0)
denotes the
n
:th derivative of
K
at
\zeta=0
, and the upper limit of integration was extended to plus infinity (no dependence
(1-\zeta)^{\frac{1}{2}}
left), which gives a negligible error in the limit
b\gg 1
.

Since
L
is an even function that does not depend on the index
n
, the integrand of Eq. (
66
) is odd when
n
is odd, and the integral is then zero. If
b\gg 1
, we then make a small error by considering only the terms with
n=0
and
n=2
in Eq. (
66
), getting

J_{1}=\frac{4b^{2}B\overline{\phi}k_{\textrm{B}}T}{\alpha^{2}}\left[\frac{e^{-\alpha}}{b}\Big(\frac{3}{\alpha}+\frac{3}{\alpha^{2}}+1\Big)+\frac{\pi^{2}\alpha^{2}e^{-\alpha}}{24b^{3}}\right],

(67)

where we used the results

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{b\zeta}}{(e^{b\zeta}+1)^{2}}d\zeta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{b},

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\zeta^{2}\frac{e^{b\zeta}}{(e^{b\zeta}+1)^{2}}d\zeta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi^{2}}{3b^{3}}.

(68)

Similarly for the second term
J_{2}
, expanding
K
as a Taylor series, but now around
\zeta=-\gamma
, allows
J_{2}
to be written as

\displaystyle J_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4b^{2}B\overline{\phi}k_{\textrm{B}}T}{\alpha^{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{K^{(n)}(-\gamma)}{n!}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(\zeta+\gamma)^{n}\frac{e^{b(\zeta+\gamma)}}{(e^{b(\zeta+\gamma)}+1)^{2}}d\zeta

(69)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4b^{2}B\overline{\phi}k_{\textrm{B}}T}{\alpha^{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{K^{(n)}(-\gamma)}{n!}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(\zeta^{\prime})^{n}\frac{e^{b\zeta^{\prime}}}{(e^{b\zeta^{\prime}}+1)^{2}}d\zeta^{\prime}.

Again, the integrand of Eq. (
69
) is odd when
n
is odd, and the integral is then zero. If
b\gg 1
, we then again make a small error by considering only the terms with
n=0
and
n=2
in Eq. (
69
), getting with the help of the integrals of Eq. (
68
)

\displaystyle J_{2}=\frac{4bB\overline{\phi}k_{\textrm{B}}T}{\alpha^{2}}e^{-\alpha(1+\gamma)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left[\frac{3(1+\gamma)^{\frac{1}{2}}}{\alpha}+\frac{3}{\alpha^{2}}+1+\gamma+\frac{\pi^{2}\alpha^{2}}{24b^{2}}\right].

The total current density is then

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle J_{1}-J_{2}

(70)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4bB\overline{\phi}k_{\textrm{B}}T}{\alpha^{2}}\Bigg\{e^{-\alpha}\Bigg[\frac{3}{\alpha}+\frac{3}{\alpha^{2}}+1+\frac{\pi^{2}\alpha^{2}}{24b^{2}}\Bigg]

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle e^{-\alpha(1+\gamma)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\Bigg[\frac{3(1+\gamma)^{\frac{1}{2}}}{\alpha}+\frac{3}{\alpha^{2}}+1+\gamma+\frac{\pi^{2}\alpha^{2}}{24b^{2}}\Bigg]\Bigg\},

which is equivalent to the generalized result Eq. (
18
) of the main text, i.e.,

\displaystyle J(V,T)=\frac{4B}{A^{4}}\Bigg\{\exp(-A{\overline{\phi}}^{\frac{1}{2}})\Big[A^{2}\overline{\phi}+3(A{\overline{\phi}}^{\frac{1}{2}}+1)+\frac{A^{4}\pi^{2}k_{\textrm{B}}^{2}T^{2}}{24}\Big]

\displaystyle-\exp\Big[-A(\overline{\phi}+eV)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big]\Bigg[A^{2}(\overline{\phi}+eV)

\displaystyle+3\Big[A(\overline{\phi}+eV)^{\frac{1}{2}}+1\Big]+\frac{A^{4}\pi^{2}k_{\textrm{B}}^{2}T^{2}}{24}\Bigg]\Bigg\},

(71)

with

A=\frac{4\pi\beta\Delta s\sqrt{2m_{\textrm{e}}}}{h}.

To derive results for the trapezoidal barrier for bias voltages
|V|<\phi_{1}/e
, we make the approximation
\beta=1
and substitute Eq. (
10
), i.e.,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\overline{\phi}=\phi_{0}-\frac{eV}{2}\\
\Delta s=d_{\text{eff}}\equiv d\end{array}\right.

into Eq. (
71
), and get

\displaystyle J=\frac{4B}{A^{4}}\Bigg\{\exp\Big[-A\left(\phi_{0}-\frac{eV}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big]\Bigg[A^{2}\left(\phi_{0}-\frac{eV}{2}\right)

\displaystyle+3A\left(\phi_{0}-\frac{eV}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+3+\frac{A^{4}\pi^{2}k_{\textrm{B}}^{2}T^{2}}{24}\Bigg]-\exp\Big[-A\left(\phi_{0}+\frac{eV}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big]

\displaystyle\Bigg[A^{2}\left(\phi_{0}+\frac{eV}{2}\right)+3A\left(\phi_{0}+\frac{eV}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+3+\frac{A^{4}\pi^{2}k_{\textrm{B}}^{2}T^{2}}{24}\Bigg]\Bigg\}

(72)

with
A=2d\sqrt{2m_{\text{e}}}/\hbar
. By differentiating Eq. (
72
) with respect to voltage we get Eq. (
19
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\frac{G}{A_{\textrm{j}}}=\frac{e^{2}}{8\pi hd^{2}}\Bigg\{\exp\left[-A\left(\phi_{0}-\frac{eV}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]\Bigg[A\left(\phi_{0}-\frac{eV}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+1

\displaystyle+\frac{A^{3}\pi^{2}k_{\textrm{B}}^{2}T^{2}}{24}\left(\phi_{0}-\frac{eV}{2}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Bigg]

\displaystyle+\exp\left[-A\left(\phi_{0}+\frac{eV}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]\Bigg[A\left(\phi_{0}+\frac{eV}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+1

\displaystyle+\frac{A^{3}\pi^{2}k_{\textrm{B}}^{2}T^{2}}{24}\left(\phi_{0}+\frac{eV}{2}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Bigg]\Bigg\}.

(73)

For low enough biases for which
eV<\phi_{0}
, Eq. (
73
) can be described well by a second-order Taylor expansion with respect to
x=eV/\phi_{0}
, giving

\displaystyle G=G_{0}\Bigg\{1+\frac{\pi^{2}}{24}\frac{(A\phi_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}})^{3}}{A\phi_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}+1}\left(\frac{k_{B}T}{\phi_{0}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{32}\frac{(A\phi_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}})^{3}}{A\phi_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}+1}\left[1+\frac{\pi^{2}}{24}(A^{2}\phi_{0}+3A\phi_{0}^{\frac{1}{2}}+3)\left(\frac{k_{B}T}{\phi_{0}}\right)^{2}\right]\left(\frac{eV}{\phi_{0}}\right)^{2}\Bigg\},

which is equivalent to Eq. (
20
), i.e.,

\displaystyle G(V,T)=\underbrace{\frac{G_{0}}{V_{0}^{2}}\Bigg[1+\underbrace{\frac{4\pi^{2}k_{\textrm{B}}^{2}T^{2}}{3}(1+C)\left(\frac{1}{(eV_{0})^{2}}+\frac{3}{32\phi_{0}^{2}C}\right)}_{=a_{T}}\Bigg]}_{=a}V^{2}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{G_{0}\left(1+\underbrace{\frac{T^{2}}{T_{0}^{2}}}_{=c_{T}}\right)}_{=c}

(74)

where the corrected parameters for zero-temperature zero-bias conductance
G_{0}
, the zero-temperature voltage curvature
V_{0}
, and the temperature curvature
T_{0}
are defined as

G_{0}=G_{0,0}(1+C),

V_{0}^{2}=V_{0,0}^{2}(1+C),

and

T_{0}^{2}=T_{0,0}^{2}(1+C),

where
C
is a dimensionless correction factor

C=\frac{\hbar}{2d\sqrt{2m_{\textrm{e}}\phi_{0}}},

and the original Simmons parameters are

G_{0,0}=\frac{e^{2}A_{\textrm{j}}\sqrt{2m_{\textrm{e}}\phi_{0}}}{h^{2}d}\textrm{exp}\biggr[\frac{-2d\sqrt{2m_{\textrm{e}}\phi_{0}}}{\hbar}\biggr],

V_{0,0}^{2}=\frac{4\hbar^{2}\phi_{0}}{e^{2}m_{\textrm{e}}d^{2}},

and

T_{0,0}^{2}=\frac{3\hbar^{2}\phi_{0}}{\pi^{2}k_{\textrm{B}}^{2}m_{\textrm{e}}d^{2}}.

We begin the derivation of the new

\displaystyle J=B\Big[eV\int_{0}^{\mu-eV}D(E_{x})dE_{x}+\int_{\mu-eV}^{\mu}(\mu-E_{x})D(E_{x})dE_{x}\Big]

(75)

By substituting Eq. (

we get

\displaystyle J=B\Bigg\{eV\int_{0}^{\mu-eV}\exp[-A(\mu+\overline{\phi}-E_{x})^{\frac{1}{2}}]dE_{x}

\displaystyle+\int_{\mu-eV}^{\mu}(\mu-E_{x})\exp[-A(\mu+\overline{\phi}-E_{x})^{\frac{1}{2}}]dE_{x}\Bigg\}.

(77)

To allow easier comparison to Simmons’ results, we write Eq. (
77
) in the form

\displaystyle J=B\Bigg\{eV\int_{0}^{\mu-eV}\exp[-A(\mu+\overline{\phi}-E_{x})^{\frac{1}{2}}]dE_{x}

\displaystyle-\overline{\phi}\int_{\mu-eV}^{\mu}\exp[-A(\mu+\overline{\phi}-E_{x})^{\frac{1}{2}}]dE_{x}

\displaystyle+\int_{\mu-eV}^{\mu}(\mu+\overline{\phi}-E_{x})\exp[-A(\mu+\overline{\phi}-E_{x})^{\frac{1}{2}}]dE_{x}\Bigg\}

(78)

which is Eq. (13) of Ref.
27

\displaystyle(2BeV/A^{2})\{[A({\overline{\phi}}+eV)^{\frac{1}{2}}+1]\exp[-A({\overline{\phi}}+eV)^{\frac{1}{2}}]

\displaystyle-[A(\overline{\phi}+\mu)^{\frac{1}{2}}+1]\exp[-A(\overline{\phi}+\mu)^{\frac{1}{2}}]\}.

(79)

If we consider the limit
\mu>eV
, which is satisfied for regular metallic electrodes up to high voltages
\sim 5
V, the second term in the braces in Eq. (
79
) is exponentially smaller than the first term and can be neglected.

The second integral gives for the second term in Eq. (
78

\displaystyle(2B\overline{\phi}/A^{2})\{[A({\overline{\phi}}+eV)^{\frac{1}{2}}+1]\exp[-A({\overline{\phi}}+eV)^{\frac{1}{2}}]

\displaystyle-[A\overline{\phi}^{\frac{1}{2}}+1]\exp[-A\overline{\phi}^{\frac{1}{2}}]\},

(80)

and by defining an integration variable
z=(\mu+\overline{\phi}-E_{x})^{\frac{1}{2}}
, the third integral gives for the third term in Eq. (
78
)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle 2B\int_{(\overline{\phi}+eV)^{\frac{1}{2}}}^{\overline{\phi}^{\frac{1}{2}}}z^{3}\exp(-Az)dz

(81)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2B\exp(-Az)\Big(\frac{z^{3}}{A}+\frac{3z^{2}}{A^{2}}+\frac{6z}{A^{3}}+\frac{6}{A^{4}}\Big)\bigg|_{(\overline{\phi}+eV)^{\frac{1}{2}}}^{\overline{\phi}^{\frac{1}{2}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(2B/A^{4})\exp(-A\overline{\phi}^{\frac{1}{2}})[A^{3}\overline{\phi}^{\frac{3}{2}}+3A^{2}\overline{\phi}+6A\overline{\phi}^{\frac{1}{2}}+6]

\displaystyle-(2B/A^{4})\exp[-A(\overline{\phi}+eV)^{\frac{1}{2}}][A^{3}(\overline{\phi}+eV)^{\frac{3}{2}}

\displaystyle+3A^{2}(\overline{\phi}+eV)+6A(\overline{\phi}+eV)^{\frac{1}{2}}+6].

Summing Eq. (
79
) without its insignificant latter term
-[A(\overline{\phi}+\mu)^{\frac{1}{2}}+1]\exp[-A(\overline{\phi}+\mu)^{\frac{1}{2}}]
, Eq. (
80
) and Eq. (
81
), we finally get

\displaystyle J=\frac{4B}{A^{4}}\Bigg\{\exp(-A{\overline{\phi}}^{\frac{1}{2}})\Big[A^{2}\overline{\phi}+3(A{\overline{\phi}}^{\frac{1}{2}}+1)\Big]

\displaystyle-\exp\Big[-A(\overline{\phi}+eV)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big]\Bigg[A^{2}(\overline{\phi}+eV)

\displaystyle+3\Big[A(\overline{\phi}+eV)^{\frac{1}{2}}+1\Big]\Bigg]\Bigg\}.

(82)

Compared to Simmons’ original result, Eq. (
7
) (Eq. 20 of Ref.
27
), Eq. (
82
) has two additional terms multiplying the exponentials,
3(A{\overline{\phi}}^{\frac{1}{2}}+1)
and
3\Big[A(\overline{\phi}+eV)^{\frac{1}{2}}+1\Big]

6z/A^{3}
and
6/A^{4}
as Simmons does, they are taken into account in Eq. (
81
). We remark that Eq. (
82
) agrees with the more general finite-temperature result Eq. (
18
) at
T=0
.

To derive results for the trapezoidal barrier for bias voltages
|V|<\phi_{1}/e
, we make the approximation
\beta=1
and substitute Eq. (
10
), i.e.,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\overline{\phi}=\phi_{0}-\frac{eV}{2}\\
\Delta s=d\end{array}\right.

into Eq. (

). We get

\displaystyle J=\frac{4B}{A^{4}}\Bigg\{\exp\Bigg[-A\left(\phi_{0}-\frac{eV}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Bigg]\Bigg[A^{2}\left(\phi_{0}-\frac{eV}{2}\right)

\displaystyle+3A\left(\phi_{0}-\frac{eV}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+3\Bigg]-\exp\Bigg[-A\left(\phi_{0}+\frac{eV}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\Bigg[A^{2}\left(\phi_{0}+\frac{eV}{2}\right)+3A\left(\phi_{0}+\frac{eV}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+3\Bigg]\Bigg\}

(83)

with
A=2d\sqrt{2m_{\text{e}}}/\hbar

83
) agrees with Eq. (
72
) in the limit
T=0
. By differentiating Eq. (

) with respect to voltage, we get

\displaystyle\frac{G}{A_{\textrm{j}}}=\frac{e^{2}}{8\pi hd^{2}}\Bigg\{\exp\left[-A\left(\phi_{0}-\frac{eV}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]\left[A\left(\phi_{0}-\frac{eV}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+1\right]

\displaystyle+\exp\left[-A\left(\phi_{0}+\frac{eV}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]\left[A\left(\phi_{0}+\frac{eV}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+1\right]\Bigg\},

(84)

which agrees with Eq. (
19
) in the limit
T=0

For low enough biases for which

which is equivalent to Eq. (

(85)

and the original Simmons parameters

V_{0,0}^{2}=\frac{4\hbar^{2}\phi_{0}}{e^{2}m_{\textrm{e}}d^{2}}.

Appendix E
Derivation of the
G-V
equation [Eq. (
25
)] from the WKB approximation

At zero temperature, the elastic tunneling current density
J
as a function of voltage
V
is described by Eq. (
3
), i.e.,

\displaystyle J=B\Big[eV\int_{0}^{\mu-eV}D(E_{x})dE_{x}+\int_{\mu-eV}^{\mu}(\mu-E_{x})D(E_{x})dE_{x}\Big]

(86)

with

B=\frac{4\pi m_{\textrm{e}}e}{h^{3}}.

Here, we consider only a rectangular tunnel barrier with
\phi_{0}=\phi_{1}=\phi_{2}
, which has physical thickness
d_{\textrm{phys}}
. A bias voltage
V
tilts the barrier, making its shape trapezoidal,
\phi(x,V)=\phi_{0}-eVx/d_{\textrm{phys}}
, assuming
V<\phi_{0}/e
. The WKB tunneling probability, Eq. (
1
), can then be integrated analytically.
By writing
s_{1}=0
,
s_{2}=d_{\textrm{phys}}
,
g=\mu+\phi_{0}-E_{x}
,
k=eV/d_{\textrm{phys}}
, Eq. (
1
) becomes

\displaystyle D(E_{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left[-\frac{4\pi\sqrt{2m^{*}}}{h}\int_{s_{1}}^{s_{2}}(\mu+\phi(x)-E_{x})^{\frac{1}{2}}dx\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left[-\frac{4\pi\sqrt{2m^{*}}}{h}\int_{0}^{d_{\textrm{phys}}}(g-kx)^{\frac{1}{2}}dx\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left[-\frac{4\pi\sqrt{2m^{*}}}{h}\frac{2}{3k}\Big(g^{\frac{3}{2}}-(g-kd_{\textrm{phys}})^{\frac{3}{2}}\Big)\right],

which is equivalent to Eq. (
24
), i.e.,

\displaystyle D_{\textrm{WKB}}(V,E_{x})=\exp\Bigg\{-\frac{A^{\prime}}{eV}\Big[(\mu+\phi_{0}-E_{x})^{\frac{3}{2}}

\displaystyle-(\mu+\phi_{0}-E_{x}-eV)^{\frac{3}{2}}\Big]\Bigg\}

(87)

with

A^{\prime}=\frac{8\pi d\sqrt{2m_{\textrm{e}}}}{3h},

where
d\equiv d_{\textrm{eff}}
now denotes the effective thickness defined in Eq. (
11
) as
d_{\textrm{eff}}=d_{\textrm{phys}}\sqrt{m^{*}/m_{\textrm{e}}}
.

Substitution of Eq. (
87
) into Eq. (
86
) yields

\displaystyle J=\underbrace{B\int_{0}^{\mu-eV}\underbrace{D_{\textrm{WKB}}(V,E_{x})eV}_{=p(V,E_{x})}dE_{x}}_{=J_{A}}

\displaystyle+B\int_{\mu-eV}^{\mu}D_{\textrm{WKB}}(V,E_{x})(\mu-E_{x})dE_{x}.

(88)

To obtain the differential conductance
G
, we need to differentiate Eq. (
88
) with respect to voltage, which we accomplish by applying the Leibniz integral rule. The voltage derivative of the term
J_{A}
is then given by

\displaystyle\frac{dJ_{A}}{dV}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B\frac{d}{dV}\left[\int_{a(V)}^{b(V)}p(V,E_{x})dE_{x}\right]

(89)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B\Big[p(V,b(V))\cdot\frac{db(V)}{dV}-p(V,a(V))\cdot\frac{da(V)}{dV}

\displaystyle+\int_{a(V)}^{b(V)}\frac{\partial{p(V,E_{x})}}{\partial{V}}dE_{x}\Big]

with
a(V)=0
and
b(V)=\mu-eV
, with the second term in Eq. (
88
) integrated in the same way by substituting
p(V,E_{x})=D_{\textrm{WKB}}(V,E_{x})(\mu-E_{x})
,
a(V)=\mu-eV
and
b(V)=\mu
in Eq. (
89
). Summing the two contributions, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{G}{A_{\textrm{j}}}=-Be^{2}VD_{\textrm{WKB}}(V,\mu-eV)

\displaystyle+B\int_{0}^{\mu-eV}\frac{\partial}{\partial{V}}\Big[D_{\textrm{WKB}}(V,E_{x})eV\Big]dE_{x}

\displaystyle+Be^{2}VD_{\textrm{WKB}}(V,\mu-eV)

\displaystyle+B\int_{\mu-eV}^{\mu}\frac{\partial}{\partial{V}}\Big[D_{\textrm{WKB}}(V,E_{x})\Big](\mu-E_{x})dE_{x},

(90)

which, after cancellation of the first and third terms and differentiation, is equivalent to Eq. (
25
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\frac{G}{A_{\textrm{j}}}=Be\Bigg(\int_{0}^{\mu-eV}D_{\textrm{WKB}}(V,E_{x})\Bigg\{1+\frac{A^{\prime}}{eV}\Bigg[(\mu+\phi_{0}-E_{x})^{\frac{3}{2}}

\displaystyle-(\mu+\phi_{0}-E_{x}-eV)^{\frac{3}{2}}-\frac{3eV}{2}(\mu+\phi_{0}-E_{x}-eV)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Bigg]\Bigg\}dE_{x}

\displaystyle+\int_{\mu-eV}^{\mu}(\mu-E_{x})D_{\textrm{WKB}}(V,E_{x})\frac{A^{\prime}}{(eV)^{2}}\Bigg[(\mu+\phi_{0}-E_{x})^{\frac{3}{2}}

\displaystyle-(\mu+\phi_{0}-E_{x}-eV)^{\frac{3}{2}}-\frac{3eV}{2}(\mu+\phi_{0}-E_{x}-eV)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Bigg]dE_{x}\Bigg).

(91)

Appendix F
Calculation of barrier parameters
d
and
\phi_{0}
and their errors after fitting Eq. (
20
) to experimental
G-V
data

The goal of this Appendix is to show that barrier parameters
d
and
\phi_{0}
can be determined by fitting Eq. (
20
) to experimental
G-V
data of a tunnel junction. We rewrite Eq. (
20
) here for convenience:

\displaystyle G(V,T)=\underbrace{\frac{G_{0}}{V_{0}^{2}}\Bigg[1+\frac{4\pi^{2}k_{\textrm{B}}^{2}T^{2}}{3}(1+C)\left(\frac{1}{(eV_{0})^{2}}+\frac{3}{32\phi_{0}^{2}C}\right)\Bigg]}_{=a}V^{2}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{G_{0}\left(1+\frac{T^{2}}{T_{0}^{2}}\right)}_{=c}

(92)

with

G_{0}=G_{0,0}(1+C),

V_{0}^{2}=V_{0,0}^{2}(1+C),

T_{0}^{2}=T_{0,0}^{2}(1+C),

G_{0,0}=\frac{e^{2}A_{\textrm{j}}\sqrt{2m_{\textrm{e}}\phi_{0}}}{h^{2}d}\textrm{exp}\biggr[\frac{-2d\sqrt{2m_{\textrm{e}}\phi_{0}}}{\hbar}\biggr],

V_{0,0}^{2}=\frac{4\hbar^{2}\phi_{0}}{e^{2}m_{\textrm{e}}d^{2}},

T_{0,0}^{2}=\frac{3\hbar^{2}\phi_{0}}{\pi^{2}k_{\textrm{B}}^{2}m_{\textrm{e}}d^{2}},

and

C=\frac{\hbar}{2d\sqrt{2m_{\textrm{e}}\phi_{0}}},

showing explicitly that Eq. (
92
) is a parabola with respect to
V
with the form
G(V)=aV^{2}+c
. To fit Eq. (
92
) to data, we use a linear least-squares algorithm, which gives the coefficients
a
and
c
and their 95 % confidence intervals. We define the errors of
a
and
c
,
\Delta a
and
\Delta c
, respectively, as half of the 95 % confidence interval lengths.

After obtaining the values of
a
and
c
we can calculate
d
and
\phi_{0}
by numerically solving the nonlinear system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}F=a-\frac{e^{4}m_{\text{e}}^{3/2}A_{\text{j}}d}{4\hbar^{2}h^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\phi_{0}}}\exp{\Big(-\frac{2d\sqrt{2m_{\text{e}}\phi_{0}}}{\hbar}\Big)}\\
\times\Bigg[1+\frac{(\pi k_{\textrm{B}}T)^{2}}{\phi_{0}}\Big(\frac{m_{\text{e}}d^{2}}{3\hbar^{2}}+\frac{d}{2\hbar}\sqrt{\frac{m_{\text{e}}}{2\phi_{0}}}+\frac{1}{8\phi_{0}}\Big)\Bigg]=0\\

(93)

that follows from Eq. (
92

\phi_{0}

\sigma_{a}
,
\sigma_{c}
and
\sigma_{A_{\text{j}}}
are the standard deviations of
\hat{a}
,
\hat{c}

and

and

A_{\text{j}}
. For the normal distribution, the standard deviation is the length of the 95 % confidence interval divided by 3.92, or the error divided by 1.96. The simulation follows by drawing one value of
\hat{a}

\hat{c}

\hat{d}
