Falls in demented patients are associated with reduced EEG functional connectivity of the dorsal attention network
Authors: Agnes Pirker-Kees, Kirsten Platho-Elwischger, Clemens Lang, Christoph Baumgartner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveFalls are common in individuals with dementia and pose a significant problem in acute hospital care. Aging and dementia affect functional brain networks including the default mode network (DMN), dorsal attention network (DAN) and salience network (SAN). The DAN and SAN are essential for the rapid detection of responses to unexpected stimuli. We hypothesize that global efficiency within these networks is reduced in patients with dementia who experience falls compared with those who do not.MethodsWe analyzed 347 inpatients with dementia from a neurological clinic in Vienna, Austria. Of 153 available EEGs, only those with normal findings were included. FC was calculated using the phase-locking-value across delts, theta, alpha, and beta bands. The characteristic path length of the DAN, SAN and DMN was computed.ResultsFallers showed reduced global efficiency in the DAN. FC between the right rostral middle frontal gyrus and left middle frontal gyrus was decreased in delta, theta, and alpha bands – regions involved in visual stimulus processing and executive control.ConclusionsReduced DAN connectivity may impair the ability to respond to unexpected stimuli, increasing fall risk in individuals with dementia. This supports the role of attention network dysfunction in motoric cognitive risk syndrome.SignificanceFalls are a significant problem in dementia, not well understood so far. Identifying network level dysfunction in dementia related falls offers a novel biomarker for risk stratification and possible targets for future interventions.Keywords: EEG, Brain network, Dementia, Falls

Introduction

Falls constitute a significant problem in dementia patients, as almost two-thirds of people with dementia fall each year (Zhang et al., 2019). In general hospitals, up to 35% of patients older than 75 years have a diagnosis of dementia (Timmons et al., 2015), and up to 25% of dementia patients in an acute hospital frequently fall during their stay (Scheel et al., 2022). This may lead to serious injuries, prolonged hospitalization, loss of independence, and further deterioration of both physical and cognitive functions.

Cognitive impairment has been consistently identified as a major risk factor for falling. Specifically, the concept of motoric cognitive risk (MCR) syndrome describes the co-occurrence of subjective cognitive decline and slow gait as a pre-dementia syndrome and predictor of adverse outcomes including falls (Chhetri et al., 2017; Verghese et al., 2014). Furthermore, neurobiological mechanisms such as amyloid pathology have been associated with falls in both preclinical and clinical Alzheimer's disease (Dao et al., 2017). These findings underscore the importance of attentional and executive functional networks in motor control, particularly during complex or dual-task walking conditions. Gait and balance require not only motor but also attentional resources, and fall risk is increased when cognitive control is compromised.

Recent research using fMRI and EEG suggests that functional network integrity, particularly in large-scale attention networks, plays a crucial role in gait regulation and fall prevention. For instance, reduced connectivity in the dorsal attention network (DAN) and salience network (SAN) has been linked to gait variability and poor motor control in Parkinson's disease (Kim et al., 2020; Mak et al., 2023), mild cognitive impairment (Ma et al., 2022), and healthy aging. However, these mechanisms have not yet been systematically studied in patients with dementia, particularly in the context of falls in general hospital setting.

Physiological aging and cognitive decline are accompanied by alterations in brain structure and function, including changes in functional connectivity (FC) within resting-state brain networks (Colangeli et al., 2016; Vecchio et al., 2014). These networks—such as the DMN, the DAN and the SAN — can be investigated using EEG-based graph theoretical approaches (Garcés et al., 2014; Rubinov and Sporns, 2010). FC is typically defined as the synchronization between brain regions (nodes), and its efficiency can be quantified by the average shortest path length in the network (Garcés et al., 2014; Rubinov and Sporns, 2010).

Among these networks, the DMN is active at rest and has been shown to be disrupted in cognitive impairment and early Alzheimer's disease (Babiloni et al., 2020; Choi et al., 2019). The DAN, responsible for externally directed attention and orienting to relevant stimuli, has been linked to cognitive reserve and is essential for coordinating complex behaviors such as ambulation (Franzmeier et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2019). The SAN plays a key role in detecting and prioritizing salient stimuli, switching between cognitive states, and modulating social and behavioral responses (Doll et al., 2015). Notably, SAN dysfunction has been implicated in neuropsychiatric disorders such as depression and in maladaptive behavioral responses to environmental demands (Lynch et al., 2024).

Despite these advances, few studies have directly examined the association between alterations in these networks and real-world fall risk in dementia. To our knowledge, no prior work has investigated whether impaired FC in these networks, as assessed by EEG, is linked to inpatient falls in this population.

We therefore aimed to evaluate whether resting-state FC within the DMN, DAN, and SAN is altered in patients with dementia who experienced a fall during hospitalization. Building on prior findings that attentional and salience networks contribute to gait regulation and fall prevention, we hypothesized that global FC efficiency within the DAN would be reduced in fallers compared to non-fallers. To test this, we analyzed EEG-derived FC and graph metrics in a cohort of patients with dementia, comparing those who fell during their hospital stay to those who did not.

Patients and methods

Patients

The Study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the City of Vienna in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was waived because of the retrospective study design. We assessed a cohort of all 348 patients (208 female) who were discharged with a diagnosis of dementia (according to ICD-10) within one year from the Neurological department of Clinic Hietzing in Vienna between January 2017 and December 2017. Within this cohort, a total of 128 falls were documented in 66 patients. For all patients, electronic charts and discharge documentation were assessed including information on medication at discharge, medical history, and co-morbidities. For a total of 154 patients (91 female, mean age 81 ± 10 years), an EEG obtained during their hospital stay was available, and 40 patients out of this group were found to have had at least one fall during their hospital stay. All EEG recordings were acquired within one week of the fall.

Selection of EEGs: EEGs from patients with conditions known to significantly alter functional connectivity (e.g., disturbances of consciousness, drowsiness, epilepsy, severe stroke, severe metabolic encephalopathy) were excluded in order to minimize confounding effects on network-level analysis.

Only EEGs classified as normal or mildly abnormal were selected for further analysis (Pirker-Kees et al., 2024). EEGs were visually reviewed by experienced clinical neurophysiologists. Recordings were classified as normal when a physiological posterior dominant rhythm was present with age-appropriate frequency, preserved reactivity, and no focal or generalized abnormalities. EEGs were classified as mildly abnormal when they showed limited or nonspecific deviations, such as mild diffuse slowing, reduced posterior dominant rhythm frequency, or subtle background disorganization, without evidence of dominant pathological patterns (Noachtar et al., 1999). EEGs were excluded if they demonstrated any of the following: marked diffuse slowing, suggestive of metabolic encephalopathy or advanced cerebral dysfunction; focal slowing consistent with structural brain lesions, including post-stroke changes; epileptiform discharges, lateralized periodic discharges (LPDs), generalized or focal periodic discharges, or electrographic seizure activity or technical insufficiency that precluded reliable quantitative analysis. Prominent epileptiform activity, ischemic lesions, or periodic discharges such as LPDs might impose strong, spatially localized and often highly synchronous electrical patterns that can dominate functional connectivity estimates and spectral network metrics (Pirovano et al., 2022; Ye et al., 2023). Therefore, EEG recordings exhibiting these features were excluded from the analysis in order to obtain a clean baseline and unbiased initial network estimates. Application of these strict quality and diagnostic criteria ensured internal consistency but resulted in a reduced number of EEGs. This process yielded a total of 23 EEGs included in the final analysis: 15 in non-falls patients (nF) and 8 in falls patients (F).

Fall assessment: Falls were systematically captured using the hospital-wide standardized fall reporting protocol. All fall events were initially documented by nursing staff using a structured fall report and subsequently reviewed and medically confirmed by a physician. The fall reports include detailed information on the circumstances of the fall, including time of day, environmental factors, use of walking aids, and the presence and severity of injuries. This established, mandatory procedure ensures comprehensive and reliable ascertainment of all in-hospital falls. In patients who experienced a fall, EEG was done within one week.

EEG recordings

EEG recordings were carried out with a Micromed EEG system with 23 electrodes (standard 10–20 electrode system plus 4–6 basal temporal electrodes) positioned according to the International 10–20 system. Impedance was kept below 5 kΩ. The sampling rate frequency was at least at 250 Hz. EEG signals were measured at rest with eyes closed for at least 20 mins.

Quantitative EEG analysis:

Data were processed in MATLAB (MathWorks, Natick, MA) and analyzed with the Brainstorm software tool (Tadel et al., 2011), which is documented and freely available for download online under the GNU general public license, and with the Brain Connectivity Toolbox (BCT) (Rubinov and Sporns, 2010). Detailed methods are described elsewhere (Pirker-Kees et al., 2024). Briefly, after visual inspection, independent component analysis (ICA) was performed for artifact detection, components containing blinks and eye movements were rejected, and EEG was segmented in artifact-free non overlapping 10 s epochs. Brain regions belonging to the DMN, the DAN and the SAN were identified by means of the Desikan-Killiany-atlas (Desikan et al., 2006), applying the assignments shown in Table 1. Specifically, the default mode network (DMN) comprised the medial prefrontal cortex, posterior cingulate cortex/precuneus, inferior parietal lobule, and lateral temporal cortex; the dorsal attention network (DAN) included the superior parietal lobule, intraparietal sulcus, and frontal eye fields; and the salience network (SAN) consisted primarily of the anterior insula and anterior cingulate cortex. Regions belonging to the DMN, DAN and SAN were selected for further investigation (Desikan et al., 2006; Kabbara et al., 2017). For each region, anatomical coordinates were defined as the centroid of the corresponding Desikan-Killiny parcel on the ICBM-152 cortical surface template.

Although EEG signals are recorded at the scalp using electrodes, individual electrodes do not map uniquely onto specific cortical regions of functional networks. Each electrode reflects a mixture of signals originating from multiple neural sources. For network analyses the EEG signals measured at the scalp were traced back from electrode level to the intracerebral localization of the neuronal sources (the scalp electrodes were used only as inputs to the inverse solution) using the standardized Low Resolution brain Electromagnetic Tomography (sLORETA) (Pascual-Marqui, 2002) (Grech et al., 2008). Accordingly, we generated a realistic head model for every subject based on the default anatomy ICBM-152 (Fonov et al., 2011; Fonov et al., 2009) and the leadfield matrix with OpenMEEG (Gramfort et al., 2010) applying the Boundary Element method (Gramfort et al., 2010). sLORETA allowed the projection of scalp EEG signals onto anatomically defined cortical regions. (Although DMN, DAN and SAN regions roughly project to frontal, parietal and temporal electrodes in the 10–20 system, all connectivity analyses were performed in source space using sLORETA to avoid volume conduction and spatial mixing inherent to scalp-level EEG).

FC was computed using the phase locking value (PLV) (Lachaux et al., 1999) between the regions belonging to the DMN, DAN and SAN. The FC in terms of the “connectivity matrix” between each pair of nodes (the previously identified regions of the DMN, DAN and SAN, Table 1) was computed over 10 s segments and then averaged over segments for each patient. For network analysis, the connection strength between each pair of nodes (i.e. the weight) was defined as their connectivity value. A proportional threshold of 0.40 was applied to the connectivity matrix (Vecchio et al., 2016). This density preserved network connectedness while reducing the influence of weak connections and is consistent with recommendations to evaluate graph-theoretical matrices at densities where network topology is stable (Rubinov and Sporns, 2010). The weighted connectivity matrices were normalized and then converted to weighted connection-length matrices as implemented in the BCT (Rubinov and Sporns, 2010). The characteristic path length was calculated, which is the average shortest path length between all pairs of nodes in the network. The global efficiency is the average inverse shortest path length in the network, a good and robust indicator for network function. A permutation test was performed, using Wilcoxon rank sum test as implemented in brainstorm (Bennett et al., 2009), a two-sided p < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant. Wilcoxon-rank-sum-test for independent and paired samples implemented in Matlab, Version 2021b, was used to compare global efficiency between both patient groups.

Results

We identified a cohort of 153 patients (90 female), mean age 81 ± 9 years who were discharged with a diagnosis of dementia within a one-year period and had an EEG done during their hospital stay. Of these patients, 33 (22%) experienced a fall during their hospital stay, while 120 patients did not fall in this period. Baseline characteristics of these patients are summarized in Table 2, documenting that in terms of demographic features and medical history no significant differences existed between patients with and without falls.

Overall, a total of 108 falls were documented in 33 patients, with 25 patients suffering from more than one fall (2–9 falls). Within the overall-cohort, most of the falls (92 falls, 84%) happened in the patient's room, primarily when moving out of bed. 52 falls (48%) happened during periods of disorientation, 2 falls occurred during episodes of agitation, while during 34 (31%) falls patients were somnolent/drowsy. None of these falls was associated with serious injuries. Among the 23 patients whose EEGs were analyzed in this study, none had qualitative or quantitative disturbances of consciousness documented at the time of EEG recording. The mean age of patients with falls was 83 ± 8 years compared to 80 ± 8 years in those without falls (n.s.). There was no difference in the use of sedative medication between the groups.

Quantitative EEG analysis:

At the nodal network level, we compared connectivity between the nodes of each network in F vs. nF. Connectivity was reduced in fallers compared to non-fallers between different brain areas across all three networks (DMN, DAN, SAN), affecting multiple frequency bands:

To name a few, in the DMN, FC was significantly reduced between the right precuneus and the left posterior cingulate gyrus in the delta, beta, and gamma bands in F vs. nF. In the DAN, FC was significantly reduced in the theta, alpha, and beta bands between the right inferior temporal gyrus and the right pars opercularis in F vs. nF. In the SAN, FC was significantly reduced between the right rostral middle frontal and the left middle frontal gyrus in the delta, theta, and alpha bands in F vs. nF. Beyond these representative findings, several additional significant functional connections were identified across the DMN, DAN, and SAN in different frequency bands. Graph-based network analysis was then performed to examine global structural brain network characteristics such as global efficiency, comparing our findings obtained in F with those in nF. In addition, characteristic path length was quantified as a measure of global network integration. Global efficiency in the DAN was significantly lower in fallers compared to non-fallers, both in the alpha band and in the beta band. This finding was paralleled by a significantly increased characteristic path length in fallers compared to non-fallers in the DAN (controls: mean ± SD = 3,9 ± 0,7; patients: mean ± SD = 4,1 ± 0,6, p = 0,0086). In the DMN and SAN, no significant differences in global efficiency were observed between fallers and non-fallers: characteristic path length did not differ significantly between groups in these networks (SAN: controls: mean ± SD = 3,2 ± 0,8, patients: mean ± SD = 2,7 ± 0,1, p = 0,77; DMN: controls: mean ± SD = 4,1 ± 0,4; patients: mean ± SD = 4,0 ± 0,6, p = 0,94). These findings suggest a network-specific reduction in top-down attentional efficiency in patients with falls.

Discussion

This study aimed to explore the relationship between alterations in functional connectivity in large-scale attentional networks and in-hospital fall risk among patients with dementia, with a particular focus on the DAN, SAN, and DMN. We observed a trend toward reduced global efficiency of the DAN in cognitively impaired individuals with a history of falls, particularly in the alpha and beta frequency bands. Although the statistical significance of these findings was marginal and the sample size limited, this pattern may point toward network level alteration associated with wall vulnerability in this population.

Given the exploratory nature of the study, the modest sample size, and the marginal statistical significance of some results, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Nevertheless, our observations are in line with previous literature indicating that attentional dysfunction contributes to gait instability and fall risk, particularly in populations with cognitive impairment (Maidan et al., 2019; Yogev-Seligmann et al., 2008). Comparable alterations of the DAN have been described in Parkinson's disease, where DAN degradation was linked to freezing of gait (Maidan et al., 2019) lending external support to the plausibility of our findings.

The DAN plays a critical role in managing attentionally demanding tasks by enabling goal-directed focus and suppressing distractions (Corbetta and Shulman, 2002). It consists of key regions, including the intraparietal sulcus, superior parietal lobule, and frontal eye fields, which collectively facilitate top-down attentional control during complex tasks (Vossel et al., 2014). Impairments within this network have been associated with deficits in attentional shifting, spatial awareness, and executive control, all of which are essential for safe navigation and fall prevention. Notably, aging and dementia are linked to deteriorations in dual- and multitasking abilities (Pashler, 1994), which depend heavily on an intact DAN. From a mechanistic perspective, reduced DAN efficiency could contribute to impaired adaptive responses to environmental demands during ambulation, thereby potentially increasing fall susceptibility, especially in cognitively impaired individuals. This interpretation remains speculative, but is consistent with models emphasizing the interaction between attention and motor control during complex behaviors such as walking (Lo et al., 2021). The functional relevance of the observed isolated interhemispheric connection between the bilateral caudal anterior cingulate cortices was observed in the faller group remains unclear. It may represent a compensatory mechanism or network reorganization in response to impaired attentional network integrity. Given the exploratory nature of these analyses and the limited sample size, however, this interpretation should be considered hypothesis-generating and warrants confirmation in larger cohorts.

Furthermore, the observed DAN disintegration in demented patients may relate to motoric cognitive risk syndrome (MCR), a preclinical marker of frailty and neurodegeneration that has been strongly associated with increased fall risk (Chhetri et al., 2017). Disrupted DAN connectivity could limit the ability to dynamically allocate attentional resources between motor and cognitive demands, leading to inefficient processing of gait-relevant sensory information and possibly contributing to maladaptive movement patterns.

At the nodal level, reduced FC between the right inferior temporal gyrus and right pars opercularis in fallers was observed. While these findings should be interpreted cautiously, previous studies have demonstrated that during the processing of unexpected stimuli alterations in DAN-related alpha band FC occur (Thut et al., 2006), and involve bilateral lateral occipital cortices during visual attention tasks (Hassan et al., 2017). These regions are implicated in visuospatial updating and grasp-planning adjustments in response to unexpected stimuli (Culham and Valyear, 2006). Taken together, these findings suggest that localized connectivity changes may contribute to broader attentional mechanisms relevant for postural control and gait adaption.

In the SAN, no significant differences in global efficiency were detected; however, localized reductions in functional connectivity between frontal regions involved in stimulus detection and attentional reorientation were observed (Kwon et al., 2021). Although subthreshold at the network level, these regional alterations may still influence attentional responsiveness during ambulation and warrant further investigation.

No significant differences in global DMN efficiency were observed, consistent with the network's predominant role in internally directed processes. Given that the DMN is typically deactivated during attentionally demanding tasks, and its relevance for gait-related control is likely limited in this context and in line with current conceptual models (Paoletti et al., 2022).

Significant differences in global efficiency were observed only within the DAN in the alpha and theta frequency bands. Nevertheless, several additional significant edge-wise differences were identified across all three resting-state networks and multiple frequency bands. While these findings indicate that fall-related alterations may extend beyond the DAN, the biological relevance of these individual connections remains unclear and requires validation in larger, adequately powered studies. In summary, our findings suggest that reduced global efficiency of the DAN may be associated with increased fall vulnerability in individuals with dementia. While these results require replication in larger independent cohorts, they provide preliminary evidence that attentional network integrity may represent a relevant neural substrate linking cognitive impairment and fall risk, and may serve as an impulse future investigation.

Limitations

Our study has some limitations, the main factor being linked to its retrospective character. Thus, to avoid distortion of data and analyses, we had to exclude many EEGs from our study due to diagnostic features or patient-factors known to affect FC (e.g. disturbances of consciousness, history of stroke, epilepsy, etc.) which could have altered the effects we wanted to investigate. This resulted in a relatively small number of EEGs included in the final analysis, the aggregation of falls with heterogeneous etiologies and this highly selected cohort limits the generalizability of the results. Second, statistical significance was marginal, suggesting that findings should be interpreted as preliminary and hypothesis-generating. EEG recordings were obtained within one week of the fall. Another limitation is the limited spatial specificity of scalp EEG–based network assignment and the susceptibility of phase-locking value measures to spurious coupling, warranting cautious interpretation of network efficiency metrics. As reduced functional connectivity is interpreted in our analysis as a predisposing factor for falls rather than a consequence of the fall itself, we assume that the temporal interval between the fall and EEG acquisition did not constitute a relevant confounding factor.

Conclusion

Despite these limitations, our results indicate that impaired global DAN efficiency and regional SAN connectivity may underlie increased fall risk in hospitalized dementia patients. These findings align with the concept of motoric cognitive risk syndrome and support further research on attentional network biomarkers for fall risk prediction.

Future prospective studies with larger samples and multimodal imaging will be essential to validate these findings and develop targeted interventions to prevent in-hospital falls.
