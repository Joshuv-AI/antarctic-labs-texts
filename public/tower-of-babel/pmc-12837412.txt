The relationship between the a body shape index and benign prostatic hyperplasia in middle-aged and elderly adults: a nationwide cohort study
Authors: Bing Li, Zhiqiang Zhang, Junping Li, Xiaoqiang Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study investigates the association between A Body Shape Index (ABSI) and risk of benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) in middle-aged and older men.MethodsData were obtained from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), including 3,223 men aged ≥ 45 years. ABSI was calculated from waist circumference, BMI, and height. Logistic regression and restricted cubic spline models were used to assess the association and potential nonlinear relationship between ABSI and incident BPH.ResultsDuring 4 years of follow-up, 260 new BPH cases were identified. A nonlinear association was observed: ABSI was significantly associated with increased BPH risk within a moderate range (7.420–8.916), but not at lower or higher ABSI levels (OR = 1.532, 95% CI: 1.015–2.314, p = 0.042).ConclusionABSI was associated with BPH risk in a nonlinear pattern. These findings suggest that ABSI may serve as a potential risk indicator, although further research is needed. Limitations include reliance on self-reported BPH diagnosis and lack of biomarker data.

Introduction

Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) is increasingly common as populations age and lifestyles shift [1, 2]. Hyperplasia within the transition zone enlarges the prostate and can impede urinary flow, causing lower urinary tract symptoms that erode quality of life and add to health-care costs [3–9]. Given this burden, markers that capture modifiable risk in mid-to-late life are clinically relevant. Visceral adiposity contributes to systemic inflammation, insulin resistance, and altered androgen–estrogen balance, all of which may influence prostatic growth. Conventional measures such as BMI inadequately capture fat distribution, whereas A Body Shape Index (ABSI) integrates waist circumference and height to better reflect central obesity. Although prior studies have examined obesity-related indices and BPH, few have evaluated ABSI—a metric specifically designed to quantify abdominal adiposity—as an independent predictor of BPH risk. This study therefore aimed to clarify this relationship using nationally representative longitudinal data [10]. Our goal was to generate evidence to support practical, non-invasive risk stratification.

Beyond examining the basic association, our analysis further explored potential nonlinear patterns using flexible spline models. These models help reveal thresholds or saturation zones that may be hidden in conventional linear analyses, thus enhancing clinical interpretability. Given ABSI’s simplicity and minimal measurement burden, any robust signal—linear or otherwise—could be readily translated to community screening and primary-care counseling.

We analyzed men in the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), using baseline examinations in 2011 and follow-up interviews in 2015. CHARLS applies multistage probability sampling across 28 provinces and collects questionnaire, examination, and laboratory data from adults aged ≥ 45 years.

Eligible participants for this analysis were men aged ≥ 45 years with complete sociodemographic information and responses in both 2011 and 2015. Fasting blood was drawn after ≥ 12 h; on-site hematology was performed, and remaining aliquots were transported under cold chain to a central laboratory for biochemical assays. Study oversight and participant consent followed approval from the Peking University IRB (IRB00001052-11015).

ABSI was computed from standardized anthropometry using the formula: ABSI = waist circumference/(BMI^{2/3} × height^{1/2}).

Anthropometric measurements followed CHARLS procedures. Variable definitions that mirror prior cohort work are summarized here to avoid redundancy.

Men who were free of BPH in 2011 but reported a physician diagnosis by 2015 were classified as incident BPH cases according to the follow-up questionnaire. Covariates specified a priori included age; education (middle school or below, high school, college or above); residence (urban or rural); marital status (married vs. non-married); smoking (never vs. current); alcohol consumption frequency (never, < 1 per month, ≥ 1 per month); sleep duration; and systolic/diastolic blood pressure measured three times with an Omron HEM-7200 (mean used).

Study population

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median (IQR) as appropriate; categorical variables as counts (percentages). Baseline characteristics and BPH incidence were compared across ABSI quartiles (Q1–Q4) using one-way ANOVA, Kruskal–Wallis, or χ² tests.

We estimated associations between ABSI and incident BPH using logistic regression with three models: Model 1: unadjusted; Model 2: adjusted for age, education, residence, marital status; Model 3: additionally adjusted for smoking, drinking frequency, sleep duration, SBP, and DBP. ABSI was modeled per SD and by quartiles. Prespecified interactions [ABSI×covariate] and subgroup analyses were examined. Nonlinearity was evaluated with restricted cubic splines (R 4.3.1; rms). Model calibration was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test and pseudo-R² statistics, while all variance inflation factors were below 2. All analytic code was validated via repeated runs to ensure stability of estimates.

Measurements

ABSI was calculated based on waist circumference (WC), height, and weight, using the following formula: ABSI = WC/(BMI^2/3 ×height^1/2).

BPH data

All questionnaire items used in this study were obtained from the standardized survey instruments of the CHARLS, rather than developed specifically for this research. CHARLS questionnaires have been validated and published previously [11]. Incident BPH was identified among participants who reported no diagnosis at baseline (2011) but answered “Yes” to the follow-up question “Has a doctor ever told you that you have benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH)?” in 2015. This self-reported physician diagnosis was the only available BPH indicator in the CHARLS dataset, consistent with prior epidemiologic analyses using the same database. Because of the structure of the CHARLS dataset, no objective diagnostic information for BPH is available. CHARLS does not collect prostate-specific clinical measurements such as the International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS), digital rectal examination findings, prostate volume or ultrasound results, or PSA testing. In addition, CHARLS is not linked to hospital electronic medical records, making retrospective validation of self-reported diagnoses impossible. Therefore, self-reported physician diagnosis is the only feasible indicator of BPH status in this study, consistent with previous epidemiologic analyses using CHARLS. Covariates included sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, and physical examination metrics. education (middle school or below/high school/college or above), Sociodemographic variables included age, residence (urban/rural), and marital status (married/unmarried, divorced, or widowed). Lifestyle factors included smoking status (never smoker/current smoker), drinking frequency (never/monthly < 1 time/monthly ≥ 1 time), and sleep duration. completed with guidance from trained interviewers., This information was collected through self-reported questionnaires Physical examination metrics included systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP), measured three times using the Omron HEM-7200 electronic sphygmomanometer, with the average taken.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables following a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation, while skewed distributions were shown as median (interquartile range). binning-based variables were expressed as frequency (percentage). One-way ANOVA, Kruskal–Wallis rank-sum H test, or chi-square test were used to compare baseline characteristics and BPH incidence across ABSI quartiles (quartiles Q1–Q4).

Three logistic models models assessed the association between ABSI and BPH risk, with ABSI analyzed as a continuous variable (per standard deviation) and as a binning-based variable (quartile groups). including age, model 1 specification was crude; model 2 specification controlling for sociodemographic factors, education, residence, and marital status; model 3 specification further controlling for lifestyle and physiological factors, such as smoking, drinking frequency, sleep duration, systolic blood pressure, and diastolic blood pressure. stratified evaluations were conducted across variables such as age, education, residence, marital status, smoking, drinking frequency, and sleep duration. Multiplicative effect modification terms [ABSI × (covariate)] were added to the logistic models to formally test for effect modification, with p-values reported. To examine potential effect modifications by sociodemographic characteristics and lifestyle factors on the ABSI-BPH relationship. To evaluate model adequacy, we calculated McFadden’s pseudo-R² values and conducted the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit goodness-of-fit test for the fully accounting logistic models model (model 3 specification). Multicollinearity among covariates was assessed using VIF diagnostics (VIFs), with all values below 2, indicating no serious collinearity. Additionally, we included multiplicative effect modification terms between ABSI and key covariates (e.g., age, smoking, drinking) to explore potential effect modification, and stratified analyses were performed accordingly. Additionally, flexible cubic spline terms were used to model the nonlinear dose-response relationship between ABSI and BPH risk. Statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.3.1. flexible cubic spline terms were implemented using the “rms” package with the rcs function to define spline terms and fit regression models. All tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Our analysis indicates that ABSI relates to BPH risk, but not uniformly across the distribution. The observed association was limited to a moderate ABSI range, suggesting that abdominal adiposity may contribute most to prostatic growth within a specific physiological window. At very low ABSI, limited visceral fat may dampen pro-inflammatory cues; at very high ABSI, systemic metabolic disturbances or selection factors could obscure adiposity-specific effects.

Nevertheless, these hypotheses remain tentative and require further empirical validation.

Results

A total of 3,223 men were included in this study (mean age 59.4 ± 8.7 years), with 260 developing BPH during follow-up. Stratified analysis

To explore potential heterogeneity in the association between ABSI and BPH risk, we conducted subgroup analyses by age, education, marital status, residence, smoking, drinking, and sleep duration. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test indicated acceptable model fit (p = 0.41), and the McFadden’s pseudo-R² for the fully adjusted model was 0.062, indicating a modest explanatory value. No evidence of multicollinearity was observed (all VIFs < 2). Interaction terms between ABSI and key covariates did not reach statistical significance (p for interaction > 0.05), and stratified analyses yielded consistent findings across subgroups. Subgroup analyses were performed to test for potential effect modification of the ABSI–BPH association. The interaction term for age was statistically significant (P = 0.029), indicating that the strength of the association differed significantly by age group. Specifically, the odds ratio (OR) for BPH per standard deviation increase in ABSI was 1.29 (95% CI: 0.91–1.84) in participants aged ≤ 65 years, and 0.74 (95% CI: 0.48–1.14) in those aged > 65 years. For all other subgroup variables, including frequent alcohol consumption (drinking 1 time/month), the interaction terms were not statistically significant (all P for interaction > 0.05). These results suggest that the association between ABSI and BPH is generally consistent across most subgroups, with the notable exception of age (Fig. 3).

Discussion

ABSI is a novel anthropometric measure that more accurately captures visceral fat characteristics compared to traditional metrics like BMI. Research on ABSI has grown, demonstrating positive linear associations with visceral fat levels, all-cause mortality, and cardiovascular disease (CVD) in the general population.

This study explored the association between ABSI and BPH using data from the CHARLS. Within a specific range, ABSI showed a positive association with BPH risk, demonstrating a clear dose-response relationship. BPH risk increased with higher ABSI values, while no association was observed at very high or very low ABSI levels. For ABSI values between 7.420 and 8.916, high ABSI may correspond to a greater proportion of visceral abdominal fat relative to peripheral fat, given specific height and weight. Excess visceral fat is linked to several adverse metabolic changes. In summary, this study is the first to confirm an association between ABSI and BPH risk, suggesting that controlling ABSI may help reduce BPH incidence.This finding enhances our understanding of BPH pathogenesis and offers new perspectives for its early prevention and intervention.

ABSI is a novel anthropometric measure that more accurately captures visceral fat characteristics compared to traditional metrics like BMI. Research on ABSI has grown, demonstrating positive linear associations with visceral fat levels, all-cause mortality, and cardiovascular disease (CVD) in the general population. ABSI strongly predicts all-cause and CVD mortality, showing a linear positive association with all-cause and CVD mortality in individuals with metabolic syndrome (MetS) after adjusting for multiple covariates. Compared to participants in the lowest ABSI quartile, those in the highest quartile have elevated risks of all-cause and CVD mortality [12]. For every 0.01-unit increase in ABSI, all-cause mortality risk rises by 20% [13]. Further studies suggest that ABSI can predict 10-year CVD risk and is closely associated with increased coronary heart disease (CHD) prevalence among obese individuals in the U.S [14, 15]. Research also indicates that ABSI may be an independent risk factor for hypertension, assisting in predicting hypertension risk [16]. A positive linear relationship has been identified between ABSI and female infertility risk, suggesting that non-invasive ABSI measurements could aid in early identification of high-risk individuals in large-scale screenings, potentially reducing infertility incidence [17]. Specific indicators of body shape and fat distribution show varied associations with inflammation in adults, and distinct inflammation patterns across obesity subtypes may influence chronic inflammation-related diseases [18]. A nonlinear, inverse L-shaped association exists between ABSI and depression scores, with higher scores observed in participants with abdominal obesity [19]. ABSI may also be used to assess the risk of abdominal aortic calcification [20].

How do ABSI and BPH interact? Subgroup analysis showed that higher ABSI was significantly associated with increased BPH risk, particularly with notable risk differences across age groups and lifestyle factors, such as drinking frequency. We found that an increased body shape index, particularly those associated with insulin resistance, may be linked to mechanisms driving prostate tissue growth and hyperplasia. Insulin resistance is often accompanied by chronic low-grade systemic inflammation, which may promote BPH progression. Specifically, insulin resistance can lead to the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines from adipose tissue, such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and C-reactive protein (CRP). These cytokines not only worsen insulin resistance but may also act directly on prostate tissue, inducing cellular proliferation and tissue remodeling [21, 22]. Chronic inflammation is regarded as a key driver in BPH pathology. Persistent inflammatory responses can induce proliferation of prostate stromal and epithelial cells and alter the local microenvironment, promoting fibrosis and tissue repair. This process aligns with the systemic inflammation driven by insulin resistance, suggesting that insulin resistance may accelerate BPH onset and progression via inflammatory pathways [23, 24]. Insulin resistance may also impact hormone metabolism, particularly sex hormone levels, influencing prostate growth regulation. Insulin resistance is commonly associated with metabolic syndrome, which often features reduced testosterone levels and relatively higher estrogen levels. These hormonal shifts may contribute to BPH development. Prostate tissue is highly androgen-dependent, and androgens such as dihydrotestosterone (DHT) promote the proliferation of prostate epithelial and stromal cells by binding to androgen receptors. The hormonal imbalance associated with insulin resistance may alter prostate sensitivity to DHT and other androgens, further promoting abnormal prostate growth [25–28]. In insulin resistance, while insulin signaling is impaired, IGF-1 signaling remains active and can promote prostate cell proliferation and survival through its receptor. IGF-1 activates the PI3K/Akt pathway, encouraging cell proliferation and inhibiting apoptosis, which may increase prostate volume and exacerbate hyperplasia. A recent study indicated that patients with metabolic syndrome have higher IPSS than those without; 59.3% of metabolic syndrome patients experienced severe symptoms, compared to 36.2% of those without. Metabolic syndrome is often accompanied by hypertension, dyslipidemia, and vascular dysfunction, which can reduce local blood flow. Impaired blood supply to the prostate tissue may trigger repair and hyperplasia responses. The combination of local ischemia and chronic inflammation may further promote stromal proliferation in the prostate. Insulin resistance and metabolic imbalance are often associated with increased oxidative stress, where excessive free radical production can damage prostate cells. Oxidative stress may worsen chronic inflammation by activating pro-inflammatory pathways and can directly disrupt cell growth regulation, leading to abnormal extracellular matrix deposition and fibrosis, thus promoting BPH progression. It is important to note that the biological mechanisms discussed—such as systemic inflammation, insulin resistance, oxidative stress, and hormonal imbalance—are hypothesized pathways that may underlie the observed association between ABSI and BPH. However, given the absence of direct biomarker or mechanistic data in our cohort, these explanations should be viewed as exploratory and speculative, intended to guide future research rather than imply causality.

Our findings note that the observed association was marginally significant, and the confidence interval approached the null value. Therefore, this result should be interpreted with caution. Interestingly, the nonlinear dose-response and the restricted cubic spline analysis analysis revealed that the association between ABSI and BPH was only statistically significant within a specific ABSI range (7.420 to 8.916). This suggests a potential threshold effect, although the underlying biological or clinical rationale for this pattern remains unclear. It is possible that at very low or very high ABSI values, other metabolic or physiological mechanisms may offset the influence of visceral fat on prostate hyperplasia. One possible explanation for the attenuated risk at higher ABSI levels is a survival or selection bias, whereby individuals with extremely high ABSI may be underrepresented due to higher comorbidities or mortality. Alternatively, it is possible that at very high levels of visceral adiposity, the pathophysiological mechanisms linking ABSI to BPH for instance inflammation, hormonal imbalance may plateau or be offset by other systemic metabolic dysfunctions. Nevertheless, this interpretation should be approached with caution, and further mechanistic studies are warranted to validate and explain this observation. Further research with larger sample sizes and more precise measurements is needed to confirm these findings. Our findings suggest that ABSI may be considered a potential non-invasive marker for identifying individuals at higher risk of BPH; however, further longitudinal and interventional studies are necessary before any clinical recommendations can be made.

From a clinical perspective, understanding obesity-related risk factors for BPH is also pertinent to surgical decision-making. Recent studies have reported favorable outcomes and safety of modern endoscopic techniques in elderly or metabolically compromised patients [29, 30]. Although our study did not assess treatment outcomes, identifying individuals at metabolic risk could facilitate earlier medical management and potentially reduce the need for surgical intervention.

Although this study identifies an association between ABSI and BPH, certain limitations should be noted. A major limitation of this study is that BPH was determined based solely on participants’ self-reported physician diagnosis. Although this approach has been widely used in CHARLS-based population studies, it may not fully reflect clinically confirmed BPH, which typically requires assessment through the IPSS, physical examination, or ultrasound imaging. The absence of such objective data could have introduced misclassification bias, likely leading to underestimation of the true association between ABSI and BPH. Future research incorporating standardized diagnostic instruments is therefore essential to validate our findings. This limitation could lead to outcome misclassification and may attenuate the strength of observed associations. While previous studies using CHARLS data have adopted similar criteria, future research incorporating objective clinical assessments is warranted to enhance diagnostic accuracy. The absence of detailed clinical records in the CHARLS dataset prevented us from excluding individuals with prostate cancer or a history of prostate surgery. This lack of exclusion may introduce bias in BPH classification, and the potential impact of this limitation should be considered when interpreting the findings. Finally, several relevant metabolic and clinical factors—including diabetes, lipid profile, physical activity, medication use (e.g., finasteride), and testosterone levels—were unavailable or incomplete in the CHARLS dataset and could not be adjusted for. The absence of these covariates may result in residual confounding, and future studies with more comprehensive clinical data are needed to validate these findings. Future studies should undertake larger, long-term cohort studies with biomarker analyses to validate these findings. Moreover, we explored whether CHARLS contained alternative variables—such as urinary symptom items or medication use—that might allow stricter confirmation of BPH status, but no such information is available in the dataset. Because misclassification is therefore unavoidable and likely non-differential with respect to ABSI, any resulting bias would tend to attenuate the observed associations toward the null, meaning that the true relationship may be underestimated.

Finally, several relevant metabolic and clinical factors—including diabetes, lipid profile, physical activity, medication use (e.g., finasteride), and testosterone levels—were unavailable or incomplete in the CHARLS dataset and could not be adjusted for. The absence of these covariates may result in residual confounding, and future studies with more comprehensive clinical data are needed to validate these findings. Future studies should undertake larger, long-term cohort studies with biomarker analyses to validate these findings. Moreover, we explored whether CHARLS contained alternative variables—such as urinary symptom items or medication use—that might allow stricter confirmation of BPH status, but no such information is available in the dataset. Because misclassification is therefore unavoidable and likely non-differential with respect to ABSI, any resulting bias would tend to attenuate the observed associations toward the null, meaning that the true relationship may be underestimated.

Although this study identifies an association between ABSI and BPH, certain limitations should be noted. A major limitation of this study is that BPH was determined based solely on participants’ self-reported physician diagnosis. Although this approach has been widely used in CHARLS-based population studies, it may not fully reflect clinically confirmed BPH, which typically requires assessment through the IPSS, physical examination, or ultrasound imaging. The absence of such objective data could have introduced misclassification bias, likely leading to underestimation of the true association between ABSI and BPH. Future research incorporating standardized diagnostic instruments is therefore essential to validate our findings. Conclusion

In summary, ABSI showed a nonlinear association with BPH risk among middle-aged and older Chinese men. These results suggest, but do not establish, a potential link between abdominal adiposity and BPH development. Further longitudinal and mechanistic studies are warranted to confirm these associations.
