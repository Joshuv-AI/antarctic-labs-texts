Fare : Failure Resilience in Learned Visual Navigation Control
Zishuo Wang, Joel Loo, David Hsu

Abstract
While imitation learning (IL) enables effective visual navigation, IL policies are prone to unpredictable failures in out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios. We advance the notion of failure-resilient policies , which not only detect failures but also recover from them automatically. Failure recognition that identifies the factors causing failure is key to informing recovery: e.g. pinpointing image regions triggering failure detections can provide cues to guide recovery. We present Fare , a framework to construct failure-resilient IL policies, embedding OOD-detection and recognition in them without using explicit failure data, and pairing them with recovery heuristics. Real-world experiments show that Fare enables failure recovery across two different policy architectures, enabling robust long-range navigation in complex environments.



I
Introduction

Visual navigation is an attractive approach to robot navigation, leveraging rich visual information from low-cost sensors
[
1
]
. Imitation learning (IL) has emerged as a key method to learn visual navigation policies
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
, but is inherently limited by training data. IL policies may fail unpredictably on inputs outside the training distribution, often without clear explanation
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
. This work develops a mechanism to enable IL policies to detect and recover from failures, supporting robust open-world navigation.

We argue that
failure resilience
in learned visual navigation extends beyond detecting failures, to also encompass automatic recovery from failures. Current approaches fall short in two ways.
Firstly, they focus primarily on detecting failures, to trigger and learn from operator interventions
[
8
]
. We contend that the ability to independently recover is needed for greater autonomy, and that
recognising
the causes of failure is key to guiding recovery.
Figure 1
shows examples of
informed recovery
:
e.g.
, given dynamic obstacles unseen in training, a resilient policy not only detects a failure but also
recognises
and highlights the moving person as the source of failure. This informs recovery actions, enabling the robot to avoid collisions and return to navigating.
Secondly, existing methods may fail to capture policies’ true failure modes, as they often rely on failure detectors external to the policy
[
9
]
. This limits their ability to accurately detect failures and recover the robot to states in which the policy performs well. We argue that failure detection and recognition should be embedded into policies, and also unified to ensure detected failures are aligned with recognised causes.

not only detects these failures but also
recognises

These motivate two desiderata for failure-resilient policies:
(i)
informed recovery guided by failure recognition, and
(ii)
unified failure detection and recognition embedded within policies. We incorporate these in
Fare
, a novel framework for building failure-resilient visual navigation policies. Following prior work, we frame failures as scenarios out-of-distribution (OOD) for the policy
[
10
,
6
,
11
,
7
]
.
Fare
augments and retrains IL policies into OOD-aware policies—embedding OOD detection and recognition into the policy’s latent space—then pairs them with informed recovery strategies. OOD recognition is defined as the task of identifying input image regions triggering OOD detections, providing spatial feedback to select corrective actions for recovery.
Fare
achieves OOD-awareness without explicit failure data, by shaping the latent space to separate ID and OOD inputs.
We show in the real world that
Fare
enables two different policies—DECISION
[
2
]
and the General Navigation Model (GNM)
[
3
]
to detect and recover from critical failures, allowing a robot to complete a challenging 300m indoor/outdoor route without intervention.

II
Related Work

II-A

Learned visual navigation

Navigating purely with high-dimensional image inputs is challenging for classical robotic systems
[
1
]
. Recent progress in visual navigation is enabled by learning perceptual modules
[
12
,
13
]
or even entire policies end-to-end
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
. Among these, end-to-end imitation learning (IL) has proven especially effective
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
, enabling robust navigation in the wild
[
14
,
15
]
. However, such policies remain opaque and offer no indication of impending failure
[
9
]
. We address this by defining
failure resilience
for end-to-end IL policies and proposing
Fare
, a framework to augment IL policies into failure-resilient policies.

II-B

Failure detection

Robust visual navigation in open-world settings hinges on the ability to detect failures
[
16
]
. Some recent approaches learn from explicit failure data: BADGR
[
17
]
heuristically labels failures in collected training data post hoc, while others learn from human interventions that precede failures
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
. Notably,
[
20
]
combines this with novelty detection to capture both user-defined failures and OOD events.

Since imitation learning (IL) datasets typically include only successful trajectories, unsupervised failure detection without explicit failure data becomes crucial when learning policies with IL. Failure detection is often framed as identifying OOD inputs that lie outside the IL dataset’s distribution
[
6
]
. This can be approached via epistemic uncertainty estimation
[
21
]
using ensembles or Bayesian neural networks
[
9
]
, but such methods are often too computationally intensive for real-time navigation with limited compute
[
22
]
.

Alternative methods seek greater efficiency, often at the cost of using detectors external to the policy which may not fully capture the its failure modes and tend toward overly conservative detections
[
9
]
. Autoencoders
[
6
,
7
]
and density estimators
[
23
,
24
]
are common approaches, but may not accurately capture the data distribution as they are trained on observations and exclude action data, often producing overly conservative detections
[
9
]
.
[
25
]
apply Random Network Distillation to learn an external detector for the joint observation-action distribution. FAIL-Detect
[
22
]
compares a range of OOD scoring approaches, and combines them with conformal prediction for OOD detection with statistical guarantees. Finally, some methods leverage LLMs to identify and explain failures
[
26
,
27
]
, though these lack efficiency and insight into the policy’s specific failure modes.

Fare
focuses on efficiency and on capturing the policy’s true failure modes, achieved by embedding failure detection within the IL policy.

II-C

Failure recovery

Robust navigation requires not only the ability to detect failures, but also to independently recover from them. Existing approaches largely terminate the episode on detection, and seek human intervention
[
28
,
9
,
8
]
. A small subset of works attempt to autonomously recover from failure by executing predefined actions open-loop—
e.g.
returning to a default state
[
7
]
or executing a default behaviour
[
6
]
. Some systems layer increasingly aggressive preset actions
[
29
]
to improve recovery chances.
[
30
]
further requests interventions and learns from subsequent recovery demonstrations.

Fare
emphasises robustness through autonomous recovery and highlights the importance of OOD recognition in providing cues for informed, efficient recovery. To build failure-resilient policies,
Fare
combines OOD-aware policies with unified OOD detection and recognition embedded inside, with a recovery policy guided by recognition results.

III
Problem formulation

This work tackles the problem of augmenting end-to-end IL policies for visual navigation with
failure resilience
. Assuming the dataset for IL contains no failure trajectories, failures can be viewed as OOD scenarios with respect to the dataset’s distribution. We decompose the problem into the subproblems of obtaining an OOD-aware policy from the IL policy, and of obtaining a recovery policy that leverages the OOD-aware policy’s OOD detection and recognition results. IL for visual navigation learns a policy
\pi_{\text{IL}}{}:\mathcal{O}{}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}{}
from a dataset
\mathcal{D}{}=\{(o_{1},a_{1}),\dots,(o_{N},a_{N})\}
, where
o_{i}\in\mathcal{O}{}
are RGB or depth images, and
a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}{}
are actions typically given as target velocities. An OOD-aware policy is
\pi_{\text{IL-OOD}}:\mathcal{O}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{M}
, mapping observation
o_{t}
to action
a_{t}
, binary OOD detection
b_{t}\in\mathcal{B}
and a representation of the location of OOD regions influencing the detection,
M_{t}\in\mathcal{M}
. We seek to obtain
\pi_{\text{IL-OOD}}{}
, given a specific policy network architecture and
\mathcal{D}{}
. Since many IL datasets only contain successful demonstrations,
\pi_{\text{IL-OOD}}{}
must be obtained without requiring additional failure data. We also seek to obtain
\pi_{\text{Rec}}{}
, a recovery policy which modulates or overrides
\pi_{\text{IL-OOD}}{}
to take corrective actions in OOD scenarios, selecting appropriate actions on the basis of spatial feedback from OOD recognition
M_{t}
.
\pi_{\text{Rec}}{}
’s actions either recover the robot to an in-distribution state, or halts it and calls for external assistance if recovery is not possible.

IV
Approach

achieves failure-resilience through augmenting learned policies with OOD-awareness. Apart from generating actions, OOD-aware policies also detect when OOD inputs are encountered (
b{}_{t}
) and localise the OOD features in the observations (
M{}_{t}
). Detection and recognition enable informed recovery from OOD or failure scenarios.
Fare
employs a heuristic recovery policy taking
b{}_{t},M{}_{t}
as inputs.

Fare
(
Figure 2
) provides a framework to modify visual navigation policies trained with IL,
\pi_{\text{IL}}{}
, into OOD-aware policies,
\pi_{\text{IL-OOD}}
. It also provides a heuristic recovery policy,
\pi_{\text{Rec}}{}
, that makes corrective actions in OOD scenarios based on OOD recognition information from
\pi_{\text{IL-OOD}}{}
.

IV-A

OOD-aware learned navigation policy,
\pi_{\text{IL-OOD}}{}

OOD-awareness involves detecting OOD scenarios and pinpointing the corresponding OOD regions in the visual input. We emphasise that OOD-awareness should be task-relevant, identifying OOD scenarios and pinpointing OOD regions related to failures on the navigation task the policy is trained for. Our policy,
\pi_{\text{IL-OOD}}{}
, takes an image input
I_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times H\times W}
and outputs action
a_{t}
, a binary OOD flag
b_{t}
, and a heatmap
M_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times W}
that identifies the OOD features in
I
.
Fare
augments a policy
\pi_{\text{IL}}{}
into an OOD-aware one in two phases. First, during training, a structured latent space capturing task-relevant factors from
I_{t}
is learned by augmenting behaviour cloning with additional regularisation. Second, during inference, conformal prediction and Grad-CAM are applied to features from the policy’s latent space for OOD detection and recognition respectively.

Learning task-relevant OOD-awareness with VIB.
We learn task-relevant latent representations
Z
, with the Variational Information Bottleneck
[
31
]
. This compresses input observations
O
into
Z
, while maximally retaining information crucial to predicting output actions
A
. Given
I(\cdot;\cdot)
as mutual information, we maximise:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{VIB}}=I(A;Z)-\beta I(Z;O)

(1)

Following
[
31
]
, we apply variational approximations to optimise this intractable objective, yielding the loss:

\max_{\phi,\theta}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{(o,a)\in\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{E}_{q_{\phi}(z|x)}[\log p_{\theta}(a|z)]-\beta\text{KL}[q_{\phi}(z|o)||p(z)]

(2)

q_{\phi},p_{\theta}
are the visual encoder and action decoder. The first term simplifies to a behaviour cloning loss, while the second term regularises the latent representation
Z
to stay close to prior
p(z)
, which we define as a unit Gaussian
\mathcal{N}(0,\textbf{I})
. This regularisation means that at inference, an OOD input
\hat{o}
is likely to produce latent feature
\hat{z}
that diverges significantly from the prior. Thus the KL divergence
\text{KL}[q_{\phi}(z|o)||\mathcal{N}(0,\textbf{I})]
can serve as an effective scalar score indicative of OOD inputs during inference.

Learning with VIB can be viewed as augmenting a standard IL setup with additional regularisation of the latent features between a policy’s visual encoder and action decoder. This serves as a lightweight approach to instill task-relevant OOD-awareness into IL policies spanning a range of architectures, without supervision with explicit failure data.

OOD detection with conformal prediction.

Binary detection
b_{t}
involves thresholding scalar OOD scores, but setting appropriate thresholds is challenging, especially since the threshold may be time-varying due to changing dynamics and scene appearance
[
22
]
. Conformal prediction (CP), particularly functional CP, provides a principled, distribution-free way to compute thresholds by generating a prediction band
C_{\alpha}{}
at a user-defined significance level
\alpha\in[0,1]
. Under mild assumptions,
C_{\alpha}{}
contains the true score with probability
1-\alpha
, enabling confident rejection of scores outside the band
[
32
,
22
]
. Functional CP is an efficient, training-free method that wraps around a sequence of scores from any model.

We apply functional CP to a calibration set of trajectories to obtain time-varying thresholds on the KL scores. Similar to
[
22
]
, we predict a one-sided
C_{\alpha}{}
that provides an upper bound on whether a score is in-distribution with probability
1-\alpha
. We calibrate on a held-out split from the IL dataset,
\mathcal{C}{}
. We chunk trajectories into segments of
T
steps to standardise length, obtaining
\mathcal{C}{}_{\text{scores}}=\{(s_{i,0},\dots,s_{i,T}):i=1,\dots,\lvert\mathcal{C}{}\rvert\}
. Following
[
32
]
, we split
\mathcal{C}{}
into
\mathcal{C}{}_{\mu}
to compute the mean score across trajectories for each timestep, and
\mathcal{C}_{w}
to compute the width of the band
C_{\alpha}{}
. Given the mean scores
\mu=(s_{0},\dots,s_{T})
,
w
is computed based on the maximum deviations from
\mu
per trajectory. Specifically, given

S=\{d_{j}:j=1,\dots,\lvert\mathcal{C}{}_{w}\rvert\}\text{ where }d_{j}=\max\limits_{t\in[0,T]}\{\mu_{t}-s_{t}\},

(3)

w
is the
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of
S
. The prediction band is the set of intervals
\{[-\infty,\mu_{t}+w]:t=1,\dots,T\}
, where scores above the upper bound are rejected as OOD.

OOD recognition with Grad-CAM.
Recognition aims to identify regions in observations
o\in\mathcal{O}{}
which contribute to the OOD detection. To do so, we apply Grad-CAM
[
33
]
, a visual explanation method for interpreting CNN outputs. Grad-CAM is a training-free module integrated into
\pi_{\text{IL-OOD}}{}
at test time, offering efficiency as it requires only a single backward pass through the visual encoder.

Grad-CAM produces recognition heatmaps that highlight spatial regions influencing the model’s predictions. It computes gradients of the prediction with respect to feature map activations in preceding convolutional layers, obtaining weights capturing the contribution of each feature map to the prediction. To localise OOD-related visual features, we apply Grad-CAM on the KL-based OOD scores predicted by the model. As deeper layers tend to capture higher-level semantic concepts (
e.g.
object parts)
[
34
]
, we use Grad-CAM on the last layer of the visual encoder
q_{\phi}
to encourage meaningful recognition results. Specifically, we compute the weight for the
k
-th feature map of the layer as:

\alpha_{k}=\frac{1}{Z}\sum\limits_{i}\sum\limits_{j}\frac{\partial\text{KL}[q_{\phi}(z_{t}|o_{t})\lVert\mathcal{N}(0,\textbf{I})]}{\partial A^{k}_{ij}}

(4)

where
A^{k}
is the
k
-th feature map and
Z
normalises across all
A^{k}
. The recognition result is:

M_{\text{Grad-CAM}}=\text{ReLU}\left(\sum_{k}\alpha_{k}A^{k}\right)

(5)

The resulting
M_{\text{Grad-CAM}}
has the same dimensions as
A^{k}
, which we upsample with bilinear interpolation to obtain
M
.

IV-B

Heuristic recovery policy,
\pi_{\text{Rec}}{}

The recovery policy aims to take informed corrective actions when OOD scenarios are detected, and otherwise pass through
\pi_{\text{IL-OOD}}{}
’s actions during normal operation. Our policy,
\pi_{\text{Rec}}{}
, selects the appropriate corrective action from a discrete set of macro-actions. This set is designed to provide primitives that
(i)

backtrack
the robot through known safe states,
(ii)

perturb
it out of the OOD scenario,
(iii)
halts the robot and
seeks human assistance
, if recovery is unrealistic and some termination condition is reached. We define the appropriate corrective action to be that which maximally avoids OOD features in the observation
o_{t}
.
\pi_{\text{Rec}}{}
uses the OOD recognition heatmap
M_{t}
to identify OOD features and inform selection of corrective actions.

Concretely,
\pi_{\text{Rec}}{}
’s action set comprises {
backtrack
,
rotate_left
,
rotate_right
,
get_help
}.
\pi_{\text{Rec}}{}
selects among the first 3 actions during recovery, with
get_help
triggered only after
t_{\text{max\_tries}}{}
attempts to recover without returning to a non-OOD state as indicated by
b{}_{t}
. To select among the first 3 actions, we discretise
M{}_{t}
along the horizontal axis into 3 bins. Each bin is marked as containing OOD features if the number of high-valued pixels exceeds a threshold.
\pi_{\text{Rec}}{}
selects
rotate_left
/
rotate_right
if the right/left bins are OOD, to perturb the robot away from the OOD region. Otherwise, it will
backtrack
, which executes a cached sequence of past actions in reverse.

V
Experiments

Our experiments aim to validate the hypotheses:
(H1)

Fare
produces OOD-aware policies that are effective at task-relevant OOD detection and recognition.
(H2)
Our
Fare
policy is effective at recovering from failures of the original visual navigation policy.
(H3)

Fare
is applicable across different visual navigation policies.

We train all methods on the dataset in
[
2
]
, comprising 30 hours of teleoperated trajectories demonstrating the task of path-following navigation in indoor and outdoor scenes. We identify four types of critical navigation failures to detect and recover from, which are OOD for this dataset:

NF1

Sensor failure.
The robot receives blacked out images. The expected outcome is to seek human assistance, as the robot cannot recover on its own.

NF2

Blocked pathways.
The robot’s path is blocked by obstacles or untraversable regions. Since the dataset’s trajectories focus on following clear paths, this is OOD. We consider
(i)

local minima
, from which recovery is possible through a short sequence of local, reactive actions, and also
(ii)

dead-ends
, which require substantially longer interventions or replanning to escape, and are irrecoverable for a local navigation policy.

NF3

Close-range dynamic obstacles.
Sudden dynamic obstacles appearing close to the robot. As successful demonstrations avoid dynamic obstacles at a distance, such situations are OOD. The expected outcome is to halt and backtrack/perturb away from the sudden obstacle, then resume navigation along the path.

NF4

Infeasible image goals.
Specific to image goal-conditioned policies where the goal is dissimilar to current observations. We focus on the irrecoverable case where the goal is not locally reachable, and the expected outcome is for the robot to seek human assistance.

We collect testing data on a Boston Dynamics Spot robot, equipped with 3 Realsense D435i cameras from which we log RGB images roughly covering a 140
∘
horizontal FoV. Our real-world experiments are conducted on the same robot, with all policies running onboard an Nvidia Orin AGX.

We evaluate performance of OOD-aware policies on OOD detection and recognition, comparing them against selected baselines (
Table I
). Three classes of baselines are selected:
density-based
approaches learn a density estimator for the training distribution;
one-class discriminators
learn to output a continuous metric without directly modelling the training distribution;
reconstruction-based
approaches indirectly model the training distribution by learning to reproduce observations, with post-hoc metrics applied onto the models’ outputs to detect and localise OOD inputs.

For density-based methods, we fit a normalising flow (NF) to observations with a Gaussian prior, using estimated likelihoods as the OOD score. For one-class discriminators, we use Random Network Distillation (RND)
[
25
]
, which trains a predictor to map training data to output of a fixed random function, using prediction error as a measure of sample novelty and the OOD score. In prior art, these two approaches detect OOD inputs but do not support recognition. Reconstruction-based methods include autoencoders (AE)
[
6
]
and variational autoencoders (VAE-R)
[
7
]
. We apply post-hoc metrics based on reconstruction error: mean-squared error for OOD detection, and pixel-wise errors for recognition. Since
Fare
’s detection/recognition metrics apply to any VIB-based model, we test them with VAE-KL, which shares weights with VAE-R but uses KL scores and Grad-CAM instead.

We collect a test set of 180 trajectories: 90 with a single failure near the end, and 90 normal (
Figure 1
). Failures are evenly distributed across three types—sensor failure, blocked paths, and close-range dynamic obstacles (
NF1
-
NF3
). We exclude infeasible image goals, which is a failure specific to image goal-conditioned architectures. Half the trajectories are in novel office and garden environments; the rest are in novel areas that resemble training data. Each trajectory is 10s long, with failures occurring at the 8s mark. A failure is detected if any frame from 8s onward is classified as OOD. To annotate ground-truth recognition, we divide each frame vertically into three bins and mark the bins containing features related to navigation failure (
e.g.
dynamic objects, blockages), similar to the approach in
subsection IV-B
.

We evaluate OOD detection using ROC curves over the raw OOD scores. Since recognition is only meaningful for detected OOD cases, we also assess accuracy of jointly detecting and localising OOD features. We report ROC curves per recognition heatmap bin, with the scores computed as
\mathbb{I}[b_{t}=1]\sum_{m\in M_{t,k}}m
, where
b_{t}=1
is an OOD detection, and
M_{t,k}
is the
k
-th bin of the heatmap
M_{t}
. Intuitively, this returns the per-bin recognition score only if a positive OOD detection occurs for the input. The results support
H1
, that
Fare
produces OOD-aware policies effective at task-relevant OOD detection/recognition. We draw two conclusions:

OOD-aware policies outperform existing methods.

Fare
’s approach of integrating OOD detectors into IL policies outperforms state-of-the-art OOD detection and recognition approaches, supporting
H1
. Both
Fare
methods achieve high AUC scores across both detection and recognition compared with density-based approaches (NF), one-class discriminators (RND) and reconstruction-based approaches (AE, VAE-R).
Fare
-DEC achieves the best performance by a clear margin, with high TPR and relatively few false positives. It also outperforms
Fare
-INet, likely since its temporally accumulated features offer richer information to distinguish OOD scenarios. Unlike other methods that use separate policies and detectors,
Fare
embeds detection and recognition in the policy, reducing computational overhead.

We hypothesised that learning from both observations and actions encourages more task-relevant representations. We find that adding actions enables more task-relevant and better-performing OOD recognition, but has little effect on OOD detection. As shown in
Figure 1
, methods using only observations perform comparably on detection but fall short on recognition compared to those using actions.
Figure 4
further illustrates this:
Fare
-DEC highlights features relevant to the path-following task (a closed door), whereas VAE-KL highlights irrelevant textures on the floor. This suggests that observations alone suffice for the coarse task of OOD detection, but actions are crucial for fine-grained recognition.

KL scores, Grad-CAM are the most effective metrics.

Results for
Recon
and
Fare
methods indicate that approaches using KL-based OOD scores for detection and Grad-CAM for recognition perform best at their respective tasks. AE and VAE-R show similar poor performance on detection and recognition when using MSE and pixel-wise reconstruction error respectively. However, VAE-KL achieves significantly stronger performance on both tasks despite having the same model weights as VAE-R, differing only in the use of KL scores and Grad-CAM as OOD measures. We attribute this to the fact that KL scores and Grad-CAM operate on
latent vectors
instead of high-dimensional reconstructed images. As latent vectors are compressed representations that learn to focus on task-relevant factors, they are less noisy information sources than reconstructed images. Qualitative analysis (
Figure 4
) bears this out: recognition results from VAE-R are significantly noisier than VAE-KL or
Fare
methods. While recent works shift the focus to methods like density-based
[
23
]
or second-order
[
35
,
22
]
approaches, we highlight that selecting the right OOD measures enables reconstruction methods to outperform these. This also validates
Fare
’s choice of scores for OOD detection and recognition.

VI
Conclusion

We define
failure-resilient policies
for learned visual navigation as those that detect and automatically recover from failures. We argue that recognising factors that trigger failures is vital to recovery, providing the necessary feedback to inform actions. We propose the
Fare
framework for failure-resilient policies: it unifies and embeds OOD detection and recognition in an IL policy’s latent space, combining it with an informed recovery policy guided by the recognition results.
Fare
works across 2 policy architectures for a range of diverse failures in different scenes, and enables learned policies to succeed at long-range navigation in complex environments. Failure resilience is a building block of safe, robust navigation, and we will extend
Fare
to handle policies with more advanced visual encoders
[
36
]
, or to use more sophisticated recovery strategies.
