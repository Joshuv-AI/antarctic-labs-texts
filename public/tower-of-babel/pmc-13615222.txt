Genetic Risk in Multiple Sclerosis: Susceptibility, Modifiers and Unresolved Questions
Authors: Jingni He, Nicholas B Blackburn, Vilija G Jokubaitis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMultiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic, immune-mediated disorder of the central nervous system (CNS) characterised by inflammation, demyelination and neurodegeneration. The aetiology of MS is complex, arising from interactions among genetic susceptibility, environmental exposures and stochastic immune processes. Over the past 2 decades, large-scale genomic studies have fundamentally shaped our understanding of MS pathogenesis, establishing disease risk as highly polygenic and predominantly driven by immune regulatory mechanisms. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified 233 common susceptibility variants, including 201 outside the major histocompatibility complex (MHC), with the strongest effects localised to the MHC, particularly HLA-DRB1*15:01. These genetic associations implicate pathways involved in antigen presentation, T- and B-cell activation, cytokine signalling and innate immune responses. Family-based studies have identified putative rare susceptibility variants, but no single gene has been confirmed to cause MS. However, genetic risk alone is insufficient to cause disease, and gene-environment interactions, most notably with Epstein-Barr virus infection, vitamin D insufficiency, obesity, smoking and sex-specific hormonal factors, are critical determinants of disease manifestation. Here, we synthesise current evidence on the genetic architecture of MS, the biological mechanisms linking genetic risk to disease susceptibility and the ways in which genetic factors intersect with environmental exposures to shape clinical outcomes. We further review emerging data on the influence of genetic variation on disease course, prognosis and treatment response. Finally, we discuss unmet needs and future directions, including the role that family studies can play in further informing our understanding of MS pathology, the need for ancestry-diverse studies, multi-omics integration and the translation of genetic insights into clinical care.Keywords: Multiple sclerosis, Genetics, Genome-wide association studies, Rare variants, Disease severity, Gene-environment interactions, Epigenetics, Pharmacogenomics

Key Summary Points

Multiple sclerosis (MS) has a substantial genetic component but is not inherited as a Mendelian disorder; susceptibility is highly polygenic and shaped by interactions between genetic and environmental factorsCommon genetic variation, particularly within the major histocompatibility complex, has transformed understanding of MS susceptibility, while the contribution of rare variants remains incompletely defined and no confirmed monogenic form of MS has been establishedThe genetic determinants of MS severity appear to differ from those of susceptibility, with emerging evidence implicating central nervous system-intrinsic pathways in disease severityGenetic susceptibility interacts with environmental exposures, including Epstein-Barr virus infection, vitamin D insufficiency, obesity and smoking, with epigenetic mechanisms providing a potential link between environmental exposures and disease biologyDespite substantial advances in MS genetics, clinical utility for risk prediction, prognosis and treatment selection remains limited; ancestry-diverse studies, multi-omics integration and independent validation are priorities for future research

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic inflammatory disease of the central nervous system (CNS) and a leading cause of non-traumatic neurological disability in adults. The disease is characterised pathologically by focal demyelinated lesions, diffuse neuroaxonal injury and gliosis and clinically by heterogeneous manifestations that include relapses, progressive disability accumulation and cognitive impairment [1]. Despite advances in disease-modifying therapy, MS remains incurable and continues to impose substantial personal, societal and economic burdens [2, 3].

MS affects more than 2.8 million people worldwide, with marked geographic variation in both prevalence and incidence [4]. The disease shows a pronounced female predominance, with female-to-male ratios approaching 3:1 across many populations [5, 6]. MS usually manifests between 20 and 40 years of age, although paediatric- and late-onset MS (after age 50) presentations occur, albeit less frequently [7, 8]. As MS typically emerges during the most productive life years, it carries enduring impacts on employment, quality of life and healthcare utilisation for those affected [9–11]. Despite significant therapeutic advances that have reduced relapse rates and inflammatory disease activity, the prevention of long-term disability accrual remains a major unmet challenge [12, 13].

The aetiology of MS has long been recognised as multifactorial. Early observations of familial clustering, twin concordance and geographic gradients suggested roles for both inherited susceptibility and environmental exposures [14]. Contemporary models conceptualise MS as arising from a complex interplay among genetic risk, environmental modifiers and immune dysregulation, ultimately leading to loss of tolerance to CNS antigens, with the myelin basic protein (MBP) recently proposed as a key CNS target antigen in MS [15]. Among these contributors, genetic factors provide a foundational framework for understanding why certain individuals are susceptible to MS, while others remain unaffected despite similar environmental exposures.

The focus of this review is to synthesise current knowledge on the genetic risk factors that contribute to the manifestation of MS. We examine the evidence supporting a genetic contribution to disease susceptibility, describe the polygenic architecture of MS risk and review the genetic determinants of disease severity. Recognising that the effects of genetic variation extend beyond inherited susceptibility alone, we also explore how genetic variation interacts with environmental exposures via epigenetic mechanisms and explore MS pharmacogenomics. These complementary areas provide a broader context for understanding how genetic variation is modified, is functionally expressed and ultimately influences disease onset, clinical heterogeneity and cellular phenotypes. We also discuss the implications of MS genetics for prognosis, treatment response and future research, with particular emphasis on unmet needs and opportunities for translation into clinical practice.

This narrative review was informed by searches of PubMed, Web of Science and Google Scholar using combinations of keywords including “multiple sclerosis”, “genetics”, “GWAS”, “Human Leukocyte Antigen (HLA)”, “rare variants”, “family sequencing”, “monogenic”, “gene–environment interaction”, “epigenetics”, “pharmacogenomics”, “treatment response” and the names of individual disease-modifying therapies. Literature published up until 1 May 2026 was reviewed for relevance and inclusion. For the pharmacogenomics section, literature searches focused on studies published from 2018 onwards to update the evidence beyond the previous systematic review by Hocevar et al. [16]. Priority was given to large-scale GWAS and meta-analyses, replicated genetic studies and studies with independent validation or mechanistic evidence. Rare variant studies using family-based or sequencing approaches and pharmacogenomic studies evaluating treatment response or serious adverse events were included. Studies not directly relevant to the genetic basis and clinical implications of MS were excluded. Preprints were considered only for rapidly evolving areas not yet well represented in the peer-reviewed literature and were identified as non-peer-reviewed.

Epidemiological Evidence for a Genetic Contribution to MS

The role of genetics in MS susceptibility is supported by converging epidemiological evidence from familial aggregation, twin studies and population-based heritability estimates. MS is not inherited in a Mendelian fashion; however, worldwide, a family history of MS is reported in 12.6% of people diagnosed with MS [17]. First-degree relatives of individuals with MS have a substantially increased risk compared with the general population. Siblings of affected individuals have an estimated lifetime risk of approximately 2–4%, representing a 20–40-fold increase over the baseline population risk [14]. Large Scandinavian multi-generational registry studies have further estimated the narrow-sense heritability of MS at 51% [18], indicating that inherited genetic factors make a substantial contribution to disease liability at the population level.

Twin studies have specifically provided further insight into the heritability of MS [19]. Concordance rates are consistently higher in monozygotic twins (approximately 20–30%) than in dizygotic twins (2–5%), with the latter resembling the risk observed in a non-twin full sibling [14, 20]. The incomplete concordance observed in monozygotic twins underscores that genetic susceptibility alone is insufficient to cause disease. Prior work has carefully examined the immune system in monozygotic twins where one twin has MS and the other does not [21, 22]. These studies provide evidence of a shifting immune profile in the MS twin, which may be in response to environmental triggers, leading to the development of MS.

The Genetic Architecture of MS Susceptibility

Building on epidemiological evidence for a substantial inherited component, advances in statistical genetics have enabled estimation of heritability attributable to common genetic variation captured by single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). SNP-based heritability estimates quantify the proportion of phenotypic variance explained by the additive effects of common GWAS variants. The largest GWAS of MS to date estimated SNP-based heritability at 19.2% [23]. Of this SNP-based heritability, approximately 39% was explained by genome-wide significant loci, increasing to ~ 48% when SNPs with suggestive associations were included [23], indicating that approximately half of the SNP-based heritability remains unexplained by identified common variants. The gap between the 51% pedigree-based estimate [18] and the 19.2% SNP-based estimate may reflect genetic variation not well captured by GWAS, including rare variants [24, 25], as well as gene-gene interactions. Differences between these estimates may also arise from methodological and modelling assumptions, as pedigree-based estimates can be influenced by non-additive genetic effects and confounding by shared environmental factors, while gene-environment interactions may further influence the genetic risk of MS [26].

The Major Histocompatibility Complex

Across the human genome, the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) on chromosome 6p21 has long been recognised as the dominant genetic determinant of MS susceptibility [27]. In particular, the HLA-DRB1*15:01 allele has been shown to confer the strongest single genetic effect, increasing the risk of MS by approximately threefold [28], with a comparable effect also observed in paediatric-onset MS (OR = 2.95) [29]. Since the earliest reports implicating HLA variation in MS risk [30], successive linkage screens [31, 32] and candidate gene and genome-wide association studies [23, 28, 33–39] have consistently replicated and reinforced the central role of the MHC in MS genetics.

The introduction of HLA imputation from genotyped SNP data further refined the genetic architecture of the MHC [40, 41], confirming HLA-DRB1*15:01 as the strongest genetic risk allele for MS. These studies also demonstrated important contributions from HLA class I loci, including HLA-A*02:01 and several HLA-B alleles. Notably, HLA-A*02:01 has been consistently associated with a protective effect against MS (odds ratio ≈ 0.67) [42], whereas other class I alleles appear to modify disease risk in an allele- and haplotype-dependent manner. In addition, several HLA class II alleles, such as HLA-DRB1*03:01, HLA-DRB1*13:03 and HLA-DQB1*03:02 [42, 43], as well as specific class II allele-allele interactions, including HLA-DQA1*01:01 with HLA-DRB1*15:01 and HLA-DQB1*03:01 with HLA-DQB1*03:02 [42], have been implicated in MS susceptibility. Notably, these findings derive largely from European populations, despite substantial ancestry-dependent heterogeneity in MHC effects. Studies in non-European populations have identified population-specific HLA risk alleles (e.g. HLA-B*53:01) as well as ancestry-modified effects of classical risk alleles, such as HLA-DQB1*06:02, for which MS risk is nearly twofold higher in individuals of European compared with African ancestral background [44].

Recent advances, including multi-ancestry HLA reference panels [45], improved imputation algorithms [46, 47] and a standardised MHC-specific fine-mapping pipeline [48], have markedly improved imputation accuracy and residue-level resolution across both class I and class II loci. Nevertheless, imputation-based approaches remain limited in resolving rare alleles, structural variation and phase-specific amino acid configurations, suggesting that additional biologically relevant MHC signals remain uncharacterised.

Non-HLA Common Variants

Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified 201 commonly occurring genetic variants that are associated with MS, located outside the HLA region [23], of which 28 adult-onset MS risk variants have also shown associations with paediatric-onset MS susceptibility [29].

Most MS-associated variants identified by GWAS lie in non-coding regions and are enriched in regulatory elements [23]. Epigenomic and transcriptomic annotations indicate that these signals preferentially localise to regulatory landscapes and cell-specific susceptibility genes in T cells, B cells and monocytes, supporting an autoimmune disease model [49–51]. Enrichment within microglial regulatory regions further implicates CNS-resident innate immune cells in MS susceptibility and the targeting of immune pathology to the central nervous system [23, 52].

Functionally, non-HLA MS risk loci can be broadly classified according to their contributions to disease pathogenesis. Many variants are implicated in T-cell activation, cytokine signalling and antigen presentation, affecting genes within key immune pathways, including IL2RA [28], IL7R [53], IL4, IL6 [54], IL12 [55], IRF5, CD24, CD58 and EVI5 [56]. Other associations implicate innate immune and metabolic processes, including mitochondrial function [23, 57].

Rare Variants and Monogenic Mimics

In MS, low-frequency coding variation has been estimated to explain an additional ~ 4–5% of heritable risk on the liability scale. A large exome-chip study identified seven low-frequency coding variants across six genes outside the extended MHC region. Two signals, in GALC and TYK2, were consistent with previous GWAS loci [37], whereas variants implicating PRF1, PRKRA, NLRP8 and HDAC7 were not in linkage disequilibrium with known common-variant signals, suggesting that low-frequency coding variation can contribute additional MS risk signals not captured by conventional GWAS [24]. Similar evidence is emerging in paediatric-onset MS, where rare and low-frequency coding variants in PRF1, together with rare variation in the MHC-region genes BRD2 and AGER, have been associated with paediatric-onset MS risk [58]. As paediatric-onset MS is a rare diagnosis, studies of rare variants remain small in sample size and necessarily focussed on candidate gene approaches, rather than genome wide.

These findings support a role for rare and low-frequency variants in MS susceptibility, but they do not imply that MS is a monogenic disorder. Other complex diseases, including Alzheimer’s disease [59] and Parkinson’s disease [60], each have monogenic familial forms, where individual rare variants are a primary driver of disease development in specific individuals and families. However, no confirmed monogenic forms of MS have been established to date. Nevertheless, multi-case MS families remain an appropriate study design for rare variant identification when coupled with genome sequencing technologies. The overarching principle is that families are an implicit enrichment strategy for rare variants through Mendelian inheritance. That is, multiple copies of an individual rare variant that may contribute to disease can be observed in multi-case families by identifying segregation of variant/s with disease [61].

Rare, multi-case families with three or more first- or second- degree relatives have been reported by several groups internationally [62–64]. The first study to apply this approach was published in 2016. Wang et al. [65] identified 37 shared variants through exome sequencing of two affected cousins from a multi-case MS family, followed by targeted sequencing in additional relatives, MS families and population cohorts. This led to the identification of a single nucleotide variant (SNV) in NR1H3 (p.Arg415Gln). The authors positioned this genetic finding as the ‘cause’ of MS in these two families, which received substantial criticism from the genetics community. This criticism stemmed in part from the presence of the variant in unaffected carriers and its relative frequency in population-based control datasets [66].

Family-based sequencing studies in MS have had several limitations. Multiple studies have focussed on the identification of a ‘single gene’ in each family [65, 67]. Some have agnostically sequenced only selected affected family members [65, 67–69], while others have sequenced families with fewer than three close relatives with MS [70, 71]. Studies have also applied an a priori biological candidate gene approach [67, 72–74], which, while valid, have again made the assumption that a ‘single gene’ will be sufficient to cause MS in a proportion of families. Finally, studies have potentially biased their analyses by including young (< 50 years old) unaffected family members, who may yet develop MS, as filtering controls [63]. Importantly, no study to date has taken their rare variant findings through to validation in a laboratory-based model.

Although no confirmed monogenic form of MS has been established, the clinical spectrum of MS does however share features with several Mendelian, single-gene diseases that can be described as ‘MS-like’, phenocopies or MS mimics [75]. Mendelian diseases sometimes misdiagnosed as MS include Fabry disease [76], cerebral autosomal dominant arteriopathy and subcortical infarct leukoencephalopathy (CADASIL) [77], leukodystrophy [78] and hereditary spastic paraplegia (HSP) [79]. Recently, we and others have employed exome sequencing in conjunction with extensive variant curation in genes known to cause Mendelian disorders [80–82]. Collectively, these findings show that misdiagnosis of MS due to an alternative monogenic condition is exceedingly rare. Nonetheless, a small subset of individuals, particularly those who have ‘red flag’ diagnostic inconsistencies, may benefit from clinical genomic evaluation at diagnosis to exclude alternative genetic explanations for their MS-like phenotype.

Genetics, Disease Course and Prognosis

While the genetic studies described above have substantially informed our understanding of MS susceptibility, the genetic determinants of the disease trajectory following diagnosis remain far less well defined.

Beyond susceptibility, HLA-DRB1*15:01 is robustly associated with age at disease onset, with heterozygous carriage linked to an approximately 1-year earlier onset and homozygosity to an onset of up to 3 years earlier [83, 84]. In contrast, despite extensive investigation, there is little consistent evidence that HLA variation substantially influences disease severity, progression, cognition or brain atrophy, suggesting that the MHC primarily governs disease initiation rather than downstream clinical course [83–88]. Similarly, analyses of non-HLA risk loci indicate that variants conferring susceptibility do not meaningfully associate with MS severity [88, 89].

In contrast to MS susceptibility, which is largely driven by immune-related genes, emerging evidence indicates that genetic modifiers of MS severity are enriched in genes related to central nervous system function [87]. Using prospectively ascertained, longitudinal clinical outcomes data obtained from the MSBase Registry, we have shown that MS severity is mediated by multiple genetic loci of small effect, mapping to genes associated with mitochondrial function, synaptic plasticity, oligodendroglial biology, cellular senescence, calcium and G-protein receptor signalling pathways [85]. Although individual variants did not surpass genome-wide significance thresholds, aggregate signals derived using machine learning approaches showed predictive utility, achieving an area under the receiver-operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) of 0.84 (95% CI 0.79–0.88) in a held-out test set, substantially outperforming a model based on clinical and demographic variables alone (AUC = 0.54), although external validation is needed to establish its generalisability [85].

Shortly thereafter, large-scale international efforts by the International MS Genetics Consortium (IMSGC) and the MultipleMS consortium identified and replicated the first variant to reach genome-wide significance for MS severity: rs10191329, intergenic at the DYSF-ZNF638 locus. While the functional mechanism remains to be defined, this locus lies proximal to genes expressed by both neuronal and glial cell lineages and may have roles in synaptic function [87]. Together, these studies converge on a model in which MS severity is shaped by CNS-intrinsic processes, including CNS resilience, synaptic integrity and function and oligodendroglial biology. Regarding severity, SNP-based heritability estimates range between 12.8–19.0% [85, 87].

Further discovery of genetic determinants of severity remains challenging. Large, deeply phenotyped cohorts with integrated genomic data are scarce, and there is a continued need for consensus around clinically meaningful definitions of disease severity. The inherent heterogeneity of MS, coupled with its diverse endophenotypes, further complicates discovery efforts, underscoring the importance of sustained international collaboration to further spearhead discovery.

Gene-Environment Interactions in MS Manifestation and Progression

Ancient DNA analyses indicate that major MS risk alleles within the HLA region increased in frequency among Bronze Age steppe pastoralist populations and spread into Europe through Yamnaya-related migrations. Research suggests this likely reflects positive selection on immunogenetic variants driven by pathogen pressures associated with changing lifestyle and environmental factors [90]. These findings highlight the deep evolutionary origins of MS susceptibility and underscore how environmental pressures have historically shaped immune-related genetic variation. In modern populations, genetic susceptibility interacts with environmental exposures to determine MS risk through several well-described gene-environment interactions.

Among these, infection with Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) represents the strongest known environmental risk factor; a large longitudinal study of a predominantly male US military cohort demonstrated that MS risk increases by approximately 32-fold following EBV seroconversion [91], although the generalisability of this estimate to broader populations remains uncertain. The putative mechanistic links between EBV infection and MS manifestation are reviewed in detail elsewhere [92]. The compelling association between EBV infection and MS risk has seen the emergence of anti-EBV trials as adjunct therapy for MS [93], whilst preventive trials are also being discussed and industry works toward developing EBV-specific vaccines. Notably, however, EBV infection is nearly ubiquitous, infecting ~ 95% of adults worldwide, indicating that, whilst EBV infection may be necessary, it is not sufficient for the development of MS and that its effect on disease manifestation likely depends on the broader genetic and environmental context. Among these, vitamin D insufficiency, reduced ultraviolet radiation exposure, adolescent obesity and smoking have emerged as the leading candidates.

Observational studies consistently show that low circulating 25-hydroxyvitamin D levels are associated with increased MS risk [94]. Mendelian randomization analyses further support a causal relationship between genetically determined vitamin D levels and disease susceptibility [95–97]. At the population level, the well-described latitudinal gradient in MS prevalence [98] implicates reduced ultraviolet radiation, and subsequently lower vitamin D, as a key environmental contributor to MS risk. Together, these epidemiological and genetic findings have driven clinical trials of vitamin D supplementation [99, 100] to evaluate its role in MS risk modification. However, vitamin D supplementation trials have produced mixed findings, with no reduction in conversion to MS following clinically isolated syndrome in the PrevANZ trial [99] but reduced MRI disease activity in the more recent D-Lay MS [100].

Mechanistic studies provide biological evidence that genetic susceptibility modifies immune responses to vitamin D. Vitamin D directly regulates expression of HLA-DRB1*15:01 via a vitamin D response element [101], linking the major genetic risk locus to environmental exposure. Additional susceptibility loci including VDR [102] and CYP27B1 [103] further implicate vitamin D metabolism in immune regulation, enabling modulation of inflammatory signalling pathways, reviewed in detail elsewhere [104]. Transcriptomic analyses of purified immune cell subsets show that endogenous vitamin D levels are associated with differential expression of hundreds of immune-related genes, including pathways related to TNF-α and NF-κB signalling. Notably, vitamin D–responsive genes are enriched near known susceptibility loci in CD4⁺ T cells [105, 106]. There is also evidence that immune cells from people with MS exhibit a blunted transcriptional response to vitamin D, suggesting that genetic susceptibility may modify the biological effects of this environmental exposure [106]. Complementary transcriptomic evidence from the PrevANZ trial showed that high-dose vitamin D3 supplementation modulates peripheral immune gene expression, with downregulation of inflammatory pathways, including TNF-α and IL-17 signalling, and upregulation of antigen presentation and antiviral response pathways.

Importantly, the relationship among ultraviolet exposure, vitamin D and MS risk appears to vary by ancestry. The US Sunshine Study showed that higher sun exposure was associated with lower risk of MS, independent of circulating vitamin D, in white and black populations, with a similar trend in Hispanics. It was only in white populations that higher serum vitamin D levels were associated with MS risk [107]. These findings suggest that ultraviolet radiation may exert additional immunomodulatory effects beyond vitamin D-dependent pathways [108], although a genetic link here is yet to be established.

Additional modifiable exposures include obesity and cigarette smoking. Epidemiological and genetic studies indicate that increased body mass index (BMI), particularly during childhood and adolescence, is associated with elevated MS susceptibility [96], and Mendelian randomization analyses further suggest that higher genetically determined BMI may also contribute to greater long-term disability [109]. Smoking similarly increases MS risk and represents one of the clearest examples of gene-environment interaction in MS, acting synergistically with HLA risk alleles, particularly with HLA-DRB1*15:01 and in the absence of HLA-A*02:01, to amplify susceptibility [110]. Although Mendelian randomization studies have failed to identify a causal link between cigarette smoking and MS risk [111], smoking and obesity appear to synergistically interact to worsen MS outcomes [112]. Together, these findings highlight the cumulative and interacting effects of environmental exposures on a genetically susceptible background.

The biological mechanisms underpinning these gene-environment interactions remain incompletely understood. Epigenetic mechanisms, of which DNA methylation is the best characterised in MS research, provide a molecular interface through which environmental exposures may influence gene expression and disease biology. Epigenome-wide association studies have demonstrated widespread differences in DNA methylation patterns between people with MS and controls, particularly within B cell and monocyte populations [113]. This suggests that epigenetic regulation contributes to disease pathogenesis beyond known genetic risk variants. Emerging evidence also indicates that paediatric-onset MS is associated with accelerated epigenetic aging relative to age-matched controls [114].

Integrative analyses further show that key environmental MS risk factors, including EBV infection, low vitamin D status, reduced sun exposure and HLA-DRB1*15:01 genotype, converge on shared epigenetic pathways, providing a unifying framework for gene-environment interactions in MS susceptibility [115]. Epigenetic mechanisms appear to extend to disease progression: accelerated epigenetic aging has been observed in immune cells of people with MS [116], and epigenome-wide association studies have identified methylation signatures associated with more aggressive clinical trajectories [117]. Together, these findings position epigenetic regulation as a key mediator linking environmental exposures to both MS susceptibility and long-term disease outcomes.

Genetics and Treatment Response

The potential role of genetics in guiding treatment selection in MS remains an area of active investigation. Although inter-individual variability in treatment response is substantial, no pharmacogenetic associations have demonstrated sufficient effect size, replication across diverse populations or sufficient utility to support their use in clinical decision-making.

A systematic review by Hocevar et al. synthesised pharmacogenomic studies in MS published up to 2018 [16]. At that time, 94% of studies focused on treatment with interferon-β and glatiramer acetate, with fewer studies evaluating natalizumab and mitoxantrone. Most studies did not take a genome-wide approach, focussing instead on candidate gene study designs [16].

Since 2018, additional studies have expanded the evidence base across MS treatments, including both genome-wide and candidate gene analyses of natalizumab, interferon-β, glatiramer acetate, dimethyl fumarate and fingolimod (Table 1). Collectively, these studies implicate genetic variation in immune regulatory pathways, Fc-receptor signalling, oxidative stress responses and HLA loci as potential modifiers of treatment response.

The earliest study linking genetic variation to treatment response in MS reported an association between HLA variation and glatiramer acetate [118]; however, it was not until recently that an HLA-glatiramer acetate association received support across multiple independent cohorts. The HLA-A*03:01–glatiramer acetate association represents the strongest validated pharmacogenomic signal in MS to date [119]. In a retrospective multi-cohort analysis, glatiramer acetate-associated T-cell receptor beta-chain (TRB) sequence patterns were identified, including an HLA-A*03:01-associated pattern that was validated across independent cohorts. Clinical analyses across the NationMS, ACP, EPIC, BIONAT and CombiRx cohorts further supported HLA-A*03:01 as a predictive treatment-response biomarker, with evidence that HLA-A*03:01 carriers derived greater benefit from glatiramer acetate than from interferon-β across multiple clinical outcomes, including a lower relapse risk.

Overall, the evidence for genomic drivers of treatment response in MS remains limited. Studies vary substantially in sample size and design, and treatment response has been defined using diverse outcome definitions, including relapse, disease progression and composite clinical and radiological measures such as NEDA [123], limiting direct comparison of effect estimates. Most reported associations also lack independent external replication, with the HLA-A*03:01–glatiramer acetate finding [119] representing a notable exception. Thus, despite several promising signals, most associations remain hypothesis-generating rather than clinically actionable. Substantial work remains before these findings can be translated into robust expectations, reliable predictions and actionable clinical recommendations [130]. Pharmacogenomic relevance in MS may extend beyond efficacy prediction to identifying patients at increased risk of serious treatment-associated adverse events. Progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML), a rare but often devastating JC virus-associated opportunistic infection, remains a major safety consideration for several immunosuppressive MS therapies, particularly natalizumab [131]. Established natalizumab-associated PML risk stratification incorporates anti-JCV antibody status, prior immunosuppressant exposure and treatment duration, but these factors do not fully explain individual susceptibility [132]. To investigate genetic contributors, a prior study [131] identified four rare immune-related variants in C8B, LY9, FCN2 and STXBP2 that were enriched in drug-exposed PML cases compared with JCV-positive, drug-exposed MS controls, with carriage of any one variant observed in 10.9% of cases versus 1.4% of controls. These genes have plausible roles in complement activation, T-cell regulation and NK-cell cytotoxicity and were not associated with MS susceptibility. This suggests they have relevance to treatment-associated PML risk rather than MS risk itself [131–133]. While further validation is required, these findings highlight the potential role of genetics in pharmacovigilance and risk mitigation for high-efficacy MS therapies.

Unmet Needs and Future Directions

Significant international effort has transformed our understanding of MS genetics, with large-scale GWAS of susceptibility and, more recently, disease severity providing detailed insights into perturbations in immune signalling pathways in MS risk and emerging contributions from CNS-related genes in MS severity. However, key gaps remain. The gap between pedigree-based and SNP-based heritability estimates remains incompletely understood, and the extent of the contribution of rare variants to MS susceptibility is not fully characterised, while putative monogenic forms of MS remain unvalidated. Efforts to close this gap are underway and will particularly benefit from research that expands into non-European MS populations [134].

A deeper understanding of MS biology will require linking genetic risk factors to longitudinal clinical and patient-reported outcomes, necessitating large, deeply phenotyped cohorts of people with MS followed over decades. Such studies will also be important for understanding the distinction between the genetic determinants of disease susceptibility and severity. In parallel, many established risk loci remain functionally unresolved. Progress will rely on fine-mapping, functional validation of causal variants and integration with transcriptomic, epigenomic and proteomic information [135–137]. Single-cell approaches, induced pluripotent stem cell models [138] and studies of CNS-resident cell types and compartmentalised immune cells offer new opportunities to link genetic risk to disease-relevant biology and disease trajectories [139].

These advancements are expected to accelerate the identification of novel therapeutic targets and inform next-generation disease-modifying therapies. However, translation into clinical practice will require careful navigation of ethical, social and practical implementation challenges, particularly in the context of genetic risk prediction and early intervention.

Conclusions

Genetic studies have fundamentally shaped our understanding of multiple sclerosis, establishing it as a highly polygenic, predominantly immune-mediated disease in which genetic susceptibility interacts with environmental exposures to drive disease onset. While genetic discoveries have provided important biological insights, their clinical utility for risk prediction, prognosis and treatment selection remains limited.

Key questions remain regarding the sources of unexplained heritability and the contribution of rare variants, the apparent distinction between the genetic determinants of disease susceptibility and severity, and the mechanisms through which genetic susceptibility interacts with environmental exposures to shape disease onset and progression. Addressing these questions will require ancestry-diverse studies and the integration of genetic, environmental and multi-omics data across the disease continuum from the prodromal phase to established disease. Such efforts will be critical to elucidating disease mechanisms and translating discovery into meaningful advances in prevention, trajectory stratification and care for people living with MS.
