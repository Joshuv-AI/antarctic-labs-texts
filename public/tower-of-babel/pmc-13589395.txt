Longitudinal Prediction of Retinal Sensitivity Based on Disease Progression Quantified From Optical Coherence Tomography in Geographic Atrophy
Authors: Klaudia Birner, Julia Mai, Daniela Boryshchuk, Florian Frommlet, Roy Schwartz, Simon Schürer-Waldheim, Oliver Leingang, Wolf Dieter-Vogl, Ursula Schmidt-Erfurth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe purpose of this study was to analyze the association between disease progression of geographic atrophy (GA) from optical coherence tomography (OCT) with retinal sensitivity (RS) in microperimetry (MP) over a 2-year follow-up period.MethodsThis is a longitudinal analysis of the OAKS Phase-III clinical trial. Both study and fellow eyes with GA that underwent imaging with the Spectralis OCT and consecutive MP examination were eligible. Pointwise quantification of ellipsoid zone (EZ) thickness, EZ and retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) loss from OCT volumes was correlated with localized RS. A longitudinal predictive model using a Markov Chain framework was implemented to predict RS change over time based on OCT biomarkers. The modeling of morphological and functional progression was based on the fellow-eye cohort.ResultsA total of 39,681 MP points from 406 patients were analyzed. In the fellow eye cohort, baseline (BSL) EZ thickness was positively associated with RS (0.3 decibel [dB]/µm, P < 0.001). Decrease in EZ thickness between visits during follow-up was significantly associated with decrease in RS (0.1 dB / 1 µm change). RS was significantly lower in MP points within EZ loss during follow-up compared with MP points within the retina with measurable EZ (P < 0.001). The largest functional decline was observed within RPE loss, also associated with the highest probability of absolute scotoma (P < 0.001). Morphological progression to EZ and RPE loss was influenced by EZ thickness and the morphology of adjacent MP points (P < 0.001).ConclusionsTwo exploratory endpoints were developed, namely quantification of EZ thickness and loss, and localized RS within high-risk OCT areas. RS decline during follow-up is associated with automatically quantified disease progression in OCT.

Approval of complement inhibition treatment for geographic atrophy (GA) secondary to age-related macular degeneration (AMD) in the United States opened a novel therapeutic universe with pivotal research toward clinically relevant endpoints in non-exudative AMD.1–3 Anatomic endpoints served as the base for regulatory approval, with lesion size based on fundus autofluorescence (FAF) defined as the primary outcome measure. Recently, more distinct retinal biomarkers derived from optical coherence tomography (OCT) such as the loss of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) and the ellipsoid zone (EZ), a correlate for photoreceptor integrity, were used to provide evidence for pathophysiological and therapeutic mechanisms.4 Current observations from clinical trials in GA highlight the challenges of defining a functional benefit in patients with non-exudative AMD in natural disease progression as well as under therapy.2 Meanwhile, growing evidence is emerging for implementation of microperimetry (MP) as a well-suited functional endpoint providing superior evidence than conventional best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) testing.5 MP allows to topographically assess changes in retinal sensitivity (RS) throughout the entire macular region and is not restricted to central visual function alone.6 Therefore, the mostly parafoveal lesion onset in GA with subclinical functional decline as well as the progressive loss in retinal function proceeding further and leading to foveal loss might be better targeted with MP compared to BCVA.2,6 However, the best-suited outcome parameter in MP during follow-up has not yet been defined, as it was proven in previous literature that mean RS is not well-correlated to GA extension.7 Meleth et al. showed that longitudinal functional loss in GA occurs in perilesional regions, whereas increase in scotomatous points over time showed significant associations with GA growth.7 Consistent with this concept, Chang et al. proposed perilesional RS as a potentially more informative functional endpoint, based on the observation that sensitivity decline is most pronounced in retinal locations adjacent to functional loss in a post hoc analysis of a clinical trial.5 More recently, Wu and al. proposed defect-mapping MP to capture sensitivity loss in relation to lesion extent and progression.8

Advances in mathematical modeling and deep-learning (DL) enable a ground-breaking shift toward OCT-based assessment of disease activity and progression in GA.9 Automated DL-based algorithms allow for fast and objective identification of subclinical biomarkers in GA from OCT volumes by quantification of morphological biomarkers relevant for retinal function, namely thinning and loss of the photoreceptor layer at the level of the EZ and loss of RPE.10,11 First cross-sectional studies revealed a correlation between these automatically quantified anatomic parameters and functional changes in RS seen by MP.12,13 Association between subclinical quantifiable OCT biomarkers, such as EZ thinning and loss, was demonstrated in post hoc analysis from the prospective OAKS trials.14 Concurrently, there has been efforts toward development of lesion-targeted, individualized MP approaches to reduce examination time and target areas with the most pronounced RS changes.15 However, longitudinal studies are indispensable to establish clinical relevance and define adequate procedures for functional monitoring in GA in clinical practice and individual patient outcomes.

Our aim was to analyze the impact of automatically quantified progression of anatomic biomarker alteration derived from OCT volumes, namely reduced EZ thickness, EZ loss, and RPE loss, to the topographically related RS decline over time from the OAKS phase III clinical trial, the largest prospective dataset available to date. Using fully automated local biomarker quantification, we assessed whether RS follows distinct trajectories within these morphological areas. We further wanted to define whether local RS can be predicted longitudinally based on EZ thickness, as well as morphological disease progression to EZ and RPE loss. Finally, we aimed to examine whether subclinical EZ thinning and surrounding morphology is associated with subsequent local morphological progression.

Methods

Patient Cohort and Inclusion Criteria

A post hoc analysis was conducted on patients from the OAKS phase III clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT03525613) who underwent standardized MP (Macular Integrity Assessment [MAIA]) and Spectralis OCT (49 B-Scans of each 512 A-scans, covering the central 20 × 20 degrees; Spectralis HRA+OCT; Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany) examinations from baseline (BSL) to month 24 in 6-month intervals. The OAKS phase III clinical trial is a prospective, multicenter, randomized, sham-controlled study for the approval of pegcetacoplan, a complement C3 and C3b inhibitor, for the treatment of GA secondary to AMD. The study inclusion and exclusion criteria were published previously.3 In short, patients above 60 years of age, BCVA ≥ 24 Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) letters (20/320 Snellen equivalent) and a total GA area between ≥ 2.5 and ≤ 17.5 mm2 on FAF imaging were eligible for study inclusion. MP was conducted in the mesopic standard setting with a 68-stimulus grid from BSL to month 24 every 6 months using the MAIA MP (iCare, Centervue, SpA, Padova, Italy) with a background luminance of 4 asb; 1.27 cd/m2; maximum intensity of 1000 asb, 36 decibel [dB] threshold dynamic range) and a Goldmann III stimulus size corresponding to 0.43 degrees (= 125 µm). The individual MP test points were arranged in 10 horizontally offset rows forming an overall oval-shaped grid. The number of points per row varied from top to bottom as follows: 2, 6, 8, 8, 10, 10, 9, 9, 7, and 2 (see Fig. 1). The maximum distance between MP points was 9.06 degrees (approximately 2.7 mm), whereas the minimum distance between neighboring points was 2 degrees (approximately 0.6 mm).14 Ability to detect the fixation target, total elapsed time to complete the 68-point MP examination of ≤ 30 minutes, reliability test duration of ≤ 20%, and willingness to undertake the MP assessment were defined as MP inclusion criteria (see also clinicaltrials.gov, NCT03525613 for study protocol).

Both study and fellow eyes with Spectralis OCT imaging and matching MAIA MP examinations at BSL and during the 24-month follow-up were eligible for inclusion. In the fellow eye cohort, all eyes were graded for presence of GA on OCT and only those with complete retinal pigment epithelial outer retinal atrophy (cRORA) presence according to the CAM definition and within the scope of the OAKS inclusion criteria were included.15 All eyes, study and fellow eyes, were graded for presence of macular neovascularization (MNV) activity defined as presence of intraretinal and subretinal fluid according to OCT MNV criteria by an ophthalmology resident trained according to reading center standards (K.B.).16 Cases in which degenerative cysts could not be clearly distinguished from MNV activity were classified as fluid. Examples of automated quantification of GA biomarkers in eyes with fluid presence are provided as Supplementary Figure S1. Supplementary Table S1 shows detailed descriptive statistics for all analyzed biomarkers for fluid presence.

Written consent was collected from each patient prior to study inclusion by each participating center. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and this post hoc analysis was approved by the Ethics Committee at the Medical University of Vienna.

Topographic Registration of OCT-Based Anatomic Features and Retinal Sensitivity Test Points

All OCT volumes were analyzed with DL-based algorithms that quantified EZ thickness, EZ loss (meaning EZ thickness = 0 µm) and clinical GA on OCT, defined as RPE loss (see Fig. 1; Supplementary Fig. S2).10,17 Automated segmentation of the three biomarkers was performed using the GA monitor version 1.0 (Retinsight, Vienna, Austria). Two convolutional neural networks were used to segment total RPE loss and the EZ layer. The RPE loss algorithm has as input a full 3D volume and outputs a 2D en face map where each A-scan is labeled as being atrophic or non-atrophic and was validated in previous literature.18 Total GA area was derived from the sum of each RPE loss lesions within one OCT volume.18,19 The EZ thickness algorithm provides a segmentation of the region between the outer border of the interdigitation zone and the inner border of the ellipsoid zone in microns (µm), which was validated against human graders in previous studies,20 as well as commonly implemented in recent literature.10,12–14,21 Areas with an EZ thickness of 0 µm were defined as EZ loss and labeled at the A-scan level in the 3D OCT volume, with the output displayed as 2D en face maps.10

Following a previously published approach,12,13,22,23 the exact MP positions on the MAIA were transformed to positions in the corresponding Spectralis. Description of the registration process, including the registration error of our method, was provided on the BSL cohort of the OAKS trial and published previously.14 In short, registration between the MAIA near infrared (NIR) and the Spectralis NIR was performed semi-automatically using retinal vessel segmentation and landmark matching on both the MAIA-NIR and Spectralis NIR. First, a U-net–based convolutional neural network (CNN) was applied for automated vessel segmentation, and matching vascular junctions (including branches, bifurcations, and crossings) between both modalities.24 Second, the automated registration was visually reviewed, and, if necessary, manually corrected by redefining corresponding junction points and recalculating the transformation matrix using the least-squares error method.12–14,21,23 After successful alignment between both modalities, EZ and RPE loss were defined based on the centerpoint of each MP stimulus. EZ thickness was calculated within a 70-µm radius (= 140-µm diameter) around each MP stimulus to cover the size of the Goldmann III stimulus, which corresponds to 0.43 degrees or 125 µm. Registration accuracy was analyzed in a subcohort at BSL.14 Median registration deviation between MAIA MP NIR and the location on OCT B-scan, as well as between MAIA MP NIR and the location on the Spectralis OCT NIR was 28.21 (interquartile range [IQR] = 18.66–46.81) µm 42.95 (IQR = 28.32–59.37) µm, respectively.14 Accordingly, each MP test point with its respective RS was assigned to one of three categories: EZ loss, RPE loss, and retina with measurable EZ (EZ thickness measurement > 0 µm). A small number of MP points with inconsistent assignment to OCT categories between visits during follow-up were defined as follow-up errors and excluded (see Results).

Development of Models for Disease Progression Modeling and Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics are presented as mean and standard deviation for normally distributed variables and as median and IQR for skewed data separately for fellow eyes and study eyes. Model estimates with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and P values were reported, with P value < 0.05 considered nominally significant. Descriptive statistics and visualization of pointwise RS were provided for both study and fellow eyes, whereas the statistical analysis and prediction of both, localized RS and morphology within individual MP points was restricted to the fellow eye cohort to analyze the natural history progression without any cofounding treatment effect.

Modeling of Retinal Sensitivity

RS was modeled separately based on OCT morphology, both at BSL (retina with measurable EZ, EZ loss, and RPE loss) and during follow-up longitudinally for OCT-based transitions, using a two-component mixture distribution. For state-conditional models during follow-up, the prediction of RS at the follow-up visit included OCT morphology from the previous visit. A two-component mixture distribution was specified: with probability p, RS took the value of −1 dB (absolute scotoma). With probability (1-p), RS followed a continuous distribution, assumed to be normal for the retina with measurable EZ and EZ loss, and a half-normal for RPE loss. At BSL, the probability of scotoma, as well as the location or scale parameters of the corresponding continuous distribution, were estimated separately for each OCT category based on the available BSL data. For transitions, the predictors of p (logistic component) and of the mean of the continuous component were specified according to each OCT category: (1) when conditioned on the retina with measurable EZ, both previous EZ thickness and change in EZ thickness were included as predictors; (2) when conditioned on EZ loss, predictors included the previous OCT category and EZ thickness if the previous OCT category was the retina with measurable EZ; and (3) when conditioned on RPE loss, predictors included the previous OCT category and EZ thickness if the previous OCT category was the retina with measurable EZ. In this case, RS followed a half-normal distribution, with the scale parameter estimated using a linear model after log-transformation of the data. Inclusion of mixture components and morphological covariates was evaluated using likelihood ratio tests (LRTs), retaining the components that significantly improved model fit. Expected RS values as a function of OCT morphology were visualized using line plots, and the fitted mixture distribution of RS, including the probability mass at −1 dB and the continuous component, was illustrated using model-based histograms, conditional on the current OCT category and stratified by previous OCT category. The initial model runs, including simulations, were restricted to MP points with complete data at BSL, month 12 (M12), and month 24 (M24). In a second step, the RS prediction models were adapted to incorporate M6 and M18 data and therefore all MP points with available BSL measurements. Comparison between RS within 20,000 simulated MP points from the statistical model and the observed RS is displayed in Supplementary Figure S3. To account for spatial dependence among MP points within the same eye, a latent spatial field based on a low-rank spatial approach was added and jointly estimated with the fixed effects using the RTMB package in R software by maximizing the marginal likelihood. A detailed description of the low-rank spatial approach is provided in the Supplementary Material (spatial methodology) and Supplementary Figure S4.

Modeling of Morphological Disease Progression of an Individual MP Point

Transition probabilities between OCT morphology states were estimated for individual MP points with complete data at BSL, M12, and M24 (including persistence within the retina with measurable EZ, EZ loss, and RPE loss, as well as transitions from the retina with measurable EZ to EZ loss or RPE loss and from EZ loss to RPE loss). Disease progression of individual MP points was modeled with a Markov chain framework. Transitions from retina with measurable EZ to EZ or RPE loss were modeled using a multinomial regression with EZ thickness as a predictor, whereas transitions from EZ loss were restricted to persistence or progression to RPE loss and modeled with a logistic regression. The RPE loss state was defined as absorbing state. EZ and RPE neighborhood risk scores at the previous visit were included as covariates in both multinomial and logistic regression models to account for neighborhood context and to estimate how neighboring morphology influences the state to which a given MP point transitions next. A detailed description of the spatially informed Markov process is provided in the Supplementary Material (spatial methodology). EZ thickness was modeled as a continuous structural variable using a first order autoregressive model (AR(1)), where the EZ thickness at the respective visit during follow-up depends on the previous EZ thickness.

Descriptive Statistics - Progression of Functional Loss by Anatomic Change in Feature

Figure 2 shows localized changes in RS stratified by morphological OCT category, restricted to MP points that were available at all three time points during follow-up (BSL, M12, and M24). Visualization of RS over time (see Fig. 2) shows a consistent structure-function relationship in disease progression, where progression in anatomic integrity is associated with functional loss. Retina with measurable EZ may remain stable with EZ thickness > 0 µm or switch to complete EZ loss slowly over 24 or faster over 12 months and then continue to RPE loss at 24 months. The fastest progression toward functional loss is seen when EZ loss might have occurred earlier and individual MP points have progressed to RPE loss as soon as after 12 months. When complete EZ disintegration has already occurred at BSL consistent with complete EZ loss (EZ thickness = 0 µm), RPE alteration follows secondarily with further loss in RS over time. RS within MP points that remained within EZ loss over time show that the functional capacity of the photoreceptor/RPE unit is further decreasing as seen with declining dB values. Complete RPE loss becomes visible on OCT dependent on the individual speed of disease progression. In Figure 2, the differential impact of each anatomic biomarker – from the retina with measurable EZ to EZ loss to RPE loss – on retinal function is displayed on the y-axis at BSL, with the highest mean RS for the retina with measurable EZ (10–19 dB), a reduced mean RS with EZ loss (7–10 dB), and a further reduced mean RS for RPE loss (1–2 dB) at BSL. Prediction of Retinal Function Based on Morphological Progression in Fellow Eye Cohort

Table 2 summarizes the prediction models for RS within the retina with measurable EZ, within EZ loss, and within RPE loss and for each respective transition. For both BSL and follow-up, an increase in EZ thickness was positively associated with RS (0.3 dB/µm for both, P < 0.001). For MP points that remained within the retina with measurable EZ during follow-up, RS was significantly associated with the decrease in EZ thickness between visits. Specifically, a 1-µm decrease in EZ thickness between visits corresponded to a 0.1 dB decrease in RS (e.g., a decrease in EZ thickness from 25 µm at the previous visit to 5 µm [Δ −20 µm], would correspond to a decrease in RS of 2.0 dB). Figure 3C displays the expected change in RS during follow-up for different changes in EZ thickness between visits: the green line displays a decrease of 20 µm, the orange line displays a decrease of 10 µm, the purple line a decrease of 5 µm, and the pink line displays no change (Δ 0 µm).

For RS within EZ loss, MP points that remained within EZ loss during follow-up (previous EZ thickness = 0 µm and current EZ thickness = 0 µm) the expected RS outside scotoma was 9.8 dB. For MP points that transitioned from the retina with measurable EZ to EZ loss, a decrease in the previous EZ thickness led to a decrease in the RS after transition to EZ loss by 0.19 dB/ µm (P < 0.001). For example, a 10 µm lower EZ thickness at the previous visit would correspond to an expected 1.9 dB lower RS after transition to EZ loss; vice-versa meaning that increasing EZ thickness would result in higher RS after transition to EZ loss. Discussion

A growing body of evidence highlights the effects of current treatment for GA on the preservation of the condition of the photoreceptor layer and the importance of concurrent developments of artificial intelligence (AI)-based image analysis of GA from OCT volumes.10,11 Our study adds unprecedented insight on the benefit of photoreceptor preservation for functional outcomes. We provide evidence for longitudinal functional changes based on quantification of individual EZ thickness, as well as prediction of functional decline based on morphological disease progression defined as OCT biomarkers, from the retina with measurable EZ to subclinical EZ loss and clinical GA as RPE loss. Our data suggest that longitudinal functional decline in MP is associated with morphological transition of the subclinical biomarker of EZ thinning and loss, whereas a high probability of functional loss (absolute scotoma) is observed within clinical GA defined as RPE loss on OCT.

The need for functional endpoints in GA is a major challenge in GA management and has been highlighted by regulatory authorities and in recent literature.2 MP with its ability to correlate point-to-point anatomic and functional conditions is able to ensure a substantially more elaborate analysis of function in non-exudative AMD compared with conventional BCVA tests.1 With this endeavor, simultaneous efforts are underway to shorten the long examination time and low correlation between mean RS and GA growth for better suited MP outcome metrics.7,21 Post hoc analysis of the Chroma und Spectri data showed that perilesional RS, defined as MP points surrounding the absolute scotomas, reveals the strongest decrease in RS over time.5 Our results add to pre-existing work and indicate that preservation of EZ integrity significantly reduces the risk of functional decline over time. Furthermore, prediction of functional loss and probability of topographic morphological disease progression can be predicted from BSL EZ thickness. MP points that remain within EZ loss over time and/ or transition from EZ loss at BSL present a priori a lower RS compared with MP points within the retina with measurable EZ at BSL. Furthermore, RS after transition to EZ loss depends on EZ thickness at the previous visit. Importantly, the area of EZ loss is associated with a functional decline, but not complete functional loss with absolute scotoma as observed within RPE loss. As shown in our previous morphological analyses, EZ thinning and loss (associated with reduced RS), precedes RPE loss (associated with absolute scotoma).10,20,25 Concomitantly, it was proven in recent literature that the speed of disease progression is tightly based on the EZ/RPE ratio at BSL.4,10 The structural importance of identifying areas of disease activity in GA was highlighted in a post hoc analysis of the OAKS and DERBY trials with stronger treatment effect at the level of the EZ compared to RPE loss under complement inhibition therapy.10 The present study highlights that localized morphological disease progression during follow-up is strongly influenced by EZ thickness at the previous visit and morphology in neighboring MP points. Our results indicate a higher probability to transition to EZ / and RPE loss, if the surrounding MP points are located within EZ and RPE loss or only EZ loss. Our statistical analysis revealed that the functional decline is a stepwise process where every µm in EZ thinning results in a corresponding functional loss. Our statistical analysis estimates a mean RS of 10 dB after transition to EZ loss with a 20% BSL probability of absolute scotoma within EZ loss compared with 69% BSL probability of an absolute scotoma within RPE loss. Hence, we postulate that a precise identification and quantification of the EZ loss area might be an additional biomarker for preservation of retinal function under therapy. The results from the current study provides the missing evidence that the preservation of the photoreceptor layer at the level of the EZ has not only morphological, but also functional implications. This has also been highlighted by the acknowledgment of EZ loss as possible outcome measure in GA trials by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA).

A recent post hoc analysis of the OAKS trial data showed that MP can be used to reveal a functional treatment effect under complement inhibition.1 However, the analysis was restricted to the central foveal 4 and 16 MP stimulus points. The authors discussed the limitations of fixed MP grids, which under-represent the spatial growth of GA lesions and therefore propose implementation of targeted MP as a solution. An image-guided approach to target GA lesions in FAF using the MP-3 and the MAIA device was described in previous literature and established that the use of targeted MP is feasible with a shorter examination time.26 Concurrently, the use of OCT lesion-targeted MP grid was introduced to catch early changes in RS sooner by targeting areas of interest with subclinical changes.15 Personalized MP grids tailored toward active GA lesion components for each patient could significantly reduce the test duration, making MP a potential useful asset in clinical trials as well as clinical routine.15

We postulate that a BSL scotoma probability of 69% within RPE loss, as well as an estimated RS of 6.7 dB within RPE loss outside of scotoma, highlights that the condition of the neurosensory retina surrounding each respective MP point influences RS, likely due to light scattering from surrounding areas and size of the MP point projected to the retina. This hypothesis is in line with recent work by Pfau et al. describing a mean RS of 6.92 ± 0.86 dB for mesopic RS within GA adjacent to the junctional zone.27,28 The previous condition of the retina at the respective MP points influences RS during follow-up, whereas the highest probability of absolute scotoma remains in MP points that stay within RPE loss over time. Notably, this result is also influenced by the statistical method of conditioning the current status on the previous status in the Markov chain process. Still, the analysis of absolute scotoma probability within clinical GA on OCT is of high clinical relevance, as the increase in scotomatous points in GA over time is an emerging research focus.1,7 Meleth et al. observed a strong correlation between the number of scotomas and the GA extension,7 whereas a post hoc analysis of the Chroma and Spectri trial found a strong correlation between GA area growth and increase in the number of scotomatous points.5 However, the authors postulate that the dichotomization of RS leads to information loss and is insufficient to catch subtle changes. Our results support this hypothesis, as we introduce measurements of EZ thickness and EZ loss as important parameters for functional outcomes.

The strength of our analysis includes the point-to-point correlation of structure and function, the availability of a large dataset of patients prospectively imaged and examined in a multicenter setting with standardized protocols, as well as comprehensive, statistical analysis including spatial correspondence between individual OCT areas underlining the applicability and clinical relevance of our results. The practical aim for improving GA management is to provide an objective structure-function association between MP and OCT to predict RS changes from automatically quantified OCT biomarker. The development of approved tools for the clinical routine are underway. In addition, this longitudinal analysis confirms and strengthens results from previous cross-sectional studies13,14 and provide the so far missing robust evidence of functional implications of the preservation of the photoreceptor layer at the level of the EZ.

Still, our study has limitations that should be considered for interpreting the outcome. First, patients that converted to neovascular AMD were not excluded from this analysis, as manual grading of fluid presence was performed and mean RS did not differ between eyes with and without fluid. A statistical model with exclusion of eyes with exudation is provided in the Supplementary Material and the results remained consistent with the reported model. Second, the effect of therapy was not considered in this paper, as this is defined as a proof-of-concept analysis of the correlation between RS and OCT during follow-up. Therefore, we included descriptive statistics and exploratory data display for both study and fellow eyes, but focused our statistical analysis and prediction models merely on the fellow eye cohort. Focusing the statistical analysis on the fellow-eye cohort sufficiently addressed the association between structural disease progression and changes in RS as the primary objective of our study. Importantly, the aim of this paper was not to demonstrate therapeutic efficacy, but rather to provide a proof of concept that quantifiable subclinical structural changes, such as longitudinal alterations in EZ thickness, are meaningfully associated with functional changes. The next step based on our findings should be taken to analyze the effect of complement inhibition on RS preservation within areas of disease activity, defined as EZ thinning and loss on OCT in the treatment versus the sham arm. Third, this is a post hoc analysis and sample size calculation of the clinical trial was not performed for the purpose of the implemented statistical methods. Therefore, the reported P values are nominal.

Conclusions

This work provides robust evidence for the value of AI-based OCT feature analyses and the functional potential of photoreceptor preservation reflected in the EZ biomarker over time. This information has break-through implications for patient management in clinical practice, as well as novel therapeutic approaches and the design of future clinical trials.
