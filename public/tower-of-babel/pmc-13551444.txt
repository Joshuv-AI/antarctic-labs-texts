Noninvasive or minimally invasive neuromodulation in animal models of acute ischemic stroke: A systematic review and meta‐analysis
Authors: Igor Caitano Salles, Mateus Paquesse Pellegrino, Jessica Borges Kroth, Clara Lucato dos Santos, Luca Schiliro Tristao, Jullyanna Sabrysna Morais Shinosaki, Wanderley Marques Bernardo, Adriana Bastos Conforto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeuromodulation therapy has emerged as a potential brain cytoprotective strategy in the hyperacute phase of ischemic stroke (IS). This systematic review aimed to evaluate the cytoprotective effects of neuromodulation in animal models of IS.MethodsA systematic search of the MEDLINE, Embase, and LILACS databases was conducted to identify in vivo preclinical studies that investigated noninvasive or minimally invasive neuromodulation therapy administered within 24 h of arterial occlusion and included a control group. The primary outcome was infarct volume, and the secondary outcomes included assessments of neurological function using validated behavioral scales. Random‐effects meta‐analyses were performed for all outcomes using the Cochrane Review Manager software (version 5.4.1). The protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews database (No. CRD42020165347).ResultsThirty studies were included in the qualitative synthesis (2347 reported animals), and 25 studies were eligible for meta‐analysis. Most studies employed middle cerebral artery occlusion models. Neuromodulation techniques included electroacupuncture, repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation, transcranial direct current stimulation, and vagus nerve stimulation. Pooled analyses demonstrated that neuromodulation significantly reduced infarct volume both in absolute (standardized mean difference [SMD]: −0.80; p < 0.001) and relative terms (SMD: −2.01; p < 0.001) and improved neurological outcomes (SMD: −1.53; p = 0.006). However, considerable heterogeneity was observed across studies and interventions.ConclusionNeuromodulation confers brain cytoprotective effects in experimental IS models; however, the high heterogeneity in stimulation parameters and outcomes limits its translational potential. Standardization of experimental protocols and reporting is required to improve reproducibility.

INTRODUCTION

Despite advances in acute ischemic stroke (IS) management over the past few decades, the global burden remains high, with an estimated 70.4 million disability‐adjusted life years lost due to IS in 2021. 1 , 2 The interruption of cerebral blood flow can lead not only to irreversible cell injury but also to neuronal dysfunction in the ischemic penumbra, a region that is potentially salvageable if prompt reperfusion is achieved. 3 Intravenous thrombolysis and mechanical thrombectomy are effective interventions to restore perfusion and prevent irreversible penumbral damage.4, 5, 6, 7 However, their use is limited by relatively narrow therapeutic windows (4.5 h for thrombolysis in the absence of perfusion imaging and 24 h for thrombectomy). 8 Numerous preclinical studies have investigated therapies aimed at protecting the ischemic penumbra, but only a small proportion of these therapies have progressed to clinical trials.9, 10, 11, 12 Examples include NXY‐059 (a free‐radical scavenger; SAINT II trial), nerinetide (an NMDA receptor modulator; ESCAPE‐NA1 trial), natalizumab (a leukocyte migration inhibitor; ACTION II trial), and edaravone‐dexborneol (a free‐radical scavenger; INSIST‐ED trial).13, 14, 15, 16 Although edaravone‐dexborneol was incorporated into the 2023 Chinese Stroke Association guidelines, 17 it has not been adopted in other major international stroke guidelines.

An ideal cytoprotective strategy would preserve penumbral tissue within a feasible clinical time window, improve outcomes, demonstrate an acceptable safety profile, and be compatible with existing treatments. 4 , 18 Such interventions include some promising neuromodulation techniques. 12 , 18 , 19 Neuromodulation refers to the use of electrical, magnetic, or optical stimuli to modulate neural activity. 20 , 21 Noninvasive methods include transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) and repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS). 20 tDCS applies low‐intensity currents via scalp electrodes to modulate neuronal excitability, whereas rTMS employs alternating magnetic fields to induce cortical stimulation. TMS is approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration for the treatment of depression and migraine. 22 , 23 Anodal tDCS (A‐tDCS) and high‐frequency rTMS enhance cortical excitability, whereas cathodal tDCS (C‐tDCS) and low‐frequency rTMS decrease excitability. 20 , 21 Other techniques include transcranial alternating current stimulation and transcranial random noise stimulation, which deliver low‐intensity currents through the scalp to modulate neural activity and connectivity. 20 , 21

Peripheral neuromodulation approaches target the cranial or peripheral nerves to affect brain function. Electroacupuncture (EA), in which a current is applied to acupuncture points, is associated with the modulation of functional connectivity 24 and cerebral blood flow. 25 Vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) is delivered invasively with implanted devices or noninvasively VNS (nVNS) via transcutaneous auricular stimulation, targeting the auricular branch of the vagus nerve. 20 , 21 , 26 , 27 nVNS has been approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration for the treatment of migraine 28 and affects neurotransmitter systems in the brain via projections from the nucleus tractus solitarius. 29 , 30

This systematic review and meta‐analysis aimed to advance translational efforts in stroke cytoprotection by synthesizing preclinical evidence on the effects of noninvasive neuromodulation during the hyperacute phase of IS. This review focused on key outcomes, including infarct volume, behavioral performance, cerebral edema, and blood–brain barrier (BBB) integrity.

METHODS

Design and registration

This systematic review was designed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) recommendations and guidelines from 2020. 31 The review was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; CRD42020165347). Ethical approval and informed consent were not required because this study was a systematic review and meta‐analysis of previously published preclinical studies.

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria were randomized preclinical animal studies that performed noninvasive or minimally invasive neuromodulation and included control interventions in animals within 24 h of experimentally induced IS. The control interventions consisted of no stimulation or sham stimulation. Noninvasive neuromodulation included EA, nVNS, tDCS, transcranial alternating current stimulation, transcranial random noise stimulation, or rTMS. Ex vivo, in vitro, and in silico studies; studies with human participants or invasive studies; and case reports, abstracts, comments, letters to the editor, and reviews were excluded. Only studies published in English were included. No restrictions were imposed on species, sex, age, or reperfusion therapy.

Search and study selection

We systematically searched scientific databases, including MEDLINE (Medical Literature Analysis and Retrieval System Online, accessed through the PubMed interface), Excerpta Medica Database (Embase), and Literature in the Health Sciences in Latin America and the Caribbean (LILACS). The keywords included, but were not limited to, “stroke,” “vagus nerve stimulation,” “electroacupuncture,” “random noise stimulation,” “repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation,” and “transcranial direct current stimulation.” The search syntax is provided in Supplementary Material S1. The search was initially conducted on May 9, 2020, and updated on April 20, 2024.

Three independent reviewers assessed the retrieved studies. Following title and abstract screening, full‐text screening was conducted. Discrepancies regarding inclusion were resolved by discussion until mutual agreement between reviewers. If discrepancies were identified across reports or if information in the manuscripts was missing or unclear, the corresponding authors were contacted via email (up to three attempts) to obtain clarification and complete the study selection process.

Data collection process

Two reviewers independently extracted the outcomes of interest from each article. Upon completion, the extracted data were compared, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion until consensus. If outcomes were not available in the text, the corresponding authors were contacted. If no response was received, values were extracted from figures using WebPlotDigitizer software (version 5, Automeris LLC, Pacifica, CA, USA) by a single reviewer. To ensure accuracy, axis calibration was performed prior to extraction. For each study, the number of animals, species, sex, age, weight, affected hemisphere, lesion site, method of occlusion, occlusion duration, occluded artery, occlusion location, animal exclusions, and reasons for exclusion were extracted.

The primary outcome was infarct size, reported as absolute volume (mm3) or relative area (%), with some studies adjusting for edema using the contralesional hemisphere. Secondary outcomes comprised physiological, behavioral, and safety endpoints. Physiological endpoints included motor‐evoked potentials, cerebral blood flow, total phosphate ratio, and expression of neurogenesis‐related markers. Behavioral endpoints encompassed neurological impairment scores, including but not limited to composite scores of motor and sensory functions, such as the Modified Neurological Severity Score (mNSS), Garcia Score, and Zea Longa scoring system that assesses motor function.32, 33, 34 Safety outcomes consisted of cerebral edema (measured by the edema index calculated as the ratio of the total volume of the ischemic hemisphere to that of the contralateral hemisphere) and BBB (measured in mL/g of cerebral tissue per min, using immunohistochemistry or magnetic resonance imaging [MRI]).

Assessment of risk of bias and reporting bias

Two reviewers independently assessed the risk of bias using SYRCLE's risk‐of‐bias (RoB) tool across the following domains: randomization, concealment of allocation, blinding, sample homogeneity, housing, and missing outcome data. 35 For each domain, the studies were classified as having high, low, or unclear risk of bias. As SYRCLE's RoB tool does not provide a standard scoring system, the reviewers qualitatively assessed the overall risk of bias across all domains. Disagreements were resolved by consensus.

Statistical methods and effect measures

Effect measures were entered into Cochrane Review Manager software (RevMan; version 5.4.1, The Cochrane Collaboration, London, UK). Forest plots, heterogeneity assessments, and standardized mean difference (SMD) calculations were performed with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), estimated with fixed‐effects (if I 2 ≤ 50%) or random‐effects (I 2 > 50%) models. Heterogeneity was assessed using the chi‐square test. The overall pooled effect for each meta‐analysis was tested using Z‐tests. A two‐sided p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Meta‐analyses were performed without restricting the number of studies in each subgroup. When a single control arm and multiple intervention arms were included in the same study, only one intervention was shown in the forest plot. The intervention was selected based on the frequency with which it was administered in the included studies. Subgroup meta‐analyses were conducted for each type of neuromodulation intervention.

Interventions

EA was performed in 18/30 (60%) studies, tDCS in 5/30 (17%), VNS in 5/30 (17%), and rTMS in 2/30 (7%) studies. The details of the neuromodulation parameters are available in Table S1.

rTMS

Buetefisch et al. 37 administered low‐frequency rTMS to adult (2–3 months old) Sprague–Dawley rats. The rTMS coil was positioned on the vertex to target the sensorimotor cortex bilaterally, and the stimulation intensity was set below the motor threshold level. In the active groups, rTMS was delivered twice for 30 min, starting at 1.5, 3, 4, or 7 h after MCA occlusion. Caglayan et al. 36 investigated the effects of high‐ and low‐frequency rTMS. Although both hemispheres were stimulated, the coil was positioned closer to the hemisphere affected by the stroke.

tDCS

Cheng et al. 42 administered bihemispheric tDCS, while Cherchi et al., 39 Kong et al., 40 Peruzzotti‐Jametti et al., 38 and Notturno et al. 41 applied ipsilesional C‐tDCS at intensities ranging from 0.1 to 0.25 mA. Peruzzotti‐Jametti et al. 38 also tested the effects of ipsilesional A‐tDCS on a set of animals.

nVNS

Surface electrodes or needles were applied to the skin overlying the vagus nerve in the neck (cervical nVNS) or to the dermatome in the external ear innervated by the vagus nerve (auricular nVNS). Ay et al. 44 and Jiang et al. 45 applied contralesional auricular nVNS, whereas Ay et al. 46 and Lindemann et al. 47 administered ipsilesional cervical nVNS. Yang et al. 43 performed contralesional cervical nVNS. The nVNS frequencies ranged from 20 to 25 Hz, and only two studies reported the current amplitudes (Table S1).

Eelectroacupuncture (EA)

Different acupoints were stimulated, and the most frequent were GV20 (Baihui, 6/18 studies, 33%) and ST36 (Zusanli, 6/18 studies, 33%), followed by GV26 (Shuigou, 4/18 studies, 22%) and LI11 (Quchi, 4/18 studies, 22%). The EA current amplitudes ranged from 0.01 to 4 mA. The most common stimulation amplitude was 1 mA.

Outcomes

Infarct size was evaluated in 622 rodents (288 Sprague–Dawley rats, 142 Wistar rats, 61 spontaneously hypertensive rats, 88 C57BL/6 mice, 25 BALB/c mice, and 18 Cx3CR1 GFP+ mice). EA was performed in 13 studies, VNS in five, tDCS in five, and rTMS in two (Table S1). The absolute infarct size (in mm3) was reported in 6/25 (24%) studies (two rTMS, two tDCS, and two VNS), as shown in Figure 3. The infarct size relative to the ipsilesional, contralesional, or both brain hemispheres was measured in 19/25 (76%) studies (Figure 4).

A significant decrease in infarct volume (mm3) was observed after rTMS (SMD, −0.90; 95% CI: −1.32 to −0.47; Z = 4.16; p < 0.001; I 2 = 8%) (two studies; Figure 3). Buetefisch et al. 37 reported decreased infarct size after low‐frequency rTMS (1 Hz) over ipsilesional sensorimotor cortex at 90% of the twitch threshold when delivered at 1.5, 3, and 4 h but not at 7 h after MCA occlusion. Caglayan et al. 36 reported no benefit of low‐frequency rTMS at 100% of the resting motor threshold 1.5 h after MCA occlusion but described benefits of high‐frequency (20 Hz) rTMS within the same time window. No difference was observed after tDCS. 39 , 41 For nVNS, the pooled estimate favored treatment, 43 , 47 although results differed across stimulation protocols.

Figure 4 shows the results of studies assessing the relative infarct area. The denominators used to calculate relative infarct area varied across studies. Among the 19 studies that measured relative infarct area, nine (47%) studies used the contralesional hemisphere, six (32%) used the combined area of both hemispheres, two (11%) used twice the contralesional hemisphere area, and two (11%) did not specify how the percentage area was determined. EA was used in 13 studies,48, 50, 51, 52, 53, 54, 56, 57, 58, 60, 62, 63, 64 nVNS in three studies,44, 45, 46 and tDCS in three studies. 38 , 40 , 42

Neuromodulation interventions significantly reduced the relative infarct area overall, albeit with high statistical heterogeneity (SMD −2.01; 95% CI: −2.47 to −1.54; Z = 8.45; p < 0.001; I 2 = 63%). Each active intervention, when analyzed separately, led to lower infarct area than control interventions.

Subgroup analyses according to time from stroke onset (≤1, 1–4, and >4 h) showed that, when all interventions were considered together, the relative infarct size was significantly reduced across all time windows (Figure S1), whereas absolute infarct size was reduced in the ≤1 and 1–4 h windows but not in the >4 h window (Figure S2). For rTMS (Figure S3), nVNS (Figure S4), and tDCS (Figure S5), treatment effects did not differ significantly across the available time windows. For EA (Figure S6), treatment administered during later time windows was more beneficial than treatment administered before stroke or within the first hour after stroke, but heterogeneity was high (I 2 = 97%).

Neurological impairment

Neurological impairment was assessed in 127 rodents: 62 Sprague–Dawley rats, 53 Wistar rats, and 12 spontaneously hypertensive rats. In four studies investigating the effects of EA and three investigating the effects of VNS, animals were assessed between 1 and 7 days after MCA occlusion. Three EA studies 50 , 51 , 60 and two VNS studies 44 , 46 used the Zea Longa score, one VNS study 47 used the Garcia score, and one EA study 57 used the mNSS. Because lower scores indicate lower deficits, except for the Garcia score, the Garcia scores were inverted before the meta‐analysis to preserve direction (Figure 5). Neuromodulation improved neurological impairments, albeit with high statistical heterogeneity (SMD, −1.53; 95% CI, −2.62 to −0.43; Z = 2.74; p = 0.006; I 2 = 84%). EA, but not VNS, showed a significant benefit. The I 2 was 91% for VNS and 80% for EA studies.

Cerebral edema and BBB integrity

Edema was assessed in one study. 38 The edema index was significantly lower after ipsilesional C‐tDCS (17.52% ± 2.64%), but not after ipsilesional A‐tDCS (26.80% ± 1.13%), than after sham stimulation (32.23% ± 2.13%) in C57BL/6 mice at a current density of 5.5 mA/cm2. A‐tDCS led to BBB dysfunction, as evidenced by immunoglobulin G extravasation assessed using immunohistochemistry with an avidin‐peroxidase reaction. 38 Conversely, VNS reduced BBB leakage, as assessed using immunohistochemistry and dynamic contrast‐enhanced MRI. 43

Other outcomes

Data on motor‐evoked potentials, expression of neurogenesis‐related markers, and phosphate ratios were not collected because of the high heterogeneity in outcomes and measurement units.

DISCUSSION

This systematic review and meta‐analysis showed significant reductions in infarct size and improved neurological outcomes in rodents treated with noninvasive neuromodulation after transient ischemia. Neuromodulation efficacy in reducing infarct size appears to depend on the timing of intervention after stroke onset. Although the overall methodological quality was acceptable, several studies were affected by unclear reporting of allocation procedures, baseline characteristics, housing conditions, and blinding. Consequently, 18 of the 30 studies (60%) were classified as having an unclear or high risk of bias.

Differences in infarct size assessment methods, stimulation targets, and outcomes likely contributed to the high statistical heterogeneity observed in the EA, VNS, and tDCS analyses, as well as in the overall pooled analysis. Evidence regarding the effects of diverse paradigms of ipsilesional rTMS 36 , 37 and C‐tDCS38, 39, 40, 41, 42 on infarct volume reduction remains limited and conflicting. For nVNS, the results were generally consistent across four studies using 20–25‐Hz stimulation for 60 min.43, 44, 45, 46 In contrast, Lindemann et al. 47 applied only two 2‐min stimulations at 25 Hz, separated by a 15 min period, which may account for the absence of a significant effect.

EA was the most investigated intervention for reducing infarct size. Of the 18 studies using EA, four 50 , 56 , 57 , 63 expressed the infarct area as a percentage of the contralesional hemisphere, five 48 , 51 , 52 , 53 , 64 as a percentage of the combined area of both hemispheres, two 58 , 62 as a percentage of twice the contralesional hemisphere area, and two 54 , 60 did not specify the method used. Five studies 25 , 49 , 55 , 59 , 61 did not assess infarct size. Most studies assessed neuromodulation effects on the relative infarct area rather than infarct volume. To correct for edema, some studies used the contralateral or duplicated contralateral hemisphere as a denominator, while others used the ipsilateral hemisphere. This difference may influence the effect magnitude but should not alter the differences between the intervention and control groups within the same methodology. Measuring infarct size in mm3 provides a direct value but does not account for inter‐animal size differences, making results less comparable across species.

Few studies assessed neurological outcomes after nVNS 44 , 46 , 47 or EA. 50 , 51 , 57 , 60 EA improved functional outcomes, although with substantial statistical heterogeneity. Five studies used the Zea Longa score, 44 , 46 , 50 , 51 , 60 while Lindemann et al. 47 used the Garcia score 32 and Xu et al. 57 used the mNSS. 33 Different scales are unlikely to explain the high heterogeneity observed in the EA effects (I 2 = 80%) and overall analysis (I 2 = 84%). Other factors, including differences in outcome assessment timing (24–168 h after stroke onset), increased variance from small sample sizes (4–10 animals per group in EA studies), and biological variability related to functional recovery, likely contributed to the heterogeneity. Furthermore, the lack of thresholds for clinically meaningful (rather than statistically significant) changes in behavioral scales in animal models poses a challenge in translating these findings to human studies.

Overall, the data suggest that time‐dependent effects may be relevant for EA but not for the other neuromodulation interventions examined. However, the interpretation of these results is limited by substantial statistical heterogeneity in the EA subgroup analyses, which may reflect differences in stimulation targets and parameters, as well as the small number of studies available for the other intervention subgroups. Given that the effects of neuromodulation are state‐dependent and that molecular and cellular responses evolve dynamically throughout the post‐stroke period, further research is needed to identify optimal stimulation paradigms for specific phases of recovery or, ideally, to tailor treatment according to biomarkers that reflect the biological state of the affected tissue, rather than relying solely on predefined time windows. 65 , 66

The absence of biomarkers that can be used to identify potentially salvageable tissue, which could help select animals that are most likely to benefit from cytoprotective interventions, is a notable limitation of the studies included in this systematic review and meta‐analysis. In clinical practice, neuroimaging is routinely used to identify patients with potentially reversible penumbral tissue injury and to guide the selection of reperfusion therapies. Advanced imaging techniques have extended the thrombectomy window to up to 24 h after stroke onset by identifying mismatch between irreversibly injured tissue and salvageable penumbra using diffusion‐weighted MRI and perfusion computed tomography or MRI. 4 , 67 Incorporating analogous imaging biomarkers into preclinical stroke models could improve the evaluation of cytoprotective interventions, enable more biologically informed treatment selection, and enhance the translational relevance of preclinical findings. 68

The studies included in this systematic review identified several mechanisms that may underlie the cytoprotective effects of neuromodulation. These included reduced microglial activation and inflammation following EA, 50 , 51 , 56 , 57 nVNS, 44 , 46 and C‐tDCS 38 , 39 ; inhibition of cortical spreading depolarizations by nVNS 47 and C‐tDCS 41 ; and reduced apoptosis following EA 63 and rTMS. 37 Additional effects reported for EA included modulation of voltage‐gated sodium channel expression 48 ; increased angiogenesis 54 ; enhanced cerebral blood flow 25 , 62 ; regulation of genes involved in neurotrophic signaling, inflammation, and apoptosis 58 ; increased cell proliferation 52 , 53 ; enhanced neuroplasticity 61 , 64 ; improved lactate‐dependent energy metabolism 49 ; and reduced generation of reactive oxygen species. 59

The mechanisms of neuromodulation vary according to the type of intervention and stimulation paradigm. Moreover, even within a single modality, the biological effects may differ according to the number of stimulation sessions. For example, suppression of glial maturation factor‐β upregulation was observed after a single session of EA but not after repeated sessions. 58 Conversely, neurological improvement was only observed after multiple sessions, highlighting the complex relationship between the biological and functional effects of neuromodulation.

Ischemia initiates a cascade of deleterious processes, including energy failure, excitotoxicity, inflammation, and activation of apoptotic pathways. 69 Accordingly, the focus of cytoprotection has evolved from neurons alone to the neurovascular unit, which comprises neurons, glial cells, endothelial cells, pericytes, and the extracellular matrix, including the basement membrane. 69 The BBB, a key component of the neurovascular unit, serves as a critical interface between the blood and brain. 70 Despite the importance of these structures, only two studies reported safety outcomes related to cerebral edema and BBB integrity. The suggestion that A‐tDCS may increase cerebral edema 38 raises important safety concerns. In contrast, the finding of reduced BBB leakage following VNS is encouraging and warrants further investigation. 43 Overall, the safety profiles of neuromodulation therapies remain insufficiently characterized and require evaluation using standardized and consistently reported outcome measures in future studies. Because many of the proposed mechanisms involve modulation of neurovascular and inflammatory pathways, assessment of safety outcomes is particularly important.

Conclusions regarding the translational potential of neuromodulation in stroke are limited by differences between animal models and clinical practice. First, because most studies used transient reperfusion models, no conclusions can be drawn for patients who are ineligible for thrombolysis or thrombectomy, or for those without spontaneous recanalization. There is an unmet need for effective interventions for these patients, which this systematic review and meta‐analysis did not address. Second, most studies included only healthy young male animals, whereas recent guidelines for animal stroke models recommend incorporating subjects with comorbidities and female animals, 71 since half of patients with stroke are women aged > 60 years with vascular risk factors such as hypertension and diabetes. 1 , 2 Furthermore, consistent reporting of stimulation parameters (current amplitude, frequency, and target) and rigorous determination of optimal paradigms within ischemia models are critical for comparability and progress. A further limitation of the included studies is that WebPlotDigitizer was used to extract data from images when the means and standard deviations were unavailable. This approach is reliable and valid. 72 Nevertheless, authors should report individual data or provide means and standard deviations to facilitate accurate synthesis.

Although preclinical evidence suggests that noninvasive neuromodulation may confer cytoprotective effects following IS, its translational relevance remains limited by methodological heterogeneity, insufficient safety reporting, and the predominance of reperfusion models in young, healthy male animals. Future preclinical studies should prioritize standardized and transparently reported stimulation protocols, rigorous safety assessments, and the inclusion of clinically relevant models. In addition, dose–response studies, parameter optimization, and mechanistic investigations are needed to determine whether neuromodulation can become an effective adjunct to standard IS therapies.
