Implantable collamer lens combined with femtosecond laser-assisted arcuate keratotomy and toric implantable collamer lens for high myopia with moderate-to-low astigmatism corrections: a comparative study
Authors: Shun An, Honglin Ye, Jian Ye, Yan Huo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo compare clinical outcomes of implantable collamer lens (ICL) combined with femtosecond laser-assisted arcuate keratotomy (FSAK) and toric implantable collamer lens (TICL) for high myopia with moderate-to-low astigmatism corrections.MethodsIn this single-center, non-randomized, parallel-group study, patients with high myopia and moderate-to-low astigmatism (0.75D ~ 2.00D) received either ICL+FSAK or TICL treatment and underwent a 3-month postoperative follow-up. We compared the efficacy, safety, predictability, and stability between the two groups, along with outcomes including vector analysis of astigmatism, intraocular pressure (IOP), corneal endothelial cell density (ECD), vault, modulation transfer function (MTF) cutoff, Strehl ratio (SR), and objective scatter index (OSI).ResultsAt three months postoperatively, median manifest astigmatism in ICL+FSAK group was − 0.25 diopter (D), similar to that in TICL group was − 0.25D (P = 0.774). At all timepoints, two groups were comparable in terms of uncorrected distance visual acuity, corrected distance visual acuity, manifest spherical equivalent, manifest astigmatism, efficacy index, safety index. At 1 day, 1 month, and 3 months postoperatively, median surgically induced astigmatism in ICL+FSAK group were 0.74 D, 0.79 D, and 0.82 D, smaller than those in TICL group (0.86 D, 0.99 D, and 1.01 D) (P < 0.05). Similarly, median correction index postoperatively were 0.69 and 0.75 in ICL+FSAK group, smaller than those in TICL group (0.82 and 0.84) at 1 month and 3 months (P < 0.05). At all timepoints, no significant differences in difference vector or absolute value of angle error occurred between two groups.Similar results also occurred in IOP, ECD, vault, MTF cutoff, SR, and OSI.ConclusionsTICL resulted in higher SIA and CI compared to FSAK, but there was no significant difference in postoperative manifest astigmatism. Therefore, both methods are excellent choices for high myopia with moderate-to-low astigmatism.Trial registration numberChiCTR2400092267. The registration date is November 13, 2024. Clinical Trial Registry: Chinese Clinical Trial Registry.Keywords: Implantable collamer lens, Myopia, Astigmatism, Femtosecond laser, Arcuate keratotomy

Introduction

By 2050, over 4.7 billion people are projected to suffer from myopia, with high myopia potentially affecting up to 20% of this population [1]. Astigmatism is another common refractive error, affecting roughly 40% of individuals [2]. It is frequently observed in highly myopic individuals, and astigmatism exceeding 0.75 D can cause noticeable symptoms such as blurred vision, double vision, and glare [3]. Therefore, the correction of astigmatism should accompany myopia correction during refractive surgery to achieve optimal visual quality. In cases of high myopic defects that render the patient unsuitable for corneal refractive surgery, phakic intraocular lenses have been used as a corrective option. These phakic lenses currently include anterior chamber iris-fixated lenses (iris-claw lenses such as Artisan and Artiflex, and Verisyse and Veriflex) and posterior chamber–implanted lenses, such as the Visian Implantable Collamer Lens (ICL), Eyecryl, and the Implantable Phakic Contact Lens (IPCL) [4–6]. All of these lenses have demonstrated good refractive outcomes; however, the iris-claw group has been associated with progressive chronic endothelial cell loss in a proportion of patients, which in some cases may require endothelial keratoplasty [7–10]. Posterior chamber phakic lenses, particularly the newer models, have proven to be much safer with respect to the corneal endothelium. In recent years [11], ICL surgery has become widely used for refractive error correction because of its broad correction range, surgical reversibility, and high visual quality. Numerous studies have validated its long-term efficacy and safety [12–14].

Multiple methods exist for astigmatism correction during ICL implantation, including toric implantable collamer lenses (TICL), limbal relaxing incisions (LRI), arcuate keratotomy (AK), and steep meridian clear corneal incision (SCCI). TICL simultaneously corrects both myopia and astigmatism across a wide correction range, making it a commonly used method. However, postoperative lens rotation may cause vision deterioration, requiring secondary surgery. Additionally, the relatively high cost of TICL and lengthy customization period impose financial and time burdens on patients [15–17]. LRI involves creating single or paired relaxing corneal incisions along the steep meridian peripherally within the limbal vascular arcade. This method corrects corneal astigmatism by flattening the steep meridian, thus reducing astigmatic power. However, LRI has a limited range of astigmatism correction, and its effectiveness decreases significantly in the early postoperative period [18, 19]. SCCI involves creating a clear corneal incision along the steep meridian of astigmatism and implanting an ICL through this incision. However, SCCI has been associated with poor postoperative efficacy, stability, and predictability [20]. AK involves creating a pair of symmetrical arcuate incisions at a depth of 70%-90% within a central corneal diameter of 7–9 mm to reduce corneal astigmatism through coupling effects [21]. Studies comparing ICL implantation combined with manual AK versus TICL implantation for high myopic astigmatism reported slightly lower predictability with manual AK [22].

In recent years, with advancements in femtosecond laser technology, femtosecond laser-assisted arcuate keratotomy (FSAK) has gained widespread clinical application. Compared to manual AK, FSAK provides greater precision in incision arc length, depth, and positioning, as well as reduced complication risks [23]. To our knowledge, no study has yet reported the efficacy of combining ICL implantation with FSAK for correcting high myopia accompanied by moderate-to-low astigmatism (search performed on October 1, 2025). Therefore, this study aims to compare the clinical outcomes of FSAK and TICL in correcting moderate-to-low astigmatism.

Methods

Trial design

This study enrolled 72 subjects (102 eyes) with high myopia who underwent ICL implantation with FSAK or TICL implantation at the Ophthalmology Department, the Army Medical Center of PLA from November 2023 to November 2024. All subjects received information regarding potential surgical risks and provided written informed consent. Participants were assigned to either the ICL implantation with FSAK group or the TICL implantation group based on patient preference. The study followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Army Medical Center of PLA (approval No. 228, 2023).

The primary outcome was the manifest astigmatism measured by subjective refraction examination at 3 months postoperatively. The secondary outcomes included uncorrected distance visual acuity (UDVA), corrected distance visual acuity (CDVA), subjective refraction, intraocular pressure (IOP), and slit-lamp examination. Additionally, the EM-3000 corneal endothelial cell counter (TOMEY Corporation, Japan), SIGMA 1000 optical coherence tomography (OCT) scanner (Tuopai, China), and OQAS II dual-channel visual quality analysis system (Visiometrics, Spain) were used to record corneal endothelial cell density (ECD), vault, modulation transfer function (MTF) cutoff, Strehl ratio (SR), and objective scatter index (OSI).

Patients

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria included: (1) age between 18 and 45 years; (2) myopia between − 6.00 D and − 10.00 D, with manifest astigmatism between − 0.75 D and − 2.00 D; (3) keratographic astigmatism axis differing less than 10° from subjective refraction astigmatism axis, with a difference less than 0.50 D; (4) central corneal endothelial cell density ≥ 2000 cells/mm² with regular cell morphology; (5) anterior chamber depth ≥ 2.80 mm with open anterior chamber angle; (6) strong desire to stop wearing spectacles, understanding and acceptance of the procedure, and reasonable expectations for surgical outcomes.

Exclusion criteria included: (1) history of ocular surface disease, intraocular surgery, or trauma; (2) organic ocular lesions including corneal endothelial disease, retinal disease, glaucoma, or cataracts; (3) systemic conditions affecting postoperative recovery; (4) irregular astigmatism.

Surgical procedure

For the TICL group: Subjects were seated upright. Following topical anesthesia, the surgeon performed artificial corneal limbal markings at 0° and 180° under a slit-lamp microscope, followed by mydriasis of the operative eye. The subject was then moved to the operating room and positioned supine on the surgical table. The surgeon first used the STAAR astigmatism adjustment chart and a Mendes ring to determine the TICL implantation position. A needle tip from a 1-ml syringe was used to mark the corneal limbus. A 3-mm clear corneal incision was made in the temporal quadrant. Viscoelastic (1% sodium hyaluronate) was instilled to fill the anterior chamber. The lens was gently advanced into the anterior chamber, with its four loops positioned beneath the iris. The toric lens was adjusted to align with the marked position. Finally, the viscoelastic was irrigated and aspirated using balanced salt solution. Postoperatively, the subject was transferred to the recovery room.

For the ICL + FSAK group: Preoperatively, corneal topography data of the operative eye were uploaded to the LENSAR femtosecond laser platform (LENSAR, USA) for surgical navigation. The subject was positioned supine on the operating table, and the operative eye was instilled with 0.4% obucaine hydrochloride eye drops for surface anesthesia. The surgeon verified the FSAK parameters: an optical zone of 8 mm, a flap depth of 90%, and a pair of symmetric arcuate incisions were made on the steep meridian, with a radial arc length based on the subject’s preoperative refraction results (Table 1). The surgical platform positioned the cornea using the topography map and initiated FSAK after successful docking of the suction ring. Figure 1 shows the illustration of the FSAK procedure. Following the procedure, the subject was transferred to another operating room. The surgeon inserted a corneal flap elevator into the base of the FSAK and moved it laterally along the curved incision to open the incision. Subsequently, the surgeon made a 3-mm clear corneal incision in the temporal quadrant, injected viscoelastic (1% sodium hyaluronate) into the anterior chamber, carefully inserted the intraocular lens, and positioned the four lens loops beneath the iris. Finally, the viscoelastic was replaced with balanced salt solution, and the subject was transferred postoperatively to the recovery room.

Outcome measures

All included patients undergo a three-month postoperative follow-up observation.The efficacy and safety of the outcomes were evaluated using the efficacy index and the safety index respectively. The efficacy index (EI) represents the ratio of postoperative UDVA to preoperative CDVA. The safety index (SI) represents the ratio of postoperative CDVA to preoperative CDVA.

Analysis of astigmatic correction

Vector analysis of astigmatism was performed using the Alpins method. Target-induced astigmatism (TIA) denotes the astigmatism vector expected to be corrected by surgery; surgically-induced astigmatism (SIA) refers to the actual astigmatism vector change achieved after surgery. The difference vector (DV) represents the difference between TIA and SIA. The correction index (CI) is the ratio of the actual correction to the target correction, with an ideal value of 1. A CI > 1 indicates overcorrection, while a CI < 1 indicates undercorrection. The angle error (AE) is the angular difference between TIA and SIA.

Statistical analysis

The experimental data were analyzed using SPSS 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality tests were conducted on all data. For normally distributed quantitative data, results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For non-normally distributed quantitative data, results are presented as median (interquartile range). Statistical Analysisncluded data from both eyes of each patient, and the measurements from the two eyes of the same individual are correlated, generalized estimating equations (GEE) were employed to compare baseline characteristics and postoperative outcomes between groups, in order to appropriately account for the inter-eye correlation. For continuous variables, a Gaussian distribution model with an identity link function was used; for binary variables, a binomial distribution model with a logit link function was applied. The results are presented as regression coefficients (β) for continuous outcomes or odds ratios (OR) for binary outcomes, along with their 95% confidence intervals (CI). All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

At 1 day, 1 week, 1 month, and 3 months postoperatively, no statistically significant differences occurred in UDVA or EI between the two groups (P > 0.05) (Table 3). Both groups showed improved UDVA at 3 months compared to preoperative levels (P < 0.001). Safety analysis

At 1 day, 1 week, 1 month, and 3 months postoperatively, no significant differences occurred in CDVA or SI between the two groups (P > 0.05) (Table 3). Both groups demonstrated improved CDVA at 3 months compared to preoperative levels (P = 0.026, P = 0.004). At 3 months postoperatively, 51 eyes (100%) in the ICL + FSAK group and 51 eyes (100%) in the TICL group showed either no change or a 1-line improvement in CDVA (LogMAR) compared to preoperative levels (Fig. 2B).

Predictability analysis

Figure 3 shows the distribution of manifest SE and manifest astigmatism at 3 months postoperatively. At 3 months postoperatively, 92% and 100% of eyes in the ICL + FSAK group had achieved manifest SE within ± 0.50 D and ± 1.00 D of attempted manifest SE, respectively. In comparison, 98% and 100% of eyes in the TICL group had achieved manifest SE within ± 0.50 D and ± 1.00 D, respectively. At 3 months postoperatively, 94% and 100% of eyes in the ICL + FSAK group had manifest astigmatism within ± 0.50 D and ± 1.00 D, respectively. In comparison, 98% and 100% of eyes in the TICL group had manifest astigmatism within ± 0.50 D and ± 1.00 D, respectively. At 3 months postoperatively, no significant difference was observed in manifest SE or manifest astigmatism between groups (P = 0.491, P = 0.774) (Table 3).

The change in manifest SE from 1 day to 3 months postoperatively was 0.25 D (0.13, 0.50) in the ICL + FSAK group and 0.38 D (0.25, 0.63) in the TICL group (P = 0.234). The change in manifest cylinder from 1 day to 3 months postoperatively was 0.25 D (0.00, 0.25) in the ICL + FSAK group and 0.25 D (0.00, 0.25) in the TICL group (P = 0.864). Discussion

FSAK is an established astigmatism correction technique commonly used in cataract surgery, with well-documented efficacy and safety [24–26]. However, no studies have reported its use in combination with ICL implantation for correcting moderate-to-low astigmatism in highly myopic subjects. This study compared ICL implantation combined with FSAK versus TICL implantation in eyes with high myopia and moderate-to-low astigmatism (-0.75 D to -2.00 D). Findings indicated that both approaches demonstrated good efficacy, safety, predictability, and stability within 3 months postoperatively. However, astigmatism vector analysis showed that TICL implantation resulted in greater SIA and CI values compared to ICL + FSAK.

In this study, both UDVA and CDVA improved significantly and stabilized rapidly postoperatively in the TICL group, consistent with previous findings [18, 23, 27]. Similar to studies by Wei et al. [28] and Zhao et al. [29], safety and efficacy indices in the TICL group were ≥ 1. Postoperative CDVA remained unchanged or improved by one line in 100% of eyes compared to preoperative values. Achieved postoperative manifest SE values fell within ± 0.50 D and ± 1.00 D of the attempted correction in 98% and 100% of eyes, respectively. Clinical outcomes of ICL implantation combined with FSAK have not previously been reported. However, Zheng et al. [23] found no significant differences in postoperative efficacy, safety, or stability between ICL implantation combined with manual AK and TICL implantation. Similarly, the current study showed no significant differences in visual acuity and refractive outcomes between the ICL combined with FSAK group and the TICL group. These results suggest that postoperative efficacy, safety, predictability, and stability are comparable between the two methods.

In this study, postoperative astigmatism significantly decreased and rapidly stabilized in both groups compared to preoperative levels. However, vector analysis of postoperative astigmatism revealed greater SIA and CI values in the TICL group compared to the ICL combined with FSAK group. The CI in the TICL group at 3 months postoperatively was 0.84 (0.71, 1.00), similar to the CI values of 0.83 ± 0.13 and 0.834 (0.783, 0.869) reported by Liu et al. [30] and Xie et al. [31], respectively. Previous studies reported CI values ranging from 0.63 to 0.95 for FSAK-corrected astigmatism [32–35]. In this study, the CI at 3 months postoperatively in the ICL combined with FSAK group was 0.75 (0.60, 1.00), similar to previous studies. However, it is lower than in the TICL group. We speculate two factors contributed to the lower CI in the FSAK group. First, most patients in the study presented with with-the-rule (WTR) astigmatism prior to surgery. Zhang et al. [36] demonstrated a positive correlation between the distance from the AK incisions to the corneal limbus and the efficacy of astigmatism correction in FSAK. Given that the cornea typically assumes a vertically oriented elliptical shape with a vertical diameter shorter than the horizontal diameter, FSAK may demonstrate inferior correction efficacy for with-the-rule (WTR) astigmatism compared to against-the-rule (ATR) and oblique astigmatism, even when identical optical zone parameters are employed. This discrepancy is attributed to the fact that FSAK incisions are positioned closer to the limbus in these cases, thereby diminishing the astigmatic correction effect. Deng et al. [37] reported CI values of 0.65 ± 0.31, 1.22 ± 0.52, and 0.80 ± 0.40 for FSAK in WTR, against-the-rule astigmatism (ATR), and oblique astigmatism, respectively, further supporting a lower CI for WTR astigmatism. Second, the discrepancy may also be attributed to differences in the femtosecond laser platforms used and variations in the FSAK parameter settings. Lin et al. [38], Zhong et al. [39], and Baharozian et al. [40] used the Femto LDV Z8 (Ziemer Ophthalmic Systems AG, Port, Switzerland), LenSx (Alcon Laboratories), and Catalys (Johnson & Johnson Vision, Santa Ana, CA, USA) femtosecond laser platforms for FSAK, yielding CI values of 0.80, 0.79, and 0.53, respectively. The CI results reported by Lin et al. and Zhong et al. are comparable to our findings; however, the CI values obtained by Baharozian et al. were significantly lower. In the study by Baharozian et al., FSAK was performed with a 9-mm optical zone, a depth of 80% of corneal thickness, an arc length restricted to only 70% of that recommended by the Donnenfeld limbal relaxing incision (LRI) nomogram, and the incisions were left unopened. In contrast, our study utilized the LENSAR femtosecond laser platform with settings of an 8-mm optical zone, a depth of 90% of corneal thickness, full arc lengths (without intentional reduction), and opened incisions post-creation. We speculate that these combined factors—specifically the laser platform and the divergent surgical parameters—may account for the significant differences observed in the CI values.However, although the ICL+FSAK group exhibited lower SIA and CI values, there was no statistically significant difference in postoperative manifest astigmatism between the two groups in our study; thus, this discrepancy lacked clinical significance. Nevertheless, a review by Zheng et al. [41] comparing FSAK and toric IOLs (TIOLs) in cataract surgery noted that the efficacy of FSAK diminishes as corneal astigmatism increases. Consequently, we hypothesize that in patients with higher degrees of astigmatism, the superior SIA and CI observed in the TICL group might translate into a clinically superior astigmatic correction compared to FSAK.

In this study, the TICL group demonstrated postoperative improvements in MTF cutoff, SR, and OSI compared to preoperative levels, consistent with previous studies [42, 43]. Shen et al. [44] reported no statistically significant differences in total HOAs, MTF-10, MTF-30, or SR between FSAK combined with trifocal IOLs and toric bifocal IOLs, similar to the present study’s FSAK group. At 1 and 3 months postoperatively, no statistically significant differences in MTF cutoff, SR, or OSI occurred between the FSAK and TICL groups. Additionally, no significant differences in postoperative IOP or ECD were observed between the two groups. Although postoperative vault did not differ significantly between groups, both gradually decreased over time, aligning with previous studies [45, 46].

This study had several limitations. First, the non-randomized design of this study is a limitation. Although we minimized the potential for selection bias through consecutive enrollment and an allocation strategy driven by informed patient preference—resulting in well-balanced baseline characteristics with no significant statistical differences—we cannot completely rule out the influence of unmeasured confounders. Future large-scale randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are warranted to further validate these findings. Second, subjects had astigmatism ranging from − 0.75 D to -2.00 D, limiting generalizability. The efficacy of FSAK for moderate-to-high astigmatism requires further study. Third, this was a single-center study using the LENSAR femtosecond laser platform for FSAK; further validation using other platforms is needed. Finally, follow-up was limited to 3 months postoperatively. Although previous studies have demonstrated that the astigmatic correction of FSAK stabilizes as early as 1 to 2 months postoperatively and remains effective for 2 to 5 years [47–49], we believe that long-term follow-up is still necessary.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that combined ICL implantation and FSAK provides comparable efficacy, safety, predictability, and stability to TICL implantation for correcting high myopia with moderate-to-low astigmatism. TICL implantation resulted in higher SIA and a higher astigmatism CI compared to FSAK, but manifest astigmatism postoperatively no difference. Therefore, both methods are excellent choices for high myopia with moderate-to-low astigmatism.
