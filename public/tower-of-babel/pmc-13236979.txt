Contact force identification in pantograph–catenary system via elastic support beam theory and enhanced l1 sparse regularization scheme
Authors: Haifei Wei, Ning Zhou, Xingshuai Zhi, Hongming Chen, Mudi Li, Yao Cheng, Weihua Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate identification of pantograph–catenary contact force remains a challenging inverse problem, particularly under high-speed operating conditions where high-frequency dynamics play a critical role. Most existing contact force identification methods are primarily limited to low-frequency ranges (typically below 20 Hz), which restricts their applicability in analyzing high-frequency phenomena such as contact strip abnormal wear and fatigue crack initiation in pantograph structures. To overcome these limitations, this study proposes a contact force identification framework with extended frequency capability for pantograph–catenary systems. Based on an elastically supported beam representation of the pantograph head with moving supports, this study establishes consistent forward and inverse dynamic models within a modal superposition framework, enabling an improved description of structural flexibility and high-frequency dynamic characteristics. Furthermore, an enhanced sparse inversion scheme is developed by incorporating a redundant cascaded dictionary with moving-average L1 regularization, which effectively improves robustness against noise and enhances the extraction of transient and high-frequency force components. Numerical simulations under various operating conditions demonstrate that the proposed method achieves accurate and stable reconstruction of contact forces over a significantly extended frequency range, outperforming conventional inversion approaches. The proposed framework provides a reliable and high-resolution tool for dynamic analysis of pantograph–catenary interactions and offers promising potential for future real-time monitoring applications.

Introduction

In modern electric multiple units (EMUs), electrical energy input and output are achieved through contact interactions in two subsystems: the roof-mounted pantograph–catenary system and the underframe wheel–rail system, forming the basic electrical energy circulation of the vehicle. Unlike the passive wheel–rail contact maintained by gravity, the pantograph–catenary interface involves an open sliding contact, which is inherently prone to instability during operation1. Typical issues include contact loss and arcing caused by geometric irregularities of the catenary or contact strip2,3, structural degradation such as cracks4,5 and suspension stiffness hardening6 due to long-term service, and aggravated wear resulting from parameter mismatches between the pantograph and catenary7–9. With increasing operating speeds and transportation demands, these phenomena become more severe and pose growing risks to operational safety. As a key indicator of pantograph–catenary coupling dynamics, the contact force has therefore attracted sustained attention10–14, and its accurate measurement remains a central topic in pantograph–catenary condition monitoring.

Contact force measurement methods can be broadly classified into direct and indirect approaches. Direct measurement, which has been widely adopted since the early stage of pantograph–catenary research and is specified in the EN 50,31715, typically installs force sensors between the contact strip and the suspension structure, combined with acceleration-based inertial and aerodynamic corrections16. However, this approach inevitably alters the structural parameters of the pantograph head and requires a 20 Hz low-pass filter, which limits the effective frequency range of the measured contact force. To overcome these limitations, researchers have developed indirect measurement methods based on fiber Bragg grating (FBG) strain sensing systems17–19. By integrating compact FBG-based force measurement modules or placing anti-interference FBG sensors on the underside of the contact strip, the contact force can be estimated through linear transformation models with inertial correction20,21. Temperature compensation strategies were further introduced to mitigate thermal effects, significantly reducing the influence of the measurement system on the pantograph head structure and improving engineering applicability22. In parallel, non-contact image-based methods have also been explored, where pantograph head responses are extracted from visual information and combined with techniques such as Kalman filtering to estimate the contact force23,24. Nevertheless, both contact-based and non-contact approaches are characterized by limited measurable frequency ranges, which restrict their applicability under high-speed operating conditions.

In fact, the frequency-domain limitations of the aforementioned contact force measurement methods originate primarily from the limitations of pantograph–catenary system modeling. Over the past decades, catenary modeling techniques have advanced rapidly in both depth and breadth, evolving from simplified beam–spring25–28 representations to finite element models29–33, absolute nodal coordinate formulations34,35, and, more recently, surrogate models based on neural networks36–38. These approaches describe the modal characteristics of the catenary from different perspectives with progressively improved accuracy. In contrast, pantograph modeling has largely remained at the level of lumped-mass or rigid-body representations. While adequate at low operating speeds, such models restrict the accurate description of pantograph modal characteristics as speed increases. Since contact force measurement along the line based on response information must rely on the pantograph rather than the catenary, an accurate and refined representation of the pantograph—particularly the pantograph head—is essential. Several studies have introduced refined pantograph models for specific operating conditions. Reference39 treated the pantograph head as a flexible component to investigate the influence of nonlinear suspension characteristics on current collection performance. References40,41 developed fully flexible pantograph models, revealing a close relationship between friction-induced self-excited vibrations in the PCS and pantograph structural modes. Furthermore, Reference42 combined experimental data with partially flexible modeling to demonstrate that pantograph structural asymmetry may play an important role in the initiation of frame cracks. Nevertheless, pantograph modeling approaches specifically oriented toward contact force measurement remain largely unexplored. Especially, existing contact force measuring methods lack a physically consistent pantograph model capable of supporting wide-band and high-speed applications.

By observation, the movement of the contact point along the contact strip and catenary during operation is physically analogous to a vehicle traversing a bridge43–45. Inspired by this, the pantograph–catenary interaction process can therefore be described as a moving load acting on a beam-like structure. For the vibration equations of beams, many mature modeling approaches have already been developed46–50, and the recent structure-preserving iteration method51–55, whether for moving beams, rotating beams, or cracked beams. Therefore, for the purpose of contact force identification, taking the pantograph as the analysis object, the problem can be characterized as a process in which the contact force moves back and forth along the contact strip. So far, moving force identification has been widely studied as a fundamental technique for load estimation. The core objective is to reconstruct the unknown input force from measured structural responses and system characteristics, which constitutes an inverse problem of the second kind. In Reference56, a moving load model for a simply supported beam was established based on modal superposition, and load identification was successfully performed using displacement, bending moment, and acceleration responses, demonstrating the feasibility of the approach. However, the resulting inverse problem is often ill-conditioned, which can significantly degrade inversion accuracy and may violate the conditions for existence, uniqueness, or convergence of the solution. To alleviate these issues, numerous regularization techniques have been proposed over the past decades. Nevertheless, no single method is universally applicable. For example, basis function methods57–59 are constrained by the expressiveness of the selected basis and have difficulty capturing complex sparse patterns, while classical Tikhonov regularization60,61 tends to produce overly smooth solutions and struggles to represent sparsity. In contrast, sparse regularization techniques62–65 are more effective in identifying localized features, but often at the expense of global characteristics, such as a stable mean load level, which is essential for engineering evaluation in PCS.

In the field of PCS, inverse methods for contact force identification have rarely been adopted due to the trade-off between engineering practicality and identification complexity. However, with the continuous increase in operating speeds, the demand for wide-band contact force acquisition has become increasingly critical. Motivated by this requirement, the present study proposes a contact force identification framework that integrates refined pantograph modeling with an enhanced sparse inversion strategy. The pantograph head is modeled as an elastically supported beam with moving supports, enabling an accurate representation of structural flexibility and moving-contact dynamics. Based on modal superposition theory, consistent forward and inverse models are established. Furthermore, a moving-average constrained L1 sparse regularization scheme with a redundant cascaded dictionary is developed, which effectively captures transient high-frequency force components while preserving the stability of the global contact force trend.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Sect. 2 derives the pan head model and establishes the direct and inverse problem formulations. Section 3 presents the proposed inverse method for contact force identification. Section 4 validates the model and method through equivalence and simulations. Section 5 discusses the effects of working conditions and sampling configurations on the inversion performance. All variables and their explanations are included in Appendix A1.

Problem formulation for pantograph–catenary contact force identification

This section formulates the contact force identification (CFI) problem based on the load characteristics of the pantograph system. The physical contact process is transformed into an inverse dynamic problem, and analytical formulations of the forward and inverse models are derived using vibration mechanics theory.

Spatial pantograph model and simplification of the pan head

The pantograph–catenary system is a spatially coupled mechanical structure consisting primarily of a pan head and a supporting frame. During operation, the frame provides the required vertical support, while the pan head directly interacts with the contact wire to ensure current collection. Owing to the zigzag arrangement of the catenary, the contact point does not remain stationary but travels back and forth along the contact strip, resulting in a moving excitation acting on the pan head. The overall configuration of the pantograph–catenary system and the local interaction between the pan head and the catenary are illustrated in Figs. 1(a) and (b). The contact strip is connected to the stable arm through suspension components. As the pantograph operates, the moving contact force acting on the strip is transmitted through the suspension to the frame, inducing vibrations throughout the pantograph structure. These vibrations are further fed back to the pan head via the suspension system, leading to coupled dynamic responses, as schematically shown in Fig. 1(c). When focusing on the pan head alone, the upper boundary is primarily subjected to the moving contact force arising from the pantograph–catenary interaction, while the lower boundary is influenced by reaction forces and vibration inputs transmitted from the stable arm.

From a modeling perspective, the pan head can therefore be treated as an elastically supported beam subjected simultaneously to a moving concentrated force and boundary excitations induced by support motion. This abstraction allows the combined effects of contact force variation and support-induced vibration to be incorporated in a unified manner, as depicted in Fig. 1(e). Within this model, the pan head dynamics are governed by both the moving contact force and the motion of the elastic supports, enabling a consistent description of the coupled excitation mechanisms.

Accordingly, the forward problem describes the vibration response of the contact strip under known contact force and support motion, whereas the inverse problem aims to reconstruct the moving contact force from measured acceleration responses and known support excitations. Throughout the identification process, the position of the contact point is assumed to be known, which can be reliably obtained using existing visual measurement techniques66,67.

Fundamental framework of inverse-method-based identification for CFI

As the contact force can be reconstructed from the measured acceleration responses through a direct inversion of the system matrix. In practice, however, this matrix is often severely ill-conditioned. As a result, even a small level of measurement noise may be greatly amplified during the inversion process, leading to unstable solutions and unreliable force reconstruction.

To mitigate this numerical difficulty, an alternative formulation is commonly adopted in which the inverse problem is recast as a constrained optimization problem rather than being solved through explicit matrix inversion. This strategy circumvents the use of poorly conditioned inverse operators and provides improved robustness in the presence of noise. Therefore, Eq. For the regularization parameters w and λL1​, a stepwise approach is adopted to determine each one. First, based on the L-curve criterion75 and using the traditional L1 sparse regularization approach, a single parameter tuning is performed by evaluating the data fit term ||HDF-A||22​ and the L1 sparse regularization term ||F||1, and the L-curve is calculated to obtain λL1​. Then, another regularization parameter w is fine-tuned by evaluating ||HDF-A||22 + λL1||F||1 and ||SDF||22, plotting the L-curve to obtain the optimal regularization parameter w at the inflection point. This process ensures the optimal balance between data fitting and prior regularization.

Since the above equation contains two L2​-norm terms and one L1​-norm term, we can combine the L2​-norm terms into a matrix form to obtain,27\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\tilde {{\boldsymbol{F}}}_{{\mathrm{Mv-D-L1}}}}=\mathop {\arg \hbox{min} }\limits_{{\tilde {F}}} \left\{ {\left\| {\left[ {\begin{array}{*{20}{c}} {{\boldsymbol{H}}{\boldsymbol{D}}} \\ {{\boldsymbol{w}}{\boldsymbol{S}}{\boldsymbol{D}}} \end{array}} \right]\tilde {F} - \left[ {\begin{array}{*{20}{c}} {\boldsymbol{A}} \\ O \end{array}} \right]} \right\|_{2}^{2}+{\lambda _{\mathrm{L}1}}{{\left\| {\tilde {{\boldsymbol{F}}}} \right\|}_1}} \right\}$$\end{document}

Simulation and discussion

This section explores the sensitivity of the proposed CFI method to key modeling and measurement parameters using numerical simulations. The relative identification errors are evaluated to analyze the effects of sensor configuration, moving-support intensity, and the inclusion of support motion on the accuracy of contact force reconstruction.

The influence of measurement points configuration and number on CFI

To investigate the optimal location on the contact strip for CFI, the strip is evenly divided into ten segments, and various combinations of sampling points are selected to obtain acceleration data. Based on the method described in Sect. 3, CFI are performed, and the inversion results are compared with the true value to determine the sampling configuration that yields the best identification performance. Unless otherwise specified, the remaining simulation parameters are the same as those listed in Table 3 and shown in Fig. 3.

To control the influence of noise on the analysis results, white noise is set at 5%. A total of twelve sampling cases are designed, as shown in Fig. 7(a). Among them, cases 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, and 11 are dual-point sampling scenarios (case 11 includes an additional central measurement point, 3 points in total). Due to the symmetric structure of the contact strip, cases 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 are single-point sampling scenarios located on one side of the strip. The relative error distributions of CFI are shown in Fig. 7(b)–(m).

Conclusions

In this study, the contact force identification problem in PCS is investigated by modeling the pan head as an elastic supported beam with support moving. By reformulating the contact force measurement as a moving load identification problem, a novel time-domain identification method based on redundant dictionary feature matching and moving average–constrained L1 sparse regularization is proposed, using only acceleration responses. Numerical simulations are conducted to validate the effectiveness of the proposed approach. The following conclusions can be drawn,

The proposed inversion method, which combines redundant dictionary feature matching with moving average-constrained L1 sparse regularization, demonstrates excellent performance in solving the CFI problem. Even under the influence of measurement noise, the method ensures acceptable accuracy of the force. This indicates good robustness and stability of the proposed approach.

Simulation results show that symmetrically placing acceleration sensors near the one-third positions of the beam yields optimal identification accuracy. However, placement near the beam center yields less satisfactory outcomes.

As the vibration intensity at the support increases, their contribution on acceleration response of the beam becomes more significant. When the support moving signal intensity reaches 40% of the maximum beam acceleration, the RPE remains within 5%. Furthermore, compared to approaches that neglect support moving, the proposed method improves identification accuracy by a factor of 2 to 6.

In summary, this work presents an attempt to reformulate the pantograph–catenary contact force measurement as a moving load identification problem on an elastic supported beam with support moving. However, various nonlinearities exist during pantograph operation, such as degradation of suspension parameters over time and local changes in beam cross-section due to carbon strip wear. These issues are critical in engineering applications and warrant further investigation.
