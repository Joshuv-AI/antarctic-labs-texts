1.
Introduction

We consider an atom with
N
electrons and a nucleus of charge
Z>0
fixed at the origin. The non-relativistic Schrödinger equation for this system is

(1.1)

\displaystyle(-\Delta+V)\psi=E\psi

for
\Delta
the Laplacian on
\mathbb{R}^{3N}
and
V
the Coulomb potential given by

(1.2)

\displaystyle V(\mathbf{x})=-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{Z}{|x_{j}|}+\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq N}\frac{1}{|x_{j}-x_{k}|}

where
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{3N}
is the collection of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
positions of the
N
particles. We consider
\psi
to be a normalized eigenfunction of this equation in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})
, and we impose no symmetry assumptions. All results can be extended to the case of molecules.

We now define the
reduced density matrices
for such
\psi
. Firstly, for each permutation
\sigma
in
S_{N}
, the symmetric group of degree
N
, we set
P_{\sigma}:\mathbb{R}^{3N}\to\mathbb{R}^{3N}
given by

\displaystyle P_{\sigma}(\mathbf{x})=(x_{\sigma(1)},\dots,x_{\sigma(N)}),\quad\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3N}.

Let
K=1,\dots,N-1
. Then, for a given
\sigma\in S_{N}
, we can define the function

\displaystyle\gamma_{\sigma}^{(K)}(\check{\mathbf{x}},\check{\mathbf{y}})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3(N-K)}}\overline{\psi(P_{\sigma}(\check{\mathbf{x}},\hat{\mathbf{x}}))}\psi(P_{\sigma}(\check{\mathbf{y}},\hat{\mathbf{x}}))\,d\hat{\mathbf{x}}

for
\check{\mathbf{x}},\,\check{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3K}
. We set
\gamma^{(K)}=\gamma_{I}^{(K)}
where
I
is the identity in
S_{N}
. Indeed, for regularity concerns the choice of
\sigma
is unimportant so often
\sigma=I
is taken. In fact, for
\psi
totally symmetric or antisymmetric we get
\gamma_{\sigma}^{(K)}=\gamma^{(K)}
for any
\sigma
.

This functions
\gamma_{\sigma}^{(K)}
are well-defined and are in fact Lipschitz continuous due to
\psi
being Lipschitz continuous as shown by Kato,
[
1
]
. These functions are analytic outside of a singular set,
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
. There have also been many recent results concerning the regularity at the singular sets, in particular at the “diagonal”, namely
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
7
]
,
[
8
]
,
[
9
]
.

The
K
-
particle reduced density matrix
is then defined as the integral operator
\mathbb{\Gamma}^{(K)}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3K})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3K})
given by

(1.3)

\displaystyle\big(\mathbb{\Gamma}^{(K)}u\big)(\check{\mathbf{x}})=\frac{1}{(N-K)!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{N}}\big(\Gamma^{(K)}_{\sigma}u\big)(\check{\mathbf{x}}),\quad u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3K}),

where

(1.4)

\displaystyle\big(\Gamma^{(K)}_{\sigma}u\big)(\check{\mathbf{x}})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3K}}\gamma_{\sigma}^{(K)}(\check{\mathbf{x}},\check{\mathbf{y}})u(\check{\mathbf{y}})\,d\check{\mathbf{y}}.

for
\check{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3K}
. Furthermore, we define
\Gamma^{(K)}=\Gamma^{(K)}_{I}
for
I
the identity in
S_{N}
. We note that the factor
1/(N-K)!
appears due to overcounting, since for many
\sigma
the integrals are identical by Fubini’s theorem. If
\psi
is totally symmetric or antisymmetric then formula (
1.3
) may be simplified by replacing the summation by the factor
N!
. The operators
T=\mathbb{\Gamma}^{(K)}
or
\Gamma^{(K)}_{\sigma}
are positive and trace class, and we label their eigenvalues as
\lambda_{n}(T)
, non-increasing counting multiplicity.

In our results, we will require that
\psi
decays exponentially. This is really no restriction at all since it holds true whenever
E
is a discrete eigenvalue, for a detailed discussion see
[
10
]
. More precisely, we consider
\psi
such that there exists
C,\kappa>0
such that

(1.5)

\displaystyle|\psi(\mathbf{x})|\leq Ce^{-\kappa|\mathbf{x}|^{\prime}},\quad\text{where}\quad|\mathbf{x}|^{\prime}=|x_{1}|+\dots+|x_{N}|

for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3N}
. The use of
|\,\cdot\,|^{\prime}
rather than the standard Euclidean norm
|\,\cdot\,|
in
\mathbb{R}^{3N}
is simply for later convenience.

We are interested in the eigenvalue decay of the operators
\Gamma^{(K)}
for
1\leq K\leq N-1
. The first significant result in this direction seems to be
[
11
]
, where it was shown that
\Gamma^{(1)}
has infinite rank for
\psi
a fermionic ground state. Using regularity estimates for
\psi
given by S. Fournais and T.Ø. Sørensen in
[
12
]
, bounds on the eigenvalue decay were then obtained by A.V. Sobolev
[
13
]
to give the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

[
13
, Theorem 1.1]

Let
\psi
be any eigenfunction of (
1.1
) obeying the exponential decay condition (
1.5
). Then there exists
C
such that, for any
n\geq 1
,

(1.6)

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}(\Gamma^{(1)}),\,\lambda_{n}(\Gamma^{(N-1)})\leq Cn^{-8/3}.

In the same manner as described in Remark
1.3
(1) below, the above bounds also immediately hold for
\Gamma_{\sigma}^{(1)},\Gamma_{\sigma}^{(N-1)}
for any
\sigma\in S_{N}
, and also for
\mathbb{\Gamma}^{(1)}
and
\mathbb{\Gamma}^{(N-1)}
. The result for
\Gamma^{(N-1)}
was not specifically mentioned in
[
13
]
, but is an immediate consequence of (
1.13
) below. The results of Theorem
1.1
were extended in
[
14
]
to give asymptotics for
\mathbb{\Gamma}^{(1)}
and
\mathbb{\Gamma}^{(N-1)}
with the same eigenvalue decay rate. See also
[
15
]
for corresponding result for the kinetic energy density matrix.

The following is our main theorem.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\psi
be any eigenfunction of (
1.1
) obeying the exponential decay condition (
1.5
). For each
2\leq K\leq N-2
there exists
C
such that, for all
n\geq 1
,

(1.7)

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}(\Gamma^{(K)})\leq Cn^{-\alpha_{K}},\quad\alpha_{K}=1+\frac{7}{3L}

where
L=\min\{K,N-K\}
.

Remark 1.3
.

(1)

The same eigenvalue bounds hold for
\Gamma_{\sigma}^{(K)}
for any
\sigma\in S_{N}
, since the proof is not sensitive to such a modification. Furthermore, the same bounds hold for
\mathbb{\Gamma}^{(K)}
. Indeed, the condition (
1.7
) is precisely that
\Gamma^{(K)}\in S_{1/\alpha_{K},\,\infty}
and these are vector spaces, see Section
3
.

(2)

\Gamma^{(K)}
is trace class for each
K
. Hence, by (
3.2
) we immediately get the bound (
1.7
) with
\alpha_{K}=1
. We therefore obtain an improvement to this for every
K
.

(3)

We do not believe the above values of
\alpha_{K}
for
2\leq K\leq N-2
are optimal. However, we do not expect these can chosen smaller than the optimal value for
K=1
, namely
\alpha_{1}=8/3
. We provide an argument based on regularity of the kernels
\gamma^{(K)}
, see the introduction for relevant references. As pointed out in
[
6
, Remark 1.2(7)]
the value
\alpha_{1}=8/3
is seen to arise from a fifth order cusp for
\gamma^{(1)}(x_{1},y_{1})
at the diagonal
x_{1}\approx y_{1}
. Consider, for example,
K=2
and take some non-zero
z,z^{\prime}
with
z\neq z^{\prime}
. Then, using the same arguments which demonstrated a fifth order cusp for
\gamma^{(1)}
, we would get that
\gamma^{(2)}(x_{1},z,y_{1},z^{\prime})
has a fifth order cusp around
x_{1}\approx y_{1}
for
x_{1},y_{1}
separated from
0,z
and
z^{\prime}
. Due to the presence of the same type of singularity, we therefore do not expect a stronger decay rate of eigenvalues.

(4)

Eigenvalue bounds (
1.7
) can tell you how well
\Gamma^{(K)}
can be approximated by projections of finite rank. This is of particular interest in the
K=2
case, where one may obtain the eigenvalue of
\psi
from knowledge of
\mathbb{\Gamma}^{(2)}
alone via an explicit formula,
[
16
, (3.1.34)]
.

The proof proceeds by factorizing
\Gamma^{(K)}
as the product
(\Psi^{(K)})^{*}\Psi^{(K)}
for a Hilbert-Schmidt operator
\Psi^{(K)}
, defined later in (
1.12
). It will then suffice to consider bounds to the singular values of
\Psi^{(K)}
. In particular we need inclusion into certain Schatten classes,
S_{p}
or
S_{p,\infty}
, defined in Section
3
.

The kernel of
\Psi^{(K)}
can be factorized appropriately using Jastrow factors, a common technique used in the study of the regularity of wavefunctions. One factor has improved smoothness compared to the original kernel as shown by the enhanced pointwise bounds given in Proposition
2.1
. This can be shown to locally lie in a Besov-Nikol’skii space of good smoothness. Such spaces are introduced in Section
4
. The singular values of the integral operator with this smoothened factor as its kernel can be estimated using the Birman-Solomyak bounds given in
[
17
]
. Finally, to get the kernel of
\Psi^{(K)}
we need to multiply with the remaining factors. Some can be considered “weights” to the Birman-Solomyak bounds, and these do not affect the decay of the singular values. Others will be considered “multipliers” on
S_{p}
or
S_{p,\infty}
. These are functions with the property that they can multiply a kernel of an operator in
S_{p}
or
S_{p,\infty}
, and the resulting operator will lie in the same space.

Notation
. For each
1\leq K\leq N
we can define

(1.8)

\displaystyle\Sigma^{(K)}=\Big\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3K}:\prod_{1\leq j\leq K}|x_{j}|\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq K}|x_{j}-x_{k}|=0\Big\}.

For simplicity, we will denote
\Sigma=\Sigma^{(N)}
which is the singular set of the Coulomb potential,
V
, given in (
1.2
). By elliptic regularity, it’s straightforward to show that if
\psi
is a weak solution to (
1.1
) then
\psi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3N}\backslash\Sigma)
.

Given some
1\leq K\leq N-1
, we will consider
\check{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3K}
and
\hat{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3(N-K)}
whose
\mathbb{R}^{3}
components are labelled as follows,

(1.9)

\displaystyle\check{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{K}),\quad\hat{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{K+1},\dots,x_{N}).

Therefore,
(\check{\mathbf{x}},\hat{\mathbf{x}})=\mathbf{x}
for
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})
an element of
\mathbb{R}^{3N}
.

Now, for any
j\in\{1,\dots,K\}
and
l\in\{K+1,\dots,N\}
we remove a variable from either
\check{\mathbf{x}}
or
\hat{\mathbf{x}}
using the following subscript notation,

(1.10)

\displaystyle\check{\mathbf{x}}_{j}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{j-1},x_{j+1},\dots,x_{K}),\quad\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{l}=(x_{K+1},\dots,x_{l-1},x_{l+1},\dots,x_{N})

with obvious modifications for
j=1
or
K
, and for
l=K+1
or
N
.
We also write

(1.11)

\displaystyle(x_{j},\check{\mathbf{x}}_{j})=\check{\mathbf{x}},\quad(x_{l},\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{l})=\hat{\mathbf{x}}.

In other words, we understand
x
in
(x,\check{\mathbf{x}}_{j})
to be placed as the
j
-th component. Similarly for
(x,\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{l})
.

1.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

We begin by writing the reduced density matrices as products of operators. In the same manner as
[
13
]
, we define the operator
\Psi_{K}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3K})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3(N-K)})
by

(1.12)

\displaystyle(\Psi^{(K)}u)(\hat{\mathbf{x}})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3K}}\psi(\check{\mathbf{x}},\hat{\mathbf{x}})u(\check{\mathbf{x}})\,d\check{\mathbf{x}},\quad u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3K})

for
\hat{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3(N-K)}
. Since
\psi\in L^{2}
, this is readily seen to be a well-defined Hilbert-Schmidt operator.

By straightforward calculations,

\displaystyle\big(\Psi^{(K)}\big)^{*}\Psi^{(K)}=\Gamma^{(K)},\qquad\Psi^{(K)}\big(\Psi^{(K)}\big)^{*}=\overline{\Gamma_{\sigma_{K}}^{(N-K)}}

where
\sigma_{K}
is such that
P_{\sigma_{K}}(\hat{\mathbf{x}},\check{\mathbf{x}})=(\check{\mathbf{x}},\hat{\mathbf{x}})
for any
\check{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3K}
,
\hat{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3(N-K)}
, and the complex conjugation of the operator is that of the kernel. By the definition of singular values we then get

(1.13)

\displaystyle s_{n}\big(\Psi^{(K)}\big)^{2}=\lambda_{n}\big(\Gamma^{(K)}\big)=\lambda_{n}\big(\Gamma_{\sigma_{K}}^{(N-K)}\big),\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

Here we also used that
s_{n}(T^{*})=s_{n}(T)
for compact
T
, see Section
3
, and that
\lambda_{n}(T)=\lambda_{n}(\overline{T})
for a positive compact integral operator.

It therefore sufficies to study the singular values of
\Psi^{(K)}
. The appropriate inclusion into
S_{q,\infty}
spaces, as defined in Section
3
, is given by the following proposition.

Proposition 1.4
.

Let
2\leq K\leq N-2
. Then
\Psi^{(K)}\in S_{q,\infty}
for
1/q=1/2+7/(6K)
.

The proof will be given in Section
6
, but we now use this to prove our main theorem.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

By (
1.13
), Proposition
1.4
, and the definition of
S_{q,\infty}
spaces, see Section
3
, we get some
C
such that

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}\big(\Gamma^{(K)}\big),\,\lambda_{n}\big(\Gamma_{\sigma_{K}}^{(N-K)}\big)\leq Cn^{-(1+7/(3K))}

for all
n\geq 1
. Since we could have taken any permuation of the variables of
\psi
in defining
\Psi^{(K)}
, it follows that we can freely replace
\Gamma_{\sigma_{K}}^{(N-K)}
by
\Gamma^{(N-K)}
in the above bound. This completes the proof.
∎

2.
Factorization of
\psi

It is known that multiplication by a so-called Jastrow factor can improve the smoothness of solutions
\psi
to (
1.1
), see, for example,
[
18
]
,
[
19
]
,
[
12
]
. We follow these authors to define, for
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3N}
,

(2.1)

\displaystyle F(\mathbf{x})=-\frac{Z}{2}\sum_{1\leq j\leq N}\tau(x_{j})+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq N}\tau(x_{j}-x_{k}),

where

(2.2)

\displaystyle\tau(x)=|x|-(1+|x|^{2})^{1/2},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}.

Using this, we set

(2.3)

\displaystyle\phi=e^{-F}\psi.

The function
\phi
lies in
C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{3N})
for every
\alpha\in[0,1)
. In
[
19
]
it was shown that an additional term may be added to
F
in (
2.1
) to get
C^{1,1}
regularity, which is equivalent to the first two derivatives being locally bounded. We’re interested in pointwise bounds to the derivatives of
\phi
. The first result of this kind was from
[
12
]
, and this method was adapted in
[
2
]
and
[
7
]
. We require a slightly different result but rather than go through the method in full, since it is fairly long, we instead obtain the following bounds by using elements of
[
2
]
and applying
[
19
]
. This is presented in Appendix
A
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\kappa
be such that (
1.5
) holds and suppose
\phi
is defined as in (
2.3
). Let
j\in\{1,\dots,N\}
. Then for each
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3}
there exists
C_{m}
, which may depend on
\psi
, such that

(2.4)

\displaystyle|\partial^{m}_{x_{j}}\phi(\mathbf{x})|\leq C_{m}e^{-\kappa|\mathbf{x}|^{\prime}}\Big(1+|x_{j}|^{\min\{2-|m|,\,0\}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq N\\
k\neq j\end{subarray}}|x_{j}-x_{k}|^{\min\{2-|m|,\,0\}}\Big)

for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3N}\backslash\Sigma
.

It will be convenient for us to extract the exponential decay. In particular, define

(2.5)

\displaystyle\mu(\mathbf{x})=\phi(\mathbf{x})\prod_{l=1}^{N}e^{\kappa(1+|x_{l}|^{2})^{1/2}}.

Using
m=0
in (
2.4
) we find that

\displaystyle|\mu(\mathbf{x})|\leq 3C_{0}\prod_{l=1}^{N}e^{-\kappa\tau(x_{l})}

which is bounded in
\mathbb{R}^{3N}
since
\tau\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
. Let
j\in\{1,\dots,N\}
. Then we can continue, by straightforward calculations, to obtain, for any
m\in\mathbb{N}^{3}_{0}
, some
C^{\prime}_{m}
such that

(2.6)

\displaystyle|\partial^{m}_{x_{j}}\mu(\mathbf{x})|\leq C^{\prime}_{m}\Big(1+|x_{j}|^{\min\{2-|m|,\,0\}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq N\\
k\neq j\end{subarray}}|x_{j}-x_{k}|^{\min\{2-|m|,\,0\}}\Big)

for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3N}\backslash\Sigma
.

We now proceed to find an appropriate factorisation of
\psi
. For every
1\leq K\leq N-1
we will use the same
\mu
which will be the part of
\psi
with the greatest smoothness. This is what will determine the singular value estimates for
\Psi^{(K)}
, defined in (
1.12
), at least for
K\geq 2
. The remaining factors are partitioned based on
K
in the following manner.

Let
1\leq K\leq N-1
. As in (
1.9
), we write
\check{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{K})
,
\hat{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{K+1},\dots,x_{N})
. Then we set

(2.7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\check{\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle=\exp\Big(-\frac{Z}{2}\sum_{l=1}^{K}\tau(x_{l})+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{l=1}^{K-1}\sum_{m=l+1}^{K}\tau(x_{l}-x_{m})\Big)\prod_{l=1}^{K}e^{-\kappa(1+|x_{l}|^{2})^{1/2}},

(2.8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(\hat{\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle=\exp\Big(-\frac{Z}{2}\sum_{l=K+1}^{N}\tau(x_{l})+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{l=K+1}^{N-1}\sum_{m=l+1}^{N}\tau(x_{l}-x_{m})\Big)\prod_{l=K+1}^{N}e^{-\kappa(1+|x_{l}|^{2})^{1/2}},

(2.9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\check{\mathbf{x}},\hat{\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle=\exp\Big(\frac{1}{4}\sum_{l=1}^{K}\sum_{m=K+1}^{N}\tau(x_{l}-x_{m})\Big).

By (
2.3
) and (
2.5
) we then obtain

(2.10)

\displaystyle\psi(\check{\mathbf{x}},\hat{\mathbf{x}})=\mathcal{A}(\check{\mathbf{x}})\mathcal{B}(\hat{\mathbf{x}})\mathcal{C}(\check{\mathbf{x}},\hat{\mathbf{x}})\mu(\check{\mathbf{x}},\hat{\mathbf{x}}).

With this decomposition of the kernel of
\Psi^{(K)}
we will apply the Birman-Solomyak bounds of singular values, Proposition
3.2
, applied to the kernel
\mu
with weights
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
. The function
\mathcal{C}
will be seen to act as a “multiplier” in the sense of Section
5
, and will not affect the obtained singular value bounds.

3.
Schatten classes and integral operators

We now introduce singular values and Schatten classes of compact operators, for more details see
[
20
, Chapter 11]
. Let
\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}
be separable Hilbert spaces and
T:\mathcal{H}_{1}\to\mathcal{H}_{2}
a compact operator. The singular values of
T
are
s_{n}(T)=\lambda_{n}(T^{*}T)^{1/2}
,
n\geq 1
, where
\lambda_{n}(T^{*}T)
are the non-zero eigenvalues of
T^{*}T
arranged non-increasing and counting multiplicity. By the Schmidt representation of a compact operator we get
s_{n}(T^{*})=s_{n}(T)
for all
n
. For
0<q<\infty
, the
q
-th Schatten class
is the space
S_{q}=S_{q}(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2})
defined as all compact
T
such that the quantity

\displaystyle\left\lVert T\right\rVert_{q}=\Big(\sum_{n}s_{n}(T)^{q}\Big)^{1/q}

is finite. This functional defines a norm on
S_{q}
for
q\geq 1
and in this case the spaces are Banach. For
0<q<\infty
we can also define the space
S_{q,\infty}=S_{q,\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2})
as all compact
T
for which
s_{n}(T)=O(n^{-1/q})
as
n\to\infty
. On such spaces we can define the functional

(3.1)

\displaystyle\left\lVert T\right\rVert_{q,\infty}=\sup_{n}n^{1/q}s_{n}(T).

We now state some inclusions. Suppose
T\in S_{p}
, then
T\in S_{p,\infty}
with

(3.2)

\displaystyle\left\lVert T\right\rVert_{p,\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert T\right\rVert_{p},

and for any
q>p
we have
T\in S_{q}
with

(3.3)

\displaystyle\left\lVert T\right\rVert_{q}\leq\left\lVert T\right\rVert_{p}.

Furthermore, if
T\in S_{p,\infty}
then for any
q>p
we have
T\in S_{q}
and

(3.4)

\displaystyle\left\lVert T\right\rVert_{q}\leq C(q,p)\left\lVert T\right\rVert_{p,\infty}.

We use the following triangle-type inequality which will be sufficient for our purposes. For more details see
[
20
, Chapter 11]
,
[
17
, Chapter 1]
and
[
21
]
.

Proposition 3.1
.

[
17
, Lemma 1.1]

Let
q\in(0,2)
and let
T_{j}\in S_{q,\infty}
,
j=1,\dots
, be a family of operators satisfying either
T_{j}^{*}T_{k}=0
for all
j\neq k
, or
T_{j}T^{*}_{k}=0
for all
j\neq k
.
Then
T=\sum_{j}T_{j}\in S_{q,\infty}
and

\displaystyle\left\lVert T\right\rVert^{q}_{q,\infty}\leq\frac{2}{2-q}\sum_{j}\left\lVert T_{j}\right\rVert^{q}_{q,\infty},

under the assumption the right-hand side is finite.

3.1.
Integral operators.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Let
T:Y\times X\to\mathbb{C}
,
b:Y\to\mathbb{C}
and
a:X\to\mathbb{C}
. Then, formally we define
\textnormal{Int}(bTa):L^{2}(X)\to L^{2}(Y)
as

(3.5)

\displaystyle\textnormal{Int}(bTa)u(y)=\int_{X}b(y)T(y,x)a(x)u(x)\,dx.

If
a=b\equiv 1
we will simply write
\textnormal{Int}(T)
. It is well known that if
T\in L^{2}(Y\times X)
then
\textnormal{Int}(T)\in S_{2}
. By imposing a smoothness condition on
T
, one may then obtain inclusion in the more refined spaces
S_{q,\infty}
for
q<2
. This is seen in the following proposition, due to M.S. Birman and M.Z. Solomyak. The statement was obtained from
[
17
, Corollaries 4.1 and 4.5, Theorems 4.4 and 4.7]
. It involves the Besov-Nikol’skii spaces,
N_{2}^{s}
, introduced in the next section.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain. Assume that the kernel
T(y,x)
,
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
x\in X
, is such that
T(y,\,\cdot\,)\in N^{s}_{2}(X)
with some
s>0
, for a.e.
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Assume that
b\in L^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
and that
a\in L^{r}(X)
, where

\displaystyle\begin{cases}r=2,&\text{if }2s>d\\
r>d/s,&\text{if }2s\leq d.\end{cases}

Then
\textnormal{Int}(bTa):L^{2}(X)\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
lies in
S_{q,\infty}
where
1/q=1/2+s/d
and

\displaystyle\left\lVert\textnormal{Int}(bTa)\right\rVert_{q,\infty}\leq C\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left\lVert T(y,\,\cdot\,)\right\rVert^{2}_{N^{s}_{2}(X)}|b(y)|^{2}\,dy\Big)^{1/2}\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{L^{r}(X)}

under the assumption the right-hand side is finite.

4.
Besov-Nikol’skii spaces

Take a function
u\in L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
1\leq q\leq\infty
, and let
h\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then define the
finite difference of order 1
as

\displaystyle\Delta_{h}^{(1)}u(x)=u(x+h)-u(x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

For
l=1,2,\dots
we then define the
finite difference of order
l
, denoted by
\Delta_{h}^{(l)}
, as
l
successive applications of
\Delta_{h}^{(1)}
. This produces the formula

\displaystyle\Delta_{h}^{(l)}u(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{l}(-1)^{j+l}{l\choose j}u(x+jh).

Additionally, we take
\Delta_{h}^{(0)}
as the identity.

Since
\left\lVert\Delta^{(1)}_{h}u\right\rVert_{q}\leq 2\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{q}
, it is immediate that

(4.1)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\Delta^{(k)}_{h}u\right\rVert_{q}=\left\lVert\Delta^{(k-l)}_{h}\Delta^{(l)}_{h}u\right\rVert_{q}\leq 2^{k-l}\left\lVert\Delta^{(l)}_{h}u\right\rVert_{q}

for integers
k>l
.

We now introduce the
Besov-Nikol’skii
spaces
N^{s}_{q}
, which exist within the spectrum of Besov spaces, and in this setting are commonly labelled
B^{s}_{q,\infty}
. Further details on these spaces may be found in
[
17
]
,
[
22
]
,
[
23
]
,
[
24
]
. Let
s>0
. A function
u
is said to lie in
N^{s}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
1\leq q\leq\infty
, if
u\in L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and there is some integer
l>s
such that

\displaystyle[u]_{s,l}:=\sup_{h\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\,h\neq 0}|h|^{-s}\left\lVert\Delta^{(l)}_{h}u\right\rVert_{q}<\infty.

In fact, by the following lemma this shows
[u]_{s,l}
will be finite for all integers
l>s
. A short proof can be found in
[
23
, Lemma 1.1]
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
u\in L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
1\leq q\leq\infty
, and suppose
[u]_{s,l}<\infty
for some integer
l>s
. Then for any other integer
m>s
we have
C
, depending only on
s,l
and
m
, such that

(4.2)

\displaystyle[u]_{s,m}\leq C[u]_{s,l}.

The spaces
N^{s}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
admit the norm

(4.3)

\displaystyle\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{N^{s}_{q}}=\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{q}}+[u]_{s,l}

for any choice of integer
l>s
, and these produce equivalent norms by the preceeding lemma. It’s often convenient to take
l=[s]+1
.

For an open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define the space
N^{s}_{q}(\Omega)
as all functions
u\in L^{q}(\Omega)
such that the following quantity is finite

(4.4)

\displaystyle\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{N^{s}_{q}(\Omega)}=\inf\left\lVert\tilde{u}\right\rVert_{N^{s}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

where the infimum is over functions
\tilde{u}\in N^{s}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
u=\tilde{u}
a.e. on
\Omega
. Other characterizations of such spaces on domains exist, but this will suffice for our purposes.

The following lemma is
[
6
, Lemma 3.1]
. This result gives a condition for a function to lie in
N^{s}_{q}
-spaces which will be convenient for our purposes.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
d\geq 2
,
1\leq q\leq d
and let
X=\{a_{j}\}_{j=1}^{M}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be some collection of
M
points. For a given
\alpha>-d/q
, we set
s=\alpha+d/q
. Suppose for some
A>0
the function
u\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash X)
obeys

\displaystyle|\nabla^{k}u(x)|\leq A\Big(1+\sum_{j=1}^{M}|x-a_{j}|^{\min\{\alpha-k,\,0\}}\Big),\quad k=0,1,\dots,[s]+1

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash X
. Then for any
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
R>0
we have
u\in N^{s}_{q}(B(z,R))
and
\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{N^{s}_{q}(B(z,R))}\leq CA
for some
C
possibly depending on
M
and
R
, but not
z
or
X
(for fixed
M
).

The following lemma gives a criteria for inclusion into
N^{s}_{q}
-spaces for functions whose restrictions to lower dimensional spaces have appropriate
N^{s}_{q}
-smoothness. It is elementary, but we have not managed to find it in the literature. It will play a vital part in the proof of our main result, Theorem
1.2
, since for particles in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, Lemma
4.2
alone would only be able to prove
N^{s}_{q}
inclusion on subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and not
\mathbb{R}^{3K}
in general, which we will require.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
d_{1},d_{2}\geq 1
and
d=d_{1}+d_{2}
. Suppose
u(\,\cdot\,,x_{2})\in N_{q}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}})
and
u(x_{1},\,\cdot\,)\in N_{q}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}})
for a.e.
x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
,
x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
, and that there is some
A>0
such that

(4.5)

\displaystyle\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}}\left\lVert u(x_{1},\,\cdot\,)\right\rVert^{q}_{N^{s}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}})}\,dx_{1}\Big)^{1/q}+\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}}\left\lVert u(\,\cdot\,,x_{2})\right\rVert^{q}_{N^{s}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}})}\,dx_{2}\Big)^{1/q}\leq A,

with the appropriate modification for
q=\infty
. Then
u\in N^{s}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{N^{s}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq CA
. Here,
C
is independent of
A
.

Remark 4.4
.

It is interesting to consider the opposite problem, that is, given a general
u\in N^{s}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, is it true that
u(\,\cdot\,,x_{2})\in N^{s}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}})
for a.e.
x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
? This is, in fact, false in general, see
[
25
]
and
[
26
]
.

Proof.

By the definition of the
N^{s}_{q}
-norm, we can use either term on the left-hand side of (
4.5
) to immediately show that
\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq A
. It remains to consider the quantity
[u]_{s,l}
for an appropriate
l
.

Take any
\mathbf{h}=(h_{1},h_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
and set
\mathbf{h}_{1}=(h_{1},0)
and
\mathbf{h}_{2}=(0,h_{2})
. Then a simple calculation shows

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathbf{h}}^{(1)}u(x_{1},x_{2})=\Delta_{\mathbf{h}_{1}}^{(1)}u(x_{1},x_{2})+\Delta_{\mathbf{h}_{2}}^{(1)}u(x_{1}+h_{1},x_{2}),

and hence by iteration,

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathbf{h}}^{(l)}u(x_{1},x_{2})=\sum_{k=0}^{l}{l\choose k}\Delta_{\mathbf{h}_{1}}^{(l-k)}\Delta_{\mathbf{h}_{2}}^{(k)}u(x_{1}+kh_{1},x_{2}).

Let
r=[s]+1
. Using the above formula for
l=2r
we get

\displaystyle\left\lVert\Delta_{\mathbf{h}}^{(2r)}u\right\rVert_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C\sum_{k=0}^{2r}\left\lVert\Delta_{\mathbf{h}_{1}}^{(2r-k)}\Delta_{\mathbf{h}_{2}}^{(k)}u\right\rVert_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

We choose finite differences of order
2r
so that in each term of the above sum, at least one of
\Delta_{\mathbf{h}_{1}}
and
\Delta_{\mathbf{h}_{2}}
is of order at least
r
. Without loss, we therefore consider terms where
k\geq r
. Terms with
k<r
are treated in a similar way, but the roles of
\mathbf{h}_{1}
and
\mathbf{h}_{2}
are interchanged. Using (
4.1
), we remove the finite difference involving
\mathbf{h}_{1}
and reduce the order of
\Delta_{\mathbf{h}_{2}}
by
k-r
,

\displaystyle\left\lVert\Delta_{\mathbf{h}_{1}}^{(2r-k)}\Delta_{\mathbf{h}_{2}}^{(k)}u\right\rVert_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq 2^{r}\left\lVert\Delta_{\mathbf{h}_{2}}^{(r)}u\right\rVert_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

Suppose
q<\infty
. Then, for
u_{x_{1}}=u(x_{1},\,\cdot\,)
defined on
\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
we have

\displaystyle|\mathbf{h}|^{-qs}\left\lVert\Delta_{\mathbf{h}_{2}}^{(r)}u\right\rVert^{q}_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq|h_{2}|^{-qs}\int\left\lVert\Delta_{h_{2}}^{(r)}u_{x_{1}}\right\rVert^{q}_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}})}\,dx_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\int[u_{x_{1}}]^{q}_{s,r}\,dx_{1}\leq A^{q}.

by (
4.5
). Similarly, for
q=\infty
we get

\displaystyle|\mathbf{h}|^{-s}\left\lVert\Delta_{\mathbf{h}_{2}}^{(r)}u\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq A.

Therefore, for some
C
,

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\,\mathbf{h}\neq 0}|\mathbf{h}|^{-s}\left\lVert\Delta_{\mathbf{h}}^{(2r)}u\right\rVert_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq CA,

which completes the proof in view of Lemma
4.1
.
∎

We now apply this result to the type of functions we’re interested in. Suppose that
u\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3N}\backslash\Sigma)
. Given
\alpha>-3/2
, let
s=\alpha+3/2
. In the following,
|\,\cdot\,|
is understood in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Let
A>0
be such that for each
j=1,\dots,N
and each
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{3}
,
|m|\leq[s]+1
,

(4.6)

\displaystyle|\partial^{m}_{x_{j}}u(\mathbf{x})|\leq A\Big(1+|x_{j}|^{\min\{\alpha-|m|,\,0\}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq N\\
k\neq j\end{subarray}}|x_{j}-x_{k}|^{\min\{\alpha-|m|,\,0\}}\Big)

for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3N}\backslash\Sigma
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\alpha>-3/2
and
s=\alpha+3/2
. Suppose
u\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3N}\backslash\Sigma)
obeys (
4.6
) for some
A>0
. Let
1\leq K\leq N
, and
X_{1},\dots,X_{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be open and bounded sets. Then there exists
C
such that

\displaystyle\left\lVert u(\,\cdot\,,\hat{\mathbf{x}})\right\rVert_{N^{s}_{2}(X_{1}\times\dots\times X_{K})}\leq CA,\quad\text{for all}\quad\hat{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3(N-K)}\backslash\Sigma^{(N-K)}

The constant
C
is dependent on the sets
X_{l}
only via
\diam(X_{l})
,
1\leq l\leq K
.

For
K=N
, the function
u(\,\cdot\,,\hat{\mathbf{x}})
should be understood simply as
u
. We use the standard definition of
\diam(X)=\sup\{|x-y|:x,y\in X\}
, for a set
X
.

Proof.

We fix some
\hat{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3(N-K)}\backslash\Sigma^{(N-K)}
throughout, whose components we label as
\hat{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{K+1},\dots,x_{N})
. Using this, we define the function
u_{\hat{\mathbf{x}}}
on
\mathbb{R}^{3K}
by
u_{\hat{\mathbf{x}}}(\check{\mathbf{x}})=u(\check{\mathbf{x}},\hat{\mathbf{x}})
for all
\check{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{K})\in\mathbb{R}^{3K}
.

We begin by localizing the function
u_{\hat{\mathbf{x}}}
on
X_{1}\times\dots\times X_{K}
. Firstly, let
\chi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
obey
0\leq\chi\leq 1
and be such that
\chi(x)=1
for
|x|\leq 1
and
\chi(x)=0
for
|x|\geq 2
. For each
j=1,\dots,K
we define
\chi_{j}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
as follows. Take some
z_{j}\in X_{j}
and set
d_{j}=\textnormal{diam}(X_{j})
, whereby
X_{j}\subset B(z_{j},d_{j})
. We then define
\chi_{j}(x)=\chi(|x-z_{j}|/d_{j})
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. This function obeys
\chi_{j}=1
on
B(z_{j},d_{j})
and
\chi_{j}=0
on
\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash B(z_{j},2d_{j})
.

Now, let

(4.7)

\displaystyle v(\check{\mathbf{x}})=\chi_{1}(x_{1})\dots\chi_{K}(x_{K})u_{\hat{\mathbf{x}}}(\check{\mathbf{x}})

for
\check{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3K}
. Then, by (
4.6
) we have some constant
C
, independent of
A
and the choice of
\hat{\mathbf{x}}
, such that for all
j=1,\dots,K
and
|m|\leq[s]+1
,

(4.8)

\displaystyle|\partial^{m}_{x_{j}}v(\check{\mathbf{x}})|\leq CA\Big(1+|x_{j}|^{\min\{\alpha-|m|,\,0\}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq N\\
k\neq j\end{subarray}}|x_{j}-x_{k}|^{\min\{\alpha-|m|,\,0\}}\Big)

for
\check{\mathbf{x}}
such that
(\check{\mathbf{x}},\hat{\mathbf{x}})\in\mathbb{R}^{3N}\backslash\Sigma
.

We now take some
1\leq j\leq K
and fix some
\check{\mathbf{x}}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{3(K-1)}
such that
(\check{\mathbf{x}}_{j},\hat{\mathbf{x}})\in\mathbb{R}^{3(N-1)}\backslash\Sigma^{(N-1)}
. Then we can define
v_{\check{\mathbf{x}}_{j}}
by
v_{\check{\mathbf{x}}_{j}}(x)=v(x,\check{\mathbf{x}}_{j})
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, where we used the notation (
1.11
). The inequality (
4.8
) becomes, with the same
C
,

(4.9)

\displaystyle|\partial_{x}^{m}v_{\check{\mathbf{x}}_{j}}(x)|\leq CA\Big(1+|x|^{\min\{\alpha-|m|,\,0\}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq N\\
k\neq j\end{subarray}}|x-x_{k}|^{\min\{\alpha-|m|,\,0\}}\Big)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash\{0,x_{1},\dots,x_{j-1},x_{j+1},\dots,x_{N}\}
, with obvious modification if
j=1
. Hence, by Lemma
4.2
with
q=2
and, for example,
z=z_{j}
and
R=2d_{j}
, we obtain
v(\,\cdot\,,\check{\mathbf{x}}_{j})\in N^{s}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
and
\left\lVert v(\,\cdot\,,\check{\mathbf{x}}_{j})\right\rVert_{N^{s}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\leq CA
for some new
C
. Here we used that
v(\,\cdot\,,\check{\mathbf{x}}_{j})
has support in
B(z_{j},2d_{j})
.

Since this holds for each
j=1,\dots,K
we can apply Lemma
4.3
, successively
K-1
times, to get
v\in N^{s}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3K})
and
\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{N^{s}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3K})}\leq CA
, for some new constant
C
. The required result then follows

\displaystyle\left\lVert u(\,\cdot\,,\hat{\mathbf{x}})\right\rVert_{N^{s}_{2}(X_{1}\times\dots\times X_{K})}\leq\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{N^{s}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3K})},

which itself comes from (
4.7
) and (
4.4
). Finally, we note that the constants have been independent of the choice of
\hat{\mathbf{x}}
.

∎

5.
Multipliers

Let
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
Y\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
. In this section we will be concerned with the spaces
S_{q}=S_{q}(L^{2}(X),L^{2}(Y))
and
S_{q,\infty}=S_{q,\infty}(L^{2}(X),L^{2}(Y))
for
0<q\leq 2
. Since
S_{q}\subset S_{2}
, each operator in the former has an integral operator representation in
L^{2}(Y\times X)
. This is clearly also true for
S_{q,\infty}
when
q<2
. We will use the same symbol
T
for both the operator and its kernel, and we will use the notation
\textnormal{Int}(bTa)
and
\textnormal{Int}(T)
introduced in (
3.5
).

Let
\Lambda:Y\times X\to\mathbb{C}
be a function. We call
\Lambda
a “multiplier” on
S_{q}
if
\textnormal{Int}(\Lambda T)

\big(=\textnormal{Int}(\Lambda(y,x)T(y,x))\big)\in S_{q}
for each
T\in S_{q}
and, in addition,

\displaystyle M_{q}(\Lambda):=\sup_{0\neq T\in S_{q}}\frac{\left\lVert\textnormal{Int}(\Lambda T)\right\rVert_{q}}{\left\lVert T\right\rVert_{q}}<\infty.

We define multipliers on
S_{q,\infty}
in the same way, but replacing
S_{q}
by
S_{q,\infty}
and using the functional
\left\lVert\,\cdot\,\right\rVert_{q,\infty}
. Due to the characterization of
S_{2}
as
L^{2}(Y\times X)
, it can be shown that the set of multipliers on
S_{2}
is exactly
L^{\infty}(Y\times X)
.

The following proposition is from
[
17
, Corollary 8.2]
. By the preceeding remark, one consequence of this result is that any multiplier on
S_{q}
for
q<2
will lie in
L^{\infty}(Y\times X)
.

Proposition 5.1
.

If
\Lambda
is a multiplier on
S_{q}
for
q<2
, then it is also a multiplier on
S_{p}
and
S_{p,\infty}
for all
p\in(q,2]
. Furthermore,

M_{p}(\Lambda)+M_{p,\infty}(\Lambda)\leq CM_{q}(\Lambda)
.

The following result is from
[
17
, Theorems 8.1 and 8.2]
.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
0<q\leq 1
. Then
\Lambda\in L^{\infty}(Y\times X)
is a multiplier on
S_{q}
if and only if
\textnormal{Int}(b\Lambda a)\in S_{q}
for all
a\in L^{2}(X)
and
b\in L^{2}(Y)
, and

\displaystyle\sup_{\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{L^{2}(X)}=\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{L^{2}(Y)}=1}\left\lVert\textnormal{Int}(b\Lambda a)\right\rVert_{q}<\infty

In which case, this quantity concides with
M_{q}(\Lambda)
.

The next lemma uses the previous two propositions and concerns multipliers which depend on fewer variables than the underlying space.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
X_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain and
X_{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
be arbitrary. Let
\omega\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{m}\times X_{1})
be such that for some
2s>d_{1}
we have
\omega(y,\,\cdot\,)\in N^{s}_{2}(X_{1})
for a.e.
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
y\mapsto\left\lVert\omega(y,\,\cdot\,)\right\rVert_{N^{s}_{2}(X_{1})}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
. Then
\Omega(y,x_{1},x_{2}):=\omega(y,x_{1})
is a multiplier in
S_{q}(L^{2}(X_{1}\times X_{2}),\,L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m}))
and
S_{q,\infty}(L^{2}(X_{1}\times X_{2}),\,L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m}))
for all
q\in(q_{0},2)
, where
1/q_{0}=1/2+s/d_{1}
. Furthermore, there exists
C
such that

(5.1)

\displaystyle M_{q}(\Omega)+M_{q,\infty}(\Omega)\leq C\esssup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left\lVert\omega(y,\,\cdot\,)\right\rVert_{N^{s}_{2}(X_{1})}.

The constant
C
is independent of
\omega
and
X_{2}
but dependent on
q,s,d_{1}
and
X_{1}
, albeit independent of translations thereof.

The utility of this lemma can be demonstrated in an example. We use the same quantities as in the lemma. Suppose
X_{2}
is bounded. It is immediate from the definition of the Besov-Nikol’skii spaces that for a.e.
y
we have
\Omega(y,\,\cdot\,)\in N^{s}_{2}(X_{1}\times X_{2})
for the same
s
. Now, if we used Propostion
3.2
directly on
\textnormal{Int}(b\Omega a):L^{2}(X_{1}\times X_{2})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
, for some appropriate
a
and
b
, we’d get inclusion in
S_{q,\infty}
for
1/q=1/2+s(d_{1}+d_{2})^{-1}
. But
\Omega
doesn’t depend on
x_{2}
, so we really should expect the value
1/q=1/2+s/d_{1}
, even if we still consider this as an operator on
L^{2}(X_{1}\times X_{2})
. This is what is shown here, at least in the case of multipliers.

Proof.

By Proposition
5.1
, it suffices to prove
\Omega
is a multiplier on
S_{q}
for each
q>q_{0}
sufficiently small. Therefore, take some
q\in(q_{0},1)
. By Proposition
5.2
we must therefore show there exists
C
, independent of
\omega
, such that

(5.2)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\textnormal{Int}(b\Omega a)\right\rVert_{q}\leq C\esssup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left\lVert\omega(y,\,\cdot\,)\right\rVert_{N^{s}_{2}(X_{1})}

for all
a\in L^{2}(X_{1}\times X_{2})
,
b\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
with
\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{L^{2}(X_{1}\times X_{2})}=\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})}=1
.

Let
a
,
b
be as above. We claim that

(5.3)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\textnormal{Int}(b\Omega a)\right\rVert_{S_{q}(L^{2}(X_{1}\times X_{2}),\,L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m}))}=\left\lVert\textnormal{Int}(b\omega\tilde{a})\right\rVert_{S_{q}(L^{2}(X_{1}),\,L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m}))}

where
\tilde{a}(x_{1})=\left\lVert a(x_{1},\,\cdot\,)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(X_{2})}
. Clearly, then,

A.1.
