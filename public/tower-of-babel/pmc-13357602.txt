Two types of sheathed archaellum structures from Haloarcula marismortui differ in their outer layer architectures
Authors: Vladimir A Meshcheryakov, Jaekyung Hyun, Satoshi Shibata, Alexey S Syutkin, Mikhail G Pyatibratov, Matthias Wolf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMany archaea swim by means of rotating helical filaments called archaella. Most archaella are about 10 nm in diameter and comprise multiple copies of the protein archaellin. Here, we describe two archaellum structures formed by the ArlA2 or ArlB archaellins from the haloarchaeon Haloarcula marismortui. We found that both filaments have an additional proteinaceous outer sheath surrounding their inner core, a feature not observed previously in archaea. The outer sheath structures of the two filaments differ fundamentally. The outer domain of ArlB archaellin rotates by 180°, forming stable dimers that likely increase the filament rigidity. In contrast, neither rotation nor dimerization of the outer domain was observed in ArlA2 filaments. 3D variability analysis demonstrated that the motions of ArlA2 and ArlB filaments are significantly distinct. Additionally, the ArlB filament displays a larger negatively charged surface area than ArlA2, which may help adaptation to higher salt concentrations. Our structural findings provide insight into how the two filaments can adapt to their different environments. Larger archaellins with additional outer domains can be found in various groups of archaea. We propose that such proteins may allow hosts to modify the properties of their archaella to enhance environmental adaptation.Subject terms: Archaeal biology, Cryoelectron microscopy

Introduction

Motility is a crucial feature of living organisms, enabling them to escape danger or move toward a food source for more favorable growth conditions. Archaea move by rotating a supercoiled helical filament called the archaellum (also known as “archaeal flagellum”). This is similar to the bacterial flagellum, which also relies on a membrane-anchored motor to rotate the extracellular helical filament and cause cell movement. However, the protein composition and structural organization of the archaellum differ significantly from those of the bacterial flagellum1. Archaellins are the major structural subunits of the archaeal filament. So far, archaeal filament structures have been determined for several archaea, including the haloarchaeon Halobacterium salinarum2–9. It has been shown that glycosylation of archaellin proteins containing N-linked tetrasaccharides significantly affects the structural and functional properties of the Hbt. salinarum archaella and is necessary for normal motility7.

The haloarchaeon Haloarcula marismortui lives in the Dead Sea, where the temperature and salinity in the upper layers vary significantly depending on the season. The salt content of the sea can range from approximately 200 to 350 grams per liter, depending on the amount of freshwater input10. The temperature can also fluctuate greatly, ranging from 10 °C in winter to 50 °C in summer11. Har. marismortui has four genes in its genome that encode archaellins: arlA1, arlA2, arlB, and arlB212. The two archaellins, ArlA2 and ArlB, were found to form the archaellum filament13. The arlB (rrnAC2198, Q5V0B8) is encoded on the main circular chromosome I and is adjacent to other arl genes that encode archaellum motor components. The arlA2 gene (pNG1026, Q5V881) is located on the pNG100 plasmid ( ~ 33 kbp). The function of the other two archaellin genes, arlB2 (E6P14_13040, A0A4P8JZY9) and arlA1 (rrnB0018, Q5UWX1), is unknown. arlB2 is located next to arlB on chromosome I but is transcribed from the opposite DNA strand. The arlA1 encodes a truncated archaellin without a leader peptide required to participate in archaellum assembly and is located on chromosome II. The isolation of a strain of Har. marismortui that had lost the pNG100 plasmid allowed for a detailed characterization of the various archaellins13,14.

Wild-type archaella from Har. marismortui are mainly composed of ArlA2 archaellin, with a small amount of ArlB. In contrast, archaella from the ΔpNG100 strain are composed exclusively of ArlB. This ΔpNG100 strain can spontaneously form from the wild-type strain under starvation conditions due to the loss of the pNG100 plasmid14. Both ArlB and ArlA2 have been shown to form functional archaella individually13–15. Previously, it was demonstrated that ArlA2 and ArlB from Har. marismortui are ecoparalogs, which means they perform the same function but differ in their adaptive properties in different environments14. One of these archaellins, ArlB, is better adapted to higher salinity, while the other, ArlA2, is better suited to lower ionic strength and to elevated temperatures13,14. Furthermore, the observed phenomenon of the synthesis of ArlA2 or ArlB archaella in Har. marismortui is similar to the well-known flagellin phase variation in enterobacteria, where the synthesis of one flagellin is suppressed in favor of the other16. In bacteria, this phase variation provides protection against host antibodies and flagellotropic bacteriophages that bind to specific sites on the flagellar surface17. It has been suggested that the archaellin switch observed in Har. marismortui may similarly protect cells from hypothetical archaellum-specific haloarchaeal phages13.

Unlike most known archaella, which have an average archaellin subunit size of about 200 amino acids (Supplementary Fig. 1), the archaella of Har. marismortui are composed of larger subunits, ArlA2 and ArlB, approximately 450 amino acids in length. This results in a thicker archaellum (approximately 20 nm) than typical archaella (approximately 10 nm)13. In contrast to the archaellins of haloarchaea, such as Hbt. salinarum and Haloferax volcanii, the archaellins of Har. marismortui lack N-glycosylation sites. Although they stain weakly with Schiff’s reagent, there is no experimental evidence of glycosylation13. The middle portion of Har. marismortui archaellins exhibits the highest degree of variability, while the N- and C-termini have a high degree of homology with typical haloarchaeal archaellins. It was suggested that the central regions of the Har. marismortui archaellins are involved in the formation of the outer filament surface, while the terminal regions play a role in intersubunit interactions and provide the backbone for the structure13. The outer region may have a function similar to glycosylation in typical archaellins, acting as a protective layer, and its increased variability could help it adapt to changing environmental conditions.

In this study, we used single-particle cryo-EM analysis to determine the structures of the ArlA2 and ArlB filaments of the Har. marismortui archaellum. We found that both filaments are composed of two distinct parts: an inner core and an outer sheath. While the inner core resembles other known archaella, the outer layer has not been previously described in archaea. The architecture of the outer sheath of ArlA2 and ArlB filaments is different. A comparative analysis of the two structures provides insight into understanding the differences in their environmental adaptation.

Results

Two proteins of Har. marismortui, ArlA2 and ArlB, were shown to form ArlA2 and ArlB archaellar filaments, respectively. Their flanking regions (domains D0-D1), which form the inner part of the filaments, exhibit a high level of sequence homology, with 81.9% identity and 93.4% similarity. The regions forming the outer sheaths are less conserved, with 42.9% identity and 62.0% similarity (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Both ArlA2 and ArlB filaments of Har. marismortui were isolated in a buffer containing a high concentration of NaCl (20% (w/w)). To reduce background noise in micrographs, right before plunging, archaella samples were diluted in a NaCl-free Tris-HCl buffer to a final concentration of 10% NaCl. Upon twofold dilution, the filaments and the tertiary structure of their subunits remain intact. This is supported by data from EM and limited proteolysis, which show that the subunits within the archaellum maintain stability, only losing it when the NaCl concentration falls below 10% (Supplementary Figs. 3, 4). ArlA2 and ArlB filaments were reconstructed by single particle analysis using CryoSPARC18. First, we performed helical reconstruction applying helical parameters previously described for the archaellum structure of Methanococcus maripaludis4. These parameters were refined to 5.54 Å rise and 108.19° twist for the ArlA2 filament and 5.58 Å rise and 108° twist for the ArlB one. The inner part densities of both filaments were of high quality and could be traced without ambiguity. However, despite high global resolution, their outer sheaths were poorly resolved. The quality of the outer sheath density of ArlA2 was improved by utilizing local refinements focused on the outer region (Supplementary Fig. 5). AlphaFold319 was used to generate the starting model for the outer domain of ArlA2. The predicted outer-domain model was fitted to the experimental map of the outer region, subjected to focused real-space refinement, and combined with the core-domain structure to create a model of the full-length ArlA2 molecule.

ArlB filaments were reconstructed without imposing any helical symmetry (Supplementary Fig. 6). The quality of the resulting Coulomb potential map of the ArlB filament was sufficient to build a complete atomic model of the individual ArlB subunit. Later, the outer sheath map was used for symmetry search with cryoSPARC, which gave helical parameters of 78.1 Å rise and 72.1° twist. The full ArlB filament could be reconstructed using helical refinement with these parameters, yielding nearly identical maps. However, no substantial improvement in quality was observed.

Each type of Har. marismortui archaellar filament is ~200 Å in diameter, twice as thick as typical archaella (Fig. 1). The double thickness of Har. marismortui archaella is due to an additional outer sheath surrounding the inner part of the filament. Both Har. marismortui archaellins comprise three domains that form different parts of the archaellar filament. The N-terminal α-helical tail (domain D0) and the adjacent globular domain (domain D1) form the inner region of the filament. The outer sheath consists of another globular domain (domain D2) connected to domain D1 by two antiparallel glycine-rich flexible linkers. Except for the linkers, no other contacts exist between the core region and the outer sheath (Fig. 2).

In general, the structures of the inner regions of Har. marismortui ArlA2 and ArlB filaments are similar to those already seen in other archaella. This region of Har. marismortui filaments is characterized by multiple contacts between subunits in several helical directions: 3-start left-handed; 4-start right-handed; 7-start right-handed. Moreover, their N-terminal α-helix contacts globular domain D2, forming a left-handed 10-start helix in the assembled filament (Supplementary Fig. 7).

A metal-binding site has been clearly resolved within domain D1 of both ArlA2 and ArlB (Supplementary Fig. 8). Based on validation by the CheckMyMetal server20, the metal is assumed to be a sodium ion with an average Me-O distance of ~2.25 Å. Hypothetically, under native conditions, the metal-binding site of ArlA2/ArlB can be occupied by other metal ions present in the Dead Sea water. For instance, the magnesium concentration in the Dead Sea is similar to that of sodium (40.65 g/L and 39.15 g/L, respectively), and the concentration of calcium is 16.86 g/L21. However, binding of other metal ions with different properties (ionic radius, coordination number, metal-O distance, etc.) might require rearrangement of the metal-binding loop conformation. Whether such a rearrangement takes place and how metal binding affects archaellin (or archaellum) conformation could be an interesting topic for future research.

The outer domain D2 of both ArlA2 and ArlB has a similar structure (rmsd is 1.63) and comprises two β-sandwich folds fused into a single bi-lobal structure (Figs. 2–4). No significant structural similarity between this part of the proteins and any deposited structures was found using the DALI server22. A characteristic feature of the ArlB archaellum filament is the formation of dimers between outer domains along the 7-start right-handed helical direction, with the outer domains rotated 180° relative to each other (Fig. 4c, d). Rotation of the outer domain results in two different types of interactions between adjacent domains: arbitrary “head-to-head” and “tail-to-tail”, with “head-to-head” interaction being much stronger than “tail-to-tail” (Fig. 4d, Table 1). The head-to-head interface consists, on average, of 14 hydrogen bonds and one salt bridge with a solvation free energy gain (ΔiG) of 0.5 kcal/mol. In contrast, on average, tail-to-tail interactions are stabilized by only two hydrogen bonds, with ΔiG of −2.3 kcal/mol.

Archaellin ArlB exists in four distinct conformations that differ by 180° rotation of their outer domains along the 7-start right-handed helical direction and in the relative orientation of their core region and outer domain (Fig. 5). These four conformations can be described as a minimal building block of the whole ArlB filament (or asymmetric unit, ASU). The ArlB outer domains form seven right-handed strands surrounding the filament core region. Each pair of these strands is formed by archaellin molecules from ASUs. However, this tetrameric symmetry is broken where one strand remains unpaired (Supplementary Fig. 9). Due to this break in symmetry, we could not obtain a high-resolution reconstruction of the outer sheath density using helical reconstruction.

The structural organization of the ArlA2 outer layer is fundamentally different. Unlike ArlB, the outer domain of ArlA2 is not rotated, and thus, all subunits of this filament are similar concerning the orientation of their domain D2. Interactions between neighboring outer domains in the ArlA2 filament are uniform and weak (Fig. 4a, b; Table 1). These weak interactions within the outer sheath suggest that the ArlA2 filament is more flexible than ArlB. To assess the flexibility of ArlA2 and ArlB filaments, we performed 3D Variability analysis in cryoSPARC23. Because domain D2 of both proteins interacts primarily along the 7-start helix, only motion along this direction was examined. The analysis revealed that although both filaments bend, their motions are distinctly different (Supplementary Movies 1–6).

Two types of motion can be observed in the ArlA2 filament (Supplementary Fig. 10, Supplementary Movies 3 and 4). Subunits on the curved side of the filament slide along the filament axis during bending, moving almost as rigid bodies with minimal relative displacement between domains D1 and D2. In contrast, subunits positioned 90° from the curved side exhibit a large shift of domain D2 relative to domain D1 along the 7-start direction, while the core region shows only minor movement. Unlike ArlA2, ArlB subunits display substantially lower motility regardless of their position within the filament (Supplementary Fig. 11, Supplementary Movies 5 and 6).

Discussion

Several structures of archaeal flagella (archaella) from various groups of archaea have been published2–9. All of them exhibited a very similar architecture with a conserved inner hydrophobic core formed by an N-terminal α-helix and a more diverse globular domain located on the filament surface. Here, we present structures of two types of sheathed archaellar filaments from the halophilic archaeon Har. marismortui. Both filaments, ArlA2 and ArlB, have an additional outer layer surrounding their inner core, making them unique among all known archaella. The two archaella exhibit quite similar internal structures but differ in the outer regions that form the sheath. Domain D2 of ArlB rotates by 180° to form stable dimers. The interactions between the rigid dimers are relatively weak, which is probably necessary to preserve the supercoiling properties of the archaellum. It has been proposed that supercoiling of the archaellum is maintained through continuous motion of individual archaellin subunits6; too rigid a filament structure might restrict this movement and impair supercoiling.

Despite the relatively high similarity in primary and spatial structure with ArlB, no rotation of the outer domain is observed in ArlA2 filaments. The interaction between the D2 domains of ArlA2 is weak, and the outer layer is more flexible than that of ArlB. This increased flexibility is evident from the poor density map of the ArlA2 outer sheath and is further supported by 3D variability analysis.

ArlA2 and ArlB are ecoparalogs that have adapted to different environmental conditions. Thus, ArlA2 is adapted to lower salinity and higher temperatures, whereas ArlB is more stable at higher salt concentrations and lower temperatures14. One strategy for protein adaptation to extreme ionic conditions is an increase in surface-exposed acidic residues, Asp and Glu. Indeed, ArlB2 domain D2 has a larger number of acidic residues along with a decrease in the content of basic residues leading to a more negatively charged molecular surface than in ArlA2 D2 (Supplementary Fig. 12). Besides, rigid dimer formation of the D2 sheath domains on the surface of ArlB filament could strengthen the stiffness of the whole archaellum, which may provide an advantage for motility in more viscous environments.

A database search using the DALI server22 yielded no convincing structural homologs of the outer domains of both ArlA2 and ArlB. It is worth noting that the D2 domains of both ArlA2 and ArlB exhibit some structural similarity to various NAD-kinases (PDB: 2AN1, 9CR3, 8KGC, 7MH7, 7QVS), with Z-scores ranging from 5.1 to 5.4 for ArlA2 and from 6.1 to 6.4 for ArlB. This may indicate the presence of additional auxiliary functionality in the external domains of Har. marismortui archaellins, similar to the proteolytic activity in the external domains of some bacterial flagellins24, and requires further studies. Also, such similarity may point out the origin of the outer domain from other non-archaellin-related proteins through horizontal gene transfer.

A search of protein sequences with BLAST25 revealed several close homologs, all belonging to the Haloarcula genus: Har. quadrata, Har. californiae, and Har. sinaiiensis. Structure prediction of these homologs using AlphaFold3 produced models of the outer domains quite similar to the D2 domain of ArlB Har. marismortui (Supplementary Fig. 13). Previously, it was found that Har. quadrata and Har. californiae possess archaella of ~20 nm in diameter, similar in thickness to the archaella of Har. marismortui26,27. Interestingly, the genome of Har. quadrata encodes two similar archaellins of high molecular weight (UniProt: A0A495QMK6 and A0A495R3F3), which differ in their outer domain structures. These proteins may serve the same function as ecoparalogs, such as ArlA2 and ArlB in Har. marismortui.

Large archaellins can be found among the different unrelated taxonomic groups of archaea. While typical archaellins have a length of about 200 a.a. and comprise only two domains – a N-terminal α-helix and a globular domain that extends to the archaellum surface, larger archaellins have additional outer domains of different lengths and structures. For example, an additional subdomain tightly bound to the globular part of the archaellin has been observed in Sulfolobus species5,8. However, this domain does not form a separate sheath; instead, it creates ridges on the surface of the filament. It has been speculated that such a structure could increase the stiffness of the Sulfolobus archaella. In contrast to Sulfolobus, many other large archaellins, as predicted by AlphaFold3, have distinct external domains separated from the inner core (Supplementary Fig. 14). Although it is challenging to predict, the diversity of the outer domain structures suggests a corresponding diversity in the filament surface architecture.

The role of large archaellins in archaellation and their evolutionary advantage is unknown. In most cases, archaea with such proteins also contain archaellin(s) with a canonical size of about 200 amino acids. Translation of larger proteins is energetically more expensive. Therefore, we propose that the larger archaellins are typically downregulated and not expressed but instead produced only in response to particular needs.

The bacterial motility organelle, the flagellum, is not evolutionarily related to the archaellum. The inner core of the bacterial filament is highly conserved, while the outer shells are highly divergent. Besides its role in motility, the outer flagellin region is also involved in other processes, such as surface adhesion and biofilm formation. Different architectures of the outer region have been observed. Outer sheaths surrounding the core of the flagellar filament have been described for several bacteria inhabiting viscous environments28,29. Analogous to Har. marismortui ArlB, the outer layer in these bacteria is also formed by 180° outer domain rotation and dimerization. This dimerization was shown to alter the filament waveform and is important for cell motility28. Bacterial and archaeal flagellar systems have evolved independently to fulfil similar functions, and as a result, they share many common properties. The sheathed architecture of the archaellum from Har. marismortui is the first example of convergent evolution between archaeal and bacterial flagella, where the formation of an outer layer modulates the properties of the entire filament.

Methods

Archaellar filaments preparation

The Har. marismortui wild-type strain B-1809 (DSM 4426, ATCC 33960) has been obtained from the All-Russian Collection of Microorganisms (VKM) in Pushchino, Russia. The ΔarlA2 strain of Har. marismortui was isolated by growing the wild type under starvation conditions. The absence of the pNG100 plasmid containing the arlA2 gene was confirmed in ΔarlA by PCR14, and archaellin ArlB synthesis was confirmed by mass spectrometry13. Har. marismortui strains were grown at 42 °C in liquid media consisting of the following components per L: 8.5 g peptone, 4.3 g yeast extract, 3.4 g glucose, 5.21 g Tris, 214 g NaCl, 17 g MgSO4 · 7H2O, 1.7 g KCl, 0.23 g CaCl2 · 2H2O, and 3 g Na-citrate, pH 7.230.

Archaella were isolated using polyethylene glycol (PEG) precipitation, following the method described in the literature31. The Har. marismortui cells were grown in 1-liter flasks with 200 ml of medium, under good aeration, until the late stationary phase. To isolate archaella, the cells were pelleted by low-speed centrifugation at 8000 x g using a Beckman J2-HS centrifuge with a JA-14 fixed-angle rotor for 30 min. This resulted in a supernatant containing released archaella. PEG 6000 was then added to the supernatant to a concentration of 4%, and archaella were precipitated from the supernatant using a Beckman Coulter ultra-centrifuge (Opima XE-90) with a fixed-angle titanium rotor (Type 45 Ti, Beckman Coulter), at 46,500 x g for 45 minutes. Then, CsCl density gradient centrifugation (12 hours at 160,820 x g, VTI-80 rotor, Beckman, USA) was used to purify the archaella. Cesium chloride was dissolved in a water-saline medium containing 20% NaCl to obtain a density of 1.36 g/cm³. This CsCl solution was used to dissolve the pelleted archaella. The fraction containing the archaella formed a visible zone in the middle of the tube. It was collected, diluted (10-fold) in saline medium, and the archaella were reprecipitated by centrifugation using a Beckman Coulter ultra-centrifuge (Opima XE-90) with a fixed-angle titanium rotor (Type 45 Ti, Beckman Coulter), at 142,400 x g for 1.5 h. The precipitated archaella were dissolved in 10 mM Tris-HCl buffer, pH 8.0, containing 20% NaCl. This yielded a relatively pure preparation of archaella with a yield of approximately 10 mg from one litre of liquid culture. The concentration of archaellin was determined using the Coomassie Plus Protein Assay Reagent Kit (Thermo Scientific, USA), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. SDS-PAGE was used to analyze the protein samples. Before loading the proteins onto the gel, they were mixed with SDS-PAGE buffer and heated for 2 minutes at 90 °C. The proteins were visualized using Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250 or Schiff’s reagent (Sigma-Aldrich), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Limited proteolysis

Limited proteolysis was carried out using trypsin (Sigma) in a 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer at pH 8.0 containing 0-20% NaCl at room temperature (21 °C). The reaction volume was 20 μL, and the enzyme/protein ratio was 1:100. Appropriate amounts of trypsin dissolved in a 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer at pH 8.0 were added to an archaella solution containing approximately 2 mg/mL of protein. The reaction was terminated by adding an equivalent amount of trypsin inhibitor derived from ovomucoid (Sigma). The products were analyzed by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and stained with Coomassie Blue G250.

Negative stain transmission electron microscopy

A total of 5 μL of sample were applied onto freshly glow-discharged carbon-coated grids, washed three times with drops of buffer and negatively stained for 1 min with 4% uranyl acetate. Grids were imaged on a JEOL JEM-1400 Flash transmission electron microscope operated at 100 kV.

Cryo-EM sample preparation and data collection

The archaella samples were diluted 2-fold with NaCl-free Tris-HCl buffer. Immediately after this, 3 μL of sample solution was deposited onto Quantifoil R1.2/1.3 holey carbon grids, which had been previously plasma-cleaned using a Gatan Solarus (Gatan Inc., USA) for 30 s in a 20% hydrogen, 80% oxygen gas mixture. The grids were blotted with filter paper for 3 seconds at 4 °C and 95% relative humidity, and vitrified by plunging into liquid ethane/propane (50:50) using a FEI Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Movies were collected on a Titan Krios cryo-TEM operating at an acceleration voltage of 300 kV. Images were recorded using EPU software (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a Falcon 3EC direct electron detector in counting mode using a pixel size of 1.083 Å/pixel at the specimen level. Image processing and reconstruction

Image processing and structural determination were performed with cryoSPARC18. Movies were motion corrected using Patch Motion Correction, and contrast transfer function (CTF) estimation was done using Patch CTF Estimation. The initial set of particles was picked using Filament Tracer with a separation distance of 80 Å and extracted at a box size of 400 Å. The particles were subjected to several rounds of 2D classification. Particles from the best classes were used for ab initio 3D reconstruction. For the ArlA2 filament reconstruction, the initial volume was refined using Helical Refinement with 5.7 Å rise and 108.2° twist as starting values. The final refinement resulted in an average resolution of 3.0 Å and a 5.54 Å rise and 108.19° twist. For the ArlA2 outer sheath reconstruction, the original set of particles was symmetry expanded with refined helical parameters and a helical symmetry order of 14. The created particle set was used for local refinement focused on the outer sheath.

For ArlB filament reconstruction, the initial ab initio reconstruction was refined asymmetrically by Homogeneous Refinement, followed by 3D Variability analysis (3DVA) in cryoSPARC23. The particle set that gave the best-looking density map was used for the next round of Homogeneous and Non-uniform Refinements. Finally, local focused refinement of the outer sheath was performed while masking out the inner core of the filaments.

For model building, the final maps were denoised and postprocessed using DeepEMhancer32. The resolution of the resulting maps was estimated using the Fourier shell correlation between two independently refined half-sets of data, using the 0.143 criterion. Local resolutions were calculated within cryoSPARC, and maps were visualized in Chimera X33.

3D variability analysis

The flexibility of ArlA2 and ArlB filaments was studied using the 3DVA tool in cryoSPARC23. For both filaments, 3DVA was performed with five variability components and a filter resolution of 5 Å. The results of 3D variability analysis were imported into the 3D variability display with simple mode and 20 intermediate frames and visualized as a volume series in UCSF Chimera34. Atomic models were fitted and refined against the first and last frames of the map series using Phenix real-space refinement35,36. Final atomic models were morphed with the morphing function in Chimera.

Model building

The initial model for the inner core was generated using the structure of Methanococcus maripaludis archaellin (PDB 5Z1L). The model was manually placed into the map and fitted as a rigid body using COOT37. The model was modified according to the corresponding protein sequence and manually built in COOT.

For the outer domain, the initial model was generated with AlphaFold 314. The model was subsequently rebuilt with COOT using locally refined density maps of either ArlA2 or ArlB outer sheath. Two parts of the archaellin, the inner core and outer domain, were merged into a single protein chain, and a filament model was generated in UCSF Chimera. The final filament model was refined against a consensus map using Phenix real-space refinement35,36. Model validation was performed with MolProbity38,39.
