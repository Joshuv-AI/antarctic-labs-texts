Hearing the shape of a Dirac drum: Dual quantum Hall states on curved surfaces
Ioachim Dusa 1,2 ,a , Denis Kochan 3,4 ,b , Maximilian Fürst 2 , Cosimo Gorini 5 ,c , Klaus Richter 2 ,d

Abstract
The geometry of a physical system is intimately related to its spectral properties, a concept colloquially referred to as “hearing the shape of a drum”. Three-dimensional topological insulator nanowires in a strong magnetic field B B generally host Dirac-type quantum Hall (QH) surface states. The surface itself is shaped by spatial variations of the wires’ cross section, yielding a curved geometrical background, the “drum”, with imprints in the corresponding QH spectra. We show that the latter are composed of two different classes. The first one is asymptotically insensitive to the surface shape, scaling as B 1 / 2 B^{1/2} , like regular planar QH states. Instead, the second has an asymptotic B B -field dependence intimately related to the wire geometry. We further demonstrate that an (axial-symmetric) curved nanowire surface possesses a reciprocal partner surface, such that the respective QH spectra are dual to each other upon exchanging angular momentum and magnetic flux. Notably, a cone-shaped nanowire, and the Corbino geometry as its limiting case, has a reciprocal partner with a dual QH spectrum that is B B -field independent , with corresponding non-magnetic QH-type states. We support our analytical findings by numerical results for B B -field ranges and wire geometries within reach of current experiment.



The idea that the geometry of a physical system is intimately related to its spectral properties was popularised almost 60 years ago, when it was asked whether one “can hear the shape of a drum”
Kac 1966
.
This essentially refers to the (frequency) spectra of confined waves, whether classical sound waves on a drumhead, light waves in dispersive media or electronic waves in a solid. In the latter case basic examples are the different electronic spectra of non-interacting particles in flat space, confined within a billiard-type potential well, in a harmonic trap, in some complex gratings or atomic corral potentials. A more spectacular one is the formation of Landau Levels (LLs), when freely dispersing electrons subject to a strong magnetic field coalesce into a discrete set of field-dependent quantum Hall states –
e.g.
of Schrödinger or Dirac type
Yoshioka 2002
;
McClure 1956

The quantum Hall phase is however an ideal platform to consider the more subtle role of metric deformations. This is notably because the latter formally allow to probe the viscosity of a quantum Hall droplet

Conclusions

—We studied the Dirac QH spectrum of axially symmetric surface states identifying two distinct branches that can be potentially realized in laboratory by surface modes of 3D TI nanowires. First, we showed that one branch – we call it geometric – allows to sense the shape of the nanowire while exploring its LL spectrum for large magnetic field. Thus 3D TIs can serve as another platform for “hearing the shape of drum”. Second, we uncovered a spectral duality of 2D Dirac equation in axial magnetic field – a sort of transformation of reciprocal radii – that maps eigenvalues and eigenfunctions between two surfaces whose radii are related by
r(s)\rightarrow\tilde{r}(s)=1/r(s)
. Third, we found a curved surface, the reciprocal cone, with peculiar counter-reaction to
B
. It carries “non-magnetic quantum Hall states”, which reorganize upon changes in
B
in a way to carry zero magnetization and thus canceling the field dependence of the corresponding eigenenergies.
We conjecture these states to exist for generic (smooth) surfaces of revolution beyond the specifics
of 3D TIs, which we verified by numerous numerical calculations.

We conclude by considering some model limitations and material-related aspects. First, our analysis was limited to orbital effects neglecting the Zeeman coupling. For coaxial magnetic fields
B_{z}
we expect the latter to have the general form

H_{Z}=\frac{\mu_{B}}{2}\bigg(\sum_{i}\,g_{i}(s,\varphi)\sigma_{i}\bigg)B_{z},\quad i=x,y,z,

(23)

with coordinate-dependent and anisotropic effective
g
-factors.
As standard in
{\bf k}\cdot{\bf p}
theory
Winkler 2003
, this Zeeman term arises from a folding down procedure and carries information about material-specific anisotropies
Liu et al. 2010
, including notably the real spin character of surface states
Zhang et al. 2012
;
Silvestrov et al. 2012
;
Brey and Fertig 2014
. Zeeman couplings in the
x
- and
y
-directions do not spoil the (chiral) structure of Eq.(
3
),
ergo
our conclusions remain valid. A
z
-term on the other hand does, so that a reciprocal surface with invariant spectrum cannot be exactly defined as done in Eqs. (
9
) and (
10
). However, the
B
-dependent Zeeman splitting is expected to be small for the field strengths considered.
To what extent the duality is broken and if a different one can still be identified, depends on the geometry and material.

Finally, we neglected standard non-magnetic disorder, as it does not qualitatively affect our conclusions as long as broadening remains smaller than the resolution of our spectra. It might play a more interesting role in transport, which however lies beyond the scope of this work.

\tilde{\Sigma}
:

\frac{1}{\tilde{r}}\left[\tilde{r}{\psi_{\tilde{\Sigma}}}^{\prime}(s)\right]^{\prime}+\left(\frac{\alpha_{m}}{\tilde{r}}+\beta\tilde{r}\right)^{2}{\psi_{\tilde{\Sigma}}}(s)=E_{\tilde{\Sigma}}{\psi_{\tilde{\Sigma}}}(s),\quad(\tilde{\Sigma})

where we assume a separation ansatz for the wavefunction:

\psi_{\Sigma}(s,\varphi)=e^{i(m+1/2)\varphi}\psi_{\Sigma}(s).

(S2)

As in the main text, we impose the transformation relations:

r=\frac{1}{\tilde{r}},\quad\psi_{{\Sigma}}=\tilde{r}\psi_{\tilde{\Sigma}},\quad\alpha_{m}\leftrightarrow\beta.

(S3)

Substituting these into the equation for
\Sigma
gives:

\tilde{r}\left[\frac{1}{\tilde{r}}(\tilde{r}\psi_{\tilde{\Sigma}})^{\prime}\right]^{\prime}+\left(\beta\tilde{r}+\frac{\alpha_{m}}{\tilde{r}}\right)^{2}\tilde{r}\psi_{\tilde{\Sigma}}=E_{\Sigma}\tilde{r}\psi_{\tilde{\Sigma}}.

(S4)

To ensure the duality between the two equations, the kinetic and potential energy terms must be identical, as the latter remain unchanged under the transformation of the angular quantum number and magnetic flux,
\alpha_{m}\leftrightarrow\beta
. This requirement leads to the condition:

\frac{1}{\tilde{r}}\left[\tilde{r}{\psi_{\tilde{\Sigma}}}^{\prime}(s)\right]^{\prime}-E_{\tilde{\Sigma}}\psi_{\tilde{\Sigma}}\overset{!}{=}\left[\frac{1}{\tilde{r}}(\tilde{r}\psi_{\tilde{\Sigma}})^{\prime}\right]^{\prime}-E_{{\Sigma}}\psi_{\tilde{\Sigma}}.

(S5)

Expanding the derivatives results in:

-E_{\tilde{\Sigma}}\psi_{\tilde{\Sigma}}\overset{!}{=}\bigg(\frac{\tilde{r}^{\prime}}{\tilde{r}}\bigg)^{\prime}\psi_{\tilde{\Sigma}}-E_{{\Sigma}}\psi_{\tilde{\Sigma}}.

(S6)

For this equality to hold, the function
\tilde{r}
must satisfy the constraint:

\bigg(\frac{\tilde{r}^{\prime}}{\tilde{r}}\bigg)^{\prime}=\text{const}\equiv c,

(S7)

which also implies a shift in energy:

E_{\tilde{\Sigma}}=E_{\Sigma}-c.

(S8)

The explicit form of the radial profile
\tilde{r}
follows from integration:

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{r}^{\prime}}{\tilde{r}}

\displaystyle=cs+d,

(S9)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\tilde{r}}{\tilde{r}}

\displaystyle=(cs+d)\mathrm{d}s,

(S10)

\displaystyle\ln\tilde{r}

\displaystyle=\frac{c}{2}s^{2}+ds+f,

(S11)

\displaystyle\tilde{r}

\displaystyle=e^{f}e^{\frac{c}{2}s^{2}+ds}\equiv\tilde{r}_{0}e^{\frac{c}{2}s^{2}+ds}.

(S12)

For the original surface
\Sigma
, the corresponding radial function is then given by:

r=\frac{1}{\tilde{r}}=r_{0}e^{-\frac{c}{2}s^{2}-ds}.

II
Analytical solutions on the nanocone

We solve the rescaled eigenvalue equation from the main text

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}0&\hat{L}^{-}\\
\hat{L}^{+}&0\end{pmatrix}\psi_{\Sigma}(s)=E_{\Sigma}\psi_{\Sigma}(s)\,,\quad

(S13)

\displaystyle\hat{L}^{\pm}=i\bigg(-\partial_{s}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{r^{\prime}(s)}{r(s)}\pm V_{\Sigma}(s)\bigg),

(S14)

on the nanocone
r(s)=s\sin(\theta/2)
.
First we rescale the wave function

\psi_{\Sigma}\to\frac{1}{\sqrt{r}}\psi_{\Sigma},

(S15)

which renders the differential operator

\hat{L}^{\pm}\to\hat{L}^{\pm}=i\bigg(-\partial_{s}\pm V_{\Sigma}(s)\bigg).

(S16)

Next, we explicitly calculate the wave function of the ground state.
To do so, we rewrite the effective mass potential as

V_{\Sigma}(s)=\frac{\alpha^{\theta}_{m}}{s}+\beta_{\theta}s,

(S17)

where we defined the quantities

\beta_{\theta}\equiv{\sin(\theta/2)}{\beta}\;\text{ and }\;\alpha_{m}^{\theta}\equiv\frac{\alpha_{m}}{\sin(\theta/2)}.

(S18)

This definition will be useful as these variables will appear as exponents.
Based on
V_{\Sigma}(s)
we see that the ground state only exists if the magnetic field
B
and the angular number
\alpha_{m}
have different signs. By plugging in, it is easy to show that the ground state in the case
B>0
and
\alpha_{m}<0
is given by

\psi_{\Sigma}^{0}(s)=\mathcal{N}_{0}\,p_{0}(s)\,\left(\begin{matrix}1\\
0\end{matrix}\right),

(S19)

with the normalization constant
\mathcal{N}_{0}
and

p_{0}(s)=s^{|\alpha_{m}^{\theta}|}\exp\Big\{-|\beta_{\theta}|s^{2}/2\Big\},

(S20)

while in the other case
B<0
and
\alpha_{m}>0
we obtain

\psi_{\Sigma}^{0}(s)=\mathcal{N}_{0}\,p_{0}(s)\,\left(\begin{matrix}0\\
1\end{matrix}\right).

(S21)

Having derived the expression for the ground state, we can proceed to analyze the explicit form of the excited states.
Using the rescaled form of the differential operator
\hat{L}^{\pm}
from Eq. (
S16
), we are ready to write down the decoupled differential equation

\hat{L}^{-}\hat{L}^{+}\psi_{\downarrow}(s)=E_{\Sigma}^{2}\psi_{\downarrow}

(S22)

for the excited states, where we order the terms by powers in
s
, namely

\bigg(-\partial^{2}_{s}+A_{1}-E_{\Sigma}^{2}+\frac{A_{2}}{s^{2}}+A_{3}s^{2}\bigg)\psi_{\downarrow}(s)=0,

(S23)

with the factors

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle=\beta_{\theta}+2\beta_{\theta}\alpha_{m}^{\theta}

(S24)

\displaystyle A_{2}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{m}^{\theta}(\alpha_{m}^{\theta}-1)

(S25)

\displaystyle A_{3}

\displaystyle=\beta_{\theta}^{2}.

(S26)

The most important properties to keep in mind are
A_{3}>0
and
A_{2}>-1/4
.
The following ansatz leads to an analytical solution

\psi_{\downarrow}(s)=\frac{\mathcal{N}}{\sqrt{s}}{p\Big(|\beta_{\theta}|s^{2}\Big)},

(S27)

with some new function
p(z)
and normalization constant
\mathcal{N}
.
After some calculation steps, one arrives at the two linearly independent solutions, which are the Whittaker functions

p\Big(|\beta_{\theta}|s^{2}\Big)=M_{\kappa,\mu}\Big(|\beta_{\theta}|s^{2}\Big)\quad\text{and}\quad W_{\kappa,\mu}\Big(|\beta_{\theta}|s^{2}\Big),

(S28)

with

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle=\frac{-A_{1}+E_{\Sigma}^{2}}{4A_{3}}\,,

(S29)

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{A_{2}+1/4}=\frac{1}{2}|\alpha^{\theta}_{m}-1/2|>0.

These Whittaker functions can be further expressed in terms of Kummer’s confluent hypergeometric functions
M(a,b,z)
and
U(a,b,z)
, with the variables
a,b,z\in\mathbb{R}
, by

\displaystyle M_{\kappa;\mu}\left(z\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-z/2\right)z^{\mu+{\tfrac{1}{2}}}M\left(\mu-\kappa+{\tfrac{1}{2}},1+2\mu,z\right),

\displaystyle W_{\kappa;\mu}\left(z\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-z/2\right)z^{\mu+{\tfrac{1}{2}}}U\left(\mu-\kappa+{\tfrac{1}{2}},1+2\mu,z\right).

Since we want the solutions to be bound states, we demand

\displaystyle\lim_{s\rightarrow 0}\psi_{\downarrow}(s)

\displaystyle\rightarrow 0,

(S31)

\displaystyle\lim_{s\rightarrow\infty}\psi_{\downarrow}(s)

\displaystyle\rightarrow 0.

The first limit in Eq. (
S31
) is fulfilled by default since
\mu>0
in Eq. (
II
) and by definition
M(a,b,0)=1
, leading to

\psi_{\downarrow}(0)=M_{\kappa;\mu}(0)=W_{\kappa;\mu}(0)=0.

(S32)

Therefore, the only relevant case we have to worry about is
s\to\infty
. The only way to achieve a vanishing solution at infinity is by requiring the Kummer’s functions
M(a,b,z)
and
U(a,b,z)
to be a polynomial, which is the case if the first argument is a non-positive number
-n
where
n\in\mathbb{N}
, which will be the principal quantum number. In our case this amounts to

\mu-\kappa+1/2\overset{!}{=}-n.

(S33)

The hypergeometric functions are then reduced to generalized Laguerre polynomials and become linearly dependent

U(-n,1+2\mu,z)\propto M(-n,1+2\mu,z)\propto L_{n}^{2\mu}(z).

(S34)

By rearranging Eq. (
S33
), we arrive at the spectrum
E_{\Sigma}
and notice that it splits into two categories, namely, for
\sgn(B)\neq\sgn(\alpha_{m})
we obtain

E^{g}_{\Sigma}(\alpha_{m},\beta,n)=\pm\sqrt{4|\beta_{\theta}|n}\underset{B\to\infty}{\propto}B^{\frac{1}{2}},

(S35)

while for
\sgn(B)=\sgn(\alpha_{m})
we have

E^{f}_{\Sigma}(\alpha_{m},\beta,n)=\pm\sqrt{4|\beta_{\theta}|\Big(\frac{1}{2}+n+|\alpha^{\theta}_{m}|\Big)}\underset{B\to\infty}{\propto}B^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(S36)

In both cases, we asymptotically get a square root dependence of the energies as a function of magnetic field
B
.

As we can see in the analytical expression, Eq. (
S36
), the conical geometry with
\theta<\pi
leads to the breaking of the
m
-degeneracy of the standard Dirac-Landau levels from Eq. (
S35
). To the best of our knowledge, these novel energy levels for massless Dirac electrons on the cone have not been calculated before. A similar problem, yet only for Schrödinger electrons, has been addressed in
Marques et al. 2001
, where a degeneracy breaking can also be observed.

Let us now take care of the wavefunctions and deliver the explicit form of the arc length spinor
\psi_{\Sigma}(s)
. Before starting, a short remark is necessary. We have already seen that the energies fall into two branches, namely for equal and opposite sign of
\alpha_{m}
and
B
, and thus the absolute sign is not of importance, only the relative one. However, this is no longer the case for the wavefunctions. We have already seen in the calculation of the zero energy levels, Eqs. (
S21
) and (
S19
), that the absolute sign of
B
and
\alpha_{m}
influences the form of the ground state wave function. The same behavior is to be expected in the calculation of the excited states. Therefore, we expect slightly different solutions for different absolute signs of
B
and
\alpha_{m}
. Fortunately, we do not need to calculate all four cases, as we can make use of time reversal transformations
\mathcal{T}
to translate solutions from
B
and
\alpha_{m}
to
-B
and
\alpha_{-m}
, since time reversal transformations flip the sign of magnetic fields and angular momentum, and thus also the sign of the angular momentum quantum number. Therefore, we compute the wavefunctions only for two cases, namely for (
B<0
,
\alpha_{m}>0
) and (
B>0
,
\alpha_{m}>0
) and then apply the time reversal operator, i.e.

\mathcal{T}=i\sigma_{y}\mathcal{K}

(S37)

on the spinor
\psi(s)
to obtain the remaining two cases. Here we denote the complex conjugation operator by
\mathcal{K}
.

Let us start with
B<0
and
\alpha_{m}>0
.
Using Eqs. (
S28
), (
II
), (
S33
) and (
S34
), the ansatz, Eq. (
S27
),
becomes

\psi_{\downarrow}(s)=\mathcal{N}p_{0}(s)L_{n}^{|\alpha_{m}^{\theta}|-1/2}(|\beta_{\theta}|s^{2}),

(S38)

using the function
p_{0}(s)
from Eq. (
S20
). The other component can easily be obtained by applying the differential operator
\hat{L}^{+}
on the down-spin component, i.e.

\psi_{\uparrow}(s)=\frac{i(-\partial_{s}+V_{\Sigma}(s))}{E_{\Sigma}}\psi_{\downarrow}(s),

(S39)

which gives us the total spinor

\psi_{\Sigma}(s)=\mathcal{N}p_{0}(s)\begin{pmatrix}{\frac{2i|\beta_{\theta}|}{|E^{g}_{\Sigma}(\alpha_{m},\beta.n)|}}sL_{n-1}^{|\alpha_{m}^{\theta}|+1/2}(|\beta_{\theta}|s^{2})\\
L_{n}^{|\alpha_{m}^{\theta}|-1/2}(|\beta_{\theta}|s^{2})\end{pmatrix}.

(S40)

We observe that the up component has one polynomial order less than the down component, which is consistent with the structure of the ground state, see Eq. (
S21
).
Performing a similar computation for the other case (
B>0
,
\alpha_{m}>0
) yields the total spinor

\psi_{\Sigma}(s)=\mathcal{N}p_{0}(s)\begin{pmatrix}\frac{2i|\beta_{\theta}|}{|E^{f}_{\Sigma}(\alpha_{m},\beta,n)|}sL_{n}^{|\alpha^{\theta}_{m}|+1/2}(|\beta_{\theta}|s^{2})\\
L_{n}^{|\alpha^{\theta}_{m}|-1/2}(|\beta_{\theta}|s^{2})\end{pmatrix},

(S41)

where this time we obtain an order higher in the up component. The effect of
\mathcal{T}
preserves the overall shape of the states, only that the up component is swapped with the down component.

III
Local current density and magnetic moment

In this section, we calculate the probability current density
\vec{j}
on general curved and rotationally symmetric surfaces
\Sigma
, which are parameterized by arc length and azimuthal angle,
(s,\varphi)
. We consider the physical radius of revolution, which is expressed as
\rho(s)
.
Subsequently we calculate the magnetic moment on curved surfaces starting from
\vec{j}
.

We start by specifying the Dirac equation in curved space

\displaystyle i\hbar\partial_{t}\psi_{\Sigma}=\hbar v_{F}\bigg[-i

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}\bigg(\partial_{s}+\frac{\rho^{\prime}(s)}{2\rho(s)}\bigg)+

(S42)

\displaystyle\sigma_{2}\bigg(-i\frac{\partial_{\varphi}}{\rho(s)}+\frac{eB_{z}}{2\hbar}\rho(s)\bigg)\bigg]\psi_{\Sigma},

where
\sigma_{i}
are the usual Pauli matrices.
A possible Lagrangian is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(t,s,\varphi)=\sqrt{-\mathcal{G}}\hbar v_{F}\psi_{\Sigma}^{\dagger}\bigg[-i\sigma_{1}\bigg(\partial_{s}+\frac{\rho^{\prime}(s)}{2\rho(s)}\bigg)+\sigma_{2}\Big(-i\frac{\partial_{\varphi}}{\rho(s)}+\frac{eB_{z}}{2\hbar}\rho(s)\Big)-i\frac{\partial_{t}}{v_{F}}\bigg]\psi_{\Sigma}.

(S43)

The action is then calculated by

S=\int\mathrm{d}t\int_{\Sigma}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}\varphi\,\mathcal{L}(t,s,\varphi).

(S44)

In our case, for a general surface of revolution we obtain
\sqrt{-\mathcal{G}}=\rho(s)
.
The Lagrangian is invariant under the global
U(1)
- transformation of the wavefunction

\displaystyle\psi_{\Sigma}\to\exp(i\alpha)\psi_{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\psi_{\Sigma}+i\alpha\psi_{\Sigma}+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{2})

(S45)

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle\psi_{\Sigma}+\alpha\Delta\psi_{\Sigma}+\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{2}).

This yields the conserved Noether current

\mathcal{J}^{\mu}:={\frac{\partial{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial(\partial_{\mu}\psi_{\Sigma})}}\Delta\psi_{\Sigma}+{\frac{\partial{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial(\partial_{\mu}\psi_{\Sigma}^{\dagger})}}\Delta\psi_{\Sigma}^{\dagger}.

(S46)

We therefore obtain explicitly

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{t}

\displaystyle=\hbar\rho(s)\psi_{\Sigma}^{\dagger}\psi_{\Sigma}

(S47)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{\varphi}

\displaystyle=\hbar v_{F}\psi_{\Sigma}^{\dagger}{\sigma_{2}}\psi_{\Sigma}

(S48)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{s}

\displaystyle=\hbar v_{F}\rho(s)\psi_{\Sigma}^{\dagger}\sigma_{1}\psi_{\Sigma}.

(S49)

By writing down
\partial_{\mu}\mathcal{J}^{\mu}=0
, one obtains

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Big(\psi_{\Sigma}^{\dagger}\psi_{\Sigma}\Big)

(S50)

\displaystyle+v_{F}\frac{1}{\rho(s)}{\partial_{\varphi}}\Big(\psi_{\Sigma}^{\dagger}{\sigma_{2}}\psi_{\Sigma}\Big)

\displaystyle+v_{F}\frac{1}{\rho(s)}\partial_{s}\Big(\rho(s)\psi_{\Sigma}^{\dagger}\sigma_{1}\psi_{\Sigma}\Big).

The spatial divergence acting on a spatial tangential vector in the coordinates
(s,\varphi)
with
\mathcal{G}_{ss}=1
,
\mathcal{G}_{\varphi\varphi}=\rho^{2}(s)
is given by

\nabla\vec{j}=\frac{1}{\rho(s)}\partial_{\varphi}j_{\varphi}+\frac{1}{\rho(s)}\partial_{s}(\rho(s)j_{s}).

(S51)

Identifying the charge density
\varrho=-e\psi^{\dagger}\psi
, we easily obtain the continuity equation

\frac{\partial\varrho}{\partial t}+\nabla\vec{j}=0,

(S52)

with (spatial) current components

\displaystyle j_{\varphi}

\displaystyle=-ev_{F}\psi_{\Sigma}^{\dagger}\sigma_{2}\psi_{\Sigma},

(S53)

\displaystyle j_{s}

\displaystyle=-ev_{F}\psi_{\Sigma}^{\dagger}\sigma_{1}\psi_{\Sigma}.

(S54)

We proceed and calculate the magnetic moment in
z
direction which reads

m_{z}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}\varphi\rho(s)\bigg(\vec{\rho}\times\vec{j}\bigg)_{z},

(S55)

where

\vec{\rho}=\begin{pmatrix}\rho(s)\cos(\varphi)\\
\rho(s)\sin(\varphi)\\
z(s)\end{pmatrix}.

(S56)

We express the current density in the local coordinates
\vec{j}=j_{\varphi}\mathbf{e}_{\varphi}+j_{s}\mathbf{e}_{s}
, with the tangential directions
\mathbf{e}_{\varphi}
and
\mathbf{e}_{s}
given by

\mathbf{e}_{\mu}=\frac{\partial_{\mu}\vec{\rho}}{|\partial_{\mu}\vec{\rho}|}.

(S57)

We realize that the surviving part in the cross product is

\displaystyle m_{z}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Sigma}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}\varphi\rho^{2}(s)j_{\varphi}=-\frac{ev_{F}}{2}\int_{\Sigma}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}\varphi\rho^{2}(s)\psi_{\Sigma}^{\dagger}\sigma_{2}\psi_{\Sigma}.

(S58)

Note that by utilizing the Hellman-Feynman theorem, it is easy to show that
m_{z}
is consistent with the thermodynamical definition

m_{z}=-\frac{\partial E_{\Sigma}}{\partial B}.

(S59)

IV
Vanishing magnetic moment for the geometry-sensitive branch

We are now prepared to analytically compute the magnetic moment
m_{z}
on the reciprocal cone for the states with
\text{sgn}(\alpha_{m})\neq\text{sgn}(B)
, corresponding to the geometry-sensitive branch. Using Eq. (
S58
), we have for the magnetization of the reciprocal surface
\tilde{r}(s)=1/r(s)
the proportionality

m_{z}\propto\int_{s_{0}}^{s_{1}}\mathrm{d}s\tilde{r}^{2}(s)\psi_{\tilde{\Sigma}}^{\{\alpha_{m};\beta\}\dagger}\sigma_{2}\psi_{\tilde{\Sigma}}^{\{\alpha_{m};\beta\}},

(S60)

where for simplicity we chose

s_{0}=0
and
s_{1}=\infty
. As in the main paper, the corresponding quantum number
\alpha_{m}
and dimensionless magnetic flux
\beta
are specified as superscripts.
Using the definition of the cone profile
r(s)=s\sin(\theta/2)
and the theorem from the main text,
\psi_{\tilde{\Sigma}}^{\{\alpha_{m};\beta\}}=r(s)\psi_{{\Sigma}}^{\{\beta;\alpha_{m}\}}
, we obtain the magnetic moment for the geometry-sensitive states on the reciprocal cone

\displaystyle m_{z}

\displaystyle\propto\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}s\frac{1}{s}p_{0}^{2}(s)sL_{n-1}^{{|Y|}+1/2}(|X|s^{2})L_{n}^{|Y|-1/2}(|X|s^{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}sp_{0}^{2}(s)L_{n-1}^{{|Y|}+1/2}(|X|s^{2})L_{n}^{|Y|-1/2}(|X|s^{2}),

(S61)

where

X\equiv\sin(\theta/2)\alpha_{m}\quad\text{and}\quad Y\equiv\frac{\beta}{\sin(\theta/2)}.

(S62)

To solve the integral we choose the transformation
z=|X|s^{2}
,
which gives

\displaystyle m_{z}

\displaystyle\propto\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{\sqrt{z}}z^{|Y|}\exp(-z)L_{n-1}^{{|Y|}+1/2}(z)L_{n}^{|Y|-1/2}(z)

(S63)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathrm{d}z}z^{|Y|-1/2}\exp(-z)L_{n-1}^{{|Y|}+1/2}(z)L_{n}^{|Y|-1/2}(z).

We further use an identity for generalized Laguerre polynomials from literature, namely

L_{n}^{(\alpha+1)}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}L_{i}^{(\alpha)}(x),

(S64)

which gives us

m_{z}\propto\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathrm{d}z}z^{|Y|-1/2}\exp(-z)L_{i}^{{|Y|}-1/2}(z)L_{n}^{|Y|-1/2}(z).

(S65)

We then use the orthogonality relation, resulting in

\displaystyle m_{z}

\displaystyle\propto\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathrm{d}z}z^{|Y|-1/2}\exp(-z)L_{i}^{{|Y|}-1/2}(z)L_{n}^{|Y|-1/2}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{\Gamma(n+|Y|-1/2+1)}{n!}\delta_{ni}=0.

(S66)

We have therefore analytically demonstrated that the magnetic moment on the reciprocal cone vanishes for the geometry-sensitive states, where
\text{sgn}(\alpha_{m})\neq\text{sgn}(B)
.

V
Asymptotic behavior of transcendental surfaces

In this paragraph we are interested in the numerical behavior of the spectrum of the transcendental surface

\frac{\mathrm{d}r}{\mathrm{d}s}\propto\sin(r),

(S67)

whose unique radius solution reads

r(s)=2\arctan(\exp(s)).

(S68)

Fig.
S1
shows the spectrum of this surface for a given angular quantum number
m

n

\sgn(B)=\sgn(\alpha_{m})
scales with the square root, while the different sign branch
\sgn(B)\neq\sgn(\alpha_{m})
scales with the fourth root. This confirms our conjecture, since

\sin(r)=r^{1}-\frac{r^{3}}{3!}+\dots,

(S69)

and hence
\eta_{\Sigma}=(2-N)/4=1/4
should be the dominant contribution.
