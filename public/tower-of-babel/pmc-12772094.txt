Mammographic findings and BI-RADS concordance in women attending a community outreach program in Uganda
Authors: Eric Kitongo, Enock Suubi Segawa, Nayiga Joyce, Nabbosa Valeria, Bugeza Samuel, Kibudde Solomon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe incidence and mortality of breast cancer in Uganda are on the rise. Therefore, the Improving Breast Cancer Care Coordination (IBCCC) program was initiated to bring breast cancer screening and diagnostic services closer to women in hard-to-reach communities all over Uganda. It was the first mammography community outreach where participants received immediate (on-site) readings. So, the level of agreement between mammographic Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System (BI-RADS) readings taken in the community (on-site) and those taken in the standard radiological reporting unit (off-site) was unknown. The current study aimed to describe the mammographic findings and determine the BI-RADS concordance between on-site and off-site readings for women attending a community outreach breast cancer early detection program in Northern Uganda.MethodsA cross-sectional design and data collected from attendees of the IBCCC program were used. Mammography had been performed, and the images had been read by the program’s radiologist. Participants were selected by simple random sampling from attendees of the program in Northern Uganda, and their mammograms were also read by our study’s radiologists. Summary statistics were calculated for mammographic findings, and the BI-RADS concordance was assessed using unweighted, linear weighted and quadratic weighted Kappa (k) statistics comparing on-site to off-site mammogram readings.Results201 participants were selected from attendees of the IBCCC program from 9th January to 5th September 2023. The mean participant age was 51 years. Most participants reported at least one symptom (70.7%); the most common symptom was pain (67.6%). The most common breast composition was scattered fibroglandular density (38.3%). Sixty-nine participants (34.3%) had a mass. Thirty-four (16.9%) participants had calcifications, of which nine (26.5%) had suspicious morphology. Off-site and on-site readings awarded a BI-RADS score of 4 or 5 to 18.4% and 13.4% of participants, respectively. The quadratic weighted BI-RADS concordance was 0.58 (95% CI: 0.44–0.71).ConclusionA considerable proportion of women in the current study had BI-RADS classifications of 4 and 5, suggesting numerous late presentations in the community. Those late presentations likely indicate poor access to breast cancer screening and diagnostic services. The BI-RADS concordance was moderate between on-site and off-site readings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15378-0.

Background

Breast cancer is a disease characterised by malignant cell growth in the epithelial cells of the ducts or lobules of the breast [1]. Although it can develop in males and females, its prevalence in women is about 100 times that in men [2]. Globally, it had the highest incidence and second-highest mortality rates among all cancers in 2022 [3]. Of 21 global regions considered, Eastern Africa had the third lowest age standardised incidence rate at 31.9 per 100,000 but the sixth highest age standardised mortality rate at 17.6 per 100,000 in the same year [4]. These statistics reveal that while the incidence of breast cancer in the region is relatively low, its mortality is disproportionately high, suggesting sub-optimal management. Furthermore, in Uganda, breast cancer has age-standardised incidence and mortality rates of 21.3 per 100,000 of the total population and 10.3 per 100,000 of the total population, respectively, giving it a case fatality rate of about 50% [5].

There is no known effective primary prevention strategy for breast cancer [6], making secondary prevention by screening and early diagnosis the best (available) approach to controlling the disease. In fact, since the 1980 s, survival with breast cancer has been steadily improving in countries with early detection programs and effective treatment strategies [1].

Based on a Dutch study, the mortality rate from breast cancer would decrease by 29.9% if women aged 40 to 74 underwent biennial mammographic screenings for the disease and those diagnosed with it received proper treatment [7]. A modelling study using data from East Africa showed that appropriate management following diagnosis could reduce relative mortality by 8 to 41% [8]. Therefore, mammographic screening and early diagnosis will also significantly reduce disease-specific mortality among women in Uganda.

However, in Uganda, the low feasibility of screening programs coupled with the different epidemiological characteristics of the disease among women of African descent has limited the use of some of these programs, especially mammography [9]. The Uganda Atomic Energy Council records show that by 2019, there were 20 registered mammography machines in Uganda, only 3 of which belonged to Government health facilities [10]. Nine of these mammography machines were registered as not functional.

The paucity of mammography machines immensely limits access to mammographic services to a small minority of the Ugandan population. Therefore, the Improving Breast Cancer Care Coordination (IBCCC) program was initiated to bring breast cancer screening and diagnostic services closer to women in hard-to-reach communities all over Uganda. The current study utilised data collected while executing that program in Northern Uganda.

The current study described the mammographic findings and determined the Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System (BI-RADS) concordance for women who attended the IBCCC program in Northern Uganda. In this context, BI-RADS concordance was between readings by the program’s radiologist (on-site readings) and the study’s radiologists (off-site readings) of mammograms. It serves as an indicator of confidence in these readings.

Methods

A cross-sectional study design nested in the IBCCC program in Northern Uganda was used. The program was an ongoing nationwide two-year prospective quality improvement program with a specific objective to conduct 100 outreaches to hard-to-reach communities and reduce provider and patient delays. However, only data collected from the Northern region were used for the current study because they consisted of the most participants with homogeneous data to accomplish both study objectives, as the approach to reading mammograms in the program differed among regions.

Outreaches involved health education talks by a trained nurse about breast cancer at selected venues within hard-to-reach communities affiliated with the five regional referral hospitals in Northern Uganda, followed by assessment of women with breast cancer-related concerns. Program personnel in a mobile mammography van from the Uganda Cancer Institute (UCI) would make a road trip to communities and provide a one-stop triple assessment for breast cancer, which entailed medical history and breast examination, mammography and breast ultrasound, and histology for BI-RADS 4 and 5 lesions.

Mammography was conducted in women aged 35 years or older, excluding breastfeeding mothers. The mammography machine used was a computed radiography machine with a single focal spot and automatic exposure, branded LILYUM, manufactured in Italy in January 2018 and mounted in a mobile van.

The van had a section for conducting mammography that was shielded appropriately from the other sections of the van and its surroundings. In another section was the equipment for conducting ultrasound scans as well as the console from which mammographic images were interpreted. The ambient lighting in this section was bright enough to facilitate ultrasound scanning, but consequently not ideal for image interpretation on the mammography console.

One radiologist with two years of experience interpreted the images and reported the results on the same day that mammography was conducted. In some cases, he performed a complementary ultrasound to improve the accuracy of their readings. He reported an average of 3 mammograms per day; however, the daily counts ranged from 1 to 15. Moreover, he had to perform various other tasks in between interpreting mammograms, such as taking clinical histories and performing physical examinations, ultrasound scans, and program administration.

Before initiating any program activities, the Uganda Cancer Institute Research and Ethics Committee and Uganda National Council of Science and Technology approved the implementation of this program. The School of Medicine Research Ethics Committee (Mak-SOMREC-2023-644) granted ethical approval and a waiver of consent for the current study, as the study posed only minimal risk to the participants, could not be executed without a waiver of consent, and the waiver would not adversely affect the participants’ rights.

The sample size of 201 women’s records was determined using the Kish-Leslie formula [11] and statistics of data obtained from on-site readings to describe the mammographic findings among women participating in the program in Northern Uganda. Records were selected by simple random sampling from the IBCCC program’s database. Records with one craniocaudal and one mediolateral oblique view per breast and complete data were included, while those with mammographic images of inadequate diagnostic quality were excluded. Image quality was assessed using the PGMI (Perfect, Good, Moderate, Inadequate) method, and images in the I category (four out of 205 images, 2.0%) were excluded.

The data collected consisted of participants’ demographics, clinical history, mammograms, and results of on-site mammographic readings. Two or three study radiologists conducted off-site readings using the Radiant DICOM reader software package version 1.1 as described below.

The first two study radiologists independently read all the mammograms. In cases where the BI-RADS scores by the first two radiologists were the same, that score was recorded as the definitive score of the mammogram. Where these scores differed, the third study radiologist would read the same mammogram independently, and their score was used as a tiebreaker. Crucially, the study radiologists were all blinded to the program radiologist’s readings until they had finished submitting all their readings. Additionally, all mammographic features described later in the results section were obtained using the study radiologists’ readings, unless otherwise stated.

The study radiologists had 3, 7, and 16 years of experience and did not have to report the results on the same day that they received the mammogram. However, they could not complement their readings with clinical history, physical examination findings, or ultrasound images, as the program radiologist could.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Stata version 15.0. Descriptive statistics, percentage agreement between on-site and off-site readings for each BI-RADS score, and the kappa statistic comparing on-site and off-site readings were obtained. The percentage agreement for each BI-RADS score was determined by dividing the number of participants for whom both on-site and off-site readings were that score by the number of all participants for whom either on-site or off-site readings or both were that score. The primary concordance result considered for this study was the quadratic weighted kappa statistic, and it was interpreted following recommendations by Landis and Koch [12] as follows: below 0.0, Poor; 0.00 to 0.20, Slight; 0.21 to 0.40, Fair; 0.41 to 0.60, Moderate; 0.61 to 0.80, Substantial; 0.81 to 1.00, Almost perfect. However, the unweighted and linear weighted kappa statistics were also calculated and reported to facilitate comparison with other studies.

Results

Two hundred one participants were selected from attendees of the IBCCC program from 9th January to 5th September 2023. The average age of the participants was 51 years, with a standard deviation of 10 years, and 43.3% were over 50 years old. Twenty participants (10.0%) had a family history of breast cancer in a first-degree relative (i.e., mother, sister, or daughter). Furthermore, 196 participants (97.5%) had given birth, with none having their first child after 30. None of the participants had a history of smoking.

A significant majority reported experiencing at least one symptom (70.7%), with pain being the most common (47.8%). Therefore, screening mammograms (mammograms performed on asymptomatic patients) were conducted among 29.3% of the participants. Discussion

Of the known risk factors for breast cancer, the most prevalent were age above 50 years (43.3%), family history of breast cancer in a first-degree relative (20%), and taking alcohol (7.4%). No patients reported a history of smoking.

The most common breast composition was scattered fibroglandular density (38.3%). Sixty-nine participants (34.3%) had a mass on mammography. Thirty-four (16.9%) participants had calcifications, nine (26.5%) of whom had suspicious morphology.

Thirty-seven (18.4%) had a BI-RADS score of 4 or 5, and the quadratic weighted BI-RADS concordance was 0.58 (95% CI: 0.44–0.71).

The IBCCC program was organised so that only patients 35 years and older would receive mammography. This detail in the program’s organisation was based on a recommendation by Galukande and Kiguli-Malwadde [9] that mammography be reserved for such women because younger women typically have dense breasts, which causes the sensitivity of the investigation to fall from 63 to 95% to 35%. However, only 5.5% of participants had extremely dense breasts, thus preserving the investigation’s sensitivity for most participants.

The breast density patterns observed in the current study were comparable with the findings of a study by Galukande and Kiguli-Malwadde [13]. In that study, 5.3% of the women had extremely dense breasts, 41.1% had fat density, 26.8% had scattered fibroglandular density, and 20.5% were heterogeneously dense.

Most of the participants in the current study had normal breasts on mammography (56.7%), which was higher than in a similar study at Mulago National Referral Hospital (MNRH), a hospital setting. In that study, the authors reported that 41% of the mammograms were normal (BI-RADS 1) [14]. This difference was expected because patient referral to MNRH is usually made when pathology is highly suspected.

Eleven participants (5.5%) scored BI-RADS 0 as the radiologists determined that further imaging, such as complementary ultrasound, would be required to award an accurate reading.

18.4% of participants were categorised as BI-RADS 4 or 5, which corresponds to the conclusions “suspicion of malignancy” and “highly suggestive of malignancy”. This percentage is higher than reported in MNRH, Nigerian, and South African studies.

In the MNRH study [14], 14% of participants had BI-RADS 4 or 5-category lesions. This percentage is lower than found in the current study despite the MNRH study being conducted among hospital patients who were expected to have more advanced diseases than those attending a community outreach program.

Nevertheless, this finding can be explained by the distribution of cancer screening and diagnostic services. Most of these services are concentrated in the central region, especially Kampala [10]. Therefore, women can seek investigation of their symptoms earlier in their evolution than those from remote regions like the northern region. The attendees of the IBCCC program probably delayed longer than women in the central region typically would because they had no alternative to the program for investigating their symptoms. Moreover, this explanation is supported by a finding by Odongo et al. [15] that delaying seeking healthcare for breast cancer symptoms was significantly associated with presenting with more advanced disease.

The current study’s percentage of participants with BI-RADS 4 or 5 lesions is also higher than the 8.2% in the Nigerian study [16]. The percentage found in the Nigerian study was considerably lower, despite that study being hospital-based and using only 12.9% screening mammograms. In comparison, our study was community-based and used 29.3% screening mammograms. Therefore, it is likely that the percentage from the current study indicates a valid excess in the percentage of participants with advanced diseases over that in the Nigerian study.

In the South African Study [17], 10.8% of mammograms were categorised as BI-RADS 3 to 5. That study used 23.4% screening mammogram data compared to 29.3% in the current study. However, the lower percentage of higher BI-RADS category lesions in that study can be explained by assuming fewer delays in seeking care for breast cancer symptoms by the women in South Africa compared to those in Northern Uganda.

The percentage agreement between on-site and off-site BI-RADS categories was highest for BI-RADS 1 (68.5%) and lowest for BI-RADS 3 (12.0%). The low agreement for this indeterminate category could be due to the on-site radiologist’s use of complementary ultrasound to reclassify such lesions into BI-RADS 2 (benign, no follow-up needed) or BI-RADS 4 (requiring biopsy).

The unweighted, linear weighted, and quadratic weighted kappa statistics comparing off-site to on-site readings of the mammograms were 0.40, 0.51, and 0.58. For further discussion, the quadratic weighted kappa statistic is preferred following a comparison with the study by Redondo et al. [18]. Its interpretation was based on recommendations by Landis and Koch [12], making the obtained kappa statistic moderate. Furthermore, it was substantially better than the 0.37 (95% CI: 0.36–0.38) observed by Redondo et al. [18].

In the study by Redondo et al. [18], 100 mammograms were read by 21 radiologists with a mean of 12 years of experience. Similar to the current study, the radiologists in the Spanish study did not have access to prior mammograms.

However, the radiologists in Redondo et al.’s study were more numerous (21 versus 3) and had more years of experience (a mean of 12 versus nine). Moreover, 21 pairwise comparisons were made for each image to calculate the kappa statistic instead of the one pairwise comparison in the current study.

Notably, making more comparisons reduced the random error, as evidenced by a narrower confidence interval in the resulting statistic, but did not improve the level of agreement. However, no report is made about the environment or equipment the radiologists used when reading the mammograms, leaving room for speculation that these were not standardised for the study and, therefore, led to the lower kappa statistic in that study.

Our study could also have had a higher concordance than the one by Redondo et al. [18] because it likely had a higher percentage of participants with higher BI-RADS scores (4 and 5). Mammograms with higher BI-RADS scores are generally easier to identify because their defining features are more pronounced. Therefore, it follows that in a study with a higher proportion of participants who have higher scores, inter-observer concordance will also be higher. However, we could not determine if this was an important difference between our study and that by Redondo et al. [18], because Redondo et al. did not report the distribution of BI-RADS scores among their study participants.

The moderate agreement in the current study could be due to differences in procedures used to conduct on-site and off-site readings. For instance, the community radiologist used complementary ultrasound to adjust the BI-RADS score for equivocal cases, which was not done for the off-site readings. Additionally, the contexts differed significantly in the two settings. For instance, the on-site radiologist had to provide the reports on the same day. Such time constraints could result in fatigue errors.

The current study’s strengths include the following. It is likely the first to report descriptive mammography findings from a community setting in Uganda. Moreover, the unweighted, linear, and quadratic weighted kappa statistics have all been reported, giving the reader options for various purposes. Furthermore, the mammograms were read by at least two radiologists, with a third acting as a tiebreaker in case of disagreement between the first two.

The current study’s limitations include the use of data previously collected for the investigation of attendees of the IBCCC program, rather than prospectively collected data. Therefore, the data quality could only be as high as that of the data already in the database. We could not employ more rigorous data collection procedures, data quality assurance and control measures, or verify suspicious values, as we did not have direct access to the study participants. Additionally, the study radiologists could not use complementary ultrasound to improve the accuracy of the mammographic findings for mammograms scored BI-RADS 3. Finally, thorough image quality assessments were not performed on the mammograms used in this study.

Conclusions

Over two-thirds of participants had generally low breast density; therefore, the accuracy of mammographic readings was likely high among them. The proportion of participants with BI-RADS 4 and 5 scores was higher in the current study than in a previous hospital-based study, suggesting later presentations of women in the current study. Additionally, the current study population likely sought care later due to poorer access to breast cancer screening and diagnostic services. On-site and off-site mammographic BI-RADS scores had a moderate concordance, reflecting a significant impact on mammographic readings’ validity using differing approaches in the two settings.

Recommendations

Since the proportion of participants with BI-RADS 4 and 5 scores was so high, we recommend that the Ministry of Health conducts more community outreach programs to reduce the proportion of women whose disease is identified later in its course due to a lack of opportunities to catch it earlier. Doing so would reduce morbidity and mortality from the disease, as treatment earlier in the disease course is less complicated and more likely to be successful. Additionally, given the only moderate concordance between on-site and off-site mammographic BI-RADS scores, healthcare institutions should facilitate continuous medical education for radiologists to improve the reproducibility of mammography readings. Moreover, organisers of similar outreach programs should also aim to have settings for mammographic readings in the community that meet the standards used in institutional radiology departments. Researchers should also conduct further studies to measure intra-rater variability with radiologists in the community and in more ideal settings, isolating the impact of environmental and procedural factors on the reproducibility of results. Furthermore, outreach programs should utilise more radiologists to minimise random errors due to fatigue.
