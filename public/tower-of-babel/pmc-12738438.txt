Evaluation of the neuroprotective activity of Momordica dioica against aluminum chloride (AlCl3)-Induced alzheimer’s disease in Wistar rats
Authors: Vrushali Neve, Sayyed Saqlain, Addepalli Veeranjaneyulu, Pawan Karwa, Harshad Kapare, Ketaki Yeralkar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Alzheimer’s disease is a brain condition that slowly erodes a person’s memory and cognitive abilities. It is caused by damage to brain cell, particularly in the hippocampus, a region crucial for memory. By 2050 worldwide no. of AD is going increases. Various therapeutic strategies have been explored for AD. The use of herbal products is one of the treatment regimens for AD. In this study, we examined how a herbal extract from Momordica dioica could potentially protect Wistar rats from AD caused by Aluminum chloride. Aluminum chloride (AlCl3) is widely used in preclinical studies to induce Alzheimer-like symptoms, as chronic exposure is known to promote oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and cholinergic dysfunction—hallmarks of Alzheimer’s disease pathology. A total of 86 Wistar rats were randomly assigned to nine experimental groups (n = 6–10 per group), including a normal control group and an Alzheimer’s disease (AD) model group. One group treated with Donepezil (2.05 mg/kg), three groups treated with different doses of herbal extract of Momordica dioica (100, 200 and 400 mg/kg), and three groups treated with a combination of Donepezil and herbal extract of Momordica dioica (std + 100, std + 200 and std + 400 mg/kg). The aluminum chloride (17 mg/kg, p.o) was administered once daily for 7 days to induce AD. From the 8th day onward, the herbal extract of Momordica dioica was administered orally for 21 consecutive days at doses of 100, 200, and 400 mg/kg to groups 4, 5, and 6 respectively, as well as in combination with Donepezil (2.05 mg/kg) in groups 7, 8, and 9. This brought the total duration of the study to 28 days.” Elevated Plus Maze and Forced Swim test was used for the behavioral assessment. After that, brain samples were collected for biochemical analysis. Herbal Extract significantly improved AlCl3-induced behavioral impairments and cognition deficits in Forced Swim Test, Elevated Plus Maze Test significantly with high dose. Then, herbal extract of Momordica dioica facilitated cholinergic activity via inhibiting acetylcholinesterase (AChE) activity. Besides, herbal extract of Momordica dioica decreased lipid peroxidation level & Nitrite level but enhanced levels of glutathione, Succinate dehydrogenase, Catalase and superoxide dismutase and results are more convincing with high dose. Histopathological analysis further confirmed reduced neuronal degeneration and better preservation of brain architecture, especially at higher doses of the herbal extract.” The results suggested that herbal extract of Momordica dioica ameliorated AlCl3-induced cognitive and memory impairments, possibly through regulating AChE activity, suppressing oxidative stress. The herbal extract of Momordica dioica significantly improved behavioral impairments and cognition deficits, particularly at high doses. It facilitates cholinergic activity by inhibiting AChE activity and reduced oxidative stress. Overall, the conclusion states that the herbal extract may ameliorate and memory impairments associated with AD.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a neurodegenerative condition that worsens over time and is linked to brain neuronal loss, which impairs memory or causes cognitive function loss [1]. The main cause of AD is age; people above the age of 65 tend to be more susceptible to developing AD. It is estimated that number of people affected with AD will reach up to 106.8 million by the year 2050 [2]. The initial signs and symptoms of AD are short-term memory loss and with the progression of the disease, it gets worse leading to cognitive and learning disability. Later on, the signs developed are mood swings, inability to perform daily activities such as calculations, forgetting names and confusion, difficulty in concentrating, and inability to create new memories [3, 4]. The mechanism involved in AD is there is formation of amyloid protein plaques which causes neuronal loss of neurons and there are observed inflammatory markers, and increased oxidative stress markers [2]. The worldwide prevalence rate of AD is about 25 million people who are currently affected by dementia, and most of them are suffering from AD. It is estimated that around 5 million cases may occur every year. AD meta-analyses have been used to investigate a wide range of topics, including Genetic risk factors, Environmental risk factors, and Diagnostic and therapeutic interventions [1–3, 5]. Some of the specific examples of recent meta-analysis on AD are: A 2019 meta-analysis involving 9,751 samples identified four novel risk loci for Alzheimer’s disease (AD), in addition to the well-established APOE gene [6]. Similarly, a 2021 meta-analysis of epigenome-wide association studies (EWAS) highlighted several differentially methylated regions in the brain associated with AD [7]. Furthermore, a 2023 meta-analysis of prospective studies suggested that physical activity in older adults may reduce the risk of developing AD [8]. Aluminum Chloride (AlCl3) is used as a potential inducer of AD in laboratory studies. However, it is important to understand that there is no conclusive evidence that aluminum exposure causes Alzheimer’s disease in humans. It’s been proven in lab tests that exposure to AlCl3 causes the development of amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles in rat and mice brains, which are some of the characteristics of Alzheimer’s disease. Aluminum chloride (AlCl3) is widely used in preclinical studies to induce neurotoxicity and cognitive deficits that mimic certain aspects of Alzheimer’s disease, including oxidative stress and cholinergic dysfunction. However, these studies typically use very high dose of aluminum chloride, which are much higher than the levels humans are typically exposed to through food, water or other environmental sources. It is important to acknowledge that acute oral administration of AlCl3 may not induce the full spectrum of Alzheimer’s disease pathology, such as amyloid plaque deposition and tau tangles, observed in human cases. However, repeated administration for 7 days has been reported to cause neurobehavioral impairments, oxidative stress, and cholinergic dysfunction sufficient for use as a validated experimental model for evaluating the neuroprotective potential of test compounds [9]. Therefore, while this model has its limitations, it remains a commonly accepted method for preliminary screening of anti-Alzheimer agents. While some studies have hinted as a potential connection between aluminum exposed and AD, human research has not definitively confirmed this link. The relationship remains uncertain, and further investigations are necessary to understand if aluminum exposure contributed to the development of this condition in humans [10–12].The study aims to explore the neuroprotective effects of Momordica dioica in aluminum chloride (AlCl3) induced Alzheimer’s disease (AD). Additionally, the study evaluated behavioral, cognitive, and emotional impairments associated with AD progression to provide insights into the broader implications of aluminum exposure and its relevance to AD pathology.

Momordicadioica

Momordicadioica, also known as spiny gourd, kakara, and parora, is a long-lived, climbing vine belonging to the gourd family. It blooms in June and July and bears fruit from September to November. The plants leaves are simple and membranous, with three to five triangular lobes. The male flowers are solitary, yellow and up to 2.8 cm long. The female flowers are also solitary, and have soft spines on their green and yellow fruit. The seeds are round enclosed in red pulp. The stems are slender and branched, with simple tendrils. This climbing vine is found throughout India, Pakistan, the Himalayas, and Sri Lanka, growing up to 1500 m in altitude. It originated in the Indo-Malayan region. Momordica dioica, commonly known as spiny gourd or teasle gourd, is a climber species renowned for its extensive medicinal properties. This plant has demonstrated significant antioxidant activity due to its rich phytochemical profile, including phenolics, flavonoids, and vitamin C. Studies also highlight its potent anti-inflammatory effects, which are attributed to its ability to inhibit pro-inflammatory mediators like prostaglandins and cytokines. Additionally, Momordica dioica exhibits wound-healing properties, enhancing tissue regeneration and collagen synthesis, making it a promising candidate for therapeutic applications in skin injuries. These properties underline its potential in treating oxidative stress-related disorders, chronic inflammation, and facilitating recovery from wounds. Materials and methods

Chemicals

AlCl3was used from the laboratory, Donepezil drug was purchased from the market. Methanol, Carboxy Methyl Cellulose (CMC), Sodium Chloride was used from the laboratory. The fruits of Momordiadioica were purchased from local market form the local vendor.

Drug preparation: Aluminum chloride (AlCl3) was freshly prepared each day by dissolving it in distilled water. Donepezil was suspended in 0.25% sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (Na-CMC) to ensure uniform oral dosing. Both were administered orally at appropriate concentrations based on body weight.

Animals

The study was performed on Wistar rats (obtained from Crystal Biological Solutions, India) weighing between 180 and 240 g at the start of the experiment. These rats were be housed in groups of three in standard laboratory cages under the controlled conditions (12-h light/dark cycle, temperature of 22 ± 5 degrees Celsius, and humidity of 50–70%).They were given unlimited access to food and water and were allowed to adjust to their new environment for at least one week before the experiment begins.

Plant collection and extraction

The fruits of Momordica dioica were collected from the local market from the local vendor. It was authenticated by Botanical Survey of India, Pune with herbarium number ASAA2. These fruits were chopped and air dried in direct sunlight. After total drying the dried fruits, seeds were separated, and the remaining part was grinded using the grinder into the fine powder. The powder was then macerated in the methanol for 48 h, in order to obtain the methanolic extract. Methanol is used as a solvent because the extract is more soluble in the polar solvent and 80% methanol is the best polar organic solvent. The methanolic extract of Momordica dioica was found to be non-toxic based on the acute oral toxicity studies. Then the methanolic extract was filtered and the filtrate was heated and stirred with help of magnetic stirrer. After stirring thick paste like material was obtained, this was used for the dosing of animals dissolving it in the CMC solution as a carrier. Animal grouping

Animals in each grouped were monitored daily for 28 days, during which their physical parameters (food and water intake, body weight) and behavioral parameters (Forced swim test, Elevated plus Maze) were assessed. After the treatment period, the animals were euthanized using carbon dioxide, a method commonly employed for terminal procedures. However, we acknowledge that CO2 may affect neurochemical profiles; therefore, in future investigations involving sensitive neurochemical endpoints, alternative methods such as decapitation under anesthesia will be employed to minimize bias. Behavioral analyses

The first behavioral test, the Forced Swim Test, evaluated the animals swimming ability and how long they remained immobile/ the second test, the Elevated Plus Maze, measured anxiety levels by observing how much time the rodents spent in the open arms of a cross-shaped maze raised above the ground. Since rodents generally prefer enclosed spaces a shorter time in the open arms suggests higher anxiety levels. The Elevated Plus Maze and Forced Swim Test are used in AD research to assess anxiety- and depression-like symptoms that are highly relevant to the disease’s pathology and progression. These tests complement memory and cognitive assessments, providing a more comprehensive evaluation of AD-related behavioral changes.

Biochemical parameters

All the animals was euthanized using the euthanasia chamber, and their brains were carefully removed through a process called perfusion to prevent blood clots and excess blood from remaining in the brain sample. The isolated brains were then rinsed wit ice-cold phosphate buffer solution to eliminate any residual blood and stored at -80℃. To prepare the brain homogenate, a tissue homogenizer was used with 0.1 M phosphate buffer at pH 7.4. The homogenate was subsequently centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 20 min to obtain the supernatant. Some of the assays used to assess oxidative stress are SOD, MDA and CAT. SOD (Superoxide Dismutase) measures the activity of antioxidant enzymes that break down harmful reactive oxygen species (ROS) called superoxide radicals. Higher SOD activity indicates stronger antioxidant defense. MDA (Malondialdehyde) Assay measures the level of malondialdehyde, a byproduct of lipid per-oxidation, which is damage caused by ROS to cell membranes. Higher MDA indicates increased oxidative stress. CAT (Catalase) Assay measures the activity of another antioxidant enzyme, catalase, which breaks down hydrogen peroxide, another type of ROS. Higher CAT activity indicates increased capacity to neutralize ROS.

Estimation of acetylcholinesterase activity

The assay mixture consisted of 13.1 µl of supernatant, 145 µl of sodium phosphate buffer with a Ph of 8, and 5 µl each of acetylcholine iodide and DTNB (Ellman reagent). The change in absorbance was monitored for 10 min at 5 s intervals at 412 nm using a micro plate reader. The outcomes were reported as micromoles of acetylcholine iodide hydrolyzed per minute per milligram of protein. Estimation of antioxidant enzymes

Superoxide dismutase (SOD): SOD activity was determined by the method of Marklund and Marklund (1974), based on the inhibition of pyrogallol autoxidation. The results were expressed as units/mg protein, where one unit of SOD activity is defined as the amount of enzyme required to inhibit the rate of pyrogallol autoxidation by 50%.

Catalase (CAT): Catalase activity was measured using the method described by Aebi (1984), by monitoring the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide at 240 nm. The activity was expressed as μmol of H2O2 decomposed/min/mg protein.

Reduced glutathione (GSH): GSH levels were estimated using Ellman’s reagent (DTNB), forming a yellow-colored complex measured at 412 nm. The results were expressed as μmol GSH/mg protein.

Discussion

Our study highlights the promising potential of herbal extract of Momordica dioica in mitigating cognitive deficits and promoting neuronal health in the context of Alzheimer’s disease (AD). Aluminum chloride (AlCl3) is known to impair cognitive functions, particularly spatial learning and memory. Administering AlCl3 for 7 days effectively induced AD-like symptoms, allowing us to evaluate the herbal extract’s neuroprotective effects against AlCl3-induced damage. Behavioral tests, such as the FST and EPM, showed that rats treated with a herbal extract at a dose of 400 mg/kg had significantly improved spatial memory and exploratory behavior. While the Morris Water Maze (MWM) is considered a gold standard for assessing spatial learning and memory, we selected the Elevated Plus Maze (EPM) and Forced Swim Test (FST) based on their established utility in evaluating anxiety-like behavior, depressive symptoms, and cognitive-affective responses relevant to AD pathology. EPM, specifically through transfer latency, can reflect hippocampal-dependent learning, and FST provides insight into motivational and emotional impairments often associated with neurodegenerative disorders. Future studies incorporating the MWM may further validate and strengthen these findings. The herbal extract significantly reduced the escape latency period (p < 0.0001) and exploration time (p < 0.0001) compared to AlCl3 treated group, suggesting enhanced cognitive function. Biochemical analysis showed the herbal extract reduced malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, indicating lower lipid peroxidation, and decreased nitrite levels, suggesting reduced oxidative stress. The extract also reversed the AlCl3-induced increase in acetylcholinesterase (AChE) levels (p < 0.05), particularly at a dose of 400 mg/kg, which is crucial for improving cholinergic function. In addition to its cholinesterase inhibitory role, Donepezil has been shown to exert indirect antioxidant effects. By increasing synaptic acetylcholine levels, Donepezil activates intracellular signaling pathways such as PI3K/Akt and MAPK, which enhance the expression of endogenous antioxidant enzymes including SOD, CAT, and GSH. Moreover, Donepezil contributes to mitochondrial stability, thereby minimizing ROS production and lipid peroxidation. These effects collectively result in reduced MDA levels and restored antioxidant defense, as previously reported in aluminum chloride-induced AD models [22–24]. Our findings are consistent with these reports, suggesting that Donepezil not only improves cholinergic function but also confers neuroprotection through redox regulation. The herbal extract of Momordica dioica significantly boosted the activity of antioxidant enzymes catalase (CAT), Glutathione (GSH) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) in rats compared to those treated with AlCl3. This suggests that the H > E is effective in reducing oxidative stress. Histopathological examination supported these findings, showing reduced neuronal and vascular degeneration and the absence of glial cell infiltration in the treatment groups. Additionally, Image J analysis indicated a higher viable cell count in the treatment groups compared to the AlCl3-only group, underscoring the extract’s protective properties against neurodegeneration. Overall, herbal extract of Momordica dioica exhibits significant neuroprotective effects, improving cognitive function and reducing oxidative stress and neuronal damage in an AlCl3-induced AD model. These findings suggest that herbal extract of Momordica dioica. could be a valuable therapeutic agent for neurodegenerative disorders like Alzheimer’s disease. While the current study utilized Elevated Plus Maze and Forced Swim Test primarily to assess anxiety-like and depression-like behaviors, it is important to note that these assays do not directly evaluate the primary cognitive deficits characteristic of Alzheimer’s disease, such as spatial memory loss. Future studies incorporating tests like the Morris Water Maze or Novel Object Recognition would provide more robust insights into the cognitive effects of Momordica dioica in AD models.”

Conclusion

The discussion above underscores the promising potential of our test drug as an alternative therapy for Alzheimer’s disease. It exhibited significant anti-Alzheimer activity by enhancing learning and memory functions, restoring antioxidant levels, and reducing elevated acetylcholine esterase (AChE) levels induced by AlCl3 administration. Moreover, histopathological analysis revealed that our herbal extract contributed to neuroregeneration and exhibited anti-apoptotic activity, as evidenced by the absence of severe neuronal degeneration and glial cell infiltration, along with improved viable cell count as confirmed by Image J analysis. Taking into consideration these comprehensive findings, it can be inferred that the polyherbal tablets hold promise as a viable therapeutic option for managing Alzheimer’s disease.
