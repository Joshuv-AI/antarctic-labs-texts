Seismic Oscillations Excited by Giant Impacts in Directly-Imaged Giant Planets
J. J. Zanazzi, Eugene Chiang, Yifan Zhou

Abstract
The radii and masses of many giant exoplanets imply their interiors each contain more than ∼ \sim 100 M ⊕ M_{\oplus} of solids. A large metal content may arise when a giant planet grows by colliding and merging with multiple ∼ \sim 10 M ⊕ M_{\oplus} solid cores. Here we show that a giant impact with a young gas giant excites long-lived seismic oscillations that can be detected photometrically. Mode lifetimes are close to the planet’s Kelvin-Helmholtz time, a significant fraction of a young planet’s age. Oscillation periods lie between tens of minutes to an hour, and variability amplitudes can exceed a percent for several million years. Beta Pictoris b is a young super-Jupiter known to be highly metal-enriched. If a Neptune-mass (17 M ⊕ M_{\oplus} ) body impacted β \beta Pictoris b in the past ∼ \sim 9–18 Myr, the planet could still be ringing with a percent-level photometric variability measurable with JWST.



I
Introduction

Extrasolar gas giants are often metal-enriched. Measurements of a giant planet’s mass and radius can be used to infer its heavy-metal mass fraction
Z_{\rm p}
.
Thorngren et al. 2016
has shown
Z_{\rm p}
often exceeds the metallicity of the host star
Z_{\star}
by large factors.
Giant planet heavy metal masses can exceed
\sim
100
M_{\oplus}
, much larger than the critical core masses of
\sim
5-20
M_{\oplus}
needed to nucleate giant planet formation. Later works have found similar correlations in the composition of gas giants with the planet’s mass, using either constraints on the planet’s bulk density, or atmospheric retrievals
(
Thorngren & Fortney 2019
;
Teske et al. 2019
;
Welbanks et al. 2019
;
Müller et al. 2020
;
Nikolov et al. 2022
;
Bean et al. 2023
;
Sun et al. 2024
;
Wang 2025
, e.g.)
.
Thorngren et al. 2016
posited this correlation arose from the preferential accretion of dust grains or planetesimals drifting through gas in the protoplanetary disk (see also e.g.
Hasegawa et al. 2018
;
Hasegawa et al. 2019
;
Shibata & Ikoma 2019
;
Shibata et al. 2020
;
Venturini & Helled 2020
;
Schneider & Bitsch 2021
;
Knierim et al. 2022
;
Morbidelli et al. 2023
;
Danti et al. 2023
;
Bitsch & Mah 2023
). Alternatively,
Ginzburg & Chiang 2020
showed that collisions between multiple protoplanet cores (‘major mergers’), each undergoing runaway gas accretion, would enrich the resultant gas giant in metals and produce a
Z_{\rm p}/Z_{\star}
ratio that decreases with
M_{\rm p}
, as observed (see also
Dawson & Murray-Clay 2013
;
Frelikh et al. 2019
;
Ogihara et al. 2021
). These two theories give competing visions for how giant planets acquire their metallicities: the first by quiescently accreting disk solids which slip into the gas gap opened by the planet, and the other by violently engulfing nearby protoplanets.

Solar System giants experience collisions large and small. The large obliquity of Uranus, as well as the planet’s rings and regular moons, could be the result of a giant impact
(
Kegerreis et al. 2018
;
Reinhardt et al. 2020
, e.g.)
. It has been proposed that the dilute core of Jupiter arose after an impact with a
10M_{\oplus}
body
(
Liu et al. 2019
, but see also
Sandnes et al. 2024
;
Meier et al. 2025
). On a much smaller scale, the comet Shoemaker-Levy 9 (SL9) struck Jupiter in 1994.
Several papers pointed out the SL9 impact would excite a spectrum of normal mode oscillations
(
Kanamori 1993
;
Marley 1994
;
Lee & van Horn 1994
;
Lognonne et al. 1994
)
. The resultant atmospheric temperature variations proved too small to be detected in the mid-infrared by the 3.6-meter telescope at the European Southern Observatory
(
Dombard & Boughn 1995
;
Mosser et al. 1996
)
. Seismic oscillations in Jupiter (too large to be excited by SL9) were eventually detected in 2011 by a team at the Nice Observatory, using radial velocity measurements gathered by the SYMPA Fourier spectro-imager
(
Schmider et al. 2007
;
Gaulme et al. 2008
;
Gaulme et al. 2011
)
. Saturn’s normal modes excite density waves within the planet’s rings
(
Marley 1991
;
Marley & Porco 1993
;
Marley 2014
, e.g.)
. A number of works
(
Hedman & Nicholson 2013
;
Hedman & Nicholson 2014
;
French et al. 2016
;
French et al. 2019
;
Hedman et al. 2019
)
used Cassini data on these waves as a seismograph to measure the oscillation amplitudes and frequencies of Saturn’s normal modes, inferring stable stratification deep within Saturn’s interior
(
Fuller 2014
;
Mankovich & Fuller 2021
)
. A mystery for both Jupiter and Saturn is how their oscillations are excited.
Markham & Stevenson 2018
favor rock storms deep in Jupiter’s atmosphere, while
Wu & Lithwick 2019
advocate collisions to excite the spectrum of
f
-modes and
p
-modes seen in Saturn.

In principle, planet-scale impacts could excite seismic oscillations in directly imaged exoplanets, which could be detected by space-based missions such as JWST and Roman. Directly imaged exoplanets tend to be young (
\lesssim 20
Myr), massive (
\gtrsim 10M_{\rm Jup}
), bright, and embedded in either protoplanetary disks
(
Keppler et al. 2018
;
Haffert et al. 2019
;
Wang et al. 2020
, PDS 70 bc,)
or debris disks (
\beta
Pic b, e.g.
Lagrange et al. 2009
;
Currie et al. 2013
;
Wang et al. 2016
;
Lagrange et al. 2019
; and HR 8799 bcde,
Marois et al. 2008
;
GRAVITY Collaboration et al. 2019
;
Faramaz et al. 2021
). Such planets could have undergone major mergers in the past few million years and had their normal modes excited to large amplitude.

In this paper, we take as a test case
\beta
Pictoris b (
\beta
Pic b). The
13M_{\rm Jup}
planet and its birth environment suggest it may have suffered multiple mergers with other massive protoplanets. Atmospheric retrievals indicate the planet’s heavy-metal mass lies between
\sim
100-300 Earth masses
(
GRAVITY Collaboration et al. 2020
;
Wang 2025
, e.g.)
. The planet’s eccentricity of
\sim
0.15
(
GRAVITY Collaboration et al. 2020
)
might have been excited by an impact with a body originating from the system’s dynamically active debris disk
(
Dawson et al. 2011
;
Rebollido et al. 2024
, e.g.)
. The young age of the
\beta
Pic moving group (
\sim
20 Myr, e.g.
Mamajek & Bell 2014
) implies that if an impact occurred, normal modes would not have much time to damp. And given the planet’s luminosity and large separation from its host star, JWST photometry might be able to detect seismic oscillations. Section
II
reviews the physics behind giant planet
f
-mode and
p
-mode oscillations. Section
III
calculates the amplitudes of normal modes excited by a major merger. Section
IV
shows that mode damping timescales from internal friction are of order the Kelvin-Helmholtz time, so oscillations can survive a significant fraction of the planet’s age. Section
V
shows JWST observations might detect the luminosity variations from these seismic oscillations. Section
VI
provides a summary, and discusses future avenues of investigation for exoplanet systems.

Giant planets support both fundamental (
f
-mode) and pressure (
p
-mode) oscillations, for which the main restoring force is pressure. The Lagrangian perturbation to the temperature for each
f
-mode and
p
-mode can be decomposed into spherical harmonics
\Delta T/T\propto Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\varphi)
. When
the oscillation is axisymmetric (azimuthal number
m=0
), the angular degree
\ell
equals the number of latitudinal (
\theta
) nodes in
\Delta T/T
(Fig.
1
). For a given
\ell
, the mode’s node number
n
counts the radial nodes in the function

\mathcal{Y}_{1}=\left(1-\frac{\rho}{\rho_{\rm av}}\right)\frac{\xi_{r}}{r}+\frac{1}{3g}\left(\frac{\delta\Phi}{r}-\frac{{\rm d}\delta\Phi}{{\rm d}r}\right),

(1)

excepting the origin, where
\delta\Phi
is the Eulerian perturbation to the gravitational potential,
g=GM_{r}/r^{2}
is the gravitational acceleration,
M_{r}=4\pi\int_{0}^{r}\rho r^{2}{\rm d}r
is total mass enclosed within radius
r
, and
\rho_{\rm av}=\frac{3M_{r}}{4\pi r^{3}}

(see
Takata 2006
, for details)
. The
f
-mode has
n=0
nodes in the
radial displacement
\xi_{r}
(since
\delta\Phi
is negligible), and has the lowest frequency
\omega
, while all
p
-modes have
n>0
, and frequencies
\omega
that increase with
n
(Figs.
2
and
3
). Fundamental modes do not exist for
\ell=0,1
.
In all calculations below, we use a
MESA
model for a super-Jupiter similar to
\beta
Pic b, with mass
M_{\rm p}=13\ M_{\rm Jup}
, radius
R_{\rm p}=1.4\ R_{\rm Jup}
, effective temperature
T_{\rm eff}=1720\ {\rm K}
, luminosity
L_{\rm p}=1.6\times 10^{-4}\ L_{\odot}
, and age
18\ {\rm Myr}
, and calculate oscillations using the
GYRE
code, with inlists given in Appendix
A
. For simplicity, we ignore how a solid core affects the oscillation spectrum, which could alter the
f
-mode and
p
-mode frequencies by several percent
(
Le Bihan & Burrows 2013
;
Fuller et al. 2014
, e.g.)
.

We define
t_{\rm th}
as the thermal time of a shell of thickness equal to the local pressure scale height
H_{P}=-({\rm d}\ln P/{\rm d}r)^{-1}
:

t_{\rm th}=\frac{4\pi r^{2}H_{P}\rho C_{P}T}{L},

(2)

where
\rho
is the density,
C_{P}
the specific heat at constant pressure,
T
the temperature,
and
L
the local luminosity
(radiative and convective).
Comparing
t_{\rm th}
to the oscillation frequency
\omega
of the
(\ell,n)=(0,1)

p
-mode in Fig.
4
(top panel), we see
t_{\rm th}\omega\gg 1
.
We conclude that this mode is approximately adiabatic everywhere (e.g.
Unno et al. 1989
;
Pfahl et al. 2008
).
Since all
f
and
p
-modes have frequencies comparable to or larger than the
(\ell,n)=(0,1)

p
-mode (Fig.
3
), these other oscillations are nearly adiabatic as well.

The luminosity emergent from the surface of the planet is carried by radiation (Fig.
4
, top panel).
Both
f
-mode and
p
-mode oscillations perturb the radiative luminosity

L_{\rm rad}=-\frac{4\pi r^{2}c}{3\kappa\rho}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}r}(aT^{4}),

(3)

where
\kappa
is the opacity,
c
the speed of light, and
a
the radiation constant.
When the oscillation is nearly adiabatic, the Lagrangian perturbations to the temperature
\Delta T/T
, density
\Delta\rho/\rho
, and opacity
\Delta\kappa/\kappa
all scale with the perturbation pressure
\Delta P/P
.
Because the pressure (and hence temperature) perturbation is nearly constant near the photosphere,

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta L}{L_{\rm rad}}

\displaystyle\approx\left(4\frac{\Delta T}{T}-\frac{\Delta\kappa}{\kappa}-\frac{\Delta\rho}{\rho}\right)\sim\frac{\Delta P}{P}.

(4)

When the pressure perturbation rises, the mode causes larger luminosity fluctuations. We note that our pressure perturbation does not vanish at the surface. This is because we match our solutions to that of a pressure wave propagating through an isothermal atmosphere
(
Unno et al. 1989
;
Christensen-Dalsgaard 2008
, e.g.)
, using the
\texttt{outer\_bound}=\texttt{JCD}
option in
GYRE
(App.
A
). See Appendix
C
for further discussion of modes above the photosphere.

III
Mode Amplitudes

An impactor impulsively imparts momentum near the surface of the planet. Because pressure modes with a large number of radial nodes have large amplitudes near the surface (Fig.
2
), impacts preferentially excite high-frequency oscillations. Oscillation amplitudes that are too high will result in shocks.
The goal of this section is to estimate the oscillation amplitudes of planetary normal modes excited by the impactor’s momentum, limited by
shocks.

.

A giant planet’s displacement from equilibrium
{\bm{\xi}}(r,\theta,\varphi,t)
may be decomposed into eigenmodes
\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}_{\ell mn}(r,\theta,\varphi){\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\omega t}
projected onto vector spherical harmonics, with radial displacement
\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}_{r,\ell mn}(r,\theta,\varphi)=\xi_{r,\ell mn}(r)Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\varphi){\bm{\hat{e}}}_{r}
and horizontal displacement
{\bm{\xi}}_{\perp,\ell mn}(r,\theta,\varphi)=\xi_{\perp,\ell mn}r{\bm{\nabla}}Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\varphi)
, multiplied by a dimensionless, time-dependent coefficient

c_{\ell mn}(t)
, i.e.

\displaystyle{\bm{\xi}}(r,\theta,\varphi,t)=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}\sum_{n}c_{\ell mn}(t)\big[\widetilde{\xi}_{r,\ell mn}(r)Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\varphi){\bm{\hat{e}}}_{r}

\displaystyle+\widetilde{\xi}_{\perp,\ell mn}(r)r{\bm{\nabla}}Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\varphi)\big].

(5)

Each oscillation causes Lagrangian perturbations to scalar quantities
X
(temperature, pressure, density, etc.) proportional to

\Delta X(r,\theta,\varphi,t)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}\sum_{n}c_{\ell mn}(t)\Delta\widetilde{X}_{\ell mn}(r)Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\varphi).

(6)

Note that while
\Delta X\propto c_{\ell mn}
, the same proportionality does not apply to
\Delta\widetilde{X}_{\ell mn}
; all tilde-variables, including
\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}_{\ell mn}
, should be thought of as “unit basis vectors”. Individual
\ell,m,n
modes (and their complex conjugates) have energies
\varepsilon=2\omega^{2}|c_{\ell mn}|^{2}\langle\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}_{\ell mn}|\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}_{\ell mn}\rangle
, where the notation
\langle{\bm{u}}|{\bm{v}}\rangle=\int{\bm{u}}^{*}{\bm{\cdot}}{\bm{v}}\rho{\rm d}V

(
Schenk et al. 2001
, e.g.)
. We normalize our eigenvectors
\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}_{\ell mn}
so that

\displaystyle E_{\rm p}=\frac{GM_{\rm p}^{2}}{R_{\rm p}}=2\omega^{2}\langle\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}_{\ell mn}|\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}_{\ell mn}\rangle

\displaystyle=2\omega^{2}\int_{0}^{R_{\rm p}}\big[|\widetilde{\xi}_{r,\ell mn}|^{2}+\ell(\ell+1)|\widetilde{\xi}_{\perp,\ell mn}|^{2}\big]\rho r^{2}{\rm d}r

(7)

From here on, we drop the
\ell,m,n
subscripts on
\widetilde{\xi}_{r}
,
\widetilde{\xi}_{\perp}
and
c
, and displacement vectors will always refer to a vector spherical harmonic (
\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}_{r}=\widetilde{\xi}_{r}Y_{\ell m}{\bm{\hat{e}}}_{r}
,
\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}_{\perp}=\widetilde{\xi}_{\perp}r{\bm{\nabla}}Y_{\ell m}
). Because the planet’s rotation frequency
\Omega_{\rm p}\ll\omega_{\rm dyn}=(GM_{\rm p}/R_{\rm p}^{3})^{1/2}\lesssim\omega
for
f
-modes and
p
-modes, we will neglect Coriolis forces
(
Lai 2021
;
Dewberry et al. 2021
;
Dewberry et al. 2022
;
Dewberry & Lai 2022
;
Lin 2023
, e.g.)
.

In what follows, we quantitatively calculate mode amplitudes after a giant impact. Section
III.1
calculates oscillations excited by the impact’s momentum
c_{\rm mom}
, while Section
III.2
calculates the maximum oscillation amplitude
c_{\rm max}
before acoustic waves undergo shocks (Sec.
III.1
). The value of
c
directly after a collision is

c=\min(c_{\rm mom},c_{\rm max}).

(8)

Heat imparted by the collision can also drive oscillations, but the dynamics are more sensitive to the uncertain thermal properties of young planets. We provide optimistically large estimates for mode amplitudes excited by impactor heating in Appendix
B
, and find them to be comparable in magnitude to those excited by the impactor’s momentum
(
Dombard & Boughn 1995
, see also)
.

III.1
Momentum Imparted After Impact

When a mode is externally driven by a force per unit mass
{\bm{f}}
, its amplitude evolves as

\dot{c}+{\rm i}\omega c+\gamma c=\frac{{\rm i}\omega}{E_{\rm p}}\langle\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}|{\bm{f}}\rangle,

(9)

where
\gamma>0
is the mode damping rate
(
Schenk et al. 2001
;
Lai & Wu 2006
, e.g.)
. We assume a body with mass
m_{\rm imp}
and velocity
v_{\rm esc}=\sqrt{2GM_{\rm p}/R_{\rm p}}
impulsively imparts momentum in the radial direction, with a force per unit volume

\rho f_{\rm mom}=-m_{\rm imp}v_{\rm esc}\delta^{(3)}({\bm{r}}-{\bm{r}}_{\rm imp})\delta(t){\bm{\hat{e}}}_{r},

(10)

where
\delta^{(3)}({\bm{r}})
is a 3-dimensional
\delta
-function, and
\delta(t)
is a 1-dimensional
\delta
-function
(
Lognonne et al. 1994
;
Dombard & Boughn 1995
;
Wu & Lithwick 2019
, e.g.)
. Plugging equation (
10
) into (
9
), we have

\displaystyle\langle\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}|{\bm{f}}_{\rm mom}\rangle=-m_{\rm imp}v_{\rm esc}\delta(t)\widetilde{\xi}_{r}(R_{\rm p})Y_{\ell m}(\theta_{\rm imp},\varphi_{\rm imp})

(11)

where (
\theta_{\rm imp}
,
\varphi_{\rm imp}
) are the latitudinal and azimuthal coordinates of the impact. If we take the impact to occur at the pole (
\theta_{\rm imp}=0
; this can be assumed without loss of generality when the planet’s rotation is negligible), the mode’s amplitude just after impact is

c_{\rm mom}=-{\rm i}\frac{m_{\rm imp}v_{\rm esc}\omega}{E_{\rm p}}\widetilde{\xi}_{r}(R_{\rm p})\sqrt{\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}}.

(12)

The factor
\sqrt{(2\ell+1)/(4\pi)}
comes from evaluating
Y_{\ell 0}|_{\theta_{\rm imp}=0}
, and
\widetilde{\xi}_{r}(R_{\rm p})
is determined after normalizing the oscillation using equation (
7
).
Equation (
12
) implies that
c_{\rm mom}
is linearly proportional to the mass of the impactor,
m_{\rm imp}
.
Because larger
\ell
and
n
modes have less inertia,
|c_{\rm mom}|
increases with the angular degree and the number of radial nodes of a mode (Fig.
5
top panel).

From here on, we will only consider zonal, axisymmetric oscillations (
m=0
).

III.2
Shock Amplitude Limit

When propagating through a medium with sound-speed
c_{\rm s}
, sound waves
develop into shocks
when their velocities
|{\bm{v}}_{\rm mode}|\approx\omega|{\bm{\xi}}|\gtrsim c_{\rm s}
. Because sound waves have radial wave-numbers
k_{r}\approx\omega/c_{\rm s}

(
Unno et al. 1989
, e.g.)
, the shock condition translates to

|{\bm{k}}{\bm{\cdot}}{\bm{\xi}}|\approx\frac{\partial}{\partial r}({\bm{\hat{e}}}_{r}{\bm{\cdot}}{\bm{\xi}})\gtrsim 1.

(13)

Therefore, the maximum amplitude that modes can reach before they shock is given by

c_{\rm max}\approx\left|\frac{{\rm d}\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}_{r}}{{\rm d}r}\right|^{-1}_{\rm max}\approx\sqrt{\frac{4\pi}{2\ell+1}}\left|\frac{{\rm d}\widetilde{\xi}_{r}}{{\rm d}r}\right|^{-1}_{\rm max},

(14)

where
|\dots|_{\rm max}
is the maximum amplitude over
r
, and the factor of
\sqrt{4\pi/(2\ell+1)}
comes from evaluating
\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}_{r}|_{\theta_{\rm imp}=0}=\widetilde{\xi}_{r}Y_{\ell m}|_{\theta_{\rm imp}=0}
. The magnitude of
|c_{\rm max}|
decreases with both
\ell
and
n
(Fig.
5
bottom panel), because these modes are more compressional and have higher
|{\rm d}\widetilde{\xi}_{r}/{\rm d}r|
.

Once excited, normal modes damp from internal friction within the giant planet. Here, we calculate the linear damping rates

for

and
p
-modes from radiative losses and turbulent convection. In agreement with
Wu & Lithwick 2019
, we find both processes to be sufficiently weak that the longest period

-modes decay over the global Kelvin-Helmholtz timescale
\gamma^{-1}\sim E_{\rm p}/L\sim 10\ {\rm Myr}
(Fig.
6
). We note, however, that our linear damping rates should be taken as lower bounds, because the steep profiles for many normal modes are such that

|\partial{\bm{\xi}}_{r}/\partial r|\sim 10^{-2}-1
after the merger, implying non-linear effects could be important (Fig.

; see e.g.
Kumar & Goldreich 1989
;
Kumar et al. 1994
;
Kumar & Goodman 1996
;
MacLeod et al. 2022
).

IV.1
Radiative Damping

As the planet oscillates, radiation slowly saps
f
and
p
-modes of their energy. The mode perturbs the radiative flux
{\bm{F}}_{\rm rad}
, causing the Lagrangian perturbation to the entropy
\Delta S
to be non-zero:

T\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Delta S=-\Delta\left(\frac{1}{\rho}{\bm{\nabla}}{\bm{\cdot}}{\bm{F}}_{\rm rad}\right).

(15)

When the thermal time
t_{\rm th}
is much longer than
\omega^{-1}
,
\Delta S
can be calculated perturbatively, in the so-called ‘quasi-adiabatic approximation’
(
Unno et al. 1989
)
. To leading order, density, temperature, and opacity variations are adiabatic, and proportional to the pressure perturbation:

\frac{\Delta T}{T},\frac{\Delta\rho}{\rho},\frac{\Delta\kappa}{\kappa}\propto\frac{\Delta P}{P}.

(16)

Adiabatic variations in
\Delta P/P
can then be used to calculate the mode’s luminosity variation, by taking the Lagrangian perturbation
\Delta L
of equation (
3
). The variation in luminosity is then inserted into equation (
15
), to find
\Delta S
to leading order.

The mode energy decreases from
radiative diffusion over the volume of the planet,
and from work done by the oscillation at the planet’s photosphere (above which radiation freely streams):

\displaystyle\dot{\varepsilon}_{\rm rad}=\int\int_{r=0}^{R_{\rm p}}\left(\Delta T^{*}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Delta S+\Delta T\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Delta S^{*}\right)\rho r^{2}{\rm d}r\,{\rm d}\Omega

\displaystyle+\left[\int\left(\Delta P^{*}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}{\bm{\xi}}+\Delta P\frac{\partial}{\partial t}{\bm{\xi}}^{*}\right){\bm{\cdot}}{\bm{\hat{e}}}_{r}\,r^{2}{\rm d}\Omega\right]_{r=R_{\rm p}}

\displaystyle=2\Re\left(\int\int_{r=0}^{R_{\rm p}}\Delta T^{*}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Delta S\,\rho r^{2}{\rm d}r\,{\rm d}\Omega\right)

\displaystyle-2\Re\left[\int\Delta P^{*}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}{\bm{\xi}}\right){\bm{\cdot}}{\bm{\hat{e}}}_{r}\,r^{2}{\rm d}\Omega\right]_{r=R_{\rm p}}\equiv\dot{\varepsilon}_{\rm diff}+\dot{\varepsilon}_{\rm photo}.

(17)

We set the photospheric radius
R_{\rm p}
to be where
\tau=2/3
. In
\dot{\varepsilon}_{\rm rad}
, each mode is added to its complex conjugate.
Because the damping rate
|c(t)|\propto{\rm e}^{-\gamma t}
is related to the change in mode energy by
\dot{\varepsilon}=2\gamma\varepsilon
, the damping rates due to diffusion in the interior and “leakage” through the photosphere are, respectively,

\displaystyle\gamma_{\rm diff}=\frac{\dot{\varepsilon}_{\rm diff}}{2\varepsilon}=\frac{\omega}{E_{\rm p}}\int_{0}^{R_{\rm p}}\Im\left(\Delta\widetilde{T}^{*}\Delta\widetilde{S}\right)\rho r^{2}{\rm d}r,

(18)

\displaystyle\gamma_{\rm photo}=\frac{\dot{\varepsilon}_{\rm photo}}{2\varepsilon}=-\frac{\omega}{E_{\rm p}}\left[\Im\left(\Delta\widetilde{P}^{*}\widetilde{\xi}_{r}\right)r^{2}\right]_{r=R_{\rm p}}.

(19)

To calculate
\gamma_{\rm diff}
, we use the
GYRE
code to calculate
\Delta\widetilde{S}
and
\Delta\widetilde{T}
under the quasi-adiabatic approximation as described above, and then integrate equation (
18
) numerically.

We show in Appendix

that for modes in a plane-parallel, isothermal atmosphere,
\gamma_{\rm photo}
is given by

\gamma_{\rm photo}\simeq\left(\frac{4\upsilon_{T}\nabla_{\rm ad}Pr^{2}F_{\rm rad}}{\rho C_{P}TE_{\rm p}}\left|\frac{\Delta\widetilde{P}}{P}\right|^{2}\right)_{r=R_{\rm p}},

(20)

where
\upsilon_{T}=-(\partial\ln\rho/\partial\ln T)_{S}
and
\nabla_{\rm ad}=(\partial\ln T/\partial\ln P)_{S}
.
The total damping rate due to radiation
\gamma_{\rm rad}=\gamma_{\rm diff}+\gamma_{\rm photo}
is plotted in the top panel of Figure

. It is smallest for
f
-modes and low-
n

p
-modes.

The contributions to
\gamma_{\rm diff}
are greatest near the photosphere where energy is transported predominantly by radiative diffusion: we neglect the entropy change of the oscillation from convection (“Case 1” of
Pesnell 1990
).
We can obtain an order-of-magnitude estimate of
\gamma_{\rm diff}
as follows. The total internal luminosity is dominated by radiation
one pressure scale-height
H_{P}

below the surface (i.e.
L\simeq L_{\rm rad}
when

), where pressure, temperature, and density drop sharply (
H_{P}/r\ll 1
). Here, the
right-hand side of (

) can be estimated as

T\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Delta S\sim\frac{\Delta\rho}{\rho}\frac{F_{\rm rad}}{\rho H_{P}}.

(21)

Because adiabatic pulsations satisfy

\frac{\Delta T}{T}\sim\frac{\Delta\rho}{\rho}\sim\frac{\Delta P}{P},

(22)

and the radiative flux at the surface is of order
F_{\rm rad}\sim L/r^{2}
, the diffusive damping rate is of order

\displaystyle\gamma_{\rm diff}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int\int_{R_{\rm p}-H_{P}}^{R_{\rm p}}\frac{F_{\rm rad}}{\rho H_{P}}\left|\frac{\Delta T}{T}\right|^{2}\rho\,r^{2}{\rm d}r\,{\rm d}\Omega

\displaystyle\sim\int_{R_{\rm p}-H_{P}}^{R_{\rm p}}\frac{L}{E_{\rm p}}\left|\frac{\Delta\widetilde{T}}{T}\right|^{2}\frac{{\rm d}r}{H_{P}}.

(23)

Low-order
p
-modes and
f
-modes
have
|\Delta\widetilde{T}/T|\sim 1
.
Since
{\rm d}r\sim H_{P}
,
the radiative damping rate should be of order the inverse Kelvin-Helmholtz timescale
L/E_{\rm p}
.
Because higher frequency modes are more
compressional (
|\Delta\widetilde{T}/T|
increases),
\gamma_{\rm diff}
increases with
\omega
.

The order of magnitude and scaling of
\gamma_{\rm photo}
are similar to those of
\gamma_{\rm diff}
. Because the radial gradient of the temperature perturbation near the planet’s photosphere goes like
({\partial}/{\partial r})(\Delta T/T)\sim({\Lambda_{-}}/{H_{P}})(\Delta T/T)
, where
\Lambda_{-}\sim\omega^{2}/\omega_{\rm ac}^{2}
is given by equation (

), and
\omega_{\rm ac}=c_{\rm s}/(2H_{P})\sim 2\pi/(3\,{\rm min})
is the acoustic cutoff frequency, the heat lost per time per mass is of order

T\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Delta S\sim-\frac{\Lambda_{-}F_{\rm rad}}{\rho H_{P}}\frac{\Delta T}{T}.

(24)

The change in density can be related to the change in entropy and temperature using the laws of thermodynamics:

\frac{\Delta\rho}{\rho}\sim-\frac{\Lambda_{-}}{H_{P}}\xi_{r}\sim\frac{\Delta T}{T}-\frac{\Delta S}{C_{P}}.

(25)

Inserting equations (

) and (
25

\displaystyle\gamma_{\rm photo}\sim-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[\int P\frac{\Delta T^{*}}{T}\frac{H_{P}}{\Lambda_{-}}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{\Delta S}{C_{P}}\right)r^{2}{\rm d}\Omega\right]_{r=R_{\rm p}}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{E_{\rm p}}\left(\frac{PF_{\rm rad}r^{2}}{\rho C_{P}T}\left|\frac{\Delta\widetilde{T}}{T}\right|^{2}\right)_{r=R_{\rm p}}\sim\frac{L}{E_{\rm p}}\left|\frac{\Delta\widetilde{T}}{T}\right|^{2}_{r=R_{\rm p}},

(26)

where we have used
P\sim\rho C_{P}T
. For low angular degree
f
-modes and
n=1

L/E_{\rm p}
. Modes with higher frequencies become more compressional (

increases), which increases
\gamma_{\rm photo}
.

IV.2
Turbulent Damping

In convective regions, turbulent eddies sap the mode energy at a rate proportional to the mode’s shear, much like an effective viscosity. This causes the mode to lose energy at the rate

\displaystyle\gamma_{\rm turb}

\displaystyle=\frac{\dot{\varepsilon}_{\rm turb}}{2\varepsilon}=\frac{\omega^{2}}{\varepsilon}\int\int_{0}^{R_{\rm p}}\nu_{\rm turb}{\bm{\nabla}}{\bm{\xi}}{\bm{:}}{\bm{\nabla}}{\bm{\xi}}\rho r^{2}{\rm d}r\,{\rm d}\Omega

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega^{2}}{E_{\rm p}}\int_{0}^{R_{\rm p}}\rho\nu_{\rm turb}X_{\ell}^{2}(r){\rm d}r,

(27)

where
(
Barker 2020
, e.g.)

\displaystyle X_{\ell}^{2}

\displaystyle=3\left|r\frac{{\rm d}\widetilde{\xi}_{r}}{{\rm d}r}-\frac{r\Delta_{\ell}}{3}\right|^{2}+\ell(\ell+1)\left|\widetilde{\xi}_{r}+r\frac{{\rm d}\widetilde{\xi}_{\perp}}{{\rm d}r}-\widetilde{\xi}_{\perp}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle+(\ell-1)\ell(\ell+1)(\ell+2)|\widetilde{\xi}_{\perp}|^{2},

(28)

\displaystyle r\Delta_{\ell}

\displaystyle=r\frac{{\rm d}\widetilde{\xi}_{r}}{{\rm d}r}+2\widetilde{\xi}_{r}-\ell(\ell+1)\widetilde{\xi}_{\perp}.

(29)

Recent hydrodynamical simulations show one can parameterize the effective viscosity
\nu_{\rm turb}
using mixing length theory:

\nu_{\rm turb}=u_{\rm turb}l_{\rm turb}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}5&\dfrac{\omega}{\omega_{\rm turb}}<0.01,\\
\frac{1}{2}\left(\dfrac{\omega_{\rm turb}}{\omega}\right)^{1/2}&0.01\leq\dfrac{\omega}{\omega_{\rm turb}}\leq 5\\
\dfrac{25}{\sqrt{20}}\left(\dfrac{\omega_{\rm turb}}{\omega}\right)^{2}&\dfrac{\omega}{\omega_{\rm turb}}>5\end{array}\right.,

(30)

where the convective velocity
u_{\rm turb}
and mixing length
l_{\rm turb}
are
MESA
outputs,
and
\omega_{\rm turb}=u_{\rm turb}/l_{\rm turb}
is the turnover frequency
(
Vidal & Barker 2020a
;
Vidal & Barker 2020b
;
Duguid et al. 2020a
;
Duguid et al. 2020b
, e.g.)
. Plugging our
f
and
p
-mode
GYRE
oscillations into equation (
27
), we find
1/\gamma_{\rm turb}
is longest for
f
-modes and low
n

p
-modes, sometimes exceeding several million years (Fig.
6
).

The dissipation from turbulent damping is largest near the planet’s surface. Because for our high-frequency modes,
\omega\gg\omega_{\rm turb}
everywhere,

\nu_{\rm turb}\sim\frac{F_{\rm conv}}{\omega^{2}\rho H_{P}},

(31)

where the flux
F_{\rm conv}\sim\rho u_{\rm turb}^{3}\sim L/r^{2}
in convective regions, with mixing lengths of order
l_{\rm turb}\sim H_{P}
. We can use (
31
) to understand the magnitude of the turbulent damping rate, by inserting it into equation (
27
), and using
X_{\ell}^{2}\sim|{\rm d}\widetilde{\xi}_{r}/{\rm d}\ln r|^{2}
:

\displaystyle\gamma_{\rm turb}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int\int_{0}^{R_{\rm p}-H_{P}}\frac{F_{\rm conv}}{\rho H_{P}}\left|\frac{\partial\xi_{r}}{\partial r}\right|^{2}\rho r^{2}{\rm d}r\,{\rm d}\Omega

\displaystyle\sim\int_{0}^{R_{\rm p}-H_{P}}\frac{L}{E_{\rm p}}\left|\frac{{\rm d}\widetilde{\xi}_{r}}{{\rm d}r}\right|^{2}\frac{{\rm d}r}{H_{P}}.

(32)

Similar to mode damping from radiative diffusion, turbulent damping causes low-order
f
and
p
-modes to decay at rates of order the inverse Kelvin-Helmholtz timescale
L/E_{\rm p}
. As the mode frequency increases, so does its shear
|{\rm d}\widetilde{\xi}_{r}/{\rm d}r|
(Fig.
2
), shortening the damping timescale
\gamma_{\rm turb}^{-1}
(Fig.
6
bottom panel).

Potential interference from stellar pulsation has been considered. Beta Pic is a
\delta

(
Kenworthy et al. 2021
, e.g.,)
. Several oscillation modes have periods similar to those expected for the planet’s oscillation period. Stellar oscillation can impact the variability in two ways. First, it introduces modulations in the stellar PSF speckle; second, it causes the scattered light of the debris disk to vary. For the first component, assuming a pessimistic scenario where the PSF subtraction cannot de-correlate the variability of the speckle, the noise can be estimated by multiplying the stellar variability by the brightness of the PSF speckle (
{\sim}

\beta

2
;
Rebollido et al. 2024

In summary, we estimate the photometric random noise to be 0.2%, the optimistic systematic noise to be 0.8% (only speckle noise), and the pessimistic systematic noise to be 2% (speckle and stellar variability).

We simulate the planet oscillation using a sinusoid, add noise, and try to recover it using a Lomb-Scargle periodogram. The photon noise is modeled as random noise, and the systematic noise is modeled as red noise. The significance of the detection is evaluated based on the false alarm probability estimation of the periodic signal. The recovery of the periodic signal is considered significant when the significance exceeds a false alarm probability of
2.86\times 10^{-7}
(
5\sigma
). In the optimistic scenario (0.8% red noise), a 1% planet oscillation can be confidently recovered; in the pessimistic scenario (2% red noise), a 3% planet oscillation can be recovered.

VI
Summary and Discussion

The vast stores of heavy metals in Jupiter-mass exoplanets
(
Thorngren et al. 2016
)
can be amassed from giant impacts
(
Ginzburg & Chiang 2020
)
. Impactors and the momentum they impart to a growing planet excite a spectrum of seismic modes (Fig.
1
; Sec.
III
). Once excited, normal mode oscillations can persist over timescales comparable to a young planet’s age (Sec.
IV
). We have applied our calculations to
\beta
Pic b, a
\sim
10-20 Myr old, 13 Jupiter mass planet still embedded in a debris disk. The giant planet’s eccentricity of
\sim
0.15 and heavy metal content of
\sim
100-300 Earth masses
(
GRAVITY Collaboration et al. 2020
;
Wang 2025
, e.g.)
suggest a violent dynamical upbringing including planet-scale collisions. We showed that JWST photometry could detect the
\ell=0
,
n=1

p
-mode excited in
\beta
Pic b by a merger with a Neptune-mass planet, if such a collision occurred within the past
\sim
9–18 Myr (Sec.
V
).

Seismology offers a direct window into giant planet interiors. Because the longest-lived normal modes have frequencies comparable to the planet’s dynamical frequency
\sim
(GM_{\rm p}/R_{\rm p}^{3})^{1/2}
, a frequency measurement would constrain the planet’s bulk density.
If rotational splitting of a mode were detected, the planet’s spin could be inferred
(
Huber et al. 2013
;
Ong 2025
, e.g.)
.
High-order acoustic modes (
n>1
) could be used to constrain the interior sound speed and its gradient
(
Aerts 2021
, e.g.)
. The detection of low-frequency gravity modes could point to regions of stable stratification, as has been done for Saturn
(
Fuller 2014
;
Mankovich & Fuller 2021
, e.g.)
.

Our treatment of seismic oscillations and their detectability can be improved. We modeled the planet as a blackbody. Accounting for the wavelength-dependent opacity of the atmosphere may alter predicted flux variations in certain wavebands
(
Townsend 1997
, e.g.)
. We also considered only the linear behavior of normal modes. Non-linear effects could affect the mode spectrum, and enhance damping rates.

Impacts are not the only way to excite oscillations in giant planets. Hot and warm Jupiters may form through high-eccentricity migration, a process whereby tidal gravitational forces from the host star excite the lowest-frequency fundamental mode to large amplitudes
(
Mardling 1995a
;
Mardling 1995b
;
Ivanov & Papaloizou 2004
;
Vick & Lai 2018
;
Wu 2018
;
Vick et al. 2019
;
Teyssandier et al. 2019
, e.g.)
. The infrared light curves of highly-eccentric massive planets (e.g. HD 80606 b;
Sikora et al. 2024
) may exhibit variations from tidally-excited
f
-modes.

Appendix B
Heat After Impact

Figure 10:
Amplitudes due to work done by impactor heating
|c_{\rm heat}|
, for modes with radial node numbers
n
and angular degrees
\ell
indicated. Symbol size decreases with
n
, from
n=0
(
f
-modes, filled circles) to
n=10
(
p
-modes, open circles). We set
\eta=0.5
and
r_{\rm bub}=0.995R_{\rm p}
. Notice
|c_{\rm heat}|
is comparable to
|c_{\rm mom}|
for the
f
-modes and
p
-modes considered here (Fig.
5
).

In addition to the impact’s momentum, heat deposited in the envelope of the planet can do work on normal modes
(
Lognonne et al. 1994
;
Dombard & Boughn 1995
;
Wu & Lithwick 2019
, e.g.)
. In this appendix, we estimate the amplitudes of oscillations after the impact heats the upper envelope. We assume after impact at
t=0
, the impactor’s heat causes oscillations to feel a constant pressure
P_{\rm bub}
inside a bubble with a surface located at the radial locations
{\bm{r}}_{\rm bub}
with a surface unit normal vector of
{\bm{\hat{n}}}_{\rm bub}
. The pressure then exerts a force per unit area of

\rho f_{\rm heat}=P_{\rm bub}\Theta(t)\delta({\bm{r}}-{\bm{r}}_{\rm bub}){\bm{\hat{n}}}_{\rm bub},

(B1)

where
\Theta(t)
is the Heavyside step function, while
\delta({\bm{r}})
is the one-dimensional Dirac delta function. By using Gauss’s law multiple times
(
Dombard & Boughn 1995
, e.g.)
, we can then express

\displaystyle\langle\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}|{\bm{f}}_{\rm heat}\rangle=\int\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}^{*}{\bm{\cdot}}\left[P_{\rm bub}\Theta(t)\delta({\bm{r}}-{\bm{r}}_{\rm bub}){\bm{\hat{n}}}_{\rm bub}\right]\rho{\rm d}V

\displaystyle=P_{\rm bub}\Theta(t)\oint_{\rm bub}({\bm{\hat{n}}}_{\rm bub}{\bm{\cdot}}\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}^{*})\rho{\rm d}A

\displaystyle=P_{\rm bub}\Theta(t)\int_{\rm bub}({\bm{\nabla}}{\bm{\cdot}}\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}^{*})\rho{\rm d}V

\displaystyle\simeq P_{\rm bub}V_{\rm bub}\Theta(t)({\bm{\nabla}}{\bm{\cdot}}\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}^{*})_{\rm bub},

(B2)

where
V_{\rm bub}
is the volume of the bubble, and
({\bm{\nabla}}{\bm{\cdot}}\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}^{*})_{\rm bub}
is evaluated at the center of the bubble.

The work done on the oscillations,
P_{\rm bub}V_{\rm bub}
, should be some fraction
\eta
of the impactor’s energy
E_{\rm imp}=GM_{\rm p}m_{\rm imp}/R_{\rm p}
:

P_{\rm bub}V_{\rm bub}=\eta E_{\rm imp}=\eta\frac{GM_{\rm p}m_{\rm imp}}{R_{\rm p}}.

(B3)

Inserting equations (
B2
) and (
B3
) into equation (
9
), and also using
({\bm{\nabla}}{\bm{\cdot}}\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}^{*})=-\Delta\widetilde{\rho}^{*}/\rho
, we find

c_{\rm heat}=-\eta\frac{m_{\rm imp}}{M_{\rm p}}\left({\bm{\nabla}}{\bm{\cdot}}\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}^{*}\right)_{\rm bub}=-\eta\frac{m_{\rm imp}}{M_{\rm p}}\left(\frac{\Delta\widetilde{\rho}^{*}}{\rho}\right)_{r_{\rm bub}}\sqrt{\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}},

(B4)

where we have assumed the bubble is located at the radius
r_{\rm bub}
, at the planet’s pole. We find
c_{\rm heat}
to be comparable in magnitude to
c_{\rm mom}
(compare Fig.
10
to Fig.
5
).

Appendix C
Heating at the Photosphere

Although many calculations take the Lagrangian pressure perturbation to vanish at the planet’s photosphere (
\Delta\widetilde{P}/P=0
at optical depth
\tau=2/3
), this boundary condition neglects how the oscillation affects the planet’s atmosphere, and in particular how modal energy is lost to the atmosphere. We follow instead Section 5.4 from the lecture notes available at
https://users-phys.au.dk/jcd/oscilnotes/Lecture_Notes_on_Stellar_Oscillations.pdf
(see also e.g.
Goldreich & Wu 1999
) to calculate how the oscillation penetrates the planet’s atmosphere. We then use this result to calculate the work done by radiative diffusion at the photosphere.

We define the planet’s photospheric radius
R_{\rm p}
as the location where the optical depth
\tau=2/3
, and take the layers above to be isothermal, so that the pressure scale height
H_{P}=-({\rm d}\ln P/{\rm d}r)^{-1}
and density scale height
H=({\rm d}\ln\rho/{\rm d}r)^{-1}
are equal:

H_{P}=H=\frac{k_{\rm B}T}{g\mu m_{\rm H}}=\text{constant}.

(C1)

Hydrostatic equilibrium

\frac{{\rm d}P}{{\rm d}r}=-g\rho=-\frac{P}{H_{P}}

(C2)

gives the solutions

P(z)=P(R_{\rm p}){\rm e}^{-z/H_{P}},\hskip 14.22636pt\rho(z)=\rho(R_{\rm p}){\rm e}^{-z/H_{P}},

(C3)

where
z=r-R_{\rm p}
is the height above the surface,
k_{\rm B}
is the Boltzman constant,
\mu
is the mean molecular weight, and
m_{\rm H}
is the mass of the hydrogen atom. Because
H_{P}\ll R_{\rm p}
, we will also assume that
z\ll R_{\rm p}
everywhere, so that the adiabatic index
\Gamma_{1}
, adiabatic sound-speed
c_{\rm s}
and Brunt-Väisällä frequency
N
satisfy

\displaystyle c_{\rm s}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma_{1}P}{\rho}=\text{constant},

(C4)

\displaystyle N^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{g}{H_{P}}\left(1-\frac{1}{\Gamma_{1}}\right)=\text{constant}.

(C5)

Near the surface (
H_{P}/r\ll 1
), the equations describing the radial displacement
\widetilde{\xi}_{r}
and Eulerian pressure perturbation
\delta\widetilde{P}
under the Cowling approximation (
\delta\widetilde{\Phi}\simeq 0
) are

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}\widetilde{\xi}_{r}}{{\rm d}z}=\frac{1}{\Gamma_{1}H_{P}}\widetilde{\xi}_{r}+\left(\frac{k_{\perp}^{2}}{\omega^{2}}-\frac{1}{c_{\rm s}^{2}}\right)\frac{\delta\widetilde{P}}{\rho},

(C6)

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}z}\left(\frac{\delta\widetilde{P}}{\rho}\right)=(\omega^{2}-N^{2})\widetilde{\xi}_{r}+\frac{N^{2}}{g}\frac{\delta\widetilde{P}}{\rho},

(C7)

where
k_{\perp}=\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}/r
is the horizontal wavenumber. Because
k_{\perp}\ll H_{P}^{-1}
, we also make the plane-parallel approximation, and assume that radial variations are larger than those in the horizontal direction. Combining equations (
C6
)-(
C7
), we have

\frac{{\rm d}^{2}\widetilde{\xi}_{r}}{{\rm d}z^{2}}-\frac{1}{H_{P}}\frac{{\rm d}\widetilde{\xi}_{r}}{{\rm d}z}+\frac{\omega^{2}}{4H_{P}^{2}\omega_{\rm ac}^{2}}\widetilde{\xi}_{r}\simeq 0,

(C8)

where

\omega_{\rm ac}=\frac{c_{\rm s}}{2H_{P}}

(C9)

is the acoustic cutoff frequency. Assuming

\frac{\widetilde{\xi}_{r}}{r},\frac{\Delta\widetilde{P}}{P}\propto\exp\left(\frac{\Lambda z}{H_{P}}\right),

(C10)

equation (
C8
) has a solution when

\Lambda=\Lambda_{\pm}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\pm\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{\omega^{2}}{\omega_{\rm ac}^{2}}\right)^{1/2}.\\

(C11)

The oscillation is evanescent (
\Lambda_{\pm}
is real) for
\omega<\omega_{\rm ac}
, because pressure cannot provide a restoring force in the atmosphere. The requirement that the energy density decays with height (
\rho\widetilde{\xi}_{r}^{2}\to 0
as
z\to\infty
) forces us to pick
\Lambda=\Lambda_{-}
. For the
f
-modes and low-order
p
modes of interest in this work,
\omega\ll\omega_{\rm ac}=2\pi/(2.5\ {\rm minutes})
for our model, and

\Lambda_{-}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{\omega^{2}}{\omega_{\rm ac}^{2}}\right)^{1/2}\simeq\frac{\omega^{2}}{4\omega_{\rm ac}^{2}}.

(C12)

Within our gray, plane-parallel atmosphere, we assume radiation has no sources or sinks of energy, so the radiative flux streams freely (
F_{\rm rad}=\text{constant}
when
z\geq 0
). Since an Eddington atmosphere varies with optical depth as

T^{4}=\frac{3}{4\sigma}F_{\rm rad}(\tau+\frac{2}{3})

(with
\sigma
the Stefan-Boltzmann constant), the Lagrangian perturbation to the radiative flux
\Delta F
when the thermal time is long (
t_{\rm th}\omega\gg 1
) and the medium optically thin (
\tau\ll 1
) is given by

\frac{\Delta F}{F_{\rm rad}}\simeq 4\frac{\Delta T}{T}\simeq 4\nabla_{\rm ad}\frac{\Delta P}{P},

(C13)

where
\nabla_{\rm ad}=(\partial\ln T/\partial\ln P)_{S}
. Therefore, the entropy perturbation at the photosphere is

T\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Delta S\bigg|_{r=R_{\rm p}}=-\frac{1}{\rho}{\bm{\nabla}}{\bm{\cdot}}\Delta{\bm{F}}\bigg|_{r=R_{\rm p}}\simeq-\frac{4\Lambda_{-}\nabla_{\rm ad}F_{\rm rad}}{\rho H_{P}}\frac{\Delta P}{P}\bigg|_{r=R_{\rm p}}.

(C14)

Also using the thermodynamic relation

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma_{1}}\frac{\Delta P}{P}-\upsilon_{T}\frac{\Delta S}{C_{P}}=\frac{\Delta\rho}{\rho}=-({\bm{\nabla}}{\bm{\cdot}}{\bm{\xi}})\simeq-\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\xi_{r},

(C15)

where
\upsilon_{T}=-(\partial\ln\rho/\partial\ln T)_{S}
, we can write
\widetilde{\xi}_{r}
in terms of
\Delta\widetilde{S}
and
\Delta\widetilde{P}
, which gives the leading-order lag between
\widetilde{\xi}_{r}
and
\Delta\widetilde{P}^{*}
at the planet’s surface:

\widetilde{\xi}_{r}=\left({\rm i}\frac{4\upsilon_{T}\nabla_{\rm ad}F_{\rm rad}}{\omega\rho C_{P}T}+\frac{H_{P}}{\Lambda_{-}\Gamma_{1}}\right)\frac{\Delta\widetilde{P}}{P}.

(C16)

Inserting (
C16
) into equation (
19
), we arrive at equation (
20
).
