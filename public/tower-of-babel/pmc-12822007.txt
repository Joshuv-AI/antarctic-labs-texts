Comparative pangenome analysis of Yersinia enterocolitica in a one health approach
Authors: Bruna Torres Furtado Martins, Rafaela da Silva Rodrigues, Luís Augusto Nero
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractYersinia enterocolitica is a Gram-negative pathogen frequently associated with swine and pork products. Despite its global importance, little is known about the genomic characteristics of Y. enterocolitica in Brazil. Furthermore, the antimicrobial resistance (AMR) in Y. enterocolitica has been associated to be driven by horizontal gene transfer, especially in environments with intensive antimicrobial use. This study aims to investigate the phylogenetic and population structure of Y. enterocolitica, and antimicrobial resistance and virulence gene distribution using genome sequences to compare isolates obtained in Brazil with other isolates deposited in online databases. In this study, a total of 998 high-quality genomes from Y. enterocolitica deposited in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) were evaluated for pangenome using the Roary software with MAFFT for alignment. Pangenome analysis and phylogenetic inference were also performed on a subset of 837 genomes from isolates obtained from both pig and human. The analyses followed the procedures determined by ModelTest-NG. ABRicate with PlasmidFinder database, Virulence Factor Database (VFDB) and CARD database were used to investigate plasmid markers, virulence genes and resistance genes. Comparative analysis with international strains from public databases suggests that specific Y. enterocolitica strains circulate in Brazil. Swine and human isolates from Brazil were consistently grouped together, suggesting a strong zoonotic link. Additionally, the study underscores the correlation between antimicrobial use in pig farming and resistance gene prevalence. Our findings contribute to the understanding of Y. enterocolitica epidemiology in Brazil and emphasize the importance of genomic surveillance under the One Health approach to prevent foodborne diseases and combat antimicrobial resistance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12420-0.Keywords: Yersinia enterocolitica, Pangenome, Swine, Brazil, One health

Introduction

Yersinia enterocolitica is a Gram-negative bacterium distributed around the world that can be isolated from several animal species [1–4]. Among these animals, pigs are of particular concern due to their high prevalence of Y. enterocolitica. A prevalence study about Y. enterocolitica carried out in China with several animal species demonstrated pigs with the highest prevalence of these bacteria, with a prevalence rate of 12.9%. Furthermore, it was revealed that pathogenic isolates comprised the majority of the Y. enterocolitica recovered from pigs (73.50%) [5].

Cases of yersiniosis in humans have been associated with the consumption of a variety of foods [6–8]. In the last report published by the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC), yersiniosis was the fourth most frequently foodborne gastrointestinal infection in humans in the European Union [9]. The disease in most cases is characterized by abdominal pain and diarrhea, but when there are complications, the pathogen can reach other organs through the bloodstream [10]. Some recent reports have been published associating Y. enterocolitica contamination with cases of arthritis and the formation of septic aneurysms in human abdominal vessels [11, 12]. Pig herds arriving at the slaughterhouses are usually pointed out as sources of carcass contamination within the industrialization environment [13]. Due to this intimate relationship with pigs, Y. enterocolitica has been identified as one of the biggest biological hazards of this type of food [13]. It was estimated that 77.3% of global cases of yersiniosis can be associated with the consumption of pork [14].

Chromosomal genes in Y. enterocolitica are associated with identification and virulence, such as inv and ail. Only in virulent strains is it possible to find the pYV plasmid, the most recognized plasmid associated with virulence in this species and used in studies for search pathogenic strains. Genes carried on plasmids help direct the invasion of the Yersinia pathogen and support bacterial survival inside the human host [10, 15]. The pYV plasmid is responsible for the production of the invasin YadA, responsible for adhesion and attachment, and other proteins known as Yops (Yersinia outer proteins), through the type III secretion system [16]. The pathogenicity of Y. enterocolitica is linked to several plasmid encoded and chromosomal virulence factors (VFs) [1, 17]: the most prominent VFs are those encoded by a ~ 70 kb plasmid for Yersinia virulence, pYV, which carries genes for a type III secretion system (T3SS) and effectors belonging to the yop virulon encoding Yersinia outer membrane proteins (Yops) [1]. Other significant VFs associated with disease include chromosomal VFs such as the ail gene that encodes an adhesin, the invasin gene invA, and yst genes that encode Yersinia thermostable enterotoxins [18].

Based on the One Health approach, focusing on food safety and security is very important, as well as the surveillance and prevention of foodborne pathogens. The nature of global food supply and consumption patterns demands a collaborative approach involving public health, animal health, and the food industry to address the challenges faced in food production. Among these challenges, antimicrobial resistance has increased prominence in recent years. There is strong evidence that resistance to multiple antimicrobials in Gram-negative bacteria is mainly related to the acquisition of genes through horizontal gene transfer. Mobile genetic elements such as plasmids, transposons, insertion sequences (ISs), gene cassettes, integrons, and resistance islands play a central role in this process, facilitating the capture, accumulation, and movement of resistance determinants across bacterial populations [19]. Y. enterocolitica is usually susceptible to aminoglycosides, chloramphenicol, tetracyclines, third-generation cephalosporins, and fluoroquinolones [10, 20]; intrinsic resistance to ampicillin, amoxicillin-clavulanate, ticarcillin, and first-generation cephalosporins generally is used for identification and guides antimicrobial susceptibility testing strategies [21]. In recent years, it is possible to observe an increased occurrence of Y. enterocolitica isolates with high levels of antimicrobial resistance [22, 23]. Furthermore, this fact is detected in the presence of genes associated with multidrug resistance in these bacteria genomes, suggests selective pressure exerted on these strains by antimicrobial use in animal production, as well as the horizontal transfer of resistance determinants in environments where these isolates are present [24, 25].

Although numerous studies have investigated the genomic features of Y. enterocolitica worldwide, little is known about its prevalence and phylogeny in Brazil, where epidemiological data remain scarce. Thus, in this study we aimed to analyze the phylogenetic and population structure of Y. enterocolitica using genome sequences to compare isolates obtained in Brazil with other isolates deposited in the NCBI online database. In addition, we pursued to investigate the dynamics of antimicrobial resistance and virulence gene distribution among Y. enterocolitica strains.

Materials and methods

Genomes retrieval and quality metrics

Initially, a total of 2,492 Yersinia enterocolitica genome sequences deposited in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) had their BioSample information retrieved. Only those with specified isolation source, host, or source type, along with geographic localization, were selected. A total of 1,574 genomes were obtained using the NCBI Datasets command-line tools (CLI, version 16.6.0) in October 2024 (https://github.com/ncbi/datasets). The quality of the genomes was assessed using CheckM v. 1.0.13 [26] excluding genomes with completeness scores below 95% or contamination levels of 5% or higher from further analysis. Additionally, Average Nucleotide Identity (ANI) was calculated using Pyani v0.2.12 (https://github.com/widdowquinn/pyani), with MUMmer (ANIm) approach, to confirm species-level identity. The analysis applied coverage and alignment identity cutoffs of > 80% and > 95%, respectively, in an all-vs-all comparison, with primary focus on comparisons against complete genomes and reference genome for the species (Supplementary Table S1). Then, the software Busco v5.0.0 was used to evaluate the integrity of the genome sequences, with the database “enterobacterales_odb10”, and only the genomes with an integrity score ≥ of 95 were retained [27]. Genome metrics were generated using QUAST 5.2.0 [28].

Pangenome analysis

Initially, 998 genomes (Table 1) from the previous quality evaluation underwent annotation using Prokka software v.1.14.5, employing default settings [29]. Pangenome analysis was performed on the GFF files from Prokka annotation, using the Roary software v.3.13.0, with MAFFT for alignment [30]. The IQ-TREE software v. 1.6.11 was used for phylogenetic inference using the core genome alignment and 1,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates [31], with the best-fit model of “GTR + I + G4” determined by ModelTest-NG v.0.1.7 [32]. The final tree was visualized using the Interactive Tree of Life (iTOL) online tool v.6 [33].

Pangenome analysis and phylogenetic inference were also performed on a subset of 837 genomes from isolates obtained from both pig and human. The analyses followed the procedures described previously, with the same best-fit model for phylogenetic inference, determined by ModelTest-NG.

Detection of plasmids, and virulence resistance and antibiotic genes

ABRicate v.1.0.1 (https://github.com/tseemann/abricate) was used in conjunction with PlasmidFinder database [34], Virulence Factor Database (VFDB) [35], and CARD database [36], using “--mincov 90” and “--minid 90”; which specify a minimum sequence coverage of 90% and a minimum sequence identity of 90%, respectively. All databases were updated on April 2, 2024. Heatmaps were constructed, and dendrograms for the clustering of resistance genes, virulence genes, and plasmid markers were generated using R version 4.3.3 and RStudio 2023.03.0 + 386 (R Core Team, 2024; RStudio Team, 2020), with standard parameters in package ComplexHeatmap (complete hierarchical clustering based on Euclidean distances) [37].

Results

A total of 1,574 genome sequences, for which BioSample information included details on isolation source, host or source type, and geographic location, were retrieved from the NCBI genome database. Quality assessment using CheckM resulted in the exclusion of 472 genomes, while ANIm analysis in Pyani excluded an additional 104. No genomes were excluded following analysis with BUSCO. Consequently, a total of 998 genomes were selected, with assembly sizes ranging from 4.32 Mb to 4.84 Mb, total contigs ranging from 1 to 476 (mean ~ 138; median: 136; mode: 110), and GC content ranging from 46.59% to 47.71% (Supplementary Table S2).

Pangenome analysis

The pangenome analysis of the 998 genomes using the Roary software identified a total of 33,245 genes that comprise the pangenome (Supplementary Figure S1). Of these, the core genes (present in ≥ 99% genomes) consisted of 2,218 genes which represents approximately 6.7% of the entire pangenome. Together, the core genes and soft-core genes (375 genes; present in 95% ≤ genomes < 99%) constitutes 7.8% of the pangenome. The accessory genome is composed of shell genes (2,461 genes; present in 15% ≤ genomes < 95%) and cloud genes (28,191 genes; present in < 15% of genomes), which together constitutes 92.2% of the pangenome. The pangenome analysis of the 837 genomes of isolates obtained from both pig and human identified a total of 27,839 genes, including 2,531 core genes (9.1%), 232 soft-core genes (0.8%), 2,178 shell genes (7.8%), and 22,898 cloud genes (82.3%) (Supplementary Figure S1). Therefore, the accessory genome is composed of 90.07% (shell and cloud genes) of the pangenome. The high proportion of accessory genes reflects substantial genomic diversity, likely reflecting adaptation to different hosts and environmental niches.

The trees were built from the consensus from bootstrap values, with a higher likelihood value, as this model has more representative data. Y. enterocolitica genomes from different isolation sources were represented in Fig. 1A and Y. enterocolitica genomes of isolates from pigs or humans were represented in Fig. 1B. Distinct clusters largely corresponded to host and geographic origin: pig isolates often clustered together, whereas human isolates were more dispersed. A few Brazilian isolates grouped closely with European and Asian isolates, suggesting shared genomic features or possible transmission links.

Plasmid, virulence and antimicrobial resistance genes

Among the 192 virulence genes detected, 29 were present in all genomes analyzed. These included genes of motility, involved in chemotaxis (cheA, cheB, cheR, cheW, cheY, cheZ) and flagellar biosynthesis (flg, flh, and fli), indicating that motility and environmental sensing are conserved core features in Y. enterocolitica (Fig. 2; Supplementary Tables S3 and S4). Brazilian isolates generally exhibited fewer virulence genes than human-derived isolates from other regions. Specifically, swine-derived Brazilian isolates lacked several genes associated with iron acquisition and secretion systems, suggesting potential host adaptation or reduced virulence (Fig. 2). The most frequently detected antimicrobial resistance genes were rosA and rosB, present in all genomes analyzed, followed by vatF (963 genomes) and y56_beta-lactamase (924 genomes) (Fig. 3; Supplementary Table S5). A virulence plasmid, IncFII(Y)_1, was the most frequently identified, present in 557 Y. enterocolitica genomes (Fig. 4; Supplementary Table S6).

The strains isolated in Brazil were not included in the group with the highest prevalence of virulence genes in this study. This group consisted of isolates derived from humans and other sources, whereas the isolates obtained from swine exhibited a smaller amount of the virulence genes investigated (Fig. 2).

The Brazilian isolates were grouped into different clusters based on the presence of antimicrobial resistance genes (Fig. 3). Nine isolates carried nine antimicrobial resistance genes: APH(3’’)-Ib, APH(6)-Id, floR, rosA, rosB, sul2, tet(A), vatF, and y56_beta-lactamase. The second group, including twelve isolates, presented seven genes (SAT-2, aadA, dfrA1, rosA, rosB, vatF, y56_beta-lactamase). Seven isolates were grouped in the largest cluster, showing only four resistance genes: rosA, rosB, vatF, and y56_beta-lactamase. Therefore, even though most Yersinia isolates demonstrated a low number of resistance genes, some Brazilian isolates were grouped with isolates from different regions carrying several antimicrobial resistance genes.

Plasmid analysis revealed that most Brazilian isolates carried two specific plasmids, IncFII(Y)1 and IncC_1. Only a small proportion of the isolates showed no detectable plasmids in the tests performed. In contrast, strains derived from swine and humans in other regions revealed a greater diversity of plasmids, as identified through analyses conducted with PlasmidFinder (Fig. 4).

Discussion

In this study, we analyzed 998 Y. enterocolitica genomes, including 28 isolates from Brazil, to investigate phylogenetic relationships, virulence factors, antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs), and plasmid content. Our results provide insights into the genomic characteristics of Brazilian isolates in the context of global diversity. All Brazilian isolates were grouped close to isolates of European origin, indicating that Brazilian Y. enterocolitica strains share a close evolutionary relationship with these strains (Fig. 1.A). Only one Brazilian isolate was relocated separately to others and closer to North American isolates and some European isolates, strictly analyzing the geographic position of the strain (Fig. 1.A). Regarding the source of isolation, Brazilian isolates from pigs and humans were positioned close to isolates obtained from humans in this tree. The isolate that is located separately from the others is also grouped close to the human isolate and other sources (Fig. 1.A). Strains isolated from humans were considered the closest to those identified through sampling in swine in Brazil (Fig. 1.B). It is possible to observe that the Brazilian isolate which remained phylogenetically distant from the others in the overall analysis maintains this pattern and clusters with human-derived isolates of North American origin (Fig. 1.B).

When considering the source of isolation, most Brazilian pig and human isolates clustered together, highlighting the potential role of swine as a reservoir for strains capable of infecting humans (Fig. 1A and B). There are several studies that present Y. enterocolitica infection in humans associated with pork and pork products [4, 38–43]. Since swine are considered the most important reservoirs of pathogenic strains for humans [44], the presence of this bacterium in swine slaughterhouses and the significant association between pork and cases of yersiniosis are to be expected. These findings support previous observations that pigs are a major source of pathogenic Y. enterocolitica for humans and underscore the relevance of integrated One Health approaches to monitor pathogen circulation between animal reservoirs and humans.

Pan-genome analysis is one of the effective approaches to better understand a clade of pathogenic bacteria based on evolutionary strategies [45, 46]. These analyzes made it possible to investigate the phylogenetic relationships between different strains of Y. enterocolitica focusing on isolates obtained in Brazil. It is possible to affirm the close phylogenetic proximity between pig isolates and human isolates, always placed together and those originating from other sources are arranged further away from the circular tree (Fig. 1). The pangenome analysis showed that the accessory genome constitutes the majority of genetic content, reflecting high genomic diversity among Y. enterocolitica isolates. Brazilian pig and human isolates consistently clustered together in pangenomic analyses, further supporting the close genetic relatedness of strains circulating between swine and humans in Brazil.

Advances in nucleotide sequencing and bioinformatics technologies have leveraged the study of epidemiological surveillance investigations [47, 48].

Due to the important association between Y. enterocolitica strains obtained in Brazil and pigs, we sought to better understand the import of these animals into the country. Thus, we analyzed isolates from pigs and humans to assess their relatedness and the potential role of carrier pigs in bacterial dissemination (Fig. 1.B). The prevalence of the pathogen in pig herds is influenced by farm biosecurity and management. Inadequate hygiene during slaughter can lead to carcass contamination and entry of the pathogen into the food chain [49]. Effective control measures in slaughter and processing environments reduce the risk of contamination in the final product [50].

The genetic improvement of pigs in Brazil has been achieved mainly through the import of animals through animal genetics companies. The Cananéia Quarantine Station receives imported pig breeders in an official location in Brazil. Between the years 2014–2023, 14,903 pigs arrived in this season and went through the quarantine period to mitigate the risk of exotic, emergency and officially controlled diseases (personal communication, MAPA - Brazilian Ministry of Agriculture). Analyzing the history of imports during this period we can see that the USA was responsible for sending 60.49% of imported pigs, Denmark comes in second place with 19.26%, followed by Canada 11.81%, France and Norway with 5.99% and 2.45%, respectively (personal communication, MAPA). The majority of imported animals come from North America and the Y. enterocolitica strains are closest to European isolates. In this case, the import of pigs was not crucial for the spread of Y. enterocolitica in Brazil. We can infer that Y. enterocolitica already exists in Brazil with its own genetic characteristics, a Brazilian profile for this species. Pathogenic Y. enterocolitica lineages have been reported to share close ancestry [49, 51, 52]. The high similarity among Brazilian Y. enterocolitica isolates suggests that swine may play an important role in the transmission of this pathogen to humans in Brazil, as previously reported in Europe [25, 49, 53].

The introduction of pigs among different pig farms plays a central role in the spread of Y. enterocolitica, as the infected animals are considered the main agent of transmission of this pathogen [54]. In Finland, studies have shown that the entry of new genotypes of Y. enterocolitica into farms is strongly associated with the purchase of animals, especially piglets originating from breeding farms already contaminated with the pathogen. These animals act as effective vectors, contributing both to the internal spread of the bacterium within fattening farms and to its dissemination among different production sites. These findings highlight the importance of implementing strict biosecurity measures and conducting sanitary monitoring of newly introduced animals, in order to prevent the introduction and establishment of pathogenic Y. enterocolitica strains in pig herds [55]. Moreover, studies indicate that certain genotypes of Y. enterocolitica may be associated with specific farms, while also being shared among different production units, particularly those sending animals to the same slaughterhouse. This finding suggests either the exchange of strains between farms or the possibility of cross-contamination within the slaughterhouse environment [49]. These results highlight the critical importance of strict biosecurity measures throughout the entire production chain, including the slaughter environment and ante-mortem practices such as proper cleaning and disinfection of vehicles used for animal transport.

The application of new molecular methodologies for the characterization of microorganisms has been revolutionizing the studies about genetic diversity in microorganisms, including Y. enterocolitica [7, 56]. Species of the genus Yersinia exhibit high genetic similarity, sharing approximately 71% of their genetic composition [57]. Although there is evidence of a common ancestor in the genus Yersinia [58]. In Fig. 2, a notable gap can be observed regarding the detection of virulence genes in strains for which the presence of plasmids could not be confirmed through the analyses performed. This finding consolidates the association between a higher virulence potential and the presence of plasmids in the genome of Y. enterocolitica strains.

The pYV plasmid is not present in brazilian strains, corroborating with Terentjeva et al. [49], the main genes identified in all Y. enterocolitica genomes analyzed are not associated with the pYV plasmid. This plasmid serves as one of the most important indicators of the Y. enterocolitica virulence potential. Multiple genes directly involved in the pathogenicity of the species can be identified on this plasmid [1, 10, 18, 59]. These included genes involved in chemotaxis, a central regulatory mechanism in bacteria, which are part of signaling systems that respond to environmental changes. These genes are not only vital for bacterial chemotaxis but also contribute to adhesion and biofilm formation, highlighting their involvement in diverse bacterial behaviors beyond just motility [60, 61]. This gene’s expression is influenced by ambient temperature, which directly impacts bacterial motility [62–66].

Other genes identified in all evaluated strains were flg, flh, and fli, essential for the assembly and function of the bacterial flagellum, which is responsible for the motility of Y. enterocolitica [67]. In addition to motility, these genes can be influencing the expression of virulence factors. There is a report about the association of fliA mutants with changes in expression of plasmid-encoded virulence genes in Y. enterocolitica [61, 68].

Antimicrobial resistance in Y. enterocolitica can be related with antimicrobial consumption patterns across different countries. The use of antimicrobial agents as growth promoters has been gradually phased out due to their clear role in the selection of resistant microorganisms that can reach humans through the food chain [69]. Antimicrobial consumption and prevalence of resistance genes in bacteria are closely associated throughout the scientific community [70, 71]. A large-scale study analyzed bacterial genomes from various regions of the world, including non-clinical environments, and observed a solid correlation between national antibiotic usage rates and the occurrence of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) in pathogenic and non-pathogenic microorganisms [70]. Supporting this, based on data from 2014 to 2021, the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) found significant associations between antimicrobial use in food-producing animals and resistance observed in bacteria from both animals and humans [72]. This result highlights the impact caused by the indiscriminate use of antimicrobials in environmental, public and animal health. The report highlighted the reduction in the use of antimicrobials was followed by a proportional decrease in resistance in Escherichia coli isolated from animal and human. This is proof of the direct impact of this practice within the production chain on public health [73]. A strong correlation was found between exposure and resistance development, linking antimicrobial use in animals to the prevalence of resistance in commensal E. coli, based on samples obtained from seven Europeans countries from poultry, cattle, and pigs [73].

Similar to global trends, there is a concern about the antimicrobial resistance challenge linked to indiscriminate use of antimicrobial in animal production in Brazil. Notably, the southern region of Brazil has been identified as an emerging hotspot for antimicrobial resistance, it is related to the intensive poultry and swine production systems in these states. In recent years, several regulatory measures were introduced with the aim of regulating, guiding and controlling this practice [74, 75]. Our results reinforce the need for effective policies promoting the rational use of antimicrobials in animal production (Fig. 3).

Plasmid analysis revealed that most Brazilian isolates carried IncFII(Y)1 and IncC_1 plasmids (Fig. 4). These plasmids are known to facilitate the transfer of ARGs in Enterobacteriaceae, and their presence correlated with higher ARG content in the genomes analyzed [49]. While the plasmid diversity in Brazilian strains was lower than in isolates from other regions, the observed plasmids may still contribute to the potential spread of antimicrobial resistance within pig production systems. The plasmids of Y. enterocolitica and other enterobacteriaceae have been characterized as “mosaic” plasmids”, because they contain genes from various different ancestors and undergo stages of genetic recombination [34, 76]. The study of plasmids, particularly for constructing evolutionary relationships, is highly challenging. However, it is crucial for understanding the dissemination of genes among bacteria. This information can provide valuable insights into population dynamics, encompassing traits that go beyond antimicrobial resistance, such as ecological influences, host repair mechanisms, conjugation, and other potential sources of interference. The main plasmids found in isolates from Brazil, IncFII(Y)1 and IncC_1, are so common in worldwide studies associated with transfer of resistance among Enterobacteriaceae (Fig. 4) [77, 78]. However, identifying these plasmids alone does not provide conclusive evidence of their transferability; conjugation experiments are required to confirm their ability to mediate gene transfer.

In a study conducted in Brazil, 34 Y. enterocolitica strains (biotypes 1 A, 2, and 4) obtained from clinical human samples and non-clinical sources only strains from biotype 4 were positive for IncFII_Y [79]. Associated with horizontal gene transfer among bacteria, especially genes of antimicrobial resistance, IncFII plasmid is capable of transferring genes in different bacterial species, mainly in Enterobacteriaceae and other Gram-negative bacteria [80].

In this study, we were able to evaluate, through the use of bioinformatics tools, the phylogenetic relationships, presence of resistance genes and virulence factors in the selected Y. enterocolitica genomes. The continuous application of these methodologies is capable of identifying emerging strains with potential risk to the human and animal population, in addition to enabling the investigation and tracking of variants of interest. Our study is one of the first to conduct this type of comparative analysis for Y. enterocolitica, and through it was possible to confirm the genetic similarity of Y. enterocolitica isolates obtained from pigs and humans’ samples in Brazil, as has already been observed in European countries. Therefore, we emphasize the importance of integrated biosafety measures on farms, transport and slaughter of these animals with the aim of minimizing the risk of infection by this pathogen. Improvements in these steps can significantly reduce food contamination and, consequently, cases of yersiniosis. Furthermore, the high presence of antimicrobial resistance genes in Brazilian strains warns about the indiscriminate use of antibiotics in Brazilian pig farming. This reinforces the urgency of more sustainable practices in the field, aiming to reduce the spread of resistance among microorganisms through food production chains. The diversity of plasmids observed in the evaluated strains suggests a more dynamic evolutionary scenario than expected for Y. enterocolitica, especially in Brazilian strains. Overall, our results demonstrate close genetic relatedness between Brazilian pig and human isolates, which confirms that swine are an important reservoir of pathogenic Y. enterocolitica in Brazil. The observed variation in virulence and resistance genes indicates heterogeneity in pathogenic potential, with implications for food safety and public health. Effective monitoring of plasmids and antibiotic resistance genes, combined with stringent biosecurity measures in swine production, is essential to limit the spread of pathogens and antimicrobial resistance along the food chain. We can highlight the importance of genomic analyses for Y. enterocolitica in an approach focused on One Health in facing contemporary challenges in health promotion and disease prevention.
