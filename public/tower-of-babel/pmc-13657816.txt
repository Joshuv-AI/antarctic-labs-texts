Efficacy and safety of antibody-drug conjugates in advanced small cell lung cancer: a systematic review and single-arm meta-analysis of prospective clinical trials
Authors: Peipei Zhang, Weixing Zhao, Jiayun Ma, Yuan Li, Huiyuan Peng, Jun Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) are being evaluated in advanced small cell lung cancer (SCLC), but the available trials include different targets, constructs, doses, and treatment settings. We aimed to determine the overall range of reported activity and safety while clarifying whether the evidence supports a clinically uniform ADC class effect.MethodsPubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), clinical-trial registries, and major oncology conference proceedings were searched from inception through July 31, 2026. Eligibility was restricted to reports with sufficient methodological and outcome information available in English; study country, recruitment region, investigator nationality, and participant ethnicity were not exclusion criteria. Prospective phase I–III studies of ADC monotherapy in adults with advanced, extensive-stage, relapsed, or refractory SCLC were eligible; no comparator was required, and only the ADC arm of comparative trials contributed to the single-arm synthesis. Outcomes were objective response rate (ORR), disease control rate (DCR), duration of response (DOR), progression-free survival (PFS), overall survival (OS), and clinically important safety events. Randomized trials were assessed using the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool and non-comparative studies using the Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies (MINORS). Random-effects models were used, with subgroup and sensitivity analyses.ResultsEighteen prospective studies comprising 20 analytic cohorts and 2,246 patients were included: 12 phase I/Ib/I–II studies, three phase II studies, and three phase III studies. The pooled ORR was 34.38% [95% confidence interval (CI): 24.96–44.43%; I2=95.3%], and the pooled DCR was 77.50% (95% CI: 67.98–85.81%; I2=94.7%). The pooled median DOR, PFS, and OS were 4.99 months (95% CI: 4.24–5.86; I2=77.2%), 4.36 months (95% CI: 3.87–4.92; I2=84.8%), and 7.48 months (95% CI: 6.15–9.10; I2=92.6%), respectively. Subgroup analyses by intervention and target indicated substantial clinical and statistical heterogeneity. Safety was summarized by individual agent, dose, and source population because adverse-event definitions and denominators were not sufficiently comparable; the predominant hematologic, gastrointestinal, pulmonary, and neurologic signals varied among products. Cochrane risk-of-bias judgments were predominantly low risk, with selected domains rated unclear or high risk. MINORS scores ranged from 11 to 15 of 16, with recurrent limitations in consecutive enrollment, blinded endpoint assessment, follow-up reporting, and prospective sample-size calculation.ConclusionsADC-based therapy shows antitumor activity in advanced SCLC, but substantial clinical and pharmacological heterogeneity limits the interpretation of pooled single-arm estimates. Agent-specific randomized trials with standardized efficacy, safety, and biomarker reporting are needed.Keywords: Small cell lung cancer (SCLC), antibody-drug conjugate (ADC), efficacy, safety, single-arm meta-analysis

Highlight box

Key findings

• Although antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) demonstrate encouraging antitumor activity in small cell lung cancer (SCLC), current evidence remains insufficient to establish their superiority over conventional chemotherapy. Future advances in target selection, ADC design, and combination strategies may improve clinical outcomes and overcome the limitations observed in earlier-generation ADCs.

What is known and what is new?

• ADCs have emerged as a promising therapeutic approach for SCLC, particularly through targeting tumor-associated antigens such as delta-like ligand 3 (DLL3), B7-H3, TROP2, and SEZ6. However, previous evidence has been fragmented and largely derived from early-phase clinical trials.

• This study represents the first systematic review and meta-analysis integrating prospective clinical trials of ADC monotherapy in SCLC. The findings highlight substantial heterogeneity across ADC targets and agents. While early DLL3-targeting ADCs such as rovalpituzumab tesirine failed to improve survival, newer-generation ADCs—particularly B7-H3-targeted agents such as ifinatamab deruxtecan—have demonstrated more promising response rates and may represent a new direction for ADC development in SCLC.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Current evidence supports prioritizing biomarker-driven patient selection and the development of next-generation ADCs with improved therapeutic indices.

• Future clinical research should focus on optimizing target selection, enhancing linker and payload technologies, and evaluating ADCs in rational combination regimens, particularly with immunotherapy, to maximize efficacy while maintaining acceptable safety profiles.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains a leading cause of cancer mortality worldwide (1,2). Small cell lung cancer (SCLC) accounts for approximately 10–15% of lung cancers and is biologically and clinically distinct from non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) (3). Its development is strongly associated with tobacco exposure, and most patients present with extensive-stage disease. Although SCLC is initially sensitive to platinum-etoposide-based chemotherapy, responses are frequently followed by early relapse, and durable survival remains uncommon (4,5). The disease is characterized by rapid proliferation, early metastatic dissemination, marked intratumoral heterogeneity, and a high propensity for recurrence. Chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immune checkpoint inhibition have improved disease control in selected settings, but treatment options after progression remain limited. These clinical features continue to create an urgent need for therapies that can deliver potent antitumor activity while maintaining an acceptable safety profile.

Early antibody-directed drug development in SCLC focused on delta-like ligand 3 (DLL3), a cell-surface protein frequently expressed in SCLC and present at relatively low levels in most normal tissues (6). Rovalpituzumab tesirine (Rova-T), a DLL3-directed antibody-drug conjugate (ADC) carrying a pyrrolobenzodiazepine payload, showed encouraging antitumor activity in its first-in-human phase I study, particularly among patients with DLL3-high tumors (7). However, the subsequent TRINITY, TAHOE, and MERU studies did not establish a favorable survival benefit-risk profile and identified clinically important toxicities, including serosal effusions, edema, thrombocytopenia, photosensitivity, and pulmonary events (8-10). Development of Rova-T was therefore discontinued. This experience was nevertheless informative: it showed that target expression alone is insufficient to determine therapeutic success and that antibody properties, linker stability, payload characteristics, drug-to-antibody ratio, internalization, dose, and patient selection must be considered together.

An ADC is conventionally composed of a target-selective antibody, a chemical linker, and a cytotoxic payload. The antibody is intended to concentrate the therapeutic construct in antigen-expressing tumor cells, whereas the linker must remain sufficiently stable in circulation and permit payload release at the intended site. The payload then produces cell killing after internalization and intracellular processing; in some constructs, membrane-permeable payloads may also produce a bystander effect in adjacent tumor cells. These components are interdependent rather than interchangeable. Differences in antigen density, internalization, linker cleavage, payload potency and permeability, conjugation chemistry, and systemic exposure can alter efficacy, resistance, and toxicity even among agents directed against the same target (11-13). Consequently, the term “ADC” describes a therapeutic platform rather than a single pharmacologically uniform intervention.

With advances in SCLC biology and ADC engineering, the clinical landscape has expanded beyond early DLL3-directed constructs. Recent programs have evaluated agents directed against B7 homolog 3 (B7-H3), seizure-related homolog 6 (SEZ6), trophoblast cell-surface antigen 2 (TROP2), human epidermal growth factor receptor 3 (HER3), and DLL3, including ifinatamab deruxtecan, YL201, HS-20093, ABBV-706, ABBV-011, sacituzumab govitecan, izalontamab brengitecan, ZL-1310, and tarlatamab (14-24). Several early-phase and randomized studies have reported clinically meaningful response signals; for example, the 12-mg/kg cohort of IDeate-Lung01 reported an objective response rate (ORR) of 48.2% with ifinatamab deruxtecan (14). Nevertheless, the reported efficacy and toxicity profiles vary substantially across targets, constructs, doses, treatment lines, prior therapies, and trial phases. These differences have complicated interpretation of the emerging evidence and have prevented a straightforward assumption that activity observed with one construct can be generalized to the entire therapeutic class.

We therefore examined whether efficacy outcomes across clinically and pharmacologically heterogeneous ADCs could be summarized without implying equivalence among agents. We updated the evidence through July 2026 and conducted a systematic review and single-arm meta-analysis to summarize efficacy, describe agent-specific safety signals, and explore heterogeneity by study design, ADC type, and target. We present this article in accordance with the PRISMA reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-1433/rc) (25,26).

Methods

Search strategy

This review was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251251353). From database inception through July 31, 2026, we searched PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) for English-language reports. To identify ongoing, unpublished, or conference-only studies, we also searched ClinicalTrials.gov, the World Health Organization International Clinical Trials Registry Platform, the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry, and proceedings of the American Society of Clinical Oncology, European Society for Medical Oncology, World Conference on Lung Cancer, and American Association for Cancer Research. Reference lists of eligible reports and relevant reviews were examined. Studies were not excluded according to the country in which they were conducted, recruitment region, investigator nationality, or participant ethnicity; eligible English-language reports from China and other Asian settings were included. Full reproducible database strategies, including search terms, Boolean operators, filters, and date limits, are provided in Appendix 1. Two reviewers independently screened titles/abstracts and full texts; disagreements were resolved by discussion with a third reviewer.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligibility was specified using population, intervention, comparator, outcomes, and study design (PICOS). Population: adults (≥18 years) with histologically or cytologically confirmed advanced, extensive-stage, relapsed, or refractory SCLC. Intervention: ADC monotherapy meeting the prespecified protocol eligibility criteria, at any prospectively evaluated dose or schedule. Comparator: no comparator was required. Randomized and non-randomized comparative studies were eligible, but only the ADC treatment arm contributed to single-arm event-rate or median-outcome synthesis; prospective single-arm trials and expansion cohorts were also eligible. Outcomes: at least one of ORR, disease control rate (DCR), duration of response (DOR), progression-free survival (PFS), overall survival (OS), treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs), treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs), grade ≥3 events, treatment discontinuation, or treatment-related death. Study design: prospective phase I, I/II, II, or III interventional studies, including dose-escalation and dose-expansion cohorts. Reports were required to provide sufficient methodological and outcome information in English; no restriction was applied according to study country, recruitment region, investigator nationality, or participant ethnicity. No arbitrary minimum follow-up was imposed because many eligible studies were early phase; however, a defined data cutoff and at least one completed prespecified tumor-response or safety assessment were required. Reviews, retrospective series, case reports, animal studies, combination-only cohorts, and reports without extractable SCLC-specific data were excluded. For overlapping reports, the most complete and latest dataset was used, while non-overlapping dose cohorts were retained separately. Extracting only ADC arms preserved the descriptive single-arm objective but discarded randomized comparative information; this limitation was acknowledged in the interpretation.

Data extraction

Two reviewers independently extracted study name and registration number, publication status, year, phase, randomized or non-randomized design, disease stage, treatment line, prior systemic therapy and immunotherapy where reported, cohort size, ADC agent, target, antibody/linker/payload characteristics where available, dose, follow-up, response-assessment method, and safety definitions. Efficacy outcomes were ORR, DCR, median DOR, median PFS, and median OS with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Safety data included any-grade and grade ≥3 TEAEs/TRAEs, hematologic toxicity, gastrointestinal toxicity, interstitial lung disease (ILD)/pneumonitis, neuropathy, dose modification, treatment discontinuation, and treatment-related death. Investigator-assessed and independent-review results were not mixed within the same cohort; the prespecified primary assessment or the most mature report was preferred. Disagreements were resolved with a third reviewer. Missing or ambiguous data were recorded as not reported rather than imputed.

Quality evaluation and risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was evaluated according to the original study design. Randomized trials were assessed independently by two reviewers using the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool, covering random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and other sources of bias. Prospective non-comparative interventional studies and extracted single-arm cohorts were assessed using the eight non-comparative items of the Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies (MINORS), with a maximum score of 16 (27). MINORS was selected because it directly addresses prospective data collection, endpoint ascertainment, follow-up, loss to follow-up, and prospective sample-size calculation in non-comparative surgical or interventional studies. It is nevertheless a reporting-based instrument and does not eliminate confounding or selection bias. Moreover, judgments made for randomized trials do not fully characterize bias in uncontrolled ADC-arm event rates once comparator information is discarded. These limitations were considered when judging confidence in the synthesized evidence.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted in R version 4.5.1 using the meta package. ORR and DCR were synthesized from event counts and cohort sizes using metaprop. Individual-study 95% CIs were calculated with the exact Clopper-Pearson method. Proportions were transformed using the Freeman-Tukey double-arcsine transformation (sm=“PFT”) and combined with inverse-variance random-effects models; between-study variance was estimated using the DerSimonian-Laird method (method.tau=“DL”). Pooled estimates were back-transformed to proportions. This transformation permits inclusion of zero-event and all-event cohorts without an ad hoc continuity correction. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran Q, I2, and τ2. Median DOR, PFS, and OS were analyzed separately using generic inverse-variance models (metagen, sm=“MLN”). Reported medians and their confidence limits were transformed to the logarithmic scale, and standard errors were reconstructed as SE[ln(median)] = [ln(upper 95% CI) − ln(lower 95% CI)]/(2 × 1.96). Random-effects pooling used the DerSimonian-Laird estimator, and summary medians and 95% prediction intervals were back-transformed to months. Cohorts without an estimable two-sided 95% CI for a reported median were excluded from that outcome-specific synthesis and were not imputed. Overall estimates were retained as broad descriptive summaries rather than class-performance benchmarks. Prespecified subgroup analyses considered intervention, target, study design, study phase, treatment line, and prior immunotherapy when reporting permitted. Robustness was examined using leave-one-out analyses and exclusions of conference-only reports, very small cohorts, and studies with lower methodological quality. Funnel plots were generated, and publication bias was assessed using the Begg and Egger tests. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on ADC categories, different targets, and other relevant factors to gain additional insights. Sensitivity analysis was performed to evaluate the reliability of the results, with P<0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Literature search

The updated search identified 18 eligible prospective studies representing 20 analytic cohorts (Figure 1).

Study characteristics

The 18 studies included 2,246 patients across 20 analytic cohorts. Twelve studies were phase I, Ib, or I/II, three were phase II, and three were phase III. Four studies used randomized designs (9,10,14,15), whereas 14 were prospective non-comparative studies or expansion cohorts (7,8,16-24,28-30). The target distribution was B7-H3, DLL3, SEZ6, TROP2, and HER3. All included data concerned monotherapy in advanced or extensive-stage SCLC; treatment settings ranged from maintenance after first-line therapy to heavily pretreated relapsed/refractory disease. The included studies were published between 2016 and 2026, with specific characteristics of each study outlined in Table 1.

Risk-of-bias assessment

The randomized trials were generally judged to be at low risk of bias across most domains using the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool, although selected domains related to blinding, incomplete outcome data, or reporting were rated as unclear or high risk. The 14 prospective non-comparative studies were assessed using MINORS; scores ranged from 11 to 15 of 16. Incomplete reporting of consecutive enrollment, blinded endpoint assessment, follow-up, and prospective sample-size calculation were the most frequent limitations. These assessments apply to the original study designs. Confidence in the single-arm synthesis is lower because treatment-arm outcomes were summarized without randomized comparators and because most evidence was early phase (Figure S1 and Table S1).

Efficacy synthesis

ORR

ORR was available for 19 cohorts comprising 2,196 patients. The random-effects pooled ORR was 34.38% (95% CI, 24.96–44.43%), with substantial heterogeneity (I2=95.3%, P<0.0001) (Figure 2). The funnel plot, sensitivity analysis, and subgroup analyses by trial type, ADC type, and target are shown in Figures S2-S6. These analyses were used to explore heterogeneity and should not be interpreted as indirect comparisons or evidence that one target or product is superior. We further assessed publication bias using the Begg and Egger tests, with P<0.05 considered statistically significant. The results of both the Begg and Egger tests indicated no significant publication bias, and the outcomes of these tests are shown in Table S2.

Forest plot of the overall response rate depicted using the sample-size-weighted random-effects model. CI, confidence interval.

DCR

DCR was available for 17 cohorts comprising 1,784 patients. The random-effects pooled DCR was 77.50% (95% CI, 67.98–85.81%), with substantial heterogeneity (I2=94.7%, P<0.0001) (Figure 3). The funnel plot, sensitivity analysis, and subgroup analyses by ADC type and target are shown in Figures S7-S10; these analyses did not remove the underlying clinical heterogeneity. We further evaluated publication bias using the Begg and Egger tests, with both tests showing no significant publication bias. The results of the Egger and Begg tests are shown in Table S2.

Forest plot of the disease control rate depicted using the sample-size-weighted random-effects model. CI, confidence interval.

DOR

Median DOR was available for 17 cohorts. The pooled median DOR was 4.99 months (95% CI, 4.24–5.86), with a 95% prediction interval of 2.70–9.20 months and substantial heterogeneity (I2=77.2%, P<0.001) (Figure 4). The funnel plot, sensitivity analysis, and subgroup analyses by ADC type and target are shown in Figures S11-S14. Because DOR is conditional on response and is affected by assessment frequency and censoring, this pooled median is descriptive rather than comparative. To verify whether there is publication bias, we conducted the Begg and Egger tests again, with P<0.05 considered statistically significant. The results of both the Begg and Egger tests indicated no significant publication bias, and the results of these tests are shown in Table S2.

Forest plot of the duration of response depicted using the sample-size-weighted random-effects model. CI, confidence interval; DOR, duration of response.

PFS and OS

PFS data were available for 20 cohorts. The pooled median PFS was 4.36 months (95% CI, 3.87–4.92), with a 95% prediction interval of 2.62–7.27 months and substantial heterogeneity (I2=84.8%, P<0.001) (Figure 5). OS data were available for 12 cohorts. The pooled median OS was 7.48 months (95% CI, 6.15–9.10), with a 95% prediction interval of 3.58–15.66 months and substantial heterogeneity (I2=92.6%, P<0.001) (Figure 6). Funnel plots, sensitivity analyses, and subgroup analyses by ADC type and target are presented in Figures S15-S20. Direct pooling of medians remains exploratory because follow-up and censoring differed across studies. We further evaluated publication bias using the Begg and Egger tests, with P<0.05 considered statistically significant. Both the Begg and Egger tests showed no significant publication bias, and the results of these tests are shown in Table S2.

Sensitivity analyses and reporting bias

Leave-one-out analyses did not identify a single study that fully explained the pooled results, although the magnitude of the estimates varied when large phase III studies or preliminary conference cohorts were removed (Figures S3,S8,S12,S16,S18). Subgroup analyses by study design, intervention, and target suggested that intervention characteristics and trial context contributed importantly to heterogeneity. Funnel plots were generated for outcomes with at least 10 contributing cohorts and are presented in Figures S2,S7,S11,S15,S17. They were interpreted descriptively and cautiously because the number of cohorts was limited and marked clinical heterogeneity can itself produce funnel-plot asymmetry.

Agent-specific safety findings

Safety reporting was not sufficiently standardized for quantitative pooling: the included studies variably reported all-cause TEAEs, investigator-attributed TRAEs, grade ≥3 events, and safety populations that did not always match the SCLC efficacy cohorts. Accordingly, safety was summarized study by study and by agent in Table 2, with the original event terminology and denominator retained for each report. Hematologic toxicity was prominent with several payload-bearing agents, whereas serosal effusions, edema, photosensitivity, ILD/pneumonitis, gastrointestinal toxicity, and neurologic or cytokine-release events varied by construct. Table 2 also distinguishes SCLC-specific safety populations from broader pan-tumor or lung-cancer safety datasets. Because event attribution, grading, dose, follow-up, and source populations differed, the reported rates should not be compared directly across products.

Discussion

SCLC remains a highly aggressive malignancy characterized by frequent relapse and limited long-term survival. In this review, the pooled ORR and DCR were 34.38% and 77.50%, respectively, while the pooled median DOR, PFS, and OS were 4.99, 4.36, and 7.48 months. These findings indicate antitumor activity across the included studies, but substantial clinical and statistical heterogeneity was present. Differences in target, molecular construct, dose, trial era, treatment line, prior therapy, and patient selection mean that the pooled estimates represent broad summaries of the available evidence rather than expected outcomes for any individual product.

Clinical heterogeneity is biologically plausible because efficacy and toxicity depend on the complete construct rather than the target alone (31-36). Antigen density, internalization, and intracellular trafficking influence tumor delivery, while interpatient and intratumoral variation in target expression may leave antigen-low subclones insufficiently exposed. Antigen loss, altered lysosomal processing, and drug-efflux mechanisms may also contribute to primary or acquired resistance. These factors explain why target expression alone cannot reliably predict clinical benefit.

Linker and payload properties further shape the therapeutic index (37-41). Linkers must remain stable in circulation while releasing the payload efficiently at the intended site; cleavable linkers and membrane-permeable payloads may enhance bystander killing but can also increase systemic exposure. Payload class, intracellular retention, susceptibility to efflux, the drug-to-antibody ratio, and dose influence both activity and toxicity. The included studies evaluated distinct payloads and dosing schedules, including pyrrolobenzodiazepine-, topoisomerase I inhibitor-, and calicheamicin-related constructs (7,14,17-20). These differences provide a biological basis for variation in response, resistance, and adverse-event patterns and show why statistical heterogeneity alone cannot establish pharmacological comparability.

The pooled efficacy outcomes therefore require cautious interpretation. Heterogeneity exceeded 90% for ORR and DCR, and prediction intervals for DOR, PFS, and OS were wide. Subgroup analyses by ADC type and target were exploratory because target, payload, dose, trial phase, treatment line, prior therapy, follow-up, and response assessment were strongly confounded. Response assessment, follow-up duration, censoring, and the maturity of survival data also varied across studies. Observed differences may therefore reflect both biological variation and differences in trial populations or design and should not be interpreted as indirect comparisons.

Results from DLL3-directed therapy illustrate why the complete construct and clinical context matter. Rova-T produced an early response signal, but later phase II and III trials did not demonstrate a favorable survival benefit-risk profile and identified toxicities that limited development (7-10). This does not invalidate DLL3 as a target; newer DLL3-directed programs use different molecular designs and dosing strategies and require independent evaluation (15,24). The same applies to B7-H3-directed agents: ifinatamab deruxtecan, YL201, and HS-20093 have shown encouraging early activity, but their constructs, doses, source populations, and maturity of follow-up differ (14,19-21). Current evidence therefore supports continued agent-specific investigation rather than a uniform target-level conclusion.

SEZ6-, TROP2-, and HER3-directed programs further broaden the treatment landscape. ABBV-011 and ABBV-706 target SEZ6 but use different payloads and development strategies (17,18). Sacituzumab govitecan has shown activity in SCLC, including TROPiCS-03, and izalontamab brengitecan has generated an early signal as a distinct HER3-directed bispecific construct (22,23,30). Evidence for these targets remains limited by small or early-phase cohorts, nonuniform eligibility criteria, and immature survival follow-up. The findings are best viewed as priorities for prospective validation rather than evidence of a definitive target hierarchy.

Safety findings also favor agent-specific interpretation. Hematologic, gastrointestinal, pulmonary, neurologic, constitutional, and infusion-related events varied across products, doses, attribution rules, and observation periods. A class-wide toxicity rate would obscure clinically relevant signals. We therefore summarized safety by agent and source population and identified when denominators came from pan-tumor or broader lung-cancer populations rather than SCLC-specific cohorts. This distinction is essential because numerical differences may reflect reporting and source-population differences as well as pharmacology.

Tumor heterogeneity and treatment resistance remain major challenges. Variation in antigen density, treatment-induced changes in target expression, DNA-damage response, and drug efflux may influence sensitivity to a given construct. Future studies should standardize target-expression assays, scoring, cutoffs, timing, and tissue sources and integrate biomarkers with construct design. Combination strategies may broaden activity but should be tested prospectively because they introduce additional toxicity and confounding. Serial tissue or circulating biomarker analyses may also help determine whether antigen loss or pathway adaptation accompanies treatment resistance.

Clinically, these findings support continued evaluation of individual agents but do not justify a class-wide interpretation. Future randomized trials should standardize molecular construct reporting, target-expression methods, platinum sensitivity, prior immunotherapy and topoisomerase I inhibitor exposure, brain metastases, treatment line, dose intensity, response assessment, follow-up, and prespecified adverse events. Mature comparative survival data and biomarker-stratified analyses are needed to determine whether a specific product improves clinically meaningful outcomes and which patients are most likely to benefit.

This review has several limitations. First, most evidence was derived from early-phase, non-comparative cohorts, and extracting only an eligible treatment arm from randomized trials forfeited the protection provided by a concurrent comparator. Second, the interventions were heterogeneous in target, antibody architecture, linker, payload, drug-to-antibody ratio, dose, treatment line, prior therapy, and trial era; subgroup analyses explored but could not eliminate this heterogeneity. Third, direct pooling of reported medians was exploratory and could not fully account for differences in censoring, assessment schedules, or follow-up; hazard ratios or survival probabilities at standardized time points would be preferable when consistently available. Fourth, some evidence was available only as conference reports, and several cohorts were small, increasing imprecision and the possibility that estimates may change with mature follow-up. Fifth, safety definitions and denominators were not standardized, and some safety statements relied on pan-tumor or broader lung-cancer populations because SCLC-specific values were unavailable. Sixth, the number of independent cohorts was limited for several outcomes, reducing the power and interpretability of funnel plots and regression-based assessments of reporting bias. Seventh, the inclusion of studies from different treatment settings—from first-line maintenance to heavily pretreated relapsed or refractory disease—limits the clinical meaning of a single overall estimate. Finally, eligibility was restricted to reports with sufficient information available in English, and Chinese-language bibliographic databases were not searched. Although eligible studies conducted in China and other Asian settings were captured through international databases, trial registries, and oncology conference proceedings, studies reported exclusively in regional-language sources may have been missed; residual language and publication bias therefore cannot be excluded.

Conclusions

This systematic review and single-arm meta-analysis indicates that ADC-based therapy can produce antitumor activity in advanced SCLC, but marked clinical and pharmacological heterogeneity limits the interpretation of pooled estimates. Agent-specific randomized trials with standardized efficacy, safety, and biomarker reporting are needed.
