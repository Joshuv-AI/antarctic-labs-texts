Error analysis of a fully discrete structure-preserving finite element scheme for a diffuse-interface model of tumour growth
Agus L. Soenjaya, Ping Lin, Thanh Tran

Abstract
. We develop a fully discrete structure-preserving finite element method for a diffuse-interface model of tumour growth. The system couples a Cahn–Hilliard type equation with a nonlinear reaction-diffusion equation for nutrient concentration and admits a dissipative energy law at the continuous level. For the discretisation, we employ a scalar auxiliary variable (SAV) formulation together with a mixed finite element method for the Cahn–Hilliard part and standard conforming finite elements for the reaction-diffusion equation in space, combined with a first-order Euler time-stepping scheme. The resulting method is linear, unconditionally energy-stable, mass-preserving, and inherits a discrete energy dissipation law associated with the SAV-based approximate energy functional, while requiring the solution of only linear systems at each time step. Under suitable regularity assumptions on the exact solution, we derive rigorous error estimates in L 2 L^{2} , H 1 H^{1} , and L ∞ L^{\infty} norms, establishing first-order accuracy in time and optimal-order accuracy in space. A key step in this analysis is the proof of boundedness of the numerical solutions in L ∞ L^{\infty} . Numerical experiments validate the theoretical convergence rates and demonstrate the robustness of the method in capturing characteristic phenomena such as aggregation and chemotactic tumour growth.



1.
Introduction

Based on the continuum theory of mixtures
[
21
]
, Hawkins-Daarud, van der Zee, and Oden develop a thermodynamically consistent four-species model of tumour growth
[
13
]
(see also
[
5
,
10
]
for related models and
[
30
]
for a simplified derivation). The formulation introduces several unknowns, which we now describe. Let
\mathscr{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be an open and bounded domain, and let
u:\mathscr{D}\to\mathbb{R}
and
n:\mathscr{D}\to\mathbb{R}
denote the tumour cell volume fraction and the nutrient-rich extracellular water volume fraction, respectively. Physical consideration dictates that the Helmholtz free energy of the system is given by

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}[u,n]:=\int_{\mathscr{D}}\left(\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}\left|{\nabla u}\right|^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}u^{2}+f(u)+\frac{1}{2\delta}n^{2}-\chi_{0}un\right)\mathrm{d}x,

where
f(u)
is the classical Cahn–Hilliard free-energy density function.
Here,
\epsilon^{2}
is a small diffusivity parameter related to the thickness of interfacial layers,
\lambda>0
is a constant to be specified later,
\chi_{0}>0
is a chemotaxis parameter, and
\delta\in(0,1)
is a small parameter that determines the relative interaction strength between the cells and nutrient species.
The chemical potentials associated to the variables
u
and
n
, respectively denoted by
\mu
and
\sigma
, are given by the variational derivative of the Helmholtz free energy (
1.1
) with respect to each variable.

The unknowns described above satisfy a system of coupled nonlinear PDEs which can be formulated as follows: Find
(u,\mu,n):\mathscr{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
satisfying

(1.2a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u=\Delta\mu+P(u)\cdot(\sigma-\mu)\qquad

for
(t,\boldsymbol{x})\in(0,T)\times\mathscr{D}
,

(1.2b)

\displaystyle\mu=-\epsilon^{2}\Delta u+\lambda u-\chi_{0}n+f^{\prime}(u)\qquad

for
(t,\boldsymbol{x})\in(0,T)\times\mathscr{D}
,

(1.2c)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}n=\Delta\sigma-P(u)\cdot(\sigma-\mu)\qquad

for
(t,\boldsymbol{x})\in(0,T)\times\mathscr{D}
,

(1.2d)

\displaystyle\sigma=\delta^{-1}n-\chi_{0}u\qquad

for
(t,\boldsymbol{x})\in(0,T)\times\mathscr{D}
,

(1.2e)

\displaystyle u(0,\boldsymbol{x})=u_{0}(\boldsymbol{x}),\quad n(0,\boldsymbol{x})=n_{0}(\boldsymbol{x})\qquad

\displaystyle\text{for }\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathscr{D},

(1.2f)

\displaystyle\partial_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}u=\partial_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\mu=\partial_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}n=0\qquad

\displaystyle\text{for }(t,\boldsymbol{x})\in(0,T)\times\partial\mathscr{D},

where
\boldsymbol{\nu}
is the outward pointing normal vector and
P
is a non-negative proliferation function. The exact assumptions on the functions
P
and
f
will be given later. As described in
[
13
]
, the model (
1.2
) has a natural four-constituent interpretation: a tumorous phase
u\approx 1
, a healthy cell phase
u\approx 0
, a nutrient-rich extracellular water phase
\sigma\approx 1
, and a nutrient-poor extracellular water phase
\sigma\approx 0
.

Relevant mathematical results on the problem (
1.2
) pertinent to the present work will be reviewed next. Unique existence of weak and strong solutions, as well as the existence of global attractor are established in
[
12
]
(see also
[
9
]
for a simplified model). A characterisation of the singular limit of the problem to the corresponding sharp-interface model is discussed in
[
15
]
, with a rigorous proof given more recently in
[
24
]
. An optimal control problem for (
1.2
) is studied in
[
16
]
. On the numerical side, several schemes to solve (
1.2
) are described in
[
13
]
based on the convex splitting approach. A linear second-order scheme is further proposed in
[
31
]
, however no error analysis is performed for the schemes in any of these papers. A Fourier-spectral first-order scheme utilising the scalar auxiliary variable (SAV) approach is later proposed in
[
28
]
. While this scheme is linear, mass-preserving, and energy-dissipative, the method is applicable mainly to rectangular domains with periodic boundary conditions, and again no error analysis is given.

More recently, numerical analysis of a first-order scheme to solve (
1.2
) employing a mass-lumping technique is performed in
[
11
]
, where convergence along a subsequence to the exact solution is shown, albeit without a convergence rate. However, this scheme is nonlinear and does not necessarily guarantee mass conservation or energy dissipation at the discrete level. In contrast, structure-preserving numerical schemes employing Fourier-spectral spatial discretisation combined with the SAV approach are proposed in
[
30
]
to solve the problem under periodic boundary conditions. For this setting, an error analysis of the time-semidiscrete case for the first-order scheme is performed, establishing an optimal
temporal
order of convergence, although it is still limited to rectangular domains
\mathscr{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with periodic boundary conditions. To the best of our knowledge, a
fully discrete
,
linear
, and
structure-preserving
numerical scheme for (
1.2
), together with a rigorous error analysis, has not yet been developed in the literature.

In this work, we aim to fill this gap by proposing a fully discrete, linear, and structure-preserving numerical method for (
1.2
), and providing its rigorous error analysis. Specifically, we employ an SAV formulation
[
26
]
together with a mixed finite element method for the Cahn–Hilliard part and standard conforming finite elements for the reaction-diffusion part, combined with a first-order Euler time-stepping scheme. This mass-preserving linear scheme inherits a discrete energy dissipation law from the SAV formulation, but differs from the one introduced in
[
30
]
to facilitate the analysis in the fully discrete setting considered here. Furthermore, we consider a three-dimensional convex domain. Despite the strongly coupled and nonlinear nature of the underlying equation, we establish unconditional energy stability and prove optimal-order convergence of the numerical scheme, assuming adequate regularity of the exact solution.

The convergence analysis carried out here also differs fundamentally from that in
[
30
]
. In particular, our finite element functions possess only
H^{1}
spatial regularity, which prevents us from using the
H^{2}
regularity estimates that play a central role in the time-semidiscrete analysis of
[
30
]
. To overcome this difficulty, we derive a number of delicate estimates for the nonlinear terms, relying on discrete Sobolev embeddings and discrete Gagliardo–Nirenberg inequalities. A key ingredient is the derivation of
L^{\infty}
bounds for the finite element solution, together with estimates for its discrete time derivative in negative-order Sobolev spaces. Furthermore, we employ the Ritz projection of the solution and establish superconvergence estimates for the difference between the finite element solution and its Ritz projection. These tools allow us to obtain optimal error estimates not only in
H^{s}
for
s\in\{0,1\}
, but also in
L^{\infty}
.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the notations and assumptions, present a formulation of the problem based on the SAV approach, and collect various preliminary results on finite element approximation. Section
3
describes the numerical scheme, establishes its mass conservation and energy stability properties, and provides the error estimates. In particular, the scheme is detailed in Algorithm
3.1
, and the main convergence result is stated in Theorem
3.18
. Section
4
reports several numerical experiments that validate the theoretical results. Finally, Section
5
concludes the paper with some final remarks.

2.
Preliminaries

Commonly used notations, standing assumptions, as well as preliminary results on the finite element approximation and SAV formulation of (
1.2
) are collected in this section.

2.1.
Notations

Let
\mathscr{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
d=1,2,3

be an open and bounded convex polytopal domain. Let
\Delta
denote the Neumann Laplacian operator. The function space
L^{p}:=L^{p}(\mathscr{D})
denotes the usual Lebesgue space of
p
-th integrable functions and
W^{s,p}:=W^{s,p}(\mathscr{D})
denotes the usual Sobolev space of
functions on
\mathscr{D}
. We
write
H^{s}:=W^{s,2}
and set
W^{0,p}=L^{p}
.

If
X
is a Banach space, the spaces
L^{p}(0,T;X)
and
W^{s,p}(0,T;X)
denote respectively the usual Lebesgue and Sobolev spaces of functions on
(0,T)
taking values in
X
. The space
\mathcal{C}([0,T];X)
denotes the space of continuous functions on
[0,T]
taking values in
X
. For simplicity, we write
W^{s,p}_{T}(W^{m,r}):=W^{s,p}(0,T;W^{m,r})
and
L^{p}_{T}(L^{q}):=L^{p}(0,T;L^{q})
. Throughout this paper, we denote the scalar product in a Hilbert space
H
by
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{H}
and its corresponding norm by
\|\cdot\|_{H}
. The scalar product in
L^{2}
will simply be denoted by
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
.

For any positive integer
s
, the dual space of
H^{s}
with respect to the dual pairing which is the extension of the
L^{2}
inner product will be denoted by
\widetilde{H}^{-s}
. This space is equipped with the norm

\displaystyle\left\|{v}\right\|_{\widetilde{H}^{-s}}:=\sup_{0\neq\varphi\in H^{s}}\,\frac{\left\langle v,\varphi\right\rangle}{\left\|{\varphi}\right\|_{H^{s}}}.

Finally, the constant
C
in the estimate denotes a generic constant which takes different values at different occurrences. If
the dependence of
C
on some variable, e.g.
T
, is highlighted, we will write
C_{T}
or

C(T)
.

2.2.
Assumptions

Assumptions on the model used in this paper are stated in this subsection.

Assumption 2.1
.

We assume that the proliferation function
P:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
lies in
W^{1,\infty}
and is non-negative. In particular,
P
is Lipschitz continuous, namely there exists a constant
C>0
such that for any
v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
,

(2.1)

\displaystyle\left|{P(v_{1})-P(v_{2})}\right|\leq C\left|{v_{1}-v_{2}}\right|.

This implies a sublinear growth for
P
:

(2.2)

\displaystyle\left|{P(v)}\right|\leq C\left(1+\left|{v}\right|\right).

Assumption 2.2
.

We assume that the Cahn–Hilliard free-energy density function
f
is of the form
f(s)=g(s)-\frac{\lambda}{2}s^{2}
, where
g\in C^{3}(\mathbb{R})
, such that
f
is bounded below. Moreover, there exists a constant
C>0
such that for
m\in\{0,1,2,3\}
and any
s\in\mathbb{R}
,

(2.3)

\displaystyle\left|{f^{(m)}(s)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left(1+\left|{s}\right|^{4-m}\right).

Here,
f^{(m)}
denotes the
m
-th derivative of
f
and
f^{(0)}=f
.

Remark 2.3
.

The commonly used double-well (quartic) potential
f
and linear proliferation function
P
satisfy the above assumptions. For concreteness, as in
[
13
,
30
]
, we fix
f(u)=\kappa u^{2}(1-u)^{2}-\frac{\lambda}{2}u^{2}
for a positive constant
\kappa
. Note that the term containing
\lambda
actually vanishes from the free energy (
1.1
), thus
\lambda
can be arbitrarily chosen. For numerical stability, we set
\lambda=4\chi_{0}^{2}
, since the cubic nonlinearity will be treated explicitly in the numerical scheme (see (
3.1b
)). This choice is not strictly necessary in practice, and we do not investigate the optimal value of
\lambda
here. A similar stabilisation approach is also employed in
[
14
,
27
]
; see
[
32
]
for a detailed study of optimal choices of
\lambda
and comparisons with other methods. The function
P
is defined to be

(2.4)

P(u)=\begin{cases}\delta p_{0}u,&\text{if $u\geq 0$},\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
p_{0}
is a positive constant denoting the proliferation growth parameter.

Remark 2.4
.

As a consequence of Assumption
2.2
, by the mean value theorem we have for
m\in\{0,1,2\}
any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\left|{f^{(m)}(x)-f^{(m)}(y)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|{f^{(m+1)}\big((1-t)x+ty\big)}\right|\right)\left|{x-y}\right|

(2.5)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(1+\left|{x}\right|^{3-m}+\left|{y}\right|^{3-m}\right)\left|{x-y}\right|,

where
C
is a constant independent of
x
or
y
.

2.3.
SAV formulations

We now present a reformulation of (
1.2
) that introduces an auxiliary variable, which will facilitate the development of numerical schemes subsequently.
Let
\mathcal{E}[u,n]
be the energy functional given by (
1.1
).
Define

(2.6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{1}[u]:=\int_{\mathscr{D}}f(u)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Note that
\mathcal{E}_{1}[u]>-B
for some constant
B
by our assumption on
f
. We introduce a scalar variable
r(t):=\mathcal{E}_{1}[u]+B>0
, and so
r(0)=\mathcal{E}_{1}[u_{0}]+B
. With these functions, we can rewrite the problem (
1.2
) following the SAV approach as follows:

(2.7a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u=\Delta\mu+P(u)\cdot(\sigma-\mu)\qquad

for
(t,\boldsymbol{x})\in(0,T)\times\mathscr{D}
,

(2.7b)

\displaystyle\mu=-\epsilon^{2}\Delta u+\lambda u-\chi_{0}n+\frac{r(t)}{\mathcal{E}_{1}[u]+B}\,f^{\prime}(u)\qquad

for
(t,\boldsymbol{x})\in(0,T)\times\mathscr{D}
,

(2.7c)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}n=\Delta\sigma-P(u)\cdot(\sigma-\mu)\qquad

for
(t,\boldsymbol{x})\in(0,T)\times\mathscr{D}
,

(2.7d)

\displaystyle\sigma=\delta^{-1}n-\chi_{0}u\qquad

for
(t,\boldsymbol{x})\in(0,T)\times\mathscr{D}
,

(2.7e)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}r=\frac{r(t)}{\mathcal{E}_{1}[u]+B}\left\langle f^{\prime}(u),\partial_{t}u\right\rangle\qquad

for
(t,\boldsymbol{x})\in(0,T)\times\mathscr{D}
,

(2.7f)

\displaystyle u(0,\boldsymbol{x})=u_{0}(\boldsymbol{x}),\quad n(0,\boldsymbol{x})=n_{0}(\boldsymbol{x})\qquad

\displaystyle\text{for }\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathscr{D},

(2.7g)

\displaystyle\partial_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}u=\partial_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\mu=\partial_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}n=0\qquad

\displaystyle\text{for }(t,\boldsymbol{x})\in(0,T)\times\partial\mathscr{D},

The system (
2.7
) consists of a fourth-order Cahn–Hilliard-type PDE with nonlinear source terms, coupled with a second-order nonlinear reaction-diffusion equation and an ODE.
Taking the inner product of (
2.7a
), (
2.7b
), (
2.7c
), and (
2.7d
) with
\mu
,
-\partial_{t}u
,
\sigma
, and
-\partial_{t}n
respectively, then adding the resulting equations to (
2.7e
), we obtain

(2.8)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{E}[u,n]\leq 0,

which is the same energy dissipation law obeyed by (
1.2
).

A weak formulation of the system (
2.7
) can now be defined.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
T>0
and
(u_{0},n_{0})\in H^{1}\times L^{2}
be given. A weak solution to the system (
2.7
) is
(u,\mu,n,r)
with regularity

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle\in H^{1}_{T}(L^{2})\cap L^{\infty}_{T}(H^{1})\cap L^{2}_{T}(H^{2}),

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle\in L^{2}_{T}(H^{1}),

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle\in H^{1}_{T}(L^{2})\cap L^{\infty}_{T}(L^{2})\cap L^{2}_{T}(H^{1}),

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle\in L^{\infty}(0,T),

such that
\big(u(0),n(0)\big)=(u_{0},n_{0})
, and that for all
(\phi,\psi,\varphi)\in H^{1}\times H^{1}\times H^{1}
and
t\in(0,T)
,

(2.9a)

\displaystyle\left\langle\partial_{t}u(t),\phi\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-\left\langle\nabla\mu(t),\nabla\phi\right\rangle+\left\langle P(u(t))\cdot(\sigma(t)-\mu(t)),\phi\right\rangle

(2.9b)

\displaystyle\left\langle\mu(t),\psi\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\epsilon^{2}\left\langle\nabla u(t),\nabla\psi\right\rangle+\lambda\left\langle u(t),\psi\right\rangle-\chi_{0}\left\langle n(t),\psi\right\rangle+\frac{r(t)}{\mathcal{E}_{1}[u(t)]+B}\left\langle f^{\prime}(u(t)),\psi\right\rangle,

(2.9c)

\displaystyle\left\langle\partial_{t}n(t),\varphi\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-\left\langle\nabla\sigma(t),\nabla\varphi\right\rangle-\left\langle P(u(t))\cdot(\sigma(t)-\mu(t)),\varphi\right\rangle,

(2.9d)

\displaystyle\sigma(t)

\displaystyle=\delta^{-1}n(t)-\chi_{0}u(t),

(2.9e)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}r(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{r(t)}{\mathcal{E}_{1}[u(t)]+B}\left\langle f^{\prime}(u(t)),\partial_{t}u(t)\right\rangle.

The assumed regularity on weak solution implies that
u\in\mathcal{C}([0,T];H^{1})
and
n\in\mathcal{C}([0,T];L^{2})
by interpolation theorems, thus the requirement
\big(u(0),n(0)\big)=(u_{0},n_{0})\in H^{1}\times L^{2}
is meaningful.

2.4.
Finite element approximation

Let
\mathscr{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d=1,2,3
, be a convex polytopal domain. Let
\mathcal{T}_{h}
be a quasi-uniform triangulation of
\mathscr{D}

into intervals (in 1D), triangles (in 2D), or tetrahedra (in 3D)
with maximal mesh-size
h
.
To discretise the problem (
2.7
), we
introduce the conforming finite element space
V_{h}\subset H^{1}
given by

(2.10)

V_{h}:=\{\phi_{h}\in\mathcal{C}(\overline{\mathscr{D}}):\phi|_{K}\in\mathcal{P}_{q}(K),\;\forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\},

where
\mathcal{P}_{q}(K)
denotes the space of polynomials of order less than or equal to
q
on
K
.

As a consequence of the Bramble–Hilbert lemma, for
p\in[1,\infty]
, there exists a constant
C
independent of
h
such that for any
v\in W^{q+1,p}
, we have

(2.11)

\displaystyle\inf_{\chi\in V_{h}}\left\{\left\|{v-\chi}\right\|_{L^{p}}+h\left\|{\nabla v-\nabla\chi)}\right\|_{L^{p}}\right\}\leq Ch^{q+1}\left\|{v}\right\|_{W^{q+1,p}}.

In the analysis, we shall use several projection and interpolation operators. The existence of such operators and the properties that they possess will be described below (also see
[
2
]
).
Firstly, there exists an orthogonal projection operator
\Pi_{h}:L^{2}\to V_{h}
such that

(2.12)

\displaystyle\left\langle\Pi_{h}v-v,\chi\right\rangle=0,\quad\forall\chi\in V_{h}.

The projection operator
\Pi_{h}
has the following boundedness and approximation properties
[
6
,
7
]
: for any
p\in[1,\infty]
, there exists a constant
C
independent of
h
such that

(2.13)

\displaystyle\left\|{\Pi_{h}v}\right\|_{W^{s,p}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|{v}\right\|_{W^{s,p}},\quad\forall v\in W^{s,p},\;s\in\{0,1\},

(2.14)

\displaystyle\left\|{v-\Pi_{h}v}\right\|_{L^{p}}+h\left\|{\nabla(v-\Pi_{h}v)}\right\|_{L^{p}}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{q+1}\left\|{v}\right\|_{W^{q+1,p}},\quad\forall v\in W^{q+1,p}.

It is well-known that
\Pi_{h}
is also bounded in
\widetilde{H}^{-1}
norm
[
3
]
.

Next, we introduce the discrete Laplacian operator
\Delta_{h}:V_{h}\to V_{h}
defined by

(2.15)

\displaystyle\left\langle\Delta_{h}v_{h},\chi\right\rangle=-\left\langle\nabla v_{h},\nabla\chi\right\rangle,\quad\forall v_{h},\chi\in V_{h}.

The discrete Laplacian operator satisfies the following estimates
[
3
,
29
]
:

(2.16)

\displaystyle\left\|{\nabla v}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{v}\right\|_{L^{2}}\left\|{\Delta_{h}v}\right\|_{L^{2}},

(2.17)

\displaystyle\left\|{\Delta_{h}v}\right\|_{\widetilde{H}^{-1}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|{\nabla v}\right\|_{L^{2}},

(2.18)

\displaystyle\left\|{\Delta_{h}v}\right\|_{\widetilde{H}^{-2}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|{v}\right\|_{L^{2}}

We also define the Ritz projection
R_{h}:H^{1}\to V_{h}
by

(2.19)

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla R_{h}v-\nabla v,\nabla\chi\right\rangle=0,\quad\forall\chi\in V_{h},\quad\text{such that }\;\left\langle R_{h}v-v,1\right\rangle=0.

Let
w:=v-R_{h}v
. The approximation property for the Ritz projection
[
2
,
18
,
19
,
23
]
holds, namely for any
p\in(1,\infty)
, there exists a constant
C
independent of
h
such that

(2.20)

\displaystyle\left\|{w}\right\|_{W^{s,p}}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{q+1-s}\left\|{v}\right\|_{W^{q+1,p}},\quad s\in\{0,1\},

(2.21)

\displaystyle\left\|{w}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{q+1}\left|{\ln h}\right|^{\frac{1}{2}}\left\|{v}\right\|_{W^{q+1,\infty}}.

We also need the following approximation property of the Ritz projection
R_{h}
in Sobolev spaces of negative indices
[
3
,
29
]
: for
0\leq s\leq q-1
and
1\leq p\leq q+1
, there exists a constant
C
independent of
h
such that

(2.22)

\displaystyle\left\|{w}\right\|_{\widetilde{H}^{-s}}\leq Ch^{s+p}\left\|{v}\right\|_{H^{p}}.

Finally, in a convex polytopal domain with quasi-uniform triangulation, the following discrete Gagliardo–Nirenberg inequalities
[
20
]
hold for any
v_{h}\in V_{h}
:

(2.23)

\displaystyle\left\|{v_{h}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|{\Delta_{h}v_{h}}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{d}{2(6-d)}}\left\|{v_{h}}\right\|_{L^{6}}^{\frac{3(4-d)}{2(6-d)}}+C\left\|{v_{h}}\right\|_{L^{6}},

(2.24)

\displaystyle\left\|{\nabla v_{h}}\right\|_{L^{3}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|{\Delta_{h}v_{h}}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{d}{6}}\left\|{\nabla v_{h}}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{6-d}{6}}+C\left\|{\nabla v_{h}}\right\|_{L^{2}}.

Here, the constant
C
depends only on
\mathscr{D}
and
d
.

3.
SAV-FEM with backward Euler time discretisation

Let
(u_{h}^{k},\mu_{h}^{k},n_{h}^{k},\sigma_{h}^{k},r_{h}^{k})
be the approximation in
[V_{h}]^{4}\times\mathbb{R}
of
\big(u(t_{k}),\mu(t_{k}),n(t_{k}),\sigma(t_{k}),r(t_{k})\big)
, where
t_{k}:=k\tau\in[0,T]
for
k=0,1,\ldots,N
and
N:=\lfloor T/\tau\rfloor
. For any function
v
, we write
v^{k}:=v(t_{k})
, and define the discrete time derivative of
v^{k+1}
by

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{t}v^{k+1}:=\frac{1}{\tau}(v^{k+1}-v^{k}).

A linear fully-discrete SAV-FEM with backward Euler time discretisation can be described as follows.
We start with
(u_{h}^{0},n_{h}^{0})=(R_{h}u_{0},R_{h}n_{0})\in V_{h}\times V_{h}
and
r_{h}^{0}=r(0)\in\mathbb{R}
for simplicity. Given

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathrm{d}_{t}u_{h}^{k+1},\phi\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-\left\langle\nabla\mu_{h}^{k+1},\nabla\phi\right\rangle+\left\langle P(u_{h}^{k})\cdot(\sigma_{h}^{k+1}-\mu_{h}^{k+1}),\phi\right\rangle,\quad

\displaystyle\forall\phi\in V_{h},

\displaystyle\left\langle\mu_{h}^{k+1},\psi\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\epsilon^{2}\left\langle\nabla u_{h}^{k+1},\nabla\psi\right\rangle+\lambda\left\langle u_{h}^{k+1},\psi\right\rangle-\chi_{0}\left\langle n_{h}^{k+1},\psi\right\rangle

(3.1b)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{r_{h}^{k}}{\mathcal{E}_{1}[u_{h}^{k}]+B}\left\langle f^{\prime}(u_{h}^{k}),\psi\right\rangle,\quad

\displaystyle\forall\psi\in V_{h},

(3.1c)

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathrm{d}_{t}n_{h}^{k+1},\varphi\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-\left\langle\nabla\sigma_{h}^{k+1},\nabla\varphi\right\rangle-\left\langle P(u_{h}^{k})\cdot(\sigma_{h}^{k+1}-\mu_{h}^{k+1}),\varphi\right\rangle,\quad

\displaystyle\forall\varphi\in V_{h},

(3.1d)

\displaystyle\sigma_{h}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\delta^{-1}n_{h}^{k+1}-\chi_{0}u_{h}^{k},

(3.1e)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{t}r_{h}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{r_{h}^{k}}{\mathcal{E}_{1}[u_{h}^{k}]+B}\left\langle f^{\prime}(u_{h}^{k}),\mathrm{d}_{t}u_{h}^{k+1}\right\rangle.

Proposition 3.2

Let

Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{\xi})

\displaystyle\quad+\epsilon^{2}\left\langle\nabla u,\nabla\psi\right\rangle+\lambda\left\langle u,\psi\right\rangle-\chi_{0}\left\langle w,\psi\right\rangle+\tau\delta^{-1}\left\langle\nabla w,\nabla\varphi\right\rangle+\tau\left\langle P(u_{h}^{k})\cdot(\delta^{-1}w-v),\varphi\right\rangle,

\displaystyle\quad+\tau\chi_{0}\left\langle P(u_{h}^{k})u_{h}^{k},\varphi-\phi\right\rangle-\frac{r_{h}^{k}}{\mathcal{E}_{1}[u_{h}^{k}]+B}\left\langle f^{\prime}(u_{h}^{k}),\psi\right\rangle,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z})

\displaystyle=\left\|{w}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\tau\left\|{\nabla v}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\epsilon^{2}\left\|{\nabla u}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\lambda\left\|{u}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-\chi_{0}\left\langle w,u\right\rangle+\tau\delta^{-1}\left\|{\nabla w}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\left|{\chi_{0}\left\langle w,u\right\rangle}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4}\left\|{w}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\chi_{0}^{2}\left\|{u}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

\displaystyle\left|{\tau\left\langle P(u_{h}^{k})v,w\right\rangle}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4}\tau\left\|{\sqrt{P(u_{h}^{k})}\,v}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\tau\left\|{\sqrt{P(u_{h}^{k})}\,w}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

\displaystyle\left|{\tau\delta^{-1}\left\langle P(u_{h}^{k})w,v\right\rangle}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4}\tau\left\|{\sqrt{P(u_{h}^{k})}\,v}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\delta^{-2}L\tau\left(1+\left\|{u_{h}^{k}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\right)\left\|{w}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

where

in (
2.2
). Therefore, continuing from (
3
), noting

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z})

\displaystyle\geq 3\chi_{0}^{2}\left\|{u}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\epsilon^{2}\left\|{\nabla u}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{\tau}{2}\left\|{\sqrt{P(u_{h}^{k})}\,v}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\tau\left\|{\nabla v}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+b_{k}\left\|{w}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\tau\delta^{-1}\left\|{\nabla w}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

Furthermore, by a standard argument, it is clear that

∎

\displaystyle\leq C(h^{2(q+1)}+\tau^{2})\,\tau\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left(1+\left\|{\mu_{h}^{j}}\right\|_{H^{1}}^{2}\right)+C\tau\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left(\left\|{\theta^{j-1}}\right\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+\left\|{\Delta_{h}\theta^{j-1}}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\left\|{\xi^{j}}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\left\|{\zeta^{j}}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C(h^{2(q+1)}+\tau^{2}),

where in the last step we used (
3.12
) and (
3.13
), thus proving (
3.76
). Finally, (
3.77
) follows from (
3.13
), (
3.76
), and the discrete Sobolev inequality in 2D.
∎

In the following proposition, we establish an auxiliary superconvergence estimate for
\left\|{\nabla\theta^{k}}\right\|_{L^{2}}
and
\left\|{\Delta_{h}\theta^{k}}\right\|_{L^{2}}
, which in turn yields an estimate for
\left\|{\theta^{k}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}
.

Proposition 3.16
.

For sufficiently small
h,\tau>0
, we have

(3.82)

\displaystyle\left\|{\nabla\theta^{k}}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\left\|{\Delta_{h}\theta^{k}}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\tau\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left\|{\Delta_{h}\xi^{j}}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq C(h^{2(q+1)}+\tau^{2}).

In particular, we have for
d\leq 3
,

(3.83)

\displaystyle\left\|{\theta^{k}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\leq C(h^{2(q+1)}+\tau^{2}),

where
C
depends on
T
, but is independent of
k
,
h
, and
\tau
.

Proof.

We set
\psi=\Delta_{h}\theta^{k+1}
in (
3.56b
) to obtain

\displaystyle\epsilon^{2}\left\|{\Delta_{h}\theta^{k+1}}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\lambda\left\|{\nabla\theta^{k+1}}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=-\left\langle\xi^{k+1}+\eta^{k+1},\Delta_{h}\theta^{k+1}\right\rangle+\lambda\left\langle\rho^{k+1},\Delta_{h}\theta^{k+1}\right\rangle-\chi_{0}\left\langle\zeta^{k+1}+\omega^{k+1},\Delta_{h}\theta^{k+1}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+r_{h}^{k}\left\langle\frac{f^{\prime}(u_{h}^{k})}{\mathcal{E}_{1}[u_{h}^{k}]+B}-\frac{f^{\prime}(u^{k})}{\mathcal{E}_{1}[u^{k}]+B},\Delta_{h}\theta^{k+1}\right\rangle+\frac{e^{k}}{\mathcal{E}_{1}[u^{k}]+B}\left\langle f^{\prime}(u^{k}),\Delta_{h}\theta^{k+1}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\left\langle\frac{r^{k}f^{\prime}(u^{k})}{\mathcal{E}_{1}[u^{k}]+B}-\frac{r^{k+1}f^{\prime}(u^{k+1})}{\mathcal{E}_{1}[u^{k+1}]+B},\Delta_{h}\theta^{k+1}\right\rangle

(3.84)

\displaystyle=P_{1}+P_{2}+\ldots+P_{6}.

The terms
P_{1}
,
P_{2}
, and
P_{3}
can be estimated by Young’s inequality. We have for any
\alpha>0
,

\displaystyle\left|{P_{1}}\right|+\left|{P_{2}}\right|+\left|{P_{3}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|{\xi^{k+1}}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+C\left\|{\zeta^{k+1}}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+Ch^{2(q+1)}+\alpha\left\|{\Delta_{h}\theta^{k+1}}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C(h^{2(q+1)}+\tau^{2})+\alpha\left\|{\Delta_{h}\theta^{k+1}}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

where in the last step we used (
3.76
) and (
3.13
). Similarly, for the next three terms, we use (
3.50
), (
3.51
), and Young’s inequality to obtain

\displaystyle\left|{P_{4}}\right|+\left|{P_{5}}\right|+\left|{P_{6}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C(h^{2(q+1)}+\tau^{2})+\alpha\left\|{\Delta_{h}\theta^{k+1}}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

Continuing from (
3
), by choosing
\alpha>0
sufficiently small to absorb the term
\left\|{\Delta_{h}\theta^{k+1}}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
, we show (
3.82
) for the first two terms on the left-hand side.

To show the inequality for the remaining term, we take
\phi=\tau\Delta_{h}\xi^{k+1}
in (
3.56
), sum over
j\in\{0,1,\ldots,k-1\}
, and apply Young’s inequality as before. Since the argument is straightforward by now, further details are omitted. Finally, inequality (
3.83
) follows by (
3.82
) and (
2.23
).
∎

Next, we show an auxiliary error estimate for
\left\|{\nabla\xi^{k}}\right\|_{L^{2}}
.

Proposition 3.17
.

For sufficiently small
h,\tau>0
, we have

(3.85)

\displaystyle\left\|{\nabla\xi^{k}}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\tau\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left\|{\nabla\Delta_{h}\xi^{j}}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq C(h^{2q}+\tau^{2}),

where
C
depends on
T
, but is independent of
k
,
h
, and
\tau
.

Proof.

Taking
\phi=\epsilon^{2}\Delta_{h}^{2}\xi^{k+1}
in (
3.56
), we have

\displaystyle\epsilon^{2}\left\|{\nabla\Delta_{h}\xi^{k+1}}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-\epsilon^{2}\left\langle\mathrm{d}_{t}\nabla\theta^{k+1},\nabla\Delta_{h}\xi^{k+1}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-\epsilon^{2}\left\langle\mathrm{d}_{t}\rho^{k+1}+\mathrm{d}_{t}u^{k+1}-\partial_{t}u^{k+1},\Delta_{h}^{2}\xi^{k+1}\right\rangle+\left\langle\big(P(u_{h}^{k})-P(u^{k+1})\big)\cdot(\sigma_{h}^{k+1}-\mu_{h}^{k+1}),\Delta_{h}^{2}\xi^{k+1}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\left\langle P(u^{k+1})\cdot\big(\delta^{-1}(\zeta^{k+1}+\omega^{k+1})-\chi_{0}(\theta^{k}+\rho^{k}+u^{k}-u^{k+1})\big),\Delta_{h}^{2}\xi^{k+1}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\quad-\left\langle P(u^{k+1})\cdot(\xi^{k+1}+\eta^{k+1}),\Delta_{h}^{2}\xi^{k+1}\right\rangle

(3.86)

\displaystyle=:Q_{1}+Q_{2}+Q_{3}+Q_{4}.

Next, setting
\psi=-\Delta_{h}\xi^{k+1}
in (

), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\left\|{\nabla\xi^{k+1}}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-\left\|{\nabla\xi^{k}}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\nabla\xi^{k+1}-\nabla\xi^{k}}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\epsilon^{2}\left\langle\nabla\mathrm{d}_{t}\theta^{k+1},\nabla\Delta_{h}\xi^{k+1}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\mathrm{d}_{t}\eta^{k+1}+\mathrm{d}_{t}\mu^{k+1}-\partial_{t}\mu^{k+1},\Delta_{h}\xi^{k+1}\right\rangle+\lambda\left\langle\mathrm{d}_{t}\theta^{k+1}+\mathrm{d}_{t}\rho^{k+1}+\mathrm{d}_{t}u^{k+1}-\partial u^{k+1},\Delta_{h}\xi^{k+1}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\quad-\chi_{0}\left\langle\mathrm{d}_{t}\zeta^{k+1}+\mathrm{d}_{t}\omega^{k+1}+\mathrm{d}_{t}n^{k+1}-\partial_{t}n^{k+1},\Delta_{h}\xi^{k+1}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\left\langle\frac{1}{\tau}\left(\frac{r_{h}^{k}}{\mathcal{E}_{1}[u_{h}^{k}]+B}-\frac{r_{h}^{k-1}}{\mathcal{E}_{1}[u_{h}^{k-1}]+B}\right)f^{\prime}(u_{h}^{k}),\Delta_{h}\xi^{k+1}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\left\langle\left(\frac{r_{h}^{k-1}}{\mathcal{E}_{1}[u_{h}^{k-1}]+B}\right)\left(\frac{f^{\prime}(u_{h}^{k})-f^{\prime}(u_{h}^{k-1})}{\tau}\right)-\left(\frac{r^{k+1}}{\mathcal{E}_{1}[u^{k+1}]+B}\right)f^{\prime\prime}(u^{k+1})\cdot\partial_{t}u^{k+1},\Delta_{h}\xi^{k+1}\right\rangle

(3.87)

\displaystyle=:R_{1}+R_{2}+\ldots+R_{5}.

We now add (
3
) and (

Estimates for
Q_{1}
to
Q_{4}
: Firstly, by Hölder’s and Young’s inequalities, noting (

), we have for any

,

\displaystyle\left|{Q_{1}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|{\mathrm{d}_{t}\rho^{k+1}+\mathrm{d}_{t}u^{k+1}-\partial_{t}u^{k+1}}\right\|_{H^{1}}\left\|{\Delta_{h}^{2}\xi^{k+1}}\right\|_{\widetilde{H}^{-1}}

Next, by Hölder’s and Young’s inequalities, noting (

\displaystyle\left|{Q_{2}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|{\theta^{k}+\rho^{k}+u^{k}-u^{k+1}}\right\|_{H^{1}}\left\|{\sigma_{h}^{k+1}-\mu_{h}^{k+1}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\left\|{\Delta_{h}^{2}\xi^{k+1}}\right\|_{\widetilde{H}^{-1}}

We can now state our main theorem, which shows optimal order convergence in various norms.

Theorem 3.18
.

Let
(u_{h}^{k},\mu_{h}^{k},n_{h}^{k},r_{h}^{k})
and
(u,\mu,n,r)
be the solutions of (

) and (
2.7

. For sufficiently small

and

\displaystyle\left\|{u_{h}^{k}-u(t_{k})}\right\|_{H^{s}}+\left\|{\mu_{h}^{k}-\mu(t_{k})}\right\|_{H^{s}}+\left\|{n_{h}^{k}-n(t_{k})}\right\|_{H^{s}}+\left|{r_{h}^{k}-r(t_{k})}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C(h^{q+1-s}+\tau).

Furthermore,

(3.89)

\displaystyle\left\|{u_{h}^{k}-u(t_{k})}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq C(h^{q+1}+\tau)\left|{\ln h}\right|^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad\text{for $d\leq 3$},

\displaystyle\left\|{n_{h}^{k}-n(t_{k})}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq C(h^{q+1}+\tau)\left|{\ln h}\right|^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad\text{for $d\leq 2$}.

The constant
C
depends on
T
, but is independent of
k
,
h
, and
\tau
.

Proof.

The error estimate (
3.88
) follows at once by noting the splitting (
3.42
), (
3.43
), (
3.44
), (
3.45
), together with the auxiliary error estimates (
3.13
), (
3.76
), (
3.82
), (
3.85
), and (
2.20
). Similarly, inequality (
3.89
) follows by the auxiliary error estimates (
3.83
), (
3.77
), and (
2.21
).
∎

\tau

\tau/2

5.
Conclusion

We have presented a fully discrete structure-preserving finite element method for a diffuse-interface model of tumour growth, combining a scalar auxiliary variable (SAV) formulation with a stabilised mixed FEM for the Cahn–Hilliard part and standard conforming FEM for the reaction-diffusion part, along with a first-order Euler time-stepping scheme. The proposed method preserves mass, satisfies a discrete energy dissipation law (albeit for an approximate energy functional), and requires solving only linear systems at each time step. Under suitable regularity assumptions, we establish rigorous error estimates in
L^{2}
,
H^{1}
, and
L^{\infty}
norms, achieving first-order temporal and optimal spatial convergence. Numerical experiments confirm the theoretical results and demonstrate the robustness of the scheme, even for less regular initial data, in capturing characteristic aggregation, as well as diffusion-driven or chemotactic tumour growth.

Future work includes extensions to higher-order temporal schemes and adaptive spatial discretisations. It would also be interesting to couple the model with Navier–Stokes
[
17
]
or Brinkman-type equations
[
8
]
to account for convective effects, or with Keller–Segel-type equations to better capture chemotactic behaviour
[
25
]
. Additionally, the use of a singular logarithmic potential is necessary to maintain the phase-field variable within its physically relevant interval
[
4
]
, and developing efficient numerical methods to handle this is part of our ongoing research.
