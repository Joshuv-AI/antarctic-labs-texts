Abstract
. Let ( f , g ) : ( S , s ) → ( ℂ 2 , 0 ) (f,g):(S,s)\to(\mathbb{C}^{2},0) be a finite morphism from a germ of normal complex analytic surface to the germ of ℂ 2 \mathbb{C}^{2} at the origin. We show that the affine algebraic curve in ℂ 2 \mathbb{C}^{2} defined by the initial Newton polynomial of a defining series of the discriminant germ of ( f , g ) (f,g) depends up to toric automorphisms only on the germs of curves defined by f f and g g . This result generalizes a theorem of Gryszka, Gwoździewicz and Parusiński, which is the special case in which ( S , s ) (S,s) is smooth. Our proof uses a common generalization of formulas of Lê, Casas-Alvero and Némethi for the intersection number of the discriminant with a germ of plane curve. It uses also a theorem of Delgado and Maugendre characterizing the special members of pencils of curves on normal surface singularities. We apply it to the pencils generated by all pairs ( f b , g a ) (f^{b},g^{a}) , for varying positive integral exponents a , b a,b , following a strategy initiated by Gwoździewicz and by Delgado and Maugendre. This is similar to the Eudoxian method of comparison of magnitudes by comparing the sizes of their positive integral multiples.

1.
Introduction

Let
D\hookrightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
be a non-zero germ of effective divisor. The
Newton polygon
of a defining series
\eta\in\mathbb{C}\{u,v\}
of
D
is independent of the choice of
\eta
. We call it the
Newton polygon

\mathcal{N}(D)
of
D
. Similarly, the
initial Newton polynomial

\eta_{\mathcal{N}}
of
\eta
, that is, the restriction of
\eta
to the union of compact faces of
\mathcal{N}(D)
, is independent of the choice of
\eta
,
up to multiplication by a non-zero constant
. Therefore the zero locus of
\eta_{\mathcal{N}}
in the affine plane
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, seen as an effective divisor in which irreducible components are counted with multiplicities, is independent of the choice of the defining series
\eta
of
D
. We call it the
initial Newton curve
of
D
.

We apply this notion to the
discriminant divisor

\Delta_{\varphi}\hookrightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
of a
finite
morphism
\varphi=(f,g):(S,s)\to(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
, where
(S,s)
is a
normal surface singularity
, that is, a germ of normal complex analytic surface. Namely, our main theorem is:

Theorem

A
.

Let
(S,s)
be a normal surface singularity and let
\varphi=(f,g):(S,s)\to(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
be
a germ of finite morphism. Then the initial Newton curve of its discriminant divisor
\Delta_{\varphi}
depends
only on the divisors
Z(f),Z(g)
defined by
f
and
g
,
up to toric automorphisms of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
.

This theorem generalizes Gryszka, Gwoździewicz and Parusiński’s
[
20
, Theorem 1.1]
, which concerned the case where
(S,s)
is
smooth
. As in their paper, we prove first that if we replace the pair
(f,g)
by a pair
((1+\alpha)f,(1+\beta)g)
in which
\alpha,\beta
are holomorphic germs of functions on
(S,s)
vanishing at
s
, then the initial Newton curve of the discriminant remains unchanged. Theorem
A
is then a direct consequence of this invariance property.
This theorem allows to define a new invariant of a pair of principal effective divisors on a normal surface singularity (see Definition
11.6
): the corresponding initial Newton curve inside
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, well-defined up to toric automorphisms.

Before the paper
[
20
]
, discriminant divisors of finite morphisms
\varphi
as above had been studied by many authors (see the papers
[

]
,
[

]
,
[

]

is that it involved for the first time

One of the main ingredients of the proof of
[
20
, Theorem 1.1]
is a formula of Casas-Alvero
[
5
, Theorem 3.1]
concerning the case when
(S,s)
is
smooth
. It expresses the intersection number
Z(F)\cdot\Delta_{\varphi}
of the discriminant divisor
\Delta_{\varphi}
of
\varphi
with a reduced principal divisor
Z(F)\hookrightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
in terms of the degree
\deg(\varphi)
of
\varphi
and of the Milnor numbers
\mu(F),\mu(\varphi^{*}F)
of
F
and of its pullback
\varphi^{*}F
to
(S,s)
. We adapt this ingredient to our context, by generalizing Casas-Alvero’s formula to the case when
both the source and the target are arbitrary normal surface singularities
:

Theorem

B
.

Let
\varphi:(S,s)\to(T,t)
be a finite morphism between normal surface singularities,
with discriminant divisor
\Delta_{\varphi}
. If the holomorphic germ
F:(T,t)\to(\mathbb{C},0)

is such that both
F
and
\varphi^{*}F
are reduced, then

\Delta_{\varphi}\cdot Z(F)=(\mu(\varphi^{*}F)-1)-\deg(\varphi)\cdot(\mu(F)-1)
.

It turns out that Theorem
B
generalizes also Lê’s
[
44
, Lemma 4.4]
, which concerns the case when both
(T,t)
and
Z(F)
are smooth. Moreover, Casas-Alvero’s formula is the special case of Némethi’s
[
40

for which
(S,s)=(\mathbb{C}^{n+1},0)
and
(T,t)=(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
.

Gryszka, Gwoździewicz and Parusiński applied Casas-Alvero’s formula to series

of the form
(v^{k}-tu^{l})^{N}-u^{l(N+1)}
, in which
t\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
is a parameter,
k,l,N\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
and
k,l
are coprime. We simplify their approach, by applying Theorem B only to series of the form
u^{b}-\lambda v^{a}
, with
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
,
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
and
a,b
coprime.

We achieve this simplification using Delgado and Maugendre’s
[
12
, Theorem 4]
, which states an equivalence of two definitions of
special members
of pencils of curves associated to germs of finite morphisms
\varphi:(S,s)\to(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
. Namely, we apply their theorem to
all
the finite morphisms
(f^{b},g^{a}):(S,s)\to(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
, for varying pairs
(a,b)
of coprime positive integers (see the proof of Proposition

This is the reason why we qualify our approach as
Eudoxian
. A tradition tells that Eudoxus had the idea to develop a theory of proportions by comparing the sizes of all integer multiples
(bM_{1},aM_{2})
of a given pair
(M_{1},M_{2})
of magnitudes of the same kind. Similarly, we study the pair
(f,g)
by looking simultaneously at all the pairs
(f^{b},g^{a})
. This idea was already used when
(S,s)
is

[
21
]
of Newton polygons of discriminants and by Delgado and Maugendre in their study
[
11
]
of combinatorial types of images of branches. But they mentioned no relations with Eudoxus’ approach to proportion theory.

As a step in our proof of Theorem

[

]
, which concerned the case when the source
(S,s)
is
smooth

Theorem

Let
(S,s)
be a normal surface singularity and
\varphi=(f,g):(S,s)\to(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
a germ
of finite morphism. Then the Newton polygon of its discriminant depends only on the
combinatorial type of the triple
(S,Z(f),Z(g))
.

This theorem is also a consequence of Michel’s
[
36

, which did not concern Newton polygons explicitly and which she proved topologically. Our approach is completely different: we use Theorem

and A’Campo’s formula of
[
1

for the Milnor number in terms of good resolutions.

2.
Background on normal surface singularities and their intersection theory

In this section we fix the notations used throughout the paper concerning normal surface singularities and effective divisors on them and we explain intersection-theoretical results needed in subsequent proofs (see Propositions
2.6
,
2.7
and
2.8
). Note that we are interested only in intersection numbers of effective divisors at least one of which is
principal
(see Definition
2.5
).

Throughout the paper,
\boxed{(S,s)}
denotes a
normal surface singularity
, that is, a germ of normal complex analytic surface. We denote by
\boxed{\mathcal{O}_{S,s}}
its local ring of germs of holomorphic functions and by
\boxed{\mathfrak{m}_{S,s}}
its maximal ideal. A
branch
on
(S,s)
is a reduced and analytically irreducible germ of curve on
(S,s)
. An
effective divisor
on
(S,s)
is a germ at
s
of complex analytic effective divisor on a representative of the germ
(S,s)
, that is, an element of the free commutative monoid generated by the branches on
(S,s)
.

If
\xi
is either a germ of holomorphic function or of holomorphic differential form of degree two on
(S,s)
, we denote by
\boxed{Z(\xi)}\hookrightarrow(S,s)
its corresponding effective divisor. That is,
Z(\xi)
is the zero locus of
\xi
in which each branch is counted with its generic multiplicity (the order of vanishing of
\xi
along it in a pointed neighborhood of
s
in a representative of
S
). As usual, the effective divisors of the form
Z(f)
with
f\in\mathcal{O}_{S,s}
are called
principal
.

In this paper we study the discriminant divisors of special types of
finite
morphisms:

Definition 2.1
.

A morphism
\psi:(X,x)\to(Y,y)
between two germs of complex spaces is called
finite
if it admits a representative which is finite in the global sense, that is, which is proper and with finite fibers.

One has the following classical characterisation of finiteness
(see
[
23
, Theorem 3.4.24]
for a more general statement):

Proposition 2.2
.

A morphism
\psi:(X,x)\to(Y,y)
is finite if and only if it admits a representative such that
\psi^{-1}(y)=\{x\}
.

Effective divisors may be pushed forward by finite morphisms:

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\varphi:(S,s)\to(T,t)
be a finite morphism between normal surface singularities and
D
be a
branch
on
(S,s)
. Then the effective divisor

\boxed{\varphi_{*}D}:=\deg(\varphi|_{D})\ \varphi(D)

on
(T,t)
is called the
direct image
of
D
by
\varphi
. Here
\varphi(D)\hookrightarrow(T,t)
is the branch on
(T,t)
defined as the reduced image of
D
by
\varphi
, and
\deg(\varphi|_{D})\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
denotes the degree of the restriction
\varphi|_{D}:D\to\varphi(D)
of
\varphi
to
D
. Then, the map
\varphi_{*}
is extended by linearity to arbitrary effective divisors on
(S,s)
.

Let us recall standard terminology about
resolutions
:

Definition 2.4
.

A
good resolution
of the normal surface singularity
(S,s)
is a proper bimeromorphic morphism

\pi:(S_{\pi},E_{\pi})\to(S,s)

whose
exceptional divisor

\boxed{E_{\pi}}:=\pi^{-1}(0)
has normal crossings and smooth irreducible components. If
h\in\mathfrak{m}_{S,s}
, a good resolution
\pi
of
(S,s)
is called a
good resolution of
h
if the
total transform

Z(\pi^{*}h)
of
Z(h)
is a divisor with normal crossings. The
strict transform
of
Z(h)
is the sum of irreducible components of the total transform which are not contained in
E_{\pi}
.

Using good resolutions, Mumford defined in
[
39
, Section II (b)]
an
intersection number

D_{1}\cdot D_{2}\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}
for pairs
(D_{1},D_{2})
of non-zero effective divisors on
S

without common branches. In this paper we use intersection numbers of effective divisors
only when one of them is
principal
(see Theorem
B
, Lemma

6.2
and Lemma
6.4
, in which the not necessarily principal divisor is either a critical or a discriminant divisor), as well as special properties of intersection numbers in this particular situation. Let us explain them.

The following assymmetric definition of intersection numbers between a not necessarily
principal divisor
D
and a principal one
Z(f)
amounts to compose normalizing parametrisations
of the branches of
D
with the germ
f
:

Definition 2.5
.

Let
(S,s)
be a normal surface singularity,
D
an effective divisor on it and

f\in\mathfrak{m}_{S,s}
.

(1)

If
D
is a branch, consider a normalization
\boxed{n_{D}}:(\mathbb{C},0)\to(D,s)

of it. Then the
intersection number of
D
and
Z(f)
is defined by:

\boxed{D\cdot Z(f)}:=\mathrm{ord}_{x}(n_{D}^{*}f),

where
n_{D}^{*}f\in\mathbb{C}\{x\}
denotes the pullback of
f
by
n_{D}
and

\boxed{\mathrm{ord}_{x}}:\mathbb{C}\{x\}\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}

denotes the
x
-adic valuation.

(2)

If
D=\sum_{i\in I}D_{i}
is a finite sum of branches
D_{i}
, then
the
intersection number of
D
and
Z(f)
is defined by:

\boxed{D\cdot Z(f)}:=\sum_{i\in I}D_{i}\cdot Z(f).

We end this section with three classical propositions. First, we need the following
projection formula
in the proofs of Theorem
B
and Lemma
6.2
:

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
\varphi:(S,s)\to(T,t)
be a finite morphism between normal surface singularities,
D
an effective divisor on
(S,s)
and
F\in\mathfrak{m}_{T,t}
. Then

D\cdot Z(\varphi^{*}F)=\varphi_{*}D\cdot Z(F)
.

We use the following
principle of conservation of number
in the proof of Theorem
B
:

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
(S,s)
be a normal surface singularity,
D
an effective divisor on it
and
f\in\mathfrak{m}_{S,s}
. Assume that

D
and
Z(f)
have no common branch. Then there exists a
neighborhood
U
of
s
in a representative of
S
and
V
of
0
in
\mathbb{C}
such that

F
has a representative defined on the whole of
U
and:

D\cdot Z(f)=D\cdot_{U}Z(f-\varepsilon)

for every
\varepsilon\in V
, where
D\cdot_{U}Z(f-\varepsilon)
denotes the global intersection number
of
D
and of the divisor of
f-\varepsilon
in
U
.

When both divisors are principal, the apparently asymmetric definition of their intersection numbers yields in fact a symmetric result, due to the following fact, which we use in the proofs of Theorem
B
, Lemma
6.4
and Proposition
8.3
:

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
(S,s)
be a normal surface singularity and
F,G\in\mathfrak{m}_{S,s}
. Assume that

Z(F)
and
Z(G)
have no common branch. Then:

Z(F)\cdot Z(G)=\deg(F,G)=\dim\frac{\mathcal{O}_{S,s}}{(F,G)},

where the first occurrence of the notation
(F,G)
denotes the finite morphism
(F,G):(S,s)\to(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
and the second one denotes the ideal generated by
F
and
G
in the local ring
\mathcal{O}_{S,s}
.

Proof.

The first equality is a consequence of Proposition
2.7
and of the fact that
\deg(F,G)
is the cardinal of a generic fiber of the morphism
(F,G)
, after choosing a convenient representative of it.
The second equality results from the fact that any normal surface singularity is Cohen-Macaulay and from
[
38
, Corollary D.6]
.

3.
A theorem of Delgado and Maugendre on special members of pencils

In this section we explain a theorem of Delgado and Maugendre which shows the equivalence of the two Definitions
3.2
and
3.4
of special members of the pencil
\mathbb{P}\varphi:(S,s)\dashrightarrow\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{1}
of germs of curves associated to a finite morphism
\varphi:=(f,g):(S,s)\to(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
(see Theorem
3.6
).

Let
(S,s)
be a normal surface singularity. Consider
f,g\in\mathfrak{m}_{S,s}
such that the morphism

\boxed{\varphi}:=(f,g):(S,s)\to(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)

is finite (see Definition
2.1
). By Proposition
2.2
, this means that the effective divisors
Z(f)
and
Z(g)
have no common branch. We look at the critical and discriminant loci of
\varphi
as effective divisors with possible non-reduced structures:

Definition 3.1
.

The
critical divisor

\boxed{\Xi_{\varphi}}\hookrightarrow(S,s)
of
\varphi
is the divisor of the differential form
df\wedge dg
. The
discriminant divisor

\boxed{\Delta_{\varphi}}\hookrightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
of
\varphi
is the direct image
\varphi_{*}(\Xi_{\varphi})
in the sense of Definition
2.3
.

One may associate to the finite morphism
\varphi
not only two divisors as in Definition
3.1
, but also a
pencil
of curves, in which some members are distinguished:

Definition 3.2
.

Let
\boxed{\mathbb{P}}:\mathbb{C}^{2}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{1}
be the projectivisation map of the complex
vector space
\mathbb{C}^{2}
. Denote by
\boxed{\mathbb{P}\varphi}:(S,s)\dashrightarrow\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{1}
the map
\mathbb{P}\circ\varphi
. We call it the
pencil generated by
\varphi
. For each
p\in\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{1}
, denote by

\boxed{L_{p}}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2}
the corresponding line, equal to the closure of

\mathbb{P}^{-1}(p)
in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
.
Then
\varphi^{*}(L_{p})\hookrightarrow(S,s)
, equal to the closure of

(\mathbb{P}\varphi)^{-1}(p)
in
(S,s)
, is the
corresponding member of the pencil generated by
\varphi
. A point
p\in\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{1}
is called

\varphi
-distinguished
if the line
L_{p}
is tangent at
0
to the discriminant

\Delta_{\varphi}
, that is, if and only if:

(1)

\Delta_{\varphi}\cdot L_{p}>\min\{\Delta_{\varphi}\cdot L_{q},\ q\in\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{1}\}.

If
p=[\alpha:\beta]\in\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{1}
, then
L_{p}=Z(\beta u-\alpha v)\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2}_{u,v}
is a
global line and the corresponding member
\varphi^{*}(L_{p})=Z(\beta f-\alpha g)\hookrightarrow(S,s)
of the pencil generated by
\varphi
is an effective divisor on
(S,s)
.
In the sequel we will concentrate on points
p
whose homogeneous coordinates are of the form
[\lambda:1]
with

\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
(see Proposition
8.2
).

One may study the pencil generated by
\varphi
using particular kinds of good resolutions in the sense of Definition
2.4
of the ambient surface singularity
(S,s)
(see
[
30
, Section 2]
for the case when
(S,s)
is smooth):

Definition 3.3
.

Let
\pi:(S_{\pi},E_{\pi})\to(S,s)
be a good resolution of
(S,s)
. It is said
to lift
the indeterminacies of
\mathbb{P}\varphi
if the map

\mathbb{P}\varphi_{\pi}:=\mathbb{P}\varphi\circ\pi:S_{\pi}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{1}

extends holomorphically to a neighborhood of
E_{\pi}
in the whole surface
S_{\pi}
. In this case we still denote by

\boxed{\mathbb{P}\varphi_{\pi}}
this extension, seen as a germ along
E_{\pi}
. An irreducible component
E_{i}
of the exceptional divisor

E_{\pi}
of
\pi
is called
\mathbb{P}\varphi_{\pi}
-dicritical

if the restriction of
\mathbb{P}\varphi_{\pi}
to
E_{i}
is surjective onto
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{1}
, that is, not constant.

Having defined
dicritical
components allows to define
special points
of the parameter line
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{1}
of the pencil:

Definition 3.4
.

Let
\pi:(S_{\pi},E_{\pi})\to(S,s)
be a good resolution of
(S,s)

which lifts the indeterminacies of
\mathbb{P}\varphi
. Denote by

\boxed{D_{\pi}}\hookrightarrow E_{\pi}
the union of the
\mathbb{P}\varphi_{\pi}
-dicritical
components of
E_{\pi}
.
A
\mathbb{P}\varphi_{\pi}
-special point
or a
\varphi
-special point
is a point of
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{1}
which is the image by
\mathbb{P}\varphi_{\pi}
of a subset of
E_{\pi}
of one of the following types:

(1)

A non-
\mathbb{P}\varphi_{\pi}
-dicritical component of
E_{\pi}
.

(2)

A singular point of
D_{\pi}
.

(3)

A critical point of the restriction of
\mathbb{P}\varphi_{\pi}
to a
\mathbb{P}\varphi_{\pi}
-dicritical component of
E_{\pi}
.

Note that if
E_{i}
is not
\mathbb{P}\varphi_{\pi}
-dicritical, then
\mathbb{P}\varphi_{\pi}
is constant in restriction to
E_{i}
, which shows that a
\mathbb{P}\varphi_{\pi}
-special point is indeed a
point
of the Riemann surface
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{1}

Proposition 3.5

The set of

4.
A generalization of formulas of Casas-Alvero, Lê and Némethi

In this section we generalize to arbitrary normal surface singularities formulas of Casas-Alvero, Lê and Némethi concerning finite morphisms between
smooth
germs of surfaces (see Theorem
B
). The generalized formula expresses the intersection number of the discriminant divisor of a finite morphism between normal surface singularities with the divisor of a reduced function on the target in terms of the Milnor numbers of the function and its pullback by the finite morphism and the degree of the morphism. We recall the definition of such Milnor numbers (see Definition
4.1
) and a formula of A’Campo expressing them in terms of good resolutions (see Theorem
4.3
). Throughout this section,
\boxed{\chi(Y)}
denotes the Euler-Poincaré characteristic of a topological space
Y
.

Consider a normal surface singularity
(T,t)
. Let
F\in\mathfrak{m}_{T,t}
be reduced, which means that the principal divisor
Z(F)
is reduced. One may define a
Milnor fibration
for it (a locally trivial fibration over a circle) following the model of
[
37
]
. Namely, one first chooses a representative of
(T,t)
embedded in some
(\mathbb{C}^{n},0)
, then one intersects it with a small enough compact euclidean ball centered at the origin, and one looks inside this intersection at the preimage by
F
of a sufficiently small euclidean circle centered at the origin of
\mathbb{C}

(see Lê
[
27
]
for such a construction in arbitrary dimension and Cisneros-Molina and Seade

[
7
]
for a survey). A
Milnor fiber
of
F
is a fiber of a Milnor fibration of it. The following definition is then independent of the previous choices, and was explored initially in this generality by Bassein
[
3
]
:

Definition 4.1
.

Let
(T,t)
be a normal surface singularity and
F\in\mathfrak{m}_{T,t}
. Its
Milnor number

\boxed{\mu(F)}
is the first Betti number of the Milnor fibers of
F
when
F
is reduced and is
+\infty
otherwise.

Remark 4.2
.

Bassein stated in
[
3
, Proof of Theorem (2.2)]
that

\mu(F)=\dim(\omega/d\mathcal{O})
,
where
\mathcal{O}
denotes the local ring of the reduced curve singularity
(Z(F),0)
and
\omega

denotes its dualising sheaf. This allowed Buchweitz and Greuel to extend in
[
4
]
the notion of
Milnor number to arbitrary (not necessarily smoothable) reduced curve singularities,
by taking the expression

\dim(\omega/d\mathcal{O})
as its definition. In the sequel we will use the notion only
in smoothable settings, therefore Definition
4.1
will be enough for us.

Milnor numbers may be expressed using good resolutions in the sense of Definition
2.4
.
The following theorem is a special case of one of A’Campo’s formulas of
[
1
, Theorem 4]
. A’Campo’s theorem concerned holomorphic functions defined on germs of smooth spaces, but his proof extends readily to our context:

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
(T,t)
be a normal surface singularity and
F\in\mathcal{O}_{T,t}
be reduced. Let
\pi:(T_{\pi},E_{\pi})\to(T,t)
be a good resolution of
F
. Denote by
E_{\pi}^{m}
the subset of
E_{\pi}
formed of the points in the neighborhood of which
Z(\pi^{*}F)
is defined in suitable local coordinates
(u,v)
by the equation
u^{m}=0
. Then:

\mu(F)=1-\sum_{m>0}m\cdot\chi(E_{\pi}^{m}).

We will use Theorem
4.3
in the proof of Proposition
9.3
below.

Let now
\varphi:(S,s)\to(T,t)
be a finite morphism between normal surface singularities, in the sense of Definition
2.1
. One may again associate to it
critical
and
discriminant divisors
. Definition
3.1
has to be slightly modified, as there is no more a canonical holomorphic differential form of degree two on the target to be pulled-back by
\varphi
(the form
df\wedge dg
being the pullback of the form
dx\wedge dy
on the target
\mathbb{C}^{2}
of the finite morphism of Definition
3.1
). Note first that in that definition, one may replace the form
dx\wedge dy
by any non-vanishing germ of holomorphic form of degree two, which yields always the same critical divisor, thus also the same discriminant divisor. One may define therefore those divisors as follows in the enlarged setting:

Definition 4.4
.

Let
\varphi:(S,s)\to(T,t)
be a finite morphism between normal surface singularities. The
critical divisor

\boxed{\Xi_{\varphi}}\hookrightarrow(S,s)
of
\varphi
is the closure of the critical divisor of the restriction of
\varphi
to the complement of
s
in a representative of
S
. The
discriminant divisor

\boxed{\Delta_{\varphi}}\hookrightarrow(T,t)
of
\varphi
is the direct image
\varphi_{*}(\Xi_{\varphi})
in the sense of Definition
2.3
.

Theorem
B
extends to arbitrary normal surface singularities the following results:

•

Casas-Alvero’s
[
5
, Theorem 3.2]
, in which both the source and the target are
smooth
. This theorem is the special case of Némethi’s
[
40
, Theorem A (a)]
for which
(S,s)=(\mathbb{C}^{n+1},0)
and
(T,t)=(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
.

•

Lê’s
[
44
, Lemma 4.4]
, published in a paper by Snoussi, which concerns the case when the target
(T,t)
is smooth and the germ
F
defines a smooth branch in
(T,t)
.

Theorem B
.

Let
\varphi:(S,s)\to(T,t)
be a finite morphism between normal surface singularities,
with critical divisor
\Xi_{\varphi}
and discriminant divisor
\Delta_{\varphi}
.
If
F\in\mathfrak{m}_{T,t}
is such that both
F
and
\varphi^{*}F\in\mathfrak{m}_{S,s}

are reduced, then:

\Delta_{\varphi}\cdot Z(F)=\Xi_{\varphi}\cdot Z(\varphi^{*}F)=(\mu(\varphi^{*}F)-1)-\deg(\varphi)\cdot(\mu(F)-1).

Proof.

Choose representatives of
(S,s)
and
(T,t)
by first intersecting
(T,t)
with a sufficiently small ball, then by taking the total preimage of this representative by
\varphi
. For
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
with small enough
|\varepsilon|
, the preimages
F^{-1}(\varepsilon)
and
(\varphi^{*}F)^{-1}(\varepsilon)
taken inside those representatives are Milnor fibers of
F
and
\varphi^{*}F
respectively.

The restriction

\boxed{\varphi_{\varepsilon}}:(\varphi^{*}F)^{-1}(\varepsilon)\to F^{-1}(\varepsilon)

of
\varphi
to
(\varphi^{*}F)^{-1}(\varepsilon)
is a ramified cover of Riemann surfaces with boundary. Denote by
\boxed{\rho}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
the
total ramification index
of
\varphi_{\varepsilon}
. The Riemann-Hurwitz formula implies that:

(3)

\chi((\varphi^{*}F)^{-1}(\varepsilon))=\deg(\varphi_{\varepsilon})\cdot\chi(F^{-1}(\varepsilon))-\rho.

We will write now differently the quantities appearing in the equality (
3
).

As a consequence of Propositions
2.7
and
2.8
, we have:

(4)

\deg(\varphi_{\varepsilon})=\deg(\varphi).

The germ
\varphi^{*}F
being assumed reduced, we deduce that
\Xi_{\varphi}
and
Z(\varphi^{*}F)
have no common branch. By Proposition

2.7
applied to
f:=\varphi^{*}F
and
D:=\Xi_{\varphi}
, we see that for
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
with small enough absolute value:

\Xi_{\varphi}\cdot Z(\varphi^{*}F)=\Xi_{\varphi}\cdot Z(\varphi^{*}F-\varepsilon)=\Xi_{\varphi}\cdot(\varphi^{*}F)^{-1}(\varepsilon).

Moreover, we may ensure that the reduction
|\Xi_{\varphi}|
of
\Xi_{\varphi}
and the smooth curve
(\varphi^{*}F)^{-1}(\varepsilon)
are transversal. If
p
is one of their intersection points, we may therefore choose local holomorphic coordinates
(x,y)
on
S
centered at
p
such that
|\Xi_{\varphi}|=Z(y)
and
(\varphi^{*}F)^{-1}(\varepsilon)=Z(x)
in a neighborhood of
p
. Denote by
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
the multiplicity of
\Xi_{\varphi}
at
p
. Therefore
\Xi_{\varphi}=mZ(y)
in a neighborhood of
p
. This implies the following value for the intersection number at the point
p
:

\Xi_{\varphi}\cdot_{p}(\varphi^{*}F)^{-1}(\varepsilon)=m.

It implies also that after choosing suitable local coordinates on
T
in a neighborhood of
\varphi(p)
, the germ of
\varphi
at
p
is given by
(x,y)\mapsto(x,y^{m+1})
. As
(\varphi^{*}F)^{-1}(\varepsilon)=Z(x)
in a neighborhood of
p
, we deduce that
m
is also the ramification index of
\varphi_{\varepsilon}
at
p
. Summing over all such intersection points
p
, we get the equality:

(5)

\rho=\Xi_{\varphi}\cdot Z(\varphi^{*}F).

The surface
F^{-1}(\varepsilon)

is a Milnor fiber of
F
. This implies that
\mu(F)
is its first Betti number. The Milnor fiber being connected, its
0
-th Betti number is
1
. As it is a connected surface with non-empty boundary, its higher Betti numbers are
0
. Therefore its Euler-Poincaré characteristic
\chi(F^{-1}(\varepsilon))
, which is by definition the alternating sum of Betti numbers, is:

(6)

\chi(F^{-1}(\varepsilon))=1-\mu(F).

An analogous reasoning proves that:

(7)

\chi((\varphi^{*}F)^{-1}(\varepsilon))=1-\mu(\varphi^{*}F).

Replacing the equalities (
4
)–(
7
) in equation (
3
), we get the second equality of the statement. The first equality
\Delta_{\varphi}\cdot Z(F)=\Xi_{\varphi}\cdot Z(\varphi^{*}F)
is in turn a consequence
of the
projection formula
(see Proposition
2.6
).
∎

At the beginning of
[
5
, Section 3]
, Casas-Alvero comments that “
In this section we give an algebraic proof of a formula […] which may be understood as a local Riemann-Hurwitz formula if the Milnor numbers are viewed as numbers of vanishing cycles.
” Then his proof of
[
5
, Theorem 3.2]
proceeds without using the Riemann-Hurwitz formula.
By contrast, our proof of Theorem
B
uses in an essential way the Riemann-Hurwitz formula for a suitable ramified cover of Riemann surfaces with boundary. In this sense, it is similar to Lê’s proof of
[
44
, Lemma 4.4]
.

In this paper we will apply Theorem
B
only to
smooth
targets (see Proposition
8.3
). We proved nevertheless the theorem in greater generality because the proof is not more complicated and because we believe that it could be useful in this form in other contexts.

5.
The Eudoxian strategy

In this section we explain for which reason the strategy to study finite morphisms
(f,g)
by looking simultaneously at all the finite morphisms
(f^{b},g^{a})
for varying pairs
(a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{2}
may be called
Eudoxian
. At the end of the section we explain in which way we use this strategy in our proofs.

In Euclid’s
[
14
, Book
V
]
is explained a theory of
proportionality
without passing through a definition of
ratio
of two magnitudes of the same kind (two lengths, two areas, two volumes, etc.) as a
number
. The core idea is that it is possible to decide when two pairs
(M_{1},M_{2})
and
(M_{3},M_{4})
of magnitudes, each of the same kind (but possibly of different kinds between the pairs, for instance two lengths and two areas)
are in the same ratio
. Namely, this is the case if for every
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
and every
\boxed{\bowtie}\ \in\{=,<,>\}
, one has:

(bM_{1}\bowtie aM_{2})\ \Longleftrightarrow\ (bM_{3}\bowtie aM_{4}).

This approach to the theory of proportions is classically attributed to Eudoxus (see Menn
[
35
, Page 188]
for a discussion of this attribution). Several authors studied the relation between this approach and Dedekind’s theory of
cuts
of
\mathbb{Q}
or
\mathbb{Q}_{>0}
(see Gardies
[
18
]
and Corry
[
8
]
). The main idea of this relation is that a pair
(M_{1},M_{2})
of magnitudes of the same kind partitions the set
\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{2}
into the three subsets

\{(a,b);\ (bM_{1}\bowtie aM_{2})\}

for
\bowtie
varying in
\{=,<,>\}
, and that by looking at the sets of quotients
a/b
corresponding to each subset one defines a cut of
\mathbb{Q}_{>0}
.

In order to define either proportionality of two pairs of magnitudes or the cut associated to one pair it is important that the two magnitudes of each pair under consideration belong to a set on which are defined both
an action of the semiring
(\mathbb{Z}_{>0},+,\cdot)
(which allows to take positive integer multiples of magnitudes) and
a total order
(which allows to compare two magnitudes).

Neither proportionality nor a cut of
\mathbb{Q}_{>0}
can be defined if the magnitudes under consideration belong to a set endowed with an action of
(\mathbb{Z}_{>0},+,\cdot)
,
but not with a total order
. This is precisely the case in our situation: we may think of two germs
f,g\in\mathfrak{m}_{S,s}
as two magnitudes of the same kind. The positive integer
b\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
acts on
f
transforming it into
f^{b}
, but there is no natural total order on
\mathfrak{m}_{S,s}
. If we consider that the central idea attributed to Eudoxus is to compare two magnitudes by studying the pairs of their multiples
even in the absence of total order
, then we may indeed say – as we do – that studying the pairs
(f,g)
using all the finite morphisms
(f^{b},g^{a}):(S,s)\to(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
is a
Eudoxian strategy
.

As far as we know, this strategy was initiated for
smooth
germs
(S,s)
by Gwozdźiewicz in
[
21
, Proof of Theorem 2.1]
and by Delgado and Maugendre in
[
11
, Introduction]
. It is in order to emphasize it for possible applications in other contexts that we decided to give it a name.

As
f^{b}
and
g^{a}
belong to a
\mathbb{C}
-algebra, one may compare them
\mathbb{C}
-linearly
and
multiplicatively
. Both viewpoints are important in this paper. That is, we consider both the differences
f^{b}-\lambda g^{a}
, with
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
and the ratios
f^{b}/g^{a}
. The object which connects those differences and ratios is the pencil
\mathbb{P}\varphi_{a,b}:(S,s)\dashrightarrow\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{1}
generated by the finite morphism
\varphi_{a,b}:=(f^{b},g^{a}):(S,s)\to(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
introduced in Section
6
. According to Definition
3.2
,
Z(f^{b}-\lambda g^{a})
is the member of the pencil corresponding to the point

[\lambda:1]\in\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{1}
and
f^{b}/g^{a}
is the expression of
\mathbb{P}\varphi_{a,b}
in one of the two canonical charts of
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{1}
. The previous objects are used as follows in this paper:

•

In Proposition
8.3
, we express the value
\mathrm{Trop}^{\Delta_{\varphi}}(a,b)
of the tropical function associated to the discriminant divisor
\Delta_{\varphi}
of
\varphi:=(f,g)
in terms of the Milnor numbers
\mu(f^{b}-\lambda g^{a})
of the functions
f^{b}-\lambda g^{a}
. This proposition leads to Proposition
9.3
, which in turn allows to prove Theorem
C
.

•

In Proposition
8.2
, we express the set of roots of the restriction of a defining series
\eta_{\Delta_{\varphi}}
of
\Delta_{\varphi}
in terms of the set of special points of the pencil
\mathbb{P}\varphi_{a,b}
. This leads to a proof of Theorem
A
.

6.
Relations between the discriminants of a family of finite morphisms

In this section we start applying the Eudoxian strategy described in Section
5
by proving intersection-theoretic relations between the discriminant divisors of the finite morphisms
(f,g)
and
(f^{b},g^{a})
, for
(a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{2}
(see Proposition
6.5
and Corollary
6.6
). Those relations involve affine curves defined by binomials (see Definition
6.1
).

Assume as before that the morphism

\varphi=(f,g):(S,s)\to(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
is finite. As a consequence of Proposition
2.2
, the morphism

\boxed{\varphi_{a,b}}:=(f^{b},g^{a}):(S,s)\to(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)

is also finite, for every
(a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{2}
. Let
\boxed{\Xi_{a,b}}\hookrightarrow(S,s)
and

\boxed{\Delta_{a,b}}\hookrightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
be the critical and discriminant divisors of
\varphi_{a,b}
.
Note that
\varphi=\varphi_{1,1}
and
\Xi_{a,b}=\Xi_{\varphi_{a,b}}
,

\Delta_{a,b}=\Delta_{\varphi_{a,b}}
(see Definition
3.1
). We explain in Remark
9.5
why we denote such pairs
(f^{b},g^{a})
instead of
(f^{a},g^{b})
.

We will study the discriminant divisors
\Delta_{a,b}
by intersecting them at the origin of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
with the following affine curves defined by binomials:

Definition 6.1
.

Let
(a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{2}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
. The
(a,b,\lambda)
-binomial curve
\boxed{B_{a,b,\lambda}}
is the affine plane algebraic curve

Z(u^{b}-\lambda v^{a})\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2}_{u,v}.

One has the following equalities of intersection numbers computed on the germ

(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
(on the left sides) and on the germ
(S,s)
(on the right sides):

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
(a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{2}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
. Then:

(1)

\Delta_{1,1}\cdot B_{a,b,\lambda}=\Xi_{1,1}\cdot Z(f^{b}-\lambda g^{a})
.

(2)

\Delta_{a,b}\cdot B_{1,1,\lambda}=\Xi_{a,b}\cdot Z(f^{b}-\lambda g^{a})
.

Proof.

The identity
(i)
results from the following sequence of equalities:

\Delta_{1,1}\cdot B_{a,b,\lambda}=(\varphi_{1,1})_{*}(\Xi_{1,1})\cdot B_{a,b,\lambda}=\Xi_{1,1}\cdot(\varphi_{1,1})^{*}(B_{a,b,\lambda})=\Xi_{1,1}\cdot Z(f^{b}-\lambda g^{a}).

The first equality uses Definition
3.1
of the discriminant divisor as direct image of the critical
divisor, the second one uses the projection formula of Proposition
2.6
and the last one uses the following consequence of Definition
6.1
of the binomial curve
B_{a,b,\lambda}
:

(8)

(\varphi_{1,1})^{*}(B_{a,b,\lambda})=Z(f^{b}-\lambda g^{a}).

The identity
(ii)
is proved similarly, using the following analog of the equality (
8
):

(9)

(\varphi_{a,b})^{*}(B_{1,1,\lambda})=Z(f^{b}-\lambda g^{a}).

∎

Note that formulae (
8
) and (
9
) give two interpretations of the principal divisor
Z(f^{b}-\lambda g^{a})\hookrightarrow(S,s)
as a pull-back of a binomial curve by a finite morphism. The following lemma compares the critical divisors of these two finite morphisms
\varphi_{1,1}
and
\varphi_{a,b}
:

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
(a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{2}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
. Then:

\Xi_{a,b}=(b-1)Z(f)+(a-1)Z(g)+\Xi_{1,1}.

Proof.

One has:

d(f^{b})\wedge d(g^{a})=(bf^{b-1}df)\wedge(ag^{a-1}dg)=abf^{b-1}g^{a-1}df\wedge dg.

Therefore:

\displaystyle\Xi_{a,b}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Z(d(f^{b})\wedge d(g^{a}))=Z(abf^{b-1}g^{a-1}df\wedge dg)=(b-1)Z(f)+(a-1)Z(g)+Z(df\wedge dg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(b-1)Z(f)+(a-1)Z(g)+\Xi_{1,1}.

∎

The effective divisor
Z(f^{b}-\lambda g^{a})\hookrightarrow(S,s)
appearing in Lemma
6.2
has the following property, the intersection numbers being computed on
(S,s)
:

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
(a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{2}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
. Then:

Z(f)\cdot Z(f^{b}-\lambda g^{a})=a\ Z(f)\cdot Z(g).

Proof.

Applying Proposition
2.8
twice, to the pairs
(f,f^{b}-\lambda g^{a})
and
(f,g^{a})
, we get:

\displaystyle Z(f)\cdot Z(f^{b}-\lambda g^{a})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dim\frac{\mathcal{O}_{S,s}}{(f,f^{b}-\lambda g^{a})}=\dim\frac{\mathcal{O}_{S,s}}{(f,\lambda g^{a})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dim\frac{\mathcal{O}_{S,s}}{(f,g^{a})}=Z(f)\cdot Z(g^{a})=a\ Z(f)\cdot Z(g).

∎

We proved Lemmas
6.2
–
6.4
in order to get the following relation between the intersection numbers
\Delta_{a,b}\cdot B_{1,1,\lambda},\Delta_{1,1}\cdot B_{a,b,\lambda}
(computed on
(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
) and
Z(f)\cdot Z(g)
(computed on
(S,s)
):

Proposition 6.5
.

Let
(a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{2}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
. Then:

\Delta_{a,b}\cdot B_{1,1,\lambda}=(2ab-a-b)Z(f)\cdot Z(g)+\Delta_{1,1}\cdot B_{a,b,\lambda}.

Proof.

By Lemma
6.3
:

\Xi_{a,b}\cdot Z(f^{b}-\lambda g^{a})=((b-1)Z(f)+(a-1)Z(g)+\Xi_{1,1})\cdot Z(f^{b}-\lambda g^{a}).

Develop the second member using the linearity of the intersection product.
By Lemma
6.4
and its analog for
Z(g)
, we get:

\displaystyle\Xi_{a,b}\cdot Z(f^{b}-\lambda g^{a})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a(b-1)Z(f)\cdot Z(g)+b(a-1)Z(g)\cdot Z(f)+\ \Xi_{1,1}\cdot Z(f^{b}-\lambda g^{a})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(a(b-1)+b(a-1))Z(f)\cdot Z(g)+\Xi_{1,1}\cdot Z(f^{b}-\lambda g^{a}).

Using now the two identities of Lemma
6.2
, we obtain the desired equality.
∎

As a consequence:

Corollary 6.6
.

Consider
\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
. The following statements are equivalent:

(1)

\Delta_{1,1}\cdot B_{a,b,\lambda_{0}}>\min\{\Delta_{1,1}\cdot B_{a,b,\lambda},\ \lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{*}\}.

(2)

\Delta_{a,b}\cdot B_{1,1,\lambda_{0}}>\min\{\Delta_{a,b}\cdot B_{1,1,\lambda},\ \lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{*}\}.

Proof.

The equivalence of the two statements results from Proposition
6.5
and from the fact that the product
(2ab-a-b)Z(f)\cdot Z(g)
is independent of
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
.
∎

Corollary
6.6
will be used in combination with Proposition
7.13
in order to characterize in Proposition
8.2
below the special points of the finite morphism

.

). There exists a canonical exact sequence
of
\mathcal{O}_{X}
-modules:

(18)

\pi^{*}\Omega^{1}_{Y}\to\Omega^{1}_{X}\to\Omega^{1}_{\pi}\to 0.

Definitions
10.3
(
2
) and
10.4
(
2
)
below of
divisorial
critical and discriminant subspaces are analogs of
Definition
3.1
. Note that for both
of them, the spaces
X
and
Y
are assumed to be
smooth
.

Definition 10.3
.

Let
\pi:X\to Y
be a finite morphism between complex analytic varieties
of the same dimension.

(1)

The
Fitting critical subspace
\boxed{\Xi^{F}_{\pi}}
of
\pi
is the subspace

Z(F_{0}(\Omega^{1}_{\pi}))
of
X
.

(2)

Assume that
X
and
Y
are
smooth
.
The
divisorial critical subspace
\boxed{\Xi^{D}_{\pi}}
of
\pi
is the effective divisor

Z(\pi^{*}\omega_{Y})
, where
\omega_{Y}
is any holomorphic nowhere zero form of
maximal degree defined on a non-empty open subset of
Y
.

A holomorphic nowhere zero form of maximal degree may not exist globally on
Y
. This poses no problem, because two such forms defined on the same open subset of
Y
lead to the same divisorial critical subspace on
X
. One may therefore work with a covering of
Y
by open subsets on which such forms exist.

The following definition is obtained by combining Definitions
10.2
and
10.3
:

Definition 10.4
.

Let
\pi:X\to Y
be a finite morphism between complex analytic varieties
of the same dimension.

(1)

The
Fitting discriminant subspace
\boxed{\Delta^{F}_{\pi}}
of
\pi

is the effective divisor
\pi^{F}_{*}\Xi^{F}_{\pi}
of
Y
.

(2)

Assume that
X
and
Y
are
smooth
.
The
divisorial discriminant subspace
\boxed{\Delta^{D}_{\pi}}
of
\pi

is the effective divisor
\pi^{D}_{*}\Xi^{D}_{\pi}
of
Y
.

In the following statement, we assume from the start that
both
X
and
Y
are
smooth
:

Proposition 10.5
.

Let
\pi:X\to Y
be a finite morphism between smooth complex analytic varieties
of the same dimension.

(1)

If
A
is an effective divisor of
X
, then we have
\pi^{F}_{*}A=\pi^{D}_{*}A
.

(2)

The Fitting and divisorial critical subspaces of
X
coincide:

\Xi^{F}_{\pi}=\Xi^{D}_{\pi}
.

(3)

The Fitting and divisorial discriminant subspaces of
Y
coincide:

\Delta^{F}_{\pi}=\Delta^{D}_{\pi}
.

Proof.

\bullet

Proof of (
1
).

Localizing in an open neighborhood of any point of
Y
and on any connected component
of its preimage by
\pi
, we may assume that
X
and
Y
are connected and that
A
is defined
by a global holomorphic function
\alpha:X\to\mathbb{C}
. Consider the standard presentation of
the
\mathcal{O}_{X}
-module
\mathcal{O}_{A}
, where the first morphism is the multiplication by
\alpha
:

\mathcal{O}_{X}\xrightarrow{\cdot\alpha}\mathcal{O}_{X}\to\mathcal{O}_{A}.

Since the direct images of exact sequences of sheaves of abelian groups under finite morphisms
remain exact (see
[
19
, Chapter I.3, Theorem 4]
), we get the following exact
sequence of
\mathcal{O}_{Y}
-modules:

(19)

\pi_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X}\xrightarrow{\cdot\alpha}\pi_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X}\to\pi_{*}\mathcal{O}_{A}.

As
X
and
Y
are smooth of the same dimension and
\pi
is a finite morphism, the
pullback by
\pi
of any local coordinate system on the target
Y
is a regular sequence on
X
.
By
[
15
, Corollary on page 154]
, we deduce that
\pi
is a
flat
morphism.
Using again the finiteness of
\pi
, it follows from
[
15
, Proposition 3.13]
that

\pi_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X}
is a locally free
\mathcal{O}_{Y}
-module.

After possibly shrinking again
Y
and
X
, we may assume therefore that
\pi_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X}

is free on
Y
. That is, there exists an isomorphism
\pi_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X}\simeq\mathcal{O}^{n}_{Y}
of

\mathcal{O}_{Y}
-modules, where
n>0
is the degree of
\pi
. The exact sequence
(
19
) becomes therefore:

\mathcal{O}^{n}_{Y}\xrightarrow{\mu_{\alpha}}\mathcal{O}^{n}_{Y}\to\pi_{*}\mathcal{O}_{A},

where
\mu_{\alpha}
is the endomorphism corresponding to the multiplication by

\alpha
on
\mathcal{O}_{X}
. Definition
10.1
yields:

(20)

F_{0}(\pi_{*}\mathcal{O}_{A})=(\det(\mu_{\alpha})),

that is, the
0
-th Fitting
\mathcal{O}_{Y}
-ideal
F_{0}(\pi_{*}\mathcal{O}_{A})
is principal, generated
by the determinant
\det(\mu_{\alpha})
. But this determinant is the
norm

N(\alpha)
of
\alpha
relative to the finite morphism
\pi
.
By
[
31
, Remark 2.19 of page 272]
, the divisorial direct image
\pi^{D}_{*}A

is defined by
N(\alpha)
, that is, by
\det(\mu_{\alpha})
. From relation
(
20
) we deduce that:

\pi_{*}^{F}A=Z(F_{0}(\pi_{*}\mathcal{O}_{A}))=Z(\det(\mu_{\alpha}))=\pi^{D}_{*}A.

\bullet

Proof of (
2
).
Consider the exact sequence (
18
)
of modules of differentials. After
shrinking
Y
and
X
, we may assume that both are embeddable
as open subsets of
\mathbb{C}^{m}
, where
m>0
is their common dimension.
Let
\underline{x}:=(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})
and
\underline{y}:=(y_{1},\dots,y_{m})
be global
coordinates on
X
and
Y
respectively. Then:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\pi^{*}\Omega^{1}_{Y}=\displaystyle{\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}}\ \mathcal{O}_{X}\pi^{*}(dy_{i}),\\
\Omega^{1}_{X}=\displaystyle{\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}}\ \mathcal{O}_{X}dx_{i},\end{array}\right.

and the exact sequence (
18
) becomes:

\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{O}_{X}\pi^{*}(dy_{i})\hookrightarrow\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{O}_{X}dx_{i}\to\Omega^{1}_{\pi}\to 0.

The matrix of the left morphism of
\mathcal{O}_{X}
-modules is
the Jacobian matrix
\mbox{Jac}_{\underline{x}}(\pi^{*}\underline{y})
of
\pi

relative to the coordinate systems
\underline{x}
and
\underline{y}
. Therefore:

(21)

F_{0}(\Omega^{1}_{\pi})=(\mbox{Jac}_{\underline{x}}(\pi^{*}\underline{y})).

Consider now the nowhere zero holomorphic form

\omega_{Y}:=dy_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dy_{m}
of maximal degree on
Y
. Then:

\pi^{*}\omega_{Y}=\pi^{*}dy_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\pi^{*}dy_{m}=\mbox{Jac}_{\underline{x}}(\pi^{*}\underline{y})\ dx_{1}\wedge\cdots dx_{m},

which yields:

Z(\pi^{*}\omega_{Y})=Z(\mbox{Jac}_{\underline{x}}(\pi^{*}\underline{y})).

Combining this equality with equality (
21
), we get:

\Xi^{F}_{\pi}=Z(F_{0}(\Omega^{1}_{\pi}))=Z(\mbox{Jac}_{\underline{x}}(\pi^{*}\underline{y}))=Z(\pi^{*}\omega_{Y})=\Xi^{D}_{\pi}.

The first and last equalities follow from Definition
10.3
.

\bullet

Proof of (
3
).
It is enough to combine the properties
of points (
1
) and (
3
):

\Delta^{F}_{\pi}=\pi_{*}^{F}\Xi^{F}_{\pi}=\pi_{*}^{F}\Xi^{D}_{\pi}=\pi_{*}^{D}\Xi^{D}_{\pi}=\Delta^{D}_{\pi}.

∎

Proposition
10.6
below was stated by Teissier in
[
47
, pages 341-342]
.
He gave the following sketch of proof: “
Indeed, the formation
of the critical subspace is [compatible with base change], as well as that of [direct image] and that
of the Fitting ideal
”. Here we give a more detailed
and formalized proof.

Proposition 10.6
.

The formation of Fitting discriminant spaces is compatible with base changes.

Proof.

Consider a finite morphism
\pi:X\to Y
satisfying the hypotheses
of Definition
10.4
. Denote by
h_{Y}:\tilde{Y}\to Y
an arbitrary morphism
of base change. It yields the following cartesian diagram:

(22)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\tilde{X}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\tilde{Y}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces h_{X}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\tilde{\pi}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lrcorner}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces h_{Y}}

Finiteness being preserved under base change, the morphism
\tilde{\pi}
is also finite.
As the formation of modules of relative
1
-forms is compatible with
base changes (see
[
48
, page 581]
), one has

\Omega^{1}_{\tilde{\pi}}=h_{X}^{*}\Omega^{1}_{\pi}
.
Therefore, using the fact that the formation of Fitting ideals is also compatible
with base changes (see
[
48
, page 570]
or
[
25
, Proposition D.4]
), we get:

F_{0}(\Omega^{1}_{\tilde{\pi}})=F_{0}(h_{X}^{*}\Omega^{1}_{\pi})=h_{X}^{*}F_{0}(\Omega^{1}_{\pi})
.
By Definition
10.3
(
1
), we deduce that

\Xi^{F}_{\tilde{\pi}}=h_{X}^{*}\Xi^{F}_{\pi}
.
This equality means that the following diagram, whose vertical arrows
are the natural embeddings, is cartesian:

(23)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Xi^{F}_{\tilde{\pi}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Xi^{F}_{\pi}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\tilde{X}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces h_{X}|_{\Xi^{F}_{\tilde{\pi}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lrcorner}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces h_{X}}

As the composition of two cartesian diagrams is again cartesian, composing (
22
)
and (
23
) we get the cartesian diagram:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Xi^{F}_{\tilde{\pi}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Xi^{F}_{\pi}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\tilde{Y}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces h_{X}|_{\Xi^{F}_{\tilde{\pi}}}}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces h_{Y}}

The formation of direct images of structure sheaves being compatible with base changes
(see
[
48
, page 571]
), we deduce that

\tilde{\pi}_{*}\mathcal{O}_{\Xi^{F}_{\tilde{\pi}}}=h_{Y}^{*}\pi_{*}\mathcal{O}_{\Xi^{F}_{\pi}}
. Therefore

F_{0}(\tilde{\pi}_{*}\mathcal{O}_{\Xi^{F}_{\tilde{\pi}}})=F_{0}(h_{Y}^{*}\pi_{*}\mathcal{O}_{\Xi^{F}_{\pi}})=h_{Y}^{*}F_{0}(\pi_{*}\mathcal{O}_{\Xi^{F}_{\pi}})
. For the last equality we have used again the
compatibility of Fitting ideals with base changes. As a consequence:

\Delta^{F}_{\tilde{\pi}}=Z(F_{0}(\tilde{\pi}_{*}\mathcal{O}_{\Xi^{F}_{\tilde{\pi}}}))=h_{Y}^{*}Z(F_{0}(\pi_{*}\mathcal{O}_{\Xi^{F}_{\pi}}))=h_{Y}^{*}\Delta^{F}_{\pi},

where the first and last equalities follow from Definition
10.4
(
1
).
The resulting equality
\Delta^{F}_{\tilde{\pi}}=h_{Y}^{*}\Delta^{F}_{\pi}
proves the claim.
∎

11.
Invariance of the initial Newton curve of a finite morphism

In this section we prove Theorem
A
of the introduction, which states that the initial Newton curve of the discriminant divisor of a finite morphism
(f,g):(S,s)\to(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
depends only on the principal divisors
Z(f),Z(g)
defined by
f
and
g
. As in
[
20
]
, we deduce this fact from a preliminary result which states that if
\alpha,\beta\in\mathfrak{m}_{S,s}
, then the initial Newton curves of the discriminant divisors of the finite morphisms
(f,g)
and
(\tilde{f},\tilde{g}):=((1+\alpha)f,(1+\beta)g)
coincide (see Proposition
11.4
). In turn, we prove this statement by looking at the univariate polynomials
\mathcal{P}_{\Delta,\mathcal{L}}
and
\mathcal{P}_{\tilde{\Delta},\mathcal{L}}
associated to the compact edges
\mathcal{L}
of the Jacobian Newton polygon
\mathcal{N}(\Delta)
of
(f,g)
, which is equal by Theorem
C
to the Jacobian Newton polygon
\mathcal{N}(\tilde{\Delta})
of
(\tilde{f},\tilde{g})
. We prove first that
\mathcal{P}_{\Delta,\mathcal{L}}
and

\mathcal{P}_{\tilde{\Delta},\mathcal{L}}
have the same set of roots (see Proposition
11.1
),
then that the corresponding multiplicities also coincide (see Proposition
11.3
).
We deduce this constancy of multiplicities from the result that the discriminants
of a certain one-parameter family of finite morphisms
depend holomorphically of the parameter (see Proposition
11.2
).

Let
f,g,\alpha,\beta\in\mathfrak{m}_{S,s}
. Define:

(24)

\boxed{\tilde{f}}:=(1+\alpha)f,\ \ \boxed{\tilde{g}}:=(1+\beta)g.

Therefore
Z(f)=Z(\tilde{f})
and
Z(g)=Z(\tilde{g})
.
We assume that
\varphi=(f,g):(S,s)\to(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
is finite. By Proposition
2.2
,
\boxed{\tilde{\varphi}}=(\tilde{f},\tilde{g}):(S,s)\to(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
is also finite. Denote by
\boxed{\Delta}
and
\boxed{\tilde{\Delta}}
their respective discriminant curves. By Theorem
C
, the Jacobian Newton polygons
\mathcal{N}(\Delta)
and
\mathcal{N}(\tilde{\Delta})
of the morphisms
\varphi
and
\tilde{\varphi}
coincide. Consider a compact edge
\mathcal{L}
of this polygon and the associated univariate polynomials
\mathcal{P}_{\Delta,\mathcal{L}}
and
\mathcal{P}_{\tilde{\Delta},\mathcal{L}}
, in the sense of Definition
7.11
.

Proposition 11.1
.

The polynomials
\mathcal{P}_{\Delta,\mathcal{L}}
and
\mathcal{P}_{\tilde{\Delta},\mathcal{L}}
have the same sets of roots in
\mathbb{C}^{*}
.

Proof.

Let
(a,b)
be the unique primitive vector of
\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{2}
which is orthogonal to
\mathcal{L}
. Define the finite morphism
\boxed{\tilde{\varphi}_{a,b}}:=(\tilde{f}^{b},\tilde{g}^{a})
similarly to the definition of the finite morphism
\varphi_{a,b}:=(f^{b},g^{a})
studied in Section
6
.
By Proposition
8.2
combined with Delgado and Maugendre’s Theorem
3.6
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
is a root of the polynomial
\mathcal{P}_{\Delta,\mathcal{L}}
if and only if
[\lambda:1]
is a
\varphi_{a,b}
-special point of
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{1}
in the sense of Definition
3.4
. Similarly,
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
is a root of the polynomial
\mathcal{P}_{\tilde{\Delta},\mathcal{L}}
if and only if
[\lambda:1]
is a
\tilde{\varphi}_{a,b}
-special point of
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Consider now a good resolution
\pi:(S_{\pi},E_{\pi})\to(S,0)
which lifts the indeterminacies of
\mathbb{P}\varphi_{a,b}
. It results from Definition
3.3
that
\pi
lifts also the indeterminacies of
\mathbb{P}\tilde{\varphi}_{a,b}
. As
\alpha,\beta\in\mathfrak{m}_{S,s}
, their pull-backs
\pi^{*}\alpha
and
\pi^{*}\beta
vanish on
E_{\pi}
.
This implies that
the restrictions to
E_{\pi}
of the morphisms
\mathbb{P}\varphi_{a,b}
and
\mathbb{P}\tilde{\varphi}_{a,b}
coincide
. As they are determined by these restrictions, the sets of
\varphi_{a,b}
-special points and of
\tilde{\varphi}_{a,b}
-special points also coincide. The proposition follows.
∎

We want to prove now that
\mathcal{P}_{\Delta,\mathcal{L}}
and
\mathcal{P}_{\tilde{\Delta},\mathcal{L}}
not only have the same sets of roots, but that the corresponding multiplicities also coincide. In order to reach this conclusion, we look now at
f
and
\tilde{f}
as members of a
holomorphic family
of elements of
\mathfrak{m}_{S,s}
, and similarly for
g
and
\tilde{g}
. Namely, consider:

\boxed{f_{t}}:=(1+t\alpha)f,\ \ \boxed{g_{t}}:=(1+t\beta)g

for every
t\in\mathbb{C}
. Therefore
f_{0}=f,f_{1}=\tilde{f}
and
g_{0}=g,g_{1}=\tilde{g}
.

Define
\boxed{\varphi_{t}}:=(f_{t},g_{t}):(S,s)\to(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
. Denote by
\boxed{\Delta_{t}}\hookrightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
the discriminant divisor of
\varphi_{t}
.

Proposition 11.2
.

The family
(\Delta_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{C}}
of discriminant divisors is holomorphic. That is, there
exists an effective divisor on
(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)\times\mathbb{C}
whose restriction to each germ of
surface
(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)\times\{t\}
is the divisor
\Delta_{t}
.

Proof.

Consider the map:

\Phi:=(f_{t},g_{t},t):(S,s)\times\mathbb{C}_{t}\to(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)\times\mathbb{C}

It is a germ of finite morphism defined along the curve
\{s\}\times\mathbb{C}
.
Consider a finite representative of it and denoted in the same way:

\Phi:S\times\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{B}\times\mathbb{C}

where
\mathbb{B}
is a suitable bidisk neighborhood of the origin of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
.
Restricting
\Phi
to the complements of the curves
\{s\}\times\mathbb{C}
and
\{0\}\times\mathbb{C}

in the source and respectively the target, we get a finite morphism of
3
-dimensional

smooth
complex varieties:

\Phi^{\circ}:S^{\circ}\times\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{B}^{\circ}\times\mathbb{C},

where
S^{\circ}:=S\setminus\{s\}
and
\mathbb{B}^{\circ}:=\mathbb{B}\setminus\{s\}
.
Consider similarly the morphisms

\varphi_{t}^{\circ}:S^{\circ}\to\mathbb{B}^{\circ},\ \forall\ t\in\mathbb{C}.

Look now at the Fitting discriminant subspace
\Delta^{F}_{\Phi}
of
\Phi
, in the sense of
Definition
10.4
. As Fitting discriminant subspaces
are compatible with base changes (see Proposition
10.6
),

\Delta^{F}_{\Phi^{\circ}}
is the restriction of
\Delta^{F}_{\Phi}
to
\mathbb{B}^{\circ}\times\mathbb{C}
.
By Proposition
10.5
applied to
\Phi^{\circ}
and to
\varphi_{t}^{\circ}
, we have:

(25)

\Delta^{F}_{\Phi^{\circ}}=\Delta^{D}_{\Phi^{\circ}},\ \ \Delta^{F}_{\varphi_{t}^{\circ}}=\Delta^{D}_{\varphi_{t}^{\circ}}.

Consider now a fixed
t\in\mathbb{C}
.

Proposition 11.3
.

The polynomials
\mathcal{P}_{\Delta,\mathcal{L}}
and
\mathcal{P}_{\tilde{\Delta},\mathcal{L}}
coincide,
up to multiplication by a non-zero constant.

Proof.

By Theorem
C
, the family
(\mathcal{N}(\Delta_{t}))_{t\in\mathbb{C}}
of Newton polygons is
constant. Let
\mathcal{L}
be a compact edge of this common Newton polygon.
By Proposition
11.1
, the polynomials
(\mathcal{P}_{\Delta_{t},\mathcal{L}})_{t\in\mathbb{C}}

have the same sets of roots in
\mathbb{C}^{*}
. Moreover, they have the same degree, equal to the
integral length of
\mathcal{L}
. As a consequence of Proposition
11.2
,
this family of polynomials in one variable is
holomorphic
, meaning
that they have coefficients which vary holomorphically in
t
. Indeed,
for a suitable neighborhood
U_{t_{0}}
of every
point
t_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
, the discriminant divisor
\Delta^{D}_{\Phi}
appearing in the proof
of Proposition
11.2
is definable in
\mathbb{B}\times U_{t_{0}}
by a holomorphic function

D(u,v,t)
, therefore
\Delta_{\tau}
is definable by
D(u,v,\tau)
, for each
\tau\in U_{t_{0}}
.

Therefore, the multisets of roots of the polynomials
(\mathcal{P}_{\Delta_{t},\mathcal{L}})_{t\in\mathbb{C}}

vary also holomorphically. As the corresponding sets are constant, this means that the
multiplicities of the roots are also constant. Therefore, the family is constant up to
multiplication by elements of
\mathbb{C}^{*}
. In particular, the polynomials
\mathcal{P}_{\Delta,\mathcal{L}}

and
\mathcal{P}_{\tilde{\Delta},\mathcal{L}}
coincide up to multiplication by elements of
\mathbb{C}^{*}
.
∎

.

The initial Newton curves of the discriminant divisors

coincide.

Proof.

Let

Remark 11.5
.

One could think that the discriminant germs
\Delta
and
\tilde{\Delta}
of Proposition
11.4
have moreover the same topological type. This is not always true, as the following example shows. Take
(S,s):=(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
, with coordinates
(x,y)
.
Consider the pair of series
(f,g):=(x-y^{2},x^{3}+y^{3})\in\mathbb{C}\{x,y\}^{2}
. The critical divisor
\Xi
of the finite morphism
\varphi:=(f,g):(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)\to(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
is defined by the Jacobian determinant of
\varphi
, which shows that
\Xi=Z(y(y+2x^{2}))
. It is therefore a union of two tangent smooth branches. Parametrizing them by
t_{1}\mapsto(t_{1},0)
and
t_{2}\mapsto(t_{2},-2t_{2}^{2})
and composing those parametrizations with
\varphi
, we deduce that
\Delta
is also a union of two tangent smooth branches. Consider then the morphism
\tilde{\varphi}:=((1+y)f,g):(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)\to(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
, which means that we take
(\alpha,\beta):=(y,0)
in equations (
24
).
Its critical divisor
\tilde{\Xi}
, computed as the vanishing divisor of the Jacobian determinant of
\tilde{\varphi}
, is
Z((1+y)y^{2}-x^{2}(x-2y-3y^{2}))
. The only compact edge of its Newton polygon joins the points
(0,2)
and
(0,3)
, which shows that
\tilde{\Xi}
is a singular branch. The support of the discriminant divisor
\tilde{\Delta}
of
\tilde{\varphi}
is therefore also a singular branch. This shows that the pairs
(f,g)
and
((1+y)f,g)
of germs of holomorphic functions determine finite morphisms whose discriminants have different topological types.

Theorem A
.

Let
(S,s)
be a normal surface singularity and let
f,g\in\mathfrak{m}_{S,s}
be such that
\varphi=(f,g):(S,s)\to(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
is a germ of finite morphism. Then the initial Newton curve of the discriminant divisor
\Delta_{\varphi}
depends only on the divisors
Z(f),Z(g)
, up to toric automorphisms of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
.

Proof.

The following proof (which is simple once Proposition 10.4 is known) is analogous to the proofs of
[
17
, Lemma 6.1]
and
[
20
, Corollary 5.3]
.
Any pair of elements of
\mathfrak{m}_{S,s}
defining
(Z(f),Z(g))
is of the form

(A\tilde{f},B\tilde{g})
, where
(A,B)\in(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{2}
and
\tilde{f},\tilde{g}
verify the relations (
24
). Consider the map:

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\boxed{\tau_{A,B}}:&\mathbb{C}^{2}&\to&\mathbb{C}^{2}\\
&(u,v)&\mapsto&(Au,Bv).\end{array}

It is a toric automorphism of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
. The finite map
\boxed{\tilde{\varphi}_{A,B}}:=(A\tilde{f},B\tilde{g}):(S,s)\to(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
satisfies the relation

\tilde{\varphi}_{A,B}=\tau_{A,B}\circ\tilde{\varphi}
.
Therefore
\Delta_{\tilde{\varphi}_{A,B}}=\tau_{A,B}(\Delta_{\tilde{\varphi}})
, which implies the analogous relation
Z(I_{\Delta_{\tilde{\varphi}_{A,B}}})=\tau_{A,B}(Z(I_{\Delta_{\tilde{\varphi}}}))
between the initial Newton curves of the discriminant divisors of
\tilde{\varphi}_{A,B}
and
\tilde{\varphi}
. But
Z(I_{\Delta_{\tilde{\varphi}}})=Z(I_{\Delta_{\varphi}})
by Proposition
11.4
, which implies that

Z(I_{\Delta_{\tilde{\varphi}_{A,B}}})=\tau_{A,B}(Z(I_{\Delta_{\varphi}}))
.
The theorem is proved.
∎

An immediate consequence of Theorem
A
is the fact that the discriminant
\Delta_{\varphi}
is Newton non-degenerate in the sense of Definition
7.5
depends only on the effective divisors
Z(f),Z(g)
. By Proposition
7.8
and Proposition
8.2
combined with Delgado and Maugendre’s Theorem
3.6
, this property holds if and only if, for each compact edge
\mathcal{L}(a,b)
of the Jacobian Newton polygon
\mathcal{N}(\Delta_{\varphi})
, there are as many
\varphi_{a,b}
-special points as the degree of the univariate polynomial
\mathcal{P}_{\Delta_{\varphi},\mathcal{L}}
, that is, as the integral length of
\mathcal{L}(a,b)
.

As explained in the introduction, Theorem
A
allows to define an invariant of pairs of principal effective divisors without common branches on normal surface singularities:

Definition 11.6
.

Let
(S,s)
be a normal surface singularity and
(D_{1},D_{2})
be a pair of principal effective divisors on
(S,s)
without common branches. The
initial Newton curve
of the triple
(S,D_{1},D_{2})
is the initial Newton curve in the sense of Definition
7.11
of the discriminant divisor of the finite morphism
(f,g):(S,s)\to(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
, where
f,g\in\mathfrak{m}_{S,s}
are any two defining functions of
D_{1},D_{2}
.

For instance, consider the normal surface singularity
(S,s)
in
\mathbb{C}^{3}
defined by an equation of the form
z^{n}-h(x,y)=0
, with
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
. Then the initial Newton curve associated to the pair of principal divisors
(Z(x),Z(y))
on
(S,s)
is the affine curve in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
defined by the equation
h_{\mathcal{N}}(x,y)=0
. Indeed, the discriminant divisor of the finite morphism
(x,y):(S,s)\to(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
is
Z(h)\hookrightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{2},0)
.
