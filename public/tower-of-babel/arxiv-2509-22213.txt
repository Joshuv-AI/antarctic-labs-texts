Accuracy-First Rényi Differential Privacy and Post-Processing Immunity
Ossi Räisä, Antti Koskela, Antti Honkela

Abstract
The accuracy-first perspective of differential privacy addresses an important shortcoming by allowing a data analyst to adaptively adjust the quantitative privacy bound instead of sticking to a predetermined bound. Existing works on the accuracy-first perspective have neglected an important property of differential privacy known as post-processing immunity, which ensures that an adversary is not able to weaken the privacy guarantee by post-processing. We address this gap by determining which existing definitions in the accuracy-first perspective have post-processing immunity, and which do not. The only definition with post-processing immunity, pure ex-post privacy, lacks useful tools for practical problems, such as an ex-post analogue of the Gaussian mechanism, and an algorithm to check if accuracy on separate private validation set is high enough. To address this, we propose a new definition based on Rényi differential privacy that has post-processing immunity, and we develop basic theory and tools needed for practical applications. We demonstrate the practicality of our theory with an application to synthetic data generation, where our algorithm successfully adjusts the privacy bound until an accuracy threshold is met on a private validation dataset.

Introduction

As the volume of data collection and capabilities of machine learning models
increase, the need for methods to use the data while maintaining the privacy
of data subjects grows ever larger. Differential privacy (DP;
Dwork et al. 2006b
)
has become the gold-standard definition of privacy to address this need.

However, differential privacy is a very strict definition. First, it requires protection
against a worst-case adversary with very strong capabilities, which necessitates
necessitates very strong protection that can heavily decrease the utility of an analysis.
Second, it requires specifying a numerical privacy bound before running the algorithm,
which precludes workflows such as reducing the privacy bound over time in case
the data becomes less sensitive, using different bounds for different
audiences
(
Rosenblatt et al. 2024
)
, and addressing low utility by decreasing the
privacy protection afterwards
(
Ligett et al. 2017
;
Nanayakkara et al. 2024
)
.

Motivated by these issues,
Ligett et al. 2017
proposed the

accuracy-first
perspective to DP, where the analyst sets a minimum utility (accuracy)
threshold, and finds the strongest privacy bound that empirically meets the threshold.
As the privacy bound now depends on the private data, standard DP definitions, known
as
ex-ante
definitions, are not applicable. Instead, this perspective requires
a new type of privacy definition that allows the privacy bound to depend on the
private data, known as
ex-post privacy
.

Ligett et al. 2017
proposed the first ex-post privacy definition,
pure ex-post privacy, and
developed a
Laplace noise reduction
mechanism that satisfies their definition.
In subsequent work,
Whitehouse et al. 2022
proposed a probabilistic
relaxation, and developed the
Brownian mechanism
, an analogue of the Gaussian
mechanism
(
Dwork et al. 2006a
)
of ex-ante DP providing higher utility.
In parallel to our work,

Ghazi et al. 2025
developed a hyperparameter tuning algorithm
for the accuracy-first perspective, and proposed ex-post privacy definitions based
on
approximate DP
(ADP,
Dwork et al. 2006a
) and
Rényi DP
(RDP;
Mironov 2017
).

However, these works have neglected an important aspect that any privacy definition should
have, known as
post-processing immunity

(
Dwork & Roth 2014
)
.
Post-processing immunity is the requirement
that any processing of the output of a private algorithm cannot decrease the privacy guarantee.
It is clear why any sensible privacy definition should have post-processing immunity: without it, an adversary could use post-processing to infer private information more easily than promised, making the guarantee weaker than promised.

Contributions

Our goal is to address this gap by determining whether previous ex-post privacy definitions have post-processing immunity, and by developing additional theory that supports mechanisms for practical problems under ex-post privacy guarantees with post-processing immunity.

1.

We propose a new notation for ex-post privacy definitions that makes the promised
privacy bound a part of the output of the algorithm, instead of making it a separate
function of the output. The new notation allows defining post-processing immunity
in an elegant way, while we were not able to find a satisfactory definition with the
old notation, and neither has any previous work
(
Ligett et al. 2017
;
Whitehouse et al. 2022
;
Ghazi et al. 2025
)
that we know of.
We then formulate the existing definitions to use our new notation.
See
Section

3.1
for details.

2.

After the translation to the new notation, we prove that the pure ex-post privacy
definition of
Ligett et al. 2017
has post-processing immunity, while
the probabilistic relaxation of
Whitehouse et al. 2022
does not have it.
This motivates the development of an alternative relaxation that both has
post-processing immunity, and supports the Brownian mechanism. See

Section

3.2
for details,
and
Table

1
for a summary.

3.

We investigate an ex-post privacy definition based on RDP, which has been proposed
in parallel by
Ghazi et al. 2025
. We prove that this definition
has post-processing immunity, along with many basic properties that are useful in constructing
practical mechanisms. In particular, we provide an algorithm that checks
whether accuracy on a private validation dataset is high enough, whereas
previous works only provide algorithms to check accuracy on the training
set
(
Ligett et al. 2017
;
Whitehouse et al. 2022
)
.
See
Section

4
.

4.

We prove that the Brownian mechanism satisfies ex-post RDP in

Section

5
. This is the first time the Brownian
mechanism has been proven to satisfy an ex-post definition with post-processing
immunity. Our proof uses a more elementary view of the
Brownian mechanism than

Whitehouse et al. 2022
, that does not require working with stochastic processes, which may be of interest to future work.

5.

We give a practical example of using the Brownian mechanism and our theory in
Section

6
.
We generate synthetic data, and ensure that the accuracy of a classifier trained on the
synthetic data on a private validation dataset is high enough on a minimal privacy budget.
We find that our implementation is able to achieve high accuracy while keeping a tight
privacy budget.

2.1
Differential Privacy Basics

Differential privacy quantitatively formalises the notion of privacy loss resulting from the release
of an output that depends on private input data. This can be formalised in several ways. For the
standard ex-ante setting with the privacy bound fixed upfront, we present two definitions. The
first is the most commonly used definition, known as

approximate DP
(ADP) or
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP.
The second is known as
Rényi DP
(RDP).

{definition}
[
Dwork et al. 2006a
]
An algorithm
\mathcal{M}\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP
for
\epsilon\geq 0
,
0\leq\delta<1
if, for all neighbouring
X,X^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
and
all measurable
A\subset\mathcal{Y}
,

\Pr(\mathcal{M}(X)\in A)\leq e^{\epsilon}\Pr(\mathcal{M}(X^{\prime})\in A)+\delta.

(1)

The case with
\delta=0
is also known as pure DP, and denoted
\epsilon
-DP.

{definition}
[
Mironov 2017
]
An algorithm
\mathcal{M}\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
is
(\alpha,\epsilon)
-RDP for

\epsilon\geq 0
,
\alpha>1
if, for all neighbouring
X,X^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
,

\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\ln\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{y\sim\mathcal{M}(X)}\left[\left(\frac{p_{\mathcal{M}{}(X)}(y)}{p_{\mathcal{M}{}(X^{\prime})}(y)}\right)^{\alpha}\right]\leq\epsilon.

(2)

It is possible to define RDP for
\alpha=1
and
\alpha=\infty
by taking a limit of the left hand side,
but we will not consider those values in this paper.

DP algorithms are also called mechanisms. We use
X\sim X^{\prime}
to denote neighbouring datasets. The neighbourhood
relation
\sim
is domain-specific, but the results in this paper, with the exception of the experiments in

Section

6
, are agnostic to the definition.

We will also make use of
privacy loss functions
(PLF) and
privacy loss random variables
(PLRV).

{definition}
[
Sommer et al. 2019
]
For random variables
P,Q
, with respective density functions
p_{P},p_{Q}
, the PLF is

\mathcal{L}(y)=\ln\frac{p_{P}(y)}{p_{Q}(y)}
and the
PLRV is
L=\mathcal{L}(y)
, where
y\sim P
.

The PLF
\mathcal{L}^{\mathcal{M}}_{X/X^{\prime}}
and PLRV
L^{\mathcal{M}}_{X/X^{\prime}}
for a mechanism
\mathcal{M}
and
datasets
X\sim X^{\prime}
is formed by setting
P=\mathcal{M}(X)
and

Q=\mathcal{M}(X^{\prime})
. The definition of
(\epsilon,0)
-DP is then equivalent to

\Pr(L^{\mathcal{M}}_{X/X^{\prime}}>\epsilon)=0

for all
X\sim X^{\prime}
.

2.2
Accuracy-First Differential Privacy

In the accuracy-first perspective of DP, the privacy bound is not fixed upfront, and can vary based on
the result of a mechanism, possibly in a data-dependent way. Since common ex-ante definitions of DP require
a fixed privacy bound
(
Dwork & Roth 2014
;
Mironov 2017
;
Dong et al. 2022
)
,
the accuracy-first perspective requires generalised definitions that can accommodate a data-dependent
privacy bound. We call these definitions ex-post
privacy definitions after the first such definition from
Ligett et al. 2017
.

{definition}
[
Ligett et al. 2017
;
Whitehouse et al. 2022
]
An algorithm
\mathcal{M}\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
is
(\mathcal{E},\delta)
-probabilistically ex-post private for

\mathcal{E}\colon\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
if, for all
X\sim X^{\prime}
,

\Pr_{y\sim\mathcal{M}(X)}(\mathcal{L}^{\mathcal{M}}_{X/X^{\prime}}(y)>\mathcal{E}(y))\leq\delta.

(3)

The original definition from
Ligett et al. 2017
has
\delta=0
.
To distinguish it from the
\delta>0
case, we will refer to the
\delta=0
case as

\mathcal{E}
-pure ex-post privacy.
These definitions accommodate a varying privacy bound by making the bound a function
\mathcal{E}
of the output

y
.

Recently,
Ghazi et al. 2025
have proposed
1

Ghazi et al. 2025

credit this definition to
Wu et al. 2019
, which is the journal version
of

an ex-post privacy definition similar to ADP, which we call
approximate ex-post privacy
to distinguish from
the previous definition.

{definition}
[
Ghazi et al. 2025
]
An algorithm
\mathcal{M}\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
is
(\mathcal{E},\delta)
-approximately ex-post private for

\mathcal{E}\colon\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
if, for all
X\sim X^{\prime}
and measurable
S\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
,

\Pr_{y\sim\mathcal{M}(X)}(y\in S)\leq\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{y\sim\mathcal{M}(X^{\prime})}[e^{\mathcal{E}(y)}\mathbb{I}_{y\in S}]+\delta.

(4)

Due to the recency of this definition, we leave closer examinations of it for
future work.

3
Post-Processing in Ex-Post Privacy

In ex-ante DP, post-processing immunity requires that processing the output of a DP mechanism without further access to the input data cannot weaken the DP guarantee
(
Dwork & Roth 2014
)
. Any sensible privacy definition
should have post-processing immunity, since if an adversary can weaken a privacy guarantee
by simply post-processing, that guarantee was not real in the first place. Indeed,
common ex-ante DP definitions have post-processing immunity
(
Dwork et al. 2006a
;
Dwork et al. 2006b
;
Bun & Steinke 2016
;
Mironov 2017
;
Dong et al. 2022
)
.

In addition,
post-processing immunity is extremely useful in constructing DP mechanisms to solve practical problems,
as it allows choosing an intermediate quantity to release with a simple DP mechanism, and
using that to solve the problem without needing to consider DP further. Many DP algorithms
for solving complex problems, such as DP-SGD
(
Rajkumar & Agarwal 2012
;
Song et al. 2013
;
Abadi et al. 2016
)
,
and DP synthetic data generation
(
Hardt et al. 2012
)
, use this
design.

In contrast to ex-ante DP, we are not aware of prior work proving that any ex-post privacy
definition has post-processing immunity.
To rectify this problem, we start with a very basic issue: the
notation used in ex-post privacy definitions
(
Section

3.1
). We then determine whether
pure ex-post privacy and
\delta
-probabilistic ex-post privacy have
post-processing immunity (
Section

3.2
). See

for a summary
of the results.

3.1
Ex-post Privacy Notation

The original notation for ex-post private mechanisms, presented in
Section

2.2
, has a major problem
when considering post-processing. All of the definitions use a function
\mathcal{E}\colon\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

that computes the privacy bound based on the output. For simplicity, in the following we simply refer to

\mathcal{E}
-ex-post privacy, leaving out the
\delta
and the exact definition, as this issue applies to all of the
definitions.

A naive statement of post-processing immunity would say that if a randomised function
f\colon\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}

is applied to the output of an
\mathcal{E}
-ex-post private
mechanism
\mathcal{M}\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
, the resulting mechanism
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}=f\circ\mathcal{M}
is
\mathcal{E}
-ex-post private.
However, this statement is not coherent: the domain of
\mathcal{E}
must match the range of the mechanism, but the
domain of
\mathcal{E}
is
\mathcal{Y}
, while the range of
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
is
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
. To state post-processing immunity, we would
need to construct a function
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\colon\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
that is in some way equivalent to
\mathcal{E}
.

However, we have not been able to come up with a satisfactory construction of
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
that works for all
f
.
One could attempt to define
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(y^{\prime})=\sup_{y\in f^{-1}(y^{\prime})}\mathcal{E}(y)
, where
f^{-1}
denotes the preimage.
However, this is potentially extremely pessimistic. For example, consider the Brownian mechanism
in the form presented by
Whitehouse et al. 2022
, where the mechanism releases the
output
y\in\mathbb{R}
and the final noise variance
T>0
, and
\mathcal{E}
is computed from
T
. Suppose that the
noise variances are calibrated in such a way that
\mathcal{E}(T)\in[0.1,10]
. Consider the post-processing

g(y,T)=y
that forgets
T
. Since a particular value of
y
as output does not rule out any

T>0
as a possible output, we have
g^{-1}(y)=\{(y,T)\mid T>0,0.1\leq\mathcal{E}(T)\leq 10\}
,
so with this definition of
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
, we have
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(y)=10
for all
y\in\mathbb{R}
.

To solve this issue, we introduce a new notation that makes the privacy
bound a part of the output of the mechanism. Mathematically, we define a mechanism in the ex-post setting to be
a randomised function
\mathcal{M}\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. We use
\mathcal{M}{}(X)_{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
to
denote the output of interest from the mechanism, and denote the returned privacy bound by

\mathcal{M}{}(X)_{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. In total, the mechanism returns the pair

\mathcal{M}(X)=(\mathcal{M}{}(X)_{y},\mathcal{M}{}(X)_{\epsilon})
.
Since
\epsilon
is part of the output,
an ex-post privacy definition must account for the fact that releasing
\epsilon
may also cause
privacy loss, so the PLF
\mathcal{L}^{\mathcal{M}}_{X/X^{\prime}}(y,\epsilon)
is now a function of
the pair
(y,\epsilon)
.

Next, we present a formulation of the probabilistic ex-post privacy definition (
Section

2.2
) using our notation. The other definitions can be
converted similarly. As the privacy bound is now part of the
mechanism’s output, we drop the
\mathcal{E}
from the definition, so it is now called
\delta
-probabilistic ex-post privacy.

{definition}
[
\delta
-probabilistic ex-post privacy]
An algorithm
\mathcal{M}\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is
\delta
-probabilistically ex-post private
if, for all
X\sim X^{\prime}
,

\Pr_{(y,\epsilon)\sim\mathcal{M}(X)}(\mathcal{L}^{\mathcal{M}}_{X/X^{\prime}}(y,\epsilon)>\epsilon)\leq\delta.

(5)

Now we can elegantly define post-processing for ex-post mechanisms. Since this is different
from function composition, we use
\odot
to denote this operation.

{definition}

Let
\mathcal{M}\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be a mechanism and let

f\colon\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
be a randomised function. We define the post-processing of

\mathcal{M}
with
f
, denoted
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}=f\odot\mathcal{M}
, to be the mechanism

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}{{}^{\prime}}(X)_{y}

\displaystyle=f\big(\mathcal{M}(X)\big),

(6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}{{}^{\prime}}(X)_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}{}(X)_{\epsilon}.

(7)

With this definition of post-processing, we can simply define post-processing immunity to mean that

\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
satisfy the same ex-post privacy notion, whether it be
\delta
-probabilistic ex-post privacy
or any other definition. In
Section

3.2
, we show that

Section

3.1
satisfies post-processing immunity when
\delta=0
, but not when
\delta>0
.

As
\epsilon
is part of the mechanism’s output, we allow post-processing both the output of interest
y

and
\epsilon
, but the returned privacy bound of the post-processed mechanism does not change.
This is in line with the capabilities of an adversary: they can arbitrarily attempt to find an easier representation
of
(y,\epsilon)
to attack, but they cannot change what the original mechanism claimed the privacy bound to
be.

Disregarding post-processing,
Section

3.1
and
Section

2.2
are equivalent in
the following sense. First, consider a pair
(
\mathcal{M}
,
\mathcal{E}
) from
Section

2.2
. We can define

\mathcal{M}{{}^{\prime}}(X)_{y}=\mathcal{M}(X)
and
\mathcal{M}{{}^{\prime}}(X)_{\epsilon}=\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{M}(X))
. Now
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
satisfies

Section

3.1
if and only if
(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{E})
satisfy
Section

2.2
.
In the other direction, if we have a mechanism
\mathcal{M}
from
Section

3.1
, we can define

\mathcal{M}^{\prime}(X)=(\mathcal{M}{}(X)_{y},\mathcal{M}{}(X)_{\epsilon})
and
\mathcal{E}(y,\epsilon)=\epsilon
. Again,

\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
satisfies
Section

2.2
if and only if
\mathcal{M}
satisfies

Section

3.1
.

3.2
Post-Processing Immunity Status for Existing Definitions

Next, we show that the original ex-post privacy definition
(
Ligett et al. 2017
)

(
Section

3.1
with
\delta=0
) has post-processing immunity. We defer all proofs
in this section to
Section

C.2
.

{theorem}
[]
Let
\mathcal{M}
be a pure ex-post private mechanism with continuous (or discrete) output distributions and let
f
be a
randomised function with continuous (or discrete) output distributions. Then the post-processed
mechanism
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}=f\odot\mathcal{M}
(see
Section

3.1
) is pure ex-post private.

Note that unlike our other theory, we were only able to prove this theorem for the continuous and discrete
special cases, and leave a more general treatment to future work.

In contrast, next we show that
\delta
-probabilistic ex-post privacy with
\delta>0

does not have post-processing immunity.
This is because
\delta
-probabilistic ex-post privacy generalises
(\epsilon,\delta)
-probabilistic-DP, which we define next.

{definition}
[
Meiser 2018
, Definition 4]
A mechanism
\mathcal{M}
is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-probabilistically DP if, for all neighbouring datasets

X,X^{\prime}
, there is a set
S^{\delta}
with
\Pr(\mathcal{M}(X)\in S^{\delta})\leq\delta
such that for all
measurable
S
,

\Pr(\mathcal{M}(X)\in S\setminus S^{\delta})\leq e^{\epsilon}\Pr(\mathcal{M}(X^{\prime})\in S\setminus S^{\delta}).

(8)

Meiser 2018
shows that Definition
3.2
does not
have post-processing immunity. The simplest counterexample has a mechanism with binary input
and four possible outputs, and a post-processing that simply combines two of the possible
outputs into one. The probabilities of each input-output pair are chosen such that
the original mechanism is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-probabilistically DP, but the post-processed one is not.

It turns out that Definition
3.2
is a special case of
\delta
-probabilistic ex-post
privacy, which means that
\delta
-probabilistic ex-post privacy cannot have post-processing immunity either when

\delta>0
.

{theorem}

[]
For mechanisms
\mathcal{M}
with constant
\mathcal{M}{}(X)_{\epsilon}=\epsilon_{c}
,
\delta
-probabilistic ex-post privacy
(Definition
3.1
) is equivalent to
(\epsilon_{c},\delta)
-probabilistic DP
(Definition
3.2
).

4
Ex-post RDP

In this section, we define ex-post RDP and prove some basic properties.
We give a Bayesian interpretation of ex-post RDP similar to that of

Mironov 2017
for ex-ante RDP in

Appendix

B
.

Definition of ex-post RDP

The definition of RDP uses the following inequality:

\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\ln\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{y\sim\mathcal{M}(X^{\prime})}\left[\left(\frac{p_{\mathcal{M}{}(X)}(y)}{p_{\mathcal{M}{}(X^{\prime})}(y)}\right)^{\alpha}\right]\leq\epsilon.

(9)

It is possible to move
\epsilon
inside the expectation and arrive at an equivalent inequality:

\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{y\sim\mathcal{M}(X^{\prime})}\left[e^{(1-\alpha)\epsilon}\left(\frac{p_{\mathcal{M}{}(X)}(y)}{p_{\mathcal{M}{}(X^{\prime})}(y)}\right)^{\alpha}\right]\leq 1.

(10)

This motivates an ex-post version of RDP:

{definition}

A mechanism
\mathcal{M}\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is
\alpha
-ex-post RDP (
\alpha>1
) if, for all neighbouring
X\sim X^{\prime}
,

\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{(y,\epsilon)\sim\mathcal{M}(X^{\prime})}\left[e^{(1-\alpha)\epsilon}\left(\frac{p_{\mathcal{M}{}(X)}(y,\epsilon)}{p_{\mathcal{M}{}(X^{\prime})}(y,\epsilon)}\right)^{\alpha}\right]\leq 1.

(11)

Note that unlike the definition of RDP, we do not permit
\alpha=1
or
\alpha=\infty
in this work, and leave the
investigation whether ex-post RDP could be extended to them for future work. The reason is that the limits used to define
these cases for RDP are not as well-understood when
\epsilon
is a random variable inside the expectation.
This definition has also been proposed in parallel work

The Brownian mechanism
(
Whitehouse et al. 2022
)
is the accuracy-first analogue of the Gaussian
mechanism
(
Dwork et al. 2006a
)
.
The idea of the mechanism is to add Gaussian noise to each release of a function

f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
like the Gaussian mechanism, and reduce the variance of the noise if
the privacy budget is increased for the next release. The added noises at each release are
correlated in such a way that the latest release contains all of the information
on the private data
X
that the previous releases contain, so the privacy costs of
multiple releases do not accumulate. The appropriate correlations are obtained from a stochastic process called
Brownian motion
. We defer the mathematical definition to

Section

C.4
in the Appendix.

We analyse the Brownian mechanism through an alternative perspective that does not use Brownian motion directly.
Consider a mechanism that, given an
initial RDP bound
(\alpha,\epsilon_{1})

\hat{s}_{1}\sim f(X)+\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{1}^{2})

(\alpha,\epsilon_{1})
-RDP. For subsequent releases

\epsilon_{i}=\mathcal{E}(\hat{s}_{1:i-1},\epsilon_{1:i-1})>\epsilon_{i-1}
with constant
\alpha

according to the extra RDP budget that
became available
with the increase

7
Conclusion

In this work, we determined
which ex-post privacy definitions have post-processing immunity and which do not.
As the one definition with
post-processing immunity does not support the Brownian mechanism, we filled this gap by proposing
a new ex-post privacy definition with post-processing immunity, the Brownian mechanism, and
supporting theory that enables application to practical problems. We demonstrated one
such application: tabular synthetic data generation, where our mechanism successfully met a
downstream classification accuracy threshold while minimising the spent privacy budget.

There remains significant room for future work. For ex-post RDP, particularly promising
directions would be the development of ex-post versions of DP-SGD
(
Abadi et al. 2016
)
and the state-of-the-art DP synthetic data generator AIM
(
McKenna et al. 2022
)
.
The latter would require at least an ex-post version of the
exponential
mechanism
(
McSherry & Talwar 2007
)
.
Another direction of future work is determining the properties of

\delta
-approximate ex-post privacy, and investigating whether an
ex-post version of Gaussian DP
(
Dong et al. 2022
)
is
possible.

Appendix A
Privacy Odometers and Ex-Post Privacy

A closely related line of work to ex-post privacy is the so-called fully adaptive composition that
studies
privacy filters
and

odometers

(
Rogers et al. 2016
;
Feldman & Zrnic 2021
;
Lécuyer 2021
;
Koskela et al. 2023
;
Whitehouse et al. 2023
;
Haney et al. 2023
)
.
While ex-ante DP composition theorems require specifying the privacy bounds at each step in
advance, fully adaptive composition considers the case where the privacy bounds can also be
chosen adaptively, based on results from previous iterations. As filters and odometers have been
developed for many DP definitions, we use
\gamma\in\Gamma
to denote a generic privacy bound
when discussing filters and odometers in general.

Privacy filters require specifying an upper bound
\gamma_{c}
on the privacy loss. The filter
keeps track of the
accrued privacy loss during the iterations, and halts the computation before the accrued privacy
loss exceeds the upper bound. The filter guarantees ex-ante DP with the bound
\gamma_{c}
.

Privacy odometers do not require specifying an upper bound beforehand. Instead, an odometer keeps track of
the accumulated privacy loss, and returns the value of the accumulator at the end of the
computation. Interpreting the value returned by the odometer is not as simple as interpreting
a filter. While a filter guarantees an ex-ante definition of DP with parameter
\gamma_{c}
,
an odometer does not do so.

We give two reasons why an odometer cannot provide an ex-ante DP guarantee.
3
3

3

Note that
Lécuyer 2021
claims that their odometer
provides
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP “for the interaction described in Algorithm 2” (of their paper).
Our arguments here show that this is false if it is indeed a claim of ex-ante

(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP. The paper of
Lécuyer 2021
does not have
any proof of this claim, only the other claim of their Theorem 2 that provides a conversion
to a probabilistic DP odometer.

The first is that
the value
\gamma(X)
returned by the odometer can depend on the data
X
. However,
ex-ante definitions of DP do not permit data-dependent privacy bounds. The second reason
is more concrete. Consider a pathological mechanism
\mathcal{M}_{P}
that releases the input

X
with probability
\frac{1}{2}
, and releases nothing otherwise. It is well-known that

\mathcal{M}_{P}
is not
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP, or
(\epsilon,\delta)
-probabilistically DP
if
\delta<\frac{1}{2}
, and that it is not

(\alpha,\epsilon)
-RDP, unless
\epsilon=\infty
in either case. However, we can analyse this mechanism
with a 1-step odometer, which can return
\epsilon=0
for a probabilistic-DP

(

(

Since we know this mechanism is not ex-ante DP with a finite

Appendix B
Interpretation of ex-post RDP

Mironov 2017
gives a Bayesian
interpretation to RDP based on Bayes factors. Let

R_{I}(X,X^{\prime})
be the Bayes factor between datasets

X\sim X^{\prime}
of an adversary with information
I
.
That is,

R_{I}(X,X^{\prime})=\frac{p(X|I)}{p(X^{\prime}|I)}.

(18)

Prior to the release of the mechanism’s output
y
, the
adversary has a prior
R_{\mathrm{prior}}
with
I=I_{\mathrm{Prior}}
, where

I_{\mathrm{Prior}}
is whatever prior information the adversary has, and
after the release, they have a posterior
R_{\mathrm{post}}
with

I=\{y\}\cup I_{\mathrm{Prior}}
.
(\alpha,\epsilon)
-RDP bounds the

\alpha-1
moment of the change in
R
as
follows
(
Mironov 2017
)
:

\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{y\sim\mathcal{M}(X)}\left[\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{post}}(X,X^{\prime})}{R_{\mathrm{prior}}(X,X^{\prime})}\right)^{\alpha-1}\right]\leq e^{(\alpha-1)\epsilon}.

(19)

We can rearrange (
19
) to

\ln\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{y\sim\mathcal{M}(X)}\left[e^{(1-\alpha)\epsilon}\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{post}}(X,X^{\prime})}{R_{\mathrm{prior}}(X,X^{\prime})}\right)^{\alpha-1}\right]\leq 0.

(20)

We can interpret the left hand side as privacy leakage at the

\alpha-1
moment exceeding

\epsilon
. Under this interpretation, RDP simply guarantees
that there is no excess leakage for a given moment.

Since
\epsilon
is inside the expectation in this form, we can
extend this interpretation to ex-post RDP. We can interpret

\ln\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{y,\epsilon\sim\mathcal{M}(X)}\left[e^{(1-\alpha)\epsilon}\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{post}}(X,X^{\prime})}{R_{\mathrm{prior}}(X,X^{\prime})}\right)^{\alpha-1}\right]

(21)

as the privacy loss at the
\alpha-1
moment exceeding
the
\epsilon
the mechanism
returned. Note that here
R_{\mathrm{post}}
has
I=\{(y,\epsilon)\}\cup I_{\mathrm{Prior}}
.
Analogously to RDP, ex-post RDP guarantees that there is no
excess privacy loss at a given moment.

{theorem}
[]
Let
\mathcal{M}
be an
\alpha
-ex-post RDP mechanism. Then

\ln\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{y,\epsilon\sim\mathcal{M}(X)}\left[e^{(1-\alpha)\epsilon}\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{post}}(X,X^{\prime})}{R_{\mathrm{prior}}(X,X^{\prime})}\right)^{\alpha-1}\right]\leq 0.

(22)

Proof.

First, by Bayes’ theorem,

\begin{split}\frac{R_{\mathrm{post}}(X,X^{\prime})}{R_{\mathrm{prior}}(X,X^{\prime})}=\frac{p_{\mathcal{M}{}(X)}(y,\epsilon)}{p_{\mathcal{M}{}(X^{\prime})}(y,\epsilon)}.\end{split}

(23)

In the expectation in
Section

4
, we first
change the dataset the expectation is taken with respect to,
and use the previous to obtain

\begin{split}\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{y,\epsilon\sim\mathcal{M}(X^{\prime})}\left[e^{(1-\alpha)\epsilon}\left(\frac{p_{\mathcal{M}{}(X)}(y,\epsilon)}{p_{\mathcal{M}{}(X^{\prime})}(y,\epsilon)}\right)^{\alpha}\right]&=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{y,\epsilon\sim\mathcal{M}(X)}\left[e^{(1-\alpha)\epsilon}\left(\frac{p_{\mathcal{M}{}(X)}(y,\epsilon)}{p_{\mathcal{M}{}(X^{\prime})}(y,\epsilon)}\right)^{\alpha-1}\right]\\
&=\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{y,\epsilon\sim\mathcal{M}(X)}\left[e^{(1-\alpha)\epsilon}\left(\frac{R_{\mathrm{post}}(X,X^{\prime})}{R_{\mathrm{prior}}(X,X^{\prime})}\right)^{\alpha-1}\right].\end{split}

(24)

Now taking the logarithm of both sides of the inequality in

Section

4
proves the claim.

\sigma_{i}^{2}\leftarrow\frac{\alpha\Delta^{2}}{2(\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{i-1})}
;

\tilde{s}_{i}\leftarrow f(X)+\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{i}^{2})
;

\hat{s}_{i}\leftarrow\frac{1}{\sum_{j=1}^{i}\frac{1}{\sigma_{j}^{2}}}\sum_{j=1}^{i}\frac{1}{\sigma_{j}^{2}}\tilde{s}_{j}
;

end for

return

\hat{s}_{1:K},\epsilon_{1:K}
;

Denote the
\hat{s}_{1:K}
outputs from
Algorithm

2
as

\hat{s}^{\mathrm{seq}}_{1:K}
and the outputs from
Algorithm

3
as

\hat{s}^{\mathrm{bro}}_{1:K}

In the Brownian mechanism
(
Whitehouse et al. 2022
)
,
\hat{s}^{\mathrm{bro}}_{1}|X\sim\mathcal{N}(f(X),T_{1})
.
When
T_{i}<T_{i-1}
,

\hat{s}^{\mathrm{bro}}_{i}|\hat{s}^{\mathrm{bro}}_{1},\dotsc,\hat{s}^{\mathrm{bro}}_{i-1},X\sim\mathcal{N}\left(f(X)+\frac{T_{i}}{T_{i-1}}(\hat{s}^{\mathrm{bro}}_{i-1}-f(X)),\frac{(T_{i-1}-T_{i})T_{i}}{T_{i-1}}\right).

(62)

We show that the outputs of Algorithm
2
have these same
conditional distributions.
Clearly
\hat{s}^{\mathrm{seq}}_{1}|X\sim\mathcal{N}(f(X),\sigma_{1}^{2})
. Since
\sigma_{1}^{2}=\frac{\alpha\Delta^{2}}{2\epsilon_{1}}=T_{1}
,

\hat{s}^{\mathrm{seq}}_{1}
has the same distribution as
\hat{s}^{\mathrm{bro}}_{1}
.

For
2\leq i\leq K
,

\begin{split}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{i}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\right)^{-1}=\left(\sum_{j=2}^{i}\frac{2(\epsilon_{j}-\epsilon_{j-1})}{\alpha\Delta^{2}}+\frac{2\epsilon_{1}}{\alpha\Delta^{2}}\right)^{-1}=\left(\frac{2\epsilon_{i}^{2}}{\alpha\Delta^{2}}\right)^{-1}=T_{i}\end{split}

(63)

and

\begin{split}\hat{s}^{\mathrm{seq}}_{i}&=T_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{i}\frac{1}{\sigma_{j}^{2}}\tilde{s}_{j}=T_{i}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\frac{1}{\sigma_{j}^{2}}\tilde{s}_{j}+\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\tilde{s}_{i}\right)=T_{i}\left(\frac{1}{T_{i-1}}\hat{s}^{\mathrm{seq}}_{i-1}+\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\tilde{s}_{i}\right).\end{split}

(64)

Since
\hat{s}^{\mathrm{seq}}_{i}
is a linear function of
\tilde{s}_{i}
, the distribution

\hat{s}^{\mathrm{seq}}_{i}|\hat{s}^{\mathrm{seq}}_{1},\dotsc,\hat{s}^{\mathrm{seq}}_{i-1},X
is
Gaussian. The mean is

\begin{split}\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}(\hat{s}^{\mathrm{seq}}_{i}|\hat{s}^{\mathrm{seq}}_{1},\dotsc,\hat{s}^{\mathrm{seq}}_{i-1},X)&=T_{i}\left(\frac{1}{T_{i-1}}\hat{s}^{\mathrm{seq}}_{i-1}+\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}(\tilde{s}_{i}|X)\right)\\
&=T_{i}\left(\frac{1}{T_{i-1}}\hat{s}^{\mathrm{seq}}_{i-1}+\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}f(X)\right)\\
&=\frac{T_{i}}{T_{i-1}}\hat{s}^{\mathrm{seq}}_{i-1}+\frac{T_{i}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}f(X)\\
&=\frac{T_{i}}{T_{i-1}}\hat{s}^{\mathrm{seq}}_{i-1}+\frac{\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{i-1}}{\epsilon_{i}}f(X)\\
&=f(X)+\frac{T_{i}}{T_{i-1}}\hat{s}^{\mathrm{seq}}_{i-1}-\frac{\epsilon_{i-1}}{\epsilon_{i}}f(X)\\
&=f(X)+\frac{T_{i}}{T_{i-1}}(\hat{s}^{\mathrm{seq}}_{i-1}-f(X))\end{split}

(65)

and the variance is

\begin{split}\Var(\hat{s}^{\mathrm{seq}}_{i}|\hat{s}^{\mathrm{seq}}_{1},\dotsc,\hat{s}^{\mathrm{seq}}_{i-1})&=T_{i}^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{4}}\Var(\tilde{s}_{i})=T_{i}^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}=T_{i}\cdot\frac{T_{i}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\\
&=T_{i}\cdot\frac{\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{i-1}}{\epsilon_{i}}=T_{i}\cdot\left(1-\frac{\epsilon_{i-1}}{\epsilon_{i}}\right)\\
&=T_{i}\cdot\frac{T_{i-1}-T_{i}}{T_{i-1}}.\end{split}

(66)

These match the Brownian mechanism.
This means that the output distributions of the Brownian mechanism and Algorithm
2

are identical.

Appendix D
Experiment Details and Extra Results

Synthetic Data Details

The summaries of real data we use are
marginal queries
, which count
how many rows of a discrete dataset have given values for
given variables. While marginal queries are very simple,
the Private-PGM algorithm
(
McKenna et al. 2019
)

is able to generate high quality synthetic data based on only
noisy values of marginal queries
(
Tao et al. 2021
;
Chen et al. 2025
)
. To choose the marginal queries,
we use the MST algorithm
(
McKenna et al. 2021
)
, to obtain an initial set,
and add the pairwise marginal queries between the classification
target and all other variables.

As we randomly split the data into train and validation sets,
we use
Section

4

to prove that our synthetic data generator is ex-post RDP.
Since we already know the Brownian mechanism is ex-post RDP,
we only need to ensure the accuracy check is RDP.
Both check mechanisms depend on the sensitivity of the accuracy,
which is
\Delta_{\mathrm{acc}}=1/n_{\mathrm{validation}}

under add-remove neighbourhood.

Plain Gaussian Details

The plain Gaussian mechanism simply releases the accuracies with
Gaussian noise. To account for multiple checks we divide the
privacy budget with the maximum number of checks, which is

m-1
if there are
m
possible
\epsilon
values.
This ensures that the composition of all checks
is
\epsilon_{\mathrm{check}}
-RDP. This means the noise variance is

\frac{\alpha\Delta_{\mathrm{acc}}^{2}(m-1)}{2\epsilon_{\mathrm{check}}}
.

SVT Details

For SVT, we use
Theorem 8, eq. (3) from
Zhu & Wang 2020
,
which requires us to add Gaussian noise to the threshold,
and Laplace noise to the accuracy values. We also examined two
other results from

Zhu & Wang 2020

that would be applicable, but found that they have too large overheads in privacy
cost to be useful in our setting.

Denote the variance of
the Gaussian by
\sigma_{1}^{2}
, and the variance of the Laplace by

\sigma_{2}^{2}
. Let
t\in(0,1)
be a variable controlling the variance split
such that the Gaussian mechanism has a
t
fraction of the total
variance, meaning

\sigma_{1}^{2}=t(\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}).

(67)

To make the Gaussian
\epsilon_{1}
-RDP for sensitivity

\Delta_{\mathrm{acc}}
and the Laplace

\epsilon_{2}
-DP for sensitivity
2\Delta_{\mathrm{acc}}
, as
required by the theorem, we need

\epsilon_{1}=\frac{\alpha\Delta_{\mathrm{acc}}^{2}}{2\sigma_{1}^{2}}\quad\quad\epsilon_{2}=\frac{2\sqrt{2}\Delta_{\mathrm{acc}}}{\sigma_{2}}.

(68)

The theorem requires

\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}=\epsilon_{\mathrm{check}}
.
Plugging in (
68
), the solution of

\sigma_{2}
from (
67
) and rearranging,
we obtain the quadratic equation

\epsilon_{\mathrm{check}}\sigma_{1}^{2}-\frac{2\sqrt{2}\Delta_{\mathrm{acc}}}{t^{\prime}}\sigma_{1}-\frac{\alpha\Delta_{\mathrm{acc}}^{2}}{2}=0

(69)

where
t^{\prime}=\sqrt{\frac{1-t}{t}}
.
The equation has a unique positive solution, which we set

\sigma_{1}
to. We then solve
\sigma_{2}
from
(
67
). Finally,
we numerically optimise
t
to minimise the total variance

\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}
.

SVT permits an arbitrary number of accuracy checks, unlike the
plain Gaussian check. However, SVT has a large overhead in the
noise variance with a small number of checks, so in our setting,
the plain Gaussian check requires less noise.

Dataset and Preprocessing

We use the UCI Adult dataset
(
Kohavi & Becker 1996
)
. We
remove the columns “fnlwgt” and “educational-num”, as the
first one is a weighting and the second contains the same
information as another column. We remove rows with missing values.
We discretise the “age”
column to 8 categories, with edges
10,20,\dotsc,90
,
and “hours-per-week” to 10 categories with edges

0,10,20,\dotsc,90
, including a category for
\geq 90
.
We discretise “capital-gain” and “capital-loss” to binary

proportions of both labels. The split is
fixed for all repeats of the experiment.

\pm
