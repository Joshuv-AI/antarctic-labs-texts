Determining Covering Array Numbers via Balanced Covering Arrays
Irene Hiess, Ludwig Kampel

Abstract
In this article we determine five previously unknown covering array numbers (CANs). We do so using properties of so called balanced covering arrays together with a computational result for these. The balance properties allow us to generalize the (computational) non-existence result for balanced covering arrays to covering arrays. Covering arrays are combinatorial designs that can be considered generalizations of orthogonal arrays, when dropping the restriction that the considered t t -tuples appear exactly λ \lambda times, and instead require them to appear at least λ \lambda times. While this generalization renders the existence of covering arrays trivial, it raises the question for their optimality, respectively the smallest number of rows, the CAN, for which a certain covering array exists. The CANs determined in this paper were tightly bound for decades, but remained ultimately unknown.

Applications of Covering Arrays

In recent years CAs have attracted much attention from practitioners due to their application in a branch of software testing, referred to as

,
where the rows of CAs give rise to software tests

The characteristic of CAs that every

[

Empirical studies suggest that in some cases the application of combinatorial testing based on CAs of strength

All these applications have in common, that the deployed tests are based on the rows of a CA.

Optimal Covering Arrays and Covering Array Numbers

The covering array number
\mathsf{CAN}_{\lambda}(t,k,v)
is defined as the minimal number
N
, such that a
\mathsf{CA}_{\lambda}(N;t,k,v)
exists.
Again, for
\lambda=1
we denote the covering array number (
CAN
for short) as
\mathsf{CAN}(t,k,v)
.
A CA with a minimal number of rows is called
optimal
.
Similarly to CAN, for given
N,t,v
the maximal number of columns
k
where a
\mathsf{CA}(N;t,k,v)
exists is denoted as
\mathsf{CAK}(N;t,v)
.
These two numbers are clearly connected via the relations
\mathsf{CAN}(t,k,v)=\min\{N:\mathsf{CAK}(N;t,v)\geq k\}
and

\mathsf{CAK}(N;t,v)=\max\{k:\mathsf{CAN}(t,k,v)\leq N\}
, see also
[
25
]
or
[
8
]
.
It is worth to mention that, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, all known CANs have been established in a constructive manner,
in the sense that a construction or computation of an optimal CA was used to establish them.
In this sense, as of this writing it is tantamount to determine an
optimal CA
and to determine a
CAN
.

In general, the problem of finding optimal CAs remains unsolved, however, there are some special cases where they are known.
As such, optimal CAs
\mathsf{CA}(N;2,k,2)
are known for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
due to a connection to maximal anti-chains and sperner-type theorems,
examined by Katona
[
26
]
as well as Kleitman and Spencer
[
7
]
.
Therefore also the value of
CAN(2,k,2)
is known for
k\geq 2
to be

\mathsf{CAN}(2,k,2)=\min_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\left\{k\leq\binom{N-1}{\left\lceil\frac{N}{2}\right\rceil}\right\}.

(1)

Further, some optimal CAs are known as they coincide with orthogonal arrays of index one (in general we have
\mathsf{OA}(\lambda v^{t},k,v,t)=\mathsf{CA}_{\lambda}(\lambda v^{t};t,k,v)
), which can for example be constructed using Bush’s construction
[
27
]
.
An introduction to orthogonal arrays, including information on their existence and their properties, is given in
[
1
]
, where we refer the interested reader for more information.
Aside from these theoretical constructions that are based on connections to other structures appearing in discrete mathematics, which typically yield a family of optimal CAs,
only a few other optimal CAs are known.
Some optimal CAs have been determined based on others by making use of bounds on CAN,
such as the elementary bound

\mathsf{CAN}(t-1,k-1,v)\leq\frac{1}{v}\mathsf{CAN}(t,k,v),

(2)

which is also mentioned in
[
14
]
, and is frequently used in other works to establish new CAN values, or to strengthen bounds on CAN as in
[
28
]
.
However, new results on optimal CAs are mostly established with the aid of computational methods, which we will review in the next section.

Contribution

In this article, the method of combining theoretical properties of
balanced covering arrays
, revisited in Section
3
, with a result from computational classification is used for the first time to establish new CANs.
Using this method, we show the lower bounds
18\leq\mathsf{CAN}(3,k,2)
for
k\in{17,18,19,20}
and
36\leq\mathsf{CAN}(4,18,2)
,
which determine the respective CANs and establish optimality of five (previously known) covering arrays.

Related Work

Classification of CAs, i.e. enumeration of non-equivalent CAs, is a useful technique to establish non-existence results.
Known equivalence actions of CAs, i.e. actions that preserve their defining properties, are permutations of columns,
permutations of rows and permutations of the symbols of a column.
See
[
29
]
for more details.
Classification of CAs has been used in several of the following works related to optimal CAs.

Upper bounds
\mathsf{CAN}(t,k,v)\leq N
are usually shown by generating a
\mathsf{CA}(N;t,k,v)
, while lower bounds on CANs are often established computationally by complete or exhaustive search algorithms.
An overview of upper and lower bounds on CAN for CAs with up to
k=14
columns is given by Colbourn et al.
[
28
]
.
The authors derived these bounds using various techniques, ranging from theoretical results and combinatorial constructions to exhaustive search and metaheuristic search techniques.
Additionally, the authors present classification results whenever feasible.
However, since their work
[
28
]
was published some additional CANs and bounds on CANs have been determined.

Chateauneuf, Colbourn and Kreher
[
30
]
show
\mathsf{CAN}(3,6,3)=33
by using a combination of a computational result, a theoretical bound and a combinatorial construction, as follows.
The bound
\mathsf{CAN}(2,5,3)\geq 11
is a computational result determined with linear integer programming that was reported in
[
3
]
.
Combining this with inequality (
2
) yields
33\leq\mathsf{CAN}(3,6,3)
.
Further, the mathematical construction presented in
[
30

allows to derive a
\mathsf{CA}(33;3,6,3)

\mathsf{CAN}(3,6,3)=33

The work of Hnich et al.
[

demonstrates how CAN values or bounds thereof can be determined computationally.
They present a constraint satisfaction problem (CSP) formulation for CA generation, which, together with a complete CSP solver, allows determining the existence of a CA for given parameters
N,t,k
and

.
Using the presented CSP formulation together with a complete CSP solver, optimality of nine CAs was shown,

Izquierdo-Marquez and Torres-Jimenez

present a classification algorithm where for a given number of rows

,

For this purpose, an array is extended with additional columns, in combination with backtracking when the array cannot be further extended.
Using the computational results from this algorithm they present five new CANs.
In an extension of their work the same authors derive additional twelve new CANs with an improved version of their algorithm
[
33
]
.
Additionally, based on the computational result
\mathsf{CAN}(2,13,3)=15
and inequality (

, i.e.
\mathsf{CAN}(3,14,3)=45
.

The same authors propose further a different type of classification algorithm in

]
, where
they use the construction underlying the proof of the inequality (

In this approach all non-equivalent CAs

In combination with inequality (

The authors report on determining 10 new CANs.

Bounds on

In 2006 Colbourn et al.

More recent works, such as Colbourn et al. 2010

Revisiting Balanced Covering Arrays

In
[
29
]

balanced CAs
were introduced as intersections of classes of covering arrays (CAs) and packing arrays (PAs).
Such balanced CAs obey to upper and lower bounds regarding the appearance of tuples in subarrays.
More detailed, balanced CAs are defined as follows:

Definition 1

(Balanced CA,
[
29
]
)
.

A
\mathsf{CA}(N;t,k,v)
is called
(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\textbf{{y}})
-balanced with
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{t})\in\mathbb{N}^{t}
and
\textbf{{y}}=(\textit{y}_{1},\ldots,\textit{y}_{t})\in\mathbb{N}^{t}
if and only if
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,t\}
in each
N\times i
subarray, each
i
-tuple over the alphabet of size
v
appears at least
\lambda_{i}
and at most
\textit{y}_{i}
times as a row of the subarray.
Such an array is denoted as
\mathsf{CA}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\textbf{{y}}}(N;t,k,v)
.

Further, the maximal number of columns for which a
(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\textbf{{y}})
-balanced CA exists is defined as

\mathsf{CAK}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\textbf{{y}}}(N;t,v):=\max\{k\in\mathbb{N}:\exists\mathsf{CA}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\textbf{{y}}}(N;t,k,v)\}
.

The work in
[
29
]
presents classification results for balanced CAs
which yield values of
\mathsf{CAK}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{\textbf{{y}}}(N;t,v)
for several parameters
N,t
and
v
.
These results are obtained by means of an exhaustive search algorithm that makes use of SAT solving or a pseudo-Boolean
solver.
Given a number of rows
N
and balance vectors
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
and
y
,
the proposed algorithm starts with an empty matrix and uses column extension
with backtracking to generate
(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\textbf{{y}})
-balanced CAs.
A lex-leader ordering is used to identify a unique representative of each class of non-equivalent balanced CAs.
To prevent from performing redundant searches, several inequalities or bounds on the balance vectors
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
and
y
are used.
For more details we kindly refer the interested reader to
[
29
]
.

In the following we repeat some of the inequalities from
[
29
, Corollary 1]
that are relevant for this work.
In addition to the obvious relation
\lambda_{t}\geq 1
,
when analyzing balanced CAs it is sufficient to consider balance vectors
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{t})
and
\textbf{{y}}=(\textit{y}_{1},\ldots,\textit{y}_{t})
satisfying the bounds given below:

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle v\cdot\lambda_{i+1},\text{ for }1\leq i<t,

(3)

\displaystyle\textit{y}_{i+1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\textit{y}_{i}-(v-1)\lambda_{i+1},\text{ for }1\leq i<t,

(4)

\displaystyle\textit{y}_{i}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle N-(v^{i}-1)\lambda_{i},\text{ for }1\leq i\leq t.

(5)

Additionally, for CAs with at least
k
columns, the bound

\lambda_{i}\geq\mathsf{CAN}_{\lambda_{t}}(t-i,k-i,v),\text{ for }1\leq i<t

(6)

can be used, which is in many cases stronger than the bound given by inequality (
3
). For the proof of these bounds we refer to
[
29
]
.

The non-existence of a
\mathsf{CA}(17;3,17,2)

Although in theory the classification algorithm we presented in
[
29
]
is capable of classifying all CAs, in practice this is not possible due to extensive runtimes. For example, we were not able to compute a full classification of balanced CAs

for all possible balance vectors
\boldsymbol{\lambda},\textbf{{y}}

[

]
we executed an additional computation for
\mathsf{CA}_{{}_{(8,2,1)}}^{{}^{(9,7,6)}}(17;3,k,2)
without a time limit.
This (single-threaded) computation, which is only a part of the one required for computationally classifying all
\mathsf{CA}(17;3,k,2)
, finished only after about
12284
hours, almost
512
days.
The results of this computation show that three non-equivalent such balanced CAs with

columns exist and further that there is no
\mathsf{CA}_{{}_{(8,2,1)}}^{{}^{(9,7,6)}}(17;3,k,2)

]
allow to generalize this non-existence result from balanced CAs with

.

\mathsf{CAK}(17;3,2)=16
.

As mentioned above,

\mathsf{CAK}_{{}_{(8,2,1)}}^{{}^{(9,7,6)}}(17;3,2)=16

for balance vectors
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
,
y
that do not impose any balance constraints on the covering

) we get
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\geq(4,2,1)

Conclusion and Outlook

In this work we showed how balance properties of CAs can be used to establish lower bounds on CANs which subsequently give optimality results for five CAs.
