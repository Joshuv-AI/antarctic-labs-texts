A case of oligodendroglioma in a sea otter (Enhydra lutris)
Authors: Chiho KEZUKA, Hisaki TOKUNO, Mizuki KURAMOCHI, Momoyo MURAMOTO, Keisuke FURUTA, Hideo AKIYOSHI, Miyuu TANAKA, Takeshi IZAWA, Mitsuru KUWAMURA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 19-year-old male sea otter (Enhydra lutris) exhibited systemic seizures, declining consciousness, temporary cardiopulmonary arrest, and right-side rotation. The sea otter died approximately one month after its initial seizure. Postmortem magnetic resonance imaging examination revealed a high-intensity region in the left frontal lobe. At necropsy, a gelatinous lesion in the left frontal lobe of the brain was revealed. Histopathologically, the lesion consisted of neoplastic proliferation of round to polyhedral monomorphic cells with a honeycomb growth pattern, small round nuclei, and cytoplasmic halos. The neoplastic cells were immunopositive for oligodendrocyte transcriptional factor-2 (OLIG2) and immunonegative for glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP). In this study, we describe the clinical signs and histopathological features of oligodendroglioma, which have rarely been reported in sea otters.Keywords: oligodendrocyte transcriptional factor-2, oligodendroglioma, sea otter, seizure

Sea otters (Enhydra lutris) are the smallest marine mammals and are listed as endangered species. While wild sea otters are frequently infected with protozoal meningoencephalitis, captive sea otters have a higher risk of neoplastic diseases because of their long lifespan; some intracranial tumors have been reported [10, 13, 18]. Some neoplastic cases in the central nervous system (CNS) have been previously reported in sea otters [14], including a cerebral T-cell lymphoma [17], a fibrosarcoma [2], and an oligodendroglioma [10, 13]. Oligodendroglioma is one of the most common glial cell tumors in domestic animals. However, only one case has been reported in sea otters, and its histological and immunohistochemical features were poorly described [10, 13]. In this study, we report the clinical signs and histopathological features of an oligodendroglioma in a captive sea otter.

A 19-year-old male sea otter, raised in Kobe Suma Aqualife Park, showed systemic seizures three times in a month. The sea otter did not contact the outside environment and the other animal species. All seizures were observed at the time of awakening, and the duration was approximately 5 min. The seizures were systemic and characterized by declining consciousness and temporary cardiopulmonary arrest. Because no clinical exanimation was performed and no other noticeable symptoms were observed, an anti-seizure drug Zonisamide was administered (3 mg/kg, twice daily) as a symptomatic treatment to prevent drowning. After starting the medication, its appetite gradually diminished. The sea otter died approximately one month after the first seizure. Necropsy was performed on the same day in Kobe Suma Aqualife Park and postmortem magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and histopathological examination were performed at the Veterinary Medical Center and the Laboratory of Veterinary Pathology, Osaka Prefecture University, respectively. MRI examination revealed a high-intensity legion in the left frontal lobe (Fig. 1A, asterisk). Additionally, fluid accumulation was observed in the left subventricular region, which spread to the left inner ear (Fig. 1B, yellow line). No other changes were noted at necropsy, except for severe emaciation.

Magnetic resonance imaging findings on horizontal (A) and sagittal (B) sections, T2-weight. A high-signal intensity is observed in the left frontal lobe (A, asterisk). A high-signal legion is observed in the subventricular region (B, yellow line).

Tissue samples were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin, processed routinely, and embedded in paraffin wax. Histological sections were cut at 3 μm and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE). The sections were subjected to immunohistochemistry (IHC) using primary antibodies for glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP; clone GA5, Cell Signaling Technology, Denver, MA, USA,1:1,500) and oligodendrocyte transcriptional factor-2 (OLIG2; clone 211F1.1, Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany, 1:200). Non-neoplasic brain tissue from the present case was used as an internal positive control. After dewaxing, antigen retrieval treatment by autoclaving with citrate buffer was performed for OLIG2. Then, the tissue sections were processed using a HistostainerTM (Nichirei Biosciences, Tokyo, Japan). The sections were treated with 5% skimmed milk in phosphate-buffered saline for 15 min and incubated with each primary antibody for 1 hr at room temperature. After incubation in 3% H2O2 for 15 min, horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody (Histofine Simple Stain MAX PO®; Nichirei Biosciences) was applied for 1 hr. Positive reactions were visualized with 3,3’- diaminobenzidine (DAB substrate kit; Nichirei Biosciences). The sections were lightly counterstained with hematoxylin. The percentage of immunopositive cells was semi-quantitatively evaluated in 5 high-power fields (HPF, equal to 0.237 mm2) by a JCVP pathologist (MK). Normal brain tissue of the present case was used as an internal positive control.

After formalin fixation, a light brown to white-gray, gelatine-like lesion was observed in the caudate nucleus around the left lateral ventricle (Fig. 2A, asterisk). Histopathological examination revealed diffuse sheets, exhibiting a honeycomb appearance, of round to polyhedral monomorphic cells with a small amount of eosinophilic cytoplasm and round nuclei (Fig. 2B). The cellular and nuclear atypia were not prominent. Mitotic figures were rarely observed (0 mitotic figures in 10 HPFs). Invasion into surrounding parenchyma, edema, hemorrhage, eosinophilic mucinous matrix and angiogenesis were observed in the surrounding cerebral parenchyma. Approximately 80% of the neoplastic cells were positive for OLIG2 (Fig. 2C), while most of them were negative for GFAP (Fig. 2D). A small number of GFAP-positive cells were pre-existing astrocytes (Fig. 2D). Thus, the present case was diagnosed as oligodendroglioma according to the classification of brain tumors in domestic animals [7].

The present case showed severe emaciation, likely caused by brain tumor-related seizure and a reduced appetite. In humans and dogs, oligodendrogliomas commonly arise in the frontal lobe and occasionally induce brain tumor-related seizure [5, 12, 15]. Brain tumor-related seizure results from physical compression, hypoxia, and tumor-induced inflammation, and has negative impacts on cognitive functions [5, 19]. In addition to seizures, the side effects of zonisamide might have contributed to the loss of appetite [1, 6, 16]. Sea otters require large amounts of energy to maintain a stable core body temperature in cold water. The standard metabolic rate of sea otters is reported to be ​2.4 times higher than that of terrestrial mammals of equal size [3, 20]. The primary cause of death in this case was severe emaciation secondary to loss of appetite, which was associated with oligodendroglioma and zonisamide administration.

Histopathologically, the neoplastic cells of the present case showed honeycomb growth pattern with OLIG2 immunopositivity, similar to human and canine oligodendrogliomas [8, 9]. Immunohistochemical glial markers were shown to work in the brain tissue of sea otters. Oligodendroglioma is the most common glial cell tumor that typically arises in the frontal lobes of middle-aged to older dogs [7]. Low-grade oligodendroglioma shows a honeycomb structure pattern in which the neoplastic cells have a small amount of cytoplasm, small round nuclei and perinuclear haloes [7]. To the best of our knowledge, only one case of oligodendroglioma has been reported in the sea otter [10, 13]. Further studies are necessary to characterize the histological features of oligodendrogliomas in sea otters.

Based on MRI findings, encephalitis was also suspected to be a differential diagnosis. T2-weighted hyperintensity is an essential marker for various disorders including inflammation, brain tumors, and edema [4]. In wild sea otters, the most common cause of the CNS dysfunction is protozoal meningoencephalitis caused by Sarcocystis neurona and Toxoplasma gondii; S. neurona infection frequently induces brainstem lesions associated with neurological signs such as ataxia, intention tremor, and seizures [18]. In the present case, the hyperintensity observed on MRI was considered to reflect peritumoral edema or congestion of the cerebrospinal fluid due to the presence of oligodendroglioma [11]. Moreover, the present case was kept only in housing conditions and the seizure was systemic. Together with the histopathological findings, these protozoal diseases were ruled out.

Here we describe the neurological symptoms and histopathological features of oligodendroglioma in a sea otter, which are similar to those of human and canine oligodendrogliomas. The present case may have died from emaciation due to brain tumor-related seizure and loss of appetite. The available information on brain tumors and the CNS diseases in aged sea otters is limited. Further case accumulation and studies on sea otter the CNS diseases are necessary in the future.
