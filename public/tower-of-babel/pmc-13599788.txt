The “Dark Side” of Pre-Retirement Work Attachment for Later-Life Mental Health: Mediating Role of Retirement Adjustment and Moderating Role of Social Network
Authors: Lecheng Chen, Xiaolei Li, Jialing Fan, Yiru Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWith rapid population aging and extended post-retirement lifespan, understanding the modifiable psychological and social processes that shape retirees’ mental health has become increasingly important for behavioral health management. However, the mechanisms linking pre-retirement work attachment and retirement adjustment to post-retirement mental health remain poorly understood.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 302 Chinese community-dwelling retirees and assessed work attachments (work-family centrality [WFC] and affective organizational commitment [AOC]), retirement adjustment (enjoyment of retirement, loss of retirement, and nostalgia for work), social network, leisure activity, and mental health (depression and life satisfaction). Hierarchical multiple regression was used to examine factors associated with retirement adjustment. Moderated mediation models were then applied to test whether social network and leisure activity moderated the mediating role of retirement adjustment between work attachments and mental health.ResultsRetirement adjustment was multidimensional. Loss of retirement was associated only with socioeconomic factors; nostalgia for work was associated only with WFC and AOC; and enjoyment of retirement was related to demographic factors, socioeconomic factors, and WFC. Higher work attachments were associated with lower enjoyment of retirement, which in turn was associated with more severe depressive symptoms and lower life satisfaction. The mediating effect of enjoyment of retirement was significant only among retirees with low or moderate levels of social network, but not among those with a high level of social network.ConclusionThis exploratory study identified distinct factors for each retirement adjustment dimension and showed that higher pre-retirement work attachments are associated with poorer later-life mental health through diminished enjoyment of retirement. A rich social network buffers this negative relationship, suggesting that strengthening social connections is a promising behavioral target for psychological interventions aimed at promoting mental health in later life. Given that the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences, future longitudinal research is needed to confirm these pathways.

Introduction

In recent decades, China has experienced rapid population aging.1 By 2050, the proportion of individuals aged 65 years and above is projected to reach 30.92%.2 The declining birth rate and increasing old-age dependency ratio further exacerbate this demographic shift, imposing substantial financial and social burdens.3 In China, aging burdens increase further as the second baby boomers (those born between 1962–1975) start to enter retirement in 2022.1 In response to this challenge, China introduced a policy to gradually delay the retirement age in 2025.4 This policy has brought renewed attention to retirement as a critical life transition that affects mental health in later life.5 In Chinese culture, work carries particular significance due to collectivist values and familial obligations, resulting in new retirees facing a dramatic shift in identity.6 Given the rising life expectancy in rapidly aging societies, where older adults may spend 30 years or more in retirement, understanding the modifiable psychological and social processes that shape post-retirement mental health has become increasingly important for behavioral health management.1 Against this backdrop, investigating the determinants of retirement adjustment and their implications for mental health is of growing significance for developing evidence-based interventions to promote well-being in later life.

Transitioning from a relatively busy and structured daily routine to retirement represents a challenge for many individuals. Retirement adjustment is defined as the process of adapting to altered life circumstances following retirement.7 Previous research has conceptualized this adjustment as a multidimensional process encompassing several key dimensions, such as enjoyment of retirement, loss of retirement, and nostalgia for work.8 Role theory emphasizes retirement as a role transition that may weaken or eliminate work roles while strengthening family and community roles.9 Difficulties in adjusting to the loss of work roles may increase depressive symptoms and reduce life satisfaction.10,11 Poor mental health, such as long-term depressive symptoms, is even associated with premature mortality.12 Although the transition phase has received considerable attention, far less is known about the mediating role of retirement adjustment between work attachments and mental health, and the mechanisms that moderate the influence of retirement adjustment on subsequent mental health.

Retirement adjustment is influenced by multiple factors.13 Studies have shown that individual attributes, family functioning, socioeconomic context, and work-related factors are associated with retirement adjustment.14,15 The life course perspective suggests that past experience carries significant implications for present and future well-being.16 Previous research within this framework supports the idea that working histories remain instrumental in shaping how individuals experience retirement adjustment.17 The impact of work attachment may be bidirectional. According to conservation of resources (COR) theory,18 individuals with sufficient resources are better equipped to meet their needs in retirement and experience more favorable outcomes than those with fewer resources. Strong work attachment has been associated with higher work engagement and job performance, facilitating the acquisition and maintenance of resources during employment.19 Consequently, such individuals may have accumulated sufficient resources over their careers and may therefore experience fewer adjustment problems in retirement.13 Yet role theory9 points to a potential “dark side” of work attachments: retirees who were highly attached to their jobs may have had fewer opportunities to invest in alternative roles. When work becomes central to one’s identity, the loss of workplace-based roles can cause identity disruption and hinder retirement adjustment.20 Given that work attachment serves as both a resource and a potential risk factor, two forms of pre-retirement work attachments warrant particular attention. The first is work-family centrality (WFC), which refers to the importance of work in an individual’s life relative to other life domains.21 The second is affective organizational commitment (AOC), defined as emotional attachment to one’s workplace.22 While WFC reflects the cognitive salience of work as a life domain, AOC captures emotional bonds with a specific organization.23 Collectively, these two forms of work attachment shape individuals’ psychological experience during retirement adjustment, and are likely to influence retirees’ capacity to derive enjoyment from post-retirement life, as well as their overall mental health.24,25

Beyond the resources and identity conflicts associated with work attachment, post-retirement social network and leisure activity represent important modifiable resources that may compensate for retirement-related losses.10,26 According to the selective optimization with compensation (SOC) theory,27 although older adults face increasing losses across multiple life domains, they can actively compensate for these losses through adaptive strategies. Retirement alters the social landscape of later life: on the one hand, it reduces structured opportunities for work-based social interaction and role fulfillment;28 on the other hand, it increases discretionary time for cultivating social connections and participating in leisure activities, which in turn may enhance social capital.29,30 Previous studies have confirmed that both of these compensatory resources are associated with higher life satisfaction and a lower likelihood of depressive symptoms.10,26 However, few studies have investigated whether these resources can buffer the adverse effects of poor retirement adjustment on mental health.

Despite significant advances, several critical gaps remain. First, previous studies have examined factors influencing retirement adjustment in isolation, leaving the joint effects of multiple resources largely unexplored,13–15 and whether work attachments influence distinct dimensions of retirement adjustment has not been examined. Second, prior evidence has linked work-related factors to retirement adjustment,23 but the mediating pathway from pre-retirement work attachments through retirement adjustment to post-retirement mental health remains unexamined. Third, the moderating roles of social network and leisure activity are poorly understood,26,30 particularly whether they can buffer the adverse effects of poor retirement adjustment on mental health. To address these gaps, the present study employs hierarchical multiple regression and moderated mediation models based on data collected from Chinese community-dwelling retirees. Specifically, this study aims to: (1) examine the predictive effects of demographic and socioeconomic variables, and work attachments on distinct dimensions of retirement adjustment; (2) investigate the mediating role of retirement adjustment between work attachments (WFC and AOC) and post-retirement mental health (depression and life satisfaction); and (3) test whether social network and leisure activity moderate the mediating relationships between retirement adjustment and mental health. Accordingly, this study proposes the following hypotheses (Figure 1): H1: Work attachments will be associated with retirement adjustment; H2: Retirement adjustment will mediate the relationship between work attachments and mental health; and H3: Social network and leisure activity will moderate the relationship between retirement adjustment and mental health. Materials and Methods

Participants

This cross-sectional study recruited participants from the Cohort for Aging Resilience Exploration of Shandong University (SDU-CARE), an ongoing cohort study investigating aging and resilience in community-dwelling middle-aged and older adults. Participants were recruited through convenience sampling from three communities in Jinan, Shandong Province, China, between July 2025 and January 2026. The inclusion criteria were: (1) aged 55 years or older; (2) formally retired for at least 24 months to ensure that the participants had fully experienced the initial retirement transition period. Retirement was defined as a permanent exit from the full-time labor market, based on two criteria: (a) a substantial reduction in working hours (fewer than 20 hours per week) and (b) reliance on pension benefits as the main source of income.31 The exclusion criteria included severe hearing or visual impairments, as well as clinically diagnosed severe cognitive and mental disorders (eg, schizophrenia and Alzheimer’s disease) that could affect questionnaire completion. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the School of Nursing and Rehabilitation, Shandong University. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

We used G*Power 3.1 to estimate the required sample size. Employing linear regression as the statistical test, with a medium effect size (f2=0.15), an α level of 0.05, a statistical power of 0.80, and a maximum of eleven predictors, the analysis indicated that 123 participants would be needed.32 According to established simulation guidelines, a sample size of 200–230 is sufficient to detect moderate mediation effects with adequate power.33

Measurements

Sociodemographic Information

Sociodemographic information included age, gender, level of education, retirement duration, number of family laborers, self-rated economic status, and occupational prestige. Level of education was measured by years of formal schooling. Retirement duration was calculated as the number of years elapsed from the official retirement date to the survey date. The number of family laborers was quantified as the total number of economically active individuals within the household. Self-rated economic status was assessed on a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating greater satisfaction with personal financial status. Occupational prestige was measured using the Standard International Occupational Prestige Scale (SIOPS).34 Based on each participant’s longest-held job before retirement, two independent coders assigned a 5-point Likert score to each occupation, with higher scores representing higher occupational prestige.

Retirement Adjustment

Retirement adjustment was assessed using the Chinese version of the Retirement Adjustment Questionnaire (RAQ),8 which consists of 12 items covering three dimensions: enjoyment of retirement (ER), loss of retirement (LR), and nostalgia for work (NW). Representative items include: “I enjoy the state of retirement” (ER), “People respect me less after retirement” (LR), and “I miss the feeling of working” (NW). Responses were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with higher scores reflecting greater intensity of the corresponding affective response.35,36 The RAQ has demonstrated good cross-cultural reliability and validity.8 In this study, Cronbach’s α was 0.75 for enjoyment of retirement, 0.72 for loss of retirement, and 0.95 for nostalgia for work.

Work-Family Centrality

WFC was measured using a 5-item scale developed by Carr,37 with a representative item being “The greatest satisfaction in my life comes from my work”. In this study, all items were designed to assess participants’ perceptions of their longest-tenured job before retirement. To minimize recall bias, the researchers confirmed with participants that the job was indeed their longest-tenured position. Consistent with prior work,8,38 the original 5-point Likert scale was modified to a dichotomous format (yes=1, no=0), considering that older adults may have difficulty distinguishing between multiple response categories. Total scores range from 0 to 5, with higher scores indicating stronger WFC. The original scale yielded a Cronbach’s α of 0.94,39 and the KR-20 coefficient in this study was 0.95.

Affective Organizational Commitment

AOC was assessed using the 6-item scale developed by Meyer and Allen,40 with a representative item being “I have an emotional attachment to my organization”. This instrument is a well-established measure of organizational commitment. Consistent with the WFC measurement,8,38 all items referred to participants’ longest-tenured job before retirement, and the original 5-point Likert scale was simplified to a dichotomous format (yes=1, no=0). Total scores range from 0 to 6, with higher scores indicating stronger AOC. The original scale yielded a Cronbach’s α of 0.90,41 and the KR-20 coefficient in this study was 0.94.

Social Network

Social network was measured using the Lubben Social Network Scale-Short Form (LSNS-6).42 The scale has a two-dimensional structure, including a family network subscale (3 items) and a friend network subscale (3 items). Participants were asked to report the number of relatives and friends they could see, talk to about private matters, and call on for help. Each item was scored on a 6-point scale (1=none, 2=one, 3=two, 4=three or four, 5=five to eight, 6=nine or more), with total scores ranging from 6 to 36. Higher scores indicate a larger and more supportive social network. This scale has been well validated in Chinese older adults,43 with a Cronbach’s α of 0.77 in this study.

Leisure Activity

Leisure activity was measured using a scale reflecting common activities among older Chinese adults.44 Leisure activities were defined as activities engaged in primarily for enjoyment, including reading, writing, playing chess, handicrafts, physical exercise, and other similar pursuits. Participants rated their frequency of participation in each activity on a 5-point scale (0=never, 1=occasionally, 2=several times per month, 3=several times per week, 4=every day). Higher total scores indicate greater engagement in leisure activities.

Depression

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the 15-item Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS-15).45 Each item requires a dichotomous response (yes=1, no=0). Total scores range from 0 to 15, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms. The GDS-15 has been translated into multiple languages and validated as a reliable tool for depression assessment. The Chinese version has demonstrated good reliability and validity,46 with a Cronbach’s α of 0.73 in the present study.

Life Satisfaction

Life satisfaction was measured using the Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS-5).47 It comprises 5 items, such as “The conditions of my life are excellent”. Participants rated their agreement with each statement on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Total scores range from 5 to 35, with higher scores reflecting greater life satisfaction. The scale has been widely used and shows excellent psychometric properties,48 with a Cronbach’s α of 0.88 in the present study.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 27.0 and Python 3. Multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) was employed to handle missing data. Five imputed datasets were generated, and results were pooled following Rubin’s rules.49 To verify the robustness of the results, after excluding participants with any missing values, sensitivity analyses were conducted by repeating all analyses on the complete-case dataset. Continuous variables were presented as means ± standard deviations (SDs), and categorical variables were summarized as frequencies (n) and percentages (%). Independent samples t-tests for continuous variables and Mann–Whitney U-tests for ordinal variables were used to examine gender differences in sample characteristics. Harman’s single-factor test was conducted to assess common method bias, and variance inflation factors (VIF) were calculated to test for multicollinearity. Independence of residuals, homoscedasticity, and normality were assessed to verify the assumptions of hierarchical regression. Three-step hierarchical multiple regression was performed to explore predictors of the three retirement adjustment dimensions, with demographic variables (age, gender, retirement duration, and level of education) entered in Model 1, socioeconomic factors (number of family laborers, occupational prestige, and self-rated economic status) added in Model 2, and work attachments (WFC and AOC) further included in Model 3.

Subsequent analyses tested the mediating role of retirement adjustment between work attachments (WFC, AOC) and mental health (depression, life satisfaction), as well as the potential moderating effects of social network and leisure activity. Age, gender, level of education, retirement duration, number of family laborers, occupational prestige and self-rated economic status were included as control variables. Variables were centered to reduce multicollinearity and enhance interpretability. The bootstrap method with 5000 resamples was used to construct 95% bias-corrected confidence intervals (CIs). An effect was considered significant if the 95% CI did not include zero. In all analyses, p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Sample Characteristics

A total of 302 community-dwelling retired adults were enrolled, including 205 females (67.88%) and 97 males (32.12%). Among them, 17 participants had missing values. The proportion of missing data ranged from 0% to 1.30% across variables, with an overall missing rate of 0.37%. Little’s MCAR test indicated a random missing pattern (χ2=117.798, df=133, p=0.824). Harman’s single-factor test showed that the first unrotated factor accounted for only 10.68% of the total variance, suggesting that common method bias was not a serious concern. Sample characteristics for the total sample and by gender are presented in Table 1. The mean age of the total sample was 70.73±6.10 years; the mean retirement duration was 18.21±8.45 years, with women having significantly longer retirement duration than men (t=−7.300, p<0.001). The mean years of formal schooling was 9.44±3.42, and the mean number of family laborers was 0.97±1.24. The majority had low (44.04%) or medium (34.11%) occupational prestige, and 72.85% of participants reported being satisfied or strongly satisfied with their economic status. Men reported significantly higher levels of work attachments than women, with significant differences observed for both WFC (t=3.858, p<0.001) and AOC (t=3.495, p<0.001). Results of Hierarchical Regression Analyses Predicting Retirement Adjustment

Hierarchical regression assumptions were evaluated prior to primary analyses. In all regression models predicting the three dimensions of retirement adjustment, VIF values for all variables were below 2.48, indicating no multicollinearity concerns. Except that the homoscedasticity assumption for nostalgia for work was not met, the other assumptions of residual independence and normality were satisfied across the three dimensions of retirement adjustment.

The results of hierarchical regression analyses are shown in Table 2. For enjoyment of retirement, Model 1 revealed that shorter retirement duration (β=−0.223, p=0.012) and higher education level (β=0.144, p=0.012) were associated with greater enjoyment. The inclusion of socioeconomic variables in Model 2 explained a small but significant increment in variance (ΔR2=0.022), with self-rated economic status emerging as a positive predictor (β=0.144, p=0.013). Model 3, which further included work attachments, significantly improved the model explanatory power (ΔR2=0.071), with WFC demonstrating the strongest negative association (β=−0.254, p<0.001). For loss of retirement, Model 1 showed no significant associations. The addition of socioeconomic factors in Model 2 yielded a substantial increase in explained variance (ΔR2=0.100). Lower self-rated economic status (β=−0.250, p<0.001), lower occupational prestige (β=−0.119, p=0.036) and a greater number of family laborers (β=0.134, p=0.016) were significantly linked with higher loss of retirement. Model 3 showed a slight improvement in model fit, but no work attachment variables reached significance. For nostalgia for work, only Model 3 yielded a significant increase in explained variance (ΔR2=0.128). Both WFC (β=0.195, p=0.002) and AOC (β=0.255, p<0.001) emerged as significant positive predictors. Considering that the nostalgia for work did not meet the homoscedasticity assumption, robust standard errors were employed to address heteroscedasticity, and the results remained consistent. Results of the Moderated Mediation Model

As shown in Table 3, the four moderated mediation models with enjoyment of retirement as the mediator and social network as the moderator were all statistically significant. In the first two models, WFC was negatively associated with enjoyment of retirement (a=−0.384, p<0.001, 95% CI [−0.541, −0.227]), which in turn was negatively associated with depression (b1=−0.135, p<0.001, 95% CI [−0.214, −0.056]) and positively associated with life satisfaction (b1=0.480, p<0.001, 95% CI [0.309, 0.652]). The direct effects of WFC on depression and life satisfaction were not significant. The moderated mediation indices with social network as the moderator were significant for both depression (effect=−0.011, 95% CI [−0.021, −0.004]) and life satisfaction (effect=0.029, 95% CI [0.012, 0.046]). The conditional indirect effects demonstrated that the mediating role of enjoyment of retirement was moderated by social network, with significant indirect effects emerging only at low and moderate levels (Supplementary Figure 1A and B). The interaction between enjoyment of retirement and social network was significant for both depression and life satisfaction, accounting for an additional 5.83% and 6.83% of the variance (ΔR2=0.0583 and 0.0683, both p<0.001). All significant associations remained significant with comparable effect sizes (Supplementary Tables 4 and 5), indicating that our findings were robust and not influenced by missing data.

Discussion

This exploratory study examined the psychological mechanisms linking pre-retirement work attachments and retirement adjustment to post-retirement mental health among Chinese retirees. Retirement is a multidimensional process: loss of retirement was associated only with socioeconomic factors; nostalgia for work was associated only with work attachments; and enjoyment of retirement was associated with demographic factors, socioeconomic factors, and work attachments. Stronger work attachments were associated with lower mental health in later life through diminished enjoyment of retirement, whereas social network acts as a critical compensatory resource that buffered the adverse effects of poor retirement adjustment.

Retirement adjustment is not a unitary psychological construct, but a complex process involving multiple ingredients such as resource loss, identity continuity and emotional experience.13,35 As the life course perspective considers retirement as a lifespan transition, individual attributes, socioeconomic context, and work-related factors shape the pathways through which the transition is accomplished.16 Loss of retirement was primarily associated with socioeconomic factors: poorer self-rated economic status, lower occupational prestige, and a greater number of family laborers were all linked to higher levels of loss. According to conservation of resources (COR) theory, retirees with higher occupational prestige and better economic status accumulate more resources during their careers, which help cushion the impact of work role exit and facilitate adjustment.15,18 Conversely, insufficient economic security may amplify feelings of loss, as retirees face a significant decline in income. A greater number of family laborers was also associated with higher loss of retirement, which may stem from feelings of uselessness arising from an inability to contribute to the family.50 These findings underscore the importance of basic economic security in enabling retirees to navigate the retirement transition smoothly and maintain positive mental health in later life. Nostalgia for work was uniquely related to work attachments, with higher WFC and AOC linked to greater nostalgia. According to role theory, for individuals who are highly invested in their work, the work role is central to their self-identity.9,23 They derive core meaning from their occupations and continue to miss work memories years after retirement, illustrating the remarkable persistence of occupational identities.25 Enjoyment of retirement was the most complex construct, shaped by demographic and socioeconomic characteristics as well as work attachments. Shorter retirement duration was associated with higher enjoyment of retirement, aligning with Atchley’s stage theory of retirement adjustment,51 which posits that retirees initially experience a “honeymoon phase” characterized by excitement upon liberation from work demands. However, as novelty fades and the reality of daily retirement life sets in, enjoyment may decline. Education level and self-rated economic status conferred protective effects, as they provide individuals with greater cognitive and material resources that facilitate adaptation.44 In contrast, WFC emerged as the strongest negative predictor of enjoyment of retirement, indicating identity disruption following occupational exit.23,25

Mediation analyses revealed that enjoyment of retirement was the only dimension linking pre-retirement work attachments to post-retirement mental health. Identity theory posits that individuals possess multiple role identities organized hierarchically by salience, the probability that a given identity will be invoked across situations.52 For individuals with higher work attachments, occupational identity occupies the apex of this hierarchy, functioning as the primary source of meaning, competence validation, and social recognition.20,52 Retirement fundamentally disrupts this identity structure: when a highly salient work role cannot be successfully transferred or replaced in post-retirement life, individuals experience identity discontinuity, manifesting as lower enjoyment of retirement and higher nostalgia for work.51 Critically, low enjoyment of retirement leads to the inability to cultivate alternative sources of purpose and meaningful engagement, manifesting as social disconnection and behavioral drift, and directly compromises psychological well-being.53 However, although significant positive associations between work attachments and nostalgia for work were identified in both hierarchical regression and mediation analyses, no significant association was found between nostalgia for work and mental health. Unlike enjoyment of retirement, nostalgia for work reflects retrospective work evaluation with ambivalent emotional valence, encompassing both positive and negative memories varying with each individual,54,55 which may obscure its relationship with mental health. Reverse causality cannot be ruled out, poor mental health may itself weaken nostalgic reflection, and unmeasured personality traits such as neuroticism may disrupt the benefits of nostalgia.56 In addition, cultural factors, particularly collectivism and family-oriented values unique to Chinese contexts, may moderate the strength of these associations with mental health.6

AOC showed a significant positive direct effect on life satisfaction independent of the indirect effect through enjoyment of retirement. Consistent with the life course perspective,16 this suggests that AOC may contribute to well-being through mechanisms beyond the retirement adjustment pathway, such as maintaining a sense of continuity or belonging even after leaving the organization. The absence of significant direct effects of work attachments on depression carries important implications: pre-retirement work attachments do not inevitably lead to poor mental health; their impact is conditional on the quality of the retirement adjustment. If retirees with strong work attachments successfully construct an enjoyable post-retirement life, the potential psychological risks can be neutralized. Cultivating a healthy perspective on work can lay a foundation for psychological resilience in later life.57

The most distinct finding of the current study concerns the moderated mediation analyses. From a selective optimization with compensation (SOC) perspective, social network functions as a compensatory resource that offsets identity losses from work role exit.18 Consistent with previous studies, when work-related resources are lost, the social network provides alternative pathways to promote mental health by offering emotional support and a sense of belonging.10 Specifically, this effect is striking: social network moderates the link between enjoyment of retirement and mental health, whereas leisure activity does not. Social network provides unique resources, including emotional support, self-worth validation, and a sense of belonging, which directly address identity threats from role loss.29,58 Retirees may have different optimal levels of leisure activity, and higher participation is not universally beneficial.59 Excessive leisure activity may sometimes even be detrimental to mental health, suggesting that retirees should identify individually suitable activity patterns.60

To our knowledge, drawing on both the life course perspective and selective optimization with compensation perspective, this exploratory study was the first to investigate the psychological mechanisms linking pre-retirement work attachments and retirement adjustment to post-retirement mental health among Chinese retirees. Social network serves as a critical buffer that neutralizes this negative indirect effect, thereby clarifying the conditional nature of work attachment’s “dark side”. These findings have important implications for behavioral health management in retirement. Beyond the fulfillment of retirees’ basic material needs, greater attention should also be paid to their mental health.61 Interventions should prioritize social network cultivation for retirees with strong work attachment and limited social connections. Evidence-based strategies include integrating social network assessments into routine retirement counseling, establishing structured peer support groups, and providing relationship-building skills training.62,63 Older adults with low or moderate levels of social network are more vulnerable to poor retirement adjustment and may benefit from these targeted interventions. For retirees with high work attachment, facilitating continued workplace engagement through alumni networks may ease identity transitions while gradually expanding social connections beyond organizational contexts.64

Despite its contributions, this study has several limitations. First, due to the cross-sectional design, causal interpretations of the mediation effects are not warranted. The current results are exploratory and the proposed mediation model needs to be verified using longitudinal data that tracks individuals from pre-retirement onward. Second, retrospective reports of WFC and AOC may be subject to recall bias, as current feelings can shape recollections of past experiences, and dichotomous responses may affect reliability and validity. Finally, the convenience sample was recruited primarily from Jinan with a higher proportion of females, limiting the generalizability of the findings. Future research should adopt prospective longitudinal designs, use more representative samples, incorporate individual personality traits and cultural factors across diverse contexts, and employ refined measurement approaches to further clarify the determinants and mechanisms of this important life transition.

Conclusion

This exploratory study confirmed that retirement adjustment is multidimensional, with each dimension influenced by distinct factors: loss of retirement was associated only with socioeconomic factors; nostalgia for work was associated only with work attachments; and enjoyment of retirement was associated with demographic factors, socioeconomic factors, and work attachments. Work attachments were associated with lower enjoyment of retirement, which in turn was associated with poorer mental health in later life, but only among retirees with low or moderate levels of social network. A rich social network buffered these adverse effects, suggesting that strengthening social connections may represent a promising behavioral target for future intervention research. From a theoretical standpoint, integrating the life course perspective and selective optimization with compensation theory suggests that retirement represents a developmental transition in which rebuilding social connections can transform vulnerability into resilience. Given that the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, prospective longitudinal studies with representative samples are needed to confirm the proposed pathways of this important life transition and to inform intervention development.
