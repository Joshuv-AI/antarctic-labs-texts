Liposomal Bupivacaine for Postoperative Analgesia After Multilevel Posterior Lumbar Fusion Surgery: A Prospective Three-Arm Randomized Study
Authors: Chunyan Dou, Suli Wang, Zihui Xu, Lizhen Wang, Ke Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study compared three standardized postoperative analgesic strategies for adults undergoing multilevel posterior lumbar fusion surgery: liposomal bupivacaine thoracolumbar interfascial plane block (LB-TLIP), ropivacaine TLIP block (Ropi-TLIP), and liposomal bupivacaine local infiltration analgesia (LB-LIA).Patients and MethodsThis prospective, randomized, open-label, three-arm study enrolled 90 adults, with 30 patients in each group. The registry-listed primary outcome comprised serial resting and movement-evoked numerical rating scale (NRS) scores at 2, 6, 12, 24, 36, 48, and 72 hours after discharge from the operating room. These profiles were analyzed longitudinally using repeated-measures fixed-effects models with participant-level cluster-robust standard errors. The 2–72-hour NRS area under the curve (AUC) was calculated as a non-registered exploratory derived summary; it was used for pilot-based sample-size planning but was not separately listed in the registry or documented in a dated statistical analysis plan. The trial was prospectively registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2500097484) on February 19, 2025.ResultsMovement-evoked NRS profiles showed significant group and group-by-time effects (P<0.001 and P=0.001, respectively). Resting NRS profiles showed a significant group effect (P=0.003) without a significant group-by-time interaction (P=0.133). The LB-LIA group generally had the lowest early pain scores. Exploratory movement-evoked AUC values were 220.0 ± 28.4, 244.6 ± 30.6, and 257.1 ± 34.4 NRS·h for LB-LIA, LB-TLIP, and Ropi-TLIP, respectively (adjusted overall P<0.001). Time to first ambulation favored LB-LIA, whereas rescue tramadol use, hospital stay, and recorded adverse events did not differ significantly.ConclusionSerial NRS trajectories differed among groups, with generally lower early scores in the LB-LIA group. Exploratory AUC summaries were directionally consistent. These findings support lower early postoperative pain scores rather than broader clinical, mechanistic, opioid-sparing, or safety superiority.Trial RegistrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry, ChiCTR2500097484; registered on February 19, 2025.

Introduction

Pain after multilevel posterior lumbar fusion is often more complex than simple incision pain. It may arise from the skin, fascia, paraspinal muscles, bone work, and the implanted fixation system. In routine postoperative care, this pain becomes most important when patients try to turn in bed, cough, sit, stand, or walk. Poor early pain control can therefore slow rehabilitation and reduce the patient’s sense of recovery. For this reason, pain management is a central part of enhanced recovery care after lumbar fusion surgery.1,2

Opioids remain useful after spine surgery, but they are not an ideal single solution. Regional analgesia and wound infiltration may reduce opioid exposure, yet conventional local-anesthetic strategies may not always provide adequate analgesia throughout the early postoperative course. This limitation has prompted interest in longer-acting formulations and in identifying the delivery route that provides the greatest clinical effect.1–3

Liposomal bupivacaine is designed to release bupivacaine over a longer period at the injection site. This property is attractive in spine surgery because pain usually continues beyond the first postoperative day. Published findings, however, have not been uniform. A recent spine-surgery meta-analysis reported lower pain and opioid consumption, whereas broader reviews have shown that effects may vary by procedure, delivery route, comparator, and outcome.4–6 Studies in lumbar spine surgery have likewise reported mixed results for surgical-site infiltration and paraspinal plane blocks, with benefits in some cohorts but no consistent improvement across pain, opioid use, recovery, or safety outcomes.7–14

Posterior lumbar surgery offers two clinically relevant routes. A TLIP block places local anesthetic into a fascial plane near the paraspinal muscles and dorsal rami. Local infiltration places the drug directly into the surgical wound and nearby tissues. These routes do not necessarily cover the same pain sources. TLIP block mainly targets a nerve-related tissue plane, while local infiltration reaches the incision, subcutaneous tissue, muscle, fascia, and deeper wound area. Evidence from randomized trials and comparative studies has supported the analgesic potential of TLIP block in lumbar surgery, although direct comparisons with wound infiltration and other paraspinal plane blocks have produced variable findings.15–25 Meta-analyses and wound-infiltration reviews emphasize heterogeneity and limited comparative evidence.17–19,26 If postoperative pain comes from several tissue layers, local infiltration may provide broader coverage. This explanation is plausible, but it was not directly tested by imaging, sensory mapping, or pharmacokinetic measurement in the present study.

The three-arm design was used to separate two linked questions. The comparison between LB-TLIP and Ropi-TLIP addresses whether the liposomal formulation adds benefit when the TLIP technique is the same. The comparison between LB-TLIP and LB-LIA addresses whether the route of liposomal bupivacaine delivery changes the clinical effect. This study therefore compared LB-TLIP, Ropi-TLIP, and LB-LIA in adults undergoing multilevel posterior lumbar fusion surgery. The registry-listed outcome comprised serial resting and movement-evoked NRS scores at the prespecified postoperative time points; 2–72-hour pain AUC was retained only as a non-registered exploratory derived summary of those serial measurements.

Patients and Methods

Study Design and Setting

This prospective, randomized, parallel-group clinical study was conducted at two tertiary hospitals in China: the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University in Hefei and Huaibei Miners General Hospital in Huaibei. The study compared three analgesic strategies for adults undergoing multilevel posterior lumbar fusion surgery. The registry listed an overall study period from January 1, 2025, to June 1, 2027, and a planned recruitment period from February 19, 2025, to July 31, 2026. Ethics approval was obtained on January 24, 2025. A separate 30-participant pilot feasibility cohort was conducted after trial registration and before recruitment of the formal randomized cohort. The pilot was used only for feasibility assessment and sample-size planning, and none of its participants was included in the formal randomization cohort or in any reported efficacy or safety analysis. Actual recruitment of the formal 90-participant randomized cohort began on September 11, 2025, and ended on March 20, 2026. The final participant completed the scheduled 72-hour postoperative assessment on March 23, 2026. Hospital discharge outcomes and in-hospital adverse events were recorded until discharge, with the final discharge-related record completed on April 3, 2026. No scheduled post-discharge follow-up was conducted for functional recovery, delayed wound complications, persistent opioid use, or sustained analgesic benefit after discharge.

The formal randomized trial was prospectively registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry as ChiCTR2500097484 on February 19, 2025, before recruitment of the formal randomized cohort. The registry described the primary outcome as “Patients’ NRS pain scores at rest and after activity at 2h, 6h, 12h, 24h, 36h, 48h, and 72h after surgery”. In this manuscript, “after activity” is reported as movement-evoked pain. Operationally, all postoperative time points in the present study were calculated from discharge from the operating room. The study protocol was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University (PJ 2024–12-80). Each participant provided written informed consent before enrollment. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants

Adult patients scheduled for elective multilevel posterior lumbar fusion and internal fixation were screened for eligibility. Patients were eligible when they had imaging-confirmed lumbar spine disease requiring lumbar fusion and internal fixation, ASA physical status I to III, aged 18–65 years, normal communication ability, and signed informed consent.

Patients were excluded if they underwent emergency surgery; had severe cardiac, hepatic, or renal dysfunction; had infection, inflammation, or injury at the puncture site; used analgesic drugs for a long period before surgery; had allergy to liposomal bupivacaine or other study anesthetic drugs; had previous lumbar surgery; were pregnant or lactating; or were unable to understand verbal or written information.

Severe cardiac dysfunction was defined as NYHA class III–IV heart failure, decompensated heart failure, myocardial infarction within 3 months, or severe refractory arrhythmia. Severe hepatic dysfunction was defined as Child-Pugh grade B or C liver cirrhosis, acute liver failure, or ALT/AST ≥3 times the upper limit of normal with persistent liver injury. Severe renal dysfunction was defined as estimated glomerular filtration rate <30 mL/min/1.73 m2, maintenance dialysis, or end-stage renal disease. These criteria were confirmed using preoperative medical records, laboratory tests, and anesthetic assessment.

Randomization and Allocation

Eligible participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1:1 ratio to the LB-TLIP, Ropi-TLIP, or LB-LIA group using the random-number table method, consistent with the trial registry. Allocation information and random numbers were placed in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. The envelopes were kept by the study coordinator and opened on the day of surgery after induction of anesthesia.

The public trial registry listed blinding as “None”; accordingly, the study was treated as open-label. Intervention providers were necessarily aware of allocation, and no formal blinding procedure or double-dummy design was implemented for participants or postoperative assessors. No assessment of blinding success was performed. To reduce measurement variability, postoperative outcomes were collected at predefined time points using standardized case-report forms, and statistical analyses were conducted using coded group labels.

Anesthesia Management

All patients received general anesthesia according to institutional protocols. Routine monitoring included electrocardiography, pulse oximetry, blood pressure monitoring, end-tidal carbon dioxide, body temperature, and bispectral index monitoring when available. Peripheral venous access was established before induction. Invasive arterial pressure monitoring was used when the anesthesiologist considered it necessary.

Anesthesia was induced with intravenous etomidate (0.2–0.5 mg/kg), sufentanil (0.3–0.5 μg/kg), and cisatracurium (0.15 mg/kg). After adequate muscle relaxation, tracheal intubation was performed. Ventilation was adjusted to keep end-tidal carbon dioxide within the normal clinical range. Anesthesia was maintained with propofol infusion (4–6 mg/kg/h) and remifentanil infusion (0.1–0.2 μg/kg/min), with or without inhaled anesthetics according to local practice. When BIS monitoring was used, the target range was about 40 to 60. Additional sufentanil could be given when persistent increases in heart rate or blood pressure suggested inadequate analgesia after other causes were considered.

Hemodynamic management followed the same general approach across groups. Mean arterial pressure and heart rate were kept within clinically acceptable ranges compared with baseline. Hypotension was treated with fluids or vasoactive drugs. Hypertension or tachycardia was managed by adjusting anesthetic or analgesic depth when appropriate. Active warming was used to help maintain body temperature. At the end of surgery, neuromuscular blockade was reversed according to routine practice. Patients were extubated after adequate spontaneous breathing, airway reflex recovery, and stable vital signs. They were then transferred to the post-anesthesia care unit for monitoring. The total doses of propofol, remifentanil, and sufentanil were recorded, along with anesthesia duration, surgery duration, estimated blood loss, and number of fused segments.

No patient received intraoperative neuromonitoring, including somatosensory evoked potentials, motor evoked potentials, or electromyography.

Analgesic Interventions

Study analgesic procedures were performed according to written standardized operating procedures by clinicians experienced in regional anesthesia or spine surgery. Total injectate volume was standardized at 30 mL across the LB-TLIP, Ropi-TLIP, and LB-LIA groups to minimize volume-related confounding. For both liposomal bupivacaine groups, the same fixed dose and volume were used. Liposomal bupivacaine was prepared from 20 mL of stock suspension at 13.3 mg/mL (total dose, 266 mg) and diluted with 10 mL of 0.9% normal saline to a final volume of 30 mL. The final concentration was 8.87 mg/mL (0.887%, approximately 0.89%). No bupivacaine hydrochloride or other local anesthetic was added to the liposomal bupivacaine solution. Product-label information was followed for dilution, compatibility precautions, and handling. The product label identifies 266 mg as the maximum adult dose for local infiltration and does not specifically identify TLIP block; TLIP administration was performed under the ethics-approved trial protocol, whereas LB-LIA was administered by surgical-site infiltration.

LB-TLIP Group

Patients in the LB-TLIP group received bilateral ultrasound-guided TLIP block using liposomal bupivacaine. Liposomal bupivacaine was supplied as 20 mL of stock suspension at 13.3 mg/mL (total dose, 266 mg) and diluted with 10 mL of 0.9% normal saline to a final volume of 30 mL, corresponding to a final concentration of 8.87 mg/mL (0.887%, approximately 0.89%). The 266-mg dose was specified in the ethics-approved trial protocol. The product-label maximum adult dose of 266 mg pertains to local infiltration, and the product label does not specifically identify TLIP block. A volume of 15 mL, containing 133 mg of liposomal bupivacaine, was injected on each side.

The L3 spinous process was identified in the longitudinal plane along the posterior midline. The ultrasound transducer was then rotated 90 degrees to obtain a transverse view and moved laterally by approximately 2–3 cm to visualize the fascial plane between the longissimus and iliocostalis muscles. Using an in-plane approach, the needle was advanced from medial to lateral. After the needle tip reached the thoracolumbar interfascial plane and negative aspiration confirmed the absence of blood or air, the injectate was administered slowly and incrementally under continuous ultrasound visualization. The injectate was deposited only within the TLIP fascial plane and was not infiltrated into subcutaneous tissue, deep muscle layers, or periosteal tissue.

TLIP procedures were performed by anesthesiologists experienced in regional anesthesia using a common written procedural protocol. The protocol standardized patient positioning, ultrasound probe placement, identification of the target fascial plane, needle trajectory, negative aspiration, slow incremental injection, and fixed bilateral volume allocation.

Ropi-TLIP Group

Patients in the Ropi-TLIP group received bilateral ultrasound-guided TLIP block using ropivacaine. Ropivacaine was diluted with 0.9% normal saline by the hospital pharmacy before surgery to a fixed final concentration of 0.33%. The total injectate volume was 30 mL, with 15 mL injected on each side, corresponding to a total ropivacaine dose of 99 mg. No liposomal bupivacaine or other local anesthetic was mixed with ropivacaine. Patient positioning, ultrasound scanning method, needle route, target plane, negative aspiration, slow incremental injection, and bilateral volume allocation were the same as those used in the LB-TLIP group.

LB-LIA Group

Patients in the LB-LIA group received a single local infiltration analgesia procedure with liposomal bupivacaine. The same liposomal bupivacaine dose and total volume used in the LB-TLIP group were used: 266 mg supplied as 20 mL of stock suspension at 13.3 mg/mL and diluted with 10 mL of 0.9% normal saline to a total volume of 30 mL. The final concentration was 8.87 mg/mL (0.887%, approximately 0.89%). No bupivacaine hydrochloride or other local anesthetic was added. The solution was prepared before surgery using only 0.9% normal saline and was used within 4 hours after preparation. Hypotonic diluents and filter needles were avoided, and a needle of 25G or larger was used.

The LB-LIA solution was administered as a single wound infiltration after completion of decompression, instrumentation, bone grafting, wound irrigation, and hemostasis, but before final layered wound closure. The total 30-mL injectate was distributed using multiple bilateral injections across the skin and subcutaneous tissues, the superficial thoracolumbar fascia and paraspinal muscle layer, and the deeper tissues surrounding the decompression and fusion area, including tissues adjacent to the fused segments and instrumentation-related soft tissues. The same total dose, total volume, broad anatomical distribution, and timing relative to closure were used throughout the LB-LIA group.

The solution was administered slowly during needle withdrawal with repeated aspiration before and during injection to reduce the risk of intravascular administration. The exact number, spacing, and per-site volume of individual injections were not prospectively recorded and are therefore not reported. The procedure was described only to the level supported by the contemporaneous clinical documentation.

Postoperative Analgesia and Rescue Analgesia

All patients received the same postoperative background analgesic regimen unless contraindicated. During the first 2 postoperative days, flurbiprofen axetil 50 mg was administered intravenously once daily. When the resting NRS score exceeded 4, tramadol 50 mg was administered intravenously as rescue analgesia. The need for rescue tramadol and cumulative tramadol use within 72 hours were recorded.

Antiemetic treatment was given when needed according to local practice. Recorded adverse events included nausea or vomiting, dizziness, delirium, chest tightness, surgical-site or deep infection, constipation, local-anesthetic systemic toxicity, nerve-root or cauda-equina-related neurological injury, and serious adverse events.

Adverse events were identified during scheduled postoperative assessments and through review of case-report forms, nursing records, anesthetic postoperative notes, attending-physician progress notes, and patient reports through the 72-hour postoperative observation period. In-hospital events were additionally recorded until discharge. No separate formal adverse-event surveillance instrument was used, and no routine post-discharge adverse-event follow-up was performed.

Delirium was a predefined adverse-event category identified from routine clinical assessment and nursing and physician documentation; a validated delirium screening instrument was not prospectively administered. Serious adverse events were identified from clinical documentation throughout hospitalization, including life-threatening events, events requiring additional intervention, events prolonging hospitalization, and events causing permanent injury. Suspected serious events were to be documented and reported according to institutional procedures.

Postoperative mobilization followed the same institutional assessment pathway in all groups. First ambulation was defined as the first time the patient independently stood and walked with assistance. Sitting up in bed or dangling the legs at the bedside was not counted. Patients were required to wear a lumbar brace, have stable vital signs, and have no severe breakthrough pain before ambulation was recorded. Ward nurses assessed blood pressure, heart rate, movement-evoked NRS, brace use, and patient tolerance before ambulation. If movement-evoked pain was greater than 4 or clinical readiness criteria were not met, ambulation was deferred. Mobilization proceeded stepwise from sitting up in bed for 30 minutes, to standing at the bedside, and then to short-distance walking. No fixed postoperative-hour target or mandatory ERAS-based early-ambulation target was implemented during the study period; the same clinical readiness criteria were applied to all three groups.

Outcome Measures

Registered Primary Outcome and Exploratory AUC Analysis

The registry described the primary outcome as “Patients’ NRS pain scores at rest and after activity at 2h, 6h, 12h, 24h, 36h, 48h, and 72h after surgery”. In this manuscript, “after activity” is termed movement-evoked pain. These serial profiles were analyzed longitudinally and are presented first in the Results. The registry did not specify a single summary estimand or a prospective testing hierarchy between movement-evoked and resting profiles or between the omnibus group and group-by-time effects. All postoperative time points were operationally calculated from discharge from the operating room. No NRS assessment was performed before 2 hours postoperatively. Movement-evoked and resting pain AUCs were therefore restricted to the 2–72-hour interval and calculated using the trapezoidal rule. AUC was not separately listed as an outcome in the registry and was not documented as a prespecified endpoint in a separate dated statistical analysis plan before formal recruitment. The AUC analyses were therefore treated as non-registered exploratory derived summaries and were not interpreted as confirmatory primary-endpoint analyses.

Secondary Outcomes

Registry-listed secondary outcomes included intraoperative anesthetic drug doses, QoR-40 scores at 24, 48, and 72 hours, postoperative adverse events, time to first ambulation, and length of hospital stay. The need for rescue tramadol and cumulative tramadol use from 0 to 72 hours were additional non-registered exploratory outcomes. Exploratory derived pain summaries included movement-evoked and resting NRS AUCs from 2 to 72 hours and their corresponding time-weighted mean scores. Individual time-point contrasts were also exploratory. The QoR-40 ranges from 40 to 200, with higher scores indicating better recovery.27

Data Collection

Baseline data were obtained from case report forms and medical records. These data included age, sex, body mass index, ASA physical status, diagnosis, study center, and number of fused segments. Perioperative data included surgery time, anesthesia time, propofol dose, remifentanil dose, total sufentanil dose, intervention details, and whether intraoperative neuromonitoring was used.

Postoperative pain scores were collected at the planned time points. QoR-40 was assessed at 24, 48, and 72 hours. Rescue analgesia, ambulation time, hospital stay, and adverse events were obtained from case report forms and clinical records. Time to first rescue tramadol was not prespecified and was not prospectively collected. Before analysis, the dataset was checked for missing values, implausible values, and inconsistent coding. Patient identifiers were removed from the analysis dataset.

Sample Size

The sample size was estimated using data from a separate 30-participant pilot feasibility cohort, with 10 participants receiving each analgesic strategy. The pilot was conducted after trial registration and before formal randomized recruitment. Pilot data were used only for feasibility assessment and preliminary estimates for formal sample-size planning, and none of the pilot participants was included in the formal 90-participant randomized cohort or in any reported efficacy or safety analysis. The observed pilot mean ± standard deviation values for movement-evoked NRS AUC from 2 to 72 hours were 225 ± 28.52 NRS·h in the LB-LIA group, 238 ± 29.56 NRS·h in the LB-TLIP group, and 255 ± 30.25 NRS·h in the Ropi-TLIP group. With a two-sided significance level of 0.05, power of 90%, and a one-way analysis-of-variance model for three independent groups in PASS version 15.0 (NCSS, LLC, Kaysville, UT, USA), the required sample size was estimated as 27 participants per group. Allowing for an anticipated 10% dropout rate, 30 participants were planned for each group, giving a total formal randomized sample of 90 participants. Movement-evoked AUC was derived from the registry-listed serial NRS measurements and was selected for sample-size planning before recruitment of the formal randomized cohort. However, AUC was not separately registered or designated as a confirmatory endpoint in a dated statistical analysis plan. Accordingly, this calculation is reported transparently as the basis for the planned sample size but does not establish formal statistical power for the longitudinal repeated-measures tests, secondary outcomes, or safety outcomes.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with supplementary sensitivity analyses conducted in R. Continuous variables were assessed using visual inspection and the Shapiro–Wilk test and are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median [interquartile range], as appropriate. Categorical variables are reported as n (%).

Baseline and perioperative variables were compared using one-way analysis of variance, the Kruskal–Wallis test, the chi-square test, or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Standardized mean differences were additionally calculated to assess baseline balance. Registry-listed serial movement-evoked and resting NRS scores were analyzed using repeated-measures fixed-effects models with patient-level cluster-robust standard errors. Models included treatment group, time, group-by-time interaction, study center, number of fused segments, and surgery duration.

Movement-evoked and resting NRS AUCs from 2 to 72 hours were calculated using the trapezoidal rule and treated as exploratory derived outcomes. AUCs, time-weighted mean pain scores, and time-specific QoR-40 scores were analyzed using covariate-adjusted ANCOVA models with HC3 robust standard errors. Adjusted mean differences and 95% confidence intervals were reported. Longitudinal QoR-40 scores were analyzed using a repeated-measures model.

Rescue tramadol use, cumulative tramadol dose, time to first ambulation, hospital stay, and adverse events were analyzed using parametric, non-parametric, categorical, or regression methods according to data distribution. Intravenous tramadol was reported primarily in milligrams. For descriptive standardization, intravenous morphine equivalents were calculated as tramadol dose × 0.1.28. This conversion was not used for inferential analyses or clinical dosing decisions.

Because no prospective statistical testing hierarchy was documented, the longitudinal NRS models were treated as the principal analyses of the registered pain outcomes, while AUCs, individual time-point comparisons, and secondary outcomes were interpreted as exploratory. Holm adjustment was applied in a post hoc sensitivity analysis across the four omnibus longitudinal NRS tests, and Holm and Benjamini–Hochberg adjustments were applied to key secondary outcomes. Clinical-relevance sensitivity analyses compared time-weighted NRS differences with a 1.0-point reference threshold and QoR-40 differences with a 6.3-point threshold.28,29 All tests were two-sided, with effect estimates and 95% confidence intervals emphasized alongside P values.

Sensitivity and Supplementary Analyses

Sensitivity analyses evaluated whether the exploratory AUC findings changed under alternative analytical approaches. These analyses included unadjusted analyses, the prespecified-covariate adjusted model, extended covariate adjustment, and non-parametric group comparisons. SMDs indicated residual imbalance in diagnosis category and number of fused segments. Number of fused segments was included in the prespecified-covariate adjusted model. Diagnosis category was not added post hoc because several category-by-group cells were small and the 90-participant sample provided limited support for a more highly parameterized model; residual confounding from baseline imbalance therefore cannot be excluded. Additional methodological details are provided in the Supplementary Methods. Detailed NRS values, full longitudinal model outputs, exploratory pairwise time-point comparisons, detailed adverse-event estimates, multiplicity-adjusted secondary outcomes, confidence intervals for key secondary outcomes, baseline standardized mean differences, and clinical-relevance sensitivity summaries are presented in the Supplementary Methods.

Results

Patient Flow and Baseline Characteristics

A total of 92 patients were assessed for eligibility. Two patients were excluded before randomization because their family members declined participation. The remaining 90 patients were randomized in a 1:1:1 ratio to the LB-TLIP, Ropi-TLIP, and LB-LIA groups, with 30 patients assigned to each group. All randomized patients completed follow-up and were included in the final analysis (Figure 1).

Conventional group-comparison P values did not show statistically significant baseline or perioperative differences (Table 1). However, standardized differences suggested residual imbalance in diagnosis category and surgical complexity, particularly the number of fused segments (Supplementary Table S10). Most patients were enrolled from the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University, with a smaller number from Huaibei Miners General Hospital. The adjusted and sensitivity analyses should therefore be interpreted as reducing, rather than eliminating, possible confounding from baseline imbalance. Rescue Analgesia and Postoperative Recovery

Rescue tramadol use within 72 hours was not significantly different. It was required in 5 patients (16.7%) in LB-TLIP, 6 patients (20.0%) in Ropi-TLIP, and 4 patients (13.3%) in LB-LIA (P=0.787; Figure 4B and Table 3). The observed 50- and 100-mg intravenous doses corresponded to approximately 5 and 10 mg IME, respectively. This outcome showed a floor effect and should not be interpreted as evidence of opioid-sparing benefit.

Time to first ambulation differed among groups. The median time was 72.0 [60.0, 77.5] hours in LB-TLIP, 72.0 [60.0, 79.5] hours in Ropi-TLIP, and 55.0 [48.0, 72.0] hours in LB-LIA (P=0.002). Safety Outcomes

Recorded adverse events did not differ significantly among groups. Any recorded adverse event occurred in 15 patients (50.0%) in LB-TLIP, 10 patients (33.3%) in Ropi-TLIP, and 14 patients (46.7%) in LB-LIA (P=0.387). The risk difference was 16.7 percentage points (95% CI, −7.9 to 41.3) for LB-TLIP versus Ropi-TLIP and 3.3 percentage points (95% CI, −21.9 to 28.6) for LB-TLIP versus LB-LIA. No serious adverse event, surgical-site or deep infection, local-anesthetic systemic toxicity, or nerve-root or cauda-equina-related neurological injury occurred. Detailed risk estimates are provided in Table 3 and Supplementary Table S9.

Nausea or vomiting was the most common recorded event, occurring in 10 patients (33.3%) in LB-TLIP, 4 patients (13.3%) in Ropi-TLIP, and 7 patients (23.3%) in LB-LIA (P=0.187). Dizziness, chest tightness, and constipation did not differ significantly among groups. No clinically documented delirium occurred in any group.

Discussion

This randomized three-arm study showed that early postoperative movement-evoked and resting NRS profiles differed among the three analgesic strategies, with generally lower pain scores in the LB-LIA group. Exploratory AUC analyses showed the same pattern. By contrast, the adjusted differences between LB-TLIP and Ropi-TLIP were not statistically significant. LB-LIA was also associated with higher early QoR-40 scores and earlier ambulation, whereas rescue tramadol consumption, hospital stay, and recorded adverse events were similar among groups.

The lower pain scores observed with LB-LIA may be related to the location of drug delivery. Multilevel posterior lumbar fusion injures several tissue layers, including skin, fascia, paraspinal muscle, periosteal tissue, and tissues surrounding the instrumentation. Local infiltration delivers liposomal bupivacaine directly into these surgically affected tissues, whereas TLIP block deposits the drug within a defined fascial plane. A randomized lumbar-fusion study similarly suggested that the effect of liposomal bupivacaine may vary according to the administration route.15 However, drug spread, sensory distribution, and pharmacokinetics were not measured; therefore, this explanation remains speculative.

LB-TLIP showed numerically lower pain burden than Ropi-TLIP, but the confidence intervals crossed zero. Thus, the present study did not demonstrate a clear advantage of the liposomal formulation when the TLIP route was unchanged. This cautious interpretation is consistent with mixed spine-surgery evidence showing that the effects of liposomal bupivacaine vary by technique, comparator, and measured outcome.5–8,14

The clinical magnitude of the observed differences should also be interpreted cautiously. No validated MCID has been established for postoperative NRS AUC after multilevel posterior lumbar fusion. The time-weighted NRS differences were below the 1-point acute postoperative pain reference threshold, and only one QoR-40 comparison exceeded the published 6.3-point reference value.28,29 Earlier ambulation in the LB-LIA group supported the pain findings, but this outcome may also have been influenced by brace use, drainage management, surgical trauma, and local mobilization practice.

Several limitations should be considered. The study was open-label; intervention providers, participants, and postoperative outcome assessors were not formally blinded, and blinding success was not assessed, so performance and detection bias cannot be excluded. The sample-size calculation was based on pilot movement-evoked NRS AUC data rather than the registry-listed longitudinal analyses, no prospective testing hierarchy was documented, and the study was not formally powered for the longitudinal omnibus, secondary recovery, rescue-analgesia, or safety outcomes. Despite covariate adjustment, residual imbalance in diagnosis indicators and the number of fused segments and residual confounding may remain. No NRS assessment was obtained before 2 hours, so the exploratory AUC analyses covered only the 2–72-hour interval. Time to first rescue tramadol was not prespecified or prospectively collected, and cumulative tramadol use showed a marked floor effect, limiting assessment of opioid-sparing benefit. No imaging assessment of injectate spread, sensory mapping, or pharmacokinetic assessment was performed; therefore, the proposed route-based mechanism remains speculative. Adverse events were ascertained through scheduled assessments and routine clinical documentation without a dedicated surveillance instrument or validated delirium screening tool; minor, transient, hypoactive, or subclinical events may have been underdetected, and the study was underpowered for uncommon events. Follow-up was limited to 72-hour and in-hospital outcomes, with no scheduled post-discharge assessment; longer-term functional recovery, delayed wound or other complications, persistent opioid use, and sustained analgesic benefit were not evaluated. Accordingly, the findings support lower early pain scores with LB-LIA but do not establish a route-specific mechanism, opioid-sparing benefit, between-group equivalence, comparable safety, or broader clinical superiority.

Conclusion

Among adults undergoing multilevel posterior lumbar fusion surgery, registry-listed serial movement-evoked and resting NRS trajectories differed among the three analgesic strategies, with generally lower early pain scores in the LB-LIA group. Non-registered exploratory AUC summaries were directionally consistent with these longitudinal findings. Time to first ambulation, a registry-listed secondary outcome, also favored LB-LIA. LB-TLIP did not show statistically significant adjusted AUC differences versus Ropi-TLIP. Rescue tramadol use, hospital stay, and recorded adverse events did not differ significantly. The observed time-weighted pain differences were below the 1.0-point clinical-reference threshold, and the study was not designed to establish broader clinical, mechanistic, opioid-sparing, or safety superiority.
