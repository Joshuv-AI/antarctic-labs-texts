Activation Characteristics and Aluminum Recovery from Coal Gangue: Structure–Phase–Reactivity Relationships Revealed by Thermal Analysis, Kinetics, and Acid Leaching
Authors: Hongwei Du, Ke Li, Tongtong Li, Xinghao Shi, Lingxian Fang, Zhao Cao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Coal gangue is a typical coal-based solid waste and its efficient utilization is of great importance for waste minimization and resource recovery. In this study, the thermal behavior, kinetic characteristics, and aluminum leaching performance of four representative coal gangue samples (XJ-1, SX-1, NX-1, and IM-1) were systematically investigated. TG–DTG analysis revealed that the decomposition process can be divided into five stages, with major mass losses occurring during organic pyrolysis and kaolinite dehydroxylation. The apparent activation energies calculated from the three isoconversional methods (FWO, KAS, and Starink) were highly consistent, with correlation coefficients (R 2) mostly above 0.95. XJ-1 and SX-1 exhibited higher E a values that increased with conversion, indicating a “difficult-to-activate” type, whereas NX-1 and IM-1 showed significant decreases in E a within the main decomposition region, corresponding to an “easy-to-activate” type. Acid leaching experiments further confirmed that NX-1 and IM-1 achieved the highest aluminum leaching efficiencies (>59%) after calcination at 750–800 °C, while XJ-1 and SX-1 remained below 50% even under optimum conditions. All samples exhibited reduced leachability at 850 °C due to recrystallization. Complementary structural and phase analyses supported these findings. Overall, this study establishes a clear structure–phase–reactivity relationship and classifies coal gangue into easy-to-activate and difficult-to-activate types, providing theoretical guidance for its classified utilization and process optimization of acid leaching.

Introduction

Coal gangue is an unavoidable solid waste generated during coal mining. On average, approximately 0.15 tonnes of coal gangue are produced for every tonne of coal. Considering China is a major coal-producing country, the cumulative stockpile of coal gangue generated from coal mining has exceeded 7 billion tonnes over the years. , The large-scale accumulation of coal gangue has caused severe ecological issues, including soil and groundwater contamination, as well as the potential for spontaneous combustion and the release of harmful gases. − However, coal gangue is not entirely useless. From the perspective of elemental value, coal gangue is rich in various high-value elements, such as silicon, aluminum, iron, titanium, calcium, magnesium, potassium, sodium, and traces of rare earth elements, − which can be used to produce high-value-added products such as building materials, ceramics, catalysts, and aerogels. , The key to extracting valuable elements lies in disrupting the chemical bonds of stable mineral structure within coal gangue, thus activating its processable reactivity. Coal gangue from different regions varies significantly in composition and structure, increasing the difficulty of resource utilization. Generally, coal gangue is mainly composed of minerals such as kaolinite, quartz and mica. Occasionally it may contain small amounts of feldspar, iron pyrites, and calcite. Take kaolinite as an example. Natural kaolinite has a stable structure, making it difficult to extract valuable elements such as silicon and aluminum. Typically, a thermal activation process is required to decompose the stable mineral structure, transforming the phase into a state that is readily soluble in acid or leachable in alkali, thereby improving the extraction efficiency of silicon and aluminum. Other minerals like quartz, mica and calcite are more stable under natural conditions, and the activation energy required for thermal activation is also higher. As a result, understanding the thermal decomposition characteristics and element release behavior of coal gangue from different regions is crucial for guiding its high-value-added utilization.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) is an effective technique for evaluating the thermal stability of materials, as well as analyzing their thermal decomposition behavior, weight loss and so on. Meanwhile, combined with kinetic modeling, TGA can be used to calculate apparent activation energy and investigate decomposition mechanisms. This provides guidance for activating the reactivity of coal gangue and promotes the efficient extraction and utilization of high-value elements within it. Ptácek et al. primarily discussed the TG-DTG pyrolysis behavior of kaolinite under nonisothermal conditions, determining that its dehydroxylation process follows a third-order reaction kinetic model, and calculated the kinetic parameters. However, their study was limited to a single-source sample and did not mention the influence of synergistic effects of multiple minerals in coal gangue. Shao et al. focused on the thermal evolution and leaching performance of kaolinite and boehmite in coal gangue, revealing the relationship between structural changes and aluminum extraction efficiency, but did not conduct in-depth kinetic modeling and quantitative analysis of mineral phase transformations. Zhang et al. established a kinetic model for extracting aluminum oxide from coal gangue according to hydrochloric acid leaching, but did not combine with the evolution of mineral structures before and after thermal treatment. Fan et al. explored the impact of thermal activation on the structure and leaching performance of coal gangue, revealing the correlation between its physical properties and aluminum extraction, but lacked an analysis of the kinetic mechanisms of thermal reaction processes. Likewise, Shao et al. conducted similar research as well. Guo et al. attempted to enhance the reactivity of coal gangue by optimizing calcination parameters and introducing additives. In addition, Zhang et al. pointed out that sodium-assisted calcination technology could improve the extraction rate of aluminum oxide. However, these studies were mostly aimed at specific cases and focused on single regions, which limited the broad applicability of their conclusions. Therefore, carrying out systematic research on the thermal behavior, structural evolution and reaction performance of cross-regional coal gangue holds significant practical importance for proposing a ‘regional activation–directional utilization’ strategy of coal gangue.

Coal gangue from different regions exhibits remarkable differences in mineral composition and crystallization, leading to different activation degrees which may be easy or difficult. Understanding the differences of coal gangue from different regions systematically and comprehensively is helpful in putting forward targeted activation strategies, which enhance the utilization of its elemental value. A systematic investigation was carried out on the thermal activation characteristics of coal gangue from four different regions in China. By DTG, XRD, FTIR, SEM-EDS, kinetic modeling and experiments on aluminum extraction via acid leaching, this study revealed the structural destruction, mineral transformation behavior and the influence mechanisms between thermal decomposition and element release during the thermal activation process of coal gangue from different regions. Eventually, the advice on regional activation strategies and high-value utilization directions for coal gangue from different regions was brought up.

Materials and Methods

Sample Preparation and Pretreatment

The coal gangue samples used in the experiment were collected from four typical coal-producing regions in China: Xinjiang (XJ), Shanxi (SX), Inner Mongolia (IM), and Ningxia (NX). Each sample was collected from the native gangue piles near local large coal mines, naturally air-dried, crushed, and ground to a particle size of less than 74 μm before being sealed and preserved. The sample codes are XJ-1, SX-1, IM-1, and NX-1. The raw coal gangue samples underwent proximate analysis (moisture content, volatile matter, ash content, fixed carbon) and chemical composition determination.

Thermal Analysis and Kinetic Parameter Calculation

THE thermal behavior test was performed using a German NETZSCH STA 449F3 simultaneous thermal analyzer. For each test, 10 mg of sample was precisely weighed and placed in an alumina crucible, which was heated from room temperature to 1000 °C under air atmosphere (flow rate 50 mL/min), with heating rates set at 5, 10, and 20 °C/min. The mass change (TG) and mass change rate (DTG) curves were recorded. The data obtained from the experiments were used for subsequent determination of kinetic parameters and identification of reaction stages.

To further quantify the activation energy (E a) of each stage of coal gangue thermal decomposition, The apparent activation energies (E a) of coal gangue were evaluated using three model-free isoconversional approaches: Flynn–Wall–Ozawa (FWO), , Kissinger–Akahira–Sunose (KAS), and Starink. , Their linearized forms are given as follows: FWO:logβ=logAEaRg(α)−0.4567EaRT KAS:KAS:ln(βT2)=lnARg(α)Ea−EaRT Starink:ln(βT1.8)=const−1.0008EaRT where β is the heating rate, T is the absolute temperature, R is the gas constant, and g(α) is the integral form of the conversion function. The slope of the fitted line yields the activation energy E a, while the intercept gives the pre-exponential factor A.

All kinetic analyses are based on the fundamental expression of nonisothermal reactions: dαdt=A·exp(−EaRT)·f(α)

m 0 is the initial mass, mt is the mass at t, and m ∞ is the final residual mass.

Extract the temperature points corresponding to different α values from the TG curve, plot the fitting curves required for the above three models, and calculate E a using the slope. The results are used to assess the differences of thermal reactivity among different coal gangue samples and to reveal the reaction stages and control mechanisms during the pyrolysis.

Thermal Activation and Acid Leaching Experiments

The thermal activation experiment was conducted using a muffle furnace to heat. The calcination temperature was set to 700, 750, 800, and 850 °C in an air atmosphere, with a heating rate of 10 °C/min and a constant temperature of 30 min at each target temperature to ensure sufficient phase transformation. After the thermal activation, the samples were naturally cooled to room temperature in air. They were ground and sieved for use in the acid leaching experiment. The acid leaching experiment used 3 mol/L nitric acid (HNO3) as the leaching agent, with a liquid-to-solid ratio of 5:1 (mL:g), a reaction temperature of 90 °C, and a leaching time of 30 min. The leaching solution was diluted and then an inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES, Thermo iCAP 7400) was employed to determine the aluminum content in the solution. Eventually, aluminum leaching rate was calculated.

Material Structure and Composition Characterization

XRD (Bruker D8 Advance) was used to identify the crystalline phases before and after heat treatment. The scan was conducted in the 2θ range of 5 to 80°, with a step size of 0.02°.

FTIR (Nicolet iS50) was used to identify the changes in the characteristic functional groups of the samples (e.g., Al–OH, Si–O–Si, and Si–O–Al).

SEM-EDS (Hitachi SU8020) was used to observe the particle morphology of the sample surface and elemental distribution.

ICP-OES was used to determine the aluminum concentration in the filtrate after acid leaching and the aluminum leaching rate would be calculated.

As shown in Table , there are notable differences in moisture content, volatile matter, ash content, and fixed carbon among coal gangue samples from different regions, reflecting the differences in their mineral phase composition and organic matter content. Moisture content (M_ad) is generally low (<2%) in which XJ is the lowest (0.55%) and NX is the highest(1.98%). Volatile matter (V_ad) content shows significant differences in which SX is 8.88% and IM is 19.89%. Generally speaking, higher organic matter content and higher content of easily decomposed minerals (like kaolinite) will lead to higher volatile matter content. The volatile matter content of IM is nearly twice that of SX, indicating greater mass loss during heating. Fixed carbon (FC_ad) content is an important indicator of residual combustibles. NX and IM samples contain higher fixed carbon content. Their combustion can provide heat during the reaction, which plays an auxiliary role in sustaining the activation process. However, SX and XJ samples have lower fixed carbon content. On the contrary, SX and XJ have higher proportion (84.45 and 79.05%, respectively) in ash content (A_ad). High ash content typically suggests that inertia and crystalline mineral phases (e.g., quartz) are dominant. Conversely, IM and NX have lower ash content (67.58 and 71.89%, respectively), indicating that they both have more reactivity, noncrystalline minerals or easily activated minerals (e.g., kaolinite).

The results of chemical composition analysis (Table ) are consistent with the trends observed in industrial analysis. Both SX and XJ exhibit relatively high SiO2 content, indicating that SX and XJ coal gangue are rich in quartz. In contrast, SiO2 content of IM and NX is lower (32.34 and 40.55%, respectively), while their Al2O3 content is higher (26.03 and 21.80%, respectively), indicating that IM and NX coal gangue are rich in kaolinite. According to the chemical analysis, it is concluded that the LOI values of IM and NX are relatively high, which may be related to their higher volatile matter and fixed carbon content. During the heating, the release of constitution water from kaolinite and the oxidation of organic matter lead to remarkable weight loss. In contrast, the LOI values of SX and XJ (19.82 and 16.40%, respectively) are much lower, consistent with their mineralogical characteristics dominated by quartz.

The XRD patterns of four coal gangue samples (Figure ) show that the mineral composition is mainly kaolinite and quartz, which can be verified from the characteristic peaks of kaolinite at 12.3 and 24.9° (001, 002) and quartz at 26.6° (101). However, the relative intensities of these peaks and the proportion of semiquantitative analysis phase reveal significant regional differences. As for IM and NX, it is obvious that the kaolinite peaks at 12.3 and 24.9° are stronger than the quartz peak at 26.6° (101), meaning that kaolinite is dominant. Semiquantitative analysis further confirms this point, with kaolinite content of 84.6% in NX and 86.8% in IM, while quartz content is 15.4 and 13.2%, respectively. This aligns with the high Al2O3 content and high LOI values showed in Table , as the mass loss of kaolinite is apparent during the heating activation. By comparison, the XRD patterns of SX and XJ show the quartz peak at 26.6° is sharp and dominant, while the kaolinite peak is relatively weak. Semiquantitative analysis proves the result as well. Because quartz minerals are stable and its mass loss is limited during the heating activation, the reactivity of SX and XJ coal gangue is low, unless they are activated by high temperature or cosolvent.

The Fourier transform infrared spectra of coal gangue samples from different origins (IM-1, SX-1, XJ-1 and NX-1) are shown in Figure . All samples exhibit distinct silicoaluminate characteristic absorption bands and interlayer water absorption bands centered at 3620 cm–1. Hydroxyl (−OH) stretching and bending vibrations are present, but the sharp peak at approximately 3695 cm–1attributed to the stretching of internal surface Al–OH groups in kaoliniteis clearly visible in NX and IM samples, yet significantly attenuated in XJ and SX. This trend indicates higher structural hydroxyl content and hydrophilicity in NX and IM coal gangue, suggesting greater kaolinite crystallinity. A pronounced peak at approximately 1030 cm–1 was observed in all samples, corresponding to the Si–O–Si asymmetric stretching in tetrahedral silicates. The bands at approximately 912 cm–1 (Al–OH bending), 796 cm–1 (Si–O symmetric stretching), and 540 cm–1 (Si–O–Al bending) exhibit sharper, more intense peaks in SX and XJ, indicating a more pronounced layered silicate structure. In contrast, NX and IM exhibit more pronounced peaks at 1480 and 870 cm–1, potentially indicating the presence of organic matter (e.g., humic acid) or residual carbon species (CC).

Overall, the Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy results indicate that coal gangue samples from the NX and IM regions contain higher kaolinite content with a well-preserved layered aluminosilicate framework and potentially greater organic matter, whereas samples from the SX and XJ regions exhibit the opposite characteristics. These distinctions are crucial for tailoring customized activation pathways and leaching strategies for coal gangue from different regions.

Stage Boundaries and Averaged Stage-Wise Mass Loss

Stage 1: Removal of free and adsorbed water (T < T 1).

In Stage 1 (T < T 1), all four samples exhibited only a slight weight loss, mainly corresponding to the evaporation of free water and physically adsorbed moisture. This observation is consistent with the proximate analysis results showing very low inherent moisture contents (M_ad = 0.55–1.98%), indicating that this stage involves only physical processes and contributes negligibly to the overall thermal behavior.

Stage 2: Oxygen adsorption and initial oxidation (T 1 – T 2).

Upon entering Stage 2 (T 1 – T 2), the TG curves displayed minimal net weight changes or even slight increases. This phenomenon is governed by two competing processes: (i) the chemisorption of oxygen molecules at surface active sites of coal gangue, and (ii) the initial scission of macromolecular side chains and bridge bonds, releasing a small fraction of volatile fragments. Under the current oxidizing atmosphere, oxygen chemisorption is considered to dominate, which is supported by the weak exothermic shoulders on the DSC curve and the very low overall mass loss (<2 wt %). Similar temporary mass stabilization caused by oxygen uptake has also been observed in coal- and shale-derived materials, , where the formation of surface oxygen-containing groups slightly increases the apparent mass before devolatilization becomes dominant. Therefore, Stage 2 can be regarded as a mild surface oxidation step rather than a true devolatilization process, which activates the surface and generates more reactive oxygen-containing sites for the subsequent decomposition of organics and minerals. The combination of these effects results in negligible changes in TG but weak shoulder peaks in the DTG curves. NX-1 and IM-1 showed slightly higher weight losses in this stage, suggesting that their structures contain more active functional groups and defects, which provide stronger initial reactivity.

Stage 3: Thermal decomposition of organics and dehydroxylation of kaolinite (T 2 – T 3, major weight loss region).

Stage 3 (T 2 – T 3) represents the dominant stage of mass loss. It is characterized by extensive pyrolysis of organic matter, accompanied by the release of CO, CO2, and light hydrocarbons, as well as the dehydroxylation of kaolinite to form amorphous metakaolin. These processes result in rapid structural collapse, leading to steep drops in TG and the appearance of pronounced DTG peaks. Compared with SX-1 (−11.67%) and XJ-1 (−12.89%), NX-1 (−20.93%) and IM-1 (−19.63%) experienced much larger weight losses in this stage, reflecting their higher kaolinite contents and greater structural susceptibility to breakdown. Notably, as the heating rate β increased from 5 to 20 °C min–1, the characteristic temperatures (T 2 – T 3) and DTG peak temperatures of all samples shifted to higher values by approximately 20–40 °C, and the DTG peak intensities increased accordingly. This behavior illustrates the combined effects of thermal hysteresis and apparent kinetic enhancement: limited heat transfer and diffusion under rapid heating delayed decomposition to higher temperatures, while the elevated temperature accelerated the reaction rate constant, leading to sharper DTG peaks.

Stage 4: Rapid combustion of fixed carbon and continued decomposition of clay minerals (T 3 – T 4).

In Stage 4 (T 3 – T 4), the samples underwent rapid oxidation of residual fixed carbon along with further decomposition of clay minerals. TG curves declined sharply again, with weight losses ranging from – 6.64% (SX-1) to −9.69% (NX-1). NX-1 and IM-1, with higher fixed carbon contents (15.04 and 11.84%, respectively), exhibited more intense weight loss and stronger exothermic peaks, which not only facilitated the removal of carbonaceous matter but also generated a “self-heating” effect that promoted structural rearrangement and pore development. By contrast, XJ-1 and SX-1, characterized by lower fixed carbon contents (8.42 and 7.25%), showed relatively smaller losses in this stage, indicating that fewer reactive components were available.

Stage 5: High-temperature stabilization and structural rearrangement (T 4 – T 5).

Stage 5 (T 4 – T 5) corresponds to the stabilization stage at elevated temperatures. Beyond 800 °C, all samples exhibited only negligible weight changes (−0.1 to −0.5%), which can be attributed to the structural rearrangement and short-range ordering of amorphous aluminosilicate phases. If the temperature or holding time is further increased, recrystallization may occur, producing new crystalline phases such as mullite and thereby reducing product reactivity. Therefore, the activation process should be carefully controlled within the window where sufficient disordering is achieved without triggering recrystallization, in order to maintain high leaching reactivity for subsequent acid dissolution.

From a physicochemical point of view, the mass-loss stages correspond to different reaction regimes. In Stages 1–2, the removal of moisture and the initial oxidation of organics require only small energy inputs, leading to low activation barriers. Stage 3 mainly involves the decomposition of organics and the dehydroxylation of kaolinite, which needs higher energy due to the breakdown of well-crystallized lattices. In the later stages (4–5), the reaction gradually shifts to diffusion or structure-controlled processes, especially when dense quartz frameworks remain or partial recrystallization occurs at high temperatures.

Kinetic Analysis and Apparent Activation Energy

While the TG–DTG analysis clearly delineates the multistage decomposition behavior of coal gangue, a more quantitative evaluation of its thermal reactivity requires kinetic modeling. In particular, the apparent activation energy (E a) provides a fundamental measure of the energy barrier associated with each conversion step, and thus reflects the ease or difficulty of structural decomposition. To this end, isoconversional model-free methods including Flynn–Wall–Ozawa (FWO), Kissinger–Akahira–Sunose (KAS), and Starink were applied to calculate E a values over a wide range of conversion fractions (α = 0.1–0.9). These methods are advantageous because they do not assume a specific reaction mechanism, and the activation energy can be obtained directly from the slope of the fitted linear equations. The results obtained by the three isoconversional methods were highly consistent, with most correlation coefficients (R 2) exceeding 0.95, verifying the reliability of the calculations.

For XJ-1, E a increased progressively with conversion, rising from ∼80 kJ mol–1 at α = 0.1 to nearly 200 kJ mol–1 at α = 0.9. This monotonic increase indicates that the early decomposition is dominated by the removal of volatiles and hydroxyl groups requiring relatively low energy, whereas the later stages involve the breakdown of inert quartz and aluminosilicate frameworks, which demand higher energy input. This behavior is consistent with the high quartz content of XJ-1, confirming its classification as a difficult-to-activate type, and also suggests that diffusion limitation inside the dense quartz skeleton further raises the apparent E a at high conversion.

For SX-1, E a decreased slightly with increasing α, suggesting that once the initial bond cleavage occurred, the subsequent reactions became less energy demanding. However, at α > 0.8, E a increased again, mainly due to the dominance of stable quartz and feldspar frameworks, which restrict mass transfer and require extra energy for structural rearrangement. Thus, SX-1, similar to XJ-1, is also categorized as a difficult-to-activate type.

In contrast, NX-1 exhibited a distinctly different trend: E a was relatively high at α = 0.1 but then decreased significantly, reaching ∼115–137 kJ mol–1 in the range of α = 0.5–0.8. This relatively high initial E a can be mainly explained by the higher kaolinite content of NX-1 and IM-1, as revealed by the XRD and FTIR results. During the early stage of thermal activation, well-crystallized kaolinite requires more energy to break the Al–OH bonds and initiate the transformation into amorphous metakaolin. At this point, the kaolinite lattice remains structurally intact, so additional energy input is needed to trigger the collapse of its layered framework. This interpretation is consistent with the FTIR spectra showing the disappearance of Al–OH vibrations and with the XRD patterns confirming the loss of kaolinite peaks after calcination. As conversion proceeded, Ea decreased due to the formation of amorphous metakaolin and the development of porous structures that facilitate gas escape and ion diffusion. Moreover, the presence of stable organic carbon may further elevate the initial E a because its combustion overlaps with kaolinite dehydroxylation, leading to coupled mineral–organic interactions at similar temperatures.

For IM-1, E a remained essentially stable across α = 0.1–0.9, within the range of 125–147 kJ mol–1. This stability indicates that the energy barrier remained relatively uniform throughout the decomposition process, consistent with its balanced mineralogical composition. The overall lower E a also supports its classification as a relatively easy-to-activate type. In contrast, SX-1 and XJ-1, which contain less kaolinite and are mainly composed of inert quartz and feldspar, exhibit lower and more stable E a values throughout the entire process. This comparison further verifies that the variation in kaolinite content among the samples is the primary factor controlling their activation behavior.

Overall, the kinetic results were in excellent agreement with the TG–DTG stage analysis. Stage 3–4 (kaolinite dehydroxylation and carbon oxidation) corresponded to lower activation energies, reflecting reaction-controlled processes, whereas Stage 5 was associated with higher activation energies, indicative of diffusion and structural rearrangement control. Combined with XRD and proximate analysis results, the comparative behavior of the four samples further confirmed that NX-1 and IM-1, which are kaolinite-rich, exhibited a clear reduction in E a during the main decomposition stages and can be fully activated at 750–800 °C with short holding times. This is mainly because the breakdown of kaolinite lattices and subsequent formation of amorphous metakaolin promote pore development and enhance gas/ion transport, thus reducing the apparent energy barrier. By contrast, XJ-1 and SX-1, characterized by high quartz content, exhibited consistently higher E a values with sharp increases at high conversion, confirming their classification as difficult-to-activate types that require higher calcination temperatures, longer residence times, or fluxing additives to achieve sufficient structural breakdown. This phenomenon can be attributed to the persistence of dense quartz–feldspar frameworks that hinder diffusion and structural collapse, resulting in a transition from reaction-controlled to diffusion-controlled regimes at high α. Therefore, the overall E a evolution clearly reflects how mineral composition governs activation behavior: quartz-rich samples show greater structural rigidity and diffusion limitation, whereas kaolinite-rich ones undergo easier amorphization and pore formation.

Acid Leaching Behavior Analysis

Based on the TG–DTG and kinetic analyses, it was demonstrated that different coal gangue samples exhibit significant variations in structural composition and thermal reactivity, which directly affect their subsequent acid leaching performance. Accordingly, the four gangue samples were further categorized into “easy-to-activate” types (NX-1 and IM-1) and “difficult-to-activate” types (XJ-1 and SX-1). To validate this classification, systematic leaching experiments were conducted using 3 mol L–1 nitric acid (HNO3) as the leaching agent, with a liquid-to-solid ratio of 5:1 (mL g–1), a reaction temperature of 90 °C, and a leaching time of 30 min. Figure presents the aluminum leaching efficiencies of the four samples at different calcination temperatures, and the results are discussed as follows.

Aluminum leaching efficiencies of different coal gangue samples under various calcination temperatures.

(1) Raw gangue stage

All samples exhibited low leaching efficiencies prior to calcination: 5.2% for XJ-1, 7.3% for SX-1, 12.7% for NX-1, and 10.6% for IM-1. These results indicate that, in the raw state, aluminum mainly exists in the form of stable crystalline phases such as kaolinite and quartz, tightly bound within a dense structure, and is therefore difficult to leach directly. NX-1 and IM-1 showed slightly higher initial leaching rates due to their relatively higher kaolinite contents, which provide more active sites for partial dissolution.

(2) Calcination at 700 °C

Upon calcination at 700 °C, the leaching efficiencies increased significantly, reaching 20.4% for XJ-1, 25.3% for SX-1, 35.2% for NX-1, and 34.3% for IM-1. The sharp increase in NX-1 and IM-1 indicates that kaolinite underwent substantial dehydroxylation at this temperature, forming amorphous metakaolin with enhanced reactivity. In contrast, the relatively quartz-rich XJ-1 and SX-1 exhibited lower improvements, with leaching efficiencies remaining below 30%.

(3) Calcination at 750 °C

At 750 °C, the leaching efficiencies continued to improve: 35.1% for XJ-1, 36.9% for SX-1, 46.8% for NX-1, and 45.3% for IM-1. The results confirm that NX-1 and IM-1 achieved leaching efficiencies exceeding 45%, demonstrating that kaolinite was almost completely dehydroxylated and converted into amorphous aluminosilicates, thereby enhancing leachability. Although XJ-1 and SX-1 also showed further improvements, their efficiencies remained below 37%, highlighting the inhibitory effect of quartz on aluminum release.

(4) Calcination at 800 °C

At 800 °C, all samples reached their maximum leaching efficiencies: 46.2% for XJ-1, 47.4% for SX-1, 62.3% for NX-1, and 59.3% for IM-1. NX-1 and IM-1 achieved more than 59% leaching efficiency, nearly double that at 700 °C, suggesting that kaolinite had been fully transformed into highly reactive metakaolin and that carbonaceous matter had combusted, exposing a porous structure favorable for acid penetration. XJ-1 and SX-1, however, exhibited only moderate improvements, with efficiencies remaining below 50%, consistent with their classification as difficult-to-activate types.

(5) Calcination at 850 °C

When the calcination temperature was raised to 850 °C, the leaching efficiencies of all samples declined, decreasing to 40.8% for XJ-1, 44.8% for SX-1, 60.3% for NX-1, and 55.8% for IM-1. The reduction is mainly attributed to the formation of mullite and other crystalline phases, which are thermodynamically stable and structurally dense, thereby inhibiting aluminum dissolution. This finding is consistent with the TG–DTG and kinetic analyses, which indicated that excessive calcination promotes structural recrystallization and reduces leaching activity.

The combined results confirm that NX-1 and IM-1, as kaolinite-rich samples, exhibit excellent leaching performance after calcination at ∼800 °C, achieving aluminum leaching efficiencies above 59%. By contrast, XJ-1 and SX-1, which are quartz-rich, remain difficult to activate, with efficiencies limited to below 50% even under optimum conditions. These findings demonstrate that an appropriate calcination window (750–800 °C) is critical for maximizing aluminum recovery, while overcalcination (>850 °C) induces recrystallization and phase transformation, leading to decreased leaching efficiency.

To further verify the mechanisms underlying the above leaching trends, XRD analyses of both calcined samples and acid leaching residues (Figure a,b) and SEM analyses were carried out. shown in Figure b, the leaching residue retained strong quartz reflections (PDF# 85–1053), whereas the characteristic peaks of kaolinite (PDF# 80–0886) were significantly weakened or disappeared, indicating that most aluminosilicate phases were effectively dissolved during acid extraction while quartz remained as the dominant inert phase. The disappearance of kaolinite reflections and the persistence of strong quartz peaks in the XRD patterns (Figure a,b), together with the porous textures observed for NX-1 and IM-1 and the dense quartz-dominated skeletons in SX-1 and XJ-1 (Figure ), provide direct structural and mineralogical evidence that explains the observed differences in leaching performance. These observations confirm that the stability of residual quartz and feldspar in the leaching residue accounts for their low reactivity and limited dissolution behavior. Specifically, the formation of amorphous metakaolin and pore development facilitated acid penetration in NX-1 and IM-1, whereas the dominance of inert quartz and compact morphologies limited the leachability of SX-1 and XJ-1. Overcalcination at 850 °C induced recrystallization and densification, further inhibiting aluminum dissolution. The XRD patterns of the leaching residues further validate the SEM observations, confirming that the residual mineral phasesparticularly quartz and feldsparplay a critical role in constraining the acid leaching efficiency due to their inertness and structural stability. These results corroborate the TG–DTG and kinetic analyses. Collectively, these results articulate a structure–phase–reactivity mapping in which kaolinite amorphization + pore development promote Al dissolution, whereas quartz persistence + recrystallization/densification hinder it.
