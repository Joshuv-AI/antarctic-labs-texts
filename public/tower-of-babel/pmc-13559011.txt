Spatial Omics in High‐Grade Gliomas: Mapping Immune–Tumor Niches for Precision Therapy
Authors: Saniyah Shaikh, Thaabit Raziq, Sulaf B Alfares, Touleen T Raslan, Ismail A Abdullah, Itika Arora, Shamsa Saleh, Mohammed Imran Khan, Ahmed Yaqinuddin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHigh‐grade gliomas (HGGs), particularly glioblastoma (GBM), remain among the most lethal human cancers despite decades of molecular profiling and therapeutic innovation. A primary reason for treatment failure is that HGG biology is spatial: malignant cell states, immune suppression, metabolic stress, and therapeutic resistance are organized into distinct anatomical and functional niches. Spatial omics technologies now enable high‐dimensional mapping of gene expression, protein signaling, immune architecture, and metabolic activity within intact tumor tissue. These approaches reveal how proneural and mesenchymal transcriptional states coexist yet localize to distinct regions, alongside hypoxic, invasive, and stem‐enriched niches. Spatial analyses show that key clinical determinants, including O6‐methylguanine‐DNA methyltransferase (MGMT)‐associated temozolomide resistance, radiotherapy tolerance in hypoxic regions, and immunotherapy failure driven by myeloid‐dominated immune exclusion, are influenced not only by molecular programs but also by cellular location. Beyond biological insight, spatial omics is reshaping clinical paradigms by enabling region‐specific patient stratification, early assessment of treatment response, and identification of therapy‐resistant reservoirs that seed recurrence. Prior bulk and single‐cell studies defined HGG cell states and pathways but often treated resistance as tumor‐wide. This review presents a spatially explicit framework that synthesizes spatial transcriptomic and immune‐profiling studies to identify tumor–immune niches and spatial bottlenecks that drive therapeutic failure and recurrence.Keywords: glioblastoma, high grade glioma, spatial biology, spatial omics

Clinical and Biological Context of High‐Grade Glioma (HGG)

High‐grade gliomas (HGGs), particularly glioblastoma (GBM, WHO grade 4), are the most common malignant primary brain tumors in adults and remain a leading cause of cancer‐related mortality in children and young adults. These tumors are aggressive, diffusely infiltrative, and universally fatal despite decades of molecular characterization and therapeutic innovation [1, 2, 3]. At the biological level, HGGs exhibit profound intratumoral heterogeneity across histologic, cellular, and molecular dimensions, encompassing diverse tumor cell states, stromal components, and immune infiltrates [1, 4, 5]. Genomic and transcriptomic profiling has revealed frequent alterations in oncogenic signaling pathways, including RAS/RAF/MAPK, PI3K/AKT/mTOR, and cell cycle regulators [1, 6]. The current standard of care, maximal safe surgical resection followed by radiotherapy with concomitant and adjuvant temozolomide (RT/TMZ), confers only modest survival benefit, with median survival limited to 12–15 months and 5‐year survival rates below 10% [2, 3, 7, 8]. Although this review focuses primarily on GBM as the most extensively characterized and clinically dominant WHO grade 4 glioma, several spatial, immune, and therapeutic resistance mechanisms discussed are broadly applicable across HGGs, including diffuse pediatric and adult HGG subtypes where indicated by the cited literature.

Spatial Organization of Tumor Cell States and Microenvironmental Niches

A defining feature of HGG biology is its highly structured spatial organization. Early bulk transcriptomic studies classified GBM into proneural (PN), mesenchymal (MES), and classical subtypes. However, single‐cell and single‐nucleus RNA‐sequencing have demonstrated that these transcriptional programs coexist within individual tumors as dynamic, interconvertible cellular states rather than as mutually exclusive lineages [9, 10, 11, 12]. PN‐like tumor cells, characterized by expression of OLIG2 and SOX2, exhibit higher proliferative capacity and reduced invasiveness. In contrast, MES‐like states, enriched for CD44, YKL40, and TGFB1, are associated with inflammatory signaling, immune infiltration, and therapeutic resistance [11, 12, 13].

Importantly, these tumor cell states are not randomly distributed. Instead, they exhibit strong spatial bias imposed by the local microenvironment. MES‐like programs are preferentially enriched within hypoxic, macrophage‐rich regions, while PN or oligodendrocyte progenitor‐like states are more commonly observed in well‐vascularized compartments [10, 12, 13]. Therapeutic intervention further accentuates this spatial patterning: RT/TMZ induces a PN‐to‐MES transition that is spatially constrained and strongly associated with radio resistance, recurrence, and poor clinical outcome [11, 12, 14]. These observations underscore that transcriptional identity in HGG is not solely genetically encoded but is actively instructed by local environmental cues.

Two microanatomical niches are central to this spatial logic. The perivascular niche, formed by intimate interactions between tumor cells, endothelial cells, and pericytes, supports stem‐like phenotypes with enhanced survival signaling and DNA damage repair capacity mediated through Notch, growth‐factor pathways, and nitric oxide (NO)‐dependent signaling. Importantly, NO exhibits context‐dependent, biphasic effects: at physiological endothelial‐derived levels it supports vascular signaling and DNA repair‐associated pathways, whereas higher or inducible NO levels can promote nitrosative stress, DNA damage, and apoptosis. This dual role is further modulated by hypoxic signaling within the vascular microenvironment. Cells residing in this niche are among the most resistant to cytotoxic therapy. In parallel, hypoxic and perinecrotic niches arise from aberrant angiogenesis and rapid tumor expansion. These regions enforce metabolic reprogramming through hypoxia‐inducible factor (HIF) signaling, promote glycolytic and lipid metabolic adaptations, and actively recruit immunosuppressive myeloid populations. Hypoxic tumor cells display slow‐cycling behavior, reduced radiosensitivity, and increased plasticity toward MES phenotypes [12, 13, 15, 16]. Together, perivascular and hypoxic niches function as complementary reservoirs of resistance and adaptation. At a larger anatomical scale, GBM exhibits a marked spatial dichotomy between the tumor core and the invasive edge. The core is characterized by high cellular density, disrupted vasculature, necrosis, hypoxia, and profound immune suppression.

In contrast, the invasive edge was comprised of diffusely infiltrating glioma cells that migrated along white‐matter tracts and perivascular routes into the surrounding normal‐appearing brain. These edge‐resident cells exhibit lower proliferation rates, altered metabolic programs, and relative protection behind an intact blood–brain barrier (BBB). This spatial organization can be conceptualized as an atlas of distinct microenvironments that impose specific tumor cell states and immune contexts (Figure 1). Clinically, this distinction is decisive: while therapy is primarily directed at the radiographically defined core, the invasive edge makes up the dominant reservoir for posttreatment recurrence [17]. Spatially Organized Therapeutic Bottlenecks in HGG

The persistent failure of HGG therapy reflects the spatial compartmentalization of resistance mechanisms rather than the absence of actionable molecular targets. One of the clearest examples is O6‐methylguanine‐DNA methyltransferase (MGMT) promoter methylation, a key biomarker for TMZ sensitivity. While MGMT methylation status is routinely assessed in bulk tumor samples, spatial analyses reveal substantial intratumoral heterogeneity, with TMZ‐sensitive and TMZ‐resistant subclones coexisting in distinct regions of the same tumor. Resistant compartments, often localized within hypoxic or perivascular niches, survive therapy and ultimately seed recurrence, a phenomenon obscured by bulk testing [10, 12, 18]. Radiotherapy resistance is similarly spatially constrained. Oxygen availability is a critical determinant of radiation efficacy, and hypoxic tumor compartments exhibit intrinsic radioresistance because of impaired fixation of DNA damage. In parallel, perivascular stem‐like niches activate robust DNA damage response programs that enable survival following RT/TMZ exposure. These niche‐specific resistance mechanisms operate concurrently, ensuring that no single therapeutic modality uniformly eradicates tumor cells across the tumor landscape [12, 13, 15].

Immunotherapy failure in HGG further reflects spatial immune compartmentalization. Despite substantial interest in immune checkpoint blockade, clinical responses have been minimal. Spatial profiling reveals that T cells are sparse and unevenly distributed, are frequently excluded from dense tumor cell regions, and are confined to perivascular, meningeal, or stromal‐adjacent interfaces. In contrast, hypoxic and mesenchymal niches are dominated by immunosuppressive monocyte‐derived macrophages that enforce T‐cell dysfunction and exhaustion through cytokine signaling and expression of checkpoint ligands. Consequently, bulk immune profiling systematically misrepresents the functional immune state of the tumor [15, 16, 18]. Finally, even when molecular targets are present, effective drug delivery is spatially inconsistent. Tumor vasculature is heterogeneously leaky and poorly perfused, limiting drug penetration into specific regions, while the invasive edge remains shielded by an intact BBB. As a result, intratumoral drug concentrations vary dramatically across spatial compartments, independent of target expression, constituting a largely underappreciated driver of pharmacologic failure in HGG [15, 18, 19].

Spatially Organized Immune Ecosystems in HGG

HGG, particularly GBM, exhibits profound immune dysfunction that cannot be adequately explained by bulk immune profiling or dissociative single‐cell approaches alone. Although single‐cell transcriptomics has clarified the diversity of immune cell states present in glioma, it fails to capture how these cells are spatially arranged relative to tumor structures, vasculature, necrosis, and one another. Spatial omics technologies, including spatial transcriptomics, multiplex immunofluorescence, and imaging mass cytometry, have revealed that immune suppression in glioma is not diffuse but instead organized into reproducible, anatomically defined immune ecosystems that strongly influence tumor evolution, immune escape, and therapeutic response [20]. These spatial immune ecosystems are shaped by gradients of hypoxia, vascular proximity, tumor cell state, and myeloid infiltration, resulting in compartmentalized immune phenotypes that coexist within the same tumor. Understanding how adaptive and innate immune cells are spatially distributed and how these distributions are constrained by tumor genotype and microenvironment is essential for interpreting immunotherapy failure and identifying spatially informed biomarkers.

Spatial Patterning of Adaptive Immune Dysfunction

Spatial transcriptomics integrated with single‐nucleus RNA sequencing demonstrates that T‐cell activity in glioblastoma follows sharp spatial gradients rather than uniform suppression. Perinecrotic and hypoxic regions are consistently associated with profoundly immunosuppressive environments, whereas perivascular niches retain relatively more pro‐inflammatory features [21]. These gradients shape distinct T‐cell fates across tumor regions.

Terminally exhausted T cells, characterized by high expression of PD‐1, TIM‐3, and the exhaustion‐stabilizing transcription factor TOX, are enriched in necrotic and hypoxic compartments. In contrast, progenitor‐like TCF1+ CD8+ T cells preferentially localize to perivascular regions, where suppressive cytokine signaling is attenuated, and antigen‐presenting interactions are more permissive [22]. This spatial segregation provides a mechanistic explanation for why immune checkpoint blockade often fails to reinvigorate antitumor immunity in glioma: therapeutically targetable T‐cell populations are confined to specific anatomical niches rather than broadly distributed.

The biological mechanisms underlying this spatial exhaustion are well established. Chronic antigen stimulation, combined with high local concentrations of immunosuppressive cytokines such as IL‐10 and TGF‐β, drives sustained expression of inhibitory receptors and stabilizes dysfunctional transcriptional programs in T cells [23]. Spatial imaging further reveals that exhausted T cells frequently neighbor IL‐10+ and PD‐L1+ myeloid cells within hypoxic compartments, indicating that local myeloid–T‐cell interactions reinforce exhaustion and shape responsiveness to immunotherapy [24, 25]. Thus, T‐cell dysfunction in glioma is best understood as a spatially enforced state rather than a global immune failure.

Myeloid Dominance and Hypoxia‐Driven Immune Suppression

Although T‐cell exhaustion represents a visible endpoint of immune dysfunction, spatial profiling consistently identifies tumor‐associated myeloid cells as the dominant immune population orchestrating suppressive niches in glioma. Brain‐resident microglia and infiltrating monocyte‐derived macrophages display distinct spatial distributions, ontogenies, and functional programs within tumors [26, 27]. Microglia preferentially localize to tumor margins and invasive edges, where they retain homeostatic markers such as TMEM119, P2RY12, and CX3CR1. In contrast, infiltrating macrophages cluster within hypoxic and perivascular cores and adopt immunosuppressive CD163+/CD206+, MHC‐II–low phenotypes associated with reduced antigen presentation and enhanced immune suppression [24, 28]. This spatial compartmentalization reflects both lineage origin and microenvironmental conditioning.

Recent spatial‐omics studies have further resolved niche‐specific myeloid programs. By integrating bulk RNA‐seq, single‐cell profiling, and Visium spatial transcriptomics, Hu et al. (2025) distinguished pseudo‐palisading perinecrotic regions from microvascular proliferation zones, revealing discrete endothelial, immune, and tumor states in each compartment [29]. These analyses identified MHC‐II–high antigen‐presenting hubs enriched for HLA‐DR+ microglia and macrophages, alongside IL‐10‐ and TGF‐β–rich immunosuppressive niches, highlighting the functional duality of myeloid cells within the same tumor [30]. Hypoxia acts as a central driver of myeloid reprogramming across these regions. Myeloid cells in hypoxic niches upregulate immunoregulatory programs and checkpoint ligands, reinforcing local immune suppression and limiting T‐cell infiltration. Integrative single‐cell and spatial analyses demonstrate that hypoxia‐associated tumor‐associated macrophage (TAM) states concentrate in necrotic cores and correlate with aggressive disease behavior [31]. Among these, MARCO+ macrophages have emerged as a mesenchymal‐associated, pro‐tumor subset linked to poor clinical outcomes, underscoring the role of metabolic stress in shaping immune dysfunction [32]. Hypoxic microenvironments activate hypoxia‐inducible factor (HIF) signaling, promoting angiogenesis, suppressing apoptosis, and enhancing DNA damage tolerance. Hypoxia also reduces sensitivity to radiotherapy and chemotherapy, creating sanctuaries for the most resilient tumor cells [33, 34]. These regions act as long‐term reservoirs of resistance and are strongly associated with recurrence.

Higher‐Order Spatial Immune Structures

Beyond individual immune cell states, spatial profiling has revealed higher‐order immune architectures in glioma that further modulate antitumor immunity. Large‐scale spatial analyses across hundreds of tumors have identified tertiary lymphoid structure (TLS)‐like aggregates in approximately 15% of gliomas [35]. Although these structures lack a fully developed germinal center organization, they contain clonal expansions of T and B cells, plasma cells expressing IgA or IgG, and dendritic cell–T‐cell interactions indicative of localized immune activity. Significantly, the presence and subtype of TLS‐like structures correlate with improved overall survival, suggesting that even immature or constrained adaptive immune organization may confer clinical benefit. However, TLS‐like aggregates in glioma are typically rare, spatially restricted, and often embedded within dense extracellular matrix rich in collagen and fibronectin, which may limit their functional maturation compared with TLS in other solid tumors [36].

Spatial immune profiling has also identified perivascular immune hubs characterized by coordinated interactions between macrophages, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and lymphocytes. These regions exhibit more inflammatory immune signatures and have been associated with prolonged survival in subsets of patients [37]. In recurrent glioblastoma, perivascular hubs exhibit further structural remodeling, suggesting that therapy reshapes rather than eradicates these immune compartments [16]. Emerging evidence additionally implicates the leptomeningeal compartment as a potential immune interface or reservoir in glioma. Although spatial‐omics characterization of this niche remains limited, early studies suggest that immune populations at meningeal boundaries may influence tumor–immune interactions and warrant further investigation [36].

Tumor‐Intrinsic Drivers of Spatial Immune Architecture

Microenvironmental factors do not solely impose spatial immune ecosystems in glioma; they are actively shaped by tumor genotype and cell state composition. Spatial transcriptomics integrated with snRNA‐seq demonstrates that tumor cell states, including neural progenitor‐like, oligodendrocyte progenitor‐like, astrocyte‐like, and MES‐like programs, segregate into discrete anatomical zones within the same tumor [21]. MES‐like tumor states preferentially localize to macrophage‐rich inflammatory regions, whereas necrosis‐adjacent compartments remain deeply immunosuppressive.

Microglia are preferentially enriched at tumor margins, where they interface with infiltrating glioma cells and contribute to early immune surveillance and perivascular‐associated remodeling. In contrast, bone marrow–derived macrophages dominate hypoxic, necrotic, and inflammatory tumor core regions, where they are recruited and sustained by tumor‐derived chemokines and cytokines. This spatial organization resolves previous ambiguity in MES–myeloid associations by linking MES‐like programs specifically to macrophage‐dominated core niches rather than microglia‐enriched margins. Tumor genetics further modulate immune niche formation. MES‐like tumor cells express ligands that promote macrophage recruitment and M2‐like polarization, reinforcing suppressive myeloid dominance [38]. Recurrent or mesenchymal‐shifted gliomas undergo extensive extracellular matrix remodeling, endothelial reprogramming, and hypoxia, collectively reshaping immune architecture [39]. Specific genetic alterations also exert direct immunomodulatory effects: EGFR amplification alters purinergic and adenosine signaling pathways that regulate macrophage recruitment, while PTEN loss reduces T‐cell infiltration and enhances production of immunosuppressive cytokines. Single‐cell studies further show that tumors harboring co‐amplification of EGFR and CDK4 are associated with increased CD163+ macrophage infiltration, directly linking genotype to spatial immune suppression [40]. Therapeutic Implications of Spatial Organization in HGG

The spatial architecture of HGG has direct and underappreciated implications for therapeutic response, resistance, and recurrence. Spatial profiling consistently demonstrates that gliomas are not composed of uniformly treatment‐sensitive or treatment‐resistant cells, but comprise coexisting spatial domains with distinct cellular compositions, signaling programs, and microenvironmental constraints. These domains differ markedly in their susceptibility to radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy, mechanistically explaining why standard treatments fail to achieve durable disease control.

Spatial Determinants of Response to Radiotherapy and TMZ

Spatial transcriptomic and proteomic analyses reveal that tumor cells are organized into discrete regions that resemble normal glial‐like states in some compartments. In contrast, others are dominated by MES tumor cells and monocyte/macrophage infiltration. These spatial domains are not merely descriptive; they strongly correlate with differential therapeutic sensitivity. MES and monocyte‐rich niches exhibit increased spatial heterogeneity, altered tumor cell state composition, and activation of stress‐response and inflammatory signaling programs. These features are consistently associated with more aggressive behavior and resistance to RT/TMZ [41].

In contrast, regions enriched for more differentiated or glial‐like tumor states exhibit greater treatment sensitivity, highlighting how spatial context modulates intrinsic tumor vulnerability. Among the most treatment‐refractory compartments is the hypoxic and perinecrotic niche. Hypoxia‐driven layers (often referred to as L1 regions) harbor immunosuppressive MES‐Hyp tumor states that correlate with poorer survival and increased likelihood of recurrence [42]. Hypoxia not only reduces radiosensitivity by limiting oxygen‐mediated fixation of DNA damage but also promotes tumor cell plasticity and survival programs that allow residual cells to repopulate the tumor after therapy. Spatial heterogeneity in driver gene expression further complicates therapeutic response. Regional variability in oncogenes and tumor suppressors, including EGFR and CDKN2B, implies that different tumor subregions may exhibit distinct sensitivities to RT/TMZ [43]. As a result, therapy may effectively debulk sensitive compartments while leaving resistant spatial reservoirs intact, seeding recurrence.

Spatial Constraints on Immunotherapy and Immune‐Targeted Strategies

Spatial omics has also clarified why immune‐based therapies, including immune checkpoint blockade, CAR‐T/CAR‐NK approaches, oncolytic viruses, and bi‐specific antibodies, have shown limited efficacy in GBM. The distribution of immunotherapy targets, immune infiltrates, and immunosuppressive signaling is highly compartmentalized rather than uniform [44]. Key immune‐modulatory molecules such as B7‐H3, STING, and VISTA are broadly expressed across spatial regions of interest, making them attractive candidates for immunotherapeutic targeting [43]. However, spatial analyses demonstrate that the functional impact of targeting these molecules depends on their local cellular and vascular context. Their expression often correlates with macrophage‐rich and vascular‐associated niches, suggesting that therapeutic efficacy may require simultaneous remodeling of the immunosuppressive microenvironment [44]. Spatial profiling further distinguishes intrinsic (tumor‐inherent) and extrinsic (MES) immune niches. Extrinsic, mesenchymal‐dominated regions are enriched with circulating immune cells, including monocytes and T cells, whereas intrinsic niches are dominated by resident microglia with limited antigen‐presenting capacity. Ligand–receptor analyses show that mesenchymal‐predominant regions are characterized by enhanced TGFB signaling and chemokine‐mediated recruitment of macrophages, reinforcing immune suppression and limiting cytotoxic T‐cell activity [41]. These findings suggest that immunotherapy failure in glioma is not due to the absence of targets, but rather to the spatial sequestration of immune cells within suppressive niches. Effective immune therapies will therefore likely require spatially informed combination strategies that both target immune checkpoints and disrupt local myeloid‐ and hypoxia‐driven suppression.

Spatial Biomarkers for Patient Stratification and Treatment Monitoring

A consistent theme emerging from spatial profiling studies is that spatially resolved tumor architectures can be quantified and leveraged as biomarkers to predict prognosis and guide therapy selection [44]. Baseline spatial mapping of protein and RNA markers, including B7‐H3, STING, and VISTA, can serve as companion diagnostics to inform immunotherapy eligibility and rational combination strategies [43]. Spatial dominance of MES lineage programs has been associated with chemotherapy resistance and early recurrence, supporting its use as a stratification marker for more intensive or alternative therapeutic regimens [39]. Similarly, high expression of invasive niche signatures, such as RG‐like cell programs (GCL_TI RG), identifies patients at elevated risk for aggressive invasion and may guide enrollment into trials targeting invasion‐associated pathways [20].

Spatial profiling can also be applied as a prescreening tool for specific resistance mechanisms. For example, Twist1 positivity detectable by scRNA‐seq or spatial assays has been associated with resistance to T‐cell–based therapies, suggesting that such patients may benefit from alternative immune‐modulatory approaches [45]. Quantification of hypoxic and necrotic compartments provides additional prognostic value. A higher proportion of layer‐1 hypoxic/perinecrotic regions reflects an aggressive, hypoxia‐driven phenotype and should be incorporated into risk stratification models to identify patients likely to exhibit treatment resistance [42]. These patients may preferentially benefit from hypoxia‐modulating therapies or strategies to improve oxygenation. Region‐specific gene expression signatures further refine prognostication. Overexpression of spatially enriched genes such as SPP1, IGF‐BP2, APOE, HIPK2, PPP1CB, S100A6, and CTSB has been linked to survival outcomes, emphasizing the importance of spatial context in biomarker interpretation [46]. Spatial technologies also enable early on‐treatment monitoring. Intraoperative fluorescence of 5‐ALA metabolite zones highlights immunoreactive margins beyond the bulk tumor and can guide targeted biopsy or re‐resection strategies to assess early therapeutic response [44]. Serial spatial profiling provides dynamic readouts of treatment efficacy, where reductions in proliferative “Prolif‐Metab” meta‐program regions indicate effective antiproliferative drug activity [42].

Spatial Constraints on Drug Delivery and Diffusion

Beyond cellular resistance mechanisms, spatial omics reveal critical physical and biochemical barriers to effective drug delivery in glioma. Quantitative spatial maps of endothelial phenotypes, metabolic gradients, vascular density, and cellular packing identify diffusion constraints that strongly influence therapeutic penetration [44]. One such constraint is the limited perfusion radius, approximately 160 μm, beyond which small‐molecule drugs and cellular therapies exhibit reduced penetration [42]. This requires careful consideration of drug properties and delivery strategies when targeting profoundly hypoxic or densely cellular regions.

Spatial analyses reveal that an inverse relationship between VEGFA expression and vascular density, indicating that regions with high angiogenic signaling may paradoxically exhibit poor functional perfusion due to aberrant vessel formation. In such contexts, anti‐VEGF strategies may normalize vasculature and improve drug diffusion rather than suppress angiogenesis [43]. The perivascular niche represents a dual‐function compartment: it serves as a conduit for immune‐cell trafficking while also acting as a barrier when endothelial cells adopt immunosuppressive phenotypes [41]. Although spatial omics does not directly measure blood–brain barrier integrity, vascular remodeling within the perivascular niche serves as an indirect indicator of altered permeability. Surrogate markers such as VEGFA and osteopontin (OPN), detectable via spatial profiling, may therefore inform regional BBB status and guide the selection of BBB‐penetrant therapies [44].

Edge‐to‐Core Dynamics and Adaptive Resistance in HGG

High‐grade gliomas are not static malignancies but dynamically strengthening ecosystems that continuously adapt under therapeutic pressure [47, 48, 49]. Rather than exhibiting uniform responses to treatment, different regions of the same tumor follow distinct evolutionary trajectories shaped by spatial context, clonal selection, epigenetic plasticity, and microenvironmental constraints [47, 50, 51, 52, 53, 54, 55]. Recurrence, therefore, reflects not simple therapeutic failure, but the survival and expansion of spatially protected, therapy‐resistant tumor populations [48, 49, 56].

Spatial Evolution Under Therapeutic Pressure

A defining feature of glioma evolution is the asymmetric response of tumor regions to therapy. Spatial transcriptomic and imaging studies consistently show that the invasive tumor edge and the tumor core differ markedly in cellular composition, metabolic state, and treatment susceptibility [47, 50, 51]. The invasive edge is typically enriched for metabolically active, proliferative tumor cells that appear relatively more responsive to radiotherapy and chemotherapy. In contrast, the tumor core contains hypoxic, necrotic, and perivascular compartments populated by slow‐cycling, stress‐adapted cells that are intrinsically more resistant to therapy [50, 51, 54]. Initial treatment, therefore, disproportionately eliminates edge‐associated populations, while core‐associated cells survive and subsequently repopulate the lesion [46, 47]. This spatially biased elimination demonstrates that treatment does not uniformly eradicate glioma cells but reshapes tumor architecture by selecting for preexisting resistant niches. Recurrence thus represents spatial reorganization rather than simple regrowth of the original tumor [47, 56].

Clonal Selection and Regional Expansion

Therapeutic intervention acts as a powerful selective filter on glioma cell populations. Although large fractions of tumor cells are destroyed, a subset of resistant clones persists. These surviving subclones expand clonally, often exhibiting enhanced aggressiveness, invasiveness, and resistance to subsequent therapy [48, 49]. Importantly, clonal selection is not spatially random. Resistant clones are preferentially retained within protective microenvironments such as hypoxic cores, perivascular niches, and immune‐excluded regions where local conditions buffer them from cytotoxic stress [54, 55]. As a result, recurrent gliomas are often genetically and functionally distinct from the primary tumor, reflecting the dominance of evolutionarily selected subclones rather than the linear progression of the original population [48, 49].

Epigenetic Plasticity as a Driver of Rapid Adaptation

Beyond genetic selection, epigenetic rewiring plays a central role in glioma adaptation under therapeutic stress. In high‐grade gliomas, treatment exposure has been shown to induce reversible shifts in DNA methylation landscapes, particularly involving global hypomethylation and focal promoter hypermethylation of tumor suppressor genes, contributing to altered transcriptional states and treatment escape. A well‐described example is the hypermethylation of the MGMT promoter, which modulates responsiveness to alkylating agents such as temozolomide and illustrates how methylation status can directly influence therapeutic sensitivity [53]. In addition, histone modification dynamics play a critical role in cellular state transitions. Alterations in histone acetylation and methylation, including changes mediated by EZH2‐driven H3K27 trimethylation, have been implicated in promoting stem‐like phenotypes and transcriptional repression of differentiation programs. These changes facilitate a shift toward glioma stem‐like cell states, which are associated with increased self‐renewal capacity and resistance to cytotoxic therapy [57].

Chromatin remodeling complexes, such as SWI/SNF (including ARID1A/SMARCA4‐associated regulation), further contribute to therapy‐induced plasticity by reshaping enhancer accessibility and enabling rapid transcriptional rewiring in response to environmental stress. In glioma, loss or functional impairment of SWI/SNF subunits such as ARID1A and SMARCA4 has been associated with increased chromatin compaction and transcriptional silencing, thereby promoting adaptive tumor cell state transitions and therapeutic resistance. Concurrently, glioma cells exhibit state transitions between proneural, classical, and mesenchymal‐like transcriptional programs, with therapy frequently enriching for a mesenchymal‐like state that is associated with invasiveness and treatment resistance [58].

Importantly, these epigenetic alterations are often reversible and do not require fixed genetic mutations, allowing tumor cells to dynamically switch phenotypes in response to fluctuating therapeutic pressures [53]. This epigenetic flexibility allows gliomas to repeatedly evade therapeutic pressure, contributing to phenotypic heterogeneity at recurrence [47]. In this context, recurrence represents not only the survival of resistant clones but also the emergence of epigenetically reprogrammed states optimized for persistence.

Adaptive Resistance Niches and Metabolic Gradients

Survival within gliomas is spatially structured rather than stochastic. Following therapy, resistant tumor cells reorganize into adaptive resistance niches, localized microenvironments that shield cells from continued therapeutic assault [54, 55]. These niches are reinforced by metabolic gradients that sustain survival under stress.

Lipid Metabolic Reprogramming

Under prolonged therapeutic pressure, glioma cells increasingly rely on lipid metabolism rather than glycolysis. Enhanced fatty acid oxidation supplies energy under nutrient limitation, while lipid droplet accumulation buffers reactive oxygen species. Concurrent remodeling of cholesterol synthesis and uptake supports membrane integrity and signaling under stress. This metabolic shift provides a durable escape mechanism that supports long‐term tumor persistence [59, 60].

Tryptophan–Kynurenine–Mediated Immune Evasion

Glioma cells also exploit metabolic pathways to suppress antitumor immunity. By activating the tryptophan–kynurenine pathway via IDO and TDO, tumor cells deplete local tryptophan and generate immunosuppressive kynurenine metabolites. These metabolites inhibit T‐cell activation and promote immune tolerance, allowing resistant niches to remain immunologically invisible [61, 62].

Integrated Model of Recurrence

Spatial heterogeneity, clonal selection, epigenetic adaptability, niche formation, and metabolic rewiring are not independent processes; they operate in concert to drive glioma recurrence [47, 56]. While therapy removes the most vulnerable populations, spatially protected regions reorganize, expand resistant clones, and adopt alternative metabolic and epigenetic states to dominate the tumor landscape [51, 56]. Gliomas, therefore, do not adapt through a single mechanism, but through the simultaneous deployment of multiple protective strategies across space and time. Understanding recurrence as a spatially driven evolutionary process reveals why single‐modality therapies fail. It highlights the need for treatment strategies that explicitly target resistant niches, metabolic dependencies, and adaptive plasticity [54, 55, 56]. Clinical Translation Roadmap

Translating spatial omics into routine clinical practice for HGG requires a systematic bench‐to‐bedside framework that ensures reliability, interpretability, and clinical utility. Spatial assays can inform patient selection, treatment monitoring, and biomarker‐driven trial stratification, but their adoption depends on standardized workflows, rapid turnaround, and integration into existing decision‐making frameworks [62, 63].

Specimen Handling and Assay Selection

High‐quality spatial data rely on optimized tissue procurement and processing, which begin with careful consideration of specimen type. Fresh frozen tissue preserves RNA integrity and is optimal for transcriptomics, whereas formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) tissue is more compatible with high‐plex proteomics and immunohistochemistry (IHC) [44, 63]. Equally important is tumor sampling to capture intratumoral heterogeneity, including the tumor core, invasive edge, hypoxic zones, and perivascular regions [44, 64]. However, obtaining representative samples from high‐grade gliomas presents important practical challenges. Many gliomas arise adjacent to deep brain structures, where aggressive sampling may increase the risk of neurological injury and limit access to spatially distinct tumor regions. As a result, tissue collection is often biased toward surgically accessible areas, potentially underrepresenting invasive margins or therapy‐resistant niches that contribute to recurrence. These limitations may be partially addressed through multimodal image‐guided sampling strategies, stereotactic navigation, integration of advanced MRI and intraoperative imaging, and coordinated sampling protocols involving neurosurgeons, neuropathologists, and molecular laboratories to maximize spatial representation while preserving patient safety.

Once adequate and representative tissue has been obtained, the selection of an appropriate spatial profiling platform becomes a critical determinant of downstream data quality and biological interpretation. The clinical question, sample throughput, and tissue‐preservation constraints should then guide the choice of analytical platform. Options include spatial transcriptomics, imaging mass cytometry, GeoMx, and CosMx. Throughout the workflow, rigorous pre‐analytical quality control is essential, as inadequate handling can cause biases from necrotic tissue, RNA degradation, or uneven staining, ultimately compromising the reliability and reproducibility of downstream spatial analyses [44, 63, 64, 65].

Analysis Turnaround and Reporting

Once tissue and platform selection are complete, data generation must be paired with standardized analytical pipelines to confirm reproducibility. Automated quality control, normalization, and deconvolution algorithms should be applied consistently across batches and platforms [66, 67]. The resulting analyses should be interpretable and actionable, highlighting clinically relevant features such as tumor niches, stem‐like reservoirs, and metabolic gradients in a format accessible to tumor boards [68, 69]. Analysis and reporting must also meet practical clinical timelines, ideally providing results within the window between surgical resection and initiation of adjuvant therapy around 1–2 weeks [70, 71]. To support reproducibility and clinical trust, reports should include sample origin, spatial resolution, cell type annotation, and confidence metrics [66, 67].

Regulatory and Economic Considerations

Beyond technical and analytical considerations, the adoption of spatial omics in routine neuro‐oncology practice also depends on regulatory approval, reimbursement, and cost‐effectiveness. Assays intended for clinical use must meet the Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA) and the College of American Pathologists (CAP) requirements for clinical diagnostics and demonstrate analytic, clinical, and utility validity [66, 71].

Reimbursement remains a challenge, as high‐plex spatial assays currently lack standardized billing codes. Therefore, integration will require alignment with existing pathology reimbursement frameworks. Demonstrating clinical and economic value is crucial. Spatial omics must improve patient stratification, reduce the use of ineffective therapies, or guide surgical planning to justify widespread implementation. Spatial omics may initially complement existing workflows as a decision‐support tool, targeting high‐risk or trial‐enrolled patients before adoption becomes feasible [71]. Overall, successful bench‐to‐bedside translation of spatial omics in HGG requires careful integration across tissue procurement, data generation, analysis, reporting, and clinical interpretation, ensuring that spatial insights are actionable, reproducible, and economically feasible within routine practice [66, 71]. Future Directions

The expanding capabilities of spatial omics promise transformative advances in the study and management of HGG. Key future directions include establishing routine spatial multi‐omics from clinical specimens, integrating spatial molecular maps with imaging and surgical navigation, and embedding spatial biomarkers in prospective clinical trials to guide therapy and monitor response.

Spatial Multi‐Omics at Single‐Cell Resolution in Routine FFPE

Recent technological progress demonstrates that spatial multi‐omics integrating transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics, and other modalities can overcome the limitations of dissociative single‐cell assays by preserving the spatial context of cells within tissue architecture. Such integrated approaches have been used to reconstruct detailed tissue atlases in cancer and other disease contexts [72, 73].

Applying these advancements to HGG, primarily to routinely available FFPE samples, would make spatial multi‐omics broadly accessible in clinical and diagnostic settings. Indeed, combining spatial transcriptomics with proteomic or metabolic approaches reveals that niches coexist and interact in situ [67, 74, 75]. Continued refinement of assay sensitivity, resolution, and reproducibility, along with robust computational frameworks, will be essential to make such single‐cell‐resolution spatial multi‐omics clinically actionable [66, 75].

Integration of Spatial Maps With Intraoperative Navigation and Radiomics

Another promising direction is the integration of spatial molecular maps with neuroimaging, radiomic data, and intraoperative navigation systems. Spatial omics can identify molecularly aggressive or therapy‐resistant regions (e.g., hypoxic, mesenchymal, or immunosuppressive niches). As shown in HGG, spatial transcriptomics can delineate region‐specific tumor cell states and immune landscapes. For instance, perinecrotic niches show immunosuppression, whereas perivascular zones are more pro‐inflammatory. By correlating these molecular niches with preoperative MRI or intraoperative imaging, surgeons might better target resections to remove high‐risk compartments while preserving normal tissue [76, 77, 78]. Moreover, radiomic features derived from imaging could be trained to predict underlying spatial molecular patterns, enabling noninvasive “virtual mapping” of tumor heterogeneity—a bridge between pathology and imaging‐based decision making. This integration could dramatically improve precision surgery and therapy planning [76, 79, 80].

Prospective Trials Embedding Spatial Biomarkers

To realize the clinical potential of spatial omics, prospective clinical trials should incorporate spatial biomarkers, defined from multi‐omic, spatially resolved data, either as stratifiers (selection of patients likely to benefit) or as surrogate/intermediate endpoints (to monitor therapeutic response or early failure). For example, spatial signatures identifying regions of invasive, metabolically active, or immunosuppressive tumor niches might guide targeted therapy, immunotherapy, or local therapies (e.g., localized drug delivery). Emerging data suggest that spatially resolved transcriptomic profiling of HGG reveals region‐specific tumor states and immune microenvironments. These insights could inform trial design and patient stratification [67, 74].

Standardizing spatial assay protocols, reporting formats, and data integration frameworks across institutions will be critical to ensure reproducibility and compatibility of spatial biomarkers in multicenter trials. The growing body of literature on spatial omics methods, as well as consensus‐building efforts in the field, provides a foundation for such standardization [66, 75, 80].

Overall, the future of spatial omics in HGG rests on translating cutting‐edge multi‐omic spatial profiling into clinically actionable diagnostics and therapeutics. Advancing routine single‐cell resolution spatial profiling (including FFPE), coupling molecular maps with imaging and surgical planning, and embedding spatial biomarkers in clinical trials have the potential to redefine prognostication, therapy planning, and personalized interventions in HGG, ultimately improving patient outcomes.

Taken together, these observations support a spatially organized model in which distinct glioma niches impose immune states, shape therapy‐specific resistance, and seed recurrence. Spatial niches drives immune states, therapy resistance, and recurrence in glioma. Spatial organization of glioma shapes immune states, therapy‐specific resistance, and patterns of recurrence. Glioma tumors are composed of distinct spatial niches, including the invasive edge, perivascular niche, and hypoxic core, each characterized by unique immune microenvironments. The invasive edge is associated with T cell exhaustion and immune exclusion, the perivascular niche functions as an immune‐privileged compartment enriched in stem‐like tumor cells, and the hypoxic core exhibits profound immunosuppression and limited therapeutic accessibility. These spatially defined immune states modulate exposure and response to standard therapies, including temozolomide, radiotherapy, immunotherapy, and anti‐angiogenic agents, leading to niche‐specific mechanisms of therapeutic failure. Resulting resistance programs such as enhanced DNA damage repair, metabolic adaptation, maintenance of stemness, and immune escape promote spatially seeded tumor evolution and recurrence. Mapping these interactions reveals actionable vulnerabilities, including niche‐targeted interventions, strategies to overcome immune exclusion, spatial biomarkers for patient stratification, and rational combination therapies informed by tumor geography.
