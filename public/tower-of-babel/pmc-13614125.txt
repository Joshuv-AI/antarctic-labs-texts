Polymeric Materials in Cancer Immunotherapy: Advances, Challenges, and Future Directions
Authors: Cong Wang, Jichuan Zhang, Jiayu Li, Kaiqi Fan, Shiqi Yin, Yong-Guang Yang, Wenbo Yao, Yuning Zhang, Tianmeng Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Despite revolutionary advances in tumor immunotherapy, its efficacy is often limited by antigen heterogeneity, immune exhaustion, and impaired immune cell infiltration caused by the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME). Polymer nanocarriers, leveraging their tunable physicochemical properties, modular architecture, and high design flexibility, offer an ideal platform to overcome these bottlenecks. They can not only protect immunotherapeutic agents (e.g., antigens, adjuvants, checkpoint inhibitors) and enable their targeted and controlled release but also enhance antigen presentation, reinvigorate effector immune cell functions, and reprogram the immunosuppressive network through spatiotemporally precise immune regulation. Furthermore, the versatility of polymer platforms empowers various cutting-edge therapeutic modalities, including cancer vaccines, adoptive cell therapies, and oncolytic viruses, demonstrating broad application potential. This review systematically explores the design principles and targeting strategies of polymer materials for tumor immunotherapy, their key roles in modulating anti-tumor immunity, and the core challenges in their clinical translation. Finally, this review envisions a new intelligent research paradigm driven by artificial intelligence (AI)-aided design and validated by clinically relevant systems such as humanized animal models to accelerate the development of next-generation polymer-based immunotherapies.

Introduction

Tumor immunotherapy, which mobilizes the patient’s own immune system to recognize and eliminate cancer cells, has become a revolutionary paradigm in oncology, achieving unprecedented clinical success in various malignancies, especially hematological cancers. − Core strategies, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), adoptive cell therapies (e.g., CAR-T), and therapeutic cancer vaccines, aim to break tumor-induced immune tolerance and restore or enhance the body’s intrinsic anti-tumor immunity. , However, a major obstacle to these strategies in treating solid tumors is the highly complex and dynamically evolving tumor microenvironment (TME).

Solid tumors are not mere masses of cancer cells but complex ecosystems. Within the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME), infiltrating immune cells (e.g., T cells, NK cells) are often functionally suppressed, while immunosuppressive cell populations (e.g., regulatory T cells (Tregs), myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), M2-type tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs)) are overactive, accompanied by abundant expression of immunosuppressive molecules (e.g., PD-L1, TGF-β). − This immunosuppressive state is not static but evolves dynamically through the phases of immune surveillance, immune editing, and ultimately immune escape. Consequently, it leads to defects in antigen presentation, T cell exhaustion, and the formation of physical/biochemical barriers, greatly limiting the efficacy of existing immunotherapies, with only a subset of patients achieving durable benefits. ,

This challenge exposes the inherent limitations of conventional immunotherapeutic agents: cytokines, nucleic acid drugs, all of which are prone to rapid clearance in vivo and lack tissue targeting. Even specific antibodies often suffer from low tumor accumulation and poor penetration into solid tumor barriers. , These agents generally fail to release their cargo at the right time and location to initiate an effective immune response cascade. Therefore, developing next-generation delivery platforms capable of precisely regulating immune responses has become key to advancing tumor immunotherapy.

In this context, polymer nanomaterials have emerged as ideal candidates for constructing intelligent immune delivery systems due to their exceptional designability and biocompatibility (Figure ). , By precisely tuning their chemical structure, size, surface charge, and functional groups, polymer nanocarriers can achieve prolonged circulation, passive targeting (via the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect), and active targeting (via surface ligands). More importantly, they can be engineered to respond to environmental stimuli (e.g., pH, enzymes, reactive oxygen species), enabling spatiotemporal controlled release of drugs at the tumor site.

Functional classification of polymeric nanoparticles for immunotherapy. Polymeric nanoparticles are categorized according to dominant immunological functions, including classical delivery systems, stimuli-responsive micelles, immunoactive polymers, nucleic acid carriers, and biomimetic platforms.

The role of polymer nanoplatforms in immunotherapy extends far beyond simple delivery. Co-delivering antigens and adjuvants, they efficiently drain to lymph nodes to synergistically activate dendritic cells, thereby robustly priming T cell responses. Loaded with small molecule inhibitors or nucleic acids, these platforms specifically deplete or reprogram immunosuppressive cells such as TAMs and MDSCs, converting cold tumors into hot ones. They also protect and deliver oncolytic viruses, engineered cytokines, or even genetic instructions for CAR-T/NK cellsoffering powerful tools for combination therapies and personalized medicine. − These unique advantages position polymer nanocarriers as a driving force propelling tumor immunotherapy forward.

This review systematically outlines the latest advances and design strategies for polymer materials in tumor immunotherapy. We first analyze the immunosuppressive traits of the TME and the resistance mechanisms that limit current therapies. Next, we highlight cutting-edge applications of polymer nanocarriers in targeted delivery and immune cell function modulation. The review then explores how these platforms enable emerging therapeutic modalities such as cancer vaccines, cell therapies, and oncolytic viruses. Finally, the review will delve into the core challenges for clinical translation and envision a new intelligent research paradigm integrating AI-driven design with validation in clinically relevant models.

Evolution of the TME and Challenges for Immunotherapy

With the deepening understanding of the nature of cancer, tumors are no longer simply viewed as local proliferations driven by oncogenes but as complex ecosystems that continuously interact with the host immune system. The TME, particularly its core component, the TIME, is a dynamically evolving regulatory hub encompassing infiltrating immune cells, immunosuppressive cells, and various signaling molecules. It plays a decisive role in tumor initiation, progression, and therapeutic response. , The state of the TIME is not static but follows the classic evolutionary path from immune surveillance to immune editing and ultimately to immune escape. , This dynamic process ultimately shapes a highly immunosuppressive local environment, which is the root cause of resistance faced by most current immunotherapeutic strategies (Figure ).

Immune Surveillance, Equilibrium, and Escape

The interaction between tumors and the immune system is often summarized as three consecutive “E” stages: Elimination (immune surveillance), Equilibrium, and Escape. This model clearly reveals the dual role of the immune system in tumorigenesis and provides a framework for understanding how the TIME shifts from an immunologically active state to deep suppression.

During the immune surveillance stage, innate and adaptive immune systems work synergistically to form the first line of defense against abnormal cells. Natural killer (NK) cells recognize and eliminate tumor precursor cells with downregulated MHC-I expression via the missing-self mechanism. Simultaneously, antigen-presenting cells (APCs) such as dendritic cells capture tumor-associated antigens, activating antigen-specific CD8+ T cells and initiating cytotoxic responses.

When the immune system fails to completely eliminate all tumor cells, both sides enter a dynamic stage of immune equilibrium. This phase is characterized by a tug-of-war between immune-mediated killing and tumor cell survival. The immune system continuously clears highly immunogenic tumor clones but cannot eradicate all cells, leading to the selection and survival of subclones with low immunogenicity or initial escape capabilities. This stage may correspond to a state of long-term latency or micrometastasis in the clinic.

Under persistent immune selection pressure, tumor cells continuously accumulate escape mechanisms, ultimately breaking through control and entering the immune escape stage. At this point, tumor cells employ intrinsic mechanisms to weaken immune recognition, such as downregulating MHC-I molecules, antigen-processing proteins (e.g., TAP1/2), or overexpressing don’t eat me signal ligands like PD-L1 and CD47, directly inhibiting the function of T cells, NK cells, and macrophages. On the other hand, and more crucially, tumors actively shape a systemic immunosuppressive microenvironment. They recruit and educate large numbers of Tregs, MDSCs, and TAMs. These cells secrete a plethora of inhibitory factors such as TGF-β, IL-10, and IDO, constructing a powerful immunosuppressive network that severely hampers the infiltration, activation, and cytotoxic functions of effector immune cells through both physical barriers and signal inhibition, thereby allowing uncontrolled tumor growth. −

Limitations of Traditional Immunotherapies in the TME

In recent years, immunotherapy has become a revolutionary paradigm in oncology. Its strategies can be categorized into active immunotherapies aimed at activating the host immune system (e.g., cancer vaccines, ICIs) and passive immunotherapies that directly provide effector components (e.g., therapeutic antibodies, CAR-T cells, cytokines). However, the clinical efficacy of these strategies in solid tumors is often suboptimal, and the core obstacle is precisely the dynamically evolving and highly immunosuppressive TIME described above. ,

For active immunotherapies, success depends on efficient antigen presentation and the robust priming and expansion of T cells. However, the function of antigen-presenting cells within the TIME is often compromised, and free peptide, protein, or mRNA antigens are highly susceptible to degradation in vivo, making it difficult to provide sustained and sufficient immune stimulation. More critically, even if T cells are successfully activated, these effector cells, when attempting to infiltrate the tumor, immediately encounter the immunosuppressive fortress composed of Tregs, MDSCs, and inhibitory cytokines, rapidly entering an exhausted state and failing to exert persistent killing effects.

Passive immunotherapies face different challenges. For example, adoptively transferred CAR-T or TCR-T cells, upon entering the solid tumor TIME, not only face direct attack from the aforementioned immunosuppressive signals but also encounter a metabolically abnormal environment (e.g., hypoxia, glucose deprivation, lactate accumulation). This leads to impaired mitochondrial function, energy exhaustion, and a rapid decline in proliferative capacity and cytotoxicity. Similarly, systemically administered immune checkpoint inhibitor antibodies or cytokines also suffer from low tumor tissue accumulation and poor penetration. Moreover, systemic administration can easily cause severe off-target toxicity, limiting their applicable doses and efficacy.

In summary, the evolved TIME establishes multiple physical, biochemical, and immune barriers, weakening or severing the therapeutic chain for both active immunotherapies that require endogenous initiation and passive immunotherapies that rely on exogenous input. Therefore, developing novel technological platforms capable of breaking through these barriers and achieving spatiotemporally precise delivery and release of immunomodulators has become key to advancing immunotherapy. Polymer nanocarriers, with their customizable physicochemical properties, good biocompatibility, and potential for multifunctional integration, offer a highly promising solution for intelligent delivery and reshaping the TIME. They not only protect drugs and enable targeted delivery but can also synergistically act across multiple levels (overcoming immunosuppression, modulating cellular metabolism, and activating local immunity) through responsive release and co-delivery of multiple agents, opening new avenues for reversing immune escape and enhancing the efficacy of existing therapies.

Targeting Strategies of Polymer Nanocarriers for Antitumor Immunity

The primary step for polymer nanocarriers to overcome obstacles in tumor immunotherapy is achieving precise spatial localization. Through rational design of their physicochemical properties, polymer carriers can efficiently deliver immunomodulators to specific sites of action (e.g., tumor tissue or lymphoid organs), laying the groundwork for subsequent immune activation.

Targeting Tumor Tissue and Tumor-Associated Vasculature

Effective accumulation of polymer nanocarriers in tumor tissue is a prerequisite for their immunomodulatory function. Passive targeting primarily relies on the EPR effect, while surface PEGylation can effectively reduce uptake by the reticuloendothelial system, significantly prolonging the circulation time of carriers and establishing a foundation for passive targeting. ,

Building upon passive targeting, active targeting strategies can further enhance the selectivity of carriers for specific cells. One of the most common strategies involves modifying ligands that target receptors overexpressed on tumor cell surfaces (e.g., folate, RGD peptides). For example, modifying ligands targeting EGFR or PD-L1 allows polymer nanoparticles to significantly increase uptake by tumor cells, thereby more effectively inducing immunogenic cell death (ICD) and releasing endogenous tumor antigens, providing fuel for initiating T cell immunity. −

More importantly, the active targeting capability of polymer carriers can be extended to immune cells within the TME. By displaying specific ligands on their surface that target dendritic cells, TAMs, or MDSCs (e.g., mannose, specific antibody fragments), polymer carriers can precisely deliver immunomodulatory drugs (e.g., agonists or inhibitors) to specific immune cell subsets, enabling local immune activation or reprogramming of immunosuppressive cells. This offers the possibility of fundamentally reversing the immunosuppressive microenvironment. −

Targeting Secondary Lymphoid Organs (Lymph Nodes, Spleen)

Lymph nodes and the spleen are critical sites for initiating adaptive immune responses. Efficient delivery of antigens and adjuvants to these organs is essential for the success of cancer vaccines. Through rational physicochemical design, polymer nanocarriers can optimize their accumulation in lymphoid organs. Studies show that precisely controlling the size of nanocarriers within the 10–100 nm range and optimizing their surface charge can significantly enhance their drainage efficiency from the injection site to draining lymph nodes and their retention within the nodes. Commonly used polymer nanocarriers like PLGA, PEG–PLGA, and PBAE benefit from this design principle. −

Beyond physicochemical optimization, surface functionalization is key to further enhancing targeting and uptake efficiency. For instance, modifying the carrier surface with glycosylated molecules that bind to high endothelial venules in lymph nodes, or with ligands recognized by C-type lectin receptors (e.g., the mannose receptor) on APCs (e.g., mannose), can actively promote carrier capture by DCs and other APCs. This active targeting strategy significantly enhances antigen cross-presentation, robustly inducing the activation and proliferation of CD8+ T cells. − Furthermore, some polymer materials themselves (e.g., chitosan and PBAE) have been shown to possess intrinsic immunoadjuvant properties, capable of activating signaling pathways such as Toll-like receptors or cGAS-STING, synergistically enhancing vaccine-induced immune responses and achieving the functional integration of material as adjuvant.

Polymer-Nanocarrier-Mediated Regulation of Immune Cell Functions

In cancer immunotherapy, effectively activating APCs, enhancing the functions of effector immune cells like T cells and NK cells, while simultaneously inhibiting or reprogramming immunosuppressive cells such as TAMs, MDSCs, and Tregs is crucial for determining the success of anti-tumor immune responses. However, traditional free immunomodulators (e.g., PRR agonists, monoclonal antibodies) commonly suffer from poor targeting, low bioavailability, and high systemic toxicity. , Polymer nanocarriers, with their precisely tunable size, morphology, and surface chemistry, provide an ideal platform for achieving targeted delivery, controlled release, and multipathway synergistic activation of immunomodulators, making them a research hotspot for overcoming the limitations of traditional strategies (Figure ).

Functional mechanisms of polymer-nanocarrier-mediated immunomodulation. Polymer nanocarriers reshape antitumor immunity through three interconnected mechanisms. First, they enable targeted delivery of immunomodulatory cargos to tumors, lymphoid organs, or specific immune cell subsets. Second, they enhance immune cell functions, including activation of antigen-presenting cells and reinvigoration of effector T cells and NK cells. Third, they reprogram immunosuppressive components within the TME, such as TAMs, MDSCs, and Tregs. Through controlled release, co-delivery, and spatiotemporally precise immune regulation, these platforms help restore effective antitumor immune responses.

Antigen-Presenting Cells (DCs, Macrophages): Activation and Functional Enhancement

APCs are the core for initiating adaptive immunity. They perceive tumor-related signals through pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) such as Toll-like receptors (TLRs), cGAS-STING, and RIG-I, subsequently processing and presenting antigens to activate T cells. − However, free PRR agonists suffer from shortcomings such as poor stability, weak targeting, and high toxicity. , Polymer nanocarriers not only protect and co-deliver antigens and adjuvants, improving APC uptake efficiency, but can also impart adjuvant functions through material design itself, enabling safe and efficient synergistic immune activation. ,,

Synergistic Activation of TLR Pathways

The TLR pathway plays a central role in APC activation. An innovative nanovaccine developed by Song et al. demonstrates the superiority of multimechanism synergistic activation. This design uses mannose-modified PLA–PEI pathogen-mimicking nanoparticles that co-load protein antigens and the TLR9 agonist CpG via electrostatic interactions, yielding MPVax-CpG/OVA particles with a particle size of 110 ± 20 nm and a zeta potential of 11 ± 2 mV. The sophistication of this design lies in its multilevel activation mechanisms: first, mannose modification promotes lymph node targeting and specific uptake by CD8+ DCs, while mannose itself can act as a TLR4 agonist; second, the PEI core effectively promotes endosomal escape of the antigen, significantly enhancing cross-presentation; finally, CpG and mannose synergistically activate TLR9 and TLR4 signaling pathways. This multipronged strategy achieved significant results in a B16 melanoma model, with single-agent treatment leading to 50% complete remission (Figure A). Similarly, Jeong et al. utilized mannose-modified PLGA nanoparticles to deliver the TLR7/8 agonist R848, successfully achieving specific targeting of TAMs and tumor-infiltrating dendritic cells. Notably, this nanosystem not only promoted DC maturation but, more importantly, reprogrammed pro-tumor M2-type TAMs toward an antitumor M1 phenotype, effectively enhancing the infiltration of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in a B16F10 melanoma model, demonstrating dual regulatory functions (Figure B).

Efficient Activation of the cGAS-STING Pathway

The cGAS-STING pathway holds a key position in antitumor immunity, but cytosolic delivery of STING agonists has been a technical bottleneck. A chitosan-manganese (CS-Mn) microparticle system developed by Liu et al. offers an innovative solution. This design leverages Mn2+ as a natural cofactor to synergistically activate the cGAS-STING pathway while enabling efficient DNA delivery. The resulting CS-Mn microparticles exhibit a mean hydrated diameter of 846.4 nm and a surface charge of 25.8 ± 0.59 mV, as determined by Malvern Zetasizer analysis. Mechanistically, CS-Mn particles selectively bind DNA and rapidly degrade under physiological conditions to form positively charged DNA assemblies, facilitating efficient cytosolic delivery of DNA to DCs. In vivo experiments demonstrated that this system significantly enhanced the inhibitory effect of radiotherapy on both primary and distant tumors and promoted the generation of stem cell-like tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells, providing a new approach for combination therapy. Furthermore, breakthroughs have been made in polymer design aimed at achieving material as adjuvant. The ultra-pH-responsive polycarbonate copolymer PC7A developed by Gao et al. is itself an efficient activator of the STING pathway. The nanovaccine constructed from it showed potent antitumor activity in multiple tumor models. Wilson et al. employed an albumin hitchhiking strategy, conjugating the STING agonist diABZI to an antialbumin nanobody, significantly prolonging its circulation half-life and enhancing tumor accumulation, effectively activating innate and adaptive immunity.

RIG-I Pathway and Other Synergistic Activation Strategies

RIG-I pathway agonists like 5′-triphosphate RNA (3pRNA) are easily degraded, limiting their application. Wilson et al. systematically screened a library of PEG-block-(DMAEMA-co-AnMA) copolymers, optimizing lead carriers capable of efficiently encapsulating 3pRNA, enhancing endosomal escape, and boosting immunostimulatory activity, successfully activating the RIG-I pathway in vivo. Building on this, Zhang et al. designed a co-delivery system based on polymer aluminum hydroxide nanoparticles (NP–3pRNA–CpG) for delivering ovalbumin (OVA) antigen along with two adjuvants: CpG oligodeoxynucleotides (CpG-ODN) and 3pRNA. This nanovaccine efficiently targets lymph nodes and is internalized by APCs, simultaneously activating TLR9 and RIG-I signaling pathways. This enhances antigen cross-presentation, induces robust humoral and cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) responses, and promotes IFN-γ secretion. Consequently, this formulation effectively inhibited B16-OVA tumor growth and significantly prolonged the survival of tumor-bearing mice.

Critical Physicochemical Parameters Dictating Pathway-Specific Activation

To guide rational design, it is essential to clarify the distinct subcellular demands of each pathway and then tune the key physicochemical parameters accordingly. Specifically, TLR7/8/9 are located in endosomes or lysosomes, so strong endosomal escape is not required. In contrast, STING resides on the ER membrane in the cytosol, which demands efficient endosomal escape (e.g., via proton sponge or pH-responsive mechanisms). RIG-I is also located in the cytosol, and its RNA agonists are highly susceptible to nuclease degradation, requiring dense polymer shielding and optimized N/P ratios. These biological differences impose distinct requirements on key physicochemical properties such as particle size (optimal 20–100 nm for lymph node drainage), − , surface charge (slightly positive or mannose-modified for DC uptake), and endosomal escape efficiency. For instance, when designing a nanoparticle that delivers a hydrophilic STING agonist (e.g., cGAMP), the primary focus must be on achieving strong endosomal escape, often using cationic polymers such as PEI or chitosan, and on stabilizing the agonist via cation complexation (e.g., with Mn2+). In contrast, when delivering a hydrophobic TLR7/8 agonist (e.g., R848), the priority shifts to efficient encapsulation in a hydrophobic polymer core (e.g., PLGA or PCL) and sustained presentation to endosomal TLRs, with minimal need for endosomal escape. Both systems should maintain a size of 20–100 nm and appropriate surface modification for DC targeting, but the core design strategy diverges fundamentally based on the agonist’s hydrophilicity and the pathway’s subcellular location.

Effector Immune Cells (T Cells, NK Cells): In Vivo Activation and Checkpoint Regulation

Immune Checkpoint Blockade and Combination Strategies

Although immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy has achieved breakthroughs, it still faces challenges such as limited response rates, resistance, and systemic toxicity. − Polymer nanocarriers offer solutions to enhance the precision and safety of ICB by improving pharmacokinetics, enabling stimuli-responsive release, and developing novel molecular formats.

Improving antibody pharmacokinetics is one of the most direct strategies (Table ). Abdi et al. developed poly­(lactic-co-glycolic acid)-methoxy poly­(ethylene glycol) (PLGA–mPEG) based nanoparticles for the targeted delivery of anti-PD-1 antibody. These nanoparticles preferentially accumulated in the spleen, which is rich in APCs, and were efficiently internalized by DCs, promoting their maturation and enhancing T cell activation. In the B16–F10 melanoma model, this nanoplatform achieved potent antitumor efficacy at low antibody doses while avoiding the lethal immune toxicity associated with high doses of free antibody. Similarly, Chen et al. designed αPDL1–RGD nanoparticles (αPDL1–RGD NPs) composed of poly­(l-glutamic acid) (PLG) backbone, RGD peptide, and PD-L1 antibody. Multivalent RGD peptides exhibit high-affinity binding to integrins, promoting the internalization of PD-L1–integrin complexes and their subsequent lysosomal degradation (Figure A). Experimental results indicated that αPDL1–RGD NPs inhibited PD-L1 membrane recycling 2.5 times more efficiently than free antibody, highlighting the advantage of nanocarrier-mediated targeted delivery in enhancing the precision and effectiveness of ICB therapy.

The development of novel molecular formats provides more diverse tools for immune checkpoint modulation. The super bispecific nanoantibody (S-BsNA) based on polystyrene nanoparticles, developed by Wang et al. co-immobilized anti-KLRG1 and anti-PD-L1 antibodies, effectively shortening the physical distance between immune cells and tumor cells. This significantly enhanced the cytolytic activity of NK cells and the phagocytic capacity of macrophages. Evaluation in tumor models showed that S-BsNA treatment was significantly more effective than traditional antibody combinations, demonstrating the great potential of novel molecular formats in enhancing antitumor immune responses. The trispecific nanoantibody (Tri-NAb) further developed by the same team represents a more advanced immune regulation strategy. This platform uses albumin/polyester nanoparticles as carriers to co-immobilize anti-PD-L1, anti-4-1BB, and anti-NKG2A antibodies, achieving multidimensional immune synergistic activation: blocking PD-L1 signaling to relieve immunosuppression, activating the T cell co-stimulatory receptor 4-1BB to enhance effector function, while inhibiting the negative regulation of NK cells to boost innate cytotoxicity. This multimechanism synergistic strategy exhibited potent antitumor activity in multiple tumor models.

Stimuli-responsive release systems further enable spatiotemporally precise drug control. The MMP-2/pH dual-responsive sAMcP micelle designed by Shuai et al. is a representative advance in this direction. The release mechanism of this system involves precise sequential control: after accumulating in tumor tissue via the EPR effect, the PEG shell detaches first in the slightly acidic TME (pH ≈ 6.5), triggering the release of anti-PD-1 antibody. Upon internalization by tumor cells, further dissociation occurs in the more acidic lysosomal environment (pH ≈ 5.0), leading to controlled release of paclitaxel (PTX). This cascade release mechanism effectively induces ICD and blocks tumor immune escape, synergistically inhibiting tumor growth while significantly reducing systemic toxicity. The CUR@PPC−αPD-1 dual-pH-responsive platform developed by the same team demonstrates an even more complex targeting and release mechanism. This nanoplatform specifically binds to circulating PD-1+ T cells and is transported along with infiltrating lymphocytes to the tumor site. Under the slightly acidic TME conditions, cleavage of the CDM linker releases the αPD-1 antibody to block the PD-1/PD-L1 axis, while the nanoparticle surface charge reverses from negative to positive, significantly enhancing uptake by tumor cells and TAMs. Subsequently, curcumin (CUR) is triggered for release in the acidic lysosomal environment, inhibiting the NF-κB signaling pathway to reduce the secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines, achieving a synergistic therapeutic effect of NF-κB inhibition combined with PD-1 blockade.

The application of nucleic acid drugs opens new avenues for checkpoint regulation at the genetic level. siRNA can silence checkpoint molecules at the gene level (Table ). A pH-responsive PDDT micelle designed by Huang et al. efficiently encapsulated and delivered siPD-L1, effectively restoring immune surveillance in vivo. To overcome the limited efficacy of single-target approaches, combination targeting strategies have gained attention. Lanxia Liu et al. constructed hybrid spherical nucleic acid nanoparticles (hSNPs) capable of simultaneously delivering a CTLA-4 aptamer and siPD-1, eliciting a stronger immune response than single-target blockade in a melanoma model. Similarly, Ahn et al. utilized PLGA nanoparticles to co-deliver siPD-1 and siPD-L1, simultaneously silencing checkpoints on both T cells and tumor cells, with effects superior to single-gene silencing strategies.

Polymeric Nanodelivery Systems for siRNA-Based Immune Checkpoint Blockade Therapy

To overcome the limitations of ICB therapy alone, researchers have explored combination delivery strategies integrating immune checkpoint modulation with complementary therapeutic approaches. This multimodal delivery system can synergistically reshape the TIME and significantly enhance the antitumor activity of effector immune cells.

Chemotherapeutic drugs can induce ICD in tumor cells, leading to the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) such as calreticulin and HMGB1, thereby establishing an immunogenic foundation for ICB therapy. Polymer nanocarriers enable precise co-delivery of chemotherapeutic drugs and immune checkpoint modulators, synergistically enhancing antitumor immunity while minimizing systemic toxicity. An oxidation-reduction responsive micelle system (A2-APM) based on poly­(ethylene glycol)-poly­(l-lysine), modified with angiopep-2 peptide on the surface to enhance penetration across the blood-tumor barrier (BTB). This platform covalently conjugated anti-PD-L1 antibody and electrostatically bound PTX, enabling tumor-specific drug release triggered by elevated glutathione (GSH) levels in the TME. PTX induced ICD to promote DC maturation, while αPD-L1 alleviated T cell suppression. In a glioblastoma (GBM) model, this system effectively inhibited the growth of primary and recurrent tumors and induced durable immune memory. The team led by Wang et al. has developed an injectable nanocomposite hydrogel formed via reversible Schiff base crosslinking between carboxymethyl chitosan (CMCS) and poly­(lactic acid)-hyperbranched polyglycerol-based bioadhesive nanoparticles (BNPs). This system enables the intraperitoneal co-delivery of the chemotherapeutic drug PTX and the immune checkpoint inhibitor anti-PD-1 antibody (αPD-1), where PTX is loaded into the hydrophobic core of BNPs and αPD-1 is embedded in the CMCS network. Endowed with injectability, self-healing ability, and biocompatibility, the hydrogel can prolong the retention time of drugs in the peritoneal cavity and achieve sustained release. In a mouse model of ovarian cancer with peritoneal metastasis, it significantly suppresses tumor metastasis, enhances T cell infiltration and activation, without inducing systemic toxicity. This work provides an efficient delivery platform for the chemoimmunotherapy of peritoneal metastatic cancers. Collectively, these studies show that polymer-based co-delivery systems can integrate the ICD-inducing properties of chemotherapy with the immune-activating effects of ICB, providing an effective strategy for converting cold tumors into hot ones and improving therapeutic responsiveness. −

Combination therapy sometimes achieves better outcomes. For example, Wang and colleagues developed a polymer prodrug-based nanoplatform. Its surface is modified with anti-PD-L1 antibody for better tumor targeting, and its core is loaded with the STING agonist diABZI. This system integrates chemotherapy, immune checkpoint blockade, and innate immune activation into a single platform. In this design, the chemotherapeutic agent GEM upregulates PD-L1 expression on tumor cells, creating more targets for immune checkpoint blockade. The anti-PD-L1 antibody then blocks the PD-1/PD-L1 axis and restores T cell effector function. Meanwhile, diABZI activates the STING pathway, inducing type I interferon production and amplifying innate immune signals. This synergistic combination significantly enhances tumor immunogenicity, effectively converting “cold” tumors into “hot” ones, and achieves potent antitumor and antimetastatic efficacy in various animal models. However, such synergistic strategies place high demands on the material design. To achieve a good therapeutic effect, one must precisely control the ratio of the combined components and their order of release.

Two quantitative considerations are essential for optimizing combination therapy. First, the drug ratio matters. Synergistic effects often occur only within a narrow ratio window, and polymer carriers can achieve precise control through chemical conjugation or by adjusting the feed ratio. It is advisible to pre-screen using combination index methods, such as the Chou-Talalay method, to identify the optimal ratio. Second, the order of release can be more important than simultaneous delivery. Several studies have shown that sequential release, for example releasing an ICD inducer first to prime the immune system and then an immune checkpoint blocker to sustain T cell activity, achieves better outcomes than releasing both agents at the same time. As demonstrated by the dual-sensitive micelle system discussed earlier, a well-designed release sequence can enhance CD8+ T cell infiltration and reduce regulatory T cells compared to simultaneous release. To realize such timing, one can tune polymer degradation kinetics by adjusting the molecular weight, hydrophobicity, or the lactide/glycolide ratio in PLGA. Other effective strategies include using multiple-stimuli-responsive linkers (for example, one payload responding to MMP-2 and another to pH) and core-shell architectures where the shell releases its cargo faster than the core. These principles help move the field from empirical co-delivery toward precisely programmed immunotherapy.

Cytokine Delivery and Local Immune Modulation

Cytokines are key signaling molecules regulating the proliferation and activity of effector immune cells such as T cells and natural killer (NK) cells. Among them, pro-inflammatory factors like Interleukin-2 (IL-2), IL-12, and chemokines such as CCL-25 can inhibit tumor cell proliferation and metastasis by promoting cytotoxic immune responses. However, cytokine therapy is limited by short half-life and severe systemic toxicity. Polymer nanocarriers enable the localized, controlled release delivery of cytokines, significantly reducing toxicity while improving efficacy.

IL-2 is a core cytokine coordinating the activation of effector immune cells, regulating the differentiation and function of multiple T cell subsets including regulatory T cells, effector T cells, and memory T cells. Early research primarily focused on single-agent polymer delivery systems for IL-2 to evaluate its stability and biological activity within nanocarriers. For example, chitosan microspheres achieved efficient encapsulation (encapsulation efficiency of 82–92%) of an IL-2 expression plasmid, enabling stable and efficient IL-2 expression after transfection into rat prostate cancer cells while maintaining plasmid integrity during encapsulation and release. More advanced strategies combine IL-2 with other treatment modalities. Reduction-responsive chitosan nanoparticles (MT NPs) simultaneously delivered PTX, surviving shRNA plasmid, and recombinant human IL-2. The ingenious design involved encapsulating PTX in the hydrophobic core to induce ICD, while iSur-pDNA and rhIL-2 were electrostatically adsorbed onto the hydrophilic shell. Triggered by high glutathione concentrations typical of the TME, PTX was rapidly released. The three components acted synergistically even at low doses, significantly enhancing antitumor efficacy. Furthermore, a biomimetic nanogel system (NG@M) coated with homologous tumor cell membranes improved the accumulation of nanocarriers in homologous tumors, enhancing drug delivery efficiency. Its accumulation in homologous tumors was approximately 4.6 times that in non-homologous tumors. The rapid release of PTX and IL-2 effectively promoted dendritic cell maturation and activated local antitumor immune responses, thereby inhibiting tumor growth and lung metastasis.

IL-12 is a potent pro-inflammatory cytokine playing a key role in activating NK cells, promoting Th1-type immune responses, and enhancing the cytotoxic activity of effector immune cells. However, systemic administration of IL-12 often induces severe inflammatory reactions and dose-dependent toxicity. MPEG-PLA and DOTAP self-assembled nanoparticles (DMP) achieved 100% loading efficiency for IL-12 plasmid DNA. In CT26 colon cancer cells, the transfection efficiency reached 50.7%. In subcutaneous and intraperitoneal colon cancer models, this system exerted significant antitumor effects by inducing tumor cell apoptosis and inhibiting angiogenesis, without observable systemic toxicity in mice. Through structural optimization, researchers further improved the performance of IL-12 delivery systems. Cationic micelles based on mPEG–PCL-modified DOTAP showed powerful antitumor efficacy in C26 colon cancer and LL/2 lung cancer models, effectively inhibiting tumor growth via both local and systemic administration without causing pathological damage to healthy tissues. Chitosan–TPP nanoparticles demonstrated organ-specific targeting ability. Following intravenous administration, they selectively accumulated in the liver, reducing the number and volume of colorectal cancer liver metastases while mitigating IL-12-induced toxicity.

Chemokine (C–C motif) ligand 25 (CCL25) is a chemokine that specifically recruits immune cells expressing its receptor, C–C chemokine receptor type 9 (CCR9) (e.g., T cells). It plays critical roles in the maintenance of intestinal immune homeostasis, as well as in the pathological processes of inflammation, tumors, and other disorders. Taking polymer nanomaterials-based targeted delivery of CCL25 as the core strategy, Sun et al. constructed a tumor acidity-responsive nanosystem, designated as NP-siCD47/CCL25 (Figure B). This nanosystem was fabricated using poly­(propylene carbonate)-dopamine (PPC-DA) modified with PEG as the key polymeric component, which adsorbs CCL25 and encapsulates CD47-targeting siRNA. In the acidic TME, the nanosystem sequentially releases CCL25 and siRNA, which potently recruits CCR9+CD8+ T cell infiltration and simultaneously downregulates CD47 expression in tumor cells, respectively. The antitumor efficacy of this nanosystem is dependent on CD8+ T cells. it significantly inhibits the growth and metastasis of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). Furthermore, when combined with programmed cell death protein 1/programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-1/PD-L1) inhibitors, the nanosystem exerts a synergistic antitumor effect and markedly prolongs the survival of tumor-bearing mice. Collectively, this study demonstrates the feasibility of using polymer nanomaterials as targeted delivery carriers for CCL25, thereby providing a novel paradigm for the immunotherapy of TNBC.

Co-delivery of multiple cytokines represents a future direction for cytokine therapy. For example, Sun et al. developed a β-cyclodextrin-polyethylenimine (CP) based co-delivery system aimed at simultaneously delivering the hydrophobic TGF-β inhibitor SB-505124 and an adenoviral vector encoding IL-12. The β-cyclodextrin component encapsulated SB-505124 for sustained release, while the 2 kDa PEI component significantly improved adenoviral transduction efficiency. This system synergistically activated CD4+ and CD8+ T cells as well as NK cells, enhanced IFN-γ secretion, delayed tumor progression, and prolonged survival in a B16 melanoma model. Additionally, Kong et al. developed a polymer nano delivery system (IL-15/2–PEG–PTMC) based on the amphiphilic block copolymer PEG–PTMC. By chemical grafting, IL-15 and IL-2 were co-loaded onto the surface of the nano micelles. Leveraging the stealth effect of PEG and the characteristics of nanoscale particle size, the in vivo half-lives of the two cytokines were significantly prolonged (5.4-fold increase for IL-15 and 3.29-fold increase for IL-2), overcoming the limitations of free cytokines such as short half-lives and the need for high-dose injections. Meanwhile, this nano delivery system achieved the synchronous targeted delivery of IL-15 and IL-2, enabling their simultaneous binding to the corresponding receptors on the surface of NK cells to synergistically activate them, enhance their recruitment in tumor tissues and cytotoxicity. Ultimately, it achieved a tumor growth inhibition rate of 65.85 ± 4.5% against tumors such as MDA-MB-231 breast cancer with favorable biosafety, providing an efficient strategy for the combined delivery of cytokines in NK cell immunotherapy.

Inhibition and Reprogramming of Immunosuppressive Cells

Tumor-Associated Macrophages

Direct depletion is the most straightforward strategy for TAM intervention. An acidic TME-supersensitive immunostimulatory nanocarrier (BLZ-945 SCNs/Pt) developed by Wang et al. showcased sophisticated drug delivery design. This carrier, composed of amphiphilic copolymer conjugated with a platinum (Pt) prodrug, rapidly disintegrated and released drugs triggered by the acidic TME. The released BLZ-945 was preferentially internalized by TAMs, depleting them by inhibiting the CSF-1/CSF-1R signaling pathway, while the smaller Pt-containing fragments penetrated tumor cells for cytotoxic action. This dual-action mechanism significantly inhibited tumor growth in a 4T1 breast cancer model while minimizing systemic toxicity.

However, directly depleting TAMs alone is insufficient to prevent the continuous recruitment of circulating monocytes to tumor tissue. Newly infiltrated monocytes can differentiate into TAMs within the TME, leading to the reconstruction of the immunosuppressive environment and reduced therapeutic efficacy. Therefore, inhibiting monocyte recruitment has become an important complementary strategy. A cationic polymer nanoparticle system (CNP/siCCR2) developed by Wang et al. efficiently delivered siRNA targeting the chemokine receptor CCR2. This formulation, by effectively blocking CCR2-mediated signaling, significantly inhibited monocyte infiltration into tumors, thereby reducing TAM accumulation. This strategy not only reversed the immunosuppressive microenvironment but also enhanced the antitumor efficacy of chemotherapeutic drugs.

Leveraging macrophage plasticity, reprogramming immunosuppressive M2-type TAMs into pro-inflammatory, antitumor M1-type macrophages offers a more proactive and durable means of reshaping the TME than simple direct depletion. A mannose-modified dendritic nanosystem, MG5-S-IMDQ, developed by Shao et al. integrated mannose ligands for M2-TAM targeting and the TLR7/8 agonist imiquimod as an immunostimulatory payload (Figure A). These nanoparticles, featuring small size, high surface charge, and spherical morphology, crossed the blood-brain barrier via adsorption-mediated transcytosis. Upon reaching the tumor site, mannose-mediated targeting promoted preferential uptake by M2-TAMs, and the released IMDQ successfully induced their repolarization toward a pro-inflammatory, antitumor M1 phenotype. In an orthotopic GL261 glioblastoma model, MG5-S-IMDQ significantly increased the infiltration of activated CD8+ T cells and prolonged survival.

From a comparative perspective, direct depletion and functional reprogramming offer distinct but complementary strategies for targeting immunosuppressive cells in the TIME. Direct depletion can rapidly reduce suppressive cell burden and provide fast relief of immune inhibition, but its efficacy may be limited by poor tumor penetration, continuous recruitment of new suppressive cells, and potential systemic toxicity. In contrast, functional reprogramming seeks to convert suppressive cells into pro-inflammatory phenotypes and may therefore support more durable remodeling of the TIME. For instance, repolarizing M2-like TAMs toward an M1-like state may preserve macrophage presence while redirecting their function toward antitumor immunity. However, stable reprogramming in vivo remains difficult because of persistent tumor-derived suppressive signals, incomplete phenotype conversion, and possible inflammatory side effects. Therefore, future polymeric platforms may achieve greater therapeutic benefit by combining the strengths of both approaches, particularly through spatiotemporally controlled or sequential delivery systems that enable initial depletion followed by sustained local reprogramming.

Myeloid-Derived Suppressor Cells

Inhibiting the recruitment and function of MDSCs is an effective way to improve the TME. Peng et al. developed an oxygen-generating nanoplatform (I-4-P@PssP) co-encapsulating IR-1048, 4-methylumbelliferone (4-MU), and perfluorooctyl bromide (PFOB) in a GSH-responsive amphiphilic polymer (mPEG-SS-PLGA) (Figure B). This nanosystem preferentially accumulated in tumor tissue via the EPR effect and released PFOB and 4-MU in response to elevated GSH levels in the TME. PFOB acted as an oxygen carrier to alleviate local hypoxia, while 4-MU relaxed the ECM and promoted vascular normalization, achieving sustained reoxygenation of the TME and effectively reducing MDSC recruitment. Both in vitro and in vivo studies demonstrated the potent antitumor efficacy of this platform.

Direct depletion of MDSCs can more thoroughly relieve immunosuppression. Ultrafine polymer micelles (MC-TG) loaded with 6-thioguanine, developed by Hubbell et al. efficiently targeted and depleted various MDSC subtypes in the spleen, lymph nodes, and tumor tissue upon intradermal injection. Compared to free 6-TG, MC-TG showed superior depletion efficiency. When combined with adoptive T cell therapy, it significantly delayed melanoma progression and improved overall survival.

Furthermore, phenotypic reprogramming strategies provide new ideas for MDSC intervention. A multifunctional nanoplatform (FA-CD@PP-CpG) based on PEI–PpIX copolymer, designed by Dong et al. successfully integrated chemotherapy, phototherapy, and immunotherapy. The uniqueness of this platform lay in the fact that low-dose docetaxel promoted cytotoxic T lymphocyte infiltration and enhanced the efficacy of anti-PD-L1 antibody, while the nanoplatform itself inhibited MDSCs and effectively reprogrammed them toward an M1 phenotype. This synergistic approach significantly enhanced CTL infiltration and antitumor immune responses.

Regulatory T Cells

Tregs mediate immune tolerance by suppressing tumor-specific T cell responses and modulating dendritic cell function, becoming a major obstacle to effective cancer immunotherapy. Metabolic intervention is a cutting-edge strategy for indirectly regulating Tregs. A hyaluronic acid-modified metal-phenolic nanodrug (HPP-Ca@GSK), self-assembled from HA-catechol and PEG-polyphenol and loaded with the lactate dehydrogenase A inhibitor GSK2837808A, was developed by Dai et al. Its innovative mechanism involved inhibiting aerobic glycolysis in tumor cells, reducing lactate production, and reconstructing a metabolic environment favorable for CD8+ T cell activation while limiting Treg survival. Under ultrasound irradiation, the PEG-IR780 component generated reactive oxygen species, synergizing with calcium overload to induce mitochondrial dysfunction and the release of DAMPs, thereby amplifying antitumor immune activation. In a 4T1 breast tumor model, ultrasound-assisted HPP-Ca@GSK treatment significantly inhibited primary and metastatic tumor growth, reduced lung metastasis, and showed superior therapeutic effects when combined with anti-CTLA-4 therapy.

Directly targeting the Treg survival environment is another effective strategy. An immunomodulatory nanoplatform (FEM@PFC) with both oxygen-replenishing and redox-responsive capabilities, developed by Sun et al. consisted of fluorinated polymer and perfluorocarbon (Figure C). The unique design allowed the PFC core to deliver oxygen, alleviate hypoxia, and reduce Treg infiltration, while the laser-responsive prodrug component consumed intracellular GSH, downregulated Foxp3 expression in Tregs, and induced ICD. In multiple tumor models including 4T1 and B16F10, FEM@PFC significantly inhibited tumor growth and lung metastasis, effectively enhancing systemic antitumor immunity.

In summary, polymer nanocarriers, with their programmable physicochemical properties and excellent biocompatibility, have become a core platform driving the development of next-generation immunotherapeutic technologies. They not only address the delivery bottlenecks of traditional immunotherapeutic agents (e.g., antigens, cytokines, nucleic acids) but, more importantly, through rational material design, they can actively participate in immune response modulation, efficiently integrating multiple treatment modalities to provide novel solutions for synergistic and personalized immunotherapy.

Polymer Nanocarriers Empowering Emerging Immunotherapeutic Strategies

In addition to the direct delivery of immunomodulatory drugs, polymer materials demonstrate innovative applications and unique advantages in three frontier immunotherapy areas: cancer vaccines, engineered cell therapies, and oncolytic virus therapies.

Tumor Vaccines and Personalized Neoantigen Platforms

The success of tumor vaccines relies on the efficient co-delivery of antigens and adjuvants to antigen-presenting cells APCs and the elicitation of robust antigen-specific T cell responses (Tables and ). Polymer nanocarriers demonstrate immense potential in this field, surpassing traditional vaccine platforms.

Application of Polymeric Nanocarriers in Cancer Vaccines: Ex Vivo Studies

Enhancing Antigen Accumulation and APC Uptake

Achieving efficient targeting of lymphoid organs is key to vaccine success. Polycarbonate-based polymers have been widely used in biomedical applications due to their excellent biocompatibility, controllable degradability, and non-toxic metabolic byproducts. Leveraging these advantages, Gao et al. developed polycarbonate-based nanoparticles (PC7A NPs). Through simple self-assembly of antigens with the synthetic polymer, these NPs efficiently delivered tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) to antigen-presenting cells APCs within draining lymph nodes (DLNs). This system significantly enhanced antigen presentation on APC surfaces and activated type I interferon-stimulated gene expression, thereby laying a solid foundation for subsequent immune responses. ,

To overcome the limitations of traditional liposomal carriers in delivery efficiency and tissue specificity, a phospholipid polymer library (PLG–PPs) based on poly­(amino acid) backbones was constructed and applied it to mRNA vaccine delivery systems. Results showed that complexes formed by PLG–PPs with mRNA exhibited excellent transfection efficiency and biocompatibility. Among them, the PA8-PL3 subtype achieved tissue-specific mRNA delivery upon intravenous injection, selectively transporting mRNA to the spleen. This strategy significantly improved the delivery efficiency and expression level of mRNA vaccines in immune-related tissues, providing a new design paradigm for developing highly targeted mRNA vaccine carriers.

Promoting Antigen Cross-Presentation and Intracellular Release

After antigen uptake by APCs, escaping from endosomes/lysosomes into the cytosol is the core step for initiating the MHC-I cross-presentation pathway and activating CTLs. Polymer nanocarriers can facilitate this process through various ingenious designs. Polymer nanoparticles can achieve endosomal escape via the proton sponge effect. The pH-sensitive polymer poly­(diisopropylamino ethyl methacrylate) (PDPA) absorbs protons within acidic endosomes (pH 6.2), causing osmotic swelling and endosome rupture, thereby releasing antigens and STING agonists into the cytosol. This process effectively activates antigen-specific CD8+ T cells and promotes the generation of memory T cells. Similarly, poly­(β-amino ester) and polyamidoamine dendrimers (DGBA NPs) enhance MHC-I-mediated cross-presentation, thereby inducing robust cytotoxic CD8+ T cell responses. , Additionally, a nanocomposite chaperone vaccine composed of poly­(ε-caprolactone) (PCL), poly­(β-amino ester) (PBAE), and mannose-PEG achieved synergistic immune enhancement through DC-targeted uptake, the proton sponge effect, and controlled antigen release. This strategy promoted both MHC-I and MHC-II-mediated antigen presentation, inducing coordinated activation of CD8+ and CD4+ T cells, resulting in a more comprehensive and durable antitumor immune response. Beyond chemical mechanisms, physical mechanical forces have also been employed to promote antigen release. A proton-driven nanotransforming vaccine (NTVs) was reported to transform from ∼100 nm spherical particles into rigid nanoplates of 5–8 μm under acidic endosomal conditions (pH 6.5). This mechanical transformation directly disrupts the endosomal membrane, promotes antigen release, and activates the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway, thereby inducing potent CD8+ T cell immunity and durable immune memory.

Synergistic Immune Activation and Immunogenicity Enhancement

The advantage of polymer nanovaccine lies not only in delivery but also in their ability to integrate and amplify immune activation signals (Figure ). Many polymer materials themselves possess immunoadjuvant activity. For instance, chitosan, a natural cationic polysaccharide, has been shown to induce mitochondrial ROS production and endogenous DNA release, thereby activating the cGAS-STING pathway, promoting DC maturation, and driving Th1-type immune responses. , In contrast, synthetic polymers exhibit tunable immunogenicity, minimizing unnecessary immune activation while their structural diversity enables efficient encapsulation of various therapeutic agents ranging from small molecules to large biomacromolecules like proteins and nucleic acids. Furthermore, by precisely regulating the physicochemical properties of polymer nanocarriers, such as size, morphology, surface charge, hydrophobicity, and chemical functionality, vaccines can achieve site-specific release, sustained DC recruitment and activation, and improved antigen presentation, ultimately eliciting robust T cell-mediated antitumor immunity. ,

Antigen processing and presentation enabled by polymer nanovaccines. Polymer-based nanovaccines improve vaccine efficacy by protecting antigens and adjuvants during delivery, facilitating lymph node drainage and uptake by antigen-presenting cells, and promoting intracellular antigen release and endosomal escape. These processes enhance antigen cross-presentation through MHC-I and antigen presentation through MHC-II, leading to coordinated activation of CD8+ cytotoxic T cells and CD4+ helper T cells, as well as the generation of durable immune memory. In addition, the intrinsic adjuvant activity and tunable physicochemical properties of selected polymer platforms further amplify dendritic cell activation and antitumor immune priming.

Polymer-Assisted Engineered Cell Therapies

Adoptive cell therapies, represented by chimeric antigen receptor T cell (CAR-T) therapy, have achieved revolutionary success in hematological malignancies. However, they still face challenges in treating solid tumors, and the manufacturing process is complex and costly. Polymer nanomaterials are providing novel solutions for optimizing and innovating cell therapies from both ex vivo manufacturing and in vivo functional perspectives.

Polymer-Assisted Engineered CAR-T

CAR-T therapy involves introducing tumor-specific CAR constructs into autologous T cells to enhance antitumor effects. In the ex vivo manufacturing stage, safely and efficiently introducing CAR genes into primary immune cells is a primary challenge. Cationic-polymer-based nanosystems, such as PEI, poly­(2-(dimethylamino)­ethyl methacrylate) (PDMAEMA), and poly­(β-amino ester) (PβAE), can significantly improve gene transfection efficiency while maintaining good biosafety. Additionally, the optimal T cell population is characterized by a high proportion of naive and memory subsets, low expression of exhaustion markers (e.g., PD-1), and strong proliferative potential. Therefore, maintaining T cell functional fitness during ex vivo activation and expansion is crucial for improving the quality and therapeutic efficacy of CAR-T cell products. To address this, Liu et al. proposed a hydrophilic monomer-mediated surface topography regulation strategy, synthesizing biocompatible dendritic poly­(N-isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPAM) microspheres to construct biomimetic artificial antigen-presenting cells (aAPCs). To construct dendritic aAPCs, these dendritic PNIPAM microspheres were subsequently coated with protein A, a Staphylococcus aureus cell wall protein with high affinity for the Fc region of IgG, enabling the assembly of CD3ε and CD28 antibodies (aCD3ε and aCD28, both IgG) onto the microsphere surface in an orientation that preserved the antigen-binding sites facing the target cells. By replicating the surface topology of natural APCs, dendritic polymer microsphere (DPM) beads promoted the proliferation of CD8+ T cells more effectively than traditional aAPCs with smooth surfaces at the same ligand density. When adoptively transferred, the expanded antigen-specific CD8+ T cells significantly inhibited tumor growth in B16-OVA tumor-bearing mice (Figure A).

To develop simplified, efficient, and controllable CAR-T generation strategies, the concept of in vivo generation of CAR-T is gaining increasing attention. This approach aims to employ advanced delivery systems, such as polymer nanoparticles, to achieve precise in vivo CAR gene delivery and editing, thereby directly inducing the formation of functional CAR-T cells in vivo. For example, Wang et al. delivered CTLA-4 siRNA to CD4+ and CD8+ T cells as well as tumor tissue using poly­(ethylene glycol)–poly­(lactic acid) (PEG–PLA) block copolymer nanoparticles, thereby increasing the abundance of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells and enhancing antitumor immune responses. More notably, Smith et al. developed biodegradable poly­(β-amino ester) nanoparticles capable of generating CAR-T cells targeting CD19+ B cells in vivo, providing a new paradigm for efficient in vivo CAR-T production and clinical translation.

Polymer-Assisted Engineered CAR-NK and CAR-M

Chimeric antigen receptor-engineered natural killer (CAR-NK) cells and chimeric antigen receptor-engineered macrophages (CAR-M) are two rapidly emerging fields in tumor immunotherapy. Polymer-based nanocarriers have become key non-viral gene delivery platforms for the genetic engineering of CAR-NK and CAR-M cells. In CAR-NK cell engineering, Park et al. developed a composite nanoparticle system based on magnetic Zn/Fe oxides. This system featured a magnetic core for targeted aggregation, surface-modified caffeic acid, and a cationic polydopamine (PDA) coating (Figure B). The PDA shell not only bound and protected nucleic acids from enzymatic degradation via electrostatic interactions but also promoted endosomal escape through the proton sponge effect, thereby improving gene release efficiency and enhancing the tumor-killing activity of CAR-NK cells. Furthermore, microenvironment-responsive delivery systems offer innovative strategies to enhance the in vivo antitumor efficacy of CAR-NK cells. Wang et al. developed a pH-responsive hemostatic hydrogel (GOSM-gel), integrated from oxidized starch-gelatin matrices with mesoporous bioactive glass nanoparticles (MBGNs), which could continuously release the METTL3 inhibitor STM2457 in the acidic tumor environment. This inhibited m6A methylation, thereby promoting the recruitment and cytotoxicity of CAR-NK cells.

In CAR-M cell engineering, polymer design primarily focuses on utilizing the high expression of mannose receptors on macrophage surfaces for targeted gene delivery. Functionalized mannose-modified polymer nanocarriers have been developed for the specific delivery of CAR constructs to TAMs. Notably, pH-responsive mannose-modified nanoparticles employ a PEG shedding mechanism to dynamically regulate targeting efficiency. At physiological pH, the PEG layer provides steric hindrance to minimize non-specific interactions, however, in the weakly acidic TME, PEG shedding exposes mannose ligands that specifically bind to the mannose receptors on TAMs, enabling efficient receptor-mediated endocytosis and CAR gene delivery. This strategy significantly reduces off-target toxicity while enhancing local immune remodeling within the TME. ,

Carrier Optimization for Oncolytic Virus Therapies

Oncolytic viruses (OVs) selectively replicate within and lyse tumor cells while stimulating antitumor immunity. However, systemic administration is often limited by rapid clearance by the immune system and poor tumor targeting. Polymer nanocoating technology, by providing OVs with an intelligent stealth cloak, significantly improves their pharmacokinetics and targeting, serving as a key tool to overcome these obstacles.

Exploiting differences between the TME and normal tissues, stimuli-responsive polymer coatings enable specific virus release. For example, coating adenovirus with a pH-responsive bioreducible polymer (PPCBA) triggered polymer degradation under the acidic conditions of the TME, achieving specific virus release and infection at the target site and improving treatment efficiency. Liu et al. developed a polyethylenimine–diselenide–poly­(ethylene glycol) (PSSP) modification system (AD@PSSP) where diselenide bond cleavage occurred under radiotherapy-induced ROS, simultaneously releasing viral particles and exposing PEI. The exposed PEI enhanced tumor radiosensitivity by inhibiting the CHEK1/CDK1 pathway and simultaneously captured tumor-associated antigens, delivering them to lymph nodes to induce long-term immune memorythereby achieving synergistic enhancement between oncolytic virotherapy and immunotherapy (Figure C).

Based on these advances, surface chemical modifications of nanoparticles further improved the systemic delivery of viruses. Cationic polymers like PEI can protect viral genomes from nuclease degradation, while the negatively charged carboxyl groups on the adenovirus surface can form electrostatic complexes with cationic compounds, preventing recognition by blood factors or neutralizing antibodies and reducing non-specific liver uptake. , For example, PEGylated magnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (MIONs) used for coating oncolytic adenovirus (MIONs-OAD) effectively shielded viral epitopes, significantly reduced liver clearance, and achieved magnetic-field-guided tumor-specific accumulation. Additionally, their PEI coating promoted endosomal escape via the proton sponge effect. Similarly, albumin-coated oncolytic adenovirus (ICOVIR15-ABD) utilizes an albumin-binding domain (ABD) to camouflage the viral capsid, which prolongs systemic circulation time and leverages albumin’s inherent tumor-targeting properties (EPR effect) to enhance viral accumulation in tumors. In summary, these studies demonstrate that polymer-assisted viral modifications effectively mitigate immune neutralization and improve tumor targeting.

Challenges in Clinical Translation and Future Trends

Key Hurdles in Clinical Translation

Although polymeric nanomedicines, as an emerging therapeutic platform, demonstrate significant potential in targeted delivery and controlled release, their clinical translation still faces a series of complex and interconnected core challenges. These challenges primarily stem from the fundamental contradiction between the inherent complexity of carrier design and the dynamic heterogeneity of biological systems.

Complexity of Multiparameter Polymer Carrier Design

Drug development, a cornerstone of modern medicine, faces the formidable challenges of high cost, long timelines, and low success rates. The traditional R&D pipeline averages approximately $2.6 billion and 10–15 years per approved drug, yet clinical success rates remain below 10%. This dilemma stems largely from the multifactorial nature of diseases, the vast chemical space, and stringent regulatory requirements, making drug discovery akin to finding a needle in a haystack. Polymer nanomedicines, as emerging therapeutic platforms, show great potential for targeted delivery and controlled release but simultaneously face unique design challenges. The core issue lies in the complexity of coupled multiple parameters: the in vivo behavior of nanocarriers is not determined by any single parameter (e.g., such as size, morphology, surface charge, or drug loading method) but by the synergistic interplay of factors including molecular structure, chain sequence, surface chemistry, and the in vivo microenvironment. More complexly, these parameters exhibit strong nonlinear coupling effects. For instance, the interaction between size and surface chemistry can significantly alter biodistribution, and minute changes in ligand density may drastically affect cellular uptake efficiency. This high-dimensional, multivariable design space makes it difficult for traditional trial-and-error approaches to capture potential synergistic or antagonistic effects, constituting a primary obstacle to the precise design of polymer carriers.

Spatiotemporally Uncontrolled Drug Release and Mismatch with Multistage Therapeutic Needs

Effective tumor immunotherapy often relies on the coordinated sequential progression of multiple therapeutic stages: early disruption of vascular barriers and remodeling of the extracellular matrix to improve drug penetration is needed before efficient, sustained delivery of chemotherapeutic or immunomodulatory drugs can occur (Figure ). However, most traditional nanocarriers employ a single burst-release pattern, rapidly releasing their payload upon reaching the tumor site, lacking adaptability to the dynamically evolving TME. This leads to chaotic drug release, resulting in excessive accumulation at the tumor periphery and insufficient penetration into the hypoxic core, failing to meet the temporal requirements of different stages such as vascular disruption, immune activation, and cell killing. Therefore, developing stimuli-responsive, cascade, or intelligently regulated multistage release systems has become a core challenge for achieving spatiotemporally precise delivery.

Key barriers to the clinical translation of polymer nanomedicines. Multiparameter design complexity, uncontrolled intratumoral drug release, and animal–human translational gaps collectively limit the therapeutic efficacy of polymer nanocarriers.

Furthermore, inherent physiological differences between animal models and humans constitute another major obstacle to clinical translation. Although animal models are indispensable for drug screening and safety evaluation, they differ significantly from humans in terms of immune system, circulatory kinetics, and metabolic pathways. These interspecies differences lead to the failure of many preclinically effective nanomedicines in human trials, highlighting the urgent need to develop more human-relevant model systems.

Future Research Paradigm: AI-Driven Intelligent Design and Closed-Loop Validation with Human-Relevant Models

The clinical translation dilemma for polymer nanomedicines originates from a dual disconnect in the traditional R&D paradigm: one between carrier design and complex biological effects, and the other between preclinical models and the authentic human environment. To break through this bottleneck, a new research paradigm integrating computational intelligence with high-fidelity biological systems is emerging.

AI-Driven Prediction and Intelligent Design

The function of polymer carriers is determined by the synergistic effects of numerous parameters (molecular weight, chemical composition, topological structure, surface functional groups), which together constitute a high-dimensional design space difficult to explore empirically. Artificial intelligence, particularly machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL), can mine vast experimental datasets to establish nonlinear mapping models from material parameters to in vivo performance (e.g., targeting efficiency, immune activation potency, toxicity), thereby shifting carrier design from trial-and-error to prediction. For instance, Huang et al. extracted key physicochemical parameters (including size, morphology, and valence state) from 5355 published studies on nanomaterials and constructed a deep neural network model to predict the enzyme-mimetic catalytic activity of nanomaterials. This model successfully identified nanomaterials with high peroxidase (POD)-like activity as well as those with strong oxidase (OXD)-like activity, and the accuracy of these predictions was subsequently verified by experimental validation. Furthermore, the combination of machine learning (ML) with high-throughput experiments has accelerated the discovery of self-assembled drug nanoparticles without the need for chemical synthesis. Traverso et al. employed high-throughput screening to analyze 1440 drug-excipient combinations, using molecular dynamics (MD)-derived interaction potentials as input features for a random forest ML model. The trained model achieved 91% cross-validation accuracy in predicting stable drug-excipient NP formation. Based on this framework, the team screened 2.1 million drug-excipient pairs and identified 38,464 combinations with high NP formation potential. Experimental validation confirmed 100 novel co-aggregated drug NPs, such as terbinafine-taurocholate NPs with potent antifungal activity and sorafenib-glycyrrhizin NPs with enhanced anticancer efficacy. This method significantly accelerated the development of high-drug-loading nanoformulations and improved the bioavailability and therapeutic performance of poorly soluble drugs.

Moreover, in nanomedicine, AI can integrate patient-specific features, physicochemical properties of nanocarriers, and pharmacokinetic parameters of drugs to construct high-precision predictive models. This approach significantly improves R&D efficiency, reduces failure risks, and promotes the realization of personalized and precision therapy. In practice, AI has been widely used to optimize the dosage and release kinetics of anticancer therapeutic drugs based on polymer nanocarriers. For instance, in personalized cancer treatment, AI can integrate tumor gene expression profiles, microenvironment characteristics, and nanocarrier-cell interaction data to predict optimal dosing regimens that maximize efficacy while minimizing adverse reactions. Lin et al. developed an AI system combining deep learning with physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling to predict the in vivo distribution and accumulation efficiency of nanoparticles based on their size, charge, and tumor type. The model not only assessed the targeting performance of various nanocarriers but also estimated optimal dosing strategies to ensure effective tumor accumulation while minimizing off-target toxicity, thereby achieving precise dose optimization.

Compared to monotherapy or synchronous combination therapy, sequential combination therapy enables stage-specific modulation of the TME, immune status, and drug sensitivity, thereby achieving higher efficacy and reducing the risk of resistance. However, a major challenge lies in the fact that the optimal timing and sequence of drug action are highly dependent on the dynamic evolution of the disease state. The key advantage of AI models is their ability to integrate multi-omics data, drug properties, and disease dynamics, utilizing machine learning algorithms to predict the best drug administration sequence and timing. For example, the PDGrapher model developed by Harvard Medical School employs graph neural networks (GNNs) to construct dynamic interaction networks of intracellular genes, proteins, and signaling pathways. This model can not only identify synergistic drug combinations but also simulate the temporal trajectories of cellular state transitions following specific molecular perturbations, thereby predicting how sequential drug interventions can restore diseased cells to a healthy phenotype. In a study on non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), PDGrapher successfully identified a potential sequential treatment regimen (initial inhibition of KDR (VEGFR2) to block angiogenesis, followed by targeting TOP2A to inhibit metastasis), which closely aligns with clinically observed effective treatment logic. These findings demonstrate that AI can capture the causal chain underlying disease reversal, providing a solid biological basis for designing sequential treatment strategies.

Human-Relevant Models: A Critical Bridge from Mouse to Human

However, even the most sophisticated AI predictions require rigorous validation through in vivo experiments. Traditional mouse models, due to their significant differences from humans in immune system, metabolism, and TME, have become a major source of misdirection in nanomedicine translation. Many nanoformulations showing excellent efficacy in conventional mice fail in humans because they cannot interact effectively with human immune cells or encounter different physiological barriers.

To address this, humanized animal models, particularly immune system-humanized mice, have become a key tool to bridge this translational gap. These models reconstruct a functional human immune system in immunodeficient mice by transplanting human hematopoietic stem cells, peripheral blood mononuclear cells, or specific immune tissues. Hu et al. constructed a hematological malignancy model integrating humanized immune system features with characteristics of spontaneous leukemia progression. This model optimized the mouse immune microenvironment through genetic editing, significantly enhancing the functionality and homogeneity of the humanized immune system. It successfully overcame the low engraftment efficiency of human cells in traditional xenograft models due to immune rejection while avoiding the immune deficiencies of conventional humanized models. More importantly, this model achieved for the first time the natural evolution of human hematopoietic stem cells into acute leukemia, accurately replicating the initiation, progression, and interaction with the host immune system of leukemia. Similarly, Keck et al. developed the Onco-HuNSG model, combining a humanized immune system with xenograft tumor models (CDX/PDX), and validated the efficacy of anti-PD-1 antibody (pembrolizumab) in non-small cell lung cancer. Results showed that in humanized mice, 11 out of 13 individuals exhibited significant tumor shrinkage, whereas anti-PD-1 treatment was ineffective in ordinary NSG mice. This clearly indicates that the antitumor effect of this therapy depends on the involvement of human immune cells, closely aligning with the mechanism of action of ICIs in clinical practice and significantly enhancing the clinical relevance of the animal model.

For polymer nanomedicines, such models constitute a highly clinically relevant validation system: they can authentically assess the carrier’s human-specific targeting ability, the responses triggered by its payload in human immune cells, and its distribution and metabolism within a humanized TME, thereby providing the most compelling early evidence for a carrier’s ultimate clinical prospects.

Despite its promise, the AI-driven closed-loop paradigm still faces important practical limitations. A major bottleneck is the lack of sufficiently large, high-quality, and standardized datasets linking polymer nanocarrier properties with in vivo immunological outcomes. Variability in polymer synthesis, characterization methods, experimental protocols, and biological readouts across studies can reduce data comparability and limit effective AI training. In addition, negative and low-performing formulations are often underreported, which further constrains model robustness. Humanized mouse models, although highly valuable, also remain imperfect representations of the human immune system, with limitations including incomplete immune reconstitution, donor-dependent heterogeneity, and imperfect lymphoid architecture. These model-level imperfections may propagate bias into the AI design loop if the resulting datasets are treated as fully representative of human biology. Nevertheless, humanized models remain irreplaceable as one of the most clinically relevant in vivo platforms currently available for evaluating human-specific immune responses. To mitigate the risk of “garbage in, garbage out”, future studies should prioritize the generation of richer and more standardized datasets from well-characterized humanized cohorts, the use of rigorously curated datasets for task-specific AI training, and broader data sharing to capture both common and cohort-specific immune features, thereby improving the robustness and translational value of the integrated design framework.

Synergistic Closed Loop of AI and Human-Relevant Models: The Core Future Paradigm

Looking ahead, the R&D paradigm for polymer nanomedicines is undergoing a fundamental transformation (Figure ). Its core lies in deeply integrating the rational design power of AI with the high clinical relevance validation power of human-relevant models, forming a self-optimizing intelligent closed-loop system of Design–Build–Test–Learn (DBTL). This paradigm stems from the consensus that more powerful AI or more realistic models alone are insufficient the key lies in the closed-loop flow and iterative learning of data. In this ecosystem, AI first generates initial design proposals for polymer carriers based on multidimensional data sources, and automated synthesis converts them into physical nanoparticles. Subsequently, these candidate carriers undergo parallel, efficient in vivo evaluation in humanized animal models that closely mimic the human immune microenvironment. Finally, these critical data are fed back to the AI models in real-time, enabling precise refinement of the understanding of structure-activity relationships and proposing next-generation designs with continuously optimized performance. This AI × Humanized Models intelligent paradigm marks a historic shift for the field from the traditional experience-dependent trial-and-error mode to a data-driven precision design mode. It not only lays a solid foundation for significantly increasing the success rate of nanomedicine R&D but also charts a clear path toward achieving truly personalized precision immunotherapy: integrating patient-specific information to design customized nanomedicines and prospectively validating them in personalized biological models, ultimately delivering safer and more effective individualized treatment regimens to the clinic.

AI-driven closed-loop development of polymer nanomedicines. Artificial-intelligence-guided design, automated synthesis, and human-relevant validation models form a closed-loop paradigm accelerating polymer nanomedicine translation.

Conclusions and Outlook

Polymer materials, through their programmable physicochemical properties and biocompatibility, have evolved from traditional delivery tools into powerful multifunctional regulatory platforms in tumor immunotherapy. This review has systematically elaborated how polymer nanocarriers achieve precise delivery of immunotherapeutic agents to tumor tissue or lymphoid organs via targeted strategies; and through functional reprogramming strategies, synergistically activate antigen-presenting cells and effector immune cells while inhibiting or reversing immunosuppressive cells, thereby effectively reshaping the TIME. Furthermore, polymer platforms have demonstrated exceptional integrative capabilities, empowering the advancement and development of emerging therapeutic modalities such as cancer vaccines, engineered cell therapies, and oncolytic viruses.

Despite the promising prospects, the clinical translation of polymer-based immune-nanomedicines still faces multiple challenges. The complexity of multiparameter carrier design and the nonlinear correlation with in vivo performance make rational design difficult. Additionally, the disparity between traditional animal models and the human immune environment, coupled with the spatiotemporal mismatch between drug release and multistage immune responses, presents obstacles on the path to translation.

Looking forward, overcoming these challenges relies on a fundamental of the research paradigm. On one hand, AI-driven rational design holds promise for solving multidimensional optimization problems, using machine learning to predict structure-activity relationships and enabling high-throughput virtual screening and intelligent design. On the other hand, establishing clinically relevant validation systems based on humanized animal models will provide more translationally predictive efficacy and safety evaluations for candidate drugs. Ultimately, the intelligent closed-loop paradigm of Design–Build–Test–Learn, driven by AI and validated by humanized models, is expected to shift the R&D of polymer nanomedicines from a trial-and-error mode to a new stage of prediction and precision validation, greatly accelerating the translation from bench to bedside.

In summary, polymer materials have demonstrated irreplaceable and unique value in tumor immunotherapy. With the deep interdisciplinary integration of materials science, immunology, and computational science, next-generation intelligent polymer platforms will undoubtedly propel the birth of more effective, safer, and more personalized immunotherapeutic strategies, providing key impetus for ultimately conquering cancer.
