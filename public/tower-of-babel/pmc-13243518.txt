Explainable clustering and ranking of student satisfaction in online learning: a hybrid FAHP-TOPSIS and SHAP-LIME approach
Authors: Sanjida Sharmin, Shefayatuj Johara Chowdhury, Farzana Tasnim, Tanjim Mahmud, Md. Eftekhar Alam, Mohammad Shahadat Hossain, Karl Andersson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The rapid transition to online learning has intensified the need to understand the multifaceted drivers of student satisfaction in virtual education environments. Student satisfaction is a cornerstone of effective digital education, influencing academic performance, learner engagement, and institutional decision-making. As a critical predictor of retention and perceived instructional quality, it directly impacts the sustainability of online learning systems. However, existing studies often lack integrated frameworks that combine subjective expert judgment, global and local model interpretability, and subgroup-specific analysis. To address this gap, this study aims to develop a hybrid decision-analytic and explainable machine learning framework to systematically assess and interpret student satisfaction in virtual classrooms. The proposed framework adopts a layered analytical design in which statistical feature relevance (ANOVA), model-based interpretability (SHAP and LIME), and expert-driven weighting under uncertainty (FAHP) are treated as complementary but non-overlapping components, with TOPSIS used exclusively for decision-level satisfaction ranking. A structured survey has been conducted among 1,469 university students, capturing responses across 21 features encompassing demographic attributes, digital readiness, behavioral patterns, and perceived instructional quality. Feature selection has been performed using the ANOVA F-test, while SHapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) and Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations (LIME) have been applied to derive global and local feature importance. To incorporate subjective expert judgment, feature weights have been calculated using the Fuzzy Analytic Hierarchy Process (FAHP), and composite satisfaction scores have been computed using the Technique for Order of Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS). These scores have been used to segment students via Agglomerative Hierarchical Clustering into low (n = 852), medium (n = 484), and high (n = 133) satisfaction cohorts. Cluster-specific SHAP and LIME analyses have uncovered distinct patterns of feature influence across subgroups. The identified satisfaction drivers align closely with constructs from established models of technology acceptance and learning motivation, including the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT), and Self-Determination Theory (SDT). High satisfaction has been linked to emotional positivity, perceived equivalence to offline instruction, and academic self-efficacy, whereas low satisfaction has been driven by affordability barriers, psychological stress, and misalignment with preferred learning modalities. The proposed framework offers a comprehensive, interpretable, and rank-aware approach to modeling student satisfaction, equipping educational stakeholders with actionable insights for designing more inclusive, equitable, and learner-centered online education environments.

Introduction

The global shift to online education, accelerated by the COVID-19 pandemic, has fundamentally transformed how students experience learning1. In the years following the pandemic, the adoption and expansion of online learning platforms have continued to grow significantly, establishing virtual education as a mainstream and enduring component of higher education worldwide2. While digital platforms have enabled flexible and scalable access to education, they have also introduced a new set of challenges related to instructional quality, student engagement, technological accessibility, and emotional well-being. In this evolving educational paradigm, understanding the determinants of student satisfaction has become a critical concern for educators, administrators, and policy makers alike3. Student satisfaction is not only a reflection of individual learning outcomes but also a key indicator of instructional effectiveness and institutional performance in virtual environments4.

Several studies have attempted to model student satisfaction using traditional statistical methods or black-box machine learning techniques5. However, these approaches often fail to capture the complex interplay between behavioral, technological, and psychological factors, and they typically lack transparency in model interpretation. Furthermore, they rarely account for subjective or expert-driven knowledge, which is essential in understanding human-centered phenomena such as satisfaction6. Student satisfaction in online learning has been extensively examined through established behavioral and motivational theories. The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) emphasizes perceived usefulness and ease of use as key determinants of satisfaction7, reflected in this study through variables such as perceived learning equivalence and emotional response toward online instruction. The Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) highlights the role of facilitating conditions and social support8, which are operationalized here through institutional assistance, access to learning resources, and lecturer attention. From a motivational perspective, Self-Determination Theory (SDT) identifies autonomy, competence, and relatedness as fundamental psychological needs influencing learner satisfaction, corresponding to variables such as self-efficacy, stress, concentration ability, and perceived instructional support9. This study operationalizes these theoretical constructs within an interpretable hybrid MCDM–XAI framework, enabling theory-consistent yet data-driven analysis of student satisfaction.

To address these limitations, the present study introduces a comprehensive hybrid framework that combines multi-criteria decision-making (MCDM)10 with explainable artificial intelligence (XAI)11 techniques. The framework begins with a structured student survey encompassing 19 attributes, including demographic factors, IT readiness, emotional responses, and perceived learning quality. Feature relevance is initially assessed through the ANOVA F-test12 to identify statistically significant variables. In parallel, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) and Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations (LIME) are employed to quantify both global and local contributions of each feature toward student satisfaction prediction, enabling interpretable insights into model behavior13.

To incorporate subjective importance, feature weights are computed using the Fuzzy Analytic Hierarchy Process (FAHP)14, and overall student satisfaction scores are derived through the Technique for Order of Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS)15. These scores are then used to segment the student population into low, medium, and high satisfaction clusters using agglomerative hierarchical clustering16. Each subgroup is further analyzed using SHAP and LIME to reveal cluster-specific feature dynamics and behavioral patterns. Although multi-criteria decision-making (MCDM) methods such as AHP, FAHP, and TOPSIS and machine learning models have individually been applied to student satisfaction analysis, their roles are often conflated or redundantly implemented in existing studies. In contrast, this work adopts a layered analytical design, where each method addresses a distinct decision-analytic objective. Statistical techniques (ANOVA) and model-based explainability (SHAP and LIME) are employed solely for data-driven feature relevance identification, whereas FAHP is used independently to capture expert-driven subjective importance under uncertainty. TOPSIS then translates these expert weights into student-level satisfaction rankings, and explainable artificial intelligence methods (SHAP and LIME) are finally leveraged for post-hoc interpretability without influencing decision weights. This structured integration ensures methodological complementarity rather than redundancy and aligns with contemporary hybrid MCDM–XAI paradigms17–20. While the proposed framework is methodologically transferable, the empirical findings are derived from a single national context and should not be generalized beyond similar educational settings without further cross-context validation.

Motivated by the need for both transparency and contextual relevance in student satisfaction modeling, this research integrates expert reasoning with interpretable, data-driven algorithms to capture the nuanced, multidimensional nature of satisfaction. This hybrid approach addresses critical gaps in existing literature, where prior models lack either explainability or sensitivity to subjective and contextual variability among learners.

Summary of Related Works.

The increasing adoption of online learning, particularly in the post-COVID-19 era, has spurred considerable research into student satisfaction and its determinants. Early studies predominantly employed traditional methods such as regression analysis and structural equation modeling to explore relationships between satisfaction and factors like instructor presence, content quality, and technological access. For example, one study examined how interaction and self-efficacy contribute to satisfaction in online courses27.

With the advent of data-driven educational analytics, machine learning techniques have gained popularity due to their capacity to uncover nonlinear patterns.Verma et al. found that Random Forest outperformed Logistic Regression and XGBoost–achieving up to 88% accuracy–and identified key predictors related to time and cost management, institutional support, and engaging group tasks, reinforcing the importance of these variables in enhancing informatics students’ happiness and satisfaction in hybrid learning environments28. Similarly, Sharif Nia et al.29 conducted a cross-national survey of 6,489 university students across nine countries and found that student engagement–strongly driven by quality of course content and online interaction mediated the relationships between content, interaction, acceptance, and both academic efficacy and satisfaction, with a robust structural equation model fit across diverse educational contexts. Ritonga et al.21 analyzed data from 246 university students in Riau using SEM path analysis and found that satisfaction significantly influences both motivation and academic achievement. Mohan et al.22, through a machine learning model (XGBoost with SHAP), examined engagement across seven Indian learning centers and concluded that confidence and content relevance are critical drivers of student engagement. Similarly, Nurdin et al.23 applied Fuzzy C-Means Clustering to responses from 500 informatics students in Indonesia, identifying that 60% were satisfied, with instructor competence and infrastructure as key factors; however, their study did not explore academic outcomes. Rahyasih et al.24 used PLS-SEM to analyze feedback from 3,206 vocational students, highlighting the importance of communication and digital media in driving satisfaction, albeit limited to the vocational context. Hazriyanti et al.25 explored online education during the COVID-19 lockdown using survey analysis among 50 students in Sumatra, identifying instructor clarity and consistency as weak points; however, the study had a small sample and did not incorporate advanced analytical models. Despite improved predictive accuracy, many ML-based approaches lack transparency, limiting their practical utility. To address this, explainable AI (XAI) methods such as SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) and Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations (LIME) have been introduced in educational contexts. Ben Said et al. applied SHAP and Permutation Importance on institutional survey data, finding that quality, interaction, and comprehension were the dominant predictors of online teaching satisfaction, whereas demographic variables such as class, gender, and nationality were statistically insignificant26.

Parallel to these data-driven methods, multi-criteria decision-making (MCDM) approaches have also been utilized in educational evaluations. The Fuzzy Analytic Hierarchy Process (FAHP) allows for expert-informed feature weighting under uncertainty, while the Technique for Order of Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS) enables ranking of alternatives. However, few studies integrate MCDM techniques with modern explainability frameworks or apply clustering for subgroup-specific insights. Recent hybrid MCDM–XAI studies increasingly emphasize the separation of expert-driven weighting from post-hoc explainability, a design principle explicitly adopted and extended in this work. The key studies relevant to this research and their main findings are summarized in Table 1. This study aims to fill this research gap by proposing a hybrid FAHP–TOPSIS and XAI framework, combined with agglomerative clustering, to deliver a robust, interpretable, and cluster-aware model of student satisfaction in online learning environments. Recent advances in decision-support research increasingly advocate hybrid MCDM–XAI paradigms, in which expert-driven weighting and ranking are treated as conceptually distinct from post-hoc model interpretability. In such frameworks, multi-criteria decision-making methods are used to encode normative or expert preferences under uncertainty, while explainable artificial intelligence techniques are applied to interpret data-driven models without influencing decision weights. This separation has been emphasized as a key design principle for ensuring transparency, trust, and methodological coherence in complex decision systems. The proposed framework explicitly adopts and extends this hybrid MCDM–XAI paradigm in the context of student satisfaction analysis by integrating FAHP–TOPSIS with dual-level explainability (SHAP and LIME) and subgroup-specific interpretation.

Methodology

Fig. 1Workflow for student satisfaction assessment in online education.

Workflow for student satisfaction assessment in online education.

This study adopts a comprehensive hybrid methodology to assess student satisfaction in online learning environments by systematically analyzing multidimensional feedback through the integration of multi-criteria decision-making (MCDM) and explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) techniques. The proposed framework consists of sequential phases, including data collection, feature selection using statistical and model-based approaches, expert-driven weighting via the Fuzzy Analytic Hierarchy Process (FAHP), satisfaction score computation using the Technique for Order of Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS), and student segmentation through agglomerative hierarchical clustering into low, medium, and high satisfaction groups. Subsequently, post-hoc interpretation is conducted using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) and Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations (LIME) to obtain both global and instance-level insights into the determinants of student satisfaction, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

Although ANOVA, SHAP, FAHP, TOPSIS, and LIME are individually well-established techniques, each component of the proposed framework fulfills a distinct and non-overlapping analytical role, as illustrated in the workflow diagram (Fig. 1). Following data preprocessing, feature selection is conducted in two complementary stages. First, ANOVA is applied solely as a statistical relevance filter to identify features exhibiting significant variance across student satisfaction responses. However, as ANOVA is limited to linear, distribution-based effects, it cannot capture nonlinear or interaction-driven relationships. To address this limitation, SHAP is subsequently employed to derive model-driven feature importance, enabling the identification of influential variables based on their marginal contribution to prediction outcomes. The final feature set is obtained by selecting the intersection of top-ranked features identified by both ANOVA and SHAP, ensuring that retained attributes are both statistically significant and predictively meaningful.

The selected features are then used as inputs to the Fuzzy Analytic Hierarchy Process (FAHP), where expert judgments expressed through fuzzy linguistic scales are incorporated to compute subjective criterion weights under uncertainty. These FAHP-derived weights are expert-driven and data-independent, and therefore serve a fundamentally different analytical purpose than SHAP-based importance scores. Next, the Technique for Order of Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS) utilizes the FAHP-derived weights to compute a rank-aware composite satisfaction score for each student, enabling systematic comparison across individuals. Based on these scores, Agglomerative Hierarchical Clustering is applied to partition students into low, medium, and high satisfaction groups. Finally, SHAP and LIME are applied in a cluster-wise manner to provide complementary global and local interpretability, allowing the identification of key influencing factors specific to each satisfaction group.

To avoid conceptual ambiguity, it is explicitly emphasized that three distinct weighting mechanisms are employed in this framework, each serving a different analytical objective. FAHP-derived weights represent expert-elicited subjective importance of criteria and are used exclusively as inputs to TOPSIS for satisfaction scoring and ranking. SHAP values quantify model-based feature contributions to XGBoost predictions and are used solely for interpretability, not for decision weighting. LIME coefficients provide instance-level surrogate explanations for individual predictions and do not influence either FAHP or TOPSIS computations. This strict separation of roles ensures methodological complementarity rather than redundancy, thereby strengthening the conceptual clarity and scientific rigor of the proposed framework.

The dataset captures temporal diversity, including both fully remote (COVID-19) and hybrid (post-pandemic) learning phases, enabling richer generalization of student perceptions. The instrument was designed to capture detailed information across 21 dimensions, including emotional response, technological accessibility, instructional quality, engagement, and self-regulated learning behavior. Sampling and data collection method

Survey Method: The data have been gathered using a structured online questionnaire distributed among students from diverse geographic regions and academic institutions. The survey instrument included a combination of multiple-choice questions, Likert-scale items, and open-ended responses to capture both quantitative and qualitative aspects of students’ experiences with online learning platforms.

Data Collection Period: The survey was conducted in two distinct phases:During COVID-19: A total of 500 responses were collected during the pandemic period, when educational institutions transitioned to fully online instruction due to lockdowns and social distancing mandates.Post COVID-19: An additional 969 responses were collected after the pandemic subsided, during which online and hybrid learning modalities continued to be offered as part of institutional flexibility initiatives.

Survey focus

The survey has been designed to comprehensively evaluate students’ perceptions and experiences with online learning, particularly in the post-pandemic educational landscape. The primary focus of the survey is to identify the cognitive, behavioral, emotional, and infrastructural factors influencing student satisfaction in digital learning environments.

To achieve this objective, the survey instrument incorporates a structured set of 21 variables, systematically categorized into the following thematic areas:Demographics and Contextual Information: Variables such as sex, location, division, and institutional type are included to examine demographic and regional diversity, enabling subgroup analysis across different educational and geographic contexts.Technological Access and Preparedness: This category includes variables such as device used, availability of resources, and IT skill level. The survey variables were explicitly designed to reflect core constructs from established theories of technology acceptance and learning motivation. Specifically, perceptual and usability-related variables–such as perceived learning equivalence, emotional response toward online classes, and perceived learning effectiveness–align with the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM). Variables related to institutional assistance, access to learning resources, lecturer attention, and technological infrastructure correspond to facilitating conditions within the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT). Furthermore, emotional, cognitive, and stress-related variables–such as self-efficacy, ability to concentrate, psychological stress, and perceived instructional support–reflect the autonomy and competence dimensions of Self-Determination Theory (SDT). This theoretical grounding ensures that both expert-driven FAHP weighting and SHAP-based model interpretation remain aligned with well-established learning theories, thereby strengthening the conceptual validity of the proposed framework.

FAHP process

The Fuzzy Analytic Hierarchy Process (FAHP) extends the conventional AHP method by incorporating fuzzy set theory to better address ambiguity and imprecision in expert judgments. By using fuzzy logic, FAHP accommodates the uncertainty associated with human reasoning during pairwise comparisons of criteria. This enhanced decision-making framework has been widely adopted in recent literature for its robustness in dealing with subjective data30.Fig. 3FAHP-based feature weighting for student satisfaction analysis.

Explainable AI (XAI) for feature interpretation

To enhance the transparency and interpretability of the machine learning model used for student satisfaction prediction, Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) techniques have been incorporated into the framework. In particular, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) and Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations (LIME) have been employed to provide both global and local insights into model behavior.XGBoost has been selected as the base model for generating SHAP and LIME explanations due to its high predictive accuracy, robustness to multicollinearity, and native compatibility with tree-based explanation methods like TreeSHAP33. Furthermore, XGBoost performs well on structured tabular data, which aligns with the feature-rich survey dataset used in this study

LIME-based local interpretability

While SHAP offers a global view, LIME provides instance-level explanations by approximating the original model locally using an interpretable surrogate model (e.g., linear regression). For each test instance, LIME perturbs the input data and observes changes in model output to learn a simplified model that mimics the behavior of the complex model around that particular point.

Cluster-wise XAI analysis

To further enhance the interpretability of the prediction model across heterogeneous student populations, SHAP and LIME analyses have been conducted separately for each satisfaction cluster (low, medium, and high) identified through agglomerative clustering. This subgroup-level interpretation reveals variations in feature influence and behavioral trends among distinct student satisfaction segments.

By integrating both SHAP and LIME, the proposed framework achieves high fidelity in explaining the model decisions, making it more reliable and actionable for educational researchers and policymakers.

Results and discussion

This section presents the empirical findings obtained through the proposed framework, which integrates multi-criteria decision analysis with explainable machine learning for evaluating student satisfaction with online learning environments. Discussion

The integrated findings from SHAP and LIME reveal a consistent pattern across satisfaction clusters, underscoring the primacy of perceptual, emotional, and institutional support–related factors in shaping students’ online learning experiences. Across all clusters, variables such as online_classes_are_helpful_as_same_as_offline_classes, feeling_about_online_class, and helped_by_college_or_university consistently emerged as dominant contributors to satisfaction, highlighting the importance of perceived instructional equivalence and institutional scaffolding. In contrast, technical competencies such as it_skills and time-related variables exhibited comparatively limited explanatory power, particularly among highly satisfied students. This indicates that satisfaction in online education is driven more strongly by affective and structural dimensions than by baseline technological proficiency. These empirical patterns are broadly consistent with established learning and technology-acceptance theories, suggesting that students’ perceptions of instructional usefulness, institutional support, and psychological well-being play a central role in shaping satisfaction outcomes. The convergence between global explanations obtained through SHAP and instance-level insights derived from LIME further reinforces the robustness and reliability of the proposed framework. The consistency of influential features across interpretability techniques strengthens confidence in the identified satisfaction drivers and provides actionable guidance for the design of learner-centered digital education strategies. In relation to existing literature, the findings of this study align with recent hybrid MCDM–XAI approaches that emphasize the integration of decision-analytic modeling with explainable machine learning for educational assessment. However, unlike prior student satisfaction studies that rely primarily on structural equation modeling (SEM) or standalone machine learning techniques, the proposed framework extends existing work by enabling both rank-aware satisfaction scoring and transparent explanation at both global and individual levels. This dual-layer interpretability enhances the practical usability of the model for institutional decision-making. In addition to the primary analysis, the added value of the proposed hybrid framework was examined through qualitative comparison with standalone approaches, including XGBoost combined with SHAP, FAHP–TOPSIS without explainability, and clustering without expert-informed weighting. While these individual methods provide partial insights, they lack either coherent satisfaction ranking, expert-driven prioritization, or transparent interpretability across satisfaction groups. In contrast, the integrated framework facilitates coherent satisfaction scoring, meaningful cluster-wise differentiation, and complementary global and local explanations, demonstrating analytical value beyond methodological complexity.

To validate the robustness of the proposed segmentation approach, a comparative analysis has been conducted between Agglomerative Hierarchical Clustering (AHC) and K-Means, both configured to produce three clusters based on FAHP-TOPSIS-derived satisfaction scores. The clustering quality has been evaluated using the Silhouette Coefficient, a widely accepted internal validation metric that quantifies intra-cluster cohesion and inter-cluster separation34. As shown in Fig. 25, AHC has achieved a higher silhouette score (0.5466) than K-Means (0.5326), indicating better-defined cluster boundaries and superior group separation. While K-Means is computationally efficient, it tends to favor balanced clusters due to its centroid-based optimization35. However, in real-world, human-centered datasets such as student satisfaction surveys, naturally imbalanced groupings are often meaningful. Prior research confirms that equal-sized clusters do not guarantee higher quality; instead, evaluation should prioritize cluster cohesion, separation, and semantic interpretability36. Moreover, earlier educational studies highlight the importance of recognizing uneven satisfaction subgroups23. The interpretability of AHC is further enhanced by its ability to retain hierarchical structure and enable dendrogram visualization. When combined with explainable AI techniques such as SHAP and LIME–shown to be effective in prior educational research22–AHC becomes a more context-aware and transparent clustering strategy, aligning well with the semantic structure of satisfaction levels (Low, Medium, High). These comparisons demonstrate that the proposed integration yields analytical value beyond methodological complexity, offering interpretability, ranking coherence, and subgroup differentiation not achievable by standalone approaches.

Theoretical implications and alignment with learning theories

The findings of this study align with well-established theories of technology adoption and learning motivation, reinforcing the theoretical grounding of the proposed framework. From the perspective of the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), constructs such as perceived equivalence between online and offline instruction and learners’ overall affective responses correspond to perceived usefulness and satisfaction, which are central determinants of technology acceptance. These constructs help explain how students evaluate the effectiveness of online learning environments in relation to their expectations and learning goals. Within the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT), variables associated with institutional support, availability of learning resources, and lecturer engagement reflect facilitating conditions that influence students’ satisfaction and sustained use of digital learning platforms. These conditions capture the extent to which the learning environment supports students’ academic activities and reduces barriers to effective participation.From a motivational perspective, Self-Determination Theory (SDT) provides a complementary lens for interpreting satisfaction dynamics. Constructs such as emotional engagement, self-efficacy, and stress relate closely to the fundamental psychological needs of autonomy, competence, and relatedness, which underpin intrinsic motivation and well-being in learning contexts. Collectively, these theoretical alignments strengthen the conceptual validity of the proposed framework and demonstrate that the modeled satisfaction drivers are grounded in robust and widely accepted learning and behavioral theories.

Conclusion

This study presented a comprehensive and interpretable framework for modeling student satisfaction in online learning environments by integrating the Fuzzy Analytic Hierarchy Process (FAHP), the Technique for Order of Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS), and explainable artificial intelligence techniques, including SHAP and LIME. Using a multidimensional dataset capturing behavioral, emotional, technological, and instructional attributes, the proposed framework effectively identified statistically relevant predictors through ANOVA and provided transparent, model-driven explanations of their influence. The combination of expert-informed weighting and rank-based satisfaction scoring enabled meaningful stratification of students into low, medium, and high satisfaction groups via agglomerative hierarchical clustering. The empirical findings consistently revealed that emotional engagement, perceived equivalence between online and offline instruction, institutional support, and psychological stress are the dominant drivers of student satisfaction, whereas demographic attributes and baseline technical competencies exert comparatively limited influence. The complementary use of global (SHAP) and local (LIME) explainability strengthened the transparency and robustness of the framework, facilitating actionable insights into both population-level trends and individual-level satisfaction dynamics. From a practical standpoint, the proposed framework offers educational institutions a transparent, data-informed decision-support tool to diagnose satisfaction gaps and prioritize targeted interventions across heterogeneous student groups. By combining expert judgment with interpretable machine learning, the framework supports learner-centered policy design and evidence-based improvement of online education systems. Theoretically, this work demonstrates the value of integrating explainable machine learning with fuzzy multi-criteria decision analysis for educational assessment, providing a theory-consistent and interpretable alternative to traditional regression- or SEM-based approaches. Despite its contributions, this study has several limitations. First, the empirical analysis is confined to higher education institutions within a single national context; therefore, generalization to other cultural or institutional settings should be approached with caution. Second, external validation was conducted on a very small subset (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n = 10$$\end{document}), which precludes inferential statistical claims and necessitates larger independent samples for confirmatory validation. Third, the methodological richness of the framework introduces implementation complexity, which may limit practical adoption without adequate computational infrastructure or access to expert knowledge. Future work will focus on cross-country and cross-institutional validation to strengthen generalizability, along with robustness testing using alternative weighting schemes and broader baseline comparisons. Longitudinal studies across academic semesters and integration with adaptive learning or recommender systems will be explored to enable real-time, personalized interventions. Additionally, incorporating behavioral analytics from learning management systems and conducting fairness-aware evaluations will further enhance the scalability, equity, and practical impact of the proposed framework.
