QuestBench: Can LLMs ask the right question to acquire information in reasoning tasks?
Belinda Z. Li, Been Kim, Zi Wang

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) have shown impressive performance on reasoning benchmarks like math and logic. While many works have largely assumed well-defined tasks, real-world queries are often underspecified and only solvable by acquiring missing information. We formalize this information-gathering problem as a constraint satisfaction problem (CSP) with missing variable assignments. Using a special case where only one necessary variable assignment is missing, we can evaluate an LLM’s ability to identify the minimal necessary question to ask. We present QuestBench , a set of underspecified reasoning tasks solvable by asking at most one question, which includes: (1) Logic-Q: logical reasoning tasks with one missing proposition, (2) Planning-Q: PDDL planning problems with partially-observed initial states, (3) GSM-Q: human-annotated grade school math problems with one unknown variable, and (4) GSME-Q: equation-based version of GSM-Q. The LLM must select the correct clarification question from multiple options. While current models excel at GSM-Q and GSME-Q, they achieve only 40-50% accuracy on Logic-Q and Planning-Q. Analysis shows that the ability to solve well-specified reasoning problems is not sufficient for success on our benchmark: models struggle to identify the right question even when they can solve the fully specified version. This highlights the need for specifically optimizing models’ information acquisition capabilities. Code and dataset are available at https://github.com/google-deepmind/questbench .

These applications typically assume all necessary information has been provided.

However, real-world scenarios often violate this assumption. Users may omit crucial details in math problems, and robots might operate in environments with partial observability. In such cases, LLMs need the ability to proactively gather missing information, for instance, by asking clarifying questions.

This work investigates whether LLMs can identify and acquire the missing information necessary to solve reasoning tasks. We
formalize this information-gathering problem as an underspecified Constraint Satisfaction Problem (CSP).
Our key idea is that many reasoning tasks can be modeled as determining the value of a target variable given a set of variables and constraints.
A problem is
underspecified
if and only if the value of the target variable cannot be inferred from the given information.
This formalization helps pinpoint the difference between semantic ambiguity (where multiple valid interpretations exist, but each yields a solvable answer) and underspecification (where the problem is unsolvable without additional information). We address the latter in this work.

Ambiguity in user requests.

Natural language queries often contain ambiguity for a variety of reasons. Prior work has examined ambiguity in the context of semantics
(
Kuhn et al., 2023b
)
, factual question-answering
(

In this paper, we focus on
underspecification

This situation can arise because users may not know what information the model lacks, or what information is necessary to complete the task. We evaluate LLMs’ ability to address underspecification in structured reasoning tasks.

Information gathering benchmarks for LLMs.

Most existing benchmarks focus on subjective or ambiguous tasks where there may be multiple valid clarifying questions, depending on context and user preference

Davani et al., 2022

(

involve inherently subjective problems where no universal “right” question exists. This makes objective evaluation of information-gathering abilities difficult in these settings.
In contrast, our work focuses on reasoning tasks with a clearly defined ground truth. For each task, the model needs to ask exactly one question, allowing for reliable evaluation of LLMs’ information-gathering capabilities.

Question-asking methods for LLMs.

Several methods have been proposed to enhance LLMs’ ability to ask clarifying questions. These methods primarily address ambiguous or knowledge-based tasks, such as identifying a good recipe
(
Andukuri et al., 2024

or asking who won a sports event
(

Pang et al., 2024

Some approaches directly prompt LLMs to ask clarifying questions

Kuhn et al., 2023a

Piriyakulkij et al., 2024
;
Grand et al., 2024
;
Handa et al., 2024
;
Hu et al., 2024

.

Zhang and Choi (2023)

While these methods are promising, they primarily focus on subjective tasks or require substantial user simulation. Our work introduces a new setting that emphasizes generating accurate clarifying questions for underspecified reasoning tasks, where the correct question is objectively determinable.

3.1
Distinguishing semantic ambiguity and underspecification

In this paper, we focus on problems that are semantically equivalent to CSPs,
2
2

2

This formulation encompasses queries where users have hard, deterministic preferences and expect a single correct answer. In general, we can think of user queries as a combination of (potentially probabilistic, fuzzy, or subjective) constraints and preferences.
which allow us to formalize underspecification.
Solving a problem thus comprises of two stages:

1.

A natural language query is parsed into a set of variables, a set of constraints, and a target variable corresponding to the desired response. In
Example

3.1
, there are variables
x_{0}
(initial eggs),
x_{1}
(eaten eggs), a constraint
y=x_{0}-x_{1}
, and a target variable
y
(current eggs) .

2.

The model solves for the target variable in the CSP.

This formulation allows us to distinguish
underspecification
(missing variables assignments or constraints in the CSP at stage 2) from
semantic ambiguity
(multiple interpretations of a natural language query, inducing a distribution over possible CSPs at step 1). While prior work extensively addresses semantic ambiguity
(
Kuhn et al., 2023b
)
, this paper specifically focuses on underspecification, which is objectively evaluable using our CSP framework.

3.2
Formalizing underspecification

We formalize underspecification as follows.
First, we define a CSP as a tuple
\langle X,D,C,A,y\rangle
:

•

X=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
is a set of
N
variables.

•

D=\{D_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
is a set of domains, where
D_{i}
is the domain of
x_{i}
.

•

C=\{c_{j}\}_{j=1}^{M}
is a set of
M
constraints. Each constraint
c_{j}
can be viewed as a boolean function that takes in as input a list of variables in
X
, denoted as
[x_{i}]_{i\in I_{j}}
, and outputs True or False. We use the shorthand
c_{j}({\bm{x}}_{j})
to denote
c_{j}([x_{i}]_{i\in I_{j}})
.

•

A=\land_{i\in I_{A}}(x_{i}=a_{i})
is a set of variable assignments, where each assignment is a pair
(x_{i},v_{i})
with
v_{i}\in D_{i}
.

•

y
is the target variable. Target
y
must satisfy the constraints and the variable assignments,
\bigwedge_{j\in[M]}c_{j}({\bm{x}}_{j})\land A.

The
Known
predicate.

For convenience, we use
\emph{Known}(x)
to denote the value of variable
x
is known.
This can occur either if
x
is directly assigned a value (i.e.
A\implies\land_{i\in I_{A}}\emph{Known}(x_{i})
), or
x
’s value can be derived from the existing assignments and constraints.
In
Example

3.1
, once
x_{0}
and
x_{1}
are known,
y
would also be known.

We can then formally define underspecified CSPs as follows.

Definition 3.1
.

A CSP is
underspecified
if and only if
\underset{j\in[M]}{\bigwedge}c_{j}({\bm{x}}_{j})\land A\implies\neg\textit{Known}(y)
. Otherwise it is
well-specified
.

We use
\emph{Known}(\mathcal{X})
over a set of variables
\mathcal{X}
to denote that the values of all variables in
\mathcal{X}
are known, i.e.,
\emph{Known}(\mathcal{X})=\bigwedge_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\emph{Known}(x)
.

If knowing a set of variables is sufficient to determine the value of another variable, we call the set the “
sufficient set
”:

Definition 3.2
.

A set of variables
\mathcal{X}\subseteq X
is a
sufficient set (for
y
)
if and only if

\displaystyle\underset{j\in[M]}{\bigwedge}c_{j}({\bm{x}}_{j})\land A\land\emph{Known}(\mathcal{X})\implies\emph{Known}(y).

For efficient question asking, we wish to find the smallest sufficient set for the target variable:

\hat{\mathcal{X}}={\argmin}_{\mathcal{X}\subseteq X}\;|\mathcal{X}|\;\quad\text{s.t.}\,\,\mathcal{X}\text{ is sufficient.}

In
Example

3.1
, a sufficient set for
y
is
\{x_{0}\}
since

\emph{Known}(\{x_{0}\})\implies\emph{Known}(y)
.

Finally, we define the special case where the size of the smallest sufficient set is 1.

Definition 3.3
.

An underspecified CSP is a
1-sufficient CSP
if the size of its smallest sufficient sets is 1. The variable in a smallest sufficient set is a
sufficient variable
of the problem.

Remarks.

There could be multiple sufficient variables for a 1-sufficient CSP (and in the general case, there could be multiple, equivalently-sized smallest sufficient sets for any underspecified CSP). In QuestBench, choosing
any
sufficient variable is treated as correct.

3.3
Why 1-sufficient CSPs?

QuestBench is comprised of 1-sufficient CSPs.
1
-sufficient CSPs are a fundamental building block towards
k
-sufficient CSPs.
Performance on simpler
1
-sufficient CSPs provides an approximate
upper bound
on a model’s capacity for information acquisition, since any
k
-sufficient CSP can become
1
-sufficient after
k-1
clarifying questions.

Another benefit of 1-sufficient CSPs is that they can be framed as multiple-choice problems which are easy to evaluate, by simply letting the model predict one choice. In contrast,
k
-sufficient problems may require questions to be asked sequentially, where the next question depends on the answer to the current question, making evaluation more complex. More discussions can be found in §
6
.

Finally, QuestBench includes significant variation in problem difficulty, even within the 1-sufficient setting, which we will describe in the next section.

3.4
Difficulty axes of 1-sufficient CSPs

Solving a 1-sufficient CSP entails identifying a sufficient variable for the target variable.
In general, 1-sufficient CSPs can be solved by brute-force or backward search.
Brute-force search checks the sufficiency of every variable one at a time. Backward search builds a search tree starting from the target variable (root node) and recursively searches all variables (child nodes) in each constraint (edges) involving the parent node.
These two methods allow us to formalize the following properties associated with the difficulty of each 1-sufficient CSP:

•

|X|
: The total number of variables.

•

|C|
: The total number of constraints.

•

d
: The depth of the backward search tree.

•

\mathbb{E}_{\text{BF}}
: The expected number of random guesses until selecting a correct question.
3
3

3

We assume the random guesses are made without replacement, so
\mathbb{E}_{\text{BF}}=\frac{|X|+1}{\mathfrak{s}+1}
, where
\mathfrak{s}
is the number of sufficient variables.

For example, in
Example

3.1
, there are
|X|=3
variables (
x_{0},x_{1},y
),
|C|=1
constraint (
y=x_{0}-x_{1}
), and
d=1
search depth.
Among choices
(a) No questions needed.
,
(b) What is the value of
x_{0}
?
,
(c) What is the value of
x_{1}
?
, only choice (b) is correct, so
the expected number of guesses
\mathbb{E}_{\text{BF}}=2
.

Planning-Q

Planning-Q is based on the Blocks World domain from PyperPlan
5
5

5

.

(
Alkhazraji et al., 2020
)
, a classic planning problem where the goal is to rearrange a set of blocks from an initial state to a goal state.
Blocks World consists of
predicates
(e.g.
(ontable ?a)
), which can be applied to blocks (e.g.
c
) to construct an
atom
(e.g.,
(ontable c)
). A
state
is a conjunction of atoms or their negations, e.g.
(ontable a), (holding b),
\neg
(on a b)
.

Not all states are valid; in a

valid state
, there are one or more actions that can be applied to it to transition to another state. Each action has a
precondition
specifying atoms the state must satisfy before applying the action, and an
effect
specifying atoms the next state must satisfy after applying the action. For example,
stack(?a, ?b)
has preconditions
(holding ?a), (clear ?b)
and effect
\neg
(holding ?a),
\neg
(clear ?b), (clear ?a), (handempty), (on ?a ?b)

Given an initial state and a goal state,

Problem Definition

The problem can be expressed as a CSP
\langle X,D,C,A,y\rangle
as follows:

•

X
is the set of all atoms (predicates applied to objects) in the initial state.

•

D=\{\{\textsc{True},\textsc{False}\}\,\forall x\in X\}
. Each atom must be either true or false.

•

A
is the set of atoms that we know to be true or false in the initial state.

•

C
is the set of constraints defining the action dynamics of the Blocks World domain. More formally, for any sequence of actions
[q_{t}]_{t\in[n]}
and their corresponding sequence of states
[s_{t}]_{t\in[n]}

s_{t}
and next state
s_{t+1}
must satisfy the precondition and effect of action
q_{t}

\displaystyle\forall p\in\textsc{pre}(q_{t}),\,s_{t}\Rightarrow p\;\text{ (preconditions of $q_{t}$ must hold in $s_{t}$)}

\displaystyle\forall e\in\textsc{effects}(q_{t}),\,s_{t+1}\Rightarrow e\;\text{ (effects of $q_{t}$ must hold in $s_{t+1}$)}

•

y
is the shortest action sequence from the initial state to the goal state.
6
6

6

It is not meaningful to set

to be
any
action sequence (not just the shortest one) to the goal, since all initial states can get to the goal state through some path in BlocksWorld, so no information needs to be gathered.

from the word problem.

Annotation Details

We recruited a total of 21 annotators (11 male, 10 female) from five countries to annotate our tasks.
Annotators were all fluent in English and between the ages of 25 – 45. We paid an average of $27 – $55 per hour per annotator.

5.3

•

In

, we used the original GSM-plus math problems as-is.

•

In

6
Discussion and Conclusion

We introduced
QuestBench
for identifying underspecification and asking clarification questions in reasoning problems.
We found that SoTA LLMs (as of 03/2025) are relatively good at identifying missing information in simple algebra problems, but struggle with complex tasks involving logic and planning.
Their performance is sometimes negatively correlated with factors that increase problem complexity, such as search depth and expected number of brute-force guesses.
Our findings suggest asking the right question requires more than the ability to reason with sufficient information.

When designing QuestBench, we made several design decisions for ease and rigor of evaluation, some of which limit the generalizability of our benchmark.
Our framework serves as a first step towards formalizing underspecification. Below are some open problems that require future work.

Constructing
k
-sufficient CSPs.
Our framework can be extended to problems with multiple missing variables. This can be done by holding out
k
variable values, instead of just one, to construct
k
-sufficient CSPs. When doing so, it is crucial to consider whether the evaluation is for selecting all
k
questions simultaneously (in a batch), selecting them sequentially (one by one), or a combination of the two: selecting batch by batch sequentially.

Simulating users that answer questions.
Successfully handling complex, real-world tasks like medical diagnosis and intent elicitation often necessitates an iterative dialogue with multiple clarification questions. A major bottleneck in developing such systems is the challenge of automating the user’s role for testing and evaluation. Building a simulator that can realistically answer the model’s queries is a non-trivial task
(
Gui and Toubia, 2023
)
.

Metrics of question-asking.
In practice, we might care about metrics beyond the correctness of clarification questions, for example, how easy the clarification questions are for the users to answer
(
Hahn et al., 2025
;
Li et al., 2023
;
Grand et al., 2024
)
.

We believe the multiple-choice format for evaluating question-asking is a necessary precursor to open-ended formats for question asking, since it is valuable for automated and rigorous evaluation. However, once a new approach makes open-ended evaluation viable and reliable, our benchmark can be readily used by simply omitting the multiple-choice options.

1.

For all other
\tilde{s_{0}}^{\prime\prime}\in\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{0}
, we check whether
\exists x\in X,\tilde{s_{0}}^{\prime\prime}=\tilde{s_{0}}^{\prime}\land x
, meaning that a single additional true atom brings us to another 1-sufficient partial state.
In this case, we assign the salient atom
x

\tilde{s_{0}}^{\prime\prime}=\tilde{s_{0}}^{\prime}\land x
to

x

\tilde{s_{0}}^{\prime\prime}
, which we know is 1-sufficient, meaning we must ask at least one other question.

2.

For all physically-valid, well-specified states
s_{0}^{\prime}\in F(\tilde{s_{0}}^{\prime})
consistent with the 1-sufficient state
\tilde{s_{0}}^{\prime}
, we check that there are at most two possible distinct optimal action sequences to the goal condition
y
:

\left|\{\Psi_{y}(s_{0}^{\prime}),\,\,\forall s_{0}^{\prime}\in F(\tilde{s_{0}}^{\prime}\}\right|\leq 2

(a)

If there is 1 unique action sequence, then we expect the LM response to be “
No questions needed
.”

(b)

If there are 2 unique action sequences
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}
, then we separated out
\tilde{S}_{0}

\tilde{S}_{0}^{(1)}
and
\tilde{S}_{0}^{(2)}
, where the optimal action sequence from all states in
\tilde{S}_{0}^{(1)}
to the goal is
\tau_{1}

\tilde{S}_{0}^{(1)}
to the goal is
\tau_{2}

\tilde{S}_{0}=\tilde{S}_{0}^{(1)}\cup\tilde{S}_{0}^{(2)}
). We find the set of differentiating attributes between
\tilde{S}_{0}^{(1)}

\tilde{S}_{0}^{(2)}

\tilde{S}_{0}^{(1)}
that aren’t present in any state of
\tilde{S}_{0}^{(2)}
, or vice versa (all attributes of states in
\tilde{S}_{0}^{(2)}
that aren’t present in any state of
\tilde{S}_{0}^{(1)}

\tau_{1}

\tau_{2}
.

This ensures that for all
\tilde{s_{0}}^{\prime}

x_{d}

\tilde{s_{0}}^{\prime}

Appendix D
Search solutions for Logic-Q and Planning-Q

D.1
Logic-Q

Brute-force Solution.

A problem in Logic-Q can be solved through brute force search as follows. We have a subroutine
infer
that allows us to infer the values of all variables consistent with the current variable assignments. For example, if
A=\{a,b\}
and we know that
a\land b\to c
, then
\texttt{infer}(A)
gives us
\{a,b,c\}
.
At a high-level,
infer
is analogous to breadth-first-search, and thus has runtime complexity
O(|X|+|C|)
.

To solve a Logic-Q problem,
we first run
infer
to get values of all variables consistent with the current assignment
A
, creating
A^{\prime}
. Next, for all unassigned variables
x_{u}\in X\backslash A^{\prime}
, we iterate through them one at a time and check whether:

1.

Including
x_{u}
in
A^{\prime}
allows us to infer the target variable is either
true
or
false
.

2.

Include
\neg x_{u}
in
A^{\prime}
allows us to infer the target variable is the
opposite assignment
as it was in case (1).

The first time 1 and 2 both hold, we have that
x_{u}
is true.

The expected runtime of brute-force can thus be computed by the expected number of variables that we need to iterate through to get to a variable in the sufficient set (upperbounded by
\mathbb{E}_{\text{BF}}
in §
3.4
), multiplied by the complexity of running the
infer
algorithm twice.
By Markov’s inequality, with probability
\geq 1-\delta
, the complexity is bounded by

\displaystyle O((|X|+|C|)\mathbb{E}_{\text{BF}}/\delta).

Backwards Search Solution.

A problem in Logic-Q can be solved through backwards search.
The procedure is the same as the backwards search used to construct the dataset, described in
Section

B.1.1
.
At each iteration, we keep track of a disjunction of conjunction of variables required to prove
y
.

The complexity of backwards search is given by the search depth
d
multiplied by the number of expansions per depth,
which is bounded by
O(3^{|X|}|C|)
(expanding up to
|C|
rules for conjunction, for which there are at most
3^{|X|}
conjunctions – each variable can be either true/false/missing from that conjunction)
Thus, the overall complexity is
O(3^{|X|}|C|d)
.

Though comprehensive backwards search is EXP-time, we can terminate early as soon as we find a disjunction consisting of the negation of all initial conditions, the goal variable, and an additional term, which would mean that asking about the value of the initial term is sufficient to infer the value of the goal variable.

If we know this disjunction is at most at depth
d
, then the runtime is bounded by

|X|^{|C|d}
, where
|X|^{|C|}
is the branching factor at each node. The branching factor comes from the cross product of at most
|X|
terms across at most
|C|
conjunctive constraints. Thus, the total runtime is given by

O(|X|^{|C|d}).

D.2
Planning-Q

Brute-force Solution.

As described in §
3.4
, our brute-force approach randomly samples from the choices without replacement, and checks each sampled choice until a correct one is found. The complexity of this approach relies on the complexity of validating whether a choice is correct or not.

Given an underspecified initial state
\tilde{s_{0}}
and a selected choice of atom
x
, we can generate all physically-plausible initial states
s^{+}_{0}\in F(\tilde{s_{0}},x)
and
s^{-}_{0}\in F(\tilde{s_{0}},\neg x)
, where function
F
maps from a partial state to all states consistent with the partial state.
We can then solve the planning problem for
all
initial states in both sets, and check whether the following holds.

1.

The shortest path
\hat{\tau}^{+}
remains the same for every initial
s^{+}_{0}\in F(\tilde{s_{0}},x)
.

2.

The shortest path
\hat{\tau}^{-}
remains the same for every initial
s^{-}_{0}\in F(\tilde{s_{0}},\neg x)
.

3.

\hat{\tau}^{+}
and
\hat{\tau}^{-}
are different.

Assume the planning tool we use is breadth-first search, which takes at most
O(|S|+|S||\mathcal{A}|)
time. We perform breadth-first search at most
|S|
times (since
|F(\tilde{s_{0}},\neg x)\cup F(\tilde{s_{0}},\neg x)|<|S|
), meaning the overall runtime of this solution is
O((|S|+|S||\mathcal{A}|)|S|)
.
The number of states is bounded by
|S|=2^{|X|}
, while the number of actions is bounded by
|\mathcal{A}|=2b+2b(b+1)=O(b^{2})
where
b
is the number of blocks in the domain, so the overall complexity of validating a choice is
O(2^{2|X|}b^{2})
.

Similar to §
D.1
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, we only need to guess
\mathbb{E}_{BF}/\delta
times. So the overall complexity is

O(2^{2|X|}b^{2}\mathbb{E}_{BF}/\delta).

Backward Search Solution.

We replace the breadth-first-search from each consistent state with a single backwards search.
Starting from the goal condition, we iterate backwards to find sets of initial states that utilize the same path to the goal. This is the same backwards search that was used to construct all well-specified partial states, described in
Section

B.2.1
. We expand backwards until we arrive at a set of partial initial states that are consistent with the given partially-observed set
\tilde{s_{0}}
, and we find the attribute that distinguishes each partial initial state from each other.

Because the number of partial states is bounded by
3^{|X|}
(each proposition can take on 3 values: true/false/unknown), the backwards breadth-first search takes
O(3^{|X|}+3^{|X|}|\mathcal{A}|)
. However, if we know that the longest path from any initial state consistent with
\tilde{s_{0}}
to the goal is of length
d
, meaning we only need to search up to depth
d
,
then the runtime is bounded by

\displaystyle O(|\mathcal{A}|^{d})=O(b^{2d})\text{ or }O(3^{|X|d})

(Each iteration, expand at most
3^{|X|}
states or
|\mathcal{A}|
actions.)

Appendix F
Computational Resources

Generating the dataset for the Logic-Q and Planning-Q mostly requires CPU resources to conduct the backwards search. The search and filtering stages for creating each domain required a few (between three to seven) days each. For experiments, we queried Gemini, GPT-4o, and Claude models through the API. We launched Gemma models on either TPU v5e node pools, or 1-2 H100 GPUs, and ran inference with them.

Evaluating each model took a few hours for Logic-Q, up to a few days for Planning-Q, and a few minutes for GSM-Q / GSME-Q.

Appendix G
Limitations

Our work includes a few key assumptions that may limit its generalizability and practicality. For example,
QuestBench
focuses exclusively on 1-sufficient CSPs, isolating the minimal case of information acquisition. However, real-world problems often include multiple missing pieces of information or more complex dependency structures.
QuestBench
represents a first attempt at dealing with underspecification, favoring formalism and ease of evaluation over generality.
Furthermore, our domains were chosen for their clear CSP formalizations, but may not capture the full range of naturalistic language understanding and ambiguity found in everyday tasks.
Finally, using multiple choice for question selection simplifies evaluation and ensures consistency, but may overestimate models’ performance compared to open-ended question generation.

Appendix H
Broader Impacts

By highlighting gaps in current LLMs’ ability to ask clarification questions, this work could guide the development of more interactive, trustworthy AI systems. Better information-seeking behavior is critical for high-stakes domains such as education, healthcare, and scientific research, where clarifying underspecified inputs can reduce misunderstandings and improve decision-making. Moreover, our benchmark offers a well-defined setting for studying model uncertainty and epistemic humility, which are increasingly important for alignment and safety. However, improved question-asking abilities could be misused in automated persuasion, e.g., models that dynamically elicit personal information through carefully crafted queries. While QuestBench does not train models to ask open-ended questions, the underlying capabilities evaluated could inform systems with stronger elicitation power. Coupling queries with rationales for the model’s query may allow user greater control over the information shared.
