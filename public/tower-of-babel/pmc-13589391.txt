The impact of the second victim phenomenon on nurse turnover intentions and absenteeism: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Jing Liu, Dandan Ma, Jinrong He, Danfeng Tan, Ling Zhang, Xin Peng, Muhammad Sohaib, Shuoting Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Second Victim Phenomenon (SVP) is the psychological trauma that healthcare workers experience due to their negative experiences with patients. Its incidence in nurses is well known, but its quantitative implications on nurse retention and attendance are poorly understood. This systematic review and meta-analysis summarizes the evidence regarding the relationship between SVP and nurse turnover intentions and absenteeism.MethodsA systematic search of the PubMed, Embase, CINAHL, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library (CENTRAL) databases was conducted through April 2026, following PRISMA guidelines. The included studies were cross-sectional studies that investigated the relationship between SVP and turnover intentions or absenteeism among nurses. Two independent reviewers used the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) to screen, extract data, and evaluate the quality. Random-effects models were used to pool effect sizes in meta-analyses. Heterogeneity was measured using both the I2 statistic and the Cochran Q test. Subgroup analyses were performed to examine differences across geographic regions, healthcare settings, and study quality. Publication bias was measured using funnel plots and the Egger test. The quality of evidence was determined according to GRADE criteria.ResultsA total of 38 cross-sectional studies were included, comprising 22,241 participants in the turnover intention analysis and 14,662 participants in the absenteeism analysis. Meta-analysis revealed that nurses experiencing SVP had significantly higher odds of turnover intention (pooled OR = 2.99, 95% CI: 2.81–3.18, p < 0.001, I² = 75.9%) and increased risk of absenteeism (pooled RR = 2.21, 95% CI: 2.05–2.37, p < 0.001, I² = 82.6%). There was significant study heterogeneity. Subgroup analyses showed that the effects were similar in varied geographic areas and health care settings. There was no significant publication bias (Egger test p > 0.05 in both results). GRADE evaluation showed low-quality evidence due to the observational study design and heterogeneity, but upgraded the effect size to large.ConclusionsThis meta-analysis provides strong evidence that SVP is significantly associated with higher turnover intention and absenteeism among nurses, posing a serious risk to the stability of the health care workforce. The results highlight the importance of organizational interventions to support nurses with SVP, such as peer support programs, psychological counseling, and changes in institutional culture. Future studies need to use longitudinal designs to examine temporal relationships and intervention studies to assess the effectiveness of support programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12912-026-05247-x.Keywords: Second victim phenomenon, Nurses, Turnover intention, Absenteeism, Meta-analysis, Systematic review, Healthcare workforce

Introduction

Healthcare staff are challenged to an unprecedented level in providing quality care and dealing with the psychological impact of negative patient events [1]. The Second Victim Phenomenon (SVP) is a psychological, emotional, and professional burden that healthcare professionals may encounter after being involved in an unwanted adverse patient event, medical error, or patient-related injury [2]. The phenomenon has become a major issue in healthcare facilities worldwide, not only in relation to the health of individual practitioners but also the functionality of the healthcare system as a whole and the safety of patients. Nurses are particularly susceptible to developing SVP, as they are the largest group in the healthcare workforce and provide round-the-clock patient care [3]. Research shows that 10–43% of nurses have reported experiencing second victim trauma after an adverse event [4]. The psychological effect may be in the form of anxiety, depression, guilt, fear of litigation, loss of confidence, and post-traumatic stress symptoms [5]. These emotional aftereffects may persist for a few months or even years after the event that triggered them, and they severely affect performance at work and the individual’s overall well-being [6].

SVP is a continuum of emotions, cognitions, and behavioral reactions that healthcare providers experience following adverse events [7]. The Second Victim Experience and Support Tool (SVEST) comprises seven dimensions of the second victim experience: psychological distress, physical distress, concerns about professional self-efficacy, support from colleagues, support from supervisors, institutional support, and turnover intentions [8]. According to this multidimensional model, the interaction between individual psychological reactions and organizational conditions in the context of the second victim experience is complex and multifaceted [9]. The rate of exposure to adverse events and resultant psychological distress is higher among nurses working in different healthcare environments and units [10]. Intensive care units, emergency departments, and surgical units have been considered especially at risk of developing SVP. Repeated experience of adverse events may result in emotional fatigue, burnout, and moral distress, which may also worsen the second victim experience [11].

The effects of SVP are not limited to personal impact but also extend to organizational impact, including workforce retention and attendance. Turnover intention, the intentional and voluntary desire to leave an organization, is not only a pivotal antecedent of turnover but has also been recognized as a significant issue in nursing workforce management [9]. The high turnover rates not only incur financial costs estimated at 1.3 to 2 times the annual salary per nurse but also undermine continuity of care, team cohesiveness, and patient safety outcomes [12]. Another area of significant organizational concern associated with SVP is Absenteeism, which can be defined as unplanned or excessive absenteeism [13]. Nurses who are psychologically distressed after adverse events can take sick leave to deal with emotional trauma, prevent any triggering situations, or deal with physical manifestations of stress. Absenteeism distorts staffing trends, adds pressure on other staff members, and may negatively affect the quality of patient care [14].

Although the links between SVP and turnover intentions or absenteeism have been recorded in individual studies, the current body of evidence is disjointed and cannot be synthesized quantitatively. Past reviews have mostly focused on qualitative accounts of the second victim experience or prevalence estimates, but have not systematically investigated the extent of the association with workforce outcomes [15]. The study-to-study heterogeneity in study design, measurement instruments, and population diversity makes a complex meta-analysis method essential for combining effect sizes and investigating sources of variation. Moreover, most available studies are cross-sectional, which makes causal inference problematic; however, combining the effect sizes across several studies can yield more accurate estimates of the strength of association and reveal regularities across settings.

This systematic review and meta-analysis will, therefore, seek to: (1) quantitatively synthesize the relationship between SVP and nurse turnover intentions; (2) test the relationship between SVP and nurse absenteeism; (3) test heterogeneity of the studies and discuss possible moderators in the form of subgroup analysis; (4) test publication bias and quality of evidence; and (5) make evidence-based recommendations on organizational inter. By addressing these aims, this research will build a solid evidence base to understand the role of SVP in shaping nursing workforce outcomes and inform strategies to address the needs of second victims in healthcare environments.

Methods

Study design and protocol

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement [16]. The Cochrane Handbook of Systematic Reviews served as the methodological framework [17]. The study protocol was pre-registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF) on May 2, 2026 (DOI https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/TVXY7) [18]. This pre- registration helps ensure transparency and reduce bias in reporting results.

Search strategy

The literature search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, CINAHL, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library (CENTRAL) from inception to April 15, 2026. The search terms were as follows: “second victim”, “nurse”, “nursing staff”, “turnover intention”, “absenteeism”, and “sick leave”. Population, exposure, and outcome terms were combined using Boolean operators, and the reference lists of included studies were identified. The full search string was applied in PubMed as follows. ((“Second Victim Phenomenon“[MeSH Terms] OR “second victim*“[Title/Abstract]) AND (“Nurses“[MeSH Terms] OR “Nursing Staff, Hospital“[MeSH Terms] OR “nurse*“[Title/Abstract]) AND (“Personnel Turnover“[MeSH Terms] OR “Absenteeism“[MeSH Terms] OR “turnover“[Title/Abstract] OR “intent to leave“[Title/Abstract] OR “sick leave“[Title/Abstract]))

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

To select studies for this review, the PICOS (Population, Intervention, Context, Outcomes, and Study Design) framework was used to ensure a complete and transparent selection of literature. To be included, studies had to target registered nurses in any clinical specialty or healthcare setting. The main exposure of concern referred to the Second Victim Phenomenon (SVP), which refers to the physical or psychological suffering of a nurse after an adverse patient experience, clinical error, or near-miss. Moreover, eligible studies were required to provide quantitative information on at least one of two main workforce outcomes: turnover intentions (self-reported intent to leave the current job or the nursing profession) or the proportion or length of sick leave, directly related to the psychological effects of a clinical event. Regarding methodological rigor, we restricted the review to analytical cross-sectional studies published in peer-reviewed journals. To perform the meta-analysis, included papers had to present adequate statistical information, i.e., Odds Ratios (OR), Risk Ratios (RR), correlation coefficients, or means and standard deviations, to compute the pooled effect sizes. To extract and interpret accurate information, the search was limited to English-language studies.

Exclusion criteria

Several criteria were used to rule out studies that were not in line with the objectives of the review. We did not include non-empirical sources, such as editorials, letters to the editor, case reports, theoretical frameworks, or gray literature (e.g., book chapters or conference abstracts) that lacked full-text peer review. Mixed healthcare studies (e.g., a mix of physicians, pharmacists, and nurses) were excluded unless information specific to nurses could be isolated. These stringent requirements were adopted to ensure that the synthesized results are directly representative of the nursing workforce’s experiences, thereby forming a strong evidence base for the meta-analysis.

Study selection

The Selection of studies was carried out in two steps to achieve methodological rigor and reduce investigator bias. First, the titles and abstracts of all records from the electronic search were screened by two independent reviewers against the preset eligibility criteria. Records that did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded at this point. After that, the full texts of all eligible articles were retrieved and independently rated by the two reviewers to determine final inclusion. Discrepancies or disagreements in the screening process were resolved through discussion and consensus; if consensus could not be reached, a third senior reviewer was consulted. The outcomes of the selection procedure are recorded in a PRISMA flow diagram, which shows the number of studies identified, sifted, and excluded, along with the reasons for exclusion at the full-text phase.

Data extraction

After the final Selection of the studies, data extraction was conducted by two independent reviewers using a standardized, pilot-tested data extraction form to ensure consistency and accuracy. The information that was extracted comprised the study characteristics (e.g., author, year of publication, geographic location, and healthcare setting), participant characteristics (e.g., sample size of registered nurses, mean age, and years of experience), and the tools that were used to measure the Second Victim Phenomenon, including the Second Victim Experience and Support Tool (SVEST). In addition, odds ratios (ORs), relative risks (RRs), and correlation coefficients (r) were obtained as raw effect sizes for the key workforce outcomes of turnover intentions and absenteeism. When essential data were not provided or supplied in a usable format, the key authors were contacted by email to provide the required information. Both reviewers cross-examined the final extraction sheet to confirm data integrity, and then the meta-analysis began.

Quality assessment

Two reviewers (MS, MD) independently assessed the methodological quality and risk of bias of each included Study using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) to assess cross-sectional studies. It is a validated tool used to evaluate studies across three basic domains: the Selection of study groups, the comparability of cohorts, and the determination of the outcome of interest. The studies were graded using a star-based system, with a maximum possible score of 10. Any differences in quality scoring were resolved after discussion or consultation with a third senior reviewer to reach consensus. Although no studies were filtered by quality score, the findings informed the GRADE certainty ratings and were used to conduct subgroup analyses by risk of bias.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

Meta-analytic procedures were performed using Review Manager (RevMan) software (Version 5.4) and R (Version 4.8.0). To ensure consistency across the diverse reporting styles of the 38 included studies, data were converted into For turnover intentions, Odds Ratios (OR) served as the primary effect size metric; for Absenteeism, Risk Ratios (RR) were calculated. In cases where studies reported results as continuous correlation coefficients (r), these were converted to ORs using standard formulae to allow a single pooled estimate. Given the anticipated variability in nursing populations and healthcare environments, a random-effects model (using the DerSimonian-Laird method) was employed for all analyses. This model was chosen as it accounts for both within-study and between-study variance. Statistical heterogeneity was rigorously assessed using Cochran’s Q test (with a significance level of p < 0.10 and the I2 statistic. Following standard conventions, I2 values of 25%, 50%, and 75% were interpreted as representing low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively.

Subgroup and bias analysis

Pre-specified subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of statistical heterogeneity. These searches limited studies based on geographic area (e.g., Asia, Europe, North America), healthcare facility (e.g., Intensive Care Units, Oncology, General Wards), and JBI risk of bias scores (High vs. Low quality). The possibility of publication bias was assessed in two ways: visually by checking funnel plots for asymmetry, and using the Egger linear regression test (significant publication bias when p < 0.05). If evidence of publication bias is found, the Duval and Tweedie trim-and-fill method would be used to estimate how the missing studies would have affected the overall cumulative outcome. Lastly, the overall confidence in the evidence supporting each major outcome was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) scale. The framework evaluated the evidence on five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency of results, indirectness of evidence, imprecision of effect estimates, and publication bias, leading to a classification of High, Moderate, Low, or Very Low certainty.

Results

Study selection

The initial search revealed 450 records. After duplicates were removed, 70 records were filtered by title and abstract, resulting in the elimination of 8 inappropriate articles. We identified 62 full-text articles to be retrieved and evaluated for eligibility. After full-text screening, 24 studies were excluded for the following reasons: non-cross-sectional study design (n = 8), lack of quantitative data (n = 7), study outcomes that did not measure turnover intentions or absenteeism (n = 5), and a study population that was not nurses. The remaining 38 studies were assessed for quality using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale. The 38 studies were of adequate quality (scoring 6 or more stars) and were included in the final meta-analysis. Study characteristics

The 38 included studies were published between 2005 and 2025, following a thorough search up to April 2026, and covered various geographic regions: Europe (n = 13, 34.2%), Asia (n = 12, 31.6%), North America (n = 9, 23.7%), and Oceania (n = 4, 10%). Turnover intention and absenteeism had total sample sizes of 22,241 and 14,662 nurses, respectively. Academic medical centers (44.7%) and community hospitals (28.9%) were the primary settings, and the majority of participants (mean 82.4) were female and worked in a medical-surgical or intensive care unit. The Second Victim Experience and Support Tool (SVEST, 63.2%) proved essential for measuring the second victim phenomenon. The most common measure of turnover intentions was the Anticipated Turnover Scale, administered as a self-report measure, and the measure of absenteeism was mostly based on self-reported sick leave (n = 18 and n = 22, respectively). Characteristics and methodological quality of the included studies (N = 38)

Quality assessment

Quality assessment using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale indicated that most studies were of high quality. 38 included studies, 26 (68.4%) scored 7–10 stars (high quality), 11 (28.9%) scored 4–6 stars (moderate quality), and 1 (2.6%) scored below 4 stars (low quality). The mean NOS score was 7.2 (SD = 1.4, range 3–10). The most common methodological strengths were: representative sampling frames (n = 32, 84.2%), validated outcome measures (n = 35, 92.1%), and sufficient response rates > 60% (n = 28, 73.7%). Methodological weaknesses included: lack of non-respondent analysis (n = 18, 47.4%), inadequate control of confounding variables (n = 15, 39.5%), and the use of self-reported measures of both exposure and outcome (n = 30, 78.9%). The risk of bias assessment indicated a low risk in most areas. The cross-sectional nature of the studies, which restricted the ability to control temporal confounding, rated selection bias as low risk in 31 studies (81.6%), measurement bias as low risk in 28 studies (73.7%), and confounding bias as moderate risk in 22 studies (57.9%). Meta-analysis results

Turnover intentions

A meta-analysis of 38 studies (22,241 participants) showed a strong positive correlation between SVP and turnover intentions. The pooled odds ratio from random-effects modeling was 2.99 (95% CI: 2.81–3.18, p < 0.001), indicating that nurses with SVP were almost three times more likely to report turnover intentions than nurses without SVP. This is illustrated in the forest plot (Fig. 3). Statistical heterogeneity was considerable (I² = 75.9%, Q = 153.40, df = 37, p < 0.001, τ² = 0.0233). The 95% prediction interval ranged from 2.22 to 4.03, indicating that although most future studies would likely find positive associations, the magnitude of the association may differ significantly across settings. The sensitivity analysis excluding the single poor-quality Study yielded the same results (pooled OR = 2.98, 95% CI: 2.80–3.17), demonstrating the robustness of the findings. Analysis of leave-one-out showed that no single study had a disproportionate effect on the pooled estimate, with the pooled ORs ranging from 2.94 to 3.03 as individual studies were sequentially dropped.

Absenteeism

A meta-analysis of 31 studies (14,662 subjects) showed a significant positive relationship between SVP and Absenteeism. The combined risk ratio was 2.21 (95% CI: 2.05–2.37, p < 0.001), indicating that nurses with SVP experience had over twice the risk of absenteeism compared to those without SVP experience (Fig. 4). Considerable heterogeneity was observed (I² = 82.6%, Q = 172.58, df = 30, p < 0.001, τ² = 0.0313). The 95% prediction interval was 1.56 to 3.13, indicating that the expected effect sizes across settings and populations vary. Sensitivity analyses ensured the consistency of results. When analysis is restricted to studies that used organizational records to measure absenteeism (n = 6), the resulting pooled RR of 2.18 (95% CI: 1.89–2.51) is similar to the overall estimate. Limiting the analysis to studies with a high NOS score (n = 21) yielded a pooled RR of 2.24 (95% CI: 2.06–2.43).

Subgroup analyses

Geographic region

To determine the effect of geographic location on the relationships between the second victim phenomenon and the primary outcomes, subgroup analysis was employed. The results showed that positive associations were consistently observed across all regions, and there was no significant evidence of between-subgroup heterogeneity for either turnover intention or absenteeism. To see the detailed breakdown of results by region, see Appendix 1.

The correlation with turnover intention was significant across all four continents (see Appendix 1). In Asia (n = 12), the pooled OR was 3.10 (95% CI: 2.76–3.48; I² = 40.4%), similar to findings in North America (n = 9; pooled OR = 3.10, 95% CI: 2.76–3.48; I² = 6.8%) (See Fig. 5). Studies conducted in Europe (n = 13) reported a pooled OR of 2.95 (95% CI: 2.68–3.25; I² = 30.6%), while Oceania (n = 4) showed a pooled OR of 2.68 (95% CI: 2.27–3.17; I² = 0.0%). The same consistency was noted with absenteeism. In Europe (n = 11), the pooled RR was 2.27 (95% CI: 2.05–2.52; I² = 42.6%). This was followed by Asia (n = 9) with a pooled RR of 2.22 (95% CI: 1.95–2.52; I² = 0.0%) and Oceania (n = 3) with a pooled RR of 2.19 (95% CI: 1.83–2.64; I² = 69.0%). Study quality

The sensitivity analysis based on methodological quality, as measured by the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), revealed that high-quality studies (NOS score ≥ 7) showed slightly larger effect sizes than those of medium-quality studies. Turnover intentions: Among high-quality studies (n = 26), the association with turnover intentions was robust, with a pooled OR of 3.05 (95% CI: 2.85–3.27; I² = 69.2%). The studies of moderate quality (n = 12) had a slightly smaller but still significant association, with a pooled OR of 2.84 (95% CI: 2.553.16; I 2 = 45.3%). For the absenteeism outcome, high-quality studies (n = 21) reported a pooled RR of 2.24 (95% CI: 2.06–2.43; I² = 78.4%). Studies categorized as moderate quality (n = 10) demonstrated a pooled RR of 2.13 (95% CI: 1.88–2.41; I² = 62.7%). The results of subgroup analysis are described in detail in Appendix 1.

Meta-regression analyses

Meta-regression analyses were conducted to determine the effects of continuous moderators on the observed between-study heterogeneity. Turnover intentions showed a marginal positive association with publication year (β = 0.018, SE = 0.009, p = 0.052). Other continuous variables, including mean participant age (β = -0.012, SE = 0.015, p = 0.423) and the percentage of female participants (β = 0.004, SE = 0.008, p = 0.617), did not emerge as significant moderators. Absenteeism did not account for a significant proportion of the observed between-study heterogeneity of any of the evaluated continuous moderators. This included publication year (β = 0.011, SE = 0.011, p = 0.329), mean participant age (β = -0.008, SE = 0.018, p = 0.656), and the percentage of female participants (β = 0.002, SE = 0.010, p = 0.841). The results of these analyses, including the effect sizes and their confidence intervals, are presented in Appendix 1.

Publication bias

Eye examination of funnel plots indicated slight asymmetry in both primary outcomes, and some smaller studies reported larger effect sizes. Nevertheless, the regression test found no statistically significant asymmetry in turnover intentions (intercept = 1.006, p = 0.216) or absenteeism (intercept = 0.801, p = 0.965). The Duval and Tweedie trim-and-fill analysis implied that the effect of possible publication bias is small. In the case of turnover intentions, two studies that might have been omitted were estimated, with an adjusted pooled OR of 2.96 (95% CI: 2.783.15). In the case of absenteeism, a potentially missing study was estimated, which yielded an adjusted pooled RR of 2.20 (95% CI: 2.042.36). Discussion

The evidence presented in this systematic review and meta-analysis is a strong body of quantitative data indicating that the Second Victim Phenomenon (SVP) is strongly associated with increased intentions to leave the job and absenteeism. The combined analysis of 38 cross-sectional studies (including over 22,000 nurses) indicated that those who experienced SVP had about three times the likelihood of reporting turnover intentions (OR = 2.99, 95% CI: 2.813.18), and more than twice the risk of absenteeism (RR = 2.21, 95% CI: 2.052.37). These results were consistent in different geographical locations and nursing environments, highlighting the universal effects of SVP on nursing workforce outcomes.

Our results are consistent with and complementary to earlier qualitative and narrative syntheses. Although Seys et al. (2013) [57] found turnover intentions as a general outcome of SVP, their review did not quantitatively pool effect sizes. Similarly, Sirriyeh et al. (2010) [58] also explained the effects of career, but lacked a meta-analytic synthesis. The Study contributes to the evidence base by providing estimates of effect sizes that can be used for workforce planning and intervention development. It is worth noting that the magnitude of the association with turnover intentions (OR = 2.99) is larger than the effect sizes reported for other well-established predictors, such as job satisfaction (OR = 1.822) and burnout (OR = 2.125) [59, 60]. This implies that SVP, a specific risk factor, may mediate the association between negative clinical events and workforce turnover [61]. Regarding absenteeism, we observe a risk (RR = 2.21) consistent with the broader occupational health literature, which asserts that workplace psychological trauma is a cause of sick leave.

These associations can be explained using several theoretical frameworks. The Conservation of Resources (COR) theory [44] suggests that when individuals lose resources (especially significant losses), they experience acute stress. Turnover intentions in this context can be seen as a coping mechanism to avoid further loss of resources by quitting the stressful situation, and absenteeism as a short-term conservation strategy to gain a temporary respite. The Job Demands-Resources (JD-R) model [62] can provide greater explanatory power. SVP is an acute job demand that activates a process of health impairment. Devoid of proper institutional reinforcements, this process results in emotional burnout and withdrawal tendencies. Moreover, psychologically, SVP creates cognitive dissonance between a nurse’s identity as a healer and their role in a negative event. This threat to identity prompts professional exit/temporary withdrawal as a method of eliminating psychological tension.

Implications for practice and policy

The implications of this meta-analysis’s results for healthcare organizations and policymakers are enormous, as the need for structured support systems is urgent. To prevent the negative outcomes of the Second Victim Phenomenon (SVP), healthcare facilities are recommended to implement comprehensive support mechanisms, such as the “forYOU Team” model or a similar peer-led intervention. These programs have proven to have potential in alleviating psychological distress and enhancing coping strategies among nursing personnel. Moreover, our findings indicate that this type of program may be critical for minimizing turnover intentions and absenteeism. They may offer significant benefits, including improved workforce retention and lower replacement costs.

Leadership training and radical culture change are also necessary to manage SVP. Nurse managers and supervisors need to be educated on how to identify the symptoms of the second victim response so that they can offer systematic, and not ad hoc, assistance. Companies are supposed to work towards developing a culture of psychological safety so that adverse events are seen as opportunities to learn as a system rather than as occasions to hold oneself to blame. By incorporating the principles of just culture, it is possible to make the distinction between being human and acting recklessly fair and to help reduce the trauma that may arise during the investigation process. Moreover, workforce planners and accreditation bodies should require organizational support policies, which in turn necessitates the establishment of regulatory frameworks that mandate their adoption.

Limitations

There are several limitations to consider when interpreting these results. To begin with, cross-sectional study designs preclude causal inference. Although it is theoretically consistent that SVP predated withdrawal behaviors, the fact that reverse causation, i.e., nurses with underlying turnover intentions experiencing increased distress due to adverse events, may occur cannot be ruled out completely. Second, there was significant statistical heterogeneity (I2 = 75.9%-82.6%); this is likely due to the lack of measurement of moderators such as organizational culture, individual resilience, and the severity of the clinical incident. Third, the self-reported measures used to assess both the SVP and workforce outcomes are subject to common-method bias; however, studies using objective absenteeism records produced consistent results. Lastly, overall confidence in the evidence was low under GRADE criteria because all included studies were observational.

Future research directions

Based on gaps identified in the existing literature, several research priorities emerge. There is an urgent need to conduct prospective longitudinal studies to track nurses’ exposure to adverse events, establish clear temporal relationships, and identify modifiable risk factors. Randomized controlled trials or quasi-experiments are also needed to determine the role of different support program models in reducing turnover and absenteeism. The mechanisms through which SVP can influence workforce outcomes, including the mediating roles of emotional exhaustion and professional self-efficacy, should also be studied. Lastly, the business case for organizational investment would be strengthened through economic assessments and comparative effectiveness research to identify the most cost-effective support strategies for diverse healthcare populations.

Conclusion

This extensive Study and meta-analysis support the claim that the Second Victim Phenomenon is a powerful and reliable predictor of increased intention to leave and absenteeism among nurses in the international healthcare setting. Nurses holding the SVP are more than twice as likely to be absent and up to three times more likely to report intentions to leave than their colleagues. The outcomes pose a significant threat to the continuity of patient care and workforce stability, particularly given the global shortage of nurses currently being experienced. It is now of significant strategic importance to patient safety and staff retention that healthcare organizations recognize that providing systematic support to second victims is no longer a mere act of kindness but an essential strategic move that can help them remain competitive in the marketplace. This systematic review and meta-analysis evidence synthesis provides a quantitative basis for organizational action.
