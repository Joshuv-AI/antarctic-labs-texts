Clinical characteristics and survival outcomes of surgically managed prenatally diagnosed neuroblastoma: a single-center, real-world study
Authors: Kongkong Cui, Peng Hong, Zaihong Hu, Jie Lin, Zhiqiang Gao, Honggang Fang, Xiaomao Tian, Qinlin Shi, Guanghui Wei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background With the advancement of ultrasound technology, prenatally diagnosed neuroblastoma (PNB) has become increasingly common. However, knowledge of this particular case remains limited and is usually confined to individual case reports. There is still a lack of research on the clinical characteristics, treatment-related complications, long-term abnormal health problems and quality of life (QoL) of patients with PNB.MethodsThis retrospective study included 49 patients with PNB treated at a tertiary center between 2012 and 2025 and 102 cases from 32 prior studies (total n = 151). Collected data encompassed demographics, tumor characteristics, treatment methods, complications, and survival outcomes. And conduct follow-up evaluations on the patient’s treatment outcome, long-term health status, and QoL.ResultsPNB originated predominantly in the adrenal glands (83.7%), with 63.3% classified as low-risk. Metastasis occurred in 30.6% of cases, primarily involving the liver (80%). MYCN amplification was rare (6.1%), and elevated 24-hour urinary vanillylmandelic acid (VMA) levels were present in only 10.2% of cases. Five-year overall survival (OS) and event-free survival (EFS) rates reached 93.3% and 86.8%, respectively. Survivors had few long-term health problems and QoL scores were comparable to those of healthy children. A comprehensive analysis of 151 patients showed a survival rate of 93.4%, further confirming that PNB has a favorable prognosis.ConclusionThis study is the largest retrospective cohort analysis of PNB to date. It systematically summarizes the clinical characteristics of PNB patients and demonstrates their favorable prognosis and QoL. Furthermore, the findings provide critical evidence to guide risk-stratified management and prenatal counseling.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-06401-1.

Introduction

Neuroblastoma (NB) is the most common extracranial solid tumor in children, originating from the neural crest cells, most commonly in the adrenal medulla or paraspinal region of the sympathetic nervous system [1]. The tumor exhibits significant heterogeneity in terms of biological behavior and prognosis, with clinical outcomes ranging from spontaneous regression to rapidly progressive and fatal outcomes. This complexity presents significant challenges in the treatment of NB [2]. With advancements in imaging technology and the increased use of routine prenatal screenings, more NB patients are being diagnosed prenatally. However, little is known about this specific group of patients. Fenart et al. first reported prenatally diagnosed neuroblastoma (PNB) via ultrasonography in 1983 [3]. Since then, most studies on PNB have been confined to case reports.

To address this gap, this study aims to analyze the clinical features, treatment strategies, and prognosis of PNB patients, including long-term follow-up evaluations of growth and development. Additionally, the study will systematically summarize and review previously reported PNB cases. The findings are anticipated to provide evidence-based guidance for clinical management of this unique patient population.

Materials and methods

Patients

We collected clinical and pathological data from patients diagnosed with PNB and treated at our institution between January 2012 and February 2025. Initial suspicion of PNB was based medical history provided by the patient’s parents and prenatal ultrasound findings. The diagnosis was confirmed by postoperative histopathological examination. Cases excluded from the study included those where NB was not detected during prenatal examination, patients who lacked key clinical data or were lost to follow-up, those without a pathologic diagnosis, and patients with other tumors or major diseases that could affect prognosis. The study was approved by the ethics committees of the participating institutions, and informed consent was not required because of the retrospective nature of the study.

Treatment and follow-up

Tumor stage and risk classification were determined using the International Neuroblastoma Staging System (INSS) and the Children’s Oncology Group (COG) risk classification system. The INSS stages classified the tumors based on the surgical findings [4]. The COG risk classification is a widely used method for evaluating NB and is based on factors such as staging, diagnostic age, histological type, MYCN status, and chromosomal abnormalities. It classifies NB into low-risk, intermediate-risk, and high-risk groups [5].

For patients with suspected PNB, we conduct comprehensive diagnostic evaluations, including ultrasound, computed tomography (CT), and the measurement of serum neuron-specific enolase (NSE) levels and urinary vanillylmandelic acid (VMA) levels. Due to the potential malignancy of the tumor, we generally favor an active surgical approach. However, for small and localized tumors without metastasis and without symptoms, the decision to undergo surgery or conservative observation can be made based on the family’s preferences. If the tumor increases in size or remains stable for several months during conservative management, surgical treatment is then recommended.

Therapeutic regimen is based on the COG protocol and the neuroblastoma treatment protocol for children by the Chinese Cancer Association’s Pediatric Oncology Committee (Chinese Children’s Cancer Group - Neuroblastoma, CCCG-NB). It is adjusted appropriately according to the chemotherapy drug sensitivity of the child and the chemotherapy reactions and side effects during the treatment process, to formulate an individualized chemotherapy regimen [6–8].

A height below the 3rd percentile for individuals of the same sex and age is considered short, based on the standardized growth curve for children aged 0–18 in China [9]. The BMI formula is weight (kg)/height (m)². BMI of less than − 2 SD indicates underweight, a BMI between + 1 SD and + 2 SD indicates overweight, and a BMI greater than or equal to + 2 SD indicates obesity. We assessed patients’ QoL through post-operative outpatient visits and telephone interviews. To assess HRQoL in children aged 2 to 18, we used the PedsQL 4.0 Generic Core Scale (PedsQL). Patients, with assistance from their parents, completed the PedsQL scale. The Chinese version of the PedsQL scale demonstrates good reliability and validity [10, 11]. Patients were regularly followed up through outpatient visits and telephone contact. The final follow-up was completed in March 2025.

Literature search and statistical analysis

We searched PubMed with the keywords “(“neuroblastoma” OR “neuroblastomas” OR “neuroblastoma tumor”) AND (“prenatal” OR “antenatal” OR “fetal” OR “in utero” OR “prenatal diagnosis” OR “fetal ultrasound”) AND (“child” OR “children” OR “pediatric” OR “infant”)” in March 2025. Additionally, we manually reviewed the references of the identified studies to ensure no relevant studies were missed. Only patients with a confirmed diagnosis and detailed information were included, and duplicate cases were excluded. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0 and R 4.3.0. The descriptive statistics were used to summarize all variables. Comparisons between the two groups were made using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Survival analyses were performed using the Kaplan-Meier method. Event-free survival (EFS) was defined as the interval from the day of diagnosis to an event or date of last follow-up. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the duration from date of diagnosis to death for any reason. P values below 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Result

Patient characteristics

This study included 49 PNB patients, comprising 26 males and 23 females. These patients were identified during routine prenatal ultrasound examinations, typically conducted in the second or third trimester of pregnancy. The median week of prenatal diagnosis was 37 weeks (range: 30–41 weeks), while the median gestational age at delivery was 38 weeks (range: 36–41 weeks). The median birth weight was 3300 g (range: 2020–4250 g). Tumors were most commonly located in the adrenal glands (83.7%), followed by the retroperitoneum (14.3%) and the pelvis (2%). Risk classification showed that 31 patients (63.3%) had low-risk disease, 15 (30.6%) had intermediate disease, and 3 (6.1%) had high-risk disease. According to the Shimada classification, 37 patients (75.5%) had the favorable type, and 12 (24.5%) had the unfavorable type. According to INSS stage, 3 patients (6.1%) were stage IV and 12 patients (24.5%) were stage IV-S. MYCN amplification was observed in 3 patients (6.1%). At presentation, 15 patients (30.6%) had metastases: 12 had liver metastases, 1 had both liver and bone marrow metastases, 1 had adrenal metastasis, and 1 had lung metastasis. The median serum NSE level was 38.8 ng/L (range: 4.6-146.7), and the median lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) level was 331.6 U/L (range: 186.3-622.4). Treatment-related complications

This study included 49 patients, of whom 11 underwent only primary tumor resection, while 38 received chemotherapy following surgery. Nineteen patients (19/49, 38.7%) experienced post-surgical complications, with some having more than one. Specifically, 11 patients (11/49, 22.4%) developed hypoalbuminemia, 9 patients (9/49, 18.4%) had anemia, of which 5 (5/49, 10.2%) required blood transfusions. Additionally, 5 patients (5/49, 10.2%) experienced electrolyte disturbances, 5 patients (5/49, 10.2%) had pneumonia, 3 patients (3/49, 6.1%) had abdominal ascites, 2 patients (2/49, 4.1%) developed hypotension, 2 patients (2/49, 4.1%) had chylothorax, 1 patient (1/49, 2.0%) suffered from abdominal compartment syndrome, and 1 patient (1/49, 2.0%) developed acute kidney injury. Notably, no patients died as a result of surgery. Among the 38 patients who received chemotherapy, 31 (31/38, 81.6%) developed chemotherapy-related complications of varying degrees. The most common reaction was bone marrow suppression, observed in 26 patients (26/38, 68.4%), followed by elevated transaminase levels in 13 patients (13/38, 34.2%), increased myocardial enzymes in 9 patients (9/38, 23.7%), electrolyte disturbances in 3 patients (3/38, 7.9%), upper respiratory infections in 3 patients (3/38, 7.9%), acute conjunctivitis in 2 patients (2/38, 5.3%), sepsis in 2 patients (2/38, 5.3%), and renal injury in 1 patient (1/38, 2.6%). Urticaria occurred in 1 patient (1/38, 2.6%). Survival outcomes

Among all the patients, 4 cases (8.2%) showed metastasis or recurrence. Among them, 1 patient had metastasis in the right axilla, 1 had metastasis in the testis, 1 had metastasis in the liver and temporal region, and died due to the progression of the disease. One patient experienced local recurrence 6 months later. Furthermore, one patient died from sepsis during chemotherapy, and another died from severe electrolyte imbalance. Two patients were diagnosed with PNB and showed tumor growth during follow-up. However, their families declined the proposed treatment plan, and no further follow-up was conducted. As a result, their survival status remains unknown and they were excluded from the survival analysis. Among the patients treated at our institution, 3 had MYCN gene amplification. All of these patients survived after treatment, with no fatalities. Additionally, 12 patients had unfavorable histology, and all survived without any deaths. Among the 15 patients with metastasis, 13 survived after treatment, while 2 died. The 5-year EFS rate for the 49 PNB patients was 86.8%, and the five-year OS rate was 93.3% (Fig. 4). The median follow-up time was 48.8 months (range: 1.2–130.5 months).

Long-term health condition

At follow-up, the median age of the 49 surviving PNB patients was 57 months (range: 4-132 months). The male to female ratio is 1.2. The median height was 107 cm (range: 59–145 cm), and the median body weight was 17.5 kg (range: 7.3–42.1 kg). Among these patients, 6.1% (3/49) were overweight, 4.1% (2/49) had obesity, and 4.2% (2/49) were underweight, as shown in Fig. 5a. HRQoL was evaluated in 31 patients aged ≥ 3 years at follow-up using the PedsQL 4.0. As summarized in Table S2, PNB survivors did not differ significantly from healthy Chinese norms for physical (87.16 ± 5.68 vs. 88.29 ± 11.39; p = 0.482), psychosocial (83.92 ± 2.90 vs. 82.99 ± 12.70; p = 0.083), emotional (81.45 ± 6.35 vs. 79.39 ± 17.71; p = 0.081), school (81.94 ± 4.95 vs. 82.71 ± 14.55; p = 0.390), or total scale scores (84.57 ± 2.87 vs. 84.94 ± 10.88; p = 0.482) [10]. Notably, social functioning was higher in PNB patients than in the norms (88.39 ± 2.70 vs. 86.94 ± 15.30; p = 0.006), with all p-values >0.05. During follow-up, parents of 23 patients (46.9%) reported that they had not disclosed the tumor diagnosis to their child, and many patients had limited or no clear recall of their cancer treatment. In comparison with a previously reported NB cohort assessed with the same instrument, both the PNB group and the Chinese norms showed higher scores across all domains than the NB cohort, as shown in Fig. 5b [12]. These findings indicate that HRQoL in PNB patients has not been significantly affected.

Literature review

The study cohort comprised 151 patients, with 102 (67.5%) identified from 32 previous PNB studies (Table S3) and 49 (32.5%) enrolled from our center. Of these, 78.8% were diagnosed late in pregnancy, including 7 (4.6%) with Stage IV and 30 (19.9%) with Stage IV-S. Only 25.8% had elevated 24-hour urinary VMA levels. Two patients experienced recurrence and survived after treatment. Ten patients died (Table S4), including two stillborn, two who died after abandoning treatment, and six who died despite receiving treatment. Notably, 8 (80%) PNB patients were stage IV or IV-S. The survival rate among PNB patients was 93.4% (141/151).

Figure S2 illustrates the impact of treatment age, chemotherapy status, tumor stage, and MYCN gene amplification on the survival outcomes of PNB patients. Regarding treatment age (Figure S2a), no significant difference in survival was observed between patients aged ≤ 30 days and those aged > 30 days (p = 1). Similarly, chemotherapy (Figure S2b) did not significantly affect survival outcomes, as no difference was found between patients who received chemotherapy and those who did not (p = 0.3). In contrast, tumor stage (Figure S2c) had a significant impact on survival, with patients in the high-stage group exhibiting a higher mortality rate compared to those in the low-stage group (p < 0.05). Furthermore, MYCN gene amplification (Figure S2d) was strongly associated with survival outcomes, with the mortality rate being significantly higher in patients with MYCN amplification compared to those without, and this difference was statistically significant (p < 0.05).

Discussion

The detection of PNB in the prenatal period is becoming more and more common due to the popularity of prenatal examination and advances in prenatal ultrasound technology [13]. Previous literature indicates that 58% of perinatally diagnosed adrenal masses are attributed to PNB [14]. In this study, 83.7% of cases of PNB originated from the adrenal glands. The identification of a solid or cystic adrenal mass through prenatal ultrasonography strongly suggests a diagnosis of PNB, which is a characteristic feature of the tumor [15]. However, attention needs to be paid to the differentiation from hydronephrosis, extralobular pulmonary sequestration, bronchogenic cyst, and adrenal hemorrhage [16]. Typical prenatal ultrasonography findings of PNB include a retroperitoneal mass, which may be solid, cystic, or mixed in nature, located superior to the kidney and distinct from the liver [17]. Most cases are detected after 32 weeks of gestation. The tumor is right-sided in approximately 60% of cases. Adrenal hemorrhage usually presents as a complex mass that evolves from hyperechoic to mixed cystic-solid, and eventually to hypoechoic as the hematoma resolves [18]. In addition, larger tumors may compress the fetal intestines and lead to polyhydramnios. Finally, when ultrasound findings remain inconclusive, fetal MRI should be performed to aid in establishing a definitive diagnosis.

PNB is known for its high rate of spontaneous regression in malignant tumors [19]. In a study by Hero et al. involving 93 patients with persistently observed tumors, 44 (47%) showed tumor regression [20]. Despite the likelihood of spontaneous regression and the generally favorable prognosis of PNB, the management of this condition remains controversial, particularly regarding the duration of continuous observation. Holgerson et al. concluded that a short period of close observation does not adversely affect outcomes when tumors are smaller than 3 cm [21]. Similarly, in the COG trial ANBL00P2, patients under 6 months with solid tumors smaller than 3.1 cm or cystic tumors less than 5.0 cm, confined to the adrenal gland, were eligible for observation with serial imaging and urine VMA/HVA monitoring [22]. Among the patients who completed the observation, 81% experienced spontaneous regression or a significant reduction in tumor size, avoiding surgical resection. However, Kerbl et al. also highlighted the risks of continued observation. In their study, 4 patients were observed: 3 had sustained tumor growth and ultimately required surgical intervention, and 1 patient developed liver metastases after 4 weeks and died at 17 months [23].

The results of our study indicated that HRQoL in patients with PNB was not affected by the disease and treatment, and it was similar to that of healthy Chinese children. This could be attributed to the higher organ metabolism and regenerative capacity in infants and young children, which reduce long-term side effects. Furthermore, since the treatments were completed before the age of 1, the patients did not retain negative memories of their medical experiences. During our follow-up visits, many families did not even tell their children that they had a tumor as a child. In addition, HRQoL was superior in patients with PNB than in patients with common NB, and the degree of malignancy of the disease, the intensity of treatment, and the age at which treatment was given may account for this difference. In addition, another study about Wilms tumor by our team showed that all patients who underwent adrenalectomy had normal ACTH levels, and no cases of hypoadrenalism were observed during the follow-up period [24].

Unfortunately, in our center, there were 2 cases of PNB patients whose families chose to abandon treatment, not including those who chose to abort after being tested during pregnancy. Therefore, the results of this study appear to be necessary and can be relied upon by clinicians to educate family members of their patients in order to minimize forgoing treatment.

This study has several limitations. First, it included only patients with pathologically confirmed PNB, excluding those with prenatal suspicion of PNB who were managed conservatively. Second, the HRQoL evaluation was based on data from only 31 of 46 survivors, introducing potential bias due to missing data. Furthermore, the genomic analysis was limited to the MYCN gene, as data for other factors, such as ALK mutations, ATRX, and segmental chromosomal aberrations, were incomplete due to the extended study time frame and resource constraints. To address these limitations, future research should expand the sample size through multi-center collaborations and long-term follow-up, particularly including patients managed conservatively, to provide a more comprehensive understanding of PNB.

Our study shows that even in the presence of MYCN amplification, poor histology or metastasis, patients with PNB have a favorable prognosis with radical resection and chemotherapy. There are fewer long-term health problems after treatment and QoL is not affected by the disease. This study provides important evidence for risk-graded management, and, prenatal counseling, and fills a gap in clinical guidance for PNB.
