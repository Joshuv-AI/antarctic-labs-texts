Unexplored Flaws in Multiple-Choice VQA Evaluations
Fabio Rosenthal, Sebastian Schmidt, Thorsten Graf, Thorsten Bagodonat, Stephan Günnemann, Leo Schwinn, [2mm] Technical University of Munich Volkswagen AG Munich Data Science Institute

Abstract
MLLM demonstrate strong capabilities in handling image-text inputs. A common way to assess this ability is through multiple-choice VQA ( VQA ). Earlier works have already revealed that these benchmarks are sensitive to answer choice order, a limitation that can be mitigated through careful design. Yet, we highlight additional, unexplored biases in prompt formatting that question the reliability of current MLLM evaluations. Specifically, we identify three key variation factors in prompt formatting and analyze their impact through a large-scale study involving seven MLLM and five VQA datasets, spanning 48 distinct prompt format variations . Our findings reveal that multiple-choice VQA is highly sensitive to minor prompt format changes, even when these changes are semantically neutral. We further demonstrate that these biases persist independently of known order biases or the MLLM ’s confidence in the correct answer. Finally, we demonstrate that existing bias mitigation strategies fail to address these newly identified biases.

PriDe
(
PriDe
).

PriDe
estimates the prior for each option ID using calibration logits derived from circularly shifted multiple-choice samples
54
.
During inference, these priors adjust the logits for each option ID, and the answer is selected based on the highest adjusted logit.
This method is only applicable to questions with the same number of options as in the calibration data, limiting its use on datasets with varying option counts.

PIA
(
PIA
).

PIA
modifies accuracy computation by comparing how often each option was correctly selected to how often it was selected overall. Unlike
PriDe
, it does not require calibration data. However, it is incompatible with datasets containing a varying number of options, because its adjustment formula assumes a fixed option set.
When the number of options changes across samples, the normalization for mitigating varying answer selection becomes inconsistent, leading to unreliable accuracy estimates.

CP-LN
(
CP-LN
).

CP-LN
adopts a different evaluation strategy: instead of including all answer options in the prompt (as shown in
Fig.

1
), only the question is provided
37
;
6
.
The perplexity is computed for each option w.r.t. the question, allowing the method to handle a variable number of options.
The option with the lowest perplexity is selected as the answer, therefore, eliminating the need for calibration data.
Since prompt formatting is not involved,
CP-LN
is robust to prompt variations.
However, it has not yet been evaluated in the context of bias mitigation
37
;
6
.

5.5
Discussion

Recommendations.

Our analysis reveals the severe impact of prompt format variations on various metrics like accuracy, benchmark ranking, and model confidence.
Here, we offer recommendations for researchers developing new
MLLM
and those creating new multiple-choice
VQA
datasets.
On the model side, we urge providers to disclose how prompt format-induced biases affect their
MLLM
,
e.g
.
, performance on varying prompt formats.
To enhance robustness, researchers should consider training
MLLM
using diverse prompt formats for multiple-choice tasks.
For datasets, we recommend evaluating multiple-choice
VQA
datasets in an open-ended format to avoid biases introduced by the prompt structure.
In particular, approaches that rely solely on the logits of option IDs
37
are suboptimal, as they can introduce additional biases due to differences in answer behaviors between single- and multi-token outputs
32
;
47
;
7
.
Moreover, dataset creators could provide a validation set to facilitate adaptation to specific prompt formats.
Finally, given that
MLLM
are frequently integrated into other models or complex applications, we encourage transparency and careful consideration of these biases throughout the development pipeline.

Limitations.

Our analysis does not cover all possible prompt format variations, and additional biases may exist in multiple-choice
VQA
evaluation.
Future work should focus on developing targeted methods to effectively mitigate prompt format-induced biases.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we investigate the prompt sensitivity of
MLLM
in multiple-choice
VQA
.
We analyze the influence of prompt format variations on
MLLM
’ performance in a comprehensive study including seven
MLLM
, five datasets, and
48
prompt format variations.
Our findings reveal that multiple-choice
VQA
evaluations are highly sensitive to minor prompt format variations, even to semantically neutral changes.
The examined modifications can substantially alter benchmarking outcomes so that the
rank can change from second-to-last to first
with
performance deltas of up to
59

pp
.
Importantly, we demonstrate that these
biases persist independently of known order biases and model confidence
, revealing a previously underexplored vulnerability in current evaluation pipelines.
Finally, we demonstrate that existing bias mitigation strategies fail to address these newly identified biases.
With this work, we aim to highlight this
critical issue
and encourage researchers to design future models and datasets with these biases in mind.

Standard linear models (e.g., linear regression) are unsuitable for this non-independent data due to their independence assumption

.
In contrast, LMMs handle such dependencies by modeling the target variable
\mathbf{y}
as:

\mathbf{y}=X\boldsymbol{\beta}+Z\mathbf{u}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}

(3)

where
\mathbf{y}
is the vector of observations,

.
The design matrices

and

and

In our application, we want to model the accuracy, therefore, our modeling design of the

\text{Accuracy}_{ijk}=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}\,\text{Option\_IDs}_{i}+\beta_{2}\,\text{Options\_delimiter}_{j}+\beta_{3}\,\text{Options\_separator}_{k}+u_{g}+\epsilon_{ijk}

u_{g}
is the random effect for group

Specifically, we fit a

using

in Python to estimate the influence of prompt format variations on accuracy.
The model includes fixed effects for
option IDs
,
option delimiter
, and
option separator
, and a random intercept for each
model–dataset
combination via this modeling equation:

A.3
Metrics

Accuracy.

The most commonly used metric for evaluating the reasoning ability of
MLLM
in
VQA
is
accuracy

12
;
3
.
The accuracy is defined as the ratio of correctly answered questions to the total number of questions in the evaluation set
12
;
3
.
The correctness assessment is performed directly on the model’s generated output sequence without applying any answer extraction mechanism (
e.g
.
, regular expressions).
An answer is considered correct only if it exactly matches the ground-truth label.
For instance, if the label is
"A"
, then
"A"
is correct, whereas
"A."
or
"A)"
are not.

Coverage.

To assess instruction-following capability, we introduce the metric

. Coverage is defined as the ratio of answers that belong to the valid option ID set to the total number of questions in the evaluation set:

\text{coverage}=\frac{s_{i}}{n}

(6)

where
s_{i}
denotes the number of answers within the option ID set and
n
is the total number of questions. A higher coverage value indicates higher instruction-following capability.

A.4

We evaluate all models in a fully deterministic setting by disabling sampling.
Specifically, we set

This ensures that each model produces identical outputs for identical inputs.
Before evaluating an

We tokenize each option ID and set the number of generated tokens to the maximum required to generate any option ID.
Furthermore, we evaluate the seven

B.3
Bias Mitigation Methods

We assess the effectiveness of several bias mitigation techniques in reducing prompt format-induced biases.
All methods are reimplemented according to the specifications provided in their respective papers.

Circular evaluation.

The circular evaluation scheme shifts the options in the prompt by one position
28
and thereby increases the number of questions -
e.g
.
, for four options it enlarges the dataset by a factor of four.
This mitigates the option position bias.
While the approach requires no retraining or calibration, it is computationally more expensive.

PIA
(
PIA
).

PIA
is a post-hoc metric that mitigates the influence of the option position bias by weighting the per-option accuracy by the number of times an
MLLM
selects a specific option
44
:

\text{PIA}=\frac{1}{M}\sum^{M}_{i=1}\frac{C_{i}}{Pr_{i}}\frac{C_{i}}{N}

(7)

where
M
is the number of options,
C_{i}
is the number of times option
i
was correctly selected,
Pr_{i}
is the number of times option
i
was selected in total and
N
is the number of questions
44
.
This method does not require a calibration; however, it is prone to variations in the prompt format, as these changes may influence the selection frequency of
MLLM
.
Furthermore, it changes the accuracy calculation, which has two disadvantages: (i) this measure is less intuitive and not comparable to standard accuracy, and (ii) it can skew the result,
e.g
.
, when a model performs overall well but it does not match the dataset’s positional distribution or when a model answers one option only rarely.

PriDe
(
PriDe
).

PriDe
estimates the prior for each option ID based on calibration data containing circularly shifted multiple-choice questions
54
.
This prior is then used to debias the predictions of an
MLLM
, adjusting the probability of each option ID according to its corresponding prior:

P_{\text{debiased}}(o_{i}\mid q,x)\propto\frac{P_{\text{observed}}(d_{i}\mid q,x)}{P_{\text{prior}}(d_{i})}

(8)

where
o_{i}
denotes the
i
-th option,
q
is the question,
x
represents the default ordering of option IDs, and
d_{i}
is the
i
-th option ID
54
.
The answer is then selected as the option ID with the highest debiased probability
54
.
For our evaluations with
PriDe
, we estimate this prior using the 4-option questions from V*Bench.
These priors are only valid for questions with the same number of options as in the calibration data; therefore, they cannot be applied to datasets with varying numbers of options.

CP-LN
(
CP-LN
).

An alternative approach to mitigating biases in
MLLM
involves modifying the answer generation process.
Rather than presenting all options to the model together with a predefined prompt format and instruction, this method calculates the perplexity of the question for each individual option.
Perplexity serves as a metric for
MLLM
, indicating how unexpected it is for the model to generate an output sequence
\mathbf{y}
given the input token sequence
\mathbf{X}_{\text{in}}
and the previously generated tokens
y_{<i}

5
;
49
;
6
:

\text{PPL}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{in}})^{\mathbf{y}}=\left(\prod_{i}^{n}P(y_{i}|\mathbf{X}_{\text{in}},y_{<i})\right)^{-\frac{1}{n}}

(9)

where
n
is the number of tokens in the sequence.
A lower perplexity value indicates a more likely prediction of the output token sequence than a higher one.
Unlike the sum of log-probabilities, perplexity is normalized and thus less sensitive to the sequence length
n
.
Given the set of perplexities
\text{PPL}^{o}(X)
computed by the
MLLM
for input
\mathbf{X}_{\text{in}}
conditioned on each option
o
, we select the predicted answer
\hat{Y}
as the option with the lowest perplexity among all options
O
:

\hat{Y}=\arg\min_{o\in O}\big(\text{PPL}^{o}(\mathbf{X}_{\text{in}})\big)

(10)

This approach is closely related to the methods used in
37
;
6
. It has been shown to be less effective than the standard multiple-choice evaluation scheme in terms of accuracy
37
, while its impact on bias mitigation remains unclear.
