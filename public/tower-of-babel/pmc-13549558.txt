Association between alpha-1-acid glycoprotein and serum uric acid in young and middle-aged US women
Authors: Jiangtao Zhu, Mengge Feng, Wenqi Wu, Jianhua Zhang, Guoliang Xu, Yujie Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Evidence on the association between alpha-1-acid glycoprotein (AGP), a systemic inflammatory marker, and serum uric acid (SUA) remains limited, particularly among young and middle-aged women. Using cross-sectional data from 614 US women aged 20 to 49 years from the 2015 to 2018 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, we assessed the AGP-SUA relationship and the mediating role of the triglyceride-glucose index using multiple linear regression, smooth curve fitting, and mediation analysis. After full adjustment, each 1 g/L increase in AGP was associated with a 0.68 mg/dL increase in SUA (β = 0.68, 95% confidence interval: 0.28–1.09, P = .001). A significant dose-response trend was observed across AGP tertiles (P for trend = .003). The triglyceride-glucose index mediated 24.52% of this association. Among several inflammatory markers, AGP showed the strongest correlation with SUA (ρ = 0.27) and the highest predictive value for hyperuricemia, although the discriminative ability was modest (AUC = 0.641). Elevated AGP is independently linked to higher SUA levels, partly through insulin resistance, highlighting AGP as a relevant inflammatory marker and the interplay between inflammation and metabolism in female hyperuricemia.

1. Introduction

Serum uric acid (SUA) is the end product of purine metabolism in humans.[1] In recent years, the global prevalence of hyperuricemia and gout has continued to rise, becoming a significant public health concern worldwide.[2] Elevated SUA levels have been consistently associated with an increased risk of various chronic diseases, including hypertension, chronic kidney disease, diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases.[3-6] These associations are believed to be partly mediated through inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction induced by excessive uric acid accumulation.[7] However, the exact pathological mechanisms underlying hyperuricemia and its related complications remain incompletely understood. Therefore, identifying high-risk populations and clarifying potential inflammatory mechanisms are critical components of current strategies.

Alpha-1-acid glycoprotein (AGP), also known as orosomucoid, is a highly glycosylated acute-phase protein synthesized by hepatocytes. Under inflammatory or stress-related conditions, AGP is rapidly released into the circulation and participates in immune regulation and inflammatory modulation.[8-10] Elevated serum AGP levels have been associated with stroke, cardiovascular diseases, sepsis, and tumors.[11-14] Accumulating evidence indicates that AGP is closely related to metabolic disorders, including obesity, diabetes, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease in women, and primary aldosteronism.[15-18] These findings suggest that AGP may serve as a systemic inflammatory marker linking metabolic dysfunction with chronic disease progression.

The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index is a reliable surrogate marker for assessing insulin resistance. As discussed above, elevated AGP reflects a state of chronic low-grade inflammation. Chronic inflammation is a well-established contributor to the development of insulin resistance,[19] which in turn has been closely linked to elevated SUA levels.[20,21] Taken together, these findings suggest a plausible biological pathway in which AGP, as an upstream inflammatory marker, may influence SUA levels directly or indirectly through insulin resistance, as reflected by the TyG index. Therefore, we hypothesize that the TyG index may serve as a key mediating pathway linking AGP-related inflammation to disturbances in uric acid metabolism.

However, evidence on the association between AGP and SUA – and its underlying mechanisms – remains scarce, particularly among young and middle-aged women. Women in this age group exhibit distinct hormonal and metabolic characteristics that may modify inflammatory responses and uric acid metabolism. Therefore, using data from the 2015 to 2018 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), this study aimed to test the hypotheses that AGP is positively associated with SUA levels and that the TyG index mediates this association among young and middle-aged women in the United States. We further postulated that insulin resistance would partially explain the association between systemic inflammation and uric acid metabolism in this population. This investigation may provide epidemiological evidence to better understand the inflammatory and metabolic pathways underlying hyperuricemia in women.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study population

This cross-sectional study analyzed data from the NHANES 2015 to 2018 survey cycles. NHANES is a nationally representative survey conducted by the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) using a complex, multistage probability sampling design. The NHANES survey protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics. All participants provided written informed consent before participation.[22] Because this study used publicly available and de-identified NHANES data, no additional institutional review board approval was required for the present secondary analysis. The initial dataset comprised 19,225 participants from the 2015 to 2018 cycle. The following exclusion criteria were applied: participants with missing AGP data; participants with missing SUA data; participants with missing data required for the calculation of the TyG index, Neutrophil-to-Lymphocyte Ratio (NLR), Platelet-to-Lymphocyte Ratio (PLR), or Systemic Inflammatory Response Index (SIRI); participants aged < 20 years; participants with missing covariate data; pregnancy, self-reported history of malignant tumors, or renal failure. Pregnant individuals, participants with malignant tumors, and those with renal failure were excluded because these conditions may substantially alter systemic inflammatory status, metabolic homeostasis, and renal uric acid handling, thereby potentially confounding the association between AGP and SUA. After applying these criteria, 614 eligible participants were included, all of whom were female. The detailed participant selection process is illustrated in Figure 1. The public data used in this study were accessed as of November 10, 2025.

2.2. Measurement of SUA

SUA data were obtained from the laboratory component of the NHANES. Venous blood samples were collected at the Mobile Examination Center, processed (including centrifugation), frozen, and stored at −30 °C before being shipped to designated laboratories for centralized analysis. SUA concentrations were measured using the timed endpoint method. In accordance with established clinical guidelines, hyperuricemia in female participants was defined as an SUA level ≥ 6 mg/dL.[23]

2.3. Measurement of AGP

AGP data were obtained from laboratory tests conducted according to NHANES protocols. This biomarker was measured in participants aged 1 to 5 years and in female participants aged 12 to 49 years. AGP was measured using an immunoturbidimetric assay. AGP antigens in the sample bind to specific antibodies to form antigen-antibody complexes, which are quantified by measuring changes in turbidity. Internal and Roche quality control materials were analyzed with each assay, and a multi-rule procedure was implemented to ensure valid results.[24]

2.4. Calculation of metabolic and inflammatory variables

The TyG index was used as a metabolic indicator of insulin resistance and served as a candidate variable for subsequent mediation analysis. It was calculated using fasting blood glucose and triglyceride levels measured after an 8 to 24-hour fast, according to the following formula: TyG = ln [TG (mg/dL) × glucose (mg/dL)/2].[25] Three systemic inflammatory indices derived from the complete blood count data were calculated: NLR, PLR, and SIRI. These indices were compared with AGP. Their associations with SUA were evaluated using correlation analysis, and their predictive performance for hyperuricemia was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The specific calculation formulas are as follows: NLR = neutrophil count/lymphocyte count.[26] PLR = platelet count/lymphocyte count.[27] SIRI = (neutrophil count × monocyte count)/lymphocyte count.[28]

2.5. Assessment of covariates

This study systematically included multiple potential confounders as covariates. Demographic variables comprised age (<35 years, ≥35 years), race/ethnicity (non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Mexican American, other Hispanic, other Race), marital status (never married, married/living with partner, divorced/widowed/separated), education level (high school or below/General Educational Development, college or above), and poverty–income ratio (<1.5, 1.5–3.49, ≥3.5). The age cutoff of 35 years was selected because it approximated the median age of the study population and is commonly used to distinguish younger and mid-reproductive-age women in epidemiological research. Body mass index (BMI) was obtained from physical examination data and categorized as <25 kg/m2, 25–29.9 kg/m2, and ≥30 kg/m2. Serum creatinine levels were obtained from the laboratory data. Additionally, a history of hypertension, diabetes, smoking, and alcohol consumption was collected from the questionnaire. Smoking history was defined as having smoked at least 100 cigarettes in their lifetime. Alcohol consumption was defined as consuming at least 12 alcoholic drinks in the past year, consistent with NHANES standard definitions. Information on dietary purine intake and uric acid-related medications (e.g., diuretics or urate-lowering therapy) was unavailable or incomplete in the analytic dataset; therefore, these variables were not included in the adjustment models and are acknowledged as potential sources of residual confounding.

2.6. Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using the R software (version 4.1.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and EmpowerStats (version 4.2; X&Y Solutions, Inc., Boston). All analyses were conducted using survey-weighted procedures in accordance with NHANES analytic guidelines. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and group differences were assessed using t-tests. Categorical variables were described as frequency and percentages (n, %), and group differences were evaluated using weighted χ2 tests. To examine the association between AGP and SUA levels, multiple linear regression models were constructed with AGP analyzed as both a continuous and categorical variable (tertiles). Three progressively adjusted models were constructed: model 1 was unadjusted; model 2 was adjusted for age, race/ethnicity, marital status, education level, poverty–income ratio, and BMI; model 3 was further adjusted for hypertension, diabetes, smoking history, alcohol consumption, and serum creatinine levels based on Model 2. Smooth curve fitting was applied to explore potential dose-response relationships between AGP and SUA levels. Subgroup analyses were conducted with interaction tests to evaluate the consistency of associations across different populations. Furthermore, mediation analysis was performed to evaluate the potential mediating role of the TyG index in the relationship between AGP and SUA. Spearman’s correlation analysis was used to evaluate the relationship between inflammatory markers (AGP, NLR, PLR, and SIRI) and SUA levels. Additionally, the area under the ROC curve was calculated to compare the diagnostic performance of the NLR, PLR, SIRI, and AGP for hyperuricemia. All statistical tests were 2-sided, and P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

3.1. Participant characteristics

After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 614 female participants aged 20 to 49 years were included in the analysis, with a mean age of 34.38 years. Participants were categorized into 3 groups based on the tertiles of AGP levels. Across increasing AGP tertiles, several demographic and clinical characteristics showed graded differences. Compared with those in the lowest AGP tertile (Q1), participants in the highest AGP tertile (Q3) were generally older, had lower levels of educational attainment and household income, and exhibited higher BMI values. The proportion of Non-Hispanic White women and individuals who were divorced or widowed was greater in the highest tertile. Higher AGP levels were associated with a higher prevalence of current smoking, diabetes, and hypertension. Regarding inflammatory and metabolic indicators, participants in the highest AGP tertile had significantly higher TyG index, NLR, and SIRI levels, whereas PLR did not differ significantly across tertiles. Both the prevalence of hyperuricemia and mean SUA concentrations increased progressively with rising AGP levels (P < .001). Detailed baseline characteristics are shown in Table 1.

3.2. Association between AGP and SUA

Multiple linear regression analyses were conducted to further investigate the relationship between AGP and SUA. As shown in Table 2, AGP was positively associated with SUA across all models. In the fully adjusted model (model 3), each 1 g/L increase in AGP was associated with a 0.68 mg/dL increase in SUA (β = 0.68, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.28–1.09, P = .001). When AGP was analyzed as tertiles, participants in the highest tertile (Q3) had significantly higher SUA levels compared with those in the lowest tertile (Q1; β = 0.35, 95% CI: 0.11–0.58, P = .011). A significant dose-response relationship was also observed (P for trend = .003). The association remained statistically significant after sequential adjustment for multiple potential confounding factors and demonstrates a significant dose-response trend.

3.3. Analysis of nonlinear relationships

To further explore the potential shape of the association, smooth curve fitting based on the fully adjusted model was performed (Fig. 2). The fitted curve demonstrated a generally linear upward pattern of SUA levels across the range of AGP concentrations, with no apparent threshold or nonlinear inflection point detected within the observed range.

Smooth curve fitting between AGP and SUA levels. AGP = alpha-1-acid glycoprotein, SUA = serum uric acid.

3.4. Subgroup analysis and interaction tests

To evaluate the consistency of the association between AGP and SUA across diverse demographic and clinical characteristics, subgroup analyses were performed with formal tests for interaction. As shown in Figure 3, no significant heterogeneity was detected across most subgroups, including those stratified by age, BMI, and underlying medical conditions (all P for interaction > .05). However, a significant interaction was observed for race/ethnicity (P for interaction = .014). This indicates that the positive association between AGP and SUA remained consistent across most populations. The association between AGP and SUA appeared stronger among Mexican American and other Hispanic participants compared with other racial/ethnic groups. However, given the relatively small sample sizes within certain racial/ethnic categories, this finding should be interpreted cautiously and considered exploratory.

Subgroup analysis of the association between AGP and SUA. AGP = alpha-1-acid glycoprotein, BMI = body mass index, CI = confidence interval, PIR = poverty–income ratio, SUA = serum uric acid.

3.5. Mediation analysis

To elucidate the mediating role of the TyG index in the association between AGP and SUA, a mediation analysis was performed. As illustrated in Figure 4, the TyG index exerted a significant partial mediating effect. The total effect of AGP on SUA was partitioned into a direct effect (β = 0.52, P = .040) and an indirect effect mediated through the TyG index (β = 0.16, P < .001), with the indirect pathway accounting for 24.52% of the total effect. These findings suggest that part of the association between AGP and SUA may be partially explained by the TyG index.

Mediation analysis of the TyG index in the AGP-SUA association. AGP = alpha-1-acid glycoprotein, SUA = serum uric acid, TyG = triglyceride-glucose.

3.6. Spearman correlation and ROC curve analyses

Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to compare the relationships between systemic inflammatory markers (AGP, NLR, PLR, and SIRI) and SUA (Fig. 5A). All markers showed statistically significant correlations with SUA (P < .05). Among them, AGP exhibited the strongest positive correlation with SUA (ρ = 0.27), followed by SIRI (ρ = 0.19) and NLR (ρ = 0.13), whereas PLR demonstrated a weak negative correlation (ρ = −0.07). Subsequently, ROC curve analysis was performed (Fig. 5B). AGP had an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.641 (95% CI: 0.576–0.706, P < .001), which was higher than that of SIRI (AUC = 0.626), NLR (AUC = 0.577), and PLR (AUC = 0.531). However, the overall discriminative ability of all markers was modest.

4. Discussion

Previous research has suggested that SUA may partially mediate the association between AGP and gallstone disease[29]; however, epidemiological evidence regarding the direct relationship between AGP and SUA remains limited. Using nationally representative NHANES data, this study examined the association between AGP and SUA among young and middle-aged women in the United States. The main findings were as follows: AGP was independently and positively associated with SUA levels after adjustment for multiple potential confounders; the TyG index statistically accounted for a proportion of this association; and subgroup analyses indicated potential heterogeneity across racial/ethnic groups. Although a recent study reported associations between SIRI and both SUA levels and hyperuricemia prevalence,[30] our correlation and ROC analyses showed that AGP exhibited the strongest correlation with SUA among the evaluated inflammatory markers and had the highest AUC. However, the discriminative performance was modest. Taken together, these results indicate that AGP may capture inflammatory processes related to uric acid metabolism, although its independent predictive utility for hyperuricemia appears limited.

The observed positive association between AGP and SUA is biologically plausible in the context of chronic low-grade inflammation. Elevated AGP concentrations are generally considered markers of chronic low-grade inflammation.[31] Several biological mechanisms have been proposed to explain a potential link between inflammation and uric acid metabolism. Inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6 have been reported to enhance xanthine oxidase activity, which may contribute to increased uric acid production.[32] Chronic inflammatory states have also been associated with increased cell turnover and purine degradation.[33] Conversely, uric acid has been shown to activate pro-inflammatory signaling pathways such as NF-κB.[34] These observations suggest a possible bidirectional relationship between inflammation and uric acid metabolism. Importantly, inflammatory activation may also influence SUA indirectly through metabolic pathways, particularly insulin resistance. This consideration provides a rationale for evaluating the TyG index as a potential mediator in the AGP-SUA association. Nevertheless, given the cross-sectional design of the present study, the temporal sequence and directionality of these relationships cannot be determined.

Consistent with this inflammatory-metabolic framework, the mediation analysis indicated that the TyG index statistically accounted for 24.52% of the observed association between AGP and SUA. Chronic inflammation may contribute to insulin resistance through disruption of insulin signaling and adipose tissue inflammation.[35,36] In turn, insulin resistance has been associated with reduced uric acid excretion and alterations in purine metabolism.[37,38] Enhanced xanthine oxidoreductase activity has also been observed in metabolic dysfunction.[39] Therefore, insulin resistance, as reflected by the TyG index, may represent 1 potential pathway linking AGP-related inflammation to higher SUA levels.

This study has several strengths, including the use of nationally representative data, standardized laboratory measurements, comprehensive adjustment for potential confounders, and application of mediation analysis. However, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and prevents determination of the temporal relationship between AGP and SUA. Second, although multiple covariates were adjusted for, residual or unmeasured confounding cannot be excluded. Third, NHANES does not provide detailed information on dietary purine intake or other lifestyle factors that may influence SUA levels. Future prospective cohort studies and mechanistic investigations are needed to clarify the causal relationship and underlying biological pathways linking AGP and uric acid metabolism.

5. Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrates that among young and middle-aged women in the United States, AGP is independently and positively associated with SUA levels, and the TyG index statistically accounts for part of this association. These findings suggest that chronic low-grade inflammation and related insulin resistance may be involved in the development of hyperuricemia in women. Further prospective studies are needed to clarify the temporal and causal relationships underlying these associations.
