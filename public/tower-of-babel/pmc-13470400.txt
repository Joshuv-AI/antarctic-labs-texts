Dynamic staff scheduling optimization algorithm for hotel management
Authors: Yehuizi Fang, Baohua Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In hotel management, traditional manual scheduling relies on experiential decision-making. It struggles to address dynamic challenges from occupancy fluctuations, periodic peaks, and sudden demands. This leads to issues like redundant labor and service gaps. To solve this problem, this study targets mid-range hotel management and proposes a staff scheduling algorithm integrating dynamic demand forecasting and multi-objective optimization. First, a demand forecasting model based on Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) is built to predict periodic labor demands of each department. Second, a multi-constraint mathematical optimization model is established. Its objectives are minimizing total operating costs, minimizing service quality losses, and maximizing employee satisfaction. The model also considers skill matching, working hour compliance, and shift fairness. Finally, an improved Genetic Simulated Annealing Algorithm (GASA) is designed to solve the model. Through adaptive coding, a multi-objective fitness function, and a dynamic adjustment mechanism, the optimal scheduling scheme is generated. Empirical analysis shows the following. Under high occupancy (90%), the algorithm reduces daily operating costs by 15.3%, shortens average customer waiting time by 58.7%, decreases average employee overtime by 65.7%, and lowers customer complaint rate by 78.3%. Under sudden demand scenarios, the algorithm’s response time is less than 5 min, and the adjustment accuracy of scheduling schemes reaches 92%. This study provides a quantitative tool for dynamic staff scheduling in hotel management, offers scenario-specific references for multi-objective resource optimization in economy hotels with similar scale.

Introduction

In recent years, the hotel industry has shown a development trend of “large-scale expansion combined with refined operation“1,2. Its service nature determines that “people” are the core production factor. Efficient collaboration of staff is required in links such as check-in at the front office, room cleaning at the housekeeping department, peak-hour service at the food and beverage department, and equipment maintenance at the engineering department. However, the “dynamic nature” and “uncertainty” of hotel operations bring significant challenges to staff scheduling3:

From the time dimension, occupancy rates show characteristics of “daily peaks and valleys (peaks at 8:00–10:00 and 18:00–22:00)”, “weekly fluctuations (higher on weekends than weekdays)”, and “monthly cycles (sharp rise during holidays)”.From the spatial and business dimensions, sudden demands like temporary banquet bookings, large group check-ins, and unexpected equipment failures can double the labor demand of a department in a short time.From the staff dimension, factors such as employees’ skill heterogeneity, working hour compliance, and satisfaction needs further increase scheduling complexity.

During peak hours, overtime or temporary staff recruitment is used to meet demands4–6. This model has three core problems:

First, demand forecasting is lagging. When the deviation between actual and expected occupancy exceeds 20%, insufficient staff during peaks and redundant labor during troughs easily occur. These observations suggest that existing studies have not yet fully addressed the dynamic and multi-objective nature of hotel staffing problems in a unified framework. Demand forecasting and scheduling optimization are often treated as separate steps, while employee satisfaction and sudden demand responses are underemphasized. Against this background, this study aims to develop an integrated approach that combines more accurate demand forecasting, balanced multi-objective optimization, and flexible dynamic adjustment. The intention is to provide a more practical and systematic tool that supports managers in making better-informed scheduling decisions, while also contributing incremental evidence to the literature on service industry resource allocation.

Some researchers established a linear regression forecasting model based on the correlation between historical hotel occupancy and room cleaning demand7,8. It realizes daily forecasting of labor demand in the housekeeping department but ignores employee skill heterogeneity and shift constraints. Other experts proposed a multi-objective scheduling model for the food and beverage industry. With “cost minimization” and “minimum customer waiting time” as objectives, the NSGA-II algorithm is used for solution9. However, this model is designed for static demands and cannot handle periodic fluctuations in hotels. Some researchers proposed an integer programming-based scheduling model for the 24-hour shift characteristics of the hotel front office. They introduced employee preference weights to optimize satisfaction, but the model does not integrate a dynamic adjustment mechanism for sudden demands. Its computational complexity increases exponentially with the number of employees.In recent years, with the development of deep learning and intelligent optimization technologies, data-driven methods have gradually become the mainstream in hotel demand forecasting and staff scheduling10–13. For demand forecasting, traditional time-series models such as ARIMA and linear regression have been widely adopted, but they struggle to capture nonlinear and periodic patterns hidden in actual operation data14–16. With the popularity of deep learning, recurrent neural networks (RNN) and long short-term memory (LSTM) networks have been introduced to forecast tourism and hotel demand, significantly improving prediction accuracy17,18. However, most existing studies separate forecasting results from scheduling decisions rather than forming a closed-loop dynamic adjustment mechanism. For staff scheduling optimization, traditional mathematical programming and heuristic algorithms are commonly used to balance labor cost and service level19. In recent studies, multi-objective optimization has received increasing attention, and hybrid intelligent algorithms such as genetic simulated annealing algorithms have shown advantages in solving efficiency and stability20. Nevertheless, few studies simultaneously consider real-time demand fluctuations, cross-department skill matching, and sudden demand response, leading to a gap between theoretical models and practical hotel operation.

Most studies treat demand as a known constant and do not establish a “forecasting-optimization” closed loop, making it impossible to respond to dynamic changes.Second, multi-objective integration is insufficient. Existing models mostly focus on the “cost-service” dual objectives and ignore the indirect impact of employee satisfaction on service quality.Third, dynamic response capacity is weak. Most algorithms are designed for static scenarios. Their adjustment efficiency is low when facing sudden demands, which cannot meet the real-time operation needs of hotels.

Analysis of dynamic staff scheduling problems in hotel management

Correlation mechanism between hotel operation and staff scheduling

The service value of hotels is realized through the interaction of “staff - business - customers”. The business characteristics of different departments determine the differentiated needs of staff scheduling10,11. The correlation logic between each department and staff scheduling is as follows:

Front office

As the “first touchpoint” of hotel services, it undertakes functions such as check-in, check-out, and customer consultation. Its workload shows obvious daily peak-valley characteristics. Demand surges during 8:00–10:00 (check-out peak) and 18:00–22:00 (check-in peak), and drops sharply during 0:00–6:00. Front office staff need skills in customer communication and system operation. They also need to work 24-hour shifts. Scheduling must balance “sufficient staff during peaks” and “labor saving during troughs” while avoiding consecutive night shifts for employees.

Housekeeping department

Its core work includes room cleaning, linen replacement, and room service. Demand directly depends on occupancy rate. For every 10% increase in occupancy rate, the required labor in the housekeeping department increases by approximately 8%-10% (calculated based on 45 min of cleaning time per room and 8 h of daily working time per employee). In addition, room demand has “periodic rigidity”. For example, rooms must be cleaned within 2 h after check-out to ensure new customer check-in. Scheduling must accurately match cleaning demand and staff supply to avoid customer complaints caused by cleaning delays.

Food and beverage department

It covers businesses such as breakfast supply, regular meal service, and banquet reception. Demand fluctuations have both “periodicity” and “suddenness”. Regular peaks occur during breakfast (7:00–9:00) and dinner (18:00–21:00). Temporary banquet bookings (e.g., a 200-person corporate dinner) can double demand in a short time. Food and beverage staff need skills in service and meal preparation. Some employees (e.g., banquet servers) can support across periods. Scheduling must flexibly adjust labor allocation to respond to sudden demands.

Recreation department

It includes services such as gym, swimming pool, and SPA. Demand is positively correlated with occupancy rate but with small fluctuation range (15%-20% fluctuation when occupancy rate is 30%-90%). Services have “appointment-based nature” (e.g., SPA requires advance booking). Scheduling can plan labor in advance based on appointment data.

Engineering department

It is responsible for equipment maintenance and facility upkeep (e.g., air conditioning, elevators, water and electricity). Demand is “sudden” - equipment failures (e.g., elevator outages, room air conditioning damage) have no fixed pattern, requiring 24-hour on-call. Engineering staff need professional skills (e.g., electrician, elevator maintenance). Skill substitutability is low. Scheduling must ensure sufficient professional staff on duty at all periods while avoiding labor redundancy.

In summary, hotel staff scheduling must focus on three core dimensions: “department characteristics - demand fluctuations - skill matching”. It needs to dynamically balance costs, services, and employee satisfaction.

Core influencing factors of dynamic scheduling

The dynamics of hotel staff scheduling result from the combined effect of multiple factors. These factors can be divided into three categories: “demand-side factors”, “staff-side factors”, and “environment-side factors“12. Details are as follows:

Demand-side factors

Demand-side factors are direct causes of labor demand fluctuations. They mainly include:

Occupancy fluctuations: As a core indicator of hotel operation, occupancy rate directly determines the basic demand of the housekeeping department, front office, and recreation department. According to 2023 operation data of Huazhu Group, the average daily occupancy rate of its economy hotels is 72%. The rate on weekends (85%) is 25% higher than that on weekdays (68%), and can reach over 95% during holidays (e.g., National Day). For every 10% fluctuation in occupancy rate, the overall labor demand fluctuates by approximately 7%-9%.Periodic peaks: All hotel departments have fixed periodic peaks. For example, the front office has morning and evening peaks, and the food and beverage department has peaks during three meals. Labor demand during peak periods is about 2–3 times that of trough periods (e.g., the front office needs 6 staff during morning peak and only 2 during trough).Sudden demands: These include temporary banquet bookings, large group check-ins, and equipment failures. For example, after a hotel receives a 300-person group check-in order, the front office needs to complete registration within 1 h. Its labor demand increases from 4 to 8 staff. When an elevator fails suddenly, the engineering department needs to deploy 2 additional maintenance staff, leading to labor shortage in other maintenance work.

Staff-side factors

Staff-side factors determine the flexibility and constraints of labor supply. They mainly include:

Skill heterogeneity: Hotel employees have significant skill differences. Some employees have cross-department support capabilities (e.g., front office staff can support food and beverage peak service), while others only have single skills (e.g., elevator maintenance staff in the engineering department). According to a 2023 staff skill survey by Meituan Hotels, employees with cross-department skills account for about 35% of the total in chain hotels. These employees are the key to scheduling flexibility.Working hour constraints: According to the Labor Law of the People’s Republic of China, employees’ daily working hours shall not exceed 8 h. Weekly overtime shall not exceed 36 h, and daily overtime shall not exceed 3 h. As a 24-hour operation industry, hotels need to arrange shifts to meet working hour constraints. For example, the front office adopts a “four-shift three-rotation” model (morning shift 8:00–16:00, middle shift 16:00–24:00, night shift 0:00–8:00, rest shift). Scheduling must avoid employees working overtime beyond the limit.Employee preferences: Employees have preferences for shift periods and overtime frequency. For example, young employees are more willing to take night shifts, while married employees prefer fixed day shifts. Excessive night shifts or overtime will affect employee satisfaction, which in turn affects service quality. According to a survey by the China Tourism Hotels Association, for every 10% increase in employee satisfaction with shift arrangements, customer satisfaction can increase by 7%.

Environment-side factors

Environment-side factors indirectly affect scheduling by influencing demand or supply13. They mainly include:

Industry seasonal characteristics: The hotel industry has obvious seasonality. During peak tourism seasons (e.g., coastal hotels in summer, ski hotels in winter), occupancy rate can reach over 90%, leading to a surge in labor demand. During off-seasons, occupancy rate is less than 40%, requiring labor cost control. For example, a coastal hotel in Sanya needs 120 staff during peak season (May-October) and only 60 during off-season (November-April).Policy and regulation changes: Adjustments in policies such as minimum wage standards and social security contribution ratios affect labor costs, which in turn influence scheduling decisions. For example, Shanghai’s minimum wage standard increased from 2,590 yuan/month to 2,690 yuan/month in 2023. Hotel labor costs increased by 3.8%. Some hotels reduced the number of temporary staff through scheduling optimization to control costs.Technology substitution effect: The application of technologies such as self-service check-in machines and intelligent cleaning robots changes the structure of labor demand. According to data from Huazhu Group, after deploying self-service check-in machines, the labor demand of the front office during peak periods decreased by 30%. Intelligent cleaning robots can undertake 40% of room cleaning work, leading to a corresponding decline in labor demand of the housekeeping department.

Pain points of existing scheduling models

In current hotel management, the mainstream staff scheduling models are fixed scheduling and manual adjustment. This model has four core pain points in dynamic environments:

Lagging demand forecasting and low labor matching accuracy

The traditional model relies on managers’ historical experience to set basic scheduling. For example, the housekeeping department schedules 10 staff based on the fixed standard of “70% occupancy rate”. It does not consider real-time demand fluctuations. When the deviation between actual and expected occupancy exceeds 20%, problems of “insufficient staff during peaks and redundant labor during troughs” easily occur.

Multi-objective imbalance and low operational efficiency

The traditional model mostly takes “cost control” as the primary objective, ignoring service quality and employee satisfaction. This is correlated with multi-objective imbalance. On one hand, to reduce costs, some hotels excessively control labor allocation. They rely on employee overtime during peak periods, resulting in an average daily overtime of over 2.5 h per employee. This far exceeds the 3-hour daily limit (cumulative weekly) stipulated by the Labor Law, may lead to decreased employee satisfaction and increased turnover rate. On the other hand, some hotels blindly increase labor allocation to improve service quality, resulting in redundant labor during troughs and a 15%-20% increase in operating costs.

Slow dynamic response and weak ability to cope with sudden demands

Facing sudden demands such as temporary banquets and equipment failures, the traditional model requires managers to adjust scheduling manually. It takes a long time (average over 30 min), which easily misses the service window.

Lack of quantitative decision-making and limited optimization space

The traditional model relies on managers’ subjective experience and lacks quantitative analysis tools. The optimization space of scheduling schemes is limited. For example, when arranging cross-department support, managers only consider “whether employees are available” instead of their skill proficiency, leading to reduced service quality. When arranging shifts, they do not quantify employee preferences, resulting in insufficient fairness. Some employees may take night shifts for 3 consecutive weeks, significantly reducing their satisfaction. In addition, the traditional model cannot quantify the trade-off relationship between “cost-service-satisfaction”. For example, it cannot determine whether “adding 1 temporary employee (cost increase of 200 yuan/day) can reduce the customer complaint rate by 1%”, leading to insufficient decision-making scientificity.

The overall research procedure of this study consists of four systematic steps. First, we analyze the dynamic characteristics of hotel labor demand and summarize the key factors and pain points of traditional scheduling. Second, we construct an LSTM-based demand forecasting model to predict the staffing requirements of each department in different periods. Third, we establish a multi‐objective constrained optimization model that considers cost, service quality, and employee satisfaction. Fourth, we design an improved genetic simulated annealing algorithm to solve the model and verify its effectiveness through empirical comparison under different occupancy rates and sudden demand scenarios. The entire framework forms a closed loop from demand forecasting to scheme optimization and dynamic adjustment.

Definitions of each core forecasting indicator are as follows:

Required number of staff in the front office per period: Refers to the number of employees needed by the front office to meet customer check-in, check-out, and consultation needs in a certain period (e.g., 8:00–10:00). The calculation formula is “(periodic passenger flow × average service time) / period duration”. The average service time includes 3 min per person for check-in, 2 min per person for check-out, and 1 min per person for customer consultation.Required number of staff in the housekeeping department per period: Refers to the number of employees needed by the housekeeping department to complete cleaning and linen replacement in a certain period. The calculation formula is “(number of rooms to be cleaned × average cleaning time) / (period duration × employee efficiency coefficient)”. The number of rooms to be cleaned = number of occupied rooms - number of cleaned rooms. The average cleaning time is 45 min per room. The employee efficiency coefficient is set based on skill proficiency (1.0 for professional housekeeping staff and 0.7 for cross-department support staff).Required number of staff in the food and beverage department per period: Refers to the number of employees needed by the food and beverage department to meet dining and banquet needs in a certain period. The calculation formula is “(number of reserved customers × service staff ratio) + (sudden passenger flow × temporary service coefficient)”. The service staff ratio is 1:10 (1 employee serves 10 customers). The temporary service coefficient is 0.5 (50% of sudden passenger flow requires temporary staff deployment).Required number of staff in the recreation department per period: Refers to the number of employees needed by the recreation department to meet customer needs for fitness, SPA, etc., in a certain period. The calculation formula is “number of recreation project reservations × project service coefficient”. The service coefficient for fitness projects is 0.1 (1 employee for 10 customers), and 1.0 for SPA projects (1 employee for 1 customer).Required number of staff in the engineering department per period: Refers to the number of employees needed by the engineering department to complete maintenance and fault repair in a certain period. The calculation formula is “number of staff needed for planned maintenance + number of staff needed for sudden failures”. The number of staff needed for planned maintenance is determined based on the maintenance plan (e.g., 2 staff per day for room facility inspection). The number of staff needed for sudden failures is calculated based on historical failure frequency (1 employee for every 2 sudden failures).

Construction of LSTM demand forecasting model

As a specialized variant of Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN), Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks mitigate the vanishing gradient problem inherent in conventional RNNs by incorporating forget gates, input gates, and output gates. They are particularly suitable for processing time-series data characterized by long-term dependencies, such as hotel demand. This section provides a complete formal definition of the LSTM demand forecasting model, specifying critical hyperparameters including the number of network layers, the number of neurons in each layer, the Xavier initialization method, full parameters of the Adam optimizer, batch size, iteration epochs, and early stopping criteria. The model adopts an input-hidden-output LSTM architecture to capture long-term temporal dependencies in hotel staffing demand, and a standardized training pipeline is implemented to guarantee stable and reproducible results.

Model structure design

The LSTM demand forecasting model adopts a structure of “input layer - hidden layer - output layer”. A multi-output model is designed for the two-dimensional “department - period” demand of hotels to predict the periodic labor demand of the front office, housekeeping department, food and beverage department, recreation department, and engineering department simultaneously. Accuracy verification and comparison of the forecasting model

To verify the forecasting accuracy of the LSTM model, it is compared with the traditional time series forecasting model ARIMA. The ARIMA model is a commonly used linear model in hotel demand forecasting, suitable for stationary time series data. It can be used as a benchmark model to verify the advantages of LSTM.

ARIMA model setting

The core parameters of the ARIMA model are (p, d, q), where p is the autoregressive order, d is the differencing order (to make the data stationary), and q is the moving average order. Parameters are determined through the following steps:

Stationarity test: The Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test is used to verify the stationarity of demand data. Results show that the original data is non-stationary (ADF statistic = -2.1, greater than the critical value − 3.5 at the 1% significance level). First-order differencing is required. Result analysis

The LSTM model outperforms the ARIMA model mainly for two reasons:

Nonlinear fitting ability: Hotel demand is affected by multiple factors such as occupancy rate, period, and sudden demands. It shows significant nonlinear characteristics (e.g., nonlinear demand growth caused by sharp rise in occupancy rate during holidays). As a linear model, ARIMA cannot capture this nonlinear relationship. The LSTM model, however, can effectively fit nonlinear data through the gating mechanism of the hidden layer, improving forecasting accuracy.Long-term dependency capture ability: Hotel demand has long-term dependency characteristics of weekly and monthly cycles. For example, occupancy rate on Fridays is higher than that on other weekdays. The ARIMA model has limited memory capacity and cannot effectively use long-term historical information. The LSTM model can store historical information for a long time through the cell state, capturing long-term dependency relationships and improving forecasting accuracy.

Multi-objective function construction

The model takes “minimizing total operating costs, minimizing service quality losses, and maximizing employee satisfaction” as three objectives to construct a multi-objective function. Due to different dimensions of each objective, normalization is first performed. Then, the objectives are integrated into a single-objective function through weight coefficients (to facilitate algorithm solution). The weight coefficients are determined by the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP). Ten mid-range hotel operation managers are invited to score. Finally, the cost weight \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{w}_{1}$$\end{document} = 0.4, service quality weight \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{w}_{2}$$\end{document}= 0.35, and employee satisfaction weight \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{w}_{3}$$\end{document} = 0.25 are determined. This reflects the priority of “cost first, service second, satisfaction supplement” in hotel operations.

These weights are determined through the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) based on expert scoring from ten hotel operation managers. The setting conforms to the actual operation logic of mid-range hotels, which usually take cost control and service quality as primary considerations while taking employee satisfaction into account. Such a weighting method makes the optimization model more consistent with real management decision-making and avoids excessive bias toward a single objective.

Objective function 1: minimize total operating costs

Total operating costs include salary costs of regular staff and temporary staff. The calculation formula is as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{min}{f}_{1}=\sum\:_{e\in\:E}\sum\:_{d\in\:D}\sum\:_{t\in\:T}{w}_{e}{h}_{e,t}+\sum\:_{d\in\:D}\sum\:_{t\in\:T}{w}_{t}{y}_{d,t}$$\end{document}

The first term is the salary cost of regular staff (both regular working hours and overtime are calculated at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{w}_{e}$$\end{document}; overtime pay is included in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{w}_{e}$$\end{document}, following the industry practice of “hourly wage including 1.5 times overtime pay”). The second term is the salary cost of temporary staff.

Objective function 2: minimize service quality losses

Service quality losses are quantified by customer waiting time losses and customer complaint rate losses. When customer waiting time exceeds \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{w}$$\end{document} or complaint rate exceeds \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{c}$$\end{document}, loss costs are incurred. Referring to the Meituan Hotel Service Report, each additional minute of customer waiting time causes a loss of 5 yuan. Each additional 0.1% of complaint rate causes a loss of 100 yuan. The calculation formula is as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{min}{f}_{2}=\sum\:_{d\in\:D}\sum\:_{t\in\:T}5\cdot\:\mathrm{max}({C}_{d,t}-{C}_{w},0)+\sum\:_{d\in\:D}1000\cdot\:\mathrm{max}({C}_{d,c}-{C}_{c},0)$$\end{document}

The first term is customer waiting time loss (5 yuan loss per additional minute). The second term is customer complaint rate loss (100 yuan loss per additional 0.1%, i.e., 1000 yuan loss per additional 1%).

Objective function 3: maximize employee satisfaction

Employee satisfaction is quantified through three dimensions: overtime hours, number of night shifts, and working hour stability. A linear function is used. Based on the 2023 Employee Satisfaction Survey by the China Tourism Hotels Association, satisfaction decreases by 5% for each additional hour of overtime, 3% for each additional night shift, and 2% for each additional 2 h of working hour fluctuation. The calculation formula is as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{max}{f}_{3}=1-\left(0.05\sum\:_{e\in\:E}\mathrm{max}({O}_{e}-\mathrm{2,0})+0.03\sum\:_{e\in\:E}\mathrm{max}({N}_{e,night}-\mathrm{1,0})+0.02\sum\:_{e\in\:E}\mathrm{max}\left(\left|\sum\:_{d\in\:D}\sum\:_{t\in\:T}{h}_{e,t}-8\right|-\mathrm{2,0}\right)\right)$$\end{document}

Here, 1 represents the highest satisfaction. The following three terms represent satisfaction declines caused by overtime hours, number of night shifts, and working hour fluctuation, respectively. Finally, the value range of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}_{3}$$\end{document} is [0,1].

Constraint condition design

The model’s constraint conditions include five categories: “labor quantity constraints”, “skill constraints”, “working hour constraints”, “shift constraints”, and “service quality constraints”. They ensure that the scheduling scheme conforms to actual hotel operations and legal requirements.

Labor quantity constraints

Labor quantity constraints ensure that the total labor supply (regular staff + temporary staff) of each department in each period meets the demand. They also consider skill proficiency coefficients (cross-department support staff have lower efficiency than professional staff). The calculation formula is as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\sum\:_{e\in\:E}{S}_{e,d}{x}_{e,d,t}+{y}_{d,t}\ge\:{R}_{d,t},\hspace{1em}\forall\:d\in\:D,\forall\:t\in\:T$$\end{document}

This constraint means that the “effective labor supply” (regular staff supply × skill proficiency coefficient + temporary staff supply) of department d in period t is not less than the demand \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}_{d,t}$$\end{document}. It avoids insufficient service capacity caused by skill mismatch.

Skill constraints

Skill constraints ensure that employees are only assigned to departments where they have the required skills. They avoid service quality decline caused by lack of skills. The calculation formula is as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{x}_{e,d,t}\le\:{S}_{e,d},\hspace{1em}\forall\:e\in\:E,\forall\:d\in\:D,\forall\:t\in\:T$$\end{document}

This constraint means that if the skill proficiency coefficient of employee e in department d is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{S}_{e,d}$$\end{document} = 0 (no skill), then \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{x}_{e,d,t}=0$$\end{document} (cannot be assigned to this department). Improved genetic simulated annealing algorithm design and experimental verification

Design of improved genetic simulated annealing algorithm (GASA)

Genetic Algorithm (GA) has the advantage of strong global search ability but tends to fall into local optimality. Simulated Annealing Algorithm (SA) has strong local search ability and can jump out of local optimality. This study combines the two to design an improved Genetic Simulated Annealing Algorithm (GASA). It adapts to the multi-constraint and multi-objective characteristics of hotel dynamic staff scheduling. The algorithm framework includes five parts: “coding design”, “fitness function”, “genetic operation”, “simulated annealing operation”, and “dynamic adjustment mechanism”.

The overall framework of the improved genetic simulated annealing algorithm (GASA) proposed in this study consists of five sequential modules:

Three-dimensional matrix coding based on employee-department-period constraints to generate feasible initial individuals;Multi-objective fitness function calculation with constraint penalty to evaluate each scheduling scheme;Adaptive genetic operations including tournament selection, employee-dimension segmented crossover, and constraint-aware dynamic mutation;Simulated annealing local search based on Metropolis criterion to jump out of local optima;Dynamic adjustment mechanism for sudden demand scenarios to realize rapid local rescheduling.

Genetic operation design

The genetic operations include selection, crossover, and mutation, all designed to satisfy hotel scheduling constraints and improve search efficiency.

Selection operation: Tournament selection with tournament size 3 is adopted to avoid premature convergence caused by over-dominant individuals. Three individuals are randomly selected each time, and the one with the highest fitness is retained as a parent. This method maintains population diversity and stability.

Crossover operation: Employee-dimension segmented crossover is used. Two parent individuals are cut along the employee dimension, and the scheduling schemes of partial employees are exchanged to generate offspring. The crossover point is randomly generated, but the skill constraints and working hour constraints of each employee are kept unchanged during crossover to ensure the feasibility of offspring individuals. The crossover probability is set to 0.8.

Mutation operation: Constraint-aware dynamic mutation is designed. For each gene position, mutation is performed with a small probability (0.03). When mutation occurs, the algorithm only flips the gene value from 0 to 1 or 1 to 0 under the premise of complying with skill constraints and working hour constraints, so as to avoid generating invalid individuals that violate constraints.

Basic scenario: static scheduling under different occupancy rates

Occupancy rate is a core factor affecting hotel labor demand. Three occupancy rate scenarios are designed: low (60%), medium (75%), and high (90%). They correspond to the typical operation states of hotels in off-season (January), flat season (May), and peak season (October). Training is conducted based on historical data of corresponding months in 2023. The LSTM demand forecasting results of each scenario are shown in Fig. 1:

Fig. 1LSTM Demand forecasting Results of Each Scenario.

LSTM Demand forecasting Results of Each Scenario.

From the LSTM prediction results shown in Fig. 1, obvious periodic demand patterns can be observed across departments. The front office and food and beverage department show typical double peaks during check-in/check-out and meal periods. The demand of the housekeeping department is relatively stable but increases significantly with occupancy rate, showing a positive correlation. The demand of the recreation department is relatively gentle, while the engineering department maintains low basic demand but is affected by sudden faults. Under high occupancy (90%), the peak demand of each department increases significantly compared with low and medium occupancy scenarios, and the peak-valley difference becomes larger, which brings higher challenges to dynamic scheduling. These patterns reflect the real periodicity and volatility of hotel labor demand and provide a basis for subsequent accurate scheduling.

Comparison method and evaluation indicators

Comparison method: traditional manual scheduling

The traditional manual scheduling currently used by hotels is selected as the benchmark for comparison. This method relies on operation managers to schedule based on experience, with the following rules16:Rules for determining the staffing of basic personnel: The hotel maintains a fixed staffing establishment across five major departments: front office, housekeeping, food and beverage, recreation, and engineering. The base number of employees in each department is determined according to the hotel’s maximum guestroom capacity and average daily passenger flow. The number of core staff is fixed and does not dynamically adjust with daily occupancy rate or hourly passenger flow fluctuations.Rules for human resources supplement during peak periods: During peak periods with significant labor shortages, such as breakfast service, concentrated check-out hours, and evening check-in peaks, the hotel does not hire temporary part-time staff; instead, it prioritizes using overtime of existing employees to fill gaps. Overtime assignments are sorted based on employees’ cumulative monthly working hours—employees with fewer overtime hours in the current month are prioritized—to ensure a relatively balanced monthly overtime workload.Rules for sudden human resource scheduling: When facing sudden situations such as group check-ins, temporary banquets, and unexpected employee leave, managers manually contact off-duty employees one by one via telephone to negotiate temporary return to work. There is no automated scheduling process, and the timeliness of staff adjustment entirely depends on the subjective coordination ability of managers.Cost and salary accounting rules: Under the manual scheduling model, employees receive a fixed monthly basic salary, and overtime hours are paid at the statutory overtime rate, with no intelligent labor optimization mechanism. Meanwhile, this model fails to consider multi-dimensional factors such as employee workload, busy–idle variation across periods, and service quality fluctuations. The scheduling scheme only meets basic operational needs.

Cost dimension analysis

The daily operating cost of the improved GASA algorithm is lower than that of traditional manual scheduling under all occupancy rates, and the improvement rate increases with the rise of occupancy rate (14.4% improvement at 60% occupancy, 15.3% at 90% occupancy). The reasons are as follows:

Traditional manual scheduling arranges shifts based on fixed rated staff numbers, leading to labor redundancy during trough periods (e.g., under 60% occupancy, the housekeeping department needs 2 staff at t1, but manual scheduling still arranges 4 staff).The improved GASA algorithm achieves accurate matching between demand and supply. It reduces redundant labor during trough periods (e.g., under 60% occupancy, only 2 staff are arranged for the housekeeping department at t1). During peak periods, it prioritizes scheduling cross-department support staff (hourly wage 25 yuan) instead of recruiting temporary staff (hourly wage 50 yuan), which is associated with lower salary costs.

Analysis of algorithm applicability and scalability

Combining the mechanism of the improved genetic simulated annealing (GASA) algorithm in this paper, the core key parameters that affect the operation time include the population size, the maximum number of iterations, the initial temperature of simulated annealing and the cooling coefficient, as well as the time periods and post scales of the scheduling business. For the GASA algorithm, the population size directly determines the number of individual traversals in the genetic global search. The larger the population setting, the higher the computational load of crossover, mutation and fitness evaluation, and the running time increases accordingly. If the population size is excessively compressed, the global optimization ability of the algorithm will be weakened. The maximum number of iterations controls the rounds of genetic evolution and simulated annealing iteration. Increasing the number of iterations can give full play to the combined advantages of the algorithm’s global exploration and local deep - exploration, but it will also increase the solution time. As the unique parameters of the improved algorithm in this paper, the initial temperature and cooling coefficient of simulated annealing directly affect the acceptance probability of inferior solutions and the convergence rhythm: the higher the initial temperature and the gentler the cooling rate, the longer the algorithm search cycle and the increased running time, but it is easier to jump out of the local optimum. If the temperature is set too low or the cooling is too fast, it will converge prematurely, and the improvement value of the GASA algorithm integrating simulated annealing cannot be reflected. Therefore, in practical applications, the key parameters can be differentially adjusted according to the scheduling scenarios: for daily regular scheduling, a moderate population, medium - level iterations and reasonable annealing parameters are adopted to balance the solution efficiency and the quality of the scheme. During complex peak passenger flows and multi - department joint scheduling, the population and iteration scales are appropriately enlarged, and a gentle annealing cooling strategy is adopted. Relying on the optimization characteristics of the GASA algorithm itself, a better manpower scheduling scheme can be obtained. At the same time, by streamlining redundant time periods and non - essential post constraints, the dimensionality of model variables can be reduced, and the running time can be effectively controlled without changing the core structure of the algorithm.

From the perspective of the model structure and experimental results, the dynamic scheduling algorithm proposed in this paper has the potential to be extended to different hotel types and complex operational scenarios. Firstly, the algorithm adopts a universal modeling framework with multi-department, multi-period, and multi-constraint characteristics. It can be adapted to the operational characteristics of hotels with different positioning by adjusting parameters such as the number of departments, time period division, skill matrix, and objective weights. For high-end/luxury hotels, which impose stricter service quality requirements and more refined professional division of labor, the model can better conform to high-star service standards by increasing the weight of the service quality sub-objective and elaborating multi-level skill constraints. For boutique hotels characterized by small staffing scales and prevalent cross-post versatility, the departmental dimension can be simplified and the model scale reduced to maintain lightweight and efficient solving. For large resorts and hotel chains, the algorithm relies on three-dimensional matrix encoding and a dynamic adjustment mechanism, supporting collaborative human resource scheduling across multiple regions and venues, thus demonstrating favorable horizontal scalability. Secondly, the experimental results show that the algorithm maintains stable performance under highly dynamic scenarios including large occupancy rate fluctuations ranging from 60% to 90%, temporary banquets, and equipment failures, with a response time of less than 5 min and core indicator fluctuations no more than 0.057. This indicates that the algorithm can cope with complex operational environments such as sudden passenger flow changes, concurrent multi-business operations, and multi-department collaboration, reflecting satisfactory scalability and robustness.

Regarding the existing research content, the scheduling optimization experiment in this paper is completed based on a single hotel case, and no independent external data is used for verification in other scenarios. Therefore, the conclusions of this paper are scenario - specific and cannot be directly extended to scenarios such as high - end hotels, resorts, and multi - store chains. The qualitative extension to other scenarios is limited to theoretical deduction. Among them, for high - end hotels, adaptation can be achieved by increasing the weight of service quality and refining the constraints on employee skill levels; for small boutique hotels, the complexity of the model can be reduced by merging department dimensions and simplifying constraint conditions; for chain hotels, a multi - store collaborative scheduling framework can be extended to realize cross - store human resource sharing.

Conclusion

This study addresses the core limitations of traditional hotel staff scheduling, including inadequate demand forecasting, imbalanced multi-objective optimization, and weak responsiveness to dynamic events, by proposing an integrated framework that combines LSTM-based demand forecasting and an improved genetic simulated annealing algorithm. Empirical results demonstrate that the LSTM model achieves an average MAPE of 7.5% across five hotel departments, representing a 39.5% reduction compared with the ARIMA model, thereby providing reliable periodic demand inputs for scheduling. The constructed three-dimensional objective model, which minimizes operational costs and service quality losses while maximizing employee satisfaction, satisfies key constraints such as skill matching, legal working-hour limits, and shift fairness. Compared with conventional manual scheduling, the proposed improved GASA algorithm reduces daily operating costs by 14.4%–15.3%, shortens average customer waiting time by 50.0%–58.7%, and decreases average employee overtime by 61.1%–65.7% under different occupancy rates. For sudden demand scenarios, the dynamic adjustment mechanism delivers a response time of less than 5 min, approximately 89% faster than manual adjustment. Robustness tests confirm that core performance indicators vary by no more than 5.7% under reasonable parameter perturbations, verifying the stability of the algorithm.

This research has certain limitations. The present model assumes uniform skill levels between permanent and temporary staff and overlooks heterogeneity in employee competence and training costs, which may restrict its applicability in highly complex staffing environments. Future research can incorporate differentiated skill grading, job fitness, and training costs to develop a more realistic heterogeneous staff scheduling model, thereby further enhancing the practical value and generalizability of the proposed method. At the same time, the research was only verified based on a single economy hotel case, so the universality of the conclusion across scenarios is relatively weak. In the future, data from multiple hotels and various business formats can be introduced for verification to further improve the applicability of the model.
