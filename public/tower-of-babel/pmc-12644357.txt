Targeting triple-negative breast cancer: apoptotic and antitumor effects of Artemisia sieberi Besser extracts
Authors: Fatimah G Albani, Sahar S Alghamdi, Sabine Matou-Nasri, Arwa Alsubait, Rasha Suliman, Rehab AlRoshody, Sarah M Huwaizi, Tlili Barhoumi, Afrah E Mohammed, Mohamed Boudjelal, Entissar S Alsuhaibani, Zeyad Alehaideb
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractArtemisia sieberi Besser (A. sieberi) has shown promise as a natural source of safe cytotoxic agents for breast cancer therapy. However, its effects on triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), a very aggressive subtype lacking targeted treatments, remain poorly studied. This study evaluates the anticancer activity of A. sieberi extracts against the MDA-MB-231 TNBC cell line, comparing results with hormone receptor-positive MCF-7 cells. Aerial parts of A. sieberi were extracted using ethanol and methanol and then chemically characterized via gas chromatography/mass spectrometry (GC/MS) to identify bioactive compounds. Cytotoxic effects were assessed using cell viability assays, while apoptosis induction was examined through flow cytometry (Annexin V/propidium iodide staining), caspase-3/-7 activation assays, and mitochondrial membrane permeabilization visualized using confocal microscopy. Western blotting analyzed the expression levels of apoptotic proteins. In silico molecular docking simulations explored the interactions between phytochemicals and apoptosis-regulating proteins. A. sieberi extracts exhibited higher cytotoxicity against MDA-MB-231 cells than against MCF-7 cells, with lower IC50 values. Treatment of TNBC cells induced apoptosis, evidenced by increased caspase activity, mitochondrial membrane permeabilization, elevated Bax, and decreased Bcl-2 expression. Additionally, colony formation assays demonstrated a significant reduction in tumorigenic potential. Computational analyses indicate that β-sitosterol and stigmasterol, among the main compounds, exhibited strong interactions with apoptosis regulators by docking with Bcl-2, supporting their promising anticancer potential. A. sieberi extracts induce potent apoptosis and exert anti-tumour effects in TNBC cells, highlighting their potential as sources of novel anticancer agents. Further isolation and characterization of active constituents are warranted for therapeutic development.Graphical abstract Keywords: Triple-negative breast cancer, MDA-MB-231, MCF-7, Apoptosis, Phytochemicals, Molecular docking, Cytotoxicity

Introduction

In recent decades, cancer researchers have increasingly focused on the use of phytomedicine in cancer therapy. Rich in bioactive compounds, plant extracts are the main source of drug development by exhibiting anticancer properties and reducing chemotherapy toxicity, making them a promising approach in cancer treatment (Jenča et al. 2024; Ng et al. 2022; Peng et al. 2023). Medicinal plants provide anticancer agents that induce natural programmed cell death (i.e., apoptosis), with recent studies showcasing their potential to promote apoptosis as a safe therapy (Li et al. 2024; Situmorang et al. 2024). Furthermore, natural plant remedies, combined with a proper diet, regular physical activity, and adequate sleep, may help fight cancer (O’Brien et al. 2022; Rock et al. 2020). Several candidate plants have been identified as natural sources of chemotherapeutic phytochemicals, which may function as anticancer, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, or antimicrobial agents (Obayemi et al. 2020).

The genus name "Artemisia" originates from the Greek word "Artemis," meaning "healthy," reflecting the significant medicinal uses of A. sieberi. This medicinal plant has traditionally been utilized in various forms, including infusions (i.e., steeping the soft plant parts in hot water), tinctures (i.e., dissolving the plant parts in alcohol), and decoctions (i.e., heating or boiling the plant parts to obtain a liquid containing bioactive compounds) (Nurlybekova et al. 2022; Son et al. 2023). Thus, A. sieberi has been used in various preparations to manage a wide range of ailments, including gastrointestinal, respiratory, infectious, and inflammatory diseases (Feng et al. 2020; Nurlybekova et al. 2022; Zaman et al. 2021). In the Middle East, A. sieberi has been traditionally used to treat cancer in general. The aerial parts are boiled and the infusion is consumed for three weeks, regardless of the type of cancer. This traditional use, combined with previous studies showing cytotoxic effects, prompted researchers to study for the first time the plant’s essential oil, which demonstrated the strongest cytotoxic activity against poorly invasive and hormone-dependent MCF-7 breast cancer cells in laboratory experiments (Break et al. 2023). Moreover, A. sieberi demonstrated growth inhibitory and apoptosis-inducing effects in vitro, particularly against liver (HepG2) and breast (MCF-7) cancer cells (Abutaha et al. 2015). Additionally, A. sieberi also showed cytotoxic activity in another study against colorectal cancer (HCT116, DLD, Colo20, and LoVo), and lung cancer (A549, cervical (HeLa) (Abutaha et al. 2015; Break et al. 2023; Maodaa et al. 2024; Nasr et al. 2020), highlighting its potential as a source of anticancer agents for further research.

The botanical and pharmaceutical importance of A. sieberi in the management of various diseases is attributed to its phytochemical composition (Al-Otibi et al. 2023). The main chemical constituents of A. sieberi are phenolic compounds, which are important anticancer agents with several beneficial effects on human health, immunity, and nutrition (Tungmunnithum et al. 2018). Phenolic compounds and flavonoids have been reported to contribute to the anticancer potential of the plant A. sieberi, including their activity against breast cancer cells MCF-7 (Singh & Patra 2018). A. sieberi has a high content of phenols (194.30 ± 0.84 mg gallic acid equivalents/g dry weight, DW), flavonoids (18.22 ± 0.14 mg quercetin equivalent/g DW), and tannins (78.98 ± 0.11 mg tannic acid equivalents/g DW). The high content of phenols, flavonoids, and tannins in Artemisia plants contributes to their anticancer properties by inhibiting the growth and spread of cancer cells (Salih et al. 2023). Although in silico studies on A. sieberi are limited, recent research on other species of the genus Artemisia has yielded important results that can be used to understand their potential mechanisms. One of them, the effect of extracts from A. annua and A. vulgaris on the C6 glioma cell line was studied, included molecular docking to explore the interactions of the main phenolic compounds extracted from these two plants with proteins involved in the apoptosis (Bax and Bcl-2) and programmed necrosis (MLKL/RIPK3) pathways, with the aim of predicting their ability to bind to target proteins and influence these pathways (Bernatoniene et al. 2024). In addition, A. campestris essential oil also demonstrated strong binding to the pancreatic cancer-associated protein phosphoinositide 3-kinase gamma, suggesting its potential as an anticancer agent. These findings support the role of Artemisia species as a potential natural resource for developing novel therapeutic strategies (Cherfi et al. 2025). Moreover, several studies that included various Artemisia species, particularly, A. herba-alba and A. Judaica, they demonstrated anticancer activity (in breast, colon, lung, and prostate cancer) via multiple mechanisms, such as inducing apoptosis, inhibiting the cell cycle, preventing angiogenesis, and reducing proliferation (Diab et al. 2025). Additionally, computational studies such as molecular docking, genetic and metabolomic analysis, and biological modeling have contributed to clarifying the mechanisms of action of the active compounds and improving the discovery and development of anticancer agents extracted from Artemisia (Diab et al. 2025).

The anticancer potential of A. sieberi in the breast cancer treatment has not been extensively studied, with limited literature primarily highlighting the cytotoxic activity of A. sieberi extracts against the poorly invasive and hormone-dependent MCF-7 cell line. There is a lack of sufficient studies regarding its cytotoxic activities against hard-to-treat breast cancer types, including non-hormone-dependent and highly invasive triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). This study aimed to determine the anticancer properties of A. sieberi aerial parts on the TNBC cell line MDA-MB-231, to investigate the underlying mechanisms involved in cell death, and to tentatively identify the A. sieberi extract-derived bioactive compounds.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) plus GlutaMax-1 (4.5 g/L D-Glucose, 25 mM HEPES, Pyruvate), fetal bovine serum (FBS), TrypLE™ Express, and Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) were obtained from Gibco® (Waltham, MA). Invitrogen NP-40 cell lysis buffer was acquired from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Carlsbad, CA, USA). Mitoxantrone dihydrochloride was procured from TOCRIS Bioscience, a bio-techne brand (Bristol, UK). Methanol and ethanol were sourced from Honeywell Riedel–de Haen (Seelze, Germany) and Merck (Kenilworth, NJ), respectively. Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was obtained from Calbiochem (San Diego, CA). All solvents used were of chromatography-grade or equivalent. Highly purified carbon dioxide (CO2) and helium gases were supplied by Saudi Industrial Gas (Dammam, Saudi Arabia). Ultra-pure water (resistivity: 18.2 MΩ·cm at 25 °C) was produced using a Millipore system (Billerica, MA).

Plant material collection and authentication

Aerial parts of A. sieberi were collected from the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA). Taxonomic authentication was performed by experts at Princess Nourah Bint Abdulrahman University (PNU). Voucher specimens (Herbarium No: 6150) were deposited in the PNU Herbarium for future reference. The samples underwent cleaning, air-drying, and storage in a dark, cool environment until further use.

Extraction of A. sieberi aerial parts

The herbal sample was pulverized using a standard electric grinder. Approximately 500 mg of the powdered sample was combined with 10 mL of methanol and ethanol solvents. Extraction was performed using a Grant Instruments XUB10 (Digital Ultrasonic Bath), Model no. XUB10, Serial no. U68130 operating at a frequency of 50 kHz for 30–40 min at maximum power, and the temperature during sonication was maintained at 20 °C using a water bath. During this process, compounds were transferred from the plant matrix to the solvent. The sonicated extract was filtered through ashless quantitative filter paper, and the filtrate was concentrated by rotary evaporation. The resulting residue was solubilized in 1 mL of DMSO and vortexed until complete dissolution. The extraction yield was calculated based on the weight of the final residue relative to the initial plant material. Chromogenic endotoxin quantitative assay

Endotoxin levels in the A. sieberi sonicated aqueous extract were quantified using the Pierce™ LAL Chromogenic Endotoxin Quantitation Kit, adhering to the manufacturer’s protocol. Endotoxin-free solutions were stored at 4 °C in the dark until further analysis.

Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS) analysis for A. sieberi methanolic extract

The methanolic extract of A. sieberi was analyzed using a Thermo Scientific GC/MS system equipped with a TriPlus RSH SMART Autosampler, TRACE 1610 GC Oven, and TSQ 9610 Triple Quadrupole Mass Spectrometer. Separation was achieved using a Thermo Scientific TG-5MS (30m × 0.25 mm, 0.25µm) capillary column. The temperature program was as follows: 0 to 5 min, 60 °C; 5 to 30 min, 60 to 300 °C; and 30 to 35 min, 300°C. Mass spectra were recorded with a scanning range of 50–650 m/z. Peak identification was performed by comparing mass spectra with reference spectra in the National Institute of standards and Technology (NIST) library.

Breast cancer cell line culture and cell-based assays

Breast cancer cell line culture and treatment

MCF-7 (#HTB-22) and MDA-MB-231 (#HTB-26) breast cancer cell lines, purchased from ATCC (Manassas, VA), were cultured in complete DMEM GlutaMAX-I medium supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS, 1% penicillin G (100 IU/mL), and streptomycin (100 µg/mL). Cell culture was maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO₂ and 95% relative humidity. Medium renewal occurred every 2 to 3 days, and cells were sub-cultured upon reaching 70–80% confluence. Cells were subjected to treatment with various concentrations of A. sieberi extracts, along with appropriate controls, including 1% DMSO (negative control, solvent used for the reconstitution of the herbal extract and corresponding to the highest concentration of the herbal extract tested) and 1 µM Mitoxantrone (Mito, a potent apoptosis inducer used as positive control) for 48 h.

Cell viability assay

Breast cancer cells (1000/well) were seeded in 96-well Greiner® plates in complete medium. Cell treatment was performed as aforementioned. Cell viability was assessed using the CellTiter-Glo® luminescent cell viability assay kit (Promega Corporation, Madison, WI) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. This assay quantifies adenosine triphosphate (ATP), a molecular indicator of metabolically active cells. The luminescent signal, proportional to the percentage of living cells, was measured using an EnVision™ microplate reader (Perkin Elmer, Waltham, MA).

Apoptosis status analysis

Apoptosis status was evaluated using the Becton Dickinson (BD) Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA) following the manufacturer’s protocol and as described previously (Alshuail et al. 2022). MDA-MB-231 cells were seeded in 6-well plates (5 × 105 cells/well) and treated with methanol extract of A. sieberi at concentrations of 100, 200, and 400 µg/mL, 1% DMSO, and 1 µM Mito for 48 h. Cells were then pelleted, resuspended in 100 µL of 1X binding buffer, and stained with Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI). Analysis was performed using a BD LSRFortessa™ | High-Parameter flow cytometer with FACSDiva™ software (BD Biosciences).

Caspase-3/−7 and mitochondrial transition pore opening activities

To visualize caspase-3/−7 activity and mitochondrial outer membrane permeability, MDA-MB-231 cells were seeded in Nunc™ Lab-Tek™ 8-well slide chamber systems (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) (5 × 104 cells/well) and treated with A. sieberi methanol extract at concentrations of 100, 200, and 400 µg/mL, 1% DMSO, and 1 µM Mito for 48 h. Caspase-3/7 activity and mitochondrial outer membrane permeability were determined using the Invitrogen Image-iT® LIVE red caspase-3 and caspase-7 detection kit and Image-iT® LIVE Mitochondrial Transition Pore Assay kit, respectively, according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Molecular Probes Inc., Life technologies, Carlsbad, CA). The images were acquired using the Zeiss cLSM 780 Microscope System and the analysis was done on ZEN Black version.

Apoptosis-related protein detection analysis

Western blot analysis was performed as previously described (Matou-Nasri et al. 2022). Proteins were separated via 12% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Diluted in blocking buffer (1% bovine serum albumin in Tris-buffered saline-Tween 20, pH 7.4), primary antibodies included mouse monoclonal anti-Bcl-2 (1:1000), rabbit monoclonal anti-Bax (1:1000), and mouse monoclonal anti-GAPDH (1:5000) provided by Abcam (Cambridge, UK). Secondary antibodies (IRDye® 680RD goat anti-rabbit and IRDye® 800RD goat anti-mouse, 1:10,000) were from LI-COR Biosciences (Lincoln, NE). Protein detection was carried out using the LI-COR Odyssey CLx Scanner, and quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ software.

Colony formation assay

MDA-MB-231 cells were seeded at 2 × 103 cells per well in 6-well plates, treated with methanolic extract of A. sieberi at concentrations of 100, 200, and 400 µg/mL, along with 1% DMSO and 1 µM Mito, and incubated for 10 days. The colonies were then fixed and stained. The percentage of colony formation relative to the untreated control was calculated.

Statistical and data analysis

Inhibition curves were generated by plotting the log10 of treatment concentrations versus the percent inhibition of MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cell viability. Half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values were determined at the concentration corresponding to 50% inhibition of maximal viability using the log-inhibition variable-slope (three- or four-parameter) model in GraphPad Prism software (version 5, San Diego, CA). Multiple images were analyzed for each experiment, and results were averaged. All measured variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments. Statistical comparisons were performed using a two-sample independent (unpaired) t-test, with comparisons made between each treatment group and the control group separately, with a p-value less than 0.05 considered significant.

Computational modeling of compounds interactions for predictions of anticancer activity of A. sieberi methanolic extract

The Prediction of Activity Spectra for Substances (PASS) online service offers predictions for various biological activities, including anticancer properties (https://www.way2drug.com/PassOnline/index.php) (Filimonov et al. 2014). The chemical structures of the identified metabolites, expressed in the Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System (SMILES) format, were used to generate these predictions. The chemical structures for all compounds were drawn using ChemDraw software.

Pharmacokinetics of A. sieberi methanolic extract compounds: absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion properties by SWISSADME webserver

The SwissADME webserver (http://www.swissadme.ch/) was used to predict the molecular characteristics of A. sieberi methanolic extract compounds. The SMILES strings were utilized for every compound to input and obtain the data. The pharmacokinetics profile involved assessment of molecular weight, lipophilicity (Log P), solubility (Log S), blood–brain barrier (BBB) penetration, gastrointestinal (GI)/oral absorption, and Lipinski’s rule of five (ROF) violation. Moreover, cytochrome P450 (CYP-P450) enzyme inhibition was predicted, CYP1A2, CYP2C19, CYP2C9, CYP2D6, and CYP3A4 were evaluated (Daina et al. 2017).

Organ and End-Point Toxicity Prediction for A. sieberi methanolic extract-derived compounds

ProTox-II webserver (https://tox.charite.de/protox3/#) was used to assess the organ and endpoint toxicity of the identified compounds (hepatotoxicity, carcinogenicity, immunotoxicity, mutagenicity, and cytotoxicity) (Banerjee et al. 2018). The toxic doses value is usually expressed as the Lethal Dose 50% (LD50) values in mg/kg of body weight. LD50 (median lethal dose) refers to the amount of a substance which is required to cause death in 50% of a test population after exposed to a substance. The server classifies compounds into six toxicity categories (from 1 to 6) based on the predicted LD50 values derived from available online databases. Class 1 refers to highly toxic compounds with a predicted LD50 of 5 mg/kg or less, and class 6 represents low toxicity compounds with an LD50 value greater than 5,000 mg/kg.

Molecular target prediction

SwissTargetPrediction (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/) online tool was utilized to predict potential targets of A. sieberi bioactive compounds. This tool relies on a ligand-based approach, using a databases of experimentally validated bioactivity data to predict the most likely molecular targets. SMILES strings were used as enter to the Swiss TargetPrediction software to predict potential protein targets. The predictions were based on Homo sapiens as the reference organism (Daina et al. 2019).

Molecular docking of the identified A. sieberi methanolic extract-derived compounds in the Bcl-2 crystal structure

A docking study was performed on the Bcl-2 crystal structure (Protein Database ID: 6O0K) for each identified compound derived from A. sieberi methanolic extract. The LigPrep tool (LigPrep, Schrödinger) was used to prepare the 2D chemical structures of the compounds. The protein grid was also generated using the receptor grid generator tool available in Maestro software (Receptor Grid Generation, Schrödinger). The docking study and the resulting poses were analyzed qualitatively and quantitatively using Glide software (Glide, Schrödinger). The molecular docking methodology was adapted based on previously published protocols using Schrödinger software tools (Alghamdi et al. 2021).

Results

Extraction, and endotoxin levels of A. sieberi aerial parts

The aerial parts of A. sieberi were extracted using the solvents, methanol and ethanol, separately. Alongside this, a sonicated aqueous extract was prepared for endotoxin analysis. The Pierce™ LAL Chromogenic Endotoxin Quantitation Kit was employed to measure the endotoxin levels in the sonicated aqueous extract of A. sieberi, following the manufacturer’s protocol. The analysis revealed an endotoxin concentration of 3.92 ± 0.10 EU/g (Endotoxin Units) in the aqueous extract. This level falls within the range considered non-toxic for the cell lines tested, ensuring that any toxic effects observed in other assays were attributed solely to the activity of the A. sieberi extract.

Methanolic extracts of A. sieberi exhibit higher cytotoxicity against MDA-MB-231 cells than against MCF-7 cells

CellTiter-Glo® luminescent cell viability assay kit was used to assess cancer cell viability in response to various concentrations of A. sieberi methanolic extract. As the extract concentration increased, cell viability decreased in a dose-dependent manner compared to untreated cell viability, set as 100% (Fig. 1). The IC50 values, reflecting the antiproliferative effect of A. sieberi methanolic extracts, were determined for each breast cancer cell line. Using MCF-7 cells, the IC50 values were 117.31 ± 43.94 µg/mL for the ethanolic extract and 143.13 ± 41.65 µg/mL for the methanolic extract. Using MDA-MB-231 cells, the IC50 values were significantly lower with 46.46 ± 29.20 µg/mL for the ethanolic extract and 37.54 ± 9.99 µg/mL than for the methanolic extract, indicating greater sensitivity to the extracts than MCF-7 cells. Additionally, Mitoxantrone, used as a positive control, exhibited much lower IC50 values such as 0.341 ± 0.114 µg/mL on MCF-7 cell viability and 0.275 ± 0.042 µg/mL on MDA-MB-231 cell viability, highlighting the potent inhibitory effects of this chemotherapeutic agent. These results suggest that the methanolic extract was more effective than the ethanolic extract, particularly against MDA-MB-231 cells. Therefore, MDA-MB-231 cells were selected for further in vitro investigations with the A. sieberi methanolic extract to explore the underlying cell death mechanisms and potential antitumorigenic effects.

Apoptotic morphological features detected in A. sieberi methanolic extract-treated TNBC MDA-MB-231 cells

Cellular imaging using a light microscope (20X) revealed cellular changes after MDA-MB-231 cell exposure to different concentrations of A. sieberi methanolic extract. Representative photomicrographs show that untreated cells and cells treated with 1% DMSO, used as the negative control, retained the typical spindle-shaped, fibroblast-like morphology characteristic of MDA-MB-231 cells. Compared to untreated and DMSO-treated cells, the Mitoxantrone-treated cells (positive control) showed typical morphological features characterizing apoptotic cells, including cell shrinkage and condensation, nuclear fragmentation and chromatin condensation, membrane blebbing, and formation of apoptotic bodies. Regarding the A. sieberi methanolic extract-treated cells, they showed the same apoptotic characteristics observed in the Mitoxantrone-treated cells, which indicated the cytotoxic activity of A. sieberi against the tested cell lines and its ability to promote apoptosis in TNBC MDA-MB-231 cells (Fig. 2).

FACS assay was applied to determine the apoptosis status of untreated and treated cells using the BD Annexin V apoptosis detection kit. (Mito)-treated cells, used as a positive control, exhibited a marked induction of apoptosis, with significant percentages of cells in both early and late apoptosis (Fig. 3). In comparison, the methanolic extract of A. sieberi also induced apoptosis in MDA-MB-231 cells. At concentrations of 100, 200, and 400 µg/mL of methanolic extract, the percentages of cells in early apoptosis were 12.13 ± 9.26, 12.10 ± 9.57, and 10.48 ± 9.90, respectively, while the percentages of cells in late apoptosis were 10.60 ± 8.80, 11.05 ± 3.31, and 29.15 ± 15.19, respectively. Compared with DMSO, the percentages of cell population in early and late apoptosis were 6.43 ± 5.95 and 4.43 ± 0.38, respectively (Fig. 3).

A. sieberi methanolic extract increased caspase-3/−7 and mitochondrial transition pore opening activity in TNBC MDA-MB-231 cells

The use of confocal laser scanning microscopy after employing the Invitrogen Image-iT™ Red Caspase-3 and Caspase-7 Detection Kit revealed increased caspase-3/−7 activity (red) in A. sieberi methanolic extract-treated MDA-MB-231 cells, compared to untreated cells, indicating enhanced apoptotic process (Fig. 4). Hoechst stain (nucleic acid dye) was used to label nuclei and visualize the total cell population. Upon A. sieberi methanolic extract treatment tested at 100, 200, and 400 µg/mL, the various percentages of cells with activated caspase-3/−7 in MDA-MB-231 cells were high reaching 70.05% with 4.15 SD (p = 8.43 × 10–7), 73.12% with 4.11 SD (p = 6.44 × 10 −7) and 90.04% with 2.52 SD (p = 1.36 × 10–6), respectively, compared to untreated and DMSO-treated cells. As expected, upon Mitoxantrone (Mito) treatment, the caspase-3/−7 activity reached 81.41% (P = 8.2 × 10–5) of the cell population (Fig. 4), compared to low percentages of cell population with activated caspase-3/−7 detected in both 1% DMSO-treated (4%) and untreated (2.66%) cells.

Using Western blot analysis, a decreased expression of the anti-apoptotic marker Bcl-2 and an increased expression of the pro-apoptotic marker Bax were observed (Fig. 6). Regarding Bax gene expression, treatment with increasing concentration of methanolic extract of A. sieberi resulted in increased gene expression compared to the untreated control group. Relative expression levels (mean ± standard deviation) were reported as: untreated (1.00 ± 0.37), DMSO (1.24 ± 0.23), 100 μg/mL (1.50 ± 0.73), 200 μg/mL (1.99 ± 0.48; P = 0.017), and 400 μg/mL (1.78 ± 0.51; P = 0.049). In contrast, Bcl-2 expression exhibited a significant, concentration-dependent decrease in gene expression. The (mean ± SD) values were: untreated (1.00 ± 0.13), DMSO (0.91 ± 0.18), 100 μg/mL (0.73 ± 0.16; P = 0.046), 200 μg/mL (0.62 ± 0.04; P = 0.0051), and 400 μg/mL (0.47 ± 0.04; P = 0.0013). The chart indicates an increased pro-apoptotic activity based on the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio assessed in A. sieberi methanolic extract-treated cells compared to untreated cells, indicating significant apoptotic activities in the treated cells (Fig. 6).

Methanolic extract of A. sieberi inhibits colony formation in TNBC MDA-MB-231 cells

The colony formation assay revealed a significant reduction in the number of colonies formed upon treatment with the methanolic extract of A. sieberi (Fig. 7). MDA-MB-231 cells treated with 100, 200, and 400 µg/mL of the extract showed a dose-dependent decrease in colony formation, with an average colony number of 9.60 ± 4.67 (p = 3.34 × 10–8), 2.87 ± 2.27 (p = 0.024), and 0.27 ± 0.43 (p = 0.0021), respectively, compared to the untreated control with an average colony number of 58.80 ± 18.94. The reduction in colony formation observed at 400 µg/mL was close to the positive control, Mitoxantrone (1 µM), which resulted in 0.07 ± 0.15 colonies. The cells treated with (1% DMSO) did not alter colony formation. Statistical analysis confirmed that treatments with 200 µg/mL and 400 µg/mL extract, as well as Mitoxantrone, significantly reduced colony formation compared to the untreated control (*p < 0.05). (Fig. 7). These findings suggest that A. sieberi extract potently disrupts the proliferative capacity of MDA-MB231 breast cancer cells, warranting further investigation into its mechanism of action.

Assessment of the pharmacokinetic properties of compounds identified in A. sieberi methanolic extract

The drug candidates follow Lipinski’s Rule of Five, that recommends ≤ 10 acceptors and ≤ 5 donors, so all tested compounds met this criterion. The molecular weight of the compounds shown in Table 3 ranged from 121.18 g/mol (C11) to 436.62 g/mol (C7) below 500 g/mol, making all these compounds suitable for drug development. The number of hydrogen bond acceptors ranged from 1 to 8, and that of donors from 1 to 3. Log Po/w values indicate how well a compound dissolves in fats vs. water (higher values are more lipophilic). Most of the compounds fell in the range of moderate (1–4), indicating balanced lipophilicity for optimal absorption and distribution. C5 (7.80) and C8 (8.02) were highly lipophilic, suggesting poor water solubility and potential bioavailability issues (Table 3 & Fig. 10). Most of the compounds were predicted to be soluble (C1, C2, C3, C4, C6, C7, C10, C11) with an acceptable degree of solubility, allowing the molecule to be permeable to cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB) while demonstrating high gastrointestinal (GI, oral) absorption. C5 and C9 were predicted to be moderately soluble, meaning they may require solubilizing agents, while C8 was predicted to be poorly soluble, which could limit its bioavailability and require modifications. Most of the compounds showed high GI absorption, meaning they could likely be well absorbed when taken orally. C5 and C8 had low absorption. Compounds C2, C3, C4, C6, C9, C10, and C11 could cross through the BBB, supporting their Central Nervous System (CNS) activity potential. On the other hand, C1, C5, C7, and C8 were predicted not to penetrate BBB, meaning they would be unlikely to affect CNS. Some compounds were predicted to inhibit liver enzymes: C6 (CYP1A2), C5 (CYP2C9), C9 (CYP1A2 and CYP2D6), and C11 (CYP1A2) (Table 3).

Molecular docking

Molecular docking was performed to assess the binding affinity of identified compounds from A. sieberi methanolic extract to the protein Bcl-2 (PDB ID: 6O0K), using Venetoclax as the reference ligand. The docking interactions were visualized with the compounds shown in the following colors: Venetoclax (green), Beta-Sitosterol (orange), Stigmasterol (violet), Neosolaniol (teal), and Vulgarin (yellow) (Fig. 12). The native ligand, Venetoclax, exhibited the highest docking score of −8.549 kcal/mol, forming pi–pi and pi–cation interactions with TYR 202, along with multiple hydrogen bonds involving GLY 145, ASP 103, and TYR 108 (Table 6). Among the tested compounds, β-Sitosterol (C8) demonstrated the highest docking score of −6.460 kcal/mol, then Stigmasterol (C5) at −5.644 kcal/mol, and Neosolaniol (C1) at −5.494 kcal/mol. Vulgarin (C2) exhibited the lowest docking score among the compounds at −5.355 kcal/mol. A few compounds exhibited specific binding interactions. Stigmasterol (C5) formed a hydrogen bond with ASP 140, while Neosolaniol (C1) established a hydrogen bond with ASP 111. The remaining compounds (C3, C4, C6, C7, C9, C10, and C11) showed no any interactions. Among the analyzed ligand, β-Sitosterol, Stigmasterol, and Neosolaniol demonstrated the best binding profiles with Bcl-2, as their binding poses suggest potential interactions within the protein’s active site. It is worth noting that Venetoclax possesses a relatively large structure with multiple heteroatoms, capable of forming extensive interactions within the active site. Moreover, it occupies a broader region of the binding pocket compared to the tested compounds. In contrast, the compounds identified from A. sieberi are structurally smaller, more lipophilic, and contain fewer heteroatoms, that may limit their binding capacity. As a result, unoccupied space remains within the Bcl-2 active site, that may contribute to the lower docking scores of these compounds compared to Venetoclax. The remaining compounds showed no docking results and were below the docking threshold (Table 6).

Discussion

Conventional breast cancer treatments, including surgery, chemotherapy, radiation, and biological therapies, often cause severe side effects that significantly impact patients' quality of life. These side effects include lymphedema, neuropathy, cardiotoxicity, osteoporosis, and immune-related side effects (Maksymowicz et al. 2023; Salata et al. 2021; Tommasi et al. 2022). In contrast, plant-derived compounds, such as extracts from Artemisia species, show promise in breast cancer treatment with fewer side effects (Kawakami et al. 2020; Shrihastini et al. 2021). Artemisia species, traditionally used for various ailments, are rich in bioactive compounds like sesquiterpenes, known for their anticancer and therapeutic properties (Al-Otibi et al. 2023; Hemmati & Nikooei 2019; Mahboubi et al. 2015). In search of effective natural anticancer drugs against highly aggressive TNBC, we evaluated the anticancer properties of A. sieberi methanolic extract which was found to be more cytotoxic in TNBC MDA-MB-231 cells than in hormone-dependent MCF-7 cells. TNBC cell death was associated with activation of the extrinsic apoptotic pathway by increased caspase-3/−7 activity, and activation of the intrinsic apoptotic pathway by enhanced outer membrane permeability, upregulation of Bax and downregulation of Bcl-2. In addition, some of the identified A. sieberi methanolic extract-derived compounds (i.e., β-sitosterol, stigmasterol, and Neosolaniol) were suggested as potential Bcl-2 inhibitors after predicting molecular interactions using docking analysis. Further in vitro and in vivo studies are still needed to validate the anticancer potential of these A. sieberi methanolic extract-derived compounds against TNBC.

The MDA-MB-231 cell line is one of the most widely used models in TNBC research, accounting for approximately 40.2% of all citations in PubMed and being extensively cited in the literature with over 18,000 references (Dai et al. 2017; Liu et al. 2019; Wagner 2022). This model is characterized by its aggressive behavior, high capacity to migrate, and spread to other tissues (Rodríguez-Cruz et al. 2024). On the other hand, some studies have demonstrated the activity of A. sieberi against other breast cancer cells such as MCF-7 (Break et al. 2023), while there are no published reports directly evaluating the effect of the extract on MDA-MB-231 cells, with exception of a recent study using a nano formulation (AgCINPs-ASE) (Irani et al. 2025). Therefore, our study focused on the use of MDA-MB-231 cells as an in vitro TNBC model.

Before starting the in vitro investigation, the aqueous extract of A. sieberi was subjected to the detection of endotoxins, lipopolysaccharides from gram-negative bacteria, which can impact in vitro studies by inducing unintended cellular responses, emphasizing the need for careful quantification (Kawakami et al. 2020; Rasuli et al. 2022). A. sieberi extract solution had endotoxin levels within the non-toxic range (Magalhães et al. 2007), ensuring that the observed effects were due to the extract itself.

The selection of the methanolic extract for further biological studies was based primarily on the results of the cell viability assay (CellTiter-Glo®), which showed a dose-dependent decrease in ATP levels, indicating decreased metabolic activity and cell viability. Notably, the methanolic extract exhibited a greater toxic effect against MDA-MB-231 (TNBC) cells compared to hormone-dependent MCF-7 cells. This effect against poorly invasive and hormone-dependent MCF-7 cells aligns with previous studies which highlighted the significant effects of A. sieberi essential oils on the same cell line (Break et al. 2023). While silver chloride nanoparticles derived from A. sieberi have shown effectiveness against MDA-MB-231 cells (Irani et al. 2024), studies on the anticancer activity of methanolic and ethanolic extracts against this aggressive TNBC subtype remain scarce. The observed higher sensitivity of MDA-MB-231 cells compared to MCF-7 underscores the potential of A. sieberi as a therapeutic option for challenging breast cancer types, emphasizing the need for further investigation.

As the first hallmark of cell death, morphological features exhibited by TNBC MDA-MB-231 cells exposed to A. sieberi methanolic extract such as cell shrinkage, nuclear fragmentation, chromatin condensation, membrane blebbing, and apoptotic bodies were observed, supporting the induction of apoptosis promoted by A. sieberi methanolic extract. These changes were gradually intensified with increasing concentrations, leading to an in-depth study of the molecular mechanism related to apoptosis.

The ability of A. sieberi extracts to induce apoptosis in cancer cells was confirmed using FACS for apoptosis status determination, confocal microscopy for the visualization of Caspase-3/−7 and mPTP activities, and Western blot technology for pro-apoptotic Bax and anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 detection. The significant increase in the percentage of apoptotic cells observed in the treated MDA-MB-231 cell population, associated with caspase-3/−7 activation, supports the conclusion that the A. sieberi methanolic extract promotes apoptosis via caspase-dependent mechanisms. The involvement of the intrinsic apoptosis pathway was further demonstrated through mPTP activation, which is associated with mitochondrial dysfunction, swelling, and membrane rupture as key steps in apoptotic cell death. In addition, detected in MDA-MB-231 cells, A. sieberi methanolic extract modulated the main mitochondrial bcl-2 family members controlling apoptosis towards induction, such as upregulated pro-apoptotic Bax and downregulated anti-apoptotic Bcl-2. Aligned with our results, A. sieberi extract increased the expression of the apoptosis-inducing gene Bax and decreased the expression of the anti-apoptotic gene Bcl-2 in HT-29 colon cancer cells (Saleh & Moradzadegan 2024). These findings are consistent with previous studies on Artemisia species that employed the MDA-MB-231 cells, showed that extracts induce mitochondrial-dependent intrinsic apoptotic pathway via activation of the Bax-mediated intrinsic pathway as well as generation of oxidative stress, leading to ROS accumulation (Das 2015). This Bax-mediated intrinsic pathway triggers mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization, which ultimately results in the release of cytochrome c, main component for apoptosome formation leading to caspase-9 activation then subsequently to caspase-3/7 activation, which causes DNA fragmentation, protein degradation and ultimately cell death (Wolf et al. 2022). While this present study primarily highlights caspase-dependent apoptosis through mitochondrial dysfunction in MDA-MB-231 cells, previous research on MCF-7 cells suggests that A. sieberi extracts may also induce caspase-independent apoptosis-like cell death, as evidenced by the lack of caspase-3 activation and inhibition of ERK signaling (Break et al. 2023). These findings indicate that A. sieberi might promote cell death through multiple pathways, including both caspase-dependent and caspase-independent mechanisms, which could contribute to its broad therapeutic potential across different breast cancer cell lines. Together, these findings underscore the multi-faceted anticancer activity of A. sieberi, involving both apoptosis induction and cell cycle regulation, which may enhance its therapeutic efficacy in treating breast cancer.

At the tumorigenic level, the colony formation assay illustrates a significant decrease in the proliferative capacity of MDA-MB-231 cells when treated with the methanolic extract of A. sieberi, specifically at high concentrations. The impaired growth of colony formation suggests that the A. sieberi methanolic extract not only induces apoptosis but also reduces the long-term ability of the cells to proliferate, which may limit their tumorigenic potential (Brix et al. 2021). It is notable that the significant decrease in colony formation at a concentration of 400 µg/mL of A. sieberi methanolic extract was almost similar to the effect of Mitoxantrone, a drug known for its chemotherapeutic properties (Preethi et al. 2022), confirming the cytotoxic efficacy of the extract. These results support the potential of A. sieberi methanolic extract as a promising candidate for further anticancer studies, particularly against TNBC.

The extract of A. sieberi was analyzed using GC/MS, with focused on the methanolic extract of A. sieberi, as this extract demonstrated chemical diversity and previously reported viability assay results. Identified compounds included Benzeneethanamine, N-Acetyltyramine, Albocycline C, 1-Naphthalenecarbonitrile, trans-Sinapyl alcohol, Vulgarin, Neosolaniol, Ethyl cholate, Stigmasterol, and β-Sitosterol. These findings align with prior studies demonstrating A. sieberi extract contains a diverse range of biologically active compounds, with sesquiterpenoids constituting the largest proportion of components, along with the presence of other compounds (El-Wassimy et al. 2019). The composition of these compounds varies depending on the plant part used, the extraction method, and the geographical location. (El-Wassimy et al. 2019; Jassbi et al. 2014). In our study, GC/MS analysis results also showed that the methanol extract of A. sieberi contains a variety of important bioactive compounds, including sesquiterpenes, alkaloids, phenols, and other compounds. FTIR studies also confirmed the presence of key functional groups such as aromatics, phenols, and esters, which explains the extract’s diverse biological activity (Al Otibi & Rizwana 2019; Maodaa et al. 2024, 2025). This reflects the chemical diversity of A. sieberi and confirms the presence of bioactive compounds that may contribute to the biological activity of the extract, supporting the current experimental results.

The PASS analysis provides valuable preliminary insights into the anticancer potential of A. sieberi metabolites. The results indicate that several compounds from A. sieberi possess strong anticancer potential. In general, using GC/MS analysis and PASS prediction software, the major secondary compounds were identified include Trichothecene mycotoxin (Neosolaniol, C1), sesquiterpene lactone (Vulgarin, C2), alkaloid/macrolide (Albocycline C, C3), and phenylpropanoid (trans-Sinapyl alcohol, C4). Among these, Neosolaniol (C1) showed the highest potential for anticancer activity, particularly against breast cancer. Steroids (such as Stigmasterol, C5 and β-Sitosterol, C8) were also detected with varying degrees of anticancer activity. These results suggest that Neosolaniol (C1) is the main active compound contributing to the extract’s efficacy against TNBC. The SwissADME predictions data provides comprehensive insights into the drug-likeness of A. sieberi compounds. Relative to previous research, plant-derived sterols, such as β-sitosterol and stigmasterol, have indicated anticancer potential (Jiang et al. 2024). These results reinforce the computational predictions obtained in this study, supporting the potential anticancer applications of the tested metabolites. The Protox II predictions further refine the safety profile of these compounds. Neosolaniol (C1) exhibits high toxicity (LD₅₀ = 36 mg/kg), making it potentially fatal if swallowed, while Vulgarin (C2) has moderate toxicity. Most other compounds are harmful but not highly toxic. In this study, SwissTargetPrediction identified multiple functional target classes for compounds of A. sieberi, with nuclear receptors, cytochrome P450 enzymes, and G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) being the most prevalent. These results are in line with several studies highlighting the therapeutic importance of these target groups in TNBC (Chen & Russo 2009; Engebraaten et al. 2013; Singh & Yadav 2021). Although SwissTargetPrediction indicated that the LXRα receptor could be a potential molecular target for β-Sitosterol, we focused on Bcl-2 because our Western blotting results showed a significant decrease in its expression and because there is previous evidence supporting its role in β-Sitosterol-induced apoptosis. For example, Vundru, 2013 (Vundru et al. 2013) reported a decrease in Bcl-2 in MDA-MB-231 cells following β-Sitosterol treatment, and Awad, 2003 (Awad et al. 2003) demonstrated that the apoptotic effect of β-Sitosterol is associated with a decrease in Bcl-2. Although the potential interaction with LXRα is important, their investigation was beyond the scope of this study and warrants future follow-up.

Docking results revealed that β-sitosterol, stigmasterol, and neosolaniol bind within the active site of Bcl-2, suggesting their potential role in modulating this anti-apoptotic protein. This finding aligns with the in vitro results, that demonstrated a reduction in Bcl-2 protein expression following treatment with A. sieberi methanolic extract, indicating a shift towards pro-apoptotic signaling. Bcl-2 inhibits apoptosis pathway by forming heterodimers with Bax and regulating calcium channels (Qian et al. 2022). Although several compounds derived from the A. sieberi methanolic extract did not exhibit high docking scores, this does not exclude their potential effect on Bcl-2, which may occur through indirect allosteric interactions, when binding to one receptor affects the other. Binding of a ligand to a distant site can alter the protein's structure and function without interacting with the typical binding pocket (Ilyas & Lee 2025; Nussinov & Tsai 2013). In addition, synergistic interactions among the components of the extract may collectively enhance biological activity, even if individual compounds do not interact in silico. These beneficial effects have been well-documented in phytochemical research, where the overall activity of the extract is comparable to the sum of its parts (Kang et al. 2023). These synergistic and allosteric effects may explain the observed downregulation of Bcl-2 protein expression in vitro, despite the lack of high-affinity binding by some individual compounds. Therefore, the combined evidence from in vitro experiments and in silico simulations suggests a multi mechanism through which A. sieberi compounds modulate Bcl-2-mediated apoptosis, that may involve both direct binding and indirect modulatory effects. Furthermore, a novel Bcl-2 inhibitor GW806742X has recently been reported as a promising therapeutic strategy against TNBC (Liu et al. 2025). Altogether, the compounds derived from the A. sieberi methanolic extract exhibiting interactions with Bcl-2 should be further investigated as potential Bcl-2 inhibitors, which could be developed for the treatment of TNBC.

Conclusions

Artemisia sieberi has demonstrated notable anticancer activity in breast cancer models, with particularly strong effects against triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) cells. The extracts significantly inhibited cell proliferation reduced tumorigenic potential, and induced apoptosis. These biological findings were reinforced by in silico molecular docking, which predicted stable interactions between A. sieberi derived compounds and critical proteins involved in cancer progression and apoptosis regulation. Together, the experimental and computational data offer a comprehensive view of A. sieberi’s potential mechanisms of action. This highlights the plant’s promise as a source of natural anticancer agents. To fully realize its therapeutic potential, further research is needed to isolate and characterize the active constituents responsible for these effects. Additionally, evaluating the efficacy of different A. sieberi extracts and purified compounds across various breast cancer subtypes including other TNBC variants other than MDA-MB-231, through both in vitro and in vivo studies will be crucial for advancing it towards clinical application. Moreover, future studies are recommended to investigate LXRα as another potential target of β-Sitosterol.
