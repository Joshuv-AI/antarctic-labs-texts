Beyond Helicobacter pylori: Prevalence, Coinfection, and Clinicopathologic Patterns of Non‐Helicobacter pylori Helicobacter (NHPH) in Human Gastric Biopsies
Authors: Daniel Vizenblit, Maya Azrad, Munir Nashashibi, Halim Roshrosh, Yaakov Maman, Avi Peretz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundNon‐ Helicobacter pylori Helicobacter (NHPH) species are increasingly recognized as human gastric colonizers, yet their prevalence, species distribution, and clinical significance remain incompletely defined, particularly in patient cohorts in the Middle East.Materials and MethodsIn this retrospective study, 830 formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded gastric biopsy samples collected from patients undergoing esophagogastroduodenoscopy at Tzafon Medical Center, northern Israel, between 2022 and 2024 were subjected to species‐specific PCR for H. pylori and NHPH species. Clinical, demographic, histopathological, and endoscopic data were correlated with PCR‐defined infection status.Results H. pylori was detected in 643/830 (77.5%), whereas at least one NHPH species was detected in 179 samples (21.6%). H. bizzozeronii was the predominant NHPH species, identified in 167/830 samples (20.1%), while H. heilmannii , H. felis , and H. ailurogastricus were rare (< 1%), and H. suis and H. salomonis were not detected. Co‐infection with H. pylori was common (18.2%), and was detected in 23.5% of all H. pylori ‐positive samples and 84.4% of NHPH‐positive samples. NHPH prevalence was higher in females and demonstrated marked seasonality, peaking in autumn. Histopathological comparison showed a significant difference in foveolar hyperplasia rates across infection groups, with higher frequencies in NHPH‐only and PCR detected negative for all species samples than in H. pylori ‐positive groups. In contrast, clinical symptoms and endoscopic findings were broadly comparable across infection strata.ConclusionsThese findings provide the first large‐scale molecular characterization of gastric NHPH in Israel and show that NHPH, predominantly H. bizzozeronii , is relatively common in this population, is usually co‐detected with H. pylori , and is associated more strongly with distinct epidemiologic and histopathologic patterns than with a distinct clinical or endoscopic phenotype.Keywords: coinfection, gastric biopsy, Helicobacter bizzozeronii, Helicobacter pylori, non‐Helicobacter pylori Helicobacter (NHPH), polymerase chain reaction (PCR), prevalence

Introduction

Gastric diseases span a wide spectrum from mild chronic gastritis to peptic ulcer disease and gastric cancer [1, 2] and constitute a significant global burden on healthcare systems. Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) is the most prevalent gastric bacterial pathogen, with approximately 50% of the world population estimated to be colonized. H. pylori infection is strongly associated with a broad spectrum of gastroduodenal diseases, including chronic gastritis, peptic ulcer disease, gastric adenocarcinoma and mucosa‐associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) lymphoma [3]. However, non–Helicobacter pylori Helicobacter (NHPH) species have also been shown to colonize the human stomach [4, 5, 6, 7] and have been identified in approximately 0.2%–6% of patients undergoing gastroscopy [8]. NHPH infection is often indistinguishable from H. pylori both clinically [9] and histologically, As a result, their prevalence and clinical significance are likely underestimated [6, 7, 9, 10, 11, 12]. Overall, data regarding the prevalence and clinical significance of NHPH species in human gastric disease remain scarce [10, 11, 13], and to date, no systematic studies have been conducted in Israel.

To place the present study in context, we compiled a focused narrative summary of representative human studies on gastric non‐ H. pylori Helicobacter infection, including study populations, diagnostic approaches, species distribution, and reported clinicopathological associations (Table 1). Relevant studies published from 2013 to 2025 were identified through targeted searches of PubMed and Google Scholar using combinations of terms related to non‐ H. pylori Helicobacter species, gastric disease, prevalence, and coinfection. This table was intended as a narrative overview rather than a formal systematic review. NHPH species encompass a heterogeneous group of organisms whose pathogenicity in humans is not yet fully defined [4, 14]. While several reports and experimental models suggest that specific species, particularly H. suis , contribute to chronic gastritis, peptic ulcer disease, gastric MALT lymphoma, and possibly gastric carcinogenesis [5, 7, 14, 15, 16], other studies have failed to demonstrate a consistent association between NHPH infection and gastric cancer or advanced premalignant lesions [17, 18] (Table 1). Overall, current data are limited by small sample sizes, heterogeneous diagnostic methods, and frequent co‐infection or prior exposure to H. pylori . Consequently, the true oncogenic potential of NHPH species and their contribution to human gastric malignancies remain controversial, underscoring the need for well‐designed epidemiological and molecular studies in different populations [7, 10, 17]. The present study aimed to assess the prevalence of NHPH in patients with gastric disease in northern Israel. Using molecular detection methods, archived gastric biopsies initially classified by routine histopathology were re‐evaluated for the presence of H. pylori and NHPH species. In addition, molecular findings were correlated with histopathological features, endoscopic findings, and patient demographic and clinical data to better characterize the epidemiology and clinical relevance of NHPH infection.

Materials and Methods

Gastric Biopsy Clinical Specimens

Study Set and Data Collection

The study set included a random selection of 830 formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) gastric biopsy samples collected from different patients experiencing gastric complaints who underwent esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) at Tzafon Medical Center between 2022 and 2024. All associated clinical data were anonymized. The study was approved by the Ethical Approval Committee of the Tzafon Medical Center (POR‐0067‐22), which waived the need for patient consent.

The following demographic and clinical data were collected from patient records: age, gender, nationality, place of residence, biopsy site, pathological changes, gastritis‐related findings and gastroscopic lesion detection. Patient signs, symptoms, and gastroscopy reasons were also collected.

Determination of NHPH Prevalence by PCR

The gastric biopsy specimens were formalin‐fixed and paraffin‐embedded prior to sectioning. FFPE tissue blocks were sectioned using a microtome to a thickness of 20 μm, and the sections were preserved in nuclease‐free tubes for subsequent DNA extraction and molecular analysis. The MagCore Genomic DNA FFPE One‐Step Kit and MagCore Automated Extraction instruments were used to extract total DNA from FFPE sectioned tissues. For positive controls, genomic DNA was purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (USA) and the German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures GmbH (DSMZ), and was also extracted from Helicobacter ATCC 43504 strain using the Promega Wizard Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Promega, Madison, USA), as described below. The extracted DNA was preserved at −80°C, until further use.

Helicobacter Species‐Specific PCR Assay

PCR was performed using the previously described specific primers and running conditions [9, 17, 19, 20, 21] (Table A1). Each 20 μL reaction mixture was comprised of 80 ng purified genomic DNA, 10 μL Hy‐Taqman master mix (Hy Laboratories Ltd., Rehovot, Israel), and 1 μL of each gene‐specific primer (0.5 μM). The remaining volume was PCR‐grade water. Bacterial DNA of the above‐mentioned control strains served as a positive control, and PCR‐grade water as a negative control. The amplified products were separated by 3% agarose gel electrophoresis. Images were acquired using a UV Imaging System (Bio‐Rad, California, USA).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software. Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages and compared between groups using the chi‐squared test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. Fisher's exact test was used when expected cell counts were small. Prevalence estimates were presented with 95% confidence intervals calculated using the Wilson method. Analyses were conducted using available PCR data only. A two‐sided p‐value of ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Large Language Models

ChatGPT 4o was utilized to enhance English writing and ensure fluency and grammatical correctness.

Results

Patient Characteristics, Demographic Features and Seasonal Infection Patterns

A total of 830 patients were included [380 males (45.8%), 450 females (54.2%)], of which 214 were aged < 18 years (25.8%), 161 aged 18–40 years (19.4%), 218 aged 41–60 years (26.3), and 237 aged ≥ 61 years (28.6) (Table 2). Patients were predominantly Jewish (655/830, 78.9%), a smaller subset were Arab (166/830, 20%) and a small minority were of other or unknown nationality (9/830, 1.1%). Residence type was primarily city (478/830, 57.6%), while the remaining patients lived in villages (244/830, 29.4%) or local councils (108/830, 13%) (Table 2).

Biopsies were obtained from the antrum in 492/830 (59.3%) cases, the corpus in 167/830 (20.1%), and from both antrum and corpus in 165/830 (19.9%) cases. In six cases (0.7%), samples were collected from other gastric sites. H. pylori was detected by PCR in 643/830 (77.5%) samples, with similar prevalence in males (294/380, 77.4%) and females (349/450, 77.6%). NHPH + H. pylori Co‐Infection Patterns

A total of 151 of the 643 H. pylori ‐positive samples (23.5%) tested positive for co‐infection with at least one NHPH species, while the majority of NHPH‐positive samples (151/179, 84.4%) were also positive for H. pylori . Among NHPH‐positive samples, H. bizzozeronii was detected in 140/151 (92.7%) co‐infected samples and 27/28 (96.4%) NHPH‐only samples, while H. heilmannii (7/151, 4.6%) and H. felis (6/151, 4.0%) were exclusively observed in the co‐infection group. Histopathological Characteristics by Helicobacter Infection Status

To assess whether PCR‐defined infection status translated into distinct tissue‐level pathology, histopathological features were compared across four groups: co‐infection ( H. pylori + and NHPH+), H. pylori only ( H. pylori + and NHPH−), NHPH only ( H. pylori − and NHPH+), and PCR‐negative ( H. pylori − and NHPH−). Foveolar hyperplasia was observed in a significantly higher proportion of NHPH‐only samples (8/28, 28.6%; p < 0.0001), as compared to co‐infected (15/151 (9.9%)), H. pylori ‐only (47/492 (9.6%)) and PCR‐negative (38/159, 23.9%) samples. Both dysplasia (0/28, 0/151, 0/492, and 1/159 (0.6%), p = 0.2381), and gastric cancer cases (0/28 (0.0%), 1/151 (0.7%), 1/492 (0.2%), and 1/159 (0.6%), p = 0.7677) were rare, with no significant between‐group differences. In a species‐focused analysis restricted to H. bizzozeronii , the predominant NHPH, the same pattern was observed, with foveolar hyperplasia more frequent in H. bizzozeronii mono‐infection (8/27, 29.6%) and PCR‐negative (38/160, 23.8%) samples than in H. bizzozeronii ‐ H. pylori co‐infection (13/140, 9.3%) and in H. pylori only (49/503, 9.7%) samples (p < 0.0001).

Endoscopic Findings by Helicobacter Infection Status

Within the NHPH‐only group, gastritis‐related endoscopic findings were observed in 13/28 cases (46.4%) and consisted of diffuse redness (10 cases), nodularity (two cases), and enlarged folds (one case). No cases of endoscopic atrophy or suspected intestinal metaplasia were observed in the NHPH‐only group. Esophagitis (5/28, 17.9%) and duodenal involvement, including any duodenal peptic lesion (5/28, 17.9%), duodenitis (5/28, 17.9%) and duodenal erosions (4/28, 14.3%). Overall, there was no evidence that NHPH infection, either alone or in combination with H. pylori , was associated with an increased frequency of gastric or duodenal pathology compared with H. pylori mono‐infection. In fact, gastritis detection rates were similar in the co‐infection and H. pylori ‐only groups (87/151, 57.6% and 278/492, 56.5%, respectively) and lower in the NHPH‐only and PCR‐negative groups (13/28, 46.4% and 76/159, 47.8%, respectively); however, this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.172). Duodenal findings were more common in the NHPH‐only group, including any duodenal peptic lesion (NHPH‐only: 5/28, 17.9%; co‐infection: 14/151, 9.3%; H. pylori only: 62/492, 12.6%; p = 0.3753) and duodenal erosions (NHPH‐only: 4/28, 14.3%; co‐infection: 11/151, 7.3%; H. pylori only: 49/492, 10.0%; p = 0.4788). In a species‐focused analysis restricted to H. bizzozeronii , spotty redness was more frequent in samples co‐infected with H. bizzozeronii and H. pylori compared with those with H. pylori mono‐infection (15/140, 10.7% vs. 23/503, 4.6%), but this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.0597).

Clinical Symptoms by Helicobacter Infection Status

Within the NHPH‐only group, abdominal pain (15/28, 53.6%), followed by vomiting (3/28, 10.7%), GERD/heartburn (3/28, 10.7%), and iron deficiency anemia (2/28, 7.1%) were the most frequently reported clinical symptoms, while melena and weight loss were not reported at all (0/28, 0.0% each). Symptom rates were broadly comparable across the four infection groups, with abdominal pain remaining the predominant symptom (75/151, 49.7% in co‐infection; 276/492, 56.1% in H. pylori only; 15/28, 53.6% in NHPH only; and 82/159, 51.6% in PCR‐negative; p = 0.4954). Rates of vomiting (p = 0.7181), GERD/heartburn (p = 0.8398), weight loss (p = 0.6999), iron deficiency anemia (p = 0.3235), and melena (p = 0.4536) did not differ significantly by infection status (Tables A8 and A9). Similarly, in a species‐focused analysis restricted to H. bizzozeronii , symptom profiles showed no between‐group differences (Tables A8 and A9).

Discussion

In the study sample of 830 gastric biopsies, NHPH was detected in 21.6%. The most common species was H. bizzozeronii (20.1%), whereas H. heilmannii , H. felis and H. ailurogastricus were very rare and H. suis and H. salomonis were not detected. Co‐infection with H. pylori was common (18.2%), while NHPH mono‐infection was uncommon (3.4%), with most NHPH‐positive biopsies also positive for H. pylori (84.4%).

Prevalence estimates of gastric NHPH in humans vary widely across geographic settings and across study designs and diagnostic approaches (Table 1). Earlier studies, often relying on histology with limited molecular confirmation, typically reported NHPH detection rates of 0.2%–6% [8]. However, more recent molecular studies demonstrated that prevalence can be substantially higher in select clinical populations and when broad PCR‐based detection is applied. For example, in well‐defined H. pylori ‐negative gastric disease cohorts in Belgium, NHPH was detected in approximately 28%–29% of cases [9], whereas routine biopsy workflows in the USA have reported lower rates of around 1%–3%, depending on the population and sampling context [22]. In addition, a recent PCR‐based study from Portugal reported H. bizzozeronii in 35.9% (14/54) of patients undergoing upper gastrointestinal endoscopy, with mixed infection with H. pylori observed in 22.2% [23]. The NHPH detection rate of 21.6% observed in the present cohort likely reflects the combined influence of local epidemiology and a sensitive PCR‐based strategy applied across a large endoscopy‐derived sample set.

The prevalence and species distribution observed here differ from many earlier reports in which human gastric NHPH infections were described as relatively uncommon and frequently dominated by pig‐associated species, such as H. suis , rather than dog‐ and cat‐associated species [8, 12, 20, 24]. In the present cohort, H. bizzozeronii was considerably more frequent as compared to other recent studies (Table 1). This The predominance of H. bizzozeronii in the present cohort is biologically plausible because H. bizzozeronii is a well‐established gastric colonizer of dogs and has been repeatedly implicated in zoonotic transmission to humans [5, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28]. In Israel, where the dog population is estimated at approximately 600,000, human exposure to canine reservoirs is likely common, although this study did not directly assess individual exposure history [29]. The findings raise the possibility that H. bizzozeronii is under‐detected in routine histology‐driven workflows without species‐specific molecular screening for NHPH. Future studies incorporating standardized exposure histories (e.g., pet ownership, intensity of dog and cat contact, and occupational animal exposure) will be important to clarify whether this species predominance reflects zoonotic exposure patterns.

NHPH prevalence, driven primarily by H. bizzozeronii , was higher in females. Although causal inference is not possible, differences in exposure patterns (e.g., pet ownership and intensity of contact with dogs and cats), host susceptibility and healthcare‐seeking behavior may all contribute to the observed female predominance. A marked seasonality signal for H. bizzozeronii was also observed, with the highest rates in autumn (29.7%), compared with spring (15.6%), summer (14.3%) and winter (19.9%) (p = 0.0002). H. pylori PCR detection similarly varied by season (83.0% in autumn vs. 72.3%–81.6% in other seasons, p = 0.007). It remains to be determined whether these patterns reflect true seasonal variation in exposure or transmission, differences in referral and sampling across seasons, or other operational factors. Future studies should aim to systematically capture season, exposure variables and key clinical confounders (e.g., recent antibiotics and acid suppression) to better define the drivers of NHPH infection.

The strong tendency toward co‐infection in the current dataset is consistent with prior works indicating that NHPH is frequently detected alongside H. pylori rather than alone [21, 23]. Several mechanisms may account for this pattern. First, shared exposure pathways may contribute, as individuals with close contact with animal reservoirs (particularly dogs and cats) may be at increased risk of acquiring NHPH, while simultaneously being exposed to human‐associated H. pylori through intrafamilial transmission or environmental routes [3]. Second, chronic H. pylori ‐associated gastritis may modify the gastric microenvironment, potentially facilitating transient colonization or persistence of other Helicobacter species. Third, referral and sampling context may bias detection; as patients undergo endoscopy primarily due to suspected H. pylori infection, NHPH testing is disproportionately performed in a cohort enriched for H. pylori , increasing the apparent frequency of co‐detection. Collectively, these considerations complicate causal attribution of mucosal injury to NHPH alone in co‐infected patients.

In the current cohort, there was no apparent difference in the severity of clinical symptoms and endoscopic findings among NHPH‐positive groups compared with H. pylori mono‐infected cases. Thus, although NHPH species have been linked to chronic gastritis [8], peptic ulcer disease [30], and, in selected reports, gastric MALT lymphoma [16, 31, 32, 33], the presented population‐level data do not support a distinctly more aggressive phenotype associated with co‐infection in this routine endoscopy‐derived setting. Clinically, NHPH detection may still be important in specific scenarios, particularly in cases of H. pylori ‐negative gastritis with suggestive histology or in gastric MALT lymphoma, where clarifying etiological factors can influence interpretation and subsequent management. In these contexts, diagnosis can be refined by using molecular testing as a complementary approach to routine histology, with the caveat that PCR identifies bacterial DNA but does not confirm active infection.

Across PCR‐defined infection categories, the clearest histopathologic distinction was observed in the distribution of foveolar hyperplasia. Foveolar hyperplasia was less frequent in H. pylori ‐positive groups (co‐infection and H. pylori mono‐infection) and more frequent in NHPH‐only and PCR‐negative groups. A similar pattern was observed when restricting the analysis to H. bizzozeronii , with the highest proportion of foveolar hyperplasia measured in H. bizzozeronii mono‐infection samples. Several interpretations of this phenomenon should be considered. First, foveolar hyperplasia is widely regarded as a reactive epithelial change and may reflect the existence of noninfectious drivers such as chemical injury, bile reflux, or medication‐associated gastropathy [34, 35], which could be more prevalent among Helicobacter‐negative patients undergoing endoscopy. Second, in the absence of H. pylori , NHPH is more likely to represent the primary colonizing organism rather than a low‐abundance co‐detected species. This may increase the likelihood that any associated epithelial changes reflect the biological impact of NHPH itself, rather than being masked by concurrent H. pylori infection. However, given the relatively small size of the NHPH‐only group, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and viewed as hypothesis‐generating. Consistent with these histopathologic observations, no clear increase in symptom burden or endoscopic severity was observed across NHPH infection groups, either alone or in combination with H. pylori , compared with H. pylori mono‐infection. This finding is clinically relevant because it suggests that NHPH can be considered a co‐occurring finding rather than as a marker of a more aggressive phenotype. Moreover, several common endoscopic endpoints are strongly influenced by noninfectious factors, including NSAID exposure, bile reflux, and other causes of reactive gastropathy, which can obscure differences attributable to Helicobacter status in routine practice. Accordingly, the clinical utility of NHPH testing may be greatest in targeted scenarios, such as H. pylori ‐negative gastritis with suggestive histology or diagnostically unresolved cases, rather than as a broad severity marker. Taken together, these findings suggest that NHPH detection is not associated with increased endoscopic severity in this cohort. In contrast, H. pylori infection has been consistently linked to characteristic endoscopic patterns in prior studies [35], which may explain the limited contribution of NHPH status to the macroscopic findings observed here. Future studies are needed to determine whether NHPH is associated with cellular‐level changes that may not always translate into detectable tissue‐level or endoscopic findings in the short term but may have long‐term implications.

This study had limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. The retrospective, single‐center design and inclusion of patients undergoing clinically indicated upper endoscopy introduce potential selection bias and limit generalizability beyond comparable endoscopy‐derived clinical populations. Although internal comparisons were performed across PCR‐defined infection categories, the absence of an asymptomatic control group precludes inference regarding prevalence in the general population. Important clinical and exposure variables were not uniformly available, including recent antibiotic therapy, proton pump inhibitor and NSAID use, and detailed zoonotic exposure data, all of which may influence both Helicobacter detection and clinicopathologic outcomes. PCR performed on FFPE‐derived tissue identifies bacterial DNA but cannot independently distinguish active infection from low‐bacterial‐level carriage or residual DNA, nor does it establish tissue localization. In addition, species attribution was not systematically confirmed across all positive samples using independent confirmatory methods. Several NHPH species were infrequent and the NHPH‐only subgroup was relatively small, limiting statistical power for species‐specific inference. Despite these limitations, the large cohort size, systematic multi‐target PCR screening and its integration with clinical, endoscopic, and histopathologic data, provided rigorous characterization of NHPH patterns within a routine clinical endoscopy setting and define a clear framework for prospective validation.

Conclusion

In this large endoscopy‐derived sample set, NHPH species were detected by multi‐target PCR in 21.6% of gastric biopsies, with H. bizzozeronii occurring in the majority of samples, most often alongside H. pylori . NHPH prevalence varied by gender and demonstrated a pronounced seasonal peak, suggesting a nonrandom epidemiologic patterning that should be tested in independent cohorts incorporating standardized exposure and medication data. For the clinical and endoscopic outcomes evaluated, NHPH detection—either alone or with H. pylori —was not associated with a clearly more severe symptom burden or macroscopic endoscopic phenotype compared with H. pylori mono‐infection, indicating that co‐detection does not necessarily imply increased disease severity in routine practice. In contrast, the higher frequency of foveolar hyperplasia in NHPH‐only infections underscores the value of molecular testing for refining infection classification and motivates focused tissue‐level and mechanistic studies. Together, these findings provide robust estimates of NHPH prevalence and species distribution within a routine clinical biopsy setting and define priorities for prospective validation of exposure determinants, seasonality and clinical contexts in which NHPH testing can add diagnostic value.
