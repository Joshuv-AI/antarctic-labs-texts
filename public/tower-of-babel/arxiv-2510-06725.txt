Continuous measurement-based holonomic quantum computation
Anirudh Lanka, Juan Garcia-Nila, Todd A. Brun

Abstract
We propose a scheme to generate holonomies using the Quantum Zeno effect, enabling logical unitary operations on quantum stabilizer codes purely through measurements. The quantum error-correcting code space is adiabatically rotated by measuring a succession of rotated stabilizer generators. When the rotation is sufficiently slow, the state remains confined to the instantaneous code space by the Zeno effect; otherwise, measurement-induced jumps can occur into a rotated orthogonal subspace. If the rotation completes a closed loop, the code state is transformed by a holonomy: a logical unitary transformation. We analytically derive the sequence of rotated stabilizer generators that produce a desired holonomy, and find the total time required to implement this procedure with a given success probability. If a measurement moves the state to the orthogonal subspace, we present a method to alter the path of the rotated observables to return the state either to the original code or the original error space with the desired holonomy; in the latter case, the holonomy is emulated. Finally, we establish conditions on the code and the measured observables that preserve the correctability of a given error set. When a code fails to meet the error-correcting conditions, our protocol remains applicable by augmenting the code with at most two ancilla qubits.



I
Introduction

Achieving robust universal quantum computation requires a delicate balance between isolating computational degrees of freedom, to minimize environmental decoherence, and maintaining precise dynamical control. Limitations due to this trade-off often result in noise-induced errors. To mitigate them, quantum information is encoded in higher-dimensional systems, with computation performed on the encoded qubits
Shor 1995
;
Steane 1996
;
Lidar and Brun 2013
. In circuit-based quantum computing (CQC), universal logical gate sets typically involve multi-qubit interactions, which can propagate errors and make them uncorrectable, requiring fault-tolerant design
Knill et al. 1998
;
Shor 1996
;
Kitaev 2003
. While transversal gates are fault-tolerant, the Eastin-Knill theorem limits their universality
Eastin and Knill 2009
.

Geometric approaches, such as holonomic and topological quantum computation, offer promising pathways to universal fault-tolerant computation
Zanardi and Rasetti 1999
;
Kitaev 2003
;
Freedman et al. 2002
;
Sjöqvist et al. 2012
;
Lidar and Brun 2013
;
Lahtinen and Pachos 2017
;
Wu et al. 2005
;
Zheng and Brun 2014
;
Oreshkov 2009
;
Zhang et al. 2018
;
Oreshkov et al. 2009a
. Holonomic Quantum Computing (HQC) performs logical operations by using the geometric phase acquired by traversing a parameterized code space along loops in a suitable manifold, where the path determines the resulting unitary operation
Zanardi and Rasetti 1999
. This method has been shown to be robust against certain systematic errors
Cesare et al. 2015
;
Oreshkov et al. 2009b
;
Buividovich and Kuvshinov 2006
;
Solinas et al. 2004
;
Chen et al. 2020
;
Liang et al. 2025
;
Shen et al. 2023
;
Florio et al. 2006
;
Solinas et al. 2012
;
Fuentes-Guridi et al. 2005
;
Sarandy and Lidar 2006
. Conversely, Topological Quantum Computing (TQC) relies on the braiding of anyonic excitations, and is inherently fault-tolerant due to its dependence on the topology rather than the specific geometry of the path
Kitaev 2003
;
Nayak et al. 2008
. While TQC remains mostly theoretical, experimental demonstrations of HQC across various quantum architectures have been proposed and some achieved
Wassner et al. 2025
;
Feng et al. 2013
;
Toyoda et al. 2013
;
Solinas et al. 2003
;
Albert et al. 2016
;
Duan et al. 2001
.

Conventionally, HQC is performed by encoding quantum information in an eigenspace of a degenerate Hamiltonian and adiabatically evolving the system along closed loops in a control parameter space
Sjöqvist et al. 2012
;
Zheng and Brun 2015
;
Zheng and Brun 2014
;
Farhi et al. 2000
. This adiabatic evolution preserves the degeneracy of the Hamiltonian and induces non-Abelian geometric phases—holonomies—that enable universal quantum computation. However, adiabatic evolution is not the only mechanism by which degeneracy can be preserved. It is well-established that the adiabatic theorem and the quantum Zeno effect are closely related
Facchi and Pascazio 2002
;
Facchi 2003
;
Kitano 1997
;
Burgarth et al. 2022
;
Facchi et al. 2005
. In this paper, we explore the possibility of generating arbitrary holonomies through frequent measurements. A similar approach, based on using sequences of projective measurements to implement
discrete
holonomies, was explored in
Mommers and Sjöqvist 2022
for spin-coherent states (SCS). In this work, we provide an in-depth analysis of both discrete- and continuous-path holonomies for arbitrary stabilizer codes.

Discrete holonomies can be generated by incrementally rotating the quantum error-correcting code space and measuring the corresponding rotated projector, continuing the process until the rotation completes a closed loop. The resulting holonomy corresponds to a logical unitary gate that depends on the chosen path. When the rotation increment is sufficiently small, the state is projected to the rotated code space with high probability due to the Zeno effect. In the limit of infinitely many
weak
measurements and infinitesimal rotations, we can generate continuous-path holonomies. Interestingly, we find that the probability to stay in the code space differ between the discrete and continuous approaches.

The measurement-based holonomic approach is particularly appealing since it provides (partial) information about the state of the system. This is in general sufficient to identify whether the system is in the correct eigenspace without further disturbing the state. If it is not, one can appropriately control the system to bring it back to the desired eigenspace. This is true regardless of whether the rotations are discrete or continuous. This is unlike conventional Hamiltonian-based HQC, where there are no measurements to reveal errors along the path.

A quantum error-correcting code is associated with a (non-unique) “correctable” set of errors, denoted by
\mathcal{E}
. The Knill-Laflamme condition gives necessary and sufficient criteria for a code to correct
\mathcal{E}
. During the holonomic procedure, as the code space is rotated, the instantaneous codes should also be able to correct the same error set
\mathcal{E}
. We find sufficient conditions to satisfy this requirement for arbitrary stabilizer codes. For codes that do not satisfy these conditions, we can make the procedure work by appending at most two ancilla qubits.

The main results of this work are:

•

We introduce a framework to generate a discrete- (continuous)-path holonomy using a sequence of projective (weak) measurements.

•

We analytically derive the probabilities of staying in the desired eigenspace in both cases.

•

In case of a measurement-induced error, where the system is projected into a state orthogonal to the instantaneous code space, we show a way to dynamically modulate the path to correct it, and bring the state back to either the original code or the error space; in the latter case, the holonomy is emulated.

•

We find sufficient conditions for the instantaneous code to satisfy the error-correcting conditions throughout the loop.

This paper is organized as follows: we provide preliminary information about the dynamics in quantum Zeno subspaces and find the associated holonomy in
Section

II
. We construct a framework to perform measurement-based HQC in
Section

III
. We first consider discrete holonomy in
Section

III.1
, and show how to achieve implicit fault-tolerance, in
Section

III.2
. In
Section

III.3
, we extend that framework to allow continuous weak measurements while simultaneously rotating the code space. We derive the error-correcting conditions and show how to satisfy them throughout the evolution in
Section

IV
. We give concluding remarks and discuss the future scope of this work in
Section

V
.

II
Preliminaries

II.1
Quantum Zeno dynamics

Suppose a quantum system with Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
undergoes a series of measurements by a time-dependent family of observables

O(t)=U(t,0)O_{0}U^{\dagger}(t,0),

(1)

where
U(t,0)
is a unitary matrix, and
O_{0}\equiv O(0)
. For the measurements to have a nontrivial effect on the state, we require that

[U(t_{1},0),U(t_{2},0)]\neq 0\quad\forall t_{1}\neq t_{2}.

(2)

In a small time interval
\delta t
, the evolution unitary is

U(t+\delta t,t)=\exp(-iH(t)\delta t)+\mathcal{O}(\delta t^{2}),

(3)

where
H(t)
is a Hamiltonian that can be time-dependent. When
\delta t\ll 1
, then
U(t+\delta t,t)\approx I
, i.e., the unitary is
weak
; the observable
O(t)
is also rotated by a small amount. Observe that
O(t)
always remains Hermitian, and its eigenvalues are unchanged, since unitary transformations preserve eigenvalues. We can write the rotated observable as

O(t)=\sum_{s}\alpha_{s}\mathbb{P}_{s}(t),

(4)

where
\mathbb{P}_{s}(t)
is the projector onto the
\alpha_{s}
-eigenspace; let us denote this eigenspace by
\mathcal{H}_{s}(t)
. We can also write
O(t)
as

O(t)=\sum_{s}\alpha_{s}\left[U(t,0)\mathbb{P}_{s}(0)U^{\dagger}(t,0)\right],

(5)

i.e., the projectors onto the eigenspaces of the rotated observables also evolve through the same unitary transformation.

Suppose that a state
\ket{\psi(t)}\in\mathcal{H}_{0}(t)
undergoes a projective measurement of
O(t+\delta t)
. The probability that the state will be left in
\mathcal{H}_{0}(t+\delta t)
is

\displaystyle p_{0}

\displaystyle=\bra{\psi(t)}\mathbb{P}_{0}(t+\delta t)\ket{\psi(t)}

(6)

\displaystyle=\bra{\psi(t)}U(t+\delta t,t)\mathbb{P}_{0}(t)U^{\dagger}(t+\delta t,t)\ket{\psi(t)}

\displaystyle=1-\mathcal{O}(\delta t^{2}),

i.e., starting from the
\alpha_{0}
-eigenspace, when the time between the subsequent measurements
\delta t\ll 1
, the probability of being left in an eigenspace with the same eigenvalue is close to unity. In other words, all states are “confined” to their respective instantaneous subspaces throughout the evolution; these subspaces are formally known as
Quantum Zeno Subspaces
(QZS). The state evolves into

\begin{split}\ket{\psi(t+\delta t)}&=\frac{\mathbb{P}_{0}(t+\delta t)\ket{\psi(t)}}{\sqrt{p_{0}}}\\
&=\ket{\psi(t)}-i[H(t),\mathbb{P}_{0}(t)]\delta t+\mathcal{O}(\delta t^{2}).\end{split}

(7)

In the limit
\delta t\rightarrow 0
, we obtain a Markovian differential equation for the state evolution:

\ket{\mathrm{d}\psi}=-i[H(t),\mathbb{P}_{0}(t)]\ket{\psi}\mathrm{d}t.

(8)

It is important to note that the evolution is
not
unitary since
\tilde{H}(t)\equiv[H(t),\mathbb{P}_{0}(t)]
is anti-Hermitian. However, as we shall see, such dynamics can produce a closed-loop holonomy within a Zeno subspace.

II.2
Generating holonomies

We now introduce several algebraic constructions that are useful in finding the holonomy. We follow the theory presented in
Zheng and Brun 2014
, and show the equivalence between the obtained holonomies in the adiabatic and Zeno regimes. This gives yet more evidence that the two effects are analogous.

Suppose we are given a collection of independent vector subspaces. A natural geometric description of this collection is the complex Grassmannian manifold
\mathcal{B}\equiv G_{N,K}(\mathbb{C})
defined by

\mathcal{B}=\{\mathbb{P}\in M(N,N;\mathbb{C})|\mathbb{P}^{2}=\mathbb{P},\mathbb{P}^{\dagger}=\mathbb{P},\text{Tr}\{\mathbb{P}\}=k\},

(9)

where
M(N,N;\mathbb{C})
is the set of all
N\times N
complex matrices. Hence, each point on
G_{N,K}(\mathbb{C})
is a
K
-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{C}^{N}
. The set of basis vectors for a given subspace is not unique; different choices of bases are related by transformations from the unitary group
U(K)
. The
fiber
at a given point in
\mathcal{B}
consists of all possible orthonormal bases for that subspace. The collection of all such fibers forms a
fiber bundle
, known as the complex Stiefel manifold
\mathcal{F}\equiv S_{N,K}(\mathbb{C})
, which is defined as

\mathcal{F}=\{L\in M(N,K;\mathbb{C})|L^{\dagger}L=I_{K}\},

(10)

where
I_{K}
is the
K
-dimensional identity. Intuitively, the columns of
L
form an orthonormal basis for a subspace. In the context of quantum error-correcting codes, a point
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{B}
represents the projector onto the code space, while a point
L
in the fiber over
\mathbb{P}
contains the code basis states.

The left action of an
N
-dimensional unitary matrix gives a transformation within
G_{N,K}(\mathbb{C})
:

U(N)\times\mathcal{B}\rightarrow\mathcal{B},\;\;\;\;(g,\mathbb{P})\mapsto g\mathbb{P}g^{\dagger}.

(11)

Define the projection map
\pi:\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}
as

\pi(L)\equiv LL^{\dagger}.

(12)

The right action of a
K
-dimensional unitary matrix provides a transformation within the fiber associated with a point on
\mathcal{B}
:

\mathcal{F}\times U(K)\rightarrow\mathcal{F},\;\;\;\;(L,h)\mapsto Lh.

(13)

It is easy to see that the bases
V
and
Vh
span the same subspace and hence correspond to the same point on
G_{N,K}(\mathbb{C})
, since

\pi(Lh)=(Lh)(Lh)^{\dagger}=Lhh^{\dagger}L^{\dagger}=LL^{\dagger}=\pi(L).

(14)

Finally, the tuple
\left(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{B},\pi,U(K)\right)
is called the
principal bundle
that we operate on.

Suppose that a point
\mathbb{P}_{0}
on
\mathcal{B}
moves along a curve. Without loss of generality, assume that this motion arises by rotating the subspace projector
\mathbb{P}_{0}
with a parameterized unitary operator
V(t)
:

\mathbb{P}(t)=V(t)\mathbb{P}_{0}V^{\dagger}(t).

(15)

The associated curve on
\mathcal{F}
depends on its
connection
. The canonical connection on
\mathcal{F}
is defined as a
\mathfrak{u}(K)
-valued
1
-form on
\mathcal{B}
:

A=L^{\dagger}(t)\frac{\mathrm{d}L(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}.

(16)

This is the unique connection that is invariant under the transformation in
Eq.

13
:

\displaystyle\tilde{A}

\displaystyle=(L(t)h)^{\dagger}\frac{\mathrm{d}(L(t)h)}{\mathrm{d}t}

(17)

\displaystyle=h^{\dagger}Ah+h^{\dagger}\mathrm{d}h,

which is called a
gauge
transformation.

We are now ready to apply this formalism to measurement-based holonomic quantum computation. For each
t
, there exists
V(t)\in\mathcal{F}
such that
\mathbb{P}(t)=L(t)L^{\dagger}(t)
. In the limit of frequent measurements by
\mathbb{P}(t)
, the Zeno effect maintains the state in the instantaneous code space throughout the evolution by the argument presented in
Section

II.1
, and hence we can substitute the state
\ket{\psi(t)}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
with the reduced state vector
\ket{\phi(t)}\in\mathbb{C}^{K}
:

\ket{\psi(t)}=L(t)\ket{\phi(t)}.

(18)

Substituting
Eq.

18
in
Eq.

8
, we obtain

\frac{\mathrm{d}\big(L(t)\ket{\phi(t)}\big)}{\mathrm{d}t}=-i\big[H(t),\mathbb{P}(t)\big]L(t)\ket{\phi(t)}.

(19)

Using
L(t)L^{\dagger}(t)=\mathbb{P}(t)
and
L^{\dagger}(t)L(t)=I_{K}
, we have

\ket{\mathrm{d}\phi}+L^{\dagger}\mathrm{d}L\ket{\phi(t)}=0,

(20)

whose solution can be represented as

\ket{\phi(t)}=\mathcal{P}\exp\left(-\int L^{\dagger}\mathrm{d}L\right)\ket{\phi(0)},

(21)

where
\mathcal{P}
indicates path ordering. Therefore,
\ket{\psi(t)}
can be written as

\ket{\psi(t)}=L(t)\mathcal{P}\exp\left(-\int L^{\dagger}\mathrm{d}L\right)L^{\dagger}(0)\ket{\psi(0)}.

(22)

If the subspace evolves through a loop and returns to the initial subspace,
\mathbb{P}(T)=\mathbb{P}(0)
, the holonomy
\Gamma\in U(K)
is defined as

\Gamma=L^{\dagger}(0)L(T)\mathcal{P}\exp\left(-\int L^{\dagger}\mathrm{d}L\right),

(23)

and the final state is

\ket{\psi(T)}=L(0)\Gamma\ket{\phi(0)}.

(24)

A general solution for the curve
L(t)
is

L(t)=V(t)L(0)h(t),

(25)

where
h(t)\in U(K)
. Intuitively, this can be thought of as a two-step process: 1) rotate the basis states
L(0)
using
V(t)
so that
\pi(\tilde{L}(t))=\mathbb{P}(t)
, where
\tilde{L}(t)\equiv V(t)L(0)
; 2) rotate the basis states within the fiber using
h(t)
, giving
L(t)
. If the condition

L^{\dagger}\frac{\mathrm{d}L}{\mathrm{d}t}=0\;\,\forall t

(26)

is satisfied, the curve
L(t)\in\mathcal{F}
is called the horizontal lift of the curve
\pi(L(t))\in\mathcal{B}
(as shown in
Fig.

1
). Then the holonomy simplifies to

\Gamma=L^{\dagger}(0)L(T)\in U(K).

(27)

Figure 1:
The horizontal lift as a unique curve in
S_{N,K}(\mathbb{C})
with the base manifold
G_{N,K}(\mathbb{C})
. The difference between the initial point
V(0)
and the final point
V(T)
is the holonomy.

Substituting
Eq.

25
into
Eq.

26
, we obtain a differential equation for
h(t)
:

\frac{\mathrm{d}h(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=-L^{\dagger}(0)V^{\dagger}(t)\frac{\mathrm{d}V(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}L(0)h(t).

(28)

It is a well-known result of differential geometry that the horizontal lift associated with a given connection for a principal bundle is unique.

Proposition 1
.

Suppose
\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathcal{B}
be a curve in
\mathcal{B}
, and let
L_{0}\in\pi^{-1}(\mathbb{P}_{0})
. Then there exists a unique horizontal lift in
\mathcal{F}
such that
L(0)=L_{0}
.

Proof.

Since the right-hand side in
Eq.

28
is skew-Hermitian,
h(t)\in U(K)\;\forall t
. Suppose
\tilde{L}(t)=V(t)L(0)
is a particular curve in
\mathcal{F}
with the associated connection
\tilde{A}=\tilde{L}^{\dagger}\mathrm{d}\tilde{L}
. For the initial condition
h(0)=I_{K}
, the solution to
Eq.

28
is

h(t)=\mathcal{P}\exp\left(-\int\tilde{A}\right),

(29)

and hence there exists a unique horizontal lift.
∎

Finally, in the context of quantum computation on a stabilizer code, we wish to apply a logical unitary
\bar{U}\in U(K)
on the code
\mathcal{C}=\mathbb{P}(0)
. Since the connection in
Eq.

16
is unique (up to the fiber) and the horizontal lift of the connection is also unique, applying a logical unitary is equivalent to finding a loop
\mathbb{P}(t)\in\mathcal{B}
, such that
\mathbb{P}(0)=\mathbb{P}(T)
, whose horizontal lift
L(t)
produces the holonomy
\Gamma=\bar{U}
. In the following section, we show a method to apply this holonomy to a logical state using continuous measurements.

III
Zeno-effect-induced holonomic quantum computation

Suppose we have a family of
[\![n,k,d]\!]
quantum stabilizer codes that are parameterized by
\phi\in\{0,2\pi\}
. Each code has the stabilizer group
S(\phi)
with generators
\{g_{i}(\phi)\}_{i=1}^{n-k}
. The parameter
\phi=0
denotes the standard code we operate on, for which the stabilizer generators are all Pauli operators, as usual. Without loss of generality, the system’s Hilbert space can be decomposed into a direct sum of the simultaneous eigenspaces of all the stabilizers:

\mathcal{H}(\phi)=\bigoplus_{s=0}^{2^{n-k}}\mathcal{H}_{s}(\phi).

(30)

By convention, the simultaneous
+1
eigenspace is chosen to be the code space, denoted by
\mathcal{H}_{0}(\phi)
. In our case, all the eigenspaces are
2^{k}
-fold degenerate. Define the projector onto the standard code space as

\mathbb{P}_{0}\equiv\mathbb{P}(0)=\prod_{j=1}^{n-k}\frac{I+g_{j}(0)}{2}.

(31)

The logical gate that we wish to apply is

G=\mathrm{exp}\left(i\theta H\right),

(32)

where
H
is a
logical
Pauli operator. We can generate a holonomy by rotating the code space by
\phi
. When the rotation forms a loop and the system is back in the original code space, the holonomy corresponds to a logical operation on the standard code.

To uniformly rotate the code space, we choose a Pauli operator
X
(not to be confused by the single-qubit Pauli matrix
\sigma^{x}
) that satisfies the following conditions:

\displaystyle\exists g\in S(0),\;\{X,g\}=0

(33a)

\displaystyle\{X,H\}=0

(33b)

\displaystyle\exp\left(2\pi iX\right)=I.

(33c)

The rotation of the code space can be described using a family of unitary operators parameterized by
\phi
:

V(\phi)=\mathrm{exp}\left(i\frac{\theta\phi}{2\pi}H\right)\mathrm{exp}\left(i\phi X\right),

(34)

and the projector onto the rotated code space is

\mathbb{P}(\phi)=V(\phi)\mathbb{P}(0)V^{\dagger}(\phi).

(35)

Observe that
V(0)=I
and
V(2\pi)=\mathrm{exp}\left(i\theta H\right)
. So, starting with a state
\ket{\bar{\psi}}
in the code space, in a purely theoretical sense, multiplying it by
V(2\pi)
applies the logical transformation we desire. However, performing that in a real system is nontrivial; the goal of this work is to provide a method using measurements to realize this.

Consider a projective measurement of all the stabilizer generators of the unrotated code on a code state
\ket{\bar{\psi}}
. Since it is already an eigenstate, the probability of obtaining the same state
\ket{\bar{\psi}}
is 1. Now suppose the code space is rotated by the unitary operator
V(\phi)
. It is easy to see that the stabilizer generators for this rotated code space are also rotated by the same unitary transformation:

g(\phi)=V(\phi)g(0)V^{\dagger}(\phi),

(36)

where
g(0)
is a stabilizer generator of the original (unrotated) code.

Consider the extreme case where a projective measurement of all the stabilizer generators of the “fully” rotated code (by
V(2\pi)
) is made on the original state
\ket{\bar{\psi}}
. This also returns the same state with unit probability, since
\mathbb{P}(2\pi)=\mathbb{P}_{0}
. In other words, since the logical operator commutes with all the stabilizers, and the rotation unitary operator at
\phi=2\pi
depends only on the logical operator
H
(and not at all on
X
), measuring the stabilizers of the fully rotated code is equivalent to measuring the stabilizers of the original code. So, to effect the logical unitary we want, we instead rotate the code space by a
small
amount, measure the generators of the rotated code, and repeat until
\phi=2\pi
.

To do this, let us decompose the unitary
V(2\pi)
into a series of unitaries:

V(2\pi)=\tilde{U}(2\pi-\delta\phi)...\tilde{U}(\delta\phi)\tilde{U}(0),

(37)

where
\tilde{U}(\phi)
is defined as

\tilde{U}(\phi)\equiv V(\phi+\delta\phi)V^{\dagger}(\phi)

(38)

Intuitively, the unitary operator
\tilde{U}(\phi)
rotates the code space
\mathbb{P}(\phi)
by a small angle
\delta\phi
. Note that
\lim_{\delta\phi\rightarrow 0}\tilde{U}(\phi)=I
, i.e., the unitary is “weak.” Since
X
anticommutes with at least one generator, the rotated generators do not have the same code space as the original space. Projectively measuring the rotated stabilizers instantaneously moves the state into an eigenstate. By the argument in
Section

II.1
, the post-measurement state is a simultaneous
+1
eigenstate of the rotated stabilizers with high probability. By repeating the procedure until
\phi=2\pi
, the code state will have undergone an evolution through a
holonomic path
and applied the logical gate
G
.

Definition 1

(Holonomic path)
.

The tuple
(V,\Phi)
with entries denoting a family of unitary operators
V(\phi)
and the corresponding final parameter
\Phi
, is a holonomic path along which the code space is rotated.

III.1
Discrete-path holonomy

To perform the holonomic evolution, one can choose between measuring either the instantaneous code space projector or the instantaneous stabilizer generators. These measurements are not the same, but they both produce the desired holonomy. Moreover, in principle it suffices to measure only one stabilizer generator.

Proposition 2
.

Adiabatically measuring one rotated stabilizer that anticommutes with
X
evolves the code space along the holonomic path
(V,\phi)
.

Proof.

Suppose
\langle S(0)\rangle_{1}=\{g_{1},g_{2},...g_{n-k}\}
is a generator set for the stabilizer group of the unrotated code such that a subset of the generators
S_{ac}(0)=\{g_{1}...g_{l}\}
anticommute with
X
(
S_{c}(0)=\{g_{l+1}...g_{n-k}\}
commute with
X
). It is easy to see that every odd (even) product of generators from
S_{ac}(0)
anticommutes (commutes) with
X
. This lets us choose a different generator set such that only one of the generators anticommutes with
X
, i.e.,
\langle S(0)\rangle_{2}=\{g_{1},g_{1}g_{2},g_{1}g_{3},g_{1}g_{l},g_{l+1}...g_{n-k}\}
. The projector onto the simultaneous
+1
eigenspace of the rotated stabilizers
\langle S(\phi)\rangle_{1}
is

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}(\phi)=&\left(\frac{I+V(\phi)g_{1}V^{\dagger}(\phi)}{2}\right)...\left(\frac{I+V(\phi)g_{l}V^{\dagger}(\phi)}{2}\right).\\
&\left(\frac{I+g_{l+1}}{2}\right)...\left(\frac{I+g_{n-k}}{2}\right),\end{split}

(39)

where we used the fact that
[V(\phi),g_{i}]=0
for
i=l+1...n-k
. Rearranging the terms above, we obtain

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}(\phi)=&\left(\frac{I+V(\phi)g_{1}V^{\dagger}(\phi)}{2}\right).\left(\frac{I+g_{1}g_{2}}{2}\right)...\left(\frac{I+g_{1}g_{l}}{2}\right)\\
&\left(\frac{I+g_{l+1}}{2}\right)...\left(\frac{I+g_{n-k}}{2}\right),\end{split}

(40)

which is the projector on to the simultaneous
+1
eigenspace of
\langle S(\phi)\rangle_{2}
.
∎

The ability to obtain the desired holonomy by just measuring a single rotating stabilizer offers interesting flexibility for practical implementation. However, we can also choose to measure all the stabilizers and use the syndrome information to do real-time quantum error correction. In this work, we show a way to correct measurement-induced errors. Similar techniques can be used to correct any correctable error.

Suppose that we measure the code space projector. It is rotated using the unitary
V(\phi)
in increments of
\delta\phi
. After every rotation, the new projector is measured; the state (up to a normalization factor) is multiplied by the rotated projector. Starting from the code state, we want to rotate the code space projector slowly enough so that the state always stays in the
+1
eigenspace; this state can then be parameterized in terms of
\phi
as the
instantaneous code state

\ket{\bar{\psi}(\phi)}
. If, on the other hand, the increment
\delta\phi
is not small enough, a projective measurement of
\mathbb{P}(\phi+\delta\phi)
can take the state
\ket{\bar{\psi}(\phi)}
to the nullspace of
\mathbb{P}(\phi+\delta\phi)
. In other words, there is a non-zero probability for the code state to “jump” into the error space.

Define
p_{\mathrm{jump}}
as the probability of a jump at any time during the procedure. For a given precision of
p_{\mathrm{jump}}
, we can find the rotation increment
\delta\phi
and hence the total number of measurements needed to complete the procedure and apply the logical gate. Observe that
1-p_{\mathrm{jump}}
is the probability that the measurements always return
+1
outcomes, in which case the unnormalized final state (denoted by
\ket{\tilde{\psi}(2\pi)}
) is obtained by multiplying the original code state by the sequence of projectors:

\begin{split}\ket{\tilde{\psi}(2\pi)}&=\mathbb{P}(2\pi)\mathbb{P}(2\pi-\delta\phi)\cdots\mathbb{P}(\delta\phi)\mathbb{P}_{0}\ket{\bar{\psi}}\\
&=[V(2\pi)\mathbb{P}_{0}V^{\dagger}(2\pi)]\cdots[V(\delta\phi)\mathbb{P}_{0}V^{\dagger}(\delta\phi)]\mathbb{P}_{0}\ket{\bar{\psi}}\\
&=G\prod_{l=1}^{\left\lceil 2\pi/\delta\phi\right\rceil}\mathbb{P}_{0}V^{\dagger}\big(l\delta\phi\big)V\big((l-1)\delta\phi\big)\mathbb{P}_{0}\ket{\bar{\psi}}.\end{split}

(41)

The normalization constant is proportional to
1-p_{\mathrm{jump}}
.
Eq.

41
contains a product of operators of the form
\mathbb{P}_{0}V^{\dagger}(\phi)V(\phi-\delta\phi)\mathbb{P}_{0}
. Using
Eq.

34
, we find that this is proportional to the projection onto the original code space, followed by a rotation about
H
. In particular, we have the following lemma (which we will use later):

Lemma 1
.

For a small rotation angle
\delta\phi
,

\mathbb{P}_{0}V^{\dagger}(\varphi)V(\varphi-\delta\phi)\mathbb{P}_{0}=c_{\varphi}e^{-i\xi_{\varphi}H}\mathbb{P}_{0},

(42)

where

\displaystyle\xi_{\varphi}

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\cos(2\varphi)\delta\phi+\mathcal{O}(\delta\phi^{2})

(43a)

\displaystyle c_{\varphi}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{\delta\phi^{2}}{2}\left(1+\frac{\theta^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}\sin^{2}(2\varphi)\right)+\mathcal{O}(\delta\phi^{3}).

(43b)

Proof.

See
Appendix

A
.
∎

To find the normalized state, we multiply (from the left) the terms
\mathbb{P}_{0}V^{\dagger}(\varphi)V(\varphi-\delta\phi)\mathbb{P}_{0}
with
\varphi=0\cdots 2\pi
. Note that the normalization constant
c_{\varphi}
becomes irrelevant since the state after the measurement is normalized— the final state depends only on the phases
\xi_{\varphi}
:

\ket{\bar{\psi}(2\pi)}=G\exp\left(\sum_{l=1}^{\left\lceil 2\pi/\delta\phi\right\rceil{}}\xi_{l\delta\phi}H\right)\ket{\bar{\psi}}.

(44)

With elementary arithmetic, one can verify that

\sum_{l=1}^{\left\lceil 2\pi/\delta\phi\right\rceil{}}\xi_{l\delta\phi}=0,

(45)

so that the final code state is indeed
G\ket{\bar{\psi}}
. We have the following theorem for the success probability:

Theorem 1
.

Starting with a system in a code state, by measuring the code space projectors, rotated by small increments of
\delta\phi
, the probability that the state stays in the rotated code space throughout the loop is

1-p_{\mathrm{jump}}=\exp\left[-\frac{\delta\phi}{2\pi}\left(\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}+4\pi^{2}\right)\right]+\mathcal{O}(\delta\phi^{2}).

(46)

Proof.

The probability that the state undergoes no jump during the holonomic procedure is equal to the square of the normalization factor in
\ket{\tilde{\psi}(2\pi)}
. Using
Lemma

1
, we obtain

\begin{split}1-p_{\mathrm{jump}}&=\left(\prod_{l=1}^{\left\lceil 2\pi/\delta\phi\right\rceil}c_{l\delta\phi}\right)^{2}\\
&=1-\frac{\delta\phi}{2\pi}\left(\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}+4\pi^{2}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\delta\phi^{2})\\
&\approx\exp\left[-\frac{\delta\phi}{2\pi}\left(\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}+4\pi^{2}\right)\right].\end{split}

(47)

∎

.

Suppose we have the

\mathcal{C}=\mathrm{span}\left\{\left(\ket{000},\ket{111}\right)\right\}
, and the stabilizer generators

(48)

But the other generator

It is easy to verify that this operator still squares to the identity and has eigenvalues

\ket{\bar{\psi}}

.

Suppose during the procedure, measuring the projector

III.2
Implicit fault-tolerance

Figure 2:
Jump into the rotated error space. When the code space is not rotated adiabatically, the state can jump into an error space defined by the operator
X
. The error state can potentially include a logical error.

During the holonomic evolution, if the stabilizer generators are not rotated slowly enough, the state can erroneously jump to the
rotated
error space as illustrated in
Fig.

2
. We find that the
error state
following the jump contains a logical error. Using this information, we can correct the logical error and still achieve the desired holonomy by dynamically modifying the path. In this work, we do not consider the effects of external noise, i.e., the errors arise solely from measurement-induced jumps. For simplicity, we present a method for constructing a correction path that can correct an error due to a single jump. This construction can, in principle, be generalized to accommodate an arbitrary number of jumps. To begin, we first determine the rotated code state immediately before the jump:

Proposition 3
.

The instantaneous code state with the holonomic path
(V,\phi)
is

\ket{\bar{\psi}(\phi)}=V(\phi)\exp\left(-i\frac{\theta}{4\pi}\sin(2\phi)H\right)\ket{\bar{\psi}}.

(50)

Proof.

If the measurements of the instantaneous code space projectors always returned the outcomes
+1
, then the state always stays in their
+1
-eigenspaces. The curve on
\mathcal{B}
can be written as

\mathbb{P}(\phi)=V(\phi)\mathbb{P}_{0}V^{\dagger}(\phi),

(51)

and the associated curve on
\mathcal{F}
can be found by solving the differential equation
Eq.

26
:

\frac{\mathrm{d}h(\phi)}{\mathrm{d}\phi}=-L^{\dagger}(0)\left(V^{\dagger}(\phi)\frac{\mathrm{d}V(\phi)}{\mathrm{d}\phi}\right)L(0)h(\phi).

(52)

Substituting
Eq.

34
in
Eq.

52
, we obtain

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}h(\phi)}{\mathrm{d}\phi}&=-iL^{\dagger}(0)\left(\frac{\theta}{2\pi}He^{i2\phi X}+X\right)L(0)h(\phi)\\
&=-i\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\cos(2\phi)L^{\dagger}(0)HL(0)h(\phi)\\
&\quad+\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\sin(2\phi)L^{\dagger}(0)HXL(0)h(\phi)\\
&\quad-iL^{\dagger}(0)XL(0)h(\phi).\end{split}

(53)

The columns of
L(0)
represent the basis states of the original code space, i.e., they are the code words. Since
X
anticommutes with a stabilizer, we can treat
X
as an “error” operator. Therefore,
XL(0)
and
HXL(0)
are the basis states of the orthogonal subspace, making
L^{\dagger}(0)XL(0)=L^{\dagger}(0)HXL(0)=0
. This simplifies
Eq.

53
into

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}h(\phi)}{\mathrm{d}\phi}&=-i\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\cos(2\phi)L^{\dagger}(0)HL(0)h(\phi)\\
&=-iF(\phi)h(\phi),\end{split}

(54)

where
F(\phi)\equiv\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\cos(2\phi)L^{\dagger}(0)HL(0)
. Since
[F(\phi),F(\phi^{\prime})]=0\;\forall\phi,\phi^{\prime}
, the solution to
Eq.

54
is simplified from a time-ordered exponential to a regular exponential:

\begin{split}h(\phi)&=\exp\left(-i\frac{\theta}{2\pi}L^{\dagger}(0)HL(0)\int_{\phi^{\prime}=0}^{\phi}\cos(2\phi^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\phi^{\prime}\right)h(0)\\
&=\exp\left(-i\frac{\theta}{4\pi}\sin(2\phi)L^{\dagger}(0)HL(0)\right)h(0).\end{split}

(55)

Without loss of generality, we can assume that the initial horizontal lift is trivial:

h(0)=I_{2^{k}}.

(56)

Since
L^{\dagger}(0)L(0)=I_{2^{k}}
, we have

L^{\dagger}(0)e^{A}L(0)=\exp[L^{\dagger}(0)AL(0)]

(57)

for any operator
A
. Then

h(\phi)=L^{\dagger}(0)\exp\left(-i\frac{\theta}{4\pi}\sin(2\phi)H\right)L(0),

(58)

and the curve
L(\phi)
can be written as

L(\phi)=V(\phi)\exp\left(-i\frac{\theta}{4\pi}\sin(2\phi)H\right)L(0),

(59)

where we used the fact that
L(0)L^{\dagger}(0)=\mathbb{P}_{0}
commutes with
H
. Using
Eq.

22
, the state at
\phi
can be written as

\ket{\bar{\psi}(\phi)}=V(\phi)\exp\left(-i\frac{\theta}{4\pi}\sin(2\phi)H\right)\ket{\bar{\psi}}.

(60)

An alternate proof to this result is given in
Appendix

E
.
∎

This is the
+1
-eigenstate of the projector on to the rotated code space. When there is no jump (to the error space) through out the protocol, we obtain
\ket{\bar{\psi}(2\pi)}=G\ket{\bar{\psi}}
, and the code state would have undergone the desired logical gate. However, with probability
1-p_{\mathrm{jump}}
, the state undergoes a jump into the error space. In the absence of external noise, the system state could evolve into only one “error” space defined by the operator
X
with the projector

\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{err}}(\zeta)=V(\zeta)X\mathbb{P}_{0}XV^{\dagger}(\zeta).

(61)

Moreover, the transition from the instantaneous code state to the instantaneous error state follows a unitary dynamic:

Proposition 4
.

In the absence of external noise, the sole source of error arises from the non-adiabatic rotations of the stabilizer generators; the resulting phase-dependent error is

E(\zeta)=V(\zeta)Xe^{-i\chi(\zeta)H}V^{\dagger}(\zeta),

(62)

where

\chi(\zeta)=\mathrm{arctan}\left(\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\sin(2\zeta)\right).

(63)

Proof.

Suppose that the rotation at
\zeta
with
0\leq\zeta\leq 2\pi
resulted in an error, thereby projecting the state into the error space
V(\zeta)(I-\mathbb{P}_{0})V^{\dagger}(\zeta)
. The instantaneous error state can be found by multiplying the original code state through the sequence of rotated projectors until
\zeta-\delta\phi
, followed by the orthogonal complement at
\zeta
:

\begin{split}\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{err}}(\zeta)}&=(I-\mathbb{P}(\zeta))\cdot\mathbb{P}(\zeta-\delta\phi)\cdots\mathbb{P}_{0}\ket{\bar{\psi}}\\
&=[V(\zeta)(I-\mathbb{P}_{0})V^{\dagger}(\zeta)]\cdots[V(\delta\phi)\mathbb{P}_{0}V^{\dagger}(\delta\phi)]\ket{\bar{\psi}}.\end{split}

(64)

Until
\zeta-\delta\phi
, the state undergoes an error-free evolution and result in
Eq.

50
. At
\zeta
, the instantaneous code state
\ket{\bar{\psi}(\zeta-\delta\phi)}
is multiplied by

\displaystyle(I-\mathbb{P}_{0})V^{\dagger}(\varphi)V(\varphi-\delta\phi)\mathbb{P}_{0}=

\displaystyle\quad\qquad\sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\delta\phi\right)\sin(2\zeta-\delta\phi)HX\mathbb{P}_{0}

(65a)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad-i\cos\left(\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\delta\phi\right)\sin(\delta\phi)X\mathbb{P}_{0}

(65b)

\displaystyle\quad\equiv d(\zeta)e^{i\xi(\zeta)H}X\mathbb{P}_{0}.

(65c)

The normalizing constant is given by

\begin{split}d(\zeta)&=\left[\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\delta\phi\right)\sin^{2}(2\zeta-\delta\phi)+\right.\\
&\left.\qquad\qquad\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\delta\phi\right)\sin^{2}(\delta\phi)\right]^{1/2},\end{split}

(66)

and the phase
\xi(\zeta)
follows

\begin{split}\tan(\xi(\zeta))&=\frac{\sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\delta\phi\right)\sin(2\zeta-\delta\phi)}{\cos\left(\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\delta\phi\right)\sin(\delta\phi)}\\
&=\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\sin(2\zeta-\delta\phi)+\mathcal{O}(\delta\phi^{2}).\end{split}

(67)

The evolved state in the error space at
\zeta
is

\begin{split}\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{err}}(\zeta)}&=V(\zeta)X\exp\left(-i\arctan\left[\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\sin(2\zeta-\delta\phi)\right]H\right)\\
&\qquad\qquad\exp\left(i\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\delta\phi H\right)\exp\left(-i\frac{\theta}{4\pi}\sin(2\zeta)H\right)\ket{\bar{\psi}}\\
&=\left[V(\zeta)\exp(i\chi(\zeta)H)XV^{\dagger}(\zeta)\right]\ket{\psi(\zeta)}+\mathcal{O}(\delta\phi),\end{split}

(68)

with
\chi(\zeta)\equiv\arctan\left(\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\sin(2\zeta)\right)
under the limit
\delta\phi\rightarrow 0
. Then we have the following error operator at
\zeta
:

E(\zeta)=V(\zeta)\exp(i\chi(\zeta)H)XV^{\dagger}(\zeta).

(69)

While we used the projector
I-\mathbb{P}(\zeta)
when there is a jump at
\zeta
to denote the orthogonal subspace, the projector can also be obtained from
Eq.

61
. One can verify that the obtained error state will be the same.
∎

Assume the error has occurred at
\phi=\zeta
. Then the state in the error space is

\begin{split}\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{err}}(\zeta)}&=E(\zeta)\ket{\bar{\psi}(\zeta)}\\
&=V(\zeta)\exp\left(i\theta_{\mathrm{err}}(\zeta)H\right))X\ket{\bar{\psi}},\end{split}

(70)

where the erroneous rotation angle
\theta_{\mathrm{err}}(\phi)
is defined as

\theta_{\mathrm{err}}(\phi)\equiv\frac{\theta}{4\pi}\sin(2\phi)+\chi(\phi).

(71)

The measurement-induced jump introduces a “fault” through a two-step process: an application of
X
, followed by a rotation of the code state about
H
by
\theta_{\mathrm{err}}(\zeta)
, introducing a logical error. This fault is a direct consequence of non-adiabatic rotation of the code space; achieving fault-tolerance then requires suppressing the probability of these faults. While slow rotations can reduce this probability, they lengthen the protocol and may potentially diminish quantum advantage. Alternatively, since we know the state after the jump, we can perform error correction. This can be done by either reversing both the
X
operation and the logical error, or by correcting only the error while tracking the Pauli frame. In the latter case, subsequent computation proceeds within the error space. Since we are measuring the stabilizers, we can detect a jump by tracking the expectation of the stabilizer that anticommute with
X
— the event of flipping to
-1
corresponds to the jump. We can then correct this error simply by restarting the holonomic procedure when a jump occurs, but with a different path.

can be stored in a Pauli frame and the subsequent computation can be performed.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose the holonomic procedure along the path
(V,\zeta)
resulted in a measurement error. Define a family of unitary operators parameterized by
\phi
:

W(\phi)=\exp\left(-i\tilde{\theta}\frac{\phi}{2\pi}H\right)V(\phi+\zeta)V^{\dagger}(\zeta).

(72)

By modifying the path to
(W,7\pi/2-\zeta)
with

\tilde{\theta}=\frac{3\pi\theta-4\pi\theta_{\mathrm{err}}(\zeta)-\theta\sin(2\zeta)}{7\pi-2\zeta-\sin(2\zeta)},

(73)

and restarting the holonomic procedure, the resulting state is in the original code space with the desired holonomy. Alternatively, modifying the path to
(W,4\pi-\zeta)
with

\tilde{\theta}=\frac{12\pi\theta+4\pi\theta_{\mathrm{err}}(\zeta)+\theta\sin(2\zeta)}{8\pi-2\zeta+\sin(2\zeta)}

(74)

results in a state is in the original error space with the desired emulated holonomy:
\ket{\psi(4\pi-\zeta)}=XG\ket{\bar{\psi}}
.

Proof.

When a jump occurs at
\zeta
, the state is projected to a subspace with projector
\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{err}}(\zeta)
. Suppose the path is modified to
W(\phi)
. It is easy to see that
W(0)=I
, so at the initial rotation angle
after
the jump,
W(0)\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{err}}(\zeta)W^{\dagger}(0)=\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{err}}(\zeta)
. Moreover,
W(\phi+\delta\phi)W^{\dagger}(\phi)=I+\mathcal{O}(\delta\phi)
, i.e., the rotation is weak. We would like to run the procedure for at least
2\pi
so that the state
\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{err}}(\zeta)}
can be brought back to the desired code space
\mathbb{P}_{0}
(or error space
X\mathbb{P}_{0}X
) with a jump probability of at most
p_{\mathrm{jump}}
. We select to restart the procedure, i.e.,
\phi=0
, and run until
\phi=7\pi/2-\zeta
(
\phi=4\pi-\zeta
) to bring the state back to the code (error) space. At the final rotation angle, we find that

W(7\pi/2-\zeta)=-i\exp\left[i\left(\frac{7}{4}(\theta-\tilde{\theta})+\frac{\tilde{\theta}\zeta}{2\pi}\right)H\right]XV^{\dagger}(\zeta),

(75)

or

W(4\pi-\zeta)=\exp\left(-i\tilde{\theta}\left(\frac{4\pi-\zeta}{2\pi}\right)H\right)\exp\left(i2\theta H\right)V^{\dagger}(\zeta).

(76)

If the state is brought back to the error space, the unitary transformation is not strictly holonomy; it is an emulation of the effective holonomy in the error space.

Suppose the curve after the jump is represented by
L^{\prime}(\phi)
. From
Eq.

70
, we can write

L^{\prime}(0)=L_{\mathrm{err}}(\zeta)\equiv V(\zeta)X\exp\left(-i\theta_{\mathrm{err}}(\zeta)H\right)L(0).

(77)

Using the path
W(\phi)
, this curve can be written as

L^{\prime}(\phi)=W(\phi)L^{\prime}(0)h^{\prime}(\phi),

(78)

and the dynamics for the horizontal lift follows

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}h^{\prime}(\phi)}{\mathrm{d}\phi}&=-L^{\prime\dagger}(0)W^{\dagger}(\phi)\frac{\mathrm{d}W(\phi)}{\mathrm{d}\phi}L^{\prime}(0)h^{\prime}(\phi)\\
&=-L^{\dagger}(0)\exp(i\theta_{\mathrm{err}}(\zeta)H)X\left(\tilde{V}^{\dagger}(\phi)\frac{\mathrm{d}\tilde{V}(\phi)}{\mathrm{d}\phi}\right)X\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\exp(-i\theta_{\mathrm{err}}(\zeta)H)L(0)h^{\prime}(\phi),\end{split}

(79)

where

\tilde{V}(\phi)\equiv\exp\left(i\frac{1}{2\pi}(\theta\zeta+(\theta-\tilde{\theta})\phi)H\right)\exp\left(i(\zeta+\phi)X\right).

(80)

Using the fact that
L^{\dagger}(0)XL(0)=L^{\dagger}(0)HXL(0)=0
, this can be written as

\frac{\mathrm{d}h^{\prime}(\phi)}{\mathrm{d}\phi}=i\cos\left(2(\zeta+\phi)\right)\left(\frac{\theta-\tilde{\theta}}{2\pi}\right)L^{\dagger}(0)HL(0)h^{\prime}(\phi),

(81)

with a solution

h^{\prime}(\phi)=L^{\dagger}(0)\exp\left(-i\left(\frac{\theta-\tilde{\theta}}{4\pi}\right)\sin(2\zeta)H\right)L(0)h^{\prime}(0).

(82)

By assumption, we have
h^{\prime}(0)=I_{K}
, and the final point on the curve (if moving to code space) is given by

\begin{split}L^{\prime}(7\pi/2-\zeta)&=W(7\pi/2-\zeta)L^{\prime}(0)h^{\prime}(7\pi/2-\zeta)\\
&=-i\exp\left(i\left[\frac{7}{4}(\theta-\tilde{\theta})+\frac{\tilde{\theta}\zeta}{2\pi}-\theta_{\mathrm{err}}(\zeta)-\right.\right.\\
&\qquad\qquad\quad\quad\left.\left.\left(\frac{\theta-\tilde{\theta}}{4\pi}\right)\sin(2\zeta)\right]H\right)L(0)\end{split}

(83)

Comparing this with the target point on the curve
L^{\prime}(7\pi/2-\zeta)=e^{i\theta H}L(0)
, we have

\tilde{\theta}=\frac{3\pi\theta-4\pi\theta_{\mathrm{err}}(\zeta)-\theta\sin(2\zeta)}{7\pi-2\zeta-\sin(2\zeta)}.

(84)

On the other hand, if the state is to be moved to the error space, the final point on the curve is

\begin{split}L^{\prime}(4\pi-\zeta)&=W(4\pi-\zeta)L^{\prime}(0)h^{\prime}(\phi)\\
&=X\exp\left(i\left[\left(\frac{4\pi-\zeta}{2\pi}\right)\tilde{\theta}-2\theta-\theta_{\mathrm{err}}(\zeta)-\right.\right.\\
&\qquad\qquad\quad\quad\left.\left.\left(\frac{\theta-\tilde{\theta}}{4\pi}\right)\sin(2\zeta)\right]H\right)L(0)\end{split}

(85)

Comparing this with the target point on the curve
L^{\prime}(4\pi-\zeta)=Xe^{i\theta H}L(0)
, we have

\tilde{\theta}=\frac{12\pi\theta+4\pi\theta_{\mathrm{err}}(\zeta)+\theta\sin(2\zeta)}{8\pi-2\zeta+\sin(2\zeta)}.

(86)

∎

When a jump happened at
\zeta
, we have
2
degrees of freedom in path selection. Specifically, the holonomic path is modified to
(W,7\pi/2-\zeta)
(respectively,
(W,4\pi-\zeta)

X\mathbb{P}_{0}X
. Note that this technique to modify the path only accounts for a single jump. We can, in principle, repeat this to find the updated paths when there are multiple jumps. Then the state should reach the target without any faults. In this work, we do not consider the influence of external noise, which will otherwise impact the path operator.

III.3
Continuous-path holonomy

The holonomic procedure described in
Section

III.1
involves making projective measurements after every small rotation by
\delta\phi
. Although that should work in principle, it is hard (and somewhat unnecessary) to perform a sequence of strong projective measurements after every rotation. Since the rotation angle is small, we can perform continuous weak measurements of the stabilizers while also simultaneously rotating them. To do that, we define angular velocity

\omega=\frac{\mathrm{d}\phi}{\mathrm{d}t}

(87)

as the
constant
rate of rotating the code space. Then, we can re-parameterize
Eq.

\displaystyle\ket{\mathrm{d}\psi}=-\frac{\kappa}{2}\sum_{j}\big(

, at which point the code space would have been rotated through a loop and back to the original code space. The code state should have acquired the desired holonomy. In principle, this rotation rate should depend on the measurement rate: measurement has to be made faster than the code space is rotated so that the state lies in the
+1

cannot be made arbitrarily small for two reasons. First, for a given measurement strength, very slow rotations correspond to significantly long gate times, which may diminish the potential quantum advantage. Second, at asymptotic times, the likelihood of transitioning into an error space becomes certain. The latter point is not entirely obvious since one would expect measurements to evolve a state into one of the eigenstates of the observable, and terminate the evolution. In fact, this is true when the observable is constant; continuously measuring it for asymptotically long times should indeed yield an eigenstate. As a result, the variance of the observable

III.3.1
Jump detection

Suppose a set of stabilizer generators
\{g(t)\}
are continuously measured. The measurement current for one channel (corresponding to a single stabilizer) reads

i(t)=\mu+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\kappa}}\frac{\mathrm{d}W_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t},

(112)

where
\mu\equiv\langle g(t)\rangle_{\rho(t)}
. Since
g(t)
are Pauli operators rotated by a unitary, their eigenvalues are preserved; the two extreme values of its expectation are
\pm 1
. A jump corresponds to a change of
\mu
from
+1
to
-1
. This can be detected using a simple hypothesis test:

\left.\begin{array}[]{l}H_{+}:\mu=+1\\
H_{-}:\mu=-1\end{array}\right\}.

(113)

Suppose the output current is averaged over a succession of time-intervals of size
\Delta t
:

\bar{i}(t)=\frac{1}{\Delta t}\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}i(t)\mathrm{d}t.

(114)

The averaged current follows a Gaussian distribution with a variable mean
\mu
and constant variance
1/(4\kappa\Delta t)
. Define
y_{k}\equiv\bar{i}(t_{k})
to be the discretized output sample. Taking the log-likelihood ratio of all the all the output samples given the means
\pm 1
, we have

S_{n}=\ln\left(\frac{p\big(\{y_{1},\cdots,y_{n}\}|\mu=-1\big)}{p\big(\{y_{1},\cdots,y_{n}\}|\mu=+1\big)}\right).

(115)

Since
y_{k}
are samples from independent and identical Gaussian distributions,

S_{n}=-8\kappa\Delta t\sum_{k=1}^{n}y_{k}.

(116)

It is easy to see that
S_{n}
will have a
V
-shaped profile; whenever the state is in the code space, the true expectations are all
+1
, and
S_{n}
will have a negative drift. When the state jumps into the error space, the expectation flips to
-1
, and
S_{n}
will then have a positive drift. The instant when there is a change in the direction corresponds to
t_{\mathrm{err}}
. To detect this change, we define

m_{k}\equiv\min_{1\leq j\leq k}S_{j}.

(117)

Then the jump time is

t_{\mathrm{err}}=\min_{k}(S_{k}-m_{k})\geq h,

(118)

for some small threshold
h
used to mitigate false positives.

The exact error operator
E(t_{\mathrm{err}})

W(t)
can be found by reparameterizing
\phi
as
\omega t
in
Eq.

69
and
Eq.

Error correcting conditions

A quantum error correcting code is designed to correct any error from a
correctable error set

\mathcal{E}

\mathcal{E}

\mathcal{E}

Definition 2

(Correctable set)

Given a

. Equivalently,
\{H,XD\}=[X,XD]=0
, i.e., all logical operators within Hamming distance
d-1
(
d-2
for even
d
) of
X
commute with
X
and anticommute with
H
.
∎

A simple numerical search demonstrates that the conditions in
Theorem

4
can be satisfied for the Shor
[\![9,1,3]\!]
code, but not for the perfect
[\![5,1,3]\!]
code or the Steane
[\![7,1,3]\!]
code. In the next section, we present a method for augmenting these codes so that the conditions can be satisfied for any stabilizer code.

IV.1
Deficient syndromes

Consider the
[\![9,1,3]\!]
Shor code with a logical operator
H=\sigma_{1}^{z}\sigma_{4}^{z}\sigma_{7}^{z}
, and
X=\sigma_{1}^{x}\sigma_{4}^{x}\sigma_{7}^{x}
, so that
X
anticommutes with
H
, and with the stabilizer generators
\sigma_{1}^{z}\sigma_{2}^{z}
,
\sigma_{4}^{z}\sigma_{5}^{z}
and
\sigma_{7}^{z}\sigma_{8}^{z}
. A numerical search shows that
\mathbf{s}_{X}\neq\mathbf{s}_{D}\;\forall D\in\mathcal{E}^{(2)}
. Hence,

\text{d}(X,m)>2\;\forall m\in\mathcal{N}(S(0)),

(125)

where
\text{d}(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the Hamming distance, and
\mathcal{N}(\cdot)
is the normalizer group. With this choice of
H
,
X
and
\mathcal{E}
, we can satisfy the error-correcting conditions throughout the loop.

It is easy to verify that the syndromes of all pairs of errors include all syndromes for the
[\![5,1,3]\!]
and the
[\![7,1,3]\!]
codes; for any
X
, there will be choices of
E_{j}E_{i}
with the same syndrome, and no
X
satisfies all the required commutations and anti-commutation. For this construction, it is helpful for a code to have more “slack” to satisfy the conditions.

We can however, modify the codes by introducing ancilla qubits to always satisfy the error-correcting conditions. Consider the Steane
[\![7,1,3]\!]
code for example; we add to this code one ancilla qubit with stabilizer generator
Z
, i.e. the extra qubit is always in the state
\ket{0}.
Then the projector on to the code space becomes

\mathbb{\bar{P}}_{0}\equiv\mathbb{P}_{0}\otimes\ket{0}_{A}\bra{0}

(126)

where the subscript
A
stands for the ancilla. The bar stands for the updated code. We choose
\bar{H}=H\otimes I_{A}
and
\bar{X}=X\otimes X_{A}
. For instance we choose
H=Z^{\otimes 7}
and
X=XZIIIII
. Assume the correctable set contains all weight-
1
Pauli operators on the eight qubits. Note that this is fewer errors than this code can correct: it can correct any single error on the seven qubits, and any error on the ancilla. We claim that this construction satisfies
Theorem

4
based on two observations:

•

X
has a syndrome different from any weight-
1
error. So
X\otimes X_{A}
has a syndrome different from any
E_{b}E_{a}
and
\mathbb{\bar{P}}_{0}\bar{X}E_{b}E_{a}\mathbb{\bar{P}}_{0}=\mathbb{\bar{P}}_{0}H\bar{X}E_{b}E_{a}\mathbb{\bar{P}}_{0}=\mathbb{\bar{P}}_{0}HE_{b}E_{a}\mathbb{\bar{P}}_{0}=0
for any
E_{b},E_{a}\in\mathcal{E}
.

•

Since the code is non-degenerate,
\mathbb{\bar{P}}_{0}E_{b}E_{a}\mathbb{\bar{P}}_{0}=0
for all
b\neq a
. For
b=a
,
E_{a}E_{a}=I
which commutes with
H
and
X
.

Note that this construction fails for the
[\![5,1,3]\!]
code; since it is “perfect”, there is a weight-
1
error corresponding to every syndrome. So any choice
X
is distance
1
away from at least one stabilizer or a logical operator— for any
X\otimes X_{A}
there is a pair
E_{b}E_{a}
with the same syndrome. However, it can be made to work by using two ancilla qubits
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
, with any
H=H\otimes I_{A_{1}}\otimes A_{2}
. Choose
H=\otimes_{i=1}^{5}\sigma_{i}^{z}
,
X=X\otimes\sigma^{X}_{A_{1}}\otimes\sigma^{X}_{A_{2}}
,
X=\sigma^{x}_{1}
. Then no
E_{b}E_{a}
will have the same syndrome as
X
and the code is non-degenerate. Here, we transformed the
[\![5,1,3]\!]
code into a
[\![7,1,3]\!]
code by adding two extra stabilizers
\sigma_{6}^{z}
and
\sigma_{7}^{z}
to the original 4 stabilizers. In fact, we find that we need at most
2
ancilla qubits such that the error-correcting conditions are satisfied for any general stabilzer code. Let us denote
d_{\mathcal{S}}
as the number of distinct syndromes of the operators in the set
\mathcal{S}
. Then we have the following theorem for the number of ancilla qubits required:

Theorem 5
.

The number of ancilla qubits required to run the holonomic procedure satisfying the error-correcting conditions for an arbitrary stabilizer code is

s=\begin{cases}0\qquad\text{if}\;d_{\mathcal{E}^{(2)}}<2^{n-k},\\
1\qquad\text{if}\;d_{\mathcal{E}}<2^{n-k}<d_{\mathcal{E}^{(2)}},\\
2\qquad\text{else}.\end{cases}

(127)

Proof.

If
d_{\mathcal{E}^{(2)}}<2^{n-k}
, then there exists at least one choice for
X
such that
\mathbf{s}_{X}\neq\mathbf{s}_{D}\;\forall D\in\mathcal{E}^{(2)}
. Then it is easy to see that
\mathbb{P}_{0}HXD\mathbb{P}_{0}=\mathbb{P}_{0}XD\mathbb{P}_{0}=0
. Moreover,
\mathbb{P}_{0}D_{a}\mathbb{P}_{0}=\gamma_{a}\mathbb{P}_{0}
trivially, and
\mathbb{P}_{0}HD_{a}\mathbb{P}_{0}\propto\mathbb{P}_{0}
by
Proposition

7
, satisfying the error-correcting conditions; there is no need for additional ancilla qubits.

If
d_{\mathcal{E}}<2^{n-k}
, then there exists at least one choice for
X
such that
\mathbf{s}_{X}\neq\mathbf{s}_{E}\;\forall E\in\mathcal{E}
. Then with one ancilla qubit
A\in\mathcal{V}
initialized in the
\ket{0}
state, we have

\begin{split}\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{0}\bar{X}D\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{0}&=\left(\mathbb{P}_{0}\otimes\ket{0}_{A}\bra{0}\right)\left(X\otimes\sigma^{x}_{A}\right)D\left(\mathbb{P}_{0}\otimes\ket{0}_{A}\bra{0}\right)\\
&=\left(\mathbb{P}_{0}X\otimes\ket{0}_{A}\bra{1}\right)D\left(\mathbb{P}_{0}\otimes\ket{0}_{A}\bra{0}\right)\\
&=0.\end{split}

(128)

We can see that this equation is always zero based on the support of
D
. Define
\mathcal{D}
(
\mathcal{V}
) as the index set of all the data (ancilla) qubits.

•

D\in\mathcal{D}
, i.e.,
D
is fully supported by the data qubits. Then
\mathbb{P}_{0}XD\mathbb{P}_{0}\otimes\ket{0}_{A}\bra{1}0\rangle_{A}\bra{0}=0
.

•

D\in\mathcal{V}
, i.e,
D
is fully supported by the ancilla qubit. Then in
(\mathbb{P}_{0}X\mathbb{P}_{0})\otimes(\ket{0}_{A}\bra{1}D\ket{0}_{A}\bra{0})
, the right factor need not be
0
. But the left factor is
0
due to
Lemma

3
.

•

D=E_{b}E_{a}
with

and

(\mathbb{P}_{0}XE_{b}\mathbb{P}_{0})\otimes(\ket{0}_{A}\bra{1}E_{a}\ket{0}_{A}\bra{0})=0
since

Similarly,
\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{0}\bar{H}\bar{X}D\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{0}=0
, satisfying the error-correcting conditions.

Finally if

\begin{split}\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{0}\bar{X}D\bar{\mathbb{P}}_{0}&=\left(\mathbb{P}_{0}\otimes\ket{00}_{A_{1}A_{2}}\bra{00}\right)\left(X\otimes\sigma^{x}_{A_{1}}\otimes\sigma^{x}_{A_{2}}\right)D\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\left(\mathbb{P}_{0}\otimes\ket{00}_{A_{1}A_{2}}\bra{00}\right)\\
&=\left(\mathbb{P}_{0}X\otimes\ket{00}_{A_{1}A_{2}}\bra{11}\right)D\left(\mathbb{P}_{0}\otimes\ket{00}_{A_{1}A_{2}}\bra{00}\right)\\
&=0.\end{split}

(129)

Again, we can see that this equation is always zero based on the support of
D
:

•

D\in\mathcal{D}
, i.e.,
D
is fully supported by the data qubits. Then
(\mathbb{P}_{0}XD\mathbb{P}_{0})\otimes(\ket{00}_{A_{1}A_{2}}\bra{11}\ket{00}_{A_{1}A_{2}}\bra{11})=0
. Note that the left factor need not be proportional to
\mathbb{P}_{0}
. But the right factor is always
0
.

•

D\in\mathcal{V}
, i.e.,
D
is fully supported by the ancilla qubits. Then in
(\mathbb{P}_{0}X\mathbb{P}_{0})\otimes(\ket{00}_{A_{1}A_{2}}\bra{11}D\ket{00}_{A_{1}A_{2}}\bra{00})
, the right factor need not be
0
. But the left factor is
0
due to
Lemma

3
.

•

D=E_{b}E_{a}
with

and

(\mathbb{P}_{0}XE_{a}\mathbb{P}_{0})\otimes(\ket{00}_{A_{1}A_{2}}\bra{11}E_{b}\ket{00}_{A_{1}A_{2}}\bra{00})=0

∎

V
Discussion

In this paper, we have proposed a framework to generate discrete and continuous-path holonomies in arbitrary stabilizer codes by continuously measuring the rotated stabilizers. When the rotation forms a loop, the holonomy corresponds to a logical rotation by an arbitrary angle in the code space. Due to inherent stochasticity in quantum measurements, the code state may occasionally jump into the rotated error space if the rotation is not made adiabatically. We showed a method to change the path so that at the end of the evolution, we still achieve the desired holonomy; this provides a flexibility in choosing the gate times. In this work, we assumed that the evolution is not impacted by any external noise. In practice, however, the system constantly interacts with its environment, causing errors that can be either Markovian or non-Markovian. Since we continuously measure the stabilizers, the Zeno effect can suppress the non-Markovian noise
Hsu and Brun 2016
;
Convy et al. 2022
;
Ahn et al. 2002
;
Lanka et al. 2025
;
Nila and Brun 2025
;
Paz-Silva et al. 2012
;
Dominy et al. 2013
. To suppress Markovian noise, however, we would have to perform error-correction. As a goal for future work, we would like to find an analogous way to change the path that depends both on the correctable error and when it occurred so that the logical gate is successfully performed. This ensures full fault-tolerance of the protocol. We also gave sufficient conditions of the measurement observables so that the KL-condition is satisfied for all the rotated codes. When these conditions are not satisfied, we can still use this protocol with at most
2
ancilla qubits.

The measurement procedure described in this work may be physically realized by interacting the data qubits with an ancilla qubit using a time-dependent Hamiltonian, and measuring the ancilla qubit instead. For smaller codes, it may be possible to engineer this Hamiltonian interaction such that it is always pairwise local
Marvian et al. 2017
;
Chen and Brun 2020
;
Hung et al. 2016
;
Haas et al. 2019
. In this work, we assumed stabilizers codes with Pauli correctable sets for simplicity. We can, in principle, generalize this to arbitrary codes. For instance, we can consider codes defined on
qudit
-systems, but encoding logical qubits. They may be better suited in designing such Hamiltonian interactions.

Proof of
Lemma

1

Assume that the rotation increment
\delta\phi\ll 1
. Then the rotation operator
V^{\dagger}(\varphi)V(\varphi-\delta\phi)
is
weak
; sandwiching it between
\mathbb{P}_{0}
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}_{0}V^{\dagger}(\varphi)V(\varphi-\delta\phi)\mathbb{P}_{0}=\mathbb{P}_{0}e^{-i\varphi X}e^{-i\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\varphi H}e^{i\frac{\theta}{2\pi}(\varphi-\delta\phi)H}e^{i(\varphi-\delta\phi)X}\mathbb{P}_{0}.

(130)

Using that fact that
\mathbb{P}_{0}X\mathbb{P}_{0}=\mathbb{P}_{0}XH\mathbb{P}_{0}=0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{0}V^{\dagger}(\varphi)V(\varphi-\delta\phi)\mathbb{P}_{0}

\displaystyle=\cos\left(\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\delta\phi\right)\cos(\delta\phi)\mathbb{P}_{0}-i\sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\delta\phi\right)\cos(2\varphi-\delta\phi)H\mathbb{P}_{0}

(131a)

\displaystyle\quad\equiv c_{\varphi}e^{-i\xi_{\varphi}H}\mathbb{P}_{0}.

(131b)

The normalizing constant
c_{\varphi}
is given by

\begin{split}c_{\varphi}&=\left[\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\delta\phi\right)\cos^{2}(\delta\phi)+\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\delta\phi\right)\cos^{2}(2\varphi-\delta\phi)\right]^{1/2}\\
&=\left[1-\left(\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\delta\phi\right)^{2}(1-\delta\phi)^{2}+\left(\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\delta\phi\right)^{2}\cos^{2}(2\varphi-\delta\phi)\right]^{1/2}+\mathcal{O}(\delta\phi^{4})\\
&=\left[1-\delta\phi^{2}\left(1+\frac{\theta^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}\sin^{2}(2\varphi-\delta\phi)\right)\right]^{1/2}+\mathcal{O}(\delta\phi^{4})\\
&=\left[1-\delta\phi^{2}\left(1+\frac{\theta^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}\sin^{2}(2\varphi)\right)\right]^{1/2}+\mathcal{O}(\delta\phi^{3})\\
&=1-\frac{\delta\phi^{2}}{2}\left(1+\frac{\theta^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}\sin^{2}(2\varphi)\right)+\mathcal{O}(\delta\phi^{3}),\end{split}

(132)

and the angle
\xi_{\varphi}
follows

\begin{split}\tan(\xi_{\varphi})&=\frac{\sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\delta\phi\right)\cos(2\varphi-\delta\phi)}{\cos\left(\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\delta\phi\right)\cos(\delta\phi)}\\
&=\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\cos(2\varphi)\delta\phi+\mathcal{O}(\delta\phi^{2}),\end{split}

(133)

where we used the fact that
\xi_{\varphi}=\arctan(\xi_{\varphi})+\mathcal{O}(\delta\phi^{3})
.

Appendix B
Variance of a constant measurement observable is a supermartingale

The state dynamics under a continuous measurement of an observable
O
can be described by the stochastic Schrödinger equation:

\ket{\mathrm{d}\psi}=-\frac{\kappa}{2}\big(O-\langle O\rangle\big)^{2}\ket{\psi}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\kappa}\big(O-\langle O\rangle\big)\ket{\psi}\mathrm{d}W_{t}.

(134)

The quantum variance is defined as
\langle\Delta O^{2}\rangle\equiv\langle O^{2}\rangle-\langle O\rangle^{2}
, where the expectations are taken with respect to
\ket{\psi(t)}
. For simplicity, let us assume that
O
is a Pauli operator, but the conclusion should remain the same for any general Hermitian observable. By Differentiating
\langle\Delta O^{2}\rangle
, we have

\begin{split}\mathrm{d}\langle\Delta O^{2}\rangle&=\mathrm{d}(1-\bra{\psi}O\ket{\psi})\\
&=-(2\langle O\rangle\mathrm{d}\langle O\rangle+(\mathrm{d}\langle O\rangle)^{2}),\end{split}

(135)

where by the rules of Itô calculus,
\mathrm{d}\langle O\rangle=\bra{\mathrm{d}\psi}O\ket{\psi}+\bra{\psi}O\ket{\mathrm{d}\psi}+\bra{\mathrm{d}\psi}O\ket{\mathrm{d}\psi}
.
Using
Eq.

134
,

\mathrm{d}\langle\Delta O^{2}\rangle=-4\kappa\langle\Delta O^{2}\rangle^{2}\mathrm{d}t-4\kappa\langle\Delta O^{2}\rangle\langle O\rangle\mathrm{d}W_{t}.

(136)

For any
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-adapted integrand
A_{t}
satisfying
\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}A_{u}^{2}\mathrm{d}u\right]<\infty
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{s}\left[\int_{0}^{t}A_{u}\mathrm{d}W_{u}\right]=\int_{0}^{s}A_{u}\mathrm{d}W_{u}\quad(s\leq t),

(137)

where
\mathbb{E}_{t}[X]=\mathbb{E}[X|\mathcal{F}_{t}]
is the conditional expectation of
X
given filtration upto time
t
. Integrating
Eq.

136
,

\langle\Delta O^{2}\rangle(t)=\langle\Delta O^{2}\rangle(0)-4\kappa\int_{0}^{t}\langle\Delta O^{2}\rangle(u)\mathrm{d}u-4\kappa\int_{0}^{t}\langle\Delta O^{2}\rangle(u)\langle O\rangle(u)\mathrm{d}W_{u}.

(138)

Taking the conditional expectation on both sides,

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{s}\left[\langle\Delta O^{2}\rangle(t)\right]&=\langle\Delta O^{2}\rangle(s)-4\kappa\int_{s}^{t}\langle\Delta O^{2}\rangle(u)\mathrm{d}u\\
&\leq\langle\Delta O^{2}\rangle(s)\end{split}

(139)

for
s\leq t
, which shows that
\langle\Delta O^{2}\rangle(t)
is a supermartingale, i.e., a process that decreases on average.

Appendix C
Measuring
g(t)
yields a mixture

To see this, consider a simple
1
-qubit example: the original unrotated code is spanned by
\ket{0}
with the single stabilizer
g=\sigma^{z}
, and the rotation operator
X=\sigma^{x}
. Since the code space is
1
-dimensional, the holonomy reduces to the Berry phase, and the logical operator
H
becomes irrelevant. However, the salient features of this protocol can still be effectively captured. The rotated stabilizer is
g(t)=\cos(2\omega t)\sigma^{z}+\sin(2\omega t)\sigma^{x}
with the instantaneous eigenstates:

\displaystyle\ket{\phi_{+}(t)}

\displaystyle=\cos(\omega t)\ket{0}+\sin(\omega t)\ket{1}

(140a)

\displaystyle\ket{\phi_{-}(t)}

\displaystyle=-\sin(\omega t)\ket{0}+\cos(\omega t)\ket{1}.

(140b)

It is convenient to represent the dynamics in the rotating frame. To find that, observe that any state can be represented as

\ket{\psi(t)}=\alpha(t)\ket{\phi_{+}(t)}+\beta(t)\ket{\phi_{-}(t)}.

(141)

Under pure rotation by
V(\delta t)
, the state is evolved into

\ket{\psi(t+\delta t)}=\alpha(t+\delta t)\ket{\phi_{+}(t+\delta t)}+\beta(t+\delta t)\ket{\phi_{-}(t+\delta t)}.

(142)

Comparing equations
Eq.

141
and
Eq.

142
, we can write the difference equation for the coefficients as

\displaystyle\alpha(t+\delta t)

\displaystyle=\cos(\omega\delta t)\alpha(t)+\sin(\omega\delta t)\beta(t)

(143a)

\displaystyle\beta(t+\delta t)

\displaystyle=-\sin(\omega\delta t)\alpha(t)+\cos(\omega\delta t)\beta(t).

(143b)

Under the limit
\delta t\rightarrow 0
, this yields a differential equation for
Hamiltonian
evolution:

\mathrm{d}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\
\beta\end{pmatrix}=\omega\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\
\beta\end{pmatrix}\mathrm{d}t.

(144)

Along with continuous measurements by
g(t)
, this becomes

\begin{split}\mathrm{d}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\
\beta\end{pmatrix}=\omega\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}&\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\
\beta\end{pmatrix}\mathrm{d}t+\frac{\kappa}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\big(1-(\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2})\big)^{2}\\
\beta\big(1+(\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2})\big)^{2}\end{pmatrix}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\kappa}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\big(1-(\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2})\big)\\
-\beta\big(1+(\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2})\big)\end{pmatrix}\mathrm{d}W_{t}.\end{split}

(145)

Since
\alpha(t)
and
\beta(t)
are real and
\alpha(t)^{2}+\beta(t)^{2}=1
, we can redefine
\alpha(t)\equiv\cos(x(t))
and
\beta(t)\equiv\sin(x(t))
, yielding a
1
-dimensional stochastic differential equation for
x
:

\mathrm{d}x=-\bigg(\omega+\frac{\kappa}{2}\sin(4x)\bigg)\mathrm{d}t-\sqrt{\kappa}\sin(2x)\mathrm{d}W_{t}.

(146)

We can write the corresponding Fokker-Planck equation as

\frac{\partial p(x,t)}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial p(x,t)}{\partial x}\bigg[\big(\omega+\frac{\kappa}{2}\sin(4x)\big)p(x,t)\bigg]+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}\bigg[\kappa\sin^{2}(2x)p(x,t)\bigg],

(147)

where
p(x,t)
is a periodic probability density function with periodicity
\pi
. We can interpret
x(t)
as follows: at every even (odd) integer multiple of
\pi/2
, the system is in the code (error) space. Asymptotically, the system goes to equilibrium, and we can set
\frac{\partial p(x,t)}{\partial t}=0
. Then a solution to
Eq.

147
(up to renormalization) is

p(x)=\frac{2J}{\kappa}\frac{\int_{x_{0}}^{x}\exp\big[-\frac{\omega}{\kappa}\cot(2x^{\prime})\big]\sin(2x^{\prime})\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}}{\exp\big[-\frac{\omega}{\kappa}\cot(2x)\big]\sin^{3}(2x)},

(148)

where
x_{0}=x(0)
, and
J
is the normalization constant which can be found by setting
\int_{x}p(x)\mathrm{d}x=1
. For small
\omega
, this peaks at
every
integer multiple of
\pi/2
, i.e., there is an equal probability for measuring the state in both the syndrome spaces— hence a mixture.

Appendix E
Instantaneous code state

For simplicity, we assume that the evolution happens with projective measurements of the rotated code space, defined by the rotation operator
Eq.

34

When the state evolves as the

\begin{split}\ket{\psi_{\zeta}}&=\mathbb{P}(\zeta)\cdot\mathbb{P}(\zeta-\delta\phi)\cdots\mathbb{P}_{0}\ket{\bar{\psi}}\\

(151)

This is a product of factors of the form
\mathbb{P}_{0}V^{\dagger}(\varphi)V(\varphi-\delta\phi)\mathbb{P}_{0}

\varphi=0...\zeta

\sum_{l}\xi_{l\delta\phi}

(152)

Re-parameterizing the angle

\ket{\psi(t)}=V(t)\exp\left(-i\frac{\theta}{4\pi}\sin(2\omega t)H\right)\ket{\bar{\psi}}.

(153)
