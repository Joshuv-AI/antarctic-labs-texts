Prevalence of Temporomandibular Disorders in Obstructive Sleep Apnea Patients and Relationship Between Temporomandibular Disorder Phenotypes and Severity of Sleep Apnea: An Observational Study
Authors: Debjani Chakraborty, Jogeswar Barman, Sangita Nath, Rubaiya Rahman, Kripesh Ranjan Sarmah, Jishnu Nath
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a chronic condition characterized by intermittent upper airway obstruction during sleep. Temporomandibular disorders (TMDs), often linked with chronic pain and disturbed sleep, may coexist with OSA, but the association remains unclear.Aim:To assess the prevalence of TMD in patients diagnosed with OSA and evaluate the relationship between TMD phenotypes and severity of sleep apnea.Materials and Methods:This cross-sectional observational study was conducted among 70 OSA-diagnosed individuals using the ApneaLink Air device. TMD was assessed through RDC/TMD clinical criteria. Associations were evaluated with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.Results:TMD signs/symptoms were found in 8.6% of OSA patients, with myofascial pain being the predominant phenotype. No significant association was observed between TMD phenotype and OSA severity (P = 0.265).Conclusion:TMDs are present in a subset of OSA patients, predominantly as muscle disorders. However, the relationship between TMD phenotypes and OSA severity is inconclusive, necessitating further longitudinal research.

INTRODUCTION

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a prevalent sleep-related breathing disorder characterized by repeated upper airway obstructions during sleep. It affects nearly one billion individuals globally, particularly adults between 30 and 69 years of age. These episodes result in intermittent hypoxia, sleep fragmentation, and systemic health consequences, including cardiovascular and metabolic dysfunctions. Patients often experience poor sleep quality, excessive daytime sleepiness, and diminished quality of life.[1]

Temporomandibular disorders (TMDs) encompass a group of musculoskeletal and neuromuscular conditions involving the temporomandibular joint (TMJ), masticatory muscles, and associated structures. Globally, TMD prevalence ranges from 5% to 12% and is more common in females.[2] Both OSA and TMDs are associated with chronic pain and disturbed sleep, suggesting possible shared pathophysiological pathways such as inflammation, neuromuscular dysfunction, and central sensitization.[3]

The OPPERA study indicated that individuals with sleep disturbances, particularly those with symptoms of OSA, may be at increased risk for developing TMD.[4] Furthermore, oral appliances used in managing OSA, such as mandibular advancement devices, have been implicated in exacerbating or triggering TMD symptoms.[5] This underlines the necessity of evaluating both conditions concurrently, especially before initiating oral appliance therapy.

Despite emerging literature, there remains a gap in understanding the correlation between OSA severity and specific TMD phenotypes. Many prior studies have not comprehensively considered sociodemographic variables, which are critical in understanding disease burden and access to care. Moreover, data from North-Eastern India remain sparse, necessitating region-specific investigations.

This study was undertaken to explore the prevalence of TMD in patients diagnosed with OSA and to investigate the association between TMD phenotypes and OSA severity, while accounting for demographic variables. Such insights are crucial in formulating integrated diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for patients suffering from sleep-disordered breathing and orofacial pain.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This cross-sectional observational study was conducted from June 2020 to November 2021 following ethical approval (RDC/29/2011/1629). Seventy adult patients diagnosed with OSA using ApneaLink Air (ResMed) were included. After informed consent, each participant was evaluated for TMD using the RDC/TMD criteria by a calibrated examiner. TMD phenotypes identified included myofascial pain, myofascial pain with limited opening, and disk displacement. Demographic data were recorded. Based on apnea–hypopnea index (AHI), OSA was classified as mild (5 ≤ AHI < 15), moderate (15 ≤ AHI < 30), and severe (AHI ≥ 30). Statistical analyses included descriptive measures and inferential tests (Chi-square, Fisher’s exact test), with significance at P < 0.05.

DISCUSSION

This study identified an 8.6% prevalence of TMD among OSA patients, predominantly presenting as myofascial pain. Compared to other studies, this prevalence is lower. Cunali et al. reported a 52% TMD prevalence among mild-to-moderate OSA patients referred for oral appliance therapy.[6] Alessandri-Bonetti et al. found signs and symptoms of TMD in 51% of OSA patients.[7] However, our results are consistent with Petit et al., who found only 2% of OSA patients had TMD.[8] Kato et al. also reported a lower prevalence and suggested a negative association between AHI and jaw symptoms.[9]

The current study showed no significant correlation between TMD phenotype and OSA severity (P = 0.265), suggesting that factors beyond airway obstruction, such as pain modulation and neuromuscular control, may play a role.[10] Male predominance in the sample (88.6%) aligns with established literature on OSA demographics, likely due to hormonal and anatomical differences.[11]

Patients in the 40–55-year age group showed the highest OSA prevalence, consistent with Indian and global data. Additionally, the study revealed a higher number of urban, highly educated, and upper socioeconomic class patients. This might reflect health-seeking behavior and better access to diagnostic facilities in urban areas.[12] Importantly, RDC/TMD criteria used in this study ensure diagnostic accuracy and reliability.

Limitations include the small sample size, absence of a control group, and gender imbalance. Future multicentric longitudinal studies are warranted to better understand the causal link and prevalence across diverse populations.

CONCLUSION

In summary, this observational study revealed that 8.6% of OSA patients showed signs and symptoms of TMD, primarily muscle disorders like myofascial pain. Although no statistically significant association was observed between TMD phenotype and OSA severity, the findings emphasize the need for clinicians to screen for TMD in OSA patients and vice versa. Demographic variables, such as age, gender, socioeconomic status, and urban residence, also appear to influence OSA prevalence. The study supports integrated, multidisciplinary evaluation and management of sleep and orofacial disorders, highlighting the necessity for further large-scale research.
