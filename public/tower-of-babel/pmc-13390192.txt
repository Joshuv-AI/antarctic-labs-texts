A sequential dual-drug delivery system for multi-target inhibition of subretinal fibrosis
Authors: Yu Zhang, Wei He, Cunzhi Wang, Baiheng Liu, Jiayao Wen, Yuqing Zhao, Yifei Zhang, Lijun Liu, Qiu Yang, Jiaxin Li, Yuhan Dong, Xiaofan Zhang, Qian Xu, Guangxi Zhai, Lei Ye, Yi Qu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSubretinal fibrosis (SRF), a severe vision-threatening complication secondary to neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD), remains a major clinical challenge due to its complex pathogenesis and prolonged disease course. The fibrotic process is driven by multiple synergistic factors, including chronic inflammation, hypoxia, epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) of retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells, and pathological neovascularization, which collectively lead to the ineffectiveness of conventional anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) monotherapies in halting disease progression.ResultsThis study developed a sequential dual-drug delivery system based on the terpolymer P(NIPAM-co-AA-co-HEMA) hydrogel (NAHGel), designed to achieve multi-targeted regulation from the pathological microenvironment to downstream fibrotic processes. NAHGel exhibits excellent thermal responsiveness, biomechanical stability, and biodegradability, and is co-loaded with matrine (MT) and cRGD-modified sorafenib liposomes (SF/cLP). The hydrogel undergoes thermal contraction to enable the rapid release of hydrophilic MT, exerting anti-inflammatory and anti-hypoxic effects. As the hydrogel progressively degrades, SF/cLP is released in a delayed manner and, under cRGD mediation, is targeted to RPE cells and vascular endothelial cells. This enables modulation of key pathways including TGF-β2/MEK1/2/ERK1/2 and HIF-α/VEGF/PDGF, thereby inhibiting EMT and endothelial proliferation. In a laser-induced SRF mouse model, MT + SF/cLP@NAHGel formed a stable in situ gel within 24 h after intravitreal injection and remained structurally intact in the dynamic vitreous environment, enabling sustained retention for up to 8 months. Imaging and histological analyses demonstrated that, compared with aflibercept and other group, this system significantly reduced choroidal neovascularization and fibrotic lesion areas. Transcriptomic analysis further confirmed its broad inhibition of signaling pathways related to inflammation, angiogenesis, and fibrosis, with marked downregulation of key mediators including VEGFA, TGF-β2, TNF-α, and HIF-1α.ConclusionMT + SF/cLP@NAHGel achieves sequential drug release and multi-targeted synergy to intervene in key pathological processes such as inflammation, hypoxia, EMT, and pathological neovascularization, thereby effectively halting SRF progression. Combining sustained therapeutic efficacy with excellent biosafety, this system holds strong translational potential as a long-acting treatment strategy for SRF and other multifactorial retinal diseases.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04390-6.

Introduction

Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is a leading cause of vision impairment worldwide, characterized by progressive degeneration of the retina–choroid complex, with a global prevalence of up to 8.69% among individuals aged 45 to 85 years [1]. Clinically, AMD is classified into dry and neovascular subtypes, with neovascular AMD (nAMD), driven by abnormal proliferation of choroidal neovascularization (CNV), accounting for 80–90% of vision loss in AMD patients [2]. Subretinal fibrosis (SRF), a terminal complication of nAMD, arises from the fibrotic transformation of CNV lesions, resulting in irreversible macular structural damage and permanent central vision loss [3]. Although anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) therapy effectively inhibits CNV and remains the standard treatment for nAMD, the incidence of SRF in patients receiving prolonged administration (≥ 5 years) increases significantly from a baseline of 13% to 56% [4]. This indicates that anti-VEGF therapy can control vascular leakage and hemorrhage, but it fails to halt the progression of SRF. Therefore, there is an urgent clinical need to develop effective therapeutic strategies targeting SRF secondary to nAMD.

Studies on SRF have demonstrated that, during the early stages of nAMD, drusen and lipid accumulation disrupts Bruch’s membrane, creating a chronically hypoxic and inflammatory retinal–choroidal microenvironment that serves as a key trigger in the SRF cascade [5]. Within this pathological microenvironment, retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells subjected to oxidative stress not only enhance local inflammation through the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-1β (IL-1β), thereby promoting CNV, but more importantly activate the TGF-β2 signaling pathway to induce epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) [6, 7]. This process results in the loss of cell polarity and the acquisition of a fibroblast-like phenotype, directly contributing to subretinal matrix deposition and scar tissue remodeling [8]. Meanwhile, hypoxic stimulation of vascular endothelial cells leads to significant upregulation of hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) expression, which further induces the secretion of angiogenic factors such as VEGF and platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) [9]. These factors not only accelerate pathological neovascularization but also drive fibrotic remodeling. Together, this pathological cascade undermines the long-term efficacy of anti-VEGF monotherapy and ultimately drives the development of SRF [10]. Therefore, therapeutic strategies for SRF secondary to nAMD should move beyond the inhibition of neovascularization alone and adopt a multi-targeted approach that addresses the pathological hypoxia–inflammation microenvironment, EMT of RPE cells under oxidative stress, and aberrant angiogenesis.

Among therapeutic candidates targeting these core pathogenic mechanisms, sorafenib (SF), a multi-target tyrosine kinase inhibitor, effectively suppresses VEGF/PDGF-mediated angiogenesis and modulates TGF-β-driven EMT, thereby exhibiting both anti-angiogenic and anti-fibrotic effects [11–13].However, its therapeutic efficacy is markedly reduced in inflammatory and hypoxic conditions and may even induce drug resistance, as observed in hepatocellular carcinoma [14]. Furthermore, chronic inflammation and tissue hypoxia serve as major pathological drivers of aberrant neovascularization and EMT of RPE cells [7]. These limitations suggest that the effective application of SF in SRF treatment require prior optimization of the pathological microenvironment. Matrine (MT), a natural alkaloid, possesses strong anti-inflammatory and anti-hypoxic properties by regulating key signaling pathways such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and HIF-1α, and also demonstrates neuroprotective effects on the optic nerve [15–17]. Based on these properties, we propose a sequential dual-drug strategy in which MT is administered initially to improve the pathological microenvironment, followed by SF to specifically inhibit EMT of RPE cells and pathological angiogenesis, thereby achieving effective control of SRF.

However, the treatment of SRF and other fundus diseases faces major challenges due to the blood–retinal barrier, which significantly limits the bioavailability of systemically administered drugs [18]. Although intravitreal injection can increase local drug concentrations, the rapid metabolism and clearance of drugs within the eye result in low bioavailability [19]. Maintaining effective drug levels often requires frequent injections, which not only increases the risk of infection but also raises concerns about off-target effects of drug that may damage retinal tissues [20]. To address these challenges, various intraocular delivery systems, including nanoparticles, colloidal nanocarriers, and polymeric implants, have been applied to the treatment of CNV [21]. However, SRF typically develops 6 to 12 months after the onset of CNV and may persist for several years, this prolonged disease course imposes stricter requirements on drug delivery systems in terms of sustained release, in vivo stability, and long-term safety [22]. Conventional nanoparticles or colloidal carriers usually fail to meet these demands, and critically, they lack the ability to achieve spatiotemporal control over sequential drug release [23]. In contrast, injectable in situ-forming hydrogels rapidly undergo gelation after administration, forming a localized drug reservoir that markedly prolong intraocular retention and release, thereby reducing injection frequency and infection risk [24]. Notably, such hydrogels can co-encapsulate both hydrophilic and hydrophobic agents or nanocarriers, enabling sequential release through mechanisms such as diffusion and polymer network relaxation [25].

In this study, a thermosensitive hydrogel delivery system was developed using a macromolecular copolymer of N-isopropylacrylamide (NIPAM), acrylic acid (AA), and hydroxyethyl methacrylate (HEMA), forming P(NIPAM-co-AA-co-HEMA) hydrogel (NAHGel). NIPAM enables rapid in situ gelation at physiological temperature, HEMA enhances mechanical stability of the hydrogel network, and AA improves hydrophilicity and biodegradability, reducing the risk of in vivo accumulation.To achieve sequential release and multi-targeted therapy, MT and cRGD-modified SF liposomes (SF/cLP) were co-loaded into the hydrogel. Upon intravitreal injection, hydrophilic MT is rapidly released to alleviate inflammation and hypoxia in the retinal–choroidal microenvironment, followed by sustained release of SF/cLP, which targets RPE cells and endothelial cells to synergistically inhibit CNV and EMT. Ultimately, this system enables effective control of SRF through coordinated, multi-targeted regulation from the pathological microenvironment to downstream fibrotic processes (Scheme 1). Furthermore, this delivery system provides new prospects for managing complex ocular disorders including diabetic retinopathy, keratitis, and glaucoma.

Results

Synthesis and characterization of the NAHGel-based delivery system

As illustrated in Fig. 1A, the ternary copolymer P(NIPAM-co-AA-co-HEMA) was successfully synthesized via free-radical copolymerization of NIPAM, AA, and HEMA under nitrogen atmosphere (Fig. 1B). In the FTIR spectrum of P(NIPAM-co-AA-co-HEMA), the absorption peaks at 3200–3500 cm⁻¹ (O–H/N–H stretching vibration) and a composite carbonyl absorption peak at 1545–1750 cm⁻¹ (C = O/C = C stretching vibration), were observed due to the presence of PNIPAM, PAA and PHEMA (Figure S1A). The doublet peaks at 1385/1365 cm⁻¹ (-CH-(CH₃)₂) were attributed to the PNIPAM and a strong band at 1150–1250 cm⁻¹ (C–O–C stretching vibration) were attributed to the PHEMA. Notably, compared with P(NIPAM-co-AA-co-HEMA), Figure S1B shows the appearance of the vinyl group peak of NIPAM, AA and HEMA at ~ 1637 cm⁻¹ (C = C), confirming the consumption of double bonds during polymerization. Furthermore, the structure of NAH was additionally characterized using proton nuclear magnetic resonance (¹H NMR) spectroscopy. The characteristic signal of the methyl group (-CH₃, “e”) from PNIPAM appeared at 1.05 ppm, and the tertiary carbon peak of the isopropyl group on the PNIPAM backbone was observed at 3.84 ppm (-CH-(CH₃)₂, “d”). In addition, the peaks at 1.05 ppm (-CH₃, “j”) and 3.32 ppm (-CH₂-CH₂OH, “k, l”) were attributed to PHEMA (Fig. 1C). These results confirm the successful synthesis of the NAH copolymer.

Subsequently, three liposomal formulations were prepared: blank LP, cLP, and SF/cLP. From the particle size distribution results, the grafting of cRGD peptide increased the particle size of LP from 103.17 ± 4.14 nm to 118.9 ± 5.89 nm, while the encapsulation of SF slightly increased the particle size to 119.7 ± 5.89 nm (Fig. 1D, E and Figure S2). The transmission electron microscopy (TEM) results confirmed that SF/cLP exhibited a spherical morphology with an average particle size ranging between 110 and 150 nm (Fig. 1F), which was consistent with the measurements obtained using the Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZSE. The surface potential of SF/cLP was designed to be −29.8 mV through precise regulation (Figure S3). Furthermore, hemocompatibility evaluation revealed that both cLP and SF/cLP exhibited hemolysis rates of less than 2%, significantly lower than the control group (Figure S4). These findings demonstrate their excellent hemocompatibility and suitability for biomedical applications.

To evaluate the thermosensitive properties of the copolymer P(NIPAM-co-AA-co-HEMA), we examined the phase transition behavior of its aqueous solution under different temperature conditions. The results demonstrated that the polymer existed as a free-flowing liquid at room temperature (25 °C) but rapidly transformed into a non-flowing gel at physiological temperature (37 °C) (Fig. 1G). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis of the internal structure of the gel revealed a uniform porous network with pore diameters ranging from 5 to 10 μm (Fig. 1H). Rheological analysis demonstrated that at 37 °C, the storage modulus (G′) values of NAHGel exceeded the loss modulus (G″) across all tested frequencies (0.01–100 Hz), indicating excellent mechanical stability of the hydrogel (Fig. 1I). In vitro degradation studies revealed that NAHGel underwent complete degradation within approximately 13 days under dynamic conditions (Fig. 1J). Notably, even under conditions simulating the relatively static environment of human tissues, complete degradation was still achieved within approximately 20 days, demonstrating its excellent biodegradability (Figure S5A).

Controlled-release capacity was further examined via a Coumarin 6 (C6) fluorescent tracer assay of cellular uptake. By co-culturing the C6/cLP@NAHGel with cells, it was found that while the fluorescence intensity of free C6 and C6/cLP@NAHGel within RPE cells and vascular endothelial cells showed no significant difference at the 2-hour time point, but as time increased to 6 h, the intracellular fluorescence signals of the C6/cLP@NAHGel group were significantly enhanced (Figure S6). To further validate the sustained drug release efficacy of the NAHGel, comprehensive in vitro drug release kinetics experiments were conducted. The results showed that, compared to the free drug, SF/cLP@NAHGel extended the release duration to 12 days under agitation and further prolonged it to approximately 17 days under static conditions. In addition, regardless of whether under dynamic or static conditions, the hydrophilic drug MT, which is directly dispersed within the hydrogel matrix, exhibited a relatively rapid release rate, whereas the hydrophobic drug SF, encapsulated within liposomes, demonstrated a significantly slower release profile (Fig. 1K and Figure S5B). Collectively, these findings confirm that the cLP@NAHGel system possesses excellent spatiotemporal controlled-release capabilities, providing a foundation for long-acting and sequential therapeutic strategies for SRF.

Anti-inflammatory and anti-emt effects in vitro

To systematically evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of each treatment in modulating inflammation and EMT, an oxidative stress model was established in ARPE-19 cells using sodium iodate (NaIO₃) induction. Following NaIO₃ exposure, cells exhibited typical oxidative stress features, including increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, elevated malondialdehyde (MDA), and decreased superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity (Fig. 2A and C). CCK-8 assay results demonstrated that, within the safe concentration range, SF (5 µM) monotherapy exerted limited cytoprotective effects, whereas MT (100 µM) significantly promoted cell viability (Figure S7 and S8). The therapeutic efficacy was further enhanced by liposomal encapsulation (MT + SF/cLP), and was most pronounced with hydrogel-based delivery (MT + SF/cLP@NAHGel) without inducing cytotoxicity in normal cells (Figure S9). Functional assays consistently demonstrated that MT + SF effectively reduced ROS accumulation, lowered MDA levels, restored SOD activity, and suppressed TNF-α and IL-1β expression, with enhanced performance observed in the NAHGel delivery systems (Fig. 2E and F).

In terms of EMT modulation, RPE cells under oxidative stress exhibited enhanced migratory capacity and underwent a mesenchymal-like phenotypic transition (Fig. 2B and D), as evidenced by upregulated expression of Fibronectin and Vimentin, downregulated ZO-1, and activation of the TGF-β2/MEK1/2/ERK1/2 signaling pathway. SF monotherapy exhibited minimal ability to reverse EMT, whereas co-administration of MT significantly enhanced its anti-EMT efficacy. The MT + SF/cLP group exhibited further suppression of EMT markers, while the MT + SF/cLP@NAHGel group demonstrated the most potent inhibition, characterized by markedly reduced expression of TGF-β2, Fibronectin, and Vimentin, along with restoration of continuous ZO-1 distribution at intercellular junctions (Fig. 2G-K). Collectively, these findings highlight the superior combinatorial efficacy of MT + SF/cLP@NAHGel in alleviating oxidative stress, reducing inflammation, and inhibiting EMT progression in RPE cells.

Anti-angiogenesis in vitro

The anti-angiogenic efficacy of various treatment formulations was further assessed through a series of in vitro experiments. Wound healing assays revealed that SF monotherapy can inhibit the migration of human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), while the combined treatment with MT and SF exerted a more pronounced inhibitory effect. This inhibitory effect was further enhanced by cRGD-modified liposomal delivery (MT + SF/cLP), with the most substantial inhibition observed in the MT + SF/cLP@NAHGel group (Fig. 3A and E). Migration and invasion assays also confirmed that, compared with SF alone, the combination treatments especially when delivered via liposomes and NAHGel, more effectively suppressed endothelial cell migration and matrix penetration (Fig. 3B and F). Furthermore, tube formation assays demonstrated that MT + SF/cLP@NAHGel exerted the most potent inhibitory effect on the formation of capillary-like structures (Fig. 3C and G). To evaluate the effect on hypoxia-induced angiogenic factor expression, a CoCl₂-induced hypoxic HUVEC model was employed. Immunofluorescence staining, Western blot, and ELISA assays confirmed that MT + SF/cLP@NAHGel most effectively suppressed the expression of HIF-1α (Fig. 3D, H, I and J), accompanied by significant inhibition of VEGF and PDGF secretion (Fig. 3K). Additionally, to evaluate the influence of ARPE-19 cell-mediated inflammatory microenvironments on angiogenesis, a co-culture model was established using NaIO₃-induced ARPE-19 cells and HUVECs. The results demonstrated that compared with SF monotherapy, MT+SF/cLP@NAHGel markedly attenuated the pro-angiogenic effect mediated by ARPE-19 cells under oxidative stress (Figure S10).

Intraocular retention and penetration

Given the requirement for long-term retention of the delivery system constructed by MT + SF/cLP@NAHGel in vivo, this study implanted it into mouse eyes via intravitreal injection and systematically evaluated its structural stability, degradation profile, and drug delivery potential within the vitreous cavity. Fundus photography revealed that MT + SF/cLP@NAHGel rapidly formed a stable hydrogel structure in the vitreous cavity within 24 h post-injection. During the subsequent 8-month observation period, the hydrogel exhibited gradual degradation while maintaining structural integrity without significant deformation. By the eighth month, the system had been almost completely degraded, and fundus autofluorescence imaging further confirmed that there was no obvious abnormality in the retinal structure, except for a few scattered residual fragments in the vitreous cavity (Fig. 4A). Subsequently, the hydrophilic Rhodamine B (RhB) and the hydrophobic C6 were employed as fluorescent tracers to further evaluate the drug delivery performance of the system. The result revealed that RhB fluorescence was detectable in the retina on the first day following injection, while the fluorescence signal of C6/cLP appeared on day 3 and gradually intensified, spreading across the entire retina by day 7 (Fig. 4B). Regarding drug retention, free C6 was completely eliminated from the retina and choroid within 2 weeks after injection due to the rapid clearance mechanisms in the vitreous. Although lipid encapsulation can moderately prolong intraocular drug residence time, the retention of encapsulated C6 typically lasts around 2 weeks. In contrast, RhB incorporated into the hydrogel remained detectable for up to 3 weeks post-injection (Figure S11). Notably, C6/cLP@NAHGel exhibited persistent fluorescence signals in the retina for up to 8 months following a single intravitreal injection (Fig. 4C). These findings indicate that the NAHGel-based delivery system not only enables sequential drug release but also offers excellent sustained-release performance, significantly extending intraocular drug retention from several weeks to several months.

In vivo evaluation of therapeutic efficacy

A laser-induced SRF mouse model was established to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy and underlying mechanisms of MT + SF/cLP@NAHGel in vivo (Fig. 5A). At week 2, optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) revealed prominent blood flow signals in the control group, indicative of CNV lesions. Compared to AFL, the MT + SF demonstrated enhanced anti-angiogenic efficacy, while MT + SF/cLP@NAHGel achieved the most pronounced therapeutic effect, as indicated by the smallest CNV lesion areas observed on both En-face and B-scan OCTA images (Fig. 5B and F). By week 6, the therapeutic differences among groups became more distinct. In the control group, CNV lesions had progressed to subretinal fibrotic plaques with almost no detectable blood flow signals. AFL exhibited limited efficacy in halting fibrotic progression, whereas MT + SF and MT + SF/cLP moderately reduced fibrotic changes but still presented with visible fibrotic lesions. In contrast, the MT + SF/cLP@NAHGel group consistently maintained superior therapeutic performance, with the smallest fibrotic lesion area observed (Fig. 5C and G). Histological analyses further validated the imaging findings, as choroidal flat-mount immunofluorescence showed the lowest levels of α-SMA and CD31 staining, indicating reduced fibrosis and neovascularization (Fig. 5D and H), while hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining revealed minimal lesion formation in the MT + SF/cLP@NAHGel group (Fig. 5E and I).

Transcriptomic reprogramming and pathway suppression

Transcriptomic profiling was performed to systematically evaluate the pathological molecular alterations induced by the treatment. KEGG and GSEA enrichment analyses of differentially expressed genes revealed that multiple inflammation-related signaling pathways, such as TNF and Toll-like receptor pathways, were significantly suppressed in the treatment group. Additionally, several fibrosis-associated pathways, including TGF-β, PI3K-AKT, MAPK, and Hippo signaling, exhibited a consistent downward trend (Fig. 6A-D and Figure S12). Gene Ontology (GO) functional annotation further demonstrated significant enrichment of key biological processes in the treatment group, including extracellular matrix remodeling, inflammatory response, complement activation, and immune regulation. Moreover, GSEA analysis also revealed that gene sets associated with inflammatory cytokines, VEGF, and classical fibrotic markers such as collagen, fibronectin, and integrins were markedly less enriched in the treatment group (Fig. 6E-H and Figure S13). Notably, gene sets associated with macrophage activation were also significantly downregulated, and immune infiltration analysis revealed a substantial decrease in the proportions of M0, M1, and M2 macrophage subtypes compared to the control group (Fig. 6I and J). Immunofluorescence staining of tissue sections further confirmed a significant reduction in F4/80-positive macrophage signal intensity within the lesion areas of the MT + SF/cLP@NAHGel group. Consistently, differential gene expression analysis revealed that MMP12, a key fibrotic effector secreted by macrophages, was significantly downregulated in the treatment group, and protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis identified TNF as a central regulatory node (Fig. 6K and Figure S14).

Moreover, strong and widespread fluorescence signals of TNF-α, HIF-1α, VEGFA, and TGF-β2 were detected in the control group. Although AFL can reduce VEGFA expression, it had limited effects on TNF-α, HIF-1α, and TGF-β2, underscoring the limitations of anti-VEGF monotherapy in controlling fibrotic progression. In contrast, combined treatments with MT + SF and MT + SF/cLP synergistically downregulated all four pathological markers, with MT + SF/cLP@NAHGel exhibiting the most pronounced suppression (Fig. 7A, B and D). Choroidal flat-mount immunofluorescence co-staining showed that the area of Collagen-1 and RPE65 double-positive regions was significantly reduced in the MT + SF/cLP@NAHGel group compared to other groups, indicating effective inhibition of EMT in RPE cells (Fig. 7C and E). Western blot analysis further confirmed that the expression levels of fibrotic markers such as α-SMA, Collagen-1, and Vimentin were markedly reduced in the MT + SF/cLP@NAHGel-treated group (Fig. 7F and G). Collectively, these results support that MT + SF/cLP@NAHGel exhibits superior therapeutic potential in controlling SRF by synergistically inhibiting multiple pathological pathways.

Biosafety evaluation‌

To systematically evaluate the biocompatibility of the delivery system, a series of assessments were conducted to monitor both local and systemic safety over an extended period. Slit-lamp examinations revealed that throughout the 8-month observation period, the anterior segment structures of the treated mice, including the cornea, iris, and lens, remained clear and transparent, with no signs of inflammation, turbidity, or neovascularization. The hydrogel structure remained clearly visible within the vitreous cavity, indicating good intraocular stability (Fig. 8A). Further OCTA imaging at 8 months post-injection showed no significant differences between the treatment and control groups in terms of peripheral retinal morphology, vascular density, or the central retinal thickness within a 5 mm radius of the optic disc (Fig. 8B, C, F, and Figure S15).To investigate whether the long-term in vivo degradation of the hydrogel could cause visual impairment or intraocular pressure (IOP) fluctuations, flash electroretinography (FERG) was employed to assess retinal electrophysiological function, and IOP was measured at regular intervals. The results showed no significant differences in a- or b-wave amplitudes between the treatment and control groups, and IOP remained stable without notable fluctuations (Fig. 8D, G, and Figure S16). HE staining showed no structural abnormalities in key ocular tissues such as the cornea, ciliary body, retina, or optic nerve head, while TUNEL staining revealed no significant increase in apoptotic cells in the treatment group (Fig. 8E and H). Additionally, pathological examination of major organs including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, and brain showed no evidence of inflammatory cell infiltration or tissue damage (Figure S17). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that MT + SF/cLP@NAHGel exhibits excellent biocompatibility at both the ocular and systemic levels, ensuring a favorable safety profile throughout its long-term degradation period.

Discussion

SRF secondary to nAMD is a chronic, progressive, and vision-threatening condition for which no effective clinical strategy currently exists to halt or reverse disease progression [3]. In previous studies on nAMD, various therapeutic agents have been explored through different drug delivery systems including aflibercept (AFL), sirolimus, infliximab, and melatonin [26, 27]. However, the majority of these efforts have focused on reducing CNV size or leakage as their main therapeutic endpoints, with limited attention paid to the subsequent development of SRF. This study addresses the long-term, cascade-like progression of SRF and proposes a sequential, multi-targeted intervention strategy using the MT + SF/cLP@NAHGel system.

Although thermosensitive hydrogel-based drug delivery systems such as Pluronic F127, HA-PEG, and NIPAM have shown promising applications in ocular diseases including CNV, keratitis, and glaucoma, their application in long-term fibrotic conditions remains critically limited [19, 24]. Traditional hydrogels commonly exhibit inadequate mechanical strength and are prone to structural deformation in the dynamic vitreous environment, thereby compromising the stability of drug delivery [21]. Moreover, some hydrogels possess poor biodegradability, leading to prolonged intraocular accumulation, which raises safety concerns and limits their application in in long-term therapy [28]. To address these limitations, a novel thermosensitive terpolymer hydrogel, NAHGel, was developed by copolymerizing NIPAM, AA, and HEMA to systematically optimize the comprehensive properties. NIPAM endows the hydrogel with thermosensitive properties, enabling a sol–gel transition near body temperature and supporting long-term sustained drug release. The introduction of AA enhances the hydrophilicity and ionic responsiveness of the system, thereby promoting controllable in vivo degradation and improving biocompatibility. HEMA strengthens hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions within the network, significantly improving the mechanical stability and anti-shrinkage capacity of the hydrogel. In vitro experiments showed that NAHGel forms a stable three-dimensional network, exhibits favorable biodegradability, and enables a sustained drug release profile without significant fluctuations under both dynamic and static conditions.

Given the elevated expression of integrins αvβ3 and αvβ5 in RPE cells and choroidal endothelial cells of AMD patients, SF was encapsulated in cRGD-modified liposomes to enable simultaneous targeted delivery to both cell types while minimizing off-target effects [29, 30]. Moreover, negatively charged hyaluronic acid (HA) and glycosaminoglycans within the vitreous cavity can restrict the movement of positively charged particles through electrostatic interactions [31]. The liposomes were engineered to possess a slight negative charge to reduce aggregation in the vitreous, with particle sizes below 200 nm to facilitate inner limiting membrane penetration and cellular uptake [32]. In the sequential release strategy, experimental results further confirmed that the hydrophilic MT and hydrophobic SF/cLP could be co-encapsulated within the NAHGel without any additional processing. Due to their distinct physicochemical properties, the MT + SF/cLP@NAHGel system undergoes thermal contraction to promote the rapid initial release of MT, while the progressive degradation of the hydrogel subsequently allows for the delayed release of SF/cLP.

EMT of RPE cells is a key pathogenic mechanism driving the progression of SRF and represents a promising therapeutic target [8]. During the pathogenesis of fibrosis, oxidative stress–driven ROS accumulation in RPE cells markedly upregulates TGF-β2 expression and activates its downstream signaling pathways, thereby initiating the EMT [33]. Concurrently, ROS also induces the expression of proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β, which further amplify local inflammation and oxidative damage, collectively promoting the sustained progression of EMT [7]. In the NaIO₃-induced ARPE-19 cell model, SF exhibits limited anti-inflammatory activity, resulting in inadequate suppression of EMT when used alone. However, pretreatment with MT effectively alleviates oxidative stress in RPE cells and reduces the levels of ROS, TNF-α, and IL-1β. Building on this, liposomal co-delivery further enhances the inhibition of the TGF-β2/MEK1/2/ERK1/2 signaling cascade in RPE cells, leading to more efficient suppression of EMT. Similarly, although SF possesses inherent anti-angiogenic activity, its combination with MT and liposomal delivery further enhances its efficacy. This combination more effectively downregulates HIF-1α expression and alleviates cellular hypoxia, thereby significantly strengthening the inhibition of HUVEC migration, tube formation, and the expression of angiogenic factors such as VEGF and PDGF. Notably, PDGF is a dual pro-angiogenic and pro-fibrotic factor, and its downregulation may contribute to simultaneous inhibition of CNV and fibrosis [34]. By comparison, the MT + SF/cLP@NAHGel group improved drug delivery efficiency and stability via the hydrogel carrier, enabling sequential release after a single drug intervention. This strategy achieves sustained, multi-targeted therapeutic effects and exhibits more potent synergistic inhibition of both EMT and neovascularization.

Considering that the vitreous cavity represents a relatively enclosed and viscous microenvironment enriched with collagen and hyaluronic acid, which is markedly distinct from the large-volume buffer systems used under in vitro sink conditions, we further evaluated and monitored the morphological evolution of NAHGel in the intraocular environment [35]. Fundus imaging and histological analysis revealed that the system not only maintained its sequential release capacity but also exhibited significantly prolonged intraocular retention compared to conventional injectable hydrogels such as PF-127, HA-PEG, and gelatin-based formulations, which typically persist for only a few weeks. Moreover, the NAHGel maintained a stable gel morphology without noticeable deformation throughout the observation period and avoided the long-term residue issues commonly associated with PNIPAM-based hydrogels. Compared with the degradation and release period observed in vitro, the system exhibited a markedly prolonged retention time in the eye. This may be attributed to the relatively limited volume of the vitreous cavity and its low-shear microenvironment, which facilitates network entanglement and densification between the exogenous hydrogel and endogenous matrix, thereby forming a larger and more stable drug reservoir [36]. In addition, the slow fluid turnover within the vitreous cavity, along with the clearance of hydrogel degradation products and nanocarriers mainly relying on vitreous–aqueous humor flow or trans-retinal transport across the blood–retinal barrier, substantially reduces both material erosion and liposome diffusion rates [28, 37]. These factors collectively contribute to the significantly extended intraocular retention.

In vivo efficacy was evaluated using a laser-induced mouse model of SRF. This model is characterized by early disruption of Bruch’s membrane, which exposes choroidal capillary endothelial cells to the subretinal space, triggering a hypoxic and inflammatory microenvironment [38]. In the absence of therapeutic intervention, CNV lesions become apparent by week 1, and subsequently progress to SRF by week 5, which closely recapitulates the disease course of SRF secondary to CNV in humans [4, 39]. When evaluating the therapeutic effect of each group of drugs, OCTA was selected as the core assessment method. As the first-line standard for clinical diagnosis of SRF, this technology enables noninvasive monitoring of retinal structural alterations and the dynamic progression of CNV, while also allowing precise quantification of fibrotic lesion areas for efficacy evaluation [40]. The results showed that although the clinically approved anti-VEGF drug AFL could inhibit the development of CNV, its therapeutic efficacy against SRF remained limited. The combination of MT and SF (MT + SF) showed better anti-angiogenic effects than AFL and partially addressed the limitations of anti-VEGF therapy, but failed to achieve satisfactory anti-fibrotic outcomes. Even with the incorporation of cLP, the therapeutic outcome of the MT + SF/cLP formulation was only moderately enhanced, and significant residual fibrotic lesions were still observed. This limitation may be attributed to the rapid clearance of free-form drugs from the vitreous cavity, resulting in insufficient intraocular retention. Previous studies have demonstrated that one of the major mechanisms driving the progression from CNV to subretinal fibrosis SRF is the insufficiency of anti-VEGF therapy, which is primarily attributed to its limited ability to sustain long-term efficacy and its narrow therapeutic target profile [10]. In contrast, MT + SF/cLP@NAHGel markedly prolongs intraocular drug retention and enhances comprehensive intervention in multiple key fibrotic pathological processes through sequential release and multi-target modulation.

Mechanistically, RNA-sequencing analysis revealed that MT + SF/cLP@NAHGel downregulated a TNF-centered inflammatory cascade, along with multiple signaling pathways associated with fibrosis and angiogenesis, including TGF-β, PI3K-AKT, MAPK, and Hippo. Immunofluorescence staining showed that the expression levels of the key pathological targets VEGFA, TNF-α, HIF-1α, and TGF-β2 were significantly reduced in the MT + SF/cLP@NAHGel group, accompanied by effective inhibition of EMT in RPE cells. In contrast, AFL mainly suppressed VEGFA, with limited impact on the other markers. Additionally, macrophage infiltration was reduced, and MMP12, a fibrosis-associated macrophage effector, was downregulated. Previous studies have confirmed that SRF is accompanied by disruption of the outer RPE barrier, with blood-derived inflammatory macrophages recruited to the lesion site via chemokines such as CXCL3 [10, 41]. These macrophages secrete MMP12 to drive extracellular matrix remodeling and promote fibrosis progression [42]. These findings further demonstrate that MT + SF/cLP@NAHGel overcomes the inherent limitations of conventional anti-VEGF monotherapy by transitioning from single-target inhibition to coordinated multi-pathway modulation.

Given prior reports that PNIPAM-based hydrogels may pose risks in ocular applications such as optical opacity, potential cytotoxicity, and degradation-induced IOP elevation or retinal toxicity, and we implemented a cautious delivery strategy and conducted comprehensive safety evaluations [43]. Specifically, except for procedures specifically designed to evaluate the in vivo degradation behavior of MT + SF/cLP@NAHGel, all other intravitreal injections were performed in the peripheral vitreous cavity, deliberately avoiding the visual axis to minimize mechanical disturbance and optical interference. A comprehensive 8-month follow-up included slit-lamp examination, fundus imaging, OCT, IOP monitoring, and ERG. The results confirmed that the cornea, iris, lens, and retina remained structurally intact and transparent throughout the observation period, while the hydrogel remained clearly visible in the vitreous without deformation, migration, or abnormal aggregation. In addition, no significant alterations in IOP or FERG responses were observed, further supporting the ocular safety of the system. Histological examination using HE staining revealed no pathological alterations in major organs. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that MT + SF/cLP@NAHGel possesses excellent ocular and systemic biocompatibility throughout its prolonged degradation and drug-release phase, thereby validating both the material design and the injection strategy as safe and feasible for long-term intraocular application.

Conclusion

SRF, as a terminal complication of nAMD, represents a critical cause of irreversible vision loss. It is characterized by a prolonged disease course and complex pathology involving the dysregulated retinal-choroidal microenvironment, abnormal neovascularization, and EMT of RPE cells. In response to this multifactorial pathogenesis, this study developed the MT + SF/cLP@NAHGel delivery system, which features sequential drug release and multi-targeted therapeutic functions. In the initial phase, MT is rapidly released from the hydrogel to effectively suppress pro-inflammatory and hypoxia-related factors such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and HIF-1α in the choroid and retina, thereby remodeling the pathological tissue microenvironment. Subsequently, cLP enables targeted delivery of SF to RPE cells and vascular endothelial cells, where it inhibits the EMT process by blocking the TGF-β2/MEK1/2/ERK1/2 signaling axis and suppresses neovascularization by downregulating VEGF and PDGF expression, ultimately preventing the progression of SRF. In addition, the sustained-release properties of the system enabled prolonged intraocular drug retention for up to 8 months, thereby enhancing its anti-fibrotic efficacy while exhibiting excellent ocular biocompatibility. Taken together, MT + SF/cLP@NAHGel represents a novel therapeutic strategy for SRF, offering the combined advantages of sequential drug release and multi-target synergistic intervention.

Preparation of SF/cLP

Preparation of SF/cLP was carried out using a standard thin-film hydration procedure. Briefly, egg yolk lecithin (9.3 mg), cholesterol (3.2 mg), cRGD-PEG-DSPE (1 mg, MedChemExpress, USA) and SF (1 mg, Selleck, USA) were accurately weighed and dissolved in 1 mL of chloroform and 1 mL of methanol. The mixed solution was transferred to a 50 mL round-bottom flask and subjected to rotary evaporation at 40 °C and 60 rpm to remove the organic solvents. The resulting lipid film was hydrated with water at 37 °C, and the suspension was centrifuged to remove free drug, yielding the final SF/cLP formulation.

Synthesis and characterization of NAH copolymer

A mixture of NIPAM, AA, and HEMA (all from Aladdin, Shanghai, China) was dissolved in 20 mL of deionized water, followed by the addition of 24 mg of tetramethylethylenediamine (TEMED). The solution was stirred at 400 rpm in an ice-water bath for 20 min under continuous nitrogen purging. Then, 30 mg of ammonium persulfate (APS) was added, and nitrogen purging was continued for another 10 min in the ice-water bath. A nitrogen-protected polymerization was maintained at room temperature for a duration of 5 h. The resulting product was purified with hot water and freeze-dried to obtain the final NAH copolymer. ^1H NMR and FTIR techniques were employed to analyze the characterization of the copolymer’s molecular structure [45].

Preparation of NAHGel and SF/cLP@NAHGel

The P(NIPAM-co-AA-co-HEMA) copolymer was dissolved in an appropriate amount of deionized water at low temperature to obtain the precursor solution. NAHGel was generated through thermal induction at 37 °C from the precursor solution. To prepare the liposome-loaded hydrogel, the SF/cLP solution was mixed with deionized water and then added to the precursor solution from the previous step, resulting in the formation of SF/cLP@NAHGel.

Cell culture

ARPE-19 cells (CVCL_0145) were maintained in DMEM/F12 with 10% FBS, and HUVECs (CVCL_F0BB) were cultured in endothelial cell-specific medium supplemented with 5% FBS. Both cell lines were purchased from Zhong Qiao Xin Zhou Biotechnology (Shanghai, China) in June 2024 and routinely subcultured in a sterile incubator at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2. All cell lines were confirmed the absence of microbial contamination prior to use in experiments.

Cell viability assay

ARPE-19 cells (1.5 × 10⁴ cells/well) and HUVECs (2 × 10⁴ cells/well) were plated separately in 96-well plates. After 24 h of incubation, cells were exposed to varying concentrations of MT (5, 10, 25, 50, 100 µM) or SF (1, 2.5, 5, 10, 20 µM) for an additional 24 h. The original medium was then removed, and each well was replaced with 100 L of fresh medium containing 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent (Abcam, Cambridge, UK), followed by a 2-hour incubation at 37℃. Absorbance (OD values) at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Tecan, Nanjing, China). Experimental data were normalized to the control group (set as 1) to calculate the relative cell viability ratios of each treatment group. MT (purity > 98%) and SF (purity > 99%) were obtained from Aladdin (Shanghai, China). After seeding ARPE-19 cells (3 × 10⁵ cells/well) into 6-well plates and culturing for 24 h, oxidative stress was induced by treating with NaIO₃ (1.5 mM) for an additional 24 h [33]. The cells were subsequently divided into four groups: SF (treated with SF during the last 12 h), MT + SF (MT for 12 h followed by SF for 12 h), MT + SF/cLP (same as MT + SF, but SF replaced with SF/cLP) and MT + SF/cLP@NAHGel (co-cultured with MT + SF/cLP@NAHGel for 24 h). MT and SF were applied at final concentrations of 50 µM and 5 µM, respectively. HUVECs (4 × 10⁵ cells/well) were plated in 6-well plates and exposed to 200 µM CoCl₂·6 H₂O (Glpbio, USA) for 24 h to simulate a hypoxic pathological microenvironment [48]. Cells were simultaneously treated with the same set of drug groups.

ELISA assay

After drug pretreatment, ARPE-19 and HUVEC cells seeded in 6-well plates were subjected to oxidative stress or hypoxia induction, respectively. Supernatants were collected from the cultured cells, and IL-1β, TNF-α, VEGF, and PDGF levels were quantified using ELISA kits (Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from RPE cells using the RNAfast 200 Total RNA Rapid Extraction Kit (Feijie Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Quantification of total RNA was carried out with a NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo), and cDNA was subsequently synthesized using the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Japan). Using the Hieff UNICON® ColorGPS SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen Biotechnology), qPCR was performed per the manufacturer’s guidelines and the primers used are detailed in Supplementary Table 1.

Immunofluorescence assay‌

Following seeding of ARPE-19 cells (5 × 104 cells/well) on coverslips in 24-well plates and subsequent treatment, cells were fixed with methanol for 20 min, blocked with 3% BSA for 30 min, and incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Following washes, samples were incubated with corresponding secondary antibodies in the dark for 1 h, then mounted and stored. Tissue sections from paraffin-embedded mouse eyes were rehydrated after deparaffinization and then treated with citrate buffer for antigen retrieval. RPE-choroid complex flatmounts and sections were similarly blocked, incubated with primary/secondary antibodies, and mounted. Sample images were acquired using a BX53F microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), and fluorescence intensity was analyzed using ImageJ software. For tissue immunofluorescence staining, antibodies used: anti-α-SMA (1:100, Proteintech, AB_2223009, Wuhan, China), anti-TGF-β2 (1:300, Proteintech, AB_10646467, Wuhan, China), anti-HIF-1α (1:400, AB_2539916, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), anti-TNF-α (1:300, Servicebio, AB_3075517, Wuhan, China), anti-VEGFA (1:400, Proteintech, AB_2880408, Wuhan, China), anti-F4/80 (1:200, Abcam, AB_1140040, USA), anti-Collagen-1 (1:100, Proteintech, AB_2082037, Wuhan, China), anti-CD31 (1:100, Abcam, AB_307284, USA) and anti-RPE65 (1:100, Abcam, AB_2181006, USA).

Western blot‌

Protein extraction from cultured cells and mouse retinal–choroidal tissues was performed using RIPA buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) under ice-cold conditions. Protein concentrations were determined via BCA assay, mixed with loading buffer, and denatured by boiling at 100 °C for 10 min. Equal protein amounts were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to nitrocellulose membranes, and blocked with 5% skim milk for 2 h at room temperature. Following overnight exposure to primary antibodies at 4 °C, membranes were treated with the corresponding secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (Meilunbio, Dalian, China), analyzed with Image J software (SCR_002285) and normalized to GAPDH. Antibodies used: anti-TGF-β2 (1:1000, AB_10646467), anti-MEK1/2 (1:10000, AB_2140649), anti-ERK1/2 (1:4000, AB_2139822), and anti-GAPDH (1:10000, AB_2263076), anti-HIF-1α (1:4000, AB_10732601), anti-Collagen-1 (1: 1000, AB_2082037), anti-α-SMA (1:4000, AB_2223009), and anti-Vimentin (1:20000, AB_2273020) (all from Proteintech, Wuhan, China).

Anti-oxidative stress in vitro

After drug pretreatment and NaIO3 stimulation, ARPE-19 cells were incubated with DMEM medium containing the DCFH-DA probe (1:1000, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) at 37 °C in the dark for 20 min. Unbound probes were removed by PBS washing, followed by incubation with Hoechst 33,342 (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for an additional 20 min. After completing the washing procedure, cells were examined using a ZEISS fluorescence microscope. Images were captured, and fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software to determine ROS levels. Simultaneously, cell lysates were obtained for quantification of SOD enzymatic activity and MDA accumulation.

Co-culture system‌

The upper chamber of a 96-well transwell insert (Corning, USA, 0.4 μm membrane pore size) was seeded with ARPE-19 cells (1.5 × 10⁴ cells/well), and HUVECs (2 × 10⁴ cells/well) were placed in the lower well. Following oxidative stress induction and drug treatment in ARPE-19 cells, the original medium in the lower chamber was replaced with CCK-8 reagent to measure HUVEC viability.

Wound healing and tube formation assay

A confluent layer of ARPE-19 cells and HUVECs was established in 6-well plates, followed by a uniform scratch across the center. The medium was then replaced with drug-containing formulations, and cells were incubated for 24 h. Cell migration into the scratched area was observed and recorded under an inverted fluorescence microscope.

HUVECs (1.5 × 10⁴ cells/well) were resuspended in 96-well plates pre-coated with Matrigel (Corning, USA) and treated with drug-containing medium for 8 h. Tubular structure formation was visualized microscopically and quantified using ImageJ software.

In vitro cell migration and invasion studies

HUVECs (3 × 10⁴ cells) suspended in 100 µL serum-free medium were seeded into Matrigel-coated or uncoated upper chambers of 24-well transwell system (Corning, USA, 8 μm membrane pore size), with 20% FBS complete medium added to the lower wells. Following 24 h of drug exposure, cells that had traversed to the underside of the membrane were fixed and stained with crystal violet, then quantified with ImageJ after microscopic observation.

Induction of SRF in mice

Female C57BL/6J mice (6–8 weeks old, Jinan Pengyue, IMSR_JAX:000664, China) were used in this study, with all animal experiments approved by the Laboratory Animal Ethics and Welfare Committee of Qilu Hospital of Shandong University (DWLL-202400100). Prior to surgery, mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection and underwent bilateral pupil dilation. Mice were immobilized on a stereotaxic apparatus, and four laser spots were uniformly applied around the optic disc (avoiding retinal vasculature) using the Supra Scan-577 (Quantel Medical, France, spot size 50 μm, duration 50 ms and power 200 mW). Effective photocoagulation was defined by bubble formation without hemorrhage [39].

Intravitreal drug delivery in mice

Under a surgical microscope, a 33G Hamilton microsyringe (Hamilton, USA) was inserted 2 mm posterior to the corneal limbus under sterile conditions [48]. Different treatment groups (PBS, AFL, MT + SF, MT + SF/cLP and MT + SF/cLP@NAHGel) were intravitreally administered (injection volume: 1 µL; final concentrations: 40 µg AFL, 500 µM MT and 100 µM SF). To better visualize the in vivo degradation of MT + SF/cLP@NAHGel within the vitreous cavity, the hydrogel was injected into the central vitreous cavity near the fundus, whereas in all other experiments, intravitreal injections were performed in the peripheral vitreous cavity. Care was taken to avoid contact with the lens or retinal tissues. Following injection, a topical antimicrobial agent was used to reduce the risk of infection.

Flash electroretinogram

Prior to the experiment, mice were dark-adapted for 12 h, then immobilized under dim red light. Active, reference, and ground electrodes were connected to the corneal surface, temporal subcutaneous region, and tail, respectively. After stabilizing respiratory rates, flash stimuli were delivered using a visual electrophysiology monitoring system (RetiMINER-C ERG, IRC, Chongqing, China), and FERG waveforms were recorded [49].

Retinal imaging via fundus photography and OCT/OCTA

Following proper immobilization, mice underwent ocular imaging using an ultra-widefield retinal imaging device (Optos, UK) to capture color fundus photographs and autofluorescence images under 488 nm excitation. Imaging depth was adjusted to optimize retinal vessel clarity. OCT/OCTA scans were performed with the RTVue XR Avanti system (Optovue Inc, Fremont, CA, USA), employing 3 × 3 mm laser speckle-centered volumetric scans. Corneal hydration was maintained throughout the procedure via intermittent application of sterile saline, with single-eye imaging duration strictly limited to 3 min to minimize risks of corneal edema or spontaneous cataract formation. Real-time positional adjustments were implemented to ensure comprehensive retinal coverage and diagnostic image quality.

Tissue preparation and histological analysis

Following euthanasia, eyes and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys) were enucleated and immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde for fixation, followed by gradient dehydration and paraffin embedding. Serial sections with a thickness of 4 μm were cut coronally along the optic nerve axis of the eye to evaluate SRF. Major organs were sectioned through their maximal cross-sectional planes. Sections were subjected to HE staining for structural analysis and TUNEL staining (Roche kit, Germany) for apoptosis detection, followed by imaging with a BX53F microscope.

Eyes from C6, C6/cLP, C6/cLP@NAHGel, RhB@NAHGel and Rhb+C6/cLP@NAHGel groups (100 ng/ml C6 and 50 µg/mL RhB) were fixed, dehydrated in graded sucrose solutions, and embedded in OCT compound prior to snap-freezing. Serial 10-µm-thick sections were cut axially along the optic nerve using a cryostat (Leica CM1950, Germany) and stored light-protected. Nanoparticle retinal distribution was visualized under a fluorescence microscope, with images processed via ZEN software (SCR_013672).

Slit-lamp examination and IOP monitoring

Following ocular cleansing and immobilization, anterior segment structures were visualized using slit-lamp biomicroscopy with optimized light intensity, objective focal length, and magnification for high-resolution imaging. IOP measurements were subsequently performed with a rebound tonometer (iCare TONOLAB, iCare, Finland), ensuring strict perpendicular alignment of the probe axis to the corneal apex [50]. Three to five valid measurements per eye were acquired, with the final IOP value calculated as the mean of the three most consistent readings. Mechanical corneal trauma was rigorously avoided throughout the procedure to preserve tissue integrity.

RNA-sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from retinal–choroidal tissues of three individual mice per group (n = 3) without pooling, and used for library construction. A cDNA library was generated for each sample and sequenced individually on the Illumina NovaSeq™ X Plus platform (LC-Bio Technology Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China). Raw reads were preprocessed with Cutadapt (version: cutadapt-1.9, SCR_011841) for adapter removal and quality filtering. The processed reads were then aligned to the mouse reference genome with HISAT2 (version: hisat2-2.2.1, SCR_015530), assembled into transcripts using StringTie (version: stringtie-2.1.6, SCR_016323), and merged into a unified annotation file via gffcompare (version: gffcompare-0.9.8). Gene expression levels were quantified using FPKM normalization via Ballgown, and differential expression was assessed with DESeq2 (RRID: SCR_015687, |fold change| ≥ 2, FDR < 0.05). Identified genes were functionally annotated through GO and KEGG enrichment analyses to investigate related biological pathways and processes.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were processed using GraphPad Prism version 10 (SCR_002798), unless specified otherwise. Statistical analysis was conducted using Student’s t-test for two groups and one-way ANOVA for comparisons involving more than two groups. Data are expressed as mean ± SD, with statistical significance defined as P < 0.05.
