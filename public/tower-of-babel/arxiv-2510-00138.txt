Semianalytical Accretion-Tracer Emission: Forming Planets Are Intrinsically Faint Journal: ApJ
Gabriel-Dominique Marleau

Abstract
Direct-imaging surveys have looked for accreting planets through their accretion tracers such as H α \alpha but have been less fruitful than expected. However, up to now, hydrogen-line emission at accreting planets has been estimated primarily with extrapolations of stellar-scaling relationships or with theoretical spherically-symmetric computations. To predict the line emission intensity during the formation phase, we wish to follow the consequences of angular momentum conservation of the material accreting onto a gas giant in a protoplanetary disc. We focus on the limiting case that magnetospheric accretion does not occur, which yields a conservative estimate of the line emission and might correspond to certain epochs during formation. We extend but simplify an existing analytical description of the multidimensional gas flow onto an accreting gas giant, the ballistic infall model, and combine this with detailed shock emission models. Applying this to data from a global planet formation model, we confirm that the line-emitting accretion rate is a minuscule fraction of the gas inflow into the Hill sphere. Also, forming planets are mostly fainter than PDS 70 b and c or WISPIT 2 b, with a maximum H α \alpha line luminosity L line ∼ 10 − 7 ​ L ⊙ L_{\textrm{line}}\sim 10^{-7}~L_{\odot} roughly independent of planet mass. Most surveys have not been sensitive to such faint planets. Other hydrogen lines in the NIR are fainter by 1–2 dex. This implies that accreting planets are fainter than from past estimates, such that the non-detections are not as constraining as thought. Accreting super-Jupiters may well be present, and a deeper look and closer in to the host stars could well reveal many forming planets.

We consider a planet surrounded by a CPD and accreting from the Hill sphere.
There is a free-fall region, the planet surface with a certain radius, and a CPD with a height and extent out to

As seems to be common in the literature, we equate

The CPD extends from the midplane (

Our goal is not to calculate the formation of a planet but rather the line emission for a given set of instantaneous values for its mass, its radius, the accretion rate onto it, and so on. These will vary over time, possibly in a complex or non-monotonic way (e.g., because of episodic accretion;

(
Adams & Batygin 2022
)

Therefore, for line-emission calculations, we only need to choose, as an input parameter, the height of the CPD, but neither the other details of its structure nor its evolution need to be computed. We are ignoring second-order effects such as the irradiation of the incoming gas by the CPD, consistent with our assumption of ballistic infall, undeflected by thermal effects (see below).

Taylor & Adams 2024
. This will have interesting consequences which we discuss later.

Equation (
10
) is approximate because it assumes axisymmetry and hydrostatic equilibrium at the Hill radius but also because it does not include the changes to the pressure and temperature structure due to the planet (e.g., through accretion heating).
However, we let the midplane density
{\rho_{\textrm{mid}}}
at the position of the planet,

{\rho_{\textrm{mid}}}=\frac{\Sigma}{\sqrt{2\pi}H_{P,\,\textrm{PPD}}},

(11)

reflect the gap opening by the planet in that we set
\Sigma
to the reduced surface density in the gap, for instance according to
Kanagawa et al. 2017
.
This
\Sigma
can be related to an unperturbed surface density
\Sigma_{0}
by choosing a viscosity
\alpha
, or one can take
\Sigma
itself as the input quantity instead of
(\Sigma_{0},\alpha)
and a model for the surface-density reduction in the gap.

To calculate line emission from the shock requires the preshock densities. Since the density is a known (chosen) function at the outer edge, it is easiest to use mass conservation along a mass “tube” from the outer edge down to the planet and the CPD to obtain the densities there.
Recalling the notation
\rho_{0}(\theta_{0})\equiv\rho(r_{0},\theta_{0})
for the density at the outer edge, we have in general

\rho(r,\theta)=\rho_{0}(\theta_{0})\frac{r_{0}^{2}|v_{r}(r_{0},\theta_{0})|}{r^{2}|v_{r}(r,\theta)|}\frac{d\mu_{0}}{d\mu},

(12)

where
\theta_{0}=\theta_{0}(r,\theta)
.
Taking the implicit derivative of the general orbit equation (Equation (
1
)) and then using the approximate orbit equation, one can rewrite the last factor in Equation (
12
) as

\displaystyle\frac{d\mu_{0}}{d\mu}

\displaystyle=\left(\left[3\mu_{0}^{2}-1\right]\zeta+1\right)^{-1}

(13a)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1+1/(2\zeta)}{3\mu-1+2\zeta+1/(2\zeta)}

(13b)

\displaystyle\rightarrow\frac{r}{2{R_{\textrm{cent}}}},

(13c)

where Equation (
13a
) is exact and is the result quoted in
1
. Equation (
13b
), which indeed correctly contains
\mu
and not
\mu_{0}
, holds to second order in
\theta_{0}
for
\theta_{0}\ll 1
but still for arbitrary
\zeta
.
The limit in Equation (
13c
) assumes that
\zeta
is large
(which in turn implies that
\theta_{0}
is small).
In the latter limit, for instance close to the planet, Equation (
13c
) makes it clear that a small range in starting angles is responsible for accretion onto a larger range of angles:
d\mu_{0}\ll d\mu
if
{R_{\textrm{cent}}}\gg R_{\textrm{p}}
.

II.2
Accretion rates

A useful diagnostic quantity is
\dot{M}_{\textrm{p,\,direct}}
, the total mass flux hitting the planet directly.
The interest of
\dot{M}_{\textrm{p,\,direct}}
is twofold: it is the minimum, “guaranteed” mass growth rate of the planet, as opposed to the mass that falls onto the CPD and that might move away from the planet. Secondly,
\dot{M}_{\textrm{p,\,direct}}
estimates within a factor of a few the mass infall rate that generates emission lines
(
83
)
.

One can calculate
\dot{M}_{\textrm{p,\,direct}}
by integrating at
R_{\textrm{p}}
the local mass flux

\dot{m}=4\pi r^{2}\rho|v_{r}|

(14)

over the free surface of the planet or at
r_{0}
over the corresponding streamlines. The latter is easier and we write:

\dot{M}_{\textrm{p,\,direct}}=\int_{\mu_{0,\,\textrm{min}}}^{1}4\pi r_{0}^{2}\rho_{0}(\theta_{0})|v_{r}(r_{0},\theta_{0})|\,d\mu_{0}.

(15)

The lower limit of the integral
\mu_{0}=\mu_{0,\,\textrm{min}}
is connected to
\mu_{\textrm{CPD}}
at
R_{\textrm{p}}
and can be obtained from the Taylor-expanded expression for the streamlines (Equation (
2
)):

\displaystyle 1-\mu_{0,\,\textrm{min}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\mu_{\textrm{CPD}}}{2}\frac{R_{\textrm{p}}}{{R_{\textrm{cent}}}}

(16a)

\displaystyle\theta_{0,\,\textrm{min}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\left(1-\mu_{\textrm{CPD}}\right)\frac{R_{\textrm{p}}}{{R_{\textrm{cent}}}}}.

(16b)

The height of the “polar cap” at the Hill radius in Equation (
15
) is small:
\Delta z=(1-\mu_{0,\,\textrm{min}}){R_{\textrm{Hill}}}\ll H_{P,\,\textrm{PPD}}
for reasonable or typical parameter values. Therefore, the
\rho_{0}
factor in Equation (
15
) is roughly constant and can be evaluated at the pole, and
v_{r}(r_{0},\theta_{0})\approx{v_{\textrm{ff},\,\infty}}(r_{0})
to an even better approximation. This yields

\displaystyle\dot{M}_{\textrm{p,\,direct}}\approx

\displaystyle\,4\pi{R_{\textrm{Hill}}}^{2}\rho_{0}(0){v_{\textrm{ff},\,\infty}}({R_{\textrm{Hill}}})\left(1-\mu_{0,\,\textrm{min}}\right)

(17a)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\,2\,\Sigma\,\sqrt{\pi GM_{\textrm{p}}{R_{\textrm{Hill}}}}\frac{(1-\mu_{\textrm{CPD}})}{H_{P,\,\textrm{PPD}}}\frac{R_{\textrm{p}}}{{f_{\textrm{cent}}}}

\displaystyle\;\times\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{{R_{\textrm{Hill}}}}{H_{P,\,\textrm{PPD}}}\right)^{2}\right],

(17b)

where
{f_{\textrm{cent}}}\equiv{R_{\textrm{cent}}}/{R_{\textrm{Hill}}}
is an important parameter discussed below.
Equation (
17
) is an excellent approximation, as we verified numerically, and it shows that for fixed other parameters,
\dot{M}_{\textrm{p,\,direct}}\propto\Sigma/{f_{\textrm{cent}}}
or
\dot{M}_{\textrm{p,\,direct}}\propto\Sigma/{R_{\textrm{cent}}}
.

Another noteworthy, and more fundamental, accretion rate is the net mass flux entering the Hill sphere,
\dot{M}_{\textrm{Hill,\,net}}
. Hydrodynamical simulations predict it to be related to the surface density of the PPD a few Hill radii away from the planet
(
Tanigawa & Watanabe 2002
)
.
Three-dimensional simulations show the presence of a net outflow away from the planet in the disc midplane (e.g.,
Tanigawa et al. 2012
;
Cilibrasi et al. 2023
). To emulate this crudely, we truncate the solution at
r_{0}
between the midplane and
\mu_{\textrm{CPD}}
, effectively setting
v=0
there, that is, not letting any material enter the Hill sphere close to the midplane.
The reasoning is that the
horseshoe orbits might have an aspect ratio comparable to that of the CPD,
as the simulations of
Machida et al. 2008
suggest, for instance.
However,
\mu_{\textrm{CPD}}
is determined for the regions closest to planet and might not be related to the azimuthally averaged dynamics between
{R_{\textrm{cent}}}
and
{R_{\textrm{Hill}}}
.
Therefore, it is not clear how exactly this truncation should be, and the current approach is meant only as an acceptable starting point.
We verified that integrating down to the midplane or only to
\mu_{\textrm{CPD}}
changes the mass influx by a factor of 0.2–0.7 over 1.5 dex in mass, which is modest.

Integrating
\dot{m}
(Equation (
14
)) at
{R_{\textrm{Hill}}}
, the net mass flux into the Hill sphere is

\displaystyle\dot{M}_{\textrm{Hill,\,net}}=

\displaystyle\,\int_{\mu_{\textrm{CPD}}}^{1}4\pi r_{0}^{2}\rho_{0}(\theta_{0})|v_{r}(r_{0},\theta_{0})|\,d\mu_{0}

(18a)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\,4\pi{R_{\textrm{Hill}}}^{3/2}\sqrt{2GM_{\textrm{p}}}\int_{\mu_{\textrm{CPD}}}^{1}\rho_{0}(\theta_{0})\,d\mu_{0}

(18b)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\,2\pi\Sigma\sqrt{2GM_{\textrm{p}}{R_{\textrm{Hill}}}}\times\Delta\!\erf,

(18c)

where we have used that
r_{0}={R_{\textrm{Hill}}}
and defined

\displaystyle\Delta\!\erf

\displaystyle\equiv\erf[x(1)]-\erf[x(\mu_{\textrm{CPD}})],

(19a)

\displaystyle x(\mu_{0})

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\mu_{0}}{\sqrt{2}}\frac{{R_{\textrm{Hill}}}}{H_{P,\,\textrm{PPD}}}=\mu_{0}\sqrt{\frac{{R_{\textrm{Hill}}}^{2}{R_{\textrm{Bondi}}}}{2a^{3}q}}.

(19b)

Equation (
18b
) is approximate but Equation (
18c
) follows exactly from it.
Since the choice of the lower integration limit to define
\dot{M}_{\textrm{Hill,\,net}}
is somewhat arbitrary, we simplified
v_{r}
to be equal to
{v_{\textrm{ff},\,\infty}}
for all angles. The exact solution is indeed not too far from radial (Figure
1
), but in general we are effectively integrating down to a location slightly closer to the pole than
\mu_{\textrm{CPD}}
. This slight approximation made it possible to obtain a semianalytical answer involving the error function.
Since
x(1)
is neither very small nor very large,
the factor
\Delta\!\erf
does not seem simplifiable, but it will turn out to be of order unity. The important property of
\dot{M}_{\textrm{Hill,\,net}}
is that it scales linearly with
\Sigma
, which we will use extensively.

For comparison,
1
define the accretion rate of material that stays within the Hill sphere
\dot{M}_{\textrm{in}}
, to which our
\dot{M}_{\textrm{Hill,\,net}}
corresponds, as an integral over all angles, that is, down to the midplane, instead of down to the CPD height. This too is a valid assumption given the uncertainties in the inflow geometry as mentioned in Section
II.1
.

Input parameters

Our model so far has several input parameters:
(M_{\textrm{p}},R_{\textrm{p}},a,\Sigma,h,{M_{\star}})

using the same parametrisation as in
83

{f_{\textrm{cent}}}

Then, we address

II.3.3
Centrifugal radius

In our approach, the centrifugal radius
{R_{\textrm{cent}}}={f_{\textrm{cent}}}{R_{\textrm{Hill}}}
is up to now unconstrained, yet it plays an important role in setting the line flux.
Gas within
{R_{\textrm{cent}}}
has sufficiently small angular momentum to accrete onto the planet, whereas gas outside it spreads outward. If
{R_{\textrm{cent}}}\gg R_{\textrm{p}}

{R_{\textrm{cent}}}
(see Equations (
4

{R_{\textrm{cent}}}
does scale the preshock density linearly (Equation (

{f_{\textrm{cent}}}={R_{\textrm{cent}}}/{R_{\textrm{Hill}}}=1/3

(
Quillen & Trilling 1998
)
, but several effects can reduce
{R_{\textrm{cent}}}
(
1
,
2
).

We draw on the simulations of
83
to estimate
{f_{\textrm{cent}}}
. We first scale our
\Sigma
to match the influx
\dot{M}_{\textrm{Hill,\,net}}
in those simulations (Equation (
22
)) and then choose
{f_{\textrm{cent}}}
to match
\dot{M}_{\textrm{p,\,direct}}
. This is straightforward since
\dot{M}_{\textrm{Hill,\,net}}
does not depend on
{f_{\textrm{cent}}}
.
Fixing
\mu_{\textrm{CPD}}=0.23

IV
Discussion

We discuss some aspects within or beyond our model.

IV.1
Interpreting the non-detections

Searches for accreting planets have revealed only few objects
(
Cugno et al. 2019
;
Zurlo et al. 2020
;
Huélamo et al. 2022
;
Follette et al. 2023
)

and our results suggest that an important factor is that only a small fraction of the accreting gas produces line emission.
Another factor is the lower line emission efficiency per unit accretion rate
at lower accretor masses
(
Aoyama et al. 2021
)
.
Together, this implies that extrapolated stellar relationships (e.g.,
Alcalá et al. 2017
;
Fiorellino et al. 2025
) overestimate survey yields, and that the non-detections are in fact less constraining than thought.

We did not analyse the separation dependence of the luminosity predictions since the migration of gas giants out to large separations is not very well understood, and eccentricity will modify the instantaneous planet–star separation too.
Even if there are planets around the stars surveyed so far and they are located in the favourable (background-limited) portion of detection curves, we do not expect that they would have been detected because they tend to be too faint for most instruments up to now.
Figure
6
suggests that the deep background-limited sensitivity values of
Cugno et al. 2023
from MagAO-X—which benefits from major instrumental upgrades at MagAO—or the Hubble Space Telescope are only starting to scratch the tip of the iceberg.
Recent discoveries with MagAO-X seem to corroborate this
(
Li et al. 2025
;
Close et al. 2025b
)
.
Therefore, decreasing the inner working angle should give access to more planets
(
Nielsen et al. 2019
;
Close 2020
)
, but the more important factor might be an improvement in the sensitivity.

In the short term, using existing technology, it thus seems promising to survey a large number of stars while emphasising long integration times. Even though there is some evidence that extinction plays a role in explaining the spectra of PDS 70 b and c
(
Stolker et al. 2020
;
Cugno et al. 2021
)
, more recent data call this somewhat into question
(
Blakely et al. 2025
)
. Also,
extinction by the accreting material close to the planet is predicted fortunately not to be a major factor overall
12
12

12

This was studied at H
\alpha
. In the NIR, the dust opacity should be even smaller, but the gas opacity could be larger or smaller in narrow regions surrounding the other hydrogen emission lines.

(
Marleau et al. 2022
)
.

Figure
6
shows that the maximal line luminosity is roughly constant with mass for all hydrogen lines. To first order, this implies that low-mass planets offer a similar contrast relative to their star compared to more massive ones and thus similar chances of detection.
However, more massive planets possibly open deeper gaps than lower-mass planets
(
Kanagawa et al. 2017

, which helps their detectability.
However, ultimately, it is not clear whether the planet-mass dependence of the remaining opacity in the gap is more important, and variability would likely complicate the matter.

IV.2
Limiting case for the accretion rate

When using the accretion rates from the population synthesis, we made an important assumption: that the planet growth rate is equal to the net mass flux into the Hill sphere (Equation (
22
)). If instead some of this mass flux served to let the CPD grow and the CPD were not efficient at transporting mass towards the planet,
a larger net mass flux into the Hill sphere would be required to have a given planet growth rate. This would imply an even higher surface density than we derived.
The accretion rate hitting the planet directly would be higher and also the line luminosities. Thus our current estimates provide lower limits on the surface densities.

We can turn this around and assume that the scenario we consider in this work—essentially, the consequence of angular momentum conservation, along with the assumption that there is no emission other than from the infall onto the planet and the CPD—describes the main factor for the small number of detected accreting planets. Then, the fact that the highest predicted luminosities are close to the detection limits up to now suggests that accreting planets cannot be much brighter, since otherwise more objects would have been detected. Therefore, not much less than
\dot{M}_{\textrm{Hill,\,net}}
can be feeding the planets.
This could be used to put constraints on the timescale for radial mass transport in CPDs (

Taylor & Adams 2024

IV.5
Neglect of magnetospheric accretion

We discuss our neglect of
the influence of magnetic fields on the accretion process onto the planet (magnetospheric accretion; e.g.,
Lovelace et al. 2011
;
Batygin 2018
) and therefore the line emission. A few comments can be made about this:

1.

There are theoretical reasons to expect young and therefore luminous gas giants to have
B\approx 0.5
–2 kG magnetic fields, which might be strong enough to truncate the CPD
(
Katarzyński et al. 2016
;
Hasegawa et al. 2021
, e.g.,)
.

2.

It is not clear whether the gas is sufficiently ionised (thermally or not) to couple to the magnetic field, with non-ideal magnetohydrodynamical effects complicating the picture further
(
Batygin 2018
;
Hasegawa et al. 2021
)
.

3.

Empirically, a (perhaps growing) list of planetary-mass objects do seem to require magnetospheric accretion or be consistent with it to explain the shape or flux ratios of accretion-tracing hydrogen lines
(
Betti et al. 2022a
;
Betti et al. 2022b
;
Ringqvist et al. 2023
;
Demars et al. 2023
;
Marleau et al. 2024
;
Viswanath et al. 2024
;
Aoyama et al. 2024
)
.

4.

If planets accrete magnetospherically, the capture radius for the infalling gas would be larger than the planetary radius because gas falling within the apex of the magnetic fields would slide down towards the planetary surface (
Batygin 2018
; Equation (45) of
1
). This would increase by a factor of a few squared the fraction of
\dot{M}_{\textrm{Hill,\,net}}
landing indirectly on the surface of the planet, a “guaranteed” minimum for the growth rate, independently of what happens to the gas which slides down the field lines outwards or lands directly on the CPD (Section
II.2
). However, it is not obvious how the gas capture affects the velocity components, especially whether the gas, when it reaches, the footpoint of the field lines, still has or has regained an appreciable radial velocity component, which would let it emit significantly shock lines.

5.

In an elegant analysis,
Batygin & Adams 2025
posited that magnetospheric accretion was active during the formation of Jupiter and were able to infer from this detailed properties about Jupiter in an entirely self-consistent picture. This lends strong support to having
N\geqslant 1
gas giant in the Galaxy to which magnetospheric accretion applies.

Therefore, magnetospheric accretion could be taking place at several accreting planets and this might change the detailed predictions of our model at an appreciable (factor-several) level. However, these modifications should be more quantitative than qualitative, and in any case, our model will apply—at least in principle—to those planets which are not accreting magnetospherically at a given time, if ever.

V
Summary and conclusion

In the context of only a few accreting planets having been discovered so far despite several searches, we studied how a non-spherically-symmetric accretion geometry onto a forming planet could help explain the rarity of accretion-line detections.
We developed a convenient approximation to the analytical multidimensional (2.5D) description of the gas flow onto an accreting gas giant forming in a PPD (
Ulrich 1976
;
1
;
Taylor & Adams 2024
). We extended this previous work by taking the exponential vertical stratification of the gas density at the Hill sphere and the possible cut-off of the equatorial inflow, due to the CPD, into account. This set-up remains limited because we consider azimuthally symmetric polar-plane dynamics as opposed to a complex 3D geometry. However, this set-up does attempt to capture the important consideration of angular momentum conservation.

Especially for planets that are massive or forming closer in to their star, the non-negligible size of the Hill sphere compared to the pressure scale height
dramatically reduces the fraction of the accretion rate hitting the planet surface directly. While the exact flow solution is tractable analytically (Appendix
A
),
the small-angle approximation we employed makes the solution easy to manipulate and to analyse to gain insight into the parameter dependence.
Combining this with models of the hydrogen-line emission from the shocks on the planet and CPD
(
Aoyama et al. 2018
)
led to predictions of the line luminosity of forming planets (Figure
6
).
We showed as main examples H
\alpha
and various NIR lines, at several of which accreting low-mass objects have been detected.
More hydrogen lines are available upon request.

An important free parameter of the model is the centrifugal radius of the accretion flow.
Its classical value of
{f_{\textrm{cent}}}=1/3
of the Hill radius is an upper bound
but, realistically, several effects reduce it (e.g.,
1
).
Therefore, we calibrated it from 2.5D radiation-hydrodynamics simulations
(
83
)
and found that
{f_{\textrm{cent}}}\approx 0.03
leads to a very good match of the preshock properties of the gas. This agrees well with an independent line of reasoning (
2
).

We applied our model to the distribution of
properties of forming gas giants
in the Bern model
(
Emsenhuber et al. 2021a
)
.
We found that H
\alpha
line luminosities reach up to
L_{\textrm{line}}\approx 2\times 10^{-7}~L_{\odot}
and that, remarkably, this is nearly independent of planet mass
14
14

14

This
is either bad news—high-mass planets are as faint as low-mass planets—or good news—low-mass planets are as bright as high-mass ones. The latter should have more weight because observations and theory agree that low-mass gas giants are more numerous
(
Cumming et al. 2008
;
Nielsen et al. 2019
;
Emsenhuber et al. 2021b
, e.g.,)
.
.
These maximum values come from planets growing at a rate up to
\dot{M}_{\textrm{growth}}\sim 10^{-5}~M_{\textrm{J}}\,\textrm{yr}^{-1}

Peak luminosities for other lines are around

In the stellar case, magnetospheric accretion allows the entire mass flux to emit.
Interestingly, the typical or even best-case sensitivities of H

Improved instrumental sensitivities might be a promising way to unearth more low-mass accretors

Our semianalytical framework is meant more for population studies than for individual systems, due to the simplified set-up.

In this work, we did not include explicitly the possible implications of magnetospheric accretion on the line emission but discussed this in Section

Planets accreting by magnetospheric accretion should be brighter for a given instantaneous mass growth rate because a larger fraction of this mass flux can generate lines: the gas joins the CPD at a large distance and therefore low velocity, moves radially inwards in the CPD, and is lifted by the magnetic field lines truncating the CPD and channelling the gas onto the surface of the planet, where it shocks. H
\alpha
and other lines can be emitted in the accretion, at the footpoint of the shock, or in both. There is a growing body of evidence that this mechanism can operate at planetary masses.

The take-away message from this work is that
accreting planets may really be much fainter than usually thought for a given growth rate, which needs on average to lead to a given planet mass within the lifetime of the disc, that is,
\langle\dot{M}_{\textrm{growth}}\rangle\sim M_{\textrm{p}}/(3~\mathrm{Myr})
.
This is in fact a positive outlook because it implies that
the non-detections up to now
leave ample room for exciting discoveries.
It seems promising to push in closer to the star, where more forming planets should be found. Upcoming instruments such as the first-generation Midinfrared ELT Imager and Spectrograph (METIS) on the Extremely Large Telescope (ELT)

should help see closer in. However, obtaining more H

Males et al. 2018
;
Males et al. 2024
;
Close et al. 2025b
).
Using longer exposure times when targetting known young planets,
especially with high-resolution spectrographs, may reveal accretion tracers at objects down to barely a few Jupiter masses.
