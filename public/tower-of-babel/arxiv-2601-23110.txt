Abstract
Let φ \varphi denote a k k -algebra endomorphism of the n n -th Weyl algebra A n ​ ( k ) A_{n}(k) over a perfect field k k of positive characteristic p p . We prove that φ \varphi can be lifted to an endomorphism of the Weyl algebra A n ​ ( W 2 ​ ( k ) ) A_{n}(W_{2}(k)) over the Witt vectors W 2 ​ ( k ) W_{2}(k) of length two over k k if and only if φ \varphi induces a Poisson morphism of the center of A n ​ ( k ) A_{n}(k) . Furthermore, we improve a result of Tsuchimoto, which enables us to conclude that these equivalent statements hold at least when deg ⁡ ( φ ) < p \deg(\varphi)<p . In particular, we conclude that φ \varphi is injective if deg ⁡ ( φ ) < p \deg(\varphi)<p .



Introduction

Let
k
be a perfect field of positive characteristic
p>0
, and let
A_{n}(k)
denote the
n
-th Weyl algebra over
k
, generated by elements

z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{2n},

subject to the relations

[z_{i},z_{j}]:=\omega_{i,j},\qquad 1\leq i,j\leq 2n,

where
\omega_{i,j}
is the
(i,j)
-th entry of the matrix

\omega=\begin{pmatrix}0&-I_{n}\\
I_{n}&0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{Mat}_{2n}(k),

and
I_{n}\in\mathrm{Mat}_{n}(k)
denotes the
n\times n
identity matrix.

In positive characteristic, the Weyl algebra is an Azumaya algebra with center
Z
equal to a polynomial ring over
k
in the
2n
variables

x_{i}:=z_{i}^{p},\quad i=1,\dots,2n.

We consider the center
Z
as a Poisson algebra with Poisson bracket
defined by

\{x_{i},x_{j}\}=-\omega_{i,j},\qquad 1\leq i,j\leq 2n.

Let
\varphi
be a
k
-algebra endomorphism of
A_{n}(k)
.
It is known that
\varphi(Z)\subseteq Z
, and one may therefore ask
under what conditions the induced endomorphism
\varphi_{Z}
of
Z

is a Poisson morphism, i.e.

\varphi_{Z}(\{f,g\})=\{\varphi_{Z}(f),\varphi_{Z}(g)\},\qquad f,g\in Z.

One typically expects this to be the case; however, counterexamples exist, even when

\varphi
is an automorphism (see Example
3.14
).
Nevertheless, some partial results are known.
Tsuchimoto
[
9
, Cor. 3.3]
proved that if the degree of
\varphi

(to be defined below) is less than
p/2
, then
\varphi_{Z}
is a
Poisson morphism. In a slightly different direction, Belov-Kanel and Kontsevich
observed
[
3
]
that if
\varphi
can be lifted to an endomorphism
of the Weyl algebra over a ring of characteristic
p^{2}
, then

\varphi_{Z}
is a Poisson morphism.
As a side remark, it has been conjectured by Belov-Kanel and Kontsevich

[
2
]

that if
k
is a field of characteristic zero, there is expected to exist a
canonical isomorphism between the automorphism group of the Weyl algebra

A_{n}(k)
and the group of symplectomorphisms of affine
2n
-space over
k
.

In this paper, we study the question of when a
k
-algebra endomorphism

\varphi
can be lifted to an endomorphism of the Weyl algebra over the
Witt vectors
W_{2}(k)
of length two over
k
. We show that such a lifting
exists precisely when the induced endomorphism
\varphi_{Z}
of the center

Z
is a Poisson morphism.
Combining this with the result of Tsuchimoto, we see that
\varphi
can
be lifted to the Witt vectors of length two if the degree of
\varphi

is less than
p/2
. Along the way, we also note that Tsuchimoto’s result
can be improved to include the case where
\varphi
has degree
less than
p
, although it holds in even greater generality
(see Thm.
3.10
). We furthermore give an if-and-only-if criterion
for
{\varphi}_{Z}
to be a Poisson morphism, determined by the solutions to
certain differential equations introduced by Tsuchimoto (see Prop.
3.15
).

In the last section (§
4
) of the paper we give a few applications of this
improved degree bound and restriction to the center.
Injectivity of
\varphi
always holds for
n=1
. Bavula conjectured it to hold in general, but Makar-Limanov gave an example of a non-injective endomorphism for
n=2
.
Under the assumption that
\varphi<p
(and, more generally, under weaker assumptions),
we prove that
\varphi
is injective.

We also show that
\varphi
is flat and an automorphism if it is birational

\deg(\varphi)<p
. These two results were previously only known for fields of
characteristic zero.
We refer to §
4
for precise statements and references.

1
Notation

Let
k
be a perfect field of positive characteristic
p>0
, and
let
A_{n}(k)
denote the
n
-th Weyl algebra over
k
. We view
A_{n}(k)
as generated by elements

z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{2n},

subject to the relations

[z_{i},z_{j}]:=\omega_{i,j},\qquad 1\leq i,j\leq 2n,

where
\omega_{i,j}
is the
(i,j)
-th entry of

\omega=\begin{pmatrix}0&-I_{n}\\
I_{n}&0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{Mat}_{2n}(k),

and
I_{n}\in\mathrm{Mat}_{n}(k)
denotes the
n\times n
identity matrix.

As a
k
-vector space,
A_{n}(k)
has basis

z_{1}^{i_{1}}z_{2}^{i_{2}}\cdots z_{2n}^{i_{2n}},\quad i_{1},\dots,i_{2n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}.

We will use multi-index notation and write such an element as
{\bf z}^{\bf i}
, where
{\bf i}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{2n})
.
The total degree of a basis element is defined by

\deg({\bf z}^{\bf i}):=i_{1}+\dots+i_{2n},

and for a general nonzero element

f=\sum_{\bf i}c_{\bf i}\,{\bf z}^{\bf i}\in A_{n}(k),\quad c_{\bf i}\in k,

we set

\deg(f):=\max\{\deg({\bf z}^{\bf i}):c_{\bf i}\neq 0\}.

The center of
A_{n}(k)
is denoted by
Z
and is a polynomial ring over
k
in
2n
variables, generated by

x_{i}:=z_{i}^{p},\qquad i=1,\dots,2n.

As a module over
Z
, the Weyl algebra is free of rank
p^{2n}
with basis

z_{1}^{i_{1}}z_{2}^{i_{2}}\cdots z_{2n}^{i_{2n}},\quad 0\leq i_{1},\dots,i_{2n}<p.

Throughout, we let
\varphi
denote a
k
-algebra endomorphism of
A_{n}(k)
.
We define the degree
\deg(\varphi)
of
\varphi
to be the maximal degree of the elements
\varphi(z_{i})
,
i=1,\dots,2n
.
It is known (
[
8
, Cor. 2]
) that
\varphi
defines a
Z
-module basis

\varphi(z_{1})^{i_{1}}\varphi(z_{2})^{i_{2}}\cdots\varphi(z_{2n})^{i_{2n}},\quad 0\leq i_{1},\dots,i_{2n}<p.

In particular, any element of
\varphi(Z)
is central, as it commutes with all basis elements.
Hence

\varphi(Z)\subseteq Z,

and thus
\varphi
, by restriction, induces a
k
-algebra endomorphism
\varphi_{Z}
of
Z
.

Throughout this article, the notation
\delta_{i,j}
is used for Kronecker’s delta.
Moreover, if
R
is a ring and
x\in R
, we write
\operatorname{ad}(x)
for the map
from
R
to itself given by the commutator with
x
.

1.1
Witt vecors

The ring of Witt vectors of length two over
k
, denoted
W_{2}(k)
,
is the set

W_{2}(k)=\{(a_{1},a_{2})\mid a_{1},a_{2}\in k\},

equipped with addition and multiplication given by

(a_{1},a_{2})+(b_{1},b_{2})=\Bigl(a_{1}+b_{1},\;a_{2}+b_{2}+\frac{1}{p}\bigl(a_{1}^{p}+b_{1}^{p}-(a_{1}+b_{1})^{p}\bigr)\Bigr),

and

(a_{1},a_{2})\cdot(b_{1},b_{2})=\Bigl(a_{1}b_{1},\;a_{1}^{p}b_{2}+b_{1}^{p}a_{2}\Bigr).

Here the expression

\frac{1}{p}\bigl(a_{1}^{p}+b_{1}^{p}-(a_{1}+b_{1})^{p}\bigr)

is interpreted as the reduction modulo
p
of an integral polynomial,
which is well-defined since the numerator is divisible by
p
in

\mathbb{Z}[a_{1},b_{1}]
.

The additive and multiplicative identities of
W_{2}(k)
are
(0,0)
and

(1,0)
, respectively. Moreover,

p\cdot(a_{1},a_{2})=(0,a_{1}^{p}).

Since
k
is a perfect field, every element
(a_{1},a_{2})\in W_{2}(k)
can be
written uniquely in the form

(a_{1},a_{2})=(a_{1},0)+p\cdot(a_{2}^{1/p},0),

where
a_{2}^{1/p}\in k
denotes the
p
-th root of
a_{2}
.
The element
(a,0)\in W_{2}(k)
is called the Teichmüller lift of
a\in k
.
From now on, whenever we regard an element
a\in k
as an element of

W_{2}(k)
, we implicitly mean its Teichmüller lift.
Thus every element of
W_{2}(k)
admits a unique expression of
the form

a+p\cdot b,

with
a,b\in k
viewed inside
W_{2}(k)
via their Teichmüller lifts.

The ring
W_{2}(k)
has characteristic
p^{2}
and comes equipped with a
surjective ring homomorphism onto
k
, sending
(a_{1},a_{2})
to
a_{1}
.
In particular,
W_{2}(k)/pW_{2}(k)
is isomorphic to
k
.

1.1.1
The Weyl algebra over
W_{2}(k)

The Weyl algebra
A_{n}(W_{2}(k))
over
W_{2}(k)
is defined similarly to

A_{n}(k)
, except that the coefficients are now taken in
W_{2}(k)
.
In particular,
A_{n}(W_{2}(k))
is a free
W_{2}(k)
-module with basis

{\bf z}^{\bf i}=z_{1}^{i_{1}}z_{2}^{i_{2}}\cdots z_{2n}^{i_{2n}},\quad i_{1},\dots,i_{2n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}.

Any element

f=\sum_{\bf i}c_{\bf i}\,{\bf z}^{\bf i}\in A_{n}(k),\quad c_{\bf i}\in k,

has an associated element

[f]=\sum_{\bf i}[c_{\bf i}]\,{\bf z}^{\bf i}\in A_{n}(W_{2}(k)),

where
[c_{\bf i}]
denotes the Teichmüller lift of
c_{\bf i}
.
We say that
[f]
is the Teichmüller lift of
f
, and we will often simply write
f
to denote this lift. In particular, every element of
A_{n}(W_{2}(k))
admits a unique decomposition

f_{1}+p\cdot f_{2},

where
f_{1},f_{2}\in A_{n}(k)
are viewed as elements of
A_{n}(W_{2}(k))
via their Teichmüller lifts.

Lemma 1.1
.

Let
u
and
v
be elements of
A_{n}(W_{2}(k))
whose images
in
A_{n}(k)
under the natural morphism lie in
Z
.
Then
[u,v]
is a central element of
A_{n}(W_{2}(k))
.

Proof.

By assumption, the operators
\operatorname{ad}(u)
and
\operatorname{ad}(v)

on
A_{n}(W_{2}(k))
take values in
p\cdot A_{n}(k)
.
Since
A_{n}(W_{2}(k))
has characteristic
p^{2}
, we
therefore conclude that the operator

\operatorname{ad}([u,v])=[\operatorname{ad}(u),\operatorname{ad}(v)]

is zero. Hence
[u,v]
is central.
∎

2
Lift of endomorphisms

In this section, we study when a
k
-algebra endomorphism

\varphi
of
A_{n}(k)
admits a lift to a
W_{2}(k)
-algebra endomorphism

\Phi
of
A_{n}(W_{2}(k))
.

Fix
\varphi
and let

u_{i}:=\varphi(z_{i}),\quad i=1,\dots,2n.

Viewing
u_{1},\dots,u_{2n}
as elements of
A_{n}(W_{2}(k))

via the Teichmüller lift, we have

[u_{i},u_{j}]=\omega_{i,j}+p\cdot u_{ij},\quad i,j=1,\dots,2n,

(2.0.1)

for some uniquely determined elements
u_{ij}\in A_{n}(k)
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
v_{1},\dots,v_{2n}
be elements of
A_{n}(k)
. Then

\Phi(z_{i})=u_{i}+p\cdot v_{i},\quad i=1,\dots,2n,

defines a lift of
\varphi
if and only if

u_{ij}+[u_{i},v_{j}]-[u_{j},v_{i}]=0,\quad i,j=1,\dots,2n.

Proof.

The map
\Phi
defines a lift of
\varphi
if and only if

[u_{i}+p\cdot v_{i},u_{j}+p\cdot v_{j}]=\omega_{i,j},\quad i,j=1,\dots,2n.

Expanding the left-hand side gives

[u_{i}+p\cdot v_{i},u_{j}+p\cdot v_{j}]=\omega_{i,j}+p\cdot\big(u_{ij}+[u_{i},v_{j}]-[u_{j},v_{i}]\big),

which immediately yields the claimed equivalence.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
u
and
v
be elements in
A_{n}(W_{2}(k))
. Suppose that

[u,v]=\kappa+p\,w,

for some
w\in A_{n}(W_{2}(k))
and
\kappa\in W_{2}(k)
. Then

[u^{t},v]=t\,\kappa\,u^{\,t-1}+p\,\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}u^{i}wu^{\,t-i-1},\quad t\in\mathbb{N}.

In particular,

[u^{p},v]=p\,\Big(\kappa\,u^{\,p-1}+\operatorname{ad}(u)^{p-1}(w)\Big).

Proof.

We prove the first identity by induction on
t\geq 1
. The case
t=1
is trivial.
Assume the identity holds for some
t\geq 1
. Then

\displaystyle[u^{\,t+1},v]

\displaystyle=u^{t}\,[u,v]+[u^{t},v]\,u

\displaystyle=u^{t}\,(\kappa+p\,w)+t\,\kappa\,u^{t}+p\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}u^{i}wu^{\,t-i}

\displaystyle=(t+1)\,\kappa\,u^{t}+p\sum_{i=0}^{t}u^{i}wu^{\,t-i},

as claimed.

For the second identity, we observe that the first identity implies

[u^{p},v]=p\,\Big(\kappa\,u^{\,p-1}+\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}u^{i}wu^{\,p-i-1}\Big).

It therefore suffices to prove the (well known) identity

\operatorname{ad}(\hat{u})^{\,p-1}(\hat{w})=\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}\hat{u}^{i}\hat{w}\hat{u}^{\,p-i-1},

for
\hat{u},\hat{w}\in A_{n}(k)
. This follows by noting that
\operatorname{ad}(\hat{u})
is the difference of
two commuting operators, multiplication by
\pm\hat{u}
from the left and right, and combining this
with the binomial identity

\binom{p-1}{i}=(-1)^{i}\quad\text{in }k.

∎

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
u
and
v
be elements of
A_{n}(W_{2}(k))
. Suppose that

[u,v]=\kappa+p\,w

for some
w\in A_{n}(W_{2}(k))
and
\kappa\in W_{2}(k)
. Then

[u^{p},v^{p}]=p\cdot\Big(-\kappa^{p}+\operatorname{ad}(v)^{\,p-1}\operatorname{ad}(u)^{\,p-1}(w)\Big).

Proof.

By Lemma
2.2
, we have

[u^{p},v]=p\,\Big(\kappa\,u^{\,p-1}+\operatorname{ad}(u)^{\,p-1}(w)\Big).

In particular, applying the same lemma to the pair
v
and
u^{p}
, we obtain

\displaystyle[v^{p},u^{p}]

\displaystyle=p\cdot\Big(-\kappa\,\operatorname{ad}(v)^{\,p-1}(u^{\,p-1})-\operatorname{ad}(v)^{\,p-1}\operatorname{ad}(u)^{\,p-1}(w)\Big)

\displaystyle=p\cdot\Big(-(p-1)!\,\kappa^{p}-\operatorname{ad}(v)^{\,p-1}\operatorname{ad}(u)^{\,p-1}(w)\Big)

\displaystyle=p\cdot\Big(\kappa^{p}-\operatorname{ad}(v)^{\,p-1}\operatorname{ad}(u)^{\,p-1}(w)\Big),

which gives the claimed identity.
∎

Theorem 2.4
.

For
i,j=1,\dots,2n
, define

c_{ij}:=\operatorname{ad}(u_{i})^{\,p-1}\operatorname{ad}(u_{j})^{\,p-1}(u_{ij})

as an element in
A_{n}(k)
. Then
c_{ij}
belongs to the center of
A_{n}(k)
, and moreover

p\cdot c_{ij}=[u_{i}^{p},u_{j}^{p}]+p\cdot\omega_{i,j}

in
A_{n}(W_{2}(k))
.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.3
, we have

[u_{i}^{p},u_{j}^{p}]=p\cdot\Big(-\omega_{i,j}+\operatorname{ad}(u_{i})^{\,p-1}\operatorname{ad}(u_{j})^{\,p-1}(u_{ij})\Big),

since

[u_{i},u_{j}]=\omega_{i,j}+p\cdot u_{ij}.

This gives the formula for
p\cdot c_{ij}
.

It remains to show that
c_{ij}
is central in
A_{n}(k)
. It follows from the formula above that this will hold if
[u_{i}^{p},u_{j}^{p}]
is central in
A_{n}(W_{2}(k))
, which is a direct consequence of Lemma
1.1
.
∎

2.1
Obstruction interpreted via cohomology

Lemma
2.1
indicates that the elements
u_{ij}
define a cohomology class
which encodes the obstruction to lifting
\varphi
to an endomorphism of
A_{n}(W_{2}(k))
.
To clarify this idea, we consider the Chevalley–Eilenberg complex of
A_{n}(k)
, viewed
as a module over an abelian Lie algebra of dimension
2n
, where the basis elements act
via the operators
\operatorname{ad}(u_{i})
.
In an appropriate setting, the operators
\operatorname{ad}(u_{i})
can be interpreted as formal derivations
of
A_{n}(k)
. Under this identification, the Chevalley–Eilenberg complex associated with
the Lie algebra generated by the
\operatorname{ad}(u_{i})
reduces to the de Rham complex of a
polynomial ring.
More precisely,
recall (as explained in Section
1
) that

A_{n}(k)
is a free module over its center

k[z_{1}^{p},\dots,z_{2n}^{p}]
with basis

u_{1}^{i_{1}}u_{2}^{i_{2}}\cdots u_{2n}^{i_{2n}},\quad 0\leq i_{1},\dots,i_{2n}<p.

Equivalently, the following set of elements also forms a basis:

\hat{u}_{1}^{i_{1}}\hat{u}_{2}^{i_{2}}\cdots\hat{u}_{2n}^{i_{2n}},\quad 0\leq i_{1},\dots,i_{2n}<p,

where

\hat{u}_{i}=-u_{n+i},\quad\hat{u}_{n+i}=u_{i}\quad\text{for }i=1,\dots,n.

Moreover, since
[u_{i},\hat{u}_{j}]=\delta_{i,j}
, we have

\operatorname{ad}(u_{l})(\hat{u}_{1}^{i_{1}}\hat{u}_{2}^{i_{2}}\cdots\hat{u}_{2n}^{i_{2n}})=i_{l}\,\hat{u}_{1}^{i_{1}}\dots\hat{u}_{l}^{i_{l}-1}\cdots\hat{u}_{2n}^{i_{2n}}.

Hence,
\operatorname{ad}(u_{l})
acts as the formal derivation with respect to
\hat{u}_{l}
.

This naturally leads us to consider the
k
-linear bijection

\psi\colon A_{n}(k)\longrightarrow S=k[y_{1},\dots,y_{2n}],

defined by

\psi\!\left(f(z_{1}^{p},\dots,z_{2n}^{p})\,\hat{u}_{1}^{i_{1}}\hat{u}_{2}^{i_{2}}\cdots\hat{u}_{2n}^{i_{2n}}\right)=f(y_{1}^{p},\dots,y_{2n}^{p})\,y_{1}^{i_{1}}y_{2}^{i_{2}}\cdots y_{2n}^{i_{2n}},

where, under this correspondence, the formal derivation

\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{i}}
on the right-hand side matches the operator

\operatorname{ad}(u_{i})
on the left-hand side.

Consider now the de Rham complex of the polynomial ring

S=k[y_{1},\dots,y_{2n}]
, which is the differential complex

0\longrightarrow S\xrightarrow{\,d\,}\Omega^{1}_{S/k}\xrightarrow{\,d\,}\Omega^{2}_{S/k}\xrightarrow{\,d\,}\cdots\xrightarrow{\,d\,}\Omega^{2n}_{S/k}\longrightarrow 0,

where

\Omega^{1}_{S/k}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{2n}S\,dy_{i}

denotes the module of Kähler differentials, and

\Omega^{l}_{S/k}=\wedge^{l}_{S}\Omega^{1}_{S/k},\quad l=1,\dots,2n.

The differential
d
is
k
-linear and is given by

d\!\left(f\,dy_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge dy_{i_{l}}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{2n}\frac{\partial f}{\partial y_{i}}\,dy_{i}\wedge dy_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge dy_{i_{l}}.

The cohomology of this complex is called the de Rham cohomology of
S
.
We denote its degree-
l
part by
H^{l}_{\mathrm{dR}}(S/k)
.
Since the field
k
has characteristic
p>0
, the de Rham cohomology of
S

is non-trivial. More precisely, for each
l=0,\dots,2n
, one has
(see, e.g.,
[
5
, Thm. 7.2]
)

H^{l}_{\mathrm{dR}}(S/k)\cong\bigoplus_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{l}\leq 2n}k[y_{1}^{p},\dots,y_{2n}^{p}]\,y_{i_{1}}^{p-1}\cdots y_{i_{l}}^{p-1}\,dy_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge dy_{i_{l}}.

As a special case, the second de Rham cohomology group is

H^{2}_{\mathrm{dR}}(S/k)\cong\bigoplus_{1\leq i<j\leq 2n}k[y_{1}^{p},\dots,y_{2n}^{p}]\,y_{i}^{p-1}y_{j}^{p-1}\,dy_{i}\wedge dy_{j}.

In particular,
H^{2}_{\mathrm{dR}}(S/k)
is a free module over the subring

k[y_{1}^{p},\dots,y_{2n}^{p}]
, generated by the classes of the differential
forms

y_{i}^{p-1}y_{j}^{p-1}\,dy_{i}\wedge dy_{j},\qquad 1\leq i<j\leq 2n.

Lemma 2.5
.

The
2
-form

\sum_{i<j}\psi(u_{ij})\,dy_{i}\wedge dy_{j}\in\Omega^{2}(S/k)

is closed.

Proof.

The closedness of this
2
-form is equivalent to the identities

\frac{\partial\psi(u_{ij})}{\partial y_{l}}+\frac{\partial\psi(u_{jl})}{\partial y_{i}}-\frac{\partial\psi(u_{il})}{\partial y_{j}}=0,

for all integers
i<j<l
.

Since
\operatorname{ad}(u_{i})
corresponds to the operator
\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{i}}

under the bijection
\psi
, this condition may equivalently be rewritten as

\operatorname{ad}(u_{l})(u_{ij})+\operatorname{ad}(u_{i})(u_{jl})-\operatorname{ad}(u_{j})(u_{il})=0,

which follows from the Jacobi identity

[u_{l},[u_{i},u_{j}]]+[u_{i},[u_{j},u_{l}]]-[u_{j},[u_{i},u_{l}]]=0

and the definition
u_{st}=[u_{s},u_{t}]
, when the calculation is performed in
A_{n}(W_{2}(k))
.
∎

Theorem 2.6
.

There exist elements
v_{1},\dots,v_{2n}
in
A_{n}(k)
such that

u_{ij}+[u_{i},v_{j}]-[u_{j},v_{i}]=c_{ij}\,\hat{u}_{i}^{\,p-1}\hat{u}_{j}^{\,p-1},

where
c_{ij}
is the central element in
A_{n}(k)
from Theorem
2.4
, defined by

p\cdot c_{ij}=[u_{i}^{p},u_{j}^{p}]+p\cdot\omega_{ij}.

Moreover, the endomorphism
\varphi
admits a lift to a
W_{2}(k)
-algebra
endomorphism of
A_{n}(W_{2}(k))
if and only if

c_{ij}=0,\qquad i,j=1,\dots,2n.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.5
and the description of
H^{2}_{\mathrm{dR}}(S/k)
,
there exist polynomials
h_{1},\dots,h_{2n}\in S
and
h_{ij}\in k[y_{1}^{p},\dots,y_{2n}^{p}]
,
for
i,j=1,\dots,2n
, such that

\sum_{i<j}\psi(u_{ij})\,dy_{i}\wedge dy_{j}=d\Big(\sum_{i}h_{i}\,dy_{i}\Big)+\sum_{i<j}h_{ij}\,y_{i}^{p-1}y_{j}^{p-1}\,dy_{i}\wedge dy_{j}.

Hence, for
i<j
, we have

\psi(u_{ij})=\frac{\partial h_{j}}{\partial y_{i}}-\frac{\partial h_{i}}{\partial y_{j}}+h_{ij}\,y_{i}^{p-1}y_{j}^{p-1},\quad i,j=1,\dots,2n.

Now choose elements
v_{i}\in A_{n}(k)
and central elements
\hat{c}_{ij}\in Z
such that

\psi(v_{i})=-h_{i},\quad\psi(\hat{c}_{ij})=h_{ij},\quad i,j=1,\dots,2n.

Since
\operatorname{ad}(u_{i})
corresponds to
\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{i}}
under
\psi
, we conclude

u_{ij}+[u_{i},v_{j}]-[u_{j},v_{i}]=\hat{c}_{ij}\,\hat{u}_{i}^{\,p-1}\hat{u}_{j}^{\,p-1},\quad i,j=1,\dots,2n,

when
i<j
.
Applying the operator

\operatorname{ad}(u_{i})^{\,p-1}\,\operatorname{ad}(u_{j})^{\,p-1}

to both sides, and using Theorem
2.4
, together with the facts that

\operatorname{ad}(u_{i})
and
\operatorname{ad}(u_{j})
commute, and that

\operatorname{ad}(u_{i})^{p}=\operatorname{ad}(u_{j})^{p}=0
,
we obtain

c_{ij}=\hat{c}_{ij},\quad i,j=1,\dots,2n,

for
i<j
. This proves the first statement.

It follows that if
c_{ij}=0
for all
i,j
, then

u_{ij}+[u_{i},v_{j}]-[u_{j},v_{i}]=0,\quad i,j=1,\dots,2n,

which by Lemma
2.1
implies that
\varphi
admits a lift.
Conversely, if
\varphi
admits a lift, then by Lemma
2.1

there exist elements
w_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,2n
, such that

u_{ij}+[u_{i},w_{j}]-[u_{j},w_{i}]=0,\quad i,j=1,\dots,2n.

Applying
\operatorname{ad}(u_{i})^{p-1}\operatorname{ad}(u_{j})^{p-1}
and using Theorem
2.4
again, we conclude

c_{ij}=0,\quad i,j=1,\dots,2n.

∎

2.2
Lift and Poisson morpisms

We consider the center
Z=k[x_{1},\dots,x_{2n}]
of the Weyl algebra
A_{n}(k)
as a Poisson algebra with the standard bracket

\{f,g\}=\sum_{l=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{l}}\frac{\partial g}{\partial x_{l+n}}-\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{l+n}}\frac{\partial g}{\partial x_{l}}\right),

or equivalently,

\{f,g\}=(\nabla f)^{T}\,\omega^{-1}\,(\nabla g),

where the gradient of
h\in Z
is defined by

\nabla h=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial h}{\partial x_{1}}\\[2.84526pt]
\vdots\\[2.84526pt]
\frac{\partial h}{\partial x_{2n}}\end{pmatrix},

and
\omega
is the matrix

\omega=\begin{pmatrix}0&-I_{n}\\[2.84526pt]
I_{n}&0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{Mat}_{2n}(k).

A
k
-algebra endomorphism
\phi:Z\to Z
is called a Poisson morphism if it preserves the bracket:

\phi(\{f,g\})=\{\phi(f),\phi(g)\},\quad\text{for all }f,g\in Z.

Equivalently, this condition can be expressed in terms of the Jacobian matrix

J_{\phi}=\Bigl(\frac{\partial\phi(x_{i})}{\partial x_{j}}\Bigr)

as

J_{\phi}\,\omega^{-1}\,J_{\phi}^{T}=\omega^{-1}.

(2.2.1)

Since
\omega
is invertible, we immediately see that
\det J_{\phi}=\pm 1
,
which implies that a Poisson morphism is étale. Furthermore, (
2.2.1
) is equivalent to the identity

J_{\phi}^{T}\,\omega\,J_{\phi}=\omega,

(2.2.2)

which expresses the invariance of the standard
2
-form

\sum_{l=1}^{n}dx_{l}\wedge dx_{l+n}

under
\phi
, that is,

\sum_{l=1}^{n}dx_{l}\wedge dx_{l+n}=\sum_{l=1}^{n}d\phi(x_{l})\wedge d\phi(x_{l+n}).

One may relate the above Poisson bracket on
Z
to commutators in

A_{n}(W_{2}(k))
. More precisely, if
f
and
g
are elements in

Z
then the commutator

[f,g]

in
A_{n}(W_{2}(k))
is contained in
p\cdot A_{n}(W_{2}(k))
.
Moreover
[f,g]
is a central element in
A_{n}(W_{2}(k))

by Lemma
1.1
, so there exists a
unique
h\in Z

such that

[f,g]=p\cdot h.

Actually

h=\{f,g\}

or in other words

\displaystyle\{f,g\}=\frac{[f,g]}{p}\in Z,

(2.2.3)

where the right hand side should be interpreted as explained
above. Actually the right hand side of (
2.2.3
) is
obviously a derivation in both entries, so for the identity
in (
2.2.3
) to be satisfied, it suffices that
it is satisfied, when
f
and
g
are both one of the
coordinate functions
x_{1},\dots,x_{2n}
. In this case,
the identity is a consequence of Theorem
2.6

applied to the case, where
\varphi
is the identity
morphism.

We now relate the remarks above to the morphism
\varphi
.
Let

J_{\varphi}=\left(\frac{\partial\varphi(x_{i})}{\partial x_{j}}\right)\in\mathrm{Mat}_{2n}(Z)

denote the Jacobian matrix associated with the restriction

\varphi_{Z}
of
\varphi
to the center
Z
, and let

C=(c_{ij})\in\mathrm{Mat}_{2n}(Z)

be the matrix whose
(i,j)
-entry is the element
c_{ij}\in Z

defined in Theorem
2.4
.

Theorem 2.7
.

One has

J_{\varphi}\,\omega^{-1}\,J_{\varphi}^{T}=\omega^{-1}+C.

In particular, the induced morphism
\varphi_{Z}
is a Poisson morphism
if and only if
\varphi
admits a lift to a
W_{2}(k)
-algebra
endomorphism of
A_{n}(W_{2}(k))
.

Proof.

The
(i,j)
-th entry of

J_{\varphi}\,\omega^{-1}\,J_{\varphi}^{T}

equals

\{\varphi(x_{i}),\varphi(x_{j})\},

which, by identity (
2.2.3
), equals

\frac{[\varphi(x_{i}),\varphi(x_{j})]}{p}.

By Theorem
2.4
, the latter equals

-\omega_{i,j}+c_{ij},

which coincides with the
(i,j)
-th entry of

\omega^{-1}+C.

This proves the first part of the claimed statement. In particular, by the discussion above,
\varphi_{Z}
is a Poisson morphism if and only if
C
is the zero matrix. Moreover, by Theorem
2.6
,
C=0
if and only if
\varphi
admits a lift to a
W_{2}(k)
-algebra endomorphism of
A_{n}(W_{2}(k))
.
∎

3
Tsuchimoto’s approach and its refinements

For completeness, we include here a description of Tsuchimoto’s approach
in
[
9
]
.
Most of this is contained in
Tsuchimoto’s paper, but the setup there,
involving connections on vector bundles, is slightly different
from the one presented below.

3.1
A trivialization of
A_{n}(k)

The Weyl algebra
A_{n}(k)
is an Azumaya algebra and as such
it has a trivialization. We will work with a specific
trivialization which will be described in the following.

Recall that the center
Z
of
A_{n}(k)
, is a
polynomial ring over
k
in
2n
variables, generated by

x_{i}:=z_{i}^{p},\qquad i=1,\dots,2n.

The polynomial ring
S=k[y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{2n}]

in
2n
variables will in the following be viewed
as a faithfully flat
extension of
Z
via the identifications

y_{i}^{p}=z_{i}^{p},\qquad i=1,\dots,2n.

Then
A_{n}(k)\otimes_{Z}S
is
isomorphic to a matrix algebra
\mathrm{Mat}_{p^{n}}(S)
over

S
. More precisely, let
M
denote the

S
-module

M=S[T_{1},T_{2},\dots,T_{n}]/(T_{1}^{p},T_{2}^{p},\dots,T_{n}^{p}).

Then
M
comes with two types of natural
S
-linear
endomorphisms; multiplication by
T_{i}
, denoted
by
\nu_{i}
, and formal derivative with respect to
T_{i}
,
denoted by
\nu_{i+n}
. Then

\displaystyle A_{n}(k)\otimes_{Z}S

\displaystyle\rightarrow\operatorname{End}_{S}(M),

\displaystyle z_{i}

\displaystyle\mapsto\nu_{i}+y_{i},

defines an explicit isomorphism of
S
-algebras

[
8
, Lemma 5]
. From now on, we identify
A_{n}(k)
with a subset
of
\operatorname{End}_{S}(M)
by this isomorphism.

3.2
A_{n}(k)
-linear endomorphisms of
M

The
k
-algebra
\operatorname{End}_{Z}(M)
of
Z
-linear endomorphisms of
M
plays an important role
in describing the endomorphisms of
A_{n}(k)
. In this section, we study certain special
elements of
\operatorname{End}_{Z}(M)
. Recall that
A_{n}(k)
is viewed as a subalgebra of
\operatorname{End}_{S}(M)
,
which in turn is a subalgebra of
\operatorname{End}_{Z}(M)
.

Let
\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{i}}

denote formal partial derivatives with respect to

y_{i}
regarded as an element in
\operatorname{End}_{Z}(M)
.
Define
Z
-linear
endomorphisms

L_{i}=\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{i}}+\hat{\nu}_{i},

for
i=1,2,\dots,2n
, where

\hat{\nu}_{i}=\begin{cases}-\nu_{i+n},&\text{if $i=1,\dots,n$,}\\
\nu_{i-n},&\text{if $i=n+1,\dots,2n$.}\end{cases}

Note that

[\hat{\nu}_{i},\nu_{j}]=-\delta_{i,j}.

In particular,

[L_{i},z_{j}]=[\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{i}}+\hat{\nu}_{i},\nu_{i}+y_{i}]=0,

for all
i,j=1,\dots,2n
, and
hence each
L_{i}
is an element
\operatorname{End}_{A_{n}(k)}(M)
.

Remark 3.1
.

In fact, the elements
L_{1},\dots,L_{2n}
, together with
S
, generate

\operatorname{End}_{A_{n}(k)}(M)
. Moreover, the centralizer of

\operatorname{End}_{A_{n}(k)}(M)
in
\operatorname{End}_{Z}(M)
equals
A_{n}(k)
. Since this result will not be
needed here, we omit the proof.

3.3
Endomorphisms of
A_{n}(k)

Let
\varphi
denote a
k
-algebra endomorphism of
A_{n}(k)
.
As explained in Section
1
, the center
Z
is preserved by
\varphi
.
Thus there is a natural extension of
\varphi_{Z}
to a
k
-algebra endomorphism of
S
.
Combining this with the identification

\operatorname{End}_{S}(M)\simeq A_{n}(k)\otimes_{Z}S,

we obtain an induced
k
-algebra endomorphism of
\operatorname{End}_{S}(M)
,
which, by abuse of notation, we also denote by
\varphi
.

Once we fix an
S
-basis of
M
, we may identify
\operatorname{End}_{S}(M)

with the matrix ring
\mathrm{Mat}_{p^{n}}(S)
and
\varphi
with an
endomorphism of
\mathrm{Mat}_{p^{n}}(S)
. Here we fix the monomial
basis

T_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots T_{n}^{i_{n}},\qquad 0\leq i_{1},\dots,i_{n}<p,

of
M
. In this basis
\nu_{i}
, for
i=1,\dots,2n
, have matrix
representations with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
. Therefore,
by the lemma below, there exist an invertible element

G\in\operatorname{End}_{S}(M)
such that

\varphi(z_{i})=G\cdot\nu_{i}\cdot G^{-1}+\overline{y_{i}},\qquad i=1,\dots,2n,

where we use the notation
\overline{r}:=\varphi(r)\in S
whenever
r\in S
.

This following lemma
should be well-known; however, since we were unable to find
a precise reference, we provide a proof below.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
R
be a unique factorization domain, and
let
\phi:\mathrm{Mat}_{m}(R)\rightarrow\mathrm{Mat}_{m}(R)
be a ring homomorphism. Then there exists
an invertible matrix
G\in\mathrm{Mat}_{m}(R)
and a ring endomorphism

\phi_{c}:R\rightarrow R
such that

\phi\big((a_{ij})\big)=G\cdot\big(\phi_{c}(a_{ij})\big)\cdot G^{-1},

for all
(a_{ij})\in\mathrm{Mat}_{m}(R)
.
Moreover, the matrix
G
is
unique up to multiplication by a units in
R
; equivalently its image in
\operatorname{PGL}_{m}(R)

is uniquely determined.

Proof.

Let
E_{ij}
, for
1\leq i,j\leq m
, denote the
(i,j)
-th standard matrix
unit in
\mathrm{Mat}_{m}(R)
and let
F_{ij}=\phi(E_{ij})
. Then
\sum_{i}F_{ii}=1
,
so in particular
F_{ii}\neq 0
for some
i
. Since
F_{ii}=F_{i1}\,F_{11}\,F_{1i}
, we must also have
F_{11}\neq 0
. Fix
a nonzero
{\bf r}=(r_{1},\dots,r_{m})\in F_{11}R^{m}
such that the
entries
r_{1},r_{2},\dots,r_{m}\in R
have no nontrivial common divisors. This is possible,
since if
{\bf r}=\beta\,{\bf r}^{\prime}
, for
{\bf r}^{\prime}\in R^{m}
and

\beta\in R
, then
{\bf r}^{\prime}=F_{11}{\bf r}^{\prime}
. For
i=1,\dots,m,
set
{\bf v}_{i}=F_{i1}{\bf r}
. Then we have the relations

F_{ij}\,{\bf v}_{l}=\delta_{jl}\cdot{\bf v}_{i}.

We claim that the vector
{\bf v}_{1},{\bf v}_{2},\dots,{\bf v}_{m}

form a basis for the free
R
-module
R^{m}
. First, linear
independence follows as

F_{1i}\,(\alpha_{1}\cdot{\bf v}_{1}+\alpha_{2}\cdot{\bf v}_{2}+\cdots+\alpha_{m}\cdot{\bf v}_{m})=\alpha_{i}\cdot{\bf r},

valid for all
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}\in R
. Next, let
{\bf v}\in R^{m}
. Since
the
m+1
elements
{\bf v}_{1},{\bf v}_{2},\dots,{\bf v}_{m},{\bf v}
are linearly dependent over
R
, there exists a nonzero element
c\in R
such that

c\cdot{\bf v}=\beta_{1}\cdot{\bf v}_{1}+\beta_{2}\cdot{\bf v}_{2}+\cdots+\beta_{m}\cdot{\bf v}_{m},

for some
\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\dots,\beta_{m}\in R
. Applying
F_{1i}

to both sides yields
c\cdot F_{1i}{\bf v}=\beta_{i}\cdot{\bf r}
. By the choice of
{\bf r}
, it follows that

c
divides each
\beta_{i}
.
Hence
{\bf v}
lies in the
R
-span of
{\bf v}_{1},{\bf v}_{2},\dots,{\bf v}_{m}
, proving
that these vectors form a basis of
R^{m}
.

Let
G\in\mathrm{Mat}_{m}(R)
denote the matrix whose columns are
{\bf v}_{1},{\bf v}_{2},\dots,{\bf v}_{m}
. Then
F_{ij}=G\cdot E_{ij}\cdot G^{-1}

as both sides of the equation agree on the basis
{\bf v}_{1},{\bf v}_{2},\dots,{\bf v}_{m}
. Now recall that the centralizer of the set
\{E_{ij}\}
equals
the center of
\mathrm{Mat}_{m}(R)
. Since
conjugation preserves centralizers, the centralizer of the set
\{F_{ij}\}

is therefore also equal to the center of
\mathrm{Mat}_{m}(R)
. Consequently, the map

\phi
must map the center of
\mathrm{Mat}_{m}(R)
to itself; that is
\phi(\alpha\cdot{\rm I})
, for
\alpha\in R
, must be of the form
\beta\cdot{\rm I}
, for some
\beta\in R
. This allows us to define

\phi_{c}
by
\phi_{c}(\alpha)=\beta
. With his notation, we
obtain

\phi\big((a_{ij})\big)=G\cdot\big(\phi_{c}(a_{ij})\big)\cdot G^{-1},

as desired. In particular,
G
must satisfy

\phi(E_{ij})=G\cdot E_{ij}\cdot G^{-1},

which implies that
G
is determined up to multiplication by an unit in the centralizer
of the set
\{E_{ij}\}
; that is up to a unit in
R
.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

Let
n=1
and consider the trace in
\operatorname{End}_{S}(M)
of an element of the form

\nu_{1}^{i_{1}}\nu_{2}^{i_{2}},\quad 0\leq i_{1},i_{2}<p.

It is a simple exercise to see that

\operatorname{tr}(\nu_{1}^{i_{1}}\nu_{2}^{i_{2}})=-\delta_{i_{1},p-1}\,\delta_{i_{2},p-1}.

Since trace is invariant under conjugation by
G
, we therefore also have

\operatorname{tr}\big(\varphi(z_{1})^{i_{1}}\varphi(z_{2})^{i_{2}}\big)=\operatorname{tr}\big((\nu_{1}+\overline{y_{1}})^{i_{1}}(\nu_{2}+\overline{y_{2}})^{i_{2}}\big)=-\delta_{i_{1},p-1}\,\delta_{i_{2},p-1}.

For general
n
, we may consider
M
as an
n
-fold tensor product of the case
n=1

above. Therefore

\operatorname{tr}\big(\varphi(z_{1})^{i_{1}}\cdots\varphi(z_{2n})^{i_{2n}}\big)=(-1)^{n}\,\delta_{i_{1},p-1}\cdots\delta_{i_{2n},p-1},\quad 0\leq i_{1},\dots,i_{2n}<p.

As mentioned in Section
1
, elements of the form

\varphi(z_{1})^{i_{1}}\cdots\varphi(z_{2n})^{i_{2n}},\quad 0\leq i_{1},\dots,i_{2n}<p,

constitute a basis for
A_{n}(k)
as a module over
Z
.
When writing an element
u\in A_{n}(k)
in this basis, it follows that
(-1)^{n}\operatorname{tr}(u)
is the coefficient of the basis element

\varphi(z_{1})^{p-1}\cdots\varphi(z_{2n})^{p-1}.

Hence,

\left(\operatorname{ad}(\varphi(z_{1}))^{p-1}\cdots\operatorname{ad}(\varphi(z_{2n}))^{p-1}\right)(u)=(-1)^{n}\operatorname{tr}(u),

so that the operator

\operatorname{ad}(\varphi(z_{1}))^{p-1}\cdots\operatorname{ad}(\varphi(z_{2n}))^{p-1}

does not depend on
\varphi
. In particular,

\operatorname{ad}(\varphi(z_{1}))^{p-1}\cdots\operatorname{ad}(\varphi(z_{2n}))^{p-1}=\operatorname{ad}(z_{1})^{p-1}\cdots\operatorname{ad}(z_{2n})^{p-1}.

3.4
Differential equations

We fix an endomorphism
\varphi
of
A_{n}(k)
and
the associated notation as above.

Proposition 3.4
.

For
i=1,\dots,2n
, the elements

L_{\varphi,i}=G\,\Biggl(\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{i}}+\sum_{l=1}^{2n}\frac{\partial\overline{y}_{l}}{\partial y_{i}}\hat{\nu}_{l}\Biggr)\,G^{-1},

in
\operatorname{End}_{Z}(M)
belong to
\operatorname{End}_{A_{n}(k)}(M)
.

Proof.

As noted in Section
1
, the algebra

A_{n}(k)
is generated as a
Z
-algebra by

\varphi(z_{1}),\dots,\varphi(z_{n}),\varphi(\partial_{1}),\dots,\varphi(\partial_{n}).

Hence if an element in
\operatorname{End}_{Z}(M)
is
\varphi(A_{n}(k))
-linear, then it is also

A_{n}(k)
-linear.
Therefore
it suffices to show that
all
L_{\varphi,i}
commute
with

\varphi(z_{1}),\dots,\varphi(z_{n}),\varphi(\partial_{1}),\dots,\varphi(\partial_{n}).

For
j=1,\dots,2n
we have

\varphi(z_{j})=G\cdot(\nu_{j}+\overline{y_{j}})\cdot G^{-1},

and a direct computation shows

\left[\nu_{j}+\overline{y_{j}},\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{i}}+\sum_{l=1}^{n}\frac{\partial\overline{y}_{l}}{\partial y_{i}}\hat{\nu}_{l}\right]=0.

Since conjugation by
G
preserves commutators, it follows that

[\varphi(z_{j}),L_{\varphi,i}]=0

for
j=1,\dots,2n
, as required.
∎

Note that
L_{\varphi,i}
coincide with

L_{i}
, when
\varphi
is the identity morphism.
For a general endomorphism
\varphi
, the elements

L_{\varphi,i}

remain naturally related to
L_{i}
, as explained
in the following result.

Corollary 3.5
.

For
i=1,\dots,2n
, we have

[L_{\varphi,i},y_{j}]=\delta_{i,j}.

In particular, there exist elements
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{2n}\in S
such that

L_{\varphi,i}=L_{i}+\gamma_{i},

for
i=1,\dots,2n
.

Proof.

The commutator relations follow directly from the definition

L_{\varphi,i}
, since
G
and
\hat{\nu}_{i}

all commute with
S
. By the observation preceding the corollary,
analogous relations hold for
L_{i}
.
Hence, the differences
L_{\varphi,i}-L_{i}

commute with all
y_{j}
, for
j=1,\dots,2n
, and thus lie in
\operatorname{End}_{S}(M)
.
As
\operatorname{End}_{S}(M)
is generated by
A_{n}(k)
and
S
,
it follows from Proposition
3.4
that these differences
are elements of the center
S
of
\operatorname{End}_{S}(M)
, which proves the existence
of
\gamma_{i}
..
∎

An important insight of Tsuchimoto is that the
p
-power map on

\operatorname{End}_{S}(M)
determines the elements
\gamma_{i}

appearing in Corollary
3.5
by a formula that does not involve
G
.
To explain this, we first recall the following result due to N. Jacobson
(
[
4
, Chapter V, §7]
).

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
R
be a
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
-algebra, and let

C\subseteq R
be a commutative subalgebra.
Let
a,b\in R
with
b\in C
, and assume that

\operatorname{ad}(a)(C)\subseteq C
. Then

(a+b)^{p}=a^{p}+b^{p}+\operatorname{ad}(a)^{p-1}(b).

As a consequence we obtain the following result.

Proposition 3.7
.

For
i=1,\dots,n
, we have the following identities in
S
:

L_{\varphi,i}^{p}=p-\frac{\partial^{{}^{\prime}}}{\partial y_{i}^{{}^{\prime}}}\Biggl(\sum_{l=1}^{n}\frac{\partial\overline{y}_{l}}{\partial y_{i}}\,\overline{y}_{l+n}\Biggr).

Here, the right-hand side should be understood as the
(p-1)
-st partial derivative
of the polynomial inside the parentheses.

Proof.

Set

a:=\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{i}}+\sum_{l=n+1}^{2n}\frac{\partial\overline{y}_{l}}{\partial y_{i}}\hat{\nu}_{l},\qquad b:=\sum_{l=1}^{n}\frac{\partial\overline{y}_{l}}{\partial y_{i}}\hat{\nu}_{l}.

Applying Lemma
3.6
with
C\subseteq\operatorname{End}_{Z}(M)

being the commutative subalgebra generated by
S
and

\hat{\nu}_{1},\dots,\hat{\nu}_{n}
gives

(a+b)^{p}=a^{p}+\operatorname{ad}(a)^{p-1}(b),

since
b^{p}=0
. Next, write

a^{\prime}:=\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{i}},\qquad b^{\prime}:=\sum_{l=n+1}^{2n}\frac{\partial\overline{y}_{l}}{\partial y_{i}}\hat{\nu}_{l}

and apply Lemma
3.6
again with the commutative subalgebra generated by

S
and
\hat{\nu}_{n+1},\dots,\hat{\nu}_{2n}
, yielding

a^{p}=(a^{\prime})^{p}+(b^{\prime})^{p}+\operatorname{ad}(a^{\prime})^{p-1}(b^{\prime})=\sum_{l=n+1}^{2n}\frac{\partial^{{}^{\prime}}\overline{y}_{l}}{\partial y_{i}^{{}^{\prime}}}\hat{\nu}_{l}=0,

since
(a^{\prime})^{p}=(b^{\prime})^{p}=0
. Hence

(a+b)^{p}=\operatorname{ad}(a)^{p-1}(b).

Now decompose

b=b_{1}+b_{2},\quad b_{1}:=\sum_{l=1}^{n}\frac{\partial\overline{y}_{l}}{\partial y_{i}}\Bigl(\hat{\nu}_{l}-\overline{y}_{n+l}\Bigr),\qquad b_{2}:=\sum_{l=1}^{n}\frac{\partial\overline{y}_{l}}{\partial y_{i}}\,\overline{y}_{n+l}.

Since
[\hat{\nu}_{n+j},\hat{\nu}_{l}]=\delta_{j,l}
, for

j,l=1,\dots,n
, we find

\operatorname{ad}(a)\Bigl(\hat{\nu}_{l}-\overline{y}_{n+l}\Bigr)=0,

and thus

\operatorname{ad}(a)^{p-1}(b_{1})=\sum_{l=1}^{n}\operatorname{ad}(a)^{p-1}\Bigl(\frac{\partial\overline{y}_{l}}{\partial y_{i}}\Bigr)\Bigl(\hat{\nu}_{l}-\overline{y}_{n+l}\Bigr)=\sum_{l=1}^{n}\frac{\partial^{{}^{\prime}}\overline{y}_{l}}{\partial y_{i}^{{}^{\prime}}}\Bigl(\hat{\nu}_{l}-\overline{y}_{n+l}\Bigr)=0.

Therefore,

(a+b)^{p}=\operatorname{ad}(a)^{p-1}(b_{2})=p-\frac{\partial^{{}^{\prime}}}{\partial y_{i}^{{}^{\prime}}}\Biggl(\sum_{l=1}^{n}\frac{\partial\overline{y}_{l}}{\partial y_{i}}\,\overline{y}_{n+l}\Biggr).

Finally, since
L_{\varphi,i}=G(a+b)G^{-1}
and
G
commutes with
S
, we obtain

L_{\varphi,i}^{p}=G(a+b)^{p}G^{-1}=p-\frac{\partial^{{}^{\prime}}}{\partial y_{i}^{{}^{\prime}}}\Biggl(\sum_{l=1}^{n}\frac{\partial\overline{y}_{l}}{\partial y_{i}}\,\overline{y}_{n+l}\Biggr),

as claimed.
∎

Lemma
3.6
also implies the following result.

Proposition 3.8
.

For
i=1,\dots,2n
and
\gamma\in S
, we have

(L_{\varphi,i}+\gamma)^{p}=L_{\varphi,i}^{p}+\gamma^{p}+p-\frac{\partial^{{}^{\prime}}\gamma}{\partial y_{i}^{{}^{\prime}}}.

Proof.

The identity follows by applying Corollary
3.5
and
Lemma
3.6
with
C=S
,
a=L_{\varphi,i}
, and
b=\gamma
.
∎

Corollary 3.9
.

The elements
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{2n}

described in Corollary
3.5
satisfy
the following differential equations:

p-\frac{\partial^{{}^{\prime}}}{\partial y_{i}^{{}^{\prime}}}\Biggl(\sum_{l=1}^{n}\frac{\partial\overline{y}_{l}}{\partial y_{i}}\,\overline{y}_{n+l}\Biggr)=\gamma_{i}^{p}+p-\frac{\partial^{{}^{\prime}}\gamma_{i}}{\partial y_{i}^{{}^{\prime}}},

Moreover, these differential equations uniquely determine

\gamma_{i}
as elements of
S
.

Proof.

Recall that
L_{i}
coincides with
L_{\varphi,i}
when
\varphi

is the identity morphism. Thus, combining
Proposition
3.7

and Proposition
3.8

(in the case where
\varphi
is the identity morphism) yields

(L_{i}+\gamma_{i})^{p}=\gamma_{i}^{p}+p-\frac{\partial^{{}^{\prime}}\gamma_{i}}{\partial y_{i}^{{}^{\prime}}}.

But
L_{\varphi,i}=L_{i}+\gamma_{i}
,
so the claimed differential equations follow by another application
of Proposition
3.7

(now for the morphism
\varphi
).

A degree argument in
S
shows that the homogeneous equation

\gamma^{p}+p-\frac{\partial^{{}^{\prime}}\gamma}{\partial y_{i}^{{}^{\prime}}}=0

only admits the trivial solution
\gamma=0
in
S
.
Hence
\gamma_{i}
is uniquely determined.
∎

3.5
Conditions for preserving the Poisson structure

In this subsection, we discuss bounds on the characteristic

p
that ensure that the induced morphism
\varphi_{Z}
of the
center is a Poisson morphism.
We use the notation and remarks introduced in Section
2.2
. The main goal of this section
is to restate and slightly strengthen a result of Tsuchimoto
(
[
9
, Cor. 3.3]
).

Theorem 3.10
.

Let
\varphi
be a
k
-algebra endomorphism of
A_{n}(k)

as in the preceding subsections. If

\deg(\varphi(z_{l}))+\deg(\varphi(z_{n+l}))<2p

for
l=1,\dots,n
, then the restriction
\varphi_{Z}

of
\varphi
to the center
Z
is a Poisson morphism.
In particular, the restriction
\varphi_{Z}
is étale and preserves
the standard
2
-form on
Z
.

We will prove this result below. Tsuchimoto proves this result
under the stronger assumptions

\deg(\varphi(z_{l}))<p/2,\quad l=1,\dots,2n.

Notice that the streghthen version
also applies in case

\deg(\varphi(z_{l}))<p,\quad l=1,\dots,2n.

The following example says that the streghthen version is
optimal in some sense.

Example 3.11
.

Let
\varphi
denote an endomorphism of the first Weyl
algebra
A_{1}(k)
of the form

\varphi(z_{1})=z_{1},\qquad\varphi(z_{2})=z_{2}+z_{2}^{p}z^{i}_{1},

for some integer
0\leq i\leq p-1
. Then by Lemma
3.6

\varphi(z_{1}^{p})=z_{1}^{p},\qquad\varphi(z_{2})=z_{2}^{p}(1-\delta_{i,p-1})+z_{2}^{p^{2}}z_{1}^{ip}.

It follows that
\varphi
is étale on the center if and only
if
i<p-1
. Note that the case
i=p-1
is not within reach
of Theorem
3.10
since

\deg(\varphi(z_{1}))+\deg(\varphi(z_{2}))=p+1+i.

Notice that when
i=0
then

\Phi(z_{1})=z_{1}-p\cdot z_{2}^{p-1}z_{1},\quad\Phi(z_{2})=z_{2}+z_{2}^{p},

defines a lifting of
\varphi
to an endomorphism

\Phi
of
A_{n}(W_{2}(k))
.
For
0<i<p-1
, liftings also exist, although they are more complicated.

Recall that by Corollary
3.5
there exist elements

\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{2n}\in S
such that

L_{\varphi,i}=L_{i}+\gamma_{i},\qquad i=1,\dots,2n.

Let
J_{\gamma}
denote the matrix

J_{\gamma}=\Bigl(\frac{\partial\gamma_{i}}{\partial y_{j}}\Bigr)\in\mathrm{Mat}_{2n}(S),

and let
\hat{J}_{\varphi}
denote the Jacobian matrix of the morphism induced by
\varphi
on
S
:

\hat{J}_{\varphi}=\Bigl(\frac{\partial\overline{y_{i}}}{\partial y_{j}}\Bigr).

Corollary 3.12
.

For
i,j=1,\dots,2n
, the following commutation relation holds:

[L_{\varphi,i},L_{\varphi,j}]=\sum_{l=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\partial\overline{y}_{n+l}}{\partial y_{i}}\frac{\partial\overline{y}_{l}}{\partial y_{j}}-\frac{\partial\overline{y}_{l}}{\partial y_{i}}\frac{\partial\overline{y}_{n+l}}{\partial y_{j}}\right).

In particular,

\hat{J}_{\varphi}^{T}\,\omega\,\hat{J}_{\varphi}=\omega+J_{\gamma}^{T}-J_{\gamma}.

Proof.

Using that

[\hat{\nu}_{s},\hat{\nu}_{t}]=\omega_{s,t},\qquad s,t=1,\dots,2n,

a straightforward computation shows that

\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{i}}+\sum_{l=1}^{2n}\frac{\partial\overline{y}_{l}}{\partial y_{i}}\hat{\nu}_{l},\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{j}}+\sum_{l=1}^{2n}\frac{\partial\overline{y}_{l}}{\partial y_{j}}\hat{\nu}_{l}\right]=\sum_{l=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\partial\overline{y}_{n+l}}{\partial y_{i}}\frac{\partial\overline{y}_{l}}{\partial y_{j}}-\frac{\partial\overline{y}_{l}}{\partial y_{i}}\frac{\partial\overline{y}_{n+l}}{\partial y_{j}}\right).

Hence,

[L_{\varphi,i},L_{\varphi,j}]=\sum_{l=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\partial\overline{y}_{n+l}}{\partial y_{i}}\frac{\partial\overline{y}_{l}}{\partial y_{j}}-\frac{\partial\overline{y}_{l}}{\partial y_{i}}\frac{\partial\overline{y}_{n+l}}{\partial y_{j}}\right),

since
G
commutes with
S
. It follows that

[L_{\varphi,i},L_{\varphi,j}]
is the
(i,j)
-th entry of the matrix

\hat{J}_{\varphi}^{T}\,\omega\,\hat{J}_{\varphi}
.
Applying this conclusion to the case where
\varphi
is the
identity morphism gives

[L_{i},L_{j}]=\omega_{i,j}.

Finally, since
L_{\varphi,i}=L_{i}+\gamma_{i}
, we have

[L_{\varphi,i},L_{\varphi,j}]=[L_{i}+\gamma_{i},L_{j}+\gamma_{j}]=[L_{i},L_{j}]+[L_{i},\gamma_{j}]-[L_{j},\gamma_{i}]=\omega_{i,j}+\frac{\partial\gamma_{j}}{\partial y_{i}}-\frac{\partial\gamma_{i}}{\partial y_{j}},

which yields the final identity stated in the corollary.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
3.10
.

Proof.

The stated assumption is equivalent to the requirement that

\deg(\overline{y}_{l})+\deg(\overline{y}_{n+l})<2p

for each
l=1,\dots,n
, where we use the standard degree
on the polynomial ring
S
. It follows that the left-hand side

p-\frac{\partial^{{}^{\prime}}}{\partial y_{i}^{{}^{\prime}}}\Biggl(\sum_{l=1}^{n}\frac{\partial\overline{y}_{l}}{\partial y_{i}}\,\overline{y}_{n+l}\Biggr)

of the differential equation defining
\gamma_{i}
in
Corollary
3.9
has degree strictly less than
p
.
On the other hand, the right-hand side

\gamma_{i}^{p}+p-\frac{\partial^{{}^{\prime}}\gamma_{i}}{\partial y_{i}^{{}^{\prime}}}

has degree at least
p
unless
\deg(\gamma_{i})\leq 0
.
In particular,
\gamma_{i}
must be a constant in
S
, and hence
the matrix
J_{\gamma}
is zero.

It now follows from Corollary
3.12
that

\hat{J}_{\varphi}^{T}\,\omega\,\hat{J}_{\varphi}=\omega.

Let
J_{\varphi}
denote the Jacobian matrix associated with the restriction

\varphi_{Z}
of
\varphi
to the center. Then

J_{\varphi}=\hat{J}_{\varphi}^{[p]}=\left(\left(\frac{\partial\overline{y}_{i}}{\partial y_{j}}\right)^{p}\right),

and hence

J_{\varphi}^{T}\,\omega\,J_{\varphi}=\omega.

The desired conclusion now follows from the discussion in Section
2.2
.
∎

Remark 3.13
.

In the case
n=1
, we only need
c_{12}
to be zero in order to lift

\varphi
to an endomorphism of
A_{n}(W_{2}(k))
. Recall that

c_{12}=\operatorname{ad}(u_{1})^{p-1}\operatorname{ad}(u_{2})^{p-1}(u_{12}),

where
u_{1}=\varphi(z_{1})
and
u_{2}=\varphi(z_{2})
, and the commutator
of
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
in
A_{n}(W_{2}(k))
determines
u_{12}\in A_{n}(k)

by the identity

[u_{1},u_{2}]=1+p\cdot u_{12}.

By the defining relations in
A_{n}(W_{2}(k))
, we furthermore know that

\deg(u_{12})\leq\deg(u_{1})+\deg(u_{2})-2.

In particular, under the same assumptions as in Theorem
3.10
, we have

\deg(u_{12})<2p-2.

Hence

c_{12}=\operatorname{ad}(u_{1})^{p-1}\operatorname{ad}(u_{2})^{p-1}(u_{12})=\operatorname{ad}(z_{1})^{p-1}\operatorname{ad}(z_{2})^{p-1}(u_{12})=0,

where we have used the conclusion of Remark
3.3
.
It follows from Theorem
2.6
that
\varphi
admits a lift to

A_{n}(W_{2}(k))
. Therefore, when
n=1
, we do not need the differential
equations in Theorem
3.9
to conclude the statement of
Theorem
3.10
.

There are examples of endomorphisms
\varphi
which are not
Poisson morphisms on the center. Surprisingly, one may even find
examples where
\varphi
is an isomorphism, as the following
example from
[
2
]
illustrates.

Example 3.14
.

Consider the second Weyl algebra
A_{2}(k)
, generated by

z_{1},z_{2},z_{3},
and
z_{4}
, and the automorphism

\varphi
of
A_{2}(k)
defined by

\varphi(z_{1})=z_{1}+z_{2}^{p}z_{3}^{p-1},\quad\varphi(z_{i})=z_{i}\text{ for }i=2,3,4.

Then, by Lemma
3.6
,

\varphi(x_{1})=x_{1}+x_{2}^{p}x_{3}^{p-1}-x_{2},\quad\varphi(x_{i})=x_{i}\text{ for }i=2,3,4.

In particular,

c_{14}=\{\varphi(x_{1}),\varphi(x_{4})\}=-1,

which shows that
\varphi_{Z}
is not a Poisson morphism.
In this case, the only nonzero entries of the matrix
C
from Theorem
2.7

are
c_{41}=1
and
c_{14}=-1
. Moreover, one may check that

\gamma_{3}=y_{2}
, while
\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{2}=\gamma_{4}=0
.

In conclusion, it is worth mentioning that the matrix
C
introduced in
Section
2
(see Theorem
2.6
), which measures the
obstruction to lifting the endomorphism
\varphi
to a
W_{2}(k)
-algebra
endomorphism of
A_{n}(W_{2}(k))
, is closely related to the matrix

J_{\gamma}^{T}-J_{\gamma}
associated with the solutions of the differential
equations in Corollary
3.9
. These matrices are linked by the
identities

\displaystyle J_{\varphi}\,\omega^{-1}\,J_{\varphi}^{T}

\displaystyle=\omega^{-1}+C,

\displaystyle\hat{J}_{\varphi}^{T}\,\omega\,\hat{J}_{\varphi}

\displaystyle=\omega+J_{\gamma}^{T}-J_{\gamma},

\displaystyle J_{\varphi}

\displaystyle=\hat{J}_{\varphi}^{[p]},

derived in Theorem
2.7
and Corollary
3.12
.
Therefore, in general and in view of the discussion in
Section
2.2.1
, the matrix
C
vanishes if and only if

J_{\gamma}
is symmetric. Tsuchimoto’s result corresponds to the special
case where
J_{\gamma}
is the zero matrix. In general, however, we obtain
the following criterion.

Proposition 3.15
.

For
i=1,\dots,2n
, let
f_{i}\in Z=k[x_{1},\dots,x_{2n}]
be the unique
solutions of the differential equation

p-\frac{\partial^{{}^{\prime}}}{\partial x_{i}^{{}^{\prime}}}\Biggl(\sum_{l=1}^{n}\frac{\partial\varphi(x_{l})}{\partial x_{i}}\,\varphi(x_{n+l})\Biggr)=f^{p}+p-\frac{\partial^{{}^{\prime}}f}{\partial x_{i}^{{}^{\prime}}}.

Then
\varphi
admits a lifting to a
W_{2}(k)
-algebra endomorphism of

A_{n}(W_{2}(k))
if and only if

\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}=\frac{\partial f_{j}}{\partial x_{i}},\quad i,j=1,\dots,2n.

Proof.

We begin by observing that
f_{i}=\gamma_{i}^{p}
for
i=1,\dots,2n
.
Thus the matrix

J=\bigl(\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}\bigr)\in\mathrm{Mat}_{2n}(Z)

is related to
J_{\gamma}
by

J=J_{\gamma}^{[p]}=\Bigl(\left(\frac{\partial\gamma_{i}}{\partial y_{j}}\right)^{p}\Bigr).

It follows that
J
is symmetric if and only if
J_{\gamma}
is symmetric.
By the remarks preceding the proposition, this completes the proof.
∎

Notice that, with the notation
J=(\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial x_{j}})
as in the proof above, we have
the identities

\displaystyle J_{\varphi}\,\omega^{-1}\,J_{\varphi}^{T}

\displaystyle=\omega^{-1}+C,

\displaystyle J_{\varphi}^{T}\,\omega\,J_{\varphi}

\displaystyle=\omega+J^{T}-J.

4
Applications and concluding remarks

In
[
1
, page 2328]
it was conjectured that any endomorphism
\varphi
of

A_{n}(k)
is injective. This question is equivalent to the injectivity
of the induced map
\varphi_{Z}
on the center.
Tsuchimoto
[
8
, Lemma 17]
proved injectivity for
n=1
,
but later Makar-Limanov
[
7
, page 793]

gave an example of a non-injective endomorphism of
A_{2}(k)
of degree

p^{2}+p-1
.
Observe that any flat (and hence also étale) endomorphism of
Z
is
injective, since
Z
is an integral domain. Thus Theorem
3.10

establishes the injectivity of
\varphi

under the stated degree bounds, of which
\deg(\varphi)<p
is a special case.

In
[
6
, Theorem 2.7, Theorem 4.3]
, we proved that endomorphisms of Weyl algebras over fields of
characteristic zero are flat and that birational endomorphisms are automorphisms.
These results crucially depend on the fact that reductions of endomorphisms
of Weyl algebras to (large) positive characteristic induce étale endomorphisms on the
center. Theorem
3.10
gives explicit bounds on the characteristic
that guarantee this property. In particular, Theorem 2.7 (flatness) and Theorem 4.3 (birationality implies automorphism) in
[
6
]
hold
for endomorphisms
\varphi
of Weyl algebras over fields of characteristic
p>\deg{\varphi}
.
