Tetraspanin OsTET8 acts as a negative regulator of root development in rice
Authors: Ran Meng, Dong Chao, Xingxiang Chen, Jinge Liu, Ruiting Liu, Bocheng Gao, Baolei Zhang, Degui Zhou, Jixing Xia, Zhufeng Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Tetraspanins (TETs) are evolutionarily conserved transmembrane scaffolding proteins that play crucial roles in plant development, reproduction, and stress responses. However, their functions in rice root development remain poorly understood. Here, we characterized OsTET8, a rice TET family member, which displays root-specific expression in rice and predominantly localizes to the root elongation zone. Subcellular localization revealed that OsTET8 is dual-targeted to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and plasma membrane. OsTET8 knockout mutants exhibited significantly elongated primary roots, increased lateral root density, and enhanced adventitious root formation, whereas root-specific overexpression suppressed these characteristics. Furthermore, cytological analyses showed reduced root cross-sectional area and cortical cell layers in both knockout mutants and overexpression lines, indicating impaired cell proliferation. Transcriptomic profiling identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) involved in intracellular redox homeostasis and jasmonic acid (JA) biosynthesis/signaling, which were further validated by qRT-PCR. Together, our findings demonstrate that OsTET8 negatively regulates rice root development, potentially through its coordination of redox homeostasis and JA signaling pathways. This study provides new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying TET-mediated root development and highlights OsTET8 as a potential target for rice improvement.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12284-025-00860-x.

Introduction

Roots play a pivotal role in plant development by providing structural support to aerial organs, facilitating the uptake of water and nutrients essential for growth, and sensing environmental conditions such as water availability, nutrient levels, and the presence of toxic elements (Meng et al. 2019). As a monocot model plant, rice (Oryza sativa L.) possesses a typical fibrous root system comprising seminal, adventitious, and lateral roots, whose growth status directly influences shoot development and final grain yield (Coudert et al. 2010; Verma et al. 2022). Thus, identifying and characterizing genes involved in rice root development holds significant potential for improving rice breeding strategies and enhancing productivity.

The regulation of root development is governed by a complex interplay of hormonal signaling, environmental cues, and endogenous genetic networks. For example, auxin promotes root cell elongation and division (Mao et al. 2020); light and temperature modulate root development by influencing phytohormone biosynthesis and signal transduction pathways (van Gelderen et al. 2018; Gholizadeh et al. 2024); genes such as ABP1 and JAZ are key regulators of root cell division and elongation at specific developmental stages (Sauer and Kleine-Vehn 2011; Wang et al. 2023); and Class III peroxidase (PRX) genes affect cell wall structure and cell elongation by regulating intracellular redox homeostasis (Tan et al. 2025). Though several genes participating in rice root development have been identified, our understanding of this process is still far from complete, and the molecular mechanisms remain to be fully explored (Meng et al. 2019; Li et al. 2024b). Further investigation is needed to elucidate the genetic architecture and regulatory networks that govern root development in rice, which will facilitate the development of stress-resilient and high-yielding crop varieties (Meng et al. 2019).

Tetraspanins (TETs) are a class of small-molecule transmembrane scaffolding proteins that share similar topology including four transmembrane (TM) domains (TM1-4), two unequal-size extracellular loops (EC1 and EC2) with conserved amino acid residues and motifs, small intracellular loop and carboxy terminal tails (Wang et al. 2012; Lang and Hochheimer 2020). These proteins are evolutionarily highly conserved in eukaryotes and are widely found in animals, plants, and fungi except yeast. CD63 was the first characterized tetraspanin which identified in early-stage human melanoma cells (Hotta et al. 1988; Pols and Klumperman 2009). Subsequently, TETs have been found in various eukaryotes, such as social amoeba (Dictyostelium discoideum), fungi (Magnaporthe grisea, a plant pathogen), plants (Arabidopsis thaliana), animals (Drosophila melanogaster), and humans (Reimann et al. 2017). In animals, TETs are involved in a multitude of biological processes that imply cell adhesion, motility, invasion, or membrane fusion as well as signaling and protein trafficking (Andreu and Yáñez-Mó 2014). However, in contrast to the considerable advances in animal TETs, plant tetraspanin studies lag far behind, leaving their functional roles largely unexplored (Wang et al. 2012; Qin et al. 2024).

Currently, 17 TET family members have been identified in Arabidopsis thaliana and 15 in Oryza sativa (Table S1), with emerging evidence suggesting their involvement in diverse aspects of plant development and defense responses (Mani et al. 2015; Reimann et al. 2017; Qin et al. 2024). Among these, the most extensively studied plant TET gene, AtTET1, orchestrates diverse developmental processes ranging from root epidermal patterning and differentiation to leaf lamina symmetry establishment, venation patterning, shoot apical meristem peripheral zone specification, and megasporogenesis (Cnops et al. 2006; Chiu et al. 2007; Lieber et al. 2011). In contrast, AtTET13 specifically promotes primary root elongation and lateral root formation, while AtTET5 and AtTET6 act redundantly to inhibit cell proliferation, thereby coordinating growth in both roots and leaves (Wang et al. 2015). Furthermore, AtTET8 and AtTET9 form a functional complex that mediates host small RNA trafficking into fungal pathogens via extracellular vesicle secretion to silence pathogen virulence genes, whereas AtTET3 enhances viral infection and intercellular movement (Cai et al. 2018; Zhu et al. 2022). Recent research on rice TETs has revealed that OsTET5, OsTET6, OsTET9, and OsTET10 collectively regulate plant height and tillering, whereas OsTET13 modulates root growth by associating with the jasmonic acid (JA) signaling pathway (Qin et al. 2024). Additionally, OsTET5 enhances salt stress tolerance by coordinating ion and reactive oxygen species (ROS) homeostasis (Mani et al. 2025). Although plant TETs have been implicated in diverse cellular processes, development, and disease pathogenesis, their precise molecular functions remain to be fully elucidated.

In this study, we identified OsTET8 as a differentially expressed TET family member through transcriptome sequencing of OsMYB36a knockout mutant seedling roots, where its expression was significantly downregulated. OsTET8 exhibits a highly tissue-specific expression pattern, predominantly localized to the root elongation zone. Functional characterization demonstrated that knockout of OsTET8 leads to elongated primary roots and enhanced formation of lateral and adventitious roots, whereas its overexpression inhibits primary root elongation and lateral root development. Moreover, cytological analyses demonstrated that both OsTET8 mutant and overexpression lines exhibit a reduced root cross-sectional area and cortical cell layer number compared to wild-type plants. Transcriptomic profiling further suggested that loss-of-function or overexpression of OsTET8 alters the expression of genes associated with intracellular redox homeostasis and JA biosynthesis/ signaling pathways. Collectively, these findings indicate that OsTET8 negatively regulates rice root growth, likely by modulating genes involved in oxidative stress balance and JA-mediated signaling.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

The japonica rice variety Nipponbare was used for wild-type line and genetic transformation in this study. Subcellular localization studies were performed using protoplasts isolated from the indica rice variety 9311. For hydroponic culture, mature and uniformly sized seeds were selected and incubated in Petri dishes lined with moistened filter paper at 37 °C for the initial 48 h. Subsequently, the uniformly germinated seeds were transferred into half-strength Kimura B solution (pH 5.6) and maintained in a controlled rice growth chamber. The nutrient solution was refreshed every 48 h to prevent nutrient depletion. Seedlings were cultivated in a growth chamber set to 30 °C under a 10/14-h (day/night) photoperiod and a light intensity of 3,000 lx. The relative humidity was maintained at approximately 60%. For soil culture, mature and uniformly sized seeds were soaked in the dark at 37 °C and allowed to germinate until they turned white. Subsequently, the seeds were sown into 6-L plastic pots filled with paddy soil. Phenotypic characteristics were measured and documented during the natural growing season at Guangxi University.

Generation of transgenic lines

The sgRNA target sequence for generating the ostet8 CRISPR-Cas9 knockout vector was designed using the online tool CRISPR-GE (http://skl.scau.edu.cn/) (Ma et al. 2015). Its potential off-target effects were further assessed in silico using the CRISPR-P 2.0 platform (http://crispr.hzau.edu.cn/CRISPR2/) (Liu et al. 2017), which predicted an extremely low risk of off-target cleavage in the rice genome. Subsequently, the designed sgRNA expression cassettes were driven by the U6 promoter system and constructed via overlapping PCR, followed by modular assembly into the pYLCRISPR/Cas9Pubi-H backbone using Bsa I restriction sites (Ma et al. 2015; Zeng et al. 2018). All experiments were performed using Cas9-free mutant lines, which were verified by PCR screening for the absence of the Cas9 transgene in the T1 generation. For the overexpression construct, the RCc3 root-specific promoter was employed to regulate the expression of OsTET8-GFP fusion protein in the pCAMBIA1300 binary vector, with in-frame cloning verified by restriction enzyme digestion. The constructs were transformed into rice calli by using Agrobacterium tumefaciens-mediated transformation as previously described (Hiei et al. 1994). Transgenic lines were confirmed by PCR genotyping and Sanger DNA sequencing. All the primers used for making the constructs are listed in Table S2.

RNA extraction and quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from various plant tissues using Trizol reagent (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer's protocol, followed by treatment with DNase I (RNase-free) to remove residual genomic DNA contamination. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the HiScript® II QRT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). qRT-PCR was performed using the CFX Connect Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) with ChamQ™ SYBR® Color qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Transcript levels were normalized to the reference gene OsHistone H3, and relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primer sequence information for qRT-PCR analysis is available in Table S2.

Subcellular localization of the OsTET8 protein

To determine the subcellular localization of OsTET8, protoplasts were isolated from shoot tissues of 12-day-old etiolated rice seedlings grown in 1/2 MS medium and transformed via PEG 4000-mediated transfection (Cao et al. 2025). Briefly, protoplasts were prepared by digesting shoot tissues for 4 h in an enzyme solution containing 0.6 M mannitol, 20 mM MES (pH 5.7), 1.5% Cellulase R10, 0.75% Macerozyme R10, 20 mM KCl, 5% BSA, and 1 M CaCl2. The digested tissues were incubated in W5 solution (154 mM NaCl, 125 mM CaCl₂, 5 mM KCl, 2 mM MES, pH 5.7) for 1 h to facilitate protoplast release. For PEG transformation, 200 µL protoplast suspension in MMG solution (0.4 M mannitol, 15 mM MgCl2, 4 mM MES, pH 5.7) was combined with 10 µg plasmid DNA and 220 µL of PEG solution (40% PEG-4000, 0.6 M mannitol, 100 mM CaCl2). The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 20 min to allow DNA uptake. After transformation, protoplasts were washed twice with W5 solution and resuspended in fresh W5. Transfected protoplasts were incubated at 28 °C in darkness for 16-18 h to enable GFP expression. Fluorescence was detected using a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope (Leica, Weztlar, Germany) with 488 nm excitation and 500–550 nm emission detection.

Phenotypic analysis of roots

The germinated seeds were placed individually on mesh platforms suspended in 0.5 mM CaCl2 solution for hydroponic growth, with roots classified as primary roots, lateral roots (emerging from pre-existing root tissues) and adventitious roots (also called crown roots, which develop from the base of the hypocotyl) (Coudert et al. 2010; Meng et al. 2019). For primary root length analysis, digital images of roots were captured with a ruler placed alongside the root at 3 days post-germination (dpg). The root length was then measured from the base of the root to the root tip using image analysis software. For lateral root number quantification, at 7 dpg, microscopic observation was used to count the lateral roots within a 1 cm region from the root base. For adventitious root number quantification, at 15 dpg, all visible adventitious roots (length ≥ 0.5 cm) that germinated from the base of the hypocotyl were manually counted. Each group included 10 biological replicates.

Fresh primary roots from 3-day-old seedlings were fixed in 4% (w/v) agarose solution for histological analysis of cellular structures. Root segments were collected 5 mm from the root tips and sectioned transversely into 100-µm slices using a razor blade. Sections were immediately transferred to slides with deionized water, then dehydrated and mounted in 50% glycerol. Sections were examined and imaged using automated differential interference contrast (DIC) microscopy. Root tissue layers (epidermis, exodermis, sclerenchyma, cortex, endodermis, pericycle, and stele) were identified according to Coudert et al. (2010). For each root cross-sectional image, the number of cortical cell layers was counted manually, while root diameter and root cross-sectional area were measured using IMAGEJ software (NIH Image, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Endogenous JA measurement

WT, ostet8-30, and OE-11 seeds were soaked in deionized water for 2 days and subsequently germinated hydroponically in 0.5 mM CaCl2 for 3 days (28 °C). Roots were excised and ground to a fine powder in liquid nitrogen. The powdered samples were extracted twice with pre-chilled acetonitrile (HPLC grade) containing the internal standard D-JA (Sigma, St. Louis, USA). The combined extracts were purified using C18 and GCB, then dried under a gentle stream of nitrogen gas, reconstituted in 1 mL of methanol:water (1:1, v/v), and finally filtered through a 0.11 µm filter membrane prior to instrumental analysis. JA content was quantified using an ultra-performance liquid chromatography-tandem triple quadrupole mass spectrometry (UPLC-QqQ-MS/MS) system (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA). Three biological replicates were performed for each group.

RNA-seq and bioinformatics analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the roots of rice seedlings cultivated in hydroponic conditions at 15-day-old seedlings, with four biological replicates prepared for both WT plants, knockout mutants (ostet8-30), and overexpression lines (OE-11). Transcriptome sequencing was conducted by Novogene Inc. using an Illumina platform. The raw reads underwent quality control preprocessing to remove low-quality data and sequencing connectors, resulting in high-quality clean reads for further analysis (Table S3). These clean reads were then mapped to the MSU7 rice reference genome (http://rice.plantbiology.msu.edu/) and annotated using the 2.2.1 aligner (Kim et al. 2019). The FPKM (fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads) of each gene was calculated based on the read count and gene length. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 software (version 1.20.0), with genes assigned as differentially expressed (DEGs) if they met the criteria of padj ≤ 0.05 and |log2(fold change)|≥ 1. Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment analysis of the DEGs was carried out using clusterProfile (version 4.2.0) software, and the top 20 significant terms for cellular component (CC), molecular function (MF), and biological process (BP) were selected for histogram representation. If there were fewer than 20 terms, all were plotted. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways enrichment analysis was achieved using clusterProfiler (version 3.8.1) software.

Results

OsTET8 as an evolutionarily conserved TET family member

In our previous study, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis of root tissues from the osmyb36a mutant, which exhibits defective Casparian strip (CS) formation, identified a significant downregulation of OsTET8 (Os05g0126100) (Wang et al. 2022), a member of the TET gene family (Table S4). These findings suggest that OsTET8 may be involved in the regulation of growth and development in rice root systems.

Bioinformatics analysis had previously indicated that OsTET8 exhibits three splice variants (Mani et al. 2015). Among them, the OsTET8.1 isoform lacks the characteristic tetraspanin 'GCCK' motif, and OsTET8.3 contains only one intron (Figure S1). The splice variant isolated from rice roots in this work was OsTET8.2, which features three exons and two introns (Figure S1 and S2A) and encodes a 289-amino-acid protein predicted to contain four transmembrane domains (Figure S2B). The protein contains four highly conserved transmembrane domains (TMs), located at amino acid residues 9–31, 46–68, 75–97, and 164–186.

Phylogenetic analysis and multiple sequence alignment of amino acid sequences from TET homologs in Arabidopsis thaliana and Oryza sativa demonstrated that OsTET8 shares significant sequence similarity with other members of the TET protein family (Figure S2C, D). Notably, among rice TET family members, OsTET8 exhibited the highest sequence identity with OsTET1 (LOC_Os01g0977100), at 63.47%, followed by OsTET13 (LOC_Os09g0425900) at 42.60% (Figure S2D). In Arabidopsis, AtTET6 (AT3G12090) showed the highest sequence identity to OsTET8, at 57.74%, followed by AtTET5 (AT4G23410) at 43.92% (Figure S2D). These findings suggest that OsTET8 and its homologs likely perform evolutionarily conserved biological functions.

To further characterize the spatial expression pattern of OsTET8 within roots, we isolated RNA from five distinct root segments (0–3, 3–6, 6–9, 9–12, and 12–15 mm from the root tip) of 5-day-old hydroponically grown rice seedlings. qRT-PCR analysis demonstrated that OsTET8 expression was highest in the 3–6 mm segment, corresponding to the root elongation zone. Expression levels gradually decreased in the 6–9, 9–12, and 12–15 mm segments, with the lowest levels detected in the 0–3 mm apical region (Fig. 1B).

Moreover, we examined the tissue-specific expression profile of OsTET8 using publicly available transcriptome data from the Rice Expression Profile Database (RiceXPro, https://ricexpro.dna.affrc.go.jp) (Sato et al. 2013). Consistent with our qRT-PCR results, OsTET8 was found to be highly expressed in root tissues (Figure S3A), particularly in the elongation and maturation zones (Figure S3B). Furthermore, transcriptomic analysis at the cellular level indicated that OsTET8 expression was significantly enriched in the endodermis and stele compared with other cell layers (Figure S3C). Taken together, these findings indicate that OsTET8 is predominantly expressed in the root elongation zone, implying a potential functional role in root development and growth regulation.

OsTET8 localized to the endoplasmic reticulum and plasma membrane

The subcellular localization of OsTET8 was investigated using a rice protoplast transient expression system. To address potential positional artifacts from fluorescent tagging, two distinct GFP fusion constructs were generated in the pYL322 vector. These included a C-terminal OsTET8-GFP fusion and an N-terminal GFP-OsTET8 fusion, both driven by the CaMV 35S promoter. These constructs were co-introduced into rice protoplasts along with well-defined organelle markers via PEG-mediated transformation. The plasma membrane was visualized using the OsRac3-mCherry marker, while the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) was identified with the OsHDEL-mCherry marker (Li et al. 2024a). Subcellular localization was assessed by confocal laser scanning microscopy at 24 h post-transfection.

Confocal imaging revealed that both OsTET8-GFP and GFP-OsTET8 fluorescence signals partially overlapped with the plasma membrane marker OsRac3-mCherry (Fig. 2A–H). However, predominant co-localization occurred with the ER marker OsHDEL-mCherry (Fig. 2I–P). OsTET8 negatively regulates rice root development

At 3 days post-germination, both ostet8-4 and ostet8-30 mutants exhibited a significant increase in primary root length of approximately 7–10 mm compared with WT plants (Fig. 3B, C). By 7 days post-germination, the mutants exhibited enhanced lateral root development, with a density significantly higher than that of the WT (Fig. 3B, D). This growth advantage was maintained at 15 days post-germination, with mutants generating significantly greater adventitious root numbers than WT plants (Fig. 3B, E). These results suggest that OsTET8 functions as a negative regulator of root development.

To further investigate the functional role of OsTET8 in root development, we generated transgenic rice lines overexpressing OsTET8 under the control of the root-specific OsRCc3 promoter (ProOsRCc3::OsTET8), enabling tissue-specific overexpression in roots (Li et al. 2018). Two independent overexpression lines, OE-2 and OE-11, were selected for subsequent analysis. qRT-PCR analysis confirmed that OsTET8 expression levels in the roots of these lines were significantly higher than those in WT plants (Figure S5). Phenotypic analyses of OE-2 and OE-11 were then performed to assess their root developmental dynamics.

At 3 days post-germination, the primary root length of OsTET8-OE seedlings was significantly shorter than that of WT plants, exhibiting a reduction of approximately 15–20 mm (Fig. 3B, C). By 7 days post-germination, the number of lateral roots in OsTET8-OE lines was significantly decreased compared to WT (Fig. 3B, D). At 15 days post-germination, OsTET8-OE seedlings displayed a slight reduction in adventitious root number relative to WT, although this difference was not statistically significant (Fig. 3B, E). These results contrast with the phenotype observed in ostet8 mutants, in which loss of OsTET8 function enhances root elongation, lateral root development, and adventitious root formation. Thus, OsTET8 plays a negative role in promoting root growth.

Moreover, we measured endogenous JA levels in 3-day-old seedling roots of WT, ostet8 mutant, and OsTET8-OE plants. The results revealed that compared with WT, both the ostet8 mutant and OsTET8-OE plants exhibited a remarkable reduction in total free JA content in their roots (Fig. 3F). Our data demonstrate that OsTET8 is required to establish normal root JA levels, with both knockout and overexpression perturbing JA homeostasis, which implies OsTET8 may function as a critical regulator in the precise maintenance of JA balance.

To further assess the impact of OsTET8 mutation or overexpression on agronomic traits, we evaluated mature plants grown in paddy soil under field conditions, focusing on key traits such as plant height, tiller number, grain length, and grain width (Figure S6). Phenotypic analysis revealed no significant differences in plant height (Figure S6H), grain length (Figure S6F, J), or grain width (Figure S6G, K) between the ostet8 mutants, OsTET8-OE lines, and the WT plants at maturity. However, ostet8 mutants exhibited a slight but consistent increase in tiller number compared to the wild type, whereas no such change was observed in OsTET8-OE lines (Figure S6I). Taken together, these findings suggest that OsTET8 is not significantly involved in the regulation of plant height, tillering, or grain size in rice, but instead functions as a negative modulator of root growth.

OsTET8 participates in regulating the proliferation of root cortex cells

To characterize the function of OsTET8 during root elongation, transverse sections of 3-day-old root tips of the WT (Nipponbare), ostet8 mutants (ostet8-4 and ostet8-30), and OsTET8-OE lines (OE-2 and OE-11) were analyzed using differential interference contrast (DIC) microscopy. The results showed that the ostet8 mutants retained the canonical root architecture, including epidermis, exodermis, sclerenchyma, cortex, endodermis, pericycle, and stele (Fig. 4A–C). However, their root diameter and cross-sectional areas were significantly reduced compared to WT, along with fewer root cortical cell layers (Fig. 4A–C, F–H). These results indicate that the loss of function of OsTET8 does not disrupt cell differentiation but impairs root cortical cell proliferation. Similarly, OsTET8-OE lines exhibited no structural abnormalities in cellular organization but displayed reduced root diameter and cross-sectional areas (Fig. 4D–E) and diminished root cortical cell layers (Fig. 4F–H), consistent with ostet8 mutants. Notably, both knockout and overexpression of OsTET8 suppressed root cortical cell proliferation without altering root cell differentiation. Transcriptome analysis identifies genes regulated by OsTET8

To gain a comprehensive understanding of the roles of OsTET8 in seed root elongation and cell development, we performed transcriptome profiling of OsTET8 knockout mutant (ostet8-30) and OsTET8 overexpression lines (OE-11), along with wild-type (WT) plants. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified with a significance threshold of padj ≤ 0.05 and |log2(fold change) ≥ 1 when comparing OE-11 and ostet8-30 to the WT, respectively.

RNA sequencing data revealed that ostet8-30 exhibited 404 DEGs compared to WT, with 198 upregulated and 206 downregulated genes. The OE-11 displayed a more pronounced transcriptional shift, with 922 DEGs relative to WT, including 385 upregulated and 537 downregulated genes (Figure S7). Further Venn diagram analysis uncovered distinct and overlapping DEG patterns: 19 genes were specifically upregulated in ostet8-30 but downregulated in OE-11; 15 genes exhibited the opposite trend (downregulated in ostet8-30, upregulated in OE-11); 59 genes were co-upregulated in both ostet8-30 and OE-11 lines; and 65 genes were co-downregulated across both transgenic genotypes compared to WT (Fig. 5A). To further identify the metabolic pathways associated with these DEGs, KEGG analysis was performed using an FDR-adjusted p < 0.05 as the cutoff (Fig. 5F–I). In the ostet8-30 vs WT comparison, DEGs showed primary enrichment in three key pathways: phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, cofactor biosynthesis, and glutathione metabolism (Fig. 5F, G). Notably, the α-linolenic acid metabolic pathway was particularly enriched among downregulated genes in OE-11 lines (Fig. 5I). Jasmonic acid (JA), a metabolic derivative of α-linolenic acid, functions as a crucial phytohormone regulating root growth and development (Sood 2023; Qin et al. 2024). Significantly, OsTET13, a homolog of OsTET8, has been shown to modulate seed root elongation through regulation of endogenous JA levels (Qin et al. 2024). These findings suggest that OsTET8 overexpression may influence rice root development by altering expression patterns of genes involved in the α-linolenic acid metabolic pathway.

Based on the GO enrichment and KEGG pathway analysis of DEGs, we speculate that the loss or overexpression of OsTET8 may modulate intracellular redox homeostasis as well as JA biosynthesis and signaling pathways, thereby influencing rice root development. Discussion

OsTET8 exhibits dual localization to the plasma membrane and ER

Tetraspanins, a large superfamily of transmembrane proteins characterized by four conserved domains, function as molecular scaffolds that mediate surface signal recognition and transduction through the assembly of tetraspanin-enriched microdomains (Pols and Klumperman 2009; Lang and Hochheimer 2020). In animals, TETs are usually localized to the plasma membrane but are also transported to multivesicular bodies (MVBs) and lysosomes, and are also highly enriched in exosomes released by the fusion of MVB to the cell surface, where they facilitate intercellular communication and detect environmental stimuli (Pols and Klumperman 2009; Andreu and Yáñez-Mó 2014). Similarly, plant TETs are localized to the plasma membrane and/or ER (Mani et al. 2025). In Arabidopsis, transient expression assays and stable transformation studies have revealed that AtTET proteins localize predominantly to the plasma membrane, whereas AtTET14, AtTET15, and AtTET17 display distinct cytoplasmic aggregates and colocalize with ER markers (Boavida et al. 2013; Zhu et al. 2022). In common bean (Phaseolus vulgaris), PvTET6 localizes predominantly to the plasma membrane, whereas PvTET3 and PvTET10 are distributed across both the plasma membrane and cytoplasmic vesicles (Jimenez-Jimenez et al. 2019).

However, the subcellular localization of TET proteins remains controversial across studies in rice. The subcellular targeting predictions for OsTETs suggested plasma membrane localization, with the exceptions of OsTET6 and OsTET7, which were predicted to localize to chloroplasts and ER, respectively (Mani et al. 2015). Transient expression assays in tobacco demonstrated plasma membrane localization for eight OsTET family members (OsTET1, 2, 4, 5, 10, 12, 13, and 14), consistent with Arabidopsis homologs reported in previous studies (Mani et al. 2015). However, recent experimental studies have demonstrated that all OsTET proteins exhibit ER co-localization when overexpressed in rice protoplasts, with the exception of OsTET4, which did not co-localize with any organelle markers (Qin et al. 2024). In this study, we addressed potential positional tagging artifacts associated with fluorescent tagging by constructing two distinct GFP fusion variants (N- and C-terminal) for OsTET8. GFP fluorescent analysis revealed that OsTET8 displays dual localization, colocalizing with both plasma membrane and ER markers in rice protoplasts (Fig. 2). This pattern aligns with the dual localization of OsTET5 observed in plasmolyzed tobacco leaf epidermal cells and in the protoplasts of rice (Mani et al. 2025).

The role of ER-localized TET proteins in plants remains unclear, but emerging evidence from animal studies suggests their potential involvement in modulating signaling pathways through the regulation of partner protein biosynthesis and subsequent trafficking to the plasma membrane or organelles (Wang et al. 2012; Mani et al. 2025). ER-localized OsTET8 may facilitate the synthesis of interaction partners and mediate their transport from the ER to subcellular compartments. As a tetraspanin, OsTET8 contains four transmembrane domains structurally implicated in protein sorting processes (Prox et al. 2012). Transmembrane proteins can traffic through the ER to the Golgi, recycle back (ER-Golgi cycling), or proceed via the trans-Golgi network (TGN) before reaching the plasma membrane (Pols and Klumperman 2009). This trafficking pathway plausibly accounts for OsTET8’s ER-plasma membrane dual localization. Furthermore, TET proteins contain potential glycosylation and palmitoylation sites, and their post-translational modifications may modulate dynamic subcellular targeting (Termini and Gillette 2017; Lang and Hochheimer 2020). However, the molecular mechanisms governing OsTET8 localization remain to be fully elucidated in future studies.

Divergent roles of TET genes in modulating root development

TET family proteins have been extensively reported to participate in plant development, reproduction, and immune responses; however, their specific roles in root development remain poorly understood (Reimann et al. 2017; Qin et al. 2024). In Arabidopsis, AtTET genes exhibit distinct gene expression patterns in roots, with AtTET15 localizing to the lateral root cap, AtTET13 enriching in the quiescent centre (QC) of primary roots and lateral root primordia (LRP), and AtTET1, AtTET5, AtTET6, and AtTET10 primarily expressed in vascular bundles (Olmos et al. 2003; Boavida et al. 2013). Phenotypic analysis of tet13-1 mutants, combined with genetic complementation studies, demonstrated that AtTET13 is essential not only for maintaining proper apical meristem size and primary root length but also for promoting lateral root initiation and emergence (Wang et al. 2015). Furthermore, genetic analyses demonstrate that AtTET5 and AtTET6 function redundantly in regulating root growth dynamics. The tet5tet6 double mutants exhibit significantly elongated primary roots compared to wild-type controls (Wang et al. 2015), indicating their cooperative role in negatively regulating root elongation.

In this study, we characterized OsTET8, a member of the rice TET gene family whose biological function in rice remains unexplored. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that OsTET8 shares high homology with OsTET1, AtTET5, and AtTET6. Consistent with previous findings, although OsTET1 and OsTET8 cluster within the same clade, OsTET1 is highly expressed in spikelets but shows low expression in roots (Mani et al. 2015), a pattern further supported by data from the RiceXPro database (Figure S8). Moreover, we examined OsTET1 expression in the ostet8 mutant and observed no significant change in its transcript levels in roots compared to the wild-type (Figure S4B). We therefore speculate that OsTET1 may play distinct functional roles, likely unrelated to root development. Expression analysis in this study demonstrated that OsTET8 is predominantly expressed in the root elongation zone (Fig. 1B). To elucidate its function, we generated ostet8 knockout mutants and overexpression lines. The ostet8 mutant exhibited significantly increased primary root length, lateral root density, and adventitious root number. Conversely, overexpression lines displayed opposite phenotypes, including markedly shortened primary roots, and reduced lateral root density (Fig. 3). These results demonstrate that OsTET8 negatively regulates root growth in rice.

In addition to OsTET8, five other OsTET genes (OsTET5, OsTET6, OsTET7, OsTET13, and OsTET14) exhibit partially overlapping expression patterns in the roots of rice seedlings (Qin et al. 2024). Among these, OsTET5, OsTET6, and OsTET7 display uniform expression throughout the root differentiation zone, whereas OsTET13 and OsTET14 are predominantly localized to the vascular bundles. Notably, OsTET13 also shows specific expression in the root cap. The ostet13 knockout mutant exhibits a longer root phenotype, suggesting that OsTET13 negatively regulates root growth (Qin et al. 2024). This regulatory role aligns with that of OsTET8, which also acts as a negative regulator of root development. In contrast, functional characterization of OsTET5 supports its role as a positive regulator of root development. Overexpression of OsTET5 enhances plant growth, leading to increased root and shoot length, whereas knockdown results in significant growth retardation, with reduced root and shoot development (Mani et al. 2025). Together, these findings highlight the functional divergence among OsTET family members in modulating root development, with some members promoting growth and others inhibiting it, potentially through distinct molecular mechanisms or tissue-specific expression patterns.

OsTET8 may regulate rice root development through the JA signaling pathway

In this study, our KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that the α-linolenic acid metabolism pathway was significantly enriched among the downregulated DEGs in OsTET8 overexpression lines (Fig. 5). Notably, several key genes involved in JA biosynthesis (OsOPR5, OsLOX8, OsHI-LOX) and JA signaling (OsRMC) were identified among these DEGs. JA, a lipid-derived phytohormone synthesized from α-linolenic acid through a series of enzymatic reactions, plays a critical role in regulating root development (Zhou et al. 2019; Mu et al. 2025). Notably, JA signaling interacts with auxin homeostasis, resulting in root growth inhibition (Verma et al. 2022). The core JA signaling module comprises the F-box protein SCFCOI1, JAZ repressor proteins, and the transcription factor MYC2, which activates downstream responses (Mu et al. 2025). Furthermore, we identified multiple MYC-responsive elements in the promoter region of OsTET8 (Table S5). As a bHLH transcription factor, MYC2 serves as the central regulator of JA signaling and is the most prominent positive mediator, orchestrating nearly all JA-dependent processes, including root growth inhibition (Han et al. 2023; Mu et al. 2025). In rice, OsMYC2 has been shown to activate the promoter of OsNOMT (a key enzyme in sakuranetin biosynthesis), thereby linking JA-mediated signaling to flavonoid metabolism (Ogawa et al. 2017; Mu et al. 2025). Therefore, we hypothesize that OsTET8 may function downstream of MYC2 or modulate JA signaling components to influence root development, warranting further investigation to validate its potential mechanisms.

Genetic evidence from OsTET13 further supports the involvement of TET family proteins in JA-mediated root growth regulation (Qin et al. 2024). ostet13 mutants exhibit reduced endogenous JA levels and root overgrowth, whereas OsTET13 overexpression increases JA accumulation and inhibits root elongation. KEGG pathway enrichment, qRT-PCR validation, endogenous JA content measurements, and exogenous MeJA and JA inhibitor treatments collectively confirm that OsTET13 regulates root length by controlling cellular JA content (Qin et al. 2024). The authors propose that OsTET13 may regulate JA biosynthesis either through lipid metabolism and membrane trafficking in the ER or by directly participating in JA metabolic processes within the ER (Qin et al. 2024). Additionally, the tet8 knockout mutant in Arabidopsis shows attenuated JA accumulation in response to mechanical wounding, suggesting conserved roles for TET proteins in JA regulation across species (Liu et al. 2020). However, the molecular mechanisms underlying the regulation of JA accumulation by OsTET13 and AtTET8 remain unclear.

JA has been widely reported to suppress root growth in various plant species, including Arabidopsis, tomato, and rice (Chen et al. 2011; Acosta et al. 2013; To et al. 2019; Waidmann et al. 2020). Intriguingly, in this study, both the ostet8 mutant and OsTET8-OE lines showed a significant decrease in endogenous JA levels in 3-day-old seedlings (Fig. 3F). Consistent with the well-established inhibitory role of JA in root elongation, the ostet8 mutants exhibited increased primary root length. By contrast, OsTET8-OE seedlings showed markedly shorter roots than the wild-type (Fig. 3B,C), despite their similarly low JA levels and corresponding downregulation of JA-related genes in both qRT-PCR and RNA-seq analyses (Figs. 5, 6 and Table 1).

These contrasting root phenotypes under similarly low JA conditions imply that OsTET8 likely influences root development through mechanisms beyond simply altering bulk JA content. Instead, it may modulate JA signaling efficacy or engage in crosstalk with other hormonal pathways. Although the present data cannot fully resolve this discrepancy, our findings clearly establish OsTET8 as a key player in JA biosynthesis or homeostasis. Future investigations should therefore focus on elucidating the molecular basis underlying this phenotype, particularly how OsTET8 influences JA signaling sensitivity and its integration with other growth regulators.
