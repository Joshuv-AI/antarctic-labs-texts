The exact travelling wave solutions of a KPP equation
Eugene Kogan

Abstract
We obtain the exact analytical traveling wave solutions of the Kolmogorov-Petrovskii-Piskunov equation with the reaction term belonging to the class of functions, which includes that of the (generalized) Fisher equation, for the particular values of the waves speed. Additionally we obtain the exact analytical traveling wave solutions of the generalized Burgers–Huxley equation. Keywords: Reaction-diffusion equation; Fisher equation; travelling waves; exact solutions.



I
Introduction

Fisher
[
1
]
first introduced a nonlinear evolution equation to investigate the wave
propagation of an advantageous gene in a population. His equation also describes the
logistic growth–diffusion process and has the form

\displaystyle u_{t}-\nu u_{xx}=ku\left(1-\frac{u}{\kappa}\right)

(1)

where
\kappa(>0)

is the carrying capacity of the environment. The term

f(u)=ku(1-u/\kappa)
represents
a nonlinear growth rate which is proportional to
u
for small
u
, but decreases as
u

increases, and vanishes when
u=\kappa
.
Equation (
1
) arises in many physical,
biological, and chemical problems involving diffusion and nonlinear growth.
It is convenient to introduce the nondimensional
quantities

\displaystyle x^{*}

\displaystyle=x\sqrt{k/\nu},

(2a)

\displaystyle t^{*}

\displaystyle=kt,

(2b)

\displaystyle u^{*}

\displaystyle=u/\kappa,

(2c)

so after
dropping the asterisks, equation (
1
) takes the
nondimensional form

\displaystyle u_{t}-u_{xx}=u\left(1-u\right).

(3)

In the same year (1937) as Fisher, Kolmogorov, Petrovsky and Piskunov (KPP)
[
2
]
introduced the more general reaction-diffusion equation, which in the nondimensional form can be presented as

\displaystyle u_{t}-u_{xx}=F(u),

(4)

where

F
is a sufficiently smooth function with the properties that

F(0)=F(1)=0
,
F^{\prime}(0)>0
and
F^{\prime}(1)<0
.

Fisher-KPP equation belongs to the class of reaction-diffusion equations: in fact, it is one of the simplest semilinear reaction-diffusion equations, which can exhibit traveling wave solutions that switch between equilibrium states given by

F(u)=0
. Such equations occur, e.g., in ecology, where it describes, like it was mentioned above, the population growth, physiology, combustion, crystallization, plasma physics, and in general phase transition problems.

Equation (
4
) admits traveling
wave solutions

\displaystyle u(x,t)=u(\xi),\hskip 14.22636pt\xi=x-ct,

(5)

where
c
is the wave speed (measured in the units of
\sqrt{\nu/k}
), and
u(\xi)
satisfies the
equation

\displaystyle u_{\xi\xi}+cu_{\xi}=-F(u)

(6)

with the boundary conditions

\displaystyle\lim_{\xi\to-\infty}u(\xi)

\displaystyle=1,

(7a)

\displaystyle\lim_{\xi\to+\infty}u(\xi)

\displaystyle=0.

(7b)

Equation (
6
) has a simple mechanical interpretation. It describes motion of the Newtonian particle with the mass 1 (
u
being the coordinate of the particle and
\xi
- the time) in the potential well
U(u)
defined by the equation

\displaystyle\frac{dU}{du}=F(u).

(8)

The motion is with friction, and the coefficient of friction is equal to
c
.
The point
u=0
is the point of stable equilibrium, and the point
u=1
- unstable.

Equation (
6
) is a nonlinear nonintegrable differential equation.
The Cauchy problem cannot be solved
by the inverse scattering transform for this equation and the problem of finding some exact
solutions for this equation is an important task.
Several methods of obtaining such solutions has been developed. We will mention here a few of them:
the tanh-function method
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
, the Jacobi elliptic function expansion
method
[
6
]
, the simplest equation method
[
7
,
8
]
, the Exp-function method
[
9
]
, the

G^{\prime}/G
expansion
method
[
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
]
, the Kudryashov method
[
14
]
, and the attached flow method

[
15
,
16
]
.

In this paper we will use the method of exclusion of the independent variable
[
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
]
, which allows to reduce the problem of integration of the second order ODE to the problem of integration of the first order ODE, to obtain the exact solutions of Eq. (
6
). Thus we were able to obtain the exact solutions of the KPP equation which to the best of our knowledge were not known before.
Additionally we obtain the exact analytical traveling wave solutions of the generalized Burgers–Huxley equation.

II
The ODE which doesn’t contain explicitly the independent variable

Equation (
6
) doesn’t contain explicitly the independent variable
\xi
. This prompts the idea to
consider
u
as the new independent variable and

\displaystyle p=u_{\xi}

(9)

as the new dependent variable.
In the new variables
(
6
) takes the form of Abel equation of the second kind
[
16
]
.

\displaystyle pp_{u}+cp=-F(u).

(10)

The boundary conditions for (
6
) were (
7
).
The boundary conditions for (
10
) are (however, see the next Section)

\displaystyle p(1)

\displaystyle=0,

(11a)

\displaystyle p(0)

\displaystyle=0.

(11b)

In search of elementary solutions of (
10
), in accordance with the well-known in mathematics principle, stating that
the more general the problem is, the easier it is to solve it, let us generalize it to

\displaystyle pp_{u}+\gamma(u)p=-F(u),

(12)

where
\gamma(u)
is some function.
Being inspired by the method of factorization
[
26
]
,
we present the r.h.s. of (
12
) as

\displaystyle-F(u)=\Phi(u)\Psi(u).

(13)

After that we realize that there exists the particular solution of (
12
)

\displaystyle p(u)=P\Phi(u),

(14)

provided the unknown multiplier
P
and the function
\gamma(u)
satisfy equation

\displaystyle P^{2}\Phi_{u}+P\gamma(p)=\Psi(u).

(15)

This includes the case of nonlinear
F(u)
considered in Ref.
[
22
]

\displaystyle F(U)=\left[u-A(u)\right]\left[\chi A^{\prime}(u)+a\right],

(16)

where
A(u)
is some function,
\chi,a
are some numerical parameters, and
\gamma(p)=c=
const.
In fact, taking

\displaystyle\Phi(u)=\left[A(u)-u\right]

(17)

we obtain (
15
) in the form

\displaystyle P^{2}\left[A^{\prime}(u)-1\right]+Pc=\chi A^{\prime}(u)+a.

(18)

Choosing
P^{2}=\chi
and
c=P+a/P
we turn (
18
) into identity.

Now let us be more specific and consider

\displaystyle F(u)=u\left(1-u^{n}\right)\left(u^{n}+a\right),

(19)

where
n>0
and
a\geq 0
are some constants,
so that (
6

\displaystyle u_{\xi\xi}+cu_{\xi}=u\left(u^{n}-1\right)\left(u^{n}+a\right).

Note that for

and

20

we may call the generalized Fisher equation
[
23
,
24
,
25
]
.
Starting from (
20
), we obtain Eq. (
10
) in the form

\displaystyle pp_{u}+cp=u\left(u^{n}-1\right)\left(u^{n}+a\right).

(21)

We can take

\displaystyle\Phi(u)=u\left(u^{n}-1\right),\hskip 28.45274pt\Psi(u)=\left(u^{n}+a\right),

(22)

after which (
15
) takes the form

\displaystyle P^{2}\left[(n+1)u^{n}-1\right]+Pc=u^{n}+a.

(23)

We immediately realize that
the solution of (
21
) satisfying the boundary conditions (
11
)

\displaystyle p(u)=p_{0}(u)\equiv Pu\left(u^{n}-1\right),

(24)

where

\displaystyle P=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}},

(25)

exists if the speed of the wave is

\displaystyle c=c_{0}\equiv P+\frac{a}{P}.

(26)

Substituting thus obtained

into (
9

\displaystyle u(\xi)=\frac{1}{\left[\exp(nP\xi)+1\right]^{1/n}}.

(27)

For the Fisher equation from (
27
) follows the well known result
[
27
]

\displaystyle u(\xi)=\frac{1}{\left[\exp\left(\xi/\sqrt{6}\right)+1\right]^{2}},

(28)

and the condition (
26

c=5/\sqrt{6}
.

To additionally illustrate the method of integration
used in this Section, in the Appendix
A
we obtain in
the framework of the method the exact solution

In Appendix
B
we’ll see how the results (

The connection between the existence of the elementary solution of (

III
The correct boundary conditions for Eq. (
10
)

In the previous Section
we postulated two boundary conditions (
11
) for (
10
). However, the equation is of the first order.
The first impression is that the problem is overdetermined, and the existence of the solution of (
10
) demands
the finetuning of the l.h.s. of the equation. Being more specific, one may think that
the solution of (
21
) will not exist for a general value of
c
.
However, the last statement immediately comes into contradiction with the results of the Appendix
C
, where the solution of (
21
) for arbitrary
c\gg 1
is presented. It looks like we have a problem.

To solve this problem we have to return to Eq. (
6
) with the boundary conditions (
7
). The boundary conditions are exceptional
[
28
]
–
u_{\xi\xi}
is not defined at
\xi=\pm\infty
.
However, for
\xi\to+\infty
the equation asymptotically becomes

\displaystyle u_{\xi\xi}+cu_{\xi\xi}+F^{\prime}(0)u=0,

(29)

and for
\xi\to-\infty
-

\displaystyle v_{\xi\xi}+cv_{\xi}-|F^{\prime}(1)|v=0,

(30)

where
v=1-u
.

The solution of (
30
) is

\displaystyle v=Ae^{s_{1}\xi}+Be^{s_{2}\xi},

(31)

where
A
and
B
are arbitrary constants, and
s_{1,2}
are the roots of the characteristic equation

\displaystyle s^{2}-cs-|F^{\prime}(1)|=0,

(32)

that is

\displaystyle s_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{c+\sqrt{c^{2}+4|F^{\prime}(1)|}}{2},

(33a)

\displaystyle s_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{c-\sqrt{c^{2}+4|F^{\prime}(1)|}}{2}.

(33b)

Because of the boundary condition (
7a
),
B=0
.
So this boundary condition should be specified to: For
\xi\to-\infty

\displaystyle 1-u\sim e^{s_{1}\xi}.

(34)

Now let us come to (
29
). The characteristic equation is

\displaystyle s^{2}+cs+F^{\prime}(0)=0,

(35)

that is

\displaystyle s_{3,4}=\frac{-c\pm\sqrt{c^{2}-4F^{\prime}(0)}}{2}.

(36)

We have either the real roots (both being negative), the double (negative) root,
or the complex roots (both with the negative real part). In the first case
solution of (
29
) is

\displaystyle u=Ae^{s_{3}\xi}+Be^{s_{4}\xi},

(37)

for the case of double root the solution is

\displaystyle u=e^{-c\xi/2}(A+B\xi),

(38)

and in the complex case the solution is

\displaystyle u=e^{-c\xi/2}\left[A\cos(\alpha\xi)+B\sin(\alpha\xi)\right],

(39)

where
\alpha=[4F^{\prime}(0)-c^{2}]/2
.
In any case the solution of (
29
) satisfies the boundary condition (
7b
) for any
A
and
B
. So the boundary condition (
7b
) can be discarded.

Because we have lost one boundary condition the solution of (
6
) contains an arbitrary constant. And here our mechanical analogy helps us to understand that this (irrelevant) constant describes the translation of the solution in ”time”
\xi
.

After we understood the true nature of the boundary conditions (
7
) for (
6
),
the situation with the boundary conditions (
11
) for Eq. (
10
) -
which are also exceptional - becomes obvious.

In the vicinity of
u=1
, Eq. (
10
) becomes asymptotically

\displaystyle qq_{v}-cq=|F^{\prime}(1)|v,

(40)

where again
v=1-u
, and
q\equiv dv/d\xi
.
Equation (
40
) has two solutions

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle=s_{1}v,

(41a)

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle=s_{2}v,

(41b)

where
s_{1,2}
are given by (
33
). Only the solution (
41a
) satisfies the condition (
7a
).
Hence
we realize that the boundary condition (
11a
) should be specified as follows: For
u\to 1

\displaystyle p=s_{1}(u-1).

(42)

(Not the difference between the proportionality sign in (
34
) and the equality sign in (
42
).)
One can easily check up that our solution (
24
) satisfies this boundary condition.

In the vicinity of
u=0
, Eq. (
10
) becomes asymptotically

\displaystyle pp_{u}+cp=-F^{\prime}(0)u.

(43)

Equation (
43
) has two solutions (we’ll consider only the case
c^{2}>4F^{\prime}(0)
)

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle=s_{3}u,

(44a)

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle=s_{4}u,

(44b)

where
s_{3,4}
are given by (
36
). Because both
s_{3}
and
s_{4}
are negative, both solutions will satisfy the boundary condition (
7b
). So we understood that the second boundary condition (
11b
) can be discarded – it is satisfied automatically.

Note that for the case considered explicitly in the previous Section
(
F(u)
being given by (
19
) and
c
being given by (

), obviously fulfilling the inequality

for any

\displaystyle s_{3}

\displaystyle s_{4}

Looking at Eq. (

So finally, in the previous Section we were solving the first order ODE
with the single boundary condition. The solution should exist for any given value of the speed
c
and shouldn’t contain an undefined constant.
We did finetune the speed
c
in the previous Section for the solution of (

Our analysis of (

. If

IV
Conclusions

We have obtained exact travelling wave solutions of the KPP equation for a wide class of the reaction terms and similar for the generalized Burgers–Huxley equation. Our method of integration of the second order differential equations is based on the choice of the original dependent variable as the new independent variable, and the derivative of the original dependent variable - as the new dependent variable. The boundary conditions for thus obtained first order differential equations were thoroughly studied.
All this gave us the opportunity to obtain the exact solutions mentioned above.

Exact solution of the generalized Duffing –Van der Pol equation

Consider the generalized Duffing –Van
der Pol equation

\displaystyle X_{tt}+\left(a-bF_{X}\right)X_{t}=F(X),

(46)

Introducing new dependent variable

\displaystyle p=X_{t}

as the independent variable,
we reduce (

\displaystyle pp_{X}+\left(a-bF_{X}\right)p=F(X).

(48)

The particular solution of (

\displaystyle p(X)=bF(X)

(49)

exists if

\displaystyle ab=1.

(50)

Looking back at Section
II
we realize that the solution corresponds to the factorization

\displaystyle F(X)=F(X)\cdot 1.

(51)

Substituting thus obtained function
p(X)
into (

) we obtain
the solution of (
46
)

\displaystyle t=\int\frac{dX}{bF(X)}.

(52)

Consider

which has two adjacent zeros at

and

(

50
)) is necessary for the absence of oscillations in the vicinity of the potential minimum, which gives the opportunity to integrate (

) in quadratures.

Let us be even more specific and consider the Duffing –Van der Pol equation from Ref.
[

\displaystyle X_{tt}+\left(\alpha+\beta X^{2}\right)X_{t}=gX-X^{3}.

(53)

Equation (

\displaystyle F(X)

\displaystyle=gX-X^{3},

(54a)

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=\alpha+\beta g/3

Thus the condition (

\displaystyle\alpha=3/\beta-\beta g/3;

(55)

integration in (
