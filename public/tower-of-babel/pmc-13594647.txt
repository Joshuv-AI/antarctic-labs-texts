Regulatory mechanism of leucine-amidated pectin on the gel properties of shrimp surimi as a salt substitute
Authors: Yilan Wang, Jiaofen Lin, Meng Yang, Fang Cao, Yiya Li, Bingli Wang, Jingna Liu, Peng Fei, Qiong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo address gel deterioration in low-salt shrimp surimi, leucine-amidated pectin (Leu-Pe, 21.89% grafting degree) was synthesized via enzymatic modification. Leu-Pe (4.0%) showed approximately 100-fold higher apparent viscosity than native pectin and formed an elasticity-dominated network. In low-salt surimi, Leu-Pe enhanced gel properties: hardness, chewiness, and gel strength reached 6.72 N, 17.77 mJ, and 9.97 N·mm, respectively, while water-holding capacity (WHC) peaked at 97.16%. Mechanistic analysis showed that Leu-Pe promoted ordered β-sheet formation and disulfide cross-linking, reducing free thiols to 254.93 μmol/g. Microstructural observations confirmed a compact filamentous network driven by hydrophobic anchoring and hydrogen bonding, which restricted water migration. These findings demonstrate that Leu-Pe provides structural reinforcement and superior water retention, offering an effective strategy for developing healthier low-salt surimi products.Keywords: Leucine-amidated pectin, Low-salt shrimp surimi, Gel properties, Water-holding capacity

Introduction

Shrimp surimi products are highly favored by consumers worldwide due to their high protein and low fat nutritional profiles, coupled with their unique elastic texture (Chen et al., 2024; Walayat et al., 2025). During traditional surimi processing, salt (NaCl, typically added at 2.0%–3.0%) plays a crucial role. Beyond its contribution to flavor, salt increases ionic strength to solubilize myofibrillar proteins (MPs), which promotes the unfolding of protein molecules and the subsequent formation of an ordered three-dimensional gel network (Monto et al., 2022). However, according to reports from the WHO, approximately 3 million people die annually from diseases related to high sodium intake, making salt reduction a priority on global public health agendas (Hu & McLean, 2025). Directly reducing salt content (e.g., to below 1.5%) significantly weakens the “salting-in effect” on myofibrillar proteins (Xie et al., 2024). This prevents protein molecules from sufficiently solubilizing and unfolding, leading to weakened protein-protein interactions. Consequently, the resulting gel network becomes loose, exhibiting decreased WHC and significantly deteriorated texture (Liu, Zhang, et al., 2025). Therefore, reconstructing a robust protein gel network under low-salt conditions has become a critical challenge to be addressed in the field of aquatic product processing.

To improve the quality of low-salt surimi products, external polysaccharide hydrocolloids (such as carrageenan, alginate, and pectin) have been introduced into these systems (Bao et al., 2025; Lan et al., 2023; Song et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2025). Among these, pectin has received considerable attention for its excellent biocompatibility. However, the application of native pectin (Na-Pe) is subject to significant limitations: it is rich in hydrophilic carboxyl and hydroxyl groups, relying primarily on electrostatic interactions with positively charged residues on protein surfaces to stabilize the network. In low-salt environments or at specific pH levels, this single, weak interaction is often insufficient to support a stable gel structure (Zhang et al., 2026). More importantly, Na-Pe lacks hydrophobic groups, making it difficult to anchor to the internal hydrophobic regions exposed within myofibrillar proteins through hydrophobic interactions, which limits its ability to further enhance gel strength in complex meat systems (Lu et al., 2024).

To address these limitations, our research group previously established an innovative method for preparing amino acid-amidated pectin using papain as a biocatalyst (Zheng et al., 2022; Zheng et al., 2023). Compared with traditional chemical synthesis, this enzymatic pathway is not only green and mild, but the resulting product—leucine-amidated pectin (Leu-Pe)—also exhibits unique “high viscosity and easy gelation” characteristics. This performance stems from the introduction of leucine hydrophobic side chains, which impart distinct amphiphilicity to the pectin molecules: the hydrophilic backbone maintains solubility, while the hydrophobic side chains provide anchoring sites for proteins and increase intermolecular physical entanglements. Based on this, the present study proposes the following scientific hypothesis: Leu-Pe, with its unique amphiphilic structure, can induce the ordering of myofibrillar protein conformations (transitioning from α-helices to β-sheets) through a synergistic mechanism of hydrophobic anchoring and hydrogen bonding. Meanwhile, the modified pectin can restructure the aqueous microenvironment, effectively compensating for gel network defects under low-salt conditions.

To validate this hypothesis, this study applied enzymatically prepared Leu-Pe to a low-salt shrimp surimi system for the first time to systematically explore its potential as an efficient salt substitute. Specifically, we first characterized the structural features and rheological behavior of Leu-Pe. Subsequently, we evaluated its effects on the texture, WHC, and rheological properties of low-salt shrimp surimi gels. Finally, the underlying mechanism of gel quality regulation was elucidated at the molecular level through microscopic observations, water distribution characterization, and protein conformation analysis, providing theoretical support for the development of healthy, low-salt shrimp surimi products.

Experiment

Materials and reagents

Frozen peeled South American white shrimp meat (Penaeus vannamei) was purchased from Qingdao Hetaichangda Food Co., Ltd. (Qingdao, China). Pectin (D-galacturonic acid ≥74.0%, degree of esterification <50.0%) and L-leucine were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Papain (≥ 2000 units/mg, using casein as substrate), 8-anilinonaphthalene-1-sulfonic acid (ANS), and 5,5′-dithiobis(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) were obtained from Shanghai Aladdin Bio-Chem Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). L-cysteine hydrochloride, ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), trichloroacetic acid (TCA), urea, and other reagents of analytical grade were supplied by various commercial suppliers (Shanghai, China).

Preparation of leucine-amidated pectin and shrimp surimi

Leucine-amidated pectin (Leu-Pe) was synthesized via a low-temperature enzymatic method. First, the papain solution was activated: 2 g of commercial papain was dissolved in 180 mL of phosphate buffer (0.05 mol/L, pH 7.0) and stirred magnetically at a low speed at 0 °C for 30 min under nitrogen-sealed conditions. To this, 20 mL of a pre-prepared activator solution (containing 0.04 mol/L L-cysteine hydrochloride and 0.04 g EDTA) was added, followed by an additional 10 min of low-speed stirring to fully activate the enzyme. Subsequently, 0.197 g of L-leucine (final concentration of 15 mmol/L) was dissolved in 100 mL of phosphate buffer (0.2 mol/L, pH 7.0) and mixed with 50 mL of the activated enzyme solution. Simultaneously, 5 g of pectin was dissolved in 150 mL of the same phosphate buffer. The pectin solution was then thoroughly mixed with the leucine/enzyme mixture, and 100 mL of anhydrous ethanol was slowly added to adjust the polarity of the system. The mixture was nitrogen-sealed and stirred at −5 °C for 24 h to facilitate leucine grafting. After the reaction, 200 mL of anhydrous ethanol was added to precipitate the product, which was then collected by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 5 min. The precipitate was stirred in 100 mL of a 15% TCA solution for 10 min to ensure complete enzyme inactivation. Finally, the sample was transferred to a dialysis bag (MWCO 35,000 Da) and dialyzed against running water for 48 h to remove buffer salts and unreacted monomers, followed by freeze-drying to obtain Leu-Pe powder.

The shrimp surimi gels were prepared according to the following procedure: frozen peeled shrimp meat was thawed overnight at 4 °C and then directly minced using a meat grinder without washing or leaching treatment to obtain homogeneous unwashed shrimp surimi. The raw unwashed shrimp surimi (RSS) was then divided into the following experimental groups: high-salt control (RSS-HS, 2% NaCl), low-salt blank control (RSS-LS, 1% NaCl, without any pectin addition), low-salt native pectin group (RSS-Na, 1% NaCl supplemented with 4% native pectin), and low-salt leucine-amidated pectin group (RSS-Leu, 1% NaCl supplemented with 4% leucine-amidated pectin). The 4% dosage for both Na-Pe and Leu-Pe was selected based on preliminary screening experiments evaluating addition levels from 1% to 5%, and the same dosage was used to ensure comparability between the two pectin treatments. During the blending process, the total moisture content was adjusted to 80% through the partitioned addition of ice water in three stages. To prevent protein denaturation, the core temperature of the samples was strictly maintained below 10 °C throughout the mincing process. The homogenized surimi was loaded into centrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 5 min to eliminate air bubbles. Subsequently, a two-step water bath heating protocol was employed for thermal gelation: the samples were first incubated at 40 °C for 30 min to facilitate initial protein cross-linking, followed by heating at 90 °C for 30 min to complete the formation of the gel network. Immediately after heating, the samples were immersed in ice water for rapid cooling. The prepared gels were stored at 4 °C for stabilization prior to subsequent physicochemical analysis (Geng et al., 2025; Liu, Zhang, et al., 2025).

Determination of grafting ratio of leucine-amidated pectin

The grafting ratio of Leu-Pe was determined using the o-phthalaldehyde (OPA) method. Briefly, 300 mg of the amidated pectin sample was precisely weighed into a sealed reaction tube, followed by the addition of 30 mL of 6 mol/L HCl. Constant-temperature hydrolysis was conducted at 110 °C for 12 h, after which the hydrolysate was cooled to room temperature.

A stock solution of L-leucine (0.5 mg/mL) was prepared and diluted to a series of concentration gradients to establish a standard calibration curve. The OPA chromogenic reagent was prepared by mixing 0.4% (w/v) OPA, 0.1% (v/v) β-mercaptoethanol, and 0.1 mol/L borate buffer (pH 9.0). For sample analysis, 1 mL of the original hydrolysate was diluted 10-fold. Then, 400 μL of the diluted solution was mixed with 400 μL of the freshly prepared OPA reagent and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 5 min. The absorbance was subsequently measured at 340 nm using a microplate reader.

The standard calibration curve was constructed as y = 0.1276x + 0.1161 (R2 = 0.9954). Structural characterization of pectin and shrimp surimi gels

The Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra of pectin samples were collected using a Nicolet IS10 FTIR spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Co., Ltd., USA). Samples were prepared using the KBr pellet method and scanned over a range of 400–4000 cm−1 with a resolution of 8 cm−1. The 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were recorded on a Bruker 600 MHz NMR spectrometer (Bruker Co., Ltd., Germany) with samples dissolved in D2O, covering a chemical shift range of 0–10 ppm. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis was performed using a Thermo Scientific K-Alpha+ spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Co., Ltd., USA) equipped with an Al Kα source (HV = 1486.6 eV), and the C1s peak (284.80 eV) was utilized for charge calibration. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of pectin samples were obtained using a Rigaku SmartLab SE diffractometer (Rigaku Co., Ltd., Japan) with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å). Scanning was conducted from 5° to 90° (2 θ) with a step size of 0.02°.

For shrimp surimi gels, FTIR and XRD characterizations were performed under the same conditions as described for the pectin samples. Specifically, FTIR analysis focused on the protein amide I band (1600–1700 cm−1). The relative contents of secondary structures were determined through deconvolution and Gaussian peak-fitting. Additionally, the water distribution within the shrimp surimi gels was characterized by low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) using an NMI20-015 V-I analyzer. Transverse relaxation time (T2) was measured to represent the mobility and distribution of water molecules in the gel network.

Rheological properties analysis

Rheological properties of the pectin solutions and shrimp surimi systems were characterized using a HAAKE MARS rheometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). For the pectin samples, Na-Pe was prepared at 4.0% (w/v), while Leu-Pe was prepared at concentrations of 0.5%, 1.0%, 2.0%, and 4.0% (w/v). Steady-state shear tests were performed at 25 °C over a shear rate range of 1–100 s−1 to record the apparent viscosity. Dynamic frequency sweeps (0.1–10 Hz at 25 °C) and temperature sweeps (20–90 °C) were conducted to determine the storage modulus (G') and loss modulus (G″), evaluating the viscoelasticity and thermal stability of the pectin-based networks.

Regarding the shrimp surimi systems, the gelation behavior of raw shrimp surimi (RSS) was monitored via temperature sweeps from 20 °C to 95 °C at a constant heating rate of 2 °C/min. For cooked shrimp surimi (CSS), the viscoelastic properties were assessed through frequency sweeps (0.1–10 Hz at 25 °C), and the structural thermal stability was further evaluated by temperature sweeps from 20 °C to 95 °C at a rate of 2 °C/min.

Texture properties and gel strength analysis of shrimp surimi gels

The texture profile analysis (TPA) of the CSS samples was conducted using a CT3 10 K texture analyzer (Brookfield Engineering Laboratories, USA). After cooling to room temperature, the gel samples were subjected to a double compression cycle using a TA10 cylindrical probe. The test parameters were configured as follows: pre-test speed of 1.0 mm/s, test speed of 0.5 mm/s, and post-test speed of 0.5 mm/s. The compression level was set to 50% of the original sample height, with a 0 s interval between the two compressions. Textural parameters, including hardness (N) from the first and second cycles, elasticity (mm), chewiness (mJ), and cohesiveness (dimensionless), were derived from the TPA curves. Gel strength was additionally determined by a puncture test using a TA44 ball probe on the same instrument. The pre-test speed was set at 0.5 mm/s, with both the test and post-test speeds at 1.0 mm/s, and a penetration depth of 5.0 mm. Breaking force (N) and breaking distance (mm) were recorded from the force-displacement curve, and gel strength (N·mm) was calculated as their product according to the following equation: Gel strength (N·mm) = Breaking force (N) × Breaking distance (mm) (Gu et al., 2026). Each group of samples was measured at least three times in parallel, and the average values were recorded.

Microstructure observation of shrimp surimi gels

The microstructure of the shrimp surimi gels was observed using a Sigma 360 field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM; ZEISS, Germany). The cooked shrimp surimi samples were cut into small cubes (approximately 5 × 5 × 3 mm) and pre-frozen at −80 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, the samples were dried in a freeze-dryer at −50 °C under a vacuum of <10 Pa for 48 h until a constant weight was achieved. The lyophilized samples were fractured using liquid nitrogen to expose the internal cross-section and then fixed onto specimen stubs for gold sputtering under vacuum. During observation, the acceleration voltage was set to 5 kV with a working distance of 8–10 mm. Representative images were captured at a magnification of 50× (scale bar = 400 μm), and at least three representative images were obtained from different areas of each sample to ensure the accuracy and reproducibility of the observations.

Determination of surface hydrophobicity and free thiol content of shrimp proteins

Surface hydrophobicity (S0-ANS) was evaluated using ANS as a fluorescent probe. Lyophilized CSS samples were dispersed in phosphate buffer (0.2 mol/L, pH 7.0) to achieve a protein concentration of 5 mg/mL. The dispersion was homogenized in an ice bath to prevent thermal denaturation. An ANS stock solution (1 mmol/L) was diluted to a working concentration of 100 μmol/L. A 900 μL aliquot of the protein sample was mixed with 100 μL of the ANS working solution and incubated in the dark for 10 min. Fluorescence intensity was measured using a Spark multi-mode microplate reader (Tecan, Switzerland) at an excitation wavelength of 390 nm and an emission wavelength of 470 nm, with a slit width of 5 nm. Relative fluorescence intensity (RFI) was calculated by subtracting the background fluorescence of the buffer and the sample without ANS.

Criteria for evaluating the effectiveness of the salt reduction strategy

The effectiveness of Leu-Pe as a salt substitute was evaluated based on a multi-dimensional assessment framework. The high-salt control group (CSS-HS, 2% NaCl) was used as the reference benchmark, and the low-salt blank control group (CSS-LS, 1% NaCl) served as the negative reference. The salt reduction strategy was considered effective when CSS-Leu (1% NaCl) achieved comparable or superior performance relative to CSS-HS across the following key indicators: puncture test indices (breaking force, breaking distance, and gel strength), gel texture properties (hardness, chewiness, and cohesiveness), rheological properties, WHC, water distribution, protein secondary structure, and microstructural characteristics.

Statistical analysis

All tests were repeated three times. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 16.0. One-way ANOVA and independent sample t-test were conducted to compare the differences, which were considered statistically significant when P < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Structure characterization

Structural characterization of leucine-amidated pectin

Fig. 1 illustrates the characterization results of Na-Pe and Leu-Pe obtained via FTIR, XPS, XRD, and 1HNMR. The FTIR spectra (Fig. 1A) show that both samples exhibit a broad peak at 3417 cm−1 corresponding to O—H stretching vibrations and a peak at 2931 cm−1 attributed to C—H stretching, indicating that the basic backbone of pectin remained intact. As shown in Fig. 1B, the intensity of the carboxyl characteristic absorption peak at 1743 cm−1 in Leu-Pe is significantly enhanced, which is attributed to the introduction of free carboxyl groups from the leucine molecules onto the pectin chain. Crucially, two new strong absorption peaks appeared at 1658 cm−1 and 1535 cm−1, corresponding to the amide I band (C <svg xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" version="1.0" width="20.666667pt" height="16.000000pt" viewBox="0 0 20.666667 16.000000" preserveAspectRatio="xMidYMid meet"><metadata> Created by potrace 1.16, written by Peter Selinger 2001-2019 </metadata><g transform="translate(1.000000,15.000000) scale(0.019444,-0.019444)" fill="currentColor" stroke="none"><path d="M0 440 l0 -40 480 0 480 0 0 40 0 40 -480 0 -480 0 0 -40z M0 280 l0 -40 480 0 480 0 0 40 0 40 -480 0 -480 0 0 -40z"/></g></svg> O stretching) and amide II band (N—H bending coupled with C—N stretching), respectively. The simultaneous appearance of these two peaks is a typical spectral characteristic of secondary amide structures, directly confirming the formation of stable amide bonds (-CO-NH-) between the pectin carboxyl groups and leucine amino groups. Furthermore, the increased intensity at 1338 cm−1 reflects the contribution of δ-CH bending vibrations from the introduced leucine side chains, while changes at 953 cm−1 and 686 cm−1 represent alterations in the vibrational modes of the pectin pyranose ring and the chemical environment of C—H bonds.

FTIR spectra, XPS spectra, XRD patterns, and 1H NMR spectra of native pectin and leucine-amidated pectin. A: FTIR spectra (4000–400 cm−1); B: FTIR spectra (2000–500 cm−1); C: High-resolution C1s peak spectra; D: High-resolution N1s peak spectra; E: XRD patterns (2θ = 5–80°); F: 1H NMR spectra.

XPS analysis further verified the formation of amide bonds. The C1s spectrum of Na-Pe (Fig. 1C) displays a typical triple-peak structure at 283.18 eV (C—C), 284.80 eV (C—O), and 286.48 eV (–COO−/–COOCH₃). Following modification, the intensity of the –COO−/–COOCH3 peak in Leu-Pe significantly decreased, while an overlapping signal attributed to amide carbonyl carbon (O=C-N-H) and residual carboxyl groups (–COO−) was detected near 287.48 eV, reflecting the chemical conversion of carboxyl groups into amide groups. The N1s spectra (Fig. 1D) showed no significant signal for Na-Pe, whereas Leu-Pe exhibited characteristic peaks for O=C–N at 398.28 eV and O=C–N–H at 400.18 eV. These nitrogen signals corroborate the amide carbonyl carbon signals in the C1s spectra, collectively confirming the successful conjugation of leucine to the pectin backbone via amide bonds.

XRD patterns (Fig. 1E) reveal the impact of amidation on the crystalline structure of pectin. Na-Pe exhibits several sharp diffraction peaks at 11.4°, 18.9°, 22.2°, and 40.2°, indicating a certain degree of crystallinity in native pectin. In contrast, the diffraction peak intensity of Leu-Pe is significantly reduced, with the pattern appearing relatively flat, which signifies a transition from a crystalline to an amorphous state. This phenomenon is attributed to the introduction of leucine side chains, which disrupt the regular arrangement of pectin molecular chains. The hydrophobic side chains of leucine hinder the ordered stacking of pectin chains through steric hindrance and the reconstruction of intermolecular interactions, thereby transforming the ordered lattice into a more flexible amorphous structure. An increase in amorphous regions implies greater conformational freedom and more active sites, which facilitates the formation of a stable interaction network between Leu-Pe and macromolecules such as myofibrillar proteins.

The 1HNMR spectra (Fig. 1F) further confirm the successful grafting of leucine. Na-Pe displays multiple characteristic signals at 5.35, 4.15, 3.90, 3.77, 3.70, and 3.62 ppm, corresponding to various protons on the galacturonic acid rings, including the anomeric proton (H-1), ring backbone protons (H-2 to H-5), and methoxy protons (-OCH₃). Following modification, new characteristic peaks appear in the Leu-Pe spectrum at 1.24 ppm and 0.94 ppm, corresponding to the γ-CH and the two methyl groups [−(CH3)2] of the leucine side chain, respectively. The appearance of these new peaks directly proves that leucine was successfully grafted onto the pectin molecules. Simultaneously, several characteristic sugar ring peaks of Na-Pe in the 3.62–4.15 ppm range are significantly weakened or disappear in Leu-Pe, indicating that leucine grafting altered the chemical environment of the pectin molecule. Notably, the strong peak at 5.35 ppm remains stable in both samples, suggesting that the pectin backbone was not degraded during the modification process.

In summary, the combined results from FTIR, XPS, XRD, and 1H NMR demonstrate that leucine amidation has successfully altered both the molecular and crystalline structures of pectin. These characterization techniques collectively confirm the formation of stable covalent bonds between leucine and the pectin backbone via amide linkages. While preserving the pectin backbone, this modification introduces hydrophilic amide groups and hydrophobic leucine side chains, creating a molecular structure with balanced amphiphilicity. Moreover, the increased amorphous character provides Leu-Pe with enhanced molecular flexibility and more active sites, establishing the molecular foundation for its interaction with myofibrillar proteins through hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and electrostatic effects (Wei et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026).

Rheological properties of leucine-amidated pectin

The rheological properties of Na-Pe and Leu-Pe at various concentrations are shown in Fig. 2. Steady-state shear measurements (Fig. 2A) reveal that all pectin solutions exhibit shear-thinning behavior, where the apparent viscosity decreases with an increasing shear rate. This phenomenon occurs as the shear force induces the orientation and disentanglement of pectin molecular chains, weakening the intermolecular interactions and the network structure, thereby reducing flow resistance (He et al., 2026). Notably, at the same concentration (4.0%), the apparent viscosity of Leu-Pe is approximately 100 times higher than that of Na-Pe. This significant difference indicates that the introduction of leucine side chains increases the intermolecular entanglement density, and the formation of amide bonds enhances chain rigidity, making Leu-Pe more resistant to disentanglement under shear stress (Zhang et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2023).

Rheological properties of native pectin and leucine-amidated pectin. A and B: Shear rate-dependent and temperature-dependent behavior of viscosity (1–100 s−1, 20–90 °C); C and D: Frequency-dependent behavior of storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) (0.1–10 Hz); E and F: Temperature-dependent behavior of storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) (20–90 °C).

The effect of temperature on the viscosity of pectin solutions is illustrated in Fig. 2B. The viscosity of all samples decreases as temperature increases, which is attributed to the weakening of non-covalent interactions, such as hydrogen bonding, at elevated temperatures. However, high-concentration Leu-Pe maintains a higher viscosity with a smaller decline across the entire temperature range, suggesting that amidation modification enhances the intermolecular interaction network and improves the thermal stability of the pectin solution. This enhanced thermal stability is of great significance for food processing procedures that involve heat treatment.

Dynamic shear measurements (Fig. 2C and D) show that both the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) of all samples increase with oscillation frequency, as high-frequency oscillation strengthens molecular chain interactions and entanglements within the network. Specifically, 4.0% Leu-Pe exhibits a G′ that is consistently and significantly higher than G″ across the frequency range, indicating the formation of an elasticity-dominated gel network. The formation of this network can be attributed to the introduction of amide groups, which reduces electrostatic repulsion between carboxyl groups while enhancing intermolecular hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions, thereby constructing a more stable and dense three-dimensional network structure. In contrast, the G′ and G″ values of Na-Pe and low-concentration Leu-Pe are relatively close, indicating a coexistence of elastic and viscous characteristics.

Temperature sweep experiments (Fig. 2E and F) further elucidate the thermal stability of the pectin gel networks. While G′ and G″ for all samples decrease with rising temperature, the patterns of decline differ. The moduli of 4.0% Leu-Pe remain relatively stable between 20 and 60 °C but show a significant decrease above 60 °C, accompanied by a reduction in the G′/G″ ratio. This suggests that amidation enhances gel strength in the low-to-medium temperature range but reduces thermal stability at high temperatures (>60 °C). This phenomenon likely results from the synergistic disruption of the hydrophobic interaction and hydrogen bond networks at high temperatures.

In conclusion, the rheological analysis demonstrates that leucine amidation significantly improves the viscosity and gel strength of pectin while enhancing its structural stability in the low-to-medium temperature range (20–60 °C). This temperature profile matches the typical processing temperatures for shrimp surimi (40–60 °C), indicating that Leu-Pe possesses excellent potential for application in surimi systems. The high viscosity and strong gelation properties of the modified pectin are expected to provide additional structural support under low-salt conditions, thereby improving the textural performance and processing stability of the products.

Gel properties analysis of shrimp surimi

Texture properties and gel strength

The textural parameters of the CSS gels for different treatment groups are summarized in Table 1, including first-cycle hardness, second-cycle hardness, elasticity, chewiness, and cohesiveness, as well as puncture test-derived indices including breaking force, breaking distance, and gel strength. Based on the first-cycle hardness and chewiness data, CSS-Na exhibited the highest performance. This is attributed to the strong electrostatic interaction network formed between the abundant carboxyl groups in native pectin and the myofibrillar proteins (Gu et al., 2026). Although the first-cycle hardness and chewiness of CSS-Leu were slightly lower than those of CSS-Na, they were significantly higher than those of the CSS-HS (high-salt) and CSS-LS (low-salt) groups. This indicates that leucine-amidated pectin (Leu-Pe) successfully formed a stable composite network with myofibrillar proteins through hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions, effectively compensating for the decreased protein solubility caused by the reduced salt concentration (Liu, Zhang, et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). Puncture test results further substantiated this conclusion: the gel strength and breaking force of CSS-Leu were comparable to those of CSS-Na, and both significantly surpassed CSS-HS and CSS-LS. Notably, CSS-Leu exhibited a breaking distance similar to that of CSS-Na but markedly greater than that of CSS-HS, suggesting that the multi-mechanism composite network constructed by Leu-Pe confers not only appreciable rigidity but also superior deformability relative to the salt-dependent network.

CSS-Leu maintained a second-cycle hardness retention rate of approximately 87%, which is comparable to the CSS-Na group (approximately 89%). Furthermore, CSS-Leu exhibited the highest cohesiveness among all groups, being significantly higher than the others. This suggests that amidated pectin effectively enhances the internal binding capacity of the gel, resulting in a more compact and stable structure. In contrast, differences in elasticity were relatively small across all groups, ranging from 4.03 to 4.31 mm, indicating that elasticity is primarily determined by the inherent properties of the myofibrillar proteins themselves, with additives having a limited impact.

In summary, while both CSS-Na and CSS-Leu performed excellently in terms of hardness and chewiness, their network construction modes differ. Native pectin forms a relatively rigid, high-strength structure primarily through electrostatic interactions, whereas Leu-Pe constructs a composite network with a better balance of strength and flexibility through multiple non-covalent mechanisms. This characteristic allows CSS-Leu to maintain high hardness while achieving superior cohesiveness and structural stability.

Rheological properties

Fig. 3 illustrates the rheological properties of the different treatment groups, covering the gelation process of raw shrimp surimi (RSS) and the viscoelastic behavior of CSS. The G′ and G″ curves during the heating process of RSS (Fig. 3A and B) reveal distinct gelation behaviors among the groups. For RSS-HS, a sharp increase in G″ was observed in the 50–60 °C range, likely due to structural rearrangement and energy dissipation caused by rapid protein aggregation under high-salt conditions. RSS-LS followed a similar but more moderate trend, reflecting a milder denaturation and aggregation process under low-salt conditions. Between 60 and 80 °C, the G′ of RSS-HS, RSS-LS, and RSS-Na increased significantly, which can be attributed to the denaturation of actin and other protein components forming a new gel network at high temperatures. RSS-Na exhibited a relatively stable trend throughout the heating process, suggesting that the addition of pectin stabilized the thermal denaturation of proteins and avoided drastic structural transitions. In contrast, RSS-Leu maintained a high modulus level and a steady trend throughout the process. This stability is likely because leucine-amidated pectin (Leu-Pe) forms a stable composite network with myofibrillar proteins through hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions at the early stages of heating, providing effective resistance to structural perturbations caused by temperature fluctuations.

Rheological properties of shrimp surimi gels under different treatments. A and B: Temperature-dependent behavior of storage modulus (G') and loss modulus (G″) of raw shrimp surimi during heating (20–95 °C, 2 °C/min); C and D: Frequency-dependent behavior of storage modulus (G') and loss modulus (G″) of cooked shrimp surimi (0.1–10 Hz); E and F: Temperature-dependent behavior of storage modulus (G') and loss modulus (G″) of cooked shrimp surimi (20–95 °C, 2 °C/min).

The frequency sweep results for CSS (Fig. 3C and D) demonstrate typical gel behavior, with G′ increasing alongside frequency. CSS-Na reached the highest G′ value, followed by CSS-HS. Although the G′ of CSS-Leu was lower than the former two, it was significantly higher than that of CSS-LS. This difference suggests that while the carboxyl groups of native pectin form a rigid network through strong electrostatic interactions with protein residues, Leu-Pe forms a relatively flexible network primarily via hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic effects. Notably, CSS-HS showed a distinct dip in G″ at low frequencies, possibly due to local structural rearrangements within the strongly cross-linked high-salt network under prolonged low-frequency deformation.

Temperature sweep results for CSS (Fig. 3E and F) further highlight the differences in thermal stability among the gel networks. While G′ and G″ for all samples decreased with rising temperature, the patterns of decline varied significantly. CSS-Na maintained a stable high modulus below 50 °C followed by a sharp drop, indicating the limited thermal stability of its electrostatic interaction network. CSS-HS began to decline significantly around 40 °C, suggesting that its cross-linked structure is highly sensitive to temperature. Although CSS-Leu did not have the highest initial modulus, it exhibited the most gradual decline across the entire temperature range without sharp attenuation. This indicates that the hydrogen-bonded and hydrophobic composite network constructed by Leu-Pe possesses superior thermal stability.

In summary, Leu-Pe significantly enhances the initial strength of the gel network during the RSS stage and renders the gelation process more stable and controllable. In the CSS stage, despite having a lower absolute modulus than the native pectin group, it demonstrates superior thermal stability and a more uniform viscoelastic response. These unique rheological characteristics suggest that Leu-Pe improves the quality of low-salt shrimp surimi products by constructing a flexible yet stable composite network that ensures gel strength while providing enhanced thermal processing stability.

Molecular structure (surface hydrophobicity and free thiol content)

Fig. 4 presents the surface hydrophobicity and free thiol content of the shrimp surimi gels under different treatments. As shown in the surface hydrophobicity results (Fig. 4A), CSS-HS exhibited the highest surface hydrophobicity, likely because high-salt conditions promoted the thorough dissolution and unfolding of myofibrillar proteins, exposing more internal hydrophobic groups to the protein surface. Notably, the surface hydrophobicity of CSS-Leu was significantly higher than those of CSS-LS and CSS-Na. This indicates that leucine-amidated pectin can moderately induce protein unfolding under low-salt conditions to expose hydrophobic groups for participating in hydrophobic interactions. Additionally, the leucine hydrophobic side chains themselves contribute to the overall surface hydrophobicity of the system. In contrast, CSS-Na showed the lowest surface hydrophobicity, possibly because the hydrophilic carboxyl groups of native pectin shielded the hydrophobic regions through electrostatic wrapping.

Surface hydrophobicity and free thiol content of shrimp surimi gels under different treatments. A: Surface hydrophobicity; B: Free thiol content (μmol/g). Different lowercase letters above the bars indicate significant differences among treatment groups (P < 0.05).

The free thiol content (Fig. 4B) reflects the influence of different treatments on the formation of disulfide bonds. CSS-HS maintained the highest free thiol content, as the full solubilization and unfolding of proteins in high-salt environments may reduce thiol oxidation, keeping more groups in a free state. The free thiol content of CSS-Na was slightly lower than that of CSS-HS, suggesting that native pectin promotes ordered protein aggregation through electrostatic interactions, making thiol groups more prone to moderate oxidation and disulfide bond formation. For CSS-LS, the free thiol content decreased further, likely due to insufficient protein dissolution and disordered aggregation, which exposes thiol groups to unstable environments and accelerates oxidation. Interestingly, CSS-Leu exhibited the lowest free thiol content. This signifies that leucine-amidated pectin promotes ordered protein aggregation through hydrophobic interactions, facilitating the proximity of thiol groups and the formation of stable disulfide cross-links.

In conclusion, CSS-Leu maintains high surface hydrophobicity while having the lowest free thiol content, indicating that Leu-Pe induces ordered aggregation and promotes appropriate disulfide bond formation, constructing a composite gel structure that balances strength and flexibility.

Moisture characteristics

Water distribution

The T2 relaxation time distribution curves of the shrimp surimi gels are shown in Fig. 5. Based on these characteristics, water within the gel is classified into three states: bound water (T21, 0.1–10 ms), immobilized water (T22, 10–100 ms), and free water (T23, 100–1000 ms) (Liu, Zhao, et al., 2025).

T2 relaxation time distribution of shrimp surimi gels under different treatments.

As shown in the main plot, T22 is the primary peak for all samples, indicating that immobilized water is the dominant form of moisture in shrimp surimi gels. Notably, the T22 peak for CSS-Leu is more symmetrical with a stable baseline compared to CSS-HS, suggesting a more uniform distribution of immobilized water within the amidated pectin-constructed network (Cao et al., 2024).

The inset (top left) reveals that the CSS-HS and CSS-LS groups exhibited the highest peak intensities for bound water (T21), while these intensities were lower and similar for the CSS-Leu and CSS-Na groups. This may be because protein unfolding or disordered aggregation under high- and low-salt conditions exposes more polar groups capable of binding water. Conversely, the formation of pectin-protein composite networks shifts more water into the immobilized state, achieving a more rational distribution.

The top right inset shows that the T23 peak for the CSS-Leu group shifted significantly to the right compared to CSS-Na, with reduced intensity and a more regular shape. This indicates that the amphiphilic structure of the amidated pectin reconstructed the aqueous microenvironment, transforming a portion of free water into an intermediate state with restricted mobility. Specifically, the leucine hydrophobic side chains regulated the dynamic behavior of free water and reduced surface water loss (Lan et al., 2023).

In summary, leucine-amidated pectin optimizes the water distribution in surimi gels through the synergistic effect of its amide groups and hydrophobic side chains, facilitating a transition from a “high free water-low bound water” state to a “low free water-high quality bound water” state. This provides the foundation for CSS-Leu to maintain superior WHC and structural stability under low-salt conditions.

Water-holding capacity and residual mass during heating

WHC and thermal water loss kinetics of the shrimp surimi gels are shown in Fig. 6. The WHC results (Fig. 6A) demonstrate that CSS-Na approached the levels of the high-salt system, indicating that native pectin improves WHC through its electrostatic interaction network. However, CSS-Leu surpassed all control groups with a WHC of 97.16%. This further improvement over CSS-Na suggests that the amphiphilic molecular structure introduced by amidation enhances the water-anchoring ability of the protein-polysaccharide composite network.

Water-holding capacity and residual mass during heating of shrimp surimi gels under different treatments. A: Water-holding capacity (%); B: Residual mass (%) during heating. Different lowercase letters above the bars in panel A indicate significant differences among treatment groups (P < 0.05).

The residual mass curves during heating (Fig. 6B) show that while all samples exhibited rapid decreases in residual mass during the initial heating phase, CSS-Leu and CSS-HS showed relatively slower decreases, which may be attributed to their stable cross-linked networks that effectively prevented rapid water loss. As heating progressed, CSS-LS showed the fastest water loss. Although CSS-Na performed well in static WHC tests, it lost water rapidly during dynamic heat treatment, suggesting that the water-retention capacity of native pectin is limited under thermal stress. In contrast, the water loss rate of CSS-Leu gradually stabilized, maintaining the highest residual mass. This confirms that the composite network constructed by the amidated pectin maintains stable water retention even during prolonged thermal processing.

Overall, leucine-amidated pectin achieves superior moisture retention through its amphiphilic structure: amide groups form hydrogen-bonded networks to enhance water binding, while hydrophobic side chains construct hydrophobic micro-regions to reduce water loss. This enables CSS-Leu to achieve or even exceed the water-retention effects of high-salt groups under low-salt conditions, fully demonstrating its potential as an effective salt substitute.

Microstructure and mechanism analysis

SEM micromorphology

Fig. 7 displays the FE-SEM images of freeze-dried shrimp surimi gels at 50× magnification (scale bar = 400 μm). Significant differences were observed in the three-dimensional network structures among the treatment groups. The CSS-HS group presented a relatively loose network with uneven pore sizes and thin, partially discontinuous pore walls. The network structure of CSS-LS was even more sparse, characterized by larger pores, thinner walls, and fewer network junctions, which is closely related to the reduced solubility of myofibrillar proteins under low-salt conditions (Sompongse et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2024).

SEM images of freeze-dried shrimp surimi gels under different treatments.

The CSS-Na group showed a transitional morphology between a flake-like porous structure and a filamentous network; although improved, it remained predominantly porous, indicating that native pectin primarily forms its network through relatively simple electrostatic interactions. In contrast, CSS-Leu exhibited unique structural features, with a large number of fine filamentous structures intertwining to form a dense and highly interconnected network system. This fine filamentous network, rich in cross-linking points, effectively encapsulates water and provides elastic support.

In summary, amidation modification significantly alters the interaction mode between pectin and shrimp proteins. By introducing hydrophobic interactions and strengthening hydrogen bonding, leucine-amidated pectin (Leu-Pe) forms the most dense and uniform gel network structure. This optimized microstructure dictates the macroscopic performance of the gel, providing a structural basis for quality improvement in low-salt shrimp surimi products.

Protein secondary structure and crystalline properties

Fig. 8 presents the FTIR spectra and XRD diffraction analysis of the freeze-dried shrimp surimi gels. Fig. 8A shows the full FTIR spectra of all samples in the range of 4000–500 cm−1. To precisely quantify the protein conformational changes, deconvolution and peak-fitting were performed on the amide I band (1700–1600 cm−1) (Fig. 8B). Four major secondary structure components were identified based on peak assignments: peaks at 1689 cm−1 and 1686 cm−1 correspond to β-turn structures; the peak near 1658 cm−1 represents α-helix structures; the peak at 1651 cm−1 reflects random coil conformations; and the peak at 1624 cm−1 is assigned to β-sheet conformations (Liao et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2018).

FTIR spectra and XRD patterns of freeze-dried shrimp surimi gels under different treatments. A: FTIR spectra (4000–500 cm−1); B: Deconvoluted amide I band spectra (1700–1600 cm−1); C: Protein secondary structure content; D: XRD patterns (2θ = 5–80°).

Based on the peak assignments (Fig. 8C), the α-helix content was highest in CSS-HS, suggesting that high-salt conditions promote moderate protein unfolding and refolding into stable α-helical backbones. The α-helix content was lower in CSS-LS, while the β-turn content was highest, indicating that the weakened ionic shielding effect under low-salt conditions increases electrostatic repulsion, hindering α-helix formation and promoting a transition toward β-turns. CSS-Na showed a more balanced secondary structure composition, likely due to native pectin promoting ordered protein aggregation and conformational rearrangement through electrostatic interactions. Notably, CSS-Leu exhibited the lowest α-helix content (approx. 24%) but the highest β-sheet content (approx. 21%, a significant increase from 15% in CSS-LS) and the lowest random coil content (approx. 27%). This is attributed to the hydrophobic interactions of leucine residues, which promote protein aggregation and the formation of ordered β-sheet layers, resulting in a more compact protein conformation and enhanced gel network stability (Yu & Zhang, 2025).

XRD patterns (Fig. 8D) showed a broad characteristic diffraction peak near 2θ ≈ 20° for all samples, indicating the semi-crystalline nature of the surimi gel system. Compared to CSS-HS, CSS-Leu exhibited the highest diffraction peak intensity and a narrower full width at half maximum (FWHM), suggesting that the addition of Leu-Pe significantly improves the crystallinity and structural order of the gel system.

These XRD findings corroborate the FTIR results, as the increased β-sheet content in CSS-Leu facilitates the formation of more regularly arranged crystalline regions.

Conclusion

In this study, leucine-amidated pectin (Leu-Pe) with a degree of grafting of 21.89% was successfully synthesized via an enzymatic method. Multi-dimensional spectroscopic analyses, including FTIR, NMR, XPS, and XRD, confirmed the covalent conjugation of leucine side chains to the pectin backbone through amide bonds, imparting unique amphiphilic structural characteristics to the molecules. Rheological measurements demonstrated that Leu-Pe formed a gel network characterized by high viscosity (approximately 100 times that of native pectin), high elasticity (G′ > G″), and excellent thermal stability, providing the structural foundation for its application as a salt substitute.

At the application level, the incorporation of Leu-Pe significantly mitigated the gel deterioration in low-salt shrimp surimi systems caused by insufficient protein solubility. Textural analysis showed that the hardness (6.72 N), chewiness (17.77 mJ), and cohesiveness (0.59) of the CSS-Leu group were significantly superior to those of the low-salt control group, approaching or even partially surpassing the levels of the high-salt control group. Puncture test results further confirmed that the gel strength of CSS-Leu was comparable to that of CSS-Na and markedly exceeded that of CSS-HS, with a breaking distance significantly greater than that of CSS-HS, collectively demonstrating the superior mechanical integrity and deformability of the Leu-Pe-constructed network. Notably, this group exhibited the most outstanding WHC (97.16%) and the lowest thermal water loss rate. These results indicate that Leu-Pe can effectively replace a portion of dietary salt, significantly enhancing the processing and eating quality of low-salt shrimp surimi products by strengthening the structural integrity of the protein-polysaccharide composite network.

Mechanism analysis further revealed the multiple synergistic effects of Leu-Pe in regulating gel quality. At the molecular level, the introduction of leucine hydrophobic side chains enhanced the hydrophobic interactions on the protein surface, promoting the conformational transition of myofibrillar proteins from random coils and α-helices to more ordered β-sheets (with relative content increasing from 15% to 21%). Simultaneously, it induced the moderate formation of disulfide bonds (free thiol content decreased to 254.93 μmol/g), thereby improving the crystallinity and order of the gel system. Regarding moisture regulation, the hydrogen-bonded network formed by amide groups and the hydrophobic micro-regions constructed by the side chains worked synergistically to effectively restrict the mobility of water molecules, facilitating the transition from free water to bound water. Microstructural observations confirmed that these intermolecular synergistic effects constructed a dense and uniform filamentous network structure, ultimately achieving the simultaneous improvement of gel strength and WHC under low-salt conditions.

This study demonstrates the potential of Leu-Pe as a high-efficiency salt substitute in low-salt shrimp surimi products, providing a theoretical basis and technical support for the molecular design of functional polysaccharides and the development of healthy meat products. Future research could focus on process optimization, application expansion across multiple food categories, and the analysis of molecular dynamics mechanisms at the protein-polysaccharide interface.
