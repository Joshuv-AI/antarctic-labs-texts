The Order-disorder Transition in Incompressible Polar Active Fluids with an Easy Axis
Leiming Chen, Chiu Fan Lee, John Toner

Abstract
Dry active matter in an anisotropic medium is of experimental relevance, and the interplay between anisotropy and the dynamics of the active matter remains under-explored. Here, we derive the hydrodynamic equations of a generic dry polar active fluid that preferentially flows along a particular axis induced by the anisotropy of the medium. We then study its critical behavior at the order-disorder transition in which the symmetry between “forward” and “back” along the special axis is spontaneously broken. We obtain the critical static and dynamic exponents, mean velocity, and two point correlation functions exactly in three dimensions, and to two-loop level in two dimensions, by mapping our class of systems to the equilibrium Ising model with dipolar interactions.



I
Introduction

The study of “Active matter” - systems consisting of self-propelled agents - and the collective properties of such systems, has been at the forefront of non-equilibrium physics and biological physics for the past two decades
Vicsek et al. 1995
;
Toner et al. 2005
;
Marchetti et al. 2013
. This research has revealed that Active matter can be fundamentally different from equilibrium systems, as exemplified by the emergence of a new state of matter that corresponds to the moving phase of generic dry polar active fluids in an isotropic medium
Vicsek et al. 1995
;
Toner and Tu 1995
;
Toner and Tu 1998
. However, dry active fluids in an
anisotropic
environment are also of experimental interest. For instance, anisotropy can be introduced by patterning the substrate that a two-dimensional (2D) active fluid, such as a collection of self-propelled particles
Bricard et al. 2013
, moves on; or by stretching a gel that a three-dimensional (3D) active fluid, such as motile cells
Zaman et al. 2006
, move within.

In this paper, we focus on active fluids in which the active particles move preferentially parallel
to a certain direction, which we refer as the “easy axis”, and will denote throughout this paper as
x
. We will denote directions orthogonal to
x
as
\mathbf{r}_{{}_{\perp}}
in
d=3
, and as
y
in
d=2
. When driven by an external field aligned with the easy axis, this active system has been shown to possess rich phase behavior
Katz et al. 1983
;
Janssen and Schmittmann 1986
;
Leung and Cardy 1986
.

In this paper, we consider systems
without
such an external field; that is, one in which the underlying dynamics has “up-down” symmetry along the easy axis. That is, if the easy axis is, e.g., vertical, the active
particles are equally likely
a priori
to move either up or down along this axis.
We show here that such a system can break this up-down symmetry
spontaneously
at a non-equilibrium phase transition. We find that, in the incompressible limit, this transition is continuous, and the corresponding critical behavior belongs to the universality class of the equilibrium, purely relaxational (i.e., “TDGL”) Ising model with dipolar interactions
Aharony 1973
;
Brézin and Zinn-Justin 1976
;
Folk et al. 1977
. Through this mapping we are able to obtain the exact scaling behavior of this transition in spatial dimension
d=3
, and estimates of the critical exponents in
d=2
, simply by using the results of
Aharony 1973
;
Brézin and Zinn-Justin 1976
;
Folk et al. 1977
.

In this mapping, the local component
v_{x}(\mathbf{r},t)
of the velocity of the self-propelled particles along the easy axis plays the role of the local magnetization

of the dipolar Ising model.

\displaystyle-{1\over(Kq_{x}^{2}+q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2})}\left\{q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}\left[\left(a+\mu_{{}_{\perp}}q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}+\mu_{x}q_{x}^{2}\right)v_{x}-f_{x}\right]+q_{\perp}^{2}\mathcal{F}_{\mathbf{q}}\left[\Gamma_{1}v_{x}\partial_{x}v_{x}+\right.\right.

(II.10)

\displaystyle\left.\left.\Gamma_{2}(\mathbf{v}_{{}_{\perp}}\cdot\mathbf{\nabla}_{{}_{\perp}})v_{x}+\Gamma_{3}v_{{}_{\perp}}\partial_{x}v_{{}_{\perp}}+bv_{x}^{3}\right]+q_{x}\left(wq_{x}v_{x}+K\mathbf{q}_{{}_{\perp}}\cdot\mathbf{f}_{{}_{\perp}}\right)\right\}\,,

where we’ve further defined
w\equiv Kc
.
Note that we focus exclusively on the EOM of
v_{x}
because it is the only soft mode at the critical transition.

III
Mean-Field Theory

We first consider the mean field theory for this system, which is simply its spatially uniform, steady state behavior in the absence of the noise
\mathbf{f}
. In such a state, the equation of motion
(
II.2
) reduces to a simple algebraic equation:

av_{x}+bv_{x}^{3}=0\,.

(III.1)

For
a>0
, the only real solution of this equation is
v_{x}=0
(we assume
b>0
always). This is the disordered phase.

For
a<0
, we have two additional solutions:

v_{x}=\pm\sqrt{-{a\over b}}\,,

(III.2)

which correspond to the ordered, broken symmetry state.

We’ll now consider fluctuations in both of these states, and at the critical point
a=0
which separates them. We’ll begin with the linear theory of those fluctuations.

IV
Linear theory

IV.1
Velocity Correlations in Fourier space

In the linear approximation the fluctuations of the order parameter
v_{x}
in the disordered state and at the critical point - that is, for
a\geq 0
- are readily
calculated.
One simply drops all of the terms nonlinear in
v_{x}
in the equation of motion (
II.10
), and then Fourier transforms in time. The result is a linear algebraic equation for the spatiotemporally Fourier transformed field
v_{x}(\mathbf{q},\omega)
, where
\omega
is the Fourier frequency, which reads:

\displaystyle-i\omega v_{x}(\mathbf{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{1\over(Kq_{x}^{2}+q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2})}\left\{q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}\left[\left(a+\mu_{{}_{\perp}}q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}+\mu_{x}q_{x}^{2}\right)v_{x}(\mathbf{q},\omega)-f_{x}(\mathbf{q},\omega)\right]+q_{x}\left(wq_{x}v_{x}(\mathbf{q},\omega)+K\mathbf{q}_{{}_{\perp}}\cdot\mathbf{f}_{{}_{\perp}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)\right)\right\}\,,

Solving this linear equation for
v_{x}(\mathbf{q},\omega)
gives

v_{x}(\mathbf{q},\omega)={q_{\perp}^{2}f_{x}+Kq_{x}\mathbf{q}_{{}_{\perp}}\cdot\mathbf{f}_{{}_{\perp}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)\over-i\omega(Kq_{x}^{2}+q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2})+aq_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}+q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}(\mu_{x}q_{x}^{2}+\mu_{{}_{\perp}}q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2})+wq_{x}^{2}}\,.

(IV.2)

Autocorrelating the result (
IV.2
) with itself, and using the correlations of the random force as given by equation (
II.4
), we find the spatio-temporally Fourier transformed velocity correlation function:

\displaystyle\langle v_{x}(\mathbf{q},\omega)v_{x}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})\rangle={2(D_{x}q_{{}_{\perp}}^{4}+D^{\prime}_{{}_{\perp}}q_{x}^{2}q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2})\delta(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\delta(\omega+\omega^{\prime})\over\omega^{2}(Kq_{x}^{2}+q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2})^{2}+\Big[aq_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}+q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}(\mu_{x}q_{x}^{2}+\mu_{{}_{\perp}}q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2})+wq_{x}^{2}\Big]^{2}}\ ,

(IV.3)

where we’ve defined

D^{\prime}_{{}_{\perp}}\equiv D_{{}_{\perp}}K^{2}
.
Integrating this over the frequencies
\omega
and
\omega^{\prime}
gives the equal-time, spatially Fourier transformed correlation function:

\displaystyle\langle v_{x}(\mathbf{q},t)v_{x}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime},t)\rangle={(D_{x}q_{{}_{\perp}}^{4}+D^{\prime}_{{}_{\perp}}q_{x}^{2}q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2})\delta(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\over(Kq_{x}^{2}+q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2})\left[aq_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}+q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}(\mu_{x}q_{x}^{2}+\mu_{{}_{\perp}}q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2})+wq_{x}^{2}\right]}\approx{(D_{x}q_{{}_{\perp}}^{4}+D^{\prime}_{{}_{\perp}}q_{x}^{2}q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2})\delta(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\over(Kq_{x}^{2}+q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2})\left(aq_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}+\mu_{{}_{\perp}}q_{{}_{\perp}}^{4}+wq_{x}^{2}\right)}\ ,

(IV.4)

Note that right at the critical point, where
a
vanishes, as
\mathbf{q}\to\mathbf{0}

the fluctuations diverge as

1\over q_{\perp}^{2}

for
q_{x}\lesssim q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}
. For all other directions of
\mathbf{q}
, this correlation function is

O(1)
. This implies anisotropic scaling:

q_{x}\sim q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}
, which we will show later to be modified by nonlinear effects in
d=2
, but not in
d=3
. We have neglected
in the denominator
\mu_{x}q_{x}^{2}q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}
with respected to
wq_{x}^{2}
in the second approximation above, which is obviously justified in the limit of small
\mathbf{q}
. We will therefore henceforth drop the
\mu_{x}
term.

The above results apply in the disordered phase
a>0
. For
a<0
, i.e., in the ordered phase, we simply need to linearize our equation of motion not about
v_{x}=0
, but, rather, about the mean field solution (
III.2
). That is, we write

\displaystyle v_{x}(\mathbf{r},t)=v_{0}+\delta v_{x}(\mathbf{r},t)

(IV.5)

with
v_{0}=\pm\sqrt{-{a\over b}}
, our mean field solution for
v_{x}
from

equation (
III.2
) above, and then expand to linear order in
\delta v_{x}
. The result is

\displaystyle-i\omega\delta v_{x}(\mathbf{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{1\over(Kq_{x}^{2}+q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2})}\left\{q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}\left[\left(-2a+\mu_{{}_{\perp}}q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}\right)\delta v_{x}(\mathbf{q},\omega)-f_{x}(\mathbf{q},\omega)\right]+q_{x}\left(wq_{x}\delta v_{x}(\mathbf{q},\omega)+\mathbf{q}_{{}_{\perp}}\cdot\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{{}_{\perp}}(\mathbf{q},\omega)\right)\right\}\,.

We note that this is
identical
to the linearized equation of motion (
IV.1
) for
v_{x}
, with only the trivial changes
v_{x}\to\delta v_{x}
and
a\to-2a=2|a|
. Hence, we can simply make these substitutions in the results of the previous subsection, to obtain

\displaystyle\langle\delta v_{x}(\mathbf{q},\omega)\delta v_{x}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})\rangle={2(D_{x}q_{{}_{\perp}}^{4}+D^{\prime}_{{}_{\perp}}q_{x}^{2}q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2})\delta(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\delta(\omega+\omega^{\prime})\over\omega^{2}(Kq_{x}^{2}+q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2})^{2}+\left[2|a|q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}+q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}(\mu_{x}q_{x}^{2}+\mu_{{}_{\perp}}q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2})+wq_{x}^{2}\right]^{2}}\ ,

(IV.7)

for the connected spatio-temporally Fourier transformed correlation function, and

\displaystyle\langle\delta v_{x}(\mathbf{q},t)\delta v_{x}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime},t)\rangle={(D_{x}q_{{}_{\perp}}^{4}+D^{\prime}_{{}_{\perp}}q_{x}^{2}q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2})\delta(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\over(Kq_{x}^{2}+q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2})\left(2|a|q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}+\mu_{{}_{\perp}}q_{{}_{\perp}}^{4}+wq_{x}^{2}\right)}\approx{(D_{x}q_{{}_{\perp}}^{4}+D^{\prime}_{{}_{\perp}}q_{x}^{2}q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2})\delta(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\over(Kq_{x}^{2}+q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2})\left(2|a|q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}+\mu_{{}_{\perp}}q_{{}_{\perp}}^{4}+wq_{x}^{2}\right)}\ ,

(IV.8)

for the equal-time, spatially Fourier transformed correlation function.

IV.2
Velocity Correlations in real space and time in the linear theory

To obtain the spatio-temporal velocity correlation function, we will Fourier transform the above results back to real space and time. We will focus on the disordered phase exclusively in the following calculations. Once we have the results for the disordered phase, we can easily construct those for the ordered phase simply by replacing
a
with
2|a|
, utilizing the striking similarity between (
IV.3
) and (
IV.7
).

Fourier transform the expression (
IV.3
) back to
real space and time to obtain

C({\bf r},t)=\int\frac{{\rm d}^{d}q\,d\omega}{(2\pi)^{d+1}}{2(D_{x}q_{{}_{\perp}}^{4}+D^{\prime}_{{}_{\perp}}q_{x}^{2}q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}){\rm e}^{{\rm i}(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}-\omega t)}\over\omega^{2}(Kq_{x}^{2}+q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2})^{2}+\left(aq_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}+\mu_{{}_{\perp}}q_{{}_{\perp}}^{4}+wq_{x}^{2}\right)^{2}}\ ,

(IV.9)

where
C({\bf r},t)
is defined by (
I.2
).

The correlation function behaves very differently
in the “critical regime” than in the “non-critical regime. Here the “critical regime” is defined as the regime in which all of the following three conditions are satisfied:

x\ll\xi_{{}_{\parallel}}^{\rm lin}\ \ \ ,\ \ \ r_{{}_{\perp}}\ll\xi_{{}_{\perp}}^{\rm lin}\ \ \ ,\ \ \ t\ll\tau_{{}_{\rm corr}}^{\rm lin}\,,

(IV.10)

where we’ve defined the correlation lengths and times for the linear theory:

\displaystyle\xi_{{}_{\perp}}^{\rm lin}\equiv\sqrt{\mu_{{}_{\perp}}\over|a|}\ \ \ ,\ \ \ \xi_{{}_{\parallel}}^{\rm lin}\equiv\sqrt{w\over\mu_{{}_{\perp}}}(\xi_{{}_{\perp}}^{\rm lin})^{2}={\sqrt{w\mu_{{}_{\perp}}}\over|a|}\,,

\displaystyle\tau_{{}_{\rm corr}}^{\rm lin}\equiv{1\over|a|}\,.

(IV.11)

The non-critical regime is everywhere else; i.e., where at least one of the conditions (
IV.10
) is violated.

Note that the correlation lengths
\xi_{{}_{\parallel}}^{\rm lin}
and
\xi_{{}_{\perp}}^{\rm lin}
both diverge as
a\to 0
. Indeed,
if
a
is a function of some experimental control parameter
p
, and
p_{c}
is the value of
p
satisfying
a(p_{c})=0
, and if, as is the usual assumption in critical phenomena
Chaikin and Lubensky 1995
,
a(p)
is a smooth, analytic function of
p
near
p_{c}
, then asymptotically close to
p_{c}
, we‘ll have

a(p)\approx C(p-p_{c})\,,

(IV.12)

with
C
a non-universal (i.e., system-dependent), non-zero constant.
Inserting this into (
IV.11
), we see that both
\xi_{{}_{\parallel}}^{\rm lin}
and
\tau_{{}_{\rm corr}}^{\rm lin}
diverge like

|p-p_{c}|^{-1}
as
p\to p_{c}
, while
\xi_{{}_{\perp}}^{\rm lin}
diverges like
|p-p_{c}|^{-1/2}
.

We’ll now determine the behavior of the spatial and temporal correlation functions, in the linear approximation, starting with the critical regime.

IV.2.1
General scaling behavior of velocity correlations in the critical regime in the linear theory

In the critical regime, where all conditions (
IV.10
) are satisfied, the integral in (
IV.9
) is dominated by
q_{{}_{\perp}}\gg 1/\xi_{{}_{\perp}}^{\rm lin}
. To see this, note first that the integral over the inner regime
q_{{}_{\perp}}\ll 1/\xi_{{}_{\perp}}^{\rm lin}
,
q_{x}\ll 1/\xi_{{}_{\parallel}}^{\rm lin}
,
\omega\ll 1/\tau_{{}_{\rm corr}}^{\rm lin}
converges. It has no contribution to the singular dependence on space
\mathbf{r}
and time
t
, since the only dependence on space
\mathbf{r}
and time
t
comes from the exponential, which, in the inner regime, is practically 1, since
\mathbf{q}_{{}_{\perp}}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{{}_{\perp}}
and
q_{x}x
are both
\ll 1
in the critical regime. So the singular spatial and temporal dependence can only comes from integrating over the outer regime where
aq_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}
is negligible.
In this limit, it is easy to see that the
aq_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}
term in the denominator in (
IV.9
) is negligible compared to the
\mu_{{}_{\perp}}q_{{}_{\perp}}^{4}
term. We will therefore drop it in this regime.

In addition, as noted earlier, in the critical regime
q_{x}\sim q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}\ll q_{{}_{\perp}}
; hence, we can drop the
Kq_{x}^{2}
term in the denominator (
IV.9
) relative to the
q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}
term. Likewise, we can drop the
D^{\prime}_{{}_{\perp}}q_{x}^{2}q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}
term in the numerator relative to the
D_{x}q_{{}_{\perp}}^{4}
term. Dropping these terms as well as the
aq_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}
term in the denominator leaves us with

C({\bf r},t)=\int\frac{{\rm d}^{d}q\,d\omega}{(2\pi)^{d+1}}{2D_{x}q_{{}_{\perp}}^{4}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}-\omega t)}\over\omega^{2}q_{{}_{\perp}}^{4}+\left(\mu_{{}_{\perp}}q_{{}_{\perp}}^{4}+wq_{x}^{2}\right)^{2}}\ .

(IV.13)

Making the change of variables of integration from
(\mathbf{q}_{{}_{\perp}},q_{x},\omega)
to
(\mathbf{Q}_{{}_{\perp}},Q_{x},\Omega)
defined by

\mathbf{q}_{{}_{\perp}}\equiv{\mathbf{Q}_{{}_{\perp}}\over r_{{}_{\perp}}}\ \ \ ,\ \ \ q_{x}\equiv{Q_{x}x_{0}^{\rm lin}\over r_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}}\ \ \ ,\ \ \ \omega\equiv{\Omega\mu_{{}_{\perp}}\over r_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}}\,,

(IV.14)

we immediately find that the real space correlation function takes a scaling form:

C({\bf r},t)=r_{{}_{\perp}}^{2\chi_{\rm lin}}F_{\rm lin}\left({x_{0}^{\rm lin}x\over r_{{}_{\perp}}^{\zeta_{\rm lin}}},{\mu_{{}_{\perp}}t\over r_{{}_{\perp}}^{z_{\rm lin}}}\right)

(IV.15)

with the crossover function
F_{\rm lin}(X,Y)
given by

F_{\rm lin}(X,Y)={2D_{x}\over\sqrt{\mu_{\perp}w}}\int\frac{{\rm d}^{d}Q\,{\rm d}\Omega}{(2\pi)^{d+1}}{Q_{{}_{\perp}}^{4}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}(\mathbf{Q}_{{}_{\perp}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}}+Q_{x}X-\Omega Y)}\over\Omega^{2}Q_{{}_{\perp}}^{4}+\left(Q_{{}_{\perp}}^{4}+Q_{x}^{2}\right)^{2}}\,,

(IV.16)

and the scaling exponents in the linear theory given by

{\zeta_{\rm lin}}={z_{\rm lin}}=2\ \ \ ,\ \ \ \chi_{\rm lin}={1-d\over 2}\,.

(IV.17)

In (
IV.14
), we’ve defined the microscopic length

x_{0}^{\rm lin}\equiv\sqrt{\mu_{{}_{\perp}}\over w}

(IV.18)

which goes to a finite constant as the transition is approached.

The scaling form (
IV.15
) implies (
I.2
) quoted in the introduction with

\chi=\chi_{\rm lin}\,,~~z=z_{\rm lin}\,,~~\zeta=\zeta_{\rm lin}\,,

(IV.19)

and the following connection between the non-universal non-critical microscopic lengths and time:

a_{{}_{\perp}}=\sqrt{a_{x}x_{0}^{\rm lin}}=\sqrt{\mu_{{}_{\perp}}\tau_{0}}\,.

(IV.20)

In three dimensions, all of the linear results for the
critical
regime just quoted continue to hold in the full theory. In two dimensions, they do not.

IV.2.2
Equal-time velocity correlations in the critical regime in the linear theory

As we did in the previous subsection, we will, for the critical regime, drop the
aq_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}
term and
the
Kq_{x}^{2}
term in the denominator in (
IV.9
) , and the
D^{\prime}_{{}_{\perp}}q_{x}^{2}q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}
term in the numerator.
Dropping these terms and setting

leaves us with

C({\bf r},0)=\int\frac{{\rm d}^{d}q\,d\omega}{(2\pi)^{d+1}}{2D_{x}q_{{}_{\perp}}^{4}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\over\omega^{2}q_{{}_{\perp}}^{4}+\left(\mu_{{}_{\perp}}q_{{}_{\perp}}^{4}+wq_{x}^{2}\right)^{2}}\ .

(IV.21)

This is an analytically tractable integral; the result in
d=3
is

\displaystyle C({\bf r},0)={D_{x}\over 8\pi\mu_{{}_{\perp}}|x|}\exp\left(-{r_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}\over 4x_{0}^{\rm lin}|x|}\right)\ \ \ ,\ \ \ (d=3)\,.

(IV.22)

Note that this result agrees with the scaling form (
IV.15
), with the crossover function at equal time given by

F_{\rm lin}(X,0)={D_{x}\over 8\pi\sqrt{\mu_{\perp}w}}\left[{\exp\left(-{1\over 4|X|}\right)\over|X|}\right]\ \ \ ,\ \ \ (d=3)\,.

(IV.23)

In
d=2
in the critical regime, we find

C({\bf r},0)={D_{x}\over 4(\mu_{{}_{\perp}}^{3}w)^{1/4}}\left[\exp\left(-{y^{2}\over 4x_{0}^{\rm lin}|x|}\right)\over\sqrt{\pi|x|}\right]\,,(d=2)\,.

(IV.24)

where we’ve taken the single Cartesian component of
\mathbf{r}_{{}_{\perp}}
in
d=2
to be
y
.

Note that this result also agrees with the scaling form (
IV.15
), with the crossover function at equal time given by

F_{\rm lin}(X,0)={D_{x}\over 4\sqrt{\pi\mu_{\perp}w}}\left[{\exp\left(-{1\over 4|X|}\right)\over\sqrt{|X|}}\right]\ \ \ ,\ \ \ (d=2)\,.

(IV.25)

IV.2.3
Equal-time velocity correlations in the non-critical regime in the linear theory

Outside the critical regime -i.e., when one or more of the conditions (
IV.10
) is violated - we can ignore the
\mu_{\perp}q_{\perp}^{4}
term in the denominator of (
IV.9
),
To see this, let’s consider this specific case
r_{{}_{\perp}}\gg\xi_{{}_{\perp}}^{\rm lin}
,
x=0
,
t=0
. In this case the integral is dominated by the regime
q_{{}_{\perp}}\ll\left(\xi_{{}_{\perp}}^{\rm lin}\right)^{-1}
, since, for
q_{{}_{\perp}}\gg\left(\xi_{{}_{\perp}}^{\rm lin}\right)^{-1}
, the magnitude of the integrand is very small, and the integral is canceled off by the oscillating exponential. Therefore, in the dominant regime,
\mu_{\perp}q_{\perp}^{4}
is negligible, since it is smaller than
aq_{\perp}^{2}
. This argument applies to other cases outside the critical regime -i.e.,
|x|\gg\xi_{{}_{\parallel}}^{\rm lin}
,
r_{{}_{\perp}}=0
,
t=0
or
|t|\gg\tau_{{}_{\rm corr}}^{\rm lin}
,
x=0
,
r_{{}_{\perp}}=0
- and leads to the same conclusion.

Ignoring the
\mu_{\perp}q_{\perp}^{4}
term in the denominator of (
IV.9
), setting
t=0
, and integrating over
\omega
gives

C({\bf r},0)=\int\frac{{\rm d}^{d}q}{(2\pi)^{d}}{(D_{x}q_{{}_{\perp}}^{4}+D^{\prime}_{{}_{\perp}}q_{x}^{2}q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}){\rm e}^{{\rm i}\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\over(Kq_{x}^{2}+q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2})(aq_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}+wq_{x}^{2})}\ .

(IV.26)

In
d=3
, it is convenient to rewrite (
IV.26
) as

C({\bf r},0)=-\nabla_{\perp}^{2}\left(\int\frac{{\rm d}^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{3}}{(D_{x}q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}+D^{\prime}_{{}_{\perp}}q_{x}^{2}){\rm e}^{{\rm i}\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\over(Kq_{x}^{2}+q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2})(aq_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}+wq_{x}^{2})}\right)\,.

(IV.27)

The integral in this expression is

\displaystyle g(\mathbf{r})=\int\frac{{\rm d}^{2}q_{{}_{\perp}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,e^{{\rm i}\mathbf{q}_{{}_{\perp}}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{{}_{\perp}}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{{\rm d}q_{x}\over 2\pi}{(D_{x}q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}+D^{\prime}_{{}_{\perp}}q_{x}^{2}){\rm e}^{{\rm i}q_{x}x}\over(Kq_{x}^{2}+q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2})(aq_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}+wq_{x}^{2})}\,.

(IV.28)

The integral over
q_{x}
can be evaluated by simple complex contour techniques, and gives

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{dq_{x}\over 2\pi}{(D_{x}q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}+D^{\prime}_{{}_{\perp}}q_{x}^{2}){\rm e}^{{\rm i}q_{x}x}\over(Kq_{x}^{2}+q_{{}_{\perp}}^{2})(aq_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}+wq_{x}^{2})}

(IV.29)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{A_{1}\exp[-{q_{\perp}|x|\over\sqrt{K}}]+B_{1}\exp[-q_{\perp}|x|\sqrt{\alpha_{\rm lin}}]}{2(aK-w)q_{\perp}}\,,~~~

where we’ve defined the dimensionless parameter

\displaystyle\alpha_{\rm lin}\equiv{a\over w}={\mu_{{}_{\perp}}\over w(\xi_{{}_{\perp}}^{\rm lin})^{2}}=\left(x_{0}^{\rm lin}\over\xi_{{}_{\perp}}^{\rm lin}\right)^{2}\,,

(IV.30)

and the dimensionful constants

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle D_{x}\sqrt{K}-{D^{\prime}_{{}_{\perp}}\over\sqrt{K}}\ ,

(IV.31)

\displaystyle B_{1}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle-{D_{x}\over\sqrt{\alpha_{\rm lin}}}+D^{\prime}_{\perp}\sqrt{\alpha_{\rm lin}}\,.

(IV.32)

In the second equality for
\alpha_{\rm lin}
, we have used our definition (
IV.11
) of the correlation length
\xi_{{}_{\perp}}
.

Inserting (
IV.29
) into (
IV.28
) gives

g(\mathbf{r})={1\over 2(aK-w)}\int\frac{{\rm d}^{2}q_{{}_{\perp}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\left({1\over q_{{}_{\perp}}}\right)\left(A_{1}\exp\bigg[i\mathbf{q}_{{}_{\perp}}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{{}_{\perp}}-{q_{{}_{\perp}}|x|\over\sqrt{K}}\bigg]+B_{1}\exp\bigg[i\mathbf{q}_{{}_{\perp}}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{{}_{\perp}}-q_{{}_{\perp}}|x|\sqrt{\alpha_{\rm lin}}\bigg]\right)\,.

(IV.33)

Evaluating the integral of the
B_{1}
term in polar coordinates gives

\displaystyle\int\frac{{\rm d}^{2}q}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\left({1\over q_{\perp}}\right)\exp\bigg[i\mathbf{q}_{{}_{\perp}}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{{}_{\perp}}-q_{\perp}|x|\sqrt{\alpha_{\rm lin}}\,\bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}{{\rm d}\theta\over(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\bigg[-q_{\perp}\bigg(|x|\sqrt{\alpha_{\rm lin}}-ir_{{}_{\perp}}\cos\theta\bigg)\bigg]dq_{\perp}

(IV.34)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}{{\rm d}\theta\over(2\pi)^{2}}\left({|x|\sqrt{\alpha_{\rm lin}}+ir_{{}_{\perp}}\cos\theta\over x^{2}\alpha_{\rm lin}+r_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}\cos^{2}\theta}\right)\,.

The
\cos\theta
factor in the numerator of this expression is odd under
\theta\to\theta+\pi
, while the denominator of the entire expression is even. Hence, that
\cos\theta
factor in the numerator integrates to
0
, and can therefore be dropped, leaving us with

\displaystyle\int\frac{{\rm d}^{2}q}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\left({1\over q_{\perp}}\right)\exp\bigg[i\mathbf{q}_{{}_{\perp}}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{{}_{\perp}}-q_{\perp}|x|\sqrt{\alpha_{\rm lin}}\,\bigg]

(IV.35)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{|x|\sqrt{\alpha_{\rm lin}}\over(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}{{\rm d}\theta\over x^{2}\alpha_{\rm lin}+r_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}\cos^{2}\theta}\,.

The final angular integral in this expression is elementary, and gives

\displaystyle\int\frac{{\rm d}^{2}q}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\left({1\over q_{\perp}}\right)\exp\bigg[i\mathbf{q}_{{}_{\perp}}\cdot\mathbf{r}_{{}_{\perp}}-q_{\perp}|x|\sqrt{\alpha_{\rm lin}}\,\bigg]

(IV.36)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{1\over 2\pi\sqrt{x^{2}\alpha_{\rm lin}+r_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}}}\,.

The
A_{1}
term can be evaluated in precisely the same way; the result is an identical expression with the replacement
\alpha_{\rm lin}\to{1\over K}
.

Using these results in our expression (
IV.33
) gives, in
d=3

g(\mathbf{r})={1\over 4\pi(aK-w)}\left[{A_{1}\over\sqrt{{x^{2}\over K}+r_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}}}+{B_{1}\over\sqrt{x^{2}\alpha_{\rm lin}+r_{{}_{\perp}}^{2}}}\right]\,.

(IV.37)

Inserting this into (
IV.27
) gives

\displaystyle C({\bf r},0)={f_{{}_{3D}}(\theta)\over r^{3}}\ \ \ ,\ \ \ d=3\,

(IV.38)

where
\theta=\tan^{-1}\left({r_{\perp}\over|x|}\right)
is the polar angle of
\mathbf{r}
from the
x
-axis,
and

\displaystyle f_{{}_{3D}}(\theta)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle{\xi_{{}_{\perp}}^{\rm lin}\over 4\pi\sqrt{\mu_{{}_{\perp}}w}\left(1-{K\alpha_{\rm lin}}\right)}\left[\left(D_{x}-D^{\prime}_{{}_{\perp}}\alpha_{\rm lin}\right){(2\alpha_{\rm lin}\cos^{2}\theta-\sin^{2}\theta)\over(\sin^{2}\theta+\alpha_{\rm lin}\cos^{2}\theta)^{5/2}}\right.

(IV.39)

\displaystyle\left.-K(KD_{x}-D^{\prime}_{{}_{\perp}})\sqrt{\alpha_{\rm lin}}{(2\cos^{2}\theta-K\sin^{2}\theta)\over(K\sin^{2}\theta+\cos^{2}\theta)^{5/2}}\right]\,.

Near the critical point, since
\xi_{{}_{\perp}}^{\rm lin}\gg x_{0}^{\rm lin}
and hence
\alpha_{\rm lin}\ll 1
,

VII
Renormalization Group analysis

To deal with the effects of the
u
non-linearity in our equation of motion (
VI.1
), we can use a variety of renormalization group approaches. If we are only interested in the
statics
- that is, the equal-time correlation functions - then we can use the static renormalization group developed by Kadanoff, Wilson, and Fisher
KWF
, applied directly to the Boltzmann weight associated with the Hamiltonian (
VI.2
). This was the approach taken by
Aharony 1973
and
Brézin and Zinn-Justin 1976
. To determine
time dependent correlations, we can supplement the RG results from the static RG with a dynamical renormalization group analysis, as was done by
Folk et al. 1977
.

We will now review these two approaches here. Our approach, and, therefore, our recursion relations, differ slightly in some technical details from those of
Aharony 1973
;
Brézin and Zinn-Justin 1976
;
Folk et al. 1977
(particularly in our choice of the Brillouin zone shape). Our results for physically observable quantities are, of course, exactly the same as theirs, since there is only one correct answer!

In the next subsection, we will explain the mechanics of the renormalization group, and present the recursion relations.

VII.1
The renormalization group (RG) approach and recursion relations in
d\leq 3

VII.1.1
The static RG

The
static
RG
KWF
starts by ignoring the time dependence of the field

v_{x}(\mathbf{r},t)
, because the steady-state probability distribution
P(\{v_{x}(\mathbf{r},t\})
of a specified spatial configuration of the field
v_{x}(\mathbf{r},t)
at any fixed time
t
is time-independent, and given simply by the Boltzmann weight

P(\{v_{x}(\mathbf{r},t\})={e^{-\beta H[\{v_{x}(\mathbf{r},t\}]}\over Z}\,,

(VII.1)

with
\beta\equiv 1/{k_{B}T}
, and
{k_{B}T}
given by equation (
VI.6
),
associated with the Hamiltonian (
VI.2
), where
Z
is the partition function for
H
. Since this is independent of time, we will, for the remainder of this discussion of the static RG, drop the time argument of
v_{x}(\mathbf{r},t)
.

The static RG now proceeds in three steps. First, we separate the field

v_{x}(\mathbf{r})
into “fast” and “slow”
components
v_{x}^{>}(\mathbf{r})
and
v_{x}^{<}(\mathbf{r})
, where the “fast”
component
v_{x}^{>}(\mathbf{r})
evolves rapidly in space, while the “slow” component
v_{x}^{<}(\mathbf{r})
evolves slowly in space. More precisely,
we write

v_{x}(\mathbf{r})=v_{x}^{<}(\mathbf{r})+v_{x}^{>}(\mathbf{r})\,,

(VII.2)

where the fast component
v_{x}^{>}(\mathbf{r})
only has support in the infinitely long
d
-dimensional hypercylinder “shell” of Fourier
space
b^{-1}\Lambda\leq|\mathbf{q}_{\perp}|\leq\Lambda
,
-\infty<q_{x}<\infty
, where
\Lambda
is an
“ultra-violet cutoff”, while the “slow” component
v_{x}^{<}(\mathbf{r})

has support in the “core”
0\leq|\mathbf{q}_{\perp}|\leq b^{-1}\Lambda
,
-\infty<q_{x}<\infty
(Hence the superscripts
>
and
<
for the fast and slow components respectively). Here

\Lambda
is of order the inverse of some microscopic length
a
(e.g., the inter-bird distance). The rescaling factor

b
is
>1
, but otherwise arbitrary. Later, to obtain
differential
recursion relations, we will take
b\equiv e^{{\rm d}\ell}
to
be close to
1
; that is, we‘ll take
{\rm d}\ell\ll 1
.

The second step of the static RG is to derive an “intermediate” Hamiltonian
H_{I}(\{v^{<}_{x}(\mathbf{r})\})
for the
slow degrees of freedom
v_{x}^{<}
by integrating the Boltzmann weight

Z^{-1}e^{-\beta H[\{v_{x}^{<}(\mathbf{r}),v_{x}^{>}(\mathbf{r})\}]}
over the “fast”
degrees of freedom
v_{x}^{>}(\mathbf{r})
. That is,
we write

Z_{I}^{-1}e^{-\beta H_{I}[\{v^{<}_{x}(\mathbf{r})\}]}=Z^{-1}\int\prod_{\mathbf{q}}{\rm d}v_{x}^{>}(\mathbf{q})\;e^{-\beta H[\{v_{x}^{<}(\mathbf{r}),v_{x}^{>}(\mathbf{r})\}]}=Z_{0}Z^{-1}e^{-\beta H_{h}[\{v_{x}^{<}(\mathbf{r})\}]}\big\langle e^{-\beta H_{a}[\{v_{x}(\mathbf{r}),v_{x}^{>}(\mathbf{r})\}]}\big\rangle_{0}^{>}\,,

(VII.3)

where
\int\prod_{\mathbf{q}}dv_{x}^{>}(\mathbf{q})
denotes an integral over
only
the fast degrees of freedom
v_{x}^{>}(\mathbf{q})
, and the symbol

\langle\ldots\rangle_{0}^{>}
denotes an average of
\ldots
over those
fast modes using
only
the Boltzmann weight

Z_{0}^{-1}e^{-\beta H_{h}[v_{x}^{>}(\mathbf{r})]}
for the purely quadratic part
of the Hamiltonian. Since this Hamiltonian is quadratic, these
averages are straightforward to evaluate in perturbation theory (which amounts to expanding the exponential in
\big\langle e^{-\beta H_{a}[\{v_{x}(\mathbf{r}),v_{x}^{>}(\mathbf{r})\}]}\big\rangle_{0}^{>}
, being averages over a purely
Gaussian distribution.

As usual in DRG calculations
fns
, we need to make approximations to perform the averaging step.

The third and final step of the static RG is to rescale time, lengths, and fields as

\displaystyle x\to xb^{\zeta}\ \ \ ,\ \ \ \mathbf{r}_{{}_{\perp}}\to\mathbf{r}_{{}_{\perp}}b\ \ \ ,\ \ \ v_{x}\to v_{x}b^{\chi}\,,

(VII.4)

to restore the Brillouin zone to its original size. The “anisotropy” exponent
\zeta
and the “roughness” exponent
\chi
are at this point arbitrary. We will later choose them to produce fixed points of the RG.

The RG now proceeds by iterating this process. The result can be summarized by
differential
recursion relations in the following (by now very standard) manner:
as already mentioned, we choose
b=1+{\rm d}\ell
with
{\rm d}\ell
differential. Instead of keeping track of the number
n
of iterations of the renormalization group, we introduce a “renormalization group time”

defined as
\ell\equiv n{\rm d}\ell
.

The resultant renormalized Hamiltonian now becomes a function of the RG time
\ell
. Since the symmetry of our system remains the same under this process, the
form
of the Hamiltonian remains that of equation (
VI.2
), but with the original (or “bare“ parameters
m
,
c_{s}
,
c_{d}
, and
u
replaced with “renormalized” values
m(\ell)
,
c_{s}(\ell)
,
c_{d}(\ell)
, and
u(\ell)

VII.2

VII.2.1

We can see immediately from the recursion relation (

The RG flows in the

This structure of the renormalization group flows is the same as that of the conventional Wilson-Fisher fixed point

In contrast to the

To show this in detail, we begin by linearizing the recursion relations (
) and (
VII.13
) about the fixed point (
VII.62

m(\ell)=m^{*}+\delta m(\ell)\ \ \ ,\ \ \ g(\ell)=g^{*}+\delta g(\ell)

) to linear order in the departures
\delta m

\displaystyle{{\rm d}\delta m\over{\rm d}\ell}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(2-{\epsilon\over 3})\delta m+6c_{s}\Lambda^{2}\delta g+O(\epsilon^{2})\delta m+O(\epsilon)\delta g\,,

\displaystyle{{\rm d}\delta g\over{\rm d}\ell}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\epsilon\delta g+O(\epsilon^{2})\delta m+O(\epsilon^{2})\delta g\,.

(VII.65)

Seeking solutions to this linearized system of an exponential form; i.e.,

\displaystyle\delta m(\ell)=S_{m}{\rm e}^{\kappa\ell}\ \ \ ,\ \ \ g(\ell)=S_{g}{\rm e}^{\kappa\ell}\,,

(VII.66)

where

\kappa

\displaystyle\kappa_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\epsilon+O(\epsilon^{2})

(VII.67)

\displaystyle\kappa_{2}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\kappa_{t}=2-\frac{1}{3}\epsilon+O(\epsilon^{2})

(VII.68)

We identify
\kappa_{t}
, which is the only positive eigenvalue, as the “thermal” eigenvalue. We use this term in the usual RG sense, which is that it determines the dependence of the correlation length on the departure of the control parameter (which is usually temperature in equilibrium problems, hence the term “thermal eigenvalue”) from its critical value.

We can see this by the following completely standard RG analysis:

Note that the general solution of our linearized recursion relations for
\delta m(\ell)

\displaystyle\delta m(\ell)=a(m_{0})e^{-\epsilon\ell}+f(m_{0})e^{\kappa_{t}\ell}\,,

(VII.69)

where the constants

and

are determined by the initial (i.e., bare) parameters of the model, and hence, in particular, the bare value
m_{0}
of
m

As in our discussion of the 3D case,
f(m_{0})
vanishes right at the critical point (i.e.,when the experimental control parameter
p
reaches the critical value
p_{c}
). We expect that near the critical point

f(m_{0}(p))\equiv m_{0}-m_{c}\approx C_{f}(p-p_{c})/p_{c}\,,

(VII.70)

where
C_{f}/p_{c}\equiv{{\rm d}f(m_{0}(p))\over{\rm d}p}|_{p=p_{c}}
is a non-universal positive constant.

Recognizing this, we can now use the expansion (
VII.70
)
to obtain the behavior of the correlation length near the critical point by the following very standard RG argument, which is similar to, but actually simpler than, the argument we used in the 3D case.

Starting with any
m_{0}
near
m_{c}

\ell^{*}(m_{0})
of
\ell

\delta m(\ell^{*})

For
m_{0}
close to
m_{c}

\ell^{*}
required to reach such a large
\delta m(\ell^{*})
will clearly be large, since the coefficient
f(m_{0})
of the exponentially growing part of the solution (
VII.69

\ell

\ell^{*}

a

VII.69

\delta m(\ell^{*})\approx f(m_{0})e^{\kappa_{t}\ell^{*}}=\left(\delta m\right)_{r}\,,

VIII
Conclusion & Outlook

In this paper, we have analyzed the universal critical behavior of an incompressible polar active fluid with an easy axis at the order-disorder transition. By using dynamic renormalization group arguments, we map our active, non-equilibrium model onto the equilibrium Ising model with long range dipolar interactions, whose static and dynamic (under model A non-conservative dynamics) behaviors have previously been studied. As a result, we are able to obtain the critical scaling laws for such systems exactly in
d=3
, and up to
O(\epsilon^{2})
in a
d=3-\epsilon
expansion in
d=2
.

As in previous studies of incompressible polar active fluid
Chen et al. 2016
;
Chen et al. 2018a
, the success of our mapping onto thermal systems relies on the irrelevance of the advective
\lambda_{1,2}
terms in the equation of motion (
II.2
). In contrast, in all active systems that have been analyzed so far in which the advective term is relevant in a dry active fluid model, the associated universal behavior has always been distinct from that of any thermal systems
Toner and Tu 1995
;
Toner and Tu 1998
;
Toner 2012
;
Chen et al. 2015
;
Chen et al. 2018b
.

Looking ahead, it would be of great interest to identify terms in other non-equilibrium systems that play a similarly key role in revealing the non-equilibrium signature of the systems.
