Health disparities and industrial emissions: a case study of semiconductor manufacturing and asthma morbidity in Austin, Texas
Authors: Sarah Chambliss, Aquiel Warner, Corwin Zigler, Emily M Hall, Catherine Cubbin, Elizabeth J Mueller, Roger D Peng, Elizabeth C Matsui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRacialized and lower-income communities often face disproportionate health risks from air pollution. In central Texas, semiconductor fabrication plants (SFPs) are a major emissions source, but their health effects and impact on disparities remain unclear. This study examined 217 census tracts in Travis County, Texas, calculating an SFP exposure score based on facility-reported emissions, weighted by proximity. Using distance-adjusted propensity score matching, this study compared 30 tracts within 2 km of SFPs to similar nearby tracts, controlling for demographics and socioeconomic indicators. Results showed SFPs were located in tracts with higher proportions of Black (12% vs. 7%) and Latinx (46% vs. 30%) residents and higher indicators of social vulnerability. Each one-standard deviation increase in SFP exposure corresponded to 9.3% higher asthma ED visits in the total population (IRR = 1.093, 95% CI: 1.017–1.172) and 12.4% in children (IRR = 1.124, 1.058–1.232). Estimates were unchanged when controlling for black carbon and sulfate. These findings indicate that semiconductor manufacturing emissions are concentrated in neighborhoods with a higher share of racialized residents and are independently linked to higher population-level rates of asthma morbidity. These findings suggest that expansion of the US semiconductor industry warrants increased public health surveillance and equity-informed decision making in future facility siting.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11111-026-00524-y.Keywords: Asthma, Air pollution, Health disparities, Respiratory health, Environmental justice

Introduction

Air pollution exposure inequalities by race and ethnicity in the United States persist alongside and independently from income-based disparities (Clark et al., 2014; Colmer et al., 2020; Jbaily et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2021), contributing to excess burdens of respiratory, cardiovascular, and metabolic diseases among minoritized populations (Akinbami et al., 2014; Daouda et al., 2021; Mork et al., 2024). Existing policies such as National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS) have lowered total air pollution levels, with positive public health impacts (Fann et al., 2012). However, significant inequalities in racial and ethnic exposure remain at magnitudes that affect health outcomes (Kravitz-Wirtz et al., 2016; Scott et al., 2023). Within-city differences in air pollution exposure may exceed regional variation, driving persistent exposure disparities (Chambliss et al., 2021; Jerrett et al., 2005; Scolio et al., 2024). Recent work has established that even small, persistent census tract-level differences within a metropolitan area are associated with higher rates of asthma acute care use (Chambliss et al., 2024). Identifying sources of localized excess exposure and linking these measures to local health outcomes is a key step forward in addressing environmental health disparities.

Prior studies have documented disproportionate exposure to major emission sources (e.g., energy generation, vehicle traffic) among racialized groups (Henneman et al., 2021; Mohai & Saha, 2015; Morello-Frosch & Jesdale, 2006; Tessum et al., 2021), and, in some cases, linked these disparities to health outcomes (Brender et al., 2011; Johnston et al., 2024). Fewer studies have quantified inequitable exposure to specific industries that play a key economic role in individual cities, such as iron and steel production in Cleveland and Detroit, freight transport in Oakland and Long Beach, and oil refining in Philadelphia and Houston (Apte et al., 2017; Carpenter & Wagner, 2019; Houston et al., 2014; Yuan et al., 2024). There are calls to understand whether and how the inequalities in exposure documented in such studies may affect health disparities, and to use those estimates to inform intervention policies (Levy, 2021; Mork et al., 2024). This study examines whether semiconductor manufacturing, a major local industry in Austin, Texas, contributes to asthma-related emergency department (ED) visit rates in surrounding neighborhoods. Briefly, we link tract-level ED visit counts from the Texas Health Care Information Collection (2016–2017) with sociodemographic measures from the American Community Survey (2013–2017 5-year estimates), facility-reported emissions, and tract-level fine particulate matter concentrations across 217 census tracts in Travis County.

Local-scale analyses face challenges that justify a pragmatic approach. Estimating the impact of a single industry type within a metropolitan area can require extensive, case-specific data collection and processing, often involving specialized expertise and coordination across data sources. Moreover, chemical transport models designed to operate on larger spatial scales depend on emission inventories that may be inaccurate for individual facilities, and the development of industry-specific data and street-level dispersion modeling may be prohibitively resource intensive (Day et al., 2019; Greco et al., 2007; Miller et al., 2006; Vardoulakis et al., 2003). Furthermore, health risks arise from complex chemical mixtures that are not represented by simple dispersion models (Carlin & Rider, 2024; Joubert et al., 2022; Schraufnagel et al., 2019). Industrial facility siting is often correlated with neighborhood demographic and socioeconomic composition of surrounding neighborhoods (Bullard, 2018; Chakraborty et al., 2011; Keet et al., 2017; Matthay & Glymour, 2022; Mohai & Saha, 2015). The co-occurrence of industry with other neighborhood-level risk factors such as poverty and lower access to health care complicates the attribution of health damages to specific exposures (Correa-Agudelo et al., 2022; Keet et al., 2017; Pollack et al., 2023; Shankardass et al., 2009). Public health data rarely match the fine spatial resolution needed to capture near-source impacts (Jerrett et al., 2013; Krieger et al., 2002), and small sample sizes can constrain statistical power.

To address these challenges, we apply a cross-sectional ecologic analysis that combines activity-weighted measures of proximity to sources with spatially explicit sociodemographic propensity matching and controls for other pollutants. This case study of semiconductor fabrication plants in Austin, Texas links emissions to observed health outcomes using government-collected data, offering a transparent and replicable framework for other metropolitan areas.

Methods

Exposures

SFP proximity

We selected semiconductor manufacturing as our case study due to its significant industrial presence in the Austin, Texas area and its role as a major source of volatile organic compounds (VOCs), which consist of a wide range of chemical species of varying toxicity and contribute to atmospheric formation of PM2.5. SFPs also emit both fine and coarse particulate matter (PM2.5 and PM10) emissions (see Supplemental Information for further context). SFPs operating in Travis County during the study period (2016–2017) were identified from facility-reported emissions data available from the Texas Commission on Environmental Quality (TCEQ). This included four SFP locations, labeled A-D (Fig. 1). For propensity-score matching and dichotomous comparisons, we categorize census tracts as “SFP adjacent” if any portion of the tract lies within 2 km from the central coordinates of an SFP facility (Figure S1). The distance of 2 km was chosen consistent with atmospheric dynamics of PM2.5 precursors, the average area of an SFP facility, and previous work classifying exposure based on proximity (Brender et al., 2011; Johnston et al., 2024; Morello-Frosch & Jesdale, 2006).

SFP exposure scores

We assigned SFP exposure scores for every tract in Travis County based on a metric that combines facility-reported emissions (in units of tons per year, TPY) and the inverse distance from between the coordinates of an SFP facility and the census tract centroid, a simplified approach to representing plume exposure as a power-law decay pattern of source-related pollution with distance from a facility.(de Mesnard, 2013; Johnston et al., 2024; McKenzie et al., 2014; Zou et al., 2009) The SFP exposure score for each census tract was calculated as the sum of the inverse distance-weighted emissions from all four SFP facilities, as shown in Eq. 1:

where SFP_exposure_scorei is the total exposure score for census tract i (in units of TPY/(km + 1), j represents each of the four individual SFP facilities, Ej is the reported annual emissions of VOCs for facility j in tons per year (TPY). For the 2017 study year, these values were 101, 11, 28, and 7 TPY for facilities A, B, C, and D, respectively, obtained from the TCEQ point source emission inventory (Texas Commission on Environmental Quality, 2023). Dij is the distance in kilometers from the centroid of census tract i to the central coordinates of facility j. A value of 1 is added to the denominator to avoid division by zero when Dij = 0 and to ensure that the contribution from any single facility does not exceed its reported emissions Ej even for tracts within 1 km. VOC emissions were chosen as a proxy for the overall activity level of a facility because they constitute the predominant mass contribution of SFPs to ambient air pollution.

Other pollutant exposures

To control for co-located emission activity not attributable to SFPs, we included ambient concentrations of select major components of PM2.5 (µg/m3) associated with other emission sources, estimated by the Atmospheric Composition Analysis Group for the year 2017 (van Donkelaar et al., 2019). Black carbon (BC) and sulfate (SO4) were chosen as two components not directly associated with semiconductor fabrication processes but potentially associated with co-located sources. BC is generated by diesel combustion in vehicles and freight transportation, and was used as a surrogate for heavy duty traffic-related pollution. SO4, which is formed from sulfur dioxide released from industrial sources, especially energy production and waste management, was used as a surrogate for co-located industry. We also examined whether the estimated effects of SFP exposure were attenuated when controlling for the concentration of organic carbon (OC), which we treat as a proxy for emitted VOCs that condense in the atmosphere (often referred to as secondary organic aerosol, or SOA). This sensitivity analysis served the purpose of exploring whether the observed association is driven specifically by the SFP’s contribution to particulate matter via this SOA formation pathway. If the SFP association remains robust after controlling for OC, this would suggest that health impacts are linked to specific characteristics of SFP emissions beyond their contribution to regional organic aerosol formation.

Demographic and socioeconomic covariates

Socioeconomic characteristics that have been identified by others as asthma-relevant social determinants of health were included in models as control covariates (Grant et al., 2022; Zárate et al., 2024). Tracts based on the 2010 US Census were used as the primary unit of analysis, as they were the smallest geographic unit at which our health data could be aggregated. Census tracts provide standardized, consistently documented geographic boundaries, although they may not directly correspond to neighborhood boundaries. Demographic and socioeconomic data were obtained from the US Census Bureau’s American Community Survey (ACS) 2013–2017 5-Year estimates and include the total population, pediatric population (children aged < 18 years), proportions of the population identifying as African American or Black alone (hereon, “Black”) and identifying as of Hispanic, Latino, or Spanish origin of any race (hereon, “Latinx”), the share of census tract residents [1] with household income below the federal poverty level (“Below the FPL”), [2] without medical insurance (“No insurance”), and [3] adults aged ≥ 25 years with less than high school educational attainment (< HS Education).

Health outcome: asthma-related emergency department visit rates

ED visit data were obtained from the Texas Department of State Health Services Texas Healthcare Information Collection (THCIC), a statewide database of healthcare activity in hospitals and health maintenance organizations operating in Texas (Zárate et al., 2024). ED visits were aggregated based on the census tract identified in each visit record, corresponding to the residence of the patient. Population-based asthma ED incidence rates (PBIR; events per 10,000 person-years) were calculated for each tract as the total number of ED visits with a primary ICD-10 asthma diagnosis (J45.x) in 2016–2017 divided by the summed two-year tract population denominator (ACS 2012–2016 5-year estimate for 2016 + ACS 2013–2017 5-year estimate for 2017), multiplied by 10,000. Records are limited to individuals seen in a stand-alone or hospital-based emergency department within Texas and include individuals subsequently hospitalized.

Statistical analysis

Association of SFP location with neighborhood attributes and propensity scores

Associations between SFP adjacency and exposure and neighborhood features were estimated using a logistic regression model predicting the odds of a census tract being adjacent to an SFP given demographic covariates (‘No insurance’, ‘Below the FPL’, ‘< HS Education’, ‘Black’, and ‘Latinx’). Odds ratios (ORs) for a 10% increase were calculated for each covariate separately, and coefficients were compared against multivariate models including all census covariates with and without the inclusion of BC and SO4.We used distance-adjusted propensity score matching to control for potential confounding in modeling the effect of SFP exposure on asthma ED visits (detailed methods available in the online supplemental information (SI)). Covariates were well balanced between the SFP-adjacent and matched group (Table S1).

Association of population-based asthma ED visit rates with SFP location

Poisson regression was used to estimate the incidence risk ratio (IRR) for census tract-level asthma-related ED visit PBIR related to SFP exposure scores among propensity-matched tracts. Base models used a doubly robust approach that controlled for the census-based covariates (percentage of census tract residents who are Black, Latinx, living below the FPL, < HS education, no insurance) used for propensity matching. Additional models included controls for co-located emissions (both SO4 and BC, with and without OC). Additional sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the focal association when using alternative approaches, including when SFP exposure is represented by simplified metrics (inverse distance without emissions weighting and SFP adjacency) and a model including all tracts with population > 0 within the county rather than the limited propensity-matched selection.

Results

Study population

In 2017, Travis County had a total population of 1.16 million, with population characteristics described in Table 1. There was a visible clustering of tracts with a high share of Black or Latinx residents in the eastern side of Travis County (Figure S1). As described in previous work by Zarate et al. (2024), there were significant similarities in the spatial patterns of indicators of low SES and higher share of minoritized residents. This spatial clustering also coincided with patterns of asthma-related ED visits. For the county as a whole, there were a total of 10,034 asthma-related ED visits for the years 2016–2017, with 4,886 among children (< 18 years). Among census tracts, the mean (SD) asthma-related ED PBIR was 22.7 (21.3) per 10,000 person-years for the total population and 55.0 (63.2) for children (Table 1).

Exposure to SFPs and other pollution

Among all census tracts in the county, the mean (SD) concentration of total PM2.5 was 7.63 (0.39) µg/m3, BC was 0.58 (0.06) µg/m3, OC was 2.84 (0.22) µg/m3, and SO4 was 1.69 (0.12) µg/m3. BC constituted a relatively small share by mass but a typical concentration for urban areas in the US (Chambliss et al., 2020), and both SO4 and OC constituted larger shares. The four SFPs were distributed across the eastern half of Travis County, with 30 tracts (14%) within 2 km of a facility (“SFP-adjacent”). Among all census tracts, the mean (sd) SFP exposure score (inverse distance-weighted emissions in units of annual tons of VOC/(km + 1), was 0.013 (0.006), with distributions shown in Figure S2; because emissions activity varies significantly across facilities, there are some SFP adjacent tracts with relatively low exposure scores, and some non-adjacent tracts near the highest emitting facility with relatively high scores. For interpretability, SFP exposure scores are normalized as z-scores in all following analyses. The highest SFP exposure scores occurred around Facility A, in the northeast portion of the county (Fig. 1). All three pollutants and SFP exposure scores showed statistical evidence of high spatial autocorrelation (Moran’s I p < 0.001), and there were moderate-to-low correlations between PM2.5 and component exposures and SFP exposure scores (Table S2). In a dichotomous comparison (Wilcoxon rank-sum test), there was a small but statistically significant difference in OC between the 30 SFP-adjacent tracts and the other 188 tracts (means of 2.95 vs. 2.82 µg/m3, p < 0.01), but no statistically significant difference in SO4 (1.70 vs. 1.69 µg/m3, p = 0.6) or BC (0.60 vs. 0.58 µg/m3, p = 0.08).

Neighborhood characteristics and predictors of SFP adjacency

Table 2 compares tract characteristics by SFP adjacency, and Figure S3 summarizes logistic models predicting adjacency. Neighborhood demographics varied significantly between census tracts adjacent to SFPs and non-adjacent tracts (Table 2 columns 2–3). SFP-adjacent tracts had a significantly higher proportion of Black residents (12% vs. 7%, p < 0.01) and Latinx residents (46% vs. 30%, p < 0.001), and higher levels of lower educational attainment, lack of insurance, and poverty. Using population-weighted means, mean SFP exposure scores among Travis County residents was 0.5 SD higher for Black residents and 0.25 SD higher for Latinx residents, and 0.25 SD lower for white residents, compared with the county-wide mean. In univariate logistic models, all race/ethnicity and socioeconomic covariates were positively associated with SFP adjacency, with statistically significance for all variables except poverty. The strongest associations were observed for percentage of Black residents, percentage of residents without health insurance, and percentage of adults with less than a high school education (Figure S3). In the multivariate model including all covariates, odds ratios remained positive for all variables except poverty, but only the percentage of Black residents remained statistically significant. Because BC and SO4 were not significantly associated with SFP adjacency in univariate models, propensity matching used only race/ethnicity and socioeconomic covariates. BC and SO4 were nonetheless included in the primary exposure-outcome models. Figure S1 maps SFP-adjacent and matched controls.

SFP exposure and asthma-related ED visits

To estimate associations between SFP exposure and asthma ED visits while reducing confounding by neighborhood sociodemographic composition, we restricted the primary analysis to distance-adjusted propensity score-matched tracts (SFP-adjacent tracts matched to nearby non-adjacent tracts with similar covariates). Among the propensity-matched tracts, an increase of one standard deviation (SD) in SFP exposure score (4.6 TPY/(km + 1)) was associated with 9.3% (Incidence risk ratio (IRR) = 1.093, 95% CI = [1.017, 1.172]) higher total-population incidence of asthma-related ED visits, and 12.4% (1.142, [1.058, 1.232]) higher incidence of asthma-related ED visits for the pediatric population (Table 3). IRR values remained consistent in models adjusting for the annual mean concentration of BC and SO4, suggesting that the estimated risk was not associated with other emission-generating activities that may be associated with tract-level SFPs. IRR values also remained consistent in models adjusting for OC, suggesting that the health risks related to proximity to SFP activity are not directly related to the contribution of SFP to the secondary organic aerosol component of PM2.5 via VOCs. Analyses examining the sensitivity of these results to different definitions of SFP exposure show that measures of SFP exposure that do not account for differences in the magnitude of emissions did not detect a significant association (Table S3). A model expanding the analysis to all tracts in the county rather than propensity-matched pairs estimated a similar effect size but wider confidence intervals.

Discussion

We found that in Travis County, Texas, SFPs were disproportionately located in neighborhoods with a higher share of Black and Latinx residents, as well as higher rates of socioeconomic indicators of social vulnerability. Further, after controlling for differences in racial and ethnic composition and socioeconomic indicators, SFP exposure was associated with higher rates of asthma-related emergency department visits, with each one SD increase in SFP exposure score associated with a 9.3% [95% CI: 1.2% − 17.2%] higher risk of asthma ED visits for the total population and a 12.4% [5.8% − 23.2%] higher risk for the population under the age of 18. There was no attenuation of IRR estimates in models that controlled for pollutants that are markers of diesel combustion (BC) and other fossil fuel burning (SO4), suggesting that the estimated risks were associated with pollution generated by semiconductor fabrication activity and not with other nearby sources.

Our exposure metric (VOC emissions divided by (distance + 1 km)) allows approximate comparisons of risk based on either a defined annual emissions total (risk varying by distance) or a defined distance (risk varying with emissions changes). For example, we may consider a facility located 2 km from a tract increasing activity at a level corresponding to an additional 10 TPY. This would correspond to ~ 7% higher asthma ED visit rates, or roughly 1 additional ED visit per year for a census tract with 6,400 residents. With 30 tracts in Travis County within 2 km of an SFP, and observed PBIR in these tracts (54.5 per 10,000 person-years) far above the county average, these findings suggest that increases in semiconductor manufacturing have meaningful implications for public health. However, these estimates should be interpreted with some caution. VOC emissions were used as a proxy for facility activity rather than direct exposure, and our sensitivity analysis suggests that estimated risks are not primarily explained by secondary organic aerosol formation. Chemical-specific emissions data available from the US EPA Toxics Release Inventory could improve risk attribution in future investigations. Nevertheless, associations remained significant after controlling for other urban pollution markers, and no associations were found when using simple distance metrics, supporting the conclusion that our estimates reflect the risks of SFP-specific activity.

Beyond implications for public health, the context of SFPs in Travis County raises issues of environmental justice. By multiple measures, exposure to SFPs is greater among Black and Latinx residents of the county, both groups with higher rates of asthma-related ED visits than white residents (Zárate et al., 2024). Population patterns in Austin are shaped by the legacy of racially discriminatory housing policies, most notably the 1928 Master Plan which institutionalized segregation (Fowler (Firm) 1957; Tretter et al., 2016). While the relationship between these historical policies and subsequent urban planning decisions is complex, there is evidence that the uneven distribution of pollution sources within the county disproportionately impacts the health of Black and Latinx residents (Chambliss et al., 2024). Investigating the role of a specific, locally relevant sector can advance efforts to mitigate environmental health disparities. While it has been shown that, at a national level, all emission sectors contribute to greater exposure for people of color, sector-specific impacts vary greatly among cities (Apte et al., 2019; Tessum et al., 2021). Although a study focused on SFPs may not be directly generalized to other urban settings, there are well-recognized examples of locally dominant industries that have disproportionately impacted minoritized populations in other American cities (Apte et al., 2017; Carpenter & Wagner, 2019; Houston et al., 2014; Yuan et al., 2024). The methodology employed here can inform further efforts to identify sources with disproportionate impacts.

Our approach uses inverse-distance weighted emissions as a transparent, replicable alternative to complex dispersion models, and these simplifying assumptions bring limitations (Brender et al., 2011). The 2 km threshold for adjacency was selected based on prior studies and inverse distance-weighting represents simple assumptions of plume dynamics, but plume dispersion varies with meteorology as well as emitted species (Chang et al., 2025). In addition, reliance on tract centroids does not represent tract-average exposures as well as population-weighted centroids or dasymetric mapping. We rely on facility-reported annual emissions estimates rather than monitoring data that could improve accuracy and consistency across facilities. However, exposure misclassification due to these simplifying assumptions is likely to be non-differential with respect to asthma outcomes, as the model’s assumptions are applied uniformly across all census tracts regardless of their health status. Further limitations to our approach are the restricted geographic domain and ecologic cross-sectional design, which may provide lower statistical power and do not account for longitudinal trends or the long-term effects of chronic SFP exposure. In addition, because our propensity-matching methods control for neighborhood racial and ethnic composition, our analysis does not address the potential mediating pathway where SFPs contribute to health disparities specifically through their disproportionate siting in minoritized communities.

This study provides two key findings with implications for public health and environmental justice. First, exposure to emissions from semiconductor fabrication plants is associated with higher rates of asthma related ED visits in Travis County, Texas. This exposure is disproportionately high in neighborhoods with a greater share of Black or Latinx residents, reflecting a pattern of environmental injustice. Methodologically, this work offers a transparent framework for linking specific industrial sources to localized health outcomes. Given the planned expansion of the semiconductor industry nationwide (LegiScan 2023; White House 2022), these results highlight the need to further investigate the toxicity of different types of SFP emissions and integrate health impact assessments and principles of environmental justice into future siting decisions.
