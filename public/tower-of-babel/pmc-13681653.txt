12-week yoga intervention effect on metabolome in Polyendocrine Metabolic Ovarian Syndrome (PMOS): An LC-MS-based study
Authors: Deepika Kumari, Hanuman Prasad Sharma, Yashwant Kumar, Neena Malhotra, Reeta Mahey, Rima Dada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPolyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS) is a complex, heterogeneous disorder affecting endocrine, reproductive, and metabolic health. It is fundamentally a systemic metabolic defect that, if not addressed in time, can compromise the function of multiple systems, resulting in conditions such as impaired glucose tolerance, insulin resistance, cardiovascular disease, dyslipidemia, fatty liver disease, and type 2 diabetes mellitus. It is a lifestyle disorder with long-term health implications, requiring a holistic approach for management.MethodsThis prospective quasi-experimental study involved 60 women (30 diagnosed with PMOS, 30 age and BMI-matched controls). Women with PMOS underwent a 12-week Yoga practice that included physical postures (Asanas), regulated breathing practice (Pranayama), and meditation (Dhyana) under the guidance of a trained therapist. Clinical characteristics and laboratory biochemical data were recorded, followed by an untargeted metabolomics analysis using high-resolution Orbitrap Fusion Tribrid Mass Spectrometer technology to study plasma metabolome changes.ResultsFollowing Yoga intervention, women with PMOS showed significant improvements in hormonal, anthropometric parameters, and lipid profile. The metabolomics analysis showed that post-Yoga intervention, several key metabolites were significantly downregulated, including glucose, fructose, d-ribose, 3-hydroxybutanoate, taurine, and methylmalonate. However, metabolites like succinate and ascorbate were upregulated. Besides, AUC suggests significant metabolic shifts following Yoga intervention.ConclusionYoga offers a holistic, integrated mind-body approach to managing the endocrine, metabolic, and reproductive dysfunction in PMOS and lowers the risk of developing long-term comorbidities.

Introduction

Polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS), previously named Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS), is a common endocrine-metabolic disorder, affecting one in eight women of reproductive age globally [1]. The precise cause is unclear, but individuals have a high risk of developing cardiovascular disease (CVD), dyslipidemia, fatty liver, impaired glucose tolerance (IGT), insulin resistance (IR), type 2 diabetes Mellitus (T2DM), and endometrial and ovarian cancer. Indian adolescents with PMOS are 4.26 times more likely to develop MetS, and have a high prevalence of other endocrine dysfunctions [2,3]. Carbohydrate, amino acid, and lipid metabolism are fundamental pathways involved in hormone production, metabolism, oocyte growth, maturation, and development [4]. Previous research has demonstrated that women with PMOS exhibit alterations in several metabolic processes, including energy production, amino acid utilization, and lipid metabolism [5]. IR is frequently observed in PMOS, which contributes to dysregulated glucose levels that are observed even in individuals with normal glucose tolerance [6]. Altogether, PMOS women had a higher prevalence of IGT, T2DM, IR, and MetS regardless of basal mass index (BMI) [7]. A robust meta-analysis showed that PMOS women are 27% less insulin sensitive than controls, regardless of BMI, age, and ethnicity [8]. In addition, Dyslipidaemia is significantly associated with clinical hyperandrogenism, impaired glucose regulation, IR, and obesity [9].

Metabolomics shows the comprehensive change in small molecular metabolites and serves as a powerful tool to understand biochemical networks for diagnosis and therapeutic purposes. There is a lack of exploration of the effect of therapeutic modalities on PMOS. As a complex lifestyle disorder, its management essentially involves lifestyle modification, such as Yoga. Yoga consists of three fundamental elements: physical postures (Asanas), regulated breathing techniques (Pranayama), and contemplative practice (Dhyana). Asanas augment pelvic flexibility, promote circulation, stimulate the endocrine gland to function optimally, and bolster metabolism, thus facilitating reproductive and general wellness. Pranayama enhances respiratory function, equilibrates the neurological system, causing a state of relaxed wakefulness, regulates the circadian rhythm, and thus is very important for oxidative eustress, hormonal, and immunological homeostasis. Meditation fosters mindfulness, emotional fortitude, and cognitive clarity, enhancing overall well-being and building emotional resilience. Regular yoga practice regulates the menstrual cycle, hormonal equilibrium, and transcript profile, especially of genes like peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARG), which is involved in carbohydrate and lipid metabolic homeostasis, and increased expression of insulin receptor and enhanced insulin sensitivity in PMOS [10,11]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to explore the effect of a 12-week Yoga intervention on the metabolome associated with PMOS compared to healthy controls using high-throughput untargeted ultra-high performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS). Our study aims to identify key metabolic alterations in PMOS compared to controls and assess the therapeutic effects of Yoga.

Material and methods

Study design

This prospective quasi-experimental study commenced after obtaining ethical clearance (Ref. No. IEC-606/15.07.2022) from the institute's ethics committee and clinical trial registration (CTRI/2022/10/046761). The study was conducted from 2022 to 2025 at AIIMS, New Delhi, India. Written informed consent was obtained from each patient before enrolment.

Participants

The 2003 Rotterdam criteria were employed to diagnose PMOS patients, necessitating the presence of a minimum of two of the following three characteristics: hyperandrogenism, ovulatory dysfunction, and polycystic ovarian morphology [12]. PMOS patients were not on any medications, supplements, therapies, dietary modifications, or exercise interventions for at least 3 months before recruitment and throughout the study period. In addition, PMOS with other causes of hyperandrogenism and ovulatory dysfunction, including Cushing Syndrome, Hyperprolactinemia, thyroid dysfunction, and adrenal or ovarian androgen-secreting tumors, were excluded from the study. Based on previous studies demonstrating the beneficial effects of Yoga in women with PMOS, a comparable sample size was considered for the present study [13]. Sample size estimation was performed assuming an alpha level of 0.05, statistical power of 80%, and a medium effect size, with an anticipated attrition rate of 15–20%, consistent with earlier Yoga-based intervention studies [11]. Accounting for potential loss to follow-up, this study involved 30 women diagnosed with PMOS.

Intervention

PMOS individual underwent a 12-week Yoga practice (5 days a week, 1.5 h daily, in person). The yoga intervention has included physical postures (Asanas), regulated breathing practice (Pranayama), and meditation (Dhyana), which have been described in detail, including frequency and duration of practices in our previously published article [14]. The intervention was delivered in person by a certified Yoga therapist at the Aarogyam facility, Department of Anatomy, All India Institute of Medical Sciences (AIIMS), New Delhi. The control group consisted of age and BMI-matched women without PMOS. Intravenous fasting blood samples were obtained from women with PMOS on the second or third day of the menstrual cycle, before (PrY PMOS) and 12 weeks following the Yoga intervention (PoY PMOS).

Sample preparation and untargeted metabolite profiling

The plasma metabolites were extracted using a methanol extraction procedure. An Orbitrap Fusion mass spectrometer (Thermo Fischer) combined with the Ultimate 3000 UHPLC equipment was utilized for LC-MS analysis. A heated electrospray was utilized to acquire both positive and negative data. A mass range of 65-1000 Da, the MS resolution was 120,000 for MS1, and 30000 for MS/MS scans was utilized. The extracted metabolites were infused into the mass spectrometer after being chromatographically separated using a Waters HSS T3 for the reverse phase and XBridge BEH Amide column for the HILIC phase. For the HILIC phase, mobile phase A was 20 mM ammonium acetate in water at pH 9.0, while mobile phase B consisted of 100% acetonitrile. In the reverse phase, solvent A was water with 0.1% formic acid, and solvent B was methanol with 0.1% formic acid. The flow rate was maintained at 0.3 mL/min for the reverse phase and 0.2 mL/min for the HILIC phase. The analysis ran for 10 min with gradient elution from 1% to 95% of phase B for the Reverse phase and the HILIC phase, 85% B to 10% B over 14 min 5 μL aliquots from each sample were injected into the system. After every 10 samples, a QC sample was run as part of quality control to monitor shifts in retention time (RT) and variations in the signal. The data-dependent mode was used for data acquisition with intensity threshold criteria for MS/MS acquisition. For MS/MS, the collision energy was kept constant at 35 ± 15 units.

Data processing

The acquired data of LC/MS were processed using default settings in the Progenesis QI software. It was used for RT alignment, deconvolution, elemental composition prediction, feature detection, and metabolite annotation. The Metascope plug was used for metabolite annotation, RT for database search, and an in-house library with accurate mass and fragmentation patterns. Cut off for RT match and fragmentation pattern was 0.5 min, and more than 30%, respectively. All samples were processed continuously in a single batch. Additionally, a pooled QC strategy was employed to reduce variability between runs. Furthermore, only those features exhibiting a coefficient of variation below 30% in pooled QC samples were deemed acceptable for subsequent analysis. Each filtered feature was manually verified and selected for the right peak.

Multivariate statistical analysis

The MetaboAnalyst 6.0 web application was used for the statistical data analysis. PrY PMOS and Control samples were analysed using an unpaired ‘t’ test, while a paired ‘t’ test was used for PoY PMOS and PrY PMOS. The data was normalized using the sum, log transformation, and Pareto scaling. To understand the clarification of groups within clusters, Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) was conducted. In the context of PLS-DA, R2 represents the proportion of variance explained by the model, reflecting its goodness of fit, while Q2 assesses the model's predictive ability through cross-validation, both of which were employed in this analysis. Moreover, a variable importance in projection (VIP) score>1 was employed for the identification of significant metabolites [15].

Significant metabolite selection

A Student t-test was used and adjusted with false discovery rate (FDR) correction for multiple hypothesis testing. A fold change (FC) threshold of 2 or greater was established to ensure that only alterations of significant biological relevance were incorporated. Furthermore, in the statistical analysis, p-values ≤0.05 were deemed significant. Only those metabolites that satisfied the criteria of fold change, VIP score, p-value, and FDR were selected for subsequent analysis.

Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) and pathway analysis

ROC and pathway analysis were performed using MetaboAnalyst 6.0. For pathway analysis, data were taken from the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG). The impact of a pathway is determined by the relative significance of matching metabolites inside that pathway, utilizing topological metrics.

Results

For the Yoga intervention group, 48 women were screened for eligibility. Of these, 38 met the inclusion criteria, and 35 provided written informed consent. After baseline assessment, 35 women were enrolled in the Yoga intervention. During the study period, 5 participants were lost to follow-up, and data from 30 participants in the Yoga group were included in the final analysis. Discussion

In the present study, following a 12-week Yoga intervention, we found DEMs associated with carbohydrate, amino acid, lipid metabolism, and antioxidant defence. This study demonstrated a significant decrease in anthropometric parameters, restoration of hormonal and lipid profiles after Yoga intervention (Table 1), consistent with previous results [13,16,17].

Our study corroborated findings of previous studies, revealing that the PMOS group exhibited elevated glucose and fructose levels as compared with controls (Fig. 3). However, a 12-week yogic intervention substantially reduced their levels, suggesting improved metabolic and glycaemic regulation, as well as a reversal of hyperinsulinemia, evidenced by an approximate 35% decrease in insulin levels (Table 1). Furthermore, endogenous liver fructose involved in lipid metabolism and adipogenesis may induce fatty liver and IR [18]. It is also strongly connected with dyslipidaemia and endocrine dysfunction in PMOS, a similar pattern observed in our current investigation, where lipid levels were altered [19,20]. Various studies reported elevated fructose levels in PMOS, both in the circulation and the follicular fluid (FF) [21,22]. Current clinical guidelines widely recommend weight loss as a first-line management strategy for PMOS [23]. This study demonstrated that decreases in glucose and fructose levels during Yoga result in enhanced metabolic control and insulin sensitivity, alleviating systemic inflammation and improving weight management. ROC curve analysis revealed that their discriminative power was excellent, indicating a robust shift in glucose metabolism following the Yoga intervention (Fig. 4). Increased levels of d-ribose trigger fast nonenzymatic glycation processes, resulting in the production of advanced glycation end products, which provoke OS and inflammation involved in IR, endocrine dysfunctions, and poor reproductive outcomes [24]. The observed drop in d-ribose levels in the current study following Yoga indicates a decrease in OS and inflammation. The results of our previous study show that Yoga improves inflammatory markers and OS, substantiating the current finding [11]. Reduced OS and inflammation may preserve oocyte quality, and perhaps improve metabolic, endocrine, and reproductive health in PMOS. The reduced xanthine levels were observed following the Yoga intervention. Elevated xanthine and xanthine oxidase (XO) activity have been associated with increased OS and metabolic risk in PMOS, indicating a shift toward a more favorable metabolic profile [25].

The increase in succinate, a crucial intermediary metabolite of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, further supports enhanced mitochondrial efficiency. Succinic acid is reported to be reduced in individuals with PMOS compared to healthy controls [26]. Ascorbate is a powerful antioxidant that reduces oxidative damage and facilitates cellular functions. Additionally, PMOS is intricately linked to inflammation, hyperandrogenism, and MetS [27]. Interestingly, elevated ascorbate levels may facilitate hormonal balance and metabolic results in women with PMOS, highlighting the comprehensive advantages of a holistic strategy such as Yoga. The observed increase in succinate and ascorbate levels following the Yoga intervention suggests modulation of mitochondrial-related metabolic pathways. Prior research on PMOS indicated improvement in mitochondrial function (OXPHOS) and integrity genes following Yoga intervention [11]. Furthermore, ornithine is a nonessential amino acid involved in the mitochondrial urea cycle, elevated and linked to ovulatory dysfunction, possible CVD, and low-grade inflammation [28,29]. The present study indicates that a reduction in ornithine levels following the Yoga intervention may contribute to enhanced ovarian function, improved cardiovascular health, and a reduction in inflammation.

Studies showed that l-glutamic acid levels were elevated in PMOS, irrespective of hyperandrogenism, obesity, and IR [26]. l-glutamic acid is found elevated in hepatic exosomes of PMOS and is also involved in multiple pathways, like the TCA cycle, butanoate metabolism, and glutathione metabolism [30]. Our study showed a reduction in l-glutamic acid levels following regular Yoga practice.

Reduced levels of 5-hydroxy-l-tryptophan (5-HTP) observed after yoga practice may reflect modulation of tryptophan metabolism and improved neuroendocrine regulation. As 5-HTP represents flux through the serotonin biosynthetic pathway, these changes likely indicate adaptive regulation toward metabolic and autonomic homeostasis. A previous study from our group on women with PMOS showed a reduction in depression severity and improved levels of brain-derived neurotropic factor (BDNF) and beta-endorphins [10].

Caprylate, capric acid, and nonanoate are medium-chain fatty acids generated during fatty acid biosynthesis. Excess free fatty acids (FFAs) activate the serine/threonine kinase pathway, leading to the tyrosine phosphorylation of insulin receptor substrate and impairing downstream insulin signaling [31]. Additionally, elevated levels of FFAs enhance β-oxidation in mitochondria, increasing the production of ROS, which contributes to elevated OS, mitochondrial dysfunction, and inflammation, key factors in the development of IR [31,32]. Their levels decreased following the Yoga practice, suggesting positive metabolic changes, including improved insulin sensitivity and glucose uptake. Also, we have seen in our previous study that Yoga decreases OS and inflammation and improves mitochondrial function [11]. Furthermore, 3-hydroxybutanoate, a ketone body derived from fatty acid catabolism, was found to be elevated in the FF and plasma in PMOS [5]. Our study further showed a decrease in 3-hydroxybutanoate after Yoga. These findings highlight the beneficial effects of Yoga on metabolism and insulin sensitivity.

Taurine is pivotal to primary bile acid synthesis. In PMOS patients, increased circulating taurine-conjugated primary bile acids are positively correlated with hyperandrogenaemia [5,33]. Beyond their traditional roles in lipid and carbohydrate metabolism and energy homeostasis, bile acids regulate endocrine functions, including activating the mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPK) pathway [34]. A post-Yoga reduction in taurine levels observed in the current study may indicate a rebalancing of bile acid profiles and attenuation of hyperandrogenic states. High methylmalonate is a functional biomarker of vitamin B12 deficiency, even when the vitamin B12 levels appear normal. Vitamin B12 (as adenosylcobalamin) acts as a cofactor for methylmalonyl CoA mutase, which catalyzes the conversion of methylmalonyl CoA to succinyl CoA (Fig. 6). Reduced enzyme activity leads to methylmalonate [35]. In the post-Yoga group, methylmalonate levels were decreased, which suggested Yoga might enhance metabolic activity that increases vitamin B12 levels, along with improving mitochondrial function and reducing proinflammatory cytokines, OS that sustains reproductive health [36]. Although the roles of a few DEMs, such as 3,4-dihydroxybenzoate, chloroacetic acid, allothreonine, formylglyceine, PI (18:0/20:4(5Z,8Z,11Z,14Z)), ureidopropionate, dihydrouracil, and gamma-glutamyl threonine, are not established in PMOS pathophysiology, their levels were reversed following Yoga intervention.Fig. 6Potential impact of Yoga on major metabolic pathways (Metabolites within the red box are upregulated, whereas those in the green box are downregulated.).

Potential impact of Yoga on major metabolic pathways (Metabolites within the red box are upregulated, whereas those in the green box are downregulated.).

The most significant strength of our study is a well-designed Yoga module and an extensive evaluation of diverse hormonal, lipid, and metabolomic data. Nonetheless, the study possesses some limitations, including its single-centric nature, a limited sample size, and the lack of an active control group for further investigation.

This study is the first to investigate the effects of Yoga on metabolomic, endocrine, and lipid profiles in women with PMOS, suggesting its potential role as a comprehensive, non-pharmacological, integrated mind-body approach as an adjunct in the management of this complex lifestyle disease.
