Teaching the Teacher: Improving Neural Network Distillability for Symbolic Regression via Jacobian Regularization
Soumyadeep Dhar, Kei Sen Fong, Mehul Motani

Abstract
Distilling large neural networks into simple, human-readable symbolic formulas is a promising path toward trustworthy and interpretable AI. However, this process is often brittle, as the complex functions learned by standard networks are poor targets for symbolic discovery, resulting in low-fidelity student models. In this work, we propose a novel training paradigm to address this challenge. Instead of passively distilling a pre-trained network, we introduce a Jacobian-based regularizer that actively encourages the “teacher” network to learn functions that are not only accurate but also inherently smoother and more amenable to distillation. We demonstrate through extensive experiments on a suite of real-world regression benchmarks that our method is highly effective. By optimizing the regularization strength for each problem, we improve the R 2 R^{2} score of the final distilled symbolic model by an average of 120% (relative) compared to the standard distillation pipeline, all while maintaining the teacher’s predictive accuracy. Our work presents a practical and principled method for significantly improving the fidelity of interpretable models extracted from complex neural networks.



Keywords:
Knowledge Distillation, Symbolic Regression, Jacobian Regularization, Explainable AI

1
Introduction

The remarkable success of deep learning has revolutionized fields from computer vision to natural language processing
(
LeCun et al. 2015
)
. These models, particularly deep neural networks (ANNs), have achieved state-of-the-art performance on a vast array of predictive tasks. However, this performance often comes at the cost of transparency. ANNs operate as “black boxes,” with their immense number of parameters forming complex, non-linear relationships that are opaque to human understanding
(
Guidotti et al. 2018
)
. This lack of interpretability poses a significant barrier to their adoption in high-stakes domains such as scientific discovery
(
Udrescu & Tegmark 2020
)
, medical diagnostics
(
Topol 2019
)
, and financial modeling, where the ability to trust, debug, and understand a model’s reasoning is as critical as its predictive accuracy.

To bridge this gap, the field of eXplainable AI (XAI) has explored various methods to shed light on these models. One promising paradigm is
knowledge distillation

(
Hinton et al. 2015
)
, where a large, complex “teacher” model transfers its knowledge to a smaller, simpler “student” model. While often used for model compression, this framework also provides a powerful pathway to interpretability by selecting a student model that is inherently transparent, a concept explored in early work on extracting decision trees from networks
(
Craven & Shavlik 1996
)
.

Among possible student models, those produced by
Symbolic Regression (SR)
are particularly compelling. Unlike other models that provide post-hoc explanations, SR aims to discover the underlying mathematical formula that maps inputs to outputs
(
Koza 1992
)
. The result is not an explanation, but the model
itself
—a fully transparent, human-readable equation. A naive two-stage pipeline for achieving this is straightforward: first, train a high-performance teacher ANN on a dataset, and second, train an SR model to mimic the predictions of this teacher.

However, the promise of this naive pipeline is seldom realized in practice. In our initial analysis, we find that this approach is brittle and struggles significantly when applied to real-world data. The complex, often chaotic functions learned by standard ANNs are poor targets for SR, resulting in low-fidelity symbolic models whose performance is not competitive with other machine learning baselines (as shown in Section
4
). This suggests a fundamental problem: the teacher network, while accurate, is a poor instructor.

Instead of passively accepting the teacher’s limitations, we ask:
can we train the teacher to be more effective at teaching?
In this work, we introduce a novel training methodology that actively encourages an ANN to learn functions that are not only accurate but also inherently more amenable to symbolic distillation. Our core contribution is a new
Jacobian-based regularization term
added to the ANN’s loss function. By penalizing the norm of the network’s Jacobian, we incentivize the model to learn functionally smoother mappings that are easier for SR algorithms to discover and represent.

Our main contributions are as follows:

•

We conduct a comprehensive analysis identifying the brittleness of the naive ANN-to-SR distillation pipeline on real-world benchmark datasets.

•

We propose a novel, principled regularization method based on the Jacobian norm to explicitly improve the distillability of neural networks.

•

We demonstrate through extensive experiments that our method dramatically improves the performance of the final symbolic student model—by an average of
120%
in R² score—often with minimal impact on the teacher’s own predictive accuracy.

•

We provide an analysis of our method’s characteristics, including the dataset-dependent nature of the optimal regularization strength, offering a path forward for developing more robust and interpretable AI systems.

2
Related Work

Our research is situated at the intersection of several key areas in machine learning: knowledge distillation, explainable AI, symbolic regression, and network regularization.

2.1
Knowledge Distillation

First popularized by Hinton et al.
(
Hinton et al. 2015
)
,
knowledge distillation
is a technique where a compact “student” model is trained to reproduce the behavior of a larger, pre-trained “teacher” network. The original motivation was primarily model compression, enabling the deployment of powerful models on resource-constrained devices. However, the paradigm is more general and has been adapted for tasks such as transferring knowledge across different data modalities and for semi-supervised learning. Our work reframes distillation’s purpose, using it not for compression, but as a mechanism to transfer knowledge from a high-performance but opaque model to one that is inherently interpretable.

2.2
Explainable AI (XAI) and Interpretable Models

The challenge of understanding black box models has given rise to the field of XAI. Current approaches can be broadly categorized.
Post-hoc
explanation methods, such as LIME
(
Ribeiro et al. 2016
)
and SHAP
(
Lundberg & Lee 2017
)
, aim to explain individual predictions of a pre-trained model without altering it. While powerful, these methods provide local explanations and do not reveal the model’s global logic. In contrast,
inherently interpretable models
are transparent by design. This category includes linear models, decision trees, and Generalized Additive Models (GAMs). Our work aligns with the goal of creating inherently interpretable models. However, instead of training a simple model directly on the data (which may yield suboptimal performance), we aim to extract a simple model from a high-performance complex teacher, seeking to combine the predictive power of deep learning with the transparency of symbolic equations.

2.3
Symbolic Regression

At the core of our student model is
Symbolic Regression (SR)
, a machine learning technique that searches the space of mathematical expressions to find the function that best fits a given dataset. Typically implemented using Genetic Programming (GP) as pioneered by Koza
(
Koza 1992
)
, SR has been a powerful tool for automated scientific discovery, rediscovering physical laws from experimental data. While SR is traditionally applied directly to data, we re-purpose it as the “student” in our distillation pipeline, tasking it with a novel goal: to discover the mathematical function implicitly learned by a neural network.

2.4
Regularization in Deep Learning

Regularization is a cornerstone of training deep neural networks. Techniques such as L1/L2 weight decay, Dropout
(
Srivastava et al. 2014
)
, and Batch Normalization are primarily designed to prevent overfitting and improve the generalization performance of the model by constraining its parameter space. Our proposed Jacobian regularization is fundamentally different in its objective. Its purpose is not to improve the teacher’s predictive accuracy, but rather to control the
functional complexity
of the learned solution. By penalizing the sensitivity of the output with respect to the input, we are regularizing the function itself to be smoother, a property that we hypothesize makes it more amenable to discovery by other methods like Symbolic Regression.

3
Methodology

In this section, we formally define the two-stage ANN-to-Symbolic-Regression pipeline and then introduce our novel regularization method designed to improve the distillability of the teacher network.

3.1
The ANN-to-SR Distillation Pipeline

Our approach builds upon a standard two-stage pipeline for distilling a trained neural network into a symbolic formula.

Stage 1: Training the Teacher Network.

Given a dataset
D=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
, where
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are the input features and
y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
are the target values, we first train a teacher model. The teacher is a deep neural network,
f_{\text{ANN}}(\cdot;\theta)
, with parameters
\theta
. The network is trained to minimize the standard Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss with respect to the ground-truth data:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}(\theta)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(y_{i}-f_{\text{ANN}}(x_{i};\theta))^{2}

(1)

The optimization process yields the trained teacher network,
f^{*}_{\text{ANN}}
, which acts as a high-performance, but opaque, oracle.

Stage 2: Distilling the Student Model.

Next, we train a student model, a symbolic function
f_{\text{SR}}(\cdot)
, to mimic the teacher. To do this, we generate a new “distillation” dataset,
D^{\prime}=\{(x_{i},\hat{y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
, where the input features are the same, but the target values are now the predictions from the trained teacher network:
\hat{y}_{i}=f^{*}_{\text{ANN}}(x_{i})
.

The Symbolic Regression (SR) algorithm, typically implemented with Genetic Programming, then searches the space of mathematical expressions,
\mathcal{F}
, to find the function
f_{\text{SR}}
that best approximates the behavior of the teacher network on this new dataset. The objective for the SR algorithm is to minimize the fitting error to the teacher’s predictions:

f_{\text{SR}}=\arg\min_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\hat{y}_{i}-f(x_{i}))^{2}

(2)

The final output,
f_{\text{SR}}
, is an interpretable mathematical formula that captures the knowledge of the teacher network.

3.2
Jacobian Regularization for Distillability

The primary limitation of the pipeline described above is that the learned function
f^{*}_{\text{ANN}}
can be unnecessarily complex and non-smooth, making it a difficult target for SR to approximate. To address this, we introduce a novel regularization term to the teacher’s training objective.

The
Jacobian matrix
of the network’s output with respect to its input, denoted
J_{x}f(x;\theta)
, is the matrix of all first-order partial derivatives. Intuitively, its norm measures the local sensitivity of the function—how much the output changes for small changes in the input. We hypothesize that by penalizing this sensitivity, we can encourage the network to learn smoother functions that are more amenable to symbolic discovery.

We propose the following regularized loss function:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}(\theta)=\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}(\theta)+\lambda\cdot\mathbb{E}_{x\sim D_{x}}\left[\|J_{x}f(x;\theta)\|_{F}^{2}\right]

(3)

Here,
\|\cdot\|_{F}^{2}
is the squared Frobenius norm of the Jacobian matrix, and
\lambda
is a hyperparameter that controls the strength of the regularization. The first term ensures the model remains accurate to the data, while the second term enforces functional smoothness. In practice, the expectation
\mathbb{E}
is approximated by taking the mean over mini-batches during stochastic gradient descent. The primary computational cost arises from computing the Jacobian, which requires a backward pass for each input feature relative to the model’s parameters. The cost scales roughly as
\mathcal{O}(d\cdot m)
, where
d
is the input dimension and
m
is the network width, making it feasible for the tabular data in our study but potentially expensive for very high-dimensional inputs. By training the teacher to minimize
\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}
, we produce a network that is not only accurate but also a more effective teacher for distillation.

for each dataset. Table

summarizes the results. Our method provides a substantial benefit on most datasets, leading to an average R² improvement of nearly
120%
while teacher accuracy remains high.

5
Discussion

Our experiments have shown that actively regularizing a teacher network can significantly improve its distillability into a symbolic model. Here, we analyze these findings, discuss the limitations of our approach, and outline promising avenues for future work.

5.1
Analysis of Findings

The central result of our work is that the proposed Jacobian regularization is highly effective, but not universally so. Its success is strongly dependent on the nature of the dataset. The dramatic
+515%
improvement on the
Concrete Strength
dataset suggests that the underlying physical relationships governing the data are inherently smooth and continuous. Our regularizer guides the ANN to a solution that aligns with this ground-truth structure, making the learned function vastly easier for Symbolic Regression to discover. Notably, our trade-off analysis shows this gain in distillability is achieved with no degradation in the teacher’s own performance.

Conversely, the lack of improvement on
Wine Quality Red
suggests a mismatch. In the Appendix, our analysis shows this dataset has a significantly lower signal-to-noise ratio, supporting the hypothesis that high label noise can be a limiting factor. For
Abalone
, the per-dataset sweep found a small optimal
\lambda
that provided a modest benefit where a larger, fixed
\lambda
was previously detrimental. This suggests that for some problems, like those with biological thresholds, a gentler regularization that only partially smooths the function is most effective. This leads to a key insight:
the most “distillable” teacher is one whose functional properties, encouraged by regularization, match the intrinsic properties of the problem.

5.2
Limitations

While our method shows significant promise, we acknowledge several limitations:

•

Computational Overhead:
The Jacobian regularization imposes a significant computational cost. Our analysis shows a roughly
10x increase in training time
on our benchmark problems, which may be a consideration for very large datasets or complex architectures.

•

Hyperparameter Tuning:
Our approach introduces a new hyperparameter,
\lambda
, and our results clearly show that the optimal value is dataset-dependent. Finding this value requires a search, adding a layer of computational cost to the modeling pipeline.

•

Generality to Student Models:
The benefits of our method did not extend to distilling into decision trees. Unlike Symbolic Regression, which uses smooth basis functions (e.g., +, -, ×), decision trees rely on piecewise-constant approximations. Jacobian smoothing minimally impacts the axis-aligned splits used by tree-based models. This important null result suggests the benefits of our method are most pronounced for certain classes of student models.

•

Not a Panacea:
As our results show, the method does not guarantee improvement on all datasets (e.g.,
Wine Quality Red
). Its success is contingent on the alignment between the “smoothness” inductive bias and the nature of the problem.

5.3
Future Work

Our findings open up several exciting directions for future research.

•

Adaptive Regularization:
A key avenue is to develop methods that can automatically tune the regularization strength
\lambda
during training, perhaps based on properties of the data or the network’s training dynamics.

•

Mitigating Computational Cost:
Research into efficient approximations of the Jacobian norm, for instance using random projections, could mitigate the computational overhead and extend our method to higher-dimensional domains.

•

Advanced Regularizers:
One could explore more sophisticated smoothness regularizers, such as penalizing the Hessian (second derivative) norm, to gain even finer control over the complexity of the learned function.

•

Application to Classification:
Extending this methodology to classification tasks is a natural next step.

6
Conclusion

Distilling the knowledge from high-performance neural networks into simple, interpretable symbolic models is a key goal for explainable AI, but the process is often unreliable. In this work, we addressed this challenge by proposing a novel method to actively improve the distillability of the teacher network. We introduced a Jacobian-based regularizer that encourages the network to learn smoother, more structured functions while maintaining high predictive accuracy. Our extensive experiments on a suite of benchmark datasets demonstrate that this approach is highly effective, improving the performance of the final distilled symbolic model by an average of 120%. This work represents a tangible step forward in creating more transparent and trustworthy AI systems, paving the way for the extraction of faithful, interpretable models from complex black boxes.

Impact Statement

This work is intended to have a positive societal impact by advancing the development of more transparent, trustworthy, and interpretable artificial intelligence. By proposing a method to more reliably distill complex neural networks into simple symbolic formulas, our research could enable experts in critical fields—such as medicine, finance, and the physical sciences—to audit, debug, and ultimately trust the predictions of AI systems. This could accelerate scientific discovery by revealing underlying mathematical relationships in data and facilitate the safer deployment of AI in high-stakes decision-making processes.

However, we must also acknowledge potential negative consequences. The primary ethical risk is that of
misplaced trust
. A user might see a simple, distilled formula and incorrectly assume it perfectly represents the full behavior of the teacher network. As distillation is an approximation, the symbolic model may fail to capture subtle but critical edge cases, such as
failing to detect rare disease subtypes in medical diagnostics
. Deploying such a simplified model in a critical application without extensive, independent validation could lead to unforeseen errors. Furthermore, like any technique that improves model understanding, our method could potentially be used to probe proprietary models or to build more interpretable systems for malicious applications.

We believe the net impact of this research is positive, as it directly addresses the critical “black box” problem in AI. Nevertheless, we urge practitioners who use these techniques to treat the resulting symbolic models not as absolute ground truth, but as powerful tools for generating hypotheses and understanding complex systems, which must be followed by rigorous domain-specific validation.
