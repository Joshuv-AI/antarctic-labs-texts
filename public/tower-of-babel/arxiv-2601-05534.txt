Blockchain Verifiable Proof of Quantum Supremacy as a Trigger for Quantum-Secure Signatures
1 st Nicholas J.C. Papadopoulos

Abstract
Blockchain is a decentralized, distributed ledger technology that ensures transparency, security, and immutability through cryptographic techniques. However, advancements in quantum computing threaten the security of classical cryptographic schemes, jeopardizing blockchain integrity once cryptographic quantum supremacy is achieved. This milestone, defined here as the realization of quantum computers to solve practical cryptographic problems, would render existing security standards vulnerable, exposing blockchain assets (currency, data, etc.) to fraud and theft. To address this risk, we propose and implement a smart contract deployable on the Ethereum blockchain, having the ability to run applications on its blockchain, that generates classically intractable puzzles by probabilistically generating large, hard-to-factor numbers without requiring secret information. This contract then serves two purposes: to establish a mechanism (1) for a trustless, unbiased proof of cryptographic quantum supremacy by verifying solutions to these puzzles, and (2) to protect user funds on Ethereum by triggering quantum-secure fallback protocols upon detecting cryptographic quantum supremacy, since it is desirable to wait as long as possible to fall back to a quantum-secure scheme because of its inherent additional cost and complexity. These mechanisms demonstrate the ability to identify cryptographic vulnerabilities and ensure a smooth transition to quantum-secure standards, safeguarding blockchain assets in a post-quantum era.



I
Introduction

Blockchain is a decentralized, distributed ledger technology
[
1
]
.
By recording transactions across a network of computers, the ledger is kept secure, immutable, and verifiable by anyone in the network.
Each record consists of a series of transactions and other key pieces of information, along with a reference to the previous record.
These records, called blocks, are linked in succession, forming a chain—hence the name blockchain.
Blockchains ensure integrity because fraudulently modifying one block necessitates modifying all subsequent blocks, as the backward references are based on a hash function derived from the previous block’s information.
This hash value is difficult to compute, making it untenable for a minority of users to sustain a fraudulent chain indefinitely.
The majority of the network will actively work on the true ledger, quickly surpassing the length of the fraudulent one, ensuring consensus on the legitimate ledger.

This process of competing ledgers, based on the ability to perform large computations, is called proof of work (PoW)
[
2
]
.
While PoW was the original technique to ensure ledger integrity, other methods, such as proof of stake (PoS), where monetary penalties are leveraged instead of massive compute power, are now being adopted by some blockchains, such as Polkadot and Ethereum
[
3
]
.
The work and resources required to execute this process of executing code to be committed onto the ledger is defined in units called gas
[
4
]
.
Each transaction is then associated with a particular gas cost, which is then paid by the transaction initiator using a market value equivalent of currency.
This payment may be supplemented by further factors, such as an additional incentive fee, and rewarded to the user performing the process.
In this paper, we refer to the amount paid by the transaction initiator as the cost of a transaction.

Cryptocurrencies rely on blockchain to create virtual currencies that ensure transparency, security, and immutability without requiring a central authority, such as a bank or government
[
5
]
.
Bitcoin was the first to utilize this arrangement
[
2
]
, and thousands of blockchains and cryptocurrencies have since emerged, each leveraging blockchain technology in unique ways.
Among these is Ethereum, a prominent blockchain with its own native cryptocurrency (ETH) and support for smart contracts and decentralized applications
[
6
]
.
Smart contracts are digital agreements with terms and conditions embedded directly into code and executed on the blockchain
[
7
]
.
Decentralized applications, similarly, are pieces of software that operate on the blockchain
[
8
]
.
All executions and system states are verifiable by anyone at any time, ensuring complete transparency and guaranteeing that the executed code and results are tamper-proof.

Blockchains rely on signature verification to authenticate the origin and integrity of transactions
[
9
]
.
Every transaction on a blockchain is cryptographically signed by the user initiating it, ensuring traceability as far back as the blockchain’s initial transaction.
In other words, anyone can verify the ownership of any transaction committed to the blockchain at any time.
Signature verification was initially developed as a form of public-key cryptography
[
10
]
, most commonly implemented through the Rivest–Shamir–Adleman (RSA) algorithm
[
11
]
. Other forms, such as the Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm (ECDSA)
[

, have been developed and are widely used, including by Ethereum.

However, classical signature verification is under threat as the new technology of quantum computing progresses
[
13
,

,

,

]
.
Quantum computing utilizes a fundamentally different paradigm compared to classical computing, leveraging quantum mechanical principles to process information in novel ways and solve certain problems that are infeasible for classical computers
[
17
]
.
While classical computers use bits to represent data as either 0s or 1s, quantum computers use quantum bits (qubits), which exhibit the unique properties of superposition and entanglement

18
,
19

.
Superposition allows qubits to exist in both the 0 and 1 states simultaneously, while entanglement enables instantaneous correlation between qubits, regardless of distance.
These properties enable quantum computers to solve certain problems, such as order-finding or large integer factorization, with polynomial efficiency

,
20
]
.

A significant milestone in quantum computing is quantum supremacy, which refers to a quantum computer solving a problem that would take a classical computer an infeasible amount of time to solve
[
21
]
.
Efforts to demonstrate quantum supremacy, such as those described by Kim, Arute, Morvan, and Neven
[
22
,

,

,

, have faced criticism or claims of limited practical significance, as discussed in
[

,

,

]
.
Nevertheless, the race towards quantum supremacy is of the utmost priority for competing organizations.
Achieving this would establish technological leadership, market dominance, and an enormous increase in reputation and prestige.
To emphasize the significance of this milestone, prominent physicist Michio Kaku said, “the nation or company that does this, will rule the world economy”, referring to creating “a workable, operationally efficient quantum computer”
[
30
]
.

Quantum supremacy introduces a significant vulnerability to blockchain security, as it exploits weaknesses in classical signature verification.
The milestone could mark the beginning of an era of risks for cryptocurrencies, including theft or fraud of enormous value.
However, the current definition of quantum supremacy does not consider the practical utility of the problems being solved.
Sometimes, the term quantum advantage is used to indicate quantum supremacy on a problem of practical use.
In this paper, we define a stricter definition, which we call cryptographic quantum supremacy, focuses on a quantum computer’s ability to bypass current cryptographic standards, namely RSA and ECDSA.

Quantum computers are expected to eventually break the security standards on which many current blockchains rely
[
31
]
.
This milestone would signify cryptographic quantum supremacy and indicate that blockchain assets, including Ethereum’s ETH, are at risk.
To mitigate this threat, quantum-secure signature verification schemes, such as the Lamport signature
[
32

, have been developed to maintain security in a post-quantum era.
While Ethereum’s flexibility allows users to implement quantum-secure protocols, such implementations can be costly and complex.
To minimize unnecessary expenses, Ethereum assets can continue using current cryptographic standards and adopt quantum-secure schemes only when the risk of quantum-based security breaches becomes imminent.

This paper proposes a smart contract that can be deployed on Ethereum to generate classically intractable puzzles without requiring secret information during generation.
The contract has been implemented and thoroughly tested using Solidity, a commonly used programming language for Ethereum, thereby validating the practicality of this smart contract and the fundamental concepts that stem from it.
Therefore, the contributions of this paper are:
establishing a mechanism (1) for trustless and unbiased proof of cryptographic quantum supremacy by verifying solutions to these puzzles, as even the generator cannot determine the solution through classical means, and (2) to protect user funds on Ethereum (and other blockchains) by triggering quantum-secure signature verification fallback protocols upon proof of this cryptographic quantum supremacy.

II
Background and Motivation

A major milestone in human history will begin the moment that cryptographic quantum supremacy has been achieved.
At this time, the two emerging fields of blockchain and quantum computing, as well as the long-existing field of classical cryptography, will change drastically.
We refer back to Michio Kaku’s quote mentioned in the introduction to highlight the importance of this milestone
[
30
]
.
Enormous efforts have been made to reach this milestone, and the three aforementioned fields each respond to it in their own ways, with swaths of discussion surrounding it never leaving the mainstream.

Jones and Mosca published the first demonstration of a quantum algorithm in 1988
[
33
]
.
The task has since made steady progress, notably beginning with a 5-qubit NMR computer and soon after a 7-qubit machine
[
34
]
.
A watershed moment occurred in 2023 when Google, in partnership with NASA and Oak Ridge National Laboratory, demonstrated quantum supremacy with their Sycamore quantum processor, which performed calculations in seconds that would allegedly take even advanced supercomputers thousands of years
[
23
]
.
Since then, numerous attempts have been made to prove quantum supremacy
[
22
,
23
,
24
,
26
,
35
,
36
]
.

However, verifying quantum supremacy has proven extraordinarily challenging.
As noted by Umesh Vazirani from UC Berkeley, “The need for strong evidence for quantum supremacy is underappreciated, but it’s important to pin this down… The basic question that must be answered for any such experiment is how confident can we be that the observed behavior is truly quantum and could not have been replicated by classical means”
[
37
]
.
In fact, many quantum supremacy claims have been refuted and even reproduced classically
[
27
,
28
,
29
]
.
Classically verifiable quantum supremacy problems currently require far too many qubits to implement on current state-of-the-art machines.
Trust issues understandable arise to such claims, then,
and a provably trustless mechanism that proves the achievement of quantum supremacy is lacking.
Generally, problems having verifiable quantum supremacy reside in the complexity class of NP-Hard problems. Categorizing types of quantum algorithms and their verifiability can be further read upon in a work by Yamakawa and Zhandry, unveiling a new problem of the sort
[
38
]
.

Classical cryptography has historically relied on the difficulty of certain mathematical problems.
One of computer science’s greatest problems is whether the set of problems whose solutions can be solved in nondeterministic polynomial time, which is assumed to be infeasible, and verified in polynomial time, which is considered easy, can also be solved in polynomial time.
Problems in the set are classified as NP problems, while the problems that are known to be both solvable and verifiable in polynomial time are classified as P. The main question asks whether P=NP.
Public key cryptography, which underpins most cryptographic protocols today, rely on NP problems being difficult to solve.
RSA, for example, relies on the well-known problem prime factorization, which is the task of decomposing a large integer into its prime factors
[
11
]
.
Hence, if P=NP, or at least the underlying mathematical problems in cryptographic protocols can be solved, then those cryptographic protocols no longer provide adequate security.

It is no wonder, then, that the publication of Shor’s algorithm, showing that quantum computers can theoretically solve prime factorization on polynomial time, marked a historical moment
[
16
]
.
Though this has been proven in theory, quantum computing hardware has not yet become powerful enough to run the algorithm.
Since this threat to cybersecurity is so impactful, countries including the United States, China, Russia, and the United Kingdom are investing heavily in quantum computing research precisely because of this strategic advantage.
The threat is so serious that security experts have coined the term “quantum apocalypse” to describe the moment when quantum computers render current encryption methods obsolete
[
39
,
40
,
41
]
.
In this paper, we refer to this moment as cryptographic quantum supremacy.
Some are preemptively taking advantage of the situation, such as agencies reportedly harvesting encrypted data with the intention of decrypting them once sufficiently powerful quantum computers become available, a strategy known as “harvest now, decrypt later”
[
42
,
43
]
.

Given that most blockchain systems today rely on such NP problems as previously described, namely ones that Shor’s algorithm can solve in polynomial time, the community needs to keep an eye on the progress of cryptographic quantum supremacy.
Harri Owen, chief strategy officer at PostQuantum, warns that once quantum computers can break current encryption, they could “almost instantly create the ability for whoever’s developed it to clear bank accounts, to completely shut down government defense systems—Bitcoin wallets will be drained”
[
39
]
.
Many quantum-secure alternatives, such as Lamport signatures and lattice-based schemes
[
32
,
44
]
, have been developed to preemptively protect against quantum attacks, but they are often more complex, consume more storage, and require more processing power than traditional methods.

Luckily, blockchain capabilities can provide a solution for both a trustless verification of cryptographic quantum supremacy as well as a fallback mechanism to protect blockchain assets.
The primary motivation of this paper is to describe such a utilization and provide an example implementation of it.
This novel take will be beneficial for all parties, providing an incentive for the development of cryptographic quantum supremacy as well as delaying costly blockchain protection schemes until necessary.
This paper delves into the necessary requirements for such a scheme, describes a way to consider solutions of this type, and proposes a concrete example of such a framework.

III
Results

This paper selects prime factorization as the best-suited puzzle to accomplish the stated goals (see Sec.
IV-B
).
Prime factorization, the task of decomposing a positive integer
n
into the set of prime numbers whose product is equal to
n
, is a computational challenge widely believed to be intractable for classical computers as the size of the factors increases.
However, Shor’s algorithm demonstrates an efficient quantum solution
[
16
]
, and since both RSA and ECDSA rely on the difficulty of this particular problem, this problem serves as an ideal test for cryptographic quantum supremacy.
Hence, if this challenge is generated on a blockchain, where the solution is never known throughout the process of generation, then if someone were to provide a solution that is also verified on the blockchain, this would be virtually indisputable proof that cryptographic quantum supremacy has been achieved.

The contract would use techniques based on Sander’s method of probabilistically generating a hard-to-factor integer
[
45
]
, elaborated on further in Sec.
IV
.
This paper refers to these generated integers as locks because providing solutions (prime factorizations) to these will unlock bounty funds held in the contract.
We conclude that the contract should generate 119 locks of 4,608 bits in order to have confidence that at least one of them will be infeasible to solve by classical means alone.
The confidence level for which we aim is about a one-in-a-billion chance that this fails.
Though we have confidence that at least one will be infeasible, the expectation, discussed in Sec.
IV
, is that 19 of them will be infeasible.

The bounty funds serve as an incentive for solvers.
Participants may attempt to provide a prime factorization for any single lock at any time.
If the solution is correct, then the lock is marked as solved, and no additional attempts to solve that particular lock will be allowed.
This process concludes when the final lock is solved,
at which point all funds held within the contract will be transferred to the final solver, a publicly readable flag will be set to indicate that the contract has been successfully solved, and subsequent interactions, such as new submissions or fund deposits, are disabled.
There will be an enforced minimum bounty, ensuring that computational costs of claiming the bounty are covered, requiring funds sufficient for at least 800,000,000 gas.
For a rough-estimate example, if the current market price is 23.8 Gwei per unit of gas, one Gwei being one-billionth of one ETH, the contract should have at least 19.04 ETH as a bounty.
To accommodate fluctuations in gas prices, these fluctuations will be monitored, and funds will be added to the bounty to ensure that the payout is always greater than the cost to solve the puzzles.
Additionally, the contract accepts donations from any user, allowing for further expansion of the reward pool and making the solving process more lucrative.

Deployment of the contract follows the Singleton pattern, ensuring that a single instance across all network instances is responsible for generating and managing the locks
[
46
]
.
After deployment, a public method that partially generates the locks must be repeatedly invoked until all locks are generated.
This is to limit the cost of each transaction so that generation happens in small pieces, both preventing a reversion of the transaction due to exceeding the gas limit, which is currently 30 million gas for Ethereum
[
47
]
, and allowing the distribution of cost over many participants rather than burdening it all onto a single user.
The completion of the generation will be marked with a flag, at which point no further invocation of the generation method will be processed, and the function accepting solution submissions will be enabled.

Various blockchain attacks are famously known and must be accounted for
[
48
,
49
,
50
,
51
,
52
]
.
Among these is front-running, a malicious technique which, with respect to this contract, would mean that a user would watch for an incoming solution, steal the solution, and submit it themselves via a transaction with a higher gas price, which users may dictate upon submission
[
52
]
.
As transactions are not necessarily committed chronologically, but rather transactions with higher priority fees are usually prioritized, users can jump the line by paying a higher fee
[
4
]
.
To prevent front-running and maintain fairness, the submission of solutions follows a commit-reveal scheme with a one-day buffer
[
52
]
.
That is, solvers must first submit an encrypted message derived from the proposed factorization and their sending address (an address, in the context of a blockchain, is essentially a unique name representing a user and their funds).
This message’s hash value is stored in the contract.
After a mandatory waiting period of one day, solvers can reveal their solution by submitting the original data used to compute the hash.
The contract verifies the solution by checking that the submitted data hashes to the stored value.

Since the sending address is included in the encryption, an attacker cannot simply submit their own copy of the original transaction of the encrypted data.
Instead, they must wait for the reveal made by the original solver, then generate a new encryption using the revealed prime factorization and their own address, and only then attempt to submit their own transaction.
To succeed, the attacker would need to flood the transaction pool, called the mempool
[
53
]
, with highly priced transactions for an entire day, preventing the original reveal from being committed, and then submit their own reveal before the original can be processed.
This delay is infeasible for attackers to overcome, as discussed in Sec.
IV
and visualized in Fig.
2
.

By combining blockchain security with economic incentives, this contract offers a trustless and transparent mechanism for demonstrating cryptographic quantum supremacy while providing monetary incentives to do so, which would be in addition to the societal incentives that organizations already have to prove cryptographic quantum supremacy.
Furthermore, this contract allows instantaneous fallback safety for Ethereum users to protect their funds once this cryptographic quantum supremacy has been detected.

IV
Discussion

IV-A

Choosing the parameters

The bit-length of the generated integers was chosen to be the easiest to solve by quantum means while being infeasible for classical computers.
The work of Sander proves that it is possible to generate difficult-to-factor numbers, known as RSA-UFOs, without prior knowledge of their factorization
[
45
]
.
This is essential in order to allow trustless generation, as any generation requiring secret knowledge on a blockchain can be potentially exploited.
An analysis by Anoncoin, another cryptocurrency using Sander’s techniques, determined that the probability of generating an RSA-UFO as approximately 0.16
[
54
]
.
Therefore, the calculation
\log(10^{-9})/\log(1-0.16)\approx 119
determines that this contract should generate 119 locks, providing a high confidence level that at least one secure lock will be generated.
Specifically, there is about a one-in-a-billion chance that all locks can easily be factored.

Note that Theorem 1 of the Sander paper states that, letting
\xi\in(\frac{1}{3},\frac{5}{12})
, the number of integers
\leq x
that have two distinct prime factors
\geq x^{\xi}
is
x(\frac{1}{2}\ln^{2}(\frac{1}{2\xi})+O(\frac{1}{ln(x)}))

[
45
]
.
We can see that, since
O(\frac{1}{ln(x)})
approaches
0
as
x
approaches
\infty
, the probability
\frac{1}{2}\ln^{2}(\frac{1}{2\xi})\approx 0.082
when
\xi=\frac{1}{3}
.
As this differs from Anoncoin’s results by only a factor of approximately 2, we choose the more cost-friendly result of generating 119 locks while being confident that at least one secure lock will be generated.

We now predict the time-scale and resource requirements of solving this puzzle both classically and quantumly.
Classical security for 3072-bit RSA keys is expected to remain robust beyond 2030, while lower-sized keys may be broken before then
[
55
]
.
We choose this sized key because the timeline for quantum supremacy is uncertain and may very well not be achieved before 2030.
The most prominent reason to expect this is that existing and near-term quantum computers are noisy and error-prone, classifying them as Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) computers
[
56
]
, which have limited reliability, utility, and scalability.
While NISQ systems show promise in outperforming classical computers for certain applications, such as optimization
[
57
,
58
]
,
their capability in integer factorization remains restricted to numbers of only a few tens or, at most, a few hundred bits
[
59
]
—far from practical use cases.
Quantum error correction holds the potential to enable fault-tolerant quantum computing
[
60
]
, allowing reliable execution of quantum programs on noisy devices.
However, achieving large-scale fault-tolerant quantum computing necessitates significant technological breakthroughs.
In quantum error correction, for instance, a logical qubit is encoded across multiple physical qubits, often requiring hundreds or thousands of them, making near-term implementation impractical.
Even with sufficient physical qubits, performing logical operations and executing real-time error detection and correction, such as in superconducting qubits within one microsecond timescales, remains a major challenge
[
61
]
.
Hence, while breaking 256-bit elliptic curve encryption would require approximately 2,330 logical qubits
[
62
]
, achieving this feat within one day would require an estimated
13\times 10^{6}
physical qubits under the current fault-tolerant regime
[
63
]
.

However, RSA-UFO generation requires generating random numbers three times the size of the desired large prime factors.
Desiring a key of at least 3,072 bits comprised of at least two primes, we want primes of at least
3072/2=1536
bits each.
Hence, the contract should generate 119 locks of
1536*3=4608
bits in order to have a confidence of
10^{-9}
that at least one lock cannot be factored by classical means alone.

The bounty mechanism aims to ensure that solvers are reimbursed for the computational costs associated with verifying puzzle solutions.
The expected number of prime factors of a number
N
is on the order of
\log(\log(N))
, so the expected number of prime factors of a 4608-bit integer is
\log(\log(2^{4608}))\approx 8

[
64
]
.
To estimate these costs, we experimentally deployed the contract with 119 locks, each being a 4608-bit integer of known factorization.
However, each also has 16 factors instead of the expected 8 to obtain a more conservative estimate.
The cost for solving a single lock was measured at 6,110,290 gas, with the vast majority of this expense arising from the Miller-Rabin primality test, used to verify that the provided factors are indeed prime
[
65
]
.
Providing correct solutions to all of these locks yielded a total gas cost of 741,048,802 (See Sec.
V-B
).
Thus, since gas cost can vary depending on various factors such as the particular state of the blockchain and the equipment running calculations to put transactions on the blockchain, a bounty of at least 800,000,000 gas should be funded to the contract to ensure enough incentive.
This amount serves as the minimum viable incentive, with adjustments made dynamically to reflect fluctuations in the Ethereum gas market.
To further enhance incentive, it is recommended that the bounty be set at least many multiples over the estimated cost at current gas prices fluctuating with market conditions.

Given the high cost of providing solutions to all locks, which would create a high barrier for any solver, we decided to implement the contract so that any one lock can be solved at any time, reducing the cost per transaction.
Since we are confident that at least one lock will be infeasible to factor classically, the community can collectively solve easy locks so that only difficult locks remain.
Hence, this ensures that solving the final lock is a satisfactory completion and proof of cryptographic quantum supremacy.
Furthermore, if one did have a powerful enough quantum computer to solve the locks, they could solve all remaining difficult locks at once, preventing the issue of not being rewarded for solving a difficult lock that is not the last to be solved.

An additional possibility is to have a gradient payout scheme.
This would entail rewarding the solver every time a lock is solved.
Since we expect that easily solvable locks will be solved first, early solutions can be rewarded with a small amount of the bounty.
This would at least serve to allow the solver to recoup some of their costs to solve puzzles that do not solve the contract.
However, since we cannot know how many locks will be classically feasible or infeasible, it is uncertain how the gradient (possibly even being flat) payout could be structured in order to keep enough of a reward for the final solver.
Hence, we consider the solving of easy factorizations to essentially be part of the initial funding to deploy, sourced by the community.

The timescale for the commit-reveal scheme was chosen to be a one-day delay between commit and reveal.
This is due to the fact that an attacker would have to flood the chain to prevent the reveal long enough for said attacker to submit their own, stolen solution.
However, assuming the reveal transaction is willing to pay market rate for transaction fees, the EIP-1559 fee mechanism
[
66
]
and its exponential adjustment makes it infeasible for an economic attacker to spam costly transactions to artificially increase the base-fee for an extended period of time.
Additionally, even if a large percentage of the proposers collude to censor, the inclusion of the reveal transaction on chain will be delayed but only as a function of the ratio of censoring to non-censoring proposers.
For example, if 90% of proposers censor, then the reveal transaction will take approximately 10 times as long as expected to be included, resulting in a delay on the order of 120 seconds given mainnet block times.
If, instead, 99% of proposers censor, then the transaction will take approximately 100 times as long, resulting in a delay on the order of 1200 seconds.
Still in these extreme regimes, reveal times on the order of a day are safe. Fig.
2
illustrates a possible commit-reveal attack and the breaking point for how a one-day delay prevents this.

IV-B

Choosing the puzzle

Several alternative puzzles were considered before selecting the integer factorization problem.
Each alternative was evaluated based on its computational cost, security, and suitability for a trustless blockchain environment.
One option considered was order-finding, which involves identifying the smallest positive integer
k
such that
a^{k}\equiv 1\pmod{n}
, given positive integers
n
and
a
coprime to
n
, where
n
is referred to as the modulus and
a
is referred to as the base.
Since order-finding can be reduced to factoring and vice versa
[
67
]
, this approach would require the generation of hard-to-factor numbers as moduli and random bases coprime to these moduli.
We compare the costs of the order-finding problems with prime factorization in two ways: verifying solutions and deploying the contract.
Verifying solutions for an order-finding puzzle (see Sec.
V-B
) resulted in gas costs ranging from 141,650 to 16,074,742 per iteration.
While this can be comparable to prime factorization, it is generally more costly due to the fact that the maximum order can reach a high limit due to the large key size.
We can use an estimate of 6,370,521,379 gas to deploying an order-finding contract, while deploying a prime factorization contract has only a 237,898,716 gas estimate (See Sec.
V-B
).
Hence, we have selected prime factorization over order-finding due to order-finding’s higher deployment cost and comparable solution verification cost in the average case.

Another option was requiring solvers to sign a message without access to the private key.
However, since quantum computers are currently not capable of reversing most of the currently used hash functions, one could not sign a message with the public address alone.
Hence, random generation of a public address is insufficient.
Furthermore, generating a private key along with the public address, as is usually done, introduces trust issues, as the contract deployer could access the private key and solve the puzzle without a quantum computer, undermining the trustless design.

Instead of generating an RSA-UFO, we considered alternatives such as generating large primes and their product (as in standard RSA key generation) or using a proof of quantumness based on cryptographic methods and randomness
[
68
]
.
However, such protocols rely on a trapdoor, or information that must remain secret from the verifier.
Once again, the requirement of secret information during generation is incompatible with a trustless blockchain environment.

Sampling problems, as surveyed by Harrow and Montanaro, were another possibility
[
69
]
.
These problems aim to demonstrate quantum supremacy through the generation of samples from specific probability distributions.
However, these problems are considered unverifiable, meaning it is not possible to verify that the samples are indeed sampled from the intended probability distribution.
As the selected puzzle for this contract must be verifiable in order to prove correctness, these sampling problems were deemed unsuitable.

Decentralized trusted setups, where a group collectively generates data to be used as a form of proof of verification, were also considered
[
70
]
.
In these setups, at least one honest participant discarding their data is enough to prevent fake proofs.
While this indeed uses minimal trust assumptions, this approach does not achieve full trustlessness.
Further exploration may reveal decentralized setups with favorable trade-offs in cost or performance, but this is left for future work.

Finally, “Verifiable Quantum Advantage without Structure”, as proposed by Yamakawa and Zhandry, appears promising
[
38
]
.
Their method uses parameters for Folded Reed-Solomon codes
[
71
,
72
]
, offering a simpler setup with potentially lower deployment and solution verification costs.
Moreover, this puzzle might require fewer qubits to solve compared to prime factorization, allowing it to serve as an early warning system rather than a confirmation of already vulnerable security.
However, the exact parameters needed for detecting blockchain security vulnerability are unclear at the moment and left for future work.

V
Methods

V-A

Quantum bounty contract implementation

All implementations were carried out using Solidity and accompanied by extensive testing, using Hardhat, to ensure robustness and correctness.
Firstly, a fundamental building block for the proposed puzzles is a random bytes accumulator
[
73
]
.

VI
Conclusion

We have described and demonstrated a practical approach to verifying cryptographic quantum supremacy through a blockchain.
This provides a fully trustless proof of the cryptographic quantum supremacy era and indicates when current security standards are virtually unquestionably vulnerable.
As a publicly viewable smart contract, this approach also serves as an indicator, signaling when blockchain accounts can automatically transition to a quantum-secure verification scheme to protect assets.
We have similarly described and demonstrated such a fallback account.

A key area of ongoing debate is the choice of the puzzle.
Included in this debate is the possibility of future advancement or alternative puzzles in general, potentially exhibiting varying levels of security, advantages, and trade-offs.
These alternatives could offer a range of options, allowing users to balance trade-offs based on their priorities.
On one extreme, the trade-off would be a shorter window of exposure to theft risk but a sooner implementation of costly verification schemes.
On the other extreme, the trade-off would be a longer window of theft risk but a delayed implementation of such schemes.

The integer factorization puzzle presented in this paper falls on the latter of the extremes.
If this puzzle is solved, then one may assume that the power of quantum computers has already surpassed the ability to break ECDSA verification schemes.
This allows users to monitor this contract as a robust mechanism to secure their assets at the latest moment in order to save on costs resulting from early safeguarding.
Tending towards the former extreme could include puzzles to prove a weaker quantum supremacy or quantum advantage, as opposed to the stronger cryptographic quantum supremacy.
Regardless of the choice of puzzle and trade-off, though, the puzzle indeed needs to be able to be generated without any knowledge or hints towards solutions while being efficiently verifiable, which continues to be an active area of research.
