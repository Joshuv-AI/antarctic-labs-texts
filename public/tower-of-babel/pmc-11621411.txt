Exploring the role of bridging nodes linking adverse childhood experiences and psychological risk factors in suicidal ideation among MSM
Authors: Huifang Xu, Lulu Xu, Zuxin Wang, Chen Xu, Fan Hu, Yong Cai, Ying Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the interplay between distal adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) and proximal psychological risk factors in predicting suicidal ideation among men who have sex with men (MSM) using network analysis. A total of 1138 MSM completed self-reported questionnaires on demographics, ACEs, depression, anxiety, interpersonal needs, and suicidal ideation. We employed Gaussian graphical models to perform a network analysis that mapped the relationships between ACEs and proximal psychological variables and identified bridge nodes. Additionally, suicidal ideation was incorporated to reevaluate the network and explore the association between the identified bridge nodes and suicidal ideation. The results show that approximately 88.3% of participants reported experiencing ACEs, with an average occurrence of 3.4 types of ACEs. Suicidal ideation was reported by 31.3% of participants. Key bridging nodes in the psychosocial network included perceived burdensomeness, thwarted belongingness, feeling tired/having little energy, and excessive worrying. Perceived burdensomeness exhibited the strongest correlation with suicidal ideation, followed by thwarted belongingness. Moreover, neglect is strongly associated with suicidal ideation. Our findings suggest that ACEs are associated with suicidal ideation and may be linked to specific proximal psychological factors that correlate with and potentially exacerbate suicidal ideation. These findings provide support to the Interpersonal Theory of Suicide, which suggests that perceived burdensomeness and thwarted belongingness are vital psychological factors in the onset of suicidal ideation. Addressing and intervening in these bridging nodes could help reduce the influence of ACEs on suicide ideation within the MSM community.Keywords: Men who have sex with men, Adverse childhood experiences, Psychological risk factors, Suicidal ideation, Network analysisSubject terms: Risk factors, Psychology

Introduction

Suicide poses a significant challenge to public health, as estimated by the World Health Organization (WHO), with over 700,000 individuals succumbing to it annually1. It is the 17th leading cause of death globally and the 4th among individuals aged 15–291. Suicidal ideation refers to an individual’s thoughts or impulses toward suicide2. Research shows that individuals with suicidal ideation are at significantly higher risk of suicide compared to those without ideation, with an overall risk ratio of 4.17 (95% CI: 3.29–5.27) 3. Although it is one of the strongest predictors of future suicidal behavior4, not all individuals with suicidal ideation will go on to attempt or die by suicide. The intensity, duration, and presence of a suicide plan can affect the predictive power of suicidal ideation5. The transition from ideation to action is often influenced by additional risk factors, such as adverse life events, mental health disorders (e.g., depression and anxiety), and social isolation6. Notably, men who have sex with men (MSM) population face a markedly high risk of suicide ideation. MSM refers to men who were assigned male at birth and engaged in sexual activities with other men, regardless of their sexual orientation (e.g., heterosexual, homosexual, bisexual, etc.)7. Compared to the general and heterosexual populations, MSM individuals show a significantly elevated risk of suicidal ideation and are considered a high-risk group for suicide8,9. A meta-analysis8 revealed that the prevalence of suicidal ideation among MSM ranged from 13.18% to 55.80%, with a lifetime prevalence of 34.97% based on a random-effects model (95% CI: 28.35–41.90). Further, a global study highlighted that MSM in Asia, North America, and South America have a 2.9, 2.1, and 7.5-fold higher risk of suicidal ideation respectively, compared to heterosexual men10. Despite these high-risk characteristics, targeted interventions and mental health resources for MSM remain relatively scarce.

The underlying causes of suicidal behavior are complex and multifactorial6. Research has identified adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) as a prevalent distal factor influencing suicidal ideation6. ACEs include childhood maltreatment, neglect, family dysfunction, and extra-family violence, which consists of bullying, witnessing community violence, and experiencing war or collective violence. These experiences are associated with an increased risk of mental health issues such as depression, anxiety, and relationship difficulties in adulthood, which are in turn well-established risk factors for suicidal ideation11. The mechanisms linking ACEs to these mental health outcomes are multifactorial, often involving emotional dysregulation, chronic stress exposure, and maladaptive coping strategies that result from early trauma11,12. A prospective study involving 9,377 participants from the British 1958 birth cohort revealed that those who endured three or more ACEs were more likely to experience severe suicidal ideation in midlife13. Similarly, a retrospective cohort study with 17,337 participants demonstrated a significant elevation in the lifetime risk of suicide attempts with an increasing number of ACEs14. Moreover, there is a documented dose–response relationship between ACEs and the onset of adult depression and anxiety15. Importantly, sexual minority individuals are likely to face a higher incidence of ACEs16,17, which profoundly impacts their mental health18. This elevated risk is often attributed to factors such as family rejection, bullying, and exposure to discrimination or violence due to their sexual orientation19,20. These mental health challenges may disproportionately affect MSM, who often face additional stressors such as societal stigma, discrimination, internalized homophobia, and rejection in same-sex relationships21. These minority stressors, including structural homophobia, can compound the effects of ACEs, further undermining coping and emotional regulation abilities22,23. This compounded stress intensifies the impact of ACEs on mental health, increasing vulnerability to conditions like depression, anxiety, and suicidal ideation.

Proximal psychological factors such as symptoms of depression and anxiety also play crucial roles as predictors of suicidal ideation24,25. Studies have shown that individuals presenting with both anxiety and depression symptoms are more likely to experience suicidal ideation, which can also be more intense4. According to the Interpersonal Theory of Suicide (IPTS)26,27, suicidal ideation emerges from the interplay between perceived burdensomeness and thwarted belongingness. Perceived burdensomeness refers to individuals feeling like a burden to important others and encompasses self-hatred and feelings of responsibility. Thwarted belongingness refers to the state where the "need to belong" is unfulfilled, including feelings of loneliness and lack of reciprocal care. Both states are linked to an increased suicidal ideation risk, particularly when they coexist26,28. When individuals also possess the capability for suicide, characterized by a reduced fear of death and increased tolerance for physical pain, suicidal ideation may lead to actual suicide attempts.

Both distal and proximal factors have been shown to predict the occurrence of suicidal ideation29. Previous research has predominantly focused on individual risk factors, leaving it unclear whether these factors independently or cumulatively contribute to suicidal ideation among MSM. ACEs, as distal risk factors, heighten an individual’s susceptibility to suicide30. Studies indicate that individuals who experience ACEs are more likely to develop mental health disorders during early adulthood, which in turn exacerbates their vulnerability to stressors later in life11,31. These early adulthood mental health issues, such as depression and anxiety, can serve as a critical pathway through which ACEs lead to an increased risk of suicidal ideation14. For instance, Fergusson et al. found that childhood adversity and family dysfunction are strongly linked to the onset of suicidal behavior in adolescence and early adulthood, with mental health disorders like depression and anxiety acting as significant mediators32. Current research lacks an examination of the critical proximal psychological variables that connect ACEs to suicidal ideation. A deeper understanding of the interconnected factors between ACEs and suicidal ideation could reveal potential intervention targets to mitigate suicidality risk in MSM, particularly among those who have experienced ACEs.

In recent years, the network approach to psychopathology has been proposed as a framework that conceptualizes mental disorders as the result of direct interactions between symptoms33. Unlike traditional models that view mental disorders as latent variables causing symptoms, the network model conceptualizes symptoms themselves as mutually interacting entities. This approach stems from graph theory and complex systems theory, using network analysis as a graph-based method where nodes represent psychological variables or symptoms, and edges depict the correlations or associations between them33. This offers a dynamic and visual framework to study and understand the intricate relationships within mental disorders. Psychological networks view symptoms as interacting entities, with some symptoms serving as “bridge nodes”. These symptoms connect different symptom clusters, facilitating the spread of symptoms across the network34. Research has found that the activation and inhibition of bridge nodes are crucial for understanding the functioning and changes within psychological networks35. For example, research has shown that perceived burdensomeness was considered a potential bridge between psychological distress and suicidal ideation36. Additionally, a network analysis of adolescent suicidal ideation revealed that adverse parenting practices may actuate and sustain suicidal ideation by altering important proximal psychological factors such as resilience, fear, and rumination, suggesting that these bridging variables may act as critical nodes that trigger adolescent suicide37.

Given the ability of network analysis to reveal interactions between different symptoms and identify key nodes, it was employed in this study to explore the bridge nodes that link distal ACEs, with proximal psychological risk factors that influence suicidal ideation. By identifying these bridge nodes, we aim to uncover how proximal factors influence the impact of ACEs on suicidal ideation, providing a comprehensive framework to understand these complex interactions. Specifically, this study seeks to identify the key proximal psychological risk symptoms that link ACEs to suicidal ideation among MSM. Our primary hypothesis is that certain psychological symptoms act as " bridge nodes " connecting the ACEs to proximal psychological risks (including depression, anxiety, and interpersonal needs), which in turn play a significant role in the development of suicidal ideation in MSM. We have provided the research hypothesis framework diagram for reference (see Figure S1). By elucidating the links between ACEs and proximal psychological factors, and identifying key factors associated with suicidal ideation, this study aims to provide valuable insights for the development of targeted suicide prevention and intervention strategies for the MSM population.

Methods

Participants and procedures

A cross-sectional survey was implemented from February to December 2022 utilizing a snowball sampling technique across three diverse Chinese cities: Shanghai (eastern region), Shenyang (northeastern region), and Kunming (southwestern region). Initial participants, termed “seed” participants, were identified with the help of non-governmental organizations or local disease prevention and control centers, selecting five to ten qualified MSM per city. Eligibility criteria included being biologically male, aged 18 years or older, and having engaged in sexual activities with other men within the past six months. Exclusion criteria encompassed those with self-reported serious mental illnesses, intellectual disabilities, or an inability to fully engage in the survey were excluded from participation. These seeds then recruited additional participants until a saturation point was reached, where no new participants could be added. The final sample comprised 409 participants from Shanghai, 566 from Shenyang, and 163 from Kunming. All participants provided written informed consent.

All participants were assured of their anonymity and were informed of their right to withdraw from the survey at any time without any consequences. Participants provided demographic information and completed a series of measures on ACEs, depression, anxiety, interpersonal needs, suicidal ideation, and other relevant variables. To avoid potential biases, such as emotional distress that could lead to response bias and affect the accuracy of subsequent answers, sensitive topics such as suicidal ideation were introduced later in the survey. Participants were rewarded with 125 RMB (approximately 18 USD) upon completing the questionnaire.

Ethical statement

All procedures contributing to this work comply with the ethical standards of the relevant national and institutional committees on human experimentation and with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, as revised in 2013. All procedures involving human subjects were approved by the Ethics Committee of the School of Public Health, Shanghai Jiao Tong University, China (Protocol #2021-HIVHRP-22-310222197604080237).

Measures

Demographic characteristics

The survey collected demographic data, such as age, sexual orientation, level of education, monthly income, marital status, employment status, and duration of local residence. This information was used to describe the participants’ basic characteristics.

Adverse childhood experiences

The Adverse Childhood Experiences International Questionnaire (ACE-IQ) developed by the WHO was employed38. This 29-item tool assesses 13 categories of ACEs divided into four dimensions: "neglect," " maltreatment," "family dysfunction," and " extra-family violence." The “neglect” dimension includes physical (3 items) and emotional neglect (2 items); “maltreatment” covers sexual abuse (4 items), physical abuse (2 items), and emotional abuse (2 items); “family dysfunction” includes lived with substance-abusing family members (1 item), lived with mentally ill or suicidal family members (1 item), lived with incarcerated family members (1 item), parental death, separation, or divorce (2 items), and domestic violence (3 items); and "extra-family violence" consists of bullying (1 item), witnessing community violence (3 items), and experiencing war/collective violence (4 items). Items concerning community and war violence were excluded from this study, as these forms of violence are less prevalent in the current stable social and public security environment in China, leaving a total of 22 items across 11 categories. Scores are assigned using a binary method as recommended by the WHO, with higher scores indicating a greater number of experienced ACE categories. The ACE-IQ scale demonstrated high internal consistency with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.814 in this study.

Proximal psychological risk factors

Depressive symptoms measurement: the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) was used to assess participants’ depressive symptoms over the last two weeks39. Due to overlap with the measurement of suicidal ideation used in this study, the final item of the PHQ-9, "thoughts of harming oneself or being better off dead," was excluded. Thus, the adapted version used here, the PHQ-8, consists of eight items. Participants indicated the frequency of symptoms experienced over the past two weeks on a scale ranging from “never” (0), "a few days" (1), "more than half the days" (2) to “almost daily” (3). The total score for the PHQ-8 ranges from 0 to 24, and the score interpretation is similar to that of the PHQ-940. The score interpretation is as follows: 0–4 indicating no depression, 5–9 suggesting mild depression, 10–14 suggesting moderate depression, and 15 or above suggesting severe depression. Higher scores indicate more severe depressive symptoms. The PHQ-8 demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.794.

Anxiety symptoms measurement: the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) was employed to evaluate anxiety symptoms in participants over the past two weeks41. It includes seven items, with responses scored from 0 (never) to 3 (almost daily), yielding a total possible score range from 0 to 21. Higher scores reflect greater anxiety severity, with scores interpreted as follows: 0–5 indicating no anxiety, 6–10 suggesting mild anxiety, 11–15 indicating moderate anxiety, and 16–21 suggesting severe anxiety. The GAD-7 showed robust internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.936.

Interpersonal needs assessment: the Interpersonal Needs Questionnaire (INQ), designed by Van Orden42, consists of 15 items that evaluate whether participants’ interpersonal needs were met in the previous week. The questionnaire encompasses two dimensions: thwarted belongingness and perceived burdensomeness. Responses are rated on a scale from 1 (not at all true for me) to 7 (very true for me), with six items requiring reverse scoring. Higher total scores indicate greater perceived burdensomeness and thwarted belongingness. The overall scale demonstrated good reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.887), with dimensions of thwarted belongingness (Cronbach’s α = 0.825) and perceived burdensomeness (Cronbach’s α = 0.954) both showing high internal consistency.

Suicide ideation

The Beck Scale for Suicide Ideation (BSI), developed by Beck et al. (1979) assesses the presence and severity of suicidal ideation in the past week and during the most severe episode of depression43. The scale consists of 19 items, divided into two factors: suicidal ideation (first five items) and suicidal intent (remaining 14 items). In this study, the first five items were used to assess recent suicidal ideation. Each item is rated on a 3-point Likert scale, with response options of “no” (scored as 0), “weak” (scored as 1), and "moderate to strong" (scored as 2). Absence of suicidal ideation is determined if responses to the fourth and fifth items are both “no”; otherwise, suicidal ideation is considered present. Total scores are summed, with higher scores indicating stronger suicidal ideation. The scale demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.885.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 25.0 for descriptive analysis and R 4.2.1 for network analysis. The normality of continuous variables was assessed; for normally distributed data, means and standard deviations were reported, while medians and interquartile ranges were used for non-normally distributed data. Categorical data were described using frequencies and percentages.

To verify our hypotheses, we initially constructed a psychosocial network to identify bridging nodes linking ACEs to proximal psychological risk factors, encompassing eight depressive symptoms, seven anxiety symptoms, and two interpersonal need dimensions. We then re-estimate the psychosocial network structure to explore the relationships between these identified bridge nodes and suicidal ideation. Additionally, we displayed a flow diagram showing how suicidal ideation was connected to ACEs and psychological symptoms from the network perspective.

Psychosocial network estimation

To explore the association between ACEs and proximal psychological risk variables among MSM, we constructed a network comprising 21 nodes. These nodes represented four dimensions of ACEs, eight items from the PHQ-8, seven from the GAD-7, and two interpersonal needs (perceived burdensomeness and thwarted belongingness).

The network structure was estimated using the Gaussian graphical model (GGM), implemented through the R package qgraph (version 1.9.8)44,45. To minimize weaker connections, we applied the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regularization coupled with the Extended Bayesian Information Criterion (EBIC) for edge selection45,46, setting the tuning parameter at 0.5 to balance sensitivity and specificity46. Spearman correlation coefficients served as the input for our analysis47. Additionally, We calculated the predictability of each node using the R package mgm (version 1.2-14)48. Predictability is visualized with circles around the nodes, indicating the extent to which changes in one node can be attributed to changes in connected nodes.

Bridge strength calculation

In the network, bridge nodes connect different symptom groups, acting as pivotal connectors within the network. The bridge strength for each node was calculated using the R package networktools (version 1.5.2). We used the 80th percentile as a cutoff point to identify bridge nodes with higher bridge strength34. This strength is quantified by summing the absolute edge weights connecting a node to nodes in different symptom groups, highlighting the node’s significance in linking disparate symptom clusters34.

Network accuracy and stability

The network’s accuracy and stability were assessed using the R package bootnet (version 1.6)44. We first used nonparametric bootstrapping to compute 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for edge weights based on 1000 bootstrap samples. The network’s stability was evaluated through the sample-dropping bootstrap method, calculating correlation stability (CS) coefficients from 1000 bootstrap samples, which assess the robustness of edge weights and bridge strengths. CS coefficients are considered adequate if they are no lower than 0.25 and preferably exceed 0.544. We also conducted differential tests on the edge weights and bridge strengths to identify significant differences.

Network associated with suicidal ideation

To further investigate the link between bridge nodes and suicidal ideation, we re-estimated the psychosocial network structure by integrating a continuous variable, which was derived from the BSI, measuring participants’ suicidal ideation over the past week. The “flow” function of the package qgraph was employed to display the ACEs and psychological variables directly or indirectly related to suicidal ideation49.

Additionally, we considered the impact of demographic variables such as age, sexual orientation, education level, monthly income, marital status, employment status, and duration of local residence on the original psychosocial network by reconstructing the network to include these factors. To accurately model the relationships between both categorical and continuous variables, we employed a Mixed Graphical Model (MGM) as recommended by Haslbeck & Waldorp50,51. The differences in edge weights between the newly estimated and initial networks termed the “Delta network”, were calculated. Smaller differences suggest a minimal impact of demographic variables on the network structure. To assess the structural similarity between the two networks in detail, we conducted further analyses using the Quadratic Assignment Procedure (QAP) (see Supplementary materials page7).

Results

Demographical characteristics

A total of 1138 MSM participated in the online survey. After implementing inclusion and exclusion criteria along with quality control measures, 1,130 questionnaires were retained for statistical analysis. The sociodemographic characteristics of the participants are summarized in Table 1. The mean age of the participants was 30.43 years, with the majority (67.1%) identifying as homosexual and a significant proportion (78.3%) having attained at least a college education. A high prevalence of ACEs was reported, with 88.3% of participants indicating they had encountered at least one type of ACE and an average occurrence of 3.4 types of ACEs. The most frequently reported ACEs were domestic violence (62.1%), emotional abuse (50.1%), and bullying (47.9%). Additionally, 354 participants (31.3%) reported experiencing suicidal ideation in the past week. Median scores on the PHQ-8 and GAD-7 were 7 and 6, respectively.

Discussion

In this study, we employed network analysis for the first time to explore the interplay between distal ACEs and proximal psychological risk factors in predicting suicidal ideation among MSM. Our findings suggest that ACEs are associated with suicidal ideation and may be linked to specific proximal psychological factors that correlate with and potentially exacerbate suicidal ideation among MSM individuals. These findings support the IPTS, which identifies perceived burdensomeness and thwarted belongingness as critical psychological factors underlying the development of suicidal ideation. This study enhances our understanding of the psychopathology of suicidal ideation and suggests that targeting specific bridge nodes in the network may help reduce suicide risk among MSM individuals with a history of ACEs.

Our data revealed that 88.3% of participants had experienced at least one type of ACE, with an average of 3.4 types of occurrences. The most common ACEs reported were domestic violence, emotional abuse, and bullying. A systematic review incorporating 63 studies using the ACE-IQ scale38 reported that approximately 75% of community participants had experienced ACEs, with an average frequency of 3 occurrences. Emotional abuse and bullying were the primary types of ACEs. This comparison reveals a potentially higher prevalence of ACEs in the MSM. Additionally, 31.3% of our study’s participants reported suicidal ideation in the previous week, a significantly higher rate than that observed in the general population in China (3.9%)52 and among heterosexual males aged 18–25 (6.7%)9. This proportion also exceeds the global pooled prevalence of suicidal ideation among MSM (21%)53 and the reported rate of 26.01% in a survey of 2,250 MSM across nine major cities in China54. These findings underscore the severity and ubiquity of suicidal ideation in the MSM population.

In the network connecting ACEs and proximal psychological factors, perceived burdensomeness (PB), thwarted belongingness (TB), feeling tired/having little energy (P4), and worrying too much about different things (J3) emerge as key bridge nodes. Previous research has demonstrated that ACEs have long-term effects on individuals’ mental health and behavioral patterns, potentially leading to difficulties in emotion regulation and unstable interpersonal relationships55,56. The findings of this study indicate that negative emotional, cognitive, and interpersonal issues among MSM are associated with ACEs, aligning with previous research findings57,58. Therefore, ACEs may contribute to increased levels of depression and anxiety as well as reduced interpersonal needs among MSM, through psychosocial networks. By understanding and addressing these bridge nodes, we can more effectively assist affected individuals in improving their mental well-being and fulfilling their interpersonal needs. However, additional research is necessary to establish the causal relationships among these associations.

The network analysis conducted after incorporating suicidal ideation revealed a significant association between childhood neglect and suicidal ideation, indicating that individuals who experienced parental neglect show a higher risk of suicide ideation, which is consistent with previous research findings59,60. There are two possible explanations for the long-term impact of childhood neglect. First, from an attachment perspective, neglected children often develop negative internal representations due to their unmet needs, which later affect their ability to form close relationships and seek emotional support61,62. Second, neglected children may encounter various psychological and social challenges and often struggle to cope with these issues as they enter adulthood, increasing the risk of depression and suicidal ideation63. According to Li et al. 64, neglect serves as a central and bridging factor in the network linking ACEs, depressive symptoms, and suicidal ideation among adolescents. Furthermore, our study identified perceived burdensomeness within the bridge nodes as having the strongest positive correlation with suicidal ideation, followed by thwarted belongingness. This suggests that perceived burdensomeness and thwarted belongingness are primary proximal risk factors connecting ACEs to suicidal ideation. We observed a stronger association between perceived burdensomeness and suicidal ideation compared to thwarted belongingness, aligning with previous research65 that consistently shows perceived burdensomeness as a more predictive factor for suicidal ideation than thwarted belongingness. These findings are consistent with the IPTS, suggesting that ACEs are associated with suicidal ideation through increases in perceived burdensomeness and thwarted belongingness. A longitudinal study over five months suggested that unmet interpersonal needs mediate the relationship between childhood trauma and suicidal ideation66. Another network analysis indicated that emotional abuse indirectly relates to suicidal ideation through perceived burdensomeness67. Moreover, the study findings demonstrated that 82% of the variation in suicidal ideation could be explained by adjacent ACEs and proximal psychological factors, highlighting their significant role in explaining suicidal ideation. Targeted interventions addressing these related symptoms may help prevent suicide, with key adjacent symptoms including A1 (neglect), PB (perceived burdensomeness), TB (thwarted belongingness), J5 (being so restless that it is hard to sit still), and J7 (fear that something terrible will happen).

According to the IPTS26, meeting interpersonal needs necessitates positive social support and the establishment of intimate relationships. For the MSM population, constructing a supportive social network, including friends, family, peers, and professional support, along with providing tailored psychological support and counseling services, can significantly reduce the occurrence of suicidal ideation. Additionally, the INQ proves to be a valuable tool for assessing suicidal ideation, especially for individuals reluctant to disclose their suicidal ideation, as they may more accurately report their feelings and thoughts on the questionnaire42,68. Moreover, research indicates a higher prevalence of ACEs among the MSM, which shows strong and long-term associations with adult health risk behaviors, underscoring the need for comprehensive prevention strategies11. These strategies should focus on preventing the occurrence of ACEs, particularly emotional and physical neglect; taking appropriate actions to minimize the impact of these experiences once recognized; and mitigating the long-term health consequences of ACEs, thereby reducing the overall disease burden for individuals.

Our study has several limitations that should be noted. Firstly, the cross-sectional design restricts our ability to infer causality from the observed associations. Future studies should employ longitudinal designs to validate and further investigate these relationships. Secondly, given the relatively concealed and smaller population size of the MSM community, strict random sampling was not feasible. Consequently, we resorted to snowball sampling, which may have introduced selection bias and non-response bias. Thirdly, the self-report nature of ACEs and suicidal ideation assessments may lead to recall bias. Fourthly, network analysis methods have inherent limitations in terms of replicability across different samples, restricting the generalizability of our findings. Lastly, a significant limitation of this study is the failure to assess the additional psychological stressors faced by MSM individuals in heterosexual marriages, such as the emotional burden of concealing their sexual identity and coping with marital pressure. Future research should explore the intersection of sexual identity, marital status, and mental health, particularly within diverse cultural contexts.

Conclusion

This study is the first to utilize network analysis to examine the intricate relationships between ACEs, proximal psychological risk factors, and suicidal ideation among MSM. Our findings corroborate the IPTS and reveal that unmet interpersonal needs partially affect the relationship between ACEs and the increased risk of suicidal ideation. This insight is pivotal for understanding both the development and the potential interventions for suicidal ideation within the MSM. The findings indicate the necessity of preventing the occurrence of ACEs and focusing on addressing their perceived burdensomeness and thwarted belongingness to reduce the prevalence of suicidal ideation among MSM.
