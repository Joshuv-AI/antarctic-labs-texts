Ferroptosis in the pathogenesis of diabetic cardiomyopathy: mechanisms and therapeutic potential
Authors: Qingqing Zhao, Ming Wang, Ning Zhou, Yan Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiabetic cardiomyopathy (DCM) is a significant complication of diabetes mellitus, often leading to heart failure and increased mortality. While its pathogenesis remains incompletely understood, key contributors include chronic hyperglycemia, hyperlipidemia, insulin resistance, myocardial fibrosis, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and aberrant cell death pathways. Emerging evidence highlights ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death, as a critical player in DCM progression. This review synthesizes current knowledge on the mechanistic links between ferroptosis and DCM, focusing on endothelial dysfunction, myocardial fibrosis, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial damage. We also discuss promising therapeutic strategies targeting ferroptosis to alleviate DCM, including pharmacological inhibitors, natural compounds, and non-coding RNAs, while identifying gaps for future research.Keywords: Diabetic cardiomyopathy, Ferroptosis, Oxidative stress, Mitochondrial dysfunction, Therapeutic targets

Introduction

Diabetic cardiomyopathy (DCM), first proposed by Rubler et al.[1, 2] in 1972, is characterized by heart failure progression[3] resulting from disturbances in glucose and lipid metabolism[4, 5], occurring independently of coronary artery disease, hypertension, or valvular heart disease. As the leading cause of death in diabetic patients, DCM exhibits persistently high incidence rates, driven by the steadily increasing global prevalence of diabetes. According to the Global Burden of Disease 2021 study, the global prevalence of diabetes reached 529 million people in 2021 and is projected to increase to 1.31 billion by 2050[6]. Consistent with this trend, the International Diabetes Federations Diabetes Altas (11th) reports that there have been 589 million adults (20–79 years) living with diabetes worldwide in 2024 (https://diabetesatlas.org/). A community-based diabetes survey revealed a wide range of DCM prevalence (11.7–67.0%) within a cohort of 10,208 diabetic patients, with diagnostic stringency significantly influencing this variation[7].

DCM typically presents with early diastolic dysfunction in the absence of clinical manifestations; significant manifestations emerge only upon progression to systolic dysfunction, underscoring its insidious clinical trajectory[2]. In the early stage, patients are often asymptomatic[8], and the only detectable abnormality is left ventricular diastolic dysfunction (elevated mitral annular velocity ratio (E/e′), reduced early-to-late diastolic flow ratio (E/A ratio). At this point, metabolic abnormalities begin in the cardiomyocytes, characterized by increased fatty acid oxidation, decreased glucose utilization, and the onset of intracellular lipid accumulation[9]. Pathologically, collagen deposition begins to appear in the myocardial interstitium[5]. As diastolic dysfunction worsens, patients develop exertional dyspnea and reduced exercise tolerance, accompanied by more pronounced interstitial fibrosis. In the final stage, typical heart failure symptoms are evident; systolic dysfunction (reduced left ventricular ejection fraction) coexists with diastolic dysfunction, diffuse myocardial fibrosis is present, and cardiomyocytes exhibit decompensated hypertrophy. Among these pathophysiological processes, insulin resistance, oxidative stress (OS), myocardial interstitial remodeling and fibrosis, and microvascular dysfunction are considered core mechanisms[10–12].

Growing evidence supports a multifactorial pathogenesis that extends beyond metabolic dysregulation to include OS[13], inflammatory responses, mitochondrial dysfunction, cardiac lipotoxicity, endoplasmic reticulum stress, impaired calcium handling, and cell death[14]. Notably, ferroptosis, an iron-dependent regulated cell death mechanism, has recently been implicated as a pivotal driver of DCM pathogenesis[15–17], positioning it as a promising therapeutic target.

The molecular mechanisms of ferroptosis

Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death first proposed and defined by the Stockwell research team[18], has been increasingly delineated over years of investigation. It is typically precipitated by disruption of cellular iron homeostasis, which catalyzes Fenton chemistry and drives the accumulation of lipid peroxides, ultimately compromising membrane integrity and causing cell death. Multiple forms of cell death (including pyroptosis, autosis, necroptosis, ferroptosis, and apoptosis) have been reported in DCM[19].

Among the various forms of programmed cell death (PCD), apoptosis is the most extensively characterized. It is a rapid, irreversible process defined biochemically and mechanistically by activation of a proteolytic cascade mediated by cysteine-dependent, aspartate-directed proteases (caspases). Ferroptosis is distinguished from other PCD modalities by two defining features. First, its initiation is independent of death-receptor signaling, in contrast to receptor-driven pathways such as extrinsic apoptosis[20]. Second, it is propelled by intracellular lipid peroxidation (LPO) (typically of polyunsaturated phospholipids) which generates cytotoxic lipid adducts that compromise plasma-membrane integrity and culminate in cell death[21, 22]. The interplay between ferroptosis and other forms of cell death has also been proposed by researchers in recent years. For instance, autophagy-dependent ferroptosis was defined in 2018[23], and the mechanisms underlying how autophagy interacts with ferroptosis have been further elucidated by subsequent studies[24].

The currently recognized mechanisms underlying ferroptosis can be summarized as follows: (1) Disruption of iron homeostasis: Excessive accumulation of intracellular iron ions catalyzes the Fenton reaction, generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) that promote iron instability and subsequent deposition; (2) LPO accumulation: LPO represents a central driver of ferroptosis initiation and progression, with polyunsaturated fatty acid-containing phospholipids serving as the critical substrates in this process[25, 26]; (3) Dysfunction of the System Xc⁻–glutathione (GSH)–glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) axis: The amino acid antiporter System Xc⁻, composed of solute carrier family 7 member 11(SLC7A11) and solute carrier family 3 Member 2(SLC3A2), imports cystine into cells to support intracellular GSH synthesis. GPX4 utilizes GSH to reduce lipid hydroperoxides to their corresponding alcohols, simultaneously oxidizing GSH to glutathione disulfide, thereby detoxifying peroxides and hydroxyl radicals produced during cellular metabolism[27, 28]. Impairment of the System Xc⁻–GSH–GPX4 axis markedly enhances susceptibility to ferroptosis. (4) Inactivation of ferroptosis-suppressor-protein 1 (FSP1) -associated pathways: The anti-ferroptotic activity of FSP1 relies on its NADH/NADPH-dependent enzymatic functions as a Coenzyme Q₁₀ oxidoreductase and vitamin K reductase[29, 30]. (5) Other modulatory factors: Additional molecules, including NADPH oxidase 4, heat shock protein β1[31], and GTP cyclohydrolase1[32] have also been implicated in the regulation of ferroptosis[33]. Excessive ferritinophagy also triggers ferroptosis intracellular. Ferritinophagy also is a selective autophagic process that specifically targets ferritin for degradation. When dysregulated, it induces uncontrolled release of iron into the cell, thereby driving the execution of ferroptosis[24]. Collectively, pathways governing iron metabolism, antioxidant defense, and LPO intricately converge to orchestrate the onset and execution of ferroptosis, a regulated cell death modality increasingly recognized as a key pathogenic mechanism in cardiovascular diseases[34].

Morphologically, cells undergoing ferroptosis display distinctive mitochondrial abnormalities (such as swelling, altered cristae density, and ruptured membranes) readily observed under electron microscopy[35, 36]. These ultrastructural hallmarks indicate that mitochondrial dysfunction plays a pivotal role in the ferroptotic process. Given the well-established link between mitochondrial integrity and cardiomyopathy, such dysfunction further implicates ferroptosis in cardiac pathogenesis. Notably, severe mitochondrial impairment promotes excessive production of ROS and labile iron, both of which amplify ferroptosis signaling[37]. Thus, mitochondria can be regarded as a critical target for modulating ferroptosis. Furthermore, iron overload exacerbates cardiac injury in diabetic settings by enhancing ROS production[38], underscoring the intimate interplay between ferroptosis and OS.

Recent studies have shown that ferroptosis contributes to the onset and progression of various cardiovascular diseases, including heart failure[39], myocardial infarction[40], cardiomyopathy[16, 41], and ischemia/reperfusion injury[42, 43]. Therapeutic inhibition of ferroptosis has shown promise in mitigating these pathological processes. In diabetic animal and cellular models, iron deposition often coincides with mitochondrial dysfunction, LPO accumulation, and other cellular abnormalities[44], collectively initiating ferroptotic death. Although systemic iron metabolism has been extensively characterized, the precise mechanisms underlying iron accumulation within cardiomyocytes under DCM conditions remain to be fully elucidated.

Iron deposition has been consistently documented in db/db mice and other diabetic models[45]. In a study using type 1 diabetic mice, cardiac labile iron exhibited a biphasic change—an initial decline during the first week following diabetes onset, followed by a gradual increase from the second week onward. This temporal fluctuation paralleled dynamics changes in the expression of genes associated with ferroptosis and LPO[46]. The transferrin receptor (TFRC) facilitates cellular iron uptake by binding divalent iron ions and mediating their internalization[47]. Beyond TFRC-dependent mechanisms, the Piezo-type mechanosensitive ion channel component 1 channel has also identified as a mediator of iron influx in response to mechanical stress, thereby aggravating ferroptosis in nucleus pulposus cells[48]. These findings suggest that multiple pathways may contribute to iron accumulation within cardiomyocytes, although further studies are required to substantiate this hypothesis.

In addition to iron import, cellular iron levels are tightly regulated by iron export mechanisms. Ferroportin 1(FPN1), encoded by the SLC40A1 gene, is the only known iron exporter in mammals[49] and plays a pivotal role in maintaining systemic iron homeostasis. The transcription factor nuclear factor erythroid2-related factor 2 (NRF2), a master regulator of antioxidant responses and ferroptosis, has been implicated in the transcriptional regulation of FPN1; notably, Fpn1 expression is markedly reduced in Nrf2-knockout mice[50]. Recent studies further indicate that impaired nuclear translocation or expression of NRF2 diminishes FPN1 transcription, thereby reducing iron efflux and promoting intracellular iron retention in diabetic rats[51, 52].

In this review, we summarize the current understanding of the relationship between ferroptosis and the diverse pathophysiological processes underlying DCM, highlight potential therapeutic targets, and discuss critical unresolved questions to guide future research.

The interplay between ferroptosis and key diabetic milieu features

Emerging evidence underscores the complex pathogenic interplay between metabolic disturbances in diabetes—namely hyperglycemia, insulin resistance (IR), and hyperlipidemia—and the induction of ferroptosis. Hyperglycemia and IR act synergistically to create a pro-ferroptotic milieu[53]. Hyperglycemia promotes OS through multiple mechanisms, including the formation of advanced glycation end products[54], and the activation of protein kinase C[55], ultimately depleting key antioxidants such as GSH and inactivating the lipid repair enzyme GPX4, the pivotal enzyme safeguarding cells against LPO–driven ferroptosis[56]. Concurrently, IR and the consequent hyperlipidemia-particularly elevated free fatty acids((FFAs) further intensify ferroptotic susceptibility by providing an abundant pool of polyunsaturated fatty acids that serve as substrates for LPO within cellular membranes. In parallel, dysregulated iron metabolism in diabetes, often manifested as intracellular iron overload, acts as a powerful catalyst for the Fenton reaction, amplifying ROS generation and accelerating membrane lipid oxidation. Collectively, the convergence of hyperglycemia, IR, hyperlipidemia, and iron overload establishes a vicious cycle that synergistically drives ferroptosis, thereby contributing to the onset and progression of diabetic complications such as nephropathy, cardiomyopathy, and neuropathy. Targeting this metabolic–ferroptotic axis represent a promising therapeutic strategy to attenuate tissue injury in diabetes.

Insulin resistance and hyperglycemia

Elevated blood glucose is a hallmark of diabetes, persisting throughout the lifespan of affected individuals. This metabolic disturbance primarily stems from IR, and the two are therefore inseparable in pathophysiological discussion. Recent evidence has shown that high glucose levels are indispensable for the induction of ferroptosis, whereas glucose deprivation effectively suppresses this process in cells[57]. Systemic hyperglycemia triggers broad metabolic perturbations that promote enhanced fatty acid oxidation within cardiomyocytes[58], consequently amplifying intracellular LPO (Fig. 1a).

Impairment of the insulin pathway also contributes to ferroptosis in diabetic hearts. Under physiological conditions, insulin binding to its receptor activates both the phosphatidylin-ositol-3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B(AKT) and Ras/mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) cascades. However, in the context of IR, the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway in vascular smooth muscle cells becomes dysfunctional[59]. In cardiomyocytes exposed to IR, expression of SLC7A11 and intracellular GSH levels are markedly reduced, thereby predisposing cells to ferroptosis[60]. Pharmacological studies have shown that suberosin, an isoprenylated coumarin compound, mitigate ferroptosis by modulating the acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4)-lysophosphatidylcholine acyltransferase 3 axis and the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, thereby alleviating thiazolidinedione-induced DCM in Sprague–Dawley rats (Fig. 1b) [61]. The Ras/MAPK signaling pathway also regulates the transcription of SLC7A11, a critical determinant of ferroptotic susceptibility[62]. Moreover, dapagliflozin, a sodium‒glucose cotransporter 2(SGLT2) inhibitor, has been reported to attenuate myocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury by suppressing ferroptosis via SLC7A11/GPX4 axis and MAPK dephosphorylation(Fig. 1b) [63]. IR is further positively correlated with vascular endothelial disorders. Computational modeling of endothelial insulin signaling indicates that IR disrupts the PI3K/nitric oxide (NO) and MAPK/endothelin-1 (ET-1) pathways, leading to diminished endothelial NO synthase activity and elevated ET-1 and adhesion molecule expression[64].

Unlike the MAPK pathway, adenosine 5’-monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) serves as a central regulator of cellular energy homeostasis. Energy stress has been shown to activate AMPK, thereby suppressing ferroptosis [57]. In cancers, AMPK activation can conversely promote ferroptosis through mechanisms involving autophagy[65] or p53 signaling[66].

In the context of DCM, IR impairs Ca2+ homeostasis, partly through the AKT/skeletal muscle preferentially expressed protein kinase/sarcoplasmic/endoplasmic reticulum Ca2⁺-ATPase 2a pathway, contributing to disease progression[67]. Furthermore, ferroptosis has been implicated in pancreatic β-cell dysfunction, with evidence suggesting its amelioration can improve diabetic phenotypes. For instance, silibinin alleviates ferroptosis in rat insulinoma (INS-1) cells exposed to palmitic acid (PA) and high glucose by enhancing PTEN-induced kinase 1(PINK1)/PARKIN-dependent mitophagy[68]. Collectively, these findings indicate that hyperglycemia and IR exacerbate ferroptosis in both endothelial cells and cardiomyocytes, thereby aggravating DCM.

Hyperlipidemia

Under IR and hyperlipidemia, cluster of differentiation 36 (CD36) is preferentially translocated to the cardiomyocyte membrane, enhancing the uptake of FFAs (Fig. 1c) [5]. This intracellular lipid overload promotes LPO, a hallmark of ferroptosis. Neuregulin-4 (NRG4), an adipokine primarily secreted by brown adipose tissue, has been identified as a protective factor in DCM[69] and other metabolic disorders, including atherosclerosis[70] and nonalcoholic steatohepatitis[71]. NRG4 improves systemic glucose and lipid metabolism, reduces IR, and counteracts ferroptosis via activation of the AMPK/NRF2 pathway[72]. Further evidence indicates that NRG4 protects against metabolic disturbances in DCM by inducing autophagy through the AMPK/ mechanistic target of rapamycin axis (Fig. 1c) [69]. Similarly, fas-associated factor 1, initially characterized as a tumor suppressor, functions as a cytoplasmic inhibitor of ferroptosis. It sequesters FFAs within a hydrophobic globular core, physically limiting their access to Fe2⁺ and thereby suppressing the initiation of LPO[73]. These findings highlight adipose tissue-derived factors as key mediators of myocardial injury, influencing cardiomyocyte metabolism and ferroptosis susceptibility. Elucidating the precise mechanisms of adipose-cardiomyocyte crosstalk represents a critical direction for future research.

Ferroptosis and cell dysfunction

Myocardial fibrosis

Emerging evidence positions ferroptosis as a key driver of myocardial fibrosis, a defining characteristic of DCM. Therapeutically, compounds like 6-gingerol show dual efficacy by concurrently attenuating ferroptosis, inflammation, and fibrosis [74]. This interplay is further illuminated by noncoding RNAs(ncRNAs): silencing the long non-coding RNAs(lncRNA) ZNFX1 antisense RNA 1(ZFAS1) suppresses ferroptosis via the microRNA-150-5p(miR-150-5p) axis, resulting in reduced fibrosis (Fig. 1d)[75]. In a similar vein, circular RNA 005077(circ_005077) counteracts ferroptosis and subsequent fibrosis under conditions of lipid toxicity by facilitating a cyclophilin A(CyPA)/neutrophil cytosolic factor 1(p47PHOX) complex that activates NADPH oxidase[76]. Therefore, targeting ferroptosis is an indispensable therapeutic strategy for ameliorating the fibrotic phenotype in DCM.

Recent evidence points to cardiomyocyte surface receptors and signaling molecules as critical mediators of fibrosis in DCM. The (pro)renin receptor, a multifunctional component of the renin–angiotensin system expressed in cardiac tissue, mitigates ferroptosis-associated pathological remodeling—including myocardial fibrosis—by modulating the nuclear receptor coactivator 4(NCOA4)-mediated ferroptosis pathway (Fig. 1d)[77]. Similarly, speckle-type POZ protein (SPOP), an adapter for E3 ubiquitin ligases, is downregulated in the diabetic myocardium, aggravating DCM pathogenesis. Conversely, SPOP overexpression ameliorates cardiac dysfunction, injury, fibrosis, hypertrophy, and ferroptosis (Fig. 1D)[78]. Another promising target, fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21), exerts therapeutic effects by inhibiting ferroptosis and alleviating OS (Fig. 1d). Mechanistically, FGF21 binds to ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1) and ferritin light chain, stabilizing ferritin complexes by suppressing both autophagy-lysosomal and proteasomal degradation. This extends ferritin half-life, enhances cellular iron storage capacity, and ultimately suppresses ferroptosis in DCM[79].

Beyond cardiomyocyte-autonomous mechanisms, crosstalk between fibroblasts and cardiomyocytes contributes to DCM pathogenesis. Under diabetic conditions, impaired matrix degradation around fibroblasts promotes a pro-fibrotic phenotype[80]. Given the causal role of ferroptosis in fibrogenesis, its suppression emerges as a viable strategy to counteract myocardial fibrosis in DCM.

Cardiomyocyte ferroptosis

The mechanisms of ferroptosis in cardiomyocytes have been the most thoroughly investigated in the context of DCM. Numerous cardiac metabolic regulators modulate this process. For instance, PA induces ferroptosis in H9C2 cardiomyoblasts, while in high-fat diet (HFD)-induced diabetic mice, cardiac injury is linked to ferritinophagy-mediated ferroptosis, marked by elevated NCOA4 and FTH1 levels[81]. Deficiency of FUN14 domain containing 1 (FUNDC1) exacerbates HFD-induced cardiac remodeling and contractile dysfunction via ACSL4-mediated ferroptosis (Fig. 2)[82]. Additionally, lysine acetyltransferase 2A(KAT2A), upregulated in DCM, epigenetically promotes ferroptosis by enhancing the transcription of TFRC and heme oxygenase 1 (Fig. 2); cardiac-specific KAT2A knockdown ameliorates DCM by repressing these genes[83]. The multifunctional regulator phosphofurin acidic cluster sorting protein 2(PACS2), elevated in diabetic cardiomyocytes, collaborates with carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1A and dihydroorotate dehydrogenase, forms a signaling axis that contributes to ferroptosis by disrupting mitochondrial function (Fig. 2)[84, 85]. Conversely, several endogenous factors protect against ferroptosis. NRG4 attenuates ferroptosis both in vivo and in vitro by activating the AMPK/NRF2 pathway[72], while irisin suppresses ferroptosis in type 1 diabetic hearts via the sirtuin1-p53-SLC7A11/GPX4 axis(Fig. 2)[86]. Other regulators include ubiquitin specific peptidase 24(USP24), which promotes ferroptosis through nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells(NF-κB) activation[87], and leucine-rich repeat-containing G protein-coupled receptor 6(LGR6), whose upregulation in diabetic hearts inhibits ferroptosis and enhances mitochondrial biogenesis via the signal transducer and activator of transcription 3(STAT3)/peroxisome proliferators-activated receptor γ Coactivator α(PGC1α) pathway (Fig. 2)[88]. Reduced retinal dehydrogenase 10 disrupts retinol metabolism, promoting lipotoxicity and ferroptosis in DCM (Fig. 2)[89]. Finally, DNA‐dependent protein kinase catalytic subunit(DNA-PKcs)-mediated phosphorylation of yes‐associated protein 1 activates ferroptosis, highlighting a potential therapeutic target (Fig. 2)[90]. These identified molecules, which interface with lipid metabolism, protein post-translational modifications, mitochondrial function, and nucleic acid regulation, collectively demonstrate that ferroptosis in cardiomyocytes is orchestrated by multiple interconnected pathways. This evidence compels a shift towards an integrated network perspective for understanding the role of ferroptosis. Furthermore, it suggests that ferroptosis may be implicated in a broader spectrum of profound pathophysiological processes, governed by a deeper and more complex layer of molecular regulation.

Several pharmacological agents confer cardiac protection under diabetic conditions by specifically targeting ferroptosis: Sulforaphane mitigates ferroptosis and related cardiac pathology by activating NRF2 via AMPK, indicating its potential utility in preventing DCM (Fig. 2)[91]. Astragaloside IV exerts broad effects—including antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities—and ameliorates DCM by attenuating CD36-mediated ferroptosis and improving myocardial contractile function (Fig. 2)[92]. Schisandrol B modulates multiple metabolic pathways, such as fatty acid degradation, GSH metabolism, and cysteine/methionine catabolism (Fig. 2). In H9C2 cardiomyoblasts, it enhances viability, suppresses ferroptosis, reduces lactate dehydrogenase release, and improves mitochondrial function, thereby alleviating diabetic cardiac injury through dual inhibition of ferroptosis and regulation of lipid metabolism[93]. Baicalin protects the heart via Sentrin-specific protease 1(SENP1)-mediated suppression of Sirtuin 3 (SIRT3) SUMOylation. Inhibition of SENP1 increases SIRT3 SUMOylation, revealing a novel mechanism underlying baicalin’s anti-ferroptotic effects (Fig. 2)[94]. The potential effects of these compounds derived from traditional Chinese medicine on ferroptosis are likely attributable to their potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities. This insight underscores the importance of further exploring traditional Chinese medicinal resources for discovering novel therapeutic agents against ferroptosis.

Endothelial cell ferroptosis

Cardiac microvascular integrity is essential for maintaining metabolic homeostasis, ensuring that oxygen and nutrient delivery meet with cardiomyocyte demand. Endothelial dysfunction is a central pathological feature of DCM, with growing evidence implicating ferroptosis as a key driver to diabetes-associated vascular injury. Hyperglycemia and proinflammatory stimuli synergistically promote endothelial ferroptosis, as demonstrated in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) exposed to high glucose (HG) and interleukin-1β (IL-1β). Further studies corroborate that HG and inflammatory mediators drive endothelial ferroptosis, which is effectively mitigated by deferoxamine(DFO) and ferrostatin-1(Fer-1) in HUVECs [95], reinforcing the central role of the p53/xCT/GSH pathway (Fig. 3a). Diabetes is also associated with dysregulated vitamin A metabolism. All-trans retinoic acid, the primary active metabolite of vitamin A, exacerbates diabetic pathology by impairing glucose-stimulated insulin secretion via the retinoid X receptor / sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1c/uncoupling protein 2 pathway, highlighting its contribution to disease progression and suggesting potential therapeutic targets[96]. Additionally, the resveratrol analog isorhapontigenin protects against endothelial ferroptosis through the peroxiredoxin 2/mitofusin 2(MFN2)/ACSL4 axis (Fig. 3a)[41]. In db/db mice, isorhapontigenin treatment improved microvascular density and perfusion, alleviated structural vascular damage, enhanced endothelial NO synthase activation and NO production, and promoted angiogenesis via phosphorylation of vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2. These findings collectively underscore ferroptosis as a key contributor to microvascular impairment in DCM and a promising interventional target[41].

The cardioprotective agent nicorandil improves cardiac function and ameliorates ventricular remodeling in DCM by attenuating microvascular injury and enhancing perfusion and structural integrity. Its benefits are mediated through the suppression of mitochondria-associated ferroptosis via PINK1/PARKIN-dependent mitophagy (Fig. 3a)[97]. Concurrently, mitogen-activated protein kinase 4 is upregulated in DCM and facilitates diabetic endothelial ferroptosis by suppressing GPX4 expression (Fig. 3a). This leads to dynamin-related protein 1(DRP1) S-nitrosylation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and subsequent microvascular impairment[37]. In conclusion, ferroptosis serves as a critical driver of microvascular pathology in DCM, with its activation being co-regulated by metabolic and inflammatory signaling. This process can be alleviated through pharmacological inhibition or specific pathway interventions, thereby providing important direction for developing novel strategies aimed at microvascular protection in DCM.

Fibroblasts

As a major cellular constituent in the heart, fibroblasts contribute significantly to the pathogenesis of DCM by driving myocardial fibrosis. Their role, however, appears context-dependent. A key mechanism by which fibroblasts influence cardiomyocytes is through paracrine signaling, whereby secreted cytokines act directly on cardiomyocytes or modify the extracellular matrix to promote fibrosis. On one hand, fibroblast-specific activation of Rho family GTPase 3 has shown to protect against adverse cardiac remodeling in DCM, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target (Fig. 3b)[98]. Similarly, another study confirmed that FGF21 protects cardiomyocytes from ferroptosis and thus alleviates DCM (Fig. 3b)[45]. On the other hand, in the hyperglycemic milieu, the myocardium secretes inflammatory factors that activate fibroblasts, thereby exacerbating fibrosis[99]. Therefore, the functional impact of fibroblasts in DCM (whether protective or detrimental) likely depends on the specific cytokines they secrete and the disease stage.

Further investigations using fibroblast-specific conditional knockout or overexpression models could clarify whether these pathways exert context-dependent protective or pathogenic effects during different stages of DCM. In addition, integrative studies combining transcriptomic, metabolomic, and spatial proteomic analyses may help identify novel fibroblast-derived paracrine mediators that influence LPO, GSH metabolism, and ferroptotic signaling in cardiomyocytes. Such efforts may ultimately lead to the development of fibroblast-targeted or paracrine-modulating therapies to prevent myocardial fibrosis and ferroptosis-associated injury in DCM.

Macrophages

Under diabetic conditions, HG promotes an inflammatory response in macrophages, which contributes to myocardial fibrosis[100]. Furthermore, macrophages secrete cytokines that impairs mitochondrial quality control in cardiomyocytes, exacerbating diabetic cardiac dysfunction (Fig. 3c)[101]. Although ferroptosis has been implicated in macrophage-related pathophysiology in other conditions, such as muscle atrophy[102], respiratory diseases[103] and cancer[104], it is regrettable that studies in DCM are scarce. In arthritis context, ferroptosis occurred in M2 macrophages releases high mobility group box 1, which interacts with toll-like receptor 4 on M1 macrophages and thus triggers the exacerbation of inflammation[105]. The interaction between different type of macrophages or between macrophages and other cells are both significant to elucidate the role of ferroptosis in macrophages. It is plausible that macrophage-driven ferroptosis also plays a critical role in DCM, likely through intercellular crosstalk with other cardiac cell types.

Intercellular crosstalk in ferroptosis

In cardiovascular disease, ferroptosis has emerged as a pivotal regulatory axis that integrates diverse cardiac cell types and orchestrates disease progression. This section delineates how intrinsic and extrinsic cardiac cells form a highly coordinated intercellular communication network, engaging multiple molecular pathways that collectively initiate, propagate, and fine-tune ferroptotic signaling.

At the immune–inflammatory interface, macrophages exert profound influence on cardiomyocytes and fibroblasts through multifaceted mechanisms. Recent evidence demonstrates that macrophage-derived mitochondria can be transferred to cardiomyocytes via clathrin- and/or lipid raft–mediated endocytosis, directly triggering ferroptotic death in the recipient cells (Fig. 4)[106]. Conversely, monocyte chemoattractant protein-induced protein 1 mitigates transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β)/Smad3-dependent fibroblast activation by restraining the p53-mediated ferroptotic cascade in macrophages and modulating their polarization state (Fig. 4)[107].

In the crosstalk between fibroblasts and cardiomyocytes, fibroblasts exhibit context-dependent dual regulatory roles. On one hand, fibroblast-derived growth factors (such as FGF21 and fibroblast growth factor 12) are secreted to confer cardioprotective effects, effectively mitigating cardiomyocyte ferroptosis through activation of specific signaling cascades (Fig. 4)[45]. On the other hand, fibroblast-secreted transforming growth factor-β1 promotes cardiac hypertrophy and fibrosis by engaging the canonical Smad2/3 signaling axis (Fig. 4)[108]. Moreover, fibroblast-derived fibroblast growth factor 2 protects endothelial cells through krüppel-like factor 2–dependent suppression of ferroptosis and enhancement of antioxidant defenses, thereby ameliorating microvascular ischemia–reperfusion injury (Fig. 4).

Beyond interactions among resident cardiac cells, exogenous components also participate in the ferroptotic communication network. Exosomes derived from human coronary thrombi have been shown to trigger ferroptosis in cultured cardiomyocytes, evidenced by reduced expression of SLC3A2 and SLC7A11 and increased levels of cleaved caspase-3 and NCOA4 (Fig. 4)[109]. Similarly, exosomal miR-140-5p secreted by adipose-tissue macrophages in obese mice induces ferroptosis by suppressing GSH synthesis in cardiomyocytes (Fig. 4)[110]. Ferroptosis also facilitates neutrophil adhesion to coronary vascular endothelium via the toll-like receptor 4–interferon signaling pathway, coordinating neutrophil recruitment to injured myocardium and amplifying inflammatory responses following cardiac transplantation (Fig. 4)[111].

Notably, mechanisms of ferroptosis-mediated intercellular communication extend beyond cardiac pathology. Apoptotic bodies derived from flap subcutaneous connective-tissue cells suppress ferroptosis through the miR-339-5p/kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1/NRF2 axis, promoting macrophage polarization toward an M2 phenotype and enhancing ischemic flap survival (Fig. 4)[112]. In the tumor microenvironment, macrophage-derived interleukin-8 shields endothelial cells from ferroptotic injury via activation of the C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 2/NF-κB pathway (Fig. 4)[113]. Conversely, cluster of differentiation 163–positive macrophages promote ferroptosis in orbital fibroblasts during thyroid-associated ophthalmopathy through the TGF-β/Smad2/3 cascade (Fig. 4)[114], whereas fibroblasts with high expression of tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinase 1 drive macrophage polarization toward a reparative M2 phenotype via the C–C motif chemokine ligand 5/G-protein-coupled receptor signaling axis (Fig. 4)[115].

In summary, ferroptosis constitutes an intricate intercellular communication network in cardiovascular disease, integrating both resident cardiac cells and systemic mediators to coordinate immune-inflammatory regulation, maintain cellular homeostasis, and propagate injury signaling. This network amplifies damage through self-reinforcing feedback loops, ultimately culminating in cardiac dysfunction. Accordingly, future therapeutic strategies for DCM should move beyond cardiomyocyte-centered protection and instead target the broader ferroptotic communication landscape. Modulating macrophage polarization, leveraging fibroblast-derived protective factors such as FGF21, or interrupting pathological exosomal signaling may represent promising approaches to attenuate or even reverse disease progression.

Subcellular events

In the context of DCM, ferroptosis emerges as a critical executor of cardiomyocyte death, driven by a cascade of subcellular dysregulations. Mitochondrially, hyperglycemia and IR compromise electron transport chain integrity, leading to excessive superoxide generation and profound OS. Concurrently, impaired sarcoplasmic/endoplasmic reticulum Ca2⁺-ATPase activity under diabetic metabolic stress results in cytoplasmic and mitochondrial Ca2⁺ overload[116]. The convergence of OS, iron toxicity, and bioenergetic failure creates a vicious cycle that depletes GSH, inactivates the key lipid repair enzyme GPX4, and ultimately culminates in iron-dependent phospholipid peroxidation, the hallmark of ferroptosis[117]. In conclusion, the orchestrated breakdown of mitochondrial integrity, redox balance, and iron homeostasis at the subcellular level constitutes a central pathway through which ferroptosis propagates diabetic myocardial injury. Oxidative stress

OS in cardiomyocytes has been closely linked to ferroptosis in DCM. Sustained OS is closey associated with both inflammatory activation and fibrotic remodeling. Several antioxidants have demonstrated efficacy in suppressing ferroptosis under diabetic conditions. Phloridzin (Phl), a natural antioxidant, significantly improves left ventricular ejection fraction and fraction shortening in DCM models, while reducing levels of myocardial injury markers such as lactate dehydrogenase and creatine phosphokinase-MB, and attenuating collagen fiber deposition. Mechanistically, Phl alleviates myocardial inflammation by inhibiting the myeloid differentiation primary response 88/NF-κB pathway and counteracts OS by regulating the NRF2/GPX4 axis, thereby reducing ferroptosis[118]. Similarly, the mitochondrial-targeted antioxidant SS-31 effectively ameliorates DCM-related pathology by improving mitochondrial function—promoting ATP production, enhancing mitochondrial membrane potential, and restoring the mitochondrial ultrastructural integrity—through activation of the mitochondrial glutathione(mitoGSH)/ mitochondrial glutathione peroxidase 4(mitoGPX4) pathway[117]. Curcumin attenuates glucose-induced cardiomyocyte ferroptosis by facilitating NRF2 nuclear translocation, upregulating antioxidant enzymes such as heme oxygenase-1, and preventing GPX4 depletion[119]. These findings collectively identify NRF2 as a potential therapeutic target in DCM[125]. Exogenous hydrogen sulfide (administered as intraperitoneal NaHS) mitigates ferroptosis and mitochondrial apoptosis in DCM by promoting the E3 ligase synoviolin—Kelch-like ECH-associated protein interaction, thereby activating NRF2/GPX4/GSH pathway[126]. In contrast, USP24 promotes ferroptosis in DCM through upregulation of NF-κB, likely mediated by reductions in GSH and SLC7A11 levels[87]. Additionally, propofol and salvianolic acid A reduce OS, ferroptosis, and CD36 expression via modulation of the AMPK signaling pathway[127].

Several clinically used drugs also exert anti-ferroptotic effects through antioxidant mechanisms. The SGLT2 inhibitor canagliflozin inhibits total iron and Fe2 + accumulation in diabetic mice and in vitro models, potentially through antioxidant and antifibrotic actions[121]. In HL-1 atrial cardiomyocytes, this effect is mediated via AMPK pathway activation[128]. Dexmedetomidine, a highly selective α2-adrenergic agonist, inhibits ferroptosis through the NRF2/GPX4 pathway, thereby alleviating adriamycin-induced cardiotoxicity[129].

Calcium overload

The Ca2⁺/calmodulin signaling pathway has been implicated in the regulation of ferroptosis and is considered a target for certain natural anticancer compounds[130]. During ferroptosis, intracellular Ca2⁺ levels rise, an effect that can be suppressed by the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1[116]. In diabetic settings, cinnamaldehyde-activated transient receptor potential A1 induces Ca2⁺ influx, which promotes calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II phosphorylation and subsequent nuclear translocation of NRF2, resulting in elevated GPX4 expression and attenuation of HG-induced endothelial ferroptosis[131]. This reveals a link between Ca2⁺ influx and ferroptosis suppression under diabetic conditions. Calcium homeostasis is also closely tied to mitochondrial function. Mitochondrial calcium uniporter deficiency has been shown to protect against iron overload-induced cardiac dysfunction by reducing ferroptosis[132], underscoring the role of mitochondrial Ca2⁺ in this form of cell death. Similarly, mitochondrial calcium uptake 1 was reported to be necessary for LPO and ferroptosis initiation following cold stress[133]. Despite these insights, the interplay between calcium dysregulation and ferroptosis has been predominantly studied in oncology[134, 135], and its relevance in DCM remains largely unexplored. Future studies should seek to elucidate the mechanistic link between Ca2⁺ signaling and ferroptosis in DCM, as well as in other cardiac and systemic diseases.

Mitochondrial homeostasis

Mitochondrial quality control—governed by fusion, fission, and mitophagy—is essential for maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis. In diabetic hearts, this process is modulated by ubiquitin-specific protease 28 via the peroxisome proliferator–activated receptor α-mitofusin 2/MFN2 axis[136]. Mitochondrial function is intimately linked to ferroptosis, and inhibiting ferroptosis has been shown to improve mitochondrial integrity. For instance, treatment with the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 significantly enhances GPX4 expression and aconitase activity, increases mitochondrial iron-sulfur clusters content, and restores mitochondrial membrane potential and complex IV activity[137].

Mitochondrial dysfunction can also trigger ferroptosis. As previously noted, SPOP overexpression ameliorates DCM-induced myocardial dysfunction, injury, fibrosis, hypertrophy and ferroptosis. Mechanistically, SPOP facilitates the ubiquitination and degradation of voltage-dependent anion channel 3 on the mitochondrial membrane, thereby suppressing ferroptosis[78].

Mitophagy also interacts with ferroptosis. The antianginal drug nicorandil suppresses ferroptosis via PINK1/PARKIN-mediated mitophagy, conferring microvascular protection[97]. Nicorandil also promotes AMPKα1 phosphorylation and its mitochondrial translocation, which in turn inhibits ACSL4-mediated mitochondrial translocation, ultimately attenuating mitochondria-associated ferroptosis[97]. Other regulators influence mitochondrial biogenesis: LGR6 overexpression alleviates ferroptosis and impaired mitochondrial biogenesis in HFD/STZ-treated mice by activating STAT3/PGC1α pathway[88]. Additionally, the SENP1/SIRT3 and plays a key role in regulating mitochondrial quality and preventing cell damage. Baicalin promotes SIRT3 deSUMOylation through SENP1, restoring mitochondrial stability and preventing cardiomyocyte death[94].

In addition, receptor-mediated mitophagy can also regulate ferroptosis in DCM. FUNDC1, a mitophagy receptor, protects against HFD–induced cardiac remodeling and contractile dysfunction; its deficiency exacerbates injury via ACSL4-driven ferroptosis[82]. PACS2, a key protein that regulates metabolic diseases, is also found to be associated with DCM. In HG and high-fat conditions, PACS2 is elevated in cardiac tissue and exacerbates ferroptosis[85]. PACS2 also regulates the protein levels of DRP1 and MFN2, suggesting a close association with mitochondrial dynamics.

Noncoding RNAs

Noncoding RNAs have been found to be essential in the diagnosis and treatment of DCM. The efficiency of circulating miR-21, a type of microRNA, has been shown to be better that of other miRNAs in the diagnosis of subclinical DCM. The lncRNA ZFAS1 was found to be elevated in the left ventricular myocardial tissue of db/db mice and HG-treated cardiomyocytes and to promote ferroptosis. ZFAS1 inhibition potentially ameliorates cardiomyocyte ferroptosis by sponging miR-150-5p to activate Cyclin D2 (CCND2), thereby mitigating ferroptotic cardiomyocyte death and attenuating DCM progression[75]. Moreover, microRNAs and circular RNAs have been established as critical regulatory factors in ferroptosis[138]. However, there is a lack of research on these RNAs in DCM. A recent study revealed that DCM mice treated with miR214-3p presented improved cardiac function and alleviated myocardial fibrosis, with upregulated GPX4 protein expression levels and significantly suppressed FTH1, ACSL4, and Cyclooxygenase-2 expression. This effect was the same in the HL-1 atrial cardiomyocytes[124]. Furthermore, long noncoding RNAs regulate ferroptosis in DCM. The lncRNA ZFSA1 works as a competing endogenous RNA that sponges miR-150-5p and downregulates CCND2, promoting ferroptosis and DCM development[139].

Although numerous ncRNAs such as miR-214-3p, ZFAS1, and circ_005077 have been identified to correlate with cardiac injury and ferroptosis, most studies remain correlative. A critical next step is to establish definitive causal links through functional genetics, pinpoint downstream effector mechanisms, and assess the translational potential of targeting these pathways in living organisms.

Therapeutic implications and pharmacological interventions

Iron chelators

Iron chelator constitutes a key therapeutic approach to suppress ferroptosis in DCM by specifically binding to intracellular labile ferrous iron (Fe2⁺), thereby depleting the essential "fuel" required for the execution of ferroptosis. In the pathological milieu of DCM, persistent hyperglycemia and IR disrupt cardiac iron homeostasis, resulting in pathologic expansion of the labile iron pool. Excess Fe2⁺ drives the Fenton reaction, producing a surge of ROS that propagate lethal peroxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids in membrane phospholipids and culminate in ferroptotic cell death. Iron chelators intervene at this fundamental stage, breaking the vicious cycle of iron-driven oxidative injury.

Classic iron chelators such as DFO and deferiprone have demonstrated cardioprotective effects in experimental DCM. Studies have shown that DFO treatment significantly reduces LPO levels in db/db diabetic mice, as well as in cardiomyocytes and endothelial cells exposed to HG/high lipid conditions[95]. It concurrently improves cardiac function, coronary microcirculatory perfusion, and myocardial metabolic-flow matching. The protective mechanisms involve not only the reduction of Fe2⁺-catalyzed ROS generation but also include the suppression of iron-activated proinflammatory signaling and recovery of mitochondrial integrity. Furthermore, iron chelators indirectly modulate iron metabolism proteins, such as by stabilizing the iron exporter ferroportin (FPN1) via NRF2-mediated pathways, thereby promoting iron efflux and re-establishing systemic iron homeostasis.

However, the clinical translation of iron chelators for DCM faces considerable challenges. First-generation agents such as DFO are limited by poor oral bioavailability, short plasma half-life, requirements for continuous administration, and potential systemic side effects—including auditory, visual, renal, and hepatic impairment. Consequently, developing novel iron chelators with enhanced myocardial specificity and safety profiles is a critical future direction. Second, as iron is an essential cofactor in numerous physiological processes, prolonged or nonselective chelation carries the risk of anemia and other undesirable sequelae. Therefore, achieving precise modulation of cardiac iron without provoking systemic iron deficiency is crucial to harnessing the therapeutic potential of this approach.

In summary, iron chelators serve as potent mechanistically grounded strategy to counteract ferroptosis in DCM by directly neutralizing labile iron. Future efforts should focus on designing tissue-specific chelators and orchestrating rational combination regimens with established therapies, such as SGLT2 inhibitors, to maximize efficacy while preserving systemic iron homeostasis. Such advances may ultimately offer renewed therapeutic hope for patients with DCM.

GPX4 activators

GPX4 serves as a critical intracellular antioxidant enzyme and a key molecular checkpoint in the regulation of ferroptosis. It utilizes reduced GSH to specifically catalyze the reduction of toxic lipid hydroperoxides to nontoxic lipid alcohols, thereby maintaining membrane integrity and serving as the ultimate gatekeeper against ferroptosis[140]. In the pathological milieu of DCM, factors including hyperglycemia[53], OS[141], and inflammation[142] collectively downregulate or inactivate GPX4. This impairment severely compromises its capacity to eliminate lipid peroxides, markedly increasing the susceptibility of cardiomyocytes to ferroptosis. Consequently, targeted enhancement of GPX4 expression or activity represents a highly promising therapeutic strategy for attenuating ferroptosis in DCM.

Currently, direct agonists of GPX4 remain relatively scarce, and most existing approaches aim to enhance its expression or activity indirectly through upstream signaling pathways. Among these, activation of the NRF2 pathway represents the principal indirect strategy[143]. For instance, the natural compound sulforaphane activates the AMPK/NRF2 axis, thereby upregulating GPX4 and its critical substrate-supplying systems (SLC7A11 and GSH), effectively suppressing myocardial ferroptosis and improving cardiac function in diabetic models. Similarly, SGLT2 inhibitors such as canagliflozin[121] have been shown to enhance the activity of the System Xc⁻/GSH/GPX4 signaling axis, reducing myocardial iron deposition accumulation and LPO. The cardioprotective effects of these compounds are, at least in part, attributable to their ability to indirectly potentiate GPX4 function.

In summary, augmenting GPX4 activity can effectively counteract the lethal wave of LPO in diabetic hearts, constituting a centra strategy to block ferroptosis. The discovery of small-molecule GPX4 agonists, together with the refinement of indirect activation approaches (including NRF2 agonists and SGLT2 inhibitors) and their rational combination with standard glucose-lowering and heart-failure therapies, holds great potential for advancing DCM treatment. However, achieving cardiomyocyte-specific targeting of GPX4 and ensuring the long-term safety of its sustained activation remain major challenges that must be addressed in future translational research.

NRF2 activators

Activation of the transcription factor NRF2 has emerged as a compelling therapeutic avenue for mitigating ferroptosis in DCM, acting through a coordinated network of multitarget antioxidant defenses. As the master regulator of cellular redox homeostasis, NRF2 orchestrates the transcription of a wide array of cytoprotective genes, thereby restoring the disrupted equilibrium between pro-oxidant and antioxidant forces in the diabetic heart[144]. In DCM, chronic hyperglycemia and IR continuously impede NRF2 nuclear translocation, leading to a collapse of its downstream defense network and rendering cardiomyocytes acutely susceptible to iron-driven LPO. Pharmacological NRF2 activation exert thus reinstates this endogenous protective axis, conferring broad and potent cardioprotective effects.

At the mechanistic level, NRF2 activation reinforces ferroptosis resistance through several convergent pathways. First, it enhances GSH metabolism by transcriptionally upregulating key components such as the xCT and glutamate-cysteine ligase catalytic subunit, thereby increasing intracellular GSH availability and sustaining GPX4-mediated detoxification of lipid peroxides. Second, NRF2 directly promotes GPX4 expression itself, consolidating the core enzymatic barrier against ferroptotic damage. Third, NRF2 modulates iron homeostasis by upregulating the iron exporter FPN1 and the iron-storage protein FTH1, which facilitate iron efflux and sequestration, thereby limiting Fenton-reaction substrates at their source. In parallel, NRF2 induces a suite of auxiliary antioxidant enzymes, including heme oxygenase 1 and NAD(P)H, collectively mitigating oxidative stress and reinforcing redox resilience[145].

A growing body of evidence demonstrates that numerous natural compounds and clinically approved drugs can effectively ameliorate DCM through activation of the NRF2 pathway. Natural products such as sulforaphane, 6-gingerol[74, 146], and curcumin[119] have been shown to promote NRF2 nuclear accumulation, thereby improving cardiac function and suppressing ferroptosis-associated markers in experimental models. Even more encouragingly, the cardiorenal protective effects of several widely used antidiabetic agents—SGLT2 inhibitors (e.g., dapagliflozin)[15]and glucagon-like peptide-1(GLP-1) receptor agonists[147, 148]—are increasingly attributed, at least in part, to NRF2 activation, providing a compelling rationale for drug repurposing and rational combination therapy.

In essence, NRF2 activation orchestrates a multilayered defense against ferroptosis, synergistically reinforcing three interdependent pillars: antioxidant defense, iron homeostasis remodeling, and GPX4 system function. By acting through endogenous and multitarget mechanisms, NRF2-centered interventions transcend traditional glucose-lowering paradigms, addressing the core pathophysiology of diabetic myocardial injury. The development of highly selective NRF2 activators, or the strategic optimization of existing NRF2-modulating agents, holds transformative promise for delaying—or even reversing—the progression of DCM.

Noncoding RNA regulators

The emerging recognition of ncRNAs as pivotal regulators of ferroptosis unveils a novel and promising therapeutic frontier in DCM. Accumulating evidence implicates distinct ncRNAs in the fine-tuning of iron metabolism and OS pathways—two molecular hallmarks driving ferroptotic cardiomyocytes injury.

Among them, miR-214-3p has been identified as a potent suppressor of ferroptosis[124], exerting cardioprotective effects through direct modulation of core regulators such as GPX4, ACSL4, and FTH1. In HL-1 atrial cardiomyocytes exposed to diabetic conditions (HFD/STZ-treated mice), upregulation of miR-214-3p effectively mitigated ferroptotic injury, suggesting that therapeutic augmentation of this microRNA could confer robust protection against diabetes-induced cardiac damage.

Conversely, other ncRNAs appear to amplify ferroptotic and oxidative stress cascades. Circular RNA circ_005077 activates the CyPA/p47PHOX/NADPH oxidase axis[76], a major generator of ROS, as demonstrated in HFD-induced diabetic rats and H9C2 cardiomyoblasts, while lncRNA-ZFAS1 exacerbates ferroptosis in db/db mice[75]. Targeted silencing of such detrimental ncRNAs may thus represent an equally crucial therapeutic strategy.

Collectively, these findings underscore an intricate ncRNA–ferroptosis regulatory network that dynamically governs cardiomyocyte fate in DCM. Acting as either suppressors or amplifiers of ferroptotic signaling, ncRNAs offer a rich repertoire of targets for precision medicine. Future interventions leveraging miRNA mimics (e.g., miR-214-3p) or antisense oligonucleotides (e.g., against circ_005077) could enable precise reprogramming of the cardiac molecular environment—attenuating ferroptosis and ultimately halting or reversing the progression of DCM.

Others

In recent years, the intricate interplay between mitochondrial dysfunction and ferroptosis in the pathogenesis of DCM has gained increasing recognition, leading to the identification of multiple promising therapeutic targets. These interventions largely converge on three mechanistic axes: preservation of mitochondrial quality control, inhibition of LPO, and reinforcement of cellular antioxidant defenses.

Several mitochondria-targeted strategies have shown notable efficacy in mitigating ferroptosis. Nicorandil[97] and silibinin[68] activate PINK1/PARKIN-mediated mitophagy, facilitating the removal of damaged mitochondria and attenuating ferroptotic stress. The mitochondria-targeted peptide SS-31[117] enhances mitochondrial redox buffering by augmenting the mitochondrial glutathione system, while irisin suppresses ferroptosis via p53 downregulation and subsequent upregulating of SLC7A11 and GPX4. Moreover, LGR6[88] improves mitochondrial function at its source by promoting mitochondrial biogenesis through the STAT3/PGC1α pathway.

Beyond mitochondrial regulation, modulation of lipid metabolism and inflammatory signaling further refines ferroptosis control. NRG4[72] activates the AMPK/NRF2 axis to coordinate antioxidant defenses, whereas FGF21 confers protection through the ATF4-FGF21-ferritin signaling cascade. Inhibition of ACSL4, using agents such as isorhapontigenin [41] or suberosin[61], directly disrupts the LPO chain reaction that drives ferroptosis. Similarly, suppression of USP24/NF-κB signaling or DNA-PKcs-mediated YAP1 phosphorylation has shown therapeutic promise by attenuating pro-ferroptotic molecular pathways.

Collectively, these findings underscore a multifaceted therapeutic landscape in which mitochondrial protection, antioxidant reinforcement, and lipid/inflammatory modulation act in concert to counteract ferroptosis. Discussion and future perspectives

In summary, this review systematically elucidates the central role of ferroptosis in the pathogenesis and progression of DCM. We emphasize that diabetes-induced metabolic disturbances—such as hyperglycemia, IR, and hyperlipidemia—converge to trigger ferroptosis through multiple pathways, including OS, mitochondrial dysfunction, intracellular iron overload, and LPO. Ferroptosis not only directly induces cardiomyocyte and microvascular endothelial cell death but also aggravates myocardial fibrosis, inflammation, and cardiac dysfunction, thereby constituting a critical pathological link between diabetic metabolic abnormalities and cardiac injury.

Several pharmacological agents—including canagliflozin[121], nicorandil[97], and certain natural compounds[93]—have been demonstrated to inhibit ferroptosis by modulating key pathways such as GPX4, NRF2, and mitophagy, suggesting promising therapeutic strategies and potential targets for DCM. For instance, nicorandil ameliorates ferroptosis by increasing mitochondrial autophagy and improving vascular remodeling in DCM. Additionally, specific ferroptosis inhibitors alleviate ferroptotic damage, further supporting their therapeutic potential. Beyond conventional drugs, small-molecule agents and noncoding RNAs (such as circ_005077(76))also exhibit anti-ferroptotic properties. Additional therapeutic opportunities may involve targeting intracellular calcium ion homeostasis, OS, mitochondrial homeostasis, and protein modifications.

Despite growing recognition of ferroptosis in DCM, several key challenges warrant further exploration. First, the complex intercellular crosstalk among cardiac cell types—including cardiomyocytes, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and macrophages—is not fully understood. Elucidating how these cells interact via paracrine signaling, immune modulation, or metabolic coupling to drive DCM progression will require advanced tools such as cell coculture systems, conditional knockout models, and spatial transcriptomics.

Second, most current evidence originates from mouse models, which differ from humans in metabolic and cardiac characteristics. Clinical trials focusing on ferroptosis-targeting therapies remain scarce. Moreover, assessing ferroptosis levels in humans are methodologically constrained. While animal studies allow direct measurement of ferroptotic markers and tissue pathology, human studies often rely on indirect serum biomarkers. For example, elevated serum levels of high-mobility group box 1 in a lupus model correlated with renal ferroptosis [154]. Identifying a broader panel of serum proteins that reflect cardiac ferroptosis in DCM patients would greatly facilitate clinical trials and biomarker development. Future research should prioritize validation via clinical samples and human cohort studies to confirm the expression patterns and pathological significance of key ferroptosis-related molecules in DCM patients.

Third, identifying sensitive and specific biomarkers of ferroptosis is essential for early DCM diagnosis. Potential candidates include circulating lipid peroxides, iron metabolism indicators, and extracellular vesicles carrying GPX4 or ACSL4. Omics analysis represents a powerful approach for mechanistic exploration; however, studies based on human samples remain scarce, with most investigations still confined to animal or cellular models [155]. Moreover, systematic collection and analysis of clinical patient data should be prioritized in future research.

Finally, combining anti-ferroptosis therapies with existing glucose-lowering agents (e.g., SGLT2 inhibitors) and heart failure drugs represent a promising treatment direction. Future studies should explore synergistic drug combinations, optimize treatment timing and dosage, and evaluate long-term safety—including potential effects on other organs or tumorigenesis—to accelerate clinical translation.
