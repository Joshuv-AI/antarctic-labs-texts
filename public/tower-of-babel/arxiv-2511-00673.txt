Lifted Successor Generation in Numeric Planning (Extended Version)
Dominik Drexler

Abstract
Most planners ground numeric planning tasks, given in a first-order-like language, into a ground task representation. However, this can lead to an exponential blowup in task representation size, which occurs in practice for hard-to-ground tasks. We extend a state-of-the-art lifted successor generator for classical planning to support numeric precondition applicability. The method enumerates maximum cliques in a substitution consistency graph. Each maximum clique represents a substitution for the variables of the action schema, yielding a ground action. We augment this graph with numeric action preconditions and prove the successor generator is exact under formally specified conditions. When the conditions fail, our generator may list inapplicable ground actions; a final applicability check filters these without affecting completeness. However, this cannot happen in 23 of 25 benchmark domains, and it occurs only in 1 domain. To the authors’ knowledge, no other lifted successor generator supports numeric action preconditions. This enables future research on lifted planning for a very rich planning fragment.



Introduction

Numeric planning extends classical planning by introducing numeric state variables, constraints, and effects, thereby enabling the modeling of richer and more realistic domains
(
Fox and Long 2003
)
. Although tasks are typically specified in a first-order-like representation, most planners fully ground them. This grounding step often performs well on many existing benchmarks but can lead to an exponential increase in representation size. At the same time, the ground representation facilitates the development of domain-independent heuristics to guide search
(
Hoffmann 2003
;
Aldinger and Nebel 2017
;
Scala et al. 2017
, e.g.,)
, which are less straightforward to define directly over first-order representations.
However, exponentially larger ground tasks may not fit into memory, causing search to fail, thereby imposing a limitation on lifted search guidance
, such as lifted heuristics
(
Chen and Thiébaux 2024
;
Borelli et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
or generalized policies
(
Wang and Thiébaux 2024
, e.g.,)
learned on smaller example tasks yet scaling to larger ones. Our work is also compatible with extensions of such to numeric planning
(
Francès, Bonet, and Geffner 2021
;
Corrêa et al. 2022
;
Horčík, Fišer, and Torralba 2022
;
Drexler, Seipp, and Geffner 2022
;
Wichlacz, Höller, and Hoffmann 2022
;
Ståhlberg, Bonet, and Geffner 2023
, e.g.,)
.

We address the
lifted successor generation
problem. More precisely, given a first-order action schema and a state, the task is to enumerate exactly those ground actions that are applicable in the state. In comparison, grounded planners often instantiate ground actions independent of a specific state upfront, which can be exponentially many, and store them in a data structure that allows fast enumeration for a given state
(
Helmert 2009
)
. In the lifted successor generation setting, however, applicability must ensure consistent object-variable substitutions across all relevant lifted representations, such as literals and numeric constraints. To the best of our knowledge, existing lifted successor generators build upon conjunctive queries
(
Corrêa et al. 2020
)
, constraint satisfaction problem
(
Francès 2017
)
, or
k
-clique enumeration in
k
-partite graphs (KPKC)
(
Ståhlberg 2023
)
.

We build upon the KPKC-based approach, initially proposed for classical planning tasks, which formulates lifted successor generation as a
k
-clique enumeration problem in
k
-partite graphs. This approach encodes an action precondition as a
k
-partite substitution consistency graph, where each
k
-clique represents an object-variable substitution of the action parameters yielding a candidate applicable ground action. The generator is sound and complete when predicates have arity at most two, meaning that it cannot generate inapplicable ground actions. However, in the general case, a verification of actual applicability is required.

We extend the KPKC-based approach to consider additional structural information from numeric planning tasks. Our approach incorporates numeric constraints into the substitution consistency graph. Consequently, we generalize the theoretical guarantees from the classical to the numeric planning fragment. Our theoretical result guarantees soundness and completeness if and only if literals, function terms, and constraints have arity at most two. We ran an empirical evaluation that validates this result, showing that inapplicable ground actions cannot occur in
23
out of
25
domains across two benchmark suites. Moreover, we observe such actions occurring in only one of the two candidate domains.

A

For an arithmetic operator

\displaystyle J\odot J^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\mathit{hull}\bigl(\{x\odot y\mid x\in J,y\in J^{\prime}~.~x\odot y\text{ is defined }\}\bigr),

. The set of free variables occurring in an element

\mathcal{A}
is a set of actions. Each

,
\mathit{pre}^{-}(a)
,
\mathit{eff}^{+}(a)
, and

for the sets of atoms of positive and negative literals in

and

,

, and

. A ground action

•

,

•

,

is defined; and if

, then
\overline{f}(\mathcal{S})
is also defined, and

•

the set of ground numeric effects in

is

, i.e., there is at most one effect
\overline{f}\otimes\overline{e}
for a ground function term
\overline{f}
or
all effects
\overline{f}\otimes\overline{e}
that share the same
\overline{f}
are unambiguous, i.e.,
\otimes\in\{\mathrel{+}=,\mathrel{-}=\}
or
\otimes\in\{\mathrel{\times}=,\mathrel{\div}=\}
.

Applying a ground action
\overline{a}
in a state

where it is applicable yields the
successor state

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
with

•

\overline{P}(\mathcal{S}^{\prime})=(\overline{P}(\mathcal{S})\setminus\mathit{eff}^{-}(\overline{a}))\cup\mathit{eff}^{+}(\overline{a})

•

\overline{f}(\mathcal{S}^{\prime})=\overline{f}(\mathcal{S})
if
\not\exists\overline{f}\otimes\overline{e}\in\mathit{eff}^{\Delta}(\overline{a})

•

\overline{f}(\mathcal{S}^{\prime})=\overline{e}(\mathcal{S})
if
\overline{f}:=\overline{e}\in\mathit{eff}^{\Delta}(\overline{a})

•

\overline{f}(\mathcal{S}^{\prime})=\overline{f}(\mathcal{S})+\sum\limits_{\overline{f}\mathrel{+}=\overline{e}\in\mathit{eff}^{\Delta}(\overline{a})}\overline{e}(\mathcal{S})-\sum\limits_{\overline{f}\mathrel{-}=\overline{e}\in\mathit{eff}^{\Delta}(\overline{a})}\overline{e}(\mathcal{S})

if
\exists\overline{f}\mathrel{+}=\overline{e}\in\mathit{eff}^{\Delta}(\overline{a})
or
\exists\overline{f}\mathrel{-}=\overline{e}\in\mathit{eff}^{\Delta}(\overline{a})

•

\overline{f}(\mathcal{S}^{\prime})=\overline{f}(\mathcal{S})\cdot\prod\limits_{\overline{f}\mathrel{\times}=\overline{e}\in\mathit{eff}^{\Delta}(\overline{a})}\overline{e}(\mathcal{S})\cdot\prod\limits_{\overline{f}\mathrel{\div}=\overline{e}\in\mathit{eff}^{\Delta}(\overline{a})}\frac{1}{\overline{e}(\mathcal{S})}

if
\exists\overline{f}\mathrel{\times}=\overline{e}\in\mathit{eff}^{\Delta}(\overline{a})
or
\exists\overline{f}\mathrel{\div}=\overline{e}\in\mathit{eff}^{\Delta}(\overline{a})

The objective for a given numeric planning task is to find a
plan
, which is a sequence of ground actions, that when successively applied starting from the initial state, yields a state where the goal holds, i.e., all ground literals and ground numeric constraints mentioned in the goal hold.

Substitution Consistency Graph

The
substitution consistency graph

\mathcal{G}_{a,\mathcal{S}}
is a tuple
\langle\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E}\rangle
where

\mathcal{V}=\{x/o\mid x\in\mathcal{X}(a),o\in\mathcal{O}\}
is the set of vertices, each representing a possible variable substitution, and

\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{V}^{2}\setminus(\mathcal{I}^{\neq}\cup\mathcal{I}^{+}\cup\mathcal{I}^{-})
is the set of edges, each representing all possible pairs of variable substitutions
(
Ståhlberg 2023
)
.
An atom
P(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})

matches
an atom
P^{\prime}(x_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,x_{n}^{\prime})
iff
P=P^{\prime}

and for all
i=1,\ldots,n
,
x_{i}
or
x_{i}^{\prime}
are variables, or else
x_{i}=x_{i}^{\prime}
, i.e., both are objects.
The sets
\mathcal{I}^{\neq},\mathcal{I}^{+},\mathcal{I}^{-}
are:

•

\mathcal{I}^{\neq}=\{\{x/o_{1},x/o_{2}\}\mid x\in\mathcal{X}(a),o_{1},o_{2}\in\mathcal{O}\}
,
i.e., the set of edges whose corresponding substitution function assigns different objects to the same variable,

•

\mathcal{I}^{+}=\{\{v,v^{\prime}\}\mid v,v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V},\exists p\in\mathit{pre}^{+}(a),\forall p^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}~.~p[v,v^{\prime}]\text{ does not match }p^{\prime}\}
,
i.e., the set of edges whose corresponding substitution function, when applied to any atom of a positive literal in the precondition, does not match any ground atom in the state.

•

\mathcal{I}^{-}=\{\{v,v^{\prime}\}\mid v,v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V},\exists p\in\mathit{pre}^{-}(a)~.~\overline{p}[v,v^{\prime}]\in\mathcal{S}\}
,
i.e., the set of edges whose corresponding substitution function, when applied to any atom of a negative literal in the precondition, always yields a ground atom that is part of the state.

From
Ståhlberg 2023
, it follows the following key theorem that allows using k-clique enumeration algorithms to generate precisely all applicable ground actions in a state.

Theorem 1
.

Consider an action
a
with arity
k\geq 2
where each literal
l\in\mathit{pre}(a)
has an arity of at most
2
, and a state
\mathcal{S}
. Then, there exists a
k
-clique
\mathcal{C}=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\}
in
\mathcal{G}_{a,\mathcal{S}}
iff all ground literals
\overline{l}=l[v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}]
with
l\in\mathit{pre}(a)
hold in
\mathcal{S}
.

Theorem
1
does not apply to numeric planning because the substitution consistency graph ignores numeric constraints in the action precondition. Nevertheless, we can use the substitution consistency graph to generate ground applicable actions for numeric planning tasks. However, this typically leads to the generation of inapplicable actions. Therefore, in the following section, we will integrate numeric constraints into the graph while generalizing Theorem
1
.

Numeric Successor Generation

In this section, we integrate numeric constraints into the edge exclusion criterion of the consistency graph. We say that a numeric constraint
c
with nonempty free variables
\mathcal{X}(c)
is
satisfiable
in a state
\mathcal{S}
, written
\mathcal{S}\vDash c
iff there exists a substitution function
\rho
in
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{X}(c),\mathcal{O})
such that the ground numeric constraint
c[\rho]
is satisfiable in
\mathcal{S}
, and otherwise it is
unsatisfiable
, written
\mathcal{S}\nvDash c
. Finally, we add an edge removal set
\mathcal{I}^{\Delta}
based on numeric constraints,
resulting in the set of edges
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{V}^{2}\setminus(\mathcal{I}^{\neq}\cup\mathcal{I}^{+}\cup\mathcal{I}^{-}\cup\mathcal{I}^{\Delta})
with

•

\mathcal{I}^{\Delta}=\{\{v,v^{\prime}\}\mid v,v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V},\exists c\in\mathit{pre}^{\Delta}(a)~.~\mathcal{S}\nvDash c[v,v^{\prime}]\}
,
i.e., unsatisfiable numeric constraints under any substitution of the remaining variables.

Soundness and Completeness

The following theorem generalizes the soundness and completeness results from Theorem
1
toward the extended consistency graph that considers numeric constraints.

Theorem 2
.

Consider an action
a
with arity
k\geq 2
where
each numeric constraint
c
in
\mathit{pre}(a)
has an arity of at most
2
,
and a state
\mathcal{S}
.
Then, there exists a
k
-clique
\mathcal{C}=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\}
in
\mathcal{G}_{a,\mathcal{S}}

iff all ground numeric constraints
\overline{c}=c[v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}]
with
c
in
\mathit{pre}(a)
hold in
\mathcal{S}
.

We can now use the consistency graph to generate bindings that yield ground actions whose precondition components hold in a given state.
Note that such actions may still be inapplicable due to problematic ground effects (see definition of applicability). However, integrating such is analogous to imposing numeric constraints.
Instead, we focus on an efficient method for underapproximating constraint unsatisfiability in the edge removal criterion of
\mathcal{I}^{\Delta}
because this problem is coNP-complete (proof in appendix).

Leaf-Localized Interval Relaxation

To efficiently underapproximate constraint unsatisfiability in the edge removal criterion of
\mathcal{I}^{\Delta}
, we adopt a leaf-localized interval relaxation. The relaxation recursively evaluates a numeric constraint in three stages: (leaf) partially evaluates each function term under the edge substitution, (inner) combines the resulting intervals using interval arithmetic to evaluate function expressions, and (root) applies existential interval comparison to decide satisfiability of the constraint. At the leaf level, we generalize assignment sets
(
Ståhlberg 2023
)
from Boolean to interval outputs. A (numeric)
assignment set
for a function symbol
F
and generic function term
f=F(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
with variables
\mathcal{X}={x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}}
in a state
\mathcal{S}
is a function
\lambda_{F,\mathcal{S}}:\bigcup_{\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{X}^{\prime},\mathcal{O})\rightarrow\mathcal{J}\cup\{\emptyset\}
with

\lambda_{F,\mathcal{S}}(\rho)=\mathit{hull}\bigl(\{f[\rho^{\prime}](\mathcal{S})\mid\rho^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{O}),\rho\subseteq\rho^{\prime}\}\bigr)

The following proposition shows that we can efficiently compute an assignment set, assuming that we restrict the number of substitutions
d
in its input domain.

Proposition 3
.

Consider a state
\mathcal{S}
with
n
ground function terms over a function symbol
F
with arity
\mathit{ar}(F)=k
and variables
\mathcal{X}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}
, and an integer
d
in
\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
We can construct an assignment set
\lambda_{F,\mathcal{S}}
for
F
in
\mathcal{S}
with domain
\bigcup_{\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{X},|\mathcal{X}^{\prime}|\leq d}\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{X}^{\prime},\mathcal{O})
in
O(n\cdot k^{d}|\mathcal{O}|^{d})
time,
O(n+k^{d}|\mathcal{O}|^{d})
space.

Intuitively,
\lambda_{F,\mathcal{S}}(\rho)
captures the range of values that the function term can take after fixing some variables (an overapproximation). More precisely, we first apply the partial substitution
\rho
to
f
. Then, we assign arbitrary objects to the remaining variables, yielding all ground function terms in
\mathcal{S}
, and take the smallest interval containing all of their values. In our setting, each such
\rho
corresponds to an edge
\{v,v^{\prime}\}
under consideration, so
d=2
suffices. Interval arithmetic preserves this overapproximation when evaluating function expressions. Finally, the existential interval comparison also overapproximates satisfiability: it may miss some unsatisfiable constraints but never declares a satisfiable one as unsatisfiable. The following corollary summarizes the key result.

Corollary 4
.

The result of Theorem
2
also holds when applying the interval-based relaxation using assignment sets, provided that each function in
\mathcal{F}
has arity at most two.

Experiments

We evaluated our successor generator on two benchmark sets: (1) all numeric domains from the 2023 IPC and (2) all numeric domains from the MinePDDL benchmark suite
(
Hill et al. 2024
)
, which are challenging for both lifted and grounded numeric planners. We consider two baseline configurations:
Grounded
, which constructs a ground task and generates a decision tree using CART
(
Breiman et al. 1984
)
with Gini-based node selection over precondition frequencies, and
Exhaustive
, which enumerates all candidate bindings consistent with type annotations. For the
k
-clique enumeration in the substitution consistency graph, we compare
Propositional
, which ignores
\mathcal{I}^{\Delta}
, against
Numeric
, which includes it. We ran each configuration using
A^{*}
search
(
Hart, Nilsson, and Raphael 1968
)
with a blind heuristic, a 30-minute time limit, and an 8 GB memory limit under the Lab toolkit
(
Seipp et al. 2017
)
.

Table
1
shows that
Exhaustive
yields the lowest coverage, highest runtime, and largest overapproximation ratio. As more edges are excluded, i.e., moving toward
Propositional
and further to
Numeric
, we observe the following trends: coverage remains stable or improves, runtime generally decreases, and the overapproximation ratio either remains constant or decreases, often drastically on MinePDDL. Most notably, the overapproximation ratio for
Numeric
is 1.00 in all but one domain, specifically the rover domain. Theoretically, soundness conflicts can occur only in rovers and pathwaysmetric, due to predicates, and in the latter, also functions/constraints of arity three.

Conclusions

We presented the first lifted successor generator for numeric planning, circumventing the worst-case exponential blowup of instantiating a ground task representation while enabling search directly over a first-order representation of numeric planning tasks. We have demonstrated that the key properties of its predecessor, initially developed for the classical planning fragment, naturally carry over to the more expressive numeric setting. Finally, we have empirically validated our theoretical results by demonstrating that it generates precisely all ground actions with applicable precondition in all but one benchmark domain, while offering computational benefits by avoiding the grounding phase.

Proof.

When constructing an assignment set, the generic function term is defined as
F(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
with distinct variables in each argument position. However, actual function terms in constraints may reuse variables, e.g.,
F(x,\ldots,x)
. In this case, assignment sets implicitly treat different argument positions as if they were independent, even though in a specific function term, they refer to the same variable. When every function has arity at most two, each function term can involve at most two variables, possibly with repetition. In this case, an assignment set with
d=2
still enumerates all relevant substitutions. Hence, partial evaluation coincides with exact evaluation, the edge removal set
\mathcal{I}^{\Delta}
remains unchanged, and the proof of Theorem

carries over.

Theorem 5
.

The numeric constraint satisfiability problem (NC-SAT) is NP-complete.

Proof.

First, we show membership. Given a numeric constraint
c
with free variables
\mathcal{X}(c)
and a state
\mathcal{S}
, we can nondeterministically guess a substitution
\rho\in\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{X}(c),\mathcal{O})
in polynomial time, since both
\mathcal{X}(c)
and
\mathcal{O}
are finite. Evaluating the ground constraint
c[\rho](\mathcal{S})
is also polynomial, as it requires computing arithmetic expressions followed by a single comparison.

Second, we prove hardness by reduction from 3-SAT. We construct, in polynomial time, a numeric constraint
c
from a 3-SAT formula
\phi
that is satisfiable in a state
\mathcal{S}
if and only if
\phi
is satisfiable.

Consider a propositional formula
\phi
over variables
Y=\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\}
with clauses
Z=\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}\}
where each clause consists of exactly three literals. For the numeric constraint
c
and state
\mathcal{S}
, we define the set of objects
\mathcal{O}=\{o_{\top},o_{\bot}\}
, the set of free variables
\mathcal{X}=\{x_{y}\mid y\in Y\}
, and the set of unary function symbols
\mathcal{F}=\{F_{y}\mid y\in Y\}
. Moreover, we define for each
y
in
Y
, the following ground functions terms with associated values
F_{y}(o_{\top})(\mathcal{S})=1
and
F_{y}(o_{\bot})(\mathcal{S})=0
. We now encode the semantics of the formula
\phi
in the constraint
c
. The construction proceeds by defining numeric gadgets for each component of
\phi
.

•

Literal gadget.
For each literal
l
over a variable
y
in
Y
, we define a literal gadget
G_{l}
that defines a ground function term as

G_{l}:=\begin{cases}F_{y}(x_{y})&\text{ if }l=y\\
1-F_{y}(x_{y})&\text{ if }l=\neg y\end{cases}

•

Clause gadget.
For each clause
z=(l_{1}\lor\ldots\lor l_{3})
, define the function expression

G_{z}:=1-\prod\limits_{i=1}^{3}(1-G_{l_{i}})

•

Formula gadget.
For the formula
\phi
, we define the numeric constraint gadget
G_{\phi}

G_{\phi}:=\left(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}G_{z_{j}}\right)=m

Finally, we show that
c
is satisfiable in a state
\mathcal{S}
if and only if the 3-SAT formula
\phi
is satisfiable.

“
\Leftarrow
”: Consider a truth assignment to the variables
Y
that satisfies the formula
\phi
. Consider the substitution function
\theta
with
\theta(x_{y})=o_{\top}
(resp
o_{\bot}
) if
y
is true (resp. false) in the truth assignment. Next, we show that
c[\theta](\mathcal{S})
is true. For any satisfied literal
l=y
(resp.
l=\neg y
), we have
G_{l}=F_{y}(x_{y})[x_{y}/o_{\top}](\mathcal{S})=1
(resp.
G_{l}=1-F_{y}(x_{y})[x_{y}/o_{\bot}](\mathcal{S})=1-0=1
). Moreover, for an unsatisfied literal
l=y
(resp.
l=\neg y
), we have
G_{l}=F_{y}(x_{y})[x_{y}/o_{\bot}]=0
(resp.
1-F_{y}(x_{y})[x_{y}/o_{\top}]=1-1=0
). More generally speaking, it holds that
G_{l}=1
if and only if
l
is satisfied, and otherwise
G_{l}=0
. Consequently, for each clause
z
, we have
G_{z}=1
because at least one literal in each clause holds, and hence at least one product term
(1-G_{l})=0
such that the whole product yields
0
and
1-0=1
. Finally,
G_{\phi}=m
, showing that the constraint is satisfied in
\mathcal{S}
.

\Rightarrow
Consider a substitution function
\theta
that satisfies the constraint
c
in
\mathcal{S}
. Observe that
G_{l}
is either
0
or
1
because
F_{y}(x_{y})[x_{y}/o_{\top}](\mathcal{S})=1
,
F_{y}(x_{y})[x_{y}/o_{\bot}]=0
, and subtracting
1
or
0
from
1
yields
0
or
1
. Consequently,
G_{z}=1
or
G_{z}=0
because the product of combinations of numbers of
0
and
1
is either
0
or
1
, and similar subtraction argument. Hence, since the constraint is satisfied, every
G_{z}
must be
1
such that the sum over all clauses yields
m
. Consequently, the product in each
G_{z}
must be
0
, which requires at least one of its
G_{l}=1
. By construction,
G_{y}=1
and
G_{\neg y}=0
if
\theta(x_{y})=o_{\top}
. Moreover,
G_{y}=0
and
G_{\neg y}=1
if
\theta(x_{y})=o_{\bot}
. Consequently, we can directly retrieve the truth assignment to the variable
y
, i.e.,
\top
if
\theta(x_{y})=o_{\top}
or
\bot
if
\theta(x_{y})=o_{\bot}
.

Theorem 6
.

The numeric constraint unsatisfiability problem (NC-UNSAT) is coNP-complete.

Proof.

It follows immediately that NC-UNSAT is coNP-complete, since it is the complement of NC-SAT, which is NP-complete.
