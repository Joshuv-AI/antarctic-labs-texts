Stepwise pathways from the olfactory cortex to central hub regions in the human brain
Authors: G. Menelaou, I. Diez, C. Zelano, G. Zhou, J. Persson, J. Sepulcre, J. K. Olofsson
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe human brain is organized as a hierarchical global network. Functional connectivity research reveals that sensory cortices are connected to corresponding association cortices via a series of intermediate nodes linked by synchronous neural activity. These sensory pathways and relay stations converge onto central cortical hubs such as the default‐mode network (DMN). The DMN regions are believed to be critical for representing concepts and, hence, language acquisition and use. Although prior research has established that major senses are placed at a similar distance from the DMN—five to six connective steps—it is still unknown how the olfactory system functionally connects to the large‐scale cortical hubs of the human brain. In this study, we investigated the connective distance from olfactory seed areas to the DMN. The connective distance involves a series of three to four intermediate steps. Furthermore, we parcellated the olfactory cortical subregions and found evidence of two distinct olfactory pathways. One emerges from the anterior olfactory nucleus and olfactory tubercle; it involves early access to the orbitofrontal cortex, known for processing reward and multisensory signals. The other emerges from the frontal and temporal regions of the piriform cortex, involving the anterior insula, intermediate frontal sulcus, and parietal operculum. The results were confirmed in a replication cohort. Our results provide evidence that olfaction has unique early access to the central cortical networks via dual pathways.

INTRODUCTION

How is the human olfactory system integrated with central brain networks? Higher cognitive functions, such as those related to memory, language, and decision‐making, are enabled by functional brain networks that are hierarchically organized; sensory pathways reach, through a series of connections, “convergence zones” of the prefrontal, temporal and parietal lobes (Mesulam, 1998). The convergence zones form hubs, central nodes in highly inter‐connected global networks in the brain (Cole et al., 2013; Uddin et al., 2019). These networks of hubs are believed to handle cognitive functions not limited to any specific sensory format but have more abstract, domain‐general roles, such as understanding concepts or making social inferences (Smallwood et al., 2021; Tong et al., 2022). Network architecture is investigated by applying graph theoretical analysis tools to patterns of cortical co‐activation obtained at rest or during cognitive tasks (Van Den Heuvel & Sporns, 2013). Results from functional connectivity magnetic resonance imaging support the notion of a functional hierarchy embedded in the global network structure of the brain, a hierarchy that ranges from sensory regions to cortical hub regions such as the nodes of the default‐mode network (DMN; Margulies et al., 2016; Smallwood et al., 2021).

Human olfaction is of particular interest in its access to convergence zones because it appears different from higher senses, such as vision and audition, both in terms of cortical organization, phylogenesis, and cognitive capabilities. Olfactory cortices have a more increased resting‐state connectivity with the hippocampus relative to other sensory cortices, which are connected to several association areas before accessing the hippocampus (Bergmann et al., 2016; Van Essen et al., 2001; Zhou et al., 2021). In contrast with other sensory systems, the olfactory system is relatively preserved from earlier mammalian forms and might have retained its relative direct access to central cortical hubs (McGann, 2017; Zhou et al., 2021). On a cognitive level, odors often show results that deviate from those of the audio‐visual senses. When odors are held in working memory, visual stimulus processing is disproportionately inhibited, suggesting odors may occupy most available working memory resources (Hörberg et al., 2020). Lexical priming of odor identity confers a larger performance benefit than observed in the visual system (Pierzchajlo et al., 2024). Attempts at naming common odors that are presented without any other cues typically result in a high level of omissions or inconsistencies. This phenomenon is only observed in olfaction (see Olofsson & Gottfried, 2015, for review). It was proposed that such “olfactory anomia,” the common inability to name odors, is a result of the cortical organization of the olfactory system. While visual object information is elaborately processed in the ventral stream, olfaction enables “early access” to the language network without receiving elaborate prior processing (Olofsson et al., 2013; Olofsson & Gottfried, 2015). Even though olfaction is known to display unique cortical and cognitive characteristics and shows preferential connectivity with the hippocampus, there is yet no systematic attempt to map the pathways from olfactory cortices to cortical hubs and compare the connective distance with other senses. Here, we will test the hypothesis that the olfactory cortex engages cortical hub networks more directly than other major sensory systems.

Our starting point is that in higher senses, such as vision and hearing, inputs are channeled along extensive sensory pathways before reaching integrative brain regions. For example, the ventral stream supports gradually more complex visual object features, mostly along its anterior pathways toward the anterior and medial temporal integrative regions (Van Essen et al., 2001). In contrast, primary olfactory cortices are located in close proximity to multimodal integration brain areas, such as the amygdala, hippocampus, temporal pole, and entorhinal cortex (Lane et al., 2020; Wilson & Mainen, 2006). Mapping the large‐scale connective architecture of the human olfactory system and how it differs from other senses might provide a biological foundation from which its unique cognitive characteristics may be understood.

The notion that olfactory stimuli have “direct access” to association brain regions has not yet been investigated in the context of large‐scale cerebral organization in humans. Recent developments in neuroscience enabled the delineation of a number of distributed cortical networks (Damoiseaux et al., 2006; Fox et al., 2005) whose nodes function as cortical integration “hubs” and receive convergent inputs from multiple systems and modalities (Buckner et al., 2009; Sepulcre et al., 2010). Flexible recruitment of these integrative networks is thought to subserve adaptive behavioral changes in response to environmental stimuli (Dodds et al., 2011; Kelly et al., 2008). A canonical view is that information flows from early unimodal regions to multimodal and cortical hub regions hierarchically (Buckner & Di Nicola, 2019; Margulies et al., 2016; Sepulcre et al., 2012). However, no study has ever profiled this progression in the olfactory domain to uncover potential direct links between early olfactory and cortical hub regions.

In this study, we characterize the progression from early olfactory to higher areas at the functional connectivity level. To this end, we use a stepwise functional connectivity (SFC) approach developed by Sepulcre et al. (2012). The SFC method tracks the patterns of spatial activity correlations that emerge from seed regions (e.g., primary sensory areas) and unfold in a sequence of connectivity steps. In prior studies, SFC revealed patterns of gradual connectivity transitions from early unimodal to multimodal and cortical hub regions across a sequence of connective associations (Pendl et al., 2017; Pretus et al., 2019; Sepulcre et al., 2012). The seed regions of the visual, auditory, and somatosensory modalities converged onto a similar set of hub regions in a similar number of steps; five to six steps are needed for the DMN regions to be linked, and at seven steps, the connective pattern is stable and regarded as having reached its end‐state. We used SFC to quantify the proximity of olfactory areas to cortical hub regions. We assessed the relative proximity of the olfactory domain to hub regions by comparing its stepwise connectivity profile with that of the visual domain, following the method established by Sepulcre et al. (2012). Results from the visual system were presented in the original study and were replicated here only for comparison purposes with the new olfactory system results. The results from somatosensory and auditory cortices are also replicated and presented in Appendix A. As a first step, we examined and compared stepwise connectivity patterns for the visual cortex and the entire olfactory cortex, which, in our approach, consisted of four subregions as defined (Mai et al., 2015). These included the anterior olfactory nucleus (AON), the olfactory tubercle (OT), and the frontally (PirF) and temporally (PirT) projecting subdivisions of the piriform cortex. We then examined patterns of stepwise connectivity for each subregion to examine the meaning of these connectivity patterns in more detail. Following recent work (Zhou et al., 2019) showing different immediate connectivity profiles of these subregions, we investigated whether they supported different pathways toward the central network hubs.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Functional magnetic resonance imaging acquisition

For this study, two 6‐min resting‐state functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) data sets were analyzed (same as in Sepulcre et al., 2012). Both data sets (data set 1; n = 100; mean age = 21.3 years; 37% male; data set 2; n = 100; mean age = 20.8 years, 39% male) were from the Brain Genomics Superstruct Project (Holmes et al., 2015). Participants reported no neurological or psychiatric disease and no history of using psychoactive medications. All participants were right‐handed English speakers and had normal or corrected‐to‐normal vision (see Sepulcre et al., 2012 for details). fMRI data were acquired on a 3T Siemens TIM Trio scanner using a 12‐channel phased‐array head coil (Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany). Functional data were acquired using a gradient‐echo echo‐planar pulse sequence (EPI; TR, 3000 ms, TE, 30 ms, voxel size 3 mm isotropic, 85° flip angle, 216 mm FOV, 47 slices, 124 time points). High‐resolution 3D T1‐weighted structural data were acquired using an MPRAGE sequence (TR, 2200 ms, TE, 1.54 ms for image 1 to 7.01 ms for image 4, flip angle, 7°, voxel size, 1.2 mm isotropic, FOV, 230 mm). Functional images encompassed the whole brain, including the entire cerebellum, while an automated alignment procedure (Van Der Kouwe et al., 2005) was used to align all slices to the anterior commissure/posterior commissure plane. Throughout the functional runs, participants were asked to remain still, stay awake and keep their eyes open. Head motion was restricted using a pillow and foam, while earplugs were provided to attenuate scanner noise.

Preprocessing

MRI data was preprocessed using FMRIB Software Library v6.0.4 (FSL) and MATLAB 2023a. First, the anatomical T1 preprocessing pipeline was performed: reorientation to right‐posterior‐inferior; alignment to anterior and posterior commissures; skull stripping; gray matter, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid segmentation; and computation of nonlinear transformation between individual space T1 and 2 mm MNI152 T1 template. Then, the fMRI preprocessing was performed: slice time correction including removing the first four volumes to allow for T1 equilibrium, compensation of systematic slice‐dependent time shifts, motion correction, normalization to the MNI standard space, and spatial smoothing with an isotropic Gaussian kernel of 8‐mm FWHM. These steps yielded a volumetric time series resampled at 2 mm cubic voxels. Index information of MNI coordinates for each participant was preserved for all connectivity analyses to yield comparable SFC maps within the sample. Temporal (band‐pass) filtering was applied to the MRI data to remove constant offsets and linear trends over each functional run while retaining frequencies below 0.08 Hz (Sepulcre et al., 2012). A linear regression approach was used to remove sources of spurious variance and their temporal derivatives. Those included (1) six parameters obtained from correction for rigid‐body head movement, (2) linear and quadratic terms, (3) five components over the body of the lateral ventricles, and (4) five components over white matter in the centrum semiovale. This process removes sources of variance, such as physiological artifacts, that are not likely to represent signals of neuronal origin (Sepulcre et al., 2012; Van Dijk et al., 2010). As global signal removal results in a shift of the distribution of correlation coefficients and makes the interpretation of negative correlations ambiguous (Chang & Glover, 2009; Sepulcre et al., 2012; Van Dijk et al., 2010), only positive correlations were used for the stepwise. Scrubbing of time points with excess head motion was interpolated in all time points with a Jenkinson frame displacement >0.2 mm. As a final step before the network analysis, we down‐sampled the MRI data to 4 mm isotropic voxels.

Stepwise connectivity

Functional connectivity reveals BOLD activity couplings between a seed region and a remote brain region across time (Friston, 1994; Friston, 2011). SFC uses its main principles to chart connectivity transitions across successive brain regions to profile the distributed set of areas that link a remote and a selected seed region (Pendl et al., 2017; Pretus et al., 2019; Sepulcre et al., 2012). The first step in achieving this is to compute whole‐brain voxel correlations of activity for each participant. To this end, individual BOLD images were converted to N‐by‐M matrices, where N denotes voxels in MNI space, and M is the 120 acquisition time points. A whole brain mask consisting of 17,722 voxels was used to extract the voxel‐level time series. Estimation of Pearson R correlations of resting time series of every voxel to every other voxel yielded 17,722‐by‐17,722 adjacency matrices for each participant. As in Sepulcre et al. (2012), these served as inputs for the SFC analysis. To minimize false associations and perform a stepwise with slow transitions, we only used 1% of the strongest connections in the connectivity matrix. This approach ensures the same sparsity in individual subject connectivity matrices compared to FDR correction that was applied before. Higher percentage of links leads to a rapid transition in the stepwise progression from olfactory cortex to DMN.

The main principles of the SFC approach are analogous to those of graph theory. In graph theory, a graph consists of nodes interlinked by edges. A node can be directly linked via an edge to another node, or two or more intermediate nodes can interpose them. The number of edges connecting two network nodes is defined as path length. Similarly, in SFC, functional connectivity associations may exist across a series of successive voxels (nodes). Two voxels may, therefore, be functionally associated directly or across a series of intermediate voxels. A step is defined as the number of connectivity links (edges) linking two given voxels. Within a voxel network, various voxel pairs can be separated by “link‐step” distances or paths of various lengths, while more than one unique path may exist for a given path length.

In the above framework, the degree of stepwise connectivity of a voxel j for a given link step distance l and a seed region i is the number of all paths that (1) connect voxel j and any voxel in seed area i, and (2) and have an exact length of l. To compute the degree of stepwise connectivity for various link‐step distances between early olfactory and remote brain areas, we created seed regions of 4 mm voxels in each of the four main subdivisions of the primary olfactory cortex in both hemispheres. The spatial coordinates for each olfactory seed were selected based on a recent parcellation of the olfactory cortex (Zhou et al., 2019). To examine possible unique characteristics of pathways linking the olfactory domain with cortical hub regions, we decided to compare its stepwise connectivity with that of the visual domain, as visual, somatosensory, and auditory seed regions gave highly similar results in the original study (Sepulcre et al., 2012), only one of these sensory pathways is reproduced here as a reference point for our olfactory target in the main analyses. The visual cortex was deemed a suitable reference because we have previously theorized that the relative proximity to integrative cortical centers may underlie some cognitive and experiential differences between olfaction and vision (Olofsson et al., 2013; Olofsson & Gottfried, 2015; Zhou et al., 2021). We created rectangular box seed regions in the visual cortex, which consisted of four isotropic 4 mm voxels in each hemisphere. This permitted having the same number of voxels for each sensory seed in both domains to maximize the comparability of connectivity outcomes. The dimensions of each visual seed box were 8 × 4 × 8 mm3, with its 8 × 8 face orientated along the coronal plane. For the olfactory domain, we selected four voxels in each hemisphere that best represented the four main subdivisions of the olfactory cortex (Figure 1). As the olfactory cortical regions are relatively small, we subsequently validated our results using smaller seeds; we used bihemispheric seed regions of two voxels for each olfactory subregion. This allowed us to investigate possible regional variations in stepwise connectivity within the olfactory cortex.

We used the adjacency matrices computed for each participant to estimate the degree of stepwise connectivity between early olfactory areas and every other brain voxel across link‐step distances of various lengths. This analysis yielded a series of SFC brain maps expressing the SFC degree for every brain voxel and any of the voxels within the seed region in the olfactory cortex for each link‐step distance. As the main objective of the study was to investigate the link‐step length of the olfactory cortex to cortical hub regions (as defined in Sepulcre et al., 2012, and following Pendl et al., 2017; Pretus et al., 2019), we measured connectivity up to the stage in which hub regions were reached and considered that an end‐state for the analysis.

Statistical analysis

SFC maps were converted into z‐maps which served as inputs for group‐level voxelwise two‐tailed one‐sample t tests to detect significance. Findings were corrected for multiple comparisons using Monte Carlo simulation clusterwise correction with 10,000 iterations to estimate the probability of false positive clusters with a p value <.05.

Visualization

The Connectome Workbench (CW) software (Marcus et al., 2013) was used to visualize the resulting SFC maps. All maps were projected onto the cerebral hemispheres of the population's average mid‐thickness surface provided with the CW software using the “volume‐to‐surface‐mapping” command. The surface images were presented using a normalized color scale from 1.96 to 4, representing the Z‐statistic of the group comparison (Figures 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and A1).

RESULTS

Stepwise connectivity of the olfactory system

All results are first presented based on data set 1, and they are then complemented by a replication based on data set 2. Results of our SFC analysis show that, as hypothesized, early olfactory areas are more proximate to cortical hub regions than the early sensory regions of other modalities. Previous results on SFC progression gradients suggest that the connectivity of early sensory areas in the visual, auditory, and somatosensory domains reaches the cortical hub regions in five to six connectivity steps. In contrast, the connectivity of early olfactory areas reaches the cortical hub regions in only three to four connectivity steps (Figure 2). More specifically, a connectivity gradient emerges from the olfactory cortex, which initially includes the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) but rapidly encompasses core regions of the DMN such as the posteromedial cortex (PMC), middle temporal cortex (MTC), medial prefrontal cortex (MPFC), and angular gyrus (AG) (Sepulcre et al., 2012; Smallwood et al., 2021).

Stepwise connectivity of olfactory subregions

To investigate the meaning of the above connectivity patterns further, we employed stepwise connectivity analyses for each individual subregion of the olfactory cortex, as their immediate connectivity profiles differ (Zhou et al., 2019). The more anterior regions of the olfactory system (i.e., the AON and the OT; Figures 3 and 4) displayed direct links with the OFC that manifested already in the first step of their connectivity profiles. In this anterior olfactory pathway, connectivity then emerged in a manner similar to that of the olfactory system when analyzed as a whole, including nodes of the DMN (e.g., MPFC, MTC, AG, and PMC). In contrast, the piriform subregions of the olfactory cortex formed a posterior olfactory pathway (Figure 5a,b); its connectivity reached the OFC in three to four steps, and the final connectivity profile included not only the core DMN nodes but also multimodal integration regions, such as the anterior insula and the parietal operculum, as well as the anterior cingulate.

The analyses presented above from data set 1 were then conducted on data set 2 for replication purposes. Results emerging from the olfactory cortices indicate similar results (Figure 6). Taken together, the stepwise connectivity profiles of the four olfactory cortices reveal two key findings. First, they all reached hub regions that encompass the core DMN network (Smallwood et al., 2021) in only three to four steps. Τhis is earlier than what is observed for the visual, auditory, and somatosensory cortices which all display similarly longer path distances (Sepulcre et al., 2012). Second, the four olfactory cortices studied here reveal two different pathways of somewhat different end‐states. The PirT and PirF (posterior pathway) arrived at a state that, apart from the core DMN, also includes the dorsal anterior cingulate and multimodal integration regions such as the operculum and the anterior insula. This is somewhat different for the end‐states for the AON and OT (anterior pathway), whose maps included, apart from the core DMN, also the OFC. The distinction between anterior and posterior pathways highlights the spatial organization of the olfactory cortex, but it should be emphasized that the two connectivity patterns overlap to a large degree.

DISCUSSION

A major question in neuroscience concerns how sensory regions are associated with integrative and transmodal brain regions (Damasio, 1989; Mesulam, 1998; Wilson & Mainen, 2006). An emerging view is that information propagates from early sensory regions to association areas until it is eventually received by distributed network system nodes responsible for higher‐order functions (Margulies et al., 2016; Sepulcre et al., 2012). We used a stepwise connectivity approach to fMRI resting state data to examine how early olfactory areas are successively linked to association areas and converge on the DMN, which is typically regarded as an end‐state at the deepest layer of the global cortical network infrastructure (Smallwood et al., 2021). Compared to findings reported in other sensory systems, we found that all primary olfactory areas are separated by relatively few connectivity steps from cortical hub regions, contrasting with nonolfactory primary sensory areas (Sepulcre et al., 2012). The core DMN includes PMC, MPFC, AG, and MTC—regions that comprised the end‐state of the SFC analyses originating from both olfactory and visual areas but were reached in fewer steps by olfaction. The DMN may act as a connectivity hub with a direct relationship with olfactory cortices, and it was recently suggested that this connective architecture informs an olfactocentric cortical system that developed during brain evolution (Waymel et al., 2020). In our focused approach, we benefited from well‐validated region of interest (ROI) masks to delineate the olfactory cortices while reducing the risk of overlap with nearby nonolfactory regions (Zhou et al., 2019). We successfully replicated our results in an independent participant sample, although we recognize that subsequent validation and improvement of our method and preprocessing choices is necessary using other data sets.

The “early olfactory access” hypothesis to DMN hubs was confirmed in all olfactory subregions. The PMC, MPFC, MTC, AG, as well as the IFG and OFC, have all recently been strongly implicated as cortical sites for the representation of concepts (Fernandino et al., 2022). The unusually early connective access of the olfactory system to these language‐relevant regions provides tentative support for the framework proposed by Olofsson and Gottfried (2015), who suggested such early access as an explanation for why odors are difficult to name. It is unclear how connectivity pathways may relate to olfactory‐behavioral function. The early olfactory access to integrative cognitive centers was recently highlighted in an fMRI experiment where verbal object cues (e.g., “lemon”) were followed by odor or picture object targets that were either expected (e.g., lemon odor or picture) or unexpected (e.g., lavender odor or picture; Pierzchajlo et al., 2024). In this experimental context, the unexpected odors were associated with large behavioral delays relative to the expected odors in a rapid cue‐target matching task. For olfactory targets, prediction error signals from unexpected odors activated the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) to a larger extent than corresponding signals from unexpected pictures. The results supported the notion that early olfactory access to integrative cortical centers is detrimental to odor‐verbal integration unless supporting cues are present (Olofsson & Gottfried, 2015). Notably, the ACC was also observed in the connective end‐state in the current study.

There were notable differences between visual and olfactory cortices, as well as differences among the olfactory cortices, in terms of their end‐state networks. Olfactory cortices projected directly to the OFC, whereas visual seed regions activated associative visual cortices; these sensory‐specific connectivity patterns may reflect recurrent projections to associative sensory areas. More specifically, among the olfactory regions, the AON/OT followed a different anterior pathway compared to the posterior pathway of the PirF/PirT. While all olfactory seed regions converged on the core DMN, these two pathways converged on a different end‐state. The AON/OT anterior pathway was characterized by an earlier and stronger involvement of the OFC relative to the PirT/PirF posterior pathway; the OFC is involved in olfactory valence and reward signals, as well as multisensory integration (Olofsson et al., 2014; Small et al., 2001). Instead, the PirT/PirF end‐state includes the anterior insula and areas of the cingulo‐opercular network; regions thought to be involved in goal‐directed behavioral tasks (Cole et al., 2013). Areas of the latter network in the IFG are considered to be critical for olfactory verbalization and working memory. These outcomes suggest two unique sensory information transformation pathways in the olfactory system, which might hint at a diversity of olfactory cortex engagement across behavioral functions.

Our outcomes align with previous physiological observations of the olfactory system in non‐human animals. Axonal tracing studies in rodents revealed projections originating from the piriform cortex that terminate in regions responsible for high‐level cognitive functions such as cognitive control (prefrontal areas), motivational and emotional evaluation of sensory information (amygdala), and multimodal integration and memory functions (entorhinal and perirhinal cortices) (Chen et al., 2014; Diodato et al., 2016; Johnson et al., 2000; Luskin & Price, 1983; Mazo et al., 2017). The organization of these projections suggests the existence of parallel output circuits that broadcast olfactory information from the piriform cortex to higher brain areas (Chen et al., 2014; Mazo et al., 2017). This organizational principle is unique to the olfactory system as other primary sensory regions typically lack direct projections to higher brain areas and channel information to sensory association centers (Johnson et al., 2000). Moreover, neurons in the piriform cortex of rodents lack columnar organization and are heterogeneous with regards to intra‐piriform projections (Haberly & Price, 1978; Luskin & Price, 1983), which contrasts with the well‐defined, topographically ordered the organization of neurons and intrinsic projections in other primary sensory areas (Detorakis & Rougier, 2012; Garrett et al., 2014; Wang, 2013). These observations led some to suggest that the olfactory cortex might be specialized to perform functions reminiscent of those of sensory association areas (Johnson et al., 2000), readily transforming information arriving from the periphery to signals intelligible to higher brain centers (Wilson & Mainen, 2006). Our findings support this view and further provide a brain‐wide profile of the possible interactions across network systems implicated in this process that compliments the above observations.

The profile of the stepwise connectivity for each subregion suggests a connectivity gradient along the anterior–posterior axis of the olfactory system. Areas in the anterior portion (i.e., the AON and OT) appear to be directly interconnected with the OFC and, through this node, to major cortical hub regions. In contrast, the more posterior part of the olfactory system, which consists of the frontal and temporal piriform cortices, appears to reach the OFC after a series of connective steps. In contrast, the IFG is uniquely accessed by the piriform cortices. Notably, these path differences emerge in an approach of this type, given the relatively small size of the olfactory regions compared to major cortical network systems. Whether the anterior/posterior distinction is theoretically meaningful concerning each subregion's functional characteristics and the olfactory system's internal organization merits further investigation.

The unique connectivity profile between early olfactory and higher brain areas entails important implications regarding the global broadcasting and functional integration of olfactory information across distributed network systems. Odors are known to be suggestive cues of autobiographical memories; memories evoked by odors have a higher emotional intensity and come from an earlier age. However, they are also superior in perceived detail (Chu & Downes, 2002). These features suggest some privileged access to higher cognitive networks (Yeshurun et al., 2009) selectively responsible for weaving olfactory information and context cues onto the fabric of memory. This notion, which is thoroughly replicated in the empirical literature (see, e.g., Larsson et al., 2014, for review), was famously articulated by Marcel Proust when describing how the familiar odor of a madeleine soaked in lime‐flower tea served as a trigger of vivid autobiographical memories and its associated context. Our findings may provide a framework for accounting for these unique associative phenomena at the distributed systems level. The more direct dialogue between primary olfactory areas and hub regions may permit the integration of olfactory information in distributed processing but also subsequent, sensory‐driven reactivation of a representation stored in cortical networks (Mandairon et al., 2014) via avenues inaccessible to cues of other modalities. In particular, interactions with nodes of the DMN, whose functionality has been associated with autobiographical and episodic memory retrieval (Konishi et al., 2015; Spreng et al., 2008), as well as internal stimulus‐independent mentation (Andrews‐Hanna, 2012; Konishi et al., 2015), could drive the engagement of unique cognitive phenomena associated with odors. The nature of this interaction should be further investigated. Resting‐state functional data could be beneficial towards this aim, given the predominant metabolic activation of DMN regions during the resting state (Spetsieris et al., 2015).

Importantly, our findings should be considered in light of potential limitations related to the approach followed to delineate connectivity associations across successive brain regions. Some include the caveats inherent to every functional connectivity approach, such that it is based on correlational data (Van Dijk et al., 2010). Functional connectivity enables the survey of the activity of the entire brain at once (Braga & Buckner, 2017), as opposed to direct recording approaches. However, the physiological meaning of identified functional connectivity associations is often unclear. Functional correlations of activity have been suggested to be constrained by structural connectivity and to index monosynaptic and polysynaptic message exchange (Van Dijk et al., 2010). Still, they could also emerge as a consequence of physiological noise and overlapping spatial smoothness (Sepulcre et al., 2012). Moreover, the identified stepwise connectivity associations pertain to activity during resting conditions. However, task‐related responses can induce context‐dependent divergences between structural and functional connectivity in sensory systems (Park & Friston, 2013). It will therefore be important to evaluate our findings using additional observations of functional connectivity patterns during nonresting conditions, even though, as noted above, some functionality associated with the identified connectivity associations is specific to the resting state. There was no sensory testing carried out as part of the data collection in this study, and the possible influence of varying olfactory acuity is at present unknown. We believe that the participants involved in this data collection were unlikely to have severe olfactory impairments, since such impairments are rare in young, healthy adults (Brämerson et al., 2004; Lee et al., 2013). It will be important to consider our findings in the context of connectivity data sets that also encompass olfactory behavioral data to further consolidate the interpretation of our results. Future applications of our methodology might include assessing olfactory‐system functional connectivity in old age, where odor identification impairment is a precursor of generalized cognitive impairment and dementia (Graves et al., 1999; Olofsson et al., 2020; Stanciu et al., 2014).
