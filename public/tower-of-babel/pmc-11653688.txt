Correlations between frailty index and inflammatory markers derived from blood cell count in the United States
Authors: Shaojie Han, Zenghui Zhang, Siyuan Cheng, Yuchen Han, Qianyun Wang, Yangbo Xi, Panpan Li, Fengjuan Li, Jun Guo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundComplete blood cell count (CBC)-derived inflammatory biomarkers are crucial indicators of adverse outcomes and possess predictive value for a wide range of diseases. The objective of this study was to evaluate the links between CBC-derived inflammatory biomarkers and frailty index.MethodsInformation was gathered from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) spanning the years 1999 to 2016. The research investigated the correlation between inflammatory biomarkers derived from CBC, including the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), systemic inflammatory response index (SIRI), and systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), and Rockwood’s frailty index, using logistic regression analyses.ResultsIn total, the cross-sectional study involved 13,507 U.S. adults aged 50 and older, with 3,729 individuals (24%) meeting the criteria for frailty. After adjusting for confounding factors, elevated levels of logNLR (odds ratio [OR] 3.45, 95% confidence interval [CI] 2.52–4.73), logMLR (OR 3.58, 95% CI 2.44–5.25), logSIRI (OR 2.77, 95% CI 2.17–3.55), and logSII (OR 1.89, 95% CI 1.44–2.48) were significantly associated with an increased frailty index in the entire study population. We also found that frailty index and inflammatory biomarkers derived from CBC have a non-linear relationship by restricted cubic spline.ConclusionsOur findings indicate a correlation between frailty index and NLR, MLR, SIRI, and SII among elderly adults. Considering their wide accessibility in both clinical and laboratory practice, these biomarkers may be recommended as potential indicators for the development of frailty.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20908-9.

Background

Frailty is marked by a deterioration in performance across various physiological systems, coupled with an elevated susceptibility to external stressors [1, 2]. It is linked with heightened rates of mortality, hospitalization, incidents of falls, and placement in long-term care facilities [1, 2]. Individuals with frailty also experience a significant personal burden, which encompasses a diminished quality of life and feelings of isolation [3]. The systematic review that consolidated data from 61,500 older individuals residing in the community, participating in studies conducted in high-income countries, the weighted average estimation for frailty was determined to be 11% [4]. The frailty is projected to surge in conjunction with the rapid growth of the elderly population. Frailty represents an emerging worldwide health challenge, with significant ramifications for both clinical healthcare and public health [5]. Comprehending the pathophysiological underpinnings of frailty is crucial for the formulation of prevention and intervention strategies. Numerous studies have provided evidence that chronic systemic inflammation serves as the fundamental cause of frailty [6–8]. Soysal et al. discovered that frailty and pre-frailty were linked to increased serum levels of IL-6 in comparison to robust individuals, and they were likewise notably linked with increased levels of white blood cells and fibrinogen [9].

Recently, in a variety of medical conditions, prognostic indicators derived from a complete blood cell count (CBC), such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), systemic immune inflammation index (SII), and systemic inflammatory response index (SIRI), have been utilized. [10–12]. These biomarkers are formulated by combining two or three parameters related to lymphocytes, monocytes, platelets, and neutrophils. The NLR, SII, and SIRI exhibit predictive capabilities for severe COVID-19 and the need for invasive mechanical ventilation support among hospitalized Mexican COVID-19 patients [13]. Findings from observational studies have indicated a substantial link between these circulatory inflammatory biomarkers and heightened risks of cardiovascular disease, dementia, and mortality in the elderly population [14–16]. A cross-sectional study, including 345 coronary heart disease patients aged ≥ 60, revealed an association between frailty phenotype, as defined by Fried’s criteria, and NLR in these elderly patients [17]. And another study revealed that elevated NLR levels were correlated with frailty in older adults residing in the community based on Rockwood’s frailty index [18]. Zhang and colleagues additionally observed that individuals with elevated NLR and SII levels faced an increased odds of having frailty phenotype over the course of the longitudinal study based on Fried’s criteria [19].

However, previous studies focused primarily on NLR, with no research yet evaluating the relationship between SIRI and frailty index. Compared to NLR, SIRI reflects both acute and chronic inflammation. Additionally, prior studies were limited to small Chinese samples, leaving uncertainty about their relevance to Americans. While the more common use of Fried’s phenotype addresses the biological cycle of frailty, our study adopts Rockwood’s multidimensional approach to better capture the accumulation of deficits with aging. We used data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) to investigate the relationship between CBC-derived inflammatory biomarkers (NLR, MLR, SIRI, and SII) and frailty index in U.S. adults aged 50 and older.

Materials and Methodology

Study population

Our evaluations relied on cross-sectional data gathered from NHANES, covering the period from 1999 to 2016. NHANES represents a health survey administered by the National Center for Health Statistics, which operates as a division within the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention [20]. We used NHANES data collected over multiple two-year cycles, and biomarker measurements, baseline data, and frailty assessments were typically gathered in the year participants entered the survey. For example, a participant enrolled in 2001 would have all relevant data collected during that year. The primary goal is to assess the health and nutritional status of the non-institutionalized civilian population in the United States, providing a comprehensive overview that is representative of the entire country. NHANES obtained ethical approval from the National Center for Health Statistics, and all participants in the study provided written informed consent. This study conforms to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Assessment of CBC-derived inflammatory biomarkers

Automated hematology analyzers are employed to measure the complete blood count, quantifying the quantity of various blood cell types (including red blood cells, white blood cells, and platelets) within a specified blood volume. We computed the SIRI, SII, NLR, and MLR using the following formulas: SIRI = (neutrophil counts × monocyte counts)/lymphocyte counts, SII = (platelet counts × neutrophil counts)/lymphocyte counts, NLR = neutrophil counts/lymphocyte counts, and MLR = monocyte counts/lymphocyte counts [12].

Frailty assessment

We used a modified frailty index based on the Rockwood frailty index, excluding ten items from the original 46 that were related to intake or nutritional status (as shown in Supplemental Table S1). This index has been validated in the NHANES cohort and applied in many previous studies [21–23]. If the patient meets a criterion, it is scored as 1 point, if not, it is scored as 0 points, and the points are added together. In our study, we used frailty index equal to 0.3 as the cutoff point to divide the study population into two groups, the fit group (frailty index ≤ 0.3)and the frail group (frailty index > 0.3).

Covariate assessment

Baseline data about the participants were obtained via questionnaires and laboratory assessment. Body mass index (BMI) was categorized into three groups: healthy weight (BMI ≤ 25.0 kg/m2), overweight (25.0 kg/m2 ≤ BMI < 30.0 kg/m2), and obese (BMI ≥ 30.0 kg/m2). The race variable was segmented into five subgroups, including non-Hispanic White, Mexican American, other Hispanic, non-Hispanic Black, and other racial backgrounds. Smoking status was dichotomized into two groups: nonsmokers were individuals who had never consumed a minimum of 100 cigarettes in their lifetime, while smokers were those who had smoked at least 100 cigarettes. Drinking status was categorized into two groups: non-drinkers were characterized as individuals who had not consumed a minimum of 12 alcoholic beverages in the past year or throughout their lifetime, while drinkers were those who had imbibed at least 12 drinks in the past year. Physical exercise was categorized into three groups: "low," "moderate," and "active." Other covariates include age, sex, family poverty income ratio (PIR), and educational level (divided into less than high school, high school graduate, college or higher). The total energy intake was determined by computing the mean of two days. Data concerning hypertension and diabetes were obtained through self-reported questionnaires.

Statistical analysis

The medians of continuous variables (interquartile range [IQR]) are displayed. The numerical values (percentages) for categorical variables are displayed. The Mann–Whitney U test (non-normal distribution) or the t-test (normal distribution) will be used to compare continuous variables. The chi-square test was utilized to compare categorical variables. Based on the quartiles (Q), we also separated the NLR, MLR, SIRI, and SII into four groups. Q1: 1.53, Q2: > 1.53, ≤ 2.08, Q3: > 2.08, ≤ 2.83 and Q4: > 2.83 for NLR (Supplemental Table S2). According to Supplemental Table S3, Q1: ≤ 0.22; Q2: > 0.22, ≤ 0.29; Q3: > 0.29, ≤ 0.38; and Q4: > 0.38 for MLR. According to Supplemental Table S4, SIRI: Q1: < 0.76; Q2: > 0.76, ≤ 1.13; Q3: > 0.76, ≤ 1.68; Q4: > 1.68. According to Supplemental Table S5, for SII, Q1 is < 339.58, Q2 is > 339.58, ≤ 481.85, Q3 is > 481.85 ≤ 689.61, and Q4 is > 689.61. The research population's various features between the NLR, MLR, SIRI, and SII quartiles were then discussed. The adjusted odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) of the relationship between CBC-derived inflammatory biomarkers and frailty index were determined using a multiple logistic regression model. In the restricted cubic spline regression analysis, we tested the non-linear relationship by comparing a linear model with a model that included non-linear (spline) terms. A p-value was calculated to assess non-linear relationship and a p-value less than 0.05 indicated that the non-linear relationship was statistically significant. Due to their nonnormal distribution, the NLR, SIRI, SII, and MLR, were log-transformed before being used in the analyses. All analyses were conducted in two models. Model 1: adjusted for age, sex, NHANES cycle, and race. Model 2: redesigned to account for Model 1 and include variables such as education level, family poverty income ratio, BMI, physical activity, total energy intake, smoking or drinking status, diabetes, and hypertension. To understand how different factors might affect the results, we carried out sensitivity analysis. To explore how various factors might influence the results, we conducted sensitivity analysis using logistic and restricted cubic spline regression, adjusting for angina/angina pectoris, heart attack, coronary heart disease, stroke, thyroid condition, cancer, arthritis, weak/failing kidneys, pulse rate, pulse pressure, platelet count, blood urea nitrogen, bicarbonate, red cell distribution width, lactate dehydrogenase, and uric acid. Statistical significance was defined as p value of two-tailed < 0.05. Each analysis was completed with R (version 4.3).

Results

Characteristics of study participants

During the period spanning 1999 to 2016, 92,062 people were involved in the NHANES. A total of 70,392 participants were omitted because they were younger than 50 years old. Subsequently, a total of 2,508 participants did not possess adequate information regarding their complete blood cell data. In addition, among the remaining participants, 5503 individuals had insufficient data for frailty assessment, and 152 people had missing covariate information. Finally, 13,507 patients were included in the analysis (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Flowchart of the study

The study cohort had a median age of 66 years [IQR 61, 74] with a maximum age of 85 years, was 46% male, and predominantly consisted of non-Hispanic white individuals (79%). In comparison to fit (FI ≤ 0.3) group, those who were frail were more likely to be elderly females, have lower levels of education, be current smokers, abstain from alcohol, exhibit physical inactivity, possess a higher BMI, and have diabetes and hypertension (p < 0.05). In frail patients, the counts of white blood cells, neutrophils, monocytes, and lymphocytes were notably higher (P < 0.05). CBC-derived inflammatory biomarkers and frailty index

Table 2 presents the correlation between the NLR, MLR, SIRI, and SII and frailty index. After controlling for covariates, a significant correlation was seen between frailty index and logNLR (OR 3.45, 95% CI 2.52–4.73), logMLR (OR 3.58, 95% CI 2.44–5.25), logSIRI (OR 2.77, 95% CI 2.17–3.55), and logSII (OR 1.89, 95% CI 1.44–2.48) in all people. All inflammatory indicators derived from CBC were categorized into four groups and analyzed for their connection with frailty index. Positive relationships between the increase in frailty index and CBC-derived markers (NLR, MLR, SIRI, and SII) were found by using model 1. Our conclusion is further demonstrated in model 2. As shown in Table 3, the OR and 95% CI for the highest quartile compared to the lowest quartile after adjusting for potential confounding variables were NLR (OR 1.73 95%CI 1.45–2.06, P trend < 0.001), MLR (OR 1.61 95%CI 1.36–1.91, P trend < 0.001), SIRI (OR1.72 95%CI 1.43–2.06, P trend < 0.001), and SII (OR 1.31 95%CI 1.09–1.57, P trend < 0.001), (Table 3). We observed a nonlinear correlation between frailty index and markers of inflammation from CBC by restricted cubic spline (Fig. 2). Discussion

Principal findings

We carried out a cross-sectional investigation involving 13,507 adults aged over 50 to explore the relationship between frailty index and inflammatory biomarkers derived from CBC in the United States. Our primary discoveries revealed a strong correlation between elevated NLR, SII, MLR, and SIRI and an augmented odd of having frailty based on Rockwood’s frailty index. Furthermore, given the widespread availability and ease of measurement of NLR, SIRI, SII, and MLR, they may be proposed as potential indicators for an increase in the frailty index.

Currently available research

Until now, numerous investigations have explored the connection between frailty index and inflammatory biomarkers derived from CBC [17–19, 24, 25]. Zhang et al. discovered a significant association between elevated logNLR (OR 2.92, p < 0.001), logPLR (OR 2.54, p < 0.001), and logSII (OR 2.34, p < 0.001) and an increased odd of frailty in all participants after adjusting for covariates based on the Fried phenotype [19]. Furthermore, prior investigations have established associations between inflammatory markers in blood cells and frailty index within specific populations. Guan et al. reported that in elderly inpatients with comorbidities, white blood cell, NLR and neutrophil count showed significant positive correlation with frailty index, while the lymphocytes exhibited a negative correlation when assessed using the 5-item FRAIL scale [26]. However, this study only involved 297 individuals and was conducted at a single site. A Chinese study concluded that frail elderly cancer patients with NLR ≥ 3 had the lowest overall survival, linking heightened systemic inflammation with reduced survival based on the FRAIL scale [25]. Hou et al. conducted a research to assess the relationship between NLR and frailty index in a cohort of 345 elderly individuals with coronary heart disease based on the Fried phenotype [17]. Patients in the fourth quartile of NLR and RDW exhibited fully-adjusted OR for the development of frailty of 2.89 (p = 0.011) and 2.49 (p = 0.040) [17].

Xu et al. observed that an elevated NLR was linked to greater odds of both prevalent and incident frailty in a longitudinal cohort study comprising over 1800 older adults residing in the community based on Rockwood’s frailty index [18]. Nishijima and their team conducted a cross-sectional study to explore the relationship between NLR and frailty, based on Rockwood’s frailty index, in older cancer patients [24]. They found that those in the highest tertile NLR group had almost four times higher chances of being frail in elderly cancer patients [24]. In short, they indicated a possible association between CBC-derived inflammatory indicators and frailty index. However, the small sample size and disease-specific research populations in the aforementioned studies hampered the generalizability of the study findings. In the above studies, with the exception of Nishijima et al., the participants in the other investigations were of Chinese ethnicity, which restricts the generalizability of the findings. Moreover, the aforementioned studies did not investigate the relationship between SIRI and frailty. Most of these studies used Fried's criteria to assess frailty, with only two studies utilizing Rockwood's frailty index [18, 24]. Our study complements the above studies by including a cross-sectional study from a national population in the United States based on Rockwood’s frailty index. Our study is the first to report a close association between SIRI and frailty, in addition to exploring traditional markers such as NLR, MLR, and SII. In addition, our study found a nonlinear relationship between inflammatory markers derived from CBC and frailty index through restricted cubic spline.

In addition to frailty, there is a substantial body of evidence investigating the link between inflammatory biomarkers derived from CBC and sarcopenia, a prevalent geriatric syndrome closely associated with frailty [19, 27, 28]. To investigate these correlations, Zhao et al. performed cross-sectional research with 4,224 people in China. They discovered that increased NLR, PLR and SII were all associated with a heightened risk of developing sarcopenia [28]. Figure 3 demonstrates that the relationship between inflammatory markers and frailty index is more pronounced in specific subgroups, including older participants and females. This suggests that these groups may be more vulnerable to the effects of systemic inflammation, contributing to their increased frailty index. As individuals age, the body's immune system may gradually weaken, leading to enhanced inflammatory responses [2]. Older adults may exhibit greater vulnerability when dealing with chronic inflammation, which can affect their physical function and quality of life [29]. In women, elevated inflammatory markers were associated with frailty index [30]. Women may be more sensitive to inflammatory responses in certain physiological and psychological aspects [30]. After identifying high-risk groups (such as older adults and women), clinicians can take preventive measures, such as improving diet and managing inflammation, to reduce the risk of developing frailty.

The possible mechanisms

It has been suggested that NLR, SII, MLR, and SIRI are indicators of inflammation. The exact mechanisms underlying the relationship between CBC-derived inflammatory biomarkers (NLR, MLR, SIRI, and SII) and frailty index remain largely unclear. There is a broad consensus that chronic low-grade inflammation may play a pivotal role in the advancement of frailty [8]. One such possible pathophysiological alteration that may be intimately associated with frailty index is inflammation [31]. Research has indicated that elevated inflammatory marker levels in the bloodstream are correlated with increased muscle mass and strength loss, reduced physical activity, accelerated declines in mobility, and higher rates of depression among older individuals [32, 33], all of which are fundamental components used in the literature to define frailty index according to prevailing criteria [34]. Many theories have been proposed to clarify how inflammation, sarcopenia, and frailty are related [8]. Prior studies have demonstrated a link between inflammation and reduced synthesis and function of insulin-like growth factor I (IGF1) which is pivotal in muscle regeneration and the preservation of muscle integrity [35]. Furthermore, there is evidence indicating that IL-1α and IL-6 can inhibit IGF1-mediated anabolism, while TNF inhibitors have the potential to reduce the production of both IGF1 and IGF-binding protein in vitro [36]. Inflammation hinders endothelial reactivity and muscle perfusion, disrupting the absorption of long branched-chain amino acids crucial for muscle energy and protein synthesis [8, 37]. Additionally, researches have indicated a substantial correlation between NLR and IL-6, CRP, and TNF-α [18, 38]. NLR, PLR, and SII are indicative of the equilibrium between the innate and adaptive immune systems [39, 40]. Age-related alterations in the immune system may contribute to heightened levels of low-grade, chronic systemic inflammation [19].

In this study, we also recognize various constraints. First, the cross-sectional design of this study introduces limitations, so the interpretation of the results should be approached with caution. Future longitudinal prospective studies are needed to more thoroughly confirm our findings. Second, the NHANES results relied on participants self-reports, potentially introducing recall bias. Third, blood cells were measured only once in the samples we included, and it may be necessary to repeat the measurements multiple times to more accurately reflect changes in inflammatory biomarkers. Fourthly, our study was limited to utilizing the frailty index to define the frailty status. It is advisable that future investigations employ the Fried frailty phenotype to better explore the connection between inflammatory biomarkers and frailty. Fifth, we categorize drinking status based on less or more than 12 drinks in the past year, which seems too crude and may lead to bias in the results when adjusting for covariates. Finally, NHANES serves as a nationally representative survey, but the applicability of these findings to other countries may be constrained. Consequently, additional research is warranted to elucidate the exact relationship among these variables.

Conclusion

In summary, we discovered that elevated NLR, MLR, SIRI, and SII levels were linked to a higher frailty index in elderly individuals in this nationwide research. These results add to the evidence regarding the possible effectiveness of these biomarkers in forecasting frailty. Furthermore, given that NLR, MLR, SII, and SIRI are widely employed in practice and are easily obtainable through laboratory assessments, they could be suggested as cost-efficient prognosticators for frailty incidents.
