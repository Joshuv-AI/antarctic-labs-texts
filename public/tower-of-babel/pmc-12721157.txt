Synthesis and applications of silver nanowires
Authors: Jie Xu, Hongtao Gu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryIn the era of artificial intelligence, emerging fields such as smart windows, flexible sensors, human-computer interaction systems, and new energy technologies have exerted a profound influence on various aspects of our daily lives. The development of these fields heavily relies on conductive materials. Consequently, the demand for conductive electrodes has experienced exponential growth. Indium tin oxide (ITO) has traditionally been the most widely adopted conductive material. However, its limitations—such as inherent brittleness, toxicity, and the scarcity of indium—hinder its suitability for use in flexible electronic devices. In this context, silver nanowires (AgNWs) have emerged as one of the most promising alternatives among various candidates, owing to their excellent electrical conductivity, high optical transparency, superior mechanical flexibility, and compatibility with diverse substrates. This review summarizes different AgNW synthesis methods, the fabrication of AgNW-based flexible transparent conductive electrodes, and introduces their applications in areas like flexible touch screen, flexible sensors, energy storage, electromagnetic interference shielding.

Introduction

With the relentless progress of electronic technology, the global shift toward flexibility, portability, and sustainability in electronic devices has become irreversible. Wearable electronic devices (e.g., smartwatches, fitness trackers, etc.), electronic skin for medical robotics, foldable displays, stretchable solar cells, and human-computer interaction systems are no longer niche innovations—they are rapidly penetrating consumer markets and industrial sectors, driven by a surge in demand for devices that adapt to human-centric design and complex application scenarios.1,2,3,4 For instance, the global flexible display market is projected to grow from $28.6 billion in 2023 to over $80 billion by 2030 (Grand View Research), while the wearable electronics market is expected to reach $214.6 billion by 2028 (Statista). At the core of these devices lies a critical component: conductive electrodes—materials that enable efficient electron transport while meeting strict requirements for transparency and mechanical durability.

Traditionally, indium tin oxide (ITO) has dominated the conductive electrode market, owing to its high optical transmittance (∼90% in the visible spectrum) and low sheet resistance (∼10 Ω/sq).5,6,7 However, three inherent limitations of ITO have increasingly become bottlenecks for the advancement of next-generation electronics, creating a pressing market need for alternatives:(1) resource scarcity and cost volatility of indium; (2) inherent brittleness limits flexible and stretchable applications; and (3) toxicity risks for environmental and biomedical applications.8,9

In response to these limitations, the research and industrial communities have raced to develop alternative conductive materials. In recent years, graphene,10,11,12,13 carbon nanotubes,14,15,16 metal nanomaterials,17,18,19 and conductive polymer materials20,21,22,23 have been explored as potential replacements for ITO. However, each of these candidates faces its own challenges: graphene and carbon nanotubes require complex synthesis processes to achieve high conductivity and large-area uniformity, conductive polymers suffer from poor mechanical stability and low conductivity under wet conditions, and most metal nanoparticles fail to balance transparency and flexibility.

Against this backdrop, silver nanowires (AgNWs) have emerged as one of the most promising alternatives, uniquely addressing the gaps left by ITO and other candidates. AgNWs combine the exceptional electrical conductivity of silver (the highest among all metals, ∼6.3 × 107 S/m) with the size-dependent advantages of nanowires—including high optical transmittance and superior mechanical flexibility due to their one-dimensional structure, which dissipates stress during deformation.24,25,26 Unlike ITO, AgNWs form a percolation network that retains conductivity even after 10,000+ bending cycles (bending radius = 1 mm); they also avoid the resource scarcity of indium and exhibit lower toxicity.27,28

Beyond electrodes for displays and wearables, AgNWs also solve critical challenges in adjacent high-growth markets. For example, most active materials for energy storage (e.g., lithium-rich manganese oxides in batteries, activated carbon in supercapacitors) have low electrical conductivity and poor flexibility. When coated onto traditional rigid electrodes, these materials suffer from limited electron transport and delamination under deformation.29,30 By dispersing AgNWs within these active materials, researchers have created flexible energy storage devices with enhanced electron transport and mechanical reinforcement.

This review focuses on literature published within the past five years, constructing a multi-dimensional analysis framework spanning AgNW synthesis, electrode fabrication, and applications. By first contextualizing the market and technical drivers for replacing ITO, we aim to highlight why AgNWs have become a focal point of research. The review is structured to (1) systematically organize the research background and development trajectory of AgNWs; (2) detail the latest advancements in AgNW synthesis and large-scale preparation; (3) analyze the fabrication of AgNW-based conductive electrodes and their application scenarios; and (4) showcase AgNWs’ performance in critical markets. Ultimately, this work identifies current challenges and future opportunities to accelerate AgNWs from laboratory-scale innovation to industrial adoption—filling the gap left by ITO in the era of flexible, sustainable electronics.

Synthesis of AgNWs

There are several common methods for preparing AgNWs, including chemical reduction methods, template methods, and electrochemical synthesis method.

Polyol method

The polyol method is a frequently employed chemical technique for the synthesis of AgNWs.31,32 Initially, polyols, such as ethylene glycol, exhibit reducing capabilities under specific temperature conditions. They are capable of progressively reducing silver ions (Ag+) within silver salts to silver atoms (Ag). In the presence of crystal seeds such as platinum particles in the reaction system, silver atoms tend to deposit preferentially on the surface of these seeds. This serves as the origin for the subsequent growth of AgNWs, known as the nucleation process. As the reaction advances, the continuously generated silver atoms will keep depositing and growing on the pre-existing crystal seeds or the already formed silver nanostructures. Figure 1A shows us the morphology of AgNWs at different reaction stages.33 Capping agents, exemplified by polyvinyl pyrrolidone (PVP), play a significant role in this procedure. PVP molecules can selectively adhere to particular crystal planes of silver crystals. Through steric hindrance and other mechanisms, they can suppress the growth of certain crystal planes, thereby facilitating the growth of silver crystals in specific directions. This is favorable for the formation of nanowire shapes and enables anisotropic growth.34 The varieties and concentrations of capping agents are of vital importance to the quality of AgNW synthesis. Different capping agents possess distinct regulatory effects on the growth of silver crystals due to their dissimilar chemical structures and properties. Zhu et al. found that as the molecular weight of PVP gradually increases, both the yield and purity of AgNWs are improved (Figures 1B–1G).35 For instance, with PVP, its molecular weight and concentration within the reaction system will influence the ultimate morphology of AgNWs. When the molar ratio of PVP to silver nitrate (AgNO3) is relatively high, the stronger adsorption of PVP molecules on the surface of silver crystals gives rise to steric hindrance and other effects, resulting in a relatively smaller diameter of the synthesized AgNWs.36,37 Controlling agents such as FeCl3,38,39 CuCl2,40 NaCl,41 or NaBr42 also have an impact on the reaction process. Zhou et al. found that the coordination of Cl−and Br−significantly increases the activation energy of the reaction and plays a key role in the nucleation and growth of AgNWs (Figures 1H–1J).43 By optimizing the concentration ratio of Cl−and Br−, high-quality AgNWs with an average length of 35.7 μm and an average diameter of 67 nm were synthesized.43 Taking FeCl3 as an example, it can interact with silver ions and other components in the solution, modifying the state and reactivity of silver ions. In turn, it affects the growth rate and final morphology of AgNWs. For example, it may form complexes with silver ions, rendering the reduction process of silver ions more intricate and controllable. Solvothermal method

Solvothermal synthesis is a technique for conducting chemical reactions under high-temperature and high-pressure conditions in a closed container (such as an autoclave), using organic solvents or non-aqueous solvents as the medium. The solvothermal method has the advantages of high purity of products, strong controllability of morphology, and a wide range of applications.45,46 Qian’s group first synthesized AgNWs with uniform morphology by the solvothermal method. This method does not require the addition of other complex reducing agents, so it will not introduce other impure elements.47

Yang et al. developed a one-pot hydrothermal method that does not require external templates or seeds, using only AgNO3 and sodium citrate to synthesize AgNWs (showed in Figures 2A and 2B).48 By investigating the effects of the molar ratio of silver ions to citrate, pH value, reaction temperature, and light irradiation, high-yield nanowires (with an average diameter of 53 ± 4 nm and a length up to 6 μm) were obtained under the optimized conditions: a 1:1 M ratio of Ag+ to citrate, pH 7.1, and 130°C. The crystallinity and the growth direction of AgNW (perpendicular to the 111 plane) is shown in Figure 2C. The microwave-assisted method uses microwaves as the heat source, enabling the reaction to proceed more rapidly.51,52 The raw materials employed herein are essentially identical to those utilized in the polyol method. However, the reaction vessel differs as it is a specially fabricated glass or polytetrafluoroethylene container that can endure microwave radiation. Subsequently, heating is carried out in a dedicated laboratory microwave oven, with a microwave frequency generally around 2.45 GHz. The reaction progress is regulated by adjusting the power and radiation duration of the microwave oven. The power can be adjusted within the range of 300–800 W, while the radiation time spans from a few minutes to several tens of minutes, contingent upon the specific reaction system and the dimensional requisites of the target AgNWs.53,54 Manuel et al. demonstrated that a higher yield of AgNWs can be obtained under the conditions of 800 W power, 40% filling capacity, and 5-min reaction time by the microwave-assisted method (showed in Figures 2G–2I).50

Template method

Zero-dimensional ions and nanoparticles can grow into one-dimensional nanowires through templates. There are mainly two types of templates: hard templates and soft templates. Hard templates can shape silver atoms into nanowires due to their pre-designed structures, thereby enabling better control over the shape, size, and overall morphology of the nanowires. The hard template method is simple and effective, but during the template removal process, a large amount of the generated nanowires may be lost, resulting in a reduction in yield. The soft template method uses chemical substances that can be dissolved in solvents as templates, and the yield is relatively higher.

The hard template, exemplified by the anodic aluminum oxide (AAO) template, exhibits a fixed shape and size with a highly regular nanopore structure, whose pore diameter can be precisely adjusted within a specific range.55,56 Figure 3A shows the schematic representation of the procedure for fabrication of metallic nanowire arrays and Figure 3B shows the SEM image of the cross section of AgNWs in the AAO template. During the synthesis of AgNWs, silver atoms grow under the spatial confinement of the template pores. This enables the accurate control of various dimensional parameters such as the diameter and length of the AgNWs, leading to the successful production of AgNWs with a uniform diameter and a controllable aspect ratio. Usually, AgNO3 is utilized as the silver source for preparing the precursor solution, which is then filled into the template pores via methods like impregnation and vacuum assistance. The reaction is carried out under appropriate temperature and reaction time conditions, during which silver ions are reduced within the pores and gradually form nanowires. Once the AgNWs are generated, the template is removed by etching with a sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution. It is of utmost importance to meticulously control the reaction time and NaOH concentration to avoid any damage to the AgNWs. Finally, the AgNWs after template removal need to be cleaned to get rid of residual template fragments, reaction reagents, and other contaminants, ensuring the purity and quality of the obtained AgNWs.57 Soft template method generally adopts a chain-like macromolecular structure, and its self-assembled local structure can guide silver nuclei and nanowires to form ordered one-dimensional structures. Commonly used soft templates include PVP58 and organic salts.59,60 Jo et al. successfully synthesized AgNWs with a diameter of 20 nm and an aspect ratio of 1,000 using 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium tetrachloroferrate (bmim [FeCl4]) as a soft template. Fabrication of silver conductive electrodes

Currently, there are various methods for preparing flexible transparent conductive electrodes. Among them, roll-to-roll (RTR) coating, spraying, spin coating, and dip coating are the mainstream preparation methods.

RTR coating

The RTR coating approach represents an efficient and highly suitable technique for large-scale manufacturing of AgNWs conductive films.61,62 Initially, a premium-quality AgNWs dispersion slurry must be formulated. This typically entails uniformly dispersing the synthesized AgNWs within an appropriate solvent system, be it an organic solvent or an aqueous system incorporated with specific additives (such as dispersing agents, binding agents), thereby ensuring that the AgNWs can be evenly distributed and firmly adhered to the substrate during the ensuing coating operation. Among the commonly employed coating techniques are blade coating and slot coating. In blade coating, the coating thickness of the AgNW slurry is regulated by adjusting the clearance between the blade and the substrate as well as the angle of the blade. In contrast, slot coating utilizes precisely controlled slots to uniformly extrude the slurry and apply it onto the substrate. Subsequently, the AgNWs coating post-coating necessitates a series of processes including drying, curing, sintering, and finally surface treatment to enhance its weather resistance.63,64,65 Wang et al. used a continuous RTR slot die coater and, by controlling the flow rate of the AgNWs ink in the slot, prepared AgNWs transparent conductive films (TCFs) with different sheet resistances (30–70 ohms/square), optical transmittances (89%–90%), and haze values (0.5%–1%).66 The entire coating process consists of three steps: slot die coating, heating, and UV treatment (Showed in Figure 4A). Kang et al. develops an RTR silver electroplating process for metal nanowire electrodes under constant current mode.67 In this process, metal nanowire networks coated on polyethylene terephthalate (PET) films are immersed in an electroplating bath containing AgNO3. A constant current is applied through an external circuit to deposit solvated Ag+ on the surface of the nanowires. By controlling the current density and electroplating time, the sheet resistance and optical transmittance of the metal nanowire network can be precisely regulated. The optimized silver-electroplated AgNWs film exhibits a sheet resistance of approximately 19 Ω/sq at an optical transmittance of 90% (550 nm). RTR coating is the gold standard for industrial-scale production. Flexible substrates (such as PET) are continuously fed through coating stations (slot-die, gravure, or Meyer rod) to apply AgNW dispersions, followed by drying and post-treatment. It enables high throughput (up to 100 m/min) and large-area fabrication. However, tension control is a key challenge: substrate stretching or wrinkling caused by uneven roll tension leads to coating defects like necking or bagging. For example, a 1% tension variation can reduce transmittance uniformity by 15% in flexible displays. Additionally, AgNW dispersions must have low viscosity (<100 cP) to flow through RTR dies, but this increases the risk of sedimentation during storage, requiring constant agitation that may damage nanowire structures. Integrating high-temperature annealing (100°C–150°C) to remove capping agents like PVP into a continuous RTR line also risks substrate deformation.

Spray coating

The spray coating method represents a relatively straightforward and uncomplicated means of fabricating AgNWs conductive films.68,69 It does not necessitate elaborate equipment and intricate procedures, thereby being easily manageable and highly appropriate for laboratory investigations and small-scale manufacturing. In comparison with alternative methods, both the equipment expenditure and the cost of raw materials are substantially lower, which significantly contributes to the reduction of production costs. Through the modulation of spraying parameters and the utilization of appropriate equipment, the production of large-area conductive films can be accomplished, fulfilling the application requisites of diverse sizes and configurations and rendering it suitable for large-scale industrial production. By precisely governing the number of spraying iterations, the solution concentration and the spraying parameters, the thickness of the conductive film can be accurately controlled, thereby enabling the fine-tuning of both the conductive and optical characteristics.70,71

However, it faces critical challenges in uniformity: droplet size (5–50 μm) and spray pressure (0.1–0.5 MPa) must be precisely adjusted to avoid pinholing or overlapping. Even minor nozzle clogging caused by aggregated AgNWs can create streaks, reducing transmittance by 5%–10% in affected areas. Additionally, material efficiency is low—only 30%–50% of the sprayed dispersion adheres to the substrate, with the rest lost as overspray, increasing silver consumption and requiring costly recovery systems. Moreover, aerosolized AgNWs pose inhalation hazards, necessitating strict ventilation and protective equipment, which adds to operational costs.

In the process of fabricating AgNW conductive films via spin coating, the flatness and surface cleanliness of the substrate play a vital role.74,75,76 Prior to spin coating, it is essential to subject the substrate to a rigorous cleaning regimen. For instance, ultrasonic cleaning techniques are employed to eliminate impurities like dust and oil residues on the surface. Subsequently, a drying process is carried out. If deemed necessary, surface modification procedures can be implemented on the substrate, such as plasma treatment, with the aim of augmenting the wettability and adhesion between the substrate and the AgNW solution.77 The meticulously prepared AgNWs solution is then gently dispensed onto the center of the substrate. The volume of the dispensed solution is determined comprehensively, taking into account various factors including the area of the substrate, the desired film thickness, and the concentration of the solution. Typically, an amount ranging from 0.1 to 1 mL of the solution is dropped per square centimeter of the substrate. The spin coating speed is a critical parameter, and it generally falls within the range of 500–5,000 rpm.78,79 A relatively low speed might result in an uneven coating of the AgNWs solution on the substrate, giving rise to a thicker and coarser film layer. Conversely, an excessively high speed could cause the AgNWs to be overly dispersed under the influence of centrifugal force, thereby making it arduous to form a continuous conductive network. Additionally, it might also lead to the loss of solution due to splashing. The spin coating time usually spans from 30 to 600 s and requires adjustment based on the viscosity of the solution, the spin speed, and the target film thickness.80,81 This is to ensure that the solution spreads uniformly across the substrate and forms a wet film with a suitable thickness.

However, the core challenges are poor scalability-substrate size is limited by the spinner chuck (usually <30 cm), and edge beading is prone to occur at the edges; the material waste rate exceeds 90%, significantly increasing costs; meanwhile, flexible substrates are easily wrinkled or torn under high centrifugal force, restricting its application in flexible electronics.

Dip coating

The dip coating method for preparing AgNWs conductive films capitalizes on the dispersibility of AgNWs within specific solutions and their interaction with the substrate to accomplish the production of conductive films.84,85 When the substrate is submerged in the dispersion liquid containing AgNWs, the AgNWs adhere to the substrate surface. As the substrate is withdrawn from the solution at a specific speed, under the influence of factors like surface tension and solution viscosity, the AgNWs form a relatively uniform distribution on the substrate, consequently constructing a conductive network and giving rise to a conductive film.86,87 Notably, relatively stringent requirements are imposed on the uniformity of the AgNWs dispersion liquid, as well as the cleanliness and flatness of the substrate material. Although the dip coating method for fabricating AgNWs conductive films offers the benefits of relatively straightforward operation and controllable costs, the control of process parameters proves to be rather challenging.

Researchers fabricated two types of flexible strain sensors with aligned AgNWs via a dip-coating process: longitudinal sensors and transverse sensors.88 The longitudinal sensor exhibits a significantly higher sensitivity (GF = 89.99) under 25% stretching compared to the transverse sensor (GF = 12.71 at 25% stretching), being 7.08 times that of the latter. However, its strain range is narrower (<25%). In contrast, the transverse sensor has a strain range of up to 60%, which meets the requirements of wearable devices (ε > 50%). Although the dip coating method for preparing AgNW conductive films has advantages such as relatively simple operation and controllable cost, the performance of the conductive films is not very stable. Therefore, the dip coating method is more widely used in the preparation of electrode materials.91,92 Xu at el. constructed a 3D dual-conductive network composite foam (MF@MA) composed of 2D MXene and 1D AgNWs on melamine foam (MF) through vacuum-assisted dip-coating technology.89 This composite foam has the characteristics of high conductivity and low density, and exhibits excellent electromagnetic interference shielding performance (48.32 dB) and Joule heating performance (reaching 90.8°C under 0.8 V). Flexible touch screen

Flexible touch screen panels (f-TSPs) have gained significant attention due to their integration of mechanical flexibility and optoelectronic properties, which are essential for emerging flexible/foldable displays.93,94 However, their durability degrades rapidly under dynamic usage (e.g., bending, folding, and rolling) compared to rigid counterparts. A critical component of f-TSPs is metallic nanowire transparent conductive electrodes (MNW TCEs), which provide electrical/optical pathways and physical support. Common MNWs include Ag, copper (Cu), and gold (Au), while typical substrates are PET or polyvinyl alcohol. The major bottleneck of existing MNW TCEs is the weak electrode-substrate bonding strength—relying on van der Waals forces—leading to delamination of MNW layers during mechanical stress, TCE failure, and reduced f-TSP durability. Previous strategies (e.g., adding adhesives, over-coating protective layers, mechanical pressing) either compromise optical properties, limit patterning feasibility, or are substrate-specific, failing to address the root issue.95,96

Tao et al. successfully fabricated AgNWs/NFC-HS TCEs with strong bonding by introducing Ag–S bonds, where AgNWs served as the conductive layer and thiol-modified nanocellulose (NFC-HS) nanopaper acted as the substrate.90 These TCEs simultaneously exhibit excellent electrical stability (the resistance remains basically stable after 60 peeling tests, and the resistance increase after 10,000 bending tests is much lower than that of AgNWs/PET), outstanding optical performance (the maximum light transmittance at 600 nm reaches 87.10%), and superior electrical performance (the minimum sheet resistance is 24 Ω/sq). The f-TSP assembled based on these TCEs can withstand more than 500 bending cycles while maintaining high sensitivity (Figures 8A–8F). This research provides an effective strategy for the preparation of TCEs used in next-generation high-durability f-TSPs. Unlike traditional methods (adhesives, over-coating) that introduce trade-offs, the use of Ag–S covalent bonding directly targets the root cause of weak adhesion. With a bond energy of 217 kJ/mol, it fundamentally improves electrode-substrate integration without compromising optical/electrical performance—this is a paradigm shift from “passive reinforcement” to “active chemical bonding.”Figure 8Application of AgNWs/NFC-HS in flexible touch screen panel (f-TSP) devices(A) Schematic illustration of the structure of f-TSP device using AgNWs/NFC-HS as its top and bottom TCEs.(B) AgNWs/NFC-HS TCE demonstrates excellent mechanical flexibility.(C) Digital photo image of the fabricated f-TSP device.(D) Schematic illustration of the durability test for the f-TSPs. The core performance of flexible touch screen depends on the balance between the transmittance and conductivity of TCEs—a key trade-off that directly determines device usability. AgNWs address this challenge through their one-dimensional network structure, but the simultaneous optimization of both properties remains a research focus. Specifically, higher AgNW network density (e.g., increasing concentration from 0.1 wt % to 0.5 wt %) creates more continuous electron transport paths, reducing sheet resistance from 80 Ω/sq to 15 Ω/sq and enhancing conductivity, yet excessive nanowire overlap scatters light, decreasing transmittance from 90% to 82% (at 550 nm). Additionally, AgNW geometry limits transmittance: thicker or longer AgNWs improve conductivity but increase light absorption and scattering, while thinner nanowires (≤30 nm) boost transparency but require higher density to maintain conductivity, forming a “threshold balance” where further reducing diameter no longer enhances transmittance without sacrificing electron transport.

Flexible sensors

Flexible sensor devices are a type of electronic devices that can be directly worn on the human body or closely integrated with the human body to achieve specific functions. They usually have the characteristics of being small, lightweight, low-power-consuming, and easy to operate, and can obtain various physiological information of the human body and environmental data in real time and communicate and interact with other devices. Common flexible sensors devices include wearable electronic devices,97 electronic skin,98 soft robotics.99,100

Wearable electronic devices

Wearable electronics is an innovative product form that integrates electronic technology with daily clothing, accessories, or body-attached devices. Owing to the large conductivity and excellent ductility, AgNWs are widely used in wearable electronic devices. However, pure AgNWs alone are difficult to convert various environmental stimuli such as temperature, humidity, pressure, vibration, and haptics into real-time and visualized electronic pulses for detecting and quantifying these stimuli. Materials with certain functions combined with AgNWs to obtain materials with special functions are increasingly favored by researchers.101,102,103

Kim et al. deposited reduced graphene oxide (rGO) and AgNWs onto fabrics using supersonic cold spray technology, successfully preparing a multifunctional conductive fabric with wearability, stretchability, and washability.104 This fabric can not only act as a strain sensor (with a gauge factor |GF|≈0.56 and a strain range of 0%–170%) and a temperature sensor, but also function as a heater (reaching a maximum temperature of 56°C under 3.5V) and a supercapacitor. (Figures 9A–9C) Additionally, it possesses hydrophobicity and antibacterial properties, this providing a new direction for the development of wearable electronic textiles. Chao et al. assembled conductive MXene nanosheets and AgNWs onto an electrospun elastic substrate to fabricate a sensor with excellent sensing performance, including an ultra-wide sensing range (up to ∼120%), ultra-high sensitivity (gauge factor up to 4,720), and an extremely low detection limit (∼0.0645%).105 This sensor can be used for monitoring human physiological signals and realizing wearable human-computer interaction (Figures 9D–9F). Meanwhile, it exhibits outstanding photothermal performance, capable of rapidly heating up to ∼51°C within 60 s under near-infrared light irradiation, which enables antibacterial and photothermal therapy. In addition, it has good biocompatibility and breathability, providing a new solution for long-term health monitoring, intelligent disease diagnosis and treatment, etc. He et al. integrated calcium alginate (CA), iron tetroxide nanoparticles (Fe3O4 NPs) and AgNWs onto textiles to prepare an ultra-light self-powered fire alarm electronic textile (SFA e-textile).106 When made into fire-fighting suits, it can realize wide-range temperature monitoring from 100°C to 400°C and repeatable fire warning functions.Figure 9Application of rGO/AgNWs in wearable electronic devices(A) The SEM and IR camera images of the rGO/AgNWs fabric under stretching. Electronic skin

Electronic skin can achieve perfect conformity with natural skin and may even function as a viable substitute. It not only enables accurate acquisition and recording of physiological and/or electrophysiological signals but also facilitates the efficient transduction of external electrical and mechanical energy into the human body, thereby providing novel solutions for health monitoring and bioengineering applications.107,108 A critical component of electronic skin is the stretchable conductor, which requires properties such as high electrical conductivity, stretchability, skin-like softness, low contact resistance, and low impedance.109,110 AgNWs precisely fulfill these performance criteria, rendering them widely utilized in the fabrication of electronic skin.

Dai et al. fabricated a self-powered sensor suitable for flexible electronic skin using polyurea (SH-PUrea) and AgNWs (Figure 10A).111 This sensor exhibits strong self-healing ability in various extreme environments such as underwater, alkali, acid-base, high and low temperatures, and the output signal does not degrade after self-healing. From the Figure 10B, we can see the output electrical performances could be almost restored to the initial states when the sensor were kept in close contact and immersed in various solutions including deionized water, 1 mol/L NaCl, 30% H2O2, 1 mol/L NaOH, and 1 mol/L HCl, respectively. In electronic skin sensors, there have always been issues with mechanical conformal contact and low contact resistance. Soft robotics

Soft robots are a new type of robot constructed mainly from flexible materials such as silicone and hydrogel. Different from traditional rigid robots, they possess a high degree of flexibility and deformability, capable of bending, stretching, and even folding flexibly like biological organisms.113,114,115

Dahiya et al. invented a preparation method for an ultra-high stretchable strain sensor. This sensor uses silicone rubber as the substrate, encapsulating multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) or AgNWs to form a sandwich structure.116 Among them, the MWCNT-based sensor has a wide sensing range of 2%–180% and excellent durability of more than 6,000 cycles, showing strong strain dependence in the 45%–120% stretching range; the AgNWs-based sensor achieves an ultra-high gauge factor of >106 under 30% strain and good stability of more than 100 cycles (Figures 11A–11C). Both types of sensors have potential application value in the field of soft robotics. Zang et al. designed a supra-LMNs electrode constructed based on supramolecular networks (Ns) and liquid metals (LMs), which possesses properties such as high conductivity (up to 16,000 S cm−1), high stretchability (approximately 1,800%), strain insensitivity (the resistance only increases by 1.3 times under 1,000% strain), room temperature instantaneous self-healing ability, and rapid recyclability.117 The assembled dielectric elastomer actuator (DEA) flexible hand can simulate human hand movements (Figure 11D). Energy storage

The transition from rigid substrates to non-rigid substrates is an inevitable trend in the research and development of energy storage devices.118,119 Flexible/stretchable electronic devices have the advantages of portability, high degree of freedom in shape and structure, and low cost, and meet the development needs of concepts such as the Internet of Things and artificial intelligence. AgNWs have high conductivity, high transparency, and high flexibility. Their network structure can maintain a stable conductive path when bent and stretched, making them a promising material for realizing flexible/stretchable self-sustainable devices.120,121,122 As scientists conduct more in-depth research on AgNWs, which are widely applied in fields such as solar cells,123,124,125 nanogenerators,126,127 supercapacitors,128,129,130 and lithium-ion batteries131,132,133 to make flexible energy storage devices.

Solar cells

With the development of technology, wearable devices such as smart watches, smart bracelets, and smart clothing are becoming increasingly popular. Nanogenerators

The significant advantage of triboelectric nanogenerators (TENGs) in low-frequency mechanical energy conversion efficiency (over 80%) enables them to demonstrate irreplaceable potential compared to traditional electromagnetic generators in the field of self-power supply.

Siuk Cheon et al. used electrospinning technology to fabricate high-performance TENGs from polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) and AgNWs, high-performance triboelectric nanogenerators based on electrospun polyvinylidene fluoride–silver nanowire composite nanofibers.137 The electrospinning process promotes the uniaxial stretching of PVDF polymer chains, forming highly oriented polar β-phases (Figure 13A). The addition of AgNWs further facilitates the formation of β-phases through electrostatic interactions and enhances charge-trapping capability. When the AgNWs content is 3 wt %, the output voltage of TENGs increases from 156 V to 240 V (Figures 13B and 13C). In addition, thermal welding treatment near the melting temperature of PVDF (160°C) can significantly improve the mechanical stability of nanofibers without affecting the performance of TENGs. Supercapacitors

Supercapacitors have a higher power density than batteries and can be charged in a few seconds or even shorter. The number of charge and discharge cycles can be as high as hundreds of thousands of times. This is very valuable for microsensors, micro-robots, and wearable devices. Therefore, it is very necessary to study flexible micro-supercapacitors. Ho et al. reports a hybrid microcapacitor with a MXene/AgNWs&BC hybrid cathode paired with a zinc anode, obtaining rare output stability and a landmark areal capacitance/energy density of up to 909 mF cm−2/227 μWh cm−2. Based on this, a stretchable flexible supercapacitor based on a semi-liquid metal circuit is further designed and prepared, which can achieve controllable and stable output voltage and energy even when stretched up to 100%.139

Based on biomass-derived hierarchical porous carbon, cellulose nanofibers, and AgNWs, Bai et al. fabricated a binder-free, self-supporting flexible capacitor via vacuum filtration.140 It exhibits a high specific capacitance of 383 F g−1 at 0.5 A g−1 and maintains 95% of its initial capacitance after 10,000 charge-discharge cycles, demonstrating excellent cycle stability. The assembled flexible supercapacitor shows unchanged electrochemical performance when bent, with an energy density of 9.3 Wh kg−1 (Figures 14A–14C). Lithium-ion batteries

Lithium-ion batteries are the most widely used energy storage devices because they have high energy density and a long life cycle. However, during the charging and discharging process of lithium-ion batteries, volume changes occur due to the absorption and release of lithium ions, which can lead to mechanical stress and thus affect the lifespan of lithium-ion batteries. Making lithium-ion batteries flexible can greatly reduce the impact of deformation.

Lithium-rich manganese-based oxide cathode materials (LRMOs) have the advantage of high energy density, but they suffer from drawbacks such as poor cycle stability, insufficient rate performance, and rapid voltage decay. Gan et al. adopted a strategy of modifying LRMO with silica-coated AgNWs (AgNWs@SiO2).131 The schematic illustration of preparation process of AgNWs@SiO2 modified LRMO cathode material is shown in the Figure 15A. This method can significantly improve the performance of LRMO by accelerating Li+ diffusion kinetics, increasing oxygen vacancies, and promoting heat dissipation: at 1C, the capacity retention rate reaches 71.6% after 500 cycles at 25°C and 58.5% after 200 cycles at 60°C, it also exhibits excellent high-rate discharge specific capacity at 10C, with a retention rate of 50.6% after 1,000 cycles at 3C (Figures 15B–15D). Electromagnetic interference shielding

With the popularization and development of modern communication technologies like surging waves spreading rapidly across the globe, every corner of human life is invisibly enveloped. From the bustling central business districts of prosperous cities, where high-rise buildings stand in rows and communication base stations are scattered everywhere, to the remote and tranquil rural areas where communication signals also spread and permeate like silk threads, human beings are constantly under the intertwined and complex influences of electromagnetic interference and electromagnetic shielding.143,144,145 In hospitals, the precise medical equipment may experience data deviations due to electromagnetic interference, threatening the diagnosis and treatment of patients. In airports, the aviation communication systems also need to be vigilant against the potential threats of electromagnetic interference to flight safety. Meanwhile, in order to ensure the normal operation of various electronic devices and systems and to protect people from the possible health hazards caused by electromagnetic radiation, electromagnetic shielding materials have emerged as the times require. They are like guarding sentinels, carving out a safe space for human beings in the ocean of electromagnetism. Traditional shielding materials generally use metals or alloy compounds, but they have problems such as poor flexibility, high density, difficult processing, and susceptibility to corrosion.146,147 We use metal nanowires instead, and the performance will be improved, but the self-assembly structure and self-supporting property of metal nanowires are poor, and usually the metal nanowires need to be compounded with polymeric materials.148,149,150

The electromagnetic interference (EMI) shielding performance of AgNW-based composites relies on two core mechanisms—reflection and absorption—whose synergy is tailored by the AgNW network structure, composite matrix, and material design. Reflection is the primary shielding mechanism for AgNW composites, driven by the exceptional electrical conductivity of Ag (≈6.3 × 107 S/m)—a key property of AgNWs highlighted throughout the review. When incident electromagnetic (EM) waves encounter the AgNW network, the mobile free electrons in AgNWs interact with the EM field, inducing an alternating current. This current generates a secondary EM field that opposes the incident wave, resulting in reflection of the wave away from the material. We quantified reflection via two key metrics: shielding effectiveness by reflection (SER) and Reflection Coefficient. SER represents the contribution of reflection to total EMI shielding. For example, in the ScPEG-coated AgNW-modified glass fiber fabric (ScPEG-coated A-GFF) developed by Liang et al., SER values are consistently lower than SEA (Shielding Effectiveness by Absorption) across different fabric layers (Figure 16E), but still account for ∼10%–20% of total shielding. Reflection Coefficient is used to measures the proportion of incident EM waves reflected. Absorption complements reflection by dissipating incident EM waves as heat, reducing “secondary interference” from reflected waves. This mechanism is amplified by the 1D structure of AgNWs and their interaction with composite matrices (e.g., ScPEG, polyborosiloxane, boron nitride nanosheets [BNNS]/aramid nanofibers [ANF]), which create pathways for EM energy dissipation. AgNWs form a 3D interconnected network within the matrix. Incident EM waves bounce between AgNW surfaces and matrix interfaces, extending their propagation path and increasing energy loss. For example, Gu et al.’s Janus (BNNS/ANF)-(AgNWs/ANF) composite film (Figures 16A–16G) uses AgNWs’ network structure to scatter waves, while BNNS/ANF layers enhance dielectric polarization—synergistically boosting absorption.151 The composite matrix introduces polar groups or heterogeneous interfaces. These induce dipole polarization or interfacial polarization when exposed to EM fields, converting wave energy into thermal energy.

In summary, AgNW composites achieve high EMI shielding via a “reflection-dominated, absorption-enhanced” mechanism: AgNWs’ high conductivity drives efficient reflection, while the composite matrix and AgNW network structure amplify absorption—together addressing the limitations of traditional metal shields (e.g., poor flexibility, high secondary interference). Conclusion and prospect

This review systematically summarizes the research progress of AgNWs in recent years, covering three core aspects: synthesis, electrode fabrication, and applications.

In terms of synthesis, it elaborates on mainstream methods such as the polyol method, solvothermal method which includes microwave-assisted processes, and template method—with hard templates such as AAO and soft templates such as PVP. The review highlights how key parameters including PVP molecular weight, Cl−/Br− concentration ratios, and reaction temperature regulate the morphology (including aspect ratio and diameter) and purity of AgNWs, providing a reference for optimizing synthesis conditions. Regarding the fabrication of conductive electrodes, it compares four mainstream techniques: RTR coating, which is suitable for large-scale production and enables controllable sheet resistance and transmittance; spray coating, which has the advantages of simple operation and low cost; spin coating, which offers high precision for small-area films; and dip coating, which features easy operation but has stability limitations. The analysis of their technical characteristics and application scenarios offers guidance for selecting appropriate processes. In applications, the review focuses on the performance of AgNWs and their composites in touch display (including flexible displays and foldable devices), flexible sensors (such as wearable electronics and electronic skin), energy storage (covering solar cells, supercapacitors and lithium-ion batteries), and electromagnetic interference (EMI) shielding. It demonstrates that AgNWs address critical challenges in these fields—such as poor flexibility of traditional materials and low conductivity of active materials—by leveraging their high conductivity, transparency, and mechanical flexibility.

Despite the progress, challenges remain: the synthesis of high-purity, high-aspect-ratio AgNWs at low cost and on a large scale needs improvement; electrode stability under long-term mechanical stress or harsh environments requires enhancement, such as through structural design or protective coatings; performance bottlenecks in applications must be addressed, including the response speed of flexible sensors, the cycle stability of energy storage devices, and the balance between shielding efficiency and flexibility in EMI shielding; and reducing raw material and manufacturing costs while improving compatibility with existing industrial processes is crucial for promoting practical applications. Future research should focus on these aspects to advance the development and industrialization of AgNWs.
