The Cayley transform on representations
Jingyu Lu, Ke Ye

Abstract
. The classical Cayley transform is a birational map between a quadratic matrix group and its Lie algebra, which was first discovered by Cayley in 1846. Because of its essential role in both pure and applied mathematics, the classical Cayley transform has been generalized from various perspectives. This paper is concerned with a representation theoretic generalization of the classical Cayley transform. The idea underlying this work is that the applicability of the classical Cayley transform heavily depends on how the Lie group is represented. The goal is to characterize irreducible representations of a Lie group, to which the classical Cayley transform is applicable. To this end, we first establish criteria of the applicability for a general Lie group. If the group is semisimple, we further obtain a geometric condition on the weight diagram of such representations. Lastly, we provide a complete classification for classical complex simple Lie groups and their compact real forms. Except for the previously known examples, spin representations of Spin ⁡ ( 8 ) \Spin(8) are the only ones on our list.

Generalized Cayley transform

Because of its essential role in both theoretical studies and practical applications, there have been several attempts to generalize the Cayley transform in the past 40 years. The first generalization is given by an analytic construction based on the Iwasawa decomposition of H-type groups

[

,

for

Problem 1.1

(Lemire-Popov-Reichstein problem)

[

Which linear algebraic group can have a generalized Cayley transform?

It is worth remarking that an equivariant birational isomorphism between
G

defined in (

is the group of

Main results

The purpose of this paper is to investigate a representation theoretic generalization of the classical Cayley transform. We notice that the applicability of the classical Cayley transform
C
depends on how we represent the group. For instance, it is straightforward to verify that
C
is applicable to
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{C})\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
. It is also applicable to
\mathrm{Ad}(\SL_{2}(\mathbb{C}))\subseteq\GL(\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C}))\simeq\GL_{3}(\mathbb{C})
where
\mathrm{Ad}:\SL_{2}(\mathbb{C})\to\GL(\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C}))
is the adjoint representation of
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{C})
. In fact,
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{C})
is a double cover of
\SO_{3}(\mathbb{C})
and
\mathrm{Ad}(\SL_{2}(\mathbb{C}))=\SO_{3}(\mathbb{C})
if we identify
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
with
\mathbb{C}^{3}
. However,
C
is not applicable to
\rho(\SL_{2}(\mathbb{C}))\subseteq\GL\left(\mathsf{S}^{3}\mathbb{C}^{2}\right)\simeq\GL_{4}(\mathbb{C})
where
\rho:\SL_{2}(\mathbb{C})\to\GL(\mathsf{S}^{3}\mathbb{C}^{2})
is the irreducible representation of
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{C})
on the third symmetric power
\mathsf{S}^{3}(\mathbb{C}^{2})
. Because of this observation, we have the following analogue of the Lemire-Popov-Reichstein (abbreviated as LPR) problem
1.1
.

Problem 1.2

(Representation theoretic LPR problem)
.

To which pair
(G,\rho)
of a Lie group
G
and its representation
\rho:G\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
, the classical Cayley transform
C
defined in (
1
) is applicable?

Here by Definition
4.1
,
C
is
applicable
to
\rho
if there is an open neighbourhood
V
of
0
in
\mathfrak{g}
such that
C\circ d\rho(V)\subseteq\rho(G)
. We notice that if
G
is a linear algebraic group and
\rho:G\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
is a faithful representation to which
C
is applicable, then clearly
G
satisfies the requirement in the LPR problem. This paper is devoted to answering Problem
1.2
. First, we establish in Theorem
4.9
the equivalence between the applicability of
C
to
\rho
and the power span property of
d\rho(\mathfrak{g})
(cf. Definition
4.5
). Next, we restrict our discussion to semisimple Lie groups in Section
5
and obtain the equivalence between the applicability of

to
\rho
and the power span property of
d\rho(\mathfrak{h})
in Theorem
5.2
. Here
\mathfrak{h}
denotes the Cartan subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
. Moreover, we prove in Theorem

that if
\rho
is irreducible and

is applicable to
\rho
, then the weight diagram of

must be the orbit of the highest weight under the action of Weyl group, possibly union with the origin. Lastly, Section

is concerned with a complete classification of irreducible representations of classical complex simple Lie groups and their compact real forms, to which

is applicable. Our classification results (cf. Theorems
6.13
and
6.15
) indicate that the only irreducible representations to which

is applicable are:

\diamond

G
is of type
A_{1}
,
B_{n}
(
n\geq 1
),
C_{n}
(
n\geq 3
) or
D_{n}
(
n\geq 3
) and
\rho
is the standard representation.

\diamond

G
is of type

and

Our results may be recognized as representation theoretic analogues of

We use capital letters such as

,

2.1.
Real and complex semisimple Lie groups

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a real semisimple Lie algebra. We denote its
complexification
by
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathbb{C}}\coloneqq\mathfrak{g}\otimes\mathbb{C}
. A
complexification of a real semisimple Lie group
G
is the simply-connected complex semisimple Lie group
G^{\mathbb{C}}
whose Lie algebra is isomorphic to
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathbb{C}}
. A
real form of a complex semisimple Lie algebra
\mathfrak{u}
is a real Lie subalgebra
\mathfrak{u}^{\prime}
of
\mathfrak{u}
such that
{\mathfrak{u}^{\prime}}^{\mathbb{C}}\simeq\mathfrak{u}
. Let
U
be a simply-connected complex semisimple Lie group. A
real form of
U
is a real Lie subgroup
U^{\prime}
of
U
such that
U
is a complexification of
U^{\prime}
.

Given a complex semisimple Lie group
U
and a representation
\rho:U\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
, the
restriction
of
\rho
to a real form
U^{\prime}
of
U
is

\Res(\rho)\coloneqq\rho|_{U^{\prime}}:U^{\prime}\to\GL(\mathbb{V}),\quad\Res(\rho)(g)=\rho(g).

Similarly, the restriction of a representation
\pi:\mathfrak{u}\to\mathfrak{gl}(\mathbb{V})
of a complex semisimple Lie algebra
\mathfrak{u}
to its real form
\mathfrak{u}_{0}
is defined by
\Res(\pi)(x)=\pi(x)
for
x\in\mathfrak{u}_{0}
.

Suppose that
\pi:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{gl}(\mathbb{V})
is a representation of a real semisimple Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
. The
induction
of
\pi
is the representation of
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathbb{C}}
defined as

\Ind(\pi):\mathfrak{g}^{\mathbb{C}}\to\mathfrak{gl}(\mathbb{V}),\quad\Ind(\pi)(x_{1}+ix_{2})=\rho(x_{1})+i\rho(x_{2}),\;x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathfrak{g}.

If
\rho:G\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
is a representation of a real semisimple Lie group
G
and
G^{\mathbb{C}}
is its complexification. The
induction
of
\rho
is the representation
\Ind(\rho):G^{\mathbb{C}}\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
uniquely determined by
\Ind(d\rho):\mathfrak{g}^{\mathbb{C}}\to\mathfrak{gl}(\mathbb{V})
. To be more explicit, we have

\Ind(\rho):G^{\mathbb{C}}\to\GL(\mathbb{V}),\quad\Ind(\rho)(\exp(x))=\exp(\Ind(d\rho)(x)),\;x\in\mathfrak{g}^{\mathbb{C}}

where
\exp
denotes the exponential map of Lie groups.

.

[

3.
Some basic facts

In this short section, we establish two basic facts that will be needed in the sequel. The first fact is about the relation between the induction and the restriction of representations.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
(resp.
\mathfrak{u}
) be a real (resp. complex) semisimple Lie algebra and let
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathbb{C}}
(resp.
\mathfrak{u}^{\prime}
) be its complexification (resp. real form). Suppose that
\pi:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{gl}(\mathbb{V})
(resp.
\rho:\mathfrak{u}\to\mathfrak{gl}(\mathbb{W})
) is a representation of
\mathfrak{g}
(resp.
\mathfrak{u}
). Then we have the followings properties:

(a)

\Res(\Ind(\pi))=\pi
and
\Ind(\Res(\rho))=\rho
.

(b)

\pi
(resp.
\rho
) is irreducible if and only if
\Ind(\pi)
(resp.
\Res(\rho)
) is irreducible.

(c)

There are irreducible representations
\pi_{j}:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{gl}(\mathbb{V}_{j})
,
1\leq j\leq s
, such that

\mathbb{V}=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{s}\mathbb{V}_{j},\quad\pi=\prod_{j=1}^{s}\pi_{j}.

Proof.

Property (
a
) follows immediately from the definition. Suppose that
\pi
is irreducible and
0\neq\mathbb{V}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{V}
is a subspace such that
\Ind(\pi)(\mathfrak{g})(\mathbb{V}’)\subseteq\mathbb{V}_{1}
. Then we have

\pi(\mathfrak{g})(\mathbb{V}^{\prime})=\Res(\Ind(\pi))(\mathfrak{g}))(\mathbb{V}’)=\Ind(\pi)(\mathfrak{g})(\mathbb{V}’)\subseteq\mathbb{V}^{\prime},

which implies that
\Ind(\pi)
is irreducible. Taking
\pi=\Res(\rho)
, we obtain that the irreducibility of
\Res(\rho)
leads to the irreducibility of
\rho=\Ind(\Res(\rho))
.

Conversely, if
\rho
is irreducible and
0\neq\mathbb{W}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{W}
is a subspace such that
\Res(\rho)(\mathfrak{u}^{\prime})(\mathbb{W}^{\prime})\subseteq\mathbb{W}^{\prime}
, then

\rho(\mathfrak{u})(\mathbb{W}^{\prime})=\Ind(\Res(\rho))\left({\mathfrak{u}^{\prime}}^{\mathbb{C}}\right)\left(\mathbb{W}^{\prime}\right)=\Res(\rho)(\mathfrak{u}^{\prime})(\mathbb{W}^{\prime})+i\Res(\rho)(\mathfrak{u}^{\prime})(\mathbb{W}^{\prime})\subseteq\mathbb{W}^{\prime}.

Thus, we obtain the irreducibility of
\Res(\rho)
. Taking
\rho=\Ind(\pi)
, we conclude that the irreducibility of
\Ind(\pi)
implies the irreducibility of
\pi=\Res(\Ind(\pi))
and this completes the proof of (
b
).

To prove (
c
), we notice that there are irreducible representations
\rho_{j}:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{gl}(\mathbb{V}_{j})
,
1\leq j\leq s
, such that

\mathbb{V}=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{s}\mathbb{V}_{j},\quad\Ind(\pi)=\prod_{j=1}^{s}\rho_{j}.

For each
1\leq j\leq s
, we define
\pi_{j}\coloneqq\Res(\rho_{j})
, which is irreducible according to (
b
). It is clear that
\pi=\Res(\Ind(\pi))=\prod_{j=1}^{s}\Res(\rho_{j})
and this proves (
c
).
∎

The second fact we will need is the generation of a semisimple Lie algebra by its Cartan subalgebra. To achieve this, we first establish two lemmas.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a complex semisimple Lie algebra and let
\mathrm{ad}:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{g})
be its adjoint representation. There exists an irreducible symmetric algebraic group
G\subseteq\GL(\mathfrak{g})
whose Lie algebra is equal to
\mathrm{ad}(\mathfrak{g})\simeq\mathfrak{g}
.

Proof.

The semisimplicity of
\mathfrak{g}
ensures that
\mathrm{ad}
is faithful. Hence we have
\mathfrak{g}\simeq\mathrm{ad}(\mathfrak{g})\subseteq\mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{g})
. According to Proposition
2.3
, there is an irreducible algebraic subgroup
G^{\prime}\subseteq\GL(\mathfrak{g})
whose Lie algebra is equal to
\mathrm{ad}(\mathfrak{g})
. We claim that
G^{\prime}
is linearly reductive. Then Lemma
2.2
implies the existence of
P\in\GL(\mathfrak{g})
such that
G\coloneqq PG^{\prime}P^{-1}
is the desired group. Therefore, it is left to prove the claim. Let
\rho:G\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
be a finite dimensional rational representation of
G
. Since the Lie algebra of
G
is isomorphic to
\mathfrak{g}
,
\rho
induces a representation
\pi:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{gl}(\mathbb{V})
. The semisimplicity of
\mathfrak{g}
implies
\pi=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\pi_{j}:\mathfrak{g}\to\bigoplus_{j=1}^{m}\mathfrak{gl}(\mathbb{V}_{j})
where
\pi_{j}:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{gl}(\mathbb{V}_{j})
is an irreducible representation for each
1\leq j\leq m
. We recall that
\pi
is induced by
\rho
, thus
\rho
admits a decomposition
\rho=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\rho_{j}:G\to\prod_{j=1}^{m}\GL(\mathbb{V}_{j})
where
\rho_{j}:G\to\GL(\mathbb{V}_{j})
is a representation inducing
\pi_{j}
. Moreover, by construction
\rho_{j}
is irreducible for each
1\leq j\leq m
.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
G
be a connected Lie group with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
. Suppose
p:\widetilde{G}\to G
is the universal covering of
G
and
q:G\to G_{0}\coloneqq G/Z(G)
is the quotient map where
Z(G)
is the center of
G
. Then we have the following commutative diagram:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{G}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\GL(\mathfrak{g})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces G}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces G_{0}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{\mathrm{Ad}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{Ad}_{0}}

where
\widetilde{\mathrm{Ad}}
(resp.
\mathrm{Ad}_{0}
) is the adjoint representation of
\widetilde{G}
(resp.
G_{0}
).

Proof.

We notice that
\ker\left(\widetilde{\mathrm{Ad}}\right)=Z\left(\widetilde{G}\right)
,
\ker(p)\subseteq Z\left(\widetilde{G}\right)
and
p\left(Z\left(\widetilde{G}\right)\right)\subseteq Z(G)=\ker(q)
. We may conclude that
\ker(q\circ p)=p^{-1}(Z(G))=Z\left(\widetilde{G}\right)
. Indeed, the inclusion
Z\left(\widetilde{G}\right)\subseteq p^{-1}(Z(G))
is obtained by

Z\left(\widetilde{G}\right)\subseteq p^{-1}\left(p\left(Z\left(\widetilde{G}\right)\right)\right)\subseteq p^{-1}\left(Z(G)\right).

For the reverse inclusion, we observe that
xyx^{-1}y^{-1}\in\ker(p)
for each
x\in p^{-1}(Z(G))
and
y\in\widetilde{G}
. The discreteness of
\ker(p)=\pi_{1}(G)
and the connectedness of
\widetilde{G}
imply that
xyx^{-1}y^{-1}=h_{x}\in G
only depends on
x
. Hence, we have
xyx^{-1}y^{-1}=1
and
p^{-1}(Z(G))\subseteq Z\left(\widetilde{G}\right)
. The commutativity of the diagram follows immediately.
∎

We conclude this section by deriving the generation of a semisimple Lie algebra by its Cartan subalgebra.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
G
be a compact semisimple Lie group with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
and let
\mathfrak{h}
be the Cartan subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
. Then we have
\mathfrak{g}=\mathrm{Ad}(G)(\mathfrak{h})
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we assume that
G
is connected. By Lemma
3.2
, there is a symmetric irreducible algebraic subgroup
G^{\prime}\subseteq\GL\left(\mathfrak{g}^{\mathbb{C}}\right)
whose Lie algebra is isomorphic to
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathbb{C}}
. Let
U\subseteq\GL\left(\mathfrak{g}^{\mathbb{C}}\right)
be the subgroup consisting of unitary elements and let
K\coloneqq G^{\prime}\cap U
. By Theorem
2.4
,
K
is connected and
\mathfrak{k}^{\mathbb{C}}\simeq\mathfrak{g}^{\mathbb{C}}
. The uniqueness of the compact form of a semisimple Lie algebra implies
\mathfrak{k}\simeq\mathfrak{g}
. Moreover, by Theorem
2.5
we have
\mathfrak{k}=\mathrm{Ad}(K)(\mathfrak{h}_{K})
where
\mathfrak{h}_{K}
is the Cartan subalgebra of
\mathfrak{k}
. Under the isomorphism
\mathfrak{k}\simeq\mathfrak{g}
, Lemma
3.3
implies
\mathfrak{g}=\mathrm{Ad}(G)\mathfrak{h}
.
∎

4.
The Cayley transform on a representation

Suppose
\mathbb{F}
is either
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
. Let
G
be a Lie group and let
\rho:G\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
be a representation of
G
. We denote by
\mathfrak{g}
the Lie algebra of
G
. Suppose
C:\mathfrak{gl}(\mathbb{V})\dasharrow\GL(\mathbb{V})
is the classical Cayley transform defined by
C(u)=(1+u)/(1-u)
.

Definition 4.1

(Cayley representation)
.

We say that
\rho
is a Cayley representation if there exist an open neighbourhood
V\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
of
0
such that
C
is defined on
d\rho(V)
and
C(d\rho(V))\subseteq\rho(G)
. We also say that the Cayley representation is applicable to
\rho
.

Clearly, given a matrix Lie group
G\subseteq\GL_{n}(\mathbb{F})
, the natural inclusion
\iota:G\hookrightarrow\GL_{n}(\mathbb{F})
is a Cayley representation if and only if
C
is applicable to
G
in the classical sense. The following basic properties are well-known for the classical Cayley transform.

Proposition 4.2

(Basic properties)
.

Let
\rho:G\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
be a representation to which the Cayley transform is applicable. For any
X\in\mathfrak{g}
such that
C
is defined at
d\rho(X)
, we have

(a)

C(-d\rho(X))C(d\rho(X))=\Id_{\mathbb{V}}
.

(b)

Denote
f(X)\coloneqq C(d\rho(X)/2)
and
g(X)\coloneqq\exp(d\rho(X))
. Then
f
is a second order approximation of
g
near the origin.

(c)

If
\pi:G\to\GL(\mathbb{W})
is Cayley, then
\rho\times\pi:G\to\GL(\mathbb{V})\times\GL(\mathbb{W})
is Cayley.

Proof.

One can verify (
a
) and (
c
) by definition. If we denote
u\coloneqq d\rho(X)
, then (
b
) follows from

f(X)-g(X)=C\left(\frac{u}{2}\right)-\exp(u)=1+u\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{u^{k}}{2^{k}}-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{u^{k}}{k!}=O(X^{3}).\qed

We remark that
\rho(G)
is a subgroup of
\GL(\mathbb{V})
, but it is not necessarily a Lie subgroup. For instance, we consider the representation of
\mathbb{R}
on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
defined by

\rho_{\alpha}:\mathbb{R}\to\GL(\mathbb{C}^{2}),\quad\rho_{\alpha}(t)=(\exp(it),\exp(i\alpha t)),

where
\alpha
is a fixed real number. Clearly,
\rho_{\alpha}(\mathbb{R})
is a Lie subgroup of
\GL(\mathbb{R}^{2})
if and only if
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}
. However, it is clear from the definition that
d\rho(\mathfrak{g})
is a Lie subalgebra of
\mathfrak{gl}(\mathbb{V})
. The following proposition indicates that the applicability of the Cayley transform only depends on
\mathfrak{g}
and its representation
d\rho:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{gl}(\mathbb{V})
.

Proposition 4.3

(Independence)
.

Let
G
be a Lie group and let
\pi:G_{0}\to G
be the universal cover of
G
. A representation
\rho:G\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
is Cayley if and only if
\rho\circ\pi:G_{0}\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
is Cayley. Moreover, if
G
is real and
G^{\mathbb{C}}
is its complexification, then
\rho
is Cayley implies that
\Ind(\rho):G^{\mathbb{C}}\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
is Cayley.

Proof.

The ’only if’ part follows immediately from the definition. We notice that
G_{0}
and
G
have the same Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
. If
\rho\circ\pi
is Cayley, then we have
C(d(\rho\circ\pi)(V))\subseteq(\rho\circ\pi)(G_{0})
from some open neighbourhood
V
of
0\in\mathfrak{g}
. Since
\pi
is a covering map, we have
d\pi=\Id_{\mathfrak{g}}
and
\pi(G_{0})=G
. This implies
C(d\rho(V))\subseteq\rho(G)
. The ’moreover’ part follows immediately from the definition.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

For any
u\in d\rho(\mathfrak{g})
with
\lVert u\rVert<\frac{1}{3}
,
C(u)
is defined. Moreover, we have
C(u)\in\rho(G)
if and only if
\log(1+u)-\log(1-u)\in d\rho(\mathfrak{g})
.

Proof.

We recall that
\exp:\mathfrak{gl}(\mathbb{V})\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
is a homeomorphism between a neighbourhood
U
of
0\in\mathfrak{gl}(\mathbb{V})
and a neighbourhood
V
of
\Id\in\GL(\mathbb{V})
. Conversely, the map
\log:\GL(\mathbb{V})\dasharrow\mathfrak{gl}(\mathbb{V})
is defined for
a\in\GL(\mathbb{V})
such that
\lVert a-\Id\rVert<1
, and it is the inverse map of
\exp
whenever its defined. Hence we may choose
V=\{a\in\GL(\mathbb{V}):\lVert a-\Id\rVert<1\}
and
U=\log(V)
.

Since
\|u\|<\frac{1}{3}
,
C(u)
is defined by definition. Moreover, we have
\left\|C(u)-\Id\right\|<1
. This implies that
C(u)\in V
and
\log
is defined at
C(u)
. Thus, we obtain
C(u)\in\rho(G)
if and only if
\log(1+u)-\log(1-u)=\log(C(u))\in\log(V\cap\rho(G))=U\cap d\rho(\mathfrak{g})\subseteq d\rho(\mathfrak{g})
.
∎

To characterize representations of
G
to which the Cayley transform is applicable, we need the definition that follows.

Definition 4.5

(Power span property)
.

Let
\mathfrak{u}
be a Lie subalgebra of
\mathfrak{gl}(\mathbb{V})
. An element
u\in\mathfrak{u}
has the power span property if
u^{2k+1}\in\mathfrak{u}
for any integer
k\geq 0
. A linear subspace
\mathbb{W}\subseteq\mathfrak{u}
has the power span property if
x^{2k+1}\in\mathbb{W}
for any element
x\in\mathbb{W}
and any integer
k\geq 0
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Suppose
u\in d\rho(\mathfrak{g})
and
\|u\|<\frac{1}{3}
. If
u
has the power span property, then
C(u)\in\rho(G)
.

Proof.

By definition,
\log(1+u)-\log(1-u)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{2k+1}u^{2k+1}
for some real numbers
c_{1},c_{3},\dots
. Moreover, this series is convergent. Since
u\in d\rho(\mathfrak{g})
has the power span property, we have
u^{2k+1}\in d\rho(\mathfrak{g})
for each integer
k\geq 0
. We notice that
d\rho(\mathfrak{g})
is finite dimensional, thus
\log(1+u)-\log(1-u)\in d\rho(\mathfrak{g})
and Lemma
4.4
implies that
C(u)\in\rho(G)
.
∎

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
\mathfrak{u}
be a Lie subalgebra of
\mathfrak{gl}(\mathbb{V})
. Then the following are equivalent:

(a)

\mathfrak{u}
has the power span property.

(b)

If
a,b\in\mathfrak{u}
then
aba\in\mathfrak{u}
.

(c)

If
a,b,c\in\mathfrak{u}
, then
abc+cba\in\mathfrak{u}
.

Proof.

Implications (
c
)
\implies
(
b
)
\implies
(
a
) are obvious by induction. Next we prove (
a
)
\implies
(
b
). If
a,b\in\mathfrak{u}
and
\mathfrak{u}
has the power span property, then
f(\mu)\coloneqq(a+\mu b)^{3}\in\mathfrak{u}
for each
\mu\in\mathbb{F}
. In particular, we have
f^{\prime}(0)=a^{2}b+aba+ba^{2}\in\mathfrak{u}
. This implies that
aba\in\mathfrak{u}
as
3aba=f^{\prime}(0)-[a,[a,b]]
. We complete the proof by deriving (
c
) from (
b
). For
a,b,c\in\mathfrak{u}
and
\mu\in\mathbb{F}
, we consider
g(\mu)\coloneqq(a+\mu c)b(a+\mu c)
. By (
b
),
g(\mu)\in\mathfrak{u}
for any
\mu\in\mathbb{F}
. This implies that
g^{\prime}(0)=abc+cba\in\mathfrak{u}
.
∎

In the following, we will establish the equivalence between the applicability of the Cayley transform and the power span property. To that end, we need the next lemma.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
G
be a Lie group and let
\rho
be a representation of
G
. If
\rho:G\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
is Cayley, then
d\rho(\mathfrak{g})
has the power span property.

Proof.

By assumption, there exists an open neighbourhood
V
of
0\in\mathfrak{g}
such that
C
is defined on
d\rho(V)
and
C(d\rho(V))\subseteq\rho(G)
. By shrinking
V
if necessary, we may assume that
\lVert d\rho(x)\rVert<1/2
for any
x\in V
. For each
x\in\mathfrak{g}
, there exists some
c>0
such that
cx\in V
. It is clear that
d\rho(x)
has the power span property if and only if
d\rho(cx)
has the power span property. Hence it is sufficient to assume that
x\in V
. We denote
u\coloneqq d\rho(x)
. By definition,
C(tu)\in\rho(G)
for any
t\in\mathbb{F}
with
\lvert\lambda\rvert\leq 1
. Lemma
4.4
implies that
\varphi(t)\coloneqq\log(1+tu)-\log(1-tu)\in d\rho(\mathfrak{g})
if
t\in\mathbb{F}
and
\lvert t\rvert\leq 1
. Therefore, for any integer
k\geq 0
, we may conclude that

\varphi^{(2k+1)}(0)=2(2k)!u^{2k+1}\in d\rho(\mathfrak{g}).\qed

Theorem 4.9

(Characterization I)
.

Let
G
be a Lie group and let
\rho
be a representation of
G
. The following are equivalent:

(a)

\rho
is a Cayley representation.

(b)

There exists an open neighbourhood
V
of
0\in\mathfrak{g}
such that
\log(1+d\rho(x))-\log(1-d\rho(x))\in d\rho(\mathfrak{g})
for any
x\in V
.

(c)

d\rho(\mathfrak{g})
has the power span property.

(d)

If
a,b\in d\rho(\mathfrak{g})
, then
aba\in d\rho(\mathfrak{g})
.

(e)

If
a,b,c\in d\rho(\mathfrak{g})
, then
abc+cba\in d\rho(\mathfrak{g})
.

Proof.

The equivalence between (
a
) and (
b
) is a direct consequence of Lemma
4.4
, while the equivalences among (
c
)–(
e
) follow from Lemma
4.7
. Moreover, Lemma
4.8
implies (
a
)
\implies
(
c
). Lastly, if (
c
) holds, then Lemma
4.6
indicates that
C(u)\in\rho(G)
for any
u\in d\rho(\mathfrak{g})
with
\lVert u\rVert<1/2
, from which we may conclude that (
a
) holds.
∎

We recall that for an abelian Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, its universal enveloping algebra
\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})
is isomorphic to
S(\mathfrak{g})\coloneqq\bigoplus_{n=0}^{\infty}S^{n}(\mathfrak{g})
, where
S^{n}(\mathfrak{g})\coloneqq\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{F}}\left\{\prod_{k=1}^{n}u_{i}:u_{i}\in\mathfrak{g},1\leq i\leq n\right\}
is the
n
-th symmetric power of
\mathfrak{g}
. Thus, we may simply identify the equivalence class
\overline{a\otimes b\otimes c}\in\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})
with
abc\in S^{3}(\mathfrak{g})
for
a,b,c\in\mathfrak{g}
.

Proposition 4.10

(Characterization II)
.

Let
G
be an abelian Lie group and let
\mathfrak{g}
be its Lie algebra. Suppose
\rho:G\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
is a representation of
G
. Then the followings are equivalent:

(a)

\rho
is a Cayley representation.

(b)

\widetilde{d\rho}\left(S^{3}(\mathfrak{g})\right)\subseteq d\rho(\mathfrak{g})
.

(c)

\widetilde{d\rho}\left(S^{2k+1}(\mathfrak{g})\right)\subseteq d\rho(\mathfrak{g})
for any integer
k\geq 0
.

Here
\widetilde{d\rho}:\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})\simeq S(\mathfrak{g})\to\mathfrak{gl}(\mathbb{V})
is the algebra homomorphism induced by
d\rho
.

Proof.

If
\widetilde{d\rho}\left(S^{3}(\mathfrak{g})\right)\subseteq d\rho(\mathfrak{g})
, then by induction we may derive
\widetilde{d\rho}\left(S^{2k+1}(\mathfrak{g})\right)\subseteq d\rho(\mathfrak{g})
for any integer
k\geq 0
. This establishes the equivalence between (
b
) and (
c
). If (
b
) holds and
x,y\in\mathfrak{g}
, then
d\rho(x)d\rho(y)d\rho(x)=\widetilde{d\rho}(xyx)\in\widetilde{d\rho}\left(S^{3}(\mathfrak{g})\right)\subseteq d\rho(\mathfrak{g})
. Theorem
4.9
implies that
\rho
is Cayley. Conversely, if (
a
) holds and
x,y,z\in\mathfrak{g}
, then by Theorem
4.9
we obtain

\widetilde{d\rho}(xyz)=\frac{1}{2}\widetilde{d\rho}(xyz+zyx)=\frac{1}{2}\left(d\rho(x)d\rho(y)d\rho(z)+d\rho(z)d\rho(y)d\rho(x)\right)\in d\rho(\mathfrak{g}).\qed

We conclude this section by a brief discussion on the applicability of the Cayley transform under an automorphism of
G
. Let
\Aut(G)
be the automorphism group of
G
. Then for each
\sigma\in\Aut(G)
and representation
\rho:G\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
, we have another representation
\rho^{\sigma}\coloneqq\rho\circ\sigma:G\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
.

Proposition 4.11
.

The representation
\rho^{\sigma}
has the following properties:

(a)

\rho^{\sigma}
is irreducible if and only if
\rho
is irreducible.

(b)

If
\rho=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}\rho_{i}
then
\rho^{\sigma}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}\rho_{i}^{\sigma}
, where
\rho_{i}:G\to\GL(\mathbb{V}_{i})
and
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{V}_{i}=\mathbb{V}
.

(c)

If
\sigma\in\Inn(G)
, then
\rho^{\sigma}
and
\rho
are isomorphic.

(d)

\rho^{\sigma}
is Cayley if and only if
\rho
is Cayley.

Proof.

All these properties can be easily verified by definition.
∎

According to Proposition
4.11
(
c
), the outer automorphism group
\Out(G)\coloneqq\Aut(G)/\Inn(G)
acts on the representation ring
R(G)
generated by isomorphism classes of representations of
G
. Moreover, by Proposition
4.11
(
d
), the applicability of the Cayley transform is stable with respect to the action of
\Out(G)
.

Corollary 4.12

(Stability I)
.

If
\rho
is a Cayley representation, then all representations in the
\Out(G)
-orbit of
\rho
in
R(G)
are Cayley.

Remark 4.13
.

According to
[
13
, Section 20.3]
,
\Out(\Spin_{8}(\mathbb{C}))
acts on
\{\mathbb{C}^{n},S^{+},S^{-}\}
as the permutation group
\mathfrak{S}_{3}
. Here
\mathbb{S}_{+},\mathbb{S}_{-}
are the two spin representations. Since
\mathbb{C}^{n}
is Cayley, Corollary
4.12
immediately implies that
\mathbb{S}_{+}
and
\mathbb{S}_{-}
are also Cayley representations.

5.
The Cayley transform for semisimple Lie groups

Let
G
be a semisimple Lie group over
\mathbb{F}
and let
\mathfrak{g}
be its Lie algebra. Assume
\rho:G\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
is a representation of
G
and
\mathfrak{h}
is a Cartan subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
. We recall that if
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
, then

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}_{0}\bigoplus\left(\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Phi}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\right),\quad\mathfrak{g}_{0}=\mathfrak{h},

where
\Phi
is the set of roots of
\mathfrak{g}
. If
\mathfrak{g}
is a compact real form, then we have

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}_{0}\bigoplus\left(\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Phi}\left(\mathfrak{g}^{\mathbb{C}}_{\alpha}\bigoplus\mathfrak{g}^{\mathbb{C}}_{-\alpha}\right)\bigcap\mathfrak{g}\right),\quad\mathfrak{g}_{0}=\mathfrak{h}.

Moreover, if
\mathfrak{g}
is a non-compact real form, then
\mathfrak{g}
admits a restricted root space decomposition
[
23
, Proposition 6.40 and Equation (6.48b)]
:

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}_{0}\bigoplus\left(\bigoplus_{\sigma\in\Sigma}\mathfrak{g}_{\sigma}\right),\quad\mathfrak{g}_{\mu}=\mathfrak{g}\bigcap\left(\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Phi,\;\alpha|_{\mathfrak{a}}=\mu}\mathfrak{g}^{\mathbb{C}}_{\alpha}\right),\quad\mu\in\Sigma\sqcup\{0\},

where
\mathfrak{a}
is some abelian subspace of
\mathfrak{g}
and
\Sigma
is the set of restricted roots of
\mathfrak{g}
. In this case,
\mathfrak{g}_{0}=\mathfrak{h}
is not necessarily true, but it is true if
\mathfrak{g}
is a split real form.

Lemma 5.1
.

Suppose that
G
is a semisimple Lie group over
\mathbb{F}
such that
\mathfrak{g}_{0}=\mathfrak{h}
. If
\rho
is Cayley, then
\widetilde{d\rho}\left(S^{3}\mathfrak{h}\right)\subseteq d\rho(\mathfrak{h})
.

Proof.

By Propositions
4.3
and
4.10
, it is clear that
\widetilde{d\rho}\left(S^{3}\mathfrak{h}\right)\subseteq d\rho(\mathfrak{g})
. Next we split our discussion into two cases:

\diamond

\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
. Let
\mathbb{V}=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\mathbb{V}_{\lambda}
be the weight space decomposition. Here
\Phi
(resp.
\Lambda
) is the set of roots (resp. weight lattice) of
\mathfrak{g}
. By definition, we have

d\rho(x)(\mathbb{V}_{\lambda})\subseteq\begin{cases}\mathbb{V}_{\lambda+\alpha}&\text{~if~}x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\\
\mathbb{V}_{\lambda}&\text{~if~}x\in\mathfrak{h}\\
\end{cases}.

Since
d\rho(\mathfrak{g})=d\rho(\mathfrak{h})\bigoplus\left(\oplus_{\alpha\in\Phi}d\rho(g_{\alpha})\right)
and
\widetilde{d\rho}\left(S^{3}\mathfrak{h}\right)\subseteq d\rho(\mathfrak{g})
, we conclude that
\widetilde{d\rho}\left(S^{3}\mathfrak{h}\right)\subseteq d\rho(\mathfrak{h})
.

\diamond

\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
. We consider
\Ind(d\rho):\mathfrak{g}^{\mathbb{C}}\to\mathfrak{gl}(\mathbb{V})
. Since
\mathfrak{h}^{\mathbb{C}}
is a Cartan subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathbb{C}}
, by the argument for
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
we have

\widetilde{\Ind(d\rho)}\left(S^{3}\mathfrak{h}^{\mathbb{C}}\right)\subseteq\Ind(d\rho)\left(\mathfrak{h}^{\mathbb{C}}\right).

Therefore, we obtain

\widetilde{d\rho}\left(S^{3}\mathfrak{h}\right)\subseteq\widetilde{\Ind(d\rho)}\left(S^{3}\mathfrak{h}^{\mathbb{C}}\right)\bigcap d\rho(\mathfrak{g})\subseteq\Ind(d\rho)\left(\mathfrak{h}^{\mathbb{C}}\right)\bigcap d\rho(\mathfrak{g}).

By assumption, we have a decomposition
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{h}\bigoplus\mathfrak{l}
for some subspace
\mathfrak{l}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
. Thus,
d\rho(\mathfrak{g})=d\rho(\mathfrak{h})\oplus d\rho(\mathfrak{l})
and
\Ind(d\rho)\left(\mathfrak{h}^{\mathbb{C}}\right)\bigcap d\rho(\mathfrak{g})=d\rho(\mathfrak{h})
. ∎

As a consequence of Lemma
5.1
, we obtain the third characterization of the applicability of the Cayley transform.

Theorem 5.2

(Characterization III)
.

Let
G
be a semisimple Lie group over
\mathbb{F}
such that
\mathfrak{g}_{0}=\mathfrak{h}
and
\mathfrak{g}=\mathrm{Ad}(G)(\mathfrak{h})
where
\mathrm{Ad}:G\to\GL(\mathfrak{g})
is the adjoint representation of
G
. Let
\rho:G\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
be a representation of
G
. Then
\rho
is Cayley if and only if
\widetilde{d\rho}\left(S^{3}\mathfrak{h}\right)\subseteq d\rho(\mathfrak{h})
.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.9
and Lemma
5.1
, it suffices to prove that
\widetilde{d\rho}\left(S^{3}\mathfrak{h}\right)\subseteq d\rho(\mathfrak{h})
ensures the power span property of
\mathfrak{g}
. To this end, we notice that each
x\in\mathfrak{g}
can be written as
x=\mathrm{Ad}(g)H
for some
g\in G
and
H\in\mathfrak{h}
. Hence, we have
d\rho(x)=d\rho(\mathrm{Ad}(g)H)=\rho(g)\left(d\rho(H)\right)\rho(g)^{-1}
, which together with Proposition
4.10
leads to

d\rho(x)^{2k+1}=\rho(g)\left(d\rho(H)\right)^{2k+1}\rho(g)^{-1}\in\rho(g)d\rho(\mathfrak{h})\rho(g)^{-1}=d\rho(\mathrm{Ad}(g)(\mathfrak{h}))\subseteq d\rho(\mathfrak{g}).\qed

5.1.
The Cayley transform on irreducible representations

In this subsection, we consider the applicability of the Cayley transform on an irreducible representation of a semisimple Lie group. Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a semisimple Lie algebra over
\mathbb{F}
and let
\pi:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{gl}(\mathbb{V})
be a representation. We suppose that
\mathbb{V}=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\mathbb{V}_{\lambda}
is the weight space decomposition of
\mathbb{V}
, where
\Lambda
is the weight lattice of
\mathfrak{g}
. Moreover, we denote the set of simple roots of
\mathfrak{g}
by
\Delta^{+}
.

5.1.1.
Geometric condition

Since a finite dimensional irreducible representation of a semisimple Lie group are characterized by its highest weight, the applicability of the Cayley transform on this representation must impose a condition on the highest weight. To formulate this condition explicitly, we first establish the following three lemmas.

Lemma 5.3
.

We have
\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\dim(\mathbb{V}_{\lambda})\lambda=0
.

Proof.

For simplicity, we denote
\omega\coloneqq\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\dim(\mathbb{V}_{\lambda})\lambda
. Assume
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
. We proceed by contradiction. Suppose that
\omega\neq 0
. There exists some
H\in\mathfrak{h}
such that
\omega(H)\neq 0
. Since
\mathfrak{h}=\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{H_{\alpha}:\alpha\in\Delta^{+}\}
, we can find some
\beta\in\Delta^{+}
such that
\omega(H_{\beta})\neq 0
. Here
H_{\alpha}\in[\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha},\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha}]
is the co-root of
\alpha\in\Delta^{+}
. By definition,
H_{\beta}=[x_{\beta},x_{-\beta}]
for some
x_{\beta}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\beta}
and
x_{-\beta}\in\mathfrak{g}_{-\beta}
. Thus, we obtain a contradiction:

\omega(H_{\beta})=\mathrm{tr}(\pi(H_{\beta}))=\mathrm{tr}\left([\pi(x_{\beta}),\pi(x_{-\beta})]\right)=0.

For
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
, we observe that
\omega
remains unchanged if we pass from
\pi
to
\Ind\pi:\mathfrak{g}^{\mathbb{C}}\to\mathfrak{gl}(\mathbb{V})
. This implies that
\omega=0
and the proof is complete.
∎

For a subset
S
of a free Noetherian
\mathbb{Z}
-module
M
, we denote by
\mathbb{Z}S
the submodule of
M
generated by
S
. The
rank of
S
is defined by
\mathrm{rank}(S)\coloneqq\mathrm{rank}(\mathbb{Z}S)
. In particular,
\mathrm{rank}(S)
is equal to the
\mathbb{K}
-dimension of the subspace of
M\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{K}
spanned by
S
over any field
\mathbb{K}
of characteristic zero.

Lemma 5.4
.

If
\pi:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{gl}(\mathbb{V})
is an irreducible representation with the highest weight
\mu
and
\dim_{\mathbb{F}}(\pi(\mathfrak{h}))=\dim_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathfrak{h})
, then
\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{O}_{\mu})=\dim_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathfrak{h})
where
\mathcal{O}_{\mu}\coloneqq\{s(\mu):s\in\mathcal{W}\}
and
\mathcal{W}
is the Weyl group of
\mathfrak{g}
.

Proof.

Let
n\coloneqq\dim_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathfrak{h})
. Suppose that elements in
S\coloneqq\{\lambda\in\Lambda:\mathbb{V}_{\lambda}\neq 0\}
are
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m}
. We define

(2)

A=[\underbrace{\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{1}}_{\dim(\mathbb{V}_{\lambda_{1}})\text{~copies}},\dots,\underbrace{\lambda_{m},\dots,\lambda_{m}}_{\dim(\mathbb{V}_{\lambda_{m}})\text{~copies}}]\in\Lambda^{\dim(\mathbb{V})}.

If we regard each
\lambda\in\Lambda\subseteq\mathfrak{h}^{\ast}
as an
n

. By Theorem
2.1
, each element in

is a convex linear combination of elements in

. Therefore, we have
\mathrm{rank}(A)=\mathrm{rank}(S)=\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{O}_{\mu})

\pi(\mathfrak{h})=\left\{A(H):H\in\mathfrak{h}\right\}
, where

(3)

A(H)\coloneqq\diag\left(\underbrace{\lambda_{1}(H),\dots,\lambda_{1}(H)}_{\dim(\mathbb{V}_{\lambda_{1}})\text{~copies}},\dots,\underbrace{\lambda_{m}(H),\dots,\lambda_{m}(H)}_{\dim(\mathbb{V}_{\lambda_{m}})\text{~copies}}\right)\in\mathbb{C}^{\dim(\mathbb{V})\times\dim(\mathbb{V})}.

This implies that
\dim_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathfrak{h})=\dim_{\mathbb{F}}(\pi(\mathfrak{h}))\leq\mathrm{rank}(A)=\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{O}_{\mu})\leq\dim_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathfrak{h})
.
∎

Lemma 5.5
.

If
\pi:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{gl}(\mathbb{V})
is an irreducible representation such that
\widetilde{\pi}\left(S^{3}\mathfrak{h}\right)\subseteq\pi(\mathfrak{h})
and
\dim_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathfrak{h})=\dim_{\mathbb{F}}(\pi(\mathfrak{h}))\eqqcolon n
, then there exist linearly independent
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n}\in\Lambda
such that

\{\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n}\}\subseteq\{\lambda\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}:\mathbb{V}_{\lambda}\neq 0\}\subseteq\{\pm\omega_{1},\dots,\pm\omega_{n}\}.

Proof.

Suppose
\Delta^{+}=\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\}
. Let
H_{j}
be the co-root of
\alpha_{j}\in\Delta^{+}
for each
1\leq j\leq n
. Since
\{H_{1},\dots,H_{n}\}
is a basis of
\mathfrak{h}
, it has the dual basis
\{\omega^{\prime}_{1},\dots,\omega^{\prime}_{n}\}
. Assume
\{\lambda\in\Lambda:\mathbb{V}_{\lambda}\neq 0\}=\{\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m}\}
. By definition, there exists column vectors
a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
such that

(4)

A=\omega^{\prime}A^{\prime},\quad\omega^{\prime}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\omega^{\prime}_{1}&\cdots&\omega^{\prime}_{n}\end{bmatrix},\quad A^{\prime}\coloneqq[\underbrace{a_{1},\cdots,a_{1}}_{\dim(\mathbb{V}_{1})\text{~copies}},\cdots,\underbrace{a_{m},\cdots,a_{m}}_{\dim(\mathbb{V}_{m})\text{~copies}}]

where
A
is defined in (
2
). Clearly,
\omega^{\prime}(\mathfrak{h})\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}
consists of vectors
\omega^{\prime}(H)\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\omega^{\prime}_{1}(H),\cdots,\omega^{\prime}_{n}(H)\end{bmatrix}
for
H\in\mathfrak{h}
. In fact, we must have
\omega^{\prime}(\mathfrak{h})=\mathbb{C}^{n}
as
\mathrm{rank}(\omega^{\prime})=n
. Let
A_{0}\coloneqq[a_{1},\cdots,a_{m}]\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times m}
.

\diamond

\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
. According to the proof of Lemma
5.4
, we have
\mathrm{rank}(A_{0})=n
. We may re-index
\lambda_{j}
’s such that the left
n\times n
submatrix of
A_{0}
has the full rank. By Gaussian elimination, we obtain a decomposition
A_{0}=GB
where
G\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}
and

B=\begin{bmatrix}I_{n}&C\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&\cdots&0&c_{1,1}&\cdots&c_{1,m-n}\\
0&1&\cdots&0&c_{2,1}&\cdots&c_{2,m-n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&1&c_{n,1}&\cdots&c_{n,m-n}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n\times m}.

It is clear that we can construct
B^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n\times\dim(\mathbb{V})}
from

such that
A^{\prime}=GB^{\prime}
. We take
\omega\coloneqq\omega^{\prime}G\in\Lambda^{n}
. By (
4
) we have
A(H)=\diag(\omega^{\prime}(H)A^{\prime})=\diag(\omega(H)B^{\prime})
for any
H\in\mathfrak{h}
. Since
S^{3}\left(\pi(\mathfrak{h})\right)=\widetilde{\pi}\left(S^{3}\mathfrak{h}\right)\subseteq\pi(\mathfrak{h})
, (
3
) implies that
A(H)^{2}A(H^{\prime})\in\pi(\mathfrak{h})
for any
H,H^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{h}
. Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be the dual basis of
\omega_{1},\dots\omega_{n}
. Then by evaluating

we obtain

c_{js}^{3}=c_{js},\quad c_{js}^{2}c_{ks}=0,\quad 1\leq s\leq m-n,\;1\leq j<k\leq n.

Therefore,
c_{js}\in\{0,-1,1\}
for all
1\leq s\leq m-n
and
1\leq j\leq n
. Moreover, for each
1\leq s\leq m-n
there is at most one
1\leq j\leq n
such that

. By (
4
), we derive that
A=\omega B^{\prime}
where
B^{\prime}\in\{0,\pm 1\}^{n\times\dim(\mathbb{V})}
. By construction, we clearly have
\omega=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}
and this completes the proof.

\diamond

\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
. We notice that
\widetilde{\pi}\left(S^{3}\mathfrak{h}\right)\subseteq\pi\left(\mathfrak{h}\right)
implies

\widetilde{\Ind(\pi)}\left(S^{3}(\mathfrak{h}^{\mathbb{C}})\right)=\widetilde{\pi}\left(S^{3}\mathfrak{h}\right)\otimes\mathbb{C}\subseteq\pi\left(\mathfrak{h}\right)\otimes\mathbb{C}=\Ind(\pi)\left(\mathfrak{h}^{\mathbb{C}}\right).

Since weights of
\pi
are the same as those of

Theorem 5.6

(Geometric condition)
.

Suppose that
G
is a semisimple Lie group over
\mathbb{F}
such that
\mathfrak{g}_{0}=\mathfrak{h}
. Let
\rho:G\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
be an irreducible representation with the highest weight
\omega_{1}
such that
\dim_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathfrak{h})=\dim_{\mathbb{F}}(d\rho(\mathfrak{h}))\eqqcolon n
. If
\rho
is Cayley, then we have the following:

(a)

Either
\{\lambda\in\Lambda:V_{\lambda}\neq 0\}=\mathcal{O}_{\omega_{1}}
or
\{\lambda\in\Lambda:V_{\lambda}\neq 0\}=\mathcal{O}_{\omega_{1}}\sqcup\{0\}
. In particular, a weight of
\mathbb{V}
is either zero or an extreme weight.

(b)

\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{O}_{\omega_{1}})=n
.

(c)

There exist
\omega_{2},\dots,\omega_{n}\in\Lambda
such that
\mathcal{O}_{\omega_{1}}=\{\pm\omega_{1},\dots,\pm\omega_{n}\}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.4
, it is clear that (
b
) holds. We prove (
a
) and (
c
) at the same time. According to Lemmas
5.1
and
5.5
, there exist
\omega^{\prime}_{1},\omega_{2},\dots,\omega_{n}\in\Lambda
such that

(5)

\{\omega^{\prime}_{1},\dots,\omega_{n}\}\subseteq\{\lambda\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}:\mathbb{V}_{\lambda}\neq 0\}\subseteq\{\pm\omega^{\prime}_{1},\dots,\pm\omega_{n}\}.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
\omega^{\prime}_{1}
or
-\omega^{\prime}_{1}
is the highest weight of
\mathbb{V}
. By swapping
\Delta^{+}
and
\Delta^{-}
if necessary, we may further assume that
\omega^{\prime}_{1}=\omega_{1}
. For simplicity, we denote

\mathcal{O}\coloneqq\mathcal{O}_{\omega_{1}},\quad S\coloneqq\{\lambda\in\Lambda:\mathbb{V}_{\lambda}\neq 0\}.

We first claim that
\omega_{j}\in\mathcal{O}
for all
1\leq j\leq n
. Indeed, if on the contrary that
\omega_{j}\not\in\mathcal{O}
for some
j
, then we may assume that
\omega_{2}\not\in\mathcal{O}
. We write
\mathcal{O}=\{\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{m}\}
where
\mu_{1}=\omega_{1}
. We recall that
\mathcal{O}\subseteq S\setminus\{0\}
and
\omega_{2}\in S=\Conv(\mathcal{O})\cap\left(\omega_{1}+\mathbb{Z}\Phi\right)
where
\Conv(\mathcal{O})
is the convex hull of
\mathcal{O}
and
\mathbb{Z}\Phi
is the root lattice of
\mathfrak{g}
. Hence, we have

\omega_{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}\mu_{i},\quad\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}=1,\quad 0\leq c_{i}<1,\;1\leq i\leq m.

If
-\omega_{2}\notin\mathcal{O}
then
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\{\pm\omega_{1},\pm\omega_{3},\dots,\pm\omega_{n}\}
. This implies that
\omega_{2}\in S\subseteq\mathrm{span}\{\omega_{1},\omega_{3},\dots\omega_{n}\}
, which contradicts to the linear independence of
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n}
. If
-\omega_{2}\in\mathcal{O}
, then
-\omega_{2}=\mu_{j}
for some
1\leq j\leq n
. This leads to a contradiction again:

(1+c_{j})\omega_{2}=\sum_{i\neq j}c_{i}\mu_{i}\in\mathrm{span}\{\mu_{i}:1\leq i\neq j\leq m\}\subseteq\mathrm{span}\{\pm\omega_{1},\pm\omega_{3},\dots,\pm\omega_{n}\}.

Next, we prove that there is some
1\leq k\leq n
, such that
-\omega_{k}\in\mathcal{O}
. Otherwise, by (
5
) we must have
\mathcal{O}=\{\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n}\}
. Then
\mathcal{O}\subseteq S\subseteq\Conv(\mathcal{O})
and (
5
) imply
S\setminus\{0\}=\mathcal{O}
. The linear independence of
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n}
indicates that
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\omega_{n}\neq 0
. Since
\dim\mathbb{V}_{\omega_{j}}=1
for each
1\leq j\leq n
, this contradicts to Lemma
5.3
.

Lastly,
\pm\omega_{k}\in\mathcal{O}
if and only if
\pm\omega_{j}\in\mathcal{O}
for each
1\leq j\leq n
, since
\mathcal{O}
is an orbit of
\mathcal{W}
. Thus, (
5
) implies that
\mathcal{O}=\{\pm\omega_{1},\dots,\pm\omega_{n}\}=S\setminus\{0\}
. This completes the proof of (
a
) and (
c
).
∎

Theorem
5.6
characterizes the set of weights of an irreducible representation
\mathbb{V}
, on which the Cayley transform is applicable. As a direct consequence, one can also describe its weight diagram. We recall that the weight diagram of a representation
\mathbb{V}
is a pair
(V,m)
, where
V\coloneqq\{\lambda\in\Lambda:\mathbb{V}_{\lambda}\neq 0\}
and
m:V\to\mathbb{N}
is defined by
m(\lambda)=\dim V_{\lambda}
.

Corollary 5.7

(Weight diagram)
.

Let
(V,m)
be the weight diagram of
\mathbb{V}
. Then
V
is a
\mathcal{W}
-invariant subset
\Lambda\simeq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, which is not contained in any low rank sublattice. It is symmetric about the origin and has
2n
or
(2n+1)
vertices,
2n
of which form a
\mathcal{W}
-orbit and
m=1
on this orbit. In the latter case, the origin is the additional vertex.

Definition 5.8

(Cayley configuration)
.

An irreducible representation of a semisimiple Lie group is called a representation with Cayley configuration if its weight diagram has the configuration described in Corollary
5.7
.

Corollary 5.9

(Self-duality)
.

For each representation
\rho:G\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
with Cayley configuration, we have
\mathbb{V}\simeq\mathbb{V}^{\ast}
as representations of
G
. In particular, If
\mathfrak{g}_{0}=\mathfrak{h}
and
\rho:G\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
is an irreducible representation such that
\dim_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathfrak{h})=\dim_{\mathbb{F}}(d\rho(\mathfrak{h}))
and
\rho
is Cayley, then
\mathbb{V}
is self-dual.

Proof.

By definition and the symmetry of the weight diagram of a representation with Cayley configuration, we have

\{\lambda\in\Lambda:\mathbb{V}_{\lambda}\neq 0\}=-\{\lambda\in\Lambda:(\mathbb{V}^{\ast})_{\lambda}\neq 0\}=\{\lambda\in\Lambda:(\mathbb{V}^{\ast})_{\lambda}\neq 0\},

from which we conclude that
\mathbb{V}\simeq\mathbb{V}^{\ast}
.
∎

We recall from Section
4
that
\Out(G)
acts on
R(G)
. By Theorem
2.1
, this induces an action of
\Out(G)
on the weight lattice
\Lambda
. In particular,
\Out(G)
also acts on the subset
R_{0}(G)
consisting of isomorphism classes of irreducible representations. The following observation is obvious .

Corollary 5.10

(Stability II)
.

If
\mathbb{V}\in R_{0}(G)
is a representation with Cayley configuration, then for each
\varphi\in\Out(G)
,
\varphi(\mathbb{V})
is also a representation with Cayley configuration.

5.1.2.
The sufficiency of the geometric condition

Theorem
5.6
(or equivalently, Corollary
5.7
) is a necessary condition for the applicability of the Cayley transform on an irreducible representation. Next we investigate its sufficiency. We begin with the existence of a representation with Cayley configuration.

Lemma 5.11

(Existence of representation with Cayley configuration)
.

Let
G
be a simply connected semisimple Lie group over
\mathbb{F}
and let
\mathcal{O}
be the
\mathcal{W}
-orbit of
\omega_{1}\in\Lambda
. Suppose that
\mathcal{O}

\Conv(\mathcal{O})\cap\left(\omega_{1}+\mathbb{Z}\Phi\right)\subseteq\mathcal{O}\cup\{0\}

\rho:G\to\GL(\mathbb{V})

a)

\{\lambda\in\Lambda:\mathbb{V}_{\lambda}\neq 0\}=\Conv(\mathcal{O})\cap(\omega_{0}+\mathbb{Z}\Phi)
.

b)

\dim\left(d\rho(\mathfrak{h})\right)=n
.

\widetilde{d\rho}\left(S^{3}\mathfrak{h}\right)\subseteq d\rho(\mathfrak{h})
for
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
and
\widetilde{d\Ind(\rho)}\left(S^{3}\mathfrak{h}^{\mathbb{C}}\right)\subseteq d\Ind(\rho)(\mathfrak{h}^{\mathbb{C}})
for
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}

Proof.

If such an irreducible representation exists, then its uniqueness follows immediately. Since
\mathcal{O}
is symmetric about the origin and
|\mathcal{O}|=2n
, we may write
\mathcal{O}=\{\pm\omega_{1},\dots,\pm\omega_{n}\}
. The assumption that
\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{O})=n
,
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n}
are linearly independent. Let
C
be the closed Weyl chamber of
\Lambda
containing
\omega_{1}
. By definition,
\Lambda=\mathcal{W}C
and
\omega_{1}\in\mathcal{O}\cap C
. Let
\rho:G\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
be the irreducible representation associated to
\omega_{1}
. We have

\mathcal{O}\subseteq\{\lambda\in\Lambda:\mathbb{V}_{\lambda}\neq 0\}=\Conv(\mathcal{O})\cap(\omega_{0}+\mathbb{Z}\Phi)\subseteq\mathcal{O}\cup\{0\}.

\diamond

\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
. Let
H_{1},\dots,H_{n}\in\mathfrak{h}
be the dual basis of
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n}
. It is obvious that
\omega_{j}(H_{k})^{3}=\omega_{j}(H_{k})
for any

. For each

, we write

. Therefore, by (
3
) we obtain that
\dim\left(d\rho(\mathfrak{h})\right)=n
and

\left(d\rho(H)\right)^{3}=\begin{cases}\diag\left(c^{3}_{1},-c^{3}_{1},\dots,c^{3}_{n},-c^{3}_{n}\right)&\text{~if~}\Conv(\mathcal{O})\cap(\omega_{0}+\mathbb{Z}\Phi)=\mathcal{O}\\
\diag\left(c^{3}_{1},-c^{3}_{1},\dots,c^{3}_{n},-c^{3}_{n},0\right)&\text{~otherwise}\end{cases}.

This implies that
\widetilde{d\rho}\left(S^{3}\mathfrak{h}\right)\subseteq d\rho(\mathfrak{h})
.

\diamond

\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
. Let
\rho^{\mathbb{C}}:G^{\mathbb{C}}\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
be the representation satisfying (
a
)–(
c
) for
G^{\mathbb{C}}
. By construction, we have
\{\lambda\in\Lambda:\mathbb{V}_{\lambda}\neq 0\}=\Conv(\mathcal{O})\cap(\omega_{0}+\mathbb{Z}\Phi)
. It is clear that
\rho=\Res(\rho^{\mathbb{C}}):G\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
is the desired representation since
\Ind\left(\Res\left(\rho^{\mathbb{C}}\right)\right)=\rho^{\mathbb{C}}
. ∎

Proposition 5.12
.

Let
G
be a simply connected compact or split real form of the complex semisimple Lie group

\mathcal{O}
be the
\mathcal{W}
-orbit of

\mathcal{O}

\Conv(\mathcal{O})\cap\left(\omega_{1}+\mathbb{Z}\Phi\right)\subseteq\mathcal{O}\cup\{0\}

\rho:G\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
such that

a)

\{\lambda\in\Lambda:\mathbb{V}_{\lambda}\neq 0\}=\Conv(\mathcal{O})\cap(\omega_{0}+\mathbb{Z}\Phi)
.

\dim\left(d\rho(\mathfrak{h})\right)=n
.

\widetilde{d\rho}\left(S^{3}\mathfrak{h}\right)\subseteq d\rho(\mathfrak{h})

Let

\lambda(H)\in\mathbb{R}

Proposition 5.13

(Characterization IV)
.

Let
G
be a compact semisimple Lie group and let

be an irreducible representation such that

is faithful. The followings are equivalent:

is a Cayley representation.

(b)

\widetilde{d\rho}\left(S^{3}\mathfrak{h}\right)\subseteq d\rho(\mathfrak{h})

\rho
is a representation with Cayley configuration.

is compact and simple then (

Since
G
is compact, we have
\mathfrak{g}_{0}=\mathfrak{h}
. Moreover, if

is also simple then all its non-trivial representations are faithful. By Proposition
3.4
, the equivalence between (
a
) and (

) follows from Theorem
5.2
. Thus, it is left to establish the equivalence between (
b
) and (

). If (
b
) holds, then
\rho
is Cayley and Theorem
5.6
leads to (

). Conversely, we consider the universal covering
\pi:\widetilde{G}\to G

is a representation of

implies that (

We notice that Proposition

is true for any semisimple Lie group

such that

and
\mathfrak{g}=\mathrm{Ad}(G)(\mathfrak{h})
. If

or

always holds. However, the next proposition implies that it is impossible to have

in these two cases.

Proposition 5.14
.

If
G
is either a complex semisimple Lie group or a split real form, then there exists
x\in\mathfrak{g}
such that
x\notin\mathrm{Ad}(G)(\mathfrak{h})
.

Proof.

If
G
is either a complex semisimple Lie group or a split real form, then we have the root space decomposition
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{h}\bigoplus\left(\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Phi}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\right)
. Let
\mathrm{Ad}:G\to\GL(\mathfrak{g})
(resp.
\mathrm{ad}:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{g})
) be the adjoint representation of
G
(resp.
\mathfrak{g}
). Given
\alpha\in\Phi
, we pick a nonzero
X_{\alpha}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
. Since
\mathrm{ad}(X_{\alpha})(\mathfrak{g}_{\beta})\subseteq\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha+\beta}
for any
\beta\in\Phi
and
\mathfrak{g}
is finite dimensional, there exists some integer

such that
\mathrm{ad}(X_{\alpha})^{N}(\mathfrak{g}_{\beta})=0
for any
\beta\in\Phi
. Therefore, if
X_{\alpha}=\mathrm{Ad}(g)(H)
for some

and

, then

0=\mathrm{ad}(X_{\alpha})^{N}=\mathrm{ad}\left(\mathrm{Ad}(g)(H)\right)^{N}=\left(\mathrm{Ad}(g)\mathrm{ad}(H)\mathrm{Ad}(g)^{-1}\right)^{N}=\mathrm{Ad}(g)\mathrm{ad}(H)^{N}\mathrm{Ad}(g)^{-1}.

This implies that
\mathrm{ad}(H)=0
. Since
\mathrm{ad}
is injective, we obtain
X_{\alpha}=H=0
which contradicts to the choice of
X_{\alpha}
.
∎

For a concrete example, we consider

. Then
\mathfrak{h}=\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{F}}\{H\}
where

. It is obvious that
\mathrm{Ad}(\SL_{2}(\mathbb{F}))(\mathfrak{h})\neq\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{F})
as

where

0&0\end{bmatrix}
.

5.2.
The Cayley transform on general representations

In this subsection, we discuss the applicability of the Cayley transform on an arbitrary representation of a semisimple Lie group
G
. We recall that every finite dimensional representation
\rho:G\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
admits a decomposition
\rho=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\rho_{j}:G\to\prod_{j=1}^{m}\GL(\mathbb{V}_{j})
where
\mathbb{V}=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{V}_{j}
and for each
1\leq j\leq m
,
\rho_{j}:G\to\GL(\mathbb{V}_{j})
is an irreducible representation.

Proposition 5.15

(Characterization V)
.

Let
G,\mathbb{V},\rho,\rho_{1},\dots,\rho_{m}
be as above. The followings are equivalent:

(a)

\rho
is Cayley.

(b)

For each
1\leq j\leq m
,
\rho_{j}
is Cayley.

(c)

There exists a semisimple Lie group
G^{\prime}
and a representation
\rho^{\prime}:G^{\prime}\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
such that
d\rho^{\prime}
is faithful,
d\rho^{\prime}(\mathfrak{g}^{\prime})=d\rho(\mathfrak{g})
and
\rho^{\prime}
is Cayley.

(d)

There exists a semisimple Lie group
G^{\prime}
and a faithful representation
\rho^{\prime}:G^{\prime}\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
such that
d\rho^{\prime}(\mathfrak{g}^{\prime})=d\rho(\mathfrak{g})
and
\rho^{\prime}
is Cayley.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.9
,
\rho
is Cayley if and only if
d\rho(\mathfrak{g})
has the power span property. The implication (
c
)
\implies
(
a
) is obvious. If (
a
) holds, we let
G^{\prime}
be the simply connected Lie group such that
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}=d\rho(\mathfrak{g})\subseteq\mathfrak{gl}(\mathbb{V})
and let
\rho^{\prime}:G^{\prime}\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
be the corresponding representation of
G^{\prime}
on
\mathbb{V}
. Then it is clear that
\rho^{\prime}
is Cayley, thus we have (
a
)
\implies
(
c
).

Since
\rho=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{m}\rho_{j}
, we have
d\rho(\mathfrak{g})=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{m}d\rho_{j}(\mathfrak{g})
. Therefore,
d\rho(\mathfrak{g})
has the power span property if and only if for each
1\leq j\leq m
,
d\rho_{j}(\mathfrak{g})
has the power span property which is equivalent to the applicability of the Cayley transform to
\rho_{j}
, by Theorem
4.9
again. This proves the equivalence between (
a
) and (
b
).

The implication (
d
)
\implies
(
c
) is trivial. Suppose
G_{1}
and
\theta:G_{1}\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
satisfy conditions in (
c
). Since
d\theta
is faithful,
\ker(\theta)
is a discrete normal subgroup of
G_{1}
. Denote
G^{\prime}\coloneqq G_{1}/\ker(\theta)
. We define
\rho^{\prime}:G^{\prime}\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
by
\rho^{\prime}\left([g_{1}]\right)\coloneqq\theta(g_{1})
where
[g_{1}]
denotes the element in
G^{\prime}
represented by
g_{1}\in G_{1}
. By definition,
\rho^{\prime}
is well-defined and faithful. We also observe that
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}=\mathfrak{g}_{1}
and
d\rho^{\prime}=d\theta
. Therefore,
\rho^{\prime}
is a representation satisfying (
d
).
∎

According to Proposition
5.15
, the applicability of the Cayley transform to
\rho:G\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
reduces to the applicability of the Cayley transform to
\rho_{j}^{\prime}:G^{\prime}\to\GL(\mathbb{V}_{j})
where
\rho^{\prime}=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{m}\rho^{\prime}_{j}
,
\mathbb{V}=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{V}_{j}
and for each
1\leq j\leq m
,
\rho^{\prime}_{j}:G^{\prime}\to\GL(\mathbb{V}_{j})
is an irreducible faithful representation. Moreover, we observe that
\widetilde{d\rho}\left(S^{3}\mathfrak{h}\right)\subseteq d\rho(\mathfrak{h})
if and onlyf if
\widetilde{d\rho_{j}}\left(S^{3}\mathfrak{h}\right)\subseteq d\rho_{j}(\mathfrak{h})
,
1\leq j\leq m
. This together with Theorem
5.6
and Proposition
5.13
leads to the proposition that follows.

Proposition 5.16

(Characterization VI)
.

Let
G,G^{\prime}
,
\mathbb{V},\mathbb{V}_{1},\dots,\mathbb{V}_{m}
and
\rho,\rho^{\prime},\rho^{\prime}_{1},\dots,\rho^{\prime}_{m}
be as above. The followings hold:

(a)

If
\mathfrak{g}_{0}=\mathfrak{h}
and
\rho
is Cayley, then for each
1\leq j\leq m
,
\rho^{\prime}_{j}
is a representation with Cayley configuration.

(b)

For a compact semisimple Lie group
G
, the followings are equivalent:

(i)

\rho
is Cayley.

(ii)

\widetilde{d\rho}\left(S^{3}\mathfrak{h}\right)\subseteq d\rho(\mathfrak{h})
.

(iii)

For each
1\leq j\leq m
,
\rho^{\prime}_{j}
is a representation with Cayley configuration.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
G
be a complex simple Lie group of type
A_{n}
and let
\rho:G\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
be a representation with Cayley configuration. If
\mathbb{V}_{0}\neq 0
, then
n=1
and
\rho
is the adjoint representation.

Proof.

By definition,
S\coloneqq\{\lambda\in\Lambda:\mathbb{V}_{\lambda}\neq 0\}=\mathcal{O}\bigsqcup\{0\}
where
\mathcal{O}\coloneqq\mathcal{W}\omega_{1}=\{\pm\omega_{1},\dots,\pm\omega_{n}\}
has rank
n
and
\omega_{1}
is the highest weight of
\mathbb{V}
. Since
S=(\omega_{1}+\mathbb{Z}\Phi)\cap\Conv(\mathcal{O})
, we must have
-\omega_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}\Phi
and hence
\omega_{1}\in\mathbb{N}\Delta^{+}
. We notice that
\mathcal{O}
is the
\mathcal{W}
-orbit of the highest weight
\omega_{1}
. Thus, elements in
\mathcal{O}
belong to different Weyl chambers. This implies that
0
and
\omega_{1}
are the only weights of
\mathbb{V}
contained in the fundamental Weyl chamber
C
. Therefore, we may conclude that
\omega_{1}\in\Delta^{+}
. According to Table
1
,
\mathcal{W}
acts transitively on
\Phi
, from which we obtain
\mathcal{O}=\Phi
. Since both
\rho
and the adjoint representation are irreducible, we conclude that they are isomorphic. By the definition of the adjoint representation, we have
(n+1)^{2}-(n+1)=\dim(\mathfrak{g})-\dim(\mathfrak{h})=|\Phi|=|\mathcal{O}|=2n
which implies
n=1
.
∎

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
G
be a complex simple Lie group of type
A_{1}
and let
\rho:G\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
be a representation with Cayley configuration. Then
\rho
is either the standard representation or the adjoint representation.

Proof.

Let
\mathbb{C}^{2}
be the standard representation of
G\simeq\SL_{2}(\mathbb{C})
. Then any irreducible representation of
G
is isomorphic to
\mathsf{S}^{d}\mathbb{C}^{2}
for some positive integer
d
. Since the set of weights of
\mathsf{S}^{d}\mathbb{C}^{2}
is
\{d-2k\}_{k=0}^{d}
, it is straightforward to verify that
\mathsf{S}^{d}\mathbb{C}^{2}
is a representation with Cayley configuration if and only if
d=1
(the standard representation) or
d=2
(the adjoint representation).
∎

Lemma 6.3
.

If
G
is a complex simple Lie group of type
A_{2}
, then
G
has no representation with Cayley configuration.

Proof.

By Table
1
, we have
\dim(\mathfrak{h})=2
and
\mathcal{W}=\mathfrak{S}_{3}
. Since the cardinality of any
\mathcal{W}
-orbit divides
|\mathcal{W}|=6
, there does not exist an orbit of cardinality
4=2\dim(\mathfrak{h})
. This implies the non-existence of a representation with Cayley configuration.
∎

Lemma 6.4
.

If
G
is a complex simple Lie group of type
A_{n}
and
\rho:G\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
is a representation with Cayley configuration, then one of the followings holds:

(a)

n=1
and
\mathbb{V}
is the standard representation
\mathbb{C}^{2}
.

(b)

n=1
and
\mathbb{V}
is the adjoint representation
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})\simeq\mathsf{S}^{2}\mathbb{C}^{2}
.

(c)

n=3
and
\mathbb{V}=\mathsf{\Lambda}^{2}\mathbb{C}^{4}
.

Proof.

By Lemmas
6.1
–
6.3
, we may assume that
0
is not a weight of
\mathbb{V}
and
n\geq 3
. Let
\omega_{1}
be the highest weight of
\mathbb{V}
. We may write
\omega_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}L_{j}
for some integers
a_{1}\geq\cdots\geq a_{n}\geq 0
. Suppose
\sigma\in\mathcal{W}
is the reflection across the hyperplane perpendicular to
L_{1}-L_{n+1}
. Then
\sigma
interchanges
L1
and
L_{n+1}
and fixes
L_{2},\dots,L_{n}
. Hence we have

\sigma(\omega_{1})=\sum_{j=2}^{n}a_{j}L_{j}+a_{1}L_{n+1},\quad\sigma(\omega_{1})-\omega_{1}=a_{1}(L_{n+1}-L_{1}).

Since
L_{n+1}-L_{1}
is a root and both
\omega_{1}
and
\sigma(\omega_{1})
are weights of
\mathbb{V}
,
\omega_{1}+k(L_{n+1}-L_{1})
is also a weight of
\mathbb{V}
for
0\leq k\leq a_{1}
. By the definition of a representation with Cayley configuration, we must have
a_{1}=1
. Hence there exists some positive integer
k\leq n
such that
\omega_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}L_{j}
. This implies that

\mathcal{W}\omega_{1}=\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{k}\omega_{s_{j}}:1\leq s_{j}\leq n+1,\;s_{j}\neq s_{j^{\prime}},\;1\leq j\neq j^{\prime}\leq k\right\}.

Therefore, we have
\binom{n+1}{k}=|\mathcal{W}\omega_{1}|=2\dim(\mathfrak{h})=2n
, which forces
(n,k)=(3,2)
as
n\geq 3
. Now that
G\simeq\SL_{4}(\mathbb{C})
and
\omega_{1}=L_{1}+L_{2}
, we have
\mathbb{V}=\mathsf{\Lambda}^{2}\mathbb{C}^{4}
.
∎

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
G
be a complex simple Lie group of type
B_{n}
(
n\geq 2
) and let
\rho:G\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
be a representation with Cayley configuration such that
\mathbb{V}_{0}\neq 0
. Then
\rho
is the standard representation.

Proof.

By definition,
S\coloneqq\{\lambda\in\Lambda:\mathbb{V}_{\lambda}\neq 0\}=\mathcal{O}\bigsqcup\{0\}
where
\mathcal{O}\coloneqq\mathcal{W}\omega_{1}=\{\pm\omega_{1},\dots,\pm\omega_{n}\}
has rank
n
and
\omega_{1}
is the highest weight of
\mathbb{V}
. By the same argument as in the proof of Lemma
6.1
, we obtain that
\omega_{1}\in\Delta^{+}
. According to Table
1
, we have

\Phi=\mathcal{W}L_{n}\bigsqcup\mathcal{W}(L_{1}-L_{2}),

from which we may conclude that either
\omega_{1}=L_{n}
or
\omega_{1}=L_{1}-L_{2}
. By the irreducibility of
\mathbb{V}
,
\rho
is either the standard representation or the adjoint representation. To exclude the latter, we notice that

implies

\dim(\mathfrak{g})-\dim(\mathfrak{h})=n(2n+1)-n>2n=2\dim(\mathfrak{h}).\qed

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
G
be a complex simple Lie group of type
B_{n}
(
n\geq 2
) and let
\rho:G\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
be a representation with Cayley configuration. Then
\rho
is either the standard representation or
n=2
and
\rho
is the spin representation.

Proof.

By Lemma
6.5
, it is sufficient to consider the case
\mathbb{V}_{0}=0
. Let
\omega_{1}
be the highest weight of
\mathbb{V}
. We may write
\omega_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}L_{j}
from some integers
a_{1}\geq\cdots a_{n}\geq 0
of the same parity. Let
\sigma\in\mathcal{W}
be the reflection such that
\sigma(L_{1})=-L_{1}
and
\sigma(L_{j})=L_{j}
if
2\leq j\leq n
. We have
\sigma(\omega_{1})-\omega_{1}=-a_{1}L_{1}
. The same argument as in the proof of Lemma
6.4
implies
a_{1}=\cdots=a_{n}=1
and
|\mathcal{W}\omega_{1}|=2^{n}>2n=2\dim(\mathfrak{h})
, which contradicts to the assumption that
\mathbb{V}
is a representation with Cayley configuration when
n\geq 3
. If
n=2
, then
\omega_{1}=\frac{1}{2}(L_{1}+L_{2})
and
\mathbb{V}
is the spin representation by definition.
∎

Repeating the argument for

Lemma 6.9
.

Let
G
be a complex simple Lie group of type
C_{n}
(
n\geq 3
) and let
\rho:G\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
be a representation with Cayley configuration. Then we have
\mathbb{V}_{0}\neq 0
.

We recall that a
spinorial representation
is an irreducible representation
\rho:G\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
with the highest weight
\omega=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}L_{i}
where
a_{1}\geq\cdots\geq a_{n-1}\geq|a_{n}|
are odd. Moreover, a
spinor
representations is an irreducible representation with the highest weight
\omega_{+}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}L_{j}
or
\omega_{-}\coloneqq\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}L_{j}\right)-L_{n}
.

Lemma 6.10
.

Let
G
be a complex simple Lie group of type
D_{n}
(
n\geq 4
) and let
\rho:G\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
be a representation with Cayley configuration. If
\rho
is spinorial, then
n=4
and
\rho
is a spinor representation.

Proof.

We first prove that
\rho
is a spinor representation. We only sketch the proof since it is similar to that of Lemma
6.6
. Let
\omega_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}L_{j}
be the highest weight of
\rho
, where
a_{1}\geq\cdots\geq a_{n-1}\geq|a_{n}|\geq 0
are some odd integers. For
2\leq j\leq n
, we let
\sigma_{j}\in\mathcal{W}
be the reflection that fixes
L_{k}
for
k\neq 1,j
and interchanges
L_{1}
and
L_{j}
. Then we have
\sigma_{j}(\omega_{1})-\omega_{1}=\frac{1}{2}(a_{j}-a_{1})(L_{1}-L_{j})
. Hence we may obtain that
a_{1}-a_{j}\leq 2
. On the other side, we let
\tau_{j}
be the reflection such that

\tau_{j}(L_{1})=-L_{j},\;\tau_{j}(L_{j})=-L_{1},\;\tau_{j}(L_{k})=L_{k},\quad 2\leq k\neq j\leq n.

We obtain
\tau_{j}(\omega_{1})-\omega_{1}=-\frac{1}{2}(a_{1}+a_{j})(L_{1}+L_{j})
from which
a_{1}+a_{j}\leq 2
. This implies
a_{k}=1
if
1\leq k\leq n-1
and
a_{n}=\pm 1
. Hence
\rho
is a spinor representation.

Next we prove that
n=4
. By Table
1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}\omega_{+}

\displaystyle=\left\{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}L_{j}:|a_{j}|=1,\;\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\equiv n\pmod{4},\;1\leq j\leq n\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}\omega_{-}

\displaystyle=\left\{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}L_{j}:|a_{j}|=1,\;\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\not\equiv n\pmod{4},\;1\leq j\leq n\right\}.

Since
\mathbb{V}
is a representation with Cayley configuration and spinorial, we have either
|\mathcal{W}\omega_{+}|=2\dim(\mathfrak{h})
or
|\mathcal{W}\omega_{-}|=2\dim(\mathfrak{h})
. This forces
2^{n-1}=2n
and hence
n=4
.

Lastly, we notice that if
n=4
then

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}\omega_{+}

\displaystyle=\left\{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{4}a_{j}L_{j}:|a_{j}|=1,\;\sum_{j=1}^{4}a_{j}\in\{-4,0,4\},\;1\leq j\leq 4\right\},

\displaystyle W\omega_{-}

\displaystyle=\left\{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{4}a_{j}L_{j}:|a_{j}|=1,\;\sum_{j=1}^{4}a_{j}\in\{-3,-1,1,3\},\;1\leq j\leq 4\right\}.

It is straightforward to verify that such spinor representations are representations with Cayley configuration.
∎

Now we are able to determine all representations with Cayley configuration.

Lemma 6.11
.

Let
G
be a simply-connected complex simple Lie group of type
D_{n}
(
n\geq 4
) and let
\rho:G\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
be a representation with Cayley configuration. Then either
\rho
is the standard representation or
n=4
and
\rho
is one of the two spinor representations.

Proof.

By Lemma
6.10
, it suffices to consider irreducible representations that are not spinorial. The rest of the proof is similar to that of Lemma
6.10
. We write the highest weight
\omega_{1}
of
\mathbb{V}
as
\omega_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}L_{j}
where
a_{1}\geq\cdots\geq a_{n-1}\geq|a_{n}|\geq 0
are some integers. For

, we may again choose different reflections to prove that
a_{1}\pm a_{j}\leq 1
, which implies that
a_{1}=1
and
a_{j}=0
for each

. According to Lemma
6.9
,

is the standard representation.
∎

6.2.
Classification

In Subsection
6.1
, we classify all representations with Cayley configuration of classical complex simple Lie groups. In this Subsection, we further classify Cayley representations for classical complex simple Lie groups and their compact real forms. To achieve the goal, we first recall isomorphisms among complex simple Lie algebras of small dimension.

Remark 6.12
.

We recall that for small dimensional complex simple Lie algebras, there are some isomorphisms among them:

(a)

\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})\simeq\mathfrak{so}_{3}(\mathbb{C})\simeq\mathfrak{sp}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
.

(b)

\mathfrak{sl}_{4}(\mathbb{C})\simeq\mathfrak{so}_{6}(\mathbb{C})
.

(c)

\mathfrak{so}_{5}(\mathbb{C})\simeq\mathfrak{sp}_{4}(\mathbb{C})
.

Moreover,
\mathfrak{so}_{2}(\mathbb{C})\simeq\mathbb{C}
and
\mathfrak{so}_{4}(\mathbb{C})\simeq\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})\times\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
are not simple.

Theorem 6.13

(Classification I)
.

Let
G
be a classical complex simple Lie group and let
\rho:G\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
be an irreducible representation. Suppose
\mathfrak{g}
is the Lie algebra of
G
. Then the followings are equivalent

(a)

\rho
is a representation with Cayley configuration.

(b)

\rho
is a Cayley representation.

(c)

One of the followings holds:

(i)

\mathfrak{g}\simeq\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
and
\mathbb{V}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{2}
. Under the identification

(v)

(vi)

\mathfrak{g}\simeq\mathfrak{sp}_{2n}(\mathbb{C})

(vii)

(viii)

\mathfrak{g}\simeq\mathfrak{so}_{2n}(\mathbb{C})

(d)

For any

Theorem 6.15

(Classification II)
.

Let
G
be a compact real Lie group and let
\rho:G\to\GL(\mathbb{V})
be an irreducible representation of
G
.
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
. Suppose
G
is simple. Let
\mathbb{V}=\oplus\mathbb{V}_{j}
be the irreducible decomposition. The Cayley transform is applicable to
\rho
if and only if for each
j
,
(\mathfrak{g},\mathbb{V}_{j})
belongs to the following list

(a)

(\mathfrak{su}_{2}(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{C}^{2})
. Under the identification
\mathfrak{su}_{2}(\mathbb{C})\simeq\mathfrak{so}_{3}(\mathbb{R})
,
\mathbb{V}
is isomorphic to the spinor representation.

(b)

\mathfrak{su}_{2}(\mathbb{C}),\mathfrak{su}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
. Under the identification
\mathfrak{su}_{2}(\mathbb{C})\simeq\mathfrak{so}_{3}(\mathbb{R})
, we have
\mathbb{V}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{3}
.

(c)

(\mathfrak{su}_{4}(\mathbb{C}),\mathsf{\Lambda}^{2}\mathbb{C}^{4})
. Under the identification
\mathfrak{su}_{4}(\mathbb{C})\simeq\mathfrak{so}_{6}(\mathbb{R})
, we have
\mathbb{V}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{6}
.

(d)

(\mathfrak{so}_{2n+1}(\mathbb{R}),\mathbb{C}^{2n+1})
(
n\geq 2
).

(e)

(\mathfrak{so}_{5}(\mathbb{R}),\mathbb{S})
where
\mathbb{S}
is the spinor representation. Under the identification
\mathfrak{so}_{5}(\mathbb{R})\simeq\mathfrak{u}_{2}(\mathbb{H})
, we have
\mathbb{V}\simeq\mathbb{H}^{2}
. Here
\mathbb{H}
denotes the quaternion algebra.

(f)

(\mathfrak{u}_{n}(\mathbb{H}),\mathbb{H}^{n})
(
n\geq 3
).

(g)

(\mathfrak{so}_{8}(\mathbb{R}),\mathbb{S}_{+})
or
(\mathfrak{so}_{8}(\mathbb{R}),\mathbb{S}_{-})
, where
\mathbb{S}_{+}
and
\mathbb{S}_{-}
are the two spinor representations.

(h)

(\mathfrak{so}_{2n}(\mathbb{R}),\mathbb{C}^{2n})
(
n\geq 4
).

Proof.

The list can be easily obtained by Proposition
5.16
, Lemmas
6.4
,
6.6
,
6.8
,
6.11
, Remarks
4.13
and

∎

Corollary 6.16
.

Let
G
be a simple Lie group over
\mathbb{F}
such that
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathbb{C}}\neq\mathfrak{spin}_{8}(\mathbb{C})
(
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
) and
\mathfrak{g}\neq\mathfrak{spin}_{8}(\mathbb{C})
