Tensor-based Dinkelbach method for computing generalized tensor eigenvalues and its applications
Haibin Chen, Wenqi Zhu, Coralia Cartis

Abstract
In this paper, we propose a novel tensor-based Dinkelbach–Type method for computing extremal tensor generalized eigenvalues. We show that the extremal tensor generalized eigenvalue can be reformulated as a critical subproblem of the classical Dinkelbach–Type method, which can subsequently be expressed as a multilinear optimization problem (MOP). The MOP is solved under a spherical constraint using an efficient proximal alternative minimization method which we rigorously establish the global convergence. Additionally, the equivalent MOP is reformulated as an unconstrained optimization problem, allowing for the analysis of the Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz (KL) exponent and providing an explicit expression for the convergence rate of the proposed algorithm. Preliminary numerical experiments on solving extremal tensor generalized eigenvalues and minimizing high-order trust-region subproblems are provided, validating the efficacy and practical utility of the proposed method.

Introduction

With the growing research interest in efficient methods for processing large-scale and high-dimensional datasets, researchers have been actively developing algorithms for analyzing high-order tensors, which are multi-dimensional arrays of data. As a fundamental component of tensor analysis, tensor eigenvalues, and eigenvectors, along with their potential applications and computational methods, have been extensively studied in the literature, for example, in
[
9
,

,
34
]
.
Several types of tensor eigenvalues have been introduced through different generalizations of the matrix case, each with specific applications. These include Z-eigenvalues
[

,
35
]
, H-eigenvalues
[
14
,
35
]
, D-eigenvalues, and others
[

The concept of tensor generalized eigenvalues was defined by Chang, Pearson, and Zhang
[

]
. For given

and

\displaystyle{\cal A}{\bf x}^{m-1}=\lambda{\cal B}{\bf x}^{m-1}.

(1)

A key advantage of the generalized eigenpair framework is its flexibility: by appropriately choosing
{\cal B}
, it can encompass multiple definitions of tensor eigenvalues, such as Z-eigenvalues, H-eigenvalues, and D-eigenvalues. For further details on selecting
{\cal B}
, we have constructed a summary table in Appendix

Computing eigenvalues of high-order tensors is an NP-hard problem, even for tensors with special structures
[
19
,
33
]
. Consequently, researchers have primarily focused on computing their extremal eigenvalues. To the best of our knowledge, various numerical algorithms have been developed to compute extremal Z-eigenvalues, H-eigenvalues, or D-eigenvalues. These include the high-order power method
[
33
]
, the SOS polynomial algorithm
[
19
]
, and the Newton method, among others
[
17
]
. However, when

is even and

is positive definite, the well-known numerical method for computing generalized tensor eigenvalues is the generalized eigenproblem adaptive power (GEAP) method, proposed by Kolda et al. in
[
25
]
. After that, an adaptive gradient (AG) approach was presented by Yu et al. in 2016 to establish both global convergence and linear

[
44
]
. Moreover, for the generalized eigenvalue of tensors with certain special structures, Zhao et al. proposed two convergent gradient projection techniques to address weakly symmetric tensors
[
46
]
. Very recently,
a normalized Newton method named NNGEM was presented to compute the generalized tensor eigenvalue problem in
[
30
]
. It was proved that
the NNGEM approach has a higher rate of convergence than the AP and GEAM approaches. Note that, the algorithms above mainly focus on solving as many eigenpairs as possible, rather than the extreme eigenpairs.

In this paper, we reformulate the extremal generalized tensor eigenvalue problem in (
1
) as a subproblem of the classical Dinkelbach–Type method.
We outline this reformulation through the following analysis. By taking the dot product with
{\bf x}
on both sides of (
1
), any solution to (
1

\lambda=\frac{{\cal A}{\bf x}^{m}}{{\cal B}{\bf x}^{m}}.

The extremal generalized eigenpairs can thus be computed by solving the following nonlinear program
[
25

\min_{{\bf x}\in S}\frac{{\cal A}{\bf x}^{m}}{{\cal B}{\bf x}^{m}},\quad\text{and}\quad\max_{{\bf x}\in S}\frac{{\cal A}{\bf x}^{m}}{{\cal B}{\bf x}^{m}},

where
S=\{{\bf x}:\|{\bf x}\|^{m}=1\}
. These relates to a class of single-ratio fractional programming problems in the form

\bar{\theta}=\min_{{\bf x}\in S}\frac{f({\bf x})}{g({\bf x})},

(2)

and can be addressed using the classical Dinkelbach–Type method
[
21
]
.

Using this tensor representation of homogeneous polynomials in (
2
), we propose a tensor-based Dinkelbach–Type method for computing the minimal generalized eigenvalue of two symmetric tensors. The proposed algorithm involves finding the root of the equation
F(\bar{\theta})=0
, where
F(\bar{\theta})
is the optimal value of the following parametric program

F(\bar{\theta})=\min_{{\bf x}\in S}\left(f({\bf x})-\bar{\theta}g({\bf x})\right).

(3)

which shares the same global optimal solutions as (
2
).
Note that are homogeneous polynomials
f({\bf x})
and
g({\bf x})
of even degree
d
. It is well known that homogeneous polynomials can be reformulated as high-order symmetric tensors applied to a vector of variables. Therefore, we show that (
3
) can be solved by a proximal alternative minimization algorithm through a multilinear optimization framework. The PAM algorithm alternates between updating each variable (or block of variables) while keeping the others fixed, incorporating a proximal step to ensure both convergence and stability. The subproblems in PAM are of lower dimensions, and each subproblem has a closed-form solution.

The main contributions of this paper are twofold:

1.

First, we establish that (
3
) is equivalent to a multilinear optimization problem (MOP) under the condition that
F(\bar{\theta})
is concave. For cases where the objective function is not concave, we introduce an augmented model with a regularization term to ensure the concavity of the resulting model. Furthermore, we prove that the augmented model and (
3
) share the same optimal solutions under unit spherical constraints.

2.

Second, leveraging the multilinear model corresponding to the augmented concave model, we propose a proximal alternating minimization (PAM) algorithm. Notably, the PAM algorithm can be interpreted as a block coordinate descent (BCD) method (see
[
38
]
) with a cyclic update rule and proximal terms tailored for polynomial optimization models. When applied to (
3
) under unit spherical constraints, the subproblems of the PAM algorithm have analytic optimal solutions.
We rigorously establish the global convergence of the PAM algorithm, using the analysis of the Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz (KL) exponent, under mild assumptions, we provide an explicit expression for the convergence rate of the proposed algorithm. Numerical experiments are conducted to compute the minimal generalized eigenvalue of symmetric tensors. Additionally, with slight modifications, the PAM algorithm can also be applied to the minimization of the high-order trust-region subproblems on the boundary.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section
2
provides a review of relevant symbols, definitions of tensors, and the Dinkelbach–Type method. In Section
3
, we present the proposed PAM algorithm and establish its convergence and convergence rate under mild assumptions. Section
4
presents preliminary numerical results, demonstrating the application of the proposed algorithm for computing the minimal generalized eigenvalue of symmetric tensors and minimizing high-order trust-region subproblems on the boundary.
Finally, Section
5
offers concluding remarks and outlines potential future research directions.

3.2
PAM Algorithm for Minimizing Dinkelbach–Type Subproblem (
4
)

In this subsection, we demonstrate that the minimization of (
4
) can be reformulated as a multilinear optimization problem under unit spherical constraints. Consequently, the Proximal Alternating Minimization (PAM) algorithm can be efficiently applied to solve (
4
) under these constraints.
The PAM algorithm minimizes alternately over each variable (or block of variables), treating the remaining variables as fixed, with each subproblem admitting a closed-form solution.

We first recall the following result to establish the equivalence between a homogeneous polynomial and a multilinear optimization problem (
(
15
, Corollary 4.2)
).

Lemma 1

If
{\cal A}
is a
d
-th order symmetric (or super-symmetric) tensor with
\hat{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
being a matrix,
then it holds that

\max_{\|\hat{N}{\bf x}\|=1}|{\cal A}{\bf x}^{m}|=\max_{\|\hat{N}{\bf x}^{(i)}\|=1,i=1,2,\ldots,d}|\langle{\cal A},{\bf x}^{(1)}\circ{\bf x}^{(2)}\circ\cdots\circ{\bf x}^{(d)}\rangle|,

where
{\bf x},{\bf x}^{(1)},{\bf x}^{(2)},\ldots,{\bf x}^{(d)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Let
\hat{N}
be the identity matrix, using Lemma
1
, we deduce the following result.

Theorem 3.1

Suppose
S:=\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}~|~\|{\bf x}\|=1\}
and
g({\bf x})>0,{\bf x}\in S
. If
f({\bf x})-\theta g({\bf x})={\cal A}(\theta){\bf x}^{d}
is concave for any
{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then it holds that

\min_{{\bf x}\in S}{\cal A}(\theta){\bf x}^{d}=\min_{{\bf x},{\bf y},\ldots,{\bf z}\in S}\langle{\cal A}(\theta),{\bf x}\circ{\bf y}\circ\cdots\circ{\bf z}\rangle.

Proof

By Lemma
1
, denote
\hat{N}
as an identity matrix with proper dimensions, it holds that

\max_{{\bf x}\in S}|{\cal A}(\theta){\bf x}^{d}|=\max_{{\bf x},{\bf y},\ldots,{\bf z}\in S}|\langle{\cal A}(\theta),{\bf x}\circ{\bf y}\circ\cdots\circ{\bf z}\rangle|.

(8)

By conditions that
{\cal A}(\theta){\bf x}^{d}
is a concave function, it follows that

{\bf y}^{\top}{\cal A}(\theta){\bf x}^{m-2}{\bf y}={\cal A}(\theta){\bf y}^{2}{\bf x}^{m-2}\leq 0,~\forall~{\bf y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

which implies that
{\cal A}(\theta){\bf x}^{d}\leq 0
(let
{\bf y}={\bf x}
above) and

\max_{{\bf x}\in S}|{\cal A}(\theta){\bf x}^{d}|=\max_{{\bf x}\in S}(-{\cal A}(\theta){\bf x}^{d}).

(9)

On the other hand, by the multilinear property of
\langle{\cal A}(\theta),{\bf x}\circ{\bf y}\circ\cdots\circ{\bf z}\rangle
, we have that

\displaystyle\max_{{\bf x},{\bf y},\ldots,{\bf z}\in S}|\langle{\cal A}(\theta),{\bf x}\circ{\bf y}\circ\cdots\circ{\bf z}\rangle|

\displaystyle=\max_{{\bf x},{\bf y},\ldots,{\bf z}\in S}\langle{\cal A}(\theta),{\bf x}\circ{\bf y}\circ\cdots\circ{\bf z}\rangle

(10)

\displaystyle=\max_{{\bf x},{\bf y},\ldots,{\bf z}\in S}(-\langle{\cal A}(\theta),{\bf x}\circ{\bf y}\circ\cdots\circ{\bf z}\rangle).

Combining (
8
)–(
10
), we know that

\min_{{\bf x}\in S}{\cal A}(\theta){\bf x}^{d}=\min_{{\bf x},{\bf y},\ldots,{\bf z}\in S}\langle{\cal A}(\theta),{\bf x}\circ{\bf y}\circ\cdots\circ{\bf z}\rangle,

and the desired result follows.
∎

Theorem
3.1
establishes that the optimal value of a homogeneous function defined on a sphere equals the optimal value of a multilinear function with the same coefficient tensors. This result provides a crucial guarantee for solving the subproblem in the Dinkelbach–Type algorithm.

Generally speaking, for Lemma
1
, the absolute value sign in the objective function on the right-hand side can be removed due to the symmetry of the constraint set. However, it is important that without the concavity condition, Lemma
1
does not necessarily lead to the result in Theorem
3.1
. A counterexample is provided below to illustrate this point.

Example 1

Denote two diagonal matrices such that

A_{1}=\left(\begin{matrix}1&~0\\
0&~-2\end{matrix}\right),~~~A_{2}=\left(\begin{matrix}2&~0\\
0&~4\end{matrix}\right).

The corresponding homogeneous polynomial and multilinear polynomial are given below:

f_{A_{1}}({\bf x})=x_{1}^{2}-2x_{2}^{2},~~~~f_{A_{1}}({\bf x},{\bf y})=x_{1}y_{1}-2x_{2}y_{2},

and

f_{A_{2}}({\bf x})=2x_{1}^{2}+4x_{2}^{2},~~~~f_{A_{2}}({\bf x},{\bf y})=2x_{1}y_{1}+4x_{2}y_{2}.

It is easy to know that
f_{A_{1}}({\bf x})
and
f_{A_{2}}({\bf x})
are not concave functions. By a direct computation, we have that

\min_{{\bf x}\in S}f_{A_{1}}({\bf x})=\min_{{\bf x},{\bf y}\in S}f_{A_{1}}({\bf x},{\bf y})=-2,

and

\min_{{\bf x}\in S}f_{A_{2}}({\bf x})=2,~~~~~\min_{{\bf x},{\bf y}\in S}f_{A_{2}}({\bf x},{\bf y})=-4,

where
S=\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}~|~x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}=1\}
.

Since
f({\bf x})-\theta g({\bf x})
in (
4
) is generally not a concave function for
{\bf x}\in S
, it is necessary to introduce an augmented model with a regularization term to ensure its concavity in order to apply the equivalence relationship in Theorem

. A bound for the size of the regularization term is provided in Lemma

Lemma 2

Suppose that

First of all, we know that

Then it holds that

\nabla h_{\alpha}({\bf x})=d{\cal A}(\theta){\bf x}^{d-1}-\alpha d({\bf x}^{\top}{\bf x})^{\frac{d}{2}-1}{\bf x},

\nabla^{2}h_{\alpha}({\bf x})=d(d-1){\cal A}(\theta){\bf x}^{d-2}-\alpha d(d-2)({\bf x}^{\top}{\bf x})^{\frac{d}{2}-2}{\bf x}{\bf x}^{\top}-\alpha d({\bf x}^{\top}{\bf x})^{\frac{d}{2}-1}I.

By conditions, for any

, we obtain that

\displaystyle{\bf y}^{\top}\nabla^{2}h_{\alpha}({\bf x}){\bf y}=

\displaystyle d(d-1){\cal A}(\theta){\bf y}^{2}{\bf x}^{d-2}-\alpha d(d-2)\|{\bf x}\|^{d-4}({\bf x}^{\top}{\bf y})^{2}-\alpha d\|{\bf x}\|^{d-2}({\bf y}^{\top}{\bf y})^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d(d-1){\cal A}(\theta){\bf y}^{2}{\bf x}^{d-2}-\alpha d(d-2)r^{d-4}({\bf x}^{\top}{\bf y})^{2}-\alpha dr^{d-2}({\bf y}^{\top}{\bf y})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle d(d-1)\|{\cal A}(\theta)\|_{F}\|{\bf y}\|^{2}r^{d-2}-\alpha d(d-2)r^{d-2}\|{\bf y}\|^{2}-\alpha dr^{d-2}\|{\bf y}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[d(d-1)\|{\cal A}(\theta)\|_{F}-\alpha d(d-2)-\alpha d\right]r^{d-2}\|{\bf y}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[d(d-1)\|{\cal A}(\theta)\|_{F}-\alpha d(d-1)\right]r^{d-2}\|{\bf y}\|^{2},

which implies that
{\bf y}^{\top}\nabla^{2}h_{\alpha}({\bf x}){\bf y}\leq 0
when
\alpha\geq\|{\cal A}(\theta)\|_{F}
, and the desired result holds.
∎

and Lemma

, the following result is true.

Proof

3.3
Convergence of the PAM Algorithm

In this subsection, we first establish the subsequential convergence of the sequence
\{{\bf t}^{(k)}\}=\{({\bf x}^{(k)},{\bf y}^{(k)},\cdots,{\bf u}^{(k)},{\bf z}^{(k)})\}
generated by Algorithm
2
. Then, leveraging the Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz (KL) property, we establish the global sequence convergence and give an explicit convergence rate.

Theorem 3.2

(Subsequential Convergence)
Let
\{{\bf t}^{(k)}\}
be an infinite sequence generated by Algorithm
2
.
Let
\bar{\gamma}=\min\{\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{d}\}
. Then the following statements hold.

(i)
For all
k
, the function sequence
\{h_{\alpha}({\bf t}^{(k)})\}
is nonincreasing and convergent. The sequence
\{{\bf t}^{(k)}\}
satisfies

\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\|{\bf t}^{(k+1)}-{\bf t}^{(k)}\|<+\infty.

(ii)
Suppose
\bar{{\bf t}}
is a cluster point of
\{{\bf t}^{(k)}\}
, then,
\bar{{\bf t}}
is a KKT point of (
11
) and

\lim_{k\rightarrow+\infty}h_{\alpha}({\bf t}^{(k)})=h_{\alpha}(\bar{{\bf t}}).

Proof

(i) By Algorithm
2
, it follows that

\displaystyle h_{\alpha}({\bf t}^{(k+1)})+\frac{\gamma_{d}}{2}\|{\bf z}^{(k+1)}-{\bf z}^{(k)}\|^{2}\leq h_{\alpha}({\bf x}^{(k+1)},\cdots,{\bf u}^{(k+1)},{\bf z}^{(k)}),

\displaystyle h_{\alpha}({\bf x}^{(k+1)},\cdots,{\bf u}^{(k+1)},{\bf z}^{(k)})+\frac{\gamma_{d-1}}{2}\|{\bf u}^{(k+1)}-{\bf u}^{(k)}\|^{2}\leq h_{\alpha}({\bf x}^{(k+1)},\cdots,{\bf u}^{(k)},{\bf z}^{(k)}),

\displaystyle\vdots~~~~~~~~~~~\vdots~~~~~~~~~~~~\vdots

\displaystyle h_{\alpha}({\bf x}^{(k+1)},{\bf y}^{(k)}\cdots,{\bf u}^{(k)},{\bf z}^{(k)})+\frac{\gamma_{1}}{2}\|{\bf x}^{(k+1)}-{\bf x}^{(k)}\|^{2}\leq h_{\alpha}({\bf t}^{(k)}),

which implies

h_{\alpha}({\bf t}^{(k+1)})+\frac{\bar{\gamma}}{2}\|{\bf t}^{(k+1)}-{\bf t}^{(k)}\|^{2}\leq h_{\alpha}({\bf t}^{(k)}).

(14)

Therefore, the sequence
\{h_{\alpha}({\bf t}^{(k)})\}
is nonincreasing. Since
h_{\alpha}({\bf t})
is bounded on the compact set
S
, we know that
\{h_{\alpha}({\bf t}^{(k)})\}
is convergent, and the sequence
\{{\bf t}^{(k)}\}
satisfies
\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\|{\bf t}^{(k+1)}-{\bf t}^{(k)}\|<+\infty
from (
14
). The conclusion (i) follows.

(ii) For the sequence
\{{\bf t}^{(k)}\}
, it has cluster points by bounded conditions. Without loss of generality, suppose that
\{{\bf t}^{(k_{j})}\}
is a subsequence of
\{{\bf t}^{(k)}\}
with cluster point
\bar{{\bf t}}=(\bar{{\bf x}},\bar{{\bf y}},\cdots,\bar{{\bf u}},\bar{{\bf z}})
. By the proof of (i), it’s clear that
\lim_{k\rightarrow+\infty}h_{\alpha}({\bf t}^{(k)})=h_{\alpha}(\bar{{\bf t}})
. On the other hand, from (i) again, it holds that

\lim_{k\rightarrow+\infty}\|{\bf t}^{(k+1)}-{\bf t}^{(k)}\|=0.

Then, we have that
\lim_{j\rightarrow+\infty}{\bf t}^{(k_{j}+1)}=\bar{{\bf t}}.
Considering the KKT system of the subproblems in Algorithm
2
, there are lagrange multipliers
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d}\in\mathbb{R}
satisfying

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\nabla_{{\bf x}}h_{\alpha}({\bf x}^{(k_{j}+1)},{\bf y}^{(k_{j})},\cdots,{\bf z}^{(k_{j})})+\gamma_{1}({\bf x}^{(k_{j}+1)}-{\bf x}^{(k_{j})})-\lambda_{1}{\bf x}^{(k_{j}+1)}={\bf 0},\\
&\nabla_{{\bf y}}h_{\alpha}({\bf x}^{(k_{j}+1)},{\bf y}^{(k_{j}+1)},\cdots,{\bf z}^{(k_{j})})+\gamma_{2}({\bf y}^{(k_{j}+1)}-{\bf y}^{(k_{j})})-\lambda_{2}{\bf y}^{(k_{j}+1)}={\bf 0},\\
&~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\vdots~~~~~~~~~~~\vdots~~~~~~~~~~~~\vdots\\
&\nabla_{{\bf z}}h_{\alpha}({\bf x}^{(k_{j}+1)},{\bf y}^{(k_{j}+1)},\cdots,{\bf z}^{(k_{j}+1)})+\gamma_{d}({\bf z}^{(k_{j}+1)}-{\bf z}^{(k_{j})})-\lambda_{d}{\bf z}^{(k_{j}+1)}={\bf 0}.\\
\end{aligned}\right.

Let
j\rightarrow+\infty
, and by the continuity of
h_{\alpha}({\bf t})
, it follows that

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\nabla_{{\bf x}}h_{\alpha}(\bar{{\bf x}},\bar{{\bf y}},\cdots,\bar{{\bf z}})-\lambda_{1}\bar{{\bf x}}={\bf 0},\\
&\nabla_{{\bf y}}h_{\alpha}(\bar{{\bf x}},\bar{{\bf y}},\cdots,\bar{{\bf z}})-\lambda_{2}\bar{{\bf y}}={\bf 0},\\
&~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\vdots~~~~~~~~~~~\vdots~~~~~~~~~\\
&\nabla_{{\bf z}}h_{\alpha}(\bar{{\bf x}},\bar{{\bf y}},\cdots,\bar{{\bf z}})-\lambda_{d}\bar{{\bf z}}={\bf 0}.\\
\end{aligned}\right.

(15)

By the fact that
\bar{{\bf x}},\bar{{\bf y}},\cdots,\bar{{\bf z}}\in S
, we obtain that

\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}=\cdots=\lambda_{d}=h_{\alpha}(\bar{{\bf x}},\bar{{\bf y}},\cdots,\bar{{\bf z}})=h_{\alpha}(\bar{{\bf t}}).

Combining this with (
15
), it holds that
\nabla h_{\alpha}(\bar{{\bf t}})-h_{\alpha}(\bar{{\bf t}})\bar{{\bf t}}={\bf 0},

and the desired result holds.
∎

Lemma 4

d(3d-3)^{n-1},&{\rm if}~~d\geq 2.\end{array}\right.

By Lemma

Theorem 3.4

,
where
\tau=d^{-1}(3d-3)^{1-dn}
.

Proof

To prove the statement, let
\bar{{\bf t}}\in\Lambda
, and let
\delta_{1},\eta>0
such that for any
{\bf t}
satisfying
\|{\bf t}-\bar{{\bf t}}\|\leq\delta_{1}
, it follows that

\varphi(\bar{{\bf t}})\leq\varphi({\bf t})\leq\varphi(\bar{{\bf t}})+\eta.

On one hand, we can write

\varphi({\bf t})=h_{\alpha}({\bf x},{\bf y},\cdots,{\bf u},{\bf z})+\iota_{S}({\bf x})+\iota_{S}({\bf y})+\cdots+\iota_{S}({\bf z}).

By a direct computation, we know that

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{{\bf x}}\varphi({\bf t})=\{\nabla_{{\bf x}}h_{\alpha}({\bf t})+\lambda_{1}{\bf x}:\lambda_{1}\in\mathbb{R}\},\\
&\partial_{{\bf y}}\varphi({\bf t})=\{\nabla_{{\bf y}}h_{\alpha}({\bf t})+\lambda_{2}{\bf y}:\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{R}\},\\
&~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\vdots~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\vdots\\
&\partial_{{\bf u}}\varphi({\bf t})=\{\nabla_{{\bf u}}h_{\alpha}({\bf t})+\lambda_{d-1}{\bf u}:\lambda_{d-1}\in\mathbb{R}\},\\
&\partial_{{\bf z}}\varphi({\bf t})=\{\nabla_{{\bf z}}h_{\alpha}({\bf t})+\lambda_{d}{\bf z}:\lambda_{d}\in\mathbb{R}\}.\\
\end{aligned}\right.

(18)

By (
18
), it implies that

\displaystyle{\rm dist}(0,\partial\varphi({\bf t}))^{2}

\displaystyle=\inf_{\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{d}\in\mathbb{R}}\|\partial_{{\bf x}}\varphi({\bf t})\|^{2}+\|\partial_{{\bf y}}\varphi({\bf t})\|^{2}+\cdots+\|\partial_{{\bf z}}\varphi({\bf t})\|^{2},

(19)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\lambda_{1}\in\mathbb{R}}(\lambda_{1}^{2}+2\lambda_{1}h_{\alpha}({\bf t})+\|\nabla_{{\bf x}}h_{\alpha}({\bf t})\|^{2})

\displaystyle+\inf_{\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{R}}(\lambda_{2}^{2}+2\lambda_{2}h_{\alpha}({\bf t})+\|\nabla_{{\bf y}}h_{\alpha}({\bf t})\|^{2})+\cdots

\displaystyle+\inf_{\lambda_{d}\in\mathbb{R}}(\lambda_{d}^{2}+2\lambda_{d}h_{\alpha}({\bf t})+\|\nabla_{{\bf z}}h_{\alpha}({\bf t})\|^{2})

\displaystyle=-dh_{\alpha}({\bf t})^{2}+\|\nabla h_{\alpha}({\bf t})\|^{2}.

On the other hand, we consider the following polynomial

f({\bf x},{\bf y},\cdots,{\bf u},{\bf z},\lambda)=h_{\alpha}({\bf t})+\frac{\lambda}{2}(\|{\bf x}\|^{2}-1)+\frac{\lambda}{2}(\|{\bf y}\|^{2}-1)+\cdots+\frac{\lambda}{2}(\|{\bf z}\|^{2}-1),

where
\lambda=-h_{\alpha}({\bf t})=-h_{\alpha}({\bf x},{\bf y},\cdots,{\bf z})
. Denote

\hat{f}({\bf x},{\bf y},\cdots,{\bf z},\lambda)=f({\bf x},{\bf y},\cdots,{\bf z},\lambda)-f(\bar{{\bf x}},\bar{{\bf y}},\cdots,\bar{{\bf z}},\bar{\lambda}),

where
\bar{\lambda}=-h_{\alpha}(\bar{{\bf x}},\bar{{\bf y}},\cdots,\bar{{\bf z}})
.
Obviously that
\hat{f}({\bf t})
is a polynomial defined in
\mathbb{R}^{nd}
with degree
d
.
By Lemma
4
, there exist
\delta^{\prime}>0,c>0

such that, for all
\|{\bf t}-\bar{{\bf t}}\|\leq\delta_{2}
, it follows

\displaystyle\|\nabla f({\bf x},{\bf y},\cdots,{\bf z},\lambda)\|

\displaystyle=\|\nabla\hat{f}({\bf x},{\bf y},\cdots,{\bf z},\lambda)\|

\displaystyle\geq c|f({\bf x},{\bf y},\cdots,{\bf z},\lambda)-f(\bar{{\bf x}},\bar{{\bf y}},\cdots,\bar{{\bf z}},\bar{\lambda})|^{1-\tau},

where
\tau=d^{-1}(3d-3)^{1-dn}
. Note that for any
{\bf t}\in\Lambda,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\nabla_{{\bf x}}f({\bf x},{\bf y},\cdots,{\bf z},\lambda)=\nabla_{{\bf x}}h_{\alpha}({\bf t})+\lambda{\bf x},\\
&\nabla_{{\bf y}}f({\bf x},{\bf y},\cdots,{\bf z},\lambda)=\nabla_{{\bf y}}h_{\alpha}({\bf t})+\lambda{\bf y},\\
&~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\vdots~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\vdots\\
&\nabla_{{\bf z}}f({\bf x},{\bf y},\cdots,{\bf z},\lambda)=\nabla_{{\bf z}}h_{\alpha}({\bf t})+\lambda{\bf z},\\
&\nabla_{\lambda}f({\bf x},{\bf y},\cdots,{\bf z},\lambda)=0,\\
\end{aligned}\right.

which implies that

\|\nabla f({\bf x},{\bf y},\cdots,{\bf z},\lambda)\|^{2}=-dh_{\alpha}({\bf t})^{2}+\|\nabla h_{\alpha}({\bf t})\|^{2}={\rm dist}(0,\partial\varphi({\bf t}))^{2},

and
f({\bf x},{\bf y},\cdots,{\bf z},\lambda)=\varphi({\bf t})
,
f(\bar{{\bf x}},\bar{{\bf y}},\cdots,\bar{{\bf z}},\bar{\lambda})=\varphi(\bar{{\bf t}})
.
Take
\delta=\min\{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\}
. Combining this with (
19
), it holds hat, for all
{\bf t}\in\Lambda
with
\|{\bf t}-\bar{{\bf t}}\|\leq\delta
,
and
\varphi(\bar{{\bf t}})\leq\varphi({\bf t})\leq\varphi(\bar{{\bf t}})+\eta
,

{\rm dist}(0,\partial\varphi({\bf t}))\geq c|f({\bf x},{\bf y},\cdots,{\bf z},\lambda)-f(\bar{{\bf x}},\bar{{\bf y}},\cdots,\bar{{\bf z}},\bar{\lambda})|^{1-\tau},

and the desired results hold.
∎

To conclude this section, we analyze the convergence rate of Algorithm
2
for the non trivial cases of
d\geq 2
and
n\geq 2
. Under reasonable assumptions, several classical results on convergence rates from the literature established the convergence rate analyses based on the KL property
[
2
,
42
,
43
]
. Specifically, if the desingularization function of
\varphi({\bf t})
is
\phi(s)=cs^{1-\alpha}
, then as shown in
[
2
]
, the following estimates hold

(i)

If
\alpha=0
, the sequence
\{{\bf t}^{(k)}\}
converges in a finite number of steps.

(ii)

If
\alpha\in(0,1/2]
, there exist constants
a>0
and
\theta\in(0,1)
such that
\|{\bf t}^{(k)}-\bar{{\bf t}}\|\leq a\theta^{k}
.

(iii)

If
\alpha\in(1/2,1)
, there exists a constant
a>0
such that

\|{\bf t}^{(k)}-\bar{{\bf t}}\|\leq ak^{-\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha-1}}.

Combining this with Theorem
3.4
(where
\alpha=1-\tau
), we obtain the following result.

Theorem 3.5

Assume
\{{\bf t}^{(k)}\}
is an infinite sequence generated by Algorithm
2
, and
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}{\bf t}^{(k)}=\bar{{\bf t}}
. For
d\geq 2
and
n\geq 2
, there exists a constant
a>0
such that

\|{\bf t}^{(k)}-\bar{{\bf t}}\|\leq ak^{-\tau/(1-2\tau)},

where
\tau=d^{-1}(3d-3)^{1-dn}
is defined in Theorem
3.4
.

Proof

By Theorem
3.4
, the merit function
\varphi({\bf t})
satisfies the KL property with exponent
1-\tau
at
\bar{{\bf t}}
. Hence, it suffices to verify that
1-\tau\in(1/2,1)
. From the definition of
\tau
, it is evident that

\tau=d^{-1}(3d-3)^{1-dn}<1/2,~\forall~d\geq 2,~n\geq 2,

which ensures the desired result.
∎

4.2
Minimizing the High-order Trust-region Subproblem

In contrast to first-order and second-order methods, high-order methods (with order
p\geq 3
) have garnered significant research interest due to their faster global and local convergence rates
[
12
]
. However, efficiently solving the associated subproblem, which involves a
p
th-order Taylor derivative, remains an open question for
p\geq 3
. Algorithm
2
represents an initial attempt to minimize such subproblems effectively under a tensor eigenvalue framework.

We consider the unconstrained nonconvex optimization problem

\min_{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\tilde{f}({\bf x}),

where
\tilde{f}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is nonconvex,
p
-times continuously differentiable (
p\geq 1
) and bounded below.
One of the key techniques in optimization involves approximating
\tilde{f}({\bf x}+{\tilde{s}})
at the current iterate
{\bf x}={\bf x}_{k}
using a
p
th-order Taylor expansion
T_{p}({\tilde{s}})
. This expansion is then minimized iteratively under the trust-region constraint
\|{\tilde{s}}\|\leq\Delta
, where
\Delta>0

\argmin_{\|{\tilde{s}}\|\leq\Delta}T_{p}({\tilde{s}}):=\tilde{f}({\bf x}_{k})+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\frac{1}{j!}\nabla_{{\bf x}}^{j}\tilde{f}({\bf x}_{k})[{\tilde{s}}]^{j}.

(21)

Here,
{\tilde{s}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\nabla_{{\bf x}}^{j}\tilde{f}({\bf x}_{k})
is a symmetric
j
th-order tensor.

For
p=1
, (
21
) reduces to the steepest descent model with step size control, while for
p=2
, it gives the trust-region model, both of which have well-established minimization algorithms. However, for
p\geq 3
, (
21
) leads to (nonconvex) high-order trust-region models
[
10
]
, whose minimization remains an open research question. While recent literature works focus on solving the high-order subproblem under an adaptive regularization setup
[
13
,
31
,
47
,
48
,
49
,
7
,
50
]
,
in this subsection, we give preliminary numerical illustrations of using the Proximal Alternating Minimization (PAM) algorithm to minimize the
p
th-order Taylor model on the trust-region boundary
\|{\tilde{s}}\|=\Delta
.

The first step involves expressing the Taylor model (
21
) in the form of a symmetric tensor expression. To achieve this, we represent
T_{p}({\tilde{s}})
as a polynomial

T_{p}({\tilde{s}})=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{n}}f_{\alpha}{\tilde{s}}^{\alpha},

where
{\tilde{s}}=[s_{1},\dotsc,s_{n}]^{\top}
,

{\tilde{s}}^{\alpha}=s_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}s_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\cdots s_{n}^{\alpha_{n}},\quad|\alpha|=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\cdots+\alpha_{n}\leq p,

and
\alpha_{1},\dotsc,\alpha_{n}\in\{0,\dotsc,p\}
. Define a symmetric tensor
{\cal T}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+1)^{p}}
with entries

{\cal T}\big\{\pi(i_{1},\dotsc,i_{p})\big\}=\frac{(p-|\alpha|)!\alpha_{1}!\cdots\alpha_{n}!}{p!}f_{\alpha},\quad i_{1},\dotsc,i_{p}\in\{0,1,\dotsc,n\},

(22)

where
{\cal T}\big\{i_{1},\dotsc,i_{p}\big\}
denotes the
[i_{1},\dotsc,i_{p}]
-th entry of the tensor
{\cal T}
,
\pi(i_{1},\dotsc,i_{p})
is the permutation of indices, and
f_{\alpha}
is the coefficient of
{\tilde{s}}^{\alpha}
. Using this definition, the Taylor model can be reformulated as the symmetric tensor expression,

\displaystyle T_{p}({\tilde{s}})={\cal T}[{\tilde{s}}_{c}]^{p}=\sum_{i_{1},\dotsc,i_{p}}{\cal T}\big\{\pi(i_{1},\dotsc,i_{p})\big\}s_{i_{1}}\dotsc s_{i_{p}},

(23)

where
{\tilde{s}}_{c}=[1,s_{1},\dotsc,s_{n}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.

The minimization of
T_{p}
on the boundary can be reformulated as a tensor eigenvalue problem.
If the minimizer of (
21
) lies on the boundary
\|{\tilde{s}}\|=\Delta
, (
21
) transforms into the Z-eigenvalue problem

\argmin_{{\tilde{s}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}}{\cal T}[{\tilde{s}}_{c}]^{p}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\|{\tilde{s}}\|=\Delta

(24)

where
{\tilde{s}}_{c}=[1,{\tilde{s}}]^{\top}=[1,s_{1},\dotsc,s_{n}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. Due to the constraint that the first entry of
{\tilde{s}}_{c}
is fixed at 1. The closed-form expression for the KKT point in the PAM subproblem (
13
) becomes

\displaystyle{\bf x}^{(k+1)}=\pm\Delta\frac{({\bf 0},I)\big[{\cal T}\tilde{{\bf y}}^{(k)}\cdots\tilde{{\bf u}}^{(k)}\tilde{{\bf z}}^{(k)}-\alpha\tilde{{\bf y}}^{(k)}\cdots\langle\tilde{{\bf u}}^{(k)},\tilde{{\bf z}}^{(k)}\rangle-\gamma_{1}\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(k)}\big]}{\bigg\|({\bf 0},I)\big[{\cal T}\tilde{{\bf y}}^{(k)}\cdots\tilde{{\bf u}}^{(k)}\tilde{{\bf z}}^{(k)}-\alpha\tilde{{\bf y}}^{(k)}\cdots\langle\tilde{{\bf u}}^{(k)},\tilde{{\bf z}}^{(k)}\rangle-\gamma_{1}\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(k)}\big]\bigg\|}.

(25)

Similar to (
13
), if the denominators of (
25
) equal zero, we may set
{\bf x}^{k+1}={\bf x}^{k}
and adjust the parameters
\gamma_{1}
and
\alpha
.
Here,
\tilde{{\bf y}}=(1,{\bf y}^{\top})^{\top}
,
\tilde{{\bf u}}=(1,{\bf u}^{\top})^{\top}
,
\tilde{{\bf z}}=(1,{\bf z}^{\top})^{\top}
,
\tilde{{\bf x}}=(1,{\bf x}^{\top})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, and
I\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the identity matrix and
({\bf 0},I)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(n+1)}
.
Let
{\tilde{s}}^{*}
be the solution of the Z-eigenvalue problem
in (
24
). Let
\lambda^{*}=({\tilde{s}}^{*})^{\top}\nabla T_{3}({\tilde{s}}^{*})/\Delta^{2}
, and define the Lagrangian multiplier as

L({\tilde{s}},\lambda):=T_{p}({\tilde{s}})+\frac{1}{2}\lambda\|{\tilde{s}}\|^{2}.

The gradient of the Lagrangian is zero at
(\lambda^{*},{\tilde{s}}^{*})
, such that

\nabla L({\tilde{s}}^{*},\lambda^{*}):=\nabla T_{p}({\tilde{s}}^{*})+\lambda^{*}{\tilde{s}}^{*}=\textbf{0}.

We provide the algorithm for minimizing the high-order trust-region subproblem in Algorithm

5
Conclusions

In this paper, we proposed a novel tensor-based Dinkelbach–Type method for computing extremal tensor generalized eigenvalues. We established the equivalence between a homogeneous polynomial optimization problem with a spherical constraint and a multilinear programming problem with multiple spherical constraints. Leveraging this equivalence, we addressed a class of homogeneous single-ratio fractional programs and proposed an efficient proximal alternating minimization (PAM) algorithm to solve the original problem. The algorithm’s subsequential and global sequence convergence were rigorously proven. We have tested our method numerically on several problems from the literature,
including computing of Z-, H-, and D-eigenpairs. Numerical experiments confirmed the efficiency and practical applicability of the proposed method.

Several intriguing and open questions remain for future investigation. First, when the objective in the polynomial optimization problem is an inhomogeneous function, does a similar equivalence with a multilinear optimization model still hold? For the homogeneous case, can the spherical constraint be generalized to other compact constraint sets? Furthermore, can the conjecture posed in Remark
4
be resolved? Although these questions present significant theoretical challenges, their resolution would yield valuable insights for implementable tensor methods.

Appendix B
Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz (KL) Property

Definition 1
(Proper, Lower Semicontinuous Function)

A function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is called a proper, lower semicontinuous function if it satisfies the following conditions:

1.

Properness:

\varphi(\mathbf{x})<+\infty
for at least one
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
\varphi(\mathbf{x})>-\infty
for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

2.

Lower Semicontinuity:
For any sequence
\{\mathbf{x}_{k}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
that converges to
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the following holds:

\varphi(\mathbf{x})\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}\varphi(\mathbf{x}_{k}).

Definition 2
(KL Property)

Let
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a proper, lower semicontinuous function. The function
\varphi
is said to satisfy the Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz (KL) property at a point
\bar{\mathbf{x}}\in\text{dom}(\partial\varphi)
if there exist
\eta\in(0,+\infty)
, a neighborhood
U
of
\bar{\mathbf{x}}
, and a continuous concave function
\psi:[0,\eta)\to[0,+\infty)
such that:

1.

\psi(0)=0
,

2.

\psi
is differentiable on
(0,\eta)
with
\psi^{\prime}(s)>0
for all
s\in(0,\eta)
,

3.

For all
\mathbf{x}\in U
such that
\varphi(\mathbf{x})>\varphi(\bar{\mathbf{x}})
and
\varphi(\mathbf{x})-\varphi(\bar{\mathbf{x}})<\eta
, the following inequality holds:

\psi^{\prime}(\varphi(\mathbf{x})-\varphi(\bar{\mathbf{x}}))\cdot\|\partial\varphi(\mathbf{x})\|\geq 1,

where
\partial\varphi(\mathbf{x})
denotes the limiting subdifferential of
\varphi
at
\mathbf{x}
.
