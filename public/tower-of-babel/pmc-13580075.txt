Facilitators and barriers to innovation and research capacity among clinical nurses: A qualitative systematic review
Authors: CongBing Yan, Xiang Wang, Chengyan Zhang, Man Xu, Xiaoxia Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInnovation and research capacity among clinical nurses is critical to advancing nursing science, improving patient outcomes, and strengthening healthcare systems. However, evidence on its facilitators and barriers remains fragmented across different cultural and practice contexts.ObjectiveTo identify, appraise, and synthesize qualitative evidence on the facilitators and barriers influencing clinical nurses’ innovation and research capacity, and to generate evidence-based suggestions for nursing practice, education, and healthcare policy.DesignA systematic review and qualitative meta-aggregation.ParticipantsClinical nurses and registered nurses practicing in healthcare settings, with sample sizes across the included studies ranging from 6 to 120 participants.MethodsThe review was conducted following the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology for qualitative evidence synthesis and reported according to the Enhancing Transparency in Reporting the Synthesis of Qualitative Research guideline. Comprehensive searches were performed in ten databases from inception to June 30, 2026, and reference lists of included articles were also screened. Methodological quality was appraised using the Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Checklist for Qualitative Research, and findings were synthesized using the meta-aggregation approach.ResultsTwenty-eight qualitative studies were included. The synthesis yielded four synthesized findings: (1) Navigating organizational culture and structural support; (2) The dual nature of professional knowledge and competence; (3) The mediating role of leadership and mentorship; and (4) The internal process of motivation and self-efficacy.ConclusionClinical nurses’ innovation and research capacity is a complex, dynamic process negotiated between constraining and enabling factors across organizational, educational, relational and personal dimensions. Moving it from an implicit to an explicit professional competency requires coordinated action from healthcare administrators, educators, and policymakers to address systemic barriers and leverage key facilitators identified in this synthesis.Keywords: Clinical nurses, Innovation capacity, Research capacity, Systematic review, Qualitative study

Introduction

The development of a robust nursing workforce equipped with innovation and research capabilities has emerged as a critical priority for global healthcare systems (Melnyk et al., 2020; Speroni et al., 2020). As the largest segment of healthcare professionals, nurses are uniquely positioned to identify clinical problems, generate evidence-based solutions, and drive transformative changes in patient care (Pintz et al., 2018; Polk, 1989).

Innovation and research capacity among clinical nurses refers to the comprehensive ability to identify researchable problems, design and implement studies, critically evaluate evidence, translate findings into practice, and develop innovative approaches to improve care delivery (Leung et al., 2016; Ammouri et al., 2014). This capacity extends beyond basic clinical competence to encompass higher-order cognitive skills including critical thinking, creativity, problem-solving, and scholarly inquiry (Schaefer and Welton, 2018; Zhang et al., 2022). Empirical evidence suggests that nurses with stronger research and innovation capabilities demonstrate improved clinical decision-making, enhanced quality of care, greater job satisfaction, and stronger organizational commitment (Atalla et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2024). Yet time-and-motion research indicates that nurses devote the majority of their working time to direct patient care and administrative duties, with limited protected time available for research activities (Hendrich et al., 2008).

The capacity for innovation and research among clinical nurses constitutes a complex, multidimensional construct that integrates knowledge, skills, attitudes, and contextual supports (Mick, 2011; Iowa Model Collaborative et al., 2017). Its formation is influenced by factors operating at multiple levels: individual (knowledge, skills, motivation, self-efficacy), interpersonal (mentorship, peer support, teamwork), organizational (culture, resources, leadership support, time allocation), and systemic (policy environment, educational preparation, professional recognition) (Heitschmidt et al., 2021; Bowles et al., 2023). Understanding these multilevel influences is critical for designing effective strategies to promote nurse engagement in research and innovation activities (Baptiste et al., 2022; Beal, 2012).

Previous reviews have examined research utilization among nurses or barriers to evidence-based practice from various perspectives. For example, Mathieson et al. (2019) conducted a systematic mixed-studies review of strategies, facilitators, and barriers to the implementation of evidence-based practice in community nursing, while Wilson et al. (2015) examined how evidence-based practice environments empower nurses across Magnet, Pathway to Excellence, and non-designated facilities. More recently, Morrison et al. (2022) synthesized mixed-methods evidence on factors influencing research activity among nurses in clinical practice, Lei et al. (2026) systematically reviewed facilitators and barriers to developing research capacity among clinical nurses, and Mackay et al. (2026) scoped the factors influencing nurses’ engagement in clinical research; a related systematic review protocol has also been published (Song et al., 2025). However, these syntheses have primarily integrated quantitative or mixed-methods evidence, or have focused on research capacity as an outcome rather than on the lived experiences through which it is formed. To date, no qualitative meta-aggregation has systematically integrated evidence on both facilitators and barriers to innovation and research capacity specifically among clinical nurses across diverse cultural and practice settings. This gap matters because the lived experiences of nurses reveal the mechanisms through which structural, educational, relational, and psychological factors operate—mechanisms that quantitative designs cannot readily capture (Thomas and Harden, 2008). Strengthening innovation and research capacity has been shown to enhance professional development, improve patient outcomes, and contribute to healthcare organizational effectiveness (Galuska et al., 2022; Nowell et al., 2017).

Meta-aggregation is a pragmatic synthesis approach that assembles findings from qualitative studies into categories and synthesized statements to produce directly actionable recommendations for practice and policy (Lockwood et al., 2015). The objective of this review was therefore to identify, appraise, and synthesize qualitative evidence on facilitators and barriers to innovation and research capacity among clinical nurses and to generate evidence-based suggestions for nursing practice, education, and healthcare policy.

Methods

The Enhancing Transparency in Reporting the Synthesis of Qualitative Research Guidelines (ENTREQ) (Tong et al., 2012) was used for the reporting of this systematic review and meta-synthesis. It provides detailed guidance to enhance the conduct and quality of qualitative syntheses by ensuring essential elements of qualitative syntheses were presented, regardless of methodology used. The protocol for this review was registered with the International Platform of Registered Systematic Review and Meta-analysis Protocols (registration number: INPLASY202670078).

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across ten electronic databases including PubMed, Embase, CINAHL, Web of Science, Scopus, Cochrane Library, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), WanFang Data, China Science and Technology Journal Database (VIP) and SinoMed. Eligible records were published from the inception of each database through June 30, 2026. The search keywords were ‘clinical nurse’, ‘registered nurse’, ‘innovation capacity’, ‘research capacity’, ‘research ability’, ‘innovative ability’, ‘qualitative research’, ‘qualitative study’, ‘grounded theory’, ‘phenomenology’ and ‘semi-structured interview’. The full search strategy is presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Eligibility criteria

The eligibility of studies was determined based on the SPIDER framework (Sample, Phenomenon of Interest, Design, Evaluation and Research type) (Cooke et al., 2012), which is specifically designed for qualitative and mixed-method evidence synthesis.

The inclusion criteria were: (1) Clinical nurses or registered nurses practicing in healthcare settings; studies with mixed professional samples (for example, nurses together with midwives or allied health professionals) were eligible when the qualitative data pertaining to nurses could be clearly identified; (2) Studies that explicitly explore experiences, perceptions, meanings, or processes related to the development, formation, or negotiation of innovation capacity, research capacity, or innovative research ability; (3) Primary qualitative study designs, including but not limited to phenomenology, grounded theory, ethnography and qualitative description. Mixed-methods studies were included only if the qualitative component can be clearly extracted and meets other criteria; (4) Studies reporting rich qualitative data on facilitators, challenges, or influential experiences in the context of the phenomenon of interest; and (5) Published in English or Chinese.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) Purely quantitative research, conference abstracts, editorials, or review papers; (2) Studies focusing on nursing students, other healthcare professional students, or non-clinical nurses; and (3) Studies lacking sufficient qualitative data for extraction.

Study selection

To ensure rigorous study screening, two reviewers worked independently throughout the process. All records were imported into EndNote 21 (Clarivate Plc, London, United Kingdom) for management and duplicate records were removed. The reviewers then conducted an initial screening based on titles and abstracts, excluding studies that clearly did not meet the inclusion criteria. For the remaining studies, full-text articles were obtained and reviewed in detail against the inclusion and exclusion criteria for final eligibility assessment. Any disagreements during the process were resolved through discussion and consensus between the reviewers. If consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer was consulted to make the final determination.

Quality appraisal

The methodological quality and rigor of all included qualitative studies were critically appraised using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Qualitative Research (Lockwood et al., 2015). This standardized tool assesses 10 key domains of qualitative research integrity, including congruence between the research methodology and the stated philosophical perspective, objectives, data collection, analysis and interpretation, as well as the research’s cultural or theoretical orientation and the representation of participants’ voices.

The appraisal was conducted independently by two reviewers. Each item was rated as ‘Yes’, ‘No’, or ‘Unclear’. Discrepancies in ratings or overall judgments were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached, if necessary, a third reviewer was consulted.

Data extraction

The following data were systematically extracted: author, publication year, country, study design, data collection methods, method of data analysis, sample size, clinical setting, years of nursing experience, gender, all author-reported findings pertaining to facilitators, barriers, or experiences of innovation and research capacity. Two reviewers extracted data from all included studies independently. Any discrepancies in the extracted data or interpretations were resolved through discussion, referring back to the original study. If consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer was consulted.

Data synthesis

The qualitative evidence synthesis was conducted using the JBI meta-aggregation approach (Lockwood et al., 2015; Aromataris et al., 2024), a recognized method for systematic reviews of qualitative evidence following a three-stage process: (1) extracting findings from the included studies; (2) grouping these findings into categories based on thematic similarities; and (3) developing synthesized statements from these categories. Each synthesized statement is assigned a credibility level based on the supporting data.

Results

Search results

Database searches yielded 6968 records. After duplicates were removed, 3126 records were screened based on title and abstract. Of these, 2845 were excluded because they did not focus on innovation or research capacity among clinical nurses, or were not qualitative studies. The remaining 281 articles were retrieved for full-text assessment. Twenty-eight studies met all inclusion criteria and were included in the present study. Synthesis results

The meta-aggregation of 28 qualitative studies yielded a comprehensive set of facilitators and barriers influencing the innovation and research capacity of clinical nurses. The synthesis yielded four interrelated meta-themes: (1) Navigating organizational culture and structural support; (2) The dual nature of professional knowledge and competence; (3) The mediating role of leadership and mentorship; and (4) The internal process of motivation and self-efficacy. Navigating organizational culture and structural support

The formation of innovation and research capacity among clinical nurses is fundamentally shaped by the organizational context in which they work. Across studies, participants consistently described the powerful influence of organizational culture, structural resources, and institutional policies on their ability to engage in research and innovation activities (Luckson et al., 2018; van Oostveen et al., 2017; Owusu et al., 2025; Alodhialah, 2025).

A significant barrier across studies was the mismatch between clinical workload demands and the time and cognitive space required for research engagement. Nurses in diverse settings reported being overwhelmed by heavy patient loads, staffing shortages, and administrative duties that left little time or energy for research activities (Roxburgh, 2006; Nkrumah et al., 2018; Owusu et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2026). The lack of protected research time was repeatedly identified as a critical structural barrier, with research work frequently displaced into nurses’ personal time (Woodward et al., 2007; Walsh et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2024). Nurses engaged in innovation additionally described the absence of established pathways for technical translation and patenting of their innovations (Yan et al., 2022; Semerci et al., 2024). In resource-limited settings, the challenges were compounded by inadequate access to research infrastructure, limited funding, and insufficient technological resources (Nkrumah et al., 2018; Kyei et al., 2023; Lang et al., 2010).

Conversely, when organizational structures actively supported research engagement, nurses reported significantly enhanced capacity and motivation. Key facilitators included dedicated research time, access to research infrastructure and databases, funding support for projects, and organizational policies that explicitly valued and rewarded research engagement (Walsh et al., 2023; O’Brien et al., 2022; Day et al., 2017; Moore et al., 2012). Healthcare institutions with explicit research strategies or academic status often provided more enabling environments, characterized by research-active cultures and expectations for nursing inquiry (Luckson et al., 2018; Newington et al., 2021; Day et al., 2017).

The dual nature of professional knowledge and competence

The development of innovation and research capacity was universally identified as dependent upon the acquisition and application of specialized knowledge and skills, while simultaneously constrained by educational gaps and deficits in research literacy (Roxburgh, 2006; Owusu et al., 2025; Wang and Li, 2026; Berthelsen and Hølge-Hazelton, 2018).

A dominant barrier was the perception of inadequate preparation for research during basic nursing education and limited opportunities for continuing research education. Nurses across studies reported feeling underprepared in research methodology, statistical analysis, literature searching and appraisal, academic writing, and grant application processes (Evans et al., 2014; Johnston et al., 2016; Kyei et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2026; O’Byrne and Smith, 2011; Segrott et al., 2006). This knowledge deficit contributed to feelings of intimidation and avoidance of research activities.

However, when nurses had access to targeted education and skill development opportunities, these experiences served as powerful facilitators. Research training programs, workshops on evidence-based practice, academic collaborations, structured internship schemes, and continuing education in research methods were consistently identified as transformative experiences that enhanced nurses’ confidence and competence (Wright et al., 2020; Owusu et al., 2025; Kim and Jeong, 2025; Gao et al., 2022). Studies from institutions with structured capacity-building programmes reported more robust educational infrastructures that systematically supported nurses’ research development (Wright et al., 2020; McKee et al., 2017; Scala et al., 2019).

The mediating role of leadership and mentorship

The process of developing innovation and research capacity was profoundly relational, mediated through interactions with nurse leaders, clinical mentors, academic researchers, and peers. These relationships could either scaffold or obstruct research engagement and capacity development (O’Brien et al., 2022; van Oostveen et al., 2017; Landeen et al., 2017).

A dominant barrier was the absence of accessible research mentors and unsupportive leadership attitudes toward nursing research. Nurses described managers who viewed research as peripheral to clinical duties, difficulty identifying and approaching experienced researchers, and the absence of visible nurse researcher role models (Lee et al., 2024; Walsh et al., 2023; Parker et al., 2017; Chen et al., 2019). In environments where research was not valued by leadership or teams, nurses reported feeling that research engagement was invisible work that went unrecognized and unrewarded (Walsh et al., 2023; Hwang et al., 2024; French and Stavropoulou, 2016).

Conversely, the presence of supportive mentors and research-affirming leadership was the most consistently identified facilitator across all studies. Nurses described transformative experiences with mentors who provided guidance, encouragement, emotional support, and practical assistance (Lee et al., 2024; O’Brien et al., 2022; Wright et al., 2020; Fullam et al., 2018; Lode et al., 2015). Effective mentors were described as accessible, experienced, encouraging, and skilled at breaking down complex research processes into manageable steps. Supportive nurse managers and leaders who protected research time, celebrated research achievements, and modelled entrepreneurial leadership created enabling environments for research and innovation capacity development (Gong et al., 2025; van Oostveen et al., 2017).

The internal process of motivation and self-efficacy

Beyond external supports and constraints, the development of innovation and research capacity was fundamentally an internal psychological process where nurses continuously negotiated their self-perceptions, motivations, and professional identities (Yang et al., 2026; Lönn et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2026).

Central barriers included low research self-efficacy, fear of failure, and the perception that research was not part of the nursing professional identity. Nurses described self-doubt about their intellectual capabilities, anxiety about statistical and methodological complexity, and the belief that research was the domain of academics rather than practicing clinicians (Yang et al., 2026; Roxburgh, 2006; Hagan and Walden, 2017; Strandberg et al., 2014). These internal barriers were often reinforced by external messages that devalued nursing research (Hwang et al., 2024; Walsh et al., 2023).

Facilitators in this domain involved the progressive development of confidence through successful experiences, the cultivation of intrinsic motivation linked to patient care improvement, and the integration of research into professional identity. As nurses accumulated positive research and innovation experiences, they described growing confidence, increasing curiosity, and the development of a researcher identity alongside their clinical identity (Yang et al., 2026; Shahsavari Isfahani et al., 2015; Yan et al., 2022; Scala et al., 2019; Tveit et al., 2015; Zhao et al., 2025). The experience of seeing one’s research lead to tangible improvements in patient care was described as particularly powerful in sustaining research engagement and building lasting research capacity (Yang et al., 2026; O’Brien et al., 2022; Khoddam et al., 2023).

Discussion

This systematic review and qualitative meta-aggregation synthesized evidence from 28 studies and identified four synthesized findings on the facilitators and barriers to clinical nurses’ innovation and research capacity: navigating organizational culture and structural support; the dual nature of professional knowledge and competence; the mediating role of leadership and mentorship; and the internal process of motivation and self-efficacy. Taken together, these findings portray innovation and research capacity not as a static individual attribute but as a dynamic, multilevel accomplishment that is continuously negotiated between nurses and their practice environments. In the following sections, we move beyond re-stating each result to interpret what these findings mean for theory, for practice, and for the future research agenda.

Organizational context as a critical enabler

The first finding reveals that organizational culture and structural support serve as the foundational context for nurses’ research engagement. This aligns with socio-technical systems theory, which posits that individual performance is shaped by the interaction between people and their organizational environments (Hendrich et al., 2008). Our findings extend previous quantitative research—which has repeatedly documented workload and time scarcity as the leading self-reported barriers to research participation (Hagan and Walden, 2017; Scala et al., 2019)—by illuminating the specific mechanisms through which organizational factors operate: time scarcity functions not only as a practical constraint but also as a symbolic signal of what the organization truly values. The finding that protected research time is a critical facilitator has significant implications for healthcare policy and management practice, suggesting that research engagement cannot be sustained as an add-on to clinical responsibilities but must be structurally integrated into nursing roles, workload models, and career ladders. This suggests a reframing of research time from a discretionary privilege to a legitimate component of professional nursing work—a shift that challenges prevailing productivity metrics built almost exclusively around direct care hours.

Knowledge development as a transformative process

The second finding illuminates the dual role of knowledge and competence in both constraining and enabling research capacity. This finding is consistent with self-efficacy theory (Bandura, 1977), which posits that confidence in one’s capabilities is shaped by mastery experiences, vicarious learning, and verbal encouragement. Our synthesis reveals that the path from research novice to competent researcher is not simply a matter of acquiring technical skills but involves a fundamental transformation in how nurses perceive themselves and their professional capabilities. The critical importance of accessible, relevant research education suggests that generic research training may be less effective than contextually embedded learning opportunities that connect directly to clinical practice (Wright et al., 2020; Gao et al., 2022; Moore et al., 2012). More provocatively, the persistence of research literacy gaps decades after evidence-based practice became a cornerstone of nursing education raises questions about whether the problem lies in individual nurses’ preparation or in a structural disconnect between academic research training and the realities of clinical work—a disconnect that academic–practice partnership models are specifically designed to bridge (Baptiste et al., 2022; Beal, 2012).

Relational dynamics as scaffolding

The third finding emphasizes the profoundly relational nature of research capacity development. This finding aligns with communities of practice theory, which posits that professional learning and identity development occur through participation in social communities (Wenger, 1998). The synthesis reveals that mentors and leaders serve not only as sources of technical guidance but as identity mediators who help nurses imagine themselves as capable researchers (Landeen et al., 2017; Fullam et al., 2018; Nowell et al., 2017). The absence of such relationships was described as leaving nurses in a kind of professional limbo where they could not envision a research-active professional self (Lee et al., 2024; Walsh et al., 2023). This interpretation carries a practical consequence that goes beyond calls for ‘more mentorship’: mentorship functions as the transmission belt through which organizational support is converted into individual capability. Structural investments—funding, infrastructure, protected time—are therefore unlikely to yield returns unless they are paired with relational infrastructure, such as formal mentorship programs and research-supportive leadership development (Heitschmidt et al., 2021; Berthelsen and Hølge-Hazelton, 2018).

The internal journey of becoming a nurse researcher

The fourth finding reveals the internal psychological work required to develop research capacity, resonating with the professional identity formation literature, which describes how individuals internalize the values, norms, and behaviors of a profession (Cruess et al., 2016). Our synthesis suggests that research capacity development involves an identity expansion in which nurses add ‘researcher’ to their existing professional identities (Lönn et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2026). The fear and self-doubt described by participants reflect the vulnerability inherent in this identity work (Yang et al., 2026). The finding that successful early research experiences are transformative highlights the importance of designing initial research experiences for success—for example, through small-scale, well-supported quality improvement or evidence-based practice projects that allow mastery experiences to accumulate before larger scholarly undertakings (Bandura, 1977; Scala et al., 2019).

Toward an integrated conceptual understanding

Read together, the four synthesized findings suggest a multilevel, reciprocal model of innovation and research capacity development. Organizational structures define what is possible; education defines what is knowable; relationships define what is imaginable; and internal motivation determines what is pursued. Crucially, the four levels interact: structural support without mentorship leaves resources unused, education without organizational permission produces frustrated capability, and motivation without scaffolding dissipates over time. This reciprocity helps explain why single-level interventions—an isolated workshop, a lone enthusiastic manager, a one-off funding stream—so often fail to produce durable research capacity (Segrott et al., 2006; Lode et al., 2015). It also positions innovation and research capacity as a property of practice systems rather than solely of individual nurses, with implications for how capacity-building programs are designed, resourced, and evaluated.

Cross-cultural considerations

The findings reveal important cross-cultural differences that warrant attention. Studies from high-resource Western settings emphasized organizational culture, mentorship quality, career pathway structures, and professional development systems as key factors (van Oostveen et al., 2017; Walsh et al., 2023; Newington et al., 2021; Lee et al., 2024). Studies from Chinese contexts highlighted the influence of educational preparation, hierarchical organizational structures, and the evolving recognition of nursing research within healthcare reform (Jiang et al., 2019; Yan et al., 2022; Gong et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2026). Studies from resource-limited settings identified fundamental infrastructure deficits and competing health priorities as overwhelming barriers (Nkrumah et al., 2018; Kyei et al., 2023; Owusu et al., 2025; Lang et al., 2010). These variations suggest that while the four synthesized findings are broadly transferable, interventions to support research capacity must be culturally and contextually adapted rather than transplanted wholesale across systems.

Implications for future research

This synthesis points to several priorities for future inquiry. First, longitudinal qualitative research is needed to trace how research identity and capacity evolve over time, particularly following structured capacity-building interventions. Second, the mechanisms linking relational scaffolding to sustained research engagement warrant theory-driven investigation, for example through realist approaches that ask what works, for whom, and in what circumstances. Third, the experiences of nurses in low- and middle-income countries remain underrepresented; future primary studies in these settings are essential to test and refine the transferability of the synthesized findings. Fourth, implementation studies are needed to evaluate multilevel interventions that combine structural, educational, and relational components, rather than evaluating single-component programs in isolation (Holden et al., 2012; Parker et al., 2017).

Implications for nursing practice and policy

The integrated findings mandate a holistic and intentional approach to supporting clinical nurses’ innovation and research capacity across organizational, educational, relational, and personal dimensions.

Healthcare administrators should prioritize structural supports including protected research time, dedicated research infrastructure, access to databases and resources, and funding mechanisms for nursing research. Organizational policies should explicitly value and reward research engagement in performance evaluations, promotion criteria, and professional recognition programs. Healthcare institutions should establish formal mentorship programs that pair novice nurse researchers with experienced mentors and create communities of practice for research collaboration (Landeen et al., 2017).

Nurse educators should ensure that research education is integrated throughout nursing curricula at both undergraduate and graduate levels, with emphasis on practical, clinically relevant research skills. Continuing education programs should offer accessible, incremental research training that accommodates working nurses’ schedules and builds competence progressively. Academic-practice partnerships can bridge the gap between research education and research practice (Bowles et al., 2023; Baptiste et al., 2022).

Nurse leaders and managers play a pivotal role as culture-setters and gatekeepers. Leadership development programs should include components on supporting nursing research, creating research-friendly climates, and mentoring staff in scholarly activities. Leaders who model research engagement and protect staff research time send powerful messages about organizational priorities (Guo et al., 2024; Xing et al., 2026).

Professional nursing organizations and policymakers should advocate for systemic supports including research funding earmarked for nursing studies, career pathways that include research expectations, and recognition of nursing research contributions to healthcare quality. Accreditation standards should incorporate research capacity as an expected outcome of nursing education programs and a criterion for healthcare organization excellence (Galuska et al., 2022).

Limitations

This synthesis has several limitations. First, while the search was comprehensive and the final included studies are geographically diverse, they may not represent all cultural perspectives, particularly from low-resource settings where research capacity challenges may differ substantially. Second, although the search strategy covered both English- and Chinese-language databases, all finally included studies were published in English; relevant studies published in other languages, and grey literature, may have been missed. Third, despite the use of a systematic approach with independent duplicate review, the screening and data extraction processes inevitably involve a degree of subjective judgment. Fourth, the concentration of included studies in the United Kingdom, Australia, China, and Ghana may limit the transferability of findings to other cultural and health-system settings. Finally, the quality assessment identified frequent limitations in researcher reflexivity reporting, which may have implications for the trustworthiness of some included studies; however, all synthesized findings were rated as unequivocal or credible and were supported by multiple studies of moderate-to-high methodological quality.

Conclusion

This systematic review and qualitative meta-aggregation indicates that clinical nurses’ innovation and research capacity is a complex process influenced by multiple interacting factors across organizational, educational, relational, and personal dimensions. It evolves through the interplay between structural supports and constraints, educational preparation and ongoing learning, mentorship relationships and the internal psychological work of building confidence and professional identity. Organizational culture and leadership support create the essential context; education and mentorship provide the tools and guidance; and internal motivation and growing self-efficacy sustain engagement over time. Consequently, cultivating robust innovation and research capacity among clinical nurses should be established as a fundamental and mandatory objective of healthcare organizations and nursing education. Achieving this goal requires dedicated collaboration among healthcare administrators, nurse educators, clinical leaders, and policymakers. Moving nursing research from the margins to the mainstream of clinical practice requires sustained, coordinated action to address the systemic barriers and leverage the facilitators identified in this synthesis.
