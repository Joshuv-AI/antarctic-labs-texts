A single-center study of secondary intussusception in children with Meckel’s diverticulum
Authors: Rongpeng Zhang, Yu Liu, Ning An
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThere are a limited number of clinical reports addressing intussusception resulting from uncomplicated Meckel’s diverticulum (MD). This paper examines the clinical characteristics and treatment strategies for intussusception secondary to MD, synthesizes clinical experiences, and offers reference value for clinicians in selecting appropriate surgical approaches. MethodsA retrospective study was conducted involving thirty patients diagnosed with intussusception secondary to MD, who underwent surgical intervention in the Department of Pediatric Surgery at Linyi People’s Hospital between June 2016 and June 2025. The patients were categorized into two groups based on the presence or absence of ectopic tissue within the diverticulum: the ectopic tissue group (Group A, 10 patients) and the non-ectopic tissue group (Group B, 20 patients). A comparative analysis of the clinical data between the two groups was performed. The results indicated that Group A included six patients with ectopic gastric mucosal gland tissue and four patients with ectopic pancreatic tissue. It was observed that, in female patients with MD, the ectopic tissue predominantly originated from gastric glands, whereas in male patients, it predominantly originated from pancreatic tissue (P = 0.035). Furthermore, compared to Group B, patients in Group A exhibited a greater distance from the ileocecal valve (P = 0.006) and increased diverticulum length (P = 0.017). However, no significant differences were found between the groups concerning age, gender, clinical symptoms, or diverticulum width (P > 0.05). ConclusionsThe clinical presentation of intussusception secondary to MD is atypical, necessitating surgical exploration and postoperative histopathological examination for a definitive diagnosis. The findings of this study indicate that ectopic tissue is not the primary etiological factor in intussusception secondary to MD. However, diverticula containing ectopic tissue tend to be marginally longer and are located at a greater distance from the ileocecal valve compared to those lacking ectopic tissue.

Meckel’s diverticulum (MD) is the most prevalent congenital malformation of the digestive tract, caused by the omphalomesenteric or vitelline duct not being completely obliterated [1]. The incidence of MD ranges from 0.3%~4.9%, with a higher prevalence in males compared to females. Complications associated with MD occur in 4.0%~9.0% of cases, and its clinical manifestations are varied, including bleeding, infection, intussusception, intestinal obstruction, perforation, and tumor formation. Notably, intussusception secondary to MD constitutes 11.0% of these complications. Intussusception is a common cause of acute abdomen in pediatric surgery, with MD being a significant etiological factor for secondary intussusception in children. The onset of intussusception secondary to MD is often insidious, making preoperative diagnosis challenging and potentially delaying treatment. This delay can result in intestinal ischemic necrosis, adversely affecting the quality of life in children and posing a risk to life [2–7]. Despite its clinical significance, there is a paucity of reports focusing solely on intussusception secondary to MD. This study reviews the clinical data of 30 pediatric patients with intussusception secondary to MD who were admitted to the Pediatric Surgery Center of Linyi People’s Hospital from June 2016 to June 2025.

Methods

This study was approved by the Ethics Committees of Linyi People’s Hospital (202508-H-022). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in 2013). Individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived.

73 patients of MD under 14 years old in the Department of Pediatric Surgery of Linyi People ‘s Hospital from June 2016 to June 2025 with complications were collected in 10 years. Thirty patients were confirmed by surgery and postoperative pathology as secondary intussusception with MD. According to the pathological result, thirty patients were divided into two groups: ectopic tissue group (Group A) and no ectopic tissue group (Group B). Basic information (age, gender, onset time), clinical manifestations (abdominal pain, vomiting, bloody stool), laboratory examination (white blood cell count (WBC), neutrophil ratio), surgical information (surgical method, distance from diverticulum to ileocecal valve, length and width of diverticulum, intestinal necrosis, ectopic tissue source) of the two groups were collected retrospectively.

Surgical techniques can be categorized into two types: (1) wedge resection of the diverticulum, which involves excision of the diverticulum without removal of the adjacent segment of the intestinal tract, and (2) intestinal resection, which entails removal of both the diverticulum and the contiguous segment of the intestine.

The software SPSS 27.0 was used to analyze the data. Numerical variables were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (‾χ ± SD) or median and interquartile range (IQR). Frequency was the method of representation for categorical variables. For statistical analysis, either Fisher’s exact test or Pearson Chi squared test was used for categorical data, whereas continuous data was analyzed with the t-test or Mann-Whitney U test. A P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

In this study, a total of 73 patients of MD with complications were collected in 10 years, including 30 patients of secondary intussusception with MD (41.1%). The cohort comprised thirty patients with a male-to-female ratio of 21:9. The age of onset varied between 2 and 161 months, with a median age of 55 months. Within this group, 7 patients (23.3%) were < 12 months old, 5 patients (16.7%) were 12–36 months old, and 18 patients (60%) were > 36 months old. Clinically, abdominal pain was observed in twenty-nine patients, vomiting in twenty-one patients, and bloody stool in ten patients.

All thirty patients underwent laparoscopic exploration, and all diverticula were located in the terminal ileum. Among them, 18 patients underwent partial resection of the intestine + intestinal anastomosis, 6 patients underwent necrotic bowel resection + intestinal anastomosis due to intestinal necrosis, and the remaining 6 patients underwent wedge resection of the diverticulum. Comparison of clinical characteristics of children with and without ectopic tissues

In this study, 27 cases (90.0%) of children presented with a MD length of ≥ 2 cm out of a total of 30 cases, while 13 cases (43.3%) exhibited a distance of ≥ 40 cm between the diverticulum and the ileocecal valve. According to the pathological results, 30 patients were divided into ectopic tissue group (Group A, 10 patients) and non-ectopic tissue group (Group B, 20 patients). Compared with Group B, Group A patients had a longer distance from the ileocecal valve (P = 0.006) and a longer diverticulum length (P = 0.017). However, there was no significant differences in age, gender, onset time, clinical manifestations, WBC, neutrophil ratio, surgical method, and diverticulum width between the two groups (P > 0.05), and there was no significant difference in the incidence of intestinal necrosis (P > 0.05). Comparison of clinical characteristics of patient with different pathological tissue sources

In this study, 10 cases (33.3%) exhibited ectopic tissue within the diverticulum, comprising 6 cases of simple gastric gland tissue and 4 cases of simple pancreatic tissue. The male-to-female ratio was 6:4, with the age of onset ranging from 9 to 145 months, with a median age of 66.9 months. Clinically, all 10 cases presented with abdominal pain, 6 cases with vomiting, and 3 cases with bloody stool. The distance from MD to the ileocecal valve ranged from 20 to 50 cm, an average distance of 41.5 cm. The length of the MD varied from 2.0 to 10 cm, an average length of 4.3 cm. The width of the diverticulum ranged from 1.0 to 3.0 cm, an average width of 1.4 cm. Comparative analysis of clinical characteristics between the two groups revealed that ectopic tissue in the female MD predominantly originated from the gastric gland, whereas in the male MD it primarily originated from the pancreas (P = 0.035). Discussion

Currently, the literature concerning complications associated with MD predominantly addresses issues such as bleeding, obstruction, and inflammation. Reports specifically examining MD secondary intussusception are relatively scarce. Nonetheless, MD is recognized as a significant etiological factor for secondary intussusception in pediatric populations, with reported incidence rates ranging from 9.5% to 38% [1, 7–9]. The majority of cases involving MD secondary intussusception are classified as either ileal or ileocecal types. Notably, non-surgical reduction via air enema is often unsuccessful in these cases, thereby necessitating surgical intervention as the definitive treatment approach [1, 2]. The mechanism by which intussusception occurs secondary to MD remains incompletely understood. Several potential explanations have been proposed: (1) MD may become ensnared within the intestinal cavity, leading to a localized narrowing of the intestinal lumen, which acts as a focal point and guides the proximal ileum into the distal intestine or even the colon, thereby causing ileal or ileocolic intussusception [8, 10]. (2) An increased density of nerve fibers between the muscular layers of the intestinal wall in the region of the diverticulum may result in more frequent impulses from interstitial cells of Cajal, intensifying diverticular peristalsis and potentially inducing intussusception [11]. (3) The presence of the diverticulum may alter the peristaltic rhythm of the adjacent bowel, thereby precipitating intussusception [6]. (4) Ectopic tissue within the diverticulum may serve as a pathological trigger point, or its mass effect may lead to intussusception [12, 13]. (5) Following the occurrence of intussusception, the MD itself may invert into the intestinal cavity, further inducing intussusception [14].

Approximately 15–50% of MD cases may contain ectopic tissue, predominantly comprising gastric mucosal tissue, pancreatic tissue, and colonic and duodenal mucosal tissue. Research indicates that the incidence of complications in diverticula with ectopic tissue is tenfold higher than in those without [6]. In symptomatic diverticula, the prevalence of ectopic gastric mucosal tissue ranges from 24.2% to 71%, which is associated with gastrointestinal bleeding. This is followed by ectopic pancreatic tissue, with an incidence of 0% to 12% [7, 15–18]. Nonetheless, some studies suggest that, compared to MD without ectopic tissue, the presence of ectopic tissue only exacerbates clinical symptoms such as abdominal pain, vomiting, and hematochezia, without increasing the incidence of related complications [19]. Ectopic tissue is typically located at the apex of MD. Although ectopic tissue can serve as the focal point for intussusception, reports of intussusception resulting from inverted MD are rare [10, 14, 20, 21]. In this study, no significant differences were observed in clinical symptoms and intestinal necrosis between MD patients with ectopic tissue and those without. The presence of ectopic tissue within the diverticulum was not identified as a primary cause of secondary intussusception, aligning with findings from previous research.

Currently, there is a consensus that diverticula with complications should be surgically excised; however, the decision to remove asymptomatic diverticula remains contentious [7]. To enhance treatment selection, both domestic and international scholars have investigated the length, width, area, and proximity to the ileocecal valve of the diverticulum to better predict the likelihood of ectopic tissue presence and associated complications. According to the Hansen CC research team, in the general population, the distance between MD and ileocecal valve is 7~ 200 cm (average 52.4 cm), the length of diverticulum is 0.4 ~ 11.0 cm (average 3.05 cm), and the diameter is 0.3 ~ 7.0 cm (average 1.58 cm) [7]. Matthias reported that the distance between MD and ileocecal valve > 40 cm has predictive value for the presence of ectopic tissue, with a sensitivity of 86% and a specificity of 89%. It is considered that the prolongation of the distance between diverticulum and ileocecal valve is a predictor of ectopic tissue in symptomatic MD [22]. Mackey found that 30% of diverticula with a length of 2 cm had complications [23]. The study of Lohsiriwat shown that the diverticulum with complications is longer, the base is wider, and the probability of ectopic tissue is higher than that without complications [24]. Park found that the length of MD greater than 2 cm is more prone to complications. The risk factors for complications in asymptomatic MD are: male patients and ectopic tissue in the diverticulum [25]. This study suggested that, MD containing ectopic tissue was both longer and located at a greater distance from the ileocecal valve compared to MD without ectopic tissue. Furthermore, this particular type of MD, when associated with intussusception, was situated even further from the ileocecal valve, increasing the likelihood of missed diagnoses. Consequently, the study employed laparoscopic exploration as a diagnostic and therapeutic approach, which significantly enhanced diagnostic accuracy and reduced the incidence of missed diagnoses.

Due to the relatively limited sample size in this study, no significant differences were observed in the clinical features among various types of ectopic tissues. Future research should aim to expand the sample size to determine whether clinical specificity differences exist between different ectopic tissues. Additionally, this study focused solely on the clinical characteristics of children with intussusception secondary to MD, subsequent studies should investigate the differences in clinical characteristics between intussusception secondary to MD and other surgically treated intussusceptions, as well as further explore the potential causes of intussusception secondary to MD.

In conclusion, the clinical presentation of intussusception secondary to MD is atypical, necessitating surgical exploration and postoperative histopathological examination for a definitive diagnosis. The findings of this study indicate that ectopic tissue is not the primary etiological factor in intussusception secondary to MD. However, diverticula containing ectopic tissue tend to be marginally longer and are located at a greater distance from the ileocecal valve compared to those lacking ectopic tissue.
