Clinical evaluation of a growth factor bioavailability enhanced allograft in anterior lumbar interbody fusion (ALIF)
Authors: Pierce D. Nunley, Stacie Tran, Marcus Stone
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInterbody fusion, especially anterior lumbar interbody fusion (ALIF), is a common treatment for spinal conditions like degenerative disc disease (DDD). Various graft materials, including autografts, allografts, and synthetic substitutes, are used to facilitate fusion, though each has limitations. A novel growth factor bioavailability-enhanced allograft (GFBA) has been introduced, which aims to enhance fusion outcomes by unlocking critical growth factors in the bone matrix.MethodsConsecutive patients who received treatment with GFBA at a single site between January 2022 and December 2022 were invited to participate prior to 12 months postoperative assessment. Those who consented were evaluated prospectively by computed tomography (CT), flexion extension X-ray, and completed questionnaires regarding pain and disability.ResultsFifty patients (64 treated levels), with a mean age of 61.5 years old, 62.0% female, and a mean body mass index (BMI) of 31.0 kg/m2 participated. Thirty-six patients (72.0%) underwent single level ALIF, and 14 (28.0%) underwent two-level ALIFs. All subjects (100%) had less than three degrees of motion upon flexion extension. CT scans showed complete bridging bone with no evidence of supplemental fixation failure for 89.1% of levels (defined as 3A), with the remaining 10.9% showing bone growth from both end plates and no sign of supplemental fixation failure (defined as 2A). Statistically significant improvements were observed in low back pain (60.1 to 29.6), left leg pain (33.8 to 21.4), and right leg pain (40.2 to 18.4), all P<0.05. No GFBA-related complications or revision/reoperations were reported.ConclusionsThis study evaluated a novel GFBA and reported similar fusion rates to published reports of iliac crest bone graft (ICBG). Significant clinical improvements and no GFBA related complications or revision/reoperations were reported. This is the first study evaluating GFBA and demonstrated its safety and efficacy in ALIF procedures for DDD.

Introduction

Interbody fusion is an established treatment option for a wide range of spinal pathologies including degenerative disc disease (DDD), herniated discs, spondylolisthesis, infections, deformity, and neoplasia, with DDD being the most common diagnosis for spine fusion surgery (1). The primary goal of interbody fusion is to provide spinal stabilization (2). More than 400,000 cases per year are performed in the United States, making interbody fusion the most performed spinal surgery (3,4). Further, in a 2018 report of operating room procedures, McDermott and Liang report a total of 455,500 spine fusions performed annually in the U.S (5).

The anterior lumbar interbody fusion (ALIF) approach was first introduced in 1932, and there has been an increasing amount of evidence to demonstrate its safety and efficacy compared to posterior approaches (6-8). Potential advantages of ALIF include increased intervertebral height as it allows for implantation of larger cages, facilitates normal lumbar lordosis, and indirect enlargement of neural foramina space (9-11).

ALIF surgeries involve the anterior placement of an interbody device in a disc space such as a cage, spacer, or structural graft to promote bony fusion (6). There are several different types of grafts that can be chosen for placement which include: autograft, mineralized bone allograft, demineralized bone matrix (DBM) and/or synthetic graft substitutes such as the calcium phosphates. In addition, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), growth factors, and synthetic peptides are being utilized to optimize fusion rates. The gold standard with which all other bone graft materials are compared remains autologous cancellous bone (12). Allogenic bone has been used as an alternative to autograft because it circumvents the morbidity of autograft harvest. However, allograft can lead to an immune response, which may result in slower fusion and cause inflammation at the treated level (13). The relative concerns over the use of either autograft or allograft have led to the development of numerous bone graft substitutes (14).

A novel fiber based growth factor bioavailability-enhanced allograft (GFBA) bioimplant that is prepared from donated human tissue using a process that unlocks the growth factors present in the bone matrix, including bone morphogenetic proteins 2 and 7, transforming growth factor beta 1 (TGF-β1), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) enhancing their bioavailability was recently introduced to the market (15,16). The purpose of this study is to evaluate the performance of GFBA when used to promote anterior interbody fusion in patients with DDD of the lumbar spine. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-25-129/rc).

Methods

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Western Institutional Review Board Copernicus Group Institutional Review Board (WCG IRB) (IRB Study No. 1356889) and informed consent was obtained from all individual participants. For this ambispective evaluation, 64 consecutive patients who had previously undergone a one- or two-level anterior lumbar fusion procedure and treated with GFBA [Natural Matrix Protein® (NMP®) Fibers, Induce Biologics USA Inc., Irvine, CA, USA] between January 2022 and December 2022 for the treatment of lumbar DDD were identified from two surgeons’ existing medical records. All surgeries were performed at a single center over a 12-month period. The surgical procedure used GFBA that was rehydrated with sterile saline and was not combined with any other graft material which was placed within an interbody cage of surgeon preference, followed by supplemental posterior fixation.

Screening and enrollment

Eligible subjects were patients who had radiological evidence of DDD that was treated with GFBA during an anterior lumbar spinal fusion procedure, were a minimum of 18 years of age, had current contact information, and were willing to participate per protocol. Patients were excluded from participation if they were currently imprisoned, experiencing a major mental illness, radiographic imaging/computed tomography (CT) was contraindicated, or if they had previous lumbar fusion or arthroplasty surgery at the treated level(s).

Patients meeting the pre-screening eligibility criteria were contacted by phone, provided a brief description of the evaluation, and if interested in participating, were scheduled for a one-time prospective assessment. The assessment included neurological examination of strength and sensation, anterior-posterior (AP)/lateral and flexion/extension radiographs, lumbar CT scan, and completion of patient reported outcomes (PROs) surveys. The surveys utilized standardized assessments for back and leg pain (100 mm Visual Analog Scale, VAS), back function (Oswestry Disability Index, ODI), and quality of life (12-Item Short Form Health Survey, SF-12). Prior to performing the prospective assessments, participants underwent Informed Consent during which all research-related questions were addressed, and participants provided written consent on the Institutional Review Board (IRB) approved form.

Of the 64 eligible patients, 4 were unable to be reached and 10 elected not to participate in the study. The final study population included 50 patients and 64 treated levels. After informed consent, participants completed the required prospective follow-up visit including a CT scan, patient-reported outcome questionnaires, neurological exam, and 4-view lumbar radiographs.

Primary endpoint

The primary endpoint of the study was radiographic fusion at approximately 12-months postoperative. Fusion was defined as a CT fusion grade of 3A (complete bridging bone with no sign of supplemental fixation failure) and less than three degrees of motion on flexion extension X-ray, see Figures 1,2 for representative imaging. The numeric-alpha scale of CT grades for bridging bone is provided as Figure 3 (17-22). Secondary endpoint

Secondary endpoints included change in PRO scores at the prospective visit compared to the preoperative scores, hospital duration, and GFBA-related complications and revision/reoperations within the 12-month postoperative period. Postoperative progress notes were reviewed, and all complications, treatments, and resolution status were documented. A complication was defined as any unintended medical occurrence following the patient’s index surgery. Each complication was classified as an adverse event if it required treatment, or as an observation if no intervention was needed. The clinician also assessed the severity of each adverse event and its relationship to GFBA. Consistent with published literature, minimal clinical important difference (MCID) for PROs postoperatively when compared to baseline were defined as a 20 mm reduction in VAS score (23), a 15-point reduction in ODI score (24,25), an increase of 3.77 points for the SF-12 physical component scale (PCS) (26) and an increase of 3.29 points for the mental component scale (MCS) (26).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analysis was performed for all primary and secondary endpoints. Non-parametric Wilcoxon Signed Rank Test was applied for pre to postoperative comparisons, with statistical significance set at alpha of 0.05. Analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel. Discussion

Advancements in biomaterial engineering have led to the development of novel alternatives to autograft to promote bony ingrowth in the setting of lumbar interbody fusion surgery. Although autologous cancellous bone from the iliac crest bone graft (ICBG) remains the gold standard to achieving high fusion rates (18-21,27-29), there are many advantages of bone graft substitutes that circumvents poor outcomes associated with autograft harvesting such as longer operating times, donor site pain, and longer hospital stay (18,30,31).

There are multiple options of bone graft substitute that are manufactured with different materials to emulate the osteogenic, osteoconductive, and osteoinductive properties of autologous bone. Options include recombinant human bone morphogenic protein-2 (rhBMP-2), DBM, and ceramic fillers. A previous review reported high fusion rates for each type of bone grafting materials, though they also present their own limitations and risks (32). rhBMP-2 has been shown to produce significant inflammatory responses and adverse structural changes including hematomas, heterotopic ossification, and osteolysis (32,33). DBM lacks mechanical strength and often necessitates mixing of additional bone graft materials to improve osteoconductive process and handling properties (34). Ceramic fillers have low tensile strength, are susceptible to fracture, and often used as a bone graft extender. Ceramics also exhibit significant variability due to differences in composition, manufacturing, porosity, and structure which impact their efficacy (35,36). An alternative bone graft option, a growth factor bioavailability enhanced allograft has been developed to overcome the limitations of existing bone graft substitutes while maintaining high fusion success through a novel processing technique enhancing the bioavailability of multiple growth factors naturally found in bone.

This study evaluated the performance of the fiber form of GFBA to promote interbody fusion from an anterior approach with supplemental posterior fixation in patients with DDD of the lumbar spine. Although image-based fusion assessment is a common primary outcome in interbody fusion studies, there is currently no universally accepted standard fusion definition.

Many studies define image-based fusion as positive signs of bony fusion, including bridging bone, and/or absence of negative signs, including dynamic measures of instability (angular or translation motion), static measures (hardware failure or loss of correction), and radiolucency (37). The U.S Food and Drug Administration (FDA) defines fusion as the absence of translational motion (>3 mm) and angular motion (>5 degrees), and continuity of bridging bone (38). A recent systematic review found that studies most commonly evaluated the continuity of bony bridging, instability, and radiolucency. Radiolucency was most commonly defined as less than 50% around the cage, while acceptable instability thresholds ranged from 1.5 to 5 mm of translation and 0 to 11 degrees of angular motion (39).

In this study, fusion was defined as continuity of bridging bone based on a CT fusion grade of 3A (complete bridging bone and no signs of supplemental fixation failure) as illustrated in Figure 3, and less than three degrees of motion on X-ray. To ensure accurate assessment, evaluations were performed using both CT and radiographs at 12 months postoperative by an independent reviewer who was blinded to the clinical status of the participants. This combined assessment approach is a widely accepted methodology as reported in a large systematic review to assess fusion (38), and found to be most reliable in detecting pseudoarthrosis in a separate meta-analysis (39). The results of this study demonstrated high fusion rates of 89.1% at 12 months postoperative, which is similar to published reports of ICBG and other bone graft substitutes (38).

Clinical studies have shown that ICBG achieves favorable fusion rates, ranging from 78.8% to 100% in single-level, uninstrumented ALIF (32,40-45) and between 71% and 98.6% in single or two-level ALIF combined with posterior fixation (46-49). However, despite these findings, graft harvesting can lead to medical complications, as highlighted in a retrospective review of 414 lumbar surgeries. This study reported incidences of major complications such as abdominal herniation, vascular trauma, deep infections, and iliac wing fractures (49). Other studies have reported persistent and significant pain at donor sites when evaluating ALIF procedures (50,51).

Numerous studies have also demonstrated the effectiveness of rhBMP-2 as a bone graft substitute to promote bony fusion in the setting of ALIF surgery which has been reported to be between 94.5–100% by 24-months postop (52-55). Despite the effectiveness of rhBMP-2, it is costly and associated with complications including subsidence. A 6-year follow-up study reported high rates of subsidence due to severe osteolysis, with 5.4% of patients requiring additional surgery (56). Separately in another study, there was a 70% incidence of subsidence in ALIF treated levels (57). In this present study, there was only one incidence of subsidence representing 1.6% of levels treated and the rate of adverse events, although were not related to the use of GFBA, align with published literature reporting on bone graft substitutes in ALIF surgery (41).

There are few clinical studies assessing the effectiveness of DBM or ceramic fillers. A case series reported a 90% fusion rate based on flexion extension with DBM in circumferential ALIF (58). However, a prospective Level 1 study demonstrated high rate of graft collapse and pseudoarthrosis in anterior cervical fusion (59). Studies on ceramic fillers, with relatively low sample sizes (n<30) have also shown varied fusion rates ranging between 79.3–95.83% (60,61). Due to limited and conflicting findings, additional large clinical studies are needed to evaluate the safety and efficacy of both DBM and ceramic fillers.

This study reported significant improvements in back and leg pain, disability index, and both mental and physical health components. The mean improvement in back pain, leg pain and disability align with published literature evaluating bone graft substitutes in fusion surgery (59,62). There were no adverse events related to the use of fiber bioimplant allograft that resulted in complications or return to the operating room for treatment of the index levels.

Limitations of this study include a single-center, non-randomized cohort which inherently introduces bias. Specifically, selection bias may arise since participation requires patient consent. However, this is a common consideration in any research study. To mitigate selection bias, patients were screened consecutively and all patients who met inclusion and exclusion criteria were invited to participate in the study. Furthermore, the choice of interbody cage was left to the surgeon’s discretion, which may have introduced variability across the patient population. However, a large, multi-center study incorporating different interbody cages would be better suited to evaluate the efficacy of GFBA independent of cage type and is necessary to confirm the generalizability of the findings from this study. Another limitation is the possible presence of hardware artifact observed in CT during the assessment of fusion. CT evaluations were supplemented with quantitative assessments utilizing flexion/extension radiographs. Range of motion assessment is widely accepted and relied upon in clinical trials, though there remains variability in intra and interobserver reliability (63). All assessments were performed at one center under precise radiograph evaluation protocol to limit such variability. In addition, this study utilized a single reviewer for fusion grading which may be a limitation; however, this provides a consistent and standardized assessment.

Conclusions

Despite the limitations, this study represents the first evaluation of GFBA with a relatively large sample size. This study is the first clinical report of GFBA and presents compelling evidence of the efficacy of GFBA in ALIF procedures for degenerative lumbar spine disease, achieving a high fusion rate and improved clinical outcomes. The results of the study position GFBA as a viable alternative to autograft.
