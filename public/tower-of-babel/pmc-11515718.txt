Genome-wide identification of CAMTA genes and their expression dependence on light and calcium signaling during seedling growth and development in mung bean
Authors: Adhityo Wicaksono, Teerapong Buaboocha
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCalmodulin-binding transcription activator (CAMTA) is comprised of a group of transcription factors and plays an important role in the Ca2+ signaling pathway, mediating various molecular responses via interactions with other transcription factors and binding to the promoter region of specific genes. Mung beans (Vigna radiata) are one of the most commonly consumed commodities in Asia. To date, CAMTA proteins have not been characterized in this important crop plant.ResultsEight paralogous VrCAMTA genes were identified and found to be distributed on five of the 11 chromosomes. The proteins possessed CG-1 DNA-binding domains with bipartite NLS signals, ankyrin domains, CaM-binding IQ motifs, and CaM-binding domain (CaMBD). The 2 kb upstream regions of VrCAMTA genes contained sequence motifs of abscisic acid-responsive elements (ABRE) and ethylene-responsive elements (ERE), and binding sites for transcription factors of the bZIP and bHLH domains. Analysis of RNA-seq data from a public repository revealed ubiquitous expression of the VrCAMTA genes, as VrCAMTA1 was expressed at the highest level in seedling leaves, whereas VrCAMTA8 was expressed at the lowest level, which agreed with the RT-qPCR analysis performed on the first true leaves. On day four after leaf emergence, all VrCAMTA genes were upregulated, with VrCAMTA1 exhibiting the highest degree of upregulation. In darkness on day 4, upregulation was not observed in most VrCAMTA genes, except VrCAMTA7, for which a low degree of upregulation was found, whereas no difference was found in VrCAMTA8 expression between light and dark conditions. Treatment with calcium ionophores enhanced VrCAMTA expression under light and/or dark conditions at different times after leaf emergence, suggesting that calcium signaling is involved in the light-induced upregulation of VrCAMTA gene expression.ConclusionsThe expression dependence of nearly all VrCAMTA genes on light and calcium signaling suggests their possible differential but likely complementary roles during the early stages of mung bean growth and development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-024-10893-z.Keywords: Fabaceae, Calcium signaling, Legumes, Seed, Transcription factor, Vigna radiata

Background

Calmodulin-binding transcription activator (CAMTA) is a transcription factor that plays a significant role in the calcium/calmodulin (CaM) signaling pathway. This protein family is found in plants e.g., in Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh [1, 2], rice (Oryza sativa L.) [3], poplar (Populus trichocarpa Torr. & A.Gray ex. Hook.) [4], etc., with 1,343 CAMTA proteins currently recorded in the Plant Transcription Factor Database. The protein is also found in nematode (Caenorhabditis elegans (Maupas, 1900)) [5], fruit fly (Drosophila melanogaster (Meigen, 1830)) [6], and human [7]. CAMTA was first identified in tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum L.) as an early ethylene-responsive gene (NtER1) that binds to CaM via a Ca2+-dependent pathway to initiate early senescence [8]. The protein relays a Ca2+/CaM signal either to the promoter of specific genes via an interaction between CAMTA CG-1 and the “CGCG” motif in the promoter region, or to other transcription factors, including CAMTA itself. This relaying action is important, as demonstrated in the literature. For example, a previous study in A. thaliana found that six CAMTA proteins were expressed specifically as responses to stress, growth, development, phytohormone signaling, and homeostasis [2]. Another study on durian (Durio zibethinus L.) identified ten CAMTA proteins, some of which are involved in fruit ripening [9].

The mung bean (Vigna radiata (L.) R. Wilczek) is a species of Fabaceae that is a popular food source in Asia, specifically Southeast Asia. Native to a few Asian and North Australian regions [10], this plant is a commodity mainly produced in India and Myanmar, which combined produce 30% of the global output of 5.3 million tons, as well as China, Indonesia, and Thailand, with a total global production yield of 721 kg/ha, produced in an area of 7.3 million ha [11]. Mung beans are widely consumed in Asian countries, especially China and India, as part of a cereal-based diet [12]. Plant seeds are either consumed directly or germinated and consumed as mung bean sprouts. The seeds contain high amounts of proteins and carbohydrates, whereas the sprouts contain high amounts of fiber [13]. To date, no study has characterized CAMTA proteins and genes in mung beans, including their expression patterns. Previous studies have associated CAMTA with phytohormones, stress responses, and plant growth and development [14].

The CAMTA proteins have been studied in several legume species (Fabaceae). One study was conducted in Medicago truncatula Gaertn., in which seven CAMTA proteins were identified, characterized, and their expression in response to hormone treatments and rhizobial infection was examined [15]. Another study identified and characterized eight CAMTA proteins in the long bean (Phaseolus vulgaris L.) and their differential expression during salt stress treatment using NaCl in leaves and roots was examined [16]. One of the recent case, in soybean (Glycine max (L.) Merr.), fifteen CAMTA genes were differentially expressed in response to salt, drought, and cold stress. Arabidopsis overexpressing GmCAMTA2 and GmCAMTA8 exhibited hypersensitivity to drought stress and downregulated the expression of stress-responsive genes [17].

In the presence of light, plant alter its physiological and developmental processes, including inhibition of embryonic stem growth and differentiation, cotyledon maturation, stem cell establishment, and activation of shoot and root apical meristems [18]. Characterized in A. thaliana, plants perceive light through four major photoreceptors: phytochrome (phy), which perceives red and far-red light [19]; cryptochrome (cry), which perceives blue light and UVA [20, 21]; phototropins (phot), which respond to blue light and play roles in phototropism, and some newly identified UVB photoreceptors [22]. Collectively these receptors affect plant morphogenesis in the absence and presence of light. The absence or limited exposure to light leads to arrested development or skotomorphogenesis, indicated by prolonged embryonic stem growth, limited root system, and underdeveloped cotyledons. Under light exposure, photomorphogenesis occurs and is indicated by short embryonic stem growth, self-regulating stem cell populations at the shoot and root apices, and expanded and photosynthetically active cotyledons [18]. In addition, one of the photoreceptors, phototropins, which mediate rapid and reversible movements such as phototropism, chloroplast movement, and stomal opening, in addition to leaf expansion and inhibition of hypocotyl elongation, induces an increase in cytosolic calcium levels; however, phototropin-mediated calcium signaling is still being studied [23]. CAMTA is one of the major transcription factors involved in calcium-mediated signaling; howerver, there are no studies on the regulation of plant CAMTA gene family by light and calcium signaling, both in the presence or absence of light.

In previous studies related to CAMTA, commonly studied abiotic stress triggers included drought [24–27], salinity [16, 28, 29], and cold [30–32]. Other studies on CAMTA have also suggested its role in responding to biotic stress [33]. For example, OsCAMTA3 has been shown to regulate resistance to rice blast disease and activating the expression of acetaldehyde dehydrogenase gene, OsALDH2B1. In turn, OsALDH2B1 can inhibit the expression of OsBZR1, a key component of the brassinosteroid signaling pathway. Interestingly, phytochrome B-mediated light signaling has been found to regulate rice resistance to sheath bright disease by inhibiting OsBZR1, suggesting a potential connection between light signaling and disease resistance [34]. However, the involvement of CAMTA genes in photomorphogenesis during the initial phase of seedling establishment has not been explored. In this study, CAMTA protein sequences in mung bean were compared with those of other plants, focusing on phylogenetic analysis, protein motifs, gene structures, and gene promoter sites using bioinformatics and gene expression analysis. Detailed bioinformatics analyses were used to characterize the protein and gene features, including the prediction of protein structures, and to visualize the DNA-binding and protein-binding elements. Gene expression analysis by RT-qPCR was used to monitor VrCAMTA expression during the initial days of mung bean seedling establishment in light and complete darkness. Additionally, the influence of calcium signaling on VrCAMTA expression under these conditions was examined using calcium ionophore treatment. This study provides a foundation for future studies related to photomorphogenesis and etiolation growth, with the goal of further understanding CAMTA functions during seedling establishment.

Methods

Germination, seedling growth, and treatment

Mung bean seeds were rinsed and submerged overnight in deionized water for imbibition. Five replicates were used per sampling day and the experiment followed a completely randomized experimental design. On the following day, the one seed group was treated in deionized water supplemented with 10 µM calcium ionophore (CI) A23187 (initially dissolved using dimethylsulfoxide/DMSO) (Sigma-Aldrich), while another group (the control) was treated with deionized water with DMSO for three hours [35, 36]. The imbibed seeds were placed in a small jar with a piece of wet paper towel (soaked in deionized water). The seeds were left to germinate for three days (plant age of 3 d, where the plumule grew from the split cotyledon) in a culture room (at 25 °C), watered with deionized water daily, either under the light or complete darkness. Sampling was conducted every two days from day 3 (day 0 of observation) of germination to day 7 (a total of 4-time sampling points). On day 9, the true leaf size was at its maximum before the emergence of new leaves. The sampled leaves were stored in nuclease-free tubes and submerged in liquid nitrogen for subsequent RNA extraction.

CAMTA sequences and confirmation of protein structures

The CAMTA protein sequences of mung beans were obtained from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) GenBank (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) gene database [37] and Plant Transcription Factor Database (https://planttfdb.gao-lab.org) annotated from the mung bean genome by keyword searches (i.e. “calmodulin-binding transcripton factor” and Vigna radiata) We subsequently performed the NCBI BLAST search using the identified protein against the V.radiata reference genome (Vradiata_ver6; GCF_000741045; submitted by Seoul National University, South Korea). Complete sequences were selected for further analysis, and redundant and partial sequences were omitted. The obtained CAMTA gene sequence on the genome, gene coding sequence (CDS), and protein sequences were checked for structural completeness and accuracy, with the signature domains and motifs as referred by Finkler et al. [14], based on the available GenBank domain information. The GenBank identifiers (ID) of the CAMTA proteins, CDS, genomic sequence from the annotated chromosome data, and chromosome ID were also acquired from the NCBI database. The gene positions within the chromosomes, chromosome sizes, number of exons, and promoter sequences 2000-bp upstream of the start codon of each gene were obtained from the genomic sequences.

To confirm the completeness of the CAMTA protein structure, the protein sequences were analyzed using the Hidden Markov Model in the Reference Proteome database in online pHMMER (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/Tools/hmmer/search/phmmer) [38], InterProScan web (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/about/interproscan) [39], from where the first Gene Ontology (GO) data were also obtained, and NCBI Conserved Domain Search (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/wrpsb.cgi) were used to confirm the structural annotation. The CaM-binding domain (CaMBD) was mapped using the Calmodulin Target Database (http://calcium.uhnres.utoronto.ca/ctdb/ctdb/sequence.html) [40]. Nuclear localization signals were detected using NLStradamus with default setting (2 state HMM static and prediction cutoff 0.6) (http://www.moseslab.csb.utoronto.ca/NLStradamus) [41], and NLS Mapper (https://nls-mapper.iab.keio.ac.jp/cgi-bin/NLS_Mapper_form.cgi) [42] was used to specifically identify the NLS sequence within the protein. Protein domain map visualizations were drawn using Domain Graph (DOG) v2.0.1 [43] using the structural confirmation results. Mung bean and A. thaliana protein-aligned data were used to identify the conservation of the bipartite NLS within the CG-1 domain and CaMBD, and graphical representatives of these patterns within the alignment were generated using WebLogo (https://weblogo.berkeley.edu/logo.cgi) [44]. In addition, eight mung bean CAMTA proteins and six identified CAMTAs of A. thaliana were subjected to Multiple Em for Motif Enrichment (MEME) [45] (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme) to identify motifs across the CAMTA proteins. In MEME, a motif is defined as an approximate sequence pattern that occurs repeatedly in a group of related sequences. Motifs detected by MEME were further examined using pHMMER [46] and NCBI BLASTP (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi) to determine whether they are similar to any known motifs.

Alignment of CAMTA gene sequences and phylogenetic analysis

To align and build a phylogenetic tree, all paralogous CAMTA gene sequences of mung beans, long beans (P. vulgaris L.), adzuki beans (Vigna angularis (Willd.) Ohwi & H. Ohashi), barrelclover (M. truncutula), and soybean (G. max) from Fabaceae; the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana; and non-vascular plants, including spreading earthmoss (Physcomitrella patens (Hedw.) Bruch & Schimp.; Funariaceae) and the flat-topped bogmoss (Sphagnum fallax (H.Klinggr.) H.Klinggr.; Sphagnaceae) as outgroups, were collected from the Plant Transcription Factor Database (http://planttfdb.gao-lab.org/index.php) and Phytozome database (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov). Similar to the preparation of VrCAMTA paralogous proteins, incomplete sequences and redundant isoforms were omitted from the list. The complete gene sequences were then aligned using ClustalW v2.1 program [47] and phylogenetically analyzed to generate a phylogenetic tree using maximum likelihood with ultrafast phylogenetic bootstrap approximation [48] and 1000 bootstrap replicates using IQ-TREE v2.3.6 [49] in Galaxy Europe (https://usegalaxy.eu) [50].

Chromosomal map and gene structures

The gene positions inside the chromosome were mapped with MG2C v2.1 (http://mg2c.iask.in/mg2c_v2.1) [51] using the information of the gene location ranges from the NCBI GenBank gene database and chromosome sizes. The gene exon-intron structures were mapped with the Gene Structure Display Server v2.0 (http://gsds.gao-lab.org) [52, 53] using the sequences of CDS, genome, and Newick format of phylogenetic tree (species-specific) of the protein sequences as the inputs. Data that included the remaining gene isoforms from the database (NCBI GenBank) and later annotated RNA-seq were used to validate the exon-intronic structure of the VrCAMTA genes.

Physicochemistry, subcellular localization, and GO analysis

The CAMTA protein sequences were submitted to ProtParam (https://web.expasy.org/protparam) [54] to acquire data on protein size, molecular weight, theoretical isoelectric point, stability index, and aliphatic index. The grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY) of each protein was used to identify the tendency of CAMTA to be hydrophilic (negative) or hydrophobic (positive) [55]. The subcellular localization and localization signals of the proteins were identified from the sequences using DeepLoc v2.0 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/service.php?DeepLoc-2.0) [56]. Protein GO terms were acquired with a search threshold of 0.7 from DeepGOWeb (https://deepgo.cbrc.kaust.edu.sa) [57] and from InterProScan [58].

Gene duplication events and selection types

Intraspecific gene evolutionary analysis for nucleotide synonymous (Ks) and non-synonymous (Ka) rates was conducted using KaKs_Calculator v3.0 [59], which employs the alignment of CDS and protein sequences performed by ParaAT v2.0 [60] in the AXT format. Both approximate methods, Yang-Nielsen (YN) [61] and modified Yang-Nielsen [62], and maximum likelihood methods, Goldman-Yang (GY) [63], model selection (MS), and model averaging (MA) [64], were used to estimate the rates of non-synonymous (Ka) and synonymous (Ks) substitutions. The Ks values were used to estimate the gene duplication event/divergence time (T): T = Ks/2λ [65, 66], where the substitution per site per year for eudicots (λ) is equal to 6.1 × 10− 9 [67]. Furthermore, a Ka/Ks ratio of < 1 between VrCAMTAs was indicative of negative mutations (any deleterious mutation during evolution is purified; hence, the existing gene sequence is conserved), while 1 indicated neutral mutations (balance between deleterious and beneficial mutations), and > 1 indicated positive mutations (any beneficial mutation is maintained, resulting in a new gene) [68].

Identification of promoter cis-acting regulatory elements and transcription factor binding sites

The 2000-bp promoter sequences of the CAMTA genes were submitted to PlantCARE (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html) [69, 70] to identify cis-acting regulatory elements responsible for phytohormones, stress responses, and growth and development, which were then categorized into heatmap tables to indicate the dominant elements of each gene. A list of cis-acting elements from a previous study was used [9]. Similarly, transcription factor binding sites were identified using PlantPAN v2.0 (http://plantpan2.itps.ncku.edu.tw/index.html) [71, 72]. Promoter analysis was performed using the available database of eudicots (A. thaliana, apple (Malus domesticus (Suckow) Borkh.), California poplar (P. trichocarpa), and soybean (G. max)). The 2-kb upstream promoter sequences from the neighboring species of Fabaceae, V. angularis and P. vulgaris were also used for consistency check, and the results are available on the Supplementary Material.

Protein 3D structure prediction

The 3D structures of the CAMTA proteins were predicted by an approach using the artificial intelligence deep learning system used by AlphaFold 2 [73] via Galaxy for Europe (https://usegalaxy.eu) [50] that employs full AlphaFold database usage. Domain highlighting was performed using Discovery Studio 2021 (BIOVIA).

RNA-seq analysis and expression heatmap of VrCAMTA on different organs

Transcriptomic reads from the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) public repository from two projects were used to build reference information about the nature of VrCAMTA expression in the different organs of mung bean. Reads for RNA-seq analysis were obtained from the Illumina HiSeq 2000 analysis of the mung bean seeds (SRR2182080), pea pods (SRR2177454), and flowers (SRR2177452) of project PRJNA276314 (Academia Sinica, Taiwan) [74]. Additionally, reads were acquired from the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 analysis of unstressed hypocotyl and roots with three replicates from cultivar H150 (landrace variety, CN900198) (SRR17976291, SRR17976306, and SRR17976305) and KPS2 (commercial variety, Kamphaeng Saen 2) (SRR17976308, SRR17976307, and SRR17976298) of project PRJNA804574 (Kasetsart University, Thailand) [75]. Finally, reads were acquired from the Illumina HiSeq 4000 analysis of leaves with three replicates from V2984 (SRR8088345, SRR8088346, and SRR8088347) and VC1973A (SRR8088339, SRR8088340, and SRR8088341) of project PRJNA497750 (Seoul National University, South Korea) [76].

The following processes for quality assay, assembly, and post-processing were performed using the Galaxy Australia online pipeline (https://usegalaxy.org.au) [50]. The reads were subjected to a quality check using FastQC to analyze potential adapter sequences, overexpressed sequences, and overall quality. Global trimming and adapter removal were performed using Trimmomatic v0.38 [77]. RNAseq alignment to the mung bean reference genome (Vradiata_ver6; GCF_000741045; submitted by Seoul National University, South Korea) was conducted using HISAT2 v2.2.1 software [78], followed by assembly and quantification using StringTie v2.2.1 software [79, 80] to obtain the transcript per million (TPM) value of the genes. The normalized log 2 of the average TPM values was used to build an expression heatmap. The heatmapper online expression heatmap generator by Genome Canada (http://www.heatmapper.ca/expression) [81] was used to draw the expression heatmaps of the VrCAMTA genes. The cropped text image of the heatmap was prepared using Microsoft Paint (11.2301.22.0).

RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis, and RT-qPCR

The leaf samples were ground at low temperature (with added liquid nitrogen) using GENEzol™ Reagent (Geneaid). The concentration and purity were determined using a spectrophotometer. The total RNA samples were then treated with DNase I (Thermo Scientific) to further purify the RNA and converted into cDNA using iScript™ Reverse Transcription Supermix (Bio-Rad). For reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR), primers were designed using Primer3Plus (https://www.primer3plus.com) [82] and the reagent of Luna® Universal qPCR Master Mix with SYBR® Green I DNA-binding dye was used. Following amplification, the relative gene expression was calculated using the Pfaffl method [83] to observe the upregulation and downregulation of genes, whereas the relative expression levels among different genes were calculated by normalizing the differences between target genes and reference genes. Two reference genes were used, namely actin (VrActin) and elongation factor-1α (VrEF-1α). The sequences of the primers are listed in Supplementary Table 1. The results were visualized in bar charts with error bars representing the standard error (SE) of the mean expression values, and the variance was checked for biological significance. The data were also statistically analyzed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan’s post hoc test for data mean comparison to confirm whether the data were significantly different (at F = 0.05) using SPSS Statistics v29.0.1 software (IBM).

Results

Identification and characterization of CAMTA

The gene and protein sequence information for nine VrCAMTAs were retrieved from NCBI GenBank. The exon-intron structures of the VrCAMTA genes were examined based on cDNA and RNA-seq data, which appeared to be consistent with the data retrieved from GenBank (Supplementary Dataset 1). Alternatively spliced transcripts have been identified for VrCAMTA1 (6), VrCAMTA3 (2), and VrCAMTA8 (4). However, after omitting the partial protein and focusing on one form of the alternatively spliced transcripts, a total of eight VrCAMTA sequences were acquired (Supplementary Table 2). The putative conserved domains and motifs of the VrCAMTA sequences were confirmed using the NCBI Conserved Domain Search and InterPro. All VrCAMTA proteins exhibited six signature domains and motifs of CAMTA proteins, comprising the CG-1 domain, bipartite NLS, TIG domain, ankyrin repeats, two IQ motifs, and CaM-binding domain or CaMBD (Fig. 1A). Eight VrCAMTA genes were distributed in five of the 11 mung bean chromosomes (Supplementary Table 2; Fig. 1B). The number of exons varied from 12 to 15, with most of them (five VrCAMTAs) having 13 exons; overall, the VrCAMTA genes had relatively longer introns than A. thaliana (Fig. 1C). Using Multiple Expectation maximizations for Motif Elicitation [45], which identifies both functional and non-functional domains of proteins, 20 putative motifs were detected in VrCAMTA proteins (Fig. 1D). Motifs 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 7 identified in both AtCAMTAs and VrCAMTAs were the six signature domains of CAMTA proteins. From left to right, motif 1 is the signature DNA-binding CG-1 motif, motifs 3 and 7 are TIG domains, motif 2 is ankyrin repeats, motif 4 is IQ motifs, and motif 5 is a CaMBD. Between AtCAMTAs and VrCAMTAs, some other detected possible motifs are found in all proteins, which included motifs 6/8/9/12. The remaining of motifs were found in specific AtCAMTA and VrCAMTA proteins and included motif 10 (AtCAMTA1/2/3/5/6 and VrCAMTA 2/4/6/7/8), motifs 11/14/15 (AtCAMTA5/6 and VrCAMTA 2/6), motifs 13/18/19 (AtCAMTA1/2/3 and VrCAMTA 4/7/8), motifs 16/17 (AtCAMTA1/2 and VrCAMTA 7/8), and motif 20 (VrCAMTA 7/8). The pHMMER analysis found no hits with any known motifs except motifs 6 and 15, which shared some similarity with the IQ motif. Both motifs were located close to the canonical IQ motifs found in CAMTA proteins.

Additional bioinformatic analysis of the protein was performed to obtain a greater overview of its physicochemical properties and subcellular localization. VrCAMTA protein lengths varied from 866 to 1105 amino acid residues, and their weights ranged from 98,626.84 to 124,631.8 Da (Table 1). Following analysis in ProtParam, the physicochemical properties of all paralogous VrCAMTA were described. The theoretical isoelectric point of all the VrCAMTA proteins ranged from 5.38 to 8.2, while the aliphatic indexes ranged from 74.66 to 83.78. The grand average values of hydropathicity (GRAVY) were entirely negative, ranging from ‒0.561 to ‒0.398, suggesting that VrCAMTA proteins have hydrophilic tendencies. Based on the protein instability index, almost all VrCAMTA proteins (except VrCAMTA2) were categorized as unstable in vitro due to their indices being higher than 40 [84]. Using DeepLoc, all VrCAMTA proteins were predicted to localize to the nucleus.

Mung bean CAMTA protein physicochemical properties

The GO terms for all VrCAMTA proteins were consistent (Supplementary Table 3). They had four prominent cellular components (CC) (cellular anatomical entity (GO:0110165), intracellular anatomical structure (GO:0005622), membrane (GO:0016020), and organelle (GO:0043226)), as well as five biological processes (BP) (cellular process (GO:0009987), metabolic process (GO:0008152), organic substance metabolic process (GO:0071704), primary metabolic process (GO:0044238), and cellular metabolic process (GO:0044237)). As the main function of CAMTA, molecular functions (MP) were consistently referred to as protein binding (GO:0005515) and DNA binding (GO:0003677).

All eight VrCAMTA structures predicted using AlphaFold 2 were visualized and annotated in accordance with the NCBI Conserved Domain and InterPro using Discovery Studio (Fig. 2). The predicted CAMTA protein structures were highly similar to the UniProt visualization of CAMTA in mung bean (protein ID: A0A1S3VJF9; VrCAMTA7, as referred to in this study), A. thaliana (CAMTA1 to CAMTA6, with respective protein ID: Q9FY74, Q6NPP4, Q8GSA7, Q9FYG2, O23463, and Q9LSP8), and rice (Oryza sativa) (protein ID: Q7XHR2). All predicted CAMTA structures had the CG-1 and TIG clearly visible with β-sheets structures. Although the three-dimensional features of the CG-1 domain in DNA binding have not yet been characterized [14], the structure visualized here possessed two α-helices with several β-strand structures. The TIG domain has been described as having several β-strands and loops with zinc knuckles, where DNA binds (the β-sheets are clearly visible in Fig. 2) [85]. Despite the structural analogy, the zinc knuckle structure with zinc ions was not available from the modeling results in this study.

Nuclear signal and calmodulin-binding domain

All VrCAMTA proteins possessed bipartite NLS located in the CG-1 domains (Fig. 3A). This bipartite NLS was marked by the (R/K)(R/K)X10 − 12(R/K)(R/K)3/5 motif [39] and was consistent with this identification (Fig. 3B). Based on an analysis using DeepLoc v2.0 (Supplementary Dataset 2), nuclear export signals (NES) were found in six of the eight VrCAMTAs (NES were not detected in VrCAMTA4 or VrCAMTA8), signified by RXXXRXXRXR (with R = hydrophobic amino acid residue, mostly leucine). Previously, NES were found in mammalian CAMTA in the CG-1 domain [7, 14].

The bipartite NLS signals are found in both groups of CAMTA in A. thaliana and mung bean. The bipartite NLS signals have their patterns consistently separated by 12 amino acid residues (A), the logo pattern map (B). The conservative motif of the CaMBD found in both groups of CAMTA in A. thaliana and mung bean (C), and the logo pattern map (D)

CaM binds to a protein independent of Ca2+ in the IQ motif and dependent on Ca2+ in the CaMBD [86]. All VrCAMTAs had WSVG[IV]LEK[VA][IV]LRWRRK[GR][SAKVM]GLRG motif for CaMBD (Fig. 3C, D), which is consistent with the results of previous studies in A. thaliana [87], cotton (Gossypium hirsutum L.) [88], strawberry (Fragaria x ananassa) [89], tomato (Solanum lycopersicum L.) [90], and durian (Durio zibethinus L.) [9].

Phylogenetic analysis of CAMTA genes in mung bean and other plant species

A phylogenetic tree was constructed to examine the evolutionary origin and relationships among the VrCAMTA genes using CAMTA gene sequences of four species from Fabaceae (one in the same genus, Vigna, as mung bean), together with A. thaliana and mosses (Physcomitrella patens and Sphagnum fallax) as outgroups (Fig. 4). These CAMTA genes formed three distinct clades, the first one containing VrCAMTA1 and VrCAMTA3, the second containing VrCAMTA2 and VrCAMTA5, and the last containing VrCAMTA4, VrCAMTA6, VrCAMTA7, and VrCAMTA8. The tree shows a close relationship between VrCAMTA and VaCAMTA, which tend to be grouped together. This can be explained simply by the fact that mung bean (V. radiata) and adzuki bean (V. angularis) belong to the same genus, Vigna. PvCAMTA from the long bean (P. vulgaris), which is also in the same subtribe as Vigna of the subfamily Faboideae, also showed close relationships with VrCAMTA genes. Next to this subtribe, GmCAMTA from soybean (G. max), which belongs to the same tribe as Vigna, and barrelclover (M. truncatula), which belongs to the same subfamily as Vigna, showed increasingly diverged relationships [91]. A. thaliana, which belongs to a different family, displayed the most distant relationships with Fabaceae species.

The Ks and Ka rates, which represent the number of synonymous and nonsynonymous substitutions per synonymous and nonsynonymous site, respectively, can be used to estimate evolutionary rates. The Ka/Ks ratio indicates a negative, neutral, or positive mutation when Ka < Ks (ratio < 1), Ka = Ks (ratio = 1), or Ka > Ks (ratio > 1), respectively [59]. Here, calculations of Ks and Ka by the KaKs_Calculator using five methods including both approximate methods (YN and MYN), and maximum likelihood methods (GY, MS, and MA) showed that the Ka/Ks ratios among VrCAMTA genes were between ~ 0.1–0.3 (Supplementary Table 4), suggesting that the evolutionary trends of VrCAMTA genes are negative mutations. The divergence time estimation of most pairs of VrCAMTA genes calculated from Ks was between ~ 200–400 MYA. Howerver, the divergence times of the three VrCAMTA pairs including VrCAMTA1-VrCAMTA3, VrCAMTA2-VrCAMTA5, and VrCAMTA6-VrCAMTA7, were estimated to be between ~ 40–80 MYA. Correspondingly, these were the closest pairs found in each of the three clades and clustered along with their orthologs from V. angularis, P. vulgaris, G. max, and M. truncatula.

Promoter cis-acting elements and transcription factor binding-sites

The 2-kb promoter sequences in all paralogous VrCAMTA genes appeared to possess a high number of abscisic acid-responsive elements (ABRE) and ethylene-responsive elements (ERE) within the list of phytohormonal response elements, none of which contained a gibberellic acid-responsive element (GARE) (Fig. 5A). The G-box was predominant in the list of elements responsible for the stress response (Fig. 5B). For growth and development (Fig. 5C), the MYB transcription factor motif was dominant, and no CAT box or circadian elements were detected. The results are also consistent with the cis-acting elements in V. angularis and P. vulgaris on phytohormones, which predominated by ABRE and ERE, and on development and growth, which are predominated by MYB (Supplementary Table 5).

Distributions of cis-acting elements found in 2000-bp of VrCAMTA promoters for phytohormone (A), abiotic and biotic stress response (B), and development and growth (C)

CAMTA has previously been identified as an ethylene-induced CaM-binding protein [92, 93]. In mung bean, all VrCAMTA gene promoters were rich in ERE (Fig. 5A), as indicated by PlantCARE. This was further confirmed by the analysis of transcription factor binding sites using PlantPAN 2.0, which showed a high number of sites for binding with the ethylene response factor (ERF) (Fig. 6). The promoter sites of VrCAMTA were also found to possess the binding sites of transcription factors with basic leucine zipper (bZIP) domains. Interestingly, all VrCAMTAs except VrCAMTA5 had promoter motifs for the CAMTA and CGCG motifs, which are binding sites for the CG-1 domain.

Discussion

Gene structure and evolutionary origin

Structurally, the number of exons in all VrCAMTA genes was consistent, and the size of the introns was hypothetically related to the adaptation mechanism in the evolutionary process. These VrCAMTA genes in mung beans were composed of 12–15 exons, with five of the VrCAMTA genes containing 13 exons. The similarity in exon size suggests that the genes share a more recent common evolutionary history. VrCAMTA3 possessed the largest intron size, which contributed to the largest gene size among the VrCAMTA genes, while VrCAMTA4 had the longest open reading frame in total, with 3317 base pairs; thus, VrCAMTA4 had the longest amino acid sequence (1105 residues). VrCAMTA2 and VrCAMTA7 also contained relatively long introns (> 5 kb). Introns play a role in transcription regulation, which may include the expression of regulating non-coding RNA [94]; however, further studies are needed to determine the full extent of the origin and regulation of the introns in these genes. In particular, long introns may lead to energy inefficiency during splicing, delay in protein production, and a higher chance of splicing errors [95].

The negative mutation evolutationary trends of VrCAMTA genes indicated that all mung bean CAMTA genes were relatively conserved throughout the evolutionary process. The oldest duplication event, when the oldest VrCAMTA diverged into all eight genes that remain today, was between 200 and 400 million years ago, which was closer to the first emergence of seed plants, approximately 385 MYA [96], and long before the rise of the first flowering plant, approximately 125 MYA [97], suggesting that CAMTA genes diverged early in plant evolution. In contrast, the newest duplication event was between ~ 40–80 million years ago for VrCAMTA genes, which were found within the same clade of the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 4). All clades that contained each VrCAMTA gene also contained orthologous CAMTA genes from nearly all the other Fabaceae species analyzed. In addition, A. thaliana CAMTA genes, which were the most distant, were found in all three major clades, suggesting that the duplication events that gave rise to CAMTA in each clade may have occurred early in flowering plant evolution. Since then, the eight VrCAMTA genes have been conserved and any mutations have been purified during evolution, as signified by negative mutations. In this study, it can be seen that mung bean CAMTA (VrCAMTA) and adzuki bean CAMTA (VaCAMTA) are closely related, as so with long bean CAMTA (PvCAMTA), compared to the rest of the non-Fabaceae plants. The genus Vigna separated from the genus Phaseolus less than 10 million years ago, and the ancestor of Vigna-Phaseolus diverged from the Glycine-Pachyrhizus-Psoralea ancestor and Psophocarpus between 10 and 20 million years ago [88]. The Milletoid clade ancestor in Fabaceae that gave birth to the present-day genera Canavalia, Apios, Cajanus, Psophocarpus, Glycine, Pachyrhizus, Psoralea, and Vigna, which split 30 million years ago, thrived 50 million years ago [98]. Of all the genes with less diverging branches (Supplementary Table 4), three occurred in the Cenozoic era (66 MYA to the present) or slightly earlier, at ~ 43–47 MYA (VrCAMTA6 and VrCAMTA7), ~ 52–55 MYA (VrCAMTA1 and VrCAMTA3), and ~ 69–75 MYA (VrCAMTA2 and VrCAMTA5). The duplication events that gave rise to these genes occurred much later in the flowering plant evolution but likely before the separation of the different genera of Fabaceae compared [99].

Promoter composition

Within the 2000-bp regions of the VrCAMTA promoter, several cis-acting regulatory elements and binding sites were found that were specifically rich. Cis-acting elements are essential for controlling gene expression, and allowing plants to respond rapidly to environmental stimuli and hormonal signals. Understanding the distribution of these elements in the VrCAMTA promoters offers insights into how these genes might be regulated in response to various signals. Regarding the phytohormonal response (Fig. 5A), ABRE and ERE were dominant, implying that VrCAMTA in mung beans is mostly responsive to abscisic acid and ethylene, two hormones that play pivotal roles in plant stress responses. This is consistent with the results of a previous study on SlCAMTA and GmCAMTA [24], and the additional comparative analysis by this study using V. angularis and P. vulgaris (Supplementary Table 5). The VrCAMTA gene promoters were highly detected in bZIP- and ERF-binding sites, which further supports the involvement of ABA- and ethylene-mediated pathways, reinforcing the role of VrCAMTA genes in stress responses, particularly drought and pathogen resistance. The results shared with the two other legume species revealed possibly conserved cis-elements, suggesting that CAMTA genes may share similar regulatory pathways across leguminous species. This highlights the potential for conserved hormones and stress regulation mechanisms that could be targeted in future breeding programs to improve stress tolerance.

The discovery and early identification of the CAMTA protein as an “ethylene-responsive gene” [8] explains its strong connection to ethylene-related processes, thus correlating with the high quantity of ERE and ERF binding sites in the promoter regions. For other phytohormone responses, such as the TGA elements associated with the auxin response [100], the elements are found in VrCAMTA1, VrCAMTA3, VrCAMTA6, and VrCAMTA7 promoters. For gibberellic acid responses, the responsible elements TATC-box [92] in VrCAMTA1 and P-box [101] in VrCAMTA2, VrCAMTA4, VrCAMTA5, and VrCAMTA7 were found, although no GARE motif [102] was detected. Nearly all VrCAMTA promoters possess the response elements of methyl jasmonate (except VrCAMTA1 and VrCAMTA5), CGTCA and TGACG motifs [103], and salicylic acid TCA elements [104], except VrCAMTA5 and VrCAMTA8. The discovery of methyl jasmonate and salicylic acid response elements indicated that VrCAMTA genes may also function in biotic stress responses, particularly during pathogen attacks. The presence of elements responsive to light, cold, and drought further suggests that these genes are involved in diverse environmental stress responses, potentially contributing to the resilience of mung beans to various growing conditions. Most promoters (except VrCAMTA7) also have an anaerobic response element (ARE) [105], and all have box-4 elements that are responsive to light [106]. Specific abiotic responses, such as low-temperature responsiveness (LTR) elements that respond to cold [107] were possessed by VrCAMTA3 and VrCAMTA8, whereas the MYB-binding site (MBS) element that responds to drought [108] was observed in VrCAMTA6 and VrCAMTA7. Similar to, but differently identified by PlantCARE, all VrCAMTA promoters contain MYB cis elements that bind to the MYB transcription factor [109].

The fact that the VrCAMTA genes had CAMTA transcription factor-binding sites recognized by CG-1 suggests that these genes were transcriptionally regulated by one another (Fig. 6), except for VrCAMTA5, which has no CAMTA binding-site. Even in the absence of CAMTA-binding sites in the promoter, the induction of transcription by CAMTA of other transcription factors (e.g., MYB, bZIP, and bHLH) may subsequently affect CAMTA gene expression. In summary, the cis-element analysis revealed that VrCAMTA genes are regulated in a complex manner by multiple hormonal and stress-related signals, emphasizing their potential role in enhancing mung bean stress tolerance. These findings provide a foundation for further functional studies of VrCAMTA genes and their applications in improving crop resilience.

Composition and prediction of protein structures

All eight paralogous VrCAMTA proteins exhibited CAMTA signature structures, comprised of a CG-1 domain with a bipartite NLS signal, a TIG domain, ankyrin repeats, IQ motifs, and CaMBD [14]. The DNA-binding CG-1 and TIG domains in the AlphaFold-predicted three-dimensional structures were prominent with β-sheet structures. The CG-1 domain binds to the target DNA cis element in the promoter [14], which consists of the CGCG motif [110], whereas the TIG domain binds to nonspecific DNA targets [111] and is involved in dimerization/protein-protein interactions [14, 84, 112, 113]. Ankyrin repeats form helix-turn-helix repeats, which play a role in protein folding and stability, as well as in protein-protein interactions [114, 115]. The calmodulin-binding region marked with IQ-motifs and CaMBD are two connected α-helix structures. The IQ motif has an α-helix conformation that binds to calmodulin EF-hand in Ca2+-independent manner [116]. In contrast, the interaction between CaMBD and calmodulin is Ca2+-dependent [117, 118]. In addition to the six signature domains of CAMTA proteins, the MEME analysis identified several putative motifs conserved among VrCAMTA proteins, while others were found only in a subset. The motifs present in all VrCAMTA proteins may represent essential structural features, such as motif 6 located between the alkyrin repeats and IQ motifs, and motif 9 positioned N-terminally to the ankyrin repeats. The presence of gaps between the functional motifs in the predicted three-dimensional structures (Fig. 2) suggests that other motifs may contribute to structural flexibility.

Hypothetical role of CAMTA in mung bean growth in the presence of light

Morphogenesis in the apical region and leaves is affected by the presence of light owing to photoreceptors and phytohormonal regulation. To our knowledge, no previous studies have been conducted to observe the effects of light on the expression of CAMTA in plants. A study on the fruit fly DmCAMTA showed its association with the G-coupled light receptor rhodopsin [6]. There are groups of photoreceptors in plants, including light-oxygen-voltage (LOV) sensors, xanthopsins, phytochromes, blue-light sensors using flavin adenine dinucleotide (BLUF), cryptochromes, and algal rhodopsins [119], though the presence of rhodopsin analogs and their functions in plants remain unknown [120]. Light is an integral part of seedling establishment. Light affects phototropism via blue light receptors belonging to the LOV sensor group of phototropins [121]. Additionally, the balance of phytohormones, for example, auxin, which determines organ initiation; cytokinin and gibberellic acid levels, which regulate cellular division; and brassinosteroid, which promotes cell proliferation and organ growth (in this case, leaf), play important roles in morphogenesis [122, 123]. Hence, some CAMTA genes that are known to respond to environmental signals and phytohormones may be differentially expressed and regulated during seedling establishment.

In the light, the RNA-seq analysis of leaves from the previous study (Fig. 7A and B) showed that VrCAMTA1 was expressed at a high level and VrCAMTA8 was expressed at low levels, not only in leaves but in almost all organs. The RNA-seq results matched the RT-qPCR results (Fig. 7C) for VrCAMTA1 as the highest and VrCAMTA8 as the lowest, which suggests that VrCAMTA1 may play a role in organ maturation, especially since it is highly expressed and reached its peak expression during the last period of observation, when the leaf became more mature. Other VrCAMTAs exhibited the same pattern of regulation as VrCAMTA1 and were significantly upregulated on the last day of observation. In addition, the upregulation of all VrCAMTA genes, except VrCAMTA8 on day 4 under light, was mostly inhibited under darkness. These results agree with the high number of box-4 and G-box elements that respond to light observed in all VrCAMTA, with the highest numbers possessed by VrCAMTA7 (six elements each). Interestingly, despite being expressed at low levels, the expression of VrCAMTA7 was highly regulated under light, second only to VrCAMTA1, suggesting that VrCAMTA7 has a role in the light response during leaf growth along with VrCAMTA1. Of all VrCAMTAs, VrCAMTA4 and VrCAMTA5 had the lowest numbers of box-4 and G-box light-responding elements (Fig. 5), but they appeared to be regulated by light, as they were also upregulated on day 4.

A brief comparison by using the eFP Browser of Arabidopsis thaliana (http://bar.utoronto.ca/efp//cgi-bin/efpWeb.cgi) [124] on the “Light series” showed some gene expression increases of AtCAMTA under different light conditions, especially with 3% sucrose, 22 °C, and constant dark (of all six AtCAMTA, and changes were detected for AtCAMTA1 [At5g09410], AtCAMTA2 [At5g64220], and AtCAMTA4 [At1g67310]). Based on the NCBI search results, the homologous CAMTA proteins of A. thaliana found in mung bean (Table 1) were AtCAMTA2 to VrCAMTA6 and VrCAMTA7, AtCAMTA3 to VrCAMTA4 and VrCAMTA8, AtCAMTA4 to VrCAMTA1 and VrCAMTA3, and AtCAMTA5 to VrCAMTA2 and VrCAMTA5.

In the absence of light, the plants were etiolated, causing stem elongation and paler leaf coloration. During etiolation, the chlorophyll-lacking etioplast is present instead of the regular chloroplast [125], a morphology with elongated hypocotyl, shortened root, and closed cotyledons arises through a process called skotomorphogenesis [126, 127], indoleacetic acid remains due to a lack of photooxidation, and in prolonged conditions, stress can occur due to the absence of photosynthesis [128].

VrCAMTAs that respond to auxin are upregulated during leaf photomorphogenesis. Two VrCAMTAs (VrCAMTA1 and VrCAMTA7) have the highest number of TGA elements (two elements in the promoter) that respond to auxin. Interestingly, based on the RT-qPCR results, both VrCAMTA1 and VrCAMTA7 were highly upregulated on day 4 (Fig. 8), suggesting crosstalk between auxins and light signals. The lower upregulation compared with its light counterpart was probably affected by auxin levels in the dark. The function of VrCAMTA genes and proteins in light requires further validation to obtain a better picture of how they play roles in the photomorphogenesis of leaves, especially during the production of plant sprout crops (e.g., mung bean, mustard seed, and alfalfa). A previous study showed rapid induction of A. thaliana CAMTA expression (withing 15 min) to phytohormone signaling, including ethylene, jasmonic acid, ABA, and salicylic acid, and various stress triggers, including heat shock, cold, UVB, and NaCl [129]. However this was observed in the 3-week-old seedlings, in which the quick responsiveness may be different from the period of seedling establishment during the first week of germination, when the first true leaf starts to emerge. Nonetheless, sampling at narrow time points from the young seedlings is necessary to observe the initial VrCAMTA responses to light and auxins and elucidate it crosstalks during mung bean seedling establishment.

Calcium signaling and alteration of CAMTA expression

Normally, the cytosolic Ca2+ concentration in a cell is four to five orders of magnitude lower than the concentration in the extracellular matrix and is controlled by limited cellular permeability, calcium pumps, and the cell’s own intracellular storage [130, 131]. Homeostasis of the cytosolic Ca2+ concentration within the cell is required to control general metabolism, regulation of cell death, secretion, cell contraction, and gene expression [132–135]. Calcium ions are a crucial part of calmodulin protein activation, as the calmodulin EF-hand binds to calcium ions, activating the protein by allowing it to bind to other proteins involved in the calcium signaling process [136]. Calmodulin can bind to the CAMTA protein to initiate the gene expression required by the cell to respond to environmental changes [14].

Calcium ionophores allow divalent ions, including calcium, to pass through the cell membrane [137]. Excess calcium can cause calcium toxicity, which reduces germination and growth rates [138]. As calcium serves as a signaling molecule within the cell, it is likely that increases in calcium levels can disrupt the signaling process, especially calmodulin signaling, and thus CAMTA functions as a transcription factor. In this study, the effect of calcium ionophore supplementation on the first two days of leaf growth (Fig. 9) indicated that calcium signal changes could alter VrCAMTA expression levels and regulation, although it is unclear on how much intracellular calcium concentration was affected during the initial periods of calcium ionophore treatments to the end of this experiment. The upregulation of most VrCAMTA genes (all except VrCAMTA3) in the presence of calcium ionophore on either Day 0 (under light) or Day 2 (under dark conditions) suggests that the upregulation of gene expression levels observed under light and/or dark conditions was probably mediated by an increase in the intracellular Ca2+ concentration. The upregulation appeared to be generally delayed (i.e. found on Day 2) under dark conditions, suggesting that under light, other associated factors may play important roles in regulating the expression of these genes (VrCAMTA2 and VrCAMTA5). In addition, some genes under dark condition i.e. VrCAMTA2 (Day 0), VrCAMTA4 (Day 0), and VrCAMTA7 (Day 2), were downregulated in the presence of calcium ionophore, indicating that calcium signaling may have an opposite effect on the expression of these genes under dark conditions. Further molecular investigations of genes associated with CAMTA expression and other genes involved in calcium signaling are needed; however, calcium signaling clearly affects the expression of these CAMTA genes in mung bean leaves during seedling establishment. This study provides a complete annotaton of the CAMTA gene family in mung bean, another popular food source in Asia, as well as knowledge on their potential involvement as effectors in the response to light in the process of photomorphogenesis during seedling development, which warrants further investigation.

Conclusions

In this study, eight VrCAMTA paralogs were identified in mung beans with structural signatures of CAMTA proteins. Although all of the VrCAMTA protein structures were predicted herein, their precise structures remain to be elucidated. The gene promoters of VrCAMTA were found to be laden with ABRE and ERE, which respond to abscisic acid and ethylene, respectively. The promoter regions were also abundant in bZIP and ERF transcription factor-binding sites. Based on the available RNA-seq data confirmed by RT-qPCR, VrCAMTA1 and VrCAMTA6 were found to be highly expressed in the first true leaves, in contrast to VrCAMTA8, which exhibited the lowest expression. VrCAMTA1 expression also exhibited the highest degree of upregulation during leaf growth among all VrCAMTA genes, indicating its potential role in leaf development and maturation. The expression dependence of these VrCAMTA genes on light and calcium signaling suggests that these signals regulate the expression of these genes, eventually modulating the activity of these Ca2+/CAM-regulated CAMTA proteins during the seedling establishment of mung bean.
