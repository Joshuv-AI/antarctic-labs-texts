Implementing brain health strategies in primary care: methods and baseline findings from the BHAM registry
Authors: Pamela G Bowen, Catherine Danielle Jones, Terina Myers, Roy Martin, Amy Knight, Andres Azuero, Tasha Smith, Connor Lancaster, Michael Brewer, Sameera Davuluri, Erin Delaney, Irfan Asif, Ronald M Lazar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundManaging modifiable risk factors in early life is associated with reduced risk of cognitive decline, stroke, and dementia later in life. This is especially important in the US Deep South, which has the highest rates of stroke and dementia nationally. While prevention efforts for brain health are gaining attention, they remain limited within primary care settings.ObjectiveTo describe the methods and baseline characteristics of the Brain Health Advocacy Mission (BHAM) Registry, an initiative aimed at implementing brain health education and intervention in central Alabama primary care clinics and building a participant registry for longitudinal brain health assessment.DesignProspective, longitudinal, single-group study evaluating changes in the McCance Brain Care Score (BCS) and adherence to brain health strategies over time.ParticipantsAdults recruited from four primary care clinics across two locations.InterventionsParticipants review individual BCS results and select personalized brain health targets to guide lifestyle change.Main MeasuresBCS (range 0–21; higher scores indicate better brain health) summarizes biologic, lifestyle, and emotional factors associated with long-term brain health.Key Results Among the initial 150 participants (72% female; 67% Black; median age 48 [IQR 39-56]; mean BCS 14.3), 61% had treated hypertension and 67% had a BMI >30 kg/m². The most frequent suboptimal BCS domains were elevated blood pressure, obesity, and physical inactivity. Top participant-selected brain health targets were body weight (20.7%), exercise (20.0%), and diet (18.7%).ConclusionsThe BHAM Registry highlights the urgent need for early brain health intervention in the Deep South, where disparities in modifiable stroke and dementia risk factors are extreme. Its integration into primary care demonstrates patient willingness to engage in prevention and offers a scalable model for improving brain health through lifestyle modification across the life span.Keywords: Brain health, Dementia prevention, Primary care, Vascular risk, Lifestyle intervention, Health disparities

Introduction

The global population of people aged ≥ 60 years old is projected to double from 1 billion in 2020 to 2.1 billion by 2050, with those aged ≥ 80 years old expected to triple to 426 million [1]. This demographic shift is accompanied by a rising prevalence of conditions that increase vulnerability to cognitive decline and stroke [2]. Nearly two-thirds of Americans experience cognitive decline by age 70 [3], and about three-quarters of strokes occur in adults ≥ 65 years old, emphasizing the need for primary prevention [4–6]. Cognitive decline and stroke share modifiable risk factors such as obesity, diabetes, and hypertension [7, 8]. According to the CDC, nearly half of US adults have hypertension, 38 million have diabetes, and 140 million are classified as obese [9–11]. Additionally, approximately 25% of US adults are physically inactive [12, 13]. These lifestyle-related factors strongly influence cardiovascular and brain health, and are key targets for dementia risk reduction [6, 7, 14].

Significant disparities in cognitive impairment and stroke burden exist across racial/ethnic groups and education levels [15–17]. People in the Deep South, a geographic region of the southeastern US that includes Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana, Mississippi, and South Carolina, have a 20–30% higher risk for developing dementia compared to other US regions [18]. Black adults in the Deep South face a higher risk of stroke and dementia compared to Black and White adults in other regions [15, 16, 19]. The Reasons for Geographic and Racial Differences in Stroke (REGARDS) study highlights the Deep South “Stroke Belt” as having among the nation’s highest levels of cardiovascular risk [20], and Alabama reports some of the country’s highest dementia and cognitive decline prevalence [21, 22]. Many of these disparities in dementia and cognitive decline are linked to preventable or treatable conditions that can be managed in primary care settings [7].

Multidomain lifestyle trials such as the Finnish Geriatric Intervention Study (FINGER) [23], the U.S. POINTER Trial [24], and the SMARRT Trial [25] have shown that structured, personalized vascular risk-management strategies can preserve cognitive performance in older at-risk adults. However, these randomized controlled trials were limited by short follow-up, high resource requirements, and recruitment of highly selected participants. Such constraints underscore the need for pragmatic, scalable primary care-based approaches that reach people earlier in the life course.

Against this backdrop, prevention efforts within primary care are especially important [26]. The American Heart Association (AHA) reports that healthy lifestyles can prevent cardiovascular disease and dementia, and identifies primary care as an ideal setting to promote brain health [2]. Research supports the integration of Life’s Essential 8 (LE8) lifestyle strategies within brain health initiatives [27]. Primary care clinics are uniquely positioned to address the public health burden of age-related brain decline because prevention is a core part of their mission and they engage patients across the lifespan [26, 28]. Yet many clinicians face time constraints and find it challenging to motivate patients to change health behaviors in busy practices [29].

The Brain Health Advocacy Mission (BHAM) Registry was created to meet this public health challenge [30]. The BHAM Registry integrates brain health promotion into family and community primary care clinics within central Alabama and adapts evidence based-prevention strategies outlined by the American Heart Association [2, 27, 31] and the Lancet Commission on Dementia Prevention, Intervention and Care [32]. BHAM’s goal is to reduce vascular risk and support sustainable lifestyle modification in a region disproportionately affected by cardiovascular disease and cognitive decline. While studies such as FINGER and SMARRT have shown that multidomain risk reduction is possible, questions remain about how to extend these strategies beyond highly selected older research cohorts to the more diverse patients seen in primary care. BHAM addresses these gaps by targeting risk earlier in adulthood and following participants longitudinally [33–35]. The purpose of this paper is to describe the BHAM Registry study design and report baseline brain health characteristics of our initial 150 study participants.

Methods

Participants

This study protocol was approved by the University of Alabama at Birmingham (UAB) Institutional Review Board (IRB-300011096). All participants provided written informed consent. Recruitment for the BHAM Registry began August 10, 2023, with a target enrollment of 1,000 adults. As of February 2025, 150 participants have been enrolled, with recruitment ongoing at two UAB Family and Community Medicine Clinics, targeting adult primary care patients. Eligible participants were ≥ 18 years old, were medically stable (i.e., no acute condition requiring urgent evaluation and able to complete consent, questionnaires, and vitals), receive primary care through UAB Family and Community Medicine and have English proficiency for study materials. Proximity to clinic sites may influence likelihood of enrollment but is not an eligibility criterion. Exclusion criteria were prior stroke; diagnosed mild cognitive impairment or dementia; neurologic disease or injury with cognitive sequelae; severe systemic disease; or severe psychiatric disorder. Recruitment currently occurs exclusively during Tuesday and Wednesday primary care sessions, with potential participants identified by electronic medical record review and clinician referral. There are approximately 8–10 patients scheduled on Tuesdays and 10–15 scheduled on Wednesdays, yielding approximately 2–3 new enrollments per week. Trained study staff contact eligible participants in advance; interested patients are scheduled to meet study personnel after their upcoming clinic visit to complete consent and baseline assessments.

Measures

At the baseline visit, participants provide sociodemographic and clinical information, including age, race, ethnicity, sex, highest educational level attained, self-reported medical history, and medication adherence. Brain care status is assessed at baseline using the McCance Brain Care Score (BCS) [36, 37], a validated tool that serves as the primary outcome measure. Additionally, participants complete the Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale [38] (GAD-7) and Patient Health Questionnaire Depression Scale [39] (PHQ-8).

McCance Brain Care Score

We distinguish brain health, defined as functional status, from brain care, which refers to modifiable behaviors and interventions aimed at maintaining or improving brain health [2, 40].The main metric for assessing brain care in our study is the BCS, which was developed and validated as an evidence-based instrument to capture physical, lifestyle, and social factors, and to engage patients in modifying risk factors through behavior change. Each BCS element has been independently associated with adverse effects on brain health [32, 41, 42], with increasing evidence that implementing a multi-domain approach is more likely to improve outcomes [43, 44]. The BCS ranges from 0 to 21 and consists of four physical components (objectively measured): blood pressure, hemoglobin A1c, total cholesterol, and body mass index (BMI). It also has five lifestyle components (nutrition, alcohol intake, smoking, aerobic activities, and sleep) and three social components (stress, relationships, and purpose in life), which are all subjectively measured. Each item on the BCS is scored based on recommendations from the AHA [45–48]. Higher BCS scores indicate better brain care, which is linked to reduced risk of dementia and stroke incidence [37, 48]. Changes in participants’ BCS will be examined longitudinally over the course of the study.

Procedure

After consent, study staff record demographics and medical history, and then administer the BCS. Study personnel obtain finger-stick blood with the ACCU-ANSWER® multi-monitoring system [49] to measure blood glucose and total cholesterol levels. After the biological measurements are collected, the remaining eight lifestyle queries are administered and the total BCS is calculated. The score is then reviewed with participants while emphasizing areas for improvement for better health outcomes. Participants select 1–3 achievable brain health targets, and the BHAM team provides individualized, healthy lifestyle strategies using publicly available, evidence-based resources from the CDC, AHA, US Department of Agriculture, the National Institutes of Health, and other related organizations[27, 50–53]. The 10-target domains based on the BCS are blood pressure management, diabetes risk reduction, cholesterol management, physical activity engagement, nutritional habits, weight control, smoking cessation, good sleep hygiene, anxiety recognition, and stress management.BHAM staff use a brief, standardized goal-setting coaching protocol applied across all 10 domains (e.g., activity ≥ 150 min/week or + 2,000 steps/day; blood pressure: sodium reduction and medication adherence; sleep: fixed sleep/wake window; diet: increasing fiber and water intake; stress: daily brief breathing practice). The first author (PGB), a certified nurse practitioner and the BHAM Clinical Director, trained all team members, each with a background in public health, in behavior coaching protocols prior to their independent engagement with participants. Study personnel assist participants in establishing realistic, attainable health goals, ensuring that each goal aligns with the individual’s priorities and capacity for change.

This endeavor is a longitudinal project that requires participants to complete follow-ups every six months, either in person or by telephone, until one of the following occurs: a diagnosis of mild cognitive impairment or dementia; the development of an exclusion condition; voluntary withdrawal from the study; or completion of the 20-year study period. Participant assessments include an initial in-person visit, a 6-month follow-up via telephone, and a 12-month in-person visit, with this cycle repeated annually. In-person visits, lasting 30–45 min, involve administration of the BCS lifestyle assessment, collection of biometric data (blood pressure, cholesterol, glucose, and BMI), updates to medical history, and completion of mental health questionnaires. For follow-up phone calls, lasting 10–15 min, participants answer BCS lifestyle questions, update current medical history, and PHQ-8 and GAD-7 questionnaires. At each visit, participants may choose to continue working on their initially selected lifestyle target or select new ones, based on their evolving goals and preferences.

Data analysis

Tabulations that characterize the BHAM Registry sample at baseline were performed using SPSS software version 30. Descriptive statistics for continuous variables were reported as means and standard deviations (SD) or medians (IQRs), and nominal variables were reported as number (n) and percentages (%). All data were entered into a secure REDCap database.

Table 1 provides demographic and clinical characteristics for the sample, which is predominantly female (72%), Black (66.7%), and most participants have some college education or more (80.6%). Median age was 48 (IQR 39–56). We defined young-to-middle-aged as 18–54 years, with 71% of the cohort falling in this range. Of the preexisting medical histories of this cohort (See Table 2), hypertension (60.7%), hyperlipidemia (32%), and diabetes (24.7%) were the three most prevalent conditions. Discussion

Baseline results from the first 150 BHAM participants demonstrate a high burden of modifiable vascular risk factors and adverse lifestyle behaviors among a primary care patient cohort in central Alabama. Most participants (60.7%) reported diagnosed hypertension, and 72.6% had “at-risk” blood pressures according to AHA guidelines. By comparison, the national average hypertension rate among US adults is around 47.6%9. Hyperlipidemia and diabetes were common, and the most frequent suboptimal BCS domains were blood pressure, BMI, and not meeting physical activity recommendations. The cohort’s median mid-to-late 40's age profile represents a key window for prevention, as earlier life behaviors strongly influence late-life cognitive and vascular outcomes [5, 54, 55].

These profiles mirror the excess stroke and dementia burden documented in Deep South populations, particularly among Black adults, and align with modifiable targets emphasized in BCS-guided primary care counseling [15, 16, 18, 19]. BHAM Registry participants reflect local demographics (over half of participants were Black) and show high prevalence of treated hypertension, diabetes, and obesity despite relatively high educational attainment [56]. Nearly a quarter reported a history of obstructive sleep apnea, which is more prevalent among Black adults and contributes to vascular and cognitive morbidity [57]. Despite many reporting optimal dietary patterns, approximately two-thirds of participants had BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2. This prevalence is markedly higher than the 42% observed among U.S. adults [10] and underscores the need to prioritize weight, physical activity, and blood pressure control.

Most participants in the cohort are women. Several factors may contribute to the lower representation of men, including social stigma, discomfort discussing health concerns, and underestimating the seriousness of their symptoms until they require emergency intervention [58–61]. Men are more likely than women to visit the emergency room for non-emergency issues instead of seeking routine primary care [58]. Encouraging men to access primary care will involve educating healthcare providers on men’s health needs, engaging with men in familiar settings, and raising awareness about the importance of preventive care and early detection of health issues through targeted messaging [62, 63]. Adapting programs like BHAM to the unique needs and preferences of men in the Deep South can make healthcare more accessible, relevant, and less intimidating for those at the highest risk for stroke and dementia [61, 62].

Public health strategies that focus on the prevention of cognitive decline should also shift toward individual risk profiling and tailoring programs toward an individual’s needs [6, 64]. The BCS tool was selected to integrate a practical, patient-friendly measure into primary care clinics that could easily capture biological, behavioral lifestyle, and emotional factors based on evidence [37]. Lifestyle behavior modification has been recommended to promote brain care, and the BCS offers an easy, viable tool to assess current practices. Research has shown that a five-point increase in the BCS was associated with a significant reduction in the risk of dementia and stroke across different age groups but particularly among adults younger than 50 years old [37, 65]. This finding is important because 71% of BHAM participants are young-to-middle-aged adults (18–54 years) receiving care for common medical conditions linked to long-term brain health [66]. Embedding BCS-guided health behavior counseling within primary care offers a real-world opportunity to track risk change over time and explore its impact on future brain disease [65].

There are limitations to the study. First, the design restricts enrollment to participants with health insurance who attend UAB clinics, limiting generalizability to non-insured individuals or rural populations. While population-level strategies to reduce dementia and stroke risk, and address health inequalities are well-documented, they are beyond the scope of this project. Still, it is notable that approximately 20% of our cohort have a high school education or less, indicating the BHAM Registry does reach some individuals with lower educational attainment despite being limited to insured primary care patients. Our approach focuses on individualized engagement by discussing personal facilitators and barriers, and offering tailored solutions to their unique circumstances and health literacy levels. There is no specific BCS threshold for optimal brain health; the objective is to encourage the highest possible score. Participants self-report their lifestyle behaviors, which lack independent verification, potentially leading to social desirability bias and overestimation of health practices. Selection bias is also possible because enrollees may be more health-conscious, medically stable, able to return for follow-up, and engaged in prevention than those who decline or cannot be reached. These factors may limit generalizability and could influence longitudinal brain-health outcomes. Future research should investigate methods to engage individuals who are less likely to participate in research, particularly those with lower health literacy or limited involvement in healthcare services. We did not collect data on those who declined to participate. However, the demographics of those who enrolled in the Registry are comparable to the known characteristics of our community. Despite these limitations, the BHAM Registry is expected to inform how sustainable, evidence-based primary prevention strategies can be implemented and adapted within primary care to improve brain and vascular health.

Conclusions

The BHAM Registry seeks to implement a practical, measurable, and personalized primary prevention strategy within primary care to encourage changes in modifiable lifestyle behaviors that support brain health and may reduce the risk of stroke and cognitive decline. Baseline results from the BHAM Registry underscore the high prevalence of vascular risk factors in a representative, Deep South primary care population and suggest that integrating brain health-focused prevention into routine clinical practice is feasible. Ongoing follow-up will be necessary to evaluate how this approach is adopted in practice and whether it is associated with changes in lifestyle behaviors and vascular risk profiles over time.
