Determinants of newborn birth size in Somaliland: an ordinal logistic regression analysis
Authors: Abdirahman Omar Ibrahim, Bashaar Ali Daud, Waleed Salahudiin Ahmed, Suhaib Muse Jama, Nagaad Abdinasir Habane, Rahma Yasin Ismail, Umulkhayr Mohamed Abadir, Amaani Abdilahi Sahal, Mohamed Said Hassan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUnderstanding the predictors of birth size is essential for designing effective interventions to reduce neonatal morbidity and mortality in Somaliland.MethodsA comprehensive cross-sectional study was conducted using the 2020 Somali Health and Demographic Survey (SHDS). An ordinal logistic regression model, a robust statistical method, was applied to identify determinants of birth size (Low, Normal, High) among 2,137 mother-infant pairs, ensuring a thorough and reliable analysis.ResultsThe prevalence of reported small, average, and large birth sizes was 9.0%, 75.0%, and 16.0%, respectively. Our study identified significant predictors of birth size, including household wealth, maternal age, and birth order. The adjusted odds of having a larger baby were significantly higher for infants from the wealthiest households compared to the poorest (AOR = 1.45; 95% CI: 1.12–1.87), for second-order births compared to first-borns (AOR = 1.38; 95% CI: 1.10–1.74), and for infants of mothers aged 30–34 compared to adolescent mothers (AOR = 2.09; 95% CI: 1.25–3.48). These findings provide valuable insights for designing targeted interventions to improve maternal and child health in Somaliland.ConclusionOur findings underscore the importance of socioeconomic status and maternal reproductive patterns in determining birth size. We recommend tailored interventions that target the identified risk factors, with a focus on nutritional support for poor households and reproductive health education for young women. Implementing these recommendations has the potential to significantly improve maternal and child health outcomes in Somaliland, offering hope for a healthier future and a brighter outlook for the region’s health. However, our findings also suggest that while factors such as antenatal care attendance are important, their impact may be limited without concurrent improvements in service quality and the empowerment of women, underscoring the need for a multifaceted public health strategy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-025-01765-3.

Introduction

Newborn birth weight is a critical indicator of maternal health, nutrition, and the quality of the intrauterine environment. The World Health Organization (WHO) categorizes birth weight as Low Birth Weight (LBW) (< 2.5 kg), Normal Birth Weight (NBW) (2.5–4 kg), and High Birth Weight (HBW) (>4 kg) [1]. LBW is a significant public health problem globally, contributing significantly to neonatal morbidity and mortality, as well as long-term health consequences such as stunting, developmental delays, and increased risk of chronic diseases in adulthood [2, 3]. In sub-Saharan Africa, the prevalence of LBW is estimated to be between 13% and 15% and is a primary driver of the region’s high neonatal mortality rates [1]. In Somalia and Somaliland, where conflict and poverty have weakened health systems, understanding the local determinants of birth size is not only crucial but also urgent for developing effective maternal and child health policies. This study aims to identify the sociodemographic and maternal factors that influence newborn birth size in Somaliland, addressing a pressing need for actionable research.

Literature review

The determinants of birth weight are multifactorial, involving a complex interplay of maternal, socioeconomic, and environmental factors. Maternal factors are paramount. Adolescent motherhood (< 20 years) is a well-established risk factor for LBW, often linked to biological immaturity, poor nutrition, and lower utilization of health services [4, 5]. Similarly, short birth intervals (< 24 months) are strongly associated with adverse birth outcomes, as they do not allow for sufficient maternal nutritional recovery between pregnancies [3]. Parity also plays a role, with first-born infants often having a lower birth weight compared to subsequent births [1].

Socioeconomic status is a significant contributor to disparities in birth outcomes. Numerous studies have demonstrated a strong link between low household wealth and an increased risk of LBW [4, 5]. Poverty limits access to nutritious food, quality healthcare, and adequate housing, all of which are essential for a healthy pregnancy. Maternal education is another powerful predictor; educated women are more likely to have better health literacy, make informed decisions about their health, and utilize services like Antenatal Care (ANC) [6].

Health service utilization, particularly ANC, is a key mediating factor. Regular ANC visits provide opportunities for health professionals to monitor fetal growth, provide nutritional counseling, and manage pregnancy-related complications, thereby reducing the risk of LBW [7]. This study will use an ordinal logistic regression model to simultaneously assess these determinants and understand their relative importance in the context of Somaliland.

Data and methodology

Data source

This study used data from the 2020 Somali Health and Demographic Survey (SHDS). The survey covered all six administrative regions of Somaliland. A total of n = 2,137 mother-infant pairs were included. The analysis was restricted to the last live birth for each mother in the five years preceding the survey to ensure data quality and minimize recall bias.

Variable Description

Dependent variable: Newborn Birth Size, an ordinal variable with three categories (1 = Low, 2 = Normal, 3 = High).Independent variables: Maternal age, education level, household wealth index (a composite measure of a household’s economic status based on factors such as income, assets, and living conditions), ANC visits (recategorized as 0 visits, 1–3 visits, and 4 or more visits based on WHO recommendations), birth interval, birth order, place of residence, and sex of the child.As a sensitivity analysis to test the robustness of the proportional odds assumption, a multinomial logistic regression was also performed.

Discussion

This study identified that fundamental socioeconomic and biological factors are the paramount drivers of newborn birth size in Somaliland. Specifically, the odds of a larger birth size were significantly higher for infants from the wealthiest households (AOR = 1.45; 95% CI: 1.12–1.87), those born to mothers aged 30–34 (AOR = 2.09; 95% CI: 1.25–3.48), and second-order births (AOR = 1.38; 95% CI: 1.10–1.74). The strong, graded relationship between household wealth and birth size aligns with the theory of social conditions as “fundamental causes” of health inequalities [8]. Wealth enables better maternal nutrition, reduces exposure to physical and psychological stress, and facilitates access to a broader range of health resources, which collectively contribute to a healthier intrauterine environment and optimal fetal growth [9, 10]. The fact that this association remained strong after adjusting for other factors underscores the pervasive impact of poverty on neonatal health in Somaliland.

The significant role of maternal age and birth order is consistent with a vast body of global research. Our results, which indicate that adolescent mothers are at a higher risk of having smaller babies, align with studies from across sub-Saharan Africa [11–13]. Teenage pregnancy is often associated with biological immaturity and competition for nutrients between the growing mother and the fetus, alongside social disadvantages such as lower rates of healthcare utilization [11]. Conversely, the finding that second and higher-order births tend to be larger than first births is a well-documented physiological phenomenon, potentially related to improved uterine blood flow and maternal adaptation after the first pregnancy [1, 14]. These reproductive patterns are crucial targets for public health intervention.

A particularly noteworthy finding from our study is the lack of a statistically significant association between ANC attendance and birth size in the final adjusted model, while maternal education showed a mixed effect. While initially appearing counterintuitive, this result offers essential insights. The non-significance of ANC attendance does not necessarily mean it is unimportant; instead, it may suggest that the quality and content of the care received, rather than mere attendance, are what truly impact birth weight [7, 15]. In resource-limited settings, ANC visits may be brief and often lack essential components, such as nutritional counseling or adequate screening for complications. Similarly, the benefits of maternal education may be mitigated by overriding contextual factors, such as extreme poverty, food insecurity, or cultural norms that limit a woman’s autonomy —a complexity noted in other fragile settings [6, 16, 17]. The implications are that policy must move beyond simply promoting access to services and focus on improving service quality and addressing the deep-seated structural barriers that prevent women from translating knowledge into better health outcomes.

To further assess the robustness of our findings, we conducted a multinomial logistic regression as a sensitivity analysis, with ‘Average’ birth size as the reference category (see Supplementary Table S1). The results corroborated our primary findings from the ordinal model. For example, infants from the wealthiest households had significantly lower odds of being born small (AOR = 0.68; 95% CI: 0.49–0.94) and higher odds of being born large (AOR = 1.35; 95% CI: 1.03–1.77) compared to those born into the poorest households. This consistency across different analytical approaches reinforces the validity of our conclusions regarding the key determinants of birth size in Somaliland.

Conclusion

This study found that household wealth, maternal age, and birth order are significant predictors of newborn birth size in Somaliland. The findings underscore the critical importance of addressing socioeconomic inequities and strengthening maternal health programs, particularly for young and first-time mothers. Policymakers must design targeted interventions that provide economic support and enhance the quality of care to improve birth outcomes and ensure every child has a healthy start to life. Importantly, this study also highlights that simply promoting service utilization may be insufficient; efforts must also focus on improving the quality of maternal health services and addressing the underlying structural barriers that limit women’s autonomy and ability to act on health knowledge. Implementing these recommendations could contribute to improved maternal and child health outcomes in Somaliland.
