A narrative review of segmentectomy versus lobectomy in elderly patients with early-stage NSCLC: challenging the longstanding standard
Authors: Chanwoo Kim, Jae Yeon Kim, Se Eun Park, Sanghyun Han, Sujeong Kim, Dohun Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectiveLobectomy has historically been regarded as the gold standard for early-stage non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). However, concerns about postoperative pulmonary function in elderly patients have fueled growing interest in segmentectomy. This review aimed to synthesize current evidence regarding the comparative outcomes of lobectomy and segmentectomy in patients aged ≥70 years, with attention to demographic trends and clinical practice patterns.MethodsWe conducted a narrative review of the literature published from 1913 through 2025, primarily using PubMed (MEDLINE) supplemented by Google Scholar and journal archives. Keywords included “lobectomy”, “segmentectomy”, “sublobar resection”, “elderly”, and “survival”. Studies were screened by six authors, and data were summarized with a focus on surgical trends, survival outcomes, and factors influencing patient selection in elderly cohorts.Key Content and FindingsHistorical trials established lobectomy as the standard due to lower recurrence risk compared with limited resection. Yet more recent evidence has shifted this paradigm. The Cancer and Leukemia Group B (CALGB) 140503 trial demonstrated the non-inferiority of sublobar resection (segmentectomy or wedge resection) compared with lobectomy for small peripheral tumors, whereas the Japan Clinical Oncology Group (JCOG) 0802/West Japan Oncology Group (WJOG) 4607L trial specifically showed that anatomical segmentectomy not only achieved oncologic non-inferiority but also improved overall survival in older patients. Population-based analyses from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER), the National Cancer Database (NCDB), and nationwide Korean datasets further show that while lobectomy remains most common, the use of segmentectomy has steadily increased, particularly among patients aged ≥70 years. These data suggest that lobectomy confers survival advantages in younger patients, whereas segmentectomy provides comparable or superior outcomes in elderly populations while preserving lung function. South Korea, one of the world’s most rapidly aging societies, illustrates how demographic shifts may influence surgical practice.ConclusionsSegmentectomy represents a clinically meaningful alternative to lobectomy in elderly patients with early-stage NSCLC. Age, along with physiologic status and tumor characteristics, should guide surgical decision-making. Further randomized trials are warranted to establish evidence-based, age-stratified guidelines.

Introduction

Lung cancer mortality and treatment approaches

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality worldwide (1). Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for approximately 83% of all lung cancer cases (2). The treatment landscape for NSCLC is highly heterogeneous and stage-dependent. For patients with locally advanced disease (Stage III), combined-modality therapy consisting of chemotherapy and/or radiation therapy remains the cornerstone of management. In recent years, the advent of targeted therapies directed against oncogenic driver mutations—including epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS), and anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK)—and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) and programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) has significantly transformed the therapeutic paradigm for advanced NSCLC. In contrast, for patients with early-stage disease (stage I and II), surgical resection continues to represent the most effective and potentially curative treatment modality, with lobectomy historically established as the standard of care following the pivotal trial by Ginsberg and Rubinstein (3). However, there is increasing interest in parenchymal-sparing procedures such as segmentectomy, which offer the potential to preserve greater pulmonary function while maintaining oncologic efficacy (4,5).

Aging trends in lung cancer

The increasing focus on segmentectomy in early-stage NSCLC is closely linked to its potential advantages in preserving pulmonary reserve, which is particularly relevant for elderly patients with diminished baseline lung function. Currently, approximately four-fifths (82%) of lung cancer survivors are aged 65 years or older (2). Moreover, this proportion is anticipated to rise further due to global demographic shifts toward an aging population (2,6,7). Similar demographic trends have been reported in South Korea, where the proportion of lung cancer patients aged 70 years or older has steadily increased over the past decade, according to multiple population-based studies using national datasets (8,9). As the prevalence of lung cancer among elderly individuals continues to increase, optimizing surgical strategies to balance oncologic outcomes with functional preservation has become an essential consideration in thoracic surgical practice. In this context, there is a growing need to establish evidence-based guidelines tailored to the unique physiologic characteristics and comorbidity profiles of elderly patients.

Objective

The objective of this review is to provide a comprehensive synthesis of current trends in surgical management—specifically the selection between lobectomy and segmentectomy—among elderly patients with lung cancer aged 70 years and older. We further aim to examine survival outcomes associated with these surgical approaches and to discuss key factors influencing patient selection in this population. For clarity, throughout this review, the term “segmentectomy” refers exclusively to anatomical segmentectomy. In contrast, procedures that include wedge resection are referred to as “sublobar resection”, “limited resection”, or more broadly, “parenchymal-sparing procedures”. We present this article in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2025-1537/rc).

Methods

A literature search was conducted in PubMed and, as supplementary sources, Google Scholar, using the following keywords: (“Carcinoma, Non-Small-Cell Lung” OR “Lung Neoplasm”) AND (“lobectomy” OR “segmentectomy” OR “sublobar resection” OR “limited resection”) AND ((“Aged” OR “70 years” OR “elderly”) OR (“survival” OR “overall survival” OR “cohort” OR “population-based”)), as well as “Population Aging” OR “Aging/epidemiology”, trend, lung cancer surgery, and pneumonectomy (Table 1). We reviewed literature published from 1913 through 2025. PubMed (MEDLINE) was searched from database inception (1946) to 20 September 2025; pre-1946 historical records were identified by manual searching, including Google Scholar and journal archives. Only articles written in English were considered, and no predefined restrictions were applied to the study type. The data sources were independently screened by six authors; discrepancies were resolved in consensus meetings among the six authors. Data analysis was conducted by two authors.

Results

Surgical approaches in NSCLC: the evolution of lobectomy and segmentectomy

Lobectomy, the surgical resection of an anatomical lobe of the lung, was first performed by Heidenhain in 1901 to treat a patient with bronchiectasis (10). In 1912, Davies attempted the first lobectomy for lung cancer (11), and in 1932, Churchill reported one of the first successful cases of lobectomy performed for lung cancer (12). Segmentectomy, in contrast, involves the resection of a bronchopulmonary segment—a smaller anatomical unit within the lung. The concept of the segment as a surgical and pathological unit was first proposed by Churchill and Belsey in 1939, providing the anatomical foundation for segmentectomy (13). In 1956, Thomas reported one of the earliest successful applications of segmentectomy for the treatment of lung cancer (14,15).

The first randomized controlled trial (RCT) between lobectomy and sublobar resection, conducted by Ginsberg and Rubinstein in 1995, established lobectomy as the standard surgical treatment for early-stage NSCLC due to concerns about local recurrence associated with limited resection (3). However, in 2022, the landscape shifted with the publication of Saji et al.’s RCT, which demonstrated that segmentectomy was found to be non-inferior to lobectomy in terms of relapse-free survival (RFS) and was notably superior in overall survival (OS) (4). This landmark finding has reignited debate regarding the optimal surgical approach for early-stage NSCLC.

Today, lobectomy continues to be the standard approach for fit patients with larger or centrally located tumors, while segmentectomy is increasingly favored in patients with small peripheral tumors and in those for whom maximal preservation of pulmonary function is a priority (16). The ongoing evolution of surgical techniques and the growing body of evidence continue to refine the indications for each approach; however, as the lung cancer population continues to age, there is a pressing need for age-stratified analyses focused on elderly patients.

Trends of lung cancer surgery in South Korea

South Korea is among the world’s most rapidly aging societies, making it a particularly informative setting for evaluating how age should guide surgical choice in early-stage NSCLC (17-19). South Korea also operates a universal National Health Insurance System (NHIS), which provides near-complete coverage of lung cancer surgeries. This system allows reliable population-based analyses of national surgical trends (9,20). Building on this nationwide database infrastructure, recent studies have provided detailed insights into temporal patterns in lung cancer epidemiology and surgical practice.

First, consistent with global patterns, the overall incidence of lung cancer has been rising in South Korea (21-23). However, the age-standardized incidence rate has remained relatively stable, indicating that the increase is largely attributable to demographic aging—which has resulted in a marked rise in the number of elderly patients diagnosed with lung cancer, particularly those aged 70 years and older (8,9). Second, all major population-based studies from South Korea have reported a steady increase in the total number of lung cancer surgeries performed over time (Table 2). Among these, lobectomy remains the most commonly performed surgical procedure and has continued to increase in absolute numbers. However, its proportion relative to all surgical approaches has declined over time (8,9,21,24). In contrast, sublobar resections such as segmentectomy and wedge resection, while initially less common, have demonstrated significant increases both in volume and proportion (9,21,24). Meanwhile, the number and proportion of pneumonectomy have decreased, accounting for only 0.5% of surgeries in 2023 (9). Recent data also reflect a shift in surgical techniques. According to a nationwide study spanning 14 years, open surgery has become increasingly rare, while minimally invasive techniques such as video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) and robotic-assisted thoracic surgery (RATS) have become more widely adopted (9). In 2023, for instance, only 291 cases were performed via open thoracotomy compared to 450 RATS cases and 13,443 VATS procedures, with VATS accounting for 96.8% of all surgeries performed that year (9). Finally, patient characteristics have also shifted significantly over the years. All population-based studies have consistently shown an increase in the proportion of female patients undergoing surgery. The proportion of female patients rose from 32% in 2010 to 44.7% in 2023 (9). Age-related trends are equally noteworthy: while surgeries among patients aged <70 years have declined, those among patients aged ≥70 years have steadily increased (8,9,21). Sociodemographic changes have also been reported, including a decrease in the proportion of rural and low-income patients and a corresponding increase in urban and middle-income patients (24). Additionally, an increasing proportion of surgeries have been performed at tertiary hospitals rather than secondary care facilities (9,21).

Collectively, these findings illustrate that both the epidemiology and surgical landscape of lung cancer in South Korea are undergoing notable transitions. While advances in surgical techniques have certainly contributed to these trends, they also appear to be driven in part by broader demographic shifts—particularly the growing proportion of elderly patients undergoing lung cancer surgery. Notably, increases in the use of parenchymal-sparing techniques such as segmentectomy may reflect evolving clinical priorities that seek to balance oncologic control with functional preservation. As the surgical population continues to age, there is a growing need to understand how different surgical approaches perform across age groups, not only in terms of feasibility but also in long-term survival outcomes.

Global evidence on lobectomy versus segmentectomy in elderly patients

Global evidence regarding lobectomy versus segmentectomy in patients with early-stage NSCLC has evolved substantially over the past three decades. Earlier studies consistently supported lobectomy as the standard of care, citing lower recurrence and better survival compared with sublobar resection (3,25,26). However, more recent randomized trials and population-based analyses have begun to challenge this view, showing that sublobar resection—particularly anatomical segmentectomy—can achieve oncologic outcomes comparable to lobectomy, and in some cases even superior survival among patients aged 70 years and older (5). Collectively, these findings highlight the growing importance of age-stratified surgical decision-making.

The pivotal RCT by Ginsberg and Rubinstein in 1995 compared lobectomy with limited resection (segmentectomy or wedge resection) in patients with peripheral T1N0 NSCLC (3). The study found no significant difference in perioperative morbidity, mortality, or late pulmonary function (3). However, limited resection was associated with a significantly higher local recurrence rate and trends toward increased overall and cancer-specific mortality (3). Based on these findings, lobectomy was considered the standard of care for early-stage NSCLC. This position was further supported by large population-based analyses. For example, Khullar et al. analyzed over 13,000 patients in the National Cancer Database (NCDB) and reported significantly better OS for lobectomy compared to segmentectomy [hazard ratio (HR) =1.33] (25). Similarly, Dai et al., using the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) registry, demonstrated that lobectomy was associated with significantly better OS and lung cancer-specific survival (LCSS) in the overall cohort (HR =1.37 for OS) (26). These earlier studies suggested a survival advantage for lobectomy, especially in relatively medically fit, younger patients.

However, more recent evidence has begun to challenge this historical standard, particularly in elderly populations. The Cancer and Leukemia Group B (CALGB) 140503 trial, a large multicenter RCT, demonstrated that in patients with peripheral NSCLC measuring 2 cm or less and pathologically confirmed node-negative status in both hilar and mediastinal lymph nodes, sublobar resection was non-inferior to lobectomy in terms of disease-free survival (DFS), with no significant difference in OS observed (27). These findings suggested that, at least in highly selected low-risk patients, sublobar resection may offer oncologic equivalence with potentially less surgical burden. Several population-based studies in elderly cohorts have reported no significant difference in OS between lobectomy and sublobar resection (segmentectomy or wedge resection), including meta-analyses and SEER-matched cohort studies focused on patients aged ≥70 or ≥75 years (28-30). These studies consistently show that the survival benefit of lobectomy observed in the general population does not necessarily extend to older adults.

Notably, recent data suggest that segmentectomy may be particularly advantageous in elderly patients. In the supplemental analysis of the Japan Clinical Oncology Group (JCOG) 0802/West Japan Oncology Group (WJOG) 4607L trial, Hattori et al. demonstrated that segmentectomy was associated with significantly better OS among patients aged ≥70 years (HR =0.52; P=0.013), while lobectomy was associated with improved RFS in younger patients (HR =1.65; P=0.049) (5). Deng et al., in a registry-based analysis of over 76,000 patients, also showed that segmentectomy was more frequently performed in patients aged ≥70 years and that its relative oncologic outcomes have improved over time (31). Taken together, these results support a shift in surgical strategy toward segmentectomy in elderly patients, particularly those with small, peripheral tumors and limited cardiopulmonary reserve (Table 3). The accumulated evidence, especially from well-designed age-stratified studies and recent randomized trials, highlights the importance of tailoring surgical approaches not only to tumor characteristics but also to patient age and physiologic condition.

Age as a primary consideration in surgical decision-making for lung cancer

While chronological age—particularly the threshold of 70 years—has emerged as an important factor in differentiating the benefits of segmentectomy versus lobectomy, it should not be viewed in isolation. Physiologic status plays a critical role in surgical risk assessment and postoperative recovery, especially in elderly patients (32). Metrics such as the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), pulmonary function parameters [e.g., forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) and diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO)], and frailty indices are increasingly employed to evaluate operative candidacy more comprehensively (33,34). Minimally invasive surgical techniques, particularly VATS and RATS, have facilitated the safe application of anatomic resections in older adults by reducing operative trauma and accelerating recovery (35). Notably, VATS segmentectomy has been associated with decreased postoperative complications, shorter hospital stays, and lower morbidity in elderly cohorts (36). Importantly, these minimally invasive approaches not only improve perioperative outcomes but may also influence the choice of surgical extent, particularly in elderly patients where minimizing surgical burden is a priority.

However, recent randomized and observational evidence underscores that age itself remains a clinically meaningful stratifier. Segmentectomy has demonstrated favorable survival outcomes specifically in patients aged 70 years and older, even when accounting for physiologic status (5). In contrast, lobectomy may still be preferred in younger or more robust patients with early-stage NSCLC, particularly when disease characteristics raise concerns about local recurrence (5). Collectively, these findings suggest that while individualized assessment remains essential, patient age—particularly beyond the seventh decade—should serve as a primary consideration when selecting the surgical approach. Segmentectomy, in this context, is emerging not merely as an alternative but as a potentially preferable option in appropriately selected elderly patients.

Strengths and limitations

This review offers a comprehensive synthesis of historical and emerging evidence related to surgical management in elderly patients with early-stage NSCLC. By integrating findings from landmark RCTs, large population-based cohort studies, and recent age-stratified analyses, the review provides a balanced and up-to-date perspective on the comparative outcomes of lobectomy and segmentectomy in patients aged 70 years and older. This focused lens on the elderly population adds clinical relevance given the ongoing demographic shift toward an aging society.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. Much of the current evidence remains derived from retrospective observational studies, which are inherently subject to selection bias and unmeasured confounders. Furthermore, while some subgroup analyses have addressed older patients, there is a relative lack of prospective, high-quality trials specifically designed to evaluate surgical outcomes in this age group. Future RCTs directly comparing lobectomy and segmentectomy in elderly patients are warranted to inform optimal surgical strategies tailored to this growing and vulnerable population.

Conclusions

Lobectomy has long been regarded as the gold standard for early-stage NSCLC, but emerging evidence suggests that segmentectomy may offer comparable—and in some cases superior—outcomes in patients aged 70 years and older. This finding is particularly relevant in the context of an aging global population, where the proportion of elderly lung cancer patients is steadily increasing. Parallel to this demographic shift, the adoption of segmentectomy has also risen, reflecting a growing clinical preference for parenchymal-sparing approaches in older adults. These trends collectively challenge the longstanding dominance of lobectomy and highlight the need for age-stratified surgical decision-making in early-stage NSCLC. While segmentectomy has clear advantages in small-sized, early-stage lung cancer, the selection of the most appropriate surgical approach should also take into account the patient’s biological age. Based on this criterion, lobectomy remains the standard approach in younger patients. Further randomized trials are needed to definitively guide surgical decision-making in elderly patients with early-stage NSCLC.
