Automotive Elevation Mapping with Interferometric Synthetic Aperture Radar
Leyla A. Kabuli, Griffin Foster

Abstract
Radar is a low-cost and ubiquitous automotive sensor, but is limited by array resolution and sensitivity when performing direction of arrival analysis. Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) is a class of techniques to improve azimuth resolution and sensitivity for radar. Interferometric SAR (InSAR) can be used to extract elevation from the variations in phase measurements in SAR images. Utilizing InSAR we show that a typical, low-resolution radar array mounted on a vehicle can be used to accurately localize detections in 3D space for both urban and agricultural environments. We generate point clouds in each environment by combining InSAR with a signal processing scheme tailored to automotive driving. This low-compute approach allows radar to be used as a primary sensor to map fine details in complex driving environments, and be used to make autonomous perception decisions.



I
Introduction

Autonomous driving requires detailed mapping of the surroundings of a vehicle to infer drivable paths. Radar, lidar, camera, and ultrasonics are common sensors used for mapping. Radar arrays are low-cost sensors that provide high accuracy in range and Doppler of targets within the field of interest. However, lidar and camera typically provide higher angular resolution and accuracy. In radar, the target
direction-of-arrival (DoA)
, the angle estimation in azimuth and elevation of the target, is typically limited due to the physical array size and number of available elements. This limitation is a significant barrier to the utilization of radar for automotive use cases, and an active area of research
[
1
]
. Automotive applications require measuring information in the plane of vehicle motion. As a result, most automotive radar sensors prioritize azimuth over elevation in array design. However, for safe driving, elevation information is necessary. In any driving environment, there are overhanging structures (e.g. bridges, lights, trees, tunnels) and passable on-ground objects (e.g. lane markers, rocks, sewer covers, soil). Without elevation, the
DoA
does not have sufficient information to determine if it is possible to safely drive under, through, or over a detected object.

We present a new application of
Interferometric Synthetic Aperture Radar (InSAR)
combined with a real-time processing scheme that handles the intricacies of automotive driving. This approach provides enhanced sensitivity and angular accuracy in both azimuth and elevation of the environment. Building on our prior work
[
2
]
, we study system accuracy and show results for general urban and agricultural environments, mapping a 3D world with human-scale features for use in safe autonomous driving. To our knowledge, this is the first work to do automotive
InSAR
for elevation mapping of automotive environments.

The development of low-cost
millimeter wave (mmWave)
radar sensors has enabled innovations in automotive radar approaches
[
3
]
.
One such approach is
Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR)
, which forms high dynamic range images with sub-degree resolution across much of the azimuthal
field of view (FOV)
. With a single
virtual antenna (VX)
, formed from a
transmit antenna (TX)
and
receive antenna (RX)
, azimuthal resolution is improved by utilizing the direction of travel to form a large, synthetic aperture
[
4
]
. As the direction of travel is primarily on a plane,
SAR
does not provide any additional elevation resolution.

Interferometry is a technique that localizes a source by comparing the phase of signals received at two sensors. This pair of sensors, or baseline, measures a phase delay that depends on the source’s location and the sensors’ relative position and orientation. This phase delay can be used to determine the source’s elevation. This method is commonly used in fields including optical imaging and radio astronomy
[
5
,
6
]
. Interferometry is often performed using an array of sensors.

InSAR
combines
SAR
and interferometry
[
7
]
to form a 3D map of an environment. Interferometry can be performed using the
VX
pairs on a radar array, forming baselines that further resolve a scene and provide elevation information. The choice of array layout with respect to the motion of travel is an important consideration when performing
InSAR
. At typical driving speeds for automotive radar, the azimuth resolution is dominated by the
SAR
point response rather than the array point response in all directions except the extreme cone centered on the direction of travel
[
4
]
. Additional baselines in the azimuth direction do not provide much in terms of resolution, but do provide additional sensitivity which is correlated with
DoA
accuracy. There is a significant advantage to using interferometry with elevation baselines as
SAR
alone does not provide enhanced resolution in that direction in the automotive case.

InSAR
is typically used to build
digital elevation models (DEMs)
of large regions and surfaces to measure the gradual evolution of geological features
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
. As these imaging targets involve large physical and temporal scales,
InSAR
is predominantly conducted at low frequency using airborne systems.

InSAR
is also incorporated in ground-based automotive radar systems, which offer more flexibility than rail-based systems
[
9
]
. A ground-based
SAR
system was mounted on a vehicle to map natural environments
[
11
]
. A car-borne
SAR
system was used to determine the slope of hills
[
12
]
. In contrast with these works, which focus on extracting elevation information for large, geological features, we map dense and detailed environments at close range, including agricultural and urban scenes. Additionally, we extract small, human-scale features for use in safe autonomous driving. Beamforming techniques have been used for 3D
SAR
imaging with elevation resolution enhancement using a vertical radar array
[
13
]
. Our elevation mapping approach is interferometric and utilizes an off-the-shelf radar array with minimal elevation components. While these works capture the environment on one side of the vehicle in an acquisition, our
SAR
system images the full frontal
FOV
of the vehicle in a single drive path. We use a robust and compact automotive
SAR
setup that is integrated with the vehicle.

we describe our signal model. In Section
III
we describe our experimental setup and processing pipeline. In Section
IV
we analyze the results of our driving scenarios and in Section
V
discuss improvements and further work.

II
Signal Model

In order to extract elevation, we start by forming
SAR
images from raw data. A separate
SAR
image is formed for each
VX
on the real array, with a common phase center defined at the origin in order to maintain relative phase between
VXs
at each coordinate position. Interferometric techniques are then used to extract phase delays from baselines on the real array, utilizing the complex-valued pixels in the
SAR
images. Source information, including elevation, is encoded in the phase delay for a baseline because the path length and resulting relative phase between a source and each
VX
is different.

For our signal model, we assume a narrow fractional bandwidth of a few percent centered in the standard automotive radar band of 78 – 81
\mathrm{GHz}
(
\lambda\approx 3.75

\mathrm{mm}
). The chirp used for imaging has a small fractional bandwidth such that we make the approximation that phase delays have no frequency dependence. We model sources as plane waves with a far-field approximation, as typical sources imaged are sufficiently far from the radar array when compared to the length of a baseline.

We assume that each range-azimuth bin in the
SAR
image contains at most one source. We can verify this assumption by considering the azimuth resolution of a
SAR
image,
\Psi_{SAR}=\frac{\lambda}{L\sin{\theta}}
where
\lambda
is the operational wavelength,
L
is the aperture in meters, and
\sin{\theta}
is the target azimuth angle. For a
1\text{\,}\mathrm{m}
aperture (reasonable for typical driving velocities) the azimuth resolution over much of the
FOV
is
<0.25^{\circ}

[
4
]
. When combined with the typical range resolution on automotive radar (10–30
\mathrm{cm}
), the resulting
SAR
image bins are sufficiently small for most sources in automotive scenes. In the case of multiple sources within one bin, we fit the geometric mean of these sources. This fit will still be valid as long as there is one dominant source. In Section
IV
, our experimental results demonstrate that the fully sampled
SAR
image bins are sufficiently small for a one-source approximation to hold in many dense scenes. Future work could extend our approach to a multi-source model.

Under the signal model assumptions described above, the signal at each pixel in the
SAR
image is represented as a single complex-valued plane wave. For the
SAR
image formed for
VX

i
, the signal can be represented in terms of magnitude and phase as
S_{i}=|S_{i}|e^{j\psi_{i}}
.
Given signals
S_{0}
and
S_{1}
from the baseline formed with
VX

0
and
VX

1
, the phase delay at a pixel can be extracted from the complex correlation of the two signals

\Delta\psi=\angle(S_{0}\cdot S_{1}^{*})=\psi_{0}-\psi_{1}.

(1)

II-A

Vertical Baseline Model

The interferometric model used to extract elevation information depends on the orientation of the baseline. The vertical baseline model is formed from two
VXs
at the same horizontal offset and different elevation offsets in the real array. As shown in Figure
1
, the elevation offset between
VXs
results in a time delay
\tau_{\phi}
, which depends on the baseline geometry and target elevation angle
\phi
. Using the vertical antenna spacing
D_{v}
and the speed of light
c
, time delay and elevation angle are related by

\tau_{\phi}=\frac{D_{v}}{c}\sin{\phi}.

(2)

Fig. 1:
Vertical Baseline Geometry: Illustration of the signal receive path from the source to the vertical baseline on the left. Dashed lines indicate the wavefront at regular intervals along the plane wave trajectory. The plane wave arrives at
\text{VX}_{1}
with time delay
\tau_{\phi}
relative to
\text{VX}_{0}
.

As the signal received from a source is a phase delay, converting time delay to phase delay in Eq. (
2
) gives

\Delta{\psi}=2\pi\frac{c}{\lambda}\tau_{\phi}=4\pi\frac{D_{v}}{\lambda}\sin{\phi}.

(3)

The additional factor of 2 accounts for the two directions in the transmit-receive path of the signals. The elevation angle is recovered from phase delay by solving for
\phi
in Eq. (
3
)

\phi=\sin^{-1}\frac{\lambda}{4\pi D_{v}}\Delta\psi.

(4)

For an array that is dense in elevation, such that baselines are of
D_{v}\leq\frac{\lambda}{4}
, Eq. (
4
) is sufficient to compute the unambiguous elevation angle of a source from a single baseline. For arrays with larger baselines, common interferometric techniques can be used to solve fringe pattern ambiguity using multiple baselines, temporal, or band information
[
5
]
.

Inevitably, there is noise in
\Delta\psi
, which affects the computed elevation angle
\phi
. As measurement noise increases, elevation accuracy decreases. The accuracy of
\phi
is therefore dependent on the
signal-to-noise ratio (S/N)
of the signal measurement, which is determined by factors including the brightness of the source, the integration length used in the image formation process, and noise in the imaging system hardware. Source properties cannot be controlled and system properties are fixed, so their effects on
S/N
cannot be mitigated.

S/N
is improved with long integration lengths in
SAR
measurements, which then improves elevation accuracy.
S/N
is further improved by incorporating signal information from multiple
VXs
, using the average phase delay across multiple vertical baselines for
\Delta\psi
in Eq. (
4
). When
N
vertical baselines are used,
S/N
is improved by a factor of
\sqrt{N}
.

II-B

3D Mapping

In
SAR
processing, there is rotational invariance about the synthetic aperture
[
4
]
. This projects elevation along the range dimension. Each 3D source is projected into the
SAR
image space, which is a 2D range-azimuth projection space represented by the horizontal
(u,v)
plane. The source’s projected position in range-azimuth space is different from its position in 2D Cartesian space
(x,y)
as visualized in Figure
2
. As elevation angle increases, the difference in position is more prominent, with the projected position of a 3D source further from its 2D Cartesian position. Each source must be de-projected from range-azimuth space in order to correctly combine elevation with range and azimuth.

Combining the range-azimuth signal information from the
SAR
image with the elevation information from the interferometric measurement forms a 3D view of the surroundings of a vehicle. This corresponds to a spherical 3D coordinate representation, with range
r
, azimuth
\theta
, and elevation
\phi
. We transform this spherical coordinate representation to
SAR
-specific Cartesian coordinates
(s_{x},s_{y},s_{z})
in order to visualize the scene with a 3D point cloud

s_{x}=r\cos{\theta},s_{y}=r\sin{\theta}\cos{\phi},s_{z}=r\sin{\theta}\sin{\phi}.

(5)

This process takes advantage of
SAR
imaging to increase azimuth resolution. Furthermore, and most importantly, the improved
S/N
and source isolation in the
SAR
image produces a high-fidelity elevation fit with the elevation extracted with the real array.

Projection Effect: A 3D source at Cartesian coordinates
(s_{x},s_{y},s_{z})
with range
r
, azimuth
\theta
and elevation
\phi
is projected to position
(s_{u},s_{v})
in range-azimuth
(u,v)
space. As elevation increases, the projected position is further out than its 2D Cartesian position
(s_{x},s_{y})
.

III
Experimental Setup and Processing

We apply this approach to agricultural and urban driving scenes, which are primary environments for automotive radar sensors.
Two radar sensor arrays were mounted on a compact car. Each radar was positioned near the front edge of the vehicle and oriented at 45 degrees from the front. This configuration provided 180 degree azimuth coverage across the front of the vehicle.
SAR
images were generated independently for both the left and right radar sensors and merged to form images covering the full
FOV
. An interferometric processing scheme based on the signal model described in Section
II
was used to extract the elevation map and 3D point cloud. Processing time for each image is under one second, with minimal computational cost, making this approach well-suited for future use in real-time automotive
InSAR
.

III-A

Radar Array

The radar sensor front ends use a TI AWR1243BOOST evaluation board. The AWR1243 operates in the 76–81
\mathrm{GHz}
band and supports 3
TXs
and 4
RXs
to generate up to 12
VXs
in single-sensor mode. The array configuration of the
TXs
and
RXs
on the evaluation board forms a dense virtual array as shown in Figure
3
. The virtual array has two elevation layers spaced
\lambda/4
apart. Each three-patch microstrip antenna element provides a wide
FOV
in azimuth (78 degrees at half power) and elevation (40 degrees at half power).

Fig. 3:
Radar Array: TI AWR1243BOOST array consists of 4
RX
(blue squares) and 3
TX
(orange diamonds) antenna elements. Antenna elements form a dense, 2-elevation layer virtual array of
VXs
(green circles).

Time-division multiplexing MIMO (TDM MIMO)
coding scheme was used to cycle through the 3
TXs
.
TDM MIMO
simplifies processing and maximizes dynamic range but limits the maximum velocity. To use
TDM MIMO
, we limit our driving velocity to 20 mph (9 m/s). Extensions to higher driving speeds will be discussed in Section
V
.

We used a modulation scheme optimized for SAR imaging in urban and agricultural environments. Each chirp was sampled with 512 samples at a rate of 18.75 Msps. The total chirp bandwidth was 820
\mathrm{MHz}
(27.3
\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro s}
pulse length with a 30
\mathrm{MHz}
/
\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro s}
ramp slope). The maximum range was 93.7
\mathrm{m}
with a range resolution of 18.3
\mathrm{cm}
. The
pulse repetition interval (PRI)
was 63.9
\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro s}
from chirp to chirp, thus for 3
TX
TDM coding the effective
PRI
was 191.7
\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro s}
. Chirp frames were structured to produce 256 chirps per TX with minimal idle time between frames to approximate regular sampling between frames. The modulation center frequency was
f_{c}=77.4

\mathrm{GHz}
resulting in a mean wavelength of
\nu_{c}=3.87

\mathrm{mm}
, the value used in determining the elevation baseline
D_{\nu}
.

For accurate
SAR
processing, we use an off-the-shelf GPS IMU sensor to localize the relative ego-vehicle position to sub-wavelength accuracy
[
14
]
. Our acquisition system utilizes a common timestamp using the GPS signal to assign position information to each chirp. Our acquisition system offers two acquisition modes, a raw data recorder and a real-time
SAR
imaging system. For this experimental setup we used the raw data recorder, which captures the unprocessed ADC samples, timestamps, and GPS IMU information to file to be processed offline. We will discuss performing this approach in real time in Section
V
, which would be possible with little additional computational cost.

III-B

SAR Imaging

SAR
images are formed per
VX
on the real array using the
Fast Back-Projection (FBP)
algorithm
[
4
]
. We use standard approximations in SAR imaging, and make the start-stop approximation across the complete TDM cycle. We image over a 1 m aperture, empirically chosen to balance maximizing the aperture length while minimizing the GPS IMU errors. For each
VX
, a 30
\mathrm{m}
x 30
\mathrm{m}
image is formed with a pixel resolution of 4
\mathrm{cm}
to oversample the range resolution. Each pixel is complex-valued, maintaining crucial phase information for the interferometric measurement.

S/N
is improved by incorporating signal information from multiple
VXs
simultaneously in
FBP
. Furthermore, interferometric measurements from multiple vertical baselines are averaged to increase
S/N
. This utilizes the maximum number of
VXs
possible in our radar array (Figure
3
).

III-C

Elevation Mapping and Processing

After SAR image formation, elevation is extracted from the interferometric measurement by applying Equation (
4
). Elevation is then combined with the range and azimuth information at each
SAR
image pixel to form a 3D representation of the scene. This initial cluster of points has no distinction between signal from real sources or noise. Noise and spurious effects are inevitable since data is acquired in real automotive environments. To extract scene structure, we use a custom series of signal processing operations, highlighting high-
S/N
information from strong sources, corresponding to real features, while suppressing low-
S/N
information from noise.

An
S/N
threshold of 15
\mathrm{dB}
separates real sources from noise. We compute
S/N
by comparing per-pixel signal magnitude to the median signal across the scene, as the median is more robust to outliers than the mean for large data. Since multiple baselines were averaged to improve
S/N
, points are further filtered based on phase variations across the
VXs
in these baselines. Removing these high variation points suppressed false arcing structures from strong point responses.

The radar has high sensitivity to close-range objects. Elevation angles exceeding 45 degrees were removed, based on the largest angles for typical scene objects and the radar
FOV
. The close-range sensitivity resulted in a constant cluster of points appearing in front of the car. This cluster was removed with a minimum radius cutoff of 2 meters and an azimuth cutoff of
\pm
15 degrees from the front. Due to multiple path effects and reflections from the ground, some points accumulated phase corresponding to points underground, which cannot be detected in our environment and with our experimental setup. A minimum elevation threshold was used to remove points that appeared underground.

After signal post-processing, the spherical coordinate representation is transformed to Cartesian coordinates using Equation (
5
). The resulting points in the scene are stored in a point cloud
[
15
]
. Point clouds are visualized using PCL Tools
[
16
]
.

Results and analysis

We apply this approach to agricultural and urban driving scenes. For the agricultural use case, we demonstrate driving through a vineyard on a poorly-paved road. For the urban use case, we show a typical driving scene: an intersection in a dense environment with other vehicles, buildings, and trees.

V
Conclusion

We have shown that Interferometric
SAR
processing can be used to enhance the elevation accuracy and
S/N
of off-the-shelf radar arrays used in common human-scale autonomous vehicle environments such as agricultural processing and urban driving. We demonstrated high-quality automotive elevation mapping with
InSAR
, forming 3D point cloud representations of urban and agricultural environments using a simple, interpretable, and low computational cost processing approach. This is a new application of
InSAR
, accounting for the complex trajectories and vehicle dynamics that are associated with driving in diverse automotive environments.

The low elevation resolution in our array is a dominant limiting factor for our elevation accuracy. In our controlled environment studies, we were able to localize nearby objects to centimeter-level accuracy even with the low elevation resolution. This is because the accuracy is related to the aperture size and the S/N of the object. In the on-vehicle tests, the range of objects was extended leading to lower S/N and lower accuracy. We used a manufacturer-supplied radar array optimized for simplicity and azimuth resolution. This is not the ideal array for our approach, as the azimuth resolution is dominated by the synthetic aperture response. A more optimal array design would primarily focus on elevation resolution. For the most flexibility in use, the ideal array would still maintain some azimuth resolution to enable moving object tracking with traditional range-Doppler processing.

With a higher elevation resolution array, we would expect vibrations of the array (e.g. due to rough roads) to become a limiting factor. Elevation accuracy would be improved by modeling vibration patterns and using an array geometry with a larger physical elevation aperture.

For our experimental set up we used
TDM MIMO
as a simple form of MIMO coding. This limits the maximum velocity of the vehicle before aliasing artifacts start appearing in the SAR image. A natural extension of this work is to use a coded MIMO scheme. This would have the benefit of extending the maximum velocity and potentially increasing sensitivity over a fixed aperture.

We used a simple phase difference model to determine the elevation angles for each
SAR
image pixel. This model used only the vertical baselines in an array. Using baselines with various orientations in a generalized approach could further enhance elevation resolution and sensitivity. Furthermore, our model made the approximation of a single source per range-azimuth bin.
