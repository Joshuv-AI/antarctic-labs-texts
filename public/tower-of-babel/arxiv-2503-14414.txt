Recovering Parameters from Edge Fluctuations: Beta-Ensembles and Critically-Spiked Models
Pierre Yves Gaudreau Lamarre

Abstract
. Let Λ = { Λ 0 , Λ 1 , Λ 2 , … } \Lambda=\{\Lambda_{0},\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2},\ldots\} be the point process that describes the edge scaling limit of either (i) ”regular” beta-ensembles with inverse temperature β > 0 \beta>0 , or (ii) the top eigenvalues of Wishart or Gaussian invariant random matrices perturbed by r 0 ≥ 1 r_{0}\geq 1 critical spikes. In other words, Λ \Lambda is the eigenvalue point process of one of the scalar or multivariate stochastic Airy operators. We prove that a single observation of Λ \Lambda suffices to recover (almost surely) either (i) β \beta in the case of beta-ensembles, or (ii) r 0 r_{0} in the case of critically-spiked models. Our proof relies on the recently-developed semigroup theory for the multivariate stochastic Airy operators. Going beyond these parameter-recovery applications, our results also (iii) refine our understanding of the rigidity properties of Λ \Lambda , and (iv) shed new light on the equality (in distribution) of stochastic Airy spectra with different dimensions and the same Robin boundary conditions.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Main Result

Let
\Theta
be the set of parameters of the form
\theta=(r,\beta,w)
, where
r\in\mathbb{N}
is any positive integer and

\beta
and
w
are defined as follows:

(1)

If
r=1
, then
\beta>0
and
w\in(-\infty,\infty]
, and

(2)

if
r>1
, then
\beta\in\{1,2,4\}
and
w=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{r})\in(-\infty,\infty]^{r}
.

Given
\theta\in\Theta
, let
\mathcal{H}_{\theta}
be the corresponding stochastic Airy operator (SAO). That is:

(1)

If
r=1
, then we let
W:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be a standard Brownian motion, and let

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\theta}=-\tfrac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}+x+\tfrac{2}{\beta^{1/2}}W^{\prime}(x),

which acts on functions
f\in L^{2}\big([0,\infty),\mathbb{R}\big)
subject to the boundary condition

\begin{cases}f(0)=0&\text{if }w=\infty\text{ (Dirichlet)},\\
f^{\prime}(0)=wf(0)&\text{if }w\in\mathbb{R}\text{ (Robin)}.\end{cases}

(2)

Let
\mathbb{F}_{1}=\mathbb{R}
,

\mathbb{F}_{2}=\mathbb{C}
, and
\mathbb{F}_{4}=\mathbb{H}
.
If
r>1
and
\beta\in\{1,2,4\}
, then
let
W_{\beta}
be a standard
r
-dimensional matrix Brownian motion on
\mathbb{F}_{\beta}

(i.e., with GOE, GUE, or GSE increments depending on whether
\beta=1,2,4
; see
[
8
, Page 2730]
), and
define

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\theta}=-\tfrac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}+rx+\sqrt{2}W_{\beta}^{\prime}(x).

This acts on
r
-dimensional vector-valued functions
f=\big(f(i,\cdot):1\leq i\leq r\big)
whose components

f(i,\cdot)\in L^{2}\big([0,\infty),\mathbb{F}_{\beta}\big)
are subject to the boundary conditions

(1.3)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}f(i,0)=0&\text{if }w_{i}=\infty\text{ (Dirichlet)},\\
f^{\prime}(i,0)=w_{i}f(i,0)&\text{if }w_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\text{ (Robin)},\end{cases}\qquad 1\leq i\leq r.

The SAOs were introduced by Edelman-Sutton
[
24
]
,
Ramírez-Rider-Virág
[
53
]
, and Bloemendal-Virág
[
7
,
8
]

to describe the edge fluctuations of various point processes of high interest in mathematical physics
and statistics. That is, depending on the choice of
\theta
, the
eigenvalues of
\mathcal{H}_{\theta}
either describe the edge fluctuations of a wide class of
beta-ensembles with so-called ”regular” external potential

[
4
,
5
,
11
,
19
,
44
,
49
,
53
,
56
]
,
or the soft-edge fluctuations of critically-spiked Wishart or Gaussian invariant ensembles with real, complex, or quaternion entries
[
2
,
7
,
8
,
45
,
50
,
59
]
.

In this paper, we are interested in the following question regarding the SAOs:

Question 1.1
.

Let
\theta\in\Theta
be fixed, and let

\Lambda^{\theta}=\big\{\Lambda^{\theta}_{0},\Lambda^{\theta}_{1},\Lambda^{\theta}_{2},\ldots\big\}

denote the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{H}_{\theta}
arranged in increasing order.
What information about
\theta
can be recovered almost-surely from
a single realization of
\Lambda^{\theta}
?

Given
(r,\beta,w)\in\Theta
, define the parameter

(1.4)

\displaystyle r_{0}=r_{0}(w)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mathbf{1}_{\{w_{i}<\infty\}};

if
r=1
, then this reduces to
r_{0}=\mathbf{1}_{\{w<\infty\}}
.
In words,
r_{0}
is the number of Robin boundary conditions imposed on
\mathcal{H}_{\theta}
’s domain
(hence
r-r_{0}
is the number of Dirichlet conditions).
Our main result asserts that if either one of
\beta
or
r_{0}
is known,
then the other parameter can be recovered almost surely from a single realization of
\Lambda^{\theta}
:

Theorem 1.2
.

There exists a deterministic function
\mathcal{T}

(which can be written as an explicit limit; see (
3.5
) for the details) such that for every
\theta\in\Theta
, one has

(1.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\Lambda^{\theta})=r_{0}+1/\beta\qquad\text{almost surely.}

1.2.
Applications

Our main motivation for proving Theorem
1.2
consists of two parameter-recovery results:

(i)

Corollary
2.4
: The temperature of any sequence of ”regular” beta-ensembles can be recovered from a single
observation of their edge fluctuations.

(ii)

Corollary
2.14
: The number of signals ”close enough” to the critical threshold in sequences of
spiked Wishart and Gaussian invariant models can be recovered
from a single observation of their top edge fluctuations.

Part of the significance of these results is that, to the best of our knowledge, all previously-known techniques to recover
these parameters use the asymptotics of the full configuration of the point process or random matrix eigenvalues in question. In sharp contrast to this, Corollaries

2.4
and
2.14
show that the same can be done using only the vanishing proportion
of particles/eigenvalues that contribute to edge scaling limits.
See Sections
2.1
and
2.2
for more details and
background, as well as open problems motivated by these results
(Open Problems
2.5
and
2.15
).

In addition to the above, we present two applications of a more theoretical nature:

(iii)

In Corollaries
2.20
and
2.22
, we explain how Theorem
1.2
refines our understanding of the rigidity properties of
\Lambda^{\theta}
(notably number rigidity).

(iv)

In Corollary
2.27
, we explain how our proof technique
sheds new light on the mysterious invariance of SAO eigenvalues with respect to adding
components with Dirichlet boundary conditions.

We refer once again to Sections
2.3
and
2.4
for more details, background, and open problems motivated by these applications.

1.3.
Proof Technique

Our approach to prove Theorem
1.2
is inspired by the theory
of inverse problems in spectral geometry. That is, the observation
that an impressive amount of information
about a differential operator’s domain can be inferred from its eigenvalues alone.
One of the most well-known manifestation of this phenomenon is the Weyl law

[
60
]
and its various extensions: If
\big\{\lambda_{k}(M,b):k\geq 0\big\}
are the eigenvalues of the Laplacian
on some compact manifold
M
subject to some boundary condition
b
(if
\partial M\neq\varnothing
),
then
M
’s dimension,
M
’s volume,
M
’s boundary area, and the boundary condition
b
(at least Dirichlet versus Neumann)
can in many cases be determined by the asymptotics of the eigenvalue counting function

\lambda\mapsto\big|\big\{k\geq 0:\lambda_{k}(M,b)\leq\lambda\big\}\big|.

In this context, Question
2.1
can be viewed as
the inverse spectral problem for the SAOs, as
the components of
\theta=(r,\beta,w)
characterize

\mathcal{H}_{\theta}
’s domain and noise. Thus,
the proof of Theorem
1.2
relies on the observation that

r_{0}+1/\beta
can be recovered from precise asymptotics of the
\Lambda_{k}^{\theta}
’s as
k\to\infty
.
That being said, instead of recovering
r_{0}+1/\beta
using the asymptotics of the eigenvalue
counting function, we do so
via the exponential traces
\mathrm{Tr}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathcal{H}_{\theta}/2}\big]=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-t\Lambda^{\theta}_{k}/2}
for small
t>0

(which is also widely used in spectral geometry; see, e.g.,
[
38
, Chapter 3]
and references therein).
More specifically, our proof of Theorem
1.2
relies on showing that

(1.6)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathcal{H}_{\theta}/2}\big]=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}t^{-3/2}+\frac{1}{2}\left(r_{0}+\frac{1}{\beta}\right)-\frac{1}{4}+\mathfrak{o}_{t}\qquad\text{for all }t\in(0,1],

where the random remainder terms
\mathfrak{o}_{t}
are such that

(1.7)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\mathfrak{o}_{t}]=o(1)\qquad\text{ as }t\to 0^{+},

and such that there exists a constant
C>0
satisfying

(1.8)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Cov}[\mathfrak{o}_{s},\mathfrak{o}_{t}]\leq C\left(\frac{\min\{s,t\}}{\max\{s,t\}}\right)^{1/4}\qquad\text{ for every }s,t\in(0,1].

In particular, the fact that any sequence
\mathfrak{o}_{t_{1}},\mathfrak{o}_{t_{2}},\mathfrak{o}_{t_{3}},\ldots
such that
t_{n}\to 0^{+}
has a bounded
variance and vanishing covariance
means that
r_{0}+1/\beta
can be recovered from (
1.6
) by
the law of large numbers, that is, by averaging the traces
\mathrm{Tr}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathcal{H}_{\theta}/2}\big]

over a sequence of
t
’s that vanishes fast enough; see (
3.5
) for details.

The main technical input in the proof of (
1.6
)
consists of the recently-developed
semigroup theory for the
multivariate
SAOs
[
32
]
, i.e., the case
r>1
(which is essential for our application
of Theorem
1.2
to the spiked models in Corollary
2.14
). This development extended prior results on
the semigroup theory of the scalar SAOs
[
29
,
39
]
, i.e., the case
r=1
.

1.4.
Questions of Optimality

Since Theorem
1.2
does not allow to recover the parameter

\theta
completely, it is natural to ask if our result is optimal. For instance, one might ask
if, in addition to
r_{0}
, it is possible to recover information about the magnitudes of the components

w_{i}
in (
1.3
) that are finite. That said, it can be shown that this cannot be achieved
with the method used in this paper.

More specifically, it is known that for some cases of
\theta
(notably,
\theta=(1,2,\infty)
; see
[
28
, Proposition 2.27]
),
the random remainder term
\mathfrak{o}_{t}
in (
1.6
) satisfies

\liminf_{t\to 0^{+}}\,\mathbf{Var}[\mathfrak{o}_{t}]>0.

Given these nontrivial fluctuations, the deterministic terms in (
1.6
) represent
the full extent of the information that can be inferred with 100% accuracy from the small
t
asymptotics
of
\mathrm{Tr}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathcal{H}_{\theta}/2}\big]
. In particular, if it is in fact possible to recover more information
about
\theta
from
\Lambda^{\theta}
, then we expect that a different approach than the one used in this
paper would be required.

Organization

In Section
2
, we provide more details on

1.2
.
Then, in Section
3
, we prove our main result.

2.
Applications and Open Problems

2.1.
Temperature Recovery in Beta-Ensembles

2.1.1.
Background

In statistical physics, the beta-ensembles model particle systems on
\mathbb{R}
subjected to
an external potential energy and pairwise logarithmic repulsion.
More specifically, let
V:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a fixed function with sufficient growth, and for every

n\in\mathbb{N}
, define the
n
-particle Hamiltonian

(2.1)

\displaystyle H_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}V(x_{i})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\log|x_{j}-x_{i}|,\qquad x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

For every
\beta>0
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the
n
-dimensional beta-ensemble with external potential
V
and inverse temperature
\beta

as the point process
\mathcal{B}^{\beta,n}=\big\{\mathcal{B}^{\beta,n}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{B}^{\beta,n}_{n}\big\}

sampled according to the Gibbs measure with density

(2.2)

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{Z_{\beta,n}}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta nH_{n}(x)}~\mathrm{d}x,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

We adopt the convention that the points in
\mathcal{B}^{\beta,n}
are in increasing order.
The constant
Z_{\beta,n}>0
, called the partition function, ensures that (
2.2
) integrates to one.

The question of whether the temperature of infinite Gibbs point processes
can be recovered from a single sample
has attracted some attention in the literature;
see, e.g.,

[
22
, Section 5.4]
and
[
21
]
and references therein.
In the case of a finite configuration, such as (
2.2
), this is of course not possible due to the
mutual absolute continuity of the particles’ joint densities with different
\beta
’s. However, the problem becomes interesting
if one asks about recovering
\beta
as the number of particles grows to infinity.
For instance, several prior works have studied the consistency of maximum likelihood and
pseudolikelihood estimators (MLEs and MPLEs) of temperature in spin glasses in the large-dimensional limit;
see, e.g.,
[
6
,
15
,
17
,
33
]
and references therein.
In view of extending this to beta-ensembles, one may then ask:

Question 2.1
.

Let
V:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
in (
2.1
) be fixed.
Are there asymptotics (as
n\to\infty
) of the particles
\mathcal{B}^{\beta,n}_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\mathcal{B}^{\beta,n}_{n}
from which

\beta
can be recovered almost surely? If so, what is the smallest number of particles needed to recover
\beta
?

To the best of our knowledge, this has not been studied in the literature.
That being said, one of the main hurdles in applying MLEs to Gibbs point processes
consists of computing the partition function (e.g.,
[
61
, Section 1]
). Therefore,
given that
Z_{\beta,n}
is very well understood for beta-ensembles—see

[
9
,
10
]
and references therein—we expect that
a similar strategy could be used to solve Question
2.1
.
To make this more concrete, consider the following heuristic (which
is informal as the asymptotics therein were calculated using Wolfram Mathematica;
see Appendix
A
):

Heuristic 2.2

(Informal)
.

Consider the particle configuration’s total energy

(2.3)

\displaystyle H_{n}(\mathcal{B}^{\beta,n})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}V(\mathcal{B}^{\beta,n}_{i})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\log\big|\mathcal{B}^{\beta,n}_{j}-\mathcal{B}^{\beta,n}_{i}\big|.

If
V(x)=x^{2}/2
, then

(2.4)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}-4\big(H_{n}(\mathcal{B}^{\beta,n})-\tfrac{3}{8}n+\tfrac{1}{2}\log n\big)-1=\log(\beta^{2}/4)-2\tfrac{\Gamma^{\prime}(1+\beta/2)}{\Gamma(1+\beta/2)}\qquad\text{in probability},

where,
\Gamma(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{z-1}\mathrm{e}^{-t}~\mathrm{d}t
denotes the Gamma function.
Given that
\beta\mapsto\log(\beta^{2}/4)-2\tfrac{\Gamma^{\prime}(1+\beta/2)}{\Gamma(1+\beta/2)}
is invertible for
\beta\in(0,\infty)

(see Appendix
A
),
we conclude that
\beta
can, in principle, be recovered from the limit (
2.4
).

As argued in Appendix
A
, the limit
(
2.4
) relies on an asymptotic analysis of the partition function.
Thus, we expect that (
2.4
) could be extended to more general
V
’s using the asymptotic expansions of
general beta-ensemble partition functions in
[
9
, (1-3)]
and
[
10
, (1.14)]
.

2.1.2.
New Result

A common feature of the statistics discussed above (i.e., MLEs, MPLEs, or the total energy in (
2.3
)) is that they use

every point
in the configuration
\mathcal{B}^{\beta,n}_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\mathcal{B}^{\beta,n}_{n}
as an input. In sharp contrast to this, Theorem
1.2

implies that the temperature of many beta-ensembles can be recovered from a much more parsimonious asymptotic,
which only depends on the vanishing proportion of particles that contribute to edge scaling limits (as the vast majority of particles instead contribute to the bulk scaling limits;
e.g.,
[
57
,
58
]
).
This is based on the edge universality of beta-ensembles, part of which can be informally summarized as follows:

Theorem 2.3

(Informal)
.

Let
V
be a regular one-cut potential. That is,
V
has an equilibrium measure
\mu_{V}

supported on a compact interval
[\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}]
, and
\mu_{V}
’s density vanishes like a square root at

\mathfrak{a}
and
\mathfrak{b}
. Under additional technical conditions on
V
,
for any edge
\mathfrak{e}\in\{\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}\}
, there exists scaling constants
\mathfrak{s}_{n}=O(n^{2/3})

such that

(2.5)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathfrak{s}_{n}\big(\mathfrak{e}-\mathcal{B}^{\beta,n}\big)=\Lambda^{(1,\beta,\infty)}\qquad\text{in distribution}.

See
[
4
,
11
,
19
,
44
,
49
,
53
,
56
]

for the details, as well as
[
5
]
for a multi-cut extension of Theorem
2.3
,
whose statement we omit for simplicity.
Given that
r_{0}=0
when
\theta=(1,\beta,\infty)
,
we thus obtain the following corollary from Theorem
1.2
:

Corollary 2.4
.

Suppose that (
2.5
) holds,
and let
\mathcal{T}
be the deterministic function in (
1.5
)/(
3.5
). For every
\beta>0
, one has

\mathcal{T}\left(\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathfrak{s}_{n}\big(\mathfrak{e}-\mathcal{B}^{\beta,n}\big)\right)=1/\beta\qquad\text{almost surely},

where the limit above is in distribution.

Similarly to Theorem
2.3
, there is an obvious
multi-cut extension to Corollary
2.4
, which we omit for simplicity.

2.1.3.
Open Problem

Following-up on Corollary
2.4
, it is natural to wonder
if it is possible to construct consistent estimators of
\beta
that (1) only depend on
\mathcal{B}^{\beta,n}
’s edge fluctuations
and (2) can be applied to finite-dimensional
models. More specifically:

Open Problem 2.5
.

Let
\mathfrak{e}
and
\mathfrak{s}_{n}
be as in (
2.5
).
Does there exist a sequence of measurable functions
\{\mathcal{T}_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
such that,
as
n\to\infty
,

(1)

\mathcal{T}_{n}
only depends on the particles
\mathcal{B}^{\beta,n}_{k}\in\mathcal{B}^{\beta,n}

such that
|\mathcal{B}^{\beta,n}_{k}-\mathfrak{e}|=o(1)
; and

(2)

\mathcal{T}_{n}(\mathcal{B}^{\beta,n})\to 1/\beta
almost surely?

If so, can one also characterize the fluctuations of
\mathcal{T}_{n}(\mathcal{B}^{\beta,n})-1/\beta
as
n\to\infty
?

Thanks to Corollary
2.4
and (
3.5
), we expect that a positive
resolution to Open Problem
2.5
could potentially rely on establishing a statement of the form

(2.6)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\left|\sum_{k\geq 0\text{ s.t. }|\mathcal{B}^{\beta,n}_{k}-\mathfrak{e}|\leq\varepsilon_{n}}\mathrm{e}^{-t_{n}\,\mathfrak{s}_{n}(\mathfrak{e}-\mathcal{B}^{\beta,n}_{k})/2}-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-t_{n}\Lambda^{(1,\beta,\infty)}_{k}/2}\right|=0

for
\varepsilon_{n},t_{n}=o(1)
. A similar result already exists in the special case where
V(x)=x^{2}/2
when
t_{n}
is replaced by a fixed
t>0
;
see the proof of
[
39
, Corollary 2.10]
.

2.2.
Detecting Critical Signals in Spiked Models

2.2.1.
Background

In the early 2000’s, Johnstone introduced the spiked Wishart ensemble

[
43
, Pages 301–304]
as a means of modeling the effect of
low-rank signals on the spectral statistics of covariance estimators in high dimension.
Some years later, Péché
[
50
]
introduced a closely related additive spiked ensemble to study
low-rank perturbations of self-adjoint matrices.
In their simplest incarnation (i.e., rotationally-symmetric noise),
these models are defined as follows:
Let
r\in\mathbb{N}
,
\beta\in\{1,2,4\}
, and
\ell=(\ell_{r-1},\ldots,\ell_{0})\in[0,\infty)^{r}
be fixed,
assuming that
\ell_{r-1}\leq\cdots\leq\ell_{0}
.

(1)

For any integers
n\geq 0
and
p\geq r
, let
D_{\beta,n,p}
be a
n\times p
matrix whose
entries are i.i.d. standard Gaussians in
\mathbb{F}_{\beta}
, and let
\Sigma_{\ell,p}
be the
p\times p
diagonal matrix with eigenvalues

1,\ldots,1,1+\ell_{r-1},\ldots,1+\ell_{0}
. We define the
(n,p)
-dimensional spiked Wishart ensemble as follows (
\cdot^{*}
denotes the conjugate transpose):

W_{\beta,\ell,n,p}=\tfrac{1}{n}D_{\beta,n,p}\Sigma_{\ell,p}D_{\beta,n,p}^{*}.

(2)

For any integer
n\geq r,
let
X_{\beta,n}=(D_{\beta,n}^{*}+D_{\beta,n})/\sqrt{2}
,
where
D_{\beta,n}
is an
n\times n
matrix whose entries are i.i.d. standard Gaussians in
\mathbb{F}_{\beta}

(in other words,
X_{\beta,n}
is a GOE, GUE, or GSE matrix, depending on whether
\beta=1
,
2
, or
4
).
Then, let
P_{\ell,n}
be the
n\times n
diagonal matrix with eigenvalues
0,\ldots,0,\ell_{r-1},\ldots,\ell_{0}
.
We define the
n
-dimensional spiked Gaussian invariant ensemble as

Y_{\beta,\ell,n}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}X_{\beta,n}+P_{\ell,n}.

The main problem involving these models consists of detecting the signal
\ell

using the noisy observations provided by the spectra of
W_{\beta,\ell,n,p}
or
Y_{\beta,\ell,n}

in the high-dimensional setting
n\asymp p
.
As a first step toward this goal, one may consider the asymptotic version
of the question:

Question 2.6
.

Let
r\in\mathbb{N}
and
\beta=\{1,2,4\}
be fixed.
In the case of the Wishart ensemble, we assume that
p=p(n)
depends on
n

in such a way that

(2.7)

\displaystyle p(n)=\gamma n\big(1+o(1)\big)\qquad\text{as }n\to\infty

for some
\gamma\in(0,\infty)
.
Let
\ell=\ell(n)\in[0,\infty)^{r}
be either fixed or dependent on
n
, and let

\mathcal{S}^{\beta,\ell,n}=\big\{\mathcal{S}^{\beta,\ell,n}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{S}^{\beta,\ell,n}_{n}\big\}

be the eigenvalues of either
W_{\beta,\ell,n,p(n)}
or
Y_{\beta,\ell,n}
, in increasing order.
Are there asymptotics of
\mathcal{S}^{\beta,\ell,n}

(as
n\to\infty
) from which any information about
the signal
\ell
can be recovered almost surely?

The first papers in this direction were by Baik-Ben Arous-Péché

[
2
]
and Péché
[
50
]
. Among other things, these results showed that
the almost-sure asymptotic detectability of each signal
\ell_{i}
depends on its size relative to the threshold

\tau=\begin{cases}\sqrt{\gamma}&\text{(Wishart)},\\
1&\text{(Gaussian invariant)}.\end{cases}

That is, the analysis of Question
2.6
is typically split into
three distinct cases:

(1)

The supercritical case, where
\ell_{i}>\tau
for at least one
0\leq i\leq r-1
;

(2)

the subcritical case, where
\ell_{r-1}\leq\cdots\leq\ell_{0}<\tau
; and

(3)

the critical case, where
\ell_{r_{\mathrm{c}}-1},\ldots,\ell_{0}=\tau+o(1)
for some
r_{\mathrm{c}}\geq 1
.

To the best of our knowledge, the current state of the art for this theory can be briefly summarized as follows.
Firstly, we state a result for the supercritical case:

Theorem 2.7

(
[
2
,
3
,
14
,
50
]
)
.

Let
\mathfrak{e}
be the top edge of the limiting empirical spectral distribution (ESD) of the
unperturbed Wishart or Gaussian invariant model, i.e.,

(2.8)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{e}=\begin{cases}(1+\sqrt{\gamma})^{2}&\text{(Wishart)},\\
2&\text{(Gaussian invariant)}.\end{cases}

Define the function

\mathcal{O}(x)=\begin{cases}(1+x)(1+\gamma/x)&\text{(Wishart)},\\
x+1/x&\text{(Gaussian invariant)}.\end{cases}

For any fixed
\ell\in[0,\infty)^{r}
and
i\geq 0
, one has

(2.9)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{S}^{\ell,\beta,n}_{n-i}=\begin{cases}\mathcal{O}(\ell_{i})&\text{if $i\leq r-1$ and $\ell_{i}>\tau$},\\
\mathfrak{e}&\text{if $i\leq r-1$ and $\ell_{i}\leq\tau$, or if $i>r-1$}\end{cases}\qquad\text{almost surely}.

Remark 2.8
.

\mathcal{O}(x)>\mathfrak{e}
is strictly increasing for
x\in(\tau,\infty)
. Thus,
(
2.9
) implies that the number and magnitude of
all supercritical signals
\ell_{i}>\tau
can be recovered from the
asymptotics of a
finite
number of the top eigenvalues of
W_{\beta,\ell,n,p}
and
Y_{\beta,\ell,n}
.

Secondly, we state a result regarding the impossibility of detecting subcritical signals
almost-surely using the eigenvalues
\mathcal{S}^{\beta,\ell,n}
alone:

Theorem 2.9

(
[
41
,
46
,
47
,
48
]
)
.

Let
\beta=1
(i.e.,
\mathbb{F}_{\beta}=\mathbb{R}
).
For any fixed
\ell\in[0,\infty)^{r}
such that
\ell_{r-1}\leq\cdots\leq\ell_{0}<\tau
,
the sequence of spiked eigenvalues
(\mathcal{S}^{\beta,\ell,n}:n\geq 1)
is contiguous with respect to the
sequence of unperturbed eigenvalues
(\mathcal{S}^{\beta,0,n}:n\geq 1)
. That is, for any sequence of
measurable maps
\{\mathcal{T}_{n}:n\geq 1\}
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{P}\big[\mathcal{T}_{n}(\mathcal{S}^{\beta,0,n})=0\big]=1\qquad\text{implies that}\qquad\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{P}\big[\mathcal{T}_{n}(\mathcal{S}^{\beta,\ell,n})=0\big]=1.

Remark 2.10
.

Despite the impossibility result in Theorem
2.9
,
one could ask if there nevertheless exist tests that distinguish between the hypotheses

(2.10)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{H}_{0}\text{ : "no perturbation"}\qquad\text{versus}\qquad\boldsymbol{H}_{\ell}\text{ : "perturbation $=\ell$"}

with nontrivial statistical power when
\ell\neq 0
is subcritical.
The short answer to this question is yes; see, e.g.,
[
25
,
41
,
42
,
47
,
48

and references therein for a non-exhaustive sample of this very sizeable literature.
A unifying feature of all these results is that the statistical tests that achieve optimal or close-to-optimal power in distinguishing (

(e.g., likelihood ratios or linear spectral statistics)
all use the full spectrum
\mathcal{S}^{\beta,\ell,n}_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\mathcal{S}^{\beta,\ell,n}_{n}
as an input. In the words of Dobriban
[
23
]
:

”
All eigenvalues matter to achieve sharp detection
of weak principal components in high-dimensional data.
”

To give a simple example of how such results look like, consider the following:

Theorem 2.11

(
[
25
,
41
]
)
.

Suppose that
\beta=1
, and that we consider the Gaussian invariant model
Y_{\beta,\ell,n}
(hence
\tau=1
).
Let
\ell\in[0,\infty)^{r}
be such that
\ell_{r-1}=\cdots=\ell_{0}=\lambda
for some fixed
\lambda\in[0,1)
.
Let
\mathcal{L}_{n}
denote the output of the likelihood ratio test (LRT) for (
2.10
)
in the
n
-dimensional model,
where
\mathcal{L}_{n}(\mathcal{S}^{\beta,\ell,n})=0
means accepting
\boldsymbol{H}_{0}
and
\mathcal{L}_{n}(\mathcal{S}^{\beta,\ell,n})=1

means rejecting
\boldsymbol{H}_{0}
. Define the error function

(2.11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{n}(\lambda)=\mathbf{P}\big[\mathcal{L}_{n}(\mathcal{S}^{\beta,\ell,n})=1~\big|~\boldsymbol{H}_{0}\text{ is true}\big]+\mathbf{P}\big[\mathcal{L}_{n}(\mathcal{S}^{\beta,\ell,n})=0~\big|~\boldsymbol{H}_{\ell}\text{ is true}\big].

The LRT minimizes (
2.11
) over all
measurable functions of
\mathcal{S}^{\beta,\ell,n}
. Moreover,

(2.12)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{n}(\lambda)=\mathrm{erfc}\left(\tfrac{r}{4}\sqrt{-\log(1-\lambda)}\right)\in(0,1),

where we use
\mathrm{erfc}(z)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{z}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-t^{2}}~\mathrm{d}t
to denote the complementary error function.

Thirdly, we did not find any works in the literature that directly address
Question
2.6
in the critical case
\ell_{r_{\mathrm{c}}-1},\ldots,\ell_{0}=\tau+o(1)
. However,
as pointed out in
[
51
, Appendix A]
, consistent estimators
of
\ell
in this case can be proved to exist by examining the asymptotic power of subcritical tests when

\ell_{i}\to\tau^{-}
(e.g., (
2.12
) goes to zero when
\ell_{i}=\lambda\to 1^{-}
).
Thus, the answer to Question
2.6
is positive in the critical case.
However, to the best of our knowledge, since this conclusion relies on the optimal subcritical estimators,
it follows from Remark
2.10
that

all eigenvalues
are used in the currently-known methods to consistently recover critical signals.

2.2.2.
New Result

Remarks
2.8
and

2.10
highlight a very sharp dichotomy between
the number of eigenvalues in
\mathcal{S}^{\beta,\ell,n}
that are required to optimally infer supercritical and subcritical signals
(respectively, a finite number of top eigenvalues versus
all eigenvalues). Given that critical signals interpolate
between these two extremes, it is thus natural to ask:

Question 2.12
.

In the context of Question
2.6
,
what is the minimal number of
eigenvalues that are needed in order to detect critical signals with probability one?

According to (
2.9
), it is impossible
to detect critical signals using any finite collection of top eigenvalues. In this view, the main
result of this paper implies that critical signals ”close enough” (see (
2.15
))
to the threshold
\tau
can be recovered from the vanishing proportion of top eigenvalues that
contribute to edge scaling limits.
This is based on the following:

Theorem 2.13

(
[
7
,
8
]
; see also
[
2
,
45
,
50
,
59
]
)
.

Let
r\in\mathbb{N}
and
\beta=\{1,2,4\}
be fixed,
let
\gamma
and
p=p(n)
be as in (
2.7
) in the Wishart case, and
suppose that
\ell=\ell(n)\in[0,\infty)^{r}

is either fixed or depends on
n
.
Suppose that for every
1\leq i\leq r
, the limit

(2.13)

\displaystyle w_{i}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\left(1+\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\right)^{-2/3}\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{1/3}\big(\sqrt{\gamma}-\ell_{i-1}\big)&\text{(Wishart)},\\
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{1/3}(1-\ell_{i-1})&\text{(Gaussian invariant)},\end{cases}

exists and is an element of
(-\infty,\infty]
; then let

.
If we define

\mathfrak{s}_{n}=\begin{cases}\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\left(1+\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\right)^{-4/3}n^{2/3}&\text{(Wishart)},\\
n^{2/3}&\text{(Gaussian invariant)},\end{cases}

\mathfrak{e}

(2.14)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathfrak{s}_{n}\big(\mathfrak{e}-\mathcal{S}^{\beta,\ell,n}\big)=\Lambda^{(r,\beta,w)}\qquad\text{in distribution}.

Thanks to (

corresponds to

(2.15)

\displaystyle\ell_{i-1}=\tau+O(n^{-1/3}),

whereas

\ell_{i-1}
being smaller than
\tau
by a difference of greater
order than

. Thus, in the present context, the parameter
r_{0}
introduced in (

) counts the number
of critical signals that are no farther than
O(n^{-1/3})
from the threshold
\tau
. Given that
one would presumably know whether
\beta=1,2,4
if presented with a realization of the random matrices
W_{\beta,\ell,n,p}

or
Y_{\beta,\ell,n}
, Theorem
1.2
then implies that the number of critical
signals counted by
r_{0}
can be determined from the top edge fluctuations of the random matrices:

.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be as in (
1.5
)/(

Under the assumptions of Theorem

,

\mathcal{T}\left(\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathfrak{s}_{n}\big(\mathfrak{e}-\mathcal{S}^{\beta,\ell,n}\big)\right)-1/\beta=r_{0}\qquad\text{almost surely},

Similarly to our result on beta-ensembles, the natural follow-up to Corollary

.

Let
\mathfrak{e}
and

2.3.
Rigidity

2.3.1.
Background

In the last 15 years, a sizeable literature on ”rigidity” properties of
point processes has been developing, starting with pioneering works by
Ghosh and Peres
[
35
,
37
]
.
We refer to
[
18
,
34
]
and references therein for a survey.
In this literature, rigidity refers to the observation that for certain strongly-correlated
point processes, some features of the point configuration inside a bounded set
is almost-surely determined by the configuration of points outside that set.
For instance:

Definition 2.16
.

Let
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a point process.
We say that
\mathcal{X}
is number rigid if for every bounded Borel set
B\subset\mathbb{R}
,
there exists a measurable map
\mathcal{M}_{B}
such that

|\mathcal{X}\cap B|=\mathcal{M}_{B}(\mathcal{X}\cap B^{c})\qquad\text{almost surely}.

(In words, the points outside
B
determine the number of points inside
B
.)
More generally, given any integer
k\geq 0
, we say that
\mathcal{X}
is
k
-moment rigid
if for every bounded Borel set
B\subset\mathbb{R}
,
there exists a measurable map
\mathcal{M}_{B}
such that

\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}\cap B}x^{k}=\mathcal{M}_{B}(\mathcal{X}\cap B^{c})\qquad\text{almost surely}.

(In words, the points outside
B
determine the
k^{\mathrm{th}}
moment of points inside
B
.)

Examples of nontrivial rigid point processes include the following:

Example 2.17
.

The Ginibre point process was shown to be
number rigid in
[
37
]
.

Example 2.18
.

The
\alpha
-Gaussian analytic function
(
\alpha
-GAF) zero point process
(i.e., the zeros of the
random entire function

z\mapsto\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{g_{k}}{(k!)^{\alpha/2}}z^{k}
, where
g_{k}
are i.i.d.
complex Gaussians) were shown to be
k
-moment rigid for all
0\leq k\leq\lfloor 1/\alpha\rfloor
in

[
36
,
37
]
.

Remark 2.19
.

Rigidity was in part introduced
to help distinguish structured point processes that are otherwise difficult to
tell apart. As stated in
[
37
, Page 1794]
:

”
While a simple visual inspection suffices
to (heuristically) distinguish a sample of the Poisson process from that of either the
Ginibre or the GAF zero process (of the same intensity), the latter two are hard to set
apart between themselves. It is therefore an interesting question to devise mathematical statistics that distinguish them.
”

From this point of view, the notion of rigidity becomes especially interesting
once we note that the results outlined in
Examples
2.17
and
2.18

are optimal, and thus serve as a means of distinguishing
the point processes described therein:

(1)

For the Ginibre process, it was shown in
[
37
]
that
the number of points is the only information about the configuration inside a bounded
set
B
that can be obtained from the configuration outside
B
.

(2)

For the
\alpha
-GAF zeros, it was shown in

[
26
,
37
]
that the
k^{\mathrm{th}}
moments
for
0\leq k\leq\lfloor 1/\alpha\rfloor
is the only information about the configuration inside a bounded
set
B
that can be obtained from the configuration outside
B
.

A formal version of these optimality results can be stated using the notion of tolerance; see,
e.g.,
[
36
, Definitions 2 and 3]
.

Going back to our main topic,
the number rigidity of
\Lambda^{\theta}
was proved in a sequence of
three papers. Namely, first for
\theta=(1,2,\infty)
in
[
12
]
, then for
\theta=(1,\beta,w)
with any choice of
\beta>0
and
w\in(-\infty,\infty]
in
[
27
]
,
and finally for all
\theta\in\Theta
in
[
32
]
.

2.3.2.
New Results

Theorem
1.2
refines current results on
\Lambda^{\theta}
’s rigidity
in two directions: On the one hand, while the results of
[
32
,
27
]

proved the existence of a single function
\mathcal{M}
such that

(2.16)

\displaystyle|\Lambda^{\theta}\cap B|=\mathcal{M}(\Lambda^{\theta}\cap B^{c})\qquad\text{almost surely}

for every bounded Borel set
B
,
they did not provide an explicit construction of
\mathcal{M}
. In this view,
Theorem
1.2
improves on the latter
by identifying the exact mechanism that creates number rigidity:
If we restrict the trace in (
1.6
) to the eigenvalues outside of
some bounded set, then the fact that
\mathrm{e}^{-t\Lambda^{\theta}_{k}/2}=1+o(1)
almost surely
as
t\to 0
for all fixed
k\geq 0
implies the following:

Corollary 2.20
.

For every bounded Borel set
B\subset\mathbb{R}
,
it holds that

(2.17)

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 0\text{ s.t. }\Lambda^{\theta}_{k}\in B^{c}}\mathrm{e}^{-t\Lambda^{\theta}_{k}/2}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}t^{-3/2}+\frac{1}{2}\left(r_{0}+\frac{1}{\beta}\right)-\frac{1}{4}-|\Lambda^{\theta}\cap B|+\mathfrak{o}_{t},\qquad t\in(0,1],

where the random remainder terms
\mathfrak{o}_{t}
satisfy (
1.7
) and (
1.8
).
This provides a clear blueprint to construct the function
\mathcal{M}
in (
2.16
) explicitly;
see Remark
3.4
for details.

On the other hand, while
the statement that
\Lambda^{\theta}
is number rigid has intrinsic interest—as it
says something nontrivial about correlations within SAO spectra—it nevertheless falls short
of the ambition for the theory of rigidity laid out in Remark
2.19
:
Given that SAO eigenvalue point processes remain very similar as we vary the parameter
\theta
,
we do not expect that different instances of
\Lambda^{\theta}
satisfy different notions of rigidity, such as the
rigidity/tolerance hierarchy observed for the Ginibre process and the
\alpha
-GAF zeroes in
Examples
2.17
and
2.18
.

Remark 2.21
.

To the best of our knowledge, the optimality of number rigidity for
\Lambda^{\theta}
(i.e., tolerance)
has not been proved.
That said, we expect that such a result could be proved using the technology developed in
[
26
]
.
(The mutual absolute continuity of Palm measures of the Airy process—i.e.,
\Lambda^{(1,2,\infty)}
—with the same number
of fixed components proved in
[
13
]
serves as further evidence of this point.)

In this context, our main result can be viewed as a first step
toward developing statistics that distinguish between different instances of SAO spectra,
which goes beyond the rigidity/tolerance hierarchy:

Corollary 2.22
.

If
\theta=(r,\beta,w)
and
\tilde{\theta}=(\tilde{r},\tilde{\beta},\tilde{w})
are such that
r_{0}+1/\beta\neq\tilde{r}_{0}+1/\tilde{\beta}
,
then
\Lambda^{\theta}
and
\Lambda^{\tilde{\theta}}
are mutually singular.

2.4.
Cancellation of Subcritical Coordinates

2.4.1.
Background

In
[
8
, Page 2734]
, Bloemendal and Virág made the curious
observation that any given spiked Wishart or Gaussian invariant model converges
to an infinite number of SAOs with different parameters: Looking back at
Theorem
2.13
, if we add a trivial component
to the spike
\ell=(\ell_{r-1},\ldots,\ell_{0})
, thus transforming it into
\tilde{\ell}=(0,\ell_{r-1},\ldots,\ell_{0})
, then the matrix model
remains unchanged. However, the parameter
r
in the limit (
2.14
)
increases by one, and the added component has a Dirichlet boundary condition (since
\ell_{r}=0
corresponds
to
w_{r+1}=\infty
in (
2.13
)).
If we combine this observation with the fact that the SAOs are obviously
invariant with respect to permutations of their components (as their noises are induced by GOE,
GUE, and GSE matrices), then we obtain the following from Theorem
2.13
:

Corollary 2.25

(
[
8
]
)
.

Let
\beta\in\{1,2,4\}
, and let
\theta=(r,\beta,w),\tilde{\theta}=(\tilde{r},\beta,\tilde{w})\in\Theta
be such that

(2.18)

\displaystyle r_{0}=\tilde{r}_{0}

(i.e.,
\mathcal{H}_{\theta}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\tilde{\theta}}
have the same number of
Robin boundary conditions) and

(2.19)

\displaystyle\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{r}\}\cap\mathbb{R}=\{\tilde{w}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{w}_{\tilde{r}}\}\cap\mathbb{R}

(i.e., the Robin boundary conditions match, up to permutation).
Then,
\Lambda^{\theta}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{distr.}}}{{=}}\Lambda^{\tilde{\theta}}
.

Given the somewhat indirect nature of the proof of this result, it is natural to ask:

Question 2.26
.

Can one prove Corollary
2.25

using the intrinsic properties of the SAOs only (i.e., without reference to the matrix models that converge to the SAOs)?

2.4.2.
New Result

While our results do not provide a complete answer to Question
2.26
,
they nevertheless shed new light on some of the delicate relationships that must exist between
\mathcal{H}_{\theta}
’s parameters
in order for Corollary
2.25
to hold.
To see this, in what follows we
let
\theta=(r,\beta,w)
with
r\geq 1
,

\beta\in\{1,2,4\}
, and
w\in(-\infty,\infty]^{r}
be fixed,
and we let
r_{0}
be defined as in (
1.4
).
Given
\kappa,\sigma,\upsilon>0
,
define the parameter
\eta=(\kappa,\sigma,\upsilon)
, and define the Schrödinger operator

\widehat{H}_{\theta,\eta}=-\tfrac{1}{2}\tfrac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}+\kappa x+\xi,

which acts on vector-valued
f\in L^{2}\big([0,\infty),\mathbb{F}_{\beta}\big)^{r}
with boundary conditions

\begin{cases}f(i,0)=0&\text{if }w_{i}=\infty\text{ (Dirichlet)},\\
f^{\prime}(i,0)=w_{i}f(i,0)&\text{if }w_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\text{ (Robin)},\end{cases}\qquad 1\leq i\leq r,

and where
\xi
is a
r\times r
matrix white noise whose diagonal components

\xi_{i,i}
have variance
\sigma^{2}
, and whose off-diagonal components
\xi_{i,j}=\xi_{j,i}^{*}
have variance
\upsilon^{2}
.
The operators
\widehat{H}_{\theta,\eta}
can be viewed as a generalization of the SAOs, since if we choose the parameter
\eta

in such a way that
\kappa=\kappa(r)=\frac{r}{2}
,
\sigma^{2}=\frac{1}{\beta}
,
and
\upsilon^{2}=\frac{1}{2}
, then
2\widehat{H}_{\theta,\eta}=\mathcal{H}_{\theta}
.

In this context, our new insight concerning Corollary
2.25

is that the trace asymptotic for
\mathcal{H}_{\theta}
in (
1.6
) is a special case of the more general asymptotic

(2.20)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-t\widehat{H}_{\theta,\eta}}\big]=\tfrac{r}{\sqrt{2\pi}\kappa}t^{-3/2}+\tfrac{1}{4}\left(2r_{0}-r+\tfrac{r\sigma^{2}}{\kappa}+\tfrac{r(r-1)\upsilon^{2}}{\kappa}\right)+\mathfrak{o}_{t}\qquad\text{for all }t\in(0,1],

where
\mathfrak{o}_{t}
satisfy (
1.7
) and (
1.8
).
With this in hand, we can obtain necessary conditions on the parameter
\eta
in order for the
cancellations in Corollary
2.25
to occur.

More specifically, let
\tilde{\theta}=(\tilde{r},\beta,\tilde{w})
be related to
\theta
in such a way that
(
2.18
) and (
2.19
) hold.
Then, consider
\eta=(\kappa,\sigma,\upsilon)
such that, similarly to the SAOs,

(2.21)

\displaystyle\kappa=\kappa(r)\text{ possibly depends on }r,

and conversely for the parameters that determine the noise’s variance,

(2.22)

\displaystyle\sigma\text{ and }\upsilon\text{ are independent of }r.

In order for the operators
\widehat{H}_{\theta,\eta}
and
\widehat{H}_{\tilde{\theta},\eta}
to generate the same eigenvalue
point process,
it is necessary that every term in the asymptotic (
2.20
) be independent of
r

(as this could be different from
\tilde{r}
),
and instead only depend on the common parameter
r_{0}=\tilde{r}_{0}
.
In order for the leading order term in (
2.20
) to be independent of
r
, it is necessary that

\kappa=\kappa(r)=\kappa_{0}r
for some
\kappa_{0}>0
independent of
r
.
If such is the case, then (
2.20
) becomes

(2.23)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-t\widehat{H}_{\theta,\eta}}\big]=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\kappa_{0}}t^{-3/2}+\tfrac{1}{4}\left(2r_{0}-r+\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{\kappa_{0}}+\tfrac{(r-1)\upsilon^{2}}{\kappa_{0}}\right)+\mathfrak{o}_{t}.

Once this is applied, the only
r
-dependence in (
2.23
) lies in the second order term,
via the function
r\mapsto(\upsilon^{2}/\kappa_{0}-1)r
. Since
\upsilon
is assumed independent of
r
, the only way for this to not depend on
r
is to have
\upsilon^{2}=\kappa_{0}
,
in which case (
2.23
) becomes

(2.24)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-t\widehat{H}_{\theta,\eta}}\big]=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\kappa_{0}}t^{-3/2}+\tfrac{1}{4}\left(2r_{0}-1+\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{\kappa_{0}}\right)+\mathfrak{o}_{t}.

We thus obtain the following result:

Corollary 2.27
.

Suppose that
\theta
and
\tilde{\theta}
satisfy (
2.18
) and (
2.19
),
and that
\eta
satisfies (
2.21
) and (
2.22
).
If
\widehat{H}_{\theta,\eta}
and
\widehat{H}_{\tilde{\theta},\eta}
generate the same eigenvalue
point process, then
\kappa(r)=r\upsilon^{2}
. In particular, if
\upsilon^{2}=\frac{1}{2}
(as is the case for the SAOs),
then the cancellation in Corollary
2.25
can only occur if
\kappa(r)=r/2
.

2.4.3.
Open Problem

Based on this partial result, we expect that a more detailed study of the joint moments of
\mathrm{Tr}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathcal{H}_{\theta}/2}\big]

(over multiple
t
’s) could be used to completely answer Question
2.26
.
As this lies outside the scope of the main applications of this paper, we leave this question open
for future investigations.

3.
Trace Asymptotics

We now embark on the proof of our trace
asymptotic. The most general incarnation
of the asymptotic is (
2.20
).
For this purpose, we
recall that,
given
\theta=(r,\beta,w)\in\Theta
and
\eta=(\kappa,\sigma,\upsilon)\in(0,\infty)^{3}
,
we denote the operator

\widehat{H}_{\theta,\eta}=-\tfrac{1}{2}\tfrac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}+\kappa x+\xi,

which acts on vector-valued
f\in L^{2}\big([0,\infty),\mathbb{F}_{\beta}\big)^{r}
with boundary conditions

\begin{cases}f(i,0)=0&\text{if }w_{i}=\infty\text{ (Dirichlet)},\\
f^{\prime}(i,0)=w_{i}f(i,0)&\text{if }w_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\text{ (Robin)},\end{cases}\qquad 1\leq i\leq r,

and where
\xi
is a
r\times r
matrix white noise whose diagonal components

\xi_{i,i}
have variance
\sigma^{2}
, and whose off-diagonal components
\xi_{i,j}=\xi_{j,i}^{*}
have variance
\upsilon^{2}
.
The statement in (
2.20
)
can be split into two claims:

Theorem 3.1
.

For every

\theta\in\Theta
and
\eta\in(0,\infty)^{3}
, one has

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\mathrm{Tr}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-t\widehat{H}_{\theta,\eta}}\big]\right]=\tfrac{r}{\sqrt{2\pi}\kappa}t^{-3/2}+\tfrac{1}{4}\left(2r_{0}-r+\tfrac{r\sigma^{2}}{\kappa}+\tfrac{r(r-1)\upsilon^{2}}{\kappa}\right)+o(1)\qquad\text{as }t\to 0^{+},

and there exists a constant
C>0
such that

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Cov}\left[\mathrm{Tr}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-s\widehat{H}_{\theta,\eta}}\big],\mathrm{Tr}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-t\widehat{H}_{\theta,\eta}}\big]\right]\leq C\left(\frac{\min\{s,t\}}{\max\{s,t\}}\right)^{1/4}\qquad\text{ for every }s,t\in(0,1].

The covariance bound in Theorem
3.1
was proved in
[
32
, (8.4)]
.
Consequently, we only need to prove (
3.1
).
The remainder of this section is organized as follows:
Before we prove (
3.1
),
in Section
3.1
we show how Theorem
3.1
is used to
construct the function
\mathcal{T}
in (
1.5
), and thus prove Theorem
1.2
.
Then, in Section
3.2
,
we state a Feynman-Kac formula proved in
[
32
]
for the expected trace
\mathbf{E}\left[\mathrm{Tr}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-t\widehat{H}_{\theta,\eta}}\big]\right]
.
Finally, in Sections
3.3
and
3.4
,
we use the Feynman-Kac formula in question to prove the asymptotics in (
3.1
).

3.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

We begin by defining
\mathcal{T}
:

Definition 3.2
.

Let
c_{1},c_{2}>0
be any fixed constants.
Define the vanishing sequence of positive numbers
1\geq t_{1}>t_{2}>\cdots>0
as

(3.3)

\displaystyle t_{n}=\mathrm{e}^{-c_{1}n},\qquad n\geq 1,

and let
1\leq N_{1}<N_{2}<\cdots
be the sequence of integers

(3.4)

\displaystyle N_{m}=\lceil m^{1+c_{2}}\rceil,\qquad m\geq 1.

Given any point configuration
\mathcal{X}=\{x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},\ldots\}
on
\mathbb{R}
, we define

(3.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{X})=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}+\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{2}{N_{m}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{m}}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-t_{n}x_{k}/2}-\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\pi}}t_{n}^{-3/2}\right)&\text{if the limit exists,}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Remark 3.3
.

There is some degree of arbitrariness in how
t_{n}
and
N_{m}
were chosen in (
3.3
)
and (
3.4
). As will be made clear by the proof in this section, any sequence of
t_{n}
’s
and
N_{m}
’s that respectively vanish and diverge ”fast enough” suffice to obtain (
1.5
).
Our specific choices in (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) are made for simplicity
and to emphasize that it is possible to be completely explicit in our definition of the map
\mathcal{T}
.

Recall that if
\eta=(\frac{r}{2},\frac{1}{\beta},\frac{1}{2})
, then
2\widehat{H}_{\theta,\eta}=\mathcal{H}_{\theta}
. Thus,
(
3.1
) implies that

\mathbf{E}\left[\mathrm{Tr}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathcal{H}_{\theta}/2}\big]\right]=\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\pi}}t^{-3/2}+\tfrac{r_{0}+1/\beta}{2}-\tfrac{1}{4}+o(1)\qquad\text{as }t\to 0^{+}.

By a trivial rearrangement of the terms in (
3.5
),
we therefore obtain that
\mathcal{T}(\Lambda^{\theta})=r_{0}+1/\beta
almost surely if we can prove that

\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{1}{N_{m}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{m}}\left(\mathrm{Tr}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-t_{n}\mathcal{H}_{\theta}/2}\big]-\mathbf{E}\left[\mathrm{Tr}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-t_{n}\mathcal{H}_{\theta}/2}\big]\right]\right)=0\qquad\text{almost surely.}

Toward this end, by Markov’s inequality, we get that for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[\left|\frac{1}{N_{m}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{m}}\left(\mathrm{Tr}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-t_{n}\mathcal{H}_{\theta}/2}\big]-\mathbf{E}\left[\mathrm{Tr}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-t_{n}\mathcal{H}_{\theta}/2}\big]\right]\right)\right|\geq m^{-\varepsilon/2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle m^{\varepsilon}~\mathbf{E}\left[\frac{1}{N_{m}^{2}}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N_{m}}\mathrm{Tr}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-t_{n}\mathcal{H}_{\theta}/2}\big]-\mathbf{E}\left[\mathrm{Tr}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-t_{n}\mathcal{H}_{\theta}/2}\big]\right]\right)^{2}\right]

(3.6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{m^{\epsilon}}{N_{m}^{2}}\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}=1}^{N_{m}}\mathbf{Cov}\left[\mathrm{Tr}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-t_{n_{1}}\mathcal{H}_{\theta}/2}\big],\mathrm{Tr}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-t_{n_{2}}\mathcal{H}_{\theta}/2}\big]\right].

By the Borel-Cantelli lemma, it suffices to show that (
3.6
) is summable in
m
.

By (
3.2
), there is a constant
C>0
independent of
N
such that

\frac{m^{\epsilon}}{N_{m}^{2}}\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}=1}^{N_{m}}\mathbf{Cov}\left[\mathrm{Tr}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-t_{n_{1}}\mathcal{H}_{\theta}/2}\big],\mathrm{Tr}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-t_{n_{2}}\mathcal{H}_{\theta}/2}\big]\right]\leq\frac{Cm^{\varepsilon}}{N_{m}^{2}}\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}=1}^{N_{m}}\left(\frac{\min\{t_{n_{1}},t_{n_{2}}\}}{\max\{t_{n_{1}},t_{n_{2}}\}}\right)^{1/4}.

With
t_{n}
defined as in (
3.3
), this simplifies to

\frac{Cm^{\varepsilon}}{N_{m}^{2}}\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}=1}^{N_{m}}\left(\frac{\min\{t_{n_{1}},t_{n_{2}}\}}{\max\{t_{n_{1}},t_{n_{2}}\}}\right)^{1/4}=\frac{Cm^{\varepsilon}}{N_{m}^{2}}\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}=1}^{N_{m}}\mathrm{e}^{-c_{1}|n_{1}-n_{2}|/4}.

We can split the above sum as follows:

\frac{Cm^{\varepsilon}}{N_{m}^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq n_{1},n_{2}\leq N_{m}\\
|n_{1}-n_{2}|\leq(4/c_{1})\log N_{m}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{e}^{-c_{1}|n_{1}-n_{2}|/4}+\frac{Cm^{\varepsilon}}{N_{m}^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq n_{1},n_{2}\leq N_{m}\\
|n_{1}-n_{2}|>(4/c_{1})\log N_{m}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{e}^{-c_{1}|n_{1}-n_{2}|/4}.

Given that
m^{1+c_{2}}\leq N_{m}\leq m^{1+c_{2}}+1
(as per (
3.4
)) and
\mathrm{e}^{-c_{1}|n_{1}-n_{2}|/4}\leq 1
, we get

\frac{Cm^{\varepsilon}}{N_{m}^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq n_{1},n_{2}\leq N_{m}\\
|n_{1}-n_{2}|\leq(4/c_{1})\log N_{m}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{e}^{-c_{1}|n_{1}-n_{2}|/4}=O\left(\frac{m^{\varepsilon}}{N_{m}^{2}}\cdot N_{m}\log N_{m}\right)=O(m^{-1-(c_{2}-\varepsilon)}\log m)

as
m\to\infty
.
Moreover,

\frac{Cm^{\varepsilon}}{N_{m}^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq n_{1},n_{2}\leq N_{m}\\
|n_{1}-n_{2}|>(4/c_{1})\log N_{m}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{e}^{-c_{1}|n_{1}-n_{2}|/4}\\
\leq\frac{Cm^{\varepsilon}\mathrm{e}^{-\log N_{m}}}{N_{m}^{2}}\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}=1}^{N_{m}}1=O(m^{\varepsilon}N_{m}^{-1})=O(m^{-1-(c_{2}-\varepsilon)})

as
m\to\infty
.
If we choose
\varepsilon<c_{2}
, then this implies that (
3.6
) is summable,
which concludes the proof that
\mathcal{T}(\Lambda^{\theta})=r_{0}+1/\beta
almost surely.

Remark 3.4
.

Following-up on the calculations performed in this section, we see that

\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{1}{N_{m}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{m}}\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\pi}}t_{n}^{-3/2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\left(r_{0}+\tfrac{1}{\beta}\right)-\tfrac{1}{4}-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-t_{n}\Lambda^{\theta}_{k}/2}\right)=0\qquad\text{almost surely},

where
t_{n}
and
N_{m}
are defined as in (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) respectively.
Moreover, for any
k\geq 0
, one has

\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{1}{N_{m}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{m}}\mathrm{e}^{-t_{n}\Lambda^{\theta}_{k}/2}=1\qquad\text{almost surely.}

Consequently,
if we define the map

\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{X})=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{1}{N_{m}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{m}}\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\pi}}t_{n}^{-3/2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\left(r_{0}+\tfrac{1}{\beta}\right)-\tfrac{1}{4}-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-t_{n}x_{k}/2}\right)&\text{if the limit exists,}\\
0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

for any point configuration
\mathcal{X}=\{x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},\ldots\}\subset\mathbb{R}
,
then (
2.16
) holds. Indeed, for any bounded set
B\subset\mathbb{R}
,
we can write

(3.7)

\mathcal{M}(\Lambda^{\theta}\cap B^{c})=\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{1}{N_{m}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{m}}\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\pi}}t_{n}^{-3/2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\left(r_{0}+\tfrac{1}{\beta}\right)-\tfrac{1}{4}-\sum_{k:\Lambda^{\theta}_{k}\in B^{c}}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-t_{n}\Lambda^{\theta}_{k}/2}\right)\\
=\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{1}{N_{m}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{m}}\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\pi}}t_{n}^{-3/2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\left(r_{0}+\tfrac{1}{\beta}\right)-\tfrac{1}{4}-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-t_{n}\Lambda^{\theta}_{k}/2}\right)+\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{1}{N_{m}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{m}}\sum_{k:\Lambda^{\theta}_{k}\in B}\mathrm{e}^{-t_{n}\Lambda^{\theta}_{k}/2};

the first limit on the second line of (
3.7
) is almost-surely zero,
and the second is almost-surely
|\Lambda^{\theta}\cap B|
.

3.2.
Proof of (
3.1
) Step 1. Expected Trace Formula

The first step in our proof of (
3.1
) is to
state the Feynman-Kac formula for
\mathbf{E}\left[\mathrm{Tr}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-t\widehat{H}_{\theta,\eta}}\big]\right]

obtained in
[
32
, Theorem 4.9]
, which is what we will use to calculate
the leading orders of the expected trace.
This is stated as Lemma
3.13
below.
Due to the complexity of the formula in question,
we must set up a sizeable amount of notation to state it. First,
we introduce the main stochastic process that drives
the Feynman-Kac formula, which is the reflected Brownian bridge and its local times:

Definition 3.5
.

Let
B
be a standard Brownian motion on
\mathbb{R}
,
and denote
B
’s transition kernel by

\Pi_{B}(t;x,y)=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-(x-y)^{2}/2t}}{\sqrt{2\pi t}},\qquad t>0,~x,y\in\mathbb{R}.

We denote

B^{x}=(B|B(0)=x)
and
B^{x,y}_{t}=(B|B(0)=x\text{ and }B(t)=y)
.
Then, let
X=|B|
be a reflected standard Brownian motion on
[0,\infty)
,
and denote
X
’s transition kernel by

\Pi_{X}(t;x,y)=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-(x-y)^{2}/2t}}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}+\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-(x+y)^{2}/2t}}{\sqrt{2\pi t}},\qquad t>0,~x,y\geq 0.

We similarly denote

X^{x}=(X|X(0)=x)
and
X^{x,y}_{t}=(X|X(0)=x\text{ and }X(t)=y)
.

Definition 3.6
.

Fix
t>0
,
and let
Z
be either
B
,
X
, or one of its conditioned version (i.e.,
B^{x}
,
X^{x}
,
B^{x,y}_{t}
, or
X^{x,y}_{t}
).
Given
0\leq u<v\leq t
,
we let
\mathfrak{L}_{[u,v)}^{0}(Z)
denote
Z
’s boundary local time collected on
the time interval
[u,v)
, that is,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{[u,v)}(Z)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\int_{u}^{v}\mathbf{1}_{\{-\varepsilon<Z(s)<\varepsilon\}}~\mathrm{d}s\qquad\text{almost surely}.

Moreover, we let
\big(L^{y}_{[u,v)}(Z):y\in[0,\infty)\big)
denote the continuous version of

Z
’s local time process on
[u,v)
, that is the process such that

\int_{u}^{v}f\big(Z(s)\big)~\mathrm{d}s=\int_{0}^{\infty}L^{y}_{[u,v)}(Z)f(y)~\mathrm{d}y\qquad\text{for every measurable }f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}.

Finally, we use the shorthands
\mathfrak{L}_{t}^{0}(Z)=\mathfrak{L}_{[0,t)}^{0}(Z)

and
L_{t}^{y}(Z)=L_{[0,t)}^{y}(Z)
.

If we were only dealing with scalar-valued Schrödinger operators on
[0,\infty)
,
then these would be the only processes that are needed to state the Feynman-Kac
formula. However, since we are dealing with the vector-valued setting, we need
more tools. First, we set up some notation to describe the function spaces related
to
\widehat{H}_{\theta,\eta}
:

Definition 3.7
.

Given
f,g:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{F}_{\beta}
, we denote

\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)g(x)~\mathrm{d}x\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|f\|_{2}=\sqrt{\langle f,f\rangle}.

We denote
\mathcal{A}=\{1,\ldots,r\}\times[0,\infty)
, and we let
\mu
denote
the measure on
\mathcal{A}
obtained by taking the product of the counting measure
on
\{1,\ldots,r\}
and the Lebesgue measure on
[0,\infty)
.
Given
f:\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{F}_{\beta}
, we denote

\|f\|_{\mu}=\sqrt{\int_{\mathcal{A}}|f(a)|^{2}~\mathrm{d}\mu(a)}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{r}\|f(i,\cdot)\|_{2}^{2}}.

Next, the vector-valued setting requires the introduction of a jump process
on
\{1,\ldots,r\}
which depends on
X
’s self-intersections as follows:

Definition 3.8
.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{0}=\varnothing
be the empty set, and
given an even integer
n\geq 2
, let
\mathcal{P}_{n}
denote the set of perfect pair matchings
of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
. That is,
\mathcal{P}_{n}
is the set

\Big\{\big\{\{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\},\{\ell_{3},\ell_{4}\},\ldots,\{\ell_{n-1},\ell_{n}\}\big\}:\ell_{i}\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\text{ and }\{\ell_{i},\ell_{i+1}\}\cap\{\ell_{j},\ell_{j+1}\}=\varnothing\text{ if }j\neq i\Big\}.

Definition 3.9
.

Let
t>0
and
x\geq 0
, let
n\geq 2
be an even integer, and let
q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
.
Given a realization of
X^{x,x}_{t}
, we let
\mathrm{si}_{t,n,q,X^{x,x}_{t}}
denote
the unique random Borel probability measure on
[0,t)^{n}
such that for every

s_{1},t_{1},\ldots,s_{n},t_{n}\in[0,t)
with
s_{k}<t_{k}
for
k=1,\ldots,n
, one has

(3.8)

\mathrm{si}_{t,n,q,X^{x,x}_{t}}\big([s_{1},t_{1})\times[s_{2},t_{2})\times\cdots\times[s_{n},t_{n})\big)\\
=\frac{1}{\|L_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\|_{2}^{2n}}\prod_{\{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\}\in q}\left\langle L_{[s_{\ell_{1}},t_{\ell_{1}})}(X^{x,x}_{t}),L_{[s_{\ell_{2}},t_{\ell_{2}})}(X^{x,x}_{t})\right\rangle

(this uniquely determines the measure since product rectangles are a determining class
for the Borel
\sigma
-algebra).

Definition 3.10
.

Let
t>0
and
x\geq 0
be fixed,
and suppose that we are given a realization of
X^{x,x}_{t}
.
For any
1\leq i\leq r
, we generate the jump process
\widehat{U}^{i}_{t}:[0,t]\to\{1,\ldots,r\}

using the following step-by-step procedure:

\bullet

Given
X^{x,x}_{t}
, sample
\widehat{N}(t)
according to the distribution

(3.9)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\big[\widehat{N}(t)=2m\big]=\left(\frac{(r-1)^{2}\|L_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\|^{2}_{2}}{2}\right)^{m}\frac{1}{m!}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{(r-1)^{2}}{2}\|L_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}},\qquad m=0,1,2,\ldots,

i.e.,
\widehat{N}(t)/2
is Poisson with parameter
\frac{(r-1)^{2}}{2}\|L_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}
.

\bullet

Given
\widehat{N}(t)
, sample
\widehat{q}
uniformly at random in
\mathcal{P}_{\widehat{N}(t)}
.

\bullet

Given
X^{x,x}_{t}
,
\widehat{N}(t)
, and
\widehat{q}
,
draw
\boldsymbol{T}=(T_{1},\ldots,T_{\widehat{N}(t)})\in[0,t]^{\widehat{N}(t)}

according to the self-intersection measure
\mathrm{si}_{t,\widehat{N}(t),\widehat{q},X^{x,x}_{t}}
. Then,
we let
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}=(\widehat{\tau}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\tau}_{\widehat{N}(t)})
be the tuple
\boldsymbol{T}
rearranged in increasing order.

\bullet

Given
\widehat{N}(t)
,
\boldsymbol{T}
, and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
, we let
\widehat{\pi}
be the random permutation
of
\{1,\ldots,\widehat{N}(t)\}

such that
\widehat{\tau}_{\widehat{\pi}(\ell)}=T_{\ell}
for all
\ell\leq\widehat{N}(t)
, and we let
\widehat{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{\widehat{N}(t)}

be the random perfect matching such that
\{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\}\in\widehat{q}
if and only if

\{\widehat{\pi}(\ell_{1}),\widehat{\pi}(\ell_{2})\}\in\widehat{p}
. In words,
\widehat{p}
ensures that each pair of times
T_{k}

that were matched by
\widehat{q}
are still matched once arranged in nondecreasing order
\widehat{\tau}_{k}
.

\bullet

Let
M^{i}=(M^{i}_{0},M^{i}_{1},M^{i}_{2},\ldots)
be a uniform random walk on
the complete graph (without self-edges) on
\{1,\ldots,r\}
with starting point
M^{i}_{0}=i
, i.e.,

(3.10)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\big[M^{i}_{k}=i_{2}~\big|~M^{i}_{k-1}=i_{1}\big]=\frac{\mathbf{1}_{\{i_{2}\neq i_{1}\}}}{r-1},\qquad k\geq 1,~i_{1},i_{2}\in\{1,\ldots,r\}.

We assume
M^{i}

is independent of
X^{x,x}_{t}
,
\widehat{N}(t)
, and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
.

\bullet

Combining all of the above, we then let

\big(\widehat{U}^{i}_{t}(s):1\leq s\leq t\big)
be the càdlàg
path with jump times
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}

and jumps
M^{i}
:

(3.11)

\displaystyle\widehat{U}^{i}_{t}(s)=i\mathbf{1}_{[0,\widehat{\tau}_{1})}(s)+\sum_{k=1}^{\widehat{N}(t)-1}M^{i}_{k}\mathbf{1}_{[\widehat{\tau}_{k},\widehat{\tau}_{k+1})}(s)+M_{\widehat{N}(t)}\mathbf{1}_{[\widehat{\tau}_{\widehat{N}(t)},t]}(s),\qquad 0\leq s\leq t.

In particular, if
\widehat{N}(t)=0
, then
\widehat{U}^{i}_{t}(t)=i
is constant.

Then, we define local times of the combined processes
(\widehat{U}^{i}_{t},X^{x,x}_{t})
as follows:
For any
t>0
and
(j,y)\in\mathcal{A}=\{1,\ldots,r\}\times[0,\infty)
, we let

(3.12)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}^{(j,0)}_{t}(\widehat{U}^{i}_{t},X^{x,x}_{t})=\sum_{k\geq 1:~U^{i}_{t}(\widehat{\tau}_{k-1})=j}\mathfrak{L}_{[\widehat{\tau}_{k-1},\widehat{\tau}_{k})\cap[0,t]}^{0}(X^{x,x}_{t})

and

(3.13)

\displaystyle L^{(j,y)}_{t}(\widehat{U}^{i}_{t},X^{x,x}_{t})=\sum_{k\geq 1:~U^{i}_{t}(\widehat{\tau}_{k-1})=j}L_{[\widehat{\tau}_{k-1},\widehat{\tau}_{k})\cap[0,t]}^{y}(X^{x,x}_{t})

respectively denote
X^{x,x}_{t}
’s boundary local time at zero and local time
collected on the intervals of
[0,t]
where the jump process
\widehat{U}^{i}_{t}
is equal to
j
.
We note that since
[u,v)\mapsto\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{[u,v)}(X^{x,x}_{t})
and
[u,v)\mapsto L^{y}_{[u,v)}(X^{x,x}_{t})

induce measures on
[0,\infty)
, it is clear that

(3.14)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{r}\mathfrak{L}^{(j,0)}_{t}(\widehat{U}^{i}_{t},X^{x,x}_{t})=\mathfrak{L}_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{j=1}^{r}L^{(j,y)}_{t}(\widehat{U}^{i}_{t},X^{x,x}_{t})=L^{y}_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t}).

Finally, the computation of the moment
\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathrm{Tr}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-t\widehat{H}_{\theta,\eta}}\big]\Big]

requires introducing the following combinatorial constant:

Definition 3.11
.

Given
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let

\mathcal{B}_{n}
denote the set of binary sequences with
n
steps,
namely, sequences of the form
m=(m_{0},m_{1},\ldots,m_{n})\in\{0,1\}^{n+1}
.
Given
h,l\in\{0,1\}
, we let
\mathcal{B}^{h,l}_{n}
denote the set of
sequences
m\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
such that
m_{0}=h
and
m_{n}=l
.

Definition 3.12
.

Let
t>0
and
1\leq i\leq r
be fixed.
Suppose we are given a realization of
\widehat{N}(t)\geq 2
, the matching
\widehat{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{\widehat{N}(t)}
,
and the process
\widehat{U}^{i}_{t}

in Definition
3.10
.
Letting
M^{i}=(M^{i}_{0},M^{i}_{1},\ldots)
be as in Definition
3.10

(i.e., the process that determines the steps taken by
\widehat{U}^{i}_{t}

in
\{1,\ldots,r\}
), we denote the jumps

J_{k}=\big(M^{i}_{k-1},M^{i}_{k}\big),\qquad k\geq 1.

Then, we let
J_{k}^{*}=\big(M^{i}_{k},M^{i}_{k-1}\big)
denote these same jumps in reverse order.
We define the combinatorial constant
\mathfrak{C}_{t}(\widehat{p},\widehat{U}^{i}_{t})
as follows:

(a)

If
\mathbb{F}_{\beta}=\mathbb{R}
(i.e.,
\beta=1
), then

\mathfrak{C}_{t}(\widehat{p},\widehat{U}^{i}_{t})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if either $J_{\ell_{1}}=J_{\ell_{2}}$ or $J_{\ell_{1}}=J_{\ell_{2}}^{*}$ for all $\{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\}\in\widehat{p}$},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(b)

If
\mathbb{F}_{\beta}=\mathbb{C}
(i.e.,
\beta=2
), then

\mathfrak{C}_{t}(\widehat{p},\widehat{U}^{i}_{t})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $J_{\ell_{1}}=J_{\ell_{2}}^{*}$ for all $\{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\}\in\widehat{p}$},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(c)

If
\mathbb{F}_{\beta}=\mathbb{H}
(i.e.,
\beta=4
), then

\mathfrak{C}_{t}(\widehat{p},\widehat{U}^{i}_{t})=\begin{cases}\mathfrak{D}_{t}(\widehat{p},\widehat{U}^{i}_{t})&\text{if either $J_{\ell_{1}}=J_{\ell_{2}}$ or $J_{\ell_{1}}=J_{\ell_{2}}^{*}$ for all $\{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\}\in\widehat{p}$},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Here, we define
\mathfrak{D}_{t}(\widehat{p},\widehat{U}^{i}_{t})
as follows: A binary sequence
m\in\mathcal{B}^{0,0}_{N(t)}

is said to respect
(\widehat{p},J)
if for every
pair
\{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\}\in\widehat{p}
, the following holds:

(c.1)

if
J_{\ell_{1}}=J_{\ell_{2}}
, then
\big((m_{\ell_{1}-1},m_{\ell_{1}}),(m_{\ell_{2}-1},m_{\ell_{2}})\big)

are equal to one of the following pairs:
\big((0,0),(1,1)\big)
,
\big((1,1),(0,0)\big)
,

\big((0,1),(1,0)\big)
, or
\big((1,0),(0,1)\big)
.

(c.2)

if
J_{\ell_{1}}=J_{\ell_{2}}^{*}
, then
\big((m_{\ell_{1}-1},m_{\ell_{1}}),(m_{\ell_{2}-1},m_{\ell_{2}})\big)

are equal to one of the following pairs:
\big((0,0),(0,0)\big)
,
\big((1,1),(1,1)\big)
,

\big((0,1),(1,0)\big)
, or
\big((1,0),(0,1)\big)
.

Lastly, we call any pair
\{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\}\in p
such that
J_{\ell_{1}}=J_{\ell_{2}}

and

\big((m_{\ell_{1}-1},m_{\ell_{1}}),(m_{\ell_{2}-1},m_{\ell_{2}})\big)=\big((0,1),(1,0)\big)\text{ or }\big((1,0),(0,1)\big)

a flip, and we let
f(m,\widehat{p},J)
denote the total number of flips that occur
in the combination of
m,\widehat{p},
and
J
.
With all this in hand, we finally define

(3.15)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}_{t}(\widehat{p},\widehat{U}^{i}_{t})=2^{-\widehat{N}(t)/2}\sum_{m\in\mathcal{B}^{0,0}_{N(t)},~m\text{ respects }(\widehat{p},J)}(-1)^{f(m,\widehat{p},J)}.

We are now finally in a position to state our Feynman-Kac formula for
the trace expectation, which is a special case of
[
32
, Theorem 4.9]
:

Lemma 3.13
.

For every
t>0
,
x\geq 0
, and
1\leq i\leq r
, define

(3.16)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}_{t}(\widehat{p},\widehat{U}^{i}_{t})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\widehat{N}(t)=0,\\
\upsilon^{\widehat{N}(t)}\mathfrak{C}_{t}(\widehat{p},\widehat{U}^{i}_{t})&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

and

(3.17)

\mathfrak{H}_{t}(\widehat{U}^{i}_{t},X^{x,x}_{t})=-\kappa\int_{0}^{t}X^{x,x}_{t}(s)~\mathrm{d}s\\
+\frac{(r-1)^{2}}{2}\|L_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\|L_{t}(\widehat{U}^{i}_{t},X^{x,x}_{t})\|_{\mu}^{2}-\sum_{j=1}^{r}w_{j}\mathfrak{L}^{(j,0)}_{t}(\widehat{U}^{i}_{t},X^{x,x}_{t}).

For every
t>0
, one has

(3.18)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\mathrm{Tr}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-t\widehat{H}_{\theta,\eta}}\big]\right]=\int_{\mathcal{A}}\Pi_{X}(t;x,x)\mathbf{E}\left[\mathfrak{M}_{t}(\widehat{p},\widehat{U}^{i}_{t})\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{H}_{t}(\widehat{U}^{i}_{t},X^{x,x}_{t})}\mathbf{1}_{\{\widehat{U}^{i}_{t}(t)=i\}}\right]~\mathrm{d}\mu(i,x).

Remark 3.14
.

Theorem 4.9 in
[
32
]
provides a formula for any joint moment

for general vector-valued Schrödinger operators

and

, then considering what is called ”Case 2”
in

]
(i.e., an operator on the half-line), and then taking the potential function

.

Remark 3.15
.

The case where

3.3.
Proof of (
3.1
) Step 2. Simplifications

While (
3.18
) provides an exact formula
for the expected trace, it is quite difficult to parse due to its complexity.
Thus, our goal in this section
is to reformulate (
3.18
) in a way that is
amenable to computation, at least for the purposes of extracting the first- and second-order asymptotics.

For this purpose, we observe that most
of the complexity in (
3.18
) comes from the contribution of the path
\widehat{U}^{i}_{t}

to the combined local times (
3.12
)
and (
3.13
) and the combinatorics of
\mathfrak{C}_{t}(\widehat{p},\widehat{U}^{i}_{t})
.
However, these terms are only difficult to understand when
\widehat{U}^{i}_{t}
’s path has numerous jumps.
In this context,
our ability to calculate
the asymptotic (
3.1
) despite the complexity of
(
3.18
)
comes from the
fact that the leading order terms in the latter
only involve realizations of
\widehat{U}^{i}_{t}
that have no jump or two jumps, thus simplifying the analysis.

In fact, the unifying theme of the simplifications performed
in this section (as evidenced by Proposition
3.16
below) is that
the exact calculation of leading terms and the estimate of remainder
terms in (
3.18
) can both be reduced to the small-time asymptotics of
three well-understood functionals of
X^{x,x}_{t}
(neither or which involve
\widehat{U}^{i}_{t}
),
namely,
\int_{0}^{t}X^{x,x}_{t}(s)~\mathrm{d}s
,
\|L_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\|_{2}
, and
\mathfrak{L}_{t}^{0}(X^{x,x}_{t})
. We postpone
the proofs of these asymptotics to Section
3.4
.

To begin making this precise, we note that
we can use (
3.18
) to split

(3.19)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\mathrm{Tr}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-t\widehat{H}_{\theta,\eta}}\big]\right]=T_{0}(t)+T_{2}(t)+T_{\geq 4}(t)

into a sum of expectations with
an indicator restricting the value of
\widehat{N}(t)
as follows:

(3.20)

\displaystyle T_{0}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{A}}\Pi_{X}(t;x,x)\mathbf{E}\left[\mathfrak{M}_{t}(\widehat{p},\widehat{U}^{i}_{t})\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{H}_{t}(\widehat{U}^{i}_{t},X^{x,x}_{t})}\mathbf{1}_{\{\widehat{U}^{i}_{t}(t)=i\}\cap\{\widehat{N}(t)=0\}}\right]~\mathrm{d}\mu(i,x),

(3.21)

\displaystyle T_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{A}}\Pi_{X}(t;x,x)\mathbf{E}\left[\mathfrak{M}_{t}(\widehat{p},\widehat{U}^{i}_{t})\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{H}_{t}(\widehat{U}^{i}_{t},X^{x,x}_{t})}\mathbf{1}_{\{\widehat{U}^{i}_{t}(t)=i\}\cap\{\widehat{N}(t)=2\}}\right]~\mathrm{d}\mu(i,x),

(3.22)

\displaystyle T_{\geq 4}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{A}}\Pi_{X}(t;x,x)\mathbf{E}\left[\mathfrak{M}_{t}(\widehat{p},\widehat{U}^{i}_{t})\mathrm{e}^{\mathfrak{H}_{t}(\widehat{U}^{i}_{t},X^{x,x}_{t})}\mathbf{1}_{\{\widehat{U}^{i}_{t}(t)=i\}\cap\{\widehat{N}(t)\geq 4\}}\right]~\mathrm{d}\mu(i,x).

We discuss the contribution
of each of these three terms to the
t\to 0^{+}
asymptotic in Sections

–

,
and then summarize our findings in Section

3.3.1.
The Constant Path

We begin by simplifying
T_{0}(t)
in (

the easiest to deal with, since if
\widehat{N}(t)=0
, then
\widehat{U}^{i}_{t}=i
is constant on

Therefore, we get the following three immediate simplifications when
\widehat{N}(t)=0

(1)

\mathfrak{M}_{t}(\widehat{p},\widehat{U}^{i}_{t})=1
by definition in (
3.16
).

(2)

Recalling (
3.12
) and (

\mathfrak{L}^{(j,0)}_{t}(\widehat{U}^{i}_{t},X^{x,x}_{t})=\begin{cases}\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})&\text{if }j=i,\\

\mathbf{1}_{\{\widehat{U}^{i}_{t}(t)=i\}}=1

\displaystyle T_{0}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Pi_{X}(t;x,x)\mathbf{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa\int_{0}^{t}X^{x,x}_{t}(s)~\mathrm{d}s+\frac{(r-1)^{2}+\sigma^{2}}{2}\|L_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}-w_{i}\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})}\mathbf{1}_{\{\widehat{N}(t)=0\}}\right]~\mathrm{d}x.

\displaystyle T_{0}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Pi_{X}(t;x,x)\mathbf{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa\int_{0}^{t}X^{x,x}_{t}(s)~\mathrm{d}s+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\|L_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}-w_{i}\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})}\right]~\mathrm{d}x.

Two Jumps

We now simplify

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\big[\big\{\widehat{U}^{i}_{t}(t)=i\big\}\cap\big\{\widehat{N}(t)=2\big\}~\big|~X^{x,x}_{t}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}\big[\big\{M^{i}_{2}=i\big\}\cap\big\{\widehat{N}(t)=2\big\}~\big|~X^{x,x}_{t}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}\big[M^{i}_{2}=i\big]\mathbf{P}\big[\widehat{N}(t)=2~\big|~X^{x,x}_{t}\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(r-1)}\cdot\frac{(r-1)^{2}\|L_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\|^{2}_{2}}{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{(r-1)^{2}}{2}\|L_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(r-1)\|L_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\|^{2}_{2}}{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{(r-1)^{2}}{2}\|L_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}}.

Thus, if we denote the two-jump random walk bridge

\tilde{U}^{i,i}_{t}=\Big(\widehat{U}^{i}_{t}~\Big|~\widehat{U}^{i}_{t}(t)=i,~\widehat{N}(t)=2\Big),

then an application of the tower property (conditional on
X^{x,x}_{t}
)
in (
3.21
) together with (
3.39
) yields

(3.40)

T_{2}(t)=\frac{(r-1)\upsilon^{2}}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Pi_{X}(t;x,x)\mathbf{E}\Bigg[\|L_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\|^{2}_{2}\\
\cdot\exp\left(-\kappa\int_{0}^{t}X^{x,x}_{t}(s)~\mathrm{d}s+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\|L_{t}(\tilde{U}^{i,i}_{t},X^{x,x}_{t})\|_{\mu}^{2}-\sum_{j=1}^{r}w_{j}\mathfrak{L}^{(j,0)}_{t}(\tilde{U}^{i,i}_{t},X^{x,x}_{t})\right)\Bigg]~\mathrm{d}x.

Without loss of generality (see Corollary
2.25
),
we can assume that

(3.41)

\displaystyle w_{1}<\cdots<w_{r_{0}}<\infty=w_{r_{0}+1}=\cdots=w_{r},

keeping in mind that all or none of the
w_{i}
could be equal to
\infty

if
r_{0}=r
or
r_{0}=0
. If we combine this with
the heuristic (
3.27
),
then we can rewrite (
3.40
) as

(3.42)

T_{2}(t)=\frac{(r-1)\upsilon^{2}}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Pi_{X}(t;x,x)\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa tx}\mathbf{E}\Bigg[\|L_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\|^{2}_{2}\\
\cdot\exp\left(-\kappa\int_{0}^{t}\big(X^{x,x}_{t}(s)-x\big)~\mathrm{d}s+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\|L_{t}(\tilde{U}^{i,i}_{t},X^{x,x}_{t})\|_{\mu}^{2}-\sum_{j\leq r_{0}}w_{j}\mathfrak{L}^{(j,0)}_{t}(\tilde{U}^{i,i}_{t},X^{x,x}_{t})\right)\\
\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{\mathcal{L}^{(j,0)}_{t}(\tilde{U}^{i,i}_{t},X^{x,x}_{t})=0,~\forall j>r_{0}\}}\Bigg]~\mathrm{d}x.

At this point, if we expand the exponential in the second line of (
3.42
) using

(3.43)

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{z}=1+\mathsf{R}_{1}(z),\qquad\text{where }|\mathsf{R}_{1}(z)|\leq|z|\mathrm{e}^{|z|},

then we see that

(3.44)

\displaystyle T_{2}(t)=\frac{(r-1)\upsilon^{2}}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Pi_{X}(t;x,x)\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa tx}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathfrak{I}^{(1)}_{i,t}(x)\Big(1+\mathfrak{I}^{(2)}_{i,t}(x)\Big)\Big]~\mathrm{d}x,

where we define

\displaystyle\mathfrak{I}^{(1)}_{i,t}(x)

\displaystyle=\|L_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\|^{2}_{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{\mathfrak{L}^{(j,0)}_{t}(\tilde{U}^{i,i}_{t},X^{x,x}_{t})=0,~\forall j>r_{0}\}},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{I}^{(2)}_{i,t}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{R}_{1}\left(-\kappa\int_{0}^{t}\big(X^{x,x}_{t}(s)-x\big)~\mathrm{d}s+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\|L_{t}(\tilde{U}^{i,i}_{t},X^{x,x}_{t})\|_{\mu}^{2}-\sum_{j\leq r_{0}}w_{j}\mathfrak{L}^{(j,0)}_{t}(\tilde{U}^{i,i}_{t},X^{x,x}_{t})\right).

We now analyze the contribution of each of these summands to
T_{2}(t)
’s asymptotics:

We begin with the contribution coming from
\mathfrak{I}^{(1)}_{i,t}(x)
. For this, we notice that

\mathbf{1}_{\{\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})=0\}}\leq\mathbf{1}_{\{\mathfrak{L}^{(j,0)}_{t}(\tilde{U}^{i,i}_{t},X^{x,x}_{t})=0,~\forall j>r_{0}\}}\leq 1,

where the first inequality follows from (
3.14
).
If we combine this with both (
3.32
) and (
3.34
),
then we obtain the
t\to 0^{+}
asymptotic

(3.45)

\displaystyle\frac{(r-1)\upsilon^{2}}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Pi_{X}(t;x,x)\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa tx}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathfrak{I}^{(1)}_{i,t}(x)\Big]~\mathrm{d}x=\frac{r(r-1)\upsilon^{2}}{4\kappa}+o(1).

Next, we analyze the contribution of
\mathfrak{I}^{(1)}_{i,t}(x)\mathfrak{I}^{(2)}_{i,t}(x)
.
On the one hand, we note that

(3.46)

\|L_{t}(\tilde{U}^{i,i}_{t},X^{x,x}_{t})\|_{\mu}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(L^{(j,y)}_{t}(\tilde{U}^{i,i}_{t},X^{x,x}_{t})\right)^{2}~\mathrm{d}y\\
\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{r}L^{(j,y)}_{t}(\tilde{U}^{i,i}_{t},X^{x,x}_{t})\right)^{2}~\mathrm{d}y=\|L_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\|_{2}^{2},

where the inequality follows from
z_{1}^{2}+\cdots+z_{n}^{2}\leq(z_{1}+\cdots+z_{n})^{2}

whenever
z_{i}\geq 0
, and the last equality follows from (
3.14
).
On the other hand, if

(3.47)

\displaystyle w_{\min}=\min\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{r}\},

then we get from (
3.14
) that

(3.48)

\displaystyle-\sum_{j\leq r_{0}}w_{j}\mathfrak{L}^{(j,0)}_{t}(\tilde{U}^{i,i}_{t},X^{x,x}_{t})\leq-w_{\min}\sum_{j=1}^{r}\mathfrak{L}^{(j,0)}_{t}(\tilde{U}^{i,i}_{t},X^{x,x}_{t})=-w_{\min}\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t}).

Combining this with the trivial bound
\mathbf{1}_{E}\leq 1
for any event
E
, we get that

(3.49)

\left|\mathfrak{I}^{(1)}_{i,t}(x)\mathfrak{I}^{(2)}_{i,t}(x)\right|\\
\leq C\|L_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\|^{2}_{2}\mathsf{R}_{1}\Bigg(\mathfrak{c}\Big(\big|{\textstyle\int_{0}^{t}}\big(X^{x,x}_{t}(s)-x\big)~\mathrm{d}s\big|+\|L_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}+\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\Big)\Bigg)

for some constants
C,\mathfrak{c}>0
.
Given that

\|L_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\|^{2}_{2}\leq\big|{\textstyle\int_{0}^{t}}\big(X^{x,x}_{t}(s)-x\big)~\mathrm{d}s\big|+\|L_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}+\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t}),

if we combine (
3.49
) with the upper bound on
\mathsf{R}_{1}
in (
3.43
),
then we get

\left|\mathfrak{I}^{(1)}_{i,t}(x)\mathfrak{I}^{(2)}_{i,t}(x)\right|\leq C\mathfrak{c}\Big(\big|{\textstyle\int_{0}^{t}}\big(X^{x,x}_{t}(s)-x\big)~\mathrm{d}s\big|+\|L_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\|_{2}+\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\Big)^{2}\\
\cdot\exp\Bigg\{\mathfrak{c}\Big(\big|{\textstyle\int_{0}^{t}}\big(X^{x,x}_{t}(s)-x\big)~\mathrm{d}s\big|+\|L_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}+\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\Big)\Bigg\}.

Thus, an application of (
3.37
) yields

(3.50)

\displaystyle\frac{(r-1)\upsilon^{2}}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Pi_{X}(t;x,x)\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa tx}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathfrak{I}^{(1)}_{i,t}(x)\mathfrak{I}^{(2)}_{i,t}(x)\Big]~\mathrm{d}x=o(1).

Summarizing the argument for
T_{2}(t)
, if we combine (
3.45
) and (
3.50
)
with (
3.44
), then we finally get

(3.51)

\displaystyle T_{2}(t)=\tfrac{r(r-1)\upsilon^{2}}{4\kappa}+o(1)\qquad\text{as }t\to 0^{+}.

3.3.3.
Four or More Jumps

We now discuss
T_{\geq 4}(t)
.
For this purpose, we begin with the observation that
the combinatorial constant introduced in Definition
3.12

has a very simple upper bound (see
[
32
, (4.2)]
):

|\mathfrak{C}_{t}(\widehat{p},\widehat{U}^{i}_{t})|\leq 1.

Using essentially the same argument as in
(
3.46
)
and (
3.48
), we get the following:

(3.52)

\displaystyle\|L_{t}(\widehat{U}^{i}_{t},X^{x,x}_{t})\|_{\mu}^{2}\leq\|L_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\|_{2}^{2},

(3.53)

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=1}^{r}w_{j}\mathfrak{L}^{(j,0)}_{t}(\widehat{U}^{i}_{t},X^{x,x}_{t})\leq-w_{\min}\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t}),

where we recall that
w_{\min}
is defined in (
3.47
).
Combining all of this into (
3.22
), we get from Jensen’s inequality
(taking the absolute value inside the
\mathrm{d}x
integral) and
|\mathfrak{M}_{t}(\widehat{p},\widehat{U}^{i}_{t})|=\upsilon^{\widehat{N}(t)}|\mathfrak{C}_{t}(\widehat{p},\widehat{U}^{i}_{t})|
that

(3.54)

|T_{\geq 4}(t)|\leq\int_{\mathcal{A}}\Pi_{X}(t;x,x)\mathbf{E}\Bigg[\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa\int_{0}^{t}X^{x,x}_{t}(s)~\mathrm{d}s+\frac{(r-1)^{2}+\sigma^{2}}{2}\|L_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}-w_{\min}\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})}\\
\cdot\upsilon^{\widehat{N}(t)}\mathbf{1}_{\{\widehat{U}^{i}_{t}(t)=i\}\cap\{\widehat{N}(t)\geq 4\}}\Bigg]~\mathrm{d}\mu(i,x).

By (
3.10
) and the independence
of
M^{i}_{k}
and
\{\widehat{N}(t),X^{x,x}_{t}\}
, we note that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\upsilon^{\widehat{N}(t)}\mathbf{1}_{\{\widehat{U}^{i}_{t}(t)=i\}\cap\{\widehat{N}(t)\geq 4\}}\bigg|X^{x,x}_{t}\right]=\frac{1}{1-r}\mathbf{E}\left[\upsilon^{\widehat{N}(t)}\mathbf{1}_{\{\widehat{N}(t)\geq 4\}}\bigg|X^{x,x}_{t}\right].

Then, by (
3.9
),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r-1}\mathbf{E}\left[\upsilon^{\widehat{N}(t)}\mathbf{1}_{\{\widehat{N}(t)\geq 4\}}\bigg|X^{x,x}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r-1}\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}\upsilon^{2m}\left(\frac{(r-1)^{2}\|L_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\|^{2}_{2}}{2}\right)^{m}\frac{1}{m!}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{(r-1)^{2}}{2}\|L_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{(r-1)^{2}}{2}\|L_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}}}{r-1}\left(\mathrm{e}^{\frac{(r-1)^{2}\upsilon^{2}}{2}\|L_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}}-1-\frac{(r-1)^{2}\upsilon^{2}}{2}\|L_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\right),

where the last line follows from
\mathrm{e}^{z}
’s Taylor expansion.
Given that
\mathrm{e}^{z}-1-z\leq z^{2}\mathrm{e}^{|z|}
when
z\geq 0
, this simplifies to

(3.55)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\upsilon^{\widehat{N}(t)}\mathbf{1}_{\{\widehat{U}^{i}_{t}(t)=i\}\cap\{\widehat{N}(t)\geq 4\}}\bigg|X^{x,x}_{t}\right]\leq\frac{(r-1)^{3}\upsilon^{4}\|L_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\|_{2}^{4}}{4}\mathrm{e}^{\frac{(\upsilon^{2}-1)(r-1)^{2}}{2}\|L_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}}.

Therefore, an application of the tower property in the left-hand side of (
3.54
) yields

(3.56)

|T_{\geq 4}(t)|\leq\frac{r(r-1)^{3}\upsilon^{4}}{4}\int_{\mathcal{A}}\Pi_{X}(t;x,x)\mathbf{E}\Bigg[\|L_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\|_{2}^{4}\\
\cdot\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa\int_{0}^{t}X^{x,x}_{t}(s)~\mathrm{d}s+\frac{\upsilon^{2}(r-1)^{2}+\sigma^{2}}{2}\|L_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}-w_{\min}\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})}\Bigg]~\mathrm{d}\mu(i,x).

On the one hand, we note that there exists a large enough constant
\mathfrak{c}>0
such that

\left(-\kappa{\textstyle{\int}_{0}^{t}X^{x,x}_{t}(s)~\mathrm{d}s+\frac{\upsilon^{2}(r-1)^{2}+\sigma^{2}}{2}\|L_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}-w_{\min}\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})}\right)\\
\leq\mathfrak{c}\Big(\big|{\textstyle\int_{0}^{t}}\big(X^{x,x}_{t}(s)-x\big)~\mathrm{d}s\big|+\|L_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}+\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\Big).

On the other hand,

\|L_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\|_{2}^{4}\leq\Big(\big|{\textstyle\int_{0}^{t}}\big(X^{x,x}_{t}(s)-x\big)~\mathrm{d}s\big|+\|L_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}+\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})\Big)^{2}.

If we plug both of these estimates into (
3.56
),
then we get from (
3.37
) that

(3.57)

\displaystyle T_{\geq 4}(t)=o(1)\qquad\text{as }t\to 0^{+}.

Proof of (
3.1
) Step 3. Reflected Brownian Bridge Asymptotics

We now conclude this paper by proving Proposition

3.4.1.
Preliminary: Brownian Motion Coupling

Reflected Brownian motion bridges are more difficult to work with than Brownian bridges.
Thus, we begin the proof of Proposition
3.16
by
examining how one can write (or estimate) expectations of functions of

X^{x,x}_{t}
as expectations of functions
B^{x,x}_{t}
and/or
B^{x,-x}_{t}
instead.
For this purpose, we recall that
in Definition
3.5
, we couple
X
with a standard
Brownian motion
B
via
X=|B|
. Under this coupling,
we note that
X^{x,x}_{t}
and a conditioned version of
B^{x}
are related as follows:

X^{x,x}_{t}(s)=\big(|B^{x}(s)|~\big|~B^{x}(t)\in\{-x,x\}\big),\qquad s\in[0,t].

Given that

\mathbf{P}\big[B^{x}(t)=\pm x~\big|~B^{x}(t)\in\{-x,x\}\big]=\frac{\Pi_{B}(t;x,\pm x)}{\Pi_{B}(t;x,x)+\Pi_{B}(t;x,-x)}=\frac{\Pi_{B}(t;x,\pm x)}{\Pi_{X}(t;x,x)}

and
\Pi_{B}(t;x,x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}
,
\Pi_{B}(t;x,-x)=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-2x^{2}/t}}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}
,
for any functional
F
, we can write

(3.58)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\Pi_{X}(t;x,x)\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa tx}\mathbf{E}\big[F\big(X^{x,x}_{t}\big)\big]~\mathrm{d}x\\
=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa tx}}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\mathbf{E}\big[F\big(|B^{x,x}_{t}|\big)\big]~\mathrm{d}x+\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa tx-2x^{2}/t}}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\mathbf{E}\big[F\big(|B^{x,-x}_{t}|\big)\big]~\mathrm{d}x.

If we replace
F(X^{x,x}_{t})
by
F(X^{x,x}_{t})\mathbf{1}_{\{\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})=0\}}
, then (
3.58
)
can be further simplified. Indeed, given that
B^{x,-x}_{t}
must obviously
reach zero as it travels from
x
to
-x
, one has
\mathbf{1}_{\{\mathfrak{L}^{0}(|B^{x,-x}_{t}|)=0\}}=0
.
Thus, in that case (
3.58
) simplifies to

(3.59)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\Pi_{X}(t;x,x)\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa tx}\mathbf{E}\big[F\big(X^{x,x}_{t}\big)\mathbf{1}_{\{\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})=0\}}\big]~\mathrm{d}x\\
=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa tx}}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\mathbf{E}\left[F\big(|B^{x,x}_{t}|\big)\mathbf{1}_{\{\mathfrak{L}^{0}(|B^{x,x}_{t}|)=0\}}\right]~\mathrm{d}x.

Next, if we write

\mathbf{1}_{\{\mathfrak{L}^{0}(|B^{x,x}_{t}|)=0\}}=\mathbf{1}_{\{\forall s\leq t,~B^{x,x}_{t}(s)\neq 0\}}=1-\mathbf{1}_{\{\exists s\leq t:~B^{x,x}_{t}(s)=0\}},

then we can write the right-hand side of (
3.59
) as

(3.60)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa tx}}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\mathbf{E}\left[F\big(|B^{x,x}_{t}|\big)\right]~\mathrm{d}x-\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa tx}}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\mathbf{E}\left[F\big(|B^{x,x}_{t}|\big)\mathbf{1}_{\{\exists s\leq t:~B^{x,x}_{t}(s)=0\}}\right]~\mathrm{d}x.

In order to simplify the second term in (
3.60
), we combine the following
observations:

(1)

By the strong Markov property and the symmetry of Brownian motion about
0
,
we notice that

\big(|B^{x,x}_{t}|~\big|~\exists s\leq t:~B^{x,x}_{t}(s)=0\big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{distr.}}}{{=}}|B^{x,-x}_{t}|

(i.e., we can couple the two processes above by reflecting the path of
B^{x,x}_{t}
after its first passage to zero, thus making it end at
-x
instead of
x
).

(2)

By using the joint density of the running minimum and the current value of a Brownian motion
(e.g.,
[
54
, Chapter III, Exercise 3.14]
), we obtain that

\mathbf{P}\big[\exists s\leq t:~B^{x,x}_{t}(s)=0\big]=\mathrm{e}^{-2x^{2}/t}.

Thus, if we turn the second expectation in (
3.60
)
into a conditional expectation given
\{\exists s\leq t:~B^{x,x}_{t}(s)=0\}

(by multiplying and dividing by the probability of that event, i.e.,
\mathrm{e}^{-2x^{2}/t}
), then we
obtain the following counterpart to (
3.58
)

(3.61)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\Pi_{X}(t;x,x)\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa tx}\mathbf{E}\big[F\big(X^{x,x}_{t}\big)\mathbf{1}_{\{\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})=0\}}\big]~\mathrm{d}x\\
=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa tx}}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\mathbf{E}\big[F\big(|B^{x,x}_{t}|\big)\big]~\mathrm{d}x-\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa tx-2x^{2}/t}}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\mathbf{E}\big[F\big(|B^{x,-x}_{t}|\big)\big]~\mathrm{d}x.

Finally, while (
3.58
) and (
3.61
)
are useful for exact calculations (and thus necessary to obtain the explicit leading order terms in our
asymptotics), simpler expressions can be used when we are only interested in upper estimates.
For this purpose, we invoke
[
31
, (5.9)]
, which states that for any nonnegative
functional
F
, one has

(3.62)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\big[F(X^{x,x}_{t})\big]\leq 2\mathbf{E}\big[F(|B^{x,x}_{t}|)\big].

We are now prepared to undertake the proofs of our technical results.

3.4.2.
Proof of (
3.31
) and (
3.33
)

By (
3.58
) and (
3.61
) with
F=1
,

\int_{0}^{\infty}\Pi_{X}(t;x,x)\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa tx}~\mathrm{d}x=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa tx}}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}~\mathrm{d}x+\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa tx-2x^{2}/t}}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}~\mathrm{d}x,

and

\int_{0}^{\infty}\Pi_{X}(t;x,x)\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa tx}\mathbf{E}\big[\mathbf{1}_{\{\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{t}(X^{x,x}_{t})=0\}}\big]~\mathrm{d}x=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa tx}}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}~\mathrm{d}x-\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa tx-2x^{2}/t}}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}~\mathrm{d}x.

We then get (
3.31
) and (
3.33
)
as follows: On the one hand,

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa tx}}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}~\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\kappa}t^{-3/2}.

On the other hand, by the change of variables
x\mapsto t^{1/2}x
and dominated convergence,

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa tx-2x^{2}/t}}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}~\mathrm{d}x=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa t^{3/2}x-2x^{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}~\mathrm{d}x=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-2x^{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}~\mathrm{d}x+o(1)=\frac{1}{4}+o(1).

3.4.3.
Proof of (
3.32
) and (
3.34
)

By (
3.58
) and (
3.61
),
the proof of (
3.32
) and (
3.34
) reduces to two claims:
As
t\to 0^{+}
, one has

(3.63)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa tx}}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\mathbf{E}\big[\|L_{t}(|B^{x,x}_{t}|)\|_{2}^{2}\big]~\mathrm{d}x=\tfrac{1}{2\kappa}+o(1),

(3.64)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa tx-2x^{2}/t}}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\mathbf{E}\big[\|L_{t}(|B^{x,-x}_{t}|)\|_{2}^{2}\big]~\mathrm{d}x=o(1).

For this purpose, it is useful to note that, by Brownian scaling
and the change of variables
y\mapsto t^{1/2}y
,

(3.65)

\|L_{t}(|B^{x,x}_{t}|)\|_{2}^{2}=\int_{0}^{\infty}L^{y}_{t}(|B^{x,\pm x}_{t}|)^{2}~\mathrm{d}y=t\int_{0}^{\infty}L^{t^{-1/2}y}_{1}(|B^{t^{-1/2}x,\pm t^{-1/2}x}_{1}|)^{2}~\mathrm{d}y\\
=t^{3/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}L^{y}_{1}(|B^{t^{-1/2}x,\pm t^{-1/2}x}_{1}|)^{2}~\mathrm{d}y=t^{3/2}\|L_{1}(|B^{t^{-1/2}x,\pm t^{-1/2}x}|)\|_{2}^{2}.

We begin with the proof of (
3.63
).
An application of (
3.65
) yields

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa tx}}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\mathbf{E}\big[\|L_{t}(|B^{x,x}_{t}|)\|_{2}^{2}\big]~\mathrm{d}x=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{t\,\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa tx}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\mathbf{E}\big[\|L_{1}(|B^{t^{-1/2}x,t^{-1/2}x}_{1}|)\|_{2}^{2}\big]~\mathrm{d}x.

If we now apply the change of variables
x\mapsto t^{-1}x
, then this becomes

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa tx}}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\mathbf{E}\big[\|L_{t}(|B^{x,x}_{t}|)\|_{2}^{2}\big]~\mathrm{d}x=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa x}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\mathbf{E}\big[\|L_{1}(|B^{t^{-3/2}x,t^{-3/2}x}_{1}|)\|_{2}^{2}\big]~\mathrm{d}x.

The proof of (
3.63
) can thus be broken down into two steps:

(3.66)

\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa x}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\mathbf{E}\big[\|L_{1}(|B^{t^{-3/2}x,t^{-3/2}x}_{1}|)\|_{2}^{2}\big]~\mathrm{d}x\\
=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa x}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\mathbf{E}\big[\|L_{1}(B^{0,0}_{1})\|_{2}^{2}\big]~\mathrm{d}x=\frac{\mathbf{E}\big[\|L_{1}(B^{0,0}_{1})\|_{2}^{2}\big]}{\sqrt{2\pi}\kappa}

and

(3.67)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\big[\|L_{1}(B^{0,0}_{1})\|_{2}^{2}\big]=\tfrac{\sqrt{\pi}}{\sqrt{2}}.

For (
3.66
), we first notice that for any
y>0
,

L_{1}^{y}(|B^{t^{-3/2}x,t^{-3/2}x}_{1}|)=L_{1}^{y}(B^{t^{-3/2}x,t^{-3/2}x}_{1})+L_{1}^{-y}(B^{t^{-3/2}x,t^{-3/2}x}_{1}).

Moreover, if we use the coupling

B^{t^{-3/2}x,t^{-3/2}x}_{1}=t^{-3/2}x+B^{0,0}_{1},

then this becomes

(3.68)

\displaystyle L_{1}^{y}(|B^{t^{-3/2}x,t^{-3/2}x}_{1}|)=L_{1}^{y-t^{-3/2}x}(B^{0,0}_{1})+L_{1}^{-y-t^{-3/2}x}(B^{0,0}_{1}).

Therefore,

(3.69)

\displaystyle\|L_{1}(|B^{t^{-3/2}x,t^{-3/2}x}_{1}|)\|_{2}^{2}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(L_{1}^{y-t^{-3/2}x}(B^{0,0}_{1})+L_{1}^{-y-t^{-3/2}x}(B^{0,0}_{1})\right)^{2}~\mathrm{d}y.

For every fixed
x>0
, there exists some finite random
t_{0}
small enough so that
B^{0,0}_{1}
’s path
does not intersect
(-\infty,-t^{-3/2}x]
for every
t<t_{0}
. Therefore,

\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\|L_{1}(|B^{t^{-3/2}x,t^{-3/2}x}_{1}|)\|_{2}^{2}=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\int_{0}^{\infty}L_{1}^{y-t^{-3/2}x}(B^{0,0}_{1})^{2}~\mathrm{d}y=\|L_{1}(B^{0,0}_{1})\|_{2}^{2}\qquad\text{almost surely.}

Consequently, (
3.66
) follows from dominated convergence if we prove that

\sup_{x>0}\mathbf{E}\left[\sup_{t>0}\|L_{1}(|B^{t^{-3/2}x,t^{-3/2}x}_{1}|)\|_{2}^{2}\right]<\infty.

This immediately follows by combining (
3.67
)
with an application of the inequality
(z_{1}+z_{1})^{2}\leq 2(z_{1}^{2}+z_{2}^{2})
to (
3.69
),
the latter of which yields

\|L_{1}(|B^{t^{-3/2}x,t^{-3/2}x}_{1}|)\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2\int_{0}^{\infty}L_{1}^{y-t^{-3/2}x}(B^{0,0}_{1})^{2}+L_{1}^{-y-t^{-3/2}x}(B^{0,0}_{1})^{2}~\mathrm{d}y=2\|L_{t}(B^{0,0}_{1})\|_{2}^{2}.

With this in hand, the only element left in the proof of (
3.63
) is
(
3.67
).
By Tonelli’s theorem, we can write

\mathbf{E}\big[\|L_{t}(B^{0,0}_{1})\|_{2}^{2}\big]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{E}\big[L^{y}_{1}(B^{0,0}_{1})^{2}\big]~\mathrm{d}x.

By
[
52
, (1)]
, we have the joint density

(3.70)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[L_{1}^{y}(B^{0})\in\mathrm{d}\ell,B^{0}(1)\in\mathrm{d}x\right]=\frac{(|y|+|x-y|+\ell)\mathrm{e}^{-(|y|+|x-y|+\ell)^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}

for all
\ell>0
and
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
.
If we evaluate this at
x=0
, further noting that
\mathbf{P}\left[B^{0}(1)\in\mathrm{d}0\right]=\Pi_{B}(1;0,0)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}},

then we get the conditional density

\mathbf{P}\left[L_{1}^{y}(B^{0,0}_{1})\in\mathrm{d}\ell\right]=(2|y|+\ell)\mathrm{e}^{-(2|y|+\ell)^{2}/2}.

Therefore, we obtain (
3.67
) through a sequence of two
exact calculations:

\mathbf{E}\left[L^{y}_{1}(B^{0,0}_{1})^{2}\right]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\ell^{2}\cdot(2|y|+\ell)\mathrm{e}^{-(2|y|+\ell)^{2}/2}~\mathrm{d}\ell=2\mathrm{e}^{-2y^{2}}-2\sqrt{2\pi}|y|\,\mathrm{erfc}(\sqrt{2}|y|),

and then

\int_{\mathbb{R}}2\mathrm{e}^{-2y^{2}}-2\sqrt{2\pi}|y|\,\mathrm{erfc}(\sqrt{2}|y|)~\mathrm{d}y=\tfrac{\sqrt{\pi}}{\sqrt{2}},

where we recall that
\mathrm{erfc}(z)=1-\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{z}\mathrm{e}^{-y^{2}}~\mathrm{d}y
is the complementary error function.

Now that (
3.63
) is proved,
the proof of (
3.32
) and (
3.34
)
relies on establishing (
3.64
).
By (
3.65
), this is equivalent to showing

t\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa tx-2x^{2}/t}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\mathbf{E}\big[\|L_{1}(|B^{t^{-1/2}x,-t^{-1/2}x}_{1}|)\|_{2}^{2}\big]~\mathrm{d}x=o(1).

If we apply the change of variables
x\mapsto t^{1/2}x
, then this becomes

t^{3/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa t^{3/2}x-2x^{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\mathbf{E}\big[\|L_{1}(|B^{x,-x}_{1}|)\|_{2}^{2}\big]~\mathrm{d}x=o(1).

As
\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa t^{3/2}x}\to 1
as
t\to 0
, by dominated convergence, we get (
3.63
)
if we show that

(3.71)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-2x^{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\mathbf{E}\big[\|L_{1}(|B^{x,-x}_{1}|)\|_{2}^{2}\big]~\mathrm{d}x<\infty.

Using a similar argument as in (
3.68
), we get

\|L_{1}(|B^{x,-x}_{1}|)\|_{2}^{2}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big(L^{y}_{1}(B^{x,-x}_{1})+L^{-y}_{1}(B^{x,-x}_{1})\Big)^{2}~\mathrm{d}y\\
\leq 2\int_{0}^{\infty}L^{y}_{1}(B^{x,-x}_{1})^{2}+L^{-y}_{1}(B^{x,-x}_{1})^{2}~\mathrm{d}y=2\|L_{t}(B^{x,-x})\|_{2}^{2}.

If we additionally use the fact that self-intersection local time is invariant
with respect to shifts in space, we get
\|L_{t}(B^{x,-x})\|_{2}^{2}=\|L_{t}(B^{0,-2x})\|_{2}^{2}
.
Thus (
3.71
) reduces to

(3.72)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-2x^{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\mathbf{E}\big[\|L_{1}(B^{0,-2x}_{1})\|_{2}^{2}\big]~\mathrm{d}x<\infty.

If we combine (
3.70
) with
\mathbf{P}[B^{0}(1)\in\mathrm{d}(-2x)]=\Pi_{B}(1;0,-2x)=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-2x^{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}
,
then we get that

\mathbf{P}\left[L_{1}^{y}(B^{0,-2x}_{1})\in\mathrm{d}\ell\right]=(|y|+|-2x-y|+\ell)\mathrm{e}^{-(|y|+|-2x-y|+\ell)^{2}/2}\mathrm{e}^{2x^{2}}.

We then get (
3.72
) through a sequence of exact calculations:
Firstly,

(3.73)

\mathbf{E}\big[L^{y}_{1}(B^{0,-2x}_{1})^{2}\big]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\ell^{2}\cdot(|y|+|-2x-y|+\ell)\mathrm{e}^{-(|y|+|-2x-y|+\ell)^{2}/2}\mathrm{e}^{2x^{2}}~\mathrm{d}\ell\\
=\mathrm{e}^{2x^{2}}\left(\sqrt{2\pi}(|2x+y|+|y|)\left(\mathrm{erf}\left(\frac{|2x+y|+|y|}{\sqrt{2}}\right)-1\right)+2\mathrm{e}^{-(|2x+y|+|y|)^{2}/2}\right),

where
\mathrm{erf}(z)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{z}\mathrm{e}^{-y^{2}}~\mathrm{d}y
denotes the error function.
Secondly, integrating the second line of (
3.73
) with
respect to
y
on all of
\mathbb{R}
yields

(3.74)

\mathbf{E}\big[\|L_{1}(B^{0,-2x}_{1})\|_{2}^{2}\big]=4\sqrt{2\pi}\mathrm{e}^{2x^{2}}x^{2}\mathrm{erf}(\sqrt{2}x)\\
+\tfrac{\sqrt{\pi}}{\sqrt{2}}\mathrm{e}^{2x^{2}}(4x^{2}+1)\mathrm{erfc}(\sqrt{2}x)+2(1-2\sqrt{2\pi}\mathrm{e}^{2x^{2}}x)x.

Thirdly, integrating the right-hand side of (
3.74
)
multiplied by
\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-2x^{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}
finally yields

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-2x^{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\mathbf{E}\big[\|L_{1}(B^{0,-2x}_{1})\|_{2}^{2}\big]~\mathrm{d}x=\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{3\sqrt{\pi}}<\infty.

This proves (
3.72
), thus concluding the proof
of (
3.32
) and (
3.34
).

3.4.4.

By (

(3.76)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa tx-2x^{2}/t}}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\mathbf{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}|B^{x,-x}_{t}(s)|-x~\mathrm{d}s\right]~\mathrm{d}x=o(1).

We begin with (
3.75

If we couple
B^{x,x}_{t}=x+B^{0,0}_{t}
and then distribute the

term in

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\left|\mathbf{E}\left[\int_{0}^{1}|x+B^{0,0}_{1}(s)|-x~\mathrm{d}s\right]\right|~\mathrm{d}x=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left|\int_{0}^{1}\mathbf{E}\big[|x+B^{0,0}_{1}(s)|-x\big]~\mathrm{d}s\right|~\mathrm{d}x<\infty,

3.75
) (actually, the stronger statement RHS of (
3.75
)
=O(t^{3/2})

since
\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa t^{3/2}x}\uparrow 1

.

is Gaussian with mean zero and variance
s(1-s)

\mathbf{E}\big[|x+B^{0,0}_{1}(s)|-x\big]=\sqrt{\frac{2s(1-s)}{\pi}}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}/2s(1-s)}-x\,\mathrm{erfc}\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{2s(1-s)}}\right).

\text{RHS of }\eqref{N(t)=0 Case Part 1 - Integral 1.1}\leq\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\frac{2s(1-s)}{\pi}}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}/2s(1-s)}+x\,\mathrm{erfc}\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{2s(1-s)}}\right)~\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}s\\

3.75

).

B^{x,-x}_{t}(s)=(1-\tfrac{s}{t})x-\tfrac{s}{t}x+B^{0,0}_{t}(s).

By the reverse triangle inequality and the triangle inequality, for any
x>0
and
s\in[0,t]
,
one has

(3.79)

\big||B^{x,-x}_{t}(s)|-x\big|=\big||(1-\tfrac{s}{t})x-\tfrac{s}{t}x+B^{0,0}_{t}(s)|-x\big|\\
\leq\big|(1-\tfrac{s}{t})x-\tfrac{s}{t}x+B^{0,0}_{t}(s)-x\big|=|B^{0,0}_{t}(s)-\tfrac{2s}{t}x|\leq|B^{0,0}_{t}(s)|+\tfrac{2s}{t}x.

\displaystyle|\text{RHS of }\eqref{N(t)=0 Case Part 1 - Integral 2}|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-2x^{2}/t}}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\mathbf{E}\left[\int_{0}^{t}|B^{0,0}_{t}(s)|+\tfrac{2s}{t}x~\mathrm{d}s\right]~\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{t}{4}\mathbf{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}|B^{0,0}_{t}(s)|\right]+\frac{t^{3/2}}{4\sqrt{2\pi}}

This proves (
3.76
).

By (

3.36
) reduces to

(3.80)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa tx}\Pi_{B}(t;x,\pm x)\mathbf{E}\big[\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{t}(B^{x,\pm x}_{t})\big]~\mathrm{d}x=o(1).

(Note that we use

instead of

By Brownian scaling,

\mathbf{E}\big[\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{t}(B^{x,\pm x}_{t})\big]=t^{1/2}\mathbf{E}\big[\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{1}(B^{t^{-1/2}x,\pm t^{-1/2}x}_{1})\big]

[
52

and

\Pi_{B}(t;x,\pm x)t^{1/2}\mathbf{E}\big[\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{1}(B^{t^{-1/2}x,t^{-1/2}\pm x}_{1})\big]\\

) thanks to the asymptotic

.

) to

(3.87)

\displaystyle t^{-1/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa tx}\mathbf{E}\Bigg[\Big(t^{3/2}\sup_{0\leq s\leq 1}|B^{0,0}_{1}(s)|+t^{3/2}\|L_{1}(|B^{x_{t},x_{t}}_{1}|)\|_{2}^{2}+t^{1/2}\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{1}(B^{x_{t},x_{t}}_{1})\Big)^{4}\Bigg]^{1/2}~\mathrm{d}x=o(1).

Since there exists some
c>0
such that
(z_{1}+z_{2}+z_{3})^{4}\leq c(z_{1}^{4}+z_{2}^{4}+z_{3}^{4})
for all
z_{i}\geq 0
(by Jensen’s inequality),
and
\sqrt{z_{1}+z_{2}+z_{3}}\leq\sqrt{z}_{1}+\sqrt{z}_{2}+\sqrt{z}_{3}
for all
z_{i}\geq 0
, we can further reduce (
3.87
) to

(3.88)

t^{-1/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa tx}\Bigg(\mathbf{E}\Big[t^{6}\sup_{0\leq s\leq 1}|B^{0,0}_{1}(s)|^{4}\Big]^{1/2}+\mathbf{E}\Big[t^{6}\|L_{1}(|B^{x_{t},x_{t}}_{1}|)\|_{2}^{8}\Big]^{1/2}\\
+\mathbf{E}\big[t^{2}\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{1}(B^{x_{t},x_{t}}_{1})^{4}\big]^{1/2}\Bigg)=o(1).

The first line of (
3.88
) vanishes thanks to the combination of three facts: Firstly,

t^{-1/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa tx}(t^{6})^{1/2}~\mathrm{d}x=\frac{t^{3/2}}{\kappa}=o(1).

Secondly,

\mathbf{E}\Big[\sup_{0\leq s\leq 1}|B^{0,0}_{1}(s)|^{4}\Big]<\infty

thanks to the fact that
|B^{0,0}_{1}|
supremum has Gaussian tails (
[
40
, Remark 3.1]
).
Thirdly, it follows from (
3.86
) that

\sup_{z>0}\mathbf{E}\big[\|L_{1}(|B^{z,z}_{1}|)\|_{2}^{8}\big]<\infty.

Thus, we now only need to show that the second line of (
3.88
) vanishes, which we simplify as
as follows:

(3.89)

\displaystyle t^{1/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa tx}\mathbf{E}\big[\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{1}(B^{t^{-1/2}x,t^{-1/2}x}_{1})^{4}\big]^{1/2}~\mathrm{d}x=o(1).

Toward this end, noting that
\kappa t\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa tx}\mathrm{d}x
is a probability measure on
[0,\infty)
and that the square root function is concave, it follows from Jensen’s inequality that

t^{1/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa tx}\mathbf{E}\big[\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{1}(B^{t^{-1/2}x,t^{-1/2}x}_{1})^{4}\big]^{1/2}~\mathrm{d}x\leq\frac{t^{1/2}}{\kappa t}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\kappa t\,\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa tx}\mathbf{E}\big[\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{1}(B^{t^{-1/2}x,t^{-1/2}x}_{1})^{4}\big]~\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/2}.

If we now group together the powers of
t
in the above, we can reduce
(
3.89
) to

(3.90)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa tx}\mathbf{E}\big[\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{1}(B^{t^{-1/2}x,t^{-1/2}x}_{1})^{4}\big]~\mathrm{d}x=o(1).

If we combine (
3.81
) with the fact that

\Pi(t;x,x)t^{1/2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}
, then we get from an exact calculation that

\mathbf{E}\big[\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{1}(B^{t^{-1/2}x,t^{-1/2}x}_{1})^{4}\big]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\ell^{4}\cdot\sqrt{2\pi}(2t^{-1/2}x+\ell)\mathrm{e}^{-(2t^{-1/2}x+\ell)^{2}/2}~\mathrm{d}\ell\\
=8\sqrt{2\pi}e^{-2x^{2}/t}(t+2x^{2})t^{-1}-8\pi(3tx+4x^{3})\mathrm{erfc}\left(\tfrac{\sqrt{2}x}{\sqrt{t}}\right)t^{-3/2}.

If we integrate the second line above multiplied by
\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa tx}
, an exact
calculation yields

(3.91)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa tx}\mathbf{E}\big[\mathfrak{L}^{0}_{1}(B^{t^{-1/2}x,t^{-1/2}x}_{1})^{4}\big]~\mathrm{d}x\\
=\frac{192\pi\mathrm{e}^{\kappa^{2}t^{3}/8}\mathrm{erfc}\left(\frac{\kappa t^{3/2}}{2\sqrt{2}}\right)}{\kappa^{4}t^{11/2}}-\frac{192\pi}{\kappa^{4}t^{11/2}}+\frac{96\sqrt{2\pi}}{\kappa^{3}t^{4}}-\frac{24\pi}{\kappa^{2}t^{5/2}}+\frac{8\sqrt{2\pi}}{\kappa t}.

To see why this vanishes,
we combine the Taylor expansions (as
z\to 0
)

\mathrm{e}^{z}=1+z+O(z^{2})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathrm{erfc}(z)=1-\tfrac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}z+\tfrac{2}{3\sqrt{\pi}}z^{3}+O(z^{5}),

which yields the
t\to 0^{+}
asymptotics

\mathrm{e}^{\kappa^{2}t^{3}/8}=1+\tfrac{\kappa^{2}t^{3}}{8}+O(t^{6})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathrm{erfc}\left(\frac{\kappa t^{3/2}}{2\sqrt{2}}\right)=1-\tfrac{\kappa t^{3/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}+\tfrac{\kappa^{3}t^{9/2}}{24\sqrt{2\pi}}+O(t^{15/2}).

If we take a product of the above, and then multiply the result by
\frac{192\pi}{\kappa^{4}t^{11/2}}
, then we get

\frac{192\pi\mathrm{e}^{\kappa^{2}t^{3}/8}\mathrm{erfc}\left(\frac{\kappa t^{3/2}}{2\sqrt{2}}\right)}{\kappa^{4}t^{11/2}}=\frac{192\pi}{\kappa^{4}t^{11/2}}-\frac{96\sqrt{2\pi}}{\kappa^{3}t^{4}}+\frac{24\pi}{\kappa^{2}t^{5/2}}-\frac{8\sqrt{2\pi}}{\kappa t}+O(t^{1/2}).

If we put this into (
3.91
),
then we obtain (
3.89
).
This finally concludes the proof of (
3.37
),
and thus of Proposition
3.16
.
Consequently, Theorem
3.1
is now proved.

Heuristic
2.2

Recall the definitions of
Z_{\beta,n}

H_{n}(\mathcal{B}^{\beta,n})

2.2
)
and (
2.3

(A.1)

In the special case where

[
55
]

yields the explicit formula

, where we define

Z_{G\beta E,n}=(2\pi)^{n/2}(n\beta/2)^{-\beta n^{2}/4+(\beta/4-1/2)n}\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{n}\Gamma(1+j\beta/2)}{\Gamma(1+\beta/2)^{n}}.

Asymptotics using several applications of the Mathematica ”Limit” function reveal that, as
n\to\infty
, one has

-\tfrac{1}{n}\tfrac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\log Z_{G\beta E,n}=\tfrac{3}{8}n-\tfrac{1}{2}\log n-\tfrac{1}{4}\left(1+\log(\beta^{2}/4)-2\tfrac{\Gamma^{\prime}(1+\beta/2)}{\Gamma(1+\beta/2)}\right)+o(1)

and
\tfrac{1}{n^{2}}\tfrac{\partial^{2}}{\partial^{2}\beta}\log Z_{G\beta E,n}=o(1)
. Combining this with (
A.1
),
we get that when
V(x)=x^{2}/2
, the limit (
2.4
) holds.
Using the well-known identity between the polygamma functions and the Hurwitz zeta function (e.g.,
[
1
, 6.4.10]
), we have that
for
\beta>0
,

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\beta}\left(\log(\beta^{2}/4)-2\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}(1+\beta/2)}{\Gamma(1+\beta/2)}\right)=\frac{2}{\beta}-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(k+1+\beta/2)^{2}}>\frac{2}{\beta}-\int_{-1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(k+1+\beta/2)^{2}}~\mathrm{d}k=0,

where the inequality follows from the integral test since
k\mapsto(k+1+\beta/2)^{-2}
is strictly decreasing.
This implies that
\beta\mapsto\log(\beta^{2}/4)-2\tfrac{\Gamma^{\prime}(1+\beta/2)}{\Gamma(1+\beta/2)}
is invertible for
\beta\in(0,\infty)
.
