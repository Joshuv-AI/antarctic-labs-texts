Implementing risk-appropriate maternity care-based triage model at a tertiary care teaching institute: an organisational quality improvement initiative to optimise risk and resources
Authors: Gowri Dorairajan, Anish Keepanasseril, Samraj Senthil Kumar, Anandhan Rajeswari, Lalgudi N Dorairajan, Rakesh Aggarwal, JIPMER ObGyn Triage Implementation Consortium
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe annual births in our hospital (a regional perinatal centre for the southeastern coastal region) had increased to nearly 19 000 in 2019, straining the resources. Reduced low-risk childbirths due to the restrictions during the COVID-19 pandemic gave us an impetus to design and implement a risk-appropriate triage model referral system. We report its implementation process and examine its effect on birth rates and quality of care.MethodsInitially, the data on childbirths (2019) and the districts where the majority belonged were analysed. We discussed the need for triaging and the implementation process with these district health administrators. In the antenatal clinic, a dedicated team triaged and referred new cases to risk-appropriate facilities near their homes. Using WhatsApp groups, information about those referred back and the critically ill transferred to our hospital was shared. The impact of model implementation was assessed by the change in the number of births, proportion of high-risk cases, quality indicators and feedback from health workers.ResultsThe average number of childbirths per month decreased from 1530 in 2019 to 900 in 2023 after the implementation of triage on 15 December 2022. The quality indicators, such as stillbirth and scar rupture, declined after implementation, but caesarean deliveries rose from 20% to 30%. Better satisfaction among personnel and a change in the pattern to more high-risk pregnancies were noted; there was a reduction in bed occupancy rates, averting overcrowding.ConclusionA ‘risk-appropriate maternity care-based triage model’ could be implemented, reducing low-risk births and improving the quality of care for high-risk women in tertiary care institutes.Keywords: Maternal health

Introduction

Various initiatives under the Millennium Development Goals increased institutional deliveries from 26% in 1992–1993 (National Family Health Survey (NFHS)-1) to more than 85% in 2019–2021 (NFHS-5).1 The term ‘institution’ includes level I with basic care to level IV(regional perinatal centres), providing multidisciplinary team care to complex and critically ill pregnant women.2 3 In the Indian context, the subcentre provides an outpatient antenatal risk assessment and follow-up alone, with the primary health centre providing the basic emergency obstetric care (EmOC) and subdistrict or district health centres acting as the first referral units providing the comprehensive EmOC. Medical teaching institutions provide level III care, where the support of other specialities’ services is available to manage high-risk pregnancies, and regional perinatal centres provide level IV care.4 Conventionally, the referral pathways in most low-income to middle-income countries (LMICs) are unidirectional, from lower facilities to regional referral centres to prioritise and optimise care for those with complications.5 Over half of the women self-refer to the tertiary or regional perinatal centres for birthing, bypassing the mid-level facilities, leading to overcrowding and straining resources and quality of care.5,10

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the travel restrictions led to only the high-risk, complex cases being referred to a tertiary hospital such as ours, reducing the number of births in that period. This gave us the impetus to design and implement a risk-appropriate maternity care-based triage model to improve the quality and optimise resources. In this report, we describe our experience implementing a triage model following risk-appropriate maternity care, referring those with lower risk to a public health facility proximate to their home and assessing its impact on the number of childbirths and quality indicators of care.

Methods

Study design and setting

This quality improvement implementation study was conducted at Jawaharlal Institute of Postgraduate Medical Education and Research, Puducherry, India. This is a government-funded teaching-cum-regional perinatal referral centre in the southeastern coastal region with 2200 beds, catering to high-risk cases from Pondicherry (Union Territory) and the nearby districts of Tamil Nadu. Department of obstetrics and gynaecology in Women and Children block has 70 antenatal and 110 postpartum beds (including 25 beds in the postcaesarean ward). There are six level II obstetric intensive care unit beds and 22 in the high dependency unit, including six beds exclusively for hypertensive patients. The services are free for patients of low socioeconomic strata, and many low-risk and moderate-risk women also give birth in the hospital.

Feedback survey results

A semiquantitative survey was conducted among the nurses (n=8) and resident doctors (n=6) working in the maternity unit before the pandemic and after triage implementation, which revealed a unanimous agreement on the shift towards high-risk cases, and a 40–50% reduction in the deliveries as well as the floor bed occupancy in the wards. Most (90%) reported improvement in intrapartum monitoring, and more time was available for rendering quality care, indicating the appropriateness of the triage model. More than half of the surveyed reported no observed patient satisfaction, especially among high-risk patients, about the improved length of time spent on care and counselling them. Dissatisfaction among those referred back to their risk-appropriate centres was also pointed out. Challenges observed included the extra time/effort for triaging and counselling. About 20% suggested extending the triage process to the staff-related/accompanied women. Trainees reported a reduction in surgical or procedure-related learning opportunities by 20%. Most nursing staff felt increased work stress, while the doctors experienced stress relief.

Discussion

Main findings

Implementing triaging and referring the low-risk pregnant women to risk-appropriate health facilities near their homes resulted in a 40% reduction in births from the prepandemic period and a 27% from the post-COVID-pre-triage implementation phase. The proportion of those with pregnancy complications such as preterm birth, multiple pregnancies, hypertensive disorders and hyperglycaemia increased post-triage. Intrapartum scar rupture rates during labour after a previous caesarean significantly declined post-triage. In the same period, the maternal mortality ratio of the nearby districts (Cuddalore and Villupuram) showed a downward trend. Even though the absolute numbers were only marginally increased, the caesarean section rates rose in the post-triage phase. The reduction was also noted in stillbirth rate, neonatal death rates and the number of maternal deaths. Better satisfaction rates, intrapartum monitoring and a change in case mix with more women with high-risk pregnancies were reported, with the resultant avoidance of overcrowding as reflected by the bed occupancy rates.

Results Interpretation and comparison with other studies

Risk-based screening at the community and district levels enables prompt referral to higher institutes.11 Recent reports support implementing risk-appropriate management to prevent severe maternal morbidity.12 13 Birthing at a higher level of care or regional perinatal centre has been shown to improve outcomes among those with increased risks.14 A study in the urban setting from Maharashtra suggested that an integrated primary, secondary and tertiary level with efficient emergency obstetric referral can improve maternal and neonatal outcomes.15

In most LMICs such as India, referrals are unidirectional from lower to higher centres.5 Earlier studies demonstrated that most pregnant women either self-referred or followed verbal advice from the grassroots workers, such as village health nurses, to the tertiary centres directly, bypassing the mid-level facilities.5

Most women often choose birthing facilities based on proximity to home, perceived quality of care, provider rapport and ability to manage complications. Studies show that referrals by primary care health workers are due to perceived fear of inability to manage complications or lack of expertise or facilities, as well as verbal referrals from the first contact in the community, resulting in bypassing the mid-level facilities.6 8 9 16 The fear of scrutiny during the maternal mortality audits also precludes low-risk retention in primary care, leading to referrals to tertiary hospitals and resultant underutilisation of infrastructure and resources in intermediate centres.17 Very few authors have studied the low-risk retention model in maternal care. In 2004, Reddy et al18 showed that increasing caregivers’ confidence reduced the antenatal and intrapartum transfers from a low-risk midwife-led labour ward to a specialist-led care unit, from 35% and 7.9%, respectively, to 23% and 2.7%.

Tamil Nadu has a robust network of comprehensive obstetric care and round-the-clock, efficient transport systems near a primary or secondary level facility, allowing specialist care access within 30–40 min. Since 2005, various confidence-building measures were instituted in Primary Health Centre (PHC) to increase the birthing rates, which provide 24×7 essential emergency obstetric services.19 20 During the COVID-19 pandemic, the state used telecommunications and digital tracking of pregnant women and imparted training to healthcare workers at various levels to maintain care for expecting mothers and optimise resource utilisation in nearby birthing centres, sustaining the improved health indicators, as shown in the UNICEF report.20,22

Key lessons learned

Risk-appropriate triaging with referral from tertiary care to nearby public health facilities is feasible, allowing more time for counselling and reassuring those with high-risk factors. Digital technology helps to bridge the different levels of care, enabling smooth referral and two-way communication, providing reassurance for a timely intervention and pre-arrival coordination to optimise the care during pregnancy and postpartum. Support and commitment from both district and hospital authorities are crucial in this ongoing quality improvement process. Periodic meetings and multiple iterations to overcome the gaps identified, along with the dissemination of information, could aid in the sustainability of the triaging process.

Future challenges and recommendations

In future, a mapping of facilities, using tools such as ‘Levels of Care Assessment Tool (LOCATe)’, based on the expertise, infrastructure and access timelines to reach the nearest lower facility, will aid in creating a referral pathway that provides risk-appropriate care closer to homes.23 The pathway details, including the conditions treated in facilities, should be widely shared with pregnant women and the healthcare personnel in the area. Regular audits of the referrals can identify gaps, guiding targeted strengthening of the lower facilities with further training and infrastructure. This will build confidence and improve the outcomes, reducing unjust or inappropriate referrals to tertiary care level centres.

Strengths and limitations

This is one of the initial explorative studies on implementing the triage model for setting risk-appropriate maternity care, with referral of uncomplicated pregnancies from the regional perinatal centre or tertiary care setting to an appropriate lower-level facility. The limitation is that the experience and outcomes were not directly assessed among women sent back to risk-appropriate health facilities after triaging as part of this report. The reduction in mortality from the district was used as an indirect indicator to suggest the continued improvement of care in the public health facilities in the district. Only 14 healthcare workers, who continued to work in the antenatal and labour ward before the pandemic and after triage implementation, were included, limiting the numbers for feedback.

Conclusion

A risk-appropriate maternity care-based triage model is implementable in a tertiary care hospital with a high childbirth volume. Triaging and referral to risk-appropriate lower levels of obstetric care optimises the number of childbirths in a tertiary care referral facility and may aid in improving the quality of care and maternal and child health indicators in the facility.
