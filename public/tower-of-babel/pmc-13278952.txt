Singing to the newborn brain uncovers early traces of specialized neural networks
Authors: Caterina Marino, Jessica Gemignani, Léo Varnet, Lorenzo Zanetto, Eugenio Baraldi, Judit Gervain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSong, a human cultural universal, fuses music and speech into a single auditory signal, one of the first among infants’ auditory experiences. While parents sing to their babies in all human cultures, we still know little about how this experience shapes babies’ perception abilities and their neural correlates. This work, therefore, investigates how the newborn brain processes song compared to its two components, speech and hummed melody. Specifically, we test the hypothesis that by regularizing the rhythm of speech, song may support language acquisition. Using functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS), we found that newborns’ (n = 40) showed stronger responses for both speech and song in the right temporoparietal regions than activation triggered by humming. Despite their clear acoustic distinctions, no significant differences were observed between the neural response of speech and song. This suggests that singing, just like speech, may represent a salient input for the newborn brain, potentially supporting early speech perception and language development.Subject terms: Development studies, Human behaviour, Language and linguistics

Introduction

In human communication, language and music represent the most abstract and structurally most complex use of sounds. Deeply rooted in human biology and cognition, these abilities are universally shared across human cultures and development1.

Humans begin perceiving external sounds during the third trimester of gestation, as the fetus’ auditory system is functional from around 25–29 weeks of gestation2. One dominant sound in the womb is maternal speech, attenuated in its higher frequencies by the amniotic fluid3. As a result, frequencies higher than 600–1000 Hz are filtered out, thus foetuses’ first experience with spoken language mainly consists of prosody, i.e., the rhythm and melody of speech, which are preserved by the low-pass filtering effect of the womb, while individual speech sounds and finer-grained phonetic details are mostly suppressed. This low-pass filtered speech signal already shapes babies’ speech perception abilities and their neural correlates. Thus, at birth, infants already process speech in sophisticated ways, and their brain already shows a privileged response to the prenatally heard language4–7.

Importantly, the prenatal auditory environment is not limited to speech, as other meaningful sounds, like maternal singing or music played by the mother, reach the fetus and elicit detectable responses8,9. Indeed, young infants exhibit early sensitivity to music, i.e., they recognize changes in melody10, show memory for songs most frequently heard during pregnancy11, and generally prefer consonant over dissonant sounds12. This suggests that the abilities to process linguistic and musical sounds both emerge early in development as fundamentals for human communication and social interaction13,14.

Song, present across all cultures of the world, combines speech and music. Its role as input to auditory development is thus particularly interesting15–17. Early exposure to singing may provide experience with rhythm, melody, and vocal patterns, underlying both speech and music. Singing shares several acoustic and prosodic features with the Infant-Directed Speech (IDS), such as exaggerated prosodic contours, slower tempo, higher pitch, and more regular rhythm18. These acoustic features are often employed in infant-caregiver interactions for socio-emotional development, bonding, emotional regulation, and learning19–21. This makes songs one of the most ecologically relevant models for investigating the shared development of language and music.

While relying on the same acoustic features, such as duration, intensity, and pitch, music and speech also differ along some key dimensions. Music uses fixed and discrete pitches patterned in established musical intervals, whereas speech tends to involve continuous, gliding tones that vary within syllables22. Further, the rhythm of songs is regular, periodic, and aligns with musical meter22,23, whereas speech has a less regular rhythm24. Also, speech has a faster tempo, with amplitude modulations occurring at 3.5–5.5 Hz, while song and music are modulated at 1–2 Hz25,26. These differences allow our auditory system to distinguish between speech and music, even when listening to noise-masked or ambiguous sounds25,26.

Given these differences between the basic underlying properties of speech and music, how do we perceive song, which blends linguistic and melodic information into the same acoustic signal27? Song has a strongly hierarchical structure, integrating syntactic and phonological information with musical rhythm and melody28,29. The complex nature of this acoustic signal requires sophisticated underlying brain mechanisms for its accurate perception30. In adults, listening to and producing songs engages bilateral brain regions, including the auditory cortex, in particular the Superior Temporal Gyrus (STG)31,32 in which a neural population responding selectively to singing has been recently identified33. Furthermore, sensorimotor areas34, motor regions35,36, and networks related to emotional processing, i.e., the amygdala and orbitofrontal cortex37,38 support song perception in adults. Recent studies further measured both shared and separate neural mechanisms, particularly in the prefrontal cortex in adults processing complex musical and linguistic rules39.

What is the developmental origin of this network? A handful of functional magnetic resonance imaging and functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) studies have attempted to investigate how the newborn brain responds to instrumental music. They found either lateralized40 or bilateral41 activation in the auditory cortices, alongside activation in the bilateral frontal cortices42 and the prefrontal cortex43, when newborns and young infants44 listened to instrumental music or digitally recorded tone sequences.

To date, however, very few studies have investigated the brain mechanisms underlying the perception of songs, i.e., music produced by the human voice, at birth45,46. This is a critical unanswered question, since both evolutionarily and ontogenetically, singing is the earliest and most readily available form of musical experience for most infants, which can be produced by any caregiver even in the absence of musical instruments or musical training, using simply the human voice, for which infants already have specialized neural responses47.

We hypothesize that beyond its affective and bonding functions, song may also serve as a powerful scaffolding or support for the development of speech and language16,17. By regularizing the rhythm and the pitch of speech, song provides a particularly well-structured input from which it may be easier for young infants to extract the sound patterns and regularities of language. Indeed, it has been shown that newborn infants readily extract patterns from regular, but not irregular, jittered sound sequences48 and that regularity supports auditory learning mechanisms at birth46. We thus hypothesize that song, somewhat similarly to IDS, may be a uniquely relevant input in supporting prosodic processing underpinning speech and language development.

This hypothesis is supported by the few existing studies16,17. In particular, a recent EEG study shows that the differential activation for sung versus speech at birth is predictive of expressive vocabulary at 18 months45. If our hypothesis is correct, we predict that newborns may process speech and song similarly despite their clear acoustic differences. We thus test how the newborn brain processes song and compare it to the processing of either of its components: speech and vocal music, i.e., hummed melody.

Accordingly, we collected hemodynamic brain responses using fNIRS from 40 full-term Italian newborns (age range 0–5 days, 19 males, 21 females). Infants were exposed to three different auditory conditions in intermixed blocks (Fig. 1A): (i) Speech in the prenatally heard language (i.e., Italian sentences from children’s stories read in infant-directed speech), (ii) Song (i.e., children’s songs in Italian), and (iii) Hummed melody (i.e., hummed melody taken from children’s songs different from those used for the Song condition and produced with no intelligible words). Hemodynamic responses were collected implementing a 20-channels configuration49 (Fig. 1C). Probes were localized over the inferior-middle frontal areas (channels: 1, 2, 4, 5 in the LH and 13, 14, 15, 16 in the RH), the temporal lobe including middle (MTG) and STG (channels: 3, 6 in the LH, 17, 19 in the RH), and the parietal lobe over the supramarginal, angular and central gyrus (channels: 7, 9, 10, 12 in the LH and 18, 20, 21, 23 in the RH), covering the classical auditory and language-related brain regions (for further detail see the “Methods” section).

All stimuli were naturally recorded instances produced by female native speakers of Italian. Stimuli were preserved in their ecological validity. None of the speakers was a professional singer or a trained speaker. From a methodological perspective, implementing naturalistic sounds was essential for mirroring the complexity of real-world listening experiences. These natural recordings preserved key features such as speaker/singer variability and dynamic fluctuations.

To confirm that these ecologically produced stimuli indeed show the acoustic differences known to distinguish music and speech, we conducted detailed analyses of their spectral and temporal characteristics, specifically their spectra, modulation spectra, and hierarchical organization or “nestedness” (for further details, see the “Methods” section).

Confirming the existence of acoustic differences between linguistic and musical input is essential to verify our central hypothesis. If the brain processes these different inputs with similar or shared networks from birth, this will corroborate the hypothesis that the rhythmic predictability and regularity of sung content could offer an opportunity to foster auditory and prosodic mechanisms supporting speech and language development.

Methods

Participants

70 healthy full-term (born at 37–42 weeks of gestation, 40 females) neonates were recruited and tested at the Maternity Ward of the Padua University Hospital in Padua, Italy. Of these, 30 were not included in the final analysis because they did not complete the experiment due to fussiness and/or crying (5), due to technical error (6), or did not provide analyzable data due to movement artifacts (19). All data rejection was performed automatically in batch prior to statistical analysis. The remaining 40 babies (38–42 weeks of gestation, 21 females, 19 males, age range 0–5 days, mean age: 2.25 days, mean birth weight 3344 g, range: 2763−4200 g, Apgar score 5 min after birth: min. 9, normal otoacoustic emissions test; 80% of Italian exposure prenatally, no known neurological or hearing abnormalities) were included in the final analysis. No data on SES, communities of descent, or ethnicity were collected. The gender of the participant was determined relying on the birth records provided by the hospital.

Ethics approval

Parents of all participating newborns gave written informed consent prior to participation. The experiment was approved by the ethics board of the Padua University Hospital (Comitato Etico della Sperimentazione Clinica (CESC) dell’Azienda Ospedale-Università Padova; CESC REF: 5300/AO/22). Figure 1B shows a picture of a newborn participant with signed parental consent for photo publication.

Stimuli

Newborns were presented with three different auditory conditions: (i) spoken sentences in Italian (Speech); (ii) Italian children’s songs (Songs), and (iii) hummed melodies of Italian children’s songs different from those used in the Song condition (Hummed melody). All stimuli originate from existing children’s stories and songs in Italian, unaltered in their linguistic and/or melodic structure (see Table 1 of SI for further information). Spoken sentences carried Italian rhythm and pitch pattern, songs and hummed melodies conformed to the rules of Western music. Hummed melodies were sung without intelligible words (i.e., with a closed mouth). Stimuli were recorded by five different Italian native speakers who were instructed to use a natural, mild infant-directed speech, and singing. To minimize the presence of explicit harmonic dissonances, for which newborns show early sensitivity50, both songs and hummed melodies were sung monophonically, i.e., a cappella, using voice without instrumental accompaniment.

Comparison of duration, intensity and mean pitch revealed slight significant differences among the stimuli (Fisher test: duration: F(2, 15) = 3.83, p = 0.045; post hocs: all non-significant; intensity: F(2, 15) = 4,35, p = 0.032; post hocs: Song–Hummed melody: p = 0.046); Mean pitch: (Welch test: F(2, 7.71) = 6.23, p = 0.025) due to the difference between Speech and Hummed melody (post hocs: p = 0.049). These pitch values fall within the typical acoustic range of the adult female voice (165–255 Hz) and are consistent with previous findings reporting higher pitch and slower tempo for singing and humming than for speech in naturalistic productions51. This supports the ecological validity of our stimuli as representative instances of natural speech, humming, and song (for further details, see Table 1).

To confirm that our stimuli indeed show the characteristic temporal and spectral properties known to distinguish music and speech, we performed detailed acoustic analyses of the stimuli (Fig. 2). Three analyses were carried out: (i) the spectrum, (ii) the amplitude (AM) and fundamental frequency (f0M) modulation spectra, and (iii) rhythmic nestedness. The stimuli were divided into 3-s-long segments for computational ease. Hummed melody and Song showed lower-frequency spectral energy and more regular rhythmic patterns than Speech, particularly around the 4 Hz beat corresponding to the eighth note musical tempo. In contrast, Speech featured greater variability in pitch and amplitude modulations, reflecting the dynamic nature of prosody. Speech also displayed higher rhythmic nestedness, suggesting a more complex hierarchical organization of rhythmic structures compared to Song and Hummed melody.

Spectrum

The Fourier spectra of the three conditions were computed to characterize the frequency contents of the stimuli. The three types of stimuli differed in their Fourier spectrum, with Hummed melody and Song exhibiting spectral energy concentrated at lower frequencies than in Speech. Accordingly, the measured timbral brightness (spectral centroid) was different between the three conditions (F(2, 9.2821) = 9.6039; p = 0.0055), and consistent with the values reported by Ozaki et al.51.

Modulation spectra

The amplitude modulation (AM) and f0 modulation (f0M) spectra, capturing the amount of envelope fluctuations and pitch fluctuations at various modulation frequencies, respectively, were computed to characterize the rhythmical structure of the stimuli. They were computed using the TSMT toolbox52, following the analysis pipeline described in Varnet et al.53. The AM spectra of Hummed melodies and (to a lesser extent) that of Songs revealed a marked rhythm at 4 Hz. This 4 Hz beat corresponds to the eighth-note tempo used by the singers, indicating that Hummed melodies and to some extent Song have a more regular rhythm than Speech. Because of the variability in the Speech condition, the mean AM peak was not significantly different between the three conditions (F(2, 9.1450) = 1.5614; p = 0.2610). In the fast modulation rates range (>5 Hz), Hummed melodies show a steep decline, while Speech and Song stimuli exhibit a richer content, indicating the presence of complex stress patterns and rubato timing, resulting in the broader and more variable modulation profiles (F(2, 8.8545) = 91.6660; p = 1.2098e-06). This can also be observed in the distribution of peaks in the individual AM spectra of the segments. Furthermore, the AMi spectrum, an alternative to the classical AM spectrum that better approximates the cochlea’s perceptual sensitivities53 resulted in estimations of best modulation frequency and bandwidth consistent with prior results from Varnet et al.53 and Ding et al.54 on speech stimuli (Supplementary Material, Fig. S1).

F0 modulation spectra were very similar across the Hummed and Song conditions. However, the Speech condition exhibited a different pattern, with larger fluctuations above 1 Hz (F(2, 8.7029) = 33.5362; p = 8.1327e-05) while fluctuations in the lower-frequency range remained comparable across all conditions. These enhanced modulations in Speech reflect the reduced pitch stability of prosodic contours, compared to melodic contours51.

Rhythm nestedness

We also computed a metric of “rhythm nestedness,” originally developed by Leong et al.55 to assess phase alignment between stress and syllable rhythms. We here adapted and extended it to capture nestedness across all rhythms in speech: prosody (0.5–0.8 Hz), stress (0.8–2.3 Hz), syllable (2.3–7 Hz), subbeat (7–20 Hz), and phonemes (20–50 Hz). The purpose of this analysis was to go beyond simple linear rhythm and explore any hierarchical relationship between different rhythmic units that are typically present in music and speech. Results indicate that Speech stimuli exhibited greater rhythmic nestedness than both Humming and Song (F(2, 8.5390) = 4.3793; p = 0.0491), possibly reflecting what is known as the prosodic hierarchy56,57, i.e., the hierarchical organization of rhythmic units at different levels in Speech.

Procedure

Newborns were tested in a state of sleep or quiet rest while lying in their cribs in a quiet room of the Maternity Ward of the Padua University Hospital. At least one parent was present throughout the testing session.

The experiment consisted of a total of 18 blocks, 6 blocks per condition. Blocks had a duration of 15.3 s on average (range: 13.2–17.2 s), and the between-block silence intervals silent intervals lasted 25, 27, or 30 s. The order of presentation of the blocks was pseudorandomized and counterbalanced across participants. The experiment lasted approx. 13 min.

Hemodynamic responses were measured with an NIRx NIRSport 2 NIRS system (source-detector separation of 3 cm; pulsated LED lights at two wavelengths of 760 and 850 nm; sampling rate of 20.3252 Hz). The optical probes, 8 sources and 8 detectors were inserted into a stretchy cap in a 20-channel configuration (10 per hemisphere) probing bilateral frontal, temporal and parietal areas, as in previous newborns NIRS studies5,49, NIRS channels were positioned over the following brain areas as follow: channels 1, 2, 4, 5 (LH) and 13, 14, 15, 16 (RH) over the frontal lobe; channels 3, 6 (LH) and 17, 19 (RH) over the temporal lobe; channels 7, 10, 12 (LH) and 18, 20, 23 (RH) query the parietal lobe; finally, channels 9 and 21 spans over both the temporal and parietal lobes, in the left and right hemisphere, respectively. The stimuli were presented through two loudspeakers elevated to the height of the newborns’ crib and positioned at a 1 m distance from the newborns’ head at an angle of 30°. A computer running E-Prime delivered the stimuli and sent time stamps to the NIRS machine.

Data analysis

Pre-processing

We used the modified Beer-Lambert Law to convert light intensities into optical densities, and then to oxygenated (HbO) and deoxygenated (HbR) concentration changes. The following absorption coefficients (μa, mM−1 × mm−1) were used: μa(HbO, 760 nm) = 0.1496, μa(HbO, 850 nm) = 0.2526, μa(HbR, 760 nm) = 0.3865 and μa(HbR, 850 nm) = 0.1798. The product of the differential pathlength factor and the optical pathlength was set to 1, resulting in concentration changes expressed in mM × mm.

Subsequently, concentration changes were band-pass filtered between 0.01 and 0.7 Hz using a digital fft filter. These cut-off frequencies were applied to suppress very low frequency components, responsible for slow drifts in the signal, as well as heart rate and other higher frequency physiological noise. A routine was then implemented for examining data quality in each channel-block pair. A channel-block pair was rejected if the light intensity reached the saturation value of 1.2 V, if the block contained motion artifacts (i.e., signal changes larger than 0.1 mM × mm over 200 ms), or both.

For the remaining non-rejected blocks, a baseline was linearly fit between the means of the 5 s preceding the onset of the stimulation and the 5 s starting 20 s after the offset of stimulation, to allow enough time for the HRF to return to baseline. This pre-processing pipeline is similar to those previously used in our laboratory49,56,57 and recently validated58. No study preregistration was performed.

Statistical analysis

To assess functional activations to each condition, we first conducted cluster-based permutation analyses59 (as is standardly used in infant fNIRS studies60,61) with t-tests comparing the hemodynamic response for a given condition to a zero baseline on oxygenated hemoglobin (HbO) and deoxygenated hemoglobin (HbR) separately. This non-parametric statistical analysis identifies regions and time windows of interest in a data-driven manner while avoiding the multiple comparison problem, and it has been employed in several NIRS studies60,61.

To assess whether the newborn brain discriminates between the three conditions, we ran the same cluster-based permutation analysis with a repeated measures ANOVA with the within-subject factor Condition (F critical = 3.114, number of permutations = 1000). Clusters displaying a significant effect of Condition were followed up with permutation tests performing pair-wise comparisons between the conditions using t-tests (t critical = 2.022, number of permutations = 1000). In all analyses, HbO and HbR were analyzed separately.

Further, to assess potential effects of habituation throughout the time course of the experiment, we extracted the mean concentration change for each trial from the clusters identified by the permutation test and submitted this trial-by-trial data to a mixed effects model. The model contained the main effect of the within-subject fixed factors Condition (Speech/Song/Hummed melody), Trial Number (1–6), and their interaction, and a random intercept for Participant. The data meet the assumptions of the statistical tests.

The corresponding effect sizes, calculated as partial eta-squared and Cohen’s d, respectively, were computed using the effectsize package. Mixed models were implemented in R using the lmer package, and pair-wise comparisons between slopes were investigated using the emtrends function of the emmeans package.

Discussion

The present study investigated how singing to young babies, a human cultural universal, may shape infants’ perceptual abilities. To answer this question, we recorded brain activity with fNIRS while newborns were exposed to ecologically valid, naturally produced speech, songs, and humming, highly common sounds in babies’ environments. Our results revealed that speech, song, and hummed melodies elicit distinct activation patterns already at birth, with song being unique in integrating linguistic and melodic information through a bilateral engagement of temporoparietal regions. Further, despite the acoustic analyses confirming the hierarchical complex nature of speech and the rhythmically regular nature of songs, no significant differences were found in how the newborn brain processes them.

More specifically, our findings revealed significant activation patterns compared to baseline for each of the three conditions, indicating that newborns differentially respond to speech, song, and humming at birth. Songs engaged left temporal and right temporoparietal networks, thus integrating regions responding to both linguistic and melodic cues, while hummed melodies produced responses in the left frontotemporal and right frontal regions. This widespread bilateral activation closely mirrors results from adult studies using similar experimental conditions27,63. Speech-related activation was observed exclusively in the right temporoparietal regions. This contrasts with findings from some newborn studies that report left-lateralized language responses5–7. However, as stable hemispheric lateralization takes time to develop64, it is not uncommon for newborns to show bilateral or even right-lateralized responses to speech65,66. Moreover, newborns’ neural responses to speech have been shown to be sensitive to contextual factors, such as the other experimental conditions together with which speech is presented65. In the current study, the combined presentation of speech, song, and humming may have emphasized prosodic—melodic and rhythmic—features of language, which are typically processed by the right hemisphere in both infants40,67,68 and adults69–71.

Second, to test the hypothesis that by regularizing the rhythmic and melodic patterns of language, song may support speech perception, e.g., through shared neural mechanisms involved in prosodic processing, we analyzed the temporal and spectral features of our stimuli and compared newborns’ brain responses across the three conditions. Our acoustic analyses confirmed that speech, song, and humming all have different spectral-temporal structures, converging with existing results25,26,51. Humming features a highly regular rhythm and relatively simple spectral content concentrated in the low frequencies. Speech exhibits a more irregular rhythm, richer spectral content, and a high degree of nestedness on multiple temporal scales, i.e., hierarchical complexity. Song lies in between, balancing these acoustic characteristics. Relevantly for our hypothesis, song, like humming, but unlike speech, shows a regular beat and a lower level of rhythm nestedness, indicating a simpler rhythmic structure. Despite these acoustic differences, crucially, the newborn brain shows heightened, while not significantly different, activation in the right temporoparietal areas in responding to both speech and song. Responses to humming were inverted and significantly different from the other two conditions. Furthermore, while speech and song showed habituation effect this heightened response was maintained for song throughout the study, while the other two conditions showed habituation effects. This sustained neural stability could be further supported by the presence of two co-occurring regularities in songs, in which musical and linguistic statistical patterns are present simultaneously72,73, thus facilitating and maintaining the processing of the acoustic signal.

Taken together, these results imply that no significant differences were observed when the newborn brain responds to naturally recorded extracts of speech and song at birth. The greater right activation of the differential response with humming suggests that processing may occur at the prosodic level. Indeed, through prenatal experience, which consists mainly of low-frequency prosodic information3,74, newborns have been shown to be particularly sensitive to prosody49,75. Importantly, since a song can trigger sustained activation unlike the other two stimuli, it offers a particularly powerful and suitable opportunity for learning.

An objection could be made, arguing that hummed melodies might resemble what babies perceive in the prenatal auditory environment in that their spectral content is in the low frequency range. However, their extremely regular rhythmic structure makes them clearly different from the low-pass filtered speech stimuli heard in the womb3,74. Thus, despite similarly reduced high-frequency contents, hummed melodies differ considerably from the sounds experienced prenatally, both in terms of spectral realization and rhythmic predictability. This may contribute to explaining why neural responses to humming appear very different from those evoked by speech or songs.

Taken together, our findings suggest that the newborn brain is already sensitive to the acoustic differences characterizing speech, song, and hummed melody. This fine-grained sensitivity shapes both the magnitude and the temporal engagement of neural responses. The greater right hemispheric engagement converges well with previous studies investigating both prosodic processing abilities67,68 and music perception40 in very young infants. Songs seem special in this regard; their more regular melodic and rhythmic contents, embedded in a simpler linguistic structure than spoken language, may support predictability, engagement, and perceptual fine-tuning. Just as the rhythmic and prosodic contents of speech are perceived in utero75, maternal singing likely plays a role in fostering the readiness of neural networks for speech perception even before birth.

Limitations

For a broader generalizability of these findings, certain limitations should be acknowledged. First, the main goal of the study was to investigate how the neonatal brain processes the real acoustic experience, using fully naturalistic and hence highly variable and mostly unedited stimuli. Although this approach allows for the examination of neural activation to ecologically valid stimuli, it cannot identify the specific acoustic cues or features that newborns use to process each of these sound categories, which further studies using synthesized stimuli will be able to address in the future. Importantly, the main objective of the study was to measure brain mechanisms supporting the global processing differences between speech, song, and hummed melodies in newborns rather than isolating acoustic, semantic, linguistic, and melodic features intrinsic in their nature. This makes the current findings highly relevant for a greater understanding of perceptual development in the real acoustic environment of infants from birth onwards.

Furthermore, we only used speech and songs in one language. Future studies will need to address whether our results are generalizable to speech and song from different languages and cultures.
