The Ricci tensor of the metric (

A special class of pp-metrics are the so-called

More generally, it can be shown

u(s)=as+b,\qquad s\in\mathbb{R},

1.3
The Ehlers–Kundt conjecture

In their 1962 article
[
13
, pp. 97]
, Ehlers and Kundt proposed the following problem, suggesting that plane waves play a privileged role among pp-waves:

“Prove the plane waves to be the only
g
-complete pp waves, no matter which topology one chooses.”

This statement is now referred to as “the Ehlers–Kundt conjecture.” Intuitively speaking, a complete, Ricci-flat pp-wave describes a gravitational field without singularities (i.e. it is geodesically complete) and without internal matter sources (i.e. it satisfies the vacuum Einstein equations). By analogy with Maxwell’s equations in vacuum, where the simplest solutions are monochromatic plane waves, the Ehlers–Kundt conjecture asserts that the gravitational plane waves play the corresponding role as the most elementary, globally regular, source-free gravitational fields.

In a more precise formulation, noting that only Ricci-flat metrics are considered in
[
13
]
, the conjecture can be stated as follows.

Conjecture 1.5

(Ehlers–Kundt)
.

A Ricci-flat pp-wave
(\mathbb{R}^{4},g)
, with metric

\mathbf{g}=2du\,dv+H(u,\mathbf{x})\,du^{2}+dx^{2}+dy^{2},

where
H\colon\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
, is geodesically complete if and only if the function
\mathbf{x}\mapsto H(u,\mathbf{x})
is polynomial of degree at most two, for all
u\in\mathbb{R}
.

Remark 1.6
.

The geodesic equations for the plane wave metrics can be integrated directly,
7
7

7

Up to integration of a linear second-order system of ordinary differential equations.
and show that plane wave the metrics are geodesically complete. (See
[
13
, Theorem 2-5.9]
or
[
6
, Chapter 13]

While quadratic behavior of
H
has been successfully shown to be critical for various geometric properties of the pp-wave spacetime, e.g. asymptotic flatness and causality (cf.
[
15
]
), the Ehlers–Kundt conjecture remains an open problem (cf.
[

, Section 5]
).

1.4
Positive results

A special case of the Ehlers–Kundt conjecture, the so-called
polynomial Ehlers–Kundt conjecture
, in which
H
is assumed to be bounded by a polynomial, has been solved by Flores and Sánchez in
[
16
]
. They work in the setting of dynamical systems and, more precisely, assume
V=-\frac{1}{2}H
to be polynomially
u
-bounded.

Definition 1.7
.

A function
V\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is
polynomially
u
-bounded
if, for all
u\in\mathbb{R}
, there exists an
\varepsilon_{0}
and a polynomial
q_{0}\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]
such that, for all
(x,y,u)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times(u_{0}-\varepsilon_{0},u_{0}+\varepsilon_{0})
, we have

V(x,y,u)\leq q_{0}(x,y).

With this notion at hand, we can state the polynomial Ehlers–Kundt conjecture as follows.

Theorem 1.8

(Polynomial Ehlers–Kundt Conjecture)
.

Let
(\mathbb{R}^{4},\mathbf{g})
be a pp-wave with the properties that

(i)

the function
V=-\frac{1}{2}H
is in
\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R})
and polynomially
u
-bounded;

(ii)

for all
u\in\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbf{x}\mapsto V(\mathbf{x},u)
is in
\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and harmonic.

Then
(\mathbb{R}^{4},\mathbf{g})
is geodesically complete if and only if
V(\cdot,\cdot,u)
is an at most quadratic polynomial for all
u\in\mathbb{R}
, i.e.
(\mathbb{R}^{4},g)
is a plane wave.

If
V
is autonomous and homogeneous (i.e. all terms are of the highest degree) then, by a suitable choice of polar coordinates, it can be written as

V(\rho,\theta)=-\rho^{n}\cos(n\theta).

In this case, it can be shown that there exist neighbourhoods
D_{k}[\rho_{0},\frac{\pi}{2n}]
of the radial trajectories
\gamma_{k}(s)=(\rho(s),\theta_{k})
for
\theta_{k}=\frac{2\pi k}{n}
, such that trajectories starting in
D_{k}[\rho_{0},\frac{\pi}{2n}]
stay within this region and are incomplete.

For the non-homogeneous and non-autonomous case, the proof works analogously by constructing appropriate radial curves
\gamma_{k}
and regions
D[\rho_{0},\theta_{+}]
with the corresponding properties.

As stated in
[
16
, pp. 7509]
, and briefly alluded to above, the natural setting for the Ehlers–Kundt conjecture (and for the more general problems discussed in
[
16
]
) is where the potential function
V\equiv-\frac{1}{2}H
is
C^{1}
in
u
, in order that the geodesic equations for the metric
\mathbf{g}
make sense classically, and
C^{2}
in the spatial variables, in order that the spatial-harmonicity condition on
H
be classically well-defined. What we will show is that, for
impulsive
waves, where the function
H
is not continuous in
u
, the Ehlers–Kundt conjecture fails.

2.4
Nonlinear distributional geometry

We now give a short but self-contained account of nonlinear distributional Lorentzian geometry. (See
[
29
,
30
]
and
[
24
, Section 3.2]
) for details.) The construction is based on Colombeau’s (special) algebra of generalised functions
[
12
]
, which provides an extension of Schwartz’s theory of distributions
[
18
]
that has maximal consistency with respect to classical analysis. It is based on regularisation of distributions via nets of smooth functions and asymptotic estimates in terms of a regularisation parameter.

We first define
{\mathcal{E}}(M)
to be the set of all nets of smooth functions
(u_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
on a manifold
M
that depend smoothly on
\varepsilon
. The
algebra of generalised functions on
M
is defined as the quotient
{\mathcal{G}}(M)\coloneqq{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{M}}(M)/{\mathcal{N}}(M)
of
moderate
modulo
negligible
nets in
{\mathcal{E}}(M)
, defined via

{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{M}}(M)\coloneqq\{(u_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}\in{\mathcal{E}}(M):\,\forall K\Subset M\ \forall P\in{\mathcal{P}}\ \exists N:\ \sup\limits_{p\in K}|Pu_{\varepsilon}(p)|=O(\varepsilon^{-N})\}\,,\\

{\mathcal{N}}(M)\coloneqq\{(u_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}\in{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{M}}(M):\ \forall K\Subset M\ \forall m:\ \sup\limits_{p\in K}|u_{\varepsilon}(p)|=O(\varepsilon^{m})\}\,.

Here
K\Subset M
means that
K
is compact and
{\mathcal{P}}
denotes the space of linear differential operators on
M
. Elements
u=[(u_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}]
of
{\mathcal{G}}(M)
are referred to as
generalised functions with representative
(u_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
and sums, products, and the Lie derivative of
u
are defined componentwise (i.e., for fixed
\varepsilon
). It can be shown that
{\mathcal{G}}(M)
is a fine sheaf of differential algebras.
Generalised tensor fields
on
M
are defined as tensor fields with
{\mathcal{G}}(M)
-coefficients, i.e.

{\mathcal{G}}^{r}_{s}(M)={\mathcal{G}}(M)\otimes_{\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(M)}{\mathcal{T}}^{r}_{s}(M).

(2.16)

Here
{\mathcal{T}}^{r}_{s}(M)
is the space of smooth
(r,s)
-tensor fields on
M
and
{\mathcal{G}}^{r}_{s}(M)
is a fine sheaf of finitely generated and projective
{\mathcal{G}}
-modules.

Distributions
u\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(M)
can be embedded into
{\mathcal{G}}(M)
via convolution (as in (
2.4
)) giving rise to a sheaf homomorphism,
\iota
, that is consistent with the product of smooth functions. A coarser way of relating generalised functions to distribution is as follows: We say that
u,v\in{\mathcal{G}}(M)
are
associated
to one another,
u\approx v
, if
u_{\varepsilon}-v_{\varepsilon}\to 0
weakly. We say that

is

if

One benefit of this framework is that one may work componentwise (i.e. for fixed
\varepsilon
) as in the classical smooth setting. In particular, since products are well defined, one may insert generalised vector fields and one-forms into generalised tensor fields, which is not possible in the distributional setting, cf.
[
35
]
. More explicitly we adopt the following definitions.

Conceptually, completeness ceases to be a rigidity criterion in the presence of a distributional wave profile. This not only settles the natural “impulsive EK” analogue in the negative, but also underscores the subtle role played by regularity in the geometric and dynamical characterizations of exact wave spacetimes.
