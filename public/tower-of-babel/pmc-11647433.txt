A deep‐learning‐based histopathology classifier for focal cortical dysplasia (FCD) unravels a complex scenario of comorbid FCD subtypes
Authors: Jörg Vorndran, Ingmar Blümcke
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveRecently, we developed a first artificial intelligence (AI)‐based digital pathology classifier for focal cortical dysplasia (FCD) as defined by the ILAE classification. Herein, we tested the usefulness of the classifier in a retrospective histopathology workup scenario.MethodsEighty‐six new cases with histopathologically confirmed FCD ILAE type Ia (FCDIa), FCDIIa, FCDIIb, mild malformation of cortical development with oligodendroglial hyperplasia in epilepsy (MOGHE), or mild malformations of cortical development were selected, 20 of which had confirmed gene mosaicism.ResultsThe classifier always recognized the correct histopathology diagnosis in four or more 1000 × 1000‐μm digital tiles in all cases. Furthermore, the final diagnosis overlapped with the largest batch of tiles assigned by the algorithm to one diagnostic entity in 80.2% of all cases. However, 86.2% of all cases revealed more than one diagnostic category. As an example, FCDIIb was identified in all of the 23 patients with histopathologically assigned FCDIIb, whereas the classifier correctly recognized FCDIIa tiles in 19 of these cases (83%), that is, dysmorphic neurons but no balloon cells. In contrast, the classifier misdiagnosed FCDIIb tiles in seven of 23 cases histopathologically assigned to FCDIIa (33%). This mandates a second look by the signing histopathologist to either confirm balloon cells or differentiate from reactive astrocytes. The algorithm also recognized coexisting architectural dysplasia, for example, vertically oriented microcolumns as in FCDIa, in 22% of cases classified as FCDII and in 62% of cases with MOGHE. Microscopic review confirmed microcolumns in the majority of tiles, suggesting that vertically oriented architectural abnormalities are more common than previously anticipated.SignificanceAn AI‐based diagnostic classifier will become a helpful tool in our future histopathology laboratory, in particular when large anatomical resections from epilepsy surgery require extensive resources. We also provide an open access web application allowing the histopathologist to virtually review digital tiles obtained from epilepsy surgery to corroborate their final diagnosis.

INTRODUCTION

Neuropathology‐based classification schemes have been developed for almost all major disease categories including brain tumors by the World Health Organization, with the latest edit in 2021, 1 and focal cortical dysplasia (FCD) by the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) and updated in 2022. 2 Continuous editing is necessary to include contemporary research and to reflect our current insight into the underlying disease pathomechanism, for example, driving gene mutations in cancer or mTOR pathway signaling in FCD. 3 Finally, multilayered diagnostic schemes were established integrating histopathology findings with neuroimaging and/or genetic testing of resected brain tissue. 2 Notwithstanding, microscopic diagnosis of neurosurgically resected human brain tissue remains the gold standard, and the panel of mandatory laboratory tools grows continuously. These include costly antibody panels, genetic testing, or DNA methylation arrays. 4 It is worrisome, however, that increasing numbers of patients will have no access to such personalized tools in modern medicine, in particular when from resource‐poor countries.

An artificial intelligence (AI)‐driven approach is one possible solution to cover the gap and increasing disparity across societies and countries. Given worldwide access to the internet to transfer large medical data files, that is, whole slide images in digital pathology, an AI‐based disease classifier can remotely offer state‐of‐the‐art histopathology diagnosis from routine hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stainings of formalin‐fixed and paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) neurosurgical tissue samples. Despite the commercial value and competition among laboratory companies and microscope vendors to develop such digital slide suites, the international research community can also provide their expertise and knowledge via an open access AI‐trained disease classifier.

In 2023, we developed a first AI‐based classifier for FCD 5 claiming to recognize many diagnostic entities defined in the FCD classification update from 2022. 2 Our algorithm was trained with digital tiles automatically extracted from whole slide images (WSIs) of manually annotated, H&E‐stained samples assigned to FCD ILAE type Ia (FCDIa), FCDIIa, FCDIIb, mild malformations of cortical development (mMCD), and mild malformation of cortical development with oligodendroglial hyperplasia in epilepsy (MOGHE), namely, using the ResNet18 convolutional deep learning network. Postmortem tissue blocks from patients without neurological disorders were used as controls, including homotypic frontal, temporal, and occipital areas and heterotypic Brodmann areas 4 and 17. Overall, the algorithm's accuracy was 98.8% in detecting 21 different anatomical regions and FCD categories. 5 The classifier was also made available as an open access web application.

Herein, we tested our FCD classifier with a series of newly acquired cases with histopathologically confirmed FCD. All of these cases underwent the laborious immunohistochemistry panel workup according to the ILAE protocol 6 as well as genetic testing in selected cases. 7 The rationale was to challenge our previous classifier with additional cases and available genetic testing results as well as a potentially complex histopathology disease scenario. The coexistence of multiple FCD subtypes was never addressed systematically and thus not reflected in the ILAE classification scheme, however. 2 , 8

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patients included into this study

We retrieved 831 FFPE tissue blocks of neurosurgically resected brain specimens from 86 individuals submitted to epilepsy surgery and stored in the department's archive. The following diagnoses were included and histopathologically corroborated according to the ILAE classification scheme of 2022 2 , 8 : mMCD (n = 5), MOGHE (n = 31), FCDIa (n = 4), FCDIIa (n = 23), and FCDIIb (n = 23; Table 1). Twenty of these patients had a confirmed brain tissue mosaicism of pathogenic variants as published elsewhere 7 , 9 : one individual with NPRL3, one with MTOR, and three with DEPDC5 variants in FCDIIa; six individuals with MTOR and one with NF1 or PTEN variants in FCDIIb; and seven individuals with SLC35A2 variants. None of these individuals was included in the previously published cohort of 125 patients used to train the deep‐learning‐based FCD classifier. 5 Microscopic sections were cut at a thickness of 3–5 μm and stained with H&E according to previously published routine neuropathology workup protocols in epilepsy surgery. 6 A Hamamatsu S60 scanner (Hamamatsu Photonics Europe) was used to generate digital WSIs with a volume of .32–5.58 GB per WSI. The total data volume of the test dataset was approximately 2.9 TB. The ethical review board of the University of Erlangen approved the study under agreement number 193_18B.

Hardware and software configuration

Data extraction, preparation, storage, and the required arithmetic operations were implemented on a local computer unit equipped with AMD CPU (AMD Ryzen Threadripper 1900X 3.8 GHz 8‐Core) and 64 Gb of RAM, running on Ubuntu Operating System 22.04.4 LTS (code: Jammy Jellyfish). The tensor calculations were performed on a Gigabyte GeForce RTX 3090 with 24 GB GPU storage, Cuda version 12.2. The input data for the model calculation were stored and provided via removable SSD hard drives with a total storage capacity of 10 TB.

In contrast to the previously published FCD classifier, the current model was trained on a ResNet152 convolutional neuronal network architecture (https://doi.org/10.1109/CVPR.2016.90, last visited April 18, 2024), pretrained on the ImageNet dataset (https://doi.org/10.1109/CVPR.2009.5206848; data not shown). Similar to our previous model, training was realized by the open source Python package and AI project fastai (https://doi.org/10.3390/info11020108, last visited June 10, 2022), for which tensor operations are based on the pytorch backend (https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1912.01703, last visited June 15, 2022). The basic network architecture was not changed and was frozen during the training process, which means none of the pretrained weights inside the model's body was changed by our training process. Our model‐specific classifier head consisted mainly of two averaging layers, followed by one flattened layer, two intermediate dense layers, and the final output layer and received as input a block of 2048 activations with a dimension of 32 × 32 pixels. This resulted in 2 177 632 trainable parameters to adapt and optimize the model to our classification task. Data management and evaluation were supported by a previously published open access library. 10

We used the same tile extraction and evaluation pipeline as previously reported. 5 In brief, a code script was developed to automatically place grids over the original WSI (Figure S1A). Each tile of this grid corresponded to 1000 × 1000 μm at a resolution of 1024 pix. 2 A coordinate transformation of the original image to the Openslide‐Image coordinate system was implemented (https://doi.org/10.4103/2153‐3539.119005) to extract a total of 447 006 tiles from our current patient cohort. All tiles were automatically fed into our model in a batch processing routine for evaluation. Classification results including predicted categories (see glossary in Data S1 of 21 trained categories) and confidence values were stored at the tile, slide, and case level and later evaluated at the case level as well as transformed in color‐coded prediction maps at the slide level (Figure S1B).

To improve transparency of the model's decision‐making process, we adapted a discriminatory approach as described at https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1512.04150 to visualize the common patterns of tissue texture trained in the model as a heat map, which can help us guide the microscopic examination of a surgical specimen. The class activation method (CAM) amplified the activations of the model's body with respect to the predicted class. By superimposition, the three‐dimensional activation matrix was converted into a two‐dimensional salience map, that is, a heatmap, by using color assignment.

RESULTS

We fed our classifier with 831 FFPE tissue blocks and HE‐stained microscopy glass slides thereof obtained from 86 individuals suffering from drug‐resistant focal epilepsy and submitted to epilepsy surgery not included into our previous FCD classifier study. 5 The algorithm extracted 444 642 tiles and assigned them to any of the predefined and previously reported 21 disease categories (see glossary in Data S1). 5 The classifier's confidence score was set to .8, which was met in 5.9% of all tiles (Table 1). These tiles were used for further analysis. Interestingly, the classifier always recognized the correct histopathology diagnosis in at least four tiles in each of the tested cases, including those carrying a known pathogenic gene variant. The minimum number of correctly assigned tiles ranged thereby from four to 176 tiles per case. At the individual case level, however, the final diagnosis overlapped with the largest batch of tiles assigned by the algorithm to one diagnostic entity in 80.2% of all cases (Table S2). We used this method herein, that is, the majority of tiles confirmed the diagnosis (MTCD), to classify a given case.

We then visualized the common patterns of tissue texture trained in the model using the CAM. The generated heatmap was then overlaid with the original image to highlight discriminative regions of increased importance for the model's decision (Figures 1, 2, 3).

The MTCD method did not achieve a correct final diagnosis in 19.7% of all cases (Table S2). Manual review of corresponding tiles identified three major challenges for the classifier in need of further consideration. Eight cases of FCDIIb contained numerically more tiles of FCDIIa, that is, just dysmorphic neurons, than tiles including balloon cells (Table 1). This resulted in the most common failure to achieve a correct diagnosis, namely, 39.1% of cases with FCDIIb (Table S2). Peer review of tiles recognized balloon cells in all the FCDIIb tiles. This is a necessary action by the signing neuropathologist, therefore, to achieve a correct diagnosis in FCDIIb cases and is supported by our new web application (see below). Our case series also included eight samples with a positive genetic testing of MTOR, NF1, or PTEN genes. Balloon‐cell‐containing tiles were correctly detected in all of these genetically defined cases, with five also correctly assigned as FCDIIb by MTCD. In the opposite direction, a total of 92 tiles of our series were erroneously assigned to FCDIIb by the algorithm (.1%). Manual review could not confirm the presence of dysmorphic neurons or balloon cells in any of these tiles (Figure 2). Some revealed enlarged, gemistocytic astrocytes or tissue edema in the vicinity of intracerebral electroencephalographic recording trajectories (in four cases), astroglial scarring as a result of a previous surgery (in two cases), or confusion of balloon cells with dysmorphic neurons (two cases). Such FCDIIb‐mimicking tiles were detected in a total of seven cases of FCDIIa and five cases of MOGHE, but none in mMCD or FCDIa. Moreover, the false tile assignment did not challenge the final diagnosis when using MTCD.

The second most common challenge was the erroneous detection of dysmorphic neurons, that is, FCDIIa, in 320 tiles of 28 cases of MOGHE, FCDIa, and mMCD compared to 8502 tiles correctly assigned to FCDII. This would have changed the diagnosis in one case of MOGHE and one of mMCD. Our new web application was designed to preselect such diagnoses for a peer microscopy review. In none of these tiles could a diagnosis of FCDIIa be achieved. Instead, the algorithm falsely assigned hypertrophic pyramidal neurons, which can be recognized by the correct orientation of the apical dendrites and their localization in layers 3 or 5. Small clusters of neurons as well as perisurgical hypoxic damage, for example, condensed nuclei in an otherwise more reddish cytoplasm, were also common distractors of the algorithm's decision‐making (Figure 3). In addition, all cases with FCDIIa and genetically proven brain mosaicism in DEPDC5, NPRL3, or MTOR were correctly assigned to FCDIIa by MTCD.

A third challenge was the occurrence of abundant microcolumns coexisting with FCDII or MOGHE in our case cohort (Figure 4). The algorithm was trained for this pattern by using samples of histopathologically confirmed FCDIa, and we therefore kept this diagnosis also in the context of comorbid FCD pathologies. The classifier recognized 1081 tiles assigned to FCDIa in four cases of FCDIIa (17%) and six cases of FCDIIb (23%), four of which also carried genetically detected pathogenic mTOR pathway variants. Microscopic review confirmed the presence of a vertically oriented architectural dysplasia suggesting a broader and complex spectrum of FCD disease pathologies. It was interesting to note that such FCDIa patterns were detected either remote from or adjacent to the principal lesion, for example, FCDIIa or FCDIIb (Figure 5). In addition, 23 cases of MOGHE also had a coexistence with FCDIa. Two of these MOGHE cases received the diagnosis of FCDIa by the MTCD method, one carrying a pathogenic SLC35A2 variant. Excessive numbers of neurons in the white matter, that is, heterotopic neurons defined as mMCD, were another frequent observation but did not challenge the classifier's MTCD decision. Heterotopic neurons had already been described as a common histopathology pattern and hallmark of FCDIa 11 and MOGHE 12 but did also occur in FCDII subtypes.

Finally, we assembled all features of our deep‐learning‐based histopathology classifier for FCD into a revised web‐based application to facilitate an automated interpretation of WSI slides of anatomically well‐preserved brain tissue obtained from epilepsy surgery (https://fcd‐classifier.eu.ngrok.io). The application can also be downloaded as a containerized demo version (https://hub.docker.com/r/joergvorndran/fcd‐classifier‐demo). The application's landing page offers several dashboards to access and review all of the information mentioned herein. It is important to note that the user can individually select among the 21 anatomical categories to be reviewed by the model (see glossary in Data S1) and that the user can verify the model's prediction ad hoc using a digital microscopy tool. However, this version has a database of readily uploaded WSI images, as the model can currently process only ndpi‐image formats (Hamamatsu, Japan) obtained from 4–5‐μm thin and H&E stained FFPE sections.

DISCUSSION

We developed an advanced histopathology classifier, trained with the deep learning ResNet152 convolutional network, and showing high confidence rates, namely, 97% for the entire classifier (see Table S1), comprising 86% for FCDIa, 96% for FCDIIa, 100% for FCDIIb, 93% for MOGHE, and 99% for mMCD. Interestingly, the classifier always recognized the correct histopathology diagnosis in at least one tile in all of the tested cases, including those carrying a known pathogenic gene variant. At the individual case level, however, the final diagnosis overlapped with the largest batch of tiles assigned by the algorithm to one diagnostic entity. Thus, although challenging because 86.2% of our cases had more than one FCD subtype, the algorithm eventually picked the correct diagnosis in 80.2% of all cases. These results open promising avenues for routine histopathology practice, particularly when large surgical tissue samples are in need of a comprehensive microscopy review.

However, the frequent coexistence of several FCD subtypes challenged the decision‐making process. Our further analysis and development of an open access online application was guided, therefore, by the practical approach to diagnose FCD subtypes as defined in the ILAE classification scheme. 2 Such a practical approach favors the algorithm's recommendation of one major disease category, which was defined herein as MTCD. Such a practical approach also considers a hierarchical decision‐making process across common FCD diagnoses; for example, the presence of FCDIIb with dysmorphic neurons and balloon cells trumps FCDIIa with dysmorphic neurons only.

Dysmorphic neurons were recognized with high confidence by the classifier despite the challenge that random cutting planes in surgical tissue slabs may obscure the cell's true diameter or anatomical orientation. We recommend, therefore, that every reader should manually confirm and witness the pathology diagnosis suggested by the classifier on as many tiles as necessary. The opportunity to gradually define the confidence score in our web application is helpful to select the most reasonable list of tiles of interest; for example, a confidence score of .9 or higher will create a much smaller list of tiles than using a confidence score of .8 or lower. This applies foremost to the recognition of balloon cells as a specifying feature in FCDIIb, as such regions may not always represent the majority of tiles, and MTCD would then propose the diagnosis of FCDIIa instead of FCDIIb. In contrast, common pitfalls challenging the algorithm's differentiation of balloon cells were reactive and enlarged astrocytes (so called gemistocytes; Figure 2) abundant along the trajectory of intracerebral depth electrode recordings or at the border zone of any other previous surgical intervention.

Following the confirmation or exclusion of FCDII variants as the first order of a hierarchical FCD classification scheme, the second layer should address the confirmation or exclusion of MOGHE. MOGHE is a new FCD entity defined in ILAE's 2022 classification update by its histopathology hallmarks of (1) clusters of increased oligodendroglia and (2) heterotopic neurons at the gray–white matter boundary. Interestingly, 50% of individuals carry brain somatic variants of the SLC35A2 gene, which was confirmed as a disease‐classifying molecular marker. 2 , 9 , 13 , 14 , 15 The relatively large number of MOGHE tiles coexisting in other FCD subtypes needs further clarification, however. Review of these tiles did not show typical MOGHE cluster rather than perivascular and perineuronal satellitosis as a common distractor. We also anticipated that cutting thickness may vary even within the same slides series due to the necessary laboratory skills. Any thinner or thicker sectioning will ultimately result in lower or higher nuclear cell numbers and thereby challenge the algorithm in recognizing categories assessed by cellularity, for example, MOGHE. A third option may include the frequently encountered hypomyelination in MOGHE, 12 which may occur in many other FCD lesions as well. 16 , 17

The third hierarchical layer should represent the diagnosis of cortical dyslamination, that is, abundant microcolumns, either as sole pathology as in FCDIa or adjacent to other malformations. Clinical presentation or molecular hallmarks of this rare FCD entity were less well defined, however. 2 , 4 , 11 One recent case series of histopathologically confirmed FCDIa described a common posterior quadrant localization in children with early onset focal epilepsy. 11 No genetic cause has hitherto been identified. Forty‐three percent of individuals assigned in our case series to FCDII and MOGHE showed patterns of architectural dysplasia with excess of vertically oriented microcolumns. This pattern has been defined by the ILAE classification scheme as FCDIa, but may also occur in associated FCD pathologies, namely, FCDIII subtypes of the ILAE classification. Our classifier was trained for vertically oriented microcolumns as presented in FCDIa, and we keep this nomenclature, therefore, in the current study. We have confirmed the microcolumns also by microscopy review (Figure 5), suggesting that vertically oriented architectural abnormalities of the neocortex are more common than previously anticipated. The coexistence of multiple FCD subtypes should thus be regarded as a complex comorbid FCD scenario (Figure 5). It has not yet been addressed systematically and is not reflected in the ILAE classification scheme. 2 , 8 The frequent association of a vertically oriented architectural dysplasia in FCDII is in support of the previously suggested hypothesis claiming a postmigrational pathomechanism. 18 , 19 Such a delayed maturation can result in a persistence of microcolumns, which is otherwise a common feature of the developing human neocortex. 19 , 20 Finally, the algorithm recorded tiles assigned to heterotopic neurons in the white matter, that is, mMCD, in a total of 32 individuals. This diagnosis is exclusively defined by the ILAE classification scheme for tissues not presenting with any other principal lesion or FCD subtype. 2 , 8 Its pathogenicity or impact in epileptogenic networks has not been scientifically confirmed. We would recommend, therefore, assigning mMCD as the final layer of the hierarchical FCD diagnosis, as it remains in need of further scientific clarification.

It was interesting to observe the high confidence of the algorithm in diagnosing complex histopathology patterns of human neocortical architecture as well as cytoarchitectural abnormalities, which otherwise needed an advanced diagnostic workup including large panels of immunohistochemical stainings and/or genetic testing. Notwithstanding, such laboratory tools are not available in many parts of the world. Notably, routine H&E staining can still provide sufficient detail to extract the distinguishing phenotype of a lesion, that is, dysmorphic neurons or balloon cells were immunohistochemically confirmed by their perikaryal accumulation of intermediate filaments. 21 However, it was not the purpose of this study to systematically test for genetically defined subtypes of FCD. We still have too few samples with a genetically confirmed alteration to train the network. and our present study was based, therefore, on the same series of 125 cases reported previously. The confidence that all genetically tested new cases were correctly labeled may support future initiatives to address this matter at the next level. A future edition of such a classifier could then be based on genetically well‐characterized samples to shortcut the expansive and demanding analysis of genomic testing. Other obstacles in this field include the ultrahigh depth of sequencing necessary to achieve a yield for genetic testing of brain mosaicism in FCD. 22 In addition, the inferior quality of FFPE tissue compared to fresh frozen surgical brain samples needs recognition to formulate a realistic expectation. Currently, only 40%–60% of cases yield a positive result by genetic testing according to sufficiently large and published case series. 3 , 7 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26

It is most likely, however, that such digital slide suite projects will pave the way toward AI‐supported histopathology diagnosis in years to come, pending the clarification of ongoing obstacles in the field of digital pathology and whole slide imaging systems. 5 , 27 Nonetheless, these applications will bridge the growing gap in knowledge and access to new diagnostic tools that are not available in many parts of the world. 28 There were several issues in need of consideration when advocating for an AI‐based diagnostic histopathology algorithm, however. First, a uniform laboratory standard of section thickness in the range of 4–5 μm and standardized H&E staining protocols must be applied 6 to be compatible with our trained dataset for the current algorithm. Second, vendors of WSI scanners have implemented their unique file formats instead of using internationally defined DICOM standards, which already exists for WSI. 27 There are open access WSI reading applications such as openslide.org, which can help to import such different formats into the AI algorithm. However, any technical update of the file format or reading header by the vendor requires prompt software adjustments to guarantee its continuous viability. This only helps the vendor to sell the AI‐based histopathology slide suit as a walled garden laboratory solution, whereas these applications should be openly available irrespective of any country's or user's socioeconomic background. Access to sufficiently fast internet and GPU clusters to upload terabytes of WSI and their deep‐learning‐based analysis remains another obstacle that should be solved in times to come, and which also blocked us from yet providing a fully functional online classifier. Instead, the current version of our web application algorithm is made available at https://fcd‐classifier.eu.ngrok.io and was designed to familiarize a user with all features and to advocate our suggested reviewing pipeline. We are confident, however, that AI‐supported digital slide suites will become the future in neuropathology, that is, Neuropathology 3.0, especially when developed and guided by highly specialized experts of the worldwide histopathology community.

The final question remains as to whether an AI‐based histopathology classifier will replace the pathology expert, despite the high legal accountability of a signed pathology report for clinical patient management. We recommend only using these tools as a diagnostic screening device for WSI obtained during the routine workup. The application may then suggest a preselection of regions of interest, pending an individually chosen level of the algorithm's detection sensitivity by the reviewer. Each reviewer must also corroborate whether technical pitfalls could have compromised the results, for example, wrinkles, dust, or coloring artifacts. Such technical errors have not been detected, however, in our current series of epilepsy surgery cases. Our current web application is thus seen as an informed helper to guide this process, in particular when the reader has little experience or may be overwhelmed by the number of tissue blocks to be processed in a routine epilepsy surgery scenario. Notwithstanding, machine learning and AI tools are increasingly recognized in neuropathology. 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 This is less so, however, in the specialty arena of neuropathology and epilepsy surgery, with only three articles published in peer‐reviewed journals so far. 5 , 10 , 33 In contrast, AI is already much appreciated in clinical epileptology, for example, neuroimaging and neurophysiology. 34 , 35 , 36 , 37

In conclusion, AI‐based classifiers represent the next wave of diagnostic innovation in neuropathology, that is, Neuropathology 3.0. 28 However, their liability will need further corroboration and testing from different laboratories around the world to challenge and address the entire spectrum of technical requirements to generate and upload WSIs and access fast GPU clusters, as well as to provide galleries for common pitfalls confusing or misleading such classifiers.
