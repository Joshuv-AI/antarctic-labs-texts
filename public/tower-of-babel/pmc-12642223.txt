How career adaptability affects university students’ job search behavior and subjective well-being: the role of career choice optimistic bias
Authors: Weihua Ouyang, Xiaoli Shu, Ruijuan Fu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeAlthough an increasing number of studies have found that career adaptability is beneficial to individual career development, research on the mechanisms by which career adaptability affects job search behavior and subjective well-being among university students is relatively scarce. Based on the career construction model of adaptation and career construction theory, two studies were conducted to explore how career adaptability influences job search behavior and subjective well-being, and under what conditions these influences occur.MethodsStudy 1 conducted a behavioral experiment with 224 Chinese university students to examine how career adaptability and career choice optimistic bias affect job search behavior and subjective well-being. Study 2 employed a multi-wave questionnaire survey of 404 college seniors to test a moderated mediation model.ResultsThe results showed that career adaptability directly affects their job search behavior and indirectly affects their subjective well-being by influencing their job search behavior. Students with high career adaptability had higher levels of subjective well-being than those with low career adaptability, regardless of their optimistic or pessimistic biases. In addition, students with high career adaptability engaged in more job search behavior when they had optimistic or not excessively pessimistic bias (no bias and low pessimistic bias). Furthermore, the mediating effect of job search behavior in linking career adaptability to subjective well-being was stronger with increased career choice optimistic bias.ConclusionThis study explores the influence mechanism of career adaptability on university students’ job search behavior and subjective well-being from the perspective of career construction. Moreover, career adaptability affects university students’ job search behavior and subjective well-being differently under varying levels of career choice optimistic bias. These findings contribute to the integration of positive psychology into career psychology.Keywords: University students, Career adaptability, Job search behavior, Subjective well-being, Career choice optimistic bias

Introduction

The university years are a critical period for shaping career trajectories. During this time, students need to engage in job searches to lay the groundwork for sustainable professional development [1, 2]. Given the widespread public attention on student employment, universities have prioritized it on their administrative agendas [3, 4]. Despite these efforts, facilitating successful employment remains a formidable challenge. Research consistently shows a positive correlation between proactive job search behavior and employment quality, as such behaviors yield more opportunities and enable informed career choices [5, 6]. Concurrently, subjective well-being, defined as individuals’ comprehensive assessments of their quality of life [7], is a crucial factor affecting students’ mental health, academic performance, and career development [8–10]. Therefore, understanding the determinants of both job search behavior and subjective well-being is essential for promoting student development and has attracted significant scholarly and administrative attention [11].

Scholars have explored the impact of personality, attitudes, and situational factors on job search behavior [12, 13], as well as the influence of personal needs, income, and social environment on subjective well-being [10, 14, 15]. These studies provide a solid foundation for investigating these outcomes in students. However, several pressing issues remain.

First, traditional career development models, which emphasize matching individual interests and abilities with job requirements, now face significant challenges. The current economic environment is characterized by uncertainty, global competition, rapid technological progress, and declining job security [16]. This complex landscape demands greater adaptability and flexibility from students [17, 18]. Therefore, it is necessary to explore how they cope with career transitions and maintain well-being from the perspective of career adaptability. As a key psychosocial resource, career adaptability enables individuals to navigate changes and challenges in their career development flexibly and effectively [19, 20].

Second, although existing research has widely discussed the importance of career adaptability, job search behavior, and subjective well-being [11, 21], we still lack an integrated understanding of how these constructs interact, especially among university students. Situated at the critical transition from campus to workplace, these students face acute employment pressures [22]. Empirical evidence shows that the level of career adaptability developed during this period predicts long-term career satisfaction and psychological adjustment [23]. Clarifying the mechanisms through which career adaptability shapes subjective well-being in this group is therefore essential.

Third, university students exhibit diverse and malleable cognitive states during their job search [24]. This malleability presents a valuable opportunity for early intervention and career guidance. However, the mechanisms through which these cognitive states influence job search behavior and subjective well-being remain poorly understood. Optimistic bias, a common cognitive state in this process [25, 26], may play a crucial moderating role in the relationships between career adaptability and both job search behavior and subjective well-being. To address these gaps, the present study examines how career adaptability affects students’ job search behavior and subjective well-being, and how career choice optimistic bias moderates these relationships.

Theory and hypothesis

Theory

The career construction model of adaptation outlines a four-stage process: from adaptive readiness to adaptability resources, adapting responses, and finally, adaptation results [20, 27]. The initial stage, adaptive readiness, refers to an individual’s willingness to respond to career challenges, transitions, and trauma [20]. This readiness is shaped by traits like cognitive ability, hope, and optimism [27, 28]. Willingness alone is not sufficient, individuals deploy adaptability resources, which are psychosocial assets that help them cope with unfamiliarity, complexity, and ambiguity. Among these, career adaptability is the primary resource [20, 27]. These resources, in turn, inform adapting responses, which are the specific behaviors and beliefs used to manage career development tasks and changing conditions [27, 28]. For students, a key adapting response is job search behavior, encompassing activities such as orienting, exploring, and establishing oneself in a career [28, 29]. Ultimately, these responses lead to adaptation results, which can be assessed by indicators such as career success, satisfaction, and subjective well-being [20, 27].

The career construction theory clarifies the relationship between adaptive readiness, adaptability resources, adapting responses, and adaptation results [16, 20]. This sequential relationship, where adaptive readiness and adaptability resources facilitate adapting responses that yield adaptation results, forms the core logic of the career construction process [20, 29]. As Savickas [20:162] notes, “better outcomes (adaptation results) are achieved by individuals who are willing (adaptive readiness) and able (adaptability resources) to perform coping behaviors that address changing conditions (adapting responses).” Applying this framework to the school-to-work transition, we can map the key variables of our study. First, career choice optimistic bias functions as a form of adaptive readiness, representing the psychological willingness to engage in career selection [27]. This intrinsic motivation provides the initial impetus for action [20, 27]. Second, career adaptability serves as the crucial adaptability resource, equipping individuals with the necessary “ability” to navigate challenges [16, 20, 27]. Driven by both willingness and ability, students then enact job search behavior as a key adapting response, bridging the gap between their internal capabilities and external opportunities [13, 20, 29]. Finally, this critical transition significantly impacts their subjective well-being, a key measure of adaptation results, due to the spillover effect of the work domain on overall life quality [30–32].

As a core component of adaptive readiness, career choice optimistic bias is particularly sensitive to the psychological preparedness required during the school-to-work transition, more so than stable personality traits [26, 27]. Variations in this readiness lead individuals to utilize their adaptability resources differently, resulting in divergent responses and outcomes [20]. Therefore, we contend that career choice optimistic bias acts as a moderator within this dynamic process. Specifically, it influences how effectively individuals translate their adaptability resources (e.g., career adaptability) into concrete adapting responses (e.g., job search behavior) and positive adaptation results (e.g., subjective well-being). Unraveling this complex interplay is the central aim of the present study.

Career adaptability and job search behavior

Career adaptability is a core concept in career construction theory and vocational psychology [33]. Defined as a psychosocial resource, it enables individuals to cope with current and anticipated career tasks, transitions, and work-related trauma [16, 34]. This construct comprises four interrelated dimensions: (1) Career concern, a future-oriented perspective on career planning; (2) Career control, taking responsibility for one’s career through self-discipline and persistence; (3) Career curiosity, actively exploring oneself and the world of work; and (4) Career confidence, belief in one’s ability to achieve career goals and overcome obstacles [16, 20, 34]. Given their interrelated nature, these four dimensions are often analyzed as a unified variable [27]. As a critical competency developed throughout one’s career, career adaptability allows individuals to respond effectively to environmental changes, fostering positive behaviors and facilitating career development [35].

Job search is an integral part of university students’ career development [32]. It is a purposeful, volitional process that begins with a commitment to pursue employment goals [6, 13]. This process is described as a “dynamic, recursive, self-regulated” pattern of behavior [6] and typically involves two main types: preparatory and active job search. Preparatory behavior involves gathering information about job opportunities and preparing application materials, while active job search behavior entails sending these materials to potential employers [13].

According to career construction theory, career adaptability directly influences adaptive responses and behaviors [20, 27]. In today’s dynamic work environments, individuals must continuously adapt to achieve career development [20]. University students with high career adaptability demonstrate greater career consciousness and preparation. Their career curiosity drives them to research future opportunities and gather relevant information [20, 35], which in turn leads to more preparatory job search behaviors [36]. Furthermore, these students can exercise career control and take responsibility for their actions. Possessing career confidence, they are more proactive in addressing career challenges [34, 37], which consequently results in more active job search behaviors [36]. Research consistently shows that career adaptability is positively related to career exploration, planning, satisfaction, and decision-making [21, 27], and it promotes proactive career behaviors among university students [29]. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

H1: Career adaptability positively affects university students’ job search behavior.

H1: Career adaptability positively affects university students’ job search behavior.

Career adaptability, job search behavior, and subjective well-being

Subjective well-being is a concern of positive psychology and an important indicator of individual career construction [38]. It is a general term for the level of well-being experienced by people based on their subjective evaluation of their lives, encompassing two primary aspects: cognitive evaluations, such as life satisfaction, and affective experiences, namely, positive emotions and negative emotions [10, 39]. Cognitive evaluations involve judgments about one’s life as a whole or in specific domains like work and health, while affective experiences are emotional responses to life events [10]. In the current context of a fast-paced life and intense talent competition, university students face an increasingly challenging employment situation. This difficulty can adversely affect their employment prospects and, consequently, diminish their subjective well-being [40].

The job search behavior of university students is a key adaptive career behavior. According to career construction theory, adaptive responses and behaviors lead to adaptation results [20]; therefore, we expect job search behavior to influence students’ subjective well-being. For university students, securing a fulfilling job is a primary goal, and their job search behavior predicts active engagement in information gathering, document preparation, and opportunity seeking [13]. This behavior is positively correlated with the number of interviews and job offers received. Such outcomes, in turn, enable students to make better choices, secure higher-quality employment, and experience greater satisfaction [5, 13], ultimately enhancing their subjective well-being [10]. Consistent with this, previous research has identified job search behavior as a strong predictor of subjective well-being [41, 42].

According to career construction theory [20], the influence of career adaptability on university students’ subjective well-being follows the “adaptation resources → adaptation responses → adaptation results” sequence. Specifically, career adaptability serves as a sustainable personal resource. It helps students proactively acquire employment information and set goals during career transitions [20, 43], which in turn manifests as more active and intentional job search behaviors [27, 34]. These job search behaviors are not merely uncertain, domain-specific actions, but rather a dynamic and purposeful process [13] that significantly increases the likelihood of obtaining desirable positions and satisfactory compensation [5]. In turn, these achievements enhance subjective well-being through feelings of accomplishment and positive affect [10]. Based on the above, we propose that:

H2: Job search behavior mediates the relationship between career adaptability and university students’ subjective well-being.

H2: Job search behavior mediates the relationship between career adaptability and university students’ subjective well-being.

The role of career choice optimistic bias

Career choice optimistic bias is prevalent among university students during job searches and represents a form of adaptive preparation [26, 27]. Some students exhibit optimistic bias, believing they are more likely to experience positive outcomes (e.g., job search success) and less likely to face negative ones (e.g., failure) [26, 44]. Others display pessimistic bias, expecting fewer positive outcomes and more negative experiences [25, 26].

People employ adaptability resources to cope with these biases [20, 25], and individuals with high career adaptability engage in more proactive behaviors [28, 45]. When students exhibit career choice optimistic bias, they anticipate positive outcomes and maintain a hopeful outlook [46]. However, when confronted with negative career events, they may overestimate their coping capacity and make irrational judgments [46, 47], potentially affecting short-term job search behavior. Students with high career adaptability can quickly recognize this bias, correct irrational decisions, and adjust strategies after setbacks [20, 26]. In such cases, optimistic bias acts as a driving force, enhancing motivation and confidence, enabling them to perceive meaningful events and hope in their careers [20, 25]. In such cases, optimistic bias serves as a driving force that enhances motivation and confidence, enabling students to perceive meaning and hope in their career experiences [20, 25]. This optimism, in turn, translates into sustained action, fostering more proactive job search behaviors [25, 29, 45].

Conversely, students with excessive pessimistic bias tend to view their future negatively and doubt their ability to overcome difficulties [48]. While mild pessimism may serve as a cautionary signal, excessive bias leads to indecisiveness and avoidance of challenges [25, 49]. This may reduce job search behavior or even discourage job-seeking altogether. Thus, under excessive pessimistic bias, job search behavior may diminish regardless of career adaptability levels, suggesting career adaptability has limited influence in such cases. The threshold at which pessimistic bias begins to moderate this relationship warrants further exploration. We therefore propose:

H3: Career choice optimistic bias moderates the relationship between career adaptability and job search behavior, such that the positive relationship is stronger under optimistic or not excessively pessimistic bias (no bias and low pessimistic bias).

H3: Career choice optimistic bias moderates the relationship between career adaptability and job search behavior, such that the positive relationship is stronger under optimistic or not excessively pessimistic bias (no bias and low pessimistic bias).

Optimistic biases are illusory beliefs characterized by unrealistic optimism and positive illusions. While they can foster positive expectations [50], their irrational aspects [47] may hinder adequate preparation and precaution, potentially leading to inappropriate persistence [51]. In contrast, pessimistic bias involves a focus on negative events and the difficulties of coping with misfortune, perceiving no hope, and fostering a reluctance to persevere [48]. Despite their seemingly opposing nature, both biases can activate an individual’s adaptability resources in the face of risk and uncertainty [25].

During the job search process, university students can draw upon career adaptability to manage the challenges posed by either pessimistic or optimistic biases [20, 52]. This adaptability equips individuals with high career readiness to effectively navigate job search changes and potential biases, leading to greater satisfaction and subjective well-being. Conversely, those with low career adaptability struggle to cope, resulting in lower satisfaction and well-being [9, 20]. Although pessimistic biases, characterized by a focus on employment difficulties, are known to reduce subjective well-being [48], they might amplify the positive link between career adaptability and well-being. This is because the challenges stemming from these biases serve to highlight the value of adaptability resources [16, 20]. Faced with the heightened negativity and uncertainty that these biases foster, students with high career adaptability can leverage their resources to more effectively navigate these difficulties, thereby preserving their well-being [10, 20, 25]. Consequently, the protective benefits of adaptability become more pronounced, leading to a stronger association between career adaptability and subjective well-being in the presence of pessimistic bias.

In contrast, when university students maintain a relatively objective and unbiased cognitive state during the job search process, they can make clearer and more realistic assessments of the job market, their own strengths and weaknesses, and potential opportunities and challenges [26, 53]. In this state, they neither excessively magnify difficulties (pessimistic bias) nor unrealistically overestimate the probability of success (optimistic bias) [26]. Regardless of their level of career adaptability, the absence of bias means individuals do not experience the additional cognitive or emotional burdens triggered by such distortions [54], and their satisfaction and well-being remain relatively stable [10]. Therefore, when no bias is present, the direct relationship between career adaptability and subjective well-being often weakens. Based on the preceding reasoning, we hypothesize that:

H4: The positive relationship between career adaptability and subjective well-being is moderated by career choice optimistic bias. Specifically, this relationship is stronger when either optimistic or pessimistic bias is present. In other words, individuals with high career adaptability will exhibit significantly higher subjective well-being under conditions of high optimistic or pessimistic bias. However, in the absence of any bias, career adaptability has no significant predictive effect on subjective well-being.

H4: The positive relationship between career adaptability and subjective well-being is moderated by career choice optimistic bias. Specifically, this relationship is stronger when either optimistic or pessimistic bias is present. In other words, individuals with high career adaptability will exhibit significantly higher subjective well-being under conditions of high optimistic or pessimistic bias. However, in the absence of any bias, career adaptability has no significant predictive effect on subjective well-being.

University students may experience either optimistic or pessimistic bias during job selection. According to career construction theory, these biases and uncertainties activate students’ adaptability resources [20, 25]. These resources help adjust mindsets and behaviors to achieve new career adaptations [20]. Students with high career adaptability tend to have greater hope [9], engage in more career exploration and job search behaviors [10, 55], and achieve more satisfying job outcomes [5, 13], all enhancing subjective well-being. However, individuals who exhibit excessive pessimistic bias, regardless of the degree of their career adaptability, may not perceive any hope and may be reluctant to cope with challenges. Such reluctance can reduce positive behaviors [48]. This leads to poorer job search outcomes and diminished well-being. We therefore propose:

H5: Career choice optimistic bias moderates the mediated relationship between career adaptability and students’ subjective well-being via job search behavior. Specifically, this mediated relationship is stronger when career choice pessimistic bias is relatively low.

H5: Career choice optimistic bias moderates the mediated relationship between career adaptability and students’ subjective well-being via job search behavior. Specifically, this mediated relationship is stronger when career choice pessimistic bias is relatively low.

The current study

This study focuses on university students in China to investigate how career adaptability influences their job search behavior and subjective well-being (see the theoretical model in Fig. 1). The selection of this cohort is strategically justified for three reasons. First, the sustained expansion of higher education since 1999 has created a highly competitive and pressurized job market for Chinese university graduates, a phenomenon of significant societal concern [58, 59] and a rich context for academic research. Second, this population is highly accessible, making it practical for large-scale data collection through questionnaires and experiments. Third, their challenges are emblematic of the broader career-entry difficulties faced by youth in today’s socio-economic environment, enhancing the generalizability of our findings.

Research model

We tested our model through two complementary studies. Study 1 was a behavioral experiment that examined the impact of career adaptability and career choice optimistic bias on students’ job search behavior and well-being. Study 2 then used a multi-wave questionnaire survey with senior students to validate the theoretical model and confirm the robustness of the results.

Study 1: behavioral experiment

Sample and procedures

The studies presented in this article were approved by the ethics committee of the first author’s affiliated university and were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki guidelines. All participants provided informed consent and took part voluntarily.

We used G*Power 3.1 software to determine the required sample size [56]. For between-group comparisons using ANOVA, we set the effect size (f) at 0.35, power (1-β) at 0.99, significance level (α) at 0.05, and included six groups. The analysis indicated a minimum total sample size of 178 participants. To account for potential attrition and enhance statistical robustness, we aimed for a larger sample.

Data were collected at a university in Guangdong Province, China. Initially, 15 counselor teachers and classroom teachers familiar with the student population were recruited to assist in participant selection. These teachers underwent standardized training on the overall purpose of the study, the concept of career adaptability, and the scenario descriptions used for screening (see Materials and measures). Each teacher was then instructed to nominate 10 students who best represented the high career adaptability profile and 10 who best represented the low adaptability profile, resulting in a preliminary pool of 150 students per group.

Teachers initially rated all students on a 5-point scale to assess their alignment with specific scenario descriptions. For the high career adaptability group, a higher score indicated a stronger match with the high-adaptability scenario, whereas for the low adaptability group, a higher score indicated a stronger match with the low-adaptability scenario. From these ratings, we selected the top 115 students from each group who scored above 3. These selected participants then read the scenario corresponding to their group and confirmed whether it matched their self-perception. Six students who indicated a mismatch were excluded, resulting in a final sample of 224 participants (112 per group). Before the formal experiment, participants completed the Career Adaptability Questionnaire. The data from this questionnaire will later be used to assess the effectiveness of assigning participants into high and low career adaptability groups.

The following experiment uses imagining a job search scenario as a situational cue to further measure students’ job search behavior and subjective well-being. This method is similar to the priming technique in social psychology, which involves exposing participants to specific stimuli to test their subsequent behaviors or attitudes [58]. Participants first engaged in a five-minute relaxation exercise, designed to help them get into a better visualization of the job search. They then imagined themselves in the job search phase, actively looking for a job. After three minutes, they completed the Career Choice Optimistic Bias Scale, the Job Search Behavior Questionnaire, and the Subjective Well-Being Questionnaire.

The final sample consisted of 224 valid participants (112 per group), with a mean age of 20.77 ± 1.89 years, including 103 males and 121 females. In terms of academic majors, 37 students (16.52%) were in engineering, 56 (25%) in science, 46 (20.54%) in liberal arts, and 85 (37.94%) in other fields. Each participant received 5 RMB (approximately $0.8) as compensation.

Experimental design

A 2 × 3 between-subjects design was used with two factors: Career adaptability (high vs. low) and career choice optimistic bias (optimistic, no, and pessimistic). The dependent variables were subjective well-being and job search behavior.

Materials and measures

To ensure the reliability and validity of our measures, all materials were adapted from established literature. The Chinese versions of the Career Adaptability Scenario Description and the Job Search Behavior Questionnaire were developed using a rigorous translation and back-translation procedure. Initially, two independent English teachers performed the forward translation (English to Chinese) and back-translation (Chinese to English). A third English teacher then resolved any discrepancies between the original and back-translated English versions. This process was repeated iteratively until the two versions were fully consistent. Finally, the finalized Chinese questionnaires were reviewed and approved by three psychologists to ensure their conceptual and linguistic accuracy.

Scenarios of career adaptability

The development of the career adaptability scenarios was grounded in the theoretical connotation of career adaptability by Savickas et al. [16] and the scenario descriptions by Ohme and Zacher [57]. To create distinct high- and low-adaptability conditions, we designed a scenario that was structured around the four core dimensions of career adaptability: concern, control, curiosity, and confidence. The description for a hypothetical student, “Student A,” was as follows:

Student A frequently (rarely) demonstrates confidence in achieving goals and overcoming obstacles, frequently (rarely) contemplates his or her future and prepares for upcoming tasks and challenges, frequently (rarely) takes responsibility for shaping his or her future development and work environment, and frequently (rarely) explores various options and new opportunities.

In this context, “Frequently” indicates high career adaptability scenario material, whereas “rarely” indicates low career adaptability scenario material.

Career adaptability

Career adaptability was assessed using the Chinese version of the Career Adapt-Abilities Scale (CAAS) [34], which has been validated in China by Hou et al. [35]. This 24-item scale measures the four dimensions of career adaptability (concern, control, curiosity, and confidence) using a five-point Likert scale (1 = “not strong,” 5 = “strongest”). Higher total scores indicate greater career adaptability. A sample item is: “I think about what my future will be like.” In the present study, the scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.95.

Career choice optimistic bias

To measure career choice optimistic bias, we integrated two paradigms: the social comparison paradigm [59] and the past-future imagery paradigm [60]. We used 20 key events (10 positive and 10 negative) selected from Wu et al.’s questionnaire [26], which assesses university students’ career choice optimistic bias. In this study, social comparisons were made between the “likelihood of it happening to oneself” and the “likelihood of it happening to others similar to oneself.” Recognizing that job search intention is a primary pathway that directly influences subsequent job search behavior [61], we used imagery of future employment to evoke participants’ feelings related to job search. The specific procedures were as follows.

To elicit participants’ genuine feelings, they were first asked to imagine themselves searching for a job. Next, they considered each key event in the job search process. They rated the likelihood of each event happening to themselves (P1) and also rated the likelihood of the same event happening to others similar to them (P2). These likelihood ratings were scored on a scale from “0” (Definitely won’t happen) to “10” (Definitely will happen). For each positive event, a difference score was calculated as (P1 - P2). For each negative event, to align the score with optimism, the difference score was calculated as (P2 - P1). The total career choice optimistic bias score was the sum of all 20 difference scores.

For the analysis in Study 1, this continuous variable was categorized. Participants were divided into three groups based on their total score: the optimistic bias group (total score > 0), the no bias group (total score = 0), and the pessimistic bias group (total score < 0). These groups consisted of 66, 64, and 94 participants, respectively.

Job search behavior

Job search behavior was assessed using the 20-item updated Job Search Behavior Scale [13]. Participants rated each item on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Higher scores reflect a greater degree of preparedness and activity in job searching. A sample item is: “Talk with friends or relatives about possible job leads.” The scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency in this study, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.91.

Subjective well-being

Subjective well-being was assessed using a nine-item Well-being Scale originally developed by Campbell et al. and subsequently revised for a Chinese context [62]. Eight items presented participants with pairs of positive and negative adjectives (e.g., “Hopeless & Hopeful”) to reflect their well-being states, while one item directly assessed overall satisfaction. All items were rated on a 7-point scale, with higher scores indicating greater subjective well-being. The scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency in this study, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.88.

Study 1 results

Validity check of the career adaptability grouping

To ensure the validity of our grouping procedure, an independent samples t-test was conducted to compare the Career Adapt-Abilities Scale scores between the high and low adaptability groups. The results revealed a statistically significant difference, t(222) = 17.69, p < .001, d = 2.12, with the high adaptability group (M = 4.06, SD = 0.59) reporting significantly higher scores than the low adaptability group (M = 2.89, SD = 0.51). This finding confirms that the grouping based on career adaptability was successful.

The effect of career adaptability and career choice optimistic bias on job search behavior

The ANOVA results indicated a significant main effect of career adaptability on job search behavior (F(1, 218) = 30.65, p < 0.001,η² = 0.12). Specifically, participants with high career adaptability exhibited significantly greater job search behavior than those with low career adaptability (Mhigh -Mlow = 1.09, p < 0.001), supporting hypothesis 1. The main effect of career choice optimistic bias was also significant (F(2, 218) = 23.31, p < 0.001, η² = 0.18). The job search behavior of the no bias group was significantly higher than that of the Pessimistic bias group (Mno - Mpess = 0.24, p < 0.001). Additionally, the job search behavior of the optimistic bias group was significantly higher than that of the Pessimistic bias group (Mopt - Mpess = 0.34, p < 0.001), and the job search behavior of the optimistic bias group was also significantly higher than that of the no bias group (Mopt - Mno = 0.10, p < 0.01).

Additionally, there was a significant interaction effect between career adaptability and career choice optimistic bias (F(2, 218) = 8.33, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.07). As depicted in Figure 2, simple effects analysis clarified this interaction. For participants in the pessimistic bias group, there was no significant difference in job search behavior between the high and low career adaptability groups (Mhigh - Mlow = 0.06, p = 0.1). In contrast, for the no bias group, the high career adaptability group displayed significantly higher job search behavior scores compared to the low group (Mhigh - Mlow = 0.45, p < 0.001). A similar pattern emerged for the optimistic bias group, where the high adaptability group again outperformed the low adaptability group (Mhigh - Mlow = 0.28, p < 0.001). These results supported Hypothesis 3.

Interaction effect of career adaptability and career choice optimistic bias on job search behavior (Study 1)

The effect of career adaptability and career choice optimistic bias on subjective well-being

The results of the ANOVA indicated that the main effect of career choice optimistic bias was significant, F(2, 218) = 10.73, p < 0.001, η² = 0.09. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons revealed that subjective well-being was significantly higher in the no bias group than in the pessimistic bias group (Mno − Mpess = 0.60, p < 0.001). Similarly, subjective well-being was significantly higher in the optimistic bias group than in the pessimistic bias group (Mopt − Mpess = 0.63, p < 0.001). However, there was no significant difference between the optimistic bias and no bias groups (Mopt − Mno = 0.03, p = 0.60).

Additionally, there was a significant interaction effect between career adaptability and career choice optimistic bias on subjective well-being (F(2,218) = 7.26, p < 0.001, η² = 0.06). Simple effects analyses (Fig. 3) showed that for individuals with a pessimistic bias, the high career adaptability group reported significantly greater subjective well-being than the low career adaptability group (Mhigh - Mlow = 0.99, p < 0.001). A similar pattern was found for those with an optimistic bias, where the high adaptability group had significantly higher subjective well-being than the low adaptability group (Mhigh - Mlow = 0.61, p < 0.001). In contrast, at the no bias level, the difference was not significant (Mhigh - Mlow = 0.16, p = 0.09). These results supported Hypothesis 4.

Study 2: questionnaire study

Sample and procedures

We recruited 500 senior undergraduate students from four universities in China (two key and two general institutions) for a three-wave longitudinal study. Data collection was facilitated by trained classroom teachers and supervisors who distributed a web-based questionnaire link via Wenjuanxing (http://wjx.com), a reputable Chinese data collection platform [63, 64]. To ensure protocol adherence, teachers were trained to assign each participant a unique identification number and to use consistent language throughout the study. To mitigate common method bias, the independent variable (career adaptability), mediator (job search behavior and career choice optimistic bias), and dependent variable (subjective well-being) were measured at three separate time points [65, 66]: T1 (beginning of senior year, first semester), T2 (beginning of second semester), and T3 (end of second semester). Participants received 3 RMB (approx. 0.5 USD) as compensation for each completed survey.

Following data collection, responses from the three waves were paired using the unique identification numbers. After excluding questionnaires with incomplete or invalid data, 404 valid responses were retained, resulting in an effective response rate of 80.80%. The final sample comprised 196 males and 208 females with a mean age of 22.96 years (SD = 1.52). The academic majors were represented as follows: engineering (19.3%), science (21.3%), liberal arts (22.3%), and other fields (37.1%). We conducted an attrition analysis comparing the 404 seniors with complete data across all three waves (T1, T2, T3) to the 64 seniors who had missing data in T2 or T3. Results showed no significant differences between the two samples, indicating that sample attrition was not a significant concern.

Measures

Career adaptability

Same as Study 1. The questionnaire in this study had good reliability (α = 0.94).

Career choice optimistic bias

Following the same procedure as in Study 1, a total career choice optimistic bias score was calculated by summing all 20 difference scores, with higher scores indicating a greater degree of optimistic bias. The scale demonstrated good internal reliability in this study (α = 0.89).

Job search behavior

Job search behavior was measured using the same scale as in Study 1. The scale demonstrated good internal reliability in this study (α = 0.90).

Subjective well-being

Subjective well-being was also assessed with the same measure as in Study 1, which showed excellent internal reliability (α = 0.92).

Control variables

Gender was included as a control variable in this study. This decision was based on evidence that gender significantly influences both key outcome variables. Specifically, research has documented gender differences in job search behavior [67], and subjective well-being is also known to vary by gender [68]. By controlling for gender, we aimed to isolate its confounding influence and more accurately assess the impact of career adaptability on job search behavior and subjective well-being.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS 23.0 and Mplus 8.3. Initially, the reliability of each questionnaire was assessed using SPSS 23.0, followed by the calculation of descriptive statistics. The structural model was then tested for fit using Mplus 8.3. Subsequently, tests for mediating and moderating effects within the structural model were primarily conducted using the PROCESS macro (Model 8) [69] in SPSS 23.0. Throughout these analyses, bias-corrected bootstrapping with 5,000 resamples was employed. In the specified model, career adaptability (T1 mean) served as the independent variable, job search behavior (T2 mean) as the mediator, and subjective well-being (T3 mean) as the dependent variable. Career choice optimistic bias (T2 mean) was specified as the moderator, with gender included as a control variable. An effect was considered statistically significant if the 95% bias-corrected confidence interval did not include zero.

The effect size for the indirect effect, kappa-squared (κ²), was 0.57, which is considered a large effect [70], suggesting that the mediating role of job search behavior is substantial. This result supports Hypothesis 2.

The moderating role of career choice optimistic bias

As shown in Table 2; Fig. 4, the interaction between career adaptability and career choice optimistic bias significantly predicted job search behavior (β = 0.05, SE = 0.01, 95%CI = [0.03, 0.06], p < 0.001). This finding indicates that career choice optimistic bias positively moderated the relationship between career adaptability and job search behavior. To illustrate this moderation effect, we generated a Johnson–Neyman plot (Fig. 5). The plot reveals that the positive relationship between career adaptability and job search behavior strengthens as levels of career choice optimistic bias increase. Specifically, this relationship became statistically significant (p < 0.001) when career choice optimistic bias exceeded − 3.1537 (−2.29 SD), a point at which the 95% confidence interval for the effect excluded zero. In practical terms, this means the effect of career adaptability on job search behavior is significant for the vast majority of students, specifically those with low pessimistic bias, no bias, and optimistic bias. These findings provide further support for Hypothesis 3.

Discussion

Based on the career construction model of adaptation and career construction theory, this study investigated how career adaptability influences university students’ job search behavior and subjective well-being, and the conditions under which these effects vary. The results revealed that career adaptability not only directly facilitates job search behavior but also indirectly improves subjective well-being through this enhanced behavior. Furthermore, career choice optimistic bias was found to moderate these relationships. These findings extend the literature by elucidating the moderating mechanisms within the career construction framework and offer practical guidance for university career services aimed at fostering positive employment outcomes and student mental health.

Career adaptability, job search behavior, and subjective well-being

A core finding of this study is that career adaptability significantly and positively predicts university students’ job search behavior. Moreover, career adaptability enhances subjective well-being indirectly, with job search behavior serving as a key mediator. This result aligns with the core tenets of career construction theory, which posits career adaptability as a crucial resource for navigating career challenges [16]. Critically, while prior research has established the importance of career adaptability [37], few studies have explicitly examined its role in the specific context of the job search process. By identifying job search behavior as a mediating mechanism, this research clarifies how career adaptability functions not only as a tool for adapting to new environments but also as a form of psychological capital that motivates proactive job seeking and, consequently, enhances well-being.

This finding also contributes to the literature on subjective well-being by extending its inquiry into the career domain. It moves beyond a traditional focus on physical and mental health [10, 71] to demonstrate how career-related actions, such as job searching, are integral to an individual’s overall sense of well-being. The empirical support for the mediating role of job search behavior enriches both positive psychology and career construction theory. Furthermore, these findings are consistent with practical evidence [23, 38], reinforcing the value of career adaptability training in higher education to enhance students’ employment readiness and motivation, and underscoring the need to integrate such training into career development programs.

The role of career choice optimistic bias

This study identifies career choice optimistic bias as a key boundary condition for the effects of career adaptability. The results indicate that the positive relationship between career adaptability and job search behavior is stronger for students who exhibit optimistic or not excessively pessimistic bias. Furthermore, the effect of career adaptability on subjective well-being is amplified in the presence of either an optimistic or pessimistic bias. Most notably, the indirect effect of career adaptability on subjective well-being, transmitted through job search behavior, strengthens as career choice optimistic bias increases. These findings demonstrate that the benefits of career adaptability are not universal but are contingent upon an individual’s cognitive biases regarding career choices.

From a psychological perspective, the Johnson-Neyman (J-N) analysis reveals critical thresholds that delineate how the effectiveness of career adaptability shifts across different levels of career choice optimistic bias. For university students with lower pessimistic bias, these findings indicate a greater propensity to adopt positive psychological adjustment strategies [72]. At this level of bias, students can more effectively leverage their career adaptability resources to navigate job search challenges, engage in proactive job search behavior, and ultimately achieve better employment outcomes [20, 73]. Furthermore, this positive outlook, even in the absence of immediate job search results, can mitigate anxiety and foster positive emotions and satisfaction, thereby enhancing their subjective well-being [74]. Conversely, students with a higher pessimistic bias are more likely to incur additional cognitive and emotional burdens during the job search process [54]. However, the results suggest that career adaptability serves as a crucial psychological buffer; individuals with high adaptability are better equipped to cope with these burdens, maintaining higher levels of satisfaction and positive emotions, which in turn sustains their subjective well-being [20].

This finding significantly expands our understanding of the mechanisms that activate career adaptability. Prior research has predominantly focused on external triggers, such as major career transitions or complex and uncertain work environments [20, 75]. Our study, however, demonstrates that internal cognitive factors, namely career choice optimistic bias, can also serve as a critical catalyst, mobilizing adaptability resources to navigate potential challenges. This insight not only aligns with but also refines Urbanaviciute et al.’s [38] argument by specifying that the “challenges” prompting activation can be internally generated cognitive states, not solely external circumstances. Consequently, the moderating role of career choice optimistic bias does more than introduce an “internal-activation” perspective; it fills a crucial theoretical gap and underscores career adaptability’s function as a universal psychological resource for navigating both external and internal adversities.

Furthermore, the moderating effect of career choice optimistic bias highlights that the positive impact of career adaptability on job search behavior and subjective well-being is contingent upon individual differences. While previous studies have established the direct benefits of positive psychological traits like adaptability for well-being [10, 39], they have often overlooked the critical role of individual cognitive biases in shaping these outcomes. By incorporating career choice optimistic bias, our research empirically supports the theoretical view that career behavior is a dynamic subject-object interaction [16]. We demonstrate that the subjective lens of an individual’s optimism fundamentally alters how the objective resource of career adaptability is translated into action and well-being. This nuanced understanding helps to illuminate the “black box” of these complex interactions, revealing the conditional pathways through which career adaptability exerts its influence.

Theoretical contributions

This study makes several significant theoretical contributions. First, it elucidates the mediating mechanism through which career adaptability influences subjective well-being. While previous research largely concentrated on the direct effects of career adaptability, our study, grounded in the career construction model of adaptation, empirically establishes job search behavior as a key intervening pathway. This finding not only enriches the literature on the influence mechanisms of career adaptability but also answers the call by Haibo et al. [76] to explore its potential pathways, thus helping to populate the career construction model of adaptation with concrete, empirical content. Second, the research clarifies the critical boundary conditions that govern the effectiveness of career adaptability. By investigating the moderating role of career choice optimistic bias, we directly address the contextual questions of when and for whom adaptability resources are most impactful. This provides novel evidence regarding the situational dependence of career adaptability and significantly expands the body of related literature. Finally, we advance the field by developing and testing a comprehensive moderated mediation model. This integrated framework, which combines the mediating role of job search behavior with the moderating role of career choice optimistic bias, offers a more nuanced understanding of the complex dynamics affecting university students’ job search behavior and subjective well-being. In doing so, our study not only responds to Rudolph et al.’s [27] call for a deeper exploration of the career adaptability process but also provides robust new empirical support for the ongoing application and development of career construction theory.

Practical implications

This study has important practical implications for career education in higher education, which are reflected in the following three aspects. First, strengthen career adaptability training. Career adaptability can improve job search behavior and subjective well-being among university students. Therefore, schools should focus on cultivating career adaptability by integrating it into professional course learning and by setting career challenges that activate students’ adaptability resources. This will help university students develop the necessary skills and adaptability to transition smoothly from school to work [38, 77]. Second, encourage university students to actively search for jobs. Career adaptability can influence students’ subjective well-being through their job search behavior. Schools can encourage active job searching and help students establish valuable career goals by offering career guidance courses or incorporating career guidance content into professional courses. As students pursue and achieve their goals, they will engage in more active job searching, which will enhance their sense of satisfaction and well-being [10] and improve their employment outcomes. Third, guide students in developing a healthy self-perception. The moderating effect of career choice optimistic bias indicates that career counseling should encourage university students to accurately assess themselves. While some degree of bias is normal, students must understand the potential harm of excessive bias on their academic performance and personal development [78]. Teachers should teach students how to properly engage in social comparisons, objectively evaluate themselves, and empathize with others to foster more accurate self-assessment. Students should be encouraged to promptly seek professional help when cognitive biases begin to adversely affect their lives and personal growth.

Limitations and future research directions

Despite its contributions, this study has several limitations. First, this study used a combination of behavioral experiments and questionnaires. The consistency of the results from these two methods helps to ensure the robustness of the findings. However, most of the data were self-reported by students. Although we made efforts to ensure the quality of the measurement items and communicated extensively with participants before data collection, some unavoidable factors, such as student attitudes and cooperation during the assessment, may have introduced confounding influences. To minimize common method variance, data were collected from multiple schools and at multiple time points during the questionnaire survey [65]. Nevertheless, future research could aim to collect data from multiple sources, including teachers, students, and peers, to more objectively analyze the relationship and mechanisms between career adaptability and subjective well-being.

Second, this study identified job search behavior as one of the mediating pathways between career adaptability and university students’ subjective well-being, but other mediating pathways may also exist. According to career construction theory, career adaptability is a psychosocial resource that enables individuals to manage career development and cope with career-related challenges [20]. The career construction process is influenced by both internal and external factors. Therefore, future studies could examine other potential mediating pathways, such as internal factors like career hope, self-efficacy, and the satisfaction of basic psychological needs, and external factors like social support. In addition, these studies could explore the cascading or serial mediating roles of both internal and external factors.

Third, this study reveals the positive impact of career adaptability on university students’ job search behavior and subjective well-being. However, it should be noted that these relationships may be influenced by the Chinese cultural context, such as collectivism, family pressure, and national employment policies. Future research should investigate the potential moderating effects of these and other cultural factors to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the underlying mechanisms. Furthermore, developing culturally sensitive interventions that target these factors will help to better support university students’ career development.

Conclusion

Although the role of career adaptability in individual career development is well-documented, the underlying mechanisms by which it influences subjective well-being via job search behavior have remained underexplored. This study addresses this gap by clarifying the “how” and “when” of career adaptability’s impact on university students’ job search behavior and subjective well-being, shedding light on how students can improve their job search strategies and well-being during the job search process. The findings revealed that career adaptability positively predicts job search behavior and, in turn, enhances university students’ subjective well-being through this behavior. Furthermore, career choice optimistic bias, as an internal factor, can amplify the effects of career adaptability and further influence university students’ subjective well-being during the job search process. Therefore, training and enhancing university students’ career adaptability represents an effective strategy for improving their job search behavior and subjective well-being.
