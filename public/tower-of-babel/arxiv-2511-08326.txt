A General Ziv–Zakai Bound for DoA Estimation in MIMO Radar Systems
Mohammadreza Bakhshizadeh Mohajer ∗ , Daniela Tuninetti † , and Luca Barletta ∗

Abstract
This paper derives a Ziv–Zakai Bound (ZZB) on the Mean Squared Error (MSE) for Direction-of-Arrival (DoA) estimation in co-located Multiple-Input Multiple-Output (MIMO) radar systems and provides closed-form expressions that hold for multi-target scenarios. Unlike classical results that address single-input multiple-output systems with complex Gaussian input signals, the developed ZZB in this paper explicitly accounts for a general input covariance matrix, target radar cross-section statistics and multiple snapshot effects, and admits a compact expression that reveals the dependence of the MSE on the number of transmit antennas, number of targets, Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) and the transmit covariance matrix. Numerical simulations validate the tightness of the ZZB in the a priori dominated region and show how the increase of the number of transmit antennas compresses the threshold SNR for the transition to the Cramér-Rao bound (CRB) while the variation of the number of targets shifts the bound’s behavior across SNR regimes. The analytical results and numerical simulations demonstrate that the ZZB is tighter than the CRB, particularly in the low SNR regime.



I
Introduction

Multiple‑Input Multiple‑Output (MIMO) radar has attracted considerable interest for modern sensing applications because, by transmitting
different waveforms from each antenna rather than scaled copies of the same signal, it provides increased degrees of freedom and greater operational flexibility
[
1
]
. These additional degrees of freedom directly benefit core estimation tasks such as Direction‑of‑Arrival (DoA) estimation, which is central to radar, sonar, and wireless sensing systems and determines spatial resolution and interference resilience
[
2
]
. At the same time, next‑generation wireless networks, such as 6G, aim to combine high‑fidelity connectivity with accurate, resilient sensing. Integrated Sensing and Communication (ISAC) meets this need by unifying sensing and communications on a common hardware platform and waveform, allowing systems to exploit MIMO’s waveform diversity to improve DoA estimation while reducing form factor and power consumption
[
3
]
.

Mean Square Error (MSE) is the standard metric for assessing estimator performance, but for nonlinear parameter estimation problems such as DoA estimation there is no closed‑form expression for the minimum achievable MSE
[
2
]
. This absence motivates the use of lower bounds on the MSE to characterize estimator performance. The Cramér–Rao Bound (CRB), derived from the inverse Fisher information, is the most widely used lower bound; however, because it characterizes only small estimation errors, it is a local bound and can be ineffective for assessing estimator performance in non‑asymptotic regimes
[
4
]
. To capture the
a priori
performance region at low SNR, a variety of Bayesian lower bounds have been developed over the past decades, including rate‑distortion–based bounds
[
5
]
, bounds derived from functional inequalities such as log‑Sobolev inequalities
[
6
,
7
]
, and bounds built on variational representations of divergence measures
[
8
]
; in this work we adopt the Ziv–Zakai Bound (ZZB)
[
9
,
10
]
as a global MSE lower bound.

The scalar ZZB was introduced in
[
9
]
, and extensions to vector parameters were developed in
[
11
]
. The vector ZZB was applied to 2‑D DoA for the single target case in
[
12
]
, while
[
2
]
generalized the ZZB from 1‑D DoA single‑target to multi‑target scenarios by using order statistics and by ordering both estimated and true DoAs to resolve permutation ambiguity. These prior works, however, focus on Single-Input Multiple-Output (SIMO) architectures and assume zero‑mean complex white Gaussian transmit signals, which limits their applicability for analyzing the sensing–communication trade‑off in ISAC systems. In this paper we extend the ZZB to co‑located MIMO radar and derive a closed‑form expression for transmitted waveforms with a general covariance matrix.

Contributions:

The main contributions of this paper are:

•

Derivation of a closed‑form ZZB for multi‑target DoA estimation in co‑located MIMO radar systems.

•

Analytical treatment for a general transmit covariance matrix that captures the most general case, including ISAC waveforms.

•

Insights into the operating regimes where the CRB becomes invalid and the ZZB provides a tight performance prediction.

II
System Model

We consider a co-located MIMO radar with
N
transmit and
M
receive antennas observing
K
point targets with DoA
\mathbold{\theta}=[\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{K}]^{\mathsf{T}}
. The received signal is expressed as

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbf{a}(\theta_{k})b_{k}\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}}(\theta_{k}){\bf\Phi}+\mathbf{Z},

(1)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}(\mathbold{\theta})\,\mathbf{B}\,\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathbold{\theta}){\bf\Phi}+\mathbf{Z},

(2)

where
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times L}
with
L
being the number of snapshots (or channel uses),
\mathbf{A}(\mathbold{\theta})=[\mathbf{a}(\theta_{1}),\dots,\mathbf{a}(\theta_{K})]
and
\mathbf{V}(\mathbold{\theta})=[\mathbf{v}(\theta_{1}),\dots,\mathbf{v}(\theta_{K})]
denote the receive and transmit steering matrices with

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}(\theta_{k})

\displaystyle=\left[e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}d_{1}\sin{\theta_{k}}},\dots,e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}d_{M}\sin{\theta_{k}}}\right]^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times 1},

(3)

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}(\theta_{k})

\displaystyle=\left[e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}d_{1}\sin{\theta_{k}}},\dots,e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}d_{N}\sin{\theta_{k}}}\right]^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times 1},

(4)

where the
d_{i}
’s are the antenna positions in the linear array, and

\mathbf{B}=\text{diag}(\mathbf{b})
is a diagonal matrix of target reflection coefficients
\mathbf{b}=[b_{1},\dots,b_{K}]
that are assumed to be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) complex random variables with finite second moment, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[|b_{k}|^{2}]<\infty
.
The rows of
{\bf\Phi}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times L}
are the transmitted waveforms in time with identically distributed columns, and
\mathbf{Z}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times L}
is Additive White Gaussian Noise (AWGN) with i.i.d.
\sim\mathcal{C}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{n}^{2})
entries.

III
CRB and ZZB Preliminaries

Permutation ambiguity denotes the invariance of the channel likelihood
f_{\mathbf{X}\mid\mathbold{\theta}}
to any relabeling of the DoA vector
\mathbold{\theta}
: the measurements depend on the set of source directions, not on their ordering. Consequently, practical estimators impose a canonical labeling (e.g., sorting the estimates in ascending order), which maps the parameters to order statistics thus modifying the joint prior pdf
f{\theta}
. Therefore, the MSE should be computed on the ordered parameters as
[
2
]

\displaystyle\text{MSE}=\frac{1}{K}\sum^{K}_{k=1}\mathbb{E}\left[(\hat{\theta}_{(k)}-\theta_{(k)})^{2}\right],

(5)

where
\hat{\theta}_{(k)}
and
\theta_{(k)}
are the
k
-th smallest order statistics in
\hat{\mathbold{\theta}}
and
\mathbold{\theta}
, respectively.

A widely used lower bound on the estimation error in DoA estimation is the CRB, which equals the inverse of the Fisher information matrix. For a random vector
\mathbold{\theta}
, a lower bound on the MSE is given by the expected CRB as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum^{K}_{k=1}\mathbb{E}\left[(\hat{\theta}_{k}-\theta_{k})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{K}{\text{tr}}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbold{\theta}}[\text{CRB}(\mathbold{\theta})]\right)

(6)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K}{\text{tr}}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbold{\theta}}[\mathbf{J}^{-1}(\mathbold{\theta})]\right),

(7)

where the
ij
-th entry of the Fisher information matrix
\mathbf{J}(\mathbold{\theta})
can be written as
[
13
]

\displaystyle J_{ij}(\mathbold{\theta})=L\>{\text{tr}}\left((\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{x}|\mathbold{\theta}})^{-1}\frac{\partial\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{x}|\mathbold{\theta}}}{\partial\theta{{}_{i}}}(\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{x}|\mathbold{\theta}})^{-1}\frac{\partial\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{x}|\mathbold{\theta}}}{\partial\theta{{}_{j}}}\right),

(8)

where
\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{x}|\mathbold{\theta}}(\mathbold{\theta})
is the covariance matrix of a column of
\mathbf{X}
given
\mathbold{\theta}
.

Since the columns of
\bf\Phi
are identically distributed, the covariance matrix of a snapshot of
\mathbf{X}
given
\mathbold{\theta}
is

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{x}|\mathbold{\theta}}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\dagger}\;\middle|\;\mathbold{\theta}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}(\mathbold{\theta})\mathbf{B}\mathbf{V}^{T}(\mathbold{\theta}){\bf\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{*}(\mathbold{\theta})\mathbf{B}^{\dagger}\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}(\mathbold{\theta})+\sigma_{n}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{M}

(9)

for each snapshot, where
\mathbf{x}
is a column of
\mathbf{X}
and
\bf\Sigma
is the covariance matrix of a column of
\bf\Phi
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\mathbf{R}}_{\mathbf{x}|\mathbold{\theta}}}{\partial\theta_{i}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\partial{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbold{\theta})}{\partial\theta_{i}}\right)\mathbf{B}\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathbold{\theta}){\bf\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{*}(\mathbold{\theta})\mathbf{B}^{\dagger}\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}(\mathbold{\theta})

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbf{A}(\mathbold{\theta})\mathbf{B}\left(\frac{\partial{\mathbf{V}}^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathbold{\theta})}{\partial\theta_{i}}\right){\bf\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{*}(\mathbold{\theta})\mathbf{B}^{\dagger}\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}(\mathbold{\theta})

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbf{A}(\mathbold{\theta})\mathbf{B}\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathbold{\theta}){\bf\Sigma}\left(\frac{\partial{\mathbf{V}}^{*}(\mathbold{\theta})}{\partial\theta_{i}}\right)\mathbf{B}^{\dagger}\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}(\mathbold{\theta})

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbf{A}(\mathbold{\theta})\mathbf{B}\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathbold{\theta}){\bf\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{*}(\mathbold{\theta})\mathbf{B}^{\dagger}\left(\frac{\partial{\mathbf{A}}^{\dagger}(\mathbold{\theta})}{\partial\theta_{i}}\right),

(10)

and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{A}(\mathbold{\theta})}{\partial\theta_{i}}

\displaystyle=\left(-j\frac{2\pi\cos{\theta_{i}}}{\lambda}\mathbf{d}_{\text{Tx}}(\mathbf{e}_{i})^{\mathsf{T}}\right)\odot\mathbf{A}(\mathbold{\theta})

(11)

\displaystyle=-j\frac{2\pi\cos{\theta_{i}}}{\lambda}\mathbf{D}_{M}\mathbf{A}(\mathbold{\theta}){\mathbf{E}}_{i},

(12)

where
\mathbf{d}_{\text{Tx}}=[d_{1},\ldots,d_{M}]^{\mathsf{T}}
,
\mathbf{D}_{M}=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{\text{Tx}})
,
\mathbf{e}_{i}
denotes the
i
th column of the identity matrix,
{\mathbf{E}}_{i}=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{e}_{i})
, and in (
12
) we used the identity
(\mathbf{y}\mathbf{x}^{\dagger})\odot\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{y}}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\dagger}
.
Similarly,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}(\mathbold{\theta})}{\partial\theta_{i}}

\displaystyle=j\frac{2\pi\cos{\theta_{i}}}{\lambda}{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}(\mathbold{\theta})\mathbf{D}_{M}^{\mathsf{T}},

(13)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathbold{\theta})}{\partial\theta_{i}}

\displaystyle=-j\frac{2\pi\cos{\theta_{i}}}{\lambda}{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathbold{\theta})\mathbf{D}_{N}^{\mathsf{T}},

(14)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{V}^{\star}(\mathbold{\theta})}{\partial\theta_{i}}

\displaystyle=j\frac{2\pi\cos{\theta_{i}}}{\lambda}\mathbf{D}_{N}\mathbf{V}^{\star}(\mathbold{\theta}){\mathbf{E}}_{i}.

(15)

Remark 1

The Bayesian CRB (BCRB) is applicable when a circular prior pdf, such as the von Mises distribution, models the distribution of
\theta
. However, the von Mises distribution is not an appropriate surrogate for a uniform prior constrained to a strict sub-interval of
[-\pi,\pi)
, as it cannot exactly represent a truncated uniform distribution over a finite angular segment. In this paper, we do not consider the BCRB, since we employ a prior pdf supported on
[\vartheta_{\min},\vartheta_{\max}]\subset[-\pi,\pi)
.

Following
[
2
]
, the ZZB for vector parameter estimation to lower bound the error correlation matrix is
[
11
, Eq. (36)]
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}^{T}\mathbf{R}_{\mathbold{\epsilon}}\mathbf{a}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\max_{\mathbold{\delta}:\mathbf{a}^{T}\mathbold{\delta}=h}\Bigg[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{K}}(f_{\mathbold{\theta}}(\mathbold{\phi})+f_{\mathbold{\theta}}(\mathbold{\phi}+\mathbold{\delta}))

\displaystyle\quad\cdot P_{\text{min}}(\mathbold{\phi},\mathbold{\phi}+\mathbold{\delta})d\mathbold{\phi}\Bigg]h\,dh,

(16)

where
P_{\text{min}}(\mathbold{\phi},\mathbold{\phi}+\mathbold{\delta})
is the minimum probability of error in the following binary hypothesis detection problem

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{0}:\mathbold{\theta}=\mathbold{\phi};\hskip 11.49994pt

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}\sim f_{\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbold{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbold{\phi})

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{1}:\mathbold{\theta}=\mathbold{\phi}+\mathbold{\delta};\hskip 11.49994pt

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}\sim f_{\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbold{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbold{\phi}+\mathbold{\delta})

(17)

with

\displaystyle\text{Pr}(\mathcal{H}_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{\mathbold{\theta}}(\mathbold{\phi})}{f_{\mathbold{\theta}}(\mathbold{\phi})+f_{\mathbold{\theta}}(\mathbold{\phi}+\mathbold{\delta})},\quad\text{Pr}(\mathcal{H}_{1})=1-\text{Pr}(\mathcal{H}_{0}).

(18)

Remark 2

The joint
a priori
pdf that should, in principle, be used in the derivation of the ZZB is that of the order statistics
\{\theta_{(k)}\}_{k}
. However, to keep the computation tractable, we instead use the joint

\displaystyle P_{\text{min}}(\mathbold{\phi},\mathbold{\phi}+\mathbold{\delta})\geq P_{e}(\mathbold{\delta})

\displaystyle\triangleq\exp{\left\{\mu(\frac{1}{2};\mathbold{\delta})+\frac{1}{8}\ddot{\mu}(\frac{1}{2};\mathbold{\delta})\right\}}\mathsf{Q}\left(\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\ddot{\mu}(\frac{1}{2};\mathbold{\delta})}\right),

(19)

where

\displaystyle\mu(s;\mathbold{\delta})=\text{ln}\int f_{\mathbf{X}\mid\mathbold{\theta}}(\mathbf{X}|\mathbold{\phi}+\mathbold{\delta})^{s}f_{\mathbf{X}\mid\mathbold{\theta}}(\mathbf{X}|\mathbold{\phi})^{1-s}d\mathbf{X},

(20)

is the semi-invariant moment generating function of
\mathbf{X}
given
\mathbold{\theta}
and

\displaystyle\mathsf{Q}(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{z}^{\infty}\exp{\left(-\frac{u^{2}}{2}\right)}du,

(21)

is the tail distribution function of the standard normal distribution.
A closed-form expression for
\mu(s;\mathbold{\delta})
, from
[
12
]
, is

\displaystyle\mu(s;\mathbold{\delta})

\displaystyle=L\Big[s\ln|\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{x}|\mathbold{\phi}}|+(1-s)\ln|\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{x}|\mathbold{\phi}+\mathbold{\delta}}|

\displaystyle\quad-\ln|s\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{x}|\mathbold{\phi}}+(1-s)\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{x}|\mathbold{\phi}+\mathbold{\delta}}|\Big],

(22)

where
\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{x}|\mathbold{\phi}}\triangleq\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{x}|\mathbold{\theta}=\mathbold{\phi}}
and
\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{x}|\mathbold{\phi}+\mathbold{\delta}}\triangleq\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{x}|\mathbold{\theta}=\mathbold{\phi}+\mathbold{\delta}}
.
The second derivative of
\mu(s;\mathbold{\delta})
with respect to
s
is

\displaystyle\ddot{\mu}(s;\mathbold{\delta})=L{\text{tr}}\left(\left[[s\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{x}|\mathbold{\phi}}+(1-s)\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{x}|\mathbold{\phi}+\mathbold{\delta}}]^{-1}\mathbf{R}_{-}\right]^{2}\right),

(23)

where
\mathbf{R}_{-}=\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{x}|\mathbold{\phi}}-\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{x}|\mathbold{\phi}+\mathbold{\delta}}
. By denoting
\mathbf{R}_{+}=\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{x}|\mathbold{\phi}}+\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{x}|\mathbold{\phi}+\mathbold{\delta}}
, and for equally likely hypotheses,

; we have

\displaystyle\mu(s;\mathbold{\delta})|_{s=\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=L\left[\frac{\ln(|\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{x}|\mathbold{\phi}}||\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{x}|\mathbold{\phi}+\mathbold{\delta}}|)}{2}-\ln\left|\frac{\mathbf{R}_{+}}{2}\right|\right],

(24)

\displaystyle\ddot{\mu}(s;\mathbold{\delta})|_{s=\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=4L{\text{tr}}\left((\mathbf{R}_{+}^{-1}\mathbf{R}_{-})^{2}\right).

(25)

IV
Generalized ZZB for Multiple Target MIMO Radar

In this section, we derive a bound of the form (
III
) for the general waveform model. We assume that, for sufficiently large
K
, the following approximation holds:

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}^{*}(\mathbold{\phi})\mathbf{B}^{\dagger}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathbold{\phi})\approx\|\mathbf{B}\|_{F}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{N},

(26)

that is, we assume that the backscattered energy is not concentrated in a single component, i.e., no individual coefficient
|b_{i}|
dominates over the others. The approximation in (
26
) makes the evaluation of the ZZB tractable, since the right-hand side (RHS) of (
26
) is independent of the hypothesis
\mathbold{\phi}
.

For brevity, we omit the detailed derivations of the ZZB and present only the resulting expressions; we have

\displaystyle\mu(s;\mathbold{\delta})|_{s=\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\approx\begin{cases}-\frac{1}{8}\mathbold{\delta}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{J}\mathbold{\delta},&\mathbold{\delta}\in\Delta\\
L\left[\ln{\frac{\left|\mathbf{I}_{N}+\frac{M}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{F}^{2}{\bf\Sigma}\right|}{\left|\mathbf{I}_{N}+\frac{M}{2\sigma_{n}^{2}}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{F}^{2}{\bf\Sigma}\right|^{2}}}\right],&\mathbold{\delta}\notin\Delta\end{cases}

(27)

and

\displaystyle\ddot{\mu}(s;\mathbold{\delta})|_{s=\frac{1}{2}}\approx\begin{cases}\mathbold{\delta}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{J}\mathbold{\delta},&\mathbold{\delta}\in\Delta\\
\alpha_{\text{G}}&\mathbold{\delta}\notin\Delta\end{cases}

(28)

where
\mathbf{J}
is the Fisher information matrix,
\alpha_{\text{G}}
is given in (
33
)
at the top of the next page, and
\Delta
is defined as

\displaystyle\Delta=\left\{\mathbold{\delta}:\mathbold{\delta}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{J}\mathbold{\delta}\leq\alpha_{\text{G}}\right\}.

(29)

Consequently,
P_{e}(\mathbold{\delta})
in (
III
) is approximated as

\displaystyle P_{e}(\mathbold{\delta})\approx\begin{cases}P_{S}(\mathbold{\delta}),&\mathbold{\delta}\in\Delta\\
P_{L},&\mathbold{\delta}\notin\Delta\end{cases}

(30)

with

\displaystyle P_{S}(\mathbold{\delta})\approx\mathsf{Q}\left(\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\mathbold{\delta}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{J}\mathbold{\delta}}\right),

(31)

and

\displaystyle P_{L}=\exp\left\{L\cdot\ln{\frac{\left|\mathbf{I}_{N}+\frac{M}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{F}^{2}{\bf\Sigma}\right|}{\left|\mathbf{I}_{N}+\frac{M}{2\sigma_{n}^{2}}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{F}^{2}{\bf\Sigma}\right|^{2}}}+\frac{\alpha_{\text{G}}}{8}\right\}\mathsf{Q}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\alpha_{\text{G}}}}{2}\right).

(32)

\alpha_{\text{G}}=4L{\text{tr}}\left(\frac{M^{2}}{2\sigma_{n}^{4}}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{F}^{4}{\bf\Sigma}^{2}+\frac{M^{4}}{8\sigma_{n}^{8}}\left[\|\mathbf{B}\|_{F}^{4}{\bf\Sigma}^{2}(\mathbf{I}_{N}+\frac{M}{2\sigma_{n}^{2}}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{F}^{2}{\bf\Sigma})^{-1}\right]^{2}-\frac{M^{3}}{2\sigma_{n}^{6}}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{F}^{6}{\bf\Sigma}^{3}(\mathbf{I}_{N}+\frac{M}{2\sigma_{n}^{2}}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{F}^{2}{\bf\Sigma})^{-1}\right)

(33)

IV-A

Derivation of the Bound

We begin by evaluating the inner integral in (
III
). Assuming that the DoAs are i.i.d.
\sim\mathcal{U}[\vartheta_{\text{min}},\vartheta_{\text{max}}]
and following the results in
[
2
]
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{K}}(f_{\mathbold{\theta}}(\mathbold{\phi})+f_{\mathbold{\theta}}(\mathbold{\phi}+\mathbold{\delta}))\times P_{\text{min}}(\mathbold{\phi},\mathbold{\phi}+\mathbold{\delta})d\mathbold{\phi}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{P_{\epsilon}(\mathbold{\delta})}{\zeta^{K}}\int_{\Phi}d\phi=\frac{P_{\epsilon}(\mathbold{\delta})}{\zeta^{K}}\prod_{k=1}^{K}(\zeta-|\delta_{k}|),

(34)

where

\displaystyle\Phi=\{\mathbold{\phi}|\phi_{k}\in[\vartheta_{\text{min}},\vartheta_{\text{max}}-|\delta_{k}|],k=1,2,\dots,K\},

(35)

is the
K
dimensional integration region, and
\zeta=\vartheta_{\text{max}}-\vartheta_{\text{min}}
denotes the range of DoAs.

Following the framework outlined in
[
2
,
12
]
, the optimization problem in (
III
) can be written as

\displaystyle\max_{\mathbold{\delta}:{\bf 1}^{\mathsf{T}}_{K}\mathbold{\delta}=\sqrt{K}h}\frac{P_{\epsilon}(\mathbold{\delta})}{\zeta^{K}}\prod_{k=1}^{K}(\zeta-|\delta_{k}|)\approx\!\!\!\!\!\max_{\mathbold{\delta}:{\bf 1}^{\mathsf{T}}_{K}\mathbold{\delta}=\sqrt{K}h}\frac{P_{L}}{\zeta^{K}}\prod_{k=1}^{K}(\zeta-|\delta_{k}|)

\displaystyle\quad+\max_{\mathbold{\delta}\in\Delta:{\bf 1}^{\mathsf{T}}_{K}\mathbold{\delta}=\sqrt{K}h}P_{S}(\mathbold{\delta})-P_{L}.

(36)

As shown in
[
2
]
, in order to maximize the first term on the RHS of (
IV-A
), given that
{\bf 1}^{\mathsf{T}}_{K}\mathbold{\delta}
is fixed, it is sufficient to consider only positive values of
\delta_{k}
. Any negative
\delta_{k}
would require increasing the magnitude of other
\delta_{k}

\displaystyle\max_{\mathbold{\delta}:{\bf 1}^{\mathsf{T}}_{K}\mathbold{\delta}=\sqrt{K}h}\frac{P_{L}}{\zeta^{K}}\prod_{k=1}^{K}(\zeta-|\delta_{k}|)=P_{L}\left(1-\frac{h}{\sqrt{K}\zeta}\right)^{K}.

(37)

Using the Lagrange multiplier method, the maximum of the second term in the RHS of (
IV-A
) occurs at

\displaystyle\mathbold{\delta}=\sqrt{K}h\frac{\mathbf{J}^{-1}{\bf 1}_{K}}{{\bf 1}_{K}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{J}^{-1}{\bf 1}_{K}},

(38)

and then we obtain

\displaystyle\max_{\mathbold{\delta}\in\Delta:{\bf 1}^{\mathsf{T}}_{K}\mathbold{\delta}=\sqrt{K}h}\quad P_{S}(\mathbold{\delta})=\mathsf{Q}\left(\frac{\sqrt{K}h}{2\sqrt{{\bf 1}_{K}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{J}^{-1}{\bf 1}_{K}}}\right).

(39)

\displaystyle\max_{\mathbold{\delta}:{\bf 1}^{\mathsf{T}}_{K}\mathbold{\delta}=\sqrt{K}h}\frac{P_{\epsilon}(\mathbold{\delta})}{\zeta^{K}}\prod_{k=1}^{K}(\zeta-|\delta_{k}|)\approx P_{L}\left(1-\frac{h}{\sqrt{K}\zeta}\right)^{K}

\displaystyle\quad+\begin{cases}\mathsf{Q}\left(\frac{\sqrt{K}h}{2\sqrt{{\bf 1}_{K}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{J}^{-1}{\bf 1}_{K}}}\right)-P_{L}&0\leq h\leq\tilde{h},\\

\displaystyle\tilde{h}\approx\min\left[\sqrt{\frac{{\bf 1}_{K}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{J}^{-1}{\bf 1}_{K}\alpha_{\text{G}}}{K}},\sqrt{K}\zeta\right],

(41)

is the derived from

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{K}h}{2\sqrt{{\bf 1}_{K}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{J}^{-1}{\bf 1}_{K}}}\approx\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\alpha},

(42)

with constraint
0\leq\tilde{h}\leq\sqrt{K}\zeta

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}{\bf 1}^{\mathsf{T}}_{K}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{\tilde{h}}\left[\mathsf{Q}\left(\frac{\sqrt{K}h}{2\sqrt{{\bf 1}_{K}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{J}^{-1}{\bf 1}_{K}}}\right)-P_{L}\right]h\,dh.

Our results substantially advance the characterization of the ZZB compared with prior work, and extend the analysis to more realistic scenarios and representative MIMO radar configurations. A limitation of the current study is that both the ZZB and the CRB have been derived under models that depend on the ensemble (average) transmit covariance matrix rather than the realized sample covariance matrix; consequently, the present expressions do not help in capturing the sensing-communication performance tradeoff controlled by the statistics of the transmit waveforms in ISAC settings

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we presented a closed-form Ziv–Zakai bound (ZZB) for multi-target direction of arrival (DoA) estimation in co-located multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) radar that explicitly incorporates a general transmit covariance matrix and practical signal assumptions. The bound generalizes prior single-input multiple-output results to co-located MIMO and integrated sensing and communications (ISAC) waveforms. We analytically characterized the roles of transmit and receive antennas, number of targets, signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), and snapshot count in setting the estimator threshold. The derived bound remains tight in the
a priori dominated
region where the Cramér-Rao bound (CRB) fails to provide meaningful limits. Numerical results corroborated these claims and illustrated practical design insights: increasing the number of transmit antennas closes the gap between ZZB and CRB at progressively lower SNRs, while increasing the number of targets alters both the
a priori
and asymptotic behaviors of the bound. We believe that
the results of this work enable the evaluation of the DoA estimation and communication performance tradeoff of ISAC systems in the presence of multiple targets.
