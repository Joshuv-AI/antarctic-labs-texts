Testing Identifiability and Transportability with Observational and Experimental Data
Konstantina Lelova, Gregory F. Cooper, Sofia Triantafillou

Abstract
Transporting causal information learned from experiments in one population to another is a critical challenge in clinical research and decision-making. Causal transportability uses causal graphs to model differences between the source and target populations and identifies conditions under which causal effects learned from experiments can be reused in a different population. Similarly, causal identifiability identifies conditions under which causal effects can be estimated from observational data. However, these approaches rely on knowing the causal graph, which is often unavailable in real-world settings. In this work, we propose a Bayesian method for assessing whether 𝐙 \mathbf{Z} -specific (conditional) causal effects are both identifiable and transportable, without knowing the causal graph. Our method combines experimental data from the source population with observational data from the target population to compute the probability that a causal effect is both identifiable from observational data and transportable. When this holds, we leverage both observational data from the target domain and experimental data from the source domain to obtain an unbiased, efficient estimator of the causal effect in the target population. Using simulations, we demonstrate that our method correctly identifies transportable causal effects and improves causal effect estimation.

Introduction

Estimating causal effects is a fundamental problem in understanding and predicting the impact of interventions.
Experimental data, where treatments are randomized, are necessary for unbiased estimation of causal effects. However, experiments are costly and difficult to implement, so experimental data are typically scarce. In addition, the results from an experimental study done in one population cannot always be transported to a different population.
In the clinical domain, clinicians often assume that the causal effect is transportable across domains, and use the causal effects estimated in a source domain to predict post-intervention outcomes in a target domain. However, without knowing the causal graph, we cannot know if the effects are indeed transportable.

Observational data are much more plentiful. However, they are not necessarily valid for causal effect estimation, due to the presence of unmeasured variables that may confound treatments and outcomes. Both observational and experimental data are becoming increasingly available: Recent findings report that 80.5% of hospitals have at least a basic EHR system
(
Adler-Milstein et al. 2017
)
. In addition, summary-level and individual participant-level data from clinical trials are increasingly being shared with the scientific community
(
Sim 2022
)
.
In this work, we propose a method for combining experimental data from a source population with observational data from the target population to test whether a causal effect is both identifiable from observational data and transportable. This allows us to use unbiased, low-variance estimators based on both data, when possible.

We adopt the framework of

Selection Diagrams.
Different domains may have different distributions for some variables. These differences can be described using

Bareinboim and Pearl 2012
. In selection diagrams, selection variables,
\mathbf{S}
(graphically depicted as square nodes) represent the mechanisms by which the two domains differ. Conditioning to different values of these variables allows us to switch between domains. We assume that each domain has a causal graph (
\mathcal{G},\mathcal{G}^{*}
for source and target domain, respectively). Differences in mechanisms are described by different functions
f_{i}
for some of the variables.

Definition 1
.

(Selection Diagram)
Let
\langle M,M^{*}\rangle

\langle\Pi,\Pi^{*}\rangle

and
\mathcal{G}^{*}

is constructed as follows:

1.

Every edge in
\mathcal{G}
and every edge in
\mathcal{G}^{*}
is also an edge in
\mathcal{D}
;

2.

D contains an extra edge
S_{i}\rightarrow V_{i}
whenever there might exist a discrepancy
f_{i}\neq f_{i}^{*}
or
P(V_{i})\neq P^{*}(V_{i})
between
M
and
M^{*}
.

When a variable does not have an incoming selection variable, this indicates the mechanism responsible for assigning values to that variable is the same in both domains. We use

, and

Transportability.
The area of transportability focuses on identifying graphical conditions which allow for transporting a causal effect
P(Y\mid do(X),\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{s})
in the source domain to a causal effect
P(Y\mid do(X),\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{s}^{*})
in the target domain. Sets that make the outcome independent of the selection variables are called
s-admissible
and are shown to satisfy the following
(
Pearl and Bareinboim 2011
)
.

Theorem 1

(S-admissibility)
.

Let
D
be the selection diagram characterizing
\Pi
and
\Pi^{*}
, and
\mathbf{S}
the set of selection variables in
D
. The
Z
-specific causal effect
P(Y\mid do(X),\mathbf{Z})
is transportable from
\Pi
to
\Pi^{*}
if
\mathbf{Z}
d-separates
Y
from
\mathbf{S}
in the
X
-manipulated version of
D
, that is,
\mathbf{Z}
satisfies
(Y\perp\!\!\!\perp\mathbf{S}\mid\mathbf{Z})_{D_{\overline{X}}}
. A set
\mathbf{Z}
that satisfies this condition is called
s-admissible
.
For s-admissible sets,

P(Y|do(X),\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{s})=P(Y|do(X),\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{s}^{*})

(3)

s-admissibility stems directly for Rule 1 of do-calculus. For faithful distributions, the reverse also holds: Eq.
3
implies that
\mathbf{Z}
is s-admissible, since
P(Y|do(X),\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{s})=P(Y|do(X),\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{s}^{*})
indicates that
S
is independent from
Y
given
\mathbf{Z}
in the post-intervention selection diagram.

Identifiability:
When experimental data are not available, the area of identifiability focuses on graphical criteria for identifying the post-intervention distribution from the observational distribution
in the same domain
(if possible), when the graph is known. For pre-treatment covariates
\mathbf{Z}
, the
P(Y|do(X),\mathbf{Z})
is identifiable from observational data when
\mathbf{Z}
satisfies the backdoor criterion for
X,Y
in the causal graph
\mathcal{G}

(
Pearl 2009
)
:

Theorem 2

(special case of Rule 2 of do-calculus)
.

Let
G
be the DAG associated with a causal model, and let
P(\cdot)
stand for the probability distribution induced by that model. Then if
(Y\perp\!\!\!\perp X\mid\mathbf{Z})_{G_{\underline{X}}}
, the following equation holds:

P(Y|do(X),\mathbf{Z})=P(Y|X,\mathbf{Z})

(4)

Sets that d-separate
Y
and
X
in
G_{\underline{X}}
are called back-door sets. Similar to s-admissible sets, for faithful distributions, Eq.
4
also implies that set
\mathbf{Z}
is a backdoor set.

The areas of transportability and identifiability provide graphical criteria that allow us to identify and generalize causal effects from observational and/or interventional distributions in different domains, when the graph is known. Unfortunately, the causal graph is often unknown. In the next sections, we propose a method for testing if these criteria hold when the graph is unknown.

Example 4

To summarize: We propose using observational and experimental data to identify if an s-admissible backdoor set exists. If such a set exists, we can use joint estimator based on observational and experimental data for the target domain. If multiple such sets exist, our method chooses the one that maximizes the marginal likelihood of the post-intervention outcome. If no such set exists, our method returns

There might be alternative estimators, based on only observational or only experimental data, which are better (i.e., include more variables that are informative for the target post-intervention outcome). However, without knowing the causal graph, we cannot know if these estimators are unbiased or not. Finding an s-admissible backdoor set ensures that the corresponding estimator will be unbiased.

[sABS-faithfulness] If

The underlying idea of our method is the following: If a set of variables

In the next section, we propose (a) a method for computing the probability that a set is an sABS and (b) a search strategy for identifying an optimal sABS, if one exists.

Related Work

Our proposed work looks for s-admissible backdoor sets, using experimental data from a source distribution and observational data from a target distribution. To our knowledge, this is the first algorithm that can compute probabilities for s-admissible backdoor sets in this setting. Our work has connections to several areas, outlined below:

Identifiability/Adjustment
One line of work focuses on identifying interventional probabilities from observational data in the same domain. For average effects,
VanderWeele and Shpitser 2011
and
Entner et al. 2013
provide sound rules for identifying adjustment sets from observational data. Adjustment sets are generalizations of backdoor sets that may include post-treatment variables.
Perkovic et al. 2017
;
Rotnitzky and Smucler 2020
;
Smucler et al. 2020
;
Witte et al. 2020
, provide graphical criteria for identifying adjustment sets. These works assume knowing the causal graph, or learning a set of graphs consistent with observational data.
(
Shpitser and Pearl 2006
;
Tian and Shpitser 2003
;
Jaber et al. 2019
)
focus on identifiability of the post-intervention distributions from observational data. These methods can answer if a causal query is identifiable from observational distribution in the same domain, based on d-separation constraints in the causal graph.
Triantafillou and Cooper 2021
use observational and experimental data to rank adjustment sets and select the best adjustment set
\mathbf{Z}
for estimating the ATE
P(Y|do(X))
. Their approach is similar to ours, but they focus on estimating average effects, and they assume the studies are performed in the same population (while they allow for some selection in the experimental data due to selection criteria).
Triantafillou and Cooper 2021
;
Triantafillou et al. 2023
use a similar approach to compute the probability that a set is an adjustment set given observational and experimental data from a single domain.

Transportability
The area of transportability focuses on generalizing causal knowledge from one or more source domains to a target domain. This problem was formally introduced by
Pearl and Bareinboim 2011
, who defined selection diagrams and provided graphical conditions for transportability, such as s-admissiblity, when the selection diagram is known.
Bareinboim and Pearl 2012
provide a complete algorithm for computing transport formulae. Extensions include transportability problems where experiments at the source can only be conducted over limited sets of variables
\mathbf{Z}

(
Bareinboim and Pearl 2013a
)
,
and meta-transportability, which transfers causal knowledge from a heterogeneous collection of source domains to a target domain where only observations are available
(
Bareinboim and Pearl 2013b
)
. Do-calculus is shown to be complete for transportability
(
Bareinboim and Pearl 2014
)
. These works form the theoretical framework for transferring causal knowledge across domains,
when the selection diagram is known.

Combining data for effect estimation.
There is also growing body of work for combining observational and experimental data in the field of potential outcomes, mostly focusing on improving the external validity of the RCT (See
Colnet et al. 2022
and references therein). Most of these works rely on the assumption of unconfoundness (known as conditional ignorability in the potential outcomes framework) and s-admissibility. Under these assumptions, they provide estimators that correct the bias of the source experimental data (with respect to the target population), by leveraging the covariate distribution of an observational sample from the target domain. However, these methods assume that unconfoundedness and s-admissibility hold for the set of measured covariates.

Yang et al. 2023
propose an elastic integrative estimator which adaptively incorporates observational data only when the observational and experimental data do not indicate significant discrepancies. Their approach ensures that the estimator performs at least as well as the RCT-only estimator, hence, they assume that the effect is transportable to the target domain. In addition, their method condition on all observed covariates. In contrast, we focus on identifying sets for which
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{z}}
is likely to hold; As a result, the methods are not directly comparable.

Causal structure learning.
When the graph is unknown, one approach is to use causal discovery methods to find the causal structure, and then use graphical criteria to determine if a set is sABS.
(
Hyttinen et al. 2014
;
Andrews et al. 2020
;
Triantafillou and Tsamardinos 2015
;
Mooij et al. 2020
)
combine observational and experimental data, possibly from multiple domains, to learn the causal graph. Additionally, some of the methods focus on combining Conditional (In) dependence constraints from the data to answer if causal effects are identifiable
(
Hyttinen et al. 2015
)
. Constraint-based methods focus on
answering if
a
\mathbf{Z}
-specific causal effect is identifiable, and return a formula for estimation based on observational data, if it is. In contrast, our method casts effect estimation as a feature selection problem, and
selects
the set that maximizes the marginal likelihood of the experimental data. Moreover, current implementations of these methods do not support mixed data, while FindsABS works with any statistical model for which marginal likelihoods can be estimated. Developing constraint-based methods that also select an optimal
\mathbf{Z}
-specific effect is interesting future work, and could work synergistically to our approach.

7
Conclusions

In this work, we introduced a Bayesian method for testing whether conditional causal effects are transportable across domains when the underlying causal structure is unknown. Our approach estimates the probability that a set of observed covariates forms an
s-admissible backdoor set
(sABS), allowing causal effect estimation from a combination of source experimental and target observational data. Unlike previous work that assumes the transportability or identifiability of causal effects based on known causal graphs, our method relies solely on data-driven inference. In simulated data, our method is shown to correctly identify s-admissible backdoor sets and improve causal effect estimation across different environments.

In the future, we plan to explore extending this method to include sample selection bias. This is frequent in experimental studies, when patients are often included in a study based on strict inclusion/exclusion criteria. Finally, applying this method to real-world datasets in clinical settings would further validate its practical utility.

Andrews et al. 2020
discuss how to perform causal discovery from different domains and experiments. Specifically, one can model domain differences with an exogenous context variable
C
and experiments with an exogenous instrumental variable
I
. Additionally, knowledge on the ordering of the variables can be included as knowledge tiers in FCI.
Andrews et al. 2020
shows that this version of FCI, called FCItiers, is complete in these settings.

We applied FCItiers with background knowledge in the data described in Sec.
10.2
. We used a binary context variable
C
to denote domain differences, and instrumental variable
I
to model the randomization of
X
in the source domain.
Andrews et al. 2020
suggest to add these variables in a tier that precedes all others. We also added a second tier that includes the pre-treatment variables, a third tier that includes the treatment, and a final tier including the outcome. Notice that
C
and
I
are identical in this setting, since randomization only happens in the source domain. In order to avoid any tests that include these two variables, we imposed that some edges are forbidden: Specifically, we did not allow any edges between
C
and
I
, between
I
and the pre-treatment covariates, between
I
and the outcome, and between
C
and
X
. We then ran FCItiers with this knowledge, to obtain the selection diagram
\mathcal{D}
. For each set
\mathbf{Z}
, we tested if
\mathbf{Z}
is an s-admissible backdoor set by testing (a) If
X
and
Y
are m-separated given
\mathbf{Z}
in
D_{\underline{X}}
(true for backdoor sets). and (b) If
C
and
Y
are m-separated given
\mathbf{Z}
in
D_{\overline{X}}
(true for s-admissible sets). If both conditions are satisfied, the set was predicted to be an s-admissible backdoor set. Since this method only outputs yes/no, we cannot compute AUCs. Instead, we computed the true positive rate and false positive rate for the predictions, and included it in the AUCs plots of Fig.
7
. As we can see, our method outperforms FCItiers in these settings.

Definition 2

(Conditional Entropy)
.

Let
P
be the full joint probability distribution over a set of variables
\boldsymbol{V}
, let
Y\in\boldsymbol{V}
be a variable, and let
\mathbf{Z}\subseteq\boldsymbol{V}\setminus\{Y\}
be a set of variables. Then, the conditional entropy of
Y
given
\mathbf{Z}
is defined as follows
[
Cover 1999
]
:

H(Y|\mathbf{Z})=-\sum_{y}{\sum_{z}{P(y,z)\cdot\log P(y|z)}}

(11)

where
y
and
z
denote the values of
Y
and
\mathbf{Z}
, respectively.

Lemma 1
.

Let
X,Y\in\boldsymbol{V}
be two variables and
\mathbf{Z}\subseteq\boldsymbol{V}\setminus\{X,Y\}
be a set of variables. Then,
H(Y|\mathbf{Z})\geq H(Y|X,\mathbf{Z})
, where the entropies are defined by Definition
2
, and the equality holds if and only if
Y{\,\perp\mkern-12.0mu\perp\,}X|\mathbf{Z}
.

Proof.

Applying the chain rule of entropy, the conditional mutual information can be computed as follows
Cover 1999
:

I(X;Y|\mathbf{Z})=H(Y|\mathbf{Z})-H(Y|X,\mathbf{Z})\,.

(12)

Given that the mutual information is nonnegative (i.e.,
I(X;Y|\mathbf{Z})\geq 0
) and
I(X;Y|\mathbf{Z})=0
if and only if
Y{\,\perp\mkern-12.0mu\perp\,}X|\mathbf{Z}
(see
Cover 1999
, page 29), it follows that:

\begin{split}H(Y|\mathbf{Z})-H(Y|X,\mathbf{Z})\geq 0\\
H(Y|\mathbf{Z})\geq H(Y|X,\mathbf{Z})\,,\end{split}

(13)

where the equality holds if and only if
Y{\,\perp\mkern-12.0mu\perp\,}X|\mathbf{Z}
.
∎
