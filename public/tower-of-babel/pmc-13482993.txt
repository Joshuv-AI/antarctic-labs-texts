Comparative DFT investigation of 1T and 2H phase Janus TaSSe monolayers as potential sensors for toxic gas molecules
Authors: Afiya Akter Piya, Anirbun Paul Arko, Akash Kumer Paul, Aditi Ahmed Ananna, Bivas Kumar Dash, Siraj Ud Daula Shamim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were employed to investigate a comparative analysis of the gas-sensing performance of 2D Janus TaSSe monolayers in both 1T and 2H structural phases. The adsorption characteristics of several toxic gases, including AsH3, H2S, NH3, COCl2, O3, and PH3, were analyzed. Our results indicate that the 1T phase is thermodynamically more stable and exhibits stronger gas–surface interactions compared to the 2H phase. Among the studied gases, AsH3 shows the highest adsorption energy (Eads), reaching − 2.4 eV on the Se-site of 1T-TaSSe and − 2.125 eV on the S-site of 2H-TaSSe. For the remaining toxic gases, the Eads range from − 0.542 eV to -1.970 eV in the 1T phase, and from − 0.187 eV to -1.706 eV in the 2H phase. Notably, PH3 demonstrates the second-highest adsorption affinity, while H2S exhibits the weakest interaction. Despite these relatively strong interactions, analyses based on charge density difference (CDD), electron localization function (ELF), and reduced density gradient (RDG) confirm that the adsorption mechanism is governed by strong physisorption through van der Waals interactions, without the formation of chemical bonds. Recovery time () analysis indicates efficient regeneration behavior. At 500 K, the for AsH3 adsorption on the S-site are 1.52106 s for 1T-TaSSe and 2.63103 s for 2H-TaSSe. In contrast, for NH3 at 300 K, significantly faster recovery times are observed, with values of 0.591 s for 1T (S-site) and 0.41 ms for 2H (S-site). These findings suggest that 1T phase is comparatively more sensitive for the selected gases, while the 2H phase is advantageous for rapid recovery in the case of weakly interacting gases such as H2S, NH3, and COCl2.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55851-9.Keywords: Janus TaSSe, Density functional theory, Toxic gas sensor, Work function, RDG analysisSubject terms: Chemistry, Materials science, Physics

Introduction

Since the first industrial revolution began in the 18th century, accelerated industrialization has increased the emission of hazardous gases into the atmosphere1. As a result, environmental contamination arising from industrial waste and harmful byproducts has become a major global concern, posing serious threats to both the environment and human health. Among these harmful gases are hydride gases (PH3, AsH3, H2S), nitrogen-based gases (NH3), halogenated gases (COCl2), and oxidizing gases (O3), which are produced from semiconductor industries, chemical industries, agricultural activities, and combustion processes2–4. Ozone (O3) is a pale blue gas with a strong odor similar to that produced by lightning or electrical equipment5,6. Exposure to O3 can reduce lung function, aggravate asthma, and irritate the respiratory system7,8. Colorless, highly toxic, and flammable gases such as Arsine (AsH3), Phosphine (PH3), and hydrogen sulfide (H2S) pose serious risks to human health9–11. Overexposure to AsH3 and PH3 can affect the central nervous system and lungs, causing symptoms such as headaches, vomiting, chest tightness, and respiratory distress12,13. Similarly, exposure to H2S may lead to irritation of the eyes and respiratory tract, dizziness, neurological disorders, and may even result in death at high concentrations14,15. Another hazardous gas is NH3, which at high concentrations can cause irritation of the eyes, skin, and throat, as well as lung inflammation and pulmonary edema that may become fatal with prolonged exposure12,16. Phosgene (COCl2) is a highly toxic gas with an odor similar to freshly cut hay at low concentrations and a strong, unpleasant smell at higher concentrations17,18. Exposure to COCl2 can severely damage the respiratory system, leading to pulmonary edema, emphysema, and even death19,20. To ensure a safe and healthy environment, continuous monitoring of these toxic gases is essential. This responsibility has motivated researchers to develop novel methods to detect these toxic gases.

Nowadays, the development of sensitive, selective, and rapid-response gas sensors has become a significant research focus21–23. Researchers are exploring a wide range of advanced sensing materials for the effective detection of toxic gases. However, conventional gas-sensing technologies often suffer from several limitations, including high operating temperatures, slow response times, and poor selectivity24. Recently, materials such as metal oxides, two-dimensional (2D) materials, transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs), graphene-based materials and MXenes have been investigated for their large surface areas and superior electronic properties25–29. Among these, TMDs have recently emerged as a particularly promising class of materials for gas sensing. The TMD family, including MoS2, WS2, TaS2, NbSe2, and others, exhibits a wide range of physical and chemical properties, along with a high surface-to-volume ratio, good carrier mobility, a tunable band gap, and useful electrical and magnetic properties30–32. More recently, researchers have focused on Janus TMDs, which generally follow the formula MXY, where M represents a transition-metal atom, X and Y are two different chalcogen atoms33,34. Janus TMDs, due to their structural asymmetry, exhibit unique properties compared to conventional TMDs, including intrinsic dipole moments, tunable electronic structures, and enhanced adsorption capability, making them promising candidates for gas sensing technologies35,36. In addition, their electronic properties can vary depending on the structural phase, such as the metallic or semi-metallic trigonal (1T) phase and the semiconducting hexagonal (2H) phase, which can further influence the gas adsorption behavior and sensing performance37,38. Moreover, density functional theory (DFT) has been widely utilized to investigate the surface interaction properties of various materials and to obtain reliable results. Wadha Alfalasi et al. conducted an atomic-scale DFT study on 2D MoSSe nanosheets, which exhibited half-metallicity and served as a promising host material for CO2, NO, NO2, CO, H2, H2S, and NH3 gases, with adsorption energies ranging from − 0.051 to -0.74928. Sifat Reza et al. observed the sensing performance of ScSSe, TiSSe, and ZrSSe nanosheets for oxygen-containing toxic gases (CO, NO, NO2, SO2). In their study, they found Sc-based Janus TMD exhibits excellent gas adsorption (− 1.41 to − 1.61 eV) in different adsorption sites39. Shawon M and Hasan M et al. studied the 1T phase of Janus TMDs nanosheets for sensing performance with nitrogen-based toxic gases (NO, NO2, NH3)40,41. Recently, Ta-based Janus TMDs have been identified as promising candidates for sensing devices due to their structural stability and tunable electronic properties42. Especially, TaSSe monolayers can exist in both 1T and 2H phases, presenting unique electronic structures that may significantly affect gas-surface interactions43. Yet, a comprehensive investigation of the adsorption features and sensing efficacy of TaSSe monolayers in the presence of hazardous gases such as AsH3, H2S, NH3, COCl2, O3, and PH3 remains largely unexplored.

This study employs first-principles DFT calculations to investigate the gas-sensing properties of Janus TaSSe nanosheets in both 1T and 2H phases. The selected toxic gases are analyzed based on adsorption behavior, charge transfer, electronic properties (including band structure, density of states, and work function), recovery time, and RDG analysis. A comparison of the two phases is conducted to provide a better understanding of the sensing mechanisms for detecting harmful gases. The results are expected to offer valuable theoretical insights for the development of high-performance nanoscale gas sensors based on Janus TMDs.

Computational details

To investigate the interactions between the TaSSe monolayer and gas molecules, DFT calculations were performed using the DMol3 module40,44. The Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) was employed to describe the exchange–correlation interactions of electrons45. This functional was used for structural optimization, adsorption energy calculations, and charge-transfer analysis due to its reliable description of geometrical properties and computational efficiency for periodic systems. Since the standard GGA-PBE functional typically underestimates band gaps, the Heyd–Scuseria–Ernzerhof (HSE06) hybrid functional was employed for unit-cell calculations in the CASTEP module to obtain more accurate electronic properties46. Although spin polarization was included to correctly describe the magnetic states, spin–orbit coupling (SOC) was not considered due to the very high computational cost of hybrid functional calculations for such large systems. The Tkatchenko–Scheffler (TS) dispersion correction was employed to improve the description of long-range van der Waals interactions47. The electronic wavefunctions were expanded using the Double Numerical plus Polarization (DNP) 4.4 basis set. This basis set significantly reduces the Basis Set Superposition Error (BSSE) and provides an accuracy level comparable to high-quality Gaussian triple-zeta basis sets, such as 6-311+G(3df,2pd), without requiring additional counterpoise corrections48,49. In this study, a 3 × 3 × 1 supercell containing 27 atoms and a 20 Å vacuum layer was used. All calculations were performed using a global cutoff radius of 5.6 Å and a k-point grid of 4 × 4 × 1, which ensures accurate and efficient calculations for the nanosheet system. During geometry optimization, the maximum force and maximum displacement were set to 0.002Ha/Å and 0.005 Å, respectively, while the energy convergence threshold was set to 1 × 10⁻⁵ Ha. The gas molecules were initially placed at energetically favorable hollow sites based on previous studies and surface symmetry, followed by full unconstrained geometry optimization to obtain the most stable adsorption configurations. Results and discussion

Geometric structures of pristine 1T and 2H TaSSe

In this study, a 3 × 3 × 1 supercell of the Janus TaSSe monolayer was constructed to investigate its gas-sensing performance. The pristine system comprises 27 atoms, consisting of 9 Ta, 9 S, and 9 Se atoms. To comprehensively evaluate the material, both the 1T and 2H structural phases of TaSSe were modeled. The calculated lattice constants for the TaSSe monolayer are 3.573 Å for the 1T phase and 3.278 Å for the 2H phase. As shown in Fig. 1, the pristine TaSSe monolayer has a tri-layer structure with the central Ta plane sandwiched between S and Se atoms, forming an intrinsic structural asymmetry. For the 1T phase, the optimized structure shows an S–Ta–Se bond angle of 91.98°, and the Ta–S and Ta–Se bond lengths were 2.52 Å and 2.63 Å, respectively. On the other hand, the optimized 2H phase exhibits an S–Ta–Se bond angle of 83.76°, with corresponding Ta–S and Ta–Se bond lengths of 2.47 Å and 2.59 Å. The electronic properties of the pristine 1T and 2H TaSSe monolayers were investigated using the high-precision HSE06 hybrid functional. Fig. 3(a–d) displays their band structures along the high-symmetry path (-M-K-) alongside the corresponding partial density of states (PDOS). The results reveal that the 1T and 2H phase exhibit metallic behavior. This finding is consistent with previous reports on 1T-TaSSe and 1T and 2H TaS2 systems42,43,51,52. The band structure and PDOS plots clearly show asymmetric spin distribution across the energy levels, confirming the spin-polarized nature of the systems. Interestingly, PBE-GGA calculations (Fig. 3(b,d)) fail to capture the intrinsic spin polarization, while the HSE06 hybrid functional is necessary to correctly describe the true electronic ground state of the monolayers. Furthermore, PDOS analysis provides a clear understanding of the elemental contributions to these electronic states. The results indicate that the energy states around the Fermi level (EF) are formed by the strong hybridization of p and d orbitals. The valence and conduction bands are mostly dominated by the p-orbitals of the S/Se and the d-orbitals of the Ta, with almost no contribution from s-orbitals. These active p-d hybridized states near the EF ensure excellent charge-transfer capabilities. This suggests that both phases are highly promising candidates for gas-sensing applications. Adsorption behavior of AsH3, H2S, NH3, COCl2, O3, and PH3 on the 1T-TaSSe

We have investigated the adsorption behavior of AsH3, H2S, NH3, COCl2, O3, and PH3 gas molecules by analyzing the Eads, the shortest adsorption distance (dmin), and the Hirshfeld charge analysis (Qt) between the monolayer and the gases, which are shown in Table 1. In this study, we have considered two adsorption sites. One is the S site, and the other is the Se site of TaSSe. The gases are adsorbed parallel to the 1T phase of the nanosheet, as illustrated in Fig. 5. On these sites, we observed that the Eads is significantly higher, and all values are negative, which means the attractive interactions and exothermic process in the system. More specifically, the Eads at the S site are − 2.399, -0.553, -0.700, -0.697, -0.707, -1.970 eV at the dmin of 3.708, 2.989, 3.21, 3.372, 3.031, and 3.231 Å for AsH3, H2S, NH3, COCl2, O3, and PH3, respectively. Among all the gases, AsH3 adsorbs more strongly than any other toxic gases, and the lowest Eads is for H2S. Thus, the orders of the Eads are AsH3 > PH3 > O3> NH3> COCl2 > H2S. Similarly, the Eads is negative and significant at the Se site of the monolayer. Numerically, the values of the Eads are − 2.40, -0.542, -0.676, -0.639, -0.837, and − 1.94 eV, and the dmin are 3.308, 3.315, 3.377, 3.499, 3.039, and 3.363 Å for AsH3, H2S, NH3, COCl2, O3, and PH3, respectively. Interestingly, the Eads order is identical to that of the S site. On both sides of the nanosheet, the energies are almost similar; however, for O3 gas, the Eads is slightly higher on the Se site. The Eads of AsH3 and PH3 are relatively high, which suggests chemisorption. However, the large dmin (e.g., 3.708 Å for AsH3) is much greater than covalent bond lengths, indicating that no chemical bond is formed. Previous studies have also shown that polar 2D materials can exhibit strong physisorption with Eads above 1.0 eV due to enhanced electrostatic and dispersion interaction54,55. For example, Meribah Jasmine et al. reported a PH3 Eads of − 1.808 eV with a 2.47 Å dmin on Mo-vacancy MoS2 without covalent bond formation, and Yuepeng Yang et al. observed physisorption of CO on ReSe2 with an Eads of − 1.49 eV and dmin of ~ 3 Å 11,56. In the Janus TaSSe monolayer, the built-in dipole caused by structural asymmetry strengthens the gas–surface interaction, resulting in dipole-enhanced strong physisorption rather than chemisorption. According to Ziyi Chai et al., the maximum Eads reported for AsH3 on Ti-doped S-vacancy-defected NiSSe (Ti-VS-NiSSe) is -1.494 eV9. Muhammad Huzaifa et al. reported that Au-functionalized Ti3C2 exhibits high sensing efficiency, with Eads of -0.54 eV (COCl2), -0.71 eV (H2S), and − 0.66 eV (NH3), which is consistent with our findings57. An Eads of -0.036 eV for PH3 on N-doped MoSe2 has been reported by Mim Khatun et al.58. An Eads of -0.118 eV for O3 on in-plane grapheme-boron nitride (G-BN) has been reported by Mahabub Hasan Rocky et al.59. Comparison with these reported nanosheets indicates that the predicted monolayer 1T-TaSSe exhibits significantly higher Eads in all cases. Thus, this nanosheet on both sites (S and Se) of the 1T phase exhibits significant sensing potential.

Actually, an isolated gas does not carry any charge; i.e., it has a charge of zero before adsorption. However, when they are adsorbed on a monolayer, they either acquire or lose charges from or to the monolayer. Thus, a positive charge indicates that electrons are being transferred from gas molecules to the monolayer surface, indicating that the nanosheet is accepting electrons. On the other hand, when the value of charge is negative, meaning the gas molecules accept electrons and the nanosheet donates them, indicating the transfer of electrons from the nanosheet to the gases. The Hirshfeld charge analysis indicates that the monolayer and the gases interact strongly, with significant Qt. For the S site, 0.012 and 0.003e of charges are transferred from AsH3 and H2S gas molecules to the nanosheet, while 0.007, 0.001, 0.167, and 0.028e of charges are transferred from the nanosheet to NH3, COCl2, O3, and PH3 gases, respectively. The Qt increases in the order of COCl2 < H2S < NH3 < AsH3 < PH3 < O3. While for the Se site, the charge of 0.012, 0.001e is transferred from the H2S and COCl2 gas molecules, whereas the monolayer transfers 0.028, 0.02, 0.226, and 0.026e of charges to the AsH3, NH3, O3, and PH3 gases, respectively. The Qt at the Se site of the 1T phase of the nanosheet is larger in all cases except for COCl2 and PH3. However, the increasing order is identical with the S Site. The significant Qt suggests strong interactions between the adsorbate and the substrate.

Adsorption behavior of AsH3, H2S, NH3, COCl2, O3, and PH3 on the 2H-TaSSe

As in the previous section, to gain deeper insight into the adsorption behavior, we also have calculated the values of Eads, dmin, and Qt for the 2H phase of the nanosheet shown in Table 2. The gases are adsorbed parallel to the 2H phase of the nanosheet, as illustrated in Fig. 6. Here we have also considered two sites (S and Se) where we adsorbed the toxic gases in parallel to the nanosheet. Similar to the 1T phase of the monolayer, a strong interaction occurs on both sites in the 2H phase of the monolayer. According to our observation, the Eads are negative, which means that an exothermic reaction of the adsorption process and also implies that attractive connections occur between the monolayer and the gases. At the S Site, the values of Eads are − 2.125, -0.250, -0.512, -0.385, -0.290, and − 1.706 eV for AsH3, H2S, NH3, COCl2, O3, and PH3 gas molecules adsorbed on the monolayer at a dmin of 3.04, 3.21, 2.814, 3.62, 3.031, and 2.857 Å, respectively. Although we obtain high adsorption energies, when compared with the S site at the 1T phase of the monolayer, the energies are much lower. The Eads decrease in the order of AsH3 > PH3 > NH3> COCl2 > O3 > H2S. Similar to the 1T phase, we also observed the highest adsorption energy when AsH3 is adsorbed and the lowest for H2S. For the Se site, the Eads are − 2.098 eV for AsH3, -0.187 eV for H2S, -0.432 eV for NH3, -0.288 eV for COCl2, -0.342 eV for O3, and − 1.679 eV for PH3, and the dmin are 2.792, 3.351, 3.29, 3.428, 3.105, 3.271 Å, respectively. Numerically, the decreasing order of the Eads is AsH3 > PH3 > NH3 > O3 > COCl2 > H2S. Similar to the 1T phase, the Eads are slightly lower at the Se site than at the S site, except for the O3 gas molecule. Also, for the Se site of the 2H phase, the Eads is much lower than that of the Se site of the 1T phase. Thus, the higher adsorption and sensing performance of the 1T-TaSSe phase compared to the 2H phase is due to its more favorable electronic structure, which enhances stronger interaction with gas molecules. This also results in slightly higher charge transfer in the 1T phase, which supports its improved sensing behavior. An Eads of -0.39 eV for O3 on B-embedded graphene has been reported by Lei Chen et al.60. In another study, Badran H et al. reported Eads of -0.307 eV (NH3), -0.340 eV (PH3), and − 0.344 eV (O3) on MnPc (mode 2)61. Additionally, Ahmed M et al. found Eads of -0.23 eV (H2S), -0.47 eV (NH3), and − 0.19 eV (COCl2) for Mobius boron-carbon-nitride62. In comparison with these studies, our predicted monolayer TaSSe exhibits significantly higher adsorption energies, indicating its superior potential for gas sensing applications. From our Hirshfeld charge analysis, we also observed, on both sites, that a considerable amount of charge is transferred between the nanosheet and the gas molecule during adsorption. For the S site, when AsH3, O3, and PH3 gas molecules are adsorbed on the monolayer, 0.011, 0.113, and 0.017e are transferred from the nanosheet to the gas molecules, respectively. However, after adsorbing H2S, NH3, and COCl2 gases, 0.074, 0.009, and 0.008e of charges are transferred from the gases to the monolayer. The order of Qt for this site is COCl2 < NH3 < AsH3 < PH3 < H2S < O3. At the Se site, the Qt after adsorption is quite similar, with 0.027, 0.019, 0.163, and 0.031e transferred from TaSSe to AsH3, NH3, O3, and PH3, respectively. On the other hand, after adsorption of H2S and COCl2, 0.009 and 0.004e of charge are transferred to TaSSe. Numerically, the order of the absolute values of the Qt is COCl2 < H2S < NH3 < AsH3 < PH3< O3. On both sites and for both phases of the nanosheet, we obtain the highest charge for the adsorption of O3 gas molecules. Such a notable charge transfer indicates a strong binding interaction within the system.

According to our analysis, the intrinsic φ values are 5.293 eV for pristine 1T-TaSSe and 5.89 eV for pristine 2H-TaSSe. The pristine 2H-TaSSe phase has a higher φ of 5.89 eV. The difference between the 1T (5.293 eV) and 2H (5.89 eV) phases arises from their different crystal structures and electronic properties, which lead to different EF positions relative to the Evacuum. The φ upon gas adsorption is a parameter for determining sensor sensitivity. For O3, the φ increases for all systems, showing a positive change. This rise indicates that charge transfers from the nanosheet to the surrounding O3 molecules, which enhances the potential barrier. Among all the gases, O3 showed the strongest response. The 1T-TaSSe phase exhibited excellent sensitivity, with a 4.07% increase on the Se site and a 2.95% increase on the S site. The 2H phase also showing up to a 2.39% increase on the Se site. This significant response suggests that TaSSe monolayers are superior for O3 detection. This opposite trend in work function variation is directly linked to the direction of charge transfer, where O3 acts as an electron acceptor from the TaSSe surface, while AsH3 donates electrons to it. Therefore, the magnitude and sign of Δφ are governed by charge redistribution, which ultimately determines the selective sensing response of the monolayer.

On the other hand, a reduction of φ is observed for H2S and AsH3 on specific sites. This decrease corresponds to the donation of electrons from the gas molecules to the nanosheets. For instance, AsH3 causes a notable φ reduction of 1.83% on the 1T-TaSSe (Se site). Similarly, H2S causes a decrease of 1.31% on the 1T-TaSSe (S site) and 1.14% on the 2H-TaSSe (S site). Other gases like NH3, COCl2, and PH3 show relatively φ (below 1%), indicating weaker interactions compared to O3. As a result, the sensing response of the TaSSe monolayers is strongly selective. The 1T-TaSSe phase shows the highest sensitivity toward O3 and a distinct negative response toward AsH3 and H2S. This demonstrates that TaSSe monolayers can be effective as a work function-type sensor for selective gas detection.

Furthermore, at 300 K and an of 1012 s-1, the are 1.64 × 10-8 s (S site) and 1.38 × 10-9 s (Se site) for H2S; 4.06 × 10-4 s (S site) and 1.84 × 10-5 s (Se site) for NH3; 2.97 × 10-6 s (S site) and 6.86 × 10-8 s (Se site) for COCl2; and 7.40 × 10-8 s (S site) and 5.58 × 10-7 s (Se site) for O3. Overall, all the adsorbents demonstrate rapid recovery from the adsorbed gas molecules except AsH3, which is highly desirable for gas sensing applications. The exceptionally long recovery time of AsH3 suggests possible permanent sensor poisoning, indicating that the 1T-TaSSe monolayer may act as an irreversible AsH3 capture material rather than a reusable sensor. Although the adsorption of AsH3 and PH3 is relatively strong, leading to longer recovery times under standard conditions, this behavior also indicates high sensitivity and stable detection capability. In practical applications, the long recovery time may limit rapid reusability and continuous sensing performance. However, previous studies have suggested that external stimulation methods, such as thermal treatment and UV irradiation, can effectively accelerate the desorption process and improve sensor regeneration69. Therefore, the proposed material may still be suitable for highly sensitive and selective gas detection applications.

Electronic properties

The DOS provides a fundamental understanding of a material electronic behavior by describing the distribution of available electronic states at various energy levels70. As shown by electronic structure analysis, both 1T and 2H phases are metallic. Gas adsorption modifies the electronic states near the EF, tuning the overall electronic properties of the material. As illustrated in Fig. 8(a–d), the Total DOS (TDOS) of 1T and 2H TaSSe monolayers was analyzed before and after the adsorption of various target gases. Gas adsorption changes the density of states near the EF (N(EF)), which affects the electrical resistance () of the metallic TaSSe monolayers. The pristine 1T-TaSSe monolayer exhibits a finite N(EF) of approximately 15.5 states/eV, while the pristine 2H-TaSSe monolayer shows a comparatively higher N(EF) value of about 37 states/eV near the EF, confirming their metallic nature. As shown in Tables 1 and 2, the variation in N(EF) strongly depends on the structural phase of the material. The 1T phase exhibits a significant redistribution of electronic states after gas adsorption, particularly for O3, where the change in DOS (N(EF)) reaches 19.35%. In contrast, the 2H phase shows comparatively smaller changes for all target gases, with the maximum (N(EF)) being only 2.70% for O3. These pronounced N(EF), especially in the 1T phase, indicate strong surface interactions between the adsorbed gas molecules and the monolayer, which are favorable for chemiresistive sensing applications. Moreover, the observed DOS modulation is consistent with the corresponding , suggesting that the proposed monolayers may effectively operate through a dual-mode sensing mechanism. For weakly interacting gases, the adsorption produces only a negligible change in the DOS, reflecting standard physisorption with minimal charge transfer. To gain deeper insights into this electronic modulation, the band structures of the stable adsorption configurations were calculated. To avoid unnecessary repetition from showing all 24 configurations, we selected the systems that showed the highest changes in electronic properties and adsorption energies. These representative systems include AsH3 (Se site), H2S (S site), COCl2 (S site), and O3 (Se site) on 1T-TaSSe (Fig. 9(a–d)); and NH3 (S site) and PH3 (Se site) on 2H-TaSSe (Fig. 9(e–f)). The charge transfer mechanism and bonding nature between the TaSSe substrate and the gas molecules were visualized using Charge Density Difference (CDD), Electron Localization Function (ELF), and Total Charge Density (TCD) analyses. These visualizations are shown in Fig. 10(a–f) for the same selected representative systems. TCD shows the overall spatial distribution of electrons, while CDD highlights the regions of charge accumulation and depletion caused by gas adsorption. The CDD maps (isosurface value of 0.005 e/Å³) display how electrons redistribute during the adsorption process. The red regions indicate electron accumulation, representing charge gain. The green regions indicate electron depletion, representing charge loss. This clearly shows the direction of charge transfer between the gas molecules and the monolayer surface. The ELF analysis measures the probability of finding localized electrons, with values ranging from 0 (delocalized) to 1 (highly localized). The ELF maps show no continuous, highly localized bonding regions bridging the gas molecules and the surface atoms of the monolayers. There are no shared electron pairs between the gas and the substrate. The TCD distributions provide a clear view of the orbital overlap. For all the gas molecules, the electron clouds of the gases remain completely separated from the TaSSe nanosheets. There is no orbital overlap or electron sharing observed between the molecules and the surfaces. The absence of localized electron sharing and the complete separation of electron clouds indicate that no chemical bonds form. Therefore, the interaction between the target gases and the pristine 1T and 2H TaSSe surfaces is entirely physical in nature, demonstrating strong physisorption.

RDG analysis

The Reduced Density Gradient (RDG) is a computational approach for visualizing and analyzing non-covalent interactions (NCIs) using the Multiwfn package71,72. It helps identify weak interactions both inside a single molecule and between different molecules. Fig. 11(a–f) displays the RDG scatter plots and their corresponding 3D NCI isosurfaces (isosurface value of 0.05 e/Å³) for selected adsorption systems: (a–d) AsH3 (Se site), H2S (S site), COCl2 (S site), and O3 (Se site) on 1T-TaSSe; and (e–f) NH3 (S site), and PH3 (Se site) on 2H-TaSSe.

RDG scatter plots and 3D NCI isosurfaces (isosurface value of 0.05 e/Å3) for selected adsorption systems: (a–d) AsH3 (Se), H2S (S), COCl2 (S), and O3 (Se) on 1T-TaSSe; and (e,f) NH3 (S), and PH3 (Se) on 2 H-TaSSe.

In the scatter plots, the RDG is placed on the vertical axis against sign on the horizontal axis. Here, is the electron density, and is the second Hessian eigenvalue73. The interactions are shown using a simple red-green-blue color scale based on the sign value, which gives clear information about the type and strength of the interaction. The blue areas (sign < 0) show strong attractive interactions, like hydrogen bonds. The green areas (sign 0) show weak attractive interactions, mostly van der Waals (vdW) forces. The red areas (sign > 0) show strong repulsive forces71,73. For all the adsorption systems in this study, the scatter plots display a highly noticeable spike right at the green region near zero. This matches the green area visible in the 3D isosurfaces forming in the gap between the gas molecules and the nanosheet. The clear presence of this green region confirms that the molecules are attached to the surface through van der Waals interactions. This finding supports our previous charge analysis, proving that the process is strong physisorption without any chemical bond formation. Finally, the minor red and blue spikes seen in the plots originate from the internal atomic forces within the gas molecules themselves, rather than any interaction with the nanosheet.

Conclusions

Using first-principles DFT calculations, we investigated the gas-sensing performance of two-dimensional 1T and 2H Janus TaSSe monolayers toward toxic gases. High-accuracy HSE06 calculations reveal that the pristine 1T and 2H phase exhibits metallic behavior. The results further demonstrate a strong phase- and gas-dependent interaction mechanism. While both surfaces exhibit weak to moderate physisorption for gases such as H2S (Eads = − 0.187 to − 0.553 eV), they show pronounced selectivity and strong affinity toward AsH3 and O3. In particular, AsH3 exhibits significant adsorption energies (Eads) of − 2.400 eV on the Se-site of 1T-TaSSe and − 2.125 eV on the S-site of 2H-TaSSe, whereas O3 demonstrates Eads of − 0.837 eV on the Se-site of 1T-TaSSe and − 0.342 eV on the Se-site of 2H-TaSSe. Compared with the 2H phase, the 1T phase exhibits superior surface reactivity. In particular, 1T-TaSSe exhibits outstanding O3 sensing performance, showing the highest work function modulation (up to + 4.07%) along with a significant 19.35% change in the DOS at the Fermi level. This strong correlation between DOS redistribution and work function variation highlights the dual-mode sensing capability of the 1T phase. In contrast, the 2 H phase shows comparatively smaller responses, with a maximum DOS change of 2.70% and work function modulation of + 2.39%. Although the target gases exhibit high Eads, detailed analyses using charge density difference (CDD), electron localization function (ELF), and reduced density gradient (RDG) clearly indicate that the interactions remain strong physisorption, dominated by van der Waals forces, without the forming chemical bonds. In addition, recovery time () analysis reveals distinct desorption behavior. A rapid of 5.91 × 10− 1 s at 300 K is obtained for NH3 adsorption on the 1T S-site, whereas strongly adsorbed species such as AsH3 require thermal activation (500 K) and UV illumination (1018 s-1) to attain practical (on the order of 1.52 × 106 s). Therefore, the 1T-TaSSe acts as an irreversible sensor for AsH3 due to high adsorption energy, whereas the 2H phase is more suitable for reversible sensing with fast recovery for weakly adsorbed gases such as H2S, NH3, and COCl2.
