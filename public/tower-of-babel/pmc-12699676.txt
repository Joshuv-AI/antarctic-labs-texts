Impact of prenatal depression, anxiety and stress on low birth weight: a retrospective case-control study in Karachi, Pakistan
Authors: Fatima Nazir, Sameera Ali Rizvi, Tahir Munir
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMaternal psychological distress is common in Pakistan, yet it remains underaddressed in antenatal care. This study aimed to examine whether prenatal depression, anxiety and stress are associated with low birth weight (LBW) and whether perceived social support mitigates risk among mothers delivering at two tertiary care hospitals in Karachi, Pakistan.MethodsThis was a retrospective case-control study conducted at Dr Ruth K.M. Pfau Civil Hospital and Jinnah Postgraduate Medical Centre. Mothers were enrolled within 48 hours of singleton live birth. Cases were mothers of infants <2500 g (n=196); controls were mothers of infants ≥2500 g (n=196). A precoded structured questionnaire was used to gather information on maternal sociodemographic factors, obstetric factors and psychological factors. Determinants were estimated using multivariable binary logistic regression; adjusted ORs (AORs) and 95% CIs were reported.ResultsMultivariable logistic regression revealed a dose-response association of psychological distress with LBW; mild-to-moderate prenatal depression (AOR 4.62; 95% CI 2.09 to 10.62), severe-to-extremely severe prenatal depression (AOR 9.30; 95% CI 3.73 to 24.38), mild-to-moderate prenatal anxiety (AOR 3.51; 95% CI 1.57 to 8.11), severe-to-extremely severe prenatal anxiety (AOR 6.57; 95% CI 2.85 to 15.85), severe-to-extremely severe prenatal stress (AOR 2.23; 95% CI 2.09 to 2.55). Meanwhile, ‘moderate’ perceived social support (AOR 0.17; 95% CI 0.06 to 0.45) and ‘high’ perceived social support (AOR 0.09; 95% CI 0.03 to 0.23) had a protective effect.ConclusionThis study underscores that maternal psychological distress during pregnancy is linked to higher odds of LBW in a dose-response manner, while social support is protective. This highlights the importance of mental health screening with stepped care and strengthened family support in antenatal care.

Introduction

Pregnancy is a vital period in a woman’s life noted for significant physiological, psychological and hormonal changes. This is a sensitive phase that may increase the susceptibility of this already vulnerable group to psychosocial distress. According to WHO, 15.6% of women in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) experience mental disorders.1 Among these, depression, anxiety and stress are the most prevalent and often comorbid mental health conditions in the prenatal phase.2 3 The factors contributing to psychological distress during pregnancy are multifaceted, including poverty, migration, exposure to violence, natural disasters, emergency and conflict situations and low social support. In South Asia, a meta-analysis reported a pooled prevalence of prenatal depression at 24.3%, with a particularly high prevalence of 32.2% in Pakistan.4 Another observational study from Pakistan has reported 18% of pregnant women to be anxious and/or depressed.5

Compromised prenatal mental health is known to negatively affect pregnancy outcomes, presenting risks for both the mother and the child. Maternal psychological distress has been reported to be adversely associated with brain structure and biochemistry in utero in the fetus.6 It may also increase the likelihood of developing neuropsychiatric, cardiovascular and metabolic conditions later in life.7 But in addition to these long-term health implications for the child,8 the short-term implications such as adverse birth outcomes, specifically low birth weight (LBW), are equally concerning. LBW is a primary contributor to neonatal morbidity and mortality. Some health consequences of LBW include an increased likelihood of impaired growth, reduced intelligence and chronic diseases emerging in adulthood, such as obesity and diabetes.9 Consequently, the high prevalence of LBW reported in LMICs is alarming. As per the Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (MICS) 2018-19, the percentage of weighed live births recorded below 2500 g was 28.7% in Karachi, Pakistan.10

Several studies have demonstrated a link between prenatal stress, depression and anxiety and the risk of delivering a LBW infant.1113 Yet in Pakistan, robust policy evidence on prenatal psychological distress and adverse birth outcomes remains limited and fragmented across small studies. Most local research has centred on postnatal distress, especially postpartum depression, leaving the prenatal period understudied, despite it being the window when prevention is most feasible. Emerging evidence suggests that depression and anxiety may be more common during pregnancy than after childbirth14. For instance, a meta-analysis in Pakistan reported a higher prevalence of prenatal depression (37%) compared with postnatal depression (30%).15

Psychological distress is a modifiable risk factor that can be managed through early identification and intervention in high-risk groups. The limited policy focus on mental health issues in women of reproductive age, coupled with the scarcity of interventions in Pakistan, may be due to a lack of comprehensive and, at times, contradictory evidence.16 This study, therefore, aimed to determine the relationship between prenatal stress, depression and anxiety and LBW among women who gave birth in two tertiary care hospitals located in Karachi, Pakistan. By producing context-specific risk gradients using scalable measures, our findings directly inform integration of mental-health screening and referral into antenatal care (ANC) pathways—an immediately actionable approach to reduce LBW and improve maternal and child health at population scale.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This institution-based, retrospective, unmatched case-control study was conducted from January 2024 to May 2024, in two tertiary care public hospitals: Dr Ruth K.M. Pfau, Civil Hospital (Civil Hospital) and Jinnah Postgraduate Medical Centre (JPMC). Both hospitals are located in Karachi, Sindh Province, Pakistan. Civil Hospital is a 1900-bed tertiary care public hospital, located in South Karachi. About 2 million outpatients report to the hospital annually. The majority of these patients belong to the lower class who are unable to afford treatment. JPMC is one of the largest government hospitals in Karachi, with 1600 beds and affiliated partnerships with the 550-bed National Institute of Child Health and the National Institute of Cardiovascular Diseases. Each year, JPMC serves approximately 1 million patients, admitting around 60 000 of them and conducting 30 000 surgeries and 15 000 deliveries at no cost.

Sample size determination

The sample size was determined using 90% power, a 95% CI, a 1:1 case-to-control ratio, the hypothetical exposure proportion of 15.6% among controls, 30.55% among cases17 and an additional 10% to account for non-response. The overall sample consisted of 392 participants, including 196 cases and 196 controls.

Sampling procedures

To allocate the sample size among the selected healthcare facilities, an equal allocation technique was used. The average number of births per month for Civil Hospital and JPMC is around 300. Since equal births per day were expected from both facilities, a ratio of 1:1 was used to select an equal number of cases from Civil Hospital and JPMC, as depicted in figure 1. The study included postnatal mothers, admitted in the postnatal gynaecological wards of the facilities, who had a singleton pregnancy and had delivered a live birth baby in the last 48 hours. If the baby’s birth weight was <2500 g, the mother was enrolled as a case; otherwise, she was considered a control. Women with a diagnosed gestational morbidity, chronic condition, pregnancy complication or a known mental illness that could hinder rational response, such as schizophrenia or any delusional disorder, were excluded from the study. The hospital birth register was checked by the data collectors on each visit to identify all the mothers whose newborns had been delivered in the past 48 hours with LBW. From this list, all the eligible mothers were selected as cases. For the same 48-hour window and facility, we compiled a control list of all eligible mothers who had delivered newborns with normal birth weight (NBW). To maintain an overall 1:1 case-to-control ratio per hospital throughout the study period, we determined the number of controls to enrol each day as the minimum of (a) the number of cases enrolled that day and (b) the number still required at that hospital to meet the cumulative 1:1 target. When the number of eligible controls exceeded the number needed that day, we drew a simple random sample without replacement of the required size from the control list using a computer-generated random sequence. When eligible controls were fewer than needed, we enrolled all available that day and carried forward the shortfall to subsequent days at the same hospital. If a selected control declined or became ineligible before the interview, we replaced them by drawing the next random ID from the same day’s control list.

Data collection procedure and tools

Data was collected after taking written informed consent from the participants. A precoded structured questionnaire, translated to the local language, was used to gather information on maternal sociodemographic factors, obstetric factors and psychological factors (online supplemental information). This questionnaire was administered by a data collector who read the questions to the participants and recorded their responses. A separate form was attached to the questionnaire that was used to collect data from the healthcare facility’s records related to neonatal birth information, such as gestational age, gender and birth weight. Birth weight (kg) was extracted verbatim from the labour ward register and cross-checked against the infant’s identification wristband. When discrepancies were present, the register value was taken as the reference because it is recorded directly from the delivery-room scale at weighing. All weights represent the first measurement taken as part of routine care.

The Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale (DASS-21) is a 21-item questionnaire, which was used to measure depression, anxiety and stress and has been previously used in pregnant women in Pakistan.18 The Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS) is a 12-item questionnaire, which was used to determine the subjective availability of emotional support. This scale has also been validated and effectively applied to the context of Pakistan in a previous study.19 DASS-21 was administered as an interviewer-read Urdu version by the data collector in a private area. Participants were instructed to rate symptoms during pregnancy (not current postpartum feelings).

The sociodemographic factors included information for both maternal and paternal characteristics such as age, education, occupation, household income and age at marriage. The obstetric history included parity, gravidity, number of ANC visits and history of adverse birth outcomes.

Depression

Depression is a mental health condition marked by enduring feelings of sadness, a diminished interest in activities and an impairment in daily functioning.21 Depression was categorised based on the DASS-21 cut-off scores: normal=0–9, mild=10-13, moderate=14–20, severe=21–27, and extremely severe=28+. It was later recoded into the following three categories during analysis: normal, mild to moderate and severe to extremely severe. This was done to account for the insignificant number of participants in certain categories.

Anxiety

Anxiety is a state of excessive worry, fear or apprehension that can interfere with daily functioning.21 Anxiety was also categorised based on the DASS-21 cut-off scores: normal=0–7, mild=8-9, moderate=10–14, severe=15–19, and extremely severe=20+. It was also reclassified into three categories: normal, mild to moderate and severe to extremely severe.

Stress

Stress refers to the physiological and psychological reactions of the body to perceived challenges or threats.21 Stress was also categorised based on the DASS-21 cut-off scores: normal=0–14, mild =15-18, moderate=19–25, severe=26-33, and extremely severe=34+. It was similarly reclassified into three categories: normal, mild to moderate and severe to extremely severe.

Perceived social support

Perceived social support describes how individuals view the availability of friends, colleagues and family members to provide both practical and emotional assistance during times of need.22 Participants were asked to indicate their level of agreement with a series of statements regarding social support using a Likert scale. Higher average scores on the full scale (or subscales) indicated greater perceived social support. It was categorised as follows: low perceived support=12-35, moderate perceived support=36-60, and high perceived support=61-84.

Statistical analysis

The collected data were entered into IBM SPSS V.21 and analysed with RStudio (V.4.1.2; Boston, USA). Continuous variables were recoded into categories using biologically important cut-offs, wherever required. Categorical variables, such as maternal age, maternal education, husband’s employment, ANC visits and history of LBW, were summarised using frequencies and proportions. Stratified analyses were performed according to the infant’s birth weight status (LBW vs NBW) to assess significant factors, with statistical associations evaluated using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. To identify the determinants of LBW, univariable logistic regression was initially conducted, followed by multivariable logistic regression while adjusting for other variables. We used purposeful selection, where variables with p≤0.25 in bivariable analysis and/or those of clinical relevance were entered into the multivariable model. Adjusted ORs (AORs) with 95% CIs were reported to quantify the strength of associations, with statistical significance defined as p<0.05. Multicollinearity was assessed via variance inflation factors (<5 considered acceptable).

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

Maternal characteristics

A total of 392 mothers participated in the study, with 196 mothers among both cases and controls. The age distribution indicated that mothers in the case group were younger compared with those in the control group. A notable educational disparity was observed, with 86 (66.2%) of the LBW group being non-literate compared with 44 (33.8%) in the NBW group.

Regarding occupation, the majority of mothers in both groups were housewives, with 192 (98.0%) in the LBW group and 186 (94.9%) in the NBW group. The age at marriage showed that most participants were married between the ages of 19 and 29 years, with similar distributions across both groups. Monthly household income was largely consistent, with approximately 70% of mothers in both groups reporting a monthly household income below 30 000 PKR or US$105. In multivariable logistic regression, after adjusting the possible confounders, such as a history of adverse birth outcome, only maternal age, maternal education, gravidity, history of LBW, preterm birth, stress, depression, anxiety and perceived social support remained to be significantly associated with LBW (p<0.05) (table 3).

Compared with mothers aged 18–23 years, those aged 30–35 years had significantly lower odds of delivering an LBW infant (AOR 0.28, 95% CI 0.10 to 0.74). In contrast, mothers who were non-literate had significantly higher odds of delivering an LBW infant compared with those with education at the intermediate level or higher (AOR 3.27, 95% CI 1.27 to 8.78). Additionally, maternal gravidity was a key factor, with mothers who had a gravidity of ≥5 having significantly higher odds of delivering an LBW infant compared with those with a gravidity of 1 (AOR 6.64, 95% CI 1.73 to 28.61). A history of previous LBW further elevated the odds, as mothers who had delivered an LBW infant before were significantly more likely to experience another LBW birth (AOR 4.61, 95% CI 1.66 to 14.21). The timing of delivery also played a critical role, with preterm births being associated with significantly higher odds of LBW compared with full-term births (AOR 3.18, 95% CI 1.47 to 7.11).

Prenatal psychological distress and LBW

Mothers with mild-to-moderate prenatal depression had significantly greater odds of giving birth to an LBW infant than those who did not experience prenatal depression (AOR 4.62, 95% CI 2.09 to 10.62). The situation was even more pronounced for mothers with severe-to-extremely severe prenatal depression, who had twice the odds of delivering an LBW infant (AOR 9.30, 95% CI 3.73 to 24.38). On the other hand, mothers reporting mild-to-moderate prenatal anxiety also had elevated odds of delivering an LBW infant compared with those without anxiety (AOR 3.51, 95% CI 1.57 to 8.11). The odds further increased, doubling for mothers with severe-to-extremely severe anxiety (AOR 6.57, 95% CI 2.85 to 15.85). Similarly, the impact of prenatal stress was evident, with severe-to-extremely severe stress associated with significantly higher odds of delivering an LBW infant compared with those reporting normal stress levels (AOR 2.32, 95% CI 2.09 to 2.55).

Perceived social support showed a protective effect. Mothers with moderate perceived social support had 83% lower odds of delivering an LBW infant compared with those with low perceived social support (AOR 0.17, 95% CI 0.06 to 0.45). The odds were further reduced to 91% in mothers with high perceived social support (AOR 0.09, 95% CI 0.03 to 0.23).

Other factors, such as a history of stillbirth, neonate’s gender, congenital malformations and mild-to-moderate stress, did not show significant associations with LBW after adjustment (p>0.05) (table 3).

Discussion

LBW is a major driver of neonatal morbidity and mortality globally, especially in LMICs like Pakistan. This study investigated the association between prenatal depression, anxiety and stress, and LBW in mothers attending two public tertiary care hospitals in Karachi, Pakistan. The findings indicate a significant association between prenatal psychological distress and the increased likelihood of delivering an LBW infant, with the severity of the distress showing a dose-response relationship.

Maternal depression emerged as a significant predictor of LBW, with mothers experiencing severe-to-extremely severe depressive symptoms having over nine times the odds of delivering an LBW infant compared with those without depression. This finding is consistent with international evidence2325 and contributes much-needed data from Pakistan, where robust studies on prenatal mental health remain limited. Within the country, findings have been mixed: Saeed et al reported a positive association in a prospective cohort, while Husain et al found no such link in a single-centre cohort design.16 26 These discrepancies may reflect differences in population characteristics, regional contexts and measurement approaches. By demonstrating a clear dose-response association in a large, hospital-based case-control sample, our study adds contextual evidence from urban public-sector facilities.

This study also found a significant association between antenatal anxiety and higher odds of LBW, consistent with findings from other studies,2729 such as Chandra et al, who demonstrated a similar association between maternal anxiety and LBW in urban India.30 Anxiety disorders during pregnancy may exacerbate physiological stress responses, adversely affecting fetal development.12 However, limited studies in Pakistan focus on anxiety and LBW, with most research examining its relationship with preterm birth. Moreover, the potential benefits of psychological interventions, such as cognitive-behavioural therapy (CBT), warrant further exploration, as preliminary evidence from Pakistan suggests that CBT can lower the risk of LBW in mothers with antenatal anxiety.31

In this study, prenatal stress emerged as another significant predictor of LBW, with mothers experiencing severe stress having more than twice the odds of delivering an LBW infant. These results correspond to those of Saeed et al32 and are consistent with broader meta-analysis linking maternal chronic stress to LBW.33 Notably, our study did not identify a significant relationship between mild-to-moderate stress and LBW. This may be attributed to the relatively small sample size within this subgroup, which could have restricted the statistical power to detect an association or potential under-reporting of stress levels, which could mask a more nuanced dose-response relationship.

Our study revealed a twofold increase in the odds of LBW as maternal depression severity escalated from 4.62 for mild-to-moderate depression to 9.30 for severe-to-extremely severe depression. A corresponding pattern was also observed for anxiety. The stronger association in our study compared with previous research11 34 35 may be due to our sample’s unique characteristics—primarily non-literate women from low socioeconomic backgrounds—and the inclusion of only inpatients, as they typically experience higher levels of distress. Additionally, variations in the tools used to assess psychological distress and the timing of assessments may contribute to differences, as women may be more vulnerable to distress at certain stages of pregnancy.36 Standardising these methodologies in future studies could help clarify these discrepancies and provide a more accurate understanding.

Psychological distress activates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, increasing glucocorticoid release that can cross the placenta and disrupt fetal development. Stress also triggers catecholamines, reducing uterine blood flow and nutrient delivery, while altered maternal appetite and weight gain patterns further compromise growth. Elevated cortisol adds to these effects, collectively inhibiting fetal growth.33 Beyond immediate perinatal outcomes, prenatal psychological distress can elevate offspring risks for neuropsychiatric and cardiometabolic disorders.68 LBW further compounds risk through long-term growth, cognitive and metabolic deficits.9

Higher perceived social support was protective against LBW in our study, aligning with prior evidence. A narrative review reported that mothers with high social support had 60% lower odds of LBW,37 findings echoed by a community-based case-control study in India,38 a randomised trial in Pakistan showing improved pregnancy experiences among anxious women39 and another case-control study reporting similar outcomes.40 Biologically, social support may buffer stress by reducing HPA axis activation and cortisol release, while socially it promotes better nutrition, ANC use and healthier behaviours. The consistency of these findings underscores the importance of integrating social support into ANC, particularly in South Asian contexts where extended family and community networks play a central role. In addition to psychological distress, maternal age and education were identified as significant predictors of LBW in this study. Mothers aged 30–35 years had a lower likelihood of giving birth to an LBW infant compared with those aged 18–23 years, aligning with earlier studies that show younger maternal age, especially under 20 years, elevates the risk of adverse birth outcomes.4143 Wang et al found that LBW risk decreases as maternal age increases until age 36 years,44 likely due to enhanced emotional, social and financial stability in older mothers, along with improved access to ANC. Conversely, maternal illiteracy was associated with significantly higher odds of LBW, corroborating evidence that maternal education improves neonatal outcomes.42 45 46

History of LBW and high gravidity (≥5 pregnancies) was also significant predictors of LBW in the current study. Mothers with a prior LBW delivery were five times more likely to deliver another LBW infant, aligning with previous studies, which suggest that complications from earlier pregnancies may persist.47 Gravidity of ≥5 increased the odds of LBW by 6.64 times, although findings on gravidity and LBW are mixed, possibly due to differences in study populations and methodologies.48 49 Finally, preterm birth was another significant risk factor for LBW, as established in prior research,50 owing to reduced fetal development time.

Husband’s employment, ANC visits, parity, miscarriage history, stillbirth history, neonate’s gender and congenital malformation were not significantly associated with LBW in the multivariable analysis. The non-association of neonate’s gender and husband’s employment aligns with previous research.51 52 However, other studies have documented the significance of parity, miscarriage, stillbirth history, congenital malformation and ANC visits as predictors of LBW.41 45 53 The high frequency of ANC visits observed in our cohort, potentially influenced by free or subsidised healthcare, may have masked any independent effects. The non-significance of congenital malformations could be attributed to the small number of cases with congenital anomalies in our sample, limiting statistical power.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study draws on a relatively large, bi-facility sample from two high-volume tertiary care hospitals, with an objectively measured outcome (birth weight) abstracted from delivery registers and cross-checked against wristbands within 48 hours of birth. Prenatal psychological distress and perceived social support were assessed using interviewer-administered Urdu versions of DASS-21 and MSPSS, applying standard cut-offs and modelling severity to demonstrate clear dose-response gradients. The analysis adjusted for key sociodemographic and obstetric factors with sensitivity checks. However, there are limitations to consider. First, this facility-based study conducted in two large public tertiary care hospitals likely over-represents lower-income, urban users of subsidised care and under-represents home and private-facility births, limiting generalisability to the broader population. Second, although we adjusted for multiple factors, residual confounding remains due to unmeasured determinants: nutritional status, health behaviours/exposures and environmental exposures. Third, the timing of psychological distress (by trimester) was not captured; heterogeneous windows of vulnerability could obscure finer dose-response patterns. Fourth, recall bias is possible because the DASS-21 was administered postpartum, requiring mothers to recall experiences during pregnancy. Lastly, the questionnaires were interviewer-administered, so social desirability and interviewer effects cannot be excluded. Moreover, while DASS-21 has been used in Pakistani perinatal studies, formal validation in pregnant Pakistani populations—and for retrospective prenatal recall—is limited, so estimates should be interpreted as associations from a screening tool rather than diagnoses. Despite these limitations, the study’s use of routinely obtainable data, blinded extraction from registers and consistent dose-response gradients across distress domains provides policy-relevant insights to inform pragmatic ANC screening and referral in similar public-sector settings.

Future studies should incorporate additional determinants such as biomarker analysis and evaluate social-support interventions as potentially low-cost complements to clinical care. Moreover, longitudinal studies that examine cause-and-effect relationships are essential for a better understanding of the influence of prenatal psychological distress on birth outcomes.

Conclusion

In this study, prenatal depression, anxiety, and stress were associated with higher odds of LBW in a dose-response manner, while perceived social support showed a protective effect. These findings highlight the public health value of embedding brief, low-cost mental health screening into ANC, supported by stepped-care management and stronger family and community support networks, to enhance maternal and child outcomes.
