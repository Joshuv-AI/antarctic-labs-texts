Bioactivity assessment and LC–MS/MS-based phenolic characterization of Gypsophila pilosa
Authors: Ali Açıkgöz, Musa Türkmen, Oguzhan Koçer, İmran Uysal, Mustafa Sevindik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this study, the antioxidant, anticholinesterase, antiproliferative activities, and phenolic compound profile of ethanol extracts of Gypsophila pilosa were comprehensively evaluated. Plant samples were collected from Şanlıurfa province, Türkiye, and after shade-drying under laboratory conditions, they were extracted with ethanol using a Soxhlet apparatus at 50 °C. The antioxidant capacity results revealed that total antioxidant status was 3.528 ± 0.085 mmol/L, total oxidant status (TOS) was 10.248 ± 0.072 µmol/L, and oxidative stress index was 0.291 ± 0.009. In addition, DPPH radical scavenging activity and ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) were determined as 92.317 ± 2.336 mg TE/g and 128.016 ± 2.480 mg TE/g, respectively, indicating a strong antioxidant potential. In anticholinesterase assays, the extract exhibited inhibitory activity against acetylcholinesterase (AChE) and butyrylcholinesterase (BChE), with IC₅₀ values of 160.163 ± 2.155 µg/mL and 223.903 ± 1.049 µg/mL, respectively. The antiproliferative activity was evaluated on A549 and MCF-7 cancer cell lines, as well as the HaCaT normal cell line. The extract demonstrated a dose-dependent reduction in cell viability in cancer cell lines, while comparatively lower cytotoxic effects were observed in normal cells at lower concentrations, suggesting a degree of selective activity. LC–MS/MS analysis revealed the presence of several bioactive phenolic compounds, including catechin hydrate, caffeic acid, gallic acid, and kaempferol, which are known for their antioxidant and cytotoxic properties. These compounds may contribute to the observed biological activities through overlapping and complementary mechanisms. In conclusion, G. pilosa exhibits notable antioxidant, enzyme inhibitory, and antiproliferative activities, which may be associated with its phenolic composition. However, further studies are required to clarify its selectivity and underlying mechanisms before potential pharmaceutical applications can be considered.

Medicinal plants, as nature’s complex chemical laboratories, are among the most valuable biological resources serving human health. Each offers a pharmacologically rich composition with unique metabolites developed to adapt to its environment and climatic conditions1. Natural extracts obtained from these plants form the basis for the discovery of new active ingredients not only in traditional treatment methods but also in the modern pharmaceutical industry2. Medicinal plants, notable for their antimicrobial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer potential, also stand out as a natural alternative that supports environmentally friendly and sustainable production3. With the deepening of scientific research, the biochemical diversity of medicinal plants has become an indispensable part not only of folk medicine but also of contemporary medicine4. Oxidative stress plays a central role in the pathogenesis of both neurodegenerative diseases and cancer. Natural antioxidants, particularly phenolic compounds, can modulate redox homeostasis by scavenging reactive oxygen species and regulating cellular signaling pathways involved in cell proliferation and apoptosis5. In parallel, cholinesterase enzymes are important not only in neurotransmission but also in cellular processes associated with tumor progression and cell survival. Therefore, evaluating antioxidant and anticholinesterase activities together with antiproliferative effects provides a more comprehensive understanding of the potential biological and pharmacological properties of plant extracts6.

In this study, the antioxidant, anticholinesterase, and antiproliferative activities of Gypsophila pilosa, along with its phenolic compound profile, were comprehensively evaluated. G. pilosa is an annual plant species belonging to the Caryophyllaceae family. It is naturally distributed in Mediterranean and Western Asian countries, particularly Turkey, Iran, Libya, Palestine, and, more recently, Egypt. The species is easily recognized by its long, dense glandular hairs, sometimes reaching up to one meter in height, and often found wild along field edges. Morphologically, the plant is covered with dense glandular hairs, a distinguishing feature of the species7,8. G. pilosa differs from other Gypsophila species in its long bracts, pedicels, and seed height7. Its flowers are white or pale pink, and its seeds are relatively larger8. The species’ distribution is concentrated in the Central Anatolia Region of Turkey, particularly in the provinces of Sivas, Erzincan, Çankırı, Eskişehir, and Ankara. Natural populations are also found in Iran, Libya, Palestine, and Egypt9. G. pilosa thrives in sandy, limeless or slightly calcareous soils rich in potassium, low in phosphorus, and moderate in nitrogen and organic matter10. While root development is limited in limestone soils, root development has been reported to increase as the sand content increases10. Cytotoxic and genotoxic studies have revealed that high concentrations of G. pilosa extracts can cause significant increases in mitotic index and chromosomal aberrations in the Allium cepa (onion) test system11,12. This is noteworthy because it demonstrates the effects of secondary metabolites in the plant on cell division and genetic stability. The Gypsophila genus is generally rich in various phenolic compounds, terpenoids, and alkaloids, particularly saponins. These compounds have high medicinal and industrial value11,13. Saponins are traditionally used in soap making and as foaming agents and emulsifiers in the textile and food industries11.

Materials and methods

The G. pilosa samples used in this study were collected from natural populations in the Şanlıurfa, Akçakale, DSİ replantation area (36° 42′56.48’’N, 38° 56′ 53.53’’ E 364 m) province of Turkey. The studied plant samples were identified by co-author botanist Dr. Sevindik. The collection of plant material was established in compliance with the national guidelines. Voucher specimens of G. pilosa were deposited in the Herbarium of Osmaniye Korkut Ata University (Osmaniye, Türkiye) under voucher number MS-1824. All plant collection procedures complied with relevant institutional, national, and international guidelines, including the IUCN Policy Statement on Research Involving Species at Risk of Extinction and the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES).” The aboveground parts of the collected plants were dried in the laboratory under shade and airflow and then prepared for analysis. During the extraction phase, 10 g of dried plant material was processed in a Soxhlet apparatus for approximately 6 h using 250 mL of ethanol at 50°C. The crude extract was concentrated using a Büchi R100 rotary evaporator at 40 °C under reduced pressure to remove the solvent. The concentrated extract was then further dried under ambient conditions to ensure the complete removal of residual ethanol. Finally, the extracts were stored at + 4 °C until experimental analyses. The Soxhlet extraction was carried out under controlled and standardized conditions, including constant temperature, solvent volume, and extraction duration, to ensure methodological consistency across experiments. Although extraction yield was not quantitatively determined in this study, all procedures were performed using identical protocols to minimize variability.

Antioxidant tests

In this study, the total antioxidant status (TAS) and total oxidant status (TOS) of ethanol extracts of G. pilosa were determined. Commercial kits from Rel Assay Diagnostics were used for the analyses, and measurements were carried out in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommended protocols. Trolox was used as the reference substance for TAS measurements, and hydrogen peroxide was used as the standard for TOS analyses. Results were calculated in millimoles/L (mmol/L) for TAS and micromoles/L (μmol/L) for TOS14,15. The oxidative stress index (OSI) was calculated by converting TAS (mmol/L) to µmol/L and then calculating the ratio of TOS to TAS. Specifically, TAS values were multiplied by 1000 to match the unit of TOS, and OSI was calculated as (TOS / TAS) × 10016.

DPPH Free radical scavenging activity analysis

DPPH free radical scavenging activities of plant extracts were determined using stock solutions prepared in DMSO at a concentration of 1 mg/mL. 1 mL of these solutions was taken, and 160 µL of a 0.267 mM DPPH solution was added and mixed. The mixtures were incubated for 30 min at room temperature under dark conditions. At the end of this period, absorbance values were measured spectrophotometrically at a wavelength of 517 nm. Analysis results are expressed in mg Trolox equivalents/g extract (mg TE/g)17. A calibration curve was prepared using Trolox as a standard at different concentrations, and the results were expressed as mg Trolox equivalents per gram of extract (mg TE/g).

Ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) analysis

The antioxidant capacities of plant extracts were determined using the ferric reducing power (FRAP) method. For this purpose, 100 µL of the extract solution was mixed with 2 mL of FRAP reagent. The FRAP reagent was prepared by combining 300 mM acetate buffer (pH 3.6), 40 mM HCl solution, 20 mM FeCl₃·6H₂O solution, and 10 mM 2,4,6-tris(2-pyridyl)-S-triazine solution at a 10:1:1 ratio. The prepared mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 4 min, after which absorbance values were measured at a wavelength of 593 nm. The results were expressed as mg Trolox equivalent/g extract (mg TE/g)17. A calibration curve was constructed using Trolox standard solutions, and results were expressed as mg Trolox equivalents per gram of extract (mg TE/g).

Anticholinesterase activity tests

In this study, the anticholinesterase activities of G. pilosa extracts were evaluated based on the colorimetric method described by Ellman et al.18. Galantamine was used as a reference substance in determining the inhibitory effects against acetylcholinesterase (AChE) and butyrylcholinesterase (BChE) enzymes. Stock solutions prepared from plant extracts were diluted in the range of 200–3.125 μg/mL. In the experimental setup, 130 μL of 0.1 M (pH 8.0) phosphate buffer, 10 μL of test sample, and 20 μL of enzyme solution (AChE or BChE) were added to each microtiter plate well, respectively. The mixtures were incubated at 25 °C under dark conditions for 10 min. At the end of the incubation period, 20 μL of DTNB [5,5'-dithiobis (2-nitrobenzoic acid)] solution and 20 μL of substrate (acetylcholine iodide or butyrylcholine iodide) were added to start the reaction. Enzymatic activity was measured spectrophotometrically at a wavelength of 412 nm; all analyses were performed in three parallel replicates. Percent inhibition was calculated from the obtained data, and IC₅₀ (μg/mL) values were determined.

Antiproliferative activity tests

In this study, the antiproliferative effects of plant extracts were investigated using lung adenocarcinoma (A549) and breast adenocarcinoma (MCF-7) cell lines. In addition, the human keratinocyte cell line (HaCaT) was used as a normal cell model to evaluate the selectivity of the extract. All cell lines were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) and cultured under standard conditions. Extract solutions were prepared and tested at concentrations of 25, 50, 100, and 200 µg/mL. When the cells reached 70–80% confluence, they were detached from the culture surface using 3.0 mL of Trypsin–EDTA solution (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA; Cat. No. T3924) and transferred into appropriate culture plates. A 24 h incubation period was applied to ensure proper cell attachment. Subsequently, the extract solutions were added and incubated for an additional 24 h. Following incubation, the culture medium was removed, and MTT solution [3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA; Cat. No. M5655] was added to each well at a concentration of 1 mg/mL. The cells were incubated at 37 °C until purple formazan crystals formed. The formed crystals were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA; Cat. No. D8418), which was also used as a negative control. Equal volumes of DMSO were applied to all experimental groups. Cell viability was determined based on absorbance (optical density, OD) measurements at 570 nm using an Epoch spectrophotometer (BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA), and results were expressed as percentage cell viability relative to the control group17. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical significance between groups was evaluated using appropriate statistical tests, and differences were considered significant at p < 0.05.

Phenolic compound analysis

The phenolic compound profile of the optimized plant extracts was determined using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS). A total of 24 phenolic reference standards, representing different subclasses of phenolic compounds, were targeted for simultaneous identification and quantification. Chromatographic separation was achieved using a reversed-phase C18 analytical column maintained at a constant temperature of 40 °C. The mobile phase consisted of a binary solvent system, where Phase A was ultrapure water containing 0.1% formic acid and Phase B was LC–MS grade methanol containing 0.1% formic acid. The analysis was performed at a constant flow rate of 0.3 mL/min with an injection volume of 2 µL for each sample. Under these optimized chromatographic and detection conditions, phenolic compounds were effectively separated and quantitatively determined with high sensitivity and reproducibility. The following 24 reference standards were screened in the optimized extracts: acetohydroxamic acid, catechin hydrate, vanillic acid, syringic acid, thymoquinone, resveratrol, myricetin, kaempferol, fumaric acid, gallic acid, protocatechuic acid, 4-hydroxybenzoic acid, caffeic acid, salicylic acid, phloridzin dihydrate, 2-hydroxycinnamic acid, oleuropein, 2-hydroxy-1,4-naphthoquinone, naringenin, silymarin, quercetin, luteolin, alizarin, and curcumin.

1.7. Statistical methods

All experiments were performed in three independent replicates, and the results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by appropriate post-hoc multiple comparison tests to evaluate differences between groups. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 18.0, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Antioxidant activity

The antioxidant activity of plants suppresses the harmful effects of free radicals, preventing the oxidative degradation of biomolecules. This property contributes both to the plant’s maintenance of its own metabolic balance and to its protective effects on human health. Natural antioxidants, particularly phenolic compounds, flavonoids, and tannins, are among the key components of this biological activity19,20. In this study, the antioxidant potential of the aerial parts of G. pilosa was evaluated. In our study, when the antioxidant activities of extracts from the aerial parts of G. pilosa were evaluated, it was observed that the plant has a remarkable potential for maintaining oxidative balance. The total antioxidant status (TAS) value was determined as 3.528 ± 0.085 mmol/L, indicating that the plant contains a high amount of antioxidant compounds that neutralize free radicals and maintain intracellular redox homeostasis. On the other hand, the limited total oxidant level (TOS) value of 10.248 ± 0.072 µmol/L suggests that the extract has a suppressive effect on oxidant production. The low oxidative stress index (OSI) value of 0.291 ± 0.009 reveals that the redox balance of G. pilosa has shifted significantly in the antioxidant direction. The DPPH radical scavenging capacity measured as 92.317 ± 2.336 mg TE/g indicates that the plant has a very high ability to neutralize free radicals through hydrogen atom or electron donation. This finding indicates the presence of radical scavenging compounds such as phenolic compounds, flavonoids, or triterpene saponins in the extract. Similarly, the 128.016 ± 2.480 mg TE/g value obtained in the FRAP test measuring ferric reducing power indicates that G. pilosa exhibits effective antioxidant behavior through the electron transfer mechanism. In the literature, it has been reported that the methanol, ethyl acetate, and petroleum ether extracts of G. pilosa were evaluated using the DPPH method, and the methanol extract, in particular, exhibited high antioxidant capacity21. This is consistent with the high DPPH and FRAP values obtained in the current study and can be explained by the fact that polar solvents extract phenolic compounds more effectively. Additionally, no findings regarding TAS, TOS, and OSI values of G. pilosa have been found in the literature. Studies on different plant species have reported TAS values for Mentha longifolia, Origanum laevigatum, Hypericum spectabile, Equisetum ramosissimum, and Tagetes patula as 6.094, 8.609, 9.306, 5.350, and 5.386 mmol/L, respectively. TOS values have been reported as 14.050, 10.685, 13.065, 11.608, and 8.287 μmol/L, respectively. OSI values were reported as 0.231, 0.124, 0.140, 0.217, and 0.154, respectively22–26. Compared to these studies, the TAS value of G. pilosa used in our study was determined to be lower than that of M. longifolia, O. laevigatum, H. spectabile, E. ramosissimum, and T. patula. The lower TAS value of G. pilosa compared to these species suggests that the antioxidant compounds of this plant may be limited in terms of quantity or diversity. This may be due to the different structural properties of phenolic or flavonoid compounds in G. pilosa or to the fact that extraction conditions affect the amount of soluble phenolics. The TOS value of G. pilosa used in our study was determined to be lower than that of M. longifolia, O. laevigatum, H. spectabile, and E. ramosissimum, and higher than that of T. patula. The lower TOS value than these species but higher than that of T. patula indicates that G. pilosa has a moderate capacity to limit oxidant production. This result suggests that certain antioxidant components of the plant effectively suppress oxidative processes, but that some strong oxidant inhibitors may be present at lower levels in this species. The OSI value of G. pilosa used in our study was determined to be higher than that of M. longifolia, O. laevigatum, H. spectabile, E. ramosissimum, and T. patula. The higher oxidative stress index (OSI) value of G. pilosa than all compared species suggests that the plant’s redox balance has a more oxidant-laden profile than other species. This may be explained by increased production of oxidant compounds due to environmental stress factors to which the plant is exposed in its natural ecological conditions, or by the relatively lower activity of antioxidant defense components. The data obtained indicate that G. pilosa may play a protective role against biochemical deterioration caused by oxidative stress and could be utilized as a natural antioxidant source in medical or pharmaceutical applications. Considering the low OSI and high FRAP values together, it appears that the plant limits oxidant production while supporting antioxidant defenses. These results suggest that G. pilosa may be effective in preventing free radical-induced damage in biological systems and has the potential for use in antioxidant formulations in the future.

Anticholinesterase activity

The anticholinesterase activity of plants helps maintain acetylcholine levels by suppressing the activity of cholinesterase enzymes involved in nervous system functions. This biological effect supports the maintenance of neurotransmission and constitutes an important mechanism for preserving cognitive functions. Naturally derived phenolic compounds, alkaloids, and terpene derivatives are noted as key components of this neuroprotective effect exhibited by plants27,28. The findings obtained in our study regarding the anticholinesterase activity of G. pilosa indicate that the plant has a significant inhibitory effect on the acetylcholinesterase (AChE) and butyrylcholinesterase (BChE) enzymes. The results indicate that the extract has a significant but moderate inhibitory effect on both enzymes. Compared to galantamine, used as the reference inhibitor, G. pilosa's activity is lower, but it is considered to be notable among naturally occurring herbal inhibitors.

Inhibition of AChE and BChE enzymes is crucial for maintaining acetylcholine levels in synaptic transmission29. In this context, the determined inhibitory effect of G. pilosa indicates that the plant contains secondary metabolites such as phenolic compounds, flavonoids, or triterpene saponins that can suppress cholinesterase activity. Reports of similar pharmacological activities in different species of the Gypsophila genus in the literature support these findings. The literature has reported that G. pallida, G. arrostii, G. tuberculosa, and G. eriocalyx species exhibit significant inhibitory effects against acetylcholinesterase and butyrylcholinesterase enzymes30. This parallelism suggests that Gypsophila species possess structurally similar secondary metabolites, and these compounds may exhibit similar binding mechanisms with cholinesterase enzymes. In conclusion, the higher inhibitory activity of G. pilosa against AChE compared to BChE suggests a differential inhibitory effect toward these enzymes. However, no enzyme kinetic or mechanistic analyses were performed in this study to determine the inhibition type or binding characteristics. Therefore, further studies involving detailed kinetic evaluations are required to clarify the inhibition mechanism. Overall, the obtained data indicate that G. pilosa may represent a promising natural source of cholinesterase inhibitors, although additional investigations are necessary to confirm its pharmacological relevance.

Antiproliferative activity

The antiproliferative effects of plants limit the growth of cancer cells by targeting biochemical pathways that regulate cell division. This effect generally occurs through apoptosis, DNA synthesis suppression, or cell cycle arrest. These properties of naturally occurring phytochemicals provide a promising basis for the development of new-generation herbal anticancer drugs31,32. In our study, the antiproliferative effects of the G. pilosa ethanol extract were evaluated on A549 and MCF-7 cancer cell lines, and the results demonstrated a clear dose-dependent reduction in cell viability in both cell models. While the control group (SF) and the solvent control (DMSO) exhibited high optical density (OD) values, a gradual decrease in cell viability was observed at concentrations of 25, 50, 100, and 200 µg/mL. The reduction in cell viability was more pronounced at higher concentrations, particularly at 100 and 200 µg/mL, indicating a concentration-dependent cytotoxic effect of the extract. In addition to its effects on cancer cell lines, the cytotoxic potential of the extract was also evaluated on the HaCaT normal human keratinocyte cell line to assess its selectivity profile. The findings indicated that the extract exerted a comparatively lower cytotoxic effect on normal cells at lower concentrations, whereas a more noticeable decrease in viability was observed at higher doses. This pattern suggests that the extract exhibits a degree of selective antiproliferative activity, although the selectivity appears to be concentration-dependent rather than absolute.

The observed antiproliferative effects may be attributed to the presence of bioactive secondary metabolites such as phenolic compounds, flavonoids, and triterpene saponins, which are known to interact with cellular pathways related to cell proliferation and survival33,34. However, no direct experimental evidence was obtained in this study to confirm specific mechanisms such as apoptosis induction or cell cycle arrest. These mechanisms are commonly associated with plant-derived anticancer agents and are consistent with the biological activities reported for the genus Gypsophila. Similarly, previous studies have demonstrated that other Gypsophila species, including G. pallida, G. arrostii, G. tuberculosa, and G. eriocalyx, exhibit significant antiproliferative effects against various cancer cell lines such as HepG2, HT-29, and MCF-7 30. The consistency between the present findings and the literature suggests that Gypsophila species may contain structurally related bioactive compounds responsible for their cytotoxic activity across different cancer models. Overall, the results indicate that G. pilosa possesses notable antiproliferative activity against cancer cell lines, with a moderate degree of selectivity toward cancer cells compared to normal cells. However, the observed cytotoxic effects at higher concentrations on normal cells highlight the need for further studies to better define its safety profile. Therefore, advanced investigations, including detailed phytochemical characterization and mechanistic studies, are required to clarify the therapeutic potential and selectivity of G. pilosa extracts.

The LC–MS/MS analysis revealed that G. pilosa extract contains a diverse range of phenolic compounds with varying concentrations, indicating a complex phytochemical composition. Among the identified compounds, catechin hydrate (10,861.00 µg/kg) was found to be the predominant component, followed by caffeic acid (5171.48 µg/kg) and gallic acid (2429.66 µg/kg). In contrast, relatively lower concentrations were detected for kaempferol (1451.95 µg/kg), vanillic acid (992.07 µg/kg), and 4-hydroxybenzoic acid (525.15 µg/kg). The high abundance of catechin hydrate is particularly noteworthy, as catechins are well known for their strong antioxidant capacity and their role in modulating cellular redox balance. These compounds are capable of scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS) and may contribute significantly to the high DPPH and FRAP values observed in the present study35,36. Similarly, caffeic acid and gallic acid are widely recognized phenolic acids with potent antioxidant and cytoprotective properties, and their presence further supports the strong antioxidant profile of the extract. Beyond their antioxidant activity, these phenolic compounds are also known to influence key biological pathways associated with enzyme inhibition and cell proliferation37,38. For instance, catechins and phenolic acids have been reported to interact with cholinesterase enzymes, contributing to anticholinesterase activity. In addition, these compounds may induce apoptosis, inhibit cell cycle progression, and regulate signaling pathways involved in cancer cell survival, which may explain the observed antiproliferative effects on A549 and MCF-7 cell lines39,40. The presence of flavonoids such as kaempferol further enhances the biological relevance of the extract, as these compounds are associated with both antioxidant and anticancer activities, including the modulation of inflammatory responses and inhibition of tumor growth41,42. Although present at relatively lower concentrations, compounds such as vanillic acid and 4-hydroxybenzoic acid may also contribute synergistically to the overall biological activity of the extract43,44. Taken together, the LC–MS/MS findings suggest that the biological activities observed in G. pilosa extract are likely associated with the combined and possibly synergistic effects of multiple phenolic compounds. This supports the hypothesis that antioxidant, anticholinesterase, and antiproliferative activities may arise from overlapping mechanisms mediated by common bioactive constituents. However, further studies focusing on the isolation of individual compounds and detailed mechanistic analyses are required to fully elucidate these interactions.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of the biological activity profile of G. pilosa, including its antioxidant, anticholinesterase, and antiproliferative properties, supported by phenolic compound analysis. The results demonstrate that the extract exhibits considerable antioxidant capacity, as evidenced by TAS, TOS, OSI, DPPH, and FRAP findings, suggesting its potential role in maintaining cellular redox balance. The observed inhibitory activity against acetylcholinesterase (AChE) and butyrylcholinesterase (BChE) indicates that G. pilosa may contribute to the modulation of cholinergic signaling, which is relevant in the context of neurodegenerative disorders. In addition, the extract exhibited dose-dependent antiproliferative effects on A549 and MCF-7 cancer cell lines. Comparative evaluation with the HaCaT normal cell line suggested a degree of selective cytotoxicity, particularly at lower concentrations, although this selectivity was found to be concentration-dependent. LC–MS/MS analysis revealed the presence of several bioactive phenolic compounds, including catechin hydrate, caffeic acid, gallic acid, and kaempferol, which are known to contribute to antioxidant, enzyme inhibitory, and antiproliferative activities. These findings suggest that the observed biological effects may be associated with overlapping and potentially complementary mechanisms mediated by these compounds. Overall, G. pilosa can be considered a promising natural source of bioactive compounds with multifunctional biological activities. However, further studies, including detailed phytochemical characterization, isolation of active constituents, and mechanistic investigations, are required to fully elucidate its therapeutic potential and safety profile.
