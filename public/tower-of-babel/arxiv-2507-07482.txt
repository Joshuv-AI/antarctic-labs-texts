Probing Axions via Spectroscopic Measurements of S-stars at the Galactic Center Preprint: FERMILAB-PUB-25-0150-T
Zhaoyu Bai, Vitor Cardoso, Yifan Chen, Tuan Do, Aurélien Hees, Huangyu Xiao, Xiao Xue

Abstract
Axions, encompassing both QCD axions and axion-like particles, can generate loop-induced quadratic couplings to electromagnetic field strength tensors, resulting in oscillatory shifts of the fine-structure constant. Near a Kerr black hole, an axion field with a Compton wavelength comparable to the event horizon can exponentially grow through the superradiance mechanism, potentially reaching a maximum amplitude near the decay constant, provided this scale is below approximately 10 16 \displaystyle 10^{16} GeV. The saturated axion cloud formed around the black hole induces characteristic oscillations in the fine-structure constant, with a period of 10 \displaystyle 10 – 40 \displaystyle 40 minutes determined by the axion mass, and a spatial profile governed by the axion wavefunction and its coupling strength. At lower axion masses, axion dark matter can form a soliton-like core characterized by a nearly constant amplitude, extending measurable variations of the fine-structure constant to greater distances. Precise spectroscopic measurements of S-stars orbiting the supermassive black hole Sgr A ∗ provide a powerful probe of these predictions, potentially excluding substantial regions of parameter space for quadratic scalar couplings to photons, owing to the high boson density near the Galactic Center.

II
Quadratic Scalar Couplings to Photons

As pseudoscalar particles, axions typically couple linearly to parity-odd operators, such as the axion-gluon coupling
\displaystyle\propto\phi\,G_{\mu\nu}\tilde{G}^{\mu\nu}
and the axion-photon coupling
\displaystyle\propto\phi\,F_{\mu\nu}\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}
. Here,
\displaystyle\phi
represents the boson field,
\displaystyle G_{\mu\nu}/\tilde{G}^{\mu\nu}
and
\displaystyle F_{\mu\nu}/\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}
are the field strength tensors and their duals for gluons and photons, respectively. However, at the one-loop level, quadratic interactions with the photon kinetic term can be generated
Kim and Perez 2024
;
Beadle et al. 2024
;
Kim et al. 2024
:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{EM}}=\frac{C_{\gamma}}{4}\frac{\phi^{2}}{f_{\phi}^{2}}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu},

(1)

where
\displaystyle f_{\phi}
is the decay constant of the axion, and
\displaystyle C_{\gamma}
is a dimensionless coefficient resulting from the breaking of the axion’s shift symmetry. For KSVZ-like QCD axion models, whose shift symmetry is broken down to a discrete symmetry,
\displaystyle C_{\gamma}\approx-3\times 10^{-5}
has been calculated from diagrams involving pions and nucleons in the loop
Kim and Perez 2024
;
Beadle et al. 2024
;
Kim et al. 2024
. Alternatively, interactions with ALPs can arise from either a QCD-like breaking to discrete shift symmetry or a breaking at ultraviolet scales, leading to a model-dependent value of
\displaystyle C_{\gamma}

Beadle et al. 2024
.

In addition to axions, quadratic couplings to photons naturally arise for scalar fields under symmetries such as
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, parameterized as
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\supset\phi^{2}{F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}}/{4\Lambda^{2}}
, where
\displaystyle\Lambda
denotes the ultraviolet scale
Stadnik and Flambaum 2015b
;
Stadnik and Flambaum 2015a
;
Stadnik and Flambaum 2016a
;
Stadnik and Flambaum 2016b
;
Hees et al. 2018
;
Hees et al. 2019
;
Masia-Roig et al. 2023
;
Bouley et al. 2023
;
Banerjee et al. 2023a
;
Gan and Liu 2023
;
Brax et al. 2023
. Comparing this with Eq. (
1
), the coupling effectively replaces
\displaystyle C_{\gamma}/f_{\phi}^{2}
with
\displaystyle 1/\Lambda^{2}
. This study will not delve into linear couplings, as tests of the fifth force and equivalence principle have already imposed stringent constraints on such interactions
Bergé et al. 2018
;
Hees et al. 2018
. These constraints can be circumvented by quadratic couplings, which mediate fifth forces at loop levels. Consequently, when the scalar field does not constitute the majority of DM, the leading constraint arises from energy loss in Supernova (SN) 1987A, setting a bound of
\displaystyle\Lambda=f_{\phi}/|C_{\gamma}|^{1/2}\geq 3
TeV

;
Cox et al. 2024
;
Gan et al. 2025

When compared to the standard electromagnetic kinetic term,
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\supset-{F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}}/{4}
, axion couplings in Eq. (
1
), and similar quadratic scalar couplings, introduce measurable shifts in the electromagnetic fine-structure constant,
\displaystyle\delta\alpha_{\text{EM}}

\frac{\delta\alpha_{\text{EM}}}{\alpha_{\text{EM}}}\simeq C_{\gamma}\frac{\phi^{2}}{f_{\phi}^{2}}\quad\text{or}\quad\frac{\phi^{2}}{\Lambda^{2}},

(2)

assuming the deviation remains substantially smaller than the original value of
\displaystyle\alpha_{\text{EM}}
.

III
Fine-Structure Constant Oscillations at the Galactic Center

Ultralight scalars, with a Compton wavelength exceeding the horizon size of a BH, can form quasi-bound clouds around the BH. These clouds, similar to hydrogen atoms, are characterized by quantum numbers
\displaystyle(n,\ell,m)
, representing the principal, orbital angular, and azimuthal numbers, respectively
Detweiler 1980
. The size of the cloud is governed by the gravitational fine-structure constant
\displaystyle\alpha_{G}\equiv G_{\rm N}M_{\rm BH}\mu
, where
\displaystyle G_{\rm N}
is Newton’s constant,
\displaystyle M_{\rm BH}
is the BH mass, and
\displaystyle\mu
is the scalar mass. The rapid rotation of BHs can select specific bound states, transitioning them from decaying states due to the horizon to exponentially growing modes
Penrose and Floyd 1971
;
Zel’Dovich 1971
;
Detweiler 1980
;
Cardoso and Yoshida 2005
;
Dolan 2007
;
Brito et al. 2015a
. This phenomenon, known as superradiance, occurs when the horizon rotates faster than the angular phase velocity of the states, parameterized by:

\frac{\alpha_{G}}{m}<\frac{a_{J}}{2\left(1+\sqrt{1-a_{J}^{2}}\right)},

(3)

where
\displaystyle a_{J}\leq 1
is the dimensionless BH spin parameter. Exponential growth for minimally coupled scalars can continue until it significantly impacts the BH spin, with the total scalar cloud mass,
\displaystyle M_{\rm cloud}
, reaching up to approximately
\displaystyle 10\%
of the BH mass
Brito et al. 2015b
;
East and Pretorius 2017
;
Herdeiro et al. 2022

\displaystyle\sim 10^{16}
GeV. This positions boson clouds outside Kerr BHs as potential testing grounds for exploring ultraviolet physics

;
Chen et al. 2025
;
Ayzenberg et al. 2025
;
Lyu et al. 2025

V
Discussion

In the Galactic Center, the vicinity of an SMBH can host a high density of ultralight bosons. Two notable examples include: (1) BH superradiance, which can exponentially amplify a boson condensate bound to the BH, driving the field value to saturate the decay constant for axions, and (2) ultralight DM, which can form a soliton core. Due to the high field values in these scenarios, observables arising from axion interactions can be significantly enhanced compared to those from terrestrial DM detection. This makes the system particularly suitable for probing couplings generated from loop-level interactions, such as quadratic axion/scalar couplings to photons. In this study, we simulate precise spectroscopic measurements of S-stars orbiting the SMBH Sgr A
∗
to investigate these couplings, focusing on axion profiles arising from either a superradiant cloud or a soliton core. The sensitivity of current data indicates that a significant portion of the parameter space can already be constrained, while near-future upgrades to observational capabilities promise deeper exploration of these regions.

While our study primarily employs a Newtonian wavefunction for the superradiant ground mode
\displaystyle(2,1,1)

S-Star Orbits in the Black Hole Frame

This section provides details on how to specify the orbit of a star in the black hole (BH) frame, where the BH spin axis aligns with the
\displaystyle\hat{z}
-axis, following the procedures outlined in Ref.
Grould et al. 2017
.

Figure S1
:
Illustration of the coordinate system in the observer’s frame, where the
\displaystyle\hat{z}
-axis points from the observer to the orbit, and the
\displaystyle\hat{x}
-axis points to the north/declination. The star’s orbit is depicted by the red line, while the BH spin direction is indicated by the blue arrow. The six orbital elements are defined in the discussion below, and the BH spin direction is determined by the inclination angle
\displaystyle i_{\rm BH}
and the position angle
\displaystyle\Omega_{\rm BH}

The orbital motion is initially defined in the observer frame, where the
\displaystyle\hat{z}
-axis points from the observer to the orbit, and the
\displaystyle\hat{x}
-axis points to the north/declination, as illustrated in Fig.
S1
. The orbit is characterized by six orbital elements:

•

The eccentricity
\displaystyle e
and semi-major axis
\displaystyle a
, which describe the size and shape of the orbit.

•

The inclination
\displaystyle i_{s}
and the longitude of the ascending node
\displaystyle\Omega_{s}
, which define the orientation of the orbital plane.

•

The argument of periapsis
\displaystyle\omega_{s}
, which specifies the direction of the orbit’s closest approach to the BH.

•

The true anomaly
\displaystyle\nu_{s}
, which indicates the phase of the star along its orbit.

In the observer frame, the orbital motion can be expressed as

\vec{r}_{s}=\frac{a(1-e^{2})}{1+e\cos\nu_{s}}\left(\cos\nu_{s}\,\hat{a}+\sin\nu_{s}\,\hat{b}\right),

(S1)

where

\hat{a}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}-\cos\omega_{s}\sin\Omega_{s}-\sin\omega_{s}\cos\Omega_{s}\cos i_{s}\\
\cos\omega_{s}\cos\Omega_{s}-\sin\omega_{s}\sin\Omega_{s}\cos i_{s}\\
\sin\omega_{s}\sin i_{s}\end{pmatrix},\quad\hat{b}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}\sin\omega_{s}\sin\Omega_{s}-\cos\omega_{s}\cos\Omega_{s}\cos i_{s}\\
-\sin\omega_{s}\cos\Omega_{s}-\cos\omega_{s}\sin\Omega_{s}\cos i_{s}\\
\cos\omega_{s}\sin i_{s}\end{pmatrix}.

(S2)

To transform into the BH frame, we first consider the BH spin direction
\displaystyle\hat{J}_{\rm BH}
, represented by the blue arrow in Fig.
S1
. This direction is specified by the inclination angle
\displaystyle i_{\rm BH}
and the position angle
\displaystyle\Omega_{\rm BH}
, expressed as

\hat{J}_{\rm BH}=\left(\sin i_{\rm BH}\cos\Omega_{\rm BH},\sin i_{\rm BH}\sin\Omega_{\rm BH},\cos i_{\rm BH}\right).

(S3)

To align
\displaystyle\hat{J}_{\rm BH}
with the
\displaystyle\hat{z}
-axis in the BH frame, we introduce a rotation matrix
\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\rm BH}
:

\mathcal{R}_{\rm BH}=\begin{pmatrix}\sin\Omega_{\rm BH}&-\cos\Omega_{\rm BH}&0\\
\cos i_{\rm BH}\cos\Omega_{\rm BH}&\cos i_{\rm BH}\sin\Omega_{\rm BH}&-\sin i_{\rm BH}\\
\sin i_{\rm BH}\cos\Omega_{\rm BH}&\sin i_{\rm BH}\sin\Omega_{\rm BH}&\cos i_{\rm BH}\end{pmatrix}.

(S4)

Using this transformation matrix, the star’s orbit in the BH frame is given by

\vec{r}_{s,{\rm BH}}=\frac{a(1-e^{2})}{1+e\cos\nu_{s}}\left(\cos\nu_{s}\,\hat{a}_{\rm BH}+\sin\nu_{s}\,\hat{b}_{\rm BH}\right),

(S5)

where

\hat{a}_{\rm BH}=\mathcal{R}_{\rm BH}\hat{a}=\begin{pmatrix}-\cos\omega_{s}\cos(\Omega_{\rm BH}-\Omega_{s})-\cos i_{s}\sin\omega_{s}\sin(\Omega_{\rm BH}-\Omega_{s})\\
\cos i_{\rm BH}\left[\cos\omega_{s}\sin(\Omega_{\rm BH}-\Omega_{s})-\cos i_{s}\sin\omega_{s}\cos(\Omega_{\rm BH}-\Omega_{s})\right]-\sin i_{\rm BH}\sin i_{s}\sin\omega_{s}\\
\sin\omega_{s}\left[\cos i_{\rm BH}\sin i_{s}-\sin i_{\rm BH}\cos i_{s}\cos(\Omega_{\rm BH}-\Omega_{s})\right]+\sin i_{\rm BH}\cos\omega_{s}\sin(\Omega_{\rm BH}-\Omega_{s})\end{pmatrix},

(S6)

and

\hat{b}_{\rm BH}=\mathcal{R}_{\rm BH}\hat{b}=\begin{pmatrix}\sin\omega_{s}\cos(\Omega_{\rm BH}-\Omega_{s})-\cos i_{s}\cos\omega_{s}\sin(\Omega_{\rm BH}-\Omega_{s})\\
-\cos i_{\rm BH}\left[\sin\omega_{s}\sin(\Omega_{\rm BH}-\Omega_{s})+\cos i_{s}\cos\omega_{s}\cos(\Omega_{\rm BH}-\Omega_{s})\right]-\sin i_{\rm BH}\cos i_{s}\sin\Omega_{s}\\
-\sin i_{\rm BH}\left[\sin\omega_{s}\sin(\Omega_{\rm BH}-\Omega_{s})+\cos i_{s}\cos\omega_{s}\cos(\Omega_{\rm BH}-\Omega_{s})\right]+\cos i_{\rm BH}\cos i_{s}\sin\Omega_{s}\end{pmatrix}.

(S7)

II
Statistics/Data Analysis

II.1
Data analysis

The data consists of spectroscopic measurements indexed by
\displaystyle I
(star),
\displaystyle n
(night), and
\displaystyle i
(exposure). The measured data
\displaystyle d_{I,n,i}
includes contributions from a potential signal
\displaystyle s_{I,n,i}(\bm{\Theta})
(dependent on parameters
\displaystyle\bm{\Theta}
) and Gaussian noise
\displaystyle n_{I,n,i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{I,n,i}^{2})
. The likelihood function is given by:

\ln\mathcal{L}(\bm{\Theta}|\{d\})=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{I,n,i}\left(\frac{d_{I,n,i}-s_{I,n,i}(\bm{\Theta})}{\sigma_{I,n,i}}\right)^{2}+\text{constant}.

(S8)

For a signal model linear in parameters (
\displaystyle s_{a}=\sum_{\mu}\mathcal{P}_{a,\mu}\Theta_{\mu}
, where
\displaystyle a\equiv\{I,n,i\}
), the maximum likelihood estimate is obtained by solving:

\frac{\partial}{\partial\Theta_{\mu}}\ln\mathcal{L}(\bm{\Theta}|\{d\})=0,

(S9)

yielding:

\displaystyle\bm{\Theta}_{\rm best-fit}=(\mathcal{P^{\prime}}^{T}\mathcal{P^{\prime}})^{-1}\mathcal{P^{\prime}}^{T}\bm{b},\quad\text{where }\ b_{a}\equiv\frac{d_{a}}{\sigma_{a}},\quad\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{a,\mu}\equiv\frac{1}{\sigma_{a}}\mathcal{P}_{a,\mu}

(S10)

Under the null hypothesis, mock data
\displaystyle d^{\rm mock}_{a}
are generated as:

d^{\rm mock}_{a}=n_{a},\quad n_{a}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{a}^{2}).

(S11)

The marginalized posterior distribution of
\displaystyle\Theta_{\rm best-fit,\mu}^{\rm mock}
derived from mock data follows:

\Theta_{\rm best-fit,\mu}^{\rm mock}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma_{\Theta_{\mu}}^{2}\right),\quad\text{with }\sigma_{\Theta_{\mu}}^{2}=\left[(\mathcal{P}^{\prime T}\mathcal{P}^{\prime})^{-1}\right]_{\mu\mu},

(S12)

where
\displaystyle\sigma_{\Theta_{\mu}}
is the theoretical uncertainty from the Fisher information matrix
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{P}^{\prime T}\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
.

An
\displaystyle x\%
confidence level exclusion limit
\displaystyle\Theta_{\mu}^{\rm up}
is defined as the value satisfying:

P\left(\Theta_{\rm best-fit,\mu}\geq\Theta_{\mu}^{\rm up}\,|\,\Theta_{\mu}^{\rm true}=\Theta_{\mu}^{\rm up}\right)=(1-x)\%.

(S13)

For Gaussian-distributed
\displaystyle\Theta_{\rm best-fit,\mu}
, this corresponds to the
\displaystyle(1-x)\%
quantile of the
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\Theta_{\mu}}^{2})
distribution.

II.2
Signal modeling

From the main part of the paper, the spectroscopic signal due to ALP is

\frac{\delta\lambda_{j}}{\lambda_{j}}=-k_{\alpha,j}\frac{\delta\alpha_{\rm EM}}{\alpha_{\rm EM}}=-k_{\alpha,j}C_{\gamma}\frac{\phi^{2}}{f_{\phi}^{2}}\,,

(S14)

where the subscript
\displaystyle j
refers to a particular atomic line,
\displaystyle k_{\alpha,j}
is the sensitivity of the atomic transition to the fine structure constant (typically
\displaystyle\sim 2
for H atomic lines).

Using the solution for the ALP scalar field
\displaystyle\phi
provided in the main part of the paper, one finds that the signal can be written as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\delta\lambda}{\lambda}=s(t,\bm{x})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=-k_{\alpha}C_{\gamma}\frac{\left(\sin\theta\phi_{0}^{\mathrm{max}}R(r)\right)^{2}}{2f_{\phi}^{2}}\left[1+\cos 2(\mu t-\varphi)\cos 2\Delta_{0}-\sin 2(\mu t-\varphi)\sin 2\Delta_{0}\right]\,

(S15a)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=s_{0}(\bm{x})\Big[A\sin(2\mu t-2\varphi)+B\cos(2\mu t-2\varphi)\Big]+\cdots\,,

(S15b)

where
\displaystyle s_{0}(x)=k_{\alpha}\Big(\sin\theta\phi_{0}^{\mathrm{max}}R(r)\Big)^{2}/2
, and the
\displaystyle\cdots
denotes terms evolving on the orbital timescale, which are not considered in this analysis focused on rapid oscillations. Note that in the data analysis, it is better to infer the
\displaystyle 2
parameters
\displaystyle A
and
\displaystyle B
since the model is linear in these parameters and to convert the resulting posterior into a posterior on the coupling parameter using
\displaystyle C_{\gamma}/f_{\phi}^{2}=\sqrt{A^{2}+B^{2}}
.

III
Generation of Mock data

In this analysis, we explore two scenarios: (i) a case of existing data for the star S0-2/S2 and (ii) a case of futuristic measurements that will be performed using the next generation of instrument.

III.1
Existing data

We consider the spectroscopic measurements of the short period star S0-2/S2 during the 2017-2018 measurement campaign, when the star was going through its periastron. The dataset is presented in Ref.
Do et al. 2019
(see Sec.
\displaystyle 1.1
from the Supplementary Materials of Ref.
Do et al. 2019
). In 2017-2018, spectroscopic data has been taken from three instruments: (i) OSIRIS at the Keck observatory, (ii) IRCS at the SUBARU telescope and (iii) NISF from the Gemini observatory.

Each night of observation, several frames of integration time between 300 and 900 s are taken. In Ref.
Do et al. 2019
, all the frames for each night are combined together to produce a daily estimate of the star radial velocity (RV). In this analysis, since we are interested in measuring fast oscillations in the spectroscopic measurements such that it is more powerful to work directly with individual frames. This would require a detailed re-analysis of all raw data presented in Sec.
\displaystyle 1.1
from the Supplementary Materials of Ref.
Do et al. 2019
, which is beyond the scope of our sensitivity analysis.

In order to perform our sensitivity analysis, we need to have a realistic estimate of the expected uncertainty of the spectroscopic measurement for each individual frame. In this sensitivity analysis, we use one night of data from the Gemini NIFS instrument and assume that the characteristic instrumental noise is representative of all spectroscopic measurements conducted between 2017 and 2019 across various instruments. Specifically, we consider the Gemini NIFS dataset from 2018-05-13, which is part of the data presented in Ref.
Do et al. 2019
, consisting of 12 frames with 600 s integration time each.
Of the 12 frames, only 8 of the individual data points were useful for individual relative RV measurements. The relative spectroscopic measurement is obtained by cross-correlating each individual spectrum with a combined spectrum of the night. For simplicity, we only used the Hydrogen line at 2.1661 microns (Br-
\displaystyle\gamma
). The individual extracted data is presented in Fig.
S2
. The median RV uncertainty is
\displaystyle 28
km/s and the RV scatter throughout the night is about
\displaystyle 24
km/s, which is consistent with the uncertainties. The results can be sensitive to how the spectra are extracted and correlated so this could be refined in the future.

In our analysis, we generated a mock dataset for 2017-2018 using the characteristic of the measurement noise from the night 2018-05-13. Specifically, we assume that 25 % of the frames will not be usable and we draw the RV uncertainty for each frame from a probability distribution that has been fitted to the distribution of the RV distribution from Fig.
S2
; namely,
\displaystyle(\sigma_{\mathrm{RV}}-10\,\mathrm{km/s})/10\,\mathrm{km/s}
is modeled by a Gamma distribution with shape parameter
\displaystyle 3

III.2
Future data

In our analysis, we also simulated data that would be representative of future instruments such as HISPEC and MODHIS
Mawet et al. 2019
;
Konopacky et al. 2023
. With these instruments, we expect a three-order-of-magnitude improvement in spectroscopic resolution, a halved cadence
Mawet et al. 2019
;
Konopacky et al. 2023
. Such instruments would also enable the detection of late-type stars. With cooler atmospheres, these stars exhibit a rich variety of spectral lines with smaller intrinsic variance, in contrast to hot, early-type stars like S0-2/S2.
Thus, we assume a reduction of
\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{RV}}
by a factor of
\displaystyle 10^{3}
.

We generate mock data incorporating these instrumental properties and spanning a time baseline ten times longer than the current dataset. To probe superradiant clouds, we consider a hypothetical late-type star with a semi-major axis equal to 10% that of S0-2/S2, which would yield a significantly larger signal due to its closer proximity to the BH. For soliton-core dark matter, we assume simultaneous spectroscopic observations of
\displaystyle 40
stars within both the telescope’s field of view and the spatial extent of the soliton core.
