Clinical significance of SQSTM1 variants in ALS: report of p.Arg119Cys and literature review
Authors: Boyang Su, Ling Li, Xiaoxiao Zheng, Hongyue Ma, Xiuli Li, Xinhong Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We analyzed the clinical features of a patient with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) carrying a novel variant in the sequestosome 1 (SQSTM1) gene and explored the genotype-phenotype association of SQSTM1 gene variants in combination with previous literature. Clinical data and genetic testing results of an ALS patient treated at our hospital were collected. Whole-exome sequencing was used to screen for ALS-related genes, and candidate variants were validated by Sanger sequencing and family analysis. A systematic search was conducted in the PubMed database using the keywords (“amyotrophic lateral sclerosis”) OR (“motor neuron disease”) AND (“SQSTM1”) to summarize the clinical and genetic characteristics of previously reported ALS patients with SQSTM1 variants. The patient was a 49-year-old male with progressive weakness in both lower limbs for one year and weakness in the left upper limb for the past three months. Electromyography showed extensive neurogenic damage. Genetic testing identified a novel heterozygous missense variant, c.355 C > T (p.Arg119Cys), in the SQSTM1 gene. Family verification revealed that his phenotypically normal mother carried the same variant. The literature search identified 58 cases of ALS associated with SQSTM1 variants. Missense variants were the most common type. We identified a novel SQSTM1 variant, c.355 C > T (p.Arg119Cys), in a ALS patient. Although this finding expands the variant spectrum, its pathogenicity remains uncertain and requires further functional validation and pedigree confirmation. Our literature review further shows that SQSTM1-associated ALS predominantly presents with limb onset, with a subset of patients exhibiting frontotemporal dementia or Paget’s disease.

Introduction

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a fatal neurodegenerative disease characterized by the progressive loss of motor neurons in the cerebral cortex and spinal cord [1]. The clinical manifestations include progressive worsening of limb weakness, muscle atrophy, and respiratory failure. Patient survival is approximately 3–5 years [2]. Approximately 10% of ALS patients are familial, while the majority are sporadic [3]. Currently, more than 40 ALS-related pathogenic genes have been identified, among which C9orf72, SOD1, TARDBP, and FUS are the most common pathogenic genes [4].

The sequestosome 1 (SQSTM1) gene is located on human chromosome 5 (5q35) and consists of eight exons encoding p62 protein [5]. p62 protein has ubiquitin-binding function and participates in cell metabolism, immune response and DNA damage repair by regulating nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) signaling pathway and autophagy [6]. p62 has been found to colocalize with SOD1, TDP-43 or FUS related aggregates in ALS, suggesting that p62 is involved in the pathogenesis of ALS [7]. In 2011, Fecto et al. first reported that SQSTM1 gene variants were associated with familial and sporadic ALS [8]. Cohort studies in different ethnic groups have confirmed the role of this gene in the pathogenesis of ALS [9, 10]. Several cohort studies based on different ethnic populations have further confirmed SQSTM1 as one of the ALS pathogenic genes, with a variant frequency of about 1–4% [11, 12]. Most SQSTM1 variants are mainly missense mutations, which are widely distributed in multiple functional domains such as PB1, UBA, ZZ, and TRAF6 [13].

This study reported a case of sporadic ALS with a novel variant in the SQSTM1 gene. Through pedigree analysis and a systematic literature review, we aim to evaluate the pathogenicity and genotype–phenotype correlation of this variant, and to enrich the variant spectrum of SQSTM1-related ALS.

Subjects and method

Clinical data collection

The patient was evaluated at the Department of Neurology of Beijing Tsinghua Chuangguang Hospital in September 2025. The clinical data of the patients were collected, including gender, age of onset, site of onset, disease duration, family history, results of neurological examination, electrophysiological examinations and imaging examination.

Genetic analyses

The peripheral blood of the patient was collected and sent to Bioengineering Inc. (Beijing, China) for whole exome sequencing (WES) to identify the causal gene. Briefly, DNA was isolated and fragmented to build a DNA library by using the Universal Plus DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (VAHTS®, Inc., China). Then, the library was sequenced using an Illumina NovaSeq6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, USA) using 150-bp paired-end reads according to the standard manual. The sequencing data was filtered and aligned with the human reference genome (GRCh37/hg19) by using the BWA Aligner, and variants were annotated by VEP. The candidate causal genes thereby discovered were then confirmed via Sanger sequencing. Meanwhile, Sanger sequencing of the same locus was performed on the parents of the patient to evaluate the inheritance pattern and pathogenicity of the variant. Through whole exome sequencing data, all known ALS-related pathogenic genes were screened to exclude the contribution of other known gene variants to the phenotype of this patient.

Literature review

We searched Web of Science, PubMed and Scopus databases of ALS associated with SQSTM1 variants, using the search criteria of (“ amyotrophic lateral sclerosis " OR " motor neuron disease “) AND (“ SQSTM1 “). Exclusion criteria: (1) non-original study; (2) non-human research; (3) no detailed clinical data; (4) not related to ALS patients. The PRISMA flow diagram is shown in Fig. 1. The clinical and genetic data of ALS patients with SQSTM1 variants in the literature were extracted, including variant site, variant type, gender, age of onset, site of onset, survival time and comorbidities.

Case report

A 49‑year‑old man complained of progressive weakness in both lower limbs that began one year prior, following a lumbar sprain. The weakness was initially more pronounced on the right side, with difficulty in right foot dorsiflexion. Symptoms worsened six months ago, with inability to lift the right thigh and a steppage gait. Over the past three months, he developed weakness and atrophy of the left upper limb, particularly the first dorsal interosseous muscle. No limb numbness, dysarthria, dizziness and other symptoms were observed. Neurological examination indicated that the patient was alert and exhibited fluent speech. The bilateral pupils were equal and round, with a brisk light reflex. The eyes demonstrated full movement in all directions, without nystagmus or diplopia. Facial sensation was symmetrical, masticatory muscle strength was normal, and corneal reflexes were intact. There was no deviation of the teeth, and no air leakage was observed upon buccal puffing. The uvula was centered, the pharyngeal reflex was present, and the tongue, when extended, was centered without evidence of tongue muscle atrophy or fibrillation. The tibialis anterior muscle and quadriceps femoris muscle of both lower limbs showed atrophy. The left dorsal first interosseous muscle and thenar muscle showed mild atrophy. Fasciculation was seen in the quadriceps femoris of both lower limbs. The proximal muscle strength of both upper limbs was grade Ⅴ, the distal muscle strength of left upper limbs was grade Ⅳ, and the distal muscle strength of right upper limbs was grade Ⅴ. The muscle strength of the proximal right lower limb was grade Ⅲ, and the dorsiflexion and plantarflexion of the foot were grade 0. The proximal muscle strength of the left lower limb was grade Ⅳ and the distal muscle strength was grade Ⅴ. Bilateral biceps and triceps reflexes were hyperactive, bilateral knee reflexes and Achilles tendon reflexes were hyperactive, and mandibular reflexes were not elicited. Hoffmann’s sign was positive on the left side and Rossolimo’s sign was positive on both sides. Finger-nose test and heel-knee-tibia test were performed without ataxia, and Romberg sign was negative. The patient walked with steppage gait and required assistance. The ALSFRS-R score was 37. He did not have cognitive impairment or sensory disturbances. Electromyography showed extensive neurogenic damage. Fibrillation potential and positive sharp waves were observed in the muscle groups of cervical, thoracic and lumbosacral segments. The duration of motor unit potential was widened, the amplitude was increased, and the recruitment pattern was reduced. Repetitive electrical stimulation was normal. Serum alkaline phosphatase levels were measured and were within the normal range (78 U/L). Bone radiography showed no signs of Paget’s disease. Brain magnetic resonance imaging and cerebrospinal fluid were normal. Pulmonary ventilation and gas exchange function test showed mildly decreased pulmonary ventilation reserve function, moderate small airway dysfunction, and severely increased percentage of residual volume. The patient was diagnosed with clinically confirmed ALS according to the revised El Escorial criteria. We treated him with riluzole and edaravone. After treatment, the weakness progressed without significant relief.

Whole-exome sequencing revealed that the patient carried a novel heterozygous missense variant c.355 C > T in SQSTM1 gene (NM_003900.5). This variant is located in exon 3 and results in the conversion of arginine to cysteine substitution at position 119 (p.Arg119Cys) (Fig. 2). The variant frequency in East Asian population from the gnomAD database was 0.0001, and it has not been reported in PubMed or ClinVar. According to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines, this variant was rated as a variant of unknown significance (VUS). Sanger sequencing verification of the family pedigree showed that his mother carried the same heterozygous variant (Fig. 3), while his father did not carry this variant (Fig. 4).

Review of ALS patients with SQSTM1 variants

We systematically reviewed previous studies (Table 1), including a total of 33 males and 25 females. The average age of onset for female patients with ALS accompanied by SQSTM1 gene variants was 62.7 ± 2.5 years, with a survival period ranging from 5 to 218 months. The average age of onset for male patients was 55.8 ± 1.9 years, with a survival period ranging from 10 to 168 months. Most cases presented with limb onset (68.1%), while a few had bulbar onset (31.9%). There were 20 cases with a clear family history and 6 cases with comorbidities (Table 2).

Discussion

In this study, we reported a sporadic ALS patient with a novel variant c.355 C > T (p.Arg119Cys) in the SQSTM1 gene, and systematically reviewed the clinical and genetic features of SQSTM1 variant-related ALS. The variant is located in the ZZ binding domain of p62 protein. The clinical phenotype was typical limb-onset ALS, without cognitive impairment or bone disease. Our findings expand the variant spectrum of SQSTM1 gene and provide a new clinical basis for understanding the role of p62 protein in the pathogenesis of ALS.

The p.Arg119Cys variant reported in this study is located in the ZZ binding domain of p62 protein. The ZZ domain is a zinc-finger domain at the N-terminus of p62 protein, consisting of about 40 amino acids, which forms a stable steric conformation by binding zinc ions [27]. The ZZ domain is highly conserved evolutionarily and its primary function is to mediate protein-protein interactions [28, 29]. Previous studies have shown that ZZ domain specifically binds to NF-κB and receptor-interacting protein kinase 1 [30, 31]. p62 protein plays an important role in the regulation of NF-κB signaling pathway and immune response [31]. In addition, the ZZ domain is involved in the binding of p62 protein to autophagy-related protein LC3, which affects the progress of autophagy [32]. In the reviewed cases, we identified nine variations within the ZZ binding domain. All of these cases presented with limb-onset symptoms. The average age of onset was 59.5 years, with a majority of females. The average time from onset to death was 48 months, and no other diseases were concurrently present. The clinical phenotype of the present case does not contradict the trend seen in these nine case reports. More cases and functional studies are needed to confirm whether there is a genotype-phenotype correlation.

The UBA domain was the most frequently mutated region of SQSTM1 [33, 34], accounting for the highest proportion (49.1%) in the literature review. Variants in this region often lead to a decrease in the ubiquitin binding ability of p62 protein, which affects cell autophagy and the clearance of abnormal protein aggregates, and leads to a more complex clinical phenotype. Variants in the UBA region are often associated with cognitive impairment, frontotemporal dementia (FTD), or Paget’s disease.

This study systematically reviewed the reported ALS patients with SQSTM1 variants. About 70% of SQSTM1 variant patients presented with limb weakness as the first symptom, while bulbar onset was relatively rare (about 25%). There was considerable heterogeneity in disease progression, and the time from onset to death or the end of follow-up ranged from 5 months to 218 months, suggesting that the clinical progression rate of SQSTM1 variant-related ALS was significantly different. The survival time may be related to factors such as variant sites, functional domains and modified genes. Pedigree analysis showed that the mother with normal phenotype carried the same c.355 C > T heterozygous variant as the patient. This finding confirmed the presence of incomplete penetrance of the variant. Table 2 shows that 37.1% of the patients in this retrospective cohort had a clear family history, and 62.9% had no family history, suggesting that a considerable proportion of SQSTM1 variant-related ALS is sporadic, and incomplete penetrance may be one of the important reasons for this phenomenon.

Several pathogenic genes have similar characteristics in ALS. Previous studies have shown that the penetrance of individuals carrying C9orf72 repeat expansions is about 80%-90% by the age of 80 years, but some carriers never develop the disease during their lifetime [33, 35]. This difference in penetrance may be related to the length of repeat expansions, modifier genes and environmental factors [36]. There were significant differences in the penetrance of different mutation sites in the SOD1 gene. The SOD1-A4V mutation has an extremely high penetrance, and disease is almost inevitable in carriers [37]. The SOD1-D90A mutation is recessive in some populations [38]. Homozygotes have a high risk of developing the disease, while heterozygotes may not develop the disease at all or at a very late age. Some family members carrying the same mutation in the TARDBP gene did not show clinical symptoms of ALS or FTD [39]. Yilmaz et al. found that in 486 patients with familial ALS, there were individuals carrying the same mutation but without lifelong disease in multiple families with SQSTM1 variants, suggesting that other modifying genes or environmental factors may be involved in the disease penetrance [10]. In this case, the mother of the patient carried the same heterozygous variant of p.Arg119Cys, further supporting the presence of incomplete penetrance. However, incomplete penetrance is only one of the possible outcomes given the small amount of family data currently available.

Recently, Wang et al. analyzed the largest cohort to date of ALS patients with SQSTM1 variants, identifying 32 patients carrying 25 different SQSTM1 mutations among 2,011 Chinese ALS patients, yielding a mutation frequency of 1.6% [9]. A meta-analysis including 7,183 ALS patients reported an overall SQSTM1 mutation frequency of 2.4% [9]. Notably, 26% of SQSTM1 variant carriers in this cohort exhibited cognitive impairment, of which 43% had behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia. Most of SQSTM1 variants in ALS are missense mutations distributed in multiple domains of p62 protein, and some of them are related to Paget bone disease and FTD. Yilmaz et al. screened 486 familial ALS patients from Germany and Sweden, identifying nine novel mutation sites and five previously reported ones [10]. Yilmaz concluded that the evidence for SQSTM1 pathogenicity remains insufficient compared to other autophagy-related genes such as TBK1 and OPTN [10]. This suggests that SQSTM1 variants in ALS should be interpreted cautiously. The p.Arg119Cys variant carried by the patient in this case report was not detected in the large Chinese cohort, indicating it is a rare variant whose pathogenic role remains unclear.

Although WES did not identify rare variants in other known ALS-associated genes in this study, we cannot completely rule out the possibility of oligogenic inheritance. ALS is considered to have an oligogenic inheritance pattern, in which rare variants in multiple genes in the same patient may jointly participate in the disease [40]. Oligogenic inheritance patterns are particularly important for genes with moderate or weak effect strengths, as a single variant may not be sufficient to cause disease independently [41]. Wang et al. found that a patient with the CHCHD10 p.Gly66val variant carried three additional variants, SQSTM1 p.His359del, UNC13A p.Leu1034Val, and SUSD1 p.Trp704Ser. These results indicate that oligogenic inheritance can occur in SQSTM1 mutation carriers [42]. Although this pattern was not observed in the results of whole-exome genetic testing in this patient, the possibility of a gene interaction with variants in noncoding regions cannot be ruled out.

SQSTM1 variants are involved in the pathogenesis of ALS through autophagy dysregulation, impaired protein clearance and neuroinflammation. At present, p62 protein and autophagy pathway have become promising therapeutic targets in ALS. Trehalose is an autophagy inducer. It has been demonstrated in the SOD1-G93A mouse model to reduce the aggregation of SOD1 protein and p62 protein, and to improve cellular autophagy [43]. It is currently being evaluated in a 2b/3 phase clinical trial [44]. Rapamycin is a mTOR-dependent autophagy activator. It has completed a phase II randomized double-blind placebo-controlled trial in 63 ALS patients, showing good safety and tolerability [45]. However, its clinical efficacy still needs further verification. In the zebrafish model with SQSTM1 knockdown, rapamycin treatment improved the motor phenotype, suggesting that the regulation of the mTOR pathway may have therapeutic significance for SQSTM1-related ALS [46]. Autophagy-targeting chimeric degraders ATC141 and ATC142 were used to bind oligomers of misfolded TDP-43 to p62 to induce lysosomal co-degradation of TDP-43 with autophagosome precursors, preserving monomeric TDP-43 [47]. Oral administration of ATC141 to TDP-43 A315T mice reduced TDP-43 accumulation and improved neuromuscular coordination and cognitive function. A recent report described a patient with ALS and the SQSTM1 p. Pro392leu variant who received an individualized regimen of dimethyl fumarate (Nrf2 activator), celecoxib (NF-κB inhibitor), and rapamycin (mTOR pathway modulator) [20]. The applicability of these strategies to patients with specific SQSTM1 variants is unknown. The functional effects of different variants on p62-mediated autophagy and NF-κB/Nrf2 signaling pathways may be significantly different, and it is important to evaluate individually before considering targeted interventions.

Our study has several limitations. First, according to the ACMG guidelines, the c.355 C > T variant was classified as VUS, and no definitive conclusion could be drawn on its pathogenicity. Of note, another variant affecting the same codon, c.355 C > G (p.Arg119Gly), has only been reported in the control individuals [26]. This suggests that the evidence for a pathogenic role of variants at this locus is limited. The lack of a family segregation analysis for functional studies is a core limitation of this study. Secondly, the number of ALS patients with SQSTM1 variations included in this review is relatively small, and it cannot fully reflect the clinical characteristics of ALS related to SQSTM1 variations. Third, the included studies were heterogeneous in terms of clinical assessment methods, duration of follow-up, and range of genetic testing, which may have introduced bias in the analysis. Fourth, our literature search was limited to English and Chinese databases. Although we supplemented searches of Chinese-language databases to include data from the Asian cohort, we cannot rule out the possibility that relevant studies published in other languages were missed. Finally, given the limited pedigree data available, incomplete penetrance is a possibility rather than a certainty. In the future, functional studies and extended family follow-up are needed to clarify the pathogenic significance of p.Arg119Cys.

Conclusion

We report a case of ALS patient carrying a novel SQSTM1 variant, c.355 C > T (p.Arg119Cys). After a literature review, we summarized the clinical and genetic features of SQSTM1-related ALS. Further functional studies and pedigree validation are needed to clarify the pathogenicity of p.Arg119Cys.
