Garré’s Chronic Sclerosing Osteomyelitis: An Overview of Clinical and Radiologic Features
Authors: Mohamed Fadil, Ayman Farouki, Rachida Saouab, Hassan En-nouali, Jamal El Fenni, Zakariya Toufga
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGarré's chronic sclerosing osteomyelitis is a rare, nonsuppurative form of chronic osteomyelitis, typically seen in children and adolescents, and characterized by periosteal bone proliferation. This case report presents an unusual manifestation in a 20-year-old male with recurrent swelling of the left cheek and trismus, in whom imaging and clinical evolution supported the diagnosis. The management approach and follow-up imaging are discussed, and the case is contextualized within the existing literature.Keywords: Garré's osteomyelitis, Mandibular periostitis, Facial swelling, Periosteal reaction, Double cortex sign

Introduction

First described by Carl Garré in 1893, Garré's osteomyelitis is a reactive, proliferative periostitis resulting from chronic low-grade irritation or infection, often of odontogenic origin. It is usually observed in the mandible of children or adolescents, with radiographic features such as periosteal bone layering and absence of suppuration [1, 2]. Although infrequent in adults, similar presentations can occur, often leading to delayed or misdiagnosis.

Case report

A 20-year-old male presented with a history of recurrent swelling of the left cheek and limited mouth opening persisting for three months. He had received multiple outpatient courses of antibiotics with only temporary relief. Upon admission, physical examination revealed a firm swelling of the left cheek and trismus. Notably, the patient was afebrile and had no systemic signs of infection.

Initial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed diffuse bone marrow edema of the left mandibular ramus, along with adjacent soft tissue and masticatory muscle edema (Fig. 1). A contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) scan revealed swelling of the muscles and adjacent soft tissues, with normal bone architecture and no evidence of periosteal reaction or cortical lysis (Fig. 2). Viral serologies and a soft tissue aspiration were negative for any bacterial or viral pathogens.

Facial MRI in coronal (A) and axial (B) T2-weighted sequences, and post-contrast axial T1-weighted sequence with gadolinium injection (C), demonstrating diffuse swelling of the left cheek involving both soft tissues and masticatory muscles, with marked contrast enhancement.

Initial contrast-enhanced facial CT scan. Bone window images (A and B) show preserved mandibular cortical bone without signs of erosion, lysis, or periosteal reaction. Soft tissue window (C) reveals swelling of the left masticatory muscles and adjacent soft tissues, with no associated abscess or fluid collection.

The patient was started on a broad-spectrum regimen of ciprofloxacin, gentamicin, and metronidazole, along with corticosteroids for three weeks. A follow-up MRI performed one month later demonstrated a reduction in bone marrow and soft tissue edema (Fig. 3). A repeat CT scan, however, revealed a solid periosteal reaction with the appearance of a "double cortex" consistent with chronic periosteal new bone formation (Fig. 4).

Follow-up facial MRI performed one month later shows a significant reduction in soft tissue and muscular swelling on the left side, with decreased contrast enhancement compared to the initial exam.

Follow-up facial CT scan obtained one month later. Coronal (A) and axial (B) bone window images reveal a fine, smooth, and regular periosteal reaction along the lateral aspect of the left mandible, producing a "double cortex" appearance consistent with chronic periostitis.

Clinically, the patient’s evolution was marked by progressive improvement in mouth opening and a noticeable reduction of facial swelling, leading to a favorable cosmetic outcome (Fig. 5).

Facial CT scan with 3D reconstruction demonstrating comparison before (A) and after (B) treatment, showing a clear reduction in left facial swelling.

These imaging findings, in conjunction with the clinical course (young patient age and recurrent swelling of the left cheek) and lack of identifiable infectious agent, supported the diagnosis of Garré's chronic sclerosing osteomyelitis.

Discussion

Garré’s chronic sclerosing osteomyelitis represents a distinctive form of chronic osteomyelitis, typically non-suppurative and slowly progressive. It is characterized by the proliferation of new bone under the periosteum in response to chronic low-grade irritation or infection [1, 3]. Although it predominantly affects the mandible in children and adolescents, adult cases are increasingly reported, particularly in individuals with delayed diagnosis or atypical presentations [2, 5].

Clinically, the disease manifests as a firm, painless facial swelling, often accompanied by trismus but without signs of acute infection such as fever or lymphadenopathy. In our case, the patient’s afebrile condition and absence of systemic symptoms were consistent with the indolent nature of Garré’s osteomyelitis.

Radiologically, the hallmark feature is a laminated periosteal reaction—often described as an “onion-skin” appearance—best demonstrated on CT. In early stages, this periosteal proliferation may be absent or subtle. MRI, however, is more sensitive for detecting early marrow and soft tissue changes, as it demonstrated diffuse edema of the mandible and surrounding muscles in our case. This highlights the complementary role of MRI in early detection and disease monitoring [6].

The delayed periosteal ossification observed on follow-up CT, presenting as a “double cortex” appearance, is a classic indicator of Garré's osteomyelitis in the healing or chronic stage. Such evolution reinforces the importance of sequential imaging when initial findings are inconclusive, particularly in persistent clinical cases.

Management primarily involves elimination of the underlying source of irritation—most commonly an odontogenic focus such as an infected or impacted tooth. In our case, however, no dental or infectious origin was identified despite thorough clinical and microbiological assessment, suggesting an idiopathic or immune-mediated pathogenesis as described in other reports [4, 6].

Antibiotic therapy remains a cornerstone of treatment, although its role is mainly empirical in cases without identifiable pathogens. Adjunctive corticosteroid therapy was introduced in our patient due to the non-suppurative nature of the disease and the prominent inflammatory component observed on MRI, characterized by bone marrow and soft tissue edema. The rationale for corticosteroid use lies in their ability to downregulate local inflammatory cytokines and reduce periosteal and muscular inflammation, thereby relieving trismus and accelerating soft tissue recovery. In this case, the combination of antibiotics and corticosteroids led to a clear clinical improvement, with progressive restoration of mouth opening and marked reduction of facial swelling. Non-surgical approaches, including long-term anti-inflammatory treatment, have been successfully employed in other reported cases [3, 4].

For recalcitrant or recurrent cases, alternative therapies such as colchicine and bisphosphonates have shown promise. Ucar et al. reported the use of colchicine in a pediatric patient with excellent results following failed NSAID therapy [4]. Similarly, Medina-Porqueres et al. documented successful treatment with intravenous bisphosphonates—such as zoledronic acid—in chronic refractory mandibular osteomyelitis in adults [6]. These agents likely exert their effects through modulation of osteoclastic activity and inflammatory cytokines.

Surgical intervention is generally reserved for severe or complicated cases with functional impairment or cosmetic deformity. In our patient, conservative treatment was sufficient, emphasizing the importance of early diagnosis and appropriate medical management in avoiding invasive procedures.

Overall, this case supports growing evidence that Garré’s osteomyelitis can occur in young adults and may not always be linked to a specific infectious trigger. It underscores the utility of sequential MRI and CT in diagnosis and follow-up and highlights the evolving role of medical therapy—including antibiotics, corticosteroids, and potentially immunomodulatory agents—in the successful management of this condition.
