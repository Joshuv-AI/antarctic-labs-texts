Burden, perception of stigma, and gender differences among informal care partners of individuals with mild cognitive impairment
Authors: Pascual Sánchez-Juan, Elena García-Arcelay, Mariló Almagro, Mircea Balasa, Gerad Piñol-Ripoll, Mercè Boada, Lamberto Landete, Inmaculada Abellan, Angel Berbel, Beatriz Espejo, Miquel Baquero, Juan Marin, Emilio Franco-Macias, Alberto Villarejo-Galende, Félix Viñuela Fernández, Inmaculada Feria Vilar, Carmen Perez-Vieitez, Norberto Rodríguez-Espinosa, Albert Puig-Pijoan, Eduard Bargay Pizarro, Eloy Rodríguez-Rodríguez, Jesús Rodrigo, Jorge Maurino, Sagrario Manzano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCaring for individuals with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) is increasingly recognized as a significant clinical challenge. However, the latent burden, driven by perceived stigma and gender-specific vulnerabilities, remains poorly characterized during these early stages of cognitive decline.ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the prevalence of care partner strain and affiliate stigma, and to identify independent correlates of psychological distress and protective factors among informal care partners of individuals with MCI.MethodsWe conducted a multicenter, observational, cross-sectional study across 19 memory clinics in Spain. Care partners (n = 196) were evaluated using the Zarit Burden Interview, Affiliate Stigma Scale, and validated instruments assessing resilience, relationship quality, coping strategies, and psychological distress. Multivariate logistic regression models identified independent predictors of burden and stigma.ResultsSignificant care partner strain affected 59.7% of the cohort, while 39.3% perceived themselves as stigmatized. Multivariate analysis revealed that high burden was independently associated with anxiety symptoms (OR = 1.13, p = 0.017) and perceived stigma (OR = 1.07, p = 0.007). High resilience served as the primary determinant protecting the 60.7% of partners who did not report significant stigma (OR = 1.05, p < 0.001). Compared to their male counterparts, female care partners were significantly younger and reported higher levels of burden, anxiety, and stigma.ConclusionsCare partner strain and affiliate stigma are correlated phenomena that emerge long before formal patient dependency. These findings underscore the need for gender-sensitive clinical frameworks and specific interventions that actively strengthen resilience immediately upon MCI diagnosis to mitigate early psychological deterioration.

Introduction

Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) represents a clinical transition between normative age-related cognitive decline and dementia.1–4 It is characterized by objective cognitive deficits that exceed expectations for an individual's age and educational background. 2 From the perspective of the individual, the MCI stage triggers a precarious identity transition. 3 Although these individuals typically maintain independence in basic self-care, they frequently exhibit subtle alterations in daily activity patterns, a reduced capacity for complex instrumental tasks, and a profound fear of future disease progression.2,3 Because many patients retain high levels of insight regarding their expanding cognitive deficits, they are uniquely vulnerable to the immediate psychosocial impacts of public stigma.5,6

As these functional and cognitive changes emerge, a parallel transformation occurs within the family unit, necessitating a shift in clinical perspective toward the role of the “care partner”.7–13 This terminology precisely reflects the collaborative nature of a relationship wherein the individual remains functionally independent but requires expanding monitoring and assistance.9,10,12 For the care partner, navigating the patient´s subtle cognitive deficits constitutes a form of “invisible labor”. This labor is characterized by constant safety monitoring and the management of understated neuropsychiatric symptoms, such as mood lability or apathy. Beyond this emotional scaffolding, care partners frequently assume responsibilities for transportation, financial management, medication adherence, and healthcare coordination, often performing these complex duties alongside formal employment.7–13 To manage accumulated relationship losses and protect the care recipient from external judgment, care partners frequently employ adaptive self-presentation strategies, such as utilizing “situational masks” or “modified-self constructs” to camouflage internal distress.7,9,13

This complex web of care tasks triggers a trajectory of psychological strain that is compounded by affiliate stigma, the internalization of societal shame and subsequent social withdrawal by the care partners themselves.14–17 Because care partners are linked to the stigmatized individual, public biases cascade down to family members through courtesy stigma. Recent multinational European research suggests that this experience of bias is heavily modified by local societal norms. 5 For instance, care partners in the United Kingdom report higher levels of social rejection than those in Mediterranean or Eastern European contexts, where close-knit “familialism” norms may view cognitive decline as a natural element of aging. 5 Nevertheless, in contexts where heavy social expectations place a disproportionate emotional weight on the family, both perceived stigma (awareness of societal bias) and affiliate stigma (internalized shame) act as robust drivers of stress, depression, and anxiety.14–16

Furthermore, these care partner dynamics are highly gendered. 18 Female care partners consistently report greater global burden and distress than their male counterparts, regardless of objective patient symptom severity or care duration.3,5 Despite these critical dynamics, significant research gaps remain regarding the early psychosocial intersection of stigma, burden, and gender within the MCI paradigm, as traditional support structures are rarely triggered until formal patient dependency occurs.

To address these gaps, the primary objective of this study was to assess the prevalence of care partner burden and affiliate stigma within an informal MCI caregiving population. Furthermore, we aimed to identify the independent correlates of burden, specifically focusing on the relationship between perceived stigma and psychological distress, while evaluating how gender influences the well-being and coping mechanisms of those providing care.

Methods

This observational, multicenter, cross-sectional study was conducted across 19 hospital-based memory clinics in Spain. The research was performed in collaboration with the Spanish Confederation of Alzheimer's Disease and Other Dementias (CEAFA), a non-governmental organization advocating for individuals affected by cognitive impairment and their families. The study received approval from the research review board of Hospital del Mar in Barcelona, Spain. All participants provided written informed consent before participating in the study.

Participants and recruitment

Eligible care partners were defined as unpaid family members or friends (e.g., spouse, partner, or adult child) providing non-reimbursed support. Inclusion required regular contact with the care recipient, established as a minimum of two interactions per week, to ensure the care partner possessed sufficient firsthand knowledge of the patient's daily functioning and neuropsychiatric status. Individuals were excluded if they demonstrated insufficient language proficiency to provide informed consent or complete the study assessments. Potential participants were identified and selected by the attending neurologists during routine clinical follow-up visits.

Clinical characterization of care recipients

To ensure diagnostic accuracy and sample homogeneity, all care recipients required an established diagnosis of MCI confirmed by a neurologist. This diagnosis was based on the National Institute on Aging and the Alzheimer's Association (NIA-AA) clinical criteria. Specifically, care recipients were required to have a score of 3 on the Global Deterioration Scale (GDS).19,20 This clinical stage is characterized by cognitive deficits evident to associates that do not yet significantly impede basic daily functioning.

Data collection

All sociodemographic and clinical characteristics were completed on-site using digital tablets during the clinical visit. For care partners, recorded sociodemographic variables included age, gender, educational attainment (years of schooling), current occupational status, and the nature of the relationship to the care recipient. For care recipients, data were collected regarding age, gender, disease duration, and cognitive status via the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE). While participants completed the self-report instruments independently, attending clinicians remained available to provide technical assistance with the digital tablets or to clarify the definitions of specific survey items as needed.

Outcome measures

The Zarit Burden Interview (ZBI-22), Affiliate Stigma Scale (ASS), short Sense of Competence Questionnaire (S-SCQ), Quality of the Caregiver-Patient Relationship Scale (QCPR), the 14-item Resilience Scale (RS-14), Duke-UNC Functional Social Support Questionnaire (FSSQ), Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), Beck Hopelessness Scale (BHS), Coping Orientation to Problems Experienced Inventory (Brief-COPE), Neuropsychiatric Inventory (NPI), and a single-item measure from the Physician Work Life Study scale were used to gather information on caregiver burden, feeling able to care for the patient, quality of the caregiver-patient relationship, resilience, perceived adequacy of social support, affiliate stigma, anxiety and depressive symptoms, hopelessness, coping strategies (effective and ineffective ways to manage caregiving), patient neuropsychiatric symptoms, and burnout, respectively.21–32

The ZBI-22 demonstrates strong psychometric properties across diverse carer populations, characterized by high internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.85-0.95), reliable stability, and robust validity. 21 This 22-item instrument utilizes a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“never”) to 4 (“nearly always”). Total scores range from 0 to 88, with higher values reflecting greater burden. Conceptually, the scale assesses five key dimensions of burden: personal strain, role strain, financial strain, self-criticism or guilt, and a perceived loss of control. Following established research thresholds, a total score of 17 or higher was used to define high burden in this study. 22 The ASS is a 22-item instrument designed to quantify the internalized stigma experienced by family members or care partners of individuals with mental illness, intellectual disabilities, or other chronic conditions. 23 The scale demonstrates satisfactory internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.82–0.94) and strong concurrent validity. Responses are aggregated to produce a total score ranging from 0 to 88, with a validated cutoff score of >31 utilized to identify a significant perception of stigmatization. The S-SCQ is a 7-item instrument designed to assess caregivers’ confidence in their skills, knowledge, and capacity to provide support and care. 24 The total score ranges from 7 to 35, with higher scores indicating a greater sense of competence. The QCPR is a 14-item instrument designed to evaluate the subjective quality of the relationship between informal caregivers and care recipients. 25 The total score ranges from 14 to 70, with higher scores indicating a more positive quality of the relationship. Resilience was measured using the 14-Item Resilience Scale (RS-14). 26 This 7-point Likert-type scale ranges from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 7 (“strongly agree”). The total score ranges between 14 to 98 with higher scores indicating greater resilience. The FSSQ is an 11-item self-report tool assessing perceived satisfaction with social support. 27 The total score ranges from 11 to 55, with higher scores indicating better perceived social support. The HADS is a 14-item self-report questionnaire designed to assess symptoms of anxiety and depression. 28 It comprises two distinct 7-item subscales: HADS-A for anxiety and HADS-D for depression. The total score for each subscale ranges from 0 to 21 points. Higher scores indicate greater symptom severity. A score of ≥8 is commonly considered to indicate a probable case for either anxiety or depression. The BHS is a 20-item true/false questionnaire measuring an individual's level of negative expectations about the future. 29 The total score ranges from 0 to 20, with higher scores indicating a greater level of hopelessness. The Brief COPE is a 28-item self-report assessing 14 coping strategies (2 items per strategy). 30 Items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = not at all, 4 = a lot). Scores for each strategy range from 2 to 8, reflecting the extent of its use, with higher scores indicating greater reported use. For analysis, the 14 strategies are grouped into 3 higher-order coping dimensions: problem-focused coping (adaptive), emotional-focused coping (potentially adaptive), and avoidant coping (maladaptive). Neuropsychiatric symptoms in care recipients were assessed using the NPI, which evaluates 12 behavioral domains based on frequency and severity. Higher total scores reflect a higher degree of neuropsychiatric disturbance. 31 Finally, burnout was assessed using a single-item measure from the Physician Work Life Study. 32 A cut-off score ≥3 indicates the presence of burnout.

Statistical analysis

For continuous variables, descriptive statistics including mean, standard deviation (SD), and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and valid percentages. To determine the independent predictors of the primary categorical outcomes, three separate binary logistic regression models were constructed (caregiver burden, perceived stigma, and female gender). Variables from the bivariate analysis showing statistical significance (p < 0.05) or a strong trend (p < 0.2) were systematically included as independent variables in the respective multivariate models. The results from the logistic regression models are presented as Odds Ratios (OR) with corresponding 95% CI. Statistical significance was established at p < 0.05 for all analyses.

The targeted sample size of 200 participants was determined a priori to ensure sufficient statistical power and model stability for multivariate logistic regression. This target follows the “events-per-variable” (EPV) criterion, which recommends a minimum of 10 to 15 observations per independent predictor to prevent model overfitting.

Results

Of the 200 care partners who initially met the eligibility criteria and provided written informed consent, four withdrew prior to completing the digital assessments. Consequently, the final analysis cohort comprised 196 informal care partners. The mean age of participants was 63.5 (SD 13.1) years, with a majority being female (62.8%). Most participants lived with the patient (73.5%) and were the patient's spouse or partner (68.9%). Caring and burden

Significant care partner burden was prevalent, affecting 59.7% (n = 117) of the cohort (mean ZBI-22 score: 22.3 [13.6], range: 2.0–73.0). Among the measured dimensions, burden in the relationship (representing interaction difficulties) and emotional well-being (representing the direct psychological and emotional toll) were identified as the most intensely impacted components.

As detailed in Table 1, burdened care partners experienced significantly higher elevated levels of anxiety, depressive symptoms, hopelessness, and perceived stigma than their non-burdened counterparts. Furthermore, these individuals reported significantly lower resilience, poorer relationship quality with the care recipient, less adequate social support, and an increased reliance on maladaptive, avoidant coping strategies. In the multivariate logistic regression model, high caregiver burden remained independently associated with both anxiety symptoms (OR = 1.13, 95% CI 1.02–1.25, p = 0.017) and perceived stigma (OR = 1.07, 95% CI 1.02–1.12, p = 0.007).

Care partners in the non-stigmatized subgroup experienced significantly lower burden, fewer symptoms of anxiety and depression, and lower hopelessness compared to the stigmatized subgroup. Additionally, these non-stigmatized individuals reported significantly higher relationship quality with their care recipients, a stronger sense of caregiving competence, and superior perceived social support. In the multivariate logistic regression model, the absence of perceived stigma was independently associated with higher levels of resilience (OR = 1.05, 95% CI 1.03–1.07, p < 0.001).

Differences between female and male care partners

The study cohort was predominantly female (62.8%, n = 123). As detailed in Table 3, female care partners were significantly younger and experienced higher levels of burden, anxiety, and perceived stigma than their male counterparts. Behaviorally, female care partners also demonstrated a significantly greater reliance on both emotion-focused and avoidant coping strategies. In the multivariate analysis, female gender was independently associated with a higher likelihood of utilizing emotion-focused coping (OR = 2.50, 95% CI 1.43–4.35; p = 0.001), experiencing more severe anxiety symptoms (OR = 1.15, 95% CI 1.06–1.25; p < 0.001), and being of a younger age (OR = 0.96, 95% CI 0.94–0.98; p < 0.001).

Discussion

Caring for an individual with MCI exerts a substantial psychosocial and economic impact, even while the care recipient maintains functional independence.33,34 Nearly 60% of our participants reported significant care partner strain, a prevalence exceeding the 36% to 48% burden reported in comparable cohorts.8,13 This variance may reflect differing clinical thresholds in the ZBI-22 instrument or the specific psychosocial support infrastructure in Spain. Such elevated strain underscores the “invisible labor” assumed by family members long before formal patient dependency occurs.12,35 These responsibilities involve managing subtle neuropsychiatric symptoms and providing emotionally demanding logistical support for executive functions, such as financial management, scheduling, and medication adherence.35,36 Crucially, this shifting dynamic alters a preexisting equal partnership into one characterized by a perceived power imbalance, driving substantial psychological distress long before the patient requires assistance with basic activities.

Our finding that relationship quality and emotional well-being were the most severely affected dimensions aligns with literature identifying subtle behavioral changes, rather than functional disability, as the primary drivers of care partner strain in MCI.8,13 Intermittent neuropsychiatric symptoms such as mood lability, apathy, and anxiety disrupt relational equilibrium long before dependency occurs. Furthermore, the association between burden and anxiety observed in this study is corroborated by existing cross-sectional and longitudinal data.8,13 This correlation is particularly urgent given that care partner burden accelerates rapidly over time, particularly when underlying Alzheimer's pathology drives the progressive cognitive decline. 8

The significant prevalence of affiliate stigma identified in our study (39.3%) underscores a pervasive psychosocial challenge. Our analysis demonstrates that stigma is independently associated with increased burden, suggesting a bidirectional relationship between the internalization of negative societal views and subjective care strain. 16 This profound emotional impact likely explains why care partners reporting stigma exhibited maladaptive responses, such as greater use of avoidant coping strategies. Our findings suggest that the use of protective emotional labor is a common response to the ambiguous loss experienced in this stage. 11 As care partners navigate the transition from a traditional familial role to a care provider identity, they adopt “modified-self constructs” or “situational masks” to camouflage internal emotional volatility and protect the care recipient from the disclosure of distressing cognitions.7,9,13

While clinical research often focuses on the risks inherent in care partnering, the 60.7% of partners who did not report stigma offer a constructive perspective on protective factors. Resilience emerged as an independent predictor of membership in the non-stigmatized group, functioning as a psychological buffer that allows partners to maintain a positive identity despite a “latent” burden.37–39 These individuals also utilized significantly fewer maladaptive avoidant coping strategies, indicating that protective mechanisms involve active, adaptive engagement rather than a mere absence of distress. Clinical interventions should therefore prioritize the cultivation of these protective assets, specifically by strengthening resilience and leveraging social support networks, to assist care partners in dealing with cognitive decline without internalizing societal bias. 40

Finally, the gender disparities observed across this cohort constitute a primary finding. Female care partners reported significantly higher subjective burden, anxiety, and perceived stigma compared to their male counterparts. This pattern of elevated distress aligns with broader research on informal care for chronic illnesses and is corroborated by meta-analyses confirming that women consistently experience a higher global burden.18,41 The increased utilization of emotion-focused and avoidant coping strategies among women in our sample aligns with established literature suggesting a greater inclination toward internal emotional regulation over external problem-solving. 42 However, in the context of persistent stress, avoidant coping can become maladaptive, fostering a negative cycle of anxiety and hopelessness. Furthermore, the younger age of female care partners reinforces the concept of gendered labor, wherein societal expectations disproportionately shift care partnering responsibilities onto women earlier in life. 18

Our findings challenge the notion that MCI care partnering is a low-impact phase and advocate for the immediate integration of psychosocial screening for both burden and affiliate stigma upon initial diagnosis. Furthermore, support interventions must be gender-tailored. Programs targeting female care partners should prioritize stress reduction, cognitive restructuring to manage internalized stigma, and the promotion of a shift from avoidant coping toward effective problem-solving strategies. Conversely, interventions for male care partners may benefit from addressing social isolation and encouraging emotional expression. Finally, the robust correlation between stigma and maladaptive coping suggests that community-based psychoeducation aimed at destigmatizing MCI and dementia is a crucial public health measure to mitigate care partner distress.

This study is subject to several limitations inherent to its cross-sectional design, which preclude the inference of causality between gender, stigma, and psychological outcomes. Furthermore, the assessment of care partner distress relied entirely on self-report instruments rather than structured clinical interviews. While tools such as the HADS and BHS are widely utilized for screening, the lack of independent clinical validation for conditions like depression may introduce social desirability bias or lead to reporting variance, particularly across genders. Future research should incorporate gold-standard diagnostic assessments to further strengthen the validity of these psychological findings. The sample was drawn from specialized memory clinics in Spain, which may limit generalizability to community-dwelling care partners or those in different cultural settings. Further research should prioritize longitudinal studies to track how gender differences in burden and stigma evolve as the care recipient transitions from MCI to dementia. Intervention studies testing the efficacy of gender-specific coping skills training and anti-stigma campaigns are also warranted. Additionally, while we characterized care recipients by age, cognitive status, and disease duration, we did not formally assess psychiatric or medical comorbidities through clinical record reviews. The absence of these data may limit our ability to account for the impact of multi-morbidity on care partner burden. Similarly, while we measured perceived social support, we did not collect data on the formal care structures or professional services utilized by the dyads. Future research should include a more granular assessment of patient health status and formal support utilization to better contextualize these psychosocial outcomes.

Conclusion

Care partnering for individuals with MCI is characterized by substantial levels of burden and affiliate stigma, both of which are associated with diminished psychological well-being. The identified gender disparities, specifically that female care partners report disproportionately higher levels of burden, anxiety, and stigma, underscore a critical requirement for early, gender-sensitive clinical frameworks.

These findings necessitate several concrete clinical and policy shifts. Clinicians should move beyond traditional patient-centered models to implement mandatory psychosocial screening for care partners immediately upon an MCI diagnosis. Addressing these stressors during this early stage is essential to prevent the “invisible labor” of monitoring and neuropsychiatric management from escalating into emotional exhaustion. Furthermore, interventions must be gender-tailored: programs for women should prioritize cognitive restructuring and a transition from avoidant to problem-focused coping, while initiatives for men should target social isolation and encourage emotional expression to overcome gendered barriers to help-seeking.
