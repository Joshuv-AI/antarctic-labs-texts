Dengue epidemiology and molecular characterization of vector mosquitoes; Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus in Mizoram, Northeast India
Authors: Van Ramliana, Ralte Vanlalawmpuii, Gabriel Rosangkima, Lalfakzuala Pautu, Hun Ropuia, Nachimuthu S Kumar, Hmar Lalthanzara
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDengue is the most important arboviral disease, and Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus are the major vectors. It is one of the most recent emerging diseases in Mizoram state with a fast-growing burden over the years. The present study aimed to explore the epidemiological patterns of dengue in Mizoram, and to identify vector mosquito species.MethodsIn the present study, two years secondary data of dengue disease in Mizoram was studied. Dengue vector mosquitoes and dengue virus serotypes circulating in vector mosquitoes in Aizawl City were identified by sequence analysis of COX1 and C-prM genes respectively.ResultsIn Mizoram State, the annual case prevalence of dengue was found to be 300.45 per 100,000 persons. Seasonality of the disease was also noted, with the peak season occurring from July to December. There is a significant positive relationship between age and the disease prevalence (p < 0.05, r = 0.909). DENV was detected in 10 pools of Ae. aegypti with a minimum infection rate (MIR) of 18.05, and 8 pools of Ae. albopictus with a minimum infection rate of 16.03. The viral isolates were found to correspond to serotypes 2 (DENV2) and 3 (DENV3). A total of 6 pools of Aedes aegypti and 4 pools of Aedes albopictus tested positive with DENV2 serotype, while 4 pools of Aedes aegypti and 3 pools of Aedes albopictus tested positive with DENV3 serotype. DENV2 serotype was more common, and multiple serotypes (DENV2 and DENV3) were also detected in two pools from one locality.ConclusionsThere is an increasing trend in dengue infection in Mizoram, and seasonality of the disease was observed with the peak season during July to December. The higher incidence rate among the adults indicated that outdoor activities may be related to the high risk of the infection. However, lack of immunity among the older people may also be a possible reason of higher disease incidence rather than the activity pattern. Early identification and frequent monitoring of infected vector mosquitoes will yield an early warning indicator for forecasting dengue epidemics in the future.Keywords: Dengue, Epidemiology, Mizoram, Serotyping, Vector identification

Introduction

Dengue fever is brought on by the dengue virus (DENV) belonging to the Flaviviridae family and the Flavivirus genus. DENV comes in four distinct serotypes [1, 2]. It is one of the most common mosquito-borne diseases affecting humans [3]. Humans contract the virus when bitten by an infected Aedes vector mosquito [4]. There is a risk of DENV infection for about 4 billion people, and over 400 million infections happen each year [5]. The reproduction and diversity of Aedes vector mosquitoes are affected by ecological changes due to urbanization, industrialization, and deforestation [6]. Africa is the origin of Aedes aegypti, while Asia is the origin of Aedes albopictus. However, these two species are currently expanding in a large number of nations. Populations living within tropical and subtropical nations run the risk of contracting arboviral illnesses [7, 8]. The swift advancement of the economy, industry, and mass migration has led to a rise in arbovirus vectors [9]. Consequently, since over a million people worldwide are afflicted by emerging arbovirus infections each year, these illnesses pose a threat to public health [10].

The first dengue case identified in India was in the year 1921, and now it has become one of the most highly affected countries in the world with high mortality and morbidity rates. All four DENV serotypes (DENV1, 2, 3, and 4) were reported to circulate in the north-eastern states of India [6]. There are abundant Aedes mosquito populations in northeast India, indicating the ideal environmental conditions for mosquito vectors and the distribution of viral diseases [11]. In North-East India, dengue cases have been reported from Manipur, Arunachal Pradesh, Nagaland, Mizoram, and Assam, and the increase in frequency of cases has been observed [12, 13]. One of the eight states in northeast India, Mizoram borders Bangladesh on the west and Myanmar on the east. It is located in an area with humid tropical to subtropical weather. Records of the Integrated Disease Surveillance Programme (IDSP), Government of Mizoram, India (unpublished data) reported the occurrence of the first dengue case in Mizoram in 2012 and the first outbreak in 2016. Dengue infection cases in this state have increased rapidly with the annual incidence rate of approximately 300/100,000 persons in recent years. The previous report indicates the presence of DENV1, DENV2, and DENV3 serotypes detected in human sera in Mizoram State [13]. The impact of DENV infections is increasing, but little is known about the DENV serotypes that are circulating in vector mosquito populations in Mizoram, northeast India. Therefore, proactive surveillance of vector mosquitoes for circulating DENV serotypes and early pathogen detection will aid in the forecasting of DENV outbreaks and the development of novel dengue control strategies. Consequently, the current study was carried out in the Aizawl city region of Mizoram State, Northeast India, to identify dengue vector mosquito species using molecular marker (COX1) and morphological characteristics, as well as to detect and serotyping the DENV present in these field collected vector mosquitoes.

Materials and methods

Study area

Mizoram, one of the smallest states, is located in the north-eastern part of India. The state shares 722 km of international borders with Myanmar and Bangladesh. The northern and western parts share domestic borders with Assam, Tripura, and Manipur states. It is a tiny state with only 21,087 square kilometers of area extending from 21˚56ˈN-24˚31ˈN and 92˚16ˈE-93˚26ˈE. It receives an annual rainfall of 254 centimeters, and features a humid sub-tropical climate with 46% relative humidity [14].

Data sources

Secondary data of dengue disease in Mizoram for the years 2012 to 2023 was obtained from the Integrated Disease Surveillance Programme (IDSP), Government of Mizoram (unpublished data). Information of patients such as age, sex, address, and test performed, date of diagnosis, etc. was obtained. Data of rainfall during the study period is also obtained from published literature [15]. Trends in disease incidence were analyzed, and a map of dengue cases at the district level was also created. Case incidence was expressed as cases per 100,000 persons per year. Seasonality and the distribution of cases among sexes and different age groups were also analyzed.

Mosquito sample collection

From July to December 2023, adult mosquito collection was carried out in the Aizawl Municipal Area of Mizoram, India. Aizawl City is semi-urban, with a hot, muggy climate and varying seasons of precipitation. The majority of the epidemic sites were close to residential neighbourhoods and business complexes, mainly in the periphery and lower elevations of the city. Furthermore, the surrounding regions of the city were covered in bushes and medium to large trees. Thirty-eight sites in 10 localities with a high prevalence of dengue cases (Fig. 1) were selected for the sample collection, and a monthly collection was carried out for six months. The present study protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee (PUC-IAEC-2021-A07, Date: 28 June, 2021). Adult mosquitoes resting indoors and outdoors were collected using a flashlight, gravid trap, and mouth aspirators, starting in the early morning through late at night, from inside and the surroundings of human dwellings and animal sheds. In addition to flashlights and mouth aspirators, five traps were set up at each site covering the area of approximately 40 m in diameter.

A map of the Aizawl city area showing 38 sample collection sites (black colored circles) from 10 different localities

Morphological identification of mosquitoes was done using standard taxonomic keys [16]. The female mosquitoes were separated into a pool of approximately 15 individual mosquitoes per pool based on the collection site, and species. Specimens for DENV detections were preserved immediately in RNAlater, stored at 4ºC for 24 h, and then at -80oC until further processing. Some specimens from each mosquito species were also preserved in absolute ethanol and stored at -20oC for molecular identification.

DNA extraction

The genomic DNA of the mosquito was isolated from the legs of each single specimen using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany) following the protocol provided by the manufacturer. A minimum of 6 DNA extracts were prepared from each species of mosquito for molecular characterization. Since DNA extraction was done for molecular characterization purpose of mosquito species, extraction was not done for specimens from all the collection sites.

PCR amplification of the COX1 gene in mosquitoes

For the molecular characterization of mosquito species a segment of COX1 gene (∼708 bp) of mosquitoes was amplified using primer pairs, LCO1490 and HCO2198 [17] in a ProFlex PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Life Technologies). The reaction mix (20 µl) consisted of 10 µl of PCR Master Mix, 0.4 µl each primer, 1 µl of genomic DNA, and 8.2 µl of nuclease-free water. The PCR cycles consisted of one cycle at 94ºC for 7 min, 35 cycles at 94ºC for 30 s, 52ºC for 45 s, 72ºC for 45 s, and a final extension at 72ºC for 7 min. PCR products were stored at -40oC until further processing for sequencing.

Viral RNA isolation and cDNA synthesis

QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany) was used in this study. Briefly, the heads and thoraxes were separated from each pool of Aedes mosquitoes, and grounded with 560 µl of lysis buffer (AVL with carrier RNA) in a tissue homogenizer (TissueLyzer LT, QIAGEN) at 50 oscillations for 2 min. Homongenate was transferred into 1.5 ml centrifuge tube, and incubated for 10 min at room temperature. Absolute ethanol (560 µl) was added, and the mixture was put onto the QIAamp Mini Column and spun down the supernatant at 8000 rpm for one minute. Washing of the precipitated RNA was done by centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 1 min using wash buffer AW1, followed by centrifugation at 14,000 rpm for 3 min using wash buffer AW2. Finally, the RNA was eluted with 60 µl of elution buffer AVE by centrifugation and stored at -80oC until further processing. Using the QIAamp cDNA Synthesis Kit (Qiagen, Germany) and the specified protocol, cDNA synthesis was carried out from the RNA samples. The generated cDNA was kept at -80oC for later use in DENV detection and serotyping. Since Armigeres subalbatus was a well known non-vector of dengue viruses, DENV detection was not done with this species.

Real-time PCR detection of DENV

The SYBR Green-based real-time PCR detection of DENV was conducted in BioRad real-time PCR system (Bio-Rad, USA) using cDNA and DENV-specific primer pairs, DENV-1 F and DENV-2R [18]. The PCR cycling condition involved initial denaturation at 95ºC for 3 min, 45 cycles of 95ºC for 15 s, and 60ºC for 30 s, followed by a melt curve analysis. In each run of the real-time PCR, a no-template control and a positive control prepared from the C-prM gene product of DENV2 (104 copies) that had been amplified successfully previously were included. Minimum infection rate (MIR) was calculated by assuming that one positive pool of mosquitoes contains one infected mosquito, and was calculated as the ratio of the total number of positive pools to the number of tested mosquitoes, multiplied by 1000.

DENV serotyping by PCR amplification

The C-prM gene (511 bp) of DENV was amplified using a pair of primers (DN-F and DN-R) for sequence analysis. PCR-based serotyping was done by amplification of the C-prM gene using DENV common forward primer (DN-F) and type-specific reverse primers (D1-R, D2-R, D3-R, and D4-R) [19]. The cycling conditions of these two PCRs include one denaturation cycle at 95ºC for 3 min, 40 cycles of 95ºC for 45 s, 55ºC for 50 s, 72ºC for 1 min, and a final extension at 72ºC for 7 min. DENV serotypes were identified by visualization of the products by agarose gel electrophoresis. Details of the primers used are presented in Table 1.

DNA sequencing and phylogenetic analysis

PCR products of the COX1 gene of mosquitoes and the C-prM gene of DENV were sequenced by Sanger’s dideoxy method on an ABI 3730XL automated sequencer (AgriGenome Labs Pvt. Ltd., SmartCity Kochi, Kerala, India) [20]. Sequences were edited and aligned using BioEdit Sequence Alignment Editor, ver. 7.0.5.3, and MEGA software (MEGA 7.0.26). To determine the identity of the sequences, blast search analysis was done using the NCBI’s GenBank nucleotide database. The phylogenetic dendrograms of genes in the present study and those obtained from the GenBank database were generated using the Maximum Likelihood method and Kimura 2-parameter model in MEGA 7.0.26 software, and were statistically supported by bootstrapping with 1000 replicates. Nucleotide sequences obtained were also deposited in the GenBank data library.

Statistical analysis

Seasonality of disease prevalence was assessed during the study period. Origin Pro 8 software (OriginLab Corp., Northampton, MA, USA) was used for descriptive analysis.

Results

Dengue epidemiology in Mizoram

Secondary data of dengue disease obtained from the Integrated Disease Surveillance Programme, Department of Health & Family Welfare, Government of Mizoram was analysed in this study. Patient’s sample reactive for rapid NS1 antigen, ELISA or rapid IgM antibody test was considered as dengue positive. In Mizoram, the first dengue case was documented in 2012, and there were six cases overall. Between 2012 and 2021, a total of 1140 dengue cases were recorded, and the average annual disease prevalence was 10.44 cases per 100,000 persons, with a range of 0.55 to 53.16 cases per 100,000 people (Table 2). However, due to the low incidence rate and uneven monthly distribution, data of disease prevalence during the first 10 years (2012 to 2021) is inappropriate for inclusion in the analysis of disease seasonality. During the year 2022 and 2023 (data pooled), there are 6556 dengue positive cases. The disease showed notable seasonality in the state with fewer cases during the months of January to June, while October marked the peak of cases (Fig. 2A). A positive relationship between age and the disease prevalence was noted (p < 0.05, r = 0.909) with the highest affected age group of 60–69 years at 456.33 per 100,000 persons followed by 70 and above years at 407.46 per 100,000 persons (Fig. 2B). Out of the 11 districts of Mizoram state, Aizawl district exhibited significantly higher annual prevalence of the disease, 689.01 cases per 100,000 persons followed by Lunglei district with 105.93 per 100,000 persons (Fig. 3). All other districts falls under 100 cases per 100,000 persons with the least cases observed in Khawzawl district (24.79/100,000 person). There is no correlation between the prevalence of the disease and district-level rainfall (data not shown). During the year 2012 to 2023, maximum rainfall recorded in the state was during the month of June to September (Fig. 2C). The disease affected slightly higher among females (51%) when compared to males (49%). Overall, the annual case prevalence in the state during the last two years (2022 and 2023) was estimated to be 300.45 per 100,000 persons (Table 2).

Identified mosquito species

A total of 1715 adult female mosquitoes were collected from 10 different localities in the Aizawl municipal area. Mosquito species were identified morphologically and confirmed by the molecular method targeting the COX1 gene. BLAST (basic local alignment search tool) analysis confirmed the present mosquito samples as Aedes aegypti, Aedes albopictus, and Armigeres subalbatus, with > 99% similarity to GenBank sequences. The PCR products of COX1 gene from each species were sequenced, edited, and then submitted to the GenBank under accession numbers PP474568, PP474569, PP152342, PP152344, PP152348, and PP152350. Armigeres subalbatus was the most common species (662) collected in the study area followed by Aedes aegypti (554) and Aedes albopictus (449). The phylogenetic analysis indicate that Aedes aegypti (AA9, AA10) collected from different sites of Aizawl city show more close relationship to the Aedes aegypti located in Viet Nam, Malaysia, Singapore, and China than in the central part of India (Fig. 4). Aedes albopictus (M9, M11) of the present study also show close relationship to the Aedes albopictus located in Viet Nam, Malaysia, Singapore, China, Thailand, and Indonesia while one of the specimen (M2) in the present study show more close relationship to the Aedes albopictus located in central India.

Serotypes of dengue virus

Female mosquitoes of each species from each collection site were pooled, and a total of 1053 female Aedes mosquitoes in 74 pools (Ae. aegypti = 39, Ae. albopictus = 35) were tested for DENV positivity. Thirty-nine pools of Ae. aegypti consisted of 554 mosquitoes, while thirty-five pools of Ae. albopictus consisted of 499 mosquitoes. DENV was detected in 10 pools of Ae. aegypti with a sample positivity rate of 25.64% (10/39) and minimum infection rate (MIR) of 18.05, and 8 pools of Ae. albopictus with a sample positivity rate of 22.85% (8/35) and MIR of 16.03. The amplification curve and product melt peaks are also shown in Fig. 5 (A and B). Of the 74 pools of Aedes species assayed, 10 pools (13.5%, 10/74) were positive for DENV2, and 6 pools (8.1%, 6/74) were positive for DENV3. It was also observed that 6 out of 10 Aedes aegypti pools were tested positive with DENV2 serotype, and 3 pools of Aedes aegypti tested positive with DENV3 serotype. Of the eight Aedes albopictus positive pools, 4 pools tested positive for DENV2 and 3 pools tested positive for DENV3 serotype. Co-infection or multiple serotypes (DENV2/DENV3) was detected in one pool each of Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus collected from one locality. The occurrence of DENV serotypes in the pools shows that DENV2 was the more commonly occurring serotype circulating in the Aizawl City area as compared to DENV3. The specificity of primers used in this study (DENV-1 F/DENV-2R) has been confirmed earlier by testing 126 different unique virus isolates, and no cross-reactivity was detected [18].

Phylogenetic analysis of dengue viruses

DENV2 and DENV3 serotypes in the present study area phylogenetically analyzed using partial C-prM gene sequences of the virus. Phylogenetic inference based on the C-prM gene sequence of DENV revealed that some of the DENV samples (DNV-1, DNV-3, DNV-4, and DNV-5) belonged to DENV serotype 2 (DENV2), while others (DNV-6, DNV-7, DNV-8, and DNV-9) belonged to DENV serotype 3 (DENV3) (Fig. 6). Notably, DENV2 serotype from the present study area formed clades with those of India, USA, China, and Bangladesh, while DENV3 of the present study clustered together with that of India, Portugal, Italy, and USA, indicating diverse global distributions of DENV2 and DENV3 serotypes. Sequences of DENV serotype 2 and 3 obtained in the present study were deposited at the NCBI database with the accession numbers as given in the figure (Fig. 6).

Discussion

In Mizoram state, 6 cases of dengue were reported for the first time in the year 2012, and the cases increased to 580 in 2016 [21]. The present epidemiological study was done using secondary data of 12 years (2012 to 2023). Based on the previous report [21] and the present findings, an increasing trend of the disease incidence was observed in Mizoram during the last 12 years. However, a reliable disease prevalence data in Mizoram state was available only on 2022 onwards. Therefore, based on the two years data (2022 and 2023), the annual cases of 300.45/100,000 persons was observed. In view of the increasing trends of the disease prevalence during the last 12 years, Mizoram state is likely to be at higher risk of dengue disease in the future. In tropical and sub-tropical areas, dengue circulates throughout the year with different peak times at different geographical areas. In endemic areas, the distribution and peak time of the cases are highly correlated with the physiographic conditions. In the present study area, the peak season is from July to December, which is similar to the cases reported in Malaysia, Vietnam, Puerto Rico, China [22] and across India [23]. In the present study area, the rainy season (June – October) followed by the DENV peak transmission (July – December) suggested the association of dengue case distribution with rainfall in Mizoram. Since dengue disease is considered as ‘urban disease’, the result of present study also showed significantly higher incidence rate of the disease in Aizawl city. Therefore, the relationship between district-wise rainfall and the disease prevalence is unclear. The disease has been reported to affect all age groups, with a higher infection rate in younger adults and children [22]. The results of the present study showed distribution of dengue cases in all age groups, with significantly higher distribution among older adults, which is in contrast with the findings in endemic areas. In endemic areas, the accumulation of population immunity in adults is one of the factors leading to the higher incidence rate among children [24]. Since the first dengue cases in Mizoram were identified only a few years ago, population immunity may not have developed in adults. This could lead to the susceptibility of all the populations to dengue virus infection. In the present study area, more outdoor activities in adults resulting in a higher chance of exposure to infected mosquitoes as well as weak immunity among the older age groups may play a role in the higher incidence rate in adults. Further investigation is needed to find the mechanism of the age shift to adults.

Dengue has become one of the most serious arboviral infections in Asian countries. Urbanization, environmental changes, and human long-distance movement resulted in the spread of vector mosquito species [25]. In India, the first dengue outbreak was recorded in 1963 [26], and Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus were identified as vectors along with all four DENV serotypes [27]. During the last decade, DENV serotypes actively circulated in different regions of India with varying prevalence [28, 29]. However, the serotypes and distribution of DENV in vector mosquitoes from Aizawl City remain unknown. There are four antigenically different serotypes of dengue viruses. Any of these infections often result in a moderate, self-limiting feverish sickness. However, infection with another serotype results in a more severe form of the disease that causes vascular and hemostatic problems (dengue hemorrhagic fever-dengue shock syndrome), which has a high fatality rate. Serotyping is required in order to preserve correct epidemiologic data and to undertake appropriate preventative, treatment, and control actions. According to the current study’s phylogenetic analysis based on the C-prM gene, the DNV-1, DNV-3, DNV-4, and DNV-5 were closely related to the serotype (DENV2) that were isolated in India, China, USA, and Bangladesh, while DNV-6, DNV-7, DNV-8, and DNV-9 were closely related to the serotype (DENV3) isolated in India, USA, Italy, and Portugal. The circulation of DENV2 and DENV3 serotypes in Ae. albopictus and Ae. aegypti collected from various localities of Aizawl City was reported for the first time. In the present study, dengue virus infection in wild-caught mosquito species in Aizawl City was assessed, and C-prM gene sequences of the circulating DENV serotypes were compared with available sequences from the NCBI GenBank data library. Eighteen of the 74 Aedes pools that were analyzed showed the presence of DENV. Additionally, it was discovered that there were 6 pools of female Ae. aegypti positive for DENV2 and 3 pools for DENV3. Four pools of Ae. albopictus were positive for DENV2, while 3 pools tested positive for DENV3. One pool each of the two species showed co-infection with DENV2 and DENV3 serotypes. However, since detection and serotyping was done using the cDNA synthesized from mosquitoes in each pool, multiple serotypes detected in the present study could be due to the presence of different individual mosquitoes infected with different DENV serotypes in the same pool. The majority of past outbreaks in India were reported to be associated with the DENV2 serotype [30–33], and the present study also indicated the presence of DENV2 more commonly than the DENV3 serotype in vector Aedes mosquitoes in Aizawl city, Mizoram.

The current study’s phylogenetic analysis based on the COX1 gene revealed that the specimen AA9 and AA10 were closely related to the Aedes aegypti that were identified in India, China, Viet Nam, Singapore, and Malaysia, while the specimen M9, and M11 were closely related to the Aedes albopictus identified in Indonesia, Singapore, Thailand, Viet Nam, Malaysia, and China. Ae. aegypti has been reported to be the most common vector species in both rural and urban regions [34]. The location of the breeding site is crucial to giving Aedes mosquitoes a spot to lay their eggs. The variety of breeding habitats affects both the quantity and kind of Aedes species found in a given area. Aedes albopictus prefers natural containers with more organic material, but Ae. aegypti prefers clean water [35]. The present study area is dominated by three species of mosquitoes with Aedes mosquitoes more commonly available when compared to Armigeres mosquitoes. Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus are among the most invasive species of mosquitoes worldwide [36]. They thrive in urban areas which provide them with favourable breeding sites [37]. Therefore, their distribution is largely driven by human activities. Since Aizawl is Mizoram’s largest city; the region is densely populated with a variety of residential and commercial structures that may add to the Aedes population under investigation. On the other hand, the Aizawl area’s surrounding vegetation, which includes taller trees and bushes, is a typical landscape element. In Aizawl, the majority of homes and structures are surrounded by attractive plants, trees, and shrubs that provide shade. These environmental factors observed in the urban area of Aizawl city provides favourable conditions for Aedes vector mosquitoes, and this seems to be the reason why Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus are more commonly found in this study area. Because Aedes mosquitoes prefer shady areas, vegetation serves as a home, a place to rest, and a source of food [38]. Additionally, temperature, wind, and relative humidity all had an impact on the prevalence and density of the Aedes species [39]. Therefore, a mix of vegetation, climate, and blood supplies may affect mosquito dispersion, which in turn may affect arboviral infection [40].

Limitations of the study

In this study, due to the limited number of cases during the first 10 years (2012–2021) most of the epidemiological analysis was done using two years data (2022–2023). Vector mosquito identification and DENV serotyping were also conducted only in the city of Aizawl. Since only the C-prM partial gene sequences were examined in this investigation, genotyping of DENV serotypes was not carried out.

Conclusions

Our study showed an increasing trend in dengue infection in Mizoram with the annual cases of 300.45/100,000 persons during 2022 and 2023. Seasonality of dengue infection was also observed with the peak season during July to December, and the older adults with more outdoor activities (and possibly with weaker immunity) were the most affected age group in Mizoram. Our study, for the first time confirms the prevalence of DENV2 and DENV3 serotypes in field collected Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus in Aizawl City, Mizoram State, India. This finding indicates the possibility of a dengue outbreak in the future. Multiple serotypes of DENV circulating in the city suggested the possibility of being hyper-endemic for dengue. Monitoring the circulating DENV in mosquitoes is important in predicting the risk of a dengue outbreak, which is crucial in identifying areas requiring early implementation of prevention and control measures. Detection and serotyping of DENV in both vector mosquitoes and human samples are crucial for the epidemiological and clinical management of dengue cases. Since there is no effective vaccine to date personal protection from mosquito bites and vector control are still reliable dengue management strategies.
