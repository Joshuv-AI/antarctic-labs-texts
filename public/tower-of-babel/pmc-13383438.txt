S100A9 lactylation facilitated sepsis-related acute respiratory distress syndrome via promoting transcription activation of IL-1β in macrophages
Authors: Xiangtao Zheng, Wen Xu, Fangchen Gong, Shunwei Huang, Xiaofeng Wang, Shanzhi Zhao, Yu Han, Huan Liu, Lei Pei, Dan Xu, Zhitao Yang, Enqiang Mao, Ranran Li, Ying Chen, Erzhen Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSepsis is a severe inflammatory condition often complicated by acute lung injury (ALI) with limited therapeutic options. S100 Calcium Binding Protein A9 (S100A9) as an alarmin is highly elevated in sepsis. We observed that S100A9 was lactylated in the lung tissues of septic mice, the role of which in regulating sepsis-related ALI remains unknown.MethodsS100A9 lactylation sites were identified in septic patients and CLP mice using immunoprecipitation and mass spectrometry. Mechanistic studies employed mutagenesis, co-immunoprecipitation, and luciferase assays.ResultsIn this study, we confirmed that S100A9 was lactylated at K4 and K94 in septic patients. Lactylated S100A9 promoted its nuclear translocation, thereby enhancing its interaction with transcription factor CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein beta (Cebpb). The complex of S100A9 and Cebpb further promoted the transcriptional activation of downstream interleukin 1 beta (IL-1β), leading to sepsis-related ALI. Moreover, the knockout of S100A9 effectively alleviated sepsis-induced inflammatory response and lung injury.ConclusionsOur findings elucidated the importance of S100A9 lactylation in regulating inflammatory responses of macrophages in sepsis-induced ALI, providing novel insights into the pathophysiology of sepsis and potential therapeutic targets for sepsis-associated organ dysfunction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-026-02939-4.Keywords: S100A9, Lactylation, Macrophage, Sepsis, Lung injury

Introduction

Sepsis is a life-threatening condition characterized by dysregulated host immune responses to infection, resulting in multi-organ dysfunction [1]. Acute lung injury is one of the most common complications in sepsis, the occurrence of which leads to higher mortality rates of sepsis despite of the advanced medical care [2, 3]. The dysregulated immune response are the hallmarks of sepsis-induced lung injury [4]. Macrophages as the important innate immune cells in lung tissues are responsible for the initiation and resolution of lung immune responses [5–7]. The early phase of hyperinflammation in sepsis is primarily driven by pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β released by activated macrophages [8]. Therefore, the overwhelming inflammatory responses of macrophages is considered as important therapeutic target for treating sepsis-related lung injury.

Immunometabolism favoring glycolysis plays a pivotal role in regulating macrophage functions [9]. Intervention of glycolysis has been demonstrated to alleviate sepsis-induced inflammation and organ damage [10, 11]. In recent years, lactate, as the end-product of glycolysis, has been demonstrated to participate in modulating cellular functions via protein lactylation. Lactylation of histone proteins has been reported to play essential roles in various diseases such as lung fibrosis, tumor progression, and sepsis. In subsequent research, lactylation modification has been shown to participate in crucial pathophysiological processes such as macrophage polarization, release of inflammatory factors, metabolic reprogramming, and regulation of tumor proliferation and metastasis [12–14], thereby revealing an important mechanism for the biological functions of lactylation.

S100 Calcium Binding Protein A9 (S100A9) is an alarmin serving as a promising diagnostic marker and therapeutic target in sepsis and sepsis-associated organ damage [15]. S100A9 is expressed predominantly in myeloid cells, including activated macrophages, neutrophils, monocytes, dendritic cells (DCs), and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) [16–18]. S100 proteins regulate various cellular processes, such as apoptosis, proliferation, differentiation, migration and invasion [19]. Studies have shown that blocking S100A9 suppressed the systemic inflammatory response in sepsis and improved endotoxemia-induced mitochondrial respiratory suppression in the heart, thereby alleviating cardiac damage [20]. We observed in integrative lactylome and proteome analyses that S100A9 was highly lactylated in macrophages from lung tissues of septic mice. However, the regulatory role of S100A9 lactylation in sepsis-related lung injury remains unclear.

In this study, we demonstrated that lactylation-dependent nuclear translocation of S100A9 enhanced its interaction with the transcription factor Cebpb, leading to the transcriptional activation of the inflammatory gene IL-1β, thereby contributing to sepsis-induced ALI. Our findings revealed the important role of S100A9 lactylation in facilitating sepsis-induced inflammatory responses of macrophages and ALI, providing novel therapeutic strategies for treating sepsis.

Materials and methods

Patient enrolment

The study included patients diagnosed with sepsis at Shanghai Ruijin Hospital’s emergency department from May 31, 2021, to October 31, 2021. Ethical approval was obtained from the hospital’s Ethics Committee under reference number 20,210,101, and all procedures adhered to institutional guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. Inclusion criteria were as follows: age between 18 and 90 years; diagnosis based on Sepsis 3.0 criteria; and hospital stay exceeding 24 h. Patients were excluded if they met any of these conditions: discharge or death within 24 h of admission, participation in other clinical studies, emergency surgery post-admission, malignant tumors, pregnancy or lactation, or missing critical clinical data. Ultimately, the study enrolled a total of 20 healthy volunteers and 38 patients with sepsis. The baseline characteristics of these groups have been previously reported [21].

Animals

In this study, male C57BL/6J mice aged 6–8 weeks and weighing 20–30 g were randomly divided into experimental and control groups. Additionally, S100A9 global knockout mice on a C57BL/6J background were included to facilitate specific investigations. These knockout mice, generated by Cyagen Biosciences, were constructed using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene targeting to completely ablate the S100A9 gene. The mices were housed under controlled conditions, maintained on a standard laboratory diet, with specific attention to temperature and light-dark cycles. All animal procedures were approved by the Shanghai Jiaotong University School of Medicine Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) (protocol JUMC2023-166-B) and strictly followed the International Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (National Academy of Sciences Health Publication No. 85 − 23, revised in 1996).

For the experiment, Inhalation of isoflurane was used to anesthetize the mice for the experimental procedure. Sepsis was induced through cecal ligation and puncture (CLP). After achieving adequate anesthesia, the abdominal fur of each mouse was shaved and the surgical site sterilized with 70% ethanol. A midline incision of 0.5 cm was made to expose the cecum, which was ligated below the ileocecal valve. Two punctures were performed on the cecum using an 18-G needle, expelling a small amount of fecal material. The cecum was carefully repositioned within the abdominal cavity. Post-surgery, each mouse received subcutaneous administration of 50 mL/kg physiological saline (0.9%). The mice were allowed to recover post-surgery and were sacrificed at predetermined time points for further analysis.

Isolation and culture of BMDMs

BMDMs were isolated from the femurs and tibias of C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) as previously described [22]. Briefly, mice were euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation followed by cervical dislocation. The femurs and tibias were excised, and the bone marrow was flushed out using sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS). The collected bone marrow cells were filtered through a 70-µm cell strainer to remove debris and erythrocytes were lysed using a hypotonic lysis buffer. The remaining cells were washed twice with PBS and resuspended in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS, 100 ng/mL macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin. The cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. After 7 days of culture, adherent cells were harvested for research. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the institutional animal care and use committee (IACUC) guidelines.

LC‒MS/MS analysis

Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis was performed at PTM Biolabs. Peptide samples were prepared by dissolving trypsic peptides in solvent A, which consisted of 0.1% formic acid, 2% acetonitrile, and water. The samples were then loaded onto a custom-made analytical column (25 cm length, 100 μm inner diameter) designed for reverse-phase separation. The peptide separation process involved a gradient elution program: starting at 7% solvent B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile) and increasing to 24% over 42 min, followed by a further increase to 32% over the next 12 min. The gradient was then rapidly raised to 80% within 3 min and maintained at this level for an additional 3 min. The entire process was conducted using a nanoElute UHPLC system (Bruker Daltonics) with a flow rate of 450 nL/min.

The ionization and detection of peptides were carried out on a timsTOF Pro mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics) using electrospray ionization at 1.75 kV. Both precursor and fragment ions were recorded on the TOF detector, with a mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) range of 100 to 1700. The instrument operated in parallel accumulation and serial fragmentation (PASEF) mode, performing 10 PASEF-MS/MS scans per cycle. A dynamic exclusion time of 30 s was applied to optimize data acquisition.

CUT&Tag assay

The CUT&Tag assay was performed by Oebiotech Ltd. (Shanghai, China). CUT&Tag assay was performed using HyperactiveTM In-Situ ChIP Library Prep Kit for Illumina (cat.no.TD901-TD902, Vazyme Biotech, China) according to manufacturer’s instruction [23]. Briefly, prepared concanavalin A-coated magnetic beads (ConA beads) were added to resuspended cells and incubated at room temperature to bound cells. Non-ionic detergent Digitonin was used to permeate cell membrane. Then, primary anti-S100A9 antibody (CST, #73425), secondary antibody and the Hyperactive pA-Tn5 Transposase were incubated with the cells that were bounded by ConA beads in order. Therefore, the Hyperactive pA-Tn5 Transposase can exactly cut off the DNA fragments that were bound with target protein. Inaddition, the cut DNA fragments can be ligated with P5 and P7 adaptors by Tn5 transposase and the libraries were amplified by PCR with the P5 and P7 primers. The purified PCR products were evaluated using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Finally, these libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq6000 platform and 150 bp paired-end reads were generated for the following analysis.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

Lung specimens were first subjected to antigen retrieval by microwave heating at 98 °C for 10 min following paraffin removal. Background reduction was achieved through blocking with a solution of 10% normal goat serum at room temperature for 60 min. For the subsequent antibody incubation, the respective primary antibodies (as detailed in the experimental section) were applied and allowed to react overnight under 4 °C conditions. Detection involved the use of Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies from Servicebio, which were incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Nuclei visualization was accomplished using DAPI staining for 5 min. Final preparation included mounting with Sigma’s mounting medium and applying diaminobenzidine-based chromogenic reaction to enhance visibility under microscopy (Nikon DS-U3).

In parallel, the BMDMs and Raw264.7 assay utilized a slightly modified protocol. The cells were treated with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 15 min to achieve fixation. Membrane permeabilization was performed using 0.1% Triton X-100 solution for 20 min. To reduce background signals, the samples were incubated in a blocking buffer containing 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at room temperature for 30 min. Following this, the cells underwent primary antibody incubation overnight under refrigerated conditions (4 °C). The detection phase involved fluorescein-conjugated secondary antibodies, which were allowed to bind for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei counterstaining and fluorescence enhancement were achieved using Antifade Mounting Medium with DAPI. The resultant slides were imaged using a Nikon DS-U3 microscope.

Western blot analysis

Proteins (10 µg) isolated from lung tissues or cultured cells were fractionated via 12.5% SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis and transferred to PVDF membranes (Bio-rad). The membranes were then incubated with blocking solution containing 5% nonfat dry milk at room temperature for 60 min. Following this, the membranes were treated with specific primary antibodies (as detailed in the Methods section) overnight under 4 °C conditions. After thorough washing to remove unbound antibodies, secondary antibodies conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (HRP) (diluted at 1:10,000) were added for 60-minute incubation at 37 °C. Finally, the membranes were washed again and subjected to chemiluminescent detection using an ECL substrate (Tanon, China). Protein bands were visualized and recorded using a Bio-Rad imaging system.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Serum IL-6, IL-1β, S100A9, lactate were measured using ELISA kits (MultiSciences Biotechnology, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistics

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation unless otherwise specified. Findings were derived from a minimum of three independent experiments. The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was confirmed with the Brown-Forsythe test. For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired two-tailed t-test was employed; for multiple group comparisons, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni post hoc testing was utilized. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9 and FlowJo software tools. A p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant in all cases.

Results

Lactate promoted S100A9 lactylation in sepsis-induced lung injury

In clinical samples, we found that the levels of glycolysis-derived lactate were significantly elevated in septic patients compared to healthy controls, which were even higher in septic patients with lung injury (Fig. 1a). A similar trend was observed for plasma pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and IL-1β (Fig. S1a, S1b). In addition, lactate levels were positively correlated with both SOFA score and APACHE II score (Fig. 1b, c), while negatively correlated with the oxygenation index (Fig. 1d). Additionally, in CLP mice, inhibition of lactate production by 2-deoxy-D-glucose and oxamate (2DG and OX) markedly alleviated lung tissue injury (Fig. S1c). Western blot analysis showed that 2DG and OX markedly reduced the pan lactylation levels in septic lung tissues (Fig. S1d). Furthermore, we found that the pan lactylation levels were confirmed to be significantly higher in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) isolated from septic patients compared to that isolated from healthy controls, which were even higher in septic patients with lung injury (Fig. 1e, Fig. S1e). In the integrative lactylome and proteome data of lung tissues from septic mice (Fig. 1f), Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis showed that lactylated proteins were predominantly enriched in signaling pathways related to inflammation, immune responses, and cytokine production (Fig. s1f). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis showed that lactylated proteins were significantly enriched in cytokine interactions and inflammatory pathways such as NF-κB pathways (Fig. S1g). The radar chart of the proteomics data revealed that the lactylation levels of S100A9 at lysine 9 and 94 (K4 and K94) were the highest among lactylated non-histone modified proteins (Fig. 1g). These findings indicated that lactylation of S100A9 may play a significant role in promoting cytokines production.

S100A9 lactylation was observed in macrophages in septic lung tissues

Immunofluorescent staining of septic lung tissue identified the lactylated levels of S100A9 were significantly higher in macrophages (F4/80+) compared to neutrophils (LY6G+) and endothelial cells (CD31+) as indicated by the increased colocalization of S100A9, Kla, and F4/80 in septic lung tissue of mice (Fig. S2a) Moreover, data from the Human Protein Atlas (https://www.proteinatlas.org/) showed that S100A9 was predominantly expressed in macrophages in human lung tissue (Fig. S2b). Upon the knockout of S100A9, LPS-induced expression and secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and IL-1β in BMDMs were significantly reduced (Fig. 2a-d), indicating the important role of S100A9 in promoting macrophage inflammatory response during sepsis. These data indicate that S100A9 may aggravate sepsis-related lung injury via promoting inflammatory response of macrophages.

S100A9 lactylation mainly occurs in macrophages of septic lung tissues. a-b ELISA analysis of macrophage supernatants showed elevated IL-6 and IL-1β levels after LPS stimulation (100ng/ml), which were reduced in S100A9 KO cells. c-d qPCR analysis of lung tissue showed increased IL-6 and IL-1β mRNA levels in WT sepsis mice, which were reduced in S100A9 KO mice. e Immunofluorescence analysis of lung tissue showed increased S100A9 lactylation in macrophages (F4/80+) from sepsis mice compared to sham controls. f Immunoprecipitation (IP) confirmed S100A9 lactylation in LPS-stimulated BMDMs (100ng/ml). g HEK-293T cells overexpressing S100A9 exhibited enhanced lactylation after LPS (1 µg/ml) and exogenous lactate (10 mM) stimulation. h-i Lactylation proteomics and mass spectrometry identified S100A9 lactylation at K4 and K94. j Overexpression of S100A9 mutants (K4R, K94R) in HEK-293T cells significantly reduced lactylation compared to wild-type S100A9, confirming the role of K4 and K94 in lactylation modification

To investigate the lactylation modification of S100A9 in lung tissues, immunofluorescence staining was performed on lung samples from both healthy and septic mice. The results revealed a significant increase in the co-localization of S100A9 with Kla and macrophage markers in the lung tissues of septic mice (Fig. 2e), suggesting marked lactylation modification of S100A9 in septic lungs, which likely occurs primarily in macrophages within the lung tissue. To confirm sepsis-induced S100A9 lactylation in macrophages, the immunoprecipitation (IP) was conducted in BMDMs and PBMCs, showing that the levels of S100A9 lactylation were significantly increased upon LPS stimuli (Fig. 2f, Fig. S2c). Moreover, we confirmed S100A9 lactylation in PBMCs from septic patients, with markedly higher levels observed in those who developed sepsis‑induced ARDS (Fig. s1h). Additionally, In HEK-293T cells with S100A9 overexpression, the lactylation levels were markedly increased upon LPS and exogenous lactate stimulation (Fig. 2g, Fig. S2d). These findings revealed that S100A9 lactylation is highly induced in macrophages under septic conditions.

In the lactylation proteomics and mass spectrometry analysis, we identified two key lactylated lysine residues (K4 and K94) on S100A9 (Fig. 2h and i). To determine which lysine residue was responsible for S100A9 lactylation, wild-type S100A9 and its mutants (K4R and K94R) were overexpressed respectively in HEK-293T cells before LPS or lactate stimulation. The results showed that the lactylation levels were significantly reduced after mutation of K4 and K94 respectively, suggesting that both residues were important sites for S100A9 lactylation (Fig. 2j). To further confirm these findings, we established S100A9 point mutation of both K4R and K94R in the Raw264.7 cell line. and verified the successful overexpression of S100A9 without lactylation through Western blot (WB), qPCR and immunofluorescent (Fig. S2e-g). Particularly, Immunofluorescent staining data revealed a significant reduction in S100A9 lactylation in Raw264.7 with S100A9 point mutation of both K4R and K94R (Fig. S2g). In summary, these data confirmed that both K4 and K94 in S100A9 were lactylated in macrophages during sepsis.

S100A9 lactylation promoted its nuclear accumulation in macrophages

Through structural analysis of S100A9, we identified a nuclear localization sequence (NLS) in S100A9 (Fig. 3a). To assess whether S100A9 can translocate into the nucleus during sepsis, we stimulated BMDMs with LPS. The results showed that the levels of S100A9 were increased both in the nucleus and cytoplasm upon LPS stimuli (Fig. 3b). Immunofluorescent staining showed that LPS stimulation significantly increased the levels of S100A9 both in the nucleus and cytoplasm (Fig. 3c). To investigate whether lactate level affects S100A9 nuclear accumulation, BMDMs were pretreated with 2DG before LPS stimulation to inhibit the production of lactate. Western blot analysis showed that 2DG significantly reduced the levels of S100A9 both in the nucleus and cytoplasm (Fig. 3d). Immunofluorescent images confirmed the decreased S100A9 both in the nucleus and cytoplasm upon the inhibition of lactate production, whereas exogenous lactate enhanced LPS-induced upregulation of S100A9 both in the nucleus and cytoplasm in BMDMs (Fig. 3e, Fig. S3b). To examine whether lactylation modification modulates S100A9 nuclear accumulation, we analyzed nuclear fractions of Raw264.7 mutant cells with or without impaired S100A9 lactylation. The results showed that lactylated S100A9 in the nucleus was significantly reduced upon S100A9 lactylation impairment (Fig. 3f, Fig. S3a). In addition, the level of lactylated S100A9 was markedly decreased in nucleus of Raw264.7 mutant cells (Fig. 3g, Fig. S3c). These findings suggest that lactate accumulation promoted S100A9 expression and lactylation in sepsis, which lead to increased nuclear retention of S100A9.

Nuclear S100A9 lactylation enhanced its interaction with transcription factor Cebpb

To explore the function of S100A9 lactylation in the nucleus, Cut & Tag analysis was performed on LPS-stimulated BMDMs. We observed that LPS stimulation significantly increased the interaction of S100A9 with the promoter regions compared to the control group (Fig. 4a). Sequencing of the interacting regions revealed that approximately 56% were located within the promoters, suggesting that S100A9 may combine with gene promoters to regulate transcription (Fig. 4b). Moreover, we analyzed its protein sequence and identified the absence of a domain capable of directly binding to DNA promoter regions, suggesting that S100A9 cannot directly regulate gene transcription. Based on this, we proposed that S100A9 might interact with transcription factors in the nucleus to regulate gene transcription. Proteomic analysis was performed on S100A9 point-mutant Raw264.7 cell line (previously constructed, Fig. 4c) and BMDMs. Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to assess sample quality (Fig. 4d). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that proteins interacting with S100A9 were enriched in DNA-binding functions (Fig. S3d). Additionally, biological process analysis indicated that these proteins were primarily involved in transcriptional regulation and immune responses (Fig. S3e). These data suggested that S100A9 may regulate inflammatory gene transcription through interaction with related transcription factors.

Lactylation of S100A9 promotes interaction between S100A9 and Cebpb. a Cut & Tag analysis reveals increased S100A9 binding at promoter regions in LPS-stimulated BMDM cells compared to unstimulated controls. b Distribution of S100A9 binding regions, with 56% localized to gene promoters, indicative of transcriptional regulatory activity. c Mass spectrometry identified nuclear proteins interacting with S100A9 in LPS-stimulated BMDMs (100ng/ml). d Principal component analysis (PCA) of proteomics data confirmed sample quality. e Upset plot analysis identified overlapping interacting proteins between mass spectrometry and proteomics results, highlighting Cebpb and Zeb2 as key transcription factors. f Immunoprecipitation confirmed increased interaction between S100A9 and Cebpb in LPS-stimulated BMDMs (100ng/ml). g Immunoprecipitation confirmed that overexpression of S100A9 and Cebpb in HEK-293T cells validated their interaction. h IP analysis revealed that lactylation-inhibiting mutations (K4R, K94R) significantly reduced the S100A9-Cebpb interaction in HEK-293T cells

Based on the mass spectrometry and proteomics screening data, intersection analysis using the upset plot showed significant overlaps between the potential binding proteins. Among others, Cebpb and zinc finger E-box binding homeobox 2 (Zeb2) were identified as the primary transcription factors interacting with S100A9 (Fig. 4e). To verify their interactions, we performed both endogenous and exogenous immunoprecipitation experiments in BMDMs. We observed the interaction between S100A9 and Zeb2 (Fig. S3f). However, downstream target gene regulated by both of Zeb2 and S100A9 was absent (Fig. S3g). As for Cebpb, increased interaction between S100A9 and Cebpb was observed upon LPS stimulation (Fig. 4f). Overexpression of both S100A9 and Cebpb in HEK-293T cells further confirmed their interaction (Fig. 4g). To investigate whether lactylation of S100A9 affects its interaction with Cebpb, we overexpressed wild-type and point-mutant S100A9 plasmids along with Cebpb in HEK-293T cells. The results showed that the interaction between S100A9 and Cebpb was significantly reduced after lactylation impairment (Fig. 4h). Additionally, immunofluorescent images of Raw264.7 cells overexpressing S100A9 showed that LPS markedly increased the colocalization of S100A9 and Cebpb, which was significantly inhibited upon S100A9 lactylation mutation (Fig. S4a). These data suggested that S100A9 lactylation may regulate the interaction with Cebpb to activate inflammatory gene transcription.

Lactylated S100A9 combine with Cebpb to regulate IL-1β transcriptional activation

To assess alterations in gene transcription following S100A9 depletion, we performed RNA sequencing on three sample types: S100A9 point-mutant RAW264.7 cells, BMDMs from S100A9 knockout and wild-type mice, and lung tissues from septic S100A9 knockout and wild-type mice. GO enrichment analysis of RNA-seq data from lung tissues revealed that the target genes regulated by S100A9 were primarily involved in immune responses and inflammatory signaling pathways (Fig. S4b). Moreover, KEGG analysis identified that differentially expressed genes involved in cytokine and inflammation-related pathways (Fig. S4c). Further analysis of RNA-seq data from BMDMs confirmed that the differentially expressed genes were consistently enriched in cytokine and inflammation pathways (Fig. S4d). We further used the Cistrome Data Browser (http://dc2.cistrome.org/#/) to search for ChIP-seq data of Cebpb in BMDMs to identify potential Cebpb target genes. We then integrated Cut & Tag-seq data, RNA-seq data from lung tissue, RNA-seq data from BMDMs and RNA-seq data from mutant RAW264.7 cells to screen target genes regulated by both S100A9 and Cebpb. The intersection of these data sets was visualized using an upset plot, in which IL-1β was identified as a common downstream target gene of both S100A9 and Cebpb (Fig. 5a). Additionally, IGV analysis revealed the LPS-induced enrichment of S100A9 at the Il1b promoter in BMDMs, suggests S100A9 modulates transcription likely by recruiting or stabilizing the transcription factor C/EBPβ (Fig. 5b). To further validate the regulatory role of Cebpb in IL-1β transcription, we performed dual-luciferase reporter assays in HEK-293T cells and found that Cebpb enhanced IL-1β transcription (Fig. S4e). We also observed a significant reduction in LPS-induced IL-1β mRNA levels in S100A9 knockout BMDMs compared to that in wild-type BMDMs with or without LPS treatment (Fig. 5c, Fig. S4f). Western blot results showed a similar reduction of IL-1β protein levels in BMDMs (Fig. 5e, S4g). Furthermore, the analysis of point-mutant Raw264.7 cell line showed a significant reduction of IL-1β mRNA levels and protein levels in S100A9-mutant cells compared to wild-type cells with or without LPS treatment (Fig. 5d, f-g, Fig. S4h-i). Moreover, we performed dual-luciferase reporter assays in HEK-293T cells to examine the combined effect of S100A9 and Cebpb on IL-1β transcription. The transcription of IL-1β was significantly enhanced upon the overexpression of both Cebpb and S100A9 compared to Cebpb overexpression alone, which was reversed when S100A9 was point-mutated (Fig. 5h). These data revealed that lactylation of S100A9 promotes its interaction with Cebpb to enhance the transcription of IL-1β.

S100A9 regulates IL-1β transcription through promoter binding and interaction with Cebpb. a UpSet plot identifies IL-1β as a shared downstream target of S100A9 and Cebpb, based on integrated Cut & Tag, RNA-seq, and ChIP-seq datasets. b IGV tracks showing enhanced S100A9 binding at the IL-1β promoter in LPS-treated BMDM cells. c qPCR demonstrates reduced IL-1β mRNA levels in S100A9 knockout BMDM cells compared to wild-type controls. d qPCR confirms decreased IL-1β mRNA expression in S100A9-mutant Raw264.7 cells versus wild-type. e Western blot analysis shows reduced IL-1β protein levels in S100A9 knockout BMDM cells. f, g Western blot reveals diminished IL-1β protein expression in S100A9-mutant Raw264.7 cells. h Dual-luciferase reporter assays in HEK-293T cells demonstrate synergistic activation of IL-1β transcription by S100A9 and Cebpb, which is abolished by S100A9 point mutation, implicating lactylation in transcriptional regulation

S100A9 promoted macrophage inflammatory activation in sepsis-related lung injury

By examining the plasma levels of S100A9 in healthy controls and sepsis patients, we found that the plasma levels of S100A9 were significantly elevated in sepsis patients compared to healthy controls (Fig. 6a). Furthermore, S100A9 level was positively correlated with the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score (Fig. 6b) and the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE II) score (Fig. 6c), while negatively correlated with the oxygenation index (Fig. 6d). These findings suggest that elevated S100A9 levels are related to the severity of sepsis and sepsis-related lung injury.

S100A9 promotes macrophage inflammatory activation regulating sepsis-related lung injury. a Plasma S100A9 levels were significantly elevated in sepsis patients compared to healthy controls. b-c S100A9 levels positively correlated with SOFA and APACHE II scores. d S100A9 levels were inversely related to the oxygenation index. e HE staining of lung tissue revealed severe injury in wild-type (WT) sepsis mice compared to sham controls, which was alleviated in S100A9 knockout (KO) mice. f Survival rates were significantly lower in WT sepsis mice than in S100A9 KO sepsis mice. g-h Plasma IL-6 and IL-1β levels were elevated in WT sepsis mice but reduced in S100A9 KO mice. i-j qPCR analysis of lung tissue showed increased IL-6 and IL-1β mRNA levels in WT sepsis mice, which were reduced in S100A9 KO mice. k Plasma S100A9 levels positively correlated with lactate levels in sepsis patients

CLP-induced mouse model of sepsis in both wide type and S100A9 knockout mice was applied to investigate the role of S100A9 in regulating sepsis-induced lung injury. H&E staining of lung tissues showed that CLP-induced lung injury was markedly alleviated in S100A9 knockout mice (Fig. 6e). In addition, the survival curve data showed that S100A9 knockout significantly increased the survival rate of CLP-induced septic mice (Fig. 6f). CLP-induced plasma levels of inflammatory cytokines interleukin 6 (IL-6) and IL-1β were also significantly reduced in S100A9 knockout mice compared to that in wide type mice (Fig. 6g and h). Moreover, the upregulation of mRNA levels of inflammatory cytokines in septic lung tissues were significantly reduced upon S100A9 depletion (Fig. 6i and j). After confirming the critical role of S100A9 in sepsis related ALI, we observed a significant positive correlation between plasma S100A9 levels and lactate in sepsis patients (Fig. 6k). These results indicate that S100A9 plays important role in exacerbating sepsis-related inflammatory responses and lung injury.

Discussion

Sepsis is a major pathogenic factor of ARDS [24]. The prognosis of ARDS caused by sepsis is worse than that of sepsis alone or ARDS patients, often rapidly progressing to MODS and even death, attracting more attention in clinical practice. However, specific treatment options for sepsis and sepsis-induced ARDS are extremely limited. Therefore, it is urgent to clarify the pathogenesis of sepsis-related ARDS and discover new therapeutic methods and targets. This study found that macrophage S100A9 in lung tissue from sepsis patients undergoes significant lactylation modification. The lactylated S100A9 further promotes its accumulation in the nucleus. Once in the nucleus, S100A9 functions as a co-transcription factor, interacting with the transcription factor Cebpb and binding to the IL-1β gene promoter region, thereby regulating IL-1β transcription and promoting macrophage inflammatory responses, leading to sepsis-associated lung injury.

Recent studies have shown that lactate regulates cell functions in an epigenetic manner. In 2019, Zhang D et al. used isotopic C13-labeled lactate and found that C13-labeled lactate groups were present on lysine residues of histones, indicating lactylation modification [25]. Proteomics studies have revealed that, in addition to histone lactylation, there are many non-histone lactylation modifications. Metabolic small molecule product lactate can act as a precursor, and under the action of acyltransferases (such as p300), non-histone proteins, such as high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) and PKM2, undergo lactylation, thus regulating downstream pathways [26, 27]. These studies have greatly advanced our understanding of lactate’s role in physiological and disease conditions, especially in sepsis-related lung metabolic functions. However, the role of lactylation modification in non-histone proteins and its impact on the development of sepsis-induced ARDS remain unclear, and whether S100A9 undergoes lactylation modification and how lactylation regulates its function are still major questions. This study found a close correlation between plasma levels of S100A9 and lactate with sepsis and sepsis-related ARDS in clinical patient samples and clinical data, and found a significant positive correlation between plasma S100A9 and lactate levels, indicating a close link between S100A9 and lactate. Further studies revealed for the first time that S100A9 undergoes lactylation modification and identified two lactylation modification sites on S100A9: K4 and K94. Our study enriches the understanding of the relationship between S100A9 and lactate and expands the range of lactylation-modified proteins in sepsis.

Classic theories suggest that during sepsis, S100A9, as an extracellular protein, interacts with cell surface homologous receptors to activate downstream signaling pathways, leading to the production and release of chemokines and pro-inflammatory cytokines. S100A9 is also abundant in the cytoplasm, where it helps control cytoskeletal rearrangement and activates reactive oxygen species-related pathways [28]. Currently, there is limited research on the role of S100A9 entering the nucleus. Recent studies have shown that S100A9 can enter the nucleus and directly promote the expression of complement factor C3 to mediate the pathogenic process of psoriasis [29]. In breast cancer, nuclear-specific expression of S100A9 is recruited to numerous promoters and enhancers, increasing the transcription of oncogenes and promoting the phenotypic transformation of breast cancer [30]. This suggests that nuclear expression of S100A9 may play a key role in regulating gene transcription, which requires further investigation. Our study found that during sepsis, S100A9 in macrophage cytoplasm accumulates in the nucleus, suggesting that S100A9 may regulate gene transcription through nuclear entry during sepsis. However, the mechanism by which S100A9 accumulates in the nucleus remains unclear. In recent years, studies have shown that epigenetic modifications of proteins can regulate their nuclear localization, including O-GlcNAc modification [31] and Neddylation modification [32]. Previous studies have suggested that protein epigenetic modifications can regulate protein localization. Therefore, could lactylation modification participate in regulating the nuclear entry process of S100A9? Our further studies found that the accumulation of S100A9 in the nucleus is not only regulated by cellular lactate levels but also by lactylation modification. That is, lactylation of S100A9 promotes its accumulation in the nucleus. These findings suggest that the lactylation process participates in regulating S100A9’s nuclear accumulation, and further imply that S100A9 may participate in gene transcription regulation once it enters the nucleus. This discovery not only enriches the understanding of S100A9’s nuclear accumulation during sepsis but also reveals the potential physiological function of lactylation modification in regulating protein localization.

The nuclear accumulation of S100A9 suggests its potential role in regulating gene transcription. Through Cut & Tag analysis, we found that S100A9 can enrich genes under sepsis conditions, with over 50% enrichment in the promoter region, thereby confirming that nuclear accumulation of S100A9 activates gene transcription. However, based on the structure of S100A9 and previous reports, it is known that S100A9 lacks a domain that directly binds to gene promoter regions [30]. Instead, it functions as a co-transcription factor to activate gene transcription. Therefore, S100A9 cannot directly regulate gene transcription as a transcription factor but instead regulates transcriptional activation by binding to other transcription factors. To identify the transcription factor that interacts with S100A9, we used immunoprecipitation, mass spectrometry, and proteomics, and ultimately confirmed that Cebpb is a potential interacting transcription factor of S100A9. We further validated their interaction through co-immunoprecipitation. Notably, previous studies have shown that lactylation modification can regulate the interaction between α-myosin heavy chain (α-MHC) and Titin in muscle cells to regulate heart structure and contraction [33]. Lactylation-modified X-ray cross-complementing protein 1 (XRCC1) enhances its affinity for importin α, promoting the strength of their interaction [34]. Similarly, our study found that lactylation modification of S100A9 can enhance its interaction with Cebpb, further explaining the physiological role of lactylation modification in promoting protein-protein interactions.

To identify the potential common downstream target genes of S100A9 and Cebpb, we further screened the S100A9 and Cebpb co-regulated downstream genes—IL-1β—through ChIP sequencing and multiple RNA-seq datasets, and demonstrated through qPCR and dual-luciferase assays that S100A9, through Cebpb, regulates IL-1β gene transcription and activation. This revealed that during sepsis, S100A9, in its role as a co-transcription factor, interacts with transcription factor Cebpb, accumulates in the IL-1β promoter region, and promotes gene transcription to mediate sepsis-related lung injury.

In this study, we revealed the specific mechanism of sepsis-induced lung injury through S100A9 lactylation modification and its interaction with transcription factor Cebpb to regulate pro-inflammatory IL-1β gene transcription. This study highlights the role of S100A9 lactylation modification in the pathogenesis of sepsis-related lung injury and provides new therapeutic strategies for sepsis-associated ARDS. However, there are several limitations in our current study. First, in addition to lactylation, S100A9 may also undergo other epigenetic modifications, such as ubiquitination, acetylation, and methylation, which could interfere with the findings and warrant further investigation. Second, the exploration of S100A9 lactylation modification sites focused primarily on macrophages in vitro, and in vitro results may not fully reflect the complex in vivo environment. Therefore, further validation should be performed using S100A9 point mutation mice for in vivo experiments. Eventually, while S100A9 is classically known as a component of the extracellular alarmin complex (S100A8/A9), our study uncovers a distinct intracellular regulatory mechanism mediated by lactylation. Our data indicate that lactylation of S100A9 does not interfere with TLR4 binding but significantly reduces its interaction with S100A8. This suggests a potential shift in the functional equilibrium of S100A9: lactylation may act as a molecular switch that not only promotes its nuclear translocation but also destabilizes the canonical heterodimer, thereby diverting it from extracellular inflammatory pathways towards nuclear transcriptional regulation. We acknowledge that the impact of this modification on the secretion dynamics of S100A9 remains to be fully elucidated. Future studies will be needed to determine whether lactylation specifically retains S100A9 within the cell to facilitate its nuclear functions, thus acting as a checkpoint between extracellular alarm signaling and intranuclear gene regulation.

Conclusions

This study elucidates that S100A9 lactylation promotes sepsis-induced acute lung injury by facilitating its interaction with transcription factor C/EBPβ to enhance IL-1β transcription. Our findings not only reveal the crucial role of S100A9 lactylation in amplifying inflammatory responses but also identify it as a potential therapeutic target for sepsis-associated ARDS.
