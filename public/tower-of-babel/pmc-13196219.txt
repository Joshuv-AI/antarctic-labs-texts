Determinants of quality of life in type 1 and type 2 diabetes patients in Palestine: a comparative cross-sectional study
Authors: Fuad Farajalla, Ayman Salameh, Abd-alrahman Halahla, Misarah Talahmmeh, Mohyeddin Al-Allama, Momin Rasras
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiabetes mellitus significantly impairs quality of life (QoL), yet comparative data between type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) in resource-limited, conflict-affected settings such as Palestine remain scarce, particularly for adults with T1DM.AimThis study assessed QoL among adults with diabetes in southern Palestine, compared outcomes between T1DM and T2DM, and identified demographic and clinical predictors influencing QoL.MethodsA cross-sectional study using convenience sampling was conducted among 393 adults recruited from diabetes clinics and healthcare centers in the southern West Bank. QoL was measured using the Revised Diabetes Quality of Life Questionnaire (RVDQOL-13). Statistical analyses included independent samples t-tests, Pearson correlation, and multiple linear regression.ResultsOverall QoL showed moderate impairment (M = 44.07, SD = 13.47), with no significant difference between participants with T1DM and T2DM. However, individuals with T1DM reported significantly poorer satisfaction scores than those with T2DM (p = 0.001). Regression analysis indicated that physical activity was significantly associated with QoL among participants with T1DM. In contrast, female gender, lower physical activity, higher HbA1c levels, and diabetic foot complications were significantly associated with poorer QoL among participants with T2DM.ConclusionPalestinian adults with T1DM and T2DM experience comparable overall QoL impairment; however, T1DM is associated with poorer treatment satisfaction. Targeting modifiable factors, particularly physical activity, glycemic control, and foot complications, may provide important opportunities for improving QoL through primary care interventions, especially for individuals with T2DM who are at higher risk of these complications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12875-026-03319-0.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is one of the most significant global health challenges, currently affecting 463 million adults worldwide, with numbers projected to reach 700 million by 2045 [1]. Both type 1 (T1DM) and type 2 (T2DM) diabetes result in high morbidity and mortality, mainly due to complications including cardiovascular disease, nephropathy, retinopathy, neuropathy, and amputations [1, 2].

Daily management requires taking medications, blood glucose monitoring, regulating diet, and exercising, placing a considerable burden on diabetes patients and greatly affecting their quality of life (QoL) [3, 4]. In the Palestinian context, these burdens are intensified by chronic illness, persistent stressors, and socioeconomic and political instability [5], further reducing QoL among both patients and healthcare professionals [6–8].

QoL is a multidimensional construct encompassing individuals’ perceptions of physical health, psychological well-being, social relationships, and environmental context, as defined by the World Health Organization (WHO, 2021). In people with DM, QoL is shaped by interconnected demographic, clinical, and behavioral factors, including the demands of self-management, the risk of complications, and broader psychosocial and environmental influences [2, 8–10]. In Palestine, stressors related to chronic disease, work, and political violence have been shown to undermine QoL among patients, clinical staff, and students [6, 11, 12].

International evidence, including studies from Thailand, Iran, Indonesia, and Spain, shows that older age, female gender, low education, low income, longer diabetes duration, poor glycemic control, complications, and inactivity predict poorer QoL [3, 13–15]. Studies have demonstrated that people with diabetes experience significantly lower QoL, resulting in poorer treatment adherence and higher healthcare utilization [1, 4]. Studies conducted in India [1], Spain [15], and Romania [16] underscore this global trend. Conversely, a higher level of education, increased income, greater engagement in physical activities, and the absence of complications are associated with an improved QoL [2, 14, 17].

Global research further demonstrates that both T1DM and T2DM populations experience diminished QoL because of complications, financial constraints, and limited specialist access [1]. High rates of diabetes DM and its complications are a concern for the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region [2, 18]. Evidence from studies conducted in Saudi Arabia suggests only a minority of patients with T2DM report having a good QoL; to the contrary, patients commonly report suffering from pain, restrictions with movement, and psychological distress [2, 17, 19]. Jordan is experiencing high complication rates for both type T1DM and T2DM, and a lack of equity in the healthcare system continues to affect the patients’ QoL [20, 21]. The United Arab Emirates (UAE) is adding to the poor QoL of T1DM and T2DM patients due to the high prevalence of obesity and sedentary lifestyles [22].

Previous Palestinian studies revealed that female gender, lower income, and having experienced either violence or a chronic stressor are statistically significant predictors of a poor QoL among students and nurses [12, 23]. Diabetes prevalence in Palestine continues to rise, currently affecting approximately 9.2% of adults [8, 24]. QoL is further compromised by chronic political instability, restricted mobility due to military checkpoints, fragmented healthcare services, medication shortages, and severe economic hardship [23, 25–27].

Despite the growing burden of both T1DM and T2DM, QoL differences between these groups remain unexplored in Palestine. No prior study has systematically compared QoL among Palestinian adults with T1DM versus T2DM or evaluated the independent effects of demographic, clinical, and lifestyle factors using validated instruments. These gaps limit evidence-based interventions and efficient resource allocation, underscoring the need for focused comparative research to inform patient-centered care and public health planning.

Therefore, this study aims to [1] compare the QoL between adults with T1DM and T2DM in the southern West Bank, Palestine, and [2] identify key demographic, clinical, and behavioral determinants of QoL in the overall diabetes population. By using the Revised Version of the Diabetes Quality of Life Questionnaire (RVDQOL-13), this research seeks to provide actionable evidence for improving diabetes care and outcomes in Palestine.

Methods

Design and setting

This study employed a cross-sectional design, which is commonly used in public health as a reliable method for examining behaviors and associations within populations [28]. The study was conducted in the southern region of the West Bank, specifically in the Hebron and Bethlehem governorates. These areas include urban centers, rural villages, and refugee camps, each with distinct socioeconomic and residential characteristics. Participants were recruited from diabetes outpatient clinics in governmental hospitals and primary healthcare centers located in the Hebron and Bethlehem governorates in the southern West Bank, Palestine. These facilities provide routine follow-up and management services for patients with both T1DM and T2DM.

Population and sampling

The study population consisted of adult patients diagnosed with T1DM or T2DM who attended diabetes outpatient clinics or primary healthcare centers in the Hebron and Bethlehem governorates. Because the exact number of patients who use these facilities is not known, the sample size was calculated using the Raosoft calculator, assuming a 95% confidence level, 5% margin of error, and a conservative 50% response distribution in the absence of prior local estimates. This approach ensures maximum sample size estimation under uncertainty. The minimum required sample size was 384 participants. The study was not specifically powered to detect differences between T1DM and T2DM subgroups; therefore, subgroup analyses should be interpreted with caution. The observed imbalance between T1DM and T2DM participants reflects the underlying distribution of diabetes types in routine clinical settings. Participants were recruited using convenience sampling.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participant inclusion criteria consist of adults (≥ 18 years) with T1DM or T2DM and the ability to read and write in Arabic; therefore, they can complete a study questionnaire independently. Participants were excluded if they had cognitive impairments, severe psychiatric disorders, or barriers to communication that would otherwise prevent them from completing the questionnaire with any level of reliability.

Instrumentation

Data were collected using a structured, paper-based, self-administered questionnaire consisting of two sections: [1] demographic and health-related characteristics and [2] the RVDQOL-13 scale. The first section included demographic variables (age, gender, marital status, educational level, monthly income, place of residence, and smoking status) and clinical variables (type of diabetes, duration of diabetes, self-reported most recent HbA1c level, presence of diabetes-related complications, and physical activity frequency).

QoL was assessed using the Arabic version of the Revised Diabetes Quality of Life Questionnaire (RVDQOL-13) [8], originally developed by Bujang et al. [29]. This instrument was designed with 13 items, divided into three domains: satisfaction (6 items), impact (4 items), and worry (3 items). Participant ratings for the Satisfaction domain will be collected on a 5-point Likert scale, where 1 is “very satisfied” and 5 is “very dissatisfied”; therefore, a total score of Satisfaction can range from 6 to 30 points. For the domains “impact” and “worry,” all items would be rated using a 5-point Likert scale, specifically “never” [1] through “always” [5]. The total score range for the domains “impact” and “worry” will be 4 to 20 and 3 to 15, respectively. The total score across all three domains can range from 13 to 65 points. A greater total score represents a worse QoL.

Physical activity was assessed based on self-reported frequency and categorized into four levels: regularly (engaging in planned physical activity ≥ 3 times per week), sometimes (1–2 times per week), rarely (< 1 time per week), and never (no regular physical activity).

Diabetes-related complications were defined as the presence of at least one physician-diagnosed condition, including hypertension, poor vision, renal failure, or diabetic foot, as reported by the participant. Each complication was recorded as a dichotomous variable (yes/no).

Validity and reliability

The original research study that validated the RVDQOL-13 showed that each domain of the RVDQOL-13 has a high level of reliability as measured by composite reliability from 0.794 (worry) to 0.922 (satisfaction). The domain with the highest reliability was satisfaction (0.922), followed by worry (0.794) and impact (0.781) [29]. The Arabic version of the RVDQOL-13 was translated and validated previously by Eshtaya et al. [8] using the WHO`s translation process of the RVDQOL-13 (like forward-translated, back-translated, and reviewed by experts) to guarantee that the content expressed in the original had valid content. The overall value of Cronbach’s alpha for the Arabic version was 0.88 [8]. In the current research, Cronbach’s alpha was used to assess the internal consistency reliability of the questionnaire. Within the final sample (N = 393), the Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were 0.912 for the satisfaction domain, 0.846 for the worry domain, 0.820 for the impact domain, and 0.816 for the overall scale, indicating good internal consistency.

A pilot test was performed on thirty patients that were not included in the final sample; the pilot study evaluated the clarity, ability to understand, and time to complete the questionnaire. The questionnaire was determined to be clear and took 15–20 min to complete; therefore, no changes were needed to be made to the questionnaire.

Data collection process

After ethics committee approval, permission was obtained from the relevant government health authorities and administrators of the selected outpatient diabetes clinics and primary healthcare centers. Data were collected between December 25, 2023, and January 30, 2024. The research team visited each clinic during routine working hours and recruited eligible patients attending follow-up visits while they waited at the clinics.

Patients received information about the study purpose, procedures, and voluntary participation. Those who agreed signed a written consent form before completing the questionnaire. Participants were assured that their responses would remain confidential and anonymous and that they could withdraw at any time without affecting their access to healthcare services.

The self-administered paper questionnaire was completed in a designated private area within each clinic. Research team members were available to answer questions without influencing responses. Completion required approximately 15–20 min. Completed questionnaires were checked for completeness, securely stored, and later entered into IBM SPSS Statistics version 28 for analysis.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 28. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participants’ characteristics and QoL scores. Independent-samples t-tests and one-way ANOVA were conducted to examine differences in QoL across categorical variables. Pearson correlation was used to assess associations between continuous variables (e.g., age, HbA1c) and QoL.

Multiple linear regression analysis was performed using the enter method, whereby all theoretically relevant demographic and clinical variables were included simultaneously to control for potential confounding and to explore their independent associations with QoL. This approach was specified a priori. Given the number of predictors relative to the subgroup sample size, particularly for T1DM, the regression results should be interpreted with caution due to the potential risk of overfitting. Higher QoL scores indicated lower QoL. Assumptions were analyzed prior to running multiple regression analysis. Multicollinearity was assessed by looking at tolerance and VIF. Independence of errors was assessed by using the Durbin-Watson statistic. Normality and homoscedasticity were assessed by looking at residual plots. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Bonferroni correction was applied for multiple outcome comparisons (e.g., QoL domains). Bivariate analyses were exploratory for regression modeling and were not adjusted; results were interpreted cautiously.

Ethical consideration

Ethical approval for this study was obtained before the commencement of data collection from the Ethics Committee of the Nursing College, Palestine Polytechnic University, Hebron, Palestine (Approval No. EA/2026/123). The research was conducted in line with ethical considerations as outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Information about the purpose, procedure, risk, and benefit of the research activities was provided completely to all participants. Written consent was obtained from all participants before pare study. Participants were informed that their participation was completely voluntary and that they could withdraw from the study and not be penalized academically or personally. No information collected was identifiable; instead, participants were assigned a numerical code to identify their questionnaire. All data were kept in a password-protected format on a computer drive that only the research team could access, consistent with international guidelines regarding data protection and research ethics.

Results

A total of 450 questionnaires were distributed, and 393 were completed, yielding a response rate of 87.3%. As shown in Table 1, patients with T1DM were significantly younger than those with T2DM (44.6 vs. 61.3 years, p < 0.001), while HbA1c levels and gender distribution were similar between groups. Significant differences were found in marital status, education level, place of residence, and diabetes duration (p < 0.01), with T1DM patients more often single, more educated, urban residents, and with shorter disease durations. Income, smoking status, and physical activity showed no significant differences. Overall, 73.0% of participants reported at least one diabetes-related complication, which was more common in T2DM patients (76.1% vs. 65.5%, p = 0.032). Poor vision and hypertension were the most frequent complications, with poor vision significantly higher in T2DM patients. Details are presented in Table 1.

Overall QoL scores indicated a moderate level of impairment among participants (44.07 ± 13.47). Across domains, the impact domain demonstrated the greatest impairment (48.23 ± 21.74), while the worry (42.96 ± 21.59) and satisfaction domains (41.84 ± 18.08) reflected moderate levels of impairment. Independent-samples t-tests were conducted to compare QoL between participants with T1DM and T2DM. No statistically significant difference was observed in overall QoL (t = 1.50, p = 0.134, d = 0.17), indicating generally comparable levels between the groups. Similarly, differences in the impact (t = − 1.23, p = 0.219, d = 0.14) and worry domains (t = 0.06, p = 0.954, d = 0.01) were not statistically significant. However, a statistically significant difference was found in the satisfaction domain, with participants with T1DM reporting poorer satisfaction-related QoL than those with T2DM (46.70 ± 21.14 vs. 39.89 ± 16.34; t = 3.42, p = 0.001, d = 0.37). In both diabetes groups, the presence of diabetes-related complications was significantly associated with poorer QoL. Participants with complications reported higher impairment scores compared with those without complications (T1DM: p = 0.008; T2DM: p = 0.001). When individual complications were assessed, significant associations were observed for poor vision (T1DM: p = 0.040; T2DM: p = 0.023) and renal failure (T1DM: p = 0.040; T2DM: p = 0.028). Diabetic foot showed a significant association with impaired QoL only among patients with T2DM (p < 0.001).

Hypertension was significantly related to poorer QoL in the T2DM group (p = 0.003) but showed no significant association among T1DM patients. Gender differences were also observed only in the T2DM group, where females reported significantly higher impairment scores than males (p = 0.010), while no significant gender difference was detected among T1DM patients (p = 0.392).

Regular physical activity demonstrated a strong relationship with QoL in both groups. Participants who engaged in regular exercise reported lower impairment scores, whereas those who never exercised had the highest scores (T1DM: p = 0.006; T2DM: p < 0.001).Educational level was significantly associated with QoL among T2DM patients (p = 0.030), with the highest impairment observed among those with elementary education, but this association was not significant in T1DM (p = 0.760).

Pearson correlation analysis showed that age was not significantly correlated with QoL in either T1DM (r = 0.129, p = 0.174) or T2DM (r = 0.038, p = 0.522). However, HbA1c showed a significant positive correlation with QoL impairment in T2DM (r = 0.196, p = 0.001), while this association did not reach significance in the T1DM group (r = 0.162, p = 0.087).

For T2DM (Table 5), the regression model was significant (F(15, 264) = 4.644, p < 0.001), explaining 20.9% of the variance in QoL (R² = 0.209, Adjusted R² = 0.164). Significant predictors included gender (B = 4.85, SE = 1.92, p = 0.012), with females reporting poorer QoL than males; frequency of physical activity (B = 2.60, SE = 0.74, p = 0.001), with less activity associated with poorer QoL; last HbA1c value (B = 0.83, SE = 0.40, p = 0.037), indicating that higher HbA1c was linked to worse QoL; and diabetic foot complication (B = -6.27, SE = 2.18, p = 0.004), with its presence associated with poorer QoL.

Discussion

This study examined differences in QoL and its determinants between adults with type T1DM and T2DM in the southern West Bank, Palestine. Overall, participants reported a moderate level of QoL impairment. Comparative analysis demonstrated that overall QoL scores, as well as the impact and worry domains, did not significantly differ between diabetes types. However, individuals with T1DM reported significantly poorer satisfaction-related QoL than those with T2DM. These findings suggest that although the overall burden of diabetes on quality of life may be comparable between groups, certain psychosocial aspects, particularly treatment satisfaction, may be more negatively experienced among individuals living with T1DM.

The overall demographic profile of the sample is consistent with regional epidemiological patterns. The majority were married and from low-to-middle-income households. As expected, participants with T1DM were significantly younger than those with T2DM, reflecting the typical earlier onset of the disease. The predominance of T2DM and the high prevalence of diabetes-related complications observed in this study are consistent with findings from studies conducted in Saudi Arabia, Jordan, and other Middle Eastern countries, where diabetes is frequently associated with aging populations, socioeconomic challenges, and increasing rates of chronic complications [17, 19, 21].

Importantly, the regression analyses indicated that determinants of QoL differed between diabetes types. For people with T1DM, although physical activity showed a statistically significant association with QoL in the regression analysis, the overall model was not statistically significant. Therefore, this finding should be interpreted cautiously and considered exploratory rather than conclusive. For people with T1DM, lifestyle behaviors may play a particularly important role in shaping well-being in this group. In contrast, among individuals with T2DM, several factors were independently associated with poorer QoL, including female gender, lower levels of physical activity, higher HbA1c values, and the presence of diabetic foot complications. These findings indicate that while lifestyle factors are important across both diabetes types, clinical and sociodemographic factors appear to be more strongly associated with QoL among individuals with T2DM [14, 21]. However, it is important to note that the regression models explained a relatively modest proportion of the variance in QoL (R² ≈ 0.21). Although the models were statistically significant, a large portion of variability in quality of life remains unexplained. This suggests that additional factors, such as psychological distress, social support, healthcare accessibility, and broader environmental stressors, may also play important roles in shaping QoL outcomes among individuals with diabetes.

The finding that women reported poorer QoL than men among individuals with T2DM is consistent with previous research conducted in Saudi Arabia and Thailand, which found that women with diabetes often experience greater psychological distress and role-related burdens associated with family responsibilities and sociocultural expectations [2, 14]. Gender differences in diabetes outcomes have also been attributed to disparities in health-seeking behavior, emotional stress, and access to supportive resources. These factors may be particularly relevant in settings with limited healthcare infrastructure and socioeconomic constraints, as they can exacerbate the challenges faced by women in managing their diabetes effectively and maintaining a good quality of life.

Physical activity emerged as a consistent factor associated with QoL outcomes. Participants who reported engaging in regular physical activity demonstrated better QoL scores compared with those reporting little or no activity. This finding aligns with international evidence showing that regular exercise contributes to improved metabolic control, reduced diabetes-related complications, and enhanced psychological well-being among individuals with diabetes [3, 14]. In resource-limited environments such as Palestine [30], lifestyle interventions that promote physical activity may represent an accessible and cost-effective strategy to improve patient-reported outcomes, particularly in enhancing glycemic control and overall well-being among individuals with diabetes.

Glycemic control was also significantly associated with QoL. Higher HbA1c levels were independently associated with poorer QoL among participants with T2DM, supporting findings from studies conducted in Iran, Saudi Arabia, and Palestine, where poor metabolic control has been linked to increased symptom burden, complications, and psychological distress [8, 13, 19]. These results emphasize the importance of sustained glycemic management not only for preventing long-term complications but also for improving patients’ perceived quality of life.

Diabetes-related complications were highly prevalent in the study population, with nearly three-quarters of participants reporting at least one. Bivariate analyses demonstrated that complications such as poor vision, renal failure, and hypertension were associated with poorer QoL. However, in the multivariate analysis, only diabetic foot remained a significant predictor of QoL among individuals with T2DM. This finding is consistent with previous studies indicating that diabetic foot complications have particularly severe consequences for daily functioning, mobility, and psychological well-being [17, 19]. The pain, disability, and risk of amputation associated with diabetic foot may substantially disrupt daily activities and social participation, thereby exerting a disproportionate impact on QoL.

Despite these associations, the present study found no significant difference in overall QoL between individuals with T1DM and T2DM. Similar findings have been reported in studies conducted in Montenegro and other European contexts, where diabetes-related complications and lifestyle factors were stronger determinants of QoL than the type of diabetes itself [9]. However, the poorer satisfaction scores observed among individuals with T1DM may reflect the lifelong nature of the condition, the need for continuous insulin therapy, and the psychological burden associated with strict self-management requirements. Previous studies among adolescents and young adults with T1DM have reported similar findings, indicating that treatment burden and social challenges may negatively influence satisfaction-related aspects of QoL [15, 31].

The moderate level of QoL impairment observed in this study also reflects the broader socioeconomic and healthcare challenges present in Palestine. Limited access to specialized diabetes services, economic hardship, and restricted mobility may complicate disease management and exacerbate the psychological burden of living with a chronic illness. Previous Palestinian studies have similarly demonstrated that social stressors and structural barriers can negatively affect quality of life among both patients and healthcare workers [7, 8].

Overall, the findings highlight the multifactorial nature of QoL among individuals with diabetes. While diabetes type itself did not significantly influence overall QoL, modifiable factors, including physical activity, glycemic control, and the prevention of complications, played a central role in determining patient outcomes. Addressing these factors through integrated diabetes management programs and lifestyle interventions may therefore represent an effective strategy to improve well-being among individuals living with diabetes in Palestine.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the use of convenience sampling may introduce selection bias and limit the representativeness of the study population. Participants were recruited only from outpatient clinics and primary healthcare centers in the Hebron and Bethlehem governorates, which may not fully reflect the experiences of individuals with diabetes across other regions of Palestine. In addition, patients who regularly attend clinical follow-up may differ from those with limited access to healthcare services, further constraining the generalizability of the results.

The cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences. Reliance on self-reported data may also introduce recall and social desirability biases. Furthermore, important confounders, such as mental health status and social support, were not measured, which may have resulted in residual confounding. The use of a single self-administered questionnaire may not have captured the full complexity of patients’ experiences.

The regression models explained only a modest proportion of the variance in QoL, indicating that other unmeasured factors, such as psychological well-being, social support, and healthcare access, may play a substantial role. In addition, multiple bivariate analyses were conducted without adjustment for multiple comparisons, which may increase the risk of Type I error. The inclusion of several predictors relative to the sample size, particularly in the T1DM subgroup, may also increase the risk of overfitting and reduce the stability of the regression estimates. Finally, the lack of longitudinal follow-up limits the ability to assess changes in QoL over time and the potential impact of interventions. These limitations should be considered when interpreting and applying the findings.

Conclusion

QoL among Palestinian adults with T1DM and T2DM is shaped by multiple sociodemographic, clinical, and behavioral factors rather than diabetes type alone. Improvements in care may benefit from focusing on lifestyle support, complication management, and psychosocial assessment. Policy should ensure equitable access to comprehensive services. Future research should use longitudinal and interventional approaches to clarify causality and assess targeted strategies to enhance QoL for diverse diabetes populations.
