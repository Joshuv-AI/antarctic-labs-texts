Inferring System and Optimal Control Parameters of Closed-Loop Systems from Partial Observations
Victor Geadah, Juncal Arbelaiz, Harrison Ritz, Nathaniel D. Daw, Jonathan D. Cohen, Jonathan W. Pillow

Abstract
We consider the joint problem of system identification and inverse optimal control for discrete-time stochastic Linear Quadratic Regulators. We analyze finite and infinite time horizons in a partially observed setting, where the state is observed noisily. To recover closed-loop system parameters, we develop inference methods based on probabilistic state-space model (SSM) techniques. First, we show that the system parameters exhibit non-identifiability in the infinite-horizon from closed-loop measurements, and we provide exact and numerical methods to disentangle the parameters. Second, to improve parameter identifiability, we show that we can further enhance recovery by either (1) incorporating additional partial measurements of the control signals or (2) moving to the finite-horizon setting. We further illustrate the performance of our methodology through numerical examples.



I
Introduction

An important problem in the analysis of scientific data is accurately inferring the properties of dynamical systems based on partial observations.
This is particularly important in neuroscience, where the neural systems that support the highly complex computations of interest are both difficult to observe, which is done for instance through neuro-imaging
[
1
]
or electrophysiology, and are often unknown a-priori.
Probabilistic state-space models provide a powerful language to model neuroscientific data
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
, however, the fact that this systems are naturally operating in closed-loop configurations is often disregarded in the inference.

A growing body of work uses control-theoretic tools to analyze the dynamics of neural systems (see e.g.,
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
). For example, recent work used a standard formulation in optimal control, the
Linear Quadratic Regulator
(LQR), to model neural dynamics in motor cortex during movement preparation
[
9
]
. While
posing neuroscientific questions and analyzing related data from a control-theoretic lens is promising,
inferring the properties of controllers from closed-loop neural recordings
poses particular statistical challenges
[
10
]
.

Work in inverse optimal control (IOC)—the problem of inferring the cost function of an optimal controller from recordings with known system dynamics—has historically focused on fully observed systems
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
.
Recent work has explored control over partially observed systems
[
14
,
15
]
, but assumes the system parameters are known.
Similarly,
inverse optimal control has been proven useful in understanding
human sensorimotor control, but existing approaches have depended on full observations and/or a known plant
[
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
]
.
Refs.
[
20
,
21
]

explored system parameter inference from a partially-observed neural system, but focused on system identification rather than inferring the parameters of the underlying control process.

Unlike engineered or known sensorimotor systems, for which
accurate models of the dynamics might be available, the dynamics of neural systems are often unknown. Furthermore, they entail closed-loop processes. Leveraging control-theoretic perspectives on such un-observed processes recently gained traction in providing strong expressivity in state-space models
[
4
]
. Similarly,
model-free

[
22
]
, or
direct
, approaches to IOC involve no knowledge of the system and bypass it altogether, and offer discernible strengths for approximate control
[
23
,
24
]
. In both cases, the study of the underlying joint parameters and identifiability is relatively unexplored.

Motivated by this problem, we focus on identifying both system parameters (e.g., system dynamics) and optimal control parameters (e.g., cost functions) from partial observations, assuming the system is operating in closed-loop with an optimal controller corresponding to the stochastic LQR.
Our approach consists of two steps. First, we leverage the analytic and tractable expectation-maximization (EM) algorithm for system identification of linear-Gaussian state space models
[
25
]
to estimate a system’s closed-loop dynamics. Second, we develop methods for inferring LQR state and action cost functions from these estimates of the closed-loop dynamics.

Our contributions are three-fold. First in Section §
III
, we provide a characterization of the non-identifiability encountered in the infinite horizon setting, as a necessary and sufficient approach to identifiability. Next, we provide two different alternatives to improve parameter identifiability, namely i) by considering the finite-horizon setting in §
IV
; and ii) by using partial measurements of the control inputs in §
V
.
In all sections, we provide both theoretical treatments and either exact or numerical methods to disentangle the parameters.

Setting and Problem Formulation

II-A

Control Problem and Assumptions

We consider the stochastic linear quadratic regulator (LQR) optimal control problem, which with a finite and known time-horizon
T>0
reads

\min_{x_{1:T},u_{0:T-1}}J_{T}(x_{1:T},u_{0:T-1})=\\
\frac{1}{T}\mathbb{E}\left[x_{T}^{\top}Q_{T}x_{T}+\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left(x_{t}^{\top}Qx_{t}+u_{t}^{\top}Ru_{t}\right)\right]

(1a)

subject to

\displaystyle x_{t+1}

\displaystyle=Ax_{t}+Bu_{t}+\omega_{t},\quad\omega_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,W)

(1b)

for
t\in\{0,\dots,T-1\}
,
x_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,W_{0})
, for the state
x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and control inputs
u_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
. The system is assumed to be driven by zero-mean Gaussian noise with covariance
W
. The LQR problem aims to minimize the cost function
J_{T}
, with parameters
Q,Q_{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, positive semi-definite matrices, and
R\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
, positive definite. The expectation is taken w.r.t.
\mathbb{P}\left(x_{0},\omega_{0:T-1}\right)
, over the initial condition
x_{0}
and the dynamics noise sequence
\omega_{0:T-1}
.

For simplicity we assume there is not a cross-term between control input and state in the cost function. The system is assumed to have access to
full
and
noiseless
measurements of its own state for feedback control. (That is, there is no need to implement a Kalman filter for control). Furthermore, for well-posedeness and uniqueness of control gain solutions below, we assume that
(A,B)
is controllable,
(A,C)
is observable
[
27
]
.

It is well-known
[
28
]
that the optimal control inputs

for the problem (
1
) are in

form

\displaystyle u^{*}_{t}

\displaystyle=-K_{t}x_{t}

\displaystyle\coloneqq-(B^{\top}P_{t+1}B+R)^{-1}(B^{\top}P_{t+1}A)x_{t}

(2)

for the control gain
K_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, determined from the Riccati recursion term

P_{t}=Q+A^{\top}P_{t+1}A\\
-A^{\top}P_{t+1}B(B^{\top}P_{t+1}B+R)^{-1}B^{\top}P_{t+1}A

(3)

and
P_{T}=Q_{T}
. This yields the closed-loop system

\displaystyle x_{t+1}=\underbrace{(A-BK_{t})}_{F_{t}}x_{t}+\omega_{t}

(4)

with time varying closed-loop linear dynamics
F_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. We depict the block diagram for the closed-loop plant in Fig.
1
. Furthermore, we will also be considering the infinite horizon setting, where we take
T\to\infty
in (
1a
), removing
Q_{T}
. In this case, the optimal control signal
u^{*}_{t}
is a linear feedback of the state as well,
but with a
static
gain

\displaystyle u^{*}_{t}

\displaystyle=-K_{ss}x_{t}=-(B^{\top}P_{ss}B+R)^{-1}(B^{\top}P_{ss}A)x_{t}

(5)

for the control gain
K_{ss}
, where
P_{ss}
satisfies the discrete algebraic Riccati equation (DARE)

P_{ss}=Q+A^{\top}P_{ss}A\\
-A^{\top}P_{ss}B(B^{\top}P_{ss}B+R)^{-1}B^{\top}P_{ss}A.

(6)

This now yields the closed-loop system

\displaystyle x_{t+1}=\underbrace{(A-BK_{ss})}_{F_{ss}}x_{t}+\omega_{t}

(7)

with time-invariant linear dynamics
F_{ss}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.

Importantly, we consider the setting in which an external observer has access only to partial and noisy measurements
y_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
of the state

for inference. In particular, we consider linear Gaussian observations

\displaystyle y_{t}

\displaystyle=Cx_{t}+\nu_{t},\quad\nu_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,V)

for

, where

.
The system (

)-(

) is sometimes referred to as an

Having introduced the problem setting, we end with some notation. Let our set of parameters

be decomposed into
\Theta_{s}
the set of system parameters
\theta_{s}=\{A,B,Q,R\}\in\Theta_{s}

II-B

Problem Formulation

An external observer has access only to partial observations
y_{0:T}
of a system under control operating in closed-loop. At a high level, the question we seek to answer in this work is: strictly from observations
y
, what can be inferred about the system parameters
\{A,B,C,W,W_{0},V\}
and the control parameters
\{Q,R\}
? We will articulate most of our results on the specific interaction between
\{A,B,Q,R\}
. Non-identifiability is expected, thus we further ask: what are additional measurements or assumptions that we can use to reach identifiability?

The basis for identifiability and parameter inference is the
marginal likelihood
,

\displaystyle p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y})=\prod_{i=1}^{N}p_{\theta}\left(y^{(i)}_{0:T}\right),

(9)

for a dataset
\mathbf{y}=\{y^{(i)}_{0:T}\}_{i=1}^{N}
. We perform parameter inference under the closed-loop statistical model
p_{\theta}
presented in (
7
)-(
8
) by seeking
\hat{\theta}\in\Theta
maximizing the marginal likelihood.

To shed light into the non-identifiability, we consider the first case of the infinite horizon setting. We plot in Fig.
2
A
what such parameter inference approach can yield from observations in this case. We can infer the closed-loop parameters
F_{ss}
, but the parameters
\{A,B,Q,R\}
are not identifiable.
This can be explained from the latent dynamics probabilities, which satisfy

\displaystyle p_{\theta}(x_{t+1}\mid x_{t},u^{*}_{t})

\displaystyle=p(x_{t+1}\mid x_{t},\theta_{s},\theta_{n})

\displaystyle=p\left(x_{t+1}\mid x_{t},\mathcal{F}(\theta_{s}),\theta_{n}\right)

(10)

and are
equivalent
to the LG-SSM latent dynamics. In
App.
A

(Prop.
4
), we formalize that under the assumption that the true model is indeed
p_{\theta}
, then parameter inference in the general statistical model defined through (
7
)-(
8
) is equivalent to parameter inference in an LG-SSM. Hence performing parameter inference can be reduced to, and is in fact equivalent to, the two following steps:

1.

Infer the closed-loop parameters
F_{ss}
from partial observations
\mathbf{y}
;

2.

Investigate the interaction of the system parameters
\theta_{s}
in the closed-loop dynamics
F_{ss}
.

In the next section §
II-C
we address the first step and discuss how in practice we maximize the marginal likelihood in (
9
) and infer the closed-loop system parameters. The second step will be the topic of Sections §
III
and §
IV
for respectively the infinite- and finite-horizon settings. Finally, in Section §
V
we explore how the introduction of additional control measurements can help relax this identifiability constraint. See Fig.
2
B
for a schematic of our approach.

II-C

Closed-Loop System Recovery

A general difficulty in fitting parameters to data in state space models is that we must marginalize over latent sequences
x_{0:T}

\displaystyle p_{\theta}(y_{0:T})

\displaystyle=\int p_{\theta}(y_{0:T}\mid x_{0:T})p_{\theta}(x_{0:T})dx_{0:T}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p_{\theta}(x_{0:T})}\left[p_{\theta}(y_{0:T}\mid x_{0:T})\right]

to evaluate the marginal likelihood. For general state space models, this integration cannot be done in closed-form. Furthermore, optimizing this expression requires optimizing parameters of the support distribution of this integral. A popular approach that circumvents the latter consists of using the
expectation-maximization
(EM) algorithm
[
31
,
32
,
25
]
to maximize (
9
). The EM algorithm is centered around the following lower bound

\displaystyle\ln p_{\theta}(\mathbf{y})\geq\mathcal{Q}\big(\theta\mid\theta^{\prime}\big)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{p(x_{0:T}\mid\mathbf{y},\theta^{\prime})}\left[\ln p(x_{0:T},\mathbf{y}\mid\theta)\right]

(11)

to the marginal
log
-likelihood, for any
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta
. The algorithm can then be summarized succinctly as recursively maximizing

\displaystyle\theta^{(j+1)}\leftarrow\argmax_{\theta}\mathcal{Q}\big(\theta\mid\theta^{(j)}\big)

(12)

over iterates
j=1,2,\dots
until convergence. This algorithm has the convenient property that it guarantees monotonic increase in the lower bound
\mathcal{Q}
, but is generally highly dependent on initialization
[
33
]
. We use the EM-algorithm to maximize the likelihood in (
9
). Further details for our problem are provided in Appendix
B
.
In Fig.
3
B
, we provide estimation errors for the recovery procedures as a function of the number of EM iterations, attesting to its convergence.

In linear Gaussian state-space models, the marginal likelihood in (
9
) can in fact be evaluated in closed-form using Kalman filtering
[
29
, See §18.3.1.3]
. Nevertheless, the EM-algorithm for the LG-SSM has closed-form updates for the parameters
\theta
and remains a standard approach for its efficiency and flexibility
[
33
]
. Importantly for us, we will leverage these closed-form updates in §
V
to showcase how the inclusion of control measurements can help us further in the identification of the parameters
\theta
.

Regardless of the parameter inference methods, since the latent variables
x_{t}
are not observed our model is only identifiable up to any invertible transformation of the latent space
[
10
,
11
]
. Indeed, if we define a new latent state as
\hat{x}_{t}=Hx_{t}
for
H\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
invertible, then the closed-loop dynamics can be redefined as
\hat{F}_{ss}=HF_{ss}H^{-1}
, with dynamics covariance
HWH^{\top}
.
The topic of invariant metrics under non-identifiability of system and control parameters dates back to original work by Kalman
[
11
,
34
]
and is an active area of research.
Here, we study the relationship of the parameters
\theta_{s}=\{A,B,Q,R\}
in setting
F_{ss}
. This helps in practice to provide estimates for
\hat{\theta}_{s}=\{\hat{A},\hat{B},\hat{Q},\hat{R}\}
from an inferred
\hat{F}_{ss}
. How identifiability considerations impact the estimates
\hat{\theta}
is out of the scope of this paper. Thus, one must exercise caution when comparing parameters
estimated
(e.g.
\hat{A}
) from this procedure with
true
(e.g.
A
), generative, parameters.

III
Identifiability in Infinite Horizon

The preceding section highlighted how our higher level goal amounts to disentangling the parameters
\{A,B,Q,R\}
from measurements of the closed-loop system. The key idea is that by having direct measurements of the closed-loop, we will be able to disentangle our control gain equation (
2
) (resp. (
5
)) from our Ricatti term (
3
) (resp. (
6
)), yielding a system of two equations.

Let us first consider the infinite time-horizon setting, where we now presume to have access to a measurement of
F_{ss}
. Strictly from our control gain definition, we have

\displaystyle F_{ss}

\displaystyle=A-BK_{ss}

\displaystyle=\left(\Id-B(R+B^{\top}P_{ss}B)^{-1}B^{\top}P_{ss}\right)A

and in particular

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\left(\Id_{n}-B(R+B^{\top}P_{ss}B)^{-1}B^{\top}P_{ss}\right)^{-1}F_{ss}

\displaystyle=\left(\left(\Id_{n}+BR^{-1}B^{\top}P_{ss}\right)^{-1}\right)^{-1}F_{ss}

\displaystyle=\left(\Id_{n}+BR^{-1}B^{\top}P_{ss}\right)F_{ss}

(13)

where we used the Woodbury matrix identity to perform the inverse. Next, shifting to the DARE in (
6
), we note

\displaystyle P_{ss}

\displaystyle=Q+A^{\top}P_{ss}A-A^{\top}P_{ss}B\left(R+B^{\top}P_{ss}B\right)^{-1}B^{\top}P_{ss}A

\displaystyle=Q+A^{\top}P_{ss}A-A^{\top}P_{ss}BK_{ss}

\displaystyle=Q+A^{\top}P_{ss}(A-BK_{ss})

such that

\displaystyle P_{ss}

\displaystyle=Q+A^{\top}P_{ss}F_{ss}

(14)

These two simple derivations together show that the interaction between the different parameters of interest can be reduced to considering a new system of equations, which we make explicit in the following proposition:

Proposition 1
.

Consider the infinite horizon (stochastic) LQR problem with system parameters
\{A,B\}
and control parameters
\{Q,R\}
. Let
(A,B)
be stabilizable, and let
F_{ss}
be the closed-loop dynamics matrix.
Then, the unique solution
P
to the Sylvester equation

\displaystyle P-A^{\top}PF_{ss}

\displaystyle=Q

(15a)

satisfies the matrix equation

\displaystyle BR^{-1}B^{\top}PF_{ss}=A-F_{ss}

(15b)

Proof:

(A,B)
stabilizable ensures that
P=P_{ss}
in (
15a
) is unique, as derived from the DARE. The proof then simply follows from our derivations in (
13
)-(
14
).

\Box

An interesting implication of the stabilizability of
(A,B)
, is that since
P
in (
15a
) is unique, this in turn implies that
F^{-1}
and
-A^{\top}
(equivalently
-A
) do not share an eigenvalue.

In Prop.
1
,
F_{ss}
is,
a-priori
, the only known parameter. As such, to make any conclusions about one or a combination of parameters without neither resorting to
P_{ss}
nor constraints on our parameters, we need to assume the others to be known.

Solving for
A

First and foremost, we may wish to solve for
A
in (
15
). This is slightly more challenging, as the system exhibits second-order tendencies in
A
. To see this, consider the simplification
B=R=\Id
. In that case, we can easily solve for
P
in (
15b
) and substitute it in (
15a
), and obtain that
A
satisfies the system (
15
) if and only if

\displaystyle Q=\left(A-F_{ss}\right)F_{ss}^{-1}-A^{\top}\left(A-F_{ss}\right)

(16)

This is a second-order matrix equation in
A
, combining of a second-order self-adjoint term with a first-order term of Sylvester-transpose form. This combination is unfortunately not (readily) amenable to common second-order matrix equation techniques
[
35
]
. Instead, we elect to use an iterative approach that draws from iterative solutions to Ricatti-type second-order equations
[
36
]
.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\{B,R,Q\}
be given, as well as
F_{ss}
. Let
P_{j}
,
j=1,2,\dots
, be the (unique) positive definite solution to the algebraic Sylvester equation

\displaystyle P_{j}-A_{j}^{\top}P_{j}F_{ss}

\displaystyle=Q

(17a)

where, recursively,

\displaystyle A_{j}

\displaystyle=\left(\Id+BR^{-1}B^{\top}P_{j-1}\right)F_{ss}

(17b)

where
A_{0}
is chosen such that it does not share any eigenvalue with
-F_{ss}^{-1}
. Then

1.

P_{ss}\leqq P_{j+1}\leqq P_{j}\leqq\dots
,
j=1,2,\dots

2.

\lim_{j\to\infty}A_{j}=A

Proof:

This result follows from a similar iterative procedure in
[
36
]
. The key idea is that instead of modifying the
F_{ss}
at every step holding
A
constant, we let
A
be adjusted so that
F_{ss}
stays constant.

1.

By our assumption on
A_{0}
,
P_{0}
defined as a solution to the Sylvester equation (
17a
) is unique and well-defined.
Let
P_{j}
be well-defined. Then as
A_{j+1}
is adjusted by the second step (
17b
), itself derived from the control gain equation, it ensures that there is a control gain
K_{j+1}
such that
A_{j+1}-BK_{j+1}=F_{ss}
. This in turn ensures that
(A_{j+1},B)
is stabilizable, and that the solution
P_{j+1}\succ 0
to the DARE (
6
) of parameters
\{A_{j+1},B,Q,R\}
is unique and well defined. Then by induction, the sequence
\{P_{j},K_{j+1}\}
for
j=1,2,\dots
is well-defined. Furthermore, it satisfies the same recursive form as in
[
36
]
, and it follows from
[
36
]
that
P_{j}-P_{j+1}\succeq 0
,
P_{j}-P_{ss}\succeq 0
.

2.

Monotonic convergence of positive operators ensures that the limit for
P_{j}
exists and the lower bound and uniqueness of
P_{ss}
yields
\lim_{j\to\infty}P_{j}=P_{ss}
. Then by Prop.
1
, it holds that

.

\Box

We require
A_{0}
to not share any eigenvalue with
-F_{ss}^{-1}
. Since
(A,B)
is stabilizable and

is invertible, then
F_{ss}
has eigenvalues of norm less than 1 and greater than 0. Possible initalizations for
A_{0}
thus include
A_{0}\in\{F_{ss},\Id,0\}
.

The above result provides monotonic and quadratic convergence (see Fig.
3
A
) to the true
A
when we have access to
F_{ss}
, and know the other parameters
\{B,R,Q\}
.
In practice, one may have only a-priori estimates or imperfect measurements of any of these parameters. The proof above only holds for the true parameters, thus convergence in this setting is not guaranteed.
Nonetheless, we have found this iterative method to fare nearly just as well as a numerical root-finding algorithm based on Newton’s method, and the proof above can give insights into the well-posedness of the problem and convergence guarantees. To assess this sensitivity, we investigated the impact of adding a scalar perturbation
\epsilon\Id
,
\epsilon>0
, to either
F_{ss}
or
Q
on our estimate for
A
. We show in Fig.
3

-

that both iterative and root finding methods handle this perturbation similarly.

Solving for
B
and
R

Next, we notice that
\{B,R\}
are in direct interaction through
BR^{-1}B^{\top}
. One can solve for

\{A,Q,F_{ss}\}
by solving for the unique

\hat{B}=BR^{-\frac{1}{2}}UR^{\frac{1}{2}}
for

unitary, arbitrary. Then

\displaystyle\hat{B}R^{-1}{\hat{B}}^{\top}

Proposition 3
.

Consider the finite horizon (stochastic) LQR problem with system parameters

(A,B)

\displaystyle BR^{-1}B^{\top}P_{T}F_{T-1}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\quad A

\displaystyle=\left(BR^{-1}B^{\top}Q+\Id\right)F_{T-1}

\displaystyle P_{T-1}

\displaystyle=Q+A^{\top}P_{T}F_{T-1}

\left(A-F_{T-1}\right)F_{T-1}^{-1}=\\

Conclusions and Future Works

In the infinite horizon, we provide exact and numerical estimation procedures for one parameter given the others; with

We have investigated the identifiability of system and control parameters from partial observations, and provided two extensions that substantially improve identifiability. Future theoretical work should explore how estimation errors during system identification influence the recovery of system and control parameters, as well as data-efficient methods for parameter inference in the finite-horizon setting.

These methods provide a promising path towards domain-specific problems of estimating the control properties of partially observed neural systems. For example, the relationship between MEG sensors and the underlying neuromagnetic sources is approximately linear
[
37
]
, suggesting that our methods for system and control recovery may be helpful for understanding human neural control processes from noninvasive recordings.
