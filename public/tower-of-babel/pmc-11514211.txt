Suppression of Pcdh8/paraxial protocadherin is required for efficient neighbor exchange in morphogenetic cell movement during zebrafish notochord formation
Authors: Masatake Kai, Makoto Kondo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn certain forms of collective cell migration, changes in neighboring cells (neighbor exchange, NE) are essential. In the axial mesoderm in zebrafish, for example, the notochord is established through cell movements known as convergence and extension (C&E), which involves NE. For NE to occur efficiently, the balance between cell-scale and supracellular stresses plays a crucial role, but the molecular basis of how these stresses are controlled remains unclear. In this study, we focused on Pcdh8/Paraxial protocadherin (PAPC), which is specifically suppressed in the region (notochord) where and at the time (early gastrula) when extensive C&E occurs. Forced expression of PAPCΔC (PAPC lacking its intracellular domain) persisted in the developing notochord and resulted in morphogenetic defects in zebrafish. PAPCΔC was found to downregulate NE in the notochord in a homophilic contact-dependent manner. By examining oil droplets inserted between cells, we revealed that while cell-scale stresses were apparently unaffected, the direction of bias in the supracellular stresses was stabilized by the introduction of PAPCΔC in the notochordal region. Taken together, our results suggest that suppression of PAPC in the notochord is required to modify supracellular stresses and provide the conditions in which NE occurs efficiently, thus promoting morphogenetic cell movements.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-76762-7.Keywords: Paraxial protocadherin (PAPC), Neighbor exchange (NE), Convergence and extension (C&E), Notochord, Supracellular stressesSubject terms: Developmental biology, Morphogenesis

Introduction

Collective cell migration drives various biological processes, including morphogenesis, wound healing and cancer metastasis1. Some forms of collective migration involve changes in neighboring cells (neighbor exchange; NE). Convergence and extension (C&E), for example, is a process that reshapes a tissue into elongated and narrower structures2,3. In C&E, the cells migrate towards the midline (convergence), simultaneously intercalating with each other to extend the tissue (extension), thus changing their neighbors. C&E is known as the main driving force during notochord formation in Xenopus and zebrafish2,3. While cells constituting the tissue undergo NE through intercalation, the integrity of the tissue itself must be maintained. It is therefore proposed that the control of cell adhesion plays a key role in collective cell migration4,5.

Mongera et al. (2018)6proposed a prerequisite for the occurrence of NEs by measuring the mechanical stresses at the cellular and tissue levels at the site of unidirectional axis elongation in the zebrafish tail. Here, single cells exhibit fluctuating cell-scale stresses that drive cell rearrangements and cell mixing. On the other hand, the presomitic mesoderm exerts stresses at the supracellular scale, thereby restricting the individual cell behaviors. For NE to occur efficiently, fluctuating cell-scale stresses should exceed relatively persistent supracellular-scale stresses. In zebrafish axis elongation, Mongera and colleagues reported that while cell-scale stresses fluctuate uniformly along the axis, persistent stresses at the supracellular scales exhibit an anteroposterior gradient6. Lower supracellular stresses in the mesodermal progenitor zone determine “fluid-like” cell behavior at the posterior end and facilitate tissue remodeling. An increase in supracellular stress determines the transition to “solid-like” cell behavior in the presomitic mesoderm. The gradient of supracellular stresses was found to be dependent on N-cadherin6; however, its uniform expression in the presomitic mesoderm and tailbud7 does not explain the mechanism involved in establishing and/or maintaining the gradient.

Pcdh8/Paraxial protocadherin (PAPC) belongs to the protocadherin family, which comprises single-pass transmembrane proteins characterized by cadherin repeats in the extracellular domain8. Robust spatio-temporal regulation of PAPC during Xenopus and zebrafish embryogenesis strongly indicates its significant role in morphogenetic events. In both Xenopus and zebrafish, PAPC is expressed in the entire marginal zone at the early gastrula stage9,10. However, as gastrulation progresses, expression in the dorsal midline is quickly and strongly suppressed, demarcating a clear boundary with paraxial expression9,10. Additionally, we previously reported that in Xenopus, the PAPC protein is downregulated and excluded from the plasma membrane in the developing notochord, indicating that it is also regulated at the protein level11. An evolutionarily conserved intracellular domain (DSR domain) is involved in the regulation of PAPC, and a mutated protein that lacks the intracellular domain (PAPCΔC) strongly localizes to the plasma membrane and persists in the notochord throughout the gastrulation period in Xenopus11. Both loss-of-function (by antisense morpholino) and gain-of-function (by overexpression) of PAPC resulted in aberrant gastrulation cell movements, indicating that precise control of PAPC is essential for efficient cell behaviors9,11. Notably, PAPC is specifically suppressed at both the mRNA and protein levels in the developing notochord10,11, where C&E (and hence NE) takes place most extensively12,13. However, how the elimination of a specific cell adhesion molecule affects morphogenetic cell behavior during notochord formation remains unclear.

In this study, we explored the significance of PAPC suppression in zebrafish notochord formation. Our results suggest that PAPC may regulate the supracellular-scale stresses that lead to the restriction of NE, and its removal is required for promoting C&E.

Results

Expression of PAPCΔC disrupts morphogenesis in zebrafish embryos

We first asked whether spatio-temporal regulation of PAPC is required for normal morphogenesis in zebrafish. We took advantage of PAPCΔC, which is resistant to degradation in the notochord during gastrulation in Xenopus11. We first ubiquitously expressed EGFP-tagged full-length PAPC (PAPC-EGFP) or PAPCΔC (PAPCΔC-EGFP) in zebrafish embryos, and confirmed that the expression of PAPCΔC-EGFP persisted in the notochord at the end of gastrulation, where PAPC-EGFP was largely suppressed (Suppl. Figure 1). We postulated that deletion of the intracellular domain of PAPC leads to abrogation of downstream signaling while preserving adhesion function. When PAPCΔC was ubiquitously expressed, embryos with various degrees of morphological defects were observed at 30 h postfertilization (hpf) (Fig. 1A, B). Many had short axes, with discernible structures of the rostral (e.g., eyes) and caudal (e.g., tail) regions (Fig. 1A). In some cases, narrowing of the distance between the eyes (and occasionally cyclopia), a phenotype often associated with defects in gastrulation cell movements, was observed (Fig. 1A inset). Expression analysis of tbxta via whole-mount in situ hybridization demonstrated that mesoderm induction was unaffected by PAPCΔC expression at the shield stage (Fig. 1C). At the tailbud stage, tbxta expression in PAPCΔC-expressing embryos was found to be broader mediolaterally and shorter anteroposteriorly than that in the controls, a phenotype typical of compromised C&E (Fig. 1C, asterisks). At the same time, the mediolateral width of the neural plate, the convergence of which is closely associated with the underlying mesoderm, appeared to be broader in PAPCΔC-expressing embryos as visualized by dlx3b expression (Fig. 1C, white bar). At the 10-somite stage, myod1 expression and live embryo observation revealed that PAPCΔC expression severely affected the morphology of the axial mesoderm, typically resulting in short and undulated tissue with uneven width (Fig. 1C, Suppl. Figure 2). In contrast, the morphology of somites was relatively unaffected even in the embryos with severely deformed axial mesoderm. We measured the mediolateral length of the third somite as a proxy for C&E in the paraxial mesoderm at the 8-somite stage. We found moderate but not significant difference (p = 0.213, Student’s t-test) between uninjected (52.2 ± 4.6 μm, eight embryos from three independent experiments) and PAPCΔC-expressing (66.1 ± 18.3 μm, six embryos from three independent experiments) embryos. These results suggest that the ubiquitous expression of PAPCΔC preferentially perturbs axial mesoderm formation (Fig. 1C, Suppl. Figure 2). We concluded that persistent PAPCΔC in the notochord suppressed morphogenetic cell movements, rather than tissue specification, during zebrafish embryogenesis.

PAPCΔC expressing cells exhibit reduced cell migration in a homophilic contact-dependent manner

Cells undergoing C&E display characteristic cellular behaviors. They first align along the mediolateral axis and elongate by producing protrusions at their mediolateral edges. These polarized cells then migrate towards the midline, simultaneously intercalating between one another and thus changing their neighboring cells2. We sought to explore the effects of ectopic PAPC expression on these distinct behaviors at the cellular level. As PAPC has homophilic cell adhesion activity, we expressed PAPCΔC-EGFP in the notochord in a mosaic fashion, to discriminate between the cell-autonomous and homophilic contact-dependent (non-cell-autonomous) effects of PAPCΔC-expressing cells. To this end, embryos were injected with membrane-mCherry mRNA at the 1-cell stage for uniform expression, followed by PAPCΔC-EGFP mRNA at the 64- to 128-cell stage for mosaic expression. During gastrulation, cells expressing PAPCΔC-EGFP in the axial mesoderm (middle region in terms of the anterior–posterior axis; see Fig. 2A) were observed either in isolation or as clusters (Fig. 3A). We then tracked cells in the developing notochord during 60–90% epiboly via time-lapse confocal microscopy and extracted parameters related to cell polarization, as well as the speed, directionality and persistence of cell migration (Figs. 2 and 3).

To evaluate cell polarization, the outline of a cell (chosen at random) was first traced manually and then fitted to an ellipse using FIJI software (Fig. 2B, C). For analyses, we classified the cells into four categories: (1) cells expressing mCherry only and surrounded by cells expressing mCherry only (i.e., not in contact with PAPCΔC-expressing cells), labeled R; (2) cells expressing mCherry only and in contact with PAPACΔC-expressing cell(s), labeled gR; (3) cells expressing PAPCΔC and surrounded by cells expressing mCherry only (i.e., not in contact with another PAPCΔC-expressing cell), labeled Gr; and (4) cells expressing PAPCΔC and in contact with (an)other cell(s) expressing PAPCΔC, labeled G. We compared the axis of elongation (indicated by the angle of the major axis: cosθ, see Fig. 2D) and the circularity (as a relevant factor for cell elongation) of the cells among the four categories (R, gR, Gr, G), and found no significant differences (p = 0.79 and 0.91 respectively, one-way ANOVA) (Fig. 2E, F). We concluded that persistent PAPCΔC in the notochord did not affect the cell polarization required for C&E, either cell-autonomously or non-cell-autonomously.

To evaluate the parameters of cell migration, we tracked the cell positions every 2.5 min for two hours in the notochord. The speed of migration was determined by the sum of the travel distance divided by time (µm/min), whereas the directionality of migration was determined by the total displacement distance over the observed period divided by the sum of the travel distance. Finally, the persistence of migration was expressed as the mean of cosθ, where θ denotes the angle of directional change between time-lapse observations (see Fig. 3B). To analyze the cell-autonomous and homophilic contact-dependent (non-cell-autonomous) effects of PAPC separately, we expressed embryos with PAPCΔC-EGFP or EGFP-PAPCΔE (a construct lacking its extracellular domain and thus regarded as nonfunctional), and categorized the cells into three groups (Fig. 3A): (1) cells expressing EGFP-PAPCΔE (control), (2) cells expressing PAPCΔC-EGFP in isolation (isolated), and (3) cells within a cluster of PAPCΔC-EGFP-expressing cells (clustered). The speed of migration was significantly different among the three groups (p = 0.019, one-way ANOVA), with a significant decrease in cells forming a cluster of PAPCΔC-expressing cells as compared to control cells or PAPCΔC-expressing cells in isolation (p = 0.040 and 0.023, respectively, Tukey’s HSD test) (Fig. 3C). Compared with control cells, PAPCΔC-expressing cells in isolation presented a comparable migration speed (p = 0.724, Tukey’s HSD test) (Fig. 3C). The results demonstrated that PAPC suppresses cell migration in the notochord in a contact-dependent manner but does not change cell motility per se in a cell-autonomous manner. While persistence was comparable among the three groups (p = 0.137, one-way ANOVA; Fig. 3E), there was a significant difference in directionality (p = 0.048, one-way ANOVA; Fig. 3D). We observed a slight but not significant reduction in directionality in isolated PAPCΔC-expressing cells compared with the control (p = 0.076, Tukey’s HSD test).

PAPCΔC-expressing cells exhibit reduced neighbor exchange in a homophilic contact-dependent manner

The finding that a cluster of PAPC-expressing cells showed reduced migration in a tissue that undergoes extensive C&E led us to ask whether PAPC is involved in NE events. For this purpose, we expressed embryos ubiquitously with mCherry and interspersedly with PAPCΔC-EGFP, and randomly selected and tracked pairs of cells that were in direct contact within the axial mesoderm (Fig. 4A). An NE event was recorded when the contact was abolished by the intercalation of a third cell (Fig. 4B, Suppl. Video). We then compared the frequency of NE events in three types of cell pairs: (1) pairs of cells expressing mCherry only (control–control), (2) pairs of cells expressing mCherry only and expressing PAPCΔC-EGFP (control–PAPCΔC-EGFP), and (3) pairs of cells expressing PAPCΔC-EGFP (PAPCΔC-EGFP–PAPCΔC-EGFP). A significant difference in NE was detected among the three types (p = 0.039, one-way ANOVA) (Fig. 4C). Furthermore, while the NE rates of the control–control and control–PAPCΔC cell pairs were comparable (p = 0.95, Tukey’s HSD test), the NE rate of the PAPCΔC–PAPCΔC cell pairs was significantly lower than that of the control–PAPCΔC cell pairs (p = 0.048, Tukey’s HSD test), and to a certain extent, lower than that of the control–control cell pairs (p = 0.071, Tukey’s HSD test) (Fig. 4C). This result indicates that the homophilic cell adhesion activity of PAPC compromises NE events during C&E, which may account for the reduced migration of cells within a cluster of PAPCΔC-expressing cells.

PAPC may regulate supracellular stresses, but not cell-scale stresses, in the developing notochord

It has been proposed that the occurrence of NE is governed by the balance between supracellular- and cell-scale-stresses6. To determine whether PAPC regulates these stresses, we inserted a mineral oil droplet among cells during zebrafish gastrulation (Fig. 5A-H) and used its outline contour as surrogate information for cellular forces. On average, the droplet was ~ 80 pl in volume and in contact with ~ 10 cells on its largest perimeter (Fig. 5I, J). The sizes (of the arc and chord) of occasional “dents” that appeared on the perimeter corresponded to the sizes of single cells (Fig. 5K). We postulated that the general outline of the droplet, as fitted to an ellipse (Fig. 5L), represents the supracellular stresses, such that the magnitude and direction of any bias in the supracellular stress should be estimated by the circularity and angle of the major axis of the ellipse, respectively. We also hypothesized, on the other hand, that the local deformation of the outline represents the cell-scale stresses, and their sizes can be estimated by the distance between the fitted ellipse and the actual local “dent” or “regression” in the contour.

We tracked the droplets every 5 min for 1.5 h during mid-gastrulation, in the axial mesoderm (where the endogenous PAPC is eliminated) or paraxial mesoderm (where the endogenous PAPC is highly expressed), in the control and PAPCΔC-overexpressing embryos. We first compared the angles of the major axis of the oil, a proxy for the direction of bias in supracellular stresses, under different conditions (Fig. 6A–D). Compared with those in the paraxial mesoderm, angles in the axial mesoderm of the control embryos were significantly more randomized over time (0.005 < α < 0.01, Mardia–Watson–Wheeler test) (Fig. 6A, B). The introduction of PAPCΔC to the axial mesoderm reduced randomness (α < 0.005, Mardia–Watson–Wheeler test) (Fig. 6A, C). In contrast, no significant change in the angle of the major axis was observed in the paraxial mesoderm between the control (with high endogenous PAPC expression) and PAPCΔC-expressing conditions (0.05 < α < 0.975, Mardia–Watson–Wheeler test) (Fig. 6B, D). These results suggested that the presence of cell-adhesion activity by PAPC stabilized the direction of the supracellular stresses.

The general circularity of the oil indicated that the degree of bias in the supracellular stress was fairly persistent during the observed period, regardless of the region (axial or paraxial mesoderm) or presence of PAPC activity (control or PAPCΔC overexpression) (Fig. 6E, F). At the same time, consistent with observations in the tail region6, the cell-scale stress fluctuated rapidly, as observed by the local changes in the oil contour (Fig. 7A–D). This cell-scale behavior was independent of the region (axial or paraxial) or PAPCΔC expression (p = 0.22, one-way ANOVA), implying that PAPC may not be involved in regulating cell-scale stresses. Overall, we propose that the exclusion of PAPC from the axial mesoderm is necessary for optimizing supracellular stresses to facilitate NE in morphogenetic cell movements.

Discussion

In this study, we expressed a mutated form of PAPC that lacks the intracellular domain (PAPCΔC) in zebrafish embryos. PAPCΔC was resistant to degradation in the axial mesoderm, where endogenous PAPC is suppressed, leading to aberrant morphogenesis (Fig. 1). PAPCΔC-expressing cells presented a decreased migration speed when in a cluster and a reduced NE rate in a homophilic contact-dependent manner (Figs. 3 and 4). This finding coincided with the observation that PAPCΔC expression modified supracellular stresses in the axial mesoderm by stabilizing the direction of their biases (Fig. 6). These results indicate that the suppression of PAPC in the axial mesoderm may play a pivotal role in the transition of cellular conditions in the region at the early gastrula by optimizing supracellular stresses for effective NE to occur and thus facilitating C&E for normal morphogenesis (summarized in Fig. 8).

A schematic summary of this study. PAPC lacking its intracellular domain (PAPCΔC) induced in zebrafish embryos was resistant to degradation in the axial mesoderm and caused morphogenetic defects (short axis). In the developing notochord, PAPCΔC-expressing cells exhibited a decrease in migration speed as well as in neighbor exchange (NE), an essential cell behavior for morphogenetic cell movements (convergence and extension; C&E). Furthermore, PAPCΔC expression was found to modify supracellular stress in the notochord region. These results suggest that spatio-temporal suppression of PAPC may be required for optimizing the balance between cell-scale and supracellular stresses to facilitate morphogenetic cell behavior in early embryogenesis.

The characteristic expression patterns of PAPC in early embryos have led researchers to extensively study its roles in early embryogenesis. The proposed processes regulated by PAPC during morphogenesis include tissue separation14,15, coordination of cell polarity16, modulation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling17,18, and modulation of Wnt/planar cell polarity signaling19. Here, we propose a hitherto unexplored function of PAPC in morphogenetic cell movements, through which it may control the efficiency of NE by regulating supracellular stress. These findings provide a new insight into the molecular mechanisms underlying morphogenetic cell movements during zebrafish embryogenesis.

In the developing zebrafish during somitogenesis, while the magnitudes of fluctuating cell-scale stresses are consistent along the anterior–posterior axis, persistent supracellular-scale stresses show a gradient, with yield stress being greater in the anterior region and lower in the posterior region, providing conditions for fluid-like cell behaviors in the elongating tail region6. The gradient of supracellular stresses was shown to be N-cadherin dependent, since it was abrogated in the cdh2−/−mutant6. However, the expression pattern of cdh2during somitogenesis7 does not seem to correspond with the supracellular stress gradient, thus the molecule that directly dictates the gradient and hence the cell behaviors (solid- or fluid-like) in this context remains unclear. In the case of PAPC in the axial mesoderm, it is rapidly and strongly downregulated at both the mRNA and protein levels, at the time and place of rigorous changes in cell behaviors, i.e. the commencement of extensive C&E. It is therefore plausible that PAPC functions as the bona fide determinant of behavioral transition in the axial mesodermal cells. In Xenopus gastrulae, depletion of PAPC in the prechordal mesoderm was found to alter cell–cell contact patterning, such as a reduction in interstitial gaps20. It is thus interesting to determine whether the cell–cell contact pattern changes in the axial mesoderm prior to the onset of C&E, and if so, whether this change is dependent on the suppression of endogenous PAPC.

Gain-of-function studies of PAPC via ubiquitous expression are fraught with a caveat, since the PAPC protein is actively degraded in the developing notochord11 (Suppl. Figure 1). To elucidate the significance of PAPC degradation, we chose to express PAPCΔC, in which the extracellular cadherin repeats and the transmembrane portion are retained but the intracellular domain is deleted. We confirmed that PAPCΔC is strongly localized to the plasma membrane and is resistant to degradation in the axial mesoderm in zebrafish (Suppl. Figure 1) as we previously reported in Xenopus11. Our previous results from Xenopus also showed that both PAPC and PAPCΔC expression led to morphogenetic defects, with PAPCΔC constantly showing more severe effects11. Consistent with these findings, forced ubiquitous expression of PAPC in zebrafish resulted in a short axis with broader and shorter notochord at the tailbud to 10-somite stages, with apparently lower penetrance than PAPCΔC expression (Suppl. Figure 2). As both the ubiquitous expression of PAPC and PAPCΔC had minor effects on the morphology of somites (Fig. 1C, Suppl. Figure 2), it is unlikely that PAPCΔC functionally disrupted endogenous PAPC which is abundant in the paraxial mesoderm. These results indicate that in early zebrafish morphogenesis PAPCΔC may function as a stable version of PAPC, presumably through meditating cell–cell interactions. Thus, the use of PAPCΔC enabled us not only to maintain its expression in the notochord but also to exclusively investigate the role of its cell adhesion activity without its function as a signaling molecule. This may partly explain why our expression of PAPCΔC had no apparent effect on cell polarity (Fig. 2E, F), whereas PAPC has been shown to regulate cell polarity via RhoA and JNK16. Likewise, in addition to regulating supracellular stress, the exclusion of PAPC in the axial mesoderm may play pleiotropic roles via its intracellular signaling activity.

In our investigation of cell migration, we revealed a significant decrease in the speed of cells in a the PAPCΔC-expressing cell cluster. This effect was dependent on homophilic cell–cell contact, since the speed of PAPCΔC-expressing cells in isolation was comparable to that of control cells (Fig. 3C). As the NE rate was reduced between pairs of PAPCΔC-expressing cells (Fig. 4C), we reasoned that the decrease in speed could be attributed to impaired cell–cell intercalation in a PAPCΔC-expressing cell cluster, although the possibility that PAPCΔC expression affects epiboly cannot be excluded. Besides cell polarity, the source of directional cues in C&E remains elusive. In lateral cells in zebrafish gastrula, a gradient of Ca2+/cadherin-mediated cell–cell adhesiveness determined by Bmp signaling guides their dorsal migration21. This leads to the assumption that PAPC might also regulate cell movements through heterophilic interactions with classical cadherins (e.g. C-cadherin15).

Owing to technical limitations, we could not quantitfy cell-scale and supracellular stresses as Mongera et al. did in their seminal work6. Nevertheless, we observed an important ‘change’ in the supracellular stresses caused by PAPCΔC expression in the axial mesoderm, as manifested by stabilization of the direction of bias (Fig. 6A, C). These findings strongly suggest that the cell adhesion activity of PAPC influences supracellular stress. For cell-scale stresses, our analysis was supposed to essentially reflect the actomyosin-dependent cortical tension of the cells, and we did not observe overt alterations in their dynamics upon PAPCΔC expression (Fig. 7). Given that the propensity for neighbor exchange is dependent on the balance between the cell-size and supracellular stresses, the ‘change’ in the supracellular stresses caused by PAPCΔC must have provided a cellular milieu that restricted the NE in the axial mesoderm. The simplest scenario is that in the control axial mesoderm the supracellular stress is lower than that in the PAPCΔC-expressing tissue, which makes it more sensitive to slight fluctuations in its direction. However, the exact nature of the modification of supracellular stresses by PAPC as well as the role of PAPC in cell-scale stresses that might have eluded our analyses remain to be elucidated.

Protocadherins are a large subgroup of the cadherin superfamily, with more than 70 genes found in mice8. Pcdh8/PAPC belongs to a nonclustered subgroup (Pcdhδ2), and in addition to their roles in development (early and neuronal), these molecules have been shown to play roles in various neurological disorders as well as tumor suppression22,23. For example, human PCDH8 was found to be inactivated in a high fraction of breast carcinomas24. PCDH8 is involved not only in the proliferation, but also in the migration of breast epithelial cells24. Growing evidence suggests that protocadherins function as signaling molecules that modulate Wnt pathways23, which may account for their role in controlling cell proliferation. Our results suggest that the cell adhesion and signaling activities of protocadherin could be separable and that in the regulation of cell migration the latter may be, at least in part, dispensable. These findings may lead to novel tumor therapies based on protocadherins, where metastasis is controlled without complicating their proliferative properties.

Methods

Ethics statement

All animal experiments were carried out in accordance with institutional and national guidelines and regulations. All protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Osaka Metropolitan University. This study was performed in compliance with the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines.

Fish husbandry and embryo collection

Adult zebrafish (Danio rerio) were maintained under normal laboratory conditions, with a 14/10 h light/dark cycle. The fertilized eggs were obtained by natural mating. The embryos were kept in tank water supplemented with methylene blue, at 28–30 °C according to the application.

Microinjection of mRNAs

cDNAs for mCherry tagged with a membrane-targeting CAAX motif (membrane-mCherry), PAPCΔC tagged with EGFP (PAPCΔC-EGFP)10, EGFP-PAPCΔE, in which the extracellular cadherin repeat was replaced with EGFP, and full-length PAPC tagged with EGFP (PAPC-EGFP) were cloned and inserted into the pCS2+ plasmid, and the mRNAs were obtained by in vitro transcription. For ubiquitous expression, embryos were injected at the 1-cell stage with approximately 20 pg of mRNA(s). To express PAPCΔC in a mosaic fashion, embryos were cultured to the 64- to 128-cell stage and the mRNA (20 pg) was injected into the yolk just beneath the blastomeres.

Classification of morphogenetic phenotypes

The morphological phenotypes of the embryos were determined at 30 hpf. Embryos were scored for normal, short (estimated axial length < 80% of normal embryo, with discernible eye and tail), or other (including loss of dorsal and/or ventral structures) phenotypes. The “short” phenotype was further classified into mild (50–80% axial length) and severe (< 50%) (see Fig. 1A, B).

In situ hybridization

The full-length cDNAs of tbxta (NM_131162.1), dlx3b (NM_131322.2) and myod1 (NM_131262.2) were obtained via RT-PCR and subsequently cloned and inserted into the pBluescript II SK(+) plasmid. Anti-sense RNA probes labeled with digoxygenin were synthesized by in vitro transcription using DIG RNA Labeling Mix (Roche, 11277073910). Whole-mount in situ hybridization using probes for tbxta (expressed in the axial chorda mesoderm), dlx3b (expressed in the neural plate border) and myod1(expressed in the paraxial mesoderm and somites) at the shield, tailbud and 10-somite stages was carried out according to previous methods25. The embryos were either uninjected or injected with mRNA for membrame-mCherry, PAPCΔC-EGFP or PAPC-EGFP. Embryos fixed at the 10-somite stage were treated for 45 s with 10 µg/mL proteinase K (Roche, 3115836001) prior to probe hybridization. Images were collected with a Leica M165 FC microscope with a Leica DFC310 FX digital camera. For embryos at the 10-somite stage, the yolk was removed, and the specimens were mounted flat for microscopy (see Fig. 1C and Suppl. Figure 2 A).

Image collection of live embryos

Live embryos at the 10-somite stage were dechorionated manually, and embedded in 3% methylcellulose in tank water and subjected to microscopy. Images were collected with Leica M165 FC microscope with Leica DFC310 FX digital camera (See Suppl. Figure 2B).

Confocal microscopy

For confocal microscopy, embryos were embedded in 0.8% low-gelling-temperature agarose (Sigma, A9414) on a glass-bottom dish (Matsunami, D910400) at the shield stage, with the shield facing the glass with a slight tilt anteriorly (Fig. 2A). Images were obtained via an LSM700 (Zeiss) with a 20x objective. In a typical setting, the pinhole size was 1 AU, the scanning speed was 6 (without averaging), the laser powers were 1% for both Diode555 and Diode448, and the zoom was 1.3. For time-lapse experiments, images were captured at 30 °C, every 2.5 min for two hours for cellular analyses (Figs. 3 and 4) and every five minutes for 1.5 h for oil droplet analyses (Figs. 5, 6 and 7).

Analyses of cell polarization

The embryos were injected with membrane-mCherry mRNA at the 1-cell stage, and subsequently with PAPCΔC-EGFP mRNA at the 64- to 128-cell stage. On confocal microscopy during the gastrula stage, cells in the axial mesoderm (see Fig. 2A) were categorized into four groups on the basis of their expression of PAPCΔC as well as their contact with (an)other PAPCΔC-expressing cell(s): (1) cells expressing mCherry only and surrounded by cells expressing mCherry only (labeled R), (2) cells expressing mCherry only and in contact with PAPACΔC-expressing cell(s) (labeled gR), (3) cells expressing PAPCΔC and surrounded by cells expressing mCherry only (labeled Gr), and (4) cells expressing PAPCΔC and in contact with (an)other PAPCΔC-expressing cell(s) (labeled G). Fifteen cells from three independent experiments were randomly selected for analysis from each category. The outline of the cell was traced manually and fitted to an ellipse with FIJI software. The angle of elongation was defined as the cosine of the angle between the major and equatorial axes. Circularity was calculated from the lengths of the major and minor axes (minor/major) (see Fig. 2).

Analyses of cell migration

PAPCΔC mRNA was injected at approximately the 64- to 128-cell-stage to express PAPCΔC either in a cluster of cells or in a mosaic fashion to evaluate the effect of homophilic contact between PAPCΔC-expressing cells. As a control, we expressed EGFP-PAPCΔE, a version of EGFP-tagged PAPC that lacks the extracellular cadherin repeats, as it is related to PAPCΔC but rationally assumed to lack its homophilic cell–cell contact activity. Starting from approximately 60% epiboly, the cells were randomly selected, and their positions (defined by the center of the nucleus) were manually tracked every 2.5 min for two hours via FIJI software. Fifteen cells from three independent experiments were analyzed. From the tracking data, three parameters (speed, directionality and persistence) were extracted. Speed was defined as the sum of the travel distance divided by time (µm/min), whereas directionality was the total displacement distance (between the first and last timepoints) divided by the sum of the travel distance. To determine persistence, the angles of cell movement between timepoints (t0 and t1, t1 and t2, etc.) were measured (θ0, θ1, etc.). Neighbor exchange rate

The embryos were injected with membrane-mCherry mRNA at the 1-cell stage, followed by PAPCΔC-EGFP mRNA at the 64- to 128-cell stage. Images of the axial mesoderm were collected every 2.5 min for two hours during gastrulation by confocal microscopy. Pairs of cells in direct contact were chosen and tracked for as long as they were within the field of view. Neighbor exchange was recorded when a third cell appeared between the original cell pair. Fifteen to 20 cell pairs per embryo were analyzed respectively for pairings expressing mCherry/mCherry (control/control), mCherry/EGFP (control/PAPCΔC) or EGFP/EGFP (PAPCΔC/PAPCΔC). The rates of occurrence of NE were obtained from three independent experiments (comprising a total of fifty cell pairs) (see Fig. 4).

Analyses of supracellular and cell-scale stresses

The embryos were injected with membrane-mCherry mRNA with or without PAPCΔC-EGFP mRNA at the 1-cell stage and cultured until approximately the dome to 30% epiboly stage. To insert an oil droplet in axial or paraxial mesodermal cells, up to ten droplets of mineral oil (Sigma, M5904) were delivered into the cellular layer at the marginal zone. The average volume of droplets was approximately 80 pl. The injected embryos were further cultured until the shield stage and examined for the precise region and depth of the droplet positions. Embryos with a droplet in either the axial or the paraxial mesoderm region were selected and subjected to confocal microscopy. Images were captured every five minutes for 1.5 h, starting at approximately 60% epiboly. The outline of the oil droplet was traced manually and fitted to an ellipse via FIJI software (see Fig. 5).

We based our analysis of mechanical stresses on the work of Mongera et al6. They used the droplets’ ellipsoidal deformation to detect mechanical stresses at supracellular scales, as well as the deviations of the droplets’ shapes from ellipsoidal deformations to detect cell-sized stress inhomogeneities. In this study, the fitted ellipse was used to evaluate the biases in supracellular stresses; the ratio between the major and minor axes (circularity) for their magnitude, and the angle of the major axis for their direction. As the orientations of the specimens varied among the experiments, the angle of the major axis was normalized as follows: At each timepoint, the angle of the major axis and the ratio between the major and minor axes (major/minor) were plotted as a vector. The average vector from one experiment was calculated, and the angle of each timepoint was normalized so that the direction of the average vector points toward 90° (approximately parallel to the anterior–posterior axis) (see Fig. 6A–D). Changes in the magnitude of biases over time were determined every 15 min by the circularity of the fitted ellipse (length of the minor axis divided by the major axis) (see Fig. 6E, F).

To evaluate the cell-scale stresses, the distance between the fitted ellipse and actual trace (D) was used. Deformation in the outline was randomly chosen, and D0 was determined for the corresponding cell. The Ds for the same cell were subsequently determined at the timepoints five minutes before (D−5) and after (D+5). The fluctuations in the cell-scale stresses was expressed as the fold change in D in two five-minute-intervals, i.e., D−5/D0 and D+5/D0. Three cases were analyzed from each of three independent experiments (see Fig. 7).

Statistical analyses

The results of cell morphology (axis of cell elongation, cell circularity), cell migration (speed, directionality, and persistence), the neighbor exchange rate and changes in cell-scale stresses were analyzed via one-way ANOVA with a post-hoc Tukey’s HSD test (Figs. 2E and F, 3C–E, 4C and 7). The distributions of the direction of bias in the supracellular stresses were compared via the Mardia–Watson–Wheeler test, a nonparametric test for homogeneity on two or more samples of circular data that has been used in other studies for direction analysis (Fig. 6A–D). In Figs. 2, 3, 4 and 6 and N indicates the number of clutches used, which is equivalent to the number of embryos used. n indicates the number of cells (Figs. 2 and 3) or number of cell pairs (Fig. 4) used.
