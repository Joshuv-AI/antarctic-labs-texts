Development of a questionnaire to better understand verbal violence among college students: preliminary results from a cluster random sample
Authors: Yanrong Zou, Yiping Li, Zilong Li, Xinqi Yu, Ailou Liu, Xiaodan Zeng, Chengwei Shao, Ying Huang, Ruiming Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study developed the College Student Verbal Violence Questionnaire and conducted a questionnaire survey using this instrument to comprehensively understand verbal violence among college students.MethodsA cross-sectional design was adopted to conduct a collective survey on three samples through Wenjuanxing (an online survey platform). We selected four higher vocational colleges in Guangzhou, China, for the sampling population. A cluster random sampling method was used to obtain data from 1,092 students. Item analysis and exploratory factor analysis were conducted on this sample, followed by a confirmatory factor analysis and reliability and validity testing with 713 students. Finally, a questionnaire survey on verbal violence was conducted involving 1,102 students.ResultsThe College Student Verbal Violence Questionnaire comprises 19 items across five dimensions: Verbal Violence Evaluation, Duration of Verbal Violence Impact, Verbal Violence Behaviors, Emotions Related to Verbal Violence, and Family Verbal Violence. Internal consistency coefficients of the overall scale and each dimension ranged from 0.750 to 0.946, and the test–retest reliability coefficients ranged from 0.742 to 0.936. The questionnaire demonstrated good content, structural, discriminant, and convergent validities. The results indicated that the level of verbal violence experienced by current college students was higher than the moderate level. Female students scored significantly higher than male students in the dimensions of Verbal Violence Evaluation, Duration of Verbal Violence Impact, Verbal Violence Behaviors, Emotions Related to Verbal Violence, and the total score of the questionnaire. College students from urban areas scored significantly higher than those from rural areas in the Emotions Related to Verbal Violence dimension. A positive correlation was observed between the level of verbal violence and mental health.ConclusionsThe College Student Verbal Violence Questionnaire is an initially promising tool for measuring verbal violence among college students. This study demonstrated that the level of verbal violence among college students positively correlated with their mental health. These findings help to accurately understand and analyze verbal violence among college students, which can help government education departments to prioritize and address the issue, thereby preventing it. However, additional research is required in the future to further enhance the tool’s validity evidence.

Background

“Verbal violence,” “peer violence,” and “sports injuries” are the three major campus injury issues that students believe need to be addressed urgently [1]. Among these, verbal violence has not received formal attention because it does not involve obvious physical harm; its process is quite insidious and ambiguous, often lacking clear evidence [2].

At present, no unified definition of verbal violence exists. French psychologist Marie-France Hirigoyen [3] believes that verbal violence is an out-and-out form of mental and psychological abuse. Karni-Vizer and Salzer [4] state that verbal violence refers to individuals’ violent behavior that involves the use of uncivilized language, such as abuse, contempt, and ridicule; it causes pain or harm to the mental and psychological states of those that experience said violence. Bourdieu [5] posits that language is a form of gentle violence that serves as a tool or medium for power relations. Jinxin [6] perceives verbal violence as an act of violence wherein an individual from a position of advantage vents aggression toward others during the language exchange process. Infante and Wigley [7] assert that verbal violence refers to an aggressive personality. Therefore, we infer that verbal violence encompasses violent behavior in which an individual—during the process of language communication with others—takes advantage of their own superiority and uses uncivilized language such as abuse, slander, and ridicule to cause mental and psychological harm and pain to others. The five most common forms of verbal violence are: being frequently interrupted while speaking, having one’s decisions and suggestions criticized and rejected, having no opportunity to prove oneself, not getting a response from others, and being ignored or treated as a “transparent person.” Verbal violence can be divided into ironic, insulting, threatening, prophetic, and commanding types [8].

Verbal violence is extremely prevalent on college campuses and can cause hidden and serious harm to the victims. A survey on campus verbal violence demonstrates that 91.1% of students have experienced verbal violence [9]. Compared with physical violence, the harm stemming from verbal violence to students is less noticeable, but its impact is relatively serious. Research has found that victims of verbal violence are most likely to have cognitive anticipation reactions and negative self-cognition tendencies [10]. Verbal violence is significantly correlated with anxiety and depression [11, 12]. Both verbal violence and physical violence in the workplace increase the risk of depression among ride-hailing drivers [13–16]. College students, as a special social group, are in a crucial stage of physical and mental development. In terms of cognition, their thinking is gradually transitioning from formal logical thinking to dialectical logical thinking, and there may still be cognitive biases [17]. In terms of emotions, they possess strong self-esteem and self-awareness [18], rich but unstable emotions, and are easily influenced by external factors. In terms of social interaction, due to the lack of effective communication skills and emotional management abilities, they are more inclined to experience problems related to verbal violence.

At present, there are many measurement tools for evaluating verbal violence; however, two problems persist. First, they are too underdeveloped, treating verbal violence as a whole without paying attention to its sub-dimensions. Second, they focus too much on a certain sub-dimension of verbal violence and are lacking in the description of other dimensions. In the research on students’ aggressive behavior [19–22], conflict behavior [23, 24], campus bullying behavior [25], and psychological violence [26, 27], some studies regard verbal violence, physical violence, and cyber violence as a whole and do not pay attention to verbal violence alone. Among the four dimensions of the Buss–Perry Aggression Questionnaire [19], verbal violence is a dimension. This questionnaire not only does not pay attention to the sub-dimensions of verbal violence but is also designed for people with aggressive tendencies or traits, which does not quite conform with the cognition of normal people. The Conflict Tactics Scale Short Form [23, 24] also has a sub-scale for verbal aggression among its five sub-scales, but it focuses on the occurrence of violent behaviors between intimate partners and is not a sub-scale that focuses on verbal violence itself. In addition to the aforementioned scales that only treat verbal violence as a sub-scale, some studies have only conducted research on one dimension of verbal violence. For example, Teicher et al. [28] used the Verbal Violence Questionnaire to survey 554 students who had experienced verbal violence in childhood. This study judged the severity of an individual’s experience of verbal violence based on the severity of verbal violence behaviors, ignoring factors such as the emotions of verbal violence and the duration of its impact [28]. The Verbal Aggressiveness Scale compiled by Infante and Wigley [7, 29] conceptualizes verbal violence as a personality trait of attacking others, focusing on the aggressive behaviors toward others during the process of interpersonal communication. This study equates verbal violence with aggressive behavior. Thus, the aforementioned studies either discuss verbal violence together with aggression, conflict, and bullying behaviors in a condensed manner, or equate verbal violence with verbal violence behaviors or aggressive behaviors; this approach lacks paying comprehensive attention to verbal violence and its sub-dimensions.

Most of the previously-cited literature is based on Western culture and may differ from the Chinese cultural context [30]. Chinese culture emphasizes “collectivism” and “harmony is precious,” while Western culture emphasizes “individualism” and “personality expression” [31]. Collectivism emphasizes the connection between individuals and others [32, 33], prioritizes collective goals instead of individual interests [34], and enables individuals to possess the ability to restrain oneself and sacrifice personal interests for others [35, 36]. Compared to an individualistic culture, people in a collectivistic culture exhibit a higher ability to empathize [37, 38]. Therefore, when verbal violence occurs, people in collectivistic cultures often begin from the interests of others, ignore the responsibilities of others, analyze the reasons for verbal violence on themselves from their own shortcomings, and then remain silent about its occurrence. In individualistic cultures, people often begin to decipher it from their own interests and attribute the occurrence of verbal violence to others. Therefore, relevant foreign research on verbal violence cannot be directly applied to China, let alone directly applied to Chinese college students.

Among the measurement tools for verbal violence in China, three questionnaires are prevalently used to research teachers’ verbal violence [39], college students’ cyber verbal violence [40], and children’s family verbal violence [41]. However, the above measurement tools have not undergone exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), lack reliability and validity tests, and their verifiability is questionable. Therefore, compiling a questionnaire on verbal violence, specifically for Chinese college students, is necessary.

According to China’s educational system, after completing 9 years of compulsory education, students embark on differentiated pathways in secondary education: those with excellent academic performance enroll in regular senior high schools, where the core focus rests on systematic learning of academic knowledge; students with relatively lower academic performance attend secondary vocational high schools, which emphasize the foundational training of skilled talents. Both groups of students in these two types of secondary schools complete a three-year senior high school education before proceeding to higher education at different levels based on their scores in the National College Entrance Examination (Gaokao). The hierarchical training model continues in higher education: regular institutions of higher education primarily aim to impart advanced theoretical knowledge, with a typical academic duration of four years. However, regular higher vocational colleges focus on skill and technical training, with a general academic duration of 3 years. These two groups of students collectively form the college student population in China.

This study focuses on students from regular higher vocational colleges, who account for more than 50% of the total enrollment in higher education, but have long remained an underrepresented group in terms of social attention. College students in regular higher vocational colleges are in the critical developmental stage of transitioning from campus to society, with not yet fully mature cognitive systems, emotional regulation abilities, and behavioral patterns. Factors such as academic pressure, strained interpersonal relationships, and loss of emotional control may trigger verbal violence behaviors [42, 43].

Social cognitive theory posits that behavior arises from dynamic interactions between individual factors (such as cognition and emotions), behavior itself, and environmental influences [44]. Similarly, the emergence of verbal violence among college students can be attributed to complex interactions between students’ internal cognition, emotions, verbal violence behaviors, and their social environment.

Cognition serves as the foundation for individuals’ understanding and judgment of events. In the context of verbal violence, college students’ cognitive level of verbal violence directly influences their behavioral tendencies and coping styles. Studies have demonstrated significant differences in attitudes toward online verbal violence and perceptions of its harm among college students, with female students scoring significantly higher than their male counterparts [42]. Overall, college students’ cognition of online verbal violence incidents is relatively rational, but a small proportion exhibits apathetic moral emotions and vague cognition of online verbal violence [45].

Emotional issues triggered by verbal violence are particularly prominent among college students. Verbal violence significantly increases victims’ levels of social loneliness, depression, and anxiety [46], and may even lead to self-harm or suicidal behaviors, with such impacts exhibiting long-term effects [47, 48]. Online verbal violence also causes emotional disturbances in bystanders, reducing their positive emotions and increasing negative emotions [49]. A long-term follow-up study revealed that compared with non-victims of campus violence, individuals who experienced campus violence may still suffer from higher levels of psychological maladjustment and physical health problems after completing their studies [50].

Verbal violence behaviors among college students are diverse and concealed [51]. The popularization of the Internet has led to the increasing prevalence of online verbal violence [52], such as malicious comments on online forums and private message harassment. In offline scenarios, behaviors such as mocking in classrooms, cold remarks in dormitories, and belittling in daily communication all cause significant psychological harm to victims. Research indicates that male students experience more verbal aggression and privacy infringement than female students [53].

As the primary environment for individual growth, the experience of family verbal violence exerts an undeniable influence on verbal violence among college students. Bandura argues that “the family is the main venue for children’s socialization, and many of their behaviors are acquired through observation”; social behaviors are learned in social contexts through four stages: attention, retention, motor reproduction, and motivation [54]. Ehrensaft et al.’s [55] 20-year empirical study revealed that individuals who witnessed or heard parental domestic violence during childhood were three times more likely to engage in violent behavior in adulthood compared to those who did not. Studies have demonstrated that the gray matter volume of the left superior temporal gyrus is significantly positively correlated with the level of parental verbal aggression. The left superior temporal gyrus gray matter volume in students who experienced parental verbal violence during childhood increased by 14.1% compared to those who did not [56].

Time is a crucial indicator for measuring the depth and persistence of harm caused by verbal violence. Black et al. [57] indicates that children growing up in long-term violent environments are prone to developing learned domestic violence behaviors, forming the intergenerational transmission of family violence, which may extend into their own nuclear families in adulthood. Neuroscience research reveals that abnormalities in the inferior longitudinal fasciculus structure of the left occipital lobe in individuals are associated with the duration of witnessing parental verbal violence during childhood and the degree of exposure to verbal violence between the ages of 7 and 13 [58]. Individuals who suffered family verbal violence in childhood may still be affected negatively in adulthood, wherein harm manifests as self-cognitive biases and interpersonal relationship disorders [59]. College students experience a critical period of personality development and psychological maturation, and the long-term impacts of verbal violence may hinder their healthy growth.

In addition, different viewpoints on the influencing factors of verbal violence exist. Certain research draws on the interpersonal metafunction theory of systemic functional linguist Halliday [60], analyzing the adolescent verbal violence in parent–child relationships, cross-sex relationships (between males and females), and teacher–student relationships from the perspectives of the tone, cognition, and responsibility of violent language; a study revealed that parental status, childhood experiences, interpersonal communication, and personal personality comprise the primary factors influencing adolescent verbal violence behaviors [2].

In general, relevant research predominantly evaluates the aggressiveness and conflict of individuals by considering verbal, physical, and cyber violence as a whole, or simply equating verbal violence with aggressive behavior. The research does not study verbal violence alone, and the existing measurement tools are difficult to directly apply to Chinese college students. Therefore, an urgent need to develop a questionnaire on the current situation of verbal violence suitable for Chinese college students persists.

Considering the prevalence and severity of verbal violence among college students, as well as limitations in existing research and assessment tools, the present study aimed to develop a questionnaire on the current status of verbal violence applicable to Chinese college students. Grounded in social cognitive theory, this study incorporates multiple dimensions, including Verbal Violence Evaluation, Verbal Violence Behaviors, Emotions Related to Verbal Violence, Duration of Verbal Violence Impact, and the influence of Family Verbal Violence.

The primary objectives of this study were to develop a verbal violence questionnaire specifically for Chinese college students, comprehensively understand the current status of verbal violence among this population, and explore its relationship with mental health. We aimed to provide initial validity evidence for this questionnaire and theoretical foundations for mental health education and verbal violence interventions in colleges and universities.

Methods

Development of the college student verbal violence questionnaire

Questionnaire development process

The questionnaire was developed in four stages. The first stage involved reviewing relevant domestic and foreign literature on verbal violence, and compiling questionnaire items on college students’ verbal violence accordingly. The questionnaire designed comprises five dimensions: Verbal Violence Evaluation, Emotions Related to Verbal Violence, Verbal Violence Behaviors, Impact Environment of Verbal Violence, and Duration of Verbal Violence Impact. There were 11 items in total in the dimension on evaluation of verbal violence: “Your relationship with your father, mother, teachers, and classmates” (four items), “Your daily Internet usage frequency” (one item), “Do you use violent language in your daily life?” (one item), and “What are the impacts of the verbal violence from parents, classmates, teachers, the Internet, and individuals?” (five items). There were seven items in total for the emotions related to verbal violence dimension: “Judging the impacts of verbal violence on individuals as a perpetrator, bystander, and victim of verbal violence” (three items) and “The degree of one’s own feelings when teachers, classmates, parents, and online media use verbal violence against the individual” (four items). Duration of Verbal Violence Impact comprises five items: four items address the question “How long does it take for you to forget after experiencing verbal violence from others (e.g., teachers, classmates, parents, or online individuals)?”; and one item focuses on “How long does it take for you to forget after you have used verbal violence?” The Impact Environment of Verbal Violence dimension includes six items, such as “Have you ever suffered from verbal violence from different groups (teachers, classmates, parents, the Internet)?” The Verbal Violence Behaviors dimension comprises a total of 12 items: “What coping methods do you adopt when others use verbal violence against you?” (four items), “What is the greatest impact on you when others use verbal violence?” (four items), and “What do you think are the reasons for others to use verbal violence?” (four items). The 41 questions from these five dimensions were developed from five aspects: verbal violence itself, family (parents, relatives), school (teachers, peers), society (media, the Internet), and the individual.

In the second stage, three professors of psychology, two associate professors of psychology, and two doctoral students were invited to evaluate the content validity of the questionnaire. After the expert review, unanimous agreement led to the exclusion of the following open-ended questions: “What coping methods do you adopt when others use verbal violence against you?” (four items) and “What do you think are the reasons for others to use verbal violence?” (four items). The experts also recommended deleting four items whose expressions overlapped with the previous and subsequent items. As per the experts’ suggestions, in total, 12 questions were deleted. Accordingly, a questionnaire on college students’ verbal violence was formed with 29 items. The questionnaire adopted a five-point scoring system; the higher the score, the higher the degree of college students’ verbal violence.

The third stage involved surveying 1,167 college students with the questionnaire evaluated by experts, conducting item analysis, deleting eight items with a correlation coefficient lower than 0.3, and then conducting EFA on the remaining 21 items. Two items with factor loadings less than 0.4 and communality less than 0.4 [61] were deleted. The final formal questionnaire assessing college students’ verbal violence contains 19 items across five subscales.

The fourth stage was to verify the goodness-of-fit of the five-factor model through CFA and to assess the reliability and validity of the questionnaire through internal consistency coefficient, discriminant validity, and convergent validity.

Participants

In this study, the method of cluster random sampling was adopted, and students from four higher vocational colleges in Guangzhou City, Guangdong Province, China were selected as the sampling population. In each college, we conducted cluster sampling with classes as the unit, randomly selecting several classes from the freshman group, across different majors. A total of 3,312 college students were surveyed. After excluding responses from 216 people with repeated student ID numbers, 151 people whose answering time was less than 30 s or longer than 1,800 s, and 62 people with incomplete questionnaire data, 2,907 valid questionnaires were finally recovered—with an overall effective recovery rate of 85.45%. Among the finally included participants, 1,543 were male students and 1,564 female students; 1,764 were from rural areas and 1,343 were from urban areas.

Before administering the questionnaire survey, we designed an informed consent form. Four teachers from four higher vocational colleges explained the purpose and methods of this study in detail to the students in class. We emphasized that participants could voluntarily take part in this study, that they could withdraw at any time during the research process without suffering any adverse effects, and that their information would be kept strictly confidential. During the teachers’ explanation, students were encouraged to ask questions and seek clarification at any time from the teachers. Thereafter, students who voluntarily agreed to participate in this study were asked to complete the informed consent form and scan the QR code to anonymously join the questionnaire survey group (either Sample 1 or Sample 2).

All participants could only take part in the questionnaire survey of either Sample 1 or Sample 2; participating in the questionnaire survey twice was strictly prohibited. During the questionnaire survey process, we used the student ID numbers as identification information and reminded them several times that they should only complete the questionnaire once. Therefore, during the screening process of the questionnaire data, we kept only one set of responses for those with duplicate student numbers. Finally, we issued a practical activity certificate to the students who participated in the questionnaire survey, which could help them get extra points in the final exam.

In Sample 1, a total of 1,167 freshmen from four higher vocational colleges in Guangzhou were surveyed through Wenjuanxing (an online survey platform) using the cluster sampling method. We deleted 38 responses with duplicate student numbers, 23 cases with response times less than 30 s or longer than 1800 s, and 14 cases with incomplete questionnaire responses. Finally, 1,092 valid questionnaires were collected, with a valid rate of 93.6%. Among the finally included participants, 546 were male students and 546 were female students; 625 students were from rural areas and 467 from urban areas.

In the Sample 2 survey group, a total of 856 students from four higher vocational colleges in Guangzhou were randomly surveyed through Wenjuanxing using cluster sampling. We deleted 79 responses with duplicate student numbers, 48 responses with response times less than 30 s or longer than 1800 s, and 16 incomplete questionnaire responses. A total of 713 valid questionnaires were collected, with a valid rate of 83.3%. Among the finally included participants, 353 were male students and 360 were female students; 394 students were from rural areas and 319 from urban areas.

To ensure that the retest sample was from Sample 2, two weeks after the survey of Sample 2, we distributed the retest questionnaire to the subjects in Sample 2. To ensure the validity of the retest, we emphasized on the purpose and importance of this survey at the beginning of the questionnaire once more, encouraging students to answer carefully. Simultaneously, to minimize the impact of external factors on the retest results, we chose to conduct the retest at a time and in an environment similar to that of the first administration of the survey.

To link participants before and after, we used student numbers as identification information. During the first survey administration, we recorded the basic information of each participant, including their student numbers. During the retest, students were required to provide their student numbers; we matched the response information of the two administrations using the student numbers. For questionnaires with repeated student numbers, we only retained one valid response. Accordingly, we were able to accurately associate and analyze the responses of the same participant before and after, to evaluate the test–retest reliability of the questionnaire. This time, 224 questionnaires were collected. We deleted 10 cases with duplicate student numbers, eight cases with response times less than 30 s or longer than 1800 s, and six cases with incomplete questionnaire responses. Finally, 200 valid questionnaires were collected, with a valid rate of 89.3%. Among the participants, 90 were male students and 110 were female students; 115 students were from rural areas, and 85 from urban areas.

Sample 3: A questionnaire survey was conducted on 1,289 students from four higher vocational colleges in Guangzhou City through Wenjuanxing on the Internet with classes as the unit. These students had not participated in the previous three questionnaire surveys. After deleting 89 responses with repeated student numbers, 72 people whose answering time was less than 30 s or longer than 1,800 s, and 26 people with incomplete questionnaires, 1,102 valid questionnaires were recovered, with an effective rate of 85.5%. Among the finally included participants, 554 were male students and 548 female students; 630 students were from rural areas and 472 from urban areas; 1,006 were freshmen, 24 were sophomores, and 72 were juniors. The educational attainment of the fathers was junior high school and below for 612 people (55.54%), high school for 276 people (25.05%), junior college for 114 people(10.34%), undergraduate for 91 people(8.26%), and postgraduate and above for 9 people(0.82%); the educational attainment of the mothers was junior high school and below for 717 people (65.06%), high school for 215 people (19.51%), junior college for 98 people(8.89%), undergraduate for 66 people(5.99%), and postgraduate and above for 6 people(0.54%).

Research instruments

Questionnaire on college students’ verbal violence

In the aforementioned four consecutive stages, the questionnaire on college students’ verbal violence was developed and verified. The final version of the scale comprises 19 items and 5 subscales, namely the Verbal Violence Evaluation (items 1–5), Duration of Verbal Violence Impact (items 6–10), Verbal Violence Behaviors (items 11–14), Emotions Related to Verbal Violence (items 15–17), and Family Verbal Violence (items 18–19). This questionnaire used a five-point Likert scale: the higher the score, the more serious the level of verbal violence suffered by the participants.

Symptom Checklist-90 (SCL-90) questionnaire

Compiled by Derogatis et al. [62] in 1973, the SCL-90 Questionnaire is chiefly used to assess whether a person possesses certain psychological symptoms and the severity of these symptoms. It includes 10 factors such as somatization, obsessive–compulsive symptoms, interpersonal sensitivity, depression, anxiety, hostility, phobia, paranoia, psychoticism, and others, with a total of 90 items. It uses a five-point Likert scoring system, where 1 indicates no symptoms and 5 indicates severe symptoms. The higher the total score, the more serious the mental health problem. In this study, the reliability of the SCL-90 Questionnaire was 0.965.

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the School of Psychology, South China Normal University (No. SCNU-PSY-2024-143). Written informed consent was obtained from all research participants involved in this study.

Data analysis

The collected data were analyzed using SPSS Version 26.0. Descriptive statistics were presented as means (M) and standard deviations (SD). Independent samples t-tests, one-way analysis of variance, and nonparametric tests were conducted based on participants’ demographic information.

Nonparametric tests do not rely on the assumption that data follow a specific distribution. They exhibit greater robustness for data with unbalanced sample sizes, unequal variances, or non-normal distributions, thereby more reliably reflecting the true differences between groups. In the current study, the sample distribution of participants’ personal background variables (including grade, major, and parental education level) was highly unbalanced. Additionally, verbal violence-related data under certain conditions exhibited unequal variances and extremely small sample sizes, making it difficult to meet the sample distribution assumptions required for parametric tests. Therefore, the Kruskal–Wallis test (a nonparametric method) [63] was employed for the overall analysis when examining differences across grades, majors, and parental education levels. If significant differences were identified, pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction [64] were further conducted to clarify the specific group differences. After Bonferroni correction, a statistically significant difference between two groups was only determined when the adjusted significance value (adj. significance) was < 0.05 [65]. This approach effectively avoided the inflation of Type I errors caused by multiple testing and ensured the rigor of the research conclusions.

In the item analysis, descriptive statistics were calculated for the 29 items after the initial screening of the questionnaire. The total scores of the verbal violence questionnaire were ranked from high to low. The top 27% of the participants were assigned to the high-score group, and the bottom 27% to the low-score group. An independent-sample t-test was performed on the scores of each item to examine the discriminative power of each item in the questionnaire.

To measure the consistency between each item of the questionnaire and its overall theme, the correlation coefficient between the score of each item and the total score was calculated. A high correlation coefficient indicates that the item is consistent with the overall measurement direction of the questionnaire and can be well integrated into the entire measurement system. Conversely, a low correlation coefficient (e.g., less than 0.3) [66] indicates that the item may exhibit a weak association with the concept of verbal violence measured by the whole questionnaire. Therefore, items with a correlation coefficient lower than 0.3 were deleted.

The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure and Bartlett’s test of sphericity were used to evaluate the suitability for factor analysis. The KMO measure ranges from 0 to 1 and is used to assess the proportion of variance among variables that could potentially exhibit common variance. A KMO value greater than 0.70 is considered suitable for factor analysis [67]. Bartlett’s test of sphericity can reveal the underlying structure among variables and test whether there is a significant correlation between variables. When p < 0.05, it indicates that the variables are correlated and that factor analysis is appropriate [68].

In conducting the factor analysis, we employed the principal axis factoring method to extract factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 without prespecifying the number of factors, and Kaiser normalized oblique rotation was used. Principal axis factoring is a factor extraction technique based on communalities [69]. By extracting factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, it focuses on exploring the underlying common factors among variables, which is more aligned with the core goal of factor analysis in investigating latent structures. Kaiser normalized oblique rotation, which allows correlations between factors, enhances the clarity and interpretability of the factor structure through normalization and optimization algorithms, while also preserving the genuine associative information among factors. It is a commonly used factor rotation strategy that balances theoretical rationality and data fit [70, 71].

CFA was performed on the data of Sample 2 using Mplus 8.3. The core estimation method employed in the analysis was maximum likelihood with robust standard errors (MLR). MLR can provide robust standard errors and chi-square test statistics when data do not meet the normality assumption, making it more suitable for the questionnaire-based social science data utilized in this study. The indices used to evaluate model fit include the CFI, TLI, RMSEA, and SRMR. Generally, CFI and TLI values greater than 0.9 indicate a good model fit, and RMSEA values less than 0.08 and SRMR values less than 0.05 imply a satisfactory model fit [72].

Convergent validity (measured by Average Variance Extracted [AVE]) and composite reliability (CR) were used for validity analysis. The AVE reflects the average proportion of variance of the items belonging to a factor that can be explained by the factor. When the AVE values of all factors in the questionnaire are greater than 0.5, it means that each factor can explain more than half of the variance of its corresponding items, indicating that the questionnaire can effectively measure different dimensions. CR measures the internal consistency and reliability of a factor. When the CR values of all factors in the questionnaire are greater than 0.7, it demonstrates that the items under each factor exhibit high reliability and stability. CR and AVE values complemented each other and jointly proved the effectiveness of the questionnaire in measuring each factor [73].

Results

Analysis of the results of the questionnaire on college students’ verbal violence

Item analysis

The total scores of the verbal violence questionnaire for all participants in Sample 1 were calculated and sorted from high to low. The top 27% were considered the high group, and the bottom 27% were considered the low group. Independent-sample t-tests were conducted to compare the scores of each item between the high and low groups. The results demonstrate significant differences in the scores between the high and low groups for each item (p < 0.01), indicating that the 29 items of the questionnaire had good discriminant validity.

The correlation coefficients between each item and the total score on the College Student Verbal Violence Questionnaire were calculated. Eight items with correlation coefficients below 0.3 (items 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 14, 15, and 17) were removed; the correlation coefficients of the remaining items with the total score ranged from 0.314 to 0.704. Factor analyses

EFA

Initial tests indicated that the data were suitable for factor analysis: KMO = 0.850, df = 210; Bartlett’s test of sphericity χ² = 10706.882, p < 0.001. Therefore, an initial EFA was conducted on the remaining 21 items of the College Student Verbal Violence Questionnaire. Without prespecifying the number of factors, principal axis factoring was used to extract factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, resulting in a total of five factors. The cumulative variance explained by these factors was 56.595%. Items were screened and factors were determined based on the criteria that factor loadings should be no less than 0.4 and communalities should exceed 0.4. Ultimately, two items (items 1 and 16) with factor loadings and communalities below 0.4 were removed, leading to the final version of the questionnaire comprising of 19 items.

A second EFA was conducted on the remaining 19 items. The results indicate that the data were suitable for this analysis: KMO = 0.853, df = 171; Bartlett’s test of sphericity χ² = 10588.32, p < 0.001. Five factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 were obtained, accounting for a cumulative variance of 61.411%.

In the EFA, the Family Verbal Violence dimension contained only two items, which is fewer than the conventional standard of four items [70, 71]. These two items focused on parental derogatory language, with factor loadings both exceeding 0.70, high communalities, and a correlation coefficient (r) greater than 0.60; Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.734, indicating good internal consistency. Similarly, although the Emotions Related to Verbal Violence dimension included only three items, their factor loadings all exceeded 0.50, with high communalities and a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.723. Considering that these two-factor structures were clear, highly homogeneous, and aligned with current societal concerns about verbal violence, we raised the eigenvalue extraction threshold to > 1.2 for stricter screening and ultimately retained these two factors.

A Harman’s single-factor test was conducted to examine common method bias [74]. The results demonstrated under unrotated conditions; five factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 were extracted; and the variance explained by the first factor was 28.952%, which is below the critical standard of 40%. Therefore, no serious common method bias was observed in this study.

Based on the EFA results, the five factors of the College Students’ Verbal Violence Scale were categorized as follows: Verbal Violence Evaluation (focusing on individuals’ assessment and judgment of the consequences of verbal violence from different sources); Duration of Verbal Violence Impact (focusing on the duration of emotional or psychological recovery after verbal violence incidents); Verbal Violence Behaviors (focusing on individuals’ explicit behavioral responses when experiencing verbal violence); Emotions Related to Verbal Violence (focusing on the emotional experiences of different roles in verbal violence, including perpetrators, bystanders, and victims); Family Verbal Violence (focusing on experiences of parental verbal violence in family settings). The final scale consists of 19 items in total. Further details are delineated in Table 2.

CFA

In accordance with the model fit index examination standards proposed by Wen et al. [75], and using the data of Sample 2, a measurement model was established, by comparing the five-factor model obtained in the EFA against the four other models (i.e., four-factor, three-factor, two-factor, and single-factor models). The rationale for constructing these alternative models (i.e., the basis for merging different factors) is as follows:

According to Frijda’s (1986) emotion theory [76], emotions are essentially action tendencies, serving as the internal psychological drivers of behaviors, whereas behaviors are the external manifestations of emotions; both belong to the “immediate response system of individuals to verbal violence.” Thus, they were merged into an emotion–behavior response factor to form a four-factor model.

Gross’s [77, 78] emotion regulation theory indicates that emotions related to verbal violence and verbal violence behaviors reflect individuals’ immediate responses to verbal violence (where emotions trigger behaviors), whereas the duration of verbal violence impact focuses on the sustainability characteristics of responses (where residual emotions and behavioral outcomes jointly determine the duration of recovery). These three factors collectively constitute a complete dynamic chain of immediate response–sustained impact, and were therefore, integrated into a response process factor to form a three-factor model.

Based on Bronfenbrenner’s [79] ecological systems theory, family verbal violence, as a form of verbal violence exposure in specific contexts (the family microsystem), directly shapes individuals’ emotional response patterns (e.g., anxiety) and behavioral strategies (e.g., avoidance) through proximal processes such as parental verbal abuse; this indicates significant associations between exposure contexts and response characteristics. Meanwhile, Weiner’s [80] attribution theory notes that verbal violence evaluation is an independent cognitive process (focusing on consequence assessment) with clear conceptual boundaries distinct from exposure and response. Thus, the first four factors were merged into an exposure–response integration factor, while verbal violence evaluation was retained as an independent factor to form a two-factor model.

The five factors focus on evaluative judgment (Verbal Violence Evaluation), duration (Duration of Verbal Violence Impact), behavioral response (Verbal Violence Behaviors), emotional experience (Emotions Related to Verbal Violence), and family context exposure (Family Verbal Violence), respectively. They possess distinct conceptual connotations and clear boundaries, which should inherently encompass independence. Therefore, the single-factor model (where all factors are merged) exists only as a comparative model. These results support the adequacy of the five-factor model.

The results indicated that the five-factor model exhibited the best fit to the data: χ²(142) = 459.963, TLI = 0.939, CFI = 0.949, RMSEA = 0.056, SRMR = 0.035. As the degree of factor merging increased, model fit substantially declined. For instance, the CFI and TLI of the four-factor model approached the marginally acceptable range, while the indices of the three-factor and simpler models were all far below the standard thresholds. In contrast, only the five-factor model achieved favorable levels of fit, with CFI (0.949) and TLI (0.939) meeting good fit criteria, and RMSEA (0.056) and SRMR (0.035) within acceptable ranges. These results fully validate the uniqueness and necessity of the five-factor model in terms of both theoretical conceptualization and data adaptability, confirming that the division into five factors is justified through both theoretical and empirical evidence (Table 4).

Discussion

This study developed the College Student Verbal Violence Questionnaire to explore the current state and factors influencing verbal violence among college students. Based on a literature review, preliminary questionnaire development, expert evaluation, item analysis, EFA, and CFA, the questionnaire developed in this study exhibits good reliability, validity, and model fit.

Development of the college student verbal violence questionnaire

At present, there is a lack of research on verbal violence among college students [81] and a shortage of scientifically valid measurement tools to assess it. After reviewing the literature [19, 21, 82], a questionnaire was initially developed based on five dimensions. Items within these dimensions were developed from five aspects: verbal violence, family (parents, relatives), school (teachers, peers), society (media, Internet), and individuals. After expert evaluation, a formal questionnaire consisting of 29 items was developed. Item analysis showed significant differences in scores between the high and low groups for each item (p < 0.01), indicating good discriminant validity. After removing eight items with a total correlation coefficient below 0.3, the first EFA was conducted, which extracted five factors with eigenvalues greater than 1. However, during the analysis, it was found that Item 1 had a communality of 0.143, and Item 16 had a communality of 0.126, both of which were below the criterion value of 0.4. Therefore, these two items were removed. A second exploratory factor analysis was conducted, which extracted five factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 and a cumulative variance of 61.411%. The final College Student Verbal Violence Questionnaire consisted of 19 items and five factors: evaluation, emotion, behavior, impact duration, and family verbal violence. Further CFA revealed that the five-factor model had good fit indices (χ²(142) = 459.963, TLI = 0.94, CFI = 0.95, RMSEA = 0.056, SRMR = 0.035), indicating good structural validity [72].

The internal consistency coefficients of the College Student Verbal Violence Questionnaire and its dimensions ranged from 0.750 to 0.946, and the test–retest reliability coefficients ranged from 0.742 to 0.936, indicating good reliability and stability. All factor AVE values of the questionnaire were greater than 0.5, the CR values were all greater than 0.7, and the square roots of the AVE values for all factors were greater than the correlation coefficients of the factors with other factors, indicating good convergent and discriminant validity [73].

In summary, the College Student Verbal Violence Questionnaire meets psychological measurement standards in terms of reliability and validity, and can be used as a measurement tool for assessing verbal violence among college students.

Current status of college student verbal violence according to the college student verbal violence questionnaire

This study found that the total average score of the College Student Verbal Violence Questionnaire was 3.145, and the average scores of each dimension ranged from 2.529 to 3.637, indicating that the current level of verbal violence suffered by college students is above the moderate level, which is consistent with the results of previous studies [20]. A moderately high level means that in the daily life and interpersonal communication of college students, the frequency or degree of verbal violence is relatively high [9, 83], which may have a certain impact on their mental health. College students who are exposed to verbal violence for a long time may develop emotional problems such as low self-esteem, anxiety, and depression [14, 15], and these problems will affect their self-cognition and self-esteem levels [10]. Simultaneously, verbal violence may also affect their interpersonal skills, leading to social withdrawal or aggressive behavior in interpersonal communication [84]. In addition, verbal violence may also interfere with the learning state of college students, making it difficult for them to concentrate on their studies, thus affecting their academic performance [85], which requires great attention.

In the College Student Verbal Violence Questionnaire, the Verbal Violence Evaluation dimension yielded the highest score (M = 3.637). This dimension focuses on individuals’ assessment of the consequences of verbal violence from diverse sources (classmates, parents, teachers, social networks, and personal interactions), indicating that in college students’ subjective cognition, the impact of verbal violence—regardless of the source group—is evaluated as relatively significant. Such high evaluation may reflect college students’ sensitivity to the consequences of verbal violence: they generally recognize that verbal violence exerts significant effects on individual psychology, interpersonal relationships, and other aspects.

Additionally, the mean score of the Verbal Violence Behaviors dimension in the questionnaire was 3.514, which was higher than the overall mean score. This indicates that when college students encounter verbal violence incidents, they may exhibit relatively distinct behavioral tendencies (e.g., emotional reactions or coping behaviors) [27]. For example, due to the loss of emotional control, they may utter aggressive remarks and display aggressive behaviors.

As primary measures, first, schools can strengthen popular science education on the consequences of verbal violence. Through courses or lectures, students can be helped to cognitively understand the impacts of verbal violence more rationally; “verbal violence is not a ‘trivial matter’” can be reinforced to reduce the tendency to trivialize it. Second, targeted coping skill training can be implemented to guide students to adopt constructive behavioral responses (such as emotional regulation and effective communication) when encountering verbal violence, instead of passively enduring it or escalating conflicts.

The present study found that Verbal Violence Evaluation, Duration of Verbal Violence Impact, Verbal Violence Behaviors, Emotions Related to Verbal Violence, and the total score on the College Student Verbal Violence Questionnaire exhibited significant differences in terms of sex, with male students scoring significantly lower than female students (p < 0.001), which is inconsistent with previous research [7]. This may be attributed to the fact that female students tend to pay more attention to interpersonal cues and emotional details during the socialization process [86]. This high sensitivity enables them to detect subtle verbal aggression more easily (e.g., sarcasm, belittlement) and evaluate its consequences more severely. Females are more inclined to adopt “emotion-focused coping” strategies, which may prolong the duration of negative experiences, thereby illustrating the significant difference in the “Duration of Impact” dimension. Additionally, it could be attributed to the substantial increase in females’ aggressive behaviors across all dimensions except physical aggression after entering adolescence, leading to significantly higher levels of anger, hostility, and verbal aggression compared to males [22]. Differences in research focus may contribute to this inconsistency. Previous studies have primarily explored violence (including verbal violence, physical violence, and sexual harassment) experienced by doctors in the workplace [14], perceptions of workplace violence [27, 87], preventive measures, as well as college students’ coping responses to aggressive behaviors (including verbal and physical aggression, anger, and hostility) [20]. In contrast, the present study solely focuses on the current status and severity of verbal violence experienced by college students. Ecological systems theory emphasizes that individual development is influenced by their microsystem, mesosystem, exosystem, and macrosystem [78]. Regarding gender differences, social culture—as part of the macrosystem—imposes different gender role expectations on men and women. Traditional patriarchal beliefs in China emphasize that men should be “tough and resilient” to avoid being easily harmed [88], which inhibits the overt expression of individual vulnerability or emotional distress [89]. This may lead men to suppress behavioral responses to verbal violence (e.g., avoiding conflicts or downplaying feelings), resulting in lower scores on the Verbal Violence Behaviors dimension. In contrast, women’s behaviors of expressing emotions and seeking support are more socially acceptable, making their behavioral responses more observable.

In terms of student origin, except for a weakly significant difference in the Emotions Related to Verbal Violence dimension (p < 0.05), no statistically significant urban–rural differences were obtained in other dimensions or the total questionnaire score, with extremely small effect sizes across all dimensions. This urban–rural similarity is consistent with previous research [90], indicating that the influence of urban–rural background on college students’ experiences of verbal violence is generally limited.

According to social cognitive theory [44, 91], as urban and rural college students converge in terms of campus social environments and information access channels, their learning patterns regarding verbal violence also tend to be similar. Urban college students scored slightly higher on the emotional dimension, which may be related to subtle differences in the “culture of emotional expression” in their growth environments: urban families and social cultures typically encourage direct emotional expression (e.g., attention to and discussion of psychological feelings), while rural cultures may emphasize “forbearance” or “pragmatism” [92, 93]. Such socialization differences may make urban college students more likely to perceive and report emotional fluctuations caused by verbal violence. However, the extremely small effect size indicates that this difference has not yet led to substantial differentiation in experiences.

With the popularization of higher education and strengthening of urban–rural exchanges, the campus social environments (e.g., peer interactions, teacher–student relationships) and information access channels (e.g., online socializing, media exposure) of rural and urban college students have gradually converged. The primary sources of verbal violence (classmates, teachers, and online platforms) are common for both urban and rural college students, leading to natural consistency in core dimensions such as their evaluation of violence consequences and behavioral responses—this is the core reason for the lack of significant differences in most dimensions.

Therefore, publicity and education can be used to enhance college students’ awareness of the consequences of verbal violence and improve all students’ emotional regulation and conflict resolution abilities. With respect to the weak difference in the emotional dimension, adequate attention can be paid to the emotional support needs of rural college students. Mental health courses can encourage emotional expression to reduce potential emotional suppression caused by the “culture of forbearance.”

Significant grade differences were observed in the Verbal Violence Evaluation (p < 0.01) and Verbal Violence Behaviors (p = 0.05) dimensions, while no significant grade differences were obtained in other dimensions or on the total questionnaire score (p > 0.05). Pairwise comparisons (with Bonferroni correction) revealed that in the Verbal Violence Evaluation dimension, the rank mean of first-year students (559.8) was significantly higher than that of second-year students (398.94, adjusted p = 0.041). This indicates that first-year students’ subjective assessment of the consequences of verbal violence (e.g., judgments on interpersonal and psychological impacts) was generally stronger than that of second-year students.

The reason for this may be that first-year students—having just entered university—face an unfamiliar campus environment and interpersonal relationship patterns (e.g., peer interactions, teacher–student communication), and pay more attention to interpersonal relationships [94]. They are more inclined to interpret verbal conflicts (such as sarcasm or belittlement) as a denial of their own adaptability, thus evaluating the consequences of verbal violence more severely. After one year of campus adaptation, second-year students have become familiar with interpersonal rules and increased their tolerance for common verbal frictions (such as playful teasing), no longer overestimating them as “violent behaviors,” leading to naturally lower subjective evaluations.

In the Verbal Violence Behaviors dimension, the rank mean of third-year students (637.56) was negligibly higher than that of first-year students (546.25; adj. p = 0.053), indicating that third-year students’ overt behavioral responses (e.g., coping or expressive tendencies) when encountering verbal violence may be more evident than those of first-year students. This may be because third-year students face practical pressures such as internships, employment, and further education, leading to more frequent verbal conflicts in interpersonal interactions involving interest competition (e.g., employment or internship opportunities) [95]. Meanwhile, after two years of campus life, students are more inclined to express dissatisfaction directly (e.g., arguing or justifying), making their behavioral responses more observable. Additionally, the extremely small sample size of second-year students (24 participants) may have reduced statistical power, resulting in the difference between third-year and first-year students not reaching strict significance (adj. p = 0.053); thus, cautious interpretation is warranted.

Based on the characteristics of grade differences, a “stage-specific precision intervention” educational strategy can be adopted: for first-year students, organizing special lectures on “Freshmen Interpersonal Adaptation and Verbal Violence Recognition” can help them distinguish between “normal frictions” and “harmful speech,” avoiding evaluation biases caused by over-sensitivity. For third-year students, offering “Workplace Communication Skills” courses can teach them how to prevent the escalation of verbal conflicts in high-pressure scenarios (e.g., internship competition, team collaboration, etc.) and reduce aggressive responses; providing one-on-one career counseling to alleviate pressures of further education and employment, thereby reducing verbal violence behaviors caused by accumulated stress at the source.

Kruskal–Wallis tests on the College Student Verbal Violence Questionnaire and its dimensions revealed significant differences across majors in all dimensions as well as in the total questionnaire score (p < 0.01 or p < 0.001), except for the Family Verbal Violence dimension. This is consistent with previous research [96], which demonstrated that students majoring in law scored significantly higher in multiple dimensions than those majoring in other subjects.

The reasons may be as follows: the training model, learning content, and thinking patterns of students majoring in law have a notable impact on their verbal sensitivity and interaction styles. Legal education centers on training in “linguistic logic” and “argumentation and reasoning” [97]. Law students are required to accurately identify implicit information such as rights, obligations, and responsibilities in verbal expressions. This professional competency makes them more sensitive to aggressiveness in speech (e.g., belittlement, negation), leading to more severe evaluations of the consequences of verbal violence (high scores in the evaluation dimension).

Law courses frequently involve conflict scenarios such as disputes, torts, and debates. Students’ long-term exposure to verbally confrontational content [98] may lead them to transfer this “professional context” to daily interactions, resulting in a stronger perception of verbal conflicts and more pronounced emotional experiences (emotional dimension) and behavioral responses (behavioral dimension). Additionally, law students may pursue careers as lawyers or judges in the future, and the professional expectation to “uphold fairness and oppose injustice” may strengthen their response intensity to “verbal injustice” (e.g., discriminatory language), thereby prolonging the duration of verbal violence impact (Duration of Verbal Violence Impact dimension).

Kruskal–Wallis test results examining the relationship between parental education levels (both mother and father’s) and scores on the College Student Verbal Violence Questionnaire dimensions and total score revealed no significant differences across all dimensions or the total score (p > 0.05)—regardless of whether parental education level was measured by mother or father’s education.

The reasons may be as follows: the primary sources of verbal violence experienced by college students are campus-based (e.g., classmates, teachers) and through online environments, instead of being limited solely to the family. Parental education levels may primarily influence interaction patterns within the family; however, college students’ experiences of verbal violence have expanded to broader social contexts, where the influence of extra-familial factors may overshadow potential differences originating within the family.

Higher parental education does not necessarily equate to “non-violent parenting”; nor does lower education inevitably imply “high verbal violence tendencies.” Parenting styles are shaped by multiple factors, including individual personality, family culture, and social environment. Education level is merely one variable and cannot independently determine patterns of verbal interaction, which may explain the absence of a significant statistical association in the results.

The total score on the College Student Verbal Violence Questionnaire was significantly positively correlated with the total score of the SCL-90 Questionnaire, indicating that a higher overall level of verbal violence experienced by college students is associated with an increased risk of mental health problems. This study revealed that the total score on the questionnaire was significantly positively correlated with scores on the depression and anxiety dimensions of the SCL-90 Questionnaire, which is consistent with existing research [11, 12]. Additionally, the study revealed that the dimensions of Duration of Verbal Violence Impact, Emotions Related to Verbal Violence, and Family Verbal Violence were significantly positively correlated with both the total SCL-90 score and its subdimensions. This indicates that students who experience long-term verbal violence (or family verbal violence) may develop psychological issues such as anxiety, depression, and low self-esteem, resulting in lower mental health levels [99].

The underlying reasons may be as follows: Prolonged exposure to a linguistic environment characterized by negation and belittlement can gradually damage an individual’s self-cognitive system, leading to a continuous decline in self-worth. Such persistent psychological stress activates the body’s stress response system, causing neuroendocrine dysregulation, which in turn impacts the brain’s emotional regulation centers and cognitive functional regions [57–59]. Meanwhile, long-term psychological stress also affects the body’s immune system and physiological functions, leading to various physical discomfort symptoms. That verbal violence is not merely a linguistic conflict but a behavior that exerts severe negative impacts on individual mental health is evident [100]; this explains the significant correlation between the Duration of Verbal Violence Impact dimension and the somatization dimension of the SCL-90.

Limitations and prospects

This study consisted of a stand-alone examination of college students’ verbal violence using the College Student Verbal Violence Questionnaire. The final determination of the five factors in the questionnaire included dimensions similar to previous research (e.g., emotion, evaluation) and incorporated emerging important topics in the field of verbal violence (e.g., family verbal violence). The development of the questionnaire was innovative in both theoretical and practical terms.

In the present study, the number of items under the two dimensions Emotion of Verbal Violence and Family Verbal Violence is less than four, which may compromise the stability of construct validity. Additionally, the tendency toward convergence in the wording of questionnaire items could potentially have affected the common variance. We recommend refining the questionnaire by expanding the sample size in future research, increasing the number of core items for each dimension, and enhancing the differential expression of items.

This study only selected certain college students from four particular higher vocational colleges in the Guangdong Province as the test subjects, limiting the group of regular undergraduate students. The sample was limited to Guangdong Province, and the potential influence of regional, cultural, and educational disparities on verbal violence was not accounted for. This limitation restricts the generalizability of the findings to populations with significant economic and cultural differences (e.g., students from central/western regions or key universities) and precludes comparisons of urban–rural disparities or educational tiers in shaping patterns of verbal violence. Subsequent studies should expand the sample scope to include different provinces and cities across the country, involve students from regular undergraduate institutions, and increase comparative studies among urban and rural areas, as well as different educational levels (such as key universities and private universities). This will allow the exploration of the differences in the manifestations of verbal violence under different regional and cultural backgrounds.

This study only collected data at the same time point through the questionnaire method; therefore, it was unable to reveal the dynamic changes or causal relationships of verbal violence (such as the time-series association between verbal violence and mental health). Moreover, this study relied on the subjective reports of the participants, which may be affected by the social desirability effect (such as concealing violent behaviors) or recall bias. In the future, experimental methods can be employed to explore the influencing factors and mechanisms of college students’ verbal violence (such as parenting styles, family social status, and self-esteem) and the causal relationships between different influencing factors and college students’ verbal violence. Longitudinal follow-up research methods can also be used to explore the long-term development trends of verbal violence and understand the formation mechanism of college students’ verbal violence. Additionally, mixed research methods (such as interviews, observations, and experiments) can be adopted to explore the methods of preventing college students’ verbal violence from multiple perspectives. For example, designing and implementing mental health education courses, training programs, or psychological counseling initiatives can enhance college students’ awareness of and ability to cope with verbal violence, while fostering their emotional management and interpersonal skills. Concurrently, studying the effectiveness and applicability of different intervention measures can provide schools and societies with effective intervention strategies and methods.

Conclusions

Through literature review, expert evaluation, item analysis, as well as EFA and CFA, this study designed the College Student Verbal Violence Questionnaire comprising 19 items with a five-factor structure, including dimensions of Verbal Violence Evaluation, Duration of Verbal Violence Impact, Verbal Violence Behaviors, Emotions Related to Verbal Violence, and Family Verbal Violence. The internal consistency coefficients and test–retest reliability coefficients for each dimension of and the total score on the questionnaire all meet psychometric standards. The questionnaire yielded good reliability and validity preliminary evidences, and can potentially serve as an effective tool for assessing verbal violence among Chinese college students.

The current level of verbal violence experienced by college students is higher than the moderate level. Female students scored significantly higher than male students in the dimensions of Verbal Violence Evaluation, Duration of Verbal Violence Impact, Verbal Violence Behaviors, Emotions Related to Verbal Violence, and the total questionnaire score. Urban college students scored significantly higher than rural college students in the Emotions Related to Verbal Violence dimension. First-year students scored significantly higher than second-year students in the Verbal Violence Evaluation dimension, while third-year students scored significantly higher than first-year students in the Verbal Violence Behaviors dimension. Except for the Family Verbal Violence dimension, significant differences across students with different majors were observed in all other dimensions and the total score, with law students scoring significantly higher in multiple dimensions than students with other majors. No significant associations were found between parental education levels and any dimension of verbal violence or the total score. The total score of college students’ verbal violence was significantly positively correlated with the total score of the SCL-90 Questionnaire.
