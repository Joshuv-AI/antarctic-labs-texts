Finetuning Generative Trajectory Model with Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback
Derun Li, Jianwei Ren, Yue Wang, Xin Wen, Pengxiang Li, Leimeng Xu, Kun Zhan, Zhongpu Xia, Peng Jia, Xianpeng Lang, Ningyi Xu, Hang Zhao

Abstract
Generating human-like and adaptive trajectories is essential for autonomous driving in dynamic environments. While generative models have shown promise in synthesizing feasible trajectories, they often fail to capture the nuanced variability of human driving styles due to dataset biases and distributional shifts. To address this, we introduce TrajHF , a human feedback-driven finetuning framework for generative trajectory models, designed to align motion planning with diverse driving preferences. TrajHF incorporates multi-conditional denoiser and reinforcement learning with human feedback to refine multi-modal trajectory generation beyond conventional imitation learning. This enables better alignment with human driving preferences while maintaining safety and feasibility constraints. TrajHF achieves PDMS of 93.95 on NavSim benchmark, significantly exceeding other methods. TrajHF sets a new paradigm for personalized and adaptable trajectory generation in autonomous driving.

Introduction

In autonomous driving, trajectory planning is responsible for generating safe, feasible, and human-like motions in complex and dynamic traffic environments. Heuristics-based learning methods
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
perform well in structured scenarios but struggle to generalize across diverse driving conditions.
Imitation learning, most prominently generative models
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
, provides a data-driven alternative. They synthesize diverse human-like trajectories, but do not explicitly capture the nuanced preferences that characterize human driving behaviors, such as individual tendencies, regional norms, and environmental factors.

Learning from demonstrations is fundamentally constrained by dataset bias. Behavior cloning only captures an average driving behavior, failing to represent the full spectrum of maneuvers. Generative imitation learning, on the other hand, learns real data distributions in the dataset, but is usually dominated by frequent modes, and hardly performs the best behavior unless sampling is conducted in an exhaustive manner.
This misalignment degrades model performance in scenarios requiring deviations from frequent demonstrations, such as aggressive adaptations in complex traffic interactions, as shown in Fig
1
.
Furthermore, human driving preferences are influenced not only by kinematic constraints but also by higher-level cognitive and social factors, such as risk tolerance, interactions with other road users, and situational awareness. Existing methods struggle to encode these latent factors, leading to planned motions that, while technically feasible, may feel unnatural, overly conservative, or unpredictably assertive. This further reduces user trust and acceptability, underscoring the need for trajectory generation methods that align with diverse human driving behaviors.

To address this challenge, we propose
TrajHF
, a novel framework to finetune generative trajectory models using human feedback to align trajectory generation with diverse human driving preferences. Human feedback, in the form of comparative trajectory rankings or explicit annotations, provides a rich supervisory signal that extends beyond conventional imitation learning paradigms. By integrating this feedback into the finetuning process, our approach enables generative models to capture human-preferred driving styles while maintaining safety and feasibility constraints.

We systematically investigate methodologies for incorporating human feedback into trajectory model finetuning. We propose a novel Multi-Conditioned Denoiser (MDC) Transformer network to generate multi-modal trajectory planning. Our design of conditioning multi-modal information gains significant improvement of trajectory generation quality, reaching PDMS 93.95 on NavSim
[
8
]
benchmark with uniform sampling and selection without any explicit guidance like anchors. Meanwhile, we explore reinforcement learning with human feedback and leverage group computation gathering rewards from each mode of generated trajectories to iteratively refine the generative trajectory distribution. Experimental results demonstrate that our method improves the “aggressiveness” of driving style of generated trajectories compared with the ground truth human preference data while preserving critical motion planning constraints. Using human feedback as an auxiliary learning signal,
TrajHF
offers a more personalized and adaptable trajectory generation paradigm, paving the way for autonomous vehicles that better reflect human driving behavior while ensuring safety and efficiency.

The primary contributions of this paper are as follows:

1.

We systematically investigate the trajectory distribution problem between the average behaviors and the human driving preferences.

2.

We propose a human feedback-driven finetuning framework for generative trajectory models, enabling alignment with diverse human driving preferences.

3.

Comprehensive experimental evaluations demonstrate the effectiveness of
TrajHF
in generating human-aligned autonomous driving trajectories across diverse scenarios.

4.

TrajHF
achieves SoTA on NavSim benchmark.

Related Work

2.1
End-to-end Trajectory Planning

Imitation learning (IL) has been widely adopted for end-to-end trajectory planning by learning from expert demonstrations
[
9
]
. UniAD
[
10
]
and ViP3D
[
11
]
leverage bird-eye-view (BEV) representation and introduce a unified transformer structure to construct planning-centric frameworks. Likewise, VAD
[
12
]
and VADv2
[
13
]
enhance this method by discretizing the trajectory space and creating a trajectory vocabulary, transforming the problem from regression to classification.
[
8
]
proposes NavSim, which establishes a new end-to-end autonomous driving planning benchmark focusing on multi-agent interactions and realistic driving scenarios. Transfuser
[
14
]
serves as a popular baseline on NavSim and a powerful encoder module, by fusing perceptual information from lidar and image data. FusionAD
[
15
]
took this fusion approach a step further by transmitting the combined perception features directly to the prediction and planning modules. Hydra-MDP
[
16
]
introduces a trajectory vocabulary for multi-target hydra-distillation, allowing the model to learn from both rule-based planners and human drivers in a scalable manner.
In contrast, our end-to-end diffusion policy for planning does not utilize any form of guide information such as anchors or vocabulary, which directly generates continuous, diverse trajectories without being constrained by predefined discretized spaces, allowing for more multi-modal, accurate and robust planning in complex environments.

2.2
Generative Trajectory Model

Generative models have emerged as a promising approach for trajectory prediction or planning, offering the ability to capture multi-modal driving behaviors. Early works apply Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)
[
17
,
18
]
and Variational Autoencoders (VAEs)
[
19
,
20
]
to learn trajectory distributions, while more recent advances leverage autoregressive models
[
7
]
and diffusion models to generate diverse and realistic motion patterns
[
4
,
5
]
.
In end-to-end autonomous driving, VBD
[
21
]
introduces a combination of denoising and behavior prediction losses alongside a query-centric Transformer encoder. DiffusionDrive
[
22
]
uses truncated diffusion policy to address the issues of mode collapse and heavy computational overhead. GoalFlow
[
3
]
integrates flow matching with goal point guidance for trajectory generation. Previous generative models focus mainly on ensuring safe and feasible motion generation by optimizing towards ground truth but lack mechanisms to incorporate the variability and complexity of human preferences, making them less effective in creating truly human-aligned autonomous driving systems, which is considered in our framework
TrajHF
.

2.3
Finetuning with Human Feedback

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)
[
23
]
has emerged as a pivotal approach for incorporating human preferences into model training through reinforcement learning. This framework has demonstrated remarkable efficacy across various domains
[
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
]
. The fundamental principle underlying RLHF involves constructing a preference-based loss from limited human feedback data, subsequently employing reinforcement learning to finetune models toward the preference distribution while preserving their original capabilities.
[
28
]
pioneered the application of RLHF to diffusion models without utilizing explicit reward models, instead implementing direct preference optimization techniques that achieved state-of-the-art results in text-to-image generation tasks. Within the autonomous driving and trajectory prediction domain,
[
29
]
implemented RLHF for lane-changing scenarios by developing a reward model trained on preference pairs of trajectory segments. To address the inherent challenges in obtaining frame-by-frame preference data,
[
30

proposed a straightforward preference reward method derived from safety-preference data with an integrated KL-divergence penalty. In this paper, we propose a direct finetuning approach based on GRPO
[
27
]
that eliminates critic models and simplifies reward calculation, while incorporating behavior cloning loss
[
31
]
to preserve the model’s original predictive capabilities.

Problem Definition

Figure 1:
Illustration of human preference problem in autonomous driving problem.
Taking aggressive driving style as an example, yellow lines indicate “normal” planning trajectories and green lines represent the “aggressive” human preferred trajectories.

Let the state space of an autonomous vehicle be denoted as
S
, where each state
s_{\tau}\in S
indicates the kinematic properties (e.g., position and heading) of the agent at timestamp
\tau
. A trajectory
x
is defined as a sequence of states,

x=\{s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{\mathcal{T}}\}

(1)

where
\mathcal{T}
is the trajectory length. The expert trajectory distribution
P_{\mathrm{data}}(x)
reflects human driving behaviors in the training dataset, whereas
P_{\theta}(x)
represents the distribution induced by a generative trajectory model parameterized by
\theta
. In a typical imitation learning setup, the goal is to minimize the discrepancy between these distributions, which arises when the model fails to capture the entire diversity of human driving preferences. This shift can be formalized as the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between the expert trajectory distribution
P_{\mathrm{data}}(x)
and the model-generated distribution
P_{\theta}(x)
:

D_{KL}(P_{\mathrm{data}}\|P_{\theta})=\sum_{x}P_{\mathrm{data}}(x)\log\frac{P_{\mathrm{data}}(x)}{P_{\theta}(x)}

(2)

A high
D_{KL}
value suggests that the generative model inadequately capture human-like driving behaviors, often due to mode collapse, where the model overfits to the mean behavior and ignores the full range of human preferences.
Human driving preferences can be characterized by a latent distribution
P_{\mathrm{pref}}(x)
, with each driver or style corresponding to a distinct sub-distribution. Since expert demonstrations typically represent an aggregate distribution rather than explicitly capturing individual variations, the actual target distribution can be seen as a mixture of preference distributions,

P_{\mathrm{data}}(x)=\sum_{k}^{K}w_{i}P_{\mathrm{pref}}^{i}(x)

(3)

where
P_{\mathrm{pref}}^{i}(x)
represents the trajectory distribution of the
i
-th driving style, and
w_{i}
is its mixture weight. Training solely on
P_{\mathrm{data}}(x)
may cause the generative model to miss the underlying multi-modal structure, leading to suboptimal performance on trajectories outside the dominant mode of the dataset.
To align the generated trajectory distribution
P_{\theta}(x)
with diverse human preferences, we introduce a human feedback finetuning approach that incorporates preference annotations into the training process. Specifically, given a set of human-ranked trajectories
HF=\{x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{N}\}
, where human annotators provide a preference ordering, we define a preference-based reward function
R(x)
such that,

P_{HF}=\arg\max_{P}\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[R\right],

(4)

where
P_{HF}(x)
represents the refined trajectory distribution incorporating human feedback. This enables the model to minimize the divergence

D_{KL}(P_{HF}\|P_{\theta})
, thereby reducing the discrepancy between the learned trajectory distribution and the human-preferred trajectory distribution.

Method

The overall architecture of our system is illustrated in Fig.
2
. It includes two key components: diffusion policy for multi-modal trajectory generation and RL finetuning which aligns the model with the human preference data.

Figure 2:
Overview of
TrajHF
system.
After pretraining, our diffusion policy generates
K
-modal trajectories conditioned on multi-modal inputs from human driving preference data via the Multi-Conditional Denoiser (MCD). Each trajectory mode is compared with the stylized ground truth trajectory to compute a reward. The
K
rewards by modes are fed into group computation and used to finetune the diffusion policy through a combination of RL loss and behavior cloning loss. The system refines the multi-modal autonomous driving planning to align with the preference style knowledge in the preference data.

4.1
Generative Trajectory Model

4.1.1
Diffusion Model Preliminaries

Diffusion models learn to represent the trajectory distribution
P_{\theta}(x)
by minimizing the
KL
-divergence defined in Eq. (
2
). We adopt Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPM)
[
32
]
as the foundational framework for the denoising process. DDPM establishes a reverse denoising process that transitions from Gaussian noise
x_{T}
to the noise-free state
x_{0}
, governed by the following transition equation:

x_{t-1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}}\left(x_{t}-\frac{1-\alpha_{t}}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}}\epsilon_{\theta}(x_{t},t)\right)+\sigma_{t}z

(5)

where
z\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
.
t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
denotes the noise level, and
\epsilon_{\theta}
represents the noise prediction network with learnable parameters
\theta
. The Gaussian noise added to the data
x
is according to a variance schedule
\beta_{t}
. Let
\alpha_{t}=1-\beta_{t}
,
\bar{\alpha}_{t}=\prod_{i=1}^{t}\alpha_{i}
and
\sigma_{t}^{2}=\beta_{t}
. The denoising model takes the current noise level and perceptual conditions as inputs to estimate the noise. We formalize the conditional distribution to be predicted as
P_{\theta}(x|o)
, where
o
is the observation conditions input, including front-view images, LiDAR sensors, historical actions and ego states. The optimization objective is formulated as a gradient descent step on:

\nabla_{\theta}\|\epsilon-\epsilon_{\theta}(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}x_{0}+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\epsilon,t,o)\|^{2}

(6)

where
\epsilon
is randomly sampled from
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
.

4.1.2
Multi-Conditional Denoiser

To solve the complex autonomous driving problem, we design a denoising transformer to generate trajectories conditioned on multi-modal perceptions, including front-view images, LiDAR data and historical states, as shown in Fig.
3
. Before introducing Gaussian noise into the model input, the ego-centric trajectory is initially projected into the action space to mitigate heteroscedasticity along the trajectory timeline. The projection is expressed as:

\begin{split}\hat{x}_{\tau}&=s_{\tau}-s_{\tau-1}\\
\hat{x}_{1}&=s_{1}\end{split}

(7)

where
\hat{x}_{\tau}
represents the agent’s action at timestep
\tau
, and
\tau
ranges from
1
to
\mathcal{T}
. This mapping ensures a reversible relationship between the two spaces, allowing the trajectory to be readily derived by accumulating actions. The subsequent noisy action is encoded by a MLP with residual, followed by modulation with state and time embeddings. Our model integrates multiple contextual conditions to guide the denoising process. Inspired by Transfuser
[
14
]
, the concatenated front-view image and LiDAR are primarily encoded by their backbones, respectively, interleaved with fusion transformers to provide visual and BEV conditions. The intermediate fused feature maps are further upscaled to serve as a higher-resolution BEV condition. These conditions interact with the noisy action through cross attention blocks over multiple iterations, ultimately producing the predicted noise, which is then utilized in the diffusion process. During inference, the model generates a pool of candidates. Rejection sampling strategy is subsequently employed to filter out unsuitable trajectories. Notably, this approach is entirely
free
from the use of anchors or vocabulary-driven mechanisms.

Figure 3:
The detailed architecture of multi-conditional denoiser.
The multi-modal perceptual input (image, LiDAR and actions) is encoded to different features which serve as the conditions of our transformer denoiser. During inference, multiple uniform sampling leads to multi-modal trajectory planning, which is then selected to the final result by the rejection score.

4.2
Driving Preferences Alignment by RL Finetuning

To align with human driving preferences, we provide reward feedback on the trajectories output by the diffusion policy, and use reinforcement learning methods to optimize the planner.

Specifically, for a particular driving style, a dataset
D
that conforms to this style is collected with a data distribution
p\left(o,x^{\mathrm{gt}}\right)
, where
x^{\mathrm{gt}}
denotes the ground truth trajectory. The collection method is provided later in
4.3
. For a given
\left(o,x^{\mathrm{gt}}\right)
, the diffusion planner
P_{\theta}
outputs trajectory
x_{0}\sim P_{\theta}\left(x_{0}|o\right)
, and the reward
R(x_{0},x^{\mathrm{gt}})
is calculated as

\left\{\begin{aligned} R\left(x_{0},x^{\mathrm{gt}}\right)&=w_{\mathrm{avg}}R_{\mathrm{avg}}\left(x_{0},x^{\mathrm{gt}}\right)+w_{\mathrm{final}}R_{\mathrm{final}}\left(x_{0},x^{\mathrm{gt}}\right)\\
R_{\mathrm{avg}}\left(x_{0},x^{\mathrm{gt}}\right)&=-\mathrm{smooth}\_\mathrm{l}1\_\mathrm{loss}\left(x_{0},x^{\mathrm{gt}}\right)\\
R_{\mathrm{final}}\left(x_{0},x^{\mathrm{gt}}\right)&=-\mathrm{smooth}\_\mathrm{l}1\_\mathrm{loss}\left(s_{\mathcal{T}},s_{\mathcal{T}}^{\mathrm{gt}}\right)\\
\end{aligned}\right..

(8)

with
w_{\mathrm{avg}}\geq 0
and
w_{\mathrm{final}}\geq 0
.

R_{\mathrm{avg}}
penalizes the average positional difference between
x_{0}
and
x^{\mathrm{gt}}
, while
R_{\mathrm{final}}
specifically penalizes their terminal difference.
Then, the policy optimization objective is to maximize

J\left(P_{\theta}\right)=\mathbb{E}_{p(o,x^{\mathrm{gt}}),P_{\theta}(x_{0}|o)}\left[R\left(x_{0},x^{\mathrm{gt}}\right)\right].

(9)

According to
[
33
,
34
]
, the trajectory generation denoising process can be expanded into a
T
-step Markov Decision Process (MDP):

\left\{\begin{aligned} \psi_{t}&\triangleq\left(o,x_{T-t}\right)\\
a_{t}&\triangleq x_{T-t-1}\\
P\left(\psi_{t+1}\mid\psi_{t},a_{t}\right)&\triangleq\left(\delta_{o},\delta_{a_{t}}\right)\\
\pi_{\theta}\left(a_{t}\mid\psi_{t}\right)&\triangleq p_{\theta}\left(x_{T-t-1}\mid x_{T-t},o\right)\\
\rho_{0}\left(\psi_{0}\right)&\triangleq\left(p\left(o\right),\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}\right))\\
R\left(\psi_{t},a_{t}\right)&\triangleq\left\{\begin{matrix}R\left(x_{0},x^{\mathrm{gt}}\right)&\,\,\mathrm{if\ }t=T-1\\
0&\mathrm{otherwise}\\
\end{matrix}\right.\\
\end{aligned}\right.,

(10)

where
\delta_{z}
is the Dirac distribution with nonzero density only at z.
By denoting
r_{t}\left(\psi_{t},a_{t}\right)=\sum_{\tau=t}^{T-1}{\gamma^{T-1-t}}R\left(\psi_{\tau},a_{\tau}\right)
as the discounted cumulative future reward from time
t
, the REINFORCE method
[
35
]
can be used to perform policy gradient updates with the RL loss function

\mathcal{L}_{RL}^{\theta}=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta},P,\rho_{0}}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}{\log\pi_{\theta}\left(a_{t}\mid\psi_{t}\right)r_{t}\left(\psi_{t},a_{t}\right)}\right].

(11)

To reduce variance, according to
[
34
]
, one can train a critic network to have an estimate
\hat{V}^{\pi_{\theta}}(\psi_{T-1})
of the state value
V^{\pi_{\theta}}(\psi_{T-1})=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta},P,\rho_{0}}\left[R\left(x_{0},x^{\mathrm{gt}}\right)|\psi_{T-1}\right]
, and replace
r_{t}\left(\psi_{t},a_{t}\right)
in Eq. (
11
) by
\hat{A}^{\pi_{\theta}}\left(\psi_{t},a_{t}\right)=\gamma^{T-1-t}\left(R\left(x_{0},x^{\mathrm{gt}}\right)-\hat{V}^{\pi_{\theta}}(\psi_{T-1})\right)
.
However, since the diffusion policy actor is pretrained, the critic typically requires a warmup period to adapt to the actor’s performance level. This not only increases training costs but also introduces another hyperparameter (warmup steps) to tune, alongside the critic’s learning rate and network architecture parameters. To streamline the finetuning process, we adopt the GRPO technique introduced by
[
36
]
. This approach calculates group relative advantages among multiple actions sampled from the actor, circumventing the need for a critic. The RL loss is designed as

\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_{RL}^{\theta}=&\mathbb{E}_{p\left(o,x^{\mathrm{gt}}\right),~\psi_{0}^{k}\sim\delta_{o}\otimes\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}\right),~\xi^{k}\sim\left(\pi_{\theta},P|\psi_{0}^{k}\right),~k=1,...,K}\\
&\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}{\log\pi_{\theta}\left(a_{t}^{k}\mid\psi_{t}^{k}\right)\gamma^{T-1-t}A_{\mathrm{gr}}^{k}}\right]}\end{aligned},

(12)

where
\xi^{k}=\left(\psi_{0}^{k},a_{0}^{k},\psi_{1}^{k},...,a_{T-1}^{k},\psi_{T}^{k}\right)
is the MDP state-action sequence,
K
is the number of sequences sampled for a given observation
o
, and

A_{\mathrm{gr}}^{k}=\frac{r_{k}-\mathrm{mean}\left(\left\{r_{1},r_{2},\cdots,r_{K}\right\}\right)}{\mathrm{std}\left(\left\{r_{1},r_{2},\cdots,r_{K}\right\}\right)}

(13)

denotes the group relative advantage with
r_{k}=R\left(x_{0}^{k},x^{\mathrm{gt}}\right)
.

Since the driving data used for finetuning is substantially smaller than the pretraining dataset, and manually designed reward functions typically only partially reflect our desired driving behaviors, relying solely on RL finetuning with return optimization might cause the planner to overfit and potentially compromise its general driving capabilities. To address this concern, we incorporate a behavioral cloning (BC) loss component, allowing the finetuned policy to preserve certain pretrained behaviors. Specifically, following the approach outlined in
[
34
]
, a copy
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
of the pretrained model
\pi
is created, and the BC loss function is designed as

\mathcal{L}_{BC}^{\theta}=-\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}^{\mathrm{ref}},P,\rho_{0}}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}{\log\pi_{\theta}\left(a_{t}\mid\psi_{t}\right)}\,\,\right],

(14)

where we sample state-action sequences using
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
and encourage the finetuned model
\pi_{\theta}
to maintain a high probability of producing the same actions as
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
. The combined finetuning loss is such

\mathcal{L}^{\theta}=\mathcal{L}_{RL}^{\theta}+\alpha\mathcal{L}_{BC}^{\theta}

(15)

with weight coefficient
\alpha\geq 0
. The algorithm is summarized in Algorithm
1
.

4.3
Preference Data Collection

Figure 4:
The mining and annotation process for constructing a human driving preference scenario dataset. Only takeover cases related to driving styles are used to extract human preference labeling, which is updated by data annotation results.

We propose an efficient process for data mining and automatic annotation to build a dataset that reflects human driving preferences, as shown in Fig
4
. This process involves labeling large amounts of driving data with distinct driving style tags. However, practical challenges arise, such as determining whether every scenario or frame requires a driving style label. These challenges and the corresponding solutions are outlined in the following steps.

Preference Scenario Mining.

Human driving typically occurs in ordinary environments, making it difficult to define specific driving styles for every decision. For instance, choosing to overtake a slow-moving vehicle may not be classified as either aggressive or conservative. However, in critical scenarios—such as overtaking vehicles using the opposite lane, shown in Fig
1
—distinct driving behaviors can be identified. These critical situations, referred to as preference scenarios, are mined from large-scale driving data. We initially attempted manual annotation, but this was inefficient. Instead, we find that human driver takeover data can help identify preference scenarios. This data is categorized into six classes (e.g., “too aggressive” or “too conservative”), each corresponding to a distinct driving style, which can be used to define rules or train models for identifying preference scenarios.

Key Frame Selection.

After identifying preference scenarios, only segments relevant to the preference need to be labeled. Rather than labeling every frame, we focus on key frames where significant actions occur, such as changes in speed or direction. If a frame is tagged too early, the defining action has not yet happened; if tagged too late, the action is already underway. Clear specifications for key frame identification allow for rule-based, automatic detection, enabling the potential large-scale annotation.

Manual Inspection.

The annotated key frames undergo random manual checks to ensure data quality. Human inspectors can update scenario definitions or introduce new preference scenarios in special cases.

5
Experiments

5.1
Experimental Setup

5.1.1
Dataset and Metrics

We conduct experiments on three datasets: NavSim
[
8
]
, LiAuto Normal dataset for pretraining, and LiAuto Preference dataset for finetuning. On the NavSim benchmark, our generative trajectory model is evaluated following the official Predictive Driver Model Score (PDMS)
[
8
]
, which quantifies driving capacity by aggregating subscores for multiple objectives such as progress and comfort. On LiAuto Normal dataset, we pretrain diffusion policy with three distinct scales of training data, sampling frames at 2Hz to ensure temporal coverage. Scale-1 consists of 810K samples from 65,628 unique clips. Scale-2 expands to 3M samples, covering 298,706 clips, while Scale-3 grows to 6M samples, covering 487,812 clips. Model performance is assessed using metrics including minADE, meanADE, minFDE and meanFDE. LiAuto Preference dataset, described in
4.3
, contains 4,489 clips with 78,041 frames, where 3,835 clips with 66,693 frames are used for training, 337 clips with 5,924 frames for validation, and 317 clips with 5,424 frames for testing. This dataset captures “aggressive” driving behaviors through human annotations.

5.1.2
Human Preference Evaluation

Evaluating driving style requires human-level semantic understanding of the current scene. For example, when there is a cyclist in front of the ego vehicle, considering the context, we can determine that overtaking the cyclist is a more aggressive behavior compared to following behind the cyclist. When using overtaking behavior as the primary criterion for aggressive style, we found that ADE and FDE metrics do not effectively reflect this behavior. Compared to an aggressive ground truth trajectory, if a model’s trajectory is overall faster or slower longitudinally, or has lateral deviations, these would result in larger ADE and FDE values. However, these longitudinal and lateral deviations are not clearly associated with whether the model generates an overtaking trajectory. Therefore, we choose to introduce human preference judgments to evaluate trajectory output styles. Specifically, based on their subjective assessment, evaluators select which trajectory better meets style requirements from a pair of trajectories in the same context.

Based on this approach, we designed a systematic human evaluation framework to compare our models. To evaluate the performance of models
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
on a set of scenes
\mathcal{S}
, we conduct human evaluation experiments. For each scene
s\in\mathcal{S}
, the two models generate trajectories
T_{m_{1}}(s)
and
T_{m_{2}}(s)
respectively. Evaluators are asked to choose from three options without knowing the source of the trajectories (double-blind assessment): prefer the trajectory generated by
m_{1}
, prefer the trajectory generated by
m_{2}
, or consider both equally effective in meeting the specified driving style.

Formally, we define the human evaluation function
h(T_{m_{1}}(s),T_{m_{2}}(s))
, whose value is:

•

1
if the evaluator prefers
T_{m_{1}}(s)

•

-1
if the evaluator prefers
T_{m_{2}}(s)

•

0
if the evaluator considers both equally effective (tie)

For each scene
s\in\mathcal{S}
, we collected evaluations from
N
evaluators. Based on these evaluations, we define the metric
Better Or Equal Rate (BOE)
: The proportion where one model is considered better than or equal to the other model

For the evaluation dataset, we manually selected 199 clips from the test set where the pretrained model’s performance exhibited significant deviations from the expected driving style. We recruited 5 professional annotators who regularly perform driving style labeling tasks to serve as evaluators. These evaluators were instructed to make preference judgments on trajectory outputs based on the specified driving style requirements and scene context. To mitigate individual bias, the preference metrics for each model were derived by averaging the scores across all evaluators, ensuring a more objective assessment of the models’ performance.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we introduce
TrajHF
, a human feedback driven finetuning framework for generative trajectory models, designed to enhance the alignment of autonomous driving trajectories with diverse human preferences. By integrating DDPM multi-modal trajectory generator with reinforcement learning finetuning,
TrajHF
enables trajectory generation that is not only safe and feasible but also more personalized and adaptable to individual driving behaviors. Experimental results demonstrate that
TrajHF
preserve key motion planning constraints verified by state-of-the-art results on NavSim benchmark, while aligning to the specific human “aggressive” driving style. By leveraging human feedback as a supervisory signal, our approach addresses the limitations of traditional imitation learning, offering a more flexible and human-aligned trajectory generation paradigm.

Limitations
The framework relies on human annotations, which can be subjective, inconsistent, and costly to obtain. The quality and diversity of feedback significantly impact the model’s ability to generalize across different driving styles. Future work looks into using human take over data to do auto-labeling as preference data collection and scaling
TrajHF
to broader driving scenarios with additional cognitive and social factors.
