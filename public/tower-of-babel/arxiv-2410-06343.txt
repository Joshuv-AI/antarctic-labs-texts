Losing Treewidth In The Presence Of Weights
Michał Włodarczyk

Abstract
In the Weighted Treewidth- η \eta Deletion problem we are given a node-weighted graph G G and we look for a vertex subset X X of minimum weight such that the treewidth of G − X G-X is at most η \eta . We show that Weighted Treewidth- η \eta Deletion admits a randomized polynomial-time constant-factor approximation algorithm for every fixed η \eta . Our algorithm also works for the more general Weighted Planar ℱ \mathcal{F} -M-Deletion problem. This work extends the results for unweighted graphs by [Fomin, Lokshtanov, Misra, Saurabh; FOCS ’12] and answers a question posed by [Agrawal, Lokshtanov, Misra, Saurabh, Zehavi; APPROX/RANDOM ’18] and [Kim, Lee, Thilikos; APPROX/RANDOM ’21]. The presented algorithm is based on a novel technique of random sampling of so-called protrusions.

Introduction

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a class of graphs.
A natural vertex deletion problem corresponding to
\mathcal{H}
is
defined as follows: given a graph
G
find a set
X\subseteq V(G)
of minimum size for which
G-X\in\mathcal{H}
.
This captures numerous fundamental graph problems such as
Vertex Cover
,
Feedback Vertex Set
, or
Vertex Planarization
. Vertex deletion problems have been long studied from the perspective of approximation algorithms, starting from the classic 2-approximation for
Vertex Cover

[
7
,
59
]
from the 70s.
It is known that for every non-trivial hereditary class
\mathcal{H}
the corresponding vertex deletion problem
is NP-hard and APX-hard
[
55
]
so the best we can hope for in polynomial time is a constant-factor approximation.
Alike for
Vertex Cover
, this can be achieved for any class
\mathcal{H}
characterized by a finite family of forbidden induced subgraphs via a linear programming relaxation (or sometimes by other means
[
38
,
52
,
60
]
).
Lund and Yannakakis
[
55
]
conjectured in the early 90s that this condition in fact captures all vertex deletion problems admitting a constant-factor approximation.
This was quickly disproved with a 2-approximation for
Feedback Vertex Set

[
5
,
10
]
as the class of acyclic graphs (i.e. forests) does not admit such a characterization.
Later, Fujito
[
35
]
constructed an infinite family of counterexamples based on matroidal properties. Nowadays, we know several more interesting counterexamples: block graphs
[
1
]
, 3-leaf power graphs
[
3
]
, (proper) interval graphs
[
13
,
68
]
, ptolemaic graphs
[
4
]
, and bounded-treewidth graphs
[
29
,
37
]
.

The landscape of positive results is grimmer when we consider
weighted vertex deletion
problems.
Here, the graph
G
is equipped with a weight function
w\colon V(G)\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
and we ask for a vertex set
X\subseteq V(G)
of minimum total weight for which
G-X\in\mathcal{H}
.
The constant-factor approximations for
Vertex Cover
,
Feedback Vertex Set
, and for classes defined by a finite family of forbidden induced subgraphs were originally presented already in the weighted setting, but apart from that we are only aware of such algorithms for ptolemaic graphs
[
4
]
,
\theta_{c}
-free graphs
[
27
,
49
]
(a special case of bounded-treewidth graphs; explained shortly), and the matroidal classes introduced by Fujito
[
35
]

Treewidth reduction and minor hitting.

The class of forests coincides with the class of graphs with treewidth one
[
18
, §7]
.
Hence a natural generalization of (
Weighted
)
Feedback Vertex Set
is (
Weighted
)
Treewidth-
\eta
Deletion

[
21
,
31
,
33

34
]
and satisfiability
[
6

.
Here, we look for a minimum-weight
treewidth-
\eta
modulator
, that is, a vertex set
X\subseteq V(G)
for which the treewidth of
G-X
is bounded by
\eta
.

Treewidth is closely related to the theory of graph minors (see
Section

3

\eta

Seymour’s Theorem implies that it can be characterized by a finite family of forbidden minors
[
63
]
.
In addition, a minor-closed graph class has bounded treewidth if and only if at least one of its forbidden minors is planar
[
61
]
.
The treewidth bound is known to be polynomial in the size of this planar graph
[
17
]
.

For a finite family of graphs
\mathcal{F}
, the (
Weighted
)
\mathcal{F}
-M-Deletion
problem (also known as
\mathcal{F}
-Minor Cover
) asks for a minimum-size (resp. minimum-weight) set
X\subseteq V(G)
for which
G-X
does not contain any graph
F\in\mathcal{F}
as a
minor
.
If the family
\mathcal{F}
contains a planar graph, we speak of (
Weighted
)
Planar

\mathcal{F}
-M-Deletion
.
Observe that
Treewidth-
\eta
Deletion
is a special case of
Planar

\mathcal{F}
-M-Deletion
. This special case is paramount to understand the general case: if
X\subseteq V(G)
is a solution to
Planar

\mathcal{F}
-M-Deletion
then
\mathrm{\textbf{tw}}(G-X)\leq\eta
for some constant
\eta
depending on
\mathcal{F}
.
Hence if we were able to compute a constant-approximate treewidth-
\eta
modulator
Y
, we could then solve the problem optimally on the bounded-treewidth graph
G-Y
, and output the union of both solutions.

Other examples of
\mathcal{F}
-M-Deletion
include deletion problems into the classes of outerplanar graphs (for
\mathcal{F}=\{K_{4},K_{3,2}\}
), planar graphs (for
\mathcal{F}=\{K_{5},K_{3,3}\}
), or classes of bounded pathwidth/treedepth.
Note that in the second case the family
\mathcal{F}
does not contain a planar graph, which corresponds to the fact that the class of planar graphs has unbounded treewidth.

Protrusion replacement.

The results of Fomin et al.
[
29
]
are based on the powerful technique of
protrusion replacement

[
26
]
.
An
r
-protrusion
in a graph
G
is a vertex subset
A\subseteq V(G)
for which
\mathrm{\textbf{tw}}(G[A])\leq r
and
|\partial_{G}(A)|\leq r
, where
\partial_{G}(A)
is the subset of vertices from
A
that have neighbors outside
A
.
The idea of protrusion replacement is to analyze the behavior of an
r
-protrusion with respect to the considered problem and replace it with an equivalent subgraph of constant size.
Apart from constant-factor approximations, the same work spawned single-exponential FPT algorithms parameterized by the solution size (under some additional restriction) and polynomial kernelizations (using a more advanced concept of a
near-protrusion
). For other applications of this technique, see e.g.,
[
9
,
12
,
32
,
43
,
54
]
.

The caveat of protrusion replacement is that in general it is only known to work for the unweighted problems.
Kim, Lee, and Thilikos
[
49
]
designed a weighted variant of this technique for the special case of
Weighted Planar
\mathcal{F}
-M-Deletion
with
\mathcal{F}=\{\theta_{c}\}
.
They suggested a roadmap to achieve a constant-factor approximation for the general case, concluding that
“(
\dots
) the most challenging step is
to design a weighted protrusion replacer”.

We remark that the techniques used by Gupta et al.
[
37
]
in their constant-factor approximation for
Planar

\mathcal{F}
-M-Deletion
also fall short to process weighted graphs.
Their algorithm repeatedly improves a solution
X
by removing a small vertex set
Y
to reduce the number of
X
-vertices in each connected component to
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{F}}(1)
.
The argument ensuring the existence of such a set
Y
relies on the properties of separators in a bounded-treewidth graph
and breaks down when we replace “small” with “of small weight”.

Apart from approximation,
\mathcal{F}
-M-Deletion
has been studied extensively from the perspective of fixed-parameter tractability (FPT)

It is known that every

Such a single-exponential parameter dependency is best possible under the Exponential Time Hypothesis already

We will consider parameterized algorithms for weighted problems under the parameterization by the solution size

More precisely, we look for a solution of minimum weight among those on at most

Therefore, we can find a solution in time

(see

A single-exponential FPT algorithm is known for

Feedback Vertex Set

[

]
.
Although we are not aware of any more general results,

\mathcal{F}
-M-Deletion

1.1
Our results

Our main conceptual message is that the need to design a weighted counterpart of the protrusion replacement technique can be circumvented.
Instead, our algorithms are based on a new structural result about protrusions and treewidth modulators.
We say that a non-empty family of
r
-protrusions
\mathcal{P}
in
G
forms an
(\eta,r,c)
-modulator hitting family
if for every treewidth-
\eta
modulator
X
in
G
at least
|\mathcal{P}|\,/\,c
sets in
\mathcal{P}
have a non-empty intersection with
X
.

Theorem 1.1
.

For each
\eta>0
,
every graph
G
with
\mathrm{\textbf{tw}}(G)>\eta
has an
\left(\eta,\mathcal{O}(\eta),2^{\mathcal{O}(\eta)}\right)
-modulator hitting family
\mathcal{P}
of size
|V(G)|^{\mathcal{O}(\eta)}
. Furthermore,
\mathcal{P}
can be constructed in time
|V(G)|^{\mathcal{O}(\eta)}
.

Equipped with a modulator hitting
family
\mathcal{P}
we can randomly sample an
\mathcal{O}(\eta)
-protrusion
A\in\mathcal{P}
and then guess a piece of an optimal solution
X\cap A
by considering
\mathcal{O}_{\eta}(1)
options.
The concrete choice of the probability distribution will be adjusted to specific applications.

This strategy bears some resemblance to random sampling of important separators
[
18
, §8]
used in the FPT algorithm for
Multicut
by Marx and Razgon
[
57
]
.
Moreover, the proof of
Theorem

1.1
also involves important separators and structures somewhat similar to
important clusters
from
[
57
]
.
We remark however that the setups of these two techniques are disparate when it comes to the separator size (constant vs. parameter), the desired subset property (bounded treewidth vs. disjointedness with a given set) and the objective (intersect a solution vs. cover vertices that are in some sense irrelevant).

Theorem

1.1
can also be compared to the algorithm by Fomin el al.
[
28
]
that involves random sampling of low-treewidth subgraphs in an apex-minor-free graph to cover an unknown small connected vertex set.
That construction however relies on totally different techniques.

Our new tool enables us to design a first constant-factor approximation for
Weighted Planar
\mathcal{F}
-M-Deletion
, extending the results by Fomin et al.
[
29
]
and Gupta et al.
[
37
]
to the weighted realm.
This resolves an open problem by Agrawal et al.
[
2
]
and Kim et al.
[
49
]
.
In the statement below,
\eta(\mathcal{F})
denotes the treewidth bound for the class of graphs excluding all
F\in\mathcal{F}
as minors.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a finite family of graphs containing a planar graph. The
Weighted
\mathcal{F}
-M-Deletion
problem admits a randomized constant-factor approximation algorithm with running time
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{F}}\left(|V(G)|^{\mathcal{O}(\eta(\mathcal{F}))}\right)
.

Notably, all the known approximation algorithms for
Weighted Planar
\mathcal{F}
-M-Deletion

[
2
,
6
,
33
]

and its special cases
[
5
,
10
,
27
,
35
,
49
]
rely on LP techniques
3
3

3

The first algorithms for
Weighted

Feedback Vertex Set

[
5
,
10
]

were originally phrased in a combinatorial language but later Chudak et al.
[
16
]
gave a simplified analysis of them in terms of
the primal-dual method.
whereas our algorithm is purely combinatorial.
In particular, we do not follow the roadmap suggested by Kim et al.
[
49
]
.
Circumventing protrusion replacement helps us avoid another issue troubling that technique, namely non-constructivity.
Instead, our treatment of protrusions relies on explicit bounds on the sizes of minimal representative boundaried graphs in a certain equivalence relation by Baste, Sau, and Thilikos
[
9
]
.
The values of the approximation factor
and the constant hidden in the

-notation
can be retraced from that work but they are at least double-exponential in
\eta(\mathcal{F})
.

As another application of
Theorem

1.1
, we present a single-exponential FPT algorithm for
Weighted Planar
\mathcal{F}
-M-Deletion
parameterized by the solution size

. As noted before, such a result could possibly be achieved by combining the protrusion decomposition by Kim et at.
[

]
with the exhaustive families described in

Nevertheless, we include it as an illustration of versatility of our approach.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a finite family of graphs containing a

-M-Deletion
can be computed in randomized time

This algorithm returns a
k
-optimal solution with constant probability; otherwise it may either output a solution of larger weight or report a failure.

2
Techniques

We will now discuss how
Theorem

1.1
facilitates designing approximation algorithms and compare our techniques to the previous works.

Protrusion replacement.

The starting point is the following randomized algorithm for unweighted
Feedback Vertex Set
.
First, we can perform a simple preprocessing to remove vertices of degree 1 and 2. Then the number of edges in an
n
-vertex graph
G
is at least
\frac{3n}{2}
.
But when
X
is a feedback vertex set then
G-X
is a forest, which can have at most
n-1
edges.
Therefore at least
\frac{1}{3}
-fraction of edges is incident to
X
.
We can thus sample a random edge and remove
one of its endpoints, also chosen at random.
The expected number of steps before
we hit a vertex from
X
is at most 6 so this algorithm yields 6-approximation in expectation.

Fomin et al.
[
29
]
invented a far-reaching generalization of this idea for the case when
X
is a treewidth-
\eta
modulator, i.e.,
\mathrm{\textbf{tw}}(G-X)\leq\eta
for some constant
\eta\in\mathbb{N}
.
Their preprocessing procedure does not merely remove vertices of low degree but it detects and simplifies
r
-protrusions.
A simple yet crucial observation is that the subgraph

G-X
can be covered by
\mathcal{O}(|N(X)|)
many
r
-protrusions for
r=2\eta+2
.
Hence if we could only ensure that each
r
-protrusion has size
\mathcal{O}_{r}(1)
then again a constant fraction of edges would be incident to
X
and we could apply the same argument as above.
This is what protrusion replacement is about: given an
r
-protrusion
A
we would like to perform a “surgery” on
G
to replace
G[A]
with a subgraph of size
\mathcal{O}_{r}(1)
that exhibits the same behavior.

The property of “being
\mathcal{F}
-minor-free” (as well as “having bounded treewidth”) can be expressed in counting monadic
second-order (CMSO) logic.
For such graph properties, Courcelle’s Theorem
[
12
, Lemma 3.2]

guarantees that subgraphs of constant-size boundary can be divided into a constant number of equivalence classes and replacing a subgraph with an equivalent one does not affect the property in question.
But this is not enough as we do not know which vertices from such a subgraph are being removed by a solution.
Protrusion replacement relies crucially on the observation that
it is cheap to take into the solution the entire boundary of an
r
-protrusion.

For a broad spectrum of problems this
property (formalized as
strong monotonicity

[
12
,
29
]
)
implies that
the difference in cost between any two solutions over an
r
-protrusion is bounded by
\mathcal{O}_{r}(1)
.
Consequently,
there may be only finitely many non-equivalent “behaviors” of an
r
-protrusion.
For each such equivalence class there is some
r
-protrusion of minimal size and it can be safely used as a replacement.
Then the maximal size of such a replacement is some (implicit) function of
r
.

This technique breaks down for weighted graphs because it is no longer true that removing the boundary of an
r
-protrusion is cheap.
Here, it is unclear whether the number of different “behaviors” can be bounded in terms of
r
alone.

Sampling with weights.

Before we deal with weighted protrusions, let us sketch how to adapt the algorithm for
Feedback Vertex Set
to the weighted setting.
First, we can still safely remove vertices of degree 1.
Even though we cannot remove all vertices of degree 2, we can preprocess the graph to ensure that each such vertex is adjacent to higher degree vertices.
After such preprocessing the number of edges is at least
\frac{5n}{4}
and so
at least
\frac{1}{5}
-fraction of edges is incident to the optimal feedback vertex set
X
.
Observe that sampling a random endpoint of a random edge
is equivalent to sampling a vertex proportionally to its degree so
\sum_{v\in X}\deg(v)\geq\frac{1}{10}\cdot\sum_{v\in V(G)}\deg(v)
.
It is futile though to sample vertices according to the same distribution as before because the solution may consist of light-weighted vertices only and guessing just a few heavy vertices can blow up the solution weight.
As we want to make the light-weighted vertices more preferable, we will sample a vertex
v
of weight
w(v)
with probability proportional to
\frac{\deg(v)}{w(v)}
.
Let
W=\sum_{v\in V(G)}\frac{\deg(v)}{w(v)}
.
Then the expected weight of the chosen vertex equals

\mathbb{E}\left[w(v)\right]=\sum_{v\in V(G)}w(v)\cdot\frac{\deg(v)}{w(v)\cdot W}=\sum_{v\in V(G)}\frac{\deg(v)}{W}.

On the other hand, the expected value of the
revealed part of the solution
equals

\mathbb{E}\left[w(v)\cdot 1_{v\in X}\right]=\sum_{v\in X}w(v)\cdot\frac{\deg(v)}{w(v)\cdot W}=\sum_{v\in X}\frac{\deg(v)}{W}\geq\frac{1}{10}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[w(v)\right].

Even though the expected number of steps before we hit a vertex from
X
might be large, the expected decrease of the optimum in each step is within a constant factor from the expected cost paid by the solution in this step.
Such an algorithm can be still proven to yield 10-approximation via a martingale argument.

Sampling with protrusions.

The main obstacle remains how to deal with a situation when only a small fraction of edges is incident to a solution because of large protrusions.
Let
X
be a treewidth-
\eta
modulator in
G
of minimum weight and
r=\mathcal{O}(\eta)
.
We exploit the structure of
G-X
in a different way: since
G-X
can be covered by few
r
-protrusions,
we will sample a set
A
from a family of
r
-protrusions that are in some sense maximal and
hope that we pick a set
A
that intersects
X
.

This is exactly the idea formalized in
Theorem

1.1
. Next, we try to guess the set
X\cap A
or some subset
Y\subseteq A
which is “at least as good”.
Even though the number of different “behaviors” of a weighted
r
-protrusion may be a priori unbounded,
we can still use Courcelle’s Theorem to bound the number of different “types” of the partial solution
X\cap A
. Intuitively speaking, it is easier to enumerate “types of a partial solution” than “types of a protrusion” because in the latter case we need to understand all the behaviors of subgraphs attainable after removal of an unknown vertex set.

Suppose now that we are given an
r
-protrusion
A
that is promised to intersect
X
.
Using the “graph gluing” technique
[
40
,
48
]
, we enumerate a family
\mathcal{A}\subseteq 2^{A}
of size
\mathcal{O}_{r}(1)
such that either
X\cap A\in\mathcal{A}
or there exists
Y\in\mathcal{A}
for which
(X\setminus A)\cup Y
is also an optimal solution.
Next, we sample a random non-empty set from
\mathcal{A}
in such a way that the probability of picking a particular
Y\in\mathcal{A}
is proportional to
\frac{1}{w(Y)}
.
By the same calculation as before, the expected value of the revealed part of a solution is within a multiplicative factor
|\mathcal{A}|=\mathcal{O}_{r}(1)
from
\mathbb{E}\left[w(Y)\right]
, which is the key observation to design a constant-factor approximation.
It is crucial that we can assume
X\cap A\neq\emptyset
because the proposed sampling procedure makes sense only for sets
Y
with a positive weight.

The vertex set
C_{4}
is enclosed by the dashed cycle.
The simple
\eta
-separation
(C_{4},N(C_{4}))

covers two edges outgoing from

and so the set
N(C_{4})\setminus X
has been marked.
In the proof we take advantage of the fact that there can be no inclusion-wise maximal simple
\eta
-separation
(C,S)
for which

is contained in the yellow subset of

.

Main proof sketch.

Finally, we provide some intuition behind the proof of
Theorem

1.1
.
It is more convenient for us not to work directly with protrusions but with pairs
(C,S)
of vertex sets, where
N(C)\subseteq S
and
\mathrm{\textbf{tw}}(G[C])+|S|
is bounded.
For technical reasons, we define an
\eta
-separation
as such a pair in which
\mathrm{\textbf{tw}}(G[C])\leq\eta
and
|S|\leq 2\eta+2
.
Note that then
C\cup S
forms a
(3\eta+2)
-protrusion.
We call an
\eta
-separation
simple
when
G[C]
is connected and
S=N(C)
.

We enumerate the family of inclusion-wise maximal simple
\eta
-separations.
Since a bounded-treewidth subgraph may contain a large subfamily of such pairs
(C_{i},S_{i})
for which
S_{i}
coincide (see
Figure

1
), we need to merge such a subfamily into a single
\eta
-separation, that is called
semi-simple
.
These structures
can be compared to
important clusters
from the work
[
57
]
.
Next, we would like every edge in the graph to be covered by some
\eta
-separation in the family, so if some edge
uv
has not been covered so far, we insert the pair
(\emptyset,\{u,v\})
to the family.
Note that this forms a valid
\eta
-separation for any
\eta\geq 1
.
Let
\mathcal{S}
denote the family constructed according to this procedure.

Consider
an unknown treewidth-
\eta
modulator
X
is
G
and let
\mathcal{S}^{-}_{X}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
denote the subset of those pairs
(C,S)
for which
X\cap(C\cup S)=\emptyset
.
Our goal is to show that
|\mathcal{S}^{-}_{X}|\leq\alpha\cdot|\mathcal{S}|
for some
\alpha(\eta)<1
.
First, for simplicity, assume that for every edge
uv
outgoing from
X
the pair
(\emptyset,\{u,v\})
belongs to
\mathcal{S}
.
Then it suffices to prove that the size of
\mathcal{S}^{-}_{X}
is bounded by
\mathcal{O}(|N(X)|)
.
This can be done using known techniques by analyzing a tree decomposition of
G-X
.
However, the assumption above is clearly unrealistic because we do not know the set
E(X,V(G)\setminus X)
and in general we cannot insert into
\mathcal{S}
pairs
(\emptyset,\{u,v\})
covered by larger
\eta
-separations as we want to keep the number of such pairs in
\mathcal{S}^{-}_{X}
bounded.

The problematic case occurs when there is some
(C,S)\in\mathcal{S}
that covers many edges outgoing from
X
and so the comparison to
|N(X)|
is no longer meaningful.
Our strategy is to
mark the vertices of
G-X
that appear as
S
-vertices
in some
(C,S)\in\mathcal{S}
covering an edge
e\in E(X,V(G)\setminus X)
.

We show that in fact the size of
\mathcal{S}^{-}_{X}
is proportional to the number of marked vertices, which is bounded by

\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{S}^{-}_{X}|)
.
The proof is based on a case analysis inspecting the relative location of
(C,S)\in\mathcal{S}^{-}_{X}
with respect to the marked set.
We employ a win-win argument: in the problematic case the existence of a large simple
\eta
-separation covering many edges in
E(X,V(G)\setminus X)
implies that a large piece of
G-X
cannot contain simple
\eta
-separations that are inclusion-wise maximal (see
Figure

1
).
These arguments allow us to bound the number of pairs
(C,S)\in\mathcal{S}^{-}_{X}
for which
C
is disjoint from the marked set.
To handle the remaining pairs, we make a connection between simple
\eta
-separations and important separators.
A key observation is that when

(C,S)
is an inclusion-wise maximal simple
\eta
-separation with
X\cap(C\cup S)=\emptyset
and
v\in C
then
S
forms an important
(v,X)
-separator.
The number of such separators of size
\leq 2\eta+2
is bounded by a function of
\eta
for each marked vertex
v\in V(G-X)
which results in the claimed bound.

For any

Treewidth.

A tree decomposition of a graph

For each

2.

For each edge

The

Lemma 3.2

(
[
18
, §7]
)
.

For every graph

|E(G)|\leq\mathrm{\textbf{tw}}(G)\cdot|V(G)|
.

A set

is a

.
We say that a vertex set

\mathrm{\textbf{tw}}(G[A])\leq\eta

Definition 3.3
.

Let

and

be a graph.

-modulator hitting family for

(i)

is non-empty,
(ii)

\eta

.

We will use the following concept in the analysis of tree decompositions.

.

Let

(
[

Let

Minors.

The operation of contracting an edge

introduces a new vertex adjacent to all of

, after which
u
and

are deleted. We say that

We say that

Let
\mathcal{F}

Whenever

contains a planar graph, there exists a constant

[
17
]

Conversely, the class of graphs of treewidth bounded by

of forbidden minors that contains a planar graph

63
]
.

The condition

by the classic result of Robertson and Seymour
[

, however there are no explicit upper bounds on the constant hidden in the

-notation.
As we want to design constructive algorithms,
we will rely on the simpler algorithm by Bodlaender, which is sufficient for our applications.

Theorem 3.6

(
[
11
]
)
.

There is a computable function

of graphs containing a planar graph,

-minor-free in time

.

The following definitions are imported from the work

Let

Martingales.

We will need the following concept from probability theory.

Definition 3.12
.

A sequence of random variables
\mathbf{X}=(X_{0},X_{1},X_{2},\dots)
is called a supermartingale if for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
it holds that (i)
\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{k}|\right]<\infty
and (ii)

\mathbb{E}\left[X_{k+1}\mid X_{0},\dots,X_{k}\right]\leq X_{k}
.

A random variable
\tau\in\mathbb{N}
is called a stopping time with respect to the process
\mathbf{X}
if for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
the event
(\tau\leq k)
depends only on
X_{0},\dots,X_{k}
.

The following theorem is also known in more general forms when the variable
\tau
may be unbounded but in our applications
\tau
will be always bounded by the number of vertices in the input graph.
Intuitively, it says that when we observe the values
X_{0},X_{1},\dots
before choosing
X_{\tau}
, then the optimal strategy maximizing
X_{\tau}
is to quit at the very beginning with
\tau=0
.

Theorem 3.13

(Doob’s Optional-Stopping Theorem
[
36
, §12.5]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{X}=(X_{0},X_{1},X_{2},\dots)

be a supermartingale and
\tau
be a stopping time
with respect to
\mathbf{X}
.
Suppose that
\mathbb{P}\left[\tau\leq n\right]=1
for some integer
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
If so, then
\mathbb{E}\left[X_{\tau}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[X_{0}\right]
.

Constructing a modulator hitting family

This section is devoted to the proof of
Theorem

.
First, we need several additional definitions (to be used only within this section).
Instead of working directly with protrusions we will phrase the construction in terms of
\eta
-separations
.

Definition 4.1
.

For a graph
G
and
\eta\geq 1
, an
\eta
-separation is a pair
(C,S)
such that
C,S\subseteq V(G)
,
N_{G}(C)\subseteq S
,
\mathrm{\textbf{tw}}(G[C])\leq\eta
, and
|S|\leq 2\eta+2
.

An
\eta
-separation

.

An
\eta
-separation
(C,S)
is called semi-simple if for every

\eta
-separation.

We define a partial order relation on

-separations

as
C_{1}\subseteq C_{2}
.

We could alternatively define an
\eta
-separation with a second parameter bounding
|S|
but it would be pointless as we never consider other bounds than

.
This bound corresponds to the maximal size of a bag in a tree decomposition of width
\eta

Since
C_{1}\subseteq C_{2}
implies

we can observe the following.

Observation 4.2
.

If
(C_{1},S_{1})
and
(C_{2},S_{2})

\eta
-separations and
(C_{1},S_{1})\vartriangleleft(C_{2},S_{2})

(C_{1}\cup S_{1})\subseteq(C_{2}\cup S_{2})
.

We say that a simple
\eta
-separation
(C_{1},S_{1})
is
\vartriangleleft
-maximal if there is no other simple
\eta
-separation
(C_{2},S_{2})
for which
(C_{1},S_{1})\vartriangleleft(C_{2},S_{2})
.

When
(C,S)
is an
\eta
-separation and

is a tree decomposition of

of width
\leq\eta
, we can easily turn it into a tree decomposition of

of width

by inserting the vertices of

into every bag of

.
Consequently, we have the following.

Observation 4.3
.

If
(C,S)
is an
\eta
-separation in
G
, then
C\cup S
is a
(3\eta+2)
-protrusion in
G
.

The definition of a simple
\eta
-separation is tailored to make a convenient connection with important separators.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
X\subseteq V(G)
satisfy
\mathrm{\textbf{tw}}(G-X)\leq\eta

(C,S)
be a simple
\eta
-separation that is
\vartriangleleft
-maximal, and

.
If

then

is an important
(v,X)
-separator.

Proof.

Since
v\in C\subseteq V(G)\setminus X
and
N(C)=S\subseteq V(G)\setminus X
, the set
S
is a
(v,X)
-separator.
We argue that

In the following construction we will use an equivalence relation

Note that when each

Definition 4.5
.

We construct the family
{\mathcal{S}}(G,\eta)
as follows.

1.

Let
\mathcal{S}_{all}(G,\eta)
be the set of all simple
\eta
-separations in

.

2.

\mathcal{S}_{max}(G,\eta)
be the set of those elements in

that are
\vartriangleleft
-maximal.

3.

Initialize
{\mathcal{S}}(G,\eta)
as empty.

4.

For each
(\approx)
-equivalence class in
\mathcal{S}_{max}(G,\eta)
insert its merge into
{\mathcal{S}}(G,\eta)
.

5.

For each

, if there is no

for which

, insert the pair
(\emptyset,\{u,v\})
into
{\mathcal{S}}(G,\eta)
.

We will refer to the pairs considered in the last step as

.
Note that every edge-pair forms an
\eta
-separation for any
\eta\geq 1
.

We make note of several important properties of this construction, to be used in the main proof.

Observation 4.6
.

The family
{\mathcal{S}}(G,\eta)
enjoys the following properties.

1.

Each element of
{\mathcal{S}}(G,\eta)
is either an edge-pair or a semi-simple
\eta
-separation.

2.

If
(C,S)\in{\mathcal{S}}(G,\eta)
is an edge-pair
(C,S)=(\emptyset,\{u,v\})
then there exists no simple
\eta
-separation
(C^{\prime},S^{\prime})
in
G
for which
\{u,v\}\subseteq C^{\prime}\cup S^{\prime}
.

3.

If
(C,S)\in{\mathcal{S}}(G,\eta)
is semi-simple and
v\in C
then
(R_{G-S}(v),S)
is a
\vartriangleleft
-maximal simple
\eta
-separation.

4.

For each edge
uv\in E(G)
there exists
(C,S)\in{\mathcal{S}}(G,\eta)
with
\{u,v\}\subseteq C\cup S
.

5.

For each

there is at most one

for which
(C,S)\in{\mathcal{S}}(G,\eta)
.

Lemma 4.7
.

For an
n
-vertex graph
G
, the family

has size

and it can be computed in time
n^{\mathcal{O}(\eta)}
.

Proof.

Each simple
\eta
-separation
(C,S)
can be characterized by the choice of
S
and the choice of a single connected component of
G-S
.
There are
n^{2\eta+2}
possibilities for

and
n
possibilities to choose a component of

Therefore,
|\mathcal{S}_{all}(G,\eta)|\leq n^{\mathcal{O}(\eta)}
.

It suffices to observe that
|{\mathcal{S}}(G,\eta)|\leq|\mathcal{S}_{max}(G,\eta)|+n^{2}\leq|\mathcal{S}_{all}(G,\eta)|+n^{2}

Identification of the
\vartriangleleft
-maximal elements and the

-equivalence classes can be easily performed in polynomial time with respect to the size of
\mathcal{S}_{all}(G,\eta)
.
∎

We explain how to enumerate the relevant partial solutions within an

A similar idea has been used for trimming the space of partial solutions in DP algorithms on tree decomposition
[

]
.
Our treatment of protrusions is a combination of ideas from
[

]
for processing protrusion decompositions and from
[

]
for processing hybrid graph decompositions.
We adapt their methods to the weighted setting.

Definition 5.1

(
[
40
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a finite family of graphs and
A
be a vertex subset in a node-weighted graph
G
.
We say that a family
\mathcal{A}\subseteq 2^{A}
of subsets of
A
is
(A,\mathcal{F})
-exhaustive if for every
\mathcal{F}
-minor hitting set
X
there exists
X_{A}\in\mathcal{A}
such that
X^{\prime}=(X\setminus A)\cup X_{A}
is also an
\mathcal{F}
-minor hitting set and
w(X^{\prime})\leq w(X)
.

Additionally, for
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
we say that a family
\mathcal{A}\subseteq 2^{A}
of size-
(\leq\ell)
subsets of
A
is
(A,\mathcal{F},\ell)
-exhaustive if for every
\mathcal{F}
-minor hitting set
X
satisfying
|X\cap A|\leq\ell
there exists
X_{A}\in\mathcal{A}
such that
X^{\prime}=(X\setminus A)\cup X_{A}
is also an
\mathcal{F}
-minor hitting set and
w(X^{\prime})\leq w(X)
.

When
X
is an optimal solution then the replacement
X_{A}
cannot be cheaper than
X\cap A
as otherwise we would have
w(X^{\prime})<w(X)
.
In particular, whenever
X
is optimal and
X\cap A
is non-empty then
w(X_{A})=w(X\cap A)>0
so
X_{A}
must be non-empty as well.

Observation 5.2
.

Let
X
be an optimal
\mathcal{F}
-minor hitting set in
G
,
A
be a vertex subset intersecting
X
, and
\mathcal{A}\subseteq 2^{A}
be an
(A,\mathcal{F})
-exhaustive family.
Then there exists a non-empty
X_{A}\in\mathcal{A}
such that
\mathsf{opt}_{\mathcal{F}}(G)-\mathsf{opt}_{\mathcal{F}}(G-X_{A})=w(X_{A})
.

An analogous statement holds for
k
-optimal solutions as well but the justification is slightly less obvious so we provide a full proof.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
k,\ell\geq 1
,
X
be a
k
-optimal
\mathcal{F}
-minor hitting set in
G
,
A
be a vertex subset satisfying
|X\cap A|=\ell
, and
\mathcal{A}\subseteq 2^{A}
be an
(A,\mathcal{F},\ell)
-exhaustive family.
Then there exists a non-empty
X_{A}\in\mathcal{A}
such that
\mathsf{opt}_{\mathcal{F},k}(G)-\mathsf{opt}_{\mathcal{F},k-\ell}(G-X_{A})=w(X_{A})
.

Proof.

By the definition of an
(A,\mathcal{F},\ell)
-exhaustive family there exists
X_{A}\in\mathcal{A}
such that
|X_{A}|\leq\ell
,
X^{\prime}=(X\setminus A)\cup X_{A}
is an
\mathcal{F}
-minor hitting set, and
w(X^{\prime})\leq w(X)
.
We have
|X^{\prime}|=|X\setminus A|+|X_{A}|\leq(k-\ell)+\ell=k
.
But
X
is
k
-optimal so in fact we must have
w(X^{\prime})=w(X)
and
w(X_{A})=w(X\cap A)
.
In particular, this implies that
X_{A}\neq\emptyset
because
w(X\cap A)>0
by assumption.
Next,
X\setminus A
forms an
\mathcal{F}
-minor hitting set in
G-X_{A}
of size
\leq k-\ell
so
\mathsf{opt}_{\mathcal{F},k-\ell}(G-X_{A})\leq w(X\setminus A)=w(X)-w(X\cap A)=\mathsf{opt}_{\mathcal{F},k}(G)-w(X_{A})
.
On the other hand, we have
\mathsf{opt}_{\mathcal{F},k}(G)\leq\mathsf{opt}_{\mathcal{F},k-\ell}(G-X_{A})+w(X_{A})
so in fact these two quantities must be equal.
∎

We take advantage of the fact that when
A
is an
r
-protrusion then the graph
G-A
can “communicate” with
A
only through
\partial(A)
.
To construct the exhaustive families, we utilize the bounds on the minimum-sized representatives in the
the relation
\equiv_{h}
over boundaried graphs
[
9
]
.
The following proof is similar in spirit to
[
41
, Lemma 5.33]
.
The difference is that here we compute partial solutions using an FPT algorithm parameterized by treewidth while therein they were computed with an FPT algorithm parameterized by the solution size.

Lemma 5.4
.

There is a computable function
f\colon\mathbb{N}^{2}\to\mathbb{N}
so that the following holds.
Let
r,h\in\mathbb{N}
and

\mathcal{F}
be a family of graphs with sizes bounded by
h
.
Next, let
A\subseteq V(G)
be an
r
-protrusion in a graph
G
.
Then there exists an
(A,\mathcal{F})
-exhaustive family
\mathcal{A}\subseteq 2^{A}
of size at most
f(h,r)
and it can be computed in time
f(h,r)\cdot|V(G)|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
.

Furthermore, for each
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
there exists an
(A,\mathcal{F},\ell)
-exhaustive family
\mathcal{A}\subseteq 2^{A}
of size at most
f(h,r)
and it can be computed in time
f(h,r)\cdot|V(G)|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
.

Proof.

Recall that
\partial_{G}(A)=N_{G}(V(G)\setminus A)
.
We will also denote
A\setminus\partial_{G}(A)
by
\triangle_{G}(A)
.
We first give an algorithm to construct a family
\mathcal{A}\subseteq 2^{A}
and then we will prove that it is
(A,\mathcal{F})
-exhaustive.

We begin by computing families
\mathcal{R}_{h}^{(\ell)}
for each
\ell\in[r]
(
Corollary

3.10
).
For each subset
S\subseteq\partial_{G}(A)
consider the graph
G-S
, in which
A\setminus S
still forms an
r
-protrusion. Let
\ell=|\partial_{G}(A)\setminus S|
and consider the
boundaried graph
\mathbf{G}_{S}=(G[A\setminus S],\partial_{G-S}(A\setminus S),\rho)
where
\rho
is an arbitrary labeling.
Next, for each
\mathbf{H}\in\mathcal{R}_{h}^{(\ell)}
that is compatible with
\mathbf{G}_{S}
, consider the graph
G_{S,H}=\mathbf{G}_{S}\oplus\mathbf{H}
.
We define the weight function on
G_{S,H}
by preserving the weights from
G
on
\triangle_{G}(A)
and setting the remaining weights to
\infty
.
Note that the size of
\mathbf{H}
is bounded by some function of
h
and
r
(
Corollary

3.10
) so the treewidth of
G_{S,H}
is likewise bounded.
Therefore, we can take advantage of
Theorem

3.11
to solve
Weighted
\mathcal{F}
-M-Deletion
on
G_{S,H}
in time
f(h,r)\cdot|V(G_{S,H})|^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
; let
X_{H}
denote the computed optimal solution.
If there is no finite-weight solution then we move on to the next
\mathbf{H}\in\mathcal{R}_{h}^{(\ell)}
, otherwise we have
X_{H}\subseteq\triangle_{G}(A)
. In this case we insert
S\cup X_{H}
into
\mathcal{A}
.
The size of
\mathcal{A}
can be bounded by the number of choices of
S
, that is,
2^{r}
, times the bound on
|\mathcal{R}_{h}^{(\ell)}|
, for
\ell\leq r
, which follows from
Corollary

3.10
.

We argue that
\mathcal{A}
is
(A,\mathcal{F})
-exhaustive.
Consider an
\mathcal{F}
-minor hitting set
X
. Let
\widehat{S}=X\cap\partial_{G}(A)
and
\mathbf{G}^{X}=(G-(X\cup\triangle_{G}(A)),\partial_{G}(A)\setminus X,\rho)
; note that
\mathbf{G}^{X}\oplus\mathbf{G}_{\widehat{S}}=G-(X\setminus\triangle_{G}(A))
.
Next, let
\mathbf{H}\in\mathcal{R}_{h}^{(\ell)}
be
a boundaried graph that is
\equiv_{h}
-equivalent to
\mathbf{G}^{X}
.
By
Observation

3.8
set
Y\subseteq\triangle_{G}(A)
is an
\mathcal{F}
-minor hitting set in
\mathbf{G}^{X}\oplus\mathbf{G}_{\widehat{S}}
if and only if it is an
\mathcal{F}
-minor hitting set in
\mathbf{H}\oplus\mathbf{G}_{\widehat{S}}
.
Consequently,
X\cap\triangle_{G}(A)
is an
\mathcal{F}
-minor hitting set in
\mathbf{H}\oplus\mathbf{G}_{\widehat{S}}
.
This means that the algorithm above must have detected some
X_{H}\subseteq\triangle_{G}(A)
of weight
w(X_{H})\leq w(X\cap\triangle_{G}(A))
and inserted
\widehat{S}\cup X_{H}
into
\mathcal{A}
.
On the other hand,
X_{H}
forms an
\mathcal{F}
-minor hitting set in
\mathbf{G}^{X}\oplus\mathbf{G}_{\widehat{S}}=G-(X\setminus\triangle_{G}(A))
.
So
X^{\prime}=(X\setminus\triangle_{G}(A))\cup X_{H}
is an
\mathcal{F}
-minor hitting set in
G
and
w(X^{\prime})\leq w(X)
.
As
X\cap\partial_{G}(A)=\widehat{S}
we can alternatively write
X^{\prime}=(X\setminus A)\cup(\widehat{S}\cup X_{H})
.
This means that
\mathcal{A}
includes a replacement for
X\cap A
and proves that
\mathcal{A}
is indeed
(A,\mathcal{F})
-exhaustive.

Constructing an
(A,\mathcal{F},\ell)
-exhaustive family is analogous.
The only difference is that when considering the graph
G_{S,H}
we compute an
\ell
-optimal solution, i.e., a minimum-weight
\mathcal{F}
-minor hitting set among those on at most
\ell
vertices.
This is doable within the same time bound (
Theorem

3.11
).∎

Applications

6.1
The approximation algorithm

For the ease of presentation, we first give an algorithm for
Weighted Treewidth-
\eta
Deletion
and then use it as a subroutine to solve general
Weighted Planar
\mathcal{F}
-M-Deletion
.
We begin with the analysis of a single iteration and compare the expected drop in the optimum to the expected cost of the sampled partial solution.

Lemma 6.1
.

For each
\eta\in\mathbb{N}
there is a constant
c\in\mathbb{N}
and an algorithm that, given a node-weighted
n
-vertex graph
G
with
\mathrm{\textbf{tw}}(G)>\eta
, runs in time
\mathcal{O}_{\eta}\left(n^{\mathcal{O}(\eta)}\right)
, and outputs a non-empty random subset
Y\subseteq V(G)
with a following guarantee.

\mathbb{E}\left[w(Y)\right]\leq c\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{opt}_{\eta}(G)-\mathsf{opt}_{\eta}(G-Y)\right]

Proof.

We apply
Theorem

1.1
to compute an
(\eta,r,c_{1})
-modulator hitting family
\mathcal{P}
for
G
with
r=\mathcal{O}(\eta)
and
c_{1}=2^{\mathcal{O}(\eta)}
.
This takes time
n^{\mathcal{O}(\eta)}
. Next, we use
Lemma

5.4
to compute, for each
A\in\mathcal{P}
, an
(A,\mathcal{F}(\eta))
-exhaustive family
\mathcal{A}_{A}
of size
\leq c_{2}
, where
c_{2}=f(\max_{F\in\mathcal{F}(\eta)}|V(F)|,r)
and
f
is the function from that lemma.
This takes time
n^{\mathcal{O}(\eta)}\cdot\mathcal{O}_{\eta}\left(n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\right)
.
Note that
c_{1},c_{2}
are constants depending only on
\eta
and
recall that a graph has treewidth bounded by
\eta
if and only if it is
\mathcal{F}(\eta)
-minor-free so
\mathsf{opt}_{\eta}(G)=\mathsf{opt}_{\mathcal{F}(\eta)}(G)
.
Now, consider the family
\mathcal{B}
of all pairs
(A,Y)
where
A\in\mathcal{P}
and
Y\in\mathcal{A}_{A}
is non-empty.
We sample an element
(A,Y)
from
\mathcal{B}
with probability proportional to
\frac{1}{w(Y)}
.
Note that
w(Y)>0
because
Y\neq\emptyset
.
Let
\widehat{Y}
denote the corresponding random variable.

We claim that the random variable
\widehat{Y}
satisfies the guarantee of the lemma.
Let
W=\sum_{(A,Y)\in\mathcal{B}}\frac{1}{w(Y)}
.
Then the probability of choosing a pair
(A,Y)
equals
\frac{1}{w(Y)\cdot W}
.
We have

\mathbb{E}\left[w(\widehat{Y})\right]=\sum_{(A,Y)\in\mathcal{B}}w(Y)\cdot\frac{1}{w(Y)\cdot W}=\frac{|\mathcal{B}|}{W}.

Now we estimate the expected value of
\mathsf{opt}_{\eta}(G)-\mathsf{opt}_{\eta}(G-\widehat{Y})
.
Clearly, for every
Y
this quantity is non-negative.
Let
X
be an optimal solution to
Weighted Treewidth-
\eta
Deletion
on
G
.
By the definition of a modulator hitting family, we know that there is a subfamily
\mathcal{P}_{X}\subseteq\mathcal{P}
of size
at least
|\mathcal{P}|/c_{1}
so that for each
A\in\mathcal{P}_{X}
we have
A\cap X\neq\emptyset
.
Furthermore,
Observation

5.2
implies that for each
A\in\mathcal{P}_{X}
there is some non-empty
Y_{A}\in\mathcal{A}_{A}
satisfying
\mathsf{opt}_{\eta}(G)-\mathsf{opt}_{\eta}(G-Y_{A})=w(Y_{A})
.
In particular, we have
(A,Y_{A})\in\mathcal{B}

and we can estimate

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{opt}_{\eta}(G)-\mathsf{opt}_{\eta}(G-\widehat{Y})\right]\geq\sum_{A\in\mathcal{P}_{X}}w(Y_{A})\cdot\frac{1}{w(Y_{A})\cdot W}=\frac{|\mathcal{P}_{X}|}{W}.

It holds that
|\mathcal{B}|\leq c_{2}\cdot|\mathcal{P}|\leq c_{2}\cdot c_{1}\cdot|\mathcal{P}_{X}|
.
Hence the claimed inequality holds for
c=c_{1}\cdot c_{2}
.
∎

In the proof it was important that when
Y\subseteq V(G)
belongs to multiple
(A_{i},\mathcal{F}(\eta))
-exhaustive families for different sets
(A_{i})
then we consider all its copies in the family
\mathcal{B}
.
Alternatively, we could multiply the probability of choosing
Y
by the number of such sets
(A_{i})
.
By the same reason we had to include the factor
\deg(v)
in the probabilities in the simplified algorithm for
Weighted

Feedback Vertex Set
in
Section

2
.

We will perform the described sampling iteratively.
At each step, the expected value of the revealed part of a solution is within a factor
c
from the cost of the chosen set
Y
.
The algorithm is terminated when the value of optimum drops to 0, so the expected value of
w(Y_{1})+w(Y_{2})+\dots
should be bounded by
c
times the initial optimum.
We will formalize this intuition by analyzing a random process being a supermartingale (see
Section

3
).

Lemma 6.2
.

For each
\eta\in\mathbb{N}
there is a constant
c\in\mathbb{N}
and a randomized algorithm that, given a node-weighted
n
-vertex graph
G
graph
G
, runs in time
\mathcal{O}_{\eta}\left(n^{\mathcal{O}(\eta)}\right)
, and outputs a treewidth-
\eta
modulator
X
in
G
such that
\mathbb{E}\left[w(X)\right]\leq c\cdot\mathsf{opt}_{\eta}(G)
.

Proof.

We will use the constant
c
defined in
Lemma

6.1
.
Consider the following random process
(X_{i})
where
X_{i}\subseteq V(G)
.
We start with
X_{0}=\emptyset
. Next, for
i\geq 0
we compute a random non-empty subset
Y_{i+1}\subseteq V(G)\setminus X_{i}
by applying
Lemma

6.1
to the graph
G_{i}=G-X_{i}
.
Then we set
X_{i+1}=X_{i}\cup Y_{i+1}
.
The process stops at iteration
\tau
when
\mathrm{\textbf{tw}}(G_{\tau})
becomes bounded by
\eta
and the algorithm returns
X=X_{\tau}
.
Note that
X_{i}
grows at each iteration so
\tau
is always bounded by
|V(G)|
.
We will show that
X
is a
c
-approximate solution in expectation.

We claim that the process
P_{i}=w(X_{i})+c\cdot\mathsf{opt}_{\eta}(G_{i})
is a supermartingale, which means that

\mathbb{E}\left[P_{i+1}\mid P_{0},\dots P_{i}\right]\leq P_{i}
.
This condition is equivalent to
\mathbb{E}\left[P_{i+1}-P_{i}\mid P_{0},\dots P_{i}\right]\leq 0
.

P_{i+1}-P_{i}=w(Y_{i+1})+c\cdot\left(\mathsf{opt}_{\eta}(G_{i+1})-\mathsf{opt}_{\eta}(G_{i})\right)=w(Y_{i+1})-c\cdot\left(\mathsf{opt}_{\eta}(G_{i})-\mathsf{opt}_{\eta}(G_{i}-Y_{i+1})\right)

Lemma

6.1
states that the expected value of such a random variable is upper bounded by 0 for any fixed graph
G_{i}
. Hence the process
(P_{i})
is indeed a supermartingale.
Consider the stopping time
\tau
, for which it holds that
\mathsf{opt}_{\eta}(G_{\tau})=0
.
We can now take advantage of the Doob’s Optional-Stopping Theorem (
3.13
) which states that
\mathbb{E}\left[P_{\tau}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[P_{0}\right]
.

\mathbb{E}\left[w(X)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[w(X_{\tau})+c\cdot\mathsf{opt}_{\eta}(G_{\tau})\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[P_{\tau}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[P_{0}\right]=w(X_{0})+c\cdot\mathsf{opt}_{\eta}(G_{0})=c\cdot\mathsf{opt}_{\eta}(G)

This concludes the proof.
∎

Finally, we solve
Weighted Planar
\mathcal{F}
-M-Deletion
by first reducing the treewidth to
\eta(\mathcal{F})
.

See
1.2

Proof.

Any
\mathcal{F}
-minor hitting set is also a treewidth-
\eta(\mathcal{F})
modulator so
it holds that
\mathsf{opt}_{\eta(\mathcal{F})}(G)\leq\mathsf{opt}_{\mathcal{F}}(G)
.
We apply
Lemma

6.2
to compute a set
X_{1}\subseteq V(G)
such that
\mathrm{\textbf{tw}}(G-X_{1})\leq\eta(\mathcal{F})
and
\mathbb{E}\left[w(X_{1})\right]\leq c\cdot\mathsf{opt}_{\eta(\mathcal{F})}(G)
.
Since the treewidth of
G-X_{1}
is bounded, we can now solve
Weighted
\mathcal{F}
-M-Deletion
optimally on
G-X_{1}
using
Theorem

3.11
.
We compute
X_{2}\subseteq V(G)\setminus X_{1}
such that
w(X_{2})=\mathsf{opt}_{\mathcal{F}}(G-X_{1})\leq\mathsf{opt}_{\mathcal{F}}(G)
.
The union
X_{1}\cup X_{2}
forms a solution that is
(c+1)
-approximate in expectation.
∎

6.2
The single-exponential FPT algorithm

Unlike for approximation, this time we cannot afford to first solve
Weighted Treewidth-
\eta
Deletion
and then process a bounded-treewidth graph, so we will treat the bounded-treewidth scenario as a special case in the branching algorithm.
The main difference compared to
Theorem

1.2
is that now we will sample both the protrusion
A\in\mathcal{P}
and the replacement
Y\in\mathcal{A}
according to a uniform distribution.
We also need to guess the integer
\ell=|X\cap A|
to guide the construction
of an
(A,\mathcal{F},\ell)
-exhaustive family;
note that we can compute such a family of size
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{F}}(1)
only when
\ell
is known.
However, we cannot sample
\ell
uniformly from
[1,k]
because when the correct value of
\ell
is always
\mathcal{O}(1)
we need to achieve constant success probability in every step.
As a remedy, we will use geometric distribution to sample
\ell
.

See
1.3

Proof.

We will abbreviate
\eta=\eta(\mathcal{F})
.
Consider an instance
(G,k)
and proceed as follows.
If
k=0
check if
G

is
\mathcal{F}
-minor-free in time
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{F}}(n)
(
Theorem

3.6
);
if yes return an empty set and otherwise abort the computation.
If
\mathrm{\textbf{tw}}(G)\leq\eta
solve the problem optimally in time
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{F}}(n^{\mathcal{O}(1)})
using
Theorem

3.11
.

We will assume now that
k\geq 1
and
\mathrm{\textbf{tw}}(G)>\eta
.
In order to prepare a recursive call, we make three random choices.

1.

Use
Theorem

1.1

to compute an
(\eta,r,c_{1})
-modulator hitting family
\mathcal{P}
for
r=\mathcal{O}(\eta)
and
c_{1}=2^{\mathcal{O}(\eta)}
.
This takes time
n^{\mathcal{O}(\eta)}
.
Then choose a set
A\in\mathcal{P}
uniformly at random.

2.

Consider a random variable
L\in[1,k]
where the probability of choosing
L=\ell
is proportional to
2^{-\ell}
.
Note that we have
\mathbb{P}\left[L=\ell\right]\geq 2^{-\ell}
for each
\ell\in[1,k]
.

3.

Use
Lemma

5.4
to compute an
(A,\mathcal{F},L)
-exhaustive family
\mathcal{A}
of size
\leq c_{2}
, where
c_{2}=f(\max_{F\in\mathcal{F}}|V(F)|,r)
and
f
is the function from that lemma. This takes time
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{F}}(n^{\mathcal{O}(1)})
.
Then choose a non-empty set
Y\in\mathcal{A}
again uniformly at random (if
\mathcal{A}=\{\emptyset\}
abort the computation).
Add
Y
to the solution and recurse on
(G-Y,k-L)
.

Let
Q(G,k)
denote the event that
the algorithm returns a
k
-optimal solution to the instance
(G,k)
under an assumption that
\mathsf{opt}_{\mathcal{F},k}(G)<\infty
. We will prove that
\mathbb{P}\left[Q(G,k)\right]\geq(2c_{1}c_{2})^{-k}
by induction on
k
.
The claim clearly holds for
k=0
so we will
assume that
k>0
and that the claim holds for
all
k^{\prime}<k
.
First, if
\mathrm{\textbf{tw}}(G)\leq\eta
then the algorithm always returns a
k
-optimal solution.
We will now assume
\mathrm{\textbf{tw}}(G)>\eta
and analyze the three random guesses performed before a recursive call.

Let
X
be a fixed
k
-optimal solution to
Weighted
\mathcal{F}
-M-Deletion
.
It is also a solution to
Weighted Treewidth-
\eta
Deletion
. By
Definition

3.3
of an
(\eta,r,c_{1})
-modulator hitting family, we know that
\mathbb{P}\left[A\cap X\neq\emptyset\right]\geq 1/c_{1}
for a random choice of
A\in\mathcal{P}
.
Suppose that
A
has been chosen successfully and let
\ell_{A}=|A\cap X|\geq 1
.
Then
\mathbb{P}\left[L=\ell_{A}\right]\geq 2^{-\ell_{A}}
.

Lemma

5.3
states that the
(A,\mathcal{F},\ell_{A})
-exhaustive family
\mathcal{A}
contains a non-empty set
X_{A}
of size
\leq\ell_{A}
for which
\mathsf{opt}_{\mathcal{F},k}(G)-\mathsf{opt}_{\mathcal{F},k-\ell_{A}}(G-X_{A})=w(X_{A})
.
The probability of choosing
Y=X_{A}
in the third step is
1/|\mathcal{A}|\geq 1/c_{2}
.
The call
(G,k)
will be processed correctly if all these guesses are correct and the call
(G-X_{A},k-\ell_{A})
is processed correctly.
We are ready to estimate the probability of
Q(G,k)
, by first analyzing the success probability for a fixed
A\in\mathcal{P}
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[Q(G,k)\mid A\cap X\neq\emptyset\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}\left[L=\ell_{A}\right]\cdot\mathbb{P}\left[Y=X_{A}\right]\cdot\mathbb{P}\left[Q(G-X_{A},k-\ell_{A})\right]

\displaystyle\geq 2^{-\ell_{A}}\cdot c_{2}^{-1}\cdot(2c_{1}c_{2})^{-(k-\ell_{A})}\geq(2c_{2})^{-1}\cdot(2c_{1}c_{2})^{-(k-1)}

We have used the inductive assumption and the fact that
\ell_{A}\geq 1
when
A\cap X\neq\emptyset
.

\mathbb{P}\left[Q(G,k)\right]\geq\mathbb{P}\left[A\cap X\neq\emptyset\right]\cdot\mathbb{P}\left[Q(G,k)\mid A\cap X\neq\emptyset\right]\geq c_{1}^{-1}\cdot(2c_{2})^{-1}\cdot(2c_{1}c_{2})^{-(k-1)}=(2c_{1}c_{2})^{-k}

We have thus proven that the success probability of the presented procedure is at least
c^{-k}
for
c=2c_{1}c_{2}
whereas its running time is
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{F}}\left(n^{\mathcal{O}(\eta)}\right)
.
Note that if the computation has not been aborted, then the returned solution has at most
k
vertices.
We repeat this procedure
c^{k}
many times and output the outcome with the smallest weight.
This gives constant probability of witnessing a
k
-optimal solution.
The total running time is
c^{k}\cdot\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{F}}\left(n^{\mathcal{O}(\eta)}\right)=2^{\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{F}}(k)}\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(\eta)}
.
∎

7
Conclusion

We have shown that constant approximation for
Planar

\mathcal{F}
-M-Deletion
can be extended to the weighted setting, by introducing a new technique that circumvents the need to perform weighted protrusion replacement.
There are several natural questions about potential refinements of
Theorem

1.2
.
Can the algorithm be derandomized?
Can we improve the running time so that the degree of the polynomial is a universal constant that does not depend on
\eta
? Can the approximation factor be bounded by a slowly growing function of
\eta
? All these improvements are possible in the unweighted realm
[
29
,
37
]
.
It is however unknown whether there exists a universal constant
c
for which every unweighted
Planar

\mathcal{F}
-M-Deletion
problem admits a
c
-approximation algorithm.

Even though our approximation factor is a computable function of the treewidth bound, this function grows very rapidly.
Is it possible to get
a “reasonable” approximation factor (say, under 100) for
Weighted Treewidth-2 Deletion
or
Weighted Outerplanar Deletion
?
In the unweighted setting,
the approximation factor (for both problems) can be pinned down to 40
[
23
,
37
]
.
As another potential direction, can we
combine the ideas from
Theorem

1.1
and the scaling lemma from
[
33
]
to
improve the status of
Weighted Planar
\mathcal{F}
-M-Deletion
on
H
-minor-free graphs from QPTAS to PTAS?

One message of our work is that
Planar

\mathcal{F}
-M-Deletion
does not get any harder for approximation or FPT algorithms when we consider weighted graphs.
What about a polynomial kernelization under parameterization by the number of vertices in the sought solution?
While this is well-understood on unweighted graphs
[
29
]
, we are only aware of a polynomial kernelization for

Weighted

Vertex Cover

[
24
]
.
This result could be possibly extended to the weighted variants of
Feedback Vertex Set
,
Treewidth-2 Deletion
, and
Outerplanar Deletion
, for which we know kernelization algorithms based on
explicit reductions rules
[
23
,
66
]
.
Does general
Weighted Planar
\mathcal{F}
-M-Deletion
admit a polynomial kernelization for every fixed family
\mathcal{F}
?
