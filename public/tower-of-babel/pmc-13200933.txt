A real-world pharmacovigilance study of four anti-non-small cell lung cancer drugs using the WHO-VigiAccess database
Authors: Chunyue Fang, Junzhu Wu, Chouqi Hu, Qirong Liu, Weijie Ma, Ling Zhang, Peng Hua, Yuzhen Ding, Yuanyuan Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The efficacy of anlotinib, gefitinib, afatinib, and osimertinib in the treatment of non-small cell lung cancer is well established, but there are gaps in the safety evidence. This study used real-world data from the World Health Organization-VigiAccess database to evaluate the post-marketing adverse drug reactions (ADRs) of these 4 drugs. ADRs data for anlotinib, gefitinib, afatinib, and osimertinib were collected from the VigiAccess database up to April 30, 2025. To identify and analyze potential ADR signals, the study employed the reporting odds ratio, proportional reporting ratio, multi-item gamma Poisson shrinker, and Bayesian confidence propagation neural network methods. A total of 52,978 adverse event reports were collected for 4 medications, comprising 1642 reports for anlotinib, 10,294 for gefitinib, 12,493 for afatinib, and 28,549 for osimertinib. In the system organ class, all these 4 drugs are generally associated with skin and subcutaneous tissue disorders and gastrointestinal disorders. However, the focus of these 4 drugs varies. In the preferred term classification for specific symptoms, some adverse reactions have high frequency and strong signal intensity rankings, and others have high frequency but not particularly prominent signal intensity. The adverse reactions of anlotinib, gefitinib, afatinib, and osimertinib share certain commonalities and characteristics, which are basically consistent with those described in the drug labels. However, there are also some specific ADRs. Clinically, personalized medication can be carried out based on the characteristics of the drugs and the patient’s condition.

1. Introduction

Among various types of lung cancer, non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for approximately 85% of cases.[1,2] The most common distant metastasis is brain metastasis. Anlotinib, gefitinib, afatinib, and osimertinib are all tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), and these 4 drugs are important treatment options for NSCLC, including brain metastases.

Anlotinib is a Chinese-origin multi-targeted anti-angiogenic small molecule TKIs, which can effectively inhibit vascular endothelial growth factor receptor, fibroblast growth factor receptor, platelet-derived growth factor receptor, and other targets, and also inhibits c-Kit, Ret, and other kinases.[3,4] It has antitumor angiogenesis and inhibition of tumor growth, and has a better therapeutic effect on patients with brain metastases from NSCLC. Gefitinib, afatinib, and osimertinib all belong to epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) TKIs, and they can effectively treat patients with EGFR mutations in NSCLC brain metastasis.

World Health Organization (WHO)-VigiAccess is one of the world’s largest publicly available databases of adverse drug reactions (ADRs), which was initiated by the WHO in 2015 to provide public access to information in the WHO global database, which reports on potential side effects of drugs. These side effects are technically known as ADRs and adverse events after immunization, which are reported by national pharmacovigilance centers or national drug regulatory agencies that participate in the WHO program for international drug monitoring.[5,6]

Although the efficacy of the aforementioned drugs is clear, there are still significant differences in their safety profiles, and most of the existing safety evidence is derived from phase III clinical trials, with insufficient research on adverse events (AEs) in complex real-world drug-use scenarios. Therefore, this study uses real-world data from the WHO Adverse Event Reporting System (VigiAccess) to assess the ADRs of the 4 drugs after their market launch, and analyzes their safety signals, providing an evidence-based basis for individualized clinical medication and risk management.

2. Methods

2.1. Data source

This study is a real-world retrospective pharmacovigilance research, and the data were obtained from the WHO-VigiAccess database. The search period concluded on April 30, 2025. The contents included the years, age groups, genders, geographical regions, as well as the number of reports on the system organ class (SOC) of ADRs and the number of reports on specific symptoms preferred term (PT) for the 4 drugs: anlotinib, gefitinib, afatinib, and osimertinib. In VigiAccess, when reports are submitted, 1 or more drugs are listed as “suspect” drugs that may have caused AEs. For each drug (anlotinib, gefitinib, afatinib, and osimertinib), the extraction we made includes all reports that list the drug as a “suspect” drug.

2.2. Data processing

VigiAccess data sources use a standardized de-duplication algorithm when entering data from national centers, which removes duplicates at the source. We calculated the relevant descriptive data, and descriptive analysis was used to characterize the adverse drug event reports related to anlotinib, gefitinib, afatinib, and osimertinib. Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) is a detailed and highly specialized set of medical terminology standards used to facilitate the international exchange of regulatory information on medical products.[7] By mapping AE to PT using MedDRA, the frequency of AE can be statistically analyzed and safety signals identified. Methods such as the reporting odds ratio (ROR) and proportional reporting ratio (PRR) can identify abnormally higher-than-expected proportions of reported ADR, thereby highlighting the risks associated with the use of a drug. The multi-item gamma Poisson shrinker (MGPS) is a statistical method for pharmacovigilance signal detection, which centers on the computation of empirical Bayes geometric mean (EBGM) based on the advantages of Bayesian statistical modeling, and can provide more reliable ADR association detection in the presence of sparse data.[8] Bayesian confidence propagation neural network (BCPNN), whose output values can be used to quantify the strength of the association between drugs and ADR, assisting clinical decision-making.[9] Signal detection algorithms (ROR, PRR, MGPS, and BCPNN) can handle the complex situation of combined medication use. They calculate the reporting imbalance by comparing the reporting frequency of a specific drug-ADR combination with the background reporting frequency of that ADR with all other drugs in the entire database. In this study, the ROR, the PRR, the MGPS, and the BCPNN combined with standardized data from MedDRA terminology to mine and analyze the signals of 4 drug-related ADRs. 2.3. Signal mining and analysis

A signal was detected when both the frequency and strength of the association between the target drug and the AE exceeded a predefined threshold, suggesting a potential disproportionality. Being simultaneously satisfied by all 4 algorithms is regarded as a positive signal: lower limit of 95% confidence interval (CI) > 1, a ≥ 3 (ROR method); PRR ≥ 2, χ2 ≥ 4, a ≥ 3 (PRR method); EBGM05 > 2 (MGPS method); IC025 > 0 (BCPNN method). Higher values of ROR and PRR indicate a stronger signal, reflecting a greater association between the target drug and the suspected AE. Subsequently, AE signals that surpass the thresholds for both report count and signal strength, as determined by the formula algorithm, were selected for further analysis. Data mining and statistical analyses are performed using Microsoft Excel, while visualization is carried out with Microsoft Excel and R 4.4.3.

3.2. SOC distribution

A total of 52,978 reports were gathered in this study, including 1642 for anlotinib, 10,294 for gefitinib, 12,493 for afatinib, and 28,549 for osimertinib. In terms of SOC distribution, anlotinib-related AEs primarily occurred in blood and lymphatic system disorders (566 cases, 23.2%), investigations (394 cases, 16.1%), skin and subcutaneous tissue disorders (304 cases, 12.4%), and gastrointestinal disorders (310 cases, 12.7%). 3.5. Newly emerged adverse reactions of drugs

The top 18-ranked PTs for the 4 drugs were compared with the ADRs recorded in the product specifications, and if a PT was not documented in the specification, it was considered a newly emerged adverse reaction. The newly appeared ADRs of the 4 drugs corresponded to the PTs marked with “*” in Tables 5 to 9. Examples include myelosuppression, granulocytopenia, and bicytopenia with anlotinib; retinal pigmentation, occult blood positive, and stridor with gefitinib; proctalgia, rhinalgia, dermatitis contact, and lip blister and sputum abnormal with afatinib; and pulmonary toxicity and eosinophilic pneumonia with osimertinib. For the emerging adverse reactions, attention should be focused on clinical practice to reduce the harm of the drug to the patient.

4. Discussion

4.1. Basic characteristics

All adverse reaction reports for anlotinib came exclusively from Asia (100%), likely because it is a domestically produced medication primarily used in that region. The pivotal phase III trial for anlotinib (ALTER0303) was conducted solely in China, involving patients from 31 centers across the country.[10] Anlotinib is considered a promising therapy for advanced NSCLC and has received approval from the China Food and Drug Administration,[11] but it has not been introduced to markets such as Europe and the United States. Similarly, reports for gefitinib (56%) and afatinib (55%) were predominantly from Asia. Compared to Western populations, the rate of EGFR mutations is significantly higher among Asians,[12] which may result in greater prescription rates of gefitinib and afatinib in Asia and consequently a higher frequency of reported adverse reactions.

Regarding age distribution, adverse reaction reports for all 4 medications were predominantly found in the 45 to 64 age group. This could be because aging leads to changes in body structure and function, along with a reduced capacity to maintain homeostasis, making the body more sensitive to these drugs.[13]

Apart from anlotinib, which had a male proportion of 56%, the reported ADRs for the other 3 drugs, about gefitinib (59%), afatinib (58%), and osimertinib (59%), showed a higher percentage in females compared to males. This could be linked to the greater prevalence of EGFR mutations in females.[14]

4.2. The SOC analysis of 4 drugs

In the SOC frequency ranking, AEs reported for anlotinib were primarily concentrated in blood and lymphatic system disorders (23.2%), investigations (16.1%), skin and subcutaneous tissue disorders (12.4%), and gastrointestinal disorders (12.7%), speculating that this drug may have notable toxic effects on the blood-forming system, digestive system, and skin.[15] The AEs of gefitinib primarily involve skin and subcutaneous tissue disorders (17.7%), general disorders and administration site conditions (13.8%), respiratory, thoracic and mediastinal disorders (10.5%), and gastrointestinal disorders (13.7%). These effects are likely linked to the high expression of EGFR in tissues such as the skin, gastrointestinal tract, and respiratory tract.[16,17] The drug’s inhibition of this receptor directly impacts the normal functions of these tissues, resulting in common side effects like rash, diarrhea, and interstitial pneumonia.

Afatinib mainly causes gastrointestinal disorders (29.2%) and skin and subcutaneous tissue disorders (20.7%), with a notably higher rate of gastrointestinal reactions compared to gefitinib. This may be due to its irreversible inhibition of EGFR and other erythroblastic leukemia viral oncogene homolog family receptors, where the stronger inhibitory action could worsen damage to the gastric mucosa.[18,19] Osimertinib shows a distinct pattern in the distribution of AEs, with general disorders and administration site conditions for 25.9%, a significantly higher proportion than other categories. This may be related to the third-generation drug’s specific inhibition of the EGFR T790M mutation.[20,21]

4.3. The PT analysis of 4 drugs

The PT discussion of the 2 dimensions of signal strength and frequency of reporting was significant in both frequency and signal strength (high frequency and high signal), suggesting that it requires special attention. For instance, anlotinib causes myelosuppression and increased blood pressure; osimertinib leads to prolonged electrocardiogram QT. Kartik Anand et al[22] found that osimertinib resulted in a higher ROR for heart failure, atrial fibrillation, and QT prolongation compared to other TKIs. Sarah Waliany et al[23] found that osimertinib was strongly associated with QT interval prolongation, supraventricular tachycardia, and heart failure relative to other EGFR (erlotinib, gefitinib, afatinib) inhibitors and targeted therapies. The results of this study showed that osimertinib resulted in a higher ROR for torsade de pointes (ROR = 29.05) and prolonged electrocardiogram QT (ROR = 14.17) compared to anlotinib, gefitinib, and afatinib, which was in agreement with their findings. High-frequency low signal suggests that these ADRs are relatively common. Examples include thrombocytopenia with anlotinib; interstitial lung disease with gefitinib. Low-frequency high signals require further evaluation with cohort or case-control studies to validate these potential risks, clarify causality, and more fully assess the safety of the drug. For example: anaphylactoid reaction of anlotinib; prerenal failure of afatinib. Crosnier et al[24] studied using data from the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System database found a significant disproportionate signal for renal failure with afatinib (ROR = 2.41, 95% CI = 1.78–3.27).

The imbalance analysis identifies statistical correlations rather than causal relationships. For complications such as bone marrow suppression or pulmonary toxicity, which are known to be late-stage NSCLC complications, possibly caused by previous treatments or related to the disease, the detected signals indicate that their reporting frequency is higher than expected, and warrant further in-depth study.

4.4. Limitations

Firstly, the voluntary nature of reporting in the database inevitably introduces reporting bias. Typically, serious and life-threatening ADRs are more likely to be actively reported by healthcare professionals or patients, whereas minor and persistent ADRs may be overlooked or underreported.

Secondly, the data is incomplete, such as the absence or inaccuracies of dosage, disease duration, and coexisting diseases. Moreover, it is impossible to conduct stratified queries by age, dosage, disease type, brain metastasis, etc. In this article, it will not be possible to explore whether the brain metastasis status affects the reporting of adverse reactions. The VigiAccess database cannot obtain clinical details such as the patient’s brain metastasis status, so the safety signals detected in this study reflect the correlation within the overall reporting population and cannot be analyzed by stratifying based on whether there is brain metastasis or not.

Thirdly, while adverse reaction reports come from regions including the Americas, Asia, Africa, Europe, and Oceania, the majority of reports are concentrated in Asia and the Americas, which may lead to regional differences in the research findings.

Fourthly, we cannot definitively attribute the signal to the target drug itself because reported ADRs may be influenced by coadministration, underlying cancer, or comorbidities. We recognize the possibility of indication bias (the disease itself causing ADR-like symptoms).

Fifth, and importantly, our study did not formally categorize or reconcile serious adverse event (SAE). Neither a fatal nor a nonfatal categorization of AE was conducted, nor was an assessment of expedited reporting timelines. This was mainly due to the inherent constraints of the WHO-VigiAccess database, which did not systematically record standardized SAE information and the necessary time limit information for analyzing AE fatal status or expedited reporting. This limitation prevented us from quantifying the frequency, severity, and risk of association with medications for SAEs, as well as from performing fatal or nonfatal categorization of AE and expediting the assessment of reporting timeframes. Therefore, the signal detection analyses in this paper were performed for all reported AEs, regardless of clinical severity. The identified signals and their intensities (such as high ROR values) reflect the reporting imbalance within the range of all reported events, rather than a specific association with severe clinical outcomes. It is recommended that prospective cohort studies be conducted to systematically classify and risk assess SAEs, conduct comprehensive analyses of the characteristics of fatal AE and compliance with rapid reporting, and provide more comprehensive evidence for drug safety risk management.

5. Conclusion

The research results provide important priority signals for the pharmacovigilance monitoring of anlotinib, gefitinib, afatinib, and osimertinib. For instance, they highlight cardiac safety as an area warranting focused evaluation for osimertinib, and gastrointestinal and renal events for afatinib. It is important to emphasize that the signals identified in this study are derived from disproportionality analysis of spontaneous reports. Their primary utility lies in generating hypotheses and prioritizing potential safety issues for further, more definitive research. They should not be interpreted as independent evidence for implementing specific risk minimization measures. In addition, the high-signal events not included in the drug instructions highlight the importance of continuous post-marketing safety monitoring. These signals obtained from real-world data can compensate for the limitations of clinical trials and provide a basis for hypothesis-based research, potential label updates, and active risk management. Future cohort studies or case-control studies are needed to verify these potential risks, clarify causality, and ultimately support the safe and precise application of the drugs.
