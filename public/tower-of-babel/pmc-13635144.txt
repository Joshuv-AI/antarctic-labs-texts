Ultra-low-power probabilistic graphical computing based on memristors enabling communications signal processing
Authors: Chunxiao Jiang, Jiawei Wang, Zhoujie Pan, Yanming Liu, Yuwan Hong, He Tian, Linling Kuang, Jianhua Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImproving the energy efficiency of baseband processors has become a critical challenge in mobile communications, especially for energy-constrained edge devices. Applications in conventional baseband signal processors usually adopt independently optimized algorithmic architectures and primarily rely on digital systems, whose energy efficiency is fundamentally limited by frequent data movement and computationally intensive dynamic operations. To overcome the fundamental energy-scaling bottleneck in modern signal processing, here we report a probabilistic graphical computing paradigm based on memristors, which not only unifies the framework of Bayesian inference-based baseband signal processing algorithms, but also achieves a substantial improvement in energy efficiency via memristor-based in-memory computing. Results demonstrate that the proposed approach achieves energy consumption below 10 pJ per bit and up to an 11.4-fold improvement in energy efficiency over conventional implementations, while maintaining error rates below 1×10−4 in the representative application. These results provide a flexible, noise-resilient route to ultra-low-power signal processing in edge devices.Subject terms: Electronic devices, Electrical and electronic engineering

Introduction

In edge communication devices operating under stringent energy constraints (e.g., RedCap 5G devices, LTE Cat.1/Cat.4 devices, 3GPP NTN edge terminals), the baseband signal processor overwhelmingly dominates the power budget of communication receivers1–3. For example, in fourth-generation long-term evolution (4G LTE) and fifth-generation (5G) mobile communications, the baseband processor consumes 71–85% and 69–79% of the receiver’s total power4, respectively. Therefore, its optimization is important for enabling sustained connectivity. Existing baseband signal processing applications encompass multiple-input and multiple-output (MIMO) detection, channel decoding, channel estimation, etc., which are commonly implemented using independently optimized algorithmic architectures. Those algorithms serving as independent hardware modules are usually integrated into a baseband processor in a divide-and-conquer manner5, while their commonality in Bayesian inference architecture has received relatively limited attention (Supplementary Note 1).

In addition, conventional baseband processors primarily rely on digital implementations6–8—such as application-specific integrated circuits (ASICs), field-programmable gate arrays (FPGAs), and software-defined radios (SDRs), which struggle to deliver the required ultra-high energy efficiency. The fundamental challenge originates from the frequent data movement9,10 and large-scale, dynamically variable digital computations11–13 required across the entire communication signal-processing chain. Specifically, conventional digital signal processing architectures, such as those based on FPGAs or SDRs implementations, exhibit limited energy efficiency, typically consuming around 10⁴ pJ per bit7,14, while ASIC implementations are slightly more efficient at ~102−103 pJ per bit15–19. Nevertheless, given the strict power constraints of communication platforms, such efficiency levels struggle to satisfy the demanding communication requirements of energy-constrained edge devices in the 6G era20. To address these challenges, it is crucial to develop ultra-low-power signal processing paradigms that should be adaptable to the diverse communication processing requirements, thereby enabling ubiquitous connectivity in the next generation of wireless networks.

As an emerging technology, memristors hold potential to overcome fundamental bottlenecks, including power consumption, computational efficiency, and scalability. The ability of memristors to electrically switch between multiple stable, non-volatile resistance states21 makes them promising candidates for next-generation computing systems, including artificial neural networks22,23, reservoir computing24, Ising solvers25, and sampling26. Owing to their highly scalable structure, memristors enable dense matrix integration via crossbar arrays27 and 3D stacking28, and align naturally with energy-efficient compute-in-memory (CIM) architectures29. This combination of attributes renders them suitable for matrix multiplication and accumulation (MAC) operations that are pivotal to AI and signal processing. Moreover, their low switching energy and negligible static power consumption contribute to improved overall energy efficiency30.

Although memristors have proven highly suitable for energy-limited edge applications, efforts to integrate them with communication signal processing remain relatively limited. Existing research on in-memory computing for wireless communications primarily focuses on individual functional modules, such as MIMO detection31–34 and constellation point identification35–37. However, these methods predominantly rely on directly mapping classical algebraic formulas, which necessitates loading dynamic channel matrix information onto the hardware. Consequently, they face significant challenges in adapting to time-varying environments due to the substantial overheads associated with repetitive memristor reprogramming and latency. Furthermore, many signal processing phases lack compatibility with vector-matrix multiplication (VMM) and predominantly operate using dynamic dataflow architectures. These factors hinder the widespread implementation of memristors in communication systems.

In this work, we propose a unified probabilistic graphical computing architecture for baseband signal processing and implement it based on memristors. It is worth mentioning that the proposed unified architecture allows the baseband signal processing applications to share the same iteration structure and the same memristor-based hardware implementations. Specifically, by configuring the values stored in the memristor array, our approach supports various communication applications, including multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) detection and low-density parity-check (LDPC) decoding. Moreover, our approach also includes in-memory computing hardware schemes incorporating memristors, sparse optimization, and dataflow level optimization to fully exploit the low-power potential of probabilistic graphical computing technology. This work unifies baseband signal processing architectures within a probabilistic graphical computing framework implemented using memristors. Through synergistic optimization of the strategies above, we achieve ultra-low energy consumption of less than 10 pJ per bit for both MIMO detection and LDPC decoding applications, while maintaining error correction capability under environmental noise. This demonstrates a potential route toward low-power deployment on edge devices.

Furthermore, dynamic deployment conflicts arise even in mathematically compatible tasks like MIMO detection, as the requirement for frequent array updates is incompatible with the high write overhead of non-volatile memristors. At the dataflow level, we propose a quantization-based static dataflow scheme that better aligns with memristor characteristics across diverse applications. We also develop a pruning strategy specifically tailored to the unified probabilistic graphical computing architecture, which enables fine-grained trade-offs between algorithmic performance and energy efficiency. Additionally, we explore practical deployment optimizations, such as algorithmic fixed-point conversion and dataflow simplification, and evaluate their applicability within the proposed architecture. Results

Memristor characteristics and device implementation

As an emerging memory technology, memristors provide key advantages such as long-term data retention, re-configurability, and low-power operation, though device-level characteristics can also influence the overall performance of hardware systems. To provide a comprehensive understanding of our study, we begin by introducing the principles, characteristics, and advantages of memristors supported by experimental statistical data from large-scale memristor arrays, as illustrated in Fig. 2.

As shown in Fig. 2a, our integration approach combines the memristor with a 2D transistor. Unlike conventional CMOS technology, which places the transistor on the bottom layer during the doping process, 2D materials offer inherent advantages for transistor fabrication (e.g., high carrier mobility, excellent gate control) and can be transferred to most lattice-matched substrates without significant loss of functionality41. This transfer compatibility allows our fabrication method to achieve greater simplicity.

Figure 2b further demonstrates the performance of resistive random-access memory (RRAM, crossbar array of memristors) across multiple critical metrics using a radar chart for intuitive visualization. These metrics include leakage power42, integration scalability42, long-term retention43, and radiation hardness44,45. In comparison with traditional memory technologies such as SRAM and DRAM, RRAM exhibits clear advantages in most aspects. Notably, RRAM achieves a favorable balance between retention time (indicating data stability) and access latency (reflecting read/write speed). A detailed comparison of RRAM with conventional memory technologies, including SRAM, DRAM, and Flash, can be found in Supplementary Fig. 6. Owing to these characteristics, RRAM is particularly suitable for low-power, high-reliability edge computing applications within communication systems.

Figure 2c compares traditional digital units with the memristor array implementation of MAC operations, which are fundamental computing blocks extensively used in AI and signal processing. Conventional digital MAC units, composed of multipliers and adders, occupy considerable area and require multiple memory accesses per operation—specifically three reads and one write. This results in high energy and latency costs46, as exemplified by models like AlexNet47, which involve 724 million MACs and require ~3 billion DRAM accesses during inference. In contrast, memristor-based crossbar arrays perform matrix-vector multiplication in a single step by applying Ohm’s and Kirchhoff’s laws, mapping weights as conductance values and computing currents in parallel. This approach achieves O(1) time complexity, drastically reducing both access and energy overhead. However, device non-idealities, such as conductance variability and on/off ratio limitations, can affect the stored weight values, thus degrading the precision of MAC operations.

Figure 2d presents the probability distributions of the low resistance state (LRS) and the high resistance state (HRS), showing that our device exhibits relatively low variability and a high switching ratio, both of which contribute to the accurate execution of MAC operations. Figure 2e illustrates the retention characteristics of our device, revealing no significant resistance change across different states after 2 × 10⁴ S at 120 °C. Based on the JEDEC JEP122H standard, this corresponds to 10 years of retention at room temperature. The experimental setup used for device testing is shown in Supplementary Fig. 7, and the readout characteristics of our devices are provided in Supplementary Fig. 8.

We benchmark the performance of our memristor devices against previously reported ones (Fig. 2f48–54), with emphasis on two key performance metrics: switching ratio and endurance. Given that edge applications typically require infrequent updates to the storage configuration, the endurance of our devices is sufficient, even when accounting for redundancy. Furthermore, the switching ratio directly influences computation accuracy and, consequently, algorithm performance. A case study illustrating the impact of memristor non-idealities on neural network inference accuracy is provided in Supplementary Fig. 9, while the complete simulation workflow is summarized in Supplementary Fig. 10.

UPGC integrates the diverse signal processing units by transforming them into a unified computational architecture. The UPGC architecture comprises three parts: (1) variable nodes {V1,V2,…,VJ} representing unknown variables {x1,x2,…,xJ}, where each xj belongs to a discrete state space M with cardinality K=∥M∥; (2) check nodes {C1,C2,…,CI} characterizing coupling relationships among unknown variables, observed variables, and the association mapping matrix; (3) connectivity between variable nodes and check nodes, which is defined by Θ. P({αli(xl)}∣l≠j) specifies the joint distribution for a vector x∈Ωkij, Ωkij denotes the set of vectors formed by the combinations of the states of variable nodes that are connected to check node i and satisfy xj=uk. The core of memristor-based UPGC lies in the iterative belief updating and propagation between variable nodes and check nodes. The operations, as defined in Eqs. (2) and (3), occur in two coupled phases per iteration: (1) Posterior Propagation (Backward Propagation): Each check node Ci computes posterior messages βij using prior messages αji from neighboring variable nodes and broadcasts the results to connected variables. (2) Prior Propagation (Forward Propagation): Each variable node {V1,V2,…,VJ} updates prior messages αji using posteriors βij from adjacent check nodes and propagates the updates to all linked check nodes. Supplementary Figs. 3-5 illustrate the temporal dataflow and parallel computation patterns.

The specific mapping strategy depends on the temporal characteristics of the association matrix Θ. For applications defined by time-invariant constraints (e.g., LDPC decoding), the values are directly mapped onto the array and remain constant during operation. However, for tasks involving time-varying channel state information (e.g., MIMO detection), direct mapping is impractical due to the conflict between rapid channel variations and the device’s write endurance. To address this, we introduce a static quantization mechanism where the interaction term involving the time-varying channel is treated as a unified Gaussian variable. Further clarification of the approximation object and its effect on message distributions is given in Supplementary Note 3~4 and Supplementary Fig. 12~13. The memristor array is configured to store the static quantization levels of this distribution, while the dynamic channel variations are handled via digital row selection logic at the input interface (see Supplementary Note 5 for details). A direct comparison between the proposed static mapping strategy and conventional dynamic remapping is further provided in Supplementary Fig. 14. This design ensures that the conductance weights remain static even under dynamic channel conditions, effectively bypassing the endurance constraint. When voltage signals proportional to VP are applied to the array rows, output currents on columns execute weighted summation according to Kirchhoff’s current law. This method eliminates power-hungry register accesses inherent in iterative digital processing and avoids complex calculations associated with digital implementations. For arbitrary-scale computations, the memristor-based UPGC framework achieves O(1) complexity per iteration via single-step analog operations—a significant reduction from traditional O(N) digital implementations. A more explicit comparison between the conventional digital dataflow and the proposed memristor-based in-memory dataflow is further provided in Supplementary Fig. 15. Additionally, programming distinct conductance values in memristors allows for the processing of a variety of applications, including but not limited to MIMO detection, channel decoding, channel estimation, multi-user identification, and others.

Sparse optimization of probabilistic graphical computing

Figure 4a shows constellation sets of transmitted symbols under typical communication scenarios with modulation orders K∈{4,8,16,64}. Figure 4b illustrates the normalized probabilities versus Euclidean distances (EDs) between the candidate vector set (composed of all possible combinations of variable node states) and the observed vector. It can be seen that the probability distribution of the candidate vectors is sparse, mainly concentrated in smaller-ED annular regions. As a result, the memristor-based UPGC circuit selectively updates only high-probability candidate vectors with smaller EDs from the observed vector and removes low-probability candidates, thereby further reducing power consumption.

In the baseline implementation without sparsity optimization, the messages propagated among check nodes and variable nodes contain all possible combinations of the variable node states. This requires activating all row voltages for RRAM array inputs and reading all column currents, as illustrated in Fig. 4c. With sparsity optimization, memristor-based UPGC prunes the messages passed among nodes to reduce the computational scale and optimizes the dataflow of message passing. From a hardware perspective, it enables dynamic activation of RRAM array row inputs and selective column reads, minimizing power consumption. The core optimization principle lies in eliminating low-contribution states in the variable node state space, where these states have a negligible impact on the probabilistic calculation due to their low probabilities. This sparsity scheme operates bidirectionally: (1) For check-to-variable node messaging, each check node transmits only the D highest-probability states from the variable nodes, reducing message volume from the full state space K to a smaller D. This reduces the message passing scale from βij={βij(1),βij(2),…,βij(K)} to βij={β~ij(1),β~ij(2),…,β~ij(D)}, where β~ij represents the messages corresponding to the D largest states in βij. (2) For the messages from variable nodes to check nodes, Z variable nodes are selected, and their values are fixed to the states with the highest probabilities. For the remaining nodes, only those messages corresponding to the W most probable states in their state set are propagated. This operation is analogous to activating only a subset of the voltage inputs in a memristor array.

The hardware system architecture for UPGC, based on sparsity optimization, is shown in Fig. 4d. First, we feed the input prior knowledge into the pruning module, which identifies the most probable candidate states and constructs a reduced set of potential states. This refined set is then passed to the computational iteration module to generate the final results. Importantly, both the pruning module and the downstream computational module can be implemented in a pipelined architecture, minimizing idle time and reducing overall system latency.

Figure 4e demonstrates the bit error rate (BER) performance of the proposed sparsity optimization scheme across various parameter configurations (D, W, Z). Through this sparsity-aware approach, near-lossless inference performance, close to the approximate maximum-likelihood benchmark, can be achieved when the sparsity ratio is below 1.5‰, with a bit error rate loss of less than 0.5 dB compared to the performance bound. More details on the impact of parameter selection on performance are provided in Supplementary Note 6. The adaptive selection of sparsity parameters under different SNR conditions and system settings is further illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 16 and discussed in Supplementary Note 7. Compared to minimum mean square error (MMSE)57 and interference cancellation (IC) detectors57, the proposed method demonstrates clear advantages in terms of both performance and complexity trade-offs. These results validate that the proposed method offers greater flexibility, supporting on-demand adjustments between performance and complexity in order to accommodate various application requirements.

As previously mentioned, RRAM devices store application-specific coefficient matrices. In the LDPC scenario, the weight values stored in memristors require no additional operations and are inherently 1-bit in nature. For the MIMO scenario, the specific statistical distribution of the weight values themselves inspired us to develop a corresponding weight compression method, which reduces the storage and computation requirements while incurring minimal performance loss. Benefiting from the aforementioned properties, we can use a single binarized memristor to store each weight value. This not only simplifies the computation process but also leverages the greater technological maturity and robustness of binarized memristors. By combining the dataflow staticization technique with the fixed-point optimization discussed earlier, we successfully map the MIMO and LDPC coefficient matrices onto a 128 × 128 RRAM crossbar. Discussion

In summary, we have presented a unified probabilistic graphical computing driven baseband signal processing architecture that unifies Bayesian inference-based signal processing algorithms within a single reconfigurable and energy-efficient hardware framework based on memristors. By leveraging the intrinsic advantages of memristor-based in-memory computing, our approach addresses the long-standing bottlenecks of data movement and dynamic operation overhead in conventional digital architectures, achieving sub–10 pJ per bit energy consumption and significantly enhanced noise robustness. The proposed architecture has been validated by large-scale fabricated memristor arrays, confirming its feasibility under realistic device non-idealities and its applicability to diverse signal processing applications, including MIMO detection and LDPC decoding. These results highlight the potential of integrating probabilistic graphical computing with emerging memory technologies to meet the requirements of edge devices, such as ultra-low power, flexibility, and reliability. Looking ahead, the unified and reconfigurable nature of our work paves the way for the broader adoption of memristor-based accelerators in heterogeneous edge computing platforms, extending their impact beyond communications to other data-intensive, inference-driven applications.

Methods

Device fabrication

The designed 1T1R structure was fabricated as follows: First, lithography and electron-beam evaporation Ti/Pd with 2 nm/35 nm thickness was selected as the back gate metal. After that, the 10 nm-thick HfO2 layer acted as the gate dielectric; this layer was deposited via ALD at 200 °C using the PICOSUN R200 Advanced system with TDMA-Hf and H2O. Subsequently, AZ601 photoresist was spin-coated, and through-hole etching of the oxide was performed in the ULVAC NE550H system with Cl2 to expose the underlying metal. Then, MOCVD-grown MoS2 on sapphire was wet-transferred: PMMA was spin-coated at 1500 rpm, baked at 180 °C for 2 min, the stack was released in deionized water, transferred to the target substrate, dried, and the PMMA was removed in acetone. MoS2 was patterned by O2 plasma etching (150 W, 150 sccm, 8 min). Contact metal Ti/Pd (5 nm/35 nm) was deposited by electron-beam evaporation. Next, a 50 nm SiO₂ interlayer was grown by ICP-PECVD at 130 °C; via holes were etched with F₂ in the NE550H. A 5 nm HfO2 resistive switching layer was then deposited by ALD at 200 °C. Finally, the 40 nm TiN top electrode was deposited by magnetron sputtering.
