Responses and driving mechanisms of leaf functional traits of Betula platyphylla along an elevational gradient in the Niyang River Basin, southeastern Tibetan Plateau
Authors: Ngawang Norbu, Rong-fang Wang, Hui Zhang, Dorgon Dolma, Ze-Long Chi, Zhe-fei Zeng, Ngawang Bonjor, Norzin Tso, Jun-wei Wang, La Qiong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Variation in plant functional traits reflects environmental adaptation. Understanding how traits shift along elevational gradients can clarify mechanisms by which species respond to environmental change. Here, we used white birch (Betula platyphylla) in the Niyang River Basin (left bank of the Yarlung Tsangpo River, Xizang) as a model species to quantify leaf trait variation along an elevational gradient and to identify key environmental drivers. Leaf functional traits were analyzed using one-way ANOVA, redundancy analysis (RDA), Pearson correlation, and principal component analysis (PCA). Coefficients of variation ranged from 5.52% to 26.14%, with leaf area showing the greatest variation and leaf water content the lowest. Most traits differed significantly among elevations: leaf area (LA), leaf length (LL), leaf width (LW), petiole length (PL), petiole base width (PBW) and leaf phosphorus content (LPC) declined with increasing elevation, whereas leaf nitrogen content (LNC) and leaf N: P ratio increased. Total chlorophyll content (TChl) and leaf water content (LWC) exhibited a hump-shaped pattern along the elevational gradient. In contrast, leaf dry matter content (LDMC) exhibited a unimodal pattern along the elevational gradient, whereas specific leaf area (SLA) and leaf mass per area (LMA) showed no significant trends with elevation. RDA indicated that soil factors were the primary drivers of leaf trait variation, explaining 50.90% of the total variance; soil total nitrogen (TN), pH, and available potassium (AK) were the most influential variables. Trait patterns suggest contrasting resource acquisition strategies across elevations: high-elevation individuals enhanced photosynthetic efficiency and shifted toward a more acquisitive “fast investment–return” strategy to cope with harsher conditions. Our findings contribute to understanding the adaptive responses of montane tree species to climate warming and improve our understanding of plant trait variation along environmental gradients.

Introduction

How plants adjust their functional traits along environmental gradients to cope with external stress is one of the central questions in ecology [1]. Plant functional traits reflect the integrated responses of plants’ strategies for resource acquisition, use and conservation, and thus can effectively reveal the mechanisms underlying plant adaptation across contrasting environments [2, 3]. Among these traits, leaves are the key organs for carbon gain and water regulation; their morphological and chemical characteristics are particularly sensitive to environmental variation [4, 5] and are widely used to assess plant growth strategies and patterns of resource allocation [6].

Elevational gradients typically integrate concurrent shifts in temperature, radiation, water availability and soil nutrients, and are therefore regarded as natural laboratories for studying plant adaptation and ecosystem functioning [7–9]. In high-elevation environments, shortened growing seasons and resource limitation often force plants to trade off between rapid growth and investment in survival [10]. Consequently, key functional traits such as leaf dry matter content (LDMC), specific leaf area (SLA), leaf nitrogen (N) and phosphorus (P) concentrations, and their stoichiometric ratios are widely used as indicators of plant resource-use strategies, photosynthetic capacity and adaptive potential [11, 12]. In recent years, a growing body of work has shown that intraspecific trait variation plays a crucial role in environmental adaptation [13], accounting for up to one quarter of total trait variation at the global scale [14]. Multiple tree species have exhibited pronounced intraspecific leaf trait responses along elevational gradients—for example, Koenigia mollis in the Himalayas and Daphniphyllum macropodum Miq. in southern China, both of which show systematic shifts in leaf morphological and chemical traits with elevation [8, 15]. However, despite these advances highlighting the importance of intraspecific plasticity, adaptive patterns among different tree species in alpine and subalpine ecosystems remain highly heterogeneous, and the underlying mechanisms are still not fully resolved.

White birch (Betula platyphylla Suk., hereafter B. platyphylla) is one of the most widespread temperate broad-leaved tree species in East Asia [16], with a broad ecological niche. On the Tibetan Plateau, within the alpine vegetation zone, B. platyphylla typically occurs at elevations of approximately 2500–4000 m [17]. Its wide elevational range provides a unique opportunity to examine intraspecific trait plasticity and adaptive strategies of a single tree species in montane environments. However, studies on how leaf functional traits of B. platyphylla respond to the complex environmental gradients of the Tibetan Plateau remain scarce.

In this study, we focused on populations of B. platyphylla in the Niyang River Basin on the left bank of the Yarlung Tsangpo River in southeastern Xizang, on the Tibetan Plateau. We systematically examined how leaf functional traits vary along an elevational gradient and how these patterns are related to environmental factors, in order to elucidate the physiological and ecological mechanisms underlying the adaptation of this species to montane environments. Specifically, we addressed the following questions: (a) how do leaf functional traits of B. platyphylla change along the elevational gradient? (b) do morphological, nutrient and stoichiometric leaf traits exhibit coordinated patterns of variation? and (c) how do environmental factors drive the differentiated responses of leaf functional traits in B. platyphylla?

This study will help to deepen our understanding of adaptive evolution in woody plants in montane ecosystems and provide a basis for predicting the functional dynamics of high-elevation forests under ongoing climate change.

Materials and methods

Study site

The study area is located in the Niyang River Basin on the left bank of the middle–lower reaches of the Yarlung Tsangpo River in southeastern Xizang, on the Tibetan Plateau (E92°10′–94°35′, N29°30′–30°30′). Elevation ranges from 2935 m to 5030 m, forming a pronounced alpine valley landform with terrain gradually descending from west to east. The region is influenced jointly by warm–humid air masses from the Indian Ocean and cold air masses from northern Asia, resulting in a typical temperate plateau monsoon climate, with a mean annual temperature of about 8.5 °C, mean annual precipitation of 842 mm, and potential evaporation up to 1766 mm [18]. Soil types along the river from upstream to downstream show clear differentiation with elevation and vegetation zones. In the high-elevation upstream area, alpine desert soil, alpine meadow soil and alpine shrub–meadow soil are dominant, whereas in the midstream and downstream areas at relatively lower elevations, subalpine meadow soil, subalpine shrub–meadow soil and brown soil prevail [19].

Field survey

In this study, B. platyphylla was selected as the focal species. Field surveys were conducted in July–August 2024 within the elevational range of 2990–4100 m corresponding to the distribution of B. platyphylla in the Niyang River Basin, southeastern Tibetan Plateau (sampling locations are detailed in Fig. 1). Based on the actual elevational distribution of B. platyphylla in the basin, five elevational bands were defined from low to high: I (2990–3200 m), II (3200–3400 m), III (3400–3600 m), IV (3600–3800 m) and V (3800–4100 m). Within each elevational band, 27–32 healthy adult B. platyphylla trees with similar diameter at breast height (DBH) and height, and with inter-individual spacing greater than 50 m, were selected. From each individual, 20 mature leaves were randomly collected from the canopy in the four cardinal directions (east, south, west and north), labeled, and placed in paper bags for subsequent measurement of leaf functional traits. Professor Junwei Wang identified the plant, and the voucher specimen (voucher # BH 13761) has been deposited in the herbarium of the Plant Laboratory at Xizang University. At the same time, topsoil (0–20 cm) was sampled using a five-point sampling method and pooled to form a composite soil sample for each site. Collected plants were growing in the wild and did not involve any protected species, no collection permits were required.

Index measurements

Determination of leaf functional traits

For each B. platyphylla individual, 20 intact, insect-free leaves of similar size were selected for measurements of leaf functional traits and ecological stoichiometric indices [20, 21]. The measured traits included leaf area (LA), leaf length (LL), leaf width (LW), petiole length (PL), petiole base width (PBW), specific leaf area (SLA), leaf dry matter content (LDMC), leaf mass per area (LMA), leaf water content (LWC) and total chlorophyll content (TChl).

Leaf fresh weight (LFW) was measured in the field using an electronic balance (precision 0.0001 g). In the laboratory, LA, LL and LW were determined with a YMJ-D leaf area meter, whereas PL and PBW were measured with a vernier caliper. Leaves were then dried in an oven at 75 °C for 48 h to constant mass and weighed to obtain leaf dry weight (LDW). Chlorophyll a (Chl a) and chlorophyll b (Chl b) contents were determined using an acetone–ethanol extraction followed by colorimetric analysis. Leaf total nitrogen content (LNC) was measured by the Kjeldahl method, and leaf total phosphorus content (LPC) was determined using the molybdenum–antimony colorimetric method. Measurement of soil physico-chemical properties

The methods for determining soil physico-chemical properties were as follows [22]: soil pH (pH) was measured using a glass-electrode pH meter; soil total nitrogen (TN) was determined by the Kjeldahl method; total phosphorus (TP) and available phosphorus (AP) were measured using the molybdenum–antimony colorimetric method; total potassium (TK) and available potassium (AK) were determined with a flame photometer; soil organic matter (OM) was measured using the external-heating potassium dichromate method; alkali-hydrolyzable nitrogen (AN) was determined by the alkali-hydrolysis diffusion method; and soil dry matter content (SDMC) was determined gravimetrically.

Data analysis

Data were first organized in Microsoft Excel 2022, and all statistical analyses were performed in R 4.3.2. Specifically, one-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test was used to assess differences in leaf functional traits and leaf element concentrations among elevations. Pearson correlation analysis was applied to examine pairwise relationships among leaf functional traits and among key soil variables. Standardized major axis (SMA) regression was performed using the ‘smatr’ package (version 3.4.8) in R to examine the scaling relationships among leaf functional traits along the elevational gradient. In addition, redundancy analysis (RDA) was performed in Canoco 5.0 to quantify the extent to which soil variables explain variation in leaf functional traits, and principal component analysis (PCA) was used to characterize patterns of leaf functional traits across elevational bands. All figures and tables were produced using R 4.3.2 and Origin 2021.

Variation in leaf functional traits of B. platyphylla along the elevational gradient

Differences in leaf functional traits of B. platyphylla among elevational bands were assessed using one-way ANOVA (Fig. 2). The results showed that, except for SLA and LMA, all other traits—LA, LL, LW, PL, PBW, LDMC and LWC—varied significantly along the elevational gradient (P < 0.05).

Specifically, LA, LL, LW, PL and PBW all decreased with increasing elevation (Fig. 2a–c, h, i; P < 0.05). Values of these traits at elevation I were significantly higher than at elevations III and V, while there were no significant differences between elevations I and II, nor among elevations III, IV and V. LDMC first increased and then decreased with elevation, reaching its highest value at elevation III, where it was significantly higher than at elevations IV and V (Fig. 2e; P < 0.05). LWC showed the opposite pattern, first decreasing and then increasing, with significantly higher values at elevation V than at elevations I, II and III (Fig. 2g; P < 0.05). SLA and LMA showed no significant differences along the elevation gradient (Fig. 2d, f; P > 0.05).

Variation in leaf N, P and their stoichiometric ratios of B. platyphylla across elevations

Figure 3 shows the variation in LNC and LPC of B. platyphylla along the elevational gradient. LNC ranged from 14.36 to 24.10 g kg⁻¹, LPC from 1.10 to 2.94 g kg⁻¹, and N: P from 6.04 to 15.62. LNC increased with elevation, with values at elevation V being significantly higher than at the other elevations (Fig. 3a; P < 0.05), whereas there were no significant differences among elevations I, II, III and IV (P > 0.05). By contrast, LPC decreased progressively with increasing elevation and differed significantly among the five elevational bands, LPC at elevation I was significantly higher than at elevations IV and V (Fig. 3b; P < 0.01). Leaf N: P increased with elevation (Fig. 3c; P < 0.05), with values at elevations I, II and III being significantly lower than at elevations IV and V.

Leaf total chlorophyll (TChl) content ranged from 1.25 to 3.32 mg g⁻¹ and exhibited a significant hump-shaped pattern along the elevational gradient, decreasing initially and then increasing (Fig. 3d; P < 0.05). TChl at elevation V was significantly higher than that at elevations II, III, and IV (P < 0.05), whereas no significant difference was detected between elevations I and V. Overall, in the Niyang River Basin, LNC and N: P increased with elevation, while LPC decreased, and TChl first declined and then increased along the elevational gradient.

Pearson correlation analysis among leaf functional traits of B. platyphylla

Pearson correlation analysis among leaf functional traits (Fig. 4) showed that LA was positively correlated with its size dimensions (LL and LW), with petiole traits (PL and PBW), and with LPC (P < 0.01), whereas LA was negatively correlated with N: P (P < 0.01).

SLA was positively correlated with LWC, LNC and TChl (P < 0.05), but negatively correlated with LDMC and LMA (P < 0.01). In contrast, LDMC and LMA were strongly positively correlated with each other (P < 0.01), and both were negatively correlated with LWC, LNC, N:P and TChl (P < 0.01). LNC was positively correlated with N: P and TChl (P < 0.01), whereas LPC was negatively correlated with N: P (P < 0.01).

RDA of leaf functional traits of B. platyphylla and soil factors

To assess the influence of soil environmental factors on leaf functional traits of B. platyphylla, RDA was performed. Soil variables explained 50.90% of the total variation in leaf functional traits, with the first two axes accounting for 29.69% and 14.18% of the variance, respectively (Fig. 6). This indicates that the first two axes captured most of the covariation between leaf traits and soil factors, with the pattern being largely dominated by the first axis.

Variation in leaf economics trade-offs of B. platyphylla along the elevational gradient

PCA of leaf functional traits of B. platyphylla revealed clear differentiation among elevations. PC1 accounted for 42.12% of the total variance and PC2 for 29.74%, together explaining 71.86% of the variation in leaf traits (Fig. 8). Traits closely associated with PC1 included LA, PL, PBW, LPC and N: P, mainly reflecting variation in nutrient use and growth strategy. Traits associated with PC2 included LDMC, LWC, TChl and LNC, which primarily characterize photosynthetic capacity, water regulation and leaf construction costs, and thus capture the internal trade-offs between resource acquisition and construction investment.

In the PCA ordination, mid- to low-elevation populations (I–III) of B. platyphylla were mainly distributed on the positive side of PC1, whereas high-elevation populations (IV–V) clustered primarily on the negative side of PC1. Specifically, mid- to low-elevation trees were associated with higher LPC and larger LA, indicating that under relatively resource-rich conditions B. platyphylla can effectively utilize P to support growth. In contrast, high-elevation trees were closely associated with higher LNC, higher TChl and higher N: P, suggesting that in harsh montane environments B. platyphylla increases investment in N and photosynthetic pigments to enhance photosynthetic efficiency and cope with stressful climatic conditions.

Discussion

Response patterns of leaf functional traits of B. platyphylla along the elevational gradient

Elevation, as a key environmental factor, strongly shapes the spatial patterns of water–energy availability and soil nutrients, and plant adaptive strategies are commonly expressed through functional traits and their combinations [23]. Along elevational gradients, plants therefore tend to exhibit marked differences in functional traits, forming characteristic vertical patterns in trait distributions [24, 25]. In this study, coefficients of variation for leaf functional traits of B. platyphylla in the Niyang River Basin ranged from 5.52% to 26.14%, and most traits showed significant shifts among elevations. LA, LL, LW, PL and PBW all decreased significantly with increasing elevation (P < 0.05), indicating distinct adaptive differentiation of B. platyphylla across the elevational gradient. This pattern is consistent with previous findings reported for Pleioblastus amarus and Abies georgei var. smithii [26, 27], and suggests that with increasing elevation B. platyphylla reduces leaf size to limit water loss and thereby enhance stress resistance.

Leaf dry matter content (LDMC) is an important indicator of plant adaptive strategies, reflecting structural carbon investment and closely linked to plant growth strategies [28]. Lower LDMC generally indicates reduced structural investment, and is often associated with lower stress tolerance and shorter life cycles [29, 30]. In this study, LDMC first increased and then decreased along the elevational gradient (P < 0.05), this suggests that with increasing elevation, B. platyphylla tends to adopt a more acquisitive strategy, reducing structural carbon investment to facilitate rapid growth and thereby cope with the shortened growing season.

Nitrogen and phosphorus are essential nutrients in leaves, involved in the formation of proteins and genetic material, and play key roles in promoting plant growth and development. In this study, LNC increased with elevation (P < 0.05), a pattern consistent with the temperature–plant physiology hypothesis (TPPH), which posits that as elevation increases and temperature decreases, plants tend to increase LNC to compensate for the reduced efficiency of leaf physiological processes under low temperatures [31]. This trend also agrees with the global meta-analysis by Midolo et al. [32], which reported increasing leaf N with elevation. Because N content in plant tissues is closely related to soil N availability [33], the positive correlation observed here between LNC and TN with increasing elevation (P < 0.05) indicates that higher TN at high elevations provides B. platyphylla with a more abundant N supply, thereby promoting N accumulation in leaves. In contrast to N, LPC showed an opposite response to elevation (Fig. 3), which is inconsistent with the common view that N and P, sharing similar uptake pathways, often exhibit coordinated responses to environmental gradients [34].

Leaf N: P stoichiometry reflects the nutritional status and growth condition of plants [35]. In this study, N:P of B. platyphylla increased with elevation (P < 0.05), consistent with the findings of Müller et al. [36], mainly due to increasing LNC and decreasing LPC. Leaf N: P is often used to infer the type of nutrient limitation; it is generally assumed that when N: P < 14, plant growth is likely N-limited [37]. Here, N:P of B. platyphylla in the Niyang River Basin was consistently below 14. Although most boreal and cold-region plants are typically considered N-limited, some studies have suggested that variation in leaf N: P is primarily driven by changes in P rather than N, with N being relatively stable [38]. Therefore, whether B. platyphylla in the Niyang River Basin is truly N-limited still requires further verification through N-addition experiments.

Chlorophyll is the primary photosynthetic pigment in plants, and its content directly affects photosynthetic efficiency [39]. In this study, TChl of B. platyphylla showed a hump-shaped pattern along the elevational gradient, first decreasing and then increasing. At low elevations, relatively high temperatures and ample radiation facilitate chlorophyll synthesis and promote photosynthesis, whereas low temperatures at high elevations can suppress enzyme activity and constrain chlorophyll production. However, the increase in TChl observed at the highest elevations suggests that B. platyphylla may alleviate low-temperature constraints through physiological acclimation mechanisms. Higher TChl enhances light capture and utilization efficiency, thereby increasing photosynthetic rates and carbon assimilation, and reflects the adoption of a more acquisitive strategy to improve the performance of B. platyphylla in high-elevation environments.

Correlations among leaf functional traits of B. platyphylla

Functional traits in leaves often act in concert, forming specific trait combinations that enable plants to cope with different environments [14, 40, 41]. Our results indicate that B. platyphylla responds to environmental heterogeneity along the elevational gradient by adjusting the coupling among key traits such as LDMC, SLA, LNC and TChl.

Both Pearson correlations (Fig. 4) and SMA results (Fig. 5) showed that LDMC and SLA were significantly negatively correlated at all elevations, similar results have been reported for dominant woody species in Mao’er Mountain, China [42]. This covariation indicates that within the leaf economics spectrum of B. platyphylla, higher LDMC is typically associated with lower SLA, reflecting a conservative strategy characterized by greater investment in structural support and defence, whereas lower LDMC combined with higher SLA corresponds to a more acquisitive strategy with rapid resource capture and faster tissue turnover. Together with the observed elevational patterns, B. platyphylla at low elevations tends to exhibit higher LDMC and lower SLA, which enhances mechanical support and stress resistance, whereas at high elevations LDMC decreases and SLA increases, indicating a shift toward a more acquisitive leaf trait syndrome that relies on higher photosynthetic returns per unit biomass to offset the constraints of a shorter growing season and low temperatures on carbon gain.

Moreover, TChl and LNC were significantly positively correlated, supporting the close linkage between photosynthetic capacity and pigment investment [43]. Because most leaf N is allocated to chloroplasts, higher LNC enhances the activity of photosynthetic enzymes and CO₂ assimilation capacity, thereby improving photosynthetic efficiency [43]. With increasing elevation, radiation intensifies while temperature decreases; by increasing LNC and TChl, B. platyphylla enhances light capture and utilization per unit time and maximizes carbon assimilation within the limited growing season [44].

Relationships between environmental factors and leaf functional traits of B. platyphylla

Environmental factors are major drivers of plant functional traits [45, 46], and environmental variables can effectively capture patterns of trait variation [47]. According to the plant economics spectrum proposed by Reich, traits of different organs covary along environmental resource gradients [48]; that is, when environmental conditions change, plants adjust their traits to optimize resource acquisition and use. In our study, RDA showed that soil factors together explained 50.90% of the variation in leaf traits of B. platyphylla. Among these, TN had the largest contribution (47.60%) and was significantly correlated with multiple leaf traits (P < 0.05), followed by pH and AK, whereas other soil variables had relatively minor effects on leaf trait variation. N and P are essential nutrients for plant growth and development; they participate in and regulate metabolic and growth processes, and their accumulation in leaves is largely constrained by soil nutrient supply [49]. Numerous studies have identified soil N as a key driver of variation in plant functional traits and functional diversity [50, 51], because the enrichment of soil N strongly influences the allocation and use of N within leaves.

In this study, soil TN and pH showed strong covariation and were both positively correlated with N: P, TChl and LNC, but negatively correlated with LDMC and LPC. These patterns are broadly consistent with previous findings [52, 53]. N is a key component of chlorophyll and photosynthesis-related enzymes, and insufficient N supply often leads to reduced chlorophyll content and constrained photosynthesis [43]. Along the elevational gradient, increases in soil TN and pH promoted N uptake and allocation in B. platyphylla, thereby enhancing LNC and TChl and improving photosynthetic capacity per unit leaf area. TChl exhibited a hump-shaped response to elevation; at high-elevation sites, relatively high TN and pH likely help to alleviate the physiological stress imposed by low temperatures and strong radiation by increasing leaf N and chlorophyll investment, which in turn enhances light capture and carbon assimilation and improves the ability of B. platyphylla to cope with high-elevation environments.

Although this study provides insights into the variation of leaf functional traits of B. platyphylla along a coastal environmental gradient, several limitations should be noted. Climatic variables (e.g., air and soil temperature, precipitation) were not included due to the limited spatial scale and lack of long-term site-specific data. Since temperature influences plant physiological processes and trait expression, incorporating microclimatic measurements would enhance understanding of environmental controls on leaf traits.

Future studies integrating fine-scale climatic monitoring with coastal soil variables would provide a more comprehensive assessment of the interactive effects of abiotic factors on trait variation in B. platyphylla.

Conclusion

Taken together, leaf traits of B. platyphylla in the Niyang River Basin of southeastern Xizang exhibited overall moderate environmental plasticity, with LWC showing the lowest variation (5.52%) and LA the highest (26.14%). Most leaf functional traits varied significantly among elevational bands, and B. platyphylla responded to elevational environmental differences through coordinated shifts and reconfiguration of leaf trait combinations. Along the elevational gradient, the leaf economics spectrum of B. platyphylla tended to shift from a more conservative strategy toward a more acquisitive strategy, characterized by reduced structural carbon investment and increased LNC and TChl, thereby enhancing photosynthetic and nutrient acquisition capacity per unit time and improving performance under cold, short growing-season conditions at high elevations. Among environmental variables, TN, pH and AK had the strongest effects on leaf traits, indicating that these soil factors are key drivers of elevational variation in leaf functional traits of B. platyphylla. Examination of leaf functional traits and their relationships with environmental factors enhances our understanding of plant adaptive responses in montane ecosystems and may provide useful insights for predicting the functional dynamics of high-elevation forests under ongoing climate change.
