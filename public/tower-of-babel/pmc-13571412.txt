Transhepatic venous access as a vessel-preserving salvage strategy for temporary hemodialysis in venous access exhaustion
Authors: Chun Kit Richard Chan, Chung Wai Wong, Hong Ching Lee, Ping Yung Chu, San Ming Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Patients with end-stage renal failure may develop progressively limited venous access for hemodialysis, particularly after repeated catheterization, thrombosis, and central venous stenosis. This may be especially problematic when temporary dialysis access is required while a permanent arteriovenous fistula is still maturing. We report the use of transhepatic tunneled double-lumen hemodialysis catheter placement as a salvage, vessel-preserving strategy in a 51-year-old woman with complex venous occlusive disease, an immature right upper extremity arteriovenous fistula, and a recently reconstructed right subclavian vein. The transhepatic route was selected to maintain dialysis while preserving the reconstructed right-sided venous outflow and protecting fistula maturation, in keeping with the Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative (KDOQI) principle of vessel preservation. The catheter functioned for 7 weeks before dysfunction developed because of inferior migration of the tip to a subdiaphragmatic position. Catheter revision with superior repositioning and vertical configuration restored satisfactory flow and allowed continued hemodialysis until the fistula became usable. The catheter was removed 4 weeks later following fistula maturation, with recurrent inferior tip migration again demonstrated at removal. This case highlights the importance of individualized vascular access planning and supports the role of transhepatic venous access as a temporary option in selected patients with exhausted conventional venous access. It also demonstrates recurrent catheter-tip migration despite revision as an important procedural limitation requiring close surveillance.

Introduction

Patients with end-stage renal failure frequently face difficulty establishing reliable vascular access for hemodialysis, particularly while awaiting maturation of permanent access. Repeated catheterization may lead to thrombosis and central venous stenosis, further limiting available access options. Contemporary vascular access planning, guided by the 2019 Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative (KDOQI) Vascular Access Guideline, emphasizes vessel preservation to protect future access sites [1]. When conventional venous routes are no longer feasible, or when they should be avoided to preserve remaining options, unconventional salvage approaches may be required.

The transhepatic route provides access to the inferior vena cava via the hepatic veins and may be considered in selected patients with severely limited central venous access. First described by Po et al. in 1994 [2], subsequent retrospective series have also demonstrated its technical feasibility and ability to provide functional dialysis access in patients with exhausted conventional venous routes [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. However, this unconventional route is not without limitations. Catheter-related complications, including thrombosis, infection, and migration, may occur and require repeat intervention [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. This report highlights the use of the transhepatic route as a vessel-preserving strategy in a patient with complex venous occlusive disease and a maturing upper extremity arteriovenous fistula, complicated by recurrent inferior catheter-tip migration despite revision.

Case report

A 51-year-old woman with end-stage renal failure due to IgA nephropathy on maintenance hemodialysis since 2017 was admitted to the intensive care unit with septicemia. She had markedly limited vascular access related to recurrent thrombosis and central venous stenosis, likely due to repeated catheterization for prior hemodialysis catheter insertion as the patient refused arteriovenous fistula previously. The bilateral internal jugular veins, subclavian veins, and right femoral vein were chronically occluded, leaving the left femoral vein as the only remaining conventional route for dialysis access (Fig. 1). One month earlier, a right upper extremity arteriovenous fistula had been created, and the right subclavian vein had undergone endovascular bypass to preserve long-term access. During the present admission, methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus was isolated from blood and catheter tip cultures, and the left femoral dialysis catheter subsequently thrombosed. The fistula remained immature, and the reconstructed right subclavian venous outflow was intentionally preserved. After multidisciplinary discussion, transhepatic venous access was selected as a temporary salvage route for hemodialysis to preserve the recently reconstructed right subclavian vein and avoid compromising maturation of the newly created right upper extremity arteriovenous fistula. No additional antibiotic prophylaxis was given pre-procedurally as the patient was receiving targeted antimicrobial therapy. Preliminary ultrasonography confirmed patency of the hepatic veins and inferior vena cava. Under local anesthesia, ultrasound guided puncture was performed with a 21G trocar needle, targeting a peripheral branch of the middle hepatic vein, with successful access obtained on the first pass (Fig. 2a). Contrast injection confirmed intravascular positioning (Fig. 2b). After the Skater set 0.018-inch guidewire advancement through the middle hepatic vein into the inferior vena cava and to the cavoatrial junction, a 6F Skater coaxial introducer sheath was inserted (Fig. 2c–d). The guidewire was then exchanged for a 0.035-inch Terumo guidewire, navigating into the right atrium. The planned tunnel exit site was marked, and local anesthetic was infiltrated along the planned subcutaneous tunnel and exit site. An exit-site incision was made, and the catheter was tunneled to the abdominal insertion site. After serial tract dilatation, a 14-F, 28-cm tunneled double-lumen PERM Split Stream hemodialysis catheter was placed with the tip positioned in the right atrium at the T10 vertebral level (Fig. 2e). Placement was technically successful and permitted immediate high-flow dialysis. Seven weeks after catheter placement, increased resistance developed in both lumens during dialysis. Fluoroscopy demonstrated inferior migration of the catheter tip to a subdiaphragmatic position at the T12 level, with buckling seen over intravenous part of the catheter (Fig. 3a). The right upper extremity arteriovenous fistula remained immature, so catheter revision was performed to maintain dialysis access. Using a guidewire exchange technique, the catheter course was realigned, and the tip was repositioned more cranially to the inferior part of T9 level, with a more vertical configuration, in an attempt to improve catheter stability (Fig. 3c). Following the initial catheter exchange, persistent low-volume blood oozing from the exit site was documented. The patient reported daily soaking of the dressing, requiring daily dressing changes. No decrease in hemoglobin level or hemodynamic instability was observed. The catheter was electively removed 4 weeks after revision following maturation of the right upper extremity arteriovenous fistula. At the time of removal, fluoroscopy demonstrated recurrent inferior migration of the catheter tip to a subdiaphragmatic position, with buckling seen over subcutaneous-hepatic segment of the catheter (Fig. 4a). Discussion

Maintaining hemodialysis access is particularly challenging in patients with advanced central venous disease and a history of repeated catheterization. Current Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative (KDOQI) clinical practice guidelines emphasize vessel preservation in the management of hemodialysis access, with preference for maintenance of a functional arteriovenous fistula whenever feasible [1]. This is especially relevant during fistula maturation, when temporary central venous access may still be required. The guidelines note that prior ipsilateral central venous catheter placement has been associated with inferior fistula outcomes, supporting attempts to avoid catheterization on the side of a newly created access [1]. Retrospective studies have similarly shown reduced cumulative access survival in patients with prior ipsilateral hemodialysis catheters [9]. In the present patient, conventional thoracic and femoral venous access sites have been exhausted. The recent right subclavian endovascular bypassand a newly created right upper extremity arteriovenous fistula further increased the importance of preserving right-sided venous outflow as part of a vessel-preservation strategy.

Transhepatic venous access has been described as an alternative route for hemodialysis catheter placement in patients with no remaining conventional venous access. Reported experience remains limited and is based mainly on small retrospective series [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. Po et al. first described the technique as a safe and feasible option in patients with exhausted access [2]. Subsequent reports, including the series by Smith et al., Younes et al., Stavropoulos et al., Al-Ghamdi et al. and Khallaf et al. [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]], have further supported its technical feasibility and ability to provide functional dialysis access in patients with exhausted conventional venous routes, while also highlighting the need for repeat intervention and ongoing catheter-related maintenance. Catheter-related complications, including thrombosis, infection, and migration, may require catheter exchange or repositioning [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. As a result, transhepatic access is generally reserved for carefully selected patients in whom traditional access routes are no longer available and is best regarded as a salvage or last-resort option rather than a routine alternative.

In our patient, transhepatic catheter placement provided temporary dialysis access while preserving the recently reconstructed right subclavian venous pathway and the maturing ipsilateral arteriovenous fistula. Catheter dysfunction later developed because of inferior migration, which is a recognized complication of transhepatic access. Although migration has been reported in previous series [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]], detailed serial fluoroscopic depiction of its direction and recurrence after revision remains limited. Khallaf et al. reported a lower migration rate using a more peripheral hepatic venous access route, which may provide a longer intrahepatic course. In the present case, although a peripheral branch of the middle hepatic vein was used in our patient, recurrent migration still occurred, indicating that peripheral access alone may not eliminate this complication.

Fluoroscopy in our case demonstrated initial inferior displacement of the catheter tip to a subdiaphragmatic position, followed by recurrent inferior migration despite repositioning. For tunneled cuffed hemodialysis catheters, KDOQI guidance recommends placement of the catheter tip in the mid-right atrium with its arterial lumen facing the mediastinum for tunneled cuffed hemodialysis catheter tips [1]. In this case, the revised tip was repositioned at the T9 level and appeared to project over the lower right atrial region rather than at the estimated mid-right atrial level. Although this restored satisfactory dialysis flow, recurrent inferior migration occurred. The relatively limited intra-atrial catheter length may therefore have reduced the margin available to accommodate subsequent inferior displacement.

Several factors may have contributed to catheter displacement, including respiratory hepatic motion, the short distance between the hepatic vein, inferior vena cava, and right atrium, and possible tract instability after catheter exchange [5,8]. Persistent low-volume exit-site bleeding requiring daily dressing changes after catheter revision may also have increased external catheter handling and inadvertent traction. The external displacement seen on removal fluoroscopy may be consistent with a contribution from catheter traction. These mechanisms remain speculative but may explain the recurrent migration observed despite technically successful revision.

Guidewire-assisted revision temporarily restored dialysis function, but recurrent inferior migration was observed at catheter removal after fistula maturation. This recurrence suggests that repositioning alone may not fully overcome the mechanical instability of transhepatic access and supports close clinical and fluoroscopic surveillance after revision.

Conclusion

This case illustrates that transhepatic venous access can provide an effective temporary route for hemodialysis in selected patients with exhausted conventional venous access. In our case, this approach allowed continuation of dialysis while preserving the recently reconstructed right subclavian venous outflow and protecting maturation of the right arm arteriovenous fistula. This strategy is consistent with the 2019 KDOQI principle of vessel preservation, which emphasizes safeguarding future access options when temporary dialysis access is required [1]. The case also provides serial fluoroscopic documentation of inferior catheter-tip migration, functional recovery after guidewire-assisted revision, and recurrent migration despite repositioning. This imaging course highlights the mechanical vulnerability of transhepatic dialysis access and indicates that catheter migration may recur even after technically successful revision. Despite recurrent migration, the catheter provided sufficient interim dialysis support until the arteriovenous fistula became functional. Transhepatic access should therefore be considered within an individualized vascular-access plan, with close clinical and fluoroscopic follow-up until durable access becomes available.
