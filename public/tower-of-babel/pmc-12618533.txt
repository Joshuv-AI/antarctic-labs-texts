Investigation of the impact of short-term tillage practices on soil properties at various depths in light soils of the highlands of Central Ethiopia
Authors: Darebe Yohannes, Kishor Purushottam Kolhe, Mihret Dananto, Markos Mathewos
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Soil degradation caused by repeated shallow tillage had remained a major challenge in Ethiopian highland farming systems. Traditional tillage often resulted in shallow disturbance, compaction, and poor residue incorporation, which reduced soil fertility and crop productivity. This study investigates the comparative effects of traditional and modified plow practices on soil properties in Chefa Kebele, Central Ethiopia, focusing on physical, mechanical, and chemical characteristics at varying soil depths (0–5, 5–10, 10–15, 15–20, and 20–25 cm), wheat-based light soils. Traditional tillage, employing the oxen-drawn ‘Maresha’ plow, was evaluated against a modified plow designed to enhance soil structure and fertility. A randomized complete block design with three replications was used, and data were analyzed using ANOVA with significance (p < 0.05) and a paired t-test. The analysis of soil physical properties with depth revealed significant variations, with the surface layer (0–25 cm) exhibiting the highest values for liquid limit, plastic limit, plastic index, bulk density, and dry density. In contrast, moisture content was significantly lowest at the surface and peaked in the 10–15 cm layer. Subsurface layers (5–20 cm) showed lower strength-related properties (PL, PI, BD, DD) and higher porosity and moisture. Soil mechanical properties showed significant variation with depth, particularly for dynamic cone penetration (DCP) resistance and cohesion, while the friction angle remains relatively consistent. The DCP resistance varies significantly, with the highest strength observed in the 5–10 cm layer and the lowest in the 20–25 cm layer. Cohesion is significantly highest in the top two layers (0–10 cm) and decreases to 1.1 kPa in the 10–20 cm depth. Soil chemical properties exhibit significant differences between the upper (0–15 cm) and lower (15–25 cm) soil layers. The topsoil has significantly higher levels of total nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and cation exchange capacity compared to the subsoil. The modified plow significantly improved soil physical properties by reducing specific gravity, increasing plastic limit, and decreasing plastic index, promoting better root penetration and water retention. Soil moisture content was increased by 10.52% under the modified plow, indicating enhanced water-holding capacity. In terms of mechanical properties, modified tillage reduced penetration resistance and friction by 15.59% and 4.76% respectively, and mitigating compaction risks. The modified plow increased soil organic carbon (10.75%), total nitrogen (35.71%), and available phosphorus (17.93%), while maintaining stable pH levels. These improvements suggested enhanced nutrient cycling and microbial activity. The modified plow optimizes soil health by improving structure, reducing compaction, and boosting fertility, thereby supporting sustainable wheat production in light-textured soils. Future research should focus on promoting modified tillage adoption among smallholder farmers, integrating it with organic amendments, and providing training for effective implementation. This research underscores the potential of tailored tillage innovations to enhance agricultural productivity and soil sustainability in Ethiopia’s highlands.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23590-y.

Introduction

Agriculture had long served as the foundation of Ethiopia’s economy, sustaining the livelihoods of rural communities and playing a vital role in food security and development1. In the Misrak Bedawacho District of Central Ethiopia, ox-drawn tillage implements remained the dominant source of farm power. The traditional Maresha, a wooden plough fitted with an iron tip, had been widely adopted due to its low cost and accessibility2. However, its use was associated with shallow soil disturbance, frequent inversion of topsoil, and limited residue incorporation, all of which contributed to soil degradation and reduced productivity3,4. In response to these challenges, alternative tillage tools were introduced with the purpose of breaking the soil more effectively, incorporating residues, and conserving soil moisture5,6.

Repeated plowing accelerates soil degradation by breaking down structure, promoting erosion, and depleting organic matter7. This reduces crop performance and contributes to ongoing food insecurity. Soil itself is a critical natural resource, forming a dynamic system of minerals, organic matter, water, and air that sustains crop growth, regulates the hydrological cycle, and stores carbon8. Healthy soil supports root development, nutrient cycling, and biodiversity, but these functions decline when soil is disturbed through intensive tillage9. In Ethiopia, it is estimated that more than 27 million hectares of farmland are affected by erosion, nutrient loss, and compaction10. Heavy reliance on shallow-working tools such as the Maresha has been identified as a major contributor to this widespread degradation.

Soil is a fundamental component of the mesosphere, is composed of minerals, organic matter, water, air, and a diverse community of microorganisms and micro-fauna. These elements interact to create a dynamic environment that supports plant growth by providing essential nutrients, maintaining moisture, and facilitating root development. It serves four critical functions: functioning as a substrate for plant development; storing, supplying, and purifying water; affecting the atmosphere; and providing a habitat for species involved in decomposition and the creation of ecosystems for other organisms. Soil is often described as the earth’s skin, with interfaces connecting the lithosphere, hydrosphere, atmosphere, and biosphere11. Because it consists of a solid layer (minerals and organic components) and a porosity phase that holds water and gases, soil is sometimes thought of as a three-state system. The goal of tillage is to establish a condition of organic matter that promotes the growth of plants. Soil tillage refers to the practice of modifying the soil through physical, chemical, or biological methods to enhance the environment for growing crops, establishing seedlings, and promoting germination12.

Any mechanical disruption of the soil, whether performed manually or with machinery during various agricultural practices, is referred to as soil tillage13. Despite its widespread use and historical significance, traditional tillage using tools like the ‘Maresha’ presents several limitations that contribute to soil degradation14. They only till shallowly, restricting root growth and nutrient access, while their use along slopes contributes to soil erosion in the country’s hilly terrain. The maresha also fails to manage weeds and crop residues effectively, leading to nutrient loss, and repeated shallow tillage compacts the soil, forming hardpans that hinder water infiltration14. Despite these technological efforts, limited evidence had been available regarding how traditional and modified tillage systems influenced soil properties under specific local conditions. Earlier studies tended to generalize findings without focusing on the unique soils and practices of Chefa Kebele in Misrak Bedawacho District15. In particular, the comparative effects of these tillage systems on key soil parameters such as bulk density, porosity, moisture retention, and nutrient dynamics remained poorly understood. This gap made it difficult for farmers and policymakers to judge the benefits of adopting modified implements. Therefore, the present study was conducted to evaluate the impact of different tillage practices on soil properties at varying depths, to provide locally relevant insights for sustainable soil management and wheat-based farming in Central Ethiopia.

To tackle these limitations, studies have prioritized enhancing traditional implements rather than discarding them altogether. Modified tillage tools are designed to improve soil penetration, reduce compaction, and prepare land with fewer passes while retaining cultural familiarity and affordability16. Adjustments such as deeper cutting angles, stronger blades, and adjustable depths enable these implements to loosen subsoil layers, improve infiltration, and enhance soil porosity17. This is particularly important in smallholder systems, where soil moisture storage and root growth are critical for reliable crop production. Nevertheless, gaps remain in understanding how modified tillage implements affect soil quality. While many studies have tested mechanical efficiency, fewer have analyzed the influence of these tools on the soil’s physical and chemical characteristics18. In addition, most evaluations have been conducted in clay-rich soils, whereas light-textured soils, common in parts of Central Ethiopia, remain underexplored. Since sandy and loamy soils are more prone to compaction and poor water retention, tillage choice in these conditions may strongly influence productivity.

In Central Ethiopia, smallholder farmers rely heavily on traditional tillage tools like the maresha, an ox-drawn plow. While culturally significant and locally adapted, these tools are inefficient and labor-intensive, leading to poor soil preparation, moisture loss, and reduced yields16. The Misrak Badawacho District offers a suitable case for such an inquiry. Farmers in the area mainly depend on animal-drawn tools, yet soil fertility decline and low crop yields are major concerns. Although modified implements have been introduced in some Ethiopian regions, their performance in Misrak Badawacho soils has not been systematically studied. Investigating their impact on soil bulk density, porosity, organic matter, and moisture at different depths can provide valuable evidence for improving tillage practices. This study, therefore, aims to evaluate and compare the effects of the traditional Maresha and modified plow on soil properties under local farming conditions in Chefa Kebele, Misrak Badawacho District. The findings are expected to inform strategies for sustainable soil management, support higher crop yields, and promote climate-resilient farming systems while preserving indigenous agricultural knowledge.

Materials and methods

Description of study area

The study was conducted in the Chefa Kebele, located in the Misrak Bedawacho District of the Hadiya Zone, in Central Ethiopia. This Kebele was chosen as a representative area for smallholder farming systems within the district, characterized by mixed crop-livestock production and a strong reliance on traditional agricultural methods, including the use of animal-drawn plows. The kebele’s local market, Chefa, lies along the main road to Wolaita Sodo, approximately 314 km from Addis Ababa. It is also situated about 10 km from the district center, Shone, and 95 km from the regional capital, Hossana Chefa Kebele, located in the Misrak Bedawacho District of the Hadiya Zone, in Central Ethiopia (Fig. 1). The geographical coordinates of 2nd Chefa Kebele are approximately 7°21′N latitude and 38°06′E longitude.Fig. 1Map of study area.

Map of study area.

History of tillage in the experimental site

The experimental site Chefa kebeles had a long history of traditional tillage practices primarily dominated by repeated use of traditional plow, which contributed to the gradual degradation of soil structure and fertility over time. Prior to the current study, the land had been cultivated continuously for several years under conventional tillage to produce wheat, characterized by frequent soil disturbance and shallow plowing depths of approximately 15–20 cm. This repeated disturbance led to compaction of the subsurface layer, reduced organic matter, and declining soil productivity, as also observed in similar Ethiopian highland soils19. Such a background justified the need to investigate the effects of alternative tillage systems on soil physical and chemical properties across different depths, aiming to identify sustainable practices suitable for the region’s agro-ecological conditions.

Design of the study

Farmers at Chefa kebele had been using a traditional plow, a pair of oxen used for land preparation before sowing of cereals, especially wheat for the consumption of households. A randomized complete block design (RCBD) was employed to minimize spatial variability across the site, with tillage operations carried out by a trained operator using a pair of oxen on light-textured soils. The field experiment compared the performance of traditional and modified plows across five soil depths: 0–5 cm, 5–10 cm, 10–15 cm, 15–20 cm, and 20–25 cm in March 2025, it operated for two weeks. The data related to the moisture content and bulk density at the field level was collected March 15 and 18, 2025.

The traditional plow, widely used in Ethiopian agriculture, consists of a wooden frame with a narrow iron share (15–20 cm width) and typically achieves a working depth of 10–12 cm (Fig. 2a). Lightweight (10–15 kg), it requires repeated passes to sufficiently loosen the soil. In contrast, the modified plow was developed as an adaptation to overcome these limitations. It features a wider V-shaped share (20 cm) with winged extensions, allowing deeper penetration up to 15 cm and cutting across a larger surface area. The design includes a reinforced wooden handle with steel fittings, weighing about 8 kg, which enhances durability and efficiency. Unlike the traditional plow, which mainly scratches the surface, the modified plow improves soil inversion, reduces clod size, and enhances moisture retention by minimizing multiple passes (Fig. 2b). The field soil samples were collected in March 2025 in the belg season for the preparation of farmers plot of land for wheat production.Fig. 2(a) Traditional plow and (b) Modified plow.

(a) Traditional plow and (b) Modified plow.

Experimental design

Soil samples for the physio-mechanical investigation were collected from a 60 m × 100 m farmland using a stratified random sampling method to ensure representativeness as shown on Fig. 3. The sampling grid was established at 0.05 m intervals across the cultivation layer, covering the entire farmland. Samples were collected at five distinct depth ranges corresponding to tillage depths (TD): 0–0.05 m, 0.05–0.10 m, 0.10–0.15 m, 0.15–0.20 m, and 0.20–0.25 m below the target cultivation depth. To ensure statistical reliability, three replications were taken for each depth range at every sampling point, resulting in a total of 15 samples per sampling location (5 depths × 3 replications). This replication strategy was applied across the entire farmland to account for spatial variability and ensure robust data. The collected samples were transported to Hawassa University Geotechnical Laboratory and Hawassa soil laboratory for detailed analysis of their physical, mechanical, and chemical properties, including texture, moisture content, bulk density, shear strength, cohesion, and plasticity indices. This methodical approach ensured precise characterization of the soil’s behavior and suitability for agricultural practices.Fig. 3Experimental sampling layout in field of Chefa kebele.

Experimental sampling layout in field of Chefa kebele.

Methods of data collection

The experiment was conducted to assess the status of soil properties, composite samples were collected from the entire experimental field using procedures in the real field to study the effect of traditional tillage and modified plow on characteristics of soil tillage, soil type, and condition and implement draft resistance during tillage operation. The air-dried soil samples were then ground and sieved to pass through a 2-mm mesh sieve and analyzed in the Hawassa University, soil laboratory center. Soil samples and in-situ measurements (moisture content and bulk density) were collected at predetermined intervals and locations within each experimental plot (Fig. 3). Data collection focused on critical soil physical, mechanical, and chemical parameters. Soil samples were obtained from each experimental plot at various sampling depths (0–5 cm, 5–10 cm, 10–15 cm, 15–20 cm, and 20–25 cm).

Mechanical soil properties

Soil penetration resistance was measured using a handheld cone penetrometer equipped with a standard cone tip. Resistance measurements were collected vertically from experimental plot, with readings taken at consistent depth intervals of 0–5 cm, 5–10 cm, 10–15 cm, 15–20 cm, and 20–25 cm. Measurements were taken when soil moisture conditions were consistent (near field capacity) across plots to minimize the influence of moisture variability on readings. Data were recorded in units of pressure (kPa). Soil penetration resistance was determined prior to and soon after tillage and periodically before planting. Wet sieving (Kemper and Rosenau method) was used to determine aggregate stability25. Soil aggregates was put on the set of sieves with opening sizes 5.0, 2.0, 1.0, 0.5, and 0.25 mm ordered from top to bottom and immersed into the distilled water and allowed to rise and lower to 1.3 cm, 35 times/min, for 3 min before being mechanically sieved. MWD = mean weight diameter, mm, xi = mean diameter of any size range i of any aggregate, mm, and wi = weight of aggregate in size range i as a fraction of total dry weight in grams.

Atterberg limits for cohesive soil samples were determined in line with ASTM D4318. Standard tests, such as the Casagrande test for determining the liquid limit (LL) and plastic limit (PL) were performed to assess the soil’s consistency and its deformation behavior under varying moisture conditions. Plasticity index (PI) is the difference between the liquid limit and the plastic limit, indicating the soil’s plasticity and potential for expansion.

To determine the specific gravity of soil, use the pycnometer method in a laboratory, which involves weighing an empty pycnometer (volumetric flask), then a pycnometer with a dry soil sample, followed by the pycnometer with soil and distilled water, and finally the pycnometer filled only with distilled water. The soil–water slurry was agitated, and air bubbles are removed using a vacuum or de-airing apparatus, ensuring a correct reading for the weight of the flask with soil and water.

ASTM D2216 Standard Test Methods was employed for the determination of the dry density of soils. To determine soil dry density at a field site, you can use field-based methods like the sand replacement method or core cutter method, which involve excavating a volume of soil, weighing the excavated soil, determining its volume, and measuring its moisture content to calculate dry density.

Soil chemical properties

Soil pH, measured in a soil–water (1:2.5) suspension using a calibrated digital pH meter26, soil organic carbon (OC) was determined using the Walkley–Black wet oxidation method, which involves oxidizing organic carbon with potassium dichromate (0.167 M) in the presence of concentrated sulfuric acid, followed by titration with ferrous ammonium sulfate (0.5 M solution)27. Organic matter content was estimated by multiplying OC by a factor of 1.72428. Total Nitrogen (TN) was determined using the Kjeldahl method29, this involves digesting the soil sample to convert organic nitrogen to ammonium, followed by distillation and titration to quantify the ammonia produced30, available phosphorus was acquired using the Olsen method for alkaline and neutral soils, using sodium bicarbonate as the extracting solution, followed by colorimetric determination using a spectrophotometer31. Exchangeable Cations (K⁺, Ca2⁺, Mg2⁺, Na⁺) was determined by extracting with 1 M ammonium acetate at pH 7.0, followed by analysis of the extract using atomic absorption spectrophotometry (AAS) or flame photometry32 and cation exchange capacity (CEC) was determined using the ammonium acetate method32. After extracting exchangeable cations, the soil sample is saturated with ammonium, excess ammonium was washed out, and the adsorbed ammonium was replaced by another cation and measured33.

Statistical data analysis

All data were subjected to two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using statistical analysis software (SAS) and a paired t-test. The homogeneity and normality of the soil parameters were tested using Levene’s and Shapiro–Wilk test, respectively, and a paired t-test was employed to detect whether there is a significant effect (P < 0.05) on soil properties at various depths. The least significant difference at a 5% probability level was used to determine whether significant differences existed among the induced soil physical properties obtained using different tillage implements34.

Results

Soil physical properties influenced by varying depths

The soil properties included in the study were tested for homogeneity and normality using Levene’s and the Shapiro–Wilk test, and the results confirmed that they satisfied the assumptions of these tests (Supplementary data). The findings in Table 1 indicated that soil physical properties varied significantly with depth. The liquid limit (LL) ranged from 38.2 to 43.5%, with the highest value observed at 0–5 cm and the lowest at 5–10 cm, showing a gradual decrease with depth before reaching a stable level in deeper layers. The plastic limit (PL) followed a similar pattern, ranging from 34.8 to 38.5%. The plasticity index (PI) remained low across all depths, with values between 3.5 and 6.0%, which indicated that the soil had low plasticity and a limited ability to deform without cracking. Liquid limit, plastic limit, and plasticity index were measured to assess soil consistency and workability, as they influence tillage efficiency, root penetration, and water retention. In light soils, these properties help determine how easily the soil can be prepared and how well it supports wheat growth. Bulk density (BD) showed slight variation with depth, with the lowest value (1.33 g/cm3) at 5–10 cm and the highest (1.41 g/cm3) at both 0–5 cm and 20–25 cm. Moisture content (MC) varied notably across depths, ranging from 16.7% at the surface (0–5 cm) to 22.8% at 10–15 cm. This reflected greater water retention at intermediate depths, likely due to lower evaporation and favorable pore distribution. Specific gravity (SG) remained fairly constant, between 2.23 and 2.26, showing little variation among depths. In contrast, dry density (DD) varied more strongly, with the lowest values (1.12 g/cm3) recorded between 10–20 cm and the highest (1.19–1.22 g/cm3) at the surface and deeper layers. Overall, the results suggested that the top and deeper soil layers were more compact and retained less water, whereas the intermediate layers (particularly 10–15 cm) provided better moisture storage and lower compaction, which created more favorable conditions for root penetration and wheat growth.

Soil physical properties influenced by tillage practices

The results in Table 2 showed that tillage practice had a significant effect on some soil physical properties. The liquid limit (LL) was slightly higher under traditional tillage (41.33%) compared to modified tillage (40.13%), though the difference was small but statistically significant (p < 0.05). The plastic limit (PL) exhibited a more pronounced difference, with traditional tillage showing a higher value (39.27%) than modified tillage (34.20%), indicating that soil under modified tillage required less water to reach the plastic state. Discussions

This study aimed to critically evaluate and compare the impacts of traditional ‘Maresha’ plow versus a modified plow on the physical, mechanical, and chemical properties of soil at various depths (0–5 cm, 5–10 cm, 10–15 cm, 15–20 cm, and 20–25 cm) in Chefa Kebele, Misrak Bedawacho District, Hadiya Zone, Central Ethiopia. The study results showed that soil physical properties like liquid limit (LL), plastic limit (PL), and plasticity index (PI) were highest at the 0–5 cm depth, indicating a higher clay and organic matter content in the top layer. Changes in soil consistency limits further supported the advantages of modified tillage. The observed reduction in liquid and plastic limits, combined with an increase in plasticity index, reflected enhanced aggregation and reduced compaction. Modified tillage improves soil structure, breaks up clods, and incorporates organic matter more effectively. This reduces the soil’s tendency to hold excessive water before changing consistency. Improved porosity and stable aggregates limit the swelling behavior of clays, lowering both the liquid limit (LL) and plastic limit (PL), and increased plasticity index 35. These results aligned with findings of Obalum et al.36 and Okoko et al.37, who noted that improved soil structure broadens workable moisture conditions, creating a more favorable seedbed. This indicates that modified tillage may provide better soil workability compared to the Maresha, which often leaves soils cloddy and compacted. As depth increased, LL and PL values declined, particularly at the 5–10 cm layer, implying a shift toward more granular particles and diminished organic matter influence. This trend was consistent with the conclusions of Geremew et al.38 and Leharwan et al.39, who attributed these patterns to leaching and natural decomposition processes that stratify soil properties. The resulting reduction in plasticity at deeper levels suggested changes in the soil’s workability and water dynamics, critical factors for root penetration and tillage efficiency, as noted by Ghezzehei 40. In addition, in line with the findings of Bezuglova et al. 41 and Senesi and Loffredo12, who showed that lower tilting preserves soil structure. Higher moisture content in the 10–20 cm zone compared to the surface likely reflects reduced evaporation losses at that depth and a more favorable pore-size distribution for water retention. In fact, surface layers suffer greater exposure to atmospheric drying and capillary breakage, while subsurface layers maintain continuity of fine pores and capillary connectivity (reducing vapor losses). Recent studies support that pore structure controls evaporative fluxes and moisture gradients in soils, particularly in the intermediate vadose zone where evaporation becomes limited by internal diffusion rather than surface desiccation 42.

The modified plow resulted in significantly reduced LL and PL values but exhibited an increased PI, indicating improved plasticity and better workability. These outcomes aligned with the research of Das et al.43, who demonstrated that reduced tillage enhances soil structure by preserving organic matter and promoting aggregate stability. The higher PI value observed under modified plow (5.93) in comparison to traditional tillage (2.67) reflected a soil environment more resistant to cracking, which is advantageous for plant establishment, particularly under water stress conditions. Similar benefits of conservation tillage were reported by Li44, who found that such practices contributed to increased structural quality and resilience under variable moisture conditions. Bulk density (BD) and dry density (DD) reached their highest values at the surface and declined at mid-depths, while total porosity (TP) showed a slight increase likely due to biological activity and root proliferation in the topsoil. These outcomes supported earlier findings Bodner et al. 45 and Govaerts et al.46, who emphasized the role of organic matter and soil biota in maintaining low BD and high porosity near the surface. Specific gravity (SG) remained constant across all layers, indicating minimal differences in mineral composition and affirming observations by Ghezzehei40, who suggested that such physical variations were more structural than mineralogical in nature. The modified plow has a higher mean moisture content (21.01% vs 19.01%) but with p > 0.05 aligns with recent literature showing small enhancements in moisture retention by improved or conservation tillage systems, but often not large enough to reach statistical significance in many contexts47, another study in Hungary comparing conservation tillage and conventional plowing found that while conservation methods tended to maintain higher soil moisture48.

The increase in soil organic carbon (SOC) and nitrogen under modified plow was consistent with findings from earlier studies that linked reduced soil disturbance and greater residue incorporation to improved soil fertility. Reduced microbial decomposition and enhanced residue retention under conservation-oriented tillage have been shown to stabilize soil aggregates and protect organic matter49. Soil organic matter (SOM) increased with depth, peaking at 22.8% in the 10–15 cm layer, which aligned with Yans et al.50, who highlighted reduced evaporation and compaction as key contributors to moisture retention in lower soil horizons. The redistribution of sulfur, with reduced concentrations at the surface and increases in deeper layers, contrasts with some previous reports that found minimal vertical redistribution of sulfur under traditional tillage Kumar et al.51. The likely explanation in this study was enhanced infiltration in light soils, which facilitated leaching to deeper horizons. Similar sulfur movement under high infiltration was noted by Ruhul52, supporting the interpretation that soil texture and water movement strongly influenced nutrient dynamics.

In terms of mechanical properties, dynamic cone penetrometer (DCP) resistance peaked at the 5–10 cm depth, indicating increased compaction possibly caused by equipment traffic or repeated tillage a phenomenon aligned with the plow pan concept described by Blume53; conversely, the lowest DCP value appeared at 20–25 cm, suggesting a looser subsoil less influenced by mechanical disturbance. The friction angle remained relatively constant across depths (26.5°–27.3°), pointing to a consistent soil texture throughout, consistent with the findings of Duddu et al.54. The resistance peak observed at a depth of 5–10 cm indicated the presence of a plow pan, likely formed by frequent shallow tillage using ox-drawn implements and erosion. This finding is consistent with previous studies that have identified compacted layers in Ethiopian and other smallholder farming systems55. Minor variations in cohesion across depths likely reflected changes in moisture content and organic matter levels, as supported by Belmonte56, who emphasized the influence of biological factors on soil cohesion. In terms of mechanical soil properties, Shah and Wu57 found the reduction of compaction risks and promotion of root growth are supported by the noticeably decreased resistance to penetration and angles of friction readings observed close to the surface in modified tillage plots.

Under the modified plow, both the angle of internal friction and DCP resistance decreased, suggesting a decrease in soil compaction and improved penetration ease. These findings align with a previous study that found conservation tillage reduced mechanical resistance, promoting better root and water movement58. Although cohesion values remained statistically similar between treatments (1.40% vs. 1.47%), the lower DCP value under modified plow (75.07 kPa) as compared to traditional tillage (88.93 kPa) suggested a less compacted and more friable soil structure. Similar patterns were documented by Bescansa et al.59, who concluded that reduced tillage helps preserve pore continuity and mitigates compaction, thereby supporting better root development without compromising structural stability.

The chemical analysis revealed that the topsoil (0–15 cm) consistently contained higher levels of organic matter and essential nutrients such as nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium compared to subsoil layers, which reinforced the concept of nutrient stratification described by Rahman et al.60. Potassium was significantly higher in the surface layer, reflecting the influence of organic inputs, fertilizer application, and limited mobility of these nutrients. These findings are consistent with reports that nutrient stratification is common in cultivated soils, with surface enrichment of immobile nutrients such as N, P, and K, and subsurface accumulation of leachable elements like sulfur61.

Modified plow further enhanced chemical fertility indicators, with increased organic carbon, total nitrogen, and macronutrient availability, consistent with the findings of62, who associated reduced tillage with improved nutrient cycling. In contrast, traditional tillage techniques encourage nitrogen stability in organic forms or immobilization within microbial biomass, which may not be easily accessible to plants. This complexity underscores the importance of effectively managing nitrogen in tillage systems to enhance productivity and reduce losses63. Likewise, a study indicating enhanced sulfur mineralization due to improved soil aeration and organic matter availability aligns with the observed higher sulfur levels in modern tillage practices64. A slight increase in pH and consistent cation exchange capacity (CEC) under modified tillage practices confirmed the findings of Ma et al.65, highlighting the importance of minimal soil disturbance in preserving soil fertility and chemical equilibrium.

The observed higher potassium (K) under the modified plow (640.07 mg/kg vs. 579.07 mg/kg; p < 0.05) suggests that this tillage system enhances K availability—likely by improving root access, reducing K losses, and promoting better incorporation of residues. This finding aligns with recent empirical work: for example, a long‐term rice-upland rotation experiment showed that continuous K fertilization together with straw return significantly increased exchangeable potassium (by 27.7–102.1%) and improved soil K supply capacity, with improved pore structure boosting root-soil interactions66. Also, in Planosols and Ferralsols, different rates and methods of K application (e.g. banding vs broadcast) had measurable effects on soil K availability and crop response, underlining that management practices can make a substantial difference in maintaining or raising soil K status67. Overall, these results highlighted modified tillage as both a structural and chemical management strategy conducive to sustainable soil health.

Conclusion

The study has clearly shown that both soil depth and tillage practices significantly affect the physical, mechanical, and chemical properties of soil. The surface layer (0–5 cm) exhibited higher values for liquid limit, plastic limit, and plasticity index, along with lower bulk density, indicating a higher organic matter content and improved structure for moisture retention and root development. In contrast, subsoil layers demonstrated lower plasticity but higher moisture content, likely due to greater compaction and reduced evaporation. Modified plow has enhanced the plasticity index and moisture retention while maintaining comparable bulk density and porosity, suggesting improved soil workability without compromising its structural integrity. Mechanical properties such as dynamic cone penetrometer (DCP) resistance and internal friction angle varied with depth and tillage method; the mid-soil layers displayed signs of compaction, possibly forming a plow pan, whereas modified tillage reduced resistance, thereby facilitating better root penetration and water infiltration.

From a chemical standpoint, the topsoil consistently contained more organic matter, nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium than the subsoil, underscoring the critical role of surface management in nutrient conservation. Modified tillage improved chemical fertility by increasing organic carbon levels and nutrient availability, although sulfur content and cation exchange capacity (CEC) showed only slight differences. Overall, the findings confirm that modified tillage enhances several key indicators of soil quality when compared to traditional tillage, particularly about moisture availability, plasticity, and nutrient status. These improvements indicate that adopting modified tillage practices can promote soil health, support sustainable agriculture, and optimize land productivity by improving both the structural and fertility aspects of the soil profile. Although this study has demonstrated the benefits of modified tillage for improving soil quality, further research is needed to evaluate its long-term effects under varying cropping systems and environmental conditions. Future studies should focus on seasonal changes in soil moisture and compaction, the durability of fertility improvements under continuous cultivation, and how tillage methods interact with biological factors such as microbial activity and root systems. Moreover, examining the cost-effectiveness and environmental impact of modified tillage at a farm scale would provide essential insights for its broader implementation and long-term sustainability.

Limitation of the study

The study was conducted over a single cropping season, which limited the ability to capture long-term cumulative effects of tillage practices. Seasonal variability in rainfall and temperature may have influenced moisture and nutrient redistribution. Furthermore, the research was site-specific to light-textured soils of Chefa Kebele, which may restrict the generalizability of findings to other soil types and agro-ecological zones. Multi-season and multi-location trials are recommended to validate the results and assess the scalability of modified tillage across diverse farming contexts.
