Associations between estimated glucose disposal rate and age-related ocular diseases in cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome stages 0–3: a large prospective cohort study
Authors: Linge Jian, Zhiqian Huang, Jiao Qi, Jiaqi Meng, Keke Zhang, Xiangjia Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study investigated the relationship between estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR) and the risk of age-related ocular diseases, including macular degeneration, glaucoma, cataracts, diabetic retinopathy (DR) and retinal detachment (RD), in individuals with stages 0–3 of cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome.Methods and analysisThis prospective cohort study included 223 120 participants from the UK Biobank. The CKM stages were defined based on adiposity, metabolic risk factors and subclinical cardiovascular disease. Lower eGDR values indicate greater insulin resistance. Outcomes were incidences of macular degeneration, glaucoma, cataract, DR and RD. HRs and 95% CIs were estimated using Cox proportional hazards models. Non-linear relationships were explored using restricted cubic splines.ResultsThe study showed that macular degeneration (HR 0.92, 95% CI 0.87 to 0.97, p=0.001) and glaucoma (HR 0.91, 95% CI 0.87 to 0.95, p<0.001) were linearly associated with eGDR. Cataracts exhibited a U-shaped association with eGDR (P non-linear=0.001) and DR exhibited an L-shaped association (P non-linear=0.018). Quartile stratification of eGDR significantly differentiated risk in DR (Q4 vs Q1: HR 0.15, 95% CI 0.04 to 0.52, p=0.003) and RD (Q4 vs Q1: HR 0.65, 95% CI 0.47 to 0.89, p=0.007). Stratified effects analysis revealed that these associations were more significant in advanced CKM syndrome stages.ConclusionseGDR is associated with ocular diseases risk in CKM syndrome, especially in advanced stages. This finding suggests the potential use of eGDR for guiding ophthalmic screening in CKM management.Keywords: Epidemiology, Public health, Vision, Cataract, Glaucoma, Macular Degeneration, Retina

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

However, evidence is limited regarding how insulin resistance, as estimated by glucose disposal rate, relates to age-related ocular diseases within the context of cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome stages.

Introduction

Age-related ocular diseases, including macular degeneration, glaucoma, cataract, diabetic retinopathy (DR) and retinal detachment (RD) are leading causes of visual impairment among the elderly, representing significant public health challenges.1 By 2050, about 1.8 billion people worldwide will be at risk of losing their sight, with 30% of people with diabetes affected by DR alone.2 Current treatment options, typically administered after the disease onsets, may not always yield optimal outcomes, resulting in limited visual rehabilitation or compromised life quality.3 Emerging evidence suggests that systemic metabolic disorders may increase the likelihood of ocular complications via shared mechanisms, such as microvascular injury and chronic inflammation.4 Insulin resistance (IR) causes mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress in retinal neurons, which promotes photoreceptor apoptosis in age-related macular degeneration (AMD).5 Chronic hyperglycaemia triggers glycotoxic damage to retinal pericytes, which initiates the formation of diabetic microaneurysms. Meanwhile, endothelial dysfunction disrupts choroidal blood flow and exacerbates drusen deposition in macular degeneration.6,8 Given the associations with systemic metabolic disorders, preventive measures and early interventions are crucial to mitigate the impact of these common ocular diseases.

The cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome is a new concept proposed by the American Heart Association (AHA) in 2023.9 It is a systemic disease caused by the complex interactions between metabolic disorders, chronic kidney disease (CKD) and cardiovascular disease (CVD).10 To facilitate prevention and management, the AHA has classified CKM syndrome into five stages, with stages 0 through 3 representing the preclinical phases.9 11 The AHA guidelines clearly recommend a multidisciplinary team approach to managing patients with stage 2 or higher.10 CKM syndrome is characterised by three pathophysiological mechanisms: IR, chronic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction. IR can induce a metabolic-inflammation-mediated cascade of microvascular injury through glycation, oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction. This process accelerates cardiovascular and kidney damage and affects retinal microcirculation through systemic microangiopathy, significantly increasing the risk of eye diseases, such as DR.12

In light of this, various methods have been developed to evaluate IR. Previous studies have used the hyperinsulinaemic-euglycaemic (HIEG) clamp and the homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) as the main indicators. However, the HIEG clamp is complicated to operate and expensive, limiting its clinical application. Additionally, HOMA-IR is not sufficiently reliable for individual assessment, so researchers urgently need to develop simple, comprehensive alternative indicators.13 14 In this context, the estimated glucose disposition rate (eGDR), a newly proposed IR evaluation index, provides a convenient way to evaluate peripheral tissue insulin sensitivity and metabolic homeostasis.12 It has been confirmed that eGDR is significantly associated with cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events and mortality in patients with CKM syndrome.15 However, under the CKM staging framework, the associations of eGDR with ocular diseases remain unclear. This study focuses on participants at CKM stages 0–3. Unlike stage 4 patients who have already experienced clinical cardiovascular events—where treatment prioritises survival maintenance—individuals in stages 0–3 bear the primary disease burden and represent a critical window for preventing target organ damage. At this disease phase, eGDR functions as a quantifiable biomarker for the underlying core pathological mechanisms of CKM, including IR, inflammation and endothelial dysfunction.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to determine the stage-specific thresholds of eGDR for the risk of the above five ocular diseases in patients with CKM syndrome stages 0–3 using a large-sample cohort analysis, which would provide the basis for constructing a stage-directed ophthalmic screening tool.

Methods

Study design and population

The UK Biobank is a substantial prospective cohort initiative that enrolled over 500 000 individuals from across the UK. Participants were aged 37–73 years during the recruitment phase from 2006 to 2010. Information for the database was collected using various methods, including touchscreen questionnaires, interviews, physical examinations, biological specimens and imaging. Detailed protocols are available at https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk/. The UK Biobank obtained ethical approval from the North West Multi-Center Research Ethics Committee, with all participants providing their informed consent electronically at the time of recruitment. Participants were excluded if they: (1) lacked adequate data on obesity (n=3165) and metabolic risk factors (n=70 005); (2) had pre-existing diagnoses of macular degeneration, glaucoma, cataract, DR or RD (n=51 569) at baseline; (3) only had CVD at baseline (n=114); (4) were classified as CKM stage 4 (n=50 864); (5) did not have all the necessary information for the eGDR calculation (n=97 835); (6) had missing data for covariates (n=4390). After applying these criteria, a total of 223 120 participants were included in the final analysis.

Definition of CKM syndrome

A classification of CKM syndrome staging (from 0 to 4) was defined based on previous studies, with specific criteria shown in online supplemental table S1.16 We classified the participants into various stages based on their health status: stage 0: no CKM health risk factors; stage 1: presence of excessive or dysfunctional adiposity; stage 2: presence of metabolic risk factors or moderate to high-risk CKD; stage 3: presence of subclinical CVD. Subclinical CVD was defined as a high predicted 10-year CVD risk or very high-risk CKD stage. A high 10-year CVD risk is characterised as 20% or above, as calculated by the basic PREVENT equation.9 17 The base equations are provided in online supplemental table S2. The classification of CKD is established according to Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes criteria, which use estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio.18 The eGFR was computed using the 2021 race and ethnicity-free Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration creatinine equation.19

Calculation of eGDR

The eGDR was calculated as follows: 21.158 − (0.09 × waist circumference (cm)) − (3.407 × hypertension (yes 1 or no 0)) − (0.551 × glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) (%)).15 Hypertension was defined according to the International Classification of Diseases (ICD), 10th revision, with codes I10, or systolic blood pressure ≥140 mm Hg, and diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mm Hg, or use of antihypertensive medication or based on general practitioner diagnosis, medication reimbursement or self-reported information. HbA1c was measured by the high-performance liquid chromatography method. Furthermore, the unit in mmol/mol was transformed to a percentage (%) using the equation: (0.09148 × HbA1c mmol/mol) + 2.152.20 The entire group was divided into eGDR quartiles, with the lowest eGDR quartile as the reference group and represented the highest IR.

Ascertainment of outcomes

The present study considered five common age-related ocular diseases in middle-aged and older adults: macular degeneration, glaucoma, cataract, DR and RD.

Incident ocular diseases were defined using the ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes, consistent with the methodologies employed in previous studies.21 A complete list of the corresponding codes for each disease is provided in online supplemental table S3. At the time of this analysis, follow-up data was available through 31 October 2022, for England; 31 August 2022, for Scotland; and 31 May 2022, for Wales. Follow-up duration was calculated from recruitment until the occurrence of the event of interest or censoring. The primary event of interest was defined as the first diagnosis of an ocular disease. Participants were right-censored at the earliest occurrence of: (1) death from any cause, (2) loss to follow-up or (3) administrative, country-specific follow-up endpoints.

Assessment of covariates

In this study, the socio-demographic and lifestyle covariates taken into consideration included age at baseline, sex, ethnicity (Asian, white, black or others), the Townsend Deprivation Index (TDI), body mass index (BMI) (≤25, >25 to <30 or ≥30 kg/m2), education level (low/high), smoking status (never, previous or current), alcohol intake frequency (never or seldom, moderate, frequent), health status (poor, fair, good or excellent), HbA1c, total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, eGFR and the use of antihypertensive, lipid-lowering and antidiabetic medications. Age was calculated using the date the person attended the assessment centre and the date of birth. The TDI was calculated based on the output area in which the patients’ postcode was located. Detailed definitions of these covariates are provided in online supplemental table S4.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics, categorised by CKM stages, were summarised as frequencies with percentages for categorical variables and means with SDs for continuous variables. Group comparisons were conducted using the Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test for continuous variables and the χ2 test for categorical variables.

The Cox proportional hazards regression model was used to examine the associations between eGDR and age-related ocular diseases in CKM stages 0–3. The results were presented as HRs with 95% CIs. We built several models with varying degrees of adjustment. Model 1 did not adjust for any covariates. Model 2 adjusted for age, sex, race, education, BMI, TDI, smoking status, alcohol consumption frequency and health status. Model 3 was a fully adjusted model with additional adjustments for HbA1c, TC, HDL, eGFR, antihypertensive drugs, antihyperglycaemic drugs and antihyperlipidaemic drugs on top of Model 2. Because eGDR is closely associated with diabetes and CKM stage is associated with diabetes progression, we categorised the participants into two groups based on CKM stage: early (stages 0–1) and late (stages 2–3). Within each group, we performed Cox regression analyses to examine stage-specific associations.

To further explore the potential non-linear relationship, restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were used. Subgroup analyses further explore changes in the relationship between eGDR and ocular disease across demographics and lifestyle, including age, sex, race and smoking status. Potential interaction effects were identified using likelihood ratio tests by comparing Cox models with or without an interaction term of the stratification factor and exposure.

Additionally, we conducted several sensitivity analyses to rigorously assess the robustness of our findings. First, we rigorously excluded participants whose follow-up time fell below 2 years. Second, we performed the identical analysis on a significantly expanded cohort (N=227 510), incorporating participants with previously missing covariate data that had been imputed. These imputations employed sophisticated chained equations, using five imputations and all other covariates as predictors, implemented via the R package ‘mice’. Finally, we constructed Fine-Gray competing risk models as a sensitivity analysis, explicitly treating death as the competing event for distinct ocular diseases.

All statistical analyses were performed using R V.4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). The statistical tests were two-sided and a p value<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Overall, 223 120 participants were involved in this study (figure 1). The mean age of participants was 54.23±7.96 years, and 57.6% were women. At baseline, 28 325 (12.2%), 22 638 (9.7%), 171 448 (73.5%) and 709 (0.3%) participants were in stage 0, 1, 2 and 3 of CKM syndrome, respectively. During a median follow-up of 13.4 years, 1356 macular degeneration cases, 1874 glaucoma cases, 8407 cataract cases, 171 DR cases and 804 RD cases were documented. Online supplemental table S5 illustrates the distribution of participants’ characteristics according to stages of CKM syndrome. Participants in the later stages were older, comprised a higher proportion of males, had lower levels of education, were more likely to be current smokers, and their eGDR values were lower as well.

Association between eGDR and ocular diseases

We used three Cox models to assess the relationships between eGDR and ocular diseases. As shown in table 1, after full adjustment in Model 3, each 1-unit increase in eGDR was significantly associated with a decreased risk of macular degeneration (HR 0.92, 95% CI 0.87 to 0.97, p=0.001), glaucoma (HR 0.91, 95% CI 0.87 to 0.95, p<0.001), cataract (HR 0.95, 95% CI 0.93 to 0.97, p<0.001) and RD (HR 0.89, 95% CI 0.83 to 0.96, p=0.002). However, no significant linear association was observed for DR (HR 0.90, 95% CI 0.80 to 1.01, p=0.078).

After analysing eGDR by quartile, we found that the risk of DR was significantly lower in Q3 (HR 0.44, 95% CI 0.21 to 0.90, p=0.024) and Q4 (HR 0.15, 95% CI 0.04 to 0.52, p=0.003) than in Q1. There was also a significant trend across quartiles (P for trend <0.001). In terms of RD, Q2 (HR 0.81, 95% CI 0.66 to 0.99, p=0.044), Q3 (HR 0.72, 95% CI 0.56 to 0.92, p=0.009) and Q4 (HR 0.65, 95% CI 0.47 to 0.89, p=0.007) had a reduced risk compared with Q1. There was a significant trend across quartiles (P for trend=0.005). However, no significant associations were observed for macular degeneration, glaucoma or cataract in Model 3, and trends between quartiles were not significant.

Stratified effects by CKM stages: early versus advanced stages

We further stratified the cohort by CKM stages to explore how the link between eGDR and ocular diseases varies across these stages (online supplemental table S6). In late CKM stages, a more significant relationship between eGDR and DR was observed in the third (HR 0.48, 95% CI 0.23 to 0.99, p=0.048) and fourth (HR 0.14, 95% CI 0.03 to 0.64, p=0.011) quartiles. No significant association was seen in early CKM stages.

For RD, the late CKM stages demonstrated a more significant protective effect than the overall cohort. The unit correlation was significant (HR 0.90, 95% CI 0.84 to 0.97, p=0.004), as was the reduction in risk in quartiles two through four (HR 0.79–0.61, all p<0.05), respectively. Again, a trend test was also significant (P for trend=0.004). There was no significant effect in the early stages.

For macular degeneration, glaucoma and cataract, the same significant unit correlation with the overall cohort was observed in advanced stages. However, no signals based on quartiles or trends were found.

Non-linear associations

We further evaluated the potential non-linear relationship between eGDR and ocular disease within CKM stages 2–3 in the fully adjusted model using RCS curves (figure 2). Macular degeneration, glaucoma and RD were linearly associated with eGDR (P non-linear=0.105, 0.091 and 0.169, respectively). Cataract showed a significant U-shaped non-linear association with eGDR (P non-linear=0.001), whereas DR showed a significant L-shaped non-linear association with eGDR (P non-linear=0.018).

Subgroup and sensitivity analysis

We further conducted subgroup analyses to evaluate the effect of eGDR on ocular diseases among different demographic and lifestyle groups (figure 3). For cataract, significant interaction effects were found in the age (P for interaction=0.001), sex (P for interaction=0.012) and smoking status (P for interaction=0.033) subgroups, suggesting that the relationship between eGDR and cataract risk was stronger among young people, men and smokers. Similarly, in younger participants, higher eGDR was associated with a greater reduction in glaucoma risk (P for interaction=0.008). In cases of RD, race was found to significantly modify the relationship between eGDR and RD risk (P for interaction=0.006), with the strongest associations observed in black populations. No other significant interactions were found.

We conducted sensitivity analyses (online supplemental tsble S7–S9), either excluding participants with less than 2 years of follow-up time, imputing missing values for all covariates, or using Fine-Gray competing risk models with death as a competing event. The results were generally consistent with the primary analysis.

Discussion

In this prospective cohort study of 223 120 participants from the UK Biobank, we evaluated for the first time the effects of eGDR on ocular diseases in patients with CKM syndrome at stages 0–3. It was found that macular degeneration, glaucoma and cataracts were linearly associated with eGDR. However, eGDR quartile stratification was able to distinguish risk specifically for DR and RD, particularly in CKM stages 2–3. The association was L-shaped for DR and U-shaped for cataract.

Previous studies have demonstrated a significant correlation between eGDR and the likelihood of developing retinopathy. A systematic review and meta-analysis of DR showed that lower eGDR values were significantly associated with a higher risk of DR. Specifically, the risk of DR decreased by 21% for every 1-unit increase in eGDR (OR 0.79, 95% CI 0.73 to 0.85).22 Additionally, another study found a significant negative correlation between eGDR and DR severity, suggesting that eGDR could be an important indicator for early detection.23 In patients with diabetes, eGDR has been linked to an increased risk of diabetic macular oedema and AMD. One study suggests that good glycaemic control was associated with reduced AMD and DR severity.24 The diabetes control and complications trial cohort demonstrated that lower eGDR at baseline predicted a higher risk of retinopathy and nephropathy in type 1 diabetes (T1D).25 Chillarón et al confirmed this finding, showing that patients with T1D and microvascular complications had significantly lower eGDR.26 Furthermore, Bhagadurshah et al demonstrated that patients with T1D and an eGDR below 4 mg×kg⁻¹×min⁻¹ were 14 times more likely to develop retinopathy than those with an eGDR above 8.27 These findings collectively support our observations.

Our study is the first to apply eGDR metrics to the assessment of five ocular diseases, with targeted analyses focused on the CKM syndrome population. We established novel linear associations with macular degeneration, glaucoma and cataract in patients with CKM syndrome. The associations between eGDR and age-related ocular diseases may be explained by the systemic pathophysiological processes of IR. A lower eGDR, reflecting greater IR, is associated with oxidative stress, inflammation and microvascular dysfunction, which collectively contribute to neuronal injury, particularly in individuals with diabetes or impaired glucose metabolism.2228,30 These mechanisms might underpin the observed associations: for instance, IR-related endothelial dysfunction could disrupt the blood-retinal barrier, influencing the progression of DR and macular degeneration31; similarly, impaired ocular blood flow and oxidative stress may contribute to retinal ganglion cell loss in glaucoma.32,34 The link with RD could involve that IR might compromise retinal pigment epithelium integrity and photoreceptor function through oxidative stress and inflammation, potentially contributing to structural instability that could predispose to RD under additional mechanical or pathological stressors.5 At the same time, DR and RD are strongly associated with each other in the progression of retinopathy. As DR worsens, the retina’s structural integrity is severely compromised, increasing the risk of RD and exacerbating the pathophysiological process of retinopathy. This leads to more severe visual impairment.35,37

The observed U-shaped association between eGDR and cataract risk presents a novel finding. The elevated risk associated with lower eGDR is consistent with the higher likelihood of chronic hyperglycaemia in IR individuals, which is known to induce osmotic stress and protein glycation within the lens.38 39 The high incidence of disease when eGDR is extremely high suggests that this phenomenon may be caused by metabolic instability. Specifically, in the context of aggressive glycaemic control, an exceedingly high eGDR could be a marker for episodic relative hypoglycaemia. Such hypoglycaemic episodes may induce oxidative stress and create an intracellular energy deficit in the avascular lens, which relies on ambient glucose for metabolic homeostasis.40,42 These combined stresses could potentially contribute to lens protein damage and cataract formation.43 44 The U-shaped association underscored the paramount importance of metabolic balance in preventing cataracts.

Furthermore, eGDR’s predictive power for ocular complications increases significantly in advanced CKM stages (stages 2–3), when metabolic dysregulation has significant clinical consequences. In the early stages of CKM (eg, simple obesity or hypertension), the retinal microvasculature may maintain function through compensatory mechanisms (eg, upregulation of vascular endothelial growth factor), even in the presence of subclinical insulin resistance.45 This may explain why there was no significant association between eGDR and the onset of ocular diseases in early CKM stages. Traditional risk factors (eg, age and blood pressure) remain dominant at this time.46 This result is also similar to the findings of previous studies. Man et al observed that lower eGFR levels were associated with the presence and severity of DR.47 Ryan Eyn Kidd Man et al found that CKM was significantly associated with moderate and severe DR.48 Unlike patients with diabetes mellitus alone, those with CKM stages 2–3 have accelerated retinal microvascular basement membrane thickening due to endothelial dysfunction and chronic inflammation. This combination makes the metabolic threshold effect more pronounced.

This study has some major strengths. To our knowledge, this is the first investigation of the association of eGDR with five ocular diseases in CKM syndrome. We provide robust prospective evidence from a substantial cohort of 233 120 participants, employing both linear and non-linear models and revealing disease-specific non-linearity. The large sample size and long follow-up period of the UK Biobank allowed us to perform the stratified analyses with sufficient statistical power, and we observed consistent results in most subpopulations. This study enriches the theoretical knowledge of the relationship between CKM syndrome and eye diseases, clarifies the bridging role of eGDR, further improves the understanding of metabolic disorders affecting eye health, and provides the basis for subsequent in-depth research on the occurrence and development of ocular complications in CKM syndrome. Our findings could help clinicians identify vulnerable populations at increased risk for ocular diseases based on eGDR thresholds at different stages of CKM syndrome.

However, there are limitations worth considering. First, the nature of an observational study makes it difficult to establish a causal relationship, even if the results of the sensitivity analyses were consistent. Second, while this study adjusted for various potential confounders, unmeasured or residual confounding factors may still exist. Third, the UK Biobank’s community-based recruitment process, which tends to enrol healthier, more affluent participants who are predominantly of European descent, may limit the generalisability of the results to the global population. Fourth, outcome ascertainment based on hospital and primary care records may have missed milder, undiagnosed cases of ocular diseases. This non-differential misclassification would likely bias the observed associations toward the null, suggesting our HRs are conservative estimates.

Conclusion

In conclusion, elevated eGDR is linearly associated with lower risks of macular degeneration, glaucoma and RD, exhibits a U-shaped relationship with cataracts and an L-shaped association with DR across CKM syndrome stages 0–3. These associations are particularly significant in advanced CKM stages, highlighting the clinical utility of eGDR as a potential biomarker for identifying individuals at elevated risk of ocular complications.
