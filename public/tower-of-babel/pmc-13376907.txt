Trunk-branch-inspired carbon fiber scaffolds with boron nitride network for heat dissipation and electromagnetic interference shielding
Authors: Ning Jia, Yuan Ji, Wei Wang, Shichang Wang, Hong Wu, Shaoyun Guo, Jianhui Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mesophase pitch-based carbon fibers (CFs) with ultrahigh axial thermal conductivity can significantly enhance through-plane heat transfer in thermal interface materials by vertical alignment, yet raise short-circuiting and electromagnetic interference (EMI) risks. Although incorporating insulating fillers or coating CFs can suppress electron migration, it often compromises thermal performance. Herein, we engineer multi-functionally trunk-branch hierarchical heterostructures to address above limitations. Vertically aligned trunk-like CF scaffolds ensure superior through-plane heat transfer. Branch-like boron nitride (BN) networks optimize impedance matching by enhancing electrical insulation and consequently improve electromagnetic wave (EMW) absorption. Meanwhile, in situ BN networks interconnect CF scaffolds, extending bidirectional thermally conductive and EMW propagation paths. At only 20.17 vol% filler, the composite exhibits a through-plane thermal conductivity of 57.96 W·m−1·K−1 (specific thermal conductivity enhancement of 1431.62 %·(vol%)−1) and an in-plane thermal conductivity of 2.93 W·m−1·K−1, together with excellent electrical insulation and absorption-dominated EMI shielding, addressing the challenge of thermal-electrical-electromagnetic coupling for next-generation electronics.

Introduction

The high-level integration and escalating power density in advanced electronics have led to a dramatic rise in heat flux density, rendering traditional thermal interface materials (TIMs) inadequate for efficient heat dissipation1–4. Among various thermally conductive fillers, carbon-based materials with one-dimensional (1D) or two- dimensional (2D) structures have garnered significant attention owing to their ultrahigh thermal conductivity in the axial or in-plane directions5–7. In particular, mesophase pitch-based carbon fiber (MPCF, denoted as CF hereafter), which exhibits axial thermal conductivity as high as 1200 W·m−1·K−1 due to its highly ordered graphitic lamellae and aligned crystalline structure, is ideally suited for constructing highly efficient heat transfer networks in TIMs8,9. Vertical alignment of CFs through external fields10–12 or template strategies can effectively leverage their anisotropic thermal conductivity, enabling the preparation of high-performance TIMs with low filler content. For example, Zhang et al.13 employed a 3D printing method to fabricate horizontally oriented CF/polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) composites, which were subsequently cured and vertically sliced. The resulting composite achieved a through-plane thermal conductivity of 35.22 W·m−1·K−1 at 60 wt% filler content. Uetani et al.14 utilized the electrically conductive properties of CFs to achieve vertical alignment under a strong electric field, and the resulting composite obtained a through-plane thermal conductivity of 23.3 W·m−1·K−1 at a filler content of 13.2 wt%. It is worth noting that vertically aligned CFs enable phonon transfer in TIMs with high efficiency, but the densely packed architecture with high electrical conductivity simultaneously introduces critical electrical hazards and intensive electromagnetic interference (EMI, including external and secondary interference) risks in high-frequency electronics. The above issues threaten challenges of device reliability and service life for CF-based TIMs, particularly in next-generation electronics requiring multifunctional integration, such as high-speed copper cables and connector housings15–17.

To overcome the electrical insulation limitations and consequently suppress secondary electromagnetic wave (EMW) reflection in CF-based TIMs, most studies have embedded thermally conductive and electrically insulating fillers (e.g., boron nitride (BN), aluminum oxide (Al2O3), and silicon carbide (SiC)) or encapsulated CFs with insulating coatings. For instance, Hu et al.18 achieved optimized electrical insulation (3.4 × 1010 Ω·cm) of TIMs by depositing the SiC ceramic layer on the surface of CFs, albeit at the cost of reducing thermal conductivity to 12.8 W·m−1·K−1. Ji et al.19 constructed a vertically aligned h-BN/CF network via multistage stretching extrusion, layer-by-layer stacking, and hot-pressing, simultaneously achieving a through-plane thermal conductivity (9.5 W·m−1·K−1) and high electrical insulation (1.89 × 1013 Ω·cm) with only 5 phr CF content. However, although the aforementioned approaches have successfully achieved significant improvements in electrical insulation by effectively impeding electron migration, they inevitably increased phonon scattering and interfacial thermal resistance, ultimately limiting the enhancement of thermal conductivity. Compounding this issue, the high aspect ratio advantage of CFs has not yet been fully utilized owing to their insufficient orientation, thereby significantly limiting the heat conduction capability of the composite system. Therefore, constructing multi-functionally integrated hierarchical heterostructures with dense and highly oriented CF networks that concurrently enable efficient through-plane phonon transport while meeting electrical insulation and absorption-dominated EMI shielding requirements remains a challenge.

Recent advances highlight that constructing hierarchical heterostructures can effectively establish optimized phonon transport pathways and integrate multiple functionalities by organizing components across multiple scales. For instance, Gao et al.20 fabricated a hierarchically heterostructured graphene paper via size-dependent assembly, in which small sheets formed vertical thermal pathways between horizontal layers stacked by larger sheets, yielding a through-plane thermal conductivity of 12.6 W·m−1·K−1. Liu et al.21 engineered a hierarchical heterostructure by decorating CFs with Fe3O4@PDA and macroscopically aligning them. The microscopic heterostructure enhanced microwave absorption through interfacial polarization, while the macroscopic CF network provided efficient thermal pathways. Similarly, Zhang et al.22 fabricated a hierarchical heterostructure with controllable Ti3C2Tx hollow microspheres sandwiched between supporting layers, achieving an EMI shielding effectiveness of 85 dB while delivering a thermal conductivity of 3.49 W·m−1·K−1, underscoring the effectiveness of structural design for concurrent thermal management and EMI attenuation. Inspired by natural systems, researchers have further leveraged biomimetic designs to achieve advanced performance. Ding et al.23 designed a bamboo-leaves-like hierarchical heterostructure, where vertically aligned CFs served as primary through-plane thermal channels (analogous to stems) and horizontal Al2O3 plates acted as thermal spreaders interconnecting adjacent fibers (analogous to leaves). This configuration significantly enhanced the through-plane thermal conductivity to 6.47 W·m−1·K−1 at a total filler loading of 16 vol%, surpassing composites containing only vertical fibers. Nevertheless, such existing hierarchical heterostructures still face inherent trade-offs: insufficient vertical continuity limits through-plane thermal conductivity, while poor horizontal interconnection restricts heat spreading.

In natural forests, tree trunks serve as continuous, high-efficiency vertical conduits for long-distance fluid transport, while branching networks ensure widespread distribution and functional collaboration, offering a powerful biomimetic blueprint for thermal management. Here, inspired by this highly interconnected and multifunctional topology, we designed a trunk-branch hierarchical heterostructure to simultaneously address the conflicting demands of heat dissipation, electrical insulation, and absorption-dominated EMI shielding. Vertically aligned and tightly stacked CF scaffolds were directly constructed as trunk-like through-plane heat transfer pathways via a cost-effective magnetic field-assisted alignment method. In parallel, branch-like BN networks were formed via bioinspired in situ growth and act as inter-fiber junctions rather than a passive insulating coating or a conventional secondary filler. Beyond a morphological analogy, the trunk-branch design is enabled by a controllable assembly-growth process that decouples electrical conduction from heat transfer, thereby overcoming the aforementioned limitations. The resulting composites (with only 20.17 vol% filler content) achieved optimized multifunctionality, combining high through-plane thermal conductivity (57.96 W·m−1·K−1) with enhanced in-plane thermal conductivity (2.93 W·m−1·K−1), excellent electrical insulation (3.24 × 108 Ω·m) and absorption-dominated EMI shielding (44.79 dB, A = 0.88) in the X-band. The high specific thermal conductivity enhancement (specific TCE = 1431.62 %·(vol%)−1) underscored the heat transfer efficiency gain enabled by the trunk‑branch-inspired topology under coupled thermal-electrical-electromagnetic constraints. This work establishes an application-driven design paradigm for the development of multifunctional TIMs based on structural assembly and functional integration.

The numerator (λcomposite-λPDMS) represents the absolute increase in thermal conductivity relative to the matrix. Normalizing by λPDMS yields a dimensionless relative enhancement, and further dividing by Vfiller quantifies the enhancement per unit filler volume fraction. Therefore, specific TCE is a normalized metric that captures the thermal conductivity enhancement gain per unit filler volume fraction, rather than a simple comparison of absolute thermal conductivity values. For instance, the filler volume fractions of 1BN-IVCS/PDMS and 4BN-IVCS/PDMS are 25.39 vol% and 20.17 vol%, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S6 shows the TG curves and calculated volume fractions). The specific TCE exhibits a significant enhancement from 1222.67 %·(vol%)−1 to 1431.62 %·(vol%)−1 upon BN incorporation, attributable to optimized heat transfer pathways (see comparison of thermal conductivity vs. filler content in Supplementary Fig. S7 and Supplementary Table S4). Figure 3e presents the specific TCE versus the through-plane thermal conductivity of our composites alongside corresponding data from prior studies, highlighting its competitive heat transfer performance (detailed in Supplementary Table S5). At low BN precursor content, the BN insulating phase disrupts the packing density of the vertically aligned CF scaffold, which reduces the continuity of the through-thickness heat transfer pathways and consequently lowers the through-plane thermal conductivity. Higher BN content facilitates continuously bidirectional heat transfer channels through the bridging effect, thereby enhancing overall heat transfer efficiency. Figure 3c demonstrates that all composites maintain good thermally conductive stability, with only marginal reduction across the 25–100 °C range. The slight decrease can be attributed to the thermal expansion of PDMS, which creates micro-gaps at the filler/PDMS interfaces, moderately impeding heat conduction. Despite this, the BN-IVCS/PDMS retains effective heat transfer, with 4BN-IVCS/PDMS maintaining a remarkable performance above 50 W·m−1·K−1 at 100 °C35,36. In particular, while exhibiting excellent through-plane conductivity, VCS/PDMS shows limited in-plane performance (1.54 W·m−1·K−1) due to point-contact between CFs along the in-plane direction with minimal overlap areas, which hinders effective phonon transfer. The incorporation of BN addresses this limitation by facilitating the formation of insulating layer on the CF surface and bridging adjacent CFs, thereby creating additional thermally conductive channels and increasing the in-plane thermal conductivity of 4BN-IVCS/PDMS to 2.93 W·m−1·K−1 (Fig. 3d). Taken together, these efficiency metrics indicate that the key advantage of the proposed trunk-branch hierarchical heterostructure lies not in maximizing a single absolute through-plane thermal conductivity, but in enabling more effective heat transfer and redistribution per unit filler, which is further evaluated in the subsequent FEM analysis.

To further demonstrate the advantages of the vertically aligned CF scaffolds in improving through-plane thermal conductivity, surface temperature changes of the composites on a hot stage (60 °C) were monitored using an infrared thermography camera, as shown in Fig. 3f, g. The infrared thermal photographs and temperature profiles reveal that all BN-IVCS/PDMS achieve thermal equilibrium within 20 s. Crucially, the maximum temperature difference between VCS/PDMS and insulating 4BN-IVCS/PDMS at equilibrium is merely 2.1 °C, confirming that BN modification preserves the inherent vertical heat transfer capability of CF scaffolds. Furthermore, the cooling performance of resulting samples under practical conditions was evaluated by sandwiching the test samples between a 15 W ceramic heater (20.0 × 20.0 × 1.0 mm3) and a heat sink (Fig. 3i). Real-time surface temperatures were recorded via the infrared thermography camera to assess thermal management performance through heating/cooling rates and equilibrium temperatures (Fig. 3h). The BN-IVCS/PDMS samples exhibit significantly different heat dissipation capabilities, which are directly correlated with their through-plane thermal conductivities (Fig. 3j, k). For example, PDMS exhibits the poorest cooling performance, reaching an equilibrium temperature of 85.07 °C due to its low thermal conductivity, severely restricting heat transfer to the heat sink. In contrast, BN-IVCS/PDMS series demonstrates excellent cooling efficiency, with 4BN-IVCS/PDMS achieving a maximum surface temperature of only 63.94 °C. This inverse relationship between equilibrium temperature and thermal conductivity underscores the critical role of through-plane heat transfer efficiency in TIM performance.

To further demonstrate the significant advantages of vertically oriented CF scaffolds in constructing continuous, high-efficiency thermally conductive pathways along the thickness direction, and to evaluate the role of BN incorporation in the overall heat transfer effect of the composites, we employed the finite element method (FEM) simulation to characterize the transient thermal responses of different composite architectures. Two idealized heat transfer models were derived from cross-sectional SEM analysis (Fig. 1b−f), and the parameters of FEM simulation are presented in the Supplementary Table S3. To separate the effect of different experimental filler volume fractions from that of architecture configurations, the FEM models were defined with the same total filler volume fraction, thereby isolating the contribution of the BN inter-fiber bridging effect. In particular, Model I represents a CF scaffold with a relatively uniform BN encapsulation layer on the CF surfaces (corresponding to 1BN-IVCS/PDMS), whereas Model II represents the trunk-branch hierarchical heterostructure in which BN is present both on the CF surfaces and within interstitial regions to form bridging pathways (corresponding to 4BN-IVCS/PDMS). The FEM results show that Model II provides more effective thermal management performance, evidenced by improved heat spreading and reduced hotspot accumulation. Under through-plane heating with a bottom heat source (Fig. 4f–h), both models exhibit rapid through-plane heat transfer along the vertically aligned CF trunks, confirming that the CF scaffold serves as the primary efficient thermal pathway in the PDMS matrix. The top-surface average temperature increases from 31.84 °C (Model I) to 33.93 °C (Model II), which indicates more effective through-thickness heat transfer via the BN inter-fiber bridging effect. Under in-plane heating with a left-side heat source (Fig. 4i–k), Model II exhibits accelerated lateral heat propagation and a reduced temperature gradient. Correspondingly, the average temperature at the right boundary increases from 25.05 °C (Model I) to 30.63 °C (Model II), reflecting enhanced heat spreading and improved temperature uniformity. Collectively, FEM couples with NEMD results suggest that the interstitial BN bridging network enhances bidirectional heat transport, provides a mechanistic basis for the experimentally observed combination of lower through-plane thermal conductivity yet higher specific TCE in 4BN-IVCS/PDMS.

Figure 4l illustrates the heat transfer mechanism in the BN-IVCS/PDMS, demonstrating high heat conduction efficiency through its multi-functionally integrated hierarchical heterostructures. In the through-plane direction, the densely packed CF scaffolds establish unimpeded phonon transport channels, enabling ultrahigh through-plane thermal conductivity. In the in-plane direction, the introduction of BN performs dual roles, forming electrically insulating layers on the CF surfaces and creating interconnected thermal bridges between adjacent fibers. As BN content increases, the secondary BN insulating network forms increasingly dense connections to the CF scaffolds, significantly enhancing the bidirectional heat transfer efficiency of the composites.

Electromagnetic interference shielding performance of VCS/PDMS and BN-IVCS/PDMS

As demonstrated in Fig. 5a–d, the total EMI SET exhibits a non-monotonic trend, which initially decreases from 50.81 dB (VCS/PDMS) to approximately 40.26 dB (2BN-IVCS/PDMS) and then partially recovers to around 44.79 dB (4BN-IVCS/PDMS). This evolution is consistent with the observed through-plane thermal conductivity. Specifically, the initial decrease in SET is dictated by the reduction in CF trunk compactness, while its subsequent recovery at higher BN loadings is attenuated by the compensating influence of BN inter-fiber bridging effect, which enhances interfacial polarization, multi-reflection, and scattering, and extends EMW waves propagation paths. Additionally, the inherent insulating characteristics of BN boost dielectric loss in the resulting composites, resulting in a significant increase in SEA and consequently improving overall shielding performance. Remarkably, all BN-IVCS/PDMS composites achieve SET values exceeding 40 dB (99.99% EMI shielding efficiency) while maintaining SER below 3 dB, demonstrating that the BN insulating network effectively optimizes the impedance matching of BN-IVCS/PDMS37–39. Figure 5e reveals an absorption-dominated EMI shielding mechanism, evidenced by the mean value of A coefficients consistently surpassing R coefficients. For instance, 4BN-IVCS/PDMS achieves an A coefficient of 0.88 versus an R coefficient of 0.12, attributable to synergistic multiscale mechanisms including conductive loss, multiple reflections and scattering, dipole polarization, and interfacial polarization40. First, the continuous, densely packed CF scaffolds establish highly electrically conductive pathways that facilitate charge carrier migration and conductive loss. Second, the hierarchically heterogeneous interfaces between CFs and BN create strong interfacial and dipole polarization under alternating electromagnetic fields, dramatically improving EMW absorption efficiency21. Third, the multi-functionally integrated hierarchical heterostructures, featuring the trunk-like CF scaffolds integrated with the branch-like BN insulating networks, construct tortuous propagation paths. These paths enhance multi-reflection and scattering of EM waves, resulting in further dissipation of EMW energy41,42. The synergistic effect not only optimizes the impedance matching to minimize surface EMW reflection but also realizes an effective absorption-dominated EMI shielding mechanism, as illustrated in Fig. 5g. The BN-IVCS/PDMS series demonstrates a shift from single-function optimization to a system-optimized performance outcome that prioritizes efficiency and moves beyond mere multifunctionality by overcoming the conventional trade-off. The 4BN-IVCS/PDMS exhibits an ultrahigh through-plane thermal conductivity of 57.96 W·m−1·K−1 alongside optimized electrical insulation properties (3.24 × 108 Ω·cm). Additionally, the composite delivers a SET of 44.79 dB while maintaining a high A coefficient of 0.88 (Fig. 5f). The thermal resistance and mechanical properties, as well as the evolution of heat transfer and EMI shielding metrics of VCS/PDMS and BN-IVCS/PDMS, are presented in Supplementary Figs. S8–S10. Electrical insulation and electrical breakdown performance of VCS/PDMS and BN-IVCS/PDMS

The electrical insulation properties of TIMs are crucial for modern electronic packaging applications. In this work, the in situ generated BN network in BN-IVCS/PDMS series simultaneously enhances both electrical insulation and electrical breakdown performance. Second, the BN incorporation not only optimizes the electric field distribution of the composites but also reduces the phenomenon of electric field concentration, interfacial charge accumulation, and electrodeposition loss. In summary, this work effectively addressed the intrinsically conflicting requirements for thermal management, electrical insulation, and absorption-dominated EMI shielding in advanced electronics by developing a trunk-branch-inspired architecture. The resulting composite exhibited an excellent through-plane thermal conductivity of 57.96 W m−1·K−1 and enhanced in-plane thermal conductivity of 2.93 W·m−1·K−1 at only 20.17 vol% filler content, with concurrent electrical insulation (3.24 × 108 Ω·cm) and absorption-dominated EMI shielding properties (SET = 44.79 dB, A = 0.88). The high specific TCE (1431.62%·(vol%)−1) highlights the superior heat transfer efficiency enabled by the architecture design. The vertically aligned CF scaffolds acted as trunk-like primary channels for through-plane phonon transfer, whereas the branch-like BN insulating networks further interconnected the adjacent CFs to promote bidirectional heat redistribution and improve impedance matching for enhanced EMW attenuation. Crucially, BN decouples electrical conduction from heat transfer, enabling high overall heat transfer efficiency and, together with high EMW absorption loss, thereby reconciling these competing functions within a single composite. This work offers fundamental insight into designing TIMs with multifunctional networks.

Methods

Materials

PDMS (Sylgard 184) was obtained from Dow Corning Co., Ltd. MPCF (XN-100-25M) with axial thermal conductivity of 900 W·m−1·K−1 was sourced from Nippon Graphite Fiber Corporation. Resole phenolic resin (PR, Boron-modified) was purchased from Bengbu Tianyu High-Temperature Resin Materials Co., Ltd. Boric acid (BA), Urea, Ethanol (EtOH), and Methanol (MeOH) were produced by Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. All chemicals were of analytical reagent grade and used directly.

Preparation of VCS and BN-IVCS

The VCS was fabricated by dispersing CFs in a resin solution, casting the suspension into a mold, and vertically aligning the fibers using commercial NdFeB magnets (0.35 T, tesla) during ~30 s of sedimentation. The aligned fibers were then compacted by vibration, and the resulting preform was cured and carbonized to obtain the VCS46. In this work, the weight ratio of CF to PR was fixed at 15:1, and the concentration of CFs in resin solution was maintained at 9 wt%. The resole phenolic resin was dissolved in 5 g of ethanol to form a resin solution. During the evaporation process, horizontal vibration was applied at 60 Hz with 50% intensity for 2 min. Furthermore, the BN-IVCS was achieved by following the steps. Specifically, urea and BA (used as BN precursor, 2:1 w/w) were first dissolved in a mixed solvent of MeOH and EtOH (2:1 v/v) at 30 °C under continuous stirring, and then several VCS were immersed in the above solution for infiltration. After solvent evaporation, the resulting BN-IVCS preforms were subjected to high-temperature ceramization (1400 °C, 2 h) to obtain xBN-IVCS, where x referred to the ratio of the total weight of BN precursor (i.e., urea and BA) to the weight of CFs (i.e., 1:1, 2:1, 3:1, 4:1). The resulting samples containing BN precursor and CFs (1:1, 2:1, 3:1, 4:1 w/w) were labeled as 1BN-IVCS, 2BN-IVCS, 3BN-IVCS, and 4BN-IVCS. Urea and BA served as nitrogen and boron sources during the ceramization process, respectively.

Preparation of VCS/PDMS and BN-IVCS/PDMS composites

Components A and B of PDMS (30:1 w/w) were homogenized at 1000 rpm for 5 min. The uniform mixture was subsequently cast into the VCS and BN-IVCS series, followed by vacuum degassing for more than 2 h. Final curing at 80 °C for 4 h yielded VCS/PDMS and BN-IVCS/PDMS composites, with excess surface adhesive carefully removed.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA, Netzsch TG 209, UK) was carried out from 30 to 900 °C with a heating rate of 10 °C/min under nitrogen atmosphere to evaluate the filler fraction in composites. The simulation time step was set to 1 fs51–53.

Modeling of FEM

The simulations were performed under precisely controlled boundary conditions: the entire computational area of two models (0.42 × 0.25 mm2) was set to an ambient temperature of 25 °C. For the through-plane configuration, the bottom boundary was prescribed as a heat source at 50 °C to drive heat conduction upward, while the left and right boundaries were treated as thermally insulated. The top surface was subjected to convective heat transfer with a heat transfer coefficient of 10 W·m−2·K−1. For the in-plane configuration, a heat source at 50 °C was applied to the left boundary to induce lateral heat conduction, while the right boundary was set to convective heat dissipation with the same heat transfer coefficient (10 W·m−2·K−1). The top and bottom boundaries were treated as thermally insulated to ensure predominantly in-plane heat flow. Finally, the heat transfer properties were analyzed based on the temperature distributions in the models, which were simulated using COMSOL Multiphysics (version: 6.1).

Modeling of waveguide simulation and RCS analysis

Electromagnetic wave propagation in the waveguide structures was simulated using ANSYS Electronics (version: 2019). To evaluate the potential for absorption under practical conditions, the radar cross-section (RCS) of the resulting samples was calculated using the HFSS module.
