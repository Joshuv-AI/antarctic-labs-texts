Recent update on renal denervation: where do we stand now?
Authors: Jonghu Shin, Young-Won Yoon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRenal denervation (RDN) has re-emerged as a potential adjunctive therapy for hypertension following advances in device technology, trial design, and patient selection. Early enthusiasm for RDN declined after neutral findings from initial sham-controlled trials; however, subsequent randomized studies have consistently demonstrated modest but significant reductions in blood pressure (BP) across a range of clinical settings. Contemporary sham-controlled trials using radiofrequency-, ultrasound-, and alcohol-mediated approaches have shown that RDN lowers office and ambulatory BP both in the presence and absence of background antihypertensive therapy. Long-term follow-up data extending up to 3 years suggest that BP-lowering effects can be sustained, with no consistent signal of late-emerging safety concerns. Recent meta-analyses further support these findings, reporting modest but consistent reductions in systolic BP compared with sham procedures. Despite these advances, several limitations remain. The magnitude of BP reduction is modest, treatment response is heterogeneous, and definitive evidence for cardiovascular outcome benefit is lacking. Current international guidelines therefore position RDN as an adjunctive, rather than replacement, therapy for carefully selected patients with uncontrolled or resistant hypertension. This review summarizes the mechanistic rationale, key clinical trial evidence, long-term durability, safety considerations, and current guideline recommendations for RDN, and discusses future directions to better define its role in hypertension management.Keywords: Renal denervation, Hypertension, Resistant hypertension, Device-based blood pressure lowering

BACKGROUND

Hypertension remains one of the leading modifiable risk factors for cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, affecting approximately 1 in 3 adults worldwide, with an estimated 1.6 billion individuals projected to have hypertension by 2025 [1]. In Korea, approximately 30% of adults—corresponding to about 13 million individuals—have hypertension [2]. Despite the availability of multiple classes of antihypertensive agents, a substantial proportion of patients fail to achieve adequate blood pressure (BP) control in routine clinical practice. Factors such as poor medication adherence, therapeutic inertia, and complex underlying pathophysiology continue to limit the effectiveness of pharmacological treatment alone [3].

Among patients with uncontrolled BP, resistant hypertension (RH) represents a particularly challenging subgroup. RH is generally defined as BP that remains above target despite the use of three or more antihypertensive agents of different classes, including a diuretic, at optimal or maximally tolerated doses [3,4]. Patients with RH are associated with a higher cardiovascular risk burden and often demonstrate increased sympathetic nervous system activity, which may partly explain the limited response to pharmacological escalation alone [3].

In this context, renal denervation (RDN) has re-emerged as a potential non-pharmacological treatment option for hypertension. Early enthusiasm for RDN waned after neutral results from the first sham-controlled trial (SYMPLICITY HTN-3 trial) [5]; however, subsequent studies incorporating improved trial design, refined procedural techniques, and better patient selection have consistently demonstrated significant reductions in BP. These findings have led to renewed interest in RDN, which is now acknowledged in recent guidelines and scientific statements published between 2023 and 2025 by major cardiovascular societies, including the European Society of Hypertension (ESH), the European Society of Cardiology (ESC), and the American Heart Association (AHA), as a potential adjunctive therapy in selected patients [6,7,8].

Given these developments, it is timely to reassess the current status of RDN in the management of hypertension. This review provides a brief update on the mechanistic rationale, contemporary clinical evidence, and practical considerations for RDN, with a focus on clarifying where this therapy currently stands.

MECHANISTIC RATIONALE FOR RDN

Hypertension is a multifactorial disorder; however, a substantial body of experimental and clinical evidence supports a central role for sympathetic nervous system overactivity in its development and maintenance [9]. Alterations in sympathetic activity are consistently observed in patients with hypertension and are associated with disease severity, resistance to pharmacological therapy, and increased cardiovascular risk [9,10]. The kidney represents an effector organ within this neurohumoral network, serving as both a target and an amplifier of sympathetic signaling [11].

The renal sympathetic nervous system is anatomically organized as a perivascular neural network that courses along the renal arteries and provides innervation to renal structures, including the renal vasculature, tubular system, and juxtaglomerular apparatus [12]. This network is composed predominantly of sympathetic fibers and includes both efferent and afferent pathways that travel in close proximity to the renal arterial wall and extend into the renal parenchyma. Through this anatomical arrangement, neural signals transmitted along the renal arteries exert direct effects on renal hemodynamics, sodium handling, renin release, and central autonomic regulation [13,14].

Activation of efferent renal sympathetic nerves promotes several pro-hypertensive mechanisms. Increased sympathetic tone induces renal arteriolar vasoconstriction, resulting in reduced renal blood flow and increased renal vascular resistance. In addition, stimulation of β1-adrenergic receptors in the juxtaglomerular apparatus enhances renin secretion, leading to activation of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system. Sympathetic signaling also augments sodium and water reabsorption through direct effects on renal tubular transporters, contributing to intravascular volume expansion and sustained elevation of arterial pressure [11,13,15,16].

In parallel, renal afferent nerves play a role in modulating central sympathetic outflow. Sensory signals arising from the kidney are transmitted to hypothalamic and brainstem autonomic centers, where they can amplify global sympathetic activity. Enhanced afferent signaling has been shown to increase systemic vascular resistance and promote neurohumoral activation, further exacerbating hypertension [14]. Experimental studies demonstrate that interruption of renal afferent input attenuates central sympathetic excitation, suggesting the bidirectional contribution of renal nerves to BP regulation [17,18]. These integrated effects of efferent and afferent nerves provide a physiological explanation for the persistence of hypertension in some patients despite multidrug antihypertensive therapy.

RDN was developed on the basis of this mechanistic framework, with the aim of disrupting both efferent and afferent renal sympathetic signaling [11,17,19]. By targeting neural pathways that influence renin release, sodium handling, renal vascular tone, and central sympathetic drive, RDN offers a biologically plausible, non-pharmacological approach to BP reduction [9,16,19]. The relative contribution of these mechanisms is likely to vary among individuals depending on baseline sympathetic activity, comorbid conditions, and neurohumoral phenotype, which may partly explain the heterogeneity of BP responses observed across clinical studies.

CONTEMPORARY EVIDENCE FROM SHAM-CONTROLLED TRIALS

The modern clinical evaluation of RDN began with SYMPLICITY HTN-3, the first large, randomized, sham-controlled trial. However, this study did not demonstrate a significant reduction in BP compared with the sham procedure, which tempered early enthusiasm for the therapy [5]. These neutral findings were attributed to several methodological challenges, including variable medication adherence, limited monitoring of background antihypertensive therapy, operator inexperience, and suboptimal performance of first-generation radiofrequency (RF) catheters, all of which may have diluted the true treatment effect [5].

Subsequent sham-controlled trials [20,21,22,23,24,25,26,27,28] were designed to address these limitations through refined patient selection, improved blinding, stricter control of antihypertensive medication use, and standardized procedural techniques. Collectively, these studies provide a more consistent assessment of the antihypertensive efficacy of RDN across different clinical settings (Table 1). In particular, the SPYRAL and RADIANCE trial programs re-evaluated RDN using contemporary device platforms and rigorous trial designs, with ambulatory BP monitoring adopted as the primary endpoint in most studies [20,21,22,23,24,25,26].

The SPYRAL HTN-OFF MED trial demonstrated that RF-based RDN significantly reduced 24-hour ambulatory systolic blood pressure (SBP) at 3 months compared with sham in patients not receiving antihypertensive medications, confirming that BP lowering could be achieved independent of pharmacological therapy [22]. In contrast, the SPYRAL HTN-ON MED trial extended these findings to patients receiving stable background antihypertensive treatment, showing additional reductions in ambulatory and office SBP, although the between-group difference in ambulatory BP did not reach statistical significance at the primary endpoint. Notably, significant reductions in office SBP were observed, underscoring the additive effect of RDN in medicated patients [21,25].

Parallel evidence emerged from the ultrasound (US)-based RADIANCE trial program. RADIANCE-HTN SOLO and RADIANCE II evaluated RDN in patients with mild to moderate hypertension following medication washout, demonstrating consistent and statistically significant reductions in daytime ambulatory SBP at short-term follow-up [20,24]. On the other hand, RADIANCE-HTN TRIO targeted patients with confirmed RH receiving standardized triple antihypertensive therapy, thereby minimizing variability in medication adherence. In this optimized pharmacological setting, US-guided RDN produced significant reduction in ambulatory SBP compared with sham, supporting its potential role as an adjunctive therapy even in patients with pharmacologically treated RH [23].

More recently, alternative denervation modalities have been explored. The TARGET BP OFF MED trial, which employed alcohol-mediated perivascular denervation, did not demonstrate a significant difference in ambulatory SBP between RDN and sham at early follow-up, although reductions in medication burden were observed [27]. In contrast, the TARGET BP I trial, conducted in patients receiving antihypertensive therapy, reported a modest but statistically significant reduction in 24-hour ambulatory SBP at 3 months compared with sham, suggesting that both the procedural technique and the treated population may influence treatment response [28].

Taken together, contemporary sham-controlled clinical trials indicate that RDN produces modest but consistent reductions in ambulatory and office BP when evaluated using rigorous trial designs and modern device platforms. Through these studies, BP lowering has been observed both in the presence and absence of background antihypertensive therapy, although the magnitude of effect varies according to patient population, medication status, and denervation technology. These findings collectively support the re-emergence of RDN as a potential adjunctive treatment option for selected patients with uncontrolled or RH, while also underscoring the importance of careful patient selection and optimized procedural techniques.

DURABILITY OF BP LOWERING AFTER RDN

While contemporary sham-controlled trials have demonstrated short-term BP–lowering effects of RDN, the durability of this effect is critical for its clinical relevance. Long-term follow-up data from several key studies provide insight into the persistence of BP reduction and the long-term safety profile of RDN (Table 2). In the SYMPLICITY HTN-3 trial, extended follow-up to 36 months showed significant reductions in office and 24-hour ambulatory BP after RDN, without evidence of late-emerging procedural complications. However, interpretation of these findings is limited by treatment crossover and changes in background antihypertensive therapy during follow-up [29]. In the 3-year follow-up of RADIANCE-HTN SOLO trial, US-based RDN was associated with sustained reductions in ambulatory BP at 36 months, despite the reintroduction of antihypertensive medications after the initial study period [30]. Similarly, long-term follow-up from the SPYRAL HTN-ON MED trial demonstrated that the BP-lowering effect of RDN were maintained for up to 36 months independent of concomitant antihypertensive therapy [31].

Although heterogeneity in trial design, medication management, and follow-up strategies limits direct comparisons, these studies support the durability of BP lowering after RDN and its potential role as a long-term adjunctive therapy in selected patients with hypertension.

EVIDENCE FROM META-ANALYSES

Recent meta-analyses of randomized sham-controlled trials have shown that RDN is associated with modest but statistically significant reductions in both ambulatory and office SBP [32,33,34,35]. These effects have been observed both in the presence and absence of background antihypertensive therapy and across different device platforms. MODERN TECHNIQUES OF RDN

Following the establishment of efficacy with second-generation RF and US-based RDN systems, recent studies have explored newer procedural strategies aimed at improving denervation precision, procedural consistency, and clinical response. These trials reflect a shift from purely anatomical ablation toward approaches incorporating refined device design, alternative energy delivery, and functional guidance [36,37,38].

The SMART trial is a multicenter, randomized, sham-controlled study that evaluated the efficacy and safety of mapping and selective renal denervation (msRDN). In this study, intra-arterial stimulation was used to classify ablation sites based on SBP response, with sites showing an SBP increase (≥ 5 mmHg) defined as “hot spots” and targeted for ablation, whereas sites with decreased or no BP response were avoided. Post-ablation stimulation was used to confirm effective denervation. The dual primary endpoints were BP control rate and change in antihypertensive drug burden, with antihypertensive medications actively titrated to achieve a target office SBP < 140 mmHg. At 6 months, the msRDN group achieved similar BP control rates compared with sham (95.4% vs. 92.8%) with a significantly lower antihypertensive drug burden (drug index 4.37 vs. 7.61, P = 0.003). Notably, 42.6% of patients in the msRDN group achieved BP control without intensification of antihypertensive therapy, compared with 27.8% in the sham group. This trial suggests a shift from anatomical to functional targeting of denervation, enabling a reduced number of ablations and real-time confirmation of effective denervation. However, as noted by the authors, whether msRDN increases responder rate remains unclear, as antihypertensive medications were actively titrated to achieve target BP levels, precluding assessment of the pure BP-lowering effect of msRDN. Nevertheless, this novel approach may improve procedural precision and reduce variability in treatment response, warranting further studies to confirm its clinical impact [36].

The IBERIS and NETROD trials further investigated contemporary RF-based RDN systems in Asian populations with uncontrolled hypertension. Both studies employed multielectrode catheters designed to enhance circumferential ablation coverage and procedural reproducibility. At 6 months, these trials demonstrated significant reductions in 24-hour ambulatory SBP compared with control groups (adjusted between-group difference, −9.4 [95% CI, −12.8 to −5.9]; P < 0.001 in IBERIS, −19.2 [95% CI, −23.0 to −15.5]; P < 0.001 in NETROD), with treatment effects numerically larger than those observed in earlier global sham-controlled trials. Although differences in study design and patient characteristics limit direct comparisons, these findings suggest that optimized ablation strategies and population selection may influence treatment response [37,38].

Collectively, these modern trials emphasize ongoing efforts to refine RDN technology and strategy beyond conventional anatomical approaches. While longer-term follow-up and validation in broader populations are required, emerging data indicate that advances in device design and denervation targeting may further improve the clinical performance of RDN in selected patients with hypertension.

SAFETY AND LIMITATIONS OF RDN

Randomized, sham-controlled trials have consistently demonstrated a favorable procedural safety profile of RDN, with no significant difference in overall adverse event rates compared with sham procedures [35]. Major periprocedural complications are uncommon, and pooled analyses have not identified a consistent signal of clinically relevant renal artery injury attributable to RDN [35,39]. In particular, the incidence of renal artery stenosis requiring intervention after RDN appears to be low, based on angiographic follow-up and meta-analytic data [39]. With respect to renal function, available evidence indicates that RDN does not result in clinically meaningful deterioration in estimated glomerular filtration rate during short- or intermediate-term follow-up [35]. Importantly, meta-analyses including patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) have similarly shown no adverse impact of RDN on renal function, suggesting an acceptable safety profile even in populations with impaired baseline renal reserve [40,41,42]. Long-term follow-up data extending up to 3 years from randomized trials and registries further support the absence of late-emerging safety concerns, providing reassurance regarding the durability of procedural safety after RDN [35,43].

However, based on current guidelines, RDN is generally not recommended in patients with an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 40 mL/min/1.73 m2. Recent data from the Global SYMPLICITY Registry DEFINE, which included patients across CKD stages (eGFR ≥ 60, 45–59, and 30–44 mL/min/1.73 m2), demonstrated sustained reductions in office SBP over 3 years without excess renal adverse events, supporting the safety and efficacy of RDN in patients with moderate-to-severe CKD [44]. In contrast, in patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD) undergoing dialysis, available evidence is still primarily derived from small cohorts and real-world observational studies [45,46,47,48].

Ongoing prospective trials are expected to address these evidence gaps. The RDN-CKD study (NCT04264403) is a multicenter, randomized, sham-controlled trial enrolling patients with stage 3a/b CKD and uncontrolled hypertension, with the primary endpoint of change in 24-hour ambulatory SBP at 6 months. The SPYRAL AFFIRM Global Clinical Study (NCT05198674) is a single-arm study including patients with more advanced renal dysfunction (eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2) to evaluate procedural safety and BP reduction. In addition, the RDN-HD study (NCT06556407) is a feasibility study in patients with ESRD and RH, assessing adverse events and BP reduction at 6 months. Collectively, these ongoing studies in patients with advanced CKD and ESRD are expected to provide important insights into the safety and efficacy of RDN and may help refine its role as a therapeutic option for difficult-to-control hypertension in this high-risk population.

Despite these reassuring safety findings, several limitations remain. First, the magnitude of BP reduction achieved with RDN is modest. Recent meta-analyses of sham-controlled trials demonstrate average reductions of approximately 5–8 mmHg in office SBP and 2–4 mmHg in 24-hour ambulatory SBP compared with sham procedures [32,33,34,35]. While these reductions are clinically meaningful at a population level, they are smaller than those typically achieved with optimized combination pharmacotherapy. Second, evidence for RDN is largely confined to BP endpoints. To date, no randomized trial has demonstrated a reduction in hard clinical outcomes such as cardiovascular events, renal outcomes, or mortality, limiting direct comparison with established antihypertensive therapies that have outcome-based evidence. Third, variability in treatment response remains a key challenge. Heterogeneity in BP reduction across trials and meta-analyses suggests that individual patient characteristics, including underlying sympathetic activity and hypertension phenotype, may influence responsiveness [35]. However, robust markers to guide patient selection have not yet been established. Fourth, interpretation of trial results is complicated by changes in medication adherence and background antihypertensive therapy during follow-up, particularly in medicated populations. Improvements in adherence after trial enrollment may attenuate between-group differences and obscure the isolated effect of the procedure. Finally, current RDN techniques lack a universally accepted intra-procedural indicator of denervation adequacy, and trial populations have generally excluded patients with advanced chronic kidney disease, secondary hypertension, or complex renal artery anatomy. These factors limit generalizability to broader hypertensive populations encountered in routine practice.

In summary, RDN appears to be a safe adjunctive therapy with modest BP-lowering efficacy. However, unresolved issues related to effect size, patient selection, procedural assessment, and the absence of outcome data currently constrain its role in hypertension management.

CURRENT GUIDELINES AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS: WHERE DO WE STAND NOW?

Position of RDN in current guidelines

Reflecting the accumulation of evidence from contemporary sham-controlled trials, recent international guidelines have reintroduced RDN as a therapeutic option for selected patients with hypertension. Current European and American guidelines acknowledge RDN as an adjunctive, non-pharmacological therapy for patients with uncontrolled or RH despite optimized lifestyle modification and pharmacological treatment (Table 3) [6,7,8].

The 2023 ESH guidelines recognize RDN as a potential add-on treatment in patients with resistant or uncontrolled hypertension, provided that secondary causes have been excluded and adherence to antihypertensive therapy has been confirmed (class II, level B) [6]. The 2024 ESC guidelines similarly state that RDN may be considered in selected patients with uncontrolled hypertension, emphasizing shared decision-making and performance in experienced centers (class IIb, level B) [7]. In the United States, recent scientific statements and guideline updates from the AHA acknowledge RDN as an emerging adjunctive option for BP control in patients who remain uncontrolled despite medical therapy (class IIb, level B-R) [8]. Across all guidelines, RDN is consistently positioned as complementary to pharmacological treatment rather than a replacement, and the lack of definitive evidence for cardiovascular outcome benefit is explicitly noted.

Future directions and unmet needs

Despite increasing guideline recognition, several challenges remain before RDN can be more broadly integrated into routine hypertension management. Future studies should focus on refining patient selection to better identify individuals most likely to derive clinically meaningful benefit, potentially incorporating markers of sympathetic activity or hypertension phenotype. Further standardization of procedural techniques and the development of reliable intra-procedural indicators of denervation adequacy are also needed to reduce variability in treatment response and improve reproducibility across centers. Most importantly, adequately powered randomized trials with long-term follow-up are required to determine whether BP reductions achieved with RDN translate into improvements in cardiovascular and renal outcomes. Such evidence will be critical in defining the long-term role of RDN within hypertension treatment algorithms.

CONCLUSIONS

Contemporary sham-controlled trials have demonstrated that RDN can achieve modest but consistent reductions in BP across different clinical settings, both in the presence and absence of background antihypertensive therapy, with a favorable procedural safety profile. Long-term follow-up data further suggest that these BP-lowering effects can be sustained over several years. On the basis of this accumulating evidence, recent international guidelines have cautiously reintroduced RDN as an adjunctive treatment option for selected patients with uncontrolled or RH. Importantly, RDN is uniformly positioned as complementary to pharmacological therapy rather than a replacement strategy.

Despite these advances, several limitations remain. Variability in treatment response, the absence of validated tools for patient selection, and the lack of definitive evidence for cardiovascular outcome benefit continue to constrain the broader adoption of RDN. Further refinement of procedural techniques, improved identification of suitable candidates, and outcome-driven clinical trials will be necessary to better define the role of RDN within contemporary hypertension management.
