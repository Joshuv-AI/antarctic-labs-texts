Numerical analysis of the large deviation regime of a kinetic equation with a nonlocal Hamilton-Jacobi limit
Hélène Hivert, Tino Laidin

Abstract
. We develop and study an asymptotic-preserving (AP) numerical scheme for a linear kinetic equation in a large deviation regime. After applying a Hopf-Cole transform to the distribution function, the system exhibits the behavior of rare events, which in the limit is governed by a non-standard, nonlocal Hamilton-Jacobi equation, as identified in [E. Bouin et al., J. Lond. Math. Soc., II. Ser., 2023]. The proposed scheme efficiently handles the stiffness introduced by scaling, with a computational cost that remains uniform with respect to the small parameter. It takes advantage of the conservation properties of the original kinetic model to overcome the numerical challenges posed by stiffness. The scheme satisfies a discrete maximum principle, preserves equilibrium states, and correctly captures the asymptotic limit, recovering the viscosity solution of the limit nonlocal Hamilton-Jacobi equation. As the limit problem is non-standard, convergence results from the literature are not directly applicable. We introduce new analytical tools based on a discrete representation formula that links the numerical scheme with the continuous setting. This allows us to prove the convergence and establish key structural properties of the method. Numerical tests support the analysis and illustrate the robustness of the scheme and the original behavior of the limit system. Keywords: Asymptotic preserving scheme, Kinetic equation, BGK equation, nonlocal Hamil-ton-Jacobi equation. 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification: 65M12, 35B40, 82C40, 35F21, 35D40, (Primary) 35Q92, (secondary)

Introduction

In this paper, we introduce and analyze a new Asymptotic Preserving (AP) scheme for a linear kinetic equation under a scaling tailored to capture rare events in the dynamics. Specifically, we consider the linear BGK equation for the unknown distribution function
f(t,x,v)
:

(1.1)

\,\partial_{t}\,f(t,x,v)+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f(t,x,v)=\rho(t,x)\mathcal{M}(v)-f(t,x,v),\quad(t,x,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}},

where
d_{x},d_{v}\in\mathbb{N}
denote the spatial and velocity dimensions. The macroscopic density is given by
\rho(t,x)=\langle f(t,x,v)\rangle\coloneq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}}f(t,x,v)\,\,\mathrm{d}v
, and the equilibrium distribution
\mathcal{M}
is defined as

\mathcal{M}(v)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d_{v}/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{|v|^{2}}{2}\right).

The equation is supplemented with an initial data
f_{\mathrm{in}}
, which will be specified later. The function
f(t,x,v)
represents the probability density of finding an individual at time
t
, position
x
, and moving at velocity
v
. The relaxation operator
\rho\mathcal{M}-f
models interactions that cause individuals to change velocity: they are removed according to the distribution
f
and reintroduced with a Maxwellian profile
\rho\mathcal{M}
while preserving mass. This corresponds to a stochastic process in which individuals randomly change velocity following a velocity jump process given by
\rho\mathcal{M}-f
.

In this work, we consider the rescaling, introduced in
[
12
]
:

(1.2)

\left(\frac{t}{\varepsilon},\frac{x}{\varepsilon^{3/2}},\frac{v}{\varepsilon^{1/2}}\right).

Under this scaling, equation (
1.1
) then reads

(
\mathcal{P}^{\varepsilon}
)

\,\partial_{t}\,f^{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f^{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(\rho^{\varepsilon}(t,x)\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}(v)-f^{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)\right),\quad(t,x,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}},

where
\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}
is the centered Gaussian of variance
\varepsilon
:

\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}(v)=\frac{1}{(2\varepsilon\pi)^{d_{v}/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{|v|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}\right).

Equation (
1.1
) has been extensively studied in both the hydrodynamic
[
2
,
36
]
and diffusive
[
9
,
19
]
regimes, but its analysis under the scaling (
1.2
) is a recent research subject. One motivation for considering this scaling comes from the following kinetic reaction-transport equation, where individuals also undergo a reproduction process:

(1.3)

\,\partial_{t}\,f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f=\rho\mathcal{M}-f+r\rho(\mathcal{M}-f),

where
r>0
is a reproduction rate. Equation (
1.3
) can be viewed as a kinetic analogue of the well-known Fisher-KPP equation. In
[
13
]
it was shown that (
1.3
) exhibits accelerated front propagation, with a velocity that grows like
t^{3/2}
. Due to this acceleration, the classical diffusive scaling is not appropriate to capture the motion of the fronts, which motivates the use of scaling (
1.2
).

The main challenge in analyzing the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
in (

P
ε

) lies in the interplay between the stiffness of the right-hand side and the concentration in velocity induced by the resampling through the distribution
\rho^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}
. To accurately capture the asymptotic behavior as
\varepsilon\to 0
, a typical strategy is to study the limit of the Hopf-Cole transform of the unknown. It is defined as

(1.4)

\varphi^{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)=-\varepsilon\ln(f^{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)).

This logarithmic transform has, for example, been used in
[
20
,
6
,
5
,
35
,
11
,
15
]
for the study of adaptive dynamics where concentration phenomena typically occur. Another way to interpret (
1.4
) is that it captures rare events occurring in the tails of the distribution
f^{\varepsilon}
, which becomes increasingly concentrated as
\varepsilon\to 0
. These rare events are precisely what drive the accelerating propagation fronts in (
1.3
)
[
13
]
.

The analysis of (

P
ε

) follows a line of reasoning similar to that used for the classical Fisher-KPP equation
[
25
,
4
,
17
]
, based on the so-called geometric optics approximation
[
27
,
25
]
. In
[
12
]
, a parallel is drawn with the heat equation under hyperbolic scaling, where the Hopf-Cole transform is shown to converge locally uniformly to the viscosity solution of a Hamilton-Jacobi equation (see, e.g.,
[
17
,
3
]
). In the case of (

P
ε

) the authors showed that
\varphi^{\varepsilon}
converges, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, towards a function
\varphi(t,x,v)
solution to:

(
\mathcal{P}^{0}
)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\max\left(\,\partial_{t}\,\varphi(t,x,v)+v\cdot\nabla_{x}\varphi(t,x,v)-1,\,\varphi(t,x,v)-\underset{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}}{\min}\left\{\varphi(t,x,v)\right\}-\frac{|v|^{2}}{2}\right)=0,\\
&\,\partial_{t}\,\left(\underset{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}}{\min}\left\{\varphi\right\}(t,x)\right)\leq 0,\\
&\,\partial_{t}\,\left(\underset{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}}{\min}\left\{\varphi\right\}(t,x)\right)=0\quad\text{if}\quad\underset{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}}{\mathrm{argmin}\,}\left\{\varphi(t,x,v)\right\}=\left\{0\right\},\quad(t,x,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{v}}.\end{aligned}\right.

For simplicity, the initial data of (

P
ε

) is now assumed to be of the form

f_{\mathrm{in}}^{\varepsilon}(x,v)=\exp\left(-\frac{\varphi_{\mathrm{in}}(x,v)}{\varepsilon}\right)

so that (

P
0

) is supplemented with an initial data
\varphi_{\mathrm{in}}
such that

is bounded and satisfies Lipschitz regularity. The precise assumptions are stated later. In addition, we assume that

does not depend on
\varepsilon
, but this assumption can be relaxed by assuming local uniform convergence in
\varepsilon

They are several key features to (

The limit system (

P
0

) is referred to as a nonlocal Hamilton–Jacobi equation, both by analogy with the classical use of the Hopf–Cole transform for parabolic equations and because it admits a unique viscosity solution, characterized via the method of sub- and super-solutions. It is well known that solutions to Hamilton-Jacobi equations are, at best, expected to have Lipschitz regularity. This low regularity presents additional challenges both for the analysis and from a numerical perspective.

It was shown in
[
12
]
that (

P
0

) admits a representation formula that characterizes the unique viscosity solution. The rigorous notion of viscosity solutions for (

P
0

) is also developed in that work, based on appropriate definitions of sub- and super-solutions. We refer the reader to
[
12
]
for a detailed discussion; the explicit form of the representation formula will be recalled later. For standard Hamilton-Jacobi equations, there is a duality between the Eulerian viewpoint
[
24
,
25
,
26
]
, where the dynamics are described by a PDE as in (

P
0

), and the Lagrangian perspective, which focuses on trajectories, as in a representation formula. In the latter case, minimizing trajectories are typically smooth. In contrast, the trajectories associated with the system (

P
0

) are piecewise linear in position and piecewise constant in velocity
[
12
]
. The construction of AP schemes offers a promising perspective for extending the analysis of
[
12
]
to other systems, such as neutron transport
[
23
]
, where the accurate treatment of rare events is equally critical.

The design of AP schemes for kinetic equations dates back to
[
30
,
32
]
. The key idea is to build a scheme for the
\varepsilon>0
problem that, in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
, converges towards a consistent discretization of the asymptotic model. Another fundamental feature of AP schemes is their ability to take time steps independent of the scaling parameter, avoiding a restrictive condition of the type
{\Delta t}\leq\varepsilon{\Delta x}
, commonly encountered in classical schemes. For an overview of such methods in the kinetic context, we refer to
[
21
,
31
]
. However, these approaches typically work directly on the distribution function and do not involve the Hopf-Cole transform (
1.4
).

In the context of biological systems, AP schemes based on logarithmic transformations have been proposed in
[
16
,
28
,
1
]
. To the best of our knowledge, the only instance of such a strategy applied to kinetic equations is found in
[
29
]
, where (
1.3
) is treated in the case of bounded velocities to study front propagation. In this work the author considered a multiplicative micro-macro decomposition that allows to treat a macroscopic part, that does not depend on velocity, and a perturbative part separately. Micro-macro schemes are typically additive
[
8
,
33
,
34
]
, but the logarithmic transform prompted the multiplicative one allowing the construction of an AP scheme for (
1.3
).

A major difference between the study in
[
29
]
and ours is the fact that they considered bounded velocities. Under this modeling assumption, it is sufficient to rescale (
1.3
) under a hyperbolic scaling
\left(\frac{t}{\varepsilon},\frac{x}{\varepsilon},v\right)
. The asymptotic limit
\varepsilon\to 0
of this problem was studied in
[
11
,
10
,
14
]
and its front propagation property showed in
[
13
]
. Because of the bounded velocities, the asymptotic limit does not depend on the velocity variable which is the typical behavior that prompted the use of micro-macro decomposition. For unbounded velocities, which is the novelty of our study compared to
[
29
]
, it is not the case and such decomposition is not relevant anymore.

The standard approach to discretize Hamilton-Jacobi equations is based on finite differences, for which robust analytical tools have been developped to study convergence towards viscosity solutions
[
18
,
37
,
7
]
. These tools were adapted in
[
29
]
, where the limiting system admits a classical Hamilton-Jacobi formulation, albeit with a Hamiltonian defined implicitly. However, in our setting, recasting (

P
0

) in Hamiltonian form remains an open problem, necessitating an alternative approach.

The goal of this paper is therefore twofold. First, we construct a numerical scheme capable of capturing the asymptotic regime
\varepsilon\to 0
in (

P
ε

) as well as the key features mentioned above. Unlike in
[
29
]
, the nature of the limit system leads us to develop a new class of AP schemes, based on the Duhamel representation of (

P
ε

) and a kind of “meso-macro” framework. In this approach, a macroscopic component evolves along the function
\varphi^{\varepsilon}
. Second, beyond the AP property, we aim to ensure that the limit scheme converges, when the discretization parameters tend to
0
, towards the viscosity solution of (

P
0

). To this end, we make use of the representation formula of (

P
0

), along with a discrete analogue, to establish convergence to the correct solution.

At a deeper level, AP schemes are not just computational tools. They can also guide analytical understanding. While they were initially introduced to efficiently handle stiffness and capture asymptotic limits, their structure can serve as a bridge to the limiting problem itself. In our case, directly discretizing the variational formulation (

P
0

) is far from straightforward. By instead designing a scheme that preserves the key features of the scaled kinetic equation (

P
ε

), we obtain, in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
, a discretization that naturally approximates the viscosity solution of (

P
0

). In addition, the derivation of a discrete representation formula for (

P
0

) not only reinforces the consistency of our scheme but also opens a path for extending this approach to other models with similar variational structures.

Construction of the numerical scheme and main results

2.1.
Construction of the numerical scheme

We now propose the derivation of an asymptotic preserving scheme for (

P
ε

). The main ideas of the discretization are as follows. First, the Duhamel formulation of (

ε

Time discretization.
Let
N_{t}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
. We set
(0,T]=\cup^{N_{t}}_{k=0}(t^{k},t^{k+1}]
with

and

(2.1)

\displaystyle f^{\varepsilon}(t^{n}+{\Delta t},x,v)

\displaystyle=f^{\varepsilon}(t^{n},x-{\Delta t}\,v,v)\,\mathrm{e}^{-{\Delta t}/\varepsilon}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\varepsilon c^{\varepsilon}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-{\Delta t}/\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{{\Delta t}}\,\mathrm{e}^{s/\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{e}^{-|v|^{2}/2\varepsilon}\rho^{\varepsilon}(t^{n}+s,x+(s-{\Delta t})v)\,\mathrm{d}s,

with

(2.2)

c^{\varepsilon}=\left\langle\exp\left(-\frac{|v|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}\right)\right\rangle.

In the spirit of
[

\varphi^{\varepsilon}
and

defined respectively as the Hopf-Cole transforms of

and

(2.3)

The semi-discrete scheme in time arises from selecting a quadrature rule to approximate the integral in (
2.1
). Due to the stiffness in
\mu^{\varepsilon}
, we adopt a right-point quadrature approximation. Let
\varphi^{\varepsilon,n}(x,v)

\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mu(t^{n}+s,x+(s-{\Delta t}v))/\varepsilon}

\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mu^{n+1}(x)/\varepsilon}
allowing what remains in the integral to be computed explicitly. One then obtains

(2.4)

\,\mathrm{e}^{-\varphi^{\varepsilon,n+1}(x,v)/\varepsilon}=\,\mathrm{e}^{-\varphi^{\varepsilon,n}(x-{\Delta t}v,v)/\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{e}^{-{\Delta t}/\varepsilon}+(1-\,\mathrm{e}^{-{\Delta t}/\varepsilon})\frac{1}{c^{\varepsilon}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{|v|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mu^{\varepsilon,n+1}(x)/\varepsilon}.

Recalling that our aim is to build an AP scheme for (

Indeed, assuming that one can formally pass to the limit

2.7
) of the limit of

(

\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\varphi\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{i_{j},j}^{n}=\alpha_{j}\varphi_{j}^{n}(x_{i_{j}})+(1-\alpha_{j})\varphi_{j}^{n}(x_{i_{j}-1})+\alpha_{j}e_{\varphi,i_{j},j}^{n}+(1-\alpha_{j})e_{\varphi,i_{j}-1,j}^{n}

Let us now show the propagation of (

). Introducing
\varphi_{j}^{n}(x_{i}-{\Delta t}v_{j})
and (

) in the update relation on
\mu_{i}^{n+1}
in (

0

\displaystyle\mu_{i}^{n+1}

\displaystyle=\underset{j\in\mathbb{Z}}{\min}\Big\{\left(\alpha_{j}\varphi_{j}^{n}(x_{i_{j}})+(1-\alpha_{j})\varphi_{j}^{n}(x_{i_{j}-1})-\varphi_{j}^{n}(x_{i}-{\Delta t}v_{j})\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\alpha_{j}e_{\varphi,i_{j},j}^{n}+(1-\alpha_{j})e_{\varphi,i_{j}-1,j}^{n}+\varphi_{j}^{n}(x_{i}-{\Delta t}v_{j})\Big\}.

\mu^{n+1}(x_{i})-2L{\Delta x}-\underset{(i,j)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}{\mathrm{sup}}\left\{|e_{\varphi,ij}^{n}|\right\}\leq\mu_{i}^{n+1}\leq\mu^{n+1}(x_{i})+2L{\Delta x}+\underset{(i,j)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}{\mathrm{sup}}\left\{|e_{\varphi,ij}^{n}|\right\},

from which one obtains (
5.15
) at step
n+1
using the induction hypothesis (

) on

. The same ideas are then applied to the equation on
\varphi_{ij}^{n+1}
in (

0

). Let

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle\coloneqq\varphi^{n}_{j}(x_{i}-{\Delta t}v_{j})+(\bar{\varphi}^{n}_{i_{j},j}-\varphi^{n}_{j}(x_{i}-{\Delta t}v_{j}))+{\Delta t},

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{v_{j}^{2}}{2}+\mu^{n+1}(x_{i})+\left(\mu_{i}^{n+1}-\mu^{n+1}(x_{i})\right),

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle\leq\varphi_{j}^{n}(x_{i}-{\Delta t}v_{j})+{\Delta t}+2L{\Delta x},

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle\geq\varphi_{j}^{n}(x_{i}-{\Delta t}v_{j})+{\Delta t}-2L{\Delta x},

(5.21)

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle\leq\frac{v_{j}^{2}}{2}+\mu^{n+1}(x_{i})+2L(n+1){\Delta x},

(5.22)

\displaystyle B

Remark 3
.

On the generalization to higher dimensions
. The generalization of Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
to higher dimensions in velocity is straightforward, as all velocity-specific tools naturally generalize to this setting. However, when considering the position variable, the monotonicity of the interpolation (
2.13
) played a crucial role in the analysis of both (

S
ε

) and (

S
0

). Therefore, the multidimensional interpolation must be carefully selected to ensure this property is preserved. On suitably structured meshes, a linear interpolation in each coordinate direction should be sufficient. Additionally, since the unknown is expected to exhibit only Lipschitz regularity, this imposes an extra constraint on the interpolation method. Finally, the commutation of the interpolation (
2.13
) with constants and translations allowed the exact preservation of Lipschitz bounds. Without these last two properties, this preservation may deteriorate over time.

6.
Numerical results

In this section, we present numerical experiments validating the properties of schemes (

S
ε

) and (

S
0

), as established in Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
. While these results were proven in an unbounded phase space, practical considerations require to restrict the computational domain.

For the spatial discretization, for the sake of simplicity, we impose periodic boundary conditions on the domain
[-x_{\star},x_{\star}]
, with a uniform grid spacing defined as

{\Delta x}=\frac{2x_{\star}}{N_{x}},\quad x_{i}=-x_{\star}+\left(i-\frac{1}{2}\right){\Delta x},\quad i\in[\![1,N_{x}]\!].

A similar discretization is applied in velocity but no boundary condition needs to be defined as there is no transport in this variable:

{\Delta v}=\frac{2v_{\star}}{N_{v}},\quad v_{j}=-v_{\star}+\left(j-\frac{1}{2}\right){\Delta v},\quad j\in[\![1,N_{v}]\!].

Unless stated otherwise, we set the discretization parameters as follows:

N_{x}=64,\quad N_{v}=61,\quad x_{\star}=10,\quad v_{\star}=10.

Note that to ensure that
v=0
belongs to the grid, we choose an odd number of points in velocity. The time step is chosen to satisfy the stability condition (
2.19
):
{\Delta t}=0.9{\Delta v}/2
. In the following, we consider the initial data

(6.1)

\varphi_{\mathrm{in}}(t,x,v)=\min\left\{3(v-3)^{2}+5,5(v+7)^{2}+2\right\}+0.9\cos\left(\frac{4\pi xv}{x_{\star}v_{\star}}\right),

which enjoys Assumption
1
, and is such that
\varphi_{\mathrm{in}}-v^{2}/2
is
L
-Lipschitz in the
v
variable. To illustrate the asymptotic-preserving property of (

S
ε

), we compare it with a standard explicit finite difference scheme on (

P
ε

). Denoting
f_{ij}^{n}
an approximation of
f(t^{n},x_{i},v_{j})
, the scheme is given by

(6.2)

\frac{f_{ij}^{n+1}-f_{ij}^{n}}{{\Delta t}}+(v\,\partial_{x}\,f^{n})_{ij}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(\rho_{i}^{n}\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}_{j}-f_{ij}^{n}),

where

(v\,\partial_{x}\,f^{n})_{ij}=v_{j}^{+}\frac{f_{ij}^{n}-f_{i-1,j}^{n}}{{\Delta x}}+v_{j}^{-}\frac{f_{i+1,j}^{n}-f_{ij}^{n}}{{\Delta x}}

is a classical upwind scheme for the transport term,
\rho_{i}^{n}=\left\langle f_{i}^{n}\right\rangle_{\Delta v}
, and
\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}_{j}=c\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}(v_{j})
with
c=\left\langle\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}\right\rangle_{\Delta v}^{-1}
being a normalization constant. To compare the results of scheme (
6.2
) to the ones of (

S
ε

), we introduce the Hopf-Cole transform of the solution to (
6.2
): for all
(n,i,j)\in[\![0,N_{t}]\!]\times[\![1,N_{x}]\!]\times[\![1,N_{v}]\!]
,

\psi_{ij}^{n}=-\varepsilon\log(f_{ij}^{n}).

Since the scheme (
6.2
) is explicit, its stability is guaranteed only under a CFL condition of the form
{\Delta t}\leq\varepsilon C{\Delta x}
, which becomes excessively restrictive as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Moreover, due to the concentration phenomena in velocity as
\varepsilon\to 0
, this scheme is expected to poorly resolve this variable in that regime. Another challenge arises from the use of the logarithm in the Hopf-Cole transform: for small values of
\varepsilon

6.2

\varepsilon\sim 1
.
