Hemodynamic characteristics and drug deposition in cerebral aneurysm sac
Authors: Digamber Singh, Abdullah Y. Usmani, Rajeev Kumar Upadhyay
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Patient-specific cerebral aneurysms exhibit complex hemodynamics insights that influence thrombus formation, wall remodeling, and therapeutic delivery. The article presents a high-fidelity, multi-parametric CFD analysis of nine distinct aneurysm cases using 3-D model-resolved meshes, non-Newtonian Casson rheology, and steady-state flow conditions. Further, the Lagrangian particle tracking was employed to assess drug transport and hemodynamic metrics such as WSS and normalized residence time (NRT) were evaluated, along with vortex dynamics, indicators including helicity (H) and Q criterion to characterize rotational flow structures. Results reveal that narrowed neck models (m1, m2, m5, m8) exhibit weak vortex structures and dominant axial washout (velocity magnitude < 0.10 m/s; NRT < 0.2), while sac-expanded geometries (m3, m4, m6, m9) support robust helical vortices (H > 40 m²/s², Q > 0) and elevated residence times. Regions of low WSS (τw < 0.5 Pa) spatially co-localize with high NRT and potential thrombogenicity, whereas high WSS (τw > 2.5 Pa) associates with jet impingement zones and possible endothelial damage. Notably, sharp WSS gradients are identified as destabilizing hemodynamic factors. Rheological analysis reveals a critical threshold at γ̇ ≈ 415 s⁻¹ (µ ≈ 6.4 mPa·s), distinguishing thrombosis-prone regions (low γ̇, high µ) from stable zones (high γ̇, low µ). Particle transport studies show that effective drug retention occurs in high-helicity, wide neck models for Stokes number (St = 0.1–1), while laminar-dominant aneurysmal flows demonstrate poor drug deposition due to axial convection. Overall, the findings underscore that aneurysm stability and therapeutic outcomes are governed by the interplay of aneurysm induced flow structures, WSS heterogeneity, non-Newtonian rheology, and vortex coherence are refining rupture risk assessments and optimizing endovascular drug delivery strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25588-y.

Introduction

Cerebral aneurysms are localized dilations of intracranial arteries, comprising approximately 5% of all intracranial vascular irregularities and posing a significant risk of rupture. Such ruptures are often associated with comorbidities such as hypertension and atrial fibrillation, though clinical correlations remain limited due to small sample sizes, which are turning to elevated morbidity and mortality rates1–3. Despite significant advancements in clinical and experimental methodologies, the driving aneurysm growth mechanisms and rupture remain inadequately understood4. Among various biomechanical contributors, hemodynamic forces particularly wall shear stress (WSS), flow recirculation, and stagnation zones are considered key determinants of pear-shaped aneurysms5. Notably, 72% of ruptured aneurysms exhibit unstable and abnormal flow patterns, and 80% are characterized by small impingement zones6.

Recent studies highlight intra-aneurysmal thrombosis, particularly in the form of microthrombi, may play a dual role in both stabilizing and destabilizing the aneurysmal wall7. However, the mechanistic relationship between flow disturbances and thrombus formation inadequately understood, due to the lack of large-scale clinical datasets8. Consequently, alternative surgical interventions are being explored as non-conventional treatment process9. It is now recognized that high pulsatile flow rates (PRF) can elevated WSS in certain regions of the aneurysmal dome, potentially triggering secondary aneurysm formation under low PRF conditions10–14. Furthermore, hemodynamic factors linked to aneurysm size played a critical role in rupture risk, giant aneurysms often exhibit complex flow patterns, low shear rates, increased blood viscosity, and reduced WSS13.

The patient-specific 3D geometries reconstructed from imaging data such as CT and MRI have significantly used for the assessment of intracranial hemodynamics7,15, but the application of patient-specific boundary conditions is crucial for enhancing the accuracy of hemodynamic predictions in cerebral aneurysm16. Minimal interaction effects have been observed between reconstruction algorithms and inlet definitions, as well as between inlet and outlet conditions2. Under mean inflow rates, derived from pulsatile cycles, various inflow waveforms produce statistically similar WSS distributions17,18. Moreover, up to 70% of variability in flow distribution can be attributed to non-negligible waveform effects. Studies suggest that using Murray’s law and out flow-splitting methods yields flow rates closest to physiological values12. However, the use of a zero-pressure outlet condition in conjunction with Murray’s law (exponent n = 3) should be avoided, and advanced out flow-splitting models are recommended19.

Coupling blood simulation with the Lagrangian particle tracking method enables the investigation of particle transport behavior within the aneurysmal dome, including the deposition of therapeutic drug particles20. Particles located along slow-moving streamlines tend to remain trapped within the aneurysm cavity, which is critical for understanding the deposition of cells and nano/microparticles in such regions. This mass transport analysis can offer valuable insights into the delivery efficiency of theranostic nanomedicines21.

Furthermore, approximately 80% of ischemic strokes are associated with complex blood flow patterns in the cerebral arterial network, yet the trajectory and behavior of embolic particles remain poorly understood22. Field-mediated flux mechanisms have been shown to enhance particle deposition in low-shear environments like aneurysmal cavities23. The inertial effects of blood flow, along with the presence of vortical structures, significantly influence hemodynamic and biomechanical parameters—often leading to localized zones of elevated WSS in patient-specific small intracranial aneurysms (IAs)24.

Incorporating non-Newtonian blood rheology models, such as the Casson or Carreau–Yasuda models, enhances the fidelity of flow simulations, especially in regions with complex vascular geometry and low shear rates, under varying thermal conditions25,26. Although zero-order models offer higher predictive capability27. The non-Newtonian behavior of blood, driven by red blood cell deformation and aggregation, is strongly implicated in intracranial aneurysm pathology11. It has also been observed that the disparity in hemodynamic parameters between Newtonian and non-Newtonian models diminishes at higher Reynolds numbers28.

Previous studies have identified key hemodynamic markers of rupture-prone, patient-specific aneurysms, including low time-averaged WSS, high recirculatory zones, and increased relative residence time (RRT) of particles29. Among influencing parameters, the mean flow rate exerts a greater effect on these risk factors than the specific shape of the flow waveform30,31. The presence of intraluminal thrombi is also associated with wall degeneration and biomechanical weakening and correlates with thin-walled regions that are more susceptible to rupture, potentially depending on thrombus burden32.

This article aims to bridge existing knowledge gaps by exploring the spatial correlation between disturbed hemodynamics and microthrombus accumulation in cerebral aneurysms. By integrating Lagrangian particle tracking with non-Newtonian CFD simulations, we analyze multiple patient-specific aneurysm geometries reconstructed from CT scans to identify regions of flow stagnation and thrombus susceptibility. The study seeks to provide mechanistic insight into flow-induced thrombogenesis and identify potential hemodynamic biomarkers for rupture risk stratification and therapeutic drug planning.

Furthermore, our findings highlight that local lumen curvature plays a significant role in governing both hemodynamic behavior and thrombotic response, suggesting that curvature may serve as a reliable and quantifiable metric for assessing rupture risk33. Aneurysmal coiling procedures have been shown to reduce rupture likelihood, particularly in cases where larger sac volumes correspond to a more substantial reduction in high-risk hemodynamic zones34. Finally, combining virtual CFD analyses with laboratory experiments offers a powerful, translational platform to enhance our understanding of aneurysmal hemodynamics and support informed, patient-specific surgical decision-making8,35–37.

Methods

Aneurysm models

The approach for reconstructing nine patient-specific intracranial aneurysm models (denoted as m1 to m9) for different clinical cases were adopted from a recent study by Singh and Usmani38, who employed computed tomography (CT) imaging due to its superior resolution compared to other modalities, making it well-suited for detailed vascular reconstruction Singh39. By integrating CT scan data into 3D models, this comprehensive approach enables conversion into analyzable formats, thereby forming the basis for in-depth investigation of cerebral hemodynamics. Intracranial aneurysms in patients with comorbid conditions such as hypertension and atrial fibrillation exhibit a markedly higher rupture risk. Additionally, morphological features such as aneurysm height and neck width as summarized in Table 1 are crucial parameters for preoperative planning. However, due to limited clinical data and incomplete understanding of rupture mechanisms, risk assessments often lack critical insights3.

The aneurysm location for the first patient is shown in Fig. 1(a–c), and detailed anatomical measurements (neck to height) for all cases are provided in Table 1. Open-source software 3D Doctor and 3D Slicer, were used to import the CT scan image datasets38. A four-step manual segmentation procedure was followed: (i) Initial thresholding: Two intensity thresholds (130 HU and 335 HU) were applied to the initial image slice to isolate pixels belonging to the region of interest. (ii) Region growing: The same threshold interval was applied to subsequent slices to propagate the region across the volume. (iii) Manual refinement: Additional pixels not automatically selected were manually added based on expert assessment of radiologist, and (iv) Removal of adjacent structures: Any adjacent vessels not relevant to the geometry of interest were removed from the segmentation. This process yielded a detailed 3D geometrical model of each aneurysm. To enhance model accuracy, surface smoothing was applied to regularize irregularities in the geometry. Finally, the 3D models were exported in stereolithography (.stl) format for subsequent numerical simulation and computational analysis20. Moreover, as per the Table 1 that includes 9 chosen models; the mean neck length of the aneurysms was approximately 50.15 mm with a standard deviation of 57.29 mm, while the mean aneurysm height was around 100.34 mm with a standard deviation of 107.74 mm. The mean neck-to-height ratio was approximately 0.79, with a standard deviation of 0.57, respectively.

Figure 1 presents the regression-based morphometric analysis of nine patient-specific intracranial aneurysm models, revealing substantial dimensional variability and morphological uniqueness. Neck length ranged from 2.71 mm (m6) to 157.34 mm (m3), while dome height varied from 3.29 mm (m1) to 297.11 mm (m7). Larger aneurysms have been shown to be more rupture-prone40, although evaluating rupture risk in small aneurysms (≤ 5 mm) remains clinically critical41.

The neck-to-height ratio, ranging from 0.20 to 1.97, illustrated distinct morphological phenotypes. Lower ratios (< 0.5 in models m4, m6, and m7) are associated with rupture-prone geometries due to adverse hemodynamics, such as reduced WSS. Linear regression revealed a strong positive correlation between neck length and dome height (R² = 0.80), suggesting proportional vascular remodeling. In contrast, weak negative correlations were observed between the neck-to-height ratio and both neck length (R² = 0.11) and dome height (R² = 0.23), indicating that taller aneurysms may develop relatively narrower necks.

Notably, high-ratio models (m1 and m5 > 1.5) may present endovascular treatment challenges, despite potential morphological stability42. These findings underscore the value of incorporating geometric indices particularly the neck-to-height ratio, into risk stratification frameworks to enhance both hemodynamic interpretation and clinical decision making for precision aneurysm management.

Numerical simulations

CFD simulations were performed using ANSYS Fluent (v.2022 R2, ANSYS Inc., Canonsburg, PA, USA), a finite-volume-based computational fluid dynamics (CFD) solver widely used for hemodynamic analysis. The pre-processing of geometries and mesh generation was carried out using ANSYS ICEM CFD, which allows robust handling of complex vascular domains. Unstructured tetrahedral meshes were generated, and local mesh refinement was applied near the vessel walls to accurately resolve boundary layer gradients. The solver employed a pressure-based coupled algorithm suitable for incompressible flow. Pressure–velocity coupling was achieved using the SIMPLEC (Semi-Implicit Method for Pressure-Linked Equations-Consistent) scheme, and second-order upwind discretization was applied for momentum and energy equations to minimize numerical diffusion. Convergence criteria were set to 10⁻⁶ for residuals, ensuring accurate wall shear stress (WSS) estimation. Post-processing of results, including velocity profiles, wall shear stress distribution, and particle deposition analysis, was performed with CFD-Post and MATLAB, which also allowed the generation of streamline plots and contour visualizations (layout of numerical setup is presented in supplementary Figure S1). Unstructured tetrahedral elements were used to discretize the flow domain of the aneurysm model shown in Fig. 2. A mesh sensitivity analysis was conducted on model m1 by progressively refining the mesh, from coarse (d/X) to highly refined (d/5X), to evaluate its impact on key computational metrics, including the number of elements (NOE), mean skewness, element quality, and spatially averaged WSS. As expected, mesh refinement substantially increased the element count, ranging from 2.5 million to 14.5 million elements, as detailed in Table 2. In addition Fig. 3, the number of elements varied significantly across models, ranging from 8.14 million (m1) to 18.4 million (m9), representing an approximate 126% rise. This variation reflects the influence of morphological complexity such as sac dilation, bifurcation angles, and vessel curvature on discretization requirements. Despite these differences, mesh quality remained consistent across all models, with average skewness 0.21 and element quality in between 0.84 and 0.85 listed in Table 3, indicating high geometric fidelity and numerically stable grids.

The transition from d/4X to d/5X alone resulted in a 38.5% increase in elements, highlighting the computational burden imposed by finer meshes (shown in Fig. 4). Average skewness decreased from ~ 0.31 (at d/X) to ~ 0.21 (at d/5X), representing a 47.5% improvement in element orthogonality and reduced distortion. Element quality peaked at ~ 0.84 for d/2X and remained consistently high (> 0.78) across all refined meshes, demonstrating stable aspect ratios and minimal deformation.

The mean WSS highly sensitive to near-wall resolution (Fig. 4), showed a maximum variation of only 3.0% between the coarse (d/X) and fine (d/5X) meshes. Importantly, no variation (0.0%) was observed between d/4X and d/5X, confirming numerical convergence. Given the negligible improvement in mesh quality beyond d/4X and the disproportionate increase in computational cost, the d/4X mesh was selected as mesh-independent and used for all subsequent simulations.

Figure 5 presents a regression analysis correlating NOE with inlet surface area (Ai) (bottom-left subplot), revealing that mesh density is influenced not only by surface area but also by geometric complexity and curvature-induced refinement. For example, models m2 and m4 exhibited moderate surface areas (~ 1.11 × 10⁷ and ~ 8.93 × 10⁶ elements, respectively) but yielded very low WSS values (0.0078 Pa and 0.0042 Pa), indicative of low-shear, recirculatory environments typically associated with aneurysmal sacs. Conversely, models m5, m8, and m9 displayed significantly higher WSS values (3.11 Pa, 2.94 Pa, and 2.78 Pa, respectively) and had NOE values exceeding 11 million, reflecting dense wall refinement and improved resolution of steep shear gradients critical for accurate hemodynamic analysis.

The top-right scatter plot further demonstrates that percentage increases in Ai do not linearly correlate with NOE, reinforcing the role of three-dimensional vascular morphology, rather than surface geometry alone, in determining mesh complexity. For instance, model m9 exhibited the highest NOE (18.4 million) despite only a moderate increase in Ai, illustrating how internal flow domain complexity (e.g., aneurysmal lobes or neck constrictions) governs computational granularity. These findings are consistent with previously reported results (Hang et al., 2022; Suza et al., 2024; Rostamian et al., 2023). Overall, the presented mesh–surface–WSS analysis emphasizes the importance of adopting tailored meshing strategies that account for anatomical heterogeneity while ensuring high mesh quality and computational efficiency for reliable hemodynamic investigations. Blood models

The non-Newtonian rheological Casson model39 is adopted in this study to characterize the shear-thinning behavior of blood. This model effectively captures the non-linear relationship between shear stress and shear rate, particularly in regions of low shear where yield stress effects are pronounced. Blood model comparison

Figures 8(a) and 8(b) compare steady-state CFD simulations of blood flow using Newtonian and Casson rheological models under a uniform inlet velocity of 88.8 mm/s, validated against the results reported by Souza et al.51. Radial velocity profiles extracted from an idealized 3D cerebral aneurysm (CA) model at a pre-aneurysmal location demonstrate that the Casson model more accurately captures hemodynamic behavior, particularly the blunted centerline velocity and steeper near-wall gradients characteristic of blood’s shear-thinning properties. The embedded velocity contour plot further highlights these differences, while Fig. 8(b) shows reduced near-wall velocities predicted by the Casson model due to shear-dependent viscosity effects, resulting in a 4.3% lower apparent viscosity (µ) compared to the Newtonian model at high shear rates. Figure 8(c) extends the analysis to a patient-specific CA model reconstructed from CT angiography, where velocity profiles along the normalized diameter (d/D) exhibit parabolic trends. Here, the Casson model predicts a blunted core flow and greater wall shear sensitivity (in some cases Evju et al.52 and Brambita et al.53 were observed transitional or weakly turbulent flow in cerebral aneurysms), with a 0.9% higher apparent viscosity near the boundary layer attributed to low-shear-rate effects. These results underscore the superior physiological fidelity of the Casson model, particularly its ability to capture shear-dependent viscosity variations critical for modeling the complex hemodynamics of cerebral aneurysms, highlighting its advantage over the Newtonian approximation in replicating in-vivo flow conditions.

Non-newtonian viscosity and shear-rate dependence in intracranial aneurysms

Figure 13 presented the spatial and quantitative behavior of blood viscosity (µeff) across nine intracranial aneurysm models (m1 to m9). The viscosity field is shown in subfigure (a), while subfigure (b) demonstrates inverse nonlinear relationship between viscosity and strain rate (γ˙), thereby revealing the shear-thinning nature of blood rheology.

Models, m2, m3, m4 and m7, has elevated apparent viscosity (> 0.01 Pa. s) predominantly near stagnant flow zones and low shear-rate pockets. These regions correspond to areas of disturbed flow, previously identified as low-WSS zones (Fig. 12), and are spatially correlated with increased thrombogenic potential. Specifically, the low-strain-rate condition (γ˙< 10 s− 1) enhances blood viscosity significantly, promoting erythrocyte aggregation and platelet activation, both of which are crucial to intra-aneurysmal thrombus (IAT) formation.

The viscosity-strain rate curves in Fig. 13(b) show a monotonically decreasing trend, confirming the non-Newtonian behavior. Thus, models like m5, m6, and m9 ​demonstrate higher peak strain rates (4 × 104 s− 1), linked with developed high-velocity jets that prevent local viscosity buildup.

Hemodynamics-guided drug particle transport in patient-specific aneurysm models

In Fig. 16, transport and deposition pattern of drug particles were analyzed in intracranial aneurysm models (m1–m9) using Lagrangian particle tracking. The spatial trajectories, retention behavior, and dispersion of drug particles were influenced primarily by aneurysmal geometry and local hemodynamic patterns, particularly the formation of vortical structures and recirculation zones.

In model m1, the particles aligned with flow path with minimal dispersion into the aneurysm sac. This was attributed to a laminar shear-dominated flow regime lacking strong separation or recirculation zones. The sac morphology did not promote substantial inflow disturbance, resulting in short particle residence time and poor intra-saccular accumulation. While, model m2 exhibited marginally higher particle penetration into the aneurysm. The curved inflow segment and bifurcated geometry led to weak vortical regions with low vorticity magnitude (∣ω∣), allowing limited transverse particle transport. However, due to insufficient flow deceleration or stagnation near the sac, the particle influx remained suboptimal.

In contrast, model m3 demonstrated a significant volume of particles retained within the aneurysm sac. The flow field exhibited a stable vortex core and a high helicity density (H = u ⋅ ω), which promoted sustained intra-saccular particle swirling and prolonged residence time. The combination of geometric complexity and flow curvature created favorable conditions for recirculation-dominated transport, enabling particles to remain confined within the aneurysm dome. A similar yet enhanced swirling pattern was observed in model m4, where distinct intra-saccular vortices emerged as a result of strong secondary flows. These regions were characterized by a positive Q-criterion (Q = 1/2(∥Ω∥2−∥S∥2) > 0), signifying a predominance of rotational over shear deformation. Such coherent vortical structures entrapped particles effectively, ensuring a more uniform spatial dispersion and increasing the likelihood of therapeutic payload retention.

Model m5, particle deposition was minimal due to the absence of strong flow deceleration and vortex formation. The aneurysm’s narrow-necked geometry failed to induce significant momentum exchange or flow separation, resulting in limited particle entry flux (Jp= cun​), where un represents the velocity component normal to the sac inlet. Consequently, the drug delivery efficiency in this model was among the lowest. On the other hand, model m6, with a nearly spherical aneurysm sac, fostered a highly symmetric and stable toroidal vortex. This structure promoted central sac accumulation and significantly increased particle residence time. The local Stokes number (St = τp/τf​), reflecting the ratio of particle response time to fluid timescale, was estimated to lie within the range of 0.1 to 1, enabling particles to respond to steady flow variations while still being influenced by inertial effects an ideal regime for efficient transport and retention55.

Model m7 exhibited extensive intra-aneurysmal swirling of particles, facilitated by its tortuous geometry and bifurcation-driven counter rotating secondary flows. These secondary motions enhanced effective diffusivity and resulted in broad, uniform particle dispersion. High helicity density and Q-criterion values confirmed the presence of coherent vortex cores that promoted robust particle retention. In Model m8, only partial penetration of particles into the sac was observed. Although the aneurysmal shape permitted some recirculation, the downstream flow acceleration rapidly swept particles past the sac, reducing their residence time. The velocity vector field indicated a dominance of axial convection with limited transverse flow, restricting particle entrapment and diminishing localized drug exposure.

Model m9, however, displayed the most effective particle retention characteristics. A well-defined spiral flow structure was formed within the aneurysm sac, driven by both geometric configuration and inlet flow momentum. The presence of sustained high Q and high-helicity zones supported the development of Lagrangian coherent structures, which acted as dynamic barriers that confined particles within the sac volume. This model thus provided optimal conditions for prolonged particle residence, enhancing the potential for localized drug delivery and therapeutic efficacy. The results reveals that models with broader necks, bulbous sac morphologies, and complex curvature such as m3, m6, and m9 demonstrate superior intra-aneurysmal particle trapping due to their ability to generate stable, vortical recirculation zones. In contrast, elongated or narrow-necked aneurysms, as observed in m1, m2, and m5, fail to support such flow structures and consequently exhibit poor particle retention. These findings underscore the significance of patient-specific flow topology and aneurysm geometry in governing drug particle dynamics and highlight the potential of hemodynamics-informed design for personalized endovascular treatment strategies.

This nonlinearity is characteristic of non-Newtonian flow regimes and complex wall-bounded shear-layer interactions found in cerebral vasculature.

Models m2, m3, m4, and m7 with ⟨Re⟩ < 0.002 exhibited disproportionately high values of ⟨Cd​⟩, exceeding 8 × 105. This suggests that viscous forces dominate the momentum transfer, resulting in elevated flow resistance and significant deceleration of particle velocity. In these conditions, the flow is characterized by low WSS and recirculatory zones, creating a prothrombotic microenvironment. Additionally, spectrum, model m8 with ⟨Re ⟩ ≈ 0.009 and low ⟨Cd⟩ reflects an inertial regime where convective transport prevails, resulting in streamlined flow and reduced particle drag.

The spatial distribution of NRT shown in Fig. 18 supports these observations. The colorimetric mapping of particle trajectories, normalized from 0 (blue) to 1 (red), reveals that models m3, m4, and m6 have regions of high NRT concentrated near the aneurysmal dome, with peak values ranging from 0.82 to 0.94 and mean NRT values of 0.71 ± 0.06, 0.74 ± 0.04, and 0.78 ± 0.05, respectively. These localized zones of prolonged residence time (NRT > 0.8) are indicative of poor flow mixing and particle entrapment, which are mechanistically linked to thrombogenesis through platelet aggregation, oxygen depletion, and increased biochemical residence.

In contrast, models m1 and m8 exhibit rapid particle clearance, with peak NRT values not exceeding 0.18 and mean NRT values of 0.12 ± 0.03 and 0.15 ± 0.02, respectively. Models m2, m5, m7, and m9 show intermediate NRT values, with peaks in the range of 0.32–0.61 and mean values between 0.28 ± 0.05 and 0.47 ± 0.07, suggesting variable retention characteristics across the sac and neck regions.

From a physiological standpoint, NRT values above 0.8 correspond to severely stagnant flow conditions, which are strongly associated with thrombus formation and aneurysm progression in prior studies. Conversely, NRT values below 0.2 reflect efficient flow turnover and a reduced risk of thrombogenic events. These findings underscore the relevance of NRT as a robust hemodynamic marker for assessing aneurysm vulnerability and for identifying potential targets for therapeutic intervention.

A cut-off transition point in the flow regime where a distinct shift from viscous-dominated to inertia-dominated transport occurs. This transition corresponds to a threshold Reynolds number, Recrit​, beyond which the drag coefficient declines rapidly and NRT decreases, reflecting enhanced convective efficiency. Theoretically, this behavior aligns with boundary layer thinning and inertial dominance over viscous shear forces.

Conclusion

The article delivers a high-fidelity, multi-parametric numerical investigation of patient-specific cerebral aneurysm geometries, incorporating non-Newtonian shear-thinning blood rheology and Lagrangian particle tracking to elucidate the complex interplay between flow dynamics, thrombus susceptibility, and targeted drug deposition. The novelty of this work lies in its integrated approach that combines spatial hemodynamic markers including wall shear stress (WSS), helicity, vorticity, and strain rate–dependent viscosity with geometric variability to uncover flow-induced mechanobiological phenomena governing aneurysmal progression and treatment efficacy.

Distinct flow regimes were observed across aneurysm models; low-velocity recirculation zones (∣u∣ < 0.10 m/s) contributed to elevated residence times (τR), promoting thrombotic microenvironments, whereas high-momentum inflow jets (∣u∣ > 0.6 m/s) induced focal WSS spikes and helical instabilities indicative of chaotic flow breakdown. Geometric asymmetry and neck inflow skewness further intensified shear layer gradients, amplifying regions of vortex breakdown, which are strongly associated with endothelial injury and wall remodeling.

Furthermore, aneurysm wall vulnerability is strongly modulated not by absolute WSS values alone but by spatial WSS gradients. Areas with low shear (< 0.5 Pa) were conducive to flow stasis, thrombogenesis, and hypoxia-driven endothelial dysfunction, while high-shear zones (> 2.5 Pa) triggered mechanotransduction-induced pro-inflammatory responses. Critically, models exhibiting mixed WSS environments with steep gradients revealed potential sites for localized fatigue and rupture initiation. This finding points toward the clinical utility of incorporating spatial WSS metrics beyond mean values into patient-specific rupture risk assessments.

The inclusion of non-Newtonian rheology allowed for a refined characterization of viscosity-strain rate coupling. Specifically, low local strain rates (γ̇ < 1.1 s⁻¹) elevated blood viscosity (µ > 9 mPa·s), exacerbating flow stagnation and thrombotic hypoxia, whereas high strain rates (γ̇ > 200 s⁻¹) maintained efficient convective transport (µ ≈ 4 mPa·s). A critical transition threshold (γ̇ ≈ 415 s⁻¹, µ ≈ 6.4 mPa·s) emerged as a novel biomechanical indicator separating stable from pro-thrombotic flow regimes. Additionally, rotational flow metrics including vorticity (ω > 600 s⁻¹), helicity (H > 40 m²/s²), and Q-criterion-based vortex coherence enabled a more nuanced classification of protective versus destabilizing recirculation zones.

Importantly, aneurysm morphology was found to directly influence drug payload deposition dynamics. Narrow-neck aneurysms (m1, m2, m5, m8) exhibited low normalized residence times (NRT < 0.2) and limited particle entrapment, whereas wide-neck models (m3, m4, m6, m9) with strong helicity and Stokes number range (St = 0.1–1) demonstrated inertial particle trapping within stable vortex cores (Q > 0), significantly enhancing local drug delivery potential. Furthermore, this study offers mechanistic insight into rupture pathophysiology and presents quantitative flow-based biomarkers with direct clinical relevance for aneurysm risk stratification and personalized therapeutic planning. Future studies may extend this framework to incorporate FSI (fluid–structure interaction) and patient-specific wall compliance, enabling even greater translational value in endovascular treatment optimization.

Limitations of the study and future work

The present study, while providing detailed insights into the curvature- and tortuosity-induced hemodynamics of cerebral aneurysms, is subject to several modeling and computational limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the rigid wall assumption neglects the dynamic coupling between the pulsatile blood flow and the deformable arterial wall. In reality, wall compliance modulates both wall shear stress (WSS) and oscillatory shear index (OSI) by absorbing part of the pulsatile energy. The rigid-wall approach likely overestimates local WSS peaks and underrepresents wall deformation–driven flow damping. Future incorporation of fluid–structure interaction (FSI) models would allow more realistic estimation of endothelial loading and wall response. Second, a uniform velocity or mass-flow inlet profile was prescribed at the inlet to isolate geometric effects. However, physiologically, inflow waveforms exhibit secondary structures and skewness due to upstream branching. This simplification may have smoothed out localized flow asymmetries and influenced the predicted vortex formation and flow division near the aneurysm neck. Applying patient-specific 3D inflow profiles from 4D Flow MRI or Doppler data in future work would refine these predictions. Third, the analysis was conducted under laminar flow assumptions, which are generally valid for intracranial arteries but may not fully capture transitional or weakly turbulent fluctuations occurring at higher Reynolds numbers or near sharp curvatures. Such instabilities could contribute to additional energy dissipation and secondary flow enhancement, potentially underestimating localized vorticity and mixing effects in the present model. Finally, other physiological factors, such as blood–wall interaction at the endothelial interface, non-Newtonian variability due to hematocrit or temperature, and boundary condition sensitivity, were not included. These simplifications were made to focus on isolating the geometric effects of curvature and tortuosity, but they inevitably limit the quantitative generalization of the results to in vivo conditions.

Future work will involve integrating FSI coupling, patient-specific inlet conditions, and transitional flow modeling that in turn will help bridge these gaps and provide a more comprehensive understanding of aneurysm hemodynamics under realistic physiological conditions.
