Phytosphingosine contributes gut microbiota metabolism to alleviate low-grade endotoxemia-induced mastitis
Authors: Yihong Zhao, Hongyan Li, Jiawen Xu, Haiqi Li, Lijuan Bao, Keyi Wu, Min Qiu, Hang Yu, Shan Shang, Yuhong He, Chongshan Yuan, Naisheng Zhang, Xiaoyu Hu, Yunhe Fu, Wenjia Li, Caijun Zhao, Jun Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecently, evidence has indicated that mastitis is closely associated with the ruminal dysbiosis caused by subacute ruminal acidosis (SARA) and subsequent low-grade endotoxemia (LGE). However, whether and how ruminal metabolic dysbiosis influences SARA-associated mastitis is still unclear.ResultsUsing untargeted metabolomics, we found that cows with SARA-associated mastitis exhibited altered ruminal metabolic profiles, particularly a reduced level of phytosphingosine (PS), compared to healthy cows. Oral administration of PS to mice alleviated LGE-induced mastitis, as evidenced by attenuated mammary injury and improved function of mammary tight junctions (TJs). Furthermore, we demonstrated that LGE induced significant gut dysbiosis, characterized by increased abundances of opportunistic pathogens such as Enterococcus, which was mitigated by PS treatment. Interestingly, transplantation of both fecal microbiota (FMT) and matched sterile supernatant (FST) from PS-treated mice alleviated LGE-induced mastitis, restored the blood-milk barrier, and modulated the gut microbiota in recipient mice following LGE exposure. Additionally, PS and PS-FMT treatments increased the abundances of fecal short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) producers, accompanied by elevated fecal SCFA levels, particularly butyric acid, in PS- and PS-FMT-treated mice. Butyric acid was negatively correlated with mammary inflammatory markers, and butyrate administration attenuated LGE-induced mastitis in mice. Mechanistically, butyrate promotes M2 macrophage polarization and inhibits NF-κB and NLRP3 inflammasome activation via G-protein-coupled receptor 41 (GPR41).ConclusionsCollectively, our results demonstrate that gut microbiota from PS-dosed mice alleviate LGE-induced mastitis in mice by promoting SCFA production and regulating macrophage polarization. Our findings provide deeper insights into gut dysbiosis-associated mastitis and highlight the potential of modulating gut microbiota and its metabolism as a strategy for managing mastitis and other related diseases. Introduction

Mastitis is a serious disease affecting both humans and animals, characterized by inflammation and tissue injury of the mammary gland. In women, mastitis increases the risk of breast cancer, while in dairy cows, it reduces milk yield and quality [1–3]. Reports indicate that more than 33% of lactating women worldwide suffer from mastitis, leading to the cessation of exclusive breastfeeding and negatively impacting newborn health [4]. The prevalence of mastitis in dairy cows varies between 20 and 68% across different countries and regions [5, 6], resulting in over $30 billion in annual global economic losses [7]. Given the classical inflammatory symptoms of redness, swelling, heat, and pain in the mammary gland, as well as the isolation of pathogens, antibiotic therapy remains the most common treatment for mastitis. However, there is insufficient evidence to demonstrate that mastitis has been effectively controlled through antibiotic intervention. Instead, an increased incidence of subclinical mastitis has been observed in modern dairy farms. Notably, the main pathogens of mastitis, such as Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), Escherichia coli (E. coli), Streptococcus, and Klebsiella, are undetectable in a significant proportion of mastitis samples, suggesting that breast infections may not be the sole cause of mastitis. Furthermore, the rise of antibiotic resistance due to the indiscriminate overuse of antibiotics poses a critical threat to both humans and animals [8]. Consequently, it is imperative to elucidate the pathogenesis of mastitis and develop effective, safe, and widely applicable strategies for its management.

The gut microbiota, a complex ecosystem comprising diverse microbes within the gut, has been shown to play a critical role in host immune regulation, barrier maintenance, and metabolic homeostasis [9–12]. Conversely, gut dysbiosis, characterized by alterations in microbial composition and function, contributes to the development of various diseases, including obesity, fatty liver disease, cancer, and infections. Emerging evidence also indicates that cows with mastitis exhibit altered gut microbial profiles, and fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) from cows with mastitis to recipient mice induces mastitis [13, 14]. Specifically, dysbiotic gut microbiota from cows with mastitis increases serum lipopolysaccharide (LPS), leading to low-grade endotoxemia (LGE) and subsequently causing mastitis via degradation of host anti-inflammatory alkaline phosphatase [14, 15]. Additionally, gut dysbiosis reduces the relative abundance of commensal short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) producers, resulting in decreased fecal SCFA levels and contributing to mastitis development [13]. Furthermore, intestinal microbiota-derived SCFAs regulate immune cell functions, including macrophage polarization [16]. Gut inflammation and microbial dysbiosis also enhance sialic acid production, which exacerbates gut dysbiosis-associated mastitis [15, 17]. Moreover, studies have demonstrated that depletion of the gut microbiota increases susceptibility to mastitis caused by pathogens such as S. aureus and E. coli [18–20]. However, supplementation with commensal microbiota-derived metabolites, including SCFAs, aryl hydrocarbon receptor ligands, and secondary bile acids, alleviates pathogen-induced mastitis in mice [18–20]. These findings suggest that elucidating the roles of gut microbiota and their metabolites in mastitis pathogenesis may facilitate the development of effective strategies for mastitis intervention.

To increase milk yield and meet the postpartum energy demands of dairy cows, their forage-based diet is commonly replaced with a high-concentrate diet (HCD) [21]. However, excessive consumption of HCD can rapidly decrease ruminal pH, leading to ruminal dysbiosis, a condition known as subacute ruminal acidosis (SARA), which subsequently increases the risk of mastitis by inducing LGE [14, 15, 22]. Using anion-mode untargeted metabolomics, our previous study revealed that cows with SARA-associated mastitis exhibited altered ruminal metabolic profiles and demonstrated that sialic acids exacerbate gut dysbiosis-induced mastitis in mice by promoting opportunistic pathogenic Enterobacteriaceae and amplifying LGE [15]. HCD-induced SARA also increases susceptibility to S. aureus-induced mastitis by inhibiting the activation of mammary TGR5 by reducing mammary deoxycholic acid levels [20]. However, more evidence is needed to better elucidate the pathogenesis of dairy cow mastitis.

Phytosphingosine (PS) is a long-chain base in sphingolipids [23], distributed in keratinocytes, microvillus membranes of the small intestine, and leukocytes [24]. Recent studies have shown that sphingolipids are one of the three main types of lipids in cell membranes [25]. Previous research has demonstrated that PS treatment enhances phytoceramide content in keratinocytes, stimulates epidermal differentiation via peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) activation, and increases skin moisture content, thereby improving skin barrier function [26]. Furthermore, sphingolipid metabolites and their roles in regulating chronic inflammation have been extensively studied [27, 28]. Dietary PS significantly decreases plasma cholesterol and triacylglycerol levels and protects the liver from fat- and cholesterol-induced steatosis [29]. Additionally, O-cyclic phytosphingosine-1-phosphate, a novel sphingosine metabolite derived from phytosphingosine-1-phosphate, alleviates neuroinflammation by decreasing glial fibrillary acidic protein [30]. PS also mitigates acute 2,4,6-trinitrobenzenesulfonic acid (TNBS)-induced colitis in mice [31]. Nevertheless, the specific correlation between PS and gut dysbiosis-associated mastitis remains unclear.

In the current study, we first investigated ruminal metabolic profiles and identified reduced ruminal PS levels in cows with SARA-associated mastitis induced by a HCD (Fig. 1A). Next, we examined the protective role of PS in LGE-induced mastitis in mice and focused on gut microbial and metabolic changes derived from PS treatment by performing FMT and sterile fecal supernatant transplantation (FST) (Fig. 1B). Finally, we explored the underlying mechanisms of PS in LGE-induced mastitis through analyzing SCFAs metabolism and validating the protective effect of butyrate on LGE-induced mastitis in mice (Fig. 1C).

Results

Cows with SARA-associated mastitis exhibit reduced levels of ruminal phytosphingosine

To investigate the ruminal metabolic profiles of cows with SARA-associated mastitis, untargeted metabolomics analysis was performed on ruminal samples collected from healthy cows and those with SARA-associated mastitis. Our previous study demonstrated that disrupted sialic acid metabolism exacerbated gut dysbiosis-induced mastitis based on negative ion mode analysis [13]. Therefore, this study aims to explore potential metabolites that may influence the development of SARA-associated mastitis using positive ion mode analysis. To ensure stable and accurate metabolome results, data quality control (QC) was conducted through Pearson correlation analysis. The high Pearson correlation of ruminal QC samples indicated reliable data quality (Fig. S1A). Moreover, the QC samples clustered tightly together in the principal component analysis (PCA) score plots (Fig. S1B, C), further confirming the reliability of the data quality. A total of 365 annotated metabolites were identified in all ruminal samples using the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and LIPID MAPS (Fig. S2A–C, Table S1). PCA revealed significant separation between ruminal samples of cows with SARA-associated mastitis and healthy ruminal samples (PC1: 71.32%, PC2: 6.18%, Fig. 2A). Additionally, distinct clustering of cows with SARA-associated mastitis compared to healthy cows was confirmed by partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) score plots (p < 0.001, Fig. S3A). The stability and reliability of PLS-DA were validated through sevenfold cross-validation (R2Y = 1.00, Q2Y = 0.98, Fig. S3A). Furthermore, a model incorporating metabolic information was constructed by fitting PLS-DA through 200 random permutation tests to confirm its ability to correctly classify new samples. The results demonstrated that the model is reliable and does not overfit, as evidenced by the intercepts of goodness-of-fit (R2) being greater than goodness-of-prediction (Q2) and the Q2 intercept being less than zero (Fig. S3B).

We identified 97 significantly differential metabolites in cows with SARA-associated mastitis compared to healthy cows (62 upregulated and 35 downregulated, Table S1, S2, and S3) by considering variable importance in projection (VIP), fold change (FC), and p-value (VIP > 1, FC > 1.2 or FC < 0.5, and p-value < 0.05). Moreover, the ruminal metabolite levels in cows with SARA-associated mastitis were significantly different from those in healthy cows, as visualized by a heatmap (Fig. 2B). The global distribution of differential metabolites was illustrated by volcano plots, which highlighted distinct metabolite levels between SARA-associated mastitis and healthy ruminal samples (Fig. 2C). A total of 3 metabolites were downregulated in SARA cows with more than a 128-fold FC (Fig. 2C), including DL-Stachydrine, 2-amino-1,3,4-octadecanetriol, and 1H-indene-3-carboxamide. Among these, the level of 2-amino-1,3,4-octadecanetriol (also known as phytosphingosine (PS); hereafter referred to as PS) was decreased 133.9-fold in SARA-associated mastitis cows compared to healthy cows (Fig. 2D). PS has been recently identified as a novel anti-inflammatory agent in intestinal inflammation [31] and exhibits protective effects against S. aureus-induced mastitis in mice [32]. Collectively, these results indicate that cows with SARA-associated mastitis exhibit altered metabolic profiles and reduced PS levels.

PS treatment alleviates LGE-induced mastitis and blood-milk barrier injury in mice

PS is a long-chain base in sphingolipids that can originate from both host and microbial sources [11, 33]. Previous research has demonstrated that PS treatment alleviates colitis and improves barrier functions [31]; however, the role of PS in SARA-associated mastitis remains unclear. To investigate the protective effects of PS in SARA-associated mastitis, we supplemented PS to mice in an LGE-induced mastitis model (Fig. 3A), as LPS is considered a major factor contributing to SARA-associated inflammatory responses [14, 22], and recurrent LPS stimulation to induce LGE has been reported to mimic SARA-associated mastitis by impairing the activity of host anti-inflammatory enzymes [14, 34]. The results showed that LGE treatment induced significant hyperplasia and inflammatory infiltration in mammary glands compared with the control group (Fig. 3B, C). In contrast, PS treatment (PL group) significantly improved mammary gland injury caused by LGE compared with the LGE group (Fig. 3B, C). Consistent with previous studies [14, 34], LGE induced marked increases in proinflammatory markers in mammary glands, including TNF-α, IL-1β, and MPO activity, compared with the control group (Fig. 3D-–F). Interestingly, mice in the PL group exhibited reduced levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and MPO activity in mammary glands compared with those in the LGE group. The blood-milk barrier is known to be disrupted during mastitis [13, 14]. Indeed, LGE treatment decreased the expression of mammary tight junction (TJ) proteins, including ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-3, compared with the control group, whereas PS treatment mitigated LGE-induced reductions in mammary TJ proteins (Fig. 3G, H). Collectively, these findings indicate that PS treatment ameliorates LGE-induced mastitis and blood-milk barrier disruption in mice.

PS treatment changes the gut microbiota in mice

The gut microbiota has been reported to play a significant role in regulating inflammatory diseases, including mastitis [13–15, 35]. We next investigated whether the protective effect of PS on LGE-induced mastitis is associated with the gut microbiota. To address this question, 16S rRNA gene sequencing was performed on fecal samples from differently treated mice. Mice in the PS group showed a slight reduction in observed OTUs compared with the control group, but no significant difference was detected (Fig. 4A). Similarly, LGE treatment exhibited comparable results to the control group. In addition, mice in the PL group demonstrated a slight increase in observed OTUs compared with those in the LGE group (Fig. 4A). Consistently, alpha diversity indices, including Chao 1, Shannon, and Simpson, showed similar trends to observed OTUs across different groups (Fig. S4A). Furthermore, PCoA based on unweighted UniFrac distance revealed distinct gut microbial structures among different groups (ANOSIM, R = 0.2476, p = 0.001, Fig. 4B). At the phylum level, mice in the PS group exhibited reduced abundances of Firmicutes and Proteobacteria and increased abundance of Bacteroidota compared with the control group (Fig. 4C). Conversely, mice in the LGE group showed increased abundances of Firmicutes and Proteobacteria and decreased abundance of Bacteroidota compared with the control group (Fig. 4C). After PS treatment, fecal abundances of Firmicutes and Proteobacteria were reduced, while Bacteroidota abundance was increased compared with the LGE group (Fig. 4C). At the genus level, mice in different groups also displayed distinct bacterial compositions (Fig. 4D). Microbial function prediction analysis using PICRUSt2 revealed differential bacterial functions among different treatment groups (Fig. S4B). To identify differentially enriched genera among groups, taxonomy indicator bubble plots were generated. The results showed that Helicobacteraceae, Sutterellaceae, and Selenomonadaceae were enriched in the PS group, Enterococcaceae and Clostridiales_Family_XI. Incertae_Sedis were enriched in the LGE group, and Rikenellaceae, Eggerthellaceae, and Oscillospiraceae were enriched in the PL group (Fig. S4C). At the genus level, HT002 and Muribaculum were enriched in the control group, Helicobacter, Bacteroidales_unclassified, Parasutterella, and Megamonas were enriched in the PS group, Ruminococcaceae_unclassified and Enterococcus were enriched in the LGE group, and Candidatus_Saccharimonas, Clostridiales_unclassified, Enterorhabdus, and Ruminococcus were enriched in the PL group (Fig. 4E). Furthermore, these findings were confirmed by linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe, log10 (LDA score) > 3) (Fig. 4F and S4D). Collectively, these results demonstrate that PS treatment alters the gut microbiota in mice with LGE-induced mastitis.

Gut microbiota and its metabolites from PS-treated mice alleviate LGE-induced mastitis and blood-milk barrier disruption

To explore the role of gut microbiota in the protective effects of PS against LGE-induced mastitis in mice, FMT was performed using samples from control (C-FMT) and PS-treated (PS-FMT) groups. Given the critical role of gut microbial metabolism in health and disease progression, sterile supernatants of fecal samples were also transplanted (FST) from the control (C-FST) and PS-treated (PS-FST) groups alongside LGE treatment (Fig. 5A). Histological analysis revealed that mice in the PS-FMT group exhibited reduced inflammatory infiltration and tissue injury compared to those in the C-FMT group (Fig. 5B, C). Notably, PS-FST also alleviated LGE-induced mastitis relative to the C-FST group (Fig. 5B, C). Although the histological score was slightly higher in the PS-FST group than in the PS-FMT group, no significant difference was detected (Fig. 5C). Consistently, mice in the PS-FMT group showed decreased levels of proinflammatory markers, including TNF-α, IL-1β, and MPO activity, compared to those in the C-FMT group (Fig. 5D-–F). Similarly, mice in the PS-FST group exhibited reduced TNF-α, IL-1β, and MPO activity in mammary glands compared to those in the C-FST group (Fig. 5D-–F), though few statistically significant differences were identified. Additionally, PS-FMT increased the expression of mammary TJ proteins compared to the C-FMT group (Fig. 5G, H). Consistently, elevated levels of ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-3 were observed in the mammary glands of mice in the PS-FST group compared to those in the C-FST group (Fig. 5G, H). Collectively, these findings indicate that gut microbiota and its metabolites from PS-treated mice mitigate LGE-induced mastitis and blood-milk barrier disruption.

PS-FMT and PS-FST change the gut microbiota in recipient mice

We further investigated changes in the gut microbiota of different recipient mice. The results showed no significant differences among groups in alpha diversity indices, including observed OTUs, Chao 1, Shannon, and Simpson (Fig. 6A and S5A). However, PCoA analysis revealed distinct clustering of gut microbial structures among groups based on unweighted UniFrac distance (ANOSIM, R = 0.3651, p = 0.001, Fig. 6B). Similar to the donor mice, recipient mice in the PS-FMT group exhibited reduced abundances of Firmicutes and Proteobacteria and increased abundances of Bacteroidota at the phylum level compared with the C-FMT group (Fig. 6C). Recipient mice in the PS-FST group also showed decreased Firmicutes and increased Bacteroidota abundances relative to the C-FST group (Fig. 6C). At the genus level, distinct microbial compositions were observed across groups, with similar patterns in the PS-FMT and PS-FST groups compared to their respective controls (C-FMT and C-FST) (Fig. 6D). Furthermore, a taxonomy indicator bubble plot demonstrated enrichment of different bacterial families in each group (Fig. S5B). Specifically, at the genus level, Clostridiales_unclassified and Bacteroidales_unclassified were enriched in the PS-FMT group, while Erysipelatoclostridium, Parabacteroides, and Lactococcus were enriched in the PS-FST group (Fig. 6E). Additionally, differential bacteria were confirmed using LEfSe analysis (log10 (LDA score) > 3) (Fig. 6F and S5C). Collectively, these findings indicate that both PS-FMT and PS-FST significantly alter the gut microbiota in recipient mice.

Increased fecal SCFA levels from PS-dosed gut microbiota contribute to alleviating LGE-induced mastitis

The alleviation of LGE-induced mastitis by PS-FST suggests that gut microbial metabolites may play a crucial role in the PS-mediated protection against mastitis. Notably, genera enriched by PS treatment or PS-FMT are predominantly SCFA producers. For example, Megamonas metabolizes substrates to produce SCFAs and alleviate inflammation [36, 37]. Ruminococcus is an important intestinal bacterium that ferments complex dietary sugars to produce SCFAs, including acetic acid, propionic acid, and butyric acid [38–40]. Clostridiales, increased by PS under LGE conditions, are well-known butyric acid producers and have been shown to alleviate inflammation [35, 41]. We then investigated whether SCFA metabolism contributes to PS-mediated protection against mastitis. As expected, mice in the PS group exhibited increased levels of SCFAs, including acetic acid, propionic acid, and butyric acid, compared with those in the control group (Fig. 7A and S6A). There was a slight decrease in propionic acid, butyric acid, and valeric acid in the LGE group, though no significant differences were detected (Fig. 7A and S6A). Additionally, while acetic acid, propionic acid, butyric acid, isobutyric acid, valeric acid, isovaleric acid, and hexanoic acid were elevated in the PL group compared with the LGE group, only butyric acid showed statistical significance (Fig. 7A and S6A). Furthermore, increased butyric acid levels were observed in the PS-FMT group compared with the C-FMT group (Fig. 7B and S6B). Consistently, we also observed a slight increase in butyric acid in the PS-FST group compared with the C-FST group (Fig. 7B and S6B). Spearman correlation analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between Megamonas and butyric acid in mice subjected to PS treatment (Fig. S6C). Well-known SCFA producers, such as Alistipes and Roseburia [13, 42], also displayed positive correlations with butyric acid in FMT or FST recipient mice, although no significant differences were identified (Fig. S6D). Interestingly, butyric acid exhibited negative correlations with opportunistic pathogens Escherichia_Shigella and Klebsiella (Fig. S6C and S6D), suggesting its protective role against inflammation and infection [13, 42]. Pearson correlation analysis demonstrated that fecal butyric acid levels were negatively correlated with mammary proinflammatory markers, including TNF-α, IL-1β, and MPO activity, in both PS-treated and FMT/FST recipient mice (Fig. 7C, D). Next, we examined whether increased butyric acid contributes to the protective effect of PS on LGE-induced mastitis in mice. As expected, mice supplemented with butyrate alleviated LGE-induced mammary injury and reduced TNF-α, IL-1β, and MPO activity compared with those in the LGE group (Fig. 7E-H). Furthermore, butyrate increased the expression of TJ proteins compared with the LGE group (Fig. 7I, J). Collectively, these findings indicate that gut microbiota-derived butyric acid from PS-treated mice contributes to ameliorating LGE-induced mastitis in mice.

Butyric acid alleviates inflammation by regulating M1 and M2 macrophages polarization

Butyric acid can improve disease processes and limit inflammatory responses by modulating M1/M2 macrophage polarization [43–46]. Mastitis caused by gut dysbiosis in mice is associated with increased recruitment of mammary macrophages and reduced butyric acid levels [13, 35]. We then investigated the role of macrophages in butyrate-mediated protection against mastitis. The results showed that, compared with the control group, as the inflammatory response was activated, the total number of macrophages and the proportion of M1 macrophages significantly increased in the LGE group, whereas the proportion of M2 macrophages significantly decreased (Fig. 8A–D). Treatment with PS or butyrate decreased the proportion of M1 macrophages and increased the proportion of M2 macrophages in the mammary glands of mice compared with the LGE group (Fig. 8A–D). A similar trend was observed in the spleen (Fig. S7A–D), indicating that butyrate also alleviates systemic inflammation induced by LGE. Additionally, we found that PS and butyrate treatments increased the expression of genes associated with M2 macrophage polarization, including IL-10 and Arg-1 (Fig. S7E–F). To investigate the extent to which macrophages mediate the protective effects of butyrate on mastitis, mice were treated with clodronate liposomes to selectively deplete macrophages in the context of PS or butyrate treatment and subjected to LPS treatment. Compared with the LGE group, both PS and clodronate liposomes alleviated mammary injury, but no further effects were observed in the PL + lipo group (Fig. 8E). Similar results were found for mastitis parameters, including TNF-α, IL-1β levels, and MPO activity (Fig. 8F–H). Similarly, compared with the LGE + lipo group, comparable effects on mammary inflammatory profiles were observed in the BL group subjected to clodronate liposome treatment (Fig. 8I–L). Overall, these findings demonstrate that butyric acid alleviates LGE-induced mastitis by regulating macrophage polarization.

Butyric acid limits the activation of NF-κB and NLRP3 pathways in macrophages via GPR41

Host inflammatory signaling pathways play a critical role in proinflammatory cytokine production and mastitis pathogenesis [14, 47]. Among these pathways, NF-κB and NLRP3 are responsible for the production of TNF-α and IL-1β, respectively. As expected, LGE-treated mice exhibited increased activation of NF-κB and NLRP3 pathways, characterized by elevated levels of p-p65, p-IκB, NLRP3, ASC, and IL-1β compared with control mice (Fig. 9A, B). Consistent with the reduced TNF-α and IL-1β levels in mammary glands, PL group mice showed decreased levels of p-p65, p-IκB, NLRP3, ASC, and IL-1β compared with LGE group mice (Fig. 9A, B), suggesting that PS treatment inhibits the activation of mammary NLRP3 and NF-κB signaling pathways. In FMT and FST experiments, PS-FMT and PS-FST treatment also limited the activation of NF-κB and NLRP3 pathways compared with their respective controls (C-FMT and C-FST) (Fig. S7H, I). In butyrate experiments, mice in the BL group also exhibited reduced activation of NF-κB and NLRP3 pathways compared with the LGE group (Fig. 9C, D). Evidence supports the notion that microbiota-derived butyric acid promotes IL-22 production and limits inflammation through GPR41 [16, 45]. We further demonstrated that butyrate increased GPR41 mRNA expression in mammary glands (Fig. 9E) and RAW264.7 cells (Fig. 9F). Next, we investigated whether butyrate attenuates inflammatory responses via GPR41 by treating cells with the GPR41-specific agonist AR420626. The results indicated that both butyrate and AR420626 treatments significantly limited the activation of NF-κB and NLRP3 (Fig. 9G, H), but no additional effects were observed in cells co-treated with butyrate and AR420626 compared with those treated with butyrate alone (Fig. 9G, H). To confirm these findings, we blunted GPR41 in RAW264.7 cells using siRNA (Fig. S7G). The results showed that GPR41 inhibition weakened the butyrate-mediated reduction in TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 (Fig. 9I–K). In summary, these results indicate that butyric acid limits the activation of NF-κB and NLRP3 pathways by activating GPR41.

Discussion

Mastitis is one of the most serious diseases in the dairy industry, causing substantial financial losses [48]. Recent studies have demonstrated that cow mastitis is not only caused by pathogen invasion of the mammary glands but is also closely linked to gastrointestinal dysbiosis [13, 14, 35, 47]. On dairy farms, SARA-associated ruminal dysbiosis has been reported to promote the development of mastitis; however, the underlying mechanism remains poorly understood. In the current study, using untargeted metabolomics, we showed that cows with SARA-associated mastitis exhibited altered ruminal metabolic profiles, particularly a reduced level of PS. Administration of mice with PS alleviated LGE-induced mastitis by promoting the production of the gut microbial metabolite butyric acid, which regulates macrophage polarization and GPR41-mediated inflammatory responses.

The gut microbiota plays a significant role in host physiological homeostasis and disease development, and metabolic changes represent one of the primary mechanisms through which the gut microbiota interacts with the host. Disruption of the gut microbiota and its metabolism is considered an important regulator of disease development. For example, gut dysbiosis-induced expansion of opportunistic E. coli and LPS release exacerbates stroke [49]. Reduced production of tryptophan-derived metabolites by the gut microbiota aggravates inflammatory diseases and impairs the efficacy of cancer immunotherapy [50, 51]. In cows with mastitis, ruminal and gut dysbiosis are observed, accompanied by increased serum LPS levels [13, 14]. FMT from cows with mastitis to recipient mice and recurrent stimulation with LPS to induce LGE confirmed the role of gut dysbiosis-derived LPS in the pathogenesis of mastitis [13, 14]. Gut dysbiosis-induced LGE is also linked to the development of diseases in distant organs. For instance, increased LGE originating from gut inflammation induces brain choroid plexus closure via up-regulation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway [52]. Other studies have shown that increased LGE induces oxidative stress and damages the blood-milk barrier of the mammary gland under SARA, contributing to the development of mastitis [53]. Interestingly, these processes are regulated by microbial metabolism. For instance, cows with mastitis exhibit increased ruminal sialic acid levels, which exacerbate gut dysbiosis-associated mastitis by facilitating pathobiont expansion, LGE, and the release of muramyl dipeptide [15, 17]. In the current study, altered metabolic profiles and reduced ruminal PS levels were detected in cows with SARA-associated mastitis.

PS is a bioactive sphingolipid widely expressed in host cells and has been reported to be primarily metabolized by host cells with minimal contribution from dietary components [54–56]. Notably, recent studies have demonstrated that PS can also be metabolized by the gut microbiota [11, 33]. However, the mechanism underlying the reduction of PS in the rumen during SARA-associated mastitis remains unclear. Administration of PS to mice alleviated LGE-induced mastitis and disruption of the blood-milk barrier by inhibiting mammary NF-κB and NLRP3 activation. These results are consistent with previous findings that PS significantly inhibits NF-κB activation and ameliorates LPS-induced inflammation in macrophages [31]. Additionally, PS has been found to mitigate skin inflammation by suppressing NF-κB and JAK/STAT signaling in keratinocytes and mice [57]. Moreover, gut microbiota-derived PS exhibits protective effects on cancer immunotherapy by regulating the MYD88-NF-κB-HLA class I pathway [11]. Our previous study also revealed that PS can alleviate S. aureus-induced mastitis in mice [32], suggesting its potential role in protecting against mastitis caused by gut dysbiosis or pathogen invasion. In this study, we found that the protective effect of PS on mastitis was at least partially regulated by the gut microbiota and its metabolites. Furthermore, most bacteria enriched in the PS treatment groups, such as Megamonas, Ruminococcus, and Roseburia [36, 58–60], possess the capacity to generate SCFAs, and these genera exhibited positive correlations with fecal butyric acid levels increased by PS treatment. Ruminococcus, known for its butyric acid-producing ability, has been linked to reduced milk somatic cell counts (SCC) and mastitis in cows [61]. Additionally, Roseburia, a well-known butyric acid producer, can ameliorate ulcerative colitis and cancer [58, 59], and limit pathobiont translocation across the gut-mammary axis to alleviate gut dysbiosis-induced mastitis [13]. Interestingly, butyric acid displayed negative correlations with opportunistic Escherichia_Shigella and Klebsiella, which aligns with previous results indicating that butyrate protects the host against inflammation and infection [13, 42]. Other studies also suggest that fecal butyric acid creates an anaerobic environment in the gut to restrict the expansion of facultative anaerobic Enterobacteriaceae [12, 13].

Mice subjected to butyric acid treatment also alleviated LGE-induced mastitis, which is consistent with previous studies showing that fecal butyric acid levels are negatively associated with the development of mastitis caused by gut dysbiosis [13]. In detail, depletion of the gut microbiota by antibiotics reduced fecal butyric acid levels and aggravated S. aureus-induced mastitis in mice by limiting NF-κB activation and impairing blood-milk barrier integrity [35]. In contrast, modulation of the gut microbiota by dietary inulin increased SCFA production and attenuated S. aureus-induced mastitis in mice by activating the HDAC3-mediated antimicrobial program in macrophages [18]. In cows with subacute mastitis, treatment with inulin reduced milk SCC and serum proinflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α and IL-1β, which were also associated with increased ruminal SCFA production [62]. Macrophages are an important source of proinflammatory cytokines in response to LPS stimulation via M1 polarization, such as TNF-α and IL-1β. Increased mammary macrophage infiltration was detected in cows with mastitis caused by gut dysbiosis or pathogen infection [13, 35]. In this study, PS and butyric acid treatment reduced the number of macrophages in the mammary glands and promoted M2 polarization. Depletion of macrophages abolished the protective effects of PS or butyric acid on mastitis. Similarly, the protective effects of butyric acid on various diseases, including liver injury, MRSA pneumonia, and myocardial fibrosis, have been reported to depend on macrophage polarization [44, 63, 64]. Moreover, Li et al. demonstrated that butyrate regulates M1/M2 macrophage polarization by modulating mitochondrial function during myocardial fibrosis [44]. These studies indicate that butyric acid-mediated macrophage polarization plays a critical role in regulating inflammation, and the specific mechanism varies depending on the disease context. Additionally, microbiota-derived butyrate can also regulate gut mucus barrier repair by activating M2 macrophages [65]. However, whether the improvement of the blood-milk barrier by butyric acid depends on macrophages requires further investigation. The production of proinflammatory cytokines also requires the activation of host signaling pathways, including NF-κB and NLRP3, which were inhibited by PS and butyric acid via GPR41 activation. Similarly, Zhao et al. demonstrated that sodium butyrate ameliorated chronic obstructive pulmonary disease through GPR43 activation [43], suggesting that the protective effects and mechanisms of butyric acid on inflammation may depend on specific inducers and disease processes.

Collectively, our results demonstrated that cows with SARA-associated mastitis exhibited altered ruminal metabolic profiles and reduced ruminal PS levels. Oral supplementation of PS in mice alleviated LGE-induced mastitis, which was associated with changes in the gut microbiota and increased production of butyric acid. Mechanistically, butyric acid promoted M2 polarization of macrophages and limited the activation of NF-κB and NLRP3 signaling pathways by activating GPR41 (Fig. 10). Our findings not only provide deeper insights into the pathogenesis of gut dysbiosis-associated mastitis but also suggest a potential strategy for intervening in mastitis and other diseases by targeting the gut microbiota and its metabolism.

Limitations of the study

While our study has demonstrated a clear role of PS in LGE-induced mastitis in mice and its potential mechanism, it remains possible that other microbial components, such as microbial extracellular vesicles, may synergistically contribute to the PS-mediated protection against mastitis. In addition, due to the physiological differences between cows and mice, as well as the limitation that the LGE-induced mastitis model in mice cannot fully simulate cow mastitis caused by ruminal dysbiosis, further exploration is needed regarding the changes in PS concentration in clinical cases of ruminal dysbiosis-induced mastitis in cows and the protective effect of PS against this condition. In particular, the mechanisms by which specific probiotic strains utilize PS for growth and produce SCFA in the process, as well as the specific mechanisms underlying the decrease in rumen PS, remain unclear.

Materials and methods

Cow treatment and ruminal samples collection

Cow treatment and rumen sample collection were performed as previously described [15]. Briefly, twelve 4- to 6-year-old Holstein cows were fed a high-concentrate diet (70% grain and 30% forage) for 8 weeks to induce SARA-associated mastitis. Rumen fluid was collected via rumenocentesis using nonpyrogenic needles (2.0 × 120 mm) and 50 mL syringes. All samples were immediately quick-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until analysis.

Untargeted metabolomics

Untargeted metabolomics of ruminal samples were performed as previously described [14, 16]. Specifically, rumen fluid was mixed with pre-cooled methanol, incubated on ice, and then centrifuged at 4 °C for 5 min at 15,000 g. The supernatant was diluted with LC–MS grade water to a final concentration of 53% methanol and transferred to a new centrifuge tube for analysis using an LC-MS/MS system (Thermo Fisher). LC-MS/MS analyses were conducted using a Vanquish UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher) coupled with an Orbitrap Q Exactive series mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher). Samples were injected onto a Hyperil Gold column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.9 μm) using a 16-min linear gradient at a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min. Next, Compound Discoverer 3.1 (CD3.1, Thermo Fisher) was used to analyze and process the raw data, including peak alignment, peak picking, and peak quantification for each metabolite. The main parameters were set as follows: retention time tolerance, 0.2 min; actual mass tolerance, 5 ppm; signal intensity tolerance, 30%; signal-to-noise ratio, 3; and minimum intensity, 100,000. Peak intensities were normalized to the total spectral intensity. Based on additive ions, molecular ion peaks, and fragment ions, the normalized data were utilized to predict the molecular formula. Subsequently, peaks were matched against the mzCloud (https://www.mzcloud.org/), mzVault, and MassList databases to obtain accurate qualitative and relative quantitative results. Statistical analyses were conducted using R (version R-3.4.3), Python (version 2.7.6), and CentOS (release 6.6). When the data were not normally distributed, normalization was attempted using the area normalization method. Metabolites were compared by querying the KEGG (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/), HMDB (http://www.hmdb.ca/), and LipidMaps (http://www.lipidmaps.org/) databases to achieve accurate qualitative and quantitative results. Additionally, principal component analysis (PCA) and partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) were performed using metaX. Univariate analysis (t-test) was applied to calculate statistical significance (p value), with thresholds set as p < 0.05, fold change (FC) > 1.2 or FC < 0.5, and variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1. Volcano plots were generated based on Log2 (FC) and -log10 (p value) to filter metabolites of interest. Statistical analyses were carried out according to the obtained qualitative and quantitative results using R (version R-3.4.3), Python (version 2.7.6), and CentOS (release 6.6). Finally, differential metabolites were screened, data were normalized, and conclusions were drawn for subsequent experiments.

LGE-induced mastitis model and animal treatments

A total of 200 specific pathogen-free (SPF) BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks old, 150 females and 50 males) were obtained from Liaoning Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Benxi, China). All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Jilin University. Mice were provided with ad libitum access to food and water and maintained under a 12-h light/dark cycle. The mice were housed at a ratio of three females to one male per cage.

For the PS treatment experiment, female mice that had confirmed delivery were randomly divided into four groups: control (C), PS, LPS-induced low-grade endotoxemia (LGE), and PS + LGE (PL). (1) C group: mice were administered vehicle (PBS-diluted DMSO) daily for 10 days; (2) PS group: mice were treated intragastrically with PS (25 mg/kg) daily for 10 days [31, 32]; (3) LGE group: mice were orally administered vehicle and intraperitoneally injected with LPS (5 μg per mouse) daily for 10 days postpartum [14]; (4) PL group: mice were treated daily with both 25 mg/kg PS and 5 μg LPS for 10 days.

For FMT and FST experiments, the feces of mice in the C group and the PS group were dissolved in PBS, and the fecal samples from each group were divided into two parts (0.05 g/mL). One part was allowed to settle naturally, and the supernatant was filtered through a 70-μm filter for FMT preparation. The other part was centrifuged (3000 rpm, 4 °C, 10 min), and the supernatant was collected and sequentially passed through 70-μm and 0.22-μm filters for FST preparation [66]. Pregnant mice were treated with a mixed antibiotic cocktail (ABX) containing 200 mg/kg ampicillin, metronidazole, and neomycin, as well as 100 mg/kg vancomycin, to deplete the commensal gut microbiota. These mice were then randomly divided into four groups: C-FMT, PS-FMT, C-FST, and PS-FST. (1) C-FMT group: mice were treated with fecal microbes from the control group; (2) PS-FMT group: mice were treated with fecal microbes from the PS group; (3) C-FST group: mice were administered fecal supernatant from the control group; (4) PS-FST group: mice were administered fecal supernatant from the PS group. For FMT or FST, mice were intragastrically treated with 200 μL fecal samples daily for 10 days and simultaneously treated with 5 μg LPS daily to induce LGE-associated mastitis.

For the butyrate treatment experiment, mice were randomly divided into three groups: control, LGE, and butyrate + LGE (BL). These mice were orally treated with either 30 mg/kg butyrate or PBS daily for 10 days [18], and mice in the LGE and BL groups were also treated with 5 μg LPS daily for 10 days.

For the macrophage clearance experiment, mice were specifically depleted of macrophages using clodronate liposomes (LIPOSOMA, China, C-005). First, mice were intraperitoneally injected with clodronate liposomes (200 μL/mouse). On the second day, mice were treated orally with PBS, PS (25 mg/kg), or butyrate (30 mg/kg). Additionally, all mice in the LGE-related groups were treated with 5 μg LPS daily for 10 days. Notably, clodronate liposomes (200 μL/mouse) were replenished once more on the fifth day of the treatment cycle. Finally, mammary glands were collected from these mice for further detection.

Cell culture and treatments

Macrophages (RAW264.7) were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gemini, USA, 900-108) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution at 37 °C under 5% CO2. For the butyrate experiment, macrophages were seeded in 6-well plates and treated with butyrate (1 mM) for 24 h before RNA extraction for qPCR analysis. For the AR420626 treatment, cells were also seeded in 6-well plates and pre-treated with butyrate (1 mM) or AR420626 (5 µM) for 2 h, followed by treatment with LPS (10 µg/mL) for 24 h. For the siRNA transfection experiment, GPR41 siRNA and negative control siRNA (Sangon Biotech, China) were transfected into cells using Lipo8000™ Transfection Reagent according to the manufacturer’s protocol. SiRNA was diluted in Opti-MEM Medium to a final concentration of 25 nM. Butyrate (1 mM) was added 48 h post-siRNA transfection. Subsequently, cells were treated with LPS (10 µg/mL) for 24 h, after which they were harvested for analysis.

Histological analysis

Mammary gland tissues used for histological analysis were collected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h. After paraffin embedding, the tissues were sectioned into 5-μm slices. The sections were then subjected to the following procedures: dewaxing with xylene (two changes, 15 min each), rehydration through a graded ethanol series (anhydrous ethanol (two changes, 5 min each), 95% ethanol (two changes, 5 min each), and 80% ethanol (two changes, 5 min each)), and hematoxylin staining for 5 min. Sections were subsequently washed in running water for 10 min, differentiated in 1% hydrochloric acid alcohol for 30 s, rinsed in distilled water for 30 s, stained with 0.5% eosin for 3 min, and washed again in distilled water for 30 s. Dehydration was performed using an ascending ethanol series (80% ethanol (two changes, 5 min each), 95% ethanol (two changes, 5 min each), and anhydrous ethanol (two changes, 5 min each)), followed by clearing with xylene (two changes, 15 min each). Sections were finally mounted with neutral resin. All sections were examined under a light microscope and scored pathologically according to previously described methods [13–15].

Mammary cytokine detection

Proinflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α and IL-1β, were detected using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Thermo Fisher) as previously described [14, 15]. Briefly, a 10% mammary gland tissue homogenate was prepared in PBS. After centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, the supernatants were collected, and TNF-α and IL-1β levels were measured at 450 nm according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Myeloperoxidase (MPO) activity assay

Mammary tissues were homogenized to prepare a 5% tissue homogenate, and reagents were prepared according to the kit instructions. MPO activity in mammary tissues was determined following the manufacturer’s instructions (Nanjing Jiancheng, China).

Total bacterial DNA extraction and 16S rRNA sequencing

Fresh fecal samples were collected and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. Total bacterial DNA was extracted from fecal samples using the magnetic bead method. The concentration and purity of the extracted DNA were assessed using 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and the DNA was diluted to 1 ng/μL with sterile water based on the determined concentration. Using the diluted DNA as a template, the hypervariable V3-V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified with specific primers (338F-806R). The PCR reaction system consisted of 15 μL Phusion High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix, 2 μM upstream and downstream primers, and approximately 10 ng of DNA template. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 98 °C for 1 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 98 °C for 10 s, annealing at 50 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s. The PCR products were extracted from 2% agarose gel, purified using the AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit (AxyPrep Biosciences, Union City, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions, and quantified using the Quantus Fluorometer (Promega, USA). Purified amplicons were pooled in equimolar amounts and subjected to paired-end sequencing on the Illumina MiSeq PE300 platform or NovaSeq PE250 platform (Illumina, San Diego, USA) following standard protocols provided by Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Cluster analysis was performed on the raw sequencing data, and sequences were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at 97% similarity using UPARSE version 7.1. The taxonomic classification of each OTU representative sequence was analyzed using RDP Classifier version 2.2 against the 16S rRNA gene database with a confidence threshold of 0.7. OTUs were annotated at various taxonomic levels, including kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, and genus. Alpha diversity and beta diversity analyses were conducted. Alpha diversity indices, such as Observed species, Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson, were used to evaluate the richness and evenness of OTUs detected in the samples. Beta diversity analysis was performed to assess differences in bacterial community structure composition using unweighted distance, and results were visualized using Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA). Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) was applied to identify differentially abundant bacterial genera across groups. Spearman correlation analysis was used to explore correlations between specific bacteria and butyric acid.

Fecal SCFAs detection

Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) concentrations were determined using targeted metabolomics [18, 35]. Specifically, fecal samples were mixed with 50 μL of 15% phosphoric acid, 100 μL of isohexic acid (125 μg/mL) as an internal standard, and 400 μL of ether. The mixture was vortexed for 1 min and then centrifuged at 12,000 × g at 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was analyzed using gas chromatography (Trace 1300, Thermo) coupled with mass spectrometry (ISQ 7000, Thermo). SCFAs concentrations were calculated based on the standard curve.

Western blotting

The total protein of mammary tissues was extracted using a tissue protein extraction kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and the protein concentration was determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher, USA). Target proteins were separated by 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). After separation, the target protein region was identified based on the molecular weight marker, and the proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes under wet transfer conditions at 350 mA for 1 h and 40 min. Subsequently, the membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in TBST at room temperature for 3 h. The membranes were then washed three times with TBST for 5 min each and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the corresponding primary antibodies, including p-p65 (#AF2006; 1:1000), p-65 (#AF5006; 1:1000), p-IκB (#AF2002; 1:1000), IκB (#AF5002; 1:1000), Occludin (#DF7504; 1:1000), ZO-1 (#AF5145;

1:1000), Claudin-3 (#AF0129; 1:1000), NLRP3 (#15101; 1:1000), ASC (#67824; 1:1000), IL-1β (#12242; 1:1000), and β-actin (#AF7018; 1:1000). On the following day, the membranes were washed three times with TBST for 10 min each and subsequently incubated with goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:10,000) or anti-mouse IgG (1:10,000) secondary antibodies at room temperature for 2 h. The membranes were then washed again three times with TBST for 10 min each. Finally, the targeted proteins were visualized using an ECL Plus Western blotting detection system (Tanon 4500, Shanghai, China), and the differences in protein expression were analyzed using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software.

Total RNA extraction and quantitative RT‑PCR

The total RNA from mammary tissues was extracted using Trizol (Invitrogen, CA, USA) as previously described [19]. Briefly, 0.1 g of tissue or collected cells were homogenized in 1 mL Trizol and mixed thoroughly. Then, 200 μL of chloroform was added to each sample and mixed vigorously. The samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 4 °C for 12 min. The aqueous phase was carefully transferred to a new tube, an equal volume of isopropanol was added, and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for 10 min before centrifugation at 12,000 rpm at 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the RNA pellet was washed with 75% ethanol under RNase-free conditions. After a final centrifugation at 7500 rpm at 4 °C for 5 min, the supernatant was removed, and the pellet was air-dried for 5–10 min. The RNA was dissolved in RNase-free water and quantified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. For reverse transcription, 1 μg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using TransStart Tip Green qPCR SuperMix (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). Subsequently, qPCR was performed using FastStart Universal SYBR Green Master Mix (ROX) (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) on a StepOne Plus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: 50 °C for 2 min, 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min, with a final melting curve analysis. All primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S4, and GAPDH served as the endogenous control. The relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, normalized to the control group.

Flow cytometry analysis

Flow cytometry was performed as previously described [46, 67]. Briefly, spleen and mammary gland tissues were collected and minced immediately. Collagenase D (Roche, Shanghai, China) was diluted in TESCA buffer (Solarbio, China), and mammary tissues were incubated with collagenase D in a 37 °C shaker for 30 min. The digestion was terminated by washing twice with PBS. After filtration through a 70-μm cell strainer, the resulting cell suspension was centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C, and the pellet was washed three times under the same conditions. Subsequently, the pellet was treated with red blood cell lysis buffer (Solarbio, China) two to three times to remove erythrocytes, and the final volume was adjusted to 1 mL with PBS. Approximately 1 × 106 cells per sample were collected after experimental treatment. Each sample was divided into two tubes: one for M1 macrophage staining and the other for M2 macrophage staining. Cells were stained with the following antibodies: PE anti-mouse F4/80 (Biolegend, 123110), FITC anti-mouse CD11b (Biolegend, 101206), APC anti-mouse CD86 (Biolegend, 105012), and APC anti-mouse CD206 (MMR) (Biolegend, 141708). F4/80+CD11b+CD86+ cells were identified as M1 macrophages, while F4/80+CD11b+CD206+ cells were identified as M2 macrophages. Notably, intracellular staining of CD206 required the use of eBioscience™ Flow Cytometry Intracellular Fixation & Permeabilization Buffer (Thermo Fisher, USA) and eBioscience™ Flow Cytometry Staining Buffer (Thermo Fisher, USA). Finally, all samples were analyzed on a flow cytometer (Becton Dickinson and Company, USA).

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as means ± SD. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 (San Diego, CA, USA). One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used for statistical analysis, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Other specific statistical methods are described in the figure legends.

The 16S rRNA gene sequencing data in the present study are available in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) repository under accession number PRJNA1013611. The untargeted metabolomics sequencing data in the present study are deposited in the OMIX, China National Center for Bioinformation/Beijing Institute of Genomics, Chinese Academy of Sciences (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/omix; accession number OMIX010223).
