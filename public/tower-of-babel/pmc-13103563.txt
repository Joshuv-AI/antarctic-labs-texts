Language-related self-efficacy of pharmacy staff in providing medication counselling to older adults in a linguistically diverse setting
Authors: Sumithra Devi Suppiah, Meagan Goh, Xilei Yang, Yi Wen Tan, Rahul Malhotra, PROMISE Study Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEffective verbal medication counselling is crucial for patients' understanding, medication adherence, and treatment outcomes. In linguistically diverse populations, language barriers can pose a significant challenge, when older patients prefer to receive information in languages other than English.ObjectiveTo assess pharmacy staff's self-efficacy in providing medication counselling to older adults in Singapore's four official languages (English, Mandarin [Chinese], Malay and Tamil) and other Chinese dialects.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional survey among 121 pharmacy staff working in public primary care clinics. Respondents reported the languages or dialects they used when counselling older patients (aged 60 and above) and completed a six-item self-efficacy scale (1–5, higher scores indicate greater self-efficacy) for each language or dialect, covering aspects of greeting patients, explaining medication information and explaining serious health risks.ResultsMean (± standard deviation) self-efficacy scores were 4.5 ± 0.6 for English, 3.7 ± 1.0 for Mandarin, 3.4 ± 1.3 for Malay, 4.5 ± 0.9 for Tamil, and between 3.0 and 3.1 (±0.9–1.0) for other Chinese dialects. Across all languages or dialects, the least self-efficacy was in explaining serious health risks.ConclusionPharmacy staff exhibited lower self-efficacy counselling in official languages other than English and in other Chinese dialects, highlighting a potential care gap. The findings underscore the need for targeted language support for pharmacy staff to enhance communication among ageing, multilingual populations, such as in Singapore.

Introduction

Effective communication between pharmacy staff and patients is essential in ensuring that patients receive accurate medication information, understand proper usage, and are aware of potential side effects or interactions. However, language barriers often limit patients' understanding of medication instructions and lead to poor health outcomes,1 especially in settings with linguistically diverse populations2, 3—such as Singapore.

Singapore has four official languages: English, Malay, Mandarin [Chinese] and Tamil.4 In 2020, out of Singaporeans aged 65 years and above, the proportion who were literate only in languages other than English ranged from 68.2% (65–74 years) to 83.3% (85 years and older). Furthermore, only 30.4% of older Singaporeans, i.e., aged 60 years and above, reported English — including English in combination with other languages — as the most frequently spoken language at home.5 In addition to the four official languages, many older adults (and others in Singapore) also communicate in other Chinese dialects including Hokkien, Teochew, Cantonese, and Hakka.6

Given the linguistic diversity of the older population in Singapore, pharmacy staff (i.e., pharmacists and pharmacy technicians) frequently encounter situations where effective communication depends on their ability to counsel patients in official languages other than English and in other Chinese dialects. This can pose challenges when delivering medication counselling to older patients who primarily communicate in these languages or dialects.7 With the growing number of older adults in Singapore and their increasing utilisation of healthcare services, including hospital admissions and outpatient attendances,8 effective communication remains critical for medication safety. Inadequate communication may lead to misunderstandings of dosing instructions, improper medication use, reduced adherence, and failure to recognise potential side effects or precautions, which may contribute to medication-related harm.9

In such contexts, not only language proficiency of pharmacy staff but also their confidence in using that language for medication-information communication becomes crucial. Drawing upon Bandura's Social Cognitive Theory, human behaviour is shaped by the reciprocal interaction between personal, environmental, and behavioural factors.10 Within this framework, self-efficacy represents a central determinant of action, referring to an individual's perceived capability to organise and execute the courses of action required to manage prospective situations.11 In healthcare settings, self-efficacy influences health professionals' communication behaviours, including their willingness to initiate and persist in communication tasks, while low self-efficacy may lead to avoidance of more complex interactions.12 It is also commonly used as an outcome measure in communication research, reflecting perceived capability, with evidence showing that communication training improves both self-efficacy and observed communication performance.13 Therefore, language-specific self-efficacy provides a theoretically grounded measure of pharmacy staff's perceived capability to deliver effective medication counselling across different linguistic contexts.

Henceforth, we explored the self-efficacy of pharmacy staff in providing medication counselling to older patients in different official languages and in other Chinese dialects in Singapore, to inform approaches for enabling transmission of accurate medication knowledge.

Methods

Setting, participants, sample size and data collection

Primary care services in Singapore are provided by both public and private healthcare institutions. This study was conducted at public primary care clinics, also known as polyclinics. As of 2025, there are 26 polyclinics, divided into three major polyclinic groups: SingHealth Polyclinics (SHP), National Healthcare Group Polyclinics (NHGP) and National University Polyclinics (NUP).14

Pharmacy staff (pharmacy technicians and pharmacists) at SHP and NHGP polyclinics who had performed medication counselling for at least one older patient in the past one year were eligible for the study. All pharmacy staff (n = 149) at five SHP and NHGP polyclinics were approached, of whom 122 (81.9%) agreed for eligibility assessment. After excluding one ineligible staff, a total of 121 (81.2%) pharmacy staff (74 from SHP and 47 from NHGP polyclinics) were recruited to respond to a self-administered questionnaire on Qualtrics®.

The questionnaire first gathered data on pharmacy staff demographics (age, sex, nationality and language(s) spoken and written [including dialect(s) spoken]), professional training and experience. Then, for each language or other Chinese dialect reported as used (spoken) during medication counselling with older patients in the past one year, pharmacy staff completed a six-item self-efficacy scale. The scale was an adapted version of the validated pharmacists' self-efficacy in communicating with Spanish speaking patients (PECS) instrument.15 Permission was obtained from the original author to adapt the scale to the Singaporean context. The scale assesses confidence in greeting patients, explaining medication information and explaining serious health risks. The overall score is based on a 5-point scale (1: not at all confident, 5: extremely confident), with higher scores indicating greater self-efficacy in using the language or dialect during medication counselling. If a participant had a missing response (i.e., “don't know” or “refused”) for only one of the six items for a reported language or dialect, then their overall score for that language or dialect was imputed based on the score for the remaining five items (i.e., overall score = score for five items/5 * 6). However, if more than two items had missing responses, the participant's overall self-efficacy score for that language or dialect was treated as missing and excluded from the analysis. This ensured the integrity of the data by maintaining sufficient completeness to calculate meaningful overall scores.

Descriptive analysis was conducted in Stata19. Summary statistics and bivariate analyses were used to examine differences across participant characteristics. Group mean differences were assessed using ANOVA or t-tests, followed by Tukey–Kramer corrected post-hoc tests.

Ethical considerations

This study is part of a health services research project titled “Prescription Medication Label Improvement for Singaporean Elderly” (PROMISE) which provides the evidence-base for developing and implementing easily understood, context- and culturally-appropriate prescription medication labels. PROMISE was approved by the SingHealth Centralised Institutional Review Board (CIRB Reference number: 2017/3023). All participants provided written informed consent.

Results

All 121 pharmacy staff had used English during medication counselling with older patients in the past one year, and Mandarin was the second most reported language (115 participants; 95.0%). A smaller proportion reported using Malay (88 participants; 72.7%) and few pharmacy staff reported using Tamil (9 participants; 7.4%). In the context of other Chinese dialects, just over half of the pharmacy staff had used Hokkien (66 participants; 54.5%), followed by Cantonese (29 participants; 24.0%) and Teochew (25 participants; 20.7%). Conversely, Hakka and Hainanese had the lowest reported usage rates (2 participants each; 1.7%). Discussion

This study assessed the self-efficacy of pharmacy staff in providing medication counselling to older patients in Singapore's four official languages and other Chinese dialects. The results provide important insights into the confidence of pharmacy staff in delivering medication counselling to patients with limited English proficiency.

Overall, pharmacy staff demonstrated relatively high self-efficacy in communicating medication information in English. As the primary medium of instruction and de facto language of Singapore's healthcare system, this was not surprising. However, they reported lower confidence for communication in official languages other than English, except for Tamil, and in other Chinese dialects. Several factors may explain this disparity. Driven by the increasing preference for English and shifts in education policies, policymakers, academics and parents have expressed growing concerns over the decline in mother tongue proficiency and the increasing prevalence of monolingualism across all ethnic groups in Singapore.16, 17, 18, 19 Although pharmacy staff may display day-to-day conversational and writing fluency in their mother tongue, this may not necessarily translate to proficiency in medical terminology rarely used in everyday conversations. Furthermore, other Chinese dialects have been at risk of disappearing as their usage continues to decline among younger generations of Singaporeans.20 The “Speak Mandarin” Campaign, launched in 1979 to actively promote the use of Mandarin over other Chinese dialects,21 may have also contributed to lower proficiency in other Chinese dialects among middle-aged Singaporeans.

Importantly, lower self-efficacy in official languages other than English and in other Chinese dialects should not be interpreted as a direct indicator of insufficient communication skills. Self-efficacy reflects perceived capability and may be shaped by factors such as limited opportunities to use specific languages or dialects in clinical practice, lack of formal training in medical terminology, and structural constraints including availability of institutional language or dialect support. As such, lower self-efficacy may reflect reduced exposure or support rather than true deficits in underlying communication ability.

Self-efficacy is also both modifiable and context-dependent. Evidence from Social Cognitive Theory indicates that self-efficacy can be strengthened through training, practice, and mastery experiences.10, 11 In healthcare settings, communication training has been shown to improve self-efficacy alongside communication performance.13 However, in multilingual care contexts, self-efficacy is also shaped by environmental and structural factors, including opportunities to use different languages or dialects in clinical practice, availability of institutional language or dialect support, and workplace demands.12 As such, improving self-efficacy may require not only individual-level training but also system-level support to enable effective communication across languages and dialects.

Implications for pharmacy practice

In multilingual settings like Singapore, pharmacy staff must be equipped to provide medication counselling to patients who speak different languages and dialects. Effective medication counselling is integral in ensuring that patients understand their prescriptions and adhere to treatment regimens. Pharmacy staff who lack confidence in communicating in the various languages and dialects spoken by their patients may face challenges in delivering optimal pharmaceutical care. Targeted efforts are therefore required to bridge language gaps and ensure that older adults who communicate in official languages other than English and in other Chinese dialects can receive appropriate medication information.

These findings may also have important implications for health equity. Lower self-efficacy in communicating complex or risk-related information in official languages other than English and in other Chinese dialects may lead to differences in the quality, completeness, and clarity of medication counselling provided to patients who only or primarily communicate in these languages or dialects. Tasks such as taking medication histories and discussing potentially serious health risks are critical for identifying drug-related problems, preventing adverse events, and supporting safe medication use. Lower confidence in performing these tasks may therefore increase the likelihood that important information is not fully elicited or effectively communicated. In multilingual care settings, these differences may disproportionately affect patients who only or primarily communicate in official languages other than English or in Chinese dialects, potentially contributing to inequities in understanding, engagement, and medication-related outcomes. This is particularly relevant for older adults, who often have more complex medication regimens and greater vulnerability to medication-related harm. While this study does not directly assess patient outcomes, the observed disparities in self-efficacy highlight the potential for language-related gaps in pharmaceutical care.

Addressing these gaps will likely require both individual-level strategies, such as targeted communication and language (and dialect) training, and system-level approaches, including the use of interpreters, multilingual resources, and workflow support to ensure equitable delivery of medication counselling across diverse patient populations.

Healthcare institutions in Singapore are actively working towards closing the gap by introducing translation applications that support Malay, Hokkien and Cantonese translations with audio playback functionality and also serve as language and dialect learning tools for staff.22 Similarly, pharmacy students have taken the initiative to equip themselves with basic conversational skills and healthcare-related terms in various mother tongue languages and dialects.23 Acknowledging existing efforts, it remains crucial that healthcare institutions continue to develop initiatives aimed at improving communication between pharmacy staff and patients.

Targeted training programs for pharmacy staff can be implemented to enhance their proficiency in official languages other than English and in other Chinese dialects, with a focus on improving conversational fluency and medical terminology to ensure effective medication counselling and patient communication. Employers could also encourage pharmacy staff to enroll in language and dialect courses and consider providing subsidies for such courses and study leave policies to motivate uptake. Additionally, pharmacies can work towards incorporating visual aids (i.e. pharmaceutical pictograms) directly on medication labels, which has proven to increase understanding of medication instructions, when counselling older patients.24, 25 Incorporating visual aids is a simple and accessible approach that not only serves to overcome language barriers but also aids understanding of medication information for older patients with limited health literacy.26 In the long-term, pharmacy schools play a pivotal role in addressing these language barriers. Introducing language and dialect training as part of the pharmacy curriculum prior to clinical practice would help to alleviate the challenge of mastering medical terminology in another language or dialect while also providing students with adequate time to achieve proficiency.

However, the feasibility of suggested language and dialect training programs will depend on the resources available, and the impact of pictograms may vary across different patient populations. Moreover, not all pharmacy staff may have the time or capacity to commit to additional language and dialect courses, especially in a fast-paced work environment. Despite these challenges, we believe these initiatives are crucial steps towards bridging language gaps and improving patient care in multilingual settings.

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first study in Singapore and elsewhere to document the self-efficacy of multilingual pharmacy staff serving older patients with limited English proficiency. Measurements of item-wise self-efficacy also allowed the identification of areas for improvement in participants speaking specific languages and dialects.

Despite the insights gained from this study, several limitations should be considered. While the study included a broad range of languages and dialects, the relatively small number of Tamil-speaking participants (n = 9) limits the generalisability of self-efficacy findings for this language group. Future studies should aim to include a larger and more diverse sample of pharmacy staff to better capture the experiences of those interacting with Tamil-speaking patients. Additionally, we acknowledge the possibility of bias in self-reported measures of self-efficacy.27 Self-efficacy levels also vary by individual interpretation, making it difficult to establish a clear definition of ‘adequate’ self-efficacy among pharmacy staff. Lastly, while we assessed pharmacy staff's self-efficacy in verbal communication, we did not explore other factors that may influence effective communication, such as cultural competence or the availability of translation resources in healthcare institutions, which could also play a role in overcoming language barriers. Future studies may also look towards comparing the self-reported self-efficacy scores with patient perceptions to explore any differences in opinions.

Conclusions

Our study assessed pharmacy staff's self-efficacy in providing medication counselling in the official languages of Singapore and in other Chinese dialects with older patients. Pharmacy staff had lower overall self-efficacy in communicating in official languages other than English (i.e., in Mandarin, Malay, and Tamil) and in other Chinese dialects, compared to English, highlighting a gap in communication confidence in meeting the diverse language needs of older adults in Singapore, many of whom who may have limited English proficiency.

These findings suggest the need for targeted support to strengthen language capabilities among pharmacy staff. Henceforth, healthcare institutions can look towards supporting pharmacy staff in language and dialect acquisition while also implementing visual aids on medication labels to enhance effective communication between staff and older adults of various literacy and language capabilities. In addition, pharmacy education programmes could incorporate training in multilingual communication to provide students with early exposure to interactions with patients from diverse language backgrounds.
