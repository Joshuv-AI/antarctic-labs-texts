Referral, enrollment, and health care use in a comprehensive patient-centered management program for osteoarthritis of the hip and knee
Authors: Trevor A Lentz, Preston Roundy, Emily Poehlein, Cynthia L Green, Richard C Mather, William Jiranek
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveOsteoarthritis management programs (OAMPs) have become a more common way to deliver patient-centered care. However, there is limited information on real-world use of these programs to guide implementation, payment policy, accessibility, and scaling in the United States. This paper describes 5-year use metrics for the Duke Joint Health Program, an OAMP embedded within a US academic health system.MethodThis analysis includes patients referred into the Program between October 2017 and April 2022. We generated descriptive statistics of referral and enrollment totals, demographics and patient-reported measures of enrollees, retention and healthcare use metrics (e.g., office visit frequency), and data capture rates for patient-reported outcomes.ResultsDuring the study period, 6863 patients were referred to the program and 4162 (61 ​%) enrolled. We observed statistically significant differences between those who did and did not enroll by age (mean difference ​± ​SE: 2.49 ​± ​2.8 years), sex (70.0 ​% vs 67.7 ​% female), race (65.1 ​% vs 55.3 ​% Caucasian/White), employment status (50.0 ​% vs 40.2 ​% retired), and insurance type (53.5 ​% vs 47.0 ​% Medicare). The median (Q1, Q3) number of visits was 2 (1, 4) and ranged from 1 to 67. The median (Q1, Q3) number of days from first to last program visit was 23 (0, 84) days. Questionnaire completion rates were 72 ​% at baseline, 46 ​% at 6 weeks, 39 ​% at 3 months, and 40 ​% at 6 and 12 months.ConclusionFindings can guide the planning, development, and implementation of future OAMPs and inform policies to ensure programs are accessible and equitable.Keywords: Multidisciplinary, Comprehensive care, Health services research, Utilization

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) management has evolved considerably over the last few decades, with clinical practice guidelines now emphasizing comprehensive, patient-centered care that addresses physical, psychological and social needs [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6]]. This approach has been driven, in part, by an improved understanding that joint disease severity explains only modest amounts of variation in OA-related pain and disability [7,8]. Consequently, comprehensive OA management programs (OAMPs) have grown significantly, as have initiatives such as the Osteoarthritis Research Society International Joint Effort Initiative focused on implementing such programs [2,9,10]. Osteoarthritis management programs (OAMPs) are operationally defined as a model of evidence-based, non-surgical OA care that has been implemented in a real-world setting, and comprises the following four components: 1) personalized OA care; 2) provided as a package of care with longitudinal reassessment and progression; 3) comprising two or more components of the core, non-surgical, non-pharmacological interventions like education, exercise, and weight-loss and; 4) optional evidence-based adjunctive treatments as required (e.g., assistive devices, psychosocial support) [2].

As OA management has evolved, so too has the landscape of healthcare payment in the United States (US). New payment models that incentivize value of care are gradually aligning to better support OAMPs [[11], [12], [13], [14]]. As value-based care models gain prominence, the need for real-world data on the use and outcomes of OAMPs become increasingly important for their growth and sustainment. There are numerous examples of OAMP implementation and scaling outside the US, particularly in Europe [[15], [16], [17], [18]]. However, data on implementation within the US, where the predominant third-party payer system has unique impacts on health care access and use, is sparse. Understanding how patients are referred to and enrolled in these programs, participant demographics, health care use patterns, and the completeness of data for outcome assessments within US healthcare settings is vital for guiding OAMP implementation.

Publishing real-world OAMP data is important for several reasons. First, it guides healthcare systems in allocating resources, staff, and budgets to meet program demands. Second, it benefits healthcare payers by providing data needed for benefit design and case rate setting, addressing common barriers to implementing comprehensive care programs for chronic conditions like OA in the US [19] Third, real-world data helps identify disparities in OAMP use, ensuring these programs are equitable and accessible. Additionally, such data highlights opportunities for growth and expansion of OAMPs.

Therefore, our goal is to describe the 5-year use metrics for one OAMP, the Duke Joint Health Program (hereafter, the Program) [20], embedded within an academic Health System in the US. The Program is structured around delivery of conservative care, including: education; nutrition guidance and weight management; physical activity and exercise; cognitive behavioral theory-based strategies for managing psychological distress; functional training; and sleep hygiene. In the Program, physical therapists with specialized training in cognitive behavioral-theory based strategy function as the central care provider (i.e., the Primary Osteoarthritis Provider, or POP) that delivers evidence-based interventions and coordinates care within a multi-disciplinary network of specialty providers. The specific metrics we aim to describe include referral and enrollment patterns, characteristics (e.g., demographics, patient-reported outcomes) of individuals that enroll in the program, retention and health care use metrics, including the frequency of office visits, and data capture rates for patient-reported outcomes.

Methods

Setting

The Program was co-developed in October of 2017 by the Department of Orthopaedic Surgery in the Duke University School of Medicine and the Department of Physical Therapy and Occupational Therapy in the Duke University Health System. Since inception, the program has grown to 31 POPs in 15 ambulatory physical therapy clinics. The Program operates within a not-for-profit health system comprised of 3 hospitals, 14 ambulatory orthopaedic clinic sites, and 17 ambulatory physical therapy clinics located in Durham, North Carolina and the surrounding region. As of fiscal year 2023, the health system attracted nearly 5 million outpatient visits. The health system serves as a regional healthcare hub, attracting many patients from beyond the immediate area. As of 2022, Raleigh-Durham-Cary region of North Carolina had a population of approximately 2.19 million, with a median age of 37.8 years. The racial composition was 57 ​% White, 21 ​% Black, and 6 ​% Asian. Eleven percent identify as Hispanic ethnicity. The region's economic status was above average, reflected in a per capita income of $46,696 and a median household income of $87,180.

Intervention and treatment monitoring

Within the health system, individuals with hip or knee symptoms undergo initial evaluation and care provided by either an orthopaedic or primary care provider. Patients diagnosed with hip and/or knee OA and deemed suitable for comprehensive OA management are offered a referral to the Program. Although the Program is open to any patient with an OA diagnosis, at present it is considered most suitable for patients who 1) are not indicated for immediate joint replacement, 2) have multiple physical, psychological or behavioral-related health needs, and/or 3) prefer comprehensive management. The Program commences with an assessment of the participant's medical and surgical history, pain levels, disability, physical activity, functional limitations, nutritional status, weight management needs, sleep patterns, psychological well-being, behavioral health, and personal goals and expectations [20]. Upon enrolling in the Program, participants begin with a clinic-based active treatment phase. During these clinic visits, POPs tailor interventions to individual needs, which may include, exercise programs, self-management strategies, nutritional and weight loss guidance, pain coping skills, sleep hygiene education, gait and functional training, neuromuscular re-education, manual therapy, and modalities. Initially, participants engage with their POP every 1–3 weeks to develop a personalized program. As they progress, visit frequency decreases to once every 4–8 weeks. Some participants may only want a single visit for home exercise and self-management education, often because they prefer less structured management or live outside the immediate area and cannot regularly travel for appointments. Between visits, POPs maintain remote communication via phone, email, or Epic MyChart, ensuring contact every 3–4 weeks. Once participants achieve self-management autonomy, they transition to the long-term management phase, which lasts about a year. During this phase, POPs follow up via phone or email at 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, and 1 year after the last clinic visit.

Study population

To optimize generalizability, we have taken a broad approach to defining the cohort included in this analysis. To be included in this analysis, eligibility criteria were 1) diagnosed with OA or the sequelae of OA as the cause of hip or knee symptoms, and 2) referred to Program before April 1, 2022. The first criterion excludes those that would be inappropriate referrals into the program (e.g., a primary diagnosis of patellar fracture), which occurs occasionally. The second criterion was established to develop a cohort that was at least one year out from their initial Program evaluation by the time we retrieved data for the analysis.

Data collection

As part of standard care, patient-reported outcome measures for pain, joint function, and general and pain-related mental health are collected via questionnaires at baseline. These measures are collected again at 6 weeks and 3, 6, and 12 months. Each week, a team of Program administrators uses an electronic health record (Epic) report to identify the next week's scheduled evaluations. Once identified, questionnaires are sent via email (Qualtrics, Provo, UT, US) at baseline and at all other time points. During this same process, the Program administrator runs a report to identify all unfinished, incomplete, or un-opened questionnaires from the week prior. The list is organized and emailed to each individual POP with instructions to personally contact the patient via phone or email during each POPs designated administrative time. POPs are also encouraged to collect questionnaires by tablet or paper if patients have not completed baseline questionnaires prior to their clinic visit.

Pain intensity

Baseline pain intensity is assessed with a numerical pain rating scale (with scores from 0 [no pain] to 10 [worst pain imaginable]), and participants rated their current pain at each follow-up visit.

Joint function

The Hip Disability and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score, Joint Replacement (HOOS JR) is a patient-reported survey measuring overall hip health and was originally designed to evaluate pain, function, and activities of daily living post joint replacement surgery [21]. Similarly, the Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score, Joint Replacement (KOOS JR) is a survey measuring overall knee health for individuals post total knee replacement by evaluating stiffness, pain, function, and activities of daily living [22]. The range for both HOOS JR and KOOS JR is 0–100, with higher scores reflecting higher function. All items are scored based on a 7-day recall period. KOOS subscale scores can be categorized into on average no, on average mild, on average moderate, on average severe, and on average extreme difficulty/pain, using cut-off values of 12.5, 37.5, 62.5, and 87.5, respectively.

General and pain-related mental health

The 10-item Optimal Screening for Prediction of Referral and Outcome Yellow Flag (OSPRO-YF) Assessment Tool evaluates general and pain-related mental health [23]. The OSPRO-YF is used because it 1) demonstrates good predictive validity for disability, chronic pain development, quality of life, and healthcare use among people with musculoskeletal conditions [[24], [25], [26], [27]], 2) efficiently assesses multiple mood-related and pain-related psychological domains with low response burden [28], and 3) was designed for use in rehabilitation settings [23]. The OSPRO-YF assesses 11 distinct general mental health (depression, anxiety, anger) and pain-specific mental health constructs (fear avoidance [work & activity], catastrophizing, kinesiophobia, pain anxiety, activity self-efficacy, pain self-efficacy, pain acceptance). We use the simple summary score method, which involves summing all item responses from the tool, with pain resilience items reverse scored [28]. Simple summary scores range from 3 to 53, with higher scores indicating higher or more significant mental health needs. Although no score thresholds have been established for the OSPRO-YF, the original cohort study among people seeking physical therapy for neck, shoulder, knee or back pain found a mean (standard deviation) score of 17.4 (6.7) points [29].

Data export procedures

All data were securely exported from one of three sources and linked by patient identifier. The three sources included an administrative referrals and enrollment database specifically designed for the Program, Qualtrics survey database, and Epic database.

Statistical analysis

Using the referrals and enrollment database, all referred participants were identified for further analysis. Of those who were referred, the number of participants who enrolled was enumerated, as well as the number of patients with Qualtrics outcomes survey and/or Epic data available. For the purposes of this study, enrollment was defined as having at least one completed Program visit. Demographic characteristics were compared between those who did and did not ultimately enroll in the program after being referred.

To characterize patterns of healthcare use, we enumerated the number of visits with a POP, defined as the number of unique visit dates from Epic, per patient. Additionally, we identified the last POP visit and calculated the number of days from first POP visit to last POP visit, representing the amount of time a patient was engaged in clinician-guided treatments. To better understand factors associated with higher use of the program, demographic characteristics and baseline questionnaire scores were compared between those who had only one program visit and those who had more than one visit.

Some participants ultimately received arthroplasty following Program enrollment. For patients who received an arthroplasty, we counted the number of days from enrollment to surgery. The cumulative incidence of surgery following Program enrollment was calculated using Kaplan-Meier time-to-event analysis, with patients who did not require surgery censored at one-year or their last follow-up timepoint, whichever was longer, as time since first visit is at least 1 year for all patients. Estimates are presented with 95 ​% confidence interval.

Lastly, we summarized patient-reported outcome questionnaire completion rates by follow-up time based on data availability, given the importance of routine outcomes assessments in OAMPs [30]. Data completeness was summarized and compared by sociodemographic categories to identify potential disparities.

Continuous variables were summarized using mean ​± ​standard deviation (SD), median with 25th and 75th percentiles (Q1, Q3), and range. Categorical variables were summarized using counts and percentages of non-missing values. Where applicable, groups were compared with T-tests for continuous variables (age) and chi-squared tests for categorical variables. SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC) was used for all analyses, and a p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Referral and enrollment rates

During the observation period, 6863 unique participants received a Program referral (Fig. 2). Of those referred to the Program, 2701 (39.4 ​%) did not enroll in the program. Of those not enrolling, 997 (36.9 ​%) never scheduled an appointment, 865 (32.0 ​%) had an appointment scheduled but were never seen for a Program evaluation, and 839 (31.1 ​%) were seen by a physical therapist but not within the Program. The remaining 4162 patients had documented evidence of being evaluated in the program, resulting in a referral-to-enrollment ratio of 0.61:1 or 61 ​%.

Flow diagram for developing final analytic dataset.

During this timeframe, a total of 188 unique providers submitted a Program referral. Of these, 88 providers submitted only one referral, 75 providers submitted between two and 49 referrals, and 25 providers submitted 50 or more referrals. The median (Q1, Q3) number of Program referrals was 2.0 (1.0, 13.8).

Program retention

The distribution of POP visits was highly skewed. The median (Q1, Q3) number of visits with a POP was 2 (1, 4), and the number of visits ranged from 1 to 67 (Fig. 3). The median (Q1, Q3) number of days from first to last POP visit was 23 (0, 84) days (Fig. 4). Older age, female sex, identifying as White, being retired, and having Medicare insurance were associated with more visits once enrolled (Supplemental Table 1), with the biggest group differences in sex and race. There were no clinically meaningful differences in questionnaire scores between those with one visit versus multiple visits. Of those enrolled in the program, 451 (10.8 ​%) had an arthroplasty procedure following enrollment, with the median (Q1, Q3) number of days from enrollment to surgery being 175 (30, 490) days. The cumulative incidence of surgery at 1 year was 9.9 ​% (95 ​% CI: 8.9–11.0 ​%).

Data completeness of qualtrics questionnaires

Of the 4162 patients enrolled in the Program, 3428 (82.4 ​%) completed at least one questionnaire. Questionnaire completion rate after baseline (among eligible patients) was highest at 6 weeks (46.1 ​%) and then remained relatively constant during follow-up at 3 months (38.6 ​%), 6 months (39.5 ​%) and 12 months (39.6 ​%).

Only 9.1 ​% of participants (N ​= ​311) completed all Qualtrics questionnaires that were available to them at each timepoint. Patients that completed all available follow-up questionnaires were, on average, older than patients who did not (mean ​± ​SD: 66.3 ​± ​9.1 vs 62.9 ​± ​10.8 years, respectively). Black/African American patients comprised a larger proportion of those with incomplete follow-up compared to those with complete follow-up (30.7 ​% vs 12.7 ​%, respectively) (Table 3).

Discussion

This study describes real-world use metrics of over 6800 patients referred to a US-based OAMP during the course of its development and maintenance. Although implementation experiences for other non-US based OAMPs have been extensively documented, this has not been the case for US-based programs. As an interdisciplinary, patient-centered program with shared decisions on visit frequency and program length between POP and patient, we observed a median of 2 clinic visits, and 23 days from first to last POP visit. The number of clinic visits is similar to numbers reported by another US-based OAMP [32], however treatment duration in our Program was notably lower (23 days vs 188 days), likely due to differences in how episode duration was defined. Within our Program, approximately 89 ​% of participants did not proceed to arthroplasty for at least one year following program initiation. Although promising, we are unable to directly compare arthroplasty rates to people who do not participate in the program, and therefore cannot infer any potential risk reduction due to participation.

Approximately 40 ​% of patients who were referred did not enroll in the Program. This proportion is higher than in other documented OAMPs [33], however it should be noted that other programs have a different design (e.g., referral from a general medical practitioner) and were outside of the US with different insurance-related access considerations. Reasons for non-enrollment in our program were not tracked but are likely multifactorial. They could include patients deciding to seek a different form of care, hesitancy by patients to engage in what might be perceived as more time-consuming care, fear of stigma related to care that has a psychological and behavioral focus, financial or transportation constraints, or inappropriate referrals. As our health system is a quaternary medical center, our referral base often lives outside of the immediate geographic area, which could also preclude initiation of the program. Strategies that may help to boost enrollment rates include 1) giving referring providers standard language to describe the program and its scope, 2) use of non-stigmatizing language when discussing psychologically focused aspects of the program (e.g., coaching versus mental health care), and 3) educating referring providers on who may be most appropriate for the program.

We observed statistically significant differences in enrollment rates based on key demographics. The most notable and likely meaningful difference was by race. Only 54.2 ​% of Black or African American patients referred to the program enrolled, while 64.6 ​% of referred Caucasian/White patients enrolled. Reasons for disparate use are not clear, but may be due to factors already known to disproportionately limit care among certain populations. These factors include limited insurance coverage and/or high co-pays, transportation challenges, and limited flexibility to schedule clinic visits during normal working hours [34]. Although the Program was designed to streamline referrals and access to comprehensive care, complexities around communication of a novel program and healthcare system navigation could also drive disparate use.

Other considerations such as therapeutic alliance and lack of race representation, an acknowledged limitation of the Program, are potential drivers of low program enrollment and engagement. Although a substantial percentage of Program patients are African American and/or Black, no African American and/or Black physical therapists were working as POPs during the data collection time period. This issue is not Program-specific. At a national level the percentage of African American and/or Black physical therapists is consistently lower than the percentage of the patient population seeking physical therapy [35]. Ensuring representativeness of health care providers, alongside reducing scheduling and access complexities, are strategies that may reduce disparate care in OAMPs.

Although we did not track all OA patients in our health system during the observation period, our annual census suggests that a small proportion of eligible patients were referred to the program. Changing established referral behaviors took time, but enrollment grew as new POPs were added and more providers became aware of the program. The low referral rates may indicate unfamiliarity with the program, lack of awareness that comprehensive care is appropriate for OA, or other reasons. While higher referral rates are a goal, we had to consider the strain on POPs and specialty providers like psychologists and dieticians. Wait times for psychological consults often exceeded 3 months, reflecting broader gaps in US mental health services [36]. We addressed this by integrating cognitive behavioral interventions into POP care and using the OSPRO-YF to triage psychological consults, reserving them for patients with pressing needs. POPs also educated patients on weight management due to long wait times for dietician services. Systems aiming to build and scale similar OAMPs should plan for specialty care provider demand, a common challenge for integrated care models [37].

Many program enrollees attended only one or two visits. Our analyses show some of the same demographic trends in low visit use as we saw for low program enrollment. The number of visits a patient attends is ultimately at the patient's discretion and, single visits are most often the result of 1) inappropriate referral that isn't identified until the initial visit, or 2) patient requesting a single visit because of long travel times or some other reason. An acknowledged limitation is that we could not account for visits outside of POP care or all remote follow-ups (by phone or Epic message/MyChart). Therefore it would be possible, for example, for the patient to see a POP once, followed by multiple visits to weight management. This scenario would reflect a single visit in our analysis.

Another major challenge that is not unique to the Program is difficulty with follow-up data collection using clinical workflows and resources [38]. Other OAMP studies have reported one-year follow-up data collection rates of between 44 ​% and 86 ​%, with some programs collecting follow-up data through program-specific registries [[15], [16], [17],39,40]. Our follow-up rates (approximately 39–46 ​%) are at the lower end of that range, but consistent with other non-OAMP studies that show more modest follow-up data collection rates (e.g., 52 ​% at 3 months and 25 ​% at one year) using clinical staff and workflows [38,41]. We intentionally do not report on questionnaire scores in this paper because making inferences on the effectiveness of the program would require a different study design. Rather, we want to report on data collection rates for a health care system-based program in the US that is not part of an established registry. Other US-based programs may be developed in a similar manner, and our findings suggest data collection infrastructure should be prioritized as part of a comprehensive implementation plan.

Readers should consider a few limitations of this analysis. First, the Program is embedded within a large academic medical center, which provided strong administrative support. The academic health system environment also provides many resources (e.g., referral options) than might be unavailable in smaller healthcare systems. Some OAMPs are predominantly or entirely delivered online or through telehealth, unlike our clinic-based Program. Other programs offer structured modules and standardized interventions (e.g. 12 supervised group exercise sessions twice a week for six weeks). OAMPs vary widely in design and scope, so the findings in this study are most relevant to programs delivered in a similar manner and environment. Second, we were unable to accurately track referrals to specialty care (e.g., psychologists, dieticians) in the Program. Although some information is available in Epic, we deemed the data too unreliable to accurately account for all referrals and healthcare service use. As a lesson learned, other healthcare systems interested in developing an OAMP should build strategies for accurately and reliably tracking referrals, use of services, and outcomes of interest outside of the program. Finally, the Program operates within the US healthcare system, which has different implementation barriers than systems outside the US. Most other documented OAMPs are outside of the US and the extent to which our findings are relevant for programs in other countries is unclear.
