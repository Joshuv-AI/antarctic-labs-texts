Reconsidering pancreatic masses: A case of IgG4-related autoimmune pancreatitis mimicking malignancy
Authors: Abdelwahed Diani, Mariam Benzalim, Mohammed Rida Bouroumane, Soumaya Alj
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutoimmune pancreatitis (AIP) is an uncommon, immune-mediated form of chronic pancreatitis characterized by lymphoplasmacytic infiltration, fibrosis, and frequently elevated serum IgG4 levels. Clinically and radiologically, AIP often mimics pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, posing a substantial diagnostic challenge that risks unnecessary surgical interventions. This article presents the case of a 53-year-old female patient with epigastric pain, obstructive jaundice, and a pancreatic head mass initially suggestive of malignancy. Detailed imaging (CT, MRI, MRCP), serological evaluation showing elevated IgG4, and a favorable response to corticosteroid therapy confirmed the diagnosis of type 1 AIP. The case underscores the importance of accurately differentiating AIP from pancreatic malignancies by integrating clinical data, imaging features—particularly the absence of significant double-duct dilatation characteristic of pancreatic cancer—the presence of renal lesions, which represent an unusual metastatic site in pancreatic malignancies, serological markers, histopathological findings, and steroid responsiveness. Early diagnosis is crucial to avoid unwarranted surgery, initiate appropriate immunosuppressive treatment, and ensure favorable long-term outcomes. This report contributes to the growing body of evidence highlighting AIP’s diagnostic complexity and underscores the necessity for a multidisciplinary approach in its management.Keywords: Autoimmune pancreatitis, IgG4-related disease, Pancreatic cancer mimic, Steroid therapy, Diagnostic imaging

Introduction

Autoimmune pancreatitis (AIP) is a rare form of chronic pancreatitis resulting from an abnormal immune-mediated process. In 1961, Sarles et al first reported a case of nonalcoholic chronic pancreatitis accompanied by hypergammaglobulinemia and predicted its association with an autoimmune process [1,2]. In 1995, Yoshida et al. were the first to introduce the term by reporting a case of chronic pancreatitis that responded to steroid therapy [[1], [2], [3]].

From a pathological standpoint, AIP is characterized by a dense lymphoplasmacytic infiltration of the pancreatic parenchyma associated with progressive fibrosis. In type 1 AIP (IgG4‑related), also referred to as lymphoplasmacytic sclerosing pancreatitis, the inflammatory infiltrate is rich in IgG4‑positive plasma cells and is typically accompanied by storiform (“whorled”) fibrosis and obliterative phlebitis involving medium‑ and small‑sized veins. A diagnostic hallmark of type 1 AIP is the presence of more than 10 IgG4‑positive plasma cells per high‑power field, often far exceeding this minimal threshold in typical cases, together with an IgG4/IgG plasma cell ratio >40%. These histopathological features, in combination with elevated serum IgG4 levels and extrapancreatic organ involvement, form the basis of contemporary diagnostic criteria for IgG4‑related AIP [4,5,6].

AIP is a rare disease, with a reported incidence of less than 1 case per 100,000 population per year; it is more common in middle-aged to older adults [4,7].

AIP and pancreatic cancer share overlapping clinical and radiological features. Both conditions present with common symptoms such as abdominal pain, unexplained weight loss, obstructive jaundice, fatigue, and a focal mass on imaging. As a result, AIP is frequently misdiagnosed as pancreatic cancer at initial presentation [[4], [5], [7]].

Imaging plays a pivotal role in differentiating autoimmune pancreatitis (AIP) from pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), particularly through the identification of characteristic features such as the absence of significant upstream ductal dilatation, delayed enhancement patterns, and the presence of extrapancreatic manifestations. However, imaging findings alone are often insufficient to establish a definitive diagnosis due to substantial overlap with malignant lesions. This diagnostic uncertainty highlights an important clinical challenge and underscores the need for a comprehensive, multidisciplinary approach integrating clinical, serological, histopathological, and therapeutic response data. In this context, we report a case of AIP closely mimicking pancreatic cancer, emphasizing the contribution of multimodal imaging and the importance of accurate diagnosis to avoid unnecessary surgical intervention.

Case report

This is a 53-year-old female patient, cholecystectomy 11 years ago, presenting with epigastric pain and vomiting for 6 months In a context of general health deterioration characterized by weight loss, anorexia, and fatigue. Clinical examination reveals minimal cutaneous and mucosal jaundice, abdominal tenderness predominantly in the epigastric region, with normal temperature and blood pressure. A laboratory work-up was performed showing biological cholestasis associated with conjugated hyperbilirubinemia, without signs of major infection and a normal lipase and a borderline elevated CA 19-9 level measured at 42 U/mL.

The patient underwent an emergency thoraco-abdomino-pelvic CT scan, highly suggestive of pancreatic head malignancy (Fig. 1). The patient completed the workup with an endoscopic ultrasound-guided biopsy, which revealed a 4 cm hypoechoic mass in the pancreatic head, invading the portal vein and the common bile duct. The histopathological analysis was inconclusive, showing hemorrhage with no evidence of malignant cells.

Axial abdominal CT scan without (A) and with (B) contrast injection demonstrating a pancreatic head mass (arrow), isodense, with homogeneous enhancement after contrast administration, measuring 42 mm in greatest diameter.

Given the discordance between radiological findings and histopathological results, the patient was referred to us for additional MRI evaluation. MRI demonstrated a tumefied and globular appearance of the pancreatic head, containing a homogeneous lesion with enhancement similar to that of the pancreatic parenchyma, no diffusion restriction, and no dilatation of the main bile duct or pancreatic duct. We also noted a diffuse, non-stenosing parietal thickening of the main bile duct with progressive gadolinium enhancement. In addition, bilateral renal nodules were observed on MRI (Fig. 2).

Pancreatic MRI in axial T1 LAVA sequences without (A) and after Gadolinium injection (B, D), and in coronal view (C): showing a mass-like lesion in the pancreatic head measuring 37 mm in greatest diameter (white arrows), parietal thickening of the main bile duct (black arrow), and multiple renal lesions (arrowheads).

Based on these findings, a chronic inflammatory origin was considered, notably autoimmune pancreatitis as first-line diagnosis, without completely ruling out a neoplastic origin. Initial CT images were reviewed and showed that the renal lesions and bile duct wall thickening were already present (Fig. 3).

Initial CT scan in axial slices (A, B, and C) after contrast injection, showing thickening of the wall of the main bile duct (black arrow), renal lesions (arrowheads), and the presence of a peripancreatic pseudocapsule (white arrows).

An IgG4 level was requested and found to be markedly elevated (291 mg/dL). The patient was started on corticosteroid therapy as a therapeutic trial with an initial dose of 30 mg per day administered for 4 weeks. After 2 weeks of corticosteroid therapy, complete resolution of vomiting and epigastric pain was observed.

A follow-up MRI performed after 7 weeks of therapy demonstrated a regression of the pancreatic swelling and lesion, as well as regression of the bile duct wall thickening and a decrease in the size and number of renal lesions (Fig. 4). The final diagnosis retained was IgG4-related autoimmune pancreatitis.

Follow-up gadolinium-enhanced T1-weighted MRI: Axial (A) and coronal (B) images demonstrate significant regression of the mass in the pancreatic head and common bile duct wall thickening.

Discussion

AIP is recognized as part of the IgG4-related disease spectrum, a fibroinflammatory process capable of affecting multiple organs [8,9]. It is a rare condition, with a prevalence of less than 1/100,000, since the evolution of diagnostic means (radiological, histological, and serological), the number of patients diagnosed with AIP, an uncommon autoimmune disease in clinical practice, has increased [1,10]. Recent studies have suggested several possible pathogenic factors in the development of AIP, though its pathogenic mechanism remains unclear. Based on genetic factors, disease-specific or related antigens, innate and adaptive immunity may be involved [11].

AIP can manifest as a focal mass in the pancreatic head, sometimes with biliary obstruction that mimics pancreatic adenocarcinoma [8,12]. AIP was ultimately diagnosed in 27% of patients who underwent the Whipple procedure after being initially suspected of having pancreatic adenocarcinoma [13]. This is a significant percentage that should not be overlooked, as surgery is not a simple and safe clinical procedure, but carries risk of many postoperative complications and risks for the patient. Fortunately, in our case, we were able to spare our patient a major surgical intervention and its associated risks and complications.

According to the International Consensus Diagnostic Criteria (ICDC) for AIP were created in 2011 to help in the diagnosis of AIP. These criteria include serological, histological, radiological findings, involvement of a non-pancreatic organ, and sensitivity to corticosteroids. The ICDC also separates AIP into 2 types: Type 1, which usually has high levels of IgG4 in the blood and affects other organs outside the pancreas, and Type 2, which does not involve IgG4 and may show special changes called granulocytic epithelial lesions (GEL) in the pancreatic tissue [1,4,11,12]. Recently, type 3 AIP has been identified as a new subtype associated with immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy used in cancer treatment [[1], [13], [14]].

The symptoms of AIP are often unclear and not specific, which makes it hard to diagnose the disease based on symptoms alone. Type 1 AIP is usually diagnosed in older adults, with most patients being over 60 years old [15,16]. Obstructive painless jaundice and upper abdominal pain are the most common complaints. Other less common symptoms include weight loss, feeling tired, or sometimes no symptoms at all [17]. Our patient presented with epigastric pain, vomiting, and cutaneo-mucosal jaundice, in a context of general health deterioration.

The biliary tract is the most commonly involved extrapancreatic site, which probably explains why there is painless jaundice in AIP patients [18,19,20] . AIP can cause others extrapancreatic lesions including, retroperitoneal fibrosis, lachrymal and salivary gland lesions, pulmonary lesions including hilar lymphadenopathy, and tubulointerstitial nephritis, hypophysitis, chronic thyroiditis, and prostatitis [18,21,17,22]. In our case, there is involvement of the main bile duct and renal involvement.

Laboratory findings can assist in the diagnosis, particularly in cases where distinguishing autoimmune pancreatitis (AIP) from pancreatic cancer presents a challenge. Serum IgG4 levels are frequently elevated in most cases of type 1 AIP; however, this marker alone is generally insufficient to confirm the diagnosis. A study published in 2001 by Hamano et al. suggested that a serum IgG4 level of 135 mg/dL had an accuracy of 97%, a sensitivity of 95%, and a specificity of 97%. Similarly, a study conducted by the Mayo Clinic in 2007 reported that an IgG4 level of 140 mg/dL showed a sensitivity of 93% but a specificity of only 36% [1,23,24].

In cases where differential diagnosis with pancreatic cancer is particularly difficult, CA19-9 levels may be helpful. Recent studies have evaluated the diagnostic value of CA19-9 in differentiating AIP from pancreatic cancer. For instance, van Heerde et al. reported that low CA19-9 levels tend to favor a diagnosis of AIP over malignancy [1,25]. Taken individually, serum IgG4 and CA19-9 levels have limited diagnostic value. However, it has been reported that when used together, their combined sensitivity and specificity may offer useful clinical value [25,26].

Imaging is essential for diagnosing autoimmune pancreatitis (AIP) and differentiating it from pancreatic cancer. AIP should be considered only after excluding pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), which is more common. CT and MRI are the modalities of choice and are most commonly used in the characterization of AIP [1,27].

Three distinct morphologic patterns of involvement have been described: difuse (up to 50–70%), focal (up to 30% in type 1 and 80% in type 2), and multifocal forms (5%) [[27], [28], [29], [30]]. Difuse enlargement of the pancreas, also known as sausage-shaped pancreas with delayed enhancement, is the most characteristic fnding in AIP with loss of lobulation (featureless pancreas) [ 26]. The capsule rim sign, seen in 30%-40% of AIP cases, is highly specific and reflects inflammatory cell infiltration and fibrosis. It appears hypointense on T1 and T2 with delayed enhancement on MRI [1,27,[31], [32], [33]]. Focal disease is less common than diffuse disease and manifests as a focal mass, often with involvement of the pancreatic head, an appearance that may mimic that of pancreatic malignancy. Focal disease tends to be relatively well demarcated, and, when present, upstream dilatation of the main pancreatic duct is typically milder than in patients with pancreatic carcinoma [1,27,30,34].

The study by Choi et al. showed that homogeneous enhancement, the duct-penetrating sign, and low ADC (<0.94×10⁻³ mm²/s) are key predictors distinguishing AIP from PDAC. The presence of at least 2 criteria provides high diagnostic accuracy (specificity 98.7%), reaching 100% specificity when all 3 are present. Additional features favoring AIP include a capsule-like rim, absence of necrosis, and less frequent vascular or peripancreatic fat invasion [35].

Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) is a noninvasive, non-ionizing imaging technique that plays a key role in detecting ductal abnormalities associated with AIP. MRCP may reveal a long segmental stricture of the pancreatic duct (involving more than one-third of its length), absence of upstream dilatation, multiple strictures, and strictures causing side branch ectasia [1,27,36]. A focal narrowing of the intrapancreatic portion of the common bile duct (CBD), accompanied by wall thickening and enhancement, may also be present and is typically indicative of IgG4-related sclerosing cholangitis. Imaging signs such as the “penetrating duct sign” or “icicle sign”—which represent smooth, tapered narrowing of the pancreatic duct—are suggestive of benign strictures seen in AIP [27,37]. A reduction or normalization of duct caliber on follow-up imaging is an early indicator of steroid responsiveness [27,28].

Additionally, EUS-FNA aids in pancreatic cancer diagnosis but requires expertise. Therefore, core-needle biopsy is preferred to ensure adequate tissue sampling [1,38]. Lastly, Tru-cut biopsy enhances sensitivity [39].

On imaging, various extrapancreatic lesions can support the diagnosis AIP. Sclerosing cholangitis is characterized by thickening of the bile duct walls with increased contrast enhancement. Retroperitoneal fibrosis typically appears on CT as a soft tissue density encasing the abdominal aorta or the bilateral common iliac arteries. Interstitial nephritis presents as areas of poor contrast enhancement and appears hypointense on T2-weighted MRI images. Additional findings may include inflammation of the lacrimal or salivary glands, interstitial pneumonia, and lymphadenopathy. Although these manifestations are not consistently observed, their presence raises the suspicion of AIP [27,29,30].

First-line therapy for autoimmune pancreatitis involves oral corticosteroids (0.4–0.6 mg/kg/d) for 1–2 months, followed by gradual tapering over several months to maintain remission and minimize adverse effects [4,40]. In cases of corticosteroid resistance or treatment failure, alternative immunosuppressive therapies may be employed, including azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil, or rituximab [4,40,41]. Biliary drainage may be needed for obstructive jaundice in AIP (autoimmune pancreatitis). Most patients have a good long-term prognosis with treatment, but relapses occur in up to 50% of cases, requiring ongoing monitoring [4,41].

Conclusion

This case illustrates key radiologic pitfalls that may lead to misdiagnosis of AIP as pancreatic cancer, particularly the presence of a focal pancreatic head mass with suspected vascular involvement. The absence of significant pancreatic duct dilatation and the presence of homogeneous enhancement were atypical for malignancy and should raise suspicion for AIP. Bilateral renal lesions represented another major pitfall, as they may mimic metastases but instead reflect IgG4-related disease. Recognizing these extrapancreatic manifestations and subtle imaging features is essential to avoid diagnostic errors and unnecessary surgery.
