Global Maxwell Tomography Using the Volume-Surface Integral Equation for Improved Estimation of Electrical Properties
Ilias I. Giannakopoulos , José E. Cruz Serrallés , Jan Paška, Martijn A. Cloos, Ryan Brown, Riccardo Lattanzi

Abstract
Objective: Global Maxwell Tomography (GMT) is a noninvasive inverse optimization method for the estimation of electrical properties (EP) from magnetic resonance (MR) measurements. GMT uses the volume integral equation (VIE) in the forward problem and assumes that the sample has negligible effect on the coil currents. Consequently, GMT calculates the coil’s incident fields with an initial EP distribution and keeps them constant for all optimization iterations. This can lead to erroneous reconstructions. This work introduces a novel version of GMT that replaces VIE with the volume-surface integral equation (VSIE), which recalculates the coil currents at every iteration based on updated EP estimates before computing the associated fields. Methods: We simulated an 8-channel transceiver coil array for 7 T brain imaging and reconstructed the EP of a realistic head model using VSIE-based GMT. We built the coil, collected experimental MR measurements, and reconstructed EP of a two-compartment phantom. Results: In simulations, VSIE-based GMT outperformed VIE-based GMT by at least 12% for both EP . In experiments, the relative difference with respect to probe-measured EP values in the inner (outer) compartment was 13% (26%) and 17% (33%) for the permittivity and conductivity, respectively. Conclusion: The use of VSIE over VIE enhances GMT’s performance by accounting for the effect of the EP on the coil currents. Significance: VSIE-based GMT does not rely on an initial EP estimate, rendering it more suitable for experimental reconstructions compared to the VIE-based GMT.

I
Introduction

The interactions of electromagnetic (EM) waves with biological tissue are determined by the underlying electrical properties (EP) of tissue
[
1
,
2
]
. Access to EP at MRI frequencies could enable the estimation of local specific absorption rate maps
[
3
,
4
]
, which are important for ultra-high field MRI
[
5
]
, the accurate planning of radiofrequency (RF) and thermal-based treatments
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
, and could lead to novel biomarkers for pathologies and treatment monitoring
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
. Several techniques have been proposed to estimate EP from MR-measurements by inverting the differential form of the Helmholtz wave equation
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
. Although fast, these methods result in artifacts associated with taking numerical derivatives of noisy data. Techniques based on the integral form of Maxwell’s equations
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
are immune to these artifacts because the integration averages out additive noise. Despite showing superior accuracy in simulation, their experimental validation has been challenging because they require knowledge of the RF fields generated by the transmit coils (i.e., incident fields) to solve the forward problem. Given that the precise estimation of these incident fields is impractical, simplifying assumptions are usually made for their calculation
[
16
,
19
]
.

Global Maxwell Tomography (GMT) is a technique for EP mapping based on the integral form of Maxwell’s equations. GMT solves an iterative inverse problem to reconstruct the relative permittivity and electric conductivity of an object, using measurements of the transmit magnetic field
[
16
,
20
]
. GMT relies on the volume integral equation (VIE) method for the forward problem
[
21
]
and on an ad-hoc regularization approach to mitigate noise in the measurements
[
22
]
. GMT demonstrated excellent performance in simulations with realistic anatomies
[
17
]
and in experiments for homogeneous phantoms
[
16
]
. Its application to heterogeneous samples has been limited by the above-mentioned required knowledge of the incident fields. Specifically, in GMT, the incident fields are calculated once for an estimated EP distribution and then used throughout the optimization, assuming that the currents on the coil conductors are not affected by the updated guesses of EP at each iteration. This has prevented the application of GMT beyond proof of principle experiments with homogeneous phantoms.

To address this limitation, here we present a new implementation of GMT that uses the volume-surface integral equation (VSIE) method
[
23
]
. The proposed VSIE-based GMT does not assume knowledge of the incident fields, but calculates the coil currents at every iteration considering the latest estimate of EP and uses them to compute updated transmit magnetic fields at each iteration. A preliminary version of this work was presented in
[
24
]
. For the remainder of this work, we adhere to the notation defined in the nomenclature.

II
Technical Background

II-A

Integral Equation Method

II-A
1
Volume Integral Equation

The interactions between the electromagnetic fields and biological tissue can be modeled using the current-based VIE as in
[
25
,
26
]
. Precisely, the tissue model can be discretized over a voxelized grid, and the VIE can be solved for the unknown body currents using the Galerkin method of moments
[
27
]
by inverting a linear system:

Z_{\rm bb}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)\bm{j}_{\rm b}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)=\text{RHS}(\bm{e}_{\rm inc}).

(1)

Z_{\rm bb}\in\mathbb{C}^{(q\cdot n_{v})\times(q\cdot n_{v})}
relates the induced polarization body current vector
\bm{j}_{\rm b}\in\mathbb{C}^{(q\cdot n_{v})\times 1}
to the right hand side (RHS) of the VIE. The RHS is a function of the incident electric field
\bm{e}_{\rm inc}\in\mathbb{C}^{(q\cdot n_{v})\times 1}
vector
[
25
]
(generated from an RF coil in MRI applications).
n_{v}
is the number of the voxels of the discretization and
q
are the basis function components per voxel.
\bm{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{v}\times 1}
is the vector of the complex relative permittivities for all voxels, defined as:

\bm{\epsilon}\equiv\bm{\epsilon_{r}}+\frac{\bm{\sigma_{e}}}{{\mathrm{i}}\omega\epsilon_{0}},

(2)

where
\omega
is the angular operating frequency of the MR scanner,
\epsilon_{0}
is the permittivity of vacuum, and
\bm{\epsilon_{r}},\bm{\sigma_{e}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{v}\times 1}
contain the scalar relative permittivity and conductivity of the sample, respectively. Access to the body currents allows the estimation of the magnetic scattered field vector
\bm{h}_{\rm sca}=K_{\rm bb}\bm{j}_{\rm b}\in\mathbb{C}^{(q\cdot n_{v})\times 1}
with the aid of the discretized Green’s function operator
K_{\rm bb}\in\mathbb{C}^{(q\cdot n_{v})\times(q\cdot n_{v})}

[
25
]
. The
\bm{B}_{1}^{(+)}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{v}\times 1}
in the body can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\bm{B}_{1}^{(+)}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)

\displaystyle=\mu_{0}F\left(\bm{h}_{\rm inc}+K_{bb}\bm{j}_{\rm b}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)\right),

(3)

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=G^{-1}\left([1\>\>{\mathrm{i}}\>\>0]\otimes[1\>\>0\>\>0\>\>0]\otimes I\right).

Here,
\mu_{0}
is the magnetic permeability of vacuum,
G
is the Gram matrix,
\bm{h}_{\rm inc}\in\mathbb{C}^{(q\cdot n_{v})\times 1}
is the incident magnetic field vector and
I\in\mathbb{N}^{n_{v}\times n_{v}}
is the identity matrix.

II-A
2
Volume-Surface Integral Equation

One can omit the coil’s incident fields (
\bm{e}_{\rm inc},\bm{h}_{\rm inc}
) by including the coil conductors in the simulation domain and solving for both the body and the coil currents simultaneously. In this case, Eq. (
1
) is replaced by the VSIE system
[
23
]
:

\begin{bmatrix}Z_{\rm cc}&Z_{\rm cb}^{\rm T}\\
Z_{\rm cb}&Z_{\rm bb}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{j}_{\rm c}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)\\
\bm{j}_{\rm b}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{v}\\
\bm{0}\end{bmatrix}.

(4)

VSIE is a simplified domain decomposition method, where the Galerkin method is used to discretize the coil and the body currents. In particular, the coil conductors can be discretized over a triangular mesh with
m
discretization elements and the coil current vector
\bm{j}_{\rm c}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times 1}
can be approximated using the Rao-Wilton-Glisson basis functions
[
28
]
.
Z_{\rm cc}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
and
Z_{\rm cb}\in\mathbb{C}^{(q\cdot n_{v})\times m}
relate the coil currents with the induced voltage
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times 1}
and the incident electric field, respectively. The
\bm{B}_{1}^{(+)}
can be computed as

\bm{B}_{1}^{(+)}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)=\mu_{0}FG^{-1}\left(K_{\rm cb}\bm{j}_{\rm c}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)+K_{\rm bb}\bm{j}_{\rm b}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)\right).

(5)

Here,
K_{\rm cb}\in\mathbb{C}^{(q\cdot n_{v})\times m}
relates the coil current vector with the incident magnetic field in each voxel of the sample
[
23
]
.

II-B

Global Maxwell Tomography

The reconstruction of the EP with GMT is performed iteratively, using the quasi-Newton optimization algorithm L-BFGS-B
[
29
]
. In each iteration of the optimizer, the VIE system is solved using the EP guess from the previous iteration to generate synthetic
\bm{B}_{1}^{(+)}
maps using (
3
). These maps are compared to the corresponding measured
\hat{\bm{B}}_{1}^{(+)}
maps within a cost function. This VIE-based GMT algorithm requires a set of incident fields (
\bm{e}_{\rm inc}
,
\bm{h}_{\rm inc}
) as the input, which remains constant for all iterations. The cost function
f\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)=f_{d}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)+\alpha f_{r}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)
that is minimized is composed by a weighted data-consistency term
f_{d}
and a regularization term
f_{r}
.
f_{d}
is given from:

\displaystyle f_{d}(\bm{\epsilon})

\displaystyle=\eta^{-1}\sqrt{\left\lVert w_{l}\circ w_{l^{\prime}}\circ\bm{\delta}_{ll^{\prime}}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}},

(6)

\displaystyle\eta

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\left\lVert w_{l}\circ w_{l^{\prime}}\circ\hat{\bm{B}}^{(+)}_{1,l}\circ\overline{\hat{\bm{B}}^{(+)}_{1,l^{\prime}}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}},

\displaystyle\bm{\delta}_{ll^{\prime}}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)

\displaystyle=\hat{\bm{B}}^{(+)}_{1,l}\circ\overline{\hat{\bm{B}}^{(+)}_{1,l^{\prime}}}-\bm{B}^{(+)}_{1,l}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)\circ\overline{\bm{B}^{(+)}_{1,l^{\prime}}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)},

where Einstein summation notation is used over the indices
l^{\prime}
(first) and
l
(second) that represent the number of measured
\hat{\bm{B}}_{1}^{(+)}
maps.
f_{r}(\bm{\epsilon})
is the so-called Match Regularizer
[
16
]
, which smooths the effect of the noise in the measurements while preserving tissue boundaries. The parameter
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
is the weight of the regularizer. The weights
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{v}\times 1}
in the data-consistency term can be set to prioritize regions of the
\hat{\bm{B}}_{1}^{(+)}
maps with higher signal-to-noise ratio (SNR).

The gradient of the data-consistency term with respect to
\bm{\epsilon}
can be computed analytically through the solution of the Hermitian adjoint system of (
1
). Specifically, one needs to first compute the derivative of
\bm{B}_{1}^{(+)}
with respect to
\bm{\epsilon}
using equation (
3
):

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{B}_{1}^{(+)}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)}{\partial\bm{\epsilon}}

\displaystyle=\mu_{0}FK_{bb}\frac{\partial\bm{j}_{\rm b}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)}{\partial\bm{\epsilon}}.

(7)

Note that the derivative of
\bm{h}_{\rm inc}
with respect to
\bm{\epsilon}
is zero
. The derivative of
\bm{j}_{\rm b}
with respect to
\bm{\epsilon}
requires the differentiation of the VIE (
1
) system as shown in
[
16
]
.

III
Theory

In this work, we propose a VSIE-based implementation of GMT where the coil’s incident EM fields are not calculated once and passed as input to the algorithm, but are rather implicitly re-calculated for each updated guess of EP at every GMT iteration, by solving the VSIE for both coil currents and body currents. While the cost function and the optimization pipeline can remain the same as in the VIE-based GMT, the synthetic
\bm{B}_{1}^{(+)}
maps must be computed using equation (
5
). Their derivatives with respect to
\bm{\epsilon}
are updated as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{B}_{1}^{(+)}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)}{\partial\bm{\epsilon}}

\displaystyle=\mu_{0}F\left(K_{bc}\frac{\partial\bm{j}_{\rm c}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)}{\partial\bm{\epsilon}}+K_{bb}\frac{\partial\bm{j}_{\rm b}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)}{\partial\bm{\epsilon}}\right),\>\>\text{or}

(8)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{B}_{1}^{(+)}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)}{\partial\bm{\epsilon}}

\displaystyle=\mu_{0}F\begin{bmatrix}K_{bc}\\
K_{bb}\end{bmatrix}^{\rm T}\frac{\partial\bm{j}_{\rm cb}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)}{\partial\bm{\epsilon}}.

Here,
\bm{j}_{\rm cb}
is the concatenated vector of both coil and body currents, and its derivative with respect to
\bm{\epsilon}
can be computed through the differentiation of the VSIE system (
4
).

The gradient of the data-consistency term can be computed using Wirtinger calculus
[
30
]
(real function of complex arguments) as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f_{d}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)}{\partial\bm{\epsilon}}

\displaystyle=\frac{-\left(\frac{\partial\bm{B}^{(+)}_{1,l}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)}{\partial\bm{\epsilon}}\bm{t}_{l,l^{\prime}}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)+\frac{\partial\overline{\bm{B}^{(+)}_{1,l}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)}}{\partial\bm{\epsilon}}\overline{\bm{t}_{l,l^{\prime}}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)}\right)}{\eta^{2}f_{d}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)},

(9)

\displaystyle\bm{t}_{l,l^{\prime}}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)

\displaystyle=\lvert\bm{w}_{l}\rvert^{2}\circ\lvert\bm{w}_{l^{\prime}}\rvert^{2}\circ\overline{\bm{\delta}_{ll^{\prime}}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)}\circ\overline{\bm{B}^{(+)}_{1,l^{\prime}}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)}.

Here, we used Einstein summation notation over the indices
l^{\prime}
(first) and
l
(second). If RF shimming
[
5
]
is not used to modulate the phase of the transmit coils, the derivative of the conjugate
\bm{B}_{1}^{(+)}
is zero. Otherwise, the conjugate derivative should also be computed, since the shimmed
\bm{B}_{1}^{(+)}
is a function of the conjugate unshimmed field. For example, let us assume that the
\bm{B}_{1}^{(+)}
is shimmed in such a way that the resulting phase at voxel
v
is zero. The phase-modulated (
\bm{\beta}
) can be re-written as:

\bm{\beta}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)=\bm{B}_{1}^{(+)}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)e^{-{\mathrm{i}}\phi\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)}=\bm{B}_{1}^{(+)}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)\frac{\overline{\bm{B}_{1,v}^{(+)}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)}}{\lvert\bm{B}_{1,v}^{(+)}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)\rvert}.

(10)

Here,
\phi
is the phase modulation applied to
\bm{B}_{1}^{(+)}
to cancel the phase at voxel
v
. The derivatives that must be used in (
9
) can be computed as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{\beta}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)}{\partial\bm{\epsilon}}

\displaystyle=e^{-{\mathrm{i}}\phi\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)}\left(I-\frac{e^{-{\mathrm{i}}\phi\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)}\bm{B}_{1}^{(+)}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)}{2\lvert\bm{B}_{1,v}^{(+)}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)\rvert}I_{:v}\right)\frac{\partial\bm{B}_{1}^{(+)}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)}{\partial\bm{\epsilon}}

(11)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\overline{\bm{\beta}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)}}{\partial\bm{\epsilon}}

\displaystyle=e^{-{\mathrm{i}}\phi\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)}\frac{\overline{\bm{B}_{1}^{(+)}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)}}{2\lvert\bm{B}_{1,v}^{(+)}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)\rvert}I_{:v}\frac{\partial\bm{B}_{1}^{(+)}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)}{\partial\bm{\epsilon}},

where
I_{:v}
is the
v
-th column of the identity matrix.

Assuming that RF shimming is not used, the co-gradient of the data-consistency term can be written with the help of (
9
) as follows:

\left(\frac{\partial f_{d}(\bm{\epsilon})}{\partial\bm{\epsilon}}\right)^{*}=-\frac{\mu_{0}X^{T}}{\eta^{2}f_{d}(\bm{\epsilon})}\left(\overline{\bm{a}(\bm{\epsilon})}\circ\bm{\zeta}_{l}(\bm{\epsilon})\right)_{l},

(12)

where
X
is

X=\begin{bmatrix}0^{m\times n_{v}}\\
\bm{1}_{q}\otimes I^{n_{v}\times n_{v}}\end{bmatrix},

(13)

and
\bm{\alpha}
is defined with the help of (
4
) as

\bm{a}(\bm{\epsilon})=-\frac{\partial\begin{bmatrix}Z_{\rm cc}&Z_{\rm cb}^{\rm T}\\
Z_{\rm cb}&Z_{\rm bb}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)\end{bmatrix}}{\partial\bm{\epsilon}}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{j}_{\rm c}(\bm{\epsilon})\\
\bm{j}_{\rm b}(\bm{\epsilon})\end{bmatrix}.

(14)

\bm{\zeta}_{l}
can be computed via (
8
) as the solution of the Hermitian adjoint system of (
4
) as follows:

\left(\begin{bmatrix}Z_{\rm cc}&Z_{\rm cb}^{\rm T}\\
Z_{\rm cb}&Z_{\rm bb}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)\end{bmatrix}\right)^{*}\bm{\zeta_{l}}(\bm{\epsilon})=\begin{bmatrix}\overline{K_{bc}}\\
K_{bb}^{*}\end{bmatrix}F^{*}\overline{\bm{t}_{l,l^{\prime}}\!\left(\bm{\epsilon}\right)}

(15)

Note that in (
14
),
\bm{j}_{\rm c}(\bm{\epsilon})
and
\bm{j}_{\rm b}(\bm{\epsilon})
have been computed by solving (
4
) with the current guess of the object’s EP.

A circuit diagram for three array elements is shown in Fig. (

Experiments

We conducted a GMT experiment with the two-compartment phantom on a
7\text{\,}\mathrm{T}
scanner (Magnetom, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). We measured
\lvert\hat{\bm{B}}_{1}^{(+)}\rvert
maps for each coil using a magnetic resonance fingerprinting technique
[
36
]
. To mitigate artifacts, we applied a median filter to the measured maps. Additionally, we extracted the relative phases between the transmit field maps using the individual coil images. Each of the eight scans required approximately
40
minutes, covering the entire phantom with a voxel isotropic resolution of
2\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}
.

Since coil cables are not modeled in our current VSIE formulation of the forward problem, we used the homogeneous phantom to calibrate scaling differences between simulated and experimental maps. In particular, we simulated the
\bm{B}_{1}^{(+)}
maps for the homogeneous phantom using the VSIE solver and the probe-measured EP. Then, we measured the corresponding experimental maps (see above) and multiplied each one by a complex weight:
\bm{q}_{l}\cdot\hat{\bm{B}}_{1}^{(+)}
,
l=1,\dots,8
. To calculate the calibration weighs, we minimized GMT’s data-consistency term over the weights
\bm{q}
instead of the EP. The
\delta
term in the data-consistency term (
6
) took the following form:

\bm{\delta}_{ll^{\prime}}\!\left(\bm{q}\right)=\left(\bm{q}_{l}\cdot\hat{\bm{B}}^{(+)}_{1,l}\right)\circ\left(\overline{\bm{q}_{l^{\prime}}\cdot\hat{\bm{B}}^{(+)}_{1,l^{\prime}}}\right)-\bm{B}^{(+)}_{1,l}\circ\overline{\bm{B}^{(+)}_{1,l^{\prime}}},

(17)

where Einstein summation notation is used over the indices
l^{\prime}
(first) and
l
(second). The experimental
\hat{\bm{B}}_{1}^{(+)}
acquired for the two-compartment phantom were then multiplied by the calibration weights, after scaling them by
\bm{q}\cdot V_{2}/V_{1}
, where
V_{2}=$80\text{\,}\mathrm{V}$
and
V_{1}=$48\text{\,}\mathrm{V}$
were the voltages applied during the two-compartment and homogeneous phantom scans, respectively. We refer to this step as
cross-calibration
. We also repeated the calibration by computing the weights
\bm{q}
using the two-compartment phantom itself and the associated EP measured with the dielectric probe (
self-calibration
), in order to assess the accuracy of the
cross-calibration
with the homogeneous phantom and its effect on GMT performance.

The GMT reconstructions were executed for
2000
iterations starting from the same homogeneous initial guess equal to the homogeneous phantom’s EP (
\epsilon_{r}=79.4
,
\sigma_{e}=$0.74\text{\,}\mathrm{S}\text{/}\mathrm{m}$
)). To account for the discrepancy between simulated and experimental conditions, we increased the weight of the regularizer
\alpha
from the value used in the simulations to
0.09
and
0.11
for the
cross-calibration
and
self-calibration
cases, respectively. In this case the weights
w
of the GMT’s data-consistency term were chosen to penalize more strongly the regions of low SNR than for the simulated VSIE-based GMT reconstructions:

w_{l}=\frac{\lvert\hat{\bm{B}}^{(+)}_{1,l}\rvert}{\max\!\left(\lvert\hat{\bm{B}}^{(+)}_{1,l}\rvert\right)},

(18)

since the experimental maps are expected to be larger differences with the synthetic ones.

IV-E

Evaluation

To evaluate the reconstructions we used the peak normalized absolute error (PNAE) following
[
16
]
. The PNAE was preferred over other error metrics, to avoid biases from voxels with small EP values. PNAE was defined as
|y-x|/\text{max}\{x\}
, where
x
is the ground truth and
y
is the reconstructed map. We also used the 3D structural similarity index measure (SSIM) to evaluate the quality of the experimental reconstruction.

VI
Discussion

In VIE-based GMT
[
16
]
, the incident fields from the coils to the sample were calculated once with a guess of EP and used throughout the GMT optimization. In the first experimental proof-of-principle of GMT, the incident fields were calculated for a uniform phantom using ground-truth EP measured with a dielectric probe
[
16
]
. This was equivalent to assuming that the incident fields were both known and not affected by the EP updates at each GMT iteration. Although the former was not realistic and was only adopted to demonstrate the GMT algorithm, the latter is a common assumption for EP reconstruction techniques based on the integral form of Maxwell’s equation, such as CSI-EPT
[
18
]
, which rely on using a constant approximation of the incident fields. In this work, we integrated the VSIE formulation into GMT, eliminating the need for a separate initial simulation step to estimate the incident fields. In the VSIE-based GMT, the incident EM fields are instead implicitly re-estimated during each GMT iteration, accounting for the effect of the updated EP on the coil currents.

There are several methods for fast solutions of the VSIE. Besides fully assembling
Z_{\rm cb}
in (
4
), one can employ the magnetic resonance Green’s function (MRGF)
[
23
]
for rapid VSIE solutions. However, this approach is most effective when one needs to simulate various coil designs loaded with the same object, but it is not efficient for multiple simulations of the same coil with varying object’s EP, as in GMT. Alternative methods, such as those based on tensor decompositions (ACA+Tucker
[
37
]
, TT-SVD
[
38
]
, Perturbation Matrix
[
39
]
), are typically preferred when
Z_{\rm cb}
exhibits a good low-rank approximation. However, this is not the case when the coil closely fits the object, as in the simulations presented in this work. Therefore, we utilized the precorrected Fast Fourier Transform method (pFFT) method
[
40
,
41
]
, which transforms the VSIE system into an equivalent VIE system by extending the volumetric computational grid to enclose both the coil and the object loading it.
The system can then be solved rapidly by utilizing FFT
[
25
]
, and Tucker decomposition-based acceleration methods
[
42
]
. Since our coil closely fits the object, the extended volumetric grid exactly matches the volumetric grid used in the VIE-based GMT, ensuring that our proposed VSIE-based GMT can be executed as quickly as the VIE-based GMT, requiring roughly two days (
500
iterations) to reconstruct EP for a sample discretized at
5\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}
) voxel isotropic resolution, and roughly ten days (
2000
iterations) to reconstruct EP for a sample discretized at
2\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}
voxel isotropic resolution
. Other integral equation formulations could also be employed for the solution of the VSIE
[
43
,
26
]
. Note however that a different formulation or method would require different calculations for the gradient (or the Hessian
[
44
]
if a full Newton optimization algorithm is used) of the data-consistency term, since the VSIE linear system has to be adjusted.

We show that the coil currents and, consequently, the incident fields, can differ between
30\%
and
50\%
from their expected ground-truth value (Fig. (
4
)), depending on the distance between the sample and the coil, when they are estimated using the same initial guess for the sample’s EP (
\epsilon_{r}=21.1
,
\sigma_{e}=$0.2\text{\,}\mathrm{S}\text{/}\mathrm{m}$
). The VSIE-based GMT optimization re-calculated the coil currents for each update of the EP, which resulted in the error decreasing to
10\%
after iterations
23
,
38
,
75
, and
90
for the cylinders with radii of
4\text{\,}\mathrm{cm}
,
6\text{\,}\mathrm{cm}
,
8\text{\,}\mathrm{cm}
, and
10\text{\,}\mathrm{cm}
, respectively. The above show that the closer the sample is to the coil’s conductors, the greater the perturbations in the currents due to changes in the EP. Therefore, the assumption of constant incident fields is valid only in the case of remote transmit coils, such as the scanner body coil at
3\text{\,}\mathrm{T}
. For experimental setups involving human subjects, this limitation can become critical, as coil loading varies significantly with subject positioning, thereby introducing substantial subject-specific changes in coil currents. As a result, failing to accurately estimate these currents can severely compromise the reliability of EP reconstructions obtained through integral equation -based EP reconstructions.

Even when using incident fields based on the true EP distribution, the VIE-GMT yielded slightly worse performance than the VSIE-based GMT (VIE-GMT I vs. VSIE-GMT in Fig. (
6
)). When the estimate of the incident fields was not based on the true EP distribution, which would be the case in actual experiments, the error in the reconstructed EP was considerably higher (VIE-GMT II and III). The complete breakdown of the technique for case III occurs because the inverse problem of GMT is ill-posed. This is due to having to estimate EP from incomplete measurements of the magnetic fields (i.e., the
\hat{z}
component of the magnetic field and the absolute phase of the
\hat{\bm{B}}_{1}^{(+)}
cannot be measured in MRI), while the EP mainly affect the electric fields, for which we cannot obtained tomographic maps. As a result, small inaccuracies in the incident fields can lead to large errors in the estimated EP distribution so the uniqueness theorem will be satisfied
[
45
]
(i.e., one set of magnetic field measurements corresponds to a unique set of EP). The proposed VSIE-based GMT formulation is more robust to these errors, as it does not require prior knowledge of the incident electromagnetic fields and instead relies only on a close match between the simulated coil model and the actual prototype. Alternatively, one could optimize the parameters of the regularizer in GMT’s cost function (
6
) to try to improve the reconstructions for the VIE-based GMT in the case of inaccurate estimates of the incident fields. Nevertheless, this could yield a prohibitively high computational cost because multiple GMT reconstructions would have to be performed to find the optimal parameters.

The VSIE-based GMT accurately reconstructs both relative permittivity and conductivity across the entire volume of a realistic head model in simulation, without relying on the assumptions inherent in other methods
[
46
]
. Techniques based on the partial differential equation form of Maxwell’s equations are typically two-dimensional and prone to noise amplification during reconstruction. To mitigate this, various strategies have been employed, including reformulating the problem as a convection-reaction equation (cr-MREPT)
[
15
]
, applying Savitzky-Golay filters
[
47
]
for computing numerical derivatives of
\bm{B}_{1}^{(+)}

[
48
]
, incorporating induced current images (first-order current density EPT) to improve the reconstruction
[
49
]
, or using the Green’s integral identity (Green EPT)
[
50
]
. While these approaches are computationally faster than integral methods like the one presented here, they introduce artifacts at tissue boundaries and sometimes produce noisy or blurred reconstructions
[
15
,
11
,
51
]
.

The VSIE-based GMT achieved comparable accuracy as the VIE-based GMT
[
16
]
in experiments for a homogeneous phantom
[
24
]
, with both approaches yielding less than
10\%
relative error for both electrical properties. Note that the VIE-GMT relied on an
8\times 8
calibration weight matrix, whereas VSIE-based GMT uses only
8
weights to calibrate the experimental measurements. The weight matrix effectively calibrated the field from each channel as a linear combination of the fields of all channels. While this approach was flexible, it could introduce potential ambiguities in the relative phases by allowing channel crosstalk to be absorbed into the calibration, which in turn can affect reconstruction accuracy. For inhomogeneous phantoms, VSIE-based GMT achieved greater per-compartment homogeneity compared to previously proposed techniques. For example, the standard deviation of the conductivity within the inner compartment in Fig. (
7
) was below
0.12\text{\,}\mathrm{S}\text{/}\mathrm{m}
, whereas for std-MREPT and Voigt’s method (Fig. 17(a,b) in
[
15
]
) the reconstructed EP were highly inhomogeneous inside a uniform compartment of a two-compartment phantom, with conductivity values ranging from approximately
0.4
to
1.4\text{\,}\mathrm{S}\text{/}\mathrm{m}
, despite a probe-measured ground truth conductivity of approximately
1.05\text{\,}\mathrm{S}\text{/}\mathrm{m}
. For cr-MREPT and constrained cr-MREPT (Fig. 17(c,d) in
[
15
]
) the reported range of conductivity values reduces to approximately
0.7
to
1.0\text{\,}\mathrm{S}\text{/}\mathrm{m}
inside the same phantom compartment. Nevertheless, note that these results can only be compared qualitatively, not quantitatively, since our experimental setup differs from previous studies in phantom design, resolution, coil geometry, and field strength. Finally, learning-based methods offer even greater computational speed and have shown great promise in in vivo reconstructions
[
52
,
53
,
54
]
, but their reliability remains unclear, making them difficult to fully trust for clinical translation yet.
Approaches that combine physics with deep learning, such as
[
55
,
56
]
, offer a promising alternative direction by enforcing the governing partial differential equations during training. In the context of EP reconstruction, methods like PIFON-EPT
[
57
]
) can simultaneously learn the EP distribution and denoise (and/or complete) the measured
\hat{\bm{B}}_{1}^{(+)}
field at any location within the object. However, because these methods typically employ relatively small, fully connected networks rather than convolutional architectures, they lack the expressive power needed to accurately reconstruct highly inhomogeneous objects.

To account for differences between the simulated and experimental coil setup, we performed a calibration of the
\hat{\bm{B}}_{1}^{(+)}
using a separate homogeneous phantom for which the EP were measured with a dielectric probe. We repeated the calibration also with the inhomogeneous phantom itself, using the probe-measured EP. This is an unrealistic scenario since it assumes that one already knows the EP they want to measure, but we performed it to verify whether calibration inaccuracy could partially explain the observed error between reconstructed and probe-measured EP. However, even when using such
self-calibration
approach, the PNAE did not change considerably. In particular, the PNAE for the inner (outer) compartment was
13\%
(
17\%
) and
17\%
(
39\%
) for the permittivity and conductivity, respectively, compared to
13\%
(
26\%
) and
17\%(33\%)
achieved with the
cross-calibration
. In the case of in vivo reconstructions, the role of the homogeneous phantom in the
cross-calibration
could potentially be fulfilled by registering the EP of a realistic head model
[
31
]
to the subject’s 3D MRI.

Although the measurements included regions of low signal, these were weighted low (see (
18
)) during the GMT reconstructions and Fig. (
8
) shows that overall the magnitude maps had similar field distributions between the calibrated measurements and the simulations. This suggests that the calibration was relatively accurate in both cases and there are other discrepancies between the experimental and simulation setups that contributed to the errors in the reconstructed EP. These could include radiation losses from the cables feeding the coil, which are not considered in the VSIE simulations. Another potential source of error not modeled in the VSIE framework is external electromagnetic radiation which can potentially be mitigated by using a shielded coil for data acquisition
[
58
]
. In this case, one needs to include the shield in the simulation and solve a hybrid VSIE as in
[
59
]
. Furthermore, the measured
\hat{\bm{B}}_{1}^{(+)}
may be affected by spurious phase offsets (Fig. (
8
)), further contributing to errors in EP estimation
[
60
]
. One approach to eliminate these error sources would be to shield the coil and further improve the model of the coil setup in the VSIE simulation. This would eventually reduce the error between simulated and experimental measurements.
Nevertheless, it remains challenging to fully capture these residual errors in the incident fields using the VSIE formulation alone. Moreover, VSIE-based GMT relies on accurate knowledge of the coil, which is a limitation, because it is difficult to consistently ensure a perfect match between the simulated coil design and the experimental prototype. To address this, future work, will explore approximating residual differences between simulated fields and experimental measurements using a weighted combination of modes from a numerical electromagnetic basis
[
61
]
. This approach will require jointly solving for both the weights and the EP, which can introduce a significant increase in the computational complexity of the inverse problem, as described in
[
62
]
.

VII
Conclusion

This work introduced a new formulation of GMT that utilizes the VSIE to account for perturbations in the coil currents due to the updated EP of the sample in each iteration of the optimization. We demonstrated VSIE-based GMT in simulations with a realistic head model. We built a
7\text{\,}\mathrm{T}
transceiver coil and validated the technique also in a phantom experiment. Future work will focus on reducing the error between experimental and simulated measurements, optimizing GMT’s convergence, and reducing acquisition speed to enable in vivo experiments.
