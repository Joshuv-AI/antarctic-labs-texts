Repeated Use of Emergency Departments for Nontraumatic Dental Conditions: Factors Associated With Being a Superutilizer
Authors: Morgan Santoro, Lisa J Heaton, Adrianna C Sonnek, Rebecca A Preston, John J O'Malley, Eric P Tranby
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDue to inadequate access to regular, affordable dental care, many patients turn to emergency departments (EDs) for the treatment of nontraumatic dental conditions (NTDCs). NTDC ED visits are a large burden on the U.S. healthcare system, with over 1.3 million visits each year and over $1 billion in associated charges. This study examines the characteristics of ED visitors and superutilizers (4+ ED visits in a calendar year) for non‐dental (general) and dental reasons; investigates the cost of care for ED visitors and superutilizers; and estimates associations between patient characteristics and the likelihood of superutilization.MethodsData obtained from the State Emergency Department Databases was used to summarise trends in visits by general and dental ED visitors and superutilizers, including visit rates and costs, across 14 states and 5 years. Logistic regression models were developed to estimate the likelihood of being dental superutilizers and superutilizers (for dental and non‐dental visits) based on the characteristics of their first ED visit (age, gender, race/ethnicity, income quartile, insurance type, presence of comorbidities and state in which ED visit occurred).ResultsOf the total number of ED visitors (N = 25 905 031), the majority (92.4%) were general (non‐dental) visitors; 6.4% were general superutilizers; 1.2% were dental visitors; and less than 1% (n = 2617) were dental superutilizers; dental superutilizers made up 0.2% of all superutilizers. Individuals who sought ED care for dental or non‐dental reasons four or more times in a year were more likely to be white, be in the second or third quartile in terms of income, and have insurance coverage through Medicaid, other dental insurance, or to be uninsured. Finally, mean and median treatment costs for general and dental superutilizers were considerably higher than for both general and dental visitors to the ED.DiscussionIndividuals seeking ED care for NTDCs four or more times in a year are more likely to be white, have insurance coverage through Medicaid, Medicare or to be uninsured, and have one or more medical comorbidities. Cost of care for superutilizers is also considerably higher than for other ED visitors. Expansion of dental services for adults, particularly those on Medicaid, is needed to ensure they can access timely preventative and restorative services and avoid the burden of dental crisis requiring a visit to the ED.

Introduction

The overutilization of emergency departments (EDs) for treatment of dental conditions is a well‐documented problem in the United States (U.S.) [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. With inadequate access to dental care, many patients turn to the ED for the treatment of nontraumatic dental conditions (NTDCs) [6], which are often preventable oral conditions including dental caries and pulpal and periapical conditions [7]. NTDCs do not include cases of oral trauma and are considered those conditions that are ‘treated more effectively, or prevented altogether, through regular care in a primary dental setting’ [7]. However, EDs are poorly equipped to manage dental conditions and often focus on symptom relief, such as opioid prescriptions, rather than treatment of the underlying dental condition [4, 8, 9, 10, 11].

NTDC ED visits are a large burden on the U.S. healthcare system, with over 1.3 million visits each year and over $1 billion in associated charges [12]. ED care is more expensive than treatment in a dental facility, and much of the cost burden is shouldered by Medicaid [3, 5, 10, 13, 14]. Many patients who visit the ED for an NTDC will go on to seek treatment in the ED for NTDCs multiple times [5, 15].

In the U.S., coverage for dental treatment varies widely by insurance type. Private dental insurance is often offered through employers, with costs shared between the employer and employee, though employers may only offer coverage to salaried employees, and not always to hourly employees [16]. Medicare, a federally managed programme for adults aged 65 and older and for some individuals with disabilities, does not include dental benefits [17]. However, beneficiaries can purchase supplementary Medicare Advantage (MA) plans independently through private insurance companies, which may provide dental coverage depending on the plan [18]. Medicaid, which provides coverage for children and adults with lower incomes, is funded federally and administered at the state level [19]. State Medicaid programmes are required by federal mandate to provide dental coverage for eligible children (defined as under 18 in some states and under 21 in others), while adult dental benefits differ significantly by state, with some offering only limited services (e.g., extractions) [19]. Individuals covered by some type of insurance (whether private, Medicare Advantage or Medicaid) receive treatment from a dental provider, who then files a claim with the insurer for reimbursement. Amounts not covered by insurance are often passed along to the individual for out‐of‐pocket payment. For those without any form of dental insurance, patients must typically cover costs entirely out of pocket or negotiate payment arrangements directly with their dentist.

Patients who utilize the ED for NTDCs are more likely to be young adults, on Medicaid or uninsured, and reside in low‐income areas [1, 5, 14, 20]. These same characteristics are associated with higher odds of NTDC revisits, or visiting the ED for NTDCs more than once. ED superutilizers, who average four to five times more ED visits per year compared with other patients, are responsible for a large portion of a growing burden on EDs [21, 22]. While several studies examine the characteristics of patients visiting EDs for NTDCs [1, 5, 14, 20], there is little literature examining dental superutilizers, patients who disproportionately utilize the ED for NTDCs.

As noted above, many individuals who use ED care for non‐urgent health care have their health care coverage through state‐managed Medicaid programmes. Dental coverage for adults on Medicaid varies significantly by state. For example, as of October 2022, the National Academy for State Health Policy (NASHP) identified eight states as offering emergency adult dental coverage (covering only emergency dental care) and 14 offering ‘limited’ coverage (covering fewer than 100 diagnostic, preventive and minor restorative procedures; $1000 or less in per‐person annual dental care expenditures) [23]. However, a number of states have expanded their Medicaid adult dental benefits as of 1 January 2023, and some offer dental benefits to specific groups of adults (e.g., pregnant persons, individuals with disabilities, etc.) [24]. Prior research suggests eliminating an adult Medicaid dental benefit results in increased ED NTDC visits [25]. As a result, there have been repeated calls for expansion of Medicaid benefits as a way to reduce ED NTDC use [26, 27]. This study has three goals: (1) examine the characteristics of ED visitors for non‐dental (general) and dental reasons, as well as characteristics of general and dental superutilizers across 14 states and 5 years; (2) investigate the cost of care for ED visitors and superutilizers, including for general and dental care; and (3) use fixed effect logistic regression models to estimate associations between patient characteristics and the likelihood of superutilization. While the U.S. healthcare and dental insurance systems present some unique challenges to accessing oral health care, aspects of these systems may mirror those of other countries. It is hoped that identifying factors associated with increased likelihood of being a superutilizer will help inform policies to redirect these patients towards more effective dental treatment and preventative care not just in the U.S., but in other regions that may face similar barriers to access to optimal dental care.

Methods

This study uses data obtained from the State Emergency Department Databases (SEDD), a set of state and year level databases which contain data on the visit level for each ED visit which does not result in admittance to the hospital [28]. This dataset contains information for ED visitors who did and did not have a visit for an NTDC, and can be used to compare NTDC visitors to non‐NTDC visitors. Data were limited to states and years containing a visit linkage variable that tracks a unique patient across multiple ED visits within a state [29]. This study includes 2016–2020 data from Arkansas, Colorado, Florida, Georgia, Iowa, Indiana, Massachusetts, Maryland, Missouri, Nebraska, New York, Utah, Vermont and Wisconsin. This study excluded visitors with missing age, income quartile or longitudinal ID fields as well as patients who died upon admittance to the ED. This study was reviewed and approved by the WCG IRB.

The data are structured at the visit level and span multiple years (2016–2020) across 14 states. Each record represents an ED visit that did not result in hospital admission. Because state‐level identifiers were used that link visits for the same patient within a state, individuals who visited the ED in more than one calendar year appear multiple times in the dataset—once for each year they had visits. This approach allows for examination of annual patterns and classification of superutilizers (defined as 4+ visits in a single year) accurately. This also means that counts of records reflect patient‐years rather than unique individuals.

Dependent Variable

The dependent variable of superutilizer was defined as visits by individuals who had visited an ED for any non‐dental reason (general superutilizer) or for an NTDC (dental superutilizer) four or more times in a given calendar year [30]. The International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD‐10‐CM codes) was used to identify NTDC conditions, as per the Association of State and Territorial Dental Directors (ASTDD)‐recommended case definition of NTDCs (Appendix A) [6]. Dental ED visits were defined as ED visits whose primary diagnosis was an NTDC.

Independent Variables

Characteristics of individuals making ED visits were examined, including: age in years; gender coded as male or female; race (Black, Hispanic, white, Native American, Asian or Pacific Islander, or Other); state of residence (Arkansas, Colorado, Florida, Georgia, Iowa, Indiana, Massachusetts, Maryland, Missouri, Nebraska, New York, Utah, Vermont and Wisconsin); primary insurance payer coded as Medicare, Medicaid, private insurance, self‐pay or other; median household income quartiles within the state of residence estimated using zip codes (fourth quartile being the highest income); total number of comorbidities (as a continuous variable); and portion of the week of the ED visit coded as week or weekend.

Statistical Analyses

The authors developed an analytical dataset, combining data across states and over time. For each patient‐year, the authors calculated the total number of ED visits overall and the number of dental ED visits. As patients could appear in multiple years, the dataset includes multiple records for the same individual across different years. The authors included dummy variables for state and year, which control for state‐level unobserved heterogeneity (e.g., policy environment, demographics), year‐specific trends (e.g., national economic conditions, federal policy changes), and unobserved person‐level heterogeneity across years. Coefficients thus represent within‐state and within‐year trends, net of the fixed effects of state and year.

A final logistic regression model examined the likelihood of becoming a dental superutilizer versus a dental visitor. For the first and final regression models, the outcome variables were imbalanced with dental superutilizers being the minority class. Therefore, the oversampling technique of upsampling was used to improve the performance of the regression models. Logistic regression models included age (continuous), gender, race/ethnicity, income quartile, insurance type, state of residence and total number of comorbidities (continuous count of documented conditions). Age was modelled as a continuous variable; exploratory checks did not indicate strong nonlinearity. Models were fit using all available data, and odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were reported.

First, the authors used descriptive statistics to summarise trends in visits by general and dental ED visitors and superutilizers, including visit rates and costs, across states and years. Second, the authors used independent variables to describe the characteristics of ED visitors stratified by visitor type: general visitor (< 4 visits in a calendar year for non‐dental reasons), dental visitor (< 4 ED visits in a calendar year, at least one being for a dental condition(s)), general superutilizer (4+ non‐dental ED visits in a calendar year) and dental superutilizer (4+ dental ED visits in a calendar year). Third, the fixed effect logistic regression models are presented.

Results

Table 1 presents demographic characteristics of ED visits stratified by visitor type. Of the total number of ED patient‐years (N = 25 905 031), the majority (92.4%) were for general (non‐dental) visits, and approximately 6.4% of visits were by general (non‐dental) superutilizers; and less than 1% (n = 2617) of visits were made by dental superutilizers. The number of unique patient‐years was 25 899 746, while the number of just unique patients was 21 213 238. While those making general ED visits were most likely to have private insurance (36.6%), general superutilizers, dental visitors and dental superutilizers were most likely to have coverage through Medicaid (47.5%, 47.3% and 64.5%, respectively; Table 1). The states with the highest percentage of dental visitors and dental superutilizers were New York (33.8%; 29.3%), Florida (20.7%; 30.4%) and Wisconsin (24.7%; 25.2%); these states also represented the largest percentages of general visitors and general superutilizers. Most dental visitors and dental superutilizers had a dental‐related ICD‐10‐CM codes for other disorders of teeth and supporting structures (K08; 79.3%; 88.9%). Dental superutilizers were more likely to visit the ED on a weekend (91.6%) compared to other ED visitors.

The annual charges for general superutilizers (mean = $116 102 [SD = $206 193]) and dental superutilizers (mean = $104 232 [SD = $199 729]) were approximately three times higher than for the two visitor (non‐superutilizer) groups (Table 2). The median and interquartile range (IQR) values followed a similar pattern, with charges for general superutilizers (median = $46 909 [IQR = $16 807–$127 488]) and dental superutilizers (median = $42 544 [IQR = $16 746–$23 310]) approximately six times higher than for general visitors (median = $6313 [$2271–$19 756]) and dental visitors (median = $6567 [$1941–$25 411]).

Discussion

Overutilization of ED services for dental and non‐dental reasons is costly and inefficient for individuals seeking care and the overall healthcare system. The findings of this study demonstrate that, overall, individuals who visit the ED for dental reasons four or more times in a year are more likely to be white or Native American; have insurance coverage through Medicaid, Medicare or be uninsured. Overall, individuals in Arkansas had significantly higher odds of being dental superutilizers than individuals living in selected other states. Finally, mean and median treatment costs for general and dental superutilizers were considerably higher than for both general and dental visitors to the ED.

Cost of care was significantly higher for superutilizers, compared to less frequent ED visitors. Prior research estimates NTDC visits cost the U.S. healthcare system over a billion dollars per year [12]. With dental superutilizers visiting EDs for NTDCs multiple times in 1 year, it is clear their dental needs are not being addressed in an effective or cost‐efficient way through ED use. In this study, similar to prior research, uninsured individuals and those with Medicaid or Medicare coverage were more likely to be superutilizers than those with private insurance [31]. Many have called for expansion of Medicaid benefits as a way to reduce ED NTDC use [26, 27]. Studies have shown a direct relationship between elimination of a Medicaid dental benefit for adults and increased ED NTDC visits [25, 32, 33]. Expanding the adult dental benefit for Medicaid beneficiaries has the promise to be a cost‐effective way of reducing expensive ED NTDC visits.

In this study, individuals who were Black, Hispanic, Asian or Pacific Islander or identified as an ‘other’ race/ethnicity were less likely to be dental superutilizers than white Medicaid enrollees, inconsistent with other studies showing individuals in Black, Hispanic and other minority populations were more likely to visit EDs for NTDCs [26, 34, 35]. White enrollees made up over half of the Medicaid enrollees in this study, which may help explain their overrepresentation as superutilizers. In a similar study of Wisconsin Medicaid enrollees; however, Okunseri and colleagues found that while nearly 50% of their sample was white, Black and indigenous individuals were more likely to visit an ED for NTDCs than white individuals [34]. Meanwhile, in a later study of Wisconsin Medicaid enrollees, white individuals were more likely to make return visits to an ED for NTDC than Black or Hispanic individuals [36]. It may be that, while Black, Hispanic and enrollees in Medicaid from other minority populations may visit EDs for NTDCs more often than white enrollees, they are less likely to make repeated visits, as seen with superutilizers. This pattern warrants additional investigation across multiple states.

Given the chronic and multifactorial nature of dental caries [37], individuals receive repeated care in EDs for this condition that is not remedied with a single procedure or treatment [7, 38]. The availability of dentists in ED settings varies greatly by each hospital, and many EDs do not employ dentists who can properly address the root cause of the problem. This leads to individuals receiving primarily palliative, rather than definitive restorative care. This lack of dental care capacity in EDs, combined with a lack of Medicaid dental benefits for adults, could lead to repeat ED visits for patients as well as increased burden of cost and suffering.

Limitations

Analysing medical record data relies on accurate diagnoses and coding; errors in one or both may lead to inaccuracies in estimates. Cross‐sectional SEDD data do not allow for causal analyses. Relatedly, as the data were in aggregate, individuals could not be tracked to determine reasons for ED visits and re‐visits on an individual level. Similarly, specific barriers to care on an individual level were not able to be assessed. This analysis used a predefined category of ‘superutilizer’ of four or more ED visits per year [21, 22], which does not allow for examining characteristics of individuals who visit the ED for NTDCs on a more granular level (e.g., one, two or three times per year).

Conclusions

Individuals seeking ED care for NTDCs four or more times in a year are more likely to be white, have insurance coverage through Medicaid, Medicare or be uninsured, live in the state of Arkansas, and have one or more medical comorbidities. Cost of care for superutilizers is also considerably higher than for other ED visitors. Expansion of dental services for adults, particularly those on Medicaid, is needed to ensure they can access timely preventative and restorative services and avoid the burden of dental crisis that would otherwise require a visit to the ED.

Logistic Regression Model Predicting the Likelihood of Becoming an ED Superutilizer (Dental or Non‐Dental) Versus a General ED Visitor (Dental or Non‐Dental)

This appendix shows results of the logistic regression model showing factors predictive of being an ED superutilizer (for any reason, dental or non‐dental) compared to an ED visitor (dental or non‐dental). Individuals identifying as female had significantly higher odds than those identifying as male of being ED superutilizers (OR = 1.25, 95% CI = 1.25, 1.26; p < 0.001). Compared to white individuals, those identifying as Black (OR = 1.17, 95% CI = 1.16, 1.18; p < 0.001) and Native American (OR = 1.13, 95% CI = 1.10, 1.15; p < 0.001) had significantly higher odds of being ED superutilizers. Meanwhile, those identifying as Asian or Pacific Islander (OR = 0.52, 95% CI = 0.51, 0.52; p < 0.001), Hispanic (OR = 0.91, 95% CI = 0.90, 0.91; p < 0.001), or ‘other’ (OR = 0.72, 95% CI = 0.72, 0.73; p < 0.001) had significantly lower odds of being ED superutilizers than white individuals.

As income increased, the likelihood of being an ED superutilizer decreased. Compared to the first (lowest) income quartile, those in the second quartile had significantly lower odds of being ED superutilizers (OR = 0.92, 95% CI = 0.92, 0.93; p < 0.001), and those in the fourth income quartile also had lower odds of being ED superutilizers (OR = 0.66, 95% CI = 0.66, 0.67; p < 0.001). Individuals with private health insurance had significantly lower odds of being ED superutilizers than those with any other type of insurance (p's > 0.0001).

Individuals with one or more comorbidities had significantly higher odds of being ED superutilizers compared to those with no comorbidities (OR = 1.92, 95% CI = 1.92, 1.92; p < 0.001). Compared to individuals in Arkansas, individuals in Colorado (OR = 1.09, 95% CI = 1.08, 1.11; p < 0.001), Florida (OR = 1.30, 95% CI = 1.28, 1.31; p < 0.001) and Utah (OR = 1.13, 95% CI = 1.11, 1.14; p < 0.001) had significantly higher odds of being ED superutilizers. Meanwhile, individuals from all other states for whom data were available (Georgia, Iowa, Maryland, New York and Wisconsin) had significantly lower odds of being ED superutilizers than those from Arkansas (p's < 0.001) (Table B1).
