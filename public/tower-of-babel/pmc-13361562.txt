Association of pre-pregnancy and pregnancy sleep on the risk of postpartum depression: a nationwide birth cohort-the Japan Environment and Children’s Study
Authors: Yoshiko Suetsugu, Takehiro Michikawa, Seiichi Morokuma, Norio Hamada, Kimiyo Kikuchi, Subaru Ikeda, Yukiyo Shimada, Kiyoko Kato, Masayuki Ochiai, Mayumi Tsuji, Masayuki Shimono, Kiyoshi Yoshino, Reiko Suga, Toshihiro Kawamoto, Shoichi Ohga, Japan Environment and Children’s Study Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe association between pre-pregnancy sleep patterns and postpartum depression as well as the modifying effect of parity, remains insufficiently understood. This study examined the longitudinal associations of sleep before and during pregnancy on the risk of postpartum depression at one month postpartum, with additional analyses stratified by parity.MethodsData were obtained from the Japan Environment and Children’s Study, a nationwide birth cohort study conducted between 2011 and 2014. A total of 65,477 women who completed questionnaires on demographic characteristics, obstetric factors, sleep patterns before and during pregnancy, and postpartum depression at one month postpartum were included. Relative risks (RRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for postpartum depression were estimated according to sleep duration, bedtime, sleep depth, and morning mood after adjusting for relevant covariates.ResultsA pre-pregnancy sleep duration of 8–9 h was associated with a lower risk of postpartum depression (RR = 0.94, 95% CI: 0.89–0.99). During pregnancy, sleeping less than 6 h and having bedtimes outside the 9:00 pm–midnight or midnight–3:00 am ranges were associated with increased risks of postpartum depression (RR = 1.19, 95% CI: 1.10–1.30; RR = 1.20, 95% CI: 1.07–1.35, respectively). Very light and light sleep were strongly associated with higher risks (RR = 1.88, 95% CI: 1.76–2.01; RR = 1.37, 95% CI: 1.31–1.44, respectively). Additionally, waking in a very bad or bad mood during pregnancy markedly increased the risk of postpartum depression (RR = 2.22, 95% CI: 1.99–2.47; RR = 1.72, 95% CI: 1.64–1.79, respectively).In parity-stratified analyses, multiparous women with bedtimes between midnight and 3:00 am before or during pregnancy had elevated risks of postpartum depression, with significant effect modification (pre-pregnancy: RR = 1.11, 95% CI: 1.03–1.19, p = 0.001; pregnancy: RR = 1.12, 95% CI: 1.04–1.22, p = 0.01). Among multiparous women, waking in a very bad or bad mood during pregnancy was also strongly associated with postpartum depression, with a significant effect modification (RR = 2.65, 95% CI: 2.28–3.09; RR = 1.93, 95% CI: 1.80–2.06, respectively, p < 0.001).ConclusionsPre-pregnancy and pregnancy sleep patterns are associated with the risk of postpartum depression. Particularly, among multiparous women, late bedtimes before and during pregnancy may increase this risk. Improving sleep habits before and during pregnancy may contribute to the prevention of postpartum depression.Trial registrationClinical trial number: Not applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09154-6.Keywords: Pre-pregnancy, Pregnancy, Sleep duration, Bedtime, Sleep quality, Postpartum depression

Background

The prevalence of postpartum depression is reported to be 10%–20% in Japan and other countries [1–3]. Postpartum depression is associated with significant emotional distress and impaired daily functioning, deteriorating family relationships and social isolation [4], and suicide [5], and may affect a baby’s physical, emotional, and cognitive development [6, 7]. Consequently, prevention and support for postpartum depression are a necessity.

Factors associated with postpartum depression include demographic, psychosocial, lifestyle habits, obstetric history, and pregnancy-related factors [8, 9].

Among these risk factors, regarding sleep, not only poor postpartum sleep quality [10–13], but also poor sleep quality during pregnancy has been likewise reported to be a risk factor for postpartum depression [14–16]. Many studies have reported sleep disturbances [17] such as a late bedtime, the number of nighttime awakenings, duration of nighttime awakenings, and decreased total and nighttime sleep duration during pregnancy [18–21]. These sleep disturbances are caused by physical symptoms and psychological symptoms [18]. These sleep disturbances during pregnancy explain poor sleep quality during pregnancy and potentially lead to postpartum depression.

Likewise, pre-pregnancy sleep may be associated with postpartum depression. However, very few studies have investigated the relationship between pre-pregnancy sleep and postpartum depression. In one such study, short pre-pregnancy sleep duration was reported to be a related factor of postpartum depression [22]. Particularly among multiparous women, a higher risk of postpartum depression has been reported when pre-pregnancy sleep duration is less than 6 h [22]. However, insufficient research has been conducted on the relationship between pre-pregnancy sleep and postpartum depression. In addition, there is a dearth of studies clarifying the temporal association between sleep, before and during pregnancy, and postpartum depression.

Furthermore, while primiparity has traditionally been associated with increased risk of postpartum depression, recent evidence indicates that this relationship is not consistent, and in some contexts, multiparous women may be at equal [23] or even greater risk [24]. Research has also reported that sleep quality in late pregnancy is associated with early postpartum maternal mental health in multiparous women [25]; therefore, we hypothesized that parity may moderate the relationship between pre-pregnancy and pregnancy sleep and depression.

Finally, previous studies have reported associations between pre-pregnancy sleep and infertility [26, 27], preterm birth [28], and newborn sleep and temperament [28] but not as a related factor for postpartum depression. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the associations of pre-pregnancy and during-pregnancy sleep on maternal depression at one month postpartum using a prospective cohort design. It also sought to clarify effect modification by parity. These findings will allow us not only to screen for women at high risk for postpartum depression due to poor sleep quality during pregnancy and implement timely preventive interventions, but also to provide suggestions for preconception care concerning sleep to prevent postpartum depression in women before they become pregnant.

Methods

Study design and participants

We used data (“jecs-ta-201901930” released in October 2019) from Japan’s nationwide prospective birth cohort study, the Japan Environment and Children’s Study (JECS), which is registered in the University Hospital Medical Information Network (UMIN) Clinical Trials Registry (number UMIN000030786). The study protocol and baseline profile have been reported elsewhere [29, 30]. Briefly, approximately 100,000 pregnant women were recruited between January 2011 and March 2014 at 15 Regional Centres, and the infants are currently being followed up.

Of all 103,060 pregnancies, we excluded 37,613 pregnancies owing to the following reasons: multiple participations (n = 5,647), multiple births (n = 948), miscarriages or stillbirths (n = 3,521), congenital anomalies (n = 3,550), missing information on maternal age at delivery (n = 11), births at < 37 or ≥ 42 gestational weeks (n = 4,454), a history of psychiatric illness (n = 4,904), and missing responses regarding sleep (n = 820), postpartum depression (n = 2,228), and covariates (n = 11,530) (Fig. 1). Births before 37 weeks or after 42 weeks were excluded because preterm and post-term deliveries are associated with elevated perinatal stress, which could introduce substantial confounding. Women with a history of mental illness were also excluded because pre-existing psychiatric conditions represent a strong confounding factor for postpartum depression risk.

Flow chart representing the study population

Finally, data from 65,447 participants who delivered singleton live births were included in the analysis.

Data collection

Participants answered a questionnaire twice during pregnancy: at recruitment in the first trimester (T1), and later in their second or third trimester (T2). They also answered a questionnaire one month after delivery (T3). The questionnaire used in this study was developed for the JECS study [see Supplementary Material 1]. The questionnaire consisted of questions about sleep and lifestyle-behavior pre-pregnancy and during early pregnancy at T1, sleep and lifestyle-behavior during mid- and late pregnancy at T2, and postpartum depressive symptoms and delivery and postpartum situations at T3. We defined “ pre-pregnancy” as the normal days before one becomes aware that one is pregnant.

Postpartum depressive symptoms

Postpartum depressive symptoms were assessed using the EPDS in the questionnaire at T3. According to previous studies [31–33], participants with a score of 9 or more out of 30 were categorized as having depressive symptoms, which was considered the optimal cutoff for screening postpartum depression in Japan.

Maternal sleep

In the T1 questionnaire, the participants were asked about their pre-pregnancy sleep duration and bedtime. We divided participants into six groups by sleep duration: < 6, 6–7, 7–8, 8–9, 9–10, and 10 ≤ h. The participants were also divided into three groups by bedtime: 9 p.m. to midnight, midnight to 3 a.m., and others (before 9:00 p.m. or at or after 3:00 a.m.). In the T2 questionnaire, participants were asked about their sleep duration and bedtime during pregnancy. We divided the participants into groups as described above for the T1 and T2 questionnaire. In addition, the T2 questionnaire included two more questions about sleep quality: 1) “How would you rate your average depth of sleep during the past month?” 2) “How would you rate your overall feeling when waking up in the morning during the past month?” The participants answered very light (very bad), relatively light (bad), normal, relatively deep (good), or very deep (very good).

Lifestyle and behavior

Information about marital status (collected at T1), educational background (years) (T2), employment status at early pregnancy (T1) and during pregnancy (T2), household income (including the periods before and during pregnancy) (million Japanese-Yen/year) (T2), parity (T1), infertility treatment (T1), feeling when pregnancy was found out (T1), smoking habits (T1/2), alcohol consumption at early pregnancy (T1/2), domestic violence in the pre-pregnancy period (T1) and during pregnancy (T2), pre-pregnancy body mass index (T1), physical activity (METs-min/week) [34, 35] in the pre-pregnancy period (T1) and during pregnancy (T2), and obstetric complications including pregnancy-related hypertensive disorders or diabetes/gestational diabetes (T2) were collected via self-administered questionnaires or transcribed from medical records.

Delivery and postpartum circumstances

Information on age at delivery, type of delivery, feeding status, and return to parents’ home were collected via self-administered questionnaires or transcribed from medical records.

Statistical analyses

We analyzed all the data using descriptive statistics and then conducted a Modified Poisson regression with a robust error variance to estimate RRs with 95% CIs of postpartum depression (EPDS ≥ 9). Regression analyses were conducted using two hierarchical models to verify the affected variables. The references were based on previous studies [19–21]. Based on evidence from both the general population [36] and pregnant women [22], sleeping approximately 7 to 8 h is associated with the lowest risk of depression; therefore, we set the reference of sleep duration at 7 to 8 h. We performed an exploratory analysis to examine whether the association between sleep and postpartum depression was modified by parity.

This study examined two separate primary exposures analyzed in entirely separate models: pre-pregnancy sleep (assessed retrospectively at T1) and pregnancy sleep (assessed prospectively at T2). For each exposure, Model 2 is the primary analysis; Model 1 results are presented in Supplementary Table 1 (St1) and Supplementary Table 2 (St2) of Supplementary Material 2 as a reference. Covariates included in Model 1 and Model 2 for each exposure were selected based on the respective DAG (Supplementary figure 1 or Supplementary figure 2 of Supplementary Material 3) and epidemiological criteria: (a) association with the sleep exposure; (b) association with postpartum depression. For the pre-pregnancy sleep model: Model 1 (St1) adjusted for T1 baseline characteristics concurrent with or prior to the pre-pregnancy exposure: maternal age, educational attainment, household income, marital status, pre-pregnancy BMI, domestic violence before pregnancy, parity, infertility treatment, feelings about pregnancy, and smoking/alcohol use. Model 2 (primary analysis; Table 2) additionally adjusted for subsequent pregnancy and delivery-related variables (measured at T2 and T3): employment status and physical activity during pregnancy, domestic violence during pregnancy, obstetric complications (gestational hypertension, gestational diabetes mellitus), gestational age at delivery, mode of delivery, feeding method, and postpartum living arrangements. For the pregnancy sleep model: Model 1 (St2) adjusted for T2 baseline characteristics concurrent with or prior to the pregnancy sleep exposure: all T1-assessed variables listed above, plus employment status at T2, physical activity at T2, and domestic violence during pregnancy (T2). Model 2 (primary analysis; Table 3) additionally adjusted for subsequent pregnancy and delivery-related variables (measured at T2 and T3): obstetric complications (gestational hypertension, gestational diabetes mellitus), gestational age at delivery, mode of delivery, feeding method, and postpartum living arrangements.

Characteristics of the participants

Pre-pregnancy sleep as a risk factor for postpartum depression

The most commonly reported sleep duration and bedtime was 7–8 h and from 9 pm to midnight, respectively (Table 2).

Regarding sleep duration, compared with women who slept 7–8 h, women who slept 8–9 h had a slightly lower risk of postpartum depression one month after delivery (RR: 0.94, 95% CI: 0.89–0.99). Regarding bedtime, compared with women whose bedtimes were between 9 pm and midnight, women whose bedtimes were at other times had an increased risk (RR:1.13, 95% CI:1.01–1.25).

Sleep during pregnancy as a risk factor for postpartum depression

The most commonly reported sleep duration and bedtime during pregnancy was 7–8 h and 9 pm to midnight, respectively (Table 3).

Regarding sleep duration, compared with women who slept 7–8 h, women who slept less than 6 h had an increased risk (RR:1.19, 95% CI:1.10–1.30).

Regarding bedtime, compared with women whose bedtimes were between 9 pm and midnight, women whose bedtimes were at other times had an increased risk (RR:1.20, 95% CI:1.07–1.35).

Regarding depth of sleep, compared with women whose depth of sleep was normal, women whose depth of sleep was very light (RR: 1.88, 95% CI:1.76–2.01) or light (RR:1.37, 95% CI:1.31–1.44) had increased RRs of postpartum depression one month after delivery, while those whose depth of sleep was deep (RR:0.89, 95% CI:0.82–0.96) or very deep (RR:0.74, 95% CI:0.61–0.90) had decreased RRs.

Regarding feelings when waking up in the morning, compared with women who reported feeling normal, women who reported feeling very bad (RR: 2.22, 95% CI:1.99–2.47) and bad (RR: 1.72, 95% CI:1.64–1.79) had increased RRs of postpartum depression one month after delivery, while those who reported feeling good (RR: 0.79, 95% CI:0.74–0.85) and very good (RR: 0.49, 95% CI:0.39–0.62) had decreased RRs.

Pre-pregnancy sleep as a risk factor for postpartum depression by parity

Regarding sleep duration among multiparous women, women who slept 8–9 h had a slightly lower risk of postpartum depression one month after delivery compared with women who slept 7–8 h RR:0.91, 95% CI:0.84–0.98) (Table 4). However, statistical effect modification by parity was not significant (p for effect modification = 0.41).

Discussion

This study employed a longitudinal design to clarify the relationship between postpartum depression and sleep patterns before and during pregnancy. The new findings of this study include the identification of associations between pre-pregnancy sleep characteristics and postpartum depression, as well as the associations between sleep patterns across the pre-pregnancy and pregnancy periods and postpartum depression stratified by parity. The findings indicate that obtaining 8–9 h of sleep prior to pregnancy was associated with a reduced risk of postpartum depression. Moreover, among multiparous women, habitual bedtime between midnight and 3 a.m. during both the pre-pregnancy and pregnancy periods was associated with an increased relative risk of postpartum depression. However, this pattern was not observed in primiparous women.

Our results for the pre-pregnancy period were not consistent with previous studies. While previous research reported that sleeping for less than 7 h before pregnancy increased the risk of postpartum depression [22], this study found that a short sleep duration was not a risk factor for postpartum depression, and that sleeping for 8 − 9 h reduced the risk. The discrepancy in results may be due to differences in the covariates.

Serotonin and tryptophan, a precursor of serotonin, have been reported as factors associated with depression [38]. Insufficient sleep increases the rate of tryptophan being converted into kynurenine and metabolites rather than serotonin [39]. Women who had sufficient sleep of 8 − 9 h in the pre-pregnancy period were thought to have had smooth metabolism of tryptophan to serotonin and sufficient serotonin levels, which may have encouraged a positive response to stress after pregnancy and reduced the risk of postpartum depression.

Our results for the period during pregnancy were consistent with previous studies. During pregnancy, it has been reported that late bedtimes and irregular bedtimes are associated with postpartum depression [40, 41]. It has also been reported that insomnia during pregnancy [42] and the relationship between short sleep duration [43, 44] and the quality of sleep [8, 14–16, 44, 45] can affect postpartum depression. It has been suggested that the quality of sleep during pregnancy, rather than simply the short sleep duration, may be related to postpartum depression [46]. Late and irregular sleep times may be associated with disruptions of the circadian rhythm, and it is thought that the serotonin levels for secreting melatonin may not have been sufficient in such cases, which increased the risk of postpartum depression [47]. In addition, sleep deprivation increases cortisol levels due to the stress response [48], and high cortisol levels have been reported to be related to depression [49]. Cortisol levels increase during pregnancy [50], and a relationship between cortisol levels during pregnancy and the risk of depression has been reported [51]. In addition, chronic short sleep and poor sleep quality during pregnancy are thought to have further increased cortisol levels, increasing the risk of developing postpartum depression. Meanwhile, pregnant women who had high-quality sleep during pregnancy likely had sufficient serotonin levels, which is thought to have reduced the risk of postpartum depression.

Analysis stratified by parity revealed that, among multiparous women, sleeping between midnight and 3 a.m. both before and during pregnancy increased the risk of postpartum depression. This association showed a significant modifying effect when compared with primiparous women. Furthermore, among multiparous women, feeling bad when waking up in the morning during pregnancy was associated with an increased risk of postpartum depression, and this modifying effect was also statistically significant. Previous studies have also reported an association between maternal night-type chronotype and postpartum depression [52]. However, no association has been reported between maternal night-type chronotype and postpartum depression in primiparous women [53]. While bedtime tends to advance during early pregnancy, it typically returns to pre-pregnancy levels by late pregnancy [20]. Mothers who were night-oriented before pregnancy likely maintained night-oriented sleep habits throughout late pregnancy and the postpartum period. Primiparous mothers, even if they are night-oriented, can adjust their own rhythms to match the newborn's sleep–wake rhythm. However, multiparous mothers are influenced not only by the newborn's sleep–wake rhythm, but also by the social activities of older siblings [54]. This may have created a situation in which it was difficult for the mother to adjust her sleep rhythm, such as by extending morning sleep time. Furthermore, multiparous women who experienced feeling bad when waking up in the morning during pregnancy may have experienced even worse feelings upon waking during the postpartum period due to this difficulty in adjusting their chronotype. To compound this, multiparous women are typically older and may experience greater fatigue due to age-related declines in sleep quality and recovery. Combined with fragmented sleep and increased caregiving demands for multiple children, this cumulative fatigue may contribute to an elevated risk of postpartum depression. These processes may have contributed to reduced sleep quality, increased maternal cortisol levels [48], and an increased risk of depression [49] among multiparous women during the postpartum period.

This study did not clarify the relationship between quality of sleep in the pre-pregnancy stage and postpartum depression. In a study of psychiatric patients, it was reported that subjective reports of insomnia were more strongly associated with depression than objective observations of insomnia [55]. Thus far, few studies have explored the relationship between pre-pregnancy sleep and postpartum depression. To clarify the relationship between pre-pregnancy sleep and depression, it is important to obtain information about subjective sleep quality in the pre-pregnancy period.

Conclusion

This study clarified the relationship between sleep before and during pregnancy and the risk of postpartum depression. A new finding, particularly among multiparous women, revealed that bedtime between midnight and 3 a.m. during both the pre-pregnancy and pregnancy periods increases the risk of postpartum depression. For multiparous women, establishing the lifestyle habit of going to bed by midnight during the pre-pregnancy stage is considered useful for avoiding an increased risk of postpartum depression.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, the sleep and depression data used in this study were obtained from self-administered questionnaires. This may have resulted in sleep not reflecting actual conditions. Moreover, the sleep-related questions are original and their validity has not been established. Although categorization may result in information loss compared to continuous analysis, this approach was adopted to facilitate clinical interpretation and comparison with prior literature. Furthermore, depression was identified with only a self-administered questionnaire survey and not a clinical diagnosis; therefore, related results must be interpreted with caution. Second, information on sleep in the pre-pregnancy period was based on participants’ recollection. This may have resulted in inaccurate reporting of pre-pregnancy status. Although it is difficult to measure sleep before pregnancy with objective measurement tools, prospective longitudinal studies starting from the pre-pregnancy period are needed to clarify the relationship between pre-pregnancy sleep and postpartum depression. Third, age of other children of multiparous women, their mental state (excluding psychiatric history), use of sleeping pills, minor complications during pregnancy, and daily social support were not included as a covariate in this study. Therefore, further research including these factors as a covariate is needed. Fourth, as social conditions have evolved since the time of the survey, caution is warranted when generalizing the findings to present-day contexts.
