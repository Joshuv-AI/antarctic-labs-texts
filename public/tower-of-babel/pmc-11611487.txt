Comparative analysis of gut microbiota of Chinese Kunming dog, German Shepherd dog, and Belgian Malinois dog
Authors: Qingmei Hu, Luguang Cheng, Xueting Cao, Feng Shi, Yunjie Ma, Liling Mo, Junyu Li, Siyi Zhu, Zichao Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ImportanceThe composition of the gut microbiota is essential for a dog’s health and its adaptation to the environment. Different bacteria can produce the same essential metabolites beneficial to health owing to bacterial functional redundancy in microbial communities.ObjectiveThis study examined the gut bacterial communities of dogs from different breeds, all kept under identical domestication conditions.MethodsNoninvasive sampling and 16S rRNA high-throughput sequencing were used to compare the composition and function of the gut microbiota of three dog breeds: the Chinese Kunming dog (CKD), German Shepherd dog (GSD), and Belgian Malinois dog (BMD).ResultsThe gut microbiota of the three dog breeds consisted of 257 species across 146 genera, 60 families, 35 orders, 15 classes, and 10 phyla. The dominant bacterial phyla across the three breeds were Firmicutes (57.44%), Fusobacteriota (28.86%), and Bacteroidota (7.63%), while the dominant bacterial genera across the three breeds were Peptostreptococcus (21.08%), Fusobacterium (18.50%), Lactobacillus (12.37%), and Cetobacter (10.29%). Further analysis revealed significant differences in the intestinal flora of the three breeds at the phylum and genus levels. The intestinal flora of BMD was significantly richer than that of CKD and GSD. The functional prediction and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes analysis showed that the primary functions of the gut microbiota in these breeds were similar, with significant enrichment in various metabolic pathways, including carbohydrate and amino acid metabolism, secondary metabolite biosynthesis, and microbial metabolism in different environments. The intestinal flora of these breeds also played a crucial role in genetic information processing, including transcription, translation, replication, and material transport.Conclusions and RelevanceThese results provide novel insights into the intestinal flora of intervention dogs and suggest novel methods to improve their health status, which help increase microbial diversity and normalize metabolite production in diseased dogs.

INTRODUCTION

The intestine, as the largest and most complex micro-ecosystem in animals, is inhabited by a wide variety of microorganisms. A delicate balance exists between these microorganisms and the host, which is affected by the external environment and by the host's internal conditions [1]. This dynamic balance is crucial for understanding how the host adapts to the environment, particularly in promoting the digestion and absorption of nutrients, maintaining the normal physiological functions of the intestinal tract, and regulating the immune system [23]. Several studies have confirmed that intestinal flora can synthesize nutrients, such as vitamin B and essential amino acids, that the host cannot synthesize through metabolic processes [4]. In addition, intestinal flora can hydrolyze carbohydrates to produce a large number of short-chain fatty acids that bind to the G protein-coupled receptors to regulate the host immune homeostasis [5].

The mammalian intestinal tract contains several microorganisms, including bacteria, fungi, viruses, and parasites [6], which exist in a symbiotic relationship with the host. Over the long course of evolution, the abundance and diversity of bacteria in these gut microorganisms change according to the host genetic signatures [789]. For example, the intestinal flora of a giant panda (Ailuropoda melanoleuca) is similar to that of a carnivore [10] and contains Clostridium, which enables it to metabolize cellulose and hemicellulose, allowing it to obtain energy and nutrition from bamboo [11]. In addition, the intestinal flora of cats and dogs can improve the bioavailability of their diets by degrading starch and cellulose using glycoside hydrolase [12]. The ratio of Sclerenchyma to Bacteroides is higher in rock sheep (Pseudois nayaur) at high altitudes than in European argali (Ovis aries musimon) at low altitudes, allowing rock sheep to adapt better to high altitudes [13]. In addition, each lemur of the family Lemuridae has a unique microbial metabolic pathway to adapt to different niches in the same environment [14].

The Chinese Kunming dog (CKD), German Shepherd dog (GSD), and Belgian Malinois dog (BMD) are breeds of Canis lupus familiaris, belonging to the family Canidae of the order Carnivora, which are distributed worldwide. The CKD, GSD, and BMD breeds are used widely as police dogs and play an important role in identifying physical evidence, searching and tracking, escorting and guarding, as well as patrolling and explosion-proofing [15]. These breeds represent different genetic backgrounds: the CKD, a locally bred working dog in China, and the GSD and BMD, which are internationally recognized breeds with distinct lineages. This genetic diversity provides a broad foundation for studying the microbiota differences influenced by breed-specific genetics [16]. Each breed is commonly used in various working roles, such as police, military, and search and rescue operations. The different environments and stress levels these breeds encounter in their work may contribute to variations in their gut microbiota, making them ideal candidates for comparative studies [17]. In addition, each breed has predispositions to certain health conditions that may be linked to their microbiota. Studying these dogs can help understand how the microbiota might influence or correlate with breed-specific health issues [18].

The dietary composition affects the intestinal flora of police dogs. For example, dietary fiber can increase the proportion of beneficial bacteria and improve the intestinal flora of police dogs [19]. In addition, nutrients, such as probiotics and prebiotics, can improve the immunity of the intestinal mucosal system of police dogs, inhibiting the invasion and colonization of various pathogens [20]. Nevertheless, there are limited reports on the intestinal flora of CKD, GSD, and BMD.

This study used noninvasive sampling and 16S rRNA high-throughput sequencing to compare and analyze the intestinal flora of CKD, GSD, and BMD inhabited under the same domestication conditions. The analysis of the fecal microbial composition of different police dog breeds can help determine the underlying causes for variations in the intestinal flora of species bred in the same habitat and understand the interactions between host and intestinal flora. The results may provide a scientific basis for intestinal flora research and provide new insights into improving the health status of police dogs. The intestinal health of police dogs can be maintained through reasonable diet adjustment, personalized nutritional supplementation, and regular detection and intervention.

METHODS

Animals and diets

The CKD, GSD, and BMD breeds used in this study were obtained from the Kunming Police Dog Base, Ministry of Public Security (Kunming, China). Each breed was raised in a farmer’s kennel in Dongcun Town, Fumin County, Kunming City, Yunnan Province (E: 102.673305°, N: 25.517169°). Animal care and handling were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Experimental Animal Welfare Ethics Committee of Kunming University. The analysis included 15 individuals: five CKD (two females and three males), five GSD (one female and four males), and five BMD (three females and two males), all with similar birth dates. The puppies were raised with their mothers until weaned. Subsequently, the puppies were transferred to independent kennels for feeding. The dog food purchased from the Mars Pet Food Co., Ltd. (China) was composed of crude protein (≥ 30%), crude fat (≥ 14.5%), crude fiber (≤ 5.0%), water-soluble chloride (≥ 0.4%, Cl−), calcium (≥ 1.0%), phosphorus (≥ 0.8%), zinc (≤ 200 mg/kg), crude ash (≤ 12.0%), water (≤ 10.0%), lysine (≥ 0.85%), and vitamin E (≥ 80 IU/kg). The puppies were not administered any antibiotics, probiotics, prebiotics, drugs, or nutrition products.

Sample collection

This study used a noninvasive sampling technique to obtain fecal samples. Three-month-old healthy puppies of similar weights were selected for this analysis (n = 15; 5 CKD: CKD1–CKD5, 5 GSD: GSD1–GSD5, and 5 BMD: BMD1–BMD5). Sterile gloves and masks were worn during sample collection, and large pieces of fresh, pollution-free feces were collected as quickly as possible. The collection of fecal samples in contact with the ground or other impurities was avoided, where possible. The fecal samples were cryopreserved at −80°C [17].

DNA extraction

The fecal samples were sent to Shanghai Meiji Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd. (China) for total microbial DNA extraction. The total microbial DNA was extracted using the E.Z.N.A soil DNA kit (OMEGA, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The DNA integrity was assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis (AGE), and the concentration and purity of the samples were detected quantitatively using the NanoDrop2000.

16S rRNA gene sequencing

The V3–V4 variable region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using the primers 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) [21]. The PCR conditions were as follows: 95°C for 3 min; 30 cycles of 95°C for 30 s, 45°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 45 s; and 72°C for 10 min. The experiments were conducted in three replicates, and the PCR products of the same sample were mixed and detected by 2% AGE. The PCR products were then cut and recovered using the AxyPrepDNA Gel Recovery Kit (AXYGEN, USA), eluted with Tris-HCl, and detected by 2% AGE. Subsequently, the PCR products were quantified using the QuantiFluorTM-ST Blue Fluorescence Quantification System (Promega, USA). The PCR products were then mixed according to their concentration and sequenced on the Miseq PE300 platform (Illumina, USA). The 16S rRNA database construction and sequencing were conducted using Shanghai Meiji Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd.

Statistical analysis

Paired-end reads obtained from Illumina sequencing were spliced based on the overlap using FLASH (https://ccb.jhu.edu/software/FLASH/index.shtml, v1.2.11) and quality controlled and filtered using fastp (https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp, v0.19.6). Subsequently, the sequences were classified into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) according to a 97% similarity level [22]. Organisms within the same OTU defined by 97% similarity are likely to occupy similar ecological niches, contributing to more accurate assessments of the community composition and function [2324]. The species diversity indices and sequencing depth were then analyzed. The OTUs were annotated using the Ribosomal Database Project Bayesian Classifier (https://sourceforge.net/projects/rdp-classifier, v2.13) against the Silva 16S rRNA database (https://www.arb-silva.de, v138). The bacterial community composition at the species level was generated using QIIME (http://qiime.org/install/index.html, v1.9.1) [25]. Statistical analysis of the sequencing data was conducted using the Kruskal–Wallis and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. The p values < 0.05 were considered significant. The principal component analysis of different groups was conducted on the Megasonson Cloud Analysis Platform (https://cloud.majorbio.com), and functional prediction analysis was conducted using PICRUSt software (http://picrust.github.io/picrust, v1.1.0).

RESULTS

DNA extraction and 16S rRNA gene sequencing

The DNA samples extracted from the fecal samples of CKD, GSD, and BMD were amplified by PCR and validated by agarose gel electrophoresis. A total of 994,123 optimized sequences with an average length of 415 bp were obtained through Illumina MiSeq sequencing and denoising. The rarefaction curves, generated using the Sob index, showed that the curves flattened as the sequencing depth increased in randomly selected data (Supplementary Fig. 1A). The species accumulation curve, constructed using R mapping, displayed the OTU ranking on the x-axis and the relative percentage content of the OTU on the y-axis. This curve indicated a gradual flattening of the species distribution, suggesting an even species distribution (Supplementary Fig. 1B).

Composition of intestinal flora of different dog breeds

The nonrepetitive sequences (excluding single sequences) were clustered into OTUs according to 97% sequence similarity. Two hundred and fifty-seven species, representing 146 genera, 60 families, 35 orders, 15 classes, and 10 phyla, were identified in the intestinal flora of the CKD, BMD, and GSD breeds. The intestinal flora of CKD contained 189 species, representing 113 genera, 52 families, 32 orders, 14 classes, and nine phyla. The intestinal flora of BMD contained 213 species, representing 125 genera, 50 families, 27 orders, 12 classes, and eight phyla. The intestinal flora of GSD contained 209 species, representing 123 genera, 94 families, 29 orders, 13 classes, and eight phyla. At the phylum level, the dominant phyla across the three breeds are Firmicutes (57.44%), Fusobacteriota (28.86%), and Bacteroidota (7.63%) (Fig. 1A). At the genus level, Lactobacillus (16.31%) was the most abundant genus in the CKD fecal samples, followed by Norank_f_Muribaculaceae (11.12%), Pasteurella (10.63%), Fusobacterium (10.27%), Cetobacterium (9.73%), Peptostreptococcus (8.54%), Norank_f_Erysipelotrichaceae (7.15%), Romboutsia (3.68%), Peptoclostridium (1.94%), Turicibacter (1.87%), Allobaculum (1.73%), Bacteroides (1.61%), and Bifidobacterium (1.23%) (Fig. 1B). In contrast, Fusobacterium (22.64%) was the most abundant genus in the BMD fecal samples, followed by Peptostreptococcus (14.55%), Norank_f_Erysipelotrichaceae (10.88%), Lactobacillus (10.52%), Cetobacterium (7.11%), Norank_f_Muribaculaceae (6.48%), Allobaculum (3.62%), Peptoclostridium (2.81%), Coriobacteriaceae_UCG-002 (2.11%), Prevotella (1.98%), and Romboutsia (1.18%) (Fig. 1B). Fusobacterium (18.69%) was the most abundant genus in the GSD fecal samples, followed by Peptoclostridium (11.22%), Prevostella (9.74%), Norank_f_Muribaculaceae (6.64%), Faecalibacterium (5.47%), Alloprevotella (4.76%), Cetobacterium (4.39%), Romboutsia (3.67%), Blautia (3.37%), Bacteroides (2.46%), Norank_f_Erysipelotrichaceae (1.97%), Peptostreptococcus (1.95%), and Ruminococcus_torques_group (1.34%) (Fig. 1B). 221 OTUs were identified in the fecal samples of CKD, BMD, and GSD by Venn analysis (Fig. 1C).

Alpha and beta diversity analysis of the intestinal flora of CKD, BMD, and GSD

Alpha diversity analysis in canine gut microbiome studies provides insights into the richness and evenness of microbial species within an individual sample [16]. In this study, alpha diversity analysis showed that the Ace (322.48) and Chao (311.10) indices of BMD were significantly higher than those of CKD (Ace index: 277.03, Chao index: 269.60) and GSD (Ace index: 270.04, Chao index: 267.96) (Fig. 4A). In addition, the Simpson index of BMD (0.09585) was higher than that of CKD (0.07911) and GSD (0.08588). The Shannon index of GSD (3.2255) was higher than that of CKD (3.1669) and BMD (3.1637) (Fig. 4B). The richness and diversity of the intestinal flora in BMD were higher than in both CKD and GSD, while the richness and diversity in CKD were higher than in GSD.

Beta diversity (β-diversity) analysis in canines provides insights into the differences in microbial communities across various dog breeds or groups [29]. The β-diversity of intestinal flora between CKD and GSD, as explained by PCO1 and PCO2, accounted for 37.95%, showing relative aggregation within species and significant differences between species (p < 0.005, Fig. 4C). PCO1 and PCO2 explained 42.93% of the β-diversity of intestinal flora between CKD and BMD. The CKD group exhibited relative intraspecies clustering, while the BMD group showed relative intraspecies dispersion, with the interspecies population structure exhibiting high similarity and reaching a significant level of differentiation (p < 0.005, Fig. 4D). PCO1 and PCO2 explained 41.70% of the intestinal flora β-diversity between GSD and BMD. The GSD group showed relative intraspecies aggregation, whereas the BMD group displayed relative intraspecies dispersion, indicating differences between species, even though these differences did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 4E). The gut microbiota β-diversity, as explained by PCO1 and PCO2, accounted for 31.32% between CKD, GSD, and BMD, showing a distinct spatial distribution (Fig. 4F). This suggests that when comparing two or three breeds, their gut flora form entirely different taxonomic clusters with significant differences between them. Among these groups, under the same domestication conditions, the microbial community was relatively diverse in the BMD group and strongly influenced by external factors such as diet, disease, and environment.

Functional prediction of the intestinal flora

Functional prediction analysis and KEGG analysis of the sequences showed that the primary functions of the intestinal flora of the CKD, BMD, and GSD were similar. The intestinal flora of these three breeds was enriched in the metabolic pathways, secondary metabolite biosynthesis, microbial metabolism in different environments, amino acid biosynthesis, carbon metabolism, ABC transporters, purine metabolism, quorum sensing, and two-component system. They were enriched in the starch and sucrose metabolism; pyrimidine metabolism; amino acid sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism; glycolysis; aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis; pyruvate metabolism; homologous recombination; cysteine and methionine metabolism; alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism; prokaryotic carbon fixation pathway; and other metabolic pathways (Fig. 5A). The clusters of orthologous groups functional classification showed that the intestinal flora of CKD, BMD, and GSD was associated with genetic information processing and metabolic pathways related to various activities, including transcription, translation, replication, material transport, and metabolism (Fig. 5B).

DISCUSSION

This study examined the intestinal flora of CKD, GSD, and BMD under the same domestication conditions. Firmicutes (57.44%), Fusobacteriota (28.86%), and Bacteroidota (7.63%) were the dominant phyla across the three breeds, whereas Peptostreptococcus (21.08%), Fusobacterium (18.50%), Lactobacillus (12.37%), and Cetobacter (10.29%) were the dominant genera across the three breeds. These results are consistent with the reports on the intestinal flora of wolf (Canis lupus) and Labrador Retriever Dogs [3031], as well as reports on human and mammalian intestinal flora [32]. The abundance of Firmicutes in mammals can be attributed to the high consumption of protein-rich food. Studies have shown that Firmicutes can digest proteins and carbohydrates and participate in metabolic processes.

In the present study, no significant differences in the intestinal flora of CKD, GSD, and BMD were observed at the species level, which could arise from a shared domestication environment and uniform dietary intake. The feeding habits can affect the intestinal flora of the hosts [3334]. Under the same feeding habits, the composition of the intestinal flora of different breeds can show convergence [35]. For example, a study on primates found that the intestinal flora of Leontopithecus chrysomelas, Saguinus midas, and Saguinus oedipus were similar and that these mammals consumed highly similar diets [636]. Furthermore, a study on Cervus dybowski and Cervus canadensis alashanicus deer breeds that primarily consumed corn and alfalfa showed that the dominant phyla in their intestinal flora were Firmicutes, Bacteroidota, and Proteobacteria [37]. In contrast, studies found that the composition of intestinal flora of the same breed can change because of different feeding habits. For example, a study found that the abundance of Firmicutes and Proteobacteria was higher in the intestinal flora of Cebus capucinus in captivity, whose main food source was invertebrates than in those who feed on fruits [38]. In this study, the intestinal flora of CKD, GSD, and BMD, which inhabited the same environment and consumed the same diet, shared similar characteristics, suggesting that the diet and environment affect the composition of the intestinal flora of the host.

The intestinal flora of canine species was reported to be affected by host species specificity [2039]. For example, the abundance of Firmicutes, Actinobacteria, Proteobacteria, Fusobacteria, Bacteroidota, and Cyanobacteria was significantly lower in the intestinal flora of a wolf than in other canine species. In addition, the abundance of Fusobacteria and Proteobacteria was significantly higher in Labrador Retriever Dogs than in other dog breeds, while the abundance of Firmicutes and Actinobacteria was significantly lower than that of other dogs [40]. Another study found that the intestinal flora of wild red foxes (Vulpes vulpes), sand foxes (Vulpes corsac), and blue foxes (Vulpes lagopus) have significantly lower intraspecific differences than interspecific differences. Moreover, the interspecific differences are unaffected by habitat degradation [41]. The present study found that the variations in the composition of intestinal flora between CKD-BMD and CKD-GSD were greater than those between BMD-GSD at the phylum and genus levels, suggesting that species specificity affects the intestinal flora of the host to a certain extent.

Previous studies have shown that age, sex, and physiological state are important factors affecting the intestinal flora of dogs [42]. For example, among captive Beagle and Cocker Spaniel breeds, the abundance of Pseudomonas adaceae and Escherichia was significantly higher. In contrast, the abundance of Actinobacteria was significantly lower in the aged group (> 7 years) than in the adult group (2–7 years) [43]. A study on the intestinal flora of Maltese found that the abundance of Bacteroidota and Prehostella was significantly higher in females than in males, while the abundance of Atopobiaceae and Megamonas was significantly higher in males than in females. In addition, there were significant differences in the beta diversities of the male and female individuals [44]. Previous studies on captive Border Collies found that the abundance of Prevostella, Peptostreptococcus, and Allisonella was significantly lower in the intestinal flora of normal-weight individuals than in overweight individuals [40]. In the present study, the composition of the intestinal flora of three police dogs showed some interspecific and intraspecific differences despite inhabiting the same domestication conditions and consuming the same food. Based on the aforementioned reports, the interspecific and intraspecific differences in the composition of the intestinal flora of CKD, GSD, and BMD may be attributed to the age, sex, and physiological state of the individuals.

The functional prediction and KEGG analysis of the intestinal flora of the three dog breeds, CKD, BMD, and GSD, showed significant similarities in their primary functions. The enrichment of the metabolic pathways, secondary metabolite biosynthesis, and various other metabolic activities suggests that despite the different genetic backgrounds and possibly different diets or environments, these dog breeds share a conserved gut microbiome functionality that is critical for maintaining basic physiological processes. This finding aligns with similar studies in other species, where the core gut microbiome functions appear to be conserved across different hosts. For example, Moya and Ferrer reported that the human gut microbiome is highly enriched in the metabolic pathways, particularly those involved in carbohydrate and amino acid metabolism, which are essential for maintaining the energy balance and overall health [45]. Similarly, the research reported that the gut microbiomes of various mammalian species, including mice and humans, show conserved functions related to nutrient metabolism, even though the microbial composition may vary [46]. The enrichment in pathways such as starch and sucrose metabolism, aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis, and amino acid metabolism in CKD, BMD, and GSD suggests that the gut microbiota of these dogs are well-adapted to diets rich in carbohydrates and proteins. This is consistent with findings from Kiarie and Mills, who reported that the canine gut microbiome is particularly efficient in the carbohydrate metabolism, which is crucial considering the typically high-carbohydrate content in commercial dog foods [16]. Moreover, the COG functional classification indicated a strong association with genetic information processing and metabolic pathways, including transcription, translation, and replication. This mirrors the findings of other studies, such as Xie et al. [47], which showed that the gut microbiota in various animals, including dogs, plays a critical role in maintaining host health by ensuring efficient genetic information processing and material transport. Overall, the functional similarities observed across these dog breeds, despite their diverse backgrounds, underscore the essential and conserved nature of the gut microbiome functions in maintaining host health. These results contribute to a broader understanding of how the gut microbiota support the physiological and metabolic needs of different hosts and highlight potential targets for improving canine health through diet and microbiome management.

In conclusion, the intestinal flora of CKD, GSD, and BMD exhibit similarities and differences in composition under the same habitat, domestication, and similar food sources. This study clarified the core species composition of police dogs at the phylum and genus taxonomic levels. The observed similarities in the gut microbiome composition and function across these breeds, particularly the enrichment in metabolic pathways and genetic information processing, underscore the conserved nature of the gut microbiome functions crucial for maintaining host health. Although environmental factors and uniform diets appear to contribute to these functional similarities, this study also acknowledged that interspecific and intraspecific differences are likely influenced by factors such as age, sex, and physiological state. These findings show that while the gut microbiome is highly adaptable, certain core functions remain conserved across different species, providing critical support to the host's physiological processes. The results of the present study elucidated the role of the intestinal microbiome in health and disease, providing a theoretical basis and technical support for the development of probiotic strains that can regulate the intestinal function of pets and maintain the intestinal microecological balance of police dogs.
