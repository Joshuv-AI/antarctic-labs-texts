Hepatocyte-Derived S100A4/A6/A10/A11 Proteins Promote Liver Fibrosis by Direct and Indirect Activation of HSCs
Authors: He Tong, Yingxue Zhang, Haiting Pan, Sarina Wang, Yan Zhao, Zhuoya Zhao, Yulong Bao, Li Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Liver fibrosis is a chronic disease caused by various etiologies, including dysregulated interactions among hepatocytes, KCs, and HSCs. Studies have shown that the S100 protein family plays an important role in liver fibrosis, especially the S100A4/A6/A10/A11 proteins; however, the effects of hepatocyte-derived S100A4/A6/A10/A11 proteins on the crosstalk between KCs and HSCs in liver fibrosis are unknown. The aim of this study was to investigate how hepatocyte-derived S100A4/A6/A10/A11 proteins modulate KC and HSC activation during liver fibrosis. Fibrotic, HCC and ICC human liver paraffin-embedded block samples were obtained. A CCl4-induced liver fibrosis model was established, and in this model, S100A4/A6/A10/A11 proteins were blocked using specific antibodies. AML-12 mouse hepatocytes, KCs, and HSCs were used in vitro. During liver fibrosis, S100A4/A6/A10/A11 proteins are released by hepatocytes and function as DAMPs that activate KCs via the p38 and JNK signaling pathways. This activation triggers the release of inflammatory cytokines, which in turn promote HSC activation and collagen deposition through a TGF-β/SMAD2-dependent mechanism. Moreover, S100A4/A6/A10/A11 proteins can directly stimulate HSC activation and collagen deposition via the TGF-β/SMAD2 pathway. Blocking S100A4/A6/A10/A11 proteins significantly attenuated the release of inflammatory cytokines and reduced collagen deposition. Clinical evidence further indicates that the expression of S100A4/A6/A10/A11 proteins is significantly upregulated in liver tissues from patients with HCC and ICC. This study reveals a dual regulatory mechanism in liver fibrosis: S100A4/A6/A10/A11 proteins derived from hepatocytes not only indirectly promote HSC activation by stimulating KCs to release pro-inflammatory factors but also directly activate HSCs. These findings highlight the potential of this group of S100 proteins as a promising therapeutic target for liver fibrosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10753-026-02524-4.

Introduction

Liver fibrosis represents a global health challenge, affecting millions worldwide and leading to severe complications such as cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma. Current diagnostic tools often lack sensitivity in early stages, and effective anti-fibrotic therapies remain limited, underscoring an urgent need for novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Liver fibrosis is linked to an abnormal liver microenvironment that is characterized by increased levels of pro-inflammatory elements and the continuous activation of hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) [1]. Nonparenchymal cells form an intercellular crosstalk network with parenchymal hepatocytes, and the dysregulation of the interactions in this network is responsible for liver fibrosis [2]. During the process of fibrosis, hepatocytes, as the core functional units, are often the earliest type of cells to be damaged. HSCs are the main source of extracellular matrix (ECM). Kupffer cells (KCs), on the other hand, drive inflammatory responses [3, 4]. Although significant progress has been made in understanding these intercellular interactions, the specific molecular mechanisms that initiate and maintain these intercellular interactions remain unclear, which somewhat hinders the development of effective anti-fibrotic therapies.

The fibrotic cascade is initiated by hepatocyte injury, in which damaged hepatocytes release damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that recruit and activate KCs and infiltrating immune cells (e.g., neutrophils and natural killer cells) [5, 6]. These immune cells, in turn, secrete pro-inflammatory (e.g., TNF-α and IL-6) [7] and pro-fibrotic (e.g., TGF-β and PDGF) [8] mediators, further activating HSCs and promoting their proliferation and excessive ECM production. Notably, this pathological process can be mitigated if DAMP production is suppressed or if pathogenic stimuli are promptly removed, leading to decreased levels of pro-inflammatory signals and HSC inactivation. Thus, deciphering the hepatocyte-mediated regulatory network governing HSC activation is of paramount importance for both understanding fibrogenesis and identifying novel therapeutic targets.

The S100 protein family, comprising 24 distinct members, is functionally classified into three principal categories: those with exclusively intracellular roles, those possessing both intracellular and extracellular functions, and those predominantly involved in extracellular regulation. These proteins are unique to vertebrates, exhibiting cell-type-specific expression profiles. Intriguingly, while not typically present under normal physiological conditions, certain S100 proteins can be induced in specific cell types during pathological states. Intracellularly, S100 proteins play pivotal roles in modulating fundamental cellular processes such as proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, calcium homeostasis, energy metabolism, inflammation, and migration/invasion. They achieve this by interacting with a diverse array of target molecules, including enzymes, cytoskeletal components, receptors, transcription factors, and nucleic acids. Furthermore, a subset of S100 proteins are externalized from cells, mediating autocrine and paracrine effects. Their extracellular actions are facilitated by the activation of various receptors, including cell surface receptors like the receptor for advanced glycation end-products (RAGE) and toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), G-protein-coupled receptors, and scavenger receptors, as well as through interactions with heparan sulfate proteoglycans and N-glycans. These extracellular S100 proteins modulate the activity of a broad spectrum of cell types, encompassing immune cells (monocytes, macrophages, microglia, neutrophils, lymphocytes, mast cells), structural cells (articular chondrocytes, endothelial and vascular smooth muscle cells, epithelial cells), and neural/muscle cells (neurons, astrocytes, Schwann cells, myoblasts, cardiomyocytes). Consequently, they contribute significantly to processes like innate and adaptive immunity, cell migration and chemotaxis, tissue development and repair, and the invasion dynamics of leukocytes and tumor cells [9–12]. This protein family plays a pivotal role in the initiation and progression of fibrotic diseases. Among its members, S100A4 is markedly upregulated in fibrosis, affecting multiple organs—including the liver, kidneys, and lungs—where it facilitates fibroblast activation and collagen deposition through the modulation of signaling pathways such as c-Myb and TGF-β/SMAD3 [13–16]. S100A6, whose expression is elevated under conditions such as liver fibrosis and systemic sclerosis, interacts with the receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE) to activate the ERK pathway, thereby driving fibrosis progression [17]. S100A10 influences the fibrinolytic system and extracellular matrix degradation through complex formation with annexin A2; loss of its function contributes to fibrin deposition and chronic inflammation [18]. S100A11 promotes fibrosis in the liver, bile duct, and myocardium by regulating the TGF-β/SMAD3 signaling pathway. Emerging evidence indicates that certain S100 proteins are dysregulated in liver fibrosis [19–21]. However, prior research has predominantly examined their expressions in immune cells or HSCs. The specific contributions of hepatocyte-derived S100A4/A6/A10/A11 proteins in modulating the critical crosstalk between KCs and HSCs remain to be systematically investigated.

In the current study, we systematically investigated the dynamic expression profiles of S100A4/6/10/11 proteins in a liver fibrosis model and revealed their sustained upregulation throughout the progression of fibrosis. Further mechanistic studies revealed a dual-pathway regulatory mechanism by which hepatocyte-derived S100A4/6/10/11 proteins promote fibrogenesis indirectly by amplifying KC-mediated inflammatory and fibrotic cascades and directly by driving profibrotic phenotypic transformation in HSCs. These findings not only elucidate the multifaceted roles of S100A4/6/10/11 proteins in liver fibrosis, offering new insights into intercellular crosstalk in liver disease but also provide a robust theoretical framework for developing multitarget antifibrotic therapies targeting the S100A signaling axis.

Materials and Methods

Patient Specimens

Human paraffin-embedded liver cirrhosis (LC), cholangiocarcinoma (ICC), and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) tissue sections were obtained from the Department of Pathology at Inner Mongolia Medical University. The collection included five samples of LC (labeled LC-No. 1 to LC-No. 5), five samples of ICC (ICC-No. 1 to ICC-No. 5), and five samples of HCC (HCC-No. 1 to HCC-No. 5).

Animals and Housing

Specific pathogen-free (SPF) male C57BL/6J mice (6–8 weeks old) were purchased from Beijing Vitalstar Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Upon arrival, the mice were acclimatized for one week under standard housing conditions prior to any experimental procedures. All the animals were housed at the Laboratory Animal Center of Inner Mongolia Medical University in individually ventilated cages under strictly controlled environmental conditions: a constant ambient temperature of 20–22℃, a relative humidity of 60 ± 10%, and a 12 h/12 h light/dark cycle. Standard laboratory rodent feed and autoclaved water were provided ad libitum. All experimental protocols involving animals were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Inner Mongolia Medical University (Approval No.: YKD202501192); the protocols were performed in strict compliance with the institutional guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals.

Animal Model of Liver Fibrosis

The mice were randomly divided into two groups: a control group (n = 50) and a carbon tetrachloride (CCl4)-induced liver fibrosis model group (n = 50). After one week of acclimatization under standard laboratory conditions, the mice in the model group received intraperitoneal injections of CCl4 diluted in olive oil (9:1, v/v) at a dosage of 5 µL per gram of body weight twice weekly. To minimize metabolic stress, the animals were fasted (with water provided ad libitum) for 12 h before and after each CCl4 administration. The control group was given an equivalent volume of olive oil vehicle on the same schedule. To track the progression of fibrosis, subsets of mice (n = 10 per group per time point) were humanely euthanized at 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 weeks after the initial injection for subsequent sample collection.

S100A4/6/10/11 Antibody Intervention

Following pre-acclimatization, mice were randomly assigned to three groups (n = 10/group): a control group, a CCl4 model group, and an S100A4/6/10/11 antibody blockade group. For the first three days, the control and CCl4 model groups received daily intravenous (i.v.) injections of 100 µL PBS. Concurrently, the S100A4/6/10/11 antibody blockade group received daily i.v. injections of 100 µL of an S100A4/6/10/11 antibody mixture (four antibodies, 5 µg each, total 20 µg). On day 4, following this pretreatment period, the CCl4 model and S100A4/6/10/11 antibody blockade groups received a single intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of CCl4 (diluted in olive oil, 9:1 v/v) at a dose of 5 µL/g body weight. The control group received an equivalent volume of olive oil via i.p. injection as a solvent control. Sample collection occurred at 48 and 120 h post-CCl4 injection. At each time point, mice were humanely euthanized. Blood was collected via orbital sinus puncture for serum isolation, and liver tissue samples were harvested by dissection.

ALT and AST Measurement

Serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) activities, which are established biomarkers of liver injury, were quantitatively assessed using commercial assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China; Cat No. C009-2-1 and C010-2-1) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After the enzymatic reactions were conducted according to the kit protocols, the absorbances of the resulting reaction products were measured at a wavelength of 510 nm using a SPARK multimode microplate reader (Tecan, Switzerland). All measurements were performed in duplicate; enzyme activities were calculated on the basis of a standardized curve and expressed in units per liter (U/L). The methods for measuring the ALT and AST levels in the liver were similar to those described above.

Tissue Collection and Processing

At each endpoint, the mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation under deep anesthesia. Whole livers were promptly excised, thoroughly rinsed in ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove excess blood, and weighed. Each liver sample was then divided into several aliquots for various downstream analyses. For biochemical and molecular studies, liver fragments were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until further processing. For histological examinations, liver sections were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for a minimum of 48 h. The fixed tissues were subsequently dehydrated using a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Embedded tissues were sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm using a rotary microtome (Leica, Germany). For routine histological assessments, the sections were mounted on glass slides and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE) according to standard protocols.

Histological Assessment

For morphological evaluations, liver tissues that were freshly isolated from the experimental mice were immediately rinsed in ice-cold physiological saline to remove excess blood. The tissues were then fixed by immersion in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 24 h at 4 °C. Following fixation, the tissue blocks were labeled, thoroughly rinsed under running tap water overnight to remove any residual fixative, and processed using a standard histological protocol. This process involved sequential dehydration in a graded ethanol series, clearing in xylene, and infiltration and embedding in paraffin wax. The embedded tissues were subsequently cut into 4-µm thick sections using a rotary microtome. For histological analysis, the sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin for general morphological examination or with Masson’s trichrome stain (Maixin Biotech, China; Cat. No. MAB0249) to specifically detect collagen deposition. After staining, the sections were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted with synthetic resin. Stained sections were visualized and imaged using a bright-field microscope (Olympus BX-53, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a high-resolution digital camera. A minimum of five random fields per liver section were examined for pathological evaluation by observers who were blinded to the experimental groups.

Immunohistochemical Analysis

The paraffin sections were baked overnight at 60 °C, followed by conventional dewaxing with xylene and hydration through a graded ethanol series. The following procedures were subsequently performed sequentially: endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by incubating the sections with 3% H2O2 in methanol for 25 min at room temperature, and antigen retrieval was conducted using citrate buffer under microwave heating. The sections were then blocked with 3% goat serum at room temperature for 45 min to minimize nonspecific binding. After blocking, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted at 1:200. Negative controls were prepared by replacing the primary antibody with PBS, while positive controls consisted of known positive tissue sections. After thorough washing with PBS, an HRP-labeled secondary antibody was applied, and the sections were incubated at room temperature for 50 min. Color development was performed using a DAB chromogenic solution, with the reaction times monitored under a microscope. The staining reaction was stopped promptly, and nuclear counterstaining was carried out with hematoxylin. Finally, the sections were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral gum. All the sections were observed and imaged using an Olympus BX-53 microscope. The primary antibodies used in the experimental group included α-SMA (Abcam, Cat. No. ab5694); F4/80 (Abcam, Cat. No. ab111101); CD11b (Abcam, Cat. No. ab133357); S100A4 (Abcam, Cat. No. ab197896); S100A6 (Abcam, Cat. No. ab181975); S100A10 (Abcam, Cat. No. ab76472); and S100A11 (Abcam, Cat. No. ab180593).

RNA Extraction and Reverse Transcription

Total RNA was extracted from the samples using TRIzol™ reagent (Invitrogen, United States; Cat. No. 15596026CN) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration and purity of the isolated RNA were determined by measuring the absorbances at 260 and 280 nm using a spectrophotometer. RNA with an A260/A280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0 was considered suitable for subsequent analyses. Subsequently, 1 µg of total RNA from each sample was reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix (Takara, Japan; Cat. No. RR036A). Reverse transcription was performed under the following conditions: 37 °C for 15 min, followed by an inactivation step at 85 °C for 5 s. The synthesized cDNA was diluted and stored at −20 °C for quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT‒qPCR) analysis.

RT‒qPCR

RT‒qPCR was performed to quantify the relative mRNA expressions of the target genes. The reactions were carried out using TB Green™ Premix Ex Taq™ II (Takara, Japan; Cat. No. RR820B) on an Applied Biosystems 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Each 20-µL reaction mixture contained 10 µL of TB Green Premix, 0.8 µL each of forward and reverse primer (10 µM), 2 µL of cDNA template, and 6.4 µL of nuclease-free water. The thermal cycling protocol was as follows: an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of amplification (95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s). Melt curve analysis was conducted at the end of each run to confirm the specificity of the amplification products. All gene-specific primers were designed using Primer-BLAST from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) and were commercially synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). The primer sequences used in this study are listed in Table 1. Relative gene expressions were quantified using the comparative 2-ΔΔCt method. The threshold cycle (Ct) values of the target genes were normalized to those of the endogenous reference gene β-actin to obtain ΔCt. The ΔΔCt value was then calculated by comparing the ΔCt of the experimental group with that of the control group. Western Blotting

Liver tissue samples (100 mg) were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and ground into powder. The powder was lysed on ice for 30 min using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. After centrifugation, the supernatant was collected, and the protein concentrations were quantified by the BCA method and adjusted to 2 µg/µL. Proteins were separated by SDS‒PAGE (80 V for the stacking gel and 120 V for the separating gel) and then transferred to a PVDF membrane via the wet transfer method (300 mA, 60–90 min). The membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h, followed by incubation with primary antibody at 4 °C overnight. After being washed with TBST, the membrane was incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature for 2 h. The protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate. Images were captured with an imaging system, and the grayscale values of the target protein and internal reference (β-actin/GAPDH) bands were analyzed using ImageJ software to determine the relative expression levels.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical Analysis: Data are presented as the means ± SDs. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was verified using the Brown–Forsythe test. For comparisons between two groups, data that met both assumptions were analyzed using unpaired t tests; otherwise, the Mann–Whitney U test was used. For multiple group comparisons, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with the Bonferroni post hoc correction was applied for parametric data, whereas the Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s post hoc corrections was used for nonparametric data. The significance levels are denoted as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001, or ns (not significant). All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software.

Results

Pathogenesis of Liver Fibrosis Involves Inflammatory Infiltration

In the mouse liver fibrosis model (Figure S1), a comprehensive evaluation of the inflammatory response revealed distinct patterns of both cellular infiltration and pro-inflammatory cytokine expression, closely correlating with the progression of fibrogenesis. Immunohistochemical analysis first demonstrated that inflammatory cell infiltration followed spatially and temporally regulated patterns. Specifically, CD11B+ myeloid cells initially accumulated in the periportal regions of the injured areas by 4 weeks, progressively increasing to maximal levels at 8 weeks, and subsequently decreasing at later time points (Fig. 1A). Concurrently, the distribution of F4/80+ macrophages exhibited a biphasic pattern: limited perivenular localization during early stages transitioned into robust parenchymal infiltration during established fibrosis (Fig. 1A). Mirroring these cellular dynamics, RT‒qPCR analysis of murine liver tissues revealed significant temporal alterations in pro-inflammatory cytokine expressions throughout fibrogenesis. Compared with vehicle-treated control mice, CCl4-treated mice exhibited markedly upregulated mRNA levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-17, and IFN-γ at all examined time points. These inflammatory mediators displayed distinct kinetic profiles: while the expressions of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-17 peaked at 8 weeks, that of IFN-γ peaked earlier, at 6 weeks (Fig. 1B). Discussion

Hepatocytes are important regulatory hubs that connect different cell types to mediate cell communication. In this study, we demonstrated that during liver fibrosis, S100A4/A6/A10/A11 proteins are primarily released by hepatocytes. On the one hand, they act as DAMPs to activate KCs, leading to the release of inflammatory factors that subsequently activate HSCs. On the other hand, they can directly activate HSCs. Previous studies have reported that S100 proteins play important roles in the onset and progression of diseases, including fibrosis, inflammatory disease and tumors [22–25]. Similarly, the S100 proteins play key roles in liver disease, especially liver fibrosis and HCC, and may be biomarkers and therapeutic targets for these diseases [13, 26, 27]. The results of the present study indicate that the S100 protein family holds significant promise as a future therapeutic and diagnostic target for hepatic pathologies, thereby providing a robust foundation for advancing related research endeavors.

Several studies have established S100A4 as a key player in fibrosis across various organs, particularly in the liver. Zhang et al. demonstrated that the downregulation of S100A4 expression ameliorates liver fibrosis, improves histological and functional recovery of the liver, and inhibits TGF-β-mediated fibrosis in HSCs by inhibiting the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway [28]. Chen et al. reported that S100A4 is upregulated in liver tissue during the progression of liver fibrosis and that the degree of liver fibrosis decreases after blocking S100A4 expression in vivo [13]. They also reported that S100A4 promotes HSC activation via upregulation of c-Myb expression. Chen et al. analyzed S100A4 levels in patients with cirrhosis and reported that serum S100A4 levels were significantly elevated [13]. Similar results were reported in a study by Louka and Ramzy, in which the expression level of S100A4 in liver tissue was positively correlated with the degree of liver fibrosis [27]. Consistent with previous findings, our study confirms that S100A4 promotes the activation of HSCs. We further elucidated a dual mechanism underlying this process: On the one hand, S100A4 activates the MAPK pathway in KCs to induce cytokine secretion, thereby indirectly activating HSCs. On the other hand, it directly targets HSCs, driving their activation via the TGF-β/SMAD2 pathway.

Like S100A4, S100A6 facilitates liver fibrosis by promoting HSC proliferation, primarily through binding to RAGE and inducing ERK activation. Xia et al. demonstrated that recombinant human S100A6 introduced to CCl4-induced mice increased liver fibrosis through the promotion of activated HSC proliferation [17]. Dai et al. reported that S100A6 is a candidate gene that may participate in HSC activation during liver fibrosis [29]. Oliver et al. reported that S100A6 is a universal marker of activated collagen-secreting myofibroblasts (MFB) at both the gene and protein expression levels [30]. Our research also revealed that S100A6 activates HSCs. This activation involves a dual mode of action: S100A6 activates KCs to release pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby indirectly activating HSCs. Concurrently, it directly acts on HSCs, promoting their activation.

Although S100A10 has been reported to be a significant predictor of diagnoses and treatment responses in liver cancer patients, few studies have investigated its role in liver fibrosis. To our knowledge, there are no prior reports on the involvement of S100A10 in liver fibrosis. Our findings demonstrate that S100A10 can activate KCs to secrete cytokines via the MAPK pathway, indirectly promoting HSC activation through TGF-β/SMAD2. Furthermore, S100A10 directly acts on HSCs, activating them via the TGF-β/SMAD2 pathway.

Furthermore, previous studies have reported that S100A11 levels in liver tissues are positively correlated with liver fibrosis and hepatocellular carcinoma [31, 32]. Zhu et al. demonstrated that S100A11 activates HSCs and fibrogenesis via the regulation of the deacetylation of SMAD3 in the TGF-β signaling pathway [19]. Another study reported that S100A11 promotes liver inflammation and fibrosis in a mouse model of NAFLD and that the upregulation of S100A11 may be a key feature in the transition from steatosis to NASH/fibrosis in mice [31]. Our research further revealed that S100A11 activates HSCs through a dual mechanism. First, it stimulates KCs to release inflammatory cytokines, indirectly activating HSCs. Second, S100A11 directly targets HSCs, promoting their activation.

Our study revealed a specific cytokine signature induced by S100A4/A6/A10/A11 proteins, involving differential production of TNF-α, IL-17, IL-12, IL-1β, IL-6, IFN-γ, and the chemokines CCL2/CCL4. This varied induction pattern suggests distinct, potentially complementary roles for these proteins in the inflammatory network. We established an indirect cascade in which S100 proteins activate KCs to release cytokines and TGF-β, which subsequently activate HSCs, highlighting KCs as pivotal intermediaries in S100-driven fibrosis. Our research consequently proposes a novel dual-action model for S100A4/A6/A10/A11 proteins in liver fibrosis. In fibrotic livers, increased levels of S100 proteins operate through two arms: (1) activating MAPK in KCs to differentially secrete inflammatory mediators, leading to indirect HSC activation via paracrine signaling; and (2) directly activating HSCs to differentiate into myofibroblasts through the TGF-β/SMAD2 pathway. This combination of direct and indirect signaling significantly accelerates fibrosis progression.

The S100 protein family is widely implicated in fibrotic processes across multiple organs, including the kidneys [23], lungs [24], skin [25], and liver [13], suggesting its potential as a fibrosis biomarker. Concurrently, S100 protein expressions are upregulated in various malignancies, such as breast cancer [33], lung cancer [34], colorectal cancer [35], pancreatic cancer [36], and HCC [37]. This highlights their critical roles in tumorigenesis and progression, positioning S100 proteins as promising biomarkers and therapeutic targets for diverse cancers, including osteosarcoma, lung, colorectal, and breast cancer [38–42]. In our study, we observed significant expressions of S100A4/A6/A10/A11 proteins in human LC, ICC, and HCC tissues. The observed variations in protein expression intensity and spatial distribution across different cases and hepatic lesion types strongly suggest a functional link between these S100 proteins and the pathogenesis and progression of liver diseases.

Our comprehensive analysis not only elucidates a novel dual-action model for S100A4/A6/A10/A11 proteins in liver fibrosis but also highlights their considerable clinical and translational potential. Given their critical roles in initiating inflammation and promoting HSC activation, S100A4/A6/A10/A11 proteins represent promising candidates for non-invasive biomarkers for liver fibrosis severity and progression. For instance, elevated serum levels of specific S100A4/A6/A10/A11 proteins could serve as early indicators for patients at risk or for monitoring therapeutic efficacy. Furthermore, by identifying specific signaling pathways (e.g., P38, JNK, ERK, and TGF-β/SMAD2) activated by S100A4/A6/A10/A11 proteins in KCs and HSCs, our findings provide a strong rationale for developing targeted anti-fibrotic therapies. Pharmacological inhibition of S100A4/A6/A10/A11 protein-receptor interactions or downstream kinases like P38/JNK/ERK could attenuate the inflammatory cascade and directly suppress fibrogenic responses in HSCs, thereby offering novel therapeutic avenues for patients with liver fibrosis. The broad implication of S100A4/A6/A10/A11 proteins in other fibrotic diseases and cancers further suggests that therapeutic strategies developed in the context of liver fibrosis might have wider applications, addressing an unmet clinical need across multiple organ systems.

Our study significantly advances the understanding of the mechanisms underlying liver fibrosis by identifying hepatocyte-derived S100A4/A6/A10/A11 proteins as critical mediators. We elucidated their dual roles: Firstly, these proteins activate the P38, JNK, and ERK signaling pathways, thereby stimulating a KC-mediated inflammatory response, with downstream factors subsequently activating HSCs. Secondly, S100A4/A6/A10/A11 directly activate HSCs. Both of these pathways ultimately converge on the TGF-β/SMAD2 pathway to promote collagen release. Despite these robust findings, certain limitations of the current study warrant consideration and inform future research directions. Firstly, the exclusive use of male mice in this study limits the generalizability of our findings to female counterparts. Secondly, while the CCl4-induced fibrosis model effectively simulates long-term and repeated inflammatory damage, it may not fully recapitulate the complex and multi-factorial pathogenesis observed in chronic human liver diseases. Thirdly, the binding receptors of S100A4/A6/A10/A11 proteins warrant further investigation. To address these limitations and expand upon our findings, future research is warranted. Specifically, investigating sex-specific differences in S100A4/A6/A10/A11 protein expression and function in hepatic fibrosis is crucial. Additionally, exploring S100A4/A6/A10/A11 protein involvement in diverse liver disease models, coupled with in vivo validation of these pathways using liver-specific knockout models, will enhance translational relevance. Further investigations should also focus on precise receptor recognition mechanisms and potential synergistic or antagonistic interactions among the various S100A4/A6/A10/A11 proteins. These efforts aim to explore the therapeutic feasibility of targeting S100A4/A6/A10/A11 proteins or their downstream signaling nodes to combat liver fibrosis. Notwithstanding these limitations, our study’s key finding that hepatocyte-derived S100A4/A6/A10/A11 proteins serve as critical nodes connecting inflammation and fibrosis within the hepatic fibrotic network remains highly significant.

In conclusion, our study unequivocally establishes hepatocyte-derived S100A4/A6/A10/A11 proteins as critical mediators, bridging inflammation and fibrosis within the hepatic fibrotic network through a novel dual-action mechanism. These findings bear significant clinical implications, positioning S100 proteins as highly promising diagnostic biomarkers for liver fibrosis and compelling therapeutic targets for the development of innovative anti-fibrotic strategies. Future research building upon these insights will be pivotal in translating these mechanistic discoveries into improved patient outcomes.
