Strange metal transport from coupling to fluctuating spins
Simone Fratini, Arnaud Ralko, Sergio Ciuchi

Abstract
Metals hosting strong electronic interactions, including high-temperature superconductors, behave in ways that do not conform to the normal Fermi liquid theory. To pinpoint the microscopic origin of this ”strange metal” behavior, here we reexamine the d.c. and frequency-dependent conductivity of the two-dimensional t-J model taking advantage of recent improvements made on the finite temperature Lanczos method, enabling numerically exact calculations at unprecedentedly low temperatures and high spectral resolution. We find that strange metallicity is pervasive in the temperature-doping phase diagram wherever anti-ferromagnetic correlations are suppressed, being instead driven by paramagnetic spin fluctuations and unrelated to quantum criticality. Our results precisely characterize the Planckian carriers responsible for both the strange metal resistivities and the unconventional optical conductivities seen in experiments, highlighting striking similarities with the universal relaxation of glasses and dielectrics.



Strongly correlated materials often exhibit resistivities that increase linearly with temperature from few to hundreds of degrees K, at odds with the standard theory of metals
[
1
]
. These anomalies are part of a broader, puzzling picture, as they also extend to the frequency domain: associated with the strange behavior of charge transport, the decay of the conductivity with frequency is slower than that associated with
exponential relaxation processes,
and therefore incompatible with normal diffusion at long times
[
2
]
.

Despite broad experimental evidence of the phenomenon, accurate numerical calculations of strange metal transport in correlated electron systems have only been attempted very recently, and there is no consensus on its precise microscopic origin. These numerical studies have mostly focused either on the Hubbard model
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
, due to its direct microscopic connection with the experimental systems, or to more phenomenonology-driven descriptions enforcing the absence of quasiparticles and the concept of self-generated randomness, such as the SYK model and its generalizations

[
8
]
, for their ability to capture the observed physical behavior.
We stress that while important insights can be gained already from the knowledge of the quasiparticle scattering rate
[
9
]
, when dealing with an unknown transport mechanism it is essential to undertake the much harder task of actually calculating the transport observables: these contain key microscopic phenomena related to quantum interferences that are instead totally absent in the one-particle properties
[
6
,
10
,
11
]
.

Here we choose to work with the t-J model. Being derived from the Hubbard model in the large-
U
limit, the t-J model retains a direct connection with the materials’ microscopics. As the Hubbard model itself, it contains the crucial anti-ferromagnetic interactions
J\propto t^{2}/U
arising from particle exchange at finite
U
. To our advantage, however, here the strength of anti-ferromagnetic correlations can be tuned independently of the correlation strength, which makes it an ideal framework to disentangle the role of spin fluctuations from the many-body effects related to the charge. Moreover,
the fact that the effective carrier number in the t-J model does not change with temperature allows us to directly address the intrinsic diffusivity of the carriers, contrasting it with proposed fundamental quantum bounds.
Our results comprehensively demonstrate strange metal behavior arising from the coupling of the hole carriers with paramagnetic spin fluctuations, unrelated to quantum criticality, and shed light on the cooperative microscopic processes underlying the phenomenon.

Model and method.—

The t-J model Hamiltonian is
H=-\sum_{ijs}t_{ij}(\tilde{c}^{\dagger}_{is}\tilde{c}_{js}+h.c.)+J_{ij}(\vec{S}_{i}\cdot\vec{S}_{j}-\frac{1}{4}n_{i}n_{j})
, where
\tilde{c}^{\dagger}_{is}
and
\tilde{c}_{js}
are projected creation and annihilation operators for electrons on sites
i
and
j
, that take into account the constraint of no local double occupancy enforced by the strong Coulomb repulsion limit,
n_{i}
is the density at site
i
and
J_{ij}
is an antiferromagnetic (AF) exchange coupling between the corresponding quantum electron spins
\vec{S}_{i}
and
\vec{S}_{j}
.
We focus on the square lattice, setting
t_{ij}=t
and
J_{ij}=J
for nearest neighbors (we take
t
as the energy unit). We also consider frustrated cases with
t_{ij}=t^{\prime}
and
J_{ij}=J^{\prime}
for next nearest neighbors.

We solve the t-J model numerically using the finite-temperature Lanczos method (FTLM)
[
12
]
, supplemented by twisted boundary condition (TBC) averaging and a recently benchmarked prescription for a reliable extrapolation of the Kubo formula to the d.c. limit
[
11
]
. Taken together, these allow to reach high spectral resolution and unprecedentedly low temperatures, allowing us to precisely characterize the the current carriers responsible for strange metallicity at the experimentally relevant low temperatures and low frequencies (details in the supplement).

We first present our results for one hole in an unfrustrated square lattice. We will show later, and this is a key result of our study, that the scattering processes revealed here remain essentially unchanged at finite hole concentrations.
Fig.

Remarkably, the resistivity in the

Whether or not this applies to strange metals
[
13
]
, we analyze this result using
the phenomenological Drude model,
\rho=m/(ne^{2}\tau)

[
14

15
]
, i.e. following the same procedure that is customarily used in experiments. In this framework, with the band mass
m
and the carrier density

are entirely encoded in the transport scattering time
\tau

m=\hbar^{2}/(2ta^{2})
for the square lattice, and taking

\hbar/\tau=2t\tilde{\rho}
. As mentioned in the introduction, it should be remembered that this quantity might differ from the quasiparticle scattering time obtained from the knowledge of the electron self-energy.

Strange metals are
defined
by the linear relation

\hbar/\tau=\alpha k_{B}T+\gamma_{0},

(1)

with
\gamma_{0}
a constant:
the
T
-linear behavior of the resistivity is ascribed to an equivalently
T
-linear dependence of the relaxation rate extracted from the Drude formula. When the numerical prefactor
\alpha
is of order
1
, the relaxation is termed Planckian
[

,
17
,
14
,
15
]
. The slope extracted from the data in Fig.
1

J=0
is
\alpha=0.94
(see also panel C): the paramagnetic state of the t-J model (or, the Hubbard model in the
U\to\infty
limit) is a Planckian metal at all temperatures.

The effect of a finite antiferromagnetic (AF) exchange coupling,
J>0
, is also depicted in Fig.
1

1

T=0

[
18
,
19
,
20
]
,
resulting in insulating behavior at low temperatures.
A
T
-linear resistivity is recovered at all
T\gtrsim J
, i.e. as soon as the AF correlations vanish (cf. Fig.
1
B). The recovery of uncorrelated spin dynamics is also indicated by the entropy approaching the value
k_{B}\log 2
per spin (see supplement).
From the Drude analysis, for all finite
J
we obtain the same Planckian slope
\alpha\simeq 1
and a residual scattering rate
\gamma_{0}\propto J
.

Despite being widely used as a useful phenomenological framework, the normal Drude description does not strictly
apply to strange metals
[
13
,
23
]
. This is because a Drude-like resistivity implies — and cannot be separated from — a Lorentzian form of the optical conductivity,
\sigma(\omega)=(1/\rho)/(1+(\omega\tau)^{2})

[
24
]
, corresponding to an exponential decay of the current correlations with time. This simple form is generally not observed in experiments:
strange metals typically exhibit instead a much slower power-law decay of the optical absorption at low frequencies,
\sigma(\omega)\sim\omega^{-c}
with
c\lesssim 1

[
2
,
25
,
26
,
27
,
28
,
1
,
23
]
, making the identification of
\hbar/\tau
with the normal exponential decay of quasiparticles debatable.

Our optical conductivity data, shown in Fig.
2
A, reproduce the behavior seen in experiments.
We find that the low-frequency spectra accurately follow a ”stretched” Drude form

\sigma(\omega)=\sigma_{0}/(1+(\omega\tau_{opt})^{c}),

(2)

shown as dashed lines.
The anomalous exponent saturates to
c\simeq 2/3
in the spin-disordered phase at
J=0
(Fig.
2
B),
which is comparable with the exponent that is commonly observed in the cuprates
[
2
,
25
,
1
,
23
]
, and incompatible with Drude-like relaxation.
Exponents
c\lesssim 1
are also found in the presence of a finite
J
whenever AF correlations are suppressed (Fig. S5 and Ref.
[
29
]
).
Importantly, a cusp-like behavior at
\omega=0
as described by Eq. (
2
) and shown in Fig.
2
A has been predicted on rigorous grounds in Ref.
[
30
]
and confirmed numerically in dimensions
d=1
, but not yet in
d=2
(see the discussion in the supplement of Ref.
[
7
]
; this low-frequency behavior, that is crucial to the correct determination of the d.c. resistivity, is missed by quantum MonteCarlo, which relies on an analytic continuation procedure that broadens the fine features of the spectra).
Normal Drude behavior is only recovered in a narrow low-temperature regime at
J=0
, where spin fluctuations are suppressed by the onset of the ferromagnetic Nagaoka state
[
31
]
(Fig.
2
B).

Due to its soundness, simplicity and accuracy in describing the experimental data, the analytical form Eq. (
2
) could be a useful tool for the analysis of optical absorption experiments, alternative to popular ”extended Drude” and multicomponent analyses
[
32
]
. But what does such an anomalous frequency dependence
imply for the charge carrier dynamics? As we show next, this peculiar shape
carries key information about the current-carrying objects responsible for strange metal transport.

Fig.
2
C shows the Fourier transform of the optical conductivity to the time domain,
F(t)=\int\sigma(\omega)\cos(\omega t)d\omega
. This quantity embodies
the decay of the carrier velocity correlations
[
24
]
.
Unsurprisingly, the time dynamics inferred from our data is much more complex than the simple exponential relaxation implied by the Drude model.
The
F(t)
traces show an initial sharp drop followed by a much slower relaxation at long times. The initial drop entails
an almost complete loss of velocity correlations.
It originates from the extremely broad optical absorption spectrum on the scale of the bandwidth itself, which is responsible for the ”bad” metallic character of charge transport

[
33
,
34
]
. Notably, the high-frequency spectrum and the related short-time decay are well captured by local theories such as the retraceable path approximation
[
35
,
21
]
(RPA, dotted lines in panels A and C) or the analogous dynamical mean-field theory (DMFT)
[
36
]
.

More interesting to our purposes is the long time behavior, that is instead associated with the anomalous low-frequency response.
The latter features a much slower relaxation (dashed lines) similar to a stretched exponential, strongly reminiscent of the charge relaxation found in glassy systems
[
37
]
and solid dielectrics
[
38
,
39
]
.
Because the transport properties are ultimately determined by the relaxation in the long-time limit, it follows that this feature is the essential one that carries the strange metal character.

The long-time relaxation processes revealed here suggest the emergence of an additional conduction channel rather than an additional source of scattering: the conductivity increases as compared to local approximations such as RPA and DMFT (low-frequency upturn in Fig.
2
A); correspondingly, the
\rho\propto\sqrt{T}
behavior characterizing these local theories transforms into the observed
\rho\propto T
(supplement, Fig. S3).
Our results indicate that the time needed to rearrange the spin environment in order to develop such additional conduction pathways is considerably longer than the timescale of indvidual hops (here the stretched relaxation appears at times
\sim 10/t
), highlighting their collective nature
[
40
,
41
]
.
The fact that non-local processes increase the conductivity agrees with what has been reported in the Hubbard model
[
6
,
42
]
.
Importantly, the two-step relaxation demonstrated here at
J=0
persists whenever AF correlations are suppressed: Fig. S5 in the Supplement shows analogous time-dynamics in the quantum paramagnetic state obtained at finite
J
and finite
t^{\prime}
(see below).

Spin and charge fluctuations.—

To get more insights into the microscopic origin of this complex transport behavior,
we show in Fig.
2
D-E the imaginary part of the momentum-integrated spin
\chi^{\prime\prime}_{s}(\omega)
and charge susceptibility
\chi^{\prime\prime}_{c}(\omega)

The spin response has an overdamped oscillator shape that peaks at a low frequency
\omega_{s}\simeq t/10

\chi^{\prime\prime}_{s}\propto\omega/(\omega^{2}+\Gamma^{2})
, that has been observed previously both in the pristine t-J model
[
43
]
and in its random
J
/random
t
generalization
[
44
]
, and experimentally in the cuprates
[
45
,
46
]
.
The charge fluctuation spectrum is instead broad and featureless, extending on the scale of the bandwidth itself (here
8t
), also reminiscent of the
charge response measured in the cuprates
[
47
,
48

.

Notably, the spin and charge timescales inferred from their respective fluctuation spectra coincide with the long and short-time processes observed in the current correlations in Fig.

Following Refs.
[

4

5

7

, we define the many-body diffusion constant

via the Nernst-Einstein relation,
\rho=1/(e^{2}\chi_{c}D)
, where

In correlated systems this type of analysis is considered to be more appropriate than the Drude model, since it does not make
any simplifying assumptions on the nature of transport.
For a single hole or, more generally, a low-density hole gas, Fermi statistics plays no role and the compressibility takes the classical expression
1/\chi_{c}=T/p
, recovering the Einstein relation
\rho=T/(e^{2}pD)

Given that the compressibility already contains an explicit

of thermodynamic origin (detailed balance),
strange metal behavior in this limit corresponds to a constant

\alpha\simeq 1

[

For a finite concentration of holes, the diffusivity can be obtained from the separate calculation of

Fig.

Planckian behavior develops from the lowest temperatures in an extended range of dopings

To disentangle the emergence of Planckian transport both from the existence of the QCP and from the presence of AF order or AF fluctuations,
we take advantage of the possibility to tune the magnetic correlations
that is enabled by the t-J model. Upon reducing

We conclude that Planckian behavior does not necessarily follow from the presence of a QCP

Concluding remarks.—

Strange metallicity
is demonstrated here to arise in strongly correlated systems whenever the hole carriers move in
a fluctuating spin environment.
The microscopic processes underlying this behavior are already realized for individual holes
and they are not crucially affected by mutual hole-hole interactions. As a result, the strange metal behavior found here naturally extends over wide ranges of doping, without requiring the existence of
extended quantum criticality
[
13
,
1
,
60
]
.

The accurate calculation of the frequency-dependent response provides us with a precise characterization the strange current carriers.
Our calculations show that strong electronic correlations cause
a rapid loss of coherence for the carriers as they hop,
in line with prevailing theoretical approaches that do not rely on the existence of quasiparticles
[
8
]
.
We demonstrate however the emergence of a second,
much slower type of relaxation of the current
that sets in at long times, being responsible for both the strange metal resistivity and the non-Drude shape of the optical conductivity.
This long-time anomaly indicates the existence of a conducting channel
that is not captured by local theories and whose timescale seems to be tightly related to the dynamics of the spin background,
both observations highlighting its collective origin.

Our findings have the following direct implications. First, the presence of current-carrying excitations with a narrow bandwidth, as suggested by their low energy scale, can directly explain the resulting T-linear resistivity
[
61
,
59
]
. Second, this scenario
would also naturally explain why quenching the spin fluctuations in the cuprates increases the resistivity and disrupts the
T
-linear behavior (see
[
62
,
63
]
and references therein), as this would directly suppress the contribution from the anomalous current carriers.
Finally, the stretched Drude absorption Eq. (
2
)
suggests a strong resemblance between the current relaxation in strange metals and the universal relaxation found in dielectric materials
[
38
,
39
]
.
In both cases,
deviations from Drude (Debye) behavior arise from the interplay of slow and fast degrees of freedom, resulting from many-body interactions and disorder: in this sense, strange metals
represent a salient extension of Jonscher’s universal relaxation concept to the metallic realm.
This could provide a new route to fully grasp
the microscopic nature of the Planckian carriers and, possibly, their role in the pairing mechanism
[
64
,
56
,
60
]
.

Methods

The finite-temperature Lanczos method (FTLM) provides an exact high-temperature expansion for static and dynamic correlation functions, by mapping the problem onto a reduced number of pseudo-eigenstates in the so-called Krylov space, that is much smaller than the size of the Hilbert space itself
[
12
]
(we use cutoffs as large as
M=1200
states). The optical conductivity,
\sigma(\omega)
, is evaluated through the Kubo formula, by summing over all of the
M^{2}
transitions between different pseudo-eigenstates. Transport properties are evaluated by taking the limit
\omega\to 0
of the optical conductivity.

The reduced Hilbert space of the t-J model allows us to reach larger system sizes than those attainable in the Hubbard model, enabling an accurate finite-size scaling analysis. Our simulations are performed on clusters of up to
N_{s}=20
sites in the case of a single hole, and up to
N_{s}=16
at finite hole concentrations, requiring modest computational resources (Hilbert space sizes of up to
2\cdot 10^{6}
states).

We use twisted boundary condition (TBC) averaging to augment the number of optical transitions, hence increasing the spectral resolution. We apply the protocol devised in Ref.
[
11
]
to deal with the spurious terms in the d.c. current introduced by the
fictitious magnetic fluxes involved in the TBC averaging procedure. Calculations targeting the properties of individual holes are performed in the canonical ensemble, using the prescription of Ref.
[
66
]
for TBC averaging. For finite-density calculations we work in the grand canonical ensemble, using the prescription of Ref.
[
67
]
.

When appiled to the t-J model, the present methodology allows us to obtain reliable results for charge transport down to the experimentally relevant window, reaching
T/t=0.02
for a single hole
and
T/t=0.1
at finite concentrations. Reaching frequency resolutions of
\Delta\omega=t/200
gives access to the low-frequency/long-time charge dynamics, which is crucial for the phenomena revealed in this work.
