Top-down perspectives on cell membrane potential and protein transcription
Authors: Javier Cervera, Michael Levin, Salvador Mafe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We have explored a simple model for the multicellular interplay between bioelectricity and protein transcription using a top-down perspective that offers new insights complementary to the commonly used bottom-up descriptions. We model the non-excitable cell bioelectrical representation of the external environment, including the neighboring cells, using voltage-gated ion channels and intercellular junctions. The simulations make three predictions: (i) shifts in membrane potential allow transitions between gene expression states, (ii) in the case of different cell potential-gated transcriptions, depolarized cells cannot control the distinct gene expressions as effectively as polarized cells, and (iii) community effects should permit to extend the single-cell control to the multicellular level. Because the spatio-temporal distributions of instructive signaling ions and molecules depend on the local electric potentials, different multicellular potentials correlate with distinct downstream gene expression patterns. A central cell is able to measure the number of neighboring cells and learn their bioelectrical state from the downward-induced membrane potential changes, which suggests that multiscale bioelectricity can operate as a top-down mechanism in development and regeneration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-31696-6.

Introduction

In biological phenomena, causation is commonly analyzed from the parts to the whole. However, in a complementary but rather controversial metaphor, top-down or downward causation (DC) has been cited when higher-level effects appear to influence lower-level cellular states and functions; see Refs1,2 and references therein. Here, we have considered a simple model for the interplay between bioelectricity and transcription in non-excitable cells that explores a top-down perspective. To this end, we invoke an experimentally based model of bioelectrical adaptive transcription that involves feedbacks at multiple size scales, emphasizing the central biological role of the cell3.

The understanding of multicellular patterns and bio-fields4,5 can benefit from descriptions at scales higher than the underlying molecular events because a top-down control offers new opportunities for external actions. In particular, multiscale bioelectricity constitutes a high-level spatial scale control node that expands from the subcellular membrane potentials of organelles such as lysosomes and mitochondria to the spatio-temporal patterns of multicellular potentials characteristic of development and regeneration4. We consider here the coupling between bioelectricity and the transcription of two model proteins. To this end, we operationally describe the internal cell representation of the external environment, including the neighboring cells, using voltage-gated ion channels and intercellular junctions. This bioelectrically-centered model5 is complementary to traditional descriptions focused on biochemical networks and biomechanical fields.

The membrane potential V, defined as the electrical potential difference between the cell inside and the external microenvironment, is central to metabolic, osmotic, developmental, and regeneration phenomena6–16. In particular, instructive polarized (high absolute value of the negative cell potential V) and depolarized (low absolute value of V) physiological windows are experimentally observed4,7,17–19. While crucial information of the body plan resides within the DNA as a basic single-cell language for making proteins, multicellular potentials can transduce short-term bioelectrical information into long-term transcriptional processes because of the coupling of the signaling ions and molecules concentrations with the local electric potentials4,5,7. In this way, the spatio-temporal patterns of cell potentials can provide downstream biochemical information for the establishment of different morphological regions4,5,15,16,20. Experimentally, voltage-sensitive dyes15,21 make evident that different anterior–posterior morphologies can result from changes in the axial electric potential4,15,20 regulated by ion channels and intercellular gap junctions. Moreover, controlled alterations of channels and junctions can result in phenotype changes4,7,20,22. Transcriptional control of mammalian cells by membrane depolarization showing the coupling between bioelectrical and biochemical signals has also been demonstrated in electrogenetic circuits11 and the role of membrane potential has been described in the onset of embryonic differentiation12. In this case, while gene expression is mediated by intracellular signal transducers and biochemical pathways, bioelectricity can also influence the timing of pluripotency genes.

The above experimental facts suggest that top-down effects reflect not only the role of the whole cell state on subcellular basic processes but also the multicellular regulation of the cell state itself on the basis of the feedback between bioelectrical and transcriptional processes4,5. In these processes, top-down phenomena arises when a central cell identifies an environmental change and uses it to tune its individual state. Recently, we have described how a multicellular aggregate could adapt to environmental toxicity by means of the feedback between the cell membrane potential and downstream biochemical processes23. We consider now the effects that the upper-scale multicellular cell potentials may provoke on the lower-scale cellular transcription following the virtual processes of Fig. 1a and b. In our model a simple component, the initially depolarized or polarized central cell, can iterate its state with time in response to a collectively computed macroscopic property, the polarized or depolarized potentials of the neighboring cells forming the multicellular aggregate. Thus, the central cell can detect the states of neighboring cells from the induced membrane potential changes that eventually impact on the protein transcription rates. We emphasize that the simulated conditions of Figs. 1a and b can be physiologically meaningful. In particular, a central cell surrounded by a finite number of normally polarized (depolarized) neighboring cells can still assume a depolarized (polarized) membrane potential when the voltage-gated intercellular connections are sufficiently weak. In particular, it is an experimental fact that relatively small depolarized patches can be surrounded by hyperpolarized extensive regions in development, regeneration, and initial tumorigenesis4,5.

The intercellular connectivity allows to integrate the individual cell state into the multicellular aggregate. This higher spatial scale permits the enhancement of morphologically instructive transitions or the collective compensation of local disruptive perturbations in the cell polarization, as needed in particular biological stages24. Experimentally, specific bioelectric changes can contribute to tail regeneration, brain repair, and the induction of ectopic eyes4,7,25–28. Also, modifications of the intercellular connectivity may trigger transitions between instructive multicellular patterns5,7,20,27,29.

In this multiscale model, the influence of the cell membrane potential on the subcellular proteins transcription (Fig. 2) is extended to a higher scale where the multicellular potentials influence the cell potential itself (Fig. 3). Also, because of the dependence of the cell potential V on the ionic concentrations and channels conductances (Fig. 2), V can contribute to the internal cell representation of the external environment, including the neighboring cells (Fig. 3). At the upper scale, the dynamics of multicellular potentials can reflect biologically-instructive external events4,32, similarly as neuronal spatio-temporal patterns constitute mental representations of the outside world33.

The initial bioelectrical state of a weakly connected central cell cannot be changed by the neighboring cells; thus, the changes observed in the proteins transcription are moderate in this case (Figs. 4a and c). On the contrary, significant top-down effects, demonstrated by the bioelectrical and transcriptional changes, are feasible for strong intercellular connectivity (Figs. 4b and d). In Figs. 4a and b, the attempts to repolarize the central cell caused by the polarized neighboring cells induce a decrease in the expression of protein A and an increase in the case of protein B. On the contrary, in Figs. 4c and d, the attempts to depolarize the central cell caused by the depolarized neighboring cells induces just the opposite changes in the protein expressions: protein A concentration increases while protein B concentration decreases.

Remarkably, the model simulations suggest that similar proteins expressions can become different by cell repolarization (Fig. 4b) while different proteins expressions can become similar by cell depolarization (Fig. 4d), as qualitatively shown in Fig. 1. Experimentally, changes in the cell polarization state can be induced by acting on the ion channel and intercellular junction conductances, which could be modified by the injection of specific mRNAs or the independent blocking of the dep and pol channels and the intercellular conductance4,24,31. These operational procedures should eventually result in downstream protein transcription changes4,23,27.

Experimentally, higher-scale effects on the central cell potential have been observed in the electrical activity of non-excitable cell aggregates34 as well as in astrocytes coupled through networks of gap junctions35. In the second case, the aggregate connectivity can compensate for local changes in the extracellular potassium concentration that would depolarize the central cell otherwise35. Taking together, Figs. 4 and 5 emphasize the role of intercellular communication in adaptive transcription and suggest the bioelectrical reprograming of cells via targeting the ion channel and junction proteins that regulate single-cell and multicellular potentials4,5,11,12,31.

Note also that abnormally depolarized cell potentials and low intercellular coupling abolish the potential-dependent transcription because the central cell cannot keep different concentrations of proteins A and B (Figs. 4a and 5a) in the model of Figs. 1 and 2. This fact emphasizes the need of establishing the specific potential-dependent transcriptions characteristic of each biosystem. Here, possible applications are electrogenetic circuits where the transcriptional control of mammalian cells is regulated by the coupling between bioelectrical and biochemical signals11, embryonic differentiation processes where the cell potential influences the timing of crucial pluripotency genes12, and the changes in cell depolarization and intercellular connectivity characteristic of the initial stages of tumorigenesis; see Refs4,31. and references therein.

Conclusions

In Biology, feedback loops that couple different mechanisms occurring at distinct scales are commonplace. Here, we have considered a simple model for the interplay between bioelectricity and transcription using a top-down perspective that emphasizes intercellular communication (Fig. 1). We characterize the bioelectrical networks using voltage-gated single-cell channels and intercellular junctions (Figs. 2 and 3). Cell potentials are particularly suited to show top-down phenomena because of their sensitivity to different environmental chemical, mechanical, and electrical signals4,5,14,39. The resulting bioelectrical adaptive transcription offers new insights complementary to the common bottom-up description. While our feedback does not address the general philosophical issues of causation, other problems that can be related to ours involve coarse-graining mechanisms2, emergence theories40,41, the nature of evolutionary dynamics42, the physical influence of initial and boundary conditions on the differential equations that represent lower-level processes43, and the role of collective intelligence across the biological scales of morphogenesis, with an emphasis on top-down strategies44,45. One attractive feature of top-down control is the possibility to induce multi-modal downstream effects (whole organogenesis, appendage regeneration, complex organ repair) with relatively simple triggers44. The ability to induce modular, context-sensitive, and self-limiting cascades without micromanaging the outcomes46 should be of potential importance to biomedical applications47.

Recent work has also examined different measures of causation, thus contributing to the understanding of the concept across disparate fields with different measures of it48. Interestingly, it was noted that macroscale effects could limit the expected uncertainties in causal relationships, leading to more robust causes at higher levels48, as loosely suggested by the community effects of Figs. 4 and 5. Note that the assumption that the update rules of Figs. 2 and 3 change with the time-dependent cell potentials immediately leads to dynamical equations and cell states that mutually influence each other41. Also, when the timescales of bioelectricty (membrane potential) and transcription (gene expression) are very different, the use of a steady-state approximation for the membrane potential changes would be possible49.

The model simulations, which could be extended to the case of biomechanically-regulated and oscillatory processes5,50–53, make three predictions: (i) shifts in membrane potential may allow transitions between gene expression states, (ii) in the case of different cell potential-gated transcriptions, depolarized cells cannot control the distinct gene expressions as effectively as polarized cells, and (iii) community effects should permit to extend the single-cell control to the multicellular level. Indeed, the multicellular system can modulate the transcription of two proteins A and B characterizing the single-cell state (Figs. 4, 5, 6). Thus, the transcriptional changes associated with cell depolarization and repolarization phenomena can produce adaptive protein expression changes modulated by the intercellular connectivity23,36,54,55, with immediate consequences on the transcription of proteins A and B. This result, which emphasizes the role of both the single-cell and multicellular scales in Biology3–5,56,57, should be significant for bioelectrically-influenced developmental and regeneration processes4,5,13.

Methods

The algorithm employed in Figs. 4 and 5 has been implemented using html/javascript. The calculations take advantage of the vastly different characteristic times of bioelectricity (cell potential) and transcription (gene expression). Initially, the time evolution of the electrical potential is numerically calculated and the protein concentrations are assumed constant. After a time much longer than the bioelectrical time but shorter than the transcription time, when the potential no longer changes, the evolution of the protein concentrations is analytically calculated (see Supplemental Information). Figure 6 requires a full numerical integration of the evolution equations and the algorithm used in this case is described in Reference36.
