Complication Rates and Clinical Outcomes of Larger Alignment Corrections Using Inverted V-shaped High Tibial Osteotomy in Severe Varus Knee Osteoarthritis: A Comparison with Smaller Corrections
Authors: Kazunori Yasuda, Koji Yabuuchi, Eiji Kondo, Jun Onodera, Dai Sato, Norimasa Iwasaki, Tomonori Yagi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Many studies have reported that larger alignment corrections in medial opening-wedge high tibial osteotomy (MOWHTO) are associated with a higher incidence of complications. However, no clinical studies have investigated whether larger alignment corrections using the inverted V-shaped high tibial osteotomy (iVHTO) procedure with locking compression plate (LCP) fixation adversely affect postoperative outcomes.Purpose:To determine whether larger alignment corrections using the iVHTO procedure with LCP fixation prolong bone union time, increase complication rates, and lead to poorer clinical outcomes compared with smaller corrections.Study Design:Cohort study; Level of evidence, 3.Methods:A total of 109 knees (96 patients) treated with iVHTO were followed up periodically for up to 24 months postoperatively, and a final follow-up examination was conducted between 3 and 6 years after surgery. The bone union was quantitatively assessed using a radiological scoring system. At the time of analysis, the 109 knees were divided into 2 groups: the larger correction group with the correction angle of ≥13° (group L, 49 knees) and the smaller correction group with the correction angle of <13° (group S, 60 knees). The 2 groups were compared with respect to bone union time, complications, knee function scores, and the Knee injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS).Results:Varus knee deformity was significantly greater in group L than in group S (P < .001), whereas postoperative alignment was valgus in both groups without significant differences. The correction angle of the tibia ranged from 13° to 19° (mean, 15.6) in group L and ranged from 9° to 12° (mean, 10.5) in group S. However, there were no significant differences between groups L and S in terms of the bone union time (12.7 vs 12.4 weeks), the complication rate (4.1% vs 3.3%), the Lysholm score (93 vs 96.5 points), and any of the 5 KOOS subscales (eg, symptoms: 83.6 vs 88 points). The postoperative clinical outcomes significantly improved in both groups at the final follow-up examination compared with preoperative scores.Conclusion:Larger alignment corrections (≥13°) using the iVHTO procedure with LCP fixation did not prolong bone union time, increase complication rates, or lead to poorer clinical outcomes compared with smaller corrections (<13°) in patients with severe varus osteoarthritic knees.

High tibial osteotomy (HTO) is a useful surgical option for active patients with a medial osteoarthritic knee who wish to continue participating in sports or performing strenuous labor. Medial opening wedge (MOW) HTO has been established as a standard procedure for mild varus osteoarthritis (OA) of the knee.4,9,10,21 However, many studies have reported that larger alignment corrections in MOWHTO are associated with a higher incidence of complications, including lateral hinge fracture, delayed union, patella baja, and increased posterior tibial slope, compared with smaller corrections.6,8,22,23,27,28,39,46 On the other hand, lateral closing wedge (LCW) HTO has been performed for OA knees with severe varus deformity. After LCWHTO, however, various complications have been reported, including peroneal nerve palsy, delayed union, nonunion, patella alta or baja, extensive bone loss from the proximal tibia, and shortening of the patellar tendon.1,2,5,7,15,17-19,26,38,41,45 Therefore, there is a need to develop a new HTO procedure for knees with severe varus OA that allows larger alignment corrections without increasing the risk of complications or compromising clinical outcomes.

The inverted V-shaped HTO (iVHTO) procedure is defined as a hemi-LCW and hemi-MOWHTO procedure with immediate bone grafting (Figure 1).1,19,31,35 The original technique using an external fixator 1 was introduced in 2006, and the modified procedure with locking compression plate (LCP) fixation 19 was described in 2017. Recently, this procedure has gained attention as a potentially useful option for treating medial OA knees with severe varus deformity, for the following reasons. 36 Recent clinical studies reported that this procedure does not affect the patellar height, posterior tibial slope, tibial length, or bone mass of the proximal tibia postoperatively.11,20 In addition, Yabuuchi et al 47 reported early completion of bone union after this procedure. However, clinical evaluation of this procedure remains insufficient. 36 Specifically, no clinical studies have investigated whether larger alignment corrections using the iVHTO procedure with LCP fixation adversely affect postoperative outcomes. Therefore, the purpose of the present study was to determine whether larger alignment corrections using the iVHTO procedure with LCP fixation prolong bone union time, increase complication rates, and lead to poorer clinical outcomes compared with smaller corrections.

Schematic picture of an inverted V-shaped high tibial osteotomy. (A) A wedge of bone (WB) was resected from the lateral aspect of the tibia. (B) After valgus correction, morselized bone chips (BC) from the resected bone were implanted into the medial opening gap.

To achieve this, the authors designed this study based on previous research findings on MOWHTO. Goshima et al 8 described that corrections of ≥13 mm significantly prolonged the time required for complete bone union (abbreviated as “bone union time”) and increased the incidence of complications compared with corrections of <13 mm. Lee et al 23 reported that corrections greater than approximately 11° increased the incidence of hinge fractures in MOWHTO surgeries. However, no previous studies on MOWHTO have performed a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis to determine the cutoff value for this type of comparison. Therefore, in the present study, we conducted an ROC analysis using data from a previously reported 46 cohort of patients who underwent MOWHTO to evaluate the predictive value of the opening correction angle for postoperative complications. As a result, the cutoff value was set at 13°. Thus, in the present study, we hypothesized that larger alignment corrections (≥13°) using the iVHTO procedure would not prolong bone union time, increase complication rates, or lead to poorer clinical outcomes compared with smaller corrections (<13°), as suggested by previous studies.20,47

Methods

Study Design

A retrospective cohort study was conducted using 129 knees from 114 patients who underwent iVHTO fixation with an LCP (TriS Inverted-V Lateral HTO Plate; Olympus Terumo Biomaterials) between April 2017 and March 2021 at our hospital. The following study protocol was approved by the ethics review board of our hospital, and informed consent was obtained from all individual participants.

The indications for the iVHTO procedure were as follows: (1) a medial OA knee (Kellgren-Lawrence [KL] grades 1-4) 16 with persistent knee pain unresponsive to at least 3 months of nonoperative therapy; and (2) a patient's desire to return to active daily activities, sports, or labor through surgical intervention. In addition, regarding the correction angle during HTO, the indications for the iVHTO procedure included either (3) a medial OA knee without patellofemoral OA, in which the planned valgus correction angle during surgery exceeded 10°; or (4) a medial OA knee with moderate (grades 2-3) patellofemoral OA, regardless of the planned valgus correction angle. The contraindications for the iVHTO procedure involved (1) a loss of knee extension <10°; (2) knee flexion <120°; (3) meniscal and/or chondral injuries in the lateral compartment; (4) severe patellofemoral OA (grade 4); (5) cruciate or collateral ligament insufficiency; (5) a history of infection in the knee or the tibia; and (6) a history of severe trauma of the leg.

The inclusion criteria of the present study were as follows: (1) knees that underwent iVHTO in accordance with the above-described indications and contraindications; and (2) patients aged between 50 and 70 years. The exclusion criteria of the present study were as follows: (1) patients with pain or impairment in the contralateral knee or in either hip or ankle joint; and (2) patients who developed incidental medical conditions or sustained trauma after surgery.

All operations were performed by 2 senior orthopaedic surgeons (Ka.Y. and E.K.) using the previously reported LCP procedure.14,19 The same rehabilitation protocol was used for all the knees after surgery. Each patient was clinically and radiologically followed up in the outpatient clinic at 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, and 24 months postoperatively, as well as at the final follow-up period, which ranged from 3 to 6 years after surgery. In addition, computed tomography (CT) was performed at 2, 3, 4, and 6 months after surgery to confirm bone union. However, once an attending physician determined that complete bone union had been achieved, no further CT scans were performed to eliminate radiation exposure. To quantitatively assess bone union time at the osteotomy site, the authors used the modified RUST (radiographic union score for tibial fractures) system, developed by Yabuuchi et al, 47 to determine bone union time after iVHTO. To evaluate the clinical outcomes, the authors collected baseline and follow-up data, including demographic characteristics, complications, Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) Score, which was the standard knee function scale in Japan,1,20,44 Lysholm Score, 25 and Knee injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS). 33

Retrospectively, 3 experienced orthopaedic surgeons (Ko.Y., J.O., and D.S.), who were blinded to all clinical information, independently reviewed each set of radiographs and CT images to determine the time to bone union at the osteotomy site. Interrater consistency was assessed among the 3 surgeons. To calculate intrarater consistency, 1 surgeon (Ko.Y.) was asked to rescore the radiographs 4 weeks after the initial assessment. Other radiological parameters related to lower-limb alignment at each knee were automatically measured using commercially available planning software (MediCAD, Hectec).

The ROC analysis was performed to evaluate the predictive capacity of the opening correction angle for postoperative complications after MOWHTO. For this analysis, we used a database (N = 85 knees) that was created in a previous study by the authors (Appendix Tables A1 and A2). This previous study investigated postoperative complications after MOWHTO. 43 An ROC curve was obtained for predicting postoperative complications using the correction angle in MOWHTO (Figure 2, Appendix Table A3). The ROC curve illustrates the diagnostic performance of the correction angle for identifying patients who experienced postoperative complications (area under the curve: 0.6522; threshold: 12.95; sensitivity: 0.4483; specificity: 0.8298; accuracy: 0.6842). Based on this analysis, the cutoff value (threshold) was set at 13° in the present study.

At the time of analysis, the authors classified the knees that underwent the iVHTO procedure into 2 groups, based on the ROC analysis: the larger correction group (group L), in which the angle corrected by iVHTO was ≥13° (Figure 3), and the smaller correction group (group S), in which the correction was <13° (Figure 4). Then, the 2 groups were compared with respect to postoperative bone union time, complications, and clinical outcomes.

Patients Available for Follow-up

Among the 114 patients (119 knees) who underwent iVHTO, 18 patients (20 knees) were lost to follow-up. Therefore, 109 knees of 96 patients were enrolled in the present study (Figure 5). The mean follow-up period was 47.2 ± 14.4 months (range, 3-6 years). The 109 knees were classified into 2 groups based on the intraoperative correction angle of the tibia: group L (≥13°) and group S (<13°). Group L included 49 knees from 42 patients (22 men and 20 women), and group S included 60 knees from 54 patients (28 men and 26 women).

Preoperative Planning

The tibial valgus correction angle is determined using Miniaci's method 29 based on a full-length standing anteroposterior (AP) radiograph. A lateral wedge resection angle was determined for each knee so that the mechanical axis in the corrected limb passed through a point on the lateral tibial plateau, which was 67% lateral to the medial edge of the tibial joint surface. A 2-dimensional simulation of the iVHTO surgery was performed for each patient to assess the appropriateness of the planned correction angle, using the planning software (MediCAD).

Surgical Procedures

The details of the iV-HTO procedure were previously reported.14,19 First, the fibula was osteotomized at the center of the shaft, using the acute oblique osteotomy and ligation procedure.44,49 Under observation using a C-arm fluoroscope, a K-wire was inserted perpendicular to the anterior surface of the proximal tibia and into the apex of the V-shaped osteotomy. A Protractor-installed Wire Insertion Guide (Olympus Terumo Biomaterial), which allowed the angles of the 2 sleeves to be matched to the planned bone resection angle for each patient, was attached to the apex K-wire. Two pairs of K-wires were then inserted from the lateral side of the tibia toward the apex wire. The coronal ascending osteotomy parallel to the anterior surface of the tibial tubercle was made. A lateral hemi-wedge bone resection was made along the 2 pairs of inserted K-wires. Next, the anteromedial periosteum and a part of the superficial medial collateral ligament were elevated from the tibia just along the medial osteotomy line. A Parallel Drill Guide (Olympus Terumo Biomaterials) was set on the medial osteotomy line. Multiple parallel holes were created in the tibia by inserting a 2 mm-diameter K-wire into this guide. Along these parallel holes, the medial tibial osteotomy was performed.

During fixation of the osteotomy site using an LCP, the previously reported quantitative technique 5 was used to achieve the planned tibial alignment correction. First, the proximal end of an LCP was precisely installed on the proximal tibia using the quantitative technique. After a valgus force was manually applied to the tibia, a compression screw was inserted into the distal tibia through a combination hole of the LCP. Inserting this screw completed the valgus correction and applied a compression force to the osteotomy site. Then, 4 locking screws were inserted into the plate's distal portion. Morselized bone chips made from the resected bone were implanted into the opening space at the medial side of the tibia.

Postoperative Management

A previously reported rehabilitation protocol14,19 was used postoperatively. Quadriceps exercises with quadriceps-setting and straight-leg raising were allowed 1 day after surgery. Passive knee motion from 0° to 90° was allowed during the first 2 weeks, and active knee motion from 0° to 120° was encouraged thereafter. Partial weightbearing with crutches and full weightbearing were allowed at 3 and 5 weeks after surgery, respectively.

Radiological Evaluations

Evaluation of Postoperative Knee Alignment and Patellar Height

Evaluation of Postoperative Knee Alignment and Patellar Height

AP radiographs of the knee and the whole lower limb were taken in the standing position, and a lateral radiograph of the knee and a skyline-view radiograph of the patellofemoral joint were taken at 30° of knee flexion in the supine position. As for dimensional parameters of the tibia, the anatomic femorotibial angle, the anatomic medial-proximal tibial angle (MPTA), the hip-knee-ankle angle (HKA), and the point at which the mechanical axis (weightbearing line) passed across the joint line (%MA) were measured on the AP radiographs, using the MediCAD software. In addition, the Caton-Deschamps index, 3 the posterior tibial slope (PTS) angle, and the Insall-Salvati ratio 12 were measured on the lateral radiograph of the knee.

2. Determination of Bone Union at the Osteotomy Site

2. Determination of Bone Union at the Osteotomy Site

To determine bone union time after iVHTO, the modified RUST system reported by Yabuuchi et al 47 was used. In this system, scoring was performed with both radiographs and CT images. On AP and lateral radiographs of the knee taken at each period after surgery, bridging callus formation at the tibial osteotomy site was assessed at each of the medial, lateral, anterior, and posterior cortices. Each cortex was given a score of 1 point if an osteotomy line was clearly visible with no callus, 2 points if there was callus formation but an osteotomy line was still visible, and 3 points if an osteotomy line was invisible, or if the cortices were completely bridged by a mature callus even though an osteotomy line was still visible. The individual scores for the 4 cortices were summed (full mark of 12 points) to give a total score for each period (abbreviated as RUST-XP).

The bone union of the gap between the lateral hemi-closing osteotomy surfaces was observed with the CT images. The gap was divided into 4 portions, including the anterocentral, posterocentral, anterolateral, and posterolateral portions, and trabecula-like bone formation in the gap was evaluated with the following scoring rules. Each gap was given a score of 1 point if a gap was clearly visible with no trabecula-like bone formation, 2 points if trabecula-like bone columns penetrating the gap were sparsely found on almost all CT slices, and 3 points if the trabecula-like bone columns penetrating the gap were abundantly observed on almost all CT slices. The individual scores for the 4 portions were summed (full mark: 12 points) to give a total score for each period (RUST-CT).

Thus, bone-healing status on radiographs and CT images obtained at the scheduled postoperative time was independently scored. The period when the total score of either RUST-XP or RUST-CT reached the maximum score of 12 points was defined as the period of complete bone union at the osteotomy site. 47

3. Classification of Bone Healing Progression

3. Classification of Bone Healing Progression

Progression of bone healing at the closing wedge osteotomy site, which was observed using the radiograms and CT images, was classified into type 1 (direct healing), type 2 (indirect healing), and type 3 (delayed union), according to the criteria reported by Yabuuchi et al 47 (Appendix Table A4).

Clinical Evaluations

Demographic data included sex, age, height, body weight, bone mass index, bone mineral density (BMD), and OA grade. The BMD was measured with a DXA bone densitometer (Discovery A, Hologic Inc), and it was shown as the rate (%) of the young adult mean. On the AP radiogram of the knee, the radiological stage of OA was assessed according to the KL grading system. 16

Postoperative complications were detected from the electronic medical record system. Delayed union was defined as an osteotomy site where bone union had not yet been completed at 4 months after surgery in the quantitative evaluation of postoperative radiographs taken over time, according to the definition reported by Yabuuchi et al. 47 Nonunion was defined as an osteotomy site where bone union had not yet been completed at 9 months after surgery.24,32,34,37 In addition, an osteotomy site that required additional surgery due to the appearance of a large translucent zone was defined as nonunion, regardless of the postoperative time. Loss of correction was defined as a reduction of the MPTA of >5° between the immediate postoperative and the final follow-up period.

Clinical evaluations of symptoms and knee functions were evaluated in each patient using the JOA score, which was the standard knee function scale in Japan,1,20,44 Lysholm score, 25 and KOOS. 33

Statistical Analysis

All data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation. Quadratic-weighted kappa was used to assess inter- and intrarater reliability concerning the radiographic union score. To compare the groups, the Mann-Whitney U test was used for continuous variables, and the Pearson chi-square test was used for discrete variables. The effects of the performed HTO on patients were evaluated by comparing preoperative data with postoperative results using a paired t test. Before the calculation, the Shapiro-Wilk test was performed to confirm that a parametric method was appropriate for this additional comparison. A commercially available software program (JMP Pro 10.0 for Windows, SAS Institute Japan) was used for statistical analysis. The significance level was set at P = .05.

The sample size of this study was determined by the number of eligible knees during the study period, as it was a retrospective cohort study. Therefore, an a priori sample size calculation could not be performed. However, to assess the sensitivity of our study, we calculated the minimum detectable standardized mean difference based on the available sample size (2-sided α = .05, 80% power). The effect size (Cohen’s d) was calculated to be 0.54, which indicated a medium effect size. In addition, to compare the bone union time between the groups, we presented 95% CIs for each group.

Results

Preoperatively, the HKA, FTA, and %MA values, which showed the degree of varus deformity of the knee, were significantly (P < .001) greater in group L than in group S (Table 2). The MPTA, which indicated the degree of varus deformity of the tibia, was significantly smaller (P < .001) in group L (82.9°) than in group S (84.5°). There were no significant differences in the PTS, the Caton-Deschamps index, or the Insall-Salvati ratio between the 2 groups (Table 2).

The intraoperative correction angle of the tibia ranged from 13° to 19° (mean, 15.6) in group L and ranged from 9° to 12° (mean, 10.5) in group S (Table 2). Time Required to Complete Bone Union

To calculate intrarater consistency, one observer (K.Yabuuchi) was asked to score the radiographs 4 weeks after the initial assessment. Concerning these scoring systems, the weighted kappa was 0.955 (95% CI, 0.953-0.974), indicating a strong agreement. Interrater reliability was evaluated by comparing the scores of the 3 observers (K.Yabuuchi, J.O., D.S.). The weighted kappa was 0.853 (95% CI, 0.821-0.884) between raters 1 and 2; 0.803 (95% CI, 0.753-0.851) between raters 2 and 3; and 0.775 (95% CI, 0.726-0.817) between raters 3 and 1, all indicating a strong agreement. Based on these scoring systems, the bone union rate and the time of bone union were determined in each patient.

In group L, only 1 knee with a delayed union required additional surgical treatment. This knee was diagnosed as a nonunion in this study. In the other 108 knees, the time required to complete bone union was shown in Table 3. The mean bone union time was 12.4 weeks in group S and 12.7 weeks in group L. No significant differences were observed between the 2 groups. Regarding the classification of bone healing progression, group L involved types 1 (direct healing), 2 (indirect healing), and 3 (delayed union) in 74.4%, 19.1%, and 6.4%, respectively, while group S included types 1, 2, and 3 in 76.3%, 18.6%, and 5.1%, respectively (Table 3). No significant difference was found between the 2 groups.

Knee Function Scores and KOOS

Preoperatively, there were no significant differences in the JOA, Lysholm, and KOOS subscale scores between the 2 groups (Table 5). Discussion

The most important finding in the present study was that there were no significant differences between group L (with a correction angle of ≥13°) and group S (with a correction angle of <13°) in terms of the postoperative bone union time, complications, JOA, Lysholm, or KOOS scores in the 3- to 6-year follow-up results after the iVHTO surgery with LCP fixation. These findings indicate that larger alignment corrections (≥13°) using the iVHTO procedure did not prolong bone union time, increase complication rates, or lead to poorer clinical outcomes compared with smaller corrections (<13°) in patients with severe varus osteoarthritic knees.

In the present study, a cutoff value of 13° was determined using ROC analysis to evaluate the predictive ability of the opening correction angle for postoperative complications after MOWHTO. It is crucial to interpret the clinical results of the present study by recognizing that knees undergoing the iVHTO procedure were classified using the cutoff value determined for knees undergoing MOWHTO. Previous studies on MOWHTO reported that the incidence of complications increases and bone union is delayed when the medial opening exceeds 11° or 13 mm.8,23 However, ROC analysis was not performed in those studies. The cutoff value of 13° used in the present study is equal to or slightly greater than the boundary values used in these previous studies. In the present study, larger corrections (≥13°) did not increase complication rates after the iVHTO procedure. Notably, this finding differs from the results reported in cohorts undergoing MOWHTO.8,23 These observations suggest that the iVHTO procedure may have different clinical characteristics from MOWHTO when larger alignment corrections are performed.

A notable feature of the present study is that bone union time after this iVHTO procedure was assessed quantitatively from radiographic and CT images. As a result, there were no significant differences in the bone union time between group L (mean, 12.7 weeks) and group S (12.4 weeks). This result suggests that larger alignment corrections using the iVHTO procedure do not prolong bone-healing time. The present study also showed that type 1 bone healing (direct healing) occurred in 74.4% of group L and 76.3% of group S, with no significant difference between the 2 groups. Yabuuchi et al 47 reported that the early bone healing observed in knees undergoing the iVHTO procedure is attributable to the predominant occurrence of type 1 bone healing. Therefore, the similar incidence of type 1 bone healing may explain why greater corrections do not prolong bone-healing time after iVHTO.

At the 3- to 6-year follow-up, there were no significant differences in postoperative JOA, Lysholm, or KOOS scores between the 2 groups. However, the radiological OA grade had been significantly more advanced in group L compared with group S at the time of the preoperative examination. These results are thought to be attributable to the following 2 findings observed in the present study. First, there were no significant differences in the postoperative HKA, %MA, FTA, and MPTA values between the 2 groups, even though the preoperative knee alignment demonstrated significantly more severe varus deformity in group L than in group S. This finding indicates that the iVHTO procedure achieved appropriate valgus correction as planned preoperatively, even in patients with severe preoperative varus deformity. Consequently, the postoperative HKA averaged 3.7° in group L and 4.1° in group S, with no significant difference between the groups. Previous studies have indicated that achieving a sufficient valgus correction of 3° to 5° in the HKA angle is essential for favorable long-term outcomes after LCWHTO.30,40,48 Therefore, the appropriate valgus correction achieved in both groups may have contributed to the comparable 3- to 6-year follow-up outcomes. Secondly, complete bone union was achieved within approximately 3 months in both groups, enabling the patients to undergo timely and adequate rehabilitation. That is, neither group of patients experienced delays in postoperative rehabilitation due to delayed bone union. As a result, the therapeutic effect of HTO was preserved equally in both groups. Rehabilitation is important after the iVHTO procedure because it is more invasive than MOWHTO.

LCWHTO has been the standard procedure for cases requiring larger alignment corrections. Many studies have reported favorable long-term outcomes of LCWHTO.30,40,43,48 However, these studies have also reported a high incidence of complications, including peroneal nerve palsy, nonunion, decreased bone mass in the proximal tibia, and shortening of the lower leg. The timing of bone union after LCWHTO has been unclear, as few clinical studies have quantitatively evaluated postoperative bone healing. Only 1 study 42 reported that the bone union time after a modified LCWHTO procedure 13 averaged 4.5 months in 80% of the 55 knees, whereas it was 6 months or longer in the remaining 20%. Furthermore, no studies have investigated whether larger alignment corrections in LCWHTO adversely affect bone healing, complication rates, or clinical outcomes. Such investigations should be conducted in the future to accurately evaluate the therapeutic potential of LCWHTO.

The present study has limitations. First, the clinical outcomes were not directly compared between the iVHTO procedure and other HTO procedures. Therefore, this study could not address whether iVHTO is superior to other HTO procedures. However, the findings of this study enhance understanding of the characteristics of the iVHTO procedure and provide a basis for future studies that directly compare iVHTO with other HTO procedures. Secondly, the number of patients in this study was not sufficient to reach a definitive conclusion. Thirdly, the long-term follow-up results remain unknown. Beyond these limitations, we believe that the present study has added important new information to the field of HTO.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that larger alignment corrections (≥13°) using the iVHTO procedure with LCP fixation did not prolong bone union time, increase complication rates, or lead to poorer clinical outcomes compared with smaller corrections (<13°) in patients with severe varus osteoarthritic knees. These findings enhance understanding of the characteristics of the iVHTO procedure and provide a basis for future studies that directly compare iVHTO with other HTO procedures.
