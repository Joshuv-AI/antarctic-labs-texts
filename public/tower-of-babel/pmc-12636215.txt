Integrating whole-genome resequencing data reveals adaptive selection signatures in sheep populations under extreme environments
Authors: Zhixu Pang, Pengkun Yang, Ke Cai, Wannian Wang, Ayoola Ebenezer Afe, Yangyang Pan, Liying Qiao, Wenzhong Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdaptation to extreme environments has shaped the genetic architecture of sheep, yet the genomic signatures remain insufficiently understood. This study aimed to uncover genomic regions and candidate genes mediating ovine adaptation to high-altitude, thermal, and arid environments. This study integrated whole-genome resequencing data from 444 individuals representing 91 native sheep populations with high-resolution climatic data encompassing nine ecological regions and eleven environmental variables. Distinct environmental clusters aligned with genomic structure, supporting ecological selection as a driver of population differentiation. Integrating Fst, LFMM, and Samβada analyses for genome–environment association, we identified a stringent set of 178 candidate genes. After screening populations from extreme environments using environmental data, we applied three selection signal analysis methods (Fst, XP-EHH, and θπ), identifying 2, 21, 10, and 13 candidate genes associated with high elevation, hot, cold, and arid environments, respectively. These results indicate that sheep adaptation to extreme environments involves coordinated, multi-system mechanisms integrating metabolism, water balance, immunity, and morphology. Key genes such as MVD and GHR support energy metabolism and thermogenesis, SLC26A4 and KCNMA1 regulate fluid and electrolyte homeostasis, FBXL3 modulates circadian rhythm, and BNC2, RXFP2, and PAPPA2 contribute to pigmentation, skeletal morphology, and fat deposition. Together, these polygenic adaptations enable sheep to maintain survival under complex ecological pressures. This study provides new insights into the mechanisms of sheep adaptation to extreme environments and offers a basis for improving breeds and enhancing stress resilience.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12281-7.Keywords: Whole-genome sequence, Local adaptation, Sheep (Ovis aries), Genome–environment association, Genome-wide selection scan

Introduction

Livestock species exhibit extensive phenotypic and genetic diversity, reflecting domestication, artificial selection, and adaptation to diverse and often extreme environments. Their successful domestication and proliferation across diverse and often harsh environments testify to their remarkable capacity for adaptation [1]. Unraveling the genetic mechanisms underlying such adaptation is a fundamental gap in evolutionary biology and critical for improving productivity, resilience, and sustainability of livestock under escalating climatic challenges. Among domestic species, sheep (Ovis aries), one of the earliest domesticated ungulates (approximately 11,000 years ago in the Fertile Crescent) [2], exemplify this adaptive potential. Since their domestication, sheep have dispersed widely across Africa, Asia, Europe, and the Americas, crossing a broad array of ecological zones, from humid lowlands and arid deserts to frigid steppes and high mountains, resulting in over a thousand recognized breeds with distinct morphological, physiological, and behavioral traits [3, 4].

Extreme environmental stressors such as hypoxia, heat, cold, and drought impose strong selective pressures, shaping the genetic makeup of populations over time [5]. These forces are particularly evident in sheep, which thrive in environments that often exceed the physiological limits of less-adapted species. The high-altitude sheep populations exhibit hypoxia tolerance associated with both physiological adjustments and genetic changes [6]. Desert-dwelling breeds demonstrate unique reproductive cycles aligned with seasonal rainfall patterns, while cold-adapted breeds display metabolic and structural adaptations that enable survival at subzero temperatures [7]. Beyond phenotypic traits, genomic studies have identified numerous candidate genes implicated in environmental adaptation, such as EPAS1, EGLN involved in oxygen transport [8], TPO, ADCY9, and RXFP2 in thermoregulation [9], HIBADH, and TRIM71 in reproduction [10], and immune function [11].

Traditionally, investigations into livestock adaptation relied on phenotypic observations, physiological measurements, and selective genotyping of candidate genes. While informative, these approaches often suffer from limited scope, inadequate resolution, and inability to disentangle environmental and genetic effects, particularly when studying polygenic traits influenced by multiple loci [12]. Recent advances in high-throughput sequencing technologies, coupled with extensive climate datasets, have revolutionized the field by enabling the integration of genomic and environmental data at an unprecedented scale. Genome–environment association (GEA) analyses, have emerged as powerful tools to identify genomic regions and variants correlated with environmental gradients, providing direct evidence of local adaptation [13, 14].

Several studies have applied such approaches in livestock, including cattle, goats, and chickens, revealing signatures of adaptation to extreme environments [15, 16]. However, in sheep, although studies has been done on specific breeds or regions, such as desert-adapted Qira Black sheep, high-altitude Tibetan sheep, and Mediterranean dairy sheep [6, 17, 18], landscape-level genomic analyses of environmental adaptation encompassing multiple extreme environments and globally distributed populations remain scarce.

Addressing this gap, the present study integrates whole-genome resequencing data from 444 individuals representing 91 sheep populations worldwide with high-resolution climatic and geographical datasets. Focusing on extreme environmental contexts, including high altitude, heat, cold, and aridity, we aim to elucidate the genetic basis of sheep adaptation to these challenging conditions. By combining selection signature analyses with GEA, this study seeks to advance our understanding of the evolutionary processes shaping genetic diversity in sheep, identify candidate genes and pathways underpinning environmental adaptation, and provide insights for conserving and improving genetic resources in the presence of climate change.

Materials and methods

Genomic dataset preparation and quality control

High-resolution whole-genome resequencing data encompassing 91 globally distributed sheep populations were retrieved from publicly accessible genomic repositories [19–25] (Table S1). These populations spanned nine biogeographic regions, including North Africa (NA), South Africa (SSA), East Asia (EA), Western Asia (WA), Eastern Europe (EE), Western Europe (WE), Latin America (LA), North America (NAm), and Southeast Asia (SA) (Table S2). Raw sequencing reads were subjected to quality control using fastp (v0.23.4) [26] to obtain high-quality clean data for downstream analyses. The filtering criteria included removal of reads with >1% ambiguous bases (N), abnormal GC content, low-quality bases, and adapter contamination. Clean reads were then aligned to the reference genome (Ovis aries, https://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genomes/all/GCF/002/742/125/GCF_002742125.1_Oar_rambouillet_v1.0/GCF_002742125.1_Oar_rambouillet_v1.0_genomic.gff.gz) using BWA (v0.7.17-r1188) [27]. For each sample, sequencing depth and genome coverage were calculated based on the alignment results, and individuals with sequencing depth < 10× were removed. SNPs quality control was performed using PLINK (v1.9) [28]. We retained only autosomal SNPs and removed individuals with more than 10% missing genotypes. SNPs were then filtered to exclude those with a call rate less than 90% a minor allele frequency (MAF) less than 0.05, or significant deviation from Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (p < 1e-6). After filtering, a dataset comprising of 444 individuals 17,700,886 SNPs was obtained for subsequent population genomic analyses.

Environmental data acquisition and population clustering

High-resolution environmental data were compiled by accessing the Climatic Research Unit Time-Series (CRU TS v4.08) global climate dataset, covering the period 1901–2023 (excluding Antarctica), obtained from the Climatic Research Unit (CRU) at the University of East Anglia, Norwich [29] available at (https://crudata.uea.ac.uk/cru/data/hrg/cru_ts_4.08/cruts.2406270035.v4.08/). T This dataset provides gridded climate surfaces at a spatial resolution of 0.5°, encompassing seven key variables: cloud cover (cld), diurnal temperature range (dtr), frost day frequency (frs), potential evapotranspiration (pet), precipitation (pre), wet day frequency (wet), and vapor pressure (vap). These climatic layers were subsequently processed into annual and monthly mean global land-area grids (longitude/latitude) using ArcMap v10.8 [30], with coordinate reference system set to GCS_WGS_1984. To capture finer-scale heterogeneity in temperature and elevation, we additionally retrieved higher-resolution (0.1667°) mean temperature (tmp) and elevation (elev) data from the WorldClim v2.1 database [31] available at https://www.worldclim.org/data/worldclim21.html, (last accessed on December 24, 2024). Pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients among the nine environmental variables were computed in R v4.2.0 [32] to assess collinearity and interdependencies.

To quantify and summarize environmental variation across the habitats of the 91 sheep populations, the terra package in R [33] was employed to statistically analyze annual and monthly averages of the eight climatic variables plus elevation. This analysis produced a matrix of 105 environmental parameters, which were then subjected to principal component analysis (PCA) to reduce dimensionality and capture orthogonal axes of ecological variation (Table S3). Based on PCA-derived scores, hierarchical clustering using Euclidean distance metrics partitioned the 91 populations into two distinct ecological clusters (CL1 and CL2), reflecting major environmental gradients.

To assess the degree of congruence between these environmentally defined clusters and underlying genomic structure, the SNP dataset was pruned to mitigate linkage disequilibrium (LD) using PLINK v1.9 [28] w with the parameters --indep-pairwise 50 10 0.2, resulting in a filtered panel of 1,069,286 SNPs for downstream analyses. Population genetic structure was then inferred using PCA on genome-wide variation and by estimating ancestry proportions at K = 2–10 with ADMIXTURE v1.30 [34].

Mapping of genomic regions implicated in environmental adaptation

To delineate genomic regions underpinning environmental adaptation in sheep, we implemented two complementary strategies: a population-differentiation approach leveraging the genetic differentiation index (FST), and a genome–environment association (GEA) framework utilizing the latent factor mixed model (LFMM), both applied to SNP data from 91 sheep populations. We used VCFtools v0.1.16 [35] software to estimate the FST using the sliding window approach utilizing window sizes of 50 kb and steps of 10 kb [36]. The LFMM analysis was conducted using the R package lfmm [37], incorporating 105 environmental variables. The lfmm_ridge_CV function was used to infer the optimal number of latent factors, followed by 1,100 iterations and a burn-in of 100 steps to generate z-scores per SNP. Genomic inflation factors were then applied to correct P-values, mitigating systematic bias and false discovery rates. Genomic intervals extending ± 25 kb from the top 1% of SNPs identified by LFMM were designated as candidate regions associated with environmental adaptability.

To further refine the detection of GEA, we employed Samβada v0.8.3 [38], a software framework optimized for landscape genomics of large-scale datasets that accounts explicitly for spatial autocorrelation and environmental heterogeneity. Samβada evaluates local associations through univariate and multivariate logistic regression models. Based on the Wald score, the top 0.01% models were retained for further scrutiny. These models yielded 1,338 SNPs exhibiting signatures of selection, mapping to 1,115 annotated genes within ± 50 kb intervals of the detected SNPs.

Characterization of genomic signatures underlying High-Altitude adaptation

To elucidate genomic loci contributing to high-altitude adaptation in sheep, we defined high-elevation habitats as regions exceeding 2,500 m above sea level, and low-elevation habitats as regions below 70 m. Based on these criteria, we curated two ecologically and geographically distinct cohorts: the high-altitude group (G-high; n = 27) and the low-altitude group (G-low; n = 43) (Table S4). Specifically, cross-population extended haplotype homozygosity (XP-EHH), FST and nucleotide diversity ratio (θπ) analyses were conducted to compare G-high and G-low populations.

These analyses were implemented within a sliding window framework, applying a 50 kb window with a 10 kb step across the genome to resolve regional patterns of differentiation. Genomic intervals residing in the top 1% tails of the empirical distributions of either FST or θπ ratio were designated as candidate regions under altitude-driven positive selection and were considered putatively adaptive to high-altitude environmental pressures. For the XP-EHH analysis [39], phased haplotypes for the populations were inferred from genotype data using Beagle v5.2 [40]. The XP-EHH scores at each SNP locus were then computed using Selscan v1.3.0 [41] and standardized with the accompanying norm utility to facilitate cross-locus comparisons. Genomic intervals spanning ± 50 kb around the top 1% of SNPs with XP-EHH scores were designated as candidate regions.

Detection of genomic loci associated with adaptation to extreme environmental temperatures

To identify genomic regions underpinning sheep adaptation to contrasting thermal regimes, we stratified populations into discrete thermal ecotypes based on the annual mean temperature (TMP) derived from high-resolution climatic datasets across all sampled populations. This stratification was refined using the Köppen–Geiger climate classification system [42] to enhance ecological validity and reduce geographic sampling bias. Specifically, sheep populations inhabiting regions with an annual TMP >25 °C, classified as either tropical rainforest (Af) or semi-arid steppe (BSh/BSk) climates, were designated as the heat-adapted group (G-hot; n = 33). In contrast, populations from regions with annual TMP < 5 °C and classified as temperate monsoon (Dwa/Dwb), subarctic continental humid (Dfc/Dfd), subarctic monsoon (Dwc/Dwd), or tundra (ET) climates constituted the cold-adapted group (G-cold; n = 42) (Table S5).

To detect genomic signatures of selection associated with these thermal ecotypes, we implemented a suite of population genomic analyses including XP-EHH, FST, and θπ metrics. Genomic intervals spanning ± 50 kb around the top 1% of SNPs with extreme positive XP-EHH scores were designated as candidate regions associated with heat adaptation, whereas those surrounding the bottom 1% of XP-EHH scores—indicative of selection in the G-cold group—were designated as candidate regions associated with cold adaptation.

For the FST analysis, we quantified inter-population allele frequency divergence between G-hot and G-cold groups at each windows using VCFtools v0.1.16 [35], using the sliding window approach utilizing window sizes of 50 kb and steps of 10 kb [36]. The windows with the top 1% of FST values are taken as candidate regions. Finally, in the θπ analysis, we calculated the genome-wide ratio of nucleotide diversity between the two groups (θπG−hot/θπG−cold), designating the top 0.5% of regions, characterized by reduced diversity in G-hot, as putative heat-adaptation loci, and the reciprocal (θπG−cold/θπG−hot, bottom 0.5%) as indicative of cold-adaptation loci.

Identification of genomic regions associated with adaptation to arid environments

The United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP) Aridity Index (AIUNEP) provides a quantitative framework for assessing regional water deficit, capturing the magnitude of annual or seasonal aridity at a given locale [43]. We computed AIUNEP values globally using ArcMap v10.8 [30], according to the equation.

PRE denotes the mean annual precipitation (mm), and PET represents the mean annual potential evapotranspiration (mm).

Following UNEP classification standards, AIUNEP values stratify climatic regimes into five categories: hyper-arid (0–0.05), arid (0.05–0.20), semi-arid (0.20–0.50), dry sub-humid (0.50–0.65), and humid (>0.65). We calculated AIUNEP values for all 91 sheep populations, facilitating their ecological stratification into distinct niches indicative of persistent water scarcity. To identify loci potentially mediating drought resilience, we selected populations inhabiting hyper-arid (0–0.05), arid (0.05–0.20), and humid (>0.65) environments, further refined using the Köppen–Geiger climate classification to ensure robust ecological characterization and balanced geographic representation [42]. To focus exclusively on populations exposed to chronic aridity, those from seasonally arid regions were excluded. The remaining populations inhabiting hyper-arid deserts (BWh/BWk) and semi-arid steppe climates (BSh/BSk) were designated as the arid group (G-arid; n = 59). Conversely, populations from humid oceanic (Cfb/Cfc) and subarctic continental humid (Dfc/Dfd) regions were assigned to the humid group (G-humid; n = 33) (Table S6). To detect genomic regions under divergent selection between these two environmental extremes, we performed genome-wide scans employing XP-EHH [39], FST, and θπ metrics. Candidate genomic regions potentially associated with arid environment tolerance were defined as ± 50 kb intervals surrounding the top 1% of SNPs identified by XP-EHH, and as 50 kb sliding windows (with a 10 kb step) showing strong signals in both FST and θπ analyses.

Annotation and biological interpretation of putative adaptive genes

The genomic intervals identified as candidate targets of selection in the preceding analyses were mapped to annotated features of the sheep genome using bedtools v2.31.1 [44], by intersecting them with gene annotation data from the NCBI genome database. This approach enabled the assignment of candidate regions to specific protein-coding genes and potential regulatory elements within the sheep genome. To assess the biological relevance of these putative adaptive genes, we performed functional enrichment analysis using DAVID v6.8 [45]. Specifically, we tested for overrepresentation of Gene Ontology (GO) terms—encompassing biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components—as well as KEGG pathways [46]. Enrichment significance was assessed using Fisher’s exact test, and only categories with P < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Results

Genotypic quality control and curation of sheep population dataset

Following stringent quality control and curation procedures, a final dataset comprising 444 genetically vetted individuals representing 91 distinct sheep populations was retained for downstream analyses (Fig. 1; Table S2). Subsequent locus-level quality filtering yielded a high-confidence panel of 1,069,286 autosomal SNPs, which satisfied thresholds for call rate, minor allele frequency, and independence, and was utilized in all subsequent population genomic analyses.

Descriptive statistics of global environmental variables across sheep habitats

We characterized the climatic heterogeneity of sheep habitats by integrating nine environmental variables (Fig. 2A), cld, dtr, frs, pet, pre, wet, vap, tmp, and elev. Correlation analyses among these variables (Fig. 2B) revealed that elevation exhibited minimal association with the other climatic parameters, with its weakest correlation observed with wet day frequency (r = 0.02). Notably, mean tmp demonstrated a strong negative correlation with both potential evapotranspiration and frost days (|r| ≥ 0.7), while potential evapotranspiration was moderately correlated with diurnal temperature range, and precipitation was moderately correlated with wet day frequency (0.5 ≤ |r| < 0.7). The PCA indicated that the first principal component (PC1) captured major gradients in temperature-associated climatic variation, with the largest contributions arising from vapor pressure (6.72%), frost days (14.89%), mean annual temperature (17.75%), potential evapotranspiration (21.75%), and cloud cover (13.34%) (Fig. 2C; Table S5).

Hierarchical environmental stratification and congruence with sheep population genomic structure

We applied PCA to the 106 environmental variables characterizing the native habitats of the 91 sheep populations. The eigenvalues of the first three principal components were 40.48, 28.20, and 12.11, respectively, cumulatively accounting for 65.72% of the total climatic variance, with PC1 and PC2 explaining 38.62% and 27.10%, respectively (Fig. 3B). Based on PCA-derived scores, hierarchical clustering of populations using Euclidean distance metrics partitioned the 91 populations into two ecologically distinct clusters: CL1 and CL2 (Fig. 3A).

Populations assigned to CL1 predominantly occupy temperate climatic zones located north of 37° N/S, encompassing primarily European and East Asian lineages (Asia: n = 29; Africa: n = 2; Europe: n = 17). In contrast, populations in CL2 are distributed in tropical and arid zones south of 37° N/S, comprising mostly African and Western Asian lineages (Africa: n = 16; Asia: n = 23; North America: n = 1; Latin America: n = 3).

To assess the extent of concordance between these environmental clusters and underlying genomic architecture, we examined the genetic structure of all 444 individuals via PCA and admixture analyses. PCA based on genome-wide SNP variation revealed clear genetic differentiation between the CL1 and CL2 groupings (Fig. 3C). Additionally, ancestry inference using ADMIXTURE v1.3.0 at K = 3 revealed that individuals originating from the same geographic regions shared highly similar ancestry components, whereas populations from distinct geographic regions exhibited different ancestry components. Moreover, even within the same geographic region, populations assigned to CL1 and CL2 displayed subtle differences in their ancestry components, reflecting the combined influence of environmental and geographic factors on genomic structure (Fig. 3D). These findings collectively support the hypothesis that divergent environmental selection has acted as a primary driver of genetic differentiation in these sheep populations. This long-term action of environmental selection has driven the accumulation of geographically structured, lineage-specific genetic variants, resulting in a coordinated pattern whereby ecological clustering and genetic differentiation are closely aligned, a hallmark of local adaptation in heterogeneous environments.

Detection of genomic loci under selection associated with broad-scale environmental gradients

To identify genomic regions putatively shaped by natural selection in response to environmental gradients, we employed a sliding-window genome scan of inter-population genetic differentiation. Specifically, FST was calculated in 50 kb windows with a 10 kb step size across the genome, and 468 regions surpassing the empirical top 1% threshold (FST ≥ 0.0223) were delineated as candidate regions, collectively encompassing 639 annotated genes (Table S7). The three most highly differentiated regions were located on chromosome 4 (54.61–54.66 Mb), chromosome 14 (14.63–14.68 Mb), and chromosome 24 (9.94–9.99 Mb), harboring genes implicated in apoptosis and organogenesis (BCAP29 [47]), immune-related pathologies (IL17C [48], CLEC16A [49]), basal metabolic processes (MVD [50]), and post-transcriptional mRNA metabolism (ZC3H18 [51]) (Fig. 4A).

Parallel GEA analysis using the latent factor mixed model (LFMM) at K = 3 latent factors revealed 10,693 SNPs significantly correlated with environmental variables. Genomic intervals extending 25 kb upstream and downstream of the top 1% of these SNPs were designated as adaptive regions, collectively mapping to 4,040 genes (Table S8). The highest-scoring SNPs by z-score resided on chromosome 4, chromosome 7, and chromosome 10, encompassing genes associated with apoptosis and development (DUS4L [52]), reproductive biology (PATL2 [53]), neurodevelopmental disorders (MYCBP2 [54], SPG11 [55]), visual function (COG5 [56], CLN5 [57]), and circadian rhythm regulation (FBXL3 [58]) (Fig. 4B).

Complementary analysis using Samβada selected the top 2,800 models (≈ 0.01% of univariate tests) based on Wald statistics, identifying 932 SNPs and annotating 1,115 genes within ± 50 kb of these SNPs (Table S9), FST (n = 639), LFMM method (n = 4040) and Samβada (n = 1115). Comparative integration of the three methods revealed 1,101 genes detected by at least two approaches, and a stringent set of 178 genes identified by all three (Fig. 4C). Subsequent functional enrichment analysis of these 178 genes via DAVID showed significant enrichment in eight biological processes (BP), one cellular component (CC), seven molecular function (MF) categories, and seven KEGG pathways (P < 0.05), implicating these genes in diverse biological mechanisms underpinning environmental adaptation (Table S10) (Fig. 4D).

Genomic signatures of selection associated with high-altitude adaptation in sheep populations

To characterize the genomic architecture underlying adaptation to the hypoxic and cold conditions of high-altitude environments, we compared allele frequency distributions between the G-high (high-altitude) and G-low (low-altitude) sheep cohorts using FST. This analysis identified 377 regions exceeding the empirical outlier threshold for inter-population differentiation. These regions are mapped to genomic regions encompassing 522 annotated genes. Among the most differentiated regions were those on chromosomes corresponding to populations 3, 7, and 10, which overlapped with genes implicated in key adaptive traits, including skeletal morphology and body conformation (RXFP2 [59, 60]), immune modulation (ARID5A [61]), growth and developmental pathways (ABCD4 [62]), visual function and ocular development (VSX2 [63]), and respiratory and energy homeostasis (KANSL3 [64]) (Fig. 5A).

Using the XP-EHH method, we identified 1303 regions within the top 1% of the scoring distribution for sheep living at high and low altitudes, respectively. For loci identified in the high-altitude-adapted sheep group, 1701 genes were annotated within ± 50 kb of the highest-scoring SNP (Table S12). We further interrogated these populations using the θπ ratio (θπG-high/θπG-low), which captures reductions in nucleotide diversity consistent with selective sweeps in the high-altitude group. This complementation analysis highlighted genomic intervals corresponding to the top 1% of θπ ratio outliers, collectively mapping to 543 genes (Table S13), 74 of which were shared with genes identified by outlier analysis using the FST and XPEHH methods (Fig. 5B).

Functional enrichment analysis of these 74 genes revealed significantly enriched in three GO BP, two CC, two MF categories, and four KEGG pathways (P < 0.05), suggesting involvement in hypoxia adaptation, energy metabolism, and developmental pathways (Table S14) (Fig. 5C). To pinpoint core genes jointly shaped by high-altitude selective pressures and broader environmental differentiation, we intersected the high-altitude-associated gene set (74 genes) with the environmentally differentiated CL1/CL2 gene set (178 genes), identifying two candidate genes, BNC2 and MITF, as putative central drivers of sheep high-altitude adaptation (Fig. 5D).

Genomic signatures of selection associated with thermal adaptation in sheep populations

To delineate genomic regions subjected to divergent selection under thermal extremes, we applied the XP-EHH approach, which detects selective sweeps that have reached or are near fixation in one population relative to another. Using XP-EHH, we identified 1333 and 1108 regions within the top 1% tail of the score distribution across comparisons of sheep inhabiting hot and cold environments. For loci identified in the hot-adapted group, 1753 genes were annotated within ± 50 kb of the top-scoring SNPs (Table S15). The highest-ranked genes in these regions encode proteins implicated in neurodevelopment and synaptic function (SLITRK3 [61]), auditory physiology (SLC26A4 [65]), reproductive biology (CBLL1 [66]), and cellular stress response and RNA processing (DICER1 [67]) (Fig. 6A and B). In contrast, loci enriched in the cold-adapted group mapped to 1440 genes (Table S16), with the top candidates involved in immune regulation and lipid signaling (LRP8 [68]), hypoxia adaptation and erythropoiesis (HBB [69]), and Golgi-associated glycosylation processes (GOLGA5 [70]) (Fig. 6C).

Integration of results from complementary FST and θπ ratio analyses further refined the candidate gene sets (Table S17, Table S18) (Fig. 6D), yielding 91 genes consistently associated with heat adaptation and 80 genes associated with cold adaptation. These 181 genes are enriched in transcriptional regulation, redox balance, intracellular trafficking, and immune and metabolic signaling pathways, collectively supporting cellular homeostasis and adaptive physiological responses under extreme temperature (Table S19).

To further pinpoint core genes jointly shaped by natural selection and temperature-driven selective pressures, we intersected the temperature-associated gene sets with the CL1/CL2 environmental adaptation gene set (178 genes) (Fig. 6E). This integrative analysis revealed 21 shared genes in the heat-adapted group (DIP2B, LIMA1, COG5, GPR22, DUS4L, BCAP29, SLC26A4, CBLL1, RORA, DMXL2, MRPS31, FOXO1, CLN5, FBXL3, RERE, ZNF599, ZNF181, SBF2, PGK2, CRISP1, BNC2) and 10 shared genes in the cold-adapted group (LRP8, RYK, CRADD, GHR, FOXQ1, NARS2, ADGRA1, KIAA1671, PDE8A, TNFSF4), underscoring their potential central roles in conferring thermal resilience through pleiotropic biological pathways.

Genomic signatures of selection associated with aridity adaptation in sheep populations

To investigate the genomic basis of sheep adaptation to water deficit and arid climatic conditions, we compared populations from contrasting moisture regimes (arid versus humid), as defined by the UNEP aridity index (AIUNEP) and the Köppen–Geiger climate classification. Subsequently, we implemented a multi-faceted genome-wide selection scan employing FST, nucleotide diversity ratio (θπ), and XP-EHH to identify loci under putative selection associated with aridity tolerance. At the 1% empirical threshold, the FST analysis detected 473 regions, mapping to 698 genes (Table S19) (Fig. 7A). The differentiated regions encompassing genes implicated in immune regulation and inflammatory response (TNFAIP3 [71]), fluid balance (SLC26A4 [72]), fat deposition (PAPPA2 [73]), and ciliogenesis and motility (KATNAL1 [74]). A parallel analysis based on the θπ ratio (θπG−arid/θπG−humid) identified 1,418 regions, encompassing 614 annotated genes (Table S20) (Fig. 7B). The top θπ outlier regions corresponding to genes involved in neurodevelopment and synaptic signaling (AOPEP [75]), cellular energy metabolism (ATP5ME [76, 77]), transmembrane transport (SLC49A3 [78]), and renin and saliva secretion pathways (KCNMA1 [79]). The XP-EHH analysis detected 1,597 genes (Table S21). Among these, ALDH1A2 [25] is implicated in cellular responses to lipid metabolism, potentially enhancing energy homeostasis under water scarcity. TBXT [80]regulates tail vertebrae number, influencing tail size and fat deposition, while PDGFD [81]is associated with fat accumulation in the tail. Together, these genes suggest that structural traits and lipid storage in tails are key mechanisms by which sheep adapt to arid environments.

Discussion

Understanding how domesticated sheep adapt to diverse and extreme environments is crucial for both evolutionary biology and sustainable animal production. We conducted a comprehensive study using whole-genome resequencing data combined with high-resolution environmental variables. This approach allowed us to investigate the genomic basis of sheep adaptation across diverse ecological gradients. Our results indicate that sheep adaptation is polygenic and multifaceted, involving coordinated responses to altitude, temperature, and aridity. The congruence between environmental clustering (Fig. 3A) and genomic differentiation (Fig. 3C) underscores local adaptation as a primary driver of population structure in this widely distributed species [87, 88].

Through gene–environment association analyses, we uncovered the complex adaptive evolutionary mechanisms that enabled sheep to thrive under diverse ecological pressures. Natural selection was found to primarily shape the adaptive genomic architecture through key biological pathways, including metabolism, immunity, development, and transcriptional regulation. Extreme environments often represent a combination of multiple stressors, such as hypoxia, strong ultraviolet radiation, and low temperatures at high altitudes, or water scarcity, heat, and food shortages in arid regions. Our findings indicate that sheep adaptation does not rely on isolated responses to single stressors but instead emerges from the coordinated regulation of multiple physiological systems. In terms of metabolism and energy balance, signals from MVD [50], involved in cholesterol biosynthesis, highlight the role of metabolic flexibility and energy storage in resource-limited environments, while PATL2 [53] suggest adaptive strategies linking reproductive regulation with fat deposition under drought conditions. Defense and homeostasis were supported by enrichment of BCAP29 [47] and chemotaxis-related genes, underscoring the importance of a robust immune system in tropical–arid regions with high pathogen loads, and by the role of FBXL3 [58] in circadian rhythm regulation. Collectively, these results demonstrate that sheep adaptation is a systemic and multi-pathway process, achieved through the integration of metabolic, immune, reproductive, and behavioral mechanisms.

Our study reveals that high-altitude sheep populations show strong selection signals in multiple functional genes involved in pigmentation, morphology, metabolism, and immunity, reflecting a coordinated multi-system adaptive strategy. Genes involved in pigmentation, such as MITF [81]and BNC2 [89], mitigate DNA damage under intense ultraviolet radiation by enhancing melanin synthesis, thereby providing photoprotection. RXFP2, a gene associated with skeletal and body size development, likely reflects selection for more compact body morphology and reinforced skeletal structure, enabling sheep to cope with low temperatures and rugged terrain [59, 60]. Signals of selection in ARID5A [61], a known regulator of immune and inflammatory responses, indicate that both ultraviolet exposure and pathogen pressure at high altitude may drive immune adaptation. ABCD4 involved in vitamin B12 transport [62] indirectly supports erythropoiesis and energy metabolism, mitigating hypoxia effects on growth and blood traits. KANSL3, associated with mitochondrial function and energy homeostasis [64], highlights the importance of efficient energy utilization under low-oxygen conditions. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that high-altitude adaptation encompasses coordinated regulation of respiration, metabolic balance, immune defense, body morphology, and UV protection, consistent with observations in in yaks [90], Tibetan antelopes [91], and humans [92].

Sheep inhabiting extreme temperature environments exhibit a coordinated, multi-system evolutionary adaptation, as evidenced by selection signals in multiple functional genes. In heat-adapted populations, selection on SLC26A4 [65] enhance ion and electrolyte transport [93], which helps maintain fluid balance and facilitates heat dissipation, possibly by improving adjusting osmoregulation. Furthermore, selection on CBLL1, associated with reproductive biology [66], suggests selective pressure to maintain fertility in hot environments. Conversely, in cold-adapted sheep populations, we observed enrichment of genes associated with lipid metabolism, thermogenesis, and body size regulation. This is consistent with previous studies of mammalian adaptation to cold environments, where cold exposure typically activates brown adipose tissue and lipid mobilization to enhance heat production [94–96]. A key candidate gene GHR [97], which influences both energy storage and brown adipose tissue thermogenesis [98]. This supporting thermal homeostasis under cold, consistent with functional studies in other mammals [99]. HBB, primarily associated to hypoxia adaptation and erythropoiesis, contributes indirectly to cold adaptation by improving oxygen transport efficiency [100], thereby supporting energy metabolism and heat production [101]. Overall, temperature adaptation in sheep is polygenic, involving interacting functional modules: metabolism, homeostatic regulation, cellular stress responses, and behavioral/neural regulation, rather than a single major-effect gene. Consistent with experimental evolution and comparative genomics studies [94, 102, 103], these findings show that adaptive responses to thermal extremes are generally complex and multifactorial.

Our genomic analysis of drought-adapted sheep populations reflecting integrated evolutionary responses to arid environments. SLC26A4, a pleiotropic gene, regulates fluid and electrolyte balance and enhances renal water reabsorption while also contributing to thermoregulation [72, 104]. A key evolutionary response to arid environments is the development of the fat tail, which serves as a crucial reservoir for energy. PAPPA2 regulates fat deposition [105] and tail morphology [21, 106], highlighting the role of tail morphology in energy storage and thermal regulation. Additionally, hair characteristics are likely modulated by KATNAL1 [74], which may influence fiber fineness to enhance heat dissipation. FBXL3 [82] is a regulator of circadian rhythm. In desert mammals [83], temperature-driven circadian entrainment synchronizes activity and metabolic cycles with daily thermal fluctuations, reflecting an adaptive strategy for optimizing energy and water use in extreme arid environments. In terms of metabolism and water management, ATP5ME [77] participates in cellular energy metabolism to improve energy efficiency. Notably, KCNMA1 [79] regulates renin and saliva secretion to maintain internal hydration. Adaptation in immunity and skin is supported by BCAP29 [107] and ATP8B4 [84], which enhance immune responses and regulate skin pigmentation, providing protection against heat, intense ultraviolet radiation, and pathogens. This indicating a broadly conserved mechanism for drought resilience. Similar adaptive mechanisms involving energy and water storage have also been observed in other arid-adapted species [108, 109]. Overall, sheep adaptation to arid environments represents a complex, polygenic process. It involves coordinated regulation of multiple functional modules: water balance, thermoregulation, energy metabolism, immune defense, and morphological adaptations. These multi-system adaptations collectively enable survival and reproduction under the combined pressures of limited water, scarce food, high temperatures, and intense solar radiation.

Despite the rigor of our analyses, several limitations remain inherent to genotype–environment studies. It is challenging to fully disentangle the effects of demographic history and genetic drift from selection, and our methods cannot account for unmeasured environmental variables. Moreover, the identified candidate genes are still putative; the causal variants may reside in non-coding regulatory regions. Therefore, functional validation through transcriptomic, epigenomic, and proteomic studies, combined with fine-scale phenotyping, will be crucial to confirm the adaptive significance of the identified loci. Comparative analyses across domesticated and wild sheep populations, as well as experimental validation under controlled conditions, will further clarify the molecular mechanisms underlying these complex adaptations.

Conclusion

This study elucidates how extreme environmental pressures have shaped the sheep genome through distinct yet interconnected selective pathways, revealing a complex landscape of polygenic adaptation to high-altitude, thermal, and arid habitats. By integrating whole-genome variation with fine-resolution climatic data, we demonstrate that environmental heterogeneity leaves discernible genomic footprints in pathways mediating physiological homeostasis, developmental plasticity, and reproductive fitness. The identification of robust candidate genes and functional networks provides a genomic foundation for improving resilience in sheep under the accelerating challenges of climate change. This study highlights the utility of landscape genomics in disentangling adaptive processes in domesticated species and underscores the importance of conserving genetic diversity as a buffer against environmental perturbations. Our findings offer novel insights not only into the evolutionary trajectories of sheep but also into the broader principles governing adaptation in vertebrate genomes under ecological stress.
