Cervical cancer and screening: Knowledge, attitudes, and adherence among university students in Italy
Authors: Silvia Angelillo, Grazia Miraglia del Giudice, Giorgia Della Polla, Italo Francesco Angelillo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe objectives of this cross-sectional survey were to assess the knowledge, attitudes, and practices toward cervical cancer screening among female university students in Italy and to identify the associated determinants. The survey was conducted in July and September 2025 in Caserta and Naples, involving 1003 respondents. The average knowledge score regarding human papillomavirus (HPV) infection and cervical cancer was 12.6 out of 20, with only 2% achieving the maximum score. Knowledge was significantly higher among participants who were older, those in the medical field, those vaccinated against HPV, those who had oral sex, and those who received information from healthcare workers (HCWs). Only 12.7% perceived themselves at high risk for HPV-related cancer, with higher concern among those not in the medical field and those who had acquired information from HCWs. More than half (56.9%) considered Pap test useful, and this was more likely among older participants, those with a higher knowledge score, those who had received HPV vaccination, and those who had acquired information from HCWs. Among screening-eligible students aged ≥25 y, 65.1% had undergone cervical cancer screening, and 72.7% adhered to the recommended intervals. Screening uptake was higher among those older and those who had engaged in sexual intercourse. These findings highlight the urgent need for targeted health education interventions, optimized HCW communication, and the dissemination of information to improve awareness, risk perception, and cervical cancer screening uptake.KEYWORDS: Cervical cancer; HPV; Italy, knowledge; screening; survey

Introduction

In 2022, cervical cancer ranked as the fourth most common cancer among women worldwide, accounting for over 660,000 new cases and 350,000 deaths.1 Nearly all cases are attributable to infections with different types of high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV). HPV vaccination and cervical cancer screening are widely recognized as fundamental pillars of public health measures and play an important role in reducing the burden of cervical cancer. In Italy, HPV vaccination is recommended and provided free of charge to females aged 12–25 y, but the coverage lag far behind the established objective of 95%, ranging from 51.2% among the 2012 birth cohort to 73.5% among the 2006 birth cohort.2,3 Moreover, national cervical cancer screening guidelines recommend free Pap testing every 3 y for women aged 25–29 y who were not vaccinated by the age of 15 and HPV DNA testing every 5 years for women aged 30 and above, regardless of vaccination status, with a target adherence rate of 90%.4 Despite these policies, only 77.5% of women aged 25–65 y have undergone cervical cancer screening within the past 2 y.5

It is essential to make sure that women are adequately informed about the importance of cervical cancer screening, since an adequate level of knowledge plays a crucial role in adherence to preventive measures.6–8 To date, investigations have been undertaken to understand the level of knowledge, attitudes, and preventive practices related to cervical cancer among young females.9–11 However, to the best of our knowledge, research conducted in the Italian context on this topic remains limited.12,13 Addressing these gaps is imperative for the development of targeted educational and prevention campaigns and to promote health-seeking behavior among this target population. Consequently, the current survey aimed to provide a comprehensive understanding of knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to cervical cancer screening and to identify associated characteristics among female university students in Italy.

Materials and methods

Setting and data collection

The cross-sectional survey was conducted from July to September 2025 in the geographic area of Caserta and Naples, southern part of Italy.

Three members of the research team approached a random sample of 1350 young female of at least 18 y of age who were students in the field of study in Architecture, Dentistry, Engineering, Healthcare Professions, Health Science, Mathematics, Medicine, Physics, and Social Sciences in the classrooms when attending an exam on randomly selected days in a public University. The members explained in detail to the participants the purposes and procedure of the survey, emphasizing the voluntary and free nature of their participation, that all gathered data would be anonymous without any personal identifier, strictly confidential, and used for only the purpose of research, that they had the right to withdraw anytime they felt to do so even after consenting without needing to justify, and that no incentives were offered. At the end of the questionnaire, participants were thanked for their time and participation. The investigators distributed a self-administered paper-based questionnaire to them in an envelope, and participants were asked to return it on the spot in the same way. Informed consent for their participation was implied by returning the questionnaire.

The minimum sample size was estimated to be at least 604 students by assuming the expected standard deviation for the knowledge score to be 5 with a 95% confidence level, and a precision of 0.4.

Questionnaire development

The questionnaire was adapted and refined from previous research conducted by some of the members of the research team.14,15 Before data collection, the questionnaire was pretested among 10 women to assess its comprehensibility, relevance, and clarity. Based on feedback from participants, minor modifications were made into the final questionnaire, and their responses were not included in the final analysis.

The average time for completing the questionnaire was about 10 min. A brief description of the survey was included at the top of the questionnaire. The questionnaire was divided into six sections. Firstly, the section covered the socio-demographic and general data of the participants, including age, sex, academic grade, field of study, marital status, sentimental relationship, preexisting medical conditions, sexual orientation, personal history of cervical cancer, and family history of cervical cancer or other cancers. The second section asked about cigarette smoking (1 item), alcohol consumption (1 item), sexual history (8 items), and HPV vaccination status (1 item). To assess sexual history, participants were asked about the occurrence of sexual penetrative intercourse and oral sexual activity, the age of sexual penetrative intercourse and oral sex debut, the number of partners in the past 12 months for both sexual penetrative intercourse and oral sex, and the frequency of condom use during sexual penetrative intercourse and oral sex, with response options ranging from never to always. The third section assessed the attitudes toward the perception of HPV infection and related cancer risk, perceived benefits of HPV vaccination and cervical cancer screening, evaluated using a 10-point Likert scale, with a score of 1 indicating “strong disagreement” and a score of 10 indicating “strong agreement.” The fourth section included participants’ questions regarding cervical cancer screening-related practices, asking among those eligible if they had previously undergone any screening and, if so, the reasons for having it performed, and the last time they had undertaken it. Respondents who had never undergone any cervical cancer screening were asked about the reasons and their willingness to undergo screening. Willingness was also asked to not screening-eligible participants. The fifth section included 12 true and false statements to assess the level of knowledge regarding HPV infection and cervical cancer screening, one multiple-response question on the risk factors for cervical cancer, including five correct answer options, all of which were selectable, and three questions about the HPV vaccination and the cervical cancer screening recommendations. Each participant's answer was scored 1 for correct and 0 for incorrect, and the total score ranged between 0 and 20. In the last section, the respondents were asked about the major source(s) from which they obtained information about cervical cancer screening, and they could give one or more answers. The last item was on the need for obtaining additional information regarding cervical cancer screening.

Statistical analysis

The data collected was analyzed using the statistical software package Stata version 18. The analysis was conducted in three stages. In the first stage, descriptive statistics, such as means with standard deviations for continuous variables, and relative frequencies for categorical variables were used to present the different characteristics. In the second stage, univariate analysis was performed using t-test and chi-square test to examine the associations between the continuous or categorical outcomes and the variables. In the third stage, all variables having a value of p ≤ 0.25 in the univariate analysis were further incorporated into the multivariate linear and logistic regression models, and the values for variables’ entry and removal in the final models were, respectively, p = 0.2 and p = 0.4 throughout a stepwise selection procedure. In the multivariate linear and logistic regression models, the results were presented, respectively, as the β coefficient and the Odds Ratios (ORs) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to estimate the strength and direction of the associations. Missing values in adjustment covariates were addressed by assuming that data were missing at random, using Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) (20 imputed datasets). Observations were excluded when data regarding age, relationship status, and field of study were missing. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p-value of less than or equal to 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The four outcomes of interest were the following: knowledge score regarding HPV infection and cervical cancer screening (continuous) (Model 1); perceived concern of developing an HPV-related cancer (continuous) (Model 2); perceived utility of Pap test (continuous) (Model 3); and eligible students aged 25 y or older who have undergone cervical cancer screening (no = 0; yes = 1) (Model 4). The independent variables included in the four models were the following: age, in years (continuous), in the medical field (no = 0; yes = 1), current sentimental relationship status (stable/casual partner = 0; single = 1), having ever smoked at least 100 cigarettes (no = 0; yes = 1), having drunk alcohol during the last month (no = 0; yes = 1), age at first sexual penetrative intercourse (<18 y = 1; ≥18 y = 2; never had = 3), number of partners for sexual penetrative intercourse in the past 12 months (continuous), having received at least one dose of HPV vaccination (no = 0; yes = 1), having acquired information about cervical cancer screening from healthcare workers (HCWs) (no = 0; yes = 1), and need of additional information regarding cervical cancer screening (no = 0; yes = 1). The independent variables age at first oral sex (<18 y = 1; ≥18 y = 2; never had = 3) and number of partners for oral sex in the past 12 months (continuous) were included in Models 1 and 2. The independent variables knowledge score regarding HPV infection and cervical cancer screening (continuous), perceived concern of being infected by HPV (continuous), and perceived concern of developing an HPV-related cancer (continuous) were included in Models 3 and 4.

Results

Of the 1350 students who were randomly selected and requested to participate, a total of 1003 responded to the questionnaire. Eighteen respondents were excluded because of missing values, resulting in an overall response rate of 72.9%. Basic socio-demographic and general characteristics of the survey participants are displayed in Table 1. The average age of respondents was 23.4 y, and the field of study was evenly distributed with 44.1% identifying as from medicine. The vast majority (92%) identified themselves as heterosexual, half (54.8%) were in a stable partner relationships, only 3.4% indicated that they had a personal history of HPV-related lesion, and 2.2% claimed to have a family history of cancer cervical cancer. The sexual behavior characteristics indicated that more than three-quarters have had a sexual penetrative intercourse and oral sex with the mean age at onset of 17 y and less than 1% before the age of 12. The majority have had at least one sexual partner in the preceding year with an average of 1.2 partners. Only 37.5% and 4.5% reported always using a condom during sexual penetrative intercourse and oral sex, respectively.

Table 2 summarizes the general knowledge responses of the study participants concerning HPV infection and cervical cancer. Less than 10% of respondents lacked a precise understanding regarding HPV transmission routes. When asked about the types of cancer associated with HPV, the majority (88.1%) correctly identified cervical cancer, whereas less than two-thirds identified oropharyngeal (64.4%), and lower number of individuals linked HPV to vulvar (55.9%) or penile (50.9%) or anal (42.8%). The most recognized risk factor for HPV infection transmission was unprotected sexual intercourse by almost all of participants (91.4%), whereas smoking cigarettes was the least recognized (21.8%). Only less than two-thirds (63.7%) knew that HPV vaccination was recommended for the female, and 71.6% and 55.5% were aware that Pap test and HPV DNA test were also recommended, respectively. The average total score of general HPV and cervical cancer knowledge was 12.6 out of a possible 20 points, and only 2% of respondents reached the maximum score. The results of the multivariate linear and logistic regression analysis conducted to explore the factors that could be associated with the different outcomes of interest, based on the multiply imputed data, are shown in Table 3. Multivariate linear regression for variables associated with participants’ knowledge regarding HPV infection and cervical cancer screening showed that five factors had a statistically significant association with the knowledge score. A significant higher mean knowledge score was found among participants of older age, those in the medical field, those who had received at least one dose of the HPV vaccination, those who had oral sex compared to those who had never engaged in oral sex, and those who had acquired information about cervical cancer screening from HCWs (Model 1).

When assessing individuals’ health beliefs regarding HPV and cervical cancer screening, measured using a 10-point Likert scale, only 6%, 12.7%, and 14.5% of participants considered themselves at high risk of acquiring an HPV infection, an HPV-related cancer, and a sexually transmitted infection, respectively. The multivariate linear regression analysis demonstrated a higher concern of developing an HPV-related cancer among students not in the medical field and those who had acquired information about cervical cancer screening from HCWs (Model 2 in Table 3). Additionally, more than half believed that the HPV vaccine was very useful in preventing related infections (56.7%) and that the Pap test (56.9%) and the HPV DNA test (53.1%) were very useful to detect cervical cancer before symptoms appear. The results of the multivariate linear regression analysis showed that older participants, those with a higher knowledge score regarding HPV infection and cervical cancer screening, those who had received at least one dose of the HPV vaccination, and those who had acquired information about cervical cancer screening from HCWs were more likely to believe that the Pap test was useful (Model 3 in Table 3).

Among the screening-eligible participants belonging from 25 y, 65.1% have been screened using Pap test or HPV DNA test, and 72.7% of them had undergone either a Pap test within the past 3 years or an HPV DNA test within the past 5 years. Logistic regression analysis showed that the likelihood of eligible students undergoing cervical cancer screening was higher among older participants (OR = 1.32; 95% CI = 1.15–1.52) and in those who had their first sexual penetrative intercourse either before (OR = 6.63; 95% CI = 2.08–21.16) or from (OR = 6.46; 95% CI = 2.02–20.64) the age of 18 compared to those who had never had it (Model 4 in Table 3). The most common reasons identified by participants for undergoing cervical cancer screening were to prevent cancer (54.1%) and the recommendation of HCWs (47%), whereas the predominant reasons for not undergoing screening included lack of recommendation by HCWs (37.5%), not perceiving themselves at risk (25%), and having received the HPV vaccination (24.4%). When asked about the willingness in undergoing the screening to respondents who had not undergone it, 85.9% were willing to undergo the screening. The main reasons for this positive attitude were if recommended by an HCW (68.1%), to prevent cervical cancer (65.5%), and the HPV may evolve into disease (37.3%), whereas the main reasons for unwillingness or uncertainty were they do not feel at risk (49.4%) and lack of recommendation by an HCW (34.6%).

Only slightly more than two-thirds (68.7%) had acquired information about cervical cancer screening, with Internet or social media (70.5%) that were the predominant sources, followed by HCWs (60.8%), family or friends (22.6%), and newspapers and television (21.1%). Almost two-thirds of the participants (64.4%) were interested in receiving more information about cervical cancer screening.

Discussion

This survey provides a comprehensive and valuable analysis of the knowledge, attitudes, and practices concerning cervical cancer screening and their influencing factors among female university students in Italy, a crucial global public health issue. The results underscore substantial knowledge deficits, a favorable attitude, and suboptimal adherence to cervical cancer screening.

Knowledge is fundamental to the early detection of cervical cancer, particularly in shaping populations’ attitudes and practices toward the screening. One of the most striking findings is the non-adequate level of knowledge among the survey participants since they were unfamiliar with or had little knowledge regarding HPV, the association between various characteristics and the development of cervical cancer, and screening. Indeed, only 2% of participants reached the maximum score regarding general HPV and cervical cancer knowledge. The non-adequate knowledge is not peculiar to this survey but largely aligns with findings from prior research among different groups of individuals, indicating that, despite increased public health efforts, improvements in knowledge remain insufficient.16–21 Since knowledge plays an important role in the uptake of screening, for a program to be effective, raising the knowledge level of the target population is of paramount importance. Therefore, this underscores the need for targeted public health interventions to address and improve knowledge.

According to the findings regarding participants’ attitudes, only 6%, 12.7%, and 14.5% of the sample perceived themselves as susceptible to acquiring an HPV infection, an HPV-related cancer, and a sexually transmitted infection, respectively. Moreover, most participants did not have a positive attitude toward the preventive measures, with slightly more than half believing that the HPV vaccine was very useful for preventing related infections, and that the Pap test and HPV DNA test were very useful for an early detection of cervical cancer before symptoms appear. The low perception of their susceptibility and the low beliefs in the utility of these tests had a repercussion, although not significantly, on screening practices. Thus, this may partially explain the finding that only two-thirds of the cervical cancer screening-eligible respondents aged 25 y or older had undergone it. This suboptimal result aligns with that from a study conducted in Serbia, where 67.2% of women have done a Pap test at any time during their lives.22 However, it was considerably higher than those reported in Egypt with 1.1%,16 in Bangladesh with 4.3%,23 in Albania with 6.7%,24 in the Gulf Cooperation countries ranging from 0% in Oman to 15.9% in Bahrain,25 and in Tanzania with 25.4%.26 The present result was lower than those observed from Brazil and Romania, where respectively 74.9%27 and 78.7%28 of participants had been screened before. Consistently, recent European evidence highlights substantial variability in screening adherence across countries, ranging from 65.8% in Serbia to 95.4% in Sweden, suggesting that important differences persist due to socioeconomic, organizational, and behavioral factors.29 The unsatisfactory participation in cervical cancer screening underscored the urgent need to implement targeted educational interventions and public health strategies in order to promote and encourage participation. However, a remarkable and encouraging result is that the vast majority (82%) of the eligible nonscreened participants expressed their intention to undergo screening in the future.

From a public health perspective, as noted earlier, it is very concerning that, despite clear evidence that cervical cancer screening, primarily Pap test and HPV DNA test, is highly effective in reducing incidence and mortality, less than two-thirds of those eligible have been screened. Participants pointed out that among their most crucial reasons for not having performed a screening were a lack of recommendation by the HCW, not perceiving themselves at risk, and having received the HPV vaccination. This is of concern, as it is well-known that this vaccination does not give 100% protection against cervical cancer and it is not a replacement for organized cancer screening; both are the most powerful preventive tools to eliminate cervical cancer. Recommendations from HCWs have been shown to positively influence young adults’ engagement in initial preventive measure practices and cervical cancer screening.30,31 Their absence is a key determinant of nonadherence,32 whereas uptake is facilitated by a shared understanding between HCWs and patients regarding care intentions and the importance of screening.33 Therefore, to improve screening uptake among young females, effective interventions may be to target HCWs to increase recommendations, enhancing communication with patients, strengthening trust within healthcare relationships, and promoting risk-reduction counseling.

The results indicate that the most common channels through which the sample obtained information regarding cervical cancer screening were Internet and social media, followed by HCWs. This is not surprising, as it has been suggested that this is the leading form of communication among young people, with many of them using multiple platforms daily.34–36 However, of concern is that Internet and social media often do not disseminate accurate health-related information, and previous surveys among different groups of individuals showed that those who had used these sources were less likely to accept or to have a positive attitude toward the screening programs37,38 or toward the vaccination for themselves or for their children.39,40 Therefore, it is mandatory to promote the dissemination on the Web of reliable and scientifically evaluated information. Moreover, the widespread use of digital platforms represents a significant opportunity for intervention through the integration of digital strategies with traditional healthcare communication. For example, patient-centered digital tools, such as mobile applications that support real-time interaction, personalized reminders, and simplified appointment scheduling, may enhance screening adherence.41,42 Moreover, less than two-thirds of respondents stated that they had received information by HCWs. HCWs, mainly gynecologists and public health professionals, play an important role in promoting screening, and they may help patients doubting/refusing to accept it. However, gaps in recommendations have been observed in this survey, and it is worrisome that only less than half of the participants received it. This is evidenced also by the already cited finding that a lack of recommendation being the most frequently reported reason for not having performed the screening among eligible participants. Previous investigations have shown that respondents who had received information from HCWs had higher knowledge about the screening and management than those who did not.43,44 To address this issue, a well-structured health education program tailored specifically for young women should be developed, and recommendations from a HCW are necessary to enhance knowledge, attitudes, and practice toward screening. Therefore, it is urgently necessary to optimize patient and HCW communication about this important aspect of healthcare for women, in order to facilitate their access to health services and make more informed health decisions.

Understanding the factors contributing to the different outcomes of interest with multivariate linear and logistic regression analysis provides insights for tailored interventions. There were clear and significant associations. The association with age may be explained by the fact that older women have access to more preventive healthcare throughout their lives and are more familiar with the importance of early detection. Also, the importance of higher knowledge among medical students compared to others cannot be overstated, as they will play a critical role in educating the public and promoting public health and preventive measures, and this may be due to their engagement in the health process. Previous studies confirmed the association between these variables and the knowledge regarding HPV and related preventive measures.45,46

This survey makes several innovative contributions to the existing literature. First, it provides updated evidence on knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding cervical cancer screening among university students in Italy, a population that remains underexplored. Second, through multivariate analyses, it offers a more comprehensive understanding of factors associated with screening uptake. Third, the findings highlight inadequate knowledge, limited confidence in the utility of preventive measure, and suboptimal adherence to cervical cancer screening, underlining the need for targeted public health interventions in this population. Finally, gaps in HCWs’ recommendations and the prominent use of Internet as an information source provide valuable insights for designing more effective and tailored prevention strategies.

There are some potential methodological limitations of the current survey that should be considered when interpreting the findings. Firstly, the cross-sectional nature of the design precluded the ability to draw conclusions about the causal relationship between different characteristics and outcomes of interest. Secondly, this was a single-site survey; thus, the sample may not reflect the entire picture of the university students’ population of Italy and the findings may have limited generalizability. Thirdly, the data were collected through a self-reported questionnaire, and for certain aspects, they may have been influenced by a recall bias, since it may be difficult for participants to accurately remember mainly for the questions about their sexual experiences, history of HPV vaccination and past cervical cancer screening that were not verified by medical record review. Fourthly, the survey is also susceptible to social desirability bias, and respondents may be reluctant to provide responses mainly regarding sexual experiences. To minimize such bias, the survey was designed to provide data in anonymity. Despite these limitations, the findings may be crucial as an important health topic for the successful implementation of interventions to increase the level of knowledge and the cervical cancer screening uptake.

In conclusion, this survey showed that respondents had a low level of knowledge, positive attitudes, and suboptimal practices toward cervical cancer screening programs. The findings underscore that there is an immediate need for the implementation by the government or medical institutions of targeted public health interventions and counseling strategies about cervical cancer for enhancing women’s awareness and knowledge and their participation in screening programs.
