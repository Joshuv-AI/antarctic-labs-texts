Iodine concentrations in human milk after maternal high therapeutic potassium iodide dosing during breastfeeding: a contribution from the ConcePTION project
Authors: Martje Van Neste, Kaytlin Krutsch, Michael Ceulemans, Heidi Demaegdt, Karlien Cheyns, Nina Nauwelaerts, Pieter Annaert, Anne Smits, Karel Allegaert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Iodine is an essential element of human milk. However, high therapeutic maternal doses of potassium iodide (KI) during breastfeeding and its effect on the infant have been insufficiently studied. Therefore, human milk samples were collected within the UmbrelLACT study to determine human milk iodine concentration (HMIC) and estimate infant exposure during and after high maternal KI intake. One mother used KI 3 × 50 mg/day as preparation for thyroidectomy at 5 months postpartum. Human milk samples were collected opportunistically during and within 2 weeks after KI discontinuation. Furthermore, milk samples were collected over 24-h periods at 3 weeks post-discontinuation and monthly from 1 month up to 5 months after discontinuation of KI intake. Breastfeeding was temporarily interrupted during maternal treatment, with partial resumption of breastfeeding 1 week after KI discontinuation, complemented with infant formula upon the participant’s decision. HMIC were analysed using inductively coupled plasma tandem mass spectrometry (ICP-MS/MS) after basic extraction with tetra-methyl ammonium hydroxide (TMAH) and infant exposure was estimated. Median HMIC were 70.8 (46.6–80.8) µg/mL during and 0.064 (0.016–0.227) µg/mL from day six after maternal treatment stop onwards. After discontinuation of KI intake, HMIC decreased quickly and remained within reference range (0.1–0.2 µg/mL). Using the infant requirement of 15 µg/kg/day, relative infant recommended dose (RIDrecommended) during and after KI intake was 70805% and 98%, respectively. The infant’s thyroid values were within normal ranges at 3 months after discontinuation of maternal treatment and no adverse events were reported for the infant. Post-discontinuation HMIC are in line with mean concentrations in Nordic countries (68–90 ng/mL). High therapeutic doses of KI should not be combined with breastfeeding, but based on these results (exclusive) breastfeeding could be resumed from 3 weeks after finalisation of maternal treatment. Further research is warranted to determine exposure and safety for breastfed infants. Registration UmbrelLACT study: NCT06042803 (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06042803?cond=umbrellact&rank=1).

Introduction

Iodine is a trace element needed to produce thyroid hormones that regulate and promote growth, metabolism and cognitive development. Iodine in foods are almost fully absorbed (>90%) as inorganic iodide and then actively transported to the thyroid. 1 Up to 80% of total body iodine is concentrated in the thyroid, as 10% of iodine is cleared from circulation with the possibility of an increase up to 80% in clearance in case of chronic iodine deficiency. Iodine is mainly excreted by renal clearance (>90%) and in small amounts via faeces and sweat. 1 As adequate iodine levels are essential, especially for women of childbearing ages and infants, salt iodisation programmes were initiated to ensure adequate iodine nutrition.1–3

The recommended iodine intake for adults was established at 150 µg/day, but iodine requirements vary throughout life. For instance, pregnant and breastfeeding women in Nordic countries are advised to increase iodine intake to 175 and 200 µg/day, respectively, as they are the iodine source for fetus and infant. 1 The WHO recommends an iodine intake of 250 µg/day for these populations. 2 Infants are more sensitive to iodine deficiency-related hypothyroidism, as they require higher iodine intake per body weight and have limited intrathyroidal iodine storage. 4 Therefore, a daily intake of 15 µg/kg was suggested for infants, 5 with other recommended intakes of 70 µg/day, 72 µg/day, 90 µg/day or 110–130 µg/day.1,2,6 Upper limits of infant iodine intake are suggested as 100 µg/kg/day, as high therapeutic doses of iodine can be used in infants to suppress thyroid hormone release.7,8

Human milk iodine concentration (HMIC) is facilitated by increased sodium/iodide symporter expression in the blood–milk barrier, augmenting HMIC to 20–50 times of plasma concentration.1,4,9 It is estimated that almost 50% of maternal iodine intake in healthy lactating women is transported to human milk, with a peak within 6 h after iodine ingestion. 1 However, HMIC is reported to fluctuate between feeding moments with concentrations of 3.1–334 ng/mL over more than 100 samples, reflecting variations in iodine exposure.9,10 Therefore, no consensus has been reached regarding the optimal HMIC. 4 A concentration of approximately 150 ng/mL was described in countries with a salt iodisation programme. 1 Consequently, a range of 100 to 200 ng/mL was suggested to ensure a positive iodine balance in infants,1,4,9 although an even wider range (60–465 ng/mL) could be proposed as well. 11 In case of inadequate maternal iodine intake, compensatory mechanisms have been described, but these might not be sufficient to reach iodine requirements in an exclusively breastfed infant. 1

The usage of potassium iodide (KI) before thyroidectomy controls hyperthyroidism by decreasing thyroid hormones release (Wolff–Chaikoff effect) to reduce the risk of intra- or post-operative thyroid storm.12,13 Since anti-thyroid medicines can avoid this thyroid storm, the main effect of KI is currently improving solidness and visualisation of the thyroid by reducing its vascularity. 12 Consequently, the benefit of KI before thyroidectomy for Graves’ disease is under discussion. 12

A thyroidectomy affects the biokinetics of iodine in the body, since iodine is no longer stored in the thyroid and urinary excretion has increased. 14 Additionally, iodine might accumulate more in some organs, such as lungs and salivary glands, and less in others, for example, spleen and bladder wall. Therefore, iodine distribution after thyroidectomy is considered to be non-uniform. 15 Mechanistically, it is also reasonable to assume that thyroidectomy affects iodine exposure in human milk, since the primary storage compartment has been removed.

Only two articles described cases of maternal high-dose KI intake during lactation.16,17 One of these articles hereby used an insensitive and imprecise bio-analytical method. 17 Hamada et al. reported elevated iodine levels in human milk during KI treatment of 10–100 mg/day (831–72 000 ng/mL) for Graves disease and most breastfed infants (n = 25/26) had normal thyroid function. 16 In a larger follow-up study, 12 infants (n = 12/100) were reported with subclinical hypothyroidism which resolved spontaneously (n = 7) or after discontinuation of maternal pharmacotherapy or breastfeeding (n = 3). 18

This case report is a result of the UmbrelLACT study and part of the Innovative Medicine Initiative (IMI) ConcePTION project and aimed to determine HMIC and its decline over time after high maternal KI intake and discontinuation. Additionally, infant exposure was estimated longitudinally. This paper is constructed following the CARE guidelines (Supplemental File). 19

Case description: Patient information and clinical setting

This case report describes a breastfeeding participant requiring high therapeutic dose of KI (50 mg 3×/day) for two weeks before thyroidectomy at 5 months postpartum.

The participant is a 28-year-old white mother living in Finland with a BMI of 30.3 kg/m2 and 32.0 kg/m2 (height: 176 cm, weight: 94 kg and 99 kg) at the first and last sampling day, respectively. No comorbidities were reported except for elevated liver function tests due to gallstones, and no specific diet was followed. The participant did not use recreational drugs but occasionally consumed alcohol. No smoking was reported during the first sampling day, and infrequent smoking was reported during the following months ranging from 10 to 20 cigarettes per month.

Relevant to iodide exposure, the national nutrition council of Finland recommends, in accordance to the WHO, implementation of iodised salt in general community, in mass catering and in the food industry to restore iodine intake. 3 However, despite an excellent iodine status from the 1960’s until the early 2000’s, Finland was scored as having an insufficient iodine intake among adults in 2017.20,21

The infant is a healthy female. She is the third breastfed child in the family and restarted receiving breastfeeding within 1 week after KI discontinuation, complemented with formula and solid foods. Before the sampling day, the infant required hospitalisation due to a urinary tract infection combined with COVID infection. During the study period, the infant grew well from approximately 69 cm and 8.0 kg at 6 months to approximately 76 cm and 8.7 kg at 10 months. The thyroid values were assessed as part of the infant’s specific clinical follow-up at 3 months after finalisation of maternal treatment.

This case report is part of the UmbrelLACT study (NCT06042803) for which ethics approval was obtained (S67204).

Timelines

The mother used KI for 2 weeks before planned thyroidectomy. As part of this study, the participant collected human milk samples opportunistically during and within 2 weeks after maternal KI treatment. Additionally, human milk samples were collected systematically over a 24-h period 3 weeks after discontinuation and every month from 1 month up to 5 months after finalisation of maternal pharmacotherapy (Figure 1).

Therapeutic interventions

The patient used a compounded preparation of KI 50 mg/ml oral 1 ml 3×/day before thyroidectomy. After thyroidectomy, the participant took thyroxin (levothyroxine, tablet, 100 µg, 1×/day, Orion Pharma), etalpha (alfacalcidol, capsule, 0.5 µg, 2×/day), calcichew-D3 forte (calcium/colecalciferol, chewable tablet, 500 mg/10 µg, 1×/day, Takeda), calcichew 500 (calcium, chewable tablet, 500 mg, 1×/day, Takeda), emgesan (magnesium hydroxide, tablet, 250 mg, 1×/day). From the second sampling day onwards, loratadin ratiopharm (loratadine, 10 mg, 1×/day, Ratiopharm) was used. Etalpha, calcichew 500 mg and emgesan were discontinued during follow-up and calcichew D3 forte was replaced by calcichew D3 extra (calcium/colecalciferol, chewable tablet, 500 mg/20 µg, 1×/day, Takeda). Fluzinc (zinc acetate, tablet, 10 mg, Apteq) were used occasionally during the third sampling day, as was acetaminophen/caffeine (1g) and ibuprofen (600 mg) during the fifth sampling day.

Follow-up and outcomes

Maternal and infant health

Clinical data and information on medicine use of the participant and infant were collected via self-reporting questionnaires. No adverse events were reported for the mother and the infant. The infant received temporarily vitamin D supplementation, and their thyroid values (free T4 19.1 pmol/L; TSH 1,7 mU/L) were within expected range at 3 months after discontinuation of maternal pharmacotherapy. 22

Human milk samples

Human milk samples were collected at the participant’s home during and after maternal treatment (Table 1). During each expression session, milk was collected using an electrical breast pump and the total volume was noted. Afterwards, a sample (±10 mL) was stored in the refrigerator (4°C) and subsequently in the freezer (−24°C).

Samples were transported to KU Leuven, Belgium, on dry ice and stored at −80°C. Human milk samples (0.5 g) were extracted in duplicate using 5 ml TMAH (25%) and 1 ml double-distilled water at 90°C for 3 h in a heating block (Digiprep, SCP Science). Following extraction, samples were diluted with 19 ml water, centrifuged at 10,000 g for 20 min, and supernatants were filtered through 0.45 μm filters (PES, Agilent). Filtered extracts (1 ml) were diluted to a final volume of 10 ml, containing 500 μg/L Te (internal standard), 0.5% TMAH, and 3% isopropanol. Total iodine concentrations were determined by ICP-MS/MS (Agilent 8800, USA) on-mass (m/z 127→127) in helium collision mode with Te internal standard (m/z 125→125). Calibration standards (1–500 μg/L) were prepared from iodine stock solution with matrix-matched TMAH, isopropanol, and Te concentrations. Cross-calibration was performed to align pulse and analogue detector responses using 100 and 500 μg/L standards. The analytical method achieved a limit of detection (LOD) of 14 ng/mL and a lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) of 46 ng/mL. Values below LLOQ were used as estimated and results are reported as median (range) throughout this article.

Human milk pharmacokinetics

A total of 36 human milk samples were collected, 6 during iodide intake and 28 during the 6 sampling days after intake was stopped. The median concentration during pharmacotherapy was 70 805 (46560–80828) ng/mL and declined to 64.3 (15.8–227.0) ng/mL within 2 weeks after pharmacotherapy and thyroidectomy (Figure 2). The area-under-the-curve (AUC) was calculated for the sampling days, resulting in a median milk AUC0–24h of 875.5 (397.3–2397.8) ng*h/mL. The median average steady-state concentration Cav,ss based on these AUC0–24h was 64.2 (26.5–173.4) ng/mL.

Estimated infant exposure

The daily infant dosage (DID) were calculated using equation (1), with a human milk intake of 150 mL/kg/day. The concentration of the opportunistically collected samples (1/day) during pharmacotherapy was used and the milk Cav,ss from the sampling days:

(1) DID(μg/kg/day)=AverageSteady−StateMilkConcentration(μg/kg/day)×InfantMilkIntake(μg/kg/day)

This resulted in a median DID of 10620 (6984-12124) µg/kg/day during pharmacotherapy and 14.7 (4.0–30.6) µg/kg/day after pharmacotherapy discontinuation.

The relative infant recommended dose (RIDrecommended) was determined by comparing the DID to the minimal recommended iodine intake for a full-term infant (15 µg/kg/day) (equation (2)). 5

(2) RIDrecommended(%)=DID(μg/kg/day)RecommendedInfantDosage(μg/kg/day)×100

The RIDrecommended ranged from 46560% to 80828% during pharmacotherapy and from 26.5% to 204.0% after pharmacotherapy. Compared to the suggested upper limit of infant iodine intake (100 µg/kg/d), 8 RIDrecommended during pharmacotherapy was at least 6984% and after maternal pharmacotherapy at most 31%.

Lastly, iodine solution in infants can be used to (fully) suppress thyroid hormone synthesis and release during neonatal thyrotoxicosis (15 mg/day) or after a nuclear emergency (32.5 mg/day). 23 These doses were compared to the DID for an exclusively breastfed infant to calculate the relative infant therapeutic dose (RIDtherapeutic; equation (3)). The RIDtherapeutic was 542% (356–618%) and 250% (164–285%) during pharmacotherapy for 15 mg/day and 32.5 mg/day, respectively. After KI treatment, the RIDtherapeutic resulted in 0.13% (0.05–1.56%) in case of neonatal thyrotoxicosis and 0.06% (0.02–0.72%) in case of nuclear emergency (Figure 3):

(3) RIDtherapeutic(%)=DID(μg/kg/day)DailyTherapeuticInfantDosage(μg/kg/day)×100

To estimate the DID specifically for this case, the HMIC and reported milk volumes of the six sampling days were used (equation (4)), with n the number of milk expressions:

(4) DID(μg/kg/day)=∑i=1n(MilkConcentrationi(μg/L)×*MilkVolumei(L)Infantweight(kg))

This resulted in a median DID of 1.33 (0.86–7.32) µg/kg/day. However, this value is dependent on the human milk intake volume of the breastfed infant. Using these DIDs, the median RIDrecommended was 8.8%, ranging from 5.7% to 48.8%.

Discussion

This case report is the first to describe HMICs longitudinally during and after high maternal KI intake (150 mg/day for 14 days) before thyroidectomy.

The HMICs during KI treatment in this case (46560–80828 ng/mL) are higher compared to breastfeeding women receiving KI for the chronic management of Graves’ disease (831–72000 ng/mL for 10–100 mg/day KI intake). 16 When only looking at the breastfeeding women with the highest dosages (100 mg/day; n = 3) in their study, the HMICs (24800 to 72000 ng/mL) are more similar to our results. 16 After discontinuation of maternal KI pharmacotherapy, the HMICs declined swiftly in our case (15.8–227.0 ng/mL) and remained low over 24 h from 3 weeks after maternal treatment onwards.

A specific range of HMIC to fulfil the infant’s requirements has not yet been determined, but a HMIC of 100 to 200 ng/mL was suggested. 1 As iodine concentrations are highly variable between populations and countries, Nordic studies were used to compare the levels of iodine during breastfeeding.1,4 The median HMIC from discontinuation of KI treatment onwards (64.3 ng/mL) is comparable to the range of median concentrations in Nordic studies (68–90 ng/mL), even though they did not differentiate between women taking iodine supplements or not.24–28

As iodine is an essential element of human milk, it is expected to have a certain level in human milk. Taking the minimal infant iodine requirements into account, the median iodine intake via human milk during maternal pharmacotherapy was > 450 times higher than the iodine requirement of infants and > 70 times higher than the suggested upper limit of infant iodine intake.5,8 Compared to the therapeutic doses used to suppress thyroid hormone release for neonatal thyrotoxicosis or nuclear emergency, the median iodine intake through human milk during maternal KI treatment was estimated, respectively, as more than five and two times higher. After completing maternal KI pharmacotherapy, the median iodine intake of the breastfed infant was resembling the recommended infant iodine intake (median RIDrecommended 98%). Consequently, the milk concentrations after completing KI treatment supply only a fraction of the iodine doses used to suppress thyroid hormone release in infants (0.06–0.13%).

During a nuclear emergency, lactating women are advised to use a single 130 mg dose of KI. 29 These results imply that HMICs during this intake might suppress thyroid hormone release in the breastfed infants and breastfeeding should consequently be interrupted. It is suspected that breastfeeding can soon be restarted, which is in line with the recommendations of Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). 30 As iodine transfer to human milk is too slow to also prevent iodine uptake in the infant’s thyroid in time of a nuclear emergency, it is still essential that the infant takes their own iodine dose (32.5 mg).

The infant in this case did not receive human milk during maternal pharmacotherapy but partial breastfeeding was restarted within a week after KI discontinuation. Within its clinical care, the infant was monitored, and thyroid values were reported to remain within normal values at 3 months after maternal KI cessation. 22 Previous cases did describe almost no adverse events in breastfed infants during maternal KI doses of 10–100 mg/day; however, the estimated RIDrecommended and RIDtherapeutic deduced from the previous paper were lower than in our case. 16

The HMICs after maternal treatment showed variation between sampling days as well as within sampling day, which is in line with results from literature. 9 As the main source of iodine is foods such as fish, eggs, cow’s milk and products containing iodised salt, these variations in HMICs might be augmented because of dietary intake and time since intake.1,9 Additionally, the main maternal storage of iodine (i.e. thyroid) was removed during surgery, resulting in an increased turnover rate of iodine and a higher fluctuation of iodine concentrations in blood, and subsequently human milk, after iodine intake.14,15 As iodine intake was not recorded in this case report, the reason for the HMIC fluctuations can not be determined.

During breastfeeding, the expression of sodium/iodide co-transporters in the blood-milk barrier are increased, implying a possible reduction of maternal thyroid exposure to the high KI dosage and its effects.1,4 Therefore, it should be further studied if the effect of high KI doses during breastfeeding is less significant in the case of a thyroidectomy. Consequently, an even higher KI dosage might be required to provide adequate maternal exposure to KI and to improve solidness and visualisation of the thyroid.

Limitations of this case report should hereby be considered. A limitation of this case study is the inherently uncertainty of sample handling, since the human milk samples were collected by the participant herself at home according to verbal and written instructions. Second, several samples were collected opportunistically (1/day). Therefore, the concentration-time profile in human milk could not be assessed during and within 2 weeks after maternal KI intake. Furthermore, logistics did not allow the collection of blood samples to compare to the human milk samples and assess milk-to-plasma ratio. Additionally, 11 samples were under the LLOQ implying there is a greater margin of error for these concentrations. Besides, HMICs varied throughout the day. As dietary intake affects iodine concentrations, dietary assessment of iodine intake could further clarify fluctuations in HMICs. 1 Unfortunately, these were not documented during the sampling days. Furthermore, these results might vary between regions that have different iodine intakes or between countries depending on its implementation of the salt iodisation programme. 3 Last, the infant was 5 months of age at the start of maternal KI treatment and following breastfeeding resumption, received mixed feeding together with complementary foods. Therefore, infant exposure to iodine could not be assessed during the first weeks and months postpartum, with higher human milk intake volume. 31

This article describes longitudinal data after high maternal KI intake in a single patient, demonstrating the steep decline of HMICs within 2 weeks after discontinuation of pharmacotherapy. These data suggest that high dosages of KI before thyroidectomy are not compatible with breastfeeding as concentrations are expected to suppress infant thyroid hormone release. However, (exclusive) breastfeeding could be restarted 3 weeks after maternal KI discontinuation. In this article, the infant did not receive human milk during maternal pharmacotherapy, which means that the general health of the infant during high dosage of maternal KI intake could not be assessed.

Further research is required to confirm these results in various breastfeeding women and the effect on the infant’s health. As high doses of KI are necessary during nuclear emergencies, these results could help shaping emergency plans. Furthermore, physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modelling and simulations could predict iodine exposure during lactation in different contexts, as well with various dietary intake regimens of iodine.

In summary, HMICs during high dosages of maternal KI intake are higher than concentrations to inhibit thyroid hormone release. Once maternal pharmacotherapy is stopped, iodine levels fall swiftly and remain low over a period of 5 months showing average concentrations of infants’ requirement or below. The effect of high dosages KI could not be assessed as the infant was not breastfed during maternal pharmacotherapy, and no dietary data were collected. However, it appears that exclusive breastfeeding could be resumed 3 weeks after discontinuation of high maternal KI usage.
