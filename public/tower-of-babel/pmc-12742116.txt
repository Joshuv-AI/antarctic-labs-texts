Time-dependent predictive performance of inflammatory markers for 30-day all-cause mortality in patients with suspected infection in a Japanese emergency department: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Moeko Omiya, Toshihiko Takada, Tetsuhiro Yano, Kotaro Fujii, Ryuto Fujiishi, Shunichi Fukuhara
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate the time-dependent predictive performance of inflammatory markers for 30-day all-cause mortality in patients with suspected infection.DesignRetrospective cohort study.SettingThe ambulatory care division of the department of general medicine or the emergency department of a single acute care hospital in Japan, from April 2015 to March 2017.ParticipantsThe participants were 977 consecutive adult patients with suspected infection defined as those who underwent at least two sets of blood culture.Primary outcome measuresThe primary outcome, ascertained from electronic medical records, was 30-day all-cause mortality. The predictive performance of three inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein (CRP), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio and red cell distribution width (RDW)) was assessed across four time intervals from symptom onset to hospital presentation: ≤6, >6 to ≤24, >24 to ≤72, and >72 hours.ResultsTime from symptom onset to hospital presentation influenced the predictive performance of inflammatory markers for 30-day all-cause mortality, and CRP ≥15 mg/dL was the most useful for identifying patients at higher risk of mortality within 6 hours (positive likelihood ratio (LR+) 7.7); however, this association weakened over time (LR+ 1.3 at >72 hours). Conversely, RDW ≥16% became more informative later in the course (LR+ 4.1 at >72 hours). For identifying patients at lower risk of 30-day all-cause mortality, CRP <5 mg/dL showed some utility after 6 hours (negative likelihood ratio (LR–) 0.5–0.7), while RDW <14% was more predictive after 72 hours (LR– 0.3).ConclusionThese findings suggest the potential utility of CRP for ruling in high-risk patients within 6 hours from symptom onset, and of RDW for both ruling in and ruling out patients after 72 hours, thereby suggesting that time-dependent variations are important when interpreting inflammatory markers in emergency care settings. However, caution is needed when interpreting these findings because of the retrospective design of this study and potential selection bias.

Introduction

Severe infections, particularly sepsis, are conditions associated with high morbidity and mortality.1 Timely and appropriate therapeutic interventions are crucial for improving their prognoses, as delays in effective antimicrobial administration in affected patients have been shown to decrease survival by 7.6% per hour.2 Consequently, early and accurate prognostic assessment is essential when patients present with signs and symptoms of infection in emergency care settings.

In clinical practice, several types of inflammatory markers (eg, C-reactive protein (CRP), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLCR) and red cell distribution width (RDW)) are used to predict the severity of infection and patient prognosis.316 These markers change over the course of infectious diseases. For instance, CRP levels typically begin to rise 4–6 hours after inflammatory stimulation, double approximately every 8 hours, and usually peak between 36 and 50 hours.17 Thus, considering the ‘time course’ of infection is crucial when using these markers to evaluate the severity of infection. Previous studies have introduced the concept of estimated CRP velocity, which is the ratio between the CRP levels on hours elapsed from symptom onset to hospital presentation. However, its usefulness has been evaluated only for distinguishing bacterial from non-bacterial infections, not for predicting the prognosis of patients with infectious disease.18 19

Given this background, the present study aimed to evaluate the characteristics associated with the time-dependent predictive performance of inflammatory markers for 30-day all-cause mortality, accounting for the time between symptom onset and hospital presentation among patients suspected of infection in emergency care settings.

Methods

This retrospective observational study was conducted at an acute care hospital in Japan (471-bed capacity) from April 2015 to March 2017. Approval was granted by the Shirakawa Kosei General Hospital institutional review board (HAKURIN 17-0003). This study adhered to the Standards for Reporting of Diagnostic Accuracy (STARD) 2015 guidelines for reporting diagnostic accuracy studies.20 The authors (TY, KF and RF) collected data from routine clinical visits documented in electronic medical records, which were reviewed by another author (TT). Any disagreements were resolved by discussion among the authors.

Patients

We included consecutive adult patients (≥18 years) presenting to either the ambulatory care division or emergency department of our hospital from April 2015 to March 2017 in whom the treating physician suspected acute infection. For this study, ‘suspicion of acute infection’ was operationally defined as the decision by physicians to order at least two sets of blood culture. This approach has been used in previous studies as a surrogate marker for clinical suspicion of infection.2124 In addition to patients presenting to the emergency department, those attending the ambulatory care division were also included because walk-in patients with acute illness in our hospital are seen in ambulatory care during the daytime. The exclusion criteria were as follows: any missing inflammatory marker values (CRP, NLCR and RDW) or prognosis, duration of illness unknown or longer than 1 week (because patients suspected of acute infection were the targeted population), and a past history of blood disorders (some of the inflammatory markers used as candidate predictors are indices of blood cells and therefore not reliable in such patients).

Candidate predictors

In this study, as index tests, we extracted three inflammatory markers that have been reported to be predictors of the prognosis of patients with infectious conditions: CRP, NLCR and RDW. These markers were measured from blood samples taken at the time of hospital presentation as part of the routine evaluation.

CRP is a non-specific acute phase protein that increases during infection or major tissue damage and is used as a diagnostic tool to assess the presence and severity of infection.311 NLCR is a marker of systemic inflammation calculated as the absolute neutrophil count divided by the absolute lymphocyte count. It reflects the balance between neutrophils and lymphocytes in the blood and is a valuable marker for detecting infection.12 13 RDW is a measure of the variation in size of red blood cells and can reflect underlying inflammatory conditions, with elevated levels associated with increased severity and mortality in infectious disease.14 15

CRP levels were measured using an automated analyser (7700; Hitachi High-Technologies, Tokyo, Japan). Complete blood counts with differential and RDW were measured using an automated haematology system (XN-3100; Sysmex, Hyogo, Japan). We explicitly did not study the value of inflammatory markers that were not routinely measured in our hospital, such as procalcitonin and other new markers. For CRP, we set cut-offs of 5, 10 and 15 mg/dL. A common threshold for inflammation is 5 mg/dL,37 while 10 mg/dL is associated with increased severity and mortality in infected patients.8 9 The 15 mg/dL cut-off was added to identify more severe inflammation.10 11 NLCR cut-offs were set at 5, 10 and 15. These values span the range reported in various studies, with NLCR >5 often associated with an increased risk of severe infection.12 13 The cut-off for RDW was determined in accordance with previous studies: 14%, 15% and 16%.14 15

Time before presentation assessment

The time before presentation between symptom onset and hospital presentation was retrospectively extracted from clinical records and calculated based on the patient’s self-reported time of initial symptom onset and the time of hospital presentation. If patients could not report the time of initial symptom onset, information from accompanying individuals such as family members was also referred to in the clinical records. The authors defined ‘symptom onset’ as the initial occurrence of symptoms suggestive of infection, based on available clinical documentation. The time before presentation was categorised as follows: ≤6, >6 to ≤24, >24 to ≤72, and >72 hours. These categories were determined based on the known kinetics of CRP17 and the quartiles of the observed duration in our data set.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was 30-day all-cause mortality ascertained from electronic medical records.25 For patients who were not admitted, we reviewed outpatient follow-up records through day 30 to ascertain their vital status.

Statistical analysis

We also assessed the proportion of missing data. As the proportion of missing data for at least one predictor was 3.74%, which was <20% across all candidate variables, we proceeded with complete case analysis.26

To compare patient characteristics between survivors and non-survivors, continuous variables were analysed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were analysed using the χ² test.

To assess the predictive performance of inflammatory markers, we calculated the following measures in each time before presentation category: positive and negative likelihood ratio (LR+ and LR–, respectively), sensitivity (Sn) and specificity (Sp). We also calculated the 95% CI of each measure. We did not conduct multivariable analyses, as the aim of this study was to explore the unadjusted, standalone predictive performance of each marker, reflecting their use in clinical practice. No covariates were included in the models.

All statistical analyses were performed using R statistical software (version 4.3.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, www.R-project.org).27

Patient and public involvement

This study did not involve patients or the public in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of the research. This study was a retrospective analysis of collected clinical data. The nature of this retrospective study did not allow for direct patient or public involvement in the research process.

Results

Patient characteristics

Of the 1256 potentially eligible patients, we excluded 45 with unknown illness duration, 177 with illness duration longer than 1 week and 19 with a history of blood disorders. In addition, 36 patients were excluded because of missing values for any inflammatory marker (CRP, NLCR or RDW) and 2 patients because of missing data for prognosis, leaving a total of 977 patients included in the final analysis (figure 1). The basic characteristics of these 977 patients are shown in table 1. Among the patients, 64 (6.6%) died within 30 days. These 64 deaths included 51 from among the 565 admitted patients and 13 from among the 412 non-admitted patients. The median age of the non-survivors was significantly higher than that of the survivors (86.5 years (IQR 79–90) vs. 80 years (IQR 65–87), p<0.001). Non-survivors had significantly higher median CRP levels (8.2 mg/dL (IQR 2.5–15.9) vs 5.4 mg/dL (IQR 1.3–13), p=0.048), NLCR (13.0 (IQR 4.9–21.5) vs 8.6 (IQR 4.6–15.7), p=0.036) and RDW (14.4% (IQR 13.2–16.3) vs 13.4% (IQR 12.8–14.4), p<0.001) than the survivors, respectively.

Regarding the probability of higher 30-day all-cause mortality, reflected by LR+, CRP demonstrated the most notable changes (figure 2). At the cut-off of 15 mg/dL, CRP showed the strongest association with mortality (LR+ 7.7, 95% CI 3.5 to 16.9) in patients who presented within 6 hours from symptom onset. However, the performance decreased over time (LR+ 1.3, 95% CI 1.1 to 1.5 at >72 hours). For lower thresholds, the LR+ at the 10 mg/dL cut-off showed a similar tendency: 3.0 (95% CI 2.2 to 4.2) within 6 hours decreased to 1.2 (95% CI 1.1 to 1.3) at >72 hours. On the other hand, predictive performance at 5 mg/dL cut-off remained relatively stable (LR+ 1.2–1.5). Similar to the CRP findings, the LR+ of NLCR and RDW increased with higher thresholds. However, the LR+ of these markers increased over time from symptom onset to hospital presentation. Notably, for RDW ≥16% at >72 hours, the LR+ reached 4.1 (95% CI 3.1 to 5.4).

Regarding the probability of lower 30-day all-cause mortality, reflected by LR–, CRP at lower thresholds showed a time-dependent pattern, demonstrating a trend of decreasing after 6 hours (figure 3). For instance, with a cut-off of 5 mg/dL, the LR– was 0.9 (95% CI 0.8 to 0.9) for ≤6 hours, 0.5 (95% CI 0.3 to 0.8) for >6 to ≤24 hours, 0.7 (95% CI 0.5 to 1.0) for >24 to ≤72 hours, and 0.5 (95% CI 0.2 to 1.1) for >72 hours. Among the other markers, RDW with a cut-off of 14% showed a time-dependent pattern, with the LR– progressively decreasing to its lowest value (0.3, 95% CI 0.2 to 0.5).

Discussion

Summary of results

This study evaluated time-dependent variations in the predictive performance of inflammatory markers (CRP, NLCR and RDW) for 30-day all-cause mortality in patients suspected of infection. Regarding the probability of higher 30-day all-cause mortality, CRP with a high cut-off value of 15 mg/dL appeared to show the highest utility within 6 hours from symptom onset, with an LR+ of 7.7 (95% CI 3.5 to 16.9), but its utility decreased after 6 hours. However, the associations with high mortality for NLCR and RDW strengthened over time, in contrast to the pattern observed for CRP. Among these inflammatory markers, RDW ≥16% after 72 hours from symptom onset showed potential utility to detect high-risk patients (LR+ 4.1, 95% CI 3.1 to 5.4).

Regarding the probability of lower 30-day all-cause mortality, CRP <5 mg/dL showed potential utility after 6 hours from symptom onset, with the LR– ranging from 0.5 to 0.7. After 72 hours, RDW <14% also showed potential for indicating lower risk (0.3, 95% CI 0.2 to 0.5).

Comparison with existing studies

Few studies have evaluated the diagnostic value of inflammatory markers while incorporating the concept of time, especially at the hour level.7912 It has been reported that the concept of estimated CRP velocity, the ratio between CRP levels on hours elapsed from symptom onset to hospital presentation, is useful in differentiating bacterial from non-bacterial infections.18 19 Our findings align with these previous findings, as bacterial infections generally pose a higher risk of mortality than most viral infections.28 29 Our study extends this concept by applying a time-dependent approach to prognostic assessments in patients suspected of infection, not only for CRP, but also for other inflammatory markers such as NLCR and RDW.

Clinical implications

All three markers showed time-dependent variation in predictive performance across the time from presentation categories. Clinicians may need to consider these time-dependent variations when interpreting these markers for risk stratification in emergency care settings. When a high cut-off value was applied, CRP appeared particularly useful for identifying high-risk patients, especially within the first 6 hours from symptom onset. An elevated CRP level within the first 6 hours from symptom onset may reflect a rapid and intense inflammatory response to severe infection. However, it could also indicate that there was an insidious phase of infection during which patients were asymptomatic, suggesting that the infectious process began before symptom onset. This latter scenario is more likely to occur in populations such as older adults and immunocompromised patients.30 31 In these cases, a high CRP level at this early presentation may serve as a surrogate marker for host factors associated with a poor prognosis and could therefore be useful for identifying high-risk patients in both scenarios.

Although low CRP is considered useful for ruling out high-risk patients in clinical settings, the present study indicated that CRP <5 mg/dL may provide little information to rule out within 6 hours from symptom onset, but aid ruling out after 6 hours. At >72 hours, RDW may be useful for both ruling in and ruling out high-risk patients.

Strengths and limitations

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study conducted to evaluate how the prognostic utility of inflammatory markers such as CRP, NLCR and RDW evolves throughout the course of an infection, potentially improving risk stratification in emergency care settings.

However, this study has some limitations. First, the external validity of the present findings may be constrained by the fact that this study was conducted at a single hospital. In particular, because this study population mainly consisted of older adults (median age 81 years), the generalisability of the findings to younger populations should be interpreted with caution. Second, the wide 95% CI, particularly for LR–, reflects limitations resulting from the small sample sizes in each category, which reduces the precision of our estimates. Given these first two limitations, future studies should be conducted across multiple centres with larger sample sizes. Third, the time before presentation between symptom onset and hospital presentation was calculated based on the patients’ self-reported times of initial symptom onset and hospital presentation. The self-reported timing of symptom onset may be imprecise. However, because it is not feasible to determine the exact time of initial symptom onset, clinicians have to rely on patient-reported information in real-world practice. Fourth, the retrospective observational design of this study is an important limitation. This design relies on the quality and completeness of data recorded in electronic medical records, which may result in missing or inaccurate information. Such limitations can introduce information bias and reduce the internal validity of the findings. Finally, in this study, we included patients who underwent at least two sets of blood culture, which is a criterion frequently used as a surrogate for suspected infection.2124 The judgement made by physicians on whether to order blood culture can be subjective. More objective criteria, such as those based on the patient’s signs and symptoms, would be more appropriate to improve the reproducibility of the present findings. Nevertheless, it is not ethical or feasible to obtain blood cultures in patients with no clinical indications in actual practice.

Conclusion

In patients with suspected infection, CRP may be useful for ruling in high-risk patients within 6 hours from symptom onset, while RDW may aid in both ruling in and ruling out patients after 72 hours. These findings suggest that considering time-dependent variations may be helpful when interpreting inflammatory markers in emergency care settings. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution because of the retrospective design of the present study and potential selection bias.
