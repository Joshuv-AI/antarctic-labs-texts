An interpretable early intensive care unit model for predicting in-hospital mortality in trauma patients combined with rib fractures: development and external validation
Authors: Liwen Hu, Penglong Zhao, Longxiao Cai, Kai Dong, Yumeng Du, Xinyue Yao, Yi Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients with rib fractures admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) are clinically heterogeneous, and early identification of those at high risk of in-hospital death remains challenging using anatomy-based descriptors alone. We aimed to develop an objective, readily implementable prediction model using routinely available variables within the first 24 hours of ICU admission and to test its generalizability in an independent institutional cohort.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed ICU patients combined with rib fractures from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) and used an independent institutional cohort for external validation. Routinely available variables recorded within the first 24 hours of ICU admission were screened to identify the most informative predictors, after which several prediction models were developed and compared. To avoid overly optimistic estimates and information leakage, the derivation cohort was first divided into a training set and an internal validation set, and all outcome-informed feature-selection and model-development procedures were restricted to the training set. Candidate predictors retained by the intersection of least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression and support vector machine-recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE) were entered directly into multivariable logistic regression (LR) for final predictor determination. Class balancing was performed only within the training process. In addition, as supplementary analyses, we explored ensemble strategies that combined the predictions of the base models and performed local institutional analyses to examine whether available thoracic trauma and overall injury severity measures materially altered model performance. Model performance was evaluated in terms of discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility.ResultsThe final multivariable model retained four independent predictors: Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II), systolic blood pressure (SBP), platelet count, and any vasopressor use. In internal validation, LR achieved the highest discrimination [area under the curve (AUC) =0.863], with sensitivity (0.800) and specificity (0.658) at the cross-validated operating threshold. In external validation, the LR model maintained good discrimination (AUC =0.873) with acceptable calibration and clinically meaningful net benefit on decision curve analysis. Supplementary ensemble analyses did not outperform LR in internal validation. In supplementary institutional analyses, Thoracic Trauma Severity Score (TTSS) alone and RibScore alone underperformed the early physiologic benchmark, whereas adding TTSS, RibScore, or Injury Severity Score (ISS) produced only limited incremental changes.ConclusionsA parsimonious, interpretable four-variable model using routinely available early ICU data provided stable internal performance and preserved discrimination in an independent external cohort. This tool may support early mortality risk stratification and triage in ICU trauma patients combined with rib fractures, with future work needed for broader multicenter validation and prospective impact evaluation.

Highlight box

Key findings

• We developed and externally validated early (first 24 h) prediction models for in-hospital mortality in intensive care unit (ICU) patients with rib fractures using routinely collected variables. A four-variable logistic regression (LR) model (Simplified Acute Physiology Score II, systolic blood pressure, platelet count, and any vasopressor use) showed stable discrimination [area under the curve (AUC) =0.863 internal; 0.873 external], acceptable calibration, and clinically meaningful net benefit on decision curve analysis (DCA).

What is known and what is new?

• Existing tools often emphasize anatomy and lack independent external validation; leakage-prone oversampling can overestimate performance in imbalanced ICU outcomes.

• We validated the model in an independent combined thoracic surgery ICU + emergency ICU cohort using harmonized definitions, restricted synthetic minority over-sampling technique to cross-validation folds, and assessed performance beyond AUC (DeLong + Holm, calibration, DCA); complex machine-learning models did not consistently outperform LR.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• This interpretable, leakage-resistant model supports early ICU risk stratification after rib fractures; next steps are multicenter recalibration and prospective, electronic health record-integrated impact evaluation.

Introduction

Thoracic trauma accounts for approximately 10% of trauma-related hospital admissions and remains a major contributor to trauma-related morbidity and mortality (1-3). Rib fractures are among the most common manifestation of thoracic injury and are encountered in approximately 10% of trauma patients overall, as well as in up to 39–40% of patients with blunt thoracic trauma (1,4). These injuries are usually caused by blunt mechanisms, particularly traffic collisions, falls, and crush injuries, whereas penetrating trauma accounts for a smaller proportion of cases (5-8). Although the overall mortality of rib fractures is lower than that of some immediately life-threatening traumatic conditions, their clinical impact is substantial because they are associated with pain, impaired ventilation, pulmonary complications, prolonged hospitalization, and increased mortality in vulnerable patients such as older adults and those with chronic cardiopulmonary disease (9-11). Importantly, compared with intracranial injury, intrathoracic organ injury, or intra-abdominal visceral injury, rib fractures are often easier to recognize early through plain radiography and even initial physical examination, including inspection and palpation (12,13). Therefore, once rib fractures are identified in trauma patients, they may provide a practical and readily available entry point for rapid early risk stratification, regardless of whether other injuries coexist.

Early risk prediction is particularly important in the intensive care unit (ICU) patients combined with rib fractures, in whom outcomes are influenced not only by fracture burden itself but also by associated injuries, comorbidities, hemodynamic instability, and evolving organ dysfunction (14-16). Previous studies have identified several factors associated with adverse outcomes after rib fractures, including advanced age, fracture severity, concomitant injuries, and chronic baseline vulnerabilities (11,17). However, many reported predictors are either not sufficiently tailored to critically ill patients or are difficult to apply efficiently in early ICU decision-making (18). Some rely on symptom reporting or detailed injury characterization, whereas others do not adequately reflect the acute physiological disturbances that often drive short-term prognosis in critically ill trauma patients (18,19). Several thoracic trauma severity scores have also been proposed, including the Thoracic Trauma Severity Score (TTSS), Chest Trauma Score (CTS), and RibScore (20-22). These scores represent important reference frameworks for risk stratification after thoracic trauma because they incorporate different combinations of physiologic, demographic, and fracture-related variables, and several have been associated with mortality in prior studies. However, their reported performance has varied across populations, and some required radiographic or trauma-specific variables may not be immediately available in a standardized form during early ICU care or across heterogeneous databases (23). Therefore, when developing a new early ICU prediction model, it is important not only to acknowledge these previously available scores but also, where feasible, to benchmark the new model against them.

Rather than focusing exclusively on patients with isolated rib fractures, this study targeted ICU trauma patients in whom rib fractures formed part of the overall injury profile, because this more closely reflects real-world clinical practice and the setting in which early risk stratification is needed. Accordingly, we aimed to develop and externally validate an interpretable early ICU prediction model for in-hospital mortality using routinely available variables obtained shortly after admission. We hypothesized that a parsimonious model based on objective early clinical data could provide clinically useful risk stratification in trauma patients combined with rib fractures, while recognizing that further work with harmonized trauma-severity data will be needed to clarify the additional contribution of thoracic and extrathoracic injury burden. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1-0395/rc).

Methods

Study design and data sources

This retrospective study used two independent cohorts: (I) a derivation cohort from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV; v2.0; 2008–2019) and (II) an external validation cohort combining patients from the thoracic surgery ICU and the emergency ICU at Jinling Hospital (January 2021 to August 2025), with harmonized predictor and outcome definitions. The MIMIC-IV cohort was used for model development and internal validation, whereas the institutional cohort was used exclusively for external validation.

In MIMIC-IV, rib fracture cases were identified using International Classification of Diseases (ICD-9 and ICD-10) diagnosis codes (Table S1). Because MIMIC-IV contains de-identified data, informed consent was waived; data access was granted after completion of the required training and approvals. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Jinling Hospital (No. 2026DZKY-070-01).

Study population and eligibility criteria

Derivation cohort (MIMIC-IV)

We included adult patients (≥18 years) admitted to the ICU with a diagnosis of rib fracture. For patients with multiple admissions, only the first hospital admission and first ICU stay were retained. Exclusion criteria were: (I) age <18 years; (II) ICU length of stay <24 hours; (III) implausible anthropometric values (e.g., weight ≤1 kg) identified by data range checks; and (IV) missing data in any predictors required for model development (complete-case analysis). Patients were not restricted to isolated rib fractures; those with concomitant traumatic injuries were also eligible, provided that rib fracture was part of the index trauma presentation requiring ICU admission.

External validation cohort (institutional)

For external validation, we assembled an independent cohort of adult ICU patients combined with rib fractures admitted between January 2021 and August 2025. Inclusion and exclusion criteria were aligned as closely as possible with those used in MIMIC-IV, and predictor and outcome definitions were harmonized across cohorts. Patients with missing values in predictors required for the final model were excluded. As in the derivation cohort, patients with concomitant traumatic injuries were eligible if rib fracture was part of the index trauma presentation requiring ICU admission, reflecting the fact that rib fractures in routine ICU practice often occur in the context of multisystem trauma rather than as isolated injuries.

Data extraction and variable definitions

Structured Query Language (SQL) was used to query and extract the clinical characteristics of patients, which included: (I) demographic characteristics: age, gender, weight, ICU length of stay (los_icu), hospital length of stay (los_hospital); (II) vital signs: heart rate (heart_rate), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), respiratory rate (resp_rate), body temperature (temperature), blood glucose (glucose), mean arterial pressure (MAP); (III) laboratory examination data: hematocrit, hemoglobin, platelet count, white blood cell count, international normalized ratio (INR), prothrombin time (PT), partial thromboplastin time (PTT), lymphocyte count (abs_lymphocytes), neutrophil count (abs_neutrophils), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), platelet-to-white blood cell ratio (PWR), platelet-to-neutrophil ratio (PPN), Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index (SII), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), chloride, creatinine, sodium, and potassium; (IV) scoring systems: blood urea nitrogen score (BUN_score), Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II), Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score, and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score; (V) complications: arrhythmias, renal failure, ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP), malignant tumor (malignant_cancer), diabetes, heart failure (congestive_heart_failure), and kidney disease (renal_disease); (VI) treatment: mechanical ventilation (mechvent) and any vasopressor use, including dopamine, epinephrine, norepinephrine, phenylephrine, vasopressin, dobutamine, and milrinone.

For each predictor, we used the earliest recorded value within the first 24 hours after ICU admission. The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality, defined as death during the index hospitalization.

Some trauma-related descriptors, including injury mechanism and selected associated injuries, were available for descriptive reporting. However, key anatomic and management-related variables for standardized cross-cohort model development, such as rib fracture number, displacement, comminution, operative stabilization, and harmonized overall trauma severity measures [e.g., abbreviated injury scale (AIS)/Injury Severity Score (ISS)], were not uniformly available across the derivation and external validation cohorts, particularly in MIMIC-IV. These variables were therefore not included in the primary derivation process. Instead, the main model was based on routinely available early ICU variables that could be consistently defined in both datasets. Because more detailed trauma-severity information was available in the institutional cohort, supplementary local analyses were performed to further characterize trauma burden and to assess whether available thoracic and overall injury severity measures materially altered model performance.

Supplementary institutional analyses

Because additional trauma-related variables were available in the institutional cohort, we performed supplementary analyses confined to that cohort to further characterize trauma burden and to contextualize the early physiologic model against locally available trauma severity measures. Specifically, we described overall and thoracic trauma burden in the external validation cohort and conducted local model extension analyses by adding ISS, Thoracic Trauma Severity Score (TTSS), or RibScore to the base early physiologic model. We also compared the base model with TTSS alone and RibScore alone as exploratory benchmarks. These analyses were intended to complement, rather than replace, the primary external validation of the derivation model.

Model development and validation

The derivation cohort was first divided into a training set (70%) and an internal validation set (30%) using outcome-stratified sampling to preserve the event proportion in both subsets. To avoid information leakage, all feature-selection and model-development procedures were restricted to the training cohort. Candidate variables recorded within the first 24 hours of ICU admission were screened using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression and support vector machine-recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE), and variables retained by both methods were entered directly into multivariable logistic regression (LR) to identify the final predictors (24). Multicollinearity among the final predictors was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), and pairwise associations were examined using Spearman correlation analysis. Because in-hospital mortality was less frequent than survival, class imbalance was addressed using the synthetic minority over-sampling technique (SMOTE) within the training folds during repeated cross-validation only. Using the final predictor set, we developed four prediction models: LR, random forest (RF), gradient boosting machine (GBM), and support vector machine (SVM). Two soft-voting ensemble strategies based on simple and weighted averaging of predicted probabilities, as well as a SAPS II–only predicted probabilities were additionally evaluated as supplementary sensitivity analyses. Because LR provided direct coefficient-based interpretability, the final LR model was also presented as a nomogram, and model interpretability was further assessed using Shapley additive explanations (SHAP).

Model performance and comparison

Model performance was assessed in terms of discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility. Discrimination was evaluated using the area under the curve (AUC). Pairwise AUC comparisons between models were performed using DeLong’s test, and the resulting P values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Holm method. Calibration was assessed using calibration curves, intercept, and slope, and clinical utility was evaluated using decision curve analysis (DCA). Additional metrics included sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, positive predictive value, negative predictive value. All metrics were calculated on the original, non-oversampled internal and external validation datasets.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation for approximately normally distributed data and as median (interquartile range) otherwise. Between-group comparisons were conducted using Student’s t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as counts (percentages) and compared using the Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test. All analyses were performed using R (v4.4.3, Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The main packages used included “glmnet” for LASSO regression (25), “caret” for model development and validation (26), “pROC” for ROC analysis and DeLong testing (27), “rms” for nomogram construction and calibration-related analyses (28,29), “PRROC” for precision-recall analysis (30), “randomForest”, “gbm”, and “e1071” for machine-learning model development, and “smotefamily” for SMOTE-based resampling where applicable (31-33).

Results

Patient selection and baseline characteristics

After applying the eligibility criteria, 437 adult ICU patients combined with rib fractures were identified in the MIMIC-IV database and included in the derivation cohort (Figure 1). The derivation cohort was randomly split (7:3) into a training set (n=306) and an internal validation set (n=131) using outcome-stratified sampling (Figure 1). An independent institutional cohort from Jinling Hospital was assembled for external validation and included 204 eligible ICU patients combined with rib fractures (Figure 1).

The institutional cohort represented a mixed trauma population rather than isolated rib fracture cases. Associated injuries were common, including craniocerebral injury (42.6%), spinal injury (31.9%), extremity fracture (20.1%), pelvic fracture (17.2%), hemopneumothorax (11.3%), liver injury (7.8%), and intestinal injury (5.9%). Compared with survivors, non-survivors had higher ISS (28.40 vs. 22.57, P=0.02) and TTSS (10.83 vs. 9.36, P=0.01), whereas RibScore did not differ between groups (2.27 vs. 2.30, P=0.89). Non-survivors were also older and had lower hematocrit, hemoglobin, and platelet counts; higher BUN levels; higher SAPS II scores; and lower GCS scores. They more frequently required mechanical ventilation and vasoactive support (Table S3).

Feature selection in the training cohort using LASSO and SVM-RFE

To reduce the candidate predictor set while avoiding information leakage, feature selection was performed exclusively in the MIMIC-IV training cohort. LASSO regression selected 14 variables from the initial 27 candidates (Figure 2A,2B). In parallel, SVM-RFE identified 20 variables with optimal performance (Figure 2C). The intersection of the two methods yielded 10 candidate variables: SAPS II, SOFA score, any vasopressor use, renal failure, PTT, age, platelet count, congestive heart failure, SBP, and INR (Figure 2D).

Multivariable LR in the training cohort

The 10 candidate variables retained by the intersection of LASSO and SVM-RFE were entered directly into a multivariable LR model in the training cohort. This model retained four independent predictors: SAPS II, any vasopressor use, platelet count, and SBP (all P<0.05; Table 2). These predictors were then carried forward for model development and subsequent validation.

Correlation and collinearity among final predictors

Multicollinearity was assessed using VIFs, which were low for all four predictors (approximately 1.061–1.270; Figure 3A, Table S4), indicating minimal collinearity. Spearman correlation analysis showed only modest pairwise correlations (Figure 3B). The strongest positive correlation was found between SAPS II and any vasopressor use (ρ =0.39), whereas the strongest negative correlation was found between SBP and any vasopressor use (ρ =−0.35). Model development and internal validation

Using the four final predictors, we developed four models: LR, RF, GBM, and SVM (Figure 4). SMOTE was applied only within training folds during repeated cross-validation, and all performance metrics were calculated on the original, non-oversampled internal and external validation datasets (Table 3). In the training set, RF achieved the highest AUC (1.000), followed by SVM and GBM (both 0.881) and LR (0.871) (Figure 4A). Holm-adjusted DeLong tests showed that RF significantly outperformed the other models, whereas differences between LR, GBM, and SVM were not significant (Figure 4C). In the internal validation set, LR showed the highest AUC (0.863), followed by GBM (0.855), SVM (0.797), and RF (0.774) (Figure 4B), with no significant pairwise differences after Holm adjustment (Figure 4F). Calibration was acceptable overall (Figure 4D,4E). At the selected operating thresholds, LR achieved sensitivity of 0.800 and specificity of 0.658, whereas RF showed higher specificity (0.838) but lower sensitivity (0.350) (Table 3). Given its favorable validation performance, interpretability, and ease of implementation, LR was selected as the primary model. Supplementary ensemble analyses did not outperform LR in internal validation and were therefore retained as sensitivity analyses (Figure S1 and Table S5).

External validation of the LR model

The LR model was externally validated in the institutional cohort of 204 ICU patients combined with rib fractures. The model demonstrated good discrimination (AUC =0.873; Figure 6A). Calibration was acceptable overall, although some deviation from perfect agreement was observed at higher predicted risks and should be interpreted cautiously given the limited number of events in the external cohort (Figure 6B). DCA demonstrated a positive net benefit across clinically relevant threshold probabilities (Figure 6C). Despite differences in case mix between the external cohort and MIMIC-IV, the preserved discrimination supported the model’s transportability across ICU settings.

Supplementary benchmark analyses of thoracic trauma severity scores in the institutional cohort

As supplementary analyses in the institutional cohort, we benchmarked the early physiologic model against available thoracic trauma severity scores. The base four-variable model showed better discrimination (AUC =0.873) than TTSS alone (AUC =0.659) and RibScore alone (AUC =0.494) (Table S6). Adding TTSS, RibScore, or ISS produced only limited numerical changes in discrimination (AUC =0.881, 0.887, and 0.890, respectively). Calibration metrics were broadly similar across the base and extended models, with calibration plots shown in Figure S2.

Discussion

Rib fractures are a common manifestation of chest trauma and remain clinically important because they are associated with pain, impaired ventilation, pulmonary complications, prolonged hospitalization, and death in vulnerable patients, particularly those requiring intensive care (34,35). Early identification of high-risk patients is therefore important for triage, monitoring, and timely intervention (36). In the present study, we developed and externally validated an interpretable early ICU model for predicting in-hospital mortality in trauma patients combined with rib fractures using four routinely available variables obtained within the first 24 hours of ICU admission: SAPS II, platelet count, SBP, and any vasopressor use. The final LR model showed stable discrimination in internal validation and preserved performance in an independent institutional cohort, suggesting potential applicability across ICU settings.

A notable finding of this study is that a parsimonious model based on early physiologic and treatment-related information performed well without requiring detailed radiographic fracture descriptors or other anatomy-based variables that are often difficult to harmonize across datasets. This is clinically relevant because short-term prognosis in critically ill trauma patients combined with rib fractures is influenced not only by injury pattern itself but also by global physiologic derangement, hemodynamic instability, organ dysfunction, and early treatment intensity. The four retained predictors appear to capture complementary dimensions of acute severity: SAPS II reflects overall physiologic burden, platelet count may reflect bleeding, inflammation, or systemic stress, SBP reflects circulatory status, and any vasopressor use serves as a pragmatic indicator of early hemodynamic support requirements (37-40).

Among these predictors, SAPS II contributed the greatest prognostic weight, which is consistent with its established role as a composite severity score in ICU populations (41). The retention of SBP and any vasopressor use further suggests that early hemodynamic context may provide prognostic information beyond global severity scoring alone (42,43). Vasopressor use should not be interpreted as a causal determinant of mortality, but rather as a marker of shock severity, circulatory fragility, and treatment intensity in the early ICU phase (44,45). Similarly, the contribution of SBP in the final model should be understood as an adjusted prognostic signal within the broader hemodynamic context. Platelet count is also best interpreted as a pragmatic prognostic marker rather than a mechanistic determinant, because thrombocytopenia in trauma and critical illness may result from multiple overlapping processes, including hemorrhage, consumption, dilution, coagulopathy, and systemic inflammation (46,47).

Although we also evaluated several machine-learning approaches and supplementary ensemble strategies, they did not show a consistent advantage over LR in internal validation. In the setting of a relatively small, clinically coherent predictor set, greater algorithmic complexity may offer limited incremental value while reducing interpretability (48). From a translational perspective, LR retained practical advantages in transparency, interpretability, and bedside implementation, supporting its use as the primary model in the present study.

Our findings should also be interpreted in the context of previously published thoracic trauma scoring systems, particularly TTSS, CTS, and RibScore (20-22). These instruments provide useful reference frameworks for risk stratification after chest trauma, but their reported performance has varied across study populations, and some radiographic or trauma-specific components may be difficult to capture in a sufficiently standardized manner for harmonized use across retrospective ICU datasets and institutions (23,49,50,51). In the present study, exploratory benchmark analyses were feasible only for TTSS and RibScore in the institutional cohort, because the corresponding variables required for uniform reconstruction of all published scores were not consistently available across datasets. Within that cohort, TTSS alone and RibScore alone showed lower discrimination than the early physiologic model, whereas adding TTSS, RibScore, or ISS resulted in only small numerical changes in model performance. Taken together, these findings suggest that, in a mixed-trauma ICU population with rib fractures, early physiologic severity may capture prognostic information beyond that provided by locally available thoracic trauma severity measures (14,18). However, these benchmark analyses should be interpreted as supportive rather than definitive, because they were confined to the institutional cohort and were not part of the primary externally validated derivation model.

The present model should therefore be interpreted as an early physiologic risk-stratification tool for ICU trauma patients combined with rib fractures, rather than as a comprehensive anatomy-based thoracic trauma severity score. This distinction is important because detailed data on rib fracture number, displacement, comminution, operative stabilization, and standardized trauma severity measures were not uniformly available across both cohorts, particularly in the derivation dataset. Although the institutional cohort allowed supplementary analyses incorporating ISS, TTSS, and RibScore, these data could not fully exclude the possibility that extrathoracic injury burden, fracture morphology, or treatment selection contributed to the observed mortality patterns (49,52). Future studies should prioritize multicenter datasets with harmonized anatomic, radiographic, and management-related variables to further refine and contextualize this early physiologic framework.

This study has several strengths, including derivation in a large ICU database, independent external validation, restriction of outcome-informed feature selection to the training cohort to reduce leakage risk, and comprehensive assessment of discrimination, calibration, and decision-analytic utility. Several limitations should also be acknowledged, including the retrospective design, incomplete harmonization of thoracic and overall trauma severity across cohorts, the exploratory local nature of the institutional benchmark analyses, and external validation in a single institutional setting. Despite these limitations, the present study suggests that a simple four-variable model based on early ICU data may provide clinically useful support for mortality risk stratification in trauma patients combined with rib fractures.

Conclusions

We developed and externally validated an interpretable in-hospital mortality prediction model for ICU trauma patients combined with rib fractures based on four routinely available early ICU variables. The model demonstrated good discrimination, acceptable calibration, and potential clinical utility for early risk stratification. Supplementary institutional analyses suggested that available thoracic trauma and overall injury severity measures provided only limited incremental value beyond the early physiologic framework in the local dataset. Future studies should prioritize prospective multicenter validation, recalibration across diverse settings, incorporation of trauma-specific radiologic and management variables, and evaluation of clinical impact when implemented as an electronic health record-integrated decision-support tool.
