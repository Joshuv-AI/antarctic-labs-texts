Background

Time series analysis is widely used to understand, model, and forecast temporal patterns in fields such as finance, healthcare, climate science, and agriculture. In agriculture applications, major food grain crops such as rice, wheat, coarse cereals, and pulses, accurate forecasting is important for ensuring food security, improving resource allocation, and mitigating risks associated with climate variability.

The ability to accurately forecast crop production helps farmers, policymakers, and supply-chain managers make informed decisions. One of the most widely embraced models is the Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) model, as it is simple and can capture linear dependence in time series data [1].

The performance of ARIMA model depends on appropriate selection of autoregressive (p), differencing (d), and moving average (q) orders, usually these orders are determined by trial and error, which is time-consuming and would not give satisfactory results [2]. To make the order selection process more automated and efficient, statistical metrics like the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) and Final Prediction error (FPE) are usually used. In this regard, several researchers have used metaheuristic algorithms to identify parameters in ARIMA or ARMA models. Grid search is computationally expensive and infeasible when p, d and q are large and with large-scale datasets [3]. Researchers used metaheuristic algorithms like Genetic Algorithms which are successful in identifying orders and parameters of ARIMA models [4,5]. Although these evolutionary strategies improved search efficiency and addressed challenges such as premature convergence and fixed operator controls (mutation and crossover), they still struggle to adapt effectively to multimodal landscapes. To achieve lower AIC values and faster convergence, the Firefly Algorithm was introduced, which motivated the adoption of swarm intelligence (SI) techniques [6]. Majority of traditional SI algorithms namely Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO), Ant Colony Optimization (ACO), and Artificial Bee Colony (ABC) use hard grid like population structures, which restricts their adaptiveness to dynamical search landscapes. To overcome these challenges, Spider Monkey Optimization (SMO) algorithm presented in the Memetic Computing was used which mimics the fission-fusion social dynamics of the spider monkeys as they forage [7,8]. The effectiveness of SMO has been demonstrated across diverse optimization problems including resource allocation and scheduling in fog computing environments [9], statistical and optimization applications [10], predictive modelling using artificial neural networks [11], deep neural network hyperparameter tuning [12], and intrusion detection systems [13]. This Spider Monkey Optimization (SMO) approach has multi-group, socially networked search based on the foraging behaviour of spider monkeys.

The Spider Monkey Optimization (SMO) algorithm provides a balanced exploration–exploitation mechanism, helping to avoid premature convergence and local minima.

Its effectiveness in forecasting time-series has been evidenced in recent research [8] in which a better model to forecast air-quality was constructed using a SMO approach. SMO demonstrates two concepts of swarm intelligence: division of labor and self-organization. Each group has their local leader and overall search process has one global leader. This paper presents an ARIMA model optimized using SMO aims to enhance accuracy of forecast by choosing orders automatically while reducing computational costs. The proposed approach will attempt to address the flaws associated with the manual grid search techniques by utilizing the benefits ofSMO to present a more efficient time series forecasting algorithm by selecting the proper orders.

Method details

Methodology

The ARIMA SMO model is based on a combination of standard ARIMA modeling and population-based metaheuristic optimization algorithm SMO to automate the process of choosing optimal model orders [7]. Instead of performing trial-and-error or grid-based search to select ARIMA order, this approach takes the ARIMA order selection as an optimization problem with the objective of selecting (p, d, q) to minimize AIC or prediction error. The order of ARIMA configuration is a candidate order configuration of each monkey in the SMO population. The position of each spider monkey represents a candidate ARIMA order configuration (p, d, q) and its fitness equals the performance of ARIMA configuration on training information. Over time the optimizer maintains the population of candidate models. The optimization process in SMO proceeds through two major phases, namely: a) Local Leader Phase and b) Global Leader Phase. The SMO algorithm incorporates a fission-fusion social restructuring mechanism to ensure the diversity of the population and to prevent premature convergence. This method provides dynamic restructuring when stagnation of performance is detected, either within the subgroup or the whole population [3]. In case stagnation is identified, subgroups can be reconfigured or agents performing poorly can be reinitiated to identify different parts of the search space. Adaptive restructuring of this kind improves the refreezing capability of the algorithm, as well as its effective search dynamics. Through repeated steps of such coordination processes, it gradually converges to a more optimal order of ARIMA. The global leader is considered as the final solution, and it will be the combination of p, d, q that performed best in the process of optimization.

Definition of ARIMA (p, d, q)

The ARIMA model denoted as ARIMA (p, d, q), is a widely used statistical framework for time series forecasting. It combines autoregressive (AR), differencing (I), and moving average (MA) components to capture temporal dependencies and underlying patterns in sequential data.

The Box–Jenkins methodology is commonly used to identify appropriate values of p, d, and q and to estimate the corresponding model parameters.

In an ARIMA (p, d, q) model, the parameter p represents the autoregressive order, which specifies the number of lagged observations included to capture the dependence of the current value on its past values. The parameter d denotes the degree of differencing, indicating the number of times the original time series is differenced to achieve stationarity. The parameter q corresponds to the moving average order, defining the number of lagged forecast errors incorporated to model short-term shock effects in the series.

3. SMO Phases for ARIMA order selection

a. Local Leader Phase

a. Local Leader Phase

During this stage, each group uses the optimal solution that was the best as identified by the local leader to optimize the parameters of the candidates. This phase focuses on the development of the existing promising solutions, which is achieved by searching the surrounding neighborhood of the local leader. This is to detect and exploit the possible enhancements thus improving the overall effectiveness of the solutions.

b. Global Leader Phase

b. Global Leader Phase

In this case, the global leader guides the whole population to the new regions in the search space. This method will avoid premature convergence and allow different exploration to avoid into local minima.

c. Decision Phase (Group Reorganization)

c. Decision Phase (Group Reorganization)

During this phase groups will reorganize periodically based on the assessment of performance. Monkeys with lower performances levels may shift to new groups, thereby allowing swarm to explore the balance on exploration and exploitation. After iteratively going through these phases, the SMO effectively seeks the ARIMA order that minimizes the selected objective function, thereby improving forecast performance and reducing computational cost compared with conventional grid-search techniques.

AIC was included because it accounts for both goodness of fit and model complexity, thereby discouraging unnecessarily complex ARIMA specifications. RMSE was included because it directly measures the magnitude of forecasting errors. Optimizing AIC alone could select a model with good in-sample fit but weaker forecasting performance, whereas optimizing RMSE alone could favour a more complex model without adequately penalizing additional parameters. The composite objective function therefore provides a compromise between parsimony and predictive accuracy.

Because AIC and RMSE are measured on different numerical scales, both quantities were normalized before they were combined. Min–max normalization was applied within each optimization run which prevents one component from dominating the fitness function solely because of its numerical scale. The weighting parameter was fixed at α=0.5, assigning equal importance to model parsimony and predictive accuracy. This value was selected a priori to avoid dataset-specific tuning and was kept constant across all datasets and competing methods. Therefore, the reported comparison does not result from adjusting the objective-function weight to favour the proposed method. Preliminary experimentation indicated that this setting provides stable convergence and consistent forecasting performance across all datasets. All experiments were repeated with fixed random seeds to ensure reproducibility and fair comparison. A random seed value of 42 was used throughout all optimization experiments.

Method validation

Data description

Annual production data for major foodgrain crops in India, including rice, wheat, coarse cereals, total cereals, pulses, and total foodgrains, were used in this study. The time frame covered in the dataset takes 75 years from 1950–51 up to 2024–25. The dataset was compiled from official government statistical sources, including the Reserve Bank of India (Series ON2120) and the Ministry of Agriculture and Farmers Welfare, Government of India (Series ON4251), as well as their historical publications spanning the period 1950–2024 [14]. The dataset contains 75 annual observations covering the period 1950–51 to 2024–25. To evaluate forecasting performance under a realistic prediction scenario, a chronological train–test split was adopted. The first 65 observations (approximately 87% of the data) were used for model training and parameter optimization, while the remaining 10 observations were reserved for out-of-sample testing. This temporal partition preserves the sequential nature of the time series and prevents information leakage from future observations into the training process. In addition to the final chronological train–test evaluation, rolling-origin forecasting was used to assess the stability of the results. At each forecast origin, the model was fitted using all observations available up to that point, and the next observation was forecasted. The origin was then advanced sequentially until the end of the evaluation period. Model selection was performed using the available training observations at each origin, and the corresponding forecasts were aggregated to calculate RMSE, MAE, and MAPE.

Experimental design and validation strategy

A chronological train–test evaluation was used to assess the forecasting performance of the proposed ARIMA–SMO framework. For each time series, the observations were ordered chronologically and divided into a training set and a test set. The training set was used for ARIMA model estimation, SMO-based order optimization, and evaluation of candidate solutions. The test set was not used during optimization or model selection and was reserved exclusively for final out-of-sample forecast evaluation. For every optimization method, including SMO, GA, PSO, BO, and Auto-ARIMA, candidate ARIMA orders were generated and evaluated using the training data only. After the optimal ARIMA order had been selected, the corresponding model was fitted using the training data and used to generate forecasts for the test period. Forecast accuracy was then assessed using RMSE, MAE, and MAPE. AIC was reported as a model-selection and goodness-of-fit measure, whereas the test-set forecasting measures were used to evaluate out-of-sample predictive performance. The random seed was fixed at 42 to ensure reproducibility of the stochastic optimization procedures. For Bayesian Optimization, the search domain was defined on the same discrete grid used by SMO. BO-generated values were rounded and clipped to this grid before ARIMA model fitting. Thus, all optimization methods were evaluated under identical ARIMA-order restrictions, and the comparison reflects differences in search behaviour rather than unequal model-complexity constraints.

Results

The convergence behaviour of the ARIMA–SMO algorithm was assessed across all six agricultural production series by tracking the global best fitness over 80 iterations (Fig. 3). Across all crops, the algorithm achieved rapid and stable convergence well within the maximum iteration limit, confirming the computational efficiency of the proposed framework over conventional grid-search approaches. The earliest convergence was observed for Pulses (fitness = 0.83102, iteration ∼1), indicating that the initial SMO population already contained a near-optimal ARIMA configuration. Total Cereals and Total Foodgrains converged sharply within the first 6–7 iterations (fitness = 0.81601 and 0.82006, respectively), reflecting the global leader mechanism's ability to rapidly redirect the swarm toward superior order combinations. Wheat exhibited a characteristic stepwise descent from ∼0.813 to 0.81040 by iteration 10, validating the sequential transition from local exploitation to global exploration inherent in the SMO's leader update phases. Coarse Cereals recorded the latest convergence (∼iteration 20, fitness = 0.78960) and the lowest final fitness value among all series, suggesting a comparatively complex optimization landscape. Rice converged smoothly to 0.80172 by iteration 15 with negligible variation. In all cases, the fitness curve remained completely flat after convergence, with no oscillation or deterioration across the remaining iterations, demonstrating that the fission-fusion social restructuring mechanism effectively prevented stagnation and premature convergence. The consistently narrow range of converged fitness values (0.78960–0.83102) across all six series further confirms the robustness and solution quality of the ARIMA–SMO framework.

The comparison of optimization-based ARIMA order selection methods for forecasting agricultural production reveals that Spider Monkey Optimization (SMO) consistently outperformed Genetic Algorithm (GA), Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO), and Bayesian Optimization (BO) across all six datasets. For rice production, SMO selected the ARIMA (2,2,0) model and achieved the lowest AIC (701.30), BIC (714.16), RMSE (78.64), MAE (58.70), MAPE (4.47%), and runtime (54.63 s), indicating superior model fit, forecasting accuracy, and computational efficiency. Similar results were observed for wheat production, where SMO selected ARIMA (0,2,2) and obtained the lowest values of AIC (636.12), BIC (648.98), RMSE (81.99), MAE (77.32), MAPE (7.16%), and runtime (81.08 s).

For coarse cereals production, SMO again demonstrated the best performance by selecting ARIMA (0,2,2) and recording the minimum AIC (620.95), BIC (627.37), RMSE (36.24), MAE (29.85), MAPE (6.33%), and runtime (38.70 s). In the case of total cereals production, the ARIMA (2,2,0) model identified by SMO yielded the lowest AIC (779.81), BIC (792.67), RMSE (41.95), MAE (30.06), MAPE (5.48%), and computational time (50.93 s). Likewise, for pulses production, SMO selected ARIMA (2,2,2) and achieved the smallest AIC (528.75), BIC (545.90), RMSE (28.78), MAE (23.59), MAPE (10.03%), and runtime (89.97 s). For total food grains production, SMO once again identified ARIMA (2,2,0) as the optimal model and produced the lowest AIC (792.64), BIC (805.50), RMSE (35.75), MAE (27.42), MAPE (6.11%), and runtime (131.38 s).

Across all six agricultural production series, SMO consistently achieved the lowest AIC, BIC, RMSE, MAE, MAPE, and runtime compared with GA, PSO, and BO. The selected ARIMA structures were generally more parsimonious while yielding superior forecasting accuracy. These results indicate that the adaptive exploration–exploitation mechanism of SMO enables more efficient identification of optimal ARIMA orders than competing optimization strategies.

Since Spider Monkey Optimization (SMO) consistently outperformed all competing metaheuristic optimization approaches in terms of forecasting accuracy and computational efficiency, an additional comparison was conducted with the widely used Auto-ARIMA procedure (Table 9). Unlike GA, PSO, BO, and SMO, which formulate ARIMA order identification as an optimization problem, Auto-ARIMA employs an information-criterion-based stepwise search strategy for automatic order selection. The comparison revealed that ARIMA-SMO consistently achieved lower out-of-sample forecasting errors across all agricultural production series. These findings indicate that optimization-driven order selection can provide substantial improvements in predictive performance. Although Auto-ARIMA required lower computational effort owing to its stepwise search mechanism, the superior forecasting accuracy obtained by ARIMA-SMO demonstrates the effectiveness of the proposed framework for agricultural production forecasting.

Further, the Diebold–Mariano test was conducted using squared forecast errors as the loss differential function. The test compared one-step-ahead out-of-sample forecasts obtained from the 10-year testing period. Negative DM statistics indicate superior forecasting accuracy of the first model listed in the comparison.

The Diebold–Mariano (DM) test results provide indicative evidence of differences in forecasting accuracy among the optimization-based ARIMA models. However, because the comparison is based on only 10 out-of-sample observations, the results should be interpreted cautiously. Across all agricultural production series, the comparisons between SMO and the competing methods yielded negative DM statistics with p-values below 0.05, indicating that the forecasts generated by the SMO-based ARIMA models were significantly more accurate than those obtained using GA, PSO, and BO. The strongest improvements were observed in the SMO versus GA comparisons, where highly significant DM statistics were consistently obtained across all datasets. The differences between SMO and BO were smaller, with p-values close to the 0.05 threshold. These results suggest a possible advantage for SMO, but they should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of practical superiority given the limited test-sample size.

The DM test further showed that BO generally outperformed GA, as evidenced by significant negative DM statistics and p-values below 0.05 across all crop categories. Similarly, PSO demonstrated improved forecasting performance relative to GA in most datasets, although the improvement for rice production was only marginally significant (p = 0.051). In contrast, the comparisons between BO and PSO were not statistically significant for any of the production series (p > 0.05), suggesting that the forecasting performances of these two optimization approaches were broadly comparable.

Overall, the DM results suggest that SMO was competitive with, and generally more accurate than, the other methods in this application. Nevertheless, the small test sample limits the strength of statistical conclusions, and the observed differences should be regarded as indicative rather than definitive.

Discussion

The results indicate that SMO is a competitive approach for automatic ARIMA order selection in the agricultural production series examined. SMO produced lower test-set errors than GA, PSO, and BO across all six series, but the improvements over BO were relatively modest, generally around 3–4% in RMSE. Because the out-of-sample evaluation used only 10 observations, these differences and the corresponding DM-test results should be interpreted cautiously rather than as definitive evidence of universal superiority.The superior performance of SMO can be attributed to its adaptive fission–fusion search mechanism, which enhances population diversity and facilitates efficient exploration of the ARIMA parameter space. Consequently, SMO is less prone to premature convergence and is able to identify more suitable model configurations. In addition to improving forecasting accuracy, SMO required less computational time than the competing optimization methods, highlighting its potential as a robust and computationally efficient framework for agricultural time-series forecasting. The observed performance reflects both the search dynamics of SMO and the prior information incorporated through its design settings, including the ARIMA-order bounds, population structure, stopping criteria, and the equal AIC–RMSE weighting. These settings were fixed before the comparative experiments and were applied consistently across the datasets; however, they should not be regarded as universally optimal. Applications involving different data frequencies, longer or shorter series, seasonal patterns, or exogenous variables may require sensitivity analysis and re-selection of the search bounds and fitness weights.

Limitations and future research directions

Although the proposed ARIMA–SMO framework achieved improved forecasting performance for the agricultural production series considered in this study, certain limitations should be recognized. The optimization process requires repeated estimation of candidate ARIMA models, resulting in higher computational effort than conventional order-selection procedures. While the observed execution times were acceptable for the datasets analyzed, computational requirements may increase as the search space expands. In addition, the proposed framework is developed for univariate ARIMA modelling and therefore remains dependent on the assumptions underlying ARIMA models. As a result, forecasting performance may be affected when dealing with highly non-linear dynamics, structural changes, or complex non-stationary behaviour that cannot be adequately captured through differencing. Future studies may extend the proposed optimization strategy to seasonal, hybrid, or other advanced forecasting frameworks and evaluate its performance on larger and more diverse datasets to further assess scalability and generalizability. A further limitation is the small out-of-sample evaluation period, which contained only 10 observations. Forecast-error estimates and DM-test statistics can be unstable in such small samples, particularly when differences between methods are modest. Although a standard DM test was used, future work should apply small-sample corrections, such as the Harvey–Leybourne–Newbold adjustment, and evaluate the methods using longer test periods, rolling-origin experiments, and larger benchmark datasets.
