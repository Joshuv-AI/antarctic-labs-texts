Convergence Analysis of Randomized Subspace Normalized SGD under Heavy-Tailed Noise
Gaku Omiya, Pierre-Louis Poirion, Akiko Takeda

Abstract
Randomized subspace methods reduce per-iteration cost; however, in nonconvex optimization, most analyses are expectation-based, and high-probability bounds remain scarce even under sub-Gaussian noise. We first prove that randomized subspace SGD (RS-SGD) admits a high-probability convergence bound under sub-Gaussian noise, achieving the same order of oracle complexity as prior in-expectation results. Motivated by the prevalence of heavy-tailed gradients in modern machine learning, we then propose randomized subspace normalized SGD (RS-NSGD), which integrates direction normalization into subspace updates. Assuming the noise has bounded p p -th moments, we establish both in-expectation and high-probability convergence guarantees, and show that RS-NSGD can achieve better oracle complexity than full-dimensional normalized SGD.



Keywords:
Randomized subspace methods; Stochastic optimization; Nonconvex optimization; Convergence analysis; Heavy-tailed noise.

Introduction

We consider the following nonconvex stochastic optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}F(x),\qquad F(x):=\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\mathcal{D}}[f(x,\xi)],

(1)

One possible way to reduce the dimension of (

This subspace-descent approach was developed as a deterministic optimization method minimizing
F

14

In machine learning, such randomized subspace methods can be used in at least two ways.
First, they can be used to reduce the memory footprint of gradient computations via forward-mode automatic differentiation (AD), which is particularly useful in large-scale models where activation memory dominates.
Computing the projected gradient

New convergence analysis to randomized subspace SGD (RS-SGD):

We investigate the theoretical properties of SGD combined with randomized subspace updates.
Recent work has studied stochastic methods that leverage random subspaces (e.g.,
5
,
7
).
However, existing convergence analyses are mostly in expectation, and high-probability guarantees remain limited, largely due to technical challenges.
In this work, we establish high-probability convergence guarantees for stochastic gradient methods with randomized subspace updates under sub-Gaussian gradient noise.
While in-expectation guarantees capture average performance, they do not quantify the probability that a single run deviates substantially from this average; high-probability analyses address this by bounding such deviations with probability at least
1-\delta

[
8
,
17
]
.
In modern machine learning, repeating training across multiple independent runs can be costly, which increases the value of single-run guarantees
[
15
]
.
Compared to the analysis of
5
, our results provide high-probability guarantees by adopting a sub-Gaussian noise assumption.
Our contribution is closely related in spirit to
15
, which analyzed standard SGD under sub-Gaussian noise; we extend this high-probability perspective to randomized subspace updates.
Now RS-SGD admits convergence guarantees in expectation under the bounded-variance assumption
and high-probability guarantees under a sub-Gaussian noise model.

However, these light-tailed noise conditions can be overly restrictive in modern machine-learning workloads, where stochastic gradients are often heavy-tailed
[
27
]
; this limitation is not caused by the randomized subspace projection but rather reflects the well-known sensitivity of SGD-type updates to heavy-tailed gradient noise
[
23
,
10
]
.
To overcome this issue, we leverage
normalized
stochastic gradient methods (NSGD),
which admit both in-expectation and high-probability convergence guarantees under the weaker bounded
p
-th moment
assumption stated later in
Section

2.3

[
11
]
.
Unlike clipping-based methods, whose analyses typically require problem- or noise-dependent parameters,
including the tail index
p

[
34
,
22
]
, NSGD can be analyzed without knowing

.

New randomized subspace method: Randomized Subspace Normalized SGD (RS-NSGD).

Building upon the randomized subspace framework, we propose a subspace-compatible normalized method.
A direct plug-in hybrid such as

x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\bar{\eta}\,\frac{P_{k}P_{k}^{\top}g_{k}}{\|g_{k}\|}

still requires the full-dimensional norm
\|g_{k}\|
, and thus falls outside the randomized-subspace model we target.
In contrast, RS-NSGD normalizes using only the available projected gradient
P_{k}^{\top}g_{k}
and performs the iteration

x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\bar{\eta}\,\frac{P_{k}P_{k}^{\top}g_{k}}{\|P_{k}^{\top}g_{k}\|},

thereby preserving the low-dimensional update structure of randomized subspace algorithms while gaining robustness to
heavy-tailed noise.
Because normalization introduces a nonlinear dependence on the random quantity
\|P_{k}^{\top}g_{k}\|
,
existing analytical tools for randomized subspace SGD do not directly apply, substantially complicating the analysis.

2.2
Properties of Haar Random Matrices

Following
14
, we construct a random matrix
P\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
using a Haar-random orthogonal matrix, because it satisfies the following four desirable properties:

(i)
For any
U\in O(d)
,

has the same distribution as
P
.

(ii)

\mathbb{E}[PP^{\top}]=I_{d}
.

(iii)

P^{\top}P=\frac{d}{r}I_{r}
, and hence
\|P\|=\sqrt{\frac{d}{r}}
.

(iv)
For any fixed

\|P^{\top}x\|>0

Conclusion

We proved high-probability convergence for randomized subspace SGD under sub-Gaussian noise, complementing prior expectation-based results. We also proposed RS-NSGD and established in-expectation and high-probability guarantees under a bounded
p
-th moment assumption, characterizing when it can improve oracle complexity over NSGD. Our guarantees rely on Haar-random subspace properties (
Section

2.2
). Extending them to other constructions (e.g., Gaussian or block-coordinate subspaces) is nontrivial; for instance, Gaussian matrices are unbounded, and block-coordinate schemes can satisfy
\mathbb{P}(P^{\top}x=0)>0
for some fixed
x\neq 0
. Establishing analogous guarantees by replacing the Haar-specific ingredients is an interesting direction for future work.

Appendix A
Related Work

A.1
Optimization via Random Subspaces

In many large-scale optimization problems arising in PDE-constrained optimization and machine learning, the memory required to store intermediate variables for reverse-mode automatic differentiation becomes prohibitive.
In such settings, forward-mode automatic differentiation becomes more attractive due to its significantly lower memory footprint.
This issue is particularly pronounced in modern machine learning models, such as deep neural networks
and large transformer architectures, where the activation memory dominates the memory usage.
However, naively computing
d
directional derivatives in a
d
-dimensional space is computationally very expensive.
One possible way to reduce computation is to work in random low-dimensional subspaces: Using a random matrix
P_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
with
r\ll d
, one approximates the full gradient
\nabla F(x_{k})
via directional derivatives along the columns of
P_{k}
and performs the update

x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\bar{\eta}P_{k}P_{k}^{\top}\nabla F(x_{k}).

This subspace-descent approach was developed for deterministic optimization
by Kozak et al.
[
14
]
.

Gradient compression techniques have been widely studied to alleviate communication bottlenecks in distributed SGD.
Classical approaches include quantization or error-feedback variants, along with low-rank projection methods such as
PowerSGD
[
26
,
12
,
30
]
. More recently, randomized linear compressors—including those based on Haar matrices—have been shown to provide convergence in non-convex optimization
[
5
]
. This provides theoretical support for using Haar matrices as randomized compressors in optimization.
Rather than communicating the full stochastic gradient
g_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
at the current iterate,
we draw a random
r
-dimensional subspace represented by a matrix
P_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}

(e.g., with orthonormal columns) and perform the projected update

x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\bar{\eta}\,P_{k}P_{k}^{\top}g_{k}\;=\;x_{k}-\bar{\eta}\,P_{k}\bigl(P_{k}^{\top}g_{k}\bigr).

In a minibatch setting where

g_{k}\;=\;\frac{1}{\bar{B}}\sum_{j=1}^{\bar{B}}\nabla f(x_{k},\xi_{k}^{j}),

the communicated quantity is the projected per-sample (or per-worker) gradient

u_{k}^{j}\;:=\;P_{k}^{\top}\nabla f(x_{k},\xi_{k}^{j})\in\mathbb{R}^{r},

and the aggregator forms

P_{k}^{\top}g_{k}\;=\;\frac{1}{\bar{B}}\sum_{j=1}^{\bar{B}}u_{k}^{j}.

Thus, each worker transmits an
r
-dimensional vector per iteration (instead of
d
dimensions),
and the server applies the update using the projected gradient

P_{k}P_{k}^{\top}g_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Flynn et al. analyze the convergence penalty introduced by compression and show that leveraging
matrix-smoothness leads to sharp convergence guarantees in expectation under the
bounded-variance assumption.

A.2
Stochastic Optimization under Heavy-Tailed Noise

Classical analyses have established in-expectation convergence for standard SGD
under the bounded-variance assumption
[
6
]
. More recently,
high-probability convergence has been shown under the sub-Gaussian
gradient noise assumption
[
15
]
. However, growing empirical and theoretical evidence indicates that stochastic gradients arising in modern
large-scale optimization can exhibit heavy-tailed behaviour, which may fall outside the classical bounded-variance assumption.
This phenomenon has been observed in a variety of settings, including deep neural network training

[
27
,
23
]
,
motivating a growing body of theoretical work on optimization under heavy-tailed noise.
In particular, clipped SGD and its variants have been extensively analyzed in this context, leading to convergence guarantees both in expectation and with high probability; see, e.g.,
[
34
,
22
]
.
However, convergence analyses often rely on carefully tuned clipping thresholds and assume knowledge of problem-dependent parameters (e.g., noise moments or smoothness constants).

A.3
Normalized Gradient Methods

Motivated by these limitations, there has been renewed interest in
normalized
gradient methods, going back to Nesterov’s normalized gradient schemes for stochastic optimization
[
20
]
.

11
study NSGD under heavy-tailed noise with bounded
p
-th moments, and establish both in-expectation and high-probability convergence guarantees in two regimes: (i) a fully parameter-free setting where the tail index
p
is unknown, and (ii) a parameter-aware setting where
p
is known.
Relatedly,
16
,
28
analyze momentum-based normalized methods under heavy-tailed noise and establish convergence guarantees in expectation; however, corresponding high-probability guarantees remain unavailable.
In fact, for momentum variants,
11
empirically suggest that obtaining a clean high-probability convergence bound with a simple
\log(1/\delta)
dependence may be challenging.

A.4
Compression Meets Heavy Tails: Limitations of Existing Work

Recently, the interaction between gradient compression and heavy-tailed noise has attracted attention.

3
analyze clipped SGD with gradient compression under heavy-tailed noise.
However, their high-probability convergence guarantees apply only in the parameter-aware regime where the tail index
p
is known.
Moreover, their guarantees require choosing the subspace dimension
r
to satisfy a lower bound
that depends on
d
,
T
, and
\delta
. In other words, for a fixed
r
, the condition can be rearranged
to yield a restriction on the allowable horizon

\epsilon
-dependent iteration complexity guarantee.

As another line of work,

13
analyze sign-based stochastic
methods (SignSGD and its variants) under heavy-tailed noise. They derive high-probability
convergence bounds, but these bounds exhibit an unfavourable dependence on the dimension

In particular, measured in the Euclidean norm, their bounds include terms of the form

when

A.5
Randomized Subspaces in Modern Large-Scale Training

We cite the following large-scale training works only as context: although they also reduce the update dimension,
their primary focus and algorithmic setting differ from ours.
In a related but distinct line of work, low-dimensional update ideas have recently gained visibility in large-scale
training—especially in LLM optimization—as practical tools for improving memory and/or communication efficiency.
Much of this literature is experiment-driven, but several works also develop stochastic convergence analyses
[

2
,

,
9

Existing analyses in this literature often focus on in-expectation guarantees under standard assumptions such as bounded variance;
high-probability bounds and heavy-tailed noise are typically not their primary focus. Rather, a central motivation in this line is
system-level efficiency in large-scale training, whereas our motivation is theory-first convergence analysis.

y=\frac{e}{2}\|u\|^{2}\sigma^{2}
.

Lemma B.3
.

Assume
\mathbb{E}[X]=0
and there exists
\sigma^{\prime}>0
such that

\displaystyle\forall u\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\qquad\mathbb{E}\big[\exp(\langle u,X\rangle)\big]\leq\exp(\|u\|^{2}\sigma^{\prime 2}).

Then with
\bar{\sigma}:=24\sqrt{e}\,\sigma^{\prime}
, we have

\displaystyle\forall|\lambda|\leq\frac{1}{\bar{\sigma}},\qquad\mathbb{E}\big[\exp(\lambda^{2}\|X\|^{2})\big]\leq 5^{d}\exp(\lambda^{2}\bar{\sigma}^{2}).

Proof.

We follow the one-dimensional argument underlying Proposition 2.6.1 in Vershynin,
and adapt it to our setting. We then combine this with a covering argument
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to control
\|X\|
.

Step 1

Fix
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
and
t>0
. For any
\lambda>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\langle u,X\rangle\geq t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\big(e^{\lambda\langle u,X\rangle}\geq e^{\lambda t}\big)\overset{(a)}{\leq}e^{-\lambda t}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{\lambda\langle u,X\rangle}\right]\overset{(b)}{\leq}\exp\!\left(-\lambda t+\lambda^{2}\sigma^{\prime 2}\|u\|^{2}\right).

(11)

Here (a) is Markov’s inequality, and (b) uses the assumed bound

\mathbb{E}[\exp(\langle v,X\rangle)]\leq\exp(\sigma^{\prime 2}\|v\|^{2})
with
v=\lambda u
.

Optimizing the right-hand side of (
11
) over
\lambda
gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\langle u,X\rangle\geq t)\leq\exp\!\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{4\sigma^{\prime 2}\|u\|^{2}}\right).

(12)

Applying (
12
) with
u
and with
-u
yields, for all
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(|\langle u,X\rangle|\geq t\big)\leq 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{4\sigma^{\prime 2}\|u\|^{2}}\right).

(13)

Step 2

For
p\geq 1
, we use the identity

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|Y|^{p}]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(|Y|\geq t)\,pt^{p-1}\,dt,

(14)

and apply it with
Y=\langle u,X\rangle
. Substituting (
13
) into
(
14
) gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\langle u,X\rangle|^{p}]

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}\int_{0}^{\infty}2\exp\!\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{4\sigma^{\prime 2}\|u\|^{2}}\right)\,pt^{p-1}\,dt

\displaystyle=p\Gamma\!\left(\frac{p}{2}\right)(2\sigma^{\prime}\|u\|)^{p}.

(15)

Here (a) uses the tail bound (
13
).

Using
\Gamma(x)\leq 3x^{x}
for
x\geq 1/2
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\langle u,X\rangle|^{p}]\leq 3p\left(\frac{p}{2}\right)^{p/2}(2\sigma^{\prime}\|u\|)^{p}.

(16)

Taking
p
-th roots in (
16
) and using
(3p)^{1/p}\leq 3
for
p\geq 1
yields

\displaystyle\forall p\geq 1,\qquad\big(\mathbb{E}[|\langle u,X\rangle|^{p}]\big)^{1/p}\leq 6\sigma^{\prime}\|u\|\sqrt{p}.

(17)

Step 3

Let
\hat{\sigma}:=6\sigma^{\prime}
. By (
17
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[(\langle u,X\rangle)^{2p}\big]\overset{(a)}{\leq}\big(\hat{\sigma}\|u\|\sqrt{2p}\big)^{2p}=\big(\hat{\sigma}^{2}\|u\|^{2}\cdot 2p\big)^{p}.

(18)

Here (a) is (
17
) applied with exponent
2p
.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\lambda^{2}(\langle u,X\rangle)^{2}}\big]

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{2p}\,\mathbb{E}[(\langle u,X\rangle)^{2p}]}{p!}

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}1+\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{2p}\,(\hat{\sigma}^{2}\|u\|^{2}\cdot 2p)^{p}}{p!}\overset{(b)}{\leq}1+\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}\big(\lambda^{2}\cdot 2\hat{\sigma}^{2}\|u\|^{2}e\big)^{p}.

(19)

Here (a) uses (
18
), and (b) uses
p!\geq(p/e)^{p}
.

Now restrict to
u\in B_{d}:=\{u:\|u\|\leq 1\}
and define
\sigma:=12\sqrt{e}\,\sigma^{\prime}
.
Then
2\hat{\sigma}^{2}e=\sigma^{2}/2
, and (
19
) gives, for
\|u\|\leq 1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\lambda^{2}(\langle u,X\rangle)^{2}}\big]\leq 1+\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2}\right)^{p}=\frac{1}{1-\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}/2}.

(20)

For
|\lambda|\leq 1/\sigma
we have
\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}/2\leq 1/2
, hence

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}/2}\overset{(a)}{\leq}\exp(\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}).

(21)

Here (a) uses
\frac{1}{1-x}\leq e^{2x}
for
x\in[0,1/2]
with
x=\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}/2
.

Combining (
20
) and (
21
) yields

\displaystyle\forall u\in B_{d},\ \forall|\lambda|\leq\frac{1}{\sigma},\qquad\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\lambda^{2}(\langle u,X\rangle)^{2}}\big]\leq e^{\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}}.

(22)

Step 4

Let
\mathcal{N}_{1/2}
be a
(1/2)
-net of
B_{d}
in Euclidean norm. Then

\displaystyle|\mathcal{N}_{1/2}|\overset{(a)}{\leq}\left(1+\frac{2}{1/2}\right)^{d}=5^{d}.

(23)

(a)  standard volumetric bound for Euclidean nets.

Moreover, since
\|X\|=\max_{v\in B_{d}}\langle v,X\rangle
, the net property implies

\displaystyle\|X\|\overset{(a)}{\leq}2\max_{z\in\mathcal{N}_{1/2}}\langle z,X\rangle,\qquad\text{and hence}\qquad\|X\|^{2}\overset{(b)}{\leq}4\max_{z\in\mathcal{N}_{1/2}}\langle z,X\rangle^{2}.

(24)

(a)  approximation of the supremum over
B_{d}
by a
(1/2)
-net,
(b)  squaring both sides.

Therefore, for
|\lambda|\leq 1/(2\sigma)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\lambda^{2}\|X\|^{2}}\big]\overset{(a)}{\leq}\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{4\lambda^{2}\max_{z\in\mathcal{N}_{1/2}}\langle z,X\rangle^{2}}\Big]\overset{(b)}{\leq}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{N}_{1/2}}\mathbb{E}\big[e^{4\lambda^{2}\langle z,X\rangle^{2}}\big].

(25)

(a)  using (
24
),
(b)  using
e^{\max_{i}a_{i}}\leq\sum_{i}e^{a_{i}}
.

For each
z\in\mathcal{N}_{1/2}\subset B_{d}
, applying (
22
)
with parameter
2\lambda
yields

\displaystyle\sum_{z\in\mathcal{N}_{1/2}}\mathbb{E}\big[e^{4\lambda^{2}\langle z,X\rangle^{2}}\big]\overset{(a)}{\leq}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{N}_{1/2}}e^{4\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}}=|\mathcal{N}_{1/2}|\,e^{4\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}}\overset{(b)}{\leq}5^{d}e^{4\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}}.

(26)

(a)  applying (
22
) with
u=z
,
(b)  using (
23
).

Finally, define
\bar{\sigma}:=2\sigma=24\sqrt{e}\,\sigma^{\prime}
. Then for
|\lambda|\leq 1/\bar{\sigma}

we have
|\lambda|\leq 1/(2\sigma)
and
4\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}=\lambda^{2}\bar{\sigma}^{2}
, so

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\lambda^{2}\|X\|^{2}}\big]\leq 5^{d}\,e^{\lambda^{2}\bar{\sigma}^{2}}.

(27)

∎

Lemma B.5
.

Assume
F
is
L
-smooth and bounded below by
F^{\ast}
.
Assume
\mathbb{E}[\nabla f(x,\xi)]=\nabla F(x)
and that

\|\nabla f(x,\xi)-\nabla F(x)\|
is
\sigma
-sub-Gaussian (in the sense of
Appendix

B
).
Let
P_{k}
be i.i.d. Haar random matrices, and let
\{x_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
be generated by the update rule

x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\bar{\eta}P_{k}P_{k}^{\top}g_{k},

where
g_{k}
denotes a mini-batch stochastic gradient estimator at
x_{k}
.
Define the events

A_{k}:=\Big\{\|P_{k}^{\top}\nabla F(x_{k})\|^{2}\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|^{2}\Big\},\qquad X:=\sum_{j=0}^{T-1}\mathbf{1}_{A_{j}}.

Let

\mu\coloneqq\begin{cases}1-I_{\frac{r}{2d}}\!\left(\frac{r}{2},\frac{d-r}{2}\right),&r<d,\\
1,&r=d,\end{cases}

where
I_{x}(a,b)
is the regularized incomplete beta function.
Then for all
s\in(0,1)
,

\mathbb{P}\big(X\leq(1-s)\mu T\big)\leq\exp\!\left(-\frac{s^{2}}{2}\mu T\right).

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{k}:=\sigma(g_{0},P_{0},\dots,g_{k-1},P_{k-1})
.
We first consider the case

r<d
.
Define

p_{k}:=\mathbb{P}(A_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k})=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{A_{k}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}].

For
\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|\neq 0
, the identity
p_{k}=\mu
follows from rotational invariance;
this argument appears in the proof of Lemma 1 in
14
.
If
\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|=0
, then
p_{k}=1
.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}p_{k}\geq\mu T.

(30)

Let
\psi(\lambda):=e^{-\lambda}-1+\lambda
and fix
\lambda>0
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{-\lambda(\mathbf{1}_{A_{k}}-p_{k})}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle=p_{k}e^{-\lambda(1-p_{k})}+(1-p_{k})e^{\lambda p_{k}}

\displaystyle=e^{\lambda p_{k}}\big(p_{k}e^{-\lambda}+(1-p_{k})\big)

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}e^{\lambda p_{k}}\exp\!\big(p_{k}(e^{-\lambda}-1)\big)=\exp\!\big(\psi(\lambda)p_{k}\big).

(31)

(a)  using
1+x\leq e^{x}
with
x=p_{k}(e^{-\lambda}-1)
.

Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{-\lambda(\mathbf{1}_{A_{k}}-p_{k})-\psi(\lambda)p_{k}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]\leq 1.

(32)

Define

M_{k}:=\exp\!\left(-\lambda\sum_{j=0}^{k}(\mathbf{1}_{A_{j}}-p_{j})-\psi(\lambda)\sum_{j=0}^{k}p_{j}\right).

By (
32
),
\{M_{k}\}
is a supermartingale, and thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[M_{T-1}]\leq 1.

(33)

Fix
s\in(0,1)
. Using (
30
), for any
\lambda>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X\leq(1-s)\mu T)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\!\left(X\leq(1-s)\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}p_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\!\left(e^{-\lambda X}\geq e^{-\lambda(1-s)\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}p_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\!\left(e^{-\lambda\left(X-\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}p_{k}\right)}\geq e^{\lambda s\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}p_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\!\left(e^{-\lambda\left(X-\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}p_{k}\right)-\psi(\lambda)\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}p_{k}}\geq e^{(\lambda s-\psi(\lambda))\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}p_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}\mathbb{P}\!\left(M_{T-1}\geq\exp\!\left((\lambda s-\psi(\lambda))\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}p_{k}\right)\right).

(34)

Here, (a) follows from the definition of
M_{k}
.

Choose
\lambda=-\log(1-s)
, so that

\displaystyle\lambda s-\psi(\lambda)=s+(1-s)\log(1-s)\overset{(a)}{\geq}\frac{s^{2}}{2}.

(35)

(a)  the standard inequality
s+(1-s)\log(1-s)\geq s^{2}/2
for
s\in(0,1)
.

Combining (
34
), (
30
), and (
35
) yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X\leq(1-s)\mu T)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\!\left(M_{T-1}\geq e^{\frac{s^{2}}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}p_{k}}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\!\left(M_{T-1}\geq e^{\frac{s^{2}}{2}\mu T}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}\mathbb{E}[M_{T-1}]\,e^{-\frac{s^{2}}{2}\mu T}\overset{(b)}{\leq}\exp\!\left(-\frac{s^{2}}{2}\mu T\right).

(36)

(a)  Markov’s inequality applied to
M_{T-1}
.
(b)  using (
33
).
This completes the proof for the case
r<d
.

When
r=d
, we have
\mu=1
and
P_{k}P_{k}^{\top}=I_{d}
, hence

\|P_{k}^{\top}\nabla F(x_{k})\|=\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|
for all
k
.
Therefore
A_{k}
holds for every
k
, and thus
X=T
.
Since
(1-s)\mu T=(1-s)T<T
, it follows that

\mathbb{P}\!\left(X\leq(1-s)\mu T\right)=0,

which completes the proof.

Theorem B.6

(Generalization of
Theorem

4.2
)
.

Suppose that
F
satisfies
Sections

2.3
and
2.3
, and that the gradient
satisfies
Sections

2.3
and
2.3
.
Let
\{x_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
be the sequence generated by the RS-SGD iteration.
Let
\bar{B}\coloneqq\lceil\max\{1,BT^{q}\}\rceil
for
B>0
and
q>0
,

\alpha\coloneqq 24\sqrt{\frac{e(e+1)}{2}}
,
and
\bar{\eta}=\frac{r}{Ld}

Further define

\mu\coloneqq\begin{cases}1-I_{\frac{r}{2d}}\!\left(\frac{r}{2},\frac{d-r}{2}\right),&r<d,\\

(37)

where

Then for any

and

\displaystyle\min_{0\leq k\leq T-1}\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|^{2}\leq\frac{4L\frac{d}{r}\Delta_{0}}{\mu T-\sqrt{2\mu T\log\!\left(\frac{1}{\delta^{\prime}}\right)}}

\displaystyle+\frac{2\frac{d}{r}\left(\frac{\alpha^{2}\sigma^{2}}{\bar{B}}\right)\bigl(d\log 5+1\bigr)}{\mu-\sqrt{2\frac{\mu}{T}\log\!\left(\frac{1}{\delta^{\prime}}\right)}}+\frac{2\frac{d}{r}\left(\frac{\alpha^{2}\sigma^{2}}{\bar{B}}\right)\log\!\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)}{\mu T-\sqrt{2\mu T\log\!\left(\frac{1}{\delta^{\prime}}\right)}}.

Remark.

Setting
\delta\leftarrow\delta/2
and
\delta^{\prime}\leftarrow\delta/2
in
Theorem

B.6
yields
Theorem

4.2
.

Proof.

Let
n_{k}:=g_{k}-\nabla F(x_{k})

\displaystyle F(x_{k+1})-F(x_{k})

\displaystyle\leq\langle\nabla F(x_{k}),x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}-\bar{\eta}\langle\nabla F(x_{k}),P_{k}P_{k}^{\top}g_{k}\rangle+\frac{L\bar{\eta}^{2}}{2}\|P_{k}P_{k}^{\top}g_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{=}-\bar{\eta}\langle\nabla F(x_{k}),P_{k}P_{k}^{\top}(\nabla F(x_{k})+n_{k})\rangle+\frac{L\bar{\eta}^{2}}{2}\|P_{k}P_{k}^{\top}(\nabla F(x_{k})+n_{k})\|^{2},

(38)

(a)  uses

(b)  uses

\displaystyle\bar{\eta}\Big(1-\frac{L\bar{\eta}}{2}\cdot\frac{d}{r}\Big)\|P_{k}^{\top}\nabla F(x_{k})\|^{2}+F(x_{k+1})-F(x_{k})\leq\Big(L\bar{\eta}^{2}\frac{d}{r}-\bar{\eta}\Big)\langle\nabla F(x_{k}),P_{k}P_{k}^{\top}n_{k}\rangle+\frac{L\bar{\eta}^{2}}{2}\cdot\frac{d}{r}\,\|P_{k}^{\top}n_{k}\|^{2}.

(39)

Substituting

\displaystyle\frac{\bar{\eta}}{2}\|P_{k}^{\top}\nabla F(x_{k})\|^{2}+F(x_{k+1})-F(x_{k})\leq\frac{\bar{\eta}}{2}\|P_{k}^{\top}n_{k}\|^{2}.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{k}:=\sigma(g_{0},P_{0},\ldots,g_{k-1},P_{k-1})

\bar{\sigma}\coloneqq\alpha\cdot\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\bar{B}}}

B
provides, for

\mathbb{E}\big[\exp(\lambda^{2}\|n_{k}\|^{2})\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}\big]\leq 5^{d}\exp(\lambda^{2}\bar{\sigma}^{2}).

(41)

which requires the condition

\lambda^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad w\leq\frac{2}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}\frac{r}{\bar{\eta}d}.

(42)

Assume (
42

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[e^{Z_{k}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[\exp(wC_{k}-d\log 5)\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}\big]

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}e^{-d\log 5}\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\exp\!\big(\tfrac{w\bar{\eta}}{2}\|P_{k}^{\top}n_{k}\|^{2}\big)\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}\Big]

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{\leq}e^{-d\log 5}\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\exp\!\big(\tfrac{w\bar{\eta}}{2}\cdot\tfrac{d}{r}\|n_{k}\|^{2}\big)\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}\Big]

(a)  uses (

),
(b)  uses

(c)  applies
Appendix

B
with the choice (

)
(which is valid under (

We next prove by backward induction that, for all

\mathbb{E}\big[e^{S_{k}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}\big]\leq\exp\!\Big(\tfrac{w\bar{\eta}}{2}\cdot\tfrac{d}{r}\bar{\sigma}^{2}\,(T-k)\Big).

)

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}\exp\!\Big(\tfrac{w\bar{\eta}}{2}\cdot\tfrac{d}{r}\bar{\sigma}^{2}\,(T-k-1)\Big)\,\mathbb{E}\big[e^{Z_{k}}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}\big]

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{\leq}\exp\!\Big(\tfrac{w\bar{\eta}}{2}\cdot\tfrac{d}{r}\bar{\sigma}^{2}\,(T-k)\Big),

(47)

By definition of

\displaystyle S_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\bigl(wC_{k}-d\log 5\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\Bigl(w\Bigl(\tfrac{\bar{\eta}}{2}\|P_{k}^{\top}\nabla F(x_{k})\|^{2}+F(x_{k+1})-F(x_{k})\Bigr)-d\log 5\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{w\bar{\eta}}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\|P_{k}^{\top}\nabla F(x_{k})\|^{2}+w\bigl(F(x_{T})-F(x_{0})\bigr)-dT\log 5.

Choose

w\coloneqq\frac{2r}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}\bar{\eta}d},

so that
(
43
) is satisfied.
Combining this choice with (
47
), we conclude that with probability
at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\frac{w\bar{\eta}}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\|P_{k}^{\top}\nabla F(x_{k})\|^{2}+w\bigl(F(x_{T})-F(x_{0})\bigr)-dT\log 5\leq\frac{w\bar{\eta}}{2}\cdot\frac{d}{r}\bar{\sigma}^{2}T+\log\frac{1}{\delta}.

Using
F(x_{0})-F(x_{T})\leq F(x_{0})-F^{\ast}\leq\Delta_{0}
,

\frac{w\bar{\eta}}{2}=\frac{1}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}\cdot\frac{r}{d}
,
and
\bar{\eta}=\frac{r}{Ld}
, this inequality simplifies to

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\|P_{k}^{\top}\nabla F(x_{k})\|^{2}\leq 2L\frac{d}{r}\Delta_{0}+\frac{d}{r}\bar{\sigma}^{2}dT\log 5+\frac{d}{r}\bar{\sigma}^{2}T+\frac{d}{r}\bar{\sigma}^{2}\log\frac{1}{\delta}.

(48)

Next, define the events

A_{k}\coloneqq\Bigl\{\|P_{k}^{\top}\nabla F(x_{k})\|^{2}\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|^{2}\Bigr\},\qquad X\coloneqq\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\mathbf{1}_{A_{k}}.

Then

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\|P_{k}^{\top}\nabla F(x_{k})\|^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|^{2}\mathbf{1}_{A_{k}}\geq\frac{X}{2}\min_{0\leq k\leq T-1}\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|^{2}.

(49)

By
Appendix

B
, for any
s\in(0,1)
,

\mathbb{P}\bigl(X\leq(1-s)\mu T\bigr)\leq\exp\!\left(-\frac{s^{2}}{2}\mu T\right).

(50)

Fix
\delta^{\prime}\in(0,1)
and assume
2\log(1/\delta^{\prime})<\mu T
.
Setting

s\coloneqq\sqrt{\frac{2}{\mu T}\log\frac{1}{\delta^{\prime}}},

which satisfies
s\in(0,1)
under this condition, (
50
) yields

\mathbb{P}\!\left(X\leq\mu T-\sqrt{2\mu T\log\frac{1}{\delta^{\prime}}}\right)\leq\delta^{\prime}.

(51)

Therefore, with probability at least
1-\delta^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\|P_{k}^{\top}\nabla F(x_{k})\|^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\mu T-\sqrt{2\mu T\log\frac{1}{\delta^{\prime}}}\Bigr)\min_{0\leq k\leq T-1}\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|^{2}.

(52)

Since
2\log(1/\delta^{\prime})<\mu T
, we have

\mu T-\sqrt{2\mu T\log(1/\delta^{\prime})}>0.

Therefore,
combining (
48
) and (
52
) and applying a union
bound shows that, with probability at least
1-\delta-\delta^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle\min_{0\leq k\leq T-1}\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|^{2}\leq\frac{4L\frac{d}{r}\Delta_{0}}{\mu T-\sqrt{2\mu T\log\frac{1}{\delta^{\prime}}}}+\frac{2\frac{d}{r}\bar{\sigma}^{2}(d\log 5+1)}{\mu-\sqrt{2\frac{\mu}{T}\log\frac{1}{\delta^{\prime}}}}+\frac{2\frac{d}{r}\bar{\sigma}^{2}\log\frac{1}{\delta}}{\mu T-\sqrt{2\mu T\log\frac{1}{\delta^{\prime}}}}.

This completes the proof.
∎

4.2

and

),
the iteration complexity satisfies

Proposition C.1

(Restatement of
Section

5
)
.

Let
x\neq 0
. Suppose
P
is constructed from a Haar-random orthogonal matrix.
Then
\|P^{\top}x\|\neq 0
almost surely, and there exists a constant

\tau>0
such that

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{PP^{\top}x}{\|P^{\top}x\|}\right]=\tau\,\frac{x}{\|x\|}.

Specifically,
\tau
is given by

\tau=\sqrt{\frac{d}{r}}\,\frac{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{r+1}{2}\right)\Gamma\!\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)\Gamma\!\left(\frac{d+1}{2}\right)}.

Here,
\Gamma(\cdot)
denotes the Gamma function.

Moreover,
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\leq\tau\leq 1.

Proof.

Let
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be defined by
g(x)\coloneqq\|P^{\top}x\|
.
For
x\neq 0
, we have
\|P^{\top}x\|\neq 0
almost surely, and thus
g
is differentiable at
x
almost surely with

\nabla g(x)=\frac{PP^{\top}x}{\|P^{\top}x\|}.

(53)

Moreover,

\left\|\nabla g(x)\right\|=\left\|\frac{PP^{\top}x}{\|P^{\top}x\|}\right\|\leq\|P\|\leq\sqrt{\frac{d}{r}}.

(54)

Hence we may interchange gradient and expectation, obtaining

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{PP^{\top}x}{\|P^{\top}x\|}\right]=\mathbb{E}[\nabla g(x)]=\nabla\mathbb{E}[g(x)].

(55)

We next compute
\mathbb{E}[g(x)]
, which can be written as

\mathbb{E}[g(x)]=\mathbb{E}[\|P^{\top}x\|]=\|x\|\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left\|P^{\top}\frac{x}{\|x\|}\right\|\right].

(56)

We use the following fact (see the proof of Lemma 1 in
[
14
]
): the random variable

Y\coloneqq\frac{r}{d}\left\|P^{\top}\frac{x}{\|x\|}\right\|^{2}

(57)

follows a Beta distribution,

Y\sim\mathrm{Beta}\!\left(\frac{r}{2},\frac{d-r}{2}\right).

(58)

Let
\alpha\coloneqq\frac{r}{2}
and
\beta\coloneqq\frac{d-r}{2}
,
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\sqrt{Y}]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{y}\,\frac{y^{\alpha-1}(1-y)^{\beta-1}}{\mathrm{B}(\alpha,\beta)}\,dy=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{y^{\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}(1-y)^{\beta-1}}{\mathrm{B}(\alpha,\beta)}\,dy

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{B}\!\left(\alpha+\frac{1}{2},\beta\right)}{\mathrm{B}(\alpha,\beta)}=\frac{\Gamma\!\left(\alpha+\frac{1}{2}\right)\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma\!\left(\alpha+\beta+\frac{1}{2}\right)\Gamma(\alpha)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{r+1}{2}\right)\Gamma\!\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{d+1}{2}\right)\Gamma\!\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)}.

(59)

By (
57
),
\left\|P^{\top}\frac{x}{\|x\|}\right\|=\sqrt{\frac{d}{r}}\,\sqrt{Y}
, hence

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left\|P^{\top}\frac{x}{\|x\|}\right\|\right]=\sqrt{\frac{d}{r}}\,\mathbb{E}[\sqrt{Y}]=\sqrt{\frac{d}{r}}\,\frac{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{r+1}{2}\right)\Gamma\!\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{d+1}{2}\right)\Gamma\!\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)}.

(60)

Substituting (
60
) into (
56
) yields

\mathbb{E}[g(x)]=\tau\,\|x\|,\qquad\tau\coloneqq\sqrt{\frac{d}{r}}\,\frac{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{r+1}{2}\right)\Gamma\!\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)\Gamma\!\left(\frac{d+1}{2}\right)}.

(61)

Taking the gradient of (
61
) and using (
55
), we conclude that

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{PP^{\top}x}{\|P^{\top}x\|}\right]=\nabla\mathbb{E}[g(x)]=\tau\,\frac{x}{\|x\|}.

It remains to show
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\leq\tau\leq 1
.
For the upper bound, since
\sqrt{\cdot}
is concave and
Y
is Beta with mean
\mathbb{E}[Y]=\frac{r}{d}
,

\tau=\sqrt{\frac{d}{r}}\mathbb{E}[\sqrt{Y}]\overset{(a)}{\leq}\sqrt{\frac{d}{r}}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[Y]}=\sqrt{\frac{d}{r}}\sqrt{\frac{r}{d}}=1.

(62)

Here, (a) follows from Jensen’s inequality.
Therefore,
\tau\leq 1
.
To derive the lower bound on
\tau
, we invoke Wendel’s inequality
[
32
]
.
By Wendel’s inequality, for any
x>0
and
0<s<1
,

\left(\frac{x}{x+s}\right)^{1-s}\leq\frac{\Gamma(x+s)}{x^{s}\Gamma(x)}\leq 1.

Setting
s=\frac{1}{2}
and multiplying by
\sqrt{x}
, we obtain

\sqrt{x}\left(\frac{x}{x+\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{1/2}\leq\frac{\Gamma\!\left(x+\frac{1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma(x)}\leq\sqrt{x}.

(63)

For
x\geq\tfrac{1}{2}
, the ratio
\frac{x}{x+\frac{1}{2}}
is minimized at
x=\tfrac{1}{2}
,
which gives
\frac{x}{x+\frac{1}{2}}\geq\frac{1}{2}
.
Taking square roots on both sides yields

\left(\frac{x}{x+\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{1/2}\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}.

(64)

Combining (
63
) (left inequality) with (
64
) and taking
x=\frac{r}{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}
gives

\frac{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{r}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)}\overset{}{\geq}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{\frac{r}{2}}.

(65)

Moreover, from (
63
) (right inequality) we obtain

\Gamma(x)/\Gamma(x+\frac{1}{2})\geq 1/\sqrt{x}
for all
x>0
, hence for
x=\frac{d}{2}
,

\frac{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{d}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\right)}\overset{}{\geq}\sqrt{\frac{2}{d}}.

(66)

Using (
65
) and (
66
) in the definition of
\tau
in (
61
), we obtain

\displaystyle\tau

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{d}{r}}\,\frac{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{r}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)}\cdot\frac{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{d}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\right)}

\displaystyle{\geq}\sqrt{\frac{d}{r}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{\frac{r}{2}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{2}{d}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}.

(67)

This completes the proof.
∎

Proposition C.2

(Restatement of
Section

5.1
)
.

Let
S
be a
d\times d
square matrix, and
P
be a Haar matrix. Then,

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{v^{\top}PP^{\top}SPP^{\top}v}{\|P^{\top}v\|^{2}}\right]=\frac{d(r-1)}{r(d-1)}\frac{v^{\top}Sv}{\|v\|^{2}}+\frac{d-r}{r(d-1)}\operatorname{tr}(S).

Proof.

Fix a vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
There exists an orthogonal matrix
\hat{Q}\in O(d)
such that

v=\|v\|\hat{Q}e_{1},

where
e_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the vector whose first component is
1
and whose remaining components are
0
.

By the invariance of the Haar matrix,
\hat{Q}P
has the same distribution as
P
.
Define

\hat{S}\coloneqq\hat{Q}^{\top}S\hat{Q}.

Then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{v^{\top}PP^{\top}SPP^{\top}v}{\|P^{\top}v\|^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{v^{\top}\hat{Q}PP^{\top}\hat{Q}^{\top}S\hat{Q}PP^{\top}\hat{Q}^{\top}v}{\|P^{\top}\hat{Q}^{\top}v\|^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{\|v\|e_{1}^{\top}PP^{\top}\hat{Q}^{\top}S\hat{Q}PP^{\top}\|v\|e_{1}}{\|P^{\top}\|v\|e_{1}\|^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{e_{1}^{\top}PP^{\top}\hat{S}PP^{\top}e_{1}}{\|P^{\top}e_{1}\|^{2}}\right].

(68)

Define the random vector

u\coloneqq\frac{PP^{\top}e_{1}}{\|P^{\top}e_{1}\|}.

Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{e_{1}^{\top}PP^{\top}\hat{S}PP^{\top}e_{1}}{\|P^{\top}e_{1}\|^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[u^{\top}\hat{S}u]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}\!\left(\mathbb{E}[uu^{\top}]\hat{S}\right),

(69)

where we used the identity

\mathbb{E}[u^{\top}\hat{S}u]=\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{tr}(u^{\top}\hat{S}u)]=\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{tr}(uu^{\top}\hat{S})]=\operatorname{tr}(\mathbb{E}[uu^{\top}]\hat{S})
.

Combining (
68
) and (
69
), we obtain

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{v^{\top}PP^{\top}SPP^{\top}v}{\|P^{\top}v\|^{2}}\right]=\operatorname{tr}(M\hat{S}),\quad M\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[uu^{\top}].

We now characterize the structure of
M
.
Consider the subset

\mathcal{S}\coloneqq\{Q\in O(d)\mid Qe_{1}=e_{1}\}.

For any
Q\in\mathcal{S}
, by the invariance of the Haar matrix,

\displaystyle Q^{\top}MQ

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[Q^{\top}uu^{\top}Q]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{Q^{\top}PP^{\top}e_{1}e_{1}^{\top}PP^{\top}Q}{\|P^{\top}e_{1}\|^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{Q^{\top}PP^{\top}Qe_{1}e_{1}^{\top}Q^{\top}PP^{\top}Q}{\|P^{\top}Qe_{1}\|^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{(Q^{\top}P)(Q^{\top}P)^{\top}e_{1}e_{1}^{\top}(Q^{\top}P)(Q^{\top}P)^{\top}}{\|(Q^{\top}P)^{\top}e_{1}\|^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{PP^{\top}e_{1}e_{1}^{\top}PP^{\top}}{\|P^{\top}e_{1}\|^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[uu^{\top}]

\displaystyle=M.

This invariance implies strong structural constraints on
M
.
First, fix
i\in\{2,\dots,d\}
and define the matrix

A_{i}\coloneqq\diag(1,\dots,1,-1,1,\dots,1)\in O(d-1),

where the
(i-1)
-th diagonal entry is
-1
and the remaining
d-2
diagonal
entries are equal to
1
.
Define

Q\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}1&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&A_{i}\end{pmatrix}.

Then
Q\in\mathcal{S}
by construction.

Since
Q
has
-1
only at the
i
-th diagonal entry and
1
at all other
diagonal entries, we compute
Q^{\top}MQ
explicitly.
Writing
M=(m_{jk})_{1\leq j,k\leq d}
, we have

\displaystyle M=Q^{\top}MQ=\begin{pmatrix}m_{1,1}&\cdots&m_{1,i-1}&-m_{1,i}&m_{1,i+1}&\cdots&m_{1,d}\\[4.0pt]
\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\[4.0pt]
m_{i-1,1}&\cdots&m_{i-1,i-1}&-m_{i-1,i}&m_{i-1,i+1}&\cdots&m_{i-1,d}\\[6.0pt]
-m_{i,1}&\cdots&-m_{i,i-1}&m_{i,i}&-m_{i,i+1}&\cdots&-m_{i,d}\\[6.0pt]
m_{i+1,1}&\cdots&m_{i+1,i-1}&-m_{i+1,i}&m_{i+1,i+1}&\cdots&m_{i+1,d}\\[4.0pt]
\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\[4.0pt]
m_{d,1}&\cdots&m_{d,i-1}&-m_{d,i}&m_{d,i+1}&\cdots&m_{d,d}\end{pmatrix}.

Since this equality holds for all
i\in\{2,\dots,d\}
, it follows that

m_{jk}=0\quad\text{for all }j\neq k,

and hence
M
must be a diagonal matrix.
Therefore,
M
can be written as

M=\begin{pmatrix}m_{1,1}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&m_{2,2}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&m_{d,d}\end{pmatrix}.

Next, take
i,j\in\{2,\dots,d\}
with
i\leq j
, and let
Q
be the permutation
matrix corresponding to the transposition
(i,j)
, i.e.,

Q\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}1&&&&&&\\
&\ddots&&&&&\\
&&0&&1&&\\
&&&\ddots&&&\\
&&1&&0&&\\
&&&&&\ddots&\\
&&&&&&1\end{pmatrix}.

The lower-right block of
Q
is a
(d-1)\times(d-1)
permutation matrix, and
hence belongs to
O(d-1)
.
Therefore,
Q\in\mathcal{S}
.

Using again the invariance
Q^{\top}MQ=M
, we obtain

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle=Q^{\top}MQ

\displaystyle=\diag(m_{1,1},\dots,m_{i-1,i-1},m_{j,j},m_{i+1,i+1},\dots,m_{j-1,j-1},m_{i,i},m_{j+1,j+1},\dots,m_{d,d}).

Since this holds for arbitrary
i,j\in\{2,\dots,d\}
with
i\leq j
, we conclude
that

m_{2,2}=m_{3,3}=\cdots=m_{d,d}.

Defining
\alpha\coloneqq m_{1,1}
and
\beta\coloneqq m_{2,2}
, we finally
obtain

M=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&0&\cdots&0\\
0&\beta&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&\beta\end{pmatrix}=(\alpha-\beta)e_{1}e_{1}^{\top}+\beta I_{d}.

We now determine
\alpha
and
\beta
.
First,

\displaystyle e_{1}^{\top}Me_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[e_{1}^{\top}uu^{\top}e_{1}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[e_{1}^{\top}\frac{PP^{\top}e_{1}e_{1}^{\top}PP^{\top}}{\|P^{\top}e_{1}\|^{2}}e_{1}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\|P^{\top}e_{1}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle=e_{1}^{\top}\mathbb{E}[PP^{\top}]e_{1}

\displaystyle=1.

On the other hand,

e_{1}^{\top}Me_{1}=\alpha,

and hence
\alpha=1
.

Next, we compute the trace of
M
:

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}(M)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}(\mathbb{E}[uu^{\top}])

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{tr}(uu^{\top})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\|u\|^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{e_{1}^{\top}PP^{\top}PP^{\top}e_{1}}{\|P^{\top}e_{1}\|^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{r}.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}(M)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}((1-\beta)e_{1}e_{1}^{\top}+\beta I_{d})

\displaystyle=(1-\beta)+\beta d.

Equating the two expressions yields

(d-1)\beta=\frac{d}{r}-1,\quad\beta=\frac{d-r}{r(d-1)}.

Therefore,

M=\frac{d(r-1)}{r(d-1)}e_{1}e_{1}^{\top}+\frac{d-r}{r(d-1)}I_{d}.

Finally,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{v^{\top}PP^{\top}SPP^{\top}v}{\|P^{\top}v\|^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}(M\hat{S})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}\!\left(\frac{d(r-1)}{r(d-1)}e_{1}e_{1}^{\top}\hat{S}+\frac{d-r}{r(d-1)}\hat{S}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{d(r-1)}{r(d-1)}e_{1}^{\top}\hat{S}e_{1}+\frac{d-r}{r(d-1)}\operatorname{tr}(\hat{S})

\displaystyle=\frac{d(r-1)}{r(d-1)}\frac{v^{\top}Sv}{\|v\|^{2}}+\frac{d-r}{r(d-1)}\operatorname{tr}(S),

which completes the proof.
∎

)

2.3

, and that the gradient

F(x+v)\leq F(x)+\langle\nabla F(x),v\rangle+\frac{1}{2}L\|v\|^{2}.

Applying this with
x=x_{k}
and
v=x_{k+1}-x_{k}=-\bar{\eta}G_{k}
, we obtain

\displaystyle F(x_{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq F(x_{k})-\bar{\eta}\,\langle\nabla F(x_{k}),G_{k}\rangle+\frac{\bar{\eta}^{2}}{2}\,L\|G_{k}\|^{2}.

By the same argument as in the proof of
Theorem

D.1
, we have

\langle\nabla F(x_{k}),G_{k}\rangle=\|P_{k}^{\top}\nabla F(x_{k})\|.

Moreover, since
\|P_{k}\|\leq\sqrt{d/r}
, it follows that

\|G_{k}\|\leq\sqrt{d/r}
and hence

L\|G_{k}\|^{2}\leq L\frac{d}{r}.

Substituting these bounds yields

F(x_{k+1})\leq F(x_{k})-\bar{\eta}\,\|P_{k}^{\top}\nabla F(x_{k})\|+\frac{\bar{\eta}^{2}}{2}\frac{d}{r}L.

Rearranging gives

\bar{\eta}\,\|P_{k}^{\top}\nabla F(x_{k})\|\leq F(x_{k})-F(x_{k+1})+\frac{\bar{\eta}^{2}}{2}\frac{d}{r}L.

Summing over
k=0,\dots,T-1
and using
F(x_{T})\geq F^{*}
yields

\bar{\eta}\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\|P_{k}^{\top}\nabla F(x_{k})\|\leq\Delta_{0}+\frac{\bar{\eta}^{2}}{2}\frac{d}{r}LT.

(83)

Define

\psi_{k}\coloneqq\|P_{k}^{\top}\nabla F(x_{k})\|,\qquad\phi_{k}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[\psi_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}],\qquad D_{k}\coloneqq\bar{\eta}(\phi_{k-1}-\psi_{k-1}),\quad k\geq 1,

with
D_{0}\coloneqq 0
.

Then
(D_{k},\mathcal{F}_{k})
is a martingale difference sequence.
Moreover, since
0\leq\psi_{k}\leq\sqrt{d/r}\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|
, we have

|D_{k}|\leq\bar{\eta}\sqrt{\frac{d}{r}}\,\|\nabla F(x_{k-1})\|.

Define

S_{k}\coloneqq\bar{\eta}\sqrt{\frac{d}{r}}\,\|\nabla F(x_{k-1})\|.

Then
S_{k}
is
\mathcal{F}_{k-1}
-measurable and satisfies

\frac{D_{k}^{2}}{S_{k}^{2}}\leq 1.

Consequently,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\exp\!\left(\frac{D_{k}^{2}}{S_{k}^{2}}\right)\Bigm|\mathcal{F}_{k-1}\right]\leq e.

Applying Lemma 11 in
From Gradient Clipping to Normalization
,
for any
\lambda>0
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\sum_{t=1}^{T}D_{t}\leq\frac{3}{4}\lambda\sum_{t=1}^{T}S_{t}^{2}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\frac{1}{\delta}.

(84)

,

S_{k}+\tfrac{1}{n}

,

\phi_{k}=\mathbb{E}[\psi_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]=\tau\,\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|.

Combining (

) with (

),
we obtain, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\bar{\eta}\tau\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|

\displaystyle\leq\Delta_{0}+\frac{\bar{\eta}^{2}}{2}\frac{d}{r}LT+\frac{3}{4}\lambda\bar{\eta}^{2}\frac{d}{r}\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|^{2}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\frac{1}{\delta}.

(85)

By
L
-smoothness,

\|\nabla F(x_{k+1})\|\leq\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|+\bar{\eta}L\sqrt{\frac{d}{r}}.

Iterating this bound yields, for all
k\in\{0,\dots,T\}
,

\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|\leq\|\nabla F(x_{0})\|+k\,\bar{\eta}L\sqrt{\frac{d}{r}}\leq\|\nabla F(x_{0})\|+T\,\bar{\eta}L\sqrt{\frac{d}{r}}.

(86)

We now choose

\lambda\coloneqq\frac{2}{3}\,\frac{\tau}{\bar{\eta}\,\frac{d}{r}\Bigl(\|\nabla F(x_{0})\|+\bar{\eta}TL\sqrt{\frac{d}{r}}\Bigr)}.

Then, using (
86
), for each
k=0,\dots,T-1
we have

\displaystyle\frac{3}{4}\lambda\,\bar{\eta}\,\frac{d}{r}\,\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{4}\lambda\,\bar{\eta}\,\frac{d}{r}\Bigl(\|\nabla F(x_{0})\|+\bar{\eta}TL\sqrt{\frac{d}{r}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{4}\cdot\frac{2}{3}\,\tau=\frac{\tau}{2}.

Substituting this pointwise bound into (
85
) gives

\bar{\eta}\frac{\tau}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|\leq\Delta_{0}+\frac{\bar{\eta}^{2}TL}{2}\frac{d}{r}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\frac{1}{\delta}.

Multiplying both sides by
\frac{2}{\bar{\eta}\tau T}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\Delta_{0}}{\bar{\eta}\tau T}+\frac{\bar{\eta}}{\tau}\frac{d}{r}L+\frac{2}{\bar{\eta}\tau T}\cdot\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\frac{1}{\delta}.

We have

\frac{1}{\lambda}=\frac{3}{2}\,\frac{\bar{\eta}\frac{d}{r}\bigl(\|\nabla F(x_{0})\|+\bar{\eta}TL\sqrt{\frac{d}{r}}\bigr)}{\tau}.

Substituting this into the last term yields

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\bar{\eta}\tau T}\cdot\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\frac{1}{\delta}

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{\tau^{2}}\left(\frac{d}{r}\frac{\|\nabla F(x_{0})\|}{T}+\bar{\eta}L\Bigl(\frac{d}{r}\Bigr)^{3/2}\right)\log\frac{1}{\delta}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\Delta_{0}}{\bar{\eta}\tau T}+\frac{\bar{\eta}}{\tau}\frac{d}{r}L+\frac{3}{\tau^{2}}\left(\frac{d}{r}\frac{\|\nabla F(x_{0})\|}{T}+\bar{\eta}L\Bigl(\frac{d}{r}\Bigr)^{3/2}\right)\log\frac{1}{\delta}.

Substituting
\bar{\eta}=\eta T^{-u}
gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|\leq\;\frac{2\Delta_{0}}{\eta\tau T^{1-u}}+\frac{\eta}{\tau T^{u}}\frac{d}{r}L+\frac{3}{\tau^{2}}\left(\frac{d}{r}\frac{\|\nabla F(x_{0})\|}{T}+\frac{\eta}{T^{u}}\Bigl(\frac{d}{r}\Bigr)^{3/2}L\right)\log\frac{1}{\delta}.

Finally, by
Appendix

C
, we have

\|\nabla F(x_{0})\|\leq\sqrt{2\Delta_{0}L}.

Substituting this bound into the last display yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{k=0}^{T-1}\|\nabla F(x_{k})\|\leq\;\frac{2\Delta_{0}}{\eta\tau T^{1-u}}+\frac{\eta}{\tau T^{u}}\frac{d}{r}L+\frac{3}{\tau^{2}}\left(\frac{d}{r}\frac{\sqrt{2\Delta_{0}L}}{T}+\frac{\eta}{T^{u}}\Bigl(\frac{d}{r}\Bigr)^{3/2}L\right)\log\frac{1}{\delta}.

This completes the proof.

Appendix E
Experimental Details

E.1
Initialization.

For each random seed, all methods start from the same initialization in both the synthetic quadratic experiment and character-level language modeling.

E.2
Stepsize tuning

For each method (and each subspace dimension
r
when applicable), we tune the stepsize
\bar{\eta}
over the
prescribed grid using three independent tuning runs, and then evaluate the selected
\bar{\eta}
on five disjoint
evaluation runs under the same oracle-call budget.

For the synthetic quadratic experiment, the tuning score of a candidate step size
\bar{\eta}
is defined as the average of

\|\nabla F(x)\|=\|\Lambda x\|
over the last
10
iterations of the run.
We then average this score over the tuning runs (across tuning seeds) and select the
\bar{\eta}
that minimizes the resulting averaged score. The resulting best stepsizes are summarized in
Table

2
.The selected stepsize is then fixed and used for all five evaluation runs.

For character-level language modeling, the tuning score of a candidate
\bar{\eta}
is defined as the final

estimated validation loss
at the end of the oracle-call budget.
The validation loss is estimated by averaging the per-character negative log-likelihood over five randomly sampled minibatches.
We average this score over tuning seeds and select the
\bar{\eta}
with the smallest averaged score. The resulting best stepsizes are summarized in
Table

3
.
The selected stepsize is fixed and used for all five evaluation runs.
