A novel cucurbit[6]uril derivative-based integrated SPME–GC analysis system for efficiently determining the migration of multiple classes of plastic additives from food contact materials
Authors: Zan Tan, Fengping Hou, Tingyi Qiao, Nan Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe migration of harmful plastic additives with diverse polarities and boiling points from plastic food contact materials (FCMs) poses a risk to human health, necessitating simple and effective analytical methods. Herein, a π-conjugated truxene (Tr) was covalently bonded with cucurbit[6]uril (Q[6]) to afford a novel Q[6]-Tr compound, which was fabricated as both a solid-phase microextraction (SPME) fiber coating and a gas chromatography (GC) stationary phase to establish an integrated SPME-GC system. The proposed method determined 9 plastic additives from 4 categories, showing good linearity (R2 ≥ 0.9863) over 5–1000 μg/L, low detection limits (0.21–0.45 μg/L), enrichment factors of 13–42, and satisfactory recoveries (70.8–97.5%), with performance superior to commercial counterparts. Practical application quantified BHT migration from takeout containers (2.341 μg/L) and DIBP from containers and plastic bags (2.504 and 4.954 μg/L). This dual-functional Q[6]-Tr system offers an organic solvent-free, universally applicable approach for multiclass additive analysis in FCMs.Keywords: Migration, Plastic additives, Food contact materials, Truxene, Cucurbit[6]uril, Dual-functional system

Introduction

Plastic products are widely used as food contact materials (FCMs) (e.g., disposable cups and takeout containers) due to their lightness, durability, and low cost (Sun & Huang, 2024). Polyolefins (e.g., polypropylene and polyethylene) serve as primary base materials, followed by copolymers such as polyesters and polyamides (Vera et al., 2022). During industrial processing, unreacted monomers (e.g., polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs)) may remain as residues and readily migrate into food owing to their low polarity and low boiling points (Singh et al., 2016). Additionally, various functional additives, such as plasticizers (Ding et al., 2025), antioxidants (Pan et al., 2025), and ultraviolet absorbers (Wang et al., 2016), are incorporated during production to improve flexibility, thermal stability, and oxidation resistance (Caux et al., 2025; Fasano et al., 2012). These additives typically exist in the plastic matrix via non-covalent interactions and tend to migrate upon contacting food (Singh et al., 2022), especially under heating conditions (Kim et al., 2023; Sapozhnikova et al., 2021). Long-term exposure to these potentially toxic substances may lead to health issues, including cardiovascular diseases and metabolic disorders (Chen et al., 2024; Khare et al., 2025; Sendra et al., 2021). Therefore, developing efficient methods for detecting trace plastic additives is of great significance for assessing the safety of FCMs.

Gas chromatography (GC) offers significant advantages in the analysis of volatile and semi-volatile complex mixtures (Oliveira et al., 2026) due to its high separation capability. Therefore, it is widely used for determining the migration of plastic additives. (Goulais et al., 2024; Tong et al., 2019). For low-polarity, low-boiling-point small molecules such as PAHs, GC or GC coupled with pretreatment techniques have proven effective (Li & Zhu, 2017). However, additives such as antioxidants and plasticizers often have more complex molecular structures, lower volatility, broader polarity and boiling point ranges. These compounds typically require more sensitive detectors such as mass spectrometry (MS). For instance, a study employed GC–MS and LC–IM–QTOF–MS to identify 50 migrants in cork stoppers (Vera et al., 2022). To improve analytical efficiency, sample pretreatment techniques have also been integrated into the workflow: dispersive liquid-liquid microextraction coupled with GC–MS enabled the detection of alkylphenol additives in juice bottles (Shabani et al., 2023), while direct immersion solid-phase microextraction (SPME) combined with GC–MS was used to screen 9 substances including plasticizers and antioxidants in disposable tableware (Jimenez Estremera et al., 2025). Another GC–MS method combined with pretreatment steps including soaking, ultrasonication, and centrifugation has been used to determine 44 phthalate esters in FCMs (Andjelković et al., 2021). Although the aforementioned GC–MS methods have achieved high accuracy in quantification (Redko et al., 2024), existing approaches generally suffer from limitations. Most reported methods target only a single class of plastic additives. Although a few can simultaneously determine multiple classes, they typically involve cumbersome pretreatment, high solvent consumption, and costly mass spectrometric detection. These drawbacks make them operationally complex, expensive, and not compliant with green chemistry principles. As a result, current techniques are inadequate for the universal detection of additive migration from daily-use plastic products (Dvorakova et al., 2024; Kuklya et al., 2024), particularly for compounds with diverse polarities and boiling points. The development of a simple, universal, and solvent-free method therefore holds significant practical value for the safety assessment of plastic products.

To address this challenge, this study adopts a simplified strategy by solely coupling SPMEwith GC. This SPME–GC technique is environmentally friendly and requires no organic solvents. It was employed to determine the migration of 4 categories (plasticizers, antioxidants, ultraviolet absorbers, and PAHs) comprising 9 plastic additives from FCMs. However, simultaneous enrichment and detection of them using commercial SPME coatings and GC columns remains challenging. This difficulty primarily arises from their diverse structures, wide boiling point and polarity ranges, imposing stringent requirements on the universality of SPME coatings and GC stationary phases. Specifically, the enrichment and thermal desorption of high-boiling-point compounds demand materials with excellent thermal stability, while effective separation of wide-polarity mixtures requires appropriate polarity compatibility of the stationary phase (Jimenez Estremera et al., 2025). Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop a novel functional material that can serve both as an SPME coating to enhance enrichment selectivity and as a GC stationary phase to improve separation performance.

In this study, we aim to develop a novel dual-functional material based on cucurbituril as the fundamental structure. Cucurbiturils possess unique hydrophobic cavities, portal and outer-surface structures, enabling interactions with analytes through host-guest interactions (Li et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024). They have been widely used in the development of analytical materials. For example, a polydopamine-based capillary GC column incorporating cucurbit[7]uril exhibited excellent separation performance for alkanes, halogenated alkanes, and Grob reagents (Zhang et al., 2016). Our research group previously prepared a sol-gel SPME coating based on cucurbit[6]uril (Q[6]), which successfully enriched 7 trace PAHs from water samples with good stability and extraction efficiency (Dong et al., 2016). These studies demonstrate the promising potential of cucurbiturils in SPME coatings and chromatographic stationary phases. Considering that the target analytes are aromatic compounds (e.g., bisphenols), Q[6] exhibits strong host-guest interactions with molecules containing aromatic structures (Barrow et al., 2015). However, conventional Q[6] lacks a π-electron system, limiting its non-covalent interactions with aromatic guests. To improve recognition and binding ability toward aromatic compounds, a π-conjugated truxene (Tr) unit was covalently attached to the Q[6] framework. This design combines host-guest interaction from Q[6] with π–π interactions from the Tr unit toward aromatic compounds (Singh et al., 2017), enabling synergistic binding of the target analytes. Accordingly, the Q[6]-Tr compound was synthesized. Subsequently, Q[6]-Tr was fabricated as an SPME fiber using the sol-gel method and as a capillary GC column using the static coating approach. An integrated SPME-GC analytical method was established by combining the two. Leveraging the multiple interaction sites of Q[6]-Tr, the method enabled the determination of the migration of 4 categories (9 compounds) of plastic additives with wide polarity and boiling point ranges from typical disposable FCMs into food simulants. The developed method demonstrates satisfactory separation efficiency, stability, accuracy, and precision.

Materials and methods

Reagents, materials and instruments

Ultrapure water (electrical resistivity 18.5 MΩ·cm) was supplied by the pure water system from Chengdu Haokang Technology Co.,. All synthetic laboratory reagents used in this study were of analytical grade. Sodium chloride (99.8%), methanol (chromatographic grade), dichloromethane (DCM, 99.9%), chloroform (>99%), 30% hydrofluoric acid (w/w). 9 plastic additive standards include: (i) plasticizers: dibutyl phthalate (DBP, ≥99.8%, Aladdin, Shanghai, China), diisobutyl phthalate (DIBP, ≥99.5%, Adamas-beta, Shanghai, China), and diamyl phthalate (DAP, ≥99.5%, Aladdin, Shanghai, China); (ii) antioxidants: 2,2′-methylenebis(6-tert-butyl-4-methylphenol) (MBP, ≥99%, Innochem, Beijing, China) and 2,6-di-tert-butyl-4-methylphenol (BHT, ≥99%, Innochem, Beijing, China); (iii) ultraviolet absorbers: 2-(2′-hydroxy-3′,5′-di-tert-butylphenyl)benzotriazole (UV–320, ≥99.5%, Aladdin, Shanghai, China) and 2-hydroxy-4-methoxybenzophenone (UV–9, ≥99.5%, Macklin, Beijing, China); (iv) polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs): acenaphthene (≥99.5%, Aladdin, Shanghai, China) and fluorene (≥99%, Macklin, Beijing, China).

Disposable cups (PET), plastic bags (PP), and biodegradable takeout containers, were purchased from local supermarkets. Silica capillary GC columns (10 m × 0.25 mm) were obtained from Yongnian Ruifeng Chromatographic Devices Co., Ltd. (Hebei, China). Stainless steel wire fibers (o.d. 150 μm) were supplied by Tianyu Metal Consumables Co., Ltd. (Dongguan, China).

The following instruments were employed in this study: a gas chromatograph (GC–2014c, Shimadzu, Japan), an Agilent 6545 Q–TOF mass spectrometer (Agilent, Singapore), a VERTEX70 Fourier transform infrared (FT–IR) spectrophotometer (Bruker, Germany). a ΣIGMA scanning electron microscope (SEM) (ZEISS, Germany), and an ESCALAB 250 XI X–ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Thermo, USA).

Preparation of steel fibers and capillary GC column

Pretreatment of stainless-steel wire fibers. One end (2.5 cm) of a 20 cm length of stainless-steel wire fiber was immersed in 30% HF solution for 25 min. Subsequently, the etched end was rinsed sequentially with ultrapure water and ethanol and then dried under vacuum at 60 °C.

Preparation of NaCl-coated Capillary GC Column. The silica capillary column was first connected to a 5 mL syringe, and DCM was injected to flush the column for 30 min. After flushing, the column was installed in a GC and conditioned at 260 °C for 3 h under N2 atmosphere to obtain the preconditioned capillary column. 2.3 g of NaCl was added to 12 mL of anhydrous methanol, and the mixture was stirred vigorously for 30 min, followed by filtration. 9 mL of the filtrate was then added to 12 mL of chloroform under vigorous stirring. Subsequently, 1 mL of methanol was supplemented, and the stirring continued for 5 min until homogeneous. Another 12 mL of chloroform was then added, and the mixture was stirred for 5 min to obtain a homogeneous NaCl-containing mixed solution. This mixed solution was introduced into the pretreated capillary column using a pressure-driven method. The injection stopped until the effluent turbidity matched that of the original solution. Finally, the capillary column loaded with the NaCl mixed solution was conditioned at 200 °C for 3 h under N2 atmosphere to obtain the NaCl-coated capillary GC column.

Preparation of PDMS/Q[6]-Tr gel-coated and other fibers

PDMS/Q[6]-Tr Gel-Coated fiber was fabricated via Sol-Gel method. In a 5 mL centrifuge tube, 20 mg of Q[6]-Tr, 500 μL of DCM, 300 μL of KH-560, 300 μL of HO-PDMS, 30 μL of MTMOS, and 50 μL of PMHS were added. The mixture was vortexed for 5 min to ensure thorough mixing. Subsequently, 300 μL of 98% TFA was added, and the mixture was vortexed for another 5 min, followed by centrifugation at 1200 r/min for 5 min to obtain the upper PDMS/Q[6]-Tr sol solution. The pretreated stainless-steel wire fiber was immersed into the PDMS/Q[6]-Tr sol solution to a depth of approximately 2 cm and kept for 2 min. After removing the fiber, the PDMS/Q[6]-Tr sol was coated onto the surface of the steel wire. The coated fiber was dried at 50 °C for 20 s. This coating process was repeated 6 times to achieve the desired coating, and the fiber was then allowed to stand at room temperature for 12 h. Finally, the fiber was conditioned under N2 atmosphere at 60 °C for 1 h, 180 °C for 1 h, and 260 °C for 1.5 h to obtain the PDMS/Q[6]-Tr gel-coated fiber. For comparison, blank PDMS gel-coated fiber, PDMS/Q[6] gel-coated fiber, and PDMS/Tr gel-coated fiber were prepared using the same procedure.

Preparation of Q[6]-Tr capillary GC column

Q[6]-Tr Capillary GC Column was fabricated by the static coating method. Q[6]-Tr (30 mg) was dissolved in 5 mL of DCM, and the solution was ultrasonicated for 10 min to ensure complete dissolution and degassing. The Q[6]-Tr solution in DCM was introduced into the NaCl-precoated capillary GC column using a pressure-driven method until free of air bubbles. Subsequently, both ends of the column were sealed, and the column was placed vertically in a 40 °C water bath to allow the DCM to evaporate slowly. After complete evaporation of DCM, the column was connected to the GC injector port and conditioned according to the following program: the temperature was raised from 40 °C to 150 °C at a rate of 2 °C/min, held at 150 °C for 3 h, then further ramped to 240 °C and held for 4 h. Thus, the Q[6]-Tr-coated capillary GC column was obtained.

Extraction procedure using PDMS/Q[6]-Tr gel-coated fiber

A methanol stock solution containing a mixture of the 9 plastic additives (Table S5) was prepared at a concentration of 1 mg/mL for each additive and stored at 4 °C. Working standard solutions were obtained by diluting the stock solution with ultrapure water as needed. An aliquot of 7.5 mL solution (including working standard solutions and actual samples) was placed into a 20 mL headspace vial. The fiber was assembled onto a 5 μL micro syringe, and the plastic additives were enriched using headspace SPME. Extraction was performed at 80 °C under stirring at 500 r/min for 5 min. Subsequently, the fiber was rapidly withdrawn and immediately inserted into the GC injection port for desorption at 250 °C for 4 min, followed by separation and determination.

GC analysis using Q[6]-Tr capillary GC column

Separation and analysis of the plastic additives were performed using the Q[6]-Tr capillary GC column. The analytical conditions were as follows: injector temperature, 260 °C; FID temperature, 270 °C; carrier gas, high-purity N2 (99.999%) at a flow rate of 1 mL/min; injection volume, 1 μL. To evaluate the separation performance of the Q[6]-Tr capillary column, samples including n-alkanes (C7–C24), fatty acid methyl esters, ketones, aldehydes, and alcohols were injected in split mode with a split ratio of 50:1. Samples extracted with the PDMS/Q[6]-Tr gel-coated SPME fiber were injected in splitless mode. All migration experiments were performed in triplicate.

Food simulants and migration experiments of plastic additives

Four liquid food simulants were employed, namely hot drinking water (approximately 85 °C), 10% (v/v) ethanol solution (approximately 25 °C, simulating alcoholic beverages), 3% (w/v) acetic acid (HAc) solution (approximately 25 °C, simulating acidic beverages), and olive oil (simulating edible oils at 40 °C and 70 °C). Detailed treatment conditions of the FCMs in the food simulants are provided in Table S1.

For disposable PET cups, 250 mL of three simulants were added separately: drinking water (85 °C), 10% (v/v) ethanol solution (25 °C) and 3% (w/v) HAc solution (25 °C). The cups were then covered for the migration test. Commercial PP plastic bags and biodegradable boxes were cut into uniform pieces (1 × 1 cm2). Subsequently, 2 mg of these pieces were placed into headspace vials, and 7.5 mL of olive oil was added. Two migration conditions were applied: high-temperature short-term (70 ± 2 °C in a water bath for 2 h) and low-temperature long-term (40 ± 1 °C in an incubator in the dark for 10 days). After the migration period, the simulants were filtered, sealed, and stored to avoid evaporation.

Results and discussion

Characterization of the PDMS/Q[6]-Tr gel-coated fiber

After fabricating the gel-coated fiber via the sol-gel method, the PDMS/Q[6]-Tr gel coating was characterized by FT–IR, thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and SEM. Fig. 1a presents a comparison of the FT-IR spectra of blank PDMS, Q[6]-Tr, and the PDMS/Q[6]-Tr gel coating. Compared with blank PDMS, the PDMS/Q[6]-Tr gel coating exhibited a significantly enhanced C—H stretching vibration peak at 2943 cm−1, confirming the successful incorporation of Q[6]-Tr into the PDMS network. Meanwhile, the characteristic carbonyl (C=O) peak of Q[6]-Tr at 1722 cm−1 appeared weakened and exhibited a red shift of 51 cm−1. Although the vibrational intensities at 1259 cm−1 (C—N) and 1020 cm−1 (C–O–C) remain largely unchanged, they show a red shift of 49 cm−1 and a blue shift of 21 cm−1, respectively. These observations not only confirm the successful incorporation of Q[6]-Tr into the gel coating structure but also indicate certain interactions between Q[6]-Tr and the gel, which contributes to the material's stability. As shown in Fig. 1b, TGA results revealed no significant mass loss of the PDMS/Q[6]-Tr gel coating below 350 °C, with only 1.6% mass loss observed at 357 °C. This demonstrates its good thermal stability and suitability for thermal desorption at GC injector temperatures of 200–300 °C. The SEM image in Fig. 1c shows a smooth, uniform, and crack-free PDMS/Q[6]-Tr gel coating surface with no visible defects, which ensures effective and stable analyte extraction. Furthermore, the thickness of the PDMS/Q[6]-Tr gel coating was measured to be approximately 42 μm (Fig. 1d).

Characterization and performance of Q[6]-Tr capillary GC column

The Q[6]-Tr capillary GC column was prepared using the static coating method, and its surface morphology was characterized by SEM. As shown in Figs. S3a–S3d, the Q[6]-Tr stationary phase exhibited a continuous and uniform film morphology with a thickness of approximately 400 nm and no macroscopic defects. This morphology is favorable for mass transfer processes, thereby facilitating efficient separation of the analytes. According to the Golay curves (Fig. S4a, b), the theoretical plate counts of this column for naphthalene and n-dodecane were calculated as 15,873 (at 20 cm/s) and 14,493 (at 15 cm/s), respectively. The average retention index difference (ΔRI/5) calculated from McReynolds constants using Supelco SPB-50 as a reference column was 132 (Table S2), indicating that the Q[6]-Tr column is a medium-polarity column. The results of temperature programmed testing showed that when the temperature was ramped to 320 °C, the Q[6]-Tr column exhibited excellent retention behavior (relative standard deviation, RSD ≤ 7.4%) and reproducibility (RSD ≤ 3.8%) (Fig. S5a, b, Table S3), demonstrating its good thermodynamic stability. The reproducibility of the Q[6]-Tr column was systematically evaluated. As summarized in Table S4, The Q[6]-Tr column exhibited good reproducibility, with intra-day and inter-day RSDs of retention times ≤0.32% and batch-to-batch RSDs ≤2.5%. Collectively, these results indicate that the fabricated Q[6]-Tr GC column possesses high column efficiency, medium polarity, satisfactory thermal stability and reproducibility.

Subsequently, the separation performance of the Q[6]-Tr GC column was evaluated for various classes of compounds ranging from low to high polarity, including eight n-alkanes (C7–C24), fatty acid methyl esters, ketones, aldehydes, and alcohols. As shown in Figs. 2a–2e, all components were fully resolved in order of increasing boiling points, with sharp and symmetrical peaks. This confirms the favorable separation capability of this column for mixtures with wide polarity and boiling point ranges. Furthermore, the Q[6]-Tr GC column was applied to separate the 9 target plastic additives, and the theoretical plate numbers (N) and resolutions (Rs) were calculated (Table S5). The theoretical plate numbers ranged from 13,553 to 19,7364, and all target analytes achieved baseline separation (Rs ≥ 2.37), indicating good resolution of the 9 additives on this column. Subsequently, the separation performance of Q[6]-Tr column was compared with three commercial GC columns (SE–30, HP–5, and WM–17) for the 9 additives. The results showed that although SE-30 and WM–17 achieved good separation, they could not fully analyze all 9 additives (Fig. 3a, c). HP–5 could analyze all 9 additives but failed to effectively separate BHT and acenaphthene (Fig. 3b). In contrast, Q[6]-Tr column not only achieved good separation and analysis of all 9 additives but also exhibited shorter retention times compared with the commercial columns (Fig. 3d). Therefore, this column meets the requirements for determining the migration of the 9 additives in FCMs.

Extraction mechanism

To elucidate the extraction performance of the fabricated PDMS/Q[6]-Tr gel-coated fiber, corresponding fibers of various materials (blank PDMS, PDMS/Q[6]-Tr, PDMS/Q[6], and PDMS/Tr) were fabricated. The commercial PDMS/DVB/CAR fiber, known for its broad applicability across a wide range of boiling points and polarities, possesses a porous structure and high specific surface area, giving it a strong adsorption capacity. Hence, it was used as a reference for comparing extraction performance. Their extraction performances toward 9 additives were compared using the Q[6]-Tr GC column. As shown in Fig. 4a, the PDMS/Q[6]-Tr fiber exhibited the best extraction efficiency. Blank PDMS and the commercial PDMS/DVB/CAR fiber could only extract 7 of the 9 analytes, failing to extract the antioxidant MBP and the ultraviolet absorber UV–320. The gel coatings containing single functional units (PDMS/Q[6] and PDMS/Tr) could extract these two analytes, but with significantly lower efficiencies compared with PDMS/Q[6]-Tr. These results indicate that the synergistic effect of Q[6] and Tr is only fully realized when they are covalently linked within a single molecule, thereby achieving satisfactory extraction performance. This synergistic mechanism may be attributed to the combined effects of the host–guest interactions from Q[6] and the π–π stacking interactions from Tr moiety.

The FT–IR and XPS spectra of MBP before and after adsorption onto the Q[6]-Tr gel coating were analyzed. All binding energies were calibrated using the C1s peak of adventitious carbon at 284.8 eV as a reference. Optimization of the Q[6]-Tr-based SPME–GC coupled analytical method

A SPME–GC analytical method based on Q[6]-Tr was established using the fabricated PDMS/Q[6]-Tr gel-coated fiber and Q[6]-Tr capillary column. To achieve optimal determination of the migration of the 9 plastic additives, key parameters including extraction temperature, pH, extraction time, ionic strength, and desorption time were systematically optimized. As shown in Fig. 5a, increasing temperature led to a marked rise in the total extraction capacity of the nine analytes, owing to promoted volatilization. The maximum capacity was attained at 80 °C, followed by a decline, which may be due to desorption of the analytes at elevated temperature. Therefore, 80 °C was selected as the optimal extraction temperature. As depicted in Fig. 5b, the extraction efficiency improved with increasing NaCl concentration, leveling off at 0.2 g/mL. Higher concentrations tended to cause increased solution viscosity or crystallization，hindering the effective migration of the analyte. Thus, 0.2 g/mL was determined as the optimal salt concentration. From Fig. 5c, the adsorption increased up to 50 min, beyond which the extraction efficiency declined due to desorption or coating saturation. Therefore, 50 min was selected as the optimal extraction time. As depicted in Fig. 5d, desorption times below 3 min resulted in incomplete desorption and poor sensitivity, whereas 4 min yielded the maximum response, indicating optimal desorption. Beyond 5 min, peak areas decreased, likely due to re-adsorption. Hence, 4 min was chosen as the optimal desorption time. As illustrated in Fig. 5e, the extraction efficiency remained relatively low at pH 4–5, increased markedly at pH 6, and achieved its highest value at pH 7. Accordingly, pH 7 was chosen as the optimal condition. Under the above optimized conditions, the 9 plastic additives were separated and analyzed using the established analytical system. As shown in Fig. 5f, all analytes exhibited sharp, symmetrical, and well-resolved chromatographic peaks, demonstrating the satisfactory analytical performance of the method.

Performance of the Q[6]-Tr-based SPME–GC coupled analytical method

Calibration curves for the 9 plastic additives were established using the proposed method under the optimized conditions. The responses of all additives showed good linearity within the tested concentration range, with satisfactory correlation coefficients (0.9863 ≤ R2 ≤ 0.9975). Based on the calibration curves, the limits of detection (LODs) and limits of quantification (LOQs) for each additive were calculated as 3σ/S and 10σ/S, respectively (Table 1). The results showed that the LODs of 0.21–0.45 μg/L, LOQs of 0.70–1.53 μg/L, and the enrichment factors (EF) of 13–42, indicating that the proposed method exhibits good sensitivity.

Determination of additive migration in real food simulant samples

The specific migration of the 9 plastic additives from disposable plastic FCMs into 4 food simulants was determined using the Q[6]-Tr-based SPME–GC analytical method (sample pretreatment procedures are detailed in Section 2.8). The analytical results are summarized in Table 3. No migration of target additives from plastic bags or takeout containers into olive oil were detected at 40 °C. However, the migration of BHT from takeout containers into olive oil was determined to be 2.341 μg/L at 70 °C, while the migration levels of DIBP from plastic bags and takeout containers into olive oil were 4.954 μg/L and 2.504 μg/L, respectively. The detectable migration of BHT and DIBP in olive oil is likely due to their good solubility in olive oil, conferred by lipophilic alkyl chains. Additionally, their relatively low boiling points may facilitate volatilization and subsequent headspace extraction. No migrated additives from disposable cups were detected in the 3 simulated food beverages under either low or high temperature. The high crystallinity and dense molecular structure of PET effectively suppress the diffusion of additive molecules. Consequently, even when trace additives are present, their migration levels may remain below the analytical detection limits. All detected concentrations were well below the limits set by EU 10/2011 (SML for BHT: 3 mg/kg; DIBP regulated under group restriction No. 36: ≤ 0.6 mg/kg as DEHP equivalents), indicating no significant safety concern. These results suggest that caution should be exercised when using plastic containers for high-temperature or high-fat foods.

The accuracy of the proposed method was further validated by recovery experiments using spiked blank samples. Three concentration levels (low, medium, and high) were spiked, and each level was measured in triplicate. The results (Table 3) showed that, except for UV–9, which exhibited relatively low recoveries of 71.1% and 70.8% at low concentrations of 100 μg/L and 500 μg/L respectively, the recoveries of most additives ranged from 81.1% to 97.5%. These results indicate that the method exhibits satisfactory accuracy and is suitable for the determination of additives in real samples.

Reproducibility and stability

The reproducibility and stability of the Q[6]-Tr-based SPME–GC analytical method were investigated. Fig. 6a, b present the peak areas and retention times of the 9 analytes after multiple analyses. The results demonstrated that the method maintained excellent stability even after 280 repeated uses spanning 160 days, with RSDs (n = 3) for both peak areas and retention times below 10%. In addition, SEM characterization of the PDMS/Q[6]-Tr gel-coated fiber after prolonged use (Fig. 6c, d) revealed that the coating remained uniform in surface morphology and cross-section and was firmly anchored to the fiber surface. These findings indicate that the Q[6]-Tr-based SPME–GC analytical method exhibits outstanding reproducibility and robustness, highlighting its potential for reliable practical applications.

Analytical GREEnness metric of Q[6]-Tr-based SPME–GC analytical method

Following the 12 principles of green analytical chemistry (Xin et al., 2025), the greenness of the proposed method was evaluated using the AGREE (Analytical GREEnness Calculator) software (Pena-Pereira et al., 2020). As illustrated in Fig. 6e, the AGREE score of 0.75 confirms the environmental friendliness of this method (Table S6). Furthermore, its applicability and operational feasibility were assessed using the Blue Applicability Grade Index (BAGI) tool (Manousi et al., 2023), yielding a score of 75.0 out of 100 (Fig. 6f). Beyond these quantitative greenness metrics, the proposed method features operational simplicity, eliminates the need for complex sample pretreatment and hazardous organic reagents, and enables rapid analysis within 20 min. Collectively, these attributes highlight its potential for green analytical chemistry applications.

Conclusion

In this study, a novel compound, Q[6]-Tr, was successfully synthesized. Using this compound, a SPME coating fiber was fabricated via the sol-gel method, and a capillary GC column stationary phase was prepared via the static coating method. An efficient integrated SPME–GC analytical system was established based on this dual-functional material, combining enrichment, separation, and determination in a single platform. This system was applied to analyze the migration of 4 categories of additives (plasticizers, antioxidants, ultraviolet absorbers, and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons) with diverse polarities and boiling point ranges from common plastic FCMs into food simulants. The migration of BHT and DIBP into olive oil was successfully detected under high-temperature conditions. The proposed analytical method leverages the synergistic interactions between Q[6]-Tr and analytes, significantly enhancing selective enrichment and separation performance. The entire analytical process is organic-solvent-free by employing only headspace microextraction. Meanwhile, efficient sample enrichment is achieved through selective adsorption by coating, thereby realizing the objectives of green and efficient analysis. It should be noted that the present method was validated within a concentration range of 5–1000 μg/L. This method offers the advantages of simple operation, rapid analysis, environmental friendliness, and reliable results. Moreover, it outperforms commercial materials in terms of both enrichment efficiency and separation performance. This work not only provides an innovative detection method for the safety risk assessment of food packaging but also offers reliable technical support for the accurate analysis of trace substances in complex matrices.
