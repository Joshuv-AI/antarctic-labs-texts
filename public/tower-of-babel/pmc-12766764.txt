Efficacy and safety of adjuvant short-course immune checkpoint inhibitors following TACE combined with MWA in patients with BCLC A–B hepatocellular carcinoma: a single-arm, prospective, phase II study protocol
Authors: Yang Fang, Mingyin Zhang, Jiacheng Chen, Zhezhu Han, Xuezhe Piao, Songnan Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with early-stage to intermediate-stage hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) face a high risk of recurrence after transarterial chemoembolisation (TACE) plus microwave ablation (MWA), often resulting in early relapse and poor prognosis. Adjuvant immunotherapy may reduce this risk by eliminating residual disease and enhancing antitumour immunity, thereby lowering recurrence and improving outcomes. However, evidence supporting adjuvant immunotherapy alone after curative locoregional therapy remains limited. This study evaluates the efficacy and safety of short-course adjuvant Tislelizumab in patients with Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer（BCLC）stage A–B HCC treated with TACE plus MWA.Method and analysisThis prospective, single-arm, phase II trial will enrol 30 patients with BCLC stage A–B HCC who have received TACE plus MWA. Eligibility requires complete response confirmed by modified Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors（mRECIST） on imaging 1 week after treatment. All patients will receive tislelizumab (200 mg every 3 weeks) for 6 months. Follow-up will continue until recurrence, death or study completion. The primary endpoint is investigator-assessed recurrence-free survival. Secondary endpoints include local tumour progression, intrahepatic distant recurrence, time to extrahepatic disease, overall survival and adverse events.Ethics and disseminationOur study was approved by the Medical Research Ethics Committee of the Yanbian University Hospital (No. 20250006). The findings of this study will be submitted for publication in peer-reviewed journals and will also be presented at multiple international conferences on interventional radiology and oncology.Trial registration numberChiCTR2500110080.Keywords: Carcinoma, Hepatocellular; Interventional radiology; Clinical Protocols; Prospective studies

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a common and aggressive malignancy with high incidence and mortality.1 Globally, it is a leading cause of cancer-related death, with over half of cases occurring in China.2 HCC is largely resistant to conventional chemoradiotherapy, so curative options are limited to surgical resection, liver transplantation or ablation.3 For early-stage to intermediate-stage patients ineligible for surgery, transarterial chemoembolisation (TACE) is the recommended therapy.4 TACE exerts a dual effect by directly eradicating tumours and modulating antitumour immunity through complex mechanisms. The ischaemia induced by TACE, together with the cytotoxicity of chemotherapy, triggers immunogenic cell death, releasing multiple tumour neoantigens that enhance effector T cell responses and remodel the tumour immune microenvironment.5 In addition, embolisation reduces blood flow, mitigating the cooling effect and thereby improving the efficacy of subsequent microwave ablation (MWA).6 Meta-analyses indicate that for tumours >5 cm, TACE combined with MWA improves overall survival (OS) and increases complete response (CR), partial response and objective response rates (ORR) compared with TACE alone.7 Nevertheless, up to 70% of patients relapse within 5 years after curative treatment, and OS remains below 50%, highlighting the urgent need to reduce recurrence and improve outcomes.8 9

For HCC patients at high risk of postcurative recurrence, guidelines prioritise reducing relapse and prolonging survival, yet no standardised adjuvant therapy exists, and only a few studies show potential benefit. The IMbrave050 trial, the first phase III multicentre study of adjuvant targeted therapy combined with immunotherapy, demonstrated a significant improvement in recurrence-free survival (RFS) over active surveillance, though this benefit was not maintained with long-term follow-up.10 Conversely, the STORM trial found that adjuvant targeted therapy alone in early-stage patients neither reduced recurrence nor improved outcomes and increased adverse events (AEs).11 Unlike tyrosine kinase inhibitors, immunotherapy enhances immune recognition and activates tumour-specific T cells for sustained antitumour effects,12 13 yielding superior long-term efficacy and a ‘tail’ effect. Thus, immunotherapy has gained growing attention in early-stage to intermediate-stage HCC. 14 15 A retrospective study by Zhang et al found that adjuvant immunotherapy improved OS and progression-free survival (PFS) in high-risk postsurgical patients.16 A phase II trial of adjuvant sintilimab in HCC patients with microvascular invasion (MVI) after hepatectomy showed significantly prolonged RFS, suggesting benefit in this population, though OS differences were not statistically significant.17 Nonetheless, the optimal adjuvant therapy for early-stage to intermediate-stage HCC remains unclear and requires further validation.

Given the lack of prospective data on adjuvant immunotherapy in early-stage to intermediate-stage HCC, we conducted a study to assess its potential benefit after TACE combined with MWA in Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer（BCLC）stage A–B patients, aiming to guide future standardised treatment.

Methods

Trial design

This prospective, single-arm, phase II study, initiated by the Affiliated Hospital of Yanbian University, began recruiting in September 2025 and is expected to end in December 2026. All patients included in the study received TACE. Patients with normal liver function and a CR on imaging, assessed by modified Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors（mRECIST）, 2 weeks after TACE proceeded to MWA. Patients who achieved CR on imaging, assessed by mRECIST, 1 week after MWA received 6 months of adjuvant tislelizumab therapy (200 mg every 3 weeks). The primary endpoint is investigator-assessed RFS; secondary endpoints include local tumour progression (LTP), intrahepatic distant recurrence (IDR), time to extrahepatic disease (TTED), OS and AEs (figure 1).

PD-1 inhibitor administration

All enrolled patients will begin tislelizumab treatment within 2 weeks after MWA, at a fixed dose of 200 mg intravenously every 3 weeks for 6 months. Dosing may be delayed in case of AEs, laboratory abnormalities or comorbidities, but dose reductions are not permitted. Treatment interruptions are allowed for a cumulative period of up to 6 weeks, whether consecutive or intermittent; otherwise, the patient will be withdrawn from the study.

Assessment

Tumour response assessment

Baseline imaging, including chest CT and abdominal and pelvic MRI, must be performed within 14 days prior to the first immunotherapy dose. Brain MRI is also required at baseline; if no lesions are detected initially, subsequent assessments may be omitted.

Tumour response will be evaluated every 6 weeks during treatment using mRECIST. Additional imaging may be performed if disease progression is suspected.

Patients who discontinue treatment due to AEs or other non-progression reasons will continue imaging follow-up until progression, withdrawal of consent, death or study completion, whichever occurs first. If treatment is continued based on investigator-assessed clinical benefit, imaging will be repeated at least 6 weeks after radiologic progression or at the next scheduled tumour assessment.

Follow-up

Safety follow-up

Each patient will undergo a safety follow-up visit after treatment discontinuation. If treatment is stopped due to disease progression identified during response assessment, safety will be evaluated at the last visit. Patients who discontinue due to any urgent AE must return for a safety follow-up 30±7 days after their last treatment.

Long-term follow-up

After study drug discontinuation, patients will be followed every 3 months until lost to follow-up, death, withdrawal of consent or study completion. Follow-up assessments include serious AEs and survival status, collected via telephone, medical records or outpatient visits.

Sample size

This single-arm, phase II prospective study will evaluate RFS as the primary endpoint. Sample size was calculated using the log-rank test with PASS 2021 software (NCSS Software). Historical data17 indicate a median RFS of 15.5 months in patients undergoing curative resection and short-course immunotherapy. Assuming an HR of 0.57, the median RFS in the study group is expected to be 27 months. With a two-sided α of 0.05, 80% power, a 16-month enrolment period and 48 months of total follow-up, approximately 26 events are required. Allowing for potential dropouts, a total of 30 participants will be enrolled (accounting for an anticipated 10% dropout rate, 33 participants will be enrolled).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses will be performed using SPSS V.29.0 and R V.4.3.1. Continuous variables will be compared using t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests, and categorical variables using χ² or Fisher’s exact tests. OS and RFS will be estimated by Kaplan-Meier analysis and compared using the log-rank test; median follow-up will be calculated with the reverse Kaplan-Meier method. HRs and 95% CIs will be estimated using Cox proportional hazards models. Variables with p<0.1 in univariate analysis will enter multivariate models via a stepwise approach. LTP, IDR, TTED and AEs will be summarised descriptively and compared between groups as appropriate.

Missing data will be assumed to be missing at random and handled by multiple imputation, with complete-case analysis performed as a sensitivity check. Exploratory subgroup analyses will assess treatment effect consistency by tumour size, number, HBV status and BCLC stage, without adjustment for multiple comparisons. All tests are two-sided, with p<0.05 considered significant.

Anticipated results

The primary endpoint is investigator-assessed RFS, with secondary endpoints including LTP, IDR, TTED, OS and AEs. We expect RFS, OS, LTP, IDR and TTED to improve relative to previous studies, while AE rates are expected to remain similar.

Discussion

Most adjuvant therapy studies for HCC focus on surgical resection, with limited data after ablation. Only a few subgroup analyses or small retrospective studies in early-stage HCC are available.10 19 Retrospective data suggest that for tumours 2–3 cm in diameter, adjuvant lenvatinib plus anti-PD-1 therapy after TACE combined with MWA significantly prolongs PFS and improves outcomes compared with TACE plus MWA alone. 19 In the IMbrave050 ablation subgroup (n=83), patients had either a single tumour 2–5 cm or ≤4 tumours each ≤5 cm. Subgroup analysis showed an IRF-assessed RFS HR of 0.61, indicating significant benefit from adjuvant therapy. Patients with multiple tumours >5 cm showed greater RFS benefit than those with tumours <5 cm.10 Based on this evidence, we enrolled BCLC stage A–B patients with relatively large tumours to maximise the benefit of adjuvant therapy after curative MWA.

HCC is generally insensitive to conventional chemotherapy or radiotherapy, limiting treatment options. For patients without vascular invasion or extrahepatic metastasis who are not candidates for surgery, TACE is the guideline-recommended first-line therapy; however, studies report a median OS far lower than that of resection or RFA.20 Combining TACE with MWA has shown synergistic effects in some studies. TACE blocks tumour blood supply, inducing hypoxia that increases sensitivity to thermal ablation, while controlling peritumoural microvascular invasion and satellite lesions, reducing local recurrence.7 Lipiodol deposition after TACE enhances heat conduction and induces local oedema, improving microwave heating rates and expanding the ablation zone. MWA also exposes tumour antigens, triggering secondary immune-mediated tumour cell killing and creating a favourable immune microenvironment for adjuvant therapy.21 22

There is currently no standardised adjuvant therapy for HCC after curative treatment. Most prior adjuvant treatments showed limited efficacy and high rates of AEs, whereas both prospective and retrospective studies of adjuvant immunotherapy have demonstrated a significant reduction in recurrence risk. One study reported that in advanced HCC patients receiving anti-PD-1 therapy who exhibited stable or atypically progressive disease, ablation increased the ORR from 10% to 24% and improved median survival.23 MWA and immunotherapy are complementary. MWA induces immunogenic tumour cell death, releasing tumour-associated antigens and damage-associated molecular patterns, which promote dendritic cell activation and CD8+ T cell infiltration, thereby enhancing the antitumour effect of PD-1 inhibitors and, in some patients, producing an ‘abscopal effect’.7 24 25 Postoperative use of immune checkpoint inhibitors as adjuvant therapy can help eradicate residual metastatic lesions and, by activating the host immune system, reduce the risk of new tumour formation.26 27 Recent studies indicate that 6 months of maintenance immunotherapy provides efficacy comparable to continuous treatment while offering good safety and improving patient adherence.28

This study has several limitations. As a single-centre trial with a small sample size, selection bias may affect generalisability. The follow-up is too short to fully assess long-term survival benefits or potential ‘tail effects’. Moreover, the short-course immunotherapy regimen has not been validated in large randomised trials, and its efficacy and safety require confirmation in multicentre, prospective studies.

In summary, this study aims to evaluate the efficacy and safety of adjuvant immunotherapy after TACE combined with MWA in BCLC stage A–B HCC patients, with the goal of generating more reliable evidence and providing new guidance for clinical practice.
