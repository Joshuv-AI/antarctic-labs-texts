An MRI-based comparative study of pediatric medial epicondyle fractures with and without concomitant elbow dislocation
Authors: Yi Mei, Qizhi Yao, Jingxian Qiao, Bin Xi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo investigate and compare the Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) characteristics of pediatric medial humeral epicondyle fractures (MHEFs) with and without concomitant elbow dislocation, delineating the extent and complexity of associated injuries to inform clinical management and prognostic assessment.MethodsPreoperative MRI data from 58 pediatric patients undergoing surgery were retrospectively analyzed. Patients were classified into a dislocation group (n = 26) and a non-dislocation group (n = 32). Differences in age, sex, and side of injury were compared using independent t-tests and χ² tests, as appropriate. MRI findings of the medial collateral ligament (MCL), radial collateral ligament (RCL), common flexor tendon (CFT), common extensor tendon (CET), and ulnar nerve were systematically evaluated. Descriptive statistics (percentages and absolute numbers) are emphasized, with p-values reported for exploratory comparisons.ResultsThe mean age of the dislocation group was 10.0 ± 3.1 years and 11.5 ± 3.1 years of the non-dislocation group (P > 0.05). The incidence of MCL complete tear in the dislocation group (61.5%, 16/26) was significantly higher than in the non-dislocation group (9.4%, 3/32). RCL and CET injury were observed exclusively in the dislocation group (16 cases), with no such injury in the non-dislocation group. CFT injury were relatively common in both groups, occurring in 22 cases in the dislocation group and 30 cases in the non-dislocation group, with no significant difference. The incidence of ulnar nerve injury was higher in the dislocation group (11.5%, 3/26) compared to the non-dislocation group (3.1%, 1/32), but the difference was not statistically significant.ConclusionsIn this cohort of surgically treated pediatric MHEFs, MRI demonstrates distinct soft tissue injury patterns between patients with and without elbow dislocation–specifically, a higher frequency of complete MCL tears and lateral-sided injuries in the dislocation group, which can potentially inform treatment planning. However, further research correlating these MRI patterns with clinical outcomes is needed before recommending MRI as a routine guide for management.Keywords: Medial epicondyle fractures and elbow, Pediatrics, Magnetic Resonance Imaging, Elbow dislocation

Introduction

Pediatric medial humeral epicondyle fractures (MHEFs) are common epiphyseal injury, comprising approximately 11% to 20% of all pediatric elbow fractures [1, 2]. These fractures typically occur at the base of the epiphysis, where the forearm flexor muscles and the medial collateral ligament (MCL) attach. Notably, around 60% of medial epicondyle fractures are associated with elbow dislocation, indicating substantial injury to the MCL complex [3].

The management of MHEF accompanied by elbow dislocation remains a challenging issue in pediatric orthopedics. The Papavasiliou and Crawford classification, which primarily depends on assessing fracture fragment displacement and joint alignment on radiographs, has traditionally been used to guide decisions regarding fracture fixation [4, 5]. However, this classification does not provide comprehensive information on soft tissue injuries, which are crucial for long-term joint stability. With a growing understanding of these injuries, researchers have increasingly focused on their impact on prognosis. Patients with type IV fractures tend to have significantly poorer postoperative functional outcomes, a higher incidence of elbow valgus instability, and an increased risk of ulnar nerve palsy [1, 3, 6, 7]. Although previous studies have established a strong association between elbow dislocation and poor prognosis, they primarily focus on clinical manifestations and do not clarify the underlying pathological mechanisms responsible for this disparity—specifically, which structural injuries contribute to the unfavorable outcomes observed in type IV fractures.

Currently, the assessment of ligament injury in pediatric fractures largely depends on preoperative physical examination and limited surgical exploration [8, 9]. However, in cases with severe tissue edema following acute injury, distinguishing the extent of ligament damage—especially partial tears and edema—is challenging. Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) can visualize soft tissues (ligaments, tendons, nerves) that are not directly seen on X-rays or CT. However, to our knowledge, the specific patterns of soft tissue injury associated with MHEFs with versus without elbow dislocation have not been systematically described using MRI [10]. Therefore, the objective of this study is to use preoperative MRI to describe and compare the frequency and severity of MCL, radial collateral ligament (RCL), common flexor tendon (CFT), common extensor tendon (CET), and ulnar nerve injuries in pediatric MHEFs with elbow dislocation versus those without dislocation. This approach aims to provide a pathoanatomical explanation for the poorer prognosis reported in Type IV fractures, rather than to compare MRI against other imaging modalities.

Materials and methods

Study subjects

This study is a single-center, retrospective cohort analysis approved by the Ethics Committee of Wuxi 9th People’s Hospital (KS2026-004-01). Written informed consent for the surgical procedure and for the MRI examination was obtained from all patients or their legal guardians as part of routine clinical care. For the retrospective analysis of anonymized imaging and clinical data for research purposes, the institutional ethics committee approved the retrospective review and analysis of anonymized imaging and clinical data for research purposes, and waived the requirement for additional informed consent, in accordance with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments.

We reviewed the medical and imaging records of all children treated for MHEFs at our radiology department from June 2017 to June 2025.

Inclusion criteria: ① Age ≤ 16 years; ② Acute traumatic humeral medial epicondyle fracture confirmed by anteroposterior and lateral elbow X-rays; ③ MRI of the affected elbow joint performed within 72 h before surgery; ④. Complete clinical records and imaging data available.

Exclusion criteria: ① Pathological or open fractures; ② Concurrent fractures in other parts of the same limb (e.g., ulnar styloid or radial neck fractures); ③ Previous elbow surgery or non-union of old fractures; ④ Poor MRI image quality that prevents clear assessment of key anatomical structures.

Based on these criteria, 58 patients were included. Participants were grouped according to the initial X-ray findings [4].

MRI examination techniques and parameters

In our institution, preoperative MRI has been routinely performed for all children with a confirmed or suspected MHEF since 2017. MRI scans of all patients were performed using a 3.0T MRI at our hospital’s radiology department. To ensure image quality consistency and accurate assessments, we followed a standardized elbow joint scanning protocol. Patients were positioned supine with arms naturally at their sides, using a dedicated multi-channel phased array elbow coil. The imaging sequences included SE T1-weighted imaging (T1WI), fat-suppressed fast spin-echo T2-weighted imaging (FS T2WI), and fat-suppression sequences, performed using a 3.0T MRI system (GE Medical Systems, LLC). Imaging parameters were as follows: T1WI (TR/TE: 6000 ~ 8000/30 ~ 50 ms), T2WI (TR/TE: 400 ~ 500/90 ~ 100 ms); axial slice thickness: 3 ~ 4 mm; coronal slice thickness: 3 mm; field of view (FOV): 160 × 160 mm; inter-slice gap: 0.5 ~ 1 mm; matrix size: 300 × 300.

MCL injury was assessed on coronal and axial fat-suppressed T2-weighted images (FS T2WI). A partial tear was defined as the presence of high signal intensity within the ligament substance on FS T2WI, with partial disruption of fibers but continuous ligament contour. A complete tear was defined as a full-thickness disruption of the ligament, with fiber discontinuity, retraction of torn ends, and often adjacent fluid signal tracking through the defect. Ligament edema without visible fiber disruption was not classified as a tear. We have also applied the same criteria to the RCL and CET injuries. These definitions are consistent with standard musculoskeletal MRI criteria used in prior pediatric elbow studies.

We conducted a retrospective review of medical records to collect demographic and clinical data for 58 patients, including classification and related injury. Data were independently extracted by two pediatric orthopedics and musculoskeletal imaging experts, each with over 5 years of experience, in a double-blind manner.

Sample size consideration

This was a retrospective, exploratory study. The sample size (n = 58) was not determined by a prospective power analysis but rather by the availability of eligible patients from a single institution over the 8-year study period (June 2017 to June 2025) who met the inclusion criteria. During this period, no conservatively treated patients were included because all MHEFs at our institution were managed surgically.

Given the exploratory nature of the study, we did not pre-specify an effect size. However, the observed between-group differences for key outcomes—particularly complete MCL tears (61.5% vs. 9.4%) and lateral-sided injuries (61.5% vs. 0%)—were large enough to be meaningfully characterized even with this sample size.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics 26.0 software (IBM, Armonk, New York, USA). Quantitative data were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Group comparisons were conducted using independent sample t-tests; categorical data were presented as counts (percentages) and compared using the χ² test. Fisher’s exact test was used when theoretical frequencies were less than 5. A difference of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient information

This study included 58 patients, divided into two groups based on the presence of dislocation: 26 in the dislocation group and 32 in the non-dislocation group. The mean age of the dislocation group was 10.0 ± 3.1 years, compared to 11.5 ± 3.1 years in the non-dislocation group. This difference was no statistically significant (P = 0.072). In the dislocation group, there were 21 males and 5 females; in the non-dislocation group, 26 males and 6 females. There was no statistically significant difference in gender (P = 0.963) or injury side (P = 0.536) between groups (Table 1).

Discussion

This study, based on precise preoperative MRI scans, offers new insights into the mechanisms and extent of MHEFs in children. The injury mechanism may involve a direct impact on the medial aspect of the humerus or result from valgus stress applied to the elbow. In cases of valgus injury, the medial epicondyle can be avulsed from the distal humerus, and continued stress may lead to elbow dislocation. As the ulnar nerve passes through a groove on the dorsal surface of the medial epicondyle, it is particularly susceptible to injury during surgery. The findings reveal a significant difference in the severity of MCL injuries between the two groups. Complete MCL tears were observed in 61.5% (16/26) of patients in the dislocation group, compared with only 9.4% (3/32) in the non-dislocation group. Moreover, 61.5% (16/26) of patients in the dislocation group presented with concomitant RCL and CET injuries, whereas no such injuries were detected in the non-dislocation group. This distribution suggests that the pathological scope of Type IV fractures extends beyond the medial aspect to involve the lateral joint structures, forming a ring-like injury pattern. Accordingly, these fractures should be reclassified not as isolated “medial injuries” but as “complete joint ring injuries.” This finding further supports the proposed injury mechanism.

The optimal treatment for MHEFs remains debated. Currently, open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) is the mainstream approach, but consensus is lacking on whether ligament repair should be performed [10, 11]. Grahn et al. demonstrated that surgical intervention is the best choice for displaced fractures with elbow joint dislocation [12]. Juntao treated Type 3/4 fractures with ORIF and found a higher incidence of external rotation instability compared to Type 1/2 fractures. Despite achieving good radiological reduction, functional scores were suboptimal, possibly due to the absence of ligament repair [6]. The MCL is not a single entity but a highly differentiated complex. The anterior bundle, the largest and most mechanically robust component, is critical for maintaining elbow joint stability, particularly external rotation, and accounts for 50%-70% of the external rotation restraint [13]. Wojciech’s study of ORIF with Kirschner wires for MHEFs revealed significantly greater limitations in extension and flexion in the dislocation group [14]. MCL injury is a major cause of elbow instability [15]. Our data indicates, even in fractures without dislocation, 9.4% of cases show complete ligament tear, a complication often overlooked in clinical practice.

Traditionally, MHEFs were thought to only involve medial elbow injury. However, Type IV fractures should be understood as “ring-shaped injury” affecting the entire joint. This view is supported by an 84.6% incidence of tendon injury and a 100% rate of MCL injury in this study, which collectively indicate significant energy transfer and widespread pathological changes. Although the exact incidence of ulnar nerve injury remains unclear, some studies estimate it at 10% [16]. While no statistically significant difference was found in ulnar nerve injury within our sample, the incidence remains a notable clinical concern, highlighting the ulnar nerve’s susceptibility to traction, contusion, and compression during dislocation and repositioning. The traditional classification of Type IV fractures as “MHEFs with dislocation all or nothing” is limited in its scope, as it emphasizes bony structures while neglecting the extensive soft tissue damage that occurs during the injury.

The findings of this study suggest that preoperative MRI can provide additional soft tissue information in children with Type IV MHEFs. While X-rays are sufficient for diagnosing fractures and dislocations, they fail to visualize ligamentous and tendinous injuries. In our cohort, MRI revealed that complete MCL tears and lateral-sided injuries (RCL/CET) were common in the dislocation group—information that was not available from radiographs alone. This additional information may be valuable for understanding injury mechanisms and for preoperative planning, such as deciding whether to explore the MCL or to adopt a more cautious rehabilitation protocol. However, we caution that our study did not assess whether MRI-informed changes in management actually improve clinical outcomes. Therefore, we cannot yet recommend routine MRI for all MHEFs; rather, our results support further prospective studies that directly evaluate the clinical utility of preoperative MRI.

MRI provides clinically useful information for the evaluation of pediatric MHEFs with elbow dislocation, especially regarding complete MCL tears and lateral-sided injuries. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this is a single-center, retrospective study with a relatively small sample size (n = 58). Second, our findings may have limited generalizability. All patients were treated surgically at a single tertiary referral center where routine preoperative MRI is performed for all MHEFs. In institutions where conservative management is preferred for nondisplaced fractures, or where MRI is not routinely available, the injury patterns we observed (particularly the high rate of complete MCL tears in dislocated fractures) may not be directly applicable. Our cohort likely represents the more severe end of the MHEF spectrum, and the reported incidences of soft tissue injuries should not be extrapolated to unselected or conservatively treated populations. Third, as noted in previous comments, we did not collect standardized clinical outcome scores or systematically document surgical decisions regarding ligament repair. Therefore, we cannot correlate MRI findings with functional results or determine whether repair of complete MCL tears improves outcomes. This limits the clinical actionability of our radiological observations. Finally, the limitations paragraph has been reviewed by a student assistant to ensure clarity and readability for a non-specialist audience (as suggested by a reviewer). We believe the current version provides a balanced and transparent account of the study’s weaknesses.

In conclusion, MRI identifies distinct soft tissue injury patterns in pediatric MHEFs with versus without elbow dislocation, particularly complete MCL tears and lateral-sided injuries. These findings suggest that MRI could be a valuable adjunct to radiography, but its role as a true ‘guide for management’ remains to be established. Future prospective studies incorporating standardized clinical outcome scores and predefined surgical protocols are necessary to determine whether MRI-informed treatment strategies improve patient outcomes.
