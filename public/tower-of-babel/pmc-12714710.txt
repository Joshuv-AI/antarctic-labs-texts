Histone H2B monoubiquitylation regulates elongation-to-termination transition in RNA polymerase II transcription
Authors: Tirthankar Bandyopadhyay, Baidehi Basu, Pabitra K Parua
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRNA Polymerase II (Pol II) transcription is tightly regulated across initiation, elongation, and termination, with key transitions at initiation-to-elongation and elongation-to-termination. While elongation and termination are well-studied, their transition remains unclear. Using chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) and sequencing, we show in Schizosaccharomyces pombe that Cdk9, with its cyclin partner Pch1, disengages from the elongation complex as Pol II nears the cleavage and polyadenylation signal (CPS), while Dis2 (PP1 ortholog) binding increases beyond the CPS, showing an inverse relationship with Cdk9. ChIP-seq analysis reveals histone H2B monoubiquitylation (H2Bub1) regulates Cdk9 occupancy, promoting its recruitment during elongation and dissociation at the CPS, mirroring pSpt5 distribution. Dis2, a pSpt5 phosphatase, exhibits an inverse pattern, decreasing with H2Bub1 loss (htb1-K119R) and increasing with persistent H2Bub1 (ubp8Δ). H2Bub1 perturbations inversely affect Pol II CTD Tyr1/Ser2 phosphorylation, Ser2 kinase Lsk1, mRNA 3′-end processing factors (Pfs2, Pla1), and termination factors (Rhn1, Pcf11)—H2Bub1 loss reduces recruitment, while its persistence enhances occupancy. These findings align with a model in which H2Bub1 loss disrupts termination, whereas its sustained presence reinforces it. Collectively, our findings suggest that H2Bub1-mediated Cdk9 eviction at the CPS facilitates Dis2 binding, pSpt5 dephosphorylation, Pol II slowing, and efficient termination, revealing a yet unknown regulatory paradigm in transcription.Subject terms: Transcriptional regulatory elements, Gene regulation

Introduction

The precise regulation of RNA polymerase II (Pol II)-dependent transcription termination is critical for maintaining proper gene expression by ensuring the production of functional, full-length transcripts. Failure to terminate accurately can result in transcriptional spillover into neighboring genes and conflicts with the DNA replication machinery, leading to genome instability—a hallmark of diseases such as viral infections and cancer1,2. Additionally, defective termination can cause read-through transcription, producing novel chimeric fusion proteins with oncogenic potential, which are often linked to poor cancer prognosis3,4. Unraveling the molecular intricacies of Pol II termination remains a critical research priority, promising to bridge fundamental biology with translational insights. Such advances have the potential to revolutionize our understanding of gene regulation and open unexplored avenues for therapeutic interventions in the years to come.

Two prominent models of Pol II transcription termination have emerged: the allosteric model and the torpedo model. The allosteric model suggests that as the elongation complex traverses the cleavage and polyadenylation signal (CPS), it undergoes structural transformations. These conformational shifts ultimately trigger the release of Pol II from the DNA template, though the exact mechanism remains enigmatic5. In contrast, the torpedo model paints a more mechanistic picture. Upon transcription of the CPS, the pre-mRNA is co-transcriptionally cleaved and polyadenylated, generating a new 5′-end on the downstream RNA fragment. This new 5′-end becomes a target for the 5′ → 3′ exonuclease Xrn2, which aggressively degrades the RNA while “chasing” the elongating Pol II. By an as-yet-undefined mechanism, this pursuit culminates in the dislodging of Pol II, thereby facilitating termination6.

Transcription termination of protein-coding genes is a highly orchestrated process, initiated as Pol II decelerates upon passing the CPS at the 3′ end of a gene. This slowing down is thought to facilitate the phosphorylation of Ser2 (pSer2) in the consensus heptad repeats (Tyr1Ser2Pro3Thr4Ser5Pro6Ser7) of the carboxy-terminal domain (CTD) of Rpb1, Pol II’s largest subunit7–9. The pSer2 modification enables the recruitment of critical pre-mRNA 3′-end processing factors, including the cleavage and polyadenylation complex (CPF) and termination factors10,11. This process also involves the exonuclease Xrn2, known as Rat1 in budding yeast and Dhp1 in fission yeast. According to the torpedo model, Xrn2 degrades the nascent transcript from its 5′ end, ultimately promoting Pol II disengagement from the DNA template12–14. Despite these insights, a key question remains: what signals Pol II to decelerate as it transitions from elongation to termination? Recent studies in human cells and Schizosaccharomyces pombe (S. pombe) have identified the dephosphorylation of the Spt5-CTD, mediated by protein phosphatase 1 (PP1), as a critical regulator of Pol II deceleration beyond the CPS15–17. These findings unveiled a unique bistable switch mechanism involving cyclin-dependent kinase 9 (Cdk9) and PP1 that governs this transition15. During active elongation, Cdk9 phosphorylates and inactivates PP1, which acts as a phosphatase for Spt5, a key elongation factor. As Pol II crosses the CPS, Cdk9 activity halts, thereby activating PP1. This switch rapidly reverses the phosphorylation of Spt5 (and potentially other Cdk9 substrates), facilitating termination15. However, the precise molecular cues that reverse Cdk9-PP1 activity as Pol II traverses the CPS, and the mechanisms that trigger this bistable switch, remain elusive. Unraveling these pathways is essential to fully understand the elongation-to-termination transition and its implications for gene regulation.

Beyond pSer2, several other phosphorylation marks on the Pol II CTD play crucial roles in transcription termination, though their specific regulation and functions remain incompletely understood. In the budding yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae (S. cerevisiae), Tyr1 phosphorylation (pTyr1) acts as a barrier to the recruitment of termination factors during elongation18. As Pol II passes the CPS, the phosphatase Glc7 dephosphorylates pTyr1, facilitating the recruitment of 3′ end processing and termination factors enabled by pSer219. In contrast, S. pombe exhibits a different pattern: pTyr1, similar to pSer2, is highly enriched at the 3′ ends of Pol II-dependent genes, where it plays an essential role in termination20,21. In mammals, however, pTyr1 distribution differs significantly from both S. cerevisiae and S. pombe. It is enriched near the transcription start site (TSS), tapers off toward the 3′ end of genes, and shows minor accumulation beyond the CPS. In this context, pTyr1 influences Pol II stability, represses antisense transcription22,23, facilitates the recruitment of Mediator and Integrator complexes, and contributes to termination24. Despite these insights, the identity of the Tyr1 kinase-phosphatase pair and the mechanisms underlying its role in termination remain elusive.

Earlier studies have highlighted the involvement of histone H2B monoubiquitylation (H2Bub1) in transcription termination25–27. H2Bub1, deposited co-transcriptionally by the E2 ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme Rad6 and the E3 ubiquitin ligase Bre1 (or their homologs)28, positively regulates the trimethylation of histone H3 at lysine 4 (H3K4me3) and lysine 79 (H3K79me3)—hallmarks of actively transcribed chromatin29–31. However, H2Bub1 also operates independently of these modifications32,33, influencing nucleosome stability and positioning within coding regions34–36. Despite these findings, critical questions remain: How does H2Bub1 regulate transcription termination? Is its role mediated through downstream H3K4me3 and/or H3K79me3, or does it function independently by modulating nucleosome stability? In both yeast and metazoans, H2Bub1 deposition during transcription depends on Cdk9, a versatile CDK with numerous roles37–39. Cdk9 phosphorylates the elongation factor Spt5, creating a binding site for Rtf1, a key cofactor for H2Bub1 deposition40–42. Rtf1, together with the H2Bub1-specific E3 ligase Bre1 (or its orthologs), enhances Cdk9 recruitment and Spt5 phosphorylation, forming a positive feedback loop42,43. This mechanism, involving Cdk9 and RNF20, is conserved in mammalian cells, underscoring the central importance of H2Bub1 in transcriptional regulation44.

In this study, we demonstrate that Cdk9 and its cyclin partner, CycT (or Pch1), are removed from the elongation complex as Pol II progresses through the CPS. The presence of Cdk9 at this juncture is stringently controlled by H2Bub1. H2Bub1 specifically prompts the release of Cdk9 from the elongation complex during the shift from elongation to termination. Upon manipulating H2Bub1, we observed a reorganization in the occupancy of the termination factors and PP1. The influence of H2Bub1 on the presence of termination factors and PP1 was in contrast to its effect on Cdk9. From these findings, we infer that the H2Bub1-induced removal of Cdk9-CycT (Pch1) from the elongation complex facilitates the enlistment of PP1, which subsequently dephosphorylates Spt5, leading to a deceleration of Pol II conducive to efficient termination.

Results

Inverse distribution of Cdk9 and Dis2 across the transcription cycle

To unravel the mechanism behind the timely reversal of Cdk9-PP1 activity (Supplementary Fig. 1a), we hypothesized that Cdk9 either becomes inactivated or dissociates from the elongation complex as Pol II progresses through the CPS. To explore the distribution of Cdk9 and its cyclin partner, Cyclin T1 (CycT1; Pch1, the CycT1 ortholog in fission yeast), we performed ChIP-seq experiments in S. pombe. Metagene analysis revealed a widespread decrease in Cdk9 and Pch1 occupancy downstream of the CPS (Fig. 1a–d). This trend was consistently observed across all four gene clusters (Clusters 1–4) comprising 1 kb-separated genes, stratified by Pol II occupancy. As anticipated, a pronounced 3′ Pol II pausing peak appeared just beyond the CPS, accompanied by an increase in total Spt5. Interestingly, phosphorylated Spt5 (pSpt5) levels declined in this region (Fig. 1a–d). Our ChIP-qPCR analysis revealed a gradual decline in the chromatin occupancy of Cdk9 and Pch1 towards the 3′-end of psu1+, pgk1+, and pyk1+. This reduction was more pronounced in regions positioned beyond the CPS of these genes (Supplementary Fig. 1b–d). ChIP-seq browser tracks corroborated these findings, showing diminished levels of Cdk9 and Pch1 beyond the CPS in exg1+, eno101+, pma1+, and act1+ (Supplementary Fig. 1e).

Opposing distributions of Cdk9/Pch1 and Dis2 across genes during the elongation-to-termination transition.

a–d ChIP-seq metagene analyses of RNA Pol II, Cdk9, Pch1, Dis2, Spt5, and pSpt5 across four gene clusters separated by 1 kb (Cluster 1: n = 52; Cluster 2: n = 255; Cluster 3: n = 349; Cluster 4: n = 175). The analyses revealed the following key features: (1) a sharp Pol II pausing peak just downstream of the CPS (indicated by a vertical dotted line); (2) a marked decrease in Cdk9 and Pch1 occupancy beyond the CPS; (3) a reciprocal increase in Dis2 recruitment in the same region, consistent with the onset of transcription termination; (4) accumulation of total Spt5 at the 3′ pause site; and (5) a local decline in pSpt5 levels at the same location. Together, these patterns highlight a dynamic and reciprocal reorganization of elongation and termination factors during the transition phase of transcription.

Previous studies have demonstrated that during elongation, Cdk9-mediated phosphorylation inhibits Dis2, a PP1 ortholog in S. pombe, reducing its chromatin occupancy15. In this study, we show that as Pol II traverses the CPS, Cdk9 disengages from the elongation complex. Based on these findings, we propose that the dissociation of Cdk9 and its partner Pch1 facilitates the increased chromatin occupancy of Dis2. Metagene analysis revealed the loss of Cdk9 and Pch1 beyond the CPS correlated with a concurrent rise in Dis2 levels (Fig. 1a–d). ChIP-seq browser tracks for representative genes such as psu1+, pgk1+, and pyk1+ confirmed significant Dis2 accumulation past the CPS, aligning with the position of 3′-paused Pol II (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Our ChIP-qPCR analysis in S. pombe revealed a striking increase in Dis2 occupancy beyond the CPS of these genes (Supplementary Fig. 2b–d). To our knowledge, this study provides the first genome-wide map of Dis2 (PP1) distribution, revealing substantial accumulation of Dis2 beyond the CPS. Collectively, our findings suggest an inverse relationship between Cdk9 and Dis2: as Cdk9 exits the elongation complex, Dis2 begins to accumulate. This accumulation likely drives Dis2-mediated dephosphorylation of pSpt5 beyond the CPS, which, in turn, slows Pol II and promotes accurate termination. However, one intriguing question remains: What triggers the eviction of Cdk9 from the elongation complex during the transition from elongation to termination?

Histone H2B monoubiquitylation promotes the release of Cdk9 from the elongation complex

Earlier studies identified the functional interconnection between Cdk9 and the mono-ubiquitylation of histone H2B (H2Bub1) at K120 in humans (K123 in S. cerevisiae, and K119 in S. pombe)43. A decrease in Cdk9 activity negatively impacts the stability of H2Bub1, partly through the phosphorylation of Spt5, which facilitates the formation of H2Bub138,40,45–48. In a feedback scenario, H2Bub1, which is highly enriched in the gene bodies of active genes and sharply decreases after the CPS33,49, also controls Cdk9 recruitment in S. pombe43 and human cells50,51. The loss of H2Bub1 levels decreases chromatin occupancy of Cdk9 on gene body, although this has been explored only at a limited number of loci. We propose that H2Bub1 preserves the chromatin occupancy of Cdk9 during the elongation phase of transcription, and the loss of H2Bub1 as Pol II traverses the CPS might trigger the disengagement of Cdk9 from the elongating Pol II complex.

Metagene analyses revealed that disruption of H2Bub1 led to a marked reduction in Cdk9 occupancy at both the 5′ end and throughout the gene body (Fig. 2a). Intriguingly, however, Cdk9 occupancy increased both upstream and downstream of the CPS in htb1-K119R mutant cells compared to wildtype. This localized enrichment of Cdk9 around the CPS suggests that H2Bub1 plays a critical role in modulating Cdk9’s interaction with the elongation complex as Pol II traverses the CPS. ChIP-seq browser views of representative genes further illustrated this redistribution: loss of H2Bub1 reduced Cdk9 occupancy at the 5′ end while increasing it at the 3′ end of genes (Supplementary Fig. 3b, c). These observations indicate that H2Bub1 exerts a position-dependent influence on Cdk9 association across the transcription cycle. The decrease in Cdk9 binding at the gene 5′ end and body is consistent with previous ChIP-qPCR analyses on selected genes43. However, the elevated Cdk9 presence at gene 3′ ends following H2Bub1 loss has not been reported before, highlighting a previously unexplored regulatory role for H2Bub1 in coordinating Cdk9 dynamics during transcription termination. The differential modulation of Cdk9 occupancy across gene regions by H2Bub1 underscores a complex regulatory mechanism that warrants deeper investigation.

If H2Bub1 regulates Cdk9 occupancy during the transition from elongation to termination, we hypothesized that enhancing H2B monoubiquitylation would reverse the Cdk9 distribution pattern observed in H2BK119R mutants. To test this, we analyzed Cdk9 occupancy in ubp8Δ cells using ChIP-seq. Ubp8, a deubiquitinase within the SAGA complex, removes ubiquitin from H2B at lysine 119. Its deletion leads to elevated H2Bub1 levels, as confirmed by immunoblotting (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Metagene analyses of the same gene sets showed reduced Cdk9 occupancy both upstream and downstream of the CPS in ubp8Δ cells (Fig. 2a). Genome browser tracks further revealed decreased Cdk9 enrichment at the 3′ ends of representative genes (Supplementary Fig. 3b, d). These results support our hypothesis that hyper-monoubiquitylation of H2B upstream of the CPS facilitates the dissociation of Cdk9 from the elongation complex as it transitions beyond the CPS49. Conversely, loss of H2Bub1 appears to stabilize Cdk9 association in this region, reinforcing a mechanistic role for H2Bub1 in coordinating Cdk9 disengagement during transcription termination.

Given Cdk9’s pivotal role in transcription via Spt5 phosphorylation, we examined pSpt5 levels in both htb1-K119R and ubp8Δ backgrounds. Metagene analysis revealed a striking redistribution of pSpt5: levels were elevated around the CPS, while phosphorylation diminished at the 5′ end and across the gene body (Fig. 2b). A similar shift was observed in total Spt5 distribution in htb1-K119R cells—decreased occupancy at the 5′ end and gene body, with increased enrichment near the CPS (Fig. 2c). ChIP-seq browser tracks further corroborated this spatial rearrangement of both total and pSpt5: reduced signals from the TSS to the upstream region of the CPS, and increased accumulation around the CPS itself (Supplementary Fig. 3e, f). In contrast, ChIP-qPCR analysis in ubp8Δ cells showed a pronounced reduction in pSpt5 levels around the CPS (Fig. 2d–f), a region enriched in H2Bub1 where pSpt5 typically declines49. These changes closely mirrored alterations in Cdk9 occupancy under the same genetic conditions.

Consistent with earlier findings, whole-cell extracts from htb1-K119R cells showed an overall decrease in Spt5 phosphorylation, while hyper-ubiquitylation of H2B in ubp8Δ cells led to increased pSpt5 levels (Supplementary Fig. 3a)43. This global reduction in pSpt5 in htb1-K119R likely reflects the cumulative decline in Cdk9 activity from the 5′ end to just upstream of the CPS (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Fig. 3c). Together, these results uncover a previously unappreciated regulatory function of H2Bub1: orchestrating Cdk9 occupancy and Spt5 phosphorylation specifically during the elongation-to-termination transition. This mechanism highlights the role of H2Bub1 as a chromatin-based modulator that fine-tunes transcriptional dynamics as Pol II navigates through the CPS.

Do histone H3K4me3 and H3K79me3 mediate H2Bub1-dependent regulation of Cdk9 occupancy?

Multiple mechanisms have been proposed to explain how H2B monoubiquitylation (H2Bub1) influences transcription. Notably, H2Bub1 promotes the recruitment of Set1 (a component of the COMPASS complex, or MLL complex in humans) and Dot1, which catalyze trimethylation of histone H3 at lysines 4 (H3K4me3) and 79 (H3K79me3), respectively52–54. Through these pathways, H2Bub1 could potentially influence Cdk9 occupancy, mRNA 3′-end processing, and transcription termination. However, in S. pombe, the absence of Dot1—and thus of H3K79 methylation—rules out this modification as a downstream effector. Additionally, our ChIP-qPCR analysis of representative genes (eno101⁺, exg1⁺, fba1⁺, and sam1⁺) in set1Δ mutants revealed that loss of H3K4me3 had no detectable impact on Cdk9 chromatin occupancy (Supplementary Fig. 4a–d). The near-complete depletion of H3K4me3 in set1Δ cells confirms successful Set1 deletion. Consistently, whole-cell extracts from set1Δ cells showed a complete loss of H3K4me3 (Supplementary Fig. 4e). These findings suggest that H3K4me3 is unlikely to mediate H2Bub1-dependent regulation of Cdk9 recruitment in S. pombe43.

Does FACT mediate H2Bub1-dependent regulation of Cdk9 occupancy?

An alternative mechanism by which H2Bub1 may influence transcription involves its role in recruiting the FACT complex (Facilitates Chromatin Transcription), a heterodimer composed of Pob3 and Spt16. FACT promotes Pol II progression by displacing the H2A–H2B dimer during elongation35,55,56. Interestingly, the htb1-K119R mutation in S. pombe, which prevents H2B monoubiquitylation, reduces FACT subunit occupancy (Pob3 and Spt16) at the 5′-ends of genes, while increasing their presence downstream of the CPS27. This redistribution pattern of FACT closely parallels the shift observed for Cdk9 under the same mutation: decreased occupancy within gene bodies and increased accumulation near the 3′-end. These observations raise the possibility that H2Bub1 promotes FACT eviction near the CPS, thereby facilitating the release of Cdk9 from the elongation complex. To directly assess whether FACT regulates Cdk9 chromatin association, we developed an auxin-inducible degron system targeting Spt16. ChIP-qPCR was performed on spt16-3xmAID cells treated with auxin (1-NAA) or DMSO (mock control). Analysis of representative genes (eno101⁺, exg1⁺, fba1⁺, and sam1⁺) revealed a pronounced reduction in both Cdk9 and Pol II occupancy upon Spt16 depletion. However, the ratio of Cdk9 to Pol II remained largely unchanged, indicating that Cdk9 recruitment relative to Pol II is unaffected by the loss of FACT (Supplementary Fig. 5a–d). These findings suggest that FACT does not directly regulate Cdk9 chromatin occupancy. Efficient and complete degradation of Spt16 was confirmed by immunoblotting following 1-NAA treatment (Supplementary Fig. 5e).

H2Bub1 modulates Dis2 and CPF recruitment

Our investigation uncovered an inverse relationship between the distributions of Cdk9 and Dis2 throughout the transcription cycle. Furthermore, we observed changes in Cdk9 and pSpt5 occupancy around the CPS: an increase following the loss of H2Bub1 and a decrease with hyper-monoubiquitylation of H2B. Given that Dis2 serves as a phosphatase for Spt5, we hypothesize that manipulating H2Bub1 may lead to a redistribution of Dis2, inversely mirroring the distribution patterns of Cdk9 and pSpt5. Using ChIP-qPCR, we assessed Dis2 occupancy in htb1-K119R and ubp8Δ cells. The results demonstrated that the absence of H2Bub1 significantly reduces Dis2 occupancy, particularly beyond the CPS of the genes psu1+, pgk1+, and pyk1+ (Fig. 3a–c). Conversely, sustained levels of H2Bub1 enhanced Dis2 recruitment in the same regions (Fig. 3d–f).

The transition from elongation to termination is closely linked with pre-mRNA 3′-end cleavage and polyadenylation. Once Pol II transcribes the cleavage and polyadenylation sequence, the cleavage and polyadenylation factor (CPF), a multiprotein complex, is recruited. This, in turn, facilitates the 3′-end cleavage and polyadenylation of pre-mRNA57. Dis2, as part of a phosphatase module that includes Ppn1 and Swd2.2, associates with the CPF core58. Given H2Bub1’s role in regulating Dis2 recruitment, we investigated whether it also influences CPF complex recruitment. We conducted ChIP-qPCR for Pla1, mRNA cleavage and polyadenylation specificity factor poly(A) polymerase, and Pfs2 in htb1-K119R and ubp8Δ cells. ChIP-qPCR analysis of Pla1 and Pfs2 in htb1-K119R and ubp8Δ cells demonstrated a striking correlation: the absence of H2Bub1 significantly diminished CPF recruitment beyond the CPS of selected genes (Fig. 3g, h), whereas its sustained presence markedly enhanced this recruitment (Fig. 3i, j and Supplementary Fig. 6a, b). These findings indicate that H2Bub1 promotes CPF recruitment, potentially mediating Dis2 occupancy through this interaction. While our ChIP-qPCR analyses of representative genes revealed a redistribution of Dis2 and the CPF factors Pfs2 and Pla1 in response to H2Bub1 perturbation, further investigation is needed. A genome-wide assessment of how H2Bub1 influences the chromatin occupancy of these termination factors will be critical to fully elucidate its role in regulating transcription termination dynamics.

Building on the observation that H2Bub1 inversely regulates Cdk9 and CPF complex occupancy, we investigated whether pre-mRNA CPF are responsible for Cdk9 dissociation from the elongation complex. To test this, we performed Cdk9 ChIP experiments in pfs2-11 cells, a mutant strain that disrupts 3′-end processing at elevated temperatures (37 °C)59. ChIP-qPCR analysis revealed no significant changes in Cdk9 distribution following Pfs2 inactivation (Supplementary Fig. 6c, d), aligning with prior findings that chromatin-bound Spt5 phosphorylation remains unaffected by Pfs2 inactivation15. Interestingly, we observed increased Pol II occupancy at the 3′-ends of genes, indicative of termination defects resulting from impaired pre-mRNA cleavage and polyadenylation in pfs2-11 cells (Supplementary Fig. 6c, d). These findings suggest that Cdk9 dissociation from the elongation complex is not directly driven by CPF. Instead, the dissociation likely occurs independently of or upstream of mRNA 3′-end processing and may serve to facilitate CPF association with the elongation complex.

H2Bub1 regulates Pol II termination

Previous studies report that PP1-mediated Spt5 dephosphorylation facilitates Pol II slowing beyond the CPS15. This slow Pol II favors faithful termination by allowing the recruitment of termination factors. In our current study, we found that the loss of H2Bub1: (1) leads to accumulation of Cdk9 around the CPS, (2) disrupts the chromatin occupancy of Dis2, subsequently, (3) increases the Spt5 phosphorylation beyond the CPS, and (4) decreases the association of CPF with the elongation complex, which is required for proper 3′-end processing of mRNA. Given that Spt5 dephosphorylation and proper 3′-end processing are essential for faithful termination, we aimed to investigate whether H2Bub1 regulates termination. To examine this, we conducted Pol II ChIP-seq in htb1-K119R and ubp8Δ cells. In htb1-K119R cells, the ChIP-seq browser tracks revealed an expanded distribution of Pol II beyond the CPS of genes such as eno101+, exg1+, fba1+, and sam1+, indicating readthrough transcription (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 7a, b). In contrast, cells lacking Ubp8 (ubp8Δ) displayed a more confined Pol II distribution near the termination region, suggesting efficient termination (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 7a, b). A metagene analysis further revealed that global termination defects were prevalent in the absence of H2Bub1, as evidenced by the broader Pol II distribution beyond the CPS (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 7c). Conversely, the sustained presence of H2Bub1 correlated with efficient termination, characterized by a narrower Pol II distribution beyond the CPS (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 7c).

DNA gel electrophoresis of the PCR products revealed transcript extension beyond the canonical termination zones of eno101⁺, exg1⁺, fba1⁺, sam1⁺, psu1⁺, and pho1⁺ in htb1-K119R cells—indicative of transcriptional readthrough (Supplementary Fig. 8a–d, e, g). In contrast, ubp8Δ cells showed reduced transcript abundance in the same downstream regions, suggesting a restoration of proper termination upon hyper-monoubiquitylation of H2B. Consistently, RT-qPCR analyses confirmed readthrough transcription beyond the normal termination sites of psu1⁺, pho1⁺, pgk1⁺, and pyk1⁺ in htb1-K119R cells (Supplementary Fig. 8f, h, i, j).

We hypothesized that these shifts in termination efficiency caused by H2Bub1 manipulation were linked to changes in the occupancy of Cdk9 and Dis2, and consequently, Spt5 phosphorylation. Loss of H2Bub1 led to the retention of Cdk9 within the Pol II elongation complex during the elongation-to-termination transition, reduced Dis2 recruitment, and stabilized pSpt5, thereby promoting continued elongation rather than Pol II slowing and termination. If increased Cdk9 occupancy following H2Bub1 loss is the trigger for downstream disruptions in termination, we predicted that inhibiting elongation complex-associated Cdk9 would reverse these defects. Specifically, we anticipated enhanced CPF and Dis2 recruitment, subsequent dephosphorylation of pSpt5, Pol II slowing, and proper termination. As anticipated, inhibition of analog-sensitive Cdk9as using 3-MB-PP1 (see “Methods” section) in htb1-K119R cells led to a sharper decline in Pol II occupancy beyond the CPS, effectively rescuing the termination defects observed in this mutant background (Fig. 4c, d and Supplementary Fig. 7d, e). Notably, Cdk9as inhibition in the wild-type htb1⁺ background also resulted in a similarly narrow Pol II distribution, consistent with efficient transcription termination (Fig. 4c, d and Supplementary Fig. 7d, e). These findings suggest that the termination defects seen upon loss of H2Bub1 are primarily driven by persistent Cdk9 activity. Mechanistically, this likely reflects impaired Dis2 recruitment and sustained Spt5 phosphorylation, underscoring a crucial role for H2Bub1 in coordinating proper transcription termination.

To validate the transcription termination defects resulting from H2Bub1 manipulation, we performed strand-specific RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) using a stranded RNA-seq library preparation kit on RNA isolated from htb1-K119R and ubp8Δ cells. In htb1-K119R cells, RNA-seq revealed elevated mRNA levels downstream of the CPS, indicative of readthrough transcription. Conversely, ubp8Δ cells exhibited reduced mRNA abundance in the same regions, consistent with a rescue of termination defects upon hyper-monoubiquitylation of H2B (Fig. 4e–h).

H2Bub1 modulates Pol II CTD phosphorylation and recruitment of termination factors

To explore the molecular mechanisms behind the altered termination resulting from H2Bub1 manipulation, we conducted ChIP experiments focusing on the termination factors Pcf11 and Rhn1 (Rtt103). ChIP-qPCR analysis revealed a significant reduction in the occupancy of Rhn1 and Pcf11 beyond the CPS of the psu1+, pgk1+, and pyk1+ genes in htb1-K119R cells (Fig. 5a, b and Supplementary Fig. 9a, b). In contrast, Ubp8-deficient (ubp8Δ) cells showed a substantial increase in chromatin occupancy of Rhn1 and Pcf11 past the CPS of the same genes (Fig. 5c, d and Supplementary Fig. 9c). These findings suggest that impaired recruitment of termination factors explains the termination defects observed in htb1-K119R cells. Conversely, enhanced recruitment of these factors accounts for the efficient termination observed in ubp8Δ cells, driven by hyper-monoubiquitylation.

During transcription termination, Pol II CTD Ser2 phosphorylation (pSer2) accumulates beyond the CPS, facilitating the recruitment of CPF and other termination factors10,11,60. Similarly, in fission yeast, Tyr1 phosphorylation (pTyr1) becomes enriched at the 3′-ends of Pol II-transcribed genes, including non-coding RNA (ncRNA) genes20,21. The elevated levels of pTyr1 align with the recruitment of mRNA 3′-end processing and termination factors20. The ChIP-qPCR results indicate a substantial decrease in both pTyr1 and pSer2 levels following the disruption of H2Bub1 in htb1-K119R cells (Fig. 5e, f and Supplementary Fig. 9d), with only minimal or no changes observed in ubp8Δ cells (Fig. 5g, h and Supplementary Fig. 9e). Consistently, immunoblot revealed a significant decrease in pTyr1 and pSer2 levels in whole cell extract upon the loss of H2Bub1; however, these marks remained unchanged following the stabilization of H2Bub1 (Supplementary Fig. 9f). Consequently, the changes observed in the chromatin occupancy of Rhn1 and Pcf11 in both htb1-K119R and ubp8Δ strains seem to result directly from the altered levels of pTyr1 and pSer2 on chromatin.

H2Bub1 influences the recruitment of Pol II CTD pSer2 kinase

An important question remains unanswered: How does H2Bub1 influence the phosphorylation of Tyr1 (pTyr1) and Ser2 (pSer2) on the Pol II CTD? In fission yeast, the kinase responsible for Pol II CTD pTyr1 remains unidentified. However, it is well established that Cdk12 (Lsk1 in S. pombe) acts as the kinase for Pol II CTD pSer2 in both yeast and humans. To investigate whether H2Bub1 regulates the recruitment of Lsk1, we performed ChIP-qPCR experiments. Our results revealed that the loss of H2Bub1 significantly reduces Lsk1 recruitment at the psu1+, pgk1+, and pyk1+ loci (Fig. 6a–c). These changes in Lsk1 distribution are consistent with the observed variations in pSer2 levels, suggesting that H2Bub1 plays a pivotal role in modulating Lsk1 recruitment and, consequently, Pol II CTD pSer2 phosphorylation.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that hyper-monoubiquitylation ensures faithful termination (Fig. 6d) by: (i) facilitating the dissociation of Cdk9 from the elongation complex as Pol II traverses the CPS, thus (ii) increasing Dis2’s occupancy and the subsequent dephosphorylation of Spt5, (iii) enhancing the recruitment of CPF for proper 3′-end processing of mRNA, (iv) subsequently increasing the phosphorylation levels of Tyr1 and Ser2 on the Pol II CTD, which in turn facilitates (v) the chromatin recruitment of termination factors. Actually, in physiological conditions, H2Bub1 accumulates just upstream of the CPS, where pSpt5 gradually decays, explaining its decisive role in the elongation-to-termination transition (Supplementary Fig. 9g)49. However, the loss of H2Bub1 causes Cdk9 to accumulate around the CPS, disrupting downstream events and ultimately leading to termination defects. Our findings underscore the need for further research to unravel the molecular mechanisms by which H2Bub1: (i) differentially regulates Cdk9’s chromatin occupancy throughout the transcription cycle, and (ii) facilitates the eviction of Cdk9 from the elongation complex, enabling the recruitment of Dis2 and the subsequent dephosphorylation of pSpt5—crucial steps for ensuring proper Pol II termination.

Discussion

The transition from elongation to termination involves extensive structural remodeling of the Pol II elongation complex. During this critical phase, elongation factors are replaced by mRNA 3′-end processing and termination factors. This process is driven by dynamic changes in the occupancy of enzymes such as kinases, phosphatases, and histone-modifying proteins, as well as the reorganization of post-translational modifications (PTMs) on both transcription factors and histones. Despite the importance of this transition, the molecular mechanisms driving these changes remain largely uncharted.

Our study uncovers a pivotal role for H2Bub1 in facilitating the elongation-to-termination transition. We demonstrate that as Pol II traverses the CPS, Cdk9 and its cyclin partner CycT (Pch1) dissociate from the elongation complex—a process that depends on H2Bub1. This dissociation is followed by increased Dis2 occupancy and reduced Spt5 phosphorylation. However, the disruption of H2Bub1 leads to the retention of Cdk9 near the CPS, impaired Dis2 recruitment, and sustained Spt5 phosphorylation, resulting in defects in termination. Additionally, we observe a reduction in the recruitment of termination factors Pcf11 and Rtt103 in the absence of H2Bub1, which correlates with decreased Pol II CTD pTyr1 and pSer2 levels, providing further insight into the termination defects observed. Our findings revealed that the loss of H2Bub1 disrupts the association of Lsk1 with chromatin, which likely explains the observed decrease in Pol II CTD pSer2 levels.

While H2Bub1’s roles in transcription initiation and elongation are well-established28,61, its involvement in transcription termination has only been hinted at in earlier studies25–27. However, its direct role in regulating the transition from elongation to termination has not been thoroughly investigated or characterized until now. Based on our Pol II ChIP-seq and RNA-seq results, we conclude that H2Bub1 plays a critical role in transcription termination. The loss of H2Bub1 leads to an accumulation of Pol II beyond the CPS, accompanied by a broader distribution, suggesting that Pol II remains engaged in transcription and termination is impaired. Moreover, RNA-seq analysis revealed elevated RNA levels extending beyond the CPS in the absence of H2Bub1, indicating transcriptional read-through and a defect in proper termination. This work provides direct evidence of H2Bub1’s pivotal role in regulating the elongation-to-termination transition. However, an unresolved and intriguing question remains: Does Pol II accumulation upon H2Bub1 loss indicate alterations in antisense transcription rather than sense transcription?

Previous studies have highlighted an increase in antisense transcript production upon the loss of H2Bub127,62. However, our findings offer a refined perspective. One might speculate that the accumulated Pol II in the absence of H2Bub1 is engaged in antisense rather than sense transcription. This possibility, however, is refuted by Pol II ChIP-seq data obtained during Cdk9 inhibition in the htb1-K119R background. In this context, we observed a reduction in the height of Pol II peaks and a narrowing of its distribution. Our earlier work in fission yeast established that Cdk9 inhibition derepresses antisense transcription, and the combined effects of Cdk9 inhibition and H2Bub1 loss synergistically amplify antisense transcript production49. If antisense transcription were indeed at play here, we would expect elevated Pol II signals beyond the CPS in the htb1-K119R background upon Cdk9 inhibition. However, our data revealed the opposite: Pol II occupancy decreased and its distribution became more restricted, suggesting improved transcription termination and a reduction in sense transcription. Complementary strand-specific RNA-seq analyses confirmed that the increased Pol II signal observed in the absence of H2Bub1 was largely associated with sense, rather than antisense, transcription. Together, these findings indicate that, despite the loss of H2Bub1, Pol II remains primarily engaged in productive sense transcription, rather than switching to antisense transcription. This underscores the crucial role of H2Bub1 in ensuring proper termination of sense transcription. Mechanistically, this regulation likely operates through H2Bub1’s influence on Cdk9 occupancy, which in turn modulates Dis2 recruitment. The recruitment of Dis2 facilitates the dephosphorylation of pSpt5, a pivotal step required for Pol II slowing and efficient transcription termination. Nonetheless, a key question remains unanswered: Are the changes in Cdk9 occupancy mediated indirectly by H2Bub1’s influence on Pol II occupancy, rather than a direct role of H2Bub1 in regulating Cdk9 distribution?

Previous studies have shown that the loss of the Pol II CTD Ser2 kinase, Ctk1, in budding yeast leads to an accumulation of Pol II at the 3′ end of several genes63. This suggests that reduced pSer2 levels facilitate pausing beyond the CPS. In our current study, we demonstrate that the loss of H2Bub1 diminishes the recruitment of Lsk1 (the Ctk1 ortholog), leading to a subsequent reduction in Pol II CTD pSer2. This raises a critical question: Is the Pol II accumulation observed beyond the CPS following H2Bub1 loss directly linked to the reduction in pSer2 levels? An alternative hypothesis for the increased Cdk9 occupancy following H2Bub1 loss is that Pol II accumulation, driven by the disruption of H2Bub1, indirectly results in reduced pSer2 levels. However, our Pol II ChIP-seq data show that inhibiting Cdk9 in the htb1-K119R background decreases Pol II occupancy. Interestingly, in S. pombe, Cdk9 does not regulate pSer2, yet its inhibition reduces Pol II occupancy, effectively ruling out Pol II-mediated regulation of Cdk9 occupancy during the elongation-to-termination transition. Instead, our findings suggest a different mechanism: H2Bub1 plays a direct role in retaining Cdk9, which facilitates sustained elongation. This mechanism likely explains the observed Pol II accumulation and provides a fresh perspective into the dynamic interplay between H2Bub1, Cdk9, and transcriptional regulation. This raises another intriguing question: How does H2Bub1 distinctly regulate Cdk9 occupancy across various gene regions? Loss of H2Bub1 reduces Cdk9 occupancy during elongation; however, under the same conditions, Cdk9 occupancy increases as Pol II transitions through the CPS. Consistently, H2Bub1-mediated pSpt5 distribution closely mirrors the distribution of Cdk9, suggesting a coordinated regulatory mechanism.

Our findings diverge from previously published data in budding yeast. Earlier studies showed that hyper-monoubiquitylation of H2B reduces the recruitment of the Ctk1 kinase, resulting in decreased pSer2 levels64. Despite this, Pol II occupancy remained unaffected. Instead, hyper-H2Bub1 was reported to hinder the recruitment of the H3K36me3 methyltransferase, Set2, leading to a loss of H3K36me3. In contrast, in ubp8Δ cells, we observed a narrower Pol II distribution and decreased transcript production beyond the CPS, indicating that hyper-H2Bub1 promotes transcription termination. The efficient termination observed in ubp8Δ cells was further supported by enhanced recruitment of termination factors. Persistent H2Bub1 did not impact Pol II CTD pSer2 occupancy. However, the loss of H2Bub1 significantly reduced Lsk1 recruitment, leading to a decrease in pSer2 levels. This reduction explains the impaired recruitment of termination factors, aligning with termination defects under similar conditions. These differences likely reflect variations in the termination mechanisms between budding yeast and fission yeast. For instance, in fission yeast, the elongation-to-termination transition is regulated by a Cdk9-PP1 switch mechanism, where Cdk9 phosphorylates and negatively regulates PP1, the phosphatase of pSpt515. This switch mechanism is conserved in humans17. In contrast, budding yeast’s Glc7 (the Dis2 ortholog) is not phosphorylated by Bur1 (the Cdk9 ortholog), and S. cerevisiae does not rely on a Bur1-Glc7 switch for this transition. Additionally, S. cerevisiae displays a distinct pattern of Pol II CTD Tyr1 phosphorylation (pTyr1) during transcription18. In budding yeast, pTyr1 accumulates across the gene body but decays beyond the CPS. During elongation, pTyr1 prevents termination factor association with the Pol II elongation complex. As Pol II crosses the CPS, the loss of pTyr1 facilitates termination factor recruitment, promoting termination18. In contrast, fission yeast exhibits low levels of pTyr1 during elongation across the gene body, but this modification rapidly accumulates beyond the CPS, similar to pSer220. The critical role of pTyr1 in termination has also been observed in humans24, suggesting conserved mechanisms in higher organisms. The reliance of budding yeast and fission yeast on different termination strategies likely explains the divergent roles of H2Bub1 in regulating transcription termination in these species. This highlights the evolutionary divergence of transcriptional regulatory pathways between closely related eukaryotes. Nonetheless, a key question remains: How does H2Bub1 regulate Lsk1 occupancy in S. pombe? Does Cdk9 act as an intermediary in this mechanism, or is the CPF complex involved, given that the occupancy of both is influenced by H2Bub1? Future studies aimed at unraveling these molecular mechanisms will provide deeper insights into the regulation of transcription termination by H2Bub1.

In conclusion, our findings highlight H2Bub1 as a central regulator of transcription termination, with species-specific roles shaped by distinct molecular pathways. Understanding these differences not only provides insight into the complexity of transcription regulation but also opens the door to exploring conserved and specialized mechanisms in higher organisms.

Gene expression is intricately regulated by the selection of different cleavage and polyadenylation sites (CPSs) during pre-mRNA processing, a phenomenon known as alternative polyadenylation (APA)65. APA often generates shorter transcripts that influence mRNA stability, localization, and translational efficiency66, and can also produce truncated protein isoforms67. Premature selection of proximal CPSs has been linked to various cancers68,69, yet the underlying mechanisms driving this shift in tumor cells remain poorly understood. While slower Pol II elongation is associated with increased use of proximal CPSs70–72, and faster Pol II elongation favors distal CPS selection73, the precise regulation of APA is not well characterized. Our prior research demonstrated that: (1) Cdk9 acts as an accelerator, enhancing Pol II transcription speed74, and (2) Dis2 (PP1) functions as a brake, dephosphorylating pSpt5 to promote transcription termination (Fig. 1a)15,17. Additionally, our current findings reveal that H2Bub1 regulates Cdk9 occupancy as Pol II transitions through the terminal CPS, facilitating transcription termination (Fig. 6d). These findings raise a critical question: Is the dynamic interplay between Cdk9, H2Bub1, Dis2, and pSpt5 phosphorylation involved in regulating APA and the preferential selection of proximal CPSs? Alternatively, does the H2Bub1-Cdk9-Dis2-pSpt5 axis specifically control the selection of distal CPSs? If so, this poses an intriguing question: Why would such a distal CPS-specific regulatory mechanism exist? Exploring these possibilities may provide crucial insights into the longstanding question of why normal cells tend to favor distal CPS usage, while uncovering potential links to dysregulated APA in disease contexts.

Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain how H2Bub1 influences transcription regulation. H2Bub1 modulates histone modifications such as H3K4me3 and H3K79me352–54, and it also facilitates the recruitment of chromatin-associated factors, including the FACT complex35,55,56. Moreover, H2Bub1-enriched chromatin can recruit the SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complex, thereby promoting transcription at specific loci75. These pathways may contribute to H2Bub1-dependent regulation of Cdk9 occupancy on chromatin. However, S. pombe lacks H3K79me3, and our results indicate that H3K4me3 does not influence Cdk9 chromatin association (Supplementary Fig. 4). Additionally, FACT is dispensable for regulating Cdk9 occupancy (Supplementary Fig. 5). Given that H2Bub1 also regulates SWI/SNF chromatin binding, it remains to be determined whether SWI/SNF serves as a downstream effector linking H2Bub1 to Cdk9 chromatin recruitment. This possibility warrants further investigation.

Although the exact molecular mechanisms remain to be elucidated, our findings underscore a pivotal role for H2Bub1 in coordinating the transition from transcription elongation to termination. Future research should focus on dissecting the crosstalk among H2Bub1, SWI/SNF, Cdk9, and termination factors to fully understand how H2Bub1 orchestrates this critical regulatory checkpoint.

Methods

Yeast strains and growth conditions

Yeast strains used in the study are listed in Supplementary Table 1. Strains were grown in YES (5% yeast extract, 30% dextrose, 250 mg/l each of adenine, leucine, lysine, histidine and uracil) media at 30 °C76,77, unless otherwise indicated. New strains were generated by mating or random spore analysis78,79. Strains with epitope tags and deletions were generated by homologous recombination of DNA fragments either synthesized by PCR or fragmented from a donor plasmid80,81. Correct marker integration was confirmed by PCR.

Chemical-genetics

Chemical-genetic approaches in both budding and fission yeast have been instrumental in elucidating the roles of cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK)-mediated phosphorylation in transcriptional regulation82–86. This strategy involves engineering kinases to accept bulky, synthetic ATP analogs by mutating a conserved gatekeeper residue within the ATP-binding pocket—typically replaced with glycine or alanine at the endogenous locus87. The resulting enzyme, termed an analog-sensitive kinase (Kinaseas), selectively accommodates these modified ATP analogs, enabling rapid and reversible modulation of kinase activity in vivo88. In fission yeast, prior studies modified Cdk9 by replacing the gatekeeper residue Thr120 with glycine, thereby expanding the ATP-binding pocket to create an analog-sensitive version, Cdk9as 84. This engineered kinase is selectively inhibited by bulky purine analogs, such as 3-MB-PP1, which have little to no effect on the wild-type enzyme84. The use of 3-MB-PP1 to inhibit Cdk9as enables precise, time-resolved interrogation of CDK function, especially when coupled with multi-omics approaches84. In our study, unless otherwise noted, we inhibited Cdk9as using 10 μM 3-MB-PP1 (Cayman Chemicals; Item No. 17860) for 5 min in both the wild-type (htb1⁺) and H2B mutant (htb1-K119R) backgrounds.

No. 11079101) at 4 °C for 1 min (with 1 min intervals on ice). Lysates were centrifuged at 16,100×g for 20 min at 4 °C. RNA Isolation and DNA gel electrophoresis

S. pombe cultures (25 ml) of strains JS78 (WT), LV259 (htb1-K119R), and TB4 (ubp8Δ) were grown to an OD₆₀₀ of ~0.5. Cells were harvested by centrifugation, flash-frozen on dry ice, and stored at –80 °C. Pellets were resuspended in 1 ml QIAzol and lysed using a Mini-Beadbeater-16 with glass beads at 4 °C for 1 min per cycle (with 1-min intervals on ice) for a total of 5 cycles. Total RNA was extracted using the miRNeasy Micro Kit (Qiagen #217084), following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quantity and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer. One microgram (1 μg) of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the SuperScript III First-Strand Synthesis Kit (Invitrogen #18080-051) with random hexamer primers. The resulting cDNA was diluted 1:300 and used as the template for PCR. PCR amplification was performed on gene body regions and read-through transcripts for eno101+, exg1+, fba1+, and sam1+ using DreamTaq PCR MasterMix (ThermoScientific #K1072) and 500 nM gene-specific primers listed in Supplementary Table 2, with a total of 35 cycles. cDNA from each sample was also amplified for the 18S rRNA region as a control. PCR products (2.5 μl) were run on 1% agarose gels and visualized under UV light using a transilluminator.

Quantitative RT-PCR (RT-qPCR)

Read-through transcription was evaluated using strand-specific RT-qPCR, with the previously synthesized cDNA serving as the template. Transcript levels of psu1⁺, pgk1⁺, pyk1⁺, and pho1⁺ were quantified using gene-specific primers listed in Supplementary Table 2. Quantification was carried out using Radiant Green 2X qPCR Mix Lo-ROX (Cat# QS1001), and the cDNA samples were diluted to 1:300 times. The reaction mixture was initially heated to 50 °C for 2 min followed by heating at 95 °C for 10 min. The PCR stage followed this 2-step hold stage, where the cDNA samples were amplified for a total of 40 cycles. In this cyclic phase, the cDNA samples were denatured at 95 °C for 15 s, and amplification and extension were carried out at 60 °C for 30 s. This was followed by the 3-stage melt curve stage, where the samples were first heated at 95 °C for 15 s, followed by heating at 60 °C for 1 min and again, and finally heating the samples at 95 °C for 15 s. Expression levels were normalized to the corresponding 18S rRNA transcript for each strain.

RNA-seq library preparation

For RNA-seq, 1 μg of total RNA from each sample was used to prepare strand-specific libraries using the NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB #E7760S), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After poly(A) selection and fragmentation, first and second-strand cDNA synthesis was performed. The resulting double-stranded cDNA was purified using SPRIselect beads (Beckman Coulter #B23317). Adapters were diluted 5-fold and used for ligation, followed by size selection (~200 nt) using SPRIselect beads. Libraries were enriched by PCR amplification (10 cycles) using NEBNext Ultra II Q5 Master Mix, Universal PCR Primer, and Index (i7) Primers (NEB #E7335S). Final libraries were purified with SPRIselect beads, and their concentration and quality were assessed using Qubit Fluorometric Quantitation and the Bioanalyzer High Sensitivity DNA Kit, respectively.

RNA-seq analysis

RNA-seq was performed as previously described in ref. 49. Paired-end sequencing (150 nt, PE150) was conducted by Admera Health. Raw FASTQ files underwent quality control and adapter trimming using the Galaxy platform. Read quality was assessed with FastQC (Galaxy v0.74), and adapters were trimmed using Trimmomatic (Galaxy v0.39)93. Cleaned reads were aligned to the S. pombe reference genome (ASM294v2) using HISAT2 (Galaxy v2.2.1 + galaxy1)94. Aligned BAM files were then processed with bamCoverage (Galaxy v3.5.4 + galaxy0) to generate normalized coverage tracks in bigWig format92.

Statistics and reproducibility

All the bar graphs were generated using Microsoft Excel (Version 16.102) and GraphPad Prism (Version 10.6.1). The p-values were calculated using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test, with statistical significance defined as p ≤ 0.05. The number of independent biological replicates is indicated as n = 3 for ChIP–qPCR and n = 2 for ChIP–seq and RNA-seq. Error bars represent the standard deviation (s.d.) among biological replicates.
