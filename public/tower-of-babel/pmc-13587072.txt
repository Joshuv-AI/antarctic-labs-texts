The implementation of diverse skin-toned female manikins in hospital provider basic life support training
Authors: Nicole L. Tegg, Ashley Hyde, Jenna Semmens, Sarra Dobson, Carol Price, Charlotte Oostveen, Colleen M. Norris
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Diverse skin-toned female CPR manikins were integrated into hospital CPR training.•Less experienced staff reported lower pre-training CPR confidence.•Nearly ¾ of participants reported greater confidence after training with female manikins.•Diverse manikins may promote more equitable CPR education and practice.

Introduction

Cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) is a critical, time-sensitive intervention that significantly improves survival and neurological outcomes after cardiac arrest in both clinical and community settings.1, 2 Evidence consistently shows that CPR quality is a major determinant of survival.3, 4 Outcomes are further influenced by factors such as rapid recognition of cardiac arrest, bystander willingness and training, use of real-time feedback devices, provider fatigue, team coordination, and system-level elements including response time and access to automated external defibrillators (AEDs).5 Collectively, these findings underscore the importance of widespread CPR training, regular skills reinforcement, and implementation of strategies that support sustained high-quality CPR across healthcare and community environments. The Heart and Stroke Foundation of Canada estimates there are 60,000 out-of-hospital cardiac arrests (OHCA) annually, with nine in 10 resulting in death, and approximately half of OHCA occur in those under 65 years of age.6 A pan-Canadian cohort study of patients admitted to hospital following OHCA reported the incidence of OHCA in women to be 34%.7

Women are less likely than men to receive bystander CPR8, 9, 10 and the use of an AED.11, 12, 13 Identified contributing factors include unconscious bias, the fear of causing harm, the potential for accusations of sexual assault, discomfort in placing hands on a woman’s chest, and the misperception that women are unlikely to experience a cardiac arrest.14, 15 This disparity may be greater for women of color, as research consistently shows OHCA rates to be lower in people of color.16, 17, 18 The majority of all video tutorials of CPR/AED applications feature lean white men, with few representations of women.19, 20 The Heart and Stroke Foundation of Canada (HSFC) has animated videos on using an AED21 and performing CPR.22 Both videos depict a male as the CPR recipient. CPR is taught using simulation manikins that commonly depict a lean, white male chest. It has been found that 95% of CPR manikins on the global market are flat-chested.23 CPR simulation courses often focus on the technical aspects of CPR rather than the psychosocial aspects, such as cultural factors and social norms.9 Possible strategies to promote equitable CPR administration and improve cardiac arrest survival rates include creating female-specific CPR videos and incorporating diverse skin-toned female manikins.19, 23

The overall aim of this quality improvement (QI) initiative was to evaluate healthcare providers’ perceptions and experiences related to the use of diverse-skin-toned female manikins in CPR training and their potential role in promoting equitable resuscitation care. Specifically, the objectives were to assess staff perceptions of potential sex- and gender-related disparities in CPR delivery; evaluate staff experiences with diverse-skin-toned female manikins during CPR training; examine the impact of incorporating these manikins on provider confidence; and determine whether perceptions and experiences differed according to providers’ years of clinical practice.

Methods

This was a QI initiative conducted from July to October 2025. The research was assessed as a QI initiative by the REB and did not require REB approval; the research adhered to the relevant ethical guidelines for QI projects.24

Setting and context

Annual Basic Life Support (BLS) Healthcare Provider Level certification is required for Covenant Health staff in patient care roles. Covenant Health is a Catholic faith-based healthcare organization operating in an integrated partnership within Alberta Health Services public system. Classes are offered four times weekly with approximately 10–12 participants per class. Six PRESTAN professional adult female manikins of diverse skin tones were purchased for use in the BLS classes. A pre- and post-QI survey was created by the Covenant Health Professional Practice & Clinical Learning team in consultation with the HSFC’s Mission, Research and Social Enterprise team and researchers from the University of Alberta. Training sessions using the female manikins were piloted at two Edmonton Zone hospitals.

Intervention and procedures

At the start of each BLS class, staff received a brief introduction to the initiative and were encouraged to rotate among the new female and traditional male manikins during skills practice. Participants were invited to complete anonymous, voluntary surveys immediately before and after the training session. The survey was hosted on REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture), with reCAPTCHA enabled.25, 26

Survey measures

Pre- and post-training surveys included closed- and open-ended questions. Closed-ended items used 5-point Likert scales to assess confidence in performing CPR, perceived realism of the manikins, and likelihood of recommending inclusive CPR training. Additional items asked about prior training with female or diverse skin-toned manikins, previous hesitation to provide CPR, perceived sex/gender differences in CPR technique, observed technique differences when using manikins with breasts, and confidence in placing an AED on someone with breasts. Open-ended questions explored concerns about performing CPR on individuals with breasts, perceived differences in CPR and AED placement, perceived value of using a manikin with breasts, and reactions to seeing diversity in BLS training.

Statistical analysis

Staff were grouped by reported years of practice (1–5, 5–10, 10–20, and 20+). Categorical variables were summarized using percentages, and associations between categorical variables and study groups were assessed using chi-squared tests or Fisher’s exact test. Between-group comparisons were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis test, and post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Dunn’s 1964 procedure with a Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Adjusted p-values are presented. Values are mean ranks unless otherwise stated. A sensitivity analysis was completed by excluding non-respondents to the post-survey (n = 70); the resulting estimates were consistent with the primary analysis, suggesting that the missing data did not substantially affect our overall findings. All analyses were completed with SPSS Statistics version 29 (IBM, Armonk, NY).

Qualitative analysis

Open-text responses were analyzed using summary template analysis,27 originally conceptualized by Hamilton28 for use in rapid-turnaround health services research. This approach aligns with QI initiatives because it prioritizes organizing data to inform programmatic decisions rather than interpretation or theory building. Open-text responses were entered verbatim into a spreadsheet. The primary author read the text responses to identify preliminary domains, using constant comparison to group similar responses. A summary table was created with each domain and exemplar responses. Domains were reviewed with input from a qualitative expert (AH). Two authors (NLT and SD) then independently assigned each response to a domain, with the final domains reviewed by the entire research team to enhance analytic rigor.

Results

Of 596 staff invited to participate, 573 (96.1%) initiated the survey, and 501 (87.4%) completed the full pre- and post-survey, representing an overall completion rate of 84.1%. Of the 573 staff members, most had over 10 years of experience in their professional roles (58.5%). The majority were women (85.9%), followed by men (9.8%), with three individuals identifying with another gender. Open-text findings

For the question “What concerns do you have about performing CPR on those with breasts?” the assigned domains included (1) landmarking or technique, (2) exposing/touching the breasts, and (3) causing additional harm (Table 3). Most staff (76.1%) indicated they had no concerns about performing CPR on individuals with breasts. The most common response domain was difficulty with landmarking or technique (16.1%), with participants worried that prominent breast tissue might complicate hand placement, make landmark identification more challenging, and hinder the delivery of compressions of appropriate depth and quality. The next most common domain involved potential issues related to exposing contact with breast tissue (5.2%), where responses referenced apprehensions about misinterpretation, the risk of accusations of sexual assault, and discomfort with undressing individuals in public. A minority of participants (2.6%) expressed concerns about causing additional harm, such as injuring breast tissue or inducing internal or external trauma.

Among those who reported “None” (no concerns about performing CPR on those with breasts), select responses included “Haven't thought about it” (20+ years); “Why is this even a question? Half of the population has breasts” (10–20 years); “No concerns they are the same as men” (10–20 years); and “I'm more concerned about how ridiculous this DEI stuff is getting that it now extends to mannequins” (10–20 years).

Staff with 1–5 practice years were more likely to report an improvement in confidence (83.8%). Open-text findings

All participants who responded “Yes” to the question “Did you notice a difference in technique (CPR or AED pad placement) when using a manikin with breasts?” noted that consideration of the breast tissue was necessary when placing the AED pads. When participants were asked about their perceptions of representation with diverse manikins in BLS training sessions, the responses were assigned to the domains of positive, negative, and neutral (Table 5). Staff with 1–5 years of practice experience were significantly more likely to express positive sentiments (91.1%), with the proportion of positive responses decreasing as years of practice increased: 5–10 years (81.6%), 10–20 years (78.6%), and over 20 years (55.2%). Discussion

This QI initiative evaluated staff perceptions of sex/gender disparities in CPR and the impact of using diverse skin-toned female manikins on provider confidence. Pre- and post-survey responses from 573 staff members were analyzed. Post-training responses indicated that incorporating diverse female manikins increased perceived confidence in providing CPR and placing an AED on people with breasts. Staff with more experience (20+ years) were more likely to report negative feelings towards the diverse female manikins and were less likely to perceive disparities in CPR administration. There was a significant association between years of practice and perceptions of equity, diversity, and inclusion (EDI), with the proportion of positive responses to the initiative decreasing as years of experience increased. Open-text responses further highlighted the ongoing need for EDI efforts and awareness of sex and gender inequities in care. Our findings suggest that incorporating diverse female manikins can enhance providers’ confidence in performing CPR, although uptake and attitudes toward EDI-focused training vary by practice experience.

Perceptions of sex/gender disparities in CPR administration varied across our sample, with professionals with more years of practice less likely to attribute these disparities to unconscious bias, lack of training, and limited access to realistic equipment. A small percentage, across all experience levels, believed there were no disparities in CPR. Junior staff were more likely to perceive unconscious bias, lack of training, and limited access to realistic equipment as contributors to disparities, aligning with current research showing that women are less likely to receive bystander CPR8, 9, 10 or have an AED used during resuscitation efforts.11, 12, 13 The differences between staff with fewer and more years of clinical practice may be due in part to the recent emphasis on equity-focused education in modern curricula, with more experienced staff potentially influenced by their beliefs about perceived disparities and ingrained patterns of practice.29 The updated 2025 guidelines include new recommendations to address hesitancies towards utilizing an AED on a woman. The new recommendations include adjusting a woman’s bra instead of removing it to address rescuer discomfort with exposing a woman’s chest, and placing the second pad horizontally on the left lateral ribs with the middle of the pad at the midaxillary line.30

Our findings are consistent with research showing that using manikins with breasts during CPR training increased comfort with providing CPR to people with breasts.31 Some clinicians reported hesitancy in pre-training surveys, citing concerns about landmarking or technique, exposing or touching breasts, and causing additional harm. In contrast, other studies have found no difference in AED use32, 33 and bystander initiation32 when using female manikins. Reluctance to expose breasts, however, has been shown to be a barrier to performing CPR,15 contributing to disparities in CPR provision between men and women in the community.20 Practicing the process of exposing breasts and practicing on female manikins may improve confidence by normalizing these steps and challenging harmful misconceptions about providing CPR to women.31, 34 Fear of being accused of sexual assault or causing injuries has also been reported in other studies,15 underscoring the need for sex-based education CPR training in which instructors can address these common concerns.20

Post-training surveys showed significant improvements in overall confidence in providing CPR on female bodies, consistent with the literature demonstrating that high-fidelity simulation-based training can positively influence real-world confidence and practice.35, 36 The Theory of Planned Behavior37 is a robust framework that posits that an individual's willingness to act is shaped by attitudes, subjective norms and perceived behavioral control. Increasing confidence through resuscitation training may strengthen perceived behavioral control and help build the self-efficacy to act and provide CPR in real-world stressful conditions.38, 39, 40

Despite comprising over half of the global population, women remain underrepresented in CPR training materials,19, 20, 41 and the standard white, flat-chested manikin does not reflect the diversity of people who may experience cardiac arrest. Integrating more diverse manikins into routine CPR training for both healthcare professionals and community members is critical to improving comfort and confidence when performing CPR on people with diverse body types. The integration of diverse female manikins is a modifiable, systems-level intervention that could help address current inequities in the administration of bystander CPR.31, 34 While 25% of the participants reported prior experience with female or diverse manikins, we are unable to follow up on where this training was received due to the anonymous nature of this QI initiative. Covenant Health utilizes diverse-skin-toned high-fidelity manikins for specialty training. Participants may also have had exposure to diverse manikins during their schooling or specialized training, such as advanced cardiovascular life support.

Persistent reliance on flat-chested, pale manikins creates a simulation bias that can limit trainees' learning, confidence, and comfort. The default use of these manikins in CPR training is a modifiable contributor to inequities in CPR. Integrating female manikins of diverse skin tones into BLS training, accompanied by gender-sensitive education, should become standard across institutions. Policy-level action is needed to embed the use of female manikins as a system-level expectation. Educators, policymakers, and healthcare professionals should advocate for integrating female manikins into practice to build confidence in providing CPR to women, thereby working towards more equitable CPR practices.

The widespread implementation of a QI EDI initiative, such as diverse-skin-toned female manikins into routine CPR training, will require careful consideration of contextual factors, including individual-level factors such as perceptions of EDI and QI initiatives. The Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research42 can be used to help identify and explain barriers to implementation success, including negative perceptions or resistance towards EDI initiatives. In our study, some participants expressed strong negative opinions towards EDI in CPR training: “I'm more concerned about how ridiculous this DEI stuff is getting that it now extends to mannequins”; “It makes me feel annoyed that someone felt it was necessary to extend DEI to mannequins (different colored and gendered) and it seems like a waste of time and money.”

This initiative highlights the potential benefits of incorporating diverse female manikins into in-hospital CPR training. Our QI initiative has several important strengths, including a large sample size and real-world multisite implementation. There is a paucity of literature exploring provider perceptions of simulation diversity, positioning this QI project as a bridge between education, equity, and clinical behaviors.

Limitations

Because survey participation was voluntary, reporting bias is possible; responses may overrepresent clinicians with a strong interest in EDI initiatives. This initiative was conducted with hospital-based healthcare professionals, which may limit generalizability beyond clinical settings. Our sample was predominantly female, which may increase comfort with training on female manikins and could limit the transferability of our results to the general public and male-dominated emergency medical services. Due to the absence of a control group, causal inference of our findings is limited. For the sex-based analysis, the sample was imbalanced across categories, which may limit statistical power. The sex-based analyses are unadjusted and may be influenced by confounding factors such as years of practice and prior CPR experience. Lastly, formal psychometric validation was not undertaken because the survey was designed as a context-specific quality improvement tool to describe staff perceptions, rather than as a standardized instrument intended to measure a latent construct or produce a validated scale score.

Conclusion

By analyzing staff perceptions, awareness, and confidence levels, this QI initiative highlights the potential benefits of incorporating diverse female manikins into in-hospital CPR training. Women represent nearly half of the global population and have longer life expectancies than men43 yet remain underrepresented in BLS training, which may perpetuate existing sex and gender biases in CPR administration.
