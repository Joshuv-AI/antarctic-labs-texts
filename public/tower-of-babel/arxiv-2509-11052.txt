Abstract
. Community-based fact-checking is promising to reduce the spread of misleading posts at scale. However, its effectiveness can be undermined by the delays in fact-check delivery. Notably, user-initiated organic comments often contain debunking information and have the potential to help mitigate this limitation. Here, we investigate the feasibility of synthesizing comments to generate timely high-quality fact-checks. To this end, we analyze over 2.2 million replies on X and introduce Commenotes, a two-phase framework that filters and synthesizes comments to facilitate fact-check delivery. Our framework reveals that fact-checking comments appear early and sufficiently: 99.3% of misleading posts receive debunking comments within the initial two hours since post publication, with synthesized commenotes successfully earning user trust for 85.8% of those posts. Additionally, a user study (N=144) found that the synthesized commenotes were often preferred, with the best-performing model achieving a 70.1% win rate over human notes and being rated as significantly more helpful.

1.
Introduction

The spread of online misinformation remains a critical challenge, with far-reaching and oftentimes negative effects on societies
(
Xu et al. 2023
;
Yu et al. 2024
)
. Social media platforms are under pressure to innovate in their content moderation strategies for effectively fact-check misleading information at scale. Among emerging strategies, community-based fact-checking has gained prominence
(
Pröllochs 2022
;
Drolsbach et al. 2024
;
Chuai et al. 2024a
)
. For instance,
\mathbb{X}
has rolled out Community Notes system to enable users collaboratively add context annotations to potentially misleading posts
(
Pröllochs 2022
)
. Community-based fact-checking is valued for its effectiveness, transparency, and ability to incorporate diverse perspectives, thereby fostering a degree of trust
(
Drolsbach et al. 2024
;
Gao et al. 2024
)
.

Despite these strengths, a key limitation of community-based fact-checking, such as current implementation of Community Notes on
\mathbb{X}
, is the speed to generate fact-checks at the early stage of misinformation diffusion
(
Chuai et al. 2024a
)
. In-depth investigation of claims can be time-consuming, often taking many hours or even days
(
Guo et al. 2022
)
. This challenge is compounded by the limited number of available fact-checkers, making it infeasible to scrutinize every piece of content. Consequently, fact-checkers are often forced to prioritize overtly false or intentionally misleading claims over material that is more intricate or nuanced
(
Robertson et al. 2020
)
. These leads to a significant time lag between post creation and note creation, with many community notes coming after the half-life of posts’ spreading
(
Chuai et al. 2024a
;
Pfeffer et al. 2023
)
. This delay allows misinformation to spread widely before corrective notes appear, severely limiting the effectiveness of the intervention
(
Chuai et al. 2024b
)
.

Recent advances in Large Language Models (LLMs) show promise for synthesizing user-initiated debunking materials into timely fact-checking notes
(
De et al. 2025
)
. We posit that the vast volume of user comments, often generated moments after a post appears, contains valuable, early-emerging debunking information. To systematically assess the feasibility of harnessing this information with LLMs, we first analyze the informational landscape of user comments, then introduce our framework, and finally evaluate its effectiveness. This leads to our first research question (RQ1):

RQ1.

Do comments on misleading posts contain debunking information, and are these debunking comments created earlier than the display of community notes?

If comments to misleading posts do contain factual corrections or fact-checking content, we proceed to our second research question (RQ2):

RQ2.

Can large language models (LLMs) effectively summarize the fact-checking information in comments and generate community-note–style fact-checks that are helpful to users?

Based on a large-scale dataset of N=2,225,260 comments across 1,841 source posts from
\mathbb{X}
’s Community Notes platform
(
Chuai et al. 2025
)
, we analyzed whether the comments contain debunking information, and their validity in generating high-quality fact-checks. For RQ1, we found that 73.1% of posts received fact-checking comments within the first hour, and a median of 36.5 such comments appeared before the corresponding community note was displayed. We further found a strong positive correlation between post popularity and the number of fact-checking comments, indicating the potential of comment-based fact-checking for viral posts.

Motivated by the high volume and rapid emergence of these user-generated fact-checks, we developed Commenotes, a two-stage framework designed to harness these comments. The framework first employs a filtering stage, using fine-tuned LMs to automatically filter relevant fact-checking comments of a post. Subsequently, a synthesis stage prompts LLMs to consolidate the information from the filtered comments into a concise, synthesized
commenote

. The framework was carefully designed to ensure accurate information extraction, minimize bias, and improve efficiency in note generation.

Towards RQ2, we conducted extensive technical evaluations and a user study. Because the choice of LLM in the synthesis stage may substantially influence output quality and biases, we treated model choice as a key factor, comparing four distinct models: gpt-4o, claude-3.7-sonnet, grok-4, and qwen2.5-72b-instruct. We found that using the comments within just two hours, 85.8% of the commenotes are already useful, and 73.9% of the commenotes are useful using the first 60 comments of a post for synthesizing. A subsequent robustness evaluation revealed that, on average, over 90% of all commenotes were rated as either “helpful” or “somewhat helpful” across all experimental conditions. Notably, commenotes showed high helpfulness even for posts without existing community notes: 92.0% of notes created for posts lacking a displayed note and 91.2% for posts with no notes at all were rated as at least “somewhat helpful.” A detailed analysis of note characteristics further confirmed a strong overall performance, with the average score combining all models exceeding 3.70 out of 5 on most quality dimensions. These results demonstrated the feasibility of Commenotes to timely and reliably synthesize comments into commenotes.

In the subsequent user study (N=144), we compared commenotes against human-written community notes
(
De et al. 2025
)
, and found that most models produced notes of comparable or superior quality. Notably, grok-4 achieved a 70.1% win rate over human notes and was significantly preferred in terms of clarity, coverage, and impariality. Furthermore, the effectiveness of the notes such as helpfulness showed no systematic correlation with post topics or user demographics like political stance, indicating the robustness of our approach across varied contexts. In summary, this paper makes three contributions:

•

The first empirical investigation into the feasibility of using user-generated comments for community-based fact-checking, showing that debunking information emerges early in the lifecycle of misleading posts.

•

The design and instantiation of Commenotes, a two-stage framework that filters fact-checking comments and synthesizes them into commenotes using LLMs.

•

Comprehensive technical evaluations and a large-scale user study (N=144), demonstrating the superior helpfulness of synthesized notes, with grok-4 achieving win rates as high as 70.1%.

to structure and interpret user perspectives. Second, we draw from research that used comments for misinformation detection, either in conjunction with original posts
(
Albahar 2021
;
Yanagi et al. 2020
)
or as a standalone signal
(
Zhang et al. 2018
;
Jiang and Wilson 2018
)
. Finally, we address the pressing need for scaling up fact-checking, where crowdsourced
(

)
and automated approaches
(

2.1.
Analyzing Users’ Comments on Social Media Platforms

Research on analyzing comments focuses on two areas:
classification and filtering
, and
semantic analysis
. Classification and filtering methods enable the creation of structured datasets from raw comments, which can be used to extract specific insights. For example, Madden et al.
(
Madden et al. 2013
)
developed a classification scheme for YouTube comments, while Kristo et al.
(
Kristo 2021
)
refined this by first filtering out spam. Structured comment data not only improves the understanding of user perspectives
(
Madden et al. 2013
)
but also helps isolate topic-relevant segments for online governance
(
Georgakopoulos et al. 2018
)
. Our research builds on this by focusing on extracting misinformation cues from factually grounded comments, analyzing semantic patterns for signals of authenticity.

In addition to classification, semantic analysis of comments reveals deeper meaning. Studies have used text mining and semantic annotation to uncover user concerns in Airbnb reviews
(
Cheng and Jin 2019
;
Collins 2015
)
and to provide more robust analysis of user feedback
(
Yadav et al. 2020
)
. Recent work underscores the value of these semantic approaches in gaining deeper insights into user perspectives
(
Bonifazi et al. 2024
)
. Our work extends these efforts by exploring the feasibility of user comments for user-centered fact-checking, moving beyond simply extracting viewpoints to assessing their utility for identifying misinformation.

2.2.
User-Centered Evidence in Misinformation Detection

Research on user comments showed that they can be valuable for misinformation detection by revealing collective perspective and emotional trends. These works focuses on two main approaches: combining comments with original content, and using comments alone.

Some studies integrate user comments with the associated post to improve detection. For instance,
Hamed et al. 2023
used emotional features from both comments and news content to train a model to identify misinformation.
Albahar 2021
and
Yanagi et al. 2020
combined news content with user comments, achieving higher recall than methods that used articles alone. In contrast, our study focuses exclusively on user-generated comments to detect misinformation, without relying on the analysis of original post.

There are also research that focused solely on comments for misinformation detection. For image-based misinformation,
Zhang et al. 2018
and
Shang et al. 2020
developed detectors using the topological, semantic, and linguistic features of comments to distinguish between authentic and manipulated images. While effective, these methods have limited applicability to other types of misinformation. For textual misinformation, Jiang et al.
(
Jiang and Wilson 2018
)
explored linguistic indicators in comments, noting differences in emotional tone and topic focus between comments on factual and misleading content. However, they found only a weak correlation between these features and the presence of misinformation
(
Jiang and Wilson 2018
)
. Our work goes beyond simple linguistic feature analysis, proposing a two-stage framework for synthesizing comments to aid in fact-checking.

2.3.
Scaling Up Fact-Checking

Scaling up traditional fact-checking by human experts is challenging due to issues of scalability and user trust
(
Pennycook and Rand 2019
;
Straub and Spradling 2022
)
. In response, researchers have explored crowdsourced and automated fact-checking as scalable alternatives
(
Kim and Walker 2020
;
Quelle and Bovet 2024
)
. Crowdsourced fact-checking, exemplified by Community Notes
(
Corp. 2025
)
, has been shown to align with expert judgments
(
Allen et al. 2021
)
, resist motivated reasoning
(
Epstein et al. 2020
)
, and reach broader online communities
(
Micallef et al. 2020
)
.

Automated fact-checking, leveraging AI models like LLMs, has been studied for its potential to complement or replace various fact-checking steps, including identifying misleading claims
(
Qi et al. 2024
)
, verifying information
(
Wang and Shu 2023
)
, and generating explanations
(
Yue et al. 2024
;
He et al. 2023
;
Zeng and Gao 2024
)
. Some research proposes fully automating the process by using LLMs to handle multimodal data and query external sources
(
Zhou et al. 2024
)
. While some work, such as that by De et al.
(
De et al. 2025
)
, focuses on synthesizing community notes, our work differentiates itself by using LLM to synthesize user-generated organic comments to support community-based fact-checking.

Other research has demonstrated the potential of LLMs to enhance collective decision-making and democratic processes
(
Yang et al. 2024
)
. LLMs have been shown to effectively aggregate diverse perspectives
(
Burton et al. 2024
)
and map opinions to consensus statements
(
Bakker et al. 2022
)
. Fish et al.
(
Fish et al. 2024
)
combined LLMs with social choice theory to generate multiple summary statements instead of a single consensus. Although their approaches inspired our work, they did not specifically test the LLMs’ feasibility on aggregating the vast volume of comments, which is this paper’s focus.

3.
Feasibility of Comments as Fact-Check Evidence

3.1.
Dataset

In this study, we examined whether user comments can serve as a source of fact-checking evidence, potentially contributing to the Community Notes system. We used the dataset collected by Chuai et al
(
Chuai et al. 2025
)
, which contains 2,225,260 direct replies (i.e., comments) across 1,841 source posts. The dataset combines three different sources: (i) the note dataset, (ii) the post dataset, and (iii) the reply dataset. For each post, we acquired the time of the note display and the timestamps of replies.
Before a note is displayed, users replying to the post can only see the original content. After a note is displayed, they can see both the post and the community note.
Because replies written after the note display may reference the note itself, we primarily focus on replies created before note publication to avoid bias.

3.2.
Methodology

Classifying fact-check comments.

We define fact-check comments as those that provide information to correct inaccuracies in a post. Such comments must meet two criteria: (1) they explicitly or implicitly point out an inaccuracy, and (2) they provide reasoning or relevant information to support this judgment. We prompted GPT-4o to identify fact-check comments, which received the original post and its direct reply, and was tasked with determining whether the reply contained fact-checking information relevant to the post.
The detailed prompt is provided in Appendix
C
. To validate model performance, we manually annotated a subset of comments, comparing human and model labels (see Appendix
A
for rating criteria).
GPT-4o achieved an accuracy of 0.82 on this validation set (
N=150
), indicating fair accuracy.

Analyze temporal distribution.
To analyze the temporal distribution of fact-check comments, we segmented the timeline following each post’s creation into 15-minute intervals. For each interval, we calculated the mean and median number of fact-check comments across all posts. We also computed the cumulative proportion of fact-check comments at each time point relative to the total number of fact-check comments. This approach allowed us to model the emergence and accumulation rate of corrective comments provided by the community.

Analyze correlation with features of posts.
We investigated how the volume of fact-checking comments correlated with three key post features known to influence content propagation and potential for misinformation: popularity, author status, and topic
(
Chuai et al. 2024b
;
Corp. 2025
)
. Following prior work
(
Wu et al. 2017
)
, we operationalized post popularity as:

s=log_{2}\frac{c}{h}+1

where
s
is the normalized popularity score,
c
is the number of comments, and
h
is the number of hours since the post was created. To prevent late comments from skewing the popularity, we considered only comments posted within the earliest 80% of each post’s timeline. Author status (i.e., verified or unverified) and post topics were extracted directly from the dataset, adhering to the methodology of Chuai et al.
(
Chuai et al. 2025
)
. We then analyzed the relationship between these features and both the absolute number and relative proportion of fact-checking comments.

4.
Commenotes: Design and Implementation

We first introduced the design of Commenotes, and then detailed the two phases. We finally introduced the implementation settings for this framework.

4.1.
Algorithm Flow

Our algorithm consists of a filtering stage and a synthesis stage (Figure
1
). In the filtering stage, we identify comments that contribute to fact-checking. In the synthesis stage, these selected comments are consolidated into
commenotes
. This two-stage architecture is essential for addressing the problem that fact-check comments is abundant yet sparse. For example, posts in our dataset receive an average of 277.43 comments within two hours, yet only 23.99 of these are fact-checks. The filtering stage is therefore critical for isolating high-quality input, which improves the accuracy and reliability of the final synthesis while reducing computational overhead.

4.2.
Filter Stage

This first stage filters comments to identify those containing fact-checks, thereby managing the large volume (see Figure
2
) and variable quality of user-generated comments
(
Park et al. 2016
)
. For this binary classification task, we fine-tuned small LMs like E5 and BERT. This approach was chosen over prompting LLMs as it yielded comparable accuracy with potentially lower computational cost (see Section
5.2.1
).

To perform the classification, we provided the model with the original post’s text alongside each comment to preserve context. A comment was classified as a fact-check if it satisfied two criteria (detailed in Section
3.2
): (1) it points out a factual error in the corresponding post, and (2) it substantiates its correlation with reasons or evidence for correction. This is similar to the prior classification
(
Jiang and Wilson 2018
)
but differed from studies that require external links as evidence, as not all fact-check comments provided external URLs
(
Bond and Garrett 2023
)
. Furthermore, to aid in our later analysis, we excluded comments whose factual content was irrelevant to the post’s claims. The model’s final output was a binary label for each comment, and the detailed prompt and criteria are available in Appendix
C
.

4.3.
Synthesize Stage

In the synthesis stage, our goal was to generate a concise, factual commenote from the filtered fact-checking comments. To manage the LLM’s context window, we set a maximum threshold for the number of comments. If more were available, we randomly sampled a subset. Before synthesis, comments were preprocessed to remove irrelevant user mentions (e.g., @username). We then applied a prompt, adapted from De et al.
(
De et al. 2025
)
, that framed the task as if the LLM was a Community Notes contributor. The prompt (1) introduced Community Notes to provide task context, (2) instructed the LLM to identify fact-check information in the comments and highlight inaccuracies in the post, and (3) emphasized avoiding biased or argumentative language and forbade mentioning the word “comments”. Finally, we instructed the LLM to output only the note itself to prevent extraneous reasoning or explanations.

To align with platform standards
(
Platform 2025
)
, we enforced a strict character limit and implemented a post-generation check to automatically regenerate any note that exceeded the limit. Furthermore, to promote diversity of perspective and avoid the potential biases of automated ranking
(
Ganiuly and Smaiyl 2024
)
, we did not use scoring or aggregation mechanisms to select a single best output.

4.4.
Implementation Details

For our implementation, we configured the model hyperparameters and data input parameters as follows. In the filtering phase, we empirically set the LLM’s temperature to 0.6 and top-p to 1.0, while all other hyperparameters were kept at their default settings. For the synthesis phase, we used the default hyperparameter configuration, as our empirical tests with alternative settings yielded no significant difference in output quality. We established a data input threshold of 300 comments for the synthesis stage. This number was chosen to balance two factors: it is well within the typical context window of modern LLMs (100K

It is also ecologically valid, as our analysis showed that 83.3% of posts receive fewer than 300 comments within the first two hours. Finally, our prompts underwent an iterative optimization process where we randomly sampled and manually evaluated approximately 50 posts per iteration, using “usefulness” as the primary metric for comparison (see Section

Technical Evaluation

5.1.
Study Setup

The evaluation focused on two aspects: the feasibility of filtering and synthesizing comments, and the robustness of LLMs to different types of posts, especially those uncovered by community notes.

Feasibility evaluation.
To assess the viability of our approach, we first evaluated automated identification of fact-checking comments, and then evaluated the synthesis of these comments into commenotes.

In the filter stage, we benchmarked the classification performance of four commonly investigated models

Wang et al. 2022

In the synthesis stage, we evaluated the capability of LLMs to generate useful commenotes from the filtered comments across two conditions:

For the temporal availability, we generated commenotes across four distinct time windows: within one hour, two hours, and five hours of the original post’s creation, and in the period before the official community note appeared. For the information volume, we generated notes from the first 15, 30, 60 and 500 comments. The 500-comment group was included specifically to test the impact of a much larger input on synthesis quality.

Robustness evaluation.
We evaluated the robustness of our approach on recent posts not covered by existing community notes. This experiment also tested Commenotes’s ability to synthesize helpful notes for novel posts, thereby mitigating the risk of data contamination where models might rely on memorized knowledge from their training sets.

We curated the dataset by sampling recent posts from
\mathbb{X}
via its official API. We targeted posts that received user requests for Community Notes, but for which no note was publicly visible at the time of sampling. This approach ensured that the content was relevant and addressed a genuine gap in the existing Community Note ecosystem. We collected posts published after Sep 1st, 2025, and categorized them based on their final Community Note status, resulting in three distinct experimental conditions. The final dataset comprised 124 posts and 24,237 corresponding comments (M=194.5 comments per post). The conditions were: (1)
with note
(N=27), where a Community Note became publicly visible at the time of sampling, (2)
without display
(N=48), where a note was written but did not meet the criteria for public display, and (3)
without note
(N=49), where no Community Notes was ever created.

For each of the 124 posts, we used Commenotes to synthesize notes with four LLMs, consistent with Section
6.3
. We then conducted a between-subjects user study with 96 participants (50 male, 46 female, with a mean age of 23.3, SD=5.6) recruited via distributing posters on different social media. Participants were randomly assigned to one of the three experimental conditions (N=32 per condition). Within each condition, participants were further assigned to evaluate the notes generated by only one of the four models. This design ensured that for any given post, the note generated by each model was evaluated by eight distinct participants. Adopting the methodology from De et al.
(
De et al. 2025
)
, we evaluated the notes based on their helpfulness and characteristics. Participants were each compensated $4.20, and the study protocol received approval from our institution’s Institutional Review Board (IRB). Since the study was conducted primarily with Chinese participants, all posts and generated notes were provided in both their original language and a translated Chinese version. We used GPT-4o for translation as past work proved even smaller LLMs could produce translation with good quality
(
Sizov et al. 2024
)
, and two experimenters manually checked the accuracy to ensure the translation did not alter the original meaning.

Feasibility of the synthesizing stage across time.
Our analysis first examined the impact of temporal availability on synthesis quality. As shown in Figure

, the proportion of useful commenotes increased significantly as the comment collection window expanded. Synthesizing comments from the first hour yielded useful notes in 73.1% of cases, which rose to 85.8% when using comments from the first two hours. Performance began to plateau thereafter. The proportion of useful notes at five hours (90.3%) was not statistically different from the two-hour condition (
p=.2584
). This saturation suggests that the most crucial fact-checking information is typically contributed within the first two hours of a post’s lifecycle, with further delays providing only marginal gains in synthesis quality.

A corresponding trend was observed for information volume, as depicted in Figure
4(b)
. To contextualize this finding, we note that on average, the first 15, 30, and 60 comments contained 2.09 (SD=2.15), 4.06 (SD=3.54), and 7.92 (SD=6.60) fact-checking comments, respectively. The LLM’s synthesis performance scaled directly with the number of these comments provided, and plateaued thereafter. This indicates that while performance improves with more input, a moderate volume of community discussion is sufficient for the model to synthesize useful notes. Based on these findings, we selected a two-hour window for generating commenotes. This timeframe strikes an effective balance between timeliness and accuracy. It allows the system to capture the vast majority of essential corrective information to produce a highly reliable note (85.8% accuracy) without the latency of waiting longer.

6.
User Study: Evaluating Commenotes with
\mathbb{X}
’s Data

We conducted a study to evaluate whether the commenotes are useful to end users, especially compared with human-written notes (i.e., the original community notes).

6.1.
Experiment Material Selection

We utilized the dataset in Section
3.1
. From this dataset, we selected 60 posts meeting four criteria: (1) having a displayed community note to serve as a comparative baseline (with 79.37% of posts fulfilling this criterion), (2) accumulating sufficient comments (
\geq
30) within 2 hours for meaningful synthesis (with 67.24% of posts fulfilling this criterion), (3) being in English to ensure consistent evaluation, and (4) representing diverse topics across Finance & Business, Politics, Entertainment, and Science & Technology categories (see Section
3.3.1
), to ensure broad coverage. Selected posts were processed through our Commenotes framework using the 2-hour comment window, based on our finding that 99.3% of the posts contain debunking comments, and 85.8% of the commenotes synthesized are rated useful. Two researchers independently verified that materials met the selection criteria and that commenotes are successfully synthesized, with failed synthesis attempts excluded to maintain sample integrity.

6.2.
Participants and Apparatus

We conducted an a priori power analysis before the experiment. The analysis targeted the detection of the within-subjects effect, with a medium effect size (f=0.25), statistical power (1-
\beta
) of .80, and a significance level (
\alpha
) of .05. The result indicated that a minimum of 35 participants per each of the four between-subjects conditions was required, totaling 140 participants. We therefore recruited 144 participants (67 males, 77 females) with a mean age of 26.2 (SD=6.6) through distributing posters on different social media platforms. Participants were required to have an age of at least 18 years old, and were not required to have used
\mathbb{X}
before. We crafted an experimental website using our institution’s server, mimicking the interface of De et al.
(
De et al. 2025
)
(with the interface shown in Figure
11
of Appendix
E
). This study got the approval of our institution’s IRB, and each participant was compensated $8.4 for their time. The study was conducted primarily with Chinese participants, therefore we provided both the original language and translated version of posts and notes, consistent with the settings in Section
5.1
.

We adopted a mixed factorial

\mathbb{X}

Each participant needed to rate the notes for 20 posts. For each post, participants first read the original post, then viewed a
pair

6.4.
Procedure

Before the study, participants were briefed on the study’s aims, content, and potential risks. They then signed informed consent before proceeding. Each participant was instructed on the meaning of the evaluation dimensions and informed that they would be shown a set of posts with corresponding debunking information. We did not tell participants that some are synthesized and some are human-written notes to avoid bias. Participants viewed each post, read the paired notes, and rated corresponding entries per post. Each participant evaluated 20 posts, randomly selected from a pool of 60, yielding 1,440 ratings per group and 12 ratings per note. The selection algorithm ensured each note received the same number of ratings. The order of commenotes and human notes was randomized to avoid order effects. We debriefed participants after the study about the study’s content.

Data Analysis

We conducted quantitative analyses of the rating data. Following common practices

For helpfulness ratings and note characteristics, we used the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, followed by linear mixed-effects regression to account for the nested structure of participants and posts. For win rate analysis, we first applied paired

For analyses on the topic and user demographic factors’ influence, we similarly employed Wilcoxon test, paired samples t-tests and linear regression models. 7.
Discussions

7.1.
Feasibility of Commenotes

Timeliness of organic debunking comments.
The current implementation of Community Notes system on
\mathbb{X}
faces significant delays in generating helpful annotations to reduce the spread of misleading posts at the early stage
(
Chuai et al. 2024b
;
Chuai et al. 2024a
)
. For instance, it takes, on average 24.29 hours (median=9.69 hours) for contributors to propose community notes based on our collected data. Additionally, community notes are updated on an hourly cycle and typically become visible only after a median delay of 22 hours
(
Chuai et al. 2024a
)
. That is, the delays originate from the system’s two inherited design mechanisms: (i) the delay from post creation to note creation, which is driven by the selection and responsiveness of contributors; and (ii) the delay from note creation to note display, i.e., the evaluation process of community notes. It has been explored to synthesize existing human-written community notes and generate “Supernotes”
(
De et al. 2025
)
, which can foster consensus among the contributors and reduce the delay from note creation to note display. Complementary to this research, our studies focus on reducing the delay from post creation to note creation and shows that fact-checking comments appear rapidly after a post’s publication, with 73.1% of posts receiving such comments within the first hour. Our findings highlight that organic user comments can provide correction information much earlier than the platform’s current Community Notes mechanisms, thereby contributing to community-based fact-checking.

Quality and accuracy of synthesized notes.
Our evaluations demonstrate the feasibility of
Commenotes{}
(Figures
6
and
8
). Specifically, grok-4 emerged as the most effective and robust model across different post topics and user demographics (Figures
9
and
10
). Despite the overall effectiveness, we observed nuanced variance in capability across models. For instance, the lower performance of qwen2.5-72b-instruct may be attributable to its smaller size, a finding that aligns with established scaling laws
(
Kaplan et al. 2020
)
. Furthermore, models exhibited different degrees of bias. Consistent with prior work indicating the bias of gpt-4o
(
Hu et al. 2025
)
, we found both gpt-4o and qwen2.5-72b-instruct were less impartial than grok-4 and claude-3.7-sonnet. Overall, our findings extend previous research
(
De et al. 2025
)
by showing that LLMs can synthesize unbiased content from diverse comments.

Besides helpfulness, Commenotes must also account for false positives and false negatives. With enough posts, we did not identify false negatives for Commenotes, where misleading posts are erroneously identified not needing to fact-check. Regarding false positives, through manual examination, our algorithm would reject to synthesize by output “could not synthesize” for those posts that might not need fact-checking. However, we did not test the probability of false positives, which could be evident if deployed in a large scale. Research undertaken to date suggests that even in the event of high overall accuracy, false positives have the potential to significantly reduce user trust in authentic news sources
(
Horne and Nevo 2025
)
. Regarding these risks, potential mitigation mechanisms could include human-assisted verification
(
Li et al. 2025
)
, uncertainty prompts
(
Zhou et al. 2023
)
, or cross-validation of evidence from multiple sources
(
Li et al. 2025
)
.

Low cost of synthesizing processes.
From an economic perspective, community notes depend on collective labor and sustained contributor effort, while commenotes scale primarily with computational resources such as inference time and API calls, which exhibit much lower marginal cost
(
Li et al. 2025
)
. For instance, by processing comments posted within the first two hours, Commenotes costs a mere average cost of $0.0065 with gpt-4.1 for each post (input 2858.5 tokens, output 94.5 tokens on average), and $0.01 with grok-4, underscoring its financial feasibility for large-scale deployment. Notably, this approach is especially well-suited for platforms that already deploy LLM, such as
\mathbb{X}
with its grok-4.

Bias and diversity of the comments and synthesized notes.
Another challenge lies in the potential amplification of bias during synthesis. Prior research showed user contributions are often unevenly distributed, risking that algorithmic summarization may privilege dominant perspectives and neglect minority or dissenting voices
(
Zhang et al. 2017
;
Dash et al. 2019
)
, leading to the overlook of important alternative viewpoints. This risk is compounded by the fact that AI models themselves can introduce and perpetuate biases from their training data
(
Ntoutsi et al. 2020
)
.

Our algorithm was designed to directly address this by (1) randomly selecting a representative sample of notes for synthesis, and (2) explicitly prompted the LLMs to produce unbiased, neutral results, guiding the model to synthesize content that accurately reflects the full spectrum of comments. Our empirical analysis provides strong support for this design. Participants consistently rated commenotes as comparable or even superior to community notes in the
Impartiality
dimension (Figure
8
), indicating our method successfully guide LLMs to produce unbiased notes. Furthermore, our analysis of note effectiveness and characteristics found no significant interaction effect between the topic or user demographics (Figures
9
and
10
), indicating that Commenotes is robust to user’s background or the specific post topic.

7.2.
Generalizability

Commenotes’s generalizability is rooted in its method of synthesizing user-generated evidence, which is user-behavior-centric rather than platform-dependent. First, this approach allows for generalizability in its deployment model. The demonstrated helpfulness of commenotes (Figure
6
), coupled with their automated generation process, facilitates a flexible hybrid model that complements rather than replaces Community Notes
(
Li et al. 2025
)
. In this model, synthesized notes can be strategically deployed to expand fact-checking coverage to posts that currently lack human-written contributions. For high-engagement or viral content, they can also augment human efforts by generating multiple notes to capture diverse perspectives, mirroring the functionality of the existing Community Notes system
(
Corp. 2025
)
. However, such scalability necessitates robust governance mechanisms to mitigate information overload and avoid amplifying particular narratives
(
Zhang et al. 2024
)
.

Besides, Commenotes could be adapted to platforms that feature analogous, structured contribution systems, including complementing Meta’s
(
CBS News 2025
)
and YouTube Shorts’
(
Youtube Official Blog 2024
)
Community Notes, and TikTok’s footnotes
(
Tiktok 2025
)
. This transferability is supported by prior research demonstrating that user comments can serve as effective features for misinformation detection
(
Bu et al. 2023
)
, and fact-checking across platforms like Facebook, Twitter, and YouTube
(
Jiang and Wilson 2018
)
. The methodology could also potentially be extended to short video platforms with similar comment structures, such as Bilibili
(
Wikipedia contributors 2025
)
or Instagram
(
Meta Transparency Center 2025
)
. However, such an adaptation would first require a thorough analysis to determine if comments on those platforms consistently contain sufficient high-quality information for fact-checking.

Another key dimension for generalization involves extending our text-focused system to handle multimodal content. Applying Commenotes to media-rich posts and comments that include images or videos may necessitate significant architectural enhancements. Specifically, this may require integrating Vision-Language Models to effectively process and synthesize evidence from these varied formats
(
Qi et al. 2024
;
Yue et al. 2024
)
.

7.3.
Broader Applications and Implications

Our findings suggest that Commenotes have several broader implications for platform governance and the design of socio-technical systems. We structure these implications around three interconnected themes: augmenting existing fact-checking workflows, generalizing the synthesis framework to other user-generated signals, and rethinking the role of community signals in real-time content moderation.

Augmenting existing fact-checking flows:
Beyond serving directly as Community Notes, the notes synthesized by Commenotes can be integrated into community-based fact-checking pipelines, such as claim detection, evidence retrieval, verdict decision and communication
(
Warren et al. 2025
)
. Synthesized notes, generated rapidly from early user comments, could serve as preliminary drafts or sources of evidence for human note writers
(
Li et al. 2025
)
, thereby accelerating their note creation process.

Generalizing the synthesis framework to other user-generated signals:
The two-stage framework of Commenotes could be adapted to harness other forms of implicit and explicit user-generated corrective signals, such as danmu
(
He et al. 2017
)
(“bullet screen”) on Bilibili platform.

Rethinking the role of community signals in real-time content moderation:
The finding that fact-checking information emerges within the first two hours provide a powerful temporal signal for intervention. Platform could integrate this signal into their governance models
(
Shim and Jhaver 2024
)
, complementing existing user-flagging mechanisms
(
Zhang et al. 2023c
)
.

8.
Limitations and Future Work

This paper has three limitations. First, our evaluations relied mainly on English-language comments and a participant pool with limited demographic diversity
(
Chuai et al. 2025
)
. While the robustness study incorporated some non-English comments, multilingual and cross-cultural contexts remain underexplored
(
Noman et al. 2024
)
. Moreover, our dataset was limited to
\mathbb{X}
, the only platform that currently provides large-scale community notes. Although
\mathbb{X}
includes a number of Chinese users and posts written in Chinese
(
Zhang et al. 2023a
)
, it cannot be considered representative of the broader Chinese social media ecosystem, where no equivalent mechanism currently exists. The applicability of our findings to domestic Chinese platforms should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Second, our synthesis pipeline capped the number of comments at 300 per post to fit within LLMs’ context windows, which may excluded threads containing additional fact-checking information. The pipeline also remained sensitive to prompt design and context length
(
Ribeiro et al. 2020
;
Brown et al. 2020
)
, indicating that replication with different parameter settings could produce variation.

Third, our user study was conducted in a controlled environment, not as a live deployment on
\mathbb{X}
. This approach, mimicking prior practice
(
De et al. 2025
)
, while ensuring experimental control, does not capture the complex dynamics of a real-world ecosystem. Another critical aspect not addressed is the system’s robustness against adversarial manipulation. Our work focused on the technical feasibility and usefulness of synthesizing comments, therefore did not test for vulnerabilities to intentional attacks, such as prompt injection
(
Liu et al. 2024
)
or coordinated comment flooding
(
Walter et al. 2021
)
, nor for unintentional harms like the amplification of early, inaccurate information. A viable path is to integrate synthesis tools into Community Note’s system, and let the notes to be rated by existing note helpfulness rating algorithm
(
Corp. 2025
)
. Future work could also explore hybrid human-AI workflows
(
Li et al. 2025
)
, or balancing strategies that prevent domination by a single viewpoint
(
Pelekis et al. 2025
)
.

9.
Conclusions

This paper introduced Commenotes, a framework designed to augment community-based fact-checking by leveraging LLMs to synthesize user-initiated comments. Our large-scale analysis of over 2.2 million replies on
\mathbb{X}
demonstrated that fact-checking information is not only abundant in user comments but also emerges rapidly, with a majority of misleading posts receiving corrective feedback well before community notes are generated. We then proposed and evaluated Commenotes, a two-stage algorithm that first filters for relevant fact-checking comments and subsequently synthesizes them into concise, high-quality notes. Technical evaluations confirmed the framework’s efficacy and robustness, showing it can produce useful notes for a vast majority of posts within two hours, and even for those posts not covered by community notes. Furthermore, a comparative user study revealed that
commenotes
were often perceived as superior, with the best-performing model’s notes being rated as significantly more helpful than their human-written counterparts, with a 70.1% win rate. These findings underscore the significant potential of Commenotes to address the critical issue of latency in community fact-checking, offering an effective pathway to providing timely and impactful context to online misinformation.

Appendix A
Annotation Guidance for ‘Fact-check’ Comments

The objective is to reliably identify comments that contain substantive, corrective information suitable for synthesizing into a fact-checking note. A comment is classified into one of two categories: fact-check comment or not a fact-check comment.

A.1.
Criteria For A ‘Fact-Check’ Comment

To be classified as a ‘Fact-check’ comment, the comment must fulfill all three of the following criteria.

1. Direct and Specific Refutation
: The comment must directly and explicitly address a specific claim made in the original post. It should not merely provide general context or an alternative viewpoint but must articulate how and why the post’s claim is inaccurate or misleading.

2. Verifiable Evidence or Source
: The comment must provide direct, verifiable evidence or a credible source to support its refutation. This goes beyond a “good-faith attempt” to provide a basis. The evidence must be readily confirmable.

\bullet

External Links
: Links to reputable news organizations, academic journals, official government reports, or established fact-checking websites (e.g., Snopes, PolitiFact).

\bullet

Specific Data Points
: Citation of specific statistics, dates, or names that can be independently verified.

\bullet

Causality
: The comment must clearly explain the causal link between the provided evidence and the refutation of the original claim.

3. Synthesized Conclusion
: The comment must provide a clear, concise summary of the corrective information. The comment itself should function as a mini-fact-check, presenting a conclusion that is easily understood by a lay audience without requiring them to visit the external link to grasp the core point.

A.2.
Criteria for ‘Not a Fact-Check’ Comment

A comment is classified as “Not a Fact-check” if it fails to meet any of the three criteria above. This category is for comments that, while potentially ‘Useful’ as defined by the other framework, do not represent a complete or methodologically sound fact-check.

\bullet

Generalized or Vague Challenges
: Comments that express disagreement without identifying the specific inaccuracies.

\bullet

Unverifiable or Anecdotal Evidence
: The comment provides a basis for its claim (e.g., “my friend said…”) but the information is not from a verifiable public source.

\bullet

Missing or Broken Links
: Links to sources are either missing, broken, or lead to a page that does not directly support the claim.

\bullet

Incomplete Information
: The comment provides a link but fails to summarize the content, leaving the user to perform the fact-checking themselves.

A.3.
Illustrative Examples

Example 1: A ‘Fact-check’ Comment

Post: “The U.S. national debt has doubled in the last five years, proof of reckless spending. #Economy”

Comment: “This is incorrect. According to data from the Treasury Department, the national debt was approximately $22 trillion five years ago and is now around $34.6 trillion. This represents an increase of about 57%, not a doubling.”

Label: Fact-check

Rationale: The comment directly refutes the “doubled” claim (Criterion 1), provides a specific, verifiable source and data points (Criterion 2), and offers a clear, synthesized conclusion (a 57% increase) that is immediately understandable (Criterion 3).

Example 2: A ‘Not a Fact-check’ Comment

Post: “There’s an unusual cloud formation over the city today. It must be a new military experiment.”

Comment: “That’s a beautiful photo! It reminds me of the sky on my trip to Arizona last year. The colors are amazing.”

Label: Not a Fact-check Comment

Rationale: The comment is entirely focused on a personal, subjective reaction to the image. It doesn’t acknowledge the claim about a “military experiment” at all. It provides no counter-evidence, alternative explanation, or any information that could be used for a fact-checking note. Its purpose is purely to express personal sentiment and share an anecdote, placing it firmly outside the scope of any fact-checking or corrective framework.

Appendix B
Annotation Guidance for ‘Useful’ Notes

For a note to be useful, it needs to provide important, meaningful context that complements the corresponding post. We detailed the criterias and examples below.

B.1.
Criteria for ‘Useful’ Notes

To be classified as Useful, a note must satisfy two primary characteristics, as defined in the methodology of the Commenotes framework:

1. Identifies Inaccuracy
: The note must explicitly or implicitly point out a factual inaccuracy, misleading claim, or lack of context in the original post.

2. Provides Substantiation
: The note must substantiate its challenge with reasons, evidence, or relevant information. This includes, but is not limited to:

\bullet

Offering Counter-Evidence
: Providing specific facts, data, or links to sources that contradict the post.

\bullet

Presenting Alternative Explanations
: Proposing a different, plausible cause for the phenomena described in the post.

\bullet

Supplying Missing Context
: Adding crucial information that reframes the claims made in the post.

Important Note for Annotators
: The validity or absolute truthfulness of the evidence provided in a note is not assessed in this phase. The guideline is to determine if the user has provided a basis for their claim, not whether that basis is itself flawlessly accurate. The presence of a good-faith attempt to offer a reason or evidence is sufficient for a “Useful” classification.

B.2.
Criteria for ‘Not Useful’ Notes

A note is classified as Not Useful if it fails to meet the criteria above. Common examples include:

1. Mere Disagreement or Emotional Reaction
: Notes that express simple agreement or disagreement (e.g., “This is fake”, “True”, “Propaganda”) without any accompanying reasoning or evidence.

2. Irrelevant Information
: Notes containing factual statements that are not relevant to the accuracy of the central claims in the post.

3. Unsubstantiated Counter-Claims
: Notes that make a different claim without providing any support.

4. Questions, Sarcasm, or General Discussion
: Notes that do not contribute fact-checking information.

B.3.
Illustrative Examples

The following examples demonstrate the application of these guidelines:

Example 1: Useful Note

Post: “The earthquake in Turkey looks like a punitive operation (HAARP) by NATO or the US against Turkey. The video shows lightning strikes, which are not normal in earthquakes, but always happen in harp operations.”

Note: “There was a thunderstorm in the area before the earthquake started. Plus, many electrical faults occur causing bright flashes. Not some crazy US attack….”

Label: Useful

Rationale: The note meets both criteria. It identifies the inaccuracy (the claim of a HAARP attack) and provides substantiation by offering specific, plausible alternative explanations (a thunderstorm and electrical faults) for the evidence presented in the post (lightning flashes).

Example 2: Not Useful Note

Post: ‘Allegedly, looting has started in Memphis. #TyreNicholsVideo [video attached]”

Note: “Fake af!!! Stop with the propaganda!!! #MemphisStrong”

Label: Not Useful

Rationale: While the note identifies a potential inaccuracy (“Fake af!!!”), it fails the second criterion. It is a mere dismissal and provides no substantiation, evidence, or reasoning to support its claim. It is an emotional reaction rather than an informative contribution.

Appendix C
Prompt to filter comments

We wrote this prompt to test the performance of LLMs on filtering fact-check comments and obtain data to analyze the features of fact-check comments and finetune models. We excluded comments containing only fact-checking information that is irrelevant to the accuracy of the post. The full prompt is as follows:

(a) You will receive a post from
\mathbb{X}
along with a comment to the post. Please check whether there is any fact-checking information in the comment that points out inaccuracies in the post. Your answer should be no if fact-checking information is irrelevant to the accuracy of the post. You should answer with “1” if there is, or “0” if there is not. Do not include any other words in your answer.

(b) POST: <post text>

(c) COMMENT: <comment text>

Appendix D
Prompt to synthesize comments

We designed this prompt to synthesize filtered comments into a concise paragraph highlighting inaccuracies in a post. To help the LLM adopt an appropriate style, we introduced the concept of Community Notes in the prompt and instructed it to write in the neutral, factual tone expected of such notes. We explicitly emphasized avoiding biased or argumentative language and prohibited any mention of the word “comments”. The prompt adopts the following structure:

(a)
\mathbb{X}
has a crowd-sourced fact-checking program called Community Notes. Here, users can write ‘notes’ on potentially misleading content. Community Notes will be shown publicly alongside the piece of content.
You will be provided with a post from
\mathbb{X}
and some comments. Your job is to find fact-check information in those comments that indicates that there is something inaccurate in the post and use that information to write a piece of Community Note yourself. It should be in unbiased language, not argumentative. It should also be within 280 characters.

(b) POST: <post text>

(c) COMMENTS: <filter comments texts separated by line break>

(d) Do not use the word “comments” in your answer. Regard the comments as your own thoughts and opinions. Answer in English.

“Answer in English.” is added because models in China, like the Qwen series, have probabilities of answering in Chinese.
