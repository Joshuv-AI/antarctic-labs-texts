Cost-effectiveness of the gluteal turnover flap for perineal wound closure after abdominoperineal resection: randomized clinical trial
Authors: R van den Berg, G D Musters, C Verhoef, H M Blommestein, P J Tanis, S Sharabiany, S Sharabiany, S Kreisel, R Hompes, J B Tuynman, O Lapid, E H J Belgers, B Singh, A A W van Geloven, R J C L M Vuylsteke, J H W de Wilt, J Melenhorst, J W A Leijtens, M Vermaas, J Rothbarth, J W A Burger, F Polat, H F J Fabry, A G J Aalbers, J H Wijsman, B Lamme, A van de Ven
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn rectal cancer patients undergoing abdominoperineal resection (APR), a gluteal turnover flap has been proposed to reduce perineal morbidity; however, its cost-effectiveness remains unclear. This multicentre randomized clinical trial evaluated the cost-effectiveness of perineal closure with a gluteal turnover flap compared with primary closure after APR for rectal cancer.MethodsThis secondary analysis of the BIOPEX-2 trial enrolled patients undergoing APR for rectal cancer at 19 hospitals (18 in the Netherlands, 1 in the UK) who were randomized in 1:1 to primary closure or gluteal turnover flap closure. Outcome assessors for wound healing and interviewers collecting cost and quality-of-life data were blinded to allocation. The outcome of interest was 12-month cost-effectiveness, including procedural costs, post-hospitalization care, wound care, rehospitalization, workforce participation, and health-related quality of life (QoL), as measured by EQ-5D™ and EQ-VAS scores collected via blinded telephone interview at ≥ 24 months. Missing data were handled by multiple imputation. Mean costs were calculated, and bootstrapping was used to estimate cost differences. QoL was compared between groups according to the presence of perineal complications.ResultsThe BIOPEX-2 trial randomized 175 patients; of these, 165 patients were analysed for the cost-effectiveness study (86 in the primary closure group, 79 in the gluteal turnover flap closure group). The mean total cost was €53 397 (95% confidence interval (c.i.) €46 645 to €60 149) for primary closure and €45 079 (95% c.i. €42 201 to €47 957) for gluteal turnover flap closure, yielding a mean cost reduction of €8318 (95% c.i. €928 to €15 709) favouring the gluteal turnover flap. The primary closure group had higher outpatient (total €71 316 versus €53 387), specialized wound (mean €1200 versus €720), and home care (mean €3842 versus €2504) costs. Patients with perineal complications reported significantly lower EQ-VAS and EQ-5D™ scores. Although these complications occurred more frequently in the primary closure group, no significant differences in QoL were observed between the randomization groups.ConclusionGluteal turnover flap closure is a cost-saving strategy for perineal wound closure following APR in patients with rectal cancer. These findings support broader implementation and offer potential benefits for both patients and healthcare providers. Registration number: NCT04004650 (http://www.clinicaltrials.gov).

Introduction

Colorectal cancer represents a major global health challenge due to its high incidence rate and substantial economic burden1. For rectal cancer specifically, the Netherlands Cancer Registry reported 3507 new cases in 20242. Radical surgery, including abdominoperineal resection (APR), remains an important treatment modality for rectal cancer3–5. Although the overall rate of APR has declined, it remains indispensable for a subgroup of patients with low rectal cancer.

APR is associated with a significant risk of perineal wound complications (30–35%), especially if combined with neoadjuvant (chemo)radiotherapy6,7. Wound healing problems often require prolonged care and, in a subset of patients, lead to chronic perineal wounds or fistulae that necessitate repeated hospital visits and additional surgical interventions, contributing to increased healthcare expenditure8.

To mitigate perineal morbidity, biological meshes and autologous tissue flaps have been investigated. The BIOPEX trial demonstrated that biological mesh reduced the incidence of perineal hernias compared with primary closure, although it did not significantly reduce wound-healing complications9. In contrast, autologous tissue flaps appear to reduce perineal wound complications10. The gluteal turnover flap is an attractive option, requiring limited dissection and leaving no additional donor site scar11,12. The BIOPEX-2 trial did not reveal superiority of the gluteal turnover flap on 30-day perineal wound healing, but the flap significantly reduced the need for reinterventions and hospital readmissions13. Whether these clinical benefits translate into lower healthcare costs is yet unknown.

This knowledge gap was investigated in the present study through a cost-effectiveness analysis using data from BIOPEX-2, comparing the overall costs and clinical outcomes of primary layered perineal closure versus gluteal turnover flap closure, including short- and long-term complications, resource utilization, and health-related quality of life.

Methods

The design of the BIOPEX-2 trial, as well as the primary analyses, have been published previously11,13. Briefly, 165 patients with primary or recurrent rectal cancer undergoing APR were enrolled between June 2019 and November 2022 across 19 centres (18 in the Netherlands, 1 in the UK) and randomized 1:1 to gluteal turnover flap closure or primary layered perineal closure. In-person wound assessments were performed at 1, 6, and 12 months by an independent observer blinded to allocation; resource use and health-related quality of life (EQ-5D™, EuroQol Visual Analogue Scale (EQ-VAS)) were collected by blinded telephone interview at ≥ 24 months.

The trial was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Amsterdam University Medical Centre (METC code 22917), and all patients provided written informed consent.

Medical care costs

Medical care costs for the index hospitalization and in-trial follow-up period were assessed using a combination of resource use and the occurrence of specific events, as described below. All costs were assessed from the perspective of the Dutch healthcare system and are reported in Euros (€; 2025).

Primary closure and gluteal turnover flap procedure costs

Primary layered perineal wound closure involved a layered approximation of the ischioanal and subcutaneous fat using interrupted Vicryl® (polyglactin 910; Ethicon, Somerville, NJ, USA) sutures. The gluteal turnover flap closure technique has been described previously12. Briefly, a half-moon-shaped island of perineal skin was incised and subsequently de-epithelialized. The underlying subcutaneous fat was dissected towards the gluteal fascia, after which the dermis was anchored to the contralateral levator ani remnant. This was followed by routine placement of a Redon suction drain and layered midline closure.

The materials required for the two perineal closure techniques were essentially the same. Additional costs arise from the increased operating time required for flap closure. Therefore, the duration, in minutes (min), of primary closure and gluteal turnover flap closure was obtained and incorporated into the total costs. Using outpatient price lists from various hospitals where patients were included, an estimate of the cost of a standard APR was made. Data from the Dutch Costing Manual14 were used to calculate the cost per minute in the operating theatre. Data on hospitalization days before and after the operation were obtained from medical records, and costs were calculated using reference prices from the Dutch Costing Manual14.

Post-hospitalization care costs

The discharge destination was determined based on patient-reported information collected during the interviews. Discharge destinations were categorized as outpatient care facilities, home settings with informal care, home settings with home care, home settings with both informal care and home care, and home settings without additional care. Information regarding the duration of informal and home care per patient was collected using a predefined questionnaire. Using the Dutch Costing Manual14, reference prices were obtained for informal and home care, differentiated by the intensity of care provided.

Wound care costs

Specific information about the type of wound care, the duration of wound care, and the need for negative pressure wound therapy was retrieved from the trial, combined with the follow-up, where this resource use was mapped. Prices of bandages and incontinence materials were retrieved from the Erasmus Medical Center, the Zaans Medical Center, and a Dutch supplier of medical devices and related home and institutional care (MEDIQ) and averaged. Expert opinion was used to estimate bandage materials used when changing dressings as part of wound care (Table S1).

Postprocedure intervention and rehospitalization costs

Information regarding postprocedure interventions, such as surgical drainage of a perineal wound infection, radiological drainage of an abscess, and repair of a perineal hernia, was retrieved from the BIOPEX-2 database and supplemented with follow-up data. The outpatient price lists from the participating hospitals were used to determine costs. Rehospitalization days due to reinterventions were obtained from the BIOPEX-2 database and multiplied by the reference prices of the Dutch Costing Manual14. Different prices were used for hospital and intensive care unit admission14.

Postprocedure labour participation

Workplace absenteeism was assessed using the Institute for Medical Technology Assessment productivity questionnaire14,15. This questionnaire analyses the type of work performed by patients, patients’ productivity after treatment, reasons for possible termination of work, and absenteeism from work. Productivity loss was calculated using friction cost and human capital methods. For the friction cost method, the duration of absenteeism from work (in hours) was estimated. The standard mean hourly wage was multiplied by the period required for an organization to restore production levels to normal following an employee's illness-related absence14. The friction period was set at 150 days, with a standard mean hourly wage of €39.88 per hour (Dutch Costing Manual14). For the human capital method, the duration of absenteeism from work (in hours) over the entire follow-up period was calculated and multiplied by the standard mean hourly wage of €39.88.

Health-related quality of life

The five-level EQ-5D™ health status instrument and the EQ-VAS were used to assess health-related quality of life for each study patient at baseline and 1, 3, 6, and 12 months after randomization, and after > 2 years of follow-up. Health state utility weights (range 0–1, with 1 indicating perfect health) were obtained from EQ-5D™ data using the Dutch tariff, which represents the preferences of the Dutch general population16.

Statistical analysis

Patients who did not undergo APR or withdrew consent after randomization were excluded, resulting in a modified intention-to-treat population that was used for data analyses. Categorical data are presented as frequencies and proportions, whereas continuous data are reported as the mean, with either the standard deviation (s.d.) or 95% confidence interval (c.i.), or as the median with interquartile ranges (i.q.r.). Fisher's exact test was used to compare discrete variables, whereas Student's t test and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test were used for normally distributed and skewed continuous data, respectively. Cost data are presented as mean and median values, with 95% c.i. or i.q.r., as applicable. Furthermore, minimum and maximum values were reported where possible. Further details on the statistical methods are provided in the supplementary methods. Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.5.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with the mice, boot, and stats packages.

Results

In the BIOPEX-2 trial, 165 patients were randomized: 86 patients underwent primary closure and 79 underwent gluteal turnover flap closure in the modified intention-to-treat population. At the long-term follow-up, 38 patients had died and 22 patients were excluded due to either an unwillingness to participate (4) or loss to follow-up (18). Consequently, 105 patients completed the questionnaire during a follow-up period > 2 years (53 in the primary closure group, 52 in the flap group; Fig. 1). The baseline characteristics of the study population and the interviewed subpopulation are presented in Table 1. The characteristics of the surgeries performed, stratified by randomization group are provided in Table S2. The overall mean postoperative follow-up duration was 3.2 years (95% c.i. 2.9 to 3.3), with comparable follow-up times in the flap and primary closure groups (mean 3.2 years (95% c.i. 3.0 to 3.5) versus 3.1 years (95% c.i. 2.9 to 3.3), respectively).

Primary closure and gluteal turnover flap procedure costs

The procedural cost without hospitalization was calculated to be €37 248 (Table S3). According to the Dutch Costing Manual14, operating room time was valued at €11.09/min. The mean operation duration for the flap procedure was 35 min longer (95% c.i. 11 to 60 min) than for primary closure, incurring an additional estimated cost of €388 (95% c.i. €122 to €665), representing a 1% increase in overall surgical costs. The mean combined hospitalization costs were €6741 (95% c.i. €5700 to €7781) for flap closure and €9806 (95% c.i. €7928 to €11 685) for primary closure (P = 0.005).

Post-hospitalization care costs

In the flap group, 3 of 52 patients (6%) were discharged to an outpatient care facility (durations of 2, 3, and 4 weeks), compared with 1 of 53 patients (2%) in the primary closure group (duration 12 weeks). According to the Dutch Costing Manual14, outpatient care costs were €849/day, amounting to aggregate expenditure of €53 387 in the flap group and €71 316 in the primary closure group. Furthermore, nine patients required specialized wound nursing, six (12%) in the primary closure group and three (6%) in the flap group. The mean outpatient specialized wound care costs were €720 and €1200, respectively.

Wound care costs

In all, 26 patients (47%) in the flap group and 27 patients (52%) in the primary closure group required home-based wound care. The mean(s.d.) duration of home care was 40(46) weeks in the flap group and 60(113) weeks in the primary closure group, with a mean(s.d.) of 19(38) and 33(35) h of wound care, respectively. The mean home care costs were lower in the flap group (€2504; 95% c.i. €1177 to €832) than in the primary closure group (€3843; 95% c.i. €1297 to €6390).

In patients with wound dressings or who required diapers, the mean(s.d.) duration of care was 16(16) weeks in the flap group and 33(48) weeks in the primary closure group. Eight patients (15%) in the each of the flap and primary closure groups received negative pressure wound therapy, with a mean duration of 2.1 (95% c.i. 0.46 to 3.8) and 7.1 (95% c.i. 3.7 to 10.5) weeks, respectively, and corresponding mean costs of €468 (95% c.i. €106 to €830) and €1393 (95% c.i. €729 to €2057), respectively.

Postprocedure intervention and rehospitalization costs

Two patients in the gluteal turnover flap group required reoperation for a perineal hernia (three repairs in total), compared with three patients in the primary closure group (seven repairs in total). Radiological abscess drainage was performed in two and eight patients (one drainage in all patients) in the gluteal turnover flap and primary closure groups, respectively, and surgical abscess drainage was performed in six patients (seven procedures) and 13 patients (18 procedures), respectively. Surgical treatment of ileus was performed in one patient in the flap group and in three patients in the primary closure group. The mean total readmission duration across the entire follow-up period was 1.5 days in the flap group and 12.8 days in the primary closure group. The estimated costs of postprocedural complications were €16 264.37 for perineal hernia repair, €2721 for surgical abscess drainage, €1075 for radiological abscess drainage, and €14 616 for ileus surgery.

Postprocedure workforce participation and informal care costs

Of the 105 patients who completed the questionnaire, 64 patients were retired at the time of surgery. Thirteen patients had to stop working temporarily, for a mean duration of 301 days (95% c.i. 170 to 434) in the flap group and 446 days (95% c.i. 170 to 723) in the primary closure group. None of the patients resumed their full preoperative working hours. Eight patients stopped working entirely. Using the friction cost method, the mean cost of absenteeism was €23 646 (95% c.i. €19 403 to €27 889) in the primary closure group and €21 630 (95% c.i. €15 973 to €27 287) in the flap group. Using the human capital method, the mean cost of absenteeism was €143 390 (95% c.i. €62 273 to €224 506) in the primary closure group and €86 259 (95% c.i. €48 484 to €124 035) in the flap group.

Quality of life

At baseline, both treatment groups had comparable EQ-VAS and EQ-5D™ scores (Table 2). At long-term follow-up, the scores remained stable or had improved slightly in both groups. The gluteal turnover flap group had a marginally greater relative improvement in mean EQ-VAS and EQ-5D™ scores than the primary closure group, although these differences were not statistically significant. From baseline to long-term follow-up, the primary closure group showed a mean increase of +2.0% in EQ-VAS and +5.7% in EQ-5D™ scores, whereas the gluteal turnover flap group showed an improvement of +5.9% in both measures. No significant differences were observed between the groups in either EQ-VAS (P = 0.227) or EQ-5D™ (P = 0.082) scores (Table 3). Repeated measures beta-regression analyses revealed that patients who experienced perineal wound complications within the first year after surgery had significantly lower EQ-VAS and EQ-5D™ scores over time than those without complications (Table 4).

Combined costs of the intervention

The mean total costs, incorporating procedural, post-hospitalization, wound care, and rehospitalization expenses, but without societal costs, were €53 397 (95% c.i. €46 645 to €60 149) in the primary closure group and €45 079 (95% c.i. €42 201 to €47 957) in the flap group. When including postprocedure workforce participation and informal care costs, the mean total cost, including productivity loss, using the friction cost method was €61 252 (95% c.i. €53 886 to €68 618) in the primary closure group and €52 661 (95% c.i. €48 224 to €57 098) in the flap group. The mean total cost, including productivity loss, using the human capital method was €98 282 (95% c.i. €71 657 to €124 908) in the primary closure group and €76 833 (95% c.i. €58 384 to €95 282) in the flap group. After bootstrapped sampling, Welch's t test mean cost difference was €8318 euros (95% c.i. €928 to €15 709) in the analyses without societal costs (P = 0.027), the mean cost difference was €8553 euros (95% c.i. €134 to €16 973) using the friction cost method (P = 0.046), and the mean cost difference was €21 450 euros (95% c.i. €−10 992 to €53 891) using the human capital method (P = 0.195), all in favour of the flap group (Fig. 2). In addition to the base-case analysis, a scenario analysis was performed on a single data set, including the mean of the 10 imputed values. The results of this analysis were consistent with the base-case results (Fig. S1).

Discussion

In this cost-effectiveness analysis of the BIOPEX-2 trial, gluteal turnover flap closure after APR was associated with lower total healthcare costs than primary layered closure, despite a small increase in operative time. The mean healthcare saving in the base-case analysis was €8318, and savings persisted when productivity losses were included via the friction cost method. Cost reductions were driven primarily by shorter hospital stays, fewer reinterventions and readmissions, and lower wound and home care requirements. The analysis is distinctive for integrating both direct and indirect medical costs, including surgical, hospitalization, post-hospitalization care, home care, and workforce participation costs, over a follow-up period exceeding 2 years.

These results are consistent with decision-analytic and comparative studies identifying flap-based reconstruction as cost-effective by preventing downstream complications, and they extend the evidence specifically to the gluteal turnover flap in the APR population17,18. Furthermore, unlike other flap-based reconstruction techniques, the gluteal turnover flap can be performed by a general surgeon with appropriate training, thereby reducing the need for a plastic surgeon and eliminating donor site morbidity. This makes it an attractive option for perineal closure after most APR procedures that do not require extensive reconstruction. Although implementing the gluteal turnover flap requires some initial training, preferably guided by colorectal surgeons experienced in the technique or in collaboration with a plastic surgeon, these training-related costs are negligible in the context of overall patient-level costs and diminish further as surgeons gain familiarity through increasing procedural volume. Outcome differences compared with previous reports likely reflect variation in flap type, defect size, patient mix, and the inclusion of indirect costs and longer follow-up in the present analysis.

A meta-analysis10 in this field demonstrated that flap closure, although limited to vertical rectus abdominis myocutaneous and gracilis flaps, was significantly more effective than primary closure in reducing perineal wound complications. Heterogeneity among the included studies regarding patient characteristics, underlying diseases, extent of resection, and flap techniques should be acknowledged. Nevertheless, these findings support the hypothesis that perineal morbidity can be reduced by filling ‘dead space’. If the extent of the resection allows for a gluteal turnover flap, this not only reduces long-term postoperative morbidity but also contributes to cost savings by shortening rehabilitation time and minimizing the need for home care.

The gluteal turnover flap group reduced the need for extended care, such as specialized wound care, prolonged wound management, or negative pressure wound therapy, each of which contributed substantially to the higher costs in the primary closure group. The distribution of total costs differed, supporting the hypothesis that the use of the gluteal turnover flap reduces the incidence of severe complications, thereby serving as a critical preventive measure against more severe perineal wound healing complications. Furthermore, the gluteal turnover flap group incurred lower costs for postprocedural workforce participation and informal care, thereby enhancing its cost-effectiveness.

Health-related quality of life did not differ significantly between randomization groups (EQ-VAS, P = 0.227; EQ-5D™, P = 0.082). This may be related to the dilution effect caused by most patients experiencing uncomplicated perineal wound healing, because patients with perineal complications had significantly lower EQ-VAS (mean 39.2%; 95% c.i. 19.5 to 54.1) and EQ-5D™ (mean 49.0%; 95% c.i. 30.2 to 63.7) scores than those without complications.

The present study was conducted using costs from a single national healthcare system, which may limit the generalizability of these findings to other healthcare settings with different cost structures.

Nonetheless, the inclusion of both direct and indirect costs, alongside the application of multiple modelling approaches, strengthens the robustness and validity of the results. Furthermore, resource use and costs were reported separately for the various components of the cost analyses, thereby enhancing the transparency of these findings and facilitating adaptation across different countries and healthcare systems, and improving the transferability of the results. Although the follow-up data were collected at non-standardized and variable time points with the potential for recall bias of resource use, a key strength of this study lies in its long-term follow-up, which enables a comprehensive analysis of the entire trajectory of perineal wound healing. Finally, although some patient data were missing, rigorous analytical methods were used, including multiple imputation and pooled estimation techniques, to reliably estimate the costs based on highly correlated measures.

Overall, the gluteal turnover flap technique offers a cost-saving alternative to primary perineal closure after abdominoperineal resection for rectal cancer. Despite slightly higher initial surgical costs, the gluteal turnover flap procedure is associated with lower total healthcare costs, driven by less severe wound complications, as reflected by shorter duration of hospitalization and home care, and fewer postprocedure interventions. Based on its cost-effectiveness, surgeons are encouraged to adopt the gluteal turnover flap technique for perineal wound closure after APR.
