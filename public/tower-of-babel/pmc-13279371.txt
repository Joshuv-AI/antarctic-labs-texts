Association between a Periconception Healthy Plant-Based Diet and Adverse Pregnancy Outcomes
Authors: McKenzie K Jancsura, William A Grobman, Jiqiang Wu, Hanna M Griffis, Michael D Wirth, David M Haas, Robert M Silver, Maisa N Feghali, Uma M Reddy, Lisa Levine, George Saade, Lynn M Yee, Karen L Lindsay, Kartik K Venkatesh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPlant-based diets are increasingly popular and may improve cardiometabolic health, but their association with adverse pregnancy outcomes (APOs) is unclear, and dietary patterns are influenced by food access.ObjectivesWe investigated whether adherence to plant-based dietary patterns was associated with APOs and whether the association varied by neighborhood food access.MethodsThis is a secondary analysis using data from the Nulliparous Pregnancy Outcomes Study-Monitoring Mothers-to-Be cohort. Diet scores for the newer Healthy Plant-Based Diet Index (hPDI) and more established Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) were derived from first-trimester Block Food Frequency Questionnaires and assessed in tertiles (T1 = “low,” T3 = “high”). APOs included hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP), gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), preterm birth (PTB), small for gestational age (SGA), large for gestational age (LGA), and stillbirth. Modified Poisson regression models were used, adjusting for age, income, education, and health insurance.ResultsAmong 7981 nulliparous individuals, higher hPDI scores were associated with lower risk of HDP [T3 compared with T1: adjusted risk ratio (aRR): 0.77; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.64, 0.92; T2 compared with T1 aRR: 0.82; 95% CI: 0.70, 0.97] and GDM (T3 compared with T1: aRR: 0.55; 95% CI: 0.39, 0.76; T2 compared with T1: aRR: 0.71; 95% CI: 0.54, 0.95). Higher DASH scores were associated with lower risk of HDP (T3 compared with T1: aRR 0.83; 95% CI: 0.70, 0.98) and GDM, albeit for the second tertile only (T2 compared with T1: aRR 0.69; 95% CI: 0.52, 0.90). Neither diet was associated with PTB, SGA, or LGA. The frequency of low food access decreased across tertiles for both the hPDI and DASH (P < 0.05), but the associations between diet and APOs did not vary by food access (P > 0.05).ConclusionsA healthy plant-based diet in early pregnancy was associated with a lower risk of developing HDP and GDM in nulliparous individuals, which was similar to a DASH diet.

Introduction

Diets that prioritize plant-based foods and deemphasize animal-based foods are increasingly popular among women of reproductive age in the United States [1]. The prevalence of a primarily vegetarian diet among reproductive-aged women doubled from 4.3% to 8.7% from 2008 to 2023 [1]. A healthy plant-based diet emphasizes foods derived from plants, including fruits, vegetables, grains, legumes, nuts, and seeds, while reducing or eliminating highly refined plant foods and animal products [2]. Many individuals report a preference for a plant-based diet due to the potential health benefits and shifting societal attitudes [3,4].

Healthy plant-based diets have been associated with a reduced risk of cardiometabolic risk factors outside of pregnancy, including high blood pressure, obesity, abnormal cholesterol, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease [5,6]. In pregnancy, data about plant-based diets have primarily been limited to more restrictive vegetarian or vegan diets [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11]], which have demonstrated associations with low infant birthweight and small-for-gestation-age (SGA) infants [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11]]. However, such studies are also less generalizable and relevant because dietary patterns shift to less restrictive plant-based dietary patterns [12]. Possible mechanisms by which a plant-based diet could prevent adverse pregnancy outcomes (APOs) include reduced oxidative stress and inflammation, improved vascular function, and improved insulin sensitivity [[13], [14], [15]].

The Healthy Plant-Based Diet Index (hPDI), first introduced in 2016, provides a nuanced assessment of plant-based diets by differentiating between the quality of plant foods (e.g., fruits and vegetables compared with refined grains) and accounts for animal foods without complete restriction of animal foods [16]. Data reporting on the association between a healthy plant-based diet and APOs are largely limited to retrospective studies or cohorts outside the United States [[17], [18], [19]].

More is known regarding the associations between the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet, a moderately plant-based diet, and APOs [[20], [21], [22], [23]]. The DASH diet emphasizes fruits, vegetables, and whole grains and limits saturated and trans-fat, sodium, and added sugar, but, notably, also includes animal products such as fish, poultry, and low-fat dairy, and does not distinguish between healthy and unhealthy plant foods [24]. The DASH diet has been well-studied in pregnancy and has been associated with a reduced risk of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP) [[20], [21], [22], [23]].

Compared with the DASH diet, a healthy plant-based diet is increasingly popular and potentially has a broader impact on pregnancy outcomes, yet pregnancy data remain limited [18,25]. The hPDI provides a potentially more precise tool to understand how variations in healthy plant-based dietary patterns are associated with pregnancy outcomes [2]. In addition, plant-based diets require adequate access to food [26,27]. Previous studies demonstrate that dietary patterns in pregnancy are affected by neighborhood-level social determinants of health, including neighborhood-level food access [28,29].Whether the relationship between a healthy plant diet and APOs varies in the setting of adequate food access is uncertain.

We assessed whether a healthy plant-based diet in the periconception period, assessed by both the relatively newer hPDI and established DASH, was associated with APOs [HDP, GDM, preterm birth (PTB), SGA, large-for-gestational-age (LGA) birthweight, and stillbirth] using contemporary data from a prospective multisite United States cohort. We further assessed whether the association between a plant-based diet and APOs was modified by neighborhood-level food access. Our goal was to primarily focus on the hPDI, for which data in pregnancy are limited, and to secondarily assess the more established DASH.

Methods

Study setting

We conducted a secondary analysis of the multicenter prospective Nulliparous Pregnancy Outcomes Study-Monitoring Mothers-to-Be (nuMoM2b) study [30]. The nuMoM2b study enrolled nulliparous individuals in the first trimester between 2010 and 2013 from 8 centers across the United States to examine risk factors for APOs. All data were centrally managed by the Data Coordinating and Analysis Center at Research Triangle International. Each site’s institutional review board approved the study before initiation, and all participants gave written informed consent for participation [30].

Participants

Criteria for inclusion in the original nuMoM2b cohort included a singleton pregnancy with fetal heart motion between 6 + 0 and 13 + 6 wk of gestation, no prior delivery at 20 wk of gestation or later, and intention to deliver at a participating site. Criteria for exclusion included age <13 y, history of ≥3 pregnancy losses, donor oocyte pregnancy, planned pregnancy termination, lethal fetal malformation, and fetal aneuploidy. In the current analysis, we included all participants with available dietary data and pregnancy outcome information.

Exposure

The primary exposure was a healthy plant-based diet measured by the recently developed hPDI and, secondarily, the more established DASH diet. The hPDI and DASH were assessed separately. For ease of clinical interpretation and consistency with prior studies, we analyzed hPDI and DASH diet scores in tertiles from tertile 1 (T1, low) to tertile 3 (T3, high) [17,31,32].

Individuals completed the modified Block 2005 Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ), a validated semiquantitative assessment of usual dietary intake [33]. Modifications reflected changes to assess the 3 mo around conception [34]. This questionnaire assessed 52 nutrients and 35 food groups from ∼120 food and beverage items and included serial adjustment items to estimate portion size. FFQ data reflected the 3-mo periconceptional period (i.e., the 3 mo before study enrollment). Details of the Block questionnaire in the current study sample have been previously reported [34,35].

To calculate the hPDI, food parameters from the FFQ were grouped into 18 domains per procedures described by Satija et al. [2,16], based on nutrient and culinary similarities: whole grains, fruits, vegetables, nuts, legumes, vegetable oils, tea and coffee, fruit juice, refined grains, potatoes, sugar-sweetened beverages, sweets and deserts, animal fat, dairy, eggs, fish, meat, and miscellaneous animal-based foods (e.g., mayonnaise and chicken noodle soup) (Supplemental Table 1) [2,16]. We summed the total amount consumed for each parameter and assigned a quintile score. Quintiles were reverse-scored for unhealthy plant-based food groups (i.e., fruit juices, refined grains, potatoes, sugar-sweetened beverages, sweets, and desserts) and animal food groups (i.e., animal fat, dairy, egg, fish, meat, and miscellaneous animal-based foods). Finally, we summed the quintile scores for the 18 domains. hPDI scores can range from 18 to 90, and higher scores reflect higher healthy plant-based dietary consumption.

To calculate the DASH diet score, food parameters were grouped under 8 domains per procedures described by Fung et al. [24]: whole grains, fruits, vegetables, nuts/legumes, low-fat dairy, sodium, sugar-sweetened beverages, and red/processed meat. Although Fung et al.’s [24] DASH scoring system may not fully capture some dietary components, this scoring system has been used in previous studies in pregnancy or in assessing cardiometabolic outcomes among women, and has been used by our team [36,37]. Similar to the hPDI, we summed the total amount consumed for each parameter and assigned a quintile score. Quintiles were reverse-scored for sodium, sugar-sweetened beverages, and red/processed meat. Finally, we summed the quintile scores for the 8 domains. DASH scores can range from 8 to 40, with higher scores reflecting greater adherence to the DASH diet.

Outcomes

Outcomes included APOs modeled individually as GDM, HDP, PTB, abnormal birthweight [i.e., either SGA (≤10th percentile) or LGA (≥90th percentile) with appropriate for gestational age (AGA) as the reference], and stillbirth (intrauterine fetal demise ≥20 wk). HDP included antenatal gestational hypertension or any preeclampsia. As part of the study, all participants had rigorous gestational dating based on a first-trimester sonogram. Trained research staff abstracted APOs from electronic medical records and verified for all participants according to study definitions [30,38]. Briefly, GDM was primarily defined per glucose tolerance testing criteria, and in addition, chart abstractors recorded whether a diagnosis of GDM was made.[39] HDP was defined as antepartum gestational hypertension or any preeclampsia, inclusive of superimposed preeclampsia, hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, low platelets syndrome, and eclampsia, regardless of the timing of onset [40]. LGA and SGA compared with AGA birthweight as the reference were assessed at birth [41]. LGA was defined as a birthweight in grams ≥90th percentile, and SGA as a birthweight in grams <10th percentile using an updated, infant sex-specific 2017 United States natality reference [42]. PTB was defined as any delivery between 20 and 36 + 6/7 wk of gestation based on the best obstetric estimate [43], which included spontaneous and medically indicated PTB. Stillbirth was defined as any intrauterine fetal demise ≥20 wk [44].

Covariates

Covariates available in the first trimester were selected a priori for inclusion in multivariable analysis based on a directed acyclic graph and further informed by the hypothesized relationship between early pregnancy diet and APOs, and included age (continuous), kilocalories (continuous), household income (≥200% federal poverty line), education (≥college education), tobacco use in pregnancy (yes/no), and health insurance (Medicaid insurance yes/no). Neighborhood-level food access was defined as living in a low-income and low-access census track (yes/no) per the Food Access Research Atlas of the Economic Research Service of the USDA [45]. According to the Food Access Research Atlas, low income is defined as living in a census tract in which >20% of the population has a household income <80% of the area median income. Low access is defined as living in a census track where more than one-third of households live >1 mile (urban) or >10 miles (rural) from the nearest food store. Residential addresses from the first trimester were geocoded using ArcGIS and were then linked at the census-tract level to the Food Access Research Atlas [28,45]. Neighborhood-level food access was assessed as a dichotomous (yes/no) variable. In the primary analysis, we did not adjust for cardiometabolic risk factors deemed to be on the causal pathway between exposure and outcome, such as prepregnancy BMI (in kg/m2) and chronic hypertension, which would result in collider bias [[46], [47], [48]].

Statistical analysis

We compared sociodemographic and clinical characteristics by tertiles of the hPDI and DASH diets using chi-square and analysis of variance tests for categorical and continuous variables, respectively. We used multivariable modified Poisson regression with robust error variance to determine the association between tertiles of the hPDI and DASH diets and APOs, and calculated adjusted risk ratios (aRR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). To assess trend effects across groups, the dietary index was modeled as a continuous variable in the Poisson regression model. We assessed effect modification in the adjusted model with an interaction term between each diet and neighborhood food access. In sensitivity analyses, we also repeated the primary analysis, adjusting for chronic hypertension, pregestational diabetes, and prepregnancy BMI, recognizing that adjustment for variables on the causal pathway between exposure and outcome may attenuate observed associations [49]. Imputation for missing covariate data, but not the primary exposures and outcomes, was performed using Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations or MICE (n = 30 imputations), and estimates were combined using Rubin’s rule [50]. All statistical analyses were performed using STATA (version 16.1; StataCorp, LLC) and R statistical software version 4.2.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). P < 0.05 based on a 2-tailed test was considered statistically significant.

Results

Among 10,038 enrolled pregnant individuals, those with missing pregnancy outcome data (n = 610; 6.1%) and missing dietary data (n = 1447; 14.4%) were excluded, resulting in a final analytic sample of 7981 (79.5%) individuals (Supplemental Figure 1). Excluded individuals were more likely to be younger, have Medicaid insurance, self-report non-Hispanic Black race and ethnicity, use tobacco, have lower levels of education, have lower household income, and live with obesity (P < 0.05 for all) (Supplemental Table 2).

Among the study sample, the median age was 28 y (IQR: 23–31) (Table 1). The median BMI was 24.5 (IQR: 21.9–28.9), 25.0% had Medicaid insurance, and 10.8% self-identified as non-Hispanic Black and 16.4% as Hispanic. The median hPDI score was 54 (IQR: 47–60), and the median DASH score was 28 (IQR: 23–31). In adjusted analyses, individuals in the highest hPDI tertile had ∼23% lower risk of HDP than those in the lowest tertile (T3 compared with T1: 11.2% compared with 15.5%; aRR: 0.77; 95% CI: 0.64, 0.92). Those in the middle tertile had ∼18% lower risk compared with the lowest tertile 12.3% compared with 15.5%; aRR: 0.82; 95% CI: 0.70, 0.97) (Table 2). Similarly, individuals in the highest tertile had ∼45% lower risk of GDM, and those in the middle tertile had ∼29% lower risk of GDM compared with those in the lowest tertile (T3 compared with T1: 3.5% compared with 5.0%; aRR: 0.55; 95% CI: 0.39, 0.76; T2 compared with T1: 4.0% compared with 5.0%; aRR: 0.71; 95% CI: 0.54, 0.95). Those in the middle tertile (but not the highest tertile) of the hPDI were ∼61% less likely to have a stillbirth (T2 compared with T1: 0.3% compared with 0.7%; aRR: 0.39; 95% CI: 0.15, 0.99).

In adjusted analyses, individuals in the highest DASH diet tertile were 17% less likely to develop HDP compared with the lowest tertile (T3 compared with T1: 11.6% compared with 14.7%; aRR: 0.83; 95% CI: 0.70, 0.98) (Table 3). Those in the middle tertile (but not the highest tertile) of the DASH diet were ∼31% less likely to develop GDM compared with the lowest tertile (T2 compared with T1: 3.6% compared with 4.8%; aRR: 0.69; 95% CI: 0.52, 0.90). Both the hPDI and DASH diets were not associated with PTB, SGA, or LGA.

In secondary analyses, the above significant associations between hPDI and DASH diets and APOs (namely HDP and GDM) did not vary by neighborhood food access (hPDI interaction P value = 0.6 for HDP and 0.2 for GDM, and DASH interaction P value = 0.8 for HDP and 0.4 for GDM in the above multivariable regression models).

In sensitivity analyses, after further adjustment for chronic hypertension, pregestational diabetes, and prepregnancy BMI in the above models, the associations between hPDI and DASH diets and pregnancy outcomes were attenuated toward the null, and some were no longer statistically significant (Supplemental Table 3).

Discussion

In a prospective nulliparous United States cohort, a healthy plant-based diet was associated with a lower risk of HDP and GDM, but not other APOs, specifically PTB, SGA, and LGA. This association was similar for the DASH diet, albeit less consistent. Although the scores of these plant-based diets were lower in neighborhoods with low food access, the association between plant-based diets and APOs did not differ by neighborhood food access.

Our findings are consistent with the limited available data specifically assessing healthy plant-based diets, as well as earlier studies that focused on the DASH diet and pregnancy outcomes [[21], [22], [23]]. Retrospective data from the Nurses’ Health Study II reported that higher hPDI scores before pregnancy were associated with a lower risk of HDP and GDM [18,25]. Specifically for HDP risk, mediation analyses suggested that a reduction in weight gain may explain the association between a healthy plant-based diet and HDP [18]. Similarly, other studies that assessed plant-based diets, but not the hPDI, found consistent associations between diets higher in plant intake and a lower risk of HDP and GDM [25,[51], [52], [53]]. A previous analysis in the nuMoM2b cohort that assessed dietary patterns based on machine learning found that a dietary pattern characterized by high intake of fruits and vegetables, whole grains, and plant proteins, compared with a high fat, sugar, and sodium dietary pattern, was associated with lower risk of HDP, particularly preeclampsia after 34 wk of gestation [51]. With regard to GDM, data primarily from non-US populations found that plant-focused dietary patterns are associated with a reduced risk of GDM, which we also found in the current study [25,52,53]. Of note, our group recently demonstrated in the nuMoM2b cohort that a low-glycemic diet in the periconceptional period was not associated with pregnancy outcomes associated with dysglycemia, including GDM and LGA, although this diet has been associated with improved cardiometabolic outcomes outside of pregnancy [54].

With regard to underlying mechanisms for the observed epidemiologic associations, the mechanisms by which plant-based diets specifically contribute to APO risk remain to be fully identified. Possible mechanisms include reduced oxidative stress and inflammation, improved vascular function, and improved insulin sensitivity [[13], [14], [15]]. Diets that are high in plant-based foods are frequently high in antioxidants [13]. With regard to the effect of a plant-based diet on insulin resistance, a recent small RCT (n = 15) showed that nonpregnant adults with type 2 diabetes who consumed a whole-food plant-based diet required less insulin 2 wk after diet intervention [15]. Given public health guidance and the fact that individuals can generally follow only 1 dietary pattern, the current analysis examined 2 different dietary patterns, and the more consistent association observed between the hPDI and a lower risk of APOs compared with the DASH diet may suggest that the hPDI is a superior plant-based diet in pregnancy. However, further clinical trials with a head-to-head comparison between plant-based diets in pregnancy will be needed.

In the current study, we found no association between diet and SGA, LGA, and PTB. We also noted inconsistent associations between a DASH diet and GDM and between the hPDI and stillbirth, which may reflect a lack of adequate statistical power to detect an association or a lack of a linear relationship. Prior data examining plant-based diets have noted inconsistent associations with fetal growth, and where the exposure was primarily restricted to subpopulations who reported exclusively vegetarian or vegan diets, which was associated with a higher risk of SGA and low birthweight, although the association is primarily attributed to micronutrient deficiencies [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11]]. The lack of association between healthy plant-based diets and PTB is consistent with most prior studies [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11],[55], [56], [57]]. One that found an association between higher DASH diet scores and lower risk of PTB assessed diet early in the third trimester, which is later than in most published studies [56].

Prior data suggest that both dietary quality and neighborhood food access (assessed using the same metrics in the current study) are individually associated with APOs [35,58]. In the current analysis, hPDI and DASH scores were lower in neighborhoods with low food access suggesting that food access may influence dietary quality. However, the overall association between plant-based or DASH diet and APOs did not differ by neighborhood food access. These findings suggest that neighborhood-level food access does not explain the association between plant-based diets and APOs.

The current analysis has limitations. The accuracy of dietary data relies on accurate participant recall. It is possible that individuals in early pregnancy were likely to report more favorable, socially acceptable dietary behavior, resulting in differential exposure misclassification bias. Second, the dietary data reflect the 3 mo around study enrollment in the first trimester, and we cannot assess how diet may have evolved during pregnancy or the effect of dietary evolution on APOs. In addition, there is a risk of residual confounding, as we did not account for other health behaviors, such as physical activity, which may influence diet and APO risk. Participants who were excluded for missing exposure and outcome were more likely to experience adverse social determinants of health and were hence likely at higher risk of a lower (or worse) plant-based dietary profile as well as APOs. Excluding these individuals likely biased the study findings to the null (nondifferential misclassification). Although most APOs studied were frequent, we may have lacked adequate power to detect differences in the rates of some rarer APOs by dietary index, such as stillbirth. In addition, the sample size may not have been adequate to identify interaction effects between dietary index and food access with pregnancy outcomes. The current analysis assessed the hPDI and DASH in categories per tertiles based on the distribution of these measures with study outcomes, as well as to be consistent with prior studies. However, such an approach may not fully capture nonlinear associations, potentially assumes a stepwise relationship, and may reduce statistical power. Although these data are a decade old, the underlying association between diet and APOs is unlikely to have changed.

Strengths of this study include prospectively collected longitudinal data from a diverse cohort of nulliparous individuals across the United States and, importantly, assessment of diet in the critical periconceptional period before study outcomes had developed. This analysis assessed the newer hPDI in addition to the more established DASH diet to provide additional context.

In conclusion, a healthy plant-based diet in the periconceptual period was associated with a lower risk of developing HDP and GDM in nulliparous individuals. Associations were more consistent for hPDI compared with DASH. Further data are needed to better understand how plant-based diets may influence the risk of APOs to further inform clinical recommendations and patient decisions.
