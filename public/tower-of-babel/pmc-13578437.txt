The peptidoglycan-binding domain regulates antimycobacterial activity of LysB by affecting its transportation and/or catalytic activity
Authors: Lijun Zhang, Yingying Zhang, Yu Shi, Fengxian Wang, Meng Chen, Jinliang Lv, Yanru Duan, Xiaohui Li, Lihua Hu, Zhengguo He, Lining Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mycobacteriophages-derived lysis proteins such as LysB are promising antibiotic alternatives to counter drug-resistant pathogenic mycobacteria such as Mycobacterium tuberculosis and nontuberculous mycobacteria. Previous work has correlated the antimycobacterial activity of LysB with its peptidoglycan-binding domain (PGBD) in the N-terminal domain (NTD), but underlying mechanistic is lacking. Here, we discovered that NTD is crucial for the antimycobacterial activity of TM4LysB due to the likely effect on TM4LysB’s translocation to its cognate substrate mycolyl-arabinogalactan-peptidoglycan (mAGP), but neither the effect on NTD’s PG-binding properties nor TM4LysB’s catalytic activity. We also found that the antimycobacterial activity of several different LysBs, including TM4LysB, D29LysB and Ms6LysB, is strongly influenced by a highly conserved super-secondary structure (SSS) in the catalytic domain which structurally distinguishes LysBs from members of the cutinase family and allows PG-binding ability of catalytic domain. The SSS is essential for both catalytic activity and probably efficient translocation of LysBs to the mAGP layer.

Introduction

Pathogenic mycobacteria, which possess unique cell walls rich in mycolic acid, glycolipid and glycopeptide lipid that confer antibiotic resistance, are associated with lung, skin and soft tissue diseases including tuberculosis and pose an ongoing public health threat. Standard of care treatment for mycobacteria infections comprises mainstay macrolide, aminoglycoside, β-lactam and rifamycin antibiotics and the antituberculosis drugs isoniazid, pyrazinamide and ethambutol1,2, yet resistance is a worsening problem, with 400,000 of the 10.8 million new TB patients in 2023 resistant to multidrug combinations of rifampicin. Nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) infection is also a significant burden especially given its tendency to recur3 – a common complication linked to the toxicities associated with the long-term use of antibiotics and the ensuing need to interrupt therapy3,4. Accordingly, novel non-antibiotic treatments targeting mycobacteria are urgently needed.

One feasible approach is the use of bacteriophages including mycobacteriophages, which have currently been applied in the clinical treatment of pulmonary infections and implantable medical device-associated infections, as well as urinary tract infections caused by Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Klebsiella pneumoniae and Staphylococcus aureus5. There are several reports of the successful treatment of mycobacterial infections with mycobacteriophages, notably a 15-year-old cystic fibrosis patient infected with the NTM Mycobacterium abscessus6. Intravenous administration of the mycobacterial phages Muddy, BPs33∆HTH-HRM10 and ZoeJ∆45 led to significant clinical improvement, including sternal wound closure, improved liver function, and resolution of infected skin nodules, and was well tolerated. Phage cocktails were also used in the personalized treatment of 20 patients infected with antibiotic-resistant mycobacteria including Mycobacterium abscessus, Mycobacterium chelonae, Mycobacterium avium and Bacillus Calmette-Guérin with good responses in five patients and partial responses in the other six, although four patients did not respond to treatment and responses were unclear in the remaining five7. All patients tolerated phage therapy well without severe side effects, and phage resistance was not observed, even in patients receiving treatment with a single phage instead of a combination7. These results suggest that phage administration can be safe and effective to treat otherwise intractable mycobacterial infections resistant to conventional antibiotics, and merits further investigation for this purpose.

Although mycobacteriophages can effectively reduce the proliferation of pathogenic mycobacteria, many further experiments would be needed to support their regulatory approval due to their unique biological nature and self-replicating behavior. In contrast, the regulatory bar to the approval of recombinant versions of antibacterial proteins is much lower. Such proteins include the two enzymes involved in mycobacteria lysis at the end of a lytic cycle – Lysin A (LysA), that hydrolyses peptidoglycan, and Lysin B (LysB), that cleaves the chemical bond between arabinogalactan and mycolic acids to release free mycolic acids and compromise the mycobacteria cell walls. Of these, LysB, specifically derived from the mycobacteriophage D29, D29LysB, is the most studied. For example, the structure of D29LysB has been solved and shown to resemble that of cutinase family enzymes8; and it shows antimycobacterial activity “from without” – when exogeneous D29LysB protein enters mycobacteria from the outside to inhibit their growth9. D29LysB can also inhibit the growth of drug-sensitive Mtb strains, activity that could be increased by addition of the surfactant Tween-80 and when used in combination with rifampicin10; killed Mtb in mouse macrophages10; and reduced Mycobacterium abscessus cell numbers by 20-fold over 9 days in an immunocompromised mouse model11,12. The next most well-studied LysB is that of the mycobacteriophage Ms6, Ms6LysB. Gil et al. were the first to demonstrate Ms6LysB’s lipolytic activity, and its ability to release mycolic acids from the mycolylarabinogalactan-peptidoglycan complex and trehalose-6,6′-dimycolate (TDM) from the outer membrane of the mycobacterial cell wall13; and Grover N et al. first reported that Ms6LysB inhibits the growth of Msm from without14. Ms6LysB is necessary to efficiently lyse Msm “from within” – by infecting Msm with mycobacteriophage (here is Ms6), enabling protein synthesis of mycobacteriophage (here is Ms6) within Msm to inhibit the growth of Msm; in its absence, lysis still occurs, but few new phage particles are released into the environment15.

The molecular mechanisms through which LysB functions from without and from within, however, are unclear. For example, the structural differences that distinguish LysB from members of the cutinase family, few of which can inhibit the growth of Mycobacterium, are unknown, as are the regulatory effects of the domains in multidomain LysB on antimycobacterial activity from without and from within. Similarly, LysB lacking an N-terminal containing a peptidoglycan (PG)-binding domain led to abrupt lysis of Msm compared with wild-type Ms616, but the exact reason why is unknown.

Here, we report the regulatory mechanisms that underly the effect of the PGBD domain on the cutinase-like domain by comparing the antimycobacterial activities of TM4LysB with those of D29LysB and Ms6LysB. We also identified and validated the sub-domain that structurally and functionally distinguishes LysB from cutinase, and disclose the results of investigations into the principle governing the function of this sub-domain.

Results

TM4LysB’s NTD inhibits antimycobacterial activity from without of the C-terminal domain (CTD)

LysB, from mycobacteriophage such as Ritsun and D29, can inhibit the growth of mycobacteria from without9–11,17 and has great potential as an antimycobacterial agent, but its activity and thermostability need to be improved before its development as a drug to treat infections in humans can commence. To evolve LysB for better antimycobacterial activity, we first sought to understand the mechanism of this activity by comparing the structures and activities of three different LysBs from the mycobacteriophages D29, TM4 and Ms6. Small samples (3 μL) of each of the three recombinantly expressed LysBs were dripped at various concentrations (2, 1, 0.5, 0.25, 0.125 and 0.0625 mg/mL) on solid culture media premixed with live Msm. After incubation, the antimycobacterial activity from without of each LysB was reflected in the clearing of their respective inhibition zones. Clear inhibition zones with gradually weakening clearing appeared for D29LysB dripped at protein concentrations of 2-0.0625 mg/mL (Fig. 1A), indicating that D29LysB could inhibit the growth of Msm in a concentration-dependent manner, consistent with the results of previous studies10. To understand the structural basis for this weak activity, we compared the structures of the two LysBs with that of D29LysB, which shows strong antimycobacterial activity from without. AlphaFold2 was used to predict the 3D-structures of TM4LysB and Ms6LysB. The results (Fig. 1B) revealed that the catalytic domains of TM4LysB and Ms6LysB contain complete catalytic residues (S164-D237-H366 and S168-D249-H318, respectively, corresponding to S82-D166-H240 of D29LysB8), the 3D-structure of which is similar to that in D29LysB (RMSDs 2.672 and 0.803 Å, respectively). However, both TM4LysB and Ms6LysB contained an additional N-terminal domain (TM4LysB_NTD M1-P81 and Ms6LysB_NTD M1-D90).

Next, we investigated the antimycobacterial activities from without of the C-terminal domains of TM4LysB and Ms6LysB (TM4LysB_CTD P82-A400, Ms6LysB_CTD T91-T332). TM4LysB_CTD produced clear circular clearing zones at protein concentrations ranging from 2 to 0.0625 mg/mL comparable in diameter to those produced by D29LysB, indicating that TM4LysB_CTD could inhibit the growth of Msm in a concentration-dependent manner. However, no halo was produced by Ms6LysB_CTD, even at the highest protein concentration (2 mg/mL), indicating that Ms6LysB_CTD does not inhibit Msm growth. Previous studies have demonstrated that replacing serine with alanine in the catalytic triad results in the loss of catalytic activity8. Consistent with this conclusion, the hydrolytically inactive mutants D29LysB (S82A), TM4LysB_CTD (S164A) and Ms6LysB (S168A) all lacked antimycobacterial activity from without, indicating that the antimycobacterial activity of LysB depends on its hydrolytic activity (Fig. 1A).

To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which TM4LysB_NTD interferes with the antimycobacterial activity from without of TM4LysB_CTD, we predicted the 3D-structure of TM4LysB_NTD (Fig. 1B, C) and then looked for homologous protein domains whose behaviors are better understood. AlphaFold2 predicted TM4LysB_NTD to present a spherical structure surrounded by four α-helices and random coils, for which Foldseek search server18 identified several homologous proteins, the top 12 of which all contained peptidoglycan-binding domains (Suppl. Table 1). Structural alignment revealed that the peptidoglycan-binding domains of 11 of these (all but 4G54) are highly similar to that of TM4LysB_NTD, with RMSDs ranging from 0.488 to 1.049 Å.

To further investigate the role of the PGBD of TM4LysB in its antimycobacterial activity, we assessed the extent to which Ms6LysB_NTD could interfere with this activity given that the latter was previously reported to contain a peptidoglycan-binding domain (PGBD)16 structurally similar to that in TM4LysB_NTD (RMSD 0.925 Å; Fig. 1C; Suppl. Fig. 1A). TM4LysB(ΔNTD):NTDMs6LysB was formed by replacing the TM4LysB_NTD with Ms6LysB_NTD (Suppl. Fig. 2) with the help of a small ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO), which could be excised and removed using the SUMO enzymes ULP1 and NTA (Suppl. Fig. 1B), and the antimycobacterial activity from without of the chimera investigated using TM4LysB and TM4LysB_CTD as controls. The results (Fig. 1D) revealed TM4LysB(ΔNTD):NTDMs6LysB’s antimycobacterial activity to be weak, comparable to that of TM4LysB, indicating that Ms6LysB_NTD could also interfere with the antimycobacterial activity of TM4LysB_CTD. These findings, that both TM4LysB_NTD and Ms6LysB_NTD contain PGBDs and that Ms6LysB_NTD, like TM4LysB_NTD, can interfere with the antimycobacterial activity of TM4LysB_CTD, suggested that the peptidoglycan-binding activity of TM4LysB_NTD might play a role in the inhibition of TM4LysB_CTD’s antimycobacterial activity.

TM4LysB’s NTD inhibits antimycobacterial activity from without probably by disrupting penetration of the C-terminal domain (CTD) into the arabinogalactan-mycolic acid layer

To verify the above hypothesis, the PGBD from TM4LysB was replaced with PGBDs from other sources and the antimycobacterial activity from without of these chimaeras tested. First, we determined the peptidoglycan-binding activity of TM4LysB_NTD and some alternative PGBDs, selecting as positive controls a PGBD from a D-Ala-A-Ala carboxypeptidase (PDB 1LBU)19 of Streptomyces albus G, one of the 12 top homologues of TM4LysB_NTD demonstrated to have peptidoglycan-binding activity, and a PGBD present at the N-terminus of Rv3915 (or CwlM) of Mtb (CwlM_NTD), an N-acetylmuramyl-l-alanine amidase purported to modulate autolysis during biofilm formation20. CwlM plays an important role in the peptidoglycan synthesis pathway by regulating the activities of MurA, MurJ and FhaA which are involved in the production of peptidoglycan21; previous studies have demonstrated that CwlM can directly hydrolyze Msm PG22. CwlM_NTD is predicted to contain two PGBDs, CwlM_PGBD1 and CwlM_PGBD223. However, direct evidence confirming the PG-binding activity of these two PGBDs remains lacking. Structural alignment shows that TM4LysB_NTD shares high similarity with CwlM_PGBD1 and CwlM_PGBD2 (RMSDs of 0.435 and 0.579 Å, respectively) (Fig. 1C). Given that the working mechanism of this type of PGBD has not yet been clarified and that mutants with weak peptidoglycan-binding activity but substantially unchanged spatial conformation are difficult to obtain, CwlM_PGBD1, CwlM_PGBD2, and CwlM_NTD were used to investigate the effects of varying PG-binding activity on the antimycobacterial activity of LysB.

After obtaining the proteins mentioned above, included with TM4LysB_NTD, Ms6LysB_NTD, 1LBU_NTD, CwlM_NTD, CwlM_PGBD1 and CwlM_PGBD2, we next probed their PG-binding activity. MsmPG was purified, incubated with proteins having PG-binding activity, and the location of these proteins detected via SDS‒PAGE using bovine serum albumin (BSA) as the blank control. Each of the above purified proteins was mixed with the MsmPG sample, and, after centrifugation, SDS‒PAGE was used to detect the protein components in the supernatant and the pellet – an experimental design based on the insolubility of PG in water and its ability to pull into the precipitate any proteins capable of PG-binding. The results (Fig. 2A) revealed that, in the absence of MsmPG, six proteins were present only in the supernatant, demonstrating their stability in the current buffer. However, protein could be detected in the pellets obtained when 1LBU_NTD or CwlM_NTD was mixed with MsmPG, with binding percentages of 95.7% and 19.5%, respectively, consistent with a previous report19 (Fig. 2B and Suppl. Data 1). However, almost no protein was obtained in the pellets obtained using TM4LysB_NTD, Ms6LysB_NTD, CwlM_PGBD1 or CwlM_PGBD2, confirming that they hardly bound MsmPG. Notably, BSA was present in the supernatant regardless of the presence or absence of MsmPG, proving that the binding activity, as shown in 1LBU_NTD and CwlM_NTD, was derived from MsmPG. Here, we noticed that Ms6LysB_NTD had no PG-binding activity, in contrast to the results of a previous work in which Ms6LysB_NTD was shown to have a PG-binding ability when it was fused with EGFP16. Next, we sought to reveal the role of TM4LysB_NTD’s peptidoglycan-binding activity in the inhibition of TM4LysB_CTD’s antimycobacterial activity by replacing the PGBD from TM4LysB_NTD with a PGBD from other sources. To precisely replace the PGBD and minimize the potential impact of sequences outside of the PGBD on protein expression or inclusion body formation, we used PROMALS3D24 to determine the exact boundary of the PGBD by secondary structure-based sequence alignment techniques. The results (Suppl. Fig. 1A) defined G12-G74 in TM4LysB, G16-Q77 in 1LBU, S18-S87 in CwlM (CwlM_PGBD1) and G105-S164 in CwlM (CwlM_PGBD2) as PGBDs. It is worth noting that the two PGBDs in CwlM_NTD defined here are exactly the same as the two ones in CwlM_NTD used in PG-binding assay (Fig. 2A). Accordingly, amino acids G12-G74 from TM4LysB were replaced with amino acids G16-Q77 of 1LBU, S18-S87 of CwlM, G105-S164 of CwlM or S18-S164 of CwlM to form TM4LysB(ΔPGBD):PGBD1LBU, TM4LysB(ΔPGBD):PGBDCwlM_PGBD1, TM4LysB(ΔPGBD):PGBDCwlM_PGBD2 and TM4LysB(ΔPGBD):PGBDCwlM_NTD (Suppl. Fig. 2). The antimycobacterial activity of TM4LysB(ΔPGBD):PGBDCwlM_PGBD1 and TM4LysB(ΔPGBD):PGBDCwlM_PGBD2 was greater than the weak antimycobacterial activity from without of TM4LysB (Fig. 2C); given that TM4LysB_NTD showed no PG-binding ability, similar to both CwlM_PGBD1 and CwlM_PGBD2 (Fig. 2A), these results indicated that the antimycobacterial activity of TM4LysB is independent of the PG-binding activity. To eliminate any potential difference in peptidoglycan binding activity between TM4LysB_PGBD and CwlM_PGBD1 or CwlM_PGBD2 that could undermine this conclusion, TM4LysB_PGBD was replaced with domains with obviously strong PG-binding activity (such as 1LBU_PGBD and CwlM_NTD) or no PG-binding activity (such as SUMO). The results (Fig. 2C) revealed that the antimycobacterial activity from without of both TM4LysB(ΔPGBD):PGBDCwlM_NTD and TM4LysB(ΔPGBD):PGBD1LBU was significantly greater than that of TM4LysB, whereas TM4LysB(ΔPGBD):SUMO had almost no antimycobacterial activity from without, similar to the weak antimycobacterial activity of TM4LysB. These results confirmed that there is no correlation between the PG-binding activity of TM4LysB_NTD and its interference with the antimycobacterial activity from without of TM4LysB_CTD.

Next, we used pull-down experiments to assess any interaction between TM4LysB_NTD and TM4LysB_CTD, which could explain why TM4LysB_NTD interfered with the antimycobacterial activity of TM4LysB_CTD from without. The affinity tags 6×His and Strep-tagII (WSHPQFEK) were introduced to TM4LysB_NTD and TM4LysB_CTD, respectively, with NTA and streptavidin beads, respectively, used for the pull-down tests. The results (Suppl. Fig. 1C) revealed that TM4LysB_CTD carrying Strep-tagII could be pulled down with 6×His, indicating that the two segments interacted with each other. However, TM4LysB_NTD carrying 6×His could not be enriched with Strep-tagII, indicating that they didn’t interact. These results suggested their mutual interactions in vitro to be extremely weak and readily disturbed by different affinity environments, such as different affinity tags and different buffer environments. We also tested whether TM4LysB_NTD could interfere with the antimycobacterial activity of TM4LysB_CTD in trans, using TM4LysB_CTD at gradient concentrations of 2, 1, 0.5, 0.25, 0.125 and 0.0625 mg/mL, as above. On this basis, the molar ratio of TM4LysB_NTD to TM4LysB_CTD was increased from 1:1 to 5:1, after which the antimycobacterial activity from without of these protein mixtures was investigated. The results (Suppl. Fig. 1D) revealed that the antimycobacterial activity from without of TM4LysB_CTD is not affected by TM4LysB_NTD concentration and therefore although TM4LysB_NTD can interfere with antimycobacterial activity from without of TM4LysB_CTD in cis, this effect may not be due to binding to TM4LysB_CTD.

Next, we investigated how NTD can affect the antimycobacterial activity from without of TM4LysB_CTD by measuring the hydrolysis of mycolyl-arabinogalactan-peptidoglycan (mAGP), the direct substrate of LysB, by TM4LysB and TM4LysB_CTD. The positive controls tetrabutylammonium (TBAH) and D29LysB exhibited significant catalytic activity, in sharp contrast to the negligible catalytic activity of the negative control which incubated solely with buffer and inactive mutant D29LysB(S82A), consistent with previous reports8 (Fig. 2D). However, although TM4LysB (S164A) was almost inactive, the hydrolytic activities of TM4LysB, TM4LysB_CTD and TM4LysB(ΔNTD):SUMO were comparable to D29LysB. These results indicated that the NTD did not affect the mAGP-hydrolysis activity of TM4LysB_CTD and that this activity did not correlate with the antimycobacterial activity from without. Examining the mAGP-hydrolysis activities of the Ms6LysB variants under the same conditions (Fig. 2D) showed that Ms6LysB(S168A) was inactive, the activity of Ms6LysB_CTD was comparable to that of TM4LysB_CTD, and the activity of Ms6LysB was comparable to that of D29LysB, further indicating that the NTD did not affect the mAGP-hydrolysis activity of Ms6LysB_CTD and that this activity did not correlate with the antimycobacterial activity from without. Here, a small amount of mycolic acids was detected in the reactions of the negative control and LysB inactive mutants because of trace amounts of mycolic acid in mAGP. Considering that LysB needs to cross the outer membrane to reach its target, the arabinogalactan-mycolic acid,, we believe that these results indirectly show that domains in the NTD interfere with the penetration of LysB into the arabinogalactan-mycolic acid layer, thereby affecting its antimycobacterial activity from without.

NTD promotes antimycobacterial activity from within of TM4LysB

Although TM4LysB_NTD interferes with antimycobacterial activity from without of TM4LysB_CTD, the role of TM4LysB_NTD in the inhibition of Msm growth from within by TM4LysB remains unclear. What is known, however, is that LysB is produced in the cytoplasm and needs to translocate the cell membrane to reach its target, the ester linkage joining the mycolic acid and arabinogalactan8,13. Accordingly, we established a system to translocate LysB. Based on previous studies in Escherichia coli which found that endolysin usually enters the periplasmic space through holes punched in the plasma membrane by Holin25, we co-expressed the coding gene for TM4LysB (gp30) and gp31, the coding gene for the bioinformatically predicated Holin in TM418, in Msm and then investigated the effects of the expression of these two genes on the growth of Msm. The backbone of pLJR962, a low-copy shuttle plasmid previously used as a coding guide RNA in CRISPRi26, was selected for this purpose because it contains a tet promoter and can therefore precisely regulate gene expression by anhydrotetracycline (ATc). Given that even trace amounts of LysB may significantly inhibit mycobacterial growth, the precise control capability of pLJR962 in heterologous expression of LysB is particularly important. Therefore, in this study, the gene encoding dCas9 in pLJR962 was replaced with our target genes (please refer to the “Methods” section for details). We co-expressed the coding genes for TM4LysB or TM4LysB_CTD with gp31, while separately expressing genes encoding TM4LysB and TM4LysB_CTD or gp31 as controls. The results (Suppl. Fig. 3A) revealed that expression of gp31 alone shows no growth inhibition against Msm, an unexpected result since Holin is typically a pore-punching protein that destroys the electrical potential of the plasma membrane25. Consistent with the result, adding gp31 did not increase the antimycobacterial activity of TM4LysB or TM4LysB_CTD. These results suggest that the Holin of TM4 is unlikely to be encoded by gp31, and its function remains to be fully elucidated—extending well beyond the scope of preliminary bioinformatic predictions18.

Instead, we employed the signal peptide of Mpt63 (Rv1926c) (Mpt63_SP, M1-A29), which has been successfully used for the transmembrane transport of target proteins in Msm27. To investigate the role of the NTD in the growth inhibitory activity from within of TM4LysB against Msm, we fused Mpt63_SP to the N-terminus of LysB using the pLJR962 vector. Given the high antimycobacterial activity from without of TM4LysB_CTD (Fig. 1A), we first added Mpt63_SP to the N-terminus of TM4LysB_CTD to form Msm-Mpt63_SP-TM4LysB_CTD. As a control, we constructed Msm-TM4LysB_CTD, which grew normally despite the presence of inducers; in contrast, the growth of Msm-Mpt63_SP-TM4LysB_CTD was slightly inhibited, demonstrating that TM4LysB_CTD can weakly function from within with the help of Mpt63_SP (Fig. 3A). Next, we fused Mpt63_SP to the N-terminus of TM4LysB_NTD, resulting in the formation of Msm-Mpt63_SP-TM4LysB_NTD and Msm-Mpt63_SP-TM4LysB, respectively. Besides, we constructed TM4LysB_NTD without Mpt63_SP, which formed Msm-TM4LysB_NTD. The Msm-TM4LysB_NTD and Msm-TM4LysB were used as control. Growth of Msm-Mpt63_SP-TM4LysB was almost completely inhibited in the presence of the inducer (Fig. 3A), whereas the other strains, such as the Msm-Mpt63_SP-TM4LysB_CTD, grew normally, demonstrating that TM4LysB could inhibit the growth of Msm from within much more efficiently than TM4LysB_CTD with the help of Mpt63_SP, an unexpected result given that the antimycobacterial activity of TM4LysB_CTD from without was much greater than that of TM4LysB (Fig. 1A). A super-secondary structure (SSS) structurally distinguishes TM4LysB and D29LysB from members of the cutinase family which bear no antimycobacterial activity from without

TM4LysB and Ms6LysB are classified as homologues of cutinases, a family of enzymes capable of hydrolyzing cutin32 and polymers33,34 and exhibiting phospholipase A and thioesterase activity35, based on their high functional and structural similarity (RMSDs of 2.672 and 0.803 Å, respectively) with D29LysB, also a homologue of cutinase belonging to the α/β hydrolase family8. Given the broad substrate selectivity of cutinases and the effective antimycobacterial activity from within of LysBs from TM4 (Fig. 3A), we wondered whether other cutinase homologues of LysBs could inhibit the growth of Mycobacterium. Accordingly, we used the Foldseek server to identify structural homologues of TM4LysB_CTD from the PDB and tested their antimycobacterial activity from without. This study revealed that the catalytic residues of the five proteins are conserved, with D29LysB and TM4LysB_CTD distinguished from their homologues by the presence of a super-secondary structure (SSS) between the catalytic residues Asp and His (D166-H240 and D237-H366, respectively). Although having clarified the indispensability of SSS for antimycobacterial activity from without of both TM4LysB_CTD and D29LysB, the role of SSS in growth inhibitory activity from within remained unclear. Accordingly, we obtained the pLJR962-based constructs expressing the chimeric TM4LysB(ΔSSS):SSS5W95, TM4LysB(ΔSSS):SSS2CZQ and TM4LysB(ΔSSS):SSS1G66 and then investigated their ability to inhibit Msm growth with the help of Mpt63_SP. The results (Fig. 5B) revealed that 25-100 ng/mL ATc could induce complete growth inhibition of Msm for TM4LysB, whereas TM4LysB(ΔSSS):SSS5W95, TM4LysB(ΔSSS):SSS2CZQ and TM4LysB(ΔSSS):SSS1G66, had little effect on the growth of Msm. The same results were also observed for D29LysB (Fig. 5B), which confirmed that SSS was essential for the growth inhibitory activity from within of both TM4LysB and D29LysB, as was their antimycobacterial activity from without. Considering that SSS is positioned between the catalytic Asp and His, surrounding the active center, we hypothesized that this region might participate in the catalytic reaction. Examination of the mAGP-hydrolysis activity of the D29LysB variants D29LysB(ΔSSS):SSS5W95, D29LysB(ΔSSS):SSS2CZQ and D29LysB(ΔSSS):SSS1G66 (Fig. 5C) revealed that their activity is equivalent to that of D29LysB (S82A), the inactive mutant of D29LysB, suggesting SSS replacement leads to the loss of catalytic activity. This showed that the SSS structurally and functionally distinguished TM4LysB and D29LysB from other members of the cutinase family and that this region was essential for both antimycobacterial activity from without and from within of TM4LysB and D29LysB.

To confirm the conservation of SSS across the entire LysB family, a sequential profile of the six LysBs (D29LysB, Ms6LysB, TM4LysB, PDRPxvLysB36, Mycobacterium_fortuitumLysB37 and RitSunLysB9) constructed by hmmbuild (Suppl. Previously, a LysB C-terminal linker domain was annotated (IPR041855, containing 634 sequences, updated on 15 May 2026), which is composed of four α-helices connecting the C-terminal α-helix back to the side of the central β-sheet8. After careful examination, 547 out of the 634 sequences were confirmed to be from mycobacteriophages (Supplementary Data 10). By analyzing the location of this C-terminal linker domain, we found that it corresponds to the SSS reported in this work. Given the conservation of the SSS described above, we believe that all the IPR041855 members are LysBs. To further measure the length range of the SSS in LysB, 547 sequences were aligned by MAFFT. The results (Supplementary Data 11) revealed that the length of the SSS ranged from 64 to 127 residues, similar to those of D29LysB, TM4LysB and Ms6LysB, confirming that all the LysBs contain this domain.

We speculated that the antimycobacterial activity from within of D29LysB might also require PG-binding activity and that this could be contributed by the SSS, based on a previous report that Ms6LysB has strong PG binding ability and that D29LysB and Ms6LysB are homologous proteins having the same physiological function and the same or similar catalytic mechanisms16. Discussion

In this study, we investigated the mechanisms underlying the antimycobacterial activities of TM4LysB by comparing these activities from without and from within with those of D29LysB and Ms6LysB, revealing the role of the PGBD in LysB function and illuminating LysB’s catalytic and regulatory mechanisms. The results showed that the NTD is important for the antimycobacterial activity from without and from within of TM4LysB potentially because of its impact on the translocation of TM4LysB to mAGP and not because of its regulation of catalytic activity or PG-binding. We also found that the antimycobacterial activity of TM4LysB, D29LysB and Ms6LysB depends on a highly conserved super-secondary structure (SSS), the linker domain located around the catalytic center allowing the PG-binding ability of the catalytic domain, which structurally distinguishes LysBs from other members of the cutinase family and affects catalytic activity and possibly the mAGP-targeted translocation. How this depends on PG-binding, however, remains unclear.

The N-termini of 18 of 70 (25.71%) mycobacteriophage LysBs identified in the cutinase family (PF01083) bear a PGBD in the NTD (Suppl. Data 6). Furthermore, the NTDs of 446 (81.54%) of all 547 known LysBs contain characterized motifs similar with that of the PGBD in the NTD of Ms6LysB, and are therefore presumed to be PGBD (Suppl. Data 11). However, the role of the N-terminal PGBD in mycobacteriophage LysB function is unknown. Previous studies of Ms6LysB confirmed that its N-terminal PGBD has PG-binding activity and that this PGBD can inhibit the lysis activity of Ms6LysB16. In this work, we explored the role of the N-terminal PGBD in antimycobacterial activity from without by directly adding LysB to the medium containing mycobaterial cells and from within by inducing the expression of LysB carrying a signal peptide in the cytoplasm of the mycobaterial cell, finding that the role of the N-terminal PGBD in antimycobacterial activity from without and from within of the same LysB was inconsistent. For example, the N-terminal PGBD inhibited antimycobacterial activity from without of TM4LysB but was required for the antimycobacterial activity from within with the help of Mpt63_SP (Figs. 1D and 3A). Besides, the effects of the N-terminal PGBD in different LysBs were inconsistent. Although N-terminal PGBD inhibited antimycobacterial activity from without of both TM4LysB and Ms6LysB(ΔSSS):SSSD29LysB (Fig. 1D, Suppl. Fig. 6B), with the assistance of Mpt63_SP, it was required for antimycobacterial activity from within of TM4LysB, but not for that of Ms6LysB(ΔSSS):SSSD29LysB (Figs. 3A and 6D). However, all active LysB proteins and their active variants containing the intact catalytic residues could hydrolyze mAGP (Figs. 2D, 5C, and 6E) except those in which the SSS had been replaced such as D29LysB(ΔSSS):SSS5W95/2CZQ/1G66. Given that the function of LysB requires not only mAGP-hydrolysis activity but also its localization to mAGP, our results indirectly implied that a) the NTD may regulate the antimycobacterial activity of LysB from without or from within by affecting its translocation to mAGP, but not by modulating its hydrolysis activity against mAGP; and b) the necessity of the SSS may be two-fold – it is critical for the catalytic activity of the LysB, and may contribute to the translocation of the LysB to the mAGP. Point (b) is based on our findings that D29LysB(ΔSSS):SSS5W95/2CZQ/1G66 could not hydrolyze mAGP (Fig. 5C); and that although the mAGP-hydrolysis activity of Ms6LysB_CTD and D29LysB are similar (Fig. 2D), Ms6LysB_CTD showed no antimycobacterial activity from without and from within until its SSS was replaced with that of D29LysB (Fig. 6D, Suppl. Fig. 6B).

The molecular mechanisms through which the NTD and SSS may affect the translocation of LysB to mAGP from the outside to the inside are incompletely understood. Those responsible for the outwards-to-inwards transport of small-molecular-weight nutrients such as glucose, glycerol, amino acids, metal ions, and phosphates, as well as the receptor-mediated import of scarce nutrients such as Fe(III)-loaded siderophores, haem, or cobalamin47 have been revealed, but those transporting extracellular proteins into cells remain unclear for most prokaryotes. One exception is Gemmata obscuriglobus, a bacterium belonging to the phylum Planctomycetes, which acquires proteins from the external environment through an energy-dependent mechanism similar to endocytosis in eukaryotes48.

The protein trafficking system relied on by Mycobacteria to transport proteins from the inside to the outside is conserved in prokaryotes. This system includes at least three pathways: the Sec and Tat pathways, which transport proteins across the inner membrane, and the type VII secretion (T7S) pathway, which mainly secretes proteins outside the outer membrane49. The Sec pathway is the main protein export pathway for all bacteria, including Mycobacteria50–52, and is crucial for their survival53. Following the previously successful application of the Sec transport system in Msm27, we used the signal peptide of Mpt63 (Rv1926c) (Mpt63_SP, M1-A29) for the transmembrane transport of all LysBs, LysB homologues, and their variants. However, the antimycobacterial activity from within of certain LysBs and their derivatives did not align with their mAGP-hydrolysis activity. For instance, four LysB variants, namely, TM4LysB_CTD, Ms6LysB, Ms6LysB_CTD, and Ms6LysB(ΔSSS):SSSD29LysB, exhibited obvious mAGP-hydrolysis activity (Figs. 2D and 6E) but only marginal antimycobacterial activity from within with the help of Mpt63_SP (Figs. 3A, 6C and 6D). Given that the Sec pathway can transport unfolded protein substrates, which require proper folding in the periplasmic space54, we speculate that these four LysB variants cannot be transported by the Sec pathway or can be transported by the Sec pathway but cannot be correctly folded in the periplasm. Consequently, we attempted to use the Tat pathway, the second highly conserved pathway that transports folded but not unfolded proteins55, to transport the proteins with strong mAGP-hydrolysis activity such as TM4LysB_CTD and Ms6LysB. This transport was accomplished with the help of a signal peptide containing the twin-arginine motif56. Here, we placed the signal peptide of PlcB (Rv2350c, MTRRQFFAKAAAATTAGAFMSLAGPIIEKAYG), a well-characterized Tat-type signal peptide57, at the N-terminus of TM4LysB_CTD and Ms6LysB, forming Msm-PlcB_SP-TM4LysB_CTD and Msm-PlcB_SP-Ms6LysB, respectively. However, the Tat pathway could not successfully transport TM4LysB_CTD and Ms6LysB into the periplasmic space, based on the antimycobacterial activity from within results (Suppl. Fig. 9) which shows that neither TM4LysB_CTD nor Ms6LysB could inhibit the growth of Msm. Together with the observation that certain LysBs [such as D29LysB, TM4LysB, TM4LysB(ΔPGBD):SUMO, TM4LysB(ΔPGBD):PGBD1LBU/CwlM_PGBD1/CwlM_PGBD2/CwlM_NTD and Ms6LysB_CTD(ΔSSS):SSSD29LysB] do inhibit the growth of Msm from within having been transported via the Sec pathway (Figs. 3B, 5B and 6D), we believe these results support the conclusion that although the highly conserved Sec pathway present in Mycobacteria can successfully transport most LysBs (8 out of 15), it can’t replace the cognate protein transport pathway of bacteriophages mediated by Holin. Therefore, introducing a modified Holin, such as those from D2958 and Ms659, with weakened membrane penetration activity, which would induce the sublethal state of the mycobacterial cell, may help to elucidate the regulatory effects of different domains on the function of LysB in an environment that better mimics physiological conditions.

Although this work indirectly demonstrated the effects of PGBDs in the NTD and the catalytic pocket on the transmembrane transport of LysB, direct evidence for cellular localization is lacking. We attempted to detect the cellular localization of LysB variants using the commonly used indicators E. coli PhoA28,29,31 (Suppl. Fig. 3D). However, it is proved to be unsuitable due to PhoA’s need to dimerize. Accordingly, a simple indicator of intracellular protein localization in Mycobacteria is still needed.

Materials & Methods

Bacterial strains and growth conditions

The bacterial strains used in this study are listed in Suppl. Table 5 contained in the Suppl. Materials. Escherichia coli strain DH5α was used for DNA cloning and Escherichia coli strain BL21(DE3) was used for protein expression. DH5α and BL21(DE3) were cultured in LB liquid or agar medium. Mycolicibacterium smegmatis MC2 155 strains were cultured in Middlebrook 7H9 medium supplemented with 0.2% glycerol, 0.05% Tween 80 or Middlebrook 7H10 agar supplemented with 0.5% glycerol. All the above strains were grown at 37 °C and shaken at 160 rpm in liquid medium.

Plasmid constructions

The following genes were optimized and synthesized by Sangon Biotech in Shanghai city, PR. China (https://store.sangon.com/): the gene encoding D29LysB was inserted into pET28a, the gene encoding Ms6LysB was inserted into pQE30, the gene encoding 2CZQ was inserted into pET22b, the genes encoding 1G66 and 1LBU were inserted into pRSFDuet-1 (Novagen), the gene encoding GFPS1-S10 was inserted into pRSFDuet-1, and the gene encoding GFPS11 was inserted into pET22b. DNA amplification, plasmid isolation, and electrophoresis were performed using standard techniques60,61, and E. coli and Mycolicibacterium smegmatis MC2 155 strains were transformed as described previously in ref. 61.

The pLJR962 plasmid is a gift from Sarah Fortune (Addgene plasmid #115162; http://n2t.net/addgene:115162; RRID:Addgene_115162), which contains an ATc inducible promoter, which is a regulated gene expression system that can regulate gene expression in presence of ATc. The system consists of an operon (tetO) and a repressor (TetR), and precise regulation of target gene expression can be achieved by adjusting the concentration of ATc. Due to the growth inhibition of Msm caused by the expression of certain genes or chimaeras in this study, precise regulation of the expression of these genes is required. Therefore, in this study, the pLJR962 vector was used to express LysB and various chimeric proteins to investigate the antimycobacterial activity from within.

The Primers, Plasmids and Strains used in this study are listed in Suppl. Tables 3, 4 and 5 contained in the Suppl. Information. Protocols for plasmid construction can be found in the section of “Supplementary Methods” contained in the Supplementary Information.

Expression and purification of proteins

BL21(DE3) carrying pET22b or pET28a or pRSFDuet-1 or pQE30 plasmids were grown in LB liquid medium supplemented with 50 μg/mL kanamycin or 100 μg/mL ampicillin to OD600 0.6, and then isopropyl-β-thiogalactoside (IPTG) was added to the culture at a final concentration of 0.3 mM and cultured at 22 °C for 16 h. The cells were collected by centrifugation at 6000 × g for 2 min and resuspended in 40 mL of lysis buffer (50 mM Tris·HCl pH 7.6, 150 mM NaCl, 40 mM imidazole). The supernatant or precipitate was recovered by ultrasonic lysis at 4 °C and centrifugation at 13,000 × g for 30 min.

For proteins tagged with 6×His other than 5W95 and 1G66, proteins were separated from the supernatant by Ni-NTA agarose chromatography (Smart-lifesciences, SA051500). After two washing steps with lysis buffer, the 6×His-tagged proteins were eluted from the resin with elution buffer (50 mM Tris·HCl pH 7.6, 150 mM NaCl, 500 mM imidazole). For the SUMO-Ms6LysB_CTD(ΔSSS):SSSD29LysB protein tagged with 6×His, the proteins eluted from the resin were collected according to the above method, and a certain amount of purified SUMO-specific proteases (ULP1) were added to digest overnight at 4 °C to remove the N-His-SUMO domain in the fusion expression protein, and the target protein Ms6LysB_CTD(ΔSSS):SSSD29LysB was obtained by Ni-NTA agarose chromatography. Ms6LysB(ΔSSS):SSSD29LysB was also purified in the same way.

In order to purify the inclusion body protein 1G66, the precipitate was resuspended in 20 mL of buffer A (8 M urea, 100 mM NaH2PO4, 10 mM Tris·HCl, pH 8.0) and placed at 16 °C overnight. The inclusion body protein solution containing 8 M urea was centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was recovered. The supernatant was dialyzed by gradient for protein renaturation. At 4 °C, the solution was dialyzed with buffer B (50 mM Tris·HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 6 M urea, pH 8.0) for 3 h, stirred at low speed with a magnetic stirrer, and the urea content in buffer B was gradually reduced to 4, 2, and 0 M, while other components remained unchanged. After the dialysis was completed, the solution was centrifuged at 4 °C, 13,000 × g for 30 min, and the supernatant was recovered. The target protein was separated from the supernatant by Ni-NTA agarose chromatography as described above.

The inclusion body protein 5W95 was purified with reference to the previous method and slightly modified62. The precipitate was resuspended in 20 mL of buffer C (50 mM Tris·HCl, 3 M NaCl, pH 7.5), centrifuged at 13,000×g for 30 min at 4°C, and the precipitate was recovered. The precipitate was resuspended in 20 mL of buffer D (50 mM Tris·HCl, 4 M guanidine hydrochloride, pH 7.5), centrifuged at 13,000×g for 30 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was recovered. The protein was separated from the supernatant by Ni-NTA agarose chromatography. The difference from the above description was that the 6×His-tagged protein was eluted in the last step using buffer E (50 mM Tris·HCl, 4 M guanidine hydrochloride, 500 mM imidazole, pH 7.5). The obtained eluate was dialyzed with lysis buffer at 4°C for 3 h and stirred at low speed with a magnetic stir bar. After dialysis, the supernatant was centrifuged at 4°C, 13,000×g for 30 min to obtain the target protein.

For proteins labeled with Strep-tagII, the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm filter and incubated with a pre-equilibrated Streptactin Beads 4FF gravity column (Smart-lifesciences, SA053005) at 16°C for 1 h. After washing with equilibration/washing buffer (100 mM Tris·HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0) for 10 column volumes, the Strep-tagII labeled protein was eluted with elution buffer (100 mM Tris·HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 2.5 mM desthiobiotin, pH 8.0).

SDS-PAGE was performed using 12% polyacrylamide gel, and protein purity was monitored by Coomassie blue staining. Protein concentration was determined by UV spectroscopy or quantified using a BCA kit (Beyotime, P0012) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Pull-down experiments

In order to detect the interaction between NTD and CTD of TM4LysB protein, we used 6×His-TM4LysB_NTD protein and Strep-TM4LysB_CTD protein for 6×Histag-Pulldown and Strep-tagII-Pulldown assays. The two proteins with a final concentration of 50 μM were incubated with 50 μL Ni-NTA Beads or Streptactin Beads at 4 °C for 2 h, and the supernatant was removed by low-speed centrifugation to recover the beads. The beads were washed 5 times with washing buffer (PBS, 137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 10 mM Na2HPO4, 2 mM KH2PO4, pH 7.4), and all samples in the pull-down experiment were detected by SDS-PAGE using 12% polyacrylamide gel.

Spot test for antibacterial ability from without

In order to explore whether LysB proteins of different bacteriophages and other chimaeras can inhibit the growth of wild-type strains of Mycolicibacterium smegmatis MC2 155 from without, the purified proteins were spotted on double-layer plates to observe the growth of Mycolicibacterium smegmatis MC2 155 at the spot. In brief, 1 mL of Mycolicibacterium smegmatis MC2 155 wild-type culture (OD600 1.0) was mixed with 5 mL of LB agar medium (0.6% agarose), and the mixture was poured on 7H10 medium to prepare double-layer agar plates, cooled and solidified for 15 min. 3 μL of serially diluted protein samples (0.0625–2.0 mg/mL) were spotted equidistantly on double-layer agar plates and air-dried for 5 min. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 16 h, and the antibacterial ability from without of the protein was evaluated by observing the clearing of the spots on the plates.

Preparation and purification of PG

PG of Mycolicibacterium smegmatis MC2 155 was prepared and purified as described previously with minor modifications63. In brief, Mycolicibacterium smegmatis MC2 155 was cultured to late logarithmic phase, and cells were collected by centrifugation at 9000 × g for 2 min at 4 °C and resuspended in ultrapure water. Cells were disrupted by ultrasound at 4 °C, and the precipitate was recovered by centrifugation at 13,000 × g for 20 min. The precipitate was resuspended in 2% SDS and stirred at room temperature overnight. The precipitate was collected by centrifugation as described above, and the precipitate was resuspended again with 2% SDS and gently stirred at 100 °C for 1 h, and cooled to room temperature. The precipitate was collected by centrifugation as described above, and after washing the precipitate three times with water, the precipitate was resuspended in 80% acetone and then centrifuged to remove SDS. The collected precipitate was washed again with water three times, centrifuged as described above, and the obtained precipitate was freeze-dried to obtain mycolyl arabinogalactan (mAGP). mAGP was resuspended in a 0.5% KOH methanol solution, stirred at 37°C for 4 d, and the precipitate was collected by centrifugation as described above, washed twice with methanol and ether respectively, and freeze-dried. The obtained arabinogalactan-PG was treated with 0.025 M H2SO4 at 37 °C for 5 d to obtain insoluble PG, which was repeatedly washed 5 times with water, and the precipitate was collected by centrifugation as described above, and freeze-dried.

Preparation and purification of mAGP

mAGP of Mycolicibacterium smegmatis MC2 155 was prepared and purified as described previously with minor modifications64. In brief, Mycolicibacterium smegmatis MC2 155 was cultured, collected by centrifugation and washed three times in phosphate buffered saline (PBS; pH 7.4). Cells were resuspended in PBS + 2% Triton X-100 (PBSX) and disrupted by ultrasound, and the precipitate was recovered by centrifugation. Insoluble fraction resuspended in PBS stir overnight at 4 °C. After centrifugation, resuspend the pellet in PBS with 2% SDS and incubated at 100 °C for 60 min; this was done three times, and then wash the pellet with H2O, 80% acetone in H2O and acetone, respectively. The resulting purified mAGP was dried by lyophilization, resuspend the mAGP-enriched cell wall material (mAGP) in PBS with 0.1% Triton X-100 and store frozen aliquots at −80 °C.

Assays for hydrolysis of Mycolicibacterium smegmatis mAGP

Assays for hydrolysis of Mycolicibacterium smegmatis mAGP as described previously with minor modifications8. In short, mAGP material was chemically hydrolyzed by addition of an equal volume of 15% tetrabutylammonium (TBAH) to 0.5 mg mAGP resuspended in PBS + 0.1% Triton X-100 and incubated at 100 °C overnight. Different proteins (200 μg) and mAGP (1 mg) were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Samples treated with TBAH and enzymes adding an equal volume of dichloromethan. The organic phase was extracted using 0.25 M HCl and water, respectively, and lipids were collected by evaporation. Samples were resuspended in dichloromethane, spotted onto silica-aluminum TLC plates, and separated by chromatography in chloroform/methanol (97:3); lipids were identified by spraying with 5% molybdophosphoric acid (in ethanol) and charring for 15 min at 110 °C.

Peptidoglycan binding assay

The binding capacity of proteins to peptidoglycan was assessed as described previously with slight modifications16. Briefly, 180 μg of each purified protein and 50 μg of BSA (Beyotime, P0012) were mixed with 1 mg of peptidoglycan and resuspended in a final volume of 200 μL buffer (50 mM Tris·HCl, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.6). The reaction system contained 15% glycerol, and a blank control group without peptidoglycan was set up. The reaction system was incubated on ice for 30 min, centrifuged at 500×g for 10 min, and the supernatant was recovered to prepare the sample. The precipitate was washed and resuspended in 500 μL buffer, centrifuged at 2000 × g for 3 min, and the washing was repeated three times to recover the precipitate to prepare the sample. SDS-PAGE was performed using 12% polyacrylamide gels, and the binding capacity of proteins to peptidoglycan was monitored by Coomassie blue staining. The SDS-PAGE was imaged using a ChemiDocTM XRS + . The percentage of bound protein was calculated using the estimated amount of protein in the pellet fraction (bound) divided by the sum of the estimated protein amount on the pellet (bound) and supernatant (unbound) fractions. BSA was used as a negative control in the experiment.

Testing the antibacterial ability from within of proteins by gene overexpression

In order to explore whether overexpression of different phage LysB proteins and other chimaeras can lead to the growth restriction of Mycolicibacterium smegmatis MC2 155, the bacterial suspension of the recombinant strains was spotted on 7H10 plates containing the inducer anhydrotetracycline (ATc) to observe the growth of colonies. In short, the recombinant strain of Mycolicibacterium smegmatis MC2 155 carrying the pLJR962 plasmid was shaken in 7H9 containing kanamycin to obtain bacteria with different OD600. 3 μL of sample was taken from each growth state (OD600 1.0–0.25) and spotted on 7H10 plates containing kanamycin or 7H10 plates containing kanamycin and ATc (25–100 ng/mL), and cultured at 37 °C for 36 h. The antibacterial ability from within of the protein was evaluated by observing the bacterial growth on the plate.

Alkaline phosphatase color reaction

As mentioned above, in order to ensure that the signal peptide of the Mpt63 protein can carry the target protein into the periplasmic space, PhoA was used for color reaction with slight modifications30. In short, the recombinant strain of Mycolicibacterium smegmatis MC2 155 carrying the pLJR962 plasmid was shaken in 7H9 containing kanamycin to obtain bacteria with different OD600. Take 3 μl of sample from each gradient (OD600 1.0–0.25) and spot it on 7H10 medium supplemented with colorimetric phosphatase indicator 5-bromo-4-chloro-3-indolyl phosphate (BCIP, 40 μg/mL, dissolved in dimethylformamide DMF), kanamycin (30 μg/mL) and ATc (100 ng/mL), and culture at 37 °C for 36 h to obtain visible blue colonies. At the same time, set up control groups with or without anhydrotetracycline or BCIP.

GFP bimolecular fluorescence complementation assay

The soluble expression of proteins after replacement of the active region SSS was evaluated using the GFP bimolecular fluorescence complementation assay as described previously with slight modifications43. Briefly, the plasmid pET22b-POI-GFPS11 was transformed into the BL21(DE3) strain carrying the pRSFduet-GFPS1-S10 plasmid by the CaCl2 method, and the transformed cells were isolated in LB medium supplemented with kanamycin, ampicillin sodium and IPTG (0.1 mM) and grown overnight at 37 °C to achieve protein co-expression. Fluorescence complementation results were observed under BWA slides using a differential interference microscope (OLYMPUS, BX53). In a parallel experiment, pET22b-D29-GFPS11 was transformed into BL21(DE3) strain carrying pRSFduet-GFPS1-S10 plasmid as a positive control.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism 8. Data analyses were performed using Microsoft Office Excel (2021). Data are presented as mean ± SD for biological independent samples. The specific n values indicated in each figure legend represent results from independent biological replicate experiments.
