Momentum-Resolved Spectroscopy of Superconductivity with the Quantum Twisting Microscope
Yuval Waschitz, Ady Stern, Yuval Oreg

Abstract
We develop a theoretical framework for probing superconductivity with momentum resolution using the quantum twisting microscope (QTM), a planar tunneling device where a graphene tip is rotated relative to a two-dimensional sample. Due to in-plane momentum conservation, the QTM directly measures the superconducting spectral function along well-defined trajectories in momentum space. The relative intensities of electron and hole excitations encode the Bogoliubov coherence factors, revealing the momentum dependence of the pairing magnitude. Three C 3 ​ z C_{3z} -related tunneling channels enable direct detection of rotational symmetry breaking, as well as nodal points in the superconducting order parameter. We apply our framework to superconductivity within the Bistritzer–MacDonald model of noninteracting electrons and the Topological Heavy Fermion model, which accounts for electron–electron interactions. Together, these capabilities establish the QTM as a direct probe of the pairing symmetry and microscopic origin of superconductivity in two-dimensional materials.



The superconducting pairing potential encodes the microscopic mechanism responsible for electron pairing. In conventional superconductors, the pairing is described by the BCS theory and is assumed to be momentum independent
Bardeen et al. 1957
. In contrast, in many unconventional superconductors, the pairing potential varies with momentum and may exhibit nodes
Tsuei and Kirtley 2000
;
Sigrist and Ueda 1991
;
Sigrist 2005
. Measurement of the pairing potential thus provides a direct connection between experimental observations and the microscopic origin of superconductivity.

In recent years, superconductivity has been discovered in several graphene-based structures, most prominently in magic-angle twisted bilayer graphene (MATBG)
Cao et al. 2018
;
Yankowitz et al. 2019
;
Lu et al. 2019
, and later in twisted trilayer graphene
Park et al. 2021
;
Hao et al. 2021
, rhombohedral trilayer graphene
Zhou et al. 2021
, and bilayer graphene
Zhou et al. 2022
. A range of experimental observations points to unconventional superconductivity, including evidence for nematicity and nodal pairing
Cao et al. 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2025
;
Banerjee et al. 2025
;
Tanaka et al. 2025
;
Oh et al. 2021
;
Kim et al. 2022
;
Park et al. 2025
. These findings suggest that graphene-based superconductors lie beyond the framework of conventional BCS theory
Lake et al. 2022
. However, the microscopic pairing mechanism remains unresolved, with numerous possible pairing symmetries and mechanisms proposed

;
Peltonen et al. 2018
;
Isobe et al. 2018
;

;

In this work, we show that the recently developed quantum twisting microscope (QTM)
Inbar et al. 2023
provides direct access to the magnitude of the superconducting pairing potential
|\Delta_{\bf k}|
in momentum space. In particular, it can detect spontaneous breaking of the
C_{3z}
symmetry of a hexagonal lattice, resolve coherence factors with momentum resolution, and reveal nodal excitations and their momenta through zero-bias conductance.

The QTM
Inbar et al. 2023
is a tunneling device in which a two-dimensional crystalline tip is rotated relative to a two-dimensional sample, forming a planar junction. A gate voltage can be applied separately to the tip (
V_{TG}
) and the sample (
V_{BG}
), while a bias voltage (
V_{b}
) between the tip and the sample can also be applied. For a schematic setup, see
Figure

1(a)
.
Because the junction has a large contact area and the tip retains its crystalline order, tunneling processes conserve in-plane momentum.
This makes the tunneling spectrum directly sensitive to the sample band structure and single-particle spectral function in energy–momentum space.
A key advantage of the QTM is the ability to continuously vary the twist angle, allowing systematic scans of the spectral functions along well-defined trajectories in momentum space.
In contrast to scanning tunneling microscopy (STM), which measures only the spatial density of states, and angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES), which accesses only occupied states, the QTM provides momentum-resolved tunneling spectroscopy of both filled and empty states with remarkable resolution.

From a theoretical perspective, the QTM has been proposed as a probe of fractionalized excitations in spin liquids
Peri et al. 2024
, magnetic order
Pichler et al. 2024
, plasmons
Wei et al. 2025a
, and phonons via inelastic tunneling
Xiao et al. 2024
. Subsequent work has established a general framework for Dirac-point spectroscopy of flat-band systems
Wei et al. 2025b
. In the context of superconductivity, momentum-conserving Josephson interferometry has been proposed as a method to probe pairing symmetry
Xiao et al. 2023
.
Experimentally, room-temperature QTM measurements have mapped the band structures of monolayer graphene (MLG) and twisted bilayer graphene (TBG)
Inbar et al. 2023
, and have probed electron–electron interaction corrections to the graphene Dirac dispersion
Lee et al. 2025
. Cryogenic implementations have dramatically extended these capabilities, demonstrating angle-tunable phonon spectroscopy through inelastic tunneling
Birkbeck et al. 2025
. Most recently, QTM studies have revealed the strongly correlated band structure of MATBG
Xiao et al. 2025
, consistent with a topological heavy-fermion description
Haule et al. 2019
;
Calderón and Bascones 2020
;
Song and Bernevig 2022
. The proven ability of the QTM to resolve quasiparticle excitations at cryogenic temperatures establishes it as a powerful and promising tool for investigating superconductivity.

In the weak-tunneling regime, the QTM current is evaluated within the tunneling Hamiltonian approximation
Mahan 1990
,

I(V_{b})=\frac{2\pi e}{\hbar}\sum_{\mathbf{k}_{T},\mathbf{k}_{S},\alpha,\beta}\left|T_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{k}_{T},\mathbf{k}_{S})\right|^{2}\int d\omega\,\times\\
A_{T,\alpha}(\mathbf{k}_{T},\omega+eV_{b})A_{S,\beta}(\mathbf{k}_{S},\omega)\left[f(\omega)-f(\omega+eV_{b})\right],

(1)

where
e
is the elementary charge,
\mathbf{k}_{T}
and
\mathbf{k}_{S}
are the Bloch-state momenta,
T_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{k}_{T},\mathbf{k}_{S})
is the tunneling matrix element between the tip (
T
) and sample (
S
),
\alpha
and
\beta
label the band indices,
A_{T,S}(\mathbf{k},\omega)
are the spectral functions of the tip and sample, and
f(\omega)
is the Fermi function. The summation over spin and valley degrees of freedom is implicit. Denoting
\mu_{T}
and
\mu_{S}
as the chemical potentials of the tip and sample, and
\phi
as their electrostatic potential difference, the general relation

-eV_{b}=\mu_{T}-\mu_{S}+\phi

(2)

always holds, setting the available window for tunneling (see
Figure

1(b)
). The chemical potentials enter
Equation

1
through the spectral function. For example, we assume that the tip is a noninteracting metal, whose spectral function in the limit of infinite quasiparticle lifetime is

A_{\alpha}(\omega,\mathbf{k})=\delta(\omega-\xi_{\mathbf{k},\alpha})
,
where
\xi_{\mathbf{k},\alpha}
denotes the tip dispersion measured relative to
\mu_{T}
. Generally, varying
V_{b}
influences
\mu_{S}
,
\mu_{T}
, and
\phi
. To simplify the discussion, we approximate
\mu_{S}
and
\mu_{T}
as independent of
V_{b}
, such that
V_{b}
induces only an electrostatic shift in
\phi
.
In practice, with appropriate gate adjustment, changes in chemical potentials can be compensated (see Appendix
A
).

In the QTM, tunneling conserves in-plane momentum up to reciprocal lattice vectors of the tip and sample top layer,
\mathbf{G}_{T}
and
\mathbf{G}_{S}
.
This implies that the tunneling matrix element has the form
Bistritzer and MacDonald 2010
;
Inbar et al. 2023
;
Wei et al. 2025b

T_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{k}_{T},\mathbf{k}_{S})=\sum_{\mathbf{G}_{T},\mathbf{G}_{S}}T_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{k}_{S}+\mathbf{G}_{S})\delta_{\mathbf{k}_{T}+\mathbf{G}_{T},\mathbf{k}_{S}+\mathbf{G}_{S}},

(3)

where
T_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{k})
quantifies the overlap between the Bloch states of the tip and sample (see
Equation

24
). For incommensurate tip–sample alignment, a fixed
\mathbf{k}_{S}
and
\mathbf{k}_{T}
correspond to at most one pair
\{\mathbf{G}_{T},\mathbf{G}_{S}\}
that satisfies momentum conservation. As a result, different Umklapp channels individually contribute to the sum without interference.

Superconductivity is described within the Bogoliubov–de Gennes (BdG) framework. Using the Nambu spinor
\Psi_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}=(c_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger},c_{-\mathbf{k}})
(suppressing spin and valley indices), where
c_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}
creates an electron in the Bloch state with momentum
\mathbf{k}
, the BdG Hamiltonian reads

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{BdG}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\Psi_{\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\begin{pmatrix}\xi_{\mathbf{k}}&\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}\\
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}^{\ast}&-\xi_{-\mathbf{k}}\end{pmatrix}\Psi_{\mathbf{k}},

(4)

where
\xi_{\mathbf{k}}
is the normal-state dispersion measured from the chemical potential and
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}
is the pairing potential (possibly matrix-valued) that couples each state to its time-reversed hole counterpart.
We focus on singlet or unitary triplet pairing, so the pairing magnitude does not depend on spin and can be represented as a scalar
Mineev et al. 1999
;
Sigrist 2005
. For intra-band pairing with a time-reversal-symmetric normal state, diagonalization of
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{BdG}}
yields
E_{\mathbf{k}}=\sqrt{\xi_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}+|\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}}
.
The single-particle spectral function of the superconductor probed by tunneling is

A(\mathbf{k},\omega)=|u_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}\,\delta(\omega-E_{\mathbf{k}})+|v_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}\,\delta(\omega+E_{\mathbf{k}}),

(5)

with the following definitions for the coherence factors:

|u_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\!\left(1+\frac{\xi_{\mathbf{k}}}{E_{\mathbf{k}}}\right),\qquad|v_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\!\left(1-\frac{\xi_{\mathbf{k}}}{E_{\mathbf{k}}}\right),

(6)

which are the probabilities of adding and removing an electron with momentum
\mathbf{k}
, respectively.
Figure

1(b)
shows a simple example of the superconducting spectral function for parabolic dispersion. We further assume a finite pair-breaking scattering rate,
\Gamma_{SC}
, which broadens the spectral function by replacing the delta function with a Lorentzian of width
\Gamma_{SC}
(see Appendix
E
).

To probe the spectral function of the sample, momentum conservation alone is not sufficient; localization is required in both momentum and energy. In the QTM, this condition is achieved by exploiting the sharp band structure of the graphene tip. In the following analysis, the tip is modeled as monolayer graphene with linear dispersion, whose circular Fermi surface has a radius set directly by the chemical potential
\mu_{T}
. As the electrostatic potential
\phi
is varied by the bias, the tip Dirac point crosses the sample bands and produces a sharp feature in the differential conductance. Consequently, tunneling becomes localized in both energy and momentum near the crossing. This constraint enables selective measurement of the sample spectral function along well-defined momentum trajectories in the moiré Brillouin zone (mBZ); see
Figure

1(c)
.

Building on the results of
Inbar et al. 2023
;
Wei et al. 2025b
, we evaluate the differential conductance (
I^{\prime\prime}\equiv d^{2}I/dV_{b}^{2}
) analytically, in the case where
\mu_{T}=0
and the sample is superconducting. In the limit of infinite quasiparticle lifetime, zero temperature and sample bands which are flat relative to those of the tip, we obtain (see Appendix
C
for details)

I^{\prime\prime}(\theta,V_{b})=\frac{\Omega e^{3}}{\hbar^{3}v_{D}^{2}}|T(\mathbf{K}_{\theta})|^{2}\times\\
\left[|v_{\mathbf{K}_{\theta}}|^{2}\,\delta(eV_{b}-E_{\mathbf{K}_{\theta}})-|u_{\mathbf{K}_{\theta}}|^{2}\,\delta(eV_{b}+E_{\mathbf{K}_{\theta}})\right],

(7)

where
\Omega
is the junction area and
v_{D}
is the Dirac group velocity in the graphene tip.
T(\mathbf{K}_{\theta})
is the tunneling matrix element between states near the tip Dirac point (at momentum
\mathbf{K}_{\theta}
, set by the rotation angle
\theta
of the tip) and the corresponding Bloch states of the sample in the normal state (see
Equation

43
for definition). From this expression, it follows that the Bogoliubov excitation spectrum can be mapped along the trajectory
\mathbf{K}_{\theta}
by varying
V_{b}
and
\theta
.
Setting
\mu_{T}=0
is not essential and is chosen for convenience, as it allows measurement of both electron and hole excitations without changing the sign of
\mu_{T}
(see Appendix
C.1
). A similar expression for the first current derivative
I^{\prime}(\theta,V_{b})
can be obtained by fixing
\phi
and varying
\mu_{T}
for small tip doping (see Appendix
C.3
).

Importantly, for an MLG tip probing a graphene-based sample, tunneling occurs predominantly between the tip and the top graphene layer of the sample. In
Equation

3
,
\mathbf{G}_{T}
and
\mathbf{G}_{S}
denote the reciprocal lattice vectors of the two rotated layers, and tunneling is restricted to the nearest Umklapp processes due to the exponential suppression of the tunneling amplitude at large momentum transfer
Bistritzer and MacDonald 2010
. Consequently, the tip Dirac point couples to three sample momenta related by
C_{3z}
rotations around the Dirac point of the sample’s top layer,
\mathbf{K}_{T}

Inbar et al. 2023
;
Wei et al. 2025b
. Within small-angle approximation, these momenta trace the following trajectories in TBG, defined relative to tip and sample shared
\Gamma
point,

\mathbf{K}_{\theta,n}=\tau\mathbf{K}_{T}+\tau\mathbf{q}_{n}\frac{\theta}{\theta_{\mathrm{tbg}}},\qquad n=1,2,3,

(8)

where
\theta_{\mathrm{tbg}}
is the TBG twist angle,
\tau=\pm 1
is the valley index,
\mathbf{q}_{n}
are the moiré reciprocal vectors of TBG, and
\theta
is the rotation angle of the tip relative to the top layer of the sample.
The three tunneling processes originate from distinct Umklapp scattering and add without interference at small incommensurate angles
Inbar et al. 2023
;
Wei et al. 2025b
, so the measured current (or its voltage derivatives) is the sum of three separate contributions. As a result, each line scan effectively yields three gap values, one for each
\mathbf{K}_{\theta,n}
. If the superconducting order parameter preserves
C_{3z}
symmetry, these lines coincide for all
\theta
, as in
Figures

2(a)
and
2(d)
.

Thus, a splitting of the peaks indicates broken rotational symmetry, as has been pointed out for

breaking due to strain in the normal state

. This behavior is illustrated in
Figures

and

.
The two panels show pairing potentials with

- and

In MATBG, for example, a nodal line aligned with the zigzag direction and crossing the

As seen in

, the QTM’s in-plane momentum conservation allows the Bogoliubov coherence factors

and

to be probed for the three lines of momenta. This is in contrast to STM measurements, which integrate over in-plane momentum, such that the measured differential conductance reflects only the density of states and averages out the momentum dependence of the coherence factors. As the tip Dirac point traces
\mathbf{K}_{\theta}
across the mBZ, the QTM resolves the quasiparticle peaks at
eV_{b\pm}=\pm E_{\mathbf{k}}
. Under the assumptions used to derive

, their contributions scale as
I^{\prime\prime}_{+}(\theta)\propto|T(\mathbf{K}_{\theta})|^{2}|u_{\mathbf{K}_{\theta}}|^{2}
for

for

Because both share the same tunneling matrix element, it cancels out in the ratio, which becomes

R(\mathbf{K}_{\theta})\equiv\frac{I^{\prime\prime}_{+}(\theta)}{I^{\prime\prime}_{-}(\theta)}=\frac{|u_{\mathbf{K}_{\theta}}|^{2}}{|v_{\mathbf{K}_{\theta}}|^{2}}=\frac{E_{\mathbf{K}_{\theta}}+\xi_{\mathbf{K}_{\theta}}}{E_{\mathbf{K}_{\theta}}-\xi_{\mathbf{K}_{\theta}}}.

(9)

through

In some systems, superconductivity might be based on a valley-polarized normal state that breaks TRS

;

;

\delta\xi_{\mathbf{k}}=\frac{\xi_{\mathbf{k}}-\xi_{-\mathbf{k}}}{2}

. The coherence factors in

is now measured relative to

In the measurement configuration discussed so far, scanning with a Dirac tip probes tunneling only in the vicinity of the tip Dirac point.
Consequently, the accessible information is restricted to a narrow region of momentum space, providing only partial insight into the superconducting pairing structure and leaving possible nodal points along the Fermi surface out of reach.

We demonstrate that operating in a different modality allows the measurement to be extended to a significantly larger portion of momentum space, and in particular enables the identification of nodal points in the pairing potential, if they exist.
To this end, we consider the case where the tip has a finite Fermi surface and focus on zero bias voltage,
V_{b}=0
.
Experimentally, this is achieved by tuning the gate voltages so that the tip chemical potential
\mu_{T}
and the electrostatic shift
\phi
vary simultaneously while maintaining
V_{b}\!\approx\!0
.
This configuration enables the measurement to probe quasiparticle excitations at the center of the superconducting gap, i.e., around
\mu_{S}
(see Appendix
G
).

In this regime, the zero-bias conductance exhibits a peak whenever the circular Fermi surface of the tip—with radius

k_{F}^{T}=|\mu_{T}|/(\hbar v_{D})

and centered at the tip Dirac momentum
\mathbf{K}_{\theta}
—crosses a nodal point
\mathbf{k}_{0}
.
This scheme therefore provides a direct method to determine the radial momentum distance between a well-defined reference point in momentum space (the tip Dirac point) and a nodal point.
Repeating the measurement for two different tip rotation angles,
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
, corresponding to Fermi circle centers
\mathbf{K}_{\theta_{1}}
and
\mathbf{K}_{\theta_{2}}
, makes it possible to triangulate the nodal momenta geometrically from the intersection of the two tip Fermi circles (see Appendix
G
for details).

\ \Gamma_{SC}=0.04~$\mathrm{m}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{V}$,\ T=0.2~$\mathrm{K}$
. We choose
\Delta_{f}=0.4~$\mathrm{m}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{V}$,\Delta_{c}=0.8~$\mathrm{m}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{V}$
as the pairing magnitudes in the
f
and
c

Up to this point, we have calculated the tunneling spectrum of the QTM for a superconductor within a noninteracting quadratic model. One may then wonder what remains of this picture once electronic interactions—particularly those responsible for flat-band physics—are taken into account. To address this question, we turn to the heavy-fermion (HF) framework, which has been demonstrated to successfully capture the correlated band structure of MATBG
Xiao et al. 2025
. This model, formulated in terms of localized

f
electrons hybridizing with itinerant Dirac-like

c
electrons, provides a natural way to extend our noninteracting discussion in a one-shot approximation while consistently incorporating strong interaction effects.

The
f
and
c
states hybridize at the
\gamma
point and, in the absence of interactions, reproduce the band structure of the BM model. The corresponding wave functions are obtained based on the BM model in the plane-wave basis. Within this basis, Ref.
Song and Bernevig 2022
derives the interacting Hamiltonian and applies a mean-field approximation to its terms. It further shows that for the integer MATBG filling, evaluating the mean-field Hamiltonian using a parent state defined by the occupation of the
f
orbitals yields results that closely match the fully self-consistent solution.

Following this one-shot mean-field approach, we calculate the band structure and tunneling matrix elements in the HF basis.
Figure

4(a)
shows the resulting band structure for the
\nu=-2
, Kramers intervalley coherent (K-IVC) state
Bultinck et al. 2020
, obtained under the simplifying assumptions of chiral symmetry (see Appendix
D.4
for details). We further examine a possible Fermi-level alignment near charge neutrality, assuming that small doping does not significantly modify the band structure. In this configuration, the Fermi energy intersects the flat
f
bands, producing a Fermi surface near the edges of the mBZ, and also crosses the dispersive
c
bands, resulting in a small Fermi pocket around the
\gamma
point. The exact topology of the Fermi surface in MATBG remains uncertain
Kang et al. 2021
;
Datta et al. 2023
;
Rai et al. 2024
;
Zhou et al. 2024
;
Calderón et al. 2025
;
Merino et al. 2025
;
Călugăru et al. 2025
; this alignment is considered because it includes two distinct types of Fermi surfaces that may occur separately or coexist.

We consider isotropic superconducting pairing and calculate the QTM spectra for a pairing located at the edge of the mBZ (
Figure

4(b)
) and at the center of the mBZ (
Figure

4(c)
). This comparison highlights a key advantage of the QTM. Whereas STM would detect a gapped spectrum in both cases, the QTM can resolve the specific tip rotation angles at which the gap appears. A gap observed at
-\theta_{\mathrm{tbg}}<\theta<0
indicates pairing predominantly among flat
f
-electron states, while a gap appearing near
\theta_{\mathrm{tbg}}
, associated with the dispersive band, signifies pairing among
c
electrons. Thus, the QTM provides a direct probe to address the open question of whether superconducting pairing in MATBG arises from
f
or
c
components, as well as the nature of the quasiparticles around the Fermi energy. For simplicity, we assume isotropic pairing between states related by spinless time-reversal symmetry
Bultinck et al. 2020
and neglect any spin or valley dependence. Our previous analysis regarding the detection of
C_{3z}
-symmetry breaking, coherence-factor ratios, and nodal point detection remains valid for the mean-field HF, since this Hamiltonian yields well-defined quasiparticle eigenstates, similar to the BM model.

Discussion and Conclusions
We have shown that the quantum twisting microscope (QTM) can be used as a momentum-resolved probe of superconductivity.
By rotating the QTM tip in real space, the tip Dirac point maps the Bogoliubov excitations
E_{\mathbf{k}}
along well-defined trajectories in momentum space.
The relative intensities of the peaks in
I^{\prime\prime}
correspond to electron and hole excitations, allowing one to directly extract the coherence factor magnitudes
|u_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}
and
|v_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}
, and hence determine the pairing amplitude
|\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}|
, even away from the Fermi momentum
k_{F}
.
Because elastic tunneling originates from three Umklapp scattering processes related by
C_{3z}
rotations, a single line scan yields three pairing measurements. Their degeneracy tests
C_{3z}
symmetry, and any splitting that appears only in the superconducting state points to nematicity in the pairing. A vanishing gap identifies a nodal point on the line scan.
A complementary, zero-bias measurement that expands the tip Fermi circle produces sharp zero-bias resonances when the circle crosses a node, enabling geometric triangulation of the nodal momenta.

The same protocols can be applied to MATBG and related moiré superconductors, and can be combined with normal-state measurements for quantitative calibration of tunneling matrix elements and for identifying the nature of the normal state.

Within the heavy-fermion description of MATBG, the QTM can further identify whether superconducting pairing arises from the flat (
f
) or dispersive (
c
) bands.

Looking ahead, extending to stronger tunneling regimes could enable phase-sensitive and momentum-conserving Andreev spectroscopy.
In addition, spin and valley selectivity (using engineered tip spin–orbit coupling) could reveal the internal flavor structure of the superconducting pairing.
Overall, the QTM provides a powerful, momentum-resolved platform to measure the pairing potential and characterize unconventional superconductivity in two-dimensional and moiré materials.

Appendix A
Electrostatics of the QTM junction

We follow Ref.
Xiao et al. 2024
to derive the electrostatic relations governing the QTM junction, with minor modifications.
For the tip (T) and sample (S), we define the electrochemical potential

\mu_{X}^{\mathrm{ec}}=\mu_{X}-\phi_{X}
with
X\in\{T,S\}
,
where
\mu_{X}
is the intrinsic chemical potential and
\phi_{X}
is the electrostatic potential.
A bias voltage
V_{b}
determines their difference as

\mu_{T}^{\mathrm{ec}}-\mu_{S}^{\mathrm{ec}}=-eV_{b}\;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\;-eV_{b}=\mu_{T}-\mu_{S}-\phi_{T}+\phi_{S},

(11)

where
\phi=\phi_{S}-\phi_{T}
is the electrostatic potential difference across the junction (in units of energy).

We consider the circuit illustrated in
Figure

1(a)
, where the tip, sample, and gates are modeled as a series of infinite parallel-plate capacitors.
Assuming a symmetric bias configuration, the potentials satisfy

\frac{eV_{b}}{2}=\mu_{S}-\phi_{S},\quad-\frac{eV_{b}}{2}=\mu_{T}-\phi_{T}.

(12)

The capacitor equations for symmetric top and bottom gates are

\displaystyle(\phi_{\mathrm{TG}}-\phi_{T})=-\frac{e^{2}}{\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{g}}}\,d_{g}\,n_{\mathrm{TG}},

(13)

\displaystyle(\phi_{T}-\phi_{S})=-\frac{e^{2}}{\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon}\,d\,(n_{T}+n_{\mathrm{TG}})=\frac{e^{2}}{\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon}\,d\,(n_{S}+n_{\mathrm{BG}}),

\displaystyle(\phi_{S}-\phi_{\mathrm{BG}})=\frac{e^{2}}{\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{g}}}\,d_{g}\,n_{\mathrm{BG}},

where
\phi_{\mathrm{TG}},\phi_{T},\phi_{S},\phi_{\mathrm{BG}}
are the electrostatic potentials of the top gate (TG), tip, sample, and bottom gate (BG).

n_{\mathrm{TG}},n_{\mathrm{BG}},n_{T},
and
n_{S}
are the corresponding charge densities.
d_{g}
and
\varepsilon_{g}
are the dielectric thickness and relative permittivity of the layers separating the top gate from the tip and the bottom gate from the sample,
while
d
and
\varepsilon
denote the dielectric thickness and relative permittivity
of the layer between the sample and the tip.
\varepsilon_{0}
is the vacuum permittivity.
Assuming metallic gates with a large density of states, their applied voltages fix the potentials such that
eV_{\mathrm{TG/BG}}=\phi_{\mathrm{TG/BG}}
.
We define the specific capacitances:

C=\frac{\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon}{d},\quad C_{g}=\frac{\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon_{g}}{d_{g}}.

(14)

The resulting system can be reduced to three compact relations:

\displaystyle e(V_{\mathrm{TG}}-V_{\mathrm{BG}})

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}}{C_{g}}(n_{T}-n_{S})-\left(1+\frac{2C}{C_{g}}\right)\phi,

(15)

\displaystyle e(V_{\mathrm{TG}}+V_{\mathrm{BG}})

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}}{C_{g}}(n_{T}+n_{S})+\mu_{T}+\mu_{S},

\displaystyle-eV_{b}

\displaystyle=\phi+\mu_{T}-\mu_{S}.

Equation

15
determines the three unknowns
\{\phi,\mu_{T},\mu_{S}\}
for a given device geometry, gates, and bias voltages.
We first consider the limit of large gate capacitance (
C_{g}\gg C
, or equivalently
d_{g}\ll d
).
This limit poses experimental challenges: increasing the thickness of the tunneling barrier significantly reduces the current across the junction,
while reducing the gate dielectric thickness may lead to leakage currents.
Nevertheless, analyzing the electrostatic equations in this limit provides useful insight into the required gate-voltage compensation.
In this regime, the equations simplify to

\displaystyle eV_{\mathrm{TG}}-\tfrac{eV_{b}}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}}{C_{g}}\,n_{T}(\mu_{T})+\mu_{T},

(16)

\displaystyle eV_{\mathrm{BG}}+\tfrac{eV_{b}}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}}{C_{g}}\,n_{S}(\mu_{S})+\mu_{S},

\displaystyle-eV_{b}

\displaystyle=\phi+\mu_{T}-\mu_{S}.

These relations show that in this limit
V_{\mathrm{TG}}-V_{b}/2
primarily controls the tip doping, while
V_{\mathrm{BG}}+V_{b}/2
controls the sample doping.
Thus, changing
V_{b}
while compensating
V_{\mathrm{TG}}
and
V_{\mathrm{BG}}
allows one to vary
\phi
without changing
\mu_{T}
and
\mu_{S}
.
Returning to the general case of
Equation

15
(without assuming
C_{g}\!\gg\!C
), we derive the conditions required to vary each of
\phi
,
\mu_{T}
, and
\mu_{S}
independently.
Defining

V_{G}=\tfrac{1}{2}(V_{\mathrm{TG}}+V_{\mathrm{BG}}),\quad V_{D}=\tfrac{1}{2}(V_{\mathrm{TG}}-V_{\mathrm{BG}}),

(17)

and differentiating
Equation

15
with respect to
\phi
, while keeping
\mu_{T}
and
\mu_{S}
constant (enforcing
\frac{\partial\mu_{T}}{\partial\phi}=\frac{\partial\mu_{S}}{\partial\phi}=0
), gives

\displaystyle e\,\frac{\partial V_{G}}{\partial\phi}

\displaystyle=0,

(18)

\displaystyle e\,\frac{\partial V_{D}}{\partial\phi}

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{1}{2}+\,\frac{C}{C_{g}}\right),

\displaystyle e\,\frac{\partial V_{b}}{\partial\phi}

\displaystyle=-1.

Therefore, a change in
\phi
can be induced directly via the bias,
e\,\Delta V_{b}=-\Delta\phi
, with gate compensation given by
\Delta V_{G}=0
and
e\,\Delta V_{D}=(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{C}{C_{g}})\,\Delta V_{b}
.
Experimentally,
C
and
C_{g}
can be determined from fits to data
Inbar et al. 2023
;
Xiao et al. 2025
;
Birkbeck et al. 2025
. If the tunneling barrier and the barrier between the junction and the gates are of the same material, the capacitance ratio
{C}/{C_{g}}
can be calculated using the ratio of their thickness.

Differentiating
Equation

15
with respect to
\mu_{T}
and
\mu_{S}
yields

\displaystyle e\,\frac{\partial V_{b}}{\partial\mu_{T}}

\displaystyle=-1,

(19)

\displaystyle e\,\frac{\partial V_{\mathrm{TG}}}{\partial\mu_{T}}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}}{C_{g}}\,\frac{\partial n_{T}}{\partial\mu_{T}}+\tfrac{1}{2},

\displaystyle e\,\frac{\partial V_{\mathrm{BG}}}{\partial\mu_{T}}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2},

and

\displaystyle e\,\frac{\partial V_{b}}{\partial\mu_{S}}

\displaystyle=1,

(20)

\displaystyle e\,\frac{\partial V_{\mathrm{TG}}}{\partial\mu_{S}}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2},

\displaystyle e\,\frac{\partial V_{\mathrm{BG}}}{\partial\mu_{S}}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}}{C_{g}}\,\frac{\partial n_{S}}{\partial\mu_{S}}+\tfrac{1}{2},

respectively. Hence, controlling
\mu_{T}
independently requires knowledge of the tip density of states, namely,
\partial n_{T}/\partial\mu_{T}
.
For a graphene tip, this dependence is well known, and only the absolute doping needs to be calibrated.
Since
\mu_{T}=0
corresponds to the Dirac point where the tip’s density of states vanishes (yielding zero tunneling current), this calibration can often be performed experimentally
Xiao et al. 2025
.
In contrast, the density of states of the sample,
n_{S}(\mu_{S})
, may have a more complex dependence, making it necessary to determine the sample doping independently using complementary probes.

Appendix B
Calculation of the tunneling current and tunneling matrix element

Here, we briefly review the tunneling current and tunneling matrix element in the QTM with a graphene tip and a sample whose top layer is graphene, following previous works
Bistritzer and MacDonald 2010
;
Inbar et al. 2023
;
Wei et al. 2025b
.
We use
Equation

1
and the sample spectral function
A_{S,\beta}(\mathbf{k}_{S},\omega)
to calculate the current, where
\beta
is the band index. We assume that the tip lifetime is negligible compared to the sample lifetime, so the tip spectral function is taken as
A_{T,\alpha}(\omega,\mathbf{k})=\delta(\omega-\xi_{\mathbf{k},\alpha})
, where
\alpha
labels the tip bands and
\xi_{\mathbf{k},\alpha}
is the dispersion measured relative to the tip chemical potential. Under this assumption, the integral over
d\omega
can be evaluated, yielding

I=\frac{2\pi e}{\hbar}\sum_{\mathbf{k}_{T},\mathbf{k}_{S},\alpha,\beta}\left|T_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{k}_{T},\mathbf{k}_{S})\right|^{2}A_{S,\beta}(\mathbf{k}_{S},\xi_{\mathbf{k}_{T},\alpha}-eV_{b})\left[f(\xi_{\mathbf{k}_{T},\alpha}-eV_{b})-f(\xi_{\mathbf{k}_{T},\alpha})\right].

(21)

Even if the tip has a finite lifetime, the convolution of two Lorentzian spectral functions remains a Lorentzian, with a width equal to the sum of the individual widths. Thus, the following analysis remains valid up to a renormalization of the effective sample lifetime.

The tunneling matrix element between the twisted graphene layers is given by
Bistritzer and MacDonald 2010

T_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{k}_{T},\mathbf{k}_{S})=\frac{1}{\Omega_{0}}\sum_{\mathbf{G}_{T},\mathbf{G}_{S}}t(\mathbf{k}_{S}+\mathbf{G}_{S})\bra{u^T_{\bs{k}_T,\alpha}}T_{\mathbf{G}_{T},\mathbf{G}_{S}}\ket{u^{S,\text{top}}_{\bs{k}_S,\beta}}\delta_{\mathbf{k}_{T}+\mathbf{G}_{T},\,\mathbf{k}_{S}+\mathbf{G}_{S}},

(22)

where
\Omega_{0}
is the unit-cell area,
t(\mathbf{k})
is the Fourier transform of the interlayer hopping amplitude,
\ket{u^T_{\bs{k}_T,\alpha}}
is the Bloch wavefunction of the tip in the sublattice basis, and
\ket{u^{S,\text{top}}_{\bs{k}_S,\beta}}
is the top-layer component of the sample Bloch wavefunction. The sum runs over reciprocal lattice vectors of the tip graphene layer (
\mathbf{G}_{T}
) and the top graphene layer of the sample (
\mathbf{G}_{S}
).

In the sublattice basis, the matrix
T_{\mathbf{G}_{T},\mathbf{G}_{S}}
is given by

T_{\mathbf{G}_{T},\mathbf{G}_{S}}=\begin{pmatrix}e^{i\mathbf{G}_{T}\cdot\bm{\tau}_{T}^{A}-i\mathbf{G}_{S}\cdot\bm{\tau}_{S}^{A}}&e^{i\mathbf{G}_{T}\cdot\bm{\tau}_{T}^{A}-i\mathbf{G}_{S}\cdot\bm{\tau}_{S}^{B}}\\
e^{i\mathbf{G}_{T}\cdot\bm{\tau}_{T}^{B}-i\mathbf{G}_{S}\cdot\bm{\tau}_{S}^{A}}&e^{i\mathbf{G}_{T}\cdot\bm{\tau}_{T}^{B}-i\mathbf{G}_{S}\cdot\bm{\tau}_{S}^{B}}\end{pmatrix}e^{\,i\mathbf{G}_{T}\cdot\mathbf{d}_{T}-i\mathbf{G}_{S}\cdot\mathbf{d}_{S}},

(23)

where
\bm{\tau}^{A/B}_{T/S}
are the positions of the A and B sublattices of the tip and the sample’s top layer, and
\mathbf{d}_{T}
,
\mathbf{d}_{S}
are the displacement vectors of the layers.

For incommensurate alignment, a unique pair
\{\mathbf{G}_{T},\mathbf{G}_{S}\}
satisfies momentum conservation for each
\{\mathbf{k}_{T},\mathbf{k}_{S}\}
. Fixing
\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{k}_{S}+\mathbf{G}_{S}=\mathbf{k}_{T}+\mathbf{G}_{T}
uniquely determines
\mathbf{k}_{S},\mathbf{G}_{S},\mathbf{k}_{T},\mathbf{G}_{T}
. Thus, we define

\left.T_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{k})\right|_{\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{k}_{S}+\mathbf{G}_{S}=\mathbf{k}_{T}+\mathbf{G}_{T}}\equiv\frac{1}{\Omega_{0}}t(\mathbf{k})\bra{u^T_{\bs{k}_T,\alpha}}T_{\mathbf{G}_{T},\mathbf{G}_{S}}\ket{u^{S,\text{top}}_{\bs{k}_S,\beta}},

(24)

recovering
Equation

3
in the main text.
Since
t(\mathbf{k})
decays exponentially with momentum, tunneling is restricted to the first Brillouin zone of the twisted tip–sample system
Bistritzer and MacDonald 2011
. Further assuming that
t(\mathbf{k})
varies weakly in this region, we approximate
t(\mathbf{k})\approx t_{0}
and write the matrices
T_{\mathbf{G}_{T},\mathbf{G}_{S}}
for the three allowed Umklapp processes, separating by valley
\tau=\pm 1
:

\displaystyle T_{1,\tau}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix},

(25)

\displaystyle T_{2,\tau}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&e^{-i\tfrac{2\pi}{3}\tau}\\
e^{i\tfrac{2\pi}{3}\tau}&1\end{pmatrix},

(26)

\displaystyle T_{3,\tau}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&e^{i\tfrac{2\pi}{3}\tau}\\
e^{-i\tfrac{2\pi}{3}\tau}&1\end{pmatrix}.

(27)

Although the phases
e^{i\tau(\mathbf{G}_{T}\cdot\mathbf{d}_{T}-\mathbf{G}_{S}\cdot\mathbf{d}_{S})}
differ between the three processes, at incommensurate angles, a given pair
(\mathbf{k}_{T},\mathbf{k}_{S})
satisfies momentum conservation through at most one channel. Thus, the three processes contribute without interference, and the phases drop out when taking absolute values.

Separating the contributions from each valley, the current becomes

I=\frac{2\pi|t_{0}|^{2}e}{\Omega^{2}_{0}\hbar}\sum_{\tau=\pm 1}\sum_{n=1}^{3}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\sum_{\mathbf{p}}\left|\bra{u^T_{\bs{p},\tau,\alpha}}T_{n,\tau}\ket{u^{S,\text{top}}_{\bs{p},\tau,\beta}}\right|^{2}A_{S,\beta}(\mathbf{p},\xi_{\mathbf{p},\alpha}-eV_{b})\left[f(\xi_{\mathbf{p},\alpha}-eV_{b})-f(\xi_{\mathbf{p},\alpha})\right],

(28)

where
\mathbf{p}
runs over the extended mBZ of the sample for each valley, and the wavefunctions and dispersions of the tip are defined relative to the rotated Dirac point of the tip, denoted as
\tau\mathbf{K}_{\theta,n}
and defined below.
\ket{u^{S,\text{top}}_{\bs{p},\tau,\beta}}
denotes the sample eigenfunction, projected to the top layer, valley
\tau
, and extended mBZ momentum
\mathbf{p}
. In the case of spin degeneracy, an additional factor of 2 should be included.

Assuming the tip is rotated by angle
\theta
relative to the sample top layer, the tip Dirac point positions under the three Umklapp processes, relative to the sample momenta, following from lattice momentum conservation are

\mathbf{K}_{\theta,n}=\mathcal{R}(\theta)(\mathbf{K}_{T}+\mathbf{G}_{n})-\mathbf{G}_{n}=\mathbf{K}_{T}+(\mathcal{R}(\theta)-\mathcal{I})(\mathbf{K}_{T}+\mathbf{G}_{n}),

(29)

where
\mathcal{R}(\theta)
is a rotation matrix,
\mathbf{K}_{T}=\tfrac{4\pi}{3a}(1,0)^{T}
is the Dirac point of the sample top layer when the
K
point is aligned with
+x
, and
a
is the graphene lattice constant.
\mathbf{G}_{1}=(0,0)^{T}
and
\mathbf{G}_{2,3}=\tfrac{4\pi}{3a}(-3/2,\pm\sqrt{3}/2)^{T}
connect the
K
point to the two other equivalent
K
points. For small twist angles,
(\mathcal{R}(\theta)-\mathcal{I})(\mathbf{K}_{T}+\mathbf{G}_{n})
is approximately parallel to the moiré vectors
\mathbf{q}_{n}
of TBG, such that
Equation

8
holds.

Finally, momentum in the extended mBZ (
\mathbf{p}
) can be folded into the first mBZ (
\mathbf{k}
) using moiré reciprocal lattice vectors. Thus,
\ket{u^{S,\text{top}}_{\bs{p},\tau,\beta}}
denotes a component of the multi-component Bloch wavefunction
\ket{u^{S}_{\bs{k},\beta}}
obtained by diagonalizing the TBG Hamiltonian (see Appendix
D
for details).

Appendix C
Deriving the singularities in the QTM current derivatives

C.1
Singularity from the tip Dirac point crossing normal bands

We review the derivation of the singularities in
d^{2}I/dV_{b}^{2}
that arise when the tip Dirac point crosses the sample bands, following Ref.
Wei et al. 2025b
.

We begin from the current equation,
Equation

1

I=\frac{2\pi e}{\hbar}\sum_{\mathbf{k}_{T},\mathbf{k}_{S}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\bigl|T_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{k}_{T},\mathbf{k}_{S})\bigr|^{2}\int d\omega\,A_{T,\alpha}(\mathbf{k}_{T},\omega+eV_{b})\,A_{S,\beta}(\mathbf{k}_{S},\omega)\,[f(\omega)-f(\omega+eV_{b})].

(30)

We assume infinite lifetimes and noninteracting states in both electrodes, so
A_{S,\beta}(\mathbf{k},\omega)=\delta(\omega-E_{\mathbf{k},\beta})
and
A_{T,\alpha}(\mathbf{k},\omega)=\delta(\omega-\xi_{\mathbf{k},\alpha})
, where
\xi_{\mathbf{k},\alpha}
and
E_{\mathbf{k},\beta}
are the tip and sample dispersions relative to
\mu_{T}
and
\mu_{S}
, respectively. After integrating over
\omega
, we replace the momentum sums by integrals. In addition, in-plane momentum is conserved,
\mathbf{k}\equiv\mathbf{k}_{S}+\mathbf{G}_{S}=\mathbf{k}_{T}+\mathbf{G}_{T}
, and incommensurate Umklapp channels contribute incoherently, so the tunneling matrix depends only on
\mathbf{k}
(see Appendix
B
). We obtain

I=\frac{2\pi e\Omega}{\hbar}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\int\!\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,|T_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{k})|^{2}\,A_{S,\beta}(\mathbf{k},\xi_{\mathbf{k},\alpha}-eV_{b})\,\bigl[f(\xi_{\mathbf{k},\alpha}-eV_{b})-f(\xi_{\mathbf{k},\alpha})\bigr],

(31)

with
\Omega
the junction area. Inserting
A_{S,\beta}
gives

I=\frac{2\pi e\Omega}{\hbar}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\int\!\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,|T_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{k})|^{2}\,\delta(\xi_{\mathbf{k},\alpha}-eV_{b}-E_{\mathbf{k},\beta})\,\bigl[f(\xi_{\mathbf{k},\alpha}-eV_{b})-f(\xi_{\mathbf{k},\alpha})\bigr].

(32)

For the tip, we use the graphene linear dispersion,
\xi_{\mathbf{k},\alpha}=\alpha\hbar v_{D}|\mathbf{k}|-\mu_{T}
, and restrict the integral to a small neighborhood of the tip Dirac point (centered at
\mathbf{k}=0
) at
T=0
. This is done under the assumption that the main contribution to the differential conductance originates from this region, since the contour describing the intersection between the graphene bands and the sample changes abruptly. Then the factor
f(\xi_{\mathbf{k},\alpha}-eV_{b})-f(\xi_{\mathbf{k},\alpha})
can be replaced by
f(-eV_{b}-\mu_{T})-f(-\mu_{T})
, yielding

I=\frac{2\pi e\Omega}{\hbar}\bigl[f(-eV_{b}-\mu_{T})-f(-\mu_{T})\bigr]\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\int\!\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,|T_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{k})|^{2}\,\delta(\xi_{\mathbf{k},\alpha}-eV_{b}-E_{\mathbf{k},\beta}).

(33)

We drop the band index
\beta
for brevity. The singular contribution arises from a limited region near the tip Dirac point, and we linearize the sample dispersion as

E_{\mathbf{k}}\approx E_{\mathbf{K}_{\theta}}+\mathbf{v}_{S}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{k}
and assume that the sample wave functions vary smoothly. We use the tip Bloch state near the Dirac point,

\ket{\psi_{\bs{k},\alpha}}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha\\
e^{i\theta_{\mathbf{k}}}\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)
, where
\theta_{\mathbf{k}}
is measured from the axis connecting the tip Dirac point to graphene
\Gamma
(we restrict to a single valley). We approximate the sample wavefunction as constant, so the tunneling matrix takes the form

T_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k})\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(\alpha T_{A}+T_{B}e^{-i\theta_{\mathbf{k}}}\bigr).

(34)

Combining these results,

I=\frac{2\pi e\Omega}{\hbar}\bigl[f(-eV_{b}-\mu_{T})-f(-\mu_{T})\bigr]\sum_{\alpha}\int\!\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\Bigl(\frac{|T_{A}|^{2}+|T_{B}|^{2}}{2}+\alpha\frac{T_{A}^{*}T_{B}}{2}e^{-i\theta_{\mathbf{k}}}+\alpha\frac{T_{A}T_{B}^{*}}{2}e^{i\theta_{\mathbf{k}}}\Bigr)\delta\!\bigl(\varepsilon-\hbar\mathbf{v}_{S}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{k}+\alpha\hbar v_{D}|\mathbf{k}|\bigr),

(35)

where
\varepsilon\equiv-eV_{b}-E_{\mathbf{K}_{\theta}}-\mu_{T}
. Defining

\mathcal{I}_{1}(\varepsilon)=\sum_{\alpha=\pm 1}\int\!\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\delta\!\bigl(\varepsilon-\hbar\mathbf{v}_{S}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{k}+\alpha\hbar v_{D}|\mathbf{k}|\bigr),\quad\mathcal{I}_{2}(\varepsilon)=\sum_{\alpha=\pm 1}\alpha\int\!\frac{d^{2}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,e^{i\theta_{\mathbf{k}}}\,\delta\!\bigl(\varepsilon-\hbar\mathbf{v}_{S}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{k}+\alpha\hbar v_{D}|\mathbf{k}|\bigr),

(36)

we evaluate
\mathcal{I}_{2}
(the evaluation of
\mathcal{I}_{1}
is similar). With
\mathbf{k}=(r\cos\theta_{\mathbf{k}},r\sin\theta_{\mathbf{k}})
and

\mathbf{v}_{S}=v_{S}(\cos\theta_{S},\sin\theta_{S})
, we have

\mathbf{v}_{S}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{k}=v_{S}r\cos(\theta_{\bs{k}}-\theta_S)
, and after the shift

\theta_{\mathbf{k}}\to\theta_{\mathbf{k}}+\theta_{S}
,

\mathcal{I}_{2}(\varepsilon)=e^{i\theta_{S}}\sum_{\alpha=\pm 1}\alpha\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\frac{d\theta_{\mathbf{k}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!dr\,r\,e^{i\theta_{\mathbf{k}}}\,\delta\!\bigl(\varepsilon-\hbar v_{S}r\cos\theta_{\mathbf{k}}+\alpha\hbar v_{D}r\bigr).

(37)

Evaluating the
r
-integral yields

\mathcal{I}_{2}(\varepsilon)=e^{i\theta_{S}}\sum_{\alpha=\pm 1}\alpha\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\frac{d\theta_{\mathbf{k}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\frac{r_{0}(\theta_{\mathbf{k}})\,\Theta[r_{0}(\theta_{\mathbf{k}})]}{\hbar\,|\alpha v_{D}-v_{S}\cos\theta_{\mathbf{k}}|}\,e^{i\theta_{\mathbf{k}}},\qquad r_{0}(\theta_{\mathbf{k}})=\frac{\varepsilon}{\hbar\,(v_{S}\cos\theta_{\mathbf{k}}-\alpha v_{D})}.

(38)

For
v_{D}>v_{S}
, the delta function describes the intersection between the sample and tip bands as a closed ellipse. For a fixed sign of
\varepsilon
, exactly one branch
\alpha=\pm 1
yields
r_{0}>0
at each angle, so the
\Theta
–selection amounts to taking half of the
\alpha
–sum with
|r_{0}|
,

\mathcal{I}_{2}(\varepsilon)=\frac{|\varepsilon|}{2\hbar^{2}}\,e^{i\theta_{S}}\sum_{\alpha=\pm 1}\alpha\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\frac{d\theta_{\mathbf{k}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\frac{e^{i\theta_{\mathbf{k}}}}{(v_{S}\cos\theta_{\mathbf{k}}-\alpha v_{D})^{2}}.

(39)

Using the following identities (for
a>b>0
)

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{e^{i\phi}\,d\phi}{(a-b\cos\phi)^{2}}=\frac{2\pi b}{(a^{2}-b^{2})^{3/2}},\qquad\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\phi}{(a-b\cos\phi)^{2}}=\frac{2\pi a}{(a^{2}-b^{2})^{3/2}},

we obtain, for
v_{D}>v_{S}
,

\mathcal{I}_{2}(\varepsilon)=\frac{v_{S}}{v_{D}}\,e^{i\theta_{S}}\,\frac{|\varepsilon|}{2\pi\hbar^{2}v_{D}^{2}}\Bigl(1-\frac{v_{S}^{2}}{v_{D}^{2}}\Bigr)^{-3/2},\qquad\mathcal{I}_{1}(\varepsilon)=\frac{|\varepsilon|}{2\pi\hbar^{2}v_{D}^{2}}\Bigl(1-\frac{v_{S}^{2}}{v_{D}^{2}}\Bigr)^{-3/2}.

(40)

Collecting terms, the Dirac point contribution to the current (at zero temperature, zero lifetime) is

I(V_{b})=\bigl[f(-eV_{b}-\mu_{T})-f(-\mu_{T})\bigr]\,\frac{\Omega e\,|T(\mathbf{K}_{\theta})|^{2}}{\hbar^{3}v_{D}^{2}}\,\Bigl(1-\frac{v_{S}^{2}}{v_{D}^{2}}\Bigr)^{-3/2}\,\bigl|eV_{b}+\mu_{T}+E_{\mathbf{K}_{\theta}}\bigr|,

(41)

In the zero-temperature limit (
T=0
), the factor
\bigl[f(-eV_{b}-\mu_{T})-f(-\mu_{T})\bigr]
simplifies to

\Theta(eV_{b}+\mu_{T})-\Theta(\mu_{T})
, where
\Theta
denotes the Heaviside step function with
\Theta(0)=\tfrac{1}{2}
.
The derivative of this function is constant except at
eV_{b}=-\mu_{T}
.
Differentiating the current twice for
eV_{b}\neq-\mu_{T}
, we obtain

\frac{d^{2}I}{dV_{b}^{2}}=\bigl[f(-eV_{b}-\mu_{T})-f(-\mu_{T})\bigr]\,\frac{2\Omega e^{3}|T(\mathbf{K}_{\theta})|^{2}}{\hbar^{3}v_{D}^{2}}\,\Bigl(1-\frac{v_{S}^{2}}{v_{D}^{2}}\Bigr)^{-3/2}\,\delta(eV_{b}+\mu_{T}+E_{\mathbf{K}_{\theta}}),

(42)

in agreement with Ref.
Wei et al. 2025b
.
The tunneling matrix element is given by

|T(\mathbf{K}_{\theta})|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(|T_{A}|^{2}+|T_{B}|^{2}+\frac{v_{S}}{v_{D}}\,[T_{A}T_{B}^{*}e^{i\theta_{S}}+{\rm c.c.}]\Bigr).

(43)

The resulting
\delta
-function singularity directly maps the sample dispersion.
Choosing
\mu_{T}>0
(
\mu_{T}<0
) restricts tunneling at the Dirac point to electron-like (hole-like) excitations.
For simplicity, we set
\mu_{T}=0
, so that the tip Dirac point probes both particle- and hole-like excitations,
and
-eV_{b}
directly traces
E_{\mathbf{K}_{\theta}}
.
This choice makes the analytical expression invalid at
V_{b}=0
,
where the region of invalidity is determined by the energy scale
k_{B}T
.
Setting
\mu_{T}=0
yields

\frac{d^{2}I}{dV_{b}^{2}}=\operatorname{sgn}(V_{b})\,\frac{\Omega e^{3}}{\hbar^{3}v_{D}^{2}}\Bigl(1-\frac{v_{S}^{2}}{v_{D}^{2}}\Bigr)^{-3/2}|T(\mathbf{K}_{\theta})|^{2}\,\delta(eV_{b}+E_{\mathbf{K}_{\theta}}),

(44)

and in the flat-band limit (
v_{S}\ll v_{D}
), this reduces to

\frac{d^{2}I}{dV_{b}^{2}}=\operatorname{sgn}(V_{b})\,\frac{\Omega e^{3}}{\hbar^{3}v_{D}^{2}}\,|T(\mathbf{K}_{\theta})|^{2}\,\delta(eV_{b}+E_{\mathbf{K}_{\theta}}).

(45)

We emphasize that the choice to set
\mu_{T}=0
is not mandatory; it is possible to perform two measurements at
\mu_{T}>0
and
\mu_{T}<0
and obtain the same information about hole- and electron-like excitations. All calculations presented in the main text are performed without these analytical approximations
and include finite temperature and lifetime broadening effects.

Generalization to a superconductor

We follow the same steps, now using the superconducting spectral function (see
Equation

5
). Because the coherence factors vary slowly within the narrow region controlling the singularity, we take
|u_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}\simeq|u_{\mathbf{K}_{\theta}}|^{2}

\pm E_{\mathbf{k}}=\pm\sqrt{\xi_{\mathbf{k}}^{{}^{\prime}2}+\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}}

is the normal-state dispersion of the sample. The result at

\frac{d^{2}I}{dV_{b}^{2}}=\frac{\Omega e^{3}}{\hbar^{3}v_{D}^{2}}\,\Bigl(1-\frac{v_{S}^{2}}{v_{D}^{2}}\Bigr)^{-3/2}\Bigl[-\,|T^{+}(\mathbf{K}_{\theta})|^{2}|u_{\mathbf{K}_{\theta}}|^{2}\,\delta(eV_{b}+E_{\mathbf{K}_{\theta}})+\,|T^{-}(\mathbf{K}_{\theta})|^{2}|v_{\mathbf{K}_{\theta}}|^{2}\,\delta(eV_{b}-E_{\mathbf{K}_{\theta}})\Bigr],

where

(47)

Here, we show that the same information obtained by scanning the sample spectral function with the tip Dirac point (varying the electrostatic shift

C.4
Broadening effects in the QTM scans

We review here the possible scanning variables in the QTM and the corresponding broadening mechanisms.
The spectral-function part of the current calculation is usually written as

A_{T}(\mathbf{k},\omega+eV_{b})A_{S}(\mathbf{k},\omega)\sim f(\omega-\phi+\mu_{S}-\epsilon_{T,\mathbf{k}})\cdot g(\omega-(\epsilon_{S,\mathbf{k}}-\mu_{S})),

where
f
and
g
are smooth functions, and
\epsilon_{S/T,\mathbf{k}}
denote the tip and sample bare dispersions.
This term, together with momentum conservation, dictates that the contribution to the current originates from the overlap between the sample and tip bands in both momentum and energy space.
Therefore, the intersection between the bands—arising from this term—depends only on
\phi
.
This consideration (together with the derivations in the previous subsections) leads to the following conclusions:

•

When scanning
\phi
with a finite tip chemical potential (
\mu_{T}\neq 0
) far from the Dirac point, the crossing of the tip Dirac point induces a singularity in
d^{2}I/dV^{2}
.
In this regime, the Fermi function is relatively flat, and the temperature does not significantly broaden the feature.
As
\phi
varies, the relative alignment of the tip and sample bands shifts, and the tunneling current as a function of bias voltage behaves as a convolution of their spectral functions,
A_{T}(\mathbf{k},\omega+eV_{b})\circledast A_{S}(\mathbf{k},\omega)
.
Consequently, the feature width is determined by the sum of the tip and sample broadenings and remains insensitive to temperature.

•

When scanning
\mu_{T}
at fixed
\phi
, assuming that
\mu_{T}
remains small and that the sample bands are relatively flat, a singularity in
dI/dV
arises when the tip chemical potential crosses the intersection contour of the tip and sample bands.
In this case, the tunneling current is proportional to
A_{T}(\mathbf{k},\omega+eV_{b})\cdot A_{S}(\mathbf{k},\omega)
with fixed
\phi
.
Since the spectral functions do not shift relative to each other, no convolution broadening occurs, and the linewidth is determined mainly by the temperature through the Fermi function and the product of two Lorentzians. For large tip broadening with small sample broadening, the response becomes effectively insensitive to the tip broadening, though the finite Fermi-circle radius introduces additional momentum uncertainty.

•

When scanning
\phi
with
\mu_{T}=0
, both broadening mechanisms contribute simultaneously, requiring low temperature and minimal tip broadening to obtain sharp features.
In this configuration, the interpretation is simplified because the
d^{2}I/dV^{2}
singularity remains localized in momentum space (at the Dirac point), allowing both electron- and hole-like excitations to be probed.
We adopt this scanning mode for the analysis presented in the main text, although the first and second scanning schemes may be advantageous when specifically aiming to suppress temperature- or tip-induced broadening, respectively.

Models for MATBG

D.1
Review of the Bistritzer–MacDonald model

We briefly review the continuum Bistritzer–MacDonald (BM) model of twisted bilayer graphene (TBG)
Bistritzer and MacDonald 2011
, following the notations and derivations of Refs.
Song et al. 2019
;
Song and Bernevig 2022
. The low-energy states originate from electronic states near the graphene Dirac points of the top and bottom layers, located at valleys
K
and
K^{\prime}
. Interlayer hopping generates a moiré Brillouin zone (mBZ) onto which the eigenstates are folded, producing moiré bands. The hexagonal mBZ is described by the reciprocal lattices

\mathbf{Q}_{+}=\{\mathbf{q}_{2}+n_{1}\mathbf{b}_{M1}+n_{2}\mathbf{b}_{M2}\},\qquad\mathbf{Q}_{-}=\{-\mathbf{q}_{2}+n_{1}\mathbf{b}_{M1}+n_{2}\mathbf{b}_{M2}\},

(52)

with
n_{1,2}\in\mathbb{Z}
and moiré reciprocal lattice vectors
\mathbf{b}_{M1}=\mathbf{q}_{2}-\mathbf{q}_{1}
and
\mathbf{b}_{M2}=\mathbf{q}_{3}-\mathbf{q}_{1}
. The three vectors
\mathbf{q}_{j}
connect the Dirac points of the two rotated layers.

We label the creation operators by
c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{Q},a,\tau}
, where
\mathbf{k}
is the crystal momentum in the mBZ,
\mathbf{Q}
is a reciprocal vector of the triangular moiré lattice (
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbf{Q}_{+}\oplus\mathbf{Q}_{-}
),
a
denotes the graphene sublattice, and
\tau=\pm
labels the valleys (
+
for
K
,
-
for
K^{\prime}
). The spin index is suppressed. These operators relate to monolayer tight-binding operators via

\displaystyle c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbf{Q}^{\pm},a,\tau=\pm}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\text{tot}}}}\sum_{\mathbf{R}\in\text{top}}e^{i(\tau\mathbf{K}_{T}+\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{Q})\cdot(\mathbf{R}+\mathbf{t}_{a})}c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{R},a},

(53)

\displaystyle c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbf{Q}^{\pm},a,\tau=\mp}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\text{tot}}}}\sum_{\mathbf{R}^{\prime}\in\text{bottom}}e^{i(\tau\mathbf{K}_{B}+\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{Q})\cdot(\mathbf{R}^{\prime}+\mathbf{t}^{\prime}_{a})}c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{R}^{\prime},a},

(54)

where
N_{\text{tot}}
is the number of unit cells,
\mathbf{K}_{T}
and
\mathbf{K}_{B}
are the
K
-point of the top and bottom layers,
\mathbf{R}
and
\mathbf{R}^{\prime}
run over the lattice sites in the top and bottom layers, and
\mathbf{t}_{a}
(
\mathbf{t}^{\prime}_{a}
) are the sublattice positions in the top (bottom) layer. Thus, for valley
\tau=+
the top-layer states are expanded over
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbf{Q}_{+}
(and for
\tau=-
over
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbf{Q}_{-}
), with the opposite assignment for the bottom layer.
In this basis, the BM Hamiltonian reads

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{BM}}=\sum_{\tau=\pm}\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathrm{mBZ}}\sum_{a,a^{\prime}}\sum_{\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}}h^{(\tau)}_{\mathbf{Q}a,\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}a^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k})\,c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{Q},a,\tau}\,c_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{Q}^{\prime},a^{\prime},\tau},

(55)

with

\displaystyle h^{(+)}_{\mathbf{Q}a,\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}a^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=v_{D}\,(\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{Q})\cdot\bm{\sigma}\,\delta_{\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}}+\sum_{j=1}^{3}[T_{j}]_{aa^{\prime}}\,\delta_{\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}\pm\mathbf{q}_{j}},

(56)

\displaystyle h^{(-)}_{\mathbf{Q}a,\mathbf{Q}^{\prime}a^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=\left(h^{(+)}_{-\mathbf{Q},a,-\mathbf{Q}^{\prime},a^{\prime}}(-\mathbf{k})\right)^{*}.

(57)

Here
v_{D}
is the graphene Dirac velocity,
\bm{\sigma}=(\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y})
acts on the sublattice indices, and the matrices
T_{j}
encode interlayer tunneling,

T_{j}=w_{aa}\sigma_{0}+w_{ab}\!\left(\sigma_{x}\cos\frac{2\pi}{3}(j-1)+\sigma_{y}\sin\frac{2\pi}{3}(j-1)\right),

(58)

where
w_{aa}
and
w_{ab}
are the tunneling amplitudes for AA and AB/BA stacking, respectively. The Hamiltonian is block diagonal in valley
\tau
, with the two valleys related by time-reversal symmetry. In this work, we use
v_{D}=5.944~$\mathrm{e}\mathrm{V}\AA$
,
w_{aa}=66~$\mathrm{m}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{V}$
, and
w_{ab}=110~$\mathrm{m}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{V}$
.
For convenience, we also define the first-quantized basis

\ket{\bs{k},\bs{Q},a,\tau}=c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{Q},a,\tau}\ket{0}.

(59)

Diagonalizing
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{BM}}
yields the TBG Bloch eigenstates
\ket{u_{\bs{k},\beta}}
with
\mathbf{k}\in\mathrm{mBZ}
, where
\beta
is the band index.

To compute a tunneling matrix element for a state in valley
\tau=\pm 1
with momentum
\mathbf{p}
(in the extended mBZ), we fold it to the first mBZ using
\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{k}+\tau\mathbf{K}_{T}-\mathbf{Q}
with
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbf{Q}_{\tau}
. We evaluate the tunneling matrix element in the extended zone using the wavefunction component associated with the reciprocal vector
\mathbf{Q}
. Defining the following two-component projection of the wavefunction

\ket{u^{S,\mathrm{top}}_{\bs{p},\tau,\beta}}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}\ket{\bs{k},\bs{Q},1,\tau}\innerproduct{\bs{k},\bs{Q},1,\tau}{u_{\bs{k},\beta}}\\[2.0pt]
\ket{\bs{k},\bs{Q},2,\tau}\innerproduct{\bs{k},\bs{Q},2,\tau}{u_{\bs{k},\beta}}\end{pmatrix},

(60)

we then insert
\ket{u^{S,\mathrm{top}}_{\bs{p},\tau,\beta}}
into
Equation

28
.

D.2
Review of the Topological Heavy Fermion model

We review the heavy-fermion description of magic-angle TBG, following Ref.
Song and Bernevig 2022
. In this picture the BM flat bands arise from hybridization between nearly localized orbitals centered at AA regions (“
f
” orbitals) and topological conduction bands (“
c
” bands). We introduce fermionic operators for the two electron kinds

f^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{R},\alpha,\tau,s},\qquad c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\beta,\tau,s},

(61)

where
\mathbf{R}
labels the AA moiré sites,
\alpha=1,2
indexes the two localized
f
orbitals,
\beta=1,\dots,4
labels the
c
-bands,
\tau=\pm
denotes the valley (
K/K^{\prime}
), and
s
is spin (suppressed when not needed).

The noninteracting heavy-fermion Hamiltonian is

\hat{H}_{\text{HF}}=H^{(c)}+H^{(fc)},

(62)

with

\displaystyle H^{(c)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{|\mathbf{k}|<\Lambda_{c}}\sum_{\beta,\beta^{\prime},\tau,s}H^{(c,\tau)}_{\beta\beta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k})\,c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\beta,\tau,s}c_{\mathbf{k},\beta^{\prime},\tau,s},

(63)

\displaystyle H^{(fc)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{|\mathbf{k}|<\Lambda_{c}}\sum_{\mathbf{R}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta,\tau,s}\Big(e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{R}}\,V^{(fc,\tau)}_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{k})\,f^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{R},\alpha,\tau,s}\,c_{\mathbf{k},\beta,\tau,s}+\text{h.c.}\Big),

(64)

where
N
is the number of moiré unit cells and
\Lambda_{c}
is a momentum cutoff for the conduction bands. The
f
electrons are assumed to have zero dispersion.

The
c
-electrons Hamiltonian is

H^{(c,\tau)}(\mathbf{k})=\begin{pmatrix}0&v_{\star}(\tau k_{x}\sigma_{0}+ik_{y}\sigma_{z})\\[2.0pt]
v_{\star}(\tau k_{x}\sigma_{0}-ik_{y}\sigma_{z})&M\sigma_{x}\end{pmatrix},

(65)

where
\sigma_{i}
are the Pauli matrices,
v_{\star}
is an effective velocity, and
M
is a band-splitting mass.

The
f
–
c
hybridization is modeled as

V^{(fc,\tau)}(\mathbf{k})=e^{-\frac{|\mathbf{k}|^{2}\lambda^{2}}{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\gamma\,\sigma_{0}+v^{\prime}_{\star}(\tau k_{x}\sigma_{x}+k_{y}\sigma_{y})&0_{2\times 2}\end{pmatrix},

(66)

where
\gamma
and
v^{\prime}_{\star}
parameterize the hybridization and
\lambda
is a fitted damping scale set by the localized
f
orbitals (see
Table

1
for the adopted parameters). In Ref.
Song and Bernevig 2022
, the
f
-electron wave functions are obtained as maximally localized Wannier functions, which span most of the flat bands except near the
\gamma
point. Wannierization yields matrix elements
\tilde{v}^{(\tau)}_{\mathbf{Q}a,\alpha}(\mathbf{k})
in a plane-wave basis
\ket{\bs{k},\bs{Q},a,\tau}
(defined in the previous subsection). The
c
electrons are defined as the remaining four bands after projecting onto the lowest six BM bands and removing the
f
sector. The transformations between the
f/c
basis and the plane-wave basis are given by

f^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}\alpha\tau}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{\mathbf{R}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{R}}f^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{R}\alpha\tau}=\sum_{\mathbf{Q},a}\tilde{v}^{(\tau)}_{\mathbf{Q}a,\alpha}(\mathbf{k})\,c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{Q},a,\tau},

(67)

and

c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}\beta\tau}=\sum_{\mathbf{Q},a}\tilde{u}^{(\tau)}_{\mathbf{Q}a,\beta}(\mathbf{k})\,c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{Q},a,\tau}.

(68)

Note that the
c
-electrons creation operator,
c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}\beta\tau}
, is not periodic in the reciprocal space, whereas
f^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}\alpha\tau}
is periodic because it is defined on the lattice. To compute tunneling matrix elements, we use the analytical approximations and fitted parameters of Ref.
Călugăru et al. 2023
. For the
f

Appendix E
Superconductor spectral function with lifetime broadening

The Green’s function of a superconductor can be written compactly using the Nambu–Gor’kov formalism
Mineev et al. 1999
:

\hat{G}_{0}(\mathbf{k},\omega_{n})=-\frac{i\omega_{n}+\xi_{\mathbf{k}}\,\tau_{3}+\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}\tau_{1}}{\omega_{n}^{2}+\xi_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}+\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}},

(81)

where
\tau_{i}
are Pauli matrices in Nambu space, and we assume intra-band pairing. The retarded and advanced Green’s functions are given by

\hat{G}^{R(A)}(\mathbf{k},\omega)=\hat{G}(\mathbf{k},\omega)|_{i\omega_{n}\!\to\!\omega\pm i0}.

(82)

This yields

\hat{G}^{R}(\mathbf{k},\omega)=\frac{\omega\tau_{0}+\xi_{\mathbf{k}}\tau_{3}+\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}\,\tau_{1}}{\omega^{2}-\xi_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}-\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}}.

(83)

Several phenomenological approaches have been developed to describe lifetime broadening in tunneling experiments on superconductors
Dynes et al. 1978
;
Herman and Hlubina 2016
;
Kondo et al. 2015
;
Norman et al. 1998
. Here we adopt the model of Ref.
Herman and Hlubina 2016
, which incorporates pair-breaking and pair-conserving scattering processes with rates
\Gamma
and
\Gamma_{s}
, respectively. The retarded Green’s function then reads

\hat{G}^{R}(\mathbf{k},\omega)=\frac{\left(1+i\Gamma_{s}/\Omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\omega)\right)\left[(\omega+i\Gamma)\tau_{0}+\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}\tau_{1}\right]+\xi_{\mathbf{k}}\tau_{3}}{(\Omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\omega)+i\Gamma_{s})^{2}-\xi_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}},

(84)

where

\Omega_{\mathbf{k}}(\omega)=\sqrt{(\omega+i\Gamma)^{2}-\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}}.

(85)

The normal component of the spectral function is

A(\mathbf{k},\omega)=-\frac{1}{\pi}\,\imaginary\,G^{R}_{11}(\mathbf{k},\omega).

(86)

For simplicity, we neglect the pair-conserving rate
\Gamma_{s}
. The Green’s function then reduces to

\hat{G}^{R}(\mathbf{k},\omega)=\frac{(\omega+i\Gamma)\tau_{0}+\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}\tau_{1}+\xi_{\mathbf{k}}\tau_{3}}{(\omega+i\Gamma)^{2}-\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}-\xi_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}}.

(87)

The resulting spectral function is

A(\mathbf{k},\omega)=-\frac{1}{\pi}\,\imaginary\,G^{R}_{11}(\mathbf{k},\omega)=\frac{1}{\pi}\left[|u_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}\,\frac{\Gamma}{(\omega-E_{\mathbf{k}})^{2}+\Gamma^{2}}+|v_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}\,\frac{\Gamma}{(\omega+E_{\mathbf{k}})^{2}+\Gamma^{2}}\right],

(88)

with
E_{\mathbf{k}}=\sqrt{\xi_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}+\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}}
and the coherence factors defined in
Equation

6
. Thus, a finite pair-breaking rate replaces the delta-function singularities by Lorentzians centered at the quasiparticle poles.

To recover the familiar Dynes formula for the density of states
Dynes et al. 1978
, we assume isotropic pairing. The density of states is

N(\omega)=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}-\frac{1}{\pi}\imaginary G_{11}^{R}(\mathbf{k},\omega)=-\frac{N_{0}}{\pi}\imaginary\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}d\xi_{\mathbf{k}}\frac{(\omega+i\Gamma)+\xi_{\mathbf{k}}}{(\omega+i\Gamma)^{2}-\Delta^{2}-\xi_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}},

(89)

where
N_{0}
is the normal-state density of states. The linear term in
\xi_{\mathbf{k}}
vanishes after integration, leaving only the first term, which can be evaluated by contour integration. The result is the Dynes formula:

N(\omega)=N_{0}\real\left[\frac{\omega+i\Gamma}{\sqrt{(\omega+i\Gamma)^{2}-\Delta^{2}}}\right].

(90)

Appendix F
Numerical details

We use
Equation

28
in our numerical calculations. The two-dimensional momentum space is discretized to perform the summation. In the calculations, singularities in the current derivatives arise from intersections of the tip and sample bands or the Fermi surfaces with the bands. The sample bands and wavefunctions vary smoothly in
\mathbf{k}
.
Accordingly, we define a relatively coarse grid on which the eigenstates and tunneling amplitudes are computed. Linear interpolations are then constructed from this coarse grid. Finally, the current in
Equation

28
is evaluated on a finer momentum grid as a function of
V_{b}
, using interpolation together with the sample spectral function. In all calculations, convergence is ensured by increasing the resolution of both grids.

Appendix G
Detecting nodal point using the tip Fermi Surface

In this section, we show how additional information about the pairing magnitude can be extracted across the entire momentum space. This is achieved by exploiting the tunability of the tip chemical potential
\mu_{T}
, which allows for the controlled expansion of its Fermi circle. We focus on measurements at
V_{b}\approx 0
, where the tip Fermi level lies near the center of the superconducting gap at
\mu_{S}
(see
Figure

11
). Under this condition,
\phi
is determined by
\mu_{T}
and
\mu_{S}
. At low temperatures, fixing
V_{b}\approx 0
ensures that tunneling occurs only between states on the tip Fermi circle and states near the middle of the superconducting gap (up to spectral-function and temperature broadening). Consequently,
\left.\frac{dI}{dV_{b}}\right|_{V_{b}=0}
can be examined as a function of the Fermi-circle radius, controlled by
\mu_{T}
.

The first important distinction is between a fully gapped superconductor and one that contains nodes.
A fully gapped superconductor has no excitations at the center of the gap, so the current at small bias is suppressed and originates only from the finite broadening of the spectral function and from states near the gap edge.
In contrast, the presence of a nodal point within the gap allows a direct contribution to the current.
Consequently, when the tip Fermi surface intersects a nodal point, a pronounced increase in the differential conductance is expected.
As
\mu_{T}
increases further and the intersection no longer exists, the conductance gradually decreases; thus, a peak in
\frac{dI}{dV_{b}}
as a function of
\mu_{T}
is expected.
