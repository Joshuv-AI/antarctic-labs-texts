Semiregular and strongly irregular boundary points for nonlocal Dirichlet problems
Anders Björn

Abstract




Abstract
.
In this paper we study nonlocal nonlinear equations of
fractional
(s,p)
-Laplacian type on
\mathbf{R}^{n}
.
We show that the irregular boundary points for
the Dirichlet problem can be divided into two
disjoint classes: semiregular and strongly irregular boundary points,
with very different behaviour.
Two fundamental tools needed to show this are the Kellogg property (from
our previous paper) and a new removability result for
solutions in the
V^{s,p}
Sobolev type space,
which we deduce more generally also for
supersolutions of
equations with a right-hand side.
Semiregular and strongly irregular points are also characterized in various ways.
Finally, it is explained how semiregularity depends on
s
and
p
.

Key words and phrases
:
boundary regularity,
capacity,
fractional
p\mspace{1mu}
-Laplacian,
irregular boundary point,
nonlocal nonlinear Dirichlet problem,
Perron solution,
semiregular,
Sobolev solution,
strongly irregular.

(2020):

1
Introduction

The aim of this paper is to study
irregular boundary points
for the nonlocal nonlinear equation
\mathcal{L}u=0
.
The operator
\mathcal{L}
is of the form

\mathcal{L}u(x)=2\,\mathrm{p.v.}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}|u(x)-u(y)|^{p-2}(u(x)-u(y))k(x,y)\,dy,

where
1<p<\infty
,
n\geq 1
and
k:\mathbf{R}^{n}\times\mathbf{R}^{n}\to[0,\infty]
is a symmetric measurable kernel
that satisfies the ellipticity condition

\frac{\Lambda^{-1}}{|x-y|^{n+sp}}\leq k(x,y)\leq\frac{\Lambda}{|x-y|^{n+sp}},

(1.1)

with
0<s<1
and
\Lambda\geq 1
.
Note that
\mathcal{L}
is the fractional
p\mspace{1mu}
-Laplacian
(-\Delta_{p})^{s}
when

k(x,y)=|x-y|^{-n-sp}
.

Nonlocal and fractional problems have been studied for a long time,
see e.g.
[
1
,
7
]
, but in recent years, starting perhaps
with the pioneering paper by Caffarelli–Silvestre
[
10
]
,
they have received
a lot more attention (see e.g. our list of references).
Fractional operators also have significant applications in various contexts,
such as the obstacle problem
[
9
,
36
]
,
material science
[
3
,
4
]
,
phase transitions
[
8
,
14
,
37
]
,
soft thin films
[
27
]
, quasi-geostrophic dynamics
[
11
]

and image processing
[
16
]
.

We assume in the rest of the introduction,
except for Theorem
1.4
,
that
\Omega\subset{\mathbf{R}^{n}}
is a nonempty bounded open set.
In the
Dirichlet problem
one seeks
a solution of
\mathcal{L}u=0

in
\Omega
which takes on some
prescribed exterior Dirichlet data

g:{\Omega^{c}}\to{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}:=[-\infty,\infty]
.
Due to the nonlocal nature of the problem the Dirichlet data
have to be prescribed on the whole complement
{\Omega^{c}}
of
\Omega
.
For this reason, the Dirichlet problem is
also called the exterior value problem or the complement value problem,
but we will call it the Dirichlet problem for short.

As in the classical setting of harmonic functions one can in general
not solve the Dirichlet problem so that the prescribed boundary values
are taken as limits on
\partial\Omega
,
even if
g
is bounded and continuous.
Hence, the Dirichlet problem has to be understood in a relaxed sense.
In this paper we
use Sobolev and Perron solutions of the Dirichlet problem.
We
consider Sobolev solutions
Hg

for Sobolev data
g
in the fractional Sobolev type space
V^{s,p}(\Omega)

naturally associated with the operator
\mathcal{L}
,
see Theorem
5.1
for the precise definition.
For the nonlinear nonlocal problems
\mathcal{L}u=0
as above,
the Dirichlet problem was solved in the Sobolev sense
by Di Castro–Kuusi–Palatucci
[
12
]
.

A boundary point
x_{0}\in\partial\Omega
is
regular

if

\lim_{\Omega\ni x\to x_{0}}Hg(x)=g(x_{0})\quad\text{for every }g\in V^{s,p}(\Omega)\cap C(\mathbf{R}^{n}).

Kim–Lee–Lee
[
22
]
obtained the Wiener criterion for regular
boundary points.
Several
other characterizations of regular boundary points
were given in our earlier paper
[
6
]
,
see also Lindgren–Lindqvist
[
28
]
.

Boundary regularity can be paraphrased in the following way:

x_{0}
is
regular if the following two conditions hold:

(I)

For every
g\in V^{s,p}(\Omega)\cap C(\mathbf{R}^{n})
, the limit

\lim_{\Omega\ni x\to x_{0}}Hg(x)
exists.

(II)

For every
g\in V^{s,p}(\Omega)\cap C(\mathbf{R}^{n})
, there is a sequence

\{y_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
such that

\Omega\ni y_{j}\to x_{0}\quad\text{and}\quad Hg(y_{j})\to g(x_{0}),\qquad\text{as }j\to\infty.

(1.2)

An irregular (i.e. nonregular) boundary point
x_{0}\in\partial\Omega

thus fails at least one of the two conditions
(I)
and
(II)
.
We say that an irregular point is
semiregular
if

(I)
holds and that it is

strongly irregular
if
(II)
holds.
Perhaps surprisingly it can never happen that both

(I)
and
(II)
fail.
This fact is part of the following main theorem in this paper.
As far as we know, our results are new also for the classical linear case
p=2
.

Theorem 1.1
.

(Trichotomy)

Let
x_{0}\in\partial\Omega
.
Then
x_{0}
is either regular, semiregular or strongly irregular.

As a direct consequence of the proof we obtain the following
result.
We refer the reader to Section
3

for the definition of the Sobolev capacity
{C_{s,p}}
.

Proposition 1.2
.

The set
S
of semiregular points is the largest relatively open subset
of
\partial\Omega\setminus\partial\overline{\Omega}
with zero
{C_{s,p}}
capacity.
It is also the largest subset of
{\Omega^{c}}
with zero
{C_{s,p}}
capacity
such that
\Omega\cup S
is open.
Moreover,

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\{x\in\partial\Omega:\text{there is $r>0$ such that ${C_{s,p}}(B(x,r)\setminus\Omega)=0$}\}

(1.3)

\displaystyle=\{x\in\partial\Omega\setminus\partial\overline{\Omega}:\text{there is $r>0$ such that ${C_{s,p}}(B(x,r)\cap\partial\Omega)=0$}\}.

A direct consequence is that semiregularity is a local property of
\Omega
,
and since regularity is a local property (by the Wiener criterion), so
is also strong irregularity.

It also follows that
S
is removable in the sense
of Theorems
1.4
and
4.2
,
with
E
and
\Omega\setminus E
therein replaced by
S
and
\Omega
.
It follows from Remark
4.1
that
S
is the
largest such removable set, at least when the right-hand side
f
is
0

in Theorem
1.4
.

If
sp>n
, then all boundary points are regular (see Corollary
5.10
).
On the contrary, when
sp\leq n
, the punctured ball (together with
Proposition
1.2
) shows that there are semiregular boundary points.
Example
5.11

exhibits
strongly irregular boundary points when
sp\leq n
.

In the definition
(II)
of strongly irregular points,
the sequence
\{y_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
is allowed to depend on
g
.
However, it is perhaps a bit surprising
that there is a universal sequence
\{y_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}

such that (
1.2
) holds
for all
bounded

g\in V^{s,p}(\Omega)\cap C(\mathbf{R}^{n})
,
see Theorem
7.2
.
When
p=2
, this is true also for

unbounded

g\in V^{s,2}(\Omega)\cap C(\mathbf{R}^{n})
,
see Theorem
7.3
.
(In the nonlinear case we do not know
if this holds for unbounded
g
.)

Other characterizations
of strongly irregular and semiregular boundary points
are proved in Sections
6
and
7
.
In particular, we show that (semi)regularity
and strong irregularity
can equivalently be defined using Perron solutions and
can conveniently be tested using only
one function in the following way.
Theorem
1.3
follows from
Theorems
5.7
,
6.1
and
7.1
.
Part
1
is from
our earlier paper
[
6
, Theorem 1.7]
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
x_{0}\in\partial\Omega
and
d_{x_{0}}=\min\{1,|x-x_{0}|\}
.
Then

1.

x_{0}
is regular if and only if

\lim_{\Omega\ni x\to x_{0}}Hd_{x_{0}}(x)=0.

2.

x_{0}
is strongly irregular if and only if
the limit

\lim_{\Omega\ni x\to x_{0}}Hd_{x_{0}}(x)\quad\text{does not exist}.

3.

x_{0}
is semiregular if and only if

\liminf_{\Omega\ni x\to x_{0}}Hd_{x_{0}}(x)>0.

Moreover, the set of strongly irregular points is exactly the set
{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\displaystyle R}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{R}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\textstyle R}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{R}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptstyle R}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{R}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptscriptstyle R}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{R}}}\setminus R
,
where
R
is the set of all regular boundary points.

There are two main ingredients needed to prove Theorem
1.1
:
The Kellogg property (Theorem
5.9
)
from our earlier paper
[
6
]
,
which says that the set
of irregular boundary points has
{C_{s,p}}
-capacity zero,
and the following new removability result.

Theorem 1.4
.

Assume that
\Omega
is a nonempty open set, which may be unbounded.
Let
E\subset\Omega
be a relatively closed set with
{C_{s,p}}(E)=0
.
Assume that
u\in V^{s,p}(\Omega\setminus E)
is a
solution
(
resp. supersolution
)

of
\mathcal{L}u=f
in
\Omega\setminus E
with
f\in V^{s,p}_{0}(\Omega)^{*}
.
Then
u
is a

(
super
)
solution
of
\mathcal{L}u=f
in
\Omega
.

In order to prove Theorem
1.1

it is enough to have the removability result for solutions
of
\mathcal{L}u=0
.
We deduce it in this more general form for supersolutions and with
right-hand side since this may be of independent interest
and the proof is essentially the same.
In Remark
4.1
we show that this result is sharp
at least when
\Omega
is bounded and the right-hand side is
f\equiv 0
.

For linear local equations
in an abstract setting, the trichotomy was developed
in detail by Lukeš–Malý
[
29
]
.
For the local nonlinear
p\mspace{1mu}
-Laplace equation
\Delta_{p}u=0
,
the trichotomy as
in Theorem
1.1
was proved by Björn
[
5
]
.
Removability results as in Theorem
1.4

for such equations are a classical topic and
can be found already in
Serrin’s pioneering paper
[
34
, Chapter II]
from 1964.
See also Heinonen–Kilpeläinen–Martio
[
19
, Theorems 7.35 and 7.36]

for removability results for weighted degenerate and singular nonlinear equations.

Nonlocal equations and Sobolev spaces

Throughout the paper, we assume that
1<p<\infty
,
that
\Omega\subset{\mathbf{R}^{n}}

is a nonempty open set,
n\geq 1
, and that
k

is a symmetric measurable kernel satisfying (
1.1
).
In Sections
3

and
5
–
8
,

\Omega
will also be assumed to be bounded.
Except for Section
8
, we
assume that

0<s<1
throughout the paper.

In order to consider weak solutions of the equation

\mathcal{L}u=0\quad\text{in}~\Omega,

(2.1)

we need
suitable function spaces.

For a measurable function
u:\Omega\to{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}:=[-\infty,\infty]
(which is
finite a.e.) we consider
the fractional seminorm

[u]_{W^{s,p}(\Omega)}=\biggl(\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{p}}{|x-y|^{n+sp}}\,dy\,dx\biggr)^{1/p}.

The fractional Sobolev space
W^{s,p}(\Omega)
consists of the functions
u
such that
the norm

\|u\|_{W^{s,p}(\Omega)}^{p}:=\|u\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p}+[u]_{W^{s,p}(\Omega)}^{p}<\infty.

The above spaces and (semi)norms go under various names,
such as fractional Sobolev,
Gagliardo–Nirenberg, Sobolev–Slobodetskiĭ and Besov.

By
W^{s,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)
we denote the space of functions that belong to
W^{s,p}(G)

for every open
G\Subset\Omega
.
As usual, by
E\Subset\Omega
we mean that
{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\displaystyle E}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{E}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\textstyle E}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{E}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptstyle E}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{E}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptscriptstyle E}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{E}}}

is a compact subset of
\Omega
.
We refer the reader to Di Nezza–Palatucci–Valdinoci
[
13
]
for properties of these spaces.

As (
2.1
) is a nonlocal equation, we
need a function space that captures integrability of
functions in the whole of
{\mathbf{R}^{n}}
.
The
tail space

L^{p-1}_{sp}(\mathbf{R}^{n})
is given by

L^{p-1}_{sp}(\mathbf{R}^{n})=\biggl\{u\text{ measurable}:\int_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}\frac{|u(y)|^{p-1}}{(1+|y|)^{n+sp}}\,dy<\infty\biggr\},

see Kassmann
[
20
]
(for
p=2
) and Di Castro–Kuusi–Palatucci
[
12
]
.
For measurable functions
u,v:\mathbf{R}^{n}\to{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}

we define the quantity

\mathcal{E}(u,v)=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}|u(x)-u(y)|^{p-2}(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y))k(x,y)\,dy\,dx,

provided that it is finite. Note that
\mathcal{E}(u,v)
is well defined for

u\in W^{s,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)\cap L^{p-1}_{sp}(\mathbf{R}^{n})
and
v\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)

and that it is linear in the second argument.
Here and later,
C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
denotes
the space of
C^{\infty}
functions with compact support in
\Omega
.
Similarly,
{\Lip_{c}}(\Omega)
denotes the space of Lipschitz
functions with compact support in
\Omega
.
The
support
of a function
u
is

\supp u=\overline{\{x:u(x)\neq 0\}}
.
As usual, we let
u_{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}}=\max\{u,0\}
and
u_{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}}}=\max\{-u,0\}
.

We use the following definition of solutions and supersolutions of (
2.1
),
which is standard by now.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
u\in W^{s,p}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)\cap L^{p-1}_{sp}(\mathbf{R}^{n})

and let
f
be a measurable function in
\Omega
.
Then

is a (weak)
solution
(resp.

)
of
\mathcal{L}u=f

\Omega

\langle f,\varphi\rangle:=\int_{\Omega}f(x)\varphi(x)\,dx

\mathcal{E}(u,\varphi)\geq\langle f,\varphi\rangle

(2.2)

for all
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)

(resp. for all nonpositive/nonnegative

If
u\in C(\Omega)
is a
solution of
\mathcal{L}u=0
in
\Omega

\mathcal{L}
-harmonic

\Omega
.

Here we have included equations
\mathcal{L}u=f
with right-hand sides

.

If

is a solution
of

For this purpose, we will use the spaces

\displaystyle\|u\|_{V^{s,p}(\Omega)}

)

.

Lemma 2.2

Let

\|u\|_{W^{s,p}(\mathbf{R}^{n})}^{p}\lesssim(1+(\diam\Omega)^{sp})[u]_{W^{s,p}(\mathbf{R}^{n})}^{p}.

,
and
a\simeq b
if
a\lesssim b\lesssim a
.

3
Capacities

In the rest of the paper, except for Section
4
,
we assume that
\Omega
is a bounded nonempty open set.

There are two
types of capacities used in this paper:
the condenser capacity
{\capp_{s,p}}
, which appears in the Wiener criterion,
and the Sobolev capacity
{C_{s,p}}
, which is convenient when dealing
with function spaces and in the Kellogg property, since it involves only
one set, unlike
{\capp_{s,p}}
.
In contrast to Kim–Lee–Lee
[
22
]

we will need capacities also for noncompact sets.
We therefore make the following definitions.

Definition 3.1
.

The
condenser capacity
of a compact set
K\Subset\Omega
is given by

{\capp_{s,p}}(K,\Omega)=\inf_{u}{[u]_{W^{s,p}(\mathbf{R}^{n})}^{p}},

where the infimum is taken over all
u\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)

such that
u\geq 1
on
K
.

Definition 3.2
.

The
Sobolev capacity
of a compact set
K\Subset{\mathbf{R}^{n}}
is defined by

{C_{s,p}}(K)=\inf_{u}{\|u\|_{W^{s,p}({\mathbf{R}^{n}})}^{p}},

(3.1)

where the infimum is taken over all
u\in C_{c}^{\infty}({\mathbf{R}^{n}})
such that

u\geq 1
on
K
.

Both capacities are then extended first to open and then to arbitrary sets
in the usual way as follows:
For open sets
G
,

\displaystyle{\capp_{s,p}}(G,\Omega)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}K\text{ compact}\\
K\Subset G\end{subarray}}{\capp_{s,p}}(K,\Omega),

\displaystyle\text{if $G\subset\Omega$},

(3.2)

\displaystyle{C_{s,p}}(G)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}K\text{ compact}\\
K\Subset G\end{subarray}}{C_{s,p}}(K),

\displaystyle\text{if $G\subset{\mathbf{R}^{n}}$}.

Similarly, for arbitrary sets
E
,

\displaystyle{\capp_{s,p}}(E,\Omega)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}G\text{ open}\\
E\subset G\subset\Omega\end{subarray}}{\capp_{s,p}}(G,\Omega),

\displaystyle\text{if $E\subset\Omega$},

(3.3)

\displaystyle{C_{s,p}}(E)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}G\text{ open}\\
E\subset G\end{subarray}}{C_{s,p}}(G),

\displaystyle\text{if $E\subset{\mathbf{R}^{n}}$}.

It is worth noticing that (
3.2
) and (
3.3
) do not change the
definition of capacity for compact sets,
see
[
6
, Section 5]
.

We will need some results
from
[
6
, Section 5]
, see therein for a more
detailed discussion.
The following result
shows that
the two capacities
{\capp_{s,p}}
and
{C_{s,p}}

have the same zero sets.

Lemma 3.3
.

(
[
6
, Proposition 5.4]
)

Let
E\Subset\Omega
.
Then

{C_{s,p}}(E)=0
if and only if
{\capp_{s,p}}(E,\Omega)=0
.

Lemma 3.4
.

(
[
6
, Lemma 5.5]
)

Let
x_{0}\in\Omega
.
Then the following are equivalent
:

1.

sp\leq n
,

2.

{C_{s,p}}(\{x_{0}\})=0
,

3.

{\capp_{s,p}}(\{x_{0}\},\Omega)=0
.

Lemma 3.5
.

(
[
6
, Lemma 5.6]
)

If
E\subset\mathbf{R}^{n}
is measurable, then

{C_{s,p}}(E)\geq|E|
, where
|\cdot|
denotes Lebesgue measure.

The following result is
convenient when calculating capacities.

Proposition 3.6
.

(
[
6
, Lemma 5.7]
)

Let
E\subset{\mathbf{R}^{n}}
.
Then

{C_{s,p}}(E)=\inf_{u}{\|u\|^{p}_{W^{s,p}({\mathbf{R}^{n}})}},

where the infimum is taken over all
u\in W^{s,p}({\mathbf{R}^{n}})
such that

0\leq u\leq 1
everywhere and
u=1

in an open set containing
E
.

A simple consequence of Proposition
3.6
is that

{C_{s,p}}
is countably subadditive.
This does not seem to be so easy to prove
directly from the three-step definition of the Sobolev capacity.
We include a short proof for the reader’s convenience.

Proposition 3.7
.

If
E=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}E_{i}\subset\mathbf{R}^{n}
, then

{C_{s,p}}(E)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{C_{s,p}}(E_{i}).

(3.4)

If also
E\subset\Omega
, then

{\capp_{s,p}}(E,\Omega)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\capp_{s,p}}(E_{i},\Omega).

(3.5)

We first consider (
3.4
).
We may assume that the right-hand side is finite.
Let
\varepsilon>0
.
By
Proposition
3.6
, there is
for each
i=1,2,\dots
,
a function
u_{i}\in W^{s,p}({\mathbf{R}^{n}})
such that

u_{i}=1
in a neighbourhood of
E_{i}
,
0\leq u\leq 1
everywhere
and

\|u_{i}\|_{W^{s,p}(\mathbf{R}^{n})}^{p}<{C_{s,p}}(E_{i})+\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{i}}.

Let
u=\sup_{i}u_{i}
.
Then
u=1
in a neighbourhood of
E
.
Moreover, for
x,y\in{\mathbf{R}^{n}}
,

|u(x)-u(y)|^{p}\leq\sup_{i}|u_{i}(x)-u_{i}(y)|^{p}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|u_{i}(x)-u_{i}(y)|^{p}

and similarly,

|u(x)|^{p}=\sup_{i}|u_{i}(x)|^{p}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}|u_{i}(x)|^{p}
.
Hence, by Proposition
3.6
again,

\displaystyle{C_{s,p}}(E)

\displaystyle\leq\|u\|_{W^{s,p}(\mathbf{R}^{n})}^{p}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\|u_{i}\|_{W^{s,p}(\mathbf{R}^{n})}^{p}

\displaystyle<\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\Bigl({C_{s,p}}(E_{i})+\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{i}}\Bigr)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{C_{s,p}}(E_{i})+\varepsilon.

Letting
\varepsilon\to 0
completes the proof of (
3.4
).

The proof of (
3.5
)
is similar, but
using
[
6
, Proposition 6.2]
instead
of Proposition
3.6
.
∎

4
Removable singularities

In contrast to most of the paper, in this section
we assume that
\Omega
is a nonempty open set that may be unbounded.

We first consider the case for supersolutions.
Let
0\leq\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
be a test function.
Since
{C_{s,p}}(E)=0
and
E\cap\supp\varphi
is compact,
for each
j=1,2,\dots
, there are open sets
G_{j}
and
G_{j}^{\prime}
such that

E\cap\supp\varphi\Subset G_{j}\Subset G_{j}^{\prime}\quad\text{and}\quad{C_{s,p}}(G_{j}^{\prime})<1/j.

Hence
{C_{s,p}}({\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\displaystyle G}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{G}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\textstyle G}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{G}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptstyle G}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{G}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptscriptstyle G}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{G}}}_{j})<1/j
, and thus by (
3.1
),
there are

\eta_{j}^{\prime}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{n})
such that
\eta_{j}^{\prime}\geq 1
on
{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\displaystyle G}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{G}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\textstyle G}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{G}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptstyle G}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{G}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptscriptstyle G}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{G}}}_{j}

and
\|\eta_{j}^{\prime}\|_{W^{s,p}(\mathbf{R}^{n})}<1/j
.
Thus there is a mollification
\eta_{j}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{n})

of
\min\{\eta_{j}^{\prime},1\}_{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}}
such that

\eta_{j}=1
on

E\cap\supp\varphi
,
0\leq\eta_{j}\leq 1
in
{\mathbf{R}^{n}}

and

\|\eta_{j}\|_{W^{s,p}(\mathbf{R}^{n})}<1/j
.
After taking a subsequence if necessary,
we can assume that also
\eta_{j}\to 0
a.e. as
j\to\infty
.
Then
\varphi_{j}:=(1-\eta_{j})\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega\setminus E)
and hence

\int_{{\mathbf{R}^{n}}}\int_{{\mathbf{R}^{n}}}|u(x)-u(y)|^{p-2}(u(x)-u(y))(\varphi_{j}(x)-\varphi_{j}(y))k(x,y)\,dy\,dx\geq\langle f,\varphi_{j}\rangle.

(4.1)

Note that
\varphi-\varphi_{j}=\varphi\eta_{j}
and

|(\varphi\eta_{j})(x)-(\varphi\eta_{j})(y)|\leq|(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y))\eta_{j}(x)|+|\varphi(y)(\eta_{j}(x)-\eta_{j}(y))|.

Since
\varphi
is bounded, we therefore get that
\varphi_{j}\to\varphi
in
L^{p}(\mathbf{R}^{n})
and

[\varphi\eta_{j}]_{W^{s,p}(\mathbf{R}^{n})}^{p}\leq 2^{p}\biggl(\int_{{\mathbf{R}^{n}}}\eta_{j}(x)^{p}\int_{{\mathbf{R}^{n}}}\frac{|\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)|^{p}}{|x-y|^{n+sp}}\,dy\,dx+[\eta_{j}]_{W^{s,p}(\mathbf{R}^{n})}^{p}\max_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}|\varphi|^{p}\biggr).

(4.2)

The last term clearly tends to 0 as
j\to\infty
by the choice of
\eta_{j}
.
Since

\eta_{j}\to 0
a.e. and

\int_{{\mathbf{R}^{n}}}\frac{|\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)|^{p}}{|x-y|^{n+sp}}\,dy\in L^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{n}),

dominated convergence implies that also the integral term in (
4.2
)
tends to 0 as
j\to\infty
.
Hence

[\varphi-\varphi_{j}]_{W^{s,p}(\mathbf{R}^{n})}^{p}=[\varphi\eta_{j}]_{W^{s,p}(\mathbf{R}^{n})}^{p}\to 0,\quad\text{as }j\to\infty.

(4.3)

Since
f\in V^{s,p}_{0}(\Omega)^{*}
and
\varphi-\varphi_{j}\in V^{s,p}_{0}(\Omega)
,
it follows that the right-hand side
in (
4.1
) converges to

\langle f,\varphi\rangle.

As for the left-hand side in (
4.1
), an application of
Hölder’s inequality with respect to
dy\,dx
, together with the fact that
\varphi=0
outside

\Omega
, shows that

\displaystyle\biggl|\int_{{\mathbf{R}^{n}}}\int_{{\mathbf{R}^{n}}}|u(x)-u(y)|^{p-2}(u(x)-u(y))((\varphi\eta_{j})(x)-(\varphi\eta_{j})(y))k(x,y)\,dy\,dx\biggr|

\displaystyle\quad\leq 2\int_{\Omega}\int_{{\mathbf{R}^{n}}}|u(x)-u(y)|^{p-1}|(\varphi\eta_{j})(x)-(\varphi\eta_{j})(y)|k(x,y)\,dy\,dx

\displaystyle\quad\leq 2\Lambda[u]_{V^{s,p}(\Omega)}^{p-1}[\varphi\eta_{j}]_{W^{s,p}(\mathbf{R}^{n})}\to 0,\quad\text{as }j\to\infty,

by (
4.3
) and the fact that
u\in V^{s,p}(\Omega\setminus E)=V^{s,p}(\Omega)
(since
|E|=0
, by
[
6
, Lemma 5.6]
).
Hence

\mathcal{E}(u,\varphi)=\lim_{j\to\infty}\mathcal{E}(u,\varphi_{j})\geq\lim_{j\to\infty}\langle f,\varphi_{j}\rangle=\langle f,\varphi\rangle.

Finally, assume that
u
is a solution of
\mathcal{L}u=f
in
\Omega\setminus E
.
Then it is both a supersolution and a subsolution in
\Omega\setminus E
, and thus
in
\Omega
by the above.
Hence, it is a solution of
\mathcal{L}u=f
in
\Omega
.
∎

Remark 4.1
.

Theorem
1.4
is sharp, at least if
\Omega
is bounded and
f\equiv 0
.
Indeed, assume that
\Omega
is bounded and let

E\subset\Omega
be a relatively closed set with
{C_{s,p}}(E)>0
.
Then
E=\bigcup_{j=1}^{\infty}K_{j}
, where
K_{j}=\{x\in E:\dist(x,{\Omega^{c}})\geq 1/j\}
.
It thus follows from
the countable subadditivity of
{C_{s,p}}

(see Proposition
3.7
)
that
there is
j
such that the compact subset
K=K_{j}\Subset E
has
{C_{s,p}}(K)>0
.
We consider the capacitary
\mathcal{L}
-potential

u
of
K
in
\Omega
,
i.e.
u=H_{\Omega\setminus K}\psi

(in the notation of Theorem
5.1
below),
where
\psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
is such that
\psi=1
on
K
.
In particular,
u
is a bounded solution of
\mathcal{L}u=0
in
\Omega\setminus E

and a supersolution of
\mathcal{L}u=0
in
\Omega
.

Suppose that
u
is a solution of
\mathcal{L}u=0
in
\Omega
.
We may assume that
u
is
\mathcal{L}
-harmonic in
\Omega
.
Lemma
2.2
shows that
u-\psi\in V^{s,p}_{0}(\Omega)
.
Then
u=H_{\Omega}\psi
.
It now follows from the
comparison principle (Theorem

)
that
u\equiv 0
in
{\mathbf{R}^{n}}
. However,
[

, Lemma 2.16(iii)]
shows that

{\capp_{s,p}}(K,\Omega)\simeq\mathcal{E}(u,u)=0
, which contradicts the assumption

by Lemma

.
Hence
u
cannot be
a solution of
\mathcal{L}u=0
in
\Omega
,
which shows the sharpness for solutions.

Let now
v=-u
which is also a solution of

\mathcal{L}v=0
in
\Omega\setminus E
.
Since
u
is a supersolution, but not a solution, it cannot
be a subsolution of
\mathcal{L}u=0
in
\Omega
.
Hence
v=-u
is a solution of
\mathcal{L}v=0
in
\Omega\setminus E

but not a supersolution of
\mathcal{L}v=0
in
\Omega
.

This also shows that Theorem

below
is sharp, at least when
\Omega
is bounded.

To prove the trichotomy for Perron solutions we need
the following removability result for bounded
\mathcal{L}
-harmonic functions.
It is a special case of the main result in
Kim–Lee
[
25
]
.
Unlike in Theorem

, here

is not assumed to
belong to
V^{s,p}(\Omega\setminus E)
, it only needs to be bounded.

Theorem 4.2
.

(
[
25
, Theorem 1.1]
)

Assume that
\Omega
is a nonempty open set, which may be unbounded.
Let
E\subset\Omega
be a relatively closed set with
{C_{s,p}}(E)=0
.
Assume that
u
is a bounded

\mathcal{L}
-harmonic function
in
\Omega\setminus E
.
Then
u
is a solution
of
\mathcal{L}u=0
in
\Omega
,
and there is an
\mathcal{L}
-harmonic function

in
\Omega

such that

in
{\mathbf{R}^{n}}\setminus E
and

a.e. in
{\mathbf{R}^{n}}
.

5
The Dirichlet problem and regular boundary points

In the rest of the paper,
we assume that
\Omega
is a bounded nonempty open set.

For functions
g\in V^{s,p}(\Omega)
one can solve
the Dirichlet problem in the Sobolev sense.
To make it precise we define the Sobolev solution
Hg
as follows.
Its existence and uniqueness follow from
Korvenpää–Kuusi–Palatucci
[
26
, Theorem 9]
and
Kim–Lee
[
24
, Theorem 4.9]
, cf.
[
6
, Theorem 3.3]
.
Here and in the rest of the paper,
we consider
the equation
\mathcal{L}u=0
with zero right-hand side.

Theorem 5.1
.

(
[
24
, Theorem 4.9]
and
[
26
, Theorem 9]
)

Let
g\in V^{s,p}(\Omega)
.
Then there is a unique function
Hg:=H_{\Omega}g:\mathbf{R}^{n}\to{\overline{\mathbf{R}}}
with the following properties
:

1.

Hg
is
\mathcal{L}
-harmonic in
\Omega
, i.e. a continuous solution of
\mathcal{L}u=0
,

2.

Hg-g\in V^{s,p}_{0}(\Omega)
, and in particular

Hg\equiv g
outside
\Omega
.

Theorem 5.2
.

(Comparison principle for
H
-solutions,
[
6
, Corollary 3.6]
)

Let
g_{1},g_{2}\in V^{s,p}(\Omega)
be such that

(g_{1}-g_{2})_{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}}\in V^{s,p}_{0}(\Omega)
.
Then
Hg_{1}\leq Hg_{2}
in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
.

Definition 5.3
.

A boundary point
x_{0}\in\partial\Omega
is
regular
(with respect to
\mathcal{L}

and
\Omega
)
if

\lim_{\Omega\ni x\to x_{0}}Hg(x)=g(x_{0})\quad\text{for every }g\in V^{s,p}(\Omega)\cap C(\mathbf{R}^{n}).

Otherwise we say that
x_{0}
is
irregular
.

This definition was used by Kim–Lee–Lee
[
22
]
.
It
was called Sobolev regularity in
Björn–Björn–Kim
[
6
]
,
but one of the main results in
[
6
, Theorem 1.7]

showed that it is equivalent to regularity for
Perron solutions.
We therefore call it just regular here.

In order to define Perron solutions we need
\mathcal{L}
-superharmonic functions,
which we next define.
We follow Korvenpää–Kuusi–Palatucci
[
26
, Definition 1]
,

[

Definition 5.4
.

A measurable function

-superharmonic

\Omega

1.

\Omega

2.

\Omega

for each open set

G
,

u_{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}}}}\in L^{p-1}_{sp}(\mathbf{R}^{n})
.

A function

\mathcal{L}
-subharmonic
in
\Omega

-u

\mathcal{L}
-superharmonic in
\Omega
.

We follow the definition in our paper

For bounded continuous

[

(who however only considered the fractional

-Laplacian with kernel

For

with the definition in

[

by Theorem 9.2 in
[

]
.

Definition 5.5
.

Let

.

The

When
{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\underline{\phantom{\displaystyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\underline{\phantom{\textstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\underline{\phantom{\scriptstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\underline{\phantom{\scriptscriptstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}}g={\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\displaystyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\textstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 0.0pt\overline{\phantom{\scriptscriptstyle P}\kern 0.0pt}$\hss}{P}}}g

and say that
