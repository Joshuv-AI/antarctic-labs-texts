Clinical profiles of patients with antibiotic-resistant infections at select tertiary hospitals in Uganda: a retrospective study of clinical records
Authors: Conrad Tumwine, Flavia Dhikusooka, Reuben Kiggundu, Joseph Musaazi, Ronald Galiwango, Elly Nuwamanya, Mackline Hope, Alex Andema, Nathan Onyachi, Andrew Odur, Stephen Obbo, Peter Mukobi, Deus Twesigye, Francis Oriokot, James Elima, Alfred Yayi, Ben Watmon, Filbert J Nyeko, Charles Olaro, Dathan M Byonanebye, Francis Kakooza, Andrew Kambugu, Jonathan Mayito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntibiotic resistance (ABR) remains a critical global health threat, with disproportionate burden in low-resource settings.MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed the clinical records of 599 patients across nine Uganda tertiary hospitals between March 2023 and April 2024. We described ABR rates and used multivariable mixed effects modified Poisson regression to determine the factors associated with ABR. We defined non-multidrug-resistance (non-MDR) as resistance to less than three antibiotic classes, MDR as resistance to at least three antibiotic classes, extremely drug-resistance (XDR) as resistance to at least one antibiotic in all but two or less antibiotic classes and pan drug resistance (PDR) as resistance to all antibiotics classes.ResultsThe 325 samples from patients with culture confirmed infections yielded 378 pathogens. Of the pathogens tested against three or more antibiotic classes (362), 126/362 (34.8%) were non-MDR and 236/362 (65.2%) MDR. Among the MDR, 165 (47.3%) were XDR, and 18 (5.2%) PDR. The prevalence of ABR was highest on surgical 168 (92%), maternity 30 (91%), and ICU 24 (89%) wards. Regression analysis identified ward type as a key predictor: surgical and “other” wards (gynecology, private, and orthopedic) had a 22% (PR, 1.22, 95% CI 1.09–1.37, p < 0.001) and 26% (PR, 1.26, 95% CI 1.05–1.52 p = 0.016) higher ABR risks, respectively. Antibiotics prescription on admission was associated with a reduced risk for ABR while age, sex, referral status, and Human Immunodeficiency Virus status were not associated with ABR. Overall ABR associated mortality was 12.7%, higher among inpatients admitted for shorter hospital days (median 8 vs. 20 days, p < 0.001), intubated patients, and those admitted to the ICU. Patients with resistance stayed in the hospital five days longer than those with no resistance. Escherichia coli, Klebsiella spp. and Staphylococcus aureus, the most isolated pathogens, were highly resistant to commonly used antibiotics, including extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL) and methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) phenotypes.ConclusionThese findings highlight the clinical impact of ABR in Uganda, especially in high-risk wards such as the ICU and among intubated patients. Strategies for strengthening antibiotic stewardship, infection prevention and diagnostics to combat ABR in Uganda are urgently needed.Trail registrationN/ASupplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13753-1.Keywords: Antimicrobial, Antibiotic resistance, Antimicrobial Stewardship, Infection prevention and control, Sub-Saharan Africa, Hospital acquired infection

Introduction

Bacterial antimicrobial resistance (AMR) accounted for 1.14 million deaths in 2021 [1], a slight decline from 1.27 million in 2019 [2], indicating that bacterial AMR is among the most critical global health threats. The estimates further show that AMR-attributable deaths will reach 1.9 million annually by 2050 if the trend is left unabated [1]. While the AMR threat is global, it is disproportionately borne by low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). In 2019, of the 1.27 million deaths attributable to AMR, 250,000 were from the WHO African region, with western sub-Saharan Africa having the highest overall all-age mortality at 27 × 3 deaths per 100 000 [3]. In the same year, Uganda experienced 7,100 deaths attributable to AMR, ranking 40th among 204, with the highest age-standardized mortality rate per 100,000 people [4]

Antimicrobial resistance is usually associated with severe disease with the need for admission to intensive care units (ICUs), high mortality, increased average length of hospital stay (ALOS), and high disability adjusted life years (DALYS) [5]. Infectious syndromes such as blood stream infections (BSIs) are notorious by nature, with BSIs caused by drug-resistant organisms resulting in worse clinical outcomes [6]. For example, carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella spp. and methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) BSIs have 9- and 2-fold higher mortality rates than susceptible pathogens do, whereas BSIs caused by vancomycin-resistant Enterococci (VREs) are more likely to be admitted to the ICU and have longer ALOSs and higher treatment costs [7–9]. These hospital-acquired infections (HAIs), which are an increasing concern in Uganda, lead to avoidable deaths and hamper the delivery of basic and specialized medical treatment, such as chemotherapy and high-risk surgeries and transplants [10, 11].

The clinical characteristics and outcomes of infectious syndromes, including those caused by drug-resistant pathogens, are influenced by underlying health risks. The health risks may include immune-compromising conditions such as cancer, diabetes, and HIV infections as well as healthcare infrastructure [12]. Despite these being disproportionately distributed between high- and low-income countries, the majority of the current data defining the clinical characteristics and outcomes are largely from high-income countries. Moreover, the usually available data from LMICs are from routine laboratory-based surveillance, which has limited accompanying clinical data [13]. While this type of data is important for disease surveillance and the design of diagnostic protocols, clinical characteristics and outcome data such as mortality, disability, ALOS, and cost are required to inform clinical protocols, planning, resource allocation, and policy formulation.

This study therefore aimed to describe the clinical profiles of patients with bacterial AMR presenting at select tertiary hospitals in Uganda. This information is critical to the AMR response, whose emerging surveillance data under the national action plan reveal an escalating problem [14–16].

Methods

We conducted a retrospective study of the clinical records of patients with infectious syndromes with at least one culture and sensitivity test admitted at nine Uganda Regional Referral Hospitals (RRHs): Arua, Gulu, Lira, Soroti, Mbale, Jinja, Masaka, Mbarara, and Kabale RRHs between 1st March 2023 and 5th April 2024. Regional referral hospitals in the Uganda healthcare system are the first level with specialist services in surgery, internal medicine, obstetrics and gynecology, and pediatrics. A RRH serves a population of approximately 2 million people across several districts, usually three to five or more [17]

The microbiology laboratory capacity of these RRHs has been developed with the support of development partners such as the Fleming Fund [18]. The laboratories receive continuous mentorship in quality management systems on the basis of strengthening laboratory management toward accreditation (SLMTA) and stepwise laboratory quality improvement process toward accreditation (SLIPTA) systems, and the majority are SANAS accredited (Stars 3–5). Qualified clinical and laboratory staff at the study sites collected and analyzed the samples. These individuals receive continuous in-service training and mentorship in diagnostic stewardship, AMR surveillance, bacteriology techniques, and laboratory quality management systems (LQMS).

Sample processing followed standard laboratory procedures; in-house reconstituted culture media, including 5% sheep blood agar, MacConkey, and chocolate agar, were used for primary isolation. Bacterial identification utilizes biochemical methods, including colonial appearance and Gram stain reactions. The culture media used were quality controlled for sterility and the ability to support growth via American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) reference bacterial strains. Mueller–Hinton agar (MHA) media performance and the potency of antibiotic discs were assessed via Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922, Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25,923, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27,853 [19]. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) was performed using the Kirby–Bauer disc diffusion method according to the pathogen antibiotic combination and breakpoints of the updated Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) M100, 32nd, 33rd and 34th editions [20–22]. Specimens were collected from patients with community- or hospital-acquired infections. The samples included both Global Antimicrobial Resistance and Use Surveillance System (GLASS) priority samples—blood, urine, urogenital, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)—and non-GLASS samples—wound swabs and pus aspirates.

Infectious syndrome and mortality determination

The diagnoses in the study were those made by the attending clinicians and documented in the clinical records. However, the diagnoses were verified, categorized, and standardized on the basis of the International Statistical Classification of Diseases 11th Revision (ICD-11) codes for infections [23] by two independent medical doctors. Disagreements among the doctors were resolved through discussion, with the supervising physician, who also provided oversight throughout the entire process. The AST results were those recorded and filed in the clinical records or from laboratory records if they were missing in the clinical records. The clinical records of the patients who died were retrieved, and the clinical and laboratory information was extracted. The resistance reported was that present during the patient’s illness or at the time of death but not determined as cause of death postmortem; hence, we report mortality associated with bacterial AMR.

Procedure of data abstraction

A data abstraction form was designed, pretested, and digitalized in REDCap [24] with inbuilt checks to ensure accurate data collection. Data was collected by a team of clinicians who were trained on the study protocol, data abstraction tool, and data collection standard operating procedures prior to data collection.

The RRHs have similar filing systems, which are paper-based, in which clinical files are kept in shelves on the basis of the year, ward, and serial patient admission hospital number. Each record retrieved was assessed for the diagnosis and AST results to determine eligibility for the study. For records without AST results but for which the request for tests was recorded in the clinical records, the results were obtained from the laboratory records.

Abstracted data were reviewed by supervisors and a data manager to ensure completeness. The variables extracted included referral status, prior antibiotic use, admission date, admission diagnosis, sampling date, comorbidities, AST results, antibiotics prescribed after culture, treatment outcome, ALOS, and discharge date or date of death, among others.

Bacterial antimicrobial-resistant infection was considered as non-multidrug resistance (Non-MDR) if the causative pathogen was resistant to less than three antibiotic classes, MDR infection (MDR) if resistant to at least three antibiotic classes, extremely drug-resistant infection (XDR) if resistant to at least one antibiotic in all classes but two or one antibiotic categories, and pan resistant infection (PDR) if resistant to antibiotic classes in all categories [25]. The XDR and PDR phenotypes were derived where at least four antibiotics appropriate for a particular pathogen were tested.

Sample size statement

We abstracted data from 599 clinical records of patients with infectious syndromes available in the study period of interest, from which 325 patients had bacterial growth and were included in the study analyses. Because of the retrospective nature, no sample size calculation was done.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using R software version 4.3.3 [26]. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient characteristics, with categorical variables presented as frequencies and percentages and continuous variables summarized as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). Group comparisons were conducted using Pearson’s chi-square test for categorical variables and Fisher’s exact test for variables with expected values less than 5. The Mann‒Whitney U test was used to compare medians for the continuous variables. A mixed-effects modified Poisson regression model with robust standard errors was used to assess factors associated with antibiotic resistance and to estimate adjusted incidence rate ratios (IRRs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals. The modified Poisson regression model was considered over the logistic regression model because of the common outcome (prevalence of ABR = 88.3%) therefore the logistic regression model would overestimate the relative risk. The modified Poisson approach with robust standard errors was chosen to provide reliable parameter estimates in the presence of potential overdispersion and variance misspecification. A random intercept for regional referral hospital was included to account for clustering and correlation of outcomes among patients treated within the same hospital. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with VIF values greater than 10 considered indicative of substantial multicollinearity. Model adequacy was evaluated by examining the distribution of residuals using quantile–quantile (Q–Q) plots and kernel density plots. Statistical significance was considered at 5% level of significance.

Results

Among the 599 patients included in the study, 54.3% (325/599) had bacterial growth, and 45.7% (274/599) had no growth (Supplementary Table S1, Table S2). A total of 378 isolates were recovered from the 325 samples with growth, of which 262 samples yielded 1 organism, 58 samples yielded 2 organisms, and 4 samples yielded 3 organisms. Of the pathogens tested on three or more antibiotic classes (362), 126/362 (34.8%) were non-MDR and 236/362 (65.2%) MDR. Among the MDR, 165(47.3%) were XDR, and 18 (5.2%) PDR, these being derived where at least four antibiotics appropriate for a particular pathogen were tested (Supplementary Table S3). Demographic characteristics

The prevalence of ABR among patients with bacterial growth was highest among individuals aged 15–34 years at 91.2% (104/114), followed by those aged 35 and above at 90.0% (104/114), and lowest among children aged 0–14 years at 80.0% (56/70). However, this difference was only marginally statistically significant (p = 0.050). In contrast, sex and referral status were not significantly associated with ABR incidence: 87.9% of females (n = 153/174) and 88.7% of males (n = 134/151) exhibited ABR (p = 0.821). 88.1% of patients not referred from other facilities (n = 208/236) and 88.8% of those referred (n = 79/89) had resistant infections (p = 0.875). (Table 1).

Clinical characteristics

The prevalence of ABR was 88.8% (79/89) in patients referred from other facilities and 88.1% (208/236) in those not referred, and these were not statistically different (p = 0.875). There were significant differences in resistance rates across hospital wards (p = 0.015), with the highest prevalence of ABR being observed in the surgical wards (91.8%, 168/183), followed by the maternity wards (90.9%, 30/33) and the ICU (88.9%, 24/27). The lowest ABR was in the pediatric wards (71.4%, 20/28). There was no significant difference in resistance between patients with symptoms for less than 2 weeks (88.4%, 190/215) and those with symptoms for more than 2 weeks (88.1%, 74/84). HIV-negative patients had a slightly higher but non-significant resistance rate (90.0%, 90/100) versus HIV-positive patients (82.1%, 32/39). The ABR in patients with comorbidities was 89.3% (67/75), whereas it was 88.0% (220/250) in those without comorbidities (p = 0.753), and these were not significantly different. (Table 1).

Treatment outcomes and length of hospital stay

The median ALOS in non-ABR patients was 14 days [IQR: 6–26] versus 19 days [IQR: 9–35] in ABR patients. For clinical outcomes, there was no significant difference in the prevalence of ABR among patients who died (86.8%) and those who recovered (88.6%). (Table 1).

Patients in surgical wards had a 22% greater ABR prevalence (adjusted PR = 1.22, 95% CI: 1.09–1.37; p < 0.001) than those on medical wards. Patients admitted to “other wards” (e.g., gynecology, private wing, and orthopedic wards) had a 26% higher ABR prevalence (aPR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.05–1.52; p = 0.016) compared to those on medical wards. In addition, patients admitted to the maternity wards had a 20% higher ABR prevalence than those admitted to the medical wards, and this association was marginally statistically significant (aPR = 1.20, 95% CI: 0.97–1.48, p = 0.093). Patients who had antibiotics prescribed at admission had a 16% lower ABR prevalence (aPR = 0.84, 95% CI: 0.78–0.91; p < 0.001) than those not prescribed antibiotics. (Table 2).

Demographic factors such as age and sex had no significant associations with ABR in the adjusted model. Similarly, clinical and treatment factors such as symptom duration (>2 weeks), HIV status, intubation, and comorbidities had no statistically significant associations with ABR (p > 0.05) in the unadjusted model and were therefore not included in the adjusted model. Each log increase in ALOS had no significant association with ABR (aPR = 1.03, 95% CI: 0.98–1.08; p = 0.199). (Table 2).

At model diagnostics: Antibiotics prescribed at admission exceeded the pre-defined threshold for multicollinearity of 10 (VIF = 14.5). At further exploration we found out that antibiotic prescription at admission was significantly associated with ward (chi-square test p-value = 0.032). Despite this, both variables were retained in the model because of their clinical relevance. For model fit, the Q-Q plots indicated that the residuals deviated from normality, which is likely attributable to insufficient number of data points and covariates, given the analysis was performed on data abstracted from hospital records rather than prospectively collected observations. Although a mixed-effects model was employed to account for the clustering of patients within facilities, the estimated random effect was negligible (variance: 4.82 × 10− 3 7; SE: 5.51 × 10− 3 6), suggesting minimal facility-level effect on the outcome. Nevertheless, the mixed-effects model was retained because the data had a hierarchical structure, with patients nested within hospital facilities, and the approach appropriately accounted for potential intra-facility correlation and dependence of observations.

Clinical outcomes of ABR patients

Among the 287 patients with ABRs, 259 patients had known clinical outcomes, of whom 33 (12.7%) died and 226 (87.3%) recovered (including clinical improvement and recovery with or without complications). Admission to the ICU, intubation, and a shorter ALOS were associated with high ABR-related mortality. (Table 3).

ABR-associated mortality varied significantly by ward (p < 0.001), with the ICU having the highest mortality (36.4%), followed by medical wards (23.1%), and pediatric wards (22.2%). The surgical wards had the lowest mortality rate (9.5%), whereas no deaths occurred in the maternity wards. Compared with non-intubated patients, intubated patients had a greater risk of ABR-associated death (40.0% vs. 6.9, p = 0.002).

Patients who were not prescribed antibiotics at admission had higher ABR-associated mortality (30.8% vs. 11.5%, p = 0.064) compared to those who were prescribed antibiotics, but this difference was nonsignificant. The median ALOS was significantly shorter for those who died than for those who recovered (8 days [IQR: 5–19] vs. 20 days [IQR: 10–35], p < 0.001). There was no significant difference in mortality between patients with documented infections (14.4%) and those with documented infections plus underlying non-infectious comorbidities (11.2%, p = 0.439). Prescription of antibiotics at time of admission and comorbid conditions were higher in patients who recovered but were not significantly associated with higher risk of ABR- associated mortality. (Table 3).

Discussion

Unlike prior evaluations that have focused on the prevalence of individual resistant pathogens or their prevalence in particular syndromes, we sought to characterize the wider clinical burden and impact of ABR on clinical outcomes.

We report a high ABR prevalence, 34.8% non-MDR versus 65.2% MDR, among inpatients with bacterial growth in nine referral hospitals in Uganda. Resistance was high in all wards but higher in the ICU, maternity ward, and surgical ward. Moreover, ABR was associated with ward type. Admission to surgical or other non-major wards (gynecology, private, and orthopedic) was independently associated with a higher risk for ABR, whereas the prescription of antibiotics at admission was associated with a reduced risk for ABR. Age, sex, referral status, and HIV status were not associated with ABR. Overall, ABR-associated mortality was 12.7%, which was higher among inpatients with meningitis, those at extreme ages (<4 and >45 years), females, intubated patients, and those admitted to the ICU. Patients with resistance stayed 5 days longer in the hospital than those with no resistance, but ALOS was not associated with ABR. Patients who died stayed in the hospital for a significantly shorter period than those who survived. Escherichia coli, Klebsiella spp., and Staphylococcus aureus, the most isolated pathogens, were highly resistant to commonly used antibiotics, including extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL) and methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) phenotypes.

The high ABR prevalence in hospital wards, especially ICU, maternity, and surgical wards, is consistent with results from a prior study in the same setting, which reported that resistance prevalence varied by ward type and was higher in specialized units such as surgical and ICU units [16, 28, 29]. The ICU is characterized by invasive procedures, high broad-spectrum antibiotic use, and the ability to handle critically ill patients [30]. Similarly, maternity and surgical wards highly use antibiotics for the prevention and treatment of surgical site infections resulting from high-risk obstetric and surgical procedures or inadequate infection prevention [31]. Therefore, the clinical environment of these wards favors hospital-acquired infections (HAIs), which are commonly associated with AMR [32], whereas high antibiotic use imparts high selection pressure, leading to the emergence of resistant pathogens [33].

The prescription of antibiotics at admission was associated with a reduced risk and mortality associated with ABR. As most of the encountered clinical resistance is selected resistance due to inappropriate antibiotic use [34], effective therapy based of appropriate guidelines or microbiology results mitigates the propagation of such resistance, which is the goal of antimicrobial stewardship. While diagnostic–antimicrobial stewardship interventions such as adherence to empirical treatment guidelines or targeted prescriptions on the basis of microbiology results, which are practiced at the study sites may have had an effect [35], other factors such as antibiotic administration before sample collection or disease severity that were not assessed could have also contributed to this result.

ABR not being associated with age, sex, referral status, or HIV status, contrasts findings of a prior evaluation in the same setting, which revealed that AMR varied significantly with age and HIV status, whereas resistance to erythromycin was greater in females than in males [16]. Other studies have also shown higher rates of AMR in immunocompromised patients, such as HIV-positive patients and cancer patients [36], and in individuals of different ages [1, 2]. The lack of association in our study could reflect the relatively homogenous population of patients across these nine hospitals or the broad range of antibiotic-resistant pathogens involved, which may not follow predictable patterns on the basis of patient demographics alone. The RRHs also function as the primary point of contact with a health facility for surrounding communities as well as distant communities referred for specialized or advanced care [37].

Over the last two decades, AMR-related deaths have shown an increasing trend, with 1.27 million and 1.14 million deaths globally occurring in 2019 and 2021, respectively [1]. Indeed, ABR had a profound impact on patient outcomes in our study: the overall mortality rate was 12.7%, which was higher in patients in the ICU, those with short hospital stays, and intubated patients. These findings are consistent with prior literature, where patients with AMR admitted to the ICU were at a higher risk of mortality [38]. These patients are more commonly intubated and remain in the hospital for a short time due to early death [38]. Despite not being statistically significant, ABR-associated mortality in our study was greater among those aged 0–14 and 35+ years than among those aged 15–35 years. This was similar to the findings of the 2019 evaluation of the global AMR burden, in which mortality was highest in those under 5 years and over 50 years of age [1]. However, this trend changed in 2021, with mortality increasing in those over 50 years of age and decreasing in those under 5 years of age, except for those with sepsis, who had a 31.1% mortality related to AMR [1].

Severe infectious syndromes such as BSIs, lower respiratory tract infections, and other deep organ infections are associated with high AMR-related deaths [39]. Our study, however, did not report any difference in mortality between patients with documented infections and those with documented infections plus underlying non-infectious comorbidities. Emerging clinical studies in the same setting indicate that sepsis, pneumonia, and meningitis are the leading causes of death due to AMR [40]. On the other hand, an extended hospital stay for patients with resistant infections implies increased clinical burden and resource utilization. Although not statistically significant, patients with resistance stayed longer in the hospital by five days, whereas those who died stayed in the hospital for a significantly short period. This mirrors findings in Ghana, where AMR patients stayed five days longer than did patients with susceptible infections [41], but contrasts the situation in France and Australia, where AMR patients stayed only 1.6 days and 2.9 days longer [42, 43]. This might result from differences in the resources and facilities used to diagnose and treat AMR.

Escherichia coli, Klebsiella spp., and Staphylococcus aureus, the most isolated pathogens in our study, had high resistance to commonly used antibiotics. These results closely relate with findings from a recent evaluation in the same setting, which also revealed that the three pathogens were the most isolated pathogens and had similar resistance patterns [16]. Of most concern is the high prevalence of notorious resistant phenotypes like ESBL, MRSA, and emerging carbapenem-resistant organisms (CROs). The high amoxicillin clavulanic acid resistance in Escherichia coli, presumably indicates a role for AmpC beta lactamases whereas the high resistance of Klebsiella spp. to imipenem/meropenem suggests presence of carbapenamase producers. However, laboratory confirmation for ESBL and carbapenamase-producing organisms was not routinely done due to logistical gaps. Prior studies have reported an ESBL prevalence of >80% and a MRSA prevalence between 46 and 56%. The MRSA prevalence was comparable to that reported in neighboring countries, Kenya (53.4%) [44] and Tanzania (43.3%) [45]. Vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus (VRE) was not tested. This situation underscores the urgent need to strengthen stewardship and infection control measures to curb the spread of these resistant pathogens.

The strengths of our study lie in the multi-hospital design and the inclusion of a wide variety of hospital wards and patient demographics, which constitute one of the first attempts to describe the clinical burden of AMR. However, several limitations should be considered, including the retrospective nature of the observational study, which led to some missing variables and limited the ability to define the causality between ABR, clinical outcomes and the variables assessed. Additionally, the study did not include information on the specific antibiotic regimens used, which could have provided deeper insight into the relationships between antibiotic therapy and resistance patterns. The study also worked with only samples with growth and in some instances only those with resistance to characterize the clinical profile of resistance. This introduced some selection bias and therefore limits the generalization of the results to the general patient population. Finally, much as the study measured several factors contributing to resistance, other significant factors like timing of antibiotic exposure versus specimen sampling were not evaluated. Antibiotic exposure before sampling significantly reduces bacteria growth and could lead to false no growth cultures results, which could have led to underestimation of AMR [46].

Conclusion

In conclusion, our findings highlight the critical public health threat posed by AMR in Uganda and the substantial clinical burden it imposes on the hospital systems. The high prevalence of resistant pathogens, especially in high-risk wards such as the ICU and surgical areas, as well as the impact of ABR on mortality and hospital stay, calls for urgent and comprehensive strategies to combat AMR, including strengthening antibiotic stewardship programs, improving infection control practices, and enhancing diagnostic capacities.
