Stochastic Processes with Modified Lognormal Distribution Featuring Flexible Upper Tail
Dionissios T. Hristopulos, Anastassia Baxevani, Giorgio Kaniadakis

Abstract
Asymmetric, non-Gaussian probability distributions are often observed in the analysis of natural and engineering datasets. The lognormal distribution is a standard model for data with skewed frequency histograms and fat tails. However, the lognormal law severely restricts the asymptotic dependence of the probability density and the hazard function for high values. Herein we present a family of three-parameter non-Gaussian probability density functions that are based on generalized κ \kappa -exponential and κ \kappa -logarithm functions and investigate its mathematical properties. These κ \kappa -lognormal densities represent continuous deformations of the lognormal with lighter right tails, controlled by the parameter κ \kappa . In addition, bimodal distributions are obtained for certain parameter combinations. We derive closed-form analytic expressions for the main statistical functions of the κ \kappa -lognormal distribution. For the moments, we derive bounds that are based on hypergeometric functions as well as series expansions. Explicit expressions for the gradient and Hessian of the negative log-likelihood are obtained to facilitate numerical maximum-likelihood estimates of the κ \kappa -lognormal parameters from data. We also formulate a joint probability density function for κ \kappa -lognormal stochastic processes by applying Jacobi’s multivariate theorem to a latent Gaussian process. Estimation of the κ \kappa -lognormal distribution based on synthetic and real data is explored. Furthermore, we investigate applications of κ \kappa -lognormal processes with different covariance kernels in time series forecasting and spatial interpolation using warped Gaussian process regression. Our results are of practical interest for modeling skewed distributions in various scientific and engineering fields.

II-B

The
\kappa
-Exponential Function

The base of the exponential function is the Euler number
e
which can be defined as the limit

e=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\right)^{n}\,.

Therefore, the natural exponential function
\exp(y),\,y\in\mathbb{R}\,,
corresponds to the limit

\exp(y)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(1+\frac{y}{n}\right)^{n},\;\mbox{where}\;n\in{\mathbb{N}}\,.

(1)

The Kaniadakis
\kappa
-exponential is defined by means of the expression
[
56
,
57
]

\exp_{\kappa}(y)\triangleq\left(\sqrt{1+{\kappa^{2}}{y^{2}}}+{y}{\kappa}\right)^{1/\kappa},

(2)

with
0\leq\kappa<1
and
y\in\mathbb{R}
. The function (
2
) emerges naturally within the framework of special relativity, where the deformation
factor
\kappa
is proportional to the reciprocal light speed

[
58
]
. In that context,
\exp_{\kappa}(y)
is the
relativistic generalization of the natural exponential
\exp(y)
. Herein we take an agnostic viewpoint and consider (
2
) as a mathematical generalization of
\exp(y)
.

By replacing
1/\kappa
with
n
and realizing that at the limit
\kappa\to 0
(
n\to\infty
),
1+{\kappa^{2}}{y^{2}}\approx 1
, it follows that the
\kappa\to 0
limit of the modified exponential (
2
) is the same as the
n\to\infty
limit in (
1
). The
\kappa
-exponential function is visually compared to the natural exponential in Fig.
1
. While in the relativistic theory the constraint
0\leq\kappa<1
makes sense (strictly speaking,
-1<\kappa<1
), mathematically the
\kappa
-exponential (
2
) and other
\kappa
-modified functions can be defined for
\kappa>1
. Herein, we thus focus on
\kappa\geq 0
.
A Taylor series expansion of
\exp_{\kappa}(y)
around
y=0
is available
[
1
]
. We elaborate on this expansion in the Supplement (Section 1).

Asymptotic behavior

The function
\exp_{\kappa}(y)
exhibits

power-law
asymptotic behavior as
y\to\infty

[
57
,
1
]
:

\exp_{\kappa}(y)\sim\big|\,2\kappa y\big|^{\,\pm 1/\kappa},\;\mathrm{as}\;y\to\pm\infty.

(3)

In light of the above, the
\kappa
-exponential
\exp_{\kappa}(-y)
exhibits a
heavy right tail
for
y\to+\infty
,
i.e.

\exp_{\kappa}(-y)\sim(2\kappa y)^{\,-1/\kappa},\;\mathrm{as}\;y\to\infty.

(4)

Hence,
\exp_{\kappa}(-y)
can be used to model probability distributions with high probability density (power law) in the tail.

Monotonicity and convexity

The
\kappa
-exponential is continuously differentiable with respect to
y
. Its derivatives are proportional to

\exp_{\kappa}(y)
, but they also involve a modifying factor that depends on
\kappa
and
y
and vanishes at
\kappa=0
.
The first two derivatives are given by the following expressions

\frac{\dd\exp_{\kappa}(y)}{\dd y}=\frac{\exp_{\kappa}(y)}{\sqrt{1+(\kappa\,y)^{2}}}\,,

(5)

\frac{\dd^{2}\exp_{\kappa}(y)}{\dd y^{2}}=\exp_{\kappa}(y)\,\frac{\sqrt{1+\kappa^{2}\,y^{2}}-\kappa^{2}\,y}{\left(\,1+\kappa^{2}\,y^{2}\,\right)^{3/2}}\,.

(6)

It follows from (
5
) that the first derivative is nonnegative for all
y\in\mathbb{R}
. Hence, the

\kappa
-exponential is a monotonically increasing function of
y
for fixed
\kappa
. Since
\exp_{\kappa}(y)
and the denominator in (
6
) are nonnegative for all
y\in\mathbb{R}
, it is easy to see that the
\kappa
-exponential is a convex function of
y
for all
\kappa\in[\,0,1\,]
. The numerator
\sqrt{1+\kappa^{2}\,y^{2}}-\kappa^{2}\,y
is clearly positive for
y<0
, while for
y>0
it is enough to observe that
1+(\kappa\,y)^{2}-\kappa^{4}\,y^{2}=1+\kappa^{2}y^{2}(1-\kappa^{2})>0
for all
\kappa\in[0,1]
.

Proposition 1
(Bounds of
\kappa
-exponential)

The
\kappa
-exponential decreases monotonically as a function of
\kappa\geq 0
for
y\geq 0
and increases monotonically as function of
\kappa\geq 0
for
y<0
. Thus,
\exp(y)\leq\exp_{\kappa}(y)
for
y<0
and
\exp(y)\geq\exp_{\kappa}(y)
for
y\geq 0
for all
\kappa\geq 0
.

Proof:

Viewing
\exp_{\kappa}(y)
as a function of both
y
and
\kappa
, its partial derivative with respect to
\kappa
is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\exp_{\kappa}(y)}{\partial\kappa}=

\displaystyle\frac{g(\kappa\,y)\,\exp_{\kappa}(y)}{\kappa^{2}\sqrt{1+{\kappa^{2}}{y^{2}}}}\,,

(7)

\displaystyle g(\kappa\,y)=

\displaystyle-\sqrt{1+{\kappa^{2}}{y^{2}}}\,\ln\left(\sqrt{1+{\kappa^{2}}{y^{2}}}+y\kappa\right)+\kappa\,y\,.

(8)

The denominator of the
\kappa
-exponential’s partial derivative is positive for all
y\in\mathbb{R}
and
\kappa>0
. The sign of the partial derivative
\partial\exp_{\kappa}(y)/\partial\kappa
is determined by the the sign of
g(\kappa y)
. To find the latter, consider that
\partial g(\kappa y)/\partial y
is given by

\frac{\partial g(\kappa y)}{\partial y}=-\frac{\ln(\sqrt{1+{\kappa^{2}}{y^{2}}}+\kappa y)\,y\kappa^{2}}{\sqrt{1+{\kappa^{2}}{y^{2}}}}\,.

For
y>0
it holds that
\sqrt{1+{\kappa^{2}}{y^{2}}}+\kappa y>1
, the logarithm of the above is positive, and thus
\partial g(\kappa y)/\partial y
is negative for
y>0
.
For
y<0
, since
\sqrt{1+{\kappa^{2}}{y^{2}}}<1+\lvert y\rvert\kappa
, it also holds that
y\,\ln(\sqrt{1+{\kappa^{2}}{y^{2}}}+\kappa y)>0
since the argument of the logarithm is less than one. Hence,
\frac{\partial g(\kappa y)}{\partial y}<0
for
y<0
as well. Finally, for
y=0
it holds that
\frac{\partial g(\kappa y)}{\partial y}=0
. Therefore,
g(\kappa y)
is a monotonically decreasing function for all
y\in\mathbb{R}
and
\kappa>0
.

The monotonic decline of
g(\cdot)
, together with the fact that
g(0)=0
, implies that
g(\cdot)
takes negative (positive) values for
y>0
(
y<0
).
Since the sign of
{\partial\exp_{\kappa}(y)}/{\partial\kappa}
is controlled by
g(\kappa y)
according to (
7
), it holds that
\partial\exp_{\kappa}(y)/\partial\kappa<0
for any
y\geq 0
and
\kappa>0
while
\partial\exp_{\kappa}(y)/\partial\kappa>0
for any
y<0
and
\kappa>0
.

At
\kappa=0
, the derivative (
7
) vanishes—as can be shown by applying de l’Hospital’s rule and noticing that the leading-order term of the Taylor expansion for
g(\kappa y)
around
\kappa=0
is proportional to
\kappa^{3}
. Thus,
\exp(y)
provides an upper bound of the
\kappa
-exponential for
y\geq 0
. For
y<0
the fact that
g(\kappa y)>0
implies that
\exp_{\kappa}(y)
is an increasing function of
\kappa
; thus,
\exp(y)\leq\exp_{\kappa}(y)
for
y<0
and the exponential provides a lower bound of
\exp_{\kappa}(y)
.
∎

II-C

The
\kappa
-Logarithm Function

The inverse of the
\kappa
-exponential is the

\kappa
-logarithm

[
1
]
, defined by the following function for
x>0
:

\ln_{\kappa}(x)\triangleq\frac{x^{\kappa}-x^{-\kappa}}{2\kappa}\,,\quad\kappa>0\,.

(9)

The
\kappa
-logarithm function is compared to the natural logarithm in Fig.
2
.

The
\kappa
-logarithm can be used to normalize skewed, non-negative data by means of the transformation
x\mapsto y\triangleq\ln_{\kappa}(x)
. The
\kappa
-logarithm transform is similar to the well-known Box-Cox transform
x\mapsto y\triangleq(x^{\lambda}-1)/\lambda

[
60
]
. Both the Box-Cox and the
\kappa
logarithm yield the logarithmic transformation at the respective limits
\lambda\to 0
and
\kappa\to 0
. However, the inverse of the
\kappa
-logarithm is the
\kappa
-exponential
y\mapsto x\triangleq\exp_{\kappa}(y)
which is stable even for
y<0
as evidenced in (
2
). In contrast, the inverse Box-Cox transform
y\mapsto x\triangleq(\lambda y+1)^{1/\lambda}
fails for
\lambda y+1<0

[
61
]
.

Taylor expansion

It follows from (
9
) that as
x\to 0
,
\ln_{\kappa}(x)\to-\infty
just as the natural logarithm. It is also straightforward to see—by writing
x^{\kappa}=\exp(\kappa\ln(x))
and using the Taylor series expansion of the exponential—that
\lim_{\kappa\to 0}\ln_{\kappa}(x)=\ln(x)
. In particular, the small-
\kappa
expansion of (
9
) yields

\ln_{\kappa}(x)=\ln(x)+\frac{1}{6}\,\kappa^{2}\,\ln(x)^{3}+\frac{1}{120}\,\kappa^{4}\,\ln(x)^{5}+\mathcal{O}(\kappa^{6})\,.

(10)

Hence, the above expansion recovers the natural logarithm for
\kappa=0
. According to expansion (
10
),
\ln_{\kappa}(x)\leq\ln(x)
for
x\leq 1
and
\ln_{\kappa}(x)>\ln(x)
for
x>1
. Hence, the difference
\ln_{\kappa}(x)-\ln(x)
changes sign at
x=1
as evidenced in Fig.
2
.

Asymptotic behavior

For
\kappa>0
, it follows that

\ln_{\kappa}(x)\sim x^{\kappa}/(2\kappa),\;\mathrm{as}\;x\to\pm\infty\,.

(11)

Monotonicity of
\kappa
-logarithm with respect to
\kappa

Partial derivatives are introduced here to consider variations of
\ln_{\kappa}(x)
with respect to both
x
and
\kappa
. The derivative of
\ln_{\kappa}(x)
with respect to
\kappa
is given by

\frac{\partial\ln_{\kappa}(x)}{\partial\kappa}=\ln(x)\,\frac{x^{k}+x^{-k}}{2\kappa}-\frac{\ln_{\kappa}(x)}{\kappa}\,.

(12)

\frac{\partial\ln_{\kappa}(x)}{\partial x}=\frac{1}{2x}\left(x^{\kappa}+x^{-\kappa}\right),

(13)

\frac{\partial^{2}\ln_{\kappa}(x)}{\partial x^{2}}=\frac{(\kappa-1)x^{\kappa}-(\kappa+1)x^{-\kappa}}{2x^{2}}\,.

(14)

Note that
\partial\ln_{\kappa}(x)/\partial x>0
for all
x\geq 0
and
\kappa>0
,
and thus the
\kappa
-logarithm is a monotonically increasing function.

Proposition 2
(
\kappa
-logarithm increases faster than the natural logarithm)

(i) The
\kappa
-logarithm defined by (
9
) increases faster than the natural logarithm for all
x>0
. (ii) The rate difference
d_{\ln}(x,\kappa)\triangleq\frac{\partial\ln_{\kappa}(x)}{\partial x}-\frac{\dd\ln(x)}{\dd x}
increases with increasing
\kappa
, indicating a faster increase of the
\kappa
-logarithm with
x
for higher
\kappa
.

Proof:

(i) We need to show that
\frac{\partial\ln_{\kappa}(x)}{\partial x}>\frac{\dd\ln(x)}{\dd x}
for all
x>0
. Since
\dd\ln(x)/\dd x=1/x
, the above is equivalent to
x^{\kappa}+x^{-\kappa}>2
for
x>0
. Defining
z\triangleq x^{\kappa}
, it suffices to show that
z+1/z>2
; since
z>0
, this is equivalent to
z^{2}-2z+1=(z-1)^{2}>0
which is true for all
z\in\mathbb{R}
(and thus for
z>0
).

(ii) The
\kappa
-derivative of the rate difference,
\frac{\partial d_{\ln}(x,\kappa)}{\partial\kappa}=\frac{\partial^{2}\ln_{\kappa}(x)}{\partial x\partial\kappa}=\frac{\ln(x)}{2x}(x^{\kappa}-x^{-\kappa})
, is nonnegative for all
x>0
: for
0<x<1
both
\ln(x)
and
x^{\kappa}-x^{-\kappa}
are negative, while both functions are positive for
x>1
.
∎

Proposition 3
(
\kappa
-logarithm convexity)

(i) For
0\leq\kappa\leq 1
, the
\kappa
-logarithm is concave for all
x>0
. (ii) For
\kappa>1
the
\kappa
-logarithm is concave for
0<x<x_{+}
and convex for
x>x_{+}
where
x_{+}\triangleq\left(\frac{\kappa+1}{\kappa-1}\right)^{1/2\kappa}
is a
\kappa
-dependent
inflection point
.

Proof:

(i) By inspection of (
14
), it follows that the second derivative
\frac{\partial^{2}\ln_{\kappa}(x)}{\partial x^{2}}
is negative for
\kappa\in[0,1]
and
x>0
, which implies that
\ln_{\kappa}(x)
is
concave
.

(ii) For
\kappa>1
, the zeros of the second-order derivative (
14
) are determined by the zeros of the function
g(z)\triangleq(\kappa-1)z-(\kappa+1)z^{-1}
which represents the numerator of the fraction, where
z\triangleq x^{\kappa}>0
. The zeros of
g(z)
are the same as the two non-zero roots of
z\,g(z)=0
, namely
z_{\pm}=\pm x_{+}^{\kappa}
. In addition, the sign of
z\,g(z)
is the same as that of
g(z)
for
z>0
. Hence, since
z\,g(z)=(\kappa-1)(z-z_{+})(z-z_{-})
, the sign of
z\,g(z)
is negative for
0<z<z_{+}
and positive for
z>z_{+}
; consequently, the sign of the second-order derivative of
\ln_{\kappa}(x)
is negative for
0\leq x<x_{+}
and positive for
x>x_{+}
where
x_{+}\triangleq\left(\frac{\kappa+1}{\kappa-1}\right)^{1/2\kappa}.
Therefore,
x_{+}
is an inflection point, where the behavior of
\ln_{\kappa}(x)
changes from concave to convex.
∎

II-D

Review of Lognormal Stochastic Processes

Definition 1
(Lognormal stochastic process)

Given a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\textsl{P})
, the collection of real-valued, scalar random variables
\{Y(\mathbf{s},\vartheta):\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{D},\,\vartheta\in\Omega\}
is a scalar, real-valued
stochastic process

Y:\mathcal{D}\times\Omega\mapsto\mathbb{R}
.
Let
\{Y(\mathbf{s};\vartheta)\}
represent a
stationary Gaussian stochastic process
with mean
\mu
, variance
\sigma^{2}
and covariance kernel
C_{Y}(\mathbf{r})
, where
\mathbf{r}
is the lag between any two points
\mathbf{s},\mathbf{s}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}
. Then, the stochastic process defined by
X(\mathbf{s};\vartheta)=\exp\left[Y(\mathbf{s};\vartheta)\right]

:\mathcal{D}\times\Omega\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{+}

is a
stationary lognormal stochastic process
.

The main statistics and probability functions of a lognormal stochastic process are given below.

1.

Marginal probability density function (PDF)

f_{X}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma\,x}\mathrm{e}^{-\left(\ln(x)-\mu\right)^{2}/2\sigma^{2}}=\frac{1}{x\sigma}\phi\left(\frac{\ln(x)-\mu}{\sigma}\right)\;,\;x\in(0,+\infty)\,,

(15)

where
\phi(\cdot)
is the PDF of the standard normal distribution.

2.

Median (
\mu_{0.5}
), mode (
x_{\ast}
), and mean (
\mu_{X}
):

\mu_{0.5}=\mathrm{e}^{\mu}\;,\;x_{\ast}=\mathrm{e}^{\mu-\sigma^{2}}\;,\;\mu_{X}=\mathrm{e}^{\mu+\sigma^{2}/2}\,.

(16)

The median of the lognormal is also known as the
geometric mean
of
X(\mathbf{s};\vartheta)
.

3.

Variance (
\sigma^{2}_{X}
) and skewness (
s_{X}
):

\sigma^{2}_{X}=\mu_{X}^{2}\,\left[\exp(\sigma^{2})-1\right]\,,\;s_{X}=\left[\exp(\sigma^{2})+2\right]\,\sqrt{\exp(\sigma^{2})-1}\,.

(17)

4.

The marginal cumulative distribution function (CDF) is defined as
F_{X}(x)=P(X(\vartheta)\leq x)
, where
x>0
:

F_{X}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\left[1+\mathrm{erf}\left(\frac{\ln(x)-\mu}{\sigma\sqrt{2}}\right)\right]=\Phi\left(\frac{\ln(x)-\mu}{\sigma}\right)\,,

(18)

where
\Phi(\cdot)
is the CDF of the standard normal distribution.

5.

The quantile function
x_{p}=Q_{X}(p)
is defined so that
P\left(X(\vartheta)\leq x_{p}\right)=p
, where
0\leq p\leq 1
:

Q_{X}(p)=\exp\left[\mu+\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}}\,\mathrm{erf}^{-1}(2p-1)\right]\,.

(19)

6.

Covariance kernel

C_{X}(\mathbf{r})=\mu_{X}^{2}\,\left(\mathrm{e}^{C_{Y}(\mathbf{r})}-1\right)\,.

(20)

III
Modified Lognormal Distribution

Definition 2
(
\kappa
-lognormal distribution)

If
Y(\vartheta):\vartheta\in\Omega
represents a scalar, real-valued Gaussian random variable in the probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\textsl{P})
with mean
\mu
, and variance
\sigma^{2}
,
the random variable
X(\vartheta)
defined by means of the
\kappa
-exponential transformation

X(\vartheta)\triangleq\exp_{\kappa}\left[Y(\vartheta)\right]=\left[\sqrt{1+Y^{2}(\vartheta){\kappa^{2}}}+Y(\vartheta){\kappa}\right]^{1/\kappa}\,,\;\kappa\geq 0\,,

(21a)

follows the
\kappa
-lognormal distribution,
\kappa{\rm LN}(\mu,\sigma,\kappa)
.
The inverse transformation is given by the
\kappa
-logarithm, that is,

Y(\vartheta)\triangleq\ln_{\kappa}\left[X(\vartheta)\right]=\frac{X^{\kappa}(\vartheta)-X^{-\kappa}(\vartheta)}{2\kappa}.

(21b)

The marginal probability functions for the
\kappa
-lognormal distribution are defined below.

\kappa

Theorem 1
(Modes of
\kappa
-lognormal PDF)

The
\kappa
-lognormal PDF defined by (
22
) is unimodal or bimodal depending on the values of
\mu,\sigma\geq 0
and
\kappa\geq 0
. For
\kappa=0
(lognormal distribution) the PDF has a single mode at
x_{{\rm mode}}=\exp(\mu-\sigma^{2})
. For
\kappa>0
the PDF can sustain up to three modes. For
0<\kappa\leq 1
the modes (at least one) are in the interval
(0,\mu_{0.5})
. For
\kappa>1
, if
\mu_{0.5}<x_{+}
, where
x_{+}=\left[(\kappa+1)/(\kappa-1)\right]^{1/2\kappa}
as defined in Proposition
3

(0,\mu_{0.5})
while a mode can potentially emerge in
(x_{+},\infty)
. For
\kappa>1
and
\mu_{0.5}>x_{+}
, the mode(s) are located inside the intervals
(0,x_{+})
or
(\mu_{0.5},\infty)
.

For
\kappa>0
the locations of the mode(s) are determined from the zeros of the
characteristic polynomial

\displaystyle p_{1}(z)=z^{6}-a\,z^{5}+b\,z^{4}-2a\,z^{3}+c\,z^{2}-a\,z-1\,,

(26)

where
a=2\mu\kappa
,

and

1.

For

2.

For

and
x_{{\rm mode};2}=x_{{\ast};3}
(the ordering

3.

If at most one of the coefficients

is non-zero, then

and the PDF is unimodal. The converse is generally not true, unless one of the following conditions holds:

\mu=0
,
\kappa=0

\sigma^{2}=\frac{1}{4\kappa(\kappa-1)}

\sigma^{2}=\frac{1}{4\kappa(\kappa+1)}
.

4.

Finally, while

(implying three modes) is not excluded by Descartes’ rule if

III-C

Moment Bounds

The moments
m_{X;\ell}(\kappa;\mu,\sigma)
in (
27
) are bounded from below by zero, since
\exp_{\kappa}(y)\geq 0
. The upper bounds depend on the values of
\mu
and
\sigma
: if the latter are such that
f_{Y}(y;\mu,\sigma^{2})

is practically zero for
y<0
, then it becomes evident from the defining integral,
that a moment of order
\ell
for
\kappa>0
is bounded from above by the same-order lognormal moment (with the same
\mu
and
\sigma
as the
\kappa
-lognormal), because
\left[\exp_{\kappa}(y)\right]^{\ell}\leq\left[\exp(y)\right]^{\ell}
for
y>0
. In fact, given the monotonic dependence of
\kappa
-exponential on
\kappa
the ordering

m_{X;\ell}(\kappa^{\prime};\mu,\sigma)<m_{X;\ell}(\kappa;\mu,\sigma)
if
\kappa^{\prime}>\kappa
holds. On the other hand, the above argument cannot be extended if
f_{Y}(y;\mu,\sigma^{2})

is non-negligible for
y<0
, because
\exp_{\kappa}(y)>\exp(y)
for
y<0
.
In the following, we derive stricter bounds on the moments of the
\kappa
-lognormal distribution.

Theorem 3
(Moment bounds)

Let
X
denote a
\kappa
-lognormal process so that
\ln_{\kappa}\left[X(\vartheta)\right]\overset{d}{=}\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
with
\mu>0
. Then, the moments
m_{X;\ell}(\kappa;\mu,\sigma)

\ell
respect the following inequalities

UB_{X;\ell}(\kappa;\mu,\sigma)\geq m_{X;\ell}(\kappa;\mu,\sigma)\geq LB_{X;\ell}(\kappa;\mu,\sigma)\,,

(29a)

where
LB_{X;\ell}(\kappa;\mu,\sigma)
and
UB_{X;\ell}(\kappa;\mu,\sigma)
represent, respectively, lower and upper bounds of
m_{X;\ell}(\kappa;\mu,\sigma)
given by

\displaystyle LB_{X;\ell}(\kappa;\mu,\sigma)=

\displaystyle\frac{(2\kappa)^{\ell/\kappa}\,2^{\frac{\ell-\kappa}{2\kappa}}\,\sigma^{\ell/\kappa}\,}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\mu^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\,\left[\Gamma\left(\frac{\ell+\kappa}{2\kappa}\right){}_{1}F_{1}\left(\frac{\ell+\kappa}{2\kappa},\,\frac{1}{2}\,;\frac{\mu^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.\quad\quad+\,\Gamma\left(\frac{\ell+2\kappa}{2\kappa}\right)\frac{\mu\sqrt{2}}{\sigma}\,{}_{1}F_{1}\left(\frac{\ell+2\kappa}{2\kappa},\,\frac{3}{2}\,;\frac{\mu^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\right]+\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\ell\mu+\ell^{2}\sigma^{2}/2}}{2}\left[1-\mathrm{erf}\left(\frac{\mu+\ell\sigma^{2}}{\sqrt{2}\sigma}\right)\right]\,,

(29b)

\displaystyle UB_{X;\ell}(\kappa;\mu,\sigma)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[1-\mathrm{erf}\left(\frac{\mu}{\sqrt{2}\sigma}\right)\right]+\frac{2^{\ell/\kappa-1}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mu^{2}/2\sigma^{2}}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\,\,\sum_{m=0}^{n_{\ast}}\,\,\frac{n_{\ast}!\,}{m!\,(n_{\ast}-m)!}\left(2\kappa^{2}\sigma^{2}\right)^{m}

\displaystyle\times\left[\Gamma\left(m+\frac{1}{2}\right)\,{}_{1}F_{1}\left(m+\frac{1}{2}\,,\frac{1}{2}\,;\frac{\mu^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)+\Gamma(m+1)\,\frac{\mu\sqrt{2}}{\sigma}\,{}_{1}F_{1}\left(m+1\,,\frac{3}{2}\,;\frac{\mu^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\right]\,.

(29c)

\Gamma(\cdot)
is the Gamma function,
{}_{1}F_{1}(a,b;z)
is the
confluent hypergeometric function

[
62
, 9.210]
,
\mathrm{erf}(\cdot)
is the error function, and
n_{\ast}=\lceil\frac{\ell}{2\kappa}\rceil
is the nearest integer that is not smaller than
\frac{\ell}{2\kappa}
.

C

Remark 5
(Scaling relation for moment bounds)

The moment bounds (
29b
) and (
29c
) respect scaling relations analogous to (
28
) for the moments, i.e.,

\displaystyle{UB}_{X;\ell}(\kappa;\mu,\sigma)=

\displaystyle{UB}_{X;1}\left(\frac{\kappa}{\ell};\ell\mu,\ell\sigma\right)\,,

(30a)

\displaystyle{LB}_{X;\ell}(\kappa;\mu,\sigma)=

\displaystyle{LB}_{X;1}\left(\frac{\kappa}{\ell};\ell\mu,\ell\sigma\right)\,.

The scaling relations follow directly from (

Power Series Expansions of the Moments

Based on the moment integral (
27
), we can develop a power series expansion for the moment of order
\ell
by expanding the
\kappa
-exponential. The expansion can be developed around different points. Below, we focus on the Taylor series expansion of the moments around
\mu
.

Theorem 4
(Power series of
\kappa
-lognormal moments)

Let
X
be a random variable that follows the
\kappa
-lognormal distribution with parameters
\mu\geq 0,\sigma\geq 0,\kappa\geq 0
and
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. Then,

m_{X;\ell}(\kappa;\mu,\sigma)=\exp_{\kappa/\ell}(\ell\,\mu)\,\left[1+\ell\,\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sigma^{2q}}{q!\,2^{q}}\,\frac{g_{2q}(\mu;\kappa,\ell)}{\left({1+\kappa^{2}\mu^{2}}\right)^{2q-1/2}}\,\right]\,,

(31a)

where the functions
g_{n}(\cdot)
satisfy the recursive relation

g_{n+1}(\mu;\kappa,\ell)=\left(1+\kappa^{2}\mu^{2}\right)\,\frac{\dd g_{n}(\mu;\kappa,\ell)}{\dd\mu}-\left[(2n-1)\kappa^{2}\mu-\ell\sqrt{1+\kappa^{2}\mu^{2}}\right]\,g_{n}(\mu;\kappa,\ell)\,,

(31b)

with
g_{1}(\mu;\kappa,\ell)=1
.

Proof:

The proof of Theorem
4
is given in Appendix
D
.
∎

Remark 7

The functions
g_{n}(\mu;\kappa,\ell)
for
n=1,2,3,4
are given by

\displaystyle g_{1}(\mu;\kappa,\ell)=

\displaystyle 1\,,

(32a)

\displaystyle g_{2}(\mu;\kappa,\ell)=

\displaystyle\ell\sqrt{1+\kappa^{2}\mu^{2}}-\mu\kappa^{2}\,,

(32b)

\displaystyle g_{3}(\mu;\kappa,\ell)=

\displaystyle-3\kappa^{2}\,\ell\mu\,\sqrt{1+\kappa^{2}\mu^{2}}-\kappa^{2}+2\kappa^{4}\mu^{2}+\ell^{2}\,\left(1+\kappa^{2}\mu^{2}\right)\,,

(32c)

\displaystyle g_{4}(\mu;\kappa,\ell)=

\displaystyle\ell\,\kappa^{2}\,\sqrt{1+\kappa^{2}\mu^{2}}\left(11\kappa^{2}\,\mu^{2}-4\right)+3\kappa^{2}\mu\left(3\kappa^{2}-2\kappa^{4}\mu^{2}-2\ell^{2}-2\kappa^{2}\mu^{2}\ell^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\ell^{3}\,\sqrt{1+\kappa^{2}\mu^{2}}\,\left(1+\kappa^{2}\mu^{2}\,\right)\,.

(32d)

From the above, only the even-order
g_{2}(\mu;\kappa,\ell)
and
g_{4}(\mu;\kappa,\ell)
contribute to the moment expansion (
31a
).

At the limit of the lognormal distribution, that is for
\kappa=0
, the moment series (
31a
) for fixed
\ell
is expressed as

m_{X;\ell}(\kappa=0;\mu,\sigma)=\exp(\ell\,\mu)\,\left[1+\ell\,\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sigma^{2q}}{q!\,2^{q}}\,g_{2q}(\mu;0,\ell)\,\right]\,,

(33a)

where the functions
g_{n}(\cdot)
satisfy the recursive equation

g_{n+1}(\mu;0,\ell)=\frac{\dd g_{n}(\mu;0,\ell)}{\dd\mu}+\ell\,g_{n}(\mu;0,\ell)\,,\;\mbox{for}\;n>1\,,

(33b)

with
g_{1}(\mu;0,\ell)=1
.
The above recursive equation is solved by

g_{n+1}(\mu;0,\ell)=\ell^{n}
.

Hence, the power series (
33a
) can be explicitly summed yielding the lognormal moments, i.e.,
m_{X;\ell}(\kappa=0;\mu,\sigma)=\exp\left(\ell\,\mu+\frac{1}{2}\ell^{2}\sigma^{2}\right)
.

III-E

Behavior of
\kappa
-Lognormal Tail versus the Lognormal Tail

As stated in the Introduction, the tail of the lognormal distribution is too long for certain applications—a comparison presented in the Supplement (Section 2) between the normal and lognormal distributions confirms this behavior.

To intuitively explore this observation, we consider the behavior of lognormal quantiles for probability values
p_{\ast}\triangleq 1-1/N
that are close to one. The quantiles
x_{\max}\triangleq Q(p_{\ast})
can be viewed as typical extremes obtained from
N=2^{L}
, where
L\in\mathbb{N}
, independent samples of a lognormal variable. We focus on the
dimensionless ratio

q_{\ast}(\kappa)\triangleq x_{\max}/\mu_{0.5}
of the “typical extreme” value over the median. This ratio represents the
normalized typical extreme
. For the lognormal distribution it is given by

\displaystyle q_{\ast}(\kappa=0)

\displaystyle\triangleq\frac{Q_{\mathrm{LN}}\left(p_{\ast}\right)}{Q_{\mathrm{LN}}\left(0.5\right)}=\exp\left[\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}}\,\mathrm{erf}^{-1}(1-2/N)\right]\,,

(34)

where
Q_{\mathrm{LN}}
is the quantile function of the lognormal distribution.
For stochastic processes, normalized typical extremes can be smaller—for given
N
—because the values at neighboring nodes are not independent.
This implies an effective system size
N_{\textrm{eff}}\approx N/\xi^{d}
(instead of
N
), where
\xi
is the correlation length of the stochastic process
[
6
, Chap. 13]
.

To highlight the tail difference between the lognormal and
\kappa
-lognormal, we calculate the normalized typical extreme as a function of
N
assuming that both distributions have the same mean,
\mu_{X}
, and variance,
\sigma^{2}_{X}
. To calculate

q_{\ast}(\kappa)
we use the
\kappa
-quantile function given by (
25
) in (
34
).
We select
\mu=0
,
\sigma=1.5
, and estimate the
\kappa
-lognormal mean,
\mu_{X}
, and variance,
\sigma^{2}_{X}
, for different
\kappa
based on the integral (
27
). Then, we obtain the lognormal mean,
\mu^{\prime}
, and variance,
{\sigma^{\prime}}^{2}
, by inverting (
16
) for the mean and (
17
) for the variance leading to

\sigma^{\prime 2}=\ln\left(1+\frac{\sigma^{2}_{X}}{\mu_{X}^{2}}\right)\,,\quad\mu^{\prime}=\ln\mu_{X}-\ln\left(\sqrt{1+\frac{\sigma^{2}_{X}}{\mu_{X}^{2}}}\,\right)\,.\quad

(35)

Equations (
35
) ensure that the lognormal and
\kappa
-lognormal distributions share the same mean and variance.

III-F

Hazard Rate of the
\kappa
-Lognormal

The
hazard rate
(also known as
hazard function
and
failure rate
),
h(x)
, is a key concept in reliability engineering and survival analysis
[
63
,
64
]
. Its definition involves the survival function,
S_{X}(x)
, also known as complementary CDF (CCDF):
S_{X}(x)=1-F_{X}(x)
. The hazard function,
h(x)
, is then defined as

h(x)=\frac{f_{X}(x)}{S_{X}(x)}.

(36)

Significance

For an ensemble of identical systems under “stress, where the latter is denoted by the random variable
X(\vartheta)
,
h(x)
is the conditional probability that a system fails at stress level
X(\vartheta)=x

considering that identical systems have survived for
X(\vartheta)\leq x
. Alternatively,
X(\vartheta)
could represent the time to failure. For example, in lifetime analysis it represents the time to catastrophic failure of a mechanical or electrical component. For systems that can sustain multiple failure events, such as geological faults, it represents the recurrence time to the next event (e.g., an earthquake above a certain threshold magnitude). For random failure events, lifetime and recurrence times follow the exponential distribution with rate parameter
\lambda
. The exponential hazard rate equals the constant
\lambda
, a result which is anticipated due to the randomness of the failure events.

Asymptotic behavior

The dependence of
h(x)
for large
x
is a matter of interest. If
h(x)
increases as
x\to\infty
, the probability of the next failure event increases with the
time elapsed since the last event
[
65
]
; for non-repairable systems, an increasing
h(x)
implies that the failure probability increases with elapsed time. Such behavior represents an aging effect
[
64
]
.
On the other hand, decreasing hazard rates indicate that the probability of new occurrences (or a single failure) is
reduced with increasing waiting time; this behavior is often observed in electronic parts and is attributed to a “burn-in” effect
[
64
]
.

Lognormal hazard rate

The hazard function of the lognormal distribution is given by

h(x)=\frac{\phi\left(\frac{\ln(x)-\mu}{\sigma}\right)}{x\,\sigma\left[1-\Phi\left(\frac{\ln(x)-\mu}{\sigma}\right)\right]}\,,\;\mbox{for}\;x\geq 0.

(37)

The hazard rate (
37
) starts at zero for
x=0
, rises to a maximum, and then slowly decreases to zero
[
66
]
. Hence, the lognormal distribution is not suitable for survival or failure-time data.
To determine the asymptotic dependence of
h(x)
we use
z(x)\triangleq(\ln(x)-\mu)/\sigma
and subsequently (dropping the dependence of
z
on
x
for convenience),
h(z)=\phi(z)\mathrm{e}^{-\mu-\sigma z}/\sigma[1-\Phi(z)]
. As
z\to\infty
,
\phi(z)
,
\mathrm{e}^{-\mu-\sigma z}
, and
1-\Phi(z)
tend to zero. To lift the indeterminacy, we use L’Hôspital’s rule,
that is,
\phi(z)/\left[1-\Phi(z)\right]\sim\phi^{\prime}(z)/\left[-\phi(z)\right]=-[\ln\phi(z)]^{\prime}\,,
where
\phi(z)=\exp(-z^{2}/2)/\sqrt{2\pi}
and the prime denotes differentiation with respect to
z
. Since
[\ln\phi(z)]^{\prime}=-z
, it follows that
h(x)\sim z(x)/x\,\sigma
, that is,

h(x)\sim\frac{\ln(x)-\mu}{x\sigma^{2}}\,,\quad x\to\infty.

(38)

\kappa
-lognormal hazard rate

Based on (
22
) and (
24
), the
\kappa
-lognormal hazard function is given by

h_{\kappa}(x)=\frac{\phi\left(\frac{\ln_{\kappa}(x)-\mu}{\sigma}\right)\left(x^{\kappa-1}+x^{-\kappa-1}\right)}{2\,\sigma\left[1-\Phi\left(\frac{\ln_{\kappa}(x)-\mu}{\sigma}\right)\right]}\,.

(39)

We investigate the asymptotic behavior of
h_{\kappa}(x)
following the same procedure, now setting
z=(\ln_{\kappa}(x)-\mu)/\sigma
and using
h_{\kappa}(x)\sim z\left(x^{\kappa-1}+x^{-\kappa-1}\right)/(2\sigma)
. Since, according to (
11
), as
x\to\infty

\ln_{\kappa}(x)\sim x^{\kappa}/(2\kappa)
and
\left(x^{\kappa-1}+x^{-\kappa-1}\right)\approx x^{\kappa-1}
, it follows that

h_{\kappa}(x)\sim\frac{x^{2\kappa-1}-2\kappa\mu x^{\kappa-1}}{4\kappa\sigma^{2}}\,,\quad x\to\infty\,,

(40)

which implies that the dominant term is
x^{2\kappa-1}/(4\kappa\,\sigma^{2})
.

It follows from the above analysis that
\kappa=0.5
corresponds to a critical point where the hazard rate asymptotically tends to the constant
1/2\sigma^{2}
. Hence, for
\kappa=0.5
the
\kappa
-lognormal behaves asymptotically as the exponential hazard rate with
\lambda=1/2\sigma^{2}
. On the other hand,
h_{\kappa}(x)
is a decreasing function of
x
for
\kappa<0.5
(thus exhibiting “burn-in” effect) and an increasing function for
\kappa>0.5
(demonstrating the aging effect). In all three cases, however,
h_{\kappa}(x=0)=0
, and thus the
\kappa
-lognormal distribution does not support the “infant mortality” (early failures) effect, unlike the Weibull distribution with shape modulus less than one
[
64
]
. Nonetheless, the
\kappa
-lognormal with
\kappa>0.5
is, unlike the lognormal, more suitable for failure-time analysis.
Plots of
h_{\kappa}(x)
for different values of
\kappa
confirming the near-zero and the asymptotic behavior are shown in Fig.
10
.

III-G

Failure of Simple Scaling Invariance

The lognornal distribution has a scaling property which ensures the invariance of the CDF under a scaling operation: If
X(\vartheta)\overset{d}{=}{\rm LN}(\mu,\sigma)
and
\lambda>0
then for
X^{\prime}(\vartheta)=\lambda X(\vartheta)
it follows that
X^{\prime}(\vartheta)\overset{d}{=}{\rm LN}(\mu^{\prime},\sigma)
, where
\mu^{\prime}=\mu+\ln\lambda
.
It can easily be shown by replacing
\ln(x)
in (
24
) with
\ln(\lambda x)
and translating
\mu\mapsto\mu^{\prime}=\mu+\ln\lambda
that the CDF remains invariant.
The
\kappa
-lognormal distribution lacks this simple scaling property for
\kappa>0
.

To study the CDF transformation of
X^{\prime}(\vartheta)=\lambda X(\vartheta)
, for
\lambda>0
we need the
\kappa
-logarithm composition law
[
57
]

\ln_{\kappa}(\lambda x)=\ln_{\kappa}(\lambda)\sqrt{1+\kappa^{2}\ln_{\kappa}^{2}(x)}+\ln_{\kappa}(x)\sqrt{1+\kappa^{2}\ln_{\kappa}^{2}(\lambda)}\,

which shows that the mapping
\left(\ln_{\kappa}(\lambda x)-\mu\right)/\sigma\mapsto\left(\ln_{\kappa}(x)-\mu^{\prime}\right)/\sigma^{\prime}
is not possible in general for
\mu^{\prime}
,
\sigma^{\prime}
independent of
x
. There are two cases when such a mapping is approximately possible.

1.

If
\kappa\,\lvert\,\ln_{\kappa}(x)\rvert\ll 1
for all
x
such that the values of
f_{X}(x)
in (
22
) exceed some arbitrary threshold (e.g.,
10^{-6}
), then the CDF is invariant under the mapping
\mu\mapsto\mu^{\prime}=\mu\sqrt{1+\kappa^{2}\ln_{\kappa}^{2}(\lambda)}+\ln_{\kappa}(\lambda)
and
\sigma\mapsto\sigma^{\prime}=\sigma\sqrt{1+\kappa^{2}\ln_{\kappa}^{2}(\lambda)}
. The condition
\kappa\,\lvert\,\ln_{\kappa}(x)\rvert\ll 1
is equivalent to

1<x\ll\exp_{\kappa}(1/\kappa)
or
1>x\gg\exp_{\kappa}(-1/\kappa)
.

2.

If
\kappa\,\lvert\,\ln_{\kappa}(x)\rvert\gg 1
for all
x
such that
f_{X}(x)
is not negligible, then the CDF is invariant under the mapping
\mu\mapsto\mu^{\prime}=\mu\left[\sqrt{1+\kappa^{2}\ln_{\kappa}^{2}(\lambda)}+\kappa\ln_{\kappa}(\lambda)\right]+\ln_{\kappa}(\lambda)
and
\sigma\mapsto\sigma^{\prime}=\sigma\left[\sqrt{1+\kappa^{2}\ln_{\kappa}^{2}(\lambda)}+\kappa\ln_{\kappa}(\lambda)\right]
. The condition
\kappa\,\lvert\,\ln_{\kappa}(x)\rvert\gg 1
is equivalent to

x\gg\exp_{\kappa}(1/\kappa)
or
x\ll\exp_{\kappa}(-1/\kappa)
.

IV
\kappa
-lognormal stochastic process

Definition 3
(
\kappa
-lognormal process)

The
\kappa
-lognormal stochastic process
\{X(\mathbf{s};\vartheta):\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,,\vartheta\in\Omega\}
in the probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\textsl{P})
is defined as the process obtained by applying the
\kappa
-exponential transformation on a scalar, real-valued Gaussian stochastic process
\{Y(\mathbf{s};\vartheta)\}
as follows

X(\mathbf{s};\vartheta)\triangleq\exp_{\kappa}\left[Y(\mathbf{s};\vartheta)\right]=\left(\sqrt{1+{\kappa^{2}}\,Y^{2}(\mathbf{s};\vartheta)}+{\kappa}\,Y(\mathbf{s};\vartheta)\right)^{1/\kappa}\,,\;\kappa\geq 0\,,

(41)

The finite-dimensional distributions of the
\kappa
-lognormal process are given below in (
43
).
The inverse transformation that recovers the latent Gaussian stochastic process is given by the
\kappa
-logarithm, that is,

Y(\mathbf{s};\vartheta)\triangleq\ln_{\kappa}\left[X(\mathbf{s};\vartheta)\right]=\frac{X^{\kappa}(\mathbf{s};\vartheta)-X^{-\kappa}(\mathbf{s};\vartheta)}{2\kappa}.

(42)

We focus on
weakly stationary
(also known as second-order stationary) stochastic processes with constant mean
\mu
and covariance kernel
C_{Y}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{s}^{\prime}\,;\,{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
that depends on the input vectors purely via the lag
\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{s}^{\prime}
, where
\mathbf{s},\mathbf{s}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the kernel parameter vector.

IV-A

Joint Density of
\kappa
-Lognormal Processes

In order to derive the joint density of the
\kappa
-lognormal process we use Jacobi’s multivariate theorem
[
1
]
.

Theorem 5
(Jabobi’s multivariate theorem)

Let
\mathbf{X}(\vartheta)=({X}_{1}(\vartheta),\ldots,{X}_{N}(\vartheta))^{\top}
and
\mathbf{Y}(\vartheta)=(Y_{1}(\vartheta),\ldots,Y_{N}(\vartheta))^{\top}
denote two
N
-dimensional continuously-valued random vectors; the index
i=1,\ldots,N
refers to the input vector
\mathbf{s}_{i}
. The vector variable
\mathbf{Y}(\vartheta)
is obtained by means of the transformation
\mathbf{Y}(\vartheta)=\mathbf{g}\,\left(\mathbf{X}(\vartheta)\right)
, where
\mathbf{g}(\cdot)
is a vector function with elements
Y_{l}=g_{l}(\mathbf{X}),\ l=1,\dots,N
.
The functions
g_{l}(\cdot)
are assumed to be continuous, possess continuous partial derivatives with respect to all inputs, and define one-to-one mappings. Then, there exist unique inverse functions
g_{l}^{-1}(\cdot)
such that
{X}_{l}(\vartheta)=g_{l}^{-1}\big(\mathbf{Y}(\vartheta)\big)\,.

In addition, the joint PDF
f_{\mathbf{Y}}(\mathbf{y})
is given by means of the following equation

f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})=f_{\mathbf{Y}}\left(\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x})\right)\,\big|\det(\mathbb{J})\big|\,,

(43)

where
\mathbb{J}
is the Jacobian of the transformation
\mathbf{y}\mapsto\mathbf{x}
.
\mathbb{J}
is given by the following matrix of
partial derivatives

\mathbb{J}=\left[{J}_{i,j}\right]_{i,j=1}^{N}\,,

(44a)

where

{J}_{i,j}=\frac{\partial g_{i}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{j}},\;i,j=1,\ldots,N.

(44b)

For the
\kappa
-lognormal process, we apply Jacobi’s multivariate theorem with the transformation
y_{i}=g_{i}(x_{i})
, where
g_{i}(x_{i})\triangleq\ln_{\kappa}(x_{i})
. Therefore,
{J}_{i,j}=\delta_{i,j}\,\frac{\partial\ln_{\kappa}(x_{i})}{\partial x_{j}}
, where
\delta_{i,j}
is the Kronecker delta, leading to
{J}_{i,i}=\frac{1}{2}\left(x_{i}^{\kappa-1}+x_{i}^{-\kappa-1}\right)
, for
i=1,\ldots,N
and
J_{i,j}=0
for
i\neq j
. Hence, assuming that
\mathbf{Y}\overset{d}{=}\mathcal{N}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu},\,\mathbf{C}_{Y}\right)
, the
\kappa
-lognormal joint PDF is given by

f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{\left(2\sqrt{2\pi}\right)^{N}\,\big|\det(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{Y}})\big|^{1/2}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\ln_{\kappa}(\mathbf{x})-\boldsymbol{\mu}\right)^{\top}\,\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{Y}}^{-1}\left(\ln_{\kappa}(\mathbf{x})-\boldsymbol{\mu}\right)}\,\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left(x_{i}^{\kappa-1}+x_{i}^{-\kappa-1}\right)\,,

(45)

where
\ln_{\kappa}(\mathbf{x})
and
\boldsymbol{\mu}
are
N\times 1
vectors with
i
-th elements
\ln_{\kappa}(x_{i})
and
\mu
respectively for all
i=1,\ldots,N
. Furthermore, the PDF (
45
) can be extended to non-stationary models by considering proper covariance kernels or by introducing temporal dependence in
\boldsymbol{\mu}
.

IV-B

Predictive Distribution

In this section, we formulate warped Gaussian process regression for the
\kappa
-lognormal stochastic process.

Warping

Let
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{S}}\triangleq(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})^{\top}
represent the sample values of the
\kappa
-lognormal process
{X}(\mathbf{s};\vartheta)
for respective features
{\mathbb{S}_{N}}=\{\mathbf{s}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{s}_{N}\}
. The sampled values may contain a noise term
\{\epsilon_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
. To keep notation simple, we do not use a different symbol for “noise contaminated” data. However, the presence of noise is accounted for by including a diagonal noise variance term in the covariance kernel. Assume that we aim to estimate
{X}(\mathbf{s};\vartheta)
for a new input
{\mathbf{s}}_{\ast}
. Using the framework of Gaussian process regression
[
4
]
, the answer involves the marginal conditional distribution of
X({\mathbf{s}}_{\ast};\vartheta)
. For processes with asymmetric probability distributions, the concept of warped Gaussian processes (w-GPs) is used
[
7
,
8
]
. In w-GPs, the observation space is warped by means of a nonlinear monotonic transformation, so that learning and prediction are performed with a
latent Gaussian process
. In spatial statistics this approach is known as Gaussian anamorphosis
[
5
,
6
]
.

\kappa
-logarithm warping

Herein, we study a warping transform implemented by means of the
\kappa
-logarithm, assuming that the resulting latent process
{{Y}}(\mathbf{s};\vartheta)
is a stationary Gaussian process with mean
\mu
, variance
\sigma^{2}
, and covariance kernel
C_{Y}(\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{s}^{\prime})
as in Definition
3
. Furthermore, we allow for contamination of the sample by Gaussian white noise with variance
\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}
.

Prediction in latent space

The latent-space sample vector
\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{S}}\triangleq\left(y_{1},\ldots,y_{N}\right)^{\top}
is obtained by applying the
\kappa
-logarithm to each element of
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{S}}
. For the latent Gaussian process
Y(\mathbf{s};\vartheta)
the marginal predictive distribution function
f_{\ast}(y_{\ast}\,|\,{\mathbf{s}}_{\ast};\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{S}},{\mathbb{S}_{N}})
becomes the conditional Gaussian
\mathcal{N}\left(\mu_{\ast},\sigma^{2}_{\ast}\right)
. The posterior mean,
\mu_{\ast}
, which represents the optimal forecast,
\hat{y}_{\ast}
, is given by

\displaystyle\mu_{\ast}=\mu+\mathbf{C}_{Y}(\mathbf{s}_{\ast},{\mathbb{S}_{N}})\,\left[\,\mathbf{C}_{Y}({\mathbb{S}_{N}},{\mathbb{S}_{N}})+\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}\,\mathbf{I}\,\right]^{-1}\,\left(\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{S}}-\mu\,\boldsymbol{1}\,\right)\,,

(46)

and the posterior variance by

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{\ast}=\sigma^{2}-\mathbf{C}_{Y}(\mathbf{s}_{\ast},{\mathbb{S}_{N}})\,\left[\,\mathbf{C}_{Y}({\mathbb{S}_{N}},{\mathbb{S}_{N}})+\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}\,\mathbf{I}\,\right]^{-1}\,\mathbf{C}_{Y}^{\top}(\mathbf{s}_{\ast},{\mathbb{S}_{N}})\,.

(47)

In the above, the following notation is used:

•

\mathbf{C}_{Y}(\mathbf{s}_{\ast},{\mathbb{S}_{N}})
is the
1\times N
kernel vector with elements
C_{Y}(\mathbf{s}_{\ast},\mathbf{s}_{i})
,
i=1,\ldots,N
,

•

\mathbf{C}_{Y}({\mathbb{S}_{N}},{\mathbb{S}_{N}})
is the
N\times N
kernel matrix with elements
C_{Y}(\mathbf{s}_{i},\mathbf{s}_{j})
,
i,j=1,\ldots,N
,

•

\mathbf{I}
is the
N\times N
identity matrix,

•

\boldsymbol{1}
is the
N\times 1
vector of ones.

Prediction in observation space

Since the marginal predictive distribution
f(y_{\ast}\,|\,{\mathbf{s}}_{\ast};\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{S}},{\mathbb{S}_{N}})
is Gaussian,
\mu_{\ast}
represents the mean, median, and mode of
f(y_{\ast}\,|\,{\mathbf{s}}_{\ast};\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{S}},{\mathbb{S}_{N}})
and the unequivocal optimal prediction in warped space. The predictive marginal PDF in the observation space is the following
\kappa
-lognormal, where
x_{\ast}\triangleq\exp_{\kappa}(y_{\ast})

f_{\ast}(x_{\ast}\,|\,{\mathbf{s}}_{\ast};\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{S}},{\mathbb{S}_{N}})=\frac{1}{2\,\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_{\ast}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\left(\ln_{\kappa}(x)_{\ast}-\mu_{\ast}\right)^{2}/2\sigma_{\ast}^{2}}\,\left(x_{\ast}^{\kappa-1}+x_{\ast}^{-\kappa-1}\right)\,.

(48)

The principle of
quantile invariance
affirms that the quantiles of a probability distribution remain invariant under a monotonic transformation. Hence, if we assume that (i)
Y(\vartheta)=g\left(X(\vartheta)\right)\overset{d}{=}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, (ii)
y_{\alpha}
is the
\alpha\in[0,1]
quantile, that is,
\Phi(y_{\alpha})=\alpha
, then
F_{\!{}_{X}}(x_{\alpha})=\alpha
where
x_{\alpha}=g^{-1}(y_{\alpha})

[
6
]
. For
\kappa
-lognormal processes,
g(\cdot)=\ln_{\kappa}(\cdot)
and
g^{-1}(\cdot)=\exp_{\kappa}(\cdot)
. Since
\Phi(\mu_{\ast})=0.5
, quantile invariance implies that the predictor
\hat{x}_{\ast}\triangleq\exp_{\kappa}(\mu_{\ast})
corresponds to the median of the predictive distribution (
48
).
In warped space, the
prediction interval
at confidence level
(1-\alpha)\times 100\%
for
0<\alpha<1
is
\left[\,\mu_{\ast}-z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\sigma_{\ast},\;\mu_{\ast}+z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\sigma_{\ast}\,\right]

where
z_{1-\alpha/2}=\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha/2)
is the
(1-\alpha/2)\times 100\%
quantile of the standard normal distribution. For example, if
\alpha=0.05
,
z_{1-\alpha/2}=\Phi^{-1}(0.975)=1.96
. Then, based on the principle of quantile invariance, the respective
prediction interval in observation space
is given by

\left[\,g^{-1}\left(\mu_{\ast}-z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\sigma_{\ast}\right),\;g^{-1}\left(\mu_{\ast}+z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\sigma_{\ast}\right)\,\right]\,.

(49)

Remark 8
(Mode predictor)

Even if
X(\mathbf{s};\vartheta)\overset{d}{=}\kappa{\rm LN}(\mu,\sigma,\kappa)
, the predictive distribution could be approximately Gaussian, namely,

f_{\ast}(x_{\ast}\,|\,{\mathbf{s}}_{\ast};\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{S}},{\mathbb{S}_{N}})\overset{d}{=}\mathcal{N}(x_{\ast}\,,\sigma_{\ast}^{2})
. As discussed in Section
III-A
(see Fig.
3
), certain combinations of
(\mu,\sigma,\kappa)
lead to nearly Gaussian PDF. In this case, the median predictor
X
at
{\mathbf{s}}_{\ast}
, that is,
\hat{x}_{\ast}\triangleq\exp_{\kappa}(\mu_{\ast})
is adequate.
If, on the other hand,
f_{\ast}(x_{\ast}\,|\,{\mathbf{s}}_{\ast};\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{S}},{\mathbb{S}_{N}})
is significantly skewed, the mode predictor could be of interest

\hat{x}_{\rm mode}(\mathbf{s}_{\ast})={\mathrm{argmax}}_{x_{\ast}\in\mathbb{R}}\,f_{\ast}(x_{\ast}\,|\,{\mathbf{s}}_{\ast};\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{S}},{\mathbb{S}_{N}})={\mathrm{argmax}}_{\{x_{\ast;r}\}_{r=1}^{R}}f_{\ast}(x_{\ast;r}\,|\,{\mathbf{s}}_{\ast};\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{S}},{\mathbb{S}_{N}})\,.

(50)

where
x_{\ast;r}
for
r=1,\ldots,R
, represent the
\kappa
-th root of the zeros of the characteristic polynomial
p_{1}(z)
(cf. Theorem
1
).

V
Estimation of
\kappa
-lognormal Parameters

V-A

Estimating the Marginal Distribution

Marginal likelihood

Maximizing the likelihood of the marginal
\kappa
-lognormal distribution with respect to the parameters given
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{S}}
is equivalent to minimizing the respective negative log-likelihood (NLL).
Based on (
22
), the NLL (normalized by the sample size
N
) for the marginal
\kappa
-lognormal distribution is given by

\mathrm{NLL}({\boldsymbol{\beta}})=\ln\left(2\,\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma\right)+\frac{1}{2N\,\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\ln_{\kappa}(x_{i})-\mu\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\,\ln\left(x^{\kappa-1}_{i}+x^{-\kappa-1}_{i}\right)\,,

(51)

where
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=(\mu,\sigma,\kappa)^{\top}
.
Respectively, the estimates of the optimal parameters
\hat{\mu},\hat{\sigma},\hat{\kappa}
are given by

\left(\hat{\mu},\hat{\sigma},\hat{\kappa}\right)=\arg\min_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}\,\mathrm{NLL}({\boldsymbol{\beta}})\,.

(52)

Gradient of NLL

The gradient
\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}\mathrm{NLL}({\boldsymbol{\beta}})=\left(\frac{\partial\mathrm{NLL}({\boldsymbol{\beta}})}{\partial\mu}\,,\frac{\partial\mathrm{NLL}({\boldsymbol{\beta}})}{\partial\sigma}\,,\frac{\partial\mathrm{NLL}({\boldsymbol{\beta}})}{\partial\kappa}\right)^{\top}
of the NLL (
51
) involves the following partial derivatives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathrm{NLL}({\boldsymbol{\beta}})}{\partial\mu}=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{N\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\ln_{\kappa}(x_{i})-\mu\right)\,,

(53a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathrm{NLL}({\boldsymbol{\beta}})}{\partial\sigma}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma}-\frac{1}{N\,\sigma^{3}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\ln_{\kappa}(x_{i})-\mu\right)^{2}\,,

(53b)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathrm{NLL}({\boldsymbol{\beta}})}{\partial\kappa}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N\,\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\ln_{\kappa}(x_{i})-\mu\right)\left[\frac{\ln(x_{i})\,\left(x_{i}^{\kappa}+x_{i}^{-\kappa}\right)}{2\kappa}-\frac{\ln_{\kappa}(x_{i})}{\kappa}\right]-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\ln(x_{i})\left(x_{i}^{\kappa-1}-x_{i}^{-\kappa-1}\right)}{\left(x_{i}^{\kappa-1}+x_{i}^{-\kappa-1}\right)}\,.

(53c)

The system of equations (
53
) that determines NLL stationary points is not in general analytically solvable. Hence, we are forced to seek numerical solutions by means of optimization methods; the latter rely on initial guesses for the parameters. Finally, the NLL Hessian matrix is evaluated in Appendix
E
.

Remark 9
(Initialization of parameter vector)

Note that the point
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{0}\triangleq(\mu_{0},\sigma_{0},\kappa_{0}\,)^{\top}
with
\kappa_{0}=0
,
\mu_{0}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ln(x_{i})
, and
\sigma^{2}_{0}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\ln(x_{i})-\mu)^{2}
is a stationary point of the NLL. It is straightforward to verify that the NLL partial derivatives with respect to
\mu
and
\sigma
vanish at this point according to (
53a
)-(
53b
). It also follows from (
53c
) that the derivative
{\partial\mathrm{NLL}({\boldsymbol{\beta}})}/{\partial\kappa}
also vanishes at
\kappa=0
: since
\lim_{\kappa\to 0}x_{i}^{\kappa}=\lim_{\kappa\to 0}x_{i}^{-\kappa}=1
, it follows that
\lim_{\kappa\to 0}\frac{1}{2\kappa}\left[\,\ln(x_{i})\,\left(x_{i}^{\kappa}+x_{i}^{-\kappa}\right)-2\ln_{\kappa}(x_{i})\right]=0
and
\lim_{\kappa\to 0}\left(x_{i}^{\kappa-1}-x_{i}^{-\kappa-1}\right)=0
; thus, both summands participating in
{\partial\mathrm{NLL}({\boldsymbol{\beta}})}/{\partial\kappa}
vanish. Therefore, using
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{0}
as initial guess in a gradient descent or a naive Newton’s algorithm will lead to the lognormal distribution.

V-B

Estimating Joint Dependence

A
\kappa
-lognormal process
{X}(\mathbf{s};\vartheta)
, as specified in Definition
3
, involves the parameter triplet
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=(\mu,\sigma,\kappa)^{\top}
of the marginal PDF, and the parameters
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
of the covariance kernel
C_{Y}(\mathbf{r};{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
associated with
Y(\mathbf{s};\vartheta)=\ln_{\kappa}\left[X(\mathbf{s};\vartheta)\right]
. MLE requires optimization of the following NLL function

\displaystyle\mathrm{NLL}({\boldsymbol{\beta}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\ln\left[\sigma^{2\,N}\det\boldsymbol{\rho}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\right]+\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2\,N}}\left(\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{S}}-\mu\,\mathbf{I}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\rho}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\,\left(\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{S}}-\mu\,\mathbf{I}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{N}{2}\ln 2\pi-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ln\left(x_{i}^{\kappa-1}+x_{i}^{-\kappa-1}\right)\,,

(54)

where
\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{S}}=\ln_{\kappa}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{S}})
,
\mathbf{I}
is an
N\times 1
vector of ones and
\boldsymbol{\rho}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
is the
N\times N
correlation matrix such that
\rho_{i,j}\triangleq C_{Y}(\mathbf{s}_{i}-\mathbf{s}_{j})/\sigma^{2}
. Based on numerical experiments, we observed that optimization of the parameter vector
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}=({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top},{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\top})^{\top}
by means of local optimization methods can lead to sub-optimal results. For certain problems (e.g., when estimating parameters for an ensemble of many realizations), global optimization methods may be prohibitively expensive. The estimation procedure that we propose employs profile likelihood and involves the following steps:

1.

Determine the parameters
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\triangleq\left(\mu,\sigma,\kappa\right)^{\top}
using maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) to fit the data to the marginal
\kappa
-lognormal distribution (cf. Section
V-A
). This step is sufficient if only the marginal dependence is of interest.

2.

Generate the transformed dataset
{\mathbf{\hat{y}}_{\mathrm{S}}}\triangleq\left(\ln_{\kappa}(x_{1}),\ldots,\ln_{\kappa}(x_{N})\right)^{\top}
by means of the
\kappa
-logarithm transformation with
\kappa=\hat{\kappa}
.

3.

Finally, keep
\hat{\kappa}
fixed and apply MLE to estimate optimal parameters
\mu,\sigma,{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
using
\mathbf{\hat{y}}_{\mathrm{S}}
.

VI-A

Estimation of Marginal Distribution from Simulated Data

To generate independent
\kappa{\rm LN}(\mu,\sigma,\kappa)
variates, we generate a
N\times 1
random vector
\mathbf{y}
drawn from
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and then apply the
\kappa
-exponential as follows to generate synthetic data

\mathbf{x}=\exp_{\kappa}(\mu+\sigma\mathbf{y})\,.

We then estimate the
\kappa{\rm LN}(\mu,\sigma,\kappa)
parameters from the samples using MLE and the simpler quantile fitting (described below).

MLE

We propose the following strategy to determine initial guesses for the parameters: (i) First, we select an initial
\kappa_{0}
; if the histogram shows signs of bimodality, we set
\kappa_{0}>1
. (ii) Then, we calculate the sample-based median,
\hat{\mu}_{0.5}
, and estimate
\mu_{0}
by inverting (
23
), which yields
\mu_{0}=\ln_{\kappa}(\hat{\mu}_{0.5},\kappa)
. (iii) Finally, we estimate
\sigma_{0}
by taking advantage of (
25
) which connects
\sigma
to the quantiles of the distribution, assuming
\mu
and
\kappa
are known. The initial guess becomes

{\sigma}_{0}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{\ln_{\kappa}\left(\hat{Q}(p_{m}),\kappa_{0}\right)-{\mu}_{0}}{\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{erf}^{-1}(2p_{m}-1)}\,,

(55)

where
\hat{Q}(p_{m})
are sample-based quantiles for
M
arbitrarily selected probability levels
p_{m}
.
Herein, we use
N=10^{3}
and
p_{m}=0.01\,m
, where
m=1,\ldots,M=99
.

The MLE solution is derived using the multistart global optimization method
[
69
]
(in the Matlab
GlobalSearch
function). In deriving the results shown in Fig.
11
and Table
II
, we use as initial guess
\kappa_{0}=1.5
, while
\mu_{0}
and
\sigma_{0}
are calculated as described above.

Quantile Fitting

This procedure aims to estimate the
\kappa{\rm LN}(\mu,\sigma,\kappa)
parameters by matching the empirical and model-based quantiles. It provides a computationally easier optimization problem than MLE. We estimate the optimal parameters by minimizing the following univariate cost function

u(\kappa)=\sqrt{\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left[\hat{Q}(p_{m})-Q\left(p_{m};\hat{\mu}(\kappa),\hat{\sigma}(\kappa),\kappa\right)\right]^{2}}\,,\,\kappa\in[0,\kappa_{\max}]\,,

(56)

where
\hat{\mu}(\kappa)=\ln_{\kappa}(\hat{\mu}_{0.5},\kappa)
and
\hat{\sigma}(\kappa)
is obtained by means of

\hat{\sigma}(\kappa)=\left[\ln_{\kappa}(\hat{Q}(0.95),\kappa)-\hat{\mu}(\kappa)\right]/{\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{erf}^{-1}(0.9)}
, that is from (
25
) using the empirical 95% quantile. The optimal parameter vector is then given by

\hat{\kappa}=\arg\min_{\kappa\in[0,\kappa_{\max}]}u(k)
, while
\hat{\mu}
,
\hat{\sigma}
are obtained by means of
\hat{\mu}=\ln_{\kappa}(\hat{\mu}_{0.5},\hat{\kappa})
and by replacing
\kappa_{0}
with
\hat{\kappa}
and
\mu_{0}
with
\hat{\mu}
in (
55
) to obtain
\hat{\sigma}
.

VI-C

Time series forecasting using
\kappa
-lognormal processes

This section investigates forecasting for stochastic processes
\{X(t;\vartheta)\}
defined by means of
X(t;\vartheta)=\exp_{\kappa}\left[Y(t;\vartheta)\right]
,
where
\{Y(t;\vartheta)\}
is a scalar, real-valued Gaussian stochastic process with mean
\mu
, variance
\sigma^{2}
and correlation kernel
\rho_{Y}(\tau\,;\,{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
;
t
is the time index and
\tau=t-t^{\prime}
is the time lag. The covariance kernel is given by
C_{Y}=\sigma^{2}\,\rho_{Y}
. Then, according to Definition
3
,
\{X(t;\vartheta)\}
is a
\kappa
-lognormal stationary stochastic process. We conduct forecasting in the framework of warped Gaussian process regression (cf. Section
IV-B
).

Simulation method

We use the multivariate normal (MVN) simulation method
[
70
, p. 50]
to generate samples of
\{X(t;\vartheta)\}
, namely time series comprising
N
times
t_{k}\in[0,T]
, for
k=1,\ldots,N
. The temporal dependence is determined by the
N\times N
covariance
matrix
{\mathbf{C}}_{Y}
with elements
[\mathbf{C}_{Y}]_{k,l}={C_{Y}}(t_{k}-t_{l})
, for
k,l=1,\ldots,N
, where
C_{Y}(\tau)
is the covariance kernel.
If
{\mathbf{C}}_{Y}={\bf A}{\bf A}^{T}
is a factorization of
{\bf C}_{Y}
, and
{\bf z}
is an
N\times 1
vector of independent random numbers from
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, the
N\times 1
vector
{\bf y}={\bf A}\,{\bf z}
is a realization of a zero-mean normal stochastic process with covariance kernel
C_{Y}(\cdot)
. Then,
\mathbf{x}=\exp_{\kappa}(\mu+\sigma\mathbf{y})
provides a realization of the
\kappa{\rm LN}(\mu,\mathbf{C}_{Y},\kappa)
stochastic process with joint PDF given by (
45
). We use the
principal square root factorization of
\mathbf{C}_{Y}
for numerical stability
[
71
]
.

Study design

We follow the procedure described below.

1.

N_{\mathrm{sim}}=500
realizations are generated using the MVN method. Each realization represents a correlated sequence sampled at times
t_{n}=n
where
n=1,\ldots N
and
N=2^{10}
. The
\kappa
-lognormal marginal is determined by the parameter vector
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=(\mu,\sigma,\kappa)
, where
\mu=1
,
\sigma=1
,
\kappa=3
. As shown in Figs.
4
and
11
, this combination of parameters generates a bimodal distribution, which is clearly non-Gaussian.
C_{Y}(\tau;{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\sigma)
is given by the linear damped harmonic oscillator (LDHO) kernel
[
72
]
in the underdamped regime

C_{Y}(\tau;{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\sigma)=\sigma^{2}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\lvert\tau\rvert/(2\tau_{c})}\left[\cos(\omega_{d}\tau)+\frac{1}{2\omega_{d}\tau_{c}}\sin(\omega_{d}\lvert\tau\rvert)\right]\,,

(57)

where
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=(\tau_{c},\omega_{d})^{\top}
,
\tau_{c}=30
is the relaxation (damping) time and
\omega_{d}=2\pi/50\approx 0.126
is the natural frequency of the damped oscillations.

2.

Each realization is split into a training set containing
N_{\rm tr}=973
data points,
N_{\rm tr}=\lfloor 0.95\,N\rfloor
, and a test set that comprises the remaining
N_{\rm te}=51
points.

3.

The model parameter vector
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\triangleq({\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\top},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top})^{\top}
is estimated over the training set using MLE as outlined in Section
V-B
.

4.

The optimal model, specified by
\hat{{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}}=(\hat{\mu},\hat{\sigma},\hat{\kappa},\hat{\omega}_{d},\hat{\tau}_{c})
is used to forecast the series at the test-set times
t_{N_{\rm tr}+k}
,
k=1,\ldots,N_{\rm te}
. We employ the multi-step (multiple output) forecasting strategy (without recursive feedback of predictions into the training set). Both median-based and mode-based predictions are used, respectively, given by the median of the predictive marginal PDF (
48
) and the mode predictor (
50
).

5.

We quantify the forecasting performance using different cross validation measures calculated by comparing the forecasts to the true test values. Finally, we average the cross-validation measures over the
N_{\mathrm{sim}}
realizations.

Parameter estimation

We apply MLE as described in Section
V-B
to estimate

{\boldsymbol{\beta}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
.
As initial estimates for
\mu
and
\sigma
we use the values obtained from the marginal NLL minimization, while
\hat{\tau}_{c;0}=T/2
and
\hat{\omega}_{d;0}=2\pi/(T/10)
are used for
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
.
The distribution of the ML parameter estimates for the ensemble of 500 realizations is summarized in the violin plots of Fig.
15
. Each plot shows the distribution of the ratio
\hat{\theta}_{i;n}/\theta_{i}
, where
\hat{\theta}_{i;n}
is the estimate of the
i
-th parameter from the set
(\mu,\sigma,\kappa,\tau_{c},\omega_{d})
based on the
n
-th realization (
n=1,\ldots,500
), and
\theta_{i}
is the true value. We excluded 31 realizations that produce unrealistic cyclical frequency estimates
\hat{\omega}_{d}>1
. As evidenced in these plots, the medians of
\hat{\sigma}
and
\hat{\omega}_{d}
, respectively, underestimate the true standard deviation and relaxation time. We suspect that this is due to the fact that the persistence of correlations, as expressed by the ratios
N/\tau_{c}\approx 34
and
N/T_{d}=N\omega_{d}/2\pi\approx 20.5
, does not allow fully resolving the variability of
X(t;\vartheta)
. For computational reasons, NLL optimization is conducted using local search with the interior-point algorithm. Hence, such large
\hat{\omega}_{d}
are likely due to local optima of the NLL function.

Multi-step forecasts are generated using both the median and mode predictors. The median predictor is based on
\hat{\mu}_{0.5}\triangleq\exp_{\kappa}(\mu_{\ast})

\mu_{\ast}
is the conditional mean of the latent Gaussian process at
{\mathbf{s}}_{\ast}
given by (
46
). The mode predictor is based on (
50
).
The
cross-validation (CV)
measures involve the following: Mean error (ME); mean absolute error (MAE); mean absolute relative error (MARE); root mean square error (RMSE); relative RMSE (RRMSE); Pearson correlation coefficient (
R

VII

Conclusion

This paper investigates the statistical properties and the estimation of the
\kappa
-lognormal probability distribution, where
\kappa>0
is a parameter that controls the shape and the asymptotic behavior of the distribution’s right tail. This work also presents a
\kappa
-lognormal stochastic process. We show that in the limit
\kappa=0
the standard lognormal distribution is recovered. For
\kappa>0
, we obtain probability distributions with flexible, lighter-than-lognormal right tails. Hence, the
\kappa
-lognormal family provides a unifying model which includes the standard lognormal as well as bimodal distributions, and it also approximates quite closely the normal distribution.
Such versatility can be useful in practice. For example, simulation-based gene expression studies find both lognormal and normal protein number distributions, which switch to bimodality by introducing a DNA looping mechanism
[
75
]
. The
\kappa
-lognormal is a candidate model for such applications. On the other hand, bimodal distributions are characterized by the location, height and width of the two peaks as well as the overall asymmetry of the distribution. It is unlikely that the three-parameter
\kappa
-lognormal distribution can adequately adapt to all of these features, but it provides a starting point for further investigation.

We present closed-form expressions for the main statistical functions (PDF, CDF, quantile function, hazard rate) of the
\kappa
-lognormal distribution and for the median. The presence of bimodality in regions of the parameter space
(\mu,\sigma,\kappa)
is established using theoretical analysis and numerical calculations. We obtain the hazard rate for the
\kappa
-lognormal distribution, and we show that for (
\kappa<1
)
\kappa>1
it is an asymptotically (decreasing) increasing function. This adaptability of the
\kappa
-lognormal renders it more suitable than the lognormal for failure-time analysis. We also derive (i) lower and upper bounds for the statistical moments in terms of the error function and hypergeometric functions and (ii) power series expansions for the moments which involve a recursive equation for the coefficients. Finally, we prove the scaling relation (
28
) which implies that moments of any positive order are obtained form the first-order moment, if the latter is known for all
(\mu,\sigma,\kappa)
.

We define
\kappa
-lognormal stochastic processes and focus on practical aspects such as model parameter estimation and simulation (generation of states).
We also show how to exploit the
\kappa
-lognormal distribution in the framework of warped Gaussian process regression. We develop predictors based on both the median and the mode of the conditional
\kappa
-lognormal distribution, and we present case studies that involve time series forecasting and interpolation of spatial data.

Even though we addressed the main goal set forth at the beginning of the paper, namely to develop a deformed lognormal probability distribution with flexible upper tail for asymmetric data, the advances presented herein raise additional questions for future exploration. Some of these challenges are briefly described below.

Lognormal deformations

Besides the PDF (
22
), other deformations of the lognormal distribution are in principle possible; the resulting modified distributions do not necessarily exhibit shorter-than-lognormal tails. For example, the exponential term that appears in the standard lognormal PDF (
22
) could be replaced by
\left[1-\frac{1}{\kappa^{\prime}}\left(\ln(x)-\mu\right)^{2}/2\sigma^{2}\right]^{\kappa^{\prime}}
; this function recovers the natural exponential term in the lognormal PDF at the limit
\kappa^{\prime}\to\infty
. Using suitable normalization, the above leads to the so-called log-kappa distribution
[
76
]
. The latter tends to the standard lognormal for
\kappa\to\infty
but presents heavier-than-lognormal tails for finite
\kappa
. The log-kappa model describes the observed distributions of magnetic field and plasma fluctuations in the
solar wind more accurately than the lognormal.

A PDF with two deformation parameters can be obtained by applying both the
\kappa
-exponential and
\kappa
-logarithm deformations leading to

f_{X}(x)=\frac{1}{Z}\,\exp_{\kappa^{\prime}}\left[-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\left(\ln_{\kappa}(x)-\mu\right)^{2}\right]\,

(59)

where
\kappa,\kappa^{\prime}>0
are two deformation parameters that satisfy
\kappa^{\prime}<2\kappa
and
Z>0
is the normalization factor. The right tail of this distribution behaves as a power-law, that is,

f_{X}(x)\sim\frac{(2\sigma^{2})^{1/\kappa^{\prime}}}{Z}\left(\frac{2\kappa}{\kappa^{\prime}}\right)^{1/\kappa^{\prime}}\,x^{-2\kappa/\kappa^{\prime}}\,,\;\mbox{for}\;x\to\infty\,.

(60)

In the special case
\kappa^{\prime}\to 0
, the deformed PDF (
59
) has some interesting properties: (1) For
\kappa=0
, the PDF (
59
) becomes the lognormal. (2) For
\kappa>0
and
x\sim 0
, the PDF is approximately lognormal, because
\ln_{\kappa}(x)\approx\ln(x)
for
x\approx 0
. (3) For
\kappa>0
and
\ln_{\kappa}(x)-\mu\gg\sigma
, it follows from (
11
) that
f_{X}(x)\sim\frac{1}{Z}\,\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2\kappa}}{8\sigma^{2}\kappa^{2}}\right)
if
\kappa>0
. (3a) If
0<2\kappa<1
, the PDF exhibits stretched exponential upper tail; (3b) for
\kappa=1
, the upper tail of the PDF is the same as for the normal PDF with zero mean and variance
4\sigma^{2}
.

Note, however, that the above deformations are not obtained by means of a closed-form transformation function
g(\cdot)
such that the transformed variable
Y(\vartheta)=g(X(\vartheta))
is normally distributed. Hence, in contrast with the
\kappa
-lognormal distribution presented herein, PDFs such as (
60
) and the log-kappa PDF do not allow straightforward extension to respective joint densities by means of Jabobi’s multivariate theorem (cf. Theorem
5
). Such lognormal deformations are therefore more suitable for single-point characterization of the underlying variables but not easily extended to a stochastic process framework.

Joint
\kappa
-lognormal densities

In addition to the joint lognormal PDF derived from the respective normal PDF and Jacobi’s multivariate theorem, several other multivariate lognormal distributions have been proposed. Such distributions do not possess a lognormal marginal but have applications in the analysis of insurance data
[
77
]
. Could the deformed exponential and logarithm functions be used to derive generalizations of such distributions?

Relation with Central limit theorem?

It is well known that by virtue of the Central limit theorem (applied in the domain of logarithms), the lognormal model represents the asymptotic distribution for multiplicative averaging, namely for the
N
-th root of the product of
N
independent, identically distributed random variables. One can then ask whether the
\kappa
-lognormal distribution appears as a pre-asymptotic form of multiplicative averaging for finite
N
or as a fixed point if the aggregated processes have long-range correlations.

Appendix B

Proof of the Moment Scaling Relation

This section presents the proof of Theorem
2
.

Proof:

The
\ell
-th order non-centered moment of a
\kappa
-logarithmic distribution is defined by means of (
65
)

m_{X;\ell}(\kappa;\mu,\sigma)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\,dx\,x^{\ell}\,f_{X}(x)\,,

(65)

with
f_{X}(x)
the PDF of the
\kappa
-lognormal distribution given in (
22
).
Using the fact that
Y=\ln_{\kappa}X\overset{d}{=}\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
and the conservation of probability under the nonlinear, monotone
\kappa
-exponential transform
X=\exp_{\kappa}(Y)
, the integral (
65
) becomes

m_{X;\ell}(\kappa;\mu,\sigma)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\,dy\,g^{\ell}(y)\,f_{Y}(y;\mu,\sigma^{2})\,=\mathbb{E}_{Y}\left[\left(\exp_{\kappa}Y\right)^{\ell}\right],

(66)

where
f_{Y}(y;\mu,\sigma^{2})=\frac{1}{\sigma}\phi(\frac{y-\mu}{\sigma})
is the PDF of the normally distributed
Y
.
Hence, the moment of order
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
is given by

\displaystyle m_{X;\ell}(\kappa;\mu,\sigma)\triangleq

\displaystyle\,\mathbb{E}_{Y}\left[\left(\exp_{\kappa}Y\right)^{\ell}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{Y}\left[\exp_{\kappa^{\prime}}(\ell\,Y)\,\right]\,.

(67)

The second equation is a result of the fact that
g^{\ell}(y)=\left[\exp_{\kappa}(y)\right]^{\ell}
and

\left[\exp_{\kappa}(y)\right]^{\ell}=\exp_{\kappa^{\prime}}(\ell\,y)\,,\;\kappa^{\prime}=\kappa/\ell.

(68)

Based on (
67
), the
\kappa
-lognormal moments are obtained from the expectation integral (
27
) which can be easily evaluated by means of numerical integration.

To prove the scaling relation (
28
), we use (
68
) and the variable transformation
\ell y\to\tilde{y}
in order to transform the moment integral (
27
) as follows

\displaystyle m_{X;\ell}(\kappa;\mu,\sigma)=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\,dy\exp_{\kappa/\ell}(\ell y)\,f_{Y}(y;\mu,\sigma^{2})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\,d\tilde{y}\ \exp_{\kappa/\ell}(\tilde{y})\,f_{Y}(\tilde{y};\ell\mu,\ell^{2}\sigma^{2})\,=m_{X;1}(\kappa/\ell;\ell\mu,\ell\sigma)\,,

where in the above
\tilde{Y}\overset{d}{=}\mathcal{N}(\ell\mu,\ell^{2}\sigma^{2})
. This concludes the proof.

Proof of Power-Series Moment Expansions

This section presents the proof of Theorem
4
.

Proof:

We use the moment integral relation (
27
) in combination with the Taylor series expansion of the
\kappa
-exponential around
\mu
. Let us denote
Q(y;\kappa,\ell)\triangleq\exp_{\kappa/\ell}(\ell y)
, so that
Q(y;\kappa,\ell)=\left(\sqrt{1+(\kappa y)^{2}}+\kappa\,y\right)^{\ell/\kappa}

Then the moment integral (

m_{X;\ell}(\kappa;\mu,\sigma)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}Q(y;\kappa,\ell)\,f_{Y}(y;\mu,\sigma)~dy\,.

(81)

The Taylor expansion of

around

Q(y;\kappa,\ell)=Q(\mu;\kappa,\ell)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}Q^{(n)}(\mu;\kappa,\ell)\,\frac{(y-\mu)^{n}}{n\,!}\,,

(82)

where
Q^{(n)}(\mu;\kappa,\ell)=\left.{\dd^{n}Q(\mu;\kappa,\ell)}/{\dd y^{n}}\right|_{y=\mu}\,

Inserting the Taylor expansion in (

m_{X;\ell}(\kappa;\mu,\sigma)=Q(\mu;\kappa,\ell)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\,Q^{(n)}(\mu;\kappa,\ell)\,\mathbb{E}[(Y-\mu)^{n}]\,.

(83)

The centered moments
\mathbb{E}[(Y-\mu)^{n}]
are calculated using the Wick-Isserlis theorem which states that while the odd powers of
\mathbb{E}\left[(Y-\mu)^{n}\right]
are equal to zero, the even powers equal
\delta_{n,2q}\,\frac{\sigma^{2q}(2q)!}{q!\,2^{q}}
, where

[
6
]
.

Next we turn to the derivatives

For
n\geq 1

(84)

with the
g_{n}(\mu;\kappa,\ell)
defined by the recursive formula (
31b
).
It is straightforward to see by direct differentiation of

Q^{(1)}(\mu;\kappa,\ell)=\ell\,Q(\mu;\kappa,\ell)/\sqrt{1+\kappa^{2}\mu^{2}}

\displaystyle Q^{(n+1)}(\mu;\kappa,\ell)=

\displaystyle\frac{\ell^{2}\,g_{n}(\mu;\kappa,\ell)}{\left({1+\kappa^{2}\mu^{2}}\right)^{n}}\,Q(\mu;\kappa,\ell)-\frac{\ell\,\kappa^{2}\,\mu\,(2n-1)g_{n}(\mu;\kappa,\ell)}{\left({1+\kappa^{2}\mu^{2}}\right)^{n+1/2}}\,Q(\mu;\kappa,\ell)

Taylor series expansion of
\kappa
-exponential

The power-series expansion of
\exp_{\kappa}(y)
around
y=0
is given by means of the following expression
[
1
]

\exp_{\kappa}(y)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\xi_{n}(\kappa)\,\frac{y^{n}}{n!}\,,\;\mbox{for}\;(\kappa y)^{2}<1,

(90)

where the
\kappa
-correction functions
\{\xi_{n}(\kappa)\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
are polynomials of
\kappa
defined by the following recurrence relations

\displaystyle\xi_{0}(\kappa)=\xi_{1}(\kappa)=1,

(91a)

\displaystyle\xi_{n}(\kappa)=\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}\left[\,1-(2j-n)\kappa\,\right],\;n>1\,.

(91b)

The polynomials
\xi_{n}(\kappa)
for the first seven orders
n=0,\ldots,6
are given by

\xi_{0}(\kappa)=\xi_{1}(\kappa)=\xi_{2}(\kappa)=1
,
\xi_{3}(\kappa)=1-\kappa^{2}
,
\xi_{4}(\kappa)=1-4\kappa^{2}
,
\xi_{5}(\kappa)=(1-\kappa^{2})(1-9\kappa^{2})
,
\xi_{6}(\kappa)=(1-4\kappa^{2})(1-16\kappa^{2})
.
The first three terms of the Taylor expansion (
90
) coincide with those of the ordinary exponential, i.e.,

\exp_{\kappa}(y)=1+y+\frac{y^{2}}{2}+(1-\kappa^{2})\,\frac{y^{3}}{3!}+\mathcal{O}(\kappa^{2}y^{4}).

The Taylor expansion implies that for
y\to 0
and
\kappa\to 0
the

\kappa
-exponential function
\exp_{\kappa}(y)
converges to the ordinary exponential. More precisely,

\exp_{\kappa}(y)=\exp(y)+\sum_{n=3}^{\infty}\left[\,\xi_{n}(\kappa)-1\right)]\,\frac{y^{n}}{n!}=\exp(y)+\mathcal{O}({\kappa^{2}\,y^{3}}).

(92)

The above is useful in perturbation analysis to express analytically the deviation between the
\kappa
-exponential and
\exp(x)
.
In addition, based on the Taylor expansion (
90
), the sign of the leading corrections of
\exp_{\kappa}(y)
with respect to
\exp(y)
is negative.

\kappa

\displaystyle\xi^{\prime}_{0}(\kappa)=

\displaystyle\xi^{\prime}_{1}(\kappa)=\xi^{\prime}_{2}(\kappa)=0\,,

(93a)

\displaystyle\xi^{\prime}_{n+2}(\kappa)=

\displaystyle\xi^{\prime}_{n}(\kappa)+n^{2}\kappa^{2}\left[1-\xi^{\prime}_{n}(\kappa)\right]\,,\;\mbox{for}\;n\geq 0\,.

(93b)

Furthermore, the leading-order in
\kappa^{2}

\xi^{\prime}_{n+2}(\kappa)

\displaystyle L(\xi^{\prime}_{n+2}(\kappa))=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\kappa^{2}\left[\,2^{2}+4^{2}+\ldots(2k)^{2}\right]\,,&\mbox{if}\;n=2k\,,\\[4.30554pt]

S4. Application to Berea Permeability Dataset

This section focuses on the application of warped-GPR to the Berea permeability dataset using a different covariance kernel for the latent Gaussian process.

We assume the latent Gaussian process has an anisotropic Matérn covariance kernel which is given by

C_{Y}({h})=\frac{\sigma^{2}\,2^{1-\nu}}{\Gamma(\nu)}\Bigg(\frac{\sqrt{2\nu}\,h}{\xi}\Bigg)^{\nu}K_{\nu}\Bigg(\frac{\sqrt{2\nu}\,h}{\xi}\Bigg)\,,

(98)

where
h\triangleq\sqrt{\mathbf{r}^{\top}{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}\,\mathbf{r}}
,
\nu
is the smoothness index,
\Gamma(\cdot)
is the Gamma function, and
K_{\nu}(\cdot)
is the modified Bessel function of the second kind of order
\nu
. Two training sets that comprise randomly selected 500 and 1100 points respectively are used. The initial value of the smoothness index is
\nu_{0}=1.5
.

The ML estimated parameters are shown in Table
XII
. For both training sets the ML estimated smoothness index is
\hat{\nu}=0.5
, which resonates with the “rough” spatial profile of the permeability. The larger training set leads to smaller nugget variance
\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}
, correlation scale
\xi
and anisotropy ratio
\rho
.

The cross-validation measures for both training sets are given in Table
XIII
. Only the statistics for the median-based predictor are listed since the mode-based predictor yields almost identical statistics (except for higher ME).
