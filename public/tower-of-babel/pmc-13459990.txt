Exploring Relevance of Graded Sputum Production in Stable COPD in Clinical Practice
Authors: Laurent Morin, Jean-Christian Borel, Nathalie Arnol, Bernard Aguilaniu
Published: 2026

Introduction

Chronic bronchitis (CB) is a frequent comorbidity in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and is associated with more severe respiratory symptoms, reduced functional capacity, an increased risk of exacerbations, and higher mortality.1–3 According to the GOLD 2025 report, the prevalence of CB in large prospective COPD cohorts ranges from 27% to 35%. CB is traditionally defined by chronic sputum production for at least three months per year over two consecutive years. However, this definition relies on retrospective patient recall and clinician interpretation, thereby introducing potential recall and reporting biases. Beyond these methodological limitations, the dichotomous definition of CB may oversimplify a clinically heterogeneous spectrum. Reducing sputum production to a binary trait potentially underestimates its clinical and prognostic significance. A more nuanced and graded characterization of expectoration could therefore contribute to improved phenotypic stratification. Complementary approaches such as computed tomography (CT) imaging to identify distal airway abnormalities (eg bronchial wall thickening or mucoid impaction) and patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) can also capture the multidimensional burden associated with chronic bronchial congestion. These include assessments of health status (COPD Assessment Test, CAT), dyspnoea during daily activities (DIRECT), dyspnoea-related disability (modified Medical Research Council scale, mMRC), and symptoms of anxiety or depression (Hospital Anxiety and Depression scale, HAD).

In an effort to standardize pulmonary consultations across practice settings, a web-based platform (COLIBRI) was implemented and made available to French pulmonologists. This tool provides a structured and harmonized digital consultation shared by pulmonologists in private practice, general hospitals, and university hospitals. The resulting nationwide clinical database has previously enabled the development and validation of a predictive algorithm capable of identifying patients with excessive physical inactivity without the need for objective daily physical activity measurements.4 This prior work illustrates the capacity of structured routine clinical data to reveal clinically meaningful associations and supports the application of this approach to a more granular characterization of sputum production in COPD. After obtaining informed consent, data are prospectively collected using a structured parent–child field architecture and include clinical, environmental, biological, and pulmonary function variables. No exclusion criteria are applied, thereby ensuring the capture of real-world clinical practice. Within this framework, sputum frequency is recorded during routine clinical assessment using three predefined categories: never (no sputum production), occasional (intermittent episodes), or regular (daily or almost daily production), allowing a standardized description of expectoration.

Methods

The objective of the present study was to determine whether a graded assessment of sputum frequency is associated with distinct clinical and physiological phenotypes, and to identify the factors independently associated with regular sputum production in stable COPD patients.

Among 6398 COPD patients enrolled by July 2024, we included stable COPD patients aged ≥35 years with airflow obstruction (FEV1/FVC ≤0.7), excluding those with incomplete spirometry or preserved ratio, exacerbation-related visits, or missing sputum-production fields. Multinomial logistic regression using stepwise selection was used to identify variables independently associated with sputum frequency. Covariates included age, sex, BMI, smoking status, exacerbation history, dyspnea (mMRC), spirometry, hyperinflation indices, comorbidities, and inhaled therapeutic load. Missing data were managed using MCMC multiple imputation; variables with >20% missingness were excluded from multivariate modeling. A sensitivity analysis was performed without replacing missing data.

Results

A first comparison between subgroups (never, occasional and regular sputum) was conducted using univariate analysis: 798 (24.1%) reported regular sputum production, 1117 (33.7%) occasional sputum, and 1394 (42.1%) none. Compared with patients without sputum, those with regular sputum displayed a consistently more severe phenotype. Frequent exacerbators (≥ 2 exacerbations per year) accounted for 41% of regular sputum producers compared with 23% (p<0.001) and 35% (p=0.047) among those without or occasional sputum, respectively. Similarly, severe exacerbations had occurred in 23% vs 16% (p<0.001) of non-sputum producers and 20.5% (p=0.013) of occasional sputum producers.

PROs revealed clear differences in regular sputum producers. The median [Q1-Q3] CAT score was higher for sputum regular producers (19 [15–25]) compared to non-producers (12 [7–18], p<0.001) and occasional producers (16 [11–23], p<0.001), and sputum regular producers also had significantly elevated HAD (12 [7–18] vs 10 [5–15], p<0.001) and DIRECT disability (11 [6–18] vs 8 [4–15], p<0.001) scores compared to non-producers. Additionally, a greater proportion of sputum regular producers experienced significant dyspnea (mMRC ≥2) in 61% versus 47% of non-producers (p<0.001) and 57% of occasional producers (p=0.003). Functional tests showed regular sputum producers had lower predicted FEV1% values (51% compared to 56% in non-producers, p<0.001), lower BMI (24.2 vs 25.3 kg/m2 in non-producers, p<0.001) and higher prevalence of active smoking (53% vs 31% in non-sputum producers, p<0.001 and 37% in occasional sputum producers, p<0.001.

Although COPD patients with regular sputum had more severe symptoms and risks, few received airway clearance treatments. Only 12.4% underwent chest physiotherapy (CPT) vs 6.9% (p<0.001) and 8.7% (p=0.023) in non- and occasional producers, respectively. Only 12.7% participated in pulmonary rehabilitation (no difference between groups). Still, these patients were prescribed more inhaled corticosteroids (ICS), which was evident in their higher use of long‑acting bronchodilators with ICS or even triple inhaled therapy.

In multivariate analyses (Table 1 with p-values), several variables remained independently associated with regular sputum production. Active smoking was the strongest predictor (OR 3.91). Male sex (OR 1.55), lower BMI (OR 0.97 per point), frequent exacerbator phenotype (OR 1.88), and increasing dyspnea severity (mMRC 2–4: OR 2.33–4.63) also remained significant. Interestingly, chest physiotherapy was independently associated with sputum production (OR 1.75) but not pulmonary rehabilitation. Conversely, regular sputum production was associated with a lower likelihood of being treated with non-ICS dual bronchodilation (OR 0.59), and a trend toward increased use of ICS-containing regimens. Sensitive analysis showed very similar results. Discussion

Patients with regular sputum production exhibited a more severe clinical presentation, including greater exacerbation burden, more severe dyspnea, male gender, lower BMI and higher rates of active smoking. This gradient of severity—from no sputum, to occasional, to regular sputum—supports the relevance of sputum frequency as a pragmatic marker of airway dysfunction and disease complexity.

These findings have important clinical implications for risk stratification and personalized management. Regular sputum constitutes a readily identifiable high‑risk subgroup, with nearly double the prevalence of frequent exacerbators and markedly impaired functional and symptomatic status. Airway-clearance strategies remain underutilized, with only a small percentage of patients receiving chest physiotherapy or enrolled in pulmonary rehabilitation. This raises questions about whether clinicians implicitly recognize sputum as a therapeutic target in stable COPD, contrasting with its consideration in the management of acute exacerbations.5,6 The GOLD 2025 report suggests that treatment goals for CB focus on reducing mucus production and viscosity, minimizing hypersecretion by controlling inflammation, improving ciliary transport, and supporting effective cough. Therapies like oscillating positive expiratory pressure (OPEP) can enhance mucus clearance and improve symptoms for people who regularly produce sputum.7 Incorporating a graded sputum evaluation into routine assessments may therefore help guide intensified interventions—including optimized inhaled therapy, structured airway‑clearance programs, smoking cessation efforts, and closer follow‑up—for patients most likely to benefit.

The results align closely with the established CB phenotype described in earlier cohorts2,3 where chronic sputum production consistently correlated with exacerbations, airflow limitation, and reduced quality of life. This report provides new evidence demonstrating that simply grading reported sputum production accurately detects this high-risk phenotype in daily practice. Most likely, the regular addition of self-administered questionnaires could refine this phenotyping.

Several strengths reinforce the robustness of these findings, including the large national cohort, systematic data entry by over 1000 pulmonologists, and rigorous multivariate modeling with imputation of missing data. However, limitations include the cross-sectional design, exclusion of variables with substantial missingness, and limited information on biology such as eosinophil count, oral corticoïde use or imaging correlates.

Conclusion

This large real-life cohort study showed that sputum production, assessed by pulmonologists during routine clinical practice, can enhance the phenotypic characterization of COPD. Regular sputum production affected approximately one-quarter of stable COPD patients and was strongly associated with modifiable factors such as active smoking and low BMI, as well as markers of clinical severity including dyspnoea, exacerbation frequency, lung function impairment, and reduced quality of life. Occasional sputum production showed similar, although less pronounced, associations with exacerbation frequency and dyspnoea severity, supporting the clinical value of grading sputum frequency rather than considering it as a binary feature. These findings suggest that systematic assessment of sputum burden could help clinicians identify patients who may benefit from closer monitoring, intensified smoking cessation and nutritional support, optimized pharmacological management, and targeted airway-clearance strategies. Despite this potential, chest physiotherapy was the main treatment consistently adapted in response to sputum burden, its overall utilization remained low. These findings highlight the urgent need for randomized controlled trials evaluating airway clearance interventions in sputum-producing COPD patients.
