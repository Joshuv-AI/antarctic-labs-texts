Enhanced functional mitochondrial donation through glucose-responsive antioxidant microcarrier-engineered native-like MSCs accelerates diabetic wound healing
Authors: Xiaoxue Yang, Lin Ma, Anqi Liu, Peisheng Liu, Xinyue Cai, Siyuan Fan, Hao Guo, Kun Xuan, Xiaoyao Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiabetes impairs wound healing due to hyperglycemia-induced vascular dysfunction. This condition triggers mitochondrial impairment, leading to ferroptosis in endothelial cells. While mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) can promote tissue repair through intercellular mitochondrial transfer, strategies to enhance their mitochondrial-donating capacity under hyperglycemic conditions remain underdeveloped. Mesenchymal condensation endows MSCs with enhanced regenerative potential and greater mitochondrial functionality. Microcarrier-based three-dimensional (3D) dynamic culture systems mimicking this process offer a promising strategy. However, effectively shielding donor MSCs from hyperglycemia-induced oxidative stress remains a key challenge in microcarrier design. Here, we engineered a glucose-responsive antioxidant biomaterial-based 3D dynamic culture system using chitosan-formylphenylboronic acid (CS-FPBA) microcarriers combined with stem cells from human exfoliated deciduous teeth (SHED) to generate native-like SHED (N-SHED). This system provides a protective niche for transplanted SHED through glucose-triggered antioxidant microcarrier degradation while simultaneously enhancing mitochondrial function and intercellular transfer. Consequently, N-SHED attenuated endothelial ferroptosis, promoted angiogenesis, and accelerated diabetic wound healing in vivo. This study presents a native-like cell culture platform that amplifies the therapeutic efficacy of MSCs by enhancing their mitochondrial-donating capacity. With strong translational potential, this strategy not only advances MSCs-based therapy for diabetic wounds but also offers a novel framework for mitochondrial-targeted regenerative medicine.Keywords: Diabetic wound healing, Three-dimensional dynamic culture system, Glucose-responsive microcarrier, Mitochondrial transfer, Ferroptosis, Stem cells from human exfoliated deciduous teeth

Introduction

Chronic refractory diabetic wounds, a severe complication of diabetes mellitus with high risks of disability and mortality, present a growing public health burden worldwide [1]. Hyperglycemia impairs wound-resident cells and compromises local vascularization, leading to delayed healing. Notably, hyperglycemia-induced mitochondrial dysfunction drives ferroptosis in endothelial cells (ECs), constituting a key mechanism underlying defective angiogenesis [2,3]. Thus, strategies aimed at restoring mitochondrial function in ECs hold promise for accelerating diabetic wound repair.

Mesenchymal stromal cells transplantation has emerged as a promising therapeutic approach for diabetic wounds. Recent studies indicate that mitochondrial transfer serves as a novel paracrine mechanism in MSCs-based therapy [4]. The intercellular transfer of mitochondria from MSCs to damaged cells has been shown to suppress ferroptosis, restore bioenergetic homeostasis, and promote tissue recovery [5]. Our previous work further demonstrated that stem cells from human exfoliated deciduous teeth (SHED), a type of MSCs, transfer mitochondria to endothelial cells and accelerate cutaneous wound healing [6]. However, the hyperglycemic inflammatory microenvironment compromises mitochondrial function and diminishes the regenerative capacity of MSCs. Current MSCs delivery systems also fail to provide adequate protection for transplanted cells within such hostile niches [7,8]. Therefore, developing a delivery platform that enhances the intrinsic regenerative properties of MSCs and facilitates functional mitochondrial transfer is crucial for advancing the translational potential of MSCs-based therapies in diabetic wound management.

During embryonic development, mesenchymal condensation occurs under extreme microenvironments characterized by oxygen and nutrient deprivation, which endows MSCs with enhanced regenerative potential and greater mitochondrial functionality [9]. Consequently, strategies that mimic physiological cell condensation have gained attention for improving MSCs performance in tissue regeneration. Three-dimensional (3D) culture systems—including low-adhesion plates, microcarriers, and 3D-printed scaffolds—closely recapitulate natural cell condensation processes [10]. Among these, microcarrier-based 3D platforms promote multidimensional cell to cell interactions, increase adhesion surface area, and enhance biological properties depending on material composition, offering a promising approach for MSCs expansion and delivery in regenerative medicine [11]. However, most available microcarriers lack the ability to protect MSCs from the oxidative stress induced by hyperglycemia, a key limitation in diabetic applications. The phenylboronic acid (PBA)-based material possesses a unique ability to undergo reversible structural transformations in high-glucose environments, enabling effective reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging [12]. However, synthetic polymers used in constructing PBA-based material systems are biologically inert and often exhibit limited cell adhesion [13,14]. In contrast, chitosan (CS) offers excellent biocompatibility, biodegradability, and intrinsic antioxidant properties [15]. By conjugating PBA groups to CS, we aim to combine the glucose responsiveness of PBA with the cell-friendly and ROS-scavenging features of CS. Nevertheless, it remains unclear whether CS-modified PBA-derived microcarriers can assemble with MSCs in 3D culture to enhance mitochondrial function, promote angiogenesis, and synergistically accelerate diabetic wound healing.

In this study, we developed a glucose-responsive biomaterial-based 3D dynamic culture system using chitosan-formylphenylboronic acid (CS-FPBA) microcarriers to generate native-like SHED (N-SHED). This platform establishes a protective niche for SHED in hyperglycemic conditions, enhances their tissue-repair capacity and functional mitochondrial transfer, and thereby mitigates endothelial ferroptosis, promotes angiogenesis, and accelerates diabetic wound healing. This integrated microcarrier-based 3D culture and delivery strategy synergistically amplifies the therapeutic effect of MSCs, offering a promising avenue for the clinical translation of MSCs-based therapies in diabetic wound management (Scheme 1).

Materials and methods

Preparation and characterization of CS-FPBA

CS-FPBA was synthesized via a Schiff base reaction between the amino groups of chitosan and the aldehyde groups of FPBA. Medium-viscosity chitosan (Macklin, China) was dissolved in 1% acetic acid (Macklin, China) with stirring for 4 h at room temperature. FPBA was then added dropwise, and the pH was adjusted to 5.0 using 1 M HCl. The mixture was stirred for 12 h at room temperature. The resulting product was dialyzed (MWCO 3.5 kDa) against distilled water (pH 5.0) for 48 h, frozen at −80 °C for 6 h, and lyophilized (−50 °C, 0.05 mBar, 48 h) to obtain dry CS-FPBA powder.

For characterization, CS and CS-FPBA were separately dissolved in absolute ethanol and deionized water (1 mg/mL). UV-Vis spectra (200-800 nm) were acquired on a WFZ-26A spectrophotometer (Tianjin Automatic Science Instrument Plant, China). Chemical modification was further confirmed by Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR; Shimadzu, Tokyo) and 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy (Agilent, USA).

Hydrogel formation and CS-FPBA microcarrier synthesis

Hydrogels were formed by dissolving CS-FPBA and Rhodamine B (Solarbio, China) in ultrapure water (pH 7.4). Borate ester bonds formed between the boronic acid groups of FPBA and the cis-diol groups of CS, leading to a crosslinked hydrogel network. The pH of the hydrogel was then adjusted to 5.5 using dilute HCl or NaOH (0.1 M) at room temperature, after which its degradation behavior was evaluated.

Microcarriers were prepared via water-in-oil emulsion. The oil phase contained 5.0 g Span 80 in 250 mL liquid paraffin. The aqueous phase consisted of CS-FPBA conjugate (1 % w/v) dissolved in 50 mL ultrapure water under magnetic stirring. Ethyldimethylaminopropyl Carbodiimide (EDC, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and N-Hydroxysuccinimide (NHS, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) were then added, and the pH was adjusted to 7.4 using 0.1 M NaOH. The aqueous phase was added to the oil phase at 1 mL/min under mechanical stirring (350 rpm, 48 h). The product was washed with hexane (Sangon Biotech, China), ethanol (Sangon Biotech, China) and ultrapure water three times separately, hydrated, frozen at −80 °C, lyophilized, and stored dry.

The morphology and structure of the microspheres were characterized by scanning electron microscope (SEM, Thermo Fisher Quattro S, Thermo Scientific, USA), and their elemental composition was analyzed by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX,S4800, Hitachi, Japan). Degradation was tested under varying pH and glucose concentrations. Antioxidant capacity was measured using an ABTS+ (LEAGENE, China) decolorization assay: microcarriers (1 mg/mL) were incubated with ABTS+ solution for 15 min in the dark, and the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 734 nm (Agilent, USA). In vivo tracking used Rhodamine B-labeled microcarriers in diabetic mouse wounds via an in vivo imaging system (PerkinElmer, USA) to further evaluate its degradation property.

Isolation and culture of SHED

Deciduous dental pulp tissues were collected following informed consent and ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of the Fourth Military Medical University (Approval No. KQ-YJ-2023-164). Freshly extracted pulp tissues were immediately placed in ice-cold sterile saline and transferred to the laboratory within 1 h. After rinsing three times with ice-cold PBS (Servicebio, China), the tissues were minced into approximately 1 mm3 pieces and digested with type I collagenase (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 1.5 h at 37 °C with gentle shaking every 20 min. The digested suspension was centrifuged, and the pellet was resuspended in α-MEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Pricella, China) and 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, USA). Cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere. Upon reaching 80-90% confluence, cells were detached using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Gibco, USA) and subcultured at appropriate seeding densities. SHED from passages 3-4 were used for subsequent experiments.

Construction and characterization of N-SHED

Construction of N-SHED: The synthesized microcarriers (100 mg) were sterilized under ultraviolet light for 6 h. Then microcarriers were transferred into a 125 mL breathable impeller culture flask (mBioR-Ves-125, Regenbio, China), and 20 mL of α-MEM medium containing 10% FBS was added. The flask was then placed on a 3Dmini bioreactor (CytoNiche, China) to allow the microcarriers to swell completely. SHED were seeded into the flask at a concentration of 3 × 106 cells/mL, and the total medium volume was brought to 50 mL. The bioreactor was set to intermittent agitation: 40 rpm for 5 min, followed by 0 rpm for 55 min, repeated over 24 h. After that, the agitation was changed to a constant 40 rpm, and the medium volume was increased to 75 mL. Thereafter, 25 mL of medium was replaced every other day. The resulting N-SHED were used for subsequent experiments. For comparison, N-SHED alone were obtained by digesting the microcarrier cultures with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA for 5 min at 37 °C.

SEM analyze: N-SHED were harvested on day 7, after fixed, dehydrated, and dried to eliminate moisture. After drying, the samples were coated with a thin conductive layer, typically gold or platinum. The prepared samples were then mounted on SEM stubs and placed in a vacuum chamber. The samples were examined under a SEM.

In vivo release analysis of N-SHED: After labeling SHED with DIR (MCE, China) fluorescent dye, DIR-labeled N-SHED was prepared as previously described. Then, pre-labeled N-SHED were then carefully implanted into diabetic skin lesion models. The changes in fluorescence intensity at the wound site were monitored daily using small animal in vivo imaging systems.

Immunofluorescence Staining of N-SHED: N-SHED were collected and fixed with 4% PFA for 30 min. Samples were then washed three times in ice-cold PBS, permeabilized using 0.3% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 15 min and subsequently blocked using 5% BSA at 37 °C for 1 h. The primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4 °C. For the next day, the samples were rewarmed at room temperature for 30 min, washed 3 times with PBS, secondary antibodies were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, samples were sealed with DAPI-containing anti-fluorescence quencher (ab104139, Abcam, UK) after being washed with PBS three times. Images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM, Olympus, Japan) and analyzed using the Image J software. The antibodies used were as follows: anti-Ki67 antibody (#9129, Cell Signaling Technology, USA, 1:200), anti-IGFBP7 antibody (ab171085, Abcam, UK, 1:200), anti-SOX2 antibody (2306s, Cell Signaling Technology, UK, 1:200), anti-Axin2 antibody (ab109307, Abcam, UK, 1:200), anti-RUNX2 antibody (#12556, Cell Signaling Technology, UK, 1:200), anti-Nestin anitibody (#33475, Cell Signaling Technology, UK, 1:200), Donkey anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) Highly Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, Alexa Fluor™ 594 (A21207, Invitrogen, USA, 1:300).

Measurement of cellular metabolic levels: Oxygen consumption rate (OCR), representing mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation capacity, was measured using a Seahorse Extracellular Flux Analyzer (XFe96, Agilent, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. N-SHED and SHED were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well in a 96-well Seahorse XFe96 Cell Culture Microplate (W26224, Agilent, USA) and grown to 80–90 % confluency at the time of measurement. Analysis was performed using the XFe96 analyser as per manufacturer's instructions.

Mitochondria detection in SHED and N-SHED

Mitochondrial staining: N-SHED were stained with MitoTracker Deep Red probe (Beyotime, China) and imaged by super-resolution confocal microscopy (ZEISS Elyra 7, Germany). Mitochondrial morphology was examined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM, HT7800, HITACHI, Japan). Mitochondria were isolated using a cell mitochondrial isolation kit (Beyotime, China). The protein concentration of the isolated mitochondria was quantified with a BCA assay kit (Beyotime, China), followed by western blot analysis to detect the expression of translocase of the Outer Mitochondrial Membrane 20 (Tom20) and cytochrome c oxidase subunit IV (COX IV).

Visualization of mitochondrial transfer in TNTs

To track mitochondrial transfer, SHED were transduced with a Ubc-mitoEGFP-SV40-Puromycin lentivirus to generate stable cells expressing eGFP-tagged mitochondria (designated SHEDGFP and N-SHEDGFP). These cells were co-cultured with AGE-treated ECs for 24 h. After co-culture, cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% PFA, and stained for cytoskeleton using TRITC-phalloidin (Yeasen, Shanghai). Samples were mounted with DAPI-containing antifade medium and imaged by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM). In a transwell-based co-culture experiment, AGE-treated ECs were pre-labeled with MitoTracker Deep Red (designated ECsmito) before co-culture with eGFP-mitochondria-expressing SHED. Following 24 h of co-culture, cells were washed and directly visualized under CLSM.

Detection of mitochondria in extracellular vesicles (EVs)

N-SHED and SHED were cultured in α-MEM supplemented with 10% EVs-depleted FBS for 48 h. The supernatant was sequentially centrifuged at 800 × g for 10 min, 2000 × g for 10 min, and 16,000 × g for 30 min to pellet EVs. The morphology of isolated EVs was analyzed by TEM, and their protein content was characterized by western blot.

Mitochondrial function inhibition of N-SHED

To inhibit mitochondrial function in N-SHED, rotenone (0.5 μM, ROT, MCE, China) and antimycin A (0.5 μM, AA, MCE, China) were added to the culture medium on day 4 and incubated for 24 h. The resulting mitochondrial-impaired N-SHED were then co-cultured with AGE-treated ECs, and ECs functional assays were performed.

Establishment of diabetic cutaneous wound healing model

To investigate diabetic wound healing, eight-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were obtained from the Animal Center of the Fourth Military Medical University and maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions with a 12 h light/dark cycle. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (approval No. 20250161). Diabetes was induced by intraperitoneal injection of streptozotocin (50 mg kg−1 daily for five consecutive days) after overnight fasting. One week after the final injection, fasting blood glucose was measured on two consecutive days; mice with levels consistently >16.7 mmol L−1 were considered diabetic. Pancreatic tissues were randomly collected from diabetic mice for histopathology. For wound healing experiments, diabetic mice were anesthetized and a full-thickness circular wound (10 mm diameter) was created aseptically on shaved dorsal skin. Mice were randomly assigned to three groups (n = 5): control (no treatment), SHED (2D-cultured SHED combined with hydrogel), and N-SHED. Treatments were applied locally. Wounds were photographed on day 0, 4, 7, 10, and 14, and wound area was quantified using ImageJ. Mice were euthanized on day 4, 7, and 14 for tissue collection.

Evaluation of N-SHED-derived mitochondrial transfer in diabetic wound healing

To examine the role of mitochondrial transfer in diabetic wound repair, N-SHED were generated from eGFP-labeled mitochondria-expressing SHED. A subset of these cells was pretreated with rotenone (ROT) and antimycin A (AA) to inhibit mitochondrial function, yielding mitochondrial-impaired eGFP-N-SHED. Both functional eGFP-N-SHED and mitochondrial-impaired eGFP-N-SHED were implanted into full-thickness diabetic wounds. Samples were harvested on day 3 post-implantation for assessment of mitochondrial transfer. In parallel, separate groups of diabetic wounds received either untreated N-SHED or mitochondrial-impaired N-SHED (without eGFP labeling), and wound-healing parameters were evaluated.

Proteomic analysis

Proteins from SHED and N-SHED were extracted to acquire peptides, and the peptides were subjected to LC-MS/MS analysis on a NanoElute UHPLC system (Bruker Daltonics, USA). The raw data were analyzed using the MaxQuant search engine (v.1.6.15.0). Tandem mass spectra were searched against Homo_sapiens_9606_SP_20241202.fasta with the P < 0.05. DEPs (fold change >1.5 and P value < 0.05) were included for further analysis based on GO databases.

RNA-seq analysis

Total RNA was extracted from ECs after different treatment using the MIsZOL Reagent (Mishushengwu, China). RNA quality was assessed with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA) and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA). Sequencing libraries were constructed with the VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit and paired-end 150 bp sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (OE Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Raw fastq reads were trimmed and filtered with fastp to obtain clean reads, which were then aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2. Gene expression levels were calculated as FPKM (fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads), and read counts were generated with HTSeq-count. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to evaluate sample reproducibility. Differential expression analysis was carried out using DESeq2, with differentially expressed genes (DEGs) defined as Q value < 0.05 and fold change >1.5. Results were visualized via volcano plots and hierarchical clustering heatmaps. Finally, GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were conducted on the DEGs.

Statistical analysis

All the data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For two group comparisons, statistical significance was assessed by Student's t-test (two-tailed) while for multiple group comparisons, statistical significance was assessed by one-way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test. Values of P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical and graph analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, USA).

Results

Development and characterization of N-SHED with enhanced regeneration potential

To create a native-like microenvironment for SHED, we synthesized glucose-responsive chitosan-4-formylphenylboronic acid (CS-FPBA) hydrogel microcarriers (∼100 μm) via a Schiff-base reaction (Fig. 1A, S1A). Successful synthesis was confirmed by comprehensive characterization: UV-Vis spectroscopy showed a phenylboronic acid-specific absorption peak at 270 nm (Fig. S1B); FT-IR analysis revealed enhanced vibrations corresponding to C=N (1665 cm−1) and C-N (1370 cm−1) bonds (Fig. S1C); 1H NMR displayed characteristic FPBA benzene proton signals (7.36-7.71 ppm) (Fig. S1D). The resulting microcarriers exhibited a uniform honeycomb-like porous architecture (Fig. S1E). The microcarriers displayed prominent glucose- and pH-responsive degradation: over 80% mass loss occurred within 96 h under high-glucose conditions (Fig. 1B and C), and approximately 80% of encapsulated rhodamine B was released at pH 5.5 (Fig. S1F and G). In vivo tracking in diabetic wounds further demonstrated gradual degradation, with only ∼10% of the fluorescent signal remaining after 4 days (Fig. S1H and I). Therefore, the synthesized CS-FPBA microcarrier exhibits good biosafety. Moreover, the CS-FPBA microcarriers exhibited potent antioxidant activity, scavenging over 52% of ABTS+ radicals within 15 min (Fig. 1D, E, S1J). Critically, DCFH-DA assays confirmed that the microcarriers effectively protected cells from AGE-induced oxidative stress (Fig. S1K–N). These data validate the successful fabrication of glucose-responsive, antioxidant CS-FPBA microcarriers capable of protecting SHED from the hostile diabetic microenvironment.

Next, we isolated SHED from the human exfoliated deciduous tooth pulp and verified its typical characteristics of MSCs, including the colony-forming ability, osteogenic and adipogenic potential, and classical surface markers (Fig. S2A–D). Then native-like SHED (N-SHED) was assembled by seeding SHED onto CS-FPBA microcarriers and culturing them in a stirred tank bioreactor for 5-9 d (Fig. S2E). Light microscopy confirmed that the cells were adhered tightly the microcarriers (Fig. S2F). SEM results revealed that SHED adhered on the porous structure of microcarriers and proliferated rapidly from day 1 to day 9 (Fig. 1F). More importantly, N-SHED showed a more rapid proliferation rate than traditional two-dimensional cultured SHED (Fig. 1G). Proteomic analysis of N-SHED was performed to comprehensive analysis biological characteristics compared to SHED. There are 230 proteins significantly upregulated in N-SHED (Fig. 1H). Further Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed N-SHED toward a pro-proliferative molecular phenotype. The most significantly enriched terms encompassed the entire DNA replication machinery and key regulators of cell cycle checkpoints, particularly the G1/S transition. This was further supported by the upregulation of molecular functions directly involved in chromatin binding, DNA synthesis, and kinase activation. Notably, the upregulation of “structural constituent of skin epidermis” among molecular functions hints at a potential lineage-primed state conducive to tissue repair (Fig. 1I and J). We then confirmed the pro-proliferative, tissue regenerative and differentiation capabilities of N-SHED using immunofluorescence staining, and found that proliferation, development, stemness maintenance, osteogenic and neurogenic differentiation related markers (Ki67, IGFBP7, Axin2, SOX2, RUNX2 and Nestin) were highly expressed in N-SHED group (Fig. 1K and L). Subsequently, we analyzed their angiogenic differentiation potential via western blotting, and the results indicated that they possess superior osteogenic and angiogenic differentiation abilities (Fig. S2G). To further investigate the cell viability of N-SHED in diabetic condition, we labeled cells in N-SHED and SHED group with DiR dye to analyze cell survival time after implantation into diabetic skin lesions. Real-time in vivo small animal imaging results demonstrated that SHED group showed little fluorescence within 5 d post-implantation, while the N-SHED group maintained detectable fluorescence signals for up to 7 d, implying N-SHED could prolong the retention time of cells in extreme microenvironment (Fig. S2H and I). Collectively, these results imply that N-SHED acquire a native-like, highly proliferative status reminiscent of enhanced tissue regeneration potential, and acquired sustained cell viability and release abilities in diabetic condition with the protection of glucose-responsive antioxidant CS-FPBA microcarriers.

N-SHED possess a high capacity for functional mitochondrial transfer

Since mitochondria are the primary energy producers and play a fundamental role in cellular function, we performed a subcellular localization analysis of differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) in the proteomic profiles of N-SHED and SHED. Mitochondrial proteins represented the largest proportion of DEPs (Fig. 2A and B). Specifically, seventeen mitochondrial-associated proteins were upregulated. Among these, enzymes involved in energy production were prominent, such as ACADVL, which participates in fatty acid oxidation, and several components of the electron transport chain. Conversely, seventeen mitochondrial proteins were downregulated, indicating a suppression of anabolic pathways, including one-carbon metabolism—a process closely linked to energy production (Fig. 2C). We next quantified mitochondrial content in SHED and N-SHED. Mito Tracker staining revealed significantly stronger fluorescence intensity in N-SHED (Fig. 2D and E). Consistent with this, total mitochondrial protein levels were higher in N-SHED (Fig. S2J), as was the expression of the canonical mitochondrial markers COX IV and Tom 20 (Fig. S2K), together indicating increased mitochondrial abundance in N-SHED. Furthermore, super-resolution confocal microscopy showed that N-SHED exhibited a greater proportion of elongated, filamentous mitochondria and a larger total mitochondrial area per cell compared to conventional SHED (Fig. 2F–H). Ultrastructural analysis further demonstrated that mitochondrial cristae in N-SHED were denser and more elongated, suggesting enhanced functional capacity (Fig. 2I). Then, the metabolic performance of N-SHED was assessed. The results of the OCR showed a significant increase in mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation levels, with enhanced basal respiration, maximal respiration, and ATP production capacity (Fig. 2J–M). The western blot results also showed that the expression of the mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation chain protein OXPHOS was significantly higher in N-SHED than in SHED (Fig. 2N). Therefore, after three-dimensional culture, N-SHED are in a high metabolic state characterized by enhanced aerobic respiration, indicating significantly improved mitochondrial function, cell proliferation, survival, and tissue repair capacity. Given that mitochondrial transfer via extracellular vesicles (EVs) and tunneling nanotubes (TNTs) represents a key mechanism in MSCs therapy, we examined mitochondrial content in these structures. N-SHED-derived EVs (N-SHED-EVs) contained higher levels of mitochondrial components and mitochondrial-derived proteins (Fig. 2O and P). In addition, using eGFP-labeled mitochondria in SHED, we observed significantly more donor-derived mitochondria within TNTs in the N-SHED group after co-culture with recipient cells (Fig. 2Q). Moreover, increased SHED-derived mitochondrial transfer to recipient cells was detected in co-culture assays both with and without direct cell-cell contact (Fig. 2R and S). Collectively, these findings indicate that N-SHED possess a greater abundance of functional mitochondria and exhibit enhanced intercellular mitochondrial transfer capacity.

N-SHED mitigate high glucose-induced impairment of angiogenesis in ECs via counteracting mitochondrial dysfunction and ferroptosis

Hyperglycemia-induced endothelial cells (ECs) dysfunction is a key driver of impaired wound healing. To systematically characterize ECs dysfunction under hyperglycemia, we performed RNA-seq analysis. GO enrichment revealed a significant downregulation of biological processes related to cell adhesion, proliferation, and angiogenesis (Fig. 3A). We then treated hyperglycemia-conditioned ECs (H-ECs) with N-SHED and repeated transcriptomic profiling. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses demonstrated that N-SHED treatment restored multiple pro-healing processes, including cell adhesion, migration, proliferation, and angiogenesis (Fig. 3B and C). Functional assays confirmed these findings. Scratch-wound and Transwell migration assays showed that advanced glycation end products (AGE) strongly suppressed ECs migration. This impairment was partially rescued by SHED, whereas N-SHED restored migration to near-normal levels (Fig. S3A–D). Similarly, tube-formation assays indicated that high glucose markedly reduced the angiogenic capacity of ECs. While SHED provided partial recovery, N-SHED exerted significantly stronger pro-angiogenic effects (Fig. 3D). Quantitative analysis of tube networks revealed that N-SHED substantially improved all key parameters—vessel number, junctions, branches, and total length—to levels comparable with normal ECs, outperforming SHED (Fig. 3E–H). Given that mitochondrial dysfunction contributes to angiogenic failure in diabetes, and considering the enhanced mitochondrial-donating capacity of N-SHED, we assessed mitochondrial status in treated ECs. Analysis of mitochondrial permeability transition pore (MPTP) opening showed that SHED partially stabilized mitochondrial permeability, whereas N-SHED co-culture significantly improved membrane integrity, restoring it to near-normal levels (Fig. 3I, K, S3F-G). JC-1 staining further indicated that SHED partially restored mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) under high glucose, while N-SHED essentially normalized ΔΨm to baseline (Fig. 3J, L, S3E). Since ferroptosis is closely linked to mitochondrial dysfunction, we evaluated cellular iron metabolism. FerroOrange staining showed that high glucose induced pronounced Fe2+ accumulation, which was partially reduced by SHED and almost completely restored by N-SHED (Fig. 3M, O, S3H-I). Moreover, N-SHED effectively suppressed lipid peroxide accumulation (BODIPY staining) and alleviated oxidative DNA damage (8-OHdG staining) to an extent similar to normal ECs (Fig. 3N, P, S3J-K). Collectively, these results demonstrate that N-SHED effectively rescues mitochondrial function, attenuates ferroptosis, and enhances the angiogenic capacity of endothelial cells under hyperglycemic stress.

The ability of N-SHED to alleviate ferroptosis and protect angiogenesis requires functional mitochondrial transfer

To further investigate the role of mitochondrial transfer from SHED in regulating endothelial cells ferroptosis, we inhibited the major modes of intercellular mitochondrial transfer, including inhibiting EVs secretion with GW4869 and TNTs formation with cytochalasin B (Cyto B) in N-SHED, then assessed ECs function under high-glucose conditions in a co-culture system. JC-1 staining showed that blocking mitochondrial transfer impaired the recovery of ECs mitochondrial membrane potential (Fig. S4A and B). FerroOrange staining confirmed that this inhibition largely abrogated the capacity to reduce labile iron levels (Fig. S4C and D). Similarly, BODIPY staining revealed a marked reduction in the suppression of lipid peroxidation compared to normal N-SHED (Fig. S4E and F). Furthermore, inhibited mitochondrial transfer in N-SHED compromised their ability to restore the angiogenic capacity of ECs under high glucose (Fig. S4G). Quantitative analysis of tube formation revealed no significant improvement in vessel number, junction points, branch points, or total vessel length compared to the control group under high glucose (Fig. S4H–K). After that, we inhibited mitochondrial function in N-SHED using rotenone (ROT) and antimycin A (AA), and then evaluated ECs function under high glucose in a co-culture system. The JC-1 staining results revealed that inhibition of mitochondrial function in N-SHED significantly impaired the restoration of mitochondrial membrane potential in ECs (Fig. 4A and B). FerroOrange staining further confirmed that mitochondrial-impaired N-SHED lost most of its capacity to lower intracellular labile iron levels (Fig. 4C and D). Similarly, BODIPY staining showed a pronounced reduction in its ability to suppress lipid peroxidation compared to functional N-SHED (Fig. 4E and F), which indicated that N-SHED failed to alleviating ferroptosis after inhibiting of mitochondrial function. Furthermore, impaired mitochondrial function in N-SHED compromised their ability to restore the angiogenic capacity of ECs under high glucose (Fig. 4G). Quantitative analysis of tube formation revealed no significant improvement in vessel number, junction points, branch points, or total vessel length compared to the control group under high glucose (Fig. 4H–K). Collectively, these results demonstrate that functional mitochondrial transfer from N-SHED is essential for mitigating ferroptosis and promoting angiogenesis in hyperglycemic conditions.

Mitochondrial transfer from N-SHED is associated with improved angiogenesis and reduced ferroptosis in diabetic wounds

Next, we examined whether mitochondrial transfer from N-SHED occurs in diabetic wounds. A type 1 diabetic mouse model was established via intraperitoneal streptozotocin (STZ) injection, with successful induction confirmed by sustained hyperglycemia (>16.7 mmol/L) and disrupted pancreatic islet morphology (Fig. S5A and B). During the wound healing process, the mice experienced a slight decrease in body weight. Meanwhile, blood glucose levels remained above 16.7 mmol/L throughout the wound healing period after successful model induction (Fig.S5 C, D). Full-thickness cutaneous wounds (10 mm diameter) were created and treated with SHED or N-SHED; tissue samples were harvested on day 7 and 14 post-wounding (Fig. 5A). Immunofluorescence analysis of wound sections showed a pronounced increase in human mitochondria in the N-SHED-treated group. Notably, a greater proportion of these mitochondria localized adjacent to CD31+ host endothelial cells, indicating enhanced mitochondrial transfer to the wound vasculature (Fig. 5B–D). Concurrently, expression of the lipid peroxidation markers 4-HNE and OxC11-BODIPY was significantly lower in N-SHED-treated wounds, consistent with attenuated ferroptosis (Fig. 5E–G). Angiogenesis was evaluated at early (day 7) and later (day 14) stages of repair. Early after injury, the density of CD31+ cells and the formation of CD31+EMCN+ neovessels were significantly higher in the N-SHED group (Fig. 5H–J). At day 14, the proportion of CD31+ cells remained elevated, and the number of mature, stabilized vessels (CD31+α-SMA+) was substantially increased compared with other groups (Fig. 5K–M). Together, these in vivo findings demonstrate that N-SHED delivers functional mitochondria to host endothelial cells in diabetic wounds, thereby suppressing ferroptosis and promoting both early neovascularization and later vessel maturation throughout the healing process.

N-SHED accelerates diabetic wounds healing

We next evaluated the regenerative potential of N-SHED in diabetic wounds. Wound closure was recorded at 0, 4, 7, 10 and 14 d (Fig. 6A), revealing that N-SHED significantly accelerated healing compared to SHED group (Fig. 6B and C). Histological analysis with H&E and Masson's trichrome staining showed that the N-SHED group exhibited faster wound contraction and re-epithelialization at day 4 (Fig. S6A–C), along with increased neovascularization by day 7 (Fig. S6D–F). By day 14, all treatments improved healing compared to the untreated group, with N-SHED showing the most pronounced pro-healing effects (Fig. 6D). Specifically, N-SHED treatment resulted in significantly reduced wound width, increased epidermal thickness, greater hair follicle counts, and enhanced collagen deposition in newly formed tissue (Fig. 6E–H). Histological staining of major organs from mice implanted with the N-SHED showed that after the degradation of microcarriers in vivo, the tissue structures of key organs such as the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys were similar to those of normal mice, with no significant inflammatory response observed (Fig. S6G). Immunofluorescence staining further demonstrated that N-SHED treatment markedly decreased expression of the diabetes-related protein receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE) and the pro-inflammatory cytokine TNF-α in the wound area (Fig. S7A–D), while upregulating the anti-inflammatory factor TGF-β more strongly than other groups (Fig. S7E and F). Additionally, expression of the wound-healing markers Col1A1 and CK19 was significantly elevated following N-SHED administration (Fig. 6I–L). Together, these results indicate that N-SHED mitigates inflammation and promotes tissue repair in diabetic wounds.

Loss of mitochondrial function ablates the ability of N-SHED to diabetic wounds healing

To further investigate the role of functional mitochondrial transfer from N-SHED in diabetic wounds healing, we generated N-SHED using eGFP-labeled mitochondria SHED and inhibited their mitochondrial function with ROT and AA. Three days after treatment, immunofluorescence staining of wound sections showed that eGFP-labeled mitochondria from N-SHED were transferred into the deep dermal region, whereas transfer was markedly reduced in the mitochondrial-inhibited group (Fig. 7A–C). We then applied either functional N-SHED or mitochondrial-impaired N-SHED to diabetic wounds and monitored healing over time (Fig. 7D). Wound closure was significantly delayed in the mitochondrial-inhibited group (Fig. 7E and F). Histological evaluation using H&E and Masson's trichrome staining further demonstrated that mitochondrial impairment substantially compromised tissue regeneration, as evidenced by reduced re-epithelialization and diminished collagen deposition (Fig. 7G–I). Additionally, immunofluorescence staining showed elevated levels of the ferroptosis markers 4-HNE and BODIPY in wounds treated with mitochondrial-impaired N-SHED, indicating aggravated ferroptosis (Fig. 7J–L). Vascularization analysis also revealed a significant reduction in blood vessel density when mitochondrial function was inhibited (Fig. 7M–O). Together, these results demonstrate that functional mitochondrial transfer from N-SHED is essential for attenuating ferroptosis, promoting angiogenesis, and accelerating tissue repair in diabetic wounds.

Discussion

Diabetes, a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by hyperglycemia, induces vascular dysfunction and tissue ischemia, ultimately leading to impaired wound healing, severe infections, and high risk of amputation [[16], [17], [18], [19]]. Mitochondrial dysfunction triggered by hyperglycemia drives ferroptosis in endothelial cells, which represents a key mechanism underlying defective angiogenesis [3,20,21]. Our previous studies have shown that SHED can transfer mitochondria to endothelial cells and promote angiogenesis in healthy settings, the hostile hyperglycemic microenvironment induces oxidative stress and compromises the functionality of transplanted cells [6]. Thus, strategies to preserve and augment the mitochondrial-donating capacity of SHED under diabetic conditions remain unexplored. In this study, we developed native-like SHED (N-SHED) through a glucose-responsive biomaterial-based 3D dynamic culture system. This approach endowed SHED with improved tissue-repair potential and enhanced functional mitochondrial transfer, enabling them to mitigate endothelial ferroptosis, promote angiogenesis, and accelerate diabetic wound healing. The 3D dynamic interaction between the biomaterial and SHED offers a novel strategy to generate energetic, native-like cellular aggregates with potent diabetic wound-healing activity, highlighting the translational potential of this engineered system for managing chronic non-healing wounds.

The diabetic hyperglycemic microenvironment impairs cellular function in both host and transplanted cells by inducing mitochondrial dysfunction, ferroptosis, and excessive ROS accumulation [2,21]. To shield transplanted SHED from ROS-driven damage and provide a scaffold with gradual environment-responsive degradation properties, we developed microcarriers using chitosan and phenylboronic acid as starting materials. These biomaterials are widely employed in hydrogel-based delivery systems owing to their biocompatibility, ease of functionalization, and relevance in controlled-release applications [11,15,22]. Unlike normal wounds, the local hyperglycemic microenvironment in diabetic wounds promotes extensive bacterial proliferation, whose metabolic byproducts can lower the wound pH to 4.5–6.0 [23]. Previous studies have shown that FPBA modified hydrogels degrade under high glucose through competitive displacement of dynamic boronate esters and pH-sensitive hydrolysis [[24], [25], [26]]. Emulsion-synthesized microcarriers based on this chemistry thus enable slow degradation in hyperglycemic or acidic wound environments. Moreover, FPBA demonstrates potent ROS-scavenging activity via boronate-H2O2 reactions and radical-adduct formation, offering therapeutic potential in oxidative-stress-related pathologies [12,27]. Chitosan, as a natural cationic polysaccharide, possesses both biocompatibility and biodegradability [24]. In the present work, CS-FPBA microcarriers were prepared via a Schiff-base reaction. Compared with conventional PBA-based polymers, CS-FPBA exhibited excellent pH sensitivity and achieve the dual functional synergy of “glucose-triggered degradation” and “sustained ROS scavenging”. This enabled controlled release of encapsulated cells, and effectively scavenge ROS in hyperglycemic microenvironments, thereby creating a protective niche for transplanted SHED. Owing to the preliminary amelioration of the inflammatory microenvironment, the subsequently released SHED exhibit enhanced efficacy in facilitating the expression of their inherent pro-regenerative capacities, including proliferation, angiogenesis promotion and collagen synthesis, ultimately establishing a self-reinforcing feedback loop.

SHED, a type of MSCs derived from deciduous tooth pulp, have been widely applied in systemic disease therapies and local tissue repair due to their accessibility, potent regenerative capacity, and anti-inflammatory properties [9,28]. Previous studies have demonstrated the therapeutic efficacy of SHED transplantation in immune-related disorders and dental-bone regeneration [29]. Our earlier work further showed that two-dimensionally cultured SHED aggregates exhibit strong pro-angiogenic potential during pulp regeneration in avulsed teeth [30,31]. More recent evidence indicates that three-dimensionally cultured SHED spheroids can accelerate skin wound healing by promoting angiogenesis [32]. Nevertheless, strategies to effectively harness SHED for wound healing under hyperglycemic conditions remain underexplored. In the present study, we developed N-SHED using a glucose-responsive biomaterial-based 3D dynamic culture system. This approach promoted angiogenesis and accelerated diabetic wound healing more effectively than single-cell administration. Further comparative studies are warranted to clarify whether the healing rate achieved with N-SHED differs significantly from other SHED culture strategies.

The therapeutic properties of MSCs are profoundly shaped by their microenvironment. Conventional two-dimensional culture systems restrict spatial organization and cell-cell interactions, often resulting in limited expansion efficiency, reduced differentiation capacity, and diminished regenerative potential. In recent years, strategies that mimic physiological cell condensation have gained attention for enhancing MSCs function in tissue regeneration [33,34]. Three-dimensional (3D) culture platforms, such as low-adhesion plates, microcarriers, and 3D-printed scaffolds, promote multidimensional cell contacts and improve biological properties, closely resembling natural cell condensation processes [35]. In our previous work, we employed 3D Table Trix microcarriers in a stirred-tank bioreactor to culture SHED, which enhanced their multipotent differentiation potential [6]. However, as collagen-based commercial carriers, they require specific collagenase for degradation and cell harvest. In the present study, we developed glucose-responsive degradable microcarriers that adapt to the hyperglycemic wound microenvironment. This 3D dynamic culture system not only promoted SHED proliferation and multipotency but also enabled sustained cell release through gradual carrier degradation in diabetic wounds. Importantly, the system endowed SHED with active metabolic level and increased functional mitochondrial content, supporting energy-demanding processes such as proliferation and differentiation. The phenotypic and functional attributes of SHED cultured under this glucose-responsive 3D system resemble those of physiologically condensed mesenchymal cells, leading us to term them “native-like SHED” (N-SHED). The underlying mechanisms through which this culture regime confers such native-like properties warrant further investigation.

Mitochondrial transfer has recently emerged as a novel mechanism of intercellular communication, representing a promising therapeutic strategy to restore cellular bioenergetics and mitigate inflammatory responses in damaged tissues [[36], [37], [38]]. MSCs serve as particularly effective mitochondrial donors, owing to their accessibility, expandability, low immunogenicity, and innate ability to mediate mitochondrial transfer through multiple mechanisms, including extracellular vesicles (EVs), gap junctions, cell fusion, and tunneling nanotubes (TNTs) [4,36,39]. Previous studies have established that bone marrow-derived MSCs transfer mitochondria via EVs to ischemic neurons, promoting functional recovery after stroke [40,41]. Our earlier work demonstrated that 3D-cultured SHED exhibit enhanced mitochondrial transfer capacity via TNTs, correlating with accelerated wound healing [6]. In the present study, we observed N-SHED-derived mitochondria or mitochondrial components within TNTs and EVs, and detected N-SHED-mitochondria in recipient endothelial cells following both direct co-culture and transwell-based co-culture systems. Furthermore, in vivo evidence showed N-SHED-derived mitochondria localized near CD31+ cells in diabetic wounds. Together, these findings suggest that N-SHED can transfer functional mitochondria to endothelial cells via EVs and TNTs. Whether additional transfer mechanisms contribute to this process, as well as whether other cell types in the wound microenvironment also receive mitochondrial transfer, remain important questions for future investigation.

Mitochondrial dysfunction and ferroptosis represent key molecular mechanisms underlying diabetic chronic complications [5]. Hyperglycemia is known to disrupt mitochondrial homeostasis, leading to altered membrane permeability and impaired cellular depolarization, while also directly triggering ferroptosis through oxidative stress and dysregulated iron metabolism [17,42]. In this study, elevated iron levels, dysregulated lipid metabolism, and detectable DNA damage collectively confirmed the occurrence of ferroptosis in our cellular model [43]. We further demonstrated that a glucose-responsive biomaterial-based 3D dynamic culture system enhances mitochondrial content in SHED and facilitates efficient mitochondrial transfer from SHED to recipient endothelial cells, thereby alleviating ferroptosis and promoting diabetic wound healing. Importantly, when mitochondrial function in N-SHED was inhibited, its ability to mitigate ferroptosis was significantly attenuated. These results underscore the essential role of functional mitochondrial transfer from N-SHED in regulating ferroptosis under high-glucose conditions. Although direct suppression of mitochondrial transfer would further clarify the causal relationship between transfer and ferroptosis, technical limitations led us to inhibit mitochondrial function in donor cells, which nonetheless substantiates the critical contribution of N-SHED-derived mitochondria in alleviating ferroptosis in hyperglycemic endothelial cells.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed a glucose-responsive antioxidant biomaterial-based 3D dynamic culture system that synergizes CS-FPBA microcarriers with SHED to generate native-like SHED (N-SHED). The system enabled SHED to maintain good stemness, undergo growth, development, proliferation, and multipotency, enriched their functional mitochondrial content, and allowed sustained cell release through gradual carrier degradation in diabetic wounds. Via functional mitochondrial transfer, N-SHED attenuated endothelial ferroptosis, promoted angiogenesis, and accelerated diabetic wound healing. This integrated 3D culture and microcarrier-mediated delivery strategy amplifies the therapeutic efficacy of MSCs, offering a promising platform for cell-based therapy in diabetic wound management.
