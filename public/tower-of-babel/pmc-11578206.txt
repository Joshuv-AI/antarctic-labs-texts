High Rate of Complications With Early Conversion Hip Arthroplasty Following Fracture Treatment
Authors: Whisper Grayson, Carlo Eikani, Meredith Benson, Nicolas Jozefowski, Nicholas M. Brown
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:Conversion total hip arthroplasty (THA) is associated with higher rates of complications compared with primary THA, with prior surgical fixation of fractures in the ipsilateral hip shown to further increase these rates. There is a scarcity of literature on the effect of timing of conversion THA on complication rates. In this study, we evaluated early (≤6 months of index surgery) and late (>6 months of index surgery) conversion to THA following prior fixation of the proximal femur or acetabulum.Methods:Ninety-one patients who underwent conversion THA following prior surgical fixation of either the proximal femur or acetabulum were identified. Index surgery, fracture characteristics, time, and cause of failure were obtained. Postoperative complications evaluated following the conversion THA included infection, revision surgery rates, dislocation, and revision rates.Results:A total of 91 patients were included, of which 22 underwent early conversion to THA. In the early conversion group, 22.7% of patients had a complication versus 11.6% of patients in the late conversion group (P = 0.194). The most common complications were deep infection (18.2% vs. 5.8%), revision surgery (22.7% vs. 10.1%), and revision THA (18.2% vs. 5.8%). The early conversion group had a markedly higher rate of perioperative blood transfusion compared with the late cohort (P = 0.013).Conclusion:In this study, we found a high rate of complications associated with early THA conversion. Patients should be counseled on the high risk for complications following early conversion to THA following failed fracture fixation.

Conversion total hip arthroplasty (THA) is associated with higher rates of complications, revisions, and readmissions compared with primary THA.1,2 This leads to increased patient morbidity and a significant economic strain to the healthcare system, with approximately 19% greater costs compared with primary THA.3,4 Prior surgical fixation of the ipsilateral hip due to a fracture has been associated with increased complications following conversion THA, with rates of blood loss and transfusions mirroring those of revision THA.5-7

Posttraumatic osteoarthritis, nonunion, and osteonecrosis are common causes of failure following surgical fixation of hip fractures, contributing to the need for eventual arthroplasty.5,8,9 These complications have led to rates of conversion THA ranging from 8% to 35% following acetabular fractures and approximately 10% following femoral neck fractures.5,9 The time to conversion THA following surgical fixation of acetabulum and femoral neck fractures averages around 1 year, with risk factors like high body mass index (BMI), increased age, and infection contributing to earlier conversion.10-13

Although prior studies have evaluated risk factors for early conversion to THA, there is a scarcity of literature on outcomes of these patients. The purpose of this study was to evaluate postoperative complication rates of conversion THA within 6 months of the index surgery.

Methods

Patient Selection

This was an Institutional Review Board-approved, single-center, retrospective cohort study conducted at a level 1 trauma center. The Current Procedural Terminology code 27132 was used to identify patients who had undergone conversion from a previous hip surgery to a THA between February 2007 and May 2022. Inclusion criteria included all patients with prior fixation of the proximal femur or acetabulum. Exclusion criteria included patients who had undergone conversion after prior hip surgeries for non–fracture-related pathologies.

Variables of interest included baseline demographics (sex, race/ethnicity, age at time of conversion THA, BMI, tobacco use, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, follow-up time, and Charlson Comorbidity Index [CCI]), index surgery, and fracture description. The primary outcomes documented were time to conversion THA and the cause of index surgery failure (arthritis/osteonecrosis, implant failure, nonunion, or infection). Secondary outcomes evaluated following the conversion THA included rates of perioperative blood transfusion and postoperative complications, including revision surgery, revision THA, hip dislocation, aseptic loosening, periprosthetic fracture, superficial surgical site infection (defined as superficial tissue infection treated with oral antibiotics), and deep surgical site infection (defined as return to the operating room for irrigation and débridement).

Surgical Descriptions

Initial fracture fixation for all acetabulum fractures involved open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF). For the proximal femur fractures, 50.0% underwent ORIF, 22.2% underwent closed reduction and percutaneous pinning (CRPP) with three cannulated screws, 14.8% underwent a hemiarthroplasty, and 13.0% underwent intramedullary nailing (IMN).

Statistical Analysis

Univariate comparisons included independent t-tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests of independence and Fisher exact tests for categorical variables. A post hoc power analysis was conducted on the full sample (n = 91) with an alpha level set at 0.05, indicating power (1-B) = 0.279.

Results

A total of 91 patients met the study criteria during the enrollment period (February 2007 and May 2022). A majority (75.8%) of the patients had a late conversion to THA (>6-months after the index surgery). In the early cohort, an average follow-up of 29.5 months (SD, 34.5) was observed compared with 35.8 months (SD, 47.2) in the late cohort. The average CCI for the early cohort was 3.5 (SD, 2.2) versus 2.3 (SD, 2.2) for the late cohort (P = 0.038).

Both cohorts were predominantly women at 63.6% and 52.2% for the early and late cohorts, respectively. Proximal femur fractures were more predominant than acetabulum fractures in both cohorts, with 59.1% and 59.4% being proximal femur fractures in the early and late cohorts, respectively. The most common index surgery among both cohorts was ORIF. In the early cohort, 68.2% of patients underwent ORIF, followed by cephalomedullary/intramedullary nail(18.2%), CRPP (9.1%), and hemiarthroplasty (4.5%). In the late conversion cohort, 71.0% of patients underwent ORIF, followed by CRPP (14.5%), hemiarthroplasty (10.1%), and cephalomedullary/intramedullary nail (4.3%). Finally, when comparing index surgery failure, a statistically significant difference was observed between the two cohorts (P = 0.00027). Patients in the early conversion group tended to fail due to implant failure, whereas the late conversion group was predominantly due to posttraumatic arthritis/osteonecrosis (Table 1).

Although higher rates were observed for every complication in the early conversion group, no statistically significant differences were found between the two groups.

When evaluating factors related to the development of a complication for the entire cohort (n = 91), a significant difference was found when considering the mechanism of failure for the index surgery. A higher incidence of nonunion was observed as the cause of failure in patients who experienced a complication post-THA, whereas most patients who did not undergo a complication had failed due to posttraumatic arthritis (P = 0.0018). Fracture location, index surgery, sex, BMI, tobacco use, or CCI did not appear to markedly affect development of a complication (Table 3). This also held true when evaluating factors related to complication development specifically in the early and late conversion cohorts, with cause of index surgery failure being markedly related to complication development post-THA (Tables 4 and 5).

Discussion

The incidence of hip fractures is expected to increase over the next decade as the population continues to age.14 Although treatment of hip fractures is generally very successful, complications are not uncommon with conversion to THA, often resulting from implant failure, posttraumatic arthritis, or nonunion.15 Although the association between prior surgical fixation of hip fractures and increased complications following conversion THA has been well studied, there is a paucity of literature describing factors that may contribute to earlier THA conversion following these injuries.5,6

In this study, we found implant failure to be associated with earlier conversion to THA, whereas posttraumatic arthritis led to later conversion. No significant differences were found in fracture location or type of fixation between the two groups. This is consistent with current literature demonstrating implant failure and nonunion to be leading causes of early conversion.16 Of note, 22.7% of the early conversion group and 40.6% of the late conversion group underwent other procedures on the ipsilateral hip in between the index surgery and conversion THA. These procedures were often performed trying to either salvage the initial surgical fixation or treat deep infections. The increased rate of surgeries in the late conversion group may attribute to the later conversion as more attempts were made in attempts to salvage the index procedure.

Early failure of hip fracture fixation is not an uncommon problem, carrying with it a high risk of patient morbidity and mortality.17 Success of surgical fixation is dependent on numerous factors, including patient demographics, type of fracture and fixation, and the quality of the surgical procedure.17 Risk factors that have been shown to predispose patients to early fixation failure include female sex, increasing age, and the presence of congenital disorders, such as developmental hip dysplasia.18

When looking at the outcomes following the conversion THA in our study, high rates of perioperative transfusion were found in both groups, with a markedly higher rate in the early conversion cohort. This aligns with the current literature demonstrating increased blood loss and need for transfusion following conversion THA compared with primary THA.6 High complication rates were seen in both groups following the conversion THA, with infections and revision surgeries among the most common. Notably, a nonstatistically significant trend was observed toward all the complications in the early conversion group.

Regardless of timing, the literature has demonstrated increased complication rates following conversion THA compared with primary THA.1 In a large database study, Douglas et al1 found high rates of prosthetic joint infection (7.7%), revision surgeries (4.0%), and hip dislocations (4.5%) compared with a primary THA cohort. In addition to the increased patient morbidity, these complications led to a significant economic strain to the healthcare system. Similar studies have supported these findings, highlighting the necessity of finding ways to mitigate these patient risks.16,19,20

This study is not without limitations, with the retrospective design presenting the potential for bias. Further limitations include the variety of index procedures and the variability in follow-up time. The higher incidences of diabetes mellitus and increased CCI scores in the early conversion group also presented a limitation to the parity between the two groups. Finally, the small cohort size is a substantial limitation contributing to a low level of power in this study. Despite having insufficient power, the data trends point toward early conversion having higher rates of complications. This is worthy of noting because it may affect clinical decisions and patient conversations. Further large-scale studies warranted to further define this difference in complication rates.

Conclusion

In this study, we found a high rate of complications associated with early THA conversion. Early conversion patients also required blood transfusion at a high rate (81.8%). Patients should be counseled on the increased rate of complications following early (≤6-months) conversion to THA following failed fracture fixation of the hip.
