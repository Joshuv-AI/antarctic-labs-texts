Abstract
. In this paper we show that Baker domains of transcendental skew products can either bulge or not, depending on the higher order terms. This is in contrast to polynomial skew products where all Fatou components with bounded orbits of an invariant attracting fiber do bulge.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 37F80; Secondary 30D05, 32H50, 37F10

1.
Introduction

A skew product is a holomorphic map
F:{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
of the form

(1.1)

F(z,w)=(f(z,w),g(w)).

Notice that
f:{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{C}}
and
g:{\mathbb{C}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
, and that the dynamics in the second component is one-dimensional.

As innocent as they may appear at first sight, their dynamics is far from being trivial. For instance, they provided the first known example of wandering domains for polynomial endomorphisms of
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}

[
ABD+16
]
.

It is natural to ask to what extent the one-dimensional dynamics in the second component influences the global two-dimensional dynamics. The simplest question is to investigate the relation between the set of normality for the one-dimensional map
g
and the one for the two-dimensional map
F
.
In general, it is difficult to pinpoint the relation between normality of iterates of a map
F=(f_{1},f_{2}):{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
and normality of iterates of its components
f_{1},f_{2}
, in however way one wants to make sense of it. The definition of normality itself for maps of
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
is not choice-free, since normality requires the target space to be compact. While it is commonly agreed that the correct compactification of
{\mathbb{C}}
is the Riemann sphere
\widehat{{\mathbb{C}}}
, the one point compactification of
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
is not a complex manifold, and there are several natural compactifications of
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
that are complex manifolds (see
[
Mor73
]
and
[
Bre73
]
for example). Here we will choose as compactification the projective space (see the discussion in
[
ABFP19
]
and Definition
2.1
).

If we assume that
w_{0}\in{\mathbb{C}}
is an attracting fixed point for
g(w)
, that is
g(w_{0})=w_{0}
and
|g^{\prime}(w_{0})|<1
, then the fiber
\{w=w_{0}\}
is an invariant curve in
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
and
f_{w_{0}}:=f(\cdot,w_{0}):{\mathbb{C}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
is a one-dimensional map acting on
\{w=w_{0}\}
. Hence, it has its own Fatou components coming from the one-dimensional theory. Since the dynamics of
F
near
\{w=w_{0}\}
is attracting in the
w
direction, it is natural to expect that each Fatou component for
f_{w_{0}}
is contained in a Fatou component for
F
. Without loss of generality, for the remainder of the paper we will assume that
w_{0}=0
.

Definition 1.1

(Bulging)
.

A Fatou component
U
for
f_{0}

bulges
if there exists a Fatou component
\Omega
for
F
such that
U\subset\Omega\cap\{w=0\}
.

By Sullivan’s No Wandering Domains Theorem
[
Sul85
]
it is known that all Fatou components of a one-dimensional polynomial are periodic or pre-periodic. Moreover, any invariant Fatou component of such polynomials is either the basin of an attracting fixed point, or a parabolic basin, or a Siegel disk.
Therefore the classification of Fatou components for one-dimensional polynomial maps implies that, except for the basin of infinity, iterates are uniformly bounded, which with some work allows to conclude that each Fatou component with bounded orbits for
f_{0}

bulges
to a Fatou component of
F
.

This fact is summarized in the following result obtained by Lilov
[
Lil04
]
.

Theorem 1.2

(Bulging for polynomial skew products,
[
Lil04
]
)
.

If
F
is a polynomial skew product and
0
is an attracting fixed point for
g
, then all Fatou components of
f_{0}
with bounded orbits bulge.

More precisely, Lilov proved in
[
Lil04
, Sect. 3.2.1]
the bulging of attracting basins of fixed points different from infinity, of parabolic basins, and of Siegel disks for
f_{0}
when
0
is an attracting fixed point for
g
.
One can also relax the hypothesis that
0
is an attracting fixed point for
g
and make it the center of a Siegel disk; in this case, the picture becomes more intricate
[
PR19
]
.

In fact, more can be asked: Is
\Omega\cap\{w=0\}

equal
to
U
? And can there be
other
Fatou components for
F
which do not intersect
\{w=0\}
, yet intersect every neighbourhood thereof? Several answers when
f(z,w)
is a polynomial can be found in the survey
[
Rai17
]
and the references therein.

When
f_{0}
is transcendental rather than polynomial, two new types of Fatou components appear:
wandering domains
(components of the Fatou set which are not pre-periodic) and
Baker domains
(periodic Fatou components on which the iterates converge to the essential singularity at infinity). One needs to resist the temptation to consider Baker domains as an analogue of parabolic domains for which the fixed point has been moved to infinity (see the classification of Baker domains
[
FH06
]
, and compare to
[
Cow81
]
). On wandering domains, the iterates of the map can converge to infinity, have both bounded and unbounded subsequences, or possibly be bounded (there are no known examples of this last case occurring, but it has not been ruled out either).

For the types of Fatou components for
f_{0}
which also occur in the polynomial case, everything works as expected and follows from the proofs of Lilov in
[
Lil04
]
.

Theorem 1.3

(
[
Lil04
, Sect. 3.2.1]
)
.

Let
F
be a transcendental skew product with an invariant attracting fiber
\{w=0\}
, such that
f_{0}
has an invariant Fatou component
U
with bounded orbits. Then
U
bulges.

In this paper we study the bulging of Baker domains, and we show that the fact that iterates tend to infinity makes it possible for Baker domains to either bulge or not depending on the choice of the perturbation. Our main result is the following theorem (see Section
2.2
for the fact that
z\rightarrow z+1+e^{-z}
has a Baker domain, and Section
3
for the definition of order in the first variable).

Main Theorem
.

Let
f_{0}(z)=z+1+e^{-z}
, let
B
be its Baker domain, and let
F(z,w)
be a skew product of the form

F(z,w)=(f_{0}(z)+wh(z,w),g(w)),

for which
g
has an attracting fixed point at
0
.
Then

•

If the order of
h
in the first variable is less than
1
, then
B
bulges.

•

If
g^{\prime}(0)=0
, and the order of
h
in the first variable is finite, then
B
bulges.

On the other hand, there exists a transcendental entire function
h(z)
such that
B
does not bulge.

In particular, if
h(z,w)
does not depend on
z
, then the order in the first variable is zero, and the Baker domain
B
bulges. The non-bulging case is first of its kind in the attracting setting. Moreover, the proof in the bulging case relies on the analysis and control of the growth given by the perturbation
wh(z,w)
in the first coordinate, which is conceptually different from the strategy used by Lilov in
[
Lil04
]
.

Bulging of wandering domains, the last type of Fatou components to consider, will be treated in a forthcoming paper by the second and last authors
[
PR25
]
.

Structure of the paper

In section
2
, we give some preliminaries on the definition of normality in
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
and on Baker domains. In section
3
, we construct examples of bulging Baker domains. In section
4
, we construct the example of a non bulging Baker domain.

Notation

We denote by
{\mathbb{C}}
the complex plane, by
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
the complex projective space of dimension 2, and by
{\mathbb{D}}(a,r)
the Euclidean disk of radius
r
centered at
a
. Given
F:{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
and a point
P=(z,w)
, we write its orbit as
(z_{n},w_{n}):=F^{n}(z,w)
.

2.
Preliminaries: Normality, Baker domains, Absorbing domains

2.1.
Normality

We compactify
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
as
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
by adding a complex line at infinity, which entails the following definition of normality
[
ABFP19
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
X\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
be a domain.
A family
\mathcal{F}\subset{\rm Hol}(X,{\mathbb{C}}^{2})
is
normal
if for every sequence
(F_{n})_{n\geq 0}\subset\mathcal{F}
there exists a subsequence
(F_{n_{k}})_{n_{k}\geq 0}
converging uniformly on compact subsets to
G\in{\rm Hol}(X,{\mathbb{P}}^{2})
.
In other words,
\mathcal{F}
is relatively compact in
{\rm Hol}(X,{\mathbb{P}}^{2})
.

We notice that for polynomial automorphisms, this definition of normality is equivalent to the one induced by the one-point compactification of
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
. Indeed by
[
FM89
]
, it is enough to look at Hénon maps, and for the latter, the filtration in
[
BS91
]
implies that every escaping orbit converges to the point
[1:0:0]\in{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
.

The following result shows that, for a given Fatou component, all limit functions are either holomorphic maps in
\operatorname{Hol}(X,{\mathbb{C}}^{2})
, or take values entirely in the compactifying part at infinity.

Lemma 2.2

(
[
ABFP19
, Lemma 2.4]
)
.

Let
X\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
be a domain. If a family
\mathcal{F}\subset\operatorname{Hol}(X,{\mathbb{C}}^{2})
is normal, then either any limit function lies in
\operatorname{Hol}(X,{\mathbb{C}}^{2})
, or the corresponding sequence diverges locally uniformly to infinity.

2.2.
Baker domains and absorbing domains

Given a transcendental entire function
f:{\mathbb{C}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}
, a
Baker domain

B
for
f
is a periodic Fatou component on which orbits tend to infinity. The first example of Baker domains was given by Fatou
[
Fat20
, Example I]
, who proved that for the function
f(z)=z+1+e^{-z}
orbits tend to infinity on the right half plane
\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\mid\operatorname{Re}z>0\}
.

One can show indirectly that Baker domains of entire functions are simply connected
[
Bak63
]
, hence biholomorphic to the unit disk
{\mathbb{D}}
by the Riemann Mapping Theorem.

Definition 2.3
.

An
absorbing domain
for a Baker domain
B
is an open, simply connected, forward invariant set
D\subset B
such that for every compact set
K\subset B
, there exists
n=n(K)\geq 0
such that
f^{n}(K)\subset D
.

It follows by results of Cowen that every Baker domain has absorbing domains (
[
Cow81
]
; compare with
[
BFJK15
]
).

We will use entire functions similar to Fatou’s first example, namely of the form
f(z)=z+a+e^{-z}
with
\operatorname{Re}a>0
. These functions are sometimes called
Fatou functions
(see for example
[
Evd16
]
). The following fact is well known (the first property is elementary; for the second one, see e.g.
[
KU05
, proof of Theorem 2.5]
).

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
f(z)=z+a+e^{-z}
with
\operatorname{Re}a>0
. Then for any
R>|\log\operatorname{Re}a|
,

•

the right half plane
\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\mid\operatorname{Re}z>R\}
is invariant under
f
and contained in an invariant Baker domain
B_{a}
.

•

the right half plane
\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\mid\operatorname{Re}z>R\}
is an absorbing domain for
B_{a}
.

We will also need the following simple lemma for Fatou’s function
f(z)=z+1+e^{-z}
. We report here the proof for the sake of completeness.

Lemma 2.5
.

Consider the function
f(z)=z+1+e^{-z}
. Then for any
\delta>0
there exists a real number
x_{0}>\log(2)
such that for all integers
k\geq 1

(2.1)

x_{k}:=f^{k}(x_{0})\in(x_{0}+k-\delta,x_{0}+k+\delta)\subset{\mathbb{R}},

and

(2.2)

|f(z)-f(\tilde{z})|<10\delta

for all
z,\tilde{z}\in\overline{{\mathbb{D}}(x_{0}+k,4\delta)}
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Proof.

Let
\delta>0
be arbitrary.
Since the series
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{j}{2}}
converges, we may choose
x_{0}>\log 2
large enough such that

(2.3)

e^{-x_{0}}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{j}{2}}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}e^{-x_{0}-\frac{j}{2}}<\delta.

and

(2.4)

e^{-x_{0}+4\delta}<\delta.

Therefore, by induction, we have that

|x_{0}+k-f^{k}(x_{0})|\leq\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}e^{-x_{0}-\frac{j}{2}}

for all integers
k\geq 1
, where empty sums are interpreted as
0
. Using (
2.3
) and the fact that
f(x)\in{\mathbb{R}}
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we obtain (
2.1
).

Finally, for property (
2.2
), let
z,\tilde{z}\in\overline{{\mathbb{D}}(x_{0}+k,4\delta)}
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then, using (
2.4
), we obtain

\begin{split}|{f(z)-f(\tilde{z})}|&\leq|{z-\tilde{z}}|+|{e^{-z}}|+|{e^{-\tilde{z}}}|\\
&\leq 8\delta+e^{-\operatorname{Re}(z)}+e^{-\operatorname{Re}(\tilde{z})}\\
&\leq 8\delta+2e^{-x_{0}-k+4\delta}\leq 8\delta+2e^{-x_{0}+4\delta}\leq 10\delta,\end{split}

which completes the proof.
∎

3.
Bulging Baker domains

In this section, we give conditions under which the Baker domain of a Fatou’s function bulges. The following result shows that if an absorbing domain bulges, so does the whole Baker domain.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
g\colon{\mathbb{C}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
be an entire function with an attracting fixed point at
0
. Let
F\colon\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
be a transcendental skew product of the form
F(z,w)=(f(z,w),g(w))
. Assume that
f_{0}
has a Baker domain
B
and let
D
be an absorbing domain for
B
. If there is a Fatou component
\Omega
for
F
such that
D\subset\Omega\cap\{w=0\}
, then
B
bulges to
\Omega
.

Proof.

Let
0<\delta_{g}<1
be such that the disk
{\mathbb{D}}(0,\delta_{g})
lies entirely within the immediate attracting basin of
g
at
0
, i.e.,
|g(w)|\leq|w|
for all
x\in{\mathbb{D}}(0,\delta_{g})
and so
g({\mathbb{D}}(0,\delta_{g}))\subset{\mathbb{D}}(0,\delta_{g})
. Moreover, up to considering an iterate, we may assume that the Baker domain
B
is invariant.

Let
\Omega
be the invariant Fatou component for
F
containing the absorbing domain
D
, that is
D\subset\Omega
. It is enough to show that for every non-empty compact set
K\subset B
we have
K\subset\Omega
.
Up to enlarging
K
, since
B
is connected we can assume that
K\cap D\neq\varnothing
.
Since
D
is an absorbing domain for
f_{0}
, there exists
N=N(K)\geq 0
such that
f_{0}^{N}(K)\subset D
. Let
Q
be a closed
\delta
-neighbourhood of
f_{0}^{N}(K)
where
\delta>0
is chosen small enough so that
Q\Subset D
.

Since
Q
is compact and
D\subset\Omega\cap\{w=0\}
, there exists
0<\varepsilon_{Q}<\delta_{g}
such that
Q\times\ D(0,\varepsilon_{Q})\Subset\Omega
.
By continuity of
F^{N}
, there is
0<\varepsilon_{K}<\varepsilon_{Q}
such that on
K\times\overline{{\mathbb{D}}(0,\varepsilon_{K})}
we have
\|F(z,w)-F(z,0)\|<\delta/2
.
In other words we can write

(3.1)

F^{N}(z,w)=(z_{N},w_{N})=(f_{0}^{N}(z)+q(z,w),g^{N}(w),)

where
q(z,w)
is a holomorphic function such that
|q(z,w)|<\delta/2
on
K\times\overline{{\mathbb{D}}(0,\varepsilon_{K})}
.

We now claim that

F^{N}(K\times\overline{{\mathbb{D}}(0,\varepsilon_{K})})\subset Q\times{\mathbb{D}}(0,\varepsilon_{Q})\subset\Omega.

Indeed, if
P=(z,w)\in K\times\overline{{\mathbb{D}}(0,\varepsilon_{K})}
, set
(z_{N},w_{N})=F^{N}(P)
. Since
|w|\leq\varepsilon_{K}<\varepsilon_{Q}<\delta_{g}
and
|g(w)|\leq|w|
on
{\mathbb{D}}(0,\delta_{g})
, we also have that
|w_{N}|\leq|w|<\varepsilon_{Q}
.
For the first coordinate,
|z_{N}-f_{0}^{N}(z)|=|q(z,w)|<\delta/2
, hence, since
f_{0}^{N}(z)\in f_{0}^{N}(K)
and
Q
is a
\delta
-neighbourhood of
f_{0}^{N}(K)
, we obtain
z_{N}\in Q
.

This implies that
K\times\overline{{\mathbb{D}}(0,\varepsilon_{K})}
is contained in a Fatou component
\Omega_{K}
of
F
(a priori, this could be a preimage of
\Omega
). However since
K\cap D\neq\varnothing
,
D\subset\Omega
, and Fatou components are connected, we have that
\Omega_{K}=\Omega
.

∎

We observe the following properties of bulging Baker domains.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
g\colon{\mathbb{C}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
be an entire function with an attracting fixed point at
0
. Let
F\colon\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
be a transcendental skew product of the form
F(z,w)=(f(z,w),g(w))
. Assume that
f_{0}
has a Baker domain
B
. If
B
bulges to a Fatou component
\Omega
, then
F^{n}\rightarrow[1:0:0]
as
n\rightarrow\infty
on
\Omega
. Moreover, if
B
coincides with the Fatou set of
f_{0}
, then
\Omega\cap\{w=0\}=B
.

Proof.

We first show that
F^{n}\rightarrow[1:0:0]
as
n\rightarrow\infty
on
\Omega
. Let
P\in B\times\{0\}
. Clearly
F^{n}(P)\rightarrow[1:0:0]
. Hence by Lemma 2.4 in
[
ABFP19
]
, for any limit function
h
on
\Omega
, we obtain that
h(\Omega)\subset\ell_{\infty}
. Let
\Delta
be a small polydisk centered at
P
and
Q=(z^{Q},w^{Q})\in\Delta
. Then
w^{Q}_{n}\rightarrow 0
because
0
is attracting for
g
, yet
\|F^{n}(Q)\|\rightarrow\infty
because for any limit function
h
on
\Omega
, we have that
h(\Omega)\subset\ell_{\infty}
. So
|z^{Q}_{n}|\rightarrow\infty
for any
Q\in\Delta
, hence
F^{n}\rightarrow[1:0:0]
on
\Delta
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. By the identity principle, we conclude that
F^{n}\rightarrow[1:0:0]
on
\Omega
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

We now show that if
P\in\Omega\cap\{w=0\}
, then
P
belongs to the Fatou set of
f_{0}
. If
f_{0}
has no other Fatou component, this implies that
\Omega\cap\{w=0\}=B
.

If
P=(z,0)\in\Omega
, then there exists an open neighbourhood
U\subset\Omega
of
P
such that
F^{n}\rightarrow[1:0:0]
on
U
. In particular,
\pi_{z}\circ F^{n}\rightarrow\infty
on
U
, where
\pi_{z}
is the projection on the first coordinate. Thus the iterates of
f_{0}
are normal on
U\cap\{w=0\}
, concluding the proof.
∎

As a corollary of Propositions
3.1
and
3.2
, it is easy to construct examples of bulging Baker domains.

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
a\in\mathbb{C}
with
\operatorname{Re}(a)>0
, let
f
be the entire function
f(z)=z+a+e^{-z}
, and let
g
be an entire function with an attracting fixed point at
0
. Let
h
be a non-constant entire function such that
h(0)=0
, and consider the skew product

F(z,w)=(f(z)+h(w),g(w)).

Then the Baker domain
B
of
f
bulges to a Fatou component
\Omega
for
F
and moreover
\Omega\cap\{w=0\}=B
.

Proof.

Thanks to Proposition
3.1
, it suffices to prove that there is a bulging right half plane
\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\mid\operatorname{Re}z>R\}
with
R>0
sufficiently large, since by Lemma
2.4
, such half plane will be an absorbing domain for
f
. Using the fact that
h(0)=0
and the continuity of
h
at
0
, an easy computation shows that for
R>0
sufficiently large and
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small,
F^{n}
converges to
[1:0:0]
as
n\rightarrow\infty
uniformly on the set

W_{\varepsilon,R}:=\{z,w\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}:\operatorname{Re}z>R,|w|<\varepsilon\},

that is, the right half plane
\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\mid\operatorname{Re}z>R\}
is bulging.
Since the only Fatou component for
f
is the Baker domain
B
, it follows from Proposition
3.2
that
\Omega\cap\{w=0\}=B
.
∎

It is reasonable to ask whether one can characterize the errors for which we have a bulging Baker domain, at least for the class of Fatou functions.
For more complex error terms, it is necessary to quantify their growth. One approach uses the order, as presented in greater generality in
[
Ron74
, Chapter 3]
and
[
Ron89
]
. Here, we restrict ourselves to the following definitions.

Definition 3.4

(cf.
[
Ron89
, Sections 1.1 and 1.2]
)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}
be entire and non-constant. The
maximum modulus
of
f
at radii
r_{1},r_{2}>0
is defined as

M(r_{1},r_{2};f):=\max_{\lvert z\rvert\leq r_{1},\lvert w\rvert\leq r_{2}}\lvert f(z,w)\rvert.

The
order of
f
is defined as

\rho(f):=\limsup_{r\to\infty}\frac{\log^{+}\log M(r,r;f)}{\log r}\geq 0.

The
order of
f
in the
z
variable, or first variable,
is defined as

\rho_{1}(f):=\limsup_{r_{1}\to\infty}\frac{\log^{+}\log M(r_{1},r_{2};f)}{\log r_{1}}\geq 0

for some
r_{2}>0
.

Remark 3.5
.

(1)

As discussed in
[
Ron89
, Section 1.2]
, the definition of
\rho_{1}(f)
is independent of the choice of
r_{2}>0
.

(2)

Every polynomial has order
0
. Similarly, any function that is polynomial in
z
, i.e., is of the form

(z,w)\mapsto\sum_{k=0}^{d}h_{k}(w)z^{k}

for
d\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
h_{k}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
holomorphic functions, has order
0
in the
z
variable.

(3)

Simple examples of non-zero, finite order in one-dimension are given by entire transcendental functions of the form

\exp_{k}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C},z\mapsto e^{z^{k}},

as they satisfy

\rho(\exp_{k})=k

for
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
g:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
be a non-constant entire function with an attracting fixed point at
0
, let
f:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
be a Fatou function of the form
f(z)=z+a+e^{-z}
for some
a\in\mathbb{C}
with
\operatorname{Re}(a)>0
, and let
h:\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}
be holomorphic with order
\rho_{1}(h)
in the first variable. If

(1)

\rho_{1}(h)<1
, or

(2)

0
is super-attracting for
g
and
\rho_{1}(h)<\infty
,

then the Baker domain of
f
bulges for the skew product
F:\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}

F(z,w)=(f(z)+wh(z,w),g(w)).

Remark 3.7
.

As a consequence, we have bulging for any error term which is polynomial in the
z
variable, so Corollary
3.3
can also be deduced as a corollary of Theorem
3.6
.

The rest of this section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
3.6

In this section we prove the following result.

Theorem 4.1

Lemma 4.2

[

Let

Let

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

In this proof, we construct a skew product with a non-bulging Baker domain on an attracting fiber using Runge’s Theorem iteratively.

g:{\mathbb{C}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
be given as in the statement, that is,
g
non-constant holomorphic,

we can apply Runge’s Theorem Lemma
4.3
to get a polynomial
h_{1}
satisfying

\|{h_{1}-\varphi_{1}}\|_{\infty,H_{1}\cup D_{1}}<\varepsilon_{1}.

By construction, we obtain properties (
4
) and (
5
). Property (
3
) follows from (
4.11
). This concludes the first step of our inductive construction.

Now, given
h_{0},\dots,h_{k-1}
,
w_{0},\dots,w_{k-1}
and
\delta_{0},\dots,\delta_{k-1}
constructed according to properties (
1
) to (
4.9
), we define the next elements of our sequences.

As before, (
4.10
) and the continuity of
w\mapsto\pi_{z}(F^{j}_{h_{k-1}}(x_{0},w))
in
0
for
j=0,\dots,k
allow us to choose
w_{k}\in{\mathbb{D}}(0,\delta_{g})\setminus\{0\}
small enough such that
|{w_{k}}|<\frac{1}{k+1}
, which is required for property (
1
), and

(4.12)

\pi_{z}(F^{j}_{h_{k-1}}(x_{0},w_{k}))\in{\mathbb{D}}(x_{j},\delta)\subset D_{j}

for all
0\leq j\leq k
.

From (
4.12
) we can, in particular, derive that the
z
-coordinates of the first
k-1
images of
(x_{0},w_{k})
under
F_{h_{k-1}}
remain in
D_{0}\cup\dots\cup D_{k-1}\subset H_{k}
. Consequently, by continuity, if an entire function
\widetilde{h}
is close enough to
h_{k-1}
on
H_{k}
, then the first
k
preimages of
(x_{0},w_{k})
under
F_{\widetilde{h}}
will stay arbitrarily close to those under
F_{h_{k-1}}
.

Thus, we find
0<\delta_{k}<1
small enough such that for any holomorphic functions
\widetilde{h}:{\mathbb{C}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
the inequality

(4.13)

\|{\widetilde{h}-h_{k-1}}\|_{\infty,H_{k}}<\delta_{k}

implies

(4.14)

|{\pi_{z}(F^{k}_{h_{k-1}}(x_{0},w_{k}))-\pi_{z}(F^{k}_{\widetilde{h}}(x_{0},w_{k})})|<\delta

and

(4.15)

\pi_{z}(F^{k}_{\widetilde{h}}(x_{0},w_{k}))\in{\mathbb{D}}(x_{j},\delta).

This yields properties (
2
) and (
4.9
).

Let
\varepsilon_{k}:=\frac{1}{2^{k+1}}\min\{\delta_{j}\mid 0\leq j\leq k\}
.
Consider the holomorphic function

(4.16)

\varphi_{k}:H_{k}\cup D_{k}\to{\mathbb{C}},z\mapsto\begin{cases}h_{k-1}(z),\;\text{ for}\;z\in H_{k},\\
-\frac{x_{k+1}}{g^{k}(w_{k})},\;\text{ for}\;z\in D_{k}.\end{cases}

Runge’s Theorem
4.3
applied to the two sets
H_{k}
and
D_{k}
yields a polynomial
h_{k}
which coincides with
\varphi_{k}
on its domain of definition
H_{k}\cup D_{k}
up to the error
\varepsilon_{k}
. Hence, properties (
4
) and (
5
) hold for
k
.

Moreover, property (
3
) holds for
k
, since
h_{k}
satisfies (
4.13
) by construction, which in turn implies (
4.15
).

This completes the construction step.
∎

Proof of Lemma
4.4
(continued).

Given the construction from Lemma
4.5
, we now show the existence of a limit function
h
which satisfies the property required by Lemma
4.4
.

First observe, that property (
1
) of Lemma
4.5
directly implies
w_{k}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
.
By writing
h_{k}
as a telescopic sum, that is,

h_{k}=\sum_{j=m}^{k-1}(h_{j+1}-h_{j})+h_{m}=h_{m}+\sum_{j=m}^{k-1}\left(h_{j+1}-h_{j}\right)

for
k\geq m
, and observing that properties (
2
) and (
5
) in Lemma
4.5
yield

\sum_{j=m}^{\infty}\|{h_{j+1}-h_{j}}\|_{\infty,H_{m}}<\sum_{j=m}^{\infty}\|{h_{j+1}-h_{j}}\|_{\infty,H_{j+1}}<\sum_{j=m}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{j+2}}<\infty,

we see that the telescopic sum forms a Cauchy sequence on
H_{m}
and therefore has a uniform limit function

\lim_{k\to\infty}h_{k}=h_{m}+\sum_{j=m}^{\infty}(h_{j+1}-h_{j})

on
H_{m}
.
Using the fact that
\bigcup_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}H_{k}={\mathbb{C}}
,
H_{k}\subset H_{k+1}
, and Weierstraß Theorem, we obtain an entire limit function
h=\lim_{k\to\infty}h_{k}
on
{\mathbb{C}}
.

Moreover, using the same argument as before, this limit function satisfies

(4.17)

\begin{split}\|{h-h_{n}}\|_{\infty,H_{n+1}}&\leq\ \sum_{k=n}^{\infty}\ \|{h_{k+1}-h_{k}}\|_{\infty,H_{n+1}}\\
&\leq\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{k+1}}\min\{\delta_{j}\mid j=0,\dots,k\}\\
&\leq\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{k+1}}\delta_{n+1}<\delta_{n+1}.\end{split}

We now verify that our construction of
h
ensures that the map

F_{h}:{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{2},(z,w)\mapsto(f(z)+wh(z),g(w))

satisfies the relation (
4.3
).
Let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. We first show that
h
is sufficiently close to the recursively defined function
h_{n}
to satisfy
\pi_{z}(F_{h}^{n}(x_{0},w_{n}))\in D_{n}
. By using (
4.17
) for
n-1
, we can apply property (
4.9
) of Lemma
4.5
to
h
and obtain

|{\pi_{z}(F_{h_{n-1}}^{n}(x_{0},w_{n}))-\pi_{z}(F_{h}^{n}(x_{0},w_{n})})|<\delta.

Similarly, we can apply property (
4.9
) of Lemma
4.5
to
h_{n}
and obtain

|{\pi_{z}(F_{h_{n-1}}^{n}(x_{0},w_{n}))-\pi_{z}(F_{h_{n}}^{n}(x_{0},w_{n})})|<\delta.

Furthermore, property (
3
) of Lemma
4.5
yields

\pi_{z}(F_{h_{n}}^{n}(x_{0},w_{n}))\in{\mathbb{D}}(x_{n},\delta),

that is

|{x_{n}-\pi_{z}(F_{h_{n}}^{n}(x_{0},w_{n})})|<\delta.

And lastly, by (
2.1
) from Lemma
2.5
, we have

|{x_{n}-(x_{0}+n)}|<\delta.

Together, we can conclude

(4.18)

\pi_{z}(F_{h}^{n}(x_{0},w_{n}))\in\overline{{\mathbb{D}}(x_{0}+n,4\delta)}=D_{n}.

Now, we can write

(4.19)

\begin{split}|{\pi_{z}(F_{h}^{n+1}(x_{0},w_{n})})|&=|{f(\pi_{z}(F_{h}^{n}(x_{0},w_{n})))+g^{n}(w_{n})h(\pi_{z}(F_{h}^{n}(x_{0},w_{n})))}|\\
&\leq|f(\pi_{z}(F_{h}^{n}(x_{0},w_{n})))-x_{n+1}|\\
&\qquad+|{g^{n}(w_{n})h(\pi_{z}(F_{h}^{n}(x_{0},w_{n})))+x_{n+1}}|\\
&\leq|{f(\pi_{z}(F_{h}^{n}(x_{0},w_{n})))-x_{n+1}}|\\
&\qquad+|{g^{n}(w_{n})}|\left|{h(\pi_{z}(F_{h}^{n}(x_{0},w_{n})))+\frac{x_{n+1}}{g^{n}(w_{n})}}\right|\\
&\leq|{f(\pi_{z}(F_{h}^{n}(x_{0},w_{n})))-x_{n+1}}|+\left\|{h+\frac{x_{n+1}}{g^{n}(w_{n})}}\right\|_{\infty,D_{n}}\end{split}

where we used (
4.18
) in the last step.

For the first term in (
4.19
), we use (
2.2
) and (
4.18
) to obtain

(4.20)

\begin{split}|{f(\pi_{z}(F_{h}^{n}(x_{0},w_{n})))-x_{n+1}}|&=|{f(\pi_{z}(F_{h}^{n}(x_{0},w_{n})))-f(x_{n})}|\\
&<10\delta\leq 1.\end{split}

Applying property (
4
) of Lemma
4.5
and inequality (
4.17
) to the second term in (
4.19
), we arrive at

(4.21)

\begin{split}\left\|{h+\frac{x_{n+1}}{g^{n}(w_{n})}}\right\|_{\infty,D_{n}}&\leq\|{h-h_{n}}\|_{\infty,D_{n}}+\left\|{h_{n}+\frac{x_{n+1}}{g^{n}(w_{n})}}\right\|_{\infty,D_{n}}\\
&\leq\|{h-h_{n}}\|_{\infty,H_{n+1}}+1\leq\delta_{n+1}+1<2.\end{split}

Combining (
4.19
), (
4.20
), and (
4.21
), we obtain

|{\pi_{z}F_{h}^{n+1}(x_{0},w_{n})}|\leq 3,

which is (
4.3
). This concludes the proof.
∎
