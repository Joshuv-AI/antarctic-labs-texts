Plasma and CSF pharmacokinetic characteristics of second-line anti-tuberculosis drugs in a patient with multidrug-resistant tuberculous meningitis
Authors: Jinlong Gao, Jie Cao, Jinmeng Li, Xiaohong Pan, Huijie Wang, Xinjun Cai, Yuanyuan Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe treatment of multidrug-resistant tuberculous meningitis (MDR-TBM) presents significant challenges, as the ability of different anti-tuberculosis (anti-TB) drugs to penetrate the blood-brain barrier varies greatly. The pharmacokinetic characteristics of second-line anti-TB drugs including bedaquiline, cycloserine, moxifloxacin and contezolid remains unclear, particularly in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF).Case presentationWe report a case of a 30-year-old female who was diagnosed with MDR-TBM. Therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) was used to determine whether each anti-TB drug’s peak concentration reached the effective range, and concentrations of each anti-TB drug in plasma and CSF of the patient after 5, 7, 9 and 12 h of medication was detected, the CSF/plasma concentration ratio of each anti-TB drug was also analyzed.ConclusionsTDM plays an important role in clinical individualized medication adjustment. Cycloserine, pyrazinamide, contezolid and moxifloxacin may have a high CSF permeability. This study provided valuable reference for the clinical management of MDR-TBM by measuring the plasma and CSF concentrations of anti-TB drugs.Keywords: Multidrug-resistant tuberculous meningitis, Cerebrospinal fluid, Concentration, Anti-tuberculosis drugs, Pharmacokinetic

Background

MDR-TB refers to TB that is resistant to both rifampicin and isoniazid. It continues to pose a significant global health challenge, particularly when it affects extrapulmonary sites such as the central nervous system (CNS) [1, 2]. Tuberculous meningitis (TBM), a severe form of TB that impacts the meninges and brain parenchyma, is associated with high mortality rates and debilitating neurological complications, especially in cases of drug resistance [3]. The emergence of MDR-TBM has further complicated treatment strategies as conventional first-line TB medications become ineffective [4]. This situation necessitates the use of second-line drugs, which often have uncertain pharmacokinetic profiles and variable penetration across the blood-brain barrier (BBB). The BBB presents a critical physiological barrier that restricts the entry of many antimicrobial agents into the CSF, where sufficient drug concentrations are crucial for eradicating Mycobacterium tuberculosis [5]. While first-line drugs such as rifampicin (which typically has poor CSF penetration) and pyrazinamide (which has relatively good CSF penetration) demonstrate variable penetration into the CSF, their effectiveness is diminished in MDR-TBM due to the development of resistance. As a result, second-line agents—including fluoroquinolones (e.g., moxifloxacin), oxazolidinones (e.g., linezolid, contezolid), cycloserine, and bedaquiline—constitute the foundation of salvage regimens [4]. However, their pharmacokinetic properties remain inadequately characterized, particularly regarding CSF exposure levels and time-dependent concentration dynamics. This knowledge gap impedes the optimization of dosing strategies, potentially leading to subtherapeutic CSF concentrations or excessive systemic toxicity, both of which can compromise clinical outcomes.

Case presentation

The patient is a 30-year-old woman. In October 2024, she experienced a migraine headache localized to one side of her head without significant cause. In November 2024, she was admitted to the emergency department of an outside hospital because of a sudden coma. On 23 January 2025, she was transferred to our hospital for treatment, physical examination showed noticeable nuchal rigidity, but no jugular vein distention was present. Additionally, internal and external drainage of the right ventricle of the brain was performed, yielding yellowish CSF. A cranial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed hydrocephalus and multiple speckled and striated high-signal shadows in some ventricles and cerebral pools of the cerebral fissure, which may indicate an infectious process. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) of the cerebrospinal fluid suggested the presence of the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex(MTBC) group. The CT image of the lungs reveals nodules, spots, and cord-like shadows with well-defined edges in the right upper lung, suggesting pulmonary tuberculosis in that region. Xpert MTB/RIF assay of the sputum sample detected MTBC and confirmed rifampicin resistance (RIF-R). A preliminary diagnosis of tuberculous meningoencephalitis and rifampicin resistant pulmonary tuberculosis has been made. Threrfore, the initial treatment regimen of anti-TB (including moxifloxacin, linezolid, ethambutol, cycloserine) was given in conjunction with meropenem, mannitol, dexamethasone and magnesium isoglycyrrhizinate. On the 7th hospital day, CSF total protein measured 0.21 g/L, showed significant regression. Considering the well-documented low CSF penetration of ethambutol, the clinical pharmacist recommended discontinuing ethambutol and adding pyrazinamide to enhance anti-TB therapy. Fluorescence PCR melting curve suggested HRS (isoniazid, rifampicin, streptomycin) resistance, and the diagnosis was consistent with MDR-TBM. It was adjusted to a MDR-TBM regimen consisting of bedaquiline, linezolid, moxifloxacin, cycloserine and pyrazinamide. However, during the treatment, the patient presented with severe anemia, and the clinical pharmacist suggested that a potential relationship with linezolid could not be ruled out. Linezolid was discontinued. On the 37th hospital day, antituberculosis treatment with the addition of contezolid, which has a lesser impact on the hematologic system. To assess CSF permeability, on the 40th hospital day, plasma and CSF were collected simultaneously after 5, 7, 9, and 12 h of medication. The CSF samples were obtained through a lumbar puncture performed between the fourth and fifth lumbar vertebrae. Then, liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry assay was used to detected concentrations of each anti-TB drug in plasma and CSF, and the CSF/plasma concentration ratio of these drugs was calculated. Methods

The UHPLC-MS/MS system consisted ofthe Xevo TQ-S IVD triple-quadrupole mass spectrometer and ACQUITY UPLC I-Class ultra–high-performance liquid chromatography system (Waters, Milford, MA). Mobile phase A was 0.1% v/v formic acid in water, and mobile phase B was methanol. The chromatography column used was the ZORBAX PRHD SB-Aq (3 · 50 mm, 1.8 mm; Agilent, Santa Clara, CA), at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min, and each sample was taken for 7.5 min. The column temperature was 40℃, and the injectionvolume was 3 mL. A gradient elution program was used as the mobile phase. The lower limit of quantification is 0.01 ug/ml. The mass spectrometry scanning mode used was electrospray ionization with multiple reaction monitoring in the positive mode. The bioanalytical method quantified total concentrations (bound + unbound) of the drugs in both plasma and CSF.

Discussion and conlusions

In the study of CSF permeability concerning anti-tuberculosis drugs, we found that permeability may be related to the physiological and pathological states of the BBB, the molecular weight of the drug, and the rate of plasma protein binding. Moxifloxacin has the highest activity against Mycobacterium tuberculosis in vitro compared with the other quinolones [6]. Our data indicated that the concentrations of moxifloxacin in CSF increased progressively with the duration of administration. The plasma concentration of moxifloxacin ranged from 2.21 to 4.06 µg/mL, all within the effective therapeutic range of 2 to 5 µg/mL [7], while the CSF concentration ranged from 0.52 to 1.6 µg/mL. At 5 h post-administration, the CSF concentration was measured at 0.52 µg/mL, corresponding to a plasma concentration of 4.06 µg/mL, resulting in a ratio of 0.13. By 12 h post-administration, the CSF concentration rose to 1.6 µg/mL, with a plasma concentration of 2.21 µg/mL, which resulted in a higher ratio of 0.72. The high permeability of moxifloxacin in CSF may be attributed to the increased permeability of the BBB during states of cerebral inflammation. Cycloserine is categorized as a class B drug for long-term treatment regimens in MDR-TB and serves as a potent therapeutic agent for TBM due to its excellent CNS penetration capabilities [8, 9]. The plasma concentration of cycloserine ranged from 32.52 to 38.85 µg/mL, while the CSF concentration ranged from 15.64 to 28.28 µg/mL, most of them within the effective therapeutic range of 20 to 60 µg/mL [7]. However, this plasma-based range is used only for comparative reference and do not represent validated CSF therapeutic targets. The CSF/plasma concentration ratio (0.48–0.73) of cycloserine was significantly better than that of other second-line drugs, its CSF concentration (15.64–28.28 µg/mL), confirming greater ability to penetrate the BBB. This property may be related to the low molecular weight (102.1 Da) and hydrophilicity of cycloserine [10]. Cycloserine is considered an important agent for MDR-TBM due to its favorable CSF penetration. However, its risk of neurotoxicity needs to be weighed in dose adjustment, and the absence of significant neuropsychiatric symptoms in this case may be related to precise dosage control under the guidance of TDM. Additionally, the plasma concentration of pyrazinamide ranged from 59.9 to 107.19 µg/mL, while the CSF concentration ranged from 69.59 to 91.48 µg/mL, but most of them out of the effective therapeutic range of 20 to 60 µg/mL [7]. The pyrazinamide’s CSF/plasma concentration ratios reached 0.95–1.53, suggesting its excellent permeability in inflamed meninges. Pyrazinamide exhibits its maximal bactericidal efficacy under acidic conditions [11], its acidic environment-dependent bactericidal mechanism is compatible with the pathological features of reduced CSF pH, further reinforcing its position in TBM therapy. In this case, the plasma (59.9–107.19 µg/mL) and CSF (69.59–91.48 µg/mL) concentrations of pyrazinamide provided critical support for controlling intracranial infection. Bedaquiline was not detected in CSF (below the LLOQ of 0.01 µg/mL) and may be closely related to its poor BBB penetration due to its high protein binding (>99.9%) in plasma and large molecular structure (555.5 Da). The low CSF drug concentration directly contributes to its limited contribution to intracranial infections. Interestingly, the study of Upton et al. [12]has shown that bedaquiline can penetrate the BBB and enter the CSF. The discrepancy between our findings may be attributed to individual variations among patients or the limitations of our detection methods. For example, the lower limit of quantification detected in the study by Upton et al. was about 0.5 ng/mL (This estimate is based on the content of the article, as no specific figures were mentioned in the text.), which is approximately 200 times more sensitive than our method (LLOQ = 0.1 µg/mL). While our results suggest very limited CSF exposure, they cannot definitively rule out minimal penetration. Therefore, the CSF permeability of bedaquiline needs to be further studied and explored. Contezolid, a novel oxazolidinone, has fewer adverse reactions than LZD, which may provide an option for the treatment of CNS TB [13]. The pharmacokinetic data of contezolid indicate that contezolid achieves CSF concentrations ranging from 0.57 to 0.64 µg/mL, representing approximately 10% of the plasma unbound drug fraction (corresponding to total plasma concentrations of 6.26–8.61 µg/mL). This finding suggests that while the unbound drug component demonstrates effective penetration across the BBB, the overall CNS penetration efficiency remains limited, as evidenced by a CSF/plasma concentration ratio of 0.07–0.15. The interpretation of these ratios must consider plasma protein binding. It is important to interpret CSF penetration in the context of each drug’s unbound fraction, as only the free (non–protein-bound) drug can cross the BBB. Literature indicates that the plasma protein binding of moxifloxacin is approximately 40%–60% [6], contezolid approximately 90% [13] and pyrazinamide about 1% [14]. No specific data on plasma protein binding were found for bedaquiline and cycloserine. It is generally accepted that cycloserine exhibits negligible plasma protein binding, whereas bedaquiline demonstrates extremely high plasma protein binding (greater than 99.9%). Considering these reported values, the apparent “poor” CSF penetration observed for highly protein-bound drugs such as bedaquiline and contezolid may underestimate the true permeability of their pharmacologically active free fraction. Conversely, the high CSF/plasma ratios observed for cycloserine and pyrazinamide are consistent with their low protein binding and high diffusibility across the BBB. However, it must be emphasized that these published protein binding data were obtained under different physiological conditions and may not fully reflect the altered plasma protein status in critically ill or inflamed states, such as MDR-TBM. Since our assay measured total drug concentrations, the reported CSF/plasma ratios for highly protein-bound drugs such as contezolid (~ 90%) and bedaquiline (>99.9%) likely underestimate the true penetration of the active, unbound drug fraction. For example, contezolid’s unbound CSF/plasma ratio may be significantly higher than the reported total ratio of 0.07–0.15. In the future studies we should directly measure unbound concentrations to accurately assess CNS penetration. These pharmacokinetic characteristics underscore the need for cautious clinical evaluation of its therapeutic utility in managing drug-resistant CNS tuberculosis infections.
