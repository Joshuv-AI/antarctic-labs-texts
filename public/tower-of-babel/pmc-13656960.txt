Design, synthesis, and biotechnological evaluation of Thiophene-based chalcones and Δ2-pyrazoline derivatives: Antibacterial, cytotoxic, and molecular docking studies
Authors: Inas S Mahdi, Ghosoun Lafta Mohsen, Ahmed Mutanabbi Abdula, Abdulkadir M Noori Jassim, Ayaat Hamdan Abd, Younis Baqi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntimicrobial resistance (AMR) and cancer are among the most extensively studied areas that still require further improvement. In the present study, four new compounds were synthesized via Claisen-Schmidt condensation reaction followed by ring closure, yielding pyrazoline heterocyclic derivatives bearing a thiophene moiety. The compound structures were confirmed using spectroscopic techniques, such as FT-IR, NMR, and GC–MS. These compounds were biologically evaluated for antimicrobial activity against two Gram-positive bacteria (Staphylococcus aureus and Streptococcus pyogenes) and two Gram-negative bacteria (Escherichia coli and Pseudomonas aeruginosa), and for anticancer activity against the human cancerous breast cell line (MCF-7). Tested compounds (1–4) exhibited antibacterial activity against the isolate strains, with MICs ranging from 128 to 512 μg/mL and MBCs from 256 to 1024 μg/mL. Compound 2 was the most potent inhibitor against MCF-7, with an IC50 of 46.71 μg/mL (180.17 μM), while the other compounds were less active. Molecular docking against glucosamine-6-phosphate synthase and tubulin indicated favorable binding affinities, with compound 4 showing the strongest predicted binding energies of −8.0 and − 7.4 kcal/mol, respectively. Overall, these results provide structure–property information for thiophene-based chalcone and their 2-pyrazoline scaffolds, and a basis for further optimization.Keywords: Antibacterial, Molecular docking, MCF-7, Pyrazoline, Spectroscopic characterization

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) remains one of the most threatening and challenging health-related issues worldwide.1, 2 AMR is a natural response to the overuse or misuse of antimicrobial medications, leading to the development of resistance mechanisms in pathogenic microorganisms against known antimicrobial drugs.3 On the other hand, cancer is one of the leading causes of death worldwide, with breast cancer among the most frequently diagnosed malignancies.4

Natural products and their derivatives have been used for centuries as leading molecules in the discovery of novel medications, including antimicrobials and anticancer agents, e.g., penicillin, cephalosporin, erythromycin, and vancomycin (antibiotics),5, 6 as well as paclitaxel, vincristine, and doxorubicin (anticancer agents).7, 8, 9

Chalcone is found naturally in plants and plays a crucial role in the biosynthesis of polyphenolic compounds.10 The chalcone skeleton consists of two aromatic rings separated by three carbons, forming an enone moiety. This enone moiety is an active functional group that undergoes Michael addition (cyclization) reactions, yielding interesting heterocyclic rings, such as pyrazoline and oxazoline, which are well documented in the literature to possess various biological activities.11, 12, 13

Thiophene-chalcone derivatives have been primarily investigated for antibacterial and anticancer activities.14 In another study, they have been described as anti-inflammatory and antioxidant agents.15 Moreover, benzo[b]thiophene–chalcone analogues have been explored as cholinesterase inhibitors.16 On the other hand, Δ2-pyrazoline derivatives, in contrast, have been widely explored as acetylcholinesterase inhibitors17 and as antibacterial agents,18 and they are frequently described as anticancer candidates in recent reviews,19 including anti-inflammatory series targeting COX-2/5-LOX in some reports.12

The target enzyme, glucosamine-6-phosphate (GlcN-6-P) synthase, catalyzes the formation of D-glucosamine-6-phosphate, a precursor of UDP-N-acetylglucosamine, which is an essential building block in bacterial cell-wall biosynthesis. GlcN-6-P synthase active site is composed of the following amino acids: threonine 302, serine 303, serine 347, glutamine 348, serine 349, threonine 352, valine 399, and alanine 602. Inhibition of GlcN-6-P synthase activity plays an essential role in preventing bacterial cell wall biosynthesis, thereby hampering bacterial growth and propagation.20 Furthermore, tubulin, composed of α- and β-tubulin heterodimers, forms microtubules that are essential for maintaining cell shape, intracellular transport, and chromosome segregation during mitosis.21, 22, 23 Antagonizing tubulin disrupts microtubule dynamics, making it a critical target for anticancer therapeutics.24 Previous studies have demonstrated the biological potential of thiophene-containing chalcones25, 26 and pyrazoline derivatives,27, 28, 29 including antibacterial and anticancer activities. However, these studies have generally investigated the two heterocyclic scaffolds separately or have focused on broader structural series. Direct comparison of thiophene-containing chalcones with their corresponding Δ2-pyrazoline derivatives, particularly with respect to the effect of scaffold transformation and para-substitution, remains comparatively limited. In the present study, compounds 1 and 2 represent thiophene-containing chalcones bearing para-bromo and para-nitro substituents, respectively, while compounds 3 and 4 are their corresponding Δ2-pyrazoline derivatives. This design provides a focused framework for examining how conversion of the chalcone scaffold into a Δ2-pyrazoline ring and variation of the para substituent influence the antibacterial and cytotoxic properties of the compounds. Despite the reported biological potential of thiophene-based chalcones and Δ2-pyrazolines, the structure–activity effects of converting thiophene-containing chalcones into their corresponding pyrazoline derivatives remain insufficiently explored. This scaffold transformation may alter molecular conformation and interactions with biological targets while retaining the thiophene moiety, providing a rational approach for investigating antibacterial and cytotoxic activity. This work describes the synthesis of 1-(aryl)-3-(thiophen-3-yl)prop-2-en-1-one derivatives (1 and 2) and their 3-(aryl)-5-(thiophen-3-yl)-4,5-dihydro-1H-pyrazole cyclization products (3 and 4). The structures of the compounds (1–4) were confirmed using FT-IR, 1H and 13C NMR, and GC–MS spectroscopy. The antibacterial activity of all compounds was evaluated against S. aureus, S. pyogenes, E. coli, and P. aeruginosa, while their anticancer potential was assessed using human breast cancer cell line (MCF-7). In silico studies performed with PyRx software provided insights into the interactions of the synthesized compounds with GlcN-6-P synthase and tubulin, supporting the design of 1-(aryl)-3-(thiophen-3-yl)prop-2-en-1-one derivative scaffolds and related cyclization products as promising candidates for dual-function as antimicrobial and anticancer agents.

Results and discussion

Chemistry

Synthesis of chalcone derivatives (1–2)

1-(Aryl)-3-(thiophen-3-yl) prop-2-en-1-one derivatives (1–2) were synthesized through a modified Claisen–Schmidt condensation reaction (Scheme 1). Equimolar quantities of the corresponding thiophene-3-carbaldehyde and methyl ketone, either 4-bromoacetophenone or 4-nitroacetophenone, were reacted in the presence of aqueous sodium hydroxide solution (40%) and ethanol as the reaction solvent. The reaction mixture was stirred at room temperature for 12 h, yielding the target chalcone derivatives (1–2) in very good to excellent isolated yields. Synthesis of pyrazoline derivatives (3–4)

3-(4-Substitutedphenyl)-5-(thiophen-3-yl)-4,5-dihydro-1H-pyrazole derivatives (3–4) were synthesized via condensation followed by the intramolecular cyclization reaction between the chalcones (1–2) with hydrazine hydrate in ethanol at room temperature. In the cyclization step, AcOH acts as an acid catalyst, facilitating the condensation of hydrazine hydrate with the chalcone carbonyl group and promoting the subsequent intramolecular ring-closure to form the Δ2-pyrazoline scaffold. After the reaction cooled to rt., the precipitate was collected using Buchner suction filtration, and the obtained dry solid was recrystallized using hot ethanol, affording the desired pyrazoline derivatives (3–4) in excellent isolated yields. Biology

Antibacterial assay

The synthesized compounds (1–4) were investigated for their potential bioactivity against two Gram-positive bacteria (S. aureus and S. pyogenes), and two Gram-negative bacteria (E. coli and P. aeruginosa). This was conducted through the determination of the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) values, as depicted in Table 1. The MIC was defined as the lowest concentration that prevented detectable bacterial growth based on the resazurin colorimetric endpoint. The MBC was defined as the lowest concentration at which no bacterial growth was observed upon subculture. An MBC/MIC ratio ≤ 4 was considered indicative of a bactericidal effect, whereas a ratio > 4 was considered indicative of a relatively bacteriostatic effect. For further information see Figs. S15 and S16 in the Supplementary Materials.

The obtained results reveal variable levels of activity among the tested species and provide insights into the bacteriostatic and bactericidal nature of the compounds. In general, all tested compounds (1–4) exhibited potent inhibitory and bactericidal activity, although their effectiveness varied among different bacterial species. For S. aureus, the MIC and MBC values were relatively low, 256 and 512 μg/mL, respectively, suggesting that all the compounds possess comparable activity against this species. The narrow difference between MBC and MIC values indicates a bactericidal effect, as bacterial killing occurs at concentrations close to the inhibitory level. Similarly, S. pyogenes displayed bactericidal activity at 1024 μg/mL and inhibition at 512 μg/mL for all tested compounds (1–4). The MBC/MIC ratios were consistent among the tested compounds and bacterial isolates. For S. pyogenes, the MBC/MIC ratio was 2 (1024/512), which is consistent with a bactericidal effect. In contrast, combinations showing MBC/MIC ratios greater than 4 may indicate a relatively bacteriostatic effect. These findings suggest that the antibacterial effect may vary depending on both the compound and the bacterial strain. This observation may be related to differences in cell wall structure and permeability typical of Gram-positive species. The results regarding to E. coli exhibited moderate MBC values (256–1024 μg/mL) and relatively low MIC values (128–512 μg/mL). Chalcones 1 and 2 demonstrated stronger bactericidal activity (MBC/MIC ≈ 1), whereas pyrazoline 3 showed the highest MBC (1024 μg/mL), suggesting reduced effectiveness and a weaker killing potential. These variations may reflect differences in the binding affinity to glucosamine-6-phosphate synthase. Regarding P. aeruginosa, the synthesized compounds (1–4) showed MIC values ranging from 128 to 512 μg/mL, but MBC values extended from 256 to 1024 μg/mL. Notably, Δ2-Pyrazoline 4 exhibited the lowest MBC value equal to 256 μg/mL, indicating the highest bactericidal potency against this strain, while all other compounds (1, 2, and 3) were less effective. The high resistance of P. aeruginosa compared with other tested bacteria aligns with its well-known multidrug-resistant nature.30, 31, 32 Finally, the antibacterial results suggest that the synthesized derivative 4 demonstrates more favorable antibacterial profiles, exhibiting bactericidal activity at concentrations comparable to their respective MIC values. These findings indicate the potential of the synthesized derivatives (1–4) as measurable antibacterial agents. In addition to MIC/MBC determination, the antibacterial activity of the synthesized derivatives1, 2, 3, 4 and ciprofloxacin as a positive control was further assessed using the agar well diffusion method. Each compound was tested at its respective MBC concentration to provide complementary evidence of antibacterial activity, and the resulting inhibition zones were measured in millimeters and are presented in Table 2.

Cytotoxicity against the MCF-7 cell line

The cytotoxic potential of the newly synthesized compounds (1–4) was investigated against MCF-7, the human breast adenocarcinoma cell line, using a concentration response design. MCF-7 cells were exposed to varying concentrations of each tested compound. The cell viability (%) was calculated to represent the antiproliferative activity. Overall, all derivatives reduced MCF-7 viability in a concentration-dependent manner, generating typical sigmoidal dose response trends when viability was plotted against log concentration (μg/mL) (Fig. 1).

Concentration–response curves showing MCF-7 cell viability (%) as a function of log₁₀ concentration (μg/mL) for the synthesized compounds (1–4). Data are presented as mean ± SD of four replicate measurements.

Clear differences in potency were observed across the present series based on IC50 values and the concentration range over which viability remained suppressed. Chalcone 2 and pyrazoline 3 were the most active derivatives, exhibiting IC₅₀ values of 46.71 μg/mL (180.17 μM) and 87.26 μg/mL (283.73 μM), respectively. In comparison, chalcone 1 showed moderate activity (IC₅₀ = 205.00 μg/mL; 701.63 μM), whereas pyrazoline 4 exhibited the weakest response. At 600 μg/mL, viability decreased to 18% for compound 2 and 36% for compound 1, confirming a marked activity gap between these derivatives. Compound 3 produced the strongest inhibition at higher concentrations, with viability values of approximately 15–18% within the 300–600 μg/mL concentration range, indicating pronounced suppression of cell proliferation at elevated exposure levels.

The behavior at intermediate and lower concentrations further distinguished the compounds. Compound 2 maintained viability below 50% up to 75 μg/mL, indicating sustained inhibition over a relatively broad concentration range. Compound 3 showed gradual recovery upon dilution; however, viability remained below ∼70% down to 31 μg/mL and approached baseline only at very low concentrations (≤ 3.9 μg/mL), consistent with persistent inhibitory activity across multiple dilution steps. In contrast, compound 1 lost activity rapidly at lower concentrations, with viability returning to 95–100% at concentrations ≤18 μg/mL. Compound 4 caused only a modest reduction in viability (≈60% at 300 μg/mL) and did not demonstrate a pronounced concentration-dependent decrease, suggesting limited cytotoxic activity under the applied conditions. Collectively, these findings indicate that cytotoxic potency follows the order compound 2 > compound 3 > compound 1 > > compound 4, identifying compounds 2 and 3 as the hit candidates for further anticancer evaluation within this series, for further information see Figs. S17–S26 in the Supplementary Materials. Comparison of the synthesized compounds indicates that both the molecular scaffold and the para-substituent influence the observed biological activity. For antibacterial activity, conversion of the chalcone scaffold into the corresponding Δ2-pyrazoline produced a strain-dependent effect. In particular, pyrazoline 4 showed markedly improved activity against P. aeruginosa (MIC = 128 μg/mL; MBC = 256 μg/mL) compared with its chalcone analogue 2, whereas pyrazoline 3 showed reduced activity against E. coli compared with chalcone 1. For cytotoxicity against MCF-7 cells, the nitro-substituted chalcone 2 was the most active compound (IC50 = 46.71 μg/mL), whereas its cyclized analogue 4 showed weak activity. In contrast, the bromo-substituted pyrazoline 3 retained greater cytotoxic activity than its corresponding chalcone 1. These findings indicate that the effect of the para substituent is dependent on the molecular scaffold rather than following a simple trend. The thiophene moiety provides a common structural feature across the series, while conversion of the α,β-unsaturated carbonyl system into the Δ2-pyrazoline ring changes the molecular geometry and hydrogen-bonding characteristics, which may contribute to the different antibacterial and cytotoxic profiles observed.

In silico studies of the synthesized compounds (1–4)

Molecular docking of synthesized compounds 1–4 into the active site of glucosamine-6-phosphate synthase

The targeted enzyme, GlcN-6-P synthase, has been identified as the enzyme of choice for antimicrobial agents.33 It is well documented that glucosamine-6-phosphate (GlcN-6-P) synthase plays a key role in the production of D-glucosamine-6-phosphate, an essential intermediate in the biosynthesis of the bacterial cell wall.34 In the first step the enzyme catalyzes the formation of GlcN-6-P from d-fructose-6-phosphate (Fru-6-P) then by the preparation of uridine-5-diphospho-N-acetyl-D-glucosamine (UDP-GlcNAc), which play crucial role in the cell wall biosynthesis in bacteria.35 The newly synthesized two classes of compounds; chalcones (1–2) and Δ2-pyrazolines (3–4) were docked in GlcN-6-P synthase (PDB: 1MOQ) active site. The molecular docking analysis of synthesized compounds (1–4) was conducted to explore their binding affinity and interaction energy within the active-site region of the target enzyme, as shown in Fig. 2. The obtained docking outcomes demonstrated distinct variations in binding energy and interaction modes, providing valuable insights into their potential inhibitory efficiency. Among the synthesized compounds, compound 4 exhibited the most favorable binding affinity (−8.0 kcal/mol), revealing a stable and favorable interaction within the enzyme's active site. This compound formed four hydrogen bonds with VAL399, GLN348, SER303, and SER349 residues, in addition to a carbon–hydrogen bond with SER347 as depicted in Fig. 2. The presence of multiple hydrogen bonds and polar interactions likely enhances the binding stability and contributes to the higher antibacterial activity observed in the in vitro assay, where compound 4 demonstrated the highest potency among all tested derivatives. The docking results provide a structure-based hypothesis that may help rationalize the observed antibacterial activity, particularly for compound 4. However, the docking scores should not be interpreted as direct evidence of biological activity or mechanism of action, and the limited number of compounds evaluated does not permit a meaningful statistical correlation between docking scores and experimental activity. On the other hand, compound 3 showed a slightly lower binding energy (−7.3 kcal/mol) compared to compound 4 but still displayed a favorable interaction profile. It formed four hydrogen bonds with SER303, LYS603, VAL399, and ALA602, along with π-anion and π-donor hydrogen bond interactions involving GLU488 and GLN348, respectively. Additionally, alkyl and π-alkyl interactions with LEU601 and LEU484 residues contributed to the stabilization of the ligand within the enzyme's hydrophobic pocket. These interactions suggest that compound 3 adopts a well-fitted orientation inside the active site. Compound 1 showed moderate binding affinity (−7.8 kcal/mol), forming two hydrogen bonds with THR302 and SER401, and a π-donor hydrogen bond with GLN:A:348. The additional alkyl and π-alkyl hydrophobic contacts with LEU601 and LEU484 stabilize the complex but to a lesser extent than compounds 3 and 4. The reduced number of polar interactions may explain the comparatively weaker binding energy and expected lower antibacterial activity. Compound 2, despite showing multiple hydrogen bonding interactions with SER349, THR352, and SER401 residues, demonstrated a less favorable binding affinity (−7.5 kcal/mol), suggesting a less stable interaction with the GlcN-6-P synthase. Additionally, hydrophobic interaction with LEU601 was also observed. Compounds showing higher number of hydrogen bonds, especially with residues such as SER, VAL, and GLN within the catalytic pocket, exhibited better docking scores and biological activities. Moreover, the presence of hydrophobic and π-π interactions with residues like LEU601 and LEU484 further stabilized the binding conformation and enhanced the overall affinity. Finally, these findings confirm that compound 4 possesses the optimal binding configuration and interaction pattern within the GlcN-6-P synthase active site, which aligns well with its superior in vitro antibacterial activity. The partial agreement between the computational and experimental results, particularly for compound 4 against P. aeruginosa, supports the docking approach as a reliable predictive tool for assessing binding potential and guiding the design of more potent derivatives.

Molecular docking of synthesized compounds (1–4) into the tubulin binding site

Tubulin found as a dimeric shape, which comprises two subunits, α and β, these are found to be structurally related but nonidentical subunits.36, 37 Colchicine and its derivatives have been reported to antagonize tubulin receptor, suppressing tubulin polymerization. This rapidly disrupts mitotic spindle formation, impairs cytoskeletal function, and arrests mitosis, thereby helping to limit metastasis in multiple cancer types.38, 39, 40

The newly synthesized compounds (1–4) were in silico studied against the colchicine binding center within the tubulin receptor (PDB: 4O2B) to study their binding affinities and physical interaction within the active center of the tubulin receptor (Fig. 3). All compounds showed favorable binding affinity ranging from −6.8 to −7.4 kcal/mol, indicating good affinity toward the tubulin active pocket. Among the synthesized ligands, pyrazoline 4 showed the lowest binding energy (−7.4 kcal/mol), followed closely by compounds 2 and 3 (−7.0 kcal/mol each), while compound 1 showed a slightly lower value (−6.8 kcal/mol). These values suggest that all four ligands have the potential to form stable complexes with the receptor. In terms of binding interactions, compound 1 was stabilized primarily by hydrophobic contacts. It formed one hydrogen bond with ASN:B:258, complemented by multiple alkyl and π-alkyl interactions involving residues CYS:B:241, ILE:B:318, ALA:B:250, ALA:A:180, and LYS:B:254. Additionally, the presence of carbon–hydrogen and π–sigma interactions with Leu248 and Leu255 of chain B contributed to the overall stability of the ligand–protein complex. The predominance of hydrophobic and π-based interactions indicates that compound 1 fits well within a predominantly nonpolar site of the receptor active site. Compound 2 exhibited a more extensive hydrogen-bonding network by forming five hydrogen bonds with Tyr224, Gln11, Ser140, Thr145, and Gly146 of chain A. These polar interactions are complemented by five alkyl and π-alkyl hydrophobic contacts with residues ALA:A:12, ALA:A:180, ALA:B:250, LYS:B:254, and LEU:B:248. The coexistence of both potent hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions implies that compound 2 achieves a balanced stabilization mechanism, which likely contributes to its relatively high binding affinity. The docking results for compound 3 indicated that hydrophobic interactions were the dominant stabilizing forces, as evidenced by seven alkyl and π-alkyl interactions involving ALA:A:180, LEU:B:248, ALA:B:250, CYS:B:241, and LEU:B:242. Furthermore, compound 3 engaged in a π-amide stacked interaction with THR:A:179 and a π-sigma interaction with LEU:B:255, which may enhance the conformational stability of the ligand within the protein binding pocket. The predominance of hydrophobic contacts suggests that compound 3 interacts effectively within the nonpolar regions of the active site, consistent with its favorable binding score (−7.0 kcal/mol). Finally, compound 4 showed two hydrogen bonds with amino acids CYS:B:241 and LEU:B:242, as well as four alkyl and π-alkyl interactions with LYS:B:352, LEU:B:248, ALA:B:250, and CYS:B:241. In addition, two π-sigma interactions were observed with LEU:B:248 and LEU:B:255.

Molecular docking poses of the synthesized compounds (1–4) within the catalytic center of tubulin receptor: (A) compound 1, (B) compound 2, (C) compound 3, and (D) compound 4.

The combined presence of multiple hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonding suggests that compound 4 achieves optimal binding through both polar and nonpolar stabilization forces, supporting its superior binding energy value (−7.4 kcal/mol). Overall, the docking results indicate that hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions are the primary forces driving ligand stabilization within the tubulin active site. Among the tested compounds, compounds 2–4 showed the strongest overall binding potentials. Although compound 4 exhibited the most favorable docking score (−7.4 kcal/mol), it showed the lowest IC50 activity in the in vitro assay. This inconsistency may be related to the predominance of hydrophobic interactions and the limited number of hydrogen bonds formed by compound 4, which could affect its ability to interact effectively with catalytically important residues. Furthermore, its hydrophobic character may influence its physicochemical and pharmacokinetic properties, potentially affecting solubility, bioavailability, and receptor–ligand interactions under biological conditions. Therefore, although compound 4 exhibited a favorable in silico binding affinity, its physicochemical properties and interaction profile may contribute to its relatively weak in vitro cytotoxic activity. However, these factors were not computationally evaluated in the present study and should therefore be considered possible explanations rather than definitive causes.

Materials and methods

Chemistry part

General

All starting materials and solvents were obtained from commercial suppliers (BDH, Sigma Aldrich, and Merck) and were used as purchased without purification. An Electrothermal capillary apparatus (Digital Stuart Scientific SMP30) was used to measure the melting range of the newly synthesized compounds, which were uncorrected. Nuclear magnetic resonance spectra (NMR) of the synthesized derivatives were collected on a Bruker Avance Neo NMR spectrometer (400 and 500 MHz). PLATINUM-ATR ALPHA II Spectrometer (Bruker) was used to collect the FT-IR spectrum. Gas chromatography-mass spectrophotometer (GC–MS) was measured on GC–MS-QP 2010 PLUS Shimadzu model.

General procedure A: synthesis of the chalcone derivatives (1–2)

Chalcone derivatives 1 and 2 were prepared through a modified Claisen–Schmidt condensation reaction using the appropriate acetophenone derivatives and thiophene-3-carbaldehyde following published procedure.41, 42, 43 A solution of either 4-bromoacetophenone or 4-nitroacetophenone (1 mmol) in ethanol (10 mL) was treated with 40% aqueous NaOH (1 mL). After stirring the mixture at room temperature for 30 min, thiophene-3-carbaldehyde (1 mmol) was introduced, and the reaction was allowed to proceed for 12 h at the same temperature. Reaction progress was monitored by TLC using ethyl acetate/hexane (1:1, v/v) as the mobile phase. Once reaction is over, ice-cold water (10 mL) was added to induce precipitation. The resulting solid was filtered by Buchner suction filtration, washed with water, the solid was allowed to dry, and the resulting solid was recrystallized using hot ethanol.

General procedure B: synthesis of the Δ2-pyrazoline derivatives (3–4)

Δ2-pyrazoline compounds (3–4) were synthesized via condensation followed by intramolecular cyclization of the chalcone with hydrazine hydrate using a modified synthetic pathway.44, 45, 46 In brief, an ethanolic solution (10 mL) containing 1-(aryl)-3-(thiophen-3-yl) prop-2-en-1-one derivatives (1–2) (10 mmol) was treated with seven drops of glacial acetic acid and stirred at rt. for 20 min. Subsequently, hydrazine hydrate (80%, 2.42 mL, 50 mmol) was added dropwise, and the resulting mixture was stirred at room temperature for 6–8 h. The reaction was monitored by TLC using eluent composed of 2:1 ethyl acetate:hexane. Upon completion, the formed precipitate was collected using Buchner suction filtration, washed with diethyl ether, and recrystallized using hot ethanol to obtain the target products.

Biology

Determination of the minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of newly synthesized compounds 1–4

The antibacterial properties of 1-(aryl)-3-(thiophen-3-yl) prop-2-en-1-one derivatives1, 2 and the 3-(aryl)-5-(thiophen-3-yl)-4,5-dihydro-1H-pyrazole derivatives3, 4 were tested against four bacterial species, including S. aureus, S. pyogenes, E. coli, and P. aeruginosa, and their MIC were determined following reported protocol.47, 48, 49 All isolates (S. aureus, S. pyogenes, E. coli, and P. aeruginosa) were obtained from urine samples of patients with urinary tract infections (UTIs) after ethical approval from the Mustansiriyah University, College of Science Research Ethics Committee (Ref.: BCSMU/1221/00033 M; August 1, 2023). Sample collection and handling were performed in accordance with the approved protocol and institutional biomedical research guidelines, and informed consent procedures were applied as required. The resazurin (Alamar Blue) solution was prepared by dissolving resazurin (0.015 g) in sterile distilled water (100 mL). The mixture was mixed very well using a vortex mixer until all resazurin solid is completely dissolved in the water. The prepared solution was stored in a fridge at 4 °C and used within one week. Two-fold serial dilutions (64–1024 μg/mL) of the synthesized derivatives were made from a 10 mg·mL−1 stock solution. Dilutions were carried out in a microtiter plate containing Mueller–Hinton broth. Except for the negative control, each well was inoculated with 20 μL of a bacterial suspension adjusted to the 0.5 McFarland standard (1.5 × 108 CFU/mL). The microtiter plates were then incubated at 37 °C for 18–20 h. Subsequently, 20 μL of resazurin dye was added to each well, followed by further incubation for 2 h to monitor any color change.

Determination of the minimum bactericidal concentrations (MBCs) of compounds 1–4

After recording the MIC endpoint, indicated by the unchanged blue color of resazurin, the MBC was evaluated by transferring the contents of wells containing concentrations above the MIC onto Mueller–Hinton agar plates. The plates were then incubated under the same bacterial growth conditions. The MBC was taken as the lowest concentration that showed no colony formation after direct plating of the well contents.50

Antibacterial screening of the compounds (1–4): inhibition zone determination (mm)

The antibacterial effect of all compounds (1–4) was further assessed at the determined MBC level for each compound against each tested bacterial isolate; S. aureus, S. pyogenes, E. coli, and P. aeruginosa, following previously published protocol.51, 52 About 0.1 mL of each bacterial isolate was cultured at 37 °C in the broth obtained from Mueller-Hinton for 18–24 h. A single colony from each culture was transferred into 5 mL of normal saline to yield a bacterial suspension of moderate turbidity, adjusted to McFarland standard (1.5 × 108 CFU/mL). Using a sterile cotton swab, a portion of the bacterial suspension was evenly distributed over the surface of Mueller-Hinton agar plates and allowed to stand for 10 min. Wells of 5 mm in diameter (three wells per plate) were punched into the agar layer. After the agar plugs were removed, 50 μL of test compounds (1–4) was carefully dispensed into each well. The inoculated plates were then incubated at 37 °C for 18 h, after which the inhibition zones' diameters were measured in millimeters.

In Vitro cytotoxicity evaluation of compounds 1–4 against MCF-7 breast cancer cells

Materials and equipment

The MCF-7 human breast adenocarcinoma cell line (HTB-22; ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) was employed in this study. RPMI 1640 medium, fetal bovine serum (FBS), and penicillin/streptomycin were supplied by Biowest (France). Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and MTT reagent [3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide] were obtained from Merck (Germany). Absorbance was measured with an ELISA microplate reader (Bio-Rad, USA). Pipettes, sterile syringes, and sterile 96-well tissue culture plates were purchased from SPL (China).

Sample preparation and dilution

A stock solution (600 μg/mL) of each synthesized compound (1–4) was prepared by dissolving the compounds in DMSO. Serial dilution was then carried out to provide final concentrations of 2.3, 4.6, 9.37, 18.7, 37.5, 75, 150, 300, and 600 μg/mL.

Cell culture, treatment, and MTT viability assay

MCF-7 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 8000 cells per well. The prepared plates were placed in an incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 24 h to permit cell attachment. After incubation, the medium composed of: penicillin/streptomycin, 10% of FBS, and RPMI-1640, was discarded and replaced with 100 μL of the synthesized compounds at serial concentrations (2.3–600 μg/mL). The resulting plates were placed in the incubator for an additional 48 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2. At the end of the treatment period, the medium was discarded, and 20 μL of MTT solution (0.5 mg/mL in PBS) was introduced into each well. The plates were returned to the incubator for 4 h under the same conditions. Thereafter, the MTT solution was removed, and the formed formazan crystals were dissolved in 100 μL of DMSO. Finally, absorbance was recorded at 570 nm using an ELISA microplate reader.53, 54

In silico studies

The crystal structures of GlcN-6-P synthase in complex with glucosamine-6-phosphate (PDB code 1MOQ) and tubulin receptor in complex with colchicine (PDB code: 4O2B) were downloaded from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org). Before performing the docking process, water molecules were omitted from the studied protein crystal structures, and any damaged or missing amino acid residues were repaired accordingly following published protocol.55, 56, 57 The prepared structures of GlcN-6-P synthase and tubulin were subsequently processed using PyRx AutoDock Vina software (0.8) with 10 output modes (poses) generated for each ligand. 3D structures of the synthesized compounds (1–4) were generated in MOL format using ChemDraw Ultra (7.0) and later converted to PDB format using the Open Babel program (3.1.1). The docking protocol was validated by re-docking the co-crystallized ligands into their respective binding sites. The re-docking reproduced the crystallographic ligand poses with RMSD values of 0.00 Å for both glucosamine-6-phosphate synthase and tubulin. All synthesized derivatives were docked into the active binding sites of GlcN-6-P synthase and tubulin after removing their respective co-crystallized ligands, glucosamine-6-phosphate and colchicine, respectively. The grid box parameters for GlcN-6-P synthase were defined as X = 31.62, Y = 17.68, and Z = − 1.44, with grid sizes of 23.87, 26.45, and 23.34, respectively. For the tubulin receptor, the corresponding grid dimensions were X = 18.22, Y = 66.14, and Z = 42.80, with grid sizes of 20.14, 19.67, and 18.83. The docking procedure calculated the binding affinities (kcal·mol−1) for each complex, and the best Auto-Dock scores were used to interpret the most favorable binding conformations. The resulting docking poses were visualized and analyzed using Discovery Studio 2021, and the reliability of the docking protocol was validated by re-docking the native ligands (glucosamine-6-phosphate and colchicine) into the active sites of their respective proteins. The accuracy and reproducibility of this molecular docking protocol were validated as previously reported.58, 59, 60

Conclusions

Two new chalcone bearing 3-thiophene moiety (1–2) and their corresponding Δ2-pyrazoline derivatives (3–4) were synthesized following Claisen–Schmidt condensation reaction to yield chalcones (1–2). These newly synthesized chalcones (1–2) were further reacted with hydrazine followed by intramolecular conjugate addition/cyclization, furnishing novel Δ2-pyrazolines (3–4). All compounds (1–4) were chemically identified and characterized using spectroscopic techniques, including FT-IR, NMR, and GC–MS spectroscopy. The newly synthesized compounds were biologically evaluated as antimicrobial agent against four bacterial species; S. aureus and S. pyogenes (Gram-positive), E. coli, and P. aeruginosa (Gram-negative). The synthesized compounds demonstrated promising antibacterial and cytotoxic activities, with the biological response depending on both the molecular scaffold and the nature of the substituent. In particular, compound 4 showed the most favorable antibacterial activity against P. aeruginosa, whereas compound 2 exhibited the most pronounced cytotoxic activity against MCF-7 cells. These findings indicate that conversion of the chalcone scaffold into the corresponding Δ2-pyrazoline derivatives can substantially modify biological activity, and that the effect of the substituent is dependent on the molecular scaffold. The present findings provide useful structure–activity insights for further optimization of thiophene-based chalcone and Δ2-pyrazoline derivatives. In order to assess the biological activity, in silico, studies of the synthesized compounds were performed into the active site of GlcN-6-P synthase and tubulin, the antimicrobial and anticancer drug targets, respectively. The docking results suggest a favorable binding energies and relevant interaction patterns inside both targets, with compound 4 giving the best predicted affinity toward GlcN-6-P synthase, consistent with its antibacterial performance. Docking into the colchicine binding site of tubulin also indicated good affinity for the synthesized compounds; however, the in vitro cytotoxicity trend suggests that physicochemical factors, e.g., solubility and effective cellular exposure, may attribute to the biological response beyond docking scores. In summary, the designed thiophene bearing chalcones and their Δ2-pyrazoline derivatives, might represent promising lead scaffolds for further optimization toward novel antibacterial and/or anticancer agents, supported by both experimental activity and in silico target binding data.
