Reversal of refractory epilepsy in an adult with GAMT deficiency: A case report and comparative literature review
Authors: Wei‐Hung Sung, Hsi Chen, Che‐Wei Hsu, Meng‐Han Tsai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveGuanidinoacetate methyltransferase (GAMT) deficiency is a rare autosomal recessive creatine biosynthesis disorder. Reports specifically focusing on adult patients remain scarce.MethodsWe report a 52‐year‐old female (the oldest documented case of GAMT deficiency to date) presenting with severe intellectual disability, refractory epilepsy, and adult‐onset disease progression. Whole‐exome sequencing identified novel compound heterozygous GAMT variants (c.265_267del and c.253G>T), and magnetic resonance spectroscopy confirmed cerebral creatine depletion. Remarkably, initiating oral creatine supplementation at age 52 achieved complete seizure freedom, normalization of the electroencephalogram (EEG), and significant motor function recovery.ResultsA comprehensive review revealed that untreated adult patients face a high risk of progressive neurological deterioration. Compared to the pediatric cohort, adults exhibited a significantly higher prevalence of movement disorders (69.2% vs. 33.1%, p < 0.001) and a trend toward a higher prevalence of epilepsy (92.3% vs. 74.2%). Especially, atonic seizures (drop attacks) are particularly characteristic of adult GAMT deficiency, with EEGs frequently demonstrating background slowing, multifocal epileptiform discharges, and electrodecremental patterns.SignificanceThis study underscores that continuous disease progression and prolonged neurotoxicity still could compromise the well‐being of adult GAMT deficiency patients. Accurate diagnosis is essential for adults presenting with developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (DEE) phenotype, as even late treatment for GAMT deficiency can still dramatically alleviate the epileptic burden and reverse motor decline.Plain Language SummaryWe report the oldest known adult diagnosed with GAMT deficiency. Our case demonstrates that starting creatine treatment even at age 52 can dramatically reduce seizures and restore motor function. Literature shows that untreated adults suffer from more severe epilepsy and movement disorders than children, with symptoms worsening over time. These findings highlight the critical need for genetic testing in adults with unexplained severe epilepsy; timely creatine supplementation can halt cognitive decline and significantly restore quality of life.

INTRODUCTION

Guanidinoacetate methyltransferase (GAMT) deficiency is a rare autosomal recessive genetic disorder caused by pathogenic variants in the GAMT gene, which is located on the short arm of chromosome 19 (19p13.3). The pathogenesis involves a defect in the second enzymatic step of the creatine biosynthesis pathway, wherein the impaired transfer of a methyl group from S‐adenosylmethionine (SAM) to guanidinoacetate (GAA) disrupts creatine synthesis. 1 This leads to the profound depletion of creatine in the brain and muscles, coupled with the accumulation of neurotoxic GAA. 2 , 3 Clinically, the condition is commonly characterized by epilepsy, intellectual disability or developmental delay (ID/DD) with prominent speech deficits, movement disorders, and behavioral disturbances. In suspected cases, initial clinical screening typically involves measuring GAA, creatine, and creatinine levels in plasma or urine. The diagnosis can be further supported by advanced modalities, such as proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy (1H‐MRS) to detect cerebral creatine depletion and GAMT enzyme activity assays, and is ultimately confirmed through molecular genetic testing. Early diagnosis and the prompt initiation of metabolic therapies are imperative to significantly improve the clinical prognosis and neurodevelopmental outcomes. 4

Developmental and epileptic encephalopathies (DEEs) are a group of severe, etiologically heterogeneous disorders characterized by drug‐resistant epilepsy accompanied by developmental delay or regression, typically presenting in infancy or childhood. Based on distinct clinical features and electroencephalographic (EEG) patterns, DEEs can be delineated into specific electroclinical syndromes, such as West syndrome (infantile epileptic spasms syndrome, IESS), Dravet syndrome, and Lennox–Gastaut syndrome (LGS). 5 While a comprehensive diagnostic workup traditionally encompasses detailed clinical evaluations, EEG, neuroimaging, and biochemical investigations, genetic testing has now emerged as a first‐line diagnostic modality. Over 900 genes have now been implicated in DEEs, 6 and whole‐exome sequencing (WES) has become a cornerstone diagnostic tool, yielding a diagnostic rate of approximately 35% to 50% in patients with DEEs. 7 , 8 Although long considered a pediatric condition, adults with DEE represent an overlooked population. Their persistent seizure burden, cognitive decline, and polypharmacy demand individualized, precision‐guided treatment strategies beyond traditional pediatric paradigms.

In this study, we report the oldest documented patient with GAMT deficiency to date, who demonstrated a remarkable improvement in seizure control following creatine supplementation. Studies specifically focusing on adult patients with GAMT deficiency remain scarce. Previously, Modi et al. compiled 23 adult cases but provided only broad epilepsy descriptions without detailed seizure semiology or EEG findings. 9 Conversely, although Mikati et al. and Yıldız et al. delineated the epileptic manifestations and EEG characteristics, their analyses centered on pediatric and adolescent cohorts. 10 , 11 To address this specific gap, we conducted an updated and comprehensive literature review targeting adult cases of GAMT deficiency, with a primary focus on characterizing their seizure semiology, EEG patterns, and outcomes. 12 Additionally, we included a pediatric cohort (age < 18 years) to investigate the age difference in symptom severity and the genotype–phenotype relationship.

METHODS

Whole‐exome sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from the patient's peripheral blood using QIAGEN DNA extraction kits (Qiagen, Germany). Library preparation and target enrichment were performed using the Enzymatic Fragmentation system with the Twist Human Core Exome EF Multiplex Complete Kit. Subsequent next‐generation sequencing performed on the Illumina NovaSeq platform achieved high coverage, with 98.5% of the target regions reaching >20X depth and 97.46% reaching >100X depth, and a Q30 base quality score of 91.87%. Resulting reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38.p14), and variants were called using Illumina DRAGEN (Dynamic Read Analysis for Genomics).

Tertiary analysis was performed on the Geneyx (v45) platform, which integrated multiple clinical and population databases, including ClinVar and gnomAD. To correlate with the patient's phenotype (epilepsy and developmental delay), clinical interpretation focused on an epilepsy panel (1045 genes) and a rare disease panel (7452 genes). The impacts of variants on protein function were evaluated using multiple in silico prediction tools (e.g., AlphaMissense, CADD, REVEL, and SpliceAI). The variant classification adhered to the 2015 American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines. 13

Literature review of adult GAMT deficiency

A thorough literature search was conducted in the PubMed database for articles published up to May 2026, utilizing the search terms “GAMT deficiency” and “Adult”, which yielded 23 studies. Of these, 7 studies were excluded because they focused exclusively on animal models, pediatric cohorts, or laboratory methods. The remaining 16 studies were included for review, focusing on individuals who were either formally diagnosed or initiated targeted therapeutic interventions at ≥18 years of age. Data extracted included: (1) basic demographics (age, sex, ethnicity, consanguinity, and history of institutionalization); (2) diagnostic findings (molecular testing, genotypes, pathogenic variants, and MRI/MRS results); and (3) clinical manifestations (intellectual or developmental delay, epilepsy, movement disorder or muscle tone abnormalities, and psychiatric and behavior disturbance). We specifically focused on descriptions of seizure or epilepsy type, EEG patterns, anti‐seizure medication (ASM) use, and progression in intellectual or neurological deterioration (PIND) 14 in adulthood. Treatment details were reviewed regarding the age at treatment initiation, regimen (creatine, ornithine supplementation, dietary arginine restriction, and benzoate sodium), therapy duration, and clinical response.

Pediatric cohort for comparison

A systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed up to May 2026 using the following query: (Guanidinoacetate N‐Methyltransferase[Mesh] OR “GAMT deficiency”[tiab] OR “CCDS2”[tiab] OR “Guanidinoacetate methyltransferase deficiency”[tiab]) AND (case* OR report* OR phenotype* OR “natural history” OR cohort* OR series) NOT (animals[Mesh] NOT humans[Mesh]). 130 results were found and exported to Rayyan (Rayyan Systems Inc., Cambridge, MA, USA). We first performed the title and abstract screening followed by a full‐text review. Studies were eligible if they reported cases under the age of 18 with molecularly confirmed GAMT deficiency and clinical features including ID/DD, seizures, and movement disorder. Exclusion criteria included: (1) animal or in vitro models, (2) review articles without patient data, and (3) studies focusing on diagnostic methodologies, such as neuroimaging or biochemical assays. Consequently, 60 studies were excluded. A comprehensive full‐text review including 70 studies was conducted. During data extraction, duplicate cases were consolidated, and patients harboring variants of uncertain significance (VUS) or likely benign variants were excluded (e.g., PMID: 37228909). Notably, a manual search of the reference lists from the retrieved articles was performed to identify and incorporate further eligible studies. Finally, clinical data from 124 pediatric cases across 59 articles were extracted for the final analysis. (Table S1).

Statistical analysis

Prevalence of clinical symptoms were compared between the adult and pediatric cohorts using Fisher's exact test for ID/DD and epilepsy, and Pearson's chi‐square test for movement disorders. Within each cohort, the Cochran‐Armitage trend test evaluated the association between genotype severity and clinical manifestations. Notably, this trend analysis was omitted for ID/DD within the adult cohort because of its 100% prevalence. Genotype severity was stratified into three tiers based on predicted pathogenicity: severe (T/T, biallelic truncating mutations), moderate (T/NT, heterozygous for a truncating mutation), and mild (NT/NT, biallelic non‐truncating mutations). Additionally, we compared the outcomes of creatine monotherapy with those of combined GAA‐lowering therapy in adult patients. The data were omitted if the outcome was not reported or was under evaluation. Outcomes were categorized into four domains: epilepsy, ID/DD, movement disorders, and “any” (defined as any improvement noted in at least one of the other three domains). All statistical computations were executed using R software (version 4.5.2, R Core Team, Vienna, Austria) via the RStudio interface. Statistical significance was defined as a two‐tailed p‐value <0.05.

RESULTS

Case report

We present a 52‐year‐old female with a history of neonatal‐onset epileptic encephalopathy, developmental delay, intellectual disability, dystonia, and ataxia. There was no family history of a similar condition (Figure 1A). According to family interviews and prior hospital records, the patient had a history of febrile seizures at 1 month of age. Developmental milestones showed she achieved independent walking at age 1, but was unable to scribble or hold a pen. Her speech was limited to vocalizations without meaningful words or gestures like waving goodbye. For activities of daily living (ADLs), she could self‐feed with a spoon but could not use chopsticks or dress independently. She attended special education classes throughout elementary and junior high school, supplemented by after‐school individualized education programs. At age 18, she was admitted to a residential care facility. She had a history of frequent absence episodes and falls since childhood without convulsive seizures, for which she received ASMs at a regional hospital.

(A) Pedigree of the proband with GAMT compound heterozygous variants; (B) Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) visualization of the GAMT variant. The alignment highlights a compound heterozygous state in the GAMT gene, comprising a single amino acid deletion c.265_267del (p.Ile89del) and a nonsense mutation c.253G>T (p.Glu85*).

At age 51, she was referred to our outpatient clinic due to functional decline accompanied by increased atonic seizures (Video S1), severe difficulty in feeding (Video S2), and becoming wheelchair‐bound within a few months. At the time of referral, she was on multiple ASMs, including oxcarbazepine (OXC), levetiracetam (LEV), lacosamide (LCM), topiramate (TPM), and valproic acid (VPA).

Clinical examination revealed involuntary movements involving the tongue, characteristic of orolingual dyskinesia. Also, the patient exhibited slow responses, accompanied by sudden, episodic head nodding resembling drop attacks. An initial EEG demonstrated diffuse background slowing, multifocal epileptiform discharges, and electrodecremental response, consistent with a DEE phenotype (Figure 2A to 2D).

EEG findings before creatine supplementation. (A) AP‐bipolar Montage. Background consisted of symmetric diffuse mixed 5–6 Hz theta and 3–4 Hz delta slowing; (B) Referential montage. Background consisted of symmetric diffuse mixed 5–6 Hz theta and 3–4 Hz delta slowing. Several electrodecrement patterns were seen; (C, D) AP‐bipolar montage. Background consisted of symmetric diffuse mixed 5–6 Hz theta and 3–4 Hz delta slowing. Multiple interictal spikes were seen over the right temporal T6) and left temporal T5 regions.

A comprehensive diagnostic workup was initiated. WES identified 4 variants: 2 variants of uncertain significance (VUS) in the ALPL and POLG genes, which were considered unrelated to the patient's phenotype. Two compound heterozygous variants, c.265_267del (p.Ile89del) and c.253G>T (p.Glu85*), of the GAMT gene were identified (Figure 1B). Currently, the c.265_267del variant is classified as a VUS in the ClinVar database, whereas the c.253G>T variant has not yet been reported. The c.265_267del variant has been previously reported in a homozygous state in another patient with GAMT deficiency. 15 Furthermore, because the genomic locations of these two variants are in close proximity, we were able to directly phase the sequencing reads using the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV), which confirmed their in trans (compound heterozygous) configuration. Based on the 2015 ACMG/AMP guidelines, the c.265_267del variant meets the criteria for PM3 (in trans), PM4 (in‐frame deletions), and PM2_supporting (rare in population databases); thus, it was classified as “likely pathogenic” according to the current guidelines. Similarly, the c.253G>T variant fulfills the criteria for PVS1 (null variant), PM2_supporting (rare in population databases), and PM3 (in trans), supporting its final classification of “likely pathogenic” for c.253G>T. Biochemical workup revealed a low serum creatinine level (0.24 mg/dL), while brain MRS demonstrated an absent or diminished creatine signal in the left thalamus and basal ganglia (Figure 3).

Proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy (1H‐MRS) of the left basal ganglia (A and B) and thalamus (C and D) demonstrates an absence of the creatine peak (at 3.0 ppm). Brain MRI and 1H‐MRS were acquired on a 1.5 T scanner (MAGNETOM Altea, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). Structural positioning images were obtained using an axial T2‐weighted turbo spin‐echo sequence (Repetition time (TR) = 5390 ms, echo time (TE) = 81 ms). 1H‐MRS was performed using a single‐voxel spin‐echo sequence. Acquisition parameters were as follows: TR = 1500 ms, TE = 30 ms, number of averages (NA) = 128, and total acquisition time (TA) = 3 min 12 s. A single spectroscopic voxel measuring 20 × 20 × 20 mm3 was placed over the left basal ganglia and thalamus region, guided by the axial T2‐weighted images. Automated post‐processing was executed on the manufacturer's workstation (syngo MR XA31, Siemens Healthineers) for metabolite ratio evaluations.

A trial of low‐dose creatine monohydrate (6 g/day, Impact Creatine, Myprotein, Manchester, UK) was initially administered, leading to marked clinical improvement. One month later, the patient became completely seizure‐free, successfully rose from her wheelchair to become ambulatory again (Video S3), and regained the ability to hold a bowl and eat independently (Video S4). A follow‐up EEG showed complete normalization, featuring a 10 Hz posterior dominant rhythm without any interictal epileptiform discharges (Figure 4). Over the 6 months following treatment, she successfully tapered her ASMs without seizure recurrence, discontinuing OXC, VPA, LEV, and TPM while remaining on LCM monotherapy. Additionally, arginine restriction and ornithine supplementation (100 mg/kg/day) were initiated to further reduce GAA toxicity (Figure S1).

Patient ages ranged from 18 to 52 years, with females (n = 15) outnumbering males (n = 10), while the gender of 4 patients was unreported. Ethnicity was documented in 17 cases, mostly from Europe or the Mediterranean region, especially Portugal (n = 7). In contrast, there were only 2 cases in Asia, 1 from Japan and our present case. Data regarding parental consanguinity were available for approximately half of the cohort (14/29), with consanguinity documented in 64.3% (9/14) of these cases. Furthermore, information concerning long‐term care was available for 9 patients, of whom 77.8% (7/9) required institutionalization. Two (2/29) premature deaths were reported. Patient 2 died at age 26 before treatment initiation. Patient 14 (the twin of patient 13) died at age 22 before any diagnostic workup for GAMT deficiency could be conducted.

Epilepsy was documented in 27 of the 29 patients; one patient (Patient 19) had no seizures, and data were unavailable for one patient (Patient 27). Data regarding seizure classification, severity, and frequency were reported inconsistently across studies. Most cases had refractory epilepsy, which was partially or poorly responsive to ASMs. Atonic seizures– also called head drop attacks or astatic seizures–were frequently described in 20.7% of the cases (6/29, Patient 4, 5, 12, 13, 15, 29). Some of the patients exhibited multiple seizure types across different ages (5/29, Patients 4, 5, 12, 13, and 29). EEG data were available in 7 cases, and shared EEG patterns were observed, such as background slowing, multifocal epileptiform discharges, and slow spike–wave bursts, which overlapped with the clinical and EEG features of Lennox–Gastaut syndrome (LGS). Lastly, the most frequently prescribed ASMs included VPA, CZP, CLB, and LEV, alongside various other agents.

All 29 patients in the cohort presented with ID/DD. Other frequent manifestations were movement disorders (86%, 20/23) and psychiatric and behavioral disturbances (87.5%, 21/24). Dystonia was the most frequently observed movement disorder (n = 11), followed by ataxia (n = 4), spasticity (n = 4), and rigidity (n = 4). Regarding psychiatric symptoms, aggression was the most common finding (n = 11), followed by autism/autistic features (n = 5), hyperactivity/attention deficit (n = 5), and agitation (n = 4). Furthermore, 14 of the 15 available cases exhibited developmental regression, primarily characterized by a decline in motor function, loss of acquired speech, or worsening of seizure severity.

Among our 29 adult patients, 4 were untreated (Patients 2, 14, 15, and 19) and 25 received creatine supplementation—15 with creatine monotherapy and 10 with additional GAA‐lowering therapy (ornithine supplementation and/or arginine restriction). Seizure improvement was the most frequently observed outcome (77.27%, 17/22), whereas movement disorder improvement was less common (66.67%, 10/15). Regarding intellectual disability and developmental delay (ID/DD), reported responses were primarily limited to improved behavior or attention span (31.82%, 7/22), with little or no significant gains observed in fine motor skills or language function. Patients receiving combined therapy exhibited a higher response rate than those on monotherapy; however, the difference reached statistical significance only for ID/DD (p = 0.0002).

Regarding patients' genotypes, there are 22 homozygotes, 4 compound heterozygotes, and 3 missing data (Patient 17, 26, 27). Among them, c.59G>C (p.Trp20Ser) was the most prevalent, which was previously recognized as a founder mutation in Portugal. 27 Notably, Patient 14 did not undergo any molecular testing, but was presumed to share the same genotype as her twin sister (Patient 13). Overall, missense mutations were the most common (46%, 12/26), while nonsense, deletion, and splicing mutations were also reported sporadically. Neither MRI nor MRS were reported in over half of the current cohort. Despite the clinical utility, the lack of neuroimaging data implies a clinical dilemma when facing poor cooperation from the patients. In our case, general anesthesia and intubation were also necessary in order to complete the exam.

Comparison of adults and pediatric populations

Of the 29 adult patients, 3 were excluded due to the absence of molecular diagnosis details, yielding a final analytical sample of 26 adults and 124 pediatric patients. Statistical analysis revealed no significant difference in the prevalence of ID/DD between the adult and pediatric cohorts. Regarding epilepsy, the prevalence was 92.3% (24/26) in the adult group compared to 74.2% (92/124) in the pediatric group; although the odds ratio (OR) was 4.17, this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.0683). In contrast, the prevalence of movement disorders was significantly higher in adults (69.2%, 18/26) than in pediatric patients (33.1%, 41/124) (OR = 4.55, 95% CI: 1.83–11.35, p = 0.0008). (Figure S2) On the other hand, the Cochran‐Armitage trend test did not reveal a statistically significant linear association between genotype severity and the prevalence of ID/DD, epilepsy, or movement disorders in either the adult or pediatric group. (Table S4).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we reported a 52‐year‐old patient (the oldest reported so far) with GAMT deficiency who presented with worsening atonic seizures together with further functional regression in mid‐age and responded dramatically to creatine supply. Our case represents the rarity of Asian adults with GAMT deficiency, except for a previous case described by Akiyama et al. 4 We also reviewed and delineated the disease course and phenotype of adult GAMT deficiency cases, finding that movement disorders and epilepsies, especially head drop attacks, are clue features for early diagnosis.

In our report, despite that blood and urinary GAA data were not accessible due to laboratory constraints, WES facilitated a prompt diagnosis and timely therapeutic intervention. Recent advancements in molecular genetics have empowered clinicians to identify the underlying etiologies of non‐specific phenotypes without relying on exhaustive conventional workups. Notably, Araújo et al. previously screened 180 institutionalized residents with severe intellectual disability and identified four individuals with GAMT deficiency. 3 This finding suggests that a substantial proportion of GAMT deficiency likely remains undiagnosed, which can be mitigated with advanced genetic testing. Meanwhile, given the relative ease of detection, expected impact of treatment, and minimal increment of cost, GAMT deficiency newborn screening has been successfully implemented in Australia, 28 Canada, 29 and the United States. 30

There is increasing recognition that DEE requires sustained attention into adulthood, as its phenotype and progression continue to evolve beyond the pediatric period. Notably, our patient experienced further cognitive decline and worsening, uncontrolled head atonic seizures in mid‐adulthood during a period of compromised care. Given her marked improvement with creatine supplementation, we hypothesize that inadequate nutritional intake may have precipitated a “creatine‐deficit crisis” on a background of compromised GAMT enzyme activity. Severe creatine depletion can exacerbate seizures, leading to a significant decline in ADLs, including the ability to self‐feed or be fed. This functional decline, in turn, reduces dietary creatine intake, creating a vicious cycle of creatine depletion and clinical symptoms worsening.

Previous literature reports that approximately 73%–92% of patients with GAMT deficiency develop epilepsy. 17 , 23 , 24 Epilepsy in GAMT deficiency manifests with various seizure types. The initial presentations are predominantly febrile convulsions or tonic and tonic–clonic seizures. As the disease progresses, atonic seizures, atypical absences, and generalized tonic–clonic seizures become frequently observed. 10 , 25 Our literature review showed that most adult GAMT patients persisted in having refractory seizures. Given the multiple seizure types, including atonic seizures, GAMT‐DEE should be considered in the differential diagnosis of Lennox–Gastaut syndrome (LGS) or Doose syndrome. Interestingly, our patient presented with childhood‐onset developmental delay, multiple seizure types, atonic and absence seizures, along with background slowing, multifocal epileptiform discharges, and electrodecrement pattern. These findings resemble the clinical presentations of LGS persisting into adulthood, which prompted us to perform WES to establish the definitive diagnosis. Similarly, Akiyama et al. reported a 38‐year‐old patient with GAMT deficiency whose previous EEG demonstrated features consistent with LGS (generalized 1.5–2.5 Hz slow spike–wave bursts). Furthermore, Mikati et al. identified common EEG patterns in GAMT deficiency, including background slowing, multifocal spikes, and generalized <3‐Hz spike‐and‐slow waves, that are commonly seen in patients with LGS. They also noted two cases presenting with hypsarrhythmia, leading to a diagnosis of West syndrome, 10 which frequently precedes LGS. Our case highlights that the presence of atonic seizures in adulthood should trigger reconsideration of the diagnosis, especially reversible metabolic disorders such as cerebral creatine deficiency, because timely treatment can yield profound clinical improvement.

Intellectual disability or developmental delay (ID/DD) was present in all 29 adults in the cohort, likely reflecting the delay in initiating creatine therapy. Stöckler‐Ipsiroglu et al. found that delayed treatment was linked to a greater risk of severe ID/DD. 24 Consistent with this, Lee et al. reported a case treated since 14 months of age who, at 19 years, has only mild ADHD and autism, normal intellect, and is performing well in college. 26 In contrast, the authors reported an adult treated case (Patient 25 in our adult cohort), in whom intractable epilepsy resolved completely, allowing withdrawal of all antiseizure medications; however, neurocognitive gains were modest, and significant disability persisted. Taken together, timely creatine supplement can affect the cognitive prognosis drastically. Although cognitive improvement is limited when treatment begins in adults, treatment for GAMT deficiency remains essential because it can markedly improve seizure control.

Movement disorders are the third most common clinical feature after ID/DD and epilepsy, affecting approximately 30%–50% of patients. Clinical manifestations vary, predominantly presenting as extrapyramidal and hyperkinetic syndromes (e.g., dystonia, ataxia, and chorea), although increased muscle tone (spasticity or rigidity) is also common. 31 Following treatment, the clinical improvement is about 50%–60%. 17 , 24 Interestingly, movement disorders are less frequently reported in patients with creatine transporter deficiency (CTD) or arginine: glycine amidinotransferase (AGAT) deficiency–conditions that also cause creatine deficiency but present with low or normal GAA–implying a link between GAA neurotoxicity and movement disorder pathogenesis. 15 , 32 , 33 In line with this, our patient presented with a combination of dystonia, ataxia, and orolingual dyskinesia, all of which improved markedly following creatine supplementation. Our preliminary statistical analysis also showed a non‐significant trend toward better clinical outcomes with combined GAA‐lowering therapy compared to creatine monotherapy.

Excessive GAA directly disrupts GABAergic transmission at GABAA receptors, impairs NA+/K+ − ATPase activity, and inhibits mitochondrial respiration, thereby lowering the seizure threshold. 34 , 35 , 36 Consequently, the marked seizure reduction observed following creatine supplementation reflects not only the restoration of cerebral high‐energy phosphate pools, but also the downregulation of endogenous GAA biosynthesis via negative feedback inhibition of AGAT. Because creatine monotherapy rarely normalizes elevated GAA, comprehensive management typically incorporates dietary arginine restriction (to reduce substrate availability) and high‐dose L‐ornithine supplementation (to competitively inhibit AGAT), collectively minimizing neurotoxic GAA accumulation. 17 This aligns with our finding when comparing creatine monotherapy with combined GAA‐lowering therapy (Table S3). Nevertheless, the small sample size (N = 22) and wide confidence intervals limit the validity and generalizability of our findings, which warrant confirmation in larger, future studies.

Our comparison analysis showed that movement disorders are more prevalent in adults. Although the difference in epilepsy prevalence between adult and pediatric patients did not reach statistical significance, an overall age‐related increase was evident. This pattern suggests that prolonged cerebral creatine depletion alongside toxic GAA accumulation in GAMT deficiency may contribute to chronic, progressive epileptogenesis, leading to delayed seizure onset and worsening in adolescence or adulthood. However, the severer phenotypes in adult patients may also reflect publication bias, as older patients likely experienced delayed diagnosis and extended exposure to untreated neurotoxicity. Indeed, timely intervention during the neonatal period or early infancy can substantially prevent the development of epilepsy. 30 , 37 , 38

In this largest (n = 150) analytical cohort, our comparison analysis showed no significant genotype–phenotype correlation in patients with GAMT deficiency, consistent with previous reports. 17 , 31 Prior literature also demonstrates that individuals carrying identical mutations may exhibit clinical severities ranging from mild to profound, 23 and even family members with the same variants can present with markedly different phenotypes. This heterogeneity may reflect the influence of epigenetic mechanisms, modifier genes, or environmental factors. 11

Several limitations of this study should be noted. First, the plasma or dried blood spot GAA measurement was not obtained because it was locally unavailable. Nevertheless, the diagnosis of GAMT deficiency was established by compound heterozygous likely pathogenic GAMT variants, marked depletion of cerebral creatine on brain MRS, a phenotype concordant with DEE, and clear clinical improvement following creatine supplementation.

In addition, some clinical parameters were lacking across the reviewed studies, such as EEG findings and ASM regimens. This paucity of data primarily reflects the rarity of the disease, inherent diagnostic challenges, and omission typical of retrospective records. A recently published patient‐centered core outcome set for creatine transporter deficiency and GAMT deficiency 39 will help address these challenges and reduce reporting bias. Furthermore, given the relatively small sample size of reported cases, further research is warranted to clarify the clinical and electroencephalographic features of GAMT deficiency, particularly in adult patients. Notably, the increasing accessibility and decreasing cost of genetic testing will likely enable the prompt discovery and treatment of more GAMT deficiency cases.

CONCLUSION

We report the oldest adult GAMT deficiency case with novel likely pathogenic GAMT variants, alongside a comprehensive characterization of the patient including seizure type and EEG patterns. Our case demonstrates that even when treatment is initiated as late as 52 years of age, it can lead to significant improvements in seizure control and reverse motor function deterioration. Our literature review indicates that untreated adult patients with GAMT deficiency frequently exhibit a more severe clinical phenotype than pediatric cohorts, particularly characterized by intractable epilepsy and movement disorder. Additionally, these individuals remain at high risk for disease progression throughout adulthood. This underscores the critical importance of arranging genetic testing in adults with unspecified DEE, as the epileptic burden can be dramatically alleviated with creatine supplementation, halting further cognitive deterioration and restoring the patient's quality of life.
