One pot sulfur(IV) fluoride-mediated deoxyaminations for the syntheses of 3,3-arylamino oxetanes and azetidines
Authors: Brodie J Thomson, Jiajing Li, Sean L Betteridge, Dillon Thiesen, Maximilian Bagus, Jolene P Reid, Glenn M Sammis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFunctionalized oxetanes and azetidines are promising bioisosteres, yet their inclusion in agrochemical and pharmaceutical candidate screening remains limited due to a lack of viable synthetic methods. Herein, we utilize XtalFluor-E® to prepare 3,3-arylamino oxetanes and 3,3-arylamino azetidines in moderate-to-high yields (30–97%) using a mild, one-step deoxyamination strategy. This work establishes a direct deoxyamination using suflur (IV) fluoride reagents. We coupled experimental screening with multivariate linear regression (MLR) analysis to gain greater insight regarding factors that influence the reaction scope. Furthermore, experiments paired with density functional theory (DFT) calculations provided key mechanistic insight into the nucleophilic substitution reactions at the sulfur(IV) center. The simplicity and short reaction times of our protocol facilitates the library syntheses of these key bioisosteres. Subject terms: Synthetic chemistry methodology, Reaction mechanisms, Chemical libraries

Introduction

Bioisostere replacement is a prevalent strategy in medicinal chemistry to improve the pharmacokinetic and toxicological profiles of a drug candidate. The successful use of this approach has fueled interest in both the development of new bioisosteres as well as more efficient routes for their preparation1–4. Five- and six-membered heterocycles have historically been the most commonly studied non-classical bioisosteres, which is reflected by their prevalence in FDA approved drugs5. Research over the past few decades has shifted to smaller, aliphatic carbo- and heterocyclic motifs as they introduce a 3D structural replacement to traditionally flat heterocycles and functional groups6. Four-membered heterocycles, such as 3,3-arylamino oxetanes (1, Fig. 1A) and 3,3-arylamino azetidines (2), are of particular interest as amide and amidine bioisosteres, respectively, due to their low molecular weight, polarity, and enhanced stability7,8. They are already showing promising bioactivity, with several 3,3-disubstituted arylamino oxetanes currently in pharmaceutical trials9. A strategy to quickly access substituted oxetane (1) and azetidine (2) libraries is, therefore, highly desirable for future development.

Methods to access 3,3-arylamino oxetanes and azetidines.

A General structure of 3,3-arylamino oxetanes (1) and 3,3-arylamino azetidines (2). B Previous efforts towards the synthesis of arylamino oxetanes. C Our strategy towards a general synthesis of both arylamino oxetanes and arylamino azetidines.

The medicinal exploration of 3,3-arylamino oxetanes and azetidines is hindered by the limited number of synthetic methods for their preparation. There are two common strategies to access amino oxetanes: (a) organometallic additions to sulfinyl ketimines10–12, or (b) synthesis of an activated oxetane that can undergo a degradative amination (Fig. 1B)13–15. Most of the existing methods follow the former strategy, which was first pioneered in 2010 by Brubaker and Hamzik10. The second strategy, recently reported by Bull and coworkers13,15, utilizes a degradative amination of aryl oxetane sulfonyl fluorides (6; Z = SO2F). Subsequently, Zhang and coworkers accessed arylamino oxetanes through the construction of benzylic trichloroacetimidates (6; Z = OCNHCCl3)14. Treatment of isolated intermediate 6 with copper triflate and the requisite amine promoted the desired degradative amination to afford arylamino oxetane 1.

There only have been a few reported syntheses of 3,3-arylamino azetidines. The first method was reported in 2006 by Heimgartner and coworkers, who examined the ring opening of azabicyclo[1.1.0]butane derivatives16. This strategy was employed in a few other protocols, including a recent photochemical reaction between azabicyclo[1.1.0]butane and a sulfonyl imine in a solution of photosensitizer and trifluorotoluene17. Shortly thereafter, Bull reported that their oxetane sulfonyl fluoride degradation methodology could also be used for the azetidines (8; Z = SO2F)15. Very recently a three-step synthesis of 3,3-arylamino azetidines was reported, utilizing either scandium- or copper-catalyzed substitution of azetidinyl trichloroacetimidates18,19.

Strategy 2 (Fig. 1B) is intriguing in its efficiency as (A) the starting alcohols are readily synthesized and (B) it provides direct access to substituted amines, unlike Strategy 1. The recent advances have clearly demonstrated the power of this approach14,15, but have two key limitations: both methods require isolation of fragmentation precursor 6 or 8, and the reaction conditions need to be rigorously inert, dry, or both. Despite these impressive early advances, there is no procedure that can directly access either 3,3-arylamino oxetanes (1) or 3,3-arylamino azetidines (2) in a single step from the corresponding alcohol (5 or 7).

To improve the accessibility of 3,3-aminoaryl oxetanes and azetidines as bioisostere targets, we sought to simplify their syntheses by developing efficient and user-friendly deoxyaminations of heterocyclic alcohols 5 and 7. We planned to couple experimental screening with multivariate linear regression (MLR) analysis as part of our methods development to build predictive models and uncover mechanistic trends across the substrate scope. Based on our experience utilizing sulfur(IV) reagents for one-pot processes20,21, we hypothesized in situ activation of 3-aryl heterocyclic alcohols may be achieved utilizing a sulfur (IV) fluoride. Sulfur (IV) fluoride reagents are known to promote deoxyfluorinations of tertiary alcohols22,23, including 3-aryl oxetanols13. Herein, we extend their known reactivity by exploring the direct quenching of the carbocation intermediate with amines to access both arylamino oxetanes and aryl amino azetidines (Fig. 1C) in moderate to high yields. Through our mechanistic analyses, we identified that competitive fluorination is largely sequestered through our optimized conditions, but can be promoted by structural factors, including steric bulk.

Results and discussion

Reaction optimization

We began our study with aryl oxetan-3-ol 5a (Table 1), as this was shown to be an effective substrate for the Bull degradative amination13. Addition of thionyl fluoride to a solution of oxetanol 5a, 2 equivalents of morpholine, and triethylamine did not afford desired arylamino oxetane 1a (entry 1), nor did a screen of modified conditions (see Supplementary Table S1 for details). Gratifyingly, switching the sulfur (IV) fluoride reagent to XtalFluor-E® led to complete consumption of starting material along with 60% conversion to the desired product (Table 1, entry 2). The remaining starting material was found to be converted to the respective alkyl fluoride. We hypothesized the fluorination was being facilitated by the formation of triethylamine hydrogen fluoride, so the base was changed to the bulkier N,N-diisopropylethyl amine (DIPEA). Using DIPEA resulted in a small increase in the conversion to 65% (entry 3). Slowing the addition rate of XtalFluor-E® (entry 4) significantly increased the conversion to 80%, which is likely due to slower rate of fluoride release from XtalFluor-E® into solution (vide infra).

We were surprised to find a moderate improvement in yield with an additional equivalent of base (entry 5), suggesting the alcohol and both equivalents of amine were being consumed as the reaction progressed. Decreasing the equivalents of XtalFluor-E® led to decreased product formation (entry 6). DAST was also a competent reagent, although oxetane 1a was only formed in 62% conversion (entry 7). It is worth noting that when the same reaction conditions were attempted with other sulfur activating species, such as thionyl chloride, no desired product could be detected in the resulting mixture.

Reaction scope for the preparation of oxetanes and azetidines

The scope of the optimised reaction was first investigated using an array of secondary aliphatic amines (Fig. 2). Although full conversion of 5a was determined to take around 3 minutes under our optimised conditions (See Supplementary Table S2), we investigated the scope with 1 hour reactions to account due to slower rates of starting material conversion when bulkier amines were tested. Piperidine proved to be a competent nucleophile, affording oxetane 1b in 66% yield. Similarly, ester functionalities and Boc-protected piperazine-containing nucleophiles were tolerated, providing 1c and 1d in 44% and 81% isolated yields, respectively. The latter substrate also allows for the possibility of subsequent piperazine functionalization. Benzylic secondary amines were effective nucleophiles, affording 1e, 1f, and 1g in 76%, 52%, and 53% yield. Steric bulk α to the nitrogen of alkyl amines was found to significantly hinder product formation, exemplified by the series 1h–1j. Unfortunately, minimal alcohol conversion was also observed when primary aliphatic amines were tested under the optimised conditions. This was likely a result of the amine reacting twice with XtalFluor-E®, which has previously been detailed in SOF2 amine activations24. TMS-protected amines could be used as alternative nucleophiles, with TMS-morpholine affording 1a in 86% yield. Similarly, TIPS-protected phenol 9a was also successful, affording deprotected alcohol 10a in 76% isolated yield.

We next explored the scope of primary anilines (Fig. 2). Similar to secondary aliphatic amines, anilines were overall effective deoxyamination nucleophiles. Electronically neutral and electron-withdrawing groups on the aniline worked particularly well affording 1k–1p in 71%–97% yield. A slight decrease in yield was observed for electron rich para-methoxy aniline, affording 1q in 60% yield. Aliphatic substitution on the aniline led to moderate to excellent yields, as both 1r and 1s were synthesized in 93% and 66% yield, respectively. Increasing steric bulk α to the aniline nitrogen decreased the yield of the products 1t and 1u. Heteroaryl nucleophiles, including imidazole and 2-aminopyridine were competent substrates, providing the corresponding amino oxetanes 1v and 1w in 47% and 70% yield. However, 2-aminothiazole only afforded 1x in 27%, possibly as a result of the competing Lewis basic sites within the ring system. Finally, we were pleased to find that tolyl oxetane 11 was an effective substrate, affording 12a in 52% yield.

The scope of 3,3-arylamino azetidines was explored using azetidinol 7 (Fig. 3), which can be readily accessed in a single synthetic step from the corresponding azetidinone13. Using the previously optimised reaction conditions, morpholine was effective for the deoxyamination, affording 2a in 50% yield. Similar trends were observed for azetidinols as were observed with oxetanes, with electron-neutral and electron-poor anilines (2b-2e) affording higher yields than electron-rich nucleophiles (2 f). Heteroaryl nucleophiles, such as 2-aminopyridine and imidazole, were effective under the optimised conditions, affording 2g and 2h in 80% and 58% yields, respectively. Acyclic aliphatic amine adduct 2i could be prepared, albeit in poor isolated yield. Attempts to prepare arylamino azetidines using bulkier acyclic alkyl amines resulted in no product formation.

Predictive modeling

Although we have demonstrated the S(IV)-promoted deoxyamination reaction across a broad range of amine substrates, the significant structural variations, make it challenging to separate the individual features of the substrate that impact the reaction outcome. We hypothesized that applying multivariate linear regression (MLR) analysis to a modular class of model substrates could offer more insightful information about substrate sensitivities25,26. The diversity of amines added to the PMP-derived oxetane substrates introduces the necessary variations in both electronic and steric environments for a robust training set, while also maintaining sufficient overlapping features for meaningful analysis. Visual inspection of the dataset reveals no obvious global trends regarding the factors that affect reactivity. Yield, as a composite measure of reactivity and product recovery, is particularly difficult to model due to the number of overlapping kinetic and non-kinetic variables it encodes27–29. In this system, yield behaves as a noisy output—sensitive not only to substrate-intrinsic properties such as nucleophilicity and sterics, but also to competing side reactions, intermediate stability, and practical issues like product isolation or volatility. These overlapping and sometimes unquantifiable factors introduce significant variance into the data, contributing to the modest performance of standard steric and electronic descriptors and making it difficult to deconvolute the specific substrate features that drive reactivity. While higher yields were generally observed with less hindered amines, reducing the steric bulk of the substituents did not always result in the highest reactivity (compare 1r to 1q). Notably, the ring substitutions had minimal impact on the amines’ behavior, with 4-substituents and those adjacent to the reaction site showing considerable tolerance.

To facilitate the model building process, energetically relevant conformations for each amine were identified through a conformational search using the OPLS3 force field. These conformations were subsequently optimised computationally at the M06-2X/def2-TZVP level of theory. With the ground state structures of the amines established, the next step involves extracting numerical features that characterize the properties of the reactive sites, along with their proximal electronic and steric environments, for MLR modeling. It is particularly challenging to succinctly quantify the reactivity properties imposed by diverse amine substrates. Our initial survey of modern steric and electronic descriptors was unsatisfactory for establishing robust correlations with yield, which included Sterimol parameters, NBO charges of conserved portions, and the LUMO, HOMO, and polarizability of the molecules30,31. The models generated exhibited consistently low levels of goodness of fit (i.e., the maximum ~0.6), and the inclusion of complicated cross-parameters in some models suggested that the model may have been overly reliant on intricate interactions rather than fundamental descriptors. This discrepancy implies that important parameters, possibly related to simpler steric or electronic features, were missing from the analysis, which could better explain the observed reactivity and improve the model’s predictive capability.

As part of our initial featurization, we had intentionally omitted IR vibrations because they could not be easily canonicalized32. Primary amines often exhibited both symmetric and asymmetric stretches, while secondary amines featured only one. This raises an important question: how can we effectively integrate IR vibrational data into our models in a way that captures the necessary features while ensuring reproducibility of results and facilitating the inclusion of new substrates? In answering this question, we envisioned a computational platform that incorporates secondary amines as deuterated analogues, allowing us to effectively decouple the stretching frequencies associated with the symmetric and asymmetric vibrational modes (Fig. 4A).

Specifically, by replacing one of the hydrogens with deuterium, distinct vibrational signatures are generated, facilitating the integration of these crucial features into our analysis. A statistically robust model was found that features the computationally derived N–H stretch and corresponding intensity, NBO charge, and Sterimol B1 values (Fig. 4A). Intensity and frequency measure different yet interrelated effects; frequency depends on force constants, bond energy, and molecular distances, while intensity is derived from the dipole moment, influenced by molecular symmetry and electronic structure. These results suggest that both steric and electronic effects impact reactivity, creating an exciting platform for utilizing our precision-based infrared-vibration parameters. However, while some deviation is anticipated due to methodological differences in yield quantification, compound 1x emerged as a statistical outlier, with the model overestimating the isolated yield. This discrepancy may arise from challenges associated with product isolation or purification efficiency. More broadly, this highlights the inherently noisy nature of yield as a modeling target: small experimental variances such as loss during workup, co-elution with byproducts, or differential volatility can introduce statistical noise that masks the underlying structure–reactivity relationships, even when predictive trends are otherwise robust.

Our initial efforts to adapt the reaction conditions to include azetidines revealed that these substrates were, in many cases, less reactive than their oxetane counterparts. For instance, compound 2a was obtained in 50% yield, compared to 86% for 1a under otherwise identical conditions. The reduced reactivity was attributed to the enhanced stability of the corresponding carbocation intermediate—likely a consequence of the stabilizing effect of the NBoc group19. Analogously, the empirical data for oxetane derivatives 10a and 12a reveal a similar trend: substituents with greater electron-donating capacity at the 4-position correlate with diminished reactivity, consistent with destabilization of the developing carbocation.

Despite this lower reactivity, the overall transferability of the reaction system suggested that our multiple linear regression (MLR) model could still be applied to this new class of substrates. However, capturing the reactivity differences between azetidines and oxetanes emerged as a critical challenge for achieving accurate predictions.

To leverage this mechanistic insight, we incorporated two representative azetidine examples to generate two different models: the morpholine derivative to capture low reactivity, and the imidazole derivative to capture high reactivity. In other words, these new models would incorporate all the experimental data shown in Fig. 4A in addition to one experimental value from Fig. 3. We computed NBO charges for both oxetane and azetidine starting materials, and manually introduced a second NBOC (i.e., the NBO of the carbon in the oxetane or azetidine that is connected to the OH) as an additional parameter to reflect the electron density differences between substrate classes. This resulted in a five-parameter model that retained a comparable R² value (R2 = 0.77).

Using this refined model, we evaluated all azetidine reactions involving amine substrates that had already been included in our predictive framework (Fig. 2). The difference in the model’s predictions compared to those reported in Fig. 3 are shown in Fig. 4B alongside the experimentally measured yields. Gratifyingly, the agreement was excellent: across all tested examples, the average absolute error in predicted yield was just 8%, demonstrating the model’s capacity to extrapolate effectively to a previously untested substrate class.

However, the requirement for distinct mechanistic interpretations, despite using shared parameters, highlights a key limitation of the current model. This limitation arises from the use of the second NBOC as a categorical differentiator between oxetanes and azetidines, which masks the need for mechanistically distinct relationships. Addressing this limitation will likely necessitate both an expansion of the training dataset and the implementation of a preliminary classification step prior to regression modeling33. Alternatively, incorporating higher-order cross-terms may also enhance model performance. Both approaches represent rational and scalable strategies for improving predictive accuracy in mechanistically heterogeneous systems.

Mechanistic studies

As noted by both our substrate screen and predictive model, steric hinderance a to the nitrogen is a key factor that negatively influenced product yields. An increase in amine steric bulk also corresponded with an experimentally observed increase in alkyl fluoride formation under the optimised conditions; 13% of alkyl fluoride product 13 was observed when morpholine was the nucleophile, while 50% was observed with 2-methylmorpholine (Fig. 5A). Given the sensitivity of the system to sterics, it was unclear why any of the amine nucleophiles could outcompete fluoride given the latter’s small ionic radius. We decided to investigate the competitive nature of fluoride vs. amine addition further to understand the influence of steric bulk under our optimised conditions. Tracking reaction progress as a function of time revealed that the desired deoxyamination product (1a) started to form immediately upon addition of XtalFluor-E®, whereas there was an induction time before the deoxyfluorination was observed (Fig. 5B). This is likely due to the higher concentration of amine relative to fluoride in the initial stages of the reaction. This also explains the increase in yield observed by decreasing the addition rate of XtalFluor-E® (Table 1, entry 3 vs. 4), as the slow addition limits the concentration of fluoride in the reaction.

To further investigate potential rate differences between amine and fluoride trapping, we performed DFT calculations using morpholine as a model nucleophile (Fig. 5C, D).

The calculations were performed at the SMD(MeCN)-ωb97x-D/def2-TZVP(ma-TZVP on F)//SMD(MeCN)-ωb97x-D/def2-SVP(ma-SVP on F) level of theory using 5 as the model substrate13. We examined the addition of both morpholine and fluoride to the carbocation intermediate derived from oxetanol 5, proceeding through a classical SN1 pathway. The calculated activation free energies indicated that both nucleophiles are kinetically competent under the reaction conditions, consistent with experimental observations. Specifically, the barrier was found to be 13.9 kcal/mol for morpholine and 2.1 kcal/mol for fluoride.

Additionally, we considered the role of fluoride trapping through base–acid complexation. Calculations revealed that the formation of 1:1 and 2:1 DIPEA:HF complexes is exergonic, with ΔG values of –6.1 and –5.6 kcal/mol, respectively (Scheme S1).

Considering the high concentration of amine at the beginning of the reaction, even relative to the oxetanol, it is also possible that it reacts with XtalFluor-E®. This would increase the steric bulk of the reagent and decrease the key rate of reaction with oxetanol 5. To explore this possible mechanistic pathway, we examined three sets of conditions with varied amine concentrations (Fig. 6). In the absence of morpholine, adding XtalFluor-E® dropwise to a solution of oxetanol 5 at –30 °C afforded only alkyl fluoride 13 (Fig. 6A), suggesting that intermediate 6 immediately underwent deoxyfluorination when it was formed in solution. In a second experiment, a single equivalent of morpholine was added drop-wise to a solution of XtalFluor-E® at –30 °C (Fig. 6B). Aminosulfinyl fluoride 14 could not be detected by 19F NMR spectroscopy, as it was likely unstable at temperatures above 0 °C34. However, when a solution of 2-aminopyridine and alcohol 1x was added directly to the cooled reaction mixture, arylamino oxetane 1x was identified by 1H NMR spectroscopy as the major product present in solution, along with trace amounts of 1a. When 2 equivalents of morpholine was added to a solution of XtalFluor-E® at –30 °C, double addition product 15 was observed by 1H NMR spectroscopy (Fig. 6C). Subsequent addition of oxetanol 5 to this solution did not lead to any conversion of starting material. These findings suggest that either 6 or 14 can readily afford a carbocation at room temperature under the reactions optimised conditions, but 15 is unable to activate the alcohol.

To complement these experimental insights and clarify the reactivity of the proposed intermediates, we conducted a series of DFT calculations with model substrates 5a and morpholine (Fig. 7). The computed activation free energies for nucleophilic attack on XtalFluor-E® were 13.8 kcal/mol for 5a (Fig. 7A) and 17.5 kcal/mol for morpholine (Fig. 7B), indicating that both reaction pathways are accessible under the one-pot conditions. Nucleophilic attack of 5a on aminosulfinyl fluoride 14 was also feasible, with a barrier of 16.7 kcal/mol (Fig. 7B). These low activation barriers are consistent with the fast reactivity observed at room temperature.

Proposed mechanisms and DFT calculations.

A Pathway involving direct addition of alcohol to fluorinating reagent. B Alternative pathway involving initial amine addition to fluorinating reagent. Numbers correspond to ΔG(sol) (kcal/mol) calculated using 5a and morpholine as the model substrates. Trivial hydrogens are omitted for clarity in the visualization of transition states.

Subsequent C–O bond cleavage via an SN1 mechanism was also favorable, with calculated barriers of 5.1 kcal/mol for the direct pathway and 9.7 kcal/mol for the amine-mediated route. These results align with experimental observations (Fig. 6A), supporting immediate deoxyfluorination in the absence of morpholine. Overall, the similar activation barriers suggest that both intermediate 6 and 14 could participate in the transformation, and neither pathway can be definitively ruled out.

The combined mechanistic studies indicate a plausible mechanism that led to the observed trends in our reaction scope. The single amine addition pathway (Figs. 6B and 7B) dominates as the reaction begins due to the high amine concentration. As the reaction proceeds and the concentration of fluoride increases, the fluoride addition pathway (Fig. 6A) becomes competitive. These mechanistic data also provide insight into the effects of steric bulk alpha to the amine. It is likely that the double amine addition pathway (Fig. 6C) dominates when bulkier and/or electron rich amine nucleophiles are utilized, as the bulkier amine-sulfur(IV) adduct preferentially reacts with a second equivalent of the amine compared to the sterically-congested oxetanol. With the amine nucleophiles effectively sequestered by 1 equivalent of the reagent, the only viable pathway left for the second equivalent is fluoride addition (Fig. 6A).

Conclusions

Overall, we have developed a facile method for the syntheses of 3,3-arylamino oxetanes (1) and 3,3-arylamino azetidines (2) using the S(IV) fluoride reagent, XtalFluor-E®. Using the same general reaction conditions for both targets, oxetanols (5) and azetidinols (7) were functionalized in a one-step protocol, affording the desired products in 30-97% isolated yields in one hour or less. This represents the shortest sequence and mildest method for the preparation of both 1 and 2 from 5 and 7, respectively. This method also represents the first example of a direct deoxyamination using sulfur(IV) fluoride reagents. We coupled experimental screening with multivariate linear regression (MLR) analysis to identify key structural factors that influenced product yield. Further calculations and experimentation helped elucidate the reaction mechanism, finding that competitive fluorination was largely sequestered through the optimised conditions. The operational simplicity of our method facilitates future drug candidate library syntheses of these important bioisosteres.

Methods

Arylamino-oxetane and -azetidine general synthesis

To a 3.7 mL or 20 mL vial equipped with a stir bar was added aryl oxetanol (0.5 mmol, 1.0 equiv), amine (1 mmol, 2.0 equiv) and DIPEA (1.5 mmol, 3.0 equiv).a The vial was sealed and purged with argon for 5 minutes while stirring to homogenise the mixture. To a separate 3.7 mL vial was dissolved XtalFluor-E® (1 mmol, 2.0 equiv) in MeCN (3 mL). The XtalFluor-E® solution was added drop-wise (22.5 mL h-1) to the amine-containing vial at room temperature with vigorous stirring. The resulting solution was stirred at room temperature for a subsequent 45 minutes. The dark orange/red solution was added to a separating funnel containing sat. sodium bicarb solution (15 mL) and DCM (15 mL). The aqueous layer was extracted with DCM 3 times before the organic layers were combined, dried over sodium sulfate and concentrated under reduced pressure. The resulting oil was purified by column chromatography.

aIf the amine was also crystalline, then MeCN (0.1 mL) was added to the vial to solubilise the mixture.
