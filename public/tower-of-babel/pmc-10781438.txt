Respiratory control minimizes diaphragmatic contraction and dry cough during pulsed-field ablation of atrial fibrillation
Authors: Ruhong Jiang, Qiang Liu, Laite Chen, Shiquan Chen, Yunhe Wang, Hui Cheng, Xia Sheng, Yaxun Sun, Lu Yu, Pei Zhang, Jianwei Lin, Zuwen Zhang, Xueyan Ding, Michael Shehata, Guosheng Fu, Chenyang Jiang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsPulsed-field ablation (PFA) is a promising new ablation modality to treat atrial fibrillation. However, PFA can cause varying degrees of diaphragmatic contraction and dry cough, especially under conscious sedation. This prospective study presents a method to minimize the impact of PFA on diaphragmatic contraction and dry cough during the procedure.Methods and resultsTwenty-eight patients underwent PFA for pulmonary vein (PV) and superior vena cava isolation under conscious sedation. Each patient received two groups of ablations in each vein: the control group allowed PFA application during any phase of respiratory cycle, while the test group used respiratory control, delivering PFA energy only at the end of expiration. A rating score system was developed to assess diaphragmatic contraction and dry cough. A total of 1401 control ablations and 4317 test ablations were performed. The test group had significantly lower scores for diaphragmatic contraction (P < 0.01) and dry cough (P < 0.001) in all PVs compared to the control group. The average relative reductions in scores for all PVs were 33–47% for diaphragmatic contraction and 67–83% for dry cough. The percentage of ablations with scores ≧2 for diaphragmatic contraction decreased significantly from 18.5–28.0% in the control group to 0.4–2.6% in the test group (P < 0.001). For dry cough, the percentage decreased from 11.9–43.7% in the control group to 0.7–2.1% in the test group.ConclusionPulsed-field ablation application at the end of expiration can reduce the severity of diaphragmatic contraction and eliminate moderate and severe dry cough during PV isolation performed under conscious sedation.Keywords: Atrial fibrillation, Pulsed-field ablation, Respiratory control, Diaphragmatic contraction, Dry cough

Introduction

Catheter ablation is recommended as the first-line treatment for paroxysmal atrial fibrillation (AF), and it utilizes two major energy sources: radiofrequency (RF) and cryoballoon ablation.1 However, severe complications and clinical adverse events have been reported in previous studies using RF or cryoballoon ablations, including pulmonary vein (PV) stenosis, phrenic nerve (PN) palsy, and atrioesophageal fistula.1–9 The overall adverse event rate in >90 000 patients treated between 2000 and 2010 in America was 6.29%.10 Pulsed-field ablation (PFA) is an emerging ablation modality to treat AF with much lower complication rates due to its non-thermal and tissue-selective nature.11,12 Pulsed-field ablation applies intermittent, high-voltage electric fields using micro- or nanosecond pulses, resulting in irreversible electroporation in myocardial cell membranes, eventually leading to cell death.13,14 This new technology reduces the likelihood of collateral injury from ablation.13,15,16 Numerous animal studies and preliminary clinical studies have demonstrated that PFA is a safe and effective alternative to RF or cryoballoon ablation for pulmonary vein isolation (PVI)13,14,17–21 and may reduce the burden of AF significantly.22

However, PFA can stimulate the PN and bronchus, which causes varying degrees of diaphragmatic contraction and dry cough.13,14,19,21,23 This effect has been reported in multiple clinical studies, especially with patients under conscious sedation.19,21,23 These side effects may result in patient discomfort, body movement, and intolerance during the PFA procedure. The strongly stimulated muscular contraction and cough can also cause patient movement and anatomical model shifting in 3D cardiac mapping systems such as CARTO 3 or EnSite. These concerns may hinder the quick adoption of PFA. At present, no workflow optimization has been reported to reduce the effect of PFA on diaphragmatic contraction and dry cough under conscious sedation. This study aims to provide a simple solution that minimizes the degree of diaphragmatic contraction and dry cough caused by PFA during PVI.

Methods

Study design and patient population

This study involves a subset of patients enrolled in the PF-Beat-AF clinical trial (PFA System for the Treatment of Paroxysmal AF) in China. The clinical trial is a prospective, multi-centre, single-arm study to evaluate the safety and effectiveness of the AccuPulse PFA system (AccuPulse Medical Technology, Suzhou, China) for treating symptomatic paroxysmal AF. The trial was approved by each participating medical centre’s ethics committee and plans to enrol a total of 160 patients aged 18–75 years old. The trial includes a subset of 28 patients that participated in this study on the impact of pulsed-field energy on stimulated diaphragmatic contraction and dry cough during AF ablation. Detailed patient inclusion and exclusion criteria are listed in Supplementary material online, Table S1.

Investigational device

The AccuPulse PFA system is intended to treat AF by inducing irreversible electroporation in atrial myocardiocytes and electrically isolating PVs in the left atrium (LA). The main components are as follows: (i) a multiple-channel PFA generator (AccuBlator®, Figure 1A), which creates the high-voltage micro-second pulsed electric field energy, and (ii) a 7.5-F AccuPulse uni-directional deflectable PFA catheter, which has a distal circular shape with 10 ring electrodes (Figure 1B). The diameter of the catheter’s distal circle can be adjusted between 15 and 20 mm to fit different PV sizes. The operator can choose which electrodes (all 10 or at least 3 contiguous electrodes) to use to deliver the PFA energy onto the endocardial surface. The voltage of the electric field created by the generator and delivered through the catheter electrodes is 1600 V. All electrodes are capable of recording intracardiac electrograms using an electrophysiology (EP) recording system or a 3D cardiac mapping system. The PFA system uses pre-set bipolar and biphasic pulsed waveforms to ensure the safety and effectiveness of ablation, with each PFA application taking <0.2 s for energy release.

Pulsed-field ablation procedure and respiratory control

A total of 28 patients participated this study after signing an informed consent statement. Pre-procedural screening including medical history, blood testing, and computed tomography (CT) scanning of heart and PV anatomy was conducted to ensure that all patients met the inclusion criteria. All procedures were performed under conscious sedation with intravenous administration of fentanyl (50 µg once, then 100–150 µg/h) and midazolam (1 mg once), and patients were able to respond to audible instructions from physicians. Intravenous heparin was administered to maintain the activated clotting time at 300–350 s during the procedures.

For the procedure, after femoral venous access, a 6-F decapolar diagnostic catheter was placed in the coronary sinus. An intracardiac echocardiography (ICE) catheter (SoundStar, Biosense Webster Inc., Irvine, CA) was placed in the right atrium to guide transeptal puncture and to determine whether there was a pre-procedure LA clot. Transeptal puncture was performed using an 8.5-F steerable sheath (Agilis NxT by Abbott or Vizigo sheath by Biosense Webster Inc.), following which a 7.5-F PFA catheter was guided into the LA until it reached the desired locations at the ostium of each PV (Figure 1C) under ICE guidance (Figure 1D). Anatomical models and bipolar voltage mapping of the LA were created using a DecaNav catheter in the CARTO 3 3D cardiac mapping system (Biosense Webster). Pre- and post-ablation PN function was evaluated by checking the normal diaphragm motion stimulated by pacing from PFA catheter electrodes near the PN. Pulsed-field ablation energy was applied to the ostium of each PV at least six times through all electrodes. After this, the catheter was rotated about 180°, and energy was applied another six or more times to cover the gap between the tip electrode and the proximal electrode on the circle and form a contiguous lesion to isolate PVs. After completing all PV isolation, the PFA catheter was placed at the superior vena cava (SVC) and ablation was performed in the same manner as PV isolation.

Two groups of ablations were applied in each PV and the SVC of patients: the control group (no respiratory control) allowed PFA application during any phase of the respiratory cycle, and the test group (with respiratory control) allowed PFA application only at the end of expiration. In the test group, the physicians instructed patients to exhale all the air in their lungs before PFA energy was delivered.

Rating scores

For each ablation, trained independent technicians observed and scored PFA-stimulated diaphragmatic contraction and dry cough using the scoring system described in Table 1. Scores were rated from 0 (no response) to 3 (strong response). Scores were rated by the same technician for each patient to ensure data consistency. For each PV or SVC, the first one or two ablations were applied without respiratory control as a control group, and following ablations were applied with respiratory control as a test group to evaluate the impact of respiratory control on diaphragmatic contraction and dry cough. In the condition that if the patient had obvious dry cough immediately after PFA application, we did not score the diaphragmatic contraction due to difficulty in adequately assessment.

Statistical analysis

This study focused on the quantitative analysis of a method to reduce the impact of PFA on diaphragm movement and coughing. For patient baseline characteristics and procedural parameters, the analysis includes number, mean, standard deviation (SD), and included 95% confidence intervals when applicable. Normality tests were performed before other analysis to check if the scores followed a normal (Gaussian) distribution. If scores did not follow a normal distribution, a non-parametric unpaired t-test was used to compare the difference between the scores of control and test ablations. Fisher’s exact test was used to compare the proportion and incidence rate between the control and test groups. The difference was considered significant if P < 0.05. All statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.3.1 (San Diego, CA, USA).

Respiratory control impact on diaphragmatic contraction and dry cough

A total of 1401 ablations in 112 PVs and 28 SVCs were applied in the control group, and a total of 4317 ablations in the same number of PVs and SVCs were applied in the test group. The first one or two ablations were applied without respiratory control as the control group for each vein, and all following ablations were applied with respiratory control as the test group. Three operators participated in the respiratory impact study, and two independent trained technicians performed the scoring. Each patient was rated by only one technician for data consistency. In all patients, no obvious skeletal muscle contraction was observed.

Table 4 compares the mean rating scores of stimulated diaphragmatic contraction and dry cough during PFA at each vein with (test group) or without respiratory control (control group). For diaphragmatic contraction, there is a noticeable trend that the scores for the right PVs and SVC were higher than those for the left PVs. Respiratory control reduced the rating scores of diaphragmatic contractions significantly for all PVs but not for the SVC. The mean score for LSPVs changed from 0.66 ± 0.77 in the control group to 0.43 ± 0.53 in the test group (P = 0.0029). The mean scores for other veins reduced from 0.74 ± 0.79 to 0.46 ± 0.52 for LIPVs (P = 0.0029), from 0.91 ± 0.82 to 0.48 ± 0.51 for RSPVs (P < 0.0001), from 0.87 ± 0.81 to 0.58 ± 0.54 for RIPVs (P < 0.0001), and from 0.86 ± 0.80 to 0.76 ± 0.45 for SVCs (P = 0.4756), in the control group and the test group, respectively. The average relative reductions in diaphragmatic contraction for the PVs and the SVC were as follows: 35% for LSPV, 38% for LIPV, 47% for RSPV, 33% for RIPV, and 12% for SVC. The LSPV, LIPV, and RIPV had similar reductions in scores, while the largest reduction occurred with the RSPV and the scores for SVC were relatively insensitive to respiratory control. These results demonstrate that respiratory control could effectively reduce stimulation to the diaphragm during PFA application on all PVs but not at the SVC.

Respiratory control reduced the mean rating scores of dry cough significantly for all PVs (P < 0.0001) but again not for the SVC (P = 0.7707), similar to diaphragmatic contraction. Table 4 shows the average reduction for all ablated veins: 79% for LSPV, 83% for LIPV, 69% for RSPV, 67% for RIPV, and 29% for SVC, as shown in Table 4. Generally, respiratory control had a larger impact on the dry cough than diaphragmatic contraction. Left PVs had a greater reduction in scores for dry cough than right PVs. With respiratory control, the dry cough was reduced to a very low or negligible level with average values around 0.1 or 0.2. It is noted that SVCs had a very low score of dry cough even in the control group, which means PFA ablation at this area rarely causes dry cough. These results demonstrate that the proposed method of using respiratory control to choose timing for PFA application can effectively eliminate severe dry cough.

Figure 2 shows the distribution of scores of diaphragmatic contraction and dry cough in each PV and the SVC. For example, in LSPVs, the percentage of ablations with scores ≧2 (moderate or severe) for diaphragmatic contraction was reduced from 18.5% without respiratory control to 1.8% with respiratory control (P < 0.0001), while the percentage of ablations with scores ≧2 for dry cough was reduced from 43.7% without respiratory control to 2.1% with respiratory control (P < 0.0001). A similar significant reduction through respiratory control in both diaphragmatic contraction and dry cough was observed for all other PVs and SVC. Right superior PVs had the greatest reduction for diaphragmatic contraction, from 28.0 to 0.8%. Left PVs had a greater reduction of dry cough than right PVs. These results are consistent with those in Table 4 and demonstrate that respiratory control can significantly reduce the degree of diaphragmatic contraction and almost eliminate moderate to severe dry cough.

Discussion

Mechanism of the impact of respiratory control on pulsed-field ablation stimulated diaphragmatic contraction and dry cough

During early studies using PFA energy to ablate cardiac tissue, there were occurrences of skeletal muscle contraction as well as nerve stimulation, and these challenges are one of the major concerns for PFA treatment of AF.13,24 Recent studies have shown that a well-designed pulse train using bipolar high-frequency pulses with short durations can effectively prevent skeletal muscle contraction without sacrificing good lesion formation.25,26 Based on previous studies and pre-clinical work, the PFA system of this study used optimized pulse parameters that effectively limit skeletal muscle contraction when ablating in the heart.

In this study, the PN stimulation was not measured by any objective means, and the results were based on the observations on the diaphragmatic movement. One critical factor for PN stimulation is proximity of the catheter to the PN.27 The left PN runs posterior to the subclavian vein and has a well-established anatomical proximity to the left atrial appendage. The right PN passes laterally over the SVC, the right atrium, and the anterior–inferior part of the RSPV.28 The distance between right PN and SVC has been reported to be 0.27 ± 0.68 mm while the distance between right PN and RSPV was 1.4 ± 1.7 mm in a published study.27 When ablating in right PVs and the SVC, the right PN is within the pulsed electrical field range and is prone to be stimulated. The diaphragmatic contraction in this study is thought to be caused mainly by PN stimulation. This statement was supported by the fact that the contraction is much stronger when the catheter is ablated at a location closer to the PN. For example, right PVs (scoring at 0.89 ± 0.81, n = 648) and the SVC had much higher scoring compared with left PVs (scoring at 0.70 ± 0.78, n = 298; P = 0.0007) during PFA. This explains why the contraction is stronger for right PVs (scoring at 0.89 ± 0.81, n = 648) and the SVC compared with left PVs (scoring at 0.70 ± 0.78, n = 298; P = 0.0007) during PFA. By training patient respiration and delivering PFA at the end of expiratory phase, the degree of diaphragmatic contraction during PVI was significantly reduced. We speculate that, at the end of the expiratory phase of the respiratory cycle, the lung is deflated, which means there is no pressure from the lung on the pericardium, resulting in an increased distance between the PVs and the right PN, while the distance change between SVC and right PN is not as obvious.

Direct stimulation to bronchi near the ablation site is one of the most probable causes for dry cough during PFA. The LSPV is closer to the bronchus than the RSPV. This explains why the scores of dry coughs were higher in the LSPV than the RSPV (1.05 ± 0.93 for LSPV vs. 0.35 ± 0.77 for RSPV). At the end of expiration, the deflated lung increased the distance between the ablation site and bronchi (refer to Supplementary material online, Figures S1 and SA2) and caused the significant reduction of scores of dry coughs (Table 4 and Figure 2). Additionally, the reduced residual air volume in the lung at the end of expiration also contributed to the reduction of severity of cough. These two factors are responsible for the significant reduction of dry cough scores during PFA in PVs with respiratory control (Table 4). As the SVC is relatively distant from bronchi, PFA in the SVC causes neglectable dry cough and respiratory control has little impact on cough score. In general, respiratory control eliminates almost all moderate to severe dry cough during PFA in PVs (Table 4 and Figure 2).

Clinical implications

Besides general anaesthesia, conscious sedation and deep sedation are the most common strategies applied during AF ablation procedures.29 Diaphragmatic contraction and dry cough are often reported and are unignorable during PFA procedures under conscious sedation.19,21 Besides patient discomfort, muscular contraction and dry cough cause the patient’s body movement or the 3D mapping model shifting during the procedure, affecting the stability of intracardiac catheters and the continuity of lesions.13 In some cases, these stimulated patient responses may interrupt the procedure and reduce the efficiency of PFA ablation. This study is the first quantitative evaluation of the impact of respiratory control on muscular contraction and dry cough and provides a clinical workflow solution to minimize patient responses during PFA. By limiting PFA energy release at the end of the expiration phase (end of exhale phase), the degree of muscular contraction and dry cough can be significantly reduced, improving patient tolerance and comfort level during the procedure under conscious sedation.

In this study, respiratory control was performed by the physician instructing the patient to hold their breath for a few seconds after the end of exhalation and delivering PFA energy within this manually created time window. This method requires pre-procedural training for the patient and intra-procedural effective cooperation between physician and patient. Therefore, deep sedation was not chosen in this study, although it has been reported to reduce patient discomfort safely.30,31 To better utilize the workflow solution reported in this study, a respiratory gating function will be developed in the next-generation PFA system. It will use real-time monitoring of the respiratory activities of the patient, track the respiratory pattern, and automatically control the timing of PFA energy release at the proper phase of the respiratory cycle. This new function may improve adoption of PFA ablation to treat patients with AF under conscious sedation or deep sedation in clinical practice.

Limitations

The placement location of the catheter tip inside the PV may affect the results of rating scores. If the circular electrodes are deeper inside the PV and closer to the PN, bronchi and lung may cause a stronger PFA application response from patients whereas if the circular electrodes are further away from the PV, the patient response will be weaker. In this study, we did not control or record the depth of the circular electrodes inside the PV or the number of electrodes in contact with left atrial wall, which may affect the consistency of the results. However, the large sample size (thousands of ablations in >100 PVs) may cover various catheter locations near PV ostia and overcome this insufficiency in the study design and control.

The PFA system used in this study has pre-set ablation parameters including the pulse voltage, pulse duration, and total number of ablations and does not allow any operator adjustment. Diaphragmatic contraction and dry cough have often been reported during PFA13,14,18,19,21,23,32 including FaraPulse PFA system (Farawave Catheter and FaraStar Generator) by Bosten Scientific Inc. and PulseSelect PFA system by Medtronic.33 The degree of stimulation may vary between different PFA systems, and there is no direct comparison. It is unknown whether the effectiveness of our solution is reproducible when using other PFA systems with different pulse settings and catheter design. It may work for the systems with similar pulse parameters including voltage, duration, and total pulse amounts used in this study. Among all published PFA system, VariPulse Catheter and TruPulse Generator (Biosense Webster Inc., Irvine, CA) have the closest voltage and duration as AccuPulse PFA system (1800 V vs. 1600 V, 250 ms vs. 180 ms).34 However, this solution may not work well for those systems with much longer pulse application duration like FaraPulse system with 2.5 s. More studies and further verification are required to make conclusion and optimization for future application of PFA system.

The rating scores used in this study were somehow subjective. It was difficult to run objective measurements for both diaphragmatic contraction and severity of cough during the procedure without bringing extra burden to the patients. The ICE images were monitored and recorded with ICE catheter inside the right atrium. However, it is hard to measure the motions in the images with diaphragmatic contraction using quantified analysis. As all patients were under conscious sedation without intubation, the airflow in the respiration airways was not able to be recorded for analysis. To better limit the subjective impact, each patient was self-reference for both control and testing groups and was rated by the same technician to ensure data consistency.

This study was focusing on the impact of respiratory control on the diaphragmatic contraction and dry cough. Each patient that participated in this study was self-referred with both control ablations and test ablations. The effect of the proposed workflow on the procedural outcomes (e.g. procedural time, success rate, and complications) could not be evaluated by a scientific way using the data from this study. This work shall be done in the future with a well-designed two-arm study.

The method of reducing dry cough during PFA reported in this study may not be effective for patients with respiratory system diseases, such as asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.

Conclusions

This study represents a novel workflow solution where PFA is applied only at the end of the expiratory phase in the respiratory cycle. This solution can effectively reduce the severity of diaphragmatic contraction and dry cough for the treatment of paroxysmal AF under conscious sedation.
