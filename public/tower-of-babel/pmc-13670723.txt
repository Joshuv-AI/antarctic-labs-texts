Intra-articular antibiotic delivery combined with one-stage revision for periprosthetic joint infection: a systematic review
Authors: Xinyue Chang, Michelle Hilda Luk, Amy Cheung, Lawrence Chun Man Lau, Thomas Ka Chun Leung, Henry Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeriprosthetic joint infection (PJI) is a serious complication after joint arthroplasty requiring surgical and antibiotic management. One-stage revision has gained attention because it reduces the number of procedures while yielding acceptable outcomes. Intra-articular (IA) antibiotic delivery has been investigated to enhance local antimicrobial exposure and address biofilm-related challenges. However, a focused systematic review of IA antibiotic delivery combined with one-stage revision for PJI is lacking. This systematic review evaluated the techniques, safety, and outcomes of this strategy.MethodsA literature search was conducted up to 30 November 2025 across PubMed (including MEDLINE), Web of Science, Embase, and the Cochrane Library. Studies enrolling adults with PJI treated by one-stage revision combined with IA antibiotic delivery and reporting clinical outcomes with at least 12 months of follow-up were included. Study characteristics, treatment protocols, outcomes, and safety data were extracted and descriptively synthesized.ResultsTen studies were identified. Background

Prosthetic joint infection (PJI) is one of the most serious complications of joint replacement surgery, and it is the most common cause of joint revision surgery [1, 2]. PJI is highly disabling, often impairing rehabilitation, requiring prolonged antibiotic therapy and hospitalization, and carrying risks of antibiotic resistance and adverse reactions [3]. This not only adversely affects patient outcomes but also increases the burden on healthcare systems.

One-stage revision is one of the surgical techniques for treating PJI [4, 5]. Its advantages include reducing patient morbidity and scar formation associated with subsequent surgeries, shortening overall treatment time, accelerating postoperative recovery, reducing hospital stay, and lowering healthcare costs [5–8]. Recent evidence suggests that one-stage revision can achieve satisfactory infection control for chronic PJI, with comparable infection eradication to two-stage revision [9, 10]. In a recent meta-analysis, Xie et al. reported a lower reinfection rate after one-stage than after two-stage revision (10.02% vs. 14.75%) [11]. Despite surgical intervention, antimicrobial agents remain critical in treatment [12]. Local antibiotic delivery enables high drug concentrations at the infection site, potentially enhancing antimicrobial activity and biofilm penetration while reducing concerns regarding systemic toxicity associated with intravenous antibiotic therapy [13, 14]. Intra-articular (IA) antibiotic delivery, as a type of local antibiotic delivery, was first reported in 1991 as a treatment option for PJI [15]. It has been investigated across several surgical strategies for PJI. Recent clinical studies have reported satisfactory infection-control outcomes following one-stage revision combined with IA antibiotic delivery [13, 16–18].

However, current evidence regarding this combined strategy remains limited and heterogeneous. Previous reviews have generally evaluated IA antibiotic delivery across mixed revision strategies rather than specifically examining its use with one-stage revision surgery. As the effectiveness of IA antibiotic delivery may depend on surgical context, a surgery-specific synthesis focusing on IA antibiotic delivery combined with one-stage revision is necessary. Therefore, this review focuses on intra-articular (IA) antibiotic delivery with one-stage revision for treating PJI. We summarize the available evidence on clinical outcomes, safety and complications, delivery techniques, and patient characteristics. By systematically integrating current findings, we aim to provide a reference for clinical decision-making and future research.

Materials and methods

Study design

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The review protocol was registered in the PROSPERO International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (Registration No. CRD420251196879).

Search strategy and study selection

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across PubMed (including MEDLINE), Web of Science, Embase, and the Cochrane Library up to 30 November 2025. No publication date restrictions were applied. Only full-text English-language studies were included. The following keywords were employed in the search: (PJI OR periprosthetic joint infection OR prosthetic joint infection OR infected arthroplasty) AND (one-stage revision OR single-stage revision OR one stage exchange) AND (antibiotic) AND (Intra-articular OR Intraarticular OR Local infusion OR Irrigation OR Negative-Pressure OR Infusion OR Catheter). Detailed search strategies for each database are provided in Supplementary Table 1 (Additional file 1). Additionally, researchers manually screened the reference lists of relevant literature to identify potentially eligible articles. This process did not uncover any additional qualifying studies. Following the removal of duplicate records, two reviewers independently screened the articles and conducted a full-text assessment of potentially eligible studies. Research identification details were not blinded to the two reviewers. Any disagreement was resolved through discussion, with a third reviewer providing a final decision when necessary.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: (1) original clinical research enrolling adults (≥ 18 years) with PJI; (2) evaluation of a one-stage revision strategy combined with adjunct intra-articular antibiotic delivery as reported by the original study, with extractable one-stage data when multiple surgical strategies were reported; (3) reporting of clinical effectiveness and/or safety outcomes; and (4) a minimum follow-up of 12 months for PJI-related outcome assessment. Studies were excluded if they were non-clinical or non-original publications, lacked relevant clinical outcomes, or did not meet the predefined eligibility criteria.

Definitions

Definitions of prosthetic joint infection were based on the diagnostic criteria reported by each individual study. Studies using consensus criteria, such as the Musculoskeletal Infection Society (MSIS) criteria [19], or explicitly stated diagnostic definitions were considered eligible. The intervention of interest was one-stage revision combined with intra-articular antibiotic delivery, including catheter-based infusion or intra-articular injection. Clinical outcomes were extracted according to the definitions reported by the original studies. Infection-control outcomes and subsequent clinical management after the index procedure, including reoperations and non-operative management when reported, were extracted and summarized based on the criteria provided by each individual study. Safety outcomes included local complications, surgical complications, and systemic complications associated with the combined treatment strategy. For infection-control outcomes, the final PJI-related follow-up assessment reported by each study was used; studies were eligible only if the follow-up duration for PJI outcome assessment was at least 12 months.

Data extraction

Data were extracted using a standardized data extraction form. Study characteristics, including study design, sample size, baseline characteristics, and follow-up duration, were collected. Data related to intra-articular antibiotic delivery combined with one-stage revision were extracted, including delivery methods, antibiotic agents, and treatment duration. Potential overlap among studies from the same institutions or author groups was assessed by comparing study periods, patient populations, and treatment protocols. Potentially overlapping studies were retained and synthesized at the study level. Such studies were included when they provided clinically relevant information from potentially distinct cohorts or addressed different aspects of the intervention. Potential cohort overlap was recorded and considered when synthesizing the extracted data. Clinical outcomes and safety outcomes were extracted according to the definitions reported by the original studies. Microbiological and treatment-related variables were collected as background information. For studies reporting multiple surgical strategies, one-stage revision subgroup data were extracted whenever available. If subgroup-specific data were unavailable, outcomes were summarized at the reported cohort level, and subgroup-specific information was recorded as not reported (NR). Other unavailable information was also recorded as NR.

Quality assessment

The risk of bias and methodological quality of eligible studies were assessed using the National Institutes of Health (NIH) quality assessment tools [20]. Appropriate evaluation scales were selected according to study design, with overall quality graded as good, fair, or poor. No studies were excluded solely based on methodological quality assessment. Quality assessments were conducted independently by two researchers, with disagreements resolved through discussion.

Statistical analysis

Study characteristics and extracted data were summarized descriptively in tables. Study-level clinical outcomes, safety outcomes, and intra-articular antibiotic delivery protocols were presented descriptively according to the data reported in the original studies. Because of inconsistent reporting, clinical and methodological heterogeneity, and potential overlap between patient cohorts, no quantitative pooling or formal meta-analysis was performed.

Results

Study selection

A total of 285 records were identified through database searching. Following duplicate removal and screening, 16 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, of which 10 studies were included in the final analysis. The study selection process is summarized in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics

The included studies were published between 2011 and 2025 and comprised eight retrospective case series and two retrospective comparative studies involving hip and knee arthroplasty populations. All included studies were retrospective in design, with only two studies including comparative control groups [13, 21]. Follow-up duration for PJI-related outcomes was at least 12 months across all included studies. All studies evaluated one-stage revision combined with intra-articular antibiotic delivery for the management of periprosthetic joint infection. Substantial heterogeneity was observed in patient populations, study designs, and intra-articular antibiotic delivery protocols. According to the NIH quality assessment tool, eight studies were rated as fair quality and two studies were rated as good quality. Considerable heterogeneity existed in the implementation of intra-articular antibiotic delivery, particularly regarding administration methods and catheter/device configuration. Hickman catheters and three-branch catheters were the most commonly reported devices in catheter-based protocols. Catheter-based protocols used intermittent intra-articular administration, whereas some knee protocols used direct intra-articular needle injection without an indwelling catheter [23, 25]. The initiation of intra-articular antibiotic therapy varied across studies, with most protocols beginning during the early postoperative period when reported; however, one study initiated therapy after microbiological confirmation [25]. The reported duration of intra-articular antibiotic therapy ranged from 14 to 42 days. Most studies combined intra-articular antibiotic delivery with systemic antibiotic therapy, although the specific systemic regimens varied among studies. Detailed protocol characteristics are summarized in Table 2.

Microbiological profiles and antibiotic selection

Antibiotic selection was primarily guided by microbiological profiles. Reported infections across the included studies comprised Gram-positive, Gram-negative, polymicrobial, fungal, and culture-negative infections. Vancomycin-based regimens were frequently reported, particularly for Gram-positive organisms including staphylococcal species [13, 16–18, 21, 22, 24, 26]. Broad-spectrum antimicrobial agents, including carbapenem-based regimens, were used in studies involving Gram-negative, polymicrobial, or culture-negative infections. Antifungal agents, including fluconazole and voriconazole, were reported in studies involving fungal infections [16, 18, 21, 23, 26]. Detailed microbiological profiles and antibiotic selection are summarized in Supplementary Table 2 (Additional file 1).

Clinical outcomes and subsequent management

Infection-control outcomes were reported across all included studies after one-stage revision combined with intra-articular antibiotic delivery. The included studies reported relatively high rates of infection control, with infection-control outcomes assessed according to the criteria reported in each individual study. Subsequent surgical interventions after index one-stage revision were reported in most studies. Reported procedures included implant-retaining debridement, debridement with insert exchange, repeat single-stage revision, spacer implantation, component removal followed by reimplantation, and salvage procedures such as above-knee amputation. Subsequent management strategies varied across studies according to the clinical circumstances, and management approaches were not uniform across studies. Non-operative management strategies were reported in several studies. Chronic suppressive antibiotic therapy was described among patients managed after treatment failure or subsequent intervention. One study reported medical management without further surgery after treatment failure [23]. Detailed outcomes and subsequent management are summarized in Table 3.

In studies using catheter-based intra-articular antibiotic delivery, catheter-related complications mainly included technical or device-related issues, such as catheter occlusion, leakage, and catheter loss. Other local adverse events included local irritation or rash, fistula or drainage-related events, and transient pressure or tenderness. Surgical complications included postoperative orthopaedic and radiographic findings, such as dislocation, periprosthetic fracture, radiolucent changes, osteotomy-related complications, arthrofibrosis, instability, and wound-related complications. Systemic complications mainly involved renal function abnormalities, acute kidney injury, antimicrobial-related adverse events, elevated serum antibiotic levels, and thrombotic events. Given the variability in complication reporting and definitions across studies, safety outcomes were synthesized descriptively without calculation of a pooled overall complication rate.

Discussion

This systematic review provides a surgery-specific evaluation of intra-articular (IA) antibiotic delivery in the setting of one-stage revision for prosthetic joint infection (PJI). To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review specifically evaluating IA antibiotic delivery within a one-stage revision framework. Ten studies were included. Individual studies reported satisfactory infection-control outcomes following one-stage revision combined with intra-articular antibiotic delivery; however, considerable variability was observed in patient selection, treatment protocols, and outcome definitions across studies. These findings suggest that IA antibiotic delivery may serve as a potential adjunctive strategy within one-stage revision for PJI. By focusing specifically on the one-stage revision setting, this review also provides a foundation for future research on localized antibiotic strategies in PJI management.

One-stage revision has become an increasingly accepted strategy for the management of PJI, with reported infection control outcomes comparable to those of two-stage revision in selected patients [1, 10, 11]. Previous studies have demonstrated satisfactory reinfection-free survival following one-stage revision, supporting its role as a surgical option for appropriately selected cases [27]. Within the one-stage revision setting, IA antibiotic delivery has been introduced as an adjunctive strategy to enhance early postoperative local antimicrobial exposure. Biofilm formation is a key factor in the persistence of PJI, as eradication of biofilm-embedded bacteria requires antibiotic concentrations 100–1000 times the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) [28]. Systemic antibiotic therapy may be limited by toxicity, restricting achievable local drug levels. In contrast, direct IA antibiotic delivery can achieve higher local antibiotic concentrations than intravenous therapy [29], potentially reaching levels sufficient to target biofilm-embedded organisms [30]. Moreover, IA administration may reduce systemic exposure and may allow flexible dose adjustment or temporary discontinuation when clinically necessary [21, 22].

Regarding safety, catheter-related issues were generally limited to the insertion site or surrounding soft tissue and included occlusion, leakage, dislodgement, and local inflammatory reactions. These events were reported in individual studies and varied in their occurrence and management. Indwelling catheters may theoretically serve as a portal for microbial entry and increase the risk of secondary infection [31, 32]. Fungal infections have also been reported, particularly in immunocompromised patients or in the setting of prolonged catheter retention, although such complications appear to be uncommon [31, 33]. While concerns regarding deep infection risk remain, previous studies of intra-articular pain catheters in total knee arthroplasty have not demonstrated increased deep infection rates [34]. In the included studies, catheter-related events were managed with catheter-related interventions or supportive measures when reported. Renal-related adverse events were among the most frequently reported systemic complications. However, no significant long-term renal impairment was documented in the included studies. The potential for high local antibiotic concentrations with limited systemic exposure represents a theoretical advantage of IA delivery, although comparative safety data remain limited [13, 16, 22]. Further studies with standardized protocols and more consistent adverse event reporting are needed to better characterize the safety profile of this adjunctive strategy.

Substantial heterogeneity was observed across the included studies with respect to antibiotic selection, dosing regimens, timing and duration of administration, as well as patient selection criteria, outcome definitions, and follow-up protocols. This methodological variability limited interstudy comparability and precluded quantitative synthesis. Most included studies were retrospective, involved small sample sizes, and lacked control groups. In addition, several reports originated from the same centers across different time periods, raising the possibility of partial overlap in patient cohorts. These limitations highlight the need for high-quality prospective comparative studies to better evaluate the clinical effectiveness and safety of IA antibiotic delivery in the setting of one-stage revision for PJI. At present, the independent contribution of IA antibiotic delivery beyond standard one-stage revision for PJI remains uncertain. Future multicenter prospective studies incorporating standardized surgical techniques, clearly defined IA antibiotic protocols, and consistent outcome reporting will be essential to better define its incremental clinical value.

Conclusions

This systematic review summarized the available evidence regarding the combined use of intra-articular antibiotic delivery and one-stage revision for PJI. Individual studies reported satisfactory infection-control outcomes and safety findings. The clinical effectiveness of this combined treatment strategy requires further evaluation. Variations in study designs and treatment protocols persist across the available literature. Further prospective comparative studies with standardized protocols and consistent outcome reporting may help better define the role of this combined treatment strategy in PJI management.
