Reformulating Energy Storage Capacity Accreditation Problem with Marginal Reliability Impact
Qian Zhang, Feng Zhao, Tongxin Zheng, Le Xie

Abstract
To enhance the efficiency of capacity markets, many electricity markets in the U.S. are adopting or planning to implement marginal capacity accreditation reforms. This paper provides new insights into energy storage capacity accreditation using Marginal Reliability Impact (MRI). We reformulate the commonly used reliability-based storage dispatch model as an optimization problem, enabling direct calculation of the MRI from the Lagrange multipliers, rather than using brute-force perturbation analysis. The analysis demonstrates that the EUE is a piecewise linear function and the storage MRI retains a non-negative property across various system scenarios. We further explore the influence of qualified capacity (QC), storage dispatch rules, and other key factors on storage accreditation, providing practical insights for system operators. Additionally, comparisons of storage capacity accreditation under different reliability criteria offer valuable guidance for policymakers in setting future standards. Numerical results from a modified California system validate our findings and highlight several important phenomena associated with the MRI-based accreditation scheme.

The Forward Capacity Market (FCM) ensures long-term resource adequacy in deregulated electricity markets by providing financial incentives for resources to commit their capacity in advance, thereby meeting reliability standards typically defined by a loss of load expectation (LOLE) of ”one day in ten years.”

In recent years, many Independent System Operators (ISOs) have been exploring or implementing new reforms targeting both the demand and supply sides of the FCM

. On the demand side, the capacity demand curve has been reconstructed from fixed capacity requirements or vertical lines to price-elastic demand curves based on the estimation of marginal reliability benefits of capacity and the value of loss load (VOLL) in FCM
[
11
,
35
]
. On the supply side, seasonal and locational factors have been incorporated into resource models to make the Resource Adequacy Assessment (RAA) process more precise, while capacity accreditation methods are being re-evaluated to appropriately account for the diverse contributions of different resources to system reliability
[
21
,
36
,
17
]
. Additional reform suggestions from the academic community have also contributed by exploring innovative methods to address the complexities of an evolving resource mix
[
3
,
30
,
32
,
39
]
. Together, these efforts aim to establish a framework where accredited capacity is more substitutable across resource types, thereby enhancing market efficiency and facilitating the transition to a decarbonized grid.

Among all the different reform ideas, marginal capacity accreditation has become a pivotal element in both demand and supply side reforms across most ISOs in the US. Unlike
average
methods, which compensate each resource based on the aggregate reliability benefit of all units of that resource type, the
marginal
approach compensates resources based on the incremental reliability benefit provided by the next unit of that type
[
16
]
. The marginal methodology offers a more precise investment signal to market participants, reflecting the true reliability value of various resources. Recognizing these advantages, the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) has approved marginal-based accreditation reforms for several ISOs in the past years
[
8
,
9
]
. Currently, there are two primary methods used in this context: marginal effective load carrying capability (ELCC) and MRI. While marginal ELCC and MRI yield similar results under the same reliability metric, their impact on the existing market has not been fully understood due to uncertainties and complex interactions with other market parameters
[
34
]
.

Despite its advantages, the marginal accreditation methods present computational challenges as compared to class-level average accreditation, primarily due to the need to accurately measure the
gradient
of each resource’s contribution to system reliability. This method typically requires calculating the incremental reliability values of individual resources ,which requires modeling each resource in detail. To alleviate these challenges, similar resources might be grouped into a resource class to ensure computational traceability
[
6
]
. These issues are particularly pronounced for energy-limited resources, especially energy storage
[
14
]
.

With energy limited resources, load cannot be assumed met independently in each operating period. For energy storage, the ability to serve load in one time period is highly dependent on the operating conditions and energy availability from previous periods, complicating the marginal contribution calculation
[
23
,
31
,
24
]
. Additionally, modeling appropriate charging and discharging orders of multiple storage resources remains a complex task. These challenges not only increase the computational burden but also raise questions about the accuracy and fairness of marginal accreditation in accurately reflecting the reliability value of diverse resources.

In this paper, we address the energy storage capacity accreditation problem using MRI. Rather than relying on perturbation results from commercial RA simulators, we reformulate the energy storage model in resource adequacy as an optimization problem. This new model generates theoretical MRI results, providing insights into the effects of various factors on storage capacity accreditation. The key contributions of this work are as follows:

•

Enhancing the understanding of energy storage’s marginal reliability impact on system performance.

•

Examining the influence of QC definitions and other factors on storage capacity accreditation, offering practical insights for system operators.

•

Comparing storage capacity accreditation under different reliability criteria, offering valuable suggestion for policymakers in setting future reliability standards

II
Marginal Reliability Impact

The MRI of a resource indicates how a small change in the resource’s qualified capacity impacts system reliability, as measured by reliability metrics like Expected Unserved Energy (EUE) or Loss of Load Expectation (LOLE). In this paper, we adopt EUE as the primary reliability metric.

II-A

MRI as a Rectifier for Qualified Capacity

Definition 1

(Marginal Reliability Impact
[
37
]
)
:

The MRI is defined as the expected unserved energy (EUE) change over a small increase in Qualified Capacity (QC), i.e.

\text{MRI}=-\frac{\partial\text{EUE}}{\partial\text{QC}}.

(1)

where the derivative is calculated around the installed capacity requirement (ICR), which is estimated by the system operator for the market clearing year.

Fig. 1:
The Calculation Process of Reliability Metrics and MRI

The process for calculating reliability metrics and MRI is illustrated in Fig.
1
, where the adequacy assessment tool is a probabilistic simulator utilizing the Monte Carlo method for resource adequacy analysis. MRI is typically computed through perturbation of the input data, though this will be reformulated in subsequent sections. To apply MRI for capacity accreditation, the MRI of an ideal resource is used as a reference.

Definition 2

(relative Marginal Reliability Impact
[
37
]
)
:

The relative Marginal Reliability Impact (rMRI) is defined as a ratio (no units) of the resource’s MRI to a perfect capacity’s MRI:

\text{rMRI}=\frac{\text{MRI}}{\text{MRI}_{\text{perfect}}}.

(2)

where the
\text{MRI}_{\text{perfect}}
is typically defined as the MRI of a thermal generator resource with
zero
outage rate.

After calculating the rMRI for each resource, the new credited capacity, i.e. Qualified MRI Capacity (QMRIC), will be calculated in MW:

\text{QMRIC}=\text{QC}\times\text{rMRI}.

(3)

The Qualified Capacity (QC) represents the
physical
capacity of resources. For instance, the QC of thermal resources is typically defined by their maximum capacity, whereas for renewable resources, it is based on the average value of their historical output profiles. In contrast, the Qualified Marginal Reliability Impact Capacity (QMRIC) reflects the
market
capacity of resources, which is directly used in the Forward Capacity Market (FCM) clearing process.

II-B

The Role of MRI in Forward Capacity Market

The Forward Capacity Market (FCM) functions as a market mechanism to ensure that power systems have adequate resources to meet future electricity demand. In ISO New England, system operators first determine the system-wide and zonal ICR based on expected system conditions three years prior to the operating period, which serves as the equilibrium point for MRI calculation. Subsequently, the QMRIC for the market is derived by discounting the QC with its rMRI. Using the estimated VOLL and resource bids, we can then construct the demand and supply curves for the FCM
[
35
]
.

Different from other
average
reliability impact methods, the MRI-based approach provides more appropriate resource accreditation results and offers a clearer economic interpretation
[
35
,
36
]
. However, to ensure the efficiency of the FCM, certain properties of EUE and MRI are desired, particularly
continuity
and
non-negativity
, respectively.

III
Energy Storage Dispatch Model

In this section, we compare different storage dispatch models, with a focus on the most commonly used model (reliability dispatch) for resource adequacy analysis.

III-A

Overview of Energy Storage Dispatch Assumption

Unlike thermal resources, the ability of energy storage to discharge in a given time period is highly dependent on the energy surplus from previous periods. As a result, the contribution of energy storage to system resource adequacy is determined primarily by the dispatch model rather than by the unit’s failure rate. In reliability analysis, dispatch models for multiple energy storage units can be classified into three categories
[
24
]
. The first,
expectation dispatch
, converts energy storage devices into non-chronological probabilistic models based on their historical behavior or average stochastic outcomes. Similar approaches are applied to intermittent resources and demand response
[
15
,
38
]
, allowing system reliability to be evaluated using conventional methods
[
1
]
.

However, under the expectation dispatch model, energy storage lacks adaptability to different operational scenarios, often resulting in an undervaluation of its resource adequacy contribution. The second category,
economic dispatch
models, more accurately reflect energy storage behavior, but by the difficulty of modeling price-responsive and intertemporal user behavior, which requires strong and uncertain assumptions over long planning horizons.

To address the limitations of the previous models,
reliability dispatch
models are used to quantify the upper bound of energy storage’s contribution to system reliability. These models are widely used by many ISOs for system resource adequacy analysis and storage capacity accreditation, which is the main focus of this paper.

III-B

Reliability Dispatch

Assume each Monte Carlo experiment will sample a profile of available capacity during the observation period
\mathcal{T}:=\{1,2,...,T\}
. The net capacity surplus is used for storage charging decisions to capture the adaptability of energy storage under the reliability dispatch model, which is defined below.

Definition 3

(Net Capacity Surplus)
:

Let
C_{t}(k)
be a possible value of total available capacity except for energy storage during time interval
t
for the
k
-th Monte Carlo experiment, where
N_{p}
Monte Carlo experiment profiles are generated independently with equal probability contribution. The
net capacity surplus
is defined as the total available capacity except for energy storage minus the demand. The net capacity surplus at time interval
t
of
k
-th Monte Carlo experiment can be expressed as:

P_{t}(k)=C_{t}(k)-L_{t}(k)

(4)

where
L_{t}(k)
is the system demand level at time interval
t
of
k
-th Monte Carlo experiment. Because the demand profiles are statistically independent, the final MRI used for capacity accreditation is computed as the average MRI across all profiles. In the following sections, we therefore restrict attention to a single profile, denoted by
P_{t}
.

Because resource adequacy is a long-term planning exercise, a key assumption of the energy storage dispatch model is that decision-makers possess only limited information about future system conditions. Consequently, rather than employing a multi-interval economic dispatch formulation, most dispatch models embedded in reliability assessment software adopt
single-interval greedy algorithms
. Under this paradigm, storage devices charge from the grid whenever surplus generation is available (
P_{t}>0
) and discharge only when required to mitigate a power deficit (
P_{t}<0
). The
cross charging
is
not
allowed in these models, which means energy storage resources are not permitted to transfer energy to one another.

In this paper, we adopt the dispatch algorithm introduced by Evans et al.
[
7
]
, which is proven to minimize the expected unserved energy (EUE) under no cross charging assumption. This algorithm has been incorporated into several RAA software tools
[
10
,
26
]
. Alternative proof techniques for related optimality results can also be found in the ramping management literature
[
33
]
.

IV
MRI-based Storage Capacity Accreditation

For large-scale systems with energy storage resources, the reliability dispatch algorithms discussed above are integrated into Monte Carlo simulations for reliability analysis
[
10
]
. As MRI calculations become increasingly important in supporting the FCM, we reformulate the MRI-based storage capacity accreditation problem to enhance both accuracy and computational efficiency.

IV-A

Preliminary

To account for correlations among input variables, MRI is commonly computed by comparing the EUE before and after a small perturbation in the input QC. This process requires numerous repeated simulations and relies on engineering judgment in selecting proper perturbation step size
[
34
]
.

To improve the consistency and accuracy of MRI calculation, we reformulate the reliability dispatch model as an optimization problem. We begin by introducing net power surplus and deficiency signals, which determine whether storage charges or discharges.

Definition 4

(Net Power Surplus and Deficiency)
:

The net power surplus
p_{t}^{+}
and deficiency
p_{t}^{-}
at the time interval
t
of one Monte Carlo experiment are defined as the positive and negative parts of
P_{t}
respectively, i.e.

\displaystyle p_{t}^{+}

\displaystyle=\max\{P_{t},0\},\quad p_{t}^{-}=\max\{-P_{t},0\}

(5)

The formulation of net power surplus and deficiency plays an important role in converting the reliability dispatch algorithm to the optimization problem, where the storage is only charged during a power surplus period, while discharged if a power deficiency occurs. After introducing the net power surplus and deficiency expression (
5
), this dispatch rule is easily considered by the following constraints:

\displaystyle S_{i,t}-S_{i,t-1}\leq p_{t}^{+}

(6)

\displaystyle S_{i,t-1}-S_{i,t}\leq p_{t}^{-}

Remark
: By construction,
p_{t}^{+}>0
implies
p_{t}^{-}=0
, and
p_{t}^{-}>0
implies
p_{t}^{+}=0
. This mutual exclusivity ensures that storage devices are charged only during surplus periods and discharged only during deficiency periods, thereby precluding
cross-charging
and remaining consistent with the greedy dispatch model used in Algorithm
1
.

IV-B

Single Energy Storage Resource

We first consider the system with only one energy storage device, where the storage device index
i
is ignored. Let
0\leq S_{t}\leq\overline{S}
and
0\leq x_{t}\leq\overline{x}
be the state of charge (SoC) and power output of energy storage, then the unserved energy can be written as:

\displaystyle\sum_{t|p_{t}^{-}>0}p_{t}^{-}-(S_{t-1}-S_{t})

(7)

Because the greedy reliability dispatch model is the optimal approach to minimize the unserved energy, the corresponding objective function can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\min_{S_{t}}\quad\sum_{t|p_{t}^{-}>0}p_{t}^{-}-(S_{t-1}-S_{t}),

(8)

which is equal to

\displaystyle\min_{S_{t}}\quad\sum_{t|p_{t}^{-}>0}(S_{t}-S_{t-1}).

(9)

Since
S_{t-1}-S_{t}\leq p_{t}^{-}
, the reliability dispatch result can also be expressed as the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{S_{t}}

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\min\{0,\,S_{t}-S_{t-1}\}

(10)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle S_{0}=\text{constant},\quad\forall t\in\mathcal{T}:

\displaystyle 0\leq S_{t}\leq\overline{S},

\displaystyle(\lambda_{1t},\lambda_{2t})

\displaystyle-\overline{x}\leq S_{t}-S_{t-1}\leq\overline{x},

\displaystyle(\lambda_{3t},\lambda_{4t})

\displaystyle-p_{t}^{-}\leq S_{t}-S_{t-1}\leq p_{t}^{+},

\displaystyle(\lambda_{5t},\lambda_{6t}).

where
\lambda_{1t},...,\lambda_{6t}
are the Lagrangian multipliers of corresponding constraints, i.e.
(5.1t),...,(5.6t)
, respectively. Because the capacity surplus profiles are independent of each other, the EUE can be calculated as the average unserved energy of
N_{s}
profiles. We are interested in the affection of parameters
\overline{S}
and
\overline{x}
to the value of EUE.

Let
V_{1}:=\sum_{i=1}^{T}\min\{0,S_{t}-S_{t-1}\}
represent the objective function of (
10
). The Lagrangian for this optimization problem parameterized by
\overline{S}
and
\overline{x}
is

\displaystyle L(\boldsymbol{S},\boldsymbol{\lambda},\overline{S},\overline{x})=V_{1}(\boldsymbol{S},\overline{S},\overline{x})-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda_{1t}S_{t}+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda_{2t}(S_{t}-\overline{S})

(11)

\displaystyle+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda_{3t}(S_{t-1}-S_{t}-\overline{x})+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda_{4t}(S_{t}-S_{t-1}-\overline{x})

\displaystyle+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda_{5t}(S_{t}-S_{t-1}-p_{t}^{+})+\sum_{i=1}^{T}\lambda_{6t}(S_{t-1}-S_{t}-p_{t}^{-})

Based on the
Envelope Theorem
, the differentiate
V_{1}
with respect to a parameter, let’s say
\overline{S}
, is equal to the partial derivative of the Lagrangian function concerning
\overline{S}
, holding all other variable constants, i.e.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial V_{1}}{\partial\overline{S}}=L_{\overline{S}}=-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda_{2t}

(12)

Similarly, for the parameter
\overline{x}
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\partial V_{1}}{\partial\overline{x}}=L_{\overline{x}}=-\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\lambda_{3t}+\lambda_{4t})

(13)

Theorem 1

(Non-negative MRI and Piecewise linear EUE: Single Energy Storage)
:

If the QC is defined as either storage energy capacity
\overline{S}
or power capacity
\overline{x}
(or any positive linear combination thereof), the MRI of energy storage is non-negative, and the EUE function is piecewise linear (both with respect to QC), regardless of system condition.

Proof.

Because the result directly solved by reliability dispatch algorithms is one of the optimal solutions of (
10
), the feasibility of the problem is always guaranteed
[
7
]
.

Let
\text{QC}=\overline{S}
for example. It is easy to show the MRI is non-negative:

\text{MRI}=-\frac{\partial\text{EUE}}{\partial\overline{S}}=-\frac{\partial V_{1}}{\partial\overline{S}}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda_{2t}\geq 0

(14)

To show the piecewise linear property of EUE, let
v(\overline{S})
be the optimal value of this problem.

Then, we will prove
v(\overline{S})
is a piecewise linear, convex, and continuous function of
\overline{S}
.

For each fixed value of
\overline{S}
, all the constraints are linear in the decision variables

\{S_{t}\}
. Although the objective

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\min\{0,\,S_{t}-S_{t-1}\}

is piecewise linear in
\{S_{t}\}
, it can be reformulated as a linear program via standard auxiliary-variable techniques. In any case, for each
\overline{S}
, this problem is
finite-dimensional
and
piecewise-linear
in
\{S_{t}\}
. Therefore, it can be treated as a
parametric linear program
in which
\overline{S}
appears
linearly
in the constraints:

S_{t}\,\leq\,\overline{S}.

The optimal value
v(\overline{S})
depends on which constraints are active, with
\overline{S}
appearing linearly in the bound
S_{t}\leq\overline{S}
. As
\overline{S}
varies, the problem’s feasible region and objective are determined by a finite number of active-constraint sets, each yielding an affine relationship. Below, we formalize this step-by-step.

1. Finite Number of Bases:

Because our piecewise-linear optimization problem has a finite number of possible active-constraint sets (bases), even though these constraints depend on
\overline{S}
, the total number of ways to pick which constraints are “active” is finite.

2. Each Basis Yields an Affine Function in
\overline{S}
:

Fix a particular set of active constraints (a basis). The solution on that basis, if it remains feasible, can be written as an affine function of
\overline{S}
. Indeed, solving

A(\overline{S})\,S=b(\overline{S})

for the basis
\{A,b\}
gives a solution

\,S^{*}(\overline{S})

that is affine in
\overline{S}
. The corresponding objective value is then also affine in
\overline{S}
.

3. The Optimal Value is the Minimum of These Affine Pieces:

As
\overline{S}
varies, different bases may become optimal. Consequently,

v(\overline{S})\;=\;\min_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\bigl\{f_{B}(\overline{S})\,\bigm|\,B\text{ is feasible and can be optimal}\bigr\},

where
\mathcal{B}
is the
finite
set of all possible bases. A pointwise minimum of finitely many affine (linear) functions is:

•

Piecewise linear:
each basis corresponds to a linear “piece.”

•

Convex:
a minimum of linear functions is convex.

•

Continuous:
a minimum of finitely many continuous functions is continuous, and piecewise linear functions have no jumps.

Putting this together implies
v(\overline{S})
must be piecewise linear, convex, and continuous in
\overline{S}
, which also means the EUE is continuous and piecewise linear with respect to the QC
[
2
]
.

When
\text{QC}=\overline{x}
, it is also easy to show the same conclusion. The basic intuition is that after converting the reliability dispatch problem into (
10
), the increasing of either storage energy or power capacity relaxes the problem’s feasible region, which has the potential to reduce the system EUE.

∎

Corollary 1

(Direct Calculation of MRI: Single Energy Storage)
:

If the QC is defined as either storage energy capacity
\overline{S}
or power capacity
\overline{x}
, the MRI can be directly calculated based on the value of Lagrangian multipliers in (
10
).

Remark
: After considering multiple Monte Carlo experiments, the MRI should be the average value of each result, but the non-negative property is still valid.

V
Discussion

V-A

Qualified Capacity

Under the MRI capacity accreditation scheme, the definition of QC is relatively flexible. For instance, defining QC as the storage’s full power capacity yields the same accreditation result as defining it as half of the storage’s power capacity.

Theorem 3

(Invariant QMRIC)
:

Let
QC_{1}
and
QC_{2}
represent the capacity qualification values derived from two different capacity qualification methods, and different qualifications do not change the dispatch models of storage resources in RAA. Suppose there exists a constant
\alpha\neq 0
such that
QC_{1}=\alpha QC_{2}
for all different storage capacities. After applying marginal reliability impact correction, the resulting qualified MRI-based capacity values
QMRIC_{1}
and
QMRIC_{2}
are identical, i.e.
QMRIC_{1}=QMRIC_{2}
.

Proof.

Based on the chain rule for derivatives, we have

\displaystyle QMRIC_{1}=

\displaystyle QC_{1}\frac{\text{MRI}}{\text{MRI}_{p}}=-\frac{QC_{1}}{\text{MRI}_{p}}\frac{\partial\text{EUE}}{\partial QC_{1}}

(19)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\alpha QC_{2}}{\text{MRI}_{p}}\frac{\partial\text{EUE}}{\partial\alpha QC_{2}}=QMRIC_{2}

∎

Theorem
3
also holds for other resources, such as thermal and renewable generators. However, for energy storage or energy-limited resources, the energy capacity plays an important part in capacity qualification. If the QC is constituted by both storage power capacity
\bar{x}
and energy capacity
\bar{S}
, their contribution factor plays an important role in QMRIC calculation. Supposing
QC=\beta_{1}\bar{x}+\beta_{2}\bar{S}
, then the QMRIC can be expressed by chain rule as:

\displaystyle QMRIC=

\displaystyle QC\frac{\text{MRI}}{\text{MRI}_{p}}=-\frac{QC}{\text{MRI}_{p}}\frac{\partial\text{EUE}}{\partial QC}

(20)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{QC}{\text{MRI}_{p}}\frac{\partial\text{EUE}}{\partial(\beta_{1}\bar{x}+\beta_{2}\bar{S})}

Noticing that the unit of
\beta_{2}
is 1/hour, how to decide the value of these contribution factors is still a big challenge and unsolved question. Currently, most US ISOs only use the storage power capacity
\bar{x}
to calculate QC.

V-B

Initial SoC

In our models (
10
) and (
16
), the initial state of charge (SoC) of the storage is treated as a constant value, independent of the corresponding storage capacity. If the initial SoC is assumed to equal the energy capacity, i.e.,
S_{0}=\bar{S}
in the single storage problem (
10
), the marginal contribution of the storage’s energy capacity to EUE changes as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial V_{1}}{\partial\overline{S}}=-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda_{2t}+\lambda_{31}-\lambda_{41}

(21)

Since energy storage is not permitted to charge to its full energy capacity at time
t=1
, the constraint associated with
\lambda_{31}
is always inactive, implying that
\lambda_{31}=0
. Therefore, (
21
) simplifies to:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial V_{1}}{\partial\overline{S}}=-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lambda_{2t}-\lambda_{41}\leq 0

(22)

Although the expression of MRI may vary slightly when assuming the initial storage equals its full capacity, the non-negative properties of MRI remain valid. Furthermore, these properties hold not only for single storage units but also for cases involving multiple energy storage systems.

V-C

Round-trip Efficiency

The round-trip efficiency (RTE) of an energy storage system is defined as the ratio of total energy output to total energy input. While various approaches exist for modeling RTE in resource adequacy analysis
[
24
,
7
]
or capacity accreditation
[
30
,
22
]
, the impact of RTE on MRI has received relatively little attention.

Similar to the commercial software GE MARS
[
10
]
, we apply round-trip efficiency during the charging process in our model. For instance, an energy storage system with an efficiency of 0.9 will store 9 MWh for every 10 MW charged over an hour. Let
\eta
represent the efficiency of a single energy storage unit; we then modify the
\lambda_{5t}
constraint in (
10
) to incorporate the RTE, as follows:

S_{t}-S_{t-1}\leq\eta p_{t}^{+}.

(23)

For cases with multiple energy storage resources, each with heterogeneous RTEs
\eta_{i}
, new variables
p_{i,t}^{+}
are introduced to represent individual charging behavior. Consequently, the original
\lambda_{i,5t}
related constraints in the optimization model (
16
) are replaced by:

\displaystyle S_{i,t}-S_{i,t-1}\leq\eta_{i}p_{i,t}^{+}

(24)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N_{s}}p_{i,t}^{+}\leq p_{t}^{+}

After considering RTE, the MRI value of energy storage might be affected, but the conclusions of non-negative MRI and continuous (piecewise linear) EUE shown in Theorem
1
and Theorem
2
are still valid.

VI-C

Guidance for System Operators on Storage Modeling

Under the MRI capacity accreditation framework, the discount factor (rMRI) discriminates to different energy storage types because of the dispatch rule. In reality, the charging and discharging orders are not decided by the left duration under the reliability dispatch rule but decided by the energy market clearing results. However, the market behavior of storage is hard to model, especially under long-term planning problems.

Except for the reliability dispatch rule, the system operator might set the dispatch priority based on other factors, such as the storage operation cost or historical performance. For example, assuming a dispatch rule
always
follows the simple charging and discharging priority: storage 1
>
storage 2
>
storage 3
>
storage 4. Based on the same Monte Carlo experiment data, the reliability contribution of all energy storage is the average reduction of EUE to 492.2 MWh, which is less than the reliability dispatch method.

On the other hand, let the MRI of a thermal generator with zero outage rate be the perfect MRI. Under the reliability dispatch model, the perfect MRI is 1.24 MWh/MW, increasing to 1.27 MWh/MW under the simple dispatch model. These two factors make the storage MRI smaller while
\text{MRI}_{\text{perfect}}
larger, together resulting in a smaller capacity discount factor (rMRI) for energy storage under a simple dispatch rule.

VI-D

Suggestions for Policymakers on Reliability Criteria

The ICR plays an important role in establishing the nominal system capacity for both the demand curve and MRI calculation in FCM
[
35
]
. In practice, since the capacity market operates as a forward market, system operators first forecast load profiles for the future capacity delivery year and then determine the corresponding ICR based on the reliability standard and resource mix required by policymakers.

The traditional resource adequacy standard, based on the LOLE of 1-day-in-10 years, is increasingly insufficient for addressing emerging risks. These risks arise from greater variability and uncertainty caused by the evolving resource mix and increasing demand levels. In response, the North American Electric Reliability Corporation (NERC) has recently been evaluating a multi-criteria approach that incorporates metrics such as loss of load hours (LOLH) and normalized expected unserved energy (NEUE)
[
18
,
27
]
. Similarly, planners outside North America facing comparable resource adequacy challenges have adopted different strategies. For example, the Australian National Energy Market Reliability Panel uses an NEUE threshold of less than or equal to 0.002 % per year as its reliability standard
[
5
]
.

Fig. 4:
System reliability under different metrics and ICR levels

For the same resource mix, Fig.
4
illustrates the values of various reliability criteria across different ICR levels, with the baseline in our test case derived from historical installed capacity and load data from California. It is clear that all the reliability criteria are non-increasing as more capacity is installed, but each criterion measures different risk aspects, with preferences toward the duration, magnitude, or frequency of load loss. The red dotted lines in Fig.

represent commonly used reliability standards: 0.1 days/year for LOLE, 2.4 hours/year for LOLH, and 0.002 % energy/year for EUE. While the baseline ICR meets the LOLH and EUE requirements with a margin, an additional 3% capacity is needed to satisfy the LOLE standard.

Policymakers’ choice of reliability standards significantly impacts energy storage capacity accreditation, which is highly sensitive to ICR. Figure

illustrates the rMRI values under different QC definitions and varying ICR levels. As ICR increases, both the system’s unserved energy hours and magnitude decrease, indicating that the reliability contribution of energy storage becomes less constrained by its energy capacity. This reduction in the MRI gap compared to a perfect thermal resource leads to an increase in storage rMRI as ICR continues to rise.

In addition to adopting more robust metrics and standards, policymakers need to investigate a broader set of design-based scenarios to ensure technically sound analyses and deeper insights. This is especially important for more accurately capturing load growth and low-probability but high-impact events. For instance, some seasonal and prompt auction reforms in ISO NE capacity market aim to reduce uncertainty in the capacity market, helping to address these challenges
[
12
]
.

VII
Conclusion

This paper presents a new approach for energy storage capacity accreditation using MRI, offering insights into the limitations of traditional resource adequacy models. By reformulating the storage accreditation problem as an optimization framework, our method enables direct and efficient calculation of the MRI, bypassing the need for computationally intensive perturbation analysis. We also provide theoretical proof of the non-negative and piecewise linear properties of storage MRI and EUE across various system scenarios, respectively. The numerical simulations validate the approach, revealing important phenomena in storage accreditation and offering practical insights for capacity market design.

The findings deepen the understanding of energy storage’s marginal reliability impact and the factors affecting capacity accreditation, including qualified capacity definitions and dispatch rules, making these insights particularly relevant to ongoing marginal accreditation reforms in U.S. ISOs. As the energy landscape continues to evolve with more renewable and storage resources, this methodology provides a foundation for improving market mechanisms, ensuring fair and precise accreditation, and supporting policy decisions.

Future research will focus on enhancing storage aggregation methods with attention to simulation step size, incorporating additional factors such as battery degradation, and exploring the relationship between the MRI of storage and renewable resources to further improve accuracy and applicability.
