Semantic Communication in Underwater IoT Networks for Meaning-Driven Connectivity
Ruhul Amin Khalil, Asiya Jehangir, Hanane Lamaazi, Sadaf Rubab, Nasir Saeed

Abstract
The Internet of Underwater Things (IoUT) is revolutionizing marine sensing and environmental monitoring, as well as subaquatic exploration, which are enabled by interconnected and intelligent subsystems. Nevertheless, the underwater communication is bounded by narrow bandwidth, high latency, and strict energy constraints that are the source of efficiency problems on traditional data-centric networks. To tackle these problematic issues, this work provides a survey on the recent advancement of Semamtic Communication(SC) for IoUT—this is a novel communication paradigm that seeks to harness not symbol raw information but rather its meaning and/or contextual significance. In this paper, we investigate the emerging advanced AI-powered frameworks, including large language models (LLMs), diffusion-based generative encoders, and federated learning (FL), that bridge semantic compression with context-aware prioritization and robust information reconstruction over noisy underwater channels. Hybrid acoustic–optical–RF architectures and edge-intelligent semantic encoders are also considered as enablers of sustainable and adaptive operations. Examples in underwater archaeology, marine ecology, and autonomous underwater vehicles (AUVs) coordination are provided as a relief to illustrate the merits of meaning-driven connectivity. The paper concludes with some recommendations, including semantic representations standardization, cross-domain interpolation, and privacy-support schemes. These issues must be addressed in the future before trustworthy SC-enabled IoUT systems can be developed for underwater communication.

These studies represent a paradigm shift in communication that focuses on communication over the underlying bits to support higher performance in networks under low-bandwidth, high-latency, and multipath interference environments.

Further, the authors in
[
10

[

Collectively, these studies highlight a new breed of AI-enabled SC systems that automatically manage semantic content to achieve trustworthy, energy-efficient, and context-aware underwater communication.

Moreover, generative and large-scale semantic models have been investigated in the context of IoUT applications to alleviate the challenge of balancing between compression, meaning retention, and real-time processing. Contributions

This paper makes the following key contributions to the field of SC for IoUT networks:

•

First, we present the first holistic and in-depth survey of SC approaches in the context of IoUT environments. The paper integrates perspectives from AI, LLMs, ontologies, and cross-layer communication frameworks to establish a unified understanding of meaning-driven underwater connectivity.

•

We then conducted a comprehensive classification and in-depth analysis of the existing SC frameworks for underwater networks, discussing their notable advantages and challenges in terms of architecture design, robustness, multimodality, and energy efficiency. The study covers implementations across the physical-, network-, and application-layer to identify significant performance tradeoffs and design gaps.

•

Then, we explore state-of-the-art AI-based methods, such as FL, DRL, and GSM, which improve semantic encoding and resilience to channel noise and adaptive connectivity. This perspective DRL, diffusion models, and transformer architectures as key enablers for autonomous and intelligent underwater communication.

•

We further conduct an in-depth exploration of SC-enabled applications in the context of environmental monitoring, underwater archaeology, marine biology, disaster management, and AUVs, demonstrating how semantic transmission and edge intelligence facilitate the enhancement of operational efficiency for real-world IoUT implementations.

•

Finally, we present some crucial open challenges and potential future research directions, and the importance of standardized semantic models. These models include generative AI-based encoding, hybrid acoustic–optical–RF modalities, energy-aware semantic encoding, and a secure privacy-preserving IoUT framework regarding large-scale deployment.

II
Fundamentals of SC in IoUT

The fundamentals of SC in IoUT networks encompass several key aspects essential for meaning-driven data exchange in challenging underwater environments. These include the core principles of SC, which guide efficient semantic extraction and transmission; the unique characteristics of underwater communication environments that impose constraints and design considerations; the architectural frameworks that enable semantic communication in IoUT systems; and the metrics used to evaluate semantic efficiency beyond traditional bit-level measures. In the following subsections, we provide a detailed discussion of each of these critical components, laying the foundation for understanding and advancing SC-enabled IoUT networks.

II-A

Key principles of SC

SC essentially changes the way data transmission was done in the past, which focused on accurate bit-level data delivery rather than conveying meaning and important information
[
6
,
9
]
. This paradigm is especially important in underwater IoUT networks because of inherent challenges, such as narrow acoustic bandwidth, fast-varying channels, high latency, and drastic energy limitations
[
19
,
8
,
21
]
. The fundamental goal of SC is to discover and extract webcast sensor data or smart multimedia features that are semantically important for understanding or decision making. In this approach, SC can eliminate redundant and irrelevant information so that users receive reliable and timely information using fewer bandwidth resources
[
10
,
20
]
.

Underwater communication faces several challenges, including multipath fading caused by signal reflections from the ocean surface and seabed, Doppler shifts resulting from motion between transmitters and receivers, and signal attenuation that complicates reliable recovery of transmitted data
[
18
,
11
]
. According to
[
22
]
, SC systems use context-aware extraction methods supported by advanced AI models, such as LLMs, generative adversarial networks (GANs), and diffusion models. These intelligent encoders perform adaptive semantic compression and content prioritization. These encoders effectively select information useful for different applications, including anomaly detection and habitat analysis, and discard data that adds little value
[
9
,
19
]
. On the receiving end, generative models reconstruct detailed semantic content, strengthening robustness against data loss, interference, and noise
[
18
,
20
]
. Because of this flexibility and fault tolerance, SC systems are well positioned to support both real-time autonomous missions and long-term monitoring tasks across underwater IoUT networks.

According to a popular theoretical framework that is used to guide SC, there are three different levels of communication. The first level is the technical level, which is responsible for making sure symbols are transmitted accurately through the channel. The second level is the semantic level, which is responsible for extracting, encoding, and interpreting semantic information. Lastly, there is the effectiveness level, which is concerned with how well the communication accomplishes the desired objective or task
[
23
,
6
]
. In practice, protocols that maximize the preservation and usability of semantic material matched with mission objectives can be designed using this layered abstraction, rather than focusing solely on low bit error rates. Optimizing resource allocations for maximal semantic efficiency is made possible by this principled approach, especially in IoUT, where limited energy and bandwidth impose strict trade-offs
[
24
]
. As a result, SC systems are becoming more popular as smart frameworks that combine SC with contextual awareness and AI-based learning to provide trustworthy, valuable, and efficient data exchange in marine settings.

II-B

Characteristics of IoUT Environments

The IoUT is a particular category of the IoT specifically designed to operate in the complex underwater environment, which accounts for over 70% of Earth’s surface
[
25
]
. In contrast with the terrestrial ones, IoUT has specific environmental and technical challenges that are expected to heavily affect the network and its communication protocols. Key characteristics include the fact that acoustic signals are the dominant communication mechanism (because electromagnetic waves do not propagate well under water), long propagation time because sound travels more slowly in water than radio frequency in air (
1.5\times 10^{3}
m/s vs
3\times 10^{8}
m/s), and limited bandwidth, which is typically less than 10 kbps
[
26
,
27
]
. Such environments have challenges such as high latency, low transmission bandwidth, network dynamic topology with water flow, and unreliable communication. In addition, the underwater sensors often encounter trouble in recharging their battery power, and accordingly, operating with an energy-efficient strategy becomes essential.

SC offers a promising approach to addressing the distinct characteristics of IoUT environments by prioritizing data relevance and informativeness over sheer volume
[
19
]
. Conventional underwater networks usually focus on collecting and transmitting all sensed data, which, however, generates redundancy and resource waste due to limited bandwidth and energy constraints in undersea environments. Semantic-driven frameworks introduce mechanisms for intelligent sensing, adaptive data transmission, and selective message forwarding guided by the meaning and context of sensed information. For example, semantic gateways can be used for preprocessing and aggregating sensor data, in which communication focuses on relaying meaningful, timely information ranked by severity according to semantics
[
10
]
. These techniques address long propagation delays and dynamic topologies by avoiding unnecessary transmissions, thereby saving bandwidth and power.

Furthermore, semantic approaches apply sophisticated models (e.g., Bayesian optimization with deep neural networks) to enable real-time optimization of sensor placement, data freshness, and communication parameters
[
18
]
. The IoUT systems can dynamically adjust transmission patterns and sensor deployment to meet application-level freshness requirements, e.g., Age of Information (AoI). It is done by representing the semantic uncertainty and variability of the underwater environment. This makes it possible to collect data accurately and in a timely manner, even in rough underwater environments where timeliness and information quality are more important than volume
[
28
]
. In the end, by assimilating SC into IoUT, the problem of underwater communication is naturally addressed, as it is a good way to improve network efficiency and application performance by optimizing message flow based on meaning and environment. II-C

SC Architecture for IoUT

The SC architecture for IoUT is designed to address the specific issues in the underwater environment. It does this through semantically meaningful data transmission and intelligent decision-making. The modular and layered design enables the complex demands for sensing, communication, processing, and applications to be accommodated efficiently
[
29
]
. Introducing SC in layers can efficiently utilize limited underwater resources, such as bandwidth, power, and computational capacity. In the following sections, we describe the four key layers of this architecture: the Sensing/Device Layer, the Network/Transport Layer (including gateways), and the Application Layer. We focus on their roles, challenges, and how semantic technologies can enhance underwater communication and data functionality. II-C
1
Sensing/Device Layer

In the IoUT, the sensing layer is a base to build the SC structure. This layer contains various underwater sensors and equipment, such as temperature, pressure, and salinity sensors, AUVs, ROVs (Remotely Operated Vehicles), and specialized image devices
[
30
]
. Such instruments measure physical, chemical, and biological characteristics in water and transform them into a digital signal. With the hostile nature of these underwater environments (High pressure, corrosive salt water, and difficult building access), robustness and energy efficiency are required in the design and deployment of sensing devices
[
31
,
32
]
. Energy is the main challenge here, because most sensors have power limitations: many of them are powered by battery packs that have only limited capacity to be recharged and then discharged, which requires smart energy management and occasional sensing activities in order to extend their lifetimes.

At this layer, SC plays an important role, performing localized preprocessing and extracting relevant features from raw sensor readings prior to transmission. Instead of funneling raw high-dimensional observations to the cloud, sensors with semantic complexity are used to selectively sample and encode signals that bring contextual or task-specific information
[
10
]
. For example, an imaging sensor could detect and localize features of coral bleaching or of oil spills that are present in the scene and transmit compressed semantic descriptors instead of full-depth images. This reduced data volume allows for efficient communication over bandwidth-limited acoustic channels and minimizes energy consumption. Moreover, the semantically enabled devices can adjust their sensing frequency as per the context of the environment or the network. which leachate more efficiently use of the scarce underwater resources
[
9
]
. Novel sensing technologies are being developed that incorporate AI at the edge and enable intelligent processing close to physical phenomena, which provides a foundation for autonomous underwater monitoring systems.

II-C
2
Network/Transport Layer

The transport and network layers provide reliable semantic data over the demanding underwater communication platform. This one includes the acoustic modems, the optic links, and the RF links with surface buoys, as well as hybrid methods of communicating, which use each channel according to specific environmental conditions (such as water turbidity, depth, or distance)
[
33
]
. Acoustic communication is the dominant long-distance underwater transmission method, but its bandwidth is narrow, and the delay and multipath fading are significant. Optical communications have high data rates, but are only viable for clear water and short-range circumstances. Hybrid protocols with acoustic/optical transition state for underwater SC networks significantly enhance the throughput and reliability
[
34
]
.

At the semantic level, this layer is enriched by meaning-aware routing and congestion control mechanisms that try to ensure that semantic-rich and urgent information is transmitted. Context-aware MAC protocols prioritize channel resources for high-priority semantic messages and postpone or ignore low-importance traffic to avoid collisions and save energy
[
35
]
. Transport protocols are used to support the long propagation delays and high error rates in underwater channels, which use semantic error resilience such as generative reconstruction and knowledge graph-aided error correction to preserve semantic fidelity despite quality degradation
[
19
]
. Delay-tolerant networking (DTN)-based solutions are embedded to deal with an intermittent connectivity environment: semantic packets are opportunistically stored and forwarded to ensure the delivery of data in spite of frequent link breaks. These collective advancements empower the network and transport layers to meet the semantics of IoUT applications’ strict quality of service (QoS) demands.

II-C
3
Processing Layer

The processing layer acts as the SC hub, where data fusion, semantic relationship extraction, context reasoning, and decision-making occur. This layer usually runs on edge computing nodes at surface buoys, shore stations, or AUVs with sufficient computation capabilities
[
36
]
. Raw sensor inputs are heavily processed to extract high-level semantic features, remove redundancy, and integrate information from different sources
[
10
,
14
]
. The DL models, e.g., LLMs, and the ontology-driven knowledge graphs are used to make sense of sensor readings, recognize events or anomalies, and derive semantic representations for communication and control. Using the RL and Bayesian optimization architectures, the processing layer may dynamically learn to optimize sensing scheduling. This, in turn, improves transmission modes and resource allocation to maximize semantic efficiency and minimize energy consumption
[
37
]
.

Moreover, the processing layer enforces interoperability between diverse IoUT devices and networks using standardized semantic ontologies and data formats
[
38
]
. This semantic interoperability is important to combine heterogeneous data sources such as marine biology sensors, geological monitors, and industrial inspection systems for consistent situational awareness and actionable insights
[
