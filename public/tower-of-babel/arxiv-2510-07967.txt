Abstract
. Lost architectural heritage is characterized by vanished structures and fragmented records, posing interpretive challenges. Taking Hanyuan Hall of the Tang dynasty’s Daming Palace as a case, our formative study with archaeologists, administrators, and visitors revealed two key issues: current practices compress layered cultural meanings into basic facts and rely on linear narratives that overlook ongoing (re)interpretations after disappearance. Therefore, we designed Pre/Absence , a VR experience grounded in the presence–absence dialectic that interweaves presence and absence of such heritage into a spatiotemporal narrative. While both improved knowledge understanding, the VR experience more significantly enhanced cultural awareness, strengthened emotional responses to loss, and fostered critical reflection on heritage’s evolving socio-cultural and political meanings. Our findings suggest that VR can transcend static reconstruction, engaging users as co-constructors of cultural meaning and offering a nuanced approach to critical heritage narrative design in HCI.

1.
INTRODUCTION

Lost architectural heritage refers to buildings, structures, or features of a built environment whose original forms have been irretrievably lost
(
Rafeiro et al. 2024
)
.
Complete loss of architectural heritage happens for many reasons, like neglect, disasters, demolition, or renovations. When such a loss happens, it not only leads to the disappearance of buildings and landscapes, but also means losing the history, culture, and memories connected to those places
(
Pérez-Hernández et al. 2020
;
Rogo and Oguge 2000
;
Garagnani et al. 2021
;
Rafeiro et al. 2024
;
Brǔha et al. 2020
)
.
The disappearance of heritage severs the connection between people and the past, threatening the continuity of culture and its sustainable preservation
(
James-Williamson et al. 2024
)
. Moreover, a lack of understanding of one’s own heritage and civilization further weakens cultural identity and the motivation to engage in preservation efforts
(
Kamel 2019
)
. Therefore, lost architectural heritage serves as a promising means to foster cultural awareness and understanding, as it embodies civilizations both present and absent within local communities.

Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) researchers have leveraged Virtual Reality (VR), Augmented Reality (AR), and Mixed Reality (MR) to interpret architectural heritage. Existing studies have primarily focused on visualizing tangible or intangible elements
(
Li et al. 2025
;
Tian et al. 2024
)
, interactive restoration
(
Gatto et al. 2025
)
, and digital guides for on-site visits
(
Xu et al. 2024
;
Liang et al. 2025
)
. Although these approaches convey the historical data, information, and knowledge of heritage, the connections between heritage and the era, society, and humanistic life are equally significant yet remain underexplored. Especially, lost architectural heritage—an inherently more complex and underexplored domain in HCI—often remains insufficiently recognized and valued in contemporary contexts, mainly because its physical absence often leads to diminished public cultural awareness
(
Pérez-Hernández et al. 2020
)
. Moreover, VR offers the potential to recreate heritage scenes within virtual spaces, enabling people to traverse time and space to experience history within an immersive environment, thereby enhancing cultural awareness and understanding
(
Mu et al. 2024
)
. Therefore, what is urgently needed is a nuanced narrative approach that can more profoundly, holistically, and critically interpret lost architectural heritage within immersive experiences
(
Rafeiro et al. 2024
)
, thereby prompting cultural awareness and understanding.

In response, we draw upon the
presence–absence dialectic
from critical heritage studies as a locus for designing immersive narrative experiences, to deeply enhance cultural awareness and understanding
(
James-Williamson et al. 2024
)
. For lost architectural heritage, this dialectic is crucial because interpretation involves not only surviving traces but also absent elements, which may be fragmented, uncertain, contested, or silenced within contemporary contexts. Specifically, we define
presence
as the material or immaterial traces that endure after destruction and continue to shape understandings of the site (e.g., ruins, historical archives, or surviving cultural practices). In contrast,
absence
refers to the irrecoverable void of physical entities, cultural practices, and collective memory caused by destruction, erasure, or oblivion (e.g., vanished architectural structures, irreproducible historical events, or neglected narratives). By analyzing the interplay between
presence
and
absence
, we aim to move beyond the current interpretation approaches in HCI, to reveal deeper cultural connotations and prompt cultural awareness and understanding.

While the significance of the
presence–absence dialectic
has been recognized as a potential, it remains unclear how it can be effectively embedded in VR narrative design for lost architectural heritage, and how such narratives influence cultural awareness and understanding. To address these gaps, we aim to explore the following research questions (RQ) in this paper:

•

RQ1:
What are the current practices, challenges, and design needs in interpreting lost architectural heritage characterized by the
presence
and
absence
?

•

RQ2:
How can a VR narrative experience be designed to embody the
presence–absence dialectic
within a spatiotemporal frame so as to prompt cultural awareness and understanding?

•

RQ3:
What are the effects of such a VR narrative experience on users’ cultural awareness and understanding of lost architectural heritage?

To address these RQs, we selected Hanyuan Hall of Daming Palace in Xi’an, China, as a representative case, which was the largest royal palace but was completely destroyed over 220 years after its completion during the Tang Dynasty. We began with a formative study involving archaeologists, heritage administrators, and visitors of the Hanyuan Hall of Daming Palace. This study revealed two key issues: current interpretation practices flatten the layered cultural meanings into basic facts, and they follow a linear, single-sided narrative that overlooks the hall’s disappearance and subsequent reinterpretations. These findings highlighted the need to both excavate deeper cultural meanings and embrace the ever-changing dynamics of heritage narratives. Drawing on the insights from the study, we designed a VR experience that critically interweaves
presence
and
absence
into a spatiotemporal narrative. We then conducted a control experiment with 28 participants using a mixed-method approach. Our findings showed that, though both the paper-based and VR-based experiences promoted participants’ cultural awareness and understanding, the VR narrative experience produced significantly greater enhancement.

In general, our main contributions to the HCI field are as follows:

•

Empirical insights into the key challenges of interpreting the history and culture of architectural lost architectural heritage, leading to the formation of 2 design goals, and then 2 proposed design features of a VR experience.

•

Pre/Absence
: a novel, theory-informed critical VR narrative experience designed for integrating
presence
and
absence
dialectical aspects of lost architectural heritage into a spatiotemporal narrative. Through this VR experience, users can critically engage with the layered history and information of a heritage to improve their cultural awareness and a deeper cultural understanding.

•

Empirical findings that show the VR narrative experience grounded in the
presence–absence dialectic
produced significantly greater gains in cultural awareness and understanding of lost architectural heritage than conventional paper-based intervention. These findings demonstrate the dialectic embedded in immersive narratives as a powerful design lens, providing broad design implications and broad reflections for future interpretive practices of lost architectural heritage in VR.

2.
THEORETICAL BACKGROUND & RELATED WORK

Our research was informed by prior work on presence and absence theory in philosophy, critical heritage studies, and HCI , cultural awareness for heritage site preservation, and the use of immersive technologies to interpret heritage sites.

2.1.
The Presence-Absence Dialectic in Critical Heritage Studies and HCI

2.1.1.
Presence-Absence Dialectics in Critical Heritage Studies

The dialectic of
presence
and
absence
originates in post-structuralist philosophy. Philosopher
Derrida emphasized that the perceived presence is
"continuously compounded with a non-presence and non-perception"
, such that absence itself also functions as a form of presence
(
Derrida 1994
;
Derrida 1973
)
. Central to this view is the notion of the
trace
, i.e., the mark of something absent that structures meaning in the present
(
Belsey 2022
;
Meier et al. 2013a
)
. Within this framework,
presence
highlights the perceptible (i.e., material remains, textual records, or embodied practices), while
absence
draws attention to what is lost, silenced, or rendered invisible. Importantly, these concepts do not exist as binary opposites but are in a dialectical relation, where presence is always shadowed by absence, and absence can be materialized through memory, imagination, and representation
(
Dogan and Kan 2020
)
. Specifically, based on the literature review, the definitions of
presence
and
absence
used in this study are as follows
(
James-Williamson et al. 2024
;
Goulding et al. 2018
;
Felder et al. 2015
;
Aykaç 2023
;
Yarker 2016
)
:

•

Presence:
refers to the material or immaterial traces of heritage that remain perceptible after its destruction, continuing to influence contemporary understanding and experience of the heritage. Examples include ruins and historical archives.

•

Absence:
refers to the irrecoverable void in physical entities, cultural practices, and collective memory caused by irreversible destruction, erasure, or oblivion, rendering them directly imperceptible. Examples include vanished architectural structures, irreproducible historical events or scenarios, and plural historical narratives ignored or hidden by single official narratives.

2.1.2.
Presence and Absence in HCI

Moreover, it is worth noting that
presence
and
absence
have had varied uses within the HCI literature. For example, the term
presence
often refers to the experience of "being there" in a mediated environment
(
Riva et al. 2003
)
, especially in VR
(
Witmer and Singer 1998
)
. Pujol-Tost has extended this concept further, proposing the notion of
cultural presence
to explain user experiences in a virtual archaeology scenario
(
Pujol-Tost 2017
)
. The concept of
telepresence
was later introduced to describe a real-time, remote communication and collaboration system. Based on this,
Skarbez et al. 2017
expanded the common understanding of
presence
as a feeling of "being there" (the illusion of place) to include the notion of "being together" (the illusion of co-presence or social presence). However, these two concepts merely concentrate on real-time connections, which overlook memories of the past. More recently,
Ishii et al. 2025
introduced the vision of
TeleAbsence
, which aims to foster illusory communication through interactive interfaces that preserve memories against the passing of time and the fear of being forgotten. Within this vision, they proposed
Presence of Absence
as a core design principle, highlighting that absence itself can be made perceptible and affective, inviting users to engage with the voids, silences, and erasures. Rather than fully reconstructing what is lost, this principle leverages absence as an active design material to provoke reflection and imagination, offering new ways for HCI research to critically interpret lost architectural heritage.

2.1.3.
Presence-Absence Dialectic as a Bridge to Design Critical Heritage Narratives in HCI

Taken together, the notion of the
presence–absence dialectic
in critical heritage studies and the broader uses of
presence
and
absence
in HCI reveal meaningful resonances. Specifically, heritage studies foreground the dialectical interplay between what remains perceptible and what is irretrievably lost, while HCI examines how presence and absence structure mediated experiences of being there, being together, or engaging with what is no longer directly accessible. Both research fields stress that
absence
is not simply a void but an active condition shaping perception, experience, and meaning. Thus, this conceptual convergence positions the
presence–absence dialectic
as a unifying philosophical lens for designing critical heritage narratives in HCI.

To enact this lens in design practice, VR offers unique affordances: it collapses spatiotemporal distances, transports users beyond the here-and-now, and blurs boundaries between real and virtual through immersive embodiment and narratives
(
Ishii et al. 2025
)
. Crucially, VR can render
absence
perceptible by presenting voids, traces, and silences that evoke memory and imagination, thereby not only signifying past
presence
but also actively “revoking”
absence
to provoke reflection
(
Dogan and Kan 2020
;
Schwind et al. 2019
;
Fisher 2012
;
Atkin 2006
;
Gallix 2011
;
Gergen 2002
)
. Therefore, our research leveraged the
presence–absence dialectic
as a design philosophy to inform the creation of in-depth lost architectural heritage narratives in VR, aiming to foster cultural awareness and deeper cultural understanding.

2.2.
Using Immersive Technologies for Architectural Heritage Interpretation

As introduced in Section
2.1
, we adopt VR as the medium to realize the critical narrative design of lost architectural heritage. In this section, we review prior work on immersive technologies in the architectural heritage domain, with a focus on contributions and limitations from an HCI perspective.

Immersive technologies such as VR, AR, and MR have been widely applied to heritage interpretation because of their affordances in 3D visualization, spatial interaction, presence, and embodiment
(
Cheng et al. 2024
)
. For instance,
Skowronski et al. 2018
visualized the reconstruction stages of the Temple of Bel through AR-based layered mapping. Tian et al.
(
Tian et al. 2024
)
designed interactive visualizations of poems related to a traditional Chinese garden.
Li et al. 2025
integrated tangible and intangible elements of a rural heritage site into a stylized VR experience. Other studies have employed immersive systems for interactive restoration
(
Gatto et al. 2025
)
or as digital guides to enrich on-site visiting experiences
(
Xu et al. 2024
;
Liang et al. 2025
)
. Collectively, these works demonstrate the potential of immersive technologies to enhance heritage learning and engagement.

However, existing approaches remain limited in how they interpret and represent heritage. Most focus narrowly on delivering historical
data
,
information
, and
knowledge
—figures, events, and locations—often in linear and didactic ways
(
Li et al. 2025
)
. Such representations may improve factual learning, but they fall short in fostering comprehensive cultural awareness or encouraging critical reflection on the deeper meanings of heritage. These limitations become especially acute in the case of
lost architectural heritage
, where the absence of physical structures demands interpretive strategies that go beyond reconstruction and factual narration. Therefore, we build on the
presence–absence dialectic
outlined in Section
2.1
to advance the current approaches of heritage interpretations in VR, aiming to prompt users’ cultural awareness and deeper cultural understanding.

2.3.
Developing Cultural Awareness for Architectural Heritage Preservation

Cultural awareness refers to a developing consciousness of culture and how culture shapes values and beliefs
(
Burchum 2002
;
Abdelazim Ahmed 2017
)
. It entails recognizing and reflecting on the meanings embedded in heritage within a given context, and is widely regarded as a prerequisite for sustainable conservation and preservation
(
Bakri et al. 2015
;
Idilfitri et al. 2015
)
. Cultural awareness is multidimensional, encompassing factual knowledge, recognition of heritage values, a sense of responsibility, emotional engagement, and behavioral intentions
(
Shimray and Ramaiah 2019
;
Karadeniz 2020
;
Karaca et al. 2016
;
Kamel 2019
;
Niu and Jin 2024
)
. These interrelated dimensions extend beyond basic knowledge acquisition and learning to embody a deeper, reflective understanding of heritage.

For
lost architectural heritage
, cultivating cultural awareness is especially critical. Once physical structures are gone, only fragmentary traces, memories, and narratives remain to sustain collective memory. Without heightened awareness, such heritage risks being overlooked or undervalued, weakening cultural identity and diminishing motivation for preservation
(
James-Williamson et al. 2024
)
. Yet, most HCI research on immersive experiences has concentrated on basic knowledge acquisition and learning outcomes rather than on fostering awareness
(
Mu et al. 2024
;
Zhao et al. 2025
;
He et al. 2024
)
. Furthermore, how VR can be designed to cultivate deeper cultural awareness—particularly in the complex context of lost architectural heritage—also remains underexplored. To address this gap, our work introduces a novel, critical VR narrative experience that operationalizes the
presence–absence dialectic
in its design, and further empirically evaluates how such narratives affect users’ cultural awareness and understanding of lost architectural heritage.

3.
STUDY SITE

Our research focuses on a UNESCO World Heritage Site that exemplifies lost architectural heritage: the Hanyuan Hall within the Daming Palace in Xi’an, China. During the Tang dynasty, Chang’an (modern day Xi’an) served as the political, economic, and cultural center of the empire
(
Hou et al. 2012
)
. The Daming Palace, constructed in 634 AD
(
Wang 2012
)
, spanned approximately 350 hectares
(
Forte 2013
)
. At its heart stood the Hanyuan Hall, built in 663 AD
(
Xu et al. 2020
)
, which functioned as the principal venue for state affairs, imperial ceremonies, diplomatic engagements, and official rituals. Over 220 years after its completion, the hall and palace were destroyed during a war, resulting in the irreversible loss of their physical structures
(
Hou et al. 2012
)
.

After its disappearance, the narrative of Hanyuan Hall persisted in lived forms. From the Ming dynasty onward, the Hanyuan’ao village gradually emerged on the former hall site, as residents gradually settled in the area. This settlement remained until it was cleared by the government to make way for contemporary heritage conservation efforts. Archaeological excavations of the site began in 1957
(
The National Cultural Heritage Administration 2022
)
. In 2010, the Daming Palace National Heritage Park was opened to the public, and a rammed-earth foundation platform was reconstructed at the original location of Hanyuan Hall, based on the limited historical evidence available.

Overall, Hanyuan Hall is a representative case for studying VR narrative design grounded in the
presence–absence dialectic
, mainly because its surviving information is fragmentary, uncertain, or even sometimes contradictory, and its cultural meanings have continuously evolved across shifting socio-cultural contexts. These characteristics make it particularly suited to exploring how VR narratives can critically present layered, multifaceted, spatiotemporal information to foster cultural awareness, a deeper and comprehensive understanding of lost architectural heritage.

4.
FORMATIVE STUDY

To understand the key challenges faced by the interpretation of Hanyuan Hall at Daming Palace, as well as the public’s perception, and then discover design opportunities to address them, we conducted a formative interview-based study with an archaeologist, four heritage site administrators, and four visitors.

4.1.
Study Methodology

4.1.1.
Participants and Procedure

As we wanted to engage with multiple stakeholders during our interviews, we sought to recruit (1) archaeologist with prior involvement in archaeological research or excavations related to the Hanyuan Hall, (2) heritage administrator with over 5 years of professional experience at the Daming Palace that had a demonstrated familiarity with its history, and (3) on-site tourists that were recruited as they were viewing the Hanyuan Hall rammed-earth foundation platform. As a result of our recruitment efforts, our interview population included one archaeologist (F1; male; 71 years old), four heritage site administrators (F2-F5; 3 females and 1 male; aged between 30 and 45, M=36.5 years, SD=5.7 years), and four visitors (F6-F9; 5 females and 4 males; aged between 19 and 35, M=27 years, SD=6.5 years). We conducted one-on-one semi-structured interviews with them, and the interview probed (1) for archaeologist: scholarly view of the values, meanings, and components of this heritage, and opinions of current and future heritage interpretations practices; (2) for heritage administrators: current practices, challenges, and plans of official heritage interpretation, challenges faced by this heritage site; (3) for visitors: their prior knowledge and perception of this heritage, on-site visiting experience, and expectations for future improvement of heritage interpretation practices.

4.1.2.
Data Collection and Analysis

The interviews were audio-recorded with participants’ consent and later
transcribed into text. Two authors conducted a thematic analysis
(
Clarke and Braun 2014
)
on the transcripts, where both authors read the transcripts to gain a sense of the data and then developed a set of initial codes independently. They then discussed their initial codebooks and reached an agreement. Following this, the emerged themes were identified and reported as challenges (Section
4.2
), design goals (Section
4.3
). Based on the challenges and design goals, we further propose design features of our VR narrative experience (Section
4.4.2
).

4.2.
Challenges

Our formative study revealed two intertwined challenges in the current interpretation practices of Hanyuan Hall. These challenges explain why existing approaches struggle to cultivate cultural awareness and a deeper understanding of this lost architectural heritage, and they directly motivate the design goals in Section
4.3
.

4.2.1.
C1. Basic-Fact-Oriented Interpretation Flattens Heritage’s Layered Meanings

Current interpretation practices are heavily oriented toward factual knowledge—dates, functions, and historical events—yet rarely convey the cultural depth embedded in the hall’s disappearance. The archaeologist noted that the site’s information is “
fragmented, interwoven with visibility and invisibility, and at times even uncertain or contradictory
” (F1), highlighting the layered nature of presence and absence. However, administrators admitted that when this complexity is simplified for public communication, “
much of the uncertainty disappears, leaving only a very basic story
” (F4).
This flattening is reflected in visitors’ experiences. One explained: “
There is only one board that introduces its basic information … I cannot either imagine its full appearance or remember the dull information presented
” (F6). Another confessed: “
Before visiting this heritage park, I nearly did not have any prior knowledge of Hanyuan Hall
” (F7). Even locals showed limited awareness: “
I definitely know it is a heritage site, but until today’s guided tour, I had no idea that it is so culturally important
” (F6). Administrators recognized this shortcoming, acknowledging that “
presenting visitors with such basic information in a direct way is somewhat superficial, and they might not really understand its deeper cultural connotations
” (F3). Others worried that without deeper awareness, “
the future preservation and inheritance might be hindered
” (F5).

Together, these accounts show how an overemphasis on factual information erodes opportunities for audiences to reflect on what has been preserved, what has vanished, and what remains obscured. As a result, interpretation practices flatten layered cultural meanings and leave the public with limited cultural awareness or critical understanding of the hall’s significance.

4.2.2.
C2. Linear and Single-Sided Narratives Neglect Ongoing Reinterpretations of Heritage Dynamics Across Time

Beyond simplification, current interpretation practices follow a linear and single-sided trajectory that emphasizes the Tang dynasty’s prosperity while overlooking its decline, disappearance, and the ongoing reinterpretations that have emerged afterward. Administrators acknowledged that
the contents are largely linear and one-sided, concentrating narrowly on the glorious past while neglecting its downfall and continuing relevance
” (F3). Visitors echoed this view, noting that official signage and videos highlight magnificence but fail to convey how meanings of the heritage have changed across time:
the signage tells me it was magnificent, but not why it disappeared or what it means now
” (F9).

The archaeologist criticized this reductive approach, stressing that
heritage interpretation should reflect a continuous life journey, where elements of prosperity, disappearance, and reappearance intertwine within ever-changing sociocultural contexts
” (F1). He argued that fragmented traces across dynasties should be connected into a
dynamic figure of the heritage’s ongoing transformations and evolving meanings
" (F1). Visitors’ comments reinforced this point, with one remarking:
After the visit, I still cannot understand the culture and history embedded in it
” (F6). Administrators also expressed concern:
Most visitors come to this heritage park for leisure purposes, not to proactively learn its history … public’s cultural awareness of this lost architectural heritage is still very weak
" (F3).

These perspectives illustrate how linear, single-sided narratives obscure the ongoing dynamics of reinterpretation—how the absence of Hanyuan Hall itself has continued to generate cultural meanings across successive eras. By failing to situate interpretation within these ever-changing dynamics in a sociocultural context, current practices leave audiences with little sense of how heritage is continuously redefined, and why its significance persists long after its physical disappearance.

4.3.
Design Goals

To prompt cultural awareness and foster comprehensive and deep cultural understanding for lost architectural heritage, we propose the following two design goals (DG) to address the challenges introduced in Section
4.2
. Specifically, we use both the vertical and horizontal axes to introduce the design goals of our research.

4.3.1.
DG1. Vertically Excavating Cultural Meanings

To address C1, DG1 is to vertically excavate the cultural meanings of the lost architectural heritage. Here, “vertical excavation” goes beyond the physical stratigraphy of the site, referring to a process of tracing cultural, historical, and social layers beneath the surface. It invites interpretation that moves past factual description to reveal the symbolic and political significance embedded in each layer.
The archaeologist explained that during interpretation he often found “
elements of continuity and disappearance, visibility and invisibility, intertwine within the story, which makes the history of this heritage feel particularly layered and fascinating to interpret
” (F1). These insights align with the theoretical foundations of the
presence–absence dialectic
introduced in Section
2.1
, which emphasizes how cultural meanings emerge through the interplay of what is preserved and what has vanished. Such perspectives reveal the need for interpretation that critically organizes fragmented traces into layered narratives, enabling visitors to engage with the deeper cultural meanings and connotations of Hanyuan Hall.

4.3.2.
DG2. Horizontally Interpreting Heritage’s Ever-Changing Dynamics

To address C2, DG2 is to embrace the ever-changing dynamics of heritage narratives rather than confining them to a fixed, linear account focused on a certain period of time. Here, “horizontal interpretation” means linking events and reinterpretations across time, setting them side by side to reveal how cultural meanings change. The archaeologist emphasized that the fragments of Hanyuan Hall “
illustrate a life journey that extends beyond its construction and destruction, showing how meanings evolve within shifting sociocultural contexts
” (F1). This insight highlights the need for interpretation that recognizes heritage not as a closed story of past glory, but as an ongoing process where cultural meanings are constantly renegotiated. By foregrounding these dynamics—spanning prosperity, disappearance, and reinterpretation across time—interpretation can reveal how Hanyuan Hall’s significance is continuously made and remade. Such an approach enables users to understand heritage as a living, evolving process, thereby deepening their cultural awareness and fostering more critical engagement with lost architectural heritage.

4.4.
Proposed Design Features

Based on the design goals, we further propose two design features that will be implemented in the VR narrative experience design (Section
5
), which are expected to help promote cultural awareness and understanding for lost architectural heritage.

4.4.1.
DF1. Critical Narrative through Presence-Absence Dialectic

In response to DG1, our VR experience design employs a critical narrative framework grounded in the
presence-absence dialectic
, to vertically excavate the cultural meanings and connotations of this heritage. This feature holistically organizes fragmented and multifaceted information by interpreting what remains, what has been lost, and what is obscured. VR technology further enables the bidirectional transformation between
presence
and
absence
, inviting users to critically reflect on these transitions. Through this process, visitors can excavate deeper cultural connotations of the heritage, fostering a comprehensive understanding and cultural awareness.

4.4.2.
DF2. Embedding Presence and Absence into a Spatiotemporal Narrative.

As for DG2, our VR experience design integrates
presence
and
absence
into a spatiotemporal narrative. This design choice directly responds to the need to embrace the ever-changing dynamics of heritage narratives, identified in our formative study. By situating the hall’s meanings across different historical stages, the spatiotemporal narrative offers a concrete method to represent how cultural significance is constantly reinterpreted before and after the site’s disappearance. Unlike current official interpretations that focus narrowly on the Tang dynasty’s prosperity, this feature highlights the dynamic evolution of meanings across temporal and spatial layers—from imperial prominence, to decline and disappearance, to its re-emergence as a heritage park today. Through this broadened and dynamic framing, users can engage with heritage as a continuously evolving process, thereby enhancing their cultural awareness and understanding.

) to make them be perceived as "
presence
" by users in VR. Regarding this consideration, we applied X design methods. Firstly, we integrated the official 3D reconstruction model of Hanyuan Hall provided by the heritage administration office, which was reconstructed based on the historical records and surveying data collected by the archaeologists. Secondly, we used Tripo
1
1

1

https://www.tripo3d.ai
, a Generative AI (GenAI) tool, to generate 3D models of the historical figures (e.g., emperor, foreign envoy) to serve as NPCs in this VR experience. Moreover, we further used AI to synthesize their voices to represent as narratives in VR. In short, the absent historical figures were represented to come alive in the contemporary context. Thirdly, we also used Midjourney
2
2

2

https://www.midjourney.com
to generate images of several well-known past historical events before the destruction of Hanyuan Hall through text-to-image attempts. Fourthly, we also emphasized people’s narratives after the disappearance of Hanyuan Hall, which have sometimes been overlooked by the official narratives. We gathered different narrators from different time periods after its destruction to build a ’narrative competition’, where they expressed their own diverse interpretations of this heritage, which were sometimes uncertain and contradictory. By doing so, users can be aware of the hidden narratives, as well as those ongoing narratives that continuously evolve and reshape the cultural meanings of Hanyuan Hall after its disappearance. Through these practices, the VR experience renders
absence
perceptible as
presence
and, in doing so, draws users’ attention to the
absence
of the lost architectural heritage.

5.2.2.
Interpreting Presence

"Interpreting presence" addresses how the remaining traces of Hanyuan Hall—such as historical archives and the rammed-earth foundation platform—are interpreted in VR. We employed two design methods: (1) direct presentation of documentary materials (curated texts, narrated audio, photos) to articulate what still remains; and (2) drone aerial photography-based 3D reconstruction of the Hanyuan Hall’s rammed-earth foundation platform to produce an in-situ 3D model presented in VR. This 3D model—showing only the reconstructed foundation rather than a full architectural structure—clarifies the site’s current condition while inviting critical reflection and awareness of its absence.

5.2.3.
Augmenting Absence

To strengthen users’ perception of
absence
, we aim to make them aware of the historical fact that this heritage has disappeared, while also evoking a strong contrast between its past glory and present emptiness. To realize this, we applied point–cloud effects in two ways when rendering the VR environment: (1) the surrounding buildings of the Daming Palace (excluding Hanyuan Hall) were represented as golden point–cloud models, in contrast with the realistic 3D reconstruction of Hanyuan Hall. This contrast highlights that the other buildings have vanished, thereby enhancing the sense of
absence
; (2) the later scene of the site was rendered with silver point–cloud effects shaped as earth mounds, implying that the palace has completely disappeared and only the mounds remain, further reinforcing the perception of
absence
.

In summary, by embedding these methods into our narrative design, we aim to construct a holistic articulation of the
presence–absence dialectic
surrounding the lost architectural heritage. In particular, these design considerations allow users to critically experience how the
presence
and
absence
of heritage dynamically interact, transform, and evolve through the augmentation of VR technology. After representing and embedding these design elements into narratives, we then organized a spatiotemporal logic, which will be introduced in Section
5.3
.

6.
USER STUDY

To evaluate how
Pre/Absence
fosters cultural awareness of lost architectural heritage, we conducted a mixed-method user study approved by the first author’s institutional ethics committee.

6.1.
Participants

A total of 28 participants were recruited for our study via social media posts (16 females, 8 males; aged between 18 and 29 years old, M = 20.8, SD = 2.5). Participants were randomly assigned to an experimental group that would use the
Pre/Absence
VR system (referred to using the ’E’ signifier herein) or a control group that would read a
paper-based
introduction of the heritage (referred to using the ’C’ signifier herein). Participants were gifted a souvenir of Hanyuan Hall as a thank you for their participation in the one-hour-long experiment.

6.2.
Procedure and Methodology

The overall procedure of this user study contained three stages:
pre-test
,
stimuli
, and
post-test
. The detailed procedure is introduced as follows:

Pre-test.
After signing a consent form, participants completed a demographics survey. Then, those in the experimental group completed a 12-question knowledge test (pre-test) that was developed by the researchers to assess key information presented in each scene of the VR experience. This test was checked and approved by two expert scholars in the history and archaeological research of Hanyuan Hall. Both groups of participants then completed a Cultural Awareness Questionnaire (pre-test) that was based on Kamel’s questionnaire
(
Kamel 2019
)
but was adapted to the Hanyuan Hall of Daming Palace context.

Stimuli.
After completing the questionnaires, those in the control group were given a paper-based document consisting of historical information about the Hanyuan Hall, presented in the form of text and image. They were then given 15 minutes to read through it. While reading, participants were not permitted to ask any questions about the document or the heritage itself. Conversely, those in the experimental group were provided with a Meta Quest 3 headset to experience
Pre/Absence
. They were asked to explore the virtual environment, its narratives, and complete all the interaction tasks in the VR experience. As participants had different levels of familiarity with VR and headsets, we also provided assistance to those who needed help with the technology when needed. The length of each participant’s VR experience thus ranged from 20 to 33 minutes. What has to be specifically emphasized is that the intervention design method of the control (paper-based document reading) and experimental group (VR-based immersive experience) adopted in this study is in accordance with the experimental protocol design of
(
Thomann et al. 2024
)
.

Post-test.
After participants read the
paper-based document
or experienced
Pre/Absence
, they all completed a randomized version of the self-developed knowledge test (post-test) and then completed the cultural awareness questionnaire again (post-test). Additionally, the experimental group completed the 24-item version of Witmer and Singer’s Presence Questionnaire (PQ)
(
Witmer et al. 2005
)
. Finally, participants took part in a one-on-one semi-structured interview and were audio recorded for later transcription. The whole procedure is illustrated in Figure
10
. Specifically, the interview questions for the experimental group probed (1) overall VR experience, (2) preferences for certain VR design features, (3) perceptions of "
presence
" and "
absence
" features and the dialectics, (4) comparisons of understanding and cultural awareness before and after VR experience, (5) emotional responses throughout the VR experience, and (6) reflections on the broader issues of using VR to interpret lost architectural heritage. As for the control group, the interview focused on exploring (1) participants’ reading experience, (2) understanding and cultural awareness, and (3) attitudes towards paper-based learning of lost architectural heritage.

7.4.
Qualitative Findings

7.4.1.
Enhanced Cultural Understanding of Related Knowledge through the VR Experience

Participants reported that the VR experience enriched their knowledge and understanding of Hanyuan Hall. Compared with text-based learning in the control group, which often provided limited engagement (C4, C11), the immersive VR setting offered two distinctive advantages. First, it can integrate multi-modal resources-texts, audio, animations, images, archaeological archives, and 3D reconstructions—into coherent narratives (E5, E9, E13). This allowed participants to acquire not only factual knowledge but also contextualized insights across architecture, politics, literature, and daily life (E1, E3). For instance, E10 noted: "
I learned detailed stories and cultural meanings surrounding Daming Palace, from the Tang emperors to poets and foreign envoys, even trivial details like how envoys changed clothes here, which broadened my understanding
". E13 introduced that, "
those AI-generated images illustrate historical scenarios, accompanied by audio-based introductions. These images provided me with a more direct understanding-being able to see the scenario of historical events is easier for me to learn related knowledge and a deep impression
". Second, participants emphasized the interactive nature of VR, which actively engaged them and aroused interest (E2), in contrast to the dullness of reading static texts. This interactivity supported deeper exploration and sustained attention, resulting in more meaningful and comprehensive knowledge acquisition in the experimental group (E2, E6, E11).

Besides the affordances of VR in facilitating knowledge learning, the novel narrative design featured by spatiotemporal storytelling was also highlighted by the participants. They reported that the VR experience introduced multiple perspectives through differentiated roles’ narratives across different periods of time. This pluralistic narration disrupted the dominance of a single historical viewpoint, allowing participants to recognize lost architectural heritage as a carrier of diverse memories. For instance, E6 reflected: "
the VR narrative is far more pluralistic; in one moment I was in the Tang dynasty, in the next I could experience later generations’ nostalgia, which gave me a more layered understanding.
" Such perspectives transformed heritage from being a "
distant concept in textbooks
" into a "
vivid and multidimensional cultural phenomenon
" (E14). Moreover, E8 reflected that "
when I saw the palace burning down, I suddenly felt how fragile cultural heritage is—it can vanish in a moment, and that loss is irreversible
". This reflection functioned as entry points into cultural meaning, not just reactions to the scenarios presented in the VR experience. However, participants from control group introduced that they "
just learned various pieces of historical information in a linear flow
" (C7), "
the overall reading experience was flat
" (C10), and "
it seems that I only learned something new, but I did not dive into this history
" (C13). These insights show that the control group can merely learn relevant knowledge, rather than dig out the deeper connotations and meanings embedded in the history, as the experimental group did.

In sum, the VR experience enhanced cultural understanding by presenting lost architectural heritage as a layered, dynamic, and evolving site of knowledge. Participants moved from fragmented impressions to a holistic and deeply contextualized understanding, acquiring both detailed factual information and critical perspectives on historical complexity. This recalled the quantitative results in Section
7.1
and Section
7.2
that the knowledge questionnaire of the control group improved, but the enhancement in cultural awareness was far less than that of the experiment group.

7.4.2.
Cultural Awareness Development through Design Features in VR Experience

Participants developed deep cultural understanding to prompt their cultural awareness through multiple design features in the VR experience, which enabled them to both "feel into" the evolving history of Hanyuan Hall and critically reflect on its disappearance and cultural significance. Two prominent VR design features are introduced as follows.

Narratives of presence-absence dialectic (
DF1
) prompted participants’ cultural awareness.
Participants highlighted that the following two aspects of the critical narratives shaped by presence-absence dialectics enhanced their cultural awareness.

Firstly, participants engaged in multi-NPC narratives, especially in Scene 4, where multiple NPCs from different backgrounds and historical periods after the Tang dynasty were brought together to share their own narratives of Hanyuan Hall. Beyond diversifying perspectives of knowledge learning, as noted in Section
7.4.1
, this design made participants realize that “
even after its loss, its narratives still exist till now and undergo diverse changes which continuously give re-interpretations to lost architectural heritage
” (E8). Some participants also explicitly reflected on the design philosophy. For instance, E9 observed: “
It seems like they are arguing about the uncertain memory of Hanyuan Hall, and I noticed the conflicts emerging from different NPCs’ narratives.
” E13 further remarked: “
It is such ’narrative competition’—consisting of speculation and imagination—that re-defines the critical issues of the lost Hanyuan Hall: what it should have been in the past and what it should mean in the present.
”

Based on this deeper understanding of the narrative design, participants articulated how their cultural awareness improved. For example, E4 noted “
I realized that heritage is not a single truth but an ongoing dialogue, it is a process of meaning-making
”. This demonstrated that participants can be aware that heritage’s meaning lies not only in recovering what has been lost, but also in continuously questioning and reinterpreting it in the present. E10 also discussed her awareness, "
I think currently the official interpretations of Hanyuan Hall provided by the authority are mainly about its past resplendence, which neglect or even erase other narratives
". Thus, this insight show that the participant recognized the narrative approach of this VR experience re-shaped cultural awareness regarding the complex narratives embedded in a lost architectural heritage-especially how the authority’s official narratives dominant mainstream heritage interpretations in contemporary settings (
presence
) and how can we critically perceive those hidden, darkened or erased narratives (
absence
).

Spatiotemporal narrative embedded with the presence-absence (
DF2
) prompted participants’ cultural awareness.

Secondly, participants situated themselves in a spatiotemporal narrative to develop cultural awareness. The VR experience design layered historical content across time, so that participants navigated scenes from different eras and saw historical archives (e.g., poems, paintings) spanning multiple dynasties. The temporal layering helped them perceive the site as evolving rather than a static relic. For example, E1 said he "
experienced its historical stories and cultural depth from ancient times to the present
", noting how the VR traced events from early Tang through later periods. Similarly, another observed that VR "
broke the wall of time
"(E3), making the lost Hanyuan Hall no longer a "past" thing in books but "a place and history we can walk into". These remarks show that encountering transitions across eras – from Tang emperor rituals to later court scenes – brought the heritage to life as a living continuum. The spatial layering demonstrated how the functions of the location of Hanyuan Hall changed over time-from royal palace to ruins, village, and heritage park. These spatial changes strengthened participants’ critical understanding of how power deconstructed and other forces re-emerged at the same location, which fosters participants’ cultural awareness of the rich history carried by space. By framing heritage as a dynamic narrative of change, participants shifted from seeing the palace as a distant, frozen concept to appreciating it as part of an ongoing temporal flow. This temporal sensibility deepened their cultural awareness: they understood historical sites not just as remnants of the past, but as evolving entities whose stories unfold across spatial and temporal scales.

Embodied interactions connected participants with lost architectural heritage to foster cultural awareness.
Embodied interactions in the VR experience strengthened presence, immersion, and engagement, thereby bridging participants’ connections with lost architectural heritage and fostering awareness.

On the one hand, multi-sensory embodiment enabled participants to immerse themselves in cultural discovery and reflection. The 3D reconstruction and environmental design created contrasts between realism and abstraction that invited critical perception. As E5 noted: “
Upon entering the VR environment, especially Scenes 3 and 4, I was amazed by the atmosphere. The combination of the reconstructed hall and surrounding point-cloud effects gave me two contrasting senses: realism and illusion. These contrasts made me reflect on the hall’s disappearance. Finally, I strongly recognize its significance in architectural scale and construction techniques, and am aware that a lost architectural heritage is not only shaped by its loss, but also by its recurrence.
” This shows how concrete visualization (
presence
) and point-cloud abstraction (
absence
) effectively aroused participants’ cultural awareness through the
presence–absence
dialectic.

AI-generated voices further deepened immersion by linking participants to historical figures (E3, E7). E4 remarked: “
It seems that I’ve traveled back in time, and hearing their voices made me suddenly realize that this lost architectural heritage is alive.
” E11 added: “
Their stories made me more immersed, and made me more willing to talk with them to learn more information.
” Notably, some participants also questioned the authenticity of AI-generated voices, recognizing that both the figures and the heritage are irretrievably lost; this productive uncertainty sharpened critical literacy about
what
digital heritage can represent and
what
it inevitably leaves out. Finally, animations—especially the palace fire—were highlighted as impactful (N=14), evoking negative emotions that foregrounded the fragility of heritage and how ever-changing politics disrupts cultural continuity. Rather than merely depicting the past, these animations catalyzed emotional awareness and confrontation with the impermanence of heritage.

On the other hand, a specific interaction task from a first-person perspective also reinforced cultural awareness. Most participants (N=12) strongly expressed their feelings at the end of the VR experience, when the archaeologist NPC invited them to pick up an ink brush and leave strokes on a scroll, symbolizing the passing of the responsibility of heritage preservation across generations. Interviews indicated that this participatory act, paired with haptic feedback, translated immersion into agency—prompting reflection on one’s role and strengthening determination to act as a cultural ambassador for preserving and representing this lost architectural heritage.

Generally, multi-sensory plausibility (to
feel present
) and interpretive ambiguity (to
recognize absence
) moved participants from recognition to reflection, while symbolic enactment (the ink-brush relay) converted felt proximity into role-based responsibility. These embodied mechanisms thus advance cultural awareness across cognitive, emotional, and behavior-oriented layers (analyzed in the following Section
7.4.3
).

As for the control group, though participants also reported that they got richer cultural awareness of Hanyuan Hall after reading the document (N=11), the factors that influence their awareness enhancement are different from the experimental group. Most of them mainly thought that, as they had little knowledge and awareness about it before, but this document provided detailed introductions for them, which helped them suddenly notice its cultural significance and historical value. However, compared with the experimental group, they did not perceive and understand the deeper connotations of the information as the experimental group did. This caused the extent of their cultural awareness improvement to be lower and remains at a surface level, which demonstrates the strong advantages of using VR experience to prompt cultural awareness for lost architectural heritage. This finding also corresponds to Section
7.2
that the participants in the experimental group showed a significantly greater improvement in cultural awareness compared to those in the control group.

7.4.3.
Participants’ Multi-dimensional Enhancement of Cultural Awareness through the VR Experience

Interview results reveal that participants’ cultural awareness was enhanced along these dimensions - cognitive, emotional, and behavioral aspects, in accordance with the theoretical foundation mentioned in Section
2.3
.

Cognitive Dimension.
Across the experimental group, participants consistently described how the VR experience expanded their cognitive dimension of cultural awareness, shifting them from
passively receiving historical facts
to
actively constructing meaning
. They no longer viewed heritage as a fixed artifact, but as a phenomenon that extends across spatiotemporal and socio-political contexts. For example, E5 articulated a broadened spatiotemporal perspective: “
In the past, I thought the story of this heritage only stayed in the Tang dynasty. Now I know even after its disappearance, its story still exists, and its functions also shift across eras.
” Similarly, E8 reflected on political meanings embedded in architecture: “
I understood how its scale encoded hierarchy and authority, and how historical figures underwent different destinies under royal regulation and politics.
”

Moreover, participants also engaged in value recognition. Several emphasized that the very disappearance of this heritage heightened their awareness of its significance. E3 highlighted continuity of cultural memory—“
it carries and reshapes cultural memory across generations though it has disappeared since the Tang dynasty
”—while E10 stressed critical reflection—“
its absence drives me to reflect on the reasons why we lose it.
” Notably, ten participants went further to question and critique both heritage and its representation, such as E1: “
VR makes its reconstruction very complete, which makes me wonder if everything has really been figured out… it seems that the virtual reconstruction has reduced its mystery and uncertainty.
” These findings suggest that cultural awareness enhancement in the cognitive domain is not monolithic, but layered: starting from
knowledge acquisition
, extending to
recognition of cultural value
, and deepening into
critical reflection
. By contrast, control group participants primarily reported superficial knowledge gains, describing text-based learning as “
a brief overview of history
” (C10) or input that “
cannot make a deep impression
” (C13).

Emotional Dimension
Participants also reported heightened cultural awareness in the
emotional dimension
. Their accounts revealed a trajectory of emotional responses ranging from
astonishment
to
sorrow
,
respect
, and
empathy
, reflecting increasingly complex emotional engagement with lost architectural heritage. Several expressed shock and awe at the architectural grandeur (E7: “
the sense of grandeur shocked me
”; E11: “
I was amazed by its magnificence
”). This initial astonishment often transformed into
sorrow
upon recognizing the heritage’s destruction (E4: “
It is so grand, and then it was destroyed by fire… I was touched
”; E1: “
I was shocked… but deeply distressed to see its current state
”). E8 reflected that "
when I saw the palace burning down, I suddenly felt how fragile cultural heritage is—it can vanish in a moment, and that loss is irreversible
". These emotions functioned as entry points into cultural meaning, not just reactions to the scenarios presented in the VR experience. Alongside these emotions, participants articulated
respect
for the artisans who built the site and for the historical continuity it symbolized (E5: “
this VR experience made me respectful for the workers who constructed this palace, as well as the history it embodies
”).

Importantly, many participants described developing
empathy
, a higher-level emotional engagement that connected them with both the heritage and the people associated with it. For instance, E3 stressed empathy for the traces of history itself: “
the greatest sense of authenticity in a fragmented history stems from respect and empathy for the details of historical traces.
” E11 connected empathy to human destinies: “
I notice this political place witnessed so many people’s destinies… I had empathy for all of them.
” Others, like E14, drew on personal experience, comparing a prior on-site visit with the VR narrative: “
I felt deeply sad and pitiful… it contained so many narratives, but contemporary practices failed to convey them.
” In contrast, control group participants characterized text-based learning as emotionally flat, often labeling it
boring
(C3) or
lacking immersion and presence
(C9).

Behavioral Dimension.
Finally, cultural awareness was enhanced in the
behavioral aspect
, where participants articulated stronger intentions to act upon their recognition of lost architectural heritage. Their accounts revealed four layers of action orientation. First, several emphasized
personal-level actions
, such as sharing the experience with peers or revisiting heritage sites (E1: “
I am more willing to bring my friends to visit this heritage park
”). Others highlighted
community-level responsibilities
, expressing determination to protect and disseminate heritage (E3: “
I am determined to contribute my efforts to protect and propagandize this site
”; E7: “
we should actively promote this heritage
”). The third cluster focused on
intergenerational transmission
, stressing the importance of education (E6: “
it is vital to educate future generations on the importance of this heritage
”; E12: “
we should ensure these stories are passed on
”). Finally, some participants reflected on this VR experience and extended their intentions towards
technological innovation
, envisioning future applications to broaden heritage engagement (E14: “
we should expand the technological applications in this context
”).

Crucially, participants often linked these behavioral intentions to their emotional engagement, especially empathy. As E12 reflected, “
this narrative experience arouses my emotional resonance, motivating me to wish that these stories can be known by more audiences.
” This indicates that
affective awareness
acted as a bridge toward
behavioral awareness
, where emotions of empathy and respect catalyzed a sense of responsibility and action. In contrast, participants in the control group seldom articulated comparable intentions, underscoring the unique role of VR experiences in translating cultural awareness from
knowing and feeling
into
being motivated and taking actions
.

To sum up, these findings reveal that participants’ cultural awareness was enhanced in the aforementioned three dimensions. Rather than isolated outcomes, these dimensions formed a layered progression, where cognitive understanding laid the foundation, emotional resonance deepened cultural connection, and behavioral intentions translated awareness into prospective action. This multi-dimensional enhancement demonstrates how cultural awareness evolves as a holistic process encompassing thought, feeling, identity, and practice.

8.
DISCUSSION

Our work introduces the design, implementation, and evaluation of a critical VR narrative experience,
Pre/Absence
, which aims to weave the
presence-absence dialectic
of the lost architectural heritage into a spatiotemporal narrative. The empirical findings revealed that the VR narrative experience can yield significantly higher promotion of cultural awareness, compared with paper-based information learning. This research contributes to the HCI field and heritage studies field in (1) grounding VR narratives of lost architectural heritage in
presence-absence dialectic
and spatiotemporal frame, and (2) offering empirical evidence into the effects of such a critical VR narrative experience on users’ cultural awareness and understanding for lost architectural heritage. Based on our design practice and findings of this research, we discuss from the following four aspects: (1) narrative design philosophy (Section
8.1
), (2) results (Section
8.2
), (3) design implications (Section
8.3
), and (4) limitations and future work (Section
8.4
).

8.1.
Reflecting on the Role of
Presence-Absence Dialectic
in Critical Heritage Narrative Design in HCI

In this paper, we introduce
presence-absence dialectic
as a theoretical foundation to guide a critical narrative of lost architectural heritage in VR. From our user study, it can be concluded that the participants can perceive its design philosophy and expressed that such narratives can help prompt cultural awareness and understanding. To this end, we reflect on the role of this narrative design concept within the HCI and heritage studies field.

The
presence–absence dialectic
highlights that
absence
is not merely a void but an active factor that shapes perception and meaning
(
Meier et al. 2013b
;
Aykaç 2023
;
Ishii et al. 2025
)
. Our findings suggest that the interplay between
presence
(e.g., reconstructions, historical archives) and
absence
(e.g., hidden narratives) in VR can emotionally sensitize users to heritage’s fragility and motivate deeper engagement. This supports calls in heritage studies to “
make absence present
” as a means to foster critical reflection rather than merely filling gaps with reconstructions
(
Ishii et al. 2025
;
Dogan and Kan 2020
)
. For HCI, this implies that immersive experience design should not only aim for photorealistic fidelity but also deliberately design for moments of incompleteness, void, and ambiguity to provoke (re) interpretations and imaginations.

Our findings also show that the
presence–absence dialectic
can be used to broaden the space of lost architectural heritage narratives. By bringing together cross-dynasty accounts, local memories, and official records, we aimed to show how the meaning of Hanyuan Hall has been reinterpreted over time, both before and after its disappearance. Yet, as critical heritage scholars remind us, diversity on the surface is not enough: it is necessary to ask whose voices are included and whose are still missing
(
Macdonald 2015
)
. In response, future HCI studies should actively bring in the local community’s memories—often shared orally or sidelined by official discourse—and treat them as central parts of the narratives. Doing so requires participatory co-creation with community members to avoid reproducing a single, "authorized" version of the past
(
Li et al. 2025
;
He et al. 2025
)
.

The use of the
presence–absence dialectic
also raises important ethical questions. Our VR design did succeed in evoking emotions such as awe and regret, yet prior research reminds us that directing users’ emotions too strongly or presenting history through a single emotional lens can oversimplify complex realities
(
Smith and Campbell 2015
;
Waterton and Watson 2013
)
. Designers need to find a balance between revealing difficult pasts and avoiding retraumatization or emotional manipulation. In practice, this means giving users control over how deeply they engage with sensitive material, presenting distressing content with care, and offering multiple emotional entry points so that people from different backgrounds can process the experience in ways that feel appropriate to them. Paying attention to these ethical aspects allows
presence–absence
to function not just as a design aesthetic but as a foundation for responsible, inclusive heritage interpretation.

8.2.
Mechanisms of Building Cultural Awareness and Understanding through Dialectical Narrative Engagement

Our empirical findings showed that participants’ cultural awareness and understanding were not simply the outcome of receiving more information input in VR, but rather emerged through their embodied engagement with the dialectical interplay of
presence
and
absence
in this immersive narrative experience. When users shifted between scenes that made tangible traces visible (e.g., the reconstructed foundations, archival images) and those that foregrounded absence (e.g., the fire animation, fragmented cross-dynasty voices), they reported both heightened emotional experiences and deeper reflections. This pattern resonates with prior absence-heritage theory
(
James-Williamson et al. 2024
)
, which highlights how "making absence present" can foster cultural awareness, resilience, and critical interpretation of heritage beyond basic factual recall. Building on these, we summarized the following mechanisms of creating cultural awareness and understanding through dialectical narrative engagement.

Stimulating empathy with the lost architectural heritage.
From the user study, we noticed that scenes of the VR experience that represented the loss of Hanyuan Hall—especially the fire animation—elicited regret, sadness, and even indignation, sensitizing participants to the vulnerability of heritage and drawing attention to the socio-political contingencies behind disappearance (E8’s refections in Section
7.4.3
). Such emotional triggers are consistent with prior work showing that "
presence of absence
" can prompt cultural awareness and critical reflection on heritage erasure and its causes
(
James-Williamson et al. 2024
;
Christopher et al. 2014
;
Ozcelik and Paprika 2010
)
.

Enabling Cognitive (Re) interpretations Across Time.
In
Pre/Absence
, we juxtaposed plural, sometimes contradictory narratives of different people across eras, and integrated different heritage elements (e.g.,
presence
and
absence
) into a spatiotemporal frame. During the user study, we noticed that some participants began to question linear, single-sided accounts and to recognize that meanings continue to be re-interpreted and re-shaped even after heritage’s disappearance. Participants also explicitly described a broadened spatiotemporal view—shifting from a Tang-only story to an understanding that the hall’s functions and meanings evolve within the ever-changing dynamics. This echoes absence-heritage scholarship that treats absence as temporally open—extending into the present and potential futures—and urges audiences to revisit and contest "authorized" narratives
(
James-Williamson et al. 2024
;
Harvey 2001
;
Meier et al. 2013b
)
.

Prompting Co-construction of Cultural Meanings.
In
Pre/Absence
, we found that during the closing ritual in Scene 5 (signing the heritage scroll with the virtual ink brush) translated immersion into agency: participants reflected on their own role in transmitting heritage forward and articulated an intention to take actions (e.g., positioning individuals as "cultural ambassadors"). This participatory turn operationalizes "making absence present" as a shared task rather than a passive viewing, resonating with the design philosophy of this interaction task in Scene 5 that foregrounds user participation in meaning-making for cultural heritage.

8.3.
Design Implications

Based on the design practice and findings derived from this research, we propose the following two design implications for future HCI practices in interpreting lost architectural heritage in VR.

8.3.1.
Design for Dialectical Engagement Rather Than Static Heritage Interpretations

Our results suggest that participants became more culturally aware when they experienced shifts between
presence
(e.g., reconstructions, historical archives) and
absence
(e.g., hidden and contradictory narratives). This indicates that VR narrative experience of lost architectural heritage should go beyond full reconstruction that presents the past as a single, fixed story. Instead, designers can create moments that let users encounter gaps, loss, and silence. Approaches such as partial reconstructions, point-cloud effects, or smooth transitions between complete and incomplete states can help users sense the fragility of heritage and reflect on its evolving cultural meaning. In this way, the VR experience moves from simply displaying history to actively involving users in reflecting on and (re) interpreting it.

8.3.2.
Design for Temporal and Narrative Plurality to Reveal Ever-Changing Dynamics

Participants noted that layering time periods and including multiple narrators helped them see Hanyuan Hall as something still being reinterpreted rather than a static lost architectural heritage. This points to the value of using multi-faceted, plural narratives in VR that place the heritage within its changing cultural, social and political contexts. Therefore, designers can weave in voices from different dynasties and communities—including those that are usually overlooked—so that users can sense how meanings shift over time. Such plurality allows future systems to move beyond linear, celebratory accounts and show heritage as something living and evolving. In turn, this approach not only deepens understanding but also invites users to reflect critically on how official and alternative narratives overlap, conflict, and shape cultural memory to prompt cultural awareness.

8.4.
Limitations and Future Work

This research also has several limitations that can inform improvements in future work. First, our sample of the user study (N=28) was small and homogeneous, mainly university students, limiting the generalizability of the participants. In the future, subsequent studies will include more historians during the evaluation stage. Secondly, the evaluation focused only on immediate responses, without tracking long-term enhancement of cultural awareness. Therefore, a longitudinal study should be conducted in the future. Thirdly, the design quality should be improved, especially the animations of the NPCs and the fluency of the whole VR experience.

9.
CONCLUSION

This paper presents
Pre/Absence
, a critical VR narrative experience grounded in the
presence–absence dialectic
to interpret lost architectural heritage through spatiotemporal narratives. Taking Hanyuan Hall of Daming Palace as a design case, we conducted a formative study with archaeologists, administrators, and visitors to identify two challenges: the reduction of layered meanings to mere facts and the neglect of heritage’s evolving dynamics. Guided by these insights, we set two design goals and implemented VR features that vertically excavate cultural meanings and horizontally embrace dynamic reinterpretations. A mixed-method experiment with 28 participants showed that, compared with paper-based interpretation,
Pre/Absence
significantly improved cultural awareness, reflective engagement, and critical understanding. Our work contributes to HCI by introducing the
presence–absence dialectic
as a lens for immersive interpretation of lost heritage and providing empirical evidence that such narratives deepen users’ cultural understanding and foster critical reflection.
