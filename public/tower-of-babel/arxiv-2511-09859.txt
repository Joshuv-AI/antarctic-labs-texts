Learning of Statistical Field Theories
Shreya Shukla, Abhijith Jayakumar, Andrey Y. Lokhov

Abstract
Recovering microscopic couplings directly from data provides a route to solving the inverse problem in statistical field theories, one that complements the traditional—often computationally intractable—forward approach of predicting observables from an action or Hamiltonian. Here, we propose an approach for the inverse problem that uniformly accommodates systems with discrete, continuous, and hybrid variables. We demonstrate accurate parameter recovery in several benchmark systems—including Wegner’s Ising gauge theory, ϕ 4 \phi^{4} theory, Schwinger and Sine-Gordon models, and mixed spin–gauge systems, and show how iterating the procedure under coarse-graining reconstructs full non-perturbative renormalization-group flows. This gives direct access to phase boundaries, fixed points, and emergent interactions without relying on perturbation theory. We also address a realistic setting where full gauge configurations may be unavailable, and reformulate learning algorithms for multiple field theories so that they are recovered directly using observables such as correlations from scattering data or quantum simulators. We anticipate that our methodology will find widespread use in practical learning of field theories in strongly coupled regimes where analytical tools might fail.

The implications of this work extend beyond technical advances. For experimentalists, our methods provide a direct route from correlations measured at different energy scales to underlying field theories, bypassing the need for full microscopic characterization. For theorists, the ability to learn non-perturbative renormalization group flows opens new windows into our understanding of strongly-coupled theories. Our methods also provide a path to infer low-energy effective field theories given experimental data. Discrete Models

I.1
1D Ising Model

We begin with the 1D Ising model to establish our methodology. The Hamiltonian takes the familiar form

\displaystyle H=-J\sum_{i=1}^{L}s_{i}s_{i+1}-h\sum_{i=1}^{L}s_{i}\;,

where

are spin variables,

50

J
is typically inferred from magnetic susceptibility measurements, while the field

corresponds to applied magnetic fields measurable in magnetometry experiments.

For discrete variables, we employ pseudolikelihood estimation

A.1

Unlike the full likelihood, the computational tractability comes from the fact that conditionals are one-dimensional distributions which can be explicitly written down including their normalization. The resulting is still exact and consistent

N^{-1/2}
scaling as the amount of data increases. The robust performance across this range establishes pseudolikelihood as a reliable tool for discrete systems, setting the stage for our RG analysis.

RG Analysis.
To extract RG flows, we implement Kadanoff’s block-spin transformation

Learning 1D Ising Model from correlations.
A key insight enables learning even when only statistical moments are available rather than full configurations. For the homogeneous (
J_{ij}=J
for all

I.2
2D Wegner’s Ising Gauge Theory

For this case, we work with the action:

\displaystyle H=-K\sum_{\square}\prod_{\ell\in\square}\sigma_{\ell}\;,

where
\sigma_{\ell}\in\{\pm 1\}
are binary variables or gauge fields living on lattice links, and the product runs around each elementary plaquette
\square
. This model, first introduced by Wegner
53
, provides one of the simplest playgrounds for lattice gauge theory.

A key observable in gauge theories is the expectation value of the Wilson loop
\langle W(C)\rangle
, where
W(C)
is defined as the trace of the product of link variables around a closed contour
C
. Its expectation value encodes how charges interact: if it decays with area of the loop, the theory exhibits confinement: the potential barrier between two test charges increases linearly with distance. The proportionality constant in this exponential area law is called the string tension
\sigma
.

The 2D classical Wegner’s Ising gauge theory is technically trivial: all plaquettes fluctuate independently and the system connects smoothly to the confined
K=0
phase. However, it remains an ideal testbed for our methods because the behavior of Wilson loops and string tension is analytically tractable and directly illustrates confinement.

RG Analysis.
Our real–space coarse graining maps the product of links on a larger plaquette to a single renormalized link in each direction for a smaller plaquette (Fig.
2(b)
), yielding an effective coupling
K_{n}
after every
n
th step. The exact Migdal–Kadanoff prediction for a
2\times 2
block is
27
;
37

K_{n+1}\;={\tanh}^{-1}(\tanh^{2}K_{n})
, and this theoretical recursion (dashed line in Fig.
2(b)
) matches our learned flow, which drives
K
toward the trivial fixed point (at zero) in the 2D
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
gauge theory. In Appendix
F
demonstrate that the RG-scheme of multiplying coarse-links to form a fine link is gauge-covariant and local. We also introduce other real-space RG schemes that preserve gauge invariance, and show convergence of
K
to the trivial fixed point.

Crucially, for the same initial coupling
K_{0}=0.45
, we extract the string tension
\sigma
directly from Wilson–loop expectation values, independently of
K
. For a rectangular loop
C
with area
A(C)
, we fit
\langle W(C)\rangle=e^{-\sigma A(C)}
to obtain
\sigma
from the data. We then compare our results with the analytic prediction
\sigma(K_{n})=-\ln\!\bigl(\tanh K_{n}\bigr)

53
;
29

2(c)
). Since larger values of
\sigma
correspond to stronger confinement, the growth of
\sigma
under coarse graining in the 2D
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
gauge theory provides a clear demonstration of how the strength of confinement increases along the RG flow.

II
Continuous Models

II.1
2D
\phi^{4}
Model

The
\phi^{4}
model serves as our first example of continuum field theory. This is also the simplest model used to demonstrate continuous phase transitions, and describes everything from the liquid-gas critical point to the Higgs mechanism in particle physics
43
. On a 2D square lattice with unit spacing, the Euclidean Hamiltonian reads

\displaystyle H=\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mu=1}^{2}(\phi_{\mathbf{x}}-\phi_{\mathbf{x}+\hat{\mu}})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}m^{2}\phi_{\mathbf{x}}^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{4}\phi_{\mathbf{x}}^{4}\right],

where
\mathbf{x}=(x,\tau)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
\phi_{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}
is a scalar field,
m^{2}
is the mass parameter (which can be negative, signaling instability of the symmetric vacuum), and
\lambda>0
ensures stability of the potential at large field values.

We use the Advanced HMC package
57
to generate samples. For continuous fields, we use score matching to learn parameters
21
. Score matching works by matching the score of the data (i.e the gradient of the energy function) to score of the model. For distributions over continuous variables in the Gibbs form, this is an effective way to fit models to statistical data while avoiding partition function computations. More details about score matching can be found in Appendix
A.2
.

Our results demonstrate accurate parameter recovery across a wide range of couplings. As shown in Fig.
, we successfully learn
\lambda
values spanning an order of magnitude.

RG Analysis and Phase Boundary.
The
\phi^{4}
theory exhibits a rich phase structure that our method captures directly from data. The system undergoes a transition between a symmetric phase where
\langle\phi\rangle=0
and a broken-symmetry phase where the field acquires a non-zero expectation value. Traditional approaches locate this transition through mean-field theory or perturbative RG, but our data-driven method maps the phase boundary without such approximations.

For coarse-graining, we take the median value of
\phi
on each block and then rescale the resulting configuration so that its total kinetic energy matches that of the original system.

Fig.
3a
reveals the phase structure in detail. These results are consistent with prior determinations of the critical line
33
. Deep in the broken phase where
m^{2}\ll-\lambda
, both couplings flow toward zero while maintaining
m^{2}<0
, indicating the system flows to a trivial theory. In the symmetric phase, both
\lambda
and
m^{2}
increase under RG, reflecting the growing stability of the symmetric vacuum as we probe larger scales. Close to the phase boundary, the flow nearly stalls —
m^{2}
barely shifts across RG steps, showing that the system is near a critical point.

Learning
\phi^{4}
theory from correlations.
A key insight is that the score matching objective can be expressed entirely in terms of statistical moments of the field configurations. One may work with full samples, but in many experimental/simulation contexts, the only available data is correlations. In
A.2.5
, we show the moment-based score matching objective in terms of correlations involving upto sextic terms
\langle\phi^{6}\rangle
and terms involving nearest neighbors. Fig.
5(b)
shows that the errors for parameters
\lambda
and
m^{2}
scale as
N^{-1/2}
, confirming that score-based learning from correlations achieves the statistical efficiency of direct configuration-based methods without requiring access to the full field data.

However, we note that this approach works best when couplings enter linearly in the Hamiltonian. For nonlinear interactions (such as when
the parameters appear inside trigonometric functions), the objective cannot be written purely in
terms of low-order moments. In such cases, one must use full field configurations rather than correlation data alone.

Sample Complexity Analysis.
From Figs.
6(a)
and
6(b)
one can see that the learning complexity grows exponentially and polynomially respectively, with coupling strength, following

This is in sharp contrast to the complexity of computing observables from a given model (the forward direction in Figure

III
Mixed Model

III.1
A hybrid
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
–U(1) Theory

Real physical systems often involve multiple types of degrees of freedom involving complex interactions. To demonstrate our framework’s versatility, we study a model combining discrete Ising spins with continuous U(1) gauge fields. While not gauge-invariant, this model is inspired by analogous gauge-Higgs systems that have been extensively studied and exhibit rich phase diagrams
15
.

The Hamiltonian couples spins on sites to gauge fields on links:

\displaystyle H=-K\sum_{x,\mu,\nu}

\displaystyle U_{\mu}(x)U_{\nu}(x+e^{\mu})U^{\dagger}_{\mu}(x+e^{\nu})U^{\dagger}_{\nu}(x)

\displaystyle-\beta\sum_{x,\mu}\sigma(x)\text{Re}{(U_{\mu}(x))}\sigma(x+e^{\mu})\;,

where
\sigma\in\{-1,+1\}
are Ising variables,
U_{\mu}\in\text{U}(1)
are link variables,
e^{\mu}
are unit vectors in direction
\mu
and the first sum runs over elementary plaquettes. The plaquette term describes gauge dynamics, while the second term represents matter-gauge coupling.

The mixed alphabet necessitates a hybrid learning strategy that combines our discrete and continuous techniques. We first learn
\beta
using pseudolikelihood on the spin degrees of freedom, treating the gauge fields as fixed external fields. The conditional probability
P(\sigma_{x}|\{\sigma_{y},U\})\propto\exp[\beta\sigma_{x}\sum_{y\sim x}\text{Re}[U]\sigma_{y}]
is the only term with spins
\sigma
, allowing its isolation. With
\beta
determined, we then apply score matching to learn the gauge coupling
K
from the continuous link variables.

This sequential scheme recovers both parameters with the expected
N^{-1/2}
error scaling. The success of this hybrid approach suggests a general strategy for complex systems: decompose the learning problem according to the structure of degrees of freedom, then combine specialized techniques for each component. The same strategy readily generalizes to gauge–Higgs systems, fermion–boson mixtures, and other composite theories across condensed
-
matter and high
-
energy contexts.

RG Analysis.
To coarse-grain the data from this model, we use our standard procedure: we map the product of links to a link in every direction and use majority rule for spins on sites. The sample

complexity curve in Fig.

\beta_{c}\simeq 1.9
, signalling criticality. Consistently, the one–step flow in the

switches from decreasing to increasing under coarse
-
graining—i.e. the
\beta
-
function for

changes sign, confirming the presence of a non
-

For fixed
K=0.1

0.01

Conclusions and Outlook

In this work, we put forward the idea that learning of field theories provides an alternative to forward-simulation approaches that become challenging or intractable at strong coupling. We have shown that it is possible to learn couplings and RG flows directly from data in a range of statistical field theories. Using pseudolikelihood for discrete systems, score matching for continuous ones, and a combination of estimators for hybrid models, we obtained accurate parameter estimates and reconstructed RG flows that agree with known analytical and numerical results, and capture non-perturbative behavior in the cases where analytical results are not known. The ability to formulate these estimators in terms of correlation functions in several relevant discrete and continuous theories makes the methods suitable even when only partial statistics are available, which is often the case with the available experimental data.

Looking ahead, there are several directions that are naturally open to exploration. An immediate path forward involves expanding our methods to fundamental and effective field theories of strong interactions, as well as testing estimators developed in this work directly on experimental data, for example neutron scattering measurements or quantum simulator outputs. In our numerical experiments, the choice of coarse-graining steps were model-specific rather than graph-agnostic; is it possible to design a data-driven approach that would discover the coarse-graining automatically or as a part of learning procedure? A longer-term goal would be to leverage recent theoretical progress in learning from non-equilibrium
6
;
12
;
16
and metastable
22
states to extend the framework to non-equilibrium dynamics, which would open the door to studying driven systems and time-dependent effective theories.

Introduced by Besag
4
, pseudolikelihood provides a computationally tractable alternative to maximum likelihood estimation for discrete statistical models where the partition function is intractable. The key insight is to replace the joint likelihood with a product of conditional likelihoods, each of which can be computed exactly without knowledge of the global normalization.

A.1.1
Mathematical Formulation

Consider a discrete statistical model with joint probability distribution

\displaystyle P(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{1}{Z(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\exp\left(-H(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})\right),

(1)

where
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{N})
represents the configuration of discrete variables,
H(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})
is the energy function parameterized by
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, and
Z(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\exp(-H(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}))
is the intractable partition function. The pseudolikelihood replaces the joint distribution with the product of conditional distributions:

\displaystyle PL(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\prod_{i=1}^{M}P(x_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{\setminus i},\boldsymbol{\theta}),

(2)

where
\mathbf{x}_{\setminus i}
denotes all variables except
x_{i}
. The crucial advantage is that each conditional probability can be computed exactly:

\displaystyle P(x_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{\setminus i},\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{\exp(-H(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}))}{\sum_{x_{i}^{\prime}}\exp(-H(\mathbf{x}_{i\to x_{i}^{\prime}};\boldsymbol{\theta}))},

(3)

where
\mathbf{x}_{i\to x_{i}^{\prime}}
denotes the configuration
\mathbf{x}
with
x_{i}
replaced by
x_{i}^{\prime}
. The denominator involves summation only over the possible values of
x_{i}
, making it computationally feasible.

A.1.2
Pseudolikelihood Estimator

The pseudolikelihood estimator maximizes the log-pseudolikelihood:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{PL}=\arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\sum_{i=j}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\log P(x_{i}^{(t)}|\mathbf{x}_{\setminus i}^{(t)},\boldsymbol{\theta}),

(4)

where
\{\mathbf{x}^{(t)}\}_{j=1}^{N}
represents
N
independent samples from the true distribution. This optimization can be performed efficiently using gradient-based methods. As
N\to\infty
(number of samples),
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{PL}\to\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{true}}
. The error scaling is typically
O((N)^{-1/2})
, which we observe empirically in our results.

A.1.3
Application to Ising Models

For the Ising model with Hamiltonian
H=-J\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}s_{i}s_{j}-h\sum_{i}s_{i}
, the conditional probability becomes:

\displaystyle P(s_{i}|\mathbf{s}_{\setminus i})=\frac{\exp\left(s_{i}\left(h+J\sum_{j\sim i}s_{j}\right)\right)}{2\cosh\left(h+J\sum_{j\sim i}s_{j}\right)},

(5)

where the sum runs over neighbors of site
i
. This leads to the negative log-pseudolikelihood objective used throughout our discrete model analysis.

A.1.4
Moment-Based Pseudolikelihood Estimator

We utilize a key property of this likelihood estimator: for several discrete models, the objective can be expressed in terms of statistical moments of data. In particular, for the
d
-dimensional classical Ising Model we show in Appendices
D
and
E
that the pseudolikelihood estimator is expressible entirely in terms of moments up to order dependent on the dimension
d
of the model. This moment-based formulation enables parameter learning for experimental data, where only moments and correlations are available, as opposed to full samples.

A.2
Score Matching

Score matching, introduced by Hyvärinen
21
, provides an elegant method for parameter estimation in continuous probability models and bypasses the need for computation of the partition function. The method is based on matching the score function (gradient of log-density) between the model and data distributions.

A.2.1
Mathematical Formulation

Consider a continuous probability model with density:

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{1}{Z(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\exp(-H(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})),

(6)

where
E(\mathbf{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta})
is the energy function and
Z(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is the partition function. The score function is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log p(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})=-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}H(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}),

(7)

where the partition function cancels out in the gradient, making the score function tractable even when
Z(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is unknown.

A.2.2
Score Matching Objective

The score matching objective minimizes the expected squared difference between the model score and the data score:

\displaystyle J(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x})}\left[\|\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})-\mathbf{s}_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}\right],

(8)

where
\mathbf{s}_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x})=\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x})
is the score of the true data distribution. Since the data score is typically unknown, Hyvärinen showed that this objective can be rewritten using integration by parts as:

\displaystyle J(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x})}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\frac{\partial s_{i}(\mathbf{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial x_{i}}+\frac{1}{2}s_{i}(\mathbf{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta})^{2}\right)\right]+\text{const},

(9)

where
s_{i}(\mathbf{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta})
is the
i
-th component of the score function, and the constant term is independent of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
.

A.2.3
Practical Implementation

Given a dataset
\{\mathbf{x}^{(t)}\}_{t=1}^{T}
sampled from the true distribution, the empirical score matching objective becomes:

\displaystyle\hat{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left[\frac{\partial s_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{(t)};\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial x_{i}}+\frac{1}{2}s_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{(t)};\boldsymbol{\theta})^{2}\right].

(10)

The score matching estimator is:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{SM}=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\hat{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta}).

(11)

This optimization can also be performed efficiently using gradient-based methods. As usual, when
N\to\infty
(number of samples),
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{SM}\to\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{true}}
. The error scaling we observe in this case is also
O((N)^{-1/2})
.

A.2.4
Application to Field Theories

For
d
-dimensional field theories with Hamiltonians of the form:

\displaystyle H[\phi]=\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mu=1}^{d}(\phi_{\mathbf{x}}-\phi_{\mathbf{x}+\hat{\mu}})^{2}+V(\phi_{\mathbf{x}})\right],

(12)

where
V(\phi)
is the potential, the score function at field point
\mathbf{x}
is:

\displaystyle s_{\mathbf{x}}[\phi]=-\frac{\delta H}{\delta\phi_{\mathbf{x}}}=\sum_{\mu=1}^{d}(\phi_{\mathbf{x}+\hat{\mu}}+\phi_{\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mu}})-2d\phi_{\mathbf{x}}-V^{\prime}(\phi_{\mathbf{x}}),

(13)

where
V^{\prime}(\phi)=dV/d\phi
is the derivative of the potential. Then the score matching objective becomes:

\displaystyle J(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{\mathbf{x}}\left[\frac{\partial s_{\mathbf{x}}[\phi^{(t)}]}{\partial\phi_{\mathbf{x}}^{(t)}}+\frac{1}{2}(s_{\mathbf{x}}[\phi^{(t)}])^{2}\right],

(14)

which can be evaluated directly from field configurations without knowledge of the partition function.

A.2.5
Moment-Based Score Matching

A key insight utilized in our work is that for many field theories, the score matching objective can be expressed entirely in terms of statistical moments of the field configurations. This allows parameter learning even when only correlation functions are available rather than full field configurations. For example, in the
\phi^{4}
theory, the score function involves terms like
\phi^{3}
,
\phi\Delta\phi
, and their derivatives. Through careful algebraic manipulations, these can be rewritten in terms of moments such as
\langle\phi^{2}\rangle
,
\langle\phi^{4}\rangle
,
\langle\phi^{6}\rangle
, and nearest-neighbor correlations
\langle\phi_{\mathbf{x}}\phi_{\mathbf{x}+\hat{\mu}}\rangle
, given by

\displaystyle J_{\text{mom.}}(m^{2},\lambda)=V\Big[

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\big((4+m^{2})^{2}\langle\phi^{2}\rangle+\lambda^{2}\langle\phi^{6}\rangle+4\langle\phi_{\text{n.n.}}^{2}\rangle+12\langle\phi_{\text{n.n.}_{1}}\phi_{\text{n.n.}_{2}}\rangle

\displaystyle-8(4+m^{2})\langle\phi\phi_{\text{n.n.}}\rangle-8\lambda\langle\phi^{3}\phi_{\text{n.n.}}\rangle+2\lambda(4+m^{2})\langle\phi^{4}\rangle\big)

\displaystyle-(m^{2}+4)-3\lambda\langle\phi^{2}\rangle\Big]\;,

(15)

where
\phi_{x}
is the field at site
x
,
\phi_{\text{n.n.}}\equiv\phi_{x+\hat{\mu}}
is the field at a nearest neighbor of
x
,
\text{n.n.}_{1},\text{n.n.}_{2}
are two distinct nearest neighbors of the same
x
,
\langle\cdot\rangle
denotes ensemble averages and
V=N^{2}
is the number of sites. This moment-based formulation enables parameter inference from experimental data where only specific correlation functions are measurable, significantly broadening the practical applicability of the method.

Appendix B
Methods

B.1
Sampling Methods

We work with square space–time lattices equipped with periodic boundary conditions. For continuous models, spatial and temporal derivatives are discretized as finite differences on the lattice, so that kinetic terms take the standard nearest–neighbor form. We then employ different Monte Carlo algorithms for sampling optimized for each model class:

•

Discrete and mixed models
(Ising, Gauge, Mixed
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
–U(1)): Metropolis algorithm with single–spin or single–link updates, supplemented by cluster updates where efficient.

•

Continuous field theories
(
\phi^{4}
, Sine–Gordon): Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) using the
AdvancedHMC.jl
package
57
.

•

Gauge theories with fermions
(Schwinger model): Hybrid Monte Carlo with pseudofermion fields using
JulianSchwingerModel.jl

32
.

For each model, we generate
10^{5}
–
10^{6}
thermalized configurations, with sufficient Monte Carlo steps between saved configurations to reduce autocorrelations.

B.2
Real-Space Coarse-Graining Schemes

We implement model-specific coarse-graining procedures that preserve the essential physical content while reducing degrees of freedom:

Ising Spin Models:

For Ising spin systems, we implement Kadanoff’s block-spin transformation
26
. Spins on
2\times 2
blocks are mapped to single block spins using the majority rule:

\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}_{X}=\begin{cases}+1&\text{if }\sum_{i\in\text{block}(X)}\sigma_{i}>0\\
-1&\text{if }\sum_{i\in\text{block}(X)}\sigma_{i}<0\\
\pm 1\text{ (random)}&\text{if }\sum_{i\in\text{block}(X)}\sigma_{i}=0\end{cases}

(16)

When the block sum is zero, the block spin is assigned randomly with equal probability. This transformation preserves the Ising form of the Hamiltonian while generating effective couplings between block spins.

Scalar Field Theories (
\phi^{4}
, Sine-Gordon, Dual Schwinger):

We apply a block-spin transformation that groups field values on
2\times 2
spatial blocks. For each block, the coarse-grained field value is taken as the median of the four constituent field values:

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}_{X}=\text{median}\{\phi_{2X},\phi_{2X+\hat{x}},\phi_{2X+\hat{y}},\phi_{2X+\hat{x}+\hat{y}}\},

(17)

where
X
labels coarse-lattice sites. To maintain physical consistency, we rescale the coarse-grained configuration to preserve the total kinetic energy:

\displaystyle\langle(\nabla\phi^{\prime})^{2}\rangle_{\text{coarse}}=\langle(\nabla\phi)^{2}\rangle_{\text{fine}}.

(18)

This kinetic energy equalization ensures that the gradient terms in the action remain properly normalized across RG steps.

Pure Gauge Theories (Ising Gauge):

For gauge link variables
\sigma_{\ell}\in\{\pm 1\}
, we implement the gauge-covariant procedure detailed in Appendix
F
. Each coarse link is obtained as the product of fine links along the corresponding direction:

\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}_{X,\mu}=\sigma_{2X,\mu}\cdot\sigma_{2X+\hat{\mu},\mu},

(19)

preserving local gauge invariance and Wilson loop observables.

Gauge Theories with Matter (Schwinger Model):

The Schwinger model requires separate treatment for gauge and fermion degrees of freedom:

•

Gauge links
: Product of gauge links as above,
U^{\prime}_{X,\mu}=U_{2X,\mu}\cdot U_{2X+\hat{\mu},\mu}

•

Pseudofermion fields
: Since we work with scalar pseudofermions rather than spinors for computational efficiency, we apply the same (complex) median + kinetic energy rescaling procedure used for scalar fields.

B.3
Renormalization Group Transformations

Our RG analysis follows a unified philosophy: generate equilibrium samples using appropriate Monte Carlo methods, learn microscopic parameters, apply real-space coarse-graining transformations, and re-learn effective parameters from the renormalized configurations. This iterative procedure reconstructs RG flows without relying on perturbative approximations.

The complete RG procedure for each model follows these steps:

1.

Initialize
: Start with bare parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
and lattice size
L_{0}
and lattice spacing
a

2.

Sample
: Generate equilibrium configurations
\{\phi^{(i)}\}
using appropriate Monte Carlo

3.

Learn
: Apply pseudolikelihood (discrete) or score matching (continuous) to extract
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}

4.

Coarse-grain
: Apply model-specific transformation to obtain
\{\phi^{\prime(i)}\}
on lattice
L_{0}/2
with spacing
2a

5.

Re-learn
: Extract effective parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}
from coarse-grained data

6.

Iterate
: Repeat steps 4-5 to trace the full RG trajectory
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\to\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}\to\boldsymbol{\theta}_{2}\to\cdots

This procedure maps out complete RG flows in parameter space, revealing fixed points, phase boundaries, and the emergence of relevant operators across energy scales. This method does not depend on couplings being in the perturbative regime; As such, it captures strong-coupling physics that traditional analytical approaches cannot access.

B.4
Phase Boundary Detection

To map phase boundaries in the
\phi^{4}
theory, we systematically scan the parameter space
(m^{2},\lambda)
and identify critical transitions through RG flow analysis. The phase boundary is located by detecting "turnaround points" where the RG flow direction changes—specifically, where the sign of the mass parameter flow
\Delta m^{2}=m^{2}_{\text{RG}}-m^{2}_{\text{initial}}
switches under coarse-graining. Our algorithm proceeds as follows:

1.

Grid scan
: For each
\lambda
value, we scan over a range of
m^{2}
values with fine spacing
\delta m^{2}

2.

RG trajectory
: At each point
(m^{2}_{i},\lambda)
, we:

(a)

Generate
N
equilibrium configurations using Hamiltonian Monte Carlo

(b)

Apply
n_{\text{steps}}
successive RG transformations

(c)

Learn effective parameters at each step to trace the full RG trajectory

(d)

Record the final mass parameter
m^{2}_{\text{final}}
after RG evolution

3.

Flow direction analysis
: Compute the flow direction
\Delta m^{2}_{i}=m^{2}_{\text{final}}-m^{2}_{i}
for each initial point

4.

Transition detection
: Identify adjacent points
(m^{2}_{i},m^{2}_{i+1})
where

\displaystyle\text{sign}(\Delta m^{2}_{i})\neq\text{sign}(\Delta m^{2}_{i+1})

(20)

5.

Critical point estimation
: Locate the critical mass as
m^{2}_{c}\approx(m^{2}_{i}+m^{2}_{i+1})/2

This method exploits the fact that the symmetric and broken-symmetry phases exhibit opposite RG flow behaviors: in the symmetric phase,
m^{2}
typically increases under coarse-graining (stabilizing the symmetric vacuum), while in the broken phase,
m^{2}
becomes more negative (deepening the symmetry-breaking potential). The phase boundary corresponds precisely to the parameter values where this flow behavior transitions.

B.5
Sample Complexity Analysis

To characterize the statistical efficiency of our parameter learning algorithms, we perform systematic sample complexity studies. For each model and parameter regime, we determine the minimum number of samples
N_{\text{min}}
required to achieve a target precision in parameter estimation. The procedure involves:

1.

Fix target parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{true}}
and precision threshold
\sigma_{\text{target}}

2.

For each parameter value of interest:

(a)

Initialize sample size
N=N_{\text{start}}

(b)

Generate
R
independent datasets of size
N
from the true model

(c)

Apply the learning algorithm to each dataset to obtain estimates
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(i)}

(d)

Compute the standard error:
\sigma_{\text{est}}=\text{std}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(1)},\ldots,\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(R)})/\sqrt{R}

(e)

If
\sigma_{\text{est}}\leq\sigma_{\text{target}}
, record
N_{\text{min}}=N
; otherwise, increase
N\rightarrow 1.1\times N
and repeat

3.

Plot
N_{\text{min}}
versus parameter values to reveal scaling relationships

This analysis reveals how sample complexity scales with coupling strength and proximity to critical points, providing practical guidance for the number of configurations needed to achieve reliable parameter estimates in different physical regimes across all model classes studied.

Appendix C
Block–Spin Probability for the 1D Ising Model

Consider a 1D Ising chain of even length
L
with periodic boundary conditions
s_{L+1}\equiv s_{1}
. We define block spins
\sigma_{i}
from pairs
(s_{2i-1},s_{2i})
using the probabilistic rule

\displaystyle p(\sigma_{i}=1\mid s_{2i-1},s_{2i})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&s_{2i-1}+s_{2i}=+2,\\[2.0pt]
\frac{1}{2}&s_{2i-1}+s_{2i}=0,\\[2.0pt]
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

\displaystyle p(\sigma_{i}=-1\mid s_{2i-1},s_{2i})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&s_{2i-1}+s_{2i}=-2,\\[2.0pt]
\frac{1}{2}&s_{2i-1}+s_{2i}=0,\\[2.0pt]
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Equivalently,

\displaystyle p(\sigma_{i}\mid s_{2i-1},s_{2i})=\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\frac{e^{\alpha\sigma_{i}(s_{2i-1}+s_{2i})}}{2\cosh\!\big(\alpha(s_{2i-1}+s_{2i})\big)}\;.

(21)

The marginal distribution of the block–spin configuration
\underline{\sigma}=\{\sigma_{i}\}
is

\displaystyle p(\underline{\sigma})=\sum_{\{s\}}p(\underline{\sigma}\mid\{s\})\,p(\{s\})=\frac{1}{Z(J,h)}\sum_{\{s\}}\prod_{j=1}^{L/2}p(\sigma_{j}\mid s_{2j-1},s_{2j})\,e^{J\sum_{i=1}^{L}s_{i}s_{i+1}+h\sum_{i=1}^{L}s_{i}}.

Using (
21
) and pulling the limit outside,

\displaystyle p(\underline{\sigma})=\frac{1}{Z(J,h)}\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\sum_{\{s\}}\exp\Bigg[J\sum_{i=1}^{L}s_{i}s_{i+1}+h\sum_{i=1}^{L}s_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{L/2}\Big(\alpha\sigma_{j}(s_{2j-1}+s_{2j})-\ln 2-\ln\cosh\!\big(\alpha(s_{2j-1}+s_{2j})\big)\Big)\Bigg].

Because
s_{2i-1},s_{2i}\in\{\pm 1\}
, one can show that

\displaystyle\ln\cosh\!\big(\alpha(s_{2i-1}+s_{2i})\big)=\frac{1+s_{2i-1}s_{2i}}{2}\,\ln\cosh(2\alpha).

(22)

Substituting (
22
) and reorganizing terms,

\displaystyle p(\underline{\sigma})

\displaystyle=\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\frac{(4\cosh 2\alpha)^{-L/4}}{Z(J,h)}\sum_{\{s\}}\exp\Bigg[J\sum_{i=1}^{L}s_{i}s_{i+1}+h\sum_{i=1}^{L}s_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{L/2}\Big(\alpha\sigma_{i}(s_{2i-1}+s_{2i})-\tfrac{1}{2}s_{2i-1}s_{2i}\ln\cosh(2\alpha)\Big)\Bigg].

Splitting the interaction and field terms into odd–even and even–odd pairs,

\displaystyle J\sum_{i=1}^{L}s_{i}s_{i+1}+h\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{i}=J\sum_{i=1}^{L/2}s_{2i-1}s_{2i}+J\sum_{i=1}^{L/2}s_{2i}s_{2i+1}+h\sum_{i=1}^{L/2}(s_{2i-1}+s_{2i}).

Hence

\displaystyle p(\underline{\sigma})

\displaystyle=\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\frac{(4\cosh 2\alpha)^{-L/4}}{Z(J,h)}\sum_{\{s\}}\exp\Bigg[\left(J-\tfrac{1}{2}\ln\cosh(2\alpha)\right)\sum_{i=1}^{L/2}s_{2i-1}s_{2i}+J\sum_{i=1}^{L/2}s_{2i}s_{2i+1}+\sum_{i=1}^{L/2}(h+\alpha\sigma_{i})(s_{2i-1}+s_{2i})\Bigg].

Transfer Matrices

Define two
2\times 2
transfer matrices acting on
(s=\pm 1)
states:

\displaystyle T_{2i-1,2i}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}e^{J+2h+2\alpha\sigma_{i}}\,(\cosh 2\alpha)^{-1}&e^{-J}\\[2.0pt]
e^{-J}&e^{J-2h-2\alpha\sigma_{i}}\,(\cosh 2\alpha)^{-1}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle T_{2i,2i+1}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}e^{J}&e^{-J}\\[2.0pt]
e^{-J}&e^{J}\end{pmatrix}.

In the
\alpha\to\infty
limit,

\displaystyle T_{2i-1,2i}(\sigma_{i}=+1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}2e^{J+2h}&e^{-J}\\[2.0pt]
e^{-J}&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad T_{2i-1,2i}(\sigma_{i}=-1)=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&e^{-J}\\[2.0pt]
e^{-J}&2e^{J-2h}\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle p(\underline{\sigma})=\frac{1}{Z(J,h)}\,\mathrm{Tr}\big(T_{1,2}T_{2,3}\cdots T_{L-1,L}T_{L,1}\big).

(23)

This trace is trivial to evaluate numerically for any configuration
\underline{\sigma}
. Assuming the block–spin distribution has the same Ising form,

\displaystyle p(\underline{\sigma})=\frac{1}{Z(J^{\prime},h^{\prime})}\exp\!\left[J^{\prime}\sum_{i=1}^{L/2}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{i+1}+h^{\prime}\sum_{i=1}^{L/2}\sigma_{i}\right],

we can extract
(J^{\prime},h^{\prime})
by evaluating (
23
) for three configurations:

1.

All
\sigma_{i}=+1
: call the trace
t_{1}
.

2.

All
\sigma_{i}=-1
: call it
t_{2}
.

3.

\sigma_{1}=-1
,
\sigma_{2\ldots n}=+1
(with
l=L/2
): call it
t_{3}
.

Then

\displaystyle\frac{e^{J^{\prime}l+h^{\prime}l}}{Z(J^{\prime},h^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=t_{1},\qquad\frac{e^{J^{\prime}l-h^{\prime}l}}{Z(J^{\prime},h^{\prime})}=t_{2},\qquad\frac{e^{J^{\prime}(l-4)+h^{\prime}(l-2)}}{Z(J^{\prime},h^{\prime})}=t_{3},

and solving,

\displaystyle J^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\ln\!\left(\frac{t_{1}}{t_{3}}\right)-\frac{1}{4l}\ln\!\left(\frac{t_{1}}{t_{2}}\right),

\displaystyle h^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2l}\ln\!\left(\frac{t_{1}}{t_{2}}\right).

(24)

Appendix D
Learning the 1D Ising Model from Two–Site Correlations

The exact conditional distribution is

\displaystyle P(\sigma_{i}\mid\sigma_{i-1},\sigma_{i+1})=\frac{\exp\left[\sigma_{i}\big(h+J(\sigma_{i-1}+\sigma_{i+1})\big)\right]}{2\cosh\!\left(h+J(\sigma_{i-1}+\sigma_{i+1})\right)}.

The per–site negative log pseudo–likelihood is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(J,h)=-\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\log P(\sigma_{i}\mid\sigma_{i-1},\sigma_{i+1})=-\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\Big[\sigma_{i}\big(h+J(\sigma_{i-1}+\sigma_{i+1})\big)-\log\big(2\cosh(h+J(\sigma_{i-1}+\sigma_{i+1}))\big)\Big].

Introduce empirical moments

\displaystyle m\equiv\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i}\sigma_{i},\qquad c_{1}\equiv\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{i+1},\qquad c_{2}\equiv\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{i+2}.

(25)

The first term simplifies to
-h\,m-2J\,c_{1}
. Let
S_{i}\equiv\sigma_{i-1}+\sigma_{i+1}\in\{+2,0,-2\}
and denote

\displaystyle P_{+2}=P(S_{i}=+2),\quad P_{0}=P(S_{i}=0),\quad P_{-2}=P(S_{i}=-2),\quad P_{+2}+P_{0}+P_{-2}=1.

Match the first two moments:

\displaystyle E[S_{i}]

\displaystyle=2(P_{+2}-P_{-2})=\langle\sigma_{i}+\sigma_{i+1}\rangle=2m,

\displaystyle E[S_{i}^{2}]

\displaystyle=4(P_{+2}+P_{-2})=\langle\sigma_{i}^{2}+\sigma_{i+1}^{2}+2\sigma_{i}\sigma_{i+1}\rangle=2+2c_{2},

giving

\displaystyle P_{+2}=\frac{1+2m+c_{2}}{4},\qquad P_{0}=\frac{1-c_{2}}{2},\qquad P_{-2}=\frac{1-2m+c_{2}}{4}.

(26)

Thus the second (log–cosh) contribution becomes

\displaystyle\frac{1+2m+c_{2}}{4}\,\log\!\big[2\cosh(h+2J)\big]+\frac{1-c_{2}}{2}\,\log\!\big[2\cosh(h)\big]+\frac{1-2m+c_{2}}{4}\,\log\!\big[2\cosh(h-2J)\big].

Final expression in terms of moments:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(J,h)=-h\,m-2J\,c_{1}+\frac{1+2m+c_{2}}{4}\log\!\big[2\cosh(h+2J)\big]+\frac{1-c_{2}}{2}\log\!\big[2\cosh(h)\big]+\frac{1-2m+c_{2}}{4}\log\!\big[2\cosh(h-2J)\big].

(27)

Appendix E
Learning the
d
–dimensional Ising Model from
2d
–Site Correlations

Consider the Ising model on a
d
–dimensional cubic lattice with
L^{d}
spins
\{\sigma_{i}\}
,
\sigma_{i}\in\{\pm 1\}
. If couplings are
heterogeneous
(site/edge dependent), the conditional distinguishes each of the

2^{d}
neighbor
configurations

\sigma_{\partial i}\in\{\pm 1\}^{2d}
, and the moment-only negative log pseudo–likelihood can be written as a weighted sum over these
2^{d}
patterns (i.e.,
2^{d}
relations per site) rather than over the
2d{+}1
values of
S_{i}
.
This gives a straightforward, dimension-agnostic extension of our correlation-based pseudolikelihood. In what follows, we will focus on the homogeneous case (
J_{ij}\equiv J
,
h_{i}\equiv h
) and its weights
P_{s}
where we have uniform coupling
J
, and field
h
. Each site
i
has
2d
nearest neighbors
j\sim i
. Define the local field

\displaystyle S_{i}\;\equiv\;\sum_{j\sim i}\sigma_{j}\;\in\;\{-2d,\,-2d+2,\,\dots,\,2d\}.

The per–site negative log pseudo–likelihood is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(J,h)=-\frac{1}{L^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{L^{d}}\log P\bigl(\sigma_{i}\mid\{\sigma_{j}\}\bigr)=-\frac{1}{L^{d}}\sum_{i}\Bigl[\sigma_{i}\,(h+J\,S_{i})\;-\;\log\bigl(2\cosh(h+J\,S_{i})\bigr)\Bigr].

We use the identities

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\sigma_{j}=+1)=\frac{1+\sigma_{j}}{2},\quad\mathbb{P}(\sigma_{j}=-1)=\frac{1-\sigma_{j}}{2}.

Suppose that
k
of the
2d
-neighbors have spin
+1
, and the total spin
S_{i}=s
. Then

\displaystyle S_{i}=s=\sum_{j=1}^{2d}\sigma_{j}=\underbrace{(+1)+\cdots+(+1)}_{k\text{ times}}\;+\;\underbrace{(-1)+\cdots+(-1)}_{(2d-k)\text{ times}}=k-(2d-k)=2k-2d.

\displaystyle s=2k-2d\quad\Longrightarrow\quad k=\frac{s+2d}{2}.

Since
s
runs over the even values
-2d,-2d+2,\dots,2d
, this
k
is always an integer. Then, if we define
A
to be the indexing set of
+1
spins of size
k
,
A\subseteq\{1,\dots,2d\}
and the probability
P_{s}\equiv\mathbb{P}\bigl(\sum_{j}\sigma_{j}=s\bigr)
, we will have

\displaystyle P_{s}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\subseteq\{1,\dots,2d\}\\
|A|=k\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}\!\Bigl[\prod_{j\in A}\frac{1+\sigma_{j}}{2}\;\prod_{j\notin A}\frac{1-\sigma_{j}}{2}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=2^{-2d}\sum_{|A|=k}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\prod_{j=1}^{2d}(1+\epsilon_{j}\sigma_{j})\Bigl]

where
\epsilon_{j}=+1
for
j\in A
and
-1
otherwise. Expanding the product over
j
, we get

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{2d}(1+\epsilon_{j}\sigma_{j})=\sum_{B\subseteq\{1,2\dots 2d\}}\left(\prod_{j\in B}\epsilon_{j}\right)\prod_{j\in B}\sigma_{j}

where we define a new indexing set
B
. Inserting this expression, and swapping the sums

\displaystyle P_{s}

\displaystyle=2^{-2d}\sum_{B\subseteq\{1,\dots,2d\}}\,\Bigl\langle\prod_{j\in B}\sigma_{j}\Bigr\rangle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\subseteq\{1,\dots,2d\}\\
|A|=k\end{subarray}}\left(\prod_{j\in B}\epsilon_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=2^{-2d}\sum_{B\subseteq\{1,\dots,2d\}}N_{s}(B)\,\Bigl\langle\prod_{j\in B}\sigma_{j}\Bigr\rangle\,.

where we define
N_{s}(B)
as

\displaystyle N_{s}(B):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\subseteq\{1,\dots,2d\}\\
|A|=k=\frac{(s+2d)}{2}\end{subarray}}\left(\prod_{j\in B}\epsilon_{j}\right)=\sum_{|A|=\frac{(s+2d)}{2}}(-1)^{|B\setminus A|}

and counts the number of minus signs coming from indices in
B
but not in
A
(indices
\in A
contribute
+1
.) Assuming translation invariance and defining,

\displaystyle m=\frac{1}{L^{d}}\sum_{i}\sigma_{i},\quad c_{1}=\frac{1}{dL^{d}}\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}.

where
c_{1}=\langle\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\rangle
is the average nearest-neighbor correlation. The negative log pseudo-likelihood loss can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(J,h)=-h\,m-2d\,J\,c_{1}+\sum_{s=-2d}^{2d}2^{-2d}\sum_{B\subseteq\{1,\dots,2d\}}N_{s}(B)\,\Bigl\langle\prod_{j\in B}\sigma_{j}\Bigr\rangle\log\big[2\cosh(h+Js)\big],

This expression is
exact
for any lattice dimension
d
, involving correlators up to order
2d
. Each
P_{s}
is thus an explicit weighted sum of all spin correlation functions of order up to
2d
, with numerical coefficients that depend only on
d
and
s
. This formulation allows the loss to be written entirely in terms of empirical moments, without approximation, provided all needed correlations are available from data. As an example, we verify this for the
d=1
case. In this case,
S_{i}=\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2}\in\{-2,0,+2\}
, where
\sigma_{i-1}\equiv\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{i+1}\equiv\sigma_{2}
. For
S_{i}=+2
,
k=2=2d
, and hence the set
A
has to be the full indexing set
\{1,2\}
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle N_{+2}(B)=+1\text{ for any set }B\subseteq\{1,2\},\qquad\text{as}\quad B\setminus A=\emptyset\;.

Substituting this into the relation for
P_{s}
with
s=S_{i}=+2
,

\displaystyle P_{+2}

\displaystyle=2^{-2}\sum_{B\subseteq\{1,2\}}(+1)\cdot\langle\prod_{j\in B}\sigma_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left(1+\langle\sigma_{1}\rangle+\langle\sigma_{2}\rangle+\langle\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left(1+2m+c_{2}\right)\;,

which matches our earlier results from
26
, and we use the notation for
m
and
c_{2}
from Eq.
25
in the previous section. Although this notation might appear bulky for small values of
d
, it nonetheless provides a formal expression for larger
d
values.

Appendix F
Wegner’s Ising Gauge Theory

In this appendix, we show that the real-space coarse-graining prescription shown in Fig.
3a
applied for this model—as well as for the Schwinger and mixed models preserves gauge invariance. We work on a square lattice with gauge group
G
and link variables

U_{x,\mu}\in G
on directed links
(x,\mu)
,
\mu\in\{\hat{x},\hat{y}\}
.
A local gauge transformation
\{g_{x}\in G\}
acts as

U_{x,\mu}\;\longmapsto\;g_{x}\,U_{x,\mu}\,g_{x+\hat{\mu}}^{-1}.

(28)

Blocking map.

Define coarse sites on the even sublattice
X=(2i,2j)
. The coarse links are straight
length–2 products of fine links in the same direction:

U^{\prime}_{X,\hat{x}}\;:=\;U_{X,\hat{x}}\,U_{X+\hat{x},\hat{x}},\qquad U^{\prime}_{X,\hat{y}}\;:=\;U_{X,\hat{y}}\,U_{X+\hat{y},\hat{y}}.\\

(29)

Claim 1 (Gauge covariance of coarse links).

Under (
28
),

\displaystyle U^{\prime}_{X,\hat{x}}

\displaystyle\longmapsto\bigl(g_{X}U_{X,\hat{x}}g_{X+\hat{x}}^{-1}\bigr)\bigl(g_{X+\hat{x}}U_{X+\hat{x},\hat{x}}g_{X+2\hat{x}}^{-1}\bigr)=g_{X}\,U^{\prime}_{X,\hat{x}}\,g_{X+2\hat{x}}^{-1},

\displaystyle U^{\prime}_{X,\hat{y}}

\displaystyle\longmapsto g_{X}\,U^{\prime}_{X,\hat{y}}\,g_{X+2\hat{y}}^{-1}.

(30)

Thus with coarse gauge factors
G_{X}:=g_{X}
we have

U^{\prime}_{X,\mu^{\prime}}\mapsto G_{X}\,U^{\prime}_{X,\mu^{\prime}}\,G_{X+\hat{\mu}^{\prime}}^{-1}
(where
\hat{\mu^{\prime}}=2\hat{\mu}
is the unit vector of the coarse lattice in direction
\mu
),
the coarse links preserve gauge-covariance in the blocked lattice.

Claim 2 (Gauge invariance of coarse plaquettes and Wilson loops).

Define the coarse
1\times 1
plaquette at
X
by

P^{\prime}_{X}\;:=\;U^{\prime}_{X,\hat{x}^{\prime}}\,U^{\prime}_{X+\hat{x}^{\prime},\hat{y}^{\prime}}\,\bigl(U^{\prime}_{X+\hat{y}^{\prime},\hat{x}^{\prime}}\bigr)^{-1}\,\bigl(U^{\prime}_{X,\hat{y}^{\prime}}\bigr)^{-1}.

(31)

From (
30
) it follows that

P^{\prime}_{X}\mapsto G_{X}\,P^{\prime}_{X}\,G_{X}^{-1}
,
so
P^{\prime}_{X}
is gauge invariant for Abelian
G
(e.g.
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
,
U(1)
),
and
\mathrm{Tr}\,P^{\prime}_{X}
is gauge invariant for non–Abelian
G
.
Any coarse Wilson loop (product of coarse links around a closed contour)
is likewise gauge invariant.

Local relation to fine plaquettes.

The fine
1\times 1
plaquette based at
x
is

P_{x}:=U_{x,\hat{x}}\,U_{x+\hat{x},\hat{y}}\,U^{-1}_{x+\hat{y},\hat{x}}\,U^{-1}_{x,\hat{y}}.

(32)

Inserting (
29
) into (
31
) shows that
P^{\prime}_{X}

is the ordered product of the four fine plaquettes in the
2\times 2
block with
corner
X
, so that
\mathrm{Tr}\,P^{\prime}_{X}=\mathrm{Tr}\!\bigl(P_{X}P_{X+\hat{x}}P_{X+\hat{y}}P_{X+\hat{x}+\hat{y}}\bigr)
is gauge invariant.
Hence the blocking is local and preserves gauge–invariant observables. This construction generalizes immediately to arbitrary compact groups in
d
dimensions by multiplying consecutive links along the
d
coordinate directions.

Figure 7

(a) The standard scheme used sends products of gauge links to another gauge link in the same direction, preserving link covariance.
(b)In an alternating scheme we send the product of top and left links of the fine lattice to the top and left links of the coarse lattice sequentially.

For this model, we also applied the blocking procedures illustrated in Figs.
3b
and
3c
. We find that in every case, the effective coupling
K
is driven monotonically to
K=0
, the trivial fixed point of the theory. Since plaquettes of the fine lattice are mapped to links of the coarse lattice, these schemes do not preserve the covariance at the link level. However, they still preserve the local gauge–invariant content of a
2\times 2
block (small Wilson loops) for Abelian Theories. Thus they produce renormalized coupling
K^{\prime}(K)
that is in agreement from theoretical decimation schemes. Each of these real–space RG constructions admits a straightforward extension to higher dimensions.

Appendix G
Score Function for the Schwinger Model

The Euclidean action of the Schwinger Model is

\displaystyle S=S_{\text{gauge}}+\psi^{\dagger}M\psi,\qquad S_{\text{gauge}}=-\sum_{\text{plaq}}\beta\bigl(1-\cos\theta_{\text{plaq}}\bigr),

where the operator
M
acts on the fermion fields
\psi(x)

\displaystyle M\psi(x)=(m+2)\psi(x)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mu=1}^{2}\Big[U_{\mu}(x)(1-\gamma_{\mu})\psi(x+\hat{\mu})+U^{\dagger}_{\mu}(x-\hat{\mu})(1+\gamma_{\mu})\psi(x-\hat{\mu})\Big],

and

\displaystyle\theta_{\text{plaq}}(i)=\theta_{x}(i)+\theta_{t}(i_{\text{right}})-\theta_{x}(i_{\text{up}})-\theta_{t}(i).

First Derivatives

Gauge part:

\displaystyle S^{\prime}_{\theta_{x}}=\frac{\partial S}{\partial\theta_{x}(i)}

\displaystyle=-\beta\left[\sin\theta_{\text{plaq}}(i_{\text{down}})-\sin\theta_{\text{plaq}}(i)\right],

\displaystyle S^{\prime}_{\theta_{t}}=\frac{\partial S}{\partial\theta_{t}(i)}

\displaystyle=-\beta\left[\sin\theta_{\text{plaq}}(i_{\text{left}})-\sin\theta_{\text{plaq}}(i)\right].

Fermion part via pseudofermions
\phi
:

\displaystyle S_{\text{pf}}=\phi^{\dagger}(MM^{\dagger})^{-1}\phi,\qquad\Psi\equiv M^{-1}\phi\ \Rightarrow\ S_{f}=\Psi^{\dagger}\Psi.

Varying
S_{f}
with respect to link angles,

\displaystyle\delta S_{f}=-2\,\Re\!\left(\Psi^{\dagger}M^{-1}\delta M\,\Psi\right),\qquad S_{f}^{\prime}=-2\,\Re\!\left(\Psi^{\dagger}M^{-1}M^{\prime}\Psi\right).

where
M^{\prime}
is the derivative of
M
. The Dirac operator elements:

\displaystyle M_{n\alpha,m\beta}

\displaystyle=(m+2)\delta_{nm}\delta_{\alpha\beta}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mu=1}^{2}\Big[(1-\gamma_{\mu})_{\alpha\beta}U_{n,\mu}\delta_{n,m-\hat{\mu}}+(1+\gamma_{\mu})_{\alpha\beta}U^{\dagger}_{m,\mu}\delta_{n,m+\hat{\mu}}\Big],

\displaystyle M^{(1,\theta_{x})}_{n\alpha,m\beta}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{2}\Big[(1-\gamma_{1})_{\alpha\beta}U_{n,1}\delta_{n,m-\hat{1}}-(1+\gamma_{1})_{\alpha\beta}U^{\dagger}_{m,1}\delta_{n,m+\hat{1}}\Big],

make the indices explicit. The matrix
M^{(1,\theta_{x})}
denotes the first derivative of
M
w.r.t
\theta_{x}
and the extension for
\theta_{t}
is straightfoward.

Second Derivatives

Gauge part:

\displaystyle S^{\prime\prime}_{\theta_{x}}=\frac{\partial^{2}S}{\partial\theta_{x}(i)^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\beta\left[\cos\theta_{\text{plaq}}(i_{\text{down}})+\cos\theta_{\text{plaq}}(i)\right],

\displaystyle S^{\prime\prime}_{\theta_{t}}=\frac{\partial^{2}S}{\partial\theta_{t}(i)^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\beta\left[\cos\theta_{\text{plaq}}(i_{\text{left}})+\cos\theta_{\text{plaq}}(i)\right].

Starting from

\displaystyle\delta^{2}S_{f}=-2\,\Re\big(\delta(\Psi^{\dagger}M^{-1}M^{\prime}\Psi)\big),

and using
\delta\Psi=-M^{-1}\delta M\,\Psi
,

\displaystyle S_{f}^{\prime\prime}=\Psi^{\dagger}\!\left(-M^{\prime\dagger}(M^{\dagger})^{-1}M^{-1}M^{\prime}-2\,M^{-1}M^{\prime}M^{-1}M^{\prime}+M^{-1}M^{\prime\prime}\right)\!\Psi.

Here
M^{\prime\prime}\equiv\partial^{2}M/\partial\theta^{2}
, e.g.

\displaystyle M^{(2,\theta_{x})}_{n\alpha,m\beta}=\frac{1}{2}\Big[(1-\gamma_{1})_{\alpha\beta}U_{n,1}\delta_{n,m-\hat{1}}+(1+\gamma_{1})_{\alpha\beta}U^{\dagger}_{m,1}\delta_{n,m+\hat{1}}\Big],

and analogously for
\theta_{t}
. Thus the score function for the gauge part of the action is

\displaystyle\text{Score}_{\theta}=\sum_{T=1}^{N_{\text{samp}}}\sum_{\theta_{x},\theta_{t}}-S_{f}^{\prime\prime}+\frac{1}{2}(S_{f}^{\prime})^{2}

where the derivative denoted by prime is with respect to
\theta_{x}
and
\theta_{t}
.

Score Function derivatives w.r.t. fermions

For real variables
\xi_{i}
sampled from
S
, the score estimator is

\displaystyle\text{Score}=\sum_{T=1}^{N_{\text{samp}}}\sum_{i}\left[-\frac{\partial^{2}S}{\partial\xi_{i}^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial\xi_{i}}\right)^{2}\right].

For complex variables
z=x+iy
,
\bar{z}=x-iy
,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial S}{\partial z}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial x}-i\frac{\partial S}{\partial y}\right),\quad\frac{\partial S}{\partial\bar{z}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial x}+i\frac{\partial S}{\partial y}\right),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}S}{\partial z\,\partial\bar{z}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}S}{\partial x^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}S}{\partial y^{2}}\right).

If
\partial S/\partial\bar{z}=\overline{\partial S/\partial z}
(which is true for
S_{f}
), then the score loss is

\displaystyle\text{Score}=\sum_{T=1}^{N_{\text{samp}}}-4\frac{\partial^{2}S}{\partial z\,\partial\bar{z}}+2\left\lVert\frac{\partial S}{\partial z}\right\rVert^{2}.

For the pseudofermion part

\displaystyle S_{f}=\sum_{ij}\phi_{i}^{*}(MM^{\dagger})^{-1}_{ij}\phi_{j}=\phi^{\dagger}(MM^{\dagger})^{-1}\phi,

we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial S_{f}}{\partial\phi_{i}^{*}}=\sum_{j}(MM^{\dagger})^{-1}_{ij}\phi_{j}=\gamma_{5}Q^{-1}\Psi,\qquad Q\equiv\gamma_{5}M=Q^{\dagger},

so that

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\left\lVert\frac{\partial S_{f}}{\partial\phi^{*}_{i}}\right\rVert^{2}=\left\lVert\gamma_{5}Q^{-1}\Psi\right\rVert^{2},\qquad\sum_{i}\frac{\partial^{2}S_{f}}{\partial\phi_{i}^{*}\partial\phi_{i}}=\mathrm{Tr}\,(MM^{\dagger})^{-1}=\mathrm{Tr}\,Q^{-2}.

Thus the score loss for pseudofermions is

\displaystyle\text{Score}_{f}=\sum_{T=1}^{N_{\text{samp}}}\left(-4\,\mathrm{Tr}\,Q^{-2}+2\left\lVert\gamma_{5}Q^{-1}\Psi\right\rVert^{2}\ \right)

The final score loss will be

Final Score

\displaystyle=\text{Score}_{\theta}+\text{Score}_{f}

\displaystyle=\sum_{T=1}^{N_{\text{samp}}}\sum_{\theta_{x},\theta_{t}}\left(-S_{\theta}^{\prime\prime}+\frac{1}{2}(S_{\theta}^{\prime})^{2}\right)+\left(-S_{f}^{\prime\prime}+\frac{1}{2}(S_{f}^{\prime})^{2}\right)+\left(-4\,\mathrm{Tr}\,Q^{-2}+2\left\lVert\gamma_{5}Q^{-1}\Psi\right\rVert^{2}\ \right)\;.
