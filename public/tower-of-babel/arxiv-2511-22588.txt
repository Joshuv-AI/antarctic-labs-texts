Extended branching Rauzy induction
Francesco Dolce, Christian B. Hughes

Abstract
. Branching Rauzy induction is a two-sided form of Rauzy induction that acts on regular interval exchange transformations (IETs). We introduce an extended form of branching Rauzy induction that applies to arbitrary standard IETs, including non-minimal ones. The procedure generalizes the branching Rauzy method with two induction steps, merging and splitting, to handle equal-length cuts and invariant components respectively. As an application, we show, via a stepwise morphic argument, that all return words in the language of an arbitrary IET cluster in the Burrows-Wheeler sense.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 37B10; Secondary 37E05, 68R15

1.
Introduction

Interval exchange transformations, or IETs, were initially introduced by Oseledec in
[
27
]
, following an earlier idea of Arnold
[
1
]
.
They have long been a subject of interest in the area of dynamical systems due to their wide applicability, ranging from symbolic dynamics to ergodic theory.
Such transformations are defined as bijections of an interval which are piecewise translations.
Each such translation is specified by a permutation on a finite set of sub-intervals and a length vector, which together determine the order and lengths of the exchanged intervals.
IETs preserve Lebesgue measure and have topological entropy
0
, but can display rich behavior.
The simplest non-trivial IETs are equivalent to rotations on a circle.

Given the partition of the interval into exchanged sub-intervals, we code the orbit of a point in the interval by recording, at each iterate of the IET, the label of the sub-interval it lands in.
This produces infinite words, called trajectories, over the alphabet associated to the sub-intervals.
The set of all finite factors of these trajectories is called the language of the IET.
We analyze these languages using tools from combinatorics on words and formal language theory.

Such transformations admit geometric induction via Rauzy induction
[
28
]
(also called Rauzy-Veech induction
[
30
]
), and other induction schemes, including induction on unions of (possibly disjoint) sub-intervals (see, e.g.,
[
31
]
). The induction arguments used in the literature often employ some assumption of
regularity
of the transformation.
The most common one is the
Keane condition
, also known as the
infinite disjoint orbit condition
(or
i.d.o.c.
).
The presence of periodic components and the reducibility of the permutation often disrupt the ability to induce on a given sub-interval in a manner which respects the underlying symbolic coding of the system.
In
[
9
]
a two-sided version of Rauzy induction was introduced.

In this paper, we develop an
extended branching Rauzy induction
that allows induction on a cylinder for
all
standard IETs, without any assumption of regularity (nor minimality) of the transformation.
This procedure augments the typical left/right induction steps of the original branching Rauzy induction with two induction steps:
merging
, which resolves equal-length right/left cuts by collapsing the two sub-intervals involved in the cut into one interval, and
splitting
, which separates invariant components (minimal or periodic) of the IET.

This construction has two principal consequences.
Firstly, for every cylinder
I_{w}
, with
w
in the language of the IET
T
, there exists a
finite
induction chain of extended branching Rauzy induction steps
\chi
such that
\chi(T)
is the first-return map of
T
to
I_{w}
.
Secondly, each step is associated to a morphism on words, so the entire composed chain of step-associated morphism yields a morphism which "transports" the induced coding back to that of the original system.
In particular, the procedure yields constructive descriptions of the set of return words in the language generated by an IET.
These additional morphisms clarify how geometric cuts manifest in the symbolic coding.

We phrase much of the language-theoretic part of this paper in terms of extension graphs, which are bipartite graphs that describe how factors can be extended on the left and right by letters of the alphabet in the underlying language.
A language such that all its extension graphs are trees is called
dendric
(see, e.g.
[
2
]
or
[
11
,
18
]
where the notion of dendricity is applied to shift spaces.).

The Burrows-Wheeler transform, introduced by Burrows and Wheeler in
[
5
]
, was originally investigated in the context of data compression and has since been studied from a symbolic and combinatorial point of view (see, e.g.,
[
7
,
16
,
17
]
).
We use
ordered alsinicity
(the notion of an extension graph being a forest) to obtain an application to Burrows-Wheeler clustering in symbolic codings of IETs.
In particular, we generalize a result in
[
7
]
by showing that, for any standard IET with permutation
\pi
, every return word in its language is
\pi
-clustering in the Burrows-Wheeler sense.
This settles a question originally raised in
[
22
]
, in which the author asked whether this result holds when
\pi
is the symmetric permutation.
Note that this result was recently shown independently in
[
17
]
using different arguments.

The paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
we recall basic notions and notation of combinatorics on words.
These notions will be used both to connect the studied dynamical systems to their symbolic coding, and to formulate questions concerning their link to the Burrows-Wheeler clustering property in combinatorial terms.
In Section
3
, we state some classical results concerning Interval Exchange Transformations.
In Section
4
, we define our extended induction steps and show that they behave as expected.
Section
5
is devoted to the link between ordered alsinicity and clustering; there we provide the necessary background for the proof of the Theorem
6.1
in Section
6
, which serves as a key application of this paper.

2.
Combinatorics on Words

For all undefined terms, we refer the reader to
[
23
]
.

2.1.
Words

An
ordered alphabet

\mathcal{A}=\{a_{1}<a_{2}<\ldots<a_{k}\}
is a set of symbols called
letters
together with a total order of its elements.
The number of distinct letters in
\mathcal{A}
is called the
cardinality
of the alphabet
\mathcal{A}
and is denoted by
{\rm Card}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)
.
In this paper, we only consider finite alphabets.

The set of
finite words

\mathcal{A}^{*}
over
\mathcal{A}
is the free monoid over
\mathcal{A}
with neutral element the
empty word
, denoted by
\varepsilon
.
The product of two words
u,v\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
is given by their composition
u\cdot v
, also written
uv
.
We denote the free semigroup over
\mathcal{A}
by
\mathcal{A}^{+}
.
That is,
\mathcal{A}^{+}=\mathcal{A}^{*}\setminus\{\varepsilon\}
.
When it is possible to write
w=pus
, with
p,u,s\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
, we call
p
(resp.,
s
,
u
) a
prefix
(resp.,
suffix
,
factor
) of
w
.

The lexicographic order naturally extends the order on
\mathcal{A}
to
\mathcal{A}^{*}
.
In particular, given two distinct words
u=u_{0}\cdots u_{m}
and
v=v_{0}\cdots v_{n}
, in
\mathcal{A}^{*}
, we say that
u<v
if and only if either
u
is a prefix of
v
, or the inequality between letters
u_{i}<v_{i}
holds at the smallest index
i
at which
u
and
v
differ.

For a given word
w=w_{0}w_{1}\cdots w_{n-1}
, where each
w_{i}\in\mathcal{A}
, we denote its length
n
by
|w|
, and the number of times
u\in\mathcal{A}^{+}
appears as a factor of
w
by
|w|_{u}
.
The Parikh vector of a word
w\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
is the vector
\Psi_{\mathcal{A}}(w)\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
defined as
(\Psi_{\mathcal{A}}(w))_{a}=|w|_{a}
for each letter
a\in\mathcal{A}
.
A word
w\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
is
pangrammatic
(in
\mathcal{A}
) if
\Psi_{\mathcal{A}}(w)
is a positive vector, i.e., if
|w|_{a}>0
for every
a\in\mathcal{A}
.
These vectors can be generalized to multisets of words over the same ordered alphabet in a natural way (see Example
2.5
below).

We call a contiguous sequence of identical letters in a word a
run
.

The
k^{\text{th}}

power
of a word
u
, for
k\in\mathbb{N}
, is defined as
u^{0}=\varepsilon
and
u^{k}=u^{k-1}u
for
k>0
.
A word
w
is said to be
primitive
if it is not the integer power of another word, i.e., if
w=u^{k}
implies
k=1
.
Two words
w,w^{\prime}
are
conjugate
if
w=uv
and
w^{\prime}=vu
for some
u,v\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
.
If a word
w
is primitive, then it has exactly
|w|
distinct conjugates.
A
Lyndon word
is a primitive word that is minimal for the lexicographic order among its conjugates.
The class of conjugates of a word
w
is also called a
circular word
or
necklace
and is denoted by
[w]
.

A (right)
infinite word
(resp.,
bi-infinite word
) over
\mathcal{A}
is a sequence

\textbf{w}=w_{0}w_{1}w_{2}\cdots

(resp.,
\textbf{w}=\cdots a_{-1}a_{0}a_{1}\cdots
), with
w_{i}\in\mathcal{A}
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
(resp., for all

i\in\mathbb{Z})
.
Similarly to finite words, we can define the set of infinite words
\mathcal{A}^{\infty}
and extend the notions of prefix, suffix and factor to
\mathcal{A}^{\omega}=\mathcal{A}^{*}\cup\mathcal{A}^{\infty}
in a natural way.
An infinite word
\textbf{w}\in\mathcal{A}^{\infty}

is
eventually periodic
if
\textbf{w}=uv^{\omega}=uvvv\cdots
for some
u,v\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
.
We say that
w
is
purely periodic
when
u=\varepsilon
.
An infinite word that is not eventually periodic is called
aperiodic
.

2.2.
Languages

By
language
, we mean a factorial and bi-extendable set
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathcal{A}^{*}
, i.e., a subset of the free monoid such that for every
w\in\mathcal{L}
, we have
v,aw,wb\in\mathcal{L}
for every factor
v
of
w
and for some
a,b\in\mathcal{A}
.
The language of an infinite word
w
is the set
\mathcal{L}(\textbf{w})
of all its factors, while the language of a finite word
w
is defined as
\mathcal{L}(w^{\omega})
.
Note that, in this context, the language of a finite word contains infinitely many elements.

A language
\mathcal{L}
is
recurrent
if, for every
v\in\mathcal{L}
,
vuv\in\mathcal{L}
for some word
u\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
.
It is
uniformly recurrent
if for every
v\in\mathcal{L}
, there exists
N=N(v)\in\mathbb{N}
such that
v
appears as a factor of every element of length
N
in
\mathcal{L}
.

The set
\mathcal{R}^{\text{left}}_{\mathcal{L}}(w)
of
left return words
to
w
in
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathcal{A}^{*}

is the set of words
u
such that
uw\in\mathcal{L}
has exactly two occurrences of
w
as factor: as a prefix and as a suffix.
Formally

\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{L}}^{\text{left}}(w)=\{u\in\mathcal{A}^{*}\;:\;uw\in(\mathcal{L}\cap w\mathcal{A}^{*})\setminus\mathcal{A}^{+}w\mathcal{A}^{+}\}.

Similarly, we can define the set of
right return words
to
w
in
\mathcal{L}
by

\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{L}}^{\text{right}}(w)=\{v\in\mathcal{A}^{*}\;:\;wv\in(\mathcal{L}\cap\mathcal{A}^{*}w)\setminus w\mathcal{A}^{+}w\}.

The two sets are related, since for every
w\in\mathcal{L}
we have

w\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{L}}^{\text{left}}(w)=\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{L}}^{\text{right}}(w)w.

Throughout the remainder of the paper, we will only deal with
left
return words.
Thus, when the language
\mathcal{L}
is clear, we will write
\mathcal{R}(w)
instead of
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{L}}^{\text{left}}(w)
.
Evidently, our results can be rephrased in terms of right return words.

2.3.
Morphisms

A
morphism
between two alphabets
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
is a map
\varphi:\mathcal{A}^{*}\to\mathcal{B}^{*}
such that

\varphi(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon

and

\varphi(uv)=\varphi(u)\varphi(v)

for every
u,v\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
.

Given two distinct letters
a,b\in\mathcal{A}
, we define the morphisms

\alpha_{a,b},\tilde{\alpha}_{a,b}:\mathcal{A}^{*}\to\mathcal{A}^{*}

as

\alpha_{a,b}=\begin{cases}a\mapsto ab\\
c\mapsto c,&c\neq a\end{cases}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\tilde{\alpha}_{a,b}=\begin{cases}a\mapsto ba\\
c\mapsto c,&c\neq a\end{cases}.

With an abuse of notation, we also define the same morphisms from
\mathcal{A}^{*}
to
\left(\mathcal{A}\sqcup\{b\}\right)^{*}
if
b\notin\mathcal{A}
.
Given two disjoint alphabets
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}
let

\iota_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}:\mathcal{A}^{*}\to\left(\mathcal{A}\sqcup\mathcal{B}\right)^{*}

be defined as the identity restricted to
\mathcal{A}^{*}
.

2.4.
Permutations

A
permutation
of
\mathcal{A}=\{a_{1}<\ldots<a_{k}\}
is a bijective automorphism of
\mathcal{A}
.
We denote the set of such permutations by
S_{\mathcal{A}}
.

To describe an element
\pi\in S_{\mathcal{A}}
, we will use either the one-line notation or the cyclic notation.
For instance, the symmetric permutation defined by

\pi(a_{i})=a_{k-i+1}
for every
1\leq i\leq k

will be denoted either as

(a_{k},a_{k-1},\ldots,a_{1})

or as the composition of the
2
-cycles

(a_{1}a_{k})(a_{2}a_{k-1})\cdots(a_{\frac{k}{2}}a_{\frac{k}{2}+1})

when
k
is even, and

(a_{1}a_{k})(a_{2}a_{k-1})\cdots(a_{\frac{k-1}{2}}a_{\frac{k+1}{2}+1})(a_{\frac{k+1}{2}})

when
k
is odd.

A permutation is
circular
if it has only one cycle.
It is
reducible
if the ordered alphabet
\mathcal{A}_{i,j}=\{a_{i}<a_{i+1}<\ldots<a_{j}\}
is invariant under
\pi
for some
i\leq j
with
j-i<k-1
, i.e.,
i>0
or
j<k
.
Note that our definition of reducibility of a permutation generalizes the classical one where
i=0
.
Evidently, if
\mathcal{A}_{i,j}
is invariant under
\pi
, then so is the alphabet
\{a_{1}<\ldots<a_{i-1}<a_{j+1}<\ldots<a_{k}\}
.

2.5.
Burrows-Wheeler transform

The Burrows-Wheeler transform is the map sending a word
w\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
to the word
{\rm bwt}_{\mathcal{A}}\left(w\right)
obtained by concatenating the last (not necessarily distinct) letters of the
|w|
conjugates of
w
sorted lexicographically on
\mathcal{A}
.
Explicitly, let
w=w_{0}\cdots w_{n-1}
be a word of length
n
over the alphabet
\mathcal{A}
, and let

w^{(0)}\leq w^{(1)}\leq\ldots\leq w^{(n-1)}
be its conjugates.
Then

{\rm bwt}_{\mathcal{A}}\left(w\right)=w^{(0)}_{n-1}w^{(1)}_{n-1}\cdots w^{(n-1)}_{n-1}

is the last column of the
n\times n
matrix

M_{\mathcal{A}}(w)=\left[\begin{matrix}w^{(0)}_{0}&w^{(0)}_{1}&\cdots&w^{(0)}_{n-1}\\
w^{(1)}_{0}&w^{(1)}_{1}&\cdots&w^{(1)}_{n-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
w^{(n-1)}_{0}&w^{(n-1)}_{1}&\cdots&w^{(n-1)}_{n-1}\end{matrix}\right].

Example 2.1
.

Consider the word

w={\tt levkoy}

over the standard ordered English alphabet

\mathcal{E}=\{{\tt a}<{\tt b}<\ldots<{\tt z}\}
.
We compute its matrix of cyclic rotations, sorted lexicographically by row, from top to bottom.

M_{\mathcal{E}}(w)=\left[\begin{matrix}{\tt e}&{\tt v}&{\tt k}&{\tt o}&{\tt y}&{\tt l}\\
{\tt k}&{\tt o}&{\tt y}&{\tt l}&{\tt e}&{\tt v}\\
{\tt l}&{\tt e}&{\tt v}&{\tt k}&{\tt o}&{\tt y}\\
{\tt o}&{\tt y}&{\tt l}&{\tt e}&{\tt v}&{\tt k}\\
{\tt v}&{\tt k}&{\tt o}&{\tt y}&{\tt l}&{\tt e}\\
{\tt y}&{\tt l}&{\tt e}&{\tt v}&{\tt k}&{\tt o}\end{matrix}\right].

Concatenating the letters of the last column of
M_{\mathcal{E}}(w)
, beginning with the first row, we obtain

{\rm bwt}_{\mathcal{E}}\left(w\right)={\tt lvykeo}
.

Example 2.2
.

Let us consider the two words

w^{\prime}={\tt peterbald}

and
w^{\prime\prime}={\tt bambino}

over the same alphabet

\mathcal{E}
as in Example
2.1
.
We compute
{\rm bwt}_{\mathcal{E}}\left(w^{\prime}\right)={\tt brltpadee}
and

{\rm bwt}_{\mathcal{E}}\left(w^{\prime\prime}\right)={\tt bombain}
.

The Burrows-Wheeler transform of the word
{\rm bwt}_{\mathcal{E}}\left(w\right)=\tt lvykeo
in Example
2.1
has six distinct runs of length one. In Example
2.2
,
{\rm bwt}_{\mathcal{E}}\left(w\right)=\tt brltpadee
has seven runs of length one and one run of length two:
\tt ee
.

The following results are well known (see, e.g.,
[
6
,
24
,
25
]
).

Proposition 2.3
.

Two words
u,v
over the same ordered alphabet
\mathcal{A}
are conjugate if and only if

{\rm bwt}_{\mathcal{A}}\left(u\right)={\rm bwt}_{\mathcal{A}}\left(v\right)
.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
and
p\in\mathbb{N}
.
A word
w
is a conjugate of
u^{p}
if and only if

{\rm bwt}_{\mathcal{A}}\left(u\right)=b_{1}\cdots b_{|u|}

and

{\rm bwt}_{\mathcal{A}}\left(w\right)=b_{1}^{p}\cdots b_{|u|}^{p}
,
with
b_{i}\in\mathcal{A}
.

In
[
24
]
it is shown that, given an ordered alphabet
\mathcal{A}
, an extended version of the Burrows-Wheeler transform, denoted
ebwt
, gives a bijection between
\mathcal{A}^{*}
and the multiset of Lyndon words (equivalently, to the set of powers of Lyndon words) over
\mathcal{A}
, where the conjugates, possibly of different lengths, are ordered using the
\omega
-order instead of the lexicographic order:
u\leq_{\omega}v
if
u^{\omega}\leq v^{\omega}
.

Example 2.5
.

Let
W
be the multiset
\{{\tt aac},{\tt ab},{\tt ab}\}
of Lyndon words over the alphabet
\mathcal{A}=\{{\tt a}<{\tt b}<{\tt c}\}
.
The Parikh vector of
W
is
\Psi_{\mathcal{A}}(W)=(4,2,1)
.
The extended Burrows-Wheeler transform of the multiset is

{\rm ebwt}_{\mathcal{A}}\left(W\right)={\tt cbbaaaa}
.
Indeed, the conjugates of
W
are:

{\tt aac}<_{\omega}{\tt ab}\leq_{\omega}{\tt ab}<_{\omega}{\tt aca}<_{\omega}{\tt ba}\leq_{\omega}{\tt ba}<_{\omega}{\tt caa}
.

Let
\pi
be a permutation over an ordered alphabet
\mathcal{A}
.
A word
w\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
is said to be
\pi
-clustering
for
\mathcal{A}
if

{\rm bwt}_{\mathcal{A}}\left(w\right)=a^{k_{1}}_{\pi(a_{1})}\cdots a^{k_{r}}_{\pi(a_{k})},

where
k_{i}=|w|_{\pi(a_{i})}
and
k={\rm Card}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)
.
A word is
perfectly clustering
(for its alphabet) if it is
\pi
-clustering with
\pi\in S_{\mathcal{A}}
the symmetric permutation.
Thus, a word is clustering if its image under the Burrows-Wheeler transform is
clustered
, i.e., the number of runs in it corresponds to the number of distinct letters.

Note that it is also possible to define the clustering property in terms of two permutations (starting with an unordered alphabet), as done, e.g., in
[
17
]
.

Example 2.6
.

Let us consider the two words
w
,
w^{\prime}
and
w^{\prime\prime}
in Examples
2.1
and
2.2
.
The words
w
and
w^{\prime}
are clustering, while
w^{\prime\prime}
is not.

The notions of clustering and perfectly clustering can be extended to multisets of words.

Example 2.7
.

The multiset
W
of Example
2.5
is (perfectly) clustering, while the multiset

W^{\prime}=\{{\tt aab},{\tt ab},{\tt ab}\}
,
defined over the same ordered alphabet, is not.
Indeed, one has

{\rm ebwt}_{\mathcal{A}}\left(W^{\prime}\right)={\tt babbaaa}
,
since the conjugates of
W^{\prime}
are:

{\tt aab}<_{\omega}{\tt aba}\leq_{\omega}{\tt ab}<_{\omega}{\tt ab}<_{\omega}{\tt baa}\leq_{\omega}{\tt ba}<_{\omega}{\tt ba}
.

The clustering property evidently depends not only on the word (resp., multiset of words), but on the order of the alphabet as well.

Example 2.8
.

Let us consider the three alphabets

\mathcal{A}=\{{\tt a}<{\tt b}<{\tt n}\},\quad\mathcal{A}^{\prime}=\{{\tt a}<{\tt n}<{\tt b}\},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{A}^{\prime\prime}=\{{\tt n}<{\tt a}<{\tt b}\}.

The word
w={\tt banana}
is pangrammatic for all three alphabets.
One has

{\rm bwt}_{\mathcal{A}}\left(w\right)={\tt nnbaaa},\quad{\rm bwt}_{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}\left(w\right)={\tt bnnaa}\quad\text{and}\quad{\rm bwt}_{\mathcal{A}^{\prime\prime}}\left(w\right)={\tt aabna}.

So,
w
is perfectly clustering for
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
, but not clustering for
\mathcal{A}^{\prime\prime}
.

Over a binary alphabet (perfectly) clustering words coincide with powers of Christoffel words and their conjugates (see
[
24
]
).
A characterization over larger alphabets in terms of factorization into palindromes is
given in
[
21
]
(see also
[
29
]
).

The following result follows immediately from Propositions
2.3
and
2.4
.

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
w=u^{p}\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
with
u
a primitive word and
p\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then
w
is
\pi
-clustering for
\mathcal{A}
if and only if
u
is
\pi
-clustering for
\mathcal{A}
.

3.
Interval Exchange Transformations

By a (real) interval we mean a left-closed and right-open interval over the real line.
Let
\mathcal{A}
be an ordered alphabet of cardinality
k
and
\pi
a permutation on
\mathcal{A}
.
An ordered partition
(I_{a})_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
of an interval
I
is a finite sequence of mutually disjoint sub-intervals of
I
such that
I_{a}
is to the left of
I_{b}
when
a<b
, and such that
\bigcup_{a\in\mathcal{A}}I_{a}=I
.
We assume that
|I_{a}|>0
for every letter
a\in\mathcal{A}
.

The
k
-
interval exchange transformation
(or
k
-
IET
or just
IET
in short)
T
associated with a partition
(I_{a})_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
of
I=[\ell,r)
and a permutation
\pi\in S_{\mathcal{A}}
is the piecewise translation defined by

T(x)=x+\tau_{a}\quad\text{if}\quad x\in I_{a},

where

\tau_{a}=\sum_{\pi^{-1}(b)<\pi^{-1}(a)}|I_{b}|-\sum_{b<a}|I_{b}|.

We define
D(T)=\displaystyle\left\{\sum_{b<a}|I_{b}|\;:\;a\in\mathcal{A}\right\}\setminus\{\ell\}
as the set of
formal discontinuities
of
T
.
The
orbit
of a point
x\in I
under
T
is the set
\{T^{k}(x)\;|\;k\in\mathbb{Z}\}
.
An IET is
periodic
if the orbit of any point
x\in I
is finite.
It is
minimal
if the orbit of any point
x\in I
is dense in the interval (with respect to the standard Euclidean topology).
It is
regular
, or satisfies the
Keane condition
or
i.d.o.c.
, if the orbits of the formal discontinuities are infinite and disjoint.
A regular IET is minimal and aperiodic (
[
20
]
), while the inverse is not true (see, e.g.,
[
3
]
).

A
connection
of an IET
T
is a triple
(x,y,n)
where
x\in D(T^{-1})
,
y\in D(T)
,
n\geq 0
and
T^{n}(x)=y
.
When
n=0
, we call the point
x=y
a
0
-
connection
.

Given an IET
T
, define the set

C(T)=\left\{T^{i}(x)\,:\,(x,y,n)\text{ is a connection},i\geq 0\right\}.

The set
C(T)

4.
Induced Interval Exchange Transformations

Let
\mathcal{A}=\{a_{1}<\ldots<a_{k}\}
be an ordered alphabet and let
\pi\in S_{\mathcal{A}}
be a permutation on
\mathcal{A}
.
Let
T
be an IET over
I=[\ell,r)
associated with a partition
(I_{a})_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
of
I
and a permutation
\pi\in S_{\mathcal{A}}
.
The
transformation induced
by
T
on a sub-interval
J\subset I
is the map
T^{\prime}:J\to J
defined by
T^{\prime}(z)=T^{\nu(z)}(z)
, where

\nu(z)=\min\{n>0\;:\;T^{n}(z)\in J\}

is called the
first return map
of
T
to
J
.
Note that
\nu(z)
is well-defined because IETs do not have wandering intervals (see, e.g.,
[
14
]
).

We now develop the induction machinery necessary to extend the results of
[
28
]
and
[
9
]
.
From any standard IET
T
, we obtain "related" IETs, i.e., IETs associated either with the same alphabet
\mathcal{A}
(possibly reordered) or with a sub-alphabet of it (possibly circularly reordered). We do this via an analysis of IET first-return map dynamics.

Assume first that
\pi
is irreducible.

4.1.
Right Rauzy step

A
right Rauzy step
is a mapping
\rho
sending
T
to the induced transformation
T^{\prime}
on
I^{\prime}=[\ell,r^{\prime})
, where
r^{\prime}
is the rightmost among the points in
D(T)\cup D(T^{-1})
.

Assume that
\pi(a_{k})\neq a_{k}
.

If
r^{\prime}
is not a connection, that is, if
|I_{a_{k}}|\neq|I_{\pi(a_{k})}|
, we call the step
non-degenerate
.
This case corresponds to the classical right Rauzy induction introduced by Rauzy in
[
28
]
.

If
|I_{a_{k}}|>|I_{\pi(a_{k})}|
then
\Omega(x)
starts with
\pi(a_{k})a_{k}
for every
x\in I_{\pi(a_{k})}
.

If
|I_{a_{k}}|<|I_{\pi(a_{k})}|
then
\Omega(x)
starts with
\pi(a_{k})a_{k}
for every
x\in T^{-1}(I_{a_{k}})
.

We first consider the former case (i.e., when
|I_{a_{k}}|>|I_{\pi(a_{k})}|
).
Let
h=\pi^{-1}(k)
.
Then
\rho(T)
is the IET associated with
\mathcal{A}
and
\pi^{\prime}\in S_{\mathcal{A}}
defined as

(1)

\pi^{\prime}(i)=\begin{cases}\pi(i)&\text{if }i\leq h\\
\pi(k)&\text{if }i=h+1\\
\pi(i)+1&\text{if }i>h+1\end{cases}.

In the latter case
(i.e., when
|I_{a_{k}}|<|I_{\pi(a_{k})}|
)

\rho(T)
is the IET associated with

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}=\{a_{1}<\ldots<a_{h}<a_{k}<a_{h+1}<\ldots<a_{k-1}\}

and

\pi\in S_{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}

defined as

(2)

\pi^{\prime}(i)=\pi(i)\quad\text{for all }1\leq i\leq k.

It is well-known that if
T
is regular, we can apply this step infinitely many times and always obtain a new regular IET over the same alphabet (possibly reordered).

Let us now consider the
degenerate
case, which occurs when
r^{\prime}
is a connection.
Equivalently, the degenerate case occurs when we have
|I_{a_{k}}|=|I_{\pi(a_{k})}|
.
In such a case,
\pi
can be either reducible or irreducible.

For every point
x\in I_{\pi(a_{k})}
, the trajectory
\Omega(x)
starts with
\pi(a_{k})a_{k}
.
The first return map of
T
to
I^{\prime}=[\ell,r^{\prime})
coincides with
T
for every point
x\notin I_{\pi(a_{k})}
and with
T^{2}
for
x\in I_{\pi(a_{k})}
.
In this case, we define
\rho(T)
to be the IET on
I^{\prime}
, partitioned by
(I_{a})_{a\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}
, with the permutation
\pi^{\prime}\in S_{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}
, where

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}=\{a_{1}<\ldots<a_{k-1}\}

and

(3)

\pi^{\prime}(a)=\begin{cases}\pi(a_{k})&\text{if }a=\pi^{-1}(a_{k})\\
\pi(a)&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

The following proposition shows that this new map is truly an IET and behaves as expected, including in the degenerate case.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
T
be a
k
-IET on the interval
I
over the alphabet
\mathcal{A}
with associated permutation
\pi\in S_{\mathcal{A}}
.
Suppose that
|I_{a_{k}}|=|I_{\pi(a_{k})}|
and
a_{k}\neq\pi(a_{k})
.
Then, the map
\rho(T)
on
I^{\prime}=[\ell,r-|I_{a_{k}}|)
over the alphabet
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}=\mathcal{A}\setminus\{a_{k}\}
with the permutation
\pi^{\prime}
as in Equation
3
is a
(k-1)
-IET.

Moreover, for all
x\in I^{\prime}
,

\Omega_{T}(x)=\varphi(\Omega_{\rho(T)}(x))
,
where
\varphi
is the morphism

\varphi:\mathcal{A}^{\prime\omega}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}^{\omega}

defined by
\varphi(\pi(a_{k}))=\pi(a_{k})a_{k}
and
\varphi(c)=c
for every letter
c\neq\pi(a_{k})
.

Proof.

Consider the first-return map
\nu
of
T
to
I
.

If
x\in I_{\pi(a_{k})}\subset I^{\prime}
, then
T(x)\in I_{a_{k}}\not\subset I^{\prime}
, but
T^{2}(x)\in I^{\prime}
.
Thus,
\nu(x)=2
.

Otherwise, if
x\in I_{a}
with
a\neq\pi(a_{k})
, then
T(x)\in T(I_{a})\subset I^{\prime}
.
Thus,
\nu(x)=1
.

Let us denote this first-return map by
T^{\prime}
.
For each each
x\in I_{a}\subset I^{\prime}
, with
a\neq\pi(a_{k})
, we have
T^{\prime}(x)=T(x)
.
For all
x\in I_{\pi(a_{k})}
, we have
T^{\prime}(x)=T^{2}(x)
.
Hence,

T^{\prime}(x)=\begin{cases}T^{2}(x)&\text{if }x\in I_{\pi(a_{k})}\\
T(x)&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

Consequently, we obtain the induced map on
I^{\prime}
with the domain alphabet
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}=\mathcal{A}\setminus\{a_{k}\}
.
In particular,
T^{\prime}
is an IET over the alphabet
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
, with
|\mathcal{A}^{\prime}|=k-1
.
Thus,
T^{\prime}
is a
(k-1)
-IET.

Reading the image sub-intervals of
T^{\prime}
in
I^{\prime}
gives a permutation of
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
obtained precisely by removing
a_{k}
from
\pi
and replacing the single occurrence of
a_{k}
in the image of
\pi
by
\pi(a_{k})
, and removing the
\pi(a_{k})
in the image of
\pi
.
This is exactly the definition of
\pi^{\prime}
.

Thus,
T^{\prime}=\rho(T)
is a
(k-1)
-IET on
I^{\prime}
over the alphabet
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
with permutation
\pi^{\prime}
, proving the first part of the statement.

Let us now consider a point
x\in I^{\prime}
.
The trajectory of
x
is

\Omega_{\rho(T)}(x)=c_{0}c_{1}c_{2}\cdots
, with every
c_{n}\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
.
Let us define the sequence of return times
\nu_{0}=0
and

\nu_{n+1}=\begin{cases}\nu_{n}+2&\text{if }c_{n}=\pi(a_{k})\\
\nu_{n}+1&\text{if }c_{n}\neq\pi(a_{k})\end{cases}.

By construction,
T^{\nu_{n}}(x)=T^{\prime n}(x)\in I^{\prime}
, and there is no visit to
I^{\prime}
at any intermediate time between
\nu_{n}
and
\nu_{n+1}
.
We claim that

\Omega_{T}(x)=\varphi(c_{0})\varphi(c_{1})\varphi(c_{2})\cdots=\varphi(\Omega_{\rho(T)}(x)).

In particular, we claim that for each
n\geq 0
, the factor of
\Omega_{T}(x)
from position
\nu_{n}
up to position
\nu_{n+1}-1
equals
\varphi(c_{n})
.
To show the equality, we deal with the following two cases.

First, suppose
c_{n}\neq\pi(a_{k})
.
An application of
T
to
T^{\prime n}(x)\in I_{c_{n}}
results in a
T(x)
which remains in
I^{\prime}
.
Consequently, the return to
I^{\prime}
occurs at time
\nu_{n}+1
and the letter at position
\nu_{n}
is exactly
\varphi(c_{n})
.

Now suppose
c_{n}=\pi(a_{k})
.
We have
T(I_{\pi(a_{k})})=I_{a_{k}}\not\subset I^{\prime}
.
The first application of
T
to an
x\in I_{\pi(a_{k})}
therefore results in an occurrence of
\pi(a_{k})
in the trajectory.
The second application of
T
returns
x
to
I^{\prime}
and results in an occurrence of
a_{k}
in the trajectory.
Hence, the two letters at positions
\nu_{n}
and
\nu_{n}+1
are
\pi(a_{k})a_{k}=\varphi(\pi(a_{k}))
, and the return occurs exactly at time
\nu_{n}+2
.

Concatenating over all

yields the desired equality.

5.
Extension Graphs and Alsinicity

In order to generalize the clustering result of
[
7
]
to all standard interval exchange transformations, and to specify the specific clustering permutation, we require order conditions and extension graphs.

Let
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathcal{A}^{*}
be a language.
The
extension graph

\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{L}}(w)
of a word
w\in\mathcal{L}
is the undirected bipartite graph with vertices the disjoint union of

L_{\mathcal{L}}(w)=\{a\in\mathcal{A}\;:\;aw\in\mathcal{L}\}\quad\text{and}\quad R_{\mathcal{L}}(w)=\{b\in\mathcal{A}\;:\;wb\in\mathcal{L}\},

and edges

B_{\mathcal{L}}(w)=\{(a,b)\in\mathcal{A}^{2}\;:\;awb\in\mathcal{L}\}.

A word
w\in L
such that
|L_{\mathcal{L}}(w)|>1
(resp., (
|R_{\mathcal{L}}(w)|>1
) is said to be
left-special
(resp.,
right-special
).
A word that is both left- and right-special is called
bispecial
.

An extension graph
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{L}}(w)
is
compatible
with two orders
<_{1}
and
<_{2}
on
\mathcal{A}
if

\forall(a,b),(c,d)\in B_{\mathcal{L}}(w),\qquad a<_{1}c\Rightarrow b\leq_{2}d.

When
\mathcal{L}
is clear from the context, we just write
\mathcal{G}(w)
,
L(w)
,
R(w)
and
B(w)
.

A language
\mathcal{L}
is said to be
dendric
if the extension graph of every
w\in\mathcal{L}
is acyclic and connected (whence the original name
tree set
in
[
2
]
).
A language
alsinic
if the extension graph of every word in it is a forest (see
[
7
]
).
Since every forest is a planar graph, for every word
w
in an alsinic language is it possible to find two order
<_{1,w}
and
<_{2,w}
such that
\mathcal{G}(w)
is compatible with them.

A language
\mathcal{L}
is
ordered dendric
(resp.,
ordered alsinic
) for two orders
<_{1}
and
<_{2}
if every
\mathcal{G}(w)
, with
w\in\mathcal{L}
, is compatible for
<_{1}
and
<_{2}
(in
[
3
]
the term
planar tree
was used since the edges do not cross when drawing the vertices of
L(w)
on the left ordered by
<_{1}
and the vertices of
R(w)
on the right ordered by
<_{2}
).

An example of a family of ordered dendric languages is given by Sturmian languages, i.e., coding of an irrational rotation on a circle.
On the other hand, Arnoux-Rauzy words on more than two letters are dendric but not ordered dendric
[
3
]
.

Ordered alsinic languages are strictly linked to IETs.

Theorem 5.1

(
[
15
,
14
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathcal{A}^{*}
.

•

\mathcal{L}
is the language of an IET if and only if it is a recurrent alsinic language.

•

\mathcal{L}
is the language of a minimal IET if and only if it is an aperiodic, uniformly recurrent alsinic language.

•

\mathcal{L}
is the language of a regular IET if and only if it is a recurrent ordered dendric set.

Note that in
[
15
,
14
]
the hypothesis of uniform recurrence was used for the last point.
However, as proved in
[
10
]
, recurrent dendric sets are also uniformly recurrent.
As observed earlier, clustering words are associated with DIETs. Recall also that a DIET can be viewed as an IET having sub-intervals of integral (or rational) length.
We make this link explicit via a reformulation of
[

For two total orders

and

states that, for a factor

In the same paper, the authors show that a primitive word

6.
Return Words in IET Languages

As an application of this induction procedure, we give a proof of the following theorem.
The same result was recently showed, independently, in
[
17
]
, using different tools.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
T
be an interval exchange transformation over the alphabet
\mathcal{A}
with a permutation
\pi\in S_{\mathcal{A}}
.
All return words in the language of
T
are
\pi
-clustering.

Before handling the general case, in which we allow our IET to have irregular components, we show the following lemma.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
T
be a regular
n
-IET,
n\geq 1
, with associated permutation
\pi
.
Let
\mathcal{L}(T)
be its language.
All return words
w\in\mathcal{L}(T)
are
\pi
-clustering.

Proof.

Let
u\in\mathcal{L}(T)
and
w\in\mathcal{R}(u)\subset\mathcal{L}(T)
.
We handle the following cases for
v\in\mathcal{L}(w^{\omega})
using Theorem
5.2
.
We denote the length
|w|
of the word
w
by
r
.
Note that, since
T
is regular,
\mathcal{L}(T)
is uniformly recurrent ordered alsinic for the orders
<_{\mathcal{A}}
and
<_{\pi}
.

First, suppose
|u|\geq|w|
.

(1)

Let
|v|\geq|w|
.
Then
v=swp
for a suffix
s
of
w
and a prefix
p
of
w
.
In the language
\mathcal{L}(w^{\omega})
, the word
v
can be extended on the left and on the right by a single letter in a unique way. Consequently,
v
is neither left-special nor right-special in
\mathcal{L}(w^{\omega})
.
As a result, the extension graph of
v
trivially satisfies the ordered alsinic condition, since it only contains one edge between two vertices.

(2)

Now suppose that
|v|<|w|
.
Then, either
v
straddles two occurrences of
w
, or
v
is a factor of
w
.
The second case is trivial, since
v
rests in
\mathcal{L}(T)
by definition.
In the former case,
v\in\mathcal{L}(w^{2})\setminus\mathcal{L}(w)\subset\mathcal{L}(uw)\subset\mathcal{L}(T)
.
Thus,
v\in\mathcal{L}(T)
, and the extension graphs of all factors of
\mathcal{L}(T)
are compatible w.r.t. the orders
<_{A}
and
<_{\pi}
as a consequence of the regularity of
T
.
Hence, the extension graph of
v
is ordered alsinic with respect to
<_{A}
and
<_{\pi}
.

Now suppose
|u|<|w|
.

(1)

Let
|v|\geq|w|
.
The argument is identical to that in the first case with
|u|\geq|w|
and
|v|\geq|w|
, and the ordered alsinicity of the extension graph of
v
with respect to the orders
<_{\mathcal{A}}
and
<_{\pi}
follows.

(2)

Finally, suppose
|v|<|w|
.
If
v\in\mathcal{L}(w)\cup\mathcal{L}(u)\subset\mathcal{L}
, then we immediately obtain the compatibility of the extension graph of
v
from the ordered alsinicity of
\mathcal{L}
.
Otherwise, if
v\in\mathcal{L}(ww)\setminus\mathcal{L}(w)
, then we can write
v=sup
.
In such a case, by a similar argument as above, the word
v
can be uniquely extended on the left by one letter and uniquely extended on the right by one letter.
Consequently, we obtain the ordered alsinicity of the extension graph of
v
with respect to the desired orders via a symmetrical argument to that of the first case.

As a consequence of the above cases and Theorem
5.2
, all return words in the language of a regular IET with associated permutation
\pi
are
\pi
-clustering in the Burrows-Wheeler sense.
∎

Remark 6.3

Note that in the proof of Lemma

In particular, the statement of Lemma

Let us recall the morphisms

\alpha_{a,b},\tilde{\alpha}_{a,b}:\mathcal{A}^{*}\to\mathcal{A}^{*}

defined in Section

as

\alpha_{a,b}=\begin{cases}a\mapsto ab\\
c\mapsto c,&c\neq a\end{cases}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\tilde{\alpha}_{a,b}=\begin{cases}a\mapsto ba\\
c\mapsto c,&c\neq a\end{cases},

with
a,b\in\mathcal{A}
,
as well as the inclusions

\iota_{\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A}}:\mathcal{B}^{*}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}^{*}

and

\iota_{\overline{\mathcal{B}},\mathcal{A}}:\overline{\mathcal{B}}^{*}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}^{*}

defined in Section
4.4
, with
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{A}
.

We use these morphisms to step back from
I_{w}
to
[\ell,r)
.
Recall that these morphims are
\alpha_{a,b}:a\mapsto ab
and
\tilde{\alpha}_{a,b}:a\mapsto ba
, which fix all other letters, as well as the inclusion
\iota_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}:\mathcal{A}^{*}\rightarrow(\mathcal{A}\sqcup\mathcal{B})^{*}
.
We begin by showing that primitivity is preserved by these morphisms.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
w
be a primitive word over an ordered alphabet
\mathcal{A}
.
Let
a\in\mathcal{A}
and
b\in\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{B}
, with
\mathcal{B}
an alphabet disjoint from
\mathcal{A}
.
For every

\varphi\in S_{\mathcal{A}}\cup\{\alpha_{a,b},\tilde{\alpha}_{a,b},\iota_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}\}
the word
\varphi(w)
is primitive.

Proof.

Let
u=\varphi(w)
.

(1)

We first consider the case
\varphi\in S_{\mathcal{A}}
.
Let
u=v^{d}
for some primitive word
v=a_{1}\ldots a_{m}\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
.
Set
v^{\prime}=\varphi^{-1}(a_{1})\cdots\varphi^{-1}(a_{m})
.
After applying the inverse permutation
\varphi^{-1}\in S_{\mathcal{A}}
, we obtain
w=v^{\prime d}
.
By the primitivity of
w
, we find that
d=1
.

(2)

Suppose now that
\varphi=\alpha_{a,b}
.
We first consider the case where
b\in\mathcal{A}
.
Let
u=v^{d}
for some primitive word
v\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
.
We claim that
v
cannot end with the letter
a
, since otherwise
u
would also end with
a
, which is impossible, since by construction every
a
in
u=\alpha_{a,b}(w)
is followed by the letter
b
.
Therefore,
v=v_{1}abv_{2}ab\cdots v_{m}c
, where
c\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\{a\}
and
v_{i}\in(\mathcal{A}\setminus\{a\})^{*}
for every
i
.
Let
v^{\prime}=v_{1}av_{2}\cdots av_{m}
.
We have
v=\alpha_{a,b}(v^{\prime})
.
Consequently,
w=v^{\prime d}
, and thus, by primitivity of
w
,
d=1
.

Now suppose that
b\notin\mathcal{A}
.
Let
u=v^{d}
for some primitive word
v\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
.
By the same argument as above, we can write

v=v_{1}abv_{2}ab\cdots v_{m}c
with
c\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\{a\}
and
v_{i}\in((\mathcal{A}\setminus\{a\})\cup\{b\})^{*}
.
Since in
u=\alpha_{a,b}(w)
every occurrence of
b
is, by construction, preceded by the letter
a
, we have
v_{i}\in(\mathcal{A}\setminus\{a\})^{*}
.
We then conclude as above.

(3)

The case
u=\tilde{\alpha}_{a,b}(w)
is proved in a symmetric way as in point (2).

(4)

The case
u=\iota_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}
is immediate.

∎

We now show that the property of being clustered is preserved by the morphisms defined in Section
4
.

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
w
be a primitive word over
\mathcal{A}=\{a_{1}<a_{2}<\ldots<a_{k}\}
.
Suppose
w
is
\pi
-clustering on
\mathcal{A}
for some permutation
\pi\in S_{\mathcal{A}}
.

(1)

If
\mu\in S_{\mathcal{A}}
, then
\mu(w)\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime*}
is
\pi^{\prime}
-clustering, with
\pi^{\prime}\in S_{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}
and
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}=\{\mu(a_{1})<\ldots<\mu(a_{k})\}
.

(2)

If
b=a_{1}
,
\pi^{-1}(a)=a_{i}
, and
\pi^{-1}(b)=a_{i+1}
for a certain
1\leq i<k
, then
\alpha_{a,b}(w)\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
is
\pi^{\prime}
-clustering, with
\pi^{\prime}\in S_{\mathcal{A}}
.

(3)

If
b=a_{k}
,
\pi^{-1}(b)=a_{i}
and
\pi^{-1}(a)=a_{i+1}
for a certain
1\leq i<k
, then
\alpha_{a,b}(w)\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
is
\pi^{\prime}
-clustering, with
\pi^{\prime}\in S_{\mathcal{A}}
.

(4)

If
\pi^{-1}(b)=a_{1}
,
a=a_{i}
and
b=a_{i+1}
for a certain
1\leq i<k
, then
\tilde{\alpha}_{a,b}(w)\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime*}
is
\pi^{\prime}
-clustering, with
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}=\{a_{i}<a_{1}<\ldots<a_{i-1}<a_{i+1}<\ldots<a_{k}\}
and
\pi^{\prime}\in S_{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}
.

(5)

If
\pi^{-1}(b)=a_{k}
,
b=a_{i}
and
a=a_{i+1}
for a certain

, then
\tilde{\alpha}_{a,b}(w)\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime*}
is
\pi^{\prime}
-clustering, with
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}=\{a_{1}<\ldots<a_{i}<a_{i+2}<\ldots<a_{k}<a_{i+1}\}
and
\pi^{\prime}\in S_{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}
.

(6)

If
b\notin\mathcal{A}
, then
\alpha_{a,b}(w)\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime*}
is
\pi^{\prime}
-clustering, with
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}=\{a_{1}<\ldots<a_{k}<b\}
and
\pi^{\prime}\in S_{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}
;
is it also
\pi^{\prime\prime}
-clustering with
\pi^{\prime\prime}\in S_{\mathcal{A}^{\prime\prime}}
when considered as word over
\mathcal{A}^{\prime\prime}=\{b<a_{1}<\ldots<a_{k}\}
.

(7)

If
\mathcal{B}=\{b_{1}<\ldots<b_{h}\}
is disjoint from
\mathcal{A}
, then
\iota_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}(w)\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime*}
is
\pi^{\prime}
-clustering, with
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}=\{a_{1}<\ldots<a_{k}<b_{1}<\ldots<b_{h}\}
and
\pi^{\prime}\in S_{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

We proceed by addressing each case separately.

(1)

The permutation
\mu
sends each letter
a_{i}
to
\mu(a_{i})
.
Since
w
is
\pi
-clustering,
{\rm bwt}_{\mathcal{A}}\left(w\right)
consists of contiguous runs - possibly of length
0
- of each letter of
\mathcal{A}
.
Since an application of
\mu
to

simply amounts to letter renaming, we immediately obtain clustering of
\mu(w)
.
Defining
\pi^{\prime}=\mu\circ\pi\circ\mu^{-1}\in S_{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}

is
\pi^{\prime}
-clustering.

(2)

Define
\pi^{\prime}\in S_{\mathcal{A}}
by modifying
\pi
such that
\pi^{-1}(a)
and
\pi^{-1}(b)
are adjacent in the cycle.
If
\pi^{-1}(a)=a_{i}
and
\pi^{-1}(b)=a_{i+1}

\pi(a)
and
\pi(b)
appear consecutively in

.
Replacing each

by

and
\pi(b)
into a single-run adjacency in

.
Thus,
\alpha_{a,b}(w)
is
\pi^{\prime}
-clustering.

(3)

This proof is symmetrical to that of the case (2).

(4)

Let
a=a_{i}
and
b=a_{i+1}
,
1\leq i\leq k
.
The letter
b
is the first letter of the permutation
\pi

The morphism

sends

We now reorder the alphabet, obtaining

Thus,
\tilde{\alpha}_{a,b}

\pi^{\prime}
-clustering.

(5)

This case is symmetric to that case (4).
In this case,

A reordered alphabet

(6)

Let

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}=\mathcal{A}\cup\{b\}

After replacing every

Thus,

is
\pi^{\prime}
-clustering on both extended alphabets.

(7)

We note that
\iota_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}(w)=w
.
No new letters appear in

This keeps the clustering unaltered, hence

Example 6.6

Let

As seen in Example

7.
Future Work

We briefly indicate two directions suggested by this work.
A first goal concerns local commutation of the morphisms in extended branching Rauzy induction.
Given the multiple choices of morphisms at each induction step, it would be valuable to identify a small set of local commutations (e.g., splitting on a periodic component, then a left Rauzy step on a minimal component is the same as doing so in the opposite order) that aid in understanding when segments of induction paths commute.
A natural problem is to identify other such local relations in the monoid generated by
\{\lambda,\rho,\sigma\}
.

A second direction is to explicitly extend the construction of Gjerde and Johansen in
[
19
]
beyond the minimal case.
In particular, the authors of this work constructed a partition in towers corresponding to the Cantor version of any minimal standard interval exchange transformation.
The incidence matrices of the corresponding Bratteli diagram were products of morphism matrices corresponding to individual right Rauzy steps (see also
[
12
]
for a review of the results of
[
19
]
from this perspective).
However, traditional right Rauzy induction is not applicable to general standard IETs.
Each merging and splitting step induces a matrix, and thus extended branching Rauzy induction could enable the explicit construction of Bratteli diagrams (and the corresponding Bratteli-Vershik models) from the Cantor system with multiple minimal components arising from an IET.
This would also provide a convenient method of recovering the associated dimension groups.
