Hydrophobic sulfur vacancy rich MoS2 encapsulation of lead-free halide perovskites for aqueous catalysis
Authors: Mohasin Tarek, Ferdous Yasmeen, MA Basith
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHalide perovskites are promising for aqueous pollutant remediation but are hindered by rapid degradation in water. Here, we engineer hydrophobically encapsulated CsSnBr3–MoS2 (CSB-MS) heterojunctions, in which sulfur-vacancy-rich MoS2 nanosheets conformally wrap CsSnBr3 (CSB) nanocrystals to form a robust n–p interface that simultaneously blocks moisture ingress and enables efficient interfacial charge transfer. Sulfur vacancies promote defect-assisted charge separation and suppress Sn2+ oxidation, leading to significantly enhanced aqueous stability. The optimized hybrid achieves 98% methylene blue and 94% levofloxacin degradation, with apparent photon efficiencies of 35.5% and 25.5%, respectively, and shows further enhancement under photo-electrocatalytic conditions. A type-II band alignment prolongs carrier lifetimes (∼70%) and reduces reaction barriers, while post-catalysis analyses confirm structural integrity after repeated cycles. These results establish a versatile interfacial design strategy for stabilizing lead-free perovskites, advancing their application in sustainable water treatment.Keywords: MoS2 nanosheets, hydrophobic n–p heterojunction sulfur vacancy, apparent quantum yield, activation energy

Introduction

The widespread release of dyes and pharmaceutical residues into aquatic systems poses serious ecological and public health risks due to their persistence and resistance to conventional treatment.1,2 Consequently, solar-driven and biased assisted advanced oxidation processes have gained attention as sustainable strategies for degrading such pollutants, contingent on catalysts that maintain high activity and durability under realistic water conditions.2,3,4,5,6,7

Metal halide perovskites are attractive photocatalysts owing to their strong light absorption, high carrier mobility, and tunable electronic structures, but their practical use in water is severely limited by moisture-induced degradation.8,9,10,11 Among lead-free candidates, cesium tin bromide (CsSnBr3) offers a narrow bandgap and favorable charge transport but remains highly susceptible to hydrolysis and Sn2+ oxidation.12,13,14 Water penetration disrupts metal-halide bonding, accelerates ion migration, and oxidizes Sn2+ to Sn4+, leading to rapid structural collapse and loss of activity.11,13,14 Because aqueous photocatalysis requires direct catalyst-water contact, conventional encapsulation strategies remain largely ineffective.15,16,17,18

Aqueous instability thus represents the key barrier to halide perovskite-based catalysis. Stabilization approaches include lattice engineering (anion/cation tuning, mixed halides, or heterovalent doping), surface passivation (organic ligands, polymers), and shell encapsulation using inorganic oxides.12,13,15 These strategies suppress water ingress and ion migration but often compromise charge extraction or redox kinetics through insulating or electronically mismatched layers.19,20,21,22,23 This trade-off between stability and activity limits radical generation and slows pollutant degradation. Accordingly, current research is shifting toward interfacial engineering, where the protective component actively mediates charge transport-enabling durable, efficient photocatalysis under aqueous conditions.

Two-dimensional MoS2 is particularly attractive as an interfacial partner because its layered structure can form conformal, nanoscale contact with perovskite domains while offering three complementary functions in water: (i) hydrophobic shielding on basal planes that reduces water adsorption and slows hydrolysis/oxidation, (ii) electronic coupling/transport that can extract or shuttle photocarriers away from the perovskite, and (iii) catalytic site density concentrated at edges and defects that participate directly in redox chemistry.24,25,26,27,28,29 In semiconducting MoS2, sulfur vacancies (VS) are among the most influential defect motifs30: they introduce localized mid-gap/near-band electronic states, modify carrier density and band bending at interfaces, and can strengthen adsorption of oxygenated intermediates that govern reactive oxygen species (ROS) formation.31,32 In heterojunction architectures, VS can therefore enhance both the thermodynamics (via improved band alignment and built-in fields) and the kinetics (via faster charge transfer and higher density of reactive adsorption sites) of aqueous pollutant degradation.33 Importantly, when MoS2 acts as a conformal shell rather than a discrete additive, it can function as a moisture barrier while remaining sufficiently conductive/defect-active to sustain interfacial electron transfer—turning the interface from a passive “coat” into a catalytically active, charge-mediating domain.34,35

Building on these interfacial advantages, coupling photocatalysis with photo-electrocatalysis further improves pollutant degradation by combining light absorption with bias-assisted charge extraction.36,37,38,39 An external bias drives directional carrier separation, enhances electron-hole utilization, and accelerates the generation of ROS, thereby overcoming the recombination and diffusion limitations often observed in standalone photocatalysis.36,37 This dual-mode operation requires materials that maintain stability under both illumination and electrochemical bias—conditions that are particularly challenging for moisture-sensitive halide perovskites. Designing heterostructures that integrate efficient charge separation with environmental resilience thus represents a key frontier in the development of next-generation catalysts for water purification.

In this work, we report hydrophobically encapsulated halide perovskite-MoS2 heterojunctions designed for dual-mode (photo- and photo-electro) catalysis in aqueous pollutant degradation. Methylene blue was used as a model dye, while colorless levofloxacin served as a representative antibiotic to eliminate dye-sensitized contributions, since photoexcited dyes can inject electrons into a semiconductor conduction band, as in dye-sensitized solar cells. Few-layer, sulfur-vacancy-rich MoS2 nanosheets conformally encapsulate CsSnBr3 (CSB) nanocrystals, forming an intimate n–p heterojunction that combines hydrophobic protection with efficient interfacial charge transfer. The MoS2 shell suppresses moisture ingress and Sn2+ oxidation, while sulfur vacancies enable defect-assisted charge separation and surface redox reactions, transforming MoS2 from a passive coating into an active catalytic and electronic component. Together, hydrophobic encapsulation, defect engineering, and heterojunction-driven charge separation establish a generalizable strategy for stabilizing lead-free halide perovskites in water while maintaining high catalytic activity.

Results and discussion

Structural, morphological and chemical states and optical characterization

The CSB-MS nano-heterojunctions were synthesized via a liquid-phase hot-injection strategy, enabling intimate coupling between hydrophilic CSB nanoparticles and hydrophobic few-layer MoS2 nanosheets.40 As schematically illustrated in Figure 1, the MoS2 nanosheets encapsulate the perovskite core, forming a hierarchical heterointerface that performs a dual function: it acts as a physical hydrophobic barrier to moisture and oxygen ingress, and simultaneously serves as a catalytic and electronic bridge facilitating efficient charge transfer. This design embodies a functional materials paradigm, where interfacial chemical engineering replaces conventional surface passivation to address the long-standing instability of tin halide perovskites in aqueous environments.

Schematic illustration of the liquid-phase hot-injection synthesis strategy for moisture resistance CSB-MS nano-hybrids

MoS2 nanosheets are subsequently integrated with CsSnBr3 (CSB) at weight ratios of 95:5 and 90:10, forming CSB-MS(5) and CSB-MS(10), respectively.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) spectra of pristine CSB (Figure 2A) exhibits the characteristic reflections of a cubic perovskite phase (space group Pm-3m), demonstrating high crystallinity and phase purity.13,14 After hybridization (Figure 2B), all dominant diffraction peaks of CSB remain unaltered, confirming that the perovskite backbone persists without distortion. The emergence of a low-intensity feature at approximately 14° (2θ), corresponding to the (0 0 2) plane of MoS2, provides compelling evidence for the successful integration of exfoliated nanosheets.41,42 The broad nature of this reflection indicates the ultrathin morphology and limited stacking order of the MoS2 layers, consistent with few-layered nanosheet incorporation rather than bulk aggregation.43

The FESEM images in Figures 2C–2F highlight the contrasting moisture stability of pristine CSB and hydrophobic MoS2 incorporated CSB-MS(5) nano-hybrids before and after immersion in water. Pristine CSB (Figure 2C) shows smooth, quasi-spherical nanoparticles with an average size of 252 nm (Figure S6A), but after immersion in water (Figure 2D), severe surface degradation, aggregation, and fragmentation occur due to hydrolysis and oxidation of Sn2+ to Sn4+, confirming its strong moisture sensitivity. In sharp contrast, the CSB-MS(5) hybrid displays remarkable morphological preservation even after prolonged exposure to water. Before immersion (Figure 2E), the hybrid shows well-dispersed nanoparticles (85 nm, Figure S6B) conformally wrapped by wrinkled few-layer MoS2 sheets, forming a compact and electronically coupled heterointerface. After immersion (Figure 2F), the nanostructure remains intact with only a minor increase in average particle size to 105 nm (Figure S6C), reflecting minimal agglomeration and negligible structural decay.

The improved stability originates from the hydrophobic nature of 2D MoS2,44,45,46,47 which acts as a barrier to moisture ingress, and from sulfur vacancies (Figure 4F) that promote charge delocalization and interfacial electronic coupling. These effects suppress the oxidation of Sn2+, thereby preserving the structural and optoelectronic integrity of the material.

EDX spectra (Figure S6D) and quantitative data (Table S1) confirm the expected stoichiometry of the composite, while elemental mapping (Figure S7) reveals a homogeneous spatial distribution of Cs, Sn, Br, Mo, and S throughout the hybrid, verifying uniform composition and consistent hybridization.

The optical response of the nano-hybrid reveals how this interfacial engineering alters light-matter interactions. UV-visible absorption spectra (inset of Figure 3A) show that the CSB-MS(5) sample exhibits a pronounced enhancement in absorption intensity compared to pristine CSB, particularly across the visible region. Tauc plot analysis (Equation 1)41,48,49 (Figure 3A) indicates a bandgap narrowing from 2.94 eV to 2.68 eV, consistent with the formation of hybridized electronic states at the CSB-MS junction. This redshift extends the absorption edge, effectively broadening solar spectral utilization and promoting visible-light-driven photocatalysis. The subsequent photoluminescence (PL) spectra (Figure 3B) reveal a strong emission peak for pristine CSB, indicative of rapid electron-hole recombination, whereas the CSB-MS(5) hybrid displays significant PL quenching. This suppression underscores the establishment of an efficient charge transfer channel between the perovskite and MoS2 domains, where photogenerated electrons migrate toward the MoS2 conduction band, thereby prolonging charge carrier lifetimes.

Electrochemical Mott-Schottky (Equation 2) measurements41,50 (Figure 3C) provide further insight into this charge transport behavior. The positive slope for MoS2 and negative slopes for CSB and CSB-MS(5) confirm the p-type and n-type characteristics, respectively, consistent with their expected semiconducting nature. The hybrid system exhibits a more negative flat-band potential (larger x-intercept, Equation 3), signifying an increase in carrier density and improved charge transfer efficiency. The resulting band alignment (Equations 4, 5, and 6) diagram (Figure 3D) reveals a favorable type-II heterojunction configuration, in which electrons flow spontaneously from CSB to MoS2, while holes move in the opposite direction—thereby minimizing recombination losses and enhancing redox kinetics. This interfacial band bending is further validated by valence-band XPS measurements (Figure 3E), where the valence band maximum (VBM) of CSB-MS(5) shifts downward to 2.22 eV, consistent with improved band alignment for photocatalytic activity.

The temporal evolution of charge dynamics, probed by time-resolved PL (Figures 3F and 3G), corroborates this efficient separation. The average carrier lifetime (Equation 7) increases from 2.16 ns in pristine CSB to 3.67 ns in CSB-MS(5), demonstrating that MoS2 serves as an electron sink that stabilizes photogenerated carriers and suppresses radiative recombination. Such prolongation of carrier lifetime is crucial for enabling multi-electron redox reactions required in pollutant degradation processes. FTIR spectra (Figure 3H) further reinforce the hydrophobic shielding effect of MoS2: characteristic Sn–Br and Cs–Br vibrational bands remain intact, while O–H stretching features associated with molecular and adsorbed water are substantially attenuated in the hybrid. This observation directly evidences reduced water adsorption and improved environmental durability imparted by the MoS2 wrapping. Raman spectroscopy (Figure 3I) identifies the E2g1 (∼383 cm−1) and A1g (∼409 cm−1) modes typical of the 2H-MoS2 phase, with a peak separation of ∼26 cm−1, confirming the presence of few-layer MoS2 sheets and the retention of their crystalline integrity.

The narrow band XPS analysis (Figures 4A–4F) elucidates the chemical environment and oxidation states within the CSB-MS(5) heterostructure. The survey spectrum confirms (Figure S8) the presence of Cs, Sn, Br, Mo, and S elements, devoid of extraneous signals, indicating high chemical purity. In the high-resolution Sn 3d spectrum (Figure 4B), the two peaks at 487.0 eV (3d5/2) and 495.4 eV (3d3/2) correspond to Sn2+ species with a spin-orbit splitting of 8.4 eV—typical of stable Sn2+ in halide perovskites.13,14 Importantly, no additional peaks attributable to Sn4+ are detected, confirming that the MoS2 encapsulation effectively inhibits the oxidation of tin under ambient conditions. The Mo 3d, Mo 3p, and S 2p spectra (Figures 4D–4F) reveal the coexistence of Mo4+ and minor Mo6+ components, along with distinct signals for S2− and a subtle shoulder at 159 eV associated with sulfur vacancies.51,52 These vacancies act as electron localization centers that facilitate catalytic reactions by promoting interfacial charge trapping and transfer.53,54,55 Collectively, the spectroscopic evidence consolidates the understanding that sulfur-vacancy-rich MoS2 not only shields CSB from moisture but also enhances its electronic activity through defect-mediated charge redistribution.

Photocatalytic performances

The photocatalytic activity of the synthesized materials was first optimized by varying catalyst dosage (10–20 mg) for MB and LFX degradation under solar light (Figures S9 and S10). As shown in ESI Figure S10, degradation efficiency increased with catalyst mass up to 15 mg, beyond which it plateaued—likely due to excess catalyst causing light scattering and reduced photon penetration. Accordingly, 15 mg was selected as the optimal dosage for subsequent studies. Time-resolved UV-Vis spectra (ESI Figure S11) display a consistent reduction in MB (∼664 nm) and LFX (∼287 nm) absorbance, confirming pollutant degradation. CSB-MS(5) achieved ∼98% MB degradation (Equation 8) in 120 min (Figure 5A), outperforming pristine CSB (∼80% in 150 min). For LFX, CSB-MS(5) reached 94% degradation (Figure 5B), compared to 70% for CSB. Corresponding pseudo-first-order kinetics (Figures 5C and 5D; Equation 9) reveal significantly higher reaction rate constants for CSB-MS(5), validating its superior photocatalytic performance. This enhancement is attributed to several synergistic factors: MoS2 nanosheets act as electron sinks, extracting photogenerated electrons from the CSB conduction band and suppressing charge recombination, while CSB ensures strong solar absorption and efficient photoexcitation. Furthermore, the formation of a type-II heterojunction between CSB and MoS256 promotes effective interfacial charge separation, prolonging charge-carrier lifetimes and boosting surface redox activity.

Quantitative evaluation via apparent photon efficiency57 supports these findings. As shown in Figure 5E, CSB-MS(5) exhibits apparent photon efficiency (Equation 10) values of 35.5% for MB and 25.5% for LFX—substantially higher than those of pristine CSB—demonstrating efficient utilization of photogenerated carriers. This performance is further corroborated by the comparative analysis presented in Table S2, where CSB-MS(5) demonstrates competitive or higher efficiency than state-of-the-art photocatalysts under similar conditions. Additionally, in Figure 5F, the activation energy (Equation 11)57 for LFX degradation decreased from 18.93 to 14.61 kJ mol−1 with the presence of CSB-MS(5) nano-hybrid (details in Figures S12 and S13), indicating reduced reaction barriers due to improved charge mobility and stabilization of reactive intermediates. Scavenger experiments57 (Figure 5G) identified electrons as the dominant reactive species, with holes and ROS contributing secondarily (absorbance spectra: Figure S14). This confirms a multi-pathway degradation process, primarily driven by efficient charge separation in the CSB-MS(5) nano-hybrid. To evaluate operational durability, recyclability tests were conducted using LFX (Figure S15). As shown in Figure 5H, CSB-MS(5) retained ∼90% of its initial activity after four cycles. This stability is credited to the MoS2 shell, which safeguards the perovskite core against aqueous degradation and photochemical instability.

To further enhance performance, photo-electrocatalytic (PEC) activity was assessed by evaluating LFX degradation under three conditions: (i) photocatalysis under light irradiation, (ii) electrocatalysis under applied bias in the dark, and (iii) combined PEC under simultaneous light irradiation and applied bias. As shown in Figure 5I, the CSB-MS(5) nano-hybrid achieved its highest degradation efficiency under PEC conditions (UV-Vis absorbance spectra: Figure S16). This improvement arises from the synergistic action of photogenerated charge carriers and externally injected electrons, which collectively enhance interfacial charge separation and accelerate radical generation. A comparative assessment (Table S3) highlights that the CSB-MS(5) heterojunction exhibits superior photocatalytic and photo-electrocatalytic efficiencies for both dye and pharmaceutical pollutant degradation compared to other reported halide and halide-perovskite-based heterostructures, underscoring its excellent stability and activity under aqueous conditions.

Post-photocatalytic characterization

Post-operational analyses (Figure 6) unequivocally demonstrate the morphological and chemical resilience of the CSB-MS(5) nano-hybrid after four consecutive photocatalytic cycles. The FESEM image (Figure 6A) shows a uniform, crack-free surface with no evidence of aggregation or etching, indicating exceptional resistance to photocorrosion and morphological degradation. This preservation arises from the encapsulation of CSB nanoparticles within the few-layer MoS2 matrix, which forms a conductive, hydrophobic, and chemically inert shell that effectively inhibits oxygen and moisture penetration, thereby sustaining the crystal integrity and interfacial stability that typically limit tin halide perovskite durability. The post-cycling XRD analysis (Figure 6B) reveals that the characteristic diffraction peaks of the CSB-MS(5) nano-hybrid remain unchanged, indicating structural stability and preservation of the crystal phase after repeated catalytic operation. Consistent with this, XPS analysis (Figures 6C, S17, and S18) reveals that the Sn 3d5/2 and 3d3/2 peaks at ∼487.0 and ∼495.4 eV, respectively, remain unchanged after repeated photocatalysis, with no Sn4+ signatures detected—confirming that the MoS2 layer efficiently prevents Sn2+ oxidation. This chemical invariance highlights MoS2’s dual function as both an electron mediator and a protective stabilizer. The Mo 3d spectra (Figure S18C) further illustrate the hybrid’s redox stability, showing only a slight increase in Mo6+ species (from 3.59% to 7.68%) and minor reductions in Mo4+ and Mo–S fractions after cycling, implying limited surface oxidation caused by transient interactions with ROS while preserving the dominant semiconducting Mo4+ state. Complementary S 2p spectra (Figure 6D) display marginal oxidation of S2− to S6+ yet retain nearly constant sulfur-vacancy content (∼21%), indicating that the defect-assisted charge-transfer pathways remain active and structurally stable. These subtle redox adjustments suggest a self-regulating equilibrium within MoS2, enabling continuous catalytic functionality without compromising the interfacial structure. Supporting this conclusion, FTIR spectra (Figure 6E) show stable Sn–Br, Cs–Br, and Mo–S vibrations with negligible change in O–H stretching bands, confirming low water adsorption and sustained hydrophobicity, while UV-vis spectra (Figure 6F) before and after catalysis remain nearly identical, evidencing preserved optical absorption and electronic structure. Collectively, these results confirm that CSB-MS(5) maintains its morphological, chemical, and optical integrity under repeated operation, underscoring its potential as a moisture-stable and long-lived lead-free perovskite heterojunction for solar-driven environmental catalysis.

Mechanistic pathways

The mechanistic pathways underlying the superior performance of the CSB-MS(5) heterojunction catalyst are illustrated in Figure 7, which elucidates the synergistic role of hydrophobic encapsulation, sulfur-vacancy-induced defect engineering, and heterojunction-mediated charge separation during photocatalytic and photo-electrocatalytic degradation. As shown in Figure 7A, the sulfur-vacancy-rich MoS2 nanosheets conformally encapsulate the CSB nanocrystals, forming an intimate n–p heterojunction interface that simultaneously provides a hydrophobic barrier and an electronic coupling channel. The basal planes of MoS2 impart water-repelling characteristics that effectively shield the moisture-sensitive CSB core from hydrolysis and oxidation, while the sulfur vacancies introduce localized defect states that act as electron reservoirs, redistributing charge carriers across the interface. This dual protection mechanism not only mitigates the oxidation of Sn2+ to Sn4+ but also stabilizes the perovskite lattice under aqueous operation, thereby maintaining its structural and functional integrity over prolonged use.

For solar-driven pollutant removal, the CSB-MS(5) composite forms a favorable staggered (type-II) heterojunction through alignment of the energy bands of CSB (n-type) and MoS2 (defect-mediated p-type) (Figure 3C). When combined in a 95:5 ratio, the Fermi levels equilibrate, leading to intermediate band edge positions Figure 7B). Valence-band XPS and optical analyses reveal a valence band maximum (VBM) of +2.223 V and a conduction band minimum (CBM) of −0.457 V versus the normal hydrogen electrode (NHE), corresponding to a band gap of 2.68 eV. This downward shift of both VBM and CBM relative to pristine CSB is attributed to interfacial electronic interactions and band bending at the n–p junction. Incorporation of MoS2 renders the CBM more negative (−0.457 V) than that of pure CSB and slightly raises the Fermi level of CSB via charge transfer, creating a configuration favorable for photocatalysis. The band-edge position and solar-driven photocatalytic mechanism of LFX degradation are schematically shown in Figure 7B.

This band alignment is ideal for photocatalysis. The CBM of MoS2 (−0.496 V) is sufficiently negative to thermodynamically reduce molecular oxygen to superoxide: O2/O2·− (≈−0.33Vvs.NHE). Meanwhile, the VBM of CSB (+2.833 V) exceeds the oxidation potentials for H2O/·OH (≈+2.27V) and OH−/·OH (≈+1.99V), enabling oxidation to hydroxyl radicals.58,59

Upon illumination with above-band-gap light, electron-hole pairs are generated and separated through the type-II band alignment. Light absorption is dominated by CSB, while MoS2 functions as a sensitizer and charge-transfer mediator. Photoexcited electrons in the CBM of CSB transfer to the lower-energy CBM of MoS2, whereas the photogenerated holes remain in CSB due to its deeper VBM. In the CSB-MS junction, Fermi-level equilibration (EF,n = EF,p) occurs via electron transfer from CSB to MoS2, inducing upward band bending in CSB and downward band bending in MoS2, thereby establishing a built-in potential: qVbi = Φp − Φn > 0. Consequently, electrons migrate from CBMCSB to CBMMoS2 and reduce O2 to O2·−, while holes accumulate in VBMCSB to oxidize H2O or OH− to ·OH. Charge separation is further enhanced by the depletion region formed at the CSB–MS junction. The space-charge region satisfies Poisson’s equation and creates an internal electric field from CSB to MoS2 that drives electrons toward MoS2 and holes toward CSB, thereby reducing radiative recombination. The radiative recombination rate is expressed as: Rrad = Bnp, where B is the bimolecular recombination coefficient. Because carrier separation lowers the product np in any single region, the photoluminescence intensity (IPL α Rrad) decreases, as reflected in the pronounced PL quenching observed for CSB-MS(5) compared to pristine CSB.

The TRPL measurements indicate that carrier lifetimes extend from ∼2.16 ns for pristine CSB to ∼3.67 ns for CSB-MS(5), representing a ∼70% increase. This prolonged carrier survival facilitates surface redox reactions and enhances photocatalytic degradation efficiency. The generated O2·− and ·OH radicals are highly oxidative, enabling non-selective degradation of MB and LFX through demethylation, ring opening, and mineralization:

Additionally, MB can undergo dye-sensitized photodegradation via photosensitization, transferring excited-state energy to the photocatalyst, whereas LFX degradation relies solely on the catalyst’s intrinsic activity.

In the PEC configuration (Figure 7C), an applied bias of 1.8 V was identified as the optimal operating potential, as it produced a stable and sustained photocurrent response. The application of a 1.8 V DC bias facilitates anodic water oxidation, H2O − > [1.8 V]OH· + H+ + e− generating hydroxyl radicals capable of attacking aromatic rings and functional groups in LFX. Direct electron transfer oxidation may also occur: LFX −> LFX·+ + e−. The resulting radical cations undergo hydrolysis, oxidation, and radical-mediated cleavage, producing low-molecular-weight acids and ultimately CO2 and H2O.

To further clarify the mechanism of photo-electrocatalytic enhancement, additional control electrochemical experiments such as cyclic voltammetry (CV), linear sweep voltammetry (LSV), and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), were performed under both dark and light illumination conditions. The results are now presented in Figure S19 of the Supporting Information. The electrochemical analyses demonstrate the enhanced photo-electrochemical performance of the CSB-MS(5)––CSB-MS(5) symmetric cell under light irradiation. The CV and LSV measurements (Figures S19A and S19B) show enhanced current and photocurrent densities under illumination, indicating improved charge separation and charge transfer efficiency due to light activation. Furthermore, the EIS Nyquist plots (Figure S19C) reveal a decrease in charge transfer resistance (Rct) under illumination, confirming enhanced interfacial charge conductivity and photoresponse behavior.

Limitations of the study

This work validates the aqueous stability and catalytic activity of MoS2-encapsulated CsSnBr3, but long-term operation under variable pH or real wastewater conditions was not assessed. Sulfur-vacancy concentrations were inferred from spectroscopy without direct atomic-scale quantification, and no in situ techniques were used to monitor reaction intermediates. Additionally, density functional theory calculations were not performed, which could further elucidate defect energetics and interfacial charge transfer.

Method details

Chemicals

All chemicals used in this study were of analytical grade and used without further purification. Cesium carbonate (Cs2CO3, 99.9%), tin(II) bromide (SnBr2), oleic acid (OA, 90%), oleylamine (OAm, 70%), and 1-octadecene (ODE, 90%) were procured from Sigma-Aldrich. Other solvents and reagents included n-hexane, ethyl acetate (EtOAc), isopropanol (IPA), ethanol, acetone, and N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP), all of research grade. Additional compounds used included molybdenum disulfide (MoS2, 99%), sodium sulfate (Na2SO4), poly(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF), methylene blue, levofloxacin (LFX), acrylamide, potassium dichromate (K2Cr2O7), and disodium ethylenediaminetetraacetate (EDTA–2Na).

Synthesis of cesium oleate

Initially, cesium oleate, the cesium precursor, was prepared by dissolving 0.407 g of Cs2CO3 in 20 mL of 1-octadecene (ODE) along with 1.3 mL of oleic acid (OA) (Figure S1). This mixture was stirred in a three-neck round-bottom flask under vacuum at 120 °C for 1 h to eliminate moisture, oxygen, and other reactive gas contaminants. Subsequently, the system was purged with argon (Ar) gas until the solution became optically clear, indicating complete dissolution of the cesium salt. The resulting cesium oleate solution was maintained under continuous Ar flow and stored for further use.

Synthesis of CsSnBr3 nanoparticles

A hot injection technique was utilized to synthesize nanostructured CsSnBr (CSB).13 To synthesize CsSnBr3 (CSB) nanoparticles3, 0.276 g of SnBr2 was added to 30 mL of ODE in a separate three-neck flask (Figure S1). The mixture was stirred at 120 °C under vacuum for 1 h to ensure complete mixing and removal of volatile species. The system was then switched to an Ar atmosphere, and coordinating ligands, oleic acid (OA) and oleylamine (OAm), were rapidly injected. After a 30-min degassing step under vacuum, the temperature was gradually increased to 160 °C. Once stabilized, preheated cesium oleate (maintained at 70 °C for 1 h) was swiftly injected using a syringe. The reaction was allowed to proceed for a controlled duration and was subsequently quenched by immersing the flask in an ice-water bath to rapidly cool the mixture to room temperature.

Purification of CsSnBr3 nanoparticles

The as-synthesized CSB nanoparticles were purified using a solvent–antisolvent precipitation method. The crude colloidal solution was mixed with ethyl acetate (EtOAc) as an antisolvent and centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 5 min. The resulting precipitate was collected and redispersed in a 1:2 mixture of n-hexane and EtOAc, followed by another centrifugation step under identical conditions. The precipitate was further washed with 5 mL of n-hexane and centrifuged at 8500 rpm for 5 min. Finally, the purified nanoparticles were dried in an oven at 120 °C for 8 h to obtain the final product.

Synthesis of MoS2 nanosheets

Few-layer MoS2 nanosheets were synthesized via an ultrasonication-assisted liquid-phase exfoliation method (Figure S2).41,42 Initially, 500 mg of bulk MoS2 powder was dispersed in 100 mL of isopropanol to form a homogeneous suspension. The dispersion was subjected to bath ultrasonication at a power of 400 W and frequency of 46 kHz for 4 h at 50 °C to facilitate efficient exfoliation into few-layer nanosheets. After sonication, the suspension was allowed to stand undisturbed for 4 h to enable sedimentation of unexfoliated or thicker particles. The upper clear supernatant, enriched with exfoliated MoS2 nanosheets, was carefully collected and dried in an oven at 120 °C for 12 h. The yield of exfoliated MoS2 was approximately 70%, calculated based on the mass of the final dried product relative to the initial bulk material.

Synthesis of CsSnBr3–MoS2 (CSB–MS) nano-hybrids

CSB-MS nanocomposites were synthesized using a modified liquid-phase hot-injection approach (Figure 1), where MoS2 nanosheets were incorporated post-nucleation. A stoichiometric amount of 0.276 g SnBr2 was dissolved in 30 mL of ODE and heated to 120 °C under vacuum for 1 h to ensure complete solubilization and degassing. The system was then purged with Ar gas, followed by rapid injection of oleic acid (OA) and oleylamine (OAm) ligands. After an additional 30-min degassing step, the temperature was raised to 160 °C under an Ar atmosphere. Preheated cesium oleate (70 °C, 1 h) was swiftly injected to initiate nucleation and growth of CSB nanoparticles. The reaction proceeded for approximately 5 min before being quenched in an ice-water bath.

Subsequently, the reaction mixture was reheated to 70 °C, and exfoliated MoS2 nanosheets were introduced under atmospheric conditions. The dispersion was stirred for 30 min at 70 °C, followed by quenching in an ice-water bath. The product was collected by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 10 min. The precipitate was washed twice with EtOAc, redispersed in n-hexane, and finally dried in an oven at 100 °C for 12 h to obtain the CSB–MS nano-hybrid material.

Characterization techniques

The crystallographic structure was determined via X-ray diffraction (XRD) (Rigaku SmartLab, Cu Kα). Surface morphology and microstructure were examined using field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) (GeminiSEM 360, Zeiss), while elemental composition was analyzed via energy dispersive X-ray (EDX) (Bruker). Vibrational properties were characterized using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) (IRSpirit, Shimadzu) and Raman spectroscopy (macroRam, Horiba). Optical properties were assessed by UV–vis (UV-2600), PL (RF-6000), and time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) (Edinburgh Instruments). Photocatalytic activity was evaluated under simulated solar light (500 W Hg–Xe lamp, 100 mW cm−2). Electrochemical and photo-electrochemical tests were conducted using a PGSTAT302N workstation (Metrohm).

Electrode preparation

In the three-electrode configuration (Figure S3), a platinum wire was used as the counter electrode, while an Ag/AgCl electrode immersed in saturated KCl solution served as the reference electrode.60,61 The working electrode was fabricated by preparing a homogeneous slurry consisting of 30 mg (80 wt %) of the synthesized active material—either CSB nanoparticles or CSB–MS nano-hybrids—and 7.5 mg (20 wt %) of polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) as a binder. The mixture was dispersed in 300 μL of N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) and sonicated for 2 h to obtain a uniform slurry.

The resulting slurry was uniformly coated onto a graphite rod with an effective surface area of 0.28 cm2. The coated electrode was subsequently dried at 100 °C for 12 h to ensure complete solvent evaporation and proper binder activation, thereby completing the fabrication of the working electrode.

Photocatalytic and photo-electrocatalytic experimental setup

The photocatalytic degradation experiments were conducted using a solar-simulated photoreactor, as schematically depicted in Figure S4.41,57 Initially, a methylene blue (MB) solution was prepared by dissolving 1.2 mg of MB in 100 mL of distilled water. The UV–vis absorption spectrum of this solution was recorded using a UV–vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-2600, Japan) to determine the initial concentration. Subsequently, 15 mg of either CSB or CSB–MS nano-hybrid photocatalyst was added to 50 mL of the MB solution. The suspension was stirred in the dark for 1 h to establish adsorption-desorption equilibrium between the dye molecules and the catalyst surface.

The photocatalytic reaction was initiated by exposing the suspension to a 500 W Hg–Xn lamp with a solar-equivalent irradiance of 100 mW cm−2. After 30 min of illumination, 6 mL of the solution was withdrawn, centrifuged at 6500 rpm for 2 min, and the absorbance spectrum of the supernatant was measured to monitor dye degradation.

For pharmaceutical pollutant degradation, a similar procedure was followed. A 1 mg LFX solution in 100 mL of distilled water was prepared, and 15 mg of catalyst was dispersed in 50 mL of this solution. The remaining steps, including dark equilibration, solar irradiation, sampling, and analysis, were identical to those used in the MB degradation experiments.

To identify the active reactive oxygen species (ROS) involved in the photocatalytic process, trapping experiments were performed under solar irradiation. Four scavengers were employed: isopropanol (IPA) for hydroxyl radicals (•OH), acrylamide for superoxide radicals (⋅O2−), potassium dichromate (K2Cr2O7) for electrons (e−), and EDTA-2Na for photogenerated holes (h+). In each case, 1.5 mM of the scavenger was added prior to illumination, and the change in degradation efficiency was analyzed to identify the dominant ROS.

To further investigate the reaction kinetics, the activation energy (Ea) was determined. A 1 mg LFX solution in 100 mL distilled water was prepared, and 15 mg of catalyst was added to 50 mL of the solution. After dark equilibration, the suspension was irradiated under controlled temperatures using a solar simulator. Absorbance measurements were recorded after 30 min of illumination to evaluate the degradation rate at each temperature, enabling calculation of Ea using Arrhenius plots.

Photo-electrocatalytic degradation of pollutants

The experimental setup for photo-electrocatalytic degradation is schematically illustrated in Figure S5. The system integrates photocatalysis and electrochemical oxidation in a three-electrode photo-electrochemical (PEC) configuration to enhance degradation efficiency. CSB–MS nano-hybrids were used as both photo-anode and cathode materials, while an Ag/AgCl (saturated KCl) electrode served as the reference electrode.

To fabricate the electrodes, a uniform slurry of the nano-hybrid catalyst was coated onto two graphite rods (effective area ∼0.28 cm2) and dried at 100 °C for 12 h. The electrodes were arranged perpendicularly such that only the anode was illuminated, minimizing unintended photo-responses at the cathode.

For the degradation experiment, 150 mL of simulated pharmaceutical wastewater was prepared by dissolving 1.5 mg of LFX in deionized water along with 1.066 g of Na2SO4, resulting in a 0.1 M supporting electrolyte. The solution was stirred in the dark for 1 h to achieve adsorption-desorption equilibrium.

The photo-electrocatalytic reaction was initiated by illuminating the photo-anode using a 500 W Hg–Xn lamp (solar simulator) with an irradiance of 100 mW cm−2, while simultaneously applying a 1.8 V DC bias under chronoamperometric conditions. This dual excitation enhances charge separation and promotes the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) via redox reactions at the catalyst surface. After 30 min of operation, 6 mL of the solution was withdrawn, centrifuged to remove suspended particles, and analyzed using a Shimadzu UV-2600 UV–vis spectrophotometer by monitoring the absorbance at the characteristic λmax of LFX.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data analysis and graphical representations were performed using Origin 2018 (OriginLab Corporation, USA). Particle size distributions from FESEM micrographs were analyzed using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, USA), and crystal structure visualization was performed using VESTA. All equations used for the determination of the optical band gap, flat-band potential, carrier lifetime, photocatalytic degradation kinetics, apparent photon efficiency, and activation energy are described in the method details section.

Photocatalytic and photo-electrocatalytic degradation experiments were performed using three independent measurements (n = 3) under identical experimental conditions, where n represents the number of independent experiments. The results are presented as the mean values, and the error bars shown in Figures 5A–5D, 5G, and 5I represent the experimental uncertainty (±5%) obtained from the independent measurements. Statistical details and quantitative parameters are provided in the corresponding figures, figure legends, results section, or Supporting Information, where appropriate. Representative FESEM micrographs shown in Figures 2C–2F include 300 nm scale bars, as indicated in the figure caption.
