In vivo confocal microscopy findings about ocular surface recovery in patients following pterygium excision combined with conjunctival autograft
Authors: Mi Xu, YunZhi Shen, ZhengWei Zhang, HongJuan Sun, Song Sun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo study cellular and neural changes around the donor and recipient areas after pterygium excision combined with autologous conjunctival transplantation using corneal confocal microscopy, we evaluated the postoperative impact of this surgical procedure.MethodsPatients with primary pterygium located on the nasal side who visited the Department of Ophthalmology of Wuxi Second People’s Hospital between April and August 2023 were enrolled. Age-matched healthy eyes were included as the control group. IVCM was performed in the affected eyes, 6 months after pterygium excision. The images of the central conjunctival cells in the recipient and donor areas, the cells of each layer of the cornea around the recipient and donor area (around 2 mm from the nasal and upper corneal margins), and the structures of the corneal margins were recorded in the affected eyes and statistically compared with the corresponding areas in the control eyes.ResultsSeventeen eyes of 14 patients with primary pterygium located on the nasal side (mean 64 ± 8.74 years old), and 17 eyes of nine patients as age-matched normal controls were included. Compared with those of normal eyes, the density of corneal pterygoid epithelium around the recipient area (5104 ± 599 cells/mm2 vs. 5700 ± 515 cells/mm2, P < 0.01) and the central conjunctival epithelial cells in the recipient areas (3984 ± 453 cells/mm2 vs. 4834 ± 244 cells/mm2, P < 0.01) were significantly lower 6 months postoperatively. However, the superficial stromal, deep stromal, and endothelial cell densities around the recipient area exhibited no significant differences (P > 0.05). Highly illuminated basal cells were visible at the nasal corneal margin, with an irregular intersection of fibrous tissue with corneal epithelial cells. More dendritic Langerhans cell infiltration was seen in the junctional area. Compared with those in the normal eyes, the densities of the corneal pterygoid epithelium (4896 ± 428 cells/mm2 vs. 5557 ± 367 cells/mm2, P < 0.01), deep stromal cells (337 ± 65 cells/mm2 vs. 419 ± 39 cells/mm2, P < 0.01), endothelial cells (2305 ± 170 cells/mm2 vs. 2547 ± 212 cells/mm2, P < 0.01) around the donor area and the central conjunctival epithelial cells (3700 ± 446 cells/mm2 vs. 4282 ± 272 cells/mm2, P < 0.01) in the donor area were significantly lower 6 months postoperatively. However, no significant differences were observed in superficial stromal cell density (P > 0.05). A slight wavy interfacial intersection was visible on the superior corneal margin, with some of the highlighted basal cells visible.ConclusionsPterygium excision combined with autologous conjunctival transplantation with corneal stem cells can better promote the repair of most corneal cells around the recipient area and fence-like structures of the corneal rim 6 months postoperatively, but there may be some damage to the corneal conjunctival structures around the donor area.Keywords: Conjunctiva, Cornea, Limbus, In vivo confocal microscope, Ocular surface, Pterygium

Introduction

Pterygium is a common ocular surface disease characterized by the proliferation of fibrovascular overgrowth of the Tenon’s capsule and bulbar conjunctiva onto the cornea. Based on the surveys, the prevalence of pterygium in different parts of the world ranges from 1.3 in the Tehran Eye Study [1] to 53% in Taiwan [2]. Pterygium can disturb the tear film, leading to abnormal dry eye and tear dynamics [3], also linked to astigmatism [4] and visual impairment. The instability of the ocular surface environment is the primary mechanism of pterygium formation, which include the formation of proliferative limbal stromal cell clusters, epithelial metaplasia, the formation of active fibrovascular tissue, inflammation, and the disruption of Bowman’s layer along the infiltrating apex of the pterygium[5]. Surgical techniques are the preferred option to treat pterygium. Pterygium excision followed by limbal-conjunctival autograft has been confirmed to reduce pterygium recurrence for both primary and recurrent cases [6].

In vivo confocal microscopy (IVCM) is a novel, non-invasive, and rapid examination technique applied in ophthalmology clinics in recent years, with good inter-observer repeatability [7, 8]. IVCM in keratitis can magnify live cellular structures hundreds of times and offer high-resolution images of all layers of the cornea, including the epithelium [9], corneal nerve changes [10], and Langerhans cells and dendritic cells [11–13], whereas it can reveal the bulbar [14, 15], palpebral [16], and meibomian glands [17] in dry eye and meibomian gland dysfunction. Many research uses corneal confocal microscopy to explore the pathophysiology of pterygium, such as the investigation of preoperative corneal layers [18], Langerhans cells [19], subepithelial nerves [20], and pterygium activity [21]. However, the postoperative therapeutic effect of pterygium has been infrequently reported, especially regarding the recovery of nasal and superior corneal conjunctival cells, limbal structures, and the ocular surface.

This study was designed to observe the damage to the nasal and superior corneal conjunctival cells and fence-like structures of the corneal limbus in the eye 6 months postoperatively.

Materials and methods

All consecutive patients who visited the ophthalmology department of Wuxi Second People’s Hospital between April and August 2023 and were diagnosed with primary nasal pterygium, with 2–4 mm extension from the limbus onto the cornea, were enrolled in the study. They underwent pterygium excision combined with conjunctival autograft. We excluded patients with pseudopterygium, associated ocular surface inflammation such as chronic dacryocystitis, trachoma, blepharitis, systemic diseases such as diabetes, history of ophthalmic surgery, history of trauma, glaucoma, fundus disease, history of contact lenses, history of recent topical ophthalmic medication, IOP > 21 mmHg, ECD < 1800 cells/mm2 in the healthy eye, and those whose data could not be measured due to extenuating circumstances. Prior to surgery, comprehensive ophthalmic examinations were performed, including visual acuity, intraocular pressure, and slit-lamp examination. The postoperative follow-up period was extended to 6 months. Written informed consent was obtained from all the patients.

Surgical technique

A single surgeon performed all surgeries under local anesthesia, and the bulbar conjunctiva was incised longitudinally at the neck of the pterygium at the corneoscleral margin. The bulbar conjunctiva and pterygium tissue were separated with pterygium. The scleral surface was cleaned to create a scleral window of the appropriate size. A 4 × 5 mm bulbar conjunctival flap with corneal margin stem cells (0.5 mm from the corneal margin) was obtained from the upper part of the same eye. The free bulbar conjunctiva was grafted onto the nasal side of the scleral window. The conjunctiva on the side near the corneal margin was fixed and sutured to the nasal side of the corneal margin. The peripheral conjunctiva was sutured with a 10-0 nylon thread interrupted with scleral sutures (Fig. 1). After the procedure, the pterygium eye was coated with Demerol eye ointment and bandaged.

Postoperative evaluation

Six months postoperatively, the affected eye underwent anterior segment photography (Topcon SL-D7) and IVCM (HRT III in vivo confocal microscope) examination. Patients were also examined using a slit lamp at 6 months postoperatively to assess the healing process and the onset of pterygium recurrence.

The cellular and neural changes around the donor and recipient areas were examined using IVCM at 6 months to observe the recovery after pterygium excision combined with autologous conjunctival transplantation with circumlimbal condition, and assess the impact of the surgical procedure on postoperative recovery. The examination area included the central and surrounding areas (approximately 2 mm from the nasal and upper corneal margins) of the recipient and donor regions (Fig. 2).

Analysis

Rostock operating software was used to calculate the average density of corneal pterygoid epithelial cells, anterior stromal layer (stromal layer immediately adjacent to Bowman’s membrane) keratocytes, posterior stromal layer (stromal layer immediately adjacent to Descemet’s membrane), endothelial cells, and conjunctival epithelial cells. Three images were analyzed for each observation point, and the average value was recorded.

SPSS 20.0 statistical software was applied for analysis. The measurements were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and the data were evaluated for normality before comparing the differences. All measurements were normally distributed. An independent samples t-test was used to compare the cellular data of the corresponding sites in eyes with pterygium with those in normal eyes. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Results

General information

Fourteen cases and 17 eyes with primary pterygium were included, seven of whom (50%) were men and seven (50%) were women. Eleven eyes (65%) had monocular pterygium, and three (35%) had bilateral pterygium. The mean age of the patients was 64 ± 8.74 years (range, 46–76 years). All pterygia were located on the nasal side, and the heads of the pterygia had invaded the cornea by 2–4 mm. For the control group, 17 eyes of nine normal individuals, including four men and five women, with a mean age of 62 ± 12 years (range, 45–83 years), were selected. Six months postoperatively, the nasal side of the cornea was clear in nine cases (52%), cloudy opacity was visible in four (24%), and patchy opacity was visible in four (24%) (Fig. 3).

Recipient areas of the affected eye

Six months postoperatively, around the recipient areas, the corneal pterygium epithelium was normal in morphology, with stromal cells visible in the stroma, highly reflective nuclei, and normal endothelial cell morphology and number. Typical wavy Vogt fence-like structures were not observed at the nasal corneal limbus; however, highly reflective basal cells were visible. The fibrous tissue intersected irregularly with the corneal epithelium, with more dendritic Langerhans cells infiltrating the intersection area (Fig. 4). The central conjunctival epithelial cells in the recipient area were polygonal and well-organized, with clear, high-gloss cell borders, visible squamous epithelial metaplasia, and cup-shaped cells (Fig. 5).

Compared with those of normal eyes, the density of corneal pterygoid epithelium (5104 ± 599 cells/mm2 vs. 5700 ± 515 cells/mm2, P < 0.01) and conjunctival epithelial cells (3984 ± 453 cells/mm2 vs. 4834 ± 244 cells/mm2, P < 0.01) were significantly lower around the recipient areas 6 months postoperatively. However, no significant differences were observed in the superficial stromal (554 ± 71 cells/mm2 vs. 573 ± 146 cells/mm2, P > 0.05), deep stromal (404 ± 39 cells/mm2 vs. 417 ± 63 cells/mm2, P > 0.05), and endothelial (2445 ± 213 cells/mm2 vs. 2362 ± 156 cells/mm2, P > 0.05) cell densities (Table 1).

Donor areas of the affected eye

Six months postoperatively, around the donor areas, the corneal pterygium was normal in morphology, with stromal cells visible in the stromal layer with highly reflective nuclei and normal endothelial cells. Slight wavy fence-like structures were observed at the upper corneal limbus; however, some hyperreflective basal cells were visible (Fig. 6). The central conjunctival epithelial cells in the donor area were elongated in shape, well-organized, with clear and hyperreflective cell borders, visible squamous epithelial metaplasia, and cup-shaped cells (Fig. 7).

Discussion

Patients with pterygium have mild foreign body sensations and other discomfort. In severe cases, it may cause vision loss affecting quality of life. Its prevalence is common in areas with more exposure to ultraviolet radiation, which induces localized damage to the limbal stem cells and focal conjunctivalization of the cornea [22]. This operation has a certain recurrence rate, and limbal stem cell transplantation combined with pterygium excision is often used, which is considered the lowest recurrence rate [23]. To the best of our knowledge, no definite research exists on the degree and trend of recovery of corneal, conjunctival cells, and palisade structure of limbus cornea after pterygium surgery. Recurrence after pterygium excision mainly occurs within 6 months postoperatively [24], with approximately 84% of pterygium recurrences occurring three months after surgery [25], and approximately 97% of pterygium recurrences occurring within 12 months after surgery [25]. In this study, the patient was not examined at one month postoperatively, considering the incomplete wound recovery and the obvious ocular symptoms such as foreign body sensation in the patient. A relevant pre-test was performed at 3 months post-operatively and no significant positive results were seen. Therefore, the final choice was made for 6 months postoperatively.

In this study, the incidence of corneal pterygoid epithelium after pterygium excision was lower than that in normal individuals. It could be influenced by pterygium excision combined with conjunctival autograft transplantation with limbal stem cells, which affects the nasal and upper limbus, the centripetal proliferation of limbal stem cells, and leads to the reduction of nasal and upper corneal epithelial cells.

Our study revealed no significant difference in the density of superficial and deep corneal stromal cells between eyes with pterygium excision and eyes with normal nasal stromal cells. This might have been because the pterygium invaded the anterior elastic layer of the cornea and below, affecting stromal cell morphology and numbers, and because pterygium excision damaged the superficial stromal layer of the cornea. No significant numbers of Langerhans cells were observed in the confocal images of the nasal side of the cornea in this study, suggesting no significant inflammatory response and that the stromal cells on the nasal side may be less affected by inflammatory factors, with downregulation of matrix metalloproteinases and increased stromal cell density [26]. In contrast, the superficial stromal cells were not significantly different. At the same time, the deep stroma was reduced compared to that observed in normal eyes. The procedure damages the overlying corneal epithelium, prompting apoptosis of the overlying keratinized cells [27], thus reducing the stromal cell count.

Six months postoperatively, the density of endothelial cells on the nasal side of the affected eyes was not significantly different from that of normal people, whereas the upper endothelial cells were reduced compared to normal people, indicating that the pterygium and the upper endothelial cells were injured during the surgery. Although there are few studies on the changes in nasal and superior corneal endothelial cells after pterygium excision, several studies have shown different results regarding the preoperative changes in endothelial cells in eyes with pterygium. Li et al. [28] showed that the endothelial cells in patients with pterygium were fewer than those of the normal group before surgery. In contrast, Papadia et al. [29] reported no significant difference between the endothelial cells of pterygium patients and those of the normal group before surgery, mainly because ultraviolet radiation-induced the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as Interleukin-6 and Interleukin-8, which induced the activation of corneal immunity, promoted the activation of dendritic cells, and thus affected the changes in the number of pterygium endothelial cells.

Wang et al. [30] explored the imaging features of the corneas in patients with corneal limbal stem cell deficiency using IVCM. They concluded that the decrease in the number of central and limbal corneal epithelial cells was associated with corneal limbal stem cell deficiency. The more severe the corneal limbal stem cell deficiency condition, the more pronounced the decrease in central and limbal corneal epithelial cells. In this study, the corneal epithelial cells in the affected eyes, 2 mm from the nasal corneal limbus and 2 mm from the superior corneal limbus, were reduced compared to those in normal eyes 6 months postoperatively, suggesting the possibility of corneal limbal stem cell deficiency at the nasal and superior corneal limbus, resulting typical fenestrated structural changes. On confocal imaging, the typical wavy Vogt fenestrated structure was not seen at the nasal corneal limbus, but highlighted basal cells were visible. The fibrous tissue intersected irregularly with the corneal epithelium, and more dendritic Langerhans cell infiltration was seen in the intersection area; slight wavy fence-like structures were observed in the upper corneal margin, but some bright basal cells were visible, presumably because the upper fence-like structures were partially transplanted to the nasal side.

The present study found a reduction in nasal lateral conjunctival epithelial density compared to normal, which may be attributed to the following factors: first, pterygium surgery was performed to remove diseased nasal tissue, which damaged the epithelial cells of the nasal lateral conjunctival graft [31]. Second, Tseng et al. [31] studied the histological changes in the nasal lateral conjunctival epithelium after pterygium excision and found that the nasal lateral conjunctival epithelium did not regenerate completely until one year postoperatively. The density of nasal non-mitotic epithelial cells increased postoperatively. In addition, re-imprinting cytology showed an increase in the nucleoplasmic ratio of the nasal conjunctival epithelium 6 months after pterygium excision combined with autologous conjunctival transplantation with corneal rim stem cells and stretching and nuclear fixation of the conjunctival epithelium, suggesting squamous metaplasia of the nasal conjunctival graft after pterygium excision [27, 31, 32]. Finally, conjunctival keratinized epithelial cells were observed in the conjunctival epithelium on the nasal side after pterygium excision [32]. In contrast, the superior conjunctival epithelium was less dense than normal, considering the surgical cause and damage to conjunctival basal cells.

Surgery is the primary treatment for pterygium, which aims to remove diseased tissue and reduce pterygium recurrence after surgery [6]. Common surgical methods to treat pterygium include bare sclera resection, conjunctival flap transplantation, and combined amniotic membrane transplantation [33]. Each surgical method has different clinical effects [34, 35]. For example, the recurrence rate after bare sclera resection is 30–90% [35]. Pterygium resection combined with amniotic membrane transplantation does not require healthy conjunctiva. However, the recurrence rate after surgery is as high as 25% [36].

Compared to amniotic membrane transplantation, pterygium excision combined with autologous conjunctival transplantation has a lower recurrence rate [23]. Pterygium excision combined with autologous conjunctival transplantation with corneal rim stem cells is the most commonly used method for treating pterygium. It can significantly reduce postoperative recurrence (postoperative recurrence rate is reported to be 0.00–14.29%) [37–39]. However, no uniform standards exist for the surgical technique. This procedure has a specific negative impact on autologous healthy conjunctiva, which can result in corneal conjunctival scar development, restricting the potential of glaucoma patients choosing glaucoma filtering surgery in the future, and even increasing the chance of eyelid adhesion [40]. Therefore, a surgical technique with a low recurrence rate and less damage to autologous healthy conjunctival tissue is needed clinically to provide better conditions for procedures requiring conjunctival removal from the same side of the eye [41]. Limbal stem cell transplantation can effectively reduce the recurrence rate because, after limbal stem cells are implanted in patients, a large number of stem cells exist around the upper cortex of the limbus. Stem cells have the potential to promote cell division, proliferation, and life extension and play an important role in the regeneration and renewal of cell tissues. Therefore, these stem cells can promote the regeneration of corneal epithelial tissue, causing the conjunctival epithelium and blood vessels to grow toward the cornea, which can effectively avoid the recurrence of pterygium. In addition, the barrier effect of stem cells has a good inhibitory effect on the inward growth of the abnormal conjunctival epithelium [42]. Therefore, pterygium excision combined with limbal stem cell transplantation can somewhat reduce pterygium recurrence.

The present study had some limitations. First, as a cross-sectional study, this study lacked data on corneal conjunctival cell density dynamics and corneal rim destruction. Further, longitudinal studies are needed to confirm this. Second, the number of cases was small, and the observation period was short; further multicenter, large-sample, long-term studies are needed. In addition, this study lacks data on corneal confocal examination of the healthy eye contralateral to the pterygium. It does not allow for a comparison between the right and left eyes. Finally, this study lacked data on the subepithelial nerves near the nasal and superior corneal margins, including nerve fiber length, density, width, curvature, branching, bead formation, and reflexivity. The occurrence of neuropathy in pterygium could not be studied.

In conclusion, this study used IVCM to show recovery at the cellular level of the nasal and superior corneal conjunctiva 6 months after pterygium surgery. Cytological differences and fenestrated disruption of the corneal conjunctiva were observed on the donor-recipient side of the affected eye 6 months after pterygium surgery, which may allow speculation on the extent of pterygium invasion in various areas of the cornea. This study suggests that pterygium excision combined with autologous conjunctival transplantation with corneal rim stem cells can promote the repair of nasal corneal cells and fenestrations, and to a certain extent, curb the inward growth of the abnormal conjunctival epithelium. However, it has a damaging effect on the superior corneal conjunctival structures.
