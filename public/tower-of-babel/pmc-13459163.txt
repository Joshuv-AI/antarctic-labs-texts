A prognostic model based on integrated clinical indicators for recurrent acute pancreatitis in patients with hypertriglyceridemia: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Junyan An, Gaoyuan Zhang, Xinling Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRecurrence after a first episode of hypertriglyceridemia-induced acute pancreatitis (HTG-AP) remains common, whereas admission-time prediction models are still limited. We aimed to identify independent predictors of 3-year recurrence and develop a practical nomogram for early risk assessment.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed consecutive patients with first-episode HTG-AP admitted between January 2019 and January 2023. Clinical variables, laboratory indices obtained within 24 h of admission, and CT-derived skeletal muscle density (SMD) were collected. All patients had at least 3 years of potential follow-up. Candidate variables were screened using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression, followed by multivariable logistic regression to identify independent predictors of recurrence. A nomogram was constructed and evaluated by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, calibration, bootstrap validation, and decision curve analysis (DCA). Sensitivity analyses included ln(CRP + 1) adjustment and Fine–Gray regression.ResultsA total of 278 patients were included in the primary logistic regression cohort, of whom 82 (29.5%) developed recurrence within 3 years. Higher triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index (OR 5.027, 95% CI 2.599–9.723, P < 0.001) and smoking history (OR 2.358, 95% CI 1.149–4.838, P = 0.019) were independent risk factors, whereas higher SMD (OR 0.868, 95% CI 0.821–0.917, P < 0.001) and albumin (OR 0.866, 95% CI 0.801–0.936, P < 0.001) were protective factors. The nomogram showed good discrimination, with an AUC of 0.896, and remained stable after bootstrap validation (AUC 0.891). Calibration, DCA, CRP-adjusted analysis, and Fine–Gray regression supported model performance and robustness.ConclusionsA nomogram integrating TyG, SMD, albumin, and smoking history showed good performance for predicting 3-year recurrence after first-episode HTG-AP and may support early risk stratification and individualized follow-up.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12944-026-02995-2.

Introduction

Acute pancreatitis (AP) is a common cause of gastrointestinal hospitalization, and recurrence after a first attack is clinically important because repeated episodes increase healthcare utilization and may accelerate progression to chronic pancreatitis [1, 2]. Among the major etiologic subtypes, hypertriglyceridemia-induced acute pancreatitis (HTG-AP) has become increasingly prominent in parallel with the rising prevalence of obesity, diabetes, and metabolic syndrome [3, 4]. HTG-AP often affects relatively young patients and carries a substantial long-term recurrence burden. Prior reports have suggested recurrence rates ranging from approximately 16% to 49%, although the estimates vary according to study design, disease definition, and follow-up duration [5, 6]. Therefore, identifying patients at increased risk of recurrence after the index episode remains a practical priority in long-term management.

Previous studies on recurrent HTG-AP have focused mainly on post-discharge triglyceride exposure, adverse lipid metabolism, diabetes or glucose abnormalities, and cigarette smoking [7–10]. More recently, the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index has been proposed as a simple surrogate marker of insulin resistance and has shown value for recurrent AP, suggesting that recurrence risk may be better captured by integrated metabolic status than by a single triglyceride measurement alone [11]. In contrast, the evidence regarding body composition is less consistent. In HTG-AP, conventional CT-derived adipose and muscle-area measurements were not clearly associated with recurrence in one study [12], whereas muscle-related CT markers and low skeletal muscle quality have been linked to worse severity or poorer in-hospital outcomes in other AP and HTG-AP cohorts [13–15]. These observations raise the possibility that skeletal muscle density (SMD), as an imaging marker of muscle quality, may provide clinically relevant information that has not been adequately examined in recurrence-oriented studies.

Taken together, the current literature suggests that recurrent HTG-AP is influenced by metabolic and lifestyle factors, but admission-time models remain underdeveloped and the contribution of muscle quality has not been well defined [10, 16]. Most available studies have either emphasized follow-up biochemical control or evaluated body composition in relation to severity rather than later recurrence [17]. We therefore conducted a retrospective cohort study of patients with first-episode HTG-AP to identify independent predictors of 3-year recurrence and to develop a clinically accessible nomogram based on variables that are available during the index hospitalization. By combining TyG with SMD, albumin, and smoking history, we aimed to provide an early risk assessment tool that may facilitate individualized follow-up and secondary prevention.

Materials and methods

Study population

This single-center retrospective cohort study consecutively enrolled patients who were first admitted to Weifang People’s Hospital and diagnosed with hypertriglyceridemia-induced acute pancreatitis (HTG-AP) between January 2019 and January 2023. Eligible patients were 18–75 years of age, experienced a first episode of disease, were admitted within 7 days of symptom onset, and had received no prior therapeutic intervention. The diagnosis and classification of acute pancreatitis (AP) were based on the revised Atlanta classification and required at least two of the following three criteria: (1) abdominal pain consistent with AP; (2) serum amylase and/or lipase levels greater than three times the upper limit of normal; and (3) characteristic findings on abdominal ultrasonography and/or computed tomography (CT) [18]. In addition, patients were required to have hypertriglyceridemia, defined as a serum triglyceride level greater than 11.3 mmol/L or a level of 5.65–11.3 mmol/L in the presence of lactescent serum. Patients with definite biliary pancreatitis, including gallstones, biliary sludge, or choledocholithiasis with evidence of biliary obstruction, were excluded. Exclusion criteria before entry into the follow-up cohort were as follows: (a) severe comorbidities on admission, including chronic cardiac, hepatic, or renal failure; (b) a history of malignancy; (c) pregnancy or labor; (d) acute exacerbation of chronic pancreatitis; and (e) prior receipt of systemic treatment at another hospital before admission. Patients who died before documented recurrence were excluded from the primary logistic cohort but included in the competing-risk sensitivity analysis, with death before recurrence treated as a competing event. To minimize selection bias, all consecutively eligible patients during the study period were included without sampling. Because this was a real-world retrospective cohort study, treatment and follow-up were conducted according to routine clinical practice.

This retrospective study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Weifang People’s Hospital (Approval No. KYLL202600304-1). The requirement for written informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective and anonymized nature of the study. All personal identifiers were removed before data analysis to protect patient privacy.

Clinical and laboratory data

Clinical and laboratory variables collected after admission were included in the analysis, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking and alcohol use, comorbidities (such as hypertension and diabetes mellitus), and concomitant conditions (such as fatty liver and ascites). Smoking history was defined as documented current or former cigarette smoking in the medical record at admission, whereas patients without documented tobacco exposure were classified as having no smoking history. Biochemical and hematologic variables measured within 24 h of admission were also recorded, including liver and renal function, blood cell counts, lipid profile, blood glucose, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), C-reactive protein (CRP), amylase, and serum calcium. The TyG index was calculated as Ln [TG (mg/dL) * GLU (mg/dL)/2]. All biochemical variables used in the analysis, including triglycerides and fasting blood glucose for TyG calculation, were obtained from the first blood sample collected within 24 h of admission and before initiation of lipid-lowering therapy, insulin infusion, or enteral/parenteral nutritional support, thereby reflecting the baseline metabolic status at disease onset. All clinical data were independently extracted by two investigators and verified by a third investigator to ensure accuracy. Missing data were minimal (< 5% for all variables), and complete-case analysis was performed without imputation to avoid introducing additional uncertainty into the model.

Assessment of body composition parameters

All patients underwent abdominal CT examination. A single axial image at the level of the third lumbar vertebra (L3) was analyzed using SliceOmatic software (version 5.0; Tomovision, Magog, Canada) for body composition assessment. Skeletal muscle was segmented using a predefined CT attenuation threshold of -29 to + 150 Hounsfield units (HU). Skeletal muscle density (SMD) was defined as the mean CT attenuation value within the segmented skeletal muscle area and expressed in HU. Image analyses were independently performed by two experienced radiologists who were blinded to the clinical data and outcomes. Any discrepancies were resolved by repeat measurement and consensus.

Follow-up and study endpoint

The primary endpoint was recurrent acute pancreatitis within 3 years after the index hospitalization. Recurrence was defined as a new AP episode occurring more than 3 months after the initial attack, after resolution of the index episode, and in the absence of definite evidence of chronic pancreatitis. Patients were enrolled between January 2019 and January 2023, and follow-up data were updated through January 2026, ensuring at least 3 years of potential follow-up for patients who survived without a competing event. Follow-up information was obtained from outpatient visits, telephone interviews at approximately 3- to 6-month intervals, and review of electronic medical records. Patients without recurrence during the 3-year observation period were classified into the non-recurrence group in the primary logistic regression cohort. For the competing-risk sensitivity analysis, death during follow-up before recurrent AP was treated as a competing event.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.1.2). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), as appropriate, and were compared using the independent-samples t test or Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables are presented as number (percentage) and were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. AP recurrence was treated as a binary outcome in the primary logistic regression analysis. Univariable analyses were first performed, and variables with P < 0.10 were entered into least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression for dimensionality reduction and predictor selection. Variables retained after LASSO selection were then entered into multivariable logistic regression to identify independent predictors of AP recurrence, on the basis of which a nomogram was constructed. Model discrimination was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, and the optimal cut-off value, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated. Model calibration was evaluated using calibration curves, the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, and the Brier score, whereas clinical utility was assessed using decision curve analysis (DCA). Internal validation was performed using the bootstrap method, and the AUC and its 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated to assess model stability and internal validity. Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the final model. First, ln(CRP + 1) was additionally adjusted for in the final multivariable logistic model to evaluate whether the association between albumin and recurrence was independent of systemic inflammation. Second, Fine–Gray competing-risk regression was performed in the cohort including the 9 patients who died before documented recurrence, with recurrent AP as the event of interest and death before recurrence as the competing event. Model stability was further examined by subgroup analyses according to age, sex, BMI, diabetes mellitus, and disease severity; AUCs were calculated within each subgroup, and interactions were assessed using the model-predicted risk score.

Results

A total of 287 patients entered the follow-up cohort and were included in the competing-risk sensitivity analysis. Among them, 9 patients died during follow-up without documented recurrence and were included only in the Fine–Gray competing-risk sensitivity analysis. The remaining 278 patients constituted the primary logistic regression cohort, of whom 82 developed AP recurrence and 196 did not, yielding an overall recurrence rate of 29.5%. Baseline characteristics

The baseline characteristics of patients with and without AP recurrence in the primary logistic regression cohort are shown in Table 1. Compared with the non-recurrence group, the recurrence group had a higher TyG index (10.99 ± 0.65 vs. 10.36 ± 0.60, P < 0.001), lower albumin levels (38.34 ± 3.46 vs. 43.38 ± 5.76, P < 0.001), higher blood glucose levels (15.55 ± 9.27 vs. 11.18 ± 5.67, P < 0.001), higher triglyceride levels [25.10 (22.27, 38.25) vs. 20.35 (14.58, 23.64), P < 0.001], and higher total cholesterol levels (11.71 ± 7.77 vs. 9.93 ± 4.77, P = 0.021). Most other clinical and laboratory variables did not differ significantly between the two groups.

Selection of independent predictors

Variables with P < 0.10 in the univariable analysis were entered into the LASSO logistic regression model for variable selection. As shown in Fig. 2A, the regression coefficients of candidate variables gradually shrank toward zero as log(λ) increased. Figure 2B presents the cross-validation binomial deviance curve, in which the two vertical dashed lines correspond to lambda.min and lambda.1se, respectively. After balancing predictive performance with retention of potentially informative variables, lambda.min was selected as the optimal penalty parameter (λ = 0.01299698), and variables with non-zero coefficients at this value were entered into the subsequent multivariable logistic regression model. As shown in Table 2, multivariable logistic regression identified SMD, ALB, TyG, and smoking history as independent predictors of AP recurrence. Specifically, SMD (OR = 0.868, 95% CI: 0.821–0.917, P < 0.001) and ALB (OR = 0.866, 95% CI: 0.801–0.936, P < 0.001) were protective factors, whereas TyG (OR = 5.027, 95% CI: 2.599–9.723, P < 0.001) and smoking history (OR = 2.358, 95% CI: 1.149–4.838, P = 0.019) were risk factors.

Construction of the AP recurrence prediction model

Based on the four independent predictors identified by multivariable logistic regression (SMD, smoking history, ALB, and TyG), a nomogram for predicting the risk of AP recurrence was constructed (Fig. 3). The model assigns a corresponding score to each predictor, and the total score is then converted into an individualized probability of AP recurrence. The nomogram indicates that lower SMD, lower ALB, higher TyG, and a positive smoking history are all associated with a higher risk of AP recurrence. Figure 4 further illustrates individualized application of the nomogram: after summing the points assigned to each predictor, the total score was approximately 225, corresponding to a predicted AP recurrence probability of approximately 94.5%, indicating that this patient was at high risk of recurrence.

Discrimination and diagnostic performance of the model

ROC analysis results are shown in Fig. 5; Table 3. The combined nomogram model demonstrated good discriminatory ability, with an AUC of 0.8964 (95% CI: 0.8597–0.9331), which was higher than those of any individual predictor. The AUCs of the individual predictors were 0.8030 (95% CI: 0.7482–0.8578) for SMD, 0.7731 (95% CI: 0.7198–0.8264) for ALB, 0.7610 (95% CI: 0.7003–0.8216) for TyG, and 0.6008 (95% CI: 0.5375–0.6641) for smoking history. Further analysis showed that the optimal cut-off value for the combined model was 0.296, with a sensitivity of 0.817, specificity of 0.816, PPV of 0.650, and NPV of 0.914. These findings indicate that the nomogram combining multiple predictors outperformed any single indicator in identifying patients at risk of AP recurrence and may be useful for early screening and risk stratification, particularly because of its high negative predictive value.

Internal validation, calibration, and clinical utility

Internal validation of the nomogram was performed using the bootstrap method. The bootstrap-validated AUC was 0.891 (95% CI: 0.854–0.926), indicating that the model maintained good discrimination and stability under repeated resampling.

As shown in Fig. 6, calibration analysis demonstrated good agreement between the bias-corrected curve and the ideal curve, indicating good consistency between predicted and observed probabilities. The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test yielded χ² = 5.4053 with 8 degrees of freedom (P = 0.7135), suggesting adequate model fit. The Brier score was 0.1187322, further supporting good overall calibration. Sensitivity and subgroup analyses

In the sensitivity analysis additionally adjusted for ln(CRP + 1), albumin remained independently associated with 3-year AP recurrence (OR = 0.869, 95% CI: 0.805–0.938, P < 0.001), indicating that its association with recurrence was not fully explained by baseline systemic inflammation. SMD, TyG, and smoking history also remained significant, whereas ln(CRP + 1) was not independently associated with recurrence (Supplementary Table 1).

Fine–Gray competing-risk analysis was performed in the 287-patient sensitivity cohort, including the 9 patients who died during follow-up without documented recurrence. After treating death before recurrence as a competing event, TyG (sHR = 1.905, 95% CI: 1.427–2.541, P < 0.001), smoking history (sHR = 1.629, 95% CI: 1.061–2.502, P = 0.026), SMD (sHR = 0.926, 95% CI: 0.900–0.953, P < 0.001), and albumin (sHR = 0.946, 95% CI: 0.911–0.982, P = 0.004) remained significantly associated with recurrence, supporting the robustness of the primary findings (Supplementary Tables 2 and Supplementary Fig. 1).

Subgroup analyses showed generally good discrimination across age, sex, BMI, diabetes mellitus, and disease severity strata, with AUCs ranging from 0.745 to 0.927. No significant interactions were observed for age, sex, BMI, or diabetes status. A significant interaction was observed for disease severity (P for interaction = 0.007), with better performance in non-severe AP than in severe AP. Nevertheless, the model retained acceptable discrimination in severe AP, although this result should be interpreted cautiously because of the smaller subgroup size (Supplementary Tables 3 and Supplementary Fig. 2).

Discussion

Early prediction of recurrence after a first episode of HTG-AP remains an important clinical challenge. Previously reported nomogram models based mainly on conventional clinical variables have shown only moderate discrimination [19], whereas more recent MRI radiomics models achieved higher accuracy [20], albeit with substantially greater technical complexity and less immediate clinical accessibility. In the present study, we developed a nomogram based on TyG, SMD, albumin, and smoking history, and the model showed good discriminative ability, with an AUC of 0.896 and a bootstrap-validated AUC of 0.891. These findings suggest that, compared with conventional clinical nomograms, our model provides stronger predictive performance, while retaining better practicality and interpretability than more technically demanding imaging-based approaches. In this respect, our study offers a clinically accessible tool for early risk stratification after first-episode HTG-AP and may help support more individualized follow-up and secondary prevention strategies.

We found that a higher TyG index was independently associated with 3-year recurrence, which is consistent with recent literature on HTG-AP and recurrent AP [6, 21]. The TyG index is widely regarded as a practical surrogate of insulin resistance and therefore reflects more than isolated hypertriglyceridemia. In patients with HTG-AP, recurrent attacks are often driven by persistent abnormalities in triglyceride handling, glucose metabolism, and low-grade metabolic inflammation [22]. Prospective data have shown that poor post-index triglyceride and glucose control is closely related to recurrence [23], while recent retrospective evidence suggests that TyG itself can identify patients at increased risk of recurrent AP [11, 24]. Our findings support this line of evidence and further suggest that TyG remains informative even when evaluated together with imaging- and nutrition-related variables. Thus, TyG appears to be a useful summary marker of the metabolic background that predisposes some patients to recurrent disease.

SMD adds an important dimension to recurrence risk assessment. Compared with skeletal muscle area alone, skeletal muscle density more directly reflects muscle quality and intramuscular fat infiltration [25]. Low SMD is increasingly regarded as an imaging marker of myosteatosis, metabolic inflexibility, and chronic inflammation [26]. Although previous AP studies have mainly linked low skeletal muscle quality to worse in-hospital outcomes and more severe disease [27, 28], our findings suggest that it may also help identify patients who remain vulnerable to recurrence after apparent recovery from the index attack. This association is biologically plausible. Poor muscle quality is closely related to insulin resistance and ectopic lipid deposition, thereby reinforcing the same adverse metabolic milieu reflected by TyG [29]. In addition, skeletal muscle is a major site of glucose disposal and metabolic buffering; once muscle quality declines, the capacity to tolerate recurrent lipid stress may also diminish [30]. Therefore, SMD may serve as an imaging marker linking metabolic dysfunction with reduced physiologic reserve in HTG-AP.

TyG and SMD should not be interpreted as completely independent phenomena [31]. Skeletal muscle is the major organ for insulin-mediated glucose disposal, and impaired muscle quality is closely related to insulin resistance, ectopic lipid deposition, and reduced metabolic flexibility [32]. Conversely, chronic insulin resistance may contribute to lipid infiltration within skeletal muscle and further impair muscle quality [33]. In HTG-AP, elevated TyG and reduced SMD may therefore act in parallel to mark patients who are less able to buffer recurrent metabolic stress. This may partly explain why combining these two variables improved prediction beyond either metric alone. From a practical standpoint, this combination suggests that recurrence risk in HTG-AP is influenced not only by circulating lipid excess but also by the patient’s capacity to metabolically tolerate that burden.

Albumin and smoking history also remained independently associated with recurrence. Lower albumin may reflect reduced nutritional reserve, greater inflammatory burden, and a diminished ability to bind circulating free fatty acids, which are central to lipotoxic injury in HTG-AP [22, 34]. Although albumin has been discussed more often in relation to disease severity than recurrence, its availability and low cost make it attractive for risk assessment at the time of admission [35]. In the CRP-adjusted sensitivity analysis, albumin remained significantly associated with recurrence, suggesting that this association was not fully explained by baseline systemic inflammation. Albumin may therefore reflect broader host reserve beyond nutritional status alone. Smoking, in contrast, is a more established recurrence-related factor in patients with recurrent acute pancreatitis and after a first episode of acute pancreatitis [10, 36, 37]. Its role is biologically plausible given the links between tobacco exposure, oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and pancreatic acinar injury [38]. Considered together, albumin and smoking history highlight two clinically useful messages: some components of recurrence risk may reflect the patient’s recovery reserve, whereas others point to modifiable exposures that should be specifically addressed during secondary prevention.

The present study has several practical strengths. By restricting the cohort to first-episode HTG-AP and excluding definite biliary pancreatitis, we reduced etiologic heterogeneity and avoided mixing incident cases with patients who had already entered a recurrent disease course. In addition, all predictors in the nomogram are readily obtainable during routine care, including TyG and albumin from admission blood tests, smoking history from bedside assessment, and SMD from abdominal CT. Compared with models based mainly on visceral adiposity or isolated biochemical abnormalities, this approach incorporates muscle quality while remaining easy to implement in clinical practice. We also performed CRP-adjusted, competing-risk, and subgroup analyses, which supported the robustness of the main findings while identifying disease severity as a potential source of heterogeneity in model performance. Accordingly, the nomogram may help identify high-risk patients at discharge or early follow-up and support more targeted secondary prevention.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. This was a single-center retrospective study, and selection bias could not be completely avoided. Although bootstrap validation suggested good internal stability, an independent external validation cohort was not available; therefore, the model should be regarded as preliminary and requires external validation in larger multicenter cohorts before routine clinical application. In addition, the sample size and number of recurrence events were relatively modest, which may have limited statistical stability, especially in subgroup analyses. The model showed lower discrimination in the severe AP subgroup, and this finding should be interpreted cautiously because the number of severe AP patients was relatively small. Although total cholesterol was collected and evaluated, HDL-C and LDL-C were not consistently available in the retrospective records. Similarly, prealbumin and formal nutritional risk scores were not consistently recorded, so CRP-adjusted sensitivity analysis was used to explore whether the albumin association was independent of inflammation. Finally, SMD measurement relied on dedicated CT-based body composition analysis and may not be readily available in all institutions.

Conclusions

We developed a clinically accessible nomogram integrating TyG, SMD, albumin, and smoking history for predicting 3-year recurrence after first-episode HTG-AP. The model performed well in internal evaluation and may support early recurrence risk stratification by integrating metabolic status, muscle quality, albumin level, and smoking exposure. Future multicenter studies are needed to externally validate this model and determine whether risk-guided interventions can reduce the burden of recurrent disease.
