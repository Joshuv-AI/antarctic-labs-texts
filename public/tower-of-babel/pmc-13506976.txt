Kinetic–rest tremor dominance for differentiating dystonic tremor from Parkinson's disease
Authors: Takashi Tsuboi, Takashi Uematsu, Miki Hashida, Yoshiki Ito, Keito Sawada, Moeka Higuchi, Tomotaka Ishizaki, Takahiko Tsugawa, Satoshi Maesawa, Kenshiro Fuse, Yuki Saito, Taiki Fukushima, Daigo Tamakoshi, Keita Hiraga, Masashi Suzuki, Ryuta Saito, Adolfo Ramirez-Zamora, Christopher W Hess, Michael S Okun, Masahisa Katsuno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDifferentiating dystonic tremor (DT) from Parkinson's disease (PD) remains challenging, particularly when dystonic signs are subtle, and DT is a major contributor to scans without evidence of dopaminergic deficit. Although dopamine transporter imaging can support the distinction, it is costly and not universally accessible. Simple diagnostic indicators less reliant on subjective clinical interpretation are needed.MethodsIn a dopamine transporter imaging–supported cohort (n = 102; DT, 51; PD, 51), we used accelerometry to characterize ordinal patterns of tremor amplitude across rest, postural, and kinetic (spiral drawing) conditions. Candidate ordinal rules were evaluated, and the rule with the highest classification accuracy was selected for subsequent blinded visual assessment by three raters within the same cohort.ResultsAccelerometry showed greater kinetic tremor amplitude in DT and greater rest tremor amplitude in PD, whereas postural tremor amplitude did not differ significantly between groups. Among candidate ordinal rules, the pairwise comparison of kinetic versus rest tremor amplitude achieved the highest overall diagnostic performance for identifying DT (90% sensitivity, 85% specificity, 88% accuracy). Blinded visual classification of this rule showed excellent inter-rater agreement (Fleiss’ κ = 0.92), high concordance with accelerometer-based classification (Cohen's κ = 0.86), and 90% sensitivity, 88% specificity, and 89% accuracy.ConclusionsA simple visual comparison of tremor amplitude during spiral drawing versus at rest required no specialized equipment and showed high reproducibility in blinded ratings. Although probabilistic and requiring validation in independent cohorts, this approach may serve as a practical bedside adjunct for distinguishing DT from PD.Keywords: dystonic tremor, Parkinson's disease, accelerometry, differential diagnosis, dopamine transporter imaging, bedside assessment

Introduction

Dystonic tremor (DT) is a common tremor syndrome characterized by the coexistence of tremor and dystonia. 1 Differentiating DT from Parkinson's disease (PD) is often challenging because dystonic signs may be subtle, and DT can present with PD-like features such as rest tremor and apparent bradykinesia.2–5 Accordingly, DT represents a major contributor to scans without evidence of dopaminergic deficit (SWEDDs).6–9 Misdiagnosis may expose patients to ineffective medications with attendant side effects and delay the initiation of appropriate treatment. Dopamine transporter imaging can support the distinction, but the examination is costly and not readily available in many clinical settings. Several bedside clues have been proposed,6,8,10–12 but their diagnostic utility is constrained by subjective interpretation and by limited validation in imaging-supported cohorts.

Accelerometry studies have shown that PD tremor predominates at rest, whereas DT is more pronounced during voluntary action.8,10,13,14 Various kinetic tasks have been used to elicit action tremor, including finger-to-nose maneuvers, spiral drawing, handwriting, and pouring. Among these, spiral drawing is a continuous, goal-directed motor task that can be assessed both visually and instrumentally.15–18 It has also been reported to modulate tremor expression differently in PD and SWEDDs. 12 However, whether this observation extends to DT–PD differentiation remains unclear.

We used accelerometry during rest, postural, and spiral drawing tasks to characterize ordinal patterns of tremor amplitude across conditions in a dopamine transporter imaging–supported cohort of patients with DT and PD. Our aim was to identify a simple adjunctive indicator less reliant on subjective judgment. We also examined whether blinded visual assessment could reproduce the accelerometry-derived ordinal rule.

Methods

Study population

This study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and the Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Health Research Involving Human Subjects by the Japanese government. The study protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee (approval number: 2020-0640), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

We prospectively enrolled patients with DT or PD who underwent clinical evaluation at the movement disorders center of Nagoya University Hospital. Among patients with a final diagnosis of DT according to the 2018 Movement Disorder Society tremor classification, 1 we included those with coexisting tremor and dystonia affecting the upper limbs. As a comparison group, we enrolled patients with PD meeting the Movement Disorder Society clinical diagnostic criteria, 19 who exhibited clinically observable upper limb tremor. Patients with coexisting movement disorders, neurological comorbidities potentially affecting tremor assessment (e.g., neuropathy, stroke), or prior neurosurgical interventions were excluded. All participants underwent dopamine transporter imaging using 123I-FP-CIT single-photon emission computed tomography. Striatal dopamine transporter uptake was evaluated using visual assessment together with age-adjusted specific binding ratio Z scores derived from a standardized reference database. 20 Normal striatal uptake was required for inclusion in the DT group, while reduced uptake was confirmed for PD. All clinical assessments were performed under usual medications in both groups. Cognitive function was assessed using Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE). Levodopa equivalent daily dose was calculated using established conversion factors. 21

Accelerometer-based tremor amplitude assessment

Triaxial accelerometers (Logical Product, Fukuoka, Japan) were attached to the dorsum of both hands while participants were seated. Tremor was video-recorded for 30 s under three standardized conditions: rest (hands hanging freely from armrests), postural (arms outstretched with pronated hands and fingers spread), and kinetic (drawing an outward spiral from center without the hand touching the desk surface). For the postural and kinetic conditions, recording was started immediately after arm elevation and after the onset of spiral drawing, respectively. No additional activation maneuvers, such as mental arithmetic, were used during the recordings. Signals were recorded at a sampling rate of 200 Hz. Accelerometer signals were linearly detrended and analyzed using short-time Fourier transform with custom Python software (https://github.com/opensesame-dii/tremor_analysis_python/tree/develop), according to our previously reported methods. 22 Peak tremor amplitude was defined as the maximum value of the power spectral density curve; if no discernible tremor peak was present, the amplitude was assigned a value of zero. The more affected side was defined as the limb showing the highest peak tremor amplitude across the three conditions, and data from that side were used for all analyses.

Exploration of ordinal diagnostic rules

We prespecified a finite set of ordinal diagnostic rules designed to reflect bedside reasoning, using only relative ordering of tremor amplitudes across conditions (no absolute thresholds or weighting). Other accelerometry-derived measures such as tremor frequency and stability indices were not incorporated because our aim was to develop rules that could be visually reproduced at the bedside. Candidate rules were defined a priori in order of increasing complexity: pairwise comparisons, extrema across three conditions, and logical combinations. They were compared primarily by classification accuracy; when accuracy was similar, preference was given to simpler rules to preserve bedside applicability.

Because ordinal pattern analysis required rankable tremor amplitudes across conditions, patients with no discernible tremor peak in ≥2 conditions were excluded from accelerometer-based ordinal analyses. For the remaining cases, ordinal rules were evaluated using strict inequalities based on relative amplitude ordering.

Visual assessment of tremor

The ordinal rule with the highest diagnostic accuracy in accelerometer-based analyses was then examined in blinded visual ratings within the same cohort. Three independent neurologists (9–14 years of clinical experience), blinded to clinical diagnosis and accelerometer findings, reviewed the video segments recorded during accelerometer assessment. For each participant, raters determined whether the selected rule was met based solely on visual comparison of tremor severity across conditions. When ratings were discordant, the final visual classification was determined by majority vote. Visual assessment was performed for all participants, including those excluded from accelerometer-based ordinal analyses.

Statistical analysis

Group differences were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test, χ2 tests, or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. Tremor amplitude was compared across conditions within group using the Friedman test, followed by post hoc pairwise comparisons with Holm-adjusted p values.

Diagnostic performance of ordinal rules was evaluated by calculating sensitivity, specificity, and overall accuracy, with DT treated as the positive diagnostic class. Inter-rater agreement for visual classification was assessed using Fleiss’ kappa, and concordance between visual and accelerometer-based classifications was evaluated using Cohen's kappa.

To assess the potential influence of disease duration on the association between the selected rule (kinetic–rest tremor dominance) and diagnosis, a multivariable logistic regression model was constructed. Because of quasi-complete separation, Firth's penalized logistic regression was used, with diagnosis (DT vs PD) as the dependent variable and visually classified kinetic–rest tremor dominance and disease duration as independent variables. A supplementary model additionally adjusted for age and sex was also fitted. To examine whether this association varied according to disease duration, an interaction term was added to the model; its significance was assessed using a profile penalized likelihood ratio test.

Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.3).

Results

Cohort characteristics

A total of 67 patients with DT and 67 with PD were initially identified. After application of the exclusion criteria summarized in Supplementary Figure S1, 51 patients with DT and 51 with PD constituted the analytic cohort (Table 1). Age, sex, and MMSE scores were comparable between groups, whereas disease duration was significantly longer in DT than in PD [mean (standard deviation), 26.6 (16.9) vs. 6.7 (4.8) years; p < 0.001]. Voice and head tremor were more frequent in DT, and cervical dystonia was present in nine patients with DT. Medication use in the PD and DT groups is summarized in Supplementary Table S1 and S2. In the PD group, the mean levodopa equivalent daily dose was 392 mg/day.

Tremor amplitude across conditions

Accelerometer analysis showed greater rest tremor amplitude in PD and greater kinetic tremor amplitude in DT, whereas postural tremor amplitude did not differ significantly between the groups (Figure 1(a) and Supplementary Table S3). Within-group comparisons showed a graded increase from rest to postural to kinetic conditions in DT and the reverse pattern in PD, with significant differences across conditions in both groups (Figure 1(a) and Supplementary Table S4).

At the individual level, all six possible ordinal patterns were observed in both groups (Figure 1(b) and Table 2), reflecting individual variability in tremor expression. Nonetheless, kinetic-dominant patterns were more frequent in DT, whereas rest-dominant patterns were more frequent in PD.

Diagnostic performance of ordinal rules

Five patients with no discernible tremor peak in two of the three conditions were excluded from accelerometer-based ordinal analyses, leaving 97 patients (DT, n = 49; PD, n = 48). Among candidate ordinal rules (Table 3), the pairwise comparison of kinetic versus rest tremor amplitude (K > R) achieved the highest accuracy (88%) with 90% sensitivity and 85% specificity. Three-condition rules and logical combinations did not improve overall accuracy, as gains in one metric were offset by losses in the other.

Because the K > R rule requires only a single pairwise comparison, ordinal classification was possible for all 102 patients. A sensitivity analysis including all the patients yielded essentially unchanged results (accuracy 88% vs. 87%; Supplementary Table S5).

Visual reproducibility

Across all 102 participants, visual classification of kinetic–rest tremor dominance showed excellent inter-rater agreement (Fleiss’ κ = 0.92) and high concordance with accelerometer-based classification (92.2%; Cohen's κ = 0.86). Sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy for identifying DT were 90%, 88%, and 89%, respectively (Table 3).

Influence of disease duration

After adjustment for disease duration using Firth's penalized logistic regression, kinetic–rest tremor dominance remained significantly associated with diagnosis (adjusted OR 36.07, 95% CI 9.40–191.83, p < 0.001; Supplementary Table S6). Disease duration was also independently associated with diagnosis in the same model (adjusted OR per year 1.19, 95% CI 1.08–1.35, p < 0.001). Results were similar in a supplementary model additionally adjusted for age and sex (Supplementary Table S6). Adding an interaction term between kinetic–rest tremor dominance and disease duration did not provide evidence that this association differed according to disease duration (OR 1.07, 95% CI 0.86–1.43, p = 0.55).

Discussion

In this dopamine transporter imaging–supported cohort, a simple visual comparison of tremor amplitude during spiral drawing and at rest showed good diagnostic performance for distinguishing DT from PD. Blinded visual ratings showed high inter-rater agreement and close concordance with accelerometer-based classification, supporting the reproducibility of this rule within the study setting. These findings suggest that kinetic–rest tremor comparison may serve as a bedside adjunct to clinical assessment, particularly where dopamine transporter imaging is not readily accessible.

Our findings are broadly consistent with prior accelerometry studies showing rest-dominant tremor in PD and relatively greater action tremor in DT.8,10,13,14 The present study extends this literature by showing that a simple kinetic-versus-rest comparison differentiated DT from PD in a dopamine transporter imaging-supported cohort.

The kinetic–rest contrast observed here may reflect differences in the relative involvement of basal ganglia and cerebello-thalamo-cortical circuits in PD and DT.23–25 The use of spiral drawing may also have accentuated this contrast, as it is a continuous kinetic task involving fine motor control. However, these interpretations were not examined directly in the present study and should not be taken as mechanistic evidence.

Several bedside features, including tremor irregularity, task- or position-specificity, macrographia, characteristic spiral drawing patterns, and the absence of true bradykinesia, have been proposed as clues for distinguishing DT from PD.6,8,10–12 However, these features often rely on more subjective clinical interpretation, and data on inter-rater reliability remain limited, particularly when dystonic signs are subtle.26–29 The present study did not compare the ordinal rule directly with these existing clues, but instead evaluated a complementary approach based on a simple relative comparison of tremor severity, without requiring an absolute severity threshold. Although this approach still depends on visual assessment, the high inter-rater agreement and agreement with accelerometry-based classification observed in the present study suggest that this judgment can be made reproducibly under standardized assessment conditions. However, because all six ordinal patterns were observed in both groups, this rule should be regarded as a probabilistic diagnostic adjunct rather than a standalone criterion.

Disease duration was substantially longer in DT than in PD, likely reflecting differences in disease course and referral patterns. This imbalance represents an important limitation of the present cross-sectional study. Kinetic–rest tremor dominance remained associated with diagnosis after adjustment for disease duration, age, and sex. In addition, the interaction analysis did not indicate that the association between kinetic–rest tremor dominance and diagnosis varied with disease duration. However, residual confounding cannot be excluded. In PD, the relative expression of rest, postural, and kinetic tremor may change over time and may be differentially influenced by medication.30–32 Comparable longitudinal data for dystonic tremor are limited, but available evidence suggests that tremor expression in dystonia may vary with body distribution and disease duration.33,34 Evidence on the pharmacological responsiveness of dystonic tremor also remains limited and less systematic. 35 In the present cohort, all assessments were performed under usual medications without standardized on–off medication assessments. Future validation in duration-matched and longitudinal cohorts under standardized medication states will therefore be needed.

Several additional limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center study from a tertiary movement disorders center, and the generalizability of these findings to other clinical populations remains to be established. Second, the ordinal rule was selected and visually validated within the same cohort, so the reported diagnostic performance may be optimistic and requires confirmation in independent cohorts. Third, the observed tremor patterns may have been influenced by the assessment protocol. Postural and kinetic tremor recordings were started immediately after arm elevation and the onset of spiral drawing, respectively, and continued for 30 s. This duration was chosen to reflect a clinically realistic observation period and exceeds the typical latency reported for re-emergent tremor.36–38 However, the protocol was not designed to evaluate tremor emerging after longer latency or latency-dependent changes in tremor expression. In addition, activation maneuvers were not included. Although this simplified task set was selected based on prior findings and was intended to preserve bedside applicability,8,12,14,39 activation maneuvers, alternative kinetic tasks, or different rest-tremor assessment conditions may alter tremor expression and yield different diagnostic performance.

In conclusion, a simple visual comparison of tremor amplitude during spiral drawing versus at rest may help distinguish DT from PD as a probabilistic bedside adjunct. The findings should be interpreted in light of the cross-sectional design, disease-duration imbalance, and validation within the same cohort. Independent validation in duration-matched and longitudinal cohorts will be needed.
