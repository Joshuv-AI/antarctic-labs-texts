Visual-Instructed Degradation Diffusion for All-in-One Image Restoration
Wenyang Luo, Haina Qin, Zewen Chen, Libin Wang, Dandan Zheng, Yuming Li, Yufan Liu, Bing Li, Weiming Hu

Abstract
Image restoration tasks like deblurring, denoising, and dehazing usually need distinct models for each degradation type, restricting their generalization in real-world scenarios with mixed or unknown degradations. In this work, we propose Defusion , a novel all-in-one image restoration framework that utilizes visual instruction-guided degradation diffusion. Unlike existing methods that rely on task-specific models or ambiguous text-based priors, Defusion constructs explicit visual instructions that align with the visual degradation patterns. These instructions are grounded by applying degradations to standardized visual elements, capturing intrinsic degradation features while agnostic to image semantics. Defusion then uses these visual instructions to guide a diffusion-based model that operates directly in the degradation space, where it reconstructs high-quality images by denoising the degradation effects with enhanced stability and generalizability. Comprehensive experiments demonstrate that Defusion outperforms state-of-the-art methods across diverse image restoration tasks, including complex and real-world degradations.

However, these approaches often struggle with generalization, requiring separate models for each degradation type, which limits their practical applicability in real-world scenarios with complex degradations.

. They use implicit or explicit prior knowledge to identify degradation patterns and guide restoration. However, existing methods face challenges in deriving effective priors that align with the visual structures of degraded images.

Implicit priors are extracted from input low-quality (LQ) images by task-specific sub-networks

;
Park et al. 2023

;

Explicit priors, typically text descriptions processed by language models

;

;
Liu et al. 2025
. Text descriptions are typically coarse or agnostic to degradations

Given the above limitations, we propose to
let visual speak for visual
and guide the image restoration model with
visual instructions
as explicit priors. Our visual instructions are constructed to demonstrate the artifacts of image degradations as visual guidance. This is achieved by applying the image corruption process (e.g. raining or noising) of the LQ images upon the visual grounds consisting of basic visual elements such as random textures, basic shapes, standard colors, etc, as shown in
Fig.

1
. The resulting visual instructions encode the exact visual effects (e.g. rain lines or noisy “dots”) of the degradation with a minimal loss of information. The primary benefit is that the resulting visual instructions are inherently aligned with visual degradation patterns. For example, the combined visual instruction in
Fig.

1
can naturally show similar distortions as the composite LQ images. This makes the visual instructions suitable for low-level vision tasks.

We propose a unified diffusion-based image restoration framework,
De
gradation Dif
fusion
, which is guided by our visual instructions to denoise in the degradation space.
The diffusion model provides generative priors for the restored images, conditioned on the LQ images through an Image Restoration Bridge (IRB) to preserve the structural priors of the LQ images and maintain visual consistency between LQ and restored images.
Visual instructions, which capture the prominent intrinsic artifacts that degradations induce on textures, colors, lightness, and other image elements, control the model to perform restoration tasks. Specifically, visual instructions are “tokenized” and hint the model of the degradations in the LQ images via a Visual Instruct Adapter (VIA).
Moreover, Defusion operates within the degradation space defined as the space of transformations between LQ and HQ images, which aligns with the visual instructions describing the degradation.
The restoration model is jointly trained on multiple tasks. During inference, it follows visual instructions to handle various degradation types.

Our contributions are summarized as follows:

1.

We introduce a novel diffusion-based all-in-one image restoration framework, dubbed Defusion, guided by visual instructions to denoise in the degradation space.

2.

Our visual instructions visually align with the image degradations and retain their visual compositional properties, giving them advantages in low-level vision tasks.

3.

We conducted comprehensive experiments on various image restoration tasks under different scenarios including complex and unseen degradations, and Defusion consistently outperforms state-of-the-art methods.

Diffusion Models for Image Restoration

Image restoration can be formulated as an image-to-image translation task, with low-quality (LQ) images as input and high-quality (HQ) images as output. Diffusion-based generative models (DMs)
Sohl-Dickstein et al. 2015
;
Ho et al. 2020
;
Song et al. 2020b

Dhariwal and Nichol 2021
;
Rombach et al. 2022
;
Luo et al. 2023c
;
Kawar et al. 2022
;
Ding et al. 2024
.

Many works

;
Xia et al. 2023
;
Ai et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2024d
;
Yu et al. 2024
;
Lin et al. 2023a
;
Jiang et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2024b
adopt the latent diffusion architecture following Stable Diffusion
Rombach et al. 2022
, where a separate autoencoder (VAE)
Kingma 2013
extracts latent codes from the LQ images to denoise. However, the VAE discards most low-level details, which harms image restoration.

Other works operate on pixels
Liu et al. 2024b
;
Kawar et al. 2022
;
Luo et al. 2023b
or frequency domain
Zhao et al. 2024
.
Among them, some works propose dedicated diffusion processes to model image degradations, where the HQ images are corrupted given the LQ images
Kawar et al. 2022
or shifts between HQ and LQ images

;
Liu et al. 2024b
;
Liu et al. 2024c
;
Shi et al. 2023
;
Yue et al. 2024
. These complicated approaches usually make approximate assumptions about HQ/LQ images or their shifts, leading to increased accumulated errors during inference. Moreover, they require training from scratch, unable to leverage prior knowledge about natural (clean) HQ images in pretrained models

;
Saharia et al. 2022
;
Ramesh et al. 2022
.
Instead, we diffuse the degradations between HQ and LQ conditioned on LQ images, guided by our visual instructions via the Visual Instruction Adapter (VIA). Our approach is simple yet effective for all-in-one image restoration, allowing for stable training and easy adaptation from pretrained models.

3.1
Visual Instructions

Motivation.

Most all-in-one image restoration estimate degradation types given the input LQ images to guide restoration
Lin et al. 2023a
;
Ai et al. 2024
;
Potlapalli et al. 2024
;
Park et al. 2023
, where the estimation becomes inaccurate for unseen degradation types.
Inspired by InstructPix2Pix
Brooks et al. 2023
, some work
Conde et al. 2024
;
Jiang et al. 2023
control the restoration process with user-provided text instructions such as “make this blurry images sharper”.
However,
languages and visual patterns reside in very different spaces
. While the high-level semantics can be aligned by visual-language pretraining
Radford et al. 2021
;
Alayrac et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2024a
, language cannot accurately convey visual details
Monsefi et al. 2024
;
Shao et al. 2023
such as how the degradations rearrange and combine pixels.

On the contrary, we
let visual speak for visual
and resort to accurately describing the visual effect of degradations through visual instructions. Ideally, the visual instructions should capture the visually relevant aspects of degradations with minimal information loss and be disentangled with image semantics to improve generalizability. Next, we describe how to construct such visual instructions.

Grounding of Degradations.

Image degradations are “dangling,” meaning that their visual effects only manifest when they exist in the context of degraded images. Therefore, we first apply degradations on some “standard images” to visually demonstrate the degradations to the restoration model. We call this process
grounding
of degradations, the “standard images” are dubbed visual grounds.

The visual grounds are carefully chosen to include various types of visual constructs, patterns, structures, etc., that may occur in natural images to reveal the full extent of degradation. We draw inspiration from image quality assessment
Mittal et al. 2012b
;
Venkatanath et al. 2015
;
Mittal et al. 2012a
and select TE42
Engineering 2024
, a family of charts commonly used for camera testing and visual analysis, to construct a pool of visual grounds. TE42 comprises a rich combination of textures and color palettes. We group the constructs in TE42 charts into concentric textures, random textures, natural textures, and colors and randomly select one construct from each group to form a visual ground.
Fig.

2
shows some examples of visual grounds.
Given an arbitrary degradation, we transform the visual ground by the degradation with random parameters. The degraded visual ground forms the visual instruction.

Degradation Tokenization.

The grounded degradations (i.e. our visual instructions) obtained above can be regarded as “visual words” that “speak” how the degradations visually distort the image content.
We further “tokenize” the grounded degradations into
degradation tokens
to guide diffusion-based image restoration, analogous to how text instructions are tokenized and guide text-to-image generation.

In our work, we tokenize our visual instructions with a Degradation Tokenizer that produces a sequence of discrete degradation token embeddings. Degradation Tokenizer compresses the perceptually unimportant constituents in the visual instructions with a quantization-regularized autoencoder inspired by LDM
Rombach et al. 2022
and VQ-VAE
Van Den Oord et al. 2017
.

Specifically, a visual instruction
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{H_{v}\times W_{v}\times 3}
is mapped to embedding
\bm{e}_{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{h_{v}\times w_{v}\times C_{v}}
(
C_{V}
is latent dimension) by an encoder
\bm{E}
. Embedding
\bm{e}_{v}
is then vector-quantized (VQ) into one of the
degradation codes

\bm{z}_{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{h_{v}\times w_{v}\times C_{v}}
in the codebook
\mathcal{Z}=\{\bm{e}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
of size
K
by

\bm{z}_{v}=\arg\min_{\bm{e}_{k}\in\mathcal{Z}}\left\{\|\bm{e}_{k}-\bm{e}_{v}\|_{2}=\|\bm{e}_{k}-\bm{E}(\bm{v})\|_{2}\right\},

(1)

where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
is Euclidean distance. The degradation code
\bm{z}_{v}
is further reconstructed by a decoder
\bm{D}
to approximate the original visual instruction
\bm{\hat{v}}=\bm{D}(\bm{z}_{v})
.

Training.

The encoder
\bm{E}
, decoder
\bm{D}
and degradation codebook
\mathcal{Z}
are jointly optimized by the following loss:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{inst}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{rec}}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{VQ}},

(2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{VQ}}=\|\text{sg}\left[\bm{E}(\bm{v})-\bm{z}_{v}\right]\|+\|\bm{E}(\bm{v})-\text{sg}\left[\bm{z}_{v}\right]\|,

(3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{rec}}=\|\bm{D}(\bm{z}_{v})-\bm{v}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{LPIPS}}(\bm{D}(\bm{z}_{v}),\bm{v}),

(4)

where
\text{sg}[\cdot]
is the stop-gradient operator
Van Den Oord et al. 2017
, and the gradient back-propagates through
\bm{z}_{v}
by straight-through estimator
Bengio et al. 2013
. The reconstruction loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{rec}}
consists of a mean-squared error (MSE) and a perceptual loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{LPIPS}}

Zhang et al. 2018b
to improve the perception of visual details, balanced by hyperparameter
\lambda
. To improve visual quality, we also adopt a hinge-based adversarial loss
Rombach et al. 2022
;
Esser et al. 2021
. We randomly replace the visual instruction
\bm{v}
with its clean visual ground with a probability of
0.1
during training.

Inference.

The trained encoder
\bm{E}
serves as our Degradation Tokenizer. The visual instructions discretized and encoded by
\bm{E}
capture the essential visual patterns and structures of degradations.
To improve the discriminability of degradations, we also encode the clean visual grounds (“null” in
Fig.

2
) without degradation and subtract their embeddings from those of
\bm{v}
and use the residuals to guide our degradation diffusion in
Section

3.2
.

3.2
Degradation Diffusion

Preliminary: Diffusion.

Diffusion models
Ho et al. 2020
;
Kingma et al. 2021
generate data
\bm{x}_{0}\sim p_{0}(\cdot)
by reversing the following forward process that “diffuses”
\bm{x}_{0}
into standard Gaussian noise
\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})
:

p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t}|\bm{x}_{0})=\mathcal{N}(\alpha_{t}\bm{x}_{0},\sigma_{t}^{2}\bm{I});\hskip 10.00002pt0\leq t\leq 1,

(5)

where
\alpha_{t},\sigma_{t}
define the noise schedule.
The reversing is achieved by solving the reverse-time stochastic differential equation (SDE)
Song et al. 2020b
;
Lu et al. 2022
of the reversed diffusion process:

\mathrm{d}\bm{x}_{t}=\left[f(t)\bm{x}_{t}-g^{2}(t)\nabla_{\bm{x}_{t}}\log p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t})\right]\mathrm{d}t+g(t)\,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\bm{w}}_{t},

(6)

where
\widetilde{\bm{w}}_{t}
is the reverse-time standard Wiener process, and

f(t)=\frac{\mathrm{d}\log\alpha_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t},\hskip 10.00002ptg^{2}(t)=-\sigma_{t}^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}\log(\alpha_{t}/\sigma_{t})}{\mathrm{d}t}.

(7)

A score model
\bm{s}_{\theta}(\bm{x_{t}},t)
is trained to estimate the score
\nabla_{\bm{x}_{t}}\log p_{t}(\bm{x}_{t})
in
Eq.

6
via denoising score matching
Vincent 2011
:

\min_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{t,\bm{\epsilon},\bm{x}_{0},\bm{x}_{t}}\left[\|\epsilon+\sigma_{t}\bm{s}_{\theta}(\bm{x_{t}},t)\|_{2}^{2}\right].

(8)

Degradation Diffusion.

Previous works
Ai et al. 2024
;
Yu et al. 2024
;
Lin et al. 2023a
;
Jiang et al. 2023
model the distribution of HQ images
\bm{x}_{\text{HQ}}
with diffusion models guided by LQ images
\bm{x}_{\text{LQ}}
, so that
\bm{x}_{0}=\bm{x}_{\text{HQ}}
in
Eq.

5
. We call them diffusion in image space. We found their formulation inefficient for instruction-guided image restoration, wherein our visual instructions primarily describe the degradations and are loosely correlated with HQ image semantics. Instead, we formulate our visual instruction guided diffusion model in
degradation space
.

Formally, denote
\mathcal{T}_{\bm{v}}(\bm{x}_{\text{LQ}})
to be the “restored” result of LQ image
\bm{x}_{\text{LQ}}
induced by visual instruction
\bm{v}
. That is,
\mathcal{T}_{\bm{v}}(\bm{x}_{\text{LQ}})
is the “cleaner” version of
\bm{x}_{\text{LQ}}
where the degradations are removed
if and only if
they are indicated by
\bm{v}
, and
\mathcal{T}_{\bm{v}}(\bm{x}_{\text{LQ}})=\bm{x}_{\text{HQ}}
when
\bm{v}
describes all degradations in
\bm{x}_{\text{LQ}}
.
The degradation space
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{v}}
induced by
\bm{v}
is defined as the set of difference vectors between LQ image set
\mathcal{S}_{\text{LQ}}
and their corresponding
\mathcal{T}_{\bm{v}}(\bm{x}_{\text{LQ}})
:

\mathcal{D}_{\bm{v}}:=\left\{\bm{x}_{\text{LQ}}-\mathcal{T}_{\bm{v}}(\bm{x}_{\text{LQ}})\,\big|\,\bm{x}_{\text{LQ}}\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{LQ}}\right\}.

(9)

The degradation diffusion diffuses
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{v}}
into the support set of standard Gaussian noise
\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})
. The degradation diffusion is conditioned on both LQ images (HQ images are unavailable during inference) and visual instruction
\bm{v}
:

\begin{split}&p_{t}(\bm{y}_{t}|\bm{y}_{0},\bm{x}_{\text{LQ}},\bm{v})=\mathcal{N}(\alpha_{t}\bm{y}_{0},\sigma_{t}^{2}\bm{I})\\
&\text{where}\,\,\bm{y}_{0}=\bm{x}_{\text{LQ}}-\mathcal{T}_{\bm{v}}(\bm{x}_{\text{LQ}}),\,\,0\leq t\leq 1.\end{split}

(10)

We train the conditioned score model
\bm{s}_{\theta}(\bm{y_{t}},t,\bm{x}_{\text{LQ}},\bm{v})
to estimate the conditioned score
\nabla_{\bm{y}_{t}}\log p_{t}(\bm{y}_{t}|\bm{x}_{\text{LQ}},\bm{v})
via the following objective:

\min_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{t,\bm{\epsilon},\bm{x}_{\text{LQ}},\bm{v},\bm{x}_{t}}\left[\|\epsilon+\sigma_{t}\bm{s}_{\theta}(\bm{y_{t}},t,\bm{x}_{\text{LQ}},\bm{v})\|_{2}^{2}\right].

(11)

After training, we numerically solve the reverse time SDE for
t=1\to 0
from
\bm{y}_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})
to obtain
\hat{\bm{y}}_{0}
as

\mathrm{d}\bm{y}_{t}=\left[f(t)\bm{y}_{t}-g^{2}(t)\bm{s}_{\theta}(\bm{y_{t}},t,\bm{x}_{\text{LQ}},\bm{v})\right]\mathrm{d}t+g(t)\,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\bm{w}}_{t},

(12)

where
f,g
are defined by
Eq.

7
. The final restored image is then computed as
\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{\bm{v}}(\bm{x}_{\text{LQ}})=\bm{x}_{\text{LQ}}-\hat{\bm{y}}_{0}
.

Our diffusion formulation in degradation space has several advantages over previous diffusion in image space
Ai et al. 2024
;
Yu et al. 2024
;
Lin et al. 2023a
;
Jiang et al. 2023
: 1) it achieves better restoration because
\bm{y}_{t}
is highly correlated with degradations; 2) training is more stable since the distribution of
\bm{y}_{t}
is more consistent than images’, which also reduces required model capacity; 3) our formulation
does nothing
when
\bm{v}
does not match the actual degradation in LQ images, leading to better controllability.
Besides, unlike
Liu et al. 2024c
;
Shi et al. 2023
;
Yue et al. 2024
, our formulation has no special requirement for
\alpha_{t},\sigma_{t}
; thus, we can adapt any pretrained diffusion model into our formulation with minimal efforts.

3.3
Conditioning

The score model
\bm{s}_{\theta}(\bm{y_{t}},t,\bm{x}_{\text{LQ}},\bm{v})
trained by
Eq.

11
is conditioned on the LQ image
\bm{x}_{\text{LQ}}
and visual instruction
\bm{v}
. We implement
\bm{s}_{\theta}
with U-Net
Ronneberger et al. 2015
and introduce our conditioning mechanisms in this section.

Image Restoration Bridge (IRB).

The IRB introduces the conditioning on LQ images. IRB first embeds LQ images into hierarchical feature maps of different resolutions with a lightweight convolutional network
Mou et al. 2024
; each resolution level of feature maps corresponds to that of the diffusion U-Net. The features are injected into the U-Net via AdaLN-Zero
Peebles and Xie 2023
. We found no benefit in concatenating the LQ image with the U-Net input
Brooks et al. 2023
. Unlike previous works
Yu et al. 2024
;
Lin et al. 2023a
;
Ai et al. 2024
, the performance deteriorates if we inject the features into the U-Net decoder with ControlNet
Zhang et al. 2023b
or a similar adapter. We conjecture the reason is that the prediction in degradation space is dissimilar to the LQ images, leading to diverging features in the U-Net encoder.

Visual Instruction Adapter (VIA).

Visual instructions control the diffusion U-Net through the VIA. Inspired by IP-Adapter
Ye et al. 2023
, we copy the cross-attention layer in the pretrained text-to-image diffusion U-Net. The query of the copied cross-attention is the (flattened) U-Net feature map, and the key and value are the degradation codes
\bm{z}_{v}
encoded from the visual instructions
\bm{v}
(see
Section

3.1
). This preserves the text-conditioning ability of the pretrained model, allowing the injection of text descriptions as additional guidance.

Experiment Settings

Datasets and Metrics.

We combined various image restoration datasets to form the training set, following
Zhang et al. 2023a
. This includes datasets for deraining such as Rain1400

Li et al. 2019

Guo et al. 2023

Ancuti et al. 2019
; desnowing datasets such as Snow100K
Liu et al. 2018
and RealSnow
Zhu et al. 2023
; raindrop removal datasets like RainDrop
Qian et al. 2018
and RainDS
Quan et al. 2021
; real denoising with SIDD
Abdelhamed et al. 2018
; motion deblurring with GoPro
Nah et al. 2017
and RealBlur
Rim et al. 2020
; defocus deblurring with DPDD
Abuolaim and Brown 2020
.

During the evaluation, we used Dense-Haze
Ancuti et al. 2019
for dehazing, Rain1400
Fu et al. 2017
for deraining, GoPro
Nah et al. 2017
for motion deblurring, RainDrop
Qian et al. 2018
for raindrop removal, DPDD
Abuolaim and Brown 2020
for defocus deblurring, Snow100K
Liu et al. 2018
for desnowing, SIDD
Abdelhamed et al. 2018
for real denoising, and LIVE1
Sheikh 2005
for JPEG artifact removal (with quality factor
q=10
) on the synthetic datasets.
For real-world degradation datasets, we evaluated our model on the NH-HAZE
Ancuti et al. 2020
dataset, LHP
Guo et al. 2023
dataset, RealBlur
Rim et al. 2020
dataset, and the RealSnow
Zhu et al. 2023
dataset.

Moreover, following the method in
Park et al. 2023
, we constructed a mixed distortion dataset on the WED
Ma et al. 2016
dataset using rain, snow, and noise distortions. We used these diverse relationships in this dataset to evaluate the model’s ability to handle complex distortions. We also introduced underwater datasets EUVP
Islam et al. 2020
and TURBID
Duarte et al. 2016
to examine the ability of our method to deal with composite distortions in special environments.
We used PSNR and SSIM
Wang et al. 2004
as distortion metrics, LPIPS
Zhang et al. 2018b
as perceptual metrics.

Implementation Details.

Defusion was trained using the Adam optimizer
Kingma 2014
with learning rates of
\beta_{1}=0.9
and
\beta_{2}=0.999
, starting with an initial learning rate of 1e-4 and decaying to 1e-6 via cosine annealing
Loshchilov and Hutter 2016
. We trained for 100 epochs on the mixed dataset using 8 NVIDIA A100 GPUs at a resolution of 256x256. Due to varying data volumes for different tasks, we adjusted the frequency of random patch cropping across datasets to balance data quantity. For data augmentation, we applied horizontal and vertical flips to all datasets. The model used the U-Net
Dhariwal and Nichol 2021
architecture, DDIM
Song et al. 2020a
as the sampling strategy during inference, and 4 timesteps for all tasks.

4.3
Ablation Study

In this section, we conduct ablation studies on visual grounding, visual instruction embedding, and degradation diffusion to demonstrate the effectiveness of the different components proposed in Defusion. Naive diffusion is used to exclude diffusion performance in degradation space. Blank grounding consists of a blank image, and simple grounding is a simple striped image. Both are used to test the high response of Grounding to distortion. Text and non-instruction are used to compare the performance of Visual Instruction instructions. The results demonstrate the excellent performance of our visual instruction and degradation diffusion designs.

5
Conclusion

We present
Defusion
, a novel image restoration framework utilizing visual instruction-guided degradation diffusion. Defusion aligns visual instructions with degradation patterns, surpassing traditional methods dependent on ambiguous text-based priors or task-specific models. Operating in degradation space, our model reconstructs high-quality images by denoising the shift between LQ and HQ images, enhancing stability and performance. Extensive experiments demonstrate Defusion’s state-of-the-art performance in various image restoration tasks, including complex real-world scenarios with multiple degradations. The proposed approach sets new state-of-the-art for multitask image restoration with a unified model based on generative models, providing a robust solution to handle a wide range of restoration applications in the real world.
