Clinical features, radiological findings, and mortality in patients with e-cigarette or vaping product use-associated lung injury: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Jenny Jurado-Hernández, Iván Álvarez-Orozco, Edison Salvador-Oscco, Brenda Caira-Chuquineyra, Hernán Andrés Marín-Agudelo, Daniel Fernandez-Guzman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the clinical features, radiological findings, and mortality in patients with e-cigarette or vaping product use-associated lung injury (EVALI).MethodsA systematic search was conducted in Medline/PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Embase, and the Virtual Health Library, up to November 2024. Observational studies reporting clinical manifestations, imaging patterns, and outcomes in patients diagnosed with EVALI according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention case definition were included. After full-text assessment, relevant data were extracted. Pooled prevalence estimates for clinical symptoms and radiological findings was calculated using a random-effects model, with Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation to stabilize variances.ResultsThirty-eight studies were included in the final analysis, comprising 36 from the United States and 2 from Canada, with patients diagnosed between 2019 and 2022 (n = 13,516). The population consisted predominantly of adolescents and young adults, with male predominance. Most patients reported vaping for more than three months prior to symptom onset (data reported in 10 studies). Constitutional symptoms were present in 87.3% of cases (n = 1878/2555), respiratory symptoms 87.1% (n = 3657/5173), and gastrointestinal manifestations in 77.1% (n = 3164/5193). The most frequent clinical manifestations were shortness of breath (82.7%), dyspnea (81.2%), fever (79.9%), cough (77.4%), and nausea/vomiting (75.0%). Hospitalization was required in 94.4% of cases (n = 386/418), with 35.7% requiring intensive care unit admission (n = 2172/5240). Overall mortality was 0.9% (n = 217/10,635), with minimal variation across studies. The most common imaging features were bilateral pulmonary involvement (98.1%) and ground-glass opacities (94.9%).ConclusionEVALI presents with a variable clinical spectrum, primarily characterized by respiratory and constitutional symptoms. Despite its relatively low-mortality-rate, the high rates of hospitalization and ICU admission underscore the severity of this condition. Early recognition and timely medical intervention are essential to reducing morbidity and mortality, particularly among adolescents and young adults who use e-cigarettes or vaping products.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-026-04349-1.Keywords: EVALI, Lung injury, E-cigarettes, Vaping, Systematic review (source: MeSH)

Introduction

The exponential increase in electronic cigarette and vaping product use over the past decade has raised significant public health concerns [1], particularly among adolescents and young adults [2, 3]. By 2022, nearly three million middle and high school students in the United States were actively using e-cigarettes, a trend mirrored globally, including in Latin America and Europe [4]. This widespread adoption has been driven by aggressive marketing, misconceptions about vaping as a harm reduction strategy compared to smoking, and the availability of appealing flavors [5, 6]. Although e-cigarettes may expose users to fewer carcinogenic combustion products than conventional cigarettes, they are not risk-free and have been associated with distinct pulmonary toxicities not observed in traditional smoking. Emerging evidence highlights its harmful effects on respiratory health, especially in adolescents, whose lungs are still developing. Vaping has been associated with pulmonary inflammation, impaired gas exchange, and alveolar damage, increasing the risk of long-term respiratory complications [7].

E-cigarettes contain a variety of toxic substances, including nicotine, volatile organic compounds, heavy metals, and chemical additives, many of which have been linked to airway remodeling, oxidative stress, and impaired surfactant function [8]. These pathological mechanisms contribute to significant respiratory impairment and, in severe cases, acute lung injury [7]. Beyond the clinical consequences, vaping places a substantial economic burden on healthcare systems due to the rising number of hospitalizations, intensive care unit (ICU) admissions, and long-term pulmonary diseases [9].

The risks associated with vaping became evident in 2019 when the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) identified an outbreak of e-cigarette or vaping-associated lung injury (EVALI) in the United States, prompting an extensive epidemiological investigation. Since then, thousands of cases have been reported worldwide, with severity ranging from mild respiratory symptoms to acute respiratory failure and death [10–12]. Characteristic clinical and radiological findings, including ground-glass opacities and bilateral consolidations, have been consistently observed in affected patients [13, 14]. While early reports were concentrated in the United States and Canada, increasing cases in countries such as Canada, Spain, Morocco, South Korea, Brazil, Argentina, Denmark, Saudi Arabia, and Ecuador between 2023 and 2025 underscore the global impact of EVALI [15–21].

Initial investigations linked EVALI to vitamin E acetate, a lipid-soluble additive found in illicit tetrahydrocannabinol (THC)-containing vaping products [22]. However, subsequent research has identified additional toxicants, including diacetyl, formaldehyde, and heavy metals (nickel, chromium, lead), which contribute to pulmonary toxicity [22]. The absence of standardized regulations and the highly variable composition of vaping liquids complicate surveillance efforts and hinder effective prevention strategies [23]. Given the continued emergence of EVALI cases worldwide, comprehensive characterization of its clinical and radiological features is essential to enable early identification and optimize management strategies.

In this context, this systematic review aims to synthesize the available evidence on clinical presentation patterns, the most frequent radiological findings, and EVALI-associated mortality. By consolidating current knowledge, this analysis seeks to provide a framework for early case detection, and inform public health policies, particularly in adolescent populations.

Methods

Study design, reporting and registration

We conducted a systematic review of observational studies following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [24] (Supplementary Material 1). The review protocol was registered in the Prospective International Registry of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under registration code CRD42024525011.

Information sources and search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across Medline/PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Embase, and the Virtual Health Library, covering studies published up to November 18, 2024. The search strategy included a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and relevant synonyms related to “E-Cigarette or Vaping Product Use-Associated Lung Injury” or “EVALI”. The search was tailored to each database to optimize retrieval of relevant studies (Supplementary Material 2). No language restrictions were applied.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they provided primary quantitative data on the clinical or radiological manifestations associated with EVALI. In vitro and animal studies were excluded, as were studies investigating heat-not-burn devices, which do not align with the definition of vaping. Eligible studies included observational research reporting the prevalence of symptoms, signs, and outcomes related to EVALI. Additionally, case series involving more than 10 patients were considered if they provided relevant clinical information. Conference abstracts and proceedings were included if they contained sufficient extractable data.

All included studies employed the CDC case definition for EVALI, which requires: (1) use of e-cigarette or vaping products within 90 days prior to symptom onset; (2) presence of pulmonary infiltrate on chest imaging; (3) absence of pulmonary infection based on laboratory testing; and (4) no alternative plausible diagnosis. We excluded studies that did not explicitly report application of these criteria or used modified definitions without validation. This standardization ensures that the clinical and radiological findings reported across studies reflect a uniform diagnostic entity, reducing case ascertainment bias.

Study selection process

Search results were imported into EndNote® (Clarivate Analytics, EndNote X9), where duplicate records were automatically removed. The remaining records were transferred to Rayyan® (Qatar Computing Research Institute, Doha, Qatar), where additional duplicates were manually eliminated. Titles and abstracts were screened independently by three pairs of researchers (BCC & ESO, IAO & JJH, and HAMA & DFG) through a blinded and independent review. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus or by consulting an adjudicator (DFG or IAO). Full-text articles of potentially eligible studies were subsequently reviewed by the same researcher pairs, with conflicts resolved through the same consensus process by discussion or adjudication.

Data extraction

Data extraction was conducted independently by the three pairs of researchers (BCC & ESO, IAO & JJH, and HAMA & DFG). Extracted data included study characteristics, participant demographics, clinical and radiological findings, and relevant outcomes (hospitalization rates, ICU admission, and mortality). Predefined spreadsheets were used for data entry, and accuracy was ensured by cross-verification. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion or consultation with an adjudicator (DFG or IAO). For each study, the longest follow-up period associated with primary or secondary outcomes was considered. If additional data were needed, corresponding authors were contacted via email; however, no responses were received.

Risk of bias

The risk of bias was independently assessed by one pair of reviewers (BCC & ESO) using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Studies Reporting Prevalence Data [25]. This tool was selected due to its suitability for evaluating symptom prevalence in observational studies, even in case series. The instrument evaluates nine criteria, including sampling appropriateness, study clarity, sample size adequacy, data collection methods, and statistical analysis. Each item was scored 1 for a “Yes” response and 0 for a “No” or “Unclear” response, with total scores ranging from 0 to 9. Studies were classified as low risk of bias (7–9 points), moderate risk (4–6 points), or high risk (0–3 points). Disagreements were resolved through consensus or by consulting a third reviewer (DFG).

Data synthesis strategy

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The “metaprop” function from the “meta” package was used to estimate pooled proportions of clinical and radiological findings. Given the expected heterogeneity among studies, a random-effects model was applied, using the DerSimonian and Laird (DL) method for estimating between-study variance (τ2). In addition, we employed the Freeman-Tukey Double Arcsine Transformation to stabilize prevalence variances. Heterogeneity across studies was assessed using I² statistics, categorized as low (< 40%), moderate (40–80%), or high (> 80%). Finally, A sensitivity analysis was conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity, including only studies with low risk of bias and excluding conference abstracts. Publication bias was not formally assessed, as existing statistical methods for prevalence meta-analyses have limited reliability for datasets with fewer than 10 studies.

Results

Study selection

The systematic search retrieved a total of 3,812 unique studies across databases. After removing 1,809 duplicates, 2,003 studies remained for initial screening. Following title and abstract screening, 1,470 articles were excluded due to irrelevance. Among the 533 full-text articles assessed, 11 were not retrievable, and 522 underwent eligibility evaluation. The most frequent exclusion reasons were case series with fewer than 10 EVALI cases (n = 398), incorrect study population (n = 18), irrelevant outcomes (n = 24), inappropriate study design (n = 31), and non-primary research articles (n = 12). One study was withdrawn post-publication. Ultimately, 38 studies met the inclusion criteria and were analyzed (Fig. 1).

Study and participant characteristics

Of the 38 included studies, 36 were conducted in the United States [26–61], and 2 in Canada [62, 63]. The studies encompassed patients diagnosed between 2019 and 2022, primarily adolescents and young adults, with a predominance of males across all cohorts. Fourteen studies reported that more than 50% of EVALI patients had vaped nicotine, while 24 studies indicated that over 50% had used Tetrahydrocannabinol-containing products. Additionally, among the 10 studies that reported the duration of vaping before clinical presentation, 7 showed a vaping duration exceeding three months (Table 1).

Risk of bias

Of the 38 included studies, 15 (39.5%) were classified as having low risk of bias, 20 (52.6%) as moderate risk, and 3 (7.9%) as high risk. The studies Adkins 2020 [29], and Werner 2020 [60], achieved the highest quality scores (9 points). Conversely, Abraham 2020 [28], Baker 2022 [63], and Panse 2020 [52], received the lowest scores (2 points). Most studies employed appropriate sampling frames (criterion 1: 33/38, 86.8%) and described data analysis with sufficient coverage of the identified sample (criterion 5: 33/38, 86.8%). Appropriate statistical analysis (criterion 8) was conducted in 37 studies (97.4%). A limitation across studies was sample size adequacy (criterion 3), which was sufficient in only 6 studies (15.8%). Subject and setting description (criterion 4) was adequate in only 10 studies (26.3%). Valid methods to identify the condition (criterion 6) were employed in 26 studies (68.4%), while standardized condition measurement (criterion 7) was present in 26 studies (68.4%). Response rate adequacy (criterion 9) was appropriately addressed in 23 studies (60.5%) (Supplementary Material 3).

Clinical manifestations and mortality

The pooled analysis showed that constitutional symptoms were reported in 87.3% (95% CI: 80.2–93.2%) of cases from 10 studies (I² = 87.63%) (Fig. 2A), respiratory symptoms in 87.1% (95% CI: 78.5–93.8%) from 11 studies (I² = 94%) (Fig. 2B), and gastrointestinal symptoms in 77.1% (95% CI: 69.5–84.1%) from 12 studies (I² = 95%) (Fig. 2C). The overall mortality rate remained relatively low at 0.9% (95% CI: 0.6–1.2%) across 13 studies with minimal heterogeneity (I² = 0%) (Fig. 2D). Considering only studies with a low risk of bias due to the heterogeneity observed, our analysis showed that the most frequently reported clinical manifestations included shortness of breath (82.7%, 95% CI: 78.7–86.3%, 7 studies, I² = 19.4%), dyspnea or respiratory distress (81.2%, 95% CI: 77.7–93.6%, 8 studies, I² = 86.7%), fever (79.9%, 95% CI: 72.6–86.3%, 11 studies, I² = 71.6%), cough (77.4%, 95% CI: 67.6–86.0%, 10 studies, I² = 80.5%), and nausea and/or vomiting (75.0%, 95% CI: 65.4–83.5%, 10 studies, I² = 82.5%). Less commonly reported symptoms were neck/back pain (10.5%, 2 studies, I² = 0%) and hemoptysis (7.2%; 95% CI: 4.8–10.0%, 6 studies, I² = 0%) (Fig. 3). The frequency of clinical manifestations, considering all studies with and without low risk of bias, is available in Supplementary Material 4.

Hospitalization and intensive care unit admission

The pooled prevalence of hospitalization was 94.4% (95% CI: 88.7–98.4%; I2 = 61.8%) across 8 studies with no high risk of bias (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, intensive care unit admission was required in 35.7% (95% CI: 25.1–47.1%; I2 = 98.2%) of 5,240 patients across 12 studies (Fig. 4B).

Radiological findings

Radiological findings from studies with low risk of bias revealed bilateral pulmonary involvement (98.1%, 95% CI: 96.1–99.6%, 9 studies, I² = 0.0%) and ground-glass opacities (94.9%, 95% CI: 83.5–100.0%, 9 studies, I² = 92.0%) as nearly universal features. Other common radiological manifestations included thickening of the respiratory tract wall (81.1%, 95% CI: 63.2–94.4%, 1 study), mediastinal and/or hilar lymphadenopathy (76.4%, 95% CI: 44.3–97.8%, 1 study), and subpleural sparing (74.1%, 95% CI: 44.8–95.4%, 8 studies, I² = 98.4%). Intermediate frequency findings included crazy paving (51.2%, 95% CI: 0.0-100.0%, 2 studies, I² = 94.7%), interlobular septal thickening (50.9%, 95% CI: 23.8–77.7%, 4 studies, I² = 78.4%), and lobular sparing (49.6%, 95% CI: 0.0-100.0%, 2 studies, I² = 95.0%). Less common findings were pleural effusion (17.5%, 95% CI: 10.2–25.9%, 6 studies, I² = 43.1%) and pneumomediastinum (9.9%, 95% CI: 3.2–19.0%, 4 studies, I² = 49.3%), while pneumothorax was rare (4.7%, 95% CI: 0.0-16.2%, 2 studies, I² = 59.8%) (Fig. 5). Discussion

Summary of main findings

Our analysis synthesized data from 38 studies evaluating the clinical presentation, hospitalization rates, mortality, and radiological findings in patients diagnosed with EVALI. Our findings reinforce prior research on the respiratory complications of vaping, demonstrating that EVALI follows a consistent clinical pattern across multiple studies. Thus, constitutional, respiratory, and gastrointestinal symptoms are the primary clinical presentations of EVALI [31, 53]. The high prevalence of dyspnea, cough, fever, and nausea aligns with the clinical profiles described in earlier reports, reinforcing the necessity of a comprehensive symptom assessment in suspected cases [7, 47]. In addition, some patient may present atypical symptoms how pericardial effusion, pericarditis [64, 65].

Our pooled mortality rate of 0.9% is slightly lower than previously reported estimates. Early CDC surveillance data indicated a mortality rate of 1.9% [10] while Werner 2020 [60] documented a rate of 2.3% in adult populations based on CDC case reports, with fatal outcomes attributed to chronic comorbidities, respiratory conditions, and cardiac diseases. The lower mortality observed in our review may be partially explained by the demographic composition of included studies, which predominantly comprised adolescent populations.

Hospitalization rates in our meta-analysis were comparable to prior reports, where estimates ranged from 80% to 95% [66], confirming the substantial healthcare burden imposed by EVALI [67]. Additionally, ICU admission rates in our study (87.1%) were higher than those reported in earlier cohorts (30–70%) [66], suggesting that more recent cases may involve greater disease severity or reflect an increased recognition of critical care needs [67]. These findings underscore the need for continued vigilance and early intervention strategies to mitigate severe disease progression.

The radiological patterns observed in our study corroborate prior findings. Bilateral pulmonary involvement, ground-glass opacities, subpleural sparing, and interlobular septal thickening were the most consistent imaging features, in agreement with reports from Sreedharan 2021 [13], and Girvin 2020 [68]. The distribution pattern of subpleural sparing, combined with septal thickening, may facilitate differentiation from COVID-19 pneumonia and hypersensitivity pneumonitis, where these features present with distinct morphological patterns. While ground-glass opacities are well-established as a hallmark of EVALI, our meta-analysis provides novel quantitative insights into less frequently reported findings. Specifically, we report precise pooled prevalence estimates for crazy paving pattern (51.2%), a finding often overlooked in individual case series but potentially indicative of disease stage and severity. Furthermore, our quantification of rare but severe manifestations, pneumomediastinum (9.9%) and pneumothorax (4.7%), provides evidence-based thresholds for identifying high-risk patients requiring intensive monitoring and aggressive intervention.

Comparison with existing literature

Our findings expand upon existing knowledge by providing robust, quantitative synthesis across adolescent and adult populations. Compared to the comprehensive report by Rao et al. [53], which described clinical features in 13 adolescent EVALI patients, our analysis of 38 studies, including adolescent-specific cohorts—confirms their observation that gastrointestinal symptoms (77.1% vs. 92% in Rao et al.) and constitutional symptoms are prominent in younger patients, while providing more precise prevalence estimates with narrower confidence intervals due to our substantially larger sample size.

Regarding Rebuli et al. [7], the American Thoracic Society Workshop Report—our radiological findings provide empirical validation for their proposed pathophysiological framework. While Rebuli et al. emphasized vitamin E acetate-induced surfactant dysfunction as the primary mechanism, our quantification of radiological patterns supports their hypothesized progression from acute exudative to organizing phases of lung injury. Ground-glass opacities represent the exudative phase, while interlobular septal thickening and crazy paving patterns indicate progression to organizing pneumonia. Our pooled estimates thus bridge the gap between pathophysiological theory and clinical-radiological practice, offering quantitative benchmarks for disease staging that were not previously available.

Pathophysiological mechanisms and future risks

EVALI is believed to result from direct lung injury caused by inhaled toxicants present in e-cigarette aerosols. Compounds such as Acenaphthylene, Antimony, Diacetyl, Formaldehyde, Nickel, Glycerol, Propylene glycol, and other components present in aerosols were found in vaping aerosols [69], these compounds have been listed by the FDA as harmful substances [70].

Multiple mechanisms have been implicated, including epithelial toxicity, oxidative stress, immune dysregulation, and surfactant dysfunction. These toxicants induce a pro-inflammatory cascade, characterized by alveolar macrophage dysfunction, reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, and epithelial barrier disruption, culminating in acute lung injury [71]. The strongest association has been with vitamin E acetate, a lipid-soluble additive used in illicit THC-containing e-liquids [10]. Inhalation of this compound disrupts surfactant homeostasis, leading to alveolar collapse and impaired gas exchange [72]. Lipid-laden macrophages observed in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid suggest that impaired lipid metabolism plays a central role in disease pathogenesis [73]. However, other lipophilic additives may exert similar effects, indicating that new formulations could present ongoing risks [74].

Beyond lipid accumulation, volatile organic compounds such as benzene, formaldehyde, and acrolein induce oxidative stress and inflammation by generating reactive oxygen species [75]. These compounds promote mitochondrial dysfunction, DNA damage, and increased pulmonary permeability, contributing to both acute and long-term lung injury [76]. Additionally, heavy metals such as nickel, chromium, and lead have been detected in e-cigarette aerosols, causing direct cytotoxicity and immune activation [77]. The chronic inhalation of these byproducts raises concerns about persistent pulmonary damage and increased susceptibility to infections [78]. Immune dysregulation has also been proposed as a key mechanism in EVALI pathogenesis [78, 79]. Pro-inflammatory cytokine release, macrophage dysfunction, and T-cell activation contribute to a sustained inflammatory response resembling chemical pneumonitis [80]. Some studies suggest that vaping alters lung microbiota and epithelial integrity, potentially predisposing individuals to secondary infections and chronic inflammation [79]. Moreover, thermal degradation of e-liquid components generates diketones and aldehydes, further exacerbating airway inflammation and oxidative injury [81].

Public health implications

Although the 2019 EVALI outbreak was primarily attributed to vitamin E acetate in illicit THC-containing products, the dynamic evolution of vaping formulations and the identification of novel toxicants continue to pose risks for future cases, as evidenced by sporadic reports published in 2024 describing cases diagnosed during earlier periods [21]. Changes in manufacturing practices, the introduction of novel additives, and potential adulteration of products could reintroduce similar outbreaks. Additionally, the rising use of synthetic cannabinoids and flavoring agents raises concerns about unrecognized toxicological effects. Given the expanding use of e-cigarettes, particularly among adolescents and young adults, ongoing surveillance and toxicological research are critical for identifying emerging risk factors and preventing future outbreaks.

Regulatory frameworks for e-cigarettes vary substantially across regions, potentially influencing product composition and EVALI risk profiles. In the United States, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) maintains a premarket authorization requirement for e-cigarette products [82], though enforcement gaps persist for disposable and synthetic nicotine devices [83]. Canada prohibits certain flavoring additives and mandates nicotine concentration limits (maximum 66 mg/mL) [84], which may reduce exposure to respiratory toxicants. The European Union’s Tobacco Products Directive (2014/40/EU) imposes strict limits on nicotine content (≤ 20 mg/mL) and requires ingredient disclosure [85], potentially explaining the lower EVALI incidence reported in European cohorts.

In Latin America, regulatory approaches remain fragmented [86]. Brazil maintains a complete ban since 2009, yet a thriving black market dominates with disposable vapes comprising 54% of market share [86]. Mexico implemented a sales prohibition in 2022 [86]; although the Supreme Court ruled the ban unconstitutional, the lower house approved in December 2024 a constitutional amendment to reinstate it, with disposable products currently accounting for 37% of the market through illicit channels [86]. Venezuela introduced a comprehensive ban in 2023, constraining the market to $15 million and forcing users to rely on unofficial distribution [86]. Conversely, Colombia and Peru permit regulated sales with weak oversight, requiring only basic safety standards [86]; Colombia exhibits a mature e-cigarette culture with 60% open-system use and 90% online purchasing, while Peru shows rising disposable popularity (29%) amid limited regulatory enforcement [86]. Argentina is transitioning toward formal regulation, planning a 20% tax policy to shift activity from the black market, though economic volatility delays implementation [86]. Chile will introduce a regulatory framework between May 2024 and January 2025, with open systems currently dominating (84%) in a stable economic environment [86]. These disparities likely contribute to regional differences in product formulations, particularly regarding vitamin E acetate and synthetic cannabinoid additives, and may account for the geographic concentration of cases observed in this review. Harmonized international surveillance systems are needed to monitor how regulatory variations affect vaping-related lung injury patterns globally.

Our findings highlight the urgent need for stricter regulatory measures on vaping products, including age restrictions, marketing limitations, labeling transparency and restrictions on flavored e-liquids that appeal to adolescents. The predominance of young patients affected by EVALI underscores the importance of targeted public health campaigns aimed at reducing vaping initiation among this population. Additionally, improved surveillance systems are necessary to track vaping-related lung injuries in real time, facilitating early intervention and policy adjustments.

Strengths and limitations

This systematic review provides a comprehensive synthesis of EVALI cases across multiple studies, applying rigorous inclusion criteria and meta-analytic techniques to quantify clinical and radiological findings. By focusing on studies with low risk of bias, our findings offer robust estimates that enhance the understanding of EVALI’s clinical course.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. Heterogeneity in age distribution across studies represents a significant confounding factor, particularly for mortality estimates. Additional heterogeneity in symptom prevalence across studies suggests that differences in study design (cross-sectional vs. case series), case definitions, and diagnostic criteria may have influenced results. The underreporting of key variables, such as long-term pulmonary outcomes, limits the ability to draw conclusions regarding the chronic effects of EVALI. The predominance of studies from the United States and Canada, rather than North America broadly, may also restrict the generalizability of our findings to other regions with different vaping product compositions and regulations. Future research should aim to evaluate potential chronic lung dysfunction, and explore alternative pathophysiological mechanisms contributing to vaping-associated lung injury.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provides a detailed characterization of EVALI’s clinical manifestations, hospitalization rates, and radiological features. The findings confirm that EVALI is a severe pulmonary condition associated with significant respiratory morbidity, emphasizing the need for early recognition, accurate diagnosis, and timely medical intervention. Despite its relatively low mortality, the high rates of ICU admission and severe respiratory symptoms underscore the substantial health risks associated with vaping. Public health efforts should focus on preventive strategies, stronger regulatory frameworks, and ongoing surveillance to mitigate the long-term consequences of vaping-related lung injury.
