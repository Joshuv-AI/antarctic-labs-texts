GLP-1 receptor agonists at the crossroads of metabolic and gut inflammation: Implications for people with type 2 diabetes and inflammatory bowel disease
Authors: Dured Dardari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Obesity and type 2 diabetes now coexist with IBD. Beyond glucose and weight effects, GLP-1 receptor agonists reduce MACE and lower systemic inflammatory biomarkers. Observational IBD data suggest acceptable safety and fewer hospitalisations and surgeries, warranting carefully designed prospective trials.

Key messages

What is already known? Overweight and obesity are increasingly common in IBD and can influence disease outcomes, extra-intestinal manifestations, surgical risk, and response to established IBD therapies.

What is new here? GLP-1 receptor agonists, now a cornerstone of modern diabetology, reduce cardiometabolic events and lower systemic inflammatory biomarkers. In people with IBD, observational data suggest acceptable safety (including no clear excess obstruction risk) and possible reductions in hospitalisation and surgery.

How can this study help patient care? For metabolically eligible patients with IBD, GLP-1 receptor agonists can deliver proven cardiometabolic benefit while offering a testable hypothesis for systemic inflammation reduction. A pragmatic prescribing framework and prospective trials with objective endpoints are needed.

Main text

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) has traditionally been framed as a disorder of intestinal inflammation and malnutrition. That paradigm is shifting. In parallel with population trends, overweight and obesity are now common among adults with IBD, and a growing proportion of patients live with type 2 diabetes (T2D) or steroid-induced dysglycaemia.1,2 This epidemiologic transition matters to diabetologists because cardiometabolic risk is increasingly embedded in IBD care, while gastrointestinal symptoms, inflammation and surgery can complicate diabetes management.

To provide context, contemporary series suggest that 15%-40% of adults with IBD have obesity and 20%-40% are overweight, with rising mean BMI across both trials and real-world cohorts.1,2 Adiposity and IBD incidence do not follow a simple linear relationship. In prospective cohorts, obesity has been reported to associate inversely with overall IBD incidence (RR 0.76; 95% CI 0.66-0.88; I2 93.2%), while increasing BMI was not consistently associated with incident IBD or exacerbation, underscoring heterogeneity and residual confounding. 3

Once IBD is established, higher adiposity, particularly visceral adipose tissue (VAT), has been linked to clinically relevant outcomes. VAT has been associated with lower rates of clinical and endoscopic remission. 4 In a population-based ulcerative colitis cohort, obesity was associated with a higher risk of IBD-related hospitalisation. 5 Beyond luminal activity, adiposity may also amplify systemic disease and procedural risk, reinforcing the need to assess metabolic status rather than treat IBD in isolation.

Obesity also influences procedural risk and drug response, domains directly relevant to multidisciplinary care. A systematic review and meta-analysis concluded that overweight and obesity are associated with higher rates of surgical complications in IBD, particularly postoperative infectious complications. 6 Pharmacologically, adipose tissue can contribute to inter-individual variability in drug exposure. For biologics, obesity has been associated with suboptimal response to anti-tumor necrosis factor (anti-TNF) therapy in systematic review and meta-analysis. 7

These clinical patterns align with a broader immunometabolic concept familiar in diabetology: T2D is characterised by chronic, low-grade systemic inflammation (‘metaflammation’), driven in part by VAT inflammation, innate immune activation, oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction. GLP-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) were developed for glycaemic control and weight loss, but accumulating evidence indicates they also reduce systemic inflammatory biomarkers beyond metabolic improvements. A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised trials reported significant reductions in inflammatory biomarkers, including C-reactive protein and tumor necrosis factor-alpha, with GLP-1RA therapy. 8

From a cardiometabolic standpoint, GLP-1RAs are now defined by outcome benefits. Cardiovascular outcome trials in T2D demonstrated reductions in major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) with semaglutide and dulaglutide.9,10 Semaglutide also reduced MACE in people with overweight or obesity and established cardiovascular disease even without diabetes. 11 For patients with IBD who meet metabolic indications, these benefits are intrinsically valuable regardless of any intestinal effect.

However, the diabetology perspective raises an additional, testable hypothesis: if GLP-1RAs reduce systemic inflammatory tone in T2D and obesity, could this dampen intestinal inflammation or systemic EIM biology in a subset of patients with IBD? Observational IBD data do not prove causality, but they make the question difficult to ignore, especially because IBD outcomes are influenced by obesity, visceral adiposity and systemic inflammation.4,6

Clinical evidence in IBD remains largely observational. In a Danish nationwide registry study including 3,751 patients with IBD and T2D (2007-2019), exposure to GLP-1-based therapies was associated with a more favourable IBD disease course. 12 A large U.S. propensity-matched cohort similarly reported improved IBD outcomes among people with T2D treated with GLP-1RAs. 13 A nationwide cohort study focusing on safety found no increased risk of ileus or intestinal obstruction with GLP-1RAs in patients with IBD. 14

Systematic syntheses support, but do not settle, the question. A peer-reviewed systematic review and meta-analysis (11 studies; 16,242 GLP-1RA-exposed patients) reported consistent short-term weight loss and suggested lower risks of surgery and hospitalisation, particularly among patients with obesity; the authors emphasised heterogeneity, confounding, and the absence of randomised trials with objective mucosal endpoints. 15

Mechanistic plausibility exists at the interface of incretin biology, immunometabolism and barrier function. Beyond weight loss and glycaemia, GLP-1-based therapies have been reported to modulate macrophage polarisation, innate immune signalling, endothelial function and oxidative stress. 16 Candidate mechanisms include improved epithelial barrier integrity, altered mucus-layer biology, bile-acid and microbiome-mediated metabolic shifts, neuroimmune signalling, and activation of mucosal repair pathways. Repair biology warrants both enthusiasm and caution in long-standing colitis. In murine models, GLP-1 receptor agonism promoted intestinal growth and crypt fission through mechanisms requiring fibroblast growth factor 7 (FGF7). 17 Such pathways may be beneficial for epithelial restitution, but they also justify prospective studies that incorporate long-term safety, dysplasia surveillance where clinically indicated, and objective mucosal endpoints rather than symptom scores alone.

Practical prescribing considerations are essential in diabetology clinics, where GLP-1RAs are often initiated and titrated. First, gastrointestinal adverse effects (nausea, early satiety, diarrhoea, constipation) are common and can overlap with IBD symptoms; slow titration, dietary counselling and proactive side-effect management are key.18,19 Symptom change should therefore be interpreted cautiously, because improved bowel frequency may reflect motility or intake effects rather than true anti-inflammatory efficacy.

Second, body-composition change matters. GLP-1RA-induced weight loss includes reductions in lean mass, and emerging data suggest that lean mass loss may be clinically meaningful, particularly in populations at baseline risk of sarcopenia, such as IBD. 18 For patients with IBD receiving GLP-1RAs, pragmatic mitigation strategies include resistance exercise, adequate protein intake, and periodic functional and/or body-composition assessment when feasible. 18

Third, contraindications and safety warnings must be individualised. Product labelling for semaglutide includes contraindications related to hypersensitivity and to personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2, and carries warnings regarding pancreatitis and gallbladder disease. 19 Advanced renal impairment and severe gastrointestinal motility disorders may warrant additional caution, particularly when dehydration from gastrointestinal adverse effects is a concern. 19

Fourth, delayed gastric emptying can alter exposure to oral medications. Although clinically important interactions are uncommon, caution is warranted for time-critical oral drugs and those with narrow therapeutic indices.19,20 In IBD, this consideration extends to selected oral immunomodulators and other chronic therapies when significant weight loss, vomiting or diarrhoea occur; therapeutic monitoring may be appropriate in selected patients.

Finally, prospective evidence is now feasible and needed. Ongoing clinical trials are investigating GLP-1RAs in IBD-relevant contexts, including NCT05196958, NCT06774079 and NCT06937099. To be practice-changing for diabetologists and gastroenterologists alike, future trials should incorporate objective intestinal endpoints (endoscopy, biomarkers such as faecal calprotectin), cardiometabolic outcomes, and body-composition and functional measures.

In conclusion, the convergence of IBD with obesity and T2D reframes GLP-1RAs as more than glucose-lowering drugs. For metabolically eligible patients with IBD, GLP-1RAs provide proven cardiometabolic benefit and a biologically plausible route to reduce systemic inflammation. Whether this translates into disease modification of IBD remains an open, but now testable, question.

Key concepts: (i) metabolic effects (weight loss, improved glycaemia) reduce visceral adiposity and systemic inflammatory tone; (ii) immunometabolic effects may modulate macrophage activation, oxidative stress and endothelial function; (iii) gut effects may include barrier protection, neuroimmune modulation and activation of mucosal repair pathways. The relative contribution of direct gut effects versus systemic metabolic inflammation reduction remains uncertain and requires prospective study.
