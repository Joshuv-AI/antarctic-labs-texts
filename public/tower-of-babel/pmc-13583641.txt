Neuroschistosomiasis Mimicking Spinal Tuberculosis: A Case Report
Authors: Muhammadu Sani Abubakar, Jabiru Muazu, Abdulwahab Elladan Yusuf, Nasir Bakiyawa Abubakar, Salisu Aliyu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Schistosomiasis is a chronic parasitic disease acquired through freshwater exposure, in which cercariae penetrate the skin and mature into adult worms. Disease manifestations largely result from granulomatous inflammation and fibrosis surrounding retained eggs. A 65-year-old widow presented with six months of lower back pain, weight loss, paraplegia (power 1/5), sphincter dysfunction, a T12 sensory level, and T9–T10 gibbus. She had received empirical antitubercular therapy for two months without improvement. Thoracolumbar MRI demonstrated vertebral and disc collapse at T10–T11 and L1–L2, with adjacent paravertebral abscesses and cord compression. Surgical biopsies revealed numerous calcified Schistosoma haematobium eggs with terminal spines, surrounded by granulomas containing epithelioid histiocytes, foreign-body giant cells, lymphocytes, and eosinophils. She received weekly praziquantel (1,200 mg) and tapered prednisolone. Motor power improved to 2/5, although urinary and faecal incontinence persisted. Neuroschistosomiasis should be considered in endemic regions, particularly when presumed spinal tuberculosis fails to respond to treatment. Definitive diagnosis requires careful clinicoradiological and histopathological evaluation.

CASE:

The index case was a 65-year-old widow who presented with a six-month history of progressive dull lower back pain that later became stabbing, with radicular pain radiating to the groin and lower limbs. This was associated with progressive bilateral lower-limb weakness, eventually rendering her bedbound, followed by urinary and faecal incontinence. She also reported low-grade fever, night sweats, fatigue, and unintentional weight loss. There was no history of chronic cough, tuberculosis exposure or previous treatment for tuberculosis, spinal trauma, or cave/abandoned-room exposure. However, she reported childhood swimming in stagnant water and fetching water from rivers and lakes, although she could not recall previous haematuria. She was initially managed at a referring centre for presumed spinal tuberculosis (Pott's disease) with rifampicin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol for two months without clinical improvement. Examination revealed an ill-looking elderly woman with preserved body habitus. Neurologically, she was conscious and oriented, with no cranial nerve abnormalities or meningeal signs. Lower-limb power was 1/5 bilaterally, with hypotonia and hyporeflexia, a T12 sensory level, reduced anal sphincter tone (DRESS grade 2), and a T9–T10 gibbus.

Investigations showed a markedly elevated ESR (120 mm/h), normal eosinophil count, negative HIV screening, and negative sputum GeneXpert for Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Chest radiography and abdominal ultrasonography were unremarkable, while urine and stool examinations revealed no ova. Whole-spine MRI demonstrated collapse of the D10 and L2 vertebral bodies, associated spinal canal stenosis and severe cord/cauda equina compression. The affected vertebrae were relatively hypointense on T1 and hyperintense on T2/STIR, with homogeneous enhancement. Moderately rim-enhancing prevertebral collections extended from D9 to D12, with a smaller epidural collection around D10 and associated cord oedema. The differential diagnoses included spinal tuberculosis, neuroschistosomiasis, spinal metastasis, and pyogenic spondylodiscitis. In view of persistent neurological deterioration despite antitubercular therapy, spinal decompressive laminectomy and biopsy were performed.

Histology demonstrated numerous calcified Schistosoma haematobium eggs with characteristic terminal spines, surrounded by granulomas composed of epithelioid histiocytes and foreign-body multinucleated giant cells, with extensive fibrosis and lymphocytic and eosinophilic infiltration; no viable eggs were identified. The patient was treated with praziquantel (50 mg/kg/day for one week, repeated after four weeks) and tapered prednisolone (0.5–1 mg/kg/day for one month), followed by neurorehabilitation. At two-month follow-up, lower-limb power had improved from 1/5 to 2/5, although she remained catheter-dependent. The case highlights the importance of considering neuroschistosomiasis in patients with destructive spinal lesions, particularly in endemic settings and when presumed spinal tuberculosis fails to respond to appropriate therapy.

Discussion

1. Epidemiology/Pathophysiology

Spinal Schistosomiasis, while generally considered a rare ectopic manifestation of a systemic parasitic infection, represents the most debilitating form of the disease. It often results from the retrograde migration of Schistosoma eggs into the Batson venous plexus. [1,2] This valveless network provides a low-pressure conduit for the ova to reach the spinal cord, particularly when elevated intra-abdominal pressure facilitates flow from the portal and pelvic systems. [2] Once deposited in the nervous tissue, these eggs trigger an intense immunological response characterized by granuloma formation and vasculitis, which can ultimately lead to ischemic necrosis and permanent neurological morbidity. [1,3,4].

Epidemiological data suggests that the condition is frequently underdiagnosed in non-endemic regions due to a lack of travel history, early screening and the fact that more than half of affected patients may present

with negative stool parasitological examinations. [2,5] As demonstrated in this index case, a 65-year-old widow, a childhood history of swimming in stagnant water and fetching water from rivers and lakes serves as the primary environmental exposure, even in the absence of recalled childhood hematuria.

Depending on the anatomical distribution of the parasitic granulomas within the spinal canal, the spectrum of clinical presentation is often categorized into medullary, myeloradicular, or conus-cauda equina syndromes (our index case has a combination of the later two syndromes). [8] Clinical manifestations typically follow an extrinsic cord compressive pattern characterized by acute or sub-acute onset of radicular back pain, progressive lower limb weakness, and autonomic disturbances (such as urinary retention) later. [2] In addition to these hallmark symptoms, male patients may frequently experience erectile dysfunction as the infection evolves into specific clinical forms such as conus medullaris or cauda equina syndrome. [2] In this index case, the patient's symptoms progressed in a typical above fashion over 6 months to a bedridden state, accompanied by fecal and urinary incontinence with associated constitutional symptoms of low-grade fever, night sweats, fatigue, and weight loss.

2. Diagnostic Challenges

A major diagnostic hurdle is that more than half of the affected patients present with negative stool parasitological examinations. [2,5] For this category of patients, it necessitates the use of high-quality cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) serology assays to detect elevated protein levels and eosinophilia, which are critical diagnostic indicators when peripheral tests remain inconclusive. [2]

Diagnosis of neuroschistosomiasis is challenging because its clinical manifestations overlap considerably with chronic infections and malignancies. The patient presented with lower back pain radiating to the groin and legs, progressive lower-limb weakness, and constitutional symptoms suggestive of Pott's disease or spinal malignancy. Examination revealed flaccid paraparesis (power 1/5), hypotonia, a T12 sensory level, and reduced anal sphincter tone (DRESS grade 2). Given the strong clinical resemblance to spinal tuberculosis, she was empirically treated with rifampicin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol for two months without improvement. This prompted further evaluation, including targeted imaging and histopathological assessment, to establish the diagnosis of neuroschistosomiasis. [4]

3. Imaging Differential Diagnosis

Beyond the morphological challenges in diagnosis, the degree of host's immune reactivity significantly influences the clinical severity and progression of this inflammatory disease. The differential diagnosis therefore remains broad, as these imaging features frequently mimic intramedullary tumors such as astrocytomas, or inflammatory conditions like transverse myelitis and dural arteriovenous fistulas. [2,3]

The eventual development of helminthic granulomas in the course of the disease further complicate the clinical picture by presenting as "string of beads" nodular patterns or tumor-like expansions. In pediatric patients, these expansions can easily be misidentified, frequently leading to unnecessary and high-risk surgical interventions. [6,7] Such architectural variations necessitate rigorous histopathological correlation or advanced molecular detection methods to distinguish the schistosomal granulomas from neoplastic tissue. [8]

4. Treatment and Outcome

Praziquantel therapy remains the cornerstone of management together with corticosteroids, the later to attenuate the intense periovular inflammatory response and prevent progressive neural damage. [8,9] Because corticosteroids serve to inhibit the inflammatory cascade and mitigate secondary neurological sequelae, their administration is considered standard in acute settings, though the optimal duration remains a subject of ongoing clinical debate. [9]

Clinical experience indicates that medical management is generally superior to surgical excision, as laminectomy and aggressive myelotomy are often discouraged due to the risk of worsening neurological function. [12] Surgical decompression is reserved strictly for cases exhibiting significant spinal cord compression or failed response to medical management (as in our index case), as biopsy risks exacerbating existing neurological deficits in the presence of inflammatory mass lesions. [9,10] In cases where biopsy is deemed essential for definitive diagnosis, the procedure is associated with significant morbidity, as the granulomatous tissue is often deeply embedded within the medullary parenchyma. [1] Consequently, clinicians must rely on a multimodal approach that integrates epidemiological risk, elevated eosinophil counts, and characteristic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) patterns to initiate empirical anti-helminthic therapy prior to irreversible structural damage. [3,11]

In our patient, the definitive diagnosis was unfortunately made post-operatively via tissue histology following the surgical intervention. This may likely carries an increased risk of delayed recovery, complications, and worsened neurological function. The medical management was only instituted after the surgical intervention, which may compound and hinder her long-term neurological recovery.

5. Lessons from the Case

Integrate Remote Exposure History:

Clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion and takes a thorough history for remote childhood exposures such as swimming in or fetching water from rivers/lakes, even in elderly patients presenting decades later in both endemic and non-endemic settings. [2,5]

Avoid Premature Diagnostic Anchoring:

Chronic constitutional symptoms combined with paraparesis should not automatically result as a conclusive evidence to a diagnosis of Spinal Tuberculosis, especially when the patient fails to improve after a 2-month intensive RIPE phase.

Prioritization of Non-Invasive Multimodal diagnostic approach:

Clinicians must rely on a multimodal approach that integrates epidemiological risk, high-quality CSF serology (elevated protein and eosinophilia), and characteristic MRI patterns to initiate empirical steroid and anti-helminthic therapy prior to irreversible structural damage. [3,11]

Medical Management First:

Surgical intervention and invasive medullary biopsies carry high morbidity and can worsen neurological outcomes. [1,12] Empirical medical management with Praziquantel single dose or given over weeks apart and tapered dose corticosteroids should be prioritized over aggressive surgical excision unless severe, mechanical spinal cord compression suggest otherwise. [9,10]

Spinal schistosomiasis, although uncommon, is a potentially devastating yet treatable cause of acute or subacute myelopathy in endemic regions. A high index of suspicion is essential in patients with progressive neurological symptoms and freshwater exposure. Because its clinical and MRI features may mimic infection, inflammation, or malignancy, diagnosis should integrate exposure history, characteristic imaging, and serological or parasitological evidence. Early treatment with praziquantel and corticosteroids is crucial to limit irreversible neurological damage and improve outcomes.
