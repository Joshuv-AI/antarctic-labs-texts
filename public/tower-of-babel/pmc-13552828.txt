Making Patient-reported Outcome Measures Patient-friendly: Applying “Patient-reported Outcomes Tools: Engaging Users and Stakeholders” Guidelines to a Graphical “Report Card” for Cleft Outcomes
Authors: Elaine Lin, Marina Sawires, Katie Brown, Angel Scialdone, Tara Pillai, Victoria Yi, Meredith Cox, Kamlesh Patel, Christopher Runyan, Alexander C. Allori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), such as the CLEFT-Q, provide valuable insights into the psychosocial and functional well-being of patients with cleft lip and/or palate. Ideally, PROM data should not only inform clinicians and researchers but should also be shared back with patients and families; however, no guidelines exist for formatting CLEFT-Q data for patient review. This study evaluated different methods of displaying CLEFT-Q data to identify visual formats best suited for patient and clinician use.Methods:Patients and families participated in a modified Delphi process to assess data presentation formats developed according to the Patient-reported Outcomes Tools: Engaging Users and Stakeholders (PROTEUS) Consortium guidelines. Participants completed interpretation tasks, rated clarity and usefulness, and provided qualitative feedback. Using iterative Delphi rounds, the visualization style, color use, and semantic markups were refined.Results:Tables and complex graphics (eg, polar charts, histograms, ridgeplots) were considered overly complex. Bar charts were well-understood, but participants preferred a simplified “horizontal number line” showing score ranges, individual scores, and comparators (eg, average scores of similar patients). “Traffic light” color motifs were rejected because they connoted “good” versus “bad,” whereas gray backgrounds were preferred for maintaining objectivity and clarity.Conclusions:Data visualization must balance information density with clarity and understandability. Statistical graphics that are preferred by clinicians and researchers were shown to be too confusing, whereas an infographic “number line” was preferred by patients and families. Our findings for CLEFT-Q and FACE-Q may be applicable to other PROMs.

Takeaways

Question: What is the optimal visual format for communicating CLEFT-Q patient-reported outcome measures data to patients and families during their multidisciplinary cleft team visit?

Findings: Through a modified Delphi process, patients and parents showed preference for simple, monochromatic horizontal number-line infographics over numerical tables or complex statistical charts. Participants disliked “traffic light” color palettes, noting they assigned emotionally biased qualitative “grades” to a child’s well-being.

Meaning: To maximize comprehension and minimize unintended emotional distress, clinicians should use simple, grayscale number lines when sharing patient-reported outcome measures to enhance shared decision-making.

INTRODUCTION

Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) are increasingly recognized as valuable adjuncts to inform treatment planning and clinical care in many clinical disciplines. PROMs capture patient-centered psychosocial and functional insights, with plastic surgery benefiting from PROMs such as those by the Q-Portfolio team (ie, BREAST-Q, FACE-Q, and CLEFT-Q).1

Despite much enthusiasm in using PROMs,2–5 their impact has been limited by an uncertainty in how to best view and interpret the data. The Patient-reported Outcomes Tools: Engaging Users and Stakeholders (PROTEUS) Consortium provides best-practice guidelines for PROM visualization, advising that formats must be “understood by providers and patients,” and tailored to the specific clinical context it is being applied to.6 However, these guidelines remain unapplied in plastic surgery.6–10 The classic adage that a picture is worth a thousand words is particularly relevant; graphical displays may make complex data more engaging and intuitive for patients and families.11 Moreover, one must also take into account patient age, cultural background, and level of health literacy.6,12

Therefore, the purpose of this project is to apply the PROTEUS guidelines to the CLEFT-Q and FACE-Q scales recommended in the International Consortium for Health Outcomes Measurement Standard Set of outcome measures for the comprehensive appraisal of multidisciplinary cleft care.2,3,13–16 This project assesses how patients and family members interpret various tabular and graphical representations of the data, specifically to identify best approaches that communicate accurate results, avoid misunderstanding, and meet their preferences. Our long-term vision is a data “report card” or “dashboard” that would be reviewed with patients/families during annual cleft team visits to assess how the patient is doing and to identify opportunities for intervention.17–20

METHODS

CLEFT-Q and FACE-Q Scales

For this qualitative project, 11 subscales were included from CLEFT-Q: face, nose, nostrils, cleft scar, teeth, jaws, psychological, school, social, speech distress, and speech function. One subscale was included from FACE-Q: lips. Each subscale normalizes its raw score to a range of 0–100. Synthetic data were prepared to represent the full range of each scale, along with typical patient responses for different cleft phenotypes, as modeled from the real-world data collected by our cleft teams.

PROTEUS Guidelines

The PROTEUS framework was developed by an international collaboration of over 50 organizations to provide guidance for designing, implementing, and managing PROMs within clinical care.6,21 Its guidelines were studied and applied to the development of the visualizations at each stage of the study.

Data Visualizations

The same data were presented using different methods: numerical table (Fig. 1A); paired bar chart (Fig. 1B); box-and-whisker and violin plots (not shown, as they were ultimately not shown to patients); radar and polar charts (Fig. 1C); and horizontal number lines with “traffic light” (red/yellow/green) coloration (Fig. 1D), grayscale, and pastel color schemes (Figs. 2A, B). For the “traffic light” and pastel color-scheme figures, terciles were used with thresholds at 50 and 75, representing relatively low, middle, and high score zones.22 This uniform scaling was chosen to maximize visual simplicity for a lay audience, avoiding the cognitive burden of dynamic lengths based on SDs from the mean. For representation of multiple scales at once, the paired bar chart (Fig. 1B), stacked number line, ridgeplot (Fig. 2C), and radar and polar charts (Fig. 1C) were considered.

Various options explored for the “report card” visualizations in the first and second round of the modified Delphi method depicting synthetic CLEFT-Q data, including (A) table, (B) paired bar chart, (C) polar chart, (D) horizontal number bar in traffic light–themed colors.

All visualizations were prepared using Python (version 3.12.2), Matplotlib (version 3.9.2), and Seaborn (version 0.13.2).23 For presentation to the patient/family, each visualization was printed in color at 600dpi on a laser printer.

Delphi Process

Patients and parents were recruited from 2 multidisciplinary cleft team clinics. All participants provided written informed consent (IRB protocol Pro00104806). Only patients aged 8 years and older were included, which aligns with the age of survey administration recommended by the Q Portfolio development team.15 Following American Medical Association and the National Institutes of Health recommendations for sixth-grade reading-level patient-education materials,24–26 we recruited parents from diverse educational backgrounds. Non-English speakers were excluded.

An initial parent focus group informed the preparation of graphical prototypes. The graphical prototypes were then evaluated via a modified Delphi process27,28 that required 4 rounds, spanning from February to May 2025. At least 10 subjects at each of the 2 institutions (total 20 subjects) participated in each round of the Delphi process.

In each round, participants viewed relevant visualizations of CLEFT-Q subscales. Each domain was briefly described using uniform, representative questions to ensure consistency and prevent administration bias. Participants were asked to perform several tasks while looking at the image, such as (1) “can you identify the individual patient’s score?”; (2) “can you identify the average score for all patients in that group?”; (3) “is the individual patient’s score higher or lower than the average score?” Correct answers indicated accurate interpretation of the data visualization being assessed. Additionally, participants rated “how easy was it to understand this figure?” (ease of understanding) and “how useful would you find this figure if it were provided to you during your cleft team clinic visits?” (usefulness) on 1–5 Likert scales. Finally, open-ended qualitative comments were solicited. This process was repeated for each visualization, and the semiquantitative and qualitative results were used to inform iterative revisions to the designs for subsequent rounds of the Delphi process.

Descriptive statistics were summarized as means for Likert scales and frequencies for categorical interpretation tasks. Differences in interpretation accuracy across the visualizations were assessed using Fisher exact and χ2 test (where appropriate). Difference in subjective Likert ratings for ease of understanding and usefulness were evaluated using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Statistical analyses were performed in Python (version 3.14.3) with statistical significance set at an α value of less than 0.05.23

Graphical Representation Was Preferred Over Numerical Tables

It was evident very early in round 1 that the numerical table (Fig. 1A) performed poorly (60% accuracy) and received the lowest usefulness rating (2.6); participants found column headers confusing and the display “cluttered.” In round 2, the paired bar chart was significantly easier to interpret (95% accuracy, P = 0.02). Subsequent rounds of the Delphi process focused exclusively on graphical representation.

Simple Visualizations Were Preferred

In round 2, the paired bar chart was the most well-received visualization (Fig. 1B). Qualitative feedback included “visually prefer, less clutter and easier to see comparisons,” “data are clear,” and “I like this the best out of all the options because you can see it at a glance.” Thus, it was carried to round 3.

In contrast, more complex visualizations performed poorly. The polar/radar chart (Fig. 1C), tested in round 2, was correctly interpreted by only 60% of participants. Participant comments included “the circular format is less familiar” and “complex—I’ve never seen anything like this before.” The ridgeplot (Fig. 2C), tested in round 3, was the least understood with only 41% of participants correctly identifying the average. Participants expressed difficulty interpreting the shapes, suggesting lack of understanding of the superimposed kernel density plot. For both of these complex visualizations, misinterpretation was common even among parents who obtained undergraduate or advanced degrees (Table 1).

Color Motifs Can Carry Subjective Bias

The “traffic light” (red/yellow/green) colored horizontal bar chart (Fig. 1D) was chosen as a way to delineate terciles for relatively low, medium, and high scores. Participants equated red as “bad,” yellow as “fair,” and green as “good.” Applying this color scheme to a child’s appearance or psychosocial well-being inherently assigns a qualitative “grade.” The color red, in particular, was associated with “failure” or “poor” performance rather than simply indicating a lower score. Many parents and patients described the visualization as overly “intense” and emotionally charged: “the colors are helpful, but most people would assume green is better—and [we] would want the kids to decide for themselves if they want [to see],” “there may be a gut feeling that red is poor, which is disheartening,” and “the traffic light could be upsetting.”

Consequently, the “traffic light” colorization was discontinued. Subsequent rounds evaluated an arbitrary pastel color motif (cyan/purple/pink terciles) and simple monochromatic (no color) number line. Although the pastel number line (Fig. 2A) was well-understood (96% correctly identified patient score), confusion remained regarding color meaning (which color or shading was “better”). Sample quotes from qualitative feedback included “no caption for colors to explain their meaning can be overwhelming and the colors are not visually aesthetic” and “the colors don’t help us know if things are going well or poorly.” This was reflected in reduced subjective scores for ease of understanding and usefulness (mean, 3.9 and 3.7, respectively) compared with the monochromatic grayscale version (mean, 4.3 and 4.1, respectively).

Statistical Details Were Overwhelming

Some visualizations (like the ridgeplot mentioned earlier) were tested to assess whether patients and families could interpret and would appreciate being given statistical details like means, medians, interquartile ranges, and distributions. Feedback was almost unanimously negative: these graphics were confusing to most parents and unintelligible to adolescent patients. In side-by-side comparisons, participants overwhelmingly preferred simpler formats, such as standard bar charts or the horizontal number line. Similarly, when viewing multiple scales, a stack of horizontal number lines was preferred to “all-in-one” condensed visualizations, such as radar/polar charts.

Grayscale Horizontal Number Line With Infographics Was Favored

Round 3 results (Table 2) showed that the grayscale horizontal number line (Fig. 2B) slightly outperformed the paired bar chart (Fig. 1B) in interpretability (mean, 4.3 versus 4.1, respectively). Positive quotes from subjects included “the formatting is clear,” “I like that the numerical scale is easy to read,” and “gray looks a little better because it’s more straightforward.”

In round 4, we wanted to test a simplified design that added back very limited statistical cues as infographic markers—specifically, a dotted line for the median score (“patients like you”), and a bold line for the individual’s score (“your score”) (Fig. 3). Overall, the final design was easy for subjects to understand from an objective and subjective standpoint. However, usefulness ratings of the grayscale horizontal number line decreased slightly (mean, 3.8) from the previous round (mean, 4.1), with comments ranging from the equivocal “depends on the person if it would be helpful” to the more favorable “I love seeing this sort of thing even with my own medical data [on it].”

DISCUSSION

In this study, several approaches to data visualization were evaluated, including simple numerical tables, statistical displays (box-and-whisker and violin plots), infographics, methods for presenting multiple scales (radar charts, polar charts, ridgeplots, and stacked number lines), and the influence of color schemes on interpretation. There are currently no standards as to how CLEFT-Q outcomes should be effectively communicated to patients. Although PROTEUS guidelines provided a valuable framework, they focus on single-domain presentation.6 This study extends these guidelines to address the simultaneous presentation of multiple outcome domains required for comprehensive cleft care.

Best in Show: The Horizontal Number Line Infographic

The monochromatic (grayscale) horizontal number line emerged victorious as the most effective and intuitive format for communicating individual-level CLEFT-Q data. Participants consistently appreciated its simplicity, clarity, and usability. The infographical markup provided additional value.

In contrast, numerical tables were perceived as overwhelming. Many participants struggled with “too many numbers” and with statistical terminology. This finding was surprising to us, as we expected tables to be a reasonable method of data presentation. Concordantly, PROTEUS discusses how summary statistics (such as percentiles and SD) can be helpful in contextualizing PROM scores.6 However, when presenting a table summarizing many outcome measures, this seems to be too much information for the average participant.

Similarly, more advanced visualizations like box-and-whisker plots, violin plots, ridgeplots, and radar/polar charts proved too difficult for most families to understand. Although such visualizations are common in medical and epidemiologic literature, there is limited evidence regarding their appropriateness for patient-facing communication.29–32 Our findings confirm that they are poorly suited for patient communication where clarity is paramount, even though medical professionals may prefer the data density of statistical graphics like polar charts or ridgeplots. Even highly educated parents (eg, an engineer or PhD holder) who did interpret the plots correctly noted, “I really like being able to see the distribution, but this may be hard for most people to follow.”

Overall, participants favored simple, linear comparisons with clear directional guidance—features embodied by the number line infographic format. The horizontal number lines allow users to easily see whether a score is above or below average, and the exact numerical value, even when patient and average scores are similar. The addition of infographic markers, such as a dotted line representing the median (“patients like you”) and a bold line for the individual score (“your score”), enhanced interpretability without adding cognitive burden. These findings reinforce PROTEUS recommendations that visualizations should be self-explanatory and accessible without extensive explanation.6,9

Color

Very surprisingly, color emerged as a critical consideration. Colors can help convey meaning, but they may also introduce unintended emotional interpretation and bias. The “traffic light” scheme (red/yellow/green) elicited particularly strong reactions, with participants associating red with failure. Parents expressed concern that such colors could be upsetting or stigmatizing to their children, who may already be dealing with psychosocial challenges.22,33,34 Alternative color palettes (cyan/pink/purple) did not elicit as strong emotional responses but did cause confusion about the relevance of the color shading.

We introduced color to delineate statistical ranges (like terciles or quartiles), but CLEFT-Q and FACE-Q scales lack qualitative translation of their 0–100 scores. That is, although researchers have reported score distributions for CLEFT-Q and FACE-Q,22 no threshold has been established for each scale under which the outcome is considered “bad” or above which the outcome is “good.” Rather, CLEFT-Q and FACE-Q scales are more akin to a yardstick, which measures one’s height but does not judge whether someone is “short” or “tall.”

Since thresholds for CLEFT-Q and FACE-Q are not yet established, data visualizations should remain monochromatic. The corollary of this is that if and when thresholds are established for each scale, color may be considered if one does want to connote “bad” and “good.” This is done by many quality improvement programs, such as the American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program.

Ethical Considerations

The PROM data in pediatric contexts raise important relational considerations. Most patients are minors, and parents play a central role in their care.35 Although some parents feel that their children should be involved in medical discussions, including viewing the outcomes reports, others feared potential harmful effects if their children were to interpret the outcomes as “bad.” One parent commented, “[Patients] shouldn’t see it themselves because I don’t want my child to think they’re less than when they’re already struggling… but as a parent I would want to know so I can get resources to help my child.” From this perspective, PROM visualizations are not just technical tools but also deeply personal.36 This raises important ethical and professional considerations for implementation of outcome “report cards” in cleft team clinic and serves as a reminder of the critical role of the clinician–patient relationship; clinicians must have a good relationship with the patient and family, and should determine suitability of sharing the data visualizations on a case-by-case basis.

To thoughtfully integrate these “report cards” into the clinical workflow, we envision generating the visualizations before the annual multidisciplinary clinic visit. A designated team member, such as the attending surgeon, would review the graphic with the patient and family. Although returning the data is not yet a standard practice in plastic surgery, this structured review provides a tangible opportunity to celebrate positive outcomes and pinpoint areas that might warrant targeted psychological support or surgical intervention.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, visualizations used synthetic data to test extreme and median scenarios; future studies must validate these formats using real-world patient data. Second, although over 50 participants were recruited from 2 institutions, the sample was modest and skewed toward parents. Future research should prioritize adolescent participation to better understand the effect of age on data literacy. All interviews were conducted in English, limiting generalizability.

Interestingly, some participants expressed skepticism about the usefulness of PROMs altogether. Suggestive comments included “we would prefer to see pictures of other children with clefts, so our child knows they’re not alone rather than knowing they feel better about themselves compared with other children” and another parent commenting, “each patient’s anatomy is different and it feels like the metrics are too general.” Children also did not find the data useful, with 1 stating “I don’t really care how the other kids are doing.” This suggests that although PROMs offer valuable insights, their real-world use may depend on thoughtful integration into clinical conversations and emotional support frameworks. This feedback will guide future iterations, focusing on how these visual tools can enhance patient–clinician interactions and drive personalized interventions.

Future Directions

Future studies should also assess how visualized CLEFT-Q PROMs augment physician–patient relationships. Tools such as the Consumer Assessment of Healthcare Providers and Systems survey may help evaluate whether such visualizations give a sense of transparency, strengthen trust, and enhance shared decision-making.37 As PROMs become increasingly embedded in clinical workflows, it remains vital to ensure that these tools align with patient values and experiences rather than solely reflecting researcher priorities.38 This is especially true in cleft patients, given the prevalence of psychosocial concerns.22,33,34

CONCLUSIONS

Using PROTEUS guidance and a modified Delphi process, we developed a patient-facing CLEFT-Q “report card” optimized for clarity and neutrality. Our findings demonstrate that although clinicians may prefer dense statistical graphics, patients and families require simple, intuitive formats. Furthermore, introducing color to delineate score ranges can cause unintended emotional distress by assigning arbitrary “grades” to a patient’s well-being. The final monochrome horizontal number line we developed effectively communicated individual scores relative to group average and minimized misinterpretations while curbing emotional bias. Integrating this visual tool into the clinical workflow may foster greater transparency and support the ultimate goal of shared decision-making in cleft care, but the lessons from this study extend beyond cleft care to other PROM instruments prominently used in plastic surgery, especially the other scales within the Q-Portfolio (eg, FACE-Q, BREAST-Q, BODY-Q, etc.).1
