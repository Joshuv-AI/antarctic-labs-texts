Implementation of a single session psychotherapy intervention in primary care integrated behavioral health: a single-arm pilot study
Authors: Anne I. Roche, Brian L. Straub, Emily B. K. Thomas, Hana-May Eadeh, Kyle A. Schofield, Alanna M. Chamberlain, Robert M. Jacobson, Alisson N. Lass, Jennifer Bailey, Kristin Vickers, Rhonda M. Merwin, Craig N. Sawchuk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSingle-session interventions offer a novel approach to improving access to evidence-based mental health services using a population-based framework for delivery. The current single-arm pilot study aimed to assess the feasibility, acceptability, and preliminary effectiveness of a single-session intervention in Mayo Clinic's primary care Integrated Behavioral Health program..MethodsParticipants were recruited from Mayo Clinic's Integrated Behavioral Health program and participated in a therapist-delivered single-session therapy intervention in primary care. The intervention could be delivered by video or in-person and was based on acceptance and commitment therapy and solution-focused brief therapy principles. Descriptive statistics and 2-tailed paired sampled t-tests were used to examine outcomes.ResultsLess than 20% of the patients contacted expressed interest in the study; however, the majority who expressed interest and screened eligible completed the single-session therapy visit (N = 34). Acceptability was high, as indicated by study participants expressing generally high levels of satisfaction with the single visit. Participants experienced significant increases from pre- to post-intervention in readiness for change and in self-reported capacity to generate pathways toward goals. From pre-intervention to two-week follow-up, participants experienced significant reductions in depression and anxiety symptoms, significant increases in psychological flexibility and values progress, and significant decreases in values obstruction.ConclusionsSingle-session interventions may be one helpful approach among a menu of stepped care options in primary care. Future work will benefit from ongoing exploration of the most appropriate processes for implementation of single-session interventions in primary care.Trial registrationThe study is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06347822); Registration Date: 03/29/2024.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12875-026-03361-y.

Background

Approximately one in five U.S. adults struggle with mental illness [1]. Evidence-based treatments exist, but only about half of individuals with a mental health disorder receive services [1]. Importantly, difficulties accessing mental health treatment are often compounded for marginalized groups [2–5]. Barriers to care within traditional approaches to psychotherapy service delivery hinder broad, accessible, and scalable dissemination [6]. The integration of behavioral health services into primary care is one population-based approach that can reduce access barriers. Primary care integrated behavioral health (IBH) prioritizes meeting patients at their time of need and delivering brief, time-limited, evidence-based psychotherapeutic interventions with stepped care options [7].

Many individuals who engage in therapy participate in only one visit [8], though often not by design. In IBH programs, a single session is similarly common [9, 10] and often sufficient, as many patients are seeking brief, problem-focused interactions. The implementation of intentionally scheduled and designed single-session interventions (SSIs) in IBH programs may offer an important avenue for serving more patients, reducing wait times, and improving access.

SSIs are conceptualized as intentional one-time interactions with a clinician or program [11]. SSIs can be transdiagnostic, delivered through multiple modalities (e.g., in-person, virtually), and approached from multiple theoretical orientations (e.g., acceptance and commitment therapy – ACT, solution-focused brief therapy - SFBT). SSIs can be delivered as an independent service or to supplement ongoing programs [11]. For example, SSIs have been offered to patients on clinic waiting lists in an effort to reduce wait-times [12, 13].

A growing literature supports the utility of SSIs for mental and behavioral health targets, including anxiety, depression, and substance use [14], coping with medical problems [15], health behavior change [16], and reducing psychological distress and hopelessness [12, 13]. Given the range of patient presentations seen in primary care and the prioritization of brief treatment, SSIs offer an important opportunity for increasing timely behavioral health access in this context. A recent study found that an SSI based in focused acceptance and commitment therapy (FACT [17]; an adaptation of ACT specifically for primary care) was effective in reducing depressive symptoms at 1-week follow-up compared to a control group [18]. Another study examining the feasibility and impact of single-session consultations in primary care implemented based on Advanced Access [19] appointment scheduling principles found that implementing SSIs was promising for improving timely access and reducing missed appointments/late cancellations [20]. Despite a limited literature, these studies lend support to the potential for SSIs in primary care.

The aim of the current study was to explore the feasibility, acceptability, and preliminary effectiveness of implementing SSIs within Mayo Clinic's IBH practice in or near Rochester, MN, USA. The authors first designed a transdiagnostic SSI based on ACT and SFBT principles for primary care patients and then conducted a single arm pilot study within IBH.

Methods

Intervention protocol development

The research team developed the intervention protocol with feedback from IBH therapy providers, integrating FACT [17] and SFBT principles. SFBT prioritizes the patient identifying their goal(s), considering how life would be different if the goal(s) were reached, and facilitating the patient’s identification and development of solutions to current problems [21]. FACT aims to increase psychological flexibility, or the capacity to be present and engage in values-based behavior, despite difficult internal experiences (e.g., thoughts, emotions) [22]. Both ACT and SFBT focus on patient values and goals rather than a therapist-driven agenda. Thus, the intervention included collaboratively exploring values and values-based goals, practicing psychological flexibility skills [23], and the co-development of an Action Plan for patients to take with them, consistent with previous work [12, 13]. See Table 1 for an outline and description of the intervention protocol and the suggested time to be spent on each section of the protocol. The study was approved by the Mayo Clinic's IRB, ID: 23-011276 and is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT number: NCT06347822).

Participants

Participants were adult English-speaking primary care patients who were referred for a psychotherapy consultation visit in Mayo Clinic's IBH program. The primary care patient population recruited for the current study included patients from five clinics located in or near Rochester, MN, USA. In Mayo Clinic's IBH practice, patients are generally referred for psychotherapy consultation by their primary care provider (PCP), by an IBH psychiatrist or nurse care coordinator, or after a social work triage encounter. Authors aimed to recruit 34 participants to complete the single-session study visit, consistent with a priori power analyses. Potential participants were individuals waiting for an initial psychotherapy consultation. Their medical charts were reviewed by the PI to assess referral indication and diagnoses listed to determine initial study eligibility. Individuals diagnosed with psychotic spectrum, bipolar I and II, or severe substance use disorder, and individuals with intellectual impairment, severe cognitive impairment, or potentially lacking capacity to consent were ineligible and thus not contacted about the study. If study staff questioned whether being contacted about the study would have potential to interfere in the patient’s clinical care trajectory, consultation with the patient’s care team occurred prior to recruitment.

Procedure

After the medical chart was reviewed, patients were sent a message via the electronic health record patient portal to invite participation. Individuals were contacted by email or phone if they did not have an active portal. If patients did not respond to the electronic invitation within three to five days, study staff reached out by phone. Individuals who did not respond or chose not to participate remained scheduled for a regular IBH therapy consultation.

If patients expressed interest in the study, study staff sent the informed consent document electronically or by mail. After consent, patients received an email with a link to complete the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) [24] and Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) [25]. Study data were collected and managed using REDCap, with study staff completing patient-reported verbal responses if needed (which occurred rarely) [26, 27]. In order to be eligible, patients needed to score ≥ 5 but not > 14 on the PHQ-9 and/or GAD-7 (indicating mild to moderate depression and/or anxiety) and respond < 2 to Item 9 of the PHQ-9 (“thoughts that you would be better off dead, or thoughts of hurting yourself in some way”). Eligible patients were then contacted by study staff to schedule the SSI. Patients selected an in-person or video SSI, consistent with the patient-centered approach in Mayo Clinic's practice. Patients were made aware that the study visit was an intentional single-session intervention but were also informed that they would keep their originally scheduled IBH psychotherapy consultation visit to allow for additional follow-up after the study visit if they desired, which was important given the pilot nature of the research study and that the SSI would be a change to the patient’s original care plan. To prevent overlap in the SSI and IBH clinical care, if a patient’s initial visit was scheduled within the study timeframe (~ 3 weeks), study staff rescheduled the patient’s original IBH psychotherapy consultation to avoid interfering with study procedures. Of note, participants were not necessarily scheduled for the original psychotherapy consultation with the same interventionist they saw for the SSI study visit (though this did happen in some cases).

Patients completed measures immediately before the SSI visit, immediately after, and at 2-week follow-up. Individuals completed questions about demographics (see Supplementary Material), social drivers of health [28], and other current mental health services (see Supplementary Material) immediately before the SSI. Patients completed measures of readiness for change [13] and belief in their capacity to reach goals [29] immediately before and after the SSI. Patients also completed measures of depression [24], anxiety [25], psychological flexibility [30, 31], and values engagement [32] immediately before the visit and at 2-week follow-up. Participants were asked to provide satisfaction ratings using the Client Satisfaction Questionnaire-8 (CSQ-8) immediately post-intervention [33]. Study measures are summarized in Table 3.

Satisfaction ratings on the CSQ-8 were supplemented with patient satisfaction questions specific to the study, including Did the visit help you develop an action plan?; How hopeful are you that the action plan will be useful?; and How motivated do you feel to use your action plan?, with scales for these questions ranging from 0 = not at all, to 4 = very much; scores were then summed to a total score (0–12). They were also invited to provide open-ended feedback. These satisfaction items specific to the study were adapted from Schleider and colleagues, 2021 [12]. Participants also completed satisfaction-focused questions specific to the study at 2-week follow-up (see Supplementary Material). At 2-week follow-up, patients were asked if they desired to keep or cancel the originally scheduled IBH therapy visit. If participants were no longer interested in further IBH psychotherapy, the original IBH visit was cancelled. Participants were also asked if they had been using their Action Plan (0 = not at all, to 4 = very much) and were invited to share additional feedback. Finally, participants were asked two questions about how helpful they felt it would be to offer SSIs to all patients currently waiting for an initial IBH psychotherapy consultation and how helpful they felt it would be to offer SSIs as a standalone service in IBH. Participants responded to these items on a 0–4 scale (0 = not at all, to 4 = very much).

Individuals were not charged for the SSI study visit, and the visit was not billed through insurance given that the visit was part of the research study. Participants were remunerated for completing study screening and assessments ($5 for screening, $5 for questionnaires at the SSI, and $10 for 2-week follow-up). Participants could receive up to $20.

Statistical analyses

Feasibility was assessed by examining the proportion of patients contacted who expressed interest and consented to enroll in the study (> 50% indicated feasibility) and the proportion of eligible individuals who ultimately completed the SSI (> 75% indicating feasibility).

Treatment fidelity was assessed by evaluating the appropriate completion of the Action Plan developed by patient and clinician in session [13]. Authors identified six core components of the intervention and Action Plan to assess fidelity. Core components included: identification of top reason for seeking services, ≥ 1 value/valued life domain identified, identification of what patient will be doing differently if session is helpful, introduction of skill, identification of 24-hour goal, and identification of 1-week goal. Fidelity was coded by a team member who is a board-certified licensed psychologist (J.B.) but who was not involved in the delivery of the intervention. Questionable responses were reviewed by another board-certified licensed psychologist (C.N.S.; not a study interventionist) to come to consensus.

Acceptability was assessed using scores on the CSQ-8 and additional feedback specific to the intervention; patients’ choice about whether to keep or cancel the originally scheduled IBH consultation was another marker of acceptability.

Change in process and outcome measures were analyzed using 2-tailed paired samples t-tests to examine preliminary effectiveness. Although effectiveness analyses were exploratory in nature, preliminary power analyses conducted in G*Power 3.1.9.6 (estimated power = 0.80) indicated a sample size of 15 participants would be needed to detect a large effect and 34 participants to detect a medium effect using paired samples t-tests.

Results

Data were cleaned, prepared for analysis, and analyzed in IBM SPSS Statistics (version 29). Outliers were examined by converting scores to standardized Z-scores. Any individual score that was above 3.0 or below − 3.0 was considered an outlier. No individual scores were determined to be outliers. Missing data were limited and were thus handled using person-mean imputation based on the completed items on the given scale or subscale. Item-level data were imputed as follows: PHQ-9: 1 item at pre-intervention, 2 items at 2-week follow-up; GAD-7: 3 items at 2-week follow-up; CompACT: 1 valued action item at pre-intervention, 1 openness-to-experience item at 2-week follow-up; Valuing Questionnaire: 1 values-progress item at pre-intervention; CSQ: 2 items. Missing items on the social drivers of health measure were not imputed.

Feasibility

As shown Fig. 1, out of 490 contacts made during recruitment, 84 individuals consented to participate in the study (17%). Seventy out of 84 individuals (83%) who consented completed screening, and 40/70 individuals who completed screening were eligible (57%). Out of the 40 individuals who screened eligible, 35 were scheduled and attended the SSI (87.5%). One participant was removed from further analyses due to an error in questionnaire administration. Twenty-nine out of the 34 study participants were able to have their SSI study visit prior to the date that their original IBH psychotherapy consultation visit was scheduled. Twenty-four out of the 34 study participants subsequently had their originally scheduled psychotherapy consultation visit rescheduled to a later date so as not to interfere with study processes, which they consented to and understood. On average, study visits took about 60 min (range: 45–81 min) to deliver, consistent with IBH practice. Of the 34 participants included in analyses, 27 completed at least part of the 2-week follow-up survey.

Treatment fidelity

Overall, fidelity was coded at 100% across all six critical components of completed Action Plans.

Acceptability

All 34 participants completed the CSQ-8 post-intervention, and the mean score was 28.65 (SD = 2.96, range 23–32), indicating high satisfaction on average. On a 0–4 scale, participants on average reported that the visit helped them to develop an Action Plan (M = 3.71; SD = 0.52), that they were hopeful plan would be useful (M = 3.29; SD = 0.72), and that they felt motivated to use the plan (M = 3.31; SD = 0.69). Combining the Action Plan items (0–12 scale), participants on average indicated satisfaction with the utility of the Action Plan overall (M = 10.28; SD = 1.59). At 2-week follow-up, individuals reported that they had used their Action Plan “somewhat” (M = 2.0; SD = 1.06).

Of the 26 patients who completed the entirety of the 2-week follow-up, the majority (61.5%, n = 16) indicated via survey response that they wished to participate in additional IBH therapy, and 10 (38.5%) indicated a desire to cancel the originally scheduled IBH visit. Chart review provided additional insight into the number of participants who ultimately attended their originally scheduled visit vs. not. Based on chart review of the full sample (N = 34), 19 (55.9%) of the individuals who completed the SSI ultimately attended their originally scheduled IBH visit and 15 (44.1%) individuals did not.

At 2-week follow-up, 65.4% of participants who answered thought that it would be “mostly” or “very much” helpful to offer an SSI for individuals currently waiting for an IBH consultation, whereas others thought it would be “somewhat” (19.2%), “a little” (11.5%), or “not at all” (3.8%) helpful. Similarly, 61.5% of participants felt that it would be “mostly” or “very much” helpful to offer SSI visits as a standalone service in IBH, whereas others felt it would be “somewhat” (19.2%), “a little” (15.4%), or “not at all” (3.8%) helpful.

Post-intervention comments shared by participants via open-ended feedback were generally positive, with some participants highlighting specific aspects of the intervention (e.g., Action Plan). Fewer participants provided open-ended feedback at 2-week follow-up, but the feedback was varied, with many noting the usefulness of the intervention and some highlighting the need for further accountability and follow-up. Preliminary effectiveness

Paired samples t-tests indicated significant increases in readiness for change and belief in capacity to identify pathways toward goals from immediately pre- to post-intervention, with medium effect sizes for both outcomes.

Results indicated significant decreases in depression and anxiety from pre-intervention to 2-week follow-up, with large effect sizes for both outcomes. When broken down by component processes, results indicated significant increases in valued action (medium effect) and openness to experience (small effect), but non-significant differences in behavioral awareness (though changes were in the expected direction). Results indicated significant reductions in values obstruction and significant increases in values progress from pre-intervention to 2-week follow-up, with medium effect sizes for both. Discussion

The current single-arm pilot study offers an important evaluation of the feasibility, acceptability, and preliminary effectiveness of an SSI offered in primary care.

Feasibility findings were variable. On the one hand, only about one fifth of individuals who were contacted about participation in the SSI research study consented to participate. On the other hand, a majority of consenting individuals who screened eligible completed the SSI, indicating that individuals who identified the SSI as a good fit were highly motivated. Additionally, approximately 43% of individuals who completed study screening were ineligible for the study, indicating more interest than the study allowed for with screening criteria requirements. These results may be reflective of study design. Participants were generally initially contacted via patient portal message. This process is impersonal, and participating in the study visit was a change to the plan the patient had likely discussed with their PCP and in some cases would have required patients to change the date of their originally scheduled IBH consultation visit to accommodate study procedures, all of which may have discouraged study participation. Further, some patients may have been experiencing more severe symptoms and felt that an SSI was not aligned with their needs. Future work will benefit from a mixed-methods implementation science approach, to more deeply understand patient, PCP, and IBH provider perspectives on referral and delivery of SSIs in IBH. For example, PCPs, who know their patients well, may be optimally situated to identify individuals who may be interested in and benefit from an SSI. For patients, learning about the service from a trusted provider may facilitate interest in the service. Standard implementation of the SSI model and referral processes would likely require ongoing education to primary care teams about the nature of SSIs and appropriate patient referrals (e.g., during team huddles, via primary care newsletters). This would allow a patient’s PCP, a known and trusted provider, to discuss an SSI referral and its potential benefits (e.g., timely access, development of an action plan) at an individual level with patients. In addition, establishing SSI referral processes within the electronic health record would help to facilitate ease of timely referrals and SSI scheduling.

Patients indicated generally high levels of satisfaction with the visit and reported it was helpful in developing an Action Plan. Open-ended feedback was generally positive, with multiple individuals commenting specifically about the value of the Action Plan. The SSI’s focus on immediately identifying a specific focus area, introducing evidence-based skills, and setting behavioral goals is unique from a typical initial psychotherapy session (focused on information gathering and diagnostic assessment) and allows patients to leave with an Action Plan. On the other hand, at 2-week follow-up, some participants did not endorse using the Action Plan often. Future iterations of this work may benefit from exploring barriers and facilitators to implementation of the Action Plan. For example, in Schleider et al.’s Single Session Consultation (SSC) protocol, participants are asked to identify people or resources that can help to implement the plan and to identify potential obstacles and solutions to implementing the plan [12, 13].

Patients’ choices surrounding whether to follow up for additional IBH psychotherapy services provided a valuable glimpse into the impact of the implementation of SSIs into clinical practice. Of the patients who completed the 2-week follow-up survey, 38% wished to cancel the originally scheduled IBH visit. Chart review indicated that in total, 56% of patients who participated in the single-session study visit did return to attend the originally scheduled IBH visit, whereas the rest did not. This reduction is a strong indicator of the SSI’s utility in reducing provider workloads and patient wait-times, which could have a significant impact at a population level if scaled more broadly. Unfortunately, we are unable to understand exactly why individuals made the choices they did in terms of follow-up, though qualitative feedback provides some insight. Perhaps those who chose to attend additional visits had a positive experience with the single session visit which left them motivated for continued engagement with therapy services. On the other hand, it is possible that these individuals felt like a single session was not nearly enough to help them reach their goals. Alternatively, participants may have chosen to attend the originally scheduled visit because they wished to follow through on the clinical care plan they had originally discussed with their PCP. For individuals who chose not to return for additional visits, it is possible that this was because they felt they got all they needed from the single session visit or because they did not feel they had the time and resources to commit to additional visits. Alternatively, it is possible that they had a more negative experience with the visit and did not wish to return for additional care; given the high levels of patient satisfaction reported, however, this is unlikely to be a common explanation. Future work will benefit from in-depth qualitative exploration of patients’ experiences of the SSI and its impact over time, as well as ongoing follow-up to understand the effect of an SSI on healthcare utilization. Prior work has demonstrated a positive impact of offering SSIs in primary care on practice-level outcomes such as access, number of patients served, and reduction in no-show/late-cancel rates [20]. Future work should continue to examine these system-level impacts.

Preliminary exploration of effectiveness data was promising. Patients completed the assessments with minimal missing data, and interventionists delivered the SSI with fidelity. Patients experienced significant pre-post changes in readiness for change (medium effect) and belief in capacity to generate pathways toward goals (medium effect), a construct that contributes to a person’s current level of hope. Readiness for change and hope have been important hypothesized mechanisms of change in SSIs [12, 13], thus, results align with the proposed targets and mechanisms of SSIs.

Participants also experienced significant reductions in depression and anxiety symptoms from pre-intervention to 2-week follow-up, both yielding large effect sizes. Mean anxiety levels fell into the minimal range at the 2-week follow-up. Though this is promising from a population health perspective, mean scores on both the PHQ-9 and the GAD-7 were in the mild range at the pre-intervention timepoint, thereby limiting generalizability to individuals with more severe self-reported symptoms.

Participants experienced significant changes from pre-intervention to 2-week follow-up on ACT process-based measures. Participants reported changes in psychological flexibility (small effect) using the total score of the CompACT-15 [31], with the valued action process seemingly the primary driver of changes in psychological flexibility, as patients experienced statistically significant increases in valued action (medium effect) and openness to experience (small effect) from pre-intervention to 2-week follow-up and non-significant changes in behavioral awareness. On a measure specific to values enactment, participants reported significant increases in progress in valued living and significant decreases in obstruction to valued living (medium effects). The significant changes in process-level outcomes align with the high level of interventional fidelity and with the content of the SSI, which focused heavily on identifying values/valued life domains and specific values-based behavioral goals. Even still, psychological flexibility skills introduced during the intervention are also meant to promote behavioral awareness and openness to experience [23]. It may be that these processes are less easily learned and maintained in the context of a single visit or are more difficult to conceptualize and target by interventionists. Future work will benefit from continued investigation of whether targeted psychotherapy processes are changing as theorized and whether changes in these processes are due to intervention effects. Future work should prioritize the identification of which specific evidence-based psychological processes may be most impactful to target in the context of an SSI. Though SSIs do not adhere to one specific theoretical orientation, previous work has indicated that introducing targeted evidence-based coping skills within the SSI is important from an effectiveness standpoint [14]. The current results indicate that transdiagnostic and process-based models, like ACT, may be a good fit for SSIs within primary care given the flexibility of delivery across presenting concerns, although additional work is needed regarding the engagement of specific ACT processes and the ability to enact patient engagement of Action Plans over time.

Limitations

Several limitations should be noted. The study was a single-arm pilot study with no control or comparison group. As such, changes in symptom and process measures, especially at 2-week follow-up, should be interpreted with caution, as it is possible that these changes were due at least in part to the passage of time or regression to the mean. The current sample consisted of primarily White (reflective of Mayo Clinic's IBH patient population) [34], metropolitan-dwelling individuals, with few reported challenges in terms of social drivers of health. Relatedly, the sample consisted of individuals who were established primary care patients, with nearly 80% reporting that they were currently taking psychiatric medications at baseline, indicating that many already had access to some level of mental health support, though a minimal number of participants reported being engaged with other psychotherapy services at baseline. Sample characteristics limit broader generalizability of findings. Study eligibility criteria restricted individuals who could participate, limiting the ability to understand whether the intervention may be a fit for patients with higher symptom severity. The follow-up time frame for the current study was two weeks. Thus, long-term impact of the SSI is unknown. Finally, though the study was conducted within primary care IBH, the structured study procedures are not indicative of the flow of clinical practice, making it difficult to fully interpret results in the context of feasibility of implementation. Specifically, participants were not charged for study visits and the sessions were not billed to insurance, limiting understanding of whether and how participants would choose to engage in the SSI if visits were billed through insurance. In addition, the fact that study participants were informed during recruitment processes that they would be able to keep an originally scheduled IBH consultation visit after the SSI study visit may have impacted their initial choice to engage in the SSI and the nature of their engagement with the visit.

Implications and future directions

The current study adds to the swiftly growing literature on SSIs [14] and to the more limited literature regarding implementation of SSIs in primary care. The potential for SSIs in this context has significant implications from a population health standpoint, as SSIs may help to increase access to evidence-based support at the time and place of need, reduce waitlists and wait-times, and bridge gaps in care systems while being resource efficient. SSIs are not meant to replace existing longer-term psychotherapy models, but for some patients, a single session may be sufficient in terms of need, preference, time, or resources. For example, some individuals may not feel they can commit the time to a full course of psychotherapy but may be willing to participate in an SSI. SSIs offered to patients currently on waitlists may also be an important point of intervention for preventing decline as patients wait for services, or perhaps giving patients what they need so that access is improved for those needing more specialized care. In general, SSIs align well with the stepped care model typically implemented within IBH programs as they can be offered as one option among a menu of options, allowing for stepping the intensity of care up or down as needed. Ultimately, even if only a small proportion of individuals in a given patient population were to participate in and benefit from an SSI, the impact of delivering this service at scale would be significant at the population level.

Given the promising support for the effectiveness of SSIs, future research on SSIs in primary care may benefit from an implementation science-focused framework. The current study’s implementation process, though executed within the clinical context, followed a research protocol, thus limiting understanding of the most workable processes for broader implementation. It will be important for future work to engage key stakeholders – patients, PCPs, leadership – to identify best practices for implementation of SSIs in primary care. Relatedly, future work should examine not only individual-level outcomes, but also provider-specific and system-level outcomes. For example, it will be important to identify the types of providers (e.g., discipline, training) who are best suited to deliver these interventions and to identify PCP and therapy provider satisfaction with the implementation of SSIs in practice. Additionally, examining practice-level outcomes (i.e., changes in wait-time, number of patients served, average total length of courses of care, and no-show/dropout rates) will be critical for understanding system-level impacts of SSIs. Relatedly, future work in primary care would benefit from exploring different models of SSI implementation (e.g., on a “one-at-a-time” basis, group-based SSIs [15, 35]. For example, in the current study, participants who chose to return for additional therapy services after their SSI study visit did not necessarily meet with the same clinician to continue the work initiated during the SSI. Though an SSI is meant to be a meaningful intervention in and of itself, successful clinical implementation of SSIs could benefit from streamlining care to prioritize coordination of services for patients within a given clinical context (e.g., meeting with the same clinician for future sessions if needed or building on the SSI content in the context of additional visits, even if with a different clinician). In addition, understanding the impact of SSIs on longer-term patient outcomes, both in terms of symptoms and well-being as well as in terms of healthcare engagement and utilization will be valuable areas for future research. For example, future work should examine whether patients who choose to participate in SSIs have previously engaged in therapy for similar or different presenting concerns and whether and when these same patients re-engage with psychotherapy services in the future. Finally, though potential billing procedures may vary across sites, understanding options for reimbursement of SSIs will be critical for promoting financial sustainability of this type of service within IBH models.

Conclusions

In conclusion, SSIs are one valuable approach among a continuum of care options offered in primary care IBH. Broader dissemination efforts will benefit from continued engagement of stakeholders to identify best practices for implementation and scalability.
