Enhanced plasmonic third-harmonic generation in graphene layers via Anderson localization induced by disordered gold gratings
Authors: Maryam Davoudi, Mohammad Sabaeian, Zeinab Zanbouri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe report the enhancement of third-harmonic generation (THG) in graphene layers in the mid-infrared frequency range through the introduction of structural disorder in plasmonic gold gratings. The structure consists of three stacked graphene sheets placed atop both periodic and disordered gold gratings on a SiO₂ substrate. The resonance frequency and THG efficiency can be tuned via electrostatic gating by adjusting the Fermi level. Introducing disorder by varying air gaps between gold elements breaks the translational symmetry, giving rise to highly localized plasmonic modes—a signature of Anderson localization. As disorder increases, transmittance changes and nonlinear enhancement becomes more pronounced. A 10% disorder nearly doubles the THG output (from 1.9 to 3.8 W), while the strongest field confinement occurs at 60%. This interplay between structural disorder, localized field enhancement, and graphene’s third-order nonlinearity enables tunable and efficient THG. The gold–graphene hybrid platform offers a promising route toward disorder-assisted nonlinear plasmonics within the range investigated in this work.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-26386-2.Keywords: Graphene, Anderson localization, Terahertz frequency, Plasmonics, Nonlinear optics, Third harmonic generationSubject terms: Materials science, Nanoscience and technology, Optics and photonics, Physics

Introduction

Plasmonics has become one of the most dynamic fields in modern optics, owing to its unique ability to confine and manipulate electromagnetic waves at subwavelength scales1–3. The fundamental principle of plasmonics relies on the excitation of collective oscillations of conduction electrons—surface plasmons—at the interface between a metal and a dielectric. These excitations allow electromagnetic fields to be concentrated into regions far smaller than the wavelength of light in free space, thereby enabling strong light–matter interactions that cannot be achieved in conventional optical systems2,4–7. Such extreme field confinement has fueled diverse applications, ranging from biochemical sensing and imaging to information processing and integrated photonic devices8,9.

In recent years, the mid-infrared (MIR) and terahertz (THz) spectral ranges have drawn particular attention in plasmonics research10,11, largely because of their relevance for molecular fingerprinting, free-space communications, and thermal imaging12. Within these frequency regimes, the integration of two-dimensional (2D) materials has opened promising opportunities for advancing plasmonic science10,11. 2D systems such as graphene, black phosphorus, and transition-metal dichalcogenides exhibit extraordinary electronic and optical properties that differ fundamentally from those of bulk semiconductors or metals10,11,13,14. Their reduced dimensionality leads to strong light–matter coupling, broadband absorption, and electrical tunability, making them highly attractive for both linear and nonlinear plasmonic applications15,16.

Among these candidates, graphene has become a cornerstone of nonlinear plasmonics. Graphene plasmons are highly tunable via electrostatic gating or chemical doping, offering dynamic control of resonance frequency and field confinement17–20. The linear dispersion of Dirac fermions in graphene gives rise to unusually strong optical nonlinearity, particularly a giant third-order susceptibility, χ(3), that is several orders of magnitude larger than in conventional bulk semiconductors21,22. Importantly, while centrosymmetry forbids second-harmonic generation (SHG) in pristine monolayer graphene20–24, third-harmonic generation (THG) is symmetry-allowed and has been both theoretically predicted and experimentally observed with high efficiency24. THG is of special interest because it requires only a single pump wavelength25 and provides access to new spectral windows, with applications in frequency conversion, imaging, and nonlinear photonic integration8,26,27.

Nevertheless, the practical exploitation of graphene’s nonlinear properties faces a key limitation: the material’s atomic-scale thickness results in an extremely small interaction volume with optical fields. Even though graphene has a very high χ(3), the actual output of nonlinear processes such as THG remains modest unless enhanced by additional mechanisms28. A well-established approach to overcome this constraint is to combine graphene with metallic nanostructures, which act as optical antennas that strongly concentrate electromagnetic fields at the graphene interface25,28. Hybrid systems incorporating periodic metallic gratings, nanoribbons, or metasurfaces can produce field enhancements of several orders of magnitude13,22,25,29,30, enabling efficient excitation of surface plasmons and amplifying nonlinear processes such as THG and four-wave mixing25,31,32. Furthermore, these hybrid platforms offer design flexibility; plasmonic resonances can be engineered through geometry, while graphene allows electrical tuning through Fermi-level control25.

Despite significant progress, most prior work has focused on perfectly periodic plasmonic structures, where the optical response is narrowband and strongly frequency-selective. Although periodicity ensures sharp resonances, it also limits the available spectral bandwidth and confines field enhancement to relatively uniform spatial regions. For nonlinear optics, where strong local confinement is essential, such uniformity may restrict the achievable efficiency. In contrast, structural disorder offers a pathway to qualitatively different physical behavior33.

When disorder is introduced into plasmonic lattices, translational symmetry is broken and multiple scattering of surface plasmon polaritons (SPPs) occurs. This process can lead to Anderson localization, where propagating waves become spatially localized due to constructive interference of scattered modes7,34,35. In plasmonic systems, Anderson-localized states act as random optical cavities that support extremely confined “hot spots” of electromagnetic fields. These localized modes can surpass the confinement achieved in periodic structures and extend the spectral response by generating multiple resonance channels and their resonant frequencies appear as transmission dips34. As disorder increases, interference effects intensify, reducing the mean free path of electrons. At a critical level of disorder or impurity concentration, electron motion is halted, and a metal–insulator phase transition occurs36–41. In graphene-based architectures, such localization is particularly appealing because the nonlinear polarization responsible for THG scales with the cube of the local electric field (P(3ω)∝E(ω)³). Hence, even moderate increases in field localization can dramatically enhance nonlinear output42.

Although disorder has been studied in the context of electronic transport and linear plasmon propagation in graphene systems35, its role in nonlinear phenomena has received little systematic attention7. The potential of engineered disorder to enhance third-harmonic generation in graphene–plasmonic hybrids remains largely unexplored. This gap is particularly significant given recent advances in nanofabrication that now enable precise introduction of nanoscale disorder through techniques such as electron-beam lithography and self-assembled nanoparticle arrays. These developments suggest that disorder-assisted nonlinear plasmonics is not only of theoretical interest but also experimentally realizable. While the present work is based on numerical simulations of the proposed structure, its fabrication is well within reach using established experimental methods, as outlined in Refs26,43. For such fabrication, the metallic grating is first defined on the SiO₂ substrate using photolithography, followed by thermal evaporation of a titanium adhesion layer and a gold layer. After the lift-off process, the grating array is formed. Subsequently, a thin Al₂O₃ layer is deposited by atomic layer deposition as a protective spacer. Finally, a trilayer graphene film grown by chemical vapor deposition (CVD) is transferred onto the entire structure using a poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA)-assisted wet-transfer method, ensuring that both the grating surface and the gaps were completely covered.

In this study, we investigate a hybrid graphene–gold metasurface designed to exploit Anderson localization for boosting THG efficiency in the MIR frequency range (28–46 THz). The proposed platform consists of three stacked graphene sheets placed on top of periodic and disordered gold gratings fabricated on a SiO₂ substrate. Using finite-element method (FEM) simulations, we analyze the linear and nonlinear optical response of the structure as a function of key parameters, including Fermi energy, incident field intensity, and the level of structural disorder. In the periodic configuration, resonance frequencies can be tuned electrostatically, and strong plasmonic fields enhance THG in a predictable manner. Introducing disorder by varying air-gap widths between gold gratings breaks translational symmetry, giving rise to multiple localized plasmonic modes characteristic of Anderson localization.

Our results reveal that disorder has a profound influence on both transmission and nonlinear response. At low to moderate disorder strengths, THG output nearly doubles compared to the periodic case, reaching several watts of radiated third-harmonic power. This enhancement arises from strongly localized plasmonic resonances that concentrate fields into nanoscale hot spots. At higher disorder levels, however, the response becomes spectrally irregular, with reduced overall efficiency despite the emergence of additional modes. These findings indicate that optimal disorder provides a powerful mechanism for tuning nonlinear performance, while excessive disorder leads to diminishing returns.

By systematically comparing periodic and disordered configurations, this work establishes that Anderson localization in graphene–plasmonic hybrids can serve as an effective route for enhancing third-harmonic generation. The concept of leveraging structural disorder to control nonlinear efficiency broadens the design space for MIR and THz photonics. Beyond its theoretical significance, the proposed platform is compatible with current nanofabrication techniques and may inspire experimental studies aimed at disorder-engineered nonlinear devices. Potential applications include compact MIR frequency converters, nonlinear imaging, and high-resolution diagnostic tools.

The intensity and output power of the THG signal are measured at the output port and used as performance metrics for analyzing the proposed structure.

Results

Periodic configuration for Au grating

In all two-dimensional simulations, the length of the graphene strips along the y-axis is assumed to be 1 m. This assumption enables the normalization of the calculated power values (in Watts), allowing for consistent and physically meaningful interpretation of the results. A TM-polarized plane wave (with the magnetic field oriented along the z-axis) with an intensity of 60 kW/cm² is normally incident from above onto the structure, covering a frequency range of 28 to 46 THz. The gold grating is assumed to be perfectly periodic, as shown in Figs. 1(a) and 1(b). Figure 2(a) shows the transmission spectra of the structure as a function of the incident frequency for different Fermi energy levels: 0.4, 0.45, 0.5, 0.55, and 0.6 eV. Graphene surface plasmons can be dynamically tuned by an external gate voltage. As expected, increasing the Fermi energy raises the carrier density at the Fermi level and shifts the resonance dip to higher frequencies (a blue shift). Consequently, the excitation frequency of the surface plasmons increases accompanied by changes in both the optical properties and transmission of the structure. It can be observed that for each Fermi level, a distinct transmission dip appears at a specific frequency, indicating the excitation of plasmons in the graphene layers. At the resonance frequency, due to strong light–matter interaction and field confinement, the transmission reaches a minimum. For instance, at a Fermi energy of 0.4 eV, the transmission drops to 0.28, while for 0.5 eV and 0.6 eV, the minimum values are 0.25 and 0.24, respectively. Figure 2(b) presents the electric field enhancement corresponding to different Fermi energy levels. For each case, the field enhancement peak coincides with the frequency where the transmission reaches its minimum. As shown, the electric field magnitude at Fermi energies of 0.4, 0.5, and 0.6 eV is enhanced by factors of approximately 444, 430, and 428, respectively, relative to the incident field.

(a) Transmission spectrum of the structure and, (b) enhancement of the incident electric field at different Fermi energies as a function of the fundamental frequency in the periodic configuration.

To investigate the excitation of SPPs via the graphene layer at the transmission dip, Fig. 3 illustrates the spatial distribution of the electric field corresponding to the incident wave at Ef = 0.5 eV along the grating, at the fundamental frequency. As seen in the transmission spectrum in Fig. 2(a), a distinct dip appears at 37.3 THz within the 28–46 THz range for Fermi energy of 0.5 eV. This decrease in transmission indicates the excitation of SPP modes along the graphene layer. It is clearly observable that surface plasmons are excited and confined on the graphene surface at the interface with air, supported by the underlying SiO₂ substrate. These localized and enhanced electric fields result in stronger light–matter interaction in the vicinity of the graphene sheet. Consequently, the concentration of electromagnetic energy in these regions can significantly enhance the system’s nonlinear optical responses.

Electric field norm distribution (V/m) of surface plasmon waves propagating on graphene at Ef = 0.5 eV for the mode at 37.3 THz in the periodic structure.

Figure 4 illustrates the frequency dependence of the output power and intensity of the third-harmonic generation for different Fermi energy levels (Ef = 0.4, 0.45, 0.5, 0.55, 0.6 eV), where the left y-axis corresponds to the output power and the right y-axis to the THG intensity. The output intensity of the THG signal is calculated using PTH/a, where “a” denotes the total width of the proposed structure. Each curve exhibits a distinct peak that corresponds to the frequency at which a minimum transmission was observed in the spectrum presented in Fig. 2(a). This frequency marks the point of enhanced light–matter interaction and stronger field confinement, resulting in a more pronounced THG response in terms of both intensity and output power. The linear and third-order conductivities of graphene are functions of the Fermi energy. As a result, increasing Ef ​shifts the resonance frequency and the peak response to higher frequencies. However, according to Eq. (6), the third-harmonic conductivity of graphene is inversely proportional to Ef, and thus increasing the Fermi energy reduces the resulting THG intensity and output power. As illustrated in Fig. 4, the output power at Ef = 0.4, 0.5, and 0.6 eV decreases from 5.2 to 1.9 and then to 0.58 W, respectively. It is important to note that the generated THG intensity and power also depend on the strength of the incident pump field.

THG output showing the power and the intensity as a function of the fundamental frequency for various Fermi energy levels in the periodic structure.

Figure 5 shows the third-harmonic generation conversion efficiency, defined as the ratio of the third-harmonic output power to the input power on a logarithmic scale, as a function of the fundamental frequency for different Fermi energies (Ef = 0.4, 0.45, 0.5, 0.55, 0.6 eV). As mentioned earlier, each Fermi energy exhibits a distinct dip at the frequency corresponding to the transmission drop in the spectrum of Fig. 2(a), which ultimately leads to the formation of peaks in both the electric field and the third-harmonic output intensity and power. These peaks represent resonances induced in the graphene layer, enhancing the efficiency of the nonlinear process. Since this plot is presented on a logarithmic scale, the closer a peak is to zero, the higher the structure’s capability to convert the input light into the third-harmonic component. According to the obtained results, the maximum conversion efficiency occurs at Ef = 0.4 eV and a frequency of 33.3 THz, with a value of − 35 dB. With increasing Ef, the efficiency decreases to − 38, −39.4, − 42.6, and − 44.5 dB, respectively, and the peaks shift toward higher frequencies. Therefore, tuning Ef allows control over both the resonance position and the conversion efficiency. However, increasing Ef reduces the third-order conductivity of graphene, leading to a decline in the THG process efficiency. A characteristic property of periodic structures is that modes are excited at distinct frequencies with well-defined intervals, analogous to the resonant modes of a Fabry–Perot oscillator. Each excitation, however, is associated with a certain linewidth that depends on the intrinsic losses of the resonator.

Third-harmonic conversion efficiency (in logarithmic scale) as a function of the fundamental frequency and graphene Fermi energy in the periodic structure.

Disordered Au grating

We now investigate THG in a trilayer graphene structure coupled with gold gratings placed at random positions. By randomly positioning the gold gratings, the periodic order between air and gold is disrupted, resulting in a disordered air configuration. This randomness gives rise to the Anderson localization phenomenon, which originates from interference between scattered waves. In such a disordered system, we expect the transmission spectrum to exhibit significant variations and fluctuations, the field confinement to become non-periodic, and the THG response to appear at various frequencies rather than being uniformly distributed along the graphene.

To implement this structure, gold strips with identical widths were placed at randomly selected positions, while the widths of the air gratings between them were randomly generated and used in the simulations. As schematically illustrated in Fig. 1(c), the width of the gold strips remains constant, whereas the width of each air gap is determined by the relation d = d0(1 + δ), where d0 = 345 nm corresponds to the air-gap width in the regular periodic configuration, and δ is a random number with a uniform distribution in the range [− ds, ds]. Based on this disorder model, simulations were performed for disorder intensities of ds= 0.1, 0.2, 0.6, and 0.8. By substituting these values into the formula for each disorder level, the corresponding range of air-gap variation is obtained, and 40 random values are selected to construct and simulate the geometry. The other simulation parameters were kept consistent with those in the previous section.

A normally incident wave with an intensity of 60 kW/cm² is directed along the z-axis toward the structure. In Fig. 6(a1–a5), the transmission spectra, and in Fig. 6(b1–b5), the third-harmonic generation output power as a function of the incident frequency are plotted for Fermi energies ranging from 0.4 to 0.6 eV at ds= 0.6. Compared to the periodic case, significant spectral variations appear around the main resonance peak due to interference effects. These oscillations originate from the interference between multiple plasmonic wavefronts propagating through the graphene layers, which is a signature of Anderson localization that determines the effective resonance frequencies of the surface plasmon modes.

(a1-a5) Transmission spectra and (b1-b5) third-harmonic output power in the disordered structure with dₛ= 0.6 for different Fermi energy levels as a function of the fundamental frequency.

The presence of multiple modes arises from the sensitivity of propagating plasmons to local variations in the effective refractive index, leading to multiple reflections within the disordered structure. Since the gold elements act as reflective boundaries, these reflections are further amplified, thereby enhancing the nonlinear optical response over a broader frequency range. As illustrated in Fig. 6(a1–a5), it is evident that increasing the Fermi energy results in a gradual change in transmission. This behavior is attributed to the dispersion relation of graphene plasmons, where the plasmon frequency increases with Ef​ at a fixed propagation constant due to the dependence of the linear surface conductivity on Ef​.

Interestingly, the maximum THG output occurs at Ef= 0.4 eV around 34.7 THz (Fig. 6(b1)), and gradually decreases as Ef increases. This trend is consistent with the periodic case (Fig. 4), indicating that lower Fermi energies are more favorable for efficient THG in both periodic and disordered structures. The reduction in THG at higher Ef can be explained by noting that although the nonlinear conductivity σ(3) is inversely proportional to Ef, an increase in Ef​ also improves the quality factor of quasi-cavities formed by Anderson-localized plasmon modes, which partially compensates for the reduction in σ(3)2–6. As a result, a moderate value of Ef can balance field localization and nonlinear efficiency.

Overall, these results demonstrate that tuning the Fermi energy provides a powerful mechanism to control both transmission and the nonlinear THG response. While strong field confinement and disorder-induced localization are essential for enhancing THG, the findings highlight the potential of graphene–gold random metasurfaces as tunable, high-efficiency nonlinear devices in the mid-infrared regime.

To gain deeper insight into the influence of THG in disordered structures, Fig. 7(a-d) presents the electric field distribution of the incident wave (fundamental frequency, FF) for the case of ds= 0.6. The simulation is carried out at Ef= 0.5 eV for four distinct resonance frequencies—29.3, 37.3, 39.0, and 41.2 THz. At each frequency, the electric field exhibits strong localization, forming irregularly distributed plasmon modes along the graphene layer due to multiple scattering events induced by structural disorder, characteristic of Anderson localization. At the lower frequency of 29.3 THz, the mode exhibits limited localization with only a few confinement centers near the structure’s center. For each resonance frequency, the localization centers along the graphene layer are randomly positioned and vary from one resonance frequency to another. It can be observed from Fig. 7(b) that the peak electric field at the localization centers is approximately 1.5 times greater than that in Fig. 3. This enhancement in field strength makes the proposed graphene-based disordered plasmonic structure more effective in enhancing THG compared to the periodic configuration. These localized plasmon modes are essential for enhancing nonlinear optical processes, such as THG, as the third-harmonic polarization scales with the cube of the local electric field (P(3ω)∝E(ω)3). The intense field confinement in the disordered structure amplifies the local nonlinear response, providing a robust mechanism for boosting THG efficiency compared to periodic structures.

To systematically examine the effect of disorder on THG performance, we compute both the transmission spectra and third-harmonic output power for varying levels of structural randomness (ds= 0.1, 0.2, 0.6, and 0.8), in comparison to the periodic reference case (ds= 0), as shown in Fig. 8(a) and 8(b). The Fermi level is fixed at Ef= 0.5 eV for all cases. In the periodic structure, only a single transmission dip appears between 28 and 46 THz, centered on the primary resonance. As disorder increases, additional resonances and spectral fluctuations emerge, accompanied by a increase in overall transmission due to stronger localization and enhanced scattering. In contrast, the THG output power benefits from low disorder. Specifically, ds= 0.1 yields highest third-harmonic output power across all cases, followed by ds= 0.2. Beyond this, for higher disorder levels (ds= 0.6 and 0.8), the output power decreases, although interference-induced modal features persist throughout the spectrum. Quantitatively, the peak THG powers for ds= 0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.6, and 0.8 are approximately 1.9, 3.8, 3.3, 0.8, and 0.7 W, respectively, occurring at resonance frequencies of 37.3, 36.6, 37.1, 39.0, and 40.4 THz. These results confirm that low disorder, by creating localized resonances, optimizes light–matter interaction and ultimately leads to an increase in the third-harmonic output power at the same resonance frequency as in the periodic case. In contrast, at disorder levels of ds= 0.6 and 0.8, the number of resonance frequencies increases, but the output power at these frequencies is lower than in the periodic structure. Comparing these two cases, ds= 0.6 performs better than ds= 0.8, because at ds= 0.8 the structural disorder is too strong, resulting in output power that is close to zero over most frequencies.

To analyze and compare the third-harmonic conversion efficiency in the periodic case (ds= 0) and in structures with different disorder levels, contour plots of efficiency as a function of Fermi energy and frequency are examined in Fig. 9(a-d). In the periodic configuration (Fig. 5), each Fermi energy corresponds to only one enhanced resonant peak, which is clearly visible in the contour map. When disorder is introduced—particularly at low levels (ds= 0.1 in panel (a) and ds= 0.2 in panel (b))—new resonant peaks and modes emerge at different frequencies, originating from interference of scattered plasmonic waves. In this case, the efficiency map still retains relatively sharp and well-defined resonant features similar to the ordered case, although the peak positions are slightly shifted. At higher disorder levels (ds​= 0.6 in panel (c) and ds= 0.8 in panel (d)), the THG response becomes more dispersed and irregular, with broader regions of enhanced efficiency. This behavior indicates that moderate disorder (panels (a) and (b)) promotes stronger field localization and the formation of stable localized modes, thereby improving the efficiency of nonlinear interactions, whereas excessive disorder (panels (c) and (d)) leads to spectral broadening and disruption of mode regularity, which ultimately reduces the efficiency of the main peaks despite the increased number of active modes.

Conclusion

In this study, we theoretically investigated a plasmonic platform composed of three layers of graphene placed on 40 gold lattices and supported by an SiO₂ substrate, within the mid-infrared range of 28 to 46 THz. An incident wave with an intensity of 60 kW/cm² was applied from above. The periodic structure achieves a field enhancement factor of 444 at Fermi energy of 0.4 eV. Specifically, at Ef= 0.5 eV, a pronounced resonance peak appears in both the transmission spectrum and the third-harmonic generation output at 37.3 THz, attributed to the excitation of surface plasmon modes, on which the effects of both periodic and disordered structures are investigated. Introducing structural disorder by varying the air gap widths between fixed-width gold elements (i.e., disordered gold gratings) results in new resonant modes emerging near the original resonance frequency, indicative of enhanced light–matter interaction. Among different disorder levels, ds= 0.1 and ds= 0.2 yield the highest enhancement in THG output power, increasing from 1.9 W in the periodic case to 3.8 W and 3.3 W, respectively. However, higher disorder strengths (ds= 0.6 and ds= 0.8) lead to stronger spectral fluctuations across frequencies but with reduced overall intensity and power. Field distribution analyses reveal that, while the periodic structure confines light uniformly within air cavities, disorder induces nonuniform, randomly localized plasmon modes. Consequently, the resonance frequencies of localized modes shift with increasing disorder. Simulation results also demonstrate a clear blue shift of resonance peaks with increasing Fermi energy. Furthermore, tuning the Fermi level effectively controls the peak positions and intensities of the THG output in the disordered structure. Overall, THG output power decreases with increasing Fermi energy in both periodic and disordered configurations. Structural disorder in gold gratings enhances THG efficiency through resonance-driven field localization, with comparable performance across multiple wavelengths, particularly at low disorder levels (ds= 0.1 and 0.2). At ds = 0.1, the output power increases at the same resonance frequency as in the periodic case, whereas at ds = 0.2, a slight frequency shift is observed. This enhancement arises from resonance effects and strong field localization due to Anderson localization. These results provide a promising platform for tunable nonlinear optical systems in the mid- infrared frequency regime. While the current work is purely theoretical, the enhanced THG response may motivate future research into practical applications, including potential use in nonlinear imaging techniques and high-resolution optical diagnostics.
