Assessment of human exposure to terpenes from fragrant plants in homes using passive sampling
Authors: Wonsik Shin, Arum Lee, Jaeho Shin, Eugene Song, Stefana Sochichiu, Yerim Koo, Yongseok Hong, Ji Hoon Seo, Thanh-Dat Pham, Pil-Gon Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe air quality benefits of indoor plants are widely recognized, but their capacity to emit volatile organic compounds (VOCs) such as terpenes remains less explored. This research quantifies the impact of a common fragrant household plant, Plectranthus tomentosa, on indoor terpene levels within residential environments. The emission characteristics of terpenes from potted and hydroculture plants were investigated using PTR-TOF-MS in a controlled chamber, and exposure levels in residential environments were assessed using passive sampler (ePTFE PS). Deploying fragrant plants, particularly hydroculture plants, led to a substantial elevation of indoor terpene concentrations. In 30 homes, hydroculture plants increased average limonene and α-pinene concentrations by 2.3 and 2.5 times, respectively, over 3 h. Our findings demonstrate that fragrant plants, particularly when hydroculture, act as a significant source of limonene and α-pinene. In contrast, the plants exhibited negligible removal efficiency for BTEX compounds. The mean estimated daily exposure dose (DED) for limonene inhalation in infants was 45.4 µg/kg/day, which was significantly higher than that in adults at 13.7 µg/kg/day when deploying hydroculture plants. These results suggest that fragrant plants should be carefully selected and managed, especially in indoor environments where sensitive individuals are present.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51194-7.Keywords: Passive sampling, Fragrant plant, Hydroculture, Indoor air, Terpenes, Daily exposure doseSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Plant sciences

Introduction

Currently, indoor plants are widely used in offices and homes1,2, and this practice is associated with numerous advantages. In the past three decades, numerous studies have demonstrated the capability of indoor plants to considerably reduce the levels of majority of urban air pollutants3–5. The high energy consumption through the utilization of artificial ventilation and air conditioners has necessitated the investigation of methods for adjusting indoor air quality and microclimate conditions with minimal or no energy consumption6. Indoor plants are utilized for their aromatic properties, which can help in deterring, repelling or attracting herbivores or insects7,8. The odors of these plants are attributable to the presence of volatile organic compounds (VOCs), such as terpenes. Currently, the advantages of indoor plants, including minimizing air pollution, aromatic properties, and low energy consumption, have attracted research interest. Nevertheless, while fragrant plants can function as an indoor purifier for VOCs, they can also be a source of terpenes, which is potentially risky at high concentrations in residential environments.

Terpenes are naturally made by plants and animals9–11, and they possess numerous advantages, including protection against abiotic and biotic stresses (such as pathogens, predators, and competitors)9, anti-insect, antimicrobial, and anti-herbivore properties12–14, and antiplasmodial, antiviral, anticancer, antidiabetic, and antidepressant properties15–19. Nevertheless, they are associated with potential adverse effects, including acting as allergens, which may lead to asthma, skin irritation, and chest tightness20–23. For instance, a previous study reported a relative increase of 17–18% in both wheezing and asthma and 1.55% in exhaled nitric oxide when the indoor concentration of limonene increased by 100%24. Particularly, its harmful impacts were considerably more pronounced in children, with an estimated two-fold increase. Consequently, limonene is now designated as a potentially hazardous chemical according to the Hazardous Chemical Information System of Australia25. Furthermore, the reaction between terpenes and oxidants, such as ozone and hydroxyl radicals, results in the production of secondary products that can adversely affect the human health26–29. These unwanted byproducts are formed through the oxidation of terpenes30. To mitigate the potential adverse effects of terpenes, it is imperative to investigate the emission of terpenes by fragrant plants in a residential environment, as people generally spend nearly 90% of their lives indoors and half of their time at home31–33.

Hydroculture (soilless cultivation in water or nutrient solution) has gained popularity as an indoor plant cultivation method due to its cleanliness, ease of maintenance, and perceived pest-deterrent properties. Previous studies have reported that hydroculture can enhance plant growth, transpiration rates, and volatile organic compound (VOC) removal capacity compared to traditional soil-based potted cultivation4. However, the increased transpiration and growth associated with hydroculture may also promote higher terpene emission rates, as the biosynthesis and emission of these compounds are closely linked to plant metabolic activity. Furthermore, terpenes emitted from plant roots may be released more readily in hydroculture systems, where soil adsorption is absent34. Despite the growing adoption of hydroculture for indoor plants, its impact on terpene emission has not been systematically compared with potted cultivation in residential settings. In addition to terpenes, volatile aromatic hydrocarbons such as benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, xylenes, and styrene (collectively referred to as BTEX and styrene) are common indoor air pollutants originating from building materials, furniture, and consumer products.

Generally, the short-term exposure to VOCs is assessed using active sampling owing to its high accuracy and efficacy35–37. However, owing to unstable concentrations and the use of large-sized equipment, active sampling is not a viable option for relatively long-term monitoring38–42. In contrast, passive sampling is straightforward, convenient, and cost-effective compared to active sampling43. Particularly, passive sampling may be a suitable alternative to active sampling for long-term monitoring, as it enables the determination of the time-averaged exposure under varying concentrations44–47. In a previous study, we designed a passive sampler consisting of an expanded polytetrafluoroethylene membrane with an adsorbent (ePTFE PS) for detecting VOCs in indoor air48. The results obtained through passive sampling when this membrane was used demonstrated a high accuracy compared to when proton-transfer-reaction mass spectrometry was used for the real-time monitoring of VOCs. Moreover, the impact of air turbulence on the sampling rate around the sampler is minimal, as the total mass transfer is determined by the resistance of the porous membrane tube of the sampler. In terms of the mass of the inserted absorbent, the sampling rate of ePTFE PS is higher than those of previously reported passive samplers48. Additionally, the production cost of the ePTFE PS is low owing to the relatively lower cost of the tubes and absorbents used for the production of the ePTFE PS. The designed sampler has been enhanced by increasing its sampling rates and making it wearable to enable personal exposure measurement49,50.

This study aimed to determine the levels of volatile aromatic hydrocarbons and monoterpenes under normal usage conditions of fragrant plants using ePTFE PS. Emission characteristics were measured in real-time in a controlled chamber, and exposure levels were assessed in 30 residential homes under both potted and hydroculture cultivation conditions.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and instrumental analyses

The targeted VOCs, including benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, m, p-, and o-xylenes, styrene, limonene, α-pinene, and selected internal standards (toluene-d8, chlorobenzene-d5) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Milwaukee, WI, USA). Dichloromethane (DCM) used for extracting the VOCs from the passive sampler was also purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Milwaukee, WI, USA).

Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) was used for the analysis of VOCs. The GC-MS used for the analysis consisted of Agilent 6890 A gas chromatograph (Santa Clara, CA, USA) and Agilent 5973 N mass-selective detector coupled with Agilent 7683 auto-injector and Agilent DB-5 column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm). All targeted VOCs were analyzed using electron ionization (EI) mode. The injection volume was set to 2 µL and the split ratio was set to 5:1. Helium was used as the carrier gas and was sustained at a constant flow rate of 1.0 mL min−1. The column oven temperature was initially set to 40 °C for 2 min, followed by a ramp of 10 °C per minute until reaching 100 °C. Subsequently, the oven temperature was continuously increased at constant rate until it reached the final temperature of 270 °C. The details of the operating conditions of GC-MS and the m/z values used for quantifying were shown in Tables S5 and S6.

Proton transfer reaction time-of-flight mass spectrometry (PTR-TOF-MS; IONICON, Innsbruck, Austria) was used for monitoring the emission of limonene from test plant with H3O+ as the selective reagent ion in real-time. The gas flow rate, inlet temperature, E/N ratio, and drift tube pressure were set to 2.00 cm3 min−1, 80.0 °C, 140 Td, and 2.3 mbar, respectively. The monoterpenes were analyzed at m/z value of 137.1325 ((C10H16)H+)51.

Passive sampling

The passive sampling for collecting indoor VOCs was conducted using ePTFE PS which was developed in our previous study. The details of the ePTFE PS used in this study are described in Fig. 1a and in our previous study48. Briefly, the ePTFE PS constituted of ePTFE tubes (ID 2 mm; OD 4 mm; Length 500 mm) and 0.9 g (dry weight) of Optipore-L493 (Sigma Aldrich) adsorbent with a high sorption capacity for VOCs52, which was packed into the ePTFE tube. The tubes were washed using Soxhlet extraction with a 50 mL mixture of n-hexane and acetone for 12 h and dried in fume hood before using it. After exposing the ePTFE PS to the VOCs, it was submerged in a 40 mL glass vial containing 10 mL of DCM including internal standards and left overnight in the fridge. Thereafter, the extracts were analyzed using GC-MS to determine the concentration of VOCs. In our previous study, the passive sampler was simultaneously deployed with PTR-TOF-MS in a test chamber to verify the performance of the sampler. The results confirmed the high precision and accuracy of passive sampler48.

Commercial fragrant plants

A well-known commercially available fragrant plant, Plectranthus tomentosa (P. tomentosa), known as vicks plant, was selected as the test plant. The test plant was purchased from a commercial flower shop in Seoul, South Korea, as a horticultural product intended for indoor use. As P. tomentosa is a commercially cultivated ornamental plant that is not listed as endangered or threatened on the IUCN Red List of Threatened Species or in the Appendices of the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES), no specific permits or licenses were required for its acquisition or use. The plant was obtained through standard commercial channels and was not collected from the wild. All experimental procedures involving this plant material complied with relevant institutional, national, and international guidelines and legislation. The test plant was cultivated in a 15-cm-diameter pot, with total height, including the pot and the plant, being 25 cm. For hydroculture experiments, the potted plants (cultivated in standard commercial potting soil composed primarily of peat moss, perlite, and organic compost) were carefully removed from the soil, and the roots were thoroughly washed with distilled water to remove residual soil particles. The cleaned plants were then transferred to glass containers (volume: approximately 500 mL) filled with distilled water. No additional nutrients were supplemented during the experimental period, as the short duration (one day) was deemed insufficient to induce nutrient deficiency effects on terpene emission. The same conversion procedure was applied for both the chamber experiments and the field panel study to ensure consistency. All plant materials used in this study were commercially purchased, and no specific permits or licenses were required. All procedures complied with relevant institutional, national, and international guidelines and legislation.

For the calibration of PTR-TOF-MS for limonene, an aliquot of a 5% (v/v) limonene solution in ethanol was introduced to the bottom of the closed chamber and allowed to volatilize. Once the solution was fully volatilized within a 10-minute period, additional aliquots were introduced to the test chamber to increase the concentration of gaseous limonene. The PTR-TOF-MS signal for limonene was then monitored through the incremental addition of the analyte to nominal concentrations ranging from 0.66 to 13 µg L−1. The signals in arbitrary units, as measured by PTR-TOF-MS, and the spiked concentration of monoterpenes in the chamber exhibited high linearity, as indicated by an R2 value of 0.99 (shown in Fig. S3).

The emission characteristics of the test plants were investigated in real-time in a 125 L closed acrylic chamber maintained at 23 ± 1 °C and 70 ± 10% of relative humidity using PTR-TOF-MS. The hydroculture and potted plants were placed in the chamber respectively for 40 min after calibrating the 125 L closed chamber for 10 min.

Sample collection

To observe the levels of indoor VOCs when fragrant plants were present in the homes, a panel of 30 volunteers was enlisted. The homes ranged in size from approximately 11 to 39 m3. During the sampling period, windows and doors were kept closed to minimize the influence of outdoor air exchange, and mechanical ventilation systems were turned off. The detailed volumes and ventilation information summarized in Table S3. Participants were instructed to refrain from using air fresheners, candles, cleaning products, or any other potential sources of VOCs during the experiment. The plants were placed in the living room area, and the passive samplers were positioned at breathing zone height (approximately 1.2–1.5 m) at a distance of 1–2 m from the plants.

On the initial day of the experiment, passive samplers were placed inside the home for approximately three hours before and after placing three potted plants. After the experiment, the three pots were removed, and all plants were cultivated using hydroculture, after which the plants were isolated in a separate space. On the following day, the same procedure as the initial day was repeated, with three hydroculture plants serving as the subjects.

After the sampling process, the ePTFE PS was dipped into the 40-mL glass vials containing 10 mL of DCM spiked with internal standards and stored overnight in the fridge for the extraction of the VOCs. The extracts were stored in a -4 °C fridge until analysis. To calculate the time-weighted average concentrations, the mass of VOCs in the 20-field blank samples was considered as the control, and the internal standards were used to calibrate the extracted samples. Field blank samples (n = 20) were prepared by briefly opening sealed ePTFE PS at sampling locations and immediately resealing them without exposure to ambient air. These blanks were transported, stored, and analyzed under the same conditions as the exposed samplers to account for any contamination during handling and transport. Daily exposure dose

The daily exposure dose (DED) from inhalation was calculated using a standard inhalation exposure equation widely adopted in environmental health risk assessment53,54. The age groups used for DED calculations (infants < 1 year, toddlers 1–5 year, children 6–10 year, teenagers 11–20 year, and adults ≥ 21 year) were adopted from the U.S. EPA Exposure Factors Handbook (2011), which classifies populations based on developmental stages with distinct physiological characteristics. This classification has been widely adopted in indoor exposure assessment studies53. Infants and toddlers have higher inhalation rates relative to body weight, making them particularly vulnerable to airborne pollutants. The DED was estimated for all five age groups to evaluate the potential exposure of all household members, including vulnerable populations who may be present in homes where fragrant plants are used. Since the panel study recruited adult volunteers as household representatives, age-specific physiological parameters (inhalation rate and body weight) were obtained from the U.S. EPA Exposure Factors Handbook (2011) to enable standardized comparisons. Quality assurance and quality control

The calibration curves about targeted VOCs were constructed at seven points ranging from 1 to 1000 µg L−1 using linear regression. The correlation coefficients (R2) for all analytes exceeded 0.999. Accuracy was assessed at the median concentration level of the calibration curves, and values ranged from 97.0% to 111.2% for all targeted VOCs. Instrumental stability was verified by analyzing DCM containing only internal standards after every ten samples. The method detection limits (MDLs) for benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, m, p-, and o-xylenes, styrene, limonene, α-pinene were previously determined in our previous study, yielding values of 0.23, 1.40, 0.90, 0.66, 0.66, 0.56, 4.76, and 1.93 µg m−3, respectively49. Each ePTFE PS was vacuum-packed immediately after fabrication and stored under clean conditions until deployment.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.3.1. Paired t-tests were employed to evaluate the significance of differences in VOC concentrations before and after plant deployment within the same household. This paired design inherently controls for between-home variability, including differences in home size, ventilation, and background VOC sources. The significance level was set at p < 0.05. Due to the limited sample size (n = 30) and the within-subject paired experimental design, multivariate analysis was not conducted; however, the paired comparison minimizes the influence of potential confounding variables by using each home as its own control.

Discussion

The results of this study demonstrate that P. tomentosa, particularly when cultivated using hydroculture, acts as a significant source of indoor terpenes, increasing limonene and α-pinene concentrations by 2.3 and 2.5 times, respectively, over a 3-hour period in residential settings. These findings are consistent with previous reports demonstrating that hydroculture enhances plant transpiration rates and VOC-related metabolic activity compared to soil-based cultivation55. For instance, it has been reported that the growth, transpiration, and emission of monoterpenes by hydroculture-cultivated Petroselinum crispum were higher than those of potted plant55. Particularly, the increase in the emission of α-pinene (more than two times) was statistically significant. Additionally, the growth rate of hydroculture plants (e.g., Syngonium podophyllum) was higher than that of potted plants4. A previous study34 reported that terpenes are synthesized and emitted from the roots and rhizomes of various plant species, and other previous study56 reported observing cineole and α-pinene adsorbed to colloidal soil particles of the litter and bare zones. The removal of soil as a physical adsorbent for root-emitted terpenes in hydroculture systems is proposed as a key mechanism contributing to the elevated airborne terpene concentrations observed in the residential panel study. The substantially higher emission rate measured for hydroculture plants (810 µg h−1) relative to potted plants (74 µg h−1) in the controlled chamber experiment supports this conclusion.

Numerous studies have demonstrated the emission of terpenes by plants. For instance, A previous study demonstrated that gynaecium emitted the highest amount of limonene (62.5%)57. Another study reported that the amounts of limonene and α-pinene emitted from the broken tips of indoor plants in 24 h are approximately 1200 and 120 µmol g−1 day−1 (dry mass), respectively58. Further, another study reported that 15 Eucalyptus species emitted 0.08–0.8 µg g−1 h−1 (leaf mass basis)59. The observed increase in limonene concentrations is particularly notable from a public health perspective. Previous epidemiological research has reported that a 100% increase in indoor limonene concentration is associated with a 17–18% relative increase in wheezing and asthma prevalence, with effects approximately two-fold more pronounced in children24. In the present study, hydroculture plants increased limonene concentrations by 2.3 times on average, and by up to 19-fold in individual homes (Volunteer 29: from 17.1 to 321.0 µg m−3), suggesting that health implications for sensitive populations warrant serious consideration. Some epidemiological studies have employed a reference exposure concentration (55 µg m−3) for limonene24,60. This value is comparable to the measured concentrations of limonene before the deployment of the potted and hydroculture plants, which were 40 and 38 µg m−3, respectively. While all calculated HQ values remained below 1 for the 3-hour sampling period, real-world exposure durations are likely to be considerably longer, as plants are typically maintained in homes continuously. Therefore, the health risks to vulnerable populations such as infants and young children should be further investigated through long-term exposure studies. Additionally, secondary products that may be through the reaction of terpenes with oxidants, such as ozone and hydroxyl radicals26–29, may cause adverse health effects30. Therefore, care should be taken when using hydroculture plants at home.

The considerable discrepancy between the laboratory-estimated limonene increase (7,300 µg over 3 h) and the field-measured increase (1,000 µg) for hydroculture plants highlights the complexity of terpene dynamics in real residential environments. Several factors likely contribute to this discrepancy. Indoor ozone and hydroxyl radicals can react with monoterpenes, leading to the degradation of emitted terpenes and the formation of secondary products such as formaldehyde and secondary organic aerosols26–29, thereby reducing the measured concentrations of parent terpenes. Furthermore, even with closed windows and doors, the air exchange rate in homes was likely higher than in the sealed acrylic chamber, resulting in dilution of emitted terpenes. Adsorption onto indoor surfaces such as walls, furniture, and fabrics, as well as differences in temperature, humidity, and light conditions between homes and the controlled chamber, may also have contributed to the observed discrepancy. These findings emphasize that laboratory-based emission measurements should be interpreted cautiously when estimating real-world indoor terpene exposure.

In contrast to terpenes, the model plant showed negligible removal efficiency for BTEX compounds within the 3-hour sampling period, with concentration differences of less than 1.2 times before and after deployment. This finding is consistent with previous observations that VOC removal by indoor plants is generally species-specific and requires extended exposure periods under well-controlled conditions1,61. The results suggest that while P. tomentosa has limited utility as a BTEX purifier, its role as a significant terpene emitter—particularly under hydroculture conditions—should be carefully considered when selecting indoor plants for residential use.

It should also be noted that the hydroculture experiment was conducted on the day following the transition from soil-based potted cultivation. While a complete metabolic adaptation to hydroculture cannot be achieved within such a short period, the monoterpenes emitted by P. tomentosa are primarily stored in leaf glandular trichomes and released through physical evaporation rather than being newly biosynthesized62,63. The higher emission observed in hydroculture is therefore more likely attributable to the removal of soil as a physical adsorbent for root-emitted terpenes than to a metabolic shift. Nevertheless, future studies should systematically investigate the time-dependent changes in terpene emission rates following the transition from potted to hydroculture cultivation to better characterize the emission dynamics.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the passive sampling was conducted over a relatively short period (3 h), which may not fully represent long-term daily exposure patterns. Actual exposure would vary depending on the duration of plant presence and daily activity patterns. Second, the study did not account for potential reactions between emitted terpenes and indoor oxidants (e.g., ozone, hydroxyl radicals), which may produce secondary pollutants such as formaldehyde and secondary organic aerosols. Third, the DED calculations relied on reference values for inhalation rates and body weights from the U.S. EPA Exposure Factors Handbook rather than individual measurements of the participating volunteers, which may introduce some uncertainty. Fourth, this study focused on a single plant species (P. tomentosa), and the emission characteristics may vary considerably across different fragrant plant species. Fifth, environmental factors such as temperature, humidity, light intensity, and soil moisture, which can influence terpene emission rates, were not systematically controlled across the 30 homes. Sixth, the absorption efficiency of terpenes through inhalation was assumed to be 100%, which may overestimate actual internal exposure. Finally, the ePTFE passive sampler measures time-weighted average concentrations, which may mask short-term peak exposure events. Future studies should incorporate long-term monitoring with higher temporal resolution, consider secondary product formation, include diverse plant species, and extend the investigation to various indoor environments.

Conclusions

This study provides novel evidence that indoor fragrant plants, particularly when cultivated using hydroculture, can serve as a significant source of terpenes in residential environments. The panel study involving 30 homes demonstrated that deploying hydroculture P. tomentosa for 3 h increased the average indoor concentrations of limonene and α-pinene by approximately 2.3 and 2.5 times, respectively, compared to background levels. In contrast, the model plant exhibited negligible removal efficiency for BTEX compounds during the same period. The DED for limonene was highest in infants (45.4 µg/kg/day) under hydroculture conditions, highlighting the vulnerability of young children. Unlike previous studies that have predominantly focused on the VOC removal capacity of indoor plants, our findings highlight the dual role of fragrant plants as both potential purifiers and significant sources of indoor air pollution. The ePTFE passive sampler employed in this study proved to be a practical, cost-effective, and reliable tool for assessing residential terpene exposure, demonstrating its utility for long-term indoor air quality monitoring. These findings underscore the importance of careful selection and management of indoor fragrant plants, especially in environments where sensitive populations such as infants and young children are present. Future research should investigate long-term exposure effects, secondary product formation from terpene-oxidant reactions, and the emission characteristics of diverse plant species under various indoor conditions.
