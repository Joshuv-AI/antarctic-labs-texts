Understanding the sign problem from an exact Path Integral Monte Carlo model of interacting harmonic fermions
Siu A. Chin

Abstract
This work shows that the recently discovered operator contraction identity for solving the discreet Path Integral of the harmonic oscillator can be applied equally to fermions in any dimension. This then yields an exactly solvable model for studying the sign problem where the Path Integral Monte Carlo energy at any time step for any number of fermions is known analytically, or can be computed numerically. It is found that repulsive/attractive pairwise interaction shifts the sign problem to larger/smaller imaginary time, but does not make it more severe than the non-interacting case. More surprisingly, for closed-shell number of fermions, the sign problem goes away at large imaginary time. Fourth-order and newly found variable-bead algorithms are used to compute ground state energies of quantum dots with up to 110 electrons and compared to results obtained by modern neural networks.

Introduction

Recent advances in Path Integral Monte Carlo (PIMC) continue to be the use of
parameterized fourth-order propagators to optimize PIMC’s convergence at the
fewest number of beads/time-slices for both bosons

problem worsens with increasing number of beads. However, because there is no
simple model for understanding PIMC for fermions at each time step, one’s knowledge
of the sign problem remains scant. For example, it is well known since Takahashi and Imada’s
calculation

Yet, an explicit proof

technique used in that proof can now be generalized to explain why there is no sign problem
for closed-shell particle states at large imaginary time. (See Sect.

In a previous work

discovered the
universal
PIMC discrete propagator for the harmonic oscillator, which has
the same functional form for all initial short-time propagators. Each short-time propagator only modifies
the argument of the universal coefficient functions. This then makes it possible
to obtain physical observables in closed forms for all short-time propagators at once.
In particular, one can compute the thermodynamic and Hamiltonian energies analytically at
each discrete imaginary time step for any short-time propagator
chin23b
.

This work now shows that this contraction identity remains valid
for the anti-symmetric determinant fermion propagator at all dimensions and for any number of fermions.
Therefore, one has a completely solvable PIMC model of harmonic fermions with analytical
thermodynamic and Hamiltonian energies.
Moreover, the model remains solvable if the pairwise interaction is also harmonic.
Therefore, the sign problem can be examined in detail, with and without interactions.

II
Free fermion propagator contractions

For the dimensionless harmonic oscillator Hamiltonian in 1D
given by

\hat{H}=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}x^{2}\equiv\hat{T}+\hat{V},

(1)

as shown in Ref.
chin23a
, the Gaussian integral needed to
compute the discrete path integral
is given by

\displaystyle\langle x^{\prime}|{\rm e}^{-a\hat{T}}{\rm e}^{-b\hat{V}}{\rm e}^{-c\hat{T}}|x^{*}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi a}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi c}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,{\rm e}^{-\frac{1}{2a}(x^{\prime}-x)^{2}-b\frac{1}{2}x^{2}-\frac{1}{2c}(x-x^{*})^{2}}

(2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\kappa}}{\rm e}^{-\nu\frac{1}{2}x^{\prime 2}}{\rm e}^{-\frac{1}{2\kappa}(x^{\prime}-x^{*})^{2}}{\rm e}^{-\mu\frac{1}{2}{x^{*}}^{2}}

(3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle x^{\prime}|{\rm e}^{-\nu\hat{V}}{\rm e}^{-\kappa\hat{T}}{\rm e}^{-\mu\hat{V}}|x^{*}\rangle

(4)

with

\kappa=a+abc+c,\quad\nu=\frac{bc}{\kappa},\quad\mu=\frac{ba}{\kappa}.

(5)

This means that one actually has an operator identity

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{-a\hat{T}}{\rm e}^{-b\hat{V}}{\rm e}^{-c\hat{T}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{-\nu\hat{V}}{\rm e}^{-\kappa\hat{T}}{\rm e}^{-\mu\hat{V}},

(6)

which contracts two free particle propagators
{\rm e}^{-a\hat{T}}
and
{\rm e}^{-c\hat{T}}
into one

{\rm e}^{-\kappa\hat{T}}
. Therefore any discrete path integral of the harmonic oscillator can be contracted
down to a single free propagator form and be evaluated analytically as

\displaystyle\langle x^{\prime}|{\rm e}^{-A\hat{V}}{\rm e}^{-B\hat{T}}{\rm e}^{-C\hat{V}}|x\rangle={\rm e}^{-A\frac{1}{2}{x^{\prime}}^{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi B}}{\rm e}^{-\frac{1}{2B}(x^{\prime}-x)^{2}}{\rm e}^{-C\frac{1}{2}x^{2}}.

(7)

Consider the case of
n
-particles in
D
-dimension with configuration vector
{\bf x}=({\bf r}_{1},{\bf r}_{2}\cdots,{\bf r}_{n})
where
each particle position vector is
{\bf r}=(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{D})
. Since the harmonic oscillator is separable in each coordinate,
the contraction (
6
) now holds for

\displaystyle\hat{T}=-\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{{\bf x}}^{2}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla_{i}^{2}\qquad{\rm and}\qquad\hat{V}=\frac{1}{2}{\bf x}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=}^{n}{\bf r}_{i}^{2}

(8)

where the
n
-particle free propagator is given by

\displaystyle g_{0}({\bf x}^{\prime},{\bf x},{\epsilon})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle{\bf x}^{\prime}|{\rm e}^{-\epsilon\hat{T}}|{\bf x}\rangle

(9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi{\epsilon})^{nD/2}}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2{\epsilon}}({\bf x}^{\prime}-{\bf x})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{(2\pi{\epsilon})^{D/2}}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2{\epsilon}}({\bf r}_{i}^{\prime}-{\bf r}_{i})^{2}\right].

For
n
fermions, the anti-symmetric free propagator
{\rm e}_{\cal A}^{-{\epsilon}\hat{T}}
in
D
dimension is given by
the determinant

\displaystyle G_{0}({\bf x}^{\prime},{\bf x},{\epsilon})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle{\bf x}^{\prime}|{\rm e}_{\cal A}^{-{\epsilon}\hat{T}}|{\bf x}\rangle

(10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n!}{\rm det}\left(\frac{1}{(2\pi{\epsilon})^{D/2}}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2{\epsilon}}({\bf r}_{i}^{\prime}-{\bf r}_{j})^{2}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{p}(-1)^{P}g_{0}({\bf x}^{\prime}_{p},{\bf x},{\epsilon})

(11)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{p}(-1)^{P}g_{0}({\bf x}^{\prime},{\bf x}_{p},{\epsilon})

(12)

where

{\bf x}_{p}
is a particle permutation of
{\bf x}
with
P=0
for even permutations and
P=1
for
odd permutations. From from (
10
),
G_{0}({\bf x}^{\prime},{\bf x},{\epsilon})
is antisymmetric
in
{\bf x}^{\prime}
and in
{\bf x}
.

The contraction of two free fermion propagators gives

\displaystyle\langle{\bf x}^{\prime}|{\rm e}_{\cal A}^{-a\hat{T}}{\rm e}^{-b\hat{V}}{\rm e}_{\cal A}^{-c\hat{T}}|{\bf x}^{*}\rangle=\int d{\bf x}\,G_{0}({\bf x}^{\prime},{\bf x},a){\rm e}^{-b\frac{1}{2}{\bf x}^{2}}G_{0}({\bf x},{\bf x}^{*},c)

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{(n!)^{2}}\sum_{p,q}(-1)^{P+Q}\int d{\bf x}\,g_{0}({\bf x}^{\prime}_{p},{\bf x},a){\rm e}^{-b\frac{1}{2}{\bf x}^{2}}g_{0}({\bf x},{\bf x}^{*}_{q},c)

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{(n!)^{2}}\sum_{p,q}(-1)^{P+Q}\langle{\bf x}^{\prime}|{\rm e}^{-a\hat{T}}{\rm e}^{-b\hat{V}}{\rm e}^{-c\hat{T}}|{\bf x}^{*}\rangle.

(13)

Since the contraction holds for the
n
-particle free propagator, the above is just

\displaystyle\langle{\bf x}^{\prime}|{\rm e}_{\cal A}^{-a\hat{T}}{\rm e}^{-b\hat{V}}{\rm e}_{\cal A}^{-c\hat{T}}|{\bf x}^{*}\rangle

\displaystyle={\rm e}^{-\nu\frac{1}{2}{\bf x}^{\prime 2}-\mu\frac{1}{2}{{\bf x}^{*}}^{2}}\frac{1}{(n!)^{2}}\sum_{p,q}(-1)^{P+Q}g_{0}({\bf x}^{\prime}_{p},{\bf x}^{*}_{q},\kappa)

(14)

\displaystyle={\rm e}^{-\nu\frac{1}{2}{\bf x}^{\prime 2}-\mu\frac{1}{2}{{\bf x}^{*}}^{2}}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{p}(-1)^{P}G_{0}({\bf x}^{\prime}_{p},{\bf x}^{*},\kappa).

From (
10
), any row permutation changes the sign according to

G_{0}({\bf x}^{\prime}_{p},{\bf x},{\epsilon})=(-1)^{P}G_{0}({\bf x}^{\prime},{\bf x},{\epsilon}).

(15)

Hence

\displaystyle\langle{\bf x}^{\prime}|{\rm e}_{\cal A}^{-a\hat{T}}{\rm e}^{-b\hat{V}}{\rm e}_{\cal A}^{-c\hat{T}}|{\bf x}^{*}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{-\nu\frac{1}{2}{\bf x}^{\prime 2}-\mu\frac{1}{2}{{\bf x}^{*}}^{2}}G_{0}({\bf x}^{\prime},{\bf x}^{*},\kappa)

(16)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle{\bf x}^{\prime}|{\rm e}^{-\nu\hat{V}}{\rm e}_{\cal A}^{-\kappa\hat{T}}{\rm e}^{-\mu\hat{V}}|{\bf x}^{*}\rangle.

Thus we have shown that the operator contraction identity applies equally to free propagators
of any number of harmonic fermions in any dimension.
This immediately implies that:

a)

Since the sign of
G_{0}({\bf x}^{\prime},{\bf x}^{*},\kappa)
depends only on permutations of particle positions
and
not
on
\kappa
,
and contractions only change
\kappa
,
the nodal structure of the free fermion propagator is preserved under all contractions.
Hence, the discrete path integral for non-interacting harmonic fermions at any imaginary time-step
has the
same
nodal structure as the free fermion propagator.

b)

For fermions confined by a general potential
V({\bf x})
, one can approximate

{\rm e}^{-bV({\bf x})}=\sum_{k}c_{k}{\rm e}^{-b_{k}{\bf x}^{2}}

(17)

by a sum of Gaussians. The contraction then spawns multiple determinant propagators via

\int d{\bf x}\,G_{0}({\bf x}^{\prime},{\bf x},a){\rm e}^{-bV({\bf x})}G_{0}({\bf x},{\bf x}^{*},c)=\sum_{k}c_{k}{\rm e}^{-\nu_{k}\frac{1}{2}{\bf x}^{\prime 2}}G_{0}({\bf x}^{\prime},{\bf x}^{*},\kappa_{k}){\rm e}^{-\mu_{k}\frac{1}{2}{{\bf x}^{*}}^{2}},

(18)

and changes the nodal structure of the discrete path integral away from that of the free propagator.

c)

This contraction holds true for the determinant free fermion propagator (
10
)
but is not true when one only samples permutations randomly in (
11
) or (
12
).
Therefore, doing fermion PIMC via
permutation sampling

;
lyu05
will not precisely reproduce
analytical results presented in this work.

Point a) explains why non-interacting harmonic fermions are as simple as free fermions. Point b) explains how generally
interacting fermions are complicated by multiple determinants.
Point c) explains that the use of the determinant propagator, while computationally more intensive,
is superior to permutation sampling. This last point is related to an old finding by Lyubartsev
lyu05
,
that while there is no sign problem for 1D fermions when using the anti-symmetric propagator, there remains a low-level sign problem if one only does permutation sampling
chin24
.
This work only uses the above anti-symmetric determinant free fermion propagator as originally
employed by Takahashi and Imada
tak84a
and subsequent authors
lyu05
;
chin15
;
dor15
;
chin24
.

For

VI
The fermion PIMC sign problem

Because the operator contraction is exact, there is no sign problem in the analytical calculations in the last section. All energies are just rational functions of
\tau
. In PIMC simulations, the partition function at
\tau=N\epsilon

is a discrete path integral of
N
short-time determinant propagators
G_{1}
given by (
21
):

Z=\int d{\bf x}_{1}\cdots d{\bf x}_{N}\,G_{1}({\bf x}_{1},{\bf x}_{2};{\epsilon})G_{1}({\bf x}_{2},{\bf x}_{3};{\epsilon})\cdots G_{1}({\bf x}_{N},{\bf x}_{1};{\epsilon}).

(68)

Since the product of determinants is not necessary positive, one decomposes the integrand into its overall sign and
absolute value,

\displaystyle G_{1}({\bf x}_{1},{\bf x}_{2};{\epsilon})\cdots G_{1}({\bf x}_{N},{\bf x}_{1};{\epsilon})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm sgn}\,|G_{1}({\bf x}_{1},{\bf x}_{2};{\epsilon})\cdots G_{1}({\bf x}_{N},{\bf x}_{1};{\epsilon})|

(69)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm sgn}\,P({\bf x}_{1},\cdots{\bf x}_{N}).

Any observable
{\cal O}({\bf x})
can then be computed as

\displaystyle\langle{\cal O}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\int d{\bf x}_{1}\cdots d{\bf x}_{N}\bar{\cal O}\,{\rm sgn}\,P({\bf x}_{1},\cdots{\bf x}_{N})}{\int d{\bf x}_{1}\cdots d{\bf x}_{N}{\rm sgn}\,P({\bf x}_{1},\cdots{\bf x}_{N})}

(70)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\int d{\bf x}_{1}\cdots d{\bf x}_{N}\bar{\cal O}\,{\rm sgn}\,P({\bf x}_{1},\cdots{\bf x}_{N})/\int d{\bf x}_{1}\cdots d{\bf x}_{N}P({\bf x}_{1},\cdots{\bf x}_{N})}{\int d{\bf x}_{1}\cdots d{\bf x}_{N}{\rm sgn}\,P({\bf x}_{1},\cdots{\bf x}_{N})/\int d{\bf x}_{1}\cdots d{\bf x}_{N}P({\bf x}_{1},\cdots{\bf x}_{N})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\langle\bar{\cal O}\,{\rm sgn}\rangle_{P}}{\langle{\rm sgn}\rangle_{P}},

where
\bar{\cal O}
is the operator averaged over each short-time propagator (the bead-average)

\bar{\cal O}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\cal O}({\bf x}_{i}).

(71)

Both the numerator and denominator of (
70
) can be computed by the Monte Carlo
method because
P({\bf x}_{1},\cdots{\bf x}_{N})
is non-negative. The sign problem arises only when
the average sign
s=\langle{\rm sgn}\rangle_{P}\rightarrow 0
such that (
70
)
can no longer be evaluated with stability.

VII
Why no sign problem for closed-shell states?

For harmonic fermions in more than one dimension, because of the energy level degeneracy, there are
closed-shell states when fermions completely filled up to the highest degenerate level. In one dimension,
because there is no degeneracy, every occupied state is a closed-shell state.
The surprising reason why there is no sign problem in closed-shell states,
is the same reason why there is no sign problem for fermions in one dimension
chin24
;
they are all closed-shell states.
The only difference is that in one dimension, there is no sign problem at all
\tau
.
In more than one dimension, that is true only at large
\tau
.

Let’s explain this with increasing dimension.
From (
10
), the two-fermion free propagator in 2D is given by

\displaystyle G_{0}({\bf r}_{1}^{\prime},{\bf r}_{2}^{\prime},{\bf r}_{1},{\bf r}_{2};{\epsilon})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{(2\pi{\epsilon})^{2}}\det\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\rm e}^{-({\bf r}^{\prime}_{1}-{\bf r}_{1})^{2}/(2{\epsilon})}&{\rm e}^{-({\bf r}^{\prime}_{1}-{\bf r}_{2})^{2}/(2{\epsilon})}\\
{\rm e}^{-({\bf r}^{\prime}_{2}-{\bf r}_{1})^{2}/(2{\epsilon})}&{\rm e}^{-({\bf r}^{\prime}_{2}-{\bf r}_{2})^{2}/(2{\epsilon})}\end{array}\right),

(75)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{(2\pi{\epsilon})^{2}}{\rm e}^{-\frac{1}{2{\epsilon}}\left[({\bf r}_{1}^{\prime}-{\bf r}_{1})^{2}+({\bf r}_{2}^{\prime}-{\bf r}_{2})^{2}\right]}\left(1-{\rm e}^{-\frac{1}{{\epsilon}}{\bf r}_{21}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf r}_{21}}\right),

where
{\bf r}_{21}^{\prime}={\bf r}_{2}^{\prime}-{\bf r}_{1}^{\prime}
and
{\bf r}_{21}={\bf r}_{2}-{\bf r}_{1}
. Its sign is
given by the sign of the round bracket above,
which in turn is
given by the sign of
{\bf r}_{21}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf r}_{21}
. Therefore one has

{\rm sgn}\Bigl(G_{0}({\bf r}_{1}^{\prime},{\bf r}_{2}^{\prime},{\bf r}_{1},{\bf r}_{2};{\epsilon})\Bigr)={\rm sgn}({\bf r}_{21}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf r}_{21}).

(76)

For a closed loop of three propagators,

\displaystyle{\rm sgn}\Bigl(G_{0}({\bf r}_{21},{\bf r}_{21}^{\prime})G_{0}({\bf r}_{21}^{\prime},{\bf r}_{21}^{\prime\prime})G_{0}({\bf r}_{21}^{\prime\prime},{\bf r}_{21})\Bigr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm sgn}\Bigl(({\bf r}_{21}\cdot{\bf r}_{21}^{\prime})({\bf r}_{21}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf r}_{21}^{\prime\prime})({\bf r}_{21}^{\prime\prime}\cdot{\bf r}_{21})\Bigr),

(77)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|{\bf r}_{21}|^{2}|{\bf r}_{21}^{\prime}|^{2}|{\bf r}_{21}^{\prime\prime}|^{2}{\rm sgn}\Bigl(\cos\theta\cos\theta^{\prime}\cos\theta^{\prime\prime}\Bigr),

one has the sign problem because the cosine functions resulting from the dot products can have both signs.
However, at one dimension, there is no dot product, no cosine functions,

\displaystyle{\rm sgn}\Bigl(G_{0}(x_{21},x_{21}^{\prime})G_{0}(x_{21}^{\prime},x_{21}^{\prime\prime})G_{0}(x_{21}^{\prime\prime},x_{21})\Bigr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm sgn}\Bigl((x_{21}x_{21}^{\prime})(x_{21}^{\prime}x_{21}^{\prime\prime})(x_{21}^{\prime\prime}x_{21})\Bigr),

(78)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm sgn}\Bigl((x_{21})^{2}(x_{21}^{\prime})^{2}(x_{21}^{\prime\prime})^{2}\Bigr)\geq 0,

and therefore no sign problem. Note that
x_{ij}
can be viewed as a
signed-distance
.
In general then, the sign of a loop of
N
two-fermion propagators is just the sign of a product of

N signed-distance squared
, which is non-negative.

To put it differently, a loop of
N
two-fermion propagators in any dimension other than one
has a sign problem because the two-fermion state is never a closed-shell state except in
one dimension.

Consider now the three-fermion free propagator from (
10
) with
{\bf s}_{i}\equiv{\bf r}^{\prime}_{i}
,

\displaystyle G_{0}({\bf s}_{i},{\bf r}_{i};\epsilon)

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{-({\bf s}^{2}_{1}+{\bf s}^{2}_{2}+{\bf s}^{2}_{3}+{\bf r}^{2}_{1}+{\bf r}^{2}_{2}+{\bf r}^{2}_{3})/(2\epsilon)}\det\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\rm e}^{{\bf s}_{1}\cdot{\bf r}_{1}/\epsilon}&{\rm e}^{{\bf s}_{1}\cdot{\bf r}_{2}/\epsilon}&{\rm e}^{{\bf s}_{1}\cdot{\bf r}_{3}/\epsilon}\\
{\rm e}^{{\bf s}_{2}\cdot{\bf r}_{1}/\epsilon}&{\rm e}^{{\bf s}_{2}\cdot{\bf r}_{2}/\epsilon}&{\rm e}^{{\bf s}_{2}\cdot{\bf r}_{3}/\epsilon}\\
{\rm e}^{{\bf s}_{3}\cdot{\bf r}_{1}/\epsilon}&{\rm e}^{{\bf s}_{3}\cdot{\bf r}_{2}/\epsilon}&{\rm e}^{{\bf s}_{3}\cdot{\bf r}_{3}/\epsilon}\end{array}\right),

its sign is given by the determinant above.
Multiply its first row by
{\rm e}^{({\bf s}_{2}-{\bf s}_{1})\cdot{\bf r}_{1}/\epsilon}\equiv{\rm e}^{{\bf s}_{21}\cdot{\bf r}_{1}/\epsilon}
and
{\rm e}^{{\bf s}_{31}\cdot{\bf r}_{1}/\epsilon}

and subtract them from the second and third row respectively gives

\displaystyle\det\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\rm e}^{{\bf s}_{1}\cdot{\bf r}_{1}/\epsilon}&{\rm e}^{{\bf s}_{1}\cdot{\bf r}_{2}/\epsilon}&{\rm e}^{{\bf s}_{1}\cdot{\bf r}_{3}/\epsilon}\\
0&{\rm e}^{{\bf s}_{2}\cdot{\bf r}_{2}/\epsilon}-{\rm e}^{{\bf s}_{1}\cdot{\bf r}_{2}/\epsilon+{\bf s}_{21}\cdot{\bf r}_{1}/\epsilon}&{\rm e}^{{\bf s}_{2}\cdot{\bf r}_{3}/\epsilon}-{\rm e}^{{\bf s}_{1}\cdot{\bf r}_{3}/\epsilon+{\bf s}_{21}\cdot{\bf r}_{1}/\epsilon}\\
0&{\rm e}^{{\bf s}_{3}\cdot{\bf r}_{2}/\epsilon}-{\rm e}^{{\bf s}_{1}\cdot{\bf r}_{2}/\epsilon+{\bf s}_{31}\cdot{\bf r}_{1}/\epsilon}&{\rm e}^{{\bf s}_{3}\cdot{\bf r}_{3}/\epsilon}-{\rm e}^{{\bf s}_{1}\cdot{\bf r}_{3}/\epsilon+{\bf s}_{31}\cdot{\bf r}_{1}/\epsilon}\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{{\bf s}_{1}\cdot{\bf r}_{1}/\epsilon}\det\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\rm e}^{{\bf s}_{2}\cdot{\bf r}_{2}/\epsilon}(1-{\rm e}^{-{\bf s}_{21}\cdot{\bf r}_{21}/\epsilon})&{\rm e}^{{\bf s}_{2}\cdot{\bf r}_{3}/\epsilon}(1-{\rm e}^{-{\bf s}_{21}\cdot{\bf r}_{31}/\epsilon})\\
{\rm e}^{{\bf s}_{3}\cdot{\bf r}_{2}/\epsilon}(1-{\rm e}^{-{\bf s}_{31}\cdot{\bf r}_{21}/\epsilon})&{\rm e}^{{\bf s}_{3}\cdot{\bf r}_{3}/\epsilon}(1-{\rm e}^{-{\bf s}_{31}\cdot{\bf r}_{31}/\epsilon}).\end{array}\right).

In the limit of
\epsilon\rightarrow\infty
, to leading order in
1/\epsilon
, the sign of the above determinant
is given by the expansion of each matrix element’s bracket,

\displaystyle\det\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}({\bf s}_{21}\cdot{\bf r}_{21})&({\bf s}_{21}\cdot{\bf r}_{31})\\
({\bf s}_{31}\cdot{\bf r}_{21})&({\bf s}_{31}\cdot{\bf r}_{31})\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle({\bf s}_{21}\cdot{\bf r}_{21})({\bf s}_{31}\cdot{\bf r}_{31})-({\bf s}_{31}\cdot{\bf r}_{21})({\bf s}_{21}\cdot{\bf r}_{31}),

(93)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle({\bf s}_{21}\times{\bf s}_{31})\cdot({\bf r}_{21}\times{\bf r}_{31}).

In more than 2D, the dot product in (
93
) will result in a cosine function, yielding the sign problems for three and more three-fermion propagators.
However, in 2D, the cross-product

({\bf s}_{21}\times{\bf s}_{31})\cdot({\bf r}_{21}\times{\bf r}_{31})\rightarrow({\bf s}_{21}\times{\bf s}_{31})_{z}({\bf r}_{21}\times{\bf r}_{31})_{z}

(94)

is only a product of
z
-component with no cosine function and therefore no sign problem.
Note that the cross-product in 2D is a
signed-area
.
Therefore, the sign of a loop of
N
three-fermion propagators in the large
\tau
limit
is just the sign of a product of
N signed-area squared
, which is also non-negative.

The generalization of the determinant in (
VII
) to four-fermion is

\displaystyle\det\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}({\bf s}_{21}\cdot{\bf r}_{21})&({\bf s}_{21}\cdot{\bf r}_{31})&({\bf s}_{21}\cdot{\bf r}_{41})\\
({\bf s}_{31}\cdot{\bf r}_{21})&({\bf s}_{31}\cdot{\bf r}_{31})&({\bf s}_{31}\cdot{\bf r}_{41})\\
({\bf s}_{41}\cdot{\bf r}_{21})&({\bf s}_{41}\cdot{\bf r}_{31})&({\bf s}_{41}\cdot{\bf r}_{41})\end{array}\right).

Since
{\bf r}_{i}
are arbitrary, setting

{\bf r}_{21}=(a_{x},0,0)
,
{\bf r}_{31}=(0,b_{y},0)
and
{\bf r}_{41}=(0,0,c_{z})
gives

\displaystyle\det\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}({\bf s}_{21})_{x}&({\bf s}_{21})_{y}&({\bf s}_{21})_{z}\\
({\bf s}_{31})_{x}&({\bf s}_{31})_{y}&({\bf s}_{31})_{z}\\
({\bf s}_{41})_{x}&({\bf s}_{41})_{y}&({\bf s}_{41})_{z}\end{array}\right)(a_{x}b_{y}c_{z})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bf s}_{21}\cdot({\bf s}_{31}\times{\bf s}_{41})(a_{x}b_{y}c_{z}),

(103)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[{\bf s}_{21}\cdot({\bf s}_{31}\times{\bf s}_{41})][{\bf r}_{21}\cdot({\bf r}_{31}\times{\bf r}_{41})],

because
(a_{x}b_{y}c_{z})
is the triple product
{\bf r}_{21}\cdot({\bf r}_{31}\times{\bf r}_{41})

and the propagator must be symmetric with respect to
{\bf s}_{i}
and
{\bf r}_{i}
. In 3D,
the triple-product is a
signed-volume
. Therefore
the sign of a loop of
N
four-fermion propagators in the large
\tau
limit
is the sign of a product of
N signed-volume squared
, which is again non-negative.
(In 2D, the triple-product vanishes,
and the sign of the four-fermion propagator is then determined by the next order term in
the expansion of
(1/\epsilon)
.)

For
D+1
fermions in a
D
-dimension harmonic oscillator,
the construction (
103
) generalizes to a product of signed volumes
formed by
D
non-degenerate vectors, whose squares are non-negative.

For larger closed-shell states, such as
n=6
in 2D, or
n=10
in 3D, the sign of the propagator at large
\tau

must be similarly given by some signed hyper-volumes. Unfortunately the method used here is too crude to
make out the shape of these volumes.

VIII
The sign problem with interaction

To see how interactions alter the sign problem, consider first the case of pairwise harmonic interaction.
The use of the exact
continuum
path integral to study fermions with pairwise
harmonic potential has been studied extensively by
Brosens, Devreese and Lemmens
bro97
;
bro98
. This work focuses instead, on understanding
the sign problem at each
discrete
time step of PIMC. For completeness, we give below a
succinct summary of the needed results.

The Fortran code for this work is written for a
n
-particle Hamiltonian
with a general pairwise interaction
\lambda|{\bf r}_{i}-{\bf r}_{j}|^{k}
, so that by simply setting
k=2,-1,-3
, the same PIMC code would generate results for the harmonic, Coulomb and dipolar interaction respectively. This means that, once the harmonic force case agrees with analytical results derived below, one can then be assured that the code is working properly for the other interactions.

For the harmonic pairwise interaction, one can write the Hamiltonian as

H=T+V=\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf p}_{i}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{{\bf r}_{i}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}({\bf r}_{i}-{\bf r}_{j})^{2}

(104)

with
m=1
.
Let
{\bf R}
be the center-of-mass (CM) vector and
{\bf u}_{i}
the position relative to
{\bf R}
:

{\bf R}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf r}_{i},\qquad\quad{\bf u}_{i}={\bf r}_{i}-{\bf R}.

(105)

It then follows that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf u}_{i}=0
and

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}({\bf R}+{\bf u}_{i})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}({\bf u}_{i}-{\bf u}_{j})^{2}

(106)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\sqrt{n}{\bf R})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(1+2n\lambda)\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf u}_{i}^{2}.

Similarly, the CM momentum is (
M=n
)

{\bf P}=M\dot{\bf R}=M\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dot{\bf r}_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf p}_{i},

(107)

and the relative to CM momentum
{\bf s}_{i}

\displaystyle{\bf s}_{i}=m\dot{\bf u}_{i}=m\dot{\bf r}_{i}-m\dot{\bf R}={\bf p}_{i}-\frac{1}{n}{\bf P}

(108)

also satisfies

\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf s}_{i}=0,

(109)

giving the kinetic energy as

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\frac{1}{n}{\bf P}+{\bf s}_{i})^{2}=\frac{1}{2n}{\bf P}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf s}_{i}^{2}.

(110)

The total Hamiltonian is then

H=\frac{1}{2n}{\bf P}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}n{\bf R}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf s}_{i}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(1+2n\lambda)\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf u}_{i}^{2}.

(111)

Comparing the center-of-mass Hamiltonian to

\frac{1}{2M}{\bf P}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}M\Omega^{2}{\bf R}^{2},

one sees that
M=n
and
\Omega=1
. Therefore the
center-of-mass energy is
\Omega=1
and the relative energy

\omega=\sqrt{1+2n\lambda}

(112)

is
n
-dependent
. For attractive interactions,
\lambda>0
and
\omega>1
.
For repulsive interactions,
-1/(2n)<\lambda<0
and
0<\omega<1
,
which means that the pairwise repulsion cannot be too strong, otherwise
the repulsion will blow up the harmonic confinement. For the ease of knowing the
exact ground state energy, we will generally fix
\omega
for all
n
and set
\lambda=(\omega^{2}-1)/(2n)
.

For the case where each component potential is generalized to

V=\frac{1}{2}x^{2}\rightarrow\frac{1}{2}\omega^{2}x^{2}

(113)

it is only necessary to replace

XI
Conclusions and future directions

In this work we have formulated a completely solvable PIMC model of harmonic fermions
with and without pairwise harmonic interactions where the Hamiltonian energy
and the sign problem can be studied in details as functions of bead and fermion numbers.
We showed that strong pairwise repulsion reduces/enhances the sign problem at short/long imaginary time.
We explained the connection between the absence of the sign problem in one dimension and
in closed-shell states. We showed that fourth-order short-time propagators are
effective in determining ground state energies of less than 30 fermions with pairwise
harmonic interactions or for sufficiently repulsive Coulomb forces. For spin-balance, unpolarized
quantum dots with 30 or more electrons, the pairwise gradient potential is too singular
for fourth-order methods to be used. However, this work found a new class of
Variable-Bead algorithms which yielded ground state energies that are only 0.5% or less
above modern neural network results for up to 90 electrons. This was completely unexpected.
The efficiency of this class of algorithms should be further tested on other quantum systems.

Path integral Monte Carlo is the granddaddy of modern neural network in that it is the only
traditional quantum Monte Carlo method with build-in “hidden-layers”. When PA is used, these layers
are completely known and fixed. The Variable-Bead propagators have hidden-layer parameters
that can be fine-tuned, making them similar to neural networks. However, these parameters
do not alter the fundamental Gaussian nature of PIMC, and therefore cannot overcome the fundamental
challenge of dealing singular potentials. Propagators such as (
127
) and (
128
)
are actually ways of approximating the
square
of the wave function.
Perhaps one can learn from neural networks and replaces
the free propagator’s Gaussian determinant by a more general type of determinant,
similar to the way Ref.
pfa20
’s neural network is parametrizing the exact wave function.

On the other hand, the VB2 and VB3 algorithms used here are much, much simpler than any fermion neural
network
nor23
;
pfa20
, suggesting that neural networks may also benefit from knowing
the structure of PIMC in their search for the ground state wave function.

Appendix C
Fourth-order algorithms

Positive time-step fourth-order short-time propagators with arbitrary number of

T
operators, or beads, have been derived in Ref.
chin06
. For completeness, we will briefly
summarize their operator form as done in Ref.
chin15
.
Consider a short-time propagator with
(N-1)
beads of the form

\displaystyle{\cal T}_{(N-1)B}^{(4)}(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{v_{1}\epsilon V}{\rm e}^{t_{2}\epsilon T}{\rm e}^{v_{2}\epsilon V}\cdots{\rm e}^{t_{N}\epsilon T}{\rm e}^{v_{N}\epsilon V}

(139)

with left-right symmetric coefficients
v_{1}=v_{N}
,
t_{2}=t_{N}
, etc..
This operator will be fourth-order if one chooses
\{t_{i}\}>0
satisfying

\sum_{i=1}^{N}t_{i}=1
, and
\{v_{i}\}
given by

v_{1}=v_{N}=\frac{1}{2}+\lambda_{2}(1-t_{2}),\quad v_{i}=-\lambda_{2}(t_{i}+t_{i+1}),

(140)

where

\lambda_{2}=-\phi^{-1}/2
,
\ \phi=1-\sum_{i=1}^{N}t_{i}^{3}
, and the gradient coefficient
u_{0}=(1/\phi-1)/24
with
the gradient potential term
u_{0}\epsilon^{3}[V,[T,V]]
distributed
left-right symmetrically among all the
v_{i}\epsilon V
terms in (
139
).
In order not to complicate the evaluation of Hamiltonian energy,
the gradient potential term must
not
be distributed to the
v_{1}
and
v_{N}

potential terms.

To illustrate the above equations, consider the case of a 3-bead propagator with
N=4
.
One then must have
t_{2}+t_{3}+t_{4}=2t_{2}+t_{3}=1
and therefore
t_{3}=1-2t_{2}
, with
t_{2}
a free parameter
in the range
0\leq t_{2}\leq 1/2
.
One can then compute that
\phi=6t_{2}(1-t_{2})^{2}
and
u_{0}=(1/(6t_{2}(1-t_{2})^{2})-1)/24
,
reproducing the
{\cal T}_{BDA}
propagator in Ref.
chin23a
with minor changes in notation.
The Hamiltonian energy can then be optimized by varying
t_{2}
to give
the Best 3-Bead algorithm BB3. Its effectiveness in solving for the non-interacting fermion
energies is shown in Fig.
3
.

Similarly, one can derive the Best 4-Bead algorithm BB4 with the additional freedom of distributing the gradient term between the central
v_{3}V
and the two adjacent
v_{2}V
potential terms. For a BB5 algorithm, one has free parameters
t_{2}
,
t_{3}
and the freedom to distribute the gradient term to the two inner or the two further out potential terms.

If the universal energy
E_{n}(w)
is known, then any algorithm of the form (
139
), with given parameter values, can be contracted
numerically
down to
\kappa_{1}
and
\mu_{1}
to determine
\gamma(\epsilon)
and therefore the Hamiltonian energy via (
64
), (
120
) or (
123
).

To compute the average sign
s=\langle sgn\rangle

(
77

\tau

s_{3}=\frac{\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}d\phi_{1}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}d\phi_{2}\cos(\phi_{1})\cos(\phi_{2})\cos(\phi_{1}+\phi_{2})}{\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}d\phi_{1}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}d\phi_{2}|\cos(\phi_{1})\cos(\phi_{2})\cos(\phi_{1}+\phi_{2})|}=\frac{I}{J}

which by symmetry

(142)

The integrand of
I
has two negative triangular regions bounded by
\phi_{1}=\pi/2
,
\phi_{2}=\pi/2
,
\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}=\pi/2
,
and
\phi_{1}=\pi/2
,
\phi_{2}=\pi/2
,
\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}=3\pi/2

(144)

N

(145)

the integral can be done numerically, either by quadrature (for small

,
