Strongly Polynomial Parallel Work-Depth Tradeoffs for Directed SSSP
Adam Karczmarz, Wojciech Nadara, Marek Sokołowski

Abstract
In this paper, we show new strongly polynomial work-depth tradeoffs for computing single-source shortest paths (SSSP) in non-negatively weighted directed graphs in parallel. Most importantly, we prove that directed SSSP can be solved within O ~ ​ ( m + n 2 − ϵ ) \widetilde{O}(m+n^{2-\epsilon}) work and O ~ ​ ( n 1 − ϵ ) \widetilde{O}(n^{1-\epsilon}) depth for some positive ϵ > 0 \epsilon>0 . In particular, for dense graphs with non-negative real weights, we provide the first nearly work-efficient strongly polynomial algorithm with sublinear depth. Our result immediately yields improved strongly polynomial parallel algorithms for min-cost flow and the assignment problem. It also leads to the first non-trivial strongly polynomial dynamic algorithm for minimum mean cycle. Moreover, we develop efficient parallel algorithms in the Word RAM model for several variants of SSSP in graphs with exponentially large edge weights.

In this paper, our focus is on strongly polynomial

[

The work of a parallel algorithm is its sequential running time, whereas the depth is the longest chain of sequential dependencies between the performed operations.
In this paper, when quantifying work and depth, we always stick to the

t\in[1,n]

Strongly polynomial parallel SSSP.

Parallel SSSP in the strongly polynomial regime appears much less understood.
A simplified version of Spencer‘s SSSP tradeoff
[

]

t\in[1,n]

Note that this constitutes a natural interpolation between parallel Dijkstra and repeated-squaring for dense graphs.
For sparse graphs,

\widetilde{O}(mn+t^{3})

Strikingly, for directed SSSP with non-negative weights, none of the known strongly polynomial parallel algorithms for directed graphs with non-trivial, truly sublinear depth achieve subquadratic work.
In particular, none attain sub-Bellman–Ford

m\in[n,n^{2}]

any

m\approx n^{2}

It is thus natural to ask:

Is there a strongly polynomial SSSP algorithm for non-negatively weighted directed graphs with

\widetilde{O}(m+n^{2-\epsilon})

\widetilde{O}(n^{1-\epsilon})
depth, for some
\epsilon>0

Note that in the

[

Spe97
]

for more than 30 years.
Interestingly, the posed question has also been known to have an affirmative answer for the special case of
undirected

[

]

Both Spencer

]
and Shi and Spencer
[

noted that their methods fail to produce non-trivial bounds
for directed graphs in the strongly polynomial regime.
They explicitly asked for strongly polynomial parallel SSSP algorithms for digraphs
with comparable bounds.

It is worth noting that, in the strongly polynomial regime, directed SSSP computation (with non-negative weights) is the bottleneck
of many state-of-the-art optimization algorithms, e.g.,
[
EK72

HJQ25

Therefore, improving the parallel SSSP bounds can immediately lead to improved parallel algorithms for those.

Applications

Strongly polynomial min-cost flow.

Theorem

1.1
has immediate applications in strongly polynomial min-cost flow computation.
The state-of-the-art (sequential) algorithm
[
Orl93
]
(see also a variant in
[
AMO93
, Section 10.7]
) in this regime runs in
\widetilde{O}(m^{2})
time.
Viewed as a parallel algorithm, it runs within
\widetilde{O}(m\cdot W_{\mathrm{SSSP}}(n,m))
work and
\widetilde{O}(m\cdot D_{\mathrm{SSSP}}(n,m))
depth (see
[
Orl93
, p. 339]
), given a strongly polynomial (non-negative) SSSP algorithm running within
W_{\mathrm{SSSP}}(n,m)
work and
D_{\mathrm{SSSP}}(n,m)
depth.
By using
Theorem

1.1
for SSSP computation therein, we obtain an algorithm with
\widetilde{O}(m^{2}+mn^{9/5}t^{17/5})
work and
\widetilde{O}(mn/t)
depth for
t\in[1,n^{1/17}]
.
For sufficiently dense graphs, this yields an improved depth bound without increasing work (ignoring polylogarithmic factors).

An analogous improvement in depth is obtained for the assignment problem, i.e., minimum-cost (perfect) matching.
The best known strongly polynomial algorithm for the assignment problem is the
successive shortest paths algorithm

(see
[
EK72
]
or
[
AMO93
, Section 12.4]
) that runs in
\widetilde{O}(nm)
time, which amounts to
O(n)
SSSP computations.
Apart from running SSSP
O(n)
times, computing the optimal assignment requires additional
O(n^{2})
work and
\widetilde{O}(n)
depth.
Hence again,
Theorem

1.1
implies a strongly polynomial parallel algorithm for the assignment problem with
\widetilde{O}(mn+n^{14/5}t^{17/5})
work and
\widetilde{O}(n^{2}/t)
depth – improving depth without increasing work for sufficiently dense graphs.

Lexicographical optimization, exponential weights.

We argue that our strongly polynomial parallel SSSP algorithm might
prove superior to the best weakly polynomial algorithms
even in a realistic word RAM model where only arithmetic operations are performed in
O(1)
time.

Suppose
G=(V,E)
is a digraph with edges assigned unique labels from
\{1,\ldots,m\}
.
Let us define the
lex-bottleneck weight
of a path in
G
to be the sequence of weights of edges on the path, sorted non-increasingly.
The
lex-bottleneck shortest path
between two vertices of the graph is the path with the lexicographically minimum lex-bottleneck weight.
That is, it is the path minimizing the maximum weight of an edge, breaking ties by the second-maximum weight, followed by the third-maximum weight etc.
Consider computing single-source lex-bottleneck paths in parallel.

Observe that the lex-bottleneck paths problem can be easily cast as SSSP problem if the edge labeled
j
is assigned an integer weight
2^{j}
.
This way, we can reach out for fast weakly-polynomial parallel SSSP algorithms (such as
[
CF23
,
RHM+23
]
) for integer weights to solve the problem.
However, even ignoring arithmetic cost, their work is
\Omega(m\log{L})
and their depth is
\Omega(\log{L})
, where
L
is the largest weight.

L=\Theta(2^{m})
in our case, so we get
\Omega(m^{2})
work- and
\Omega(m)
depth bounds.

On the other hand, our SSSP algorithm works in the comparison-addition model, so it could run within the same or slightly worse bounds on the (parallel) word RAM if only we could support all the performed operations on edge/path weights in low work and depth.
We indeed show that this is possible when using weights of the form
2^{x}
(where
x\in[0,m]
is an integer), even when these weights are not necessarily distinct.
In such a case, we still achieve
\widetilde{O}(m+n^{2-\epsilon})
work and
\widetilde{O}(n^{1-\epsilon})
depth on the parallel word RAM.
These developments are discussed in
Section

6
.

Dynamic minimum mean cycle and min-ratio cycle.

Finally,
Theorem

1.1
might be useful combined with parametric search
[
Meg83
]
to yield new strongly polynomial bounds in the
sequential
setting as well.
As an example, we show the first non-trivial strongly polynomial
dynamic
algorithm maintaining the minimum mean cycle.
The minimum mean cycle problem is a classical graph problem with applications in, e.g., min-cost-flow computation
[
Tar85
]
and Markov Decision Processes (e.g.,
[
Mad02
]
).
It can be solved in strongly polynomial
O(nm)
time
[
Kar78
]
.

We prove that, using
Theorem

1.1
, we can devise a procedure updating the minimum mean cycle in
\widetilde{O}(mn^{1-1/22})
worst-case time after an edge insertion,
i.e., polynomially faster than recompute-from-scratch. To the best of our knowledge, before our work, no non-trivial dynamic minimum mean cycle algorithms, or even applications of parametric search in the dynamic setting, have been described so far.
In fact, our dynamic algorithm applies even to a more general minimum cost-to-time ratio (min-ratio) cycle problem
[
DBR67
,
Law72
]
,
whose static state-of-the-art strongly polynomial bound is currently
\widetilde{O}(m^{3/4}n^{3/2})

[
BHK17
]
.
See
Section

7
.
We believe that our parallel SSSP algorithm may find more applications in combination with parametric search in the future.

1.2
Technical summary

Our starting point is a variant of the general strategy used by
[
Spe97
]
: to simulate
t
consecutive steps
of Dijkstra‘s algorithm using a variant of low-depth repeated squaring.
Repeated squaring can identify the
t
closest vertices

from each

G
in
\widetilde{O}(m+nt^{2})
work and
\widetilde{O}(1)

[

,
Coh97
]
.
Note that
N_{{t}}({s})
contains precisely the
t
first vertices that Dijkstra‘s algorithm with source

would discover.
To allow reusing this idea for identifying subsequent groups of
t
remaining vertices nearest from the source
s
,
it is enough to ’’contract‘‘
N_{{t}}({s})
into
s
and adjust the graph‘s weights properly.
Unfortunately, the sets
N_{{t}}({u})
computed in one iteration might not be very useful in later iterations if they overlap with
N_{{t}}({s})
significantly.
Thus, the basic strategy (
Section

3
) simply repeats
t
closest vertices computation

\lceil n/t\rceil
times; this requires at least
\Theta(n^{2}t)
work.

Roughly speaking, the main technical development of
[
Spe97
]
is a certain decremental data structure for maintaining
4
4

4

Actually, the
nearby list
data structure of
[
Spe97
]
maintains much more relaxed objects and only supports deletions with a certain structure imposed, but this is strong enough for the studied applications.

N_{{t}}({u})
subject to vertex deletions from
G
.
This way, the sets of closest
t
vertices do not need to be recomputed from scratch when vertices are deemed discovered.
Sadly, the amortized analysis in
[
Spe97
]
is inherently weakly polynomial; it charges costly
t
-nearest vertices recomputations for each
u

to a factor-
2
increase in the radius of a ball containing

.

We use a different approach.
Our main idea is to apply a more depth-costly preprocessing computing static nearest-neighbors-like data (the

) that:

1.

remains useful through
\operatorname{poly}(n)
steps of discovering
t
closest remaining vertices, and

2.

allows locating
N_{{t}}({s})
by running the low-depth algorithm of
[

]

The overall strategy in the preprocessing is to identify a sublinear set of

In a way

In this paper, we deal with non-negatively real-weighted digraphs
G=(V,E)
.
We use

G

W.l.o.g. we assume

We write

By

We also use the standard notation

We define a path

.
If

Paths need not be simple, i.e.,

.
We denote by

2.1
Simplifying assumptions

We make the following additional simplifying assumptions, somewhat similar to those in
[

]
.

Assumption 1
.

For any path

G

.

Assumption 2
.

For any fixed

P_{2}=u\to y

G
, where

, have the same weight.

In

Although ensuring them might require using

-size vector-weights instead of scalar weights, for brevity, we often neglect that and pretend they still come from

.
E.g., we may write

In the following, we also focus only on computing the distances from a single source in
G
, and not the actual paths or an SSSP tree.
This is due to the fact that equipped with single-source distances and
1
(i.e., assuming positive weights), an SSSP tree can be found by picking for each
v
(in parallel) an arbitrary
tight
edge
e=uv
satisfying
\mathrm{dist}(s,u)+w(uv)=\mathrm{dist}(s,v)
.

3
The basic trade-off for directed SSSP

In this section, we present the basic trade-off for non-negative SSSP in directed graphs.
At a high level, this is a simplification of Spencer‘s SSSP algorithm
[
Spe97
]
that one would arrive at after depriving it of the efficient but complex data structures exploiting the bounded-weights assumption.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a weighted digraph with weights in
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and a source vertex
s\in V
.
When computing distances from the source
s
, w.l.o.g. we can assume that
s
has no incoming edges.
Moreover, we can w.l.o.g. assume that
G
has no parallel edges.

Let
t\in[1,n]
be an integer.
For simplicity, assume w.l.o.g. that
n=t\alpha+1
for some integer
\alpha\geq 1
.

The general strategy behind all SSSP algorithms presented in this paper is as follows. Similarly to Dijkstra‘s algorithm, we maintain a set
S\subseteq V
of vertices whose distance from
s
has already been established.
Initially, we set
S=\{s\}
.
While
S\neq V
, we repeatedly perform a
discovery step
identifying the next
t
vertices from
V\setminus S
that are closest to
s
in small depth.
If we perform each such step in
\widetilde{O}(1)
depth, the overall depth will be
\widetilde{O}(\alpha)=\widetilde{O}(n/t)
.

The remainder of this section is devoted to showing a basic implementation of the above strategy.
Let
N_{{t}}({u})\subseteq V
be the set of
t
nearest vertices from
u
wrt.
\mathrm{dist}(u,\cdot)
and distinct from
u
.
If fewer than
t
vertices are reachable from
u
in
G
, then we define
N_{{t}}({u})
to contain all these vertices.
Note that
N_{{t}}({u})
is defined unambiguously by
2
.

To proceed, we need the following folklore generalization of the repeated squaring-based APSP algorithm.
Its variants have been previously used in the context of SSSP in both directed
[
Spe97
]
and undirected graphs
[
Coh97
,
SS99
]
.
The lemma is essentially proved in
[
SS99
, Lemma 1]
.
For completeness, we include its proof sketch in
Appendix

B
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
t\in[1,n]
.
Using
\widetilde{O}(nt^{2}+m)
work and
\widetilde{O}(1)
depth, one can compute for each
u\in V

the set
N_{{t}}({u})
along with the corresponding distances from
u
.

Note that
Lemma

3.1
allows performing the first discovery step of the general strategy, i.e., identifying the
t
closest vertices from
s
in
G
within
\widetilde{O}(nt^{2}+m)
work and
\widetilde{O}(1)
depth.
Afterwards, we have
{S=\{s\}\cup N_{{t}}({s})}
.
Since in the next step we need to identify the
t

next
closest vertices from
s
, i.e., skipping those we have already found, the computed sets
N_{{t}}({u})
might be of no use anymore. For example, it‘s easy to construct an example where all the sets
N_{{t}}({u})
are equal.

Nevertheless, we would still like to reapply
Lemma

3.1
for finding the
t
nearest vertices in
V\setminus S
from
s
in a black-box way by setting up a graph
G^{\prime}
on
V^{\prime}\subseteq V\setminus S\cup\{s\}
where the sought vertices correspond to the
t
nearest vertices from
s
in
G^{\prime}
.
One way to achieve that is to contract
\{s\}\cup N_{{t}}({s})
into the new source vertex
s
, and shift the corresponding weights of edges previously originating in
N_{{t}}({s})
accordingly.
The following definition formalizes the contraction that we use.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
X\subseteq V\setminus\{s\}
. Let
{G_{0}^{\prime}=(V\setminus X,E^{\prime})}
where
E^{\prime}
contains:

•

for each edge
uv\in E
with
u,v\in V\setminus X
, the edge
uv
with the same weight
w(uv)
as in
G
,

•

for each edge
xv\in E
with
x\in X
and
v\in V\setminus X
, the edge
sv
with weight
\mathrm{dist}_{G}(s,x)+w(xv)
.

We say that
G^{\prime}
is obtained by
contracting
X
into
s
, if
G^{\prime}
is obtained from
G_{0}^{\prime}
by discarding parallel edges except those with the smallest weights.

The following lemma shows the key properties of the contracted graph.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
G^{\prime}
be obtained from
G
by contracting
N_{{t}}({s})
into
s
as in
3.2
.
Then:

•

for any
v\in V\setminus N_{{t}}({s})
we have
\mathrm{dist}_{G}(s,v)=\mathrm{dist}_{G^{\prime}}(s,v)
.

•

for all
u,v\in V\setminus N_{{t}}({s})
we have
\mathrm{dist}_{G}(u,v)\leq\mathrm{dist}_{G^{\prime}}(u,v)
.

Proof.

First of all, it is easy to verify that
G^{\prime}
satisfies Assumptions
1
and
2

if these are ensured in
G
as described in
Section

2.1
.

Consider the former item.
For any shortest path
P=s\to v
in
G
, there is a corresponding path
P^{\prime}=s\to v
in
G^{\prime}
of the same weight.
Hence,
\mathrm{dist}_{G^{\prime}}(s,v)\leq\mathrm{dist}_{G}(s,v)
.
On the other hand, by construction, a path
P^{\prime}=s\to v
in
G^{\prime}
does not pass through
s
as an intermediate vertex.
Hence, there is a corresponding path in
G
of weight no larger than
w(P^{\prime})
obtained by mapping the initial edge of
P^{\prime}
to a path in
G
.
As a result,
\mathrm{dist}_{G}(s,v)\leq\mathrm{dist}_{G^{\prime}}(s,v)
.

Now consider the latter item. Let
P^{\prime}
be the shortest
u\to v
path in
G^{\prime}
.

P^{\prime}
does not go through
s
, since
s
has no incoming edges in either
G
or
G^{\prime}
.
Consequently, every edge of
P^{\prime}
is an edge of
G
with the same weight.
Hence,
P^{\prime}\subseteq G
and we obtain
\mathrm{dist}_{G^{\prime}}(u,v)\geq\mathrm{dist}_{G}(u,v)
, as desired.
∎

By
Lemma

3.3
, we can specify the general strategy as follows.
While
V\neq\{s\}
:

1.

compute
N_{{t}}({s})
,

2.

record the distances to these vertices,

3.

contract
N_{{t}}({s})
into
s
.

Above, we assume that in item (c), the original graph
G
is replaced by the contracted graph; in particular,
V
shrinks into
V\setminus N_{{t}}({s})
.
This way, we obtain an SSSP instance of the same kind.
Note that the vertex set is shrunk in every step by
t
vertices, so the number of discovery steps is
O(n/t)
.

Let us now explain how the adjacency lists are maintained under the contractions into
s
in this strategy.
Whenever some
N_{{t}}({s})
is contracted, for all outgoing edges
xy
of
N_{{t}}({s})
in parallel, we add
sy
to the adjacency list of
s
with updated weight unless
y\in\{s\}\cup N_{{t}}({s})
or
s

already had an outgoing edge
sy
.
In the latter case, we might decrease the weight of
sy
if
\mathrm{dist}_{G}(sx)+w(xy)<w(sy)
.
Observe that this way, every edge of the initial
G
is updated at most once in the process.
The contraction can be performed in near-optimal work and
\widetilde{O}(1)
depth if the adjacency lists are stored as parallel dictionaries, e.g., parallel balanced BSTs with edges keyed by their heads.
Therefore, maintaining the adjacency lists of
G
under the performed contractions can be implemented in near-optimal work and depth.
As a result, the time bottleneck of the computation lies in computing the nearest vertices.
If we use
Lemma

3.1
for that, the depth is near-linear in the number of discovery steps, i.e.,
\widetilde{O}(n/t)
, whereas the total work is
\widetilde{O}((n/t)\cdot(nt^{2}+m))=\widetilde{O}(n^{2}t+mn/t)
.

A closer inspection into the algorithm behind
Lemma

3.1
shows that the
\widetilde{O}(m)
term in the work bound in
Lemma

3.1
could be avoided if the input graph was provided with adjacency lists with all but the top-
t
outgoing edges according to the weight filtered out.
Note that we can perform such filtering and maintain it subject to contractions within
\widetilde{O}(m)
total work and
\widetilde{O}(n/t)
total depth if we assume that the adjacency lists are additionally stored as parallel balanced BSTs sorted by edge weights and supporting near-linear work and
\widetilde{O}(1)
-depth batch insertions and deletions (see, e.g.
[
BFS16
,
PVW83
]
).
This observation reduces the required work to
\widetilde{O}(n^{2}t+m)=\widetilde{O}(n^{2}t)
.

We summarize the described basic algorithm with the following theorem.

Theorem 3.4

(based on
[
Spe97
]
)
.

Let
t\in[1,n]
.
Let
G=(V,E)
be a weighted digraph with weights in
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.
Single-source distances in
G
can be computed within
\widetilde{O}(n^{2}t)
work and
\widetilde{O}(n/t)
depth.

4
An improved trade-off for sparse graphs

In this section, we show that the general strategy from
Section

3
can be implemented more efficiently for sparse enough graphs.

Recall that the basic implementation from
Section

3
recomputes, upon each discovery step, the nearest
t
vertices from all
v\in V
on
entire

G
(with previously discovered vertices contracted into
s
) in low depth using
Lemma

3.1
.
Moreover, only the set
N_{{t}}({s})
is used in a single discovery step, even though
Lemma

3.1
yields the sets
N_{{t}}({v})
for all
v\in V
as a by-product; these are disregarded.

At a very high level, we will address the two downsides.
We first propose an additional preprocessing that computes nearest-neighbors-like data that is useful through multiple discovery steps.
Then, we describe a more sophisticated implementation of a discovery step computing
N_{{t}}({s})
.
This algorithm runs the low-depth algorithm of
Lemma

3.1
on a smaller auxiliary graph
H
devised from the preprocessed data, that preserves distances from
s
to its nearby vertices.

In the following, we assume that every vertex
except possibly the source

s
has degree
O(1)
and thus
m=O(n)
.
This assumption can be dropped in a standard way by vertex splitting (see e.g.,
[
DMM+25
, Section 2]
); in general, the reduction increases the number of vertices to
\Theta(m)
, though.

We will also often use the notation
G-X
, for some
X\subseteq V
, to denote the graph
G[V\setminus X]
, i.e.,
G
with
vertices
X
removed.

The algorithm operates in
phases
of
\ell\leq n/t
discovery steps; we will pick
\ell
later.
Every one of the
\lceil\frac{n-1}{\ell\cdot t}\rceil
phases starts with a costly preprocessing that will help perform the next
\ell
steps.

4.1
Preprocessing

The goal of the preprocessing is to compute analogues of
N_{{t}}({v})
that are robust against vertex deletions.
Specifically, the preprocessing delivers:

1.

A subset
Z\subseteq V
of
heavy
vertices (including
s
) that require special treatment.

2.

For each
u\in V
, a
near-list

\mathrm{NL}(u)

containing
u
and at most
t
other vertices from
V
.
Every vertex

stored in
\mathrm{NL}(u)
is accompanied with a

.
Formally,
\mathrm{NL}(u)\subseteq V\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, but we will sometimes
write

if

is stored with some distance in
\mathrm{NL}(u)
.
Note that the distances stored in
\mathrm{NL}(u)
are distinct by

Assume w.l.o.g. that
|V|\geq t+1
.
We call the vertices

Let us now comment on the intuition behind heavy vertices and the obtained near-lists.
Recall that, in the worst case, in the basic strategy, it could happen that the

discovered vertices cover all the sets
N_{{t}}({v})
,
v\in V
, making these sets useless in the following discovery step.
To circumvent this problem, the close vertices are identified gradually while the processing proceeds.
Vertices close to many
v\in V
at once are detected early on and excluded from further consideration,
preventing any particular vertex from appearing in the near-lists too frequently.
Heavy vertices

are precisely the said excluded vertices, whose ’’congestion‘‘ reached some chosen threshold.
As one would expect, excluding the subset

from consideration requires a more subtle definition of what a near-list

is; it is impossible to guarantee that
\mathrm{NL}(u)
still contains

.
Instead, one can ensure that
\mathrm{NL}(u)
contains, roughly speaking, some vertices that are closer in

than the

We now formalize the above intuition. Let
p\in[2,n]
be another integer parameter.
We want the heavy vertices and the near-lists to have together the following properties:

1.

Each vertex
u\in V
appears in
O(p)
near-lists.

2.

Each heavy vertex
z\in Z
appears in
\Theta(p)
near-lists.

3.

If
(v,d)\in\mathrm{NL}(u)
(that is,

is the distance accompanying

in
\mathrm{NL}(u)
), then

equals the weight of some

path in
G
.

4.

Fix

.
If
\mathrm{NL}(u)=\{(v_{0},d_{0}),\ldots,(v_{k},d_{k})\}
, and
0=d_{0}<d_{1}<\ldots<d_{k}
, then:

(a)

If a vertex

is reachable from

in

and

, then

and
{d_{t}<\mathrm{dist}_{G-Z}(u,v)}

(b)

.

The remainder of this section is devoted to proving the following.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let

be an integer and assume all vertices of

except

have degree
O(1)
.
A subset

of heavy vertices of size

, along with a collection of near-lists

\widetilde{O}(nt)
work and
\widetilde{O}(t)
depth.

Initially, we put

and the set

might grow in time.
For each
u\in V

The Dijkstra-like procedure will be truncated after visiting

Initially,
\mathrm{NL}(u)=\{(u,0)\}

However, we require such an edge to connect

The key of

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:queue-key}\min\left\{d_{i}+w(v_{i}y):v_{i}y\in E,v_{i},y\notin Z,0\leq i\leq j\right\}.

(1)

In a single

Once

•

•

4.1.2
Running time

Each of the
n
parallel Dijkstra runs visits at most
t+1
vertices
v
other than

(recall that
{s\in Z}
initially).
Processing a visited vertex

and subsequently reinserting that many vertices with updated keys into the relevant queues.
These updates can be performed in parallel for all outgoing edges using a parallel balanced BST,
and thus processing a vertex costs
\widetilde{O}(\mathrm{outdeg}(v))
work and
\widetilde{O}(1)
depth.
Since out-degrees except
\mathrm{outdeg}(s)
are
O(1)
by our sparsity assumption, both total work and depth per Dijkstra run is
\widetilde{O}(t)
.

Identifying new heavy vertices upon synchronization, and maintaining the queues and their keys to account for that
can be realized in
\widetilde{O}(1)
depth if we store counters of the numbers of appearances of individual vertices

in the near-lists constructed so far.
The additional work needed for that can be charged to the operations performed by the Dijkstra runs, as introducing new heavy vertices can only remove elements that the Dijkstra runs inserted into the relevant parallel trees.

We conclude that the total work used by the preprocessing is
\widetilde{O}(nt)
, and the total depth is
\widetilde{O}(t)
.

4.3
Running time analysis

Let us summarize the total work and depth of the proposed SSSP algorithm that applies the near-list computation every
\ell
discovery steps, and uses that to enable nearest vertices computation on a subgraph of
G
as opposed to the entire graph
G
.

Recall that due to our sparsity assumption, we have
m=O(n)
.
We have already argued in
Section

3
that maintaining the adjacency lists (and even the top-
t

\widetilde{O}(n)
total work and
\widetilde{O}(n/t)
depth.

By
Lemma

\widetilde{O}(nt)

and
\widetilde{O}(t)

This preprocessing happens
O(n/(\ell t))

\widetilde{O}(n^{2}/\ell)
, whereas the required depth is
\widetilde{O}(n/\ell)
.

Finally, given the preprocessing, the total work consumed while performing the

discovery steps is
\widetilde{O}((n/t)\cdot(n/p+\ell p)t^{4})=\widetilde{O}(n(n/p+\ell p)t^{3})
.
All the discovery steps run in near-optimal
\widetilde{O}(1)
depth, so they are performed within
\widetilde{O}(n/t)
depth in total.

To sum up, the presented SSSP algorithm runs within
\widetilde{O}(n(n/p+\ell p)t^{3}+n^{2}/\ell)
work and
{\widetilde{O}(n/t+n/\ell)}
depth under the constant-degree assumption.

Let us now appropriately set the parameters
p
and
\ell
as a function of
n
and the parameter
t
.
The constraints for the parameters are
p\in[2,n]
,
t\in[1,n]
and
\ell\in[1,n/t]
.

First of all, note that the choice
\ell<t
is suboptimal: if this is the case, setting
t\coloneqq\ell
decreases the work
and does not increase the depth asymptotically.

Note that the optimal choice for
p
is such that
n/p=\ell p
, i.e.,
p=\sqrt{n/\ell}
.
In such a case, the work bound becomes
\widetilde{O}(n^{3/2}\ell^{1/2}t^{3}+n^{2}/\ell)
.

Observe that if
t<\ell<n/t
then the optimal choice for
\ell
is
\ell=n^{1/3}/t^{2}
.
Importantly, this is only valid for
t\in[1,n^{1/9})
by
\ell>t
.
With such an
\ell
, we conclude that

should be set to
p=n^{1/3}t\in[1,n]
.
So for
t\in[1,n^{1/9}]
, we can achieve work
\widetilde{O}(n^{5/3}t^{2})
and depth
\widetilde{O}(n/t)
.

If
\ell=n/t
, then the work is
\widetilde{O}(n^{2}t^{5/2})
, so the obtained trade-off is no better than in the basic algorithm described in
Section

3
.

If
\ell=t
, then the work is
\widetilde{O}(n^{3/2}t^{7/2}+n^{2}/t)
, which is
\widetilde{O}(n^{3/2}t^{7/2})
for
t\geq n^{1/9}
.
This bound is tighter than
\widetilde{O}(n^{5/3}t^{2})
only for
t<n^{1/9}
, but allows choosing
t\in[1,n^{1/2}]
, so that
\ell=t\leq n/t
is satisfied.
For such
\ell
, we infer

To sum up, if we aim at depth

, then for

the work bound is
\widetilde{O}(n^{3/2}t^{7/2})
, whereas for

We can combine these cases into a single bound

It is worth noting that this bound is only better than in the basic algorithm (
Section

3
) for
t\leq n^{1/5}
.
Finally, by dropping the

-degree assumption, we obtain the bound
{\widetilde{O}(m^{3/2}t^{7/2}+m^{5/3}t^{2})}

5
Lifting the algorithm to non-sparse graphs

The assumption about
G
having constant degrees was crucial for the analysis of the algorithm presented in
Section

4
.
More specifically, it allowed us to efficiently bound the size of the subgraph which we computed

in (

1.

We could bound

by

since the number of occurrences of a single vertex in near-lists was at most

2.

We had

as the vertices of the graph (except the source) had constant outdegree.

Our ultimate aim is to design an algorithm for general graphs with work
\widetilde{O}(n^{2-\epsilon}+m)
and depth
\widetilde{O}(n^{1-\epsilon})
for some

. For these graphs, we cannot use the bounded-degree assumption anymore, so we need to work around both issues above.
The latter problem is easier to deal with: We can simply use the bound

rather than

and get a slightly worse, but still satisfactory, bound on

.
However, the first issue turns out to be much more prominent. To handle it, we are going to partially reuse the precomputed objects between the phases of the algorithm (rather than discard all precomputed information after each phase).
More precisely, we will additionally compute sets of
permanently heavy
vertices and
alive edges
that will be maintained (and efficiently updated) between the phases. The permanently heavy vertices will exhibit weaker structural properties than the heavy vertices in the sparse variant of the algorithm. Because of that, at the beginning of each phase we will still need to additionally compute an additional set of
temporarily heavy
vertices.
Then, the set of heavy vertices – understood as all permanently or temporarily heavy vertices – will satisfy additional structural properties, which will eventually allow us to argue the effectiveness of the improved algorithm.

in which they occur. For all the remaining vertices, we add to

all of their incoming edges from

from pending out-adjacency lists where they occur to reflect the fact that they were already considered. This concludes the description of a single step. Note that a step can be implemented in
\widetilde{O}(1)

t
outgoing alive edges whose pending out-adjacency list is not empty yet.

Let us analyze the resulting
Z_{0}
and
G_{0}
and prove that they satisfy the required properties.
First, it follows from the stopping condition that all vertices have either at least
t
alive outgoing edges, or all of their edges have been considered, in which case all their outgoing edges whose heads do not end up in
Z_{0}
are included in

. Hence, item
1
holds.

Next, for each vertex
v
, in a single step we add to

at most
2t
edges with tail

(and in particular no edges if the outdegree of

already exceeds
t
). Therefore, the outdegree of
v
cannot exceed
3t
after each step, which proves
2
.

The property
3
holds, because whenever we were to add too many incoming edges to a vertex

, we specifically limit ourselves to choosing some
p-\mathrm{indeg}_{G_{0}}(u)
edges of the new candidate edges to be added to
E(G_{0})
, so that the indegree of such vertex in
G_{0}
becomes exactly
p
.

As for each
z\in Z_{0}
we have
\mathrm{indeg}_{G_{0}}(z)=p
and
\sum_{v\in V}\mathrm{indeg}_{G_{0}}(v)=\sum_{v\in V}\mathrm{outdeg}_{G_{0}}(v)\leq 3nt
, we conclude that
|Z_{0}|\leq 3nt/p=O(nt/p)
, asserting

.

The last property

follows from the fact that we consider edges outgoing from each vertex

in the increasing order of their weights. Each edge extracted from
P_{v}
for consideration and whose head does not end up in

is included in

.

Let us now analyse the performance of this algorithm. Let us note that in each step, except for the last one, there had to be a vertex whose outdegree in
G_{0}
was still less than
t
after that step and not all of its outgoing edges were considered yet. It means that in that step, we processed its
2t
outgoing edges and more than
t
of them did not get added to

.
By construction, for each such edge, its head was introduced to
Z_{0}
in that step. Therefore, at least
t
new vertices were introduced to
Z_{0}
in this step. It follows that the number of steps is
O(n/t)
, and as each step can be performed in
\widetilde{O}(1)
depth. Thus, the total depth of this algorithm is
\widetilde{O}(n/t)
. As each edge is considered only once, the total work is naturally
\widetilde{O}(m)
.
∎

At the beginning of our improved shortest paths algorithm, we initialize

and

with the sets computed by the algorithm of

We now show that these objects can be efficiently maintained under contractions of vertices into the source vertex

Lemma 5.2
.

Let

and

be as in

Proof.

We remark that from the definition, all sets

only once and can exit it only once too (when it is a part of

After the contraction of

into

resulting from the contraction may violate the conditions

The latter issue can be easily resolved: after each contraction, it suffices to restrict

to its top-

For the former issue, we reapply the same process as we did in the initialization.
That is, we repeat the step outlined in the proof of
Lemma

5.1
as long as there exists a vertex with less than

We point out that in order to do this efficiently, we do not discard the pending out-adjacency lists

all vertices belonging to

The time analysis is analogous to that in the proof of
Lemma

5.1
. Let us call a step

if it was the last performed step after performing a contraction, or

otherwise. Again, during each non-concluding step, at least

∎

This concludes the description and the proof of the correctness of the improved algorithm for non-sparse graphs.
It remains to summarize the efficiency of the algorithm.

5.4
Running time analysis

The initialization of permanently heavy vertices and alive edges takes
\widetilde{O}(m)
work and
\widetilde{O}(n/t)
depth according to
Lemma

5.1
. They are updated after each discovery step. Since there are at most
\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{t}\right\rfloor
such updates, all these updates take
\widetilde{O}(m)
work and
\widetilde{O}(n/t)
depth in total, according to
Lemma

5.2
. The contractions themselves take
\widetilde{O}(m)
work and
\widetilde{O}(n/t)
depth in total as well.

At the beginning of each phase, we perform the computation of near-lists and improved near-lists. The computation of near-lists takes
\widetilde{O}(nt^{2})
work and
\widetilde{O}(t)
depth, while their improvement takes
\widetilde{O}(nt^{2})
work and
\widetilde{O}(1)
depth. Since there are
O(n/(\ell t))
phases, the computation of these objects contributes
\widetilde{O}(n^{2}t/\ell)
to the total work and
\widetilde{O}(n/\ell)
to the total depth.

We also perform
O(n/t)
discovery steps, where each of them takes
\widetilde{O}(nt^{5}/p+\ell t^{5}p^{3})
work and
\widetilde{O}(1)
depth, hence in total they contribute
\widetilde{O}(n^{2}t^{4}/p+n\ell t^{4}p^{3})
to the total work and
\widetilde{O}(n/t)
to the total depth.

Summing up, the total work required by our algorithm is
\widetilde{O}(m+n^{2}t^{4}/p+n\ell t^{4}p^{3}+n^{2}t/\ell)
, while its total depth is
\widetilde{O}(n/t+n/\ell)
.

First, let us note that it is suboptimal to choose
t>\ell
, because decreasing
t
to
\ell
also decreases the work and does not increase depth asymptotically. The work is minimized for a value of
p
such that
n^{2}t^{4}/p=n\ell t^{4}p^{3}
; hence
p=n^{1/4}/\ell^{1/4}
. Since
1\leq\ell\leq n
, we note that
p
defined this way will also satisfy
1\leq p\leq n
, hence it is always a valid choice.
Plugging
p
into the expression bounding the work, it becomes
\widetilde{O}(m+n^{7/4}t^{4}\ell^{1/4}+n^{2}t/\ell)
.
This expression is minimized for a value of
\ell
such that
n^{7/4}t^{4}\ell^{1/4}=n^{2}t/\ell
; hence we obtain
\ell=n^{1/5}/t^{12/5}
. We note that such a choice always leads to
\ell t\leq n
, which is a required condition for the number of phases to be valid.
Since
t\leq\ell
, we have
t\leq n^{1/17}
.
Plugging
\ell
into the expression for work, it becomes
\widetilde{O}(m+n^{9/5}t^{17/5})
, which shows that for any value of a parameter
1\leq t\leq n^{1/17}
, we have an algorithm whose work is
\widetilde{O}(m+n^{9/5}t^{17/5})
and whose depth is
\widetilde{O}(n/t)
.

6
Shortest paths with exponential and lexicographic weights

In this section, we will showcase an application of
Theorem

1.1
to the graphs whose edge weights and path weights are inherently polynomially unbounded: single-source lexicographic bottleneck shortest paths and single-source binary shortest paths. Namely, we prove the following theorems.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a weighted digraph with weights in
\mathbb{N}
.
Single-source lexicographic bottleneck shortest paths in
G
can be computed within
\widetilde{O}(m+n^{9/5}t^{21/5})
work and
\widetilde{O}(n/t)
depth for
t\in[1,n^{1/21}]
.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a weighted digraph, where each edge
e
has weight of the form
2^{w_{e}}
,
w_{e}\in\mathbb{N}
, and its weight is given on input as
w_{e}
.
Single-source distances in
G
can be computed within
\widetilde{O}(m+n^{9/5}t^{21/5})
work and
\widetilde{O}(n/t)
depth for
t\in[1,n^{1/21}]
.

It turns out that both problems can be solved efficiently via the following unified framework: First, we observe that all path weights appearing in the runtime of the algorithm can be manipulated and compared effectively – they can be added to each other in the setting of this algorithm with additional
\widetilde{O}(t)
work and
\widetilde{O}(1)
depth overhead, and they can be compared with
\widetilde{O}(1)
work and depth overhead.
Therefore, given this efficient implementation of path weight operations, we can simply invoke the algorithm of
Theorem

1.1
to solve any instance of binary or lexicographic bottleneck shortest paths efficiently.
The details follow below.

Suppose that
\mathbb{M}=(S,+,0,\leq)
is a totally ordered commutative monoid (representing possible path weights), and
W\subseteq S
is a finite set of permissible edge weights.
We say that
\mathbb{M}

efficiently supports
W
if there exists a parallel data structure maintaining representations of elements of
\mathbb{M}
such that:

•

a representation of
0
can be constructed in work and depth
\widetilde{O}(1)
,

•

given a representation of
a\in\mathbb{M}
and
k
elements
w_{1},\ldots,w_{k}
of
W
, the representation of
a+w_{1}+\ldots+w_{k}
can be constructed in work
\widetilde{O}(k)
and depth
\widetilde{O}(1)
,
without
destroying the representation of
a
, and

•

elements of
\mathbb{M}
can be compared in work and depth
\widetilde{O}(1)
.

The data structure operates under the assumption that each stored element of
\mathbb{M}
is a sum of at most
n^{O(1)}
elements of
W
; then, the
\widetilde{O}(\cdot)
notation hides factors poly-logarithmic in
n
and
|W|
.

Consider the case of single-source binary shortest paths.
Given an
n
-vertex,
m
-edge input graph with each edge
e
of weight
2^{w_{e}}
, we choose
\mathbb{M}_{\rm bin}=\mathbb{N}
(i.e., path weights are non-negative integers) and
W_{\rm bin}=\{2^{i}\,\mid\,i\in\{0,1,\ldots,m^{2}-1\}\}
(i.e., edge weights are integer powers of two smaller than
2^{m^{2}}
).
The condition that edge weights are smaller than
2^{m^{2}}
can be ensured in
\widetilde{O}(n+m)
time – without changing the shape of the single-source shortest paths tree – by applying the following observation repeatedly: Suppose that
L,\delta\in\mathbb{N}
are integers such that
\delta\geq m
and each
w_{e}
is either at most
L
or at least
L+\delta
.
Then all input values
w_{e}
greater than or equal to
L+\delta
can be decreased by
\delta-(m-1)
without changing the shape of the structure of shortest paths.
Similarly, instances of lexicographic bottleneck shortest paths are modeled by choosing
\mathbb{M}_{\rm lex}=(S,\cup,\emptyset,\leq_{\rm lex})
, where
S
is the family of finite multisets of non-negative integers and
\leq_{\rm lex}
is the lexicographic comparison of two multisets.
Formally, we say that
A\leq_{\rm lex}B
if one of the following holds:
A=\emptyset
,
\max A<\max B
, or
\max A=\max B
and
A\setminus\{\max A\}\leq_{\rm lex}B\setminus\{\max B\}
.
We also choose
W_{\rm lex}=\{\{0\},\{1\},\ldots,\{m-1\}\}
.
Input edge weights can be assumed to be smaller than
m
after a straightforward initial preprocessing of the graph.

We delay proving that
\mathbb{M}_{\rm bin}
(and, respectively,
\mathbb{M}_{\rm lex}
) efficiently supports
W_{\rm bin}
(resp.,
W_{\rm lex}
) until later in this section.
Instead, we will first focus on the following result:

Lemma 6.3
.

Suppose
\mathbb{M}
efficiently supports
W
.
Then a single-source shortest path tree in a directed graph with edge weights in
W
, where the path weights are evaluated in
\mathbb{M}
, can be computed within
\widetilde{O}(m+n^{9/5}t^{21/5})
work and
\widetilde{O}(n/t)
depth for
t\in[1,n^{1/21}]
.

Proof.

The key difference between this statement and the
Theorem

1.1
is the fact that the arithmetic operations on
\mathbb{M}
guaranteed by efficiently supporting
W
are not as general as operations on
\mathbb{R}
in the addition-comparison model. Namely, given
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
, the addition-comparison model assumes that we can compute
a+b
in
O(1)
time, while given
c,d\in\mathbb{M}
, we are not provided with the operation of efficiently constructing the value
c+d
. However, this can be remedied if we assume that
d
admits a representation as a sum of a small number of elements of
W
. Hence, in this proof we will revisit all steps of the algorithm of
Theorem

1.1
and verify that every value
a\in\mathbb{M}
representing a path weight is obtained as a sum of: the already-constructed weight of another path and at most
t
other edge weights.
Therefore, a data structure efficiently supporting
W
will incur only an
\widetilde{O}(t)
multiplicative overhead to the total work of the algorithm of
Theorem

1.1
(in particular, only to the parts of the algorithm where new path weights are constructed) and
\widetilde{O}(1)
overhead to the total depth.

Before we start analyzing the specific phases of the algorithm, we remark that even though we assume that edges of the input graph are representable as single elements of
W
, this stops being true after the operation of contraction of a set
U\subseteq V
into the source
s
as after that operation the edges outgoing of
s
represent weights of some paths in the original graph. However,
s
will be the only vertex with outgoing edges that are not representable as single elements of
W
and there are no edges incoming to
s
, so whenever the algorithm appends an edge to the end of a path, it is never an edge going out of
s
(unless the path is a single-vertex path
s
, which is not an issue, either: this path has weight
0
).

First, we consider the computation of permanently heavy vertices and alive edges (
Lemma

5.1
). In this part, we never compute any additional path weights; it suffices to maintain the weights of the edges going out from each vertex sorted increasingly. Hence, this part takes
\widetilde{O}(m)
work and
\widetilde{O}(n/t)
depth.

Then comes the computation of near-lists
\mathrm{NL}(u)
of
Lemma

5.3
and their improvement to
\mathrm{NL}^{\prime}(u)
of
Lemma

5.4
. The path weights that we construct in these parts are always of form
d_{i}+w(v_{i}y)
, where
d_{i}
is an already-constructed path weight and
w(v_{i}y)
is the weight of a single edge. Hence, we get the same work and depth guarantees, which are
\widetilde{O}(nt^{2})
for work in both parts,
\widetilde{O}(t)
for depth of
Lemma

5.3
, and
\widetilde{O}(1)
for depth of improving near-lists. As these precomputation phases are done at the beginning of each of
O(n/(\ell t))
phases, the total work required for all such precomputation stages will be
\widetilde{O}(n^{2}t/\ell)
, while their total depth will be
\widetilde{O}(n/\ell)
.

The next step is the construction of the graphs
G_{t}
and
H
of
Lemma

5.6
. It does not refer to any path weights whatsoever, so their construction requires the same work and depth as previously, which are
\widetilde{O}(nt^{4}/p+\ell t^{4}p^{3})
and
\widetilde{O}(1)
, respectively.

After computing
H
comes the discovery step based on
Lemma

3.1
– the only part of the algorithm where the difference between the computational models appears. In the algorithm of
Lemma

3.1
, we construct the weights of paths as
d^{i}(u,v)+d^{i}(v,y)
for some vertices
u,v,y\in V
, where
d^{i}
is the function representing the weight of the lightest path containing at most
2^{i}
hops. However, whenever we use
d^{i}(a,b)
in such an expression, we are guaranteed that
b\in N_{t}^{i}(a)
, which is the set of
t+1
closest vertices to
a
(including
a
) when considering paths of up to
2^{i}
hops. Because of that, it is obvious that the path certifying
d^{i}(a,b)
cannot have more than
t
hops. Moreover, in the expression
d^{i}(u,v)+d^{i}(v,y)
, if
u\neq v
, the path certifying
d^{i}(v,y)
will not contain any edge going out from
s
(we remark that we cannot argue the same thing for the path certifying
d^{i}(u,v)
), hence
d^{i}(v,y)
is representable as a sum of at most
t
elements of
W
(it is straightforward to also explicitly maintain the
(\leq t)
elements of
W
whose sum is
d^{i}(v,y)
, without further overhead to work and depth) and the expression
d^{i}(u,v)+d^{i}(v,y)
can be efficiently constructed in
\widetilde{O}(t)
work and
\widetilde{O}(1)
depth. As such, in our case we obtain the same
\widetilde{O}(1)
depth guarantee as
Lemma

3.1
; and the work guarantee worsens by a factor of
\widetilde{O}(t)
: now it is
O(|V(H)|t^{3}+|E(H)|t)=\widetilde{O}(nt^{6}/p+\ell t^{6}p^{3})
. Recall that there are
O(n/t)
discovery steps, so the total work incurred by them will be
\widetilde{O}(n^{2}t^{5}/p+n\ell t^{5}p^{3})

The new edge weights constructed during the contraction process are of the form
\mathrm{dist}(s,x)+w(x,v)
, so they can be constructed in
\mathbb{M}
with
\widetilde{O}(1)
overhead for both work and depth, resulting in
\widetilde{O}(m)
work and
\widetilde{O}(n/t)
depth across the whole algorithm.

As the last step, we perform the update of permanently heavy vertices and alive edges of
Lemma

5.2
. Similarly to the case of their initial computation, this part retains the work and depth guarantees of the original algorithm, that is,
\widetilde{O}(m)
work and
\widetilde{O}(n/t)
depth across the entire process.

Summing up, the total work changes to
\widetilde{O}(m+n^{2}t^{5}/p+n\ell t^{5}p^{3}+n^{2}t/\ell)
, while the total depth remains at
\widetilde{O}(n/t+n/\ell)
. Performing a similar running time analysis as in
Section

5.4
, for
{p=n^{1/4}/\ell^{1/4}}
and
\ell=n^{1/5}/t^{16/5}
we obtain an algorithm whose work is
\widetilde{O}(n^{9/5}t^{21/5})
and whose depth is
\widetilde{O}(n/t)
, for any choice of parameter
1\leq t\leq n^{1/21}
.
∎

Having established
Lemma

6.3
, it remains to show that
\mathbb{M}_{\rm bin}
(resp.,
\mathbb{M}_{\rm lex}
) can efficiently support
W_{\rm bin}
(resp.,
W_{\rm lex}
).
In both cases, we will represent elements of
\mathbb{M}_{\rm bin}
and
\mathbb{M}_{\rm lex}
using logarithmic-depth persistent binary trees. When implemented correctly, this will allow us to compare two elements of the semigroup by performing a single tree descent along the tree representation of the elements.
Formally, we will reduce the problem at hand to a dynamic binary tree comparison data structure.
In what follows, we assume that each non-leaf vertex of a binary tree may have one or two children (left and right).

We then use the following notion of a
label
of a node in a binary tree.
The label
W(x)
of a node
x
in a tree
T
is a word over
\{{\tt L},{\tt R}\}
naturally describing the path from the root of
T
to
x
: Following this unique simple path, we write
{\tt L}
when descending to the left child, and
{\tt R}
when descending to the right child. The nodes of the binary trees that we will consider will be assigned colors from a fixed set
\Sigma
(maintaining some auxiliary information about the elements of
\mathbb{M}
that will facilitate efficient comparisons of these elements).

For two binary trees
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
by
\mathrm{iso}(T_{1},T_{2})
let us denote the set of nodes
x\in T_{1}
such that there exists a node
y\in T_{2}
such that
W(x)=W(y)
and the corresponding subtrees rooted at
x
and
y

are isomorphic (with the isomorphism respecting colors in a natural way). It is clear to see that
V(T_{1})\setminus\mathrm{iso}(T_{1},T_{2})
is a prefix of
T_{1}
, that is, it is either empty, or it is a connected subset of vertices of
T_{1}
containing its root. Consequently, let us define
\mathrm{diff}(T_{1},T_{2})=T_{1}[V(T_{1})\setminus\mathrm{iso}(T_{1},T_{2})]
.

Lemma 6.4
.

There exists a deterministic parallel dynamic data structure maintaining a family of nonempty binary trees of depth at most
b\in\mathbb{N}
and with nodes colored with colors from a fixed set
\Sigma
, supporting the following operations:

•

Create and return a one-node binary tree, in work and depth
\widetilde{O}(1)
.

•

Given binary trees
T
and
T^{+}
, create and return a unique tree
T^{\prime}
such that
\mathrm{diff}(T^{\prime},T)=T^{+}
. The operation does
not destroy
the original tree
T
, i.e., after the operation, the family contains both
T
and
T^{\prime}
.
The operation is performed in work
\widetilde{O}(|T^{+}|)
and depth
\widetilde{O}(b)
.

•

Given two binary trees
T_{1}
,
T_{2}
, either determine that
T_{1}=T_{2}
, or find the first node that belongs to both
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
that witnesses that
T_{1}\neq T_{2}
.
Formally, find the lexicographically smallest word
w
over
\{{\tt L},{\tt R}\}
such that there exists a node
x_{1}
in
T_{1}
and a node
x_{2}
in
T_{2}
, both with label
w
, such that either exactly one of
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
has a left (right) child, or
x_{1},x_{2}
are nodes of different colors.
The operation is performed in work and depth
\widetilde{O}(b)
.

The data structure allows multiple queries of the same type to be performed in parallel, i.e., any batch of single-node tree creation queries can be performed in depth
\widetilde{O}(1)
, and a batch of prefix-replacement queries or tree comparisons can be performed in depth
\widetilde{O}(b)
.

Proof.

In the data structure, we will dynamically assign to each node of each tree an integer identifier, so that any two nodes stored in the data structure are given the same identifier if and only if the subtrees of the respective trees rooted at these nodes are isomorphic (with the isomorphism naturally respecting colors).
This approach resembles the parallel variant of the
dictionary of basic factors
by Karp, Miller, and Rosenberg
[
KMR72
]
, implemented e.g. by Crochemore and Rytter
[
CR91
]
.
Here, each node
x
is assigned a triple
(c,{\rm id}_{\tt L},{\rm id}_{\tt R})
, where
c
is the color of
x
,
{\rm id}_{\tt L}
is the identifier of the left child of
x
(or
\bot
if
x
has no left child), and
{\rm id}_{\tt R}
is the identifier of the right child of
x
(or
\bot
if
x
has no right child).
We maintain a parallel dictionary
{\cal D}
storing a mapping
\mu
from all such triples to unique identifiers.
Then the identifier of
x
is precisely
\mu(c,{\rm id}_{\tt L},{\rm id}_{\tt R})
.
We also maintain the number
I
, initially
0
, of unique identifiers assigned so far.

Creating a one-node tree with the only node
x
of color
c
requires us to verify if
\mu(c,\bot,\bot)
already exists; in this case, the identifier of
x
is precisely
\mu(c,\bot,\bot)
; otherwise, we define the identifier of
x
to be
I
, define
\mu(c,\bot,\bot)\coloneqq I
and increase
I
by
1
.
In case of multiple single-node tree creation queries, we group the trees by the color of the node and ensure that the nodes of the same color are assigned the same identifier.
This can be done in parallel in depth
\widetilde{O}(1)
.

For prefix-replacement queries, we stress that the required work complexity
\widetilde{O}(|T^{+}|)
may be significantly smaller than
O(|T^{\prime}|)
, which is the size of the representation of the tree
T^{\prime}
we need to construct. This is made possible by the fact that the representations of
T
and
T^{\prime}
are allowed to share nodes of
\mathrm{iso}(T,T^{\prime})
, which are already allocated as part of
T
. Hence, we do not have to allocate these nodes again for the representation of
T^{\prime}
, and it only suffices to properly set pointers from the nodes of
T^{\prime}
to
\mathrm{iso}(T,T^{\prime})
. That method is precisely the
node (path) copying method
of design of fully persistent data structures
[
DSST86
]
.

Firstly, for each node
x\in T^{+}
, we determine its corresponding node
B(x)\in T
with the property that
W(x)=W(B(x))
(or
\perp
if such
B(x)
does not exist). This part can be performed in
\widetilde{O}(|T^{+}|)
work and
\widetilde{O}(b)
depth by performing synchronous descents in
T
and
T^{+}
in parallel for each
x\in T^{+}
. Afterwards, for each
x\in T^{+}
that does not have a left child, we check if there exists
y\in T
such that
W(y)=W(x){\tt L}
(where
W(x){\tt L}
denotes the label of
x
concatenated with a single letter
L
), and if yes, we set
y
as the left child of
x
. We perform this check by verifying if
B(x)
has a left child. We perform the analogous procedure for the right children as well.

It is clear that the identifiers of nodes of
\mathrm{iso}(T^{\prime},T)
are the same as the identifiers of the corresponding nodes of
\mathrm{iso}(T,T^{\prime})
, so the only nodes that require the assignment of identifiers are the nodes of
T^{+}
.
We will compute these identifiers bottom-up.
That is, for each
i=b,b-1,\ldots,1
in order, we gather all nodes of
T^{+}
at depth
i
, map each node to its assigned triple
(c,{\rm id}_{\tt L},{\rm id}_{\tt R})
, and use
{\cal D}
to determine if the mapping
\mu
already defines the identifier
\mu(c,{\rm id}_{\tt L},{\rm id}_{\tt R})
of the node; if this is the case, the work for the given node is done.
Then we allocate new consecutive identifiers, starting from
I
upwards, to all nodes for which the identifier is still unknown.
Again, by grouping the nodes with the same assigned triple, we ensure that the nodes with the same assigned triple are allocated the same identifier.
Then we update the mapping
\mu
for all assigned triples with freshly allocated identifiers.
The entire procedure takes work
\widetilde{O}(|T^{+}|)
and depth
\widetilde{O}(b)
. A batch of updates can also be processed in the same depth by performing the procedure for all queries in the batch, in parallel, for each
i=b,b-1,\ldots,1
in order.

Now we can compare two trees
T_{1},T_{2}
in work and depth
O(b)
by performing a single descent down the trees: First, letting
x_{1}
(resp.,
x_{2}
) be the root of
T_{1}
(resp.,
T_{2}
), we verify that the identifiers of
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
are different (otherwise,
T_{1}=T_{2}
).
If this is the case, we progress
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
down
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
, respectively, until
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
witness that
T_{1}\neq T_{2}
(i.e., the two nodes have different sets of children, or have different colors).
While performing the descent, we replace both
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
with their respective left children (if their identifiers are different), and with their respective right children otherwise (in which case these are guaranteed to have different identifiers).
It is straightforward to verify that this procedure returns a witness that
T_{1}\neq T_{2}
in work and depth
\widetilde{O}(b)
; and naturally, multiple such tree comparisons can be performed fully in parallel.
∎

We now use
Lemma

6.4
to show that the discussed semigroups can support their respective edge weight sets.

Lemma 6.5
.

\mathbb{M}_{\rm lex}
can efficiently support

.

Proof.

For convenience, assume that
m=2^{b}
for some

.
Let
\Sigma\coloneqq\mathbb{N}
.
We assign to an element

of
\mathbb{M}_{\rm lex}
(i.e., a multiset of non-negative integers below

) a rooted binary tree

defined as follows: First, create a full binary tree with

from left to right.
The leaf of index

receives the color equal to the number of occurrences of

in

.
Non-leaf nodes receive color
0
.
Eventually, all leaves of color
0
are removed from
T(A)
, as well as non-leaf vertices whose subtrees do not contain any leaves of color other than

.

We initialize a data structure of
Lemma

6.4
to maintain the family of trees

for every considered set

.
It is trivial to see that

can be constructed in time and work
\widetilde{O}(1)
.
Similarly, given an element
A
of
\mathbb{M}_{\rm lex}
represented by the tree

, it is straightforward to add
k

to

Essentially, we reuse the classic Brent–Kung implementation of the

While the details are fairly straightforward, we include them below for completeness.

•

Then, the tree

•

Otherwise, if at least one of the trees

•

Otherwise, let

7
Incremental minimum cost-to-time ratio cycle

In this section, we will use our improved shortest paths algorithm to design an efficient data structure for the incremental variant of the generalization of the minimum-mean cycle problem, dubbed
Minimum Cost-to-Time Ratio Cycle
.
In the static version of the problem, we are given a directed graph
G
with
n
vertices and
m
edges, where each edge
e
has an arbitrary real cost
c(e)
and positive real time
t(e)
.
The task is to determine a cycle
C
in
G
with the minimum ratio of the total cost to the total time (further simply referred to as
minimum-ratio cycle
), that is, the cycle minimizing the value
\frac{\sum_{e\in E(C)}c(e)}{\sum_{e\in E(C)}t(e)}
.
In the incremental variant, edges are dynamically inserted into the graph, and after each update, our aim is to report the current minimum-ratio cycle in
G
(or correctly state that
G
is acyclic).

Our result is as follows:

Theorem 7.1
.

There exists an incremental data structure for
Minimum Cost-to-Time Ratio Cycle
in an
n
-vertex graph.
The insertion of the
m
-th edge into the graph is processed in time
\widetilde{O}(mn^{21/22})
.

The problem was first analyzed in the static setting by Dantzig, Blattner, and Rao
[
DBR67
]
and Lawler
[
Law72
]
.
In this setting, the two best strongly polynomial algorithms were given by Bringmann, Hansen, and Krinninger
[
BHK17
]
, who find a minimum-ratio cycle in an
n
-vertex,
m
-edge graph in time
\widetilde{O}(m^{3/4}n^{3/2})
or
n^{3}/2^{\Omega(\sqrt{\log n})}
.
Their algorithm reduces the minimum-ratio cycle problem to multiple instances of single-source shortest paths in graphs without negative cycles.
We observe that in the incremental setting, we can actually guarantee that all such instances contain only edges of non-negative weights.
This allows us to process edge insertions more efficiently than by recomputing the answer from scratch on every graph update.
In particular, this allows us to achieve
\widetilde{O}(mn^{1-\epsilon})
edge insertion time, which – to the best of our knowledge – has not been achieved before for this problem in the strongly polynomial model.

Approach.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.
We say that a potential function
\phi:V(G)\to\mathbb{R}

attests

\lambda
in
G
if
c(uv)-\lambda t(uv)-\phi(u)+\phi(v)\geq 0
for every edge
uv\in E(G)
.
We use the fact that:

Lemma 7.2

(see
[
BHK17
, Lemma 2, 4]
and references therein)
.

There exists a potential function attesting
\lambda
in
G
if and only if
\lambda
does not exceed the minimum ratio of a cycle in
G
.

The implementation of our data structure maintains, apart from the graph
G
itself:

•

a minimum-ratio cycle
C
and its cost-to-time ratio
\lambda^{\star}
, and

•

a potential function
\phi:V(G)\to\mathbb{R}
attesting
\lambda^{\star}
in
G
.

(As a special case, if
G
is acyclic, we define that
C=\bot
,
\lambda^{\star}=+\infty
and
\phi
is undefined.)

Our edge insertion algorithm exploits the technique of
parametric search
, introduced in a work of Megiddo
[
Meg81
]
and already used successfully in the static variant of minimum-ratio cycle by Megiddo
[
Meg81
]
and Bringmann, Hansen, and Krinninger
[
BHK17
]
.
This method allows us to design an efficient strongly polynomial sequential algorithm determining the value of a hidden real parameter
\lambda^{\star}
(in our case, precisely the minimum ratio of a cycle in a graph), given two strongly polynomial algorithms – a low-depth parallel algorithm and an efficient sequential algorithm –
comparing
a given input parameter
\lambda
with
\lambda^{\star}
.
Below we give the formal statement of the technique; an intuitive explanation of its implementation can be found in
[
BHK17
, Section 2.1]
.

Theorem 7.3

(
[
Meg81
,
AST94
]
)
.

Let
\lambda^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}
be a hidden real value.
Suppose that there exist two strongly polynomial algorithms that input a real value
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and test whether
\lambda\leq\lambda^{\star}
:

•

a parallel algorithm with work
W_{p}
and depth
D_{p}
, and

•

a sequential algorithm with time complexity
T_{s}
.

We moreover require that in the parallel algorithm, all comparisons involving
\lambda
test the sign of a polynomial in
\lambda
of constant degree.
Then there exists a sequential strongly polynomial algorithm computing
\lambda^{\star}
in time
O(W_{p}+D_{p}T_{s}\log W_{p})
.

Armed with
Theorem

7.3
, we can implement the edge insertion.
Suppose
G
is a directed graph just after an insertion of the edge
pq
.
Let
C_{\rm old}
be a minimum-ratio cycle in
G-pq
(of ratio
\lambda_{\rm old}
) and
\phi_{\rm old}
be a potential function attesting
\lambda_{\rm old}
in
G-pq
.
Our aim is to determine a minimum-ratio cycle
C_{\rm new}
in
G
(of ratio
\lambda_{\rm new}
) and a potential function
\phi_{\rm new}
attesting
\lambda_{\rm new}
in
G
.
For our convenience, we assume that
m\geq n
,
C_{\rm old}\neq\bot
and
\lambda_{\rm old}<+\infty
; it is straightforward to adapt the following description to the case where
m<n
or
G-pq
is acyclic.

Observe that
\lambda_{\rm new}=\min\{\lambda_{\rm old},\lambda_{pq}\}\leq\lambda_{\rm old}
, where
\lambda_{pq}
is the minimum-ratio cycle containing the edge
pq
(where
\lambda_{pq}=+\infty
if no cycle in
G
contains
pq
).
Moreover,

\lambda_{pq}=\min\left\{\frac{c(pq)+\sum_{e\in E(P)}c(e)}{t(pq)+\sum_{e\in E(P)}t(e)}\,\colon P\text{ is a~path from $q$ to $p$ in $G-pq$}\right\}.

In particular, for
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
we have
\lambda\leq\lambda_{pq}
if and only if for every path
P
from
q
to
p
in
G-pq
we have
\lambda\leq\frac{c(pq)+\sum_{e\in E(P)}c(e)}{t(pq)+\sum_{e\in E(P)}t(e)}
, or equivalently
\sum_{e\in E(P)}(c(e)-\lambda t(e))\geq\lambda t(pq)-c(pq)
.

Therefore, we can test whether
\lambda\leq\lambda_{\rm new}
for a given
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
as follows.
If
\lambda>\lambda_{\rm old}
, then also
\lambda>\lambda_{\rm new}
and the test is negative.
So suppose that
\lambda\leq\lambda_{\rm old}
; then
\lambda\leq\lambda_{\rm new}
if and only if
\lambda\leq\lambda_{pq}
.
Construct an edge-weighted graph
G_{\lambda}
with the same set of edges as
G-pq
, where an edge
uv\in E(G^{\prime})
is assigned weight
w_{\lambda}(uv)\coloneqq c(uv)-\lambda t(uv)-\phi_{\rm old}(u)+\phi_{\rm old}(v)
.
Under our assumption, for every edge
uv\in E(G_{\lambda})
, we have

w_{\lambda}(uv)=c(uv)-\lambda t(uv)-\phi_{\rm old}(u)+\phi_{\rm old}(v)\geq c(uv)-\lambda_{\rm old}t(uv)-\phi_{\rm old}(u)+\phi_{\rm old}(v)\geq 0,

since
\phi_{\rm old}
attests
\lambda_{\rm old}
in
G-pq
.
Therefore,
G_{\lambda}
has non-negative edge weights.
Moreover, every path
P
from
q
to
p
has total weight
\phi_{\rm old}(p)-\phi_{\rm old}(q)+\sum_{e\in E(P)}(c(e)-\lambda t(e))
, so
\lambda\leq\lambda_{pq}
if and only if every path from
q
to
p
in
G_{\lambda}
has total weight at least
\Gamma_{\lambda}\coloneqq\lambda t(pq)-c(pq)+\phi_{\rm old}(p)-\phi_{\rm old}(q)
.
Hence, in
O(m)
work and
O(1)
depth, testing whether
\lambda\leq\lambda_{\rm new}
can be reduced to a single instance of non-negatively weighted single-source shortest paths in
G_{\lambda}
.
Such an instance can be solved by:

•

a parallel algorithm with work
W_{p}=\widetilde{O}(m+n^{1+21/22})
and depth
D_{p}=\widetilde{O}(n^{21/22})
(
Theorem

1.1
for
t=n^{1/22}
), and

•

a sequential algorithm with running time
T_{s}=\widetilde{O}(n+m)
(the usual Dijkstra‘s algorithm).

It can be verified that all edge and path weights in the parallel algorithm of
Theorem

1.1
are linear functions of
\lambda
.
Thanks to that,
Theorem

7.3
applies and there exists a sequential strongly polynomial algorithm computing
\lambda_{\rm new}
in time
O(W_{p}+D_{p}T_{s}\log W_{p})=\widetilde{O}(mn^{21/22})
.

We now determine
C_{\rm new}
.
If
\lambda_{\rm new}=\lambda_{\rm old}
, we can simply take
C_{\rm new}=C_{\rm old}
.
Otherwise,
C_{\rm new}
is a cycle containing the new edge
pq
.
In this case, the shortest path
P
in
G_{\lambda_{\rm new}}
has total weight exactly
\Gamma_{\lambda_{\rm new}}
(and this path can be found by a single run of Dijkstra‘s algorithm in
G_{\lambda_{\rm new}}
), and then
C_{\rm new}
is constructed by concatenating
pq
with
P
.

It remains to update
\phi_{\rm old}
to
\phi_{\rm new}
.
Let
d:V(G)\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cup\{+\infty\}
denote the distance from
q
to each vertex of
G_{\lambda_{\rm new}}
(where we set
d(v)=+\infty
if
v
is unreachable from
q
in
G_{\lambda_{\rm new}}
).
For each
v\in V(G)
, we set
\phi_{\rm new}(v)=\phi_{\rm old}(v)-\delta(v)
, where

\delta(v)\coloneqq\begin{cases}d(v)&\text{if }d(v)<+\infty,\\
\Delta&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

for some large enough value
\Delta\geq 0
(to be determined later).
We verify now that
\phi_{\rm new}
attests
\lambda_{\rm new}
in
G
.
Pick an edge
uv\in E(G)
, aiming to show that

f(uv)\coloneqq c(uv)-\lambda_{\rm new}t(uv)-\phi_{\rm new}(u)+\phi_{\rm new}(v)\geq 0.

If
uv\neq pq
, then

f(uv)\,=\,c(uv)-\lambda_{\rm new}t(uv)-\phi_{\rm old}(u)+\phi_{\rm old}(v)+\delta(u)-\delta(v)\,=\,w_{\lambda_{\rm new}}(uv)+\delta(u)-\delta(v).

If
d(u)<+\infty
, then
d(v)<+\infty
and
f(uv)=w_{\lambda_{\rm new}}(uv)+d(u)-d(v)\geq 0
since
d
is a distance function in
G_{\lambda_{\rm new}}
.
If
d(u)=d(v)=+\infty
, then
f(uv)=w_{\lambda_{\rm new}}(uv)\geq 0
.
Finally, if
d(u)=+\infty
and
d(v)<+\infty
, then
f(uv)=w_{\lambda_{\rm new}}(uv)+\Delta-d(v)
and it is enough to ensure that
\Delta\geq d(v)-w_{\lambda_{\rm new}}(uv)
.

On the other hand, if
uv=pq
, then
\delta(q)=0
and

f(pq)\,=\,c(pq)-\lambda_{\rm new}t(pq)-\phi_{\rm old}(p)+\phi_{\rm old}(q)+\delta(p)-\delta(q)\,=\,\delta(p)-\Gamma_{\lambda_{\rm new}}.

If
d(p)<+\infty
, then
\delta(p)\geq\Gamma_{\lambda_{\rm new}}
follows from
\lambda_{\rm new}\leq\lambda_{pq}
.
If
d(p)=+\infty
, then
f(pq)=\Delta-\Gamma_{\lambda_{\rm new}}
and it is enough to ensure that
\Delta\geq\Gamma_{\lambda_{\rm new}}
.

Hence
\phi_{\rm new}
attests
G
for

\Delta=\max\{0,\,\Gamma_{\lambda_{\rm new}},\,\max\{d(v)-w_{\lambda_{\rm new}}(uv):uv\in E(G-pq),\,d(u)=+\infty,\,d(v)<+\infty\}\}.

This concludes the edge insertion algorithm.
We have shown it is correct and recomputes the minimum-ratio cycle and the potential function attesting the minimum ratio of a cycle in
G
in time
\widetilde{O}(mn^{21/22})
, thereby proving
Theorem

7.1
.

Appendix A
Simplifying assumptions

To see that Assumptions
1
and
2
can be always ensured in the addition-comparison model with no loss of generality, interpret
V
as integers
\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
and for each
uv=e\in E
, transform the weight of
e
into the triple
w^{\prime}(e):=(w(e),1,v-u)

and add the tuples coordinate-wise when computing path weights.
Then, use the lexicographical order on triples to compare path lengths and distances from a single origin.
This way, the original edge weights are only manipulated via additions and comparisons, as desired.
On the other hand, manipulating the latter two coordinates of the augmented weights
w^{\prime}
of paths with
\operatorname{poly}(n)
edges
only requires
O(1)
-time arithmetic operations on integers of magnitude
\operatorname{poly}(n)
; these are standard in the word RAM model.

With this transformation, observe that
1
is guaranteed by non-negativity of
w
and since the second coordinate of
w^{\prime}(P^{\prime})
is strictly smaller than the second coordinate of
w^{\prime}(P)
.
Similarly,
2
is satisfied since
w^{\prime}(P_{1})
and
w^{\prime}(P_{2})
, by a telescoping sum argument, have distinct third coordinates
u-x\neq u-y
.
If we optimize the distances lexicographically, the weights of shortest paths after the transformation have their respective first coordinates equal to the corresponding original distances in
G
, i.e., original distances can be retrieved trivially having solved the problem for transformed weights.

). Moreover, the corresponding values
\delta(y)
are equal to
d^{i+1}(u,y)
.

Proof of the claim.
Let us fix
y\in N_{t}^{i+1}(u)
. We aim to prove that
y\in N^{\prime}(u)
and that it is one of the
t+1
vertices with the smallest value of
\delta
. Let
P
be a shortest path from
u
to
y
of at most
2^{i+1}
hops, and let
v
be any vertex on
P
such that its subpaths
P_{1}
from
u
to
v
and
P_{2}
from
v
to
y
consist of at most
2^{i}
hops. We have

d^{i+1}(u,y)=w(P_{1})+w(P_{2})\geq d^{i}(u,v)+d^{i}(v,y)\geq d^{i+1}(u,y),

so
w(P_{1})=d^{i}(u,v)
and
w(P_{2})=d^{i}(v,y)
. Moreover,
w(P_{1})\leq w(P)=d^{i+1}(u,y)
by non-negativity of weights, so
d^{i}(u,v)\leq d^{i+1}(u,y)
. We now note that
v\in N_{t}^{i}(u)
, as otherwise there are at least
t+1
distinct vertices
z\neq v
such that
d^{i}(u,z)<d^{i}(u,v)
(recall
2
) which implies
d^{i+1}(u,z)\leq d^{i}(u,z)<d^{i}(u,v)\leq d^{i+1}(u,y)
and thus
y\not\in N_{t}^{i+1}(u)
, a contradiction.
We can similarly show that
y\in N_{t}^{i}(v)
, as otherwise there exist at least
t+1
vertices
z
such that
d^{i}(v,z)<d^{i}(v,y)
and for all such
z
we have

d^{i+1}(u,z)\leq d^{i}(u,v)+d^{i}(v,z)<w(P_{1})+d^{i}(v,y)=w(P_{1})+w(P_{2})=w(P)=d^{i+1}(u,y),

a contradiction. The facts that
v\in N_{t}^{i}(u)
and
y\in N_{t}^{i}(v)
imply that
y\in N^{\prime}(u)
and that
\delta(y)\leq d^{i}(u,v)+d^{i}(v,y)=d^{i+1}(u,y)
. However, since for any vertices
a,b,c
we have
d^{i+1}(a,c)\leq d^{i}(a,b)+d^{i}(b,c)
, it is clear that
\delta(z)\geq d^{i+1}(u,z)
for any
z\in N^{\prime}(u)
. This implies
\delta(y)=d^{i+1}(u,y)
and
\delta(y)
indeed is among
t+1
lowest values of
\delta
, finishing the proof.

\lhd

The claim above shows that
N^{i+1}(u)
can be constructed by computing
N^{\prime}(u)
and the corresponding values
\delta(y)
, and taking the
t+1
lowest values of
\delta
. It is possible to do so for a particular
u\in V
in
\widetilde{O}(t^{2})
work and
\widetilde{O}(1)
depth using parallel sorting.
Doing so for all vertices
u\in V
in parallel takes
\widetilde{O}(nt^{2})
work and
\widetilde{O}(1)
depth.
We repeat this computation for subsequent
i=0,\ldots,\ell-1=\left\lceil\log n\right\rceil-1
, achieving the goal of computing
N_{t}(u)\coloneqq N_{t}^{\ell}(u)
along with the corresponding distances
\mathrm{dist}(u,v)\coloneqq d^{\ell}(u,v)
within
\widetilde{O}(nt^{2})
work and
\widetilde{O}(\ell)=\widetilde{O}(1)
depth.
∎
