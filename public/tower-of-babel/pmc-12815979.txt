Signatures of Trained Immunity Following mRNA Vaccination: Differences Between mRNA-1273 and BNT162b2
Authors: Martin Kunc, Bradley Joseph Whitehead, Lars Jørgen Østergaard, Martin Tolstrup, Christine Stabell Benn, Christian Morberg Wejse, Peter Nejsum
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTrained immunity, a de-facto innate immune memory, has been extensively studied in response to live-attenuated vaccines, but its presence following the new COVID-19 vaccines has not yet been fully elucidated. In this study, we investigate markers of trained immunity in individuals vaccinated with mRNA-1273 or BNT162b2. As part of the vaccine roll-out in Denmark and recruited for a comparative study. Our primary objective was to determine whether these vaccines elicit lasting changes in innate immune responses, particularly in monocyte populations and cytokine production following stimulation with a panel of agonists. The study was conducted at four time points: Day-0 (pre-vaccination), Day-28, Day-90, and Day-180 post-vaccination. We observed no significant differences in monocyte subpopulations between vaccine groups; however, cytokine and chemokine analysis revealed distinct immune signatures. While IL-6 and TNFα production remained unchanged after ex-vivo restimulation in the BNT162b2 group, individuals vaccinated with mRNA-1273 exhibited a sustained increase in the production of these cytokines, persisting for up to 180 days post-vaccination. Additionally, CCL2, a key chemokine involved in monocyte recruitment, was upregulated following mRNA-1273 vaccination but decreased in the BNT162b2 group, further supporting the finding of differential innate immune responses between the two vaccines. In conclusion, our study provides evidence that mRNA-1273, but not BNT162b2, induces immune responses consistent with the concept of trained immunity. Introduction

The concept of using mRNA as a therapeutic agent dates to the 1980s, when the first studies demonstrated its potential for direct delivery into eukaryotic cells [1]. However, it was the development and approval of mRNA vaccines against COVID-19 that marked a breakthrough in this field of preventive vaccinology [2]. These vaccines utilize lipid nanoparticles to encapsulate mRNA encoding the antigenic protein of interest, enabling robust immune responses even in the absence of traditional adjuvants [3]. Following the success of mRNA-based COVID-19 vaccines, rapid advancements have been made toward developing mRNA vaccines for a variety of infectious diseases [4]. A key area of innovation includes the development of self-replicating mRNA vaccines, which aim to enhance cost-effectiveness by reducing the required dose and potentially enabling single-dose administration [5, 6]. As research in this field continues to expand, mRNA technology is expected to play a crucial role in future vaccination strategies.

In response to the COVID-19 pandemic, mRNA-1273 (Spikevax®, Moderna, Inc., Cambridge, MA, USA) and BNT162b2 (Comirnaty®, Pfizer/BioNTech, New York, NY, USA/Mainz, Germany) were developed and successfully deployed. Despite utilizing the same mRNA technology, differences in antibody responses have been observed, with higher antibody titers reported in individuals vaccinated with mRNA-1273 [7–10]. Nevertheless, both vaccines confer some protection through activation of both innate and adaptive immune responses [11, 12].

The innate immune system plays a critical role in orchestrating protective adaptive immune responses following vaccination. A growing body of research suggests that not only adaptive immunity possesses immunological memory, but innate immune responses can also undergo long-term functional reprogramming, a phenomenon termed trained immunity [13]. Trained immunity is characterized by epigenetic and metabolic rewiring of innate immune cells, such as monocytes, macrophages and their bone marrow progenitors, leading to an enhanced response upon subsequent exposure to pathogens [13, 14]. This concept has been extensively studied in the context of live attenuated vaccines, such as the Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) vaccine, which has been shown to reduce infant mortality beyond its target pathogen [15–17]. Similar effects have been observed with other stimuli: vaccinia [18, 19], measles vaccine [20], oral polio vaccine [21], trivalent live attenuated influenza vaccines [22], and β-glucan [23]. More recently, Murphy [24] reported increased frequencies of circulating monocytes and enhanced cytokine and chemokine responses following adenovirus-vectored SARS-CoV-2 vaccination, suggesting the potential for trained immunity induction by alternative vaccine platforms. The concept was also behind the early trials of BCG for heterologous protection against COVID-19 which were initiated prior to the advent of mRNA vaccines [25].

Given that lipid nanoparticle (LNP) delivered mRNA vaccines elicit strong immune responses even without adjuvants [3, 26], their potential to induce trained immunity remains an open question. Some studies suggest that mRNA vaccines can induce short-term epigenetic modifications in innate immune cells [27], while others report no significant differences or mild decrease in cytokine responses post-vaccination [28–31]. However, majority of the studies focused only on BNT162b2 mRNA vaccine. To address these discrepancies, and to quantitate potential differences between BNT162b2 and mRNA-1273, we examined signatures of trained immunity markers in individuals vaccinated with either BNT162b2 or mRNA-1273. Our findings indicate the presence of trained immunity hallmarks, with mRNA-1273 vaccine yielding more pronounced effects compared with BNT162b2. These results provide new insights into the potential of mRNA vaccines to elicit trained immunity and contribute to a broader understanding of the overall immunological impact of this novel vaccine platform.

Methods

Cohort

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were obtained from the National Cohort Study of Effectiveness and Safety of SARS-CoV-2 Vaccines (ENFORCE) repository. ENFORCE is a non-randomized, parallel-group, open-label, observational study evaluating individuals who received a COVID-19 vaccine as part of the Danish Vaccination Program [32, 33] and were recruited to give blood for comparative studies. In Denmark people had the option to choose between the two types of mRNA vaccines. We know from the full cohort that there were no differences in age, sex and comorbidities between those choosing mRNA-1273 and BNT162b2. For this analysis, we used samples collected during the first four main scheduled study visits: 0 to 14 days prior to the first vaccination, 0 to 5 days prior to the second vaccination, and at Day 90 (± 14 days) and Day 180 (± 14 days) post-first vaccination (Fig. 1 A). The samples used in this study originated from participants vaccinated either with mRNA-1273 or BNT162b2. The study population included male and female participants aged over 50 years (Fig. 1B C). The participants in this study were selected from cohort used in study of Dietz [34] based on their low comorbidity score (CCI = 0). The study protocol was approved by the Danish Medicines Agency (#2020–006003-42), and the National Committee on Health Research Ethics (#1–10-72–337-20). All participants provided informed written consent.

Assessment of Monocyte Population

The proportion of monocytes within the PBMC population was determined using flow cytometry. A total of 1 × 10⁶ PBMCs were incubated with Human Fc Block (BD Bioscience, cat#564220) diluted in Brilliant Stain Buffer (BD Bioscience, cat#566349) for 10 min to prevent nonspecific binding. Subsequently, cells were stained with same fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies panel as in Borup [35] specific for CD14 (BD Bioscience, cat#55742) and CD16 (BD Bioscience, cat#612944). Cell viability was assessed using the Zombie Violet Fixable Viability Kit (BioLegend, cat#423113) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Samples were acquired on an LSR Fortessa X-20 Cell Analyzer (BD Bioscience). Unstained cells and fluorescence-minus-one (FMO) controls were used to account for background staining and to establish gating strategies. Data was analyzed using FlowJo software (BD Bioscience).

Monocytes were identified as a percentage of the total PBMC population and classified into three subsets: classical (CD14⁺, CD16⁻), non-classical (CD14⁻, CD16⁺), and intermediate (CD14⁺, CD16⁺).

PBMC Stimulation

The culture medium was prepared using RPMI 1640 medium (Serana, cat# L0498-500) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS, Serana, cat# S-FBS-SA-025) and 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin (Biowest, cat# L0022-100). The medium was pre-warmed to 37 °C and 100 µL containing 1 × 105 PBMC was added to each well of a round-bottom 96-well plate (Sarstedt, cat# 83.3925.500). The cells were incubated overnight at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2​.

The following day, cells were stimulated with one of the following ligands: lipopolysaccharide (LPS, InvivoGen, cat# tlrl-eklps, 5 ng/mL, TLR-4), Resiquimod (R848, InvivoGen, cat# tlrl-r848, 5 µg/mL, TLR-7/8), Curdlan (InvivoGen, cat# tlrl-curd, 10 µg/mL, TLR-4/2), or Pam3CSK4 (InvivoGen, cat# tlrl-pms, 10 µg/mL, TLR-1/2). Unstimulated cells served as controls. Stimulation was carried out for 24 h under the same incubation conditions. Following the stimulation period, supernatants were collected and stored at −80 °C for subsequent analyses.

Cytokine and Chemokine Production by PBMC

The quantity of cytokine or chemokine production was measured in supernatants using ELISA (DuoSet, R&D systems) according to manufacturer instructions. The cytokines measured included IL-6 (cat# DY206-05), IL-10 (cat# DY217B-05), TNFα (cat# DY210-05) and IFNγ (cat# DY285B-05). The chemokines measured included CCL2 (cat# DY279-05) and CXCL1 (cat# DY275-05).

SARS-CoV-2 Antibody Profiling

The antibody data analyzed in this study were derived from the original dataset generated by Dietz et al. [34], ensuring consistency with the cohort of participants included in the present work. A full description of the experimental procedures is available in the original publication [34]; here, we provide a concise overview of the methodology relevant to our analysis.

Serum IgG antibodies against SARS-CoV-2 antigens were quantified using the Meso Scale Diagnostics Multiantigen Serology Assay, performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, serum samples were diluted at a ratio of 1:5000 prior to assay processing. The electrochemiluminescence signal was subsequently measured using a MESO SECTOR S 600 Reader (Meso Scale Diagnostics), providing quantitative antibody profiles across multiple viral antigens.

Statistics

Data analysis and visualization were performed using Prism software (GraphPad, version 10). Outliers were identified and removed from the dataset using the ROUT method with a 1% threshold. Normality and heteroscedasticity of the data were assessed, and when the assumptions for parametric testing were met, a one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was used to determine statistical significance. For datasets that did not meet these assumptions, the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test was applied. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Whole blood samples were collected from participants who received either mRNA-1273 or BNT162b2 according to study design depicted on Fig. 1A. The age range of participants was 49–65 years (Fig. 1B), and 8 males and 4 females were included in each group (Fig. 1 C). PBMC were isolated and used in all experimental assays.

No significant differences were observed in the overall percentage of monocytes in peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) samples between the groups (Fig. 2A). In the mRNA-1273 group, monocyte frequencies ranged from 1.06% to 2.84%, while in the BNT162b2 group, they ranged from 1.04% to 2.88%. Similarly, when monocytes were subdivided into Classical (CD14⁺CD16⁻), Non-classical (CD14⁻CD16⁺), and Intermediate (CD14⁺CD16⁺) subsets: no significant differences were detected between the mRNA-1273 (Fig. 2B) and BNT162b2 (Fig. 2C) groups, either before or after the vaccination. Classical monocytes constituted the majority, accounting for 76.83 ± 6.28% in the mRNA-1273 group and 74.89 ± 5% in the BNT162b2 group, with the remaining ~ 15% being evenly distributed between the Non-classical and Intermediate subsets.

Concentrations of cytokines (IL-6, IL-10, IFNγ, TNFα) and chemokines (CCL2, CXCL1) in culture supernatants were quantified by ELISA from samples of participants vaccinated with mRNA-1273 (Fig. 4) or BNT162b2 (Fig. 5) after ex-vivo restimulation with a panel of agonists. Overall, the mRNA-1273 group exhibited more pronounced cytokine production dynamics compared to the BNT162b2 group. In the mRNA-1273 cohort, R848 stimulation elicited a significant increase in IL-6 production on Day 28 that persisted through Day 180 (Fig. 4A), with a comparable sustained IL-6 response observed following LPS stimulation. TNFα production showed divergent kinetics; Pam3Csk4 stimulation induced a sustained TNFα response through Day 180 post-vaccination with mRNA-1273, whereas R848-induced TNFα peaked on Day 28 and subsequently declined (Fig. 4C). Additionally, Pam3Csk4 stimulation resulted in a significant increase in CXCL1 levels on Day 28 that persisted until Day 180 (Fig. 4F). Although Curdlan stimulation only modestly elevated CXCL1 levels on Day 90 post-vaccination, it was notably effective in inducing IFNγ production, with a persistent increase observed through Day 180 (Fig. 4D). Similarly, CCL2 production was enhanced by Curdlan stimulation, although its levels gradually decreased by Day 180 (Fig. 4E). While LPS stimulation maintained a stable CCL2 response in the mRNA-1273 group throughout the observation period, the same stimulus elicited a significant decrease in CCL2 levels in the BNT162b2 group (Fig. 5E). This marked reduction in CCL2 production was the most conspicuous difference in the BNT162b2 cohort, as other stimulants produced negligible or no significant responses following BNT162b2 vaccination (Fig. 5).

A direct comparison of immune responses between the two vaccine groups revealed significant differences, with participants receiving mRNA-1273 exhibiting consistently higher cytokine and chemokine production than those vaccinated with BNT162b2 (Fig. 6). IL-6 production (Fig. 6A) was significantly elevated in the mRNA-1273 group on Day 28 following stimulation with LPS, R848, and Pam3Csk4, although this difference remained stable only for R848 stimulation up to Day 180. A similar pattern was observed for TNFα production (Fig. 6C), which remained higher following R848 stimulation from Day 28 to Day 180 in the mRNA-1273 group. In contrast, the chemokine CCL2 (Fig. 6E) displayed an inverse pattern, with consistently higher levels in the mRNA-1273 group following stimulation with LPS and Pam3Csk4, rather than with R848. Similarly, CXCL1 production (Fig. 6F) remained elevated in the mRNA-1273 compared to the BNT162b2 group from Day 28 to Day 180 following Pam3Csk4 stimulation. However, this difference was also present at baseline (Day 0), suggesting it may not be solely attributable to vaccination. No significant differences were observed between vaccine groups for IL-10 (Fig. 6B) or IFNγ (Fig. 6D) at any timepoint.

Discussion

In this study, we leveraged the ENFORCE biobank of samples [32, 33] to explore potential induction of trained immunity in individuals vaccinated with mRNA vaccines, specifically mRNA-1273 and BNT162b2. Our primary aim was to determine whether these mRNA vaccines can elicit markers of trained immunity and whether they differ between the two vaccine types. Although much of the research on trained immunity has focused on live-attenuated vaccines such as BCG [36], earlier studies have demonstrated the induction of trained immunity markers in individuals vaccinated with the adenoviral vaccine ChAdOx1 nCoV-19 (AstraZeneca) [24], whereas studies conducted primarily on the BNT162b2 has indicated innate tolerance particularly to viral ligands [29–31]. Our analyses focused on immune cell responses, particularly monocyte populations and cytokine production following TLR ligand stimulation, as indicators of a potential trained immunity response.

Given the well-established mechanistic framework of trained immunity, including epigenetic and metabolic reprogramming of innate immune cells and their progenitors [13, 37, 38] and the unavailability of samples suitable for direct epigenetic profiling, our study focused on evaluating the downstream functional consequences of trained immunity following stimulation. As defined by Netea [13] trained immunity represents “the long-term functional reprogramming of innate immune cells, which is evoked by exogenous or endogenous insults, and which leads to an altered response towards a second challenge after the return to a non-activated state.”

In our cohort, we observed consistent alterations in cytokine and chemokine production following ex vivo stimulation at multiple post-vaccination time points, relative to baseline. These findings indicate a sustained alteration in the functional phenotype of the innate immune system. Importantly, as all follow-up assessments were conducted ≥ 28 days after vaccination, well beyond the circulating half-life of monocytes (typically 2–7 days), we infer that these responses reflect a reprogrammed state established after the resolution of the initial immune activation. Accordingly, we apply the term trained immunity in the interpretation and discussion of our results, as the two key criteria: (1) return to a homeostatic/non-activated state, and (2) altered responsiveness upon secondary stimulation, are both satisfied.

Monocytes are key mediators of trained immunity despite their relatively short half-life of 5–7 days [13]. Indeed, induction of long-term changes at the level of myelopoiesis is a hallmark of trained innate immunity. Previous investigations have shown transient upregulation of intermediate monocytes at specific time points following vaccination with Pfizer’s BNT162b2. For example, Arunachalam [39] observed an increase in intermediate monocytes two days post-vaccination (after both the first and second doses) with levels returning to baseline by day 7. In contrast, Saresella [40] reported that both classical and intermediate monocytes were upregulated up to seven days following the first dose of BNT162b2, with classical monocytes remaining elevated even one month post-vaccination. Moreover, Murphy [24] demonstrated that the monocyte CD14 marker remained significantly elevated up to 83 days post-vaccination with the adenoviral ChAdOx1 nCoV-19. However, we observed no differences in the composition of monocyte subpopulations in our dataset. This lack of difference may be attributable to sampling outside of the critical window, given the dynamic and short-lived nature of these cells. It is also important to note that trained immunity is also induced in hematopoietic stem cells, contributing to long-term innate immune memory [13], and while epigenetic modifications in monocytes following BNT162b2 vaccination have been reported [27], these modifications do not appear to be sustained over the long term.

To further investigate trained immunity markers, we stimulated PBMCs with TLR ligands and measured the cytokine and chemokine responses using ELISA. Although the use of total PBMC populations does not allow for the precise assignment of functional responses to individual immune cell subsets, this approach provides a broader and integrative perspective on the overall induction of trained immunity. We focused on cytokines such as IL-6 and TNFα, which are commonly recognized as primary biomarkers of trained immunity [13, 41, 42], and on the chemokine CCL2, an important chemoattractant for monocytes [43, 44]. IL-6 is a pleiotropic cytokine with a critical role in initiating inflammation and acting as an endogenous pyrogen. As such, it is an early mediator of the inflammatory response to a diverse range of insults, including bacterial and viral infections [45]. Our results revealed no significant changes in IL-6 production in the BNT162b2 group, aligning with recent studies that also reported no effect of the BNT162b2 vaccine on IL-6 production [28–31, 46, 47]. Notably, only one study has shown significant upregulation of IL-6 (persisting for up to 8 months) in the BNT162b2 vaccinated group after stimulation of PBMC with M. tuberculosis [48]. A similar IL-6 upregulation was observed in individuals vaccinated with ChAdOx1 nCoV-19, persisting until Day-56 in response to LPS stimulation [24, 46]. In our study, we found a comparable pattern among individuals vaccinated with mRNA-1273, where IL-6 levels increased by Day-28 and remained elevated through Day-180.

Enhanced TNFα production upon secondary stimulation is also associated with trained immunity, as observed after BCG vaccination, intranasal adenovirus vector vaccination, or by β-glucan [49–51]. TNFα, a pro-inflammatory cytokine predominantly produced by monocytes and macrophages, is rapidly induced in response to pathogens. Similar to IL-6, our data revealed no significant changes in TNFα levels after ex-vivo restimulation in the BNT162b2 group—a finding that is consistent with several recent studies [28–31, 46, 47]. In contrast, the mRNA-1273 group demonstrated a significant upregulation of TNFα production capacity, which persisted until Day-180 after stimulation with Pam3Csk4. A similar effect was reported by Nakayama [52] in participants vaccinated with mRNA-1273, where TNFα production remained elevated for up to three months, even in the absence of additional stimulation. Conversely, studies on adenoviral vaccines such as ChAdOx1 nCoV-19 have shown a decrease in TNFα production following PBMC stimulation [24, 31, 46].

The role of CCL2—a chemokine crucial for monocyte migration and infiltration—is less clear. Studies on CCL2 activation following vaccination with BNT162b2 are conflicting. A murine study reported strong upregulation of CCL2 after immunization [53], although this study did not include further cell stimulation. In contrast, Hellgren [28] described stable, low levels of CCL2 production following stimulation with either LPS or R848 in human participants vaccinated with BNT162b2. Similarly, among those vaccinated with ChAdOx1 nCoV-19, some studies have noted upregulation [31] while others have observed a downregulation [24] of CCL2. In our experiments, the BNT162b2 group showed a steady decrease in CCL2 levels after stimulation with R848, whereas the mRNA-1273 group maintained stable levels. A statistically significant increase was observed after stimulation with LPS or Curdlan in the mRNA-1273 group. These contradictory findings highlight the need for further research to clarify the effects of vaccination on CCL2 dynamics.

Overall, two major trends emerged from our findings. First, the immune response following stimulation with R848 was significantly stronger than with other stimuli. This observation is likely related to the intrinsic adjuvant activity of mRNA vaccines [3], which, despite the absence of added adjuvants, stimulate TLR3, TLR7, and TLR8 pathways—key receptors for single-stranded RNA [26]. The level of TLR pathway stimulation depends on the type of RNA used in the vaccine. Naturally occurring RNA can strongly stimulate the immune response, but certain RNA modifications or different sources of RNA can impact this stimulation [54]. The most prominent modification enhancing the efficacy of mRNA vaccine used in both approved COVID-19 mRNA vaccines is pseudouridine modification [55]. The incorporation of pseudouridine in mRNA vaccines diminished the antiviral immune response against the introduced mRNA while significantly increasing vaccine efficacy against symptomatic disease, from 48% to over 90% [56]. Therefore, the activation of TLR3/7/8, which contributes to the adjuvant effect of mRNA vaccines, is likely not caused by the mRNA itself but rather by the use of ionizable lipids and lipidoids in the LNP delivery system [57–60]. Since R848 activates both TLR7 and TLR8, the robust response observed in our study may reflect not only the intrinsic potency of this stimulus but also an overlap with vaccine-induced signaling pathways or modulation of responsiveness following vaccination. Although this response cannot be strictly defined as trained immunity but more as immune priming [61], as it is triggered by a related stimulus, it could nevertheless enhance protection against pathogens engaging the same receptors.

Second, our data consistently showed that the mRNA-1273 vaccine induced a stronger immune response compared to BNT162b2 after stimulation. Previous studies have also indicated that mRNA-1273 produces a more robust antibody response [7–10], and our findings confirm this trend. Although both vaccines utilize the same mRNA sequence for antigen production, several factors may contribute to the observed differences. Notably, the recommended dose for BNT162b2 is 30 µg per injection [11], which is approximately one-third of the 100 µg dose used for mRNA-1273 [12]. Additionally, while both vaccines employ lipid nanoparticles for mRNA delivery, the composition of these nanoparticles differs. For instance, Gote [62] demonstrated that the proprietary lipids in the mRNA-1273 (SM-102) formulation [63] are about three times more efficient at delivering mRNA compared to ALC-0315 used in BNT162b2 [64]. Similar results that mRNA-1237 perform better in intramuscular delivery of mRNA and antibody production were reported by Zhang [65]. Moreover, several other factors can influence the efficacy of mRNA vaccines, including the 5’ cap method and structure used [66], the length and composition of the 5’ and 3’ untranslated regions [65], and inefficient purification during manufacturing, which may leave residual DNA templates in the final product [66]. These variations, along with other potential factors, could contribute to the differences in immune responses observed.

In summary, our study provides evidence that mRNA vaccination, with the mRNA-1273 platform, induces measurable long-term changes in innate immune responses that align with the concept of trained immunity. In contrast, no such effects were observed for the BNT162b2 vaccine. Despite the relatively small cohort size (n = 12), we detected this statistically robust differences between the vaccine groups. While the limited sample size may restrict the generalizability of our findings, it is noteworthy that similar observations have been reported in studies with comparable or even smaller cohorts: for example, Murphy [24] (n = 10) following adenoviral vaccination, and Stevens [46] (n = 13). The observed differences in cytokine production and monocyte activation underscore the importance of vaccine formulation, including mRNA dose and lipid nanoparticle composition, in shaping the immune response. These findings have significant implications for the design of next-generation vaccines. By harnessing the intrinsic adjuvant properties of mRNA and optimizing delivery systems, future vaccines may not only induce robust protection against their target pathogens but also offer cross-protection against a broader range of infectious agents.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrates that mRNA-1273 induces measurable changes in innate immune responses consistent with long-term induction of trained immunity. In contrast, there were observed no such effects following the BNT162b2 vaccination. While no significant monocyte alterations were detected, cytokine analyses revealed a stronger and more sustained response in the mRNA-1273 group. These differences may be attributed to variations in mRNA dose and lipid nanoparticle composition. Our findings highlight the potential of optimizing vaccine formulations to enhance immune responses, offering insights for next-generation vaccine development that could provide broader protection beyond the target pathogen.
