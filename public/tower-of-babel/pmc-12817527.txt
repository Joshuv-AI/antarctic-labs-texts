“Majority of the time, I know I’m right”: understanding the decisions behind persistently high ED utilization among patients with substance use disorders
Authors: Marik Moen, J. David Gatz, Benoit Stryckman, Justin Wing, Kate Dunn, Aditi Ringwala, Shannon Harrington, Sanyukta Deshmukh, Julie Kurek, Aaron Greenblatt, Daniel B. Gingold
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients with persistently high emergency department (ED) utilization (10 + visits in two consecutive years) represent a unique and complex population. We sought to understand drivers of persistently high ED use among individuals with substance use disorders (SUD) to optimize care delivery and improve health for this population.MethodsThis mixed-methods study examines healthcare utilization and decision-making among patients in an opioid treatment program with a co-located primary care clinic within one mile of the ED in a Mid-Atlantic urban academic health system. Quantitative data were extracted from health records to assess utilization, comorbidities, and health-related social needs. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with a subset of 12 patients and thematic analyses explored motivations for ED use.ResultsAmong 454 identified patients, 19 met criteria for persistently high ED use with a mean of 23 ED visits and average $35,677 ED charges per patient. Cohort patients had multiple chronic conditions and unmet social needs: 63% had cardiovascular disease; 74% had hepatitis C; 84% had non-psychotic mental illness, and > 50% experienced financial and housing insecurity or social isolation. Qualitative analysis identified key factors influencing ED use, including perceived illness severity, convenience, interpersonal interactions with healthcare providers, the impact of SUD, and unmet social needs. Most patients preferred outpatient care for routine health management, yet described barriers to this care, including limited access and unfamiliarity with available services. Patients experienced stigma in hospital settings, yet they viewed the ED as the most logical option for their care needs.ConclusionPatients with SUD and persistently high ED utilization often seek emergency care due to a perceived need for immediate access to complex interventions, reflecting similar motivations to those of the broader ED patient population. Despite primary care engagement, patients face intersecting medical, behavioral, mental health, and social challenges that interfere with managing complex chronic conditions and influence healthcare-seeking behaviors. Our findings can inform quality improvement efforts to strengthen care for similar populations, such as removing barriers to outpatient services and addressing SUD stigma. Policy and system-level interventions targeting structural determinants—such as healthcare models, payment structures, social isolation, and poverty—are needed to better support this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13829-9.

Introduction

People who have persistently high emergency department (ED) utilization, often defined as four or more ED visits in two consecutive years, have unique characteristics and needs, even compared to patients with high ED use that does not persist over multiple years [1–3]. Perhaps unsurprisingly, the most common predictive factor identified for persistent high ED use is a history of high ED use in the prior year [2–6]. Other common factors include high prevalence of chronic conditions, multiple comorbidities [1–4, 7, 8], and poverty [1–5, 7, 9]. Studies have also found associations with frequent ED use and conditions of homelessness, mental health and substance use [1, 5–11].

Within the broader group of individuals with frequent ED visits, a subset known as “super-utilizers” is often defined as those making 10 or more ED visits per year [1, 11]. These patients typically have complex healthcare needs, including multiple chronic conditions or functional limitations, and they experience higher mortality rates while incurring significant healthcare costs [6, 11, 12].

People with substance use disorders (SUDs) have higher recurrent ED use compared to the general population and account for 2–3% of ED visits nationally [8]. To improve care and address the high burdens on human health and life [6], and health systems (high costs, crowding, and staff moral distress [13, 14]), there is a clear need to better understand persistently high ED users, especially those with SUD.

Our study provides insight into the experiences of people with persistently high ED visits (10 + visits in two consecutive years) at a Mid-Atlantic, urban academic hospital who also have substance use disorders, especially opioid use disorders, and are empaneled in an outpatient Opioid Treatment Program (OTP) with onsite primary care clinic and wraparound services. We share the results of qualitative interviews exploring patient reasons for seeking healthcare at the ED and barriers to increasing use of primary care outpatient services. We also describe health services utilization patterns and medical, psychiatric, and social comorbidities for this complex patient population.

The aim of this study is to develop a deeper understanding of conditions, experiences, and factors that influence high ED utilization to inform interventions to optimize ED and hospital usage.

Methods

Study setting

Our study setting includes an urban academic ED in a Mid-Atlantic City and an OTP with a co-located comprehensive primary care clinic, the Health and Recovery Practice (HARP) within the same healthcare system, less than 1 mile apart. HARP is one of a few such programs nationwide, offering infectious disease, psychiatry, psychology, and harm reduction services in addition to adult primary preventative and acute (urgent) care services [15]. HARP seeks to offer holistic care services including a DropIn Center with community health worker support, snacks/coffee, transportation assistance to primary and specialty care, nurse care coordination, and wellness group activities.

Study design

This mixed-methods study integrates quantitative and qualitative data to gain a deeper understanding of persistent high ED use among our sub-population to inform actionable improvements in patient care. This study constitutes part of the assessment phase of a quality improvement (QI) initiative collaboratively conducted in 2023–2024 by an ED and an outpatient substance use treatment program. The University of Maryland, Baltimore IRB classified this as non-human subjects research for QI purposes (HP-00104636).

Participants and sampling

The study population included patients from the OTP between October 1, 2020, and September 30, 2021 (see Fig. 1). Mutual patients, defined as clinic patients having at least one ED or hospital encounter within our partner hospital system, were identified in CRISP (Chesapeake Regional Information System for our Patients), Maryland’s health information exchange (HIE) system, and clinic enrollment records.

We initially attempted to engage patients (via direct clinic outreach and via phone) over 8 months. We were unable to reach some patients due to inactive phone numbers, or no outreach responses, or patients who were deceased. To gain more insights from patients like those in the original query results, we then identified a convenience sample of opioid clinic patients who had similar high use patterns (10 + ED or inpatient visits over 2 consecutive years) during October 2021-September 2023. This convenience sample was combined with the initial data query to identify patients eligible for quantitative analysis and invitation to interview. (Fig. 1)

Data collection and measures

Information for all 19 patients exhibiting persistent extremely high ED use was obtained via manual chart review (Epic platform) and abstraction to a standardized form to record data for analysis. Patient information on demographics, common diagnoses related to hospital and primary care visits, medical comorbidities, and documented health-related social needs (HRSN) was gathered from the HIE and Epic. Data were gathered from different sections of the patient’s chart such as the Problem List, Epic Social Determinants of Health (SDOH) screening tool (comprised of validated and unvalidated SDOH measures), and free text searching for medical, mental health, and HRSN conditions. The HIE was used to quantify healthcare utilization frequencies (ED visits and hospitalizations) and associated health system financial charges during the study period. Six people (JW, DG, JDG, SG, JK, SH) conducted abstraction. Study team members performing abstraction were not blinded by the study hypothesis, although they were blinded to qualitative data at the time of abstraction. The lead author (MM) conducted review of data of 10% of records for reliability of consistent findings. Any abstraction discrepancies were discussed and resolved in regular team meetings.

Patients meeting interview eligibility criteria were invited to participate in a voluntary ~ 30-minute interview to share their insights. Patient participants received $50 upon completion of the interview. Qualitative data were gathered using semi-structured interviews, which included questions exploring patient-reported factors as to why they sought care in the hospital versus the outpatient setting, whether social- (e.g., shelter, food) or addiction-related conditions influenced where they sought healthcare and what factors might influence them to seek more care in an outpatient setting. (See Interview guide in supplemental materials.) Interviews were recorded via Zoom or Microsoft Word dictation feature to capture voice-to-text transcription. Interview recordings and transcripts were stored on secure servers. Interview recordings were then reviewed by two study team members to edit and assure transcript accuracy, or, in the case of four long interviews, a professional transcriptionist reviewed recordings and provided transcripts.

Analysis

Quantitative and qualitative analysis

Chart review and HIE data were analyzed via descriptive statistics. Data from the interviews underwent a two-phase qualitative analysis.

Thematic analysis

Data collection and analysis occurred concurrently. Two study team members reviewed transcripts and audio files to perform thematic and inductive coding of the transcripts (MM, KD). After the initial 6 interviews, we determined we had not reached saturation, and we extended our efforts to include patients with more recent persistent use. We achieved saturation after 12 interviews, no new themes emerged and all categories were well-developed, consistent with standards for thematic saturation in qualitative research [16]. Next, codes and transcripts were further reviewed (by MM, KD, JW) via deductive analysis, and codes and aligning illustrative quotes were placed in an Excel spreadsheet al.igned with the categories of the interview questions for framework analysis. Then, the original reviewers and two other team members (DG, JDG) reviewed the codes, quotes and assessed corresponding patterns and themes applying a descriptive qualitative approach to interpreting the meaning and context of the data. The emerging themes were reviewed by other study team members (BS, SH). Team members met to discuss and resolve areas of diverging opinions, settling on revisions and regroupings of the emerging themes into the final major themes and subthemes. A descriptive qualitative approach was applied to interpret data meaning and context.

Reflexivity statement

The study team have clinical experiences that influence their viewpoints, including working with patients in outpatient addiction treatment and primary care (MM, KD, SH, AG, and AR) and in the ED (JDG, DBG, SD, JK), as a health economist (BS) with experience in healthcare services research of this population, and as a medical student (JW) without clinical experience of addictions or emergency care.

Results

Quantitative findings

Out of 988 patients empaneled in the clinic, 454 were identified in the HIE as having a hospital (ED or hospital admission) encounter within our hospital during the study period (See Fig. 1). Of those, 418 (92%) patients had ED visits in the study period (336 in first year and 332 in second year, as shown by the dark and light bars respectively in Fig. 2). As demonstrated in the distribution of frequencies of ED visits across both years of the study in Figs. 2 and 95 patients (28.3%) in year 1 and 121 (36.4%) in year 2 had 4 + visits/year (defined as high ED utilizers). Seventeen patients (5.1%) and 22 patients (6.6%) have ≥ 10 visits in one period. Finally, 13 patients had persistent extremely high ED utilization, with ≥ 10 visits per year in both years.

The prevalence of common medical conditions among this cohort --as documented in ED/hospital encounters during the 12-month period prior to interview date– are listed in Table 3. These conditions include skin/soft tissue infections, alcohol and substance use complications or withdrawal, respiratory complications, chest pain, and abdominal pain. Nine patients (47%) had life-threatening events (e.g., overdose, respiratory failure, sepsis, suicide attempt).

Over 70% of patients also had > 3 chronic comorbidities including cardiovascular conditions (hypertension and CHF), diabetes, and respiratory conditions (asthma, COPD), infections (HIV and hepatitis C), chronic wounds, and mental health conditions (depression, anxiety, bipolar disorder.). Common reasons for outpatient visits included opioid and alcohol use disorder, chest pain, and management of chronic conditions such as wounds, edema, diabetes, HIV, and hypertension.

All patients had an opioid use disorder, many had poly-substance use, and 95% of patients received methadone treatment for opioid use disorder as they are current/former patients of the methadone clinic. Eighteen of the 19 in the cohort had primary care encounters and 11 of the 12 interviewed patients were engaged in primary care: 6 at HARP and 5 outside of HARP.

Qualitative findings

We interviewed 12 patient participants between March 2023 and June 2024; they identified as male [8], female [3]; non-Hispanic Black/African American [5] and non-Hispanic white [7]. Patients were aged 29 to 60 years old at the time of interview, with a mean age of 42. Five major qualitative themes were identified – factors influencing when, where, and why the interviewed participant patients sought care and descriptions of their experiences in seeking care. Images depicting qualitative themes are presented in Fig. 3. A quote elucidating each theme are displayed in Table 5.

Severity of the condition: I Know When It’s Bad

The prevailing theme of why patients chose to seek care in the ED was the perceived severity of their conditions. Patients referenced symptom intensity (e.g., systemic infections with fevers, persistent nausea and vomiting, and severe pain) as driving their decision to seek care in the ED- and they thought they were acting logically. For example, one patient shared they thought they were doing what they were supposed to do in seeking care for a severe condition at the hospital (IN3 Table 5, Line 1) and that they generally knew when things were severe enough to necessitate going to the hospital (IN3 Table 5, Lines 2–5).

Two subthemes related to exacerbation of chronic conditions and acute-on-chronic pain were identified. Patients described going to the hospital for aggravations of pre-existing wounds, COPD, infections, prior injuries, and gastroparesis. Patients described episodes of acute pain, often an exacerbation of chronic pain syndromes, that were so severe they needed to go to the ED to alleviate this pain. Patients described a third subtheme of how they do not prefer to be in the hospital and only go when they feel their condition becomes severe. A patient described chronice pain and chronic injury were driving care seeking only if it got to the point of being too bad, feeling like it was an emergency (IN6, Table 5, Line 6–8).

Immediacy and convenience of access to appropriate care: The Right Place for Right Care, Right Away

Convenient access to appropriate care was another major theme of why patients sought care in the ED. More specifically, patients emphasized how the ED offers access to the right care, or the appropriate level of care, and the resources and expertise that they felt were necessary for their conditions. They shared that these resources are often only immediately available at the hospital. Two initial key subthemes included proximity and efficiency (e.g., time to be seen). Patients reported that patients often go to the ED because it is conveniently close and accessible (either by foot or public transportation). One patient stated explicitly, it’s about proximity- the hospital is just the closest point of care (IN-1 Table 5, Line 9) Respondents also described how long they will have to wait to be seen once they arrive at the facility when choosing between local emergency departments. These perceptions were based on prior experience and word of mouth (Table 5 Line 11).

Patients described two additional and related subthemes - availability of appropriate care, services, and medication. One patient described needing hospital-level interventions for a serious condition where they were initially discharged from the ED and then called back for admission (Table 5, Line 12). Patients also described having access to certain treatments (e.g., imaging, surgery, labs) and access to appropriate expertise. Further, specific medications may not be readily available at outpatient clinics or pharmacies, while hospitals offered easy, immediate access to the medications they needed (Table 5 Lines 10, 13, 14, 15).

Inter-personal interactions: The Vibes

Patients described the attitudes of staff and perceived treatment by providers as well as the familiarity between patients and the healthcare team as important. Patients expressed mixed feelings about using the ED, frustration with ED wait times, describing poor treatment at times, feel judged or misunderstood by the medical team especially related to pain and opioid use, and their knowledge of their own condition is often underestimated (Table 5, Lines 16–18).

Despite these challenges, patients also expressed gratitude toward hospitals that consistently deliver compassionate, high-quality care, highlighting positive experiences at some institutions over others. Respondents reported prioritizing hospitals where staff are familiar with their medical history and provide appropriate treatments (Table 5 line 24). For example, a patient said they choose a hospital facility specifically based on staff attitudes, where staff don’t treat you terrible (Table 5 Line 19). Patients further described some hospital staff always trying to make patients feel comfortable, connecting them to vital services to address their complex needs. (Table 5 Lines 20–21).

Similarly, patients expressed valuing outpatient care due to trust and rapport with primary care clinic providers and positive experiences with staff, including feeling like the clinic was a safe haven where they feel welcome and safe (Table 5, Line 22).

Impact of substance use disorders: It’s Like Groundhog’s Day

Patients were divided in their views of the role of substance use disorders on their healthcare seeking behaviors. Frequently, patients stated that SUD did not affect their hospital/ED use, that addiction would make no difference on their healthcare utilization (Table 5, Lines 25–26).

Others described that drug use affects their health and healthcare utilization is a major way, especially related to chronic wounds, mental health, and homelessness. They described the experience of addiction and being in poor health and having to be in a hospital as a vicious and lonely cycle they are trapped in (Table 5 Lines 27–28).

Certain patients focused on how the hospital team manages SUD and how they treat people who use drugs. This identified a third subtheme of managing withdrawal. Patients described delays in managing withdrawal (especially opioids) and judgmental attitudes of healthcare providers about their need for pain medications or methadone. They emphasized that hospitals could to more to establish systems for preventing withdrawal or providing more immediate relief for persons experiencing withdrawal. Patients shared that enhanced approaches to managing withdrawal and persons with SUD may improve the experience for patients and staff alike, as patients would feel better, not be as irritable, and may not leave the hospital without completing treatment (Table 5 Lines 29–31).

Health-related social and psycho-social needs: From Sidewalk to Palace, I Feel all Alone

Patients reported multiple unmet social needs like insufficient income, food, shelter, and transportation. Patients described their living conditions as having a significant impact on their health. A patient acknowledged that an emergency room could be a source of shelter, with a mutual understanding between staff and the patient about what needed to be said in order for the hospital to let them stay (Table 5, Line 33). A lack of hygiene for managing wounds while being homeless was shared as an insight into why they would get sick and need to go to the hospital (Table 5 Line 32). Food insecurity was noted as a barrier to managing health and chronic conditions, especially for patients with diabetes, congestive heart failure, HIV, and constipation (Table 5, Lines 34–35). Transportation was cited as a barrier to get to other care settings and frequently interacted with the theme of proximity as a driver of ED use (Table 5 Line 36).

Other respondents reported that their social/living situations did not influence their healthcare seeking behavior, that even if they lived in a “palace,” they would still have the same healthcare seeking behaviors. (Table 5, Lines 37–38).

Finally, patients described feelings of social isolation and emotional health related to stress, anxiety, and grief [17]. Respondents reported being alone all day, and many patients had lost people who were close to them, affecting their physical and mental health (Table 5 Lines 39–40).

Discussion

This mixed-methods study integrated quantitative data from medical records on health status and healthcare utilization with qualitative data from patient interviews to explore the drivers of persistently high emergency department (ED) use among patients with substance use disorders (SUD) engaged in a co-located opioid treatment and primary care clinic. Through thematic analysis, we identified key motivations for ED use, including a perceived need for immediate and appropriate complex medical care. Despite regular engagement in outpatient care, study patients faced overlapping medical, behavioral, and social challenges that complicated chronic disease management and influenced their care-seeking behavior.

First, similar to national data, our patients demonstrate high levels of medical and mental health comorbidities, and high levels of poverty, housing and food insecurity, and social isolation [18, 19]. These findings align with prior research showing individuals with chronic conditions and who are poor are higher users of ED care. Peppe et al. (2007) note that 84% of chronic high ED users have chronic health conditions (rates similar to our cohort), and that people < 200% of the Federal Poverty Level (FPL) have 2.5 greater relative risk of high ED use relative to those >200% of the FPL [18]. At an average age of 40, many cohort patients have lived decades in poverty, experienced low educational attainment, endured intergenerational trauma and abuse, and struggled with decades of substance use disorder. These factors significantly limit opportunities to live a healthy life and resources for engaging in longitudinal primary or specialty care, making frequent ED use a most logical option for managing their health.

Second, individuals prioritize the perceived severity of their condition when deciding where to seek emergency care. [19] In qualitative findings, patients reported going to the ED due to the need for immediate care of urgent conditions or the perceived seriousness of their condition (including acute medical issues, pain, chronic disease exacerbations, injuries, mental health crises) and lack of access to readily available primary care. These findings validate prior research showing that patients with high ED use seek care for the same reasons as the general population: perceived severe conditions needing immediate and expert care [20–23]. Our data highlight that patients’ primary priority is to feel better as quickly as possible.

Third, patients described convenience and expedience as common drivers for seeking healthcare services. Patients desire convenient, timely, personalized, expert, and compassionate care, which they reported receiving at specific hospitals and outpatient clinics. Contrary to assumptions, patients were actively enrolled in primary care practices but still used the ED because it provided what they needed at the moment. Scholarship in this area notes that high users of ED care also high users of outpatient services [18, 24] and most interviewed patients reported regular engagement with their primary care practitioners describing strong, trusting relationships with them. In general, the patients knew how to get in touch with and get care from their primary care office. Many reported they would not choose to spend time in the ED if they did not have a need to be there.

Transportation, hours of operations and not having necessary services or medications in primary care settings were described as barriers (in addition, some patients reported lack of awareness regarding available outpatient resources). While some outpatient care clinics provide rideshare services, this transportation is not widely available, and then often only for regularly scheduled appointments. That is how, at the time that patients perceive the need to seek care, the ED may be the most convenient location based on hours, location, and range of service provided. These perceptions align with common justifications for ED use in the literature, with many patients not wanting to be there but knowing the language to stay to get the desired services [25].

We describe inconsistencies in patient responses in many categories, reinforcing that persons with substance use disorders who frequently seek care in the ED are not monolithic in their experiences or perceptions. Even as each person in this study has opioid use disorder, yet many did not see substance use as having major effects on their health and healthcare seeking. Perhaps this is not surprising, as people with addictions may have compromised awareness of symptoms of their disorder, and lack of insights into the effect of SUD on behaviors and/or compliance with care and treatment [26, 27].

Perhaps an unexpected inconsistency is our findings related to how participants described the effects of social conditions, especially housing, on health and healthcare. Many respondents did not perceive housing insecurity or homelessness to influence their health or healthcare seeking behaviors. Yet, some patients in this cohort who often faced housing insecurity, expressed impatience with other patients whom they perceived to be seeking ED care for less urgent matters, including social needs like homelessness (Table 5 Lines 11, 16) [28]. These feelings of impatience and frustration may mirror the stigma and judgment they themselves encountered from healthcare providers regarding their frequent ED use and substance use history [25, 28, 29]. Such experiences underscore the broader issue of stigma within healthcare settings, which can discourage individuals from seeking care, contribute to feelings of alienation, and sometimes lead patients to leave the hospital prematurely against medical advice [25, 29].

Our findings align with Billings & Raven [7] who challenge the misconception that frequent ED users are primarily individuals with behavioral health conditions or non-urgent needs [7, 18]. In reality, many frequent ED users carry a substantial disease burden and engage significantly in primary and specialty care services [7, 18]. Notably, many patients were actively engaged in substance use and SUD treatment but lacked insight into the impact of their own substance use on health, healthcare needs, and health services utilization patterns [30]. Some study patients disengaged from care during the study period while others faced fatal outcomes, highlighting the importance of maintaining support services until individuals can reduce substance use and address broader health needs [7, 20].

Limitations

Our findings are based on a small, single-site sample of patients with SUD and persistently high ED use who have access to primary care at the site of their addiction treatment program. The sample size was sufficient to achieve saturation in qualitative data. Patients in this sample have high levels of comorbid conditions and unmet social needs. While generalizability is limited, the sample is representative of other patients with persistently high levels of ED use. The selection of this unique sample demonstrates that patients may continue to use ED at high levels despite access to primary care, aligning with prior work identifying that high ED users are not obtaining medical services exclusively at the ED but also utilize outpatient services at a greater rate than low ED users [18].

Finally, it is important to note that despite averaging 23 visits in a 12-month period, many patient participants reported that frequent hospital visits were not ideal. Patients described discomfort with ED conditions and feeling judged for their substance use or frequent ED utilization. This stigmatization can discourage patients from seeking care, feeling like they don’t receive quality care, or prompt them to leave the hospital prematurely—sometimes against medical advice.

The experiences shared by these patients highlight the need for compassionate, high-quality, and stigma-free care across hospital and outpatient settings. The findings underscore an urgent need for better-coordinated care systems between the ED and outpatient SUD and primary care practices. They also point to the importance of enhanced, targeted outpatient resources, and tailored outreach and education for individuals with complex health and social needs. Efforts must also focus on addressing root barriers such as poverty, housing instability, and social isolation. Policies should shift from penalizing hospitals for frequent care use to incentivizing sustained engagement, coordination, and high-quality care across settings. Healthcare systems and providers should adopt integrated care models to ensure continuous and holistic care, addressing both medical and social needs with patient-centered approaches that respect individuals’ immediate needs and preferences.

Future research should focus on longitudinal studies to track long-term outcomes of patients with high ED utilization and SUD, evaluating the effectiveness of various interventions aimed at reducing ED visits and improving outpatient care. Expanding research to include larger and more diverse populations can help generalize findings and develop strategies applicable across different settings and demographics. These implications can guide quality improvement projects, inform policy revisions, and shape future research to enhance care for patients with SUD and multiple chronic comorbidities, ultimately prolonging health and longevity.

Conclusion

Patients with persistently high emergency department utilization often seek care in this setting because they seek immediate access to address perceived severe conditions that may require complex healthcare interventions. The intersection of complex medical, behavioral, and social factors complicates patients’ ability to manage chronic health conditions and contributes to health and healthcare-seeking behaviors. Prioritizing policies to address social isolation and poverty while reducing stigma and improving services related to substance use disorders and better integration of care across settings may help patients stabilize or improve their health status. Engaging this patient population to inform quality improvement strategies may facilitate uptake of relevant services in outpatient and inpatient settings and strengthen the healthcare system’s ability to provide high-quality, efficient care.
