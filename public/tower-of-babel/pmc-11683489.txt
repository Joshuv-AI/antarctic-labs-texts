The Effect of Water Co-adsorption on the Adsorption of Formaldehyde in Fe-HHTP-MOF Metal–Organic Materials
Authors: Sanjiang Bie, Siyu Nie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBecause of the existence of moisture in indoor air, it is still a serious challenge to capture formaldehyde indoors with the metal–organic material Fe-HHTP-MOF. To explore the relationship between the structure and performance of Fe-HHTP-MOF in dry and humid air, molecular dynamics simulation was used to study the adsorption amount of Fe-HHTP-MOF for formaldehyde and water under different temperatures and adsorption pressures, as well as the adsorption amount of Fe-HHTP-MOF for formaldehyde in the presence of both water and formaldehyde, and the differences in adsorption of formaldehyde and water by Fe-HHTP-MOF were compared and analyzed when water coexisted. The results show that under single-component isothermal adsorption, the hydrogen bond energy formed by Fe-HHTP-MOF adsorbing H2O molecules is much greater than the van der Waals energy formed by adsorbing HCHO molecules. In a dry state, as the temperature increases, the adsorption amount of HCHO molecules decreases. When the temperature rises to 313.15 K, even if the temperature is further increased, the effect on the adsorption amount of HCHO molecules is small. Moreover, after the adsorption pressure is greater than 7.78 MPa, the adsorption amount of HCHO molecules tends to flatten. When H2O molecules coexist, at the same adsorption pressure and temperature, H2O molecules with polar functional groups preferentially occupy the adsorption sites, and the adsorption amount of HCHO molecules in the presence of H2O is lower than that in the dry state. As the temperature increases, under the same adsorption pressure, the intermolecular interaction strengthens, and the molecular activity increases. The influence of water molecules on the adsorption of HCHO by Fe-HHTP-MOF weakens, and HCHO molecules reach the saturated adsorption site ahead of time. The adsorption energy of H2O is greater than that of HCHO, indicating that the presence of water may hinder the capture of HCHO.

Introduction

According to relevant data from indoor air monitoring, more than 90% of newly decorated rooms in our country have serious problems with harmful gas pollution.1 Even at low concentrations, VOCs have a strong risk of carcinogenesis and teratogenicity. Consequently, the World Health Organization has classified it as a carcinogen and a significant indoor air pollutant.2

Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) are porous crystalline materials constructed by connecting metal center atoms with organic groups. Due to their high porosity, large specific surface area, and versatile physicochemical structures, MOFs have applications in gas adsorption, storage, separation, and catalysis.3,4 Many MOFs demonstrate strong adsorption of formaldehyde, with studies revealing their potential in its removal.5 Xie et al.6 and He et al.7 demonstrated that MOFs exhibit advantages in capturing VOCs compared to carbon-based adsorbents. Liang et al.8 and Zhang et al.9 improved the visible-light absorption of MOFs and prolonged the lifetime of photogenerated charges in these materials by introducing metal nanoparticles, thereby boosting their capacity to remove environmental pollutants. Shen et al.10 immobilized highly dispersed Pd nanoparticles in MOFs, which was beneficial to the synchronous photocatalytic degradation of organic pollutants such as HCHO. The highly porous and crystalline MOF ZIF-8 synthesized by Hou et al.11 significantly increased the adsorption capacity of HCHO. Chen et al.12 prepared MIL-100(Fe)/α-Fe2O3 using a simple one-step hydrothermal method to effectively remove organic pollutants in the air by adjusting the coordination of Fe ions. Yao et al.13 introduced synthesized nanotitanium dioxide particles into MOF materials to purify VOCs. Chen et al.14 synthesized tricrystalline TiO2 with a mesoporous structure by hydrothermal method at low temperature. Researchers15−17 have achieved effective adsorption of low-concentration formaldehyde by creating adsorption sites through metal atom coordination.

As scholars delved deeper into MOFs, Wang et al.18 found that most MOFS were less stable and had a weaker capture capacity for HCHO under high indoor humidity conditions. Bobbitt et al.19 showed that the presence of water affects MOF capture and the catalytic degradation of VOCs. Ghosh et al.20 calculated the pure water isotherms by the Monte Carlo method, comparing the capture of ammonia by three hydrophobic MOFs under dry and humid conditions. The results showed that hydrophobic MOFs are suitable for ammonia capture under humid conditions. Many researchers have shown that21−25 nonpolar and highly amorphous adsorbents have low adsorption capacity and poor selectivity for HCHO in humid air. Therefore, the competitive relationship between water and formaldehyde under wet and nonwet conditions and the relationship between MOFs and humidity need to be further studied.

Considering the trapping capacity of MOFs for HCHO under both dry and humid conditions, we selected the representative Fe-HHTP-MOF molecule to analyze its saturated adsorption capacity for HCHO and H2O, as well as its isothermal adsorption behavior for HCHO under conditions of dryness and humidity coexisting. The adsorption amount of MOFS for HCHO and the adsorption difference of the HCHO molecules were analyzed at 288.15, 303.15, 313.15, 323.15, and 333.15 K, and the adsorption pressure was 0.01–10 MPa under the conditions of drying and water coexisting; the adsorption effect of Fe-HHTP-MOF on HCHO was obtained, which is very important to study how to provide a basis for subsequent research on how high-performance MOFs can remove formaldehyde in dry and humid air from a molecular perspective and to screen MOFs suitable for the low-pressure capture of formaldehyde.

Model Construction and Parameter Selection

Fe-HHTP-MOF Structure Model

The CIF structure of the Fe-HHTP-MOF molecule was obtained from the Cambridge Crystal Data Center (CCDC) and imported into MS to construct the molecule, as shown in Figures 1 and 2. The periodic boundary conditions were constructed to make the Fe-HHTP-MOF molecule with periodic structure (a = b = c = 32.8977 Å, α = β = γ = 90°), and molecular dynamics and molecular mechanics were simulated by a Forcite module; the minimum energy structure model of the Fe-HHTP-MOF molecule with periodic boundary conditions was obtained by using the NVT ensemble annealing and Nose temperature control methods.

Construction of the Adsorption Model

Construction of the Saturated Adsorption Model

The molecular structure models of H2O and HCHO were drawn with the MS software. The force field parameters were Dreiding, and the planar formaldehyde model was adopted. The bond lengths of H–C and C=O were set at 1.14 and 1.51 Å, respectively, while the H–C=O angle was maintained at 120°. The lowest energy configurations of H2O and HCHO were obtained by molecular dynamics simulation using the Forcite module. The adsorption of H2O and HCHO molecules onto Fe-HHTP-MOF was analyzed using the Sorption module at temperatures of 288.15, 303.15, 313.15, 323.15, and 333.15 K. Structural models of Fe-HHTP-MOF with adsorbed H2O and HCHO molecules were then obtained through the Monte Carlo method.

Establishment of Isothermal Adsorption Model

The effects of the pressure and temperature on the adsorption characteristics of H2O and HCHO molecules in the Fe-HHTP-MOF structure are different. To study the adsorption capacity of Fe-HHTP-MOF for H2O and HCHO molecules, the structure of Fe-HHTP-MOF was imported into the adsorption isotherm task in a Sorption module; the molecular structure model of HCHO was obtained at 288.15, 303.15, 313.15, 323.15, and 333.15 K; and the adsorption pressure ranged from 0.01 to 10 MPa.

Grand Canonical Ensemble Monte Carlo Method (GCMC)

GCMC simulations were conducted on the Fe-HHTP-MOF molecular structure to evaluate the adsorption performance of these structures in the adsorption isotherm task. In the screening stage, 4 × 106 Monte Carlo cycles were performed to estimate the sorption isotherm of HCHO in each Fe-HHTP-MOF, including maximum loading of 1 × 106 cycles and production run of 1 × 106 cycles. Temperature cycles were achieved by 4 Monte Carlo cycles at the same probability. For multicomponent adsorption, the simulated temperatures were maintained at 288.15, 303.15, 313.15, 323.15, and 333.15 K; the pressure range was from 0.01 to 10 MPa; and the molar ratio of HCHO/H2O was 32:18. For GCMC simulation underwater coexistence conditions, a total of 4 × 106 Monte Carlo cycles were performed.

The larger the adsorption energy, the stronger the adsorption capacity. In order to explore the adsorption energy of Fe-HHTP-MOF on HCHO and H2O, the Forcite module dynamics task item was used to calculate the adsorption energy of the Fe-HHTP-MOF saturated adsorption of HCHO molecules or H2O molecules. The larger the absolute value of the adsorption energy, the greater the interaction force between Fe-HHTP-MOF and HCHO or H2O is.

Result Analysis

Fe-HHTP-MOF Adsorbed Formaldehyde and Water Respectively

In the study of Fe-HHTP-MOF adsorbing formaldehyde and water separately, it was found that only van der Waals can participate in the adsorption of formaldehyde and only van der Waals and hydrogen bonding can participate in the adsorption of water. Figure 3 shows the van der Waals energy and molecular structure of Fe-HHTP-MOF adsorbing HCHO, Figure 4 shows the van der Waals energy (left) and hydrogen bonding energy (right) of Fe-HHTP-MOF adsorbing H2O, and Figure 5 shows the molecular structure model of Fe-HHTP-MOF adsorbing H2O.

Van der Waals energy and molecular structure of Fe-HHTP-MOF adsorbing HCHO.

Van der Waals energy (left) and hydrogen bonding energy (right) of Fe-HHTP-MOF adsorbing H2O.

Molecular structure model of Fe-HHTP-MOF adsorbing H2O.

In Figures 3 and 4, the van der Waals energy and hydrogen bond energy in the Fe-HHTP-MOF-HCHO and Fe-HHTP-MOF-H2O binary systems decrease with the number of molecules.

As the relative molecular mass increases, so does the van der Waals energy within the system. This leads to more HCHO or H2O molecules being adsorbed by Fe-HHTP-MOF, thereby reducing the number of free HCHO or H2O molecules. The lowest point indicates the saturated adsorption amounts of 260 N/ (u.c) for HCHO molecules and 480 N/(u.c) for H2O molecules. The saturated adsorption amount for H2O molecules is 1.85 times greater than that for HCHO molecules, suggesting a higher adsorption capacity of Fe-HHTP-MOF for H2O molecules compared to that for HCHO molecules.

To investigate in-depth the role of hydrogen bonds in the microreaction of Fe-HHTP-MOF adsorbing H2O molecules, the bond lengths and angles of hydrogen bonds in Fe-HHTP-MOF adsorbed H2O molecules were calculated through molecular simulation. These were then compared with the bond lengths and angles of H2O molecules in pure water, as shown in Figures 6 and 7.

Hydrogen bond length and hydrogen bond angle in pure water.

Hydrogen bond length and angle of Fe-HHTP-MOF-adsorbed water molecules.

As can be seen from Figures 6 and 7, the hydrogen bond length in aqueous solutions primarily centers around 2.08 Å, while the hydrogen bond angle predominantly ranges around 178°. Postadsorption of H2O molecules by Fe-HHTP-MOF, the peak hydrogen bond length is approximately 2.15 Å with angles ranging between 160 and 170°. The increase in hydrogen bond length by 0.07 Å and the decrease in angle suggest that the bond between Fe-HHTP-MOF and H2O molecules undergoes torsion, altering the hydrogen bond length. This torsion leads to the formation of hydrogen bond functional groups, primarily from the dissociation of H2O molecules into −H and −OH bonds. Fe-HHTP-MOF provides adsorption sites for these oxygen-containing functional groups, while the broken −H bonds react with the oxygen atoms in Fe-HHTP-MOF to form −OH bonds. Some H2O molecules aggregate, leading to their dissociation and the formation of oxygen atoms, which are preferentially inserted into the oxygen vacancies of Fe-HHTP-MOF. Consequently, this influences the structure and functionality of the Fe-HHTP-MOF molecule.

As shown in Table 1 and Figure 8, with the increase of adsorption pressure, the adsorption amount of HCHO molecules in Fe-HHTP-MOF under the dry state showed an increasing trend. The adsorption quantity of HCHO molecules by Fe-HHTP-MOF increased sharply from 0.01 to 4.45 MPa, then rose gradually between 4.45 and 7.78 MPa, and tended to plateau beyond 7.78 MPa. Consequently, it can be deduced that at a constant temperature, increased adsorption pressure enhances Fe-HHTP-MOF’s ability to adsorb HCHO. Conversely, at constant adsorption pressure, the quantity of HCHO molecules adsorbed by Fe-HHTP-MOF in a dry state diminishes with rising temperature. The decreasing trend is obvious from 288.15 to 303.15 K, and after 303.15 K, the influence of temperature on HCHO molecule adsorption amount weakens. This is because the activity of HCHO molecules and H2O molecules increases with the increase of temperature, and the oxygen-containing functional groups in Fe-HHTP-MOF break, reducing the oxygen-containing functional groups and leading to a decrease in the adsorption sites of HCHO molecules, and as the temperature further increases, the adsorption amount of HCHO molecules decreases, indicating that temperature has an inhibitory effect on the adsorption of HCHO molecules by Fe-HHTP-MOF.

As shown in Figures 9–13, under the same adsorption pressure and temperature, the adsorption amount of HCHO molecules is smaller when H2O molecules coexist than when it is dry; this indicates that the presence of H2O molecules affects the adsorption capacity of Fe-HHTP-MOF for HCHO molecules. When the presence of H2O molecules and HCHO molecules exists simultaneously, a portion of H2O molecules enters Fe-HHTP-MOF, leading to a decrease in the adsorption amount of HCHO molecules and a weakening of the ability of Fe-HHTP-MOF to capture HCHO molecules. Moreover, as the temperature increases under the same pressure, when H2O molecules coexist, the adsorption amount of HCHO molecules decreases. Although Fe-HHTP-MOF has a high adsorption capacity for HCHO molecules, the Fe atoms in Fe-HHTP-MOF also provide adsorption sites for HCHO molecules. However, due to the unstable connection between the metal center atom and the organic part atom in Fe-HHTP-MOF, it is particularly susceptible to the influence of H2O molecules. Therefore, H2O molecules with polar functional groups preferentially occupy the adsorption sites, hindering the capture of HCHO.

As shown in Tables 1 and 2, the difference in HCHO molecules’ adsorption amount between the dry state and the coexistence of H2O molecules was calculated. At 288.15 K and 8.89 MPa, compared with the coexistence of H2O molecules, the difference in the adsorption amount of Fe-HHTP-MOF for HCHO under dry conditions reaches its maximum, with a maximum value of 21.90 mmol/g; at 303.15 K and 7.78 MPa, the maximum difference in adsorption amount is 20.02 mmol/g; at 313.15 K and 6.67 MPa, the maximum difference in adsorption amount is 18.96 mmol/g; at 323.15 K and 5.56 MPa, the maximum difference in adsorption amount is 17.58 mmol/g; and at 333.15 K and 3.34 MPa, the maximum difference in adsorption capacity is 15.72 mmol/g. This indicates that as the temperature increases, intermolecular interactions strengthen, molecular activity increases, and the influence of H2O molecules on the adsorption of HCHO molecules by Fe-HHTP-MOF weakens. A portion of the adsorbed H2O molecules come out to provide adsorption sites for HCHO molecules, leading to HCHO molecules reaching saturation adsorption sites earlier. Additionally, under low-pressure conditions, the difference in HCHO adsorption can still reach its maximum value.

Analysis of Adsorption Energy Results for HCHO and H2O in Fe-HHTP-MOF

By calculation, the adsorption energies of HCHO and H2O molecules on coal with Fe-HHTP-MOF were obtained, as shown in Figure 14.

Adsorption energies of HCHO and H2O molecules.

As shown in Figure 14, the absolute adsorption energy of Fe-HHTP-MOF for H2O molecules is greater than that of the Fe-HHTP-MOF molecules. The negative adsorption energy indicates that the adsorption process is more spontaneous, the interaction energy between molecules is stronger, and the adsorption system is more stable. Therefore, the interaction force between Fe-HHTP-MOF and H2O molecules is larger than that between the Fe-HHDP-MOF molecules. Moreover, the presence of H2O is not conducive to Fe-HHTP-MOF capturing HCHO molecules.

Conclusions

1.Only van der Waals energy can participate in the adsorption of HCHO molecules by Fe-HHTP-MOF, while the hydrogen bonding energy is mainly involved in the adsorption of H2O molecules, followed by van der Waals energy, with hydrogen bonding energy much greater than van der Waals energy. The saturated adsorption amount of Fe-HHTP-MOF for H2O molecules is about 1.85 times that of HCHO molecules, indicating that Fe-HHTP-MOF has a greater adsorption capacity for H2O molecules than HCHO molecules.

The saturated adsorption amount of Fe-HHTP-MOF for H2O molecules is about 1.85 times that of HCHO molecules, indicating that Fe-HHTP-MOF has a greater adsorption capacity for H2O molecules than HCHO molecules.

2.The saturated adsorption capacity of Fe-HHTP-MOF for H2O molecules is approximately 1.85 times that for HCHO molecules, demonstrating a higher affinity of Fe-HHTP-MOF for H2O over HCHO molecules. Within the Fe-HHTP-MOF and H2O mixed system, the peak hydrogen bond length increases by 0.07 Å and the hydrogen bond angle decreases. These alterations in hydrogen bond angle and length directly contribute to Fe-HHTP-MOF’s strong adsorption capacity for H2O molecules.
