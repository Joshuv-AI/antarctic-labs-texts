Identify Candidate Intervention and Prevention Targets for Internet Gaming Disorder: Evidence from Network Analysis and Simulation Interventions
Authors: Chenguang Zhao, Wei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Internet gaming disorder (IGD) involves persistent and recurrent gaming behavior that produces a range of negative consequences for affected individuals [1, 2]. Both the fifth edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) and the eleventh edition of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11) identify IGD as a condition warranting further investigation, thereby underscoring its growing relevance as a global mental health concern [3, 4]. Because the DSM-5 conceptualizes IGD as nine symptoms, examining the interrelationships among these symptoms and their relative importance becomes essential for informing more effective prevention and intervention strategies.

The network theory of psychopathology conceptualizes mental disorders as systems composed of interacting symptoms, with psychopathology emerging from direct symptom-symptom interactions rather than from a single underlying latent cause [5, 6]. From this perspective, interventions that selectively target central symptoms may enhance treatment efficiency by disrupting key pathways that sustain the symptom network [6, 7]. However, empirical research has rarely tested whether intervening on central symptoms produces broader alleviation across the entire symptom network. This gap appears to stem primarily from methodological constraints: conventional intervention approaches lack the precision to selectively manipulate individual symptoms, which has limited researchers’ ability to evaluate symptom-specific intervention effects at the network level [8–10].

Simulation-based intervention methods developed by Lunansky offer a novel avenue for addressing this methodological challenge [11]. By systematically manipulating properties of the symptom network – such as altering the activation states of individual symptoms – researchers can examine how changes in specific nodes influence overall network dynamics. Within this framework, symptom parameters can be adjusted by increasing or decreasing a node’s intrinsic tendency toward activation [11]. Decreasing a symptom’s activation propensity reduces the likelihood that the symptom will become activated, thereby approximating the effect of a targeted clinical intervention on that symptom; this procedure is referred to as an alleviating intervention. Conversely, increasing a symptom’s activation propensity renders the symptom more susceptible to activation, a process conceptualized as an aggravating intervention [12–14]. Together, these simulation strategies enable a systematic evaluation of how symptom-specific interventions may shape the behavior of the broader psychopathological network.

Using a network analytic framework combined with simulation-based interventions, this study aimed to identify candidate prevention and intervention targets for IGD. Unlike centrality analysis, the simulation approach distinguishes the projected effects of alleviating versus aggravating interventions and quantifies the magnitude of network-wide change when a specific symptom is targeted. It also offers a replicable computational framework for testing whether theoretically salient symptoms operate as functionally influential nodes in the symptom network. The findings aim to provide theoretical guidance for future longitudinal and intervention research in IGD and may inform simulation-based intervention studies in other behavioral and substance-related addictions.

Methods

Participants

This cross-sectional survey was conducted among undergraduate students between September and October 2024 in six universities located in Henan, Hubei, and Hunan Provinces, China. All included participants were active gamers, defined as individuals who reported playing Internet games every day. Participants were aged 16–27 years (M = 18.94, SD = 2.76). The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the relevant institutional ethics committee. Electronic informed consent was obtained online through the WenJuanXing platform (www.wjx.cn) before participants began the survey. To prevent duplicate submissions, the WenJuanXing platform was configured to permit only one submission per IP address.

A total of 2806 responses were initially collected. To ensure data quality, responses were excluded based on four criteria: (a) completion speed (n = 44), with responses averaging less than 2 s per item excluded because this indicated insufficient engagement; (b) response invariance (n = 72), including mechanical patterns such as selecting the same option across all items or alternating responses irrespective of item content; (c) failure to answer the embedded attention-check items correctly (n = 148); and (d) incomplete submission of the administered items (n = 12). After these exclusions, 2530 valid responses were retained for analysis, including 1,081 males (42.7%) and 1,449 females (57.3%).

Measures

Internet gaming disorder scale nine-item short form, IGDS-SF9: The scale was developed by Pontes and Griffiths based on the DSM-5 [15]. This scale assesses IGD through nine items corresponding to the nine core criteria in DSM-5: preoccupation, withdrawal, tolerance, loss of control, loss of interest, compulsive, deception, escape, relationship disruption. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale rating from 1 (never) to 5 (always), with higher scores indicating greater IGD severity. In this study, the Cronbach’s α was 0.91 and McDonald’s ω was 0.92.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were conducted using R (version 4.3.3). The analytical procedure comprised two stages: network model construction and intervention simulation.

First, an Ising model was constructed to represent the psychopathological dynamic systems. The Ising model uses binary data, meaning that symptoms can be “present” or “absent.” Model estimation was performed using logistic regression analyses, with edge weights corresponding to the regression coefficients. In the present study, these states were operationalized as no endorsement (0; original response = 1, “never”) and any endorsement (1; original responses = 2–5, ranging from “rarely” to “always”), respectively, rather than clinically defined symptom absence and presence [12, 16]. Then, the Ising model was estimated using the bootnet package, with the IsingFit estimator selected and the tuning parameter set to 0.25 [17]. The resulting network was visualized using the qgraph package. In the network, nodes represented individual IGD symptoms, whereas edges reflected the logistic regression coefficients between symptom pairs. Blue edges indicated positive associations between nodes [18].

Second, the NodeIdentifyR algorithm (NIRA) was used to identify the projected most efficient, symptom-specific intervention target in psychological networks [11]. NIRA generated 5,000 observations or simulated “participants” for which symptoms are assessed after interventions. Two types of intervention simulations were applied: alleviating interventions decrease a symptom’s threshold by subtracting two standard deviations from its original threshold parameter, and aggravating interventions increase two standard deviations to its original parameter [11]. To assess projected changes in total symptom activation, NIRA computed total sum scores after each threshold manipulation and compared them with the original simulated network. Subsequently, the NIRA index was defined as the absolute difference between the sum score of the original network and that of each post-intervention network. A larger absolute difference indicated a larger projected change in the simulated network sum score. Symptoms were ranked according to the magnitude of this model-based change [11].

Estimated Ising Network Model

The Ising model for IGD symptoms is shown in Figure 1a. The edge between IGD2 and IGD3 exhibited the highest weight, followed by the edges linking IGD4–IGD5, IGD7–IGD9, IGD8–IGD9, IGD6–IGD7, and IGD6–IGD8. These edge weights were significantly stronger than those of most edges in the network, suggesting stronger interrelations among the corresponding symptoms (online suppl. Fig. S4). The centrality indices for 9 IGD symptoms are shown in Figure 1b. IGD6 exhibited the highest values across all three centrality indices – strength, closeness, and betweenness – indicating that this node functioned as a core node within the network. Network stability and accuracy were evaluated using the bootnet package. Edge-weight accuracy was evaluated via nonparametric bootstrapped confidence intervals, and centrality stability was examined using case-dropping bootstrap. The CS coefficients were 0.75 for strength, 0.52 for closeness, and 0.44 for betweenness, indicating stable centrality strength and closeness estimates, whereas betweenness should be interpreted with caution. Simulation of Alleviating and Aggravating Interventions

Figure 2 presents the projected network sum scores following the symptom-specific threshold manipulations implemented using NIRA. Under the alleviating simulation (Fig. 2a), manipulation of IGD6 (compulsivity) produced the largest projected reduction in the network sum score, from 4.78 to 2.66. Under the aggravating simulation (Fig. 2b), manipulation of IGD5 (loss of interest in alternative activities) produced the largest projected increase, from 4.71 to 6.67. These rankings describe projected changes within the estimated model and should not be interpreted as evidence of clinical effectiveness or prospective risk.

To examine the robustness of the findings to the binarization threshold, we repeated the network estimation and NIRA simulations using an alternative coding scheme in which responses of 1 (“never”) and 2 (“rarely”) were coded as 0 and responses of 3–5 were coded as 1. The top-ranked symptoms under the alleviating and aggravating simulations remained unchanged; detailed results are provided in online supplementary Figure S3.

Following the approach of Lunansky et al. [11], the NIRA indices were associated with marginal endorsement rates (r = 0.94 and −0.94 for the alleviating and aggravating conditions, respectively) and estimated threshold parameters (r = 0.79 and −0.98), but showed weaker associations with strength centrality (r = 0.41 and −0.01), suggesting that the rankings partly reflected baseline activation propensity and the available room for change rather than centrality alone.

Discussion

The present study applied network analysis and model-based threshold manipulations to compare projected changes in total symptom activation within an estimated IGD symptom network. Under the alleviating simulation, compulsivity was the top-ranked simulated target, whereas loss of interest in alternative activities was the top-ranked symptom under the aggravating simulation. These results describe symptom-specific differences in projected activation within the estimated Ising model. They do not establish causal interactions among symptoms or demonstrate real-world preventive or treatment effects. Instead, the findings provide hypotheses that can be evaluated in future longitudinal and experimental studies.

Compulsivity emerged as the top-ranked simulated target under the alleviating condition. In the estimated network, compulsivity exhibited the highest values across all three centrality indices – strength, closeness, and betweenness – indicating that it occupied a prominent structural position within the cross-sectional IGD symptom network. Alleviating interventions that reduce the activation propensity of compulsivity may produce the largest projected reduction in overall symptom activation within the simulated network. From a theoretical perspective, addiction represents the transition from goal-directed engagement to habitual behavior, which may subsequently develop into compulsive patterns [19, 20]. Our findings are consistent with habit-formation models of addiction and identify compulsivity as a top-ranked simulated target warranting future empirical validation.

Loss of interest in alternative activities was the top-ranked simulated target under the aggravating condition. This symptom represents a decline in engagement with alternative, real-world activities [21, 22]. Within the estimated model, aggravating threshold manipulation of loss of interest was associated with the largest projected increase in overall symptom activation. The compensation hypothesis posits that when individuals’ psychosocial needs are unmet or frustrated in real life, they may lose interest in offline activities and subsequently seek compensatory gratification through internet use, which constitutes an important risk pathway for the development of addictive behaviors [23, 24]. These findings suggest that early stage prevention efforts may require distinct symptom targets from those prioritized for intervention, emphasizing the importance of monitoring loss of interest in alternative activities.

By combining network analysis with model-based threshold manipulations, the present study compared projected symptom changes under alleviating and aggravating simulation conditions. Compulsivity and loss of interest in alternative activities were the top-ranked symptoms under the two respective conditions. The findings demonstrate that intervention- and prevention-oriented targets differ, with compulsivity serving as a central symptom for the candidate intervention target and loss of interest in alternative activities representing the candidate prevention target. However, these findings do not constitute an empirical test of network theory or evidence of clinical effectiveness. Longitudinal and experimental studies are required to determine whether changes in these symptoms precede or produce broader changes in IGD symptomatology.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, applying NIRA to the Ising model required binarizing the IGDS-SF9 Likert-type responses. Although this strategy follows the original NIRA framework [11], it inevitably loses information from the original ordinal scale. Second, because the IGDS-SF9 items assess the same disorder construct, the dense positive network may partly reflect shared variance associated with a general IGD severity dimension. Third, the cross-sectional design precludes inferences about temporal dynamics or causal pathways among symptoms. Finally, the sample consisted of Chinese university students without clinical diagnosis, limiting generalizability to clinical IGD populations. Future studies should use longitudinal designs, clinically diagnosed samples, and simulation-based methods compatible with ordinal or continuous network models to validate and extend the present findings.
