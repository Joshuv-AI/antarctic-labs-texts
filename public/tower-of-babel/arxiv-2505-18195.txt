Acoustic and Machine Learning Methods for Speech-Based Suicide Risk Assessment: A Systematic Review
Ambre Marie, Marine Garnier, Thomas Bertin, Laura Machart, Guillaume Dardenne, Gwenolé Quellec, Sofian Berrouiguet

Abstract
Suicide remains a public health challenge, necessitating improved detection methods to facilitate timely intervention and treatment. This systematic review evaluates the role of Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML) in assessing suicide risk through acoustic analysis of speech. Following PRISMA guidelines, we analyzed 33 articles selected from PubMed, Cochrane, Scopus, and Web of Science databases. These studies primarily explored acoustic differences between individuals at risk of suicide (RS) and those not at risk (NRS), and evaluated ML classifier performance. Findings consistently showed significant acoustic feature variations between RS and NRS populations, particularly involving jitter, fundamental frequency (F0), Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCC), and power spectral density (PSD). Classifier effectiveness varied based on algorithms, modalities, and speech elicitation methods, with multimodal approaches integrating acoustic, linguistic, and metadata features demonstrating superior performance. However, limitations such as methodological variability, small sample sizes, lack of longitudinal data, and limited linguistic and demographic diversity restrict generalizability. Future research should focus on standardizing methods, expanding multimodal analyses, and utilizing larger, diverse datasets to support AI integration in clinical suicide risk assessment.

1
Introduction

Suicide remains a major public health concern in many societies, with prevention efforts failing to significantly reduce suicide rates in most countries
[
30
]
. It represents one of the main challenges for mental health professionals and public health authorities. According to the 2020 report by the World Health Organization (WHO), suicide continues to be one of the ten leading causes of death worldwide. Current methods for assessing suicide risk primarily rely on rigorous clinical evaluations, which depend on the clinician’s experience and the consideration of multiple factors such as the patient’s personal and family history, sociodemographic characteristics, and anamnestic data. These assessments are largely based on self-reported information, leaving patients free to disclose or withhold their suicidal thoughts
[
5
]
.

To enhance suicide risk assessment, the development of objective, sensitive, and reproducible tools is essential
[
13
]
. The emergence of Artificial Intelligence (AI) and, more specifically, Machine Learning (ML) models in the health field has opened new possibilities for improving such assessments. ML models, which rely on statistical, mathematical, and computational techniques, enable the development of predictive algorithms with minimal human intervention by leveraging large-scale datasets
[
41
]
. These models are particularly effective in classification tasks and are increasingly applied in mental health research, especially in the automated diagnosis of psychiatric disorders such as schizophrenia and depression
[
26
]
. AI-driven tools could potentially assist in suicide risk assessment by identifying specific patterns among at-risk individuals, particularly through the analysis of bio-signals or acoustic features of speech.

Depressive and suicidal states are often associated with significant affective disturbances, leading to physiological and cognitive changes that directly influence vocal production and speech characteristics. These disturbances alter vocal output through complex neuromuscular and cognitive coordination, resulting in measurable modifications in acoustic features
[
13
]
. A study by
Koops et al. 2021
compared audio recordings of depressed patients and healthy controls, revealing significant differences in some acoustic features between the two groups. Depressed individuals were found to exhibit a lower speech rate, less pitch variability and lower pitch, increased jitter and shimmer, and a lower harmonics-to-noise ratio. These features are respectively associated with psychomotor slowing, monotony and reduced expressiveness, instability in vocal fold vibration, and reduced vocal clarity.
Various studies have explored the potential of ML models in identifying depression through acoustic, linguistic, and facial expression features
[
13
,
11
,
54
]
. Similarly, AI-based tools incorporating ML techniques to analyze acoustic features could provide clinicians with additional, reliable, and reproducible support in assessing suicidal risk.

Previous reviews have explored the use of acoustic features in mental health assessment, including suicide risk.
Cummins et al. 2015
provided an overview of speech-based markers of affective disorders (e.g., suicidal ideation and depression), and included studies up to 2014. More recently,
Iyer and Meyer 2022
conducted a systematic review addressing suicide risk detection through acoustic analysis, covering literature up to early 2022.

Building on previous work, the present review extends the temporal coverage up to 2025 and applies an expanded set of search terms to capture a broader range of studies. It provides a comprehensive synthesis of recent advances in acoustic-based suicide risk assessment, including the types of vocal features explored, the performance of machine learning classifiers, and the contribution of multimodal approaches.

Accordingly, this systematic review addresses three research questions:

1.

What acoustic features have been identified as potential indicators of suicide risk in individuals, and how reliably do they differentiate between those at risk and those not at risk?

2.

How effective are machine learning classifiers based on acoustic features in assessing and predicting suicide risk, and what methodological factors influence their performance?

3.

What is the added value of multimodal approaches - combining acoustic data with other modalities such as linguistic, visual, behavioral, or demographic features - in improving the accuracy and specificity of suicide risk assessment?

2
Methods

2.1
Research strategy

This systematic literature review was conducted following PRISMA guidelines and included two main searches covering publications from 2000 to 2025. The first search was performed using bibliographic databases PubMed, Cochrane, Scopus, and Web-of-Science. To reduce publication bias, grey literature was identified through The Grey Literature Report. Initial searches targeted titles and abstracts using the keywords: [suicid*] AND ([vocal] OR [acoustic*] OR [prosod*]).

An updated search was conducted using PubMed, Cochrane, Scopus, and Web-of-Science with an expanded keyword set, searching titles, abstracts, and keywords :

[suicid*] AND ([speech analysis] OR [voice features] OR [prosody] OR [acoustic markers] OR [speech biometrics] OR [voice quality] OR [phonation] OR [vocal characteristics])

References from identified articles were manually screened, resulting in the inclusion of three additional studies. A complementary search was conducted using Consensus, with the query: "What machine learning classifiers or acoustic parameters have been used to assess suicide risk in patients based on voice analysis?", which led to the inclusion of two further studies.

2.2
Selection of studies

Studies selected addressed the analysis of acoustic features in individuals at suicide risk (RS) or evaluated classifiers for suicide risk assessment. In this review, RS was defined in a general way to include suicidal ideation, previous suicide attempts, psychometric scale indications, or clinical evaluations of suicide risk. Only studies taking into account acoustic features from voice recordings were included. Studies limited to verbal analysis were excluded. Eligible study designs included experimental, observational, analytical or descriptive studies, and conference papers. Literature reviews and theoretical papers without clinical populations were excluded.

2.3
Data extraction and analysis

Both searches followed a three-step screening process. After removing duplicates, titles were reviewed, followed by abstracts. Finally, potentially eligible full texts were examined to exclude irrelevant studies. Bibliographic references were managed and de-duplicated using Zotero throughout the process.

3.3
Sample sizes, study design, and sample characteristics

The majority of studies included in this review were observational, analytical, and monocentric (57.57%, 19/33), with a balanced distribution between retrospective (52.63%, 10/19) and prospective (47.37%, 9/19) designs. Controlled prospective studies employing analytical methods were less frequent, with 18.18% (6/33) conducted in a monocentric setting and 9.09% (3/33) in a multicentric setting. Detailed methodological characteristics are illustrated in Figure

Sample sizes ranged from 16 to 1,179 participants, with a median of 67 (average 144).
With regard to population characteristics, 66.67% (22/33) of studies included both men and women, whereas 21.21% (7/33) focused exclusively on men and 3.03% (1/33) on women.

The most frequently studied age group was 25-65 years, included in 27.27% (9/33) of studies, highlighting a predominant focus on adults. Overall, adults represented 54.55% (18/33) of the studied populations, while adolescents were included in 15.15% (5/33) of studies. A substantial proportion of studies (33.33%, 11/33) did not specify an age category.

Several studies targeted specific subpopulations. Hospitalized patients or those admitted to emergency services were the most represented, along with individuals diagnosed with depression, bipolar disorder, or Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) (each 21.21%, 7/33). Military personnel and veterans were examined in 6.06% (2/33) of studies, while psychological helpline callers were included in 9.09% (3/33). Finally, online and social media suicide risk assessment was addressed in one study (3.03%).

3.4
Objectives of the studies

The majority of studies (81.82%, 27/33) focused on evaluating the ability of statistical or machine learning classifiers to distinguish between individuals at suicide risk (RS) and those not at risk (NRS) and assign them to the appropriate group.

A smaller subset (18.18%, 6/33) instead aimed to identify statistically significant differences in acoustic features between RS and NRS individuals without employing classification models. These studies sought to highlight relevant acoustic features that could serve as potential indicators of suicide risk.

Beyond these primary objectives, several studies included secondary objectives. Statistical hypothesis testing to assess significant differences in acoustic features between RS and NRS individuals was conducted in 30.30% (10/33) of studies. Additionally, 12.12% (4/33) assessed the effectiveness of combining acoustic features with additional metadata, including demographics, psychological history, or physiological signals such as EEG. Real-time or longitudinal monitoring methods for suicide risk detection were explored in 9.09% (3/33) of studies.

3.5
Studies using a statistical test

Several studies have employed statistical tests to identify differences in acoustic features between RS and NRS individuals. These studies explore various vocal characteristics across different speaking tasks.

Saavedra et al. 2021
aimed to describe the differences in acoustic features between RS and NRS individuals, analyzing voice recordings from tasks with varying levels of speech constraint.
Their results showed that vowel vocalization significantly differed between groups: RS individuals exhibited lower F1 and F2 formants (p
<
0.01) and higher jitter values (p
<
0.001). Regarding fundamental frequency (F0), no significant difference was observed between RS and NRS individuals during the open-ended question task when considering the entire sample (p = 0.11). However, when analyzed by gender, F0 was significantly lower in RS women (p = 0.027), while no differences were found in men. In the text-reading task, F0 was significantly lower in RS individuals overall (p = 0.029), with this effect confirmed regardless of gender.

Wahidah et al. 2015b
found that Power Spectral Density (PSD) varied significantly between two interview sessions conducted during a suicidal crisis and after therapeutic intervention, highlighting the potential for vocal features to reflect dynamic changes in mental state.

Other studies combined statistical tests with machine learning approaches to assess the discriminative power of acoustic features.
Venek et al. 2017
identified significant differences in F0, Normalized Amplitude Quotient (NAQ), peakslope, and F1 between RS and NRS adolescents. The study also found significant differences between first-time and repeat attempters, particularly in NAQ and Quasi-Open Quotient (QOQ). Similarly,
Stasak et al. 2021
identified Open Quotient (OQ), NAQ, and peakslope as significantly different between groups, suggesting that RS adolescents have breathier voices compared to their NRS counterparts.
Wahidah et al. 2015a
found that timing-based features, such as voiced-to-voiced and silence-to-silence transitions, as well as voiced interval lengths, were higher in RS individuals compared to depressed patients. While these descriptive differences suggest slower speech rate and longer vowel duration in high-risk speech, no statistical significance testing was reported to formally support these observations.

Recent studies have expanded upon these findings.
Figueroa et al. 2024
examined the influence of vowel type (e.g. /a/, /e/, /i/, /o/, /u/) on suicide risk classification among adolescents, identifying significant variations in F0, jitter, and harmonics-to-noise ratio (HNRdB). Gender-specific differences were also observed, with RS women showing greater temporal variability in jitter and men exhibiting lower F0 and HNRdB values. Similarly,
Gerczuk et al. 2024
investigated gender-based differences in suicide risk assessment by analysing acoustic features such as spectral slope, F0, and formant bandwidths. Using Mann–Whitney U tests, it was found that RS men displayed increased vocal agitation (e.g. higher arousal, spectral energy shifts), while RS women exhibited speech patterns aligned with reduced vocal expressiveness (e.g. lower pitch, narrower formant bandwidths).

Pillai et al. 2023
explored the generalizability of speech-based suicidal ideation detection across datasets, highlighting that while spectral features such as spectral slope and formant frequencies were key markers of RS status, cross-dataset generalization remained a challenge.
Iyer et al. 2022a
,
Iyer et al. 2022b
studied telehealth suicide prevention calls, identifying significant differences in spectral slope, loudness, and frequency-based features between RS and NRS individuals, reinforcing prior findings on the monotonic and breathy characteristics of RS speech.

Belouali et al. 2021
reported that US Army veterans classified as RS exhibited significantly lower Delta Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficient 11 (MFCC11) values compared to NRS veterans (p
<
0.001). This suggested a reduction in energy variation, indicative of a more monotonous vocal pattern. Additionally, increased Glottal Closure Instant (GCI) variability was observed, further supporting the hypothesis that breathiness and vocal irregularity are associated with increased suicide risk.

3.6
Classifiers used

Twenty-nine studies used classifiers. Sixteen studies (55.17%) tested a single type of classifier, seven studies (24.14%) tested two types, three studies (10.34%) tested three types, two studies (6.9%) tested five types, and one study (3.45%) tested six types.

The most frequently used classifier was the Support Vector Machine (SVM), appearing in 12 studies (41.38%). Other commonly tested classifiers included Random Forest (RF) in five studies (17.24%), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGB) and Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) in four studies (13.79%), followed by Logistic Regression (LR) and Quadratic Discriminant Analysis (QDA) each in three studies (10.34%). Several classifiers, such as Gradient Boosting (GB), k-Nearest Neighbors (kNN), and Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) were each used in two or fewer studies.

Deep Neural Networks (DNN), Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), HuBERT, Wav2vec2.0, VGGish-Z, Large Language Models (LLM), and Hidden Markov Models (HMM) were each tested in only one study. While DNN may have been limited by the small sample size constraints of most studies, the other models—such as HuBERT, Wav2vec2.0, and LLMs—may have been underutilized due to their relatively recent emergence in speech-related tasks and the computational costs associated with their deployment (Table
2
).

Cross-validation was applied in all studies using a classifier. The most common method was K-Fold Cross-Validation, used in 55.17% of the validations (16/29). Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation was used in 11 studies (37.93%), while Holdout Cross-Validation was employed in six studies (20.69%). Only one study (3.45%) applied Nested Cross-Validation.

Performance of classifiers

The discriminative performance of classifiers in identifying suicide risk through vocal analysis was primarily expressed using accuracy (23/29; 79.31%) and Area Under the Receiver-Operating Characteristic curve (AUC) (7/29; 24.14%) (Table
3
). Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), F1-score, and recall were less frequently reported metrics.

Previous studies reported strong classifier performances, such as an accuracy of 75% with Support Vector Machines (SVM) and 81.25% with Hidden Markov Models (HMM) in longer recordings, with HMM preferred due to its sequential analysis capability
[
38

. Recent studies achieved notably higher AUC values of 0.97 and 0.985 with Gradient Boosting in two distinct studies involving helpline callers
[

However, some classifiers demonstrated limitations, particularly in differentiating RS individuals from depressed NRS patients, due to shared acoustic patterns associated with common psychopathological processes, yielding modest performances around 60% accuracy

Recent studies have identified additional influencing factors beyond acoustics.
Min et al. 2023
emphasized longitudinal within-person changes, where acoustic features showed an AUC of 0.67 in baseline assessments, declining to 0.56 after two to four months, indicating variability and temporal dynamics in acoustic features.
Amiriparian et al. 2024
demonstrated that integrating metadata such as prior suicide attempts or access to firearms drastically improved classification from a baseline balanced accuracy of 66.2% (speech alone) to 94.4% when combined with such demographic data.

Gender-specific models demonstrated improved classifier performance, achieving an enhanced balanced accuracy of 81% due to differing vocal correlates between men and women experiencing suicidal risk
[
17
]
.

The type of speech input notably impacted classifier performance. Speech obtained from answering questions was the most common input (52.94%), closely followed by speech from reading and open talking (each 44.12%). Among reading tasks, controlled passages such as the "Rainbow Passage" - used in 20.59% of studies - were associated with more consistent and reliable results, likely due to reduced variability in linguistic and phonetic content. This finding aligns with earlier studies highlighting the advantages of standardized reading material for acoustic analysis

3.8.1
Relevant acoustic features

Several studies evaluated classifier performance using a single acoustic feature.
Ozdas et al. 2000
demonstrated that jitter alone differentiated suicidal from non-suicidal individuals with an accuracy of 80%.
Yingthawornsuk et al. 2006
achieved accuracies of 77% (maintenance session) and 82% (reading session) using only power spectral density (PSD) to discriminate between suicidal risk (RS) and depressive or remitted patients. Similarly,
Wahidah et al. 2015b
identified significant differences in spectral density power (SDP) between treatment sessions using PSD exclusively.
Kaymaz-Keskinpala et al. 2007b
,
Ozdas et al. 2004b
used only Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCC), reaching respectively 80% and 93% accuracy in distinguishing RS from depressed individuals, with results sometimes lower depending on patient gender and speech task.

Studies employing multiple features such as jitter, shimmer, and related spectral characteristics reported strong performance. By combining jitter with spectral slope,
Ozdas et al. 2004a
achieved accuracies of 85% (RS vs. healthy) and 75% (RS vs. depressed NRS).
Cohen et al. 2023
, using jitter, shimmer, fundamental frequency (F0), and intensity, reported an AUC of 0.65 for acoustic features alone, improved to 0.76 through multimodal fusion.
Gerczuk et al. 2024
achieved 81% balanced accuracy using jitter, shimmer, peakslope, spectral slope, intensity, and pause duration in gender-specific modeling, while
Cui et al. 2024
attained 80.7% accuracy and an F1-score of 84.6% by combining similar features including MFCC and formants.

Studies using MFCC and formants produced notable outcomes.
Min et al. 2023
reported accuracies of 68.7% (between-person ANN) and up to 79.2% (within-person XGB) using MFCC, formants, pitch, F0, and energy.
Iyer et al. 2022a
,
Iyer et al. 2022b
demonstrated an AUC of 97.39% and reported an exceptional accuracy of 99.85% (AUC = 0.985) with similar acoustic features.
Yünden et al. 2024
achieved 90% accuracy primarily using MFCC, formants, pitch, intensity, and speech interval features.

PSD-centered studies also reported high classification accuracies.
Kaymaz-Keskinpala et al. 2007a
obtained 87% accuracy using PSD2, while
Yingthawornsuk 2007
achieved an accuracy of 88.5% employing PSD2 and center frequencies (CF3, CF4). In the study by
France et al. 2000
, features such as amplitude modulation (AM), formants F1 and F3, and PSD3 led to classification accuracies of 75% for both RS and control groups, and 71% for depressed individuals.

Research analyzing voiced-silent intervals and speech timing features also showed promising results.
Hashim et al. 2012
demonstrated classification accuracies ranging from 75% to 100%, and
Figueroa et al. 2024
identified significant acoustic differences (p
<
0.05) in interval length, pitch, and speech timing.
Amiriparian et al. 2024
achieved a baseline accuracy of 66.2% using acoustic features alone, significantly increasing to 94.4% with added metadata.

Finally, advanced acoustic features including zero crossing rate and entropy of energy provided valuable insights.
Chen et al. 2024
reported an F1-score of 76.96% for negative emotion recognition, whereas
Pillai et al. 2023
achieved AUC values ranging from 0.62 to 0.68 across multiple datasets.

3.8.2
Other modalities

Among the 29 studies evaluating classifiers, 21 (72.41%) utilized a unimodal acoustic approach, 3 (10.34%) used bimodal approaches, and 8 (27.59%) employed multimodal (three or more modalities) methodologies, incorporating linguistic, visual, clinician interaction dynamics, or metadata, alongside acoustic data (Tables
2
and
3
).

Several unimodal studies demonstrated strong classification results using acoustic features alone.
Scherer et al. 2013
reported accuracies of 81.25% (Hidden Markov Model, HMM) and 75% (Support Vector Machine, SVM).
Ozdas et al. 2004b
achieved 80% accuracy distinguishing suicidal risk (RS) from depressed individuals using MFCC.
Hashim et al. 2012
obtained 75%-100% accuracy using voiced-silent interval features, while
France et al. 2000
reported accuracies of around 75% for RS and controls using amplitude modulation (AM), formants (F1, F3), and PSD3. Similarly,
Yingthawornsuk 2007
reached 90.33% accuracy with PSD and center frequencies (CF2, CF3), and
Iyer et al. 2022b
achieved a very high accuracy of 99.85% (AUROC=0.985).

Bimodal approaches combining acoustic with another modality also showed notable enhancements in classification performance.
Stasak et al. 2021
improved accuracy from 54–69% (acoustic features alone) to 77–78% when incorporating speech disfluency features.
Cui et al. 2024
, employing acoustic and linguistic modalities, reported a best accuracy of 80.7% and an F1-score of 84.6%.

Multimodal studies frequently showed substantial improvements compared to unimodal approaches.
Venek et al. 2017
reached 90% accuracy by combining acoustic and linguistic features with interaction dynamics, such as turn-taking behavior, response latency, and speaking time.
Pestian et al. 2017
improved from an acoustic-only AUC of 0.79 to 0.92 when adding linguistic features, including word and word-pair frequencies derived from interview transcripts. Similarly,
Belouali et al. 2021
reported AUC improvement from 0.76 (acoustic alone) to 0.80 (acoustic and linguistic features).
Shah et al. 2019
observed progressive improvements from an AUC of 0.62 (acoustic), 0.72 (acoustic and verbal), to 0.74 with the addition of visual features. These included motion cues (e.g. average displacement of hands and shoulders) and facial behavior (e.g. gaze aversion and action units related to negative emotions).

Amiriparian et al. 2024
significantly increased classification accuracy from 66.2% (acoustic features alone) to 94.4% when integrating patient metadata, including history of suicide attempts, access to firearms or lethal medication, hopelessness, trauma, substance abuse, and depressive symptom scores. Likewise,
Cohen et al. 2023
reported an AUC enhancement from 0.65 (single modality) to 0.76 through multimodal decision fusion.

4
Discussion

4.1
Main results

This literature review synthesizes current research assessing acoustic features and classifiers for the evaluation of suicidal behavior. Despite the limited number of identified studies, growing interest in AI applications in mental health has emerged, reflected by an increase in publications addressing vocal markers in suicide risk prediction. Findings across reviewed studies indicate substantial promise for employing acoustic features as objective, non-invasive indicators of suicide risk.

Acoustic features such as jitter, MFCC, power spectral density (PSD), formants, fundamental frequency (F0), pitch, and intensity consistently demonstrated strong discriminative performance. Notably, vocal timing patterns and PSD sub-bands achieved classification accuracies ranging from 90.3% to 95%, aligning with findings from
Iyer and Meyer 2022
. In particular, recent studies incorporating multimodal or bimodal approaches reported improved accuracy compared to unimodal methods, aligning with observations by
Barua et al. 2024
, who observed superior performance from multimodal approaches in mental health disorder detection.

Classifier effectiveness varied, with Random Forest, Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGB), and Gradient Boosting models frequently reporting high accuracy and AUC values, supporting recent findings by
Ehtemam et al. 2024
. Gradient Boosting classifiers, specifically, achieved AUC scores up to 0.985, highlighting their robustness in acoustic-based suicide risk classification tasks.

Additionally, studies exploring digital and acoustic features, such as those reported by
Holmgren et al. 2022
, reinforce the potential of integrating speech analysis with broader digital indicators (e.g., sleep disturbances, heart rate variability) for enhanced suicide risk assessment. The reviewed studies demonstrated comparable sensitivity (up to 86%) and specificity (up to 70%) in acoustic-based suicide risk detection, supporting the reliability of voice-derived features within digital assessment frameworks.

Gender-specific and subgroup analyses further improved classifier performance, indicating differential acoustic patterns among demographic groups. These results underscore the importance of personalized models to effectively discriminate between risk groups, as observed in multiple reviewed studies.

Recent advancements captured in this review underline the potential utility of acoustic features and sophisticated machine learning models in suicide risk assessment, supporting their eventual integration into clinical and telehealth settings.

4.2
Limitations of the studies

Several limitations were identified across the studies included in this systematic review. One major limitation is the variability in sample sizes, which ranged from very small samples (fewer than 30 participants) to larger cohorts (over 1,000 participants). This variability introduces heterogeneity that restricts direct comparisons and limits the generalizability of findings. Smaller sample sizes particularly constrain the statistical power and reliability of classifier performance results.

Another notable limitation involves methodological heterogeneity among the studies, particularly regarding acoustic feature extraction methods, speech tasks employed (e.g., spontaneous speech, text reading, structured interviews), and recording conditions (controlled laboratory settings versus telehealth or clinical environments). Such methodological diversity complicates comparisons across studies and can introduce biases related to environmental factors or the nature of speech elicitation tasks.

There is also a significant issue with the lack of standardized protocols for assessing and reporting classifier performance metrics. While accuracy and AUC were commonly reported, other important metrics such as sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and F1-score were inconsistently used or omitted altogether. This inconsistency prevents comprehensive evaluation of model performance and limits comparative assessments across studies.

Moreover, most studies relied on cross-sectional or retrospective designs, restricting their ability to capture dynamic changes in suicidal risk over time. Few studies adopted longitudinal designs, which are essential for understanding temporal variations in vocal features and their practical relevance in real-world clinical scenarios.

An additional limitation concerns the predominant use of English-speaking populations, with limited representation from other linguistic and cultural backgrounds. Language and cultural differences can significantly influence vocal characteristics, highlighting the need for more diverse participant samples to enhance generalizability.

Furthermore, most studies did not report whether participants were taking medication. Certain psychotropic drugs, due to their anticholinergic and antidopaminergic effects, can cause speech alterations such as dysarthria, dry mouth, and changes in acoustic features
[
42
]
. Similarly, the consumption of drugs, caffeine, or alcohol was rarely documented. However,
France et al. 2000
noted that patients on medication were no more likely to be misclassified than those not receiving treatment. Additionally, few studies considered potential confounding factors such as speech or language disorders or respiratory conditions.

Finally, exploration into integrating acoustic features with multimodal data remained limited. Despite evidence suggesting superior performance from multimodal approaches, relatively few studies employed comprehensive multimodal methods incorporating visual, linguistic, or contextual metadata. This gap restricts a full understanding of the potential benefits of combined data sources.

In addition, studies with very deep classifiers that directly analyze raw acoustic data were excluded from this review because of their current lack of interpretability. These advanced deep learning models often do not provide clear explanations of the features analyzed, limiting their clinical applicability. Future research should prioritize the development of deep yet explainable artificial intelligence methods to ensure transparency and usability in clinical practice.

4.2.1
Lack of specificity

While previous sections have reviewed classifier performance in detecting suicidal risk, one key limitation emerges across multiple studies: the lack of specificity in distinguishing suicidal risk (RS) from other clinically similar states, such as MDD, anxiety, or general psychological distress.

Several authors have highlighted the limitations of classifiers in distinguishing suicidal risk (RS) from individuals not at risk (NRS) who present with depressive disorders
[
34
,
9
,
32
]
. These limitations likely reflect the lack of specificity in acoustic feature variations observed among RS individuals. Indeed, acoustic features such as reduced fundamental frequency (F0), increased jitter, and spectral alterations are commonly observed in both RS and depressive states, complicating the accurate differentiation of these populations
[
13
,
15
]
.

Recent studies further emphasize the challenge of specificity, demonstrating considerable overlap in acoustic profiles among groups experiencing different types or intensities of psychological distress. For instance,
Cohen et al. 2023
reported that acoustic features alone struggled to discriminate effectively between RS and depressive individuals, with noticeable improvement only through multimodal integration. Similarly,
Min et al. 2023
found decreased classifier effectiveness when applied across diverse contexts and populations, reflecting limited generalizability and specificity.

Additionally, accurately differentiating varying levels of suicidal severity using acoustic features remains challenging, due to subtle emotional variations and context-dependent speech characteristics
[
8
,
35
]
. While some studies observed strong discriminative power of acoustic features in high-distress contexts, such as helpline calls
[
21
,
22
]
, other studies highlighted reduced specificity when dealing with less extreme clinical scenarios or mixed populations
[
40
,
17
,
53
]
.

However, multimodal approaches consistently enhanced specificity by combining acoustic features with linguistic content, visual signals, or metadata
[
2
,
12
,
17
]
. These findings suggest that, although acoustic features alone may lack the granularity required to distinguish RS from other states, they remain valuable components in multimodal frameworks.

4.3
Research directions and perspectives

The methods used for audio data collection were not standardized and varied across studies. Data were recorded in controlled (laboratory), semi-controlled, or ecological environments, such as home recordings. One of the key challenges for future research remains the development of a standardized approach to audio data collection to ensure consistency across studies. Additionally, ethical constraints associated with recording and storing sensitive speech data necessitate collaborative efforts to build large-scale, well-annotated databases, while ensuring data privacy and patient consent.

Most studies have demonstrated that multimodal approaches significantly enhance classifier performance, offering promising avenues for future research. Integration of acoustic analysis with linguistic, visual, behavioral, or demographic features —particularly during patient-clinician interactions— could improve robustness and classification accuracy
[
7
,
46
,
2
,
12
,
10
]
. For instance,
Amiriparian et al. 2024
highlighted a significant enhancement in classification accuracy when acoustic features were combined with metadata, such as prior suicide attempts or access to firearms. Similarly,
Cohen et al. 2023
showed improved classification through multimodal decision fusion, suggesting that single-modality models alone might be insufficient for accurate suicide risk detection.

Moreover, the method of speech collection has been shown to impact classification performance. Automatic speech samples obtained from text reading generally yield better results compared to spontaneous speech
[
51
,
52
]
. This is because machine learning models can focus solely on acoustic features rather than semantic variability, leading to more stable predictions.
Yingthawornsuk and Shiavi 2008
suggested that standardized text-reading tasks could serve as an alternative to spontaneous speech recordings, particularly for specific patient populations, enhancing reproducibility and comparability across studies. Recent studies further support this notion, emphasizing that controlled speech tasks result in more reliable acoustic features, improving model performance and generalizability
[
15
,
53
]
.

With the widespread use of smartphones, digital communication tools, and increasing presence on social media, new opportunities for remote and real-time suicide risk monitoring are emerging
[
36
]
. ML models embedded in smartphone applications could enable the continuous assessment of speech patterns through phone conversations, voice messages, or video content from social media platforms. Monitoring interactions on suicide prevention hotlines or online support platforms also presents valuable opportunities for early detection
[
21
,
22
,
8
]
. Additionally, longitudinal and within-person analyses of acoustic changes over time, as explored by
Min et al. 2023
, represent another promising avenue for capturing dynamic risk states and improving temporal sensitivity of classifiers.

A promising direction for future research is the joint analysis of acoustic (prosody, voice quality, rhythm) and semantic content (lexical choice, sentiment analysis, coherence of discourse). By combining speech signal features with automated transcription analysis, ML models could gain a more comprehensive understanding of both how patients speak and what they express, potentially enhancing early detection of suicidal risk
[
35
,
40
]
. This hybrid approach may provide clinicians with more interpretable and clinically relevant insights, ultimately improving decision-making and patient outcomes.

The recent 1st SpeechWellness Challenge (SW1) addresses several of these limitations by providing a standardized dataset and common evaluation metrics for suicidal risk detection using acoustic methods. SW1 includes speech data from 600 Chinese adolescents performing diverse tasks : spontaneous speech, reading tasks, emotional expression descriptions. Beyond offering data variety, the challenge implements multimodal approaches combining acoustic and linguistic features, with clearly anonymized and ethically validated data, overcoming previous issues of standardization, limited language representation (primarily English studies), and multimodal integration. SW1 represents an important step towards comprehensive, ethically-sound, and cross-culturally validated methods in suicide risk assessment
[
49
]
.

4.4
Ethical questioning and data security

Despite the significant contributions of AI in healthcare, its integration into clinical practice raises ethical, legal, and security concerns that must be carefully addressed. AI applications offer promising opportunities to enhance diagnosis and treatment, but also introduce challenges related to data privacy, algorithmic bias, and medico-legal accountability
[
29
,
27
]
. Ensuring robust data anonymization and obtaining explicit patient consent for the collection and use of personal health data are critical challenges that must be tackled before widespread adoption.

Additionally, AI-driven decision-making poses legal and ethical dilemmas, particularly concerning the role of clinicians and medico-legal responsibility in suicide risk assessment. A major concern is the reliance on black-box AI models, whose complexity and lack of transparency interfere with their acceptance in clinical settings. The explainability and interpretability of AI are therefore key factors in ensuring trust and accountability in medical decision-making
[
6
,
1
]
.

To safely and effectively implement AI for suicide risk detection, a well-defined medico-legal framework must be established, alongside strict adherence to data protection regulations and ethical guidelines. Moreover, cybersecurity risks remain a critical challenge, as the increasing complexity of AI models exposes healthcare systems to potential data breaches and misuse of sensitive patient information
[
23
]
.

Ultimately, AI should be regarded as a complementary tool, supporting but not replacing clinical expertise. The final decision should remain in the hands of clinicians, who must integrate AI-driven insights alongside recollection, psychological, and contextual factors to ensure comprehensive and patient-centered care.

5
Conclusion

This systematic review highlights the significant potential of acoustic features analyzed through AI and ML techniques in the context of suicide risk assessment. Despite promising results demonstrating the discriminative power of various acoustic features and multimodal approaches, current studies are constrained by methodological inconsistencies, small and homogeneous samples, and limited longitudinal data. The specificity of acoustic features in differentiating suicidal risk from depressive disorders and varying risk severity remains challenging, necessitating multimodal integration for improved accuracy. Future research must prioritize the development of standardized data collection protocols, larger and culturally diverse participant samples, and longitudinal methodologies. Addressing ethical considerations and data security concerns will also be crucial for the successful translation of AI-driven acoustic analysis tools into practical, reliable clinical aids for suicide prevention.

Features

Energy contour variability

(standard deviation)

Describes how energy fluctuates across the speech signal,

potentially reflecting emotional expressiveness.

Energy transitions

and variations

Changes in speech energy across time or syllables,

reflecting stress, rhythm, or emotion.

Intensity (dB)

Perceived loudness of the voice,

Loudness

A psychoacoustic measure of perceived sound strength,

influenced by both intensity and frequency content.

Psycho-acoustic sharpness

Describes how “sharp” or high-pitched a sound feels,

influenced by high-frequency energy distribution.

Root Mean Squared amplitude (dB)

A statistical measure of signal amplitude over time,

used to assess loudness and vocal strength.

Formant

Features

Formant Bandwidth (BW),

e.g., F1 Bandwidth mean,

Formant2 width (Hz)

Width of the resonance peak around each formant frequency (e.g., F1 Bandwidth),

related to the damping of vocal tract resonances.

Formant Energy

(EF1, EF2, EF3)

including

EF1db, EF2db, EF3db,

EF1N, EF2N

Energy associated with each formant.

Variants like EF1db express energy in decibels, while EF1N is typically a normalized value.

Formant Frequencies

(F1, F2, F3)

Frequencies corresponding to the resonant peaks of the vocal tract.

F1 relates to vowel height, F2 to vowel frontness,

F3 to speaker-specific traits like emotion or gender.

Glottal

and

Voice

Quality

Features

Depth of subharmonics (Hz)

Measures how deeply subharmonics (additional low-frequency components) are present in the voice signal,

which can indicate irregular vocal fold vibration or vocal effort.

Glottal Closure Instant

(GCI) variability

Temporal variability in the instants when the vocal folds close during phonation.

High variability can suggest irregular phonation.

Harmonics-to-Noise Ratio

(HNR, HNRdB)

Ratio of periodic (harmonic) energy to aperiodic (noise) energy in the voice signal.

Higher values indicate clearer, more stable phonation.

Jitter

(e.g., jitter local, jitter ppq5)

Measures cycle-to-cycle variations in fundamental frequency (F0), indicating pitch instability.

"Jitter local" is the average relative difference between consecutive cycles,

"ppq5" is a smoothed version over five cycles.

Normalized Amplitude

Quotient

(NAQ)

Ratio of glottal pulse amplitude to the maximum flow declination rate, normalized by F0.

It’s a marker of voice quality and breathiness.

Peakslope

Sharpness of the glottal excitation;

used to detect tense vs. breathy voice qualities.

Quasi-Open Quotient (QOQ)

Proportion of the glottal cycle during which the glottis is open.

Larger values indicate a more breathy voice.

Shimmer

Measures amplitude variation between consecutive glottal cycles, representing amplitude instability.

High shimmer can indicate voice disorders or emotional stress.

Voice quality ratings

(GRBASI scale)

A perceptual rating system including:

G: Grade (overall voice abnormality); R: Roughness; B: Breathiness; A: Asthenia (weakness); S: Strain; I: Instability

Prosodic

and

Pitch-

Related

Features

50th quartile frequency

(Hz)

Also known as the median frequency; half of the spectral energy lies below this frequency.

It reflects spectral balance.

F0 percentiles

(e.g., 80th percentile)

A statistical description of the distribution of fundamental frequency (pitch) values;

e.g., 80th percentile is the F0 value below which 80% of the data fall.

Fundamental Frequency

(F0 in Hz)

(variations in F0 across different speech tasks denoted as

F0*, F0**, F0***)

The base frequency of vocal fold vibration, perceived as pitch.

Measures of pitch change across different tasks, often used to assess prosodic adaptability or emotion.

Pitch variations over time

Temporal dynamics of F0,

reflecting intonation, expressiveness, or stress.

Spectral

and

Energy-

Based

Features

AlphaRatio mean

The ratio of spectral energy in low vs. high frequency bands (e.g., 50-1000 Hz vs. 1-5 kHz).

Higher values suggest a darker, less bright voice.

Chroma vector and deviation

From music signal processing, describing the intensity of each pitch class (e.g., C, C#, D, …) within a frame.

Deviation reflects stability or variability of harmonic structure.

Entropy of spectral energy

A measure of spectral flatness or unpredictability.

Higher entropy implies more noise-like, less tonal signals.

Flatness spectral roll-off

Indicates how quickly energy drops in higher frequencies;

flatter roll-off = more even spectral energy.

Hammarberg Index mean

The ratio of the strongest energy peak below 2 kHz to the strongest above 2 kHz,

used to assess vocal effort and brightness.

Mel-Frequency

Cepstral Coefficients

(MFCC),

including Delta MFCC11

Widely used features representing the short-term power spectrum of sound,

transformed to match human auditory perception.

Delta MFCC11 is a first-order temporal derivative of the 11th coefficient.

Peak Location (Hz)

Frequency at which the maximum amplitude occurs in the spectrum.

Power Spectral Density (PSD)

Shows how power is distributed over frequency.

Useful for detecting vocal characteristics and noise patterns.

SlopeV0-500Hz mean

The slope of the spectral energy between 0 and 500 Hz.

Can reveal vocal fold tension or phonatory style.

Spectral slope (Hz)

Overall slope of the energy spectrum;

steeper slopes often indicate breathier or less strained voices.

Speech Fluency

and

Disfluency

Speech disfluency measures

(hesitations, repetitions, etc.)

Indicators of disruptions in speech flow.

Useful for assessing anxiety, cognitive load, or mental health conditions.

Temporal

and

Rhythm

Features

Interval Length

Duration between vocal events (e.g., syllables or phonemes),

associated with speech tempo and rhythm.

Pause duration

Time during which no speech is present.

Can indicate cognitive load, hesitation, or emotional state.

Speech time duration

Total time during which the speaker is actively talking, excluding silences.

Transition features

Timing or spectral features that describe how smoothly

one phoneme or syllable transitions to the next.

Zero crossing rate

Number of times the signal crosses zero amplitude,

often used to distinguish voiced from unvoiced sounds.

France et al. 2000

Evaluate the ability of classifiers to discriminate

between RS, depressed, and control subjects

based on acoustic features.

- AM, F1, F3, and PSD were the most effective discriminators.

- Quadratic discriminant function achieved classification accuracy

of 75% (RS), 71% (depression), and 75% (control).

Multimodal classifiers

significantly improve classification

performance over single-feature models.

Ozdas et al. 2000

Assess the effectiveness of jitter in distinguishing

RS from NRS individuals, with statistical comparisons

between groups.

- ANOVA test: Jitter showed a significant difference

between RS and NRS (
p=0.0069
).

Classifier performance: 80% accuracy in differentiating

suicidal from non-suicidal individuals.

Jitter-based classification effectively discriminates

suicidal speech from healthy speech.

Ozdas et al. 2004b

Assess the discrimination performance between

RS, depressed, and control subjects using

MFCC acoustic features.

80% accuracy in classifying RS vs. depressed using the first

four MFCC with a GMM classifier.

MFCC-based classification performed well,

with GMM significantly outperforming the unimodal

Gaussian approach.

Ozdas et al. 2004a

Evaluate the discrimination performance between

RS, depressed, and control subjects using

multiple acoustic features.

T-test: Jitter was significantly different only between RS and CS;

spectral slope differed across all groups.

Classifier performance: - Jitter alone: 80% accuracy for RS vs. CS,

but poor for RS vs. depressed (60%).

- Spectral slope: 74% accuracy for RS vs. depressed.

- Combined jitter + slope: 85% accuracy (RS vs. CS), 75% (RS vs. depressed).

Multiparametric classification significantly improves

discrimination accuracy, highlighting the superiority of

feature combination over single-feature models.

Yingthawornsuk et al. 2006

Use a ML model to discriminate RS from NRS

individuals based on acoustic features.

Maintenance session: 77% accuracy for differentiating

depressed from RS patients, 85% for RS vs. remitted patients.

Reading session: 82% accuracy for RS vs. depressed patients.

\to
PSD was the most effective feature for distinguishing

remitted speech from depressive and RS speech.

Reading-based classification showed strong performance

and may be a valuable approach for suicide risk assessment.

Kaymaz-Keskinpala et al. 2007b

Distinguish RS from MDD patients

Best : 93% accuracy for RS classification in male reading task

Higher results for only RS or only MDD patients, lower for both

Evaluate the classification performance between RS,

depressed, and remitted individuals using spectral

acoustic features.

- 87% accuracy for women (reading session) using PSD2.

- Classification improved from 69.23% to 72.53% (maintenance session)

and 67.82% to 73.37% (reading session) when using 8 MFCC instead of 4.

MFCC and PSDs effectively distinguish RS from NRS,

with better performance in controlled reading sessions

than in interviews.

Yingthawornsuk 2007

Evaluate the classification performance between RS

and depressed NRS women using spectral acoustic features.

Interview session: PSD1, PSD2, CF2, and BW2

were the most discriminating features.

Reading session: PSD1, PSD2, and CF2

achieved 90.33% accuracy.

PSD1, PSD2, CF2, and BW2 effectively distinguish RS

from NRS women, with better classification

performance in the reading session.

Yingthawornsuk and Shiavi 2008

Evaluate the classification performance between RS,

depressed, and remitted men using spectral

acoustic features with a GMM classifier.

- Depressed vs. RS: 88.5% accuracy

- Remitted vs. RS: 90.25% accuracy

Spontaneous (interview) session:

- Depressed vs. RS: 85.58% accuracy

- Remitted vs. RS: 81.08% accuracy

Most discriminating features: CF4, CF3, PSD2

Classification accuracy varies by speech type,

with better performance in automatic (reading)

speech samples than in spontaneous (interview) speech.

Compare speaker normalization techniques

Accuracy from 75% to 90% depending on technique
