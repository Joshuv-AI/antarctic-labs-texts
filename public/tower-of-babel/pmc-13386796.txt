Malignant lymph nodes of the head and neck: imaging pearls, pitfalls, and practical approach to reporting in the era of extranodal extension
Authors: Pavan M Brahmbhatt, Emma Bentley, Mark A Edgar, Patricia A Rhyner, Alok A Bhatt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate characterization and reporting of cervical lymph nodes is critical when evaluating head and neck malignancies, as findings directly impact staging, treatment planning, and prognosis. Knowledge of lymphatic drainage patterns predicts nodal spread of disease and conversely, may help in identifying primary malignancy. Variability in size criteria for an abnormal lymph node across literature makes reporting difficult when used in isolation; however, careful attention to morphologic changes improves diagnostic accuracy. FDG-PET imaging serves as a valuable complementary tool when available, detecting metabolically active nodes that may appear normal on anatomic imaging. Identifying radiologic features of extranodal extension impacts AJCC staging and subsequently, treatment course. For lymphoproliferative disorders, diffuse lymphadenopathy presents a challenge in reporting size and number of lymph nodes. This article provides a systematic, reproducible checklist approach for lymph node reporting in the head and neck, thereby reducing inter-reader variability and producing a useful radiologic report for the multidisciplinary team.Clinical trial number Not applicable.Keywords: Lymph nodes, Head and neck malignancy, Extranodal extension, Staging, Oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma, Lymphoproliferative disorders, Reporting

Introduction

Identification and accurate characterization of lymph nodes are essential due to their potential involvement in various diseases, particularly primary head and neck malignancies. Lymph nodes serve as critical filtration points within the reticuloendothelial system and regulate immune response through interactions between lymphocytes and antigen-presenting cells [1]. Lymph nodes are often the first site for metastasis, making detection vital for accurate cancer staging, prognosis, and treatment planning. Mischaracterization can lead to incorrect diagnoses and suboptimal treatment, underscoring need for precise imaging evaluation and standardized reporting. Contrast-enhanced CT or MRI, in conjunction with PET imaging has significantly enhanced our ability to assess lymph node abnormalities, yet challenges remain in distinguishing between benign and malignant nodes. A comprehensive understanding of lymph node anatomy, pathology, and normal versus abnormal imaging characteristics is crucial for improving diagnostic accuracy and optimizing patient care. This article reviews cervical lymph node anatomy and drainage patterns, imaging features of abnormal lymph nodes, and key reporting considerations in primary head and neck malignancies and lymphoproliferative disease with particular emphasis on extranodal extension.

Lymph node anatomy

Lymph nodes are small, reniform or kidney shaped structures that play a crucial role in the immune system by filtering lymph fluid, trapping foreign particles, and facilitating activation of lymphocytes. Anatomically, lymph nodes consist of multiple lymphoid lobules surrounded by lymph-filled sinuses enclosed by a capsule (Fig. 1). The lobules comprise three main compartments: cortex, paracortex, and medulla, each housing specific B and T lymphocytes [1]. On CT and MRI, normal lymph nodes are well-defined, homogenous structures with a central fatty hilum, while on ultrasound they appear as hypoechoic structures with a central echogenic hilum (Fig. 2) [2, 3]. The arterial supply to lymph nodes is typically derived from small branches of nearby arteries, while venous drainage follows a similar pattern, ensuring sufficient circulation and immune surveillance [1].

Intranodal disease usually changes the imaging appearance of the node. Abnormal lymph nodes may enlarge, have altered shape and appearance, or increase in visible number. Changes in shape and appearance may be seen before size abnormality and include inhomogeneity, heterogeneous enhancement, and loss of normal fatty hilum (Fig. 3) [2, 4]. Familiarity with normal variations in lymph node appearance with age is necessary to avoid false positives. In general, larger lymph nodes are present in younger individuals. With increasing age, nodes decrease in number and become smaller due to fibrosis, lipomatosis, and a decrease in postcapillary vessels, ultimately leading to decreased lymphoid tissue [5]. Correlation with PET imaging, which detects abnormally increased metabolic activity, provides additional information and is complementary to CT, MRI, and ultrasound when available [6, 7].

Head & neck lymphatic drainage patterns

Familiarity with drainage patterns of head and neck lymph nodes is crucial for predicting spread of disease in primary head and neck cancers. Conversely, abnormal lymph nodes in a particular region can help identify location of a primary head and neck malignancy. Cervical lymph nodes and lymphatic drainage in the neck can be organized based on anatomic location or seven stations/levels, each receiving lymphatic fluid from distinct anatomical areas. Station locations and boundaries are illustrated in Fig. 4 and drainage territories of each station are outlined in Table 1. All stations ultimately drain into the jugular trunk and then to the lymphatic duct on the right and the thoracic duct on the left [8]. Knowledge of drainage directs search pattern; for example, in the setting of oropharyngeal carcinoma, level II and III nodal stations must carefully be interrogated for metastatic nodal disease. Lymph nodes not assigned to nodal stations I to VII include the parotid and retropharyngeal lymph nodes, which are often overlooked on imaging. The parotid space, suboccipital, and retro- or subauricular regions are the main nodal basins for cutaneous carcinomas of the scalp [9]. Nasopharyngeal carcinoma often metastasizes to retropharyngeal lymph nodes [10]. Significant changes in lymphatic drainage can occur following surgery and radiation, and therefore, post-treatment imaging must be interpreted in the context of given therapy. Studies have shown surgical lymph node dissection can cause significant disruptions in normal lymphatic flow, including vessel rupture and development of collateral lymphatic vessels [11]. In head and neck cancer, treatment often results in lymphatic dermal backflow, potentially leading to lymphedema and enlarged lymph nodes. Lymphedema is often transient but can persist in some patients. In the post-treatment setting, an enlarging lymph node may be located at an atypical and unexpected location and must be differentiated between normal enlargement due to altered lymphatic drainage or recurrent nodal metastatic disease [12]. PET imaging is helpful in distinguishing a new metastatic lymph node in this setting, and biopsy may be necessary for definitive characterization [6, 7].

Imaging findings in metastatic lymph nodes

The American Joint Commission on Cancer (AJCC) guidelines on staging head and neck cancers considers three categories when evaluating metastatic cervical lymph nodes for staging: nodal size in greatest dimension, laterality of a lymph node (ipsilateral versus contralateral), and presence of extranodal extension [13, 14]. A radiology report, therefore, must mention these findings.

Size criteria

Variability exists in literature regarding size criteria to classify a node as abnormal, leading to confusion amongst radiologists, referring clinicians, and patients. There is the usual balance between sensitivity and specificity of disease. Classifications with a larger size cut off have lower sensitivity for disease detection, missing small but pathologic nodes, but higher specificity with low false positive results. Some publications classify pathologically enlarged lymph nodes as greater than 1.5 cm in longitudinal diameter of level I and II nodes, greater than 0.8 cm in retropharyngeal nodes, and greater than 1 cm in other cervical nodal chains. Others favor using the short axis and consider nodes abnormal if lymph nodes at level I and II exceed 1.1 cm, retropharyngeal nodes exceed 0.5 cm, and other cervical nodes exceed 1 cm [15, 16]. Lack of consensus in literature leads to significant variability in radiology reports which may cause confusion in staging and treatment response. The probability of metastasis increases with increasing abnormal nodal size [4]. In cases of primary head and neck malignancy, we report long-axis measurement to ensure concordance with AJCC staging and replicate what the examining clinician reports. In general, lymph nodes larger than 6 cm designate N3 disease, while nodes between 3 and 6 cm designate N2 disease for HPV-negative oropharyngeal carcinoma, hypopharyngeal and laryngeal carcinomas. Nodal masses greater than 6 cm are usually conglomerate nodes with extranodal extension of disease [13, 14]. Although size is a helpful factor in identifying metastasis, it is estimated up to 50% of metastatic nodes measure less than 0.5 cm, underscoring the importance of morphologic and metabolic changes and avoiding overreliance on size criteria alone [17]. Reactive nodes present additional difficulty, as they may be considered abnormal by size criteria but be a normal physiologic response. For this reason, most patients at our institution with a newly diagnosed head and neck primary malignancy undergo a contrast-enhanced CT and PET imaging (Fig. 5); exceptions include those with T1 disease in the setting of vocal cord or oral cavity carcinoma because the risk for nodal disease and distant metastasis is extremely low [6, 7, 18].

Imaging findings

Anatomic nodal characteristics are obtained with CT, MRI, and ultrasound, while metabolic characteristics are obtained with PET imaging. Key morphologic changes suggesting metastatic disease include loss of reniform shape, loss of fatty hilum, and necrotic and cystic changes (Figs. 3 and 6) [2, 3, 15]. Necrosis within a lymph node appears as internal debris, which may be due to severe infection, ischemia, or rapid tumor growth. A cystic lymph node has a well-defined fluid-filled cavity, which may be a manifestation of necrosis. HPV-associated oropharyngeal carcinoma (HPV-OPSCC) results in degradation of keratin and cellular debris within the metastatic lymph node deposits. As a result, blockage of lymphatic flow causes necrosis and, ultimately, a cystic appearance [19, 20]. Necrosis and cystic changes have the highest specificity for metastatic disease [4].

On ultrasound, nodal disease manifests as a more rounded shape and/or loss of the echogenic fatty hilum (Fig. 7). In some cases, tumor infiltration may result in cortical thickening. On CT, a heterogeneous node with areas of central low attenuation is consistent with necrosis, while a well-defined fluid-filled cavity with peripheral enhancement is consistent with cystic change; both are considered abnormal regardless of size. On MRI, nodal necrosis and cystic change show high T2 and low T1 signal with heterogeneous post-contrast enhancement (Fig. 6) [2, 4]. Necrosis and cystic change in nodes are commonly seen in the setting of HPV-OPSCC and papillary thyroid carcinoma (Figs. 6 and 7, respectively) but can also be seen in cases of nasopharyngeal carcinoma and lymphoma [19–21]. A mixed cystic and calcified lymph node favors papillary thyroid carcinoma [22]. In an adult patient, a cystic lymph node should not be mistaken for a branchial cleft cyst or abscess (Fig. 8). In other words, necrotic or cystic lymph nodes at level II should be considered metastasis until proven otherwise [23].

PET imaging plays an important and complementary role in evaluating head and neck malignancy, allowing for accurate staging of nodal and distant metastatic disease. The workhorse tracer for malignancy assessment is F-18 Fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG), which allows for semi-quantitative assessment of tumor compared to normal background uptake [6, 7]. Since size criteria alone has low sensitivity for identifying abnormal nodes, correlating anatomic CT findings with metabolic PET imaging allows for improved identification of metastatic nodes that maintain normal size and otherwise may have been missed (Fig. 5). An SUVmax threshold of approximately 4.05 has been reported for malignant cervical nodes (sensitivity 92%, specificity 88%) [24]. Although the negative predictive value of FDG PET-CT for cervical nodal disease is high (92%), the corresponding false negative rate (29%) and positive predictive values reportedly ranging from 13% to 94% warrants caution in the era of increasing non-surgical treatment and clinical staging [25]. Necrotic and cystic lymph nodes, although clearly abnormal, may not have corresponding hypermetabolic activity; uptake, if any, is more likely to be at the periphery and absent in the central necrotic and cystic portion (Fig. 8).

Extranodal extension

Extranodal extension (ENE) is often discussed during tumor boards because its presence serves as a negative prognostic factor and is associated with a high risk for disease progression and metastasis [26]. Definitive identification of ENE on imaging, referred to as iENE, may be used to guide treatment, potentially decreasing toxicity and cost of trimodal therapy – surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy. The term ENE has evolved from extracapsular extension, which was defined as extension of tumor beyond the lymph node capsule into the surrounding tissues. However, tumor can also extend beyond the lymph node parenchyma at the hilum where there is no capsule, thus the term ENE is now considered appropriate terminology [26, 27]. Histopathology was the gold standard for determining ENE (Fig. 9), however, iENE has been identified as an independent risk factor for overall survival of patients with head and neck cancer, and therefore, has been incorporated into AJCC version 9 of nasopharyngeal carcinoma, HPV-OPSCC, and salivary glands [28, 29].

Several grading systems have been proposed in the literature; all recognizing imaging findings are on a spectrum ranging from infiltration of surrounding fat and progressing to involvement of adjacent structures. In July 2024, a Delphi consensus recommendation by Henson et al. consisting of an international consortium of 21 national head and neck oncology groups was released to assist in identifying and standardizing reporting of iENE. Recent biopsy which may result in perinodal fat stranding and capsule irregularity was identified as a potential pitfall for determining iENE. The group also proposed a four-tier classification system, Grades 0 to 3. Grade 0 is defined as absence of iENE, Grade 1 as irregular, ill-defined nodal margins or extension into perinodal fat, Grade 2 as tumor extension beyond the nodal margin of two or more adjoining nodes, thus appearing as a conglomerate, and Grade 3 as clear extension into surrounding structures. A size greater than 3 cm carries a higher risk for ENE [4]. Capsular thickening and necrotic or cystic changes are not recommended as criteria for diagnosing iENE [26].

More recently, a joint task force from the Asian-Oceanian Society of Neuroradiology and Head & Neck Radiology (AOSHNR), American Society of Head and Neck Radiology (ASHNR), and European Society of Head & Neck Radiology (ESHNR) provides radiologists with a structured framework for identifying iENE. This task force stratifies iENE into four categories – no iENE, perinodal fat invasion, coalescent nodes, and adjacent structure invasion—and limits grading to unequivocal radiologic findings [30]. To minimize overcalling, it introduces detailed, structure-specific criteria (e.g., loss of fat planes with adjacent muscle does not constitute muscle invasion; arterial involvement requires near-circumferential encasement; venous involvement requires tumor thrombus). This classification system is offered in the AJCC Version 9 for HPV-OPSCC, however, grading level has not been integrated into the N category [29].

Recent investigations evaluating iENE demonstrate important limitations in diagnostic performance and reproducibility. Established radiographic criteria for iENE on CT demonstrate limited interobserver agreement, even among expert neuroradiologists and across multidisciplinary clinicians, raising concerns regarding reliability in routine clinical decision-making [31, 32]. Although MRI may offer relatively higher specificity compared with CT, overall diagnostic consistency remains suboptimal [33]. Together, these findings underscore the need for stronger standardized interpretation frameworks, and potentially adjunctive diagnostic tools to improve preoperative ENE assessment and risk stratification. A discussion with multidisciplinary tumor board stakeholders to establish consensus can help ensure consistent reporting across readers within an institution and supports uniform patient care. At our center, unequivocal iENE is defined as definitive extension into adjacent musculature, salivary glands, cutaneous soft tissue, or neurovascular structures. Findings that are equivocal or merely suggestive are considered ENE-negative to avoid stage migration. Spectrum of equivocal versus unequivocal imaging examples are given in Fig. 10. Clinical or pathological ENE designates N3 category disease for HPV-negative oropharyngeal and laryngeal carcinoma, according to the AJCC 8th edition [14]. Incorporation of iENE is seen in the AJCC 9th version of nasopharyngeal carcinoma staging where advanced or unequivocal iENE designates N3 category disease without the need for pathologic confirmation [32]. In AJCC Version 9 for HPV-OPSCC, unequivocal iENE now increases the clinical N category by one level [27]. For salivary gland tumors, unequivocal iENE is N2 category disease. These updates reflect the importance of imaging in staging and prognostic significance of iENE, which is associated with worse outcomes, thus warranting intensified treatment [13, 14, 29, 34].

Lymphoproliferative disorders

Lymphoproliferative diseases (LPD) require special consideration with respect to nodal reporting and measurements. Lymphocytes are made in bone marrow and are found in blood and lymph tissue throughout the body. They play an integral role in immunity and have two main subtypes: B-cells (humoral immunity) and T-cells (cell-mediated immunity). LPD refers to the overproduction of lymphocytes, often occurring in immunocompromised patients. LPD may be secondary to an immunologic process predisposing the patient to lymphoma or other lymphoid blood cancers (T- and B-cell subtypes of leukemias and lymphomas). While both leukemia and lymphoma can present with lymphadenopathy, nodal lymphoma is more common as the disease originates in lymphatic tissue, whereas leukemia typically originates in bone marrow (Fig. 11) [35].

As treatment for lymphoma advances, individualized treatment plans and evaluation of treatment response are crucial. Multiple classification systems have been proposed for nodal assessments to better characterize radiologic response to treatment. The original Cheson criteria, the revised Cheson criteria, and the Lugano criteria (a further revision of Cheson) have all utilized bi-dimensional nodal assessment to stage treatment response. LYRIC (Lymphoma Response to Immunomodulatory Therapy Criteria) was developed as a modification to Lugano in patients on immunotherapy and provides three indeterminate response categories, which also rely on bi-dimensional nodal evaluation. RECIL criteria are based on RECIST 1.1 and is a simplified version of Lugano, requiring only three target lesions (as opposed to 6) and uses uni-dimensional nodal assessment. Selected target lesions should be dispersed throughout the body for the most accurate systemic assessment regardless of criteria used, and ideally includes retroperitoneal and mediastinal targets, if present. To be considered a target lesion by Lugano criteria, nodal disease must have a greatest transverse diameter (GTD) > 1.5 cm, and extranodal disease must have GTD > 1 cm. The RECIL criterion simplifies the definition of a target lesion to any nodal or extranodal disease ≥ 1.5 cm GTD [36]. Given potential for widespread involvement of lymphoma and variability in requested measurements by referring providers or clinical trials, we recommend reporting four lymph nodes in the neck, two on the right and two on the left, with bi-dimensional size measured on axial images.

For primary head and neck malignancies, harmonizing the report with AJCC criteria used for nodal staging is best. Report the maximum diameter of any nodes which are consistent or believed to be involved with disease using radiologic features described earlier. Use PET imaging as a complementary modality to evaluate for hypermetabolic uptake in lymph nodes, especially those perceived to be normal on CT and are located at the station of expected primary drainage. In addition to maximum diameter, mention laterality (right or left neck) of the abnormal node. Lastly, look for features of unequivocal extranodal extension and consolidate all findings into the report, with a concise summary in the impression. For reproducibility, clearly provide series and image numbers of nodes assessed in the findings section, allowing for better evaluation of treatment response on follow up examinations, or for potential biopsy request.

Lymphoproliferative diseases present a unique challenge due to extensive lymphadenopathy and various classifications. At our institution we provide both short- and long-axis nodal measurements on axial images, typically two in the right neck and two in the left neck in cases of diffuse disease, along with documentation of series and image number of each lymph node. This reporting style allows for up to four target lesions in the head and neck alone and bi-dimensional characterization allows for evaluation of treatment response regardless of the criterion used by treating clinicians or by clinical trials.

Borderline lymph nodes, which are not enlarged by size criteria or do not have abnormal morphology should be mentioned in the report, alerting the clinician to possible disease involvement or need for follow-up. Multiple times during the day, radiologists make decisions about whether a lymph node is normal or represents disease. While following described recommendations is important, the clinical setting and good judgment should also influence the imaging report. An elderly patient with multiple lymph nodes more in number than typically seen, but not enlarged by size criteria, may prompt workup for lymphoproliferative disease. In a young, healthy patient without known malignant disease or acute infection, one should not report a level II 1.6 cm node as pathologic. Thus, the phrase “resist the urge to measure normal nodes” has become dogma in head and neck imaging.

It is in the setting of malignant disease that objective criteria are necessary. Anatomic criteria alone are flawed; size, shape and whether nodes are in a cluster may aid detecting nodal metastatic disease but can be equivocal in cases. A 2.0 cm necrotic node is always pathologic in malignant head and neck disease, especially when the node is in the appropriate drainage basin. However, a round 1.4 cm node in level III in a patient with pyriform sinus cancer, for example, tests our imperfect criteria. PET scan has added sensitivity and specificity but is also imperfect due to variability in uptake.

Conclusion

Imaging and accurate characterization of lymph nodes is an integral part in the workup and staging of primary head and neck malignancies and lymphoproliferative disease. Often, limitations exist in distinguishing abnormal from normal lymph nodes on CT, MRI, or ultrasound, and may need a complementary PET scan or biopsy for definitive characterization; it is critical we accept imaging and interpretative limitations. Familiarity with drainage patterns, morphologic changes, and features of ENE on imaging prevents overreliance on size criteria alone and ensures a high-quality report. A systematic and standardized method for evaluation and reporting must be used to reduce inter-reader variability, increase accuracy, and facilitate meaningful contribution to the multidisciplinary team.
