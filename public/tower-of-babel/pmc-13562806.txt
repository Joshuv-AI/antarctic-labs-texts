Integrating the Health Belief Model and the Theory of Planned Behavior to Decode Willingness to Pay for Cancer Screening: Evidence From Community‐Based Cross‐Sectional Surveys in China
Authors: Jianwei Wang, Shuangshuang Cao, Xingzhou Wang, Jiaojiao Li, Zhongli Wang, Gaopei Zhu, Fuzhong Xue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundWillingness to pay for cancer screening has been widely adopted to examine barriers to screening. This study integrated the Health Belief Model and the Theory of Planned Behavior to explore the correlational pathways linked to WTP‐CS among Chinese community‐dwelling residents.MethodsA multistage stratified survey was carried out in a cancer early screening demonstration zone. Questionnaires assessed demographic, psychosocial, HBM, TPB variables, and WTP‐CS. Hierarchical logistic regression identified correlates; bivariate correlation analyses described pairwise associations; path analysis tested the hypothesized HBM‐TPB integration theoretical framework.ResultsA total of 4575 residents (mean age 70.63 years, SD = 7.85) were enrolled. Hierarchical logistic regression models showed incremental variance for WTP‐CS reaching 34.5% across Model 1 to Model 4 (Δ McFadden R 2 = 34.5%). Path analysis revealed adequate model fit (CFI = 0.94, RMSEA = 0.05) and explained 48.91% of the variance in WTP‐CS. TPB components dominated both the direct and indirect pathways. Subjective norms showed the strongest positive association with WTP‐CS (β = 0.313, p < 0.001). Perceived benefits displayed a positive indirect association with WTP‐CS via perceived confidence and subjective norms (β = 0.062, p < 0.001). Age demonstrated a negative correlational linkage with WTP‐CS through a sequential chain involving knowledge, perceived benefits, and attitude towards behaviors (β = −0.007, p < 0.001). The observed patterns supported the hypothesized pathways of the HBM‐TPB integration framework.ConclusionA potential intention‐behavior gap was observed, as favorable WTP‐CS did not necessarily correspond to higher actual screening participation. The HBM‐TPB framework tentatively maps multi‐layered direct and indirect correlational pathways between demographic, psychological, HBM, TPB variables and WTP‐CS among Chinese community‐dwelling residents. This framework may explore plausible socio‐psychological correlational linkages associated with WTP‐CS, expand empirical research, and deliver tentative, context‐specific insights for subsequent investigations.Trial RegistrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry, ID: ChiCTR2400088321Keywords: cancer screening, Health Belief Model, integration, path analysis, Theory of Planned Behavior, willingness to pay

Introduction

Cancer represents a formidable global health challenge. In 2022 alone, the world witnessed 20 million new cancer cases and 9.7 million cancer‐related deaths, projected to surge to 35 million annual cases by 2050 [1, 2]. Early screening reduces mortality, evidenced by 20% and > 50% declines in lung and breast cancer deaths, respectively, through timely detection and treatment advances [3, 4, 5]. Despite the established efficacy, global screening adherence remains suboptimal [6, 7]. As documented, participation rates in developing countries fall below 50%, while in China's urban screening programs, the rates range from 17% to 40% [8].

Common barriers to cancer screening include low health literacy, socioeconomic disparities, procedural discomfort, and financial burdens [9]. Notably, the financial burden has emerged as a significant challenge to participation in cancer screening [10]. To assess the financial viability of services, willingness to pay (WTP) has been extensively reported as a proportion of the actual price of the service [11]. Accordingly, a growing number of studies have taken willingness to pay for cancer screening (WTP‐CS) as a core outcome variable to examine public subjective attitudes and behavioral intentions towards screening [12, 13, 14, 15]. By employing discrete choice experiments, preference elicitation methods, meta‐analyses, and regression modeling, current WTP‐CS studies have improved our understanding of public comprehension of cancer screening payment thresholds, marginal substitution rates, and relevant correlates of screening payment, providing empirical evidence for optimizing population‐based cancer screening strategies [16, 17, 18]. While prior studies have extensively explored factors associated with WTP‐CS, two major methodological limitations remain: (1) most WTP‐CS investigations lack a theoretical framework of social behavior in research design, which weakens the explanatory power of the models [19, 20], and (2) some existing studies predominantly focus on independent variable associations while neglecting complex interrelationships and sequential pathways linked to WTP‐CS, which hinders comprehensive elucidation of its underlying correlational structures [13]. Accordingly, this study aims to enrich evidence concerning cancer screening promotion by investigating public cognitive and motivational correlates of WTP‐CS and exploring how these psychological variables correlate with individuals' WTP‐CS.

Addressing the “intention‐behavior gap” in WTP‐CS necessitates the integration of behavioral change theories [21]. The Health Belief Model (HBM) and the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) remain the best‐known theories in health behavior research. HBM emphasizes risk perception, incorporating susceptibility, severity, benefits, barriers, confidence or self‐efficacy, health motivation, and cues to action [22]. Bertakis adapted the HBM standardized compliance questionnaire, which consists of three constructs: perceived susceptibility, severity, and benefits [23]. Aiken et al. [24] developed a 17‐item HBM scale encompassing four constructs. The Champion's Health Belief Model Scale (CHBMS) has been expanded to six constructs (susceptibility, severity, benefits, barriers, health motivation, and confidence) in the context of breast cancer screening [25]. Concurrently, TPB links behavior intention to attitude towards behaviors, subjective norms, and perceived behavior control [26]. Conceptually, it should be clarified that WTP is neither equivalent to behavioral intention as defined by the TPB, nor to actual cancer screening behavior. WTP quantifies the hypothetical monetary amount an individual is prepared to pay for a service, reflecting people's economic evaluation and perceived value. Accordingly, this study defines WTP‐CS as behavioral readiness, a construct that is correlated with but essentially different from cancer screening intention. This research approach conforms to the prevailing paradigm in environmental and health economics, where WTP is widely regarded as an independent and valid outcome variable rather than a direct proxy for behavioral intention [27, 28]. However, while HBM and TPB are grounded in the expectancy value theory, they provide distinct viewpoints on behavioral decision‐making processes [29]. Multiple studies have confirmed the limitations of standalone models: the HBM neglects social pressures and socio‐ecological disparities [30], while the TPB overlooks motivational drivers [31]. Integrating these frameworks is thereby expected to enhance predictive power and support a holistic analysis of the correlational pathways associated with WTP‐CS [32]. The integration explores pathways from cognitive variables to WTP‐CS and offers references for formulating targeted interventions to enhance WTP‐CS and advancing policy design and theoretical applications in WTP‐CS research.

This study developed an HBM‐TPB integration theoretical framework to examine correlates and mediating pathways of WTP‐CS in the Chinese community settings (Figure 1). The two main objectives of this study were: (a) to explore the associated variables of WTP‐CS by gradually incorporating four different categories of variables, namely demographic variables, psychosocial variables, HBM constructs, and TPB components; and (b) to explore the direct and indirect pathways between associated variables and WTP‐CS via path analysis under the HBM‐TPB integration theoretical framework. Consistent with the above research objectives, a conceptual model was developed to illustrate four core correlational pathways:

Demographic variables exhibit directly correlational paths with psychosocial variables and correlate with the HBM constructs, TPB components, and WTP‐CS through direct and indirect pathways.

Psychosocial variables correlate with the HBM constructs, TPB components, and WTP‐CS via direct and indirect pathways.

The HBM constructs present direct correlational links with the TPB components and relate to WTP‐CS through direct and indirect pathways.

The TPB component shows direct and indirect correlational pathways with the WTP‐CS.

Methods

Study Design

A population‐based cross‐sectional study used a multistage, stratified random sampling method to survey community‐dwelling residents in the Boxing District administratively under Binzhou City, Shandong Province. This district was purposively selected as it has been designated a provincial‐level demonstration zone for cancer early screening programs by the Health Commission of Shandong Province, which facilitated a systematic investigation of the associations between the HBM‐TPB integration theoretical framework variables and population‐level WTP‐CS. In detail, people who met the following inclusion criteria were included: (1) aged 40 or above, (2) permanent residents with a local household registration of at least 6 months, (3) showed no evidence of psychiatric conditions or difficulty communicating, and (4) without a previous diagnosis of cancer; people were excluded if they had a documented history of cancer before enrolment.

Measure

Since there was no existing questionnaire to fit the measurement requirements of this study, researchers developed the survey instruments by drawing inspiration from existing HBM and TPB questionnaires that have been successfully used in breast cancer screening [33], health beliefs of colorectal cancer screening [34, 35], cervical cancer screening intention [36]. Studies focused on cancer risk perception [37], social network [38, 39], social support [40, 41], and cancer stigma [42, 43] have also inspired the preparation of the questionnaire. The questionnaire used in this study was specifically developed for the current research with reference to the above published questionnaires, which consisted of five sections: demographic variables, psychosocial variables, HBM constructs, TPB components, and WTP‐CS (Table S1).

Demographic Variables

Demographic variables included gender, age, marital status, educational level, work status, household monthly income per capita, health insurance, physical exercise, self‐rated health, and family history of tumors.

Psychosocial Variables

Cancer Screening Knowledge Questionnaire

A 10‐item questionnaire was developed to assess participants' knowledge regarding cancer screening, which consisted of statements describing effective cancer prevention measures, screening, and management. The questionnaire was adapted from the national survey scale of cancer literacy in China [44], with targeted revisions and content optimization tailored to the research scenario and objectives of this study. One example question is “Cancer screening is a special physical examination for cancer based on the actual situation of individuals, which has been able to detect some common types of cancers at an early stage.” Participants were asked to respond with “yes,” “no,” or “not sure” to each item, with 1 mark awarded for a correct answer and 0 for an incorrect answer or unclear options. Total scores (0 to 10) were calculated for each of the 10 items to reflect respondents' overall level of knowledge, with higher scores indicating a higher level of knowledge. The internal consistency of the questionnaire is reliable, with a Cronbach's alpha coefficient (α) of 0.81.

Cancer Stigma Questionnaire

A 25‐item questionnaire was adapted from studies by Marlow et al., who developed the Cancer Stigma Scale (CASS) to measure attitudes towards cancer in non‐cancer patients [42]. The questionnaire consists of 25 items, divided into six domains: Awkwardness, Severity, Avoidance, Policy Opposition, Personal Responsibility, and Financial Discrimination. Each item was measured following a 6‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree). The questionnaire has been tested, and Cronbach's α for the overall scale was 0.85, demonstrating adequate internal consistency.

Social Support Questionnaire

An 8‐item questionnaire based on the modified Medical Outcomes Study Social Support Survey (mMOS‐SS) [40]. Two dimensions correspond to emotional social support (someone available to have a good time with; to turn to for suggestions about dealing with a personal problem; who understands your problems; to love and make you feel wanted), and instrumental social support (help if confined to bed; to take you to the doctor; to prepare meals if you are unable; to help with daily chores) [45]. Scores for each item were scaled from 1 to 5, with higher scores indicating greater support. The Cronbach's α for the social support questionnaire was 0.96, indicating excellent internal consistency reliability, demonstrating high homogeneity among items and stable measurement of the target construct.

Social Network Questionnaire

Utilized the 6‐item Lubben Social Networking Scale (LSNS‐6), a standardized abbreviated version of the LSNS created by Lubben and Gironda [46]. With high internal consistency, a stable factor structure, and a high correlation with standardized variables, it is one of the most widely used instruments for assessing and measuring social network and is commonly employed in healthcare research [47]. The LSNS‐6 includes six items that measure the size of active and intimate networks of family and friends with whom they could talk to or call for help. The LSNS‐6 is constructed from a set of three questions that assess relatives' ties and a comparable set of three questions that assess friends' ties, each with three questions: (1) How many relatives/friends do you see or hear from at least once a month? (2) How many relatives/friends do you feel at ease with that you can talk about private matters? (3) How many relatives/friends do you feel close to such that you could call on them for help? Every question has 5 same options as answers: 0 = none, 1 = one, 2 = two, 3 = three or four, 4 = five through eight, and 5 = nine or more. The maximum score of the LSNS‐6 is 30, which is an equally weighted sum of the six items. The LSNS‐6 demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach's α of 0.87.

Risk Perception Questionnaire

The 6‐item Cancer Worry Scale (CWS‐6) was utilized in this study, a commonly used instrument to assess cancer worry, rated on a 4‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“never”) to 4 (“almost always”). Possible scores range from 6 to 24, with higher scores indicating more worry [48]. As a well‐established cancer risk perception scale, the CWS is valuable for predicting cancer risk perception [37]. The internal consistency of the Risk Perception Questionnaire was excellent, with a Cronbach's alpha of 0.93.

HBM Constructs

Existing studies operationalize HBM constructs inconsistently [49]. To address this heterogeneity, we employed a dual approach: (1) retaining the standardized CHBMS for core HBM assessment, and (2) supplementing with a 6‐item Cues to Action questionnaire guided by HBM theory. This design enhances conceptual coverage while ensuring psychometric comparability with prior CHBMS applications.

Champion's Health Belief Model Scale (CHBMS)

The 36‐item scale has six subscales and an additional subscale asking participants to comment on the instrument itself [35]: perceived susceptibility (5 items), perceived severity (7 items), perceived benefits (6 items), perceived barriers (6 items), health motivation (4 items), perceived confidence (5 items), and scale items (3 items). Items are scored on a Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with higher scores indicating a greater positive belief about cancer screening. In the present study, Cronbach's α for the CHBMS was 0.83.

Cues to Action Questionnaire

A 6‐item questionnaire was developed to evaluate contextual triggers for WTP‐CS. Items assessed exposure to screening‐promoting stimuli (e.g., “Have you verified the authenticity of a cancer screening notification (e.g., phone call/text message)?”) using a dichotomous response format (yes = 1, no = 0), with total scores ranging from 0 to 6. The Cues to Action Questionnaire demonstrated good internal consistency with an acceptable Cronbach's α of 0.83.

TPB Components

TPB Cancer Screening Intention Questionnaire

A 23‐item questionnaire of the TPB Scale for Cervical Cancer Screening Intention was utilized [36], comprising four dimensions: attitude towards behaviors (7 items), subjective norms (6 items), perceived behavioral control (7 items), and behavioral intention (3 items). Responses were recorded on a 5‐point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree), and scores for each item were scaled from 1 to 5. WTP‐CS was defined as behavioral readiness in this study, which differs from behavioral intention. Given our focus on individuals' screening readiness instead of pure psychological intention, behavioral intention was excluded from the analysis. Three dimensions of the questionnaire (attitude towards behaviors, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control) were adopted for measurement, and the three dimensions presented good internal consistency confidence (20 items, Cronbach's α = 0.88).

It should be acknowledged that no formal pilot testing was conducted prior to the main survey, primarily due to project timeline constraints. We therefore recognize this as a methodological limitation, and future researchers seeking to replicate or adapt our instruments are encouraged to incorporate a pilot phase to assess item clarity, comprehension, and administration feasibility. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA), confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), composite reliability (CR), and average variance extracted (AVE) were conducted on eight measurement instruments using a sample of 4575 participants to test their structural, convergent, and internal consistency validity. All questionnaires satisfied the prerequisites for factor analysis. Most core subdimensions attained acceptable reliability and convergent validity, while suboptimal CFA fit and weak AVE values for several subscales may be attributable to the large sample size, cultural adaptation, and limited item numbers (Tables S2–S4). Subsequent empirical analyses require cautious interpretation of dimensions with insufficient validity.

WTP‐CS

Using two questions, we assessed participants' WTP‐CS reference to the contingent valuation method (CVM) [50]. First, we asked participants a binary choice question: “Would you be willing to pay for cancer screening if you had to pay part of the cost out of pocket?” (Yes/No). Then, in an open‐ended follow‐up question, participants who answered “Yes” were asked to indicate the maximum amount they would be willing to pay. Based on the response patterns, we created a composite willingness variable with two mutually exclusive categories: (1) willing (respondents who answered “Yes” to the first question and indicated a greater than zero amount in the second question); and (2) unwilling (respondents who answered “No” to the first question or answered “Yes” to the first question but paradoxically indicated an amount willing to pay of zero in a subsequent question).

Data Collection

Data for this study were obtained from a baseline survey of an ongoing clinical trial (registry ID: ChiCTR2400088321). The questionnaire included a consent form that outlined the study details; participants who agreed to the consent continued to the questionnaire. After completing the questionnaire, each participant received a gift incentive of 10 Chinese Yuan (CNY) (approximately USD 1.41). Uniformly trained and qualified surveyors administered face‐to‐face surveys. Respondents with higher literacy levels were encouraged to complete the surveys independently. At the same time, surveyors were available to address inquiries and promptly verify the accuracy of the completed surveys. Upon completion of the survey by the investigator, a trained auditor reviewed the questionnaire and provided a signature of approval. 4600 questionnaires were distributed, and 4575 questionnaires were received from September 4 to 10, 2024. After reviewing the sample by the eligibility criteria, we obtained a sample of 4575 valid responses, and we defined a willing group of 4044 and an unwilling group of 531 according to the WTP‐CS (Figure 2).

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics summarized continuous variables as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and categorical variables as frequencies (percentages). Univariate analysis was performed to screen potential correlates of WTP‐CS. Independent t‐tests were used for group comparisons of continuous variables, while the chi‐square, Fisher's exact test, and Cochran‐Armitage test were adopted for categorical variables.

The hierarchical logistic regression was further employed to explore independent core correlates of WTP‐CS while controlling for confounding factors, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Model 1 incorporated demographic characteristics that exhibited statistically significant associations with the outcome in univariate analyses. Model 2 further added statistically significant psychosocial variables found in the univariate analysis. Model 3 entered perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, perceived benefits, perceived barriers, perceived confidence, health motivation, and cues to action; these were HBM constructs. Model 4 entered TPB components, which were attitude towards behaviors, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control.

Correlation analysis was used to assess pairwise associations across all study variables, which provided preliminary evidence of associations consistent with the hypothesized relationships and informed the subsequent path analysis based on the HBM‐TPB theoretical framework. Correlations were assessed using Pearson's correlation for continuous‐continuous variables, Point‐Biserial for continuous‐binary variables, Spearman's rank correlation for ordered categorical variables, and χ 2 tests with Cramer's V effect sizes for categorical variables.

Given the complex interrelationships between covariates, path analysis systematically examined the direct and indirect pathways between demographic variables, psychological variables, HBM constructs, TPB components, and the WTP‐CS. The model fit of the path analysis was assessed via widely recognized fit indices, including the Goodness‐of‐fit index (GFI), Adjusted goodness‐of‐fit index (AGFI), Comparative fit index (CFI), Root‐mean‐square error of approximation (RMSEA), Standardized root mean square residual (SRMR), and Tucker‐Lewis index (TLI). Acceptable model fit was considered as RMSEA < 0.08, and SRMR < 0.08, GFI, AGFI, CFI, and TLI ≥ 0.90, values of RMSEA < 0.05 indicated excellent fitting performance [51]. To verify the robustness of the hypothesized correlational pathways, stratified sensitivity analyses by gender were further conducted. Separate path models with identical structural specifications were estimated for male and female subgroups, and the abovementioned fit criteria were applied to judge the fitting performance of subgroup models. All data analyses were performed using R version 4.4.3.

Results

Participant Characteristics

Among 4575 community‐dwelling participants, merely 10.64% (487/4575) reported prior specialized cancer screening uptake, whereas 88.39% (4044/4575) demonstrated WTP‐CS, with a median acceptable payment of 300 Chinese Yuan (CNY; interquartile range [IQR], P25–P75: 100–1000 CNY). Per 2024 official data from the National Bureau of Statistics of China, national rural residents had an annual per capita disposable income of 23,119 CNY (median = 19,605 CNY) [52]. The univariate correlational analyses displayed significant statistical associations between demographic variables, psychological variables, HBM constructs, TPB components and WTP‐CS (Table 1). Demographic variables: The willing group participants were younger (70.45 ± 7.84 vs. 71.96 ± 7.79 years; p < 0.001) and more likely to have higher levels of education (p = 0.041). Self‐rated health was positively associated with WTP‐CS (p = 0.029), while gender, marital status, and household monthly income per capita were not significantly associated with WTP‐CS. Psychosocial variables: Greater knowledge (5.32 ± 2.83 vs. 3.95 ± 3.09; p < 0.001), social network (13.70 ± 7.95 vs. 12.05 ± 8.17; p < 0.001), and lower stigma (82.71 ± 15.73 vs. 85.10 ± 15.90; p = 0.012) predicted WTP‐CS. HBM constructs: WTP‐CS correlated with higher perceived benefits (24.24 ± 3.47 vs. 19.66 ± 5.57; p < 0.001), confidence (17.88 ± 3.93 vs. 12.68 ± 4.82; p < 0.001), health motivation (16.75 ± 2.12 vs. 15.28 ± 2.85; p < 0.001), and cues to action (0.81 ± 1.51 vs. 0.36 ± 0.93; p < 0.001), alongside reduced barriers (21.78 ± 4.57 vs. 20.84 ± 4.70; p < 0.001). TPB components: Positive attitude towards behaviors (28.20 ± 3.78 vs. 22.47 ± 6.06; p < 0.001), adherence to subjective norms (24.54 ± 3.73 vs. 18.97 ± 5.81; p < 0.001), and perceived behavioral control (24.96 ± 3.29 vs. 21.16 ± 3.40; p < 0.001) were significantly higher in the willing group. WTP‐CS was positively associated with younger age, psychosocial variables (knowledge, social network, and risk perception), favorable HBM constructs (perceived benefits, confidence), and TPB components (attitude towards behaviors, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control), while stigma and poor self‐rated health were negatively associated with WTP‐CS.

Result of Logistic Regression

Hierarchical logistic regression analysis was performed to examine the association between demographic variables, psychological variables, HBM constructs, TPB components and WTP‐CS based on the univariate analysis (Table 2). Four models were constructed, progressively incorporating variables to assess their incremental explanatory power. Model fit was evaluated using McFadden's R 2, Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Model 1 only included demographic variables, which explained 1.88% of the variance in WTP‐CS (AIC = 3255.20, BIC = 3358.05). Model 2 added psychosocial variables, yielding an explained variance of 4.91% (AIC = 3165.79, BIC = 3300.79). Model 3 further added the HBM constructs, increasing the explained variance to 24.99% (AIC = 2517.94, BIC = 2691.50). Model 4 finally integrated the TPB components, with the total explained variance rising to 34.51% (AIC = 2211.18, BIC = 2404.03). The final hierarchical regression model indicated that multiple factors were significantly correlated with WTP‐CS. Specifically, perceived confidence (OR = 1.110, 95% CI [1.076, 1.144], p < 0.001), benefits (OR = 1.078, 95% CI [1.043, 1.114], p < 0.001), barriers (OR = 0.963, 95% CI [0.933, 0.995], p < 0.05)., attitude towards behaviors (OR = 1.082, 95% CI [1.046, 1.120], p < 0.001), subjective norms (OR = 1.091, 95% CI [1.059, 1.124], p < 0.001), and perceived behavioral control (OR = 1.266, 95% CI [1.216, 1.318], p < 0.001) significantly were associated with WTP‐CS.

Result of Correlation Analysis

Significant correlations were observed among demographic variables, psychosocial variables, HBM constructs, and TPB components (Figure 3). Age was negatively correlated with education level (r = −0.30, p < 0.001), household monthly income per capita (r = −0.21, p < 0.001), and knowledge (r = −0.20, p < 0.001). Higher levels of education were associated with increased household monthly income per capita (r = 0.23, p < 0.001). Regular physical exercise positively correlated with self‐rated health (r = 0.21, p < 0.001). Notably, perceived benefits demonstrated strong correlations with health motivation (r = 0.53, p < 0.001) and attitude towards behaviors (r = 0.64, p < 0.001). WTP‐CS was positively associated with perceived benefits (r = 0.36, p < 0.001) and inversely correlated with stigma (r = −0.05, p < 0.01). Significant correlations also emerged between perceived behavioral control and WTP‐CS (r = 0.35, p < 0.001), as well as between perceived confidence and attitude towards behaviors (r = 0.57, p < 0.001). Perceived barriers were strongly associated with severity (r = −0.52, p < 0.001) and stigma (r = −0.32, p < 0.001), whereas social support was associated with decreased stigma (r = −0.14, p < 0.001) and increased knowledge (r = 0.10, p < 0.001). Result of Path Analysis

The correlation analysis showed a complex correlation between demographic variables, psychosocial variables, HBM constructs, and TPB components. To further examine correlational associations and plausible statistical pathways of the WTP‐CS, this study employed path analysis to establish an integrated prediction model for WTP‐CS including demographic variables, psychosocial variables, HBM constructs, and TPB components. A comprehensive set of pathways associated with WTP‐CS was estimated; however, considering the ease of interpretation, a few more representative and important pathways were selected for presentation in this study, as shown in Figure 4 and Table 3. The results of model fitting showed a corrected χ 2 = 1747.75 with df = 162 (p < 0.001). Given the high sensitivity of the chi‐square test to large sample sizes (N = 4575), additional goodness‐of‐fit indices were adopted to comprehensively evaluate the model performance. The resulting fit indices were acceptable, including GFI = 0.973, AGFI = 0.964, CFI = 0.942, RMSEA = 0.046, SRMR = 0.044, and TLI = 0.976. The coefficient of determination (R 2) for the final model was 0.489, indicating that the variables in the model could explained nearly half of the total variance in WTP‐CS.

Stratified sensitivity analyses by gender were conducted to examine the robustness of the hypothesized path model. Subgroup fit statistics were reported as follows. For males, the model produced χ 2 = 1171.235 (df = 162), with GFI = 0.975, CFI = 0.947, SRMR = 0.043, and RMSEA = 0.044. For females, χ 2 = 812.247 (df = 162), GFI = 0.963, CFI = 0.927, SRMR = 0.053, and RMSEA = 0.054. All fit metrics met the criteria for acceptable model fit. Overall, adequate model fit was attained in both gender subgroups, confirming the robustness and generalizability of the HBM‐TPB theoretical integration framework for characterizing correlates of WTP‐CS among Chinese community‐dwelling residents.

This study adopted cross‐sectional data, so all direct and indirect paths generated by the path analysis are statistically fitted correlational pathways. These associations cannot be taken as evidence of causal relationships between variables, and no causal inference can be drawn from the present results. In terms of the direct pathways within the fitted path model, the path analysis results showed six variables with statistically significant direct associations with the WTP‐CS. Subjective norms exhibited the strongest direct association (β = 0.313), followed by perceived behavioral control (β = 0.301) and attitude towards behaviors (β = 0.177), cues to action (β = 0.146), health insurance (β = 0.087), and knowledge (β = 0.069) (all p < 0.001) also demonstrated statistically significant but comparatively weaker direct associations. These results reflect the strong statistical correlation between social and cognitive variables and WTP‐CS.

For indirect pathways, multiple pathways mediated the relationships between demographic, psychosocial, HBM, TPB variables, and WTP‐CS. Notably, the attitude towards behaviors showed indirect associations through subjective norms (β = 0.154, p < 0.001) and perceived behavioral control (β = 0.123, p < 0.001). Perceived benefits demonstrated indirect associations with WTP‐CS via perceived confidence and subjective norms (β = 0.062, p < 0.001). Conversely, age showed a negative association with WTP‐CS through a sequential pathway involving knowledge, perceived benefits, and attitude towards behaviors (β = −0.007, p < 0.001). Stigma showed a negative association with WTP‐CS through perceived confidence and subjective norms (β = −0.008, p < 0.001), indicating complex mediating relationships.

In this path model, the total coefficients of each variable on WTP‐CS represent the combination of its direct and indirect pathway coefficients. Notably, given the cross‐sectional design, all total effects should be interpreted as statistical associations rather than causal predictive relationships. The total coefficient of attitude towards behaviors emerged as the strongest predictor (β = 0.454), followed by perceived benefits (β = 0.434), knowledge (β = 0.241), and perceived barriers (β = 0.195) (all p < 0.001). Social support (β = 0.103) also showed robust total coefficients, whereas age (β = −0.042) and stigma (β = −0.026) exhibited net negative associations (all p < 0.001). These findings highlight the interplay between direct and indirect pathways, underscoring the importance of considering both associations in designing WTP‐CS programs. Discussion

This study developed an HBM‐TPB integration theoretical framework integrating demographic variables, psychosocial variables, HBM constructs, and TPB components to explore the key correlates of WTP‐CS among Chinese community‐dwelling residents. Prior empirical evidence has demonstrated that integrated HBM‐TPB frameworks exhibit greater explanatory power for actual cancer screening uptake relative to single theoretical models [53]. The integrated structure addresses the limitations of the standalone HBM, which insufficiently accounts for social norms and contextual social influences while also compensating for gaps in the TPB regarding pre‐existing cognitive precursors including risk perception and intrinsic health motivation. Compared with the utility maximization framework conventionally adopted in WTP research, the HBM‐TPB integration theoretical framework explicitly captures multi‐layer correlational transmissions between psychological cognitions and social factors, offering a refined theoretical lens to unpack the formation of community‐dwelling residents' WTP‐CS.

These findings were broadly consistent with the ‘Determinants Of Screening upTake (DOST)’ model [32], a synthesized framework that integrated the HBM, TPB, and Theory of Care‐Seeking Behavior (TCSB) to delineate multilevel barriers to cancer screening. Although both the DOST model and our HBM‐TPB integrated framework draw upon behavioral theories to understand screening‐related decision‐making, the two frameworks differ in conceptual scope. DOST is a multidimensional screening uptake framework that integrates multilevel screening determinants using socio‐ecological approach, integrating individual psychosocial factors with health‐service delivery determinants (e.g., accessibility, availability, affordability, acceptability) as well as broader healthcare and policy environments, with actual screening uptake as its principal outcome, whereas the present HBM–TPB framework specifically examines socio‐psychological correlational pathways associated with willingness to pay for cancer screening. Refined constructs including cancer screening knowledge, cancer stigma, and social support were incorporated, and path analysis was utilized to quantify the direct and indirect intra‐individual correlational pathways associated with WTP‐CS. To conduct a more holistic micro–macro evaluation of WTP‐CS in subsequent studies, macro system‐level components of the DOST model could be integrated into the individual‐centered HBM‐TPB analytical framework established in the present research. Notably, beyond psychological determinants captured by the HBM‐TPB integration theoretical framework, WTP‐CS also reflects an individual's economic capacity, competing financial priorities, expectations regarding healthcare financing, and broader policy context. Accordingly, WTP‐CS should not be interpreted merely as a direct proxy for behavioral motivation or screening intention. As outlined in the Introduction, it represents a composite behavioral readiness integrating economic evaluation and perceived value, which is conceptually distinct from TPB‐defined behavioral intention and actual screening uptake. This interpretation aligns with established perspectives within economics [27, 28], and we explicitly recognized this conceptual distinction in the present study.

Hierarchical logistic regression was applied to systematically assess the incremental explanatory contribution of sequential variable blocks to WTP‐CS. Demographic variables accounted for only a small proportion of the total variance in WTP‐CS. Sequential inclusion of psychological variables, HBM constructs, and TPB components produced successive improvements in final model explanatory capacity, with the addition of health belief and planned behavioral constructs generating the most prominent gain in explained variance [54, 55]. This pattern is consistent with prior research on WTP‐CS [20, 56], which reports stronger correlations between individual psychological and cognitive states and WTP‐CS, whereas demographic variables accounted for a small share of the total variance. The observation also echoes a core tenet of the DOST model: modifiable psychological and belief‐related constructs, rather than fixed demographic characteristics, represent priority targets for intervention development [32]. These findings revealed that strategies stratified purely by demographic profiles exhibit weak correlational correspondence with WTP‐CS. Initiatives associated with cognitive and psychological constructs display stronger correlational alignment with residents' WTP‐CS [57].

Path analysis identified multiple correlational pathways, which may provide tentative empirical implications for community‐level strategies associated with WTP‐CS. Subjective norms exhibited the strongest direct correlation with WTP‐CS, the result is consistent with the collectivistic cultural values and interpersonal interaction characteristics commonly observed in Chinese communities [58]. In urban and rural Chinese grassroots settings, particularly rural villages, the perceived expectations and behavioral demonstration of key stakeholders (e.g., family members, community members, and healthcare workers) are closely correlated with the normative pressure surrounding individuals' willingness to pay for screening services [59]. Interpersonal trust established through long‐term localized community interactions tends to exhibit stronger associations with favorable health communication outcomes relative to general cancer screening educational materials. Consistent with the findings of the present study, a body of local evidence from China has observed higher screening participation rates in contexts with community health practitioner‐led mobilization activities, which may be correlated with the embedded credibility and persuasive attributes of frontline community health workers [60, 61]. Interventions aiming to boost WTP‐CS may utilize local acquaintance‐centered social networks. Feasible community‐level strategies include strengthening cancer screening payment education campaigns led by community health practitioners, nurturing community opinion leaders for cancer screening engagement, developing neighborhood role models for voluntary screening payment, and rolling out family‐based bundled screening payment schemes. These community‐focused initiatives may correlate with more positive social normative perceptions towards self‐funded screening, which could further show positive associations with higher WTP‐CS levels. Both perceived behavioral control and attitude towards behaviors exhibited significant positive direct correlations with WTP‐CS. This observation implies that favorable personal assessments of cancer screening services and individuals' self‐perceived ability to complete screening procedures constitute key correlates of community‐dwelling residents' WTP‐CS. From a population intervention perspective, targeted service optimization measures, such as simplifying screening workflows, eliminating logistical barriers to participation, and providing full, standardized, reader‐friendly information on the entire cancer screening process may correlate with elevated perceived behavioral control among residents. Meanwhile, tailored health education campaigns focusing on cancer prevention and control, which highlight the advantages of early cancer detection and diagnosis in alleviating long‐term physical and economic disease burdens, may correlate with the formation of positive attitudes towards cancer screening among community‐dwelling residents.

Perceived benefits displayed indirect correlations with WTP‐CS through a serial mediating pathway involving perceived confidence and subjective norms. This correlational chain may suggest that cognitive awareness of screening benefits is unlikely to exert independent correlational associations with WTP‐CS. These cognitive perceptions tend to correlate first with elevated screening confidence, which may in turn be linked to WTP‐CS through social normative. As such, reliance on knowledge popularization alone may be insufficient to address the practical demands of health education for cancer prevention and control. Supplementary hands‐on guidance and social support interventions could help facilitate potential correlational transitions from health‐related cognition to comparatively higher WTP‐CS. Age held negative correlational linkages with WTP‐CS through the serial mediation chain of “screening knowledge—perceived benefits—attitude towards behaviors”. This pathway may imply that the comparatively lower WTP‐CS observed among elderly subgroups could be largely correlated with insufficient reserves of cancer screening knowledge [62]. Targeted health promotion initiatives developed for older adults may consider adopting plain‐language educational materials tailored for populations with limited health literacy, alongside offline seminars and individualized counseling, which might help reduce potential cognitive gaps across distinct age cohorts.

Notably, several findings of the present study appear to diverge from mainstream conclusions in existing literature, which may warrant further contextual interpretation based on the local research setting. Although most respondents in the current sample reported positive WTP‐CS, the actual population‐level uptake of cancer screening in the surveyed region remained relatively low, suggesting a notable intention‐behavior gap. This discrepancy may indicate that WTP‐CS primarily reflects individuals' subjective behavioral inclination, rather than serving as a direct proxy for real‐life screening participation. Consistent with the core proposition of the DOST model, actual screening engagement may be jointly correlated with multi‐level factors spanning individual beneficiary characteristics and healthcare system attributes. Even among community‐dwelling residents with positive WTP‐CS, external contextual constraints, including insufficient service accessibility, time burden, and institutional limitations, might attenuate the correlational strength between WTP‐CS and subsequent screening behavior. Uninsured participants tended to demonstrate higher WTP‐CS than several insured subgroups, a pattern that may be correlated with their heightened perception of financial toxicity attributable to advanced cancer treatment. Without health insurance coverage [63], individuals may be more willing to bear modest upfront screening costs to mitigate the risk of catastrophic medical expenditures caused by late‐stage cancer diagnosis. Notably, China has made remarkable progress in promoting cancer early diagnosis and treatment, with continuous expansion of screening coverage and increased governmental funding investment. Nevertheless, population‐based cancer screening has not yet been incorporated into the national medical insurance reimbursement catalog as a routine non‐therapeutic healthcare service, and the out‐of‐pocket cost of screening remains a commonly neglected barrier for public participation [64]. In this context, expanding insurance reimbursement coverage for evidence‐based cancer screening programs may yield dual potential correlational benefits. Such policy optimization may directly reduce the financial barriers to screening uptake, while positive policy signals may indirectly strengthen community‐dwelling residents' perceived benefits of cancer screening. This dynamic may facilitate a mutually reinforcing correlational cycle characterized by reduced structural barriers, improved health beliefs, and elevated WTP‐CS. Generalization of the present findings to other contexts should be made with caution, as the analytical conclusions may not be directly applicable without comprehensive consideration of local demographic and social conditions, healthcare service delivery models, regional economic development levels, and prevailing health insurance policy frameworks.

Correlation analysis complex interconnections among demographic variables, psychosocial variables, HBM constructs, TPB components and the WTP‐CS, with distinct bivariate patterns observed for age, education, income, screening knowledge, perceived benefits, cancer stigma, and social support. Key HBM constructs, particularly perceived benefits, were strongly correlated with attitude towards behavior, perceived confidence, health motivation and further positively associated with WTP‐CS, whereas cancer stigma showed weak negative correlations with WTP‐CS despite its strong linkage with perceived severity. Social support was correlated with reduced cancer stigma and enhanced screening knowledge, presenting an indirect associational linkage with higher WTP‐CS. The complex correlational patterns observed across all study variables suggest that multidimensional designs should be adopted in future research on WTP‐CS and in the development of targeted population intervention strategies.

Although the HBM‐TPB integration theoretical framework achieved acceptable psychometric performance, several latent constructs, especially the TPB F3 subscale (perceived behavioral control) and certain HBM dimensions (e.g., cues to action), showed relatively weak convergent validity and suboptimal model fit. Consequently, associations involving these constructs should be interpreted with caution, as measurement error may have attenuated or inflated the observed relationships. While the overall correlational patterns remain informative, conclusions regarding these specific pathways should be considered exploratory and warrant confirmation in future studies using refined measurement instruments. We therefore advise readers to treat findings related to these constructs as preliminary, pending further psychometric validation.

Limitation

Although the present study yields several meaningful correlational findings, two major limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study sample was recruited exclusively from community residents within a provincial cancer early screening demonstration zone in China, which carries unique regional cultural, economic, and healthcare contextual characteristics. Such geographic and demographic specificity may restrict the external generalizability of the detected correlational pathways to populations residing in regions with divergent healthcare systems, socioeconomic conditions, or cultural norms. Cross‐regional and cross‐national replicated analyses are required to examine whether the observed behavioral correlational patterns remain stable, especially in low‐ and middle‐income countries confronted with similar barriers to population‐based cancer screening. In addition, the sample consisted predominantly of adults with a mean age above 70 years; the health cognition and WTP‐CS patterns identified herein may only reflect features of this elderly subgroup. Considerable caution is therefore needed when extrapolating these results to other age cohorts or broader community populations. Second, the cross‐sectional design adopted in this study prohibits robust temporal reasoning and definitive causal judgment for the HBM‐TPB integration theoretical framework. All hypothesized correlational chains among latent constructs were established merely on theoretical deductions from prior scholarship, which represents an inherent methodological constraint of structural equation modeling based on one‐off cross‐sectional data. Longitudinal tracking research or controlled intervention trials are therefore warranted to re‐examine the correlational pathways identified in the current analysis. Such follow‐up studies may further disentangle how constructs within the HBM‐TPB integration theoretical framework correlate with long‐term shifts in community‐dwelling residents' WTP‐CS.

Conclusion

The present study observed a potential intention‐behavior gap in the surveyed community‐dwelling residents, in which favorable WTP‐CS did not necessarily correspond to higher actual screening participation rates. Such a mismatch may imply the existence of unaddressed implementation barriers in real‐world community screening delivery systems. An HBM‐TPB integration theoretical framework (“Demographic—Psychological—HBM—TPB—WTP‐CS”) was constructed and the adopted hierarchical modeling strategy effectively improved the explanatory capacity of the analytical model. Path analysis further delineated reasonable multi‐layered direct and indirect correlational pathways underlying WTP‐CS, with subjective norms and health insurance exhibiting direct associations, whereas screening knowledge and social support primarily correlated with WTP‐CS via indirect pathways. These findings may expand the empirical foundation of WTP‐CS research and deliver tentative, context‐specific insights for subsequent investigations, especially within comparable developing‐country contexts. This study also yields translational implications for public health practice: (1) it might provide a theory‐based perspective to interpret the WTP‐CS, which may inform future targeted behavioral interventions to promote the transformation of subjective WTP‐CS into actual screening payment; (2) the empirical correlational model developed in this study could serve as a preliminary analytical reference for optimizing population‐based WTP‐CS strategies in resource‐limited primary healthcare contexts.
