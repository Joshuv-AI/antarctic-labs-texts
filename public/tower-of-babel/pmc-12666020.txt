‘Climate Change and Health Indicators’ and ‘Surgical System Strengthening’: an opportunity for synergy
Authors: Callum Forbes, Radzi Hamzah, Elizabeth McLeod, John G Meara, Ayla Gerk, Joseph Aryankalayil, Rashi Jhunjhunwala, Kee B Park, Craig D McClain, Maria Jose Garcia Fuentes, Alfredo Borrero Vega, Tarsicio Uribe-Leitz, Joshua S Ng Kamstra
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractClimate change is a public health emergency. Yet, incongruously, the healthcare sector is a significant source of global greenhouse gas emissions. Surgical systems are uniquely situated to address this public health crisis due to the high carbon footprint associated with surgical care delivery and the ability of strong surgical systems to foster broader climate resilience. There is an urgent need for climate change and health indicators (CCHIs) specific to surgical care to address the environmental impacts of surgery and reduce the impacts of climate change on the health of individuals, populations and surgical care delivery. In proposing a set of example CCHIs pertinent to surgical care, we call the surgical community to action to improve and refine existing indicators while simultaneously engaging in national and regional surgical system strengthening efforts. Moreover, aligning such efforts can help bridge existing climate and health funding gaps which, to date, have proved a critical barrier to ensuring effective healthcare mitigation and adaptation.Keywords: Environmental health, Health policy, Health systems, Health systems evaluation, Surgery

The climate crisis is the greatest public health emergency of the 21st century, threatening the health of populations and the integrity of the healthcare systems on which they depend.1 The healthcare sector, which contributes almost 5% of global greenhouse gas emissions and in the USA contributes 8.5% of national emissions, is under-recognised in driving the climate crisis.2 3 The sector faces two urgent tasks: acknowledging and reducing its contributions to climate change (termed mitigation) and preparing itself for the unfolding climate and extreme weather emergencies that increasingly threaten the continuity of care delivery (adaptation, box 1). Indeed, a Lancet Commission on Sustainable Healthcare is in progress with the express aim of ‘facilitating a rapid transition to low-carbon, sustainable, resilient health systems’.4 There is a critical need for evidence-based healthcare sustainability and resilience indicators to ensure progress on these tasks. Surgical systems sit at the nexus of climate change and health, being particularly vulnerable to disruption, while at the same time being energy-intensive and waste-generating.5 Strong and sustainable surgical systems are thus key to delivering effective national and regional climate mitigation and adaptation. The global surgery movement for universal access to safe, affordable surgical and anaesthesia care must be aligned with the principles of environmental sustainability and healthcare resilience and is uniquely positioned to lead the way in building a set of core climate change and health indicators (CCHIs) for surgery.

Mitigation and adaptation

Mitigation refers to strategies that aim to reduce the drivers of climate change. Specifically, this involves reducing greenhouse gas emissions and environmental destruction and enhancing carbon sinks.

Adaptation refers to strategies that aim to reduce or modify the impacts or damages from the climate crisis.

The impacts of the climate crisis are already being experienced. As a global healthcare community, we can limit the extent of these impacts now and for future generations. Both approaches are required, in parallel, to effectively address the climate crisis.

The climate crisis is a health crisis

The multiple ways in which climate change impacts human health are increasingly recognised. Reflecting the urgent need for action, the Lancet Countdown team produces an annual update on the issue,2 and at the 77th World Health Assembly, a landmark Climate Change and Health resolution was adopted by 194 WHO member states.6 Direct impacts on health result from the changing prevalence of cardiovascular, respiratory and vector-borne diseases, and the increasing frequency and severity of humanitarian emergencies caused by sudden-onset events such as heat waves and wildfires, floods and tropical storms. In addition to the immediate crisis, heatwaves and extreme weather events can have cascading effects on infrastructure, supply chains, the health workforce, and the economic and policy environments in which health systems are situated. Climate change also indirectly affects health via socioeconomic determinants, such as food and water scarcity and displacement of populations. These impacts are not distributed equally between and within countries and regions; rather, they exacerbate existing global, regional and national health inequalities.7 Within countries, women and marginalised communities tend to experience the health effects of climate change most acutely. At present, the burden of these impacts falls heaviest on the Global South, but high-income country systems face an increasing risk of catastrophic events as climate tipping points are crossed.

Despite the growing urgency of climate-related health impacts, funding remains alarmingly low. As part of the 2015 Paris Agreement, wealthy nations pledged to mobilise $100 billion annually for climate action by 2020; this target was only met in 2022.8 From 2016 to 2022, low-income countries (LICs) and small island developing states (SIDS)—among the most vulnerable—received just 17% and 2% of global climate finance, respectively.8 Of the adaptation spending from 2009 to 2019, a mere 0.2% was directed towards healthcare-focused projects,9 and in 2022, less than 18% of the UN Green Climate Fund’s adaptation spending went to initiatives with potential health benefits.2 Meanwhile, under 2% of international donor health aid supports environment-related efforts.10

Although finance commitments for climate and health specifically rose to $7.1 billion in 2022 (up from under $1 billion in 2018),11 this still pales in comparison to the $4.5 trillion spent on healthcare in the USA alone that same year.12 The 2023 Lancet Countdown report highlighted the funding gap and urgently called for increased investment to confront the intersecting crises of climate and health.2 A potential source of future funding is the Fund for Responding to Loss and Damage (FRLD), agreed on at COP27 in 2022 to support countries most affected by climate change, although it has yet to be operationalised.

Climate change and access to surgical care: parallel public health crises

In 2015, the Lancet Commission on Global Surgery (LCoGS) described the profound global shortfall in access to surgical care; over 70% of the world’s population lacks access to safe, timely and affordable quality care, with unmet needs disproportionately impacting low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). The landmark report calculated that to provide care for the 5.3 billion people lacking access to surgery worldwide, an additional 153 million procedures were required per year.13 As well as highlighting the sheer scale of need, the commission debunked the myth that surgical care is not cost-effective,14 serving as a call to action for the global surgery movement. Since 2015, concerted advocacy has called for a dramatic scale-up of surgical care capacity.

The world’s population has now passed 8 billion, and climate change is increasing the unmet burden of surgical disease.5 15 16 According to one modelled estimate, an additional 7 million surgeries would have been needed between 2004 and 2014 to address climate-related natural disasters beyond baseline population surgical needs15; this number is projected to increase as the frequency and intensity of extreme weather events increase. Yet, given that operating theatres are up to six times more energy-intensive than other clinical care areas17 and, along with delivery suites, generate between 50% and 70% of all hospital waste,18 19 such an upscaling of care would have major implications for the environmental footprint of the healthcare sector. While essential for attempts to deliver Universal Health Coverage (UHC), any investment in funding for surgical system strengthening must be undertaken with environmental sustainability and resilience as a principal consideration.

Using a climate justice lens, it is the opinion of the authors that high-income country surgical systems have a responsibility to lower their carbon footprint while maintaining high-quality care, while low-income country surgical systems have an opportunity to build a culture of sustainability into ongoing efforts to scale up surgical access.

If approached correctly, investment in strong and sustainable surgical systems can help build broader healthcare system resilience to external stressors such as climate change.20 21 Such benefits were evident during the COVID-19 pandemic when surgical care’s ‘staff, stuff, space, and systems’ were often used to help keep health systems afloat. While many millions died, the situation would have been much worse were it not for the capacity mobilised through the cancellation of elective surgical procedures and the consequent reallocation of surgical care infrastructure and workforce. Evidence also supports the value of strong local surgical systems in helping to reduce the burden of disease and the economic impact of natural disasters;22 the ability to mobilise surgical capacity early is critical in managing the impacts of sudden-onset climate pressures. The authors are compelled to note, however, that health system resilience has its limits and that a strong surgical system is no replacement for immediate coordinated international action to reduce carbon emissions. If emissions do not dramatically decrease, all health systems will experience catastrophic failures with increasing frequency due to disasters, extreme demand, supply chain disruptions and economic shocks.

National Surgical Plans as an opportunity for climate mitigation and adaptation

The LCoGS introduced a framework for National Surgical, Obstetric and Anaesthesia Plans (NSOAPs) to address surgical access gaps. To date, 11 countries across sub-Saharan Africa, Southeast Asia, South America and the Pacific have developed such plans, with others underway.

NSOAPs offer an underused chance to integrate climate resilience into health planning. Early NSOAPs and the LCoGS framework focused on access and quality, overlooking climate-related impacts. However, as the climate crisis intensifies, embedding climate considerations into NSOAPs is increasingly vital, offering co-benefits across system strengthening, disaster preparedness and climate adaptation.20 23

Ecuador’s National Surgery Strengthening Plan (NSSP, the country’s NSOAP), launched in November 2023, is the first to address climate change explicitly.24 25 It includes climate risk and health system preparedness as a core strategic domain, with climate considerations embedded in its monitoring and evaluation (table 1). The plan reflects local climate realities, like the El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO). However, given the limited availability of evidence-based, actionable indicators of health system resilience in the literature, the validity of the indicators included within the plan remains unproven.

The need for health sector resilience indicators

Climate change and health indicators (CCHIs) are summary measures designed to link and describe the relationship between climate change and health in a standardised manner. Such indicators can help communicate existing evidence to key stakeholders, support decision-makers in implementing effective strategies to address the health consequences of climate change and assist with long-term monitoring of progress made towards the achievement of broader national and regional mitigation and adaptation. CCHIs do exist, with the Lancet Countdown group notable in driving progress on this front. Since publishing their first report in 2015 through to their most recent 2023 report,2 the team has developed indicators to track the global impacts of climate change on health across five domains (box 2).

Adapted from the World Meteorological Organisation’s guidelines for climate indicators, the Lancet Countdown team recently outlined five quality criteria for ‘climate change impacts, exposures, and vulnerability indicators’ (ie, domain 1) that might reasonably be expanded to all CCHIs.26 Namely, they proposed that indicators should be representative, relevant, robust, reproducible and timely.

Evidence-based, actionable indicators specific to health system resilience have not been well-defined and agreed on. Effective progress monitoring based on such metrics is crucial for ensuring accountability and informing reliable funding mechanisms for climate and health adaptation. Of the Lancet Countdown indicators, those of domain 2—‘adaptation, planning, and resilience for health’—are the most pertinent to health systems particularly in LMICs. However, they do not provide a concrete roadmap for nations most at risk from the impacts of climate change to help guide and measure progress towards building healthcare system resilience. Rather, the indicators focus on measures such as national compliance with adaptation assessment and planning, and quantitative tracking of multilateral global funding.

The WHO has produced several publications on climate-resilient health systems.27,29 In 2022, they produced a report on ‘Measuring Climate Resilience of Health Systems’,30 which provides a framework and methods for measuring health sector resilience, as well as criteria for selecting indicators. In 2024, the WHO and other UN organisations also released an updated compendium on health and the environment,31 intended to serve as an easy-to-use repository for stakeholders and policymakers globally. However, the suggested indicators provided in both reports predominantly focus on high-level policy and are either qualitative or related to outcomes. A set of evidence-based, trackable, quantitative and operational indicators would more effectively inform frameworks for health system resilience.

Aligning efforts

The 2023 Lancet Countdown report argued that adaptation efforts continue to be insufficient, with scarce funding and insufficient evidence of how best to build and track resilience often leading to well-intentioned but harmful ‘maladaptation’; for example, use of electricity for air conditioning to address heat stress has resulted in a 73% increase in emissions from 2000 to 2021 and 23 000 deaths from PM2.5 in 2022. The report highlighted that as the impacts of climate change increase, the challenges to achieving adaptation will continue to mount—this is not an issue we can afford to postpone for another day.

How can the LMICs and SIDS most affected by the impacts of climate change ensure health system resilience? Where should they invest their limited resources? As a global health community, we need to develop a set of indicators and evidence-based standards that can be adapted and used across various contexts to guide the building of resilient health systems. The Alliance for Transformative Action on Climate and Health (ATACH)32 was established by the WHO in 2022 and includes over 80 member states and various stakeholders. This initiative aims to fulfil COP26’s ambitions to enhance climate resilience and sustainability in health systems. One of ATACH’s working groups is focused on indicators for measuring progress in low-carbon sustainable health systems (LCSHS). Although this could lead to the development of clear metrics for measuring and delivering climate resilience, the alliance has yet to define the language and objectives required to develop these. Without them, tracking progress and ensuring accountability among member states and stakeholders remains challenging.

In tables2 3, we present a set of proposed mitigation and adaptation indicators pertinent to surgical care and linked to the six NSOAP policy domains, namely infrastructure, workforce, service delivery, financing, information management and governance. These indicators are intended to serve as a starting point from which ongoing efforts to deliver trackable progress on health sector sustainability and resilience can commence. We also present this as a call to action for ongoing efforts to refine and update such CCHIs.

To this end, we advocate using a consensus-building approach, such as a Delphi or consensus conference methodology. Expertise must be sought from regions most impacted by climate change; stakeholders from sub-Saharan Africa, Southeast Asia, Central and South America, and the Pacific, and a wide range of stakeholders including frontline healthcare workers and members of rural and Indigenous communities, must be engaged in such a process. This approach is advocated for in the most recent Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) report, which acknowledged that mitigation and adaptation initiatives that incorporate social equity considerations and meaningful participation of Indigenous peoples, local communities and vulnerable populations can maximise co-benefits and minimise trade-offs.33

Investments in surgical system strengthening can result in compound gains in building a health sector resilient to external shocks and stressors. Similarly, significant representation from the surgical community—including frontline specialist surgical, anaesthesia, obstetric and nursing care providers—is vital for the success of urgently required climate and health mitigation and adaptation policy and planning. First and foremost, the global surgery movement can help enhance the effectiveness of mitigation and adaptation efforts by spearheading the creation of quantifiable and trackable health system sustainability and resilience indicators, ensuring that surgical systems are adequately considered and incorporated into sustainability and resilience metrics and quality standards.

Almost a decade on from LCoGS, it is crucial that surgical system strengthening be situated at the centre of ongoing health sector climate mitigation and adaptation efforts. Surgical system strengthening should be built into disaster risk reduction and climate change policy, and likewise disaster risk reduction and climate change should be considered within national surgical planning. This can ensure both the financial resources and the political backing for the simultaneous and synergised improvement in access to high-quality surgical care and national and regional climate adaptation efforts. As countries fulfil their commitments to the FRLD, an Overton window opens for climate change and health to access new funding streams. The global surgery movement can and must serve as a positive example to the wider health community in effectively supporting climate mitigation and adaptation for the most underserved and marginalised communities across the globe.
