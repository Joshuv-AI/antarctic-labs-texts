Catoni Contextual Bandits are Robust to Heavy-tailed Rewards
Chenlu Ye, Yujia Jin, Alekh Agarwal, Tong Zhang

Abstract
Typical contextual bandit algorithms assume that the rewards at each round lie in some fixed range [ 0 , R ] [0,R] , and their regret scales polynomially with this reward range R R . However, many practical scenarios naturally involve heavy-tailed rewards or rewards where the worst-case range can be substantially larger than the variance. In this paper, we develop an algorithmic approach building on Catoni’s estimator from robust statistics, and apply it to contextual bandits with general function approximation. When the variance of the reward at each round is known, we use a variance-weighted regression approach and establish a regret bound that depends only on the cumulative reward variance and logarithmically on the reward range R R as well as the number of rounds T T . For the unknown-variance case, we further propose a careful peeling-based algorithm and remove the need for cumbersome variance estimation. With additional dependence on the fourth moment, our algorithm also enjoys a variance-based bound with logarithmic reward-range dependence. Moreover, we demonstrate the optimality of the leading-order term in our regret bound through a matching lower bound.

Introduction

Minimax optimal regret bounds in the worst-case over problem instances for contextual bandit learning are relatively well-understood in the literature, both using policy-based approaches in the agnostic case, and regression-based approaches in the realizable case. A variety of algorithms attain these bounds in both settings, and the minimax optimality implies that the bounds are unimprovable in general. When the expected reward of each action is realizable using some function class
\mathcal{F}
available to the learner, this optimal regret scales as
O(R\sqrt{Td_{\mathcal{F}}\ln N_{\mathcal{F}}})
, where
R
is the range of the rewards,
T
is the number of rounds,
d_{\mathcal{F}}
is a complexity notion for
\mathcal{F}

(
Russo and Van Roy,, 2013
)
, and
N_{\mathcal{F}}
is the covering number of
\mathcal{F}
.

However, this worst-case behavior arises only when the rewards span their entire range
[0,R]
with a significant probability, a phenomenon not typical in practice. Even for a common case of binary rewards in
\{0,R\}
for instance, the expected reward is often relatively close to
0
in common click/no-click style recommendation settings with low clickthrough rates. Consequently, the expectation, variance and even higher moments of the reward are much smaller than the worst-case range. More generally, rewards with heavier tails naturally arise when considering waiting times in wireless communication networks
(
Nair et al.,, 2013
)
, stock prices in financial markets
(

;
Hull,, 2012
)
, or value returns for online advertising
(

;
Jebarajakirthy et al.,, 2021
)
. In this paper, we study the design of contextual bandit algorithms that can leverage such structures to have regret guarantees dependent polynomially on the reward variance, with only a mild logarithmic scaling with the range parameter
R

Such variance-based regret bounds have received significant attention recently, under the topic of

The general question of designing a robust contextual bandit algorithm under the heavy-tailed reward (or a reward with a large range) for general function approximation is still lacking in the literature.

1.1
Our contributions

This work considers a different route for robustness to heavy-tailed rewards, building on the well-studied Catoni’s mean estimator from the robust statistics literature. We design a contextual bandit (CB) algorithm that uses the Catoni mean as a robust device for constructing a regression error estimator for the
excess loss
, given some function class
\mathcal{F}
for predicting the expected reward. Using the variance-dependent concentration of the Catoni estimator, we conduct a careful analysis of our algorithm and show that its regret scales as
\widetilde{O}\big(\sqrt{\sum_{t\in[T]}\sigma_{t}^{2}\cdot d_{\mathcal{F}}\ln N_{\mathcal{F}}}+d_{\mathcal{F}}\ln N_{\mathcal{F}}\big)
, when the reward variance
\sigma_{t}
is known at each round
t
.

Since reward variance information is seldom available in practice, we refine our approach for cases with unknown variances by employing a multi-level uncertainty estimation for the expected rewards of a carefully chosen subset of actions. For this approach, we obtain regret guarantees dependent on the fourth moment of the reward, while still maintaining a logarithmic scaling in
R
. Formally, the regret scales as
\widetilde{O}\bigl(d_{\mathcal{F}}\sqrt{\sum_{t\in[T]}\sigma_{t}^{2}\cdot\ln N_{\mathcal{F}}}+d_{\mathcal{F}}(\ln N_{\mathcal{F}})^{3/4}\bigr)
. Notably, our method does not rely on some other function class to help predict the per-round variance as a function. Instead, we estimate a robust averaged variance quantity, and show that it approximates the averaged true variance up to logarithmic factors in
R
.

Overall, our results significantly improve the state-of-the-art in variance-aware regret guarantees, that are amenable to practical reward structures. We summarize our results relative to the most relevant prior literature in Table
1
.

3
Bandits with Known Variance

In this section, we present upper and lower bounds, when the per-round variance of each action is known to the learner.

3.1
Lower Bound

We start with a minimax lower bound for the class of multi-armed bandit problems where the variance of each action’s reward is known to the learner.

Theorem 1
.

For any integer
T>0
, there exists a contextual bandit problem such that any
\pi=\{\pi_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
will incur regret at least
\Omega(\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sigma_{t}^{2}})
, where
\{\sigma_{t}=\mathrm{Var}_{x_{t}\sim\pi_{t}}[y_{t}]\}_{t=1}^{T}
and the expectation is jointly over any randomness in the environment as well as the algorithm.

In other words, the theorem states that the regret of any contextual bandit algorithm scales with the square root of the sum of the variances of the rewards for its chosen actions. That is, it rules out a regret bound which scales solely as the variance of the reward of the optimal policy’s actions.

The detailed proof is deferred to Appendix
B.1
. The intuition is to construct two bandit instances, each with two arms
\{x_{1},x_{2}\}
. In the first instance, the arm
x_{1}
has a deterministic reward while
x_{2}
has a higher expected reward, but with a large variance. In the second instance,
x_{2}
has a smaller mean reward. Clearly, the optimal action
x_{1}
has a variance of zero in the first instance, but any algorithm needs sufficiently many draws of
x_{2}
as well to distinguish between the two instances. In the sequel, we will present a matching upper bound for our robust estimator.

3.2
Upper Bound for Known Variance

Catoni Estimator

We first introduce
\mathsf{Catoni}

Audibert and Catoni, (2011)
(see also
(
Lugosi and Mendelson,, 2019

Lugosi and Mendelson, (2019, section 2.2)

\displaystyle\Psi(x)=\begin{cases}\log(1+x+x^{2}/2)\quad\text{if}~x\geq 0,\\
-\log(1-x+x^{2}/2)\quad\text{if}~x<0.\end{cases}

Then for some parameter
\theta>0

\mathsf{Catoni}_{\theta}(\{Z_{i}\}_{i\in[t]})
is the unique zero of the antisymmetric increasing function

\displaystyle f(x;\{Z_{i}\}_{i\in[t]},\theta):=\sum_{i\in[t]}\Psi(\theta(Z_{i}-x)).

(2)

We first provide the following result about the concentration properties of the

(Informal)

To illustrate the intuition clearly, we ignore the covering number in this subsection, and assume that the function space

Part I: Concentration of excess loss

Recall that for any
f,f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}

L_{t}(f,f^{\prime})
to estimate the excess loss
R_{t}(f,f^{\prime})
in (
4
) under heavy-tailed noise. Here the conditional expectation of the variable
Z_{i}(f,f^{\prime})
is
\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}|x_{i}]=(f(x_{i})-f^{\prime}(x_{i}))(f^{\prime}(x_{i})-f^{\star}(x_{i}))/\bar{\sigma}_{i}^{2}
. Since the standard Hoeffding’s inequality leads to the error dependent on the uniform noise bound
R
, which can be extremely large in our setting, we can utilize the robustness of the Catoni estimator via Lemma
1
to obtain the following lemma.

Lemma 2
.

For all large enough time steps
t
and two fixed
f,f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
, with a proper choice of parameters
\alpha
,
\hat{\beta}_{t}
and
\epsilon
, we have with probability at least
1-\delta/N^{2}
,

\displaystyle\Big|L_{t}(f,f^{\prime})-R_{t}(f,f^{\prime})\Big|

\displaystyle=2\Big|t\mathsf{Catoni}_{\theta_{t}(f,f^{\prime})}(\{Z_{i}(f,f^{\prime})\}_{i\in[t]})-\sum_{i\in[t]}\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}|x_{i}]\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}V_{t}(f,f^{\prime})+\frac{1}{3}\hat{\beta}_{t}^{2},

where
V_{t}(f,f^{\prime})=\sum_{i\in[t]}(f(x_{i})-f^{\prime}(x_{i})))^{2}/\bar{\sigma}_{i}^{2}
.

The values of the parameters are shown in Lemma
7
, and the proof is deferred to Appendix
B.2
. Importantly,
\hat{\beta}_{t}^{2}
in the lemma above only has
\log R
dependence.

Part II: Sharpness of the confidence set

Next, we show that for our choice of
\hat{\beta}_{t}
, the true function
f^{\star}\in\mathcal{F}_{t}
with a high probability for
t\in[T]
that are appropriately large. To prove this, we define
L_{t}(f):=\max_{f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}}L_{t}(f,f^{\prime})
, apply Lemma
2
with
f=\hat{f}_{t}
, and take a minimum over
f\in\mathcal{F}
on both sides of the inequality to get the following result.

Lemma 3
.

Under the conditions of Lemma
2
, we have for all large enough
t\in[T]
with probability at least
1-\delta

\displaystyle L_{t}(\hat{f}_{t})\geq

\displaystyle\min_{f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}}\Big\{V_{t}(\hat{f}_{t},f^{\star})-V_{t}(f^{\prime},f^{\star})-\frac{1}{2}V_{t}(\hat{f}_{t},f^{\prime})-\frac{1}{3}\hat{\beta}_{t}^{2}\Big\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{3}V_{t}(\hat{f}_{t},f^{\star})-\frac{1}{3}\hat{\beta}_{t}^{2},

where the minimizer for

The proof is deferred to Appendix

. An analogous argument also yields an upper bound

, as shown in Appendix

. Furthermore, since
\hat{f}_{t}

\displaystyle 0\geq L_{t}(\hat{f}_{t})-L_{t}(f^{\star})\geq\frac{2}{3}V_{t}(\hat{f}_{t},f^{\star})-\frac{2}{3}\hat{\beta}_{t}^{2},

which leads to

Ultimately, if the event

Theorem 3

This theorem provides a variance-dependent upper bound when variances are unknown, which matches the upper bound when variances are observed (Theorem

When compared to the upper bound
\widetilde{O}(d\sqrt{\sum_{t\in[T]}\sigma_{t}^{2}}+d^{3.5}T^{1/4})

(

for the linear setting with dimension

\sum_{t\in[T]}\sigma_{t}^{2}=o(\sqrt{T})
. We give more intuition on why our algorithm admits this sharper bound in the proof sketch below, with details deferred to Appendix

Proof Sketch

The main challenges for the variance-agnostic algorithm are: (I) how to obtain the concentration inequalities when the weights are not based on the noise variance; (II) how to make accurate substitutions for the sum of variance
\sum_{i\in[t]}\sigma_{i}^{2}/w_{i}^{2}
in the parameters; and (III) how to deal with the regret of each level
l
. The insight of solving challenges (I) and (III) basically follows previous work
(
Zhao et al., 2023b,
;
Pacchiano,, 2024
)
, but for (II), because of the heavy-tailed setting, our contribution is designing the robust Catoni variance estimator, and demonstrating the estimator almost has the same order as the true average-variance
\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}^{2}/w_{i}^{2}
up to constants with logarithmic dependence on the reward range
R
. We address these challenges in the following three parts, respectively.

Part I: Average variance bound for concentration

In this part, we study the concentration of the excess loss for each level. For clearer illustration, we omit level
l
when there is no confusion and denote
D_{i}=D_{i}^{l}(x_{i})
for short. Distinct from the known variance case where one directly takes the variance
\sigma_{t}
as weights to derive an upper bound for the variance of
Z_{i}(f,f^{\prime})
, we start with an alternate bound in terms of the weights
w_{i}
:

\displaystyle S_{t}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{i\in\Psi_{t}}\mathrm{Var}[Z_{i}(f,f^{\prime})]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\Psi_{t}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{1}{w_{i}^{2}}(f(x_{i})-f^{\prime}(x_{i}))^{2}(f^{\star}(x_{i})-y_{i})^{2}\,\Big|\,x_{i}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in\Psi_{t}}\frac{(f(x_{i})-f^{\prime}(x_{i}))^{2}}{w_{i}^{2}}\cdot\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}}{w_{i}^{2}}.

When the variance is known, and
w_{i}=\sigma_{i}
as in Algorithm
1
, the second term in the final inequality is uniformly equal to
1
.

When the variance is unknown, we can no longer weight the variances, instead, we uniformly bound the first term and aggregate the second term as shown below:

\displaystyle S_{t}\leq

\displaystyle\max_{i\in\Psi_{t}}\frac{(f(x_{i})-f^{\prime}(x_{i}))^{2}}{w_{i}^{2}}\cdot\sum_{i\in\Psi_{t}}\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}}{w_{i}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\underbrace{\max_{i\in\Psi_{t}}\frac{D_{i}^{2}}{w_{i}^{2}}\cdot\Big(\sum_{\tau\in[i-1]}\frac{(f(x_{\tau})-f^{\prime}(x_{\tau}))^{2}}{w_{\tau}^{2}}+\lambda\Big)}_{\displaystyle\text{Uniform~bound}~\leq 2^{-2l}\cdot 4\hat{\beta}_{t-1}^{2}}\cdot\sum_{i\in\Psi_{t}}\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}}{w_{i}^{2}},

where the first inequality uses the definition of
D_{i}
, and the uniform bound holds because
D_{i}/w_{i}\leq 2^{-l}
at level
l
from Algorithm
2
. Also
f,f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
implies that

\displaystyle\sum_{\tau\in[i-1]}\frac{(f(x_{\tau})-f^{\prime}(x_{\tau}))^{2}}{w_{\tau}^{2}}\leq

\displaystyle 2\sum_{\tau\in[t-1]}\frac{(f(x_{\tau})-\hat{f}_{t-1}(x_{\tau}))^{2}}{w_{\tau}^{2}}+2\sum_{\tau\in[t-1]}\frac{(f^{\prime}(x_{\tau})-\hat{f}_{t-1}(x_{\tau}))^{2}}{w_{\tau}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4\hat{\beta}_{t-1}^{2}.

Here the first inequality applies the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, and the second inequality follows from the definition of
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
.

Therefore, by not requiring a uniform bound on the closeness of the variances and the weights, we can successfully derive the concentration inequality for
L_{t}(f,f^{\prime})-R_{t}(f,f^{\prime})
.

Part II: Accuracy of variance estimation.

For any
t\in[t]
, let
V_{i}(\hat{f}_{t-1})=(y_{i}-\hat{f}_{t-1}(x_{i}))^{2}/w_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[V_{i}(\hat{f}_{t-1})|x_{i}]=(\sigma_{i}^{2}+(f^{\star}(x_{i})-\hat{f}_{t-1}(x_{i}))^{2})/w_{i}^{2}.

Then, by using Lemma

\displaystyle\Big|t\mathsf{Catoni}_{\theta_{\mathrm{Var}}^{t}}\big(\big\{V_{i}(\hat{f}_{t-1})\big\}_{i\in\Psi_{t}}\big)-\sum_{i\in\Psi_{t}}\mathbb{E}[V_{i}(\hat{f}_{t-1})|x_{i}]\Big|.

\displaystyle\Big|\sum_{i\in\Psi_{t}}\mathbb{E}[V_{i}(\hat{f}_{t-1})|x_{i}]-\sum_{i\in\Psi_{t}}\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}}{w_{i}^{2}}\Big|

5
Conclusion

In this work, we consider contextual bandits under heavy-tailed rewards (rewards with a large range
R
) with general function approximation. The key novelty of our approach is the application of Catoni’s mean estimator for non-linear settings based on the observation that excess loss estimation is the correct object to robustify. For the known-variance case, the Catoni-OFUL algorithm combines the adaptive Catoni estimator and the variance-weighted optimization. The algorithm enjoys a variance-based regret bound with only polynomial dependence on
R
. When the per-round variance is unknown, our proposed variance-agnostic Catoni bandit algorithm carefully peels the samples based on their uncertainty and utilizes a plug-in estimator for the sum of variances. The algorithm also obtains a variance-based bound depending on
R
logarithmically, but has a worse dependence on the eluder dimension. Improving this is left as a future direction. We also provide a lower bound to show that our regret bounds are optimal in the leading-order term.

For the future work, since the Catoni estimator is a general device from robust statistics, it might also be useful to investigate if it enables us to handle other forms of noise, such as adversarial corruption
(
He et al.,, 2022
;
Ye et al.,, 2023
;
Ye et al., 2024b,
)
. Additionally, while we obtain information-theoretic results in this paper, the algorithms are not easy to implement, both because OFUL-style algorithms are always tricky due to the version space structure, and the function-dependent choice of
\theta
in the way we invoke the Catoni estimator makes things even harder. It would also be interesting to extend the results to general MDPs.

Additional Related Works

Variance-weighted regression.

Variance-weighted regression has been studied for light-tailed noises for both contextual bandits and Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) with linear and general function approximation. Specifically,
Zhou et al., (2021)
;
Zhou and Gu, (2022)
apply variance-weighted regression to obtain second-order bounds for linear contextual bandits under the known variance case. They also use the weighting technique for linear mixture MDPs under unknown variance case, where they assume that the variance can be realized by a linear function class. Similar weighted regression also appears in MDPs with linear and general function approximation to achieve the optimal regret bound
(
Agarwal et al.,, 2023
;
He et al.,, 2023
;
Zhao et al., 2023a,
)
, and in the adversarial corruption settings to make the algorithm robust to adversarial attacks
(
He et al.,, 2022
;
Ye et al.,, 2023
;
Ye et al., 2024b,
;
Ye et al., 2024a,
)
.

Heavy-tailed rewards in bandits and RL.

The topic of
robustness to heavy-tailed rewards
has received a considerable amount of attention recently.
Bubeck et al., (2013)
are the first to study heavy-tailed rewards in multi-armed bandits. More generally, robust mean estimators
(
Lugosi and Mendelson,, 2019
)
such as median-of-means, truncated mean and Catoni’s mean have been applied to linear contextual bandits
(
Medina and Yang,, 2016
;
Shao et al.,, 2018
;
Xue et al.,, 2020
;
Huang et al.,, 2024
;
Li and Sun,, 2024
)

B.1
Proof for the Lower Bound

Proof of Theorem
1
.

For any
0\leq\sigma\leq 1/2
,
0\leq\epsilon\leq\sigma/2
and
R>\sqrt{3}
, define three distributions

\displaystyle P_{\sigma}=\sigma(1+R^{-1}),\quad P_{\sigma,\epsilon}^{+}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}2\sigma,&\text{w.p.}~\frac{\sigma+\epsilon}{2\sigma},\\
2\sigma R,&\text{w.p.}~\frac{\sigma+\epsilon}{2\sigma R^{2}},\\
0,&\text{w.p.}~1-\frac{(\sigma+\epsilon)(1+R^{-2})}{2\sigma},\end{array}\right.\quad P_{\sigma,\epsilon}^{-}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}2\sigma,&\text{w.p.}~\frac{\sigma-\epsilon}{2\sigma},\\
2\sigma R,&\text{w.p.}~\frac{\sigma-\epsilon}{2\sigma R^{2}},\\
0,&\text{w.p.}~1-\frac{(\sigma-\epsilon)(1+R^{-2})}{2\sigma},\end{array}\right.

We have the means

\mu_{\sigma}=\sigma(1+R^{-1}),\qquad\mu_{\sigma,\epsilon}^{+}=(\sigma+\epsilon)(1+R^{-1}),\qquad\mu_{\sigma,\epsilon}^{-}=(\sigma-\epsilon)(1+R^{-1}),

and variance

\displaystyle V_{\sigma}=0,

(11)

\displaystyle V_{\sigma,\epsilon}^{+}=(\sigma+\epsilon)(4\sigma-(1+R^{-1})^{2}\sigma-(1+R^{-1})^{2}\epsilon)\leq 6\sigma^{2},

\displaystyle V_{\sigma,\epsilon}^{-}=(\sigma-\epsilon)(4\sigma-(1+R^{-1})^{2}\sigma+(1+R^{-1})^{2}\epsilon)\leq 2\sigma^{2}.

Thus, the rewards induced by the last two distributions
P_{\sigma,\epsilon}^{+},P_{\sigma,\epsilon}^{-}
have large
L_{1}
norm (
2\sigma R
) and bounded variances.

Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(P_{\sigma,\epsilon}^{-}\|P_{\sigma,\epsilon}^{+})=

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma-\epsilon}{2\sigma}\log\frac{\sigma-\epsilon}{\sigma+\epsilon}+\frac{\sigma-\epsilon}{2\sigma R^{2}}\log\frac{\sigma-\epsilon}{\sigma+\epsilon}+\Big(1-\frac{(\sigma-\epsilon)(1+R^{-2})}{2\sigma}\Big)\log\frac{\sigma+\epsilon-\frac{\sigma-\epsilon}{R^{2}}}{\sigma-\epsilon-\frac{\sigma+\epsilon}{R^{2}}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1+R^{-2})\frac{\sigma-\epsilon}{2\sigma}\log\frac{\sigma-\epsilon}{\sigma+\epsilon}+(1+R^{-2})\frac{\sigma+\epsilon}{2\sigma}\log\frac{\sigma+\epsilon}{\sigma-\epsilon}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4(1+R^{-2})\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}.

Fix a policy
\pi
. Now, we construct two
2
-armed bandits and let the context space
\mathcal{X}=\emptyset
. For the first bandit
B_{1}
, the reward of the first arm
R_{1}(a_{1})\sim P_{\sigma}
, and the reward of the second arm
R_{1}(a_{2})\sim P_{\sigma,\epsilon}^{-}
. Thus, the first arm
a_{1}
is the optimal arm for
B_{1}
. For the second bandit, we have
R_{2}(a_{1})\sim P_{\sigma}
, and
R_{2}(a_{2})\sim P_{\sigma,\epsilon}^{+}
, and the second arm is the optimal arm for
B_{2}
but with a large variance. For
i=1,2
, let
\mathbb{P}_{i}
denote the distribution generated by the bandit environment
B_{i}
, and let
\mathbb{E}_{i}
denote the expectations under
\mathbb{P}_{i}
. Then, we have

\mathbb{E}_{1}[R_{T}]\geq\mathbb{P}_{1}(N_{T}(1)\leq T/2)\cdot\frac{T\epsilon}{2},\quad\mathbb{E}_{2}[R_{T}]\geq\mathbb{P}_{2}(N_{T}(1)\geq T/2)\cdot\frac{T\epsilon}{2}.

Then, by Bretagnolle-Huber inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{1}[R_{T}]+\mathbb{E}_{2}[R_{T}]\geq\frac{T\epsilon}{2}\big(\mathbb{P}_{1}(N_{T}(1)\leq T/2)+\mathbb{P}_{2}(N_{T}(1)>T/2)\big)\geq\frac{T\epsilon}{4}\exp(-\mathrm{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{1}\|P_{2})).

We also have

\mathrm{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{1}\|\mathbb{P}_{2})=\mathbb{E}_{1}[N_{T}(2)]\mathrm{KL}(P_{\sigma,\epsilon}^{-}\|P_{\sigma,\epsilon}^{+})\leq\frac{4(1+R^{-2})T\epsilon^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}.

Thus, we have

\mathbb{E}_{1}[R_{T}]+\mathbb{E}_{2}[R_{T}]\geq\frac{T\epsilon}{4}\exp\Big(-\frac{4(1+R^{-2})T\epsilon^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\Big)

By choosing
\epsilon=\sqrt{\sigma^{2}/4(1+R^{-2})T}
, we have

\displaystyle\max_{i}\{\mathbb{E}_{i}[R_{T}]\}=\Omega(\sqrt{\sigma^{2}T}).

(12)

Since the variance accumulates only when the arm
x_{2}
is pulled,

\displaystyle T\geq\mathbb{E}_{1}[N_{T}(2)]=\mathbb{E}_{1}\Big[\sum_{t=1}^{T}I(x_{t}=x_{2})\Big]\geq\frac{\mathbb{E}_{1}[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sigma_{t}^{2}]}{6\sigma^{2}},

\displaystyle T\geq\mathbb{E}_{2}[N_{T}(2)]=\mathbb{E}_{2}\Big[\sum_{t=1}^{T}I(x_{t}=x_{2})\Big]\geq\frac{\mathbb{E}_{2}[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sigma_{t}^{2}]}{2\sigma^{2}},

where the last inequalities for both lines uses (
11
). Then, we have

T\geq\max\Big\{\frac{\mathbb{E}_{1}[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sigma_{t}^{2}]}{6\sigma^{2}},\frac{\mathbb{E}_{2}[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sigma_{t}^{2}]}{2\sigma^{2}}\Big\},

which together with (
12
) imply that

\max_{i\in\{1,2\}}\{\mathbb{E}_{i}[R_{T}]\}=\Omega\bigg(\sqrt{\max\Big\{\mathbb{E}_{1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sigma_{t}^{2},\mathbb{E}_{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sigma_{t}^{2}\Big\}}\bigg).

∎

Part III: Bounding the regret conditioning on good events.

We now recall the definition that
\mathcal{T}:=\{t\in[T]:t\geq 7\iota^{2}(\delta_{n,T})\}

Proof of Theorem
6
.

be the function maximizer in set

\displaystyle R_{T}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t\in[T]}r_{t}=\sum_{t\in[T]}\left(f^{\star}(x_{t}^{\star})-f^{\star}(x_{t})\right)\leq 2L_{f}\left(7\iota^{2}(\delta_{n,T})+2\right)+\sum_{t-1\in\mathcal{T}}\left(f^{n_{t}}(x_{t})-f^{\star}(x_{t})\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2L_{f}\left(7\iota^{2}(\delta_{n,T})+2\right)+\sum_{t-1\in\mathcal{T}}\left(\left|f^{n_{t}}(x_{t})-\hat{f}_{t-1}(x_{t})\right|+\left|\hat{f}_{t-1}(x_{t})-f^{\star}(x_{t})\right|\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{\leq}}O\left(L_{f}\cdot\iota^{2}(\delta_{n,T})\right)+\sum_{t-1\in\mathcal{T}}\left[\bar{\sigma}_{t}D_{\mathcal{F}}(x_{t},\bar{\sigma}_{t};x_{[t-1]},\bar{\sigma}_{[t-1]})\cdot\left(\sqrt{\sum_{i\in[t-1]}\frac{1}{\bar{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}\left(f^{n_{t}}(x_{i})-\hat{f}_{t-1}(x_{i})\right)^{2}+\lambda}\right.\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 150.00023pt\left.\left.+\sqrt{\sum_{i\in[t-1]}\frac{1}{\bar{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}\left(f^{\star}(x_{i})-\hat{f}_{t-1}(x_{i})\right)^{2}+\lambda}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(ii)}}{{\leq}}O\left(L_{f}\cdot\iota^{2}(\delta_{n,T})\right)+\sum_{t-1\in\mathcal{T}}2\bar{\sigma}_{t}D_{\mathcal{F}}(x_{t},\bar{\sigma}_{t};x_{[t-1]},\bar{\sigma}_{[t-1]})\cdot\left(\sqrt{\hat{\beta}_{t-1}^{2}+\lambda}\right),

where we use
(i)
the definition of
D_{\alpha}
for each
t\in[T]
and
(ii)
the definition of
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
and that
f^{n_{t}},f^{\star}\in\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
conditioning on
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
.

Combining this with the range bound that
[0,1]
of each individual reward one may receive by assumption, one can conclude that

R_{T}\leq O\left(L_{f}\cdot\iota^{2}(\delta_{n,T})\right)+2L_{f}\sum_{t-1\in\mathcal{T}}\min\left(1,\bar{\sigma}_{t}D_{\mathcal{F}}(x_{t},\bar{\sigma}_{t};x_{[t-1]},\bar{\sigma}_{[t-1]})\sqrt{\hat{\beta}_{t-1}^{2}+\lambda}\right).

(27)

To finally bound the regret, we bound the second term in RHS of
R_{T}
expression in (
27
) respectively. These steps mainly follow Lemma 4.4 in
Zhou and Gu, (2022)
. We can decompose the terms by considering
\mathcal{I}_{1}=\{t-1\in\mathcal{T}|D_{\mathcal{F}}(x_{t},\bar{\sigma}_{t};x_{[t-1]},\bar{\sigma}_{[t-1]})\geq 1\}
and
\mathcal{I}_{2}=\{t-1\in\mathcal{T},t\notin\mathcal{I}_{1}\}
.

For the first set, we bound its size naively by

|\mathcal{I}_{1}|\leq\sum_{t\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}\min\left(1,D^{2}_{\mathcal{F}}(x_{t},\bar{\sigma}_{t};x_{[t-1]},\bar{\sigma}_{[t-1]})\right)\leq\mathrm{dim}_{\alpha,T}(\mathcal{F}).

For the second set, we bound the summation of terms of interest contraining on
\mathcal{I}_{2}
by

\displaystyle\sum_{t\in\mathcal{I}_{2}}\bar{\sigma}_{t}\sqrt{\hat{\beta}^{2}_{t-1}+\lambda}\cdot D_{\mathcal{F}}(x_{t},\bar{\sigma}_{t};x_{[t-1]},\bar{\sigma}_{[t-1]})

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\leq\sum_{t\in\mathcal{I}_{2},\bar{\sigma}_{t}=\sigma_{t}~\text{or}~\alpha}\bar{\sigma}_{t}\sqrt{\hat{\beta}^{2}_{t-1}+\lambda}\cdot D_{\mathcal{F}}(x_{t},\bar{\sigma}_{t};x_{[t-1]},\bar{\sigma}_{[t-1]})

\displaystyle\hskip 40.00006pt+\sum_{t\in\mathcal{I}_{2},\bar{\sigma}_{t}=4\sqrt{2\iota(\delta_{n,t,l})L_{f}D_{\mathcal{F}}(x_{t};x_{[t-1]},\bar{\sigma}_{[t-1]})}}\bar{\sigma}_{t}\sqrt{\hat{\beta}^{2}_{t-1}+\lambda}\cdot D_{\mathcal{F}}(x_{t},\bar{\sigma}_{t};x_{[t-1]},\bar{\sigma}_{[t-1]})

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{\leq}}\sum_{t\in[T]}\left(\sigma_{t}+\alpha\right)\sqrt{\hat{\beta}_{t-1}^{2}+\lambda}\cdot D_{\mathcal{F}}(x_{t},\bar{\sigma}_{t};x_{[t-1]},\bar{\sigma}_{[t-1]})+\sum_{t\in[T]}32L_{f}\iota(\delta_{n,t,l})\sqrt{\hat{\beta}_{t-1}^{2}+\lambda}\cdot D^{2}_{\mathcal{F}}(x_{t},\bar{\sigma}_{t};x_{[t-1]},\bar{\sigma}_{[t-1]})

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(ii)}}{{\leq}}\sqrt{2\sum_{t\in[T]}(\hat{\beta}_{t-1}^{2}+\lambda)(\sigma_{t}^{2}+\alpha^{2})}\sqrt{\mathrm{dim}_{\alpha,T}(\mathcal{F})}+16L_{f}\iota(\delta_{n,t,l})\max_{t\in[T]}\sqrt{\hat{\beta}_{t-1}^{2}+\lambda}\cdot\mathrm{dim}_{\alpha,T}(\mathcal{F}).

Here for
(i)
we use the condition for each distinct set and for
(ii)
we use Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for the first term and the definition of
\mathrm{dim}_{\alpha}
for both terms.

Consequently plugging these back in (
27
) and take supremum over
x:|x|=T
, we conclude that with probability at least
1-2\delta
,

\displaystyle R_{T}

\displaystyle=O\left(L_{f}\cdot\iota^{2}(\delta_{n,T})+\mathrm{dim}_{\alpha,T}(\mathcal{F})+L_{f}\cdot\iota(\delta_{n,t,l})\cdot\max_{t\in[T]}\sqrt{\hat{\beta}_{t-1}^{2}+\lambda}\cdot\mathrm{dim}_{\alpha,T}(\mathcal{F})\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\left.+L_{f}\sqrt{\sum_{t\in[T]}\left(\hat{\beta}_{t-1}^{2}+\lambda\right)\left(\sigma_{t}^{2}+\alpha^{2}\right)}\cdot\sqrt{\mathrm{dim}_{\alpha,T}(\mathcal{F})}\right)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{O}\left(L_{f}\cdot\log\mathcal{N}\left(\mathcal{F},\frac{1}{L_{f}T^{2}}\right)\cdot\mathrm{dim}_{\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}},T}(\mathcal{F})+L_{f}\sqrt{\sum_{t\in[T]}\sigma_{t}^{2}}\cdot\sqrt{\mathrm{dim}_{\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}},T}(\mathcal{F})\cdot\log\mathcal{N}\left(\mathcal{F},\frac{1}{L_{f}T^{2}}\right)}\right),

where for the last inequality we pick
\lambda=\Theta(1)
,
\alpha=1/\sqrt{T}
.
