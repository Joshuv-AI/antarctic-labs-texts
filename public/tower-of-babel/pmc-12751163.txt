miCDER: a context-aware transformer model for joint miRNA-disease entity and multi-level regulatory relation extraction
Authors: Jiangcheng Shi, Lijun Wang, Lixue Liu, Jinhao Su
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDysregulation of microRNAs (miRNAs) is closely linked to the progression of diverse human diseases. However, the lack of a standardized, fine-grained dataset of miRNA–disease regulatory interactions and the limited ability of existing methods to capture multi-level regulatory relations hinder full understanding of these mechanisms.ResultsWe constructed a fine-grained, multi-level annotated biomedical dataset covering ten entity types and thirteen relation categories, tailored for modeling microRNA–disease interactions. We then propose miCDER, a transformer-based model that jointly extracts entities and relations through contextual encoding and inter-span transformer attention. miCDER achieves state-of-the-art performance in both named entity recognition (NER F1 = 87.34%) and relation extraction (RE F1 = 77.28%), significantly outperforming all methods, including SpERT, STER, BiLSTM-CRF, and CNN. Compared to the strongest baseline SpERT, our model achieved improvements of 8.84% in NER F1 and 12.35% in RE F1, respectively. To validate generalizability, we evaluated miCDER on two public benchmark datasets and compared it with state-of-the-art methods, achieving competitive performance on CoNLL04 (NER F1 = 88.83%, RE F1 = 72.31%) and superior results on the biomedical ADE dataset (NER F1 = 90.42%, RE F1 = 82.81%). Applied to 27,051 curated miRNA-related text segments from PubMed, miCDER automatically extracted 93,221 high-confidence regulatory triplets to construct the miRNA–disease knowledge graph, MAAD-HCD-KG. Among these, 1,735 targeting relations not found in the microRNA target database (miRTarBase) demonstrate miCDER’s ability to capture literature-supported regulatory associations that are not yet included in manually curated databases.ConclusionOur proposed miCDER model can serve as an effective tool for extracting fine-grained regulatory information from biomedical texts. Background

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) represent a class of small non-coding RNAs that regulate gene expression at the post-transcriptional level [1]. A growing body of evidence indicates that miRNAs are crucial regulators of a wide array of biological processes, including cell differentiation, proliferation, and apoptosis [2–4]. Aberrant miRNA expression is implicated in a wide range of pathological conditions such as cancer [5, 6], Alzheimer’s disease [7, 8], and cardiovascular disorders [9, 10]. Importantly, the regulatory functions of miRNAs exhibit significant directionality and are highly dependent on the specific context [11]. First, directionality is reflected in the bidirectional causal relationship between miRNAs and diseases: pathological conditions can alter miRNA expression, and miRNAs, in turn, can influence disease progression. For example, miR-125b is downregulated in breast cancer, resulting in increased ETS1 expression, which illustrates the regulatory influence from disease to miRNA [12]. Conversely, miR-125b facilitates glioblastoma progression by inhibiting apoptotic pathways, exemplifying the regulatory influence from miRNA to disease [13]. Second, the functional effects of miRNAs are highly contingent upon their specific biological context, indicating that the same miRNA can have divergent effects under varying biological conditions. For example, miR-21 promotes tumor development through the PTEN/PI3K-AKT signaling pathway in various cancers [14, 15], yet it provides cardioprotective effects by targeting PDCD4 and activating AP-1 in cardiac conditions [16]. These findings underscore the need for fine-grained annotations of miRNA–disease associations that capture both their directionality and mechanistic specificity.

In recent years, research on miRNA–disease association prediction has advanced considerably, evolving through distinct methodological phases. Early approaches primarily utilized single data sources such as miRNA functional similarity or disease phenotypic similarity, employing statistical methods and network propagation algorithms [17]. Jiang et al. [18] pioneered the field with a hypergeometric distribution–based algorithm, establishing the foundational “guilt-by-association” principle. This was subsequently advanced by score-based methods such as WBSMDA [19], which integrated multiple similarity measures to predict associations without requiring negative samples. The field then progressed to more sophisticated network-based approaches. RWRMDA [20] applied random walk with restart on functional similarity networks, while semi-supervised methods like RLSMDA [21] addressed the absence of negative samples using regularized least squares. RBMMMDA [22] further expanded prediction capability by leveraging restricted Boltzmann machines to capture specific association types. A significant paradigm shift emerged toward multi-source integration. Path-based methods such as PBMDA [23] exploited heterogeneous network structures to enhance miRNA–disease association prediction, while systematic reviews by Huang et al. [24] highlighted the evolution of fusion strategies and their significance in advancing the field. Notable integrated models include MDHGI [25], which combines matrix factorization with heterogeneous graph inference, and GAEMDA [26], which adopts graph autoencoders for unsupervised feature learning.

More recently, graph neural networks have become central for deep representation learning. NIMCGCN [27] employs multi-hop neighborhood aggregation in multilayer GCNs, MMGCN [28] integrates heterogeneous data via multi-view attention with adaptive feature weighting, and HGANMDA [29] constructs miRNA–disease–lncRNA heterogeneous graphs applying hierarchical attention at node and semantic levels, followed by bilinear decoding for association reconstruction. As systematically reviewed by Huang et al. [30, 31], these advances have emphasized cross-modal fusion strategies and proposed standardized evaluation frameworks, both of which improve reproducibility and facilitate systematic assessment across the field.

Parallel to the rapid development of computational methods, significant progress has been made in building miRNA-disease association data resources. Initially, databases like miR2Disease [32] compiled miRNA-disease associations through manual curation. Nevertheless, this database is relatively limited in size and lacks comprehensive coverage. In contrast, the human microRNA disease database (HMDD v4.0) [33] now includes over 53,000 experimentally validated entries, and miRTarBase [34] incorporates data from luciferase reporter assays and cross-linking immunoprecipitation sequencing experiments to construct miRNA-target gene interaction networks associated with disease phenotypes. Additionally, the database of Differentially Expressed miRNAs in human Cancers (dbDEMC) [35] is dedicated to the differential expression of miRNAs across various cancer types.

Facing the explosive growth of biomedical literature, miRNA-related articles in PubMed had already exceeded 180,000 by 2024, making traditional manual curation impractical and highlighting the urgent need for automated information extraction methods. Early computational approaches to named entity recognition (NER) and relation extraction (RE) primarily relied on rule-based methods, which often fail to capture the complex semantics inherent in biomedical texts. The advent of deep learning improved performance significantly: BiLSTM-CRF [36] combined the sequence modeling capability of bidirectional long short-term memory networks with the global optimization of conditional random fields, enhancing entity boundary detection accuracy, while CNNs [37] excelled at extracting local features in RE, effectively identifying semantic relationships between entity pairs. However, in practical information extraction tasks, pipeline architectures are often adopted, performing NER first, followed by RE. This approach suffers from cascading errors and prevents joint optimization, limiting overall performance [38]. To overcome these limitations, joint extraction has emerged as a research focus, with the Transformer architecture providing a powerful foundation for modeling long-distance dependencies via self-attention mechanisms.

Building on this foundation, transformer-based models pretrained on domain-specific corpora, such as BioBERT [39], BioMegatron [40], and SciBERT [41], have significantly improved the performance of biomedical information extraction tasks. In the general domain, span-based models like SpERT [42] have further improved joint entity and relation extraction by employing span enumeration and joint optimization strategies. Recently proposed STER [43] introduces a contrastive student-teacher learning framework for joint entity and relation extraction, where teacher networks leverage privileged features—information available only during training that supports entity and relation detection. The student network learns to integrate knowledge from the teacher networks via contrastive learning, enabling improved modeling of interactions between entities and relations and achieving state-of-the-art performance on general-domain benchmarks. However, existing research faces the following key challenges: First, most miRNA-disease association research primarily focuses on binary classification problems of whether associations exist between miRNAs and diseases. This simple binary relationship modeling cannot capture the complex regulatory mechanisms of miRNA-disease interactions, particularly lacking fine-grained characterization of regulatory directionality, multi-level regulatory networks, and mechanistic specificity. Second, although related databases are expanding, annotation granularity and quality remain limited, mostly modeling simple binary associations without detailed directional or pathway-level information. Third, while current information extraction techniques perform well in general domains, they still cannot explicitly model directionality or multi-hop regulatory cascades, which are characteristic of miRNA–disease interactions. Currently, no specialized joint extraction framework exists for capturing the complex, multi-entity regulatory relationships in this domain.

To address these challenges, we present the following key contributions:

We propose miCDER, a context-aware transformer model for the joint extraction of miRNA–disease entities and their multi-level regulatory relations. By integrating context-aware span encoding with inter-span contextual transformer attention, miCDER effectively captures complex interactions between entities and relations, achieving state-of-the-art performance. This approach addresses the limitations of traditional pipeline methods and demonstrates robust generalization across both biomedical and general-domain datasets.

Dataset construction

The biomedical relation extraction dataset developed in this study is based on the authoritative HMDD v4.0, which manually curates miRNA–disease association data from PubMed literature sentences reporting such associations, serving as a foundation for systematically investigating their regulatory relationships and underlying molecular mechanisms. Through manual curation, we identified and selected text segments from the literature that encompass comprehensive regulatory pathways, as well as multiple nested clauses and subclauses. This approach was employed to ensure that the data samples demonstrate multi-layered regulatory mechanisms and complex syntactic structures. The objective of this strategy is to augment the model’s ability to discern intricate semantic relationships among biomedical entities that extend across sentence boundaries. Consequently, we successfully extracted 641 high-quality biomedical relational instances. Each data instance comprises semantically complete text segments, which may consist of either single sentences or multi-sentence composites. This design maintains sentence-level contextual information, thereby optimizing multi-granularity semantic understanding and enabling the model to effectively capture cross-sentence dependencies.

The annotation schema rigorously delineates 10 entity types and 13 relation types, with comprehensive definitions and statistical summaries detailed in Tables S1 and S2 in the Supplementary material. The entity types encompass fundamental biomedical concepts, such as miRNAs, diseases, and genes, among others, with annotations preserving the original nomenclature from the source literature to ensure semantic accuracy. The relation types focus on nuanced regulatory roles, adhering to the following annotation principles:

Relation type classification: The regulatory nature of interactions is determined based on explicit modulatory verbs—verbs such as induce and activate are annotated as promote, whereas suppress and inhibit are annotated as negatively regulate.

Pathway integrity: Multi-hop annotation enables the capture of complete regulatory pathways. For example, a sentence might describe a scenario in which a miRNA regulates a gene, which subsequently modulates a signaling pathway, ultimately affecting disease progression. In these instances, intermediate entities, such as genes, proteins, and signaling pathways, are not merely linguistic placeholders but serve as mechanistic mediators within molecular biology. By explicitly representing these intermediates and their interactions (e.g., miRNA ⟶ gene ⟶ pathway ⟶ disease), our approach maintains the continuity of regulatory chains, thereby accurately reflecting biological processes that traverse multiple molecular layers. This preservation of pathway integrity is essential for downstream applications, including causal inference and the construction of biomedical knowledge graphs.

To assess the model’s generalization capabilities and mitigate data bias, we employed a text block-level stratified sampling strategy to divide the dataset into training, validation, and test sets. Specifically, we utilized 13 distinct relation types as stratification variables to ensure that the distribution of relations in the training, validation and test sets accurately reflects that of the original dataset. Each data item consists of either a single sentence or a multi-sentence composite text, which may include multiple semantically related sentences and instances of relations. Ultimately, the training set comprised 461 data items (71.9%), the validation set included 108 data items (16.8%), and the test set contained 72 data items (11.2%). Given that each data item may encompass multiple entities and relations, and that certain entity and relation categories, such as miRNA entities and upregulate relations, are more prevalent in our dataset, we sought to regulate the distribution of each entity and relation type across the training, validation, and test sets to approximate an 8:1:1 ratio. This strategy ensures that categories with high frequency are sufficiently represented in all subsets while preserving the overall diversity of less frequent types. As a result, although the exact ratios may vary slightly for each category, the dataset splits maintain a balanced and representative distribution, thereby facilitating robust model training.

The distribution of entities and relations is as follows: the training dataset comprises 2,275 entity instances and 1,893 relation instances, the validation dataset includes 420 entity instances and 346 relation instances, and the test dataset contains 303 entity instances and 240 relation instances. miCDER

The miCDER model is designed to perform joint extraction of named entities and their relations between miRNAs and complex human diseases. By integrating explicit entity boundaries into span representations and employing an inter-span contextual transformer attention mechanism to derive relational representations, the model demonstrates enhanced suitability for biomedical relation extraction tasks. Model training

Our experiments employ PubMedBERT as the text encoder, utilizing the AdamW optimizer for training, with an initial learning rate of 5 × 10⁻⁵ and a weight decay coefficient of 0.03 for parameter updates. The training process was conducted over 60 epochs with a batch size of 4, incorporating a dropout rate of 0.3 to enhance the model’s generalization capabilities. To address the issue of lengthy entities common in biomedical texts, the maximum entity span limit was extended to 15 words. During the training process, we implemented a negative sampling strategy to enhance the model’s robustness. This technique, widely adopted in relation extraction tasks, involves constructing negative samples from the dataset, such as pairs of entities that do not hold any target relation, and labeling them as “no relation” for training purposes. This approach improves the model’s ability to distinguish irrelevant entity pairs. The negative sampling strategy was configured with a sampling quantity of 150 for both entities and relations. The experimental setup was deployed on an NVIDIA RTX 3060 computing platform.

Evaluation metrics

We conducted an evaluation of the model’s performance in both named entity recognition and relation extraction tasks. For the named entity recognition task, accurate entity recognition requires both precise alignment of text spans and semantic consistency of type labels. In assessing relation triples, it is necessary to ensure not only the accuracy of relation types but also that the boundaries and types of associated entities adhere to labeling criteria.

We employed precision, recall, and macro-averaged F1 score (Macro-F1) as the main evaluation metrics, consistent with the gold standard for joint entity and relation extraction. The macro-averaged approach calculates F1-scores for each category independently and then averages them arithmetically. This ensures equal weighting of low-frequency categories (e.g., the “Sponging” relation with only 24 samples) and high-frequency categories (e.g., the “Upregulate” relation with 393 samples), thereby mitigating the bias caused by the dominance of large categories in micro-averaged metrics.

In addition to these primary metrics, we report Accuracy, Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC), and Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (AUPR) to provide complementary perspectives on model performance. For entity recognition, metrics are computed over all spans appearing in either the ground truth or model predictions, and for relation extraction over all entity pairs appearing in either. This union-based evaluation strategy ensures task-relevant evaluation while avoiding computational complexity and inflation from trivial negatives.

Construction of the miRNA–disease regulatory knowledge graph

To systematically organize and represent the intricate regulatory relationships identified by our model, we constructed a structured knowledge graph, termed MAAD-HCD-KG, using the Neo4j graph database. In this graph, biological entities are modeled as nodes, and their regulatory or associative interactions are represented as directed edges, enabling efficient storage, retrieval, and analysis of complex miRNA–disease associations.

We applied our model miCDER to a corpus of 27,051 curated text segments derived from miRNA-related articles indexed in PubMed. This process yielded 93,221 high-confidence triplets, each comprising two biological entities connected by a directional relation. Provenance information, such as the source sentence and associated PubMed ID, was also retained for each triplet. These structured outputs provide a robust foundation for constructing a fine-grained miRNA regulatory knowledge graph.

In Neo4j, the MAAD-HCD-KG comprises over 40,000 unique nodes and more than 90,000 edges, forming a structured and queryable resource for exploring miRNA regulation. It supports flexible querying via the Cypher language, enabling researchers to retrieve specific miRNA-disease associations, explore multi-hop regulatory pathways, and visualize key subnetworks. Performance on miRNA-disease dataset

To thoroughly evaluate the performance of our proposed model, we conducted a series of multi-dimensional comparative experiments using miRNA–disease regulatory relationship datasets, with all models trained from scratch. The models used for comparison included SpERT, STER, BiLSTM-CRF, and CNN-based methodologies. The evaluation was conducted from two complementary perspectives: (1) Independent Task: For the NER task, we conducted an enhanced BiLSTM-CRF [46] model, which can handle nested entities using a dynamic hierarchical neural network. For the RE task, we adopted the CNN model proposed by Zeng et al. [47], which leveraged gold-standard entity annotations to minimize error propagation and provided an objective evaluation of the model’s capability in capturing multi-class relationships. (2) Joint Extraction Task: We implemented the popular span-based joint extraction model, SpERT, in addition to the STER model, a teacher-student learning framework, designed to enhance joint extraction performance by leveraging privileged features.

Results on the CoNLL04 and ADE test sets

On the CoNLL04 dataset, compared with other baselines such as SpERT with an F1 score of 68.06% and KdNre [51] with 72.08%, miCDER maintains competitive relation extraction results while achieving the highest precision of 75.18%. Its relation extraction performance is also comparable to STER and significantly better than the reproduced STER implementation, as well as TableF-GCN [52]. Moreover, miCDER demonstrates distinct advantages over previous methodologies, including Multi-head [53] and Relation-Metric [54]. These results demonstrate miCDER’s robustness even in general-domain news data with relatively simple relational structures. Given that relation extraction is inherently more challenging than entity recognition, miCDER’s strong F1 score further highlights its effectiveness and capability in capturing complex relational patterns.

On the ADE dataset, miCDER delivers comprehensively superior performance, leading all baselines in every key metric. It achieves the highest scores across all three metrics for both entity recognition (Precision, Recall, F1) and relation extraction (Precision, Recall, F1). Compared with other baselines, miCDER’s entity recognition F1 score reached 90.42%, and its relation extraction F1 score of 82.81% significantly outperformed SpERT and KdNre, as well as surpassing TableF-GCN and the reproduced STER implementation. These results demonstrate miCDER’s ability to effectively capture complex biomedical relations.

In summary, the proposed miCDER model exhibits strong competitiveness against both TableF-GCN and STER across the CoNLL04 and ADE datasets. In terms of the comprehensive F1 metric, miCDER surpasses TableF-GCN in three key aspects: relation extraction F1 on CoNLL04, as well as entity recognition F1 and relation extraction F1 on ADE. Moreover, it outperforms the reproduced STER across all four F1 scores—entity and relation F1 on both datasets. The significant improvement in relation extraction F1, a core and challenging objective in joint extraction, is particularly noteworthy and underscores the efficacy of our approach.

To provide deeper insights into model characteristics, we conduct a comprehensive comparison with STER across two benchmark datasets. On the CoNLL04 dataset, miCDER demonstrates competitive performance despite STER’s advantages, which underscores STER’s proficiency in processing general-domain text with straightforward relational patterns through its use of privileged feature mechanisms. Conversely, on the ADE dataset, the performance disparity is notably reduced: while STER achieves a slightly higher NER F1 score, miCDER significantly outperforms STER in relation extraction. This performance trend underscores fundamental architectural differences: STER’s contrastive learning with privileged features excels in entity recognition within general domains, whereas miCDER, with its inter-span contextual transformer attention and specialization in biomedical domains (utilizing PubMedBERT and span width embeddings), offers considerable advantages for modeling complex relationships. Notably, on our miRNA–disease dataset, which is characterized by nested entities and 13 fine-grained relation types, miCDER consistently surpasses STER. This outcome illustrates that miCDER’s specialized architecture is particularly effective for extracting complex, domain-specific regulatory relationships.

This cross-dataset performance variation can be attributed to differences in dataset characteristics. miCDER’s advantages are more pronounced in scenarios featuring complex relationship structures, fine-grained relationship types, and nested entity representations. Such datasets demand fine-grained semantic distinctions and multi-hop contextual understanding, precisely the challenges that miCDER’s cross-span contextual transformer attention mechanism addresses effectively. Conversely, the CoNLL04 dataset exemplifies scenarios with relatively simple relational patterns: it contains only five relation types with clear semantic boundaries, entities are typically short spans such as person names or organizations, and most relations occur as direct pairwise associations (e.g., Person–Organization, Person–Location). These characteristics reduce the need for sophisticated contextual modeling or multi-hop reasoning. These baselines, including STER, SpERT, and TableF-GCN, generally excel in general-domain tasks due to their reliance on mechanisms optimized for direct entity–relation mapping and relatively simpler relational structures.

In summary, miCDER delivers the greatest value in scenarios characterized by:

Ablation study

To assess the efficacy of each component within the model, a comprehensive ablation study was performed using our dataset of miRNA-disease complex regulatory associations. As illustrated in Table 5, the complete model demonstrated optimal performance on the test set, achieving an F1-score of 77.28%, precision of 82.99%, and recall of 73.45%. The exclusion of the entity boundary and inter-span contextual transformer attention components resulted in reductions in F1-scores by 2.23% and 1.36%, respectively, thereby substantiating the importance of these components in modeling long-distance entity relations. Substituting PubMedBERT with generalized encoder BERT led to the most significant performance decline, with a 12.28% decrease in the F1-score and a substantial reduction in accuracy to 67.07%, underscoring the limitations of general-purpose text encoders when applied to biomedical relation extraction. Furthermore, the removal of inter-span contextual max-pooling led to a 1.53% decrease in the model’s F1-score. In conclusion, each component significantly and positively contributes to the overall performance of the model.

Discussion

In the present paper, we designed the miCDER model grounded in a Transformer architecture to perform automated joint extraction of intricate, multi-level miRNA–disease regulatory relationships. This model effectively addresses the limitations of traditional methods by leveraging contextual encoding and cross-entity attention mechanisms, enabling it to capture long-distance dependencies and complex multi-entity interactions. Based on the entities and relationships automatically extracted by our model, we constructed a miRNA–disease regulatory knowledge graph, providing a structured and queryable resource to facilitate biomedical research. However, this study is subject to several limitations: (1) The core Transformer-based architecture requires substantial computational resources during training, particularly when handling large-scale data inference tasks. (2) The annotation of high-quality, fine-grained biomedical text is both time-intensive and costly, which constrains the model’s scalability to larger datasets. (3) As the dataset is annotated only on segments of individual literature paragraphs, the model encounters significant challenges in extracting miRNA–disease regulatory relationships across multiple documents or chapters.

It is important to note that our model primarily extracts associations reported in the literature rather than directly predicting entirely new biological relationships, reflecting the inherent imbalance between known and unknown miRNA–disease associations in available databases. To address this challenge, future work will explore leveraging additional literature sources, integrating temporal dynamics and external knowledge graphs, and adopting semi-supervised, self-supervised, or incremental learning paradigms to enhance the identification of rare or unrecorded associations and improve the model’s generalization capabilities.

In the future, our work will rely on the constructed MAAD-HCD-KG knowledge graph to provide high-quality training data for prediction models, thereby offering valuable support for miRNA–disease association prediction. Furthermore, the captured multi-level regulatory relationships create opportunities for developing advanced graph neural network methods, such as hypergraph neural networks for modeling complex multi-entity interactions and temporal graph networks for capturing dynamic regulatory patterns. Future research can integrate this rich semantic information with semi-supervised learning frameworks to systematically enhance the ability of prediction models to capture complex regulatory relationships, thereby advancing the development of more comprehensive and accurate miRNA-disease association prediction methods.

Conclusions

In this study, we propose miCDER, a context-aware transformer model for fine-grained and directional extraction of multi-level miRNA–disease regulatory relationships. Additionally, we developed the inaugural dedicated biomedical dataset specifically aimed at capturing complex miRNA–disease regulatory interactions. Our experimental results show that miCDER achieves robust performance, with a named entity recognition (NER) precision of 91.61% (F1 = 87.34%) and a relation extraction (RE) precision of 82.99% (F1 = 77.28%), outperforming existing baselines. Leveraging our model, we constructed MAAD-HCD-KG, an extensive miRNA–disease regulatory knowledge graph implemented using Neo4j. MAAD-HCD-KG incorporates 93,221 high-confidence regulatory triplets identified from 27,051 curated PubMed text segments, covering 5,764 unique miRNAs and 6,437 distinct diseases. This knowledge graph facilitates structured storage, flexible querying, and network-based analysis of fine-grained regulatory pathways. Notably, miCDER identified 1,735 novel targeting relations absent from miRTarBase, highlighting its capability to uncover literature-supported associations not yet included in manually curated databases. These results demonstrate that our model effectively extracts complex regulatory information from biomedical literature, providing a reliable foundation for downstream knowledge discovery and biomedical research.
