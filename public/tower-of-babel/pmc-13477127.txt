Staged cardiac and pulmonary resection for primary cardiac sarcoma
Authors: Marvin D. Atkins, Marcell Székely, Fernando Ramirez Del Val, Maria G. Lopez-Trevino, Edward A. Graviss, Warren C. Naselsky, Vinod Ravi, Michael J. Reardon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesPrimary cardiac sarcoma is exceptionally rare, and the need for anatomical lung resection to achieve complete removal markedly increases operative risk. To reduce bleeding into the pneumonectomy space and mitigate hazards of single-stage cardiac and pulmonary resections, we adopted a staged strategy performing lung resection only after cardiopulmonary bypass–related coagulopathy has resolved. This study evaluates the outcomes of this approach.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed our institutional cardiac tumor database (April 1998 to October 2025) for primary cardiac sarcomas requiring anatomical lung resection. Fourteen patients underwent the “Texas Two-Step” approach—cardiac sarcoma resection followed by delayed pneumonectomy or lobectomy. Outcomes were compared with a historical cohort who underwent concomitant pneumonectomy during cardiac sarcoma resection (n = 7). Primary end points were operative and 1-, 2-, and 3-year survival, with recurrence as a secondary end point.ResultsThe median patient age was 42 years. Tumors involved the left atrium with pulmonary venous invasion in 50% and the pulmonary arteries in 50%. Pneumonectomy was required in 78.6%, and lobectomy in 21.4%. R0 resection was achieved in 64.3%. There was no operative mortality compared with 43% in the historical cohort. One-, 2-, and 3-year survival was 64%, 53.5%, and 42.8%, with significantly better survival than controls (P = .0075). Median survival was 28.1 months. Recurrence occurred in 50%, predominantly metastatic. The historical cohort required significantly more transfusions (median, 8 vs 1 unit, P = .006).ConclusionsThe Texas Two-Step strategy reduces operative mortality and transfusion needs in patients requiring lung resection for cardiac sarcoma and facilitates safer management in high-volume multidisciplinary centers.

Survival after cardiac sarcoma resection with concomitant and staged lung resection.

Central Message

Promising short-term mortality and morbidity can be achieved with the use of a staged approach for the treatment of complex primary cardiac sarcomas requiring cardiac and lung resections.

Perspective

Primary cardiac sarcomas requiring lung resection pose extreme operative challenges. Concomitant cardiac and lung resection are traditionally associated with high mortality due to bleeding and pulmonary complications. A staged “Texas Two-Step” approach reduces early operative deaths and offers improved survival for this highly aggressive disease.

Primary cardiac sarcomas (PCS) are extremely rare tumors, and institutional surgical experience regarding these malignant tumors is generally limited to case reports and small case series. Despite advances in contemporary treatment strategies and systemic therapies over the past 20 years, outcomes after surgical resection remain suboptimal, even though surgery continues to be the primary treatment modality for PCS when anatomically feasible.1,2 According to our previously published single-institutional experience, as a referral center with a multidisciplinary cardiac tumor program with 122 cases, postoperative survival at 1 and 3 years was 57.1% and 24.5%, respectively.3,4 Furthermore, PCS that also require a lobectomy or pneumonectomy to obtain an R0 resection represents an extreme challenge, with poor operative survival reported.5

Our prospectively collected cardiac tumor database currently contains 171 cases of surgical resection for PCS. To increase the likelihood of R0 resection, aggressive operative techniques, such as partial or total cardiac autotransplantation and pulmonary root and main pulmonary artery (PA) resections, are often required.3,5, 6, 7, 8 Procedural complexity is further increased when extensive cardiac resection is coupled with the need for lung resection, especially pneumonectomy. Both left atrial and PA sarcomas may exhibit substantial pulmonary involvement that requires pneumonectomy to achieve complete resection.3,5,7 In our previously reported experience, we found that patients who required concomitant pneumonectomy and complex cardiac resection had a greater operative mortality compared with those who did not (43% vs 11%).5 On the basis of this observation, we developed a staged procedure, called the “Texas Two-Step,” when first the cardiac resection is done on cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), and later, when coagulopathy is managed, a pneumonectomy or lobectomy is performed.9

There is a paucity of data on the optimal timing for lung resection in cases of complex cardiac tumors. In this study, we aim to describe our institutional experience, focusing on perioperative and short-term outcomes with the Texas Two-Step procedure for the treatment of PCS requiring additional pulmonary resection.

Methods

We retrospectively reviewed our prospectively collected institutional review board–approved cardiac tumor database from April 1998 to October 2025 for PCS resections that required lung resection. The authors attest that all research was conducted in accordance with institutional human and animal research regulations and Food and Drug Administration guidelines. All patients provided informed consent for publication under our institutional review board protocol (Pro00006836, December 20, 2011).

From a total of 171 operated cardiac sarcoma cases, an anatomical lung resection was performed in a delayed, staged method in 14 patients (Texas-Two-Step). Patients who were not treated with the Texas Two-Step procedure before our institution of this procedure were used as historical controls (concomitant pneumonectomy group, n = 7). Primary end points were operative mortality and survival at 1, 2, and 3 years from surgery. The secondary end point was local or metastatic recurrence during follow-up. Beginning in 2016, the staged strategy was adopted as the institutional standard. Before this, concomitant cardiac and major lung resections were performed. After 2016, concomitant resections were undertaken only in rare intraoperative emergency situations and were excluded from the present analysis. Patients undergoing limited wedge resections were also excluded.

Demographic, operative, and perioperative data were obtained with direct patient chart reviews. Details regarding the histologic type and location of the tumors were extracted from pathology and imaging reports. Complete follow-up was available in 13 of 14 patients who underwent the Texas Two-Step (93%) and all historical patients. Long-term survival data were missing for 1 patient.

Preoperative Workup

A multidisciplinary cardiac tumor team that included oncologists, radiologists, cardiologists, and cardiac surgeons evaluated all patients and agreed upon their surgical candidacy. Our current practice includes first obtaining a biopsy specimen for all right heart tumors and, if possible, in other cardiac locations to allow histologic diagnosis. If biopsy-proven sarcoma is present, neoadjuvant chemotherapy with doxorubicin/ifosfamide or pembrolizumab is initiated for 6 cycles (for all histologic subtypes) before surgical intervention is attempted. Patients did not undergo neoadjuvant treatment in cases in which histologic diagnosis could not be obtained because of anatomic location or when there was surgical urgency. For recurrent tumors, adjuvant chemotherapy after the first resection were administered. Evaluation of local disease and distant metastasis were made using preoperative imaging modalities including computed tomography (CT) of the chest and abdomen, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), positron emission tomography (PET)-CT, and cardiac echocardiography. Important functional measurements on echocardiography and cardiac MRI were collected, such as right and left heart function, and dimensions. Preoperatively, all patients underwent pulmonary function testing and split ventilation-perfusion scan to assess the feasibility and extent of lung resection. Coronary anatomy and potential coronary artery disease were evaluated with left cardiac catheterization with coronary angiography in most cases. Cardiac MRI was extremely important in this cohort, because sarcomas affecting or obstructing the PA were often misdiagnosed previously as pulmonary emboli. MRI can be used to examine tissue perfusion, which is the main difference between clots and PA tumors. Resectability was determined on the basis of anatomic, oncologic, and physiologic criteria. Anatomic contraindications included cases of extensive bilateral parenchymal invasion or PA involvement beyond the lobar branches despite neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Physiologic contraindication was insufficient predicted postoperative pulmonary reserve on preoperative pulmonary function testing. Oncologic contraindication was the diagnosis of distant metastatic disease beyond the thoracic cavity on PET-CT. Additional contraindications included advanced age, significant comorbidities, or frailty precluding major surgery. In selected young patients with preserved functional status and limited disease burden, palliative resection was considered when complete resection was not achievable but symptom relief or short-term survival benefit was anticipated. Otherwise, R0 resection and potential curative treatment were planned.

Operative Strategy

All patients in this cohort were operated on by one of the senior authors (M.J.R. or M.D.A.). The operative techniques of partial and total cardiac autotransplantation have been previously described by our group (Video 1).5,6,9 In partial autotransplantation, the aorta, PA, and superior vena cava are divided, whereas the inferior vena cava is left intact. This approach provides access to complex left atrial structures for resection and may facilitate a technically easier and shorter reconstruction. Patients who underwent the Texas Two-Step procedure were kept intubated in between the cardiac and the lung resection. Eligibility for the second-stage lung resection was based on objective laboratory and clinical criteria. Patients were considered ready when international normalized ratio, activated partial thromboplastin time, and fibrinogen were within normal limits, chest tube output had slowed significantly, and hemodynamic stability and preserved end-organ function were achieved without the use of vasoactive agents. When these criteria were met, lung resection was performed. In cases of significant bleeding or hemodynamic instability, delayed chest closure was performed with planned re-exploration at 24 hours. Lung resection was subsequently scheduled approximately 24 hours after definitive chest closure, provided the aforementioned criteria were met. As a development of the formerly described technique, robotic approaches were used for the last 3 anatomic lung resections. In case of absent lung perfusion and nonfunctional lung parenchyma, pneumonectomy was planned on the affected side to achieve possible R0 resection. Pulmonary endarterectomy was performed when tumor involvement extended proximally within a PA but distal lung perfusion was preserved. Lobectomy was reserved for cases in which the first-order PA branches were not involved and adequate pulmonary reserve was confirmed preoperatively.

Statistical Analysis

Operative mortality was defined as all-cause death occurring within 30 days of surgery or during the same hospitalization. Survival data were obtained from our internal research data repository and routine patient follow-up. Continuous variables are expressed as median and interquartile range (IQR) and compared using Mann-Whitney U test, if appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Longitudinal survival and local/metastatic recurrence were estimated using Kaplan-Meier analysis. Survival between the historical and Texas Two-Step cohorts was compared using the log-rank test. All statistical analyses were performed using R 4.0.3 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Fourteen patients underwent the Texas Two-Step procedure at our institution during the examined 27-year period. Five patients were female (35.7%), and the median age at surgery was 42 years (IQR, 38-70 years). Median time from diagnosis to surgery was 8.5 months (IQR, 3-11.8 months). Most patients were White.

Histologic tumor types included 9 (64.3%) high-grade angiosarcoma, 2 (14.3%) undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcomas, 1 (7.1%) leiomyosarcoma, 1 (7.1%) monophasic synovial cell sarcoma, and 1 (7.1%) high-grade spindle cell sarcoma in the Texas Two-Step cohort. Historical concomitant cases included 3 (42.9%) high-grade angiosarcoma, 2 (28.6%) high-grade spindle cell sarcoma, 1 (14.3%) myxoid sarcoma, and 1 (14.3%) leiomyosarcoma. The most common cardiac anatomical sites involved within the Texas Two-Step cohort were the left atrium, with invasion into the pulmonary veins or (n = 7, 50%) the PA with invasion into the branch pulmonary arteries (n = 7, 50%) (Figures 1 and 2). One tumor invaded the left ventricle, and in another case, a PA sarcoma invaded the right ventricular outflow tract. The tumor affected the right atrium in 2 cases. The lungs were affected by tumor in all cases, with the left side in 6 (42.9%), and the right side in 8 patients (57.1%). The tumor invaded one of the hilar regions of the lungs in 4 cases (28.6%). Preoperative distant metastasis (outside of the mediastinum) was ruled out in all patients using a combination of CT of the abdomen, MRI of the brain, and PET-CT. In the Texas Two-Step cohort, 2 (14.3%) patients had local recurrence after previous cardiac sarcoma resection. Of the 2 patients with a recurrent tumor, one was a third-time sternotomy after 2 failed sarcoma resections 2 and 5 years before the Texas Two-Step procedure. The other case was a second-time sternotomy after previous debulking of a left atrial sarcoma, with tumor invasion of the left upper pulmonary vein and left lung.

All patients were symptomatic at diagnosis. Because one half of the tumors were PA sarcomas, pulmonary hypertension was observed in 8 cases (57.1%) and right ventricular dilation could be seen in 3 patients (21.4%) before surgery. Split lung function showed less than 5% perfusion to one of the lungs in 7 cases (50%). Neoadjuvant chemotherapy was administered in 10 cases (71.4%) to reduce the tumor size and increase the chance of R0 resection. The predominant neoadjuvant therapy was doxorubicin/ifosfamide chemotherapy (n = 9). Pembrolizumab monotherapy was used in 1 patient. The largest median tumor size was 6 cm (IQR, 4.8-9.8 cm) before neoadjuvant chemotherapy and reduced after 4 to 6 cycles to a median of 5.6 cm (IQR, 3.8-8.2 cm). Volumetric tumor reactions were observed in 7 patients (70%) and metabolic response rate was 100%, confirmed by PET-CT. Two patients underwent an urgent operation at the time of diagnosis because of near-bilateral PA obstruction and hemodynamic instability. The comparison between the Texas Two-Step (n = 14 patients) and our historical PCS resection with a concomitant pneumonectomy group (n = 7 patients) showed significantly different survival (P = .0075) favoring the former. Operative mortality and survival at 1, 2, and 3 years after the Texas Two-Step and concomitant pneumonectomy cohort was 0% versus 43%, 64% versus 14.3%, 53.5% versus 14.3%, and 42.8% versus 0%, respectively (Figure 3, C). In the historical cohort, the mechanism of death in the 3 patients who died perioperatively was pulmonary edema of the remaining unilateral lung, leading to respiratory failure and subsequent cardiac arrest. For the Texas Two-Step cohort, a median of 1 (IQR, 0-3) red blood cell unit was required. In the concomitant pneumonectomy group, a median of 8 (IQR, 4-21) red blood cell units were transfused perioperatively. Patients who underwent a concomitant pneumonectomy required significantly more transfusions compared with the Texas Two-Step cohort (P = .006). In the concomitant pneumonectomy cohort, all patients had an autotransplantation (100%); however, in the Texas Two-Step group, only 21.4% of the patients underwent cardiac autotransplantation. An exploratory sensitivity analysis comparing patients with angiosarcoma (n = 12) and nonangiosarcoma (n = 9) histology demonstrated no significant difference in overall survival (P = .312). Similarly, operative mortality did not differ between the 2 groups (P = .553).

Discussion

Our study has several important findings. Mainly, the Texas Two-Step procedure is a safe approach for the treatment of PCS requiring both extensive cardiac and pulmonary resections. Despite the similar complexity in the oncologic resection, we had no operative mortality in this series, representing a substantial improvement over our previously published 30-day mortality of 43% using cardiac autotransplantation and concomitant pneumonectomy.5 Thus, the Texas Two-Step was associated with a reduction in operative mortality. Compared with our overall cardiac sarcoma experience, l- and 2-year survival did not differ, a finding probably associated with the complexity of these cases and the aggressive nature of this disease.3 Metastatic recurrence was observed at a median of 14 months after the Texas Two-Step procedure.

The indication for concomitant anatomic cardiac and lung resection for malignant diseases is rare, and cardiothoracic surgeons rarely face this challenge.10 However, as an institution with a high-volume multidisciplinary cardiac tumor program, the presence of this situation is not uncommon. General principles for treating PCS have been previously described.2, 3, 4,11 The approach to PCS with direct lung involvement is more problematic. Earlier in our experience, we performed simultaneous anatomic lung resections after extensive cardiac tumor resections using CPB. This approach resulted in a high 30-day mortality of 43%.5 Coagulopathy after an extensive on-bypass cardiac resection led to bleeding into the pneumonectomy space that was difficult to control. This resulted in multiple transfusions and the eventual pulmonary edema in the remaining lung as the cause of death. The Two-Step procedure was developed to allow the patient to recover from any post-CPB coagulopathy before the pulmonary resection.

Our initial results using this staged procedure were encouraging, with 3 cases having no operative mortality.9 This report extends our clinical experience and outcomes with the Texas Two-Step procedure, as a staged anatomical cardiac and lung resection technique for the treatment of PCS with invasion to the lungs. Angiosarcoma was the most common histological tumor type, although all cases had extensive cardiac and lung involvement. On the basis of the initial symptoms and imaging findings, many patients with PA sarcoma were misdiagnosed at others centers as having PA thromboembolism and were administered anticoagulation, with no significant improvement noted.7 Cardiac MRI was a crucial tool in the differential diagnosis regarding these patients to evaluate tissue perfusion to PA masses. Complex cardiac resection and reconstruction techniques were often used in these patients, consistent with other reported cohorts of patients with cardiac sarcoma.3,5 After the division and closure of the right or left PA, we waited a median of 1.5 days to manage coagulopathy, metabolic derangements, hemodynamics, and end-organ functions then a pneumonectomy/lobectomy was performed. The relatively long procedural times for the lung resections reflect the technical challenges of dissecting the dense chest adhesions caused by these tumors. With the strategy to stage the procedure, we could avoid the invasive median sternothoracotomy incisions, which we used earlier in concomitant patients. Besides reducing the invasiveness, we could create a better exposure for a pneumonectomy with 2 separate incisions and allowed the use of robotic technology.

Despite the complexity and invasiveness of the procedure, operative mortality was 0% with the Texas Two-Step approach, which represents a promising improvement compared with our previous 43% operative mortality with concomitant pneumonectomies (Figure 3, C). The survival benefit was also evident at 1 year (64% vs 14.3%) and 2 years (53.5% vs 14.3%) and was significant in patients undergoing the staged resection technique (P = .0075). In addition, the patients after the Texas Two-Step procedure required significantly less blood transfusions (median of 1 vs 8 units), which likely played a role in the reduction of mortality, caused by pulmonary edema associated with multiple blood transfusions. The successful management of coagulopathy before the planned lung resection could minimize bleeding complications into the pneumonectomy space. Furthermore, since our earlier concomitant cases, our cumulative experience in treating this frail PCS patient population has markedly increased, which may have contributed to the improved outcomes observed.

Compared with our general experience with PCS, survival after the Texas Two-Step (with a high, 64.3% R0 resection rate) at 1 and 3 years is comparable with other patients with a cardiac sarcoma, not requiring pulmonary resection (64% vs 57.1% at 1 year and 42.8% vs 24.5% at 3 years).3 The Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results database reports a median survival of 14 months for patients with cardiac sarcoma undergoing surgical resection.4 In comparison, the Texas Two-Step cohort demonstrated a median survival of 21.8 months. However, disease-free survival in our series was only 58.3% at 1 year and 29.2% at 2 years, postoperatively. These results further highlight the aggressive nature of PCS and the need to centralize their treatment in centers of excellence, where multidisciplinary cardiac tumor teams are available.2, 3, 4,11 There is an explicit need for better systemic immunotherapy/chemotherapy methods, which would likely increase long-term survival of this devastating systemic disease.1

Study Limitations

Despite being the largest series reported for staged cardiac and lung resection for PCS, this study is limited by its retrospective, single-center design and small sample size. Differences in surgical techniques, such as universal cardiac autotransplantation in the historical cohort versus only 21% in the Texas Two-Step group, perioperative patient management, patient selection, and potential chronological bias may have influenced the outcomes. Given the rarity of this condition and the limited sample size, adjustment for historical bias was not feasible. Therefore, the comparison should be interpreted with caution and is intended to reflect an institutional evolution in surgical strategy rather than a controlled comparative analysis.

Conclusions

Primary cardiac sarcoma with extensive direct lung involvement that requires both extensive cardiac resection as well as pulmonary resection is rare but can be managed with reasonable operative mortality by staging the procedures. This approach resulted in a significant improvement in operative mortality and a reduction in transfusions compared with historical controls who underwent similarly complex resections. We would also like to emphasize the role of a multidisciplinary tumor team in the decision-making for these complex cases.
