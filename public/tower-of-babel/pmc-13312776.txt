Traditional medicine use among adults living with hypertension in Cape Verde: A WHO STEPWISE study
Authors: Joshua Okyere, Castro Ayebeng, Kwamena Sekyi Dickson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLike many African countries, the people of Cape Verde hold strongly to traditional heritage and customs, including the use of traditional medicine in treating ailments. While existing studies in Cape Verde have explored the use of traditional medicine in treating and managing infectious and parasitic diseases, such as malaria, little is known about its use among adults living with hypertension. Given the high prevalence of hypertension in Cape Verde, it is imperative to understand whether those living with the condition use traditional medicine. As such, we assess the factors associated with traditional medicine use among adults living with hypertension in the archipelago nation of Cape Verde.MethodsWe analyzed the 2020 WHO STEPS survey for Cape Verde. With a sample size of 1,008 men and women, we performed descriptive analysis as well as bivariable and multivariable logistic regression in STATA 18. Variables were selected into the multivariable logistic regression through a stepwise approach.ResultsThe results indicate that 35.7% (95%CI: 31.5–40.2) of adults living with hypertension used traditional medicine. Women had more than twice the odds of using traditional medicine compared to men (AOR = 2.08, 95%CI: 1.48–2.92). Individuals aged 50 years and older had higher odds of using traditional medicine than those aged 18–29 years (AOR = 2.20; 95%CI: 1.01–4.81). Furthermore, those who did not consume alcohol had significantly higher odds of using traditional medicine compared to those who did (AOR = 1.48, 95%CI: 1.10–1.99). Also, individuals in a hypertensive crisis stage had over twice the odds of using traditional medicine compared to those in the elevated hypertension stage (AOR = 2.45, 95%CI: 1.25–4.80).ConclusionThis study provides population level evidence that traditional medicine use is common among adults living with hypertension in Cape Verde, with more than one third reporting its use. It is shaped by demographic and behavioral factors including sex, age, alcohol consumption, and hypertensive crisis. These findings highlight the importance of routine clinical engagement and patient centered discussions regarding traditional medicine use among individuals with hypertension to support safe and coordinated disease management.

Background

Cape Verde, also known as the Republic of Cabo Verde, is an archipelago consisting of ten islands [1]. This country has a good health standing, with life expectancy estimated at 73.3 years [2]. However, this high life expectancy has been accompanied by an increase in the prevalence of non-communicable diseases (NCDs), including cardiovascular diseases, cancers, and diabetes, among others [3]. The World Health Organization’s (WHO) country report on Cape Verde indicates that NCDs account for 62% of the burden of disease in the country [3]. Specifically, cardiovascular diseases rank highest in terms of age-standardized mortality rate (249.9 per 100,000 persons) [3]. Given that hypertension is a high-risk factor for cardiovascular diseases [4, 5], it is necessary to understand the dynamics of hypertension and its management in Cape Verde.

The prevalence of hypertension increased from 29% in 2017 [6] to 32% in 2018 [7], and later to 34% in 2019 [1]. Evidence from Cape Verde shows that the steady increase in the prevalence of hypertension is driven by an ageing population, urbanization, and lifestyle changes, including unhealthy dietary patterns and sedentary behavior [1, 3, 6, 7]. Despite this increase in the prevalence of hypertension in Cape Verde, only 68.4% are aware of their status; furthermore, only 51.3% of those who are aware take medications to control their condition [1]. To further exacerbate the situation, only 23.8% of those taking medications for hypertension have controlled blood pressure [1]. This trend and these patterns raise serious concerns about the hypertension medication adherence situation in the country.

Despite the government’s efforts to integrate modern healthcare systems and promote NCD management, many Cape Verdeans continue to rely on traditional medicine as a trusted source for managing diseases. The World Health Organization (WHO) [8] defines traditional medicine as “codified or non-codified systems for healthcare and well-being, comprising practices, skills, knowledge and philosophies originating in different historical and cultural contexts, which are distinct from and pre-date biomedicine, evolving with science for current use from an experience-based origin”. However, in the context of this study, it refers to the use of traditional remedies, which include medicinal plants, herbal concoctions, and spiritual practices, in treating ill health conditions [9, 10].

Studies from other sub-Saharan African countries, such as South Africa [11] and Ethiopia [12], have also demonstrated how persons living with chronic diseases like HIV use traditional medicine. In Cape Verde, existing evidence confirms the continued use of traditional remedies, particularly in the management of malaria and other common ailments [9, 10]. Traditional medicine remains widely accessible, culturally acceptable, and often preferred due to perceived effectiveness, affordability, and limited trust in biomedical services. This reliance presents important challenges for chronic disease control because unregulated traditional therapies may delay formal care seeking, complicate disease monitoring, and contribute to suboptimal treatment adherence in conditions that require continuous biomedical management.

Despite the presence of traditional healing practices in Cape Verde [9, 10], there is limited empirical work on their use among adults with hypertension. The available studies focus primarily on infectious diseases and general health care practices [9], creating an evidence gap regarding chronic non-communicable diseases. It remains unknown how widespread traditional medicine use is among persons living with hypertension, and it is equally unclear whether usage varies across hypertension severity categories, such as elevated blood pressure, stage I, stage II, or hypertensive crisis. As such, understanding the scope and determinants of this practice within this sociocultural context is essential for informing healthcare policies that promote safe and effective hypertension management strategies in the country. Against this background, we based on the WHO STEPwise approach to NCD risk factor surveillance (STEPS), seek to fill this gap by assessing the use of traditional medicine among the adult Cape Verdeans living with hypertension.

Methods

Design and data source

Data from the 2020 WHO STEPS survey on non-communicable disease (NCD) risk factors were collected in Cape Verde between February and March 2020. This nationally representative survey was structured in three sequential phases. In the first phase, socio-demographic and behavioral data were collected. The second phase involved obtaining physical measurements, including height, weight, and blood pressure [13, 14]. The final phase involved biochemical assessments, which entailed collecting blood and urine samples that were analyzed for key markers, including blood glucose, cholesterol levels, and salt intake. The survey, which targeted adults aged 18–69, employed a multi-stage probability sampling design to ensure representativeness across this age group in Cape Verde [13, 14]. A total of 4,563 adults participated in the first two phases, with 2,436 providing samples for biochemical analysis in the third phase. However, in this study, we restricted our analysis to 1,008 men and women living with hypertension who had complete data on the study variables. Normotensive participants, unaware of their hypertension status, or who had missing responses, were excluded from the analytic sample (see Fig. 1).

Measures

Outcome variable

Traditional medicine use was the outcome variable. This variable was derived from the question: “Are you taking traditional medicine for high blood pressure?” This question was restricted to only those who were knowledgeable of their hypertension status. Responding ‘yes’ meant that the participant had used traditional medicine.

Explanatory variables

Informed by previous literature on traditional medicine use [15–18], we selected ten explanatory variables. These included sex (men and women), age (18–69 years), educational level (no formal education, primary, secondary and tertiary), marital status (never married, married, cohabiting, previously married), residence (urban and rural), consumption of alcohol (no and yes), currently employed (no and yes), visited doctor in the last 12 months (no and yes), had a heart attack/stroke (no and yes), and hypertension stage (elevated, stage 1, stage 2, and hypertensive crisis). We classified hypertension stage into the following categories based on systolic and diastolic blood pressure measurements: elevated (120–129/80 mmHg), stage I (130–139/80–89 mmHg), stage II (≥ 140/90 mmHg), and hypertensive crisis (≥ 180/120 mmHg) [19]. For blood pressure, three measurements were taken on the right arm using a sphygmomanometer (OMRON®), with a 3-minute interval between each, with the first measurement taken after the participant had rested for 15 min [13].

Statistical analysis

The data was weighted by applying the appropriate sample weight to account for any sampling biases. We performed descriptive analysis to assess the proportion of adults living with hypertension who used traditional medicine. This involved some cross-tabulations to ascertain the distribution of the proportion across the various participant characteristics. We then performed a bivariable logistic regression analysis to assess the association between the explanatory variable and traditional medicine use. A multivariable logistic regression followed this. A hybrid model-building strategy was employed. Initial variable selection was performed using backward stepwise elimination with a removal threshold of p < 0.05. However, to ensure a robust estimation of effect sizes, key socio-demographic variables (specifically education, health facility visit, and residence) were retained in the final multivariable model as fixed covariates, regardless of their statistical significance. This ensures both statistical parsimony and theoretical validity. Results from the multivariable logistic regression were presented in adjusted odds ratio (AOR) with the corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). The analyses were computed in STATA version 18 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA), considering the complex survey design. Statistical significance was pegged at p < 0.05.

Results

Participants’ characteristics

The majority of the sample were women (56.8%) and resided in urban areas (60.9%) (Table 1). Most participants were aged 50 years and older (51.1%), had a primary or basic level of education (52.3%), and were never married (36.1%). More than half of the participants reported consuming alcohol (68.1%), and nearly three-quarters had visited a doctor in the past 12 months (73.9%). Most participants were currently employed (54.4%) and had stage 2 hypertension (56.9%).

Prevalence of traditional medicine use in the management of hypertension

The results indicate that 35.7% (95%CI: 31.5–40.2) of adults used traditional medicine to manage their hypertension (Table 1). We found a significantly high use of traditional medicine among female adults living with hypertension (45.1%) and older adults (50 years and older) (45.2%). Persons with no formal education (46.8%), married individuals (42.2%), and rural residents (41.8%) had a higher prevalence of using traditional medicine in managing hypertension. Currently employed individuals (38.6%) and those in the hypertensive crisis stage (48.8%) use traditional medicine more than the unemployed and those at the elevated stage. We also found high use of traditional medicine among individuals who did not have a heart attack/stroke (35.8%), those who did not consume alcohol (42.7%), and those who visited the doctor in the 12 months preceding the survey (39.5%).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the proportion of persons living with hypertension who use traditional medicine in the management of their condition, and the associated factors. The study shows that almost four in ten adult Cape Verdeans living with hypertension resorted to the use of traditional medicine. The estimated prevalence of traditional medicine use is comparatively higher than what has been estimated among persons living with hypertension in sub-Saharan Africa (24%) [15]. It is also higher in comparison to a study conducted in Tanzania that found traditional medicine to be common among 24.4% of persons living with hypertension [16]. However, the observed prevalence is lower when compared to traditional medicine utilization in non-hypertensive chronic health conditions like HIV (43.7%) [20]. Unlike Lassale et al.’ study [15] that was facility-based study from eight countries, our study is a population-based study. This may explain the high prevalence of traditional medicine use in our study in comparison to Lassale et al. [15]. Nonetheless, the moderately high prevalence suggests that traditional medicine may be perceived as an accessible, affordable, culturally accepted, and possibly effective option for adult Cape Verdeans living with hypertension. This underscores a need for the Ministry of Health and health departments to consider the integration of traditional medicine/services in mainstream healthcare service provision to persons living with hypertension. The country can take a cue from Ghana, which has integrated traditional medicine into its mainstream healthcare system [21]. Such actions will be needed to ensure that persons living with hypertension who rely on traditional medicine receive coordinated, safe, and effective care, bridging the gap between traditional treatment approaches and improving health outcomes.

Consistent with Lassale et al.’s study [15], we found a statistically significant association between sex and traditional medicine use among persons living with hypertension. Women had twice the odds of using traditional medicine in managing their condition as men. This finding is inconsistent with a study conducted in Ghana that found males to be more likely to use traditional medicine in hypertension management [22]. It, however, aligns with a study among Malaysian adults that found an 80% increased likelihood of traditional medicine use among females [23]. Culturally, women often play a central role in health-seeking behaviors within families, thus, leading them to explore various healthcare options, including traditional remedies. Women may also be more open to discussing their health challenges and sharing knowledge about alternative treatments, hence, fostering a greater awareness and use of traditional medicine.

Our study also indicates that the odds of persons living with hypertension using traditional medicine in the management of their condition increases with age. Thus, older adults are more likely to use traditional medicine. Similar findings have been reported in Ethiopia [24], where adults older than 45 years have a 62% higher likelihood of using traditional medicine. The findings also corroborate Ali-Shtayeh et al. [25], who found that adults older than 50 years have higher odds of using traditional medicine in hypertension management. Older adults may often be more connected to cultural traditions and practices, including the use of traditional medicine, which may have been a primary healthcare option in their earlier years. These individuals may have developed a long-standing trust in traditional remedies, believing in their efficacy based on personal or community experiences. We further postulate that the perception that traditional medicine offers a more holistic or natural approach to managing chronic conditions like hypertension may also appeal to older individuals who are wary of the side effects of modern medications.

An interesting finding from this study is the association between hypertension stage and traditional medicine use, where those in the hypertensive crisis stage had twice the odds of using traditional medicine as their counterparts in the early stages of hypertension. This association may be explained from the perspective that individuals in more advanced stages of hypertension may experience severe symptoms [26, 27]. Consequently, they may feel frustrated and perceive conventional treatments as inadequate or slow to provide relief [24]. This situation pushes them to try all available avenues for relief, including traditional remedies, in an attempt to manage their condition more effectively.

Our findings suggest that individuals who did not consume alcohol have higher odds of using traditional medicine in hypertension management. It is unclear why this association exists as there were no existing studies to compare with. As such, further research is needed to understand this association. Notwithstanding, we argue that the higher odds of traditional medicine use among non-alcohol consumers may relate to behavioral identity and lifestyle orientation. Abstaining from alcohol is often embedded within broader lifestyle choices that reflect moral, ethical, or health related values, including the avoidance of substances perceived as harmful and the preference for practices considered natural or holistic [28]. Within this context, the use of traditional medicine may reinforce a behavioral identity centered on conscious living and wellness-oriented choices. Positive attitudes toward traditional or herbal remedies may therefore function as identity markers that signal commitment to culturally rooted or natural approaches to health management [28].

Implications for policy and practice

From our study, it is indicative that among the population of adults living with hypertension, almost four in ten use traditional medicine. This means that Cape Verde, as a country, and its Ministry of Health cannot ignore the fact that traditional medicine is used by this key population. The priority for policymakers should therefore be to promote safe and coordinated management of hypertension within a pluralistic healthcare environment where both biomedical and traditional practices coexist. The Ministry of Health could strengthen initiatives that encourage individuals living with hypertension to maintain regular clinical monitoring while ensuring that any use of traditional medicine does not interfere with prescribed treatment. Integrating patient education within primary healthcare services could also help clinicians engage constructively with patients about their use of traditional remedies, thereby supporting safer and more informed treatment decisions. The finding that individuals in a hypertensive crisis stage are more likely to use traditional medicine underscores the need for targeted educational campaigns to raise awareness about the risks of substituting or delaying conventional treatment at critical stages of the disease. Future studies should consider testing a bidirectional causal relationship between hypertension stage and traditional medicine use. Also, the government of Cape Verde must invest in clinical research to investigate the safety and efficacy of traditional medicine in hypertension management.

Strengths and limitations

The study is the first in Cape Verde to explore traditional medicine use in hypertension management. As such, it contributes significantly to understanding the hypertension management dynamics in the islands of Cape Verde. Additionally, the sampling technique and weighting of the WHO STEPSWISE for NCD guarantees that the findings are generalizable to the adult population of the country. Nonetheless, certain limitations must be acknowledged. The absence of certain critical confounders in the dataset (e.g., availability of social support, distance to healthcare facility, cost of anti-hypertensive medications, etc.) is a limitation. We are unable to establish causality as the data used is based on a cross-sectional design. The secondary data used does not also provide a qualitative response as to why persons living with hypertension used traditional medicine. The variable for traditional medicine use was self-reported; there is the possibility of social desirability bias and risk of under-reporting. Also, the analysis did not capture those using traditional medicine alongside conventional anti-hypertensive medications. There is also the possibility of reverse association between the stages of hypertension and traditional medicine use. However, that was not the assessed in this study.

Conclusion

This study provides population level evidence that traditional medicine use is common among adults living with hypertension in Cape Verde, with more than one third reporting its use. It is shaped by demographic and behavioral factors including sex, age, alcohol consumption, and hypertensive crisis. These findings highlight the importance of routine clinical engagement and patient centered discussions regarding traditional medicine use among individuals with hypertension to support safe and coordinated disease management. Given that traditional practices remain an active component of health-seeking behavior, future work should prioritize rigorous evaluation of the safety and effectiveness of commonly used traditional remedies before any consideration of formal acceptance within hypertension management.
