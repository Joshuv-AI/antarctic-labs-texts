Multivariate Distribution-Free Nonparametric Testing: Generalizing Wilcoxon’s Tests via Optimal Transport
Zhen Huanglabel=e1]zh2395@columbia.edu, Bodhisattva Senlabel=e2]bodhi@stat.columbia.edu

Abstract
This paper reviews recent advancements in the application of optimal transport (OT) to multivariate distribution-free nonparametric testing . Inspired by classical rank-based methods, such as Wilcoxon’s rank-sum and signed-rank tests, we explore how OT-based ranks and signs generalize these concepts to multivariate settings, while preserving key properties, including distribution-freeness, robustness, and efficiency. Using the framework of asymptotic relative efficiency (ARE), we compare the power of the proposed (generalized Wilcoxon) tests against the Hotelling’s T 2 T^{2} test. The ARE lower bounds reveal the Hodges-Lehmann [ 47 ] and Chernoff-Savage [ 13 ] phenomena in the context of multivariate location testing, underscoring the high power and efficiency of the proposed methods. We also demonstrate how OT-based ranks and signs can be seamlessly integrated with more modern techniques, such as kernel methods, to develop universally consistent, distribution-free tests. Additionally, we present novel results on the construction of consistent and distribution-free kernel-based tests for multivariate symmetry, leveraging OT-based ranks and signs.

Introduction

Nonparametric testing is a class of statistical methods that do not rely on strong parametric assumptions. Frank Wilcoxon’s 1945 paper
[
94
]
was a significant breakthrough in the history of (classical) nonparametric statistics as it introduced two widely used nonparametric tests: the Wilcoxon’s
rank-sum test
and the Wilcoxon’s
signed-rank test
. These two tests utilize the ranks of the (univariate) data to test whether two samples come from the same distribution, and whether one sample has center 0, respectively. Moreover, both these rank-based tests are
distribution-free
, i.e., the distribution of the test statistics, under the null hypothesis, does not depend on the data generating distribution — a consequence of the fact that for exchangeable data, the vector of ranks follows the uniform distribution over all
n!
permutations of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

Apart from their simplicity, robustness and distribution-freeness, a striking feature of these tests that makes them a strong alternative to the usual two-sample
t
-test and paired
t
-test are the efficiency results of Hodges-Lehmann
[
47
]
and Chernoff-Savage
[
13
]
. These results state that Wilcoxon’s tests (with Gaussian score) are never worse than the
t
-tests in the sense of
asymptotic relative efficiency
(ARE), against location shift alternatives
[

Since Wilcoxon’s seminal work

Since these tests rely on ranks, a more fundamental question that arises is: “How can one define multivariate ranks that lead to distribution-free testing procedures?”. Existing extensions of multivariate ranks — such as component-wise ranks

Introduction to Optimal Transport

Optimal transport
(OT), or “measure transportation”, is the problem of finding a “nice” function
{F}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
that transports a given measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
to another measure
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. More specifically, we say
{F}
pushes
\mu
to
\nu
, written as
{F}\#\mu=\nu
, if for
\mathbf{X}\sim\mu
, we have
{F}(\mathbf{X})\sim\nu
. In the 1781 pioneering work of Gaspard Monge
[
67
]
, the following problem (Monge’s problem) was introduced:

\inf_{F}\int c(\mathbf{x},{F}(\mathbf{x})){\rm d}\mu(\mathbf{x}),\quad{\rm subject\ to\ }\;\;{F}\#\mu=\nu.

(1)

Here
c(\cdot,\cdot)
is a cost function. Monge initially worked with the cost function
c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|
, for
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. A useful and popular cost function is the squared loss
c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|^{2}
.
The minimizer of (
1
), if it exists, is referred to as an
optimal transport map
.

The main challenge in Monge’s problem is that a transport map may not always exist, and the optimization problem is inherently non-convex, making it difficult to solve. To address these limitations, Leonid Kantorovich
[
55
]
introduced a relaxation of (
1
) in the 1940s. Let
\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
denote the set of all joint (Borel) distributions
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})
with marginals
\mathbf{X}\sim\mu
and
\mathbf{Y}\sim\nu
. The Kantorovich problem then seeks to solve

\inf_{\pi\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\int c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}){\rm d}\pi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}).

(2)

A key advantage of Kantorovich’s formulation is that the optimization problem (
2
) is convex — and, in fact, linear — ensuring the existence of a solution under mild conditions, even in cases where no deterministic transport map satisfies Monge’s original formulation.

A fundamental result in this field is Brenier’s theorem
[
11
]
, which states that if
\mu
is a Lebesgue absolutely continuous distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, and both
\mu
and
\nu
have finite second moments, and the cost function is given by the squared Euclidean distance
c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|^{2}
, then the OT problem (
1
) admits a

\mu
-a.e. unique solution. Moreover, this solution is given by the gradient of a convex function. Additionally, under these conditions, Kantorovich’s relaxation (
2
) is tight, meaning that it has the same optimal value as Monge’s problem. Further, the solution to Kantorovich’s problem is given by the optimal coupling
\pi=(\mathrm{Id},F)\#\mu
,
1
1

1

In this notation,
(\mathrm{Id},F)\#\mu
represents the pushforward measure of
\mu
under the mapping
\mathbf{x}\mapsto(\mathbf{x},F(\mathbf{x}))
.
where
F
is the
\mu
-a.e. unique OT map solving (
1
).

A significant extension of Brenier’s result was later provided by McCann
[
64
]
, who adopted a geometric approach and eliminated all moment assumptions. Specifically, if
\mu
is absolutely continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, there exists a
\mu
-a.e. unique transport map
F
, expressed as the gradient of a convex function, such that
F\#\mu=\nu
. Moreover, if
\mu
and
\nu
have finite second moments and
c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|^{2}
(so that problems (
1
) and (
2
) are well-defined), then
F
also serves as the OT map in Monge’s problem (
1
). For a comprehensive treatment of OT, see
[
92
,
91
]
.

Multivariate Ranks via OT

There are various motivations for the definition of OT-based ranks in the literature
[
14

33

18
]
. Here we present a simple and natural motivation as given by
[
18

Denote by
\mathcal{P}_{\rm ac}(\mathbb{R}^{p})
the class of all Borel probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
with a Lebesgue density.
Suppose that we are given a collection of
n
(distinct) i.i.d. random variables
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}

\mathbb{R}
having a distribution
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{\rm ac}(\mathbb{R}^{p})

Let
\mathcal{S}_{n}
be the set of all permutations of
\{1,\ldots,n\}

\hat{\sigma}:=\argmin_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big|X_{\sigma(i)}-\frac{i}{n}\Big|^{2},\;\;{\rm and}\hskip 9.24994pt{\rm rank}(X_{i})=\hat{\sigma}^{-1}(i).

In order to minimize the above quantity,
\sigma
has to be a permutation that ranks
\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}
from the smallest to the largest.

Using the language of OT, the rank map
\hat{R}_{n}
assigns
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
to elements of the set
\{\frac{1}{n},\frac{2}{n},...,\frac{n}{n}\}
by solving the OT problem

\hat{R}_{n}:=\argmin_{T:T\#\mu_{n}=\nu_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|X_{i}-T(X_{i})|^{2},

(3)

where
\mu_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}
is the empirical distribution of the observed data (here
\delta_{a}
denotes the Dirac delta measure at
a

\nu_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i/n}
is the empirical distribution of
\{\frac{1}{n},\frac{2}{n},\ldots,\frac{n}{n}\}
— a natural discretization of Unif
([0,1])
, the uniform distribution on
[0,1]

The OT problem (
3

Suppose
\mathbf{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{n}

3.1
Multivariate Wilcoxon’s Rank-Sum Test

In this subsection, we discuss the
multivariate Wilcoxon’s rank-sum test
introduced in
[
16
]
. Suppose we have two independent samples
\{\mathbf{X}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\{\mathbf{Y}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
, drawn from distributions
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
, respectively. We are interested in testing the hypothesis:

{\rm H}_{0}:\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}\qquad\mbox{against}\qquad{\rm H}_{1}:\mu_{1}\neq\mu_{2}.

(5)

Similar to its classical one-dimensional counterpart, the multivariate Wilcoxon’s rank-sum statistic first computes the sample rank map
\hat{R}_{m,n}
on the pooled sample, i.e.,
\hat{R}_{m,n}:\{\mathbf{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{m},\mathbf{Y}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{Y}_{n}\}\to\{\mathbf{h}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{h}_{m+n}\}
. The test statistic, as proposed in
[
16
]
, is given by:

T_{m,n}^{\nu}:=\frac{mn}{m+n}\Big\|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\hat{R}_{m,n}(\mathbf{X}_{i})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{R}_{m,n}(\mathbf{Y}_{i})\Big\|^{2}.

A more general version of the test statistic incorporates a score function
J(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
, which is injective and Lebesgue a.e. continuous, yielding the statistics
[
16
]
:

T_{m,n}^{\nu,J}:=\frac{mn}{m+n}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{m,n}^{\nu,J})^{\top}\Sigma_{\rm ERD}^{-1}\mathbf{\Delta}_{m,n}^{\nu,J},

where

\mathbf{\Delta}_{m,n}^{\nu,J}:=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}J\left(\hat{R}_{m,n}(\mathbf{X}_{i})\right)-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}J\left(\hat{R}_{m,n}(\mathbf{Y}_{i})\right).

Here,
\Sigma_{\rm ERD}
denotes the covariance matrix of the push-forward distribution
J\#\nu
. This distribution, referred to as the effective reference distribution (ERD) in
[
16
]
, can be interpreted as the distribution of the score-transformed ranks under the null hypothesis.

Both
T_{m,n}^{\nu}
and
T_{m,n}^{\nu,J}
are functions of the OT-based ranks, so their distributions under the null (i.e., when
\mathbf{X}_{i}
’s and
\mathbf{Y}_{j}
’s come from the same distribution) are known. This yields finite sample distribution-free critical values for testing (
5
).
For large sample sizes, a normal approximation can be applied. In particular,
T_{m,n}^{\nu,J}
converges weakly to a chi-squared distribution with
p
degrees of freedom, as established in
[
16
, Theorem 3.1]
.

While distribution-freeness is a highly desirable property, it alone does not guarantee an effective testing procedure. A test must also exhibit good power properties to be practically useful.
A key reason the classical Wilcoxon rank-sum test is a powerful alternative to the two-sample

t
-test lies in the seminal results of Hodges-Lehmann
[
47
]
and Chernoff-Savage
[
13
]
. These results state that, against location shift alternatives: the ARE of Wilcoxon’s rank-sum test with respect to the
two-sample
t
-test is at least 0.864
[
47
]
, and the ARE of the Gaussian score-transformed Wilcoxon test relative to the
t
-test is at least 1
[
13
]
.
This is particularly remarkable because, in the one-dimensional case, the two-sample

t
-test is the uniformly most powerful unbiased (UMPU) test when the data are Gaussian
[
59
]
. Interestingly, the multivariate Wilcoxon rank-sum test introduced above exhibits a similar efficiency property when compared to Hotelling’s

T^{2}
test
[
49
]
, which is the multivariate analogue of the

t
-test.

The Hotelling’s
T^{2}
test, though classical and simple, remains one of the most popular and useful multivariate two-sample tests today. Its test statistic is given by

T_{m,n}:=\frac{mn}{m+n}(\bar{\textbf{X}}-\bar{\textbf{Y}})^{\top}S_{m,n}^{-1}(\bar{\textbf{X}}-\bar{\textbf{Y}}),

where
\bar{\mathbf{X}}:=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{X}_{i}
and
\bar{\mathbf{Y}}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{Y}_{i}
are the sample means, and
S_{m,n}
is the sample covariance matrix of the pooled data.

The
asymptotic relative efficiency
(ARE) of a level-
\alpha
test
T_{1}
relative to another level-
\alpha
test
T_{2}
is, roughly speaking, the limiting ratio of the sample sizes required for
T_{2}
to achieve a given power
\beta\in(\alpha,1)
compared to
T_{1}
, where the limit is taken along a sequence of alternatives converging to the null.

For instance, if the ARE of
T_{1}
with respect to
T_{2}
is 0.9, this intuitively means that
T_{2}
requires 10% fewer observations than
T_{1}
to attain the same power
\beta
. A formal definition of ARE can be found at
[
16
, Definition C.2]
(also see
[
90
, Chapter 14]
). Although in general the ARE can depend on the choice of
\alpha
and
\beta
, in our applications it does not.

Although both Hotelling’s
T^{2}
test and its distribution-free analogue described above can be used to test the general two-sample hypothesis (
5

3.2
Two-Sample Tests Based on Rank Kernel MMD

While the multivariate Wilcoxon’s rank-sum test (from Section

T^{2}
test as seen in the previous section), it is however not consistent against all (fixed) alternatives. When the difference between the two samples does not come from a location shift, the multivariate Wilcoxon’s rank-sum test could be powerless.
One approach to gain (universal) consistency while still being distribution-free is to combine the OT-based ranks with kernel method, which will be described below.

A kernel
K(\cdot,\cdot)
is a
positive definite
function, meaning that for any

.

K(\cdot,\cdot)
is called a
characteristic
kernel, when the following holds:
\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}
if and only if

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}\sim\mu_{1}}K(\mathbf{X},\cdot)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}\sim\mu_{2}}K(\mathbf{X},\cdot)
. Examples of characteristic kernels include
Gaussian kernel
K(u,v):=\exp(-\sigma\|u-v\|^{2})

(here
\|\cdot\|_{1}

L_{1}
-norm); and

K(u,v):={2}^{-1}(\lVert u\rVert^{\alpha}+\lVert v\rVert^{\alpha}-\lVert u-v\rVert^{\alpha})
,
for
\alpha\in(0,2)

[
73
]
. See
[
84
,
25
,
85
,
83
,
86
]
for sufficient conditions that imply that

is characteristic. Given a characteristic kernel

, the maximum mean discrepancy (MMD)
[

]
is a distance on the space of probability distributions defined as

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle MMD}^{2}(P,Q):=\mathbb{E}K(\Delta_{1}^{P},\Delta_{2}^{P})

(6)

\displaystyle+\,\mathbb{E}K(\Delta_{1}^{Q},\Delta_{2}^{Q})-2\mathbb{E}K(\Delta_{1}^{P},\Delta_{1}^{Q}),

where
\Delta_{1}^{P},\Delta_{2}^{P}\sim P
,
\Delta_{1}^{Q},\Delta_{2}^{Q}\sim Q
are mutually independent.

When the two samples

and

follow the same distribution, their score-transformed ranks

and

as the test statistic:

\gamma_{m,n}^{\nu,J}:=\frac{mn}{m+n}\left[w_{m,n}^{(1)}+w_{m,n}^{(2)}-b_{m,n}\right],

where

\displaystyle w_{m,n}^{(1)}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{m(m-1)}\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq m}K\left(J(\hat{R}_{m,n}(X_{i})),J(\hat{R}_{m,n}(X_{j}))\right),

\displaystyle w_{m,n}^{(2)}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq n}K\left(J(\hat{R}_{m,n}(Y_{i})),J(\hat{R}_{m,n}(Y_{j}))\right),

\displaystyle b_{m,n}

\displaystyle:=\frac{2}{mn}\sum_{1\leq i\leq m}\sum_{1\leq j\leq n}K\left(J(\hat{R}_{m,n}(X_{i})),J(\hat{R}_{m,n}(Y_{j}))\right).

As a function of the OT ranks,

).
Apart from distribution-freeness, it also retains the universal consistency property of the classical MMD two-sample test:

Theorem 3.2

(Universal consistency; Theorem 4.1 of
[
16
]
)

Let the score function

be injective. Under mild assumptions on the reference distribution and the kernel, and
\frac{m}{m+n}\to\lambda\in(0,1)

for large values of

Under

[

[

also shows that the test based on

Remark 3.1

(A framework for constructing distribution-free tests)
.

OT-based ranks provide a general mechanism for transforming any test statistic into a distribution-free version by replacing the original observations with their pooled OT-based ranks. This approach, analogous to how we obtained the (generalized) Wilcoxon rank-sum test in Section
3.1
and the rank kernel MMD test in Section

[

Moreover, this principle extends beyond two-sample testing; see Section

In the context of independence testing, marginal OT-based ranks are used to construct distribution-free test statistics, following the same principle that underlies the derivation of Spearman’s rank correlation
[

from Pearson’s correlation coefficient.

4.1
Multivariate Wilcoxon’s Signed-Rank Test

With the notions of OT-based signs and absolute ranks defined above, the multivariate Wilcoxon’s signed-rank test statistic can be defined as
\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i})R_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i})

4.2
Universally Consistent Tests for Multivariate Symmetry

While the multivariate Wilcoxon’s signed-rank test is highly powerful in detecting deviations in location (
12

[
51
, Theorem 3.2]
, it is however not necessarily consistent or powerful against the general alternative of
{\rm H}_{\mathcal{G},0}
in (
8
).
Note that the multivariate Wilcoxon’s signed-rank test rejects the null when the sum of signed-ranks is far from the origin, therefore it requires the signed-ranks to have mean
\mathbf{0}
under the null to be powerful, formulated by the assumption
-\mathbf{I}_{p}\in\mathcal{G}
in its consistency theorem
[
51
, Corollary 3.1]
.
This excludes symmetries such as exchangeability, reflection, and zonal symmetry:

1.

Exchangeability

[
4
,
48
,
81
,
65
,
69
,
21
,
54
]
. Here,
\mathcal{G}
consists of all permutation matrices of order

. Exchangeability is an important concept in various statistical models
[
93
,
69
]
and can be related to treatment-control pairs, as bivariate exchangeability holds under the null hypothesis of no treatment effect
[
74
]
.

2.

Reflection

[
50
]
. The Householder matrix
P:=\mathbf{I}_{p}-2\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}^{\top}
describes a reflection about the hyperplane orthogonal to the unit vector
\mathbf{u}
. The corresponding group is
\mathcal{G}=\{P,\mathbf{I}_{p}\}
.
A closely related notion of symmetry is
axial
symmetry
[
52
]
, where
\mathcal{G}=\{-P,\mathbf{I}_{p}\}
.

3.

Zonal
symmetry
[
5
, Example 4]
. This notion of symmetry arises in certain geophysical problems. Let

denote the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{3}

\mathcal{G}
consists of all rotations in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
that leave the north pole-south pole axis of

fixed. Thus, each element of
\mathcal{G}
corresponds to a rotation in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

In this subsection, we present novel results that combine kernel MMD and OT-based signed-ranks to form new tests for
\mathcal{G}
-symmetry

{\rm H}_{\mathcal{G},0}:{\bf X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}Q{\bf X}\;\;\forall\;Q\in\mathcal{G}\ \quad\text{vs.}\quad\ {\rm H}_{\mathcal{G},1}:\text{not }{\rm H}_{\mathcal{G},0}

(14)

that are (i) distribution-free, (ii) universally consistent, and (iii) adapt to a broader class of
\mathcal{G}
-symmetries.

The proposed procedure is motivated by a key observation on the signed-ranks defined via OT:
\mathbf{X}
is
\mathcal{G}
-symmetric if and only if the population signed-rank of
\mathbf{X}
follows the known distribution
\nu_{S}
(the symmetrized reference distribution, defined in Theorem

).

Theorem 4.3
.

Suppose Assumption
4.1
holds,
\mathbf{X}\sim\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{\rm ac}(\mathbb{R}^{p})
and
\nu_{S}
has a Lebesgue density.
Then the following statements are equivalent:

1.

\mathbf{X}
is
\mathcal{G}
-symmetric.

2.

\nabla\psi(\mathbf{X})
(
\nabla\psi(\cdot)
is defined in Theorem
4.1
) is
\mathcal{G}
-symmetric.

3.

\nabla\psi(\mathbf{X})\sim\nu_{S}
.

A proof of Theorem
4.3
is given in Appendix
A.1
. With the above fact, we can use the difference (as measured by MMD
[
29
]
) between the empirical distribution of the sample signed-ranks and this known distribution
\nu_{S}
as the test statistic to test (
14
).

Recall the definition of MMD in (
6
). If the kernel
K
is characteristic, MMD is a distance on the space of probability measures
[
29
]
. In particular,
{\rm MMD}(P,Q)\geq 0
, and
{\rm MMD}(P,Q)=0
if and only if
P=Q
.

From Theorem
4.3
,
\mathbf{X}
is
\mathcal{G}
-symmetric if and only if
{\rm MMD}(\nabla\psi(\mathbf{X}),\nu_{S})=0
.
The empirical distribution of sample signed-ranks — denoted by
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\hat{U}_{i}}
where
\hat{U}_{i}:=S_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i})R_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i})
— provides an approximation for the distribution of the population signed-rank

.
Therefore, we propose to use

\displaystyle T_{n}:={\rm MMD}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\hat{U}_{i}},\nu_{S}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}K(\hat{U}_{i},\hat{U}_{j})+\mathbb{E}\left[K(U,U^{\prime})\right]-\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{U}\left[K(\hat{U}_{i},U)\right],

as our test statistic for the hypothesis test (
14
). Here
U
,
U^{\prime}
are i.i.d. from
\nu_{S}
.

While there could be various choices of the characteristic kernel

, here we recommend the use of the Gaussian kernel

K(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}):=\exp(-\sigma\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2})
, as in such a case,
\mathbb{E}_{U}\left[K(\hat{U}_{i},U)\right]
and
\mathbb{E}\left[K(U,U^{\prime})\right]
have closed form expressions:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U}\left[K(\hat{U}_{i},U)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\sigma+1)^{p/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\sigma}{2\sigma+1}\hat{U}_{i}^{\top}\hat{U}_{i}\right),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[K(U,U^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(4\sigma+1)^{p/2}}.

Remark 4.1

(Distribution-Freeness)
.

The test statistic
T_{n}
is a function of the OT-based signed-ranks, and consequently has a known distribution under
{\rm H}_{\mathcal{G},0}
(see (
14
)). Observe that the signed-ranks
(\hat{U}_{1},\ldots,\hat{U}_{n})
have the same distribution as
(S_{1}R_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{1}),\ldots,S_{n}R_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{n}))
, where the absolute ranks
(R_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{1}),\ldots,R_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{n}))
are uniformly distributed over the
n!
permutations of
(\mathbf{h}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{h}_{n})
, and is independent of the signs
(S_{1},\ldots,S_{n})
, with
S_{1},\ldots,S_{n}
i.i.d. uniform over
\mathcal{G}
. Note that this null distribution is distribution-free, i.e., it does not depend on the distribution of
\mathbf{X}
, and consequently, distribution-free critical values can be obtained for testing the null hypothesis (
14
).

The following theorem (proved in Appendix
A.2
) establishes the consistency of our test statistic.

Theorem 4.4

(Consistency)
.

Suppose Assumption
4.1
holds, and
\mathbf{X}\sim\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{\rm ac}(\mathbb{R}^{p})
. Let
\nabla\psi(\cdot)
be defined as in Theorem
4.1
. If the kernel
K(\cdot,\cdot)
is bounded and continuous, then, as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle T_{n}\overset{a.s.}{\longrightarrow}{\rm MMD}^{2}(\nabla\psi(\mathbf{X}),\nu_{S}).

From the consistency theorem above,
T_{n}=o_{p}(1)
under
{\rm H}_{\mathcal{G},0}
, and
T_{n}={\rm MMD}(\nabla\psi(\mathbf{X}),\nu_{S})+o_{p}(1)
under
{\rm H}_{\mathcal{G},1}
. If the kernel
K(\cdot,\cdot)
is characteristic,
{\rm MMD}(\nabla\psi(\mathbf{X}),\nu_{S})>0
under
{\rm H}_{\mathcal{G},1}
. Therefore, the test that rejects
{\rm H}_{\mathcal{G},0}
for large values of
T_{n}
is consistent against all fixed alternatives where
\mathbf{X}
is not
\mathcal{G}
-symmetric.

Remark 4.2

(Broader class of
\mathcal{G}
)
.

Theorem
4.4
does not require
-\mathbf{I}_{p}\in\mathcal{G}
which was assumed in the consistency theorem of multivariate Wilcoxon’s signed-rank test
[
51
, Corollary 3.1]
.
Therefore, beyond central, sign, and spherical symmetries, it is also applicable to other notions of symmetry such as exchangeability, reflection, and zonal symmetry.

To the best of our knowledge, our proposed test for symmetry is the first test that simultaneously satisfies the three aforementioned desirable properties (i.e., distribution-freeness, universal consistency, and applicability to general symmetry).
Numerical experiments for the new method are presented in Appendix
B
, which shows that the new test is among the best-performing methods against various types of alternatives.

Observe that our test statistic
T_{n}
is not a standard
V
-statistic because the sample signed-ranks are neither mutually independent nor marginally distributed as the population signed-rank. Thus, unlike classical results in the MMD literature,
T_{n}
may not exhibit an asymptotic distribution as an infinite mixture of chi-squares. To better characterize its asymptotic behavior, we demonstrate that, after appropriate normalization,
T_{n}
satisfies a central limit theorem in the reproducing kernel Hilbert space.

4.3
Limiting Behavior of
T_{n}
under
\mathcal{G}
-symmetry

In this subsection, we provide a characterization of the asymptotic behavior of the proposed test statistic
T_{n}
under the null hypothesis of
\mathcal{G}
-symmetry. In particular, we show that the test statistic
T_{n}
, after appropriate centering, satisfies a central limit theorem.

Assume that
\mathbf{X}
is
\mathcal{G}
-symmetric, from which it follows that
{\rm MMD}^{2}(\nabla\psi(\mathbf{X}),\nu_{S})=0
. A natural question to ask is: “Will
nT_{n}
converge in distribution to an infinite mixture of chi-squares (which is the limiting null distribution of the classical MMD for i.i.d. data
[
29
]
)?”

The answer, however, is negative. Note that
T_{n}
is not a standard
V
-statistic like the empirical MMD
[
30
]
. Firstly, the sample signed-ranks
\hat{U}_{1},\ldots,\hat{U}_{n}
, which are the products of the signs and absolute ranks, are not independent (as the absolute ranks are a permuted version of
\mathbf{h}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{h}_{n}
; see Remark
4.1
). Secondly, the marginal distribution of
\hat{U}_{i}
is not
\nu_{S}
. In fact, under the null hypothesis (
14
),
\hat{U}_{i}
marginally follows
\nu_{n,S}
, where
\nu_{n,S}
denotes the distribution of
S\mathbf{H}_{n}
with
S\sim{\rm Uniform}(\mathcal{G})
independent of
\mathbf{H}_{n}\sim\nu_{n}
. The limiting behavior of
nT_{n}={\rm MMD}^{2}\big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\hat{U}_{i}},\nu_{S}\big)
may depend on how
\nu_{n,S}
converges to
\nu_{S}
. Even if
\nu_{n,S}
approaches
\nu_{S}
at
n^{-1/2}
-rate (e.g.,
\mathbf{h}_{1},\mathbf{h}_{2},\ldots
are drawn i.i.d. from
\nu
),
nT_{n}
may still diverge (see Remark
4.3
below). It turns out that in order to have a limiting distribution being an infinite mixture of chi-squares, the suitable centering required for
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\hat{U}_{i}}
is
\nu_{n,S}
instead of
\nu_{S}
. When centered around
\nu_{n,S}
,
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\hat{U}_{i}}

follows a central limit theorem, as shown below.

Theorem 4.5
.

Assume that the kernel
K(\cdot,\cdot)
is bounded and continuous, and
\nu_{n}
converges weakly to
\nu
. Let
\mathcal{H}
denote the RKHS associated with the kernel
K(\cdot,\cdot)
. Under the null hypothesis of
\mathcal{G}
-symmetry, we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K(\hat{U}_{i},\cdot)-\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{n,S}}\left[K(U,\cdot)\right]\Big)\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}\langle\phi_{i},\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}Z_{i},

and consequently,

\displaystyle n{\rm MMD}^{2}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\hat{U}_{i}},\nu_{n,S}\Big)

\displaystyle\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\lambda_{i}}Z_{i}^{2},\hskip 9.24994pt{\rm as\ }n\to\infty.

Here
Z_{i}
’s are i.i.d. standard normal variables, and
\lambda_{i}\geq 0
, orthonormal
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}\subset\mathcal{H}
(
i\geq 1
) only depend on the kernel
K(\cdot,\cdot)
,
\mathcal{G}
, and
\nu
.

See Appendix
A.3
for the proof of Theorem
4.5
. In the above result, the
\lambda_{i}
’s and the
\phi_{i}
’s are the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of a trace-class operator over
\mathcal{H}
; see (
17
) and (
18
) in Appendix
A.3
for the exact expression of the operator.
The proof leverages recent theoretical tools from
[
23
]
, which work directly with random functionals on the reproducing kernel Hilbert space to analyze kernel-based tests.

Remark 4.3

(Implication for the null distribution of
T_{n}
)
.

Observe that

\displaystyle n{T}_{n}

\displaystyle=n{\rm MMD}^{2}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\hat{U}_{i}},\nu_{S}\Big)

\displaystyle=n\Bigg\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K(\hat{U}_{i},\cdot)-\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\nu_{S}}K(U,\cdot)\Bigg\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\Bigg\|\underbrace{\sqrt{n}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K(\hat{U}_{i},\cdot)-\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\nu_{n,S}}K(U,\cdot)\Big)}_{{\rm converge\ in\ distribution\ by\ Theorem}~\ref{thm:recentered_CLT}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\vphantom{\sqrt{n}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K(\hat{U}_{i},\cdot)-\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\nu_{n,S}}K(U,\cdot)\right)}\sqrt{n}\left(\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\nu_{n,S}}K(U,\cdot)-\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\nu_{S}}K(U,\cdot)\right)}_{\text{\rm non-random}}\Bigg\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2},

where the first term above converges in distribution by Theorem
4.5
, and the second term is non-random. If
\sqrt{n}\left(\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\nu_{n,S}}K(U,\cdot)-\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\nu_{S}}K(U,\cdot)\right)
converges to
0\in\mathcal{H}
as
n\to\infty
, then
nT_{n}
will have the same limiting distribution
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\lambda_{i}}Z_{i}^{2}
as in Theorem
4.5
. However, this requirement on the reference distribution may be too strong and hard to obtain. For example, if
\mathbf{h}_{1},\mathbf{h}_{2},\ldots
are i.i.d. from
\nu
(and fixed), then by the law of the iterated logarithm
[
57
, Theorem 4.1]
,
\sqrt{n}\big(\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\nu_{n,S}}K(U,\cdot)-\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\nu_{S}}K(U,\cdot)\big)
will be of order
\sqrt{\log\log n}
, and will not converge to 0 or any constant vector.

It may seem appealing from Theorem
4.5
to use the statistic
n{\rm MMD}^{2}\big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\hat{U}_{i}},\nu_{n,S}\big)
for testing the hypothesis of
\mathcal{G}
-symmetry, as this statistic is not only distribution-free in finite samples, but also has a known asymptotic distribution (the infinite mixture of chi-squares).
However, computing
{\rm MMD}^{2}\big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\hat{U}_{i}},\nu_{n,S}\big)
is typically more difficult than
T_{n}={\rm MMD}^{2}\big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\hat{U}_{i}},\nu_{S}\big)
, as
\mathbb{E}_{U,U^{\prime}\sim\nu_{n,S}}\big[K(U,U^{\prime})\big]
and
\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\nu_{n,S}}\big[K(\hat{U}_{i},U)\big]
may not have a simple closed form. The computational complexity to evaluate them can be high, especially when
|\mathcal{G}|
is large or infinite. Thus, from a practical perspective, we recommend using the statistic
T_{n}
.

6
Open Problems

In this final section, we highlight a few open questions that arise from the discussions in this paper.

While the multivariate (generalized) Wilcoxon’s rank-sum and signed-rank tests extend the Hodges-Lehmann and Chernoff-Savage lower bounds to the multivariate setting, the current results remain restricted to specific subclasses of distributions, such as those with independent components or elliptically symmetric distributions. Whether these results hold in more generality remains an open question. From a technical perspective, the main challenge in extending these results lies in the lack of a precise characterization of the OT map from
\mu
to
\nu
in more general settings.

OT-based signs and signed-ranks possess many desirable properties, such as distribution-freeness under the hypothesis of
\mathcal{G}
-symmetry, making them useful for constructing powerful tests for
\mathcal{G}
-symmetry (or its location-shift subproblem), as discussed in Section
4
. However, other widely studied notions of symmetry, such as elliptical symmetry
15
15

15

An elliptically symmetric random vector is an affine transformation (i.e.,
\mathbf{x}\mapsto A\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}
) of a spherically symmetric random vector.
, do not naturally fit within the
\mathcal{G}
-symmetry framework. In fact, under the assumption of elliptical symmetry, it has been shown that Mahalanobis ranks and signs are important invariant statistics which can be used to design optimal tests for location and shape parameters
[
38
,
41
]
. Whether the theory of OT can provide distribution-free methods for testing elliptical symmetry or lead to powerful tests in this setting remains an open problem.

The two-sample test based on OT ranks and kernel MMD (see Section
3.2
) has non-trivial Pitman efficiency against
O(1/\sqrt{m+n})
alternatives
[
16
, Theorem 4.3]
. A natural question is whether the one-sample test based on OT signed-ranks and kernel MMD (see Sections
4.2
and
4.3
) also achieves non-trivial Pitman efficiency. In the two-sample problem, it was shown that the OT-based sample ranks
\hat{R}_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{i})
, appearing in the test statistic, can be replaced by their oracle population counterparts
R(\mathbf{X}_{i})
without affecting the asymptotic null distribution (a Hájek-type representation result), using the permutation distribution under the null
[
16
, Theorem C.1]
. However, this permutation technique is not applicable in the one-sample setting. It remains unknown whether a similar Hájek representation result holds in the one-sample problem and, consequently, whether nontrivial Pitman efficiency can be established in this context.

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{w}(\mathbf{h}_{i}).

Since
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\mathbf{h}_{i}}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\nu
and

f_{w}
is a bounded continuous function, we have

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{w}(\mathbf{h}_{i})=\mathbb{E}f_{w}(\mathbf{H}),

where
\mathbf{H}\sim\nu
.
Define a bilinear form

\displaystyle\sigma(w_{p},w_{q}):=\mathbb{E}_{S,\mathbf{H}}\Big[\big[w_{p}(S\mathbf{H})-\mathbb{E}_{S^{\prime}}[w_{p}(S^{\prime}\mathbf{H})]\big]

(18)

\displaystyle\cdot\big[w_{q}(S\mathbf{H})-\mathbb{E}_{S^{\prime\prime}}[w_{q}(S^{\prime\prime}\mathbf{H})]\big]\Big].

It can be seen that
\sigma^{2}=\sigma(w,w)
.
Thus, condition
G_{0}
holds.

Next, we verify condition
G_{1}
. Let
(\phi_{i})_{i\geq 1}
be an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}
. We have

\displaystyle\sum_{i\geq 1}\sigma(\phi_{i},\phi_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\geq 1}\mathbb{E}_{S,\mathbf{H}}\left[(\phi_{i}(S\mathbf{H})-\mathbb{E}_{S^{\prime}}\phi_{i}(S^{\prime}\mathbf{H}))^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\geq 1}\mathbb{E}_{S,\mathbf{H}}\left[\phi_{i}(S\mathbf{H})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\geq 1}\mathbb{E}_{S,\mathbf{H}}\left[\langle\phi_{i},K(S\mathbf{H},\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{S,\mathbf{H}}\left[\|K(S\mathbf{H},\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{S,\mathbf{H}}\left[K(S\mathbf{H},S\mathbf{H})\right]<\infty.

Hence, condition
G_{1}
holds.

Finally, we verify condition
G_{2}
.
By Markov inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{k=i}^{\infty}S_{n}(\phi_{k})^{2}\geq\varepsilon\Big)\leq\varepsilon^{-1}\sum_{k=i}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[S_{n}(\phi_{k})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{-1}\sum_{k=i}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\Big(\Big\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}[\phi_{k}(S_{j}\mathbf{h}_{j})-\mathbb{E}_{S^{\prime}}\phi_{k}(S^{\prime}\mathbf{h}_{j})]\Big\}^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{-1}\sum_{k=i}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}[\phi_{k}(S_{j}\mathbf{h}_{j})-\mathbb{E}_{S^{\prime}}\phi_{k}(S^{\prime}\mathbf{h}_{j})]^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{-1}\sum_{k=i}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}[\phi_{k}(S_{j}\mathbf{h}_{j})]^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{-1}\sum_{k=i}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{S}\left([\phi_{k}(S\mathbf{h}_{j})]^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{-1}\sum_{k=i}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{S}\left(\langle\phi_{k},K(S\mathbf{h}_{j},\cdot)\rangle^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{-1}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{S}\Big(\left\|P_{V_{i-1}^{\perp}}K(S\mathbf{h}_{j},\cdot)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\Big),

where
P_{V_{i-1}^{\perp}}
denotes the orthogonal projection onto
{\rm span}\{\phi_{k}\}_{k=i}^{\infty}
.
The continuity of
K(\cdot,\cdot)
implies that
f(\mathbf{h}):=\mathbb{E}_{S}\Big(\left\|P_{V_{i-1}^{\perp}}K(S\mathbf{h},\cdot)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\Big)
is a continuous function in
\mathbf{h}
. Therefore, it follows from
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\mathbf{h}_{i}}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\nu
that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{S}\Big(\Big\|P_{V_{i-1}^{\perp}}K(S\mathbf{h}_{j},\cdot)\Big\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\Big)=\mathbb{E}_{S,\mathbf{H}}\Big(\Big\|P_{V_{i-1}^{\perp}}K(S\mathbf{H},\cdot)\Big\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\Big).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{k=i}^{\infty}S_{n}(\phi_{k})^{2}\geq\varepsilon\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{i\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{S,\mathbf{H}}\Big(\left\|P_{V_{i-1}^{\perp}}K(S\mathbf{H},\cdot)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\Big)=0.

The last equality follows from the dominated convergence theorem, since

\Big\|P_{V_{i-1}^{\perp}}K(S\mathbf{H},\cdot)\Big\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\leq K(S\mathbf{H},S\mathbf{H})
is bounded.

Hence, all three conditions of Theorem
A.1
are verified, and thus the CLT is established. ∎

Real Data Example
:
In analyzing the returns of multiple financial assets over time, testing for exchangeability helps determine whether the returns can be considered as coming from a common distribution. This, in turn, can inform decisions about grouping assets for downstream analysis and applications. For example, when multiple assets are exchangeable, semi-static hedging techniques can be employed for multi-asset barrier options
[
66
]
.

We examine the monthly returns of equities from various companies over a five-year period, spanning from January 1, 2019, to December 1, 2023. Let
P_{t}
denote the adjusted
18
18

18

The
adjusted close price
accounts for stock splits, dividends, and capital gain distributions. The data used in this analysis are sourced from
https://finance.yahoo.com
.
close price of an asset on the first trading day of the
t
-th month. The monthly return is then computed as
R_{t}=\frac{P_{t}}{P_{t-1}}-1
.

We first analyze the monthly returns of three technology companies: NVIDIA Corporation, Apple Inc., and Tesla, Inc., testing whether their returns are exchangeable. Using the same setting and choice of
\nu
as above, we obtain a
p
-value of 0.87, suggesting no strong evidence against exchangeability. This result may stem from the fact that all three companies belong to the technology sector and exhibit similar return behaviors.

However, when replacing Tesla with the healthcare company Pfizer Inc., the exchangeability test for NVIDIA Corporation, Apple Inc., and Pfizer Inc. yields a highly significant
p
-value
<0.001
. This indicates that companies from different sectors may have fundamentally different return dynamics. The factors influencing the healthcare company Pfizer’s returns likely differ substantially from those driving the returns of technology companies.
