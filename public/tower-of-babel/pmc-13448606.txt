Beyond performance metrics: evaluating clinical artificial intelligence (AI) post deployment
Authors: Derek J Baughman, Woo Yeon Park, Khyzer B Aziz
Published: 2026

Soltani et al present a practical roadmap for evaluating, implementing, and governing clinical artificial intelligence (AI) systems.1 As health systems move from aspiration to adoption, their framework appropriately emphasizes the full AI governance lifecycle, from problem identification through post-deployment monitoring.

Implementation within the electronic health record (EHR) is often as consequential as model performance itself. Even highly accurate systems may fail if recommendations are difficult to interpret, poorly integrated into clinical workflows, or misaligned with how clinicians make decisions under pressure. In acute care environments where time is short and consequences are immediate, usability, cognitive burden, and implementation fidelity may matter as much as discrimination or calibration. Deployed AI systems should also help clinicians identify the patient-level evidence underlying a recommendation, enabling efficient verification within the EHR, and preserving responsible clinical judgment. User acceptance should therefore be evaluated prospectively alongside technical performance.2

The challenge becomes more complex after deployment. We propose the concept of ‘AI conformance’—the gradual reshaping of clinician behavior in response to algorithmic recommendations—as a distinct post-deployment phenomenon. At that point, the question is not whether an AI system agrees with clinicians, but whether clinicians begin changing their behavior in response to its recommendations. Such agreements may reflect appropriate adoption of evidence-based guidance, automation bias, workflow adaptation, or some combination.3 Regardless, evaluation frameworks should incorporate measures of reference-standard reliability, clinician-AI interaction, override behavior, and longitudinal changes in practice patterns. A related concern is AI-induced deskilling, where prolonged reliance on algorithmic support may erode the clinical competencies needed to question, contextualize, or override recommendations. Although these risks remain incompletely understood, they warrant explicit attention in post-deployment monitoring plans.4

The authors’ discussion of bias also merits further emphasis. Table 1 appropriately summarizes common bias categories; however, recent work has identified more than 200 distinct sources of bias across the real-world healthcare evidence lifecycle.5 As healthcare data become increasingly multimodal, longitudinal, and institutionally embedded, potential bias pathways will expand. The aim is not to eliminate bias once and for all, but to build governance systems capable of identifying, quantifying, monitoring, and mitigating bias throughout deployment.

Soltani et al provide an important foundation for safe AI adoption in acute care surgery.1 Future evaluation efforts should recognize that deployment is not the end of evaluation but the beginning of a more demanding phase—one in which workflow integration, behavioral adaptation, imperfect reference standards, deskilling, and multidimensional bias must be assessed alongside performance.
