Machine learning and deep learning predictive models for prognosis in patients with atrial fibrillation: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Xiaoyi Wang, Tristan John Bampton, Dhani Dharmaprani, Rajiv Mahajan, Lyle John Palmer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo summarise the performance of machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) prognostic models for atrial fibrillation (AF), compare their relative performances with non-artificial intelligence (AI) methods, and to identify key research gaps.Methods and analysisWe searched PubMed, Embase, Scopus, the Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and ProQuest from inception to 21 October 2024 for cohort, case-control, cross-sectional, and randomised controlled studies that used ML or DL models to predict clinical outcomes in AF patients. Studies were excluded if they focused on non-AF populations, lacked model performance evaluation, or were abstracts, reviews, or other non-primary research articles. Extracted information included study characteristics, patient demographics, model details and validation strategies. Reporting quality and risk of bias were assessed using the TRIPOD+AI (Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis for AI) and PROBAST+AI (Prediction model Risk Of Bias ASsessment Tool for AI) checklists. The primary outcome was model discrimination, measured by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Meta-analyses were conducted, with heterogeneity assessed via Cochran’s Q test and I² statistics.ResultsOf the 7128 studies identified, 81 fulfilled the selection criteria. Among these, 57 applied ML models (81 models total) and 24 used DL models (31 models total). Commonly predicted outcomes included AF recurrence (n=43), ischaemic stroke (n=20), all-cause mortality (n=15), major bleeding (n=11), heart failure (n=3), major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) (n=3) and thromboembolic events (n=3). AI models exhibited moderate-to-good predictive performance, ranging from a pooled AUC of 0.71 (95% CI 0.66 to 0.76) for major bleeding to 0.85 (95% CI 0.79 to 0.92) for heart failure. Significant heterogeneity was observed across studies (I² 87%–100%). When evaluated on the same datasets, both AI model types generally outperformed risk scores and regression-based models. PROBAST+AI assessment identified high risk of bias in 66 studies (81%) for model development and 68 studies (84%) for model evaluation, primarily due to inadequate handling of missing data and underpowered datasets.ConclusionAI models show great promise in AF prognosis tasks and generally outperform non-AI prediction methods. The substantial heterogeneity limits the clinical interpretability of pooled AUCs and warrants cautious interpretation. Standardised reporting and multimodal data integration will be essential to improving model reliability and clinical applicability of AI prognostic models for AF.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42024606885.Keywords: Neural Networks, Computer; Artificial intelligence; Public Health; Machine Learning; Deep Learning

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common sustained arrhythmia and a major contributor to mortality and morbidity from serious complications. Previous systematic reviews have predominantly focused on AF recurrence after ablation, rarely benchmarked artificial intelligence (AI) models against clinical risk scores or traditional regression models, and have not incorporated detailed assessments of reporting quality and risk of bias using new AI-specific tools such as TRIPOD+AI (Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis for AI) and PROBAST+AI (Prediction model Risk Of Bias ASsessment Tool for AI).

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This is the first comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis of prognostic models for all AF outcomes using and comparing machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) algorithms with non-AI prediction methods. AI models achieved moderate-to-good discrimination overall and generally outperformed risk scores and regression models when evaluated on the same datasets. However, many clinically relevant AF outcomes remain underinvestigated, and existing studies are often limited by lack of external validation, high risk of bias and incomplete reporting.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

This systematic review highlights the growing role of AI in prognostic prediction for AF, while revealing major methodological and reporting shortcomings that hinder clinical translation. Future research should more closely adhere to TRIPOD+AI and PROBAST+AI guidelines to enhance transparency and reproducibility. Further exploration of DL and multimodal data fusion is warranted to improve the accuracy, generalisability and clinical utility of AI-based prognostic models for AF.

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common sustained arrhythmia and is associated with substantial mortality and morbidity from stroke, heart failure and thromboembolism.1–3 In 2019, the global prevalence of AF reached 59 million cases,4 marking a 76% increase over the preceding 9 years. The rising prevalence and incidence of AF have resulted in significant health and economic burdens on society.5 6 Accurately predicting patients at high risk of adverse outcomes is essential for early intervention strategies and optimising resource allocation, thereby reducing healthcare costs and improving patient outcomes.

Extant AF prognostic risk scores, including CHADS2 (Congestive heart failure, Hypertension, Age≥75 years, Diabetes mellitus and prior Stroke),7 CHA2DS2-VASc (CHADS2, with two points for an age of 75 or greater, and additional points for other vascular disease, an age of 65–74, or being female (sex category))8 and HAS-BLED (Hypertension, Abnormal renal/liver function, Stroke, Bleeding history or predisposition, Labile INR, Elderly, Drugs/alcohol concomitantly),9 are widely used clinically to predict future stroke and major bleeding risks in patients with AF. However, these risk scores have only modest prognostic performance (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC)10 0.55–0.65)11 and significant constraints that limit their clinical utility. They do not incorporate several important risk factors, such as the specific phenotype of AF and electrocardiographic features, and they do not account for the varying contributions of individual risk factors.12 Such scores are based on static measurements made at initial diagnosis, which inadequately capture dynamic, time-dependent characteristics such as AF burden13 and do not accommodate complex, non-linear relationships between risk factors. These limitations highlight the need for more clinically useful and comprehensive prognostic risk prediction models for AF.

Machine learning (ML) automatically recognises patterns in large datasets and generates predictions based on these patterns.14 Unlike conventional clinical prediction models using standard regression methods, ML techniques can discover novel complex patterns to process non-linear relationships and complex interactions between variables.15 Deep learning (DL) is a subset of ML that uses multilayered neural networks to automatically learn patterns and representations from high-dimensional data,16 such as medical images. ML and DL have been increasingly applied in clinical practice14 17 and hold substantial promise for predicting outcomes in patients with AF.

Our systematic review aimed to evaluate the predictive performance of existing ML-based and DL-based prognostic models for AF outcomes, compare these models with non-artificial intelligence (AI) prediction methods and identify gaps in current predictive modelling practices and areas for future improvement.

Methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

We included all studies that used ML or DL models to predict clinical outcomes in patients with AF. Studies involving animals, non-AF patient populations or those lacking model performance evaluation were excluded, as were abstracts, posters, correspondences, review articles, letters, comments, editorials and errata. We defined ML algorithms as anything more complex than non-penalised regression models, including methods such as decision trees, random forest and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost). We considered DL to be a subset of ML that encompasses deep neural networks with two or more hidden layers, such as convolutional neural networks.

We searched PubMed, Embase, Scopus, the Cochrane Library, Web of Science and ProQuest using search terms related to atrial fibrillation, artificial intelligence, machine learning, deep learning and predictive models for studies published in English from database inception to 21 October 2024. We investigated all clinical outcomes, that is, no outcome-specific terms were included. Manual searches were conducted to review articles and references cited in the included studies. Full search strategies are provided in online supplemental tables 1a–f.

Studies were uploaded to EndNote V.2118 and then to Covidence19 for two rounds of deduplication. XW and TJB independently screened titles and abstracts based on predefined criteria. Studies meeting the criteria or those with unclear eligibility underwent full-text review by both reviewers. Any disagreements were resolved by a third reviewer (LJP, RM). Meta-analyses were conducted when at least three studies reported consistent performance metrics and clinical outcomes.

Our protocol was prospectively registered on PROSPERO (CRD42024606885) and conducted following PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines.20

Data analysis

Before data collection, we designed a table based on the CHARMS (CHecklist for critical Appraisal and data extraction for systematic Reviews of prediction Modelling Studies) checklist21; 14 data fields were extracted by XW for each study (online supplemental table 2).

Studies were first grouped based on the clinical outcome investigated and then further grouped by patient characteristics (eg, comorbidities or care setting) or treatment interventions (eg, ablation or anticoagulation). Within each outcome group, studies were classified into ML and DL subgroups. For studies reporting multiple models for the same outcome, the model with the best performance (prioritising AUC) was selected. For studies reporting multiple outcomes, all outcomes were included. Subgroup analyses were conducted, where sufficient data and adequate reporting were available, stratified by model type, patient characteristics/treatment interventions, follow-up duration or type of study (retrospective or prospective).

The reporting quality was assessed using TRIPOD+AI (Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis for AI) (online supplemental table 3), and risk of bias was evaluated using PROBAST+AI (Prediction model Risk Of Bias ASsessment Tool for AI) (online supplemental tables 4a, b), both by XW.22 23 Details of the assessment frameworks are provided in online supplemental material 1. Sensitivity analyses were performed by excluding studies at high risk of bias. All included studies were cohort studies, except for two randomised controlled trials24 25; sensitivity analyses by study design were therefore not feasible.

The discriminatory performance of models was primarily assessed using AUC, which provides an overall measure of model discrimination. Secondary metrics included accuracy, sensitivity, specificity and other related measures. For each outcome, AUCs were pooled separately for internal and external validation.26 Given the anticipated substantial heterogeneity across studies due to differences in predictors, study populations, model types and outcome definitions, random effects meta-analytical models were applied and pooled AUCs were used as descriptive quantitative summaries of reported performance. Interstudy heterogeneity was quantified using Cochran’s Q test, with significant heterogeneity defined as a p value<0.1 or I²>50%.27 If heterogeneity was not substantial (I²≤50% and p>0.1), a fixed effects model was applied. Evidence of publication bias was assessed using funnel plots for asymmetry and Egger’s and Begg’s test28 29 for meta-analyses including ≥10 studies, with results considered to indicate potential publication bias when p<0.1. Meta-analyses and comparison of nested AUCs were conducted using the MedCalc software V.23.1.3.30 Where studies also reported non-AI models for AF outcomes, model performance was compared with that of AI models in the same study population. Further details are included in online supplemental material 2.

Title and abstract screening excluded 3182 irrelevant studies, and full-text review excluded a further 235 studies (online supplemental figure 1). Prediction of AF recurrence

The pooled AUC for predicting AF recurrence across all models (internal validation) was 0.77 (95% CI 0.74 to 0.81; figure 1; I²=87%, p<0.0001). There was no evidence of publication bias with Egger’s test (p=0.58) or Begg’s test (p=0.89) (online supplemental figure 2). The median accuracy was 0.80 (IQR 0.77–0.86), the median sensitivity was 0.77 (IQR 0.71–0.84) and the median specificity was 0.79 (IQR 0.75–0.88). ML models had a pooled AUC of 0.75 (95% CI 0.71 to 0.79; I²=80%, p<0.0001) and DL models a pooled AUC of 0.82 (95% CI 0.77 to 0.86; I²=85%, p<0.0001). Among studies that reported follow-up duration (n=40, 93%), the median follow-up was 12 months (IQR 12.0–12.0), with 31 (78%) having a follow-up period≤12 months. When stratified by intervention type, the pooled AUC for predicting AF recurrence after catheter ablation was 0.80 (95% CI 0.77 to 0.83; I²=79%, p<0.0001), with ML models achieving 0.78 (95% CI 0.75 to 0.82; I²=58%, p=0.0001) and DL models 0.82 (95% CI 0.77 to 0.86; I²=85%, p<0.0001). All models of cardioversion and surgical ablation were ML-based. For cardioversion, all models had follow-up periods≤12 months, with a pooled AUC of 0.65 (95% CI 0.58 to 0.71; I²=76%, p=0.002). For surgical ablation, the pooled AUC was 0.75 (95% CI 0.69 to 0.82; I²=0%, p=0.85). No studies of AF recurrence reported results from external validation.

ML/DL versus other published non-AI models

Several previous systematic reviews have summarised the performance of non-AI prognostic models for AF outcomes, including logistic regression and commonly used clinical risk scores (eg, CHADS2, CHA2DS2-VASc, HAS-BLED). Reported pooled AUCs for these models generally ranged from 0.64 to 0.86 across outcomes such as AF recurrence, ischaemic stroke, all-cause mortality and major bleeding. Detailed results are provided in online supplemental material 6.

ML/DL versus non-AI models trained on the same dataset

Some included studies compared the performance of ML or DL predictive models with clinical risk scores or regression models in the same study population. Among 81 ML models, 25 compared performance with risk scores25 34 37 42 47 49 68 73 74 80–82 90 93 97 and 33 with regression models12 15 24 31 36 43 55 58 64 72 75 77 83 84 88 92 94 98–100 in the same study population. All comparisons with risk scores reported superior performance of ML models, with 12 (48%) reporting statistically significant superiority,42 47 49 68 73 74 81 82 5 (15%) reporting non-significant differences34 90 and the remaining 8 comparisons25 37 80 93 97 did not report statistical significance. Of the comparisons with regression models, 6 (18%) found ML models to perform worse than logistic regression,72 98 while the remaining 27 comparisons (82%) reported superior performance, including 3 (9%) with statistically significant superiority.92 100 Among 31 DL models, 6 studies compared DL performance to risk scores35 57 60 86 95 96 and 13 to regression models.54 56 62 67 69 76 102 All comparisons with risk scores reported better performance of DL models, with two reporting statistically superior performance.35 57 All comparisons with regression models found DL models superior, with two reporting statistically superior performance.56 Overall, across 77 direct comparisons between AI and non-AI approaches, 92% reported superior performance of ML/DL models, with 28% demonstrating statistically significant performance improvements.

To better understand model performance across specific predicted outcomes, we compared ML/DL models with non-AI methods for specific outcomes. For AF recurrence, two ML models (22%) reported significantly better performance than regression models after Holm-Bonferroni correction.31 84 Two DL models (50%) showed significant improvements over risk scores,86 96 but none of the comparisons with regression models for this outcome reached statistical significance. For ischaemic stroke prediction, four ML models (50%) showed significant improvements over risk scores,42 47 68 97 while comparisons with regression models were not statistically significant. For all-cause mortality, two ML models (67%) showed significantly better performance compared with risk scores,34 74 and one model (20%) reported significant improvement over regression models.72 In predicting major bleeding, three ML models (60%) reported significant improvements over risk scores,42 73 74 while no significant differences were observed when compared with regression models.

The primary contributor to high risk of bias was an insufficient number of participants with the outcome, noted in 46 studies (57%) based on the events per variable (EPV) criterion, where EPV<10.12 31–34 36 38 39 41 42 44 45 47 50 54 58 59 61 63–65 69–74 76–78 80 81 83–85 89 91 93 94 97 98 100 101 103–105 107 After excluding studies that did not report the number of events or predictors, 32 ML models were found to have an EPV<10 in their training cohorts,12 31 33 34 36 41 42 47 58 64 70–74 77 80 81 83 85 91 94 100 103 107 with a median of 94.0 positive events (IQR 61.0–145.0) and 27.0 predictors (IQR 15.0–58.0). Three DL models reported an EPV<10,61 65 76 with a median of 47.0 positive events (IQR 4.0–1178.0) and 112.0 predictors (IQR 11.0–669.0).

The second most common contributor to risk of bias was inadequate handling of missing data, observed in 35 studies (43%). Among these, 18 studies used simple imputation methods such as mean, median and fixed-value imputation31 35 48 49 58 70 72 74 75 77 81 86 92 95 96 102 106 107; and 17 studies conducted complete case analysis without confirming that the data fulfilled the assumption of missing completely at random (MCAR).32 42 50 52 53 57 59–61 66 69 76 85 94 98 103 105 19 (23%) studies appropriately employed advanced imputation methods such as multiple imputation.12 15 25 37 43 44 55 56 64 71 73 80 82 87 88 90 97 99 100 The remaining 27 studies (33%) did not report their methods for handling missing data.

Sensitivity analyses

Ideally, sensitivity analyses excluding studies at high risk of bias should be performed for each outcome. However, only six studies15 40 46 62 87 90 predicting AF recurrence met the PROBAST+AI criteria of low or unclear risk of bias in both model development and evaluation, with at least three studies reporting AUC values for the same outcome to allow meta-analysis. When high-risk studies were excluded, the pooled AUC for AF recurrence prediction was 0.78 (95% CI 0.73 to 0.83; online supplemental figure 8; I²=74%, p=0.002), compared with 0.77 (95% CI 0.74 to 0.81; figure 1; I²=87%, p<0.0001) in the main analysis including all studies. The exclusion of high-risk studies slightly reduced heterogeneity but did not materially change the overall effect estimate, indicating that this sensitivity analysis did not show better performance compared with the main analysis.

Discussion

Our systematic review has synthesised evidence from 81 prognostic studies using ML or DL models to predict clinical outcomes in patients with AF. The current study provides a comprehensive, contemporary summary of AI prognostic modelling in patients with AF. When compared with non-AI prediction methods trained on the same dataset, ML models consistently outperformed clinical risk scores and generally outperformed regression models. DL models showed superior performance over both risk scores and regression models. Significantly better performance was more often observed versus risk scores than regression models. However, this evidence does not consider the high risk of bias present in most of the included AI studies or the general lack of external validation.

Under-researched outcomes

Outcomes that were able to be meta-analysed included AF recurrence, ischaemic stroke, all-cause mortality, major bleeding, heart failure and MACE. However, 67% of AF-associated outcomes could not be meta-analysed due to insufficient independent studies, indicating that many important AF outcomes—such as haemorrhagic stroke, cognitive impairment, cardiovascular-associated mortality and myocardial infarction—remain under-researched. Further research on these outcomes is clearly needed.

Differences in input data between ML and DL models

Prognostic models based on ML and DL differed in their input variables, as illustrated by online supplemental tables 7a, b. ML models rely more on structured input variables, often presented as tabular data, such as demographic information, medical history, medication use, laboratory results and imaging features, which typically require manual feature engineering for extraction and selection. In contrast, DL models are far better at handling unstructured data, directly extracting high-dimensional and complex features from raw data, such as raw ECG signals, three-dimensional reconstructed images or multispectral images (see online supplemental material 8). While tabular data provides interpretable and structured information, DL can capture subtle differences in image information to identify distinguishing features.63 The difference in input modalities constitutes a major confounder in performance comparisons; conversely, when both approaches are restricted to the same tabular feature set, the observed performance gap often shrinks or disappears (see online supplemental material 9).

The best-performing DL models exhibited good levels of performance. Further research will be required to investigate the potential clinical utility of these DL models, including further validation, silent trials109 and randomised controlled trials.110 Overall, the common limitations we found in the included studies suggest that there is room for improvement in AI prognostic modelling of AF outcomes and that performance will continue to improve as these limitations are dealt with in future studies. Many included studies demonstrated that DL-based multimodal fusion improved the prediction of patient outcomes63 69 86 87 96 (see online supplemental material 10).

Reporting quality and transparency

The overall picture provided by the TRIPOD+AI22 and PROBAST+AI23 reporting guidelines is of a nascent field where published studies are often hard to interpret due to their many limitations. Application of TRIPOD+AI to the included studies suggested that their completeness was generally insufficient, revealing critical gaps in reporting quality. The most frequent deficiencies were in patient and public involvement, open science and discussion items, each with a median completeness of 60% or less. Notably, only five studies (6%) made analytical code available; the remaining studies neither shared the code nor explained the reasons for withholding it. While some studies attempted to address issues of confounding and model interpretability (see online supplemental material 11), these efforts were limited and inconsistent. The lack of transparency hinders reproducibility and undermines the credibility of AI research. Future studies should prioritise standardised reporting, robust study design and the incorporation of fairness and patient perspectives to enhance the utility and reliability of AI-based prognostic models.

Sources of high risk of bias

Application of PROBAST+AI to the included studies suggested that analysis was the domain most frequently associated with a high risk of bias. The primary contributor in many studies (57%) was the insufficient number of participants with the outcome. Low EPV leads to poorer predictive performance, model overfitting and wider CIs.111 The median training set size in ML models was 2953.0 (IQR 502.5–12 872.0) and in DL models was 1055.0 (IQR 295.0–3764.5). This is counterintuitive, given that DL relies on ‘big’ data to develop robust data-driven models.112 It is likely that the relatively smaller training sets used to construct DL models limited the prognostic performance of the models. The annotation of medical data required for supervised learning is also expensive, tedious and time-consuming.113 There is a notable lack of publicly available databases containing detailed clinical and long-term follow-up information for patients with AF.114 The lack of public data makes it difficult for researchers to obtain sufficient samples to develop and validate effective AI models and to compare results.

The second most common contributor to the high risk of bias was the inadequate handling of missing data. Among studies inadequately addressing missing data (43%), the most used method was simple imputation. While straightforward to implement, simple imputation underestimates data uncertainty, potentially causing biased parameter estimates and unstable results.115 The second most common approach was complete case analysis (ie, listwise deletion)116 without verifying MCAR. Even if data are missing at random, the results of listwise deletion will likely be biased.117 Even when the MCAR assumption holds, discarding a large amount of data would have significantly reduced the efficiency of parameter estimation. PROBAST+AI guidelines recommend multiple imputation as the preferred method for handling missing data, as it produces less biased results and more accurate SEs and p values.23

Calibration was a frequently overlooked issue; only 28% of studies reported calibration measures. Calibration is essential for assessing predictive performance, as models with high AUC might remain clinically useless or even harmful if poorly calibrated.118 Likewise, only 12% of studies reported decision curve analysis. From a clinical perspective, a model with a high AUC but a decision curve below that of the ‘treat all’ strategy would be less useful than treating all patients without model guidance.119 This highlights the need for more consistent evaluation of clinical utility.

Between-study heterogeneity and limited population diversity

Substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies within each outcome grouping, even among those with large samples. This likely reflects differences in predictors, populations, model types and outcome definitions. Predictor selection, data preprocessing, model architecture and adherence to reporting standards varied widely, contributing further to heterogeneity.

Across all outcomes, between-study heterogeneity was consistently high (I² 87%–100%), indicating that the included studies do not estimate a common underlying effect size; pooled AUCs should therefore be interpreted only as descriptive quantitative summaries of reported performance. When stratified by model type, heterogeneity remained high for both ML (I²≥80) and DL (I²≥85) models across outcomes. Notably, in subsequent subgroup analyses, heterogeneity patterns differed between ML and DL models. When analyses were restricted to≤12 month outcomes, heterogeneity dropped markedly for ML models (I² 7%–36%) but remained high for DL models (I² 88%–99%). In analyses stratified by patient characteristics/treatment interventions, AF recurrence after catheter ablation showed reduced heterogeneity for ML models (I²=58%), whereas DL models remained highly heterogeneous (I²=85%). Most other outcomes, including ischaemic stroke and major bleeding, still showed extreme heterogeneity (I²≥91%) irrespective of follow-up duration or patient subgroup. Low heterogeneity was observed only in a few cases, such as ML models predicting all-cause mortality in patients with no selected characteristics or treatment interventions (I²=16%). Stratification by type of study showed that, for AF recurrence after catheter ablation, prospective cohorts had lower heterogeneity and heterogeneity collapsed for major bleeding in prospective ML models among patients with no selected characteristics or treatment interventions (I²=0%). In contrast, ischaemic stroke in such patients consistently showed extreme heterogeneity (I² 97%–99%), regardless of the type of study. These findings suggest that differences in follow-up duration, patient selection and type of study can partly explain variability among ML studies, whereas the substantial heterogeneity among DL studies may arise from other sources. The sensitivity analysis excluding high-risk studies reduced heterogeneity only modestly and did not materially change pooled AUCs, suggesting that the observed heterogeneity is largely structural rather than driven solely by study quality.

Population diversity was limited among the included studies. Only 10% of studies reported participants’ ethnicity or genetic ancestry, despite evidence that AF outcomes differ across ethnic/ancestry groups.120–124 Of those studies that reported ethnicity, most had predominantly European or East Asian cohorts. All studies reported geographical location, with East Asia, North America and Western Europe dominating. These findings suggest that current research has limited data from under-represented regions such as South Asia, Africa, Latin America and the Middle East. This lack of diversity may reduce model generalisability and equity.124 Future studies should prioritise these populations to ensure fair and applicable AF prognostic modelling.

Lack of external validation

Alarmingly, external validation was lacking in most included studies (88%). Validation is a critical component of AI model evaluation, ensuring reliability and overall effectiveness.26 External validation is essential to evaluate how well a model performs in independent, real-world settings before clinical implementation.125 Among externally validated models, 9 (83%) demonstrated poorer performance on external datasets, with two studies reporting substantial drops.12 99 Such findings highlight that any apparent advantages of AI models observed in internal analyses must be interpreted with caution, given the lack of consistent external validation. A plausible explanation is overfitting, where the models perform well on the training data but struggle to make accurate predictions on unseen test data. To address this limitation, future research should study larger sample sizes, select essential predictors to avoid model overcomplexity (eg, using regularisation methods) and apply data augmentation techniques such as bootstrap or oversampling to enhance the proportion of positive events. While all included studies already employed hold-out or cross-validation, further strategies such as regularisation and dropout could further reduce overfitting and improve generalisability.126 Without proper external validation, models developed on limited datasets might exhibit reduced predictive validity and increased risk of bias when applied to new populations, leading to significant research waste.127

Strengths and limitations of our review

The current study is the first comprehensive systematic review of ML and DL models for AF prognosis. The strengths of our systematic review and meta-analyses include a comprehensive literature search, independent screening, data extraction, strict adherence to PRISMA guidelines and the use of TRIPOD+AI and PROBAST+AI for quality and bias assessment. Our meta-analyses provide a quantitative summary of reported model performance across multiple common outcomes. The major limitation was the substantial heterogeneity among studies, restricting the meaningful synthesis of model performance and limiting reliable comparisons between ML and DL algorithms. Considerations regarding the recalculation of CIs can be found in online supplemental material 12.

Conclusions

Our systematic review and meta-analyses have demonstrated that both ML and DL models consistently outperformed risk scores and, to a lesser extent, regression models. Research on certain AF outcomes, such as haemorrhagic stroke, is notably scarce, underscoring the need for further investigation in these areas. Effectively increasing EPV and handling missing data are critical to minimising bias. Future research should prioritise external validation and standardised reporting, adhering to TRIPOD+AI and PROBAST+AI guidelines. Further exploration of DL is clearly required, particularly in developing models with enhanced generalisability and capabilities for multimodal data fusion, to improve the accuracy and clinical utility of AF prognostic models.
