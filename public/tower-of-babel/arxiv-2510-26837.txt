Abstract
We present force characterizations of two newly developed insect-scale propulsors—one single-tailed and one double-tailed—for microrobotic swimmers that leverage fluid–structure interaction (FSI) to generate thrust. The designs of these two devices were inspired by anguilliform swimming and are driven by soft tails excited by high-work-density (HWD) actuators powered by shape-memory alloy (SMA) wires. While these propulsors have been demonstrated to be suitable for microrobotic aquatic locomotion and controllable with simple architectures for trajectory tracking in the two-dimensional ( 𝟐 \boldsymbol{2} D) space, the characteristics and magnitudes of the associated forces have not been studied systematically. In the research presented here, we adopted a theoretical framework based on the notion of reactive forces and obtained experimental data for characterization using a custom-built µN-resolution force sensor. We measured maximum and cycle-averaged force values with multi-test means of respectively 0.45 \boldsymbol{0.45} mN and 2.97 \boldsymbol{2.97}\, µN , for the tested single-tail propulsor. For the dual-tail propulsor, we measured maximum and cycle-averaged force values with multi-test means of 0.61 \boldsymbol{0.61} mN and 22.6 \boldsymbol{22.6}\, µN , respectively. These results represent the first measurements of the instantaneous thrust generated by insect-scale propulsors of this type and provide insights into FSI for efficient microrobotic propulsion.

II
Reactive-Force Model

In this section, we present the theoretical framework used to model the forces produced by
VLEIBot-type
propulsors. Employing the
large-amplitude

elongated-body
approach presented in
[
25
]
, we model the lateral undulation of a propulsive
thin
tail as a traveling wave with the characteristics shown in
Fig.
2
(a)
. As seen in
Figs.
2
(a) and (b)
, the triplet of unit vectors
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{N}}=\{\boldsymbol{n}_{1},\boldsymbol{n}_{2},\boldsymbol{n}_{3}\}
denotes the inertial frame of reference, fixed to the planet Earth, used to describe the kinematics of the tail and analyzing the dynamics of the fluid in its close proximity. Similarly, the triplet of unit vectors
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}=\{\boldsymbol{b}_{1},\boldsymbol{b}_{2},\boldsymbol{b}_{3}\}
denotes the
body-fixed
frame of reference. In agreement with these definitions, the directions of
\boldsymbol{b}_{1}
,
\boldsymbol{b}_{2}
, and
\boldsymbol{b}_{3}
coincide with those of
\boldsymbol{n}_{1}
,
\boldsymbol{n}_{2}
, and
\boldsymbol{n}_{3}
at any given instant, and
\boldsymbol{b}_{1}
is collinear with the midline of the tail when in the extended condition—i.e., the planform of the tail is perpendicular to the
\boldsymbol{n}_{1}
–
\boldsymbol{n}_{2}
plane. Consistent with these idealizations, we assume that the tail does not twist during undulation and, therefore, that the forces acting along the
\boldsymbol{b}_{3}
direction are negligible. To track the forces acting on the tail—all assumed inside the
\boldsymbol{n}_{1}
–
\boldsymbol{n}_{2}
plane—we define the Lagrangian coordinate
s\in\left[0,l\right]
, according to which the arc length
s
is the distance from the origin of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
—along the orthogonal projection of the tail onto
\boldsymbol{n}_{1}
–
\boldsymbol{n}_{2}
—to a vertical infinitesimal element of volume, and
l
is the total length of the tail.

The position of a point on the centerline of the tail, relative to the origin of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{N}}
, at the Lagrangian coordinate
s
and time
t
is given by
\boldsymbol{r}(s,t)=\left[x(s,t)~y(s,t)~0\right]^{T}
. For modeling purposes, we assume that the tail moves from right to left at a constant speed
U
—information embedded in
x(s,t)
—and that the associated traveling wave travels from left to right, as shown in
Fig.
2
(a)
. Under these assumptions,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
is also an inertial frame. The tail material (AirTech A
4000
R
14417
) is assumed inextensible and, as a consequence,
\left\|\partial{\boldsymbol{r}}/\partial{s}\right\|_{2}=1
and
\left[ds/dt\right]_{\text{tail}}=0
, for all
(s,t)
. Thus, the velocity of a point on the centerline of the tail can be found as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{b}}(s,t)=\frac{D\boldsymbol{r}}{Dt}=\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{r}}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{r}}{\partial s}\left[\frac{ds}{dt}\right]_{\text{tail}}=\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{r}}{\partial t}.

(1)

Also, we define a local Lagrangian frame,
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}
, with its primary axis aligned with the unit tangent vector of the tail centerline,
\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{t}}(s,t)
, and its secondary axis aligned with the unit normal vector of the tail centerline,
\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{n}}(s,t)
, as shown in
Fig.
2
(a)
. Using these definitions, (
1
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}_{\text{b}}(s,t)=v_{\text{b},\text{n}}(s,t)\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{n}}(s,t)+v_{\text{b},\text{t}}(s,t)\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{t}}(s,t).

(2)

Next, following Lighthill’s approach, we write the velocity of a parcel of fluid at
(s,t)
in
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}
coordinates as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}(s,t)=v_{\text{n}}(s,t)\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{n}}(s,t)+v_{\text{t}}(s,t)\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{t}}(s,t),

(3)

where,
v_{\text{n}}=\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{n}}
and
v_{\text{t}}=\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{t}}
.

In this approach, we assume that the fluid is inviscid and that cannot penetrate the surface of the tail. From the
no-penetration
condition, it immediately follows that
v_{\text{n}}=v_{\text{b},\text{n}}
; therefore, both
v_{\text{n}}
and
{\partial v_{\text{n}}}/{\partial t}
can be estimated from the kinematics of the tail’s centerline. From the
inviscid-fluid
condition, it follows that, in general,
v_{\text{t}}\neq v_{\text{b},\text{t}}
, which implies that the
no-slip
condition does not apply as a parcel of fluid can slide along the surface of the tail; therefore,
v_{\text{t}}=\left[ds/dt\right]_{\text{fluid}}
cannot be directly estimated from the kinematics of the tail’s centerline.

Reactive theory as presented
in
[
25
]
is based on the notion of
added mass
, which is an idealization of the volume per unit length of incompressible fluid displaced when the modeled tail undulates while submerged in the surrounding medium. Here, we describe the added mass per unit length along the tail as

\displaystyle m(s)=2\rho\beta h(s),

(4)

where
\rho
is the density of the fluid;
\beta
is the thickness of the tail material; and,
h(s)=0.694\sqrt{l-s}
defines the shape profile of the parabolic tail considered in this paper and shown in
Fig.
2
(b)
. In this approach,
resistive
forces associated with fluid flow produced by viscosity are assumed negligible. Thus, as explained in the Appendix, we only consider the forces acting on the tail that arise from the reaction to accelerating the corresponding added mass. Namely, using Newton’s laws and including Lighthill’s assumptions, we obtain that the total instantaneous reactive force exerted on the undulating tail is given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{F}_{\text{r}}(t)=-\int_{0}^{l}m(s)\left(\frac{\partial v_{\text{n}}}{\partial t}+v_{\text{t}}\frac{\partial v_{\text{n}}}{\partial s}\right)\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{n}}(s,t)ds.

(5)

Then, by extracting the component along the direction of locomotion,
\boldsymbol{b}_{1}
, we obtain the magnitude of the
signed
instantaneous thrust force,

\displaystyle F_{\text{th}}=-\int_{0}^{l}m(s)\left(\frac{\partial v_{\text{n}}}{\partial t}+v_{\text{t}}\frac{\partial v_{\text{n}}}{\partial s}\right)\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{n}}\cdot\boldsymbol{b}_{1}ds,

(6)

and its corresponding time average,

\displaystyle\bar{F}_{\text{th}}=-\int_{0}^{l}m(s)\left\langle\left(\frac{\partial v_{\text{n}}}{\partial t}+v_{\text{t}}\frac{\partial v_{\text{n}}}{\partial s}\right)u_{\text{n},1}\right\rangle ds,

(7)

where
u_{\text{n},1}=\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{n}}\cdot\boldsymbol{b}_{1}
and
\langle\cdot\rangle
denotes the time average of the corresponding function over an undulation cycle. The integrals specified by (
6
) and (
7
) can be separated into an unsteady term and a convective term. The unsteady term represents the reaction force exerted on the tail due to direct normal acceleration of the fluid parcel in contact with it; this term can be significant even when the undulating tail does not travel relative to the inertial frame—
i.e.,
U=0
—and the instantaneous curvature of the tail is small. In contrast, the convective term can be considered negligible for
U=0
and a small curvature; additionally, it is difficult to estimate
v_{\text{t}}
from video footage without the use of advanced techniques such as
particle image velocimetry
(PIV). Therefore, for the experiments and numerical estimations presented in
Section
IV
, we consider that
v_{\text{t}}=0
because thrust is measured with the undulating tail attached to a static sensor.

As discussed in
[
25
]
, over an undulation cycle—through a traveling wave like the one depicted in
Fig.
2
(a)
—the actuator delivers a finite amount of mechanical energy at the connection with the tail. Therefore, assuming that the elastic energy stored in the tail material is negligible, the
cycle-averaged
power carried by a traveling wave along the tail can be estimated as

\displaystyle P_{\text{w}}(s)=\frac{1}{2}m(s)\left\langle v_{\text{n}}^{2}(s,t)\right\rangle v_{\text{w}},

(8)

where
v_{\text{w}}
is the traveling velocity of the wave, which is determined by the actuation frequency. Using basic kinematics, we conclude that to maximize the stroke amplitude during operation,
\left\langle v_{\text{n}}^{2}(s,t)\right\rangle
must increase and, due to energy dissipation,
P_{\text{w}}(s)
is expected to decrease along
s
. This observation indicates that a
high-performance
propulsion system of the type considered here must necessarily combine
high-work-density
(HWD) actuation and a
soft-tail
design with a decreasing
h(s)
along
s
. As already mentioned, the model specified by (
6
) and (
7
) neglects resistive forces such as
skin-friction
and
boundary-layer
dissipation, which become more dominant as the Reynolds number decreases. Therefore, to describe force generation at low Reynolds numbers (
\text{Re}<1
),
resistive-force
models are necessary. The propulsors studied in the presented research operate at Reynolds numbers in the range from about
300
to
900
, and it is not immediately obvious that the approach presented here is suitable for this flow regime. To assess this and other issues, we developed the
µN-resolution
force sensor discussed in the next section.

Dynamic Modeling for Design

From simple experiments, we estimate that the instantaneous hydrodynamic forces generated by the microswimmers shown in

1
must exhibit oscillatory components with sizes as small as
6
µN
over the range of
0
to
1.0

. Therefore, the sensor we presented in

]
—with a resolution of

µN
—is not well suited for the task at hand. This limitation prompted us to improve the resolution of existing

6.85

4
(a)

4
(a)
, a static mapping between the force value,
F
—defined as the signed magnitude of the corresponding vector force—and the displacement of the structure’s target plate,
\delta
, follows Hooke’s law,
\delta=F/k_{\text{tot}}
, where the elastic constant can be calculated as
k_{\text{tot}}=24EI/l_{\text{b}}^{3}
. In this description,

is the Young modulus of the structure’s material (

36
);

is the length of the DCS, as defined in

(a)
; and,

is the moment of inertia of each composing beam’s

, where

is the width of the beam, as defined in Fig.
4
(a), and

is the thickness of the Invar-
36
material. Using basic beam theory, the DCS can be modeled as an equivalent

\displaystyle\left|\delta(\omega_{0})\right|=\frac{F_{0}}{k_{\text{tot}}\left[\eta^{2}+(1-\omega_{0}^{2}/\omega_{\text{n}}^{2})^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}},

(9)

in which

is a known constant amplitude;

is a known constant frequency;

is a hysteretic loss coefficient used to account for energy losses, provided by the manufacturer of the

36

is the undamped natural frequency of the equivalent dynamics of the structure, with the equivalent mass

. During development, through an iterative design process, we kept the sensor dimensions reported in
[

]

Hz
is shown in

. As seen, the natural frequency of the system is

V
Conclusion

We presented a study of the characteristics of the thrust generated by two newly developed
insect-scale
propulsors that leverage the FSI between unactuated soft tails and their surrounding fluid to function. The first actuator studied is
single-tailed
, and the second is double-tailed. In this approach, a driving actuator generates a wave that travels along a tail to produce hydrodynamic forces useful for locomotion. To describe the mechanism of thrust generation, we performed an analysis based on reactive theory that provides insights for the design and development of
high-performance

insect-scale
propulsors. To experimentally measure the instantaneous thrust generated by the two studied devices during static tests, we designed and fabricated a
high-sensitivity

µN-resolution
force sensor. Employing this sensor, we discovered that the tested
single-tail
propulsor generates relatively small
cycle-averaged
force values, with the best case being
2.97
µN
, which occurs at low actuation frequencies (
\leq 2
Hz
); by comparison, we measured
cycle-averaged
force values of up to
22.6
µN
for the
dual-tail
propulsor.

Interestingly, we also found that, at respectively
0.45
mN
and
0.61
mN
, the measured peak force values for the
single-tail
and
dual-tail
cases are very similar. Considering these data, we hypothesize that the
dual-tail
propulsor generates a significantly higher average thrust due to the interaction between the wake structures created by its two composing soft tails. This hypothesis is consistent with the finding that the
reactive-force
model presented in
Section
II
estimates force values similar to those measured for the
single-tail
case; in contrast, a noticeable mismatch between the modeled and experimentally obtained propulsion forces occurs in the
dual-tail
case. This discrepancy is most likely due to the omission of the convective term in the formulation of (
6
), which seems to be necessary to account for interaction between wake structures. In the near future, we will refine the
reactive-force
model presented in
Section
II
to account for
wake-structure
phenomena. Aligned with this objective, we will improve our understanding of the dynamics relating actuation output with thrust for locomotion, which is essential for developing advanced control schemes for
insect-scale
aquatic robots.

Appendix: Reactive-Force Model

In this appendix, we review the fundamental ideas introduced by Lighthill in
[
25
]
to model the total reactive force acting on a slender undulating tail. Here, we start by finding the material acceleration of a parcel of fluid at
\left(s,t\right)
as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{a}(s,t)=\frac{D\boldsymbol{v}}{Dt}=a_{\text{n}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{n}}+a_{\text{t}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{t}},

(10)

with

\displaystyle\begin{split}a_{\text{n}}(s,t)&=\frac{Dv_{\text{n}}}{Dt}+v_{\text{t}}\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial t}+v_{\text{t}}^{2}\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial s},\\
a_{\text{t}}(s,t)&=\frac{Dv_{\text{t}}}{Dt}-v_{\text{n}}\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial t}-v_{\text{n}}v_{\text{t}}\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial s},\end{split}

(11)

where
\theta(s,t)
is the local tangent angle of the tail centerline from
\boldsymbol{b}_{1}
. Next, we invoke Newton’s second and third laws to obtain the hydrodynamic force per unit length exerted by the fluid on the tail,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hskip-15.49994pt\boldsymbol{f}(s,t)&=-m(s)\boldsymbol{a}(s,t)\\
&=-m(s)\left[\left(\frac{Dv_{\text{n}}}{Dt}+v_{\text{t}}\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial t}+v_{\text{t}}^{2}\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial s}\right)\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{n}}\right.\\
&\left.\hskip 30.99988pt+\left(\frac{Dv_{\text{t}}}{Dt}-v_{\text{n}}\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial t}-v_{\text{n}}v_{\text{t}}\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial s}\right)\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{t}}\right],\end{split}

(12)

where
m(s)
is the added mass per unit length, which in this case is specified by (
4
). Additionally, we consider that (i) the motion of the tail’s centerline is confined to the
\boldsymbol{b}_{1}
–
\boldsymbol{b}_{2}
; (ii)
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}
does not rotate relative to
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{N}}
; (iii) the local curvature defined by
\theta(s,t)
, and its variations over
s
and
t
are small; and, following Lighthill’s assumptions, (iv) reactive forces arise from the normal acceleration of the displaced fluid mass and act perpendicularly on the tail surface. Directly from these assumptions it follows that the reactive force per unit length is given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{f}_{\text{r}}(s,t)=-m(s)\boldsymbol{a}_{\text{n}}(s,t)=-m(s)\frac{Dv_{\text{n}}}{Dt}\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{n}}(s,t),

(13)

in which the material derivative of the normal velocity component,
v_{\text{n}}
, satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{Dv_{\text{n}}}{Dt}=\frac{\partial v_{\text{n}}}{\partial t}+\frac{ds}{dt}\frac{\partial v_{\text{n}}}{\partial s}=\frac{\partial v_{\text{n}}}{\partial t}+v_{\text{t}}\frac{\partial v_{\text{n}}}{\partial s}.

(14)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{f}_{\text{r}}(s,t)=-m(s)\left(\frac{\partial v_{\text{n}}}{\partial t}+v_{\text{t}}\frac{\partial v_{\text{n}}}{\partial s}\right)\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{n}}(s,t),

(15)

which allows us to compute the total instantaneous reactive force acting on the tail as specified by (
5
).
