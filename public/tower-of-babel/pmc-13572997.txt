Real-world effectiveness of fluticasone propionate–azelastine nasal spray in moderate-to-severe nonallergic and perennial allergic rhinitis

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough combined intranasal corticosteroid–antihistamine sprays are approved for allergic rhinitis, evidence supporting their effectiveness in nonallergic rhinitis (NAR) remains limited.ObjectiveTo compare the treatment response to fluticasone propionate–azelastine (FP-Aze) for treating NAR and perennial allergic rhinitis (PAR).MethodsThis prospective observational real-world study enrolled adults with moderate-to-severe PAR or NAR. Participants received FP–Aze nasal spray (50/137 μg), 1 spray per nostril twice daily for 4 weeks. The primary endpoint was the change from baseline in the visual analog scale (VAS) for rhinitis, ranging from 0 to 100 mm. Treatment effectiveness in NAR was assessed for noninferiority, with a prespecified margin of 23 mm on the VAS relative to PAR. Secondary outcomes included evaluation of non-nasal symptoms and quality of life (QoL).ResultsPatients with moderate-to-severe PAR (n = 108) and NAR (n = 62) were included. FP-Aze significantly improved nasal symptoms in both groups. At Week 4, adjusted mean changes (95% CI) in VAS for rhinitis were −52.20 mm (−57.37, −47.03) in PAR and −48.59 mm (−55.64, −41.54) in NAR. The between-group difference in mean change was 3.61 mm (−5.58, 12.79), meeting the noninferiority criterion. QoL improved significantly in both groups, with no significant between-group differences. Sensitivity analyses excluding patients who used as-needed oral antihistamines yielded consistent results. Treatment was well tolerated, with a bitter taste as the most commonly reported adverse event; no serious adverse events were observed.ConclusionThe effectiveness of FP-Aze in moderate-to-severe NAR was noninferior to PAR on the VAS for rhinitis, supporting its use in this population. These findings help address an important evidence gap, as data supporting FP-Aze in NAR remain limited.

Introduction

In a population-based adult cohort, moderate-to-severe disease was reported in 40% of patients with allergic rhinitis (AR) and 24% of those with nonallergic rhinitis (NAR).1 Increasing AR severity was associated with marked deterioration in quality of life (QoL).2 Importantly, patients with NAR experienced a comparable degree of QoL impairment to those with AR,3,4 suggesting an urgent need for effective treatment in clinical practice.

Current guidelines recommend combined intranasal corticosteroid and intranasal antihistamine (INCS–INAH) for moderate-to-severe persistent AR, particularly when symptom control with INCS alone is inadequate.5,6 Two INCS–INAH products, fluticasone propionate–azelastine (FP-Aze) and mometasone furoate–olopatadine (MF-Olo), are approved by the U.S. FDA for seasonal AR (SAR),7,8 but not yet for perennial AR (PAR). A network meta-analysis indirectly compared the 2 agents and found no significant difference in their treatment effects.9 Only 1 randomized controlled trial (RCT) has demonstrated the superior efficacy of MF-Olo in PAR compared with placebo,10 and no clinical studies have yet assessed FP-Aze in this population.

Symptoms of NAR are usually present year-round and resemble those of PAR. NAR encompasses various subtypes, including drug-induced rhinitis, rhinitis in the elderly, hormonal rhinitis, occupational rhinitis, and gustatory rhinitis.6,11 Up to half of cases lack an apparent trigger and are classified as idiopathic or vasomotor rhinitis, which features increased nasal hyperreactivity (NHR).12 INCS-INAH offers dual mechanisms: corticosteroids reduce inflammation in specific NAR subtypes,13 while antihistamines block transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) receptors to decrease NHR.14 Consequently, INCS-INAH has emerged as a potential treatment option for NAR. Although current guideline recommendations, mainly based on expert opinion, endorse INCS-INAH for moderate-to-severe persistent NAR,6 no clinical studies have evaluated its effectiveness in this population.

In this prospective observational study, we compared the treatment response to FP-Aze in patients with moderate-to-severe NAR and PAR. The primary outcome was improvement in symptoms, assessed using the visual analog scale (VAS) for rhinitis. Secondary outcomes included improvement in individual nasal and non-nasal symptoms, QoL, adherence, and adverse events (AEs) associated with the study drug.

Methods

Study design

This prospective observational real-world study recruited adult participants with moderate-to-severe PAR and NAR from 2 tertiary care hospitals between January 2022 and December 2024. The study flow diagram is illustrated in Fig. 1. The study was conducted in accordance with the STROBE15 (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) reporting guidelines (Table E1) and was approved by the Multi-Faculty Cooperative Institutional Review Board. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to study entry. This prospective observational study was conducted according to a prespecified protocol but was not registered in a clinical trial registry because no study-specific intervention or treatment allocation was performed.Fig. 1The study flow diagram. Participants

Eligible participants were 18 years of age or older with a history of PAR or NAR for more than 6 months and with moderate-to-severe disease, defined by sleep disturbance, impairment of daily activities, academic or work-related difficulties, or other bothersome symptoms. Participants who received a fixed-dose combination of FP–Aze (50/137 μg), administered as 1 spray per nostril twice daily for 4 weeks, were included. Use of as-needed second-generation oral antihistamines once daily as rescue medication during the study was permitted.

Exclusion criteria included participants with mild rhinitis, SAR, rhinosinusitis, nasal polyps, clinically significant nasal septal deviation, and pregnancy or lactation. Participants who had received allergen immunotherapy, used systemic corticosteroids within 8 weeks, used oral or nasal decongestants within 7 days, used INCS within 4 weeks, used oral or intranasal H1 antihistamines within 10 days, or used antileukotrienes within 4 weeks before enrollment were also excluded. All washout periods were prespecified in the study protocol and applied prospectively at enrollment.

All participants underwent standardized clinical interviews and physical examinations by study physicians. Disease severity was classified according to the standard guidelines.6,16 Symptom triggers related to aeroallergens (house dust mites, cockroaches, pets, and pollens) and nonspecific irritants or pollutants (strong odors, vehicle exhaust, air pollution, and tobacco smoke) were systematically assessed. Skin prick testing (SPT) for common aeroallergens was performed unless valid results were available within the preceding 12 months. For patients with negative SPT results or findings inconsistent with their clinical presentation, serum-specific immunoglobulin E (sIgE) testing was performed using the ImmunoCAP system (Phadia, Uppsala, Sweden), with values > 0.35 kUA/L considered positive.

Patients with rhinitis symptoms associated with exposure to at least 1 indoor allergen and confirmed positive SPT or sIgE results were classified as having PAR. Patients with negative SPT and sIgE results were classified as having NAR. To further characterize NAR, detailed clinical histories were obtained to identify potential triggers, including symptom exacerbation during workdays, after meals, during hormonal changes, or in association with endocrine disorders. Concomitant medications potentially associated with drug-induced rhinitis were carefully assessed. Based on clinical history and rhinoscopic findings, patients were categorized as having senile rhinitis, occupational rhinitis, rhinitis medicamentosa, drug-induced rhinitis, hormonal rhinitis, smoking-related rhinitis, or gustatory rhinitis, in accordance with the EAACI position paper11 and established diagnostic algorithms.17 Patients with symptoms triggered exclusively by nonspecific stimuli without an identifiable cause were classified as having idiopathic or vasomotor rhinitis.18

Outcome assessment

The primary outcome was the mean change from baseline in the VAS for rhinitis after 4 weeks of FP–Aze treatment in patients with PAR and NAR. Rhinitis symptom severity was assessed using a 100-mm VAS, with 0 indicating no symptoms and 100 the most severe symptoms. Similar VAS assessments were used to evaluate conjunctivitis and associated symptoms, including postnasal drip, nasal voice, facial pressure, hyposmia, and cough. Individual nasal symptoms (nasal congestion, nasal itching, sneezing, and rhinorrhea) and ocular itching were rated on a 4-point scale from 0 (absent) to 3 (severe). The total nasal symptom score (TNSS) was calculated as the sum of the 4 individual nasal symptom scores, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 12. Participants recorded their nasal and ocular symptoms daily and their associated symptoms at baseline and week 4.

Health-related QoL was assessed at baseline and at week 4 using the validated Rhinoconjunctivitis Quality of Life Questionnaire (RCQ-36).19 The RCQ-36 comprises 36 items across 6 domains (symptoms, physical functioning, role limitations, sleep, social functioning, and emotions), with 2 additional items assessing general health status and absenteeism. Total scores range from 34 to 170, with lower scores indicating better QoL. Further details on the RCQ-36 domains are provided in Table E2.

Participants recorded daily adherence to the study drug and AEs throughout the study. Adherence was verified by comparing the observed reduction in device weight at week 4 with the expected weight reduction for each participant.

Sample size estimation and statistical analysis

Sample size calculations were based on the primary endpoint, the change from baseline in the VAS for rhinitis. The effectiveness of FP–Aze in NAR was prespecified as noninferior to its efficacy in PAR, with a noninferiority margin of 23 mm on the VAS for rhinitis. This margin corresponds to the previously reported minimal clinically important difference (MCID).20 Based on an assumed standard deviation (SD) of 20 mm, derived from Wongsa et al,4 which enrolled both PAR and NAR patients, the same SD was considered applicable to both groups in the present study. Allowing for a 5% loss to follow-up, a total sample size of 60 patients (30 per group) was required to provide 99% power to demonstrate noninferiority using a one-sided significance level of α = 0.025. The minimum required sample size of 60 patients (30 per group) represented a statistical threshold rather than a target enrollment cap. Accordingly, all consecutive eligible patients presenting during the study period were enrolled. The final distribution of PAR and NAR patients reflected the case mix encountered in routine tertiary care practice.

All analyses were conducted in STATA version 17 (StataCorp, College Station, TX). Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were assessed for normality and summarized as means with SD or medians with interquartile ranges, as appropriate. Between-group comparisons were conducted using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, the Student’s t-test for normally distributed continuous variables, and the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed variables.

Primary and secondary outcomes were analyzed using multilevel linear models with an unstructured variance–covariance matrix to account for within-participant correlation across repeated follow-up visits. Fixed effects included diagnosis group, baseline VAS for rhinitis, follow-up visit, and their three-way interaction (diagnosis group × baseline VAS for rhinitis × follow-up visit). A two-level random-intercept and random-slope model was specified, with participant-level random intercepts and participant-level random slopes for the follow-up visit. To support comparability and estimate the association of diagnosis group with the outcomes, models were adjusted for prespecified potential confounders, including age, age at disease onset, disease duration, baseline VAS for conjunctivitis, presence of allergic conjunctivitis, exposure to non-specific irritants and pollutants, and prior medication use. These covariates were prespecified based on clinical relevance and their potential to influence rhinitis symptom severity and treatment response.1 To evaluate the non-inferiority of the primary outcome, we used the upper limit of the 95% confidence interval (CI) for the between-group difference in the mean change in the VAS for rhinitis. Due to the prospective design and comprehensive data collection protocols of this study, there were no missing values for daily symptoms and potential prognostic factors. Consequently, complete-case data from all enrolled patients were available and included in the final analysis.

Prespecified subgroup analyses were performed according to baseline VAS for rhinitis (<70 vs ≥ 70 mm), and sensitivity analyses excluded patients who used as-needed oral antihistamines during the study period. All secondary endpoints and subgroup analyses are exploratory and hypothesis-generating. No adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied to secondary or subgroup analyses.

Results

Of 170 enrolled participants, all completed the 4-week follow-up visit (completion rate: 100%; Figure E1). Therefore, the analysis population included all enrolled participants. A total of 108 PAR and 62 NAR patients treated with FP–Aze were included in the final analysis. Among moderate-to-severe NAR cases, idiopathic (vasomotor) rhinitis predominated (85.5%), with smaller proportions of occupational (8.1%), drug-induced (4.8%), and gustatory rhinitis (1.6%) (Figure E2).

Baseline characteristics of recruited participants

Table 1 shows the demographic and baseline characteristics of patients with PAR and with NAR. Patients with PAR were younger, had earlier disease onset, and experienced a longer disease duration than those with NAR. Most patients reported persistent symptoms, with no differences between the groups. Patients with PAR exhibited significantly higher mean scores than those with NAR for nasal itching, rhinorrhea, eye itching, TNSS, and VAS for postnasal drip, while the nasal congestion score was borderline different, and sneezing scores were similar. In contrast, VAS for rhinitis, VAS for conjunctivitis, and VAS for other associated symptoms (nasal voice, facial pressure, hyposmia, and cough) were comparable between the groups. House dust mites were the most frequently identified aeroallergen among patients with PAR. Patients sensitized to pet allergens owned pets during the study and reported clinically relevant symptoms upon exposure. All PAR patients with grass pollen sensitization had concomitant sensitization to indoor allergens and did not report seasonal symptoms. Non-specific irritant triggers were commonly reported in both groups, with no significant between-group differences. Comorbid conjunctivitis and atopic dermatitis were more common in PAR, while asthma rates were similar between groups. Prior to the study, INCS use was comparable, whereas oral antihistamine use was more common among PAR patients. During the study, oral antihistamine use was comparable between the 2 groups. Adherence to FP-Aze was high and similar across both groups.

Effectiveness of FP-Aze in patients with PAR and NAR

Table 2 summarizes changes in symptom scores following FP–Aze treatment. For the primary endpoint, VAS for rhinitis improved substantially in both groups, with adjusted mean changes (95% CI) at week 4 of −52.20 mm (−57.37, −47.03) in the PAR group and −48.59 mm (−55.64, −41.54) in the NAR group. The adjusted between-group difference in mean change was 3.61 mm (−5.58, 12.79). Since the upper bound of the 95% CI (12.79 mm) falls below the prespecified noninferiority margin of 23 mm, noninferiority is demonstrated (Fig. 2). The upper bound also falls within the 75% MCID margin (17 mm) but marginally exceeds the 50% MCID margin (12 mm); however, the sensitivity analysis modeling baseline severity as a continuous variable Among patients with NAR, baseline VAS for rhinitis was higher in the VAS ≥70 group than in the VAS <70 group (82.52 vs 49.06 mm). The between-stratum gap in absolute VAS for rhinitis narrowed from 33.45 mm at baseline to 12.64 mm at week 4, but the strata did not converge: the VAS ≥70 group retained a higher absolute VAS for rhinitis at week 4 than the VAS <70 group (Table E5). Subgroup analyses excluding patients using as-needed oral antihistamines

Improvement in symptom scores

Consistent with the primary analysis, FP–Aze treatment was associated with improvements in individual nasal symptom scores, eye itching, and TNSS in both PAR and NAR patients who were not taking oral antihistamines. The magnitude of improvement was consistently greater in patients with PAR (Table E7). In contrast, changes in VAS for rhinitis (Figure E3) and VAS for conjunctivitis were similar between groups, mirroring the primary findings. Likewise, when stratified by baseline VAS severity (Table E8), PAR patients with VAS ≥70 demonstrated significantly greater improvements in most individual nasal symptoms, TNSS, and VAS for rhinitis (Figure E4) than those with VAS <70. Changes in eye itching scores and VAS for conjunctivitis did not differ significantly across severity strata at most time points. In contrast, severity-related differences were modest in NAR patients.

Improvement in quality of life

Among patients not using oral antihistamines, improvements from baseline in RCQ-36 scores were observed in both PAR and NAR (Table E9). As in the primary analysis, improvements in total and most domain-specific RCQ-36 scores were comparable between groups, except for greater improvements in the rhinitis symptom and sleep domains in PAR. Similarly, PAR patients with higher baseline severity experienced greater improvements in total and most domain-specific RCQ-36 scores than those with lower severity. In contrast, severity-related differences were less evident among patients with NAR (Table E10).

Adverse events

Table E11 summarizes treatment-related adverse events (AEs) during FP–Aze therapy. Overall, bitter taste was the most commonly reported AE (82.4%), followed by postnasal drip, throat irritation, and epistaxis. These AEs were generally mild and did not lead to treatment discontinuation. No serious AEs were recorded during the study period.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of the real-world effectiveness of FP-Aze in patients with PAR and NAR. Overall, FP-Aze was associated with comparable improvements in symptoms and QoL in both phenotypes. The observed difference in magnitude of response is consistent with underlying pathophysiological and demographic differences and should not be interpreted as a causal treatment-effect differential. Sensitivity analyses excluding patients who used as-needed oral antihistamines (34.7% of participants) yielded results consistent with the primary analysis, indicating that concomitant antihistamine use was unlikely to have materially influenced the observed treatment effects. The most frequently reported AE was a bitter taste, and none led to treatment discontinuation.

The potential role of combined FP-Aze therapy in the treatment of NAR is of considerable interest. Banov et al.21 demonstrated the efficacy of azelastine in patients with vasomotor rhinitis, also referred to as idiopathic NAR, leading to its approval by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for this indication.22 A subsequent mechanistic study has shown that azelastine reduces NHR by inhibiting TRPV1 receptors, a key pathophysiologic pathway in NAR.14 This mechanism is reflected in the substantial treatment response to FP–Aze observed in our NAR patients, who were predominantly diagnosed with vasomotor rhinitis, a subtype in which NHR plays a central role and in which inflammation is relatively limited. The modest severity-related differences in treatment response observed in NAR patients may reflect statistical artifacts, including regression to the mean and limited subgroup sample sizes, as much as true pathophysiological heterogeneity. This interpretation is therefore speculative and requires prospective validation in adequately powered studies. Given the association between symptom severity and irritant burden,23 prospective studies incorporating validated irritant burden assessments, such as structured questionnaires quantifying nonspecific trigger frequency and intensity, alongside objective measures of NHR (eg, capsaicin or cold dry air provocation) are needed to evaluate irritant-induced symptom burden as a clinical predictor of FP-Aze response in NAR.

Fluticasone propionate has demonstrated effectiveness in NAR, with and without nasal eosinophilia.24 The U.S. FDA approves FP for the treatment of NAR, as well as PAR and SAR.25 Notably, evidence indicated that INCS was effective primarily in selected NAR subtypes characterized by eosinophilic or mast cell–predominant inflammation, with limited benefit in neutrophil-predominant disease.13 Consequently, treatment responses to INCS monotherapy may vary in general NAR populations, as reported by the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews.26 Nevertheless, given the limited use of nasal cytology in clinical practice, treating patients with moderate-to-severe NAR with FP-Aze, which reduces both inflammation and NHR, offers a broad-spectrum, pragmatic therapeutic option consistent with the Rhinitis 2020 guideline recommendation.6

Previous RCTs showed that FP-Aze was superior to either agent alone in SAR, with limited evidence in PAR.27, 28, 29 Our study extends the existing literature by demonstrating the effectiveness of FP-Aze in patients with PAR. Notably, greater treatment benefit was observed among our PAR patients with higher baseline severity (VAS ≥70 mm), a threshold previously reported to differentiate moderate from severe disease.30 Given that a substantial proportion of our patients with PAR reported nonallergic triggers, consistent with a mixed rhinitis phenotype, azelastine may also have a role in targeting NHR, in addition to the FP effect on allergic inflammation.14,31 However, diagnosing mixed rhinitis should be based on validated diagnostic tools rather than solely on the presence of a limited number of nonallergic triggers.23 Our findings indicate that FP–Aze could be considered a first-line treatment option for patients with severe AR (VAS ≥70), a threshold associated with suboptimal responses to INCS monotherapy.32 This approach is consistent with current clinical practice guidelines, which favor combined INCS-INAH sprays over INCS monotherapy for moderate-to-severe AR.6,33

The VAS for rhinitis, used as the primary outcome in this study, has been established to measure symptom severity and its association with QoL in PAR and NAR.4 After treatment with FP–Aze, reductions in VAS for rhinitis from baseline exceeded the MCID of 23 mm20 in both the PAR and NAR groups. This clinically meaningful improvement was maintained in a subgroup analysis excluding patients on as-needed oral antihistamines. Both PAR and NAR achieved clinically meaningful improvements in QoL, with RCQ-36 score reductions exceeding the MCID of 10 points.34 However, because QoL noninferiority was not prespecified and the upper bound of the 95% CI for the between-group difference (10.96 points) slightly exceeded the MCID, a clinically meaningful QoL disadvantage for NAR cannot be formally excluded. Consequently, the apparent similarity in QoL improvement between groups should be interpreted with caution and regarded as hypothesis-generating.

Strengths of our study include a direct comparison of PAR and NAR, both with persistent year-round symptoms, which minimized confounding by seasonal variation in symptom assessment, and the use of predefined criteria for moderate-to-severe disease, which reflect a typical real-world population and enhance clinical relevance.2 Some limitations should also be acknowledged. First, as a real-world study without a placebo control group, it precludes separating the drug effect from the placebo response and natural history. Nevertheless, the sufficient sample size, prospective follow-up, and use of validated patient-reported outcome measures strengthen the validity of our conclusions. Second, mixed rhinitis was not formally confirmed in PAR patients, limiting interpretation of the extent to which nonallergic mechanisms, including NHR, contributed to the observed treatment response. In addition, because nasal provocation testing and nasal cytology were not performed, local allergic rhinitis and nonallergic rhinitis with eosinophilia syndrome (NARES) could not be excluded among patients classified as having NAR. Consequently, the study may have compared clinically heterogeneous and partially overlapping phenotypes, limiting mechanistic interpretation of treatment responses and the generalizability of our findings. Third, although prespecified confounders were included in the multilevel models, residual confounding from unmeasured variables cannot be excluded, an inherent limitation of observational study design. Further well-designed RCTs are warranted to evaluate the comparative efficacy of FP–Aze versus FP or azelastine monotherapy and placebo in patients with NAR.

In conclusion, this prospective observational study demonstrates that FP-Aze was associated with clinically meaningful, well-tolerated real-world treatment responses in both moderate-to-severe PAR and NAR. These findings support the use of FP-Aze in NAR and fill a significant evidence gap, as prior studies have primarily focused on the efficacy of FP–Aze in patients with AR. Nevertheless, the generalizability of our findings may be limited by the tertiary-care setting, the predominance of idiopathic/vasomotor rhinitis among NAR patients, the predominantly female study population, and the use of a QoL instrument developed and validated in a Thai population.
