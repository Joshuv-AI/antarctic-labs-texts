Association of metabolic score for visceral fat with cardiovascular risk and total mortality in patients with type 2 diabetes: insights from ACCORD study
Authors: Peiqi Tang, Ying Xin, Maojun Liu, Cheng Zeng, Xinqun Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the association between METS-VF and cardiovascular outcomes as well as total mortality (TM) in type 2 diabetes.MethodsParticipants were drawn from the Action to Control Cardiovascular Risk in Diabetes (ACCORD) study. Cox proportional hazards regression analyses and Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were employed to assess the relationship between METS-VF and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) and TM. Additionally, the incremental predictive value of METS-VF was evaluated.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 8.82 years, 1,914 participants (18.96%) died, including 656(6.49%) from cardiovascular causes. The study proved higher METS-VF were significantly positively associated with MACEs and TM in Cox models. RCS analyses revealed a J-shaped non-linear relationship between METS-VF and both MACEs and TM, with inflection points of 6.62 and 7.31, respectively. Above these inflection points, METS-VF was positively correlated with MACEs (HR 1.70, 95% CI 1.27–2.27) and TM (HR 2.04, 95% CI 1.37–3.03). Stratified analysis identified that METS-VF had a significant predictive value for MACEs particularly in patients with type 2 diabetes who are not taking aspirin.ConclusionsMETS-VF is significantly associated with elevated risk of MACEs and TM in patients with type 2 diabetes, exhibiting a clear inflection point. These findings indicate that METS-VF may serve as a robust biomarker for predicting the risk of cardiovascular events in patients with type 2 diabetes, underscoring the importance of managing visceral obesity.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a chronic metabolic disease characterized by abnormal elevation of blood glucose levels, and it has become a worldwide epidemic and major health burden, especially type 2 diabetes [1]. A large number of studies show a strong association between diabetes and cardiovascular disease [2, 3], with cardiovascular disease (CVD) being the leading cause of morbidity and mortality in patients with type 2 diabetes [4] .Many models have been developed to predict the risk of CVD in patients with diabetes, but the utility of most predictive models remains to be examined due to the lack of extensive simulations and external clinical validation [5]. Therefore, it is crucial to find a reliable indicator for early prediction of total mortality and CVD in patients with diabetes.

As the rate of obesity continues to rise, the risk of type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease-related deaths caused by obesity is also increasing [6]. Studies have shown that the distribution of adipose tissue is a key factor in determining an individual’s risk of obesity-related metabolic and cardiovascular complications [7].Further evidence shows that the harmful effects of visceral fat accumulation are much greater than those of subcutaneous fat [8].At the same time, accumulation of visceral adipose tissue (VAT) is considered an independent risk factor for both CVD and type 2 diabetes [9–11]. Imaging techniques such as dual X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) and bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) are commonly used to assess body composition. However, their accuracy in providing specific information about adipose tissue distribution remains limited [12].In clinical settings, the gold standards for measuring visceral obesity include MRI, CT, and DXA [13]. However, these methods have some limitations. They are expensive, require specialized equipment, and may expose participants to radiation. Professional interpretation is also needed, which limits their use.

The Metabolic Score for Visceral Fat (METS-VF) is derived from the Metabolic Score for Insulin Resistance (METS-IR), waist-to-height ratio (WHtr), age, and sex. It is an indicator that quantifies visceral adipose tissue (VAT). Compared to other visceral fat indicators, magnetic resonance imaging (the gold standard for assessing visceral fat), and other imaging techniques such as computed tomography, dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, and bioelectrical impedance analysis, the METS-VF shows better performance [14].Recent studies have also emphasized the association between METS-VF and type 2 diabetes as well as cardiovascular disease [14–17].Nowadays, there are still insufficient studies on the association between METS-VF and CVD in Western populations, particularly among patients with type 2 diabetes. This study aims to analyze the association of METS-VF with total mortality and cardiovascular risk in Western populations with type 2 diabetes.

Materials and methods

Study population

This study used data from the ACCORD study. The basic principles, design, and main results of the ACCORD study have been reported previously [18–20]. Between June 2001 and October 2005, participants were recruited for the ACCORD trial at 77 sites in the United States and Canada. A total of 10,251 participants were enrolled in the ACCORD trial, with an average age of 62 years. The median duration of type 2 diabetes was 10 years. The mean hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) level was 8.3%. All participants had either a history of cardiovascular disease or cardiovascular risk factors. Subsequently, all surviving ACCORD participants had the opportunity to enroll in the ACCORDION study, which collected and analyzed data on cardiovascular and other health-related outcomes and measures between May 2011 and October 2014. The design of the ACCORD/ACCORDION study was approved by Wake Forest University (coordinating center) and the Institutional Review Boards of each participating clinical site. All participants signed written informed consent. After excluding individuals with missing values for fasting glucose, triglycerides, HDL-c, height, weight, sex, age, or waist circumference (N = 158), and those missing cardiovascular outcome events (N = 0), the final analysis included 10,093 participants (Fig. 1).

Data collection and outcomes

Data were collected on participants’ demographics (age, sex, race, education, and living status), physical examinations, and laboratory blood tests. In this study, the primary outcome was the incidence of MACEs, including non-fatal myocardial infarction (MI), non-fatal stroke, and death from cardiovascular causes. The secondary outcome was total mortality. The research team used a combination of phone follow-up and outpatient visits to systematically track participants every 2 to 4 months. At each follow-up, the researchers recorded detailed medical information about the participants. Major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) were strictly reviewed and confirmed by a dedicated morbidity and mortality committee.

Assessment of METS-VF

METS-VF was calculated using the following formula: METS-VF=3.239✕[Ln (WHtr)]3+0.011✕[Ln (METS-IR)]3+0.319✕sex (male = 1, female = 0)+4.466 + 0.594✕[Ln (Age) (year)] (Age was recorded in years. Sex was treated as a binary variable, with 1 for male and 0 for female).The metabolic score for insulin resistance (METS-IR) was calculated using the following formula: METS-IR= (Ln ((2✕Glu+TG)✕BMI)]/Ln (HDL-c). In addition, waist-to-height ratio (WHtr) is calculated as WHtr=waist circumference (cm)/height (cm).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA), R (The R Foundation, Vienna, Austria) and Empower Stats (X&Y Solutions, Inc., Boston, USA). Participants were divided into four groups according to the quartiles of their METS-VF levels. Baseline characteristics were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), frequency and percentage, median, or interquartile range, depending on the data distribution. Continuous variables were compared using analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal-Wallis test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate the hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the association between METS-VF and MACEs and TM. Multivariable models were then used to adjust for potential confounders. Model 1 was adjusted for sex, age. Model 2 was adjusted for sex, age, race, history of cardiovascular disease, live alone, previous hypertension, previous hyperlipidemia, duration of diabetes, proteinuria, heart failure, depression, EDU, and smoking status. Model 3 was further adjusted for BMI, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, HbA1c, total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, eGFR, alanine aminotransferase, use of calcium channel blockers, Beta blockers, sulfonylurea, biguanides, thiazolidinediones, insulin, aspirin, and statins.

Kaplan-Meier survival curves were used to visualize changes in survival rates over time. The survival curves were estimated based on the time of outcome events for the patients. The log-rank test was then used to compare these survival curves. Stratified analyses were conducted to assess the interaction effects of potential confounding categorical variables, including sex, age, race, history of cardiovascular disease, previous hypertension, glycemic control, use of aspirin, and other relevant variables. Subgroup analyses were performed using Cox regression. If the p-value for interaction was not significant, the results across different strata were considered consistent and reliable. Conversely, a significant p-value indicated that there were different effects in specific subgroups. Multivariable models with three levels of progressive adjustment were used to control for potential confounders of MACEs. In addition, we used the C statistic, net reclassification improvement (NRI), and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) to assess the incremental predictive value of METS-VF beyond conventional risk factors.

Results

Baseline characteristics according to quartiles of METS-VF

A total of 10,093 participants were included at baseline. The mean age was 62.80 ± 6.65 years. Among them, 61.49% were male and 38.51% were female. The mean METS-VF was 7.38 ± 0.41. Participants were divided into four groups based on quartiles of baseline METS-VF (Table 1). Individuals in the higher quartiles of METS-VF were more likely to be male and predominantly White. They also had more frequent smoking behaviors. Additionally, they had higher rates of previous cardiovascular events, depression, hypertension, proteinuria, and heart failure. Participants in the higher quartiles also had higher BMI, ALT, TG, and fasting blood glucose (FBG) levels. Conversely, their TC, LDL, HDL-C, HbA1c, and eGFR levels were lower. They were more likely to take beta-blockers, statins, insulin, biguanides, and thiazolidinediones. They were less likely to use cholesterol absorption inhibitors and meglitinides.

Association of METS-VF with maces and TM

During a median follow-up of 8.82 years, 1,791 (17.74%) MACEs were identified, including 656 (6.49%) CVD mortality, 924 (9.15%) non-fatal MI, and 476 (4.71%) non-fatal strokes. In total, 1,914 participants (18.96%) died. To illustrate outcomes among patients with different METS-VF levels, we generated Kaplan-Meier survival curves (Fig. 2). As shown in the figure, the incidence of MACEs increased sharply with higher METS-VF quartiles. Similarly, the risks of CVD mortality, non-fatal MI, and non-fatal stroke also increased with higher METS-VF quartiles.

Kaplan-Meier curves for MACEs and total mortality based on quartiles of baseline METS-VF. (A) MACEs; (B) CVD mortality; (C) Non-fatal MI; (D) Non-fatal stroke; (E) Total mortality

In addition, we performed Cox regression analyses for total mortality and MACEs (including CVD mortality, non-fatal MI, and non-fatal stroke) (Table 2). In the fully adjusted continuous model, each one-unit increase in METS-VF was associated with a 37% higher risk of MACEs in the overall population(HR = 1.37, 95%CI : 1.06–1.77). Notably, each unit increase in METS-VF was associated with a 2.14-fold higher risk of non-fatal stroke (HR = 2.14, 95% CI: 1.28–3.57). In the fully adjusted categorical model, we also found a significant positive correlation between METS-VF and both MACEs and total mortality (METS-VF-MACEs: p = 0.0210; METS-VF-TM: p = 0.0024).

The potential nonlinear relationships between METS-VF and maces as well as TM

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) model was used to analyze the potential nonlinear relationships between METS-VF and the outcomes. The RCS results showed a clear nonlinear association for both MACEs (p -nonlinear = 0.012) and total mortality (TM) (p-nonlinear = 0.004) (Fig. 3). Interestingly, although there was a clear nonlinear relationship between METS-VF and the incidence of MACEs, further analysis showed that this nonlinear association was significant only for CVD mortality (p-nonlinear = 0.005), and it was not significant for non-fatal MI and non-fatal stroke (p > 0.05) (Fig. 4). The inflection points for MACEs and TM were identified as 6.62 and 7.31, respectively, using a recursive method. The effect sizes and confidence intervals on either side of the inflection points were determined by a two-piece Cox proportional hazards regression model (Table 3). When baseline METS-VF was less than 6.62, there was negative association between METS-VF and the risk of MACEs (HR: 0.48, 95% CI: 0.25–0.92). However, when baseline METS-VF was 6.62 or higher, METS-VF was positively associated with the risk of MACEs (HR: 1.70, 95% CI: 1.27–2.27). When baseline METS-VF was less than 7.31, there was no significant association between METS-VF and TM (HR: 1.01, 95% CI: 0.77–1.32). However, when baseline METS-VF was 7.31 or higher, METS-VF was positively associated with the risk of TM (HR: 2.04, 95% CI: 1.37–3.03).

Interaction and subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses were conducted based on sex, age, race, family history of CVD, previous hypertension, heart failure, smoking, HbA1c levels, and medications used (insulins, aspirin) to explore the association between METS-VF and MACEs as well as TM. As shown in Fig. 5, the incidence of MACEs and TM associated with METS-VF remains stable across sex, age, race, family history of CVD, previous hypertension, heart failure, and smoking. No interactions were observed. A significant interaction was found between METS-VF and the occurrence of MACEs in patients who used aspirin (P < 0.01). We also found that in people taking aspirin, each one-unit increase in METS-VF was associated with a 14% increase in the incidence of MACEs (HR 1.14, 95% CI: 0.96–1.36). In those not taking aspirin, each one-unit increase in METS-VF was linked to a 71% increase in the incidence of MACEs (HR 1.71, 95% CI: 1.41–2.08). This indicates that among patients with type 2 diabetes who are not taking aspirin, METS-VF has a unique predictive value for the occurrence of MACEs.

Incremental predictive value of METS-VF

To evaluate the predictive value of METS-VF, we further analyzed the C-statistic, NRI, and IDI (Table 4). After adding METS-VF to the basic model, the C-statistic of the traditional model increased from 0.626 to 0.631 (P = 0.015). In addition, risk reclassification and discrimination also improved, with an IDI of 0.2% (95% CI: 0.001–0.004; P < 0.001) and an NRI of 4.5% (95% CI: 0.019–0.070; P = 0.070). Similar results were observed for TM. These findings suggest that adding METS-VF improved the predictive accuracy for both MACEs and TM.

Discussion

This study confirms that METS-VF, as a novel indicator for assessing visceral fat, is significantly associated with cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk and TM in Western populations with type 2 diabetes. This finding corroborates recent conclusions from Yang et al.‘s study of individuals with diabetes and prediabetes in the United States [21], collectively establishing the predictive value of METS-VF in Western populations. The results of this large population-based study showed a significant positive association between higher METS-VF and MACEs and TM after adjusting for all potential covariates, which may be related to insulin resistance. RCS analysis further indicated a significant nonlinear dose-response relationship between METS-VF and TM and MACEs. Our findings suggest a J-shaped association between METS-VF and total mortality and MACEs in type 2 diabetes. Threshold effect analysis showed that the inflection points for TM and MACEs were 7.31 and 6.62 for METS-VF, respectively. Furthermore, subgroup analysis showed that elevated METS-VF was significantly associated with increased MACEs incidence in individuals not taking aspirin. This increased risk may be due to a hypercoagulable state in patients with type 2 diabetes. Hyperglycemia and insulin resistance can lead to platelet overactivation and an increased risk of thrombosis. Therefore, aspirin-free patients may face higher cardiovascular risk factors. Our study emphasizes the importance of METS-VF as a valuable clinical indicator of prognosis for TM and MACEs in patients with type 2 diabetes. Furthermore, METS-VF is crucial in disease risk stratification and prognosis.

Obesity is one of the main causes of diabetes and cardiovascular disease. On the one hand, obesity may induce structural and functional changes in the cardiovascular system to accommodate overweight. On the other hand, excess adipose tissue is strongly associated with inflammation, hypoxia, mitochondrial dysfunction, glucose abnormalities, lipid metabolism and endothelial dysfunction [22–25]. The accumulation of visceral adipose tissue (VAT) among adipose tissues is particularly harmful to health. VAT accumulation has been linked to a higher risk of morbidity and mortality due to hypertension [26], coronary heart disease, metabolic syndrome, and certain cancers [27]. Conventional anthropometric measures of VAT include WC, BMI, and WHtr. While these measures are associated with cardiometabolic disease, they are limited in their ability to distinguish between subcutaneous and visceral fat.

METS-VF is a novel lipid marker that has been shown to be a cost-effective and readily available method of assessing visceral fat. Previous studies have revealed its clinical predictive value in various diseases. Jiayu et al. [28] demonstrated a positive correlation between METS-VF and carotid atherosclerosis. Meanwhile, Jun-Chi et al. [29, 30] showed that METS-VF is an independent predictor of the severity of coronary and carotid artery calcification, as well as a key risk factor for stroke. These studies indirectly support our findings. Harshita et al. [31] conducted a cross-sectional study involving 3,445 participants and found that METS-VF was a superior predictor of diabetes risk compared with traditional markers of visceral fat. This finding is in general agreement with our findings. In addition, the METS-VF considers the METS-IR, a critical factor in evaluating obesity levels and cardiometabolic health. The METS-VF evaluates both visceral adiposity and cardiometabolic health, making it a more effective predictor of MACEs in individuals with type 2 diabetes.

Current research increasingly reveals the role of genetic background in shaping metabolic phenotypes and cardiovascular disease [32–34].Emerging evidence highlights that even in clearly defined genetic disorders such as familial hypercholesterolemia, patients’ glycemic status and atherosclerosis prevalence exhibit significant variations depending on their specific genetic background [35]. This indicates that genetic susceptibility largely guides the manifestation of cardiometabolic risk. Against this backdrop, METS-VF likely transcends being an isolated fat index to become a comprehensive phenotypic marker identifying an individual’s specific position within the broad metabolic continuum. Its numerical variations and associations with cardiovascular outcomes may precisely reflect underlying genetic and metabolic heterogeneity. For such high-risk phenotypes identified by METS-VF, clinical management faces the dual challenges of achieving potent lipid-lowering effects and ensuring sustained adherence. Inclisiran, a small interfering RNA therapy targeting PCSK9, offers a novel pathway [36]. It achieves precise regulation by specifically silencing PCSK9 mRNA in hepatocytes and strategically ensures sustained efficacy through a once-every-six-months dosing regimen. If inclisiran is further employed under the guidance of METS-VF, it may propel disease management from population-based epidemiological evidence toward personalized clinical practice.

The exact mechanisms linking visceral fat accumulation to increased cardiovascular risk in type 2 diabetes remain elusive. However, the recognized mechanisms may fall into two categories: inflammatory response and insulin resistance (IR). Visceral adipose tissue encompasses perivascular fat deposits (e.g., around the thoracic aorta, abdominal aorta, coronary arteries, etc.). When this tissue undergoes inflammatory transformation under pathological conditions, it promotes vasoconstriction [37] and endothelial dysfunction [38] through paracrine effects. Simultaneously, visceral adipose tissue triggers systemic inflammation by secreting multiple pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., leptin and chemokines), further leading to adipokine imbalance, which directly impair vascular endothelial function [27, 39]. It is noteworthy that visceral fat accumulation can induce a dyslipidemia phenotype characterized by elevated triglycerides, reduced HDL-C, and increased small dense LDL particles. This synergistically exacerbates vascular injury alongside inflammation and lipotoxicity. In this process, dysregulated triglyceride metabolism plays a central role in early vascular remodeling [40], while lowering LDL-C can mitigate this damage by improving arterial stiffness [41], further confirming the close association between metabolic dysfunction and vascular impairment. Moreover, visceral obesity, primarily manifested as accumulation of visceral adipose tissue (VAT) and epicardial adipose tissue (EAT), which exacerbate cardiovascular disease through inflammation, mechanical stress, neurological dysfunction, and fibrosis [42, 43]. Notably, visceral fat accumulation and insulin resistance exhibit a mutually reinforcing relationship: obesity-induced inflammation contributes to insulin resistance, accelerating the progression of type 2 diabetes complications [44]. Moreover, the insulin resistance and lipotoxic environment [45]accompanying visceral fat accumulation jointly exacerbate mitochondrial dysfunction. This process involves impaired activity of mitochondrial respiratory chain complexes and activation of NADPH oxidase, leading to excessive reactive oxygen species production. This further amplifies oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, and endothelial damage [46], forming a vicious cycle that drives cardiovascular pathology. Besides, elevated insulin levels increase the risk of platelet aggregation, vasoconstriction, and thrombosis. This, in turn, affects lipid metabolism and sympathetic nervous system activity, contributing to hypertension, dyslipidemia, and atherosclerosis [47].

Although the association between METS-VF and CVD in type 2 diabetes has been demonstrated in Chinese populations [48], our study further validated and deepened the predictive value of METS-VF for cardiovascular risk in Western populations with type 2 diabetes. In addition, existing studies are often limited by small sample sizes and a lack of geographic and demographic diversity, which may prevent accurate generalization of findings. This study is based on the ACCORD database, which is a large-scale and nationally representative study of Americans and Canadians. Additionally, this study effectively adjusted for confounders and used RCS analyses to assess the nonlinear relationship between METS-VF and cardiovascular risk in diabetes mellitus, thereby improving the reliability of the results.

This study has several limitations. First, as an observational study, we were unable to establish a causal relationship between METS-VF and mortality. Second, this study only assessed the prognostic value of baseline METS-VF. It did not investigate the association between changes in METS-VF over time and cardiovascular mortality. It is unclear whether dynamic changes in METS-VF during follow-up provide better predictive value than baseline measurements alone. Future research should focus on employing longitudinal designs to chart the dynamic trajectory of METS-VF through repeated measurements. Third, although this study adjusted for a series of confounding factors in its analysis, residual confounding that was not fully measured may still exist. The study did not comprehensively collect certain potential confounding variables, including individual dietary patterns, objective physical activity indicators, and specific circulating inflammatory markers. These factors may have complex associations with visceral fat distribution and cardiovascular outcomes, and their omission may affect the precision of the effect estimates in this study. Specifically, a high-sugar diet may simultaneously promote visceral fat accumulation and exacerbate systemic inflammation, thereby obscuring the true association between visceral fat and cardiovascular risk. Incorporating objective measurements of these indicators through systematic inclusion could further validate the findings of this study. Additionally, we did not adjust for diabetes duration. The progression of diabetes is a dynamic process; however, in this study, diabetes status was assessed only at baseline. This may introduce potential bias and affect the study results. Fourth, the study population consisted primarily of individuals from the general populations of the United States and Canada. Further research with individuals from different racial backgrounds and countries is necessary to assess the generalizability of these findings. Therefore, caution should be exercised when extrapolating these findings to other racial groups.

Conclusion

In Conclusion, the results of this study suggest that METS-VF is a valuable predictor of TM, MACEs and non-fatal stroke in patients with type 2 diabetes. Routine monitoring of METS-VF may help assess cardiovascular risk and prognosis in patients with type 2 diabetes.
