Association between admission serum potassium level and 30-day all-cause mortality in patients with emergency admission for heart failure: A secondary analysis based on a Norwegian retrospective cohort
Authors: Kaijun Zhang, Penghui Yang, Min Cheng, Dan Yin, Mi Li, Ping Xiang
Published: 2023

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a clinical syndrome resulting from structural and/or functional cardiac abnormalities, and is a rapidly growing global public health problem [1,2]. Despite significant improvements in clinical outcomes for HF patients through the exploration of multiple evidence-based drugs and device therapies, mortality remains a major concern [[3], [4], [5]].

Serum potassium (sK), the most abundant cation in humans and a common hematological indicator of clinical concern, plays a crucial role in nerve conduction and myocardial function, especially in maintaining resting transmembrane potential and a normal cardiac rhythm [6]. Potassium abnormalities, including hypokalemia and hyperkalemia, are common manifestations in patients with HF, and are strongly associated with the risk of death from heart failure [7,8].

Several studies have explored the relationship between sK levels and various clinical outcomes in patients with HF [[8], [9], [10], [11], [12]]. However, differences have been observed in the relationship between sK levels (particularly low sK levels) and outcomes of patients with HF in different selected populations. Those findings suggest that the relationship between sK and the risk of death may very subtly across different populations of selected HF patients. Moreover, few studies were found on the relationship between admission sK levels and the prognosis of patients with HF [9,13].

Therefore, based upon a retrospective cohort study, we aimed to assess the relationship between admission sK levels and 30-day all-cause mortality in patients admitted to an emergency department for HF. Our goal was to provide clinical guidance for a preliminary assessment of the prognosis of such patients using this easily obtainable hematological indicator.

Methods

Data source

The dataset employed in our study, initially collated and published by Tazmini et al., has been duly authorized for secondary analysis and is available for access via the Dryad Digital Repository [14]. Therefore, such a secondary study based on original data with different research hypothesis was permitted.

Study design and participants

This was a secondary analysis based on a single-center retrospective cohort study conducted at Diakonhjemmet Hospital in Oslo, Norway, from 2010 to 2015. It included 31,966 unique patients with 62,991 registered encounters in the Emergency Department (ED) rather than hospitalized.

Patients with a primary diagnosis of heart failure (ICD-10, I50) according to the International Classification of Diseases 10th Revision (ICD-10) were selected from the original data, and only information related to the first hospitalization to this emergency department of each patient were considered. Patients with missing data of sK levels or incorrectly recorded days of death were excluded. Fig. 1 shows the details of patients’ selection.

Flowchart of screening the study population.

The original study was approved by Norwegian Regional Committee for Medical and Health Research Ethics South East, and all data were anonymized, thus no separate ethical approval and informed consent were required for our secondary study [14]. Finally, our study was conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations to ensure the highest standards of research ethics.

Outcome and data collection

The primary outcome was 30-day all-cause mortality in patients with emergency admission for HF after the first admission.

Demographic data, disease diagnosis, laboratory data were extracted from the original data. All laboratory results in this study were the first-time laboratory tests after admission based on the original data. Some correction indices were calculated. Glucose corrected serum-sodium = serum-sodium + [(serum-glucose– 5,6)/5,6] × 2, 4) [15]. Albumin-corrected calcium levels = measured serum-calcium level +0.020 × (41.3 –serum-albumin), 41.3 g/L is the albumin median [16]. Performed selectively based on the patient's clinical indications, serum phosphate, serum magnesium had many missing items.

Comorbidities

ICD-coded secondary diagnoses in the raw data were used to identify comorbidities, including angina pectoris (ICD-10: I20, except I20.0), acute coronary syndrome (unstable angina ICD-10 I20.0; ST elevation myocardial infarction and non-ST elevation myocardial infarction ICD-10: I21), malnutrition (ICD-10: E40-E46), pneumonia (ICD-10: J98, J69, J11-18), cancer (ICD-10: C0–C9, Z51.0–3), coronary heart disease (ICD-10: I25), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (ICD-10: J42-44), renal insufficiency (ICD-10: N18), atrial fibrillation/atrial flutter (ICD-10: I48), hypertension (ICD-10: I10), hyperlipidemia (ICD-8: E78), diabetes (ICD-10: E10-E14). All the above comorbidities were treated as categorical variables according to their presence or absence.

Statistical analysis

Means ± standard deviations (SD) or medians (interquartile range) were used to denote continuous variables and categorical variables were express as percentages. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test for continuous variables with normal distributions, Kruskal-Wallis H tests for continuous variables with skewed distributions, and chi-square tests (or Fisher's exact test) for categorical variables were used to analyze between-group differences.

Univariate logistic regression analyses were used to assess the association between admission sK levels and outcome 30-day all-cause mortality. Three models were carried out to control effects of confounding factors. Covariates were selected based on the relationship between the covariates and 30-day mortality or the ability to change the effect value by more than 10 % [17]. Threshold effect was explored using a two-piecewise linear regression analysis according to the smoothing plot.

Quartiles of serum potassium were used to test the stability of the multiple regression results, and a linearity test was performed by assigning medians to each sK quartile as a continuous variable in the models [18]. Then, the relationship was further explored by dividing sK into a 3-category variable and an 8-category variable.

Serum phosphate and magnesium levels with a large portion of missing data were converted into categorical variables. And, dummy variables were used to identify missing values of covariates (16). Moreover, multiple imputations (MI) based on five replications and a chained equation approach in the R MI procedure were used to process the missing data, and sensitivity analysis were performed to compare the primary data and interpolation data (Supplementary Material Fig. 1 and Table 1)(17).

Subsequently, based on previous literature [10,12], we converted sK for continuous variables into 3 categorical variables (<4, 4–4.5, >4.5 mol/L) and 8 categorical variables (2.6–3.4, 3.5–3.8, 3.9–4.1, 4.2–4.4, 4.5–4.7, 4.8–5.0, 5.1–5.5, 5.6–7.1 mol/L). The categorical variables obtained from these three different classifications were included in Crude Model, Model I and Model II. In Model II, high level sK was associated with 30-day mortality (>5 mmol/L in the 3-category variable, OR 4.18, 95 % CI 2.21–7.91, P < 0.001; 5.6–7.1 mmol/L in the 8-category variable, OR 9.42, 95 % CI 3.06–28.97, P < 0.001).

Smoothing plot and two-piecewise linear regression analysis

Smoothing plot for all three models showed a nonlinear relationship between sK and 30-day all-cause mortality (Fig. 2). In Model II, which adjusted for more variables, a J-shaped nonlinear relationship was found. Subsequently, cutoff values for all three models were identified by two-piecewise linear regression analysis and the cut-off value was 4.7 mmol/L in Model II (log-likelihood ratio test 0.001) (Table 4). The risk of 30-day all-cause mortality increased with the increase of sK level when the sK level exceeded 4.7 mmol/L (OR = 6.06, 95 % CI 2.67–13.73, P < 0.001).

Sensitivity analysis

Higher sK group was significantly associated with an increased risk of 30-day mortality (＞4.7 mmmol/L, or >5 mmol/L in the 3-category variable, or 5.6–7.1 mmol/L in the 8-category variable).

E-values were calculated to assess sensitivity to unmeasured confounding. Unless unmeasured confounding was present, the primary outcome was stable and highly positively correlated with sK (OR ≥ 3.12) and 30-day mortality (OR ≥ 3.12).

Discussion

In this secondary analysis of a sample of a Norwegian population, we have identified a statistically significant positive association between admission sK and 30-day all-cause mortality in patients with emergency admission for HF, after adjusting for potential confounding factors. Furthermore, we have observed that hyperkalemia, defined sK levels greater than 5 mmol/L in the 3-category variable or 5.6–7.1 mmol/L in the 8-category variable significantly increase the risk of 30-day all-cause mortality for those unselected patients admitted to the emergency department for HF, as compared to patients with normal range of admission sK. Controversially, no such association was observed in patients with low sK. Smoothing plot, and two-piecewise linear regression analysis revealed a J-shaped nonlinear relationship and identified a cut-off value 4.7 mmol/L. Sensitivity analysis demonstrated the robustness of our findings in male, female, and older than 70 years old.

Hyperkalemia is the most common electrolyte disorder in patients with HF.

Given its crucial role in cardiac excitability and arrhythmias, serum potassium disturbance is associated with an increased risk of ventricular arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death [22,23]. Hypokalemia could increase depolarization, automaticity and excitability, prolonging action potentials and QT dispersion of cardiac muscle cell [24]. Hyperkalemia decreases the concentration gradient across the cardiac muscle cell membrane, thereby shortening the duration of the action potential. As hyperkalemia worsens, severe bradycardia or cardiac arrest may ensue [25].

Several studies on the association between sK and adverse outcomes in patients with HF, suggesting that potassium disorders are associated with an increased risk of death. A United Kingdom study of 21,334 patients newly diagnosed with HF exhibited a U-shaped association pattern between adjusted mortality incidence rate ratios (IRR) and serum potassium, indicating that both hypokalemia and hyperkalemia were associated with an increased risk of death [11]. Similarly, a Danish National Registry of Investigators study that included 19,549 patients showed a U-shaped relationship between sK levels and 90-day short-term mortality risk in patients with chronic HF and highlighted the lower and upper levels of the normal serum potassium range were associated with a significantly increased risk of short-term mortality in patients with chronic HF [10]. Also, a state-of-the-art-review from Journal of the American College of Cardiology (JACC) showed a similar U-shaped relationship, suggesting that low serum potassium levels (<3.5–5.0 mmol/L) may predict a similar risk of death as high potassium levels (>5.5–6.0 mmol/L) [8]. It should be noted that the populations in these studies appeared to be skewed towards patients with chronic HF. Conversely, in additional studies, low sK levels were not observed to be associated with poor outcomes in patients with HF. Including 26, 865 patients >74 years old hospitalized for decompensated HF, a study from the Spanish National Registry of Heart Failure (RICA) showed that admission hyperkalemia predicted a higher intermediate risk of readmission and death in HF, while, no similar association was observed in hypokalemia [9]. A nonrandomized propensity score-matched study that enrolled 5, 881 elderly patients hospitalized for HF in 106 Alabama hospitals in the United States showed no significant correlation between hypokalemia (<4.0 or <3.5 mmol/L) and short- or long-term mortality or readmission rates compared to normokalemia (4.0–5.0 mmol/L) [12]. Seemly, heterogeneity of populations should be considered for the relationship between sK and the risk of death in selected patients. Moreover, few studies were found on the relationship between admission sK levels and the prognosis of patients with HF [9,13].

Upon reviewing the literature, it is evident that hyperkalemia increases death in HF, while the association between hypokalemia and outcomes in HF patients is less clear and various across different populations, including differences in age, ethnicity, race, geography, and diet [[26], [27], [28], [29]]. Patients in our population were of advanced age (81.65 ± 11.07, years), which may suggest a more advantaged stage of HF and a higher likelihood of pump failure deaths rather than sudden cardiac death associated with hypokalemia [[30], [31], [32]]. To explore this possibility, we performed a stratified analysis of age which indicated a J-shaped relationship between sK and mortality in patients elderly (＞70 years) (Supplemental Fig. 2 and Table 2). Similar to the findings of Valebtova, M [12], a higher proportion of hospitalized patients in our study suffered from more advanced HF and died due to pump failure, which may partially explain the lack of association between hypokalemia and mortality. Consistent with the findings of Formiga F et al. [9] and Valentova M et al. [12], our findings provide more evidence that admission sK levels correlate with clinical outcomes in selected HF populations. Our study population included an unselected group of patients admitted to the emergency department for HF, including those with new-onset HF and acute episodes of chronic HF, with the aim of predicting their short-term prognosis based on admission sK levels.

Strengths and limitations

In our study, the association between admission sK and 30-day mortality was explored systematically by using multivariate analysis, smoothing plot and two-piecewise linear regression analysis and sensitive analysis, with appropriate adjustments made for covariates, ultimately yielding the identification of a J-shaped nonlinear relationship and the determination of the cutoff value. The robustness of our model was further bolstered when divided the admission sK levels as a categorical variable in both 3-category and 8-category designs. Limitations should also be considered. Firstly, this was a secondary study obtained data of a Norwegian population from Dryad Digital Repository that unmeasured confounding factors cannot be completely excluded, such as the use of multiple medications (diuretics, β-blocker, etc), records of left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and New York Heart Association (NYHA) classification, though E-values were utilized. Secondly, no sK levels dynamic changes were considered, and we focused on admission sK levels only which limited our ability to fully assess the potential mechanisms at play. Additionally, this J-shaped relationship needs to be further investigated to the broader HF populations considering the racial heterogeneity. Lastly, the population in this study was predominantly middle-aged and elderly, and the generalizability of the results to the pediatric HF population requires further exploration.

Conclusions

Admission serum potassium level at initial evaluation in patients presenting to the emergency department for heart failure was positively associated with 30-day all-cause mortality with a J-shaped nonlinear relationship.
