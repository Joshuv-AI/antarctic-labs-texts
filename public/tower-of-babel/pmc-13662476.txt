Targeting calciphylaxis: the emergence of vitamin K as a potential treatment
Authors: Maria Fusaro, Smeeta Sinha, Antonio Bellasi, Markus Ketteler, Carlo Alfieri, Martin H de Borst, Ditte Hansen, Pietro Manuel Ferraro, Juan Miguel Díaz-Tocados, Mathias Haarhaus, Mehmet Kanbay, Maurizio Gallieni, Paolo Simioni, Sandro Giannini, Sharon Huish, Althea Cossettini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCalciphylaxis, or calcific uraemic arteriolopathy, is a rare, life-threatening vascular disorder predominantly affecting patients with advanced chronic kidney disease (CKD), especially those on dialysis. It is characterized by arteriolar calcification, thrombosis, and painful ischemic skin lesions that frequently ulcerate, contributing to high morbidity and mortality. Calciphylaxis arises from medial arterial calcification, endothelial injury, and a pro-inflammatory, pro-thrombotic milieu. Disruption of vitamin K–dependent pathways, particularly impaired γ-carboxylation of matrix Gla protein (MGP), a potent inhibitor of vascular calcification, has emerged as a key mechanistic contributor. Functional vitamin K deficiency is common in CKD and is exacerbated by vitamin K antagonists such as warfarin, which markedly increase calciphylaxis risk. Observational studies show elevated levels of inactive MGP and other undercarboxylated Gla proteins in affected patients, linking vitamin K insufficiency to disease pathogenesis. Case reports and preliminary interventional studies suggest that vitamin K supplementation could restore MGP activity, reduce vascular calcification, and support wound healing, robust randomized controlled trial data are lacking. Ongoing trials, including the BEAT-Calci adaptive platform, aim to clarify the efficacy, optimal dosing, and safety of vitamin K as a targeted therapeutic strategy. Precision medicine approaches using vitamin K biomarkers and individualized anticoagulation may further improve outcomes. In conclusion, vitamin K–dependent pathways represent a promising mechanistic and potentially modifiable target in calciphylaxis. High-quality clinical trials are urgently needed to validate supplementation strategies and develop evidence-based, individualized management protocols for this severe condition.Keywords: calciphylaxis, CKD, CKD-MBD, vascular calcification, vitamin K

INTRODUCTION

Calciphylaxis, also known as calcific uremic arteriolopathy, is a rare but life-threatening vascular disorder predominantly affecting individuals with chronic kidney disease (CKD), especially those with end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) receiving dialysis [1–3]. It is characterized by calcification and thrombosis of dermal and subcutaneous arterioles, leading to painful ischaemic skin lesions that frequently ulcerate and become infected. Although incidence estimates vary due to underdiagnosis, the largest US dataset reported an incidence of 35 per 10 000 patient-years among haemodialysis patients [3]. Calciphylaxis is associated with substantial morbidity and a poor prognosis, with reported 1-year mortality rates ranging from 36% to 74% [4–6].

Diagnosis and management remain challenging because of the lack of standardized diagnostic criteria, overlapping clinical presentations, and the limitations and risks of skin biopsy [3, 6, 7]. Treatment strategies are largely based on case reports and expert opinion, and require a multidisciplinary approach including wound care, pain management, dialysis optimization, and correction of mineral bone abnormalities. Sodium thiosulfate remains widely used despite limited evidence and frequent adverse effects [3, 8, 9], while other interventions such as parathyroidectomy, hyperbaric oxygen therapy, and surgical debridement also lack robust supporting data. Consequently, clinical practice remains highly heterogeneous [1, 9, 10].

Our working group recently published a comprehensive overview of calciphylaxis diagnosis, management, and future directions [11]. This review specifically focuses on vitamin K as a key mechanistic and potentially modifiable factor in calciphylaxis pathogenesis and treatment. Evidence implicates disruption of vitamin K–dependent pathways, particularly impaired γ-carboxylation of matrix Gla protein (MGP), a potent inhibitor of vascular calcification, in disease development and progression [12]. Functional vitamin K deficiency is common in advanced CKD and may be exacerbated by vitamin K antagonists such as warfarin, consistently associated with increased calciphylaxis risk [1, 9, 13, 14].

In this review, we present the current evidence linking vitamin K deficiency to vascular calcification, thrombosis, and calciphylaxis, summarizing current evidence and ongoing trials. We examine the role of vitamin K in the disease pathophysiology, discuss its potential as a biomarker and therapeutic target, and highlight current uncertainties that require addressing to inform future interventional studies. By narrowing the focus to vitamin K, this review aims to advance a more mechanistically informed approach to calciphylaxis, for which effective, disease-modifying therapies are urgently needed.

Pathophysiology of calciphylaxis

Calciphylaxis arises from medial arterial calcification, endothelial injury, intimal fibrosis, and thrombosis, which compromise small- to medium-sized vessels and impair tissue perfusion [14, 15]. These changes predominantly affect the dermal and subcutaneous microvasculature, causing tissue ischaemia, necrosis, and the painful skin ulcers characteristic of calciphylaxis, although extracutaneous involvement has also been reported [9, 16]. As outlined in our recent paper, these processes develop within the metabolic, inflammatory, and pro-thrombotic milieu of advanced CKD, where disruption of vitamin K–dependent pathways has emerged as a potentially important modifier of disease progression [11, 12].

Vascular calcification

Vascular calcification (VC) is highly prevalent in dialysis populations and represents a central feature of calciphylaxis [17, 18]. In advanced CKD, VC is driven by disturbances in mineral metabolism, chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, extracellular matrix alterations, and loss of endogenous calcification inhibitors [18–20]. Accumulation and maturation of calciprotein particles (CPPs) reflect this imbalance and are associated with increased calcification propensity and adverse cardiovascular outcomes [21–23]. A key mechanism underlying medial VC is the osteogenic transdifferentiation of vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), characterized by loss of smooth muscle markers and increased expression of osteogenic genes such as RUNX2 [17, 19, 24, 25]. Hyperphosphataemia promotes this process through enhanced PiT-1 activity and activation of Wnt/β-catenin signalling [26, 27].

Pro-inflammatory and pro-coagulant milieu

Calciphylaxis develops within the chronic pro-inflammatory and pro-coagulant state of advanced CKD. Uremic toxins and persistent immune activation contribute to endothelial dysfunction, reduced nitric oxide bioavailability, fibrointimal remodelling and vascular stiffness. In parallel, haemostatic abnormalities, including reduced activity of endogenous anticoagulants, platelet activation, and impaired fibrinolysis, predispose individuals to microvascular thrombosis. These pro-inflammatory and pro-coagulant mechanisms intersect to promote the VC, occlusion, and tissue ischemia that characterize calciphylaxis [15].

Vitamin K is required for gamma-carboxylation (γ-carboxylation) of several coagulation factors and endogenous anticoagulants, including protein C and protein S, a process essential for their biological activity [28]. Functional vitamin K deficiency may therefore exacerbate haemostatic imbalance in susceptible individuals, particularly in the pro-thrombotic milieu of advanced CKD [15]. In addition, vitamin K has been shown to exert anti-inflammatory effects in experimental models, including modulation of nuclear factor-κB signalling and reductions in pro-inflammatory cytokine expression, suggesting a potential contributory role beyond coagulation pathways [29].

Vitamin K

Vitamin K acts as a critical molecular switch for the γ-carboxylation of proteins essential to both haemostasis and vascular integrity. In the setting of CKD, a functional vitamin K deficiency leads to a pro-calcific and inflammatory milieu that lowers the threshold for vascular complications and calciphylaxis.

Vitamin K comprises three main forms which differ in their side chain structure and sources. Vitamin K1, or phylloquinone (PK), is mainly found in green leafy vegetables and is characterized by a phytyl side chain [30, 31]. Vitamin K2 refers to a group of compounds known as menaquinones (MKn), which contain a variable number of isoprenic units in their side chain and are primarily found in fermented foods or produced by intestinal bacteria [31]. A notable source of MK-7 is Natto, a Japanese soybean-based food fermented by Bacillus subtilis. Unlike other menaquinones, MK-4 (menatetrenone) is synthetized endogenously from PK. This conversion is mediated by a prenyltransferase UBIAD1 (UbiA prenyltransferase domain-containing protein 1) [32]. Several tissues, including the pancreas, testes, and vessel walls, can convert PK directly into MK-4. Lastly, vitamin K3 (menadione) is a synthetic form that lacks a side chain, must be converted, primarily in the liver, into MK-4 to become biologically active.

Sources, absorption, and metabolism

Vitamin K absorption mimics dietary lipid pathways, requiring micellar solubilization and lymphatic transport via chylomicrons. Once in circulation, vitamin K is transported to various tissues via lipoproteins, including LDL, HDL, and triglyceride-rich lipoproteins (TRLs) [33].

Comparative studies assessing the distribution of Vitamin K-isoforms (1000 μg of K1, 1000 μg of MK-4, and 200 μg of MK-7) in both healthy individuals and dialysis patients have revealed significant alterations under uremic conditions [34]. Notably, uremic HDL particles exhibit an abnormal uptake profile, absorbing more MK-4 than normal HDL but substantially less of the long-lived MK-7. This impaired MK-7 loading may reduce its cellular delivery and biological activity. Despite these functional changes, no significant differences in renal UBIAD1 protein or gene expression have been observed between CKD and control models [34].

Regarding extra-hepatic delivery, in vitro studies have demonstrated that primary osteoblasts internalize PK from all major lipoprotein fractions, with an efficiency order of LDL > TRL > HDL. TRL-mediated uptake depends on heparan sulfate proteoglycans and apolipoprotein E (ApoE) [35], supporting the involvement of LDLR family receptors on the osteoblast surface. Given their physiological role in postprandial lipid transport, TRLs are considered particularly relevant carriers for skeletal vitamin K delivery.

Furthermore, fasting plasma PK concentrations are strongly influenced by the common ApoE genotype, being highest in individuals with the apoE2 variant and lowest in apoE4 carriers. This reflects the known correlation between ApoE phenotypes and the hepatic clearance rate of chylomicron remnants, slowest in apoE2 and fastest in apoE4, suggesting that prolonged circulating residence time may enhance vitamin K availability for bone uptake [35].

Vitamin K–dependent proteins relevant to calciphylaxis

Vitamin K acts as an essential coenzyme for γ-glutamyl carboxylase, enabling the carboxylation reaction required to activate more than 15 Vitamin K-Dependent Proteins (VKDPs), including coagulation factors and extra-hepatic proteins such as MGP, Growth Arrest-Specific 6 (Gas6), Gla-rich protein (GRP), and Bone Gla Protein (BGP/Osteocalcin) [36] (Fig. 1).

Matrix Gla protein (MGP)

MGP is an 84-amino acid, 12 kDa VKDP secreted by chondrocytes and VSMCs, with expression in bone, heart, vessels, kidneys, and cartilage [37–39]. It is the most potent endogenous inhibitor of VC process [40], acting by binding nascent hydroxyapatite crystals and limiting their deposition within the arterial wall, as well as promoting clearance of mineral complexes.

MGP activation is essential for vascular health and depends on vitamin K–dependent carboxylation at five γ-carboxyglutamic acid (Gla) residues [41], required for high-affinity calcium binding. In addition, MGP is phosphorylated at three serine residues in the N-terminal cluster [42], a process important for secretion from VSMCs and chondrocytes and for full anti-calcific activity. The active form (p-cMGP) inhibits calcification and participates in CPP formation with Fetuin-A, contributing to mineral homeostasis [43].

Additionally, MGP directly antagonizes Bone Morphogenetic Protein-2, blocking its receptor binding and inhibiting the osteogenic differentiation of VSMCs. To achieve biological activity, MGP requires vitamin K as a substrate [40]. Insufficient vitamin K levels result in the accumulation of inactive MGP isoforms: uncarboxylated MGP (ucMGP) and dephosphorylated-uncarboxylated form (d-pucMGP) [40]. Among these, dp-ucMGP is currently the most widely used biomarker of vitamin K status, although its interpretation in advanced CKD is complicated by potential effects of reduced kidney function and other disease-related factors. Circulating dp-ucMGP levels increase progressively with the severity of chronic CKD, being 4.5–6.5 times higher in haemodialysis patients compared to healthy controls [44–46].

Gas6

Gas6 is a pleiotropic VKDP [47] that regulates key cellular processes, including survival and phagocytosis, through binding to the Tyro3, Axl, and Mer tyrosine kinase receptors. Highly expressed in the kidneys, heart, and lungs [48], Gas6 has been proposed to complement MGP in mediating vitamin K’s protective effects against VC by preventing apoptosis in endothelial cells and VSMCs [49, 50]. In vitro evidence supports this link, showing that phosphate-induced VSMC calcification correlates with Gas6 downregulation, while the protective effects of statins [51], testosterone [50], taurine [52], and alpha-lipoic acid [49] appear to be mediated by Gas6 mRNA stabilization or expression. Furthermore, the pro-calcific effects of warfarin are partly attributed to its interference with the Gas6/Axl pathway. A study conducted on animal models further demonstrated that warfarin causes the suppression of Gas6 activity in VSMC-conditioned medium [53], so it could no longer phosphorylate its receptor Axl [54]. The resulting blockade of this downstream signalling cascade triggers a pro-apoptotic shift in VSMCs, which appears to directly facilitate the progression of VC.

However, the in vivo role of Gas6 remains controversial. Recent investigations comparing Gas6-knockout (Gas6−/−) and wild-type (WT) mice across various models, including warfarin administration, uninephrectomy with high phosphate diet, and uraemia induced by electrocautery demonstrated no significant differences in vascular or organ calcification between genotypes [47]. These findings contrast with earlier in vitro data, suggesting that while Gas6 is involved in cellular survival pathways, its individual contribution to systemic calcification inhibition in vivo may be less decisive than previously hypothesized, potentially due to other compensatory mechanisms [47].

Other Gla proteins

GRP is a vitamin K-dependent circulating protein that modulates ectopic mineralization. Like MGP, it requires post-translational γ-carboxylation of glutamate residues to achieve biological activity and exert systemic effects. Its primary roles include suppressing bone-related gene expression and preventing trans-differentiation of VSMCs into osteoblast-like phenotypes. Consequently, GRP acts as a potent inhibitor of mineral deposition and a negative regulator of osteogenic transition within the vascular wall [55].

While GRP gene expression is upregulated in pro-calcific environments to counteract mineral stress, prolonged stimulation can deplete local vitamin K stores. This impairs γ-carboxylation, leading to accumulation of undercarboxylated GRP isoforms that lack inhibitory efficacy and facilitate vascular mineralization [56]. Its clinical relevance as a biomarker was highlighted in a study of 101 peritoneal dialysis patients, where serum total GRP (tGRP) levels were significantly reduced in those with extensive calcifications. In this population, tGRP was an independent determinant of VC, showing negative associations with calcium, phosphate, and inflammatory markers [57].

Complementing this network is Osteocalcin (Bone Gla Protein, BGP), a matrix protein secreted by osteoblasts and osteogenic VSMCs [58]. It undergoes carboxylation of three glutamine residues at positions 17, 21, and 24. This conversion from undercarboxylated (ucOC) to carboxylated OC (cOC) enables binding to hydroxyapatite crystals within the bone matrix [59]. Beyond binding, BGP plays a dual role by inhibiting excessive mineralization and modulating mineral maturation [59].

Unlike GRP, carboxylated osteocalcin promotes mineralization and differentiation via upregulation of Runx2 and alkaline phosphatase activity [60]. γ-carboxylation enhances binding affinity for calcium and hydroxyapatite, facilitating mineral incorporation into the extracellular matrix. In vitamin K deficiency, osteocalcin remains undercarboxylated, losing affinity for calcium deposits. A protective role against VC has been suggested, highlighting an inverse relationship between osteocalcin levels and vascular damage. The VIKI Study (Vitamin K Italian Study), involving 387 HD patients across 18 Italian centres, reported [61] reduced BGP levels in patients with aortic and iliac calcifications and in those with VFs. The MINOS study [62], conducted in 774 French men over 10 years, showed that higher serum osteocalcin levels are associated with lower incidence of aortic calcifications and reduced 10-year all-cause mortality.

This protective effect is partly explained by the Osteocalcin-Adiponectin axis. Experimental studies in mice [63] and patients [64] with CKD suggest that undercarboxylated or total BGP stimulates adiponectin secretion from adipocytes. Adiponectin, an anti-inflammatory adipokine, protects the arterial wall by preventing phenotypic transdifferentiation of VSMCs into osteoblast-like cells, thereby inhibiting calcification process.

Beyond carboxylation, vitamin K, particularly MK-4, acts as a transcriptional signalling molecule via the steroid and xenobiotic receptor (SXR/PXR) [65]. Vitamin K1 activates this pathway only after conversion to MK-4. In human osteoblasts, SXR activation induces a genetic program essential for bone health [65], upregulating Msx2 to drive cellular differentiation [65] and increasing expression of Matrilin-2 (Matn2) and Tsukushi (Tsk), which are important for extracellular matrix organization and collagen assembly. Disruption of SXR-mediated signalling may therefore impair bone material properties of the bone quality and contribute to increased fracture risk in CKD.

Vitamin K deficiency and calciphylaxis

Prevalence of vitamin K deficiency in CKD/dialysis patients

Recommendations for daily vitamin K intake lack global consensus, with guidelines varying from the American Adequate Intake (90–120 μg/day) (National Institutes of Health, Office of Dietary Supplements) to the higher Italian LARN standards (140–170 μg/day) [66] and the lower Europe EFSA recommendations (50–70 μg/day) [67, 68]. This variability is further compounded by the uncertain contribution of intestinal bacterial production and the clinical realization that the absence of abnormal bleeding, while indicative of functional hepatic coagulation, does not guarantee sufficient levels for bone and vascular health [68]. Consequently, traditional markers are being replaced by indirect surrogate measurements of undercarboxylated VKDPs. Diagnosis of deficiency now relies on specific thresholds for these markers, such as PIVKA-II (>2 nmol/l) [69], ucBGP (>20% or ≥4.5 ng/ml) [70], and especially dp-ucMGP (>500 pmol/l) [71]. Direct K1 measurement remains an informative tool, with the definition of deficiency shifting from <0.3 nmol/l in the general population [72] to <0.4 nmol/l in patients with CKD (Fig. 2) [73].

Clinical evidence across multiple cohorts confirms a high prevalence of vitamin K deficiency in CKD. In a study from Evenepoel et al. conducted on 468 de novo kidney transplant recipients, median plasma dp-ucMGP was 1150 nmol/l and 421 patients (90%) had elevated dp-ucMGP levels (>500 nmol/l), indicating vitamin K insufficiency [74]. Similarly, studies by Boxma [75] and Keyzer [76] have reported vitamin K deficiency rates exceeding 80% in kidney transplant recipients.

Recent evidence highlights the potential role of MGP activation status in the pathogenesis of calciphylaxis. In a case-control study involving 40 haemodialysis patients (20 cases with calciphylaxis and 20 matched controls), patients with calciphylaxis exhibited significantly higher plasma levels of both ucMGP and carboxylated MGP (cMGP) [12]. Furthermore, the relative concentration of cMGP, defined as the fraction of total MGP that underwent activation (cMGP/[cMGP + ucMGP]), was significantly lower in patients with calciphylaxis compared to controls (0.58 ± 0.02 vs. 0.69 ± 0.03; P = .003).

Therapeutic implications

Potential role of vitamin K supplementation in prevention/treatment

Vitamin K supplementation has been proposed as a potential strategy to prevent or treat vascular calcification and related complications, although several challenges remain regarding the optimal dose, formulation, availability, and safety. A systematic review including 1533 patients from 14 studies, five of which on CKD patients stage G3-G5D and one on kidney transplant recipients, evaluated vitamin K supplementation [77]. The interventions included vitamin K2 (mainly MK-7) at doses ranging from 90 to 400 μg/day and vitamin K1 at doses from 500 μg to 10 mg/day. Menadione was administered to kidney transplanted patients at a dose of 5 mg, thrice weekly. Overall, vitamin K supplementation seemed to have a significant effect on coronary artery calcification (CAC) scores, slowing down the progression of CAC and significantly reducing circulating levels of dephosphorylated-uncarboxylated matrix Gla protein (dp-ucMGP) without a significant increase in adverse events compared with control groups. However, interpretation of these findings requires caution. Most studies were relatively small, used heterogeneous vitamin K formulations and doses, and evaluated surrogate endpoints. Furthermore, consistent effects on clinically relevant outcomes have not yet been demonstrated.

Challenges: optimal dose, formulation (K1 vs K2)—availability, safety considerations

Importantly, not all forms of vitamin K share the same pharmacokinetic and biological properties. A comparative study [78] of vitamin K1 and vitamin K2 (MK-7) have shown marked differences in bioavailability and duration of action. Although both reach peak serum concentrations ∼4 h after administration, K1 rapidly returns to baseline within hours, whereas MK-7 remains detectable for up to 72–96 h due to its substantially longer half-life (∼3 days). Among vitamin K2 isoforms, MK-4 is considered highly biologically active at the tissue level, whereas MK-7 demonstrates superior bioavailability and prolonged circulation owing to its increased hydrophobicity and lipoprotein-mediated transport [78]. In contrast, phylloquinone (K1) has a much shorter half-life of ∼1–2 h [78]. Available studies have demonstrated a favourable safety profile of vitamin K supplementation, with no major adverse events reported [79–92].

Despite these pharmacological advantages, evidence in CKD populations remains inconsistent. While randomized trials suggest that phylloquinone or MK-4 may provide benefits on bone health [93, 94, 95], studies evaluating vascular calcification, particularly with MK-7 supplementation, have yielded heterogeneous (Table 1). Interpretation is further complicated by differences among MK-7 formulations, including natural fermentation-derived and synthetic preparations.

Dose adequacy also remains unresolved. The doses commonly used in nephrology studies, typically around 360 μg/day or lower, may be insufficient for CKD patients [96], who frequently exhibit profound vitamin K deficiency and altered metabolism related to the uremic milieu. Indeed, studies in advanced CKD have explored considerably higher doses, including phylloquinone up to 10 mg/day and MK-7 doses exceeding 1080 μg/day [96]. The ongoing VIKIPEDIA trial is investigating whether a higher MK-7 dose (1000 μg/day) may provide greater effects on VC in peritoneal dialysis patients [97]. Moreover, potential sex-related differences in response to MK-7 supplementation [98], which should be considered in future trials. Therefore, further well-designed randomized trials are needed to identify the optimal formulation, dosing strategy, and safety profile of vitamin K supplementation in this high-risk population.

Clinical evidence on vitamin K supplementation

Case reports and case series in calciphylaxis patients

Preliminary evidence from case reports suggests that vitamin K supplementation may have potential as an adjunctive approach in patients with calciphylaxis, particularly when conventional therapies are costly or poorly tolerated. An 82-year-old dialysis patient with severe hyperparathyroidism and extensive VC achieved complete resolution of necrotic ulcers within 3 months of starting oral phylloquinone (10 mg thrice weekly) [99]. Her condition recurred upon cessation of supplementation but resolved again after reintroducing intravenous vitamin K, underscoring a potentially protective effect. Similarly, a 43-year-old woman with a history of extensive bowel resection and severe vitamin K deficiency (<0.3 nmol/l) developed calciphylaxis despite being normocalcaemic and not taking warfarin. Her lesions completely healed over 12 months following high-dose intravenous vitamin K supplementation (10 mg at each dialysis session) and increased dialysis frequency with lower calcium dialysate [100].

These observations suggest that assessment of vitamin K status may provide useful insights in the clinical management of calciphylaxis, while controlled trials are needed to clarify the role of supplementation and define standardized therapeutic strategies in this high-risk population.

Interventional studies in CKD and dialysis populations

Recent clinical research focuses on structured interventional trials to evaluate the efficacy of vitamin K in mitigating VC and treating calciphylaxis [11]. A landmark contribution comes from the VitaVasK study, which administered 10 mg of vitamin K1 weekly to haemodialysis patients over a 1-year period. The results demonstrated a significant reduction in VC, with a 72% decrease in the Agatston score (P = .028) [84]. Complementing these findings, an experimental follow-up by Kaesler et al. suggested vitamin K1 as a more effective therapeutic choice than vitamin K2 in dialysis patients, due to potential loss of anti-calcification properties that vitamin K2 may undergo in this population [34]. Evidence from populations without CKD also suggests a potential role of vitamin K in modulating vascular calcification. In the VitaK-CAC study, 167 patients were randomized to receive MK-7 (360 μg/day) or placebo for 2 years, resulting in a modest but significant attenuation of coronary artery calcification progression compared with placebo [101].

Saritas et al. [84] demonstrated significant reductions in thoracic aortic calcification (−56%) and coronary artery calcification (−68%) with K1 supplementation (5 mg three times weekly) over 18 months in haemodialysis patients. Similarly, Macias-Cervantes et al. [88] reported a 55% reduction in progression of coronary artery calcification with K1 at 10 mg three times weekly over 12 months. However, these findings are not uniform, as other studies, especially with K2 supplementation, observed no significant effect on VC (Table 1). The reasons for these discrepant findings remain unclear and may relate to differences in study populations, baseline vitamin K status, vitamin K formulation, dosing regimens, duration of follow-up, and the use of surrogate rather than clinical endpoints.

Limitations of current data

An obstacle in calciphylaxis treatment is underdiagnosis and clinical mimicry, with cases often recognized only after severe necrotic lesions develop, causing delayed diagnosis, worse outcomes, and underreporting that limit prevalence estimates. The lack of a non-invasive diagnostic gold standard further hinders early identification and data collection.

The available literature is almost exclusively limited to retrospective case reports, with no published case series or prospective clinical trials, lacking statistical power to validate standardized therapies; current evidence relies on anecdotal or observational data subject to bias and confounding.

Considerations in patients with calciphylaxis on anticoagulation and Vitamin K pathways

Warfarin, a vitamin K antagonist (VKA), inhibits γ-carboxylation of VKDPs including MGP, reducing its calcification-inhibitory function. Regulatory safety communications and case reports describe an association between warfarin use and calciphylaxis, leading to recommendations to discontinue warfarin when clinically feasible (https://www.gov.uk/drug-safety-update/warfarin-reports-of-calciphylaxis) [12, 102]. Observational data consistently show higher warfarin exposure among patients with calciphylaxis, plausibly mediated by impaired MGP activation and loss of VC inhibition [1, 9, 13, 14].

A large retrospective cohort study of 2234 dialysis patients identified VKA therapy as a risk factor, with warfarin use associated with higher calciphylaxis risk compared with no treatment [103]. Although the absolute incidence of warfarin-associated calciphylaxis among patients receiving warfarin is unknown, warfarin exposure has been reported in ∼40%–50% of patients diagnosed with calciphylaxis and has been associated with increased risk compared with non-exposed patients [3].

Experimental data from Schurgers et al. showed that in rats, co-administration of vitamin K1 with warfarin restored hepatic coagulation factor activity but failed to activate peripheral VKDPs such as MGP, leading to medial calcification [104]. Only warfarin cessation followed by high-dose vitamin K1 or K2 restored intravascular MGP activation and achieved partial regression of calcification. These findings are supported by the German National Registry [9], where 52% of 253 calciphylaxis cases were receiving VKAs prior to disease onset. Consequently, VKA withdrawal (25%) and vitamin K supplementation (18%) have emerged as therapeutic strategies.

A related clinical entity, non-uremic warfarin-associated calciphylaxis (WAC), has been described in patients without CKD, predominantly females [105] after prolonged exposure. Unlike uremic calciphylaxis, WAC appears to have higher survival when identified early and managed by switching warfarin to heparin or low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) [105, 106].

Despite these risks, anticoagulation in dialysis is largely guided by atrial fibrillation recommendations derived from non-ESKD cohorts. Observational studies suggest a paradoxical increase in stroke risk, up to 8-fold [107], in dialysis patients on warfarin. Consequently, personalized approaches and the use of non-vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulants (NOACs) are increasingly advocated [108].

Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) and vitamin K pathways

Direct Oral Anticoagulants (DOACs), such as apixaban, do not interfere with vitamin K recycling or γ-carboxylation of MGP and therefore offer a mechanistic advantage in patients at risk of calciphylaxis requiring anticoagulation. Although CKD-specific randomized trials are lacking, retrospective series suggest DOACs can be safely used [109, 110]. They may be associated with less progression of VC, although bleeding risk must always be considered when selecting therapy [111].

Future directions and research gaps

Development of reliable biomarkers for vitamin K status

Reliable biomarkers of vitamin K status are needed to identify patients at high risk for calciphylaxis, guide early intervention, and monitor treatment response. Research validating VKDPs against clinical endpoints (e.g. wound score and quality of life measures) is needed to establish these markers as tools in clinical practice.

Mechanistic studies on Gla protein biology

Observational data show lower cMGP in calciphylaxis cases versus controls, implicating vitamin K deficiency in disease pathogenesis independent of warfarin exposure [14]. Experimental and translational research should clarify how vitamin K status, γ-carboxylase activity, and MGP conformational states influence VSMC phenotype, microvascular thrombosis, and calcification. Insights into Gla protein regulation could identify novel intervention targets beyond supplementation.

Precision medicine approaches using vitamin K biomarkers

Precision medicine frameworks leveraging clinical factors, vitamin K biomarkers, genomics, and metabolomics could enable individualized therapeutic approaches. Biomarker-enriched trial designs may improve efficiency in rare diseases like calciphylaxis by identifying subgroups most likely to benefit from vitamin K–centred interventions or DOAC-based anticoagulation strategies.

Ongoing clinical trials

The BEAT-Calci adaptive platform trial (NCT05018221) is a multi-centre international study evaluating multiple therapies for calciphylaxis, including vitamin K1 as a targeted intervention. Using an adaptive design, the trial tests vitamin K1 alongside sodium thiosulfate, magnesium citrate, and dialysis membrane modifications, with arms adjusted according to accumulating evidence. Wound healing, measured using the 8-point BEAT-Calci Wound Assessment Scale, is the primary outcome, with secondary endpoints including pain and mortality. The trial began recruitment in 2021, aims to enrol 350 participants, and includes 26 weeks of active follow-up with longer-term passive follow-up extending to 4.5 years.

The Evaluation of Vitamin K Supplementation for Calcific Uremic Arteriolopathy (VitK-CUA) study was a pilot RCT that randomized patients to vitamin K1 or placebo; results have not been published (NCT02278692).

Future randomized controlled trials

Future studies should determine whether vitamin K supplementation should be targeted to patients with biochemical evidence of functional vitamin K deficiency, rather than administered empirically. Moreover, despite mechanistic links between vitamin K deficiency and calciphylaxis and observational reports of benefit, no RCTs have demonstrated that vitamin K supplementation improves clinically meaningful outcomes. Trials in broader CKD populations show improved vitamin K status but uncertain effects on VC progression. Well-designed RCTs stratified by baseline vitamin K status and incorporating validated clinical and biomarker endpoints are urgently needed to confirm efficacy, define optimal dosing, and identify responsive subgroups.

CONCLUSION

Vitamin K–dependent pathways are central to calciphylaxis pathophysiology, linking impaired γ-carboxylation of Gla proteins with vascular calcification and clinical disease. Avoiding vitamin K antagonism in anticoagulation and vitamin K supplementation are mechanistically plausible preventive and therapeutic strategies. However, high-quality randomized trial evidence is lacking, and current practice relies on observational data and mechanistic insights. Given the rarity and severity of calciphylaxis, future research integrating validated vitamin K biomarkers, mechanistic studies of Gla protein biology, and adaptive trial designs such as BEAT-Calci will be essential for evidence-based, individualized management.
