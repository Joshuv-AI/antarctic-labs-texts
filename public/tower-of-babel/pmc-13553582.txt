Patterns and Risk Factors of Aseptic Loosening in a Hydroxyapatite-Coated Cementless Quadrangular Taper Femoral Stem: A Single-Center Cohort Study
Authors: Rohit Bansal, Christiaan H Righolt, Trevor C Gascoyne, Eric R Bohm, Colin D Burnell, Thomas R Turgeon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere have been several reports of metaphyseal debonding of hydroxyapatite (HA)-coated cementless quadrangular taper femoral stems leading to revisions for aseptic loosening. This study aimed to assess the characteristics and risk factors of this type of revision.MethodsA single-center cohort of total hip arthroplasty patients who received a cementless HA-coated Corail stem between 2006-2023 was identified. Cox proportional hazard models were used to estimate the hazard ratios (HRs) and confidence intervals (CIs) of the association between implant/patient characteristics and revision for aseptic loosening. Dorr type and canal fill was assessed for all patients undergoing revision.ResultsThe study identified 3876 patients leading to 27 aseptic loosening revisions and 91 other revisions. Stem size <10 was associated with aseptic loosening, HR = 9.1 (95% CI 3.4-24.2). Taller patients, metal-on-metal bearings, and collarless stems were all associated with higher risk. Half of patients with aseptic loosening had Dorr type A femurs and half had undersized stems. Initial radiographs showed little to no radiolucency for these patients. Radiolucency increased after several postoperative years. Only 20% of patients with other reasons for revision had undersized stems and these patients exhibited less radiolucency.ConclusionsSmaller, undersized HA-coated cementless quadrangular taper stems appear to be at significantly increased risk of aseptic loosening in midterm follow-up. Surgeons using quadrangle tapered cementless stems should be cautious when broaching suggests a stem size smaller than preoperative templating recommended, especially in patients with narrow femoral canals and should consider using a collar whenever possible with this stem design.Keywords: Total hip arthroplasty, Aseptic loosening, Hydroxyapatite stem, Revision

Introduction

Total hip arthroplasty (THA) leads to substantial improvement in function [[1], [2], [3]] and health-related quality of life [4] in patients with hip osteoarthritis. Stable fixation is key for long-term survivorship of THA and can be achieved with cemented or press-fit components. For implants that fail and undergo revision, aseptic loosening is the listed diagnosis in 12% of cases [5].

A previous study identified 18 cases in which HA-coated stems where revised and the hydroxyapatite (HA) coating was no longer present after the stem was removed, a phenomenon described as “metaphyseal debonding” [6]. Another study noted some cases of aseptic loosening of an HA-coated cementless quadrangular taper femoral stem displayed a similar phenomenon and attributed this to metal-on-metal bearings [7]. A later case study of a single patient reported proximal loosening of the stem, while the distal stem remained well-fixed [8]. Despite these studies, literature on the aseptic loosening of HA-coated stems remains scarce and the possible etiology behind this process remains poorly understood.

This study aimed to explore the risk factors of revisions for aseptic loosening of HA-coated stems and assess radiolucency along revised stems to understand which patients are at risk of this complication and what radiographic evidence may exist.

Material and methods

A cohort study was performed at an academic center consisting of 6 fellowship-trained arthroplasty surgeons. Every patient assessed by the group is asked to consent to be prospectively enrolled in a research database for future retrospective studies. Only patients who had consented to participate in research studies and were part of the institutional research database were included (approximately 90% of all patients). This study was approved by the local Institutional Review Board.

Cohort study for revisions for aseptic loosening

The study cohort consisted of all patients who were part of the research database and who received a primary THA with a collared or collarless cementless HA-coated quadrangular tapered stem (Corail; DePuy Synthes, Warsaw, IN) between January 1, 2006, and November 14, 2023, through a lateral or posterior approach. Two patients were excluded who received a revision stem during their primary THA.

Patient sex, age, height, weight, and body mass index were collected from the research database. Subsequent revisions were identified from the institutional research database and revisions were classified as aseptic loosening, when entered as aseptic loosening or osteolysis. We included osteolysis in this group as this phenomenon could be interpreted as osteolysis due to the HA debonding that is clearly visible in some of these explanted stems. A representative patient’s radiographs are shown in Figure 1. The stem is well-fixed initially, and radiolucency only becomes apparent after several years. All other reasons for revision were classified as “other.”

Anteroposterior radiographs of a single patient between primary surgery and revision surgery for aseptic loosening.

Cox proportional hazard models were used to estimate the hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) of the association between patient and implant characteristics and mid-term revision for aseptic loosening. Time-at-risk started at the primary surgery and ended at the earliest of the date of the first revision, the date of death of the patient (N = 225), the end of the study period, or 3650 days after the primary surgery. Follow-up was limited to 10 years, as previous studies noted this as a phenomenon primarily occurring several years after surgery [6,7]. There was no a priori hypothesis on the etiology of this phenomenon or its behavior in relation to other possible outcomes, so there was no assumption that other reasons for revision were a competing risk. In arthroplasty, it is generally accepted to ignore competing risks when the proportion of patients with a competing risk is 10% or less [9]. As the proportion of patients with a revision (at any time, including for loosening) was around 3% and the proportion that died (at any time) was around 6%, competing risk analysis was not performed. Crude and adjusted analyses were performed and included the factors with a statistically significant effect in the final models.

Radiographic review of radiolucency

All available preoperative and postoperative radiographs for patients in the study cohort that had a later revision were examined. The proximal-distal length of radiolucency in each Gruen zone was measured [10] for each anteroposterior radiograph and canal fill was classified as adequate or undersized. Preoperative radiographs were used to determine the Dorr classification [11]; when preoperative radiographs were unavailable, the contralateral femur was assessed from the earliest available postoperative radiograph, if it was intact. Agfa Impax (version 6.6.1.4024; Mortsel, Belgium) and Orthopedic Tools software (version 3.0.2.3; Mortsel, Belgium) were used to measure images. The medial and lateral lengths of radiolucent zones with mid-term (revision within 10 years) and long-term (revision after 10 years) loosening were compared as were these measures for all other revision cases.

Stata 18 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas) was used for all data analysis.

Results

Revisions for aseptic loosening

This study identified 3876 THAs of which 27 were revised for aseptic loosening and 91 for other reasons (Table 1). Patients ultimately revised for aseptic loosening, were younger (median 53 years old vs 67 years old for patients revised for other reasons) and had smaller stems (48% size <10 compared to 7% for patients without revision and 13% for patients revised for other reasons). For the patients revised for aseptic loosening, 21 were revised in the first 10 years, split evenly between men and women (Table 2). Half (11/21) of these patients had a small stem (size <10), 10 patients had a high-offset stem, and 16 patients had a collarless stem. There was no statistically significant difference in time-to-revision for aseptic loosening between size <10 and size 10-14: P = .348 in a two-sample, two-sided student t-test.

Small stem size (size <10) was associated with an increased risk of aseptic loosening, HR = 9.1 (95% CI 3.4-24.2; Table 3). Taller patients, ≥180 cm, had an increased risk of aseptic loosening as well, HR = 3.8 (1.3-11.7). Metal-on-metal bearings and collarless stems were also associated with a higher risk of aseptic loosening in adjusted models. Stem offset, gender, and weight were, however, not associated with an increased risk once adjusted for confounding variables (Table 3).

Radiolucency

Preoperative radiographs for a few (3/21) of the aseptic loosening patients were unavailable for analysis, but it was found that half of the remaining 18 patients had Dorr type A femurs (Table 4). For patients with other reasons for revision, this proportion was only 32% (P = .131 in a one-sided Fisher’s exact test). From postoperative radiographs, it was noted that half (10/20) of aseptic loosening patients had undersized stems, whereas this was only noted for one-fifth (15/75) of patients later revised for other reasons (P = .010 in a one-sided Fisher’s exact test).

Patients with aseptic loosening generally developed radiolucency several years after evidence of initial proximal and distal fixation. The radiolucency initiated proximally, generally on the medial and lateral sides of the femoral stem and then progressed distally over time. The radiolucent lines were typically longer on the lateral than the medial side for these patients and, in some cases, progressed distally along the entire stem. In contrast, patients revised for other reasons mostly displayed radiolucency only on 1 side of the stem (medial or lateral), if it developed at all (Fig. 2).

Discussion

This study found that taller, younger patients with small stems were at an increased risk of revision for aseptic loosening and that this risk was also higher for collarless stems and metal-on-metal bearings. Dorr type A femurs were more common in these patients and that radiographic evidence of loosening was more pronounced on the lateral side of the stem, although the medial side typically had substantial radiolucency as well.

Earlier reports on the metaphyseal debonding of these stems seem to have linked this to the use of metal-on-metal bearings [6,7]. This was seen as an alternative failure mode for metal-on-metal bearings. These types of bearings are no longer used [12], yet we found these failures of aseptic loosening still occur for other bearings. Metal-on-metal bearings had an HR of 2.2 (95% CI 1.3-3.7) for these stems as well in a British registry study [13].

A study of this stem in the Norwegian registry noted standard collared stems were at reduced risk of all-cause revision, HR = 0.4 (95% CI 0.3-0.6), and revision for aseptic loosening, HR = 0.3 (95% CI 0.2-0.5) [14]. An Australian registry study showed a similar effect, and noted that collared stems had lower revision risk due to a lower rate of late periprosthetic fractures [15]. A meta-analysis with a high amount of heterogeneity noted that this difference in periprosthetic fracture risk was the main difference between collared and collarless versions of this stem. Finite element analysis showed that there were no significant differences between collared and collarless versions of this stem in terms of load transfer and stability of the stem [16]. A lab study of these stems similarly showed no difference in stability between collarless and collared stems [17].

Several registry studies have looked at small stems and all-cause revision and reported increased risks for these stems. The National Joint Registry defined small as size 8-10 and reported a HR of 1.8 (95% CI 1.4-2.4) [13]. The Australian registry compared sizes 8-9, as did this study, and noted most revisions were for loosening and reported an all-cause revision HR of 6.2 (95% CI 3.8-10.1) [18]. The Norwegian registry noted that sizes 8-11 had an HR of all-cause revision of 1.4 (95% CI 1.1-1.6) [14].

A 172-patient radiographic study of this same stem found that patients with any abnormal radiographic findings were typically younger, had stem size ≤12, and an increased femoral offset [19]. A 636-patient study found that radiolucency in Gruen zone 7 (medial-proximal) occurred in 93 patients, the vast majority of which had collarless stems [20]. These authors also noted that the odds ratio of zone 7 radiolucency was 2.6 (95% CI 1.1-6.2) if a stem was undersized by 2 or more sizes [20]. A follow-up of this study with radiographic analysis at 10 years identified the same risk factors [21]. A study of 1337 THAs with these stems noted that 20% of cases were undersized [22], which is the proportion of undersized stems found in this study in patients revised for other reasons than aseptic loosening. They also noted that undersizing is more common in smaller stem sizes, 51% of size 9 and 56% of size 8 stems were undersized according to their radiographic review [22]. A lab study showed that distal micromotion is significantly higher for undersized stems than appropriately sized stems [17], which itself is a predictor for later aseptic loosening [23,24].

Based on the results of this study, the authors hypothesize that these cases of aseptic loosening are largely due to the use of undersized stems. Younger patients with presumably more dense bone and Dorr type A femurs create the impression at surgery of a stable broach and implant prompting surgeons to accept an undersized implant. The increased risk in taller patients (height ≥180 cm) supports this hypothesis, as taller individuals generally have larger femurs and consequently stems size 8 or 9 would generally be undersized for them. Undersized stems are more likely to require high offset to restore tissue balancing and femoral offset and minimize impingement. The high offset femoral components for this system were historically only available as collarless stems. This appears to explain some of this study’s findings of aseptic loosening in collarless versions of this stem. This would also explain the recent findings from the Australian registry for which small high-offset versions of this stem were at increased risk of loosening [25].While the initial press-fit of the undersized stem was adequate for initial fixation, the flexibility of the small titanium without adequate bone support appears to have led to progressive proximal to distal aseptic loosening. Unlike Courtin et al. [19] who suggest this as a reason for radiological abnormalities at a single follow-up time, this study suggests that these stems initially appear stable and osseointegrated upon early radiographic examination. The authors further hypothesize that the metaphyseal debonding of the HA coating is evidence of initial osseointegration and that mechanical forces separate this osseointegrated coating from the metal for some patients (see Fig. 3 eg, extracted stems).

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study is the large patient cohort with a single stem design. Despite the large number of patients, the number of revisions for aseptic loosening is relatively small, which limits the precision of the estimates. A further limitation is that the radiographic analysis was only feasible for digital images and those older analog radiographs were unable to be retrieved. Regardless, these radiographic results form a consistent hypothesis in combination with the regression results.

As implant details are entered into the institutional research database using bar code scanners in the operating room, it is unlikely that errors in implant characteristics affect the results. Reason for revision is noted by the surgeon right after surgery, but would be adjusted when infection is identified from culture and sensitivity results. It is unlikely that a surgeon would not classify a poorly fixed stem as either aseptic loosening or osteolysis, with perhaps the exception of infection, in which case the etiology of this process would have been different. The radiographic measurements have inherent measurement error of approximately 1 millimeter. This does not affect the interpretation of these results, as the length of radiolucency is at least an order of magnitude larger.

Conclusions

In this study, patients receiving small HA-coated cementless quadrangular taper femoral stems were at 9-fold risk of aseptic loosening. Undersized stems were far more common in the aseptic loosing revisions than in revisions for all other causes. Subjects with height >180 cm were also at a significantly increased risk suggesting that these small, undersized stems are at increased risk of aseptic loosing despite initial fixation. Surgeons using quadrangle tapered cementless stems should be cautious when broaching suggests a stem size that would be smaller than templating would suggest, especially in patients with narrow femoral canals and should consider using collared implants with this stem design, where possible.
