An algebraic approach to circle packing
Daniel V. Mathews, Orion Zymaris

Abstract
. We show that for a finite simplicial complex triangulating a disc, its Euclidean circle packings can be found by solving a system of polynomial equations. We also present a similar system of equations for unbranched circle packings. The variables in these equations are associated to corners of triangles in the complex, with one equation for each interior vertex, interior edge, and face. The vertex equations are generalisations of the Descartes circle theorem, of higher degree but more symmetric than those previously found by the authors. We also provide some connections between the spinorial approach of previous work of the authors, and classical Euclidean geometry.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Overview

In this paper we present an approach to certain circle packing problems based on solving systems of polynomial equations. Given a finite simplicial complex
K
triangulating a disc, we introduce a system of variables and polynomial equations, which we call the
circle packing equations
for
K
. We show that the positive real solutions of this system of equations correspond precisely to certain circle packings realising
K
. The variables correspond to the corners of triangles in
K
, and the circle packing equations consist of a
triangle equation
for each triangle of
K
,
edge equations
for each interior edge of
K
, and
vertex equations
for each interior vertex of
K
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
K
be a finite simplicial complex triangulating an oriented disc. Then positive real solutions of the circle packing equations for
K
correspond bijectively to similarity classes of Euclidean circle packings realising
K
.

Thus, the set of (similarity classes of) circle packings realising
K
has the structure of a
semialgebraic set
, given by the circle packing equations and the inequalities that each variable be positive.

The circle packing equations are somewhat analogous to Thurston’s approach to finding hyperbolic structures on triangulated 3-manifolds, using gluing equations
[
7
,
8
]
. Indeed, it is well known that circle packing theory is closely related to the theory of hyperbolic 3-manifolds, at least since Thurston’s proof of the Koebe–Andreev–Thurston theorem using hyperbolic geometry in
[
7
, ch. 13]
.

Our approach to circle packing in this paper can be regarded as a development of the spinorial approach taken in
[
4
]
. Although the results of this paper do not require spinors, they depend crucially on the variables used in that work, there denoted
m_{j}
, which arose from spinors. (We also give alternative proofs of some of our results here using spinors.) There is a sense, which we will explain, in which our circle packing equations can be regarded allegorically as spinorial “square roots of geometry”. We also provide some connections between this spinorial approach and some classic Euclidean triangle geometry. This connection relies crucially on an observation of Ian Agol
[
1
]
, that the variables
m_{j}
introduced in
[
4
]
have a simple trigonometric interpretation.

Along the way, we give a new generalisation of the Descartes circle theorem, i.e. an equation relating the curvatures of circles in a flower. This equation has higher degree in the
m_{j}
than the equation given in
[
4
]
but is more symmetric. The vertex equations in our circle packing equations are precisely these generalised Descartes equations. We also discuss the relationship of these Descartes equations to those of
[
4
]
, addressing a minor issue in that paper (not affecting the result) in the process.

Theorem 1.2

(Symmetric generalised Descartes circle theorem)
.

Let the circles
C_{\infty}
and
C_{j}
(
j\in\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
) in an
n
-flower have curvatures
\kappa_{\infty},\kappa_{j}
respectively. Define
m_{j}
for
j\in\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
as

(1.3)

m_{j}=\sqrt{\left(\frac{\kappa_{j}}{\kappa_{\infty}}+1\right)\left(\frac{\kappa_{j-1}}{\kappa_{\infty}}+1\right)-1}.

Then

(1.4)

\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left(m_{j}+i\right)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left(m_{j}-i\right).

Similarly to
[
4
]
, complex numbers are essential to the proof but a mere convenience in stating equation (

). The left and right hand sides of (
1.4
) are complex conjugates of each other, so their equality is equivalent to their imaginary part being zero. The equation above is thus equivalent to

(1.5)

\sum_{I}(-1)^{\frac{n-|I|-1}{2}}\prod_{j\in I}m_{j}=0,

where the sum is over subsets
I\subseteq\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
such that
|I|\equiv n-1
mod
2
.

When

, the equation becomes

m_{1}m_{2}+m_{2}m_{3}+m_{3}m_{1}=1

from which the classic Descartes equation can be recovered.

1.2.
Circle packing equations

Our general reference for circle packing is Stephenson’s classic book

Let

C_{v}
for each vertex

The triangles around an interior vertex

1.3.
Geometric meaning of the circle packing equations

The variables
m_{\Delta,v}
in our circle packing equations are related to curvatures of circles by equations like (
1.3
), but they also have a straightforward geometric interpretation: they are cotangents of half the angles in the corresponding corners of triangles in the map
\Phi
realising
K
.

In
[
4
]
, the variables
m_{j}
arose from spinorial considerations, as follows. Let
K
be a combinatorial
n
-flower, with central vertex
v_{\infty}
and other vertices
v_{j}
over
j\in\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
in anticlockwise cyclic order. A circle packing realising
K
, after inversion in the central circle, can be regarded as a collection of horocycles in the conformal disc model of the hyperbolic plane, and by the first author’s work
[
2
]
these can be associated to spinors
(\xi,\eta)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Thus we obtain spinors
(\xi_{j},\eta_{j})
for each
j\in\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
, and we define associated complex variables
z_{j}=\xi_{j}+i\eta_{j}
. Then then
m_{j}
arose as the real part of
z_{j-1}\overline{z_{j}}
.

Agol’s observation
[
1
]
leads to the equality

m_{j}=\cot\left(\frac{\theta_{j}}{2}\right),

where
\theta_{j}
is the Euclidean angle subtended at
\Phi(v_{\infty})
by
\Phi(v_{j-1})
and
\Phi(v_{j})
.

More generally, in a realisation
\Phi\colon K\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
, each triangle
\Delta
of
K
is realised as a Euclidean triangle. We denote by
\theta_{\Delta,v}
the angle in the corner
(\Delta,v)
. Then we have the following.

Proposition 1.11
.

Let
K
be a simplicial complex triangulating an oriented disc. Suppose
\{m_{\Delta,v}\}_{(\Delta,v)\in C(K)}
is a positive real solution of the circle packing equations for
K
, corresponding by Theorem
1.1
to a circle packing with realisation
\Phi\colon K\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
, circle curvatures
\{\kappa_{v}\}_{v\in V(K)}
, and angles
\{\theta_{\Delta,v}\}_{(\Delta,v)\in C(K)}
. Then each
m_{\Delta,v}
is related to the angles
\theta_{\Delta,v}
by

m_{\Delta,v}=\cot\left(\frac{\theta_{\Delta,v}}{2}\right),

and to the curvatures
\kappa_{v}
by

(1.12)

m_{\Delta,v}=\sqrt{\left(\frac{\kappa_{u}}{\kappa_{v}}+1\right)\left(\frac{\kappa_{w}}{\kappa_{v}}+1\right)-1}=\frac{\sqrt{\kappa_{u}\kappa_{v}+\kappa_{v}\kappa_{w}+\kappa_{w}\kappa_{u}}}{\kappa_{v}}.

where
u,v,w
are the vertices of
\Delta
.

The same relations hold for solutions of the unbranched circle packing equations, since they form a subset of solutions of the circle packing equations.

As we will see, the vertex equations can be interpreted as saying that the angles around a vertex sum to a multiple of
2\pi
; the unbranched vertex equations as saying that angles sum to exactly
2\pi
; and the triangle equations as saying that the angles in a triangle sum to
\pi
. We prove this in Section
2.2
. In other words, angle sum conditions on the angles
\theta_{\bullet}
yield polynomial conditions in the variables
m_{\bullet}=\cot(\theta_{\bullet}/2)
.

The edge equations can also be interpreted via elementary Euclidean geometry, relating the cotangents of half angles in a triangle to the curvatures of its Soddy circles. The relevant Euclidean fact is Lemma
2.4
and we apply it to edge equations in Lemma
4.2
.

There are several senses in which the circle packing equations are, roughly speaking, “spinorial”. In addition to the variables
m_{\bullet}
arising naturally from spinors as in
[
4
]
, it is clear from the above that the
half
-angles in the realisation
\Phi
are crucial variables. These half angles arise quite literally from square roots of complex numbers. As we will see in Lemma
2.3
, for an angle
\theta
and its corresponding
m
variable,

e^{i\theta}=\frac{m+i}{m-i}.

The numerator and denominator being conjugates, it follows that
\arg(m_{j}+i)=\arg(m_{j}-i)=\theta/2
, so that each
m_{j}+i
is, up to a positive real multiple, a square root of
e^{i\theta}
. The vertex and triangle equations are derived straightforwardly from elementary complex number geometry using these
(m_{j}+i)
.

From the variables
m_{\Delta,v}
in the circle packing equations, the curvatures
\kappa_{v}
of the circles
C_{v}
can be recovered, but only up to an overall constant. Indeed, as seen in (
1.12
),
m_{\Delta,v}
only depends on the
ratios
of curvatures. If all
m_{\Delta,v}
are known, then along an edge
e
with vertices
v,w
, letting
\Delta
be a triangle adjacent to
e
, we have

\frac{m_{\Delta,v}}{m_{\Delta,w}}=\frac{\kappa_{w}}{\kappa_{v}}.

This follows straightforwardly from using the final expression of (
1.12
) for each
m_{\bullet}
. Indeed, the edge equations (
1.7
) can be interpreted as saying that this ratio of curvatures should not depend on the choice of
\Delta
adjacent to
e
. The fact that the
m_{\bullet}
only determine the
\kappa_{v}
up to an overall factor is why Theorem
1.1
refers to
similarity
classes of circle packings. We can say that the curvatures
\{\kappa_{v}\}_{v\in V}
are determined up to an overall factor, or as a point of a projective space
\mathbb{R}_{+}P^{n-1}
, which is
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
modulo scaling by positive factors, and homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n-1}
.

By the boundary value theorem of circle packing theory (e.g.
[
6
, thm. 11.6]
), after specifying the curvatures of boundary circles and branch structure at each internal vertex, there is a circle packing realising
K
, unique up to isometry. Combining this with Theorem
1.1
we obtain the following.

Corollary 1.13
.

Let the boundary vertices of
K
be
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
, let
\beta
be a branch structure on
K
, and let
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
. Then there exists a unique positive real solution
\{m_{\Delta,v}\}_{(\Delta,v)\in C(K)}
of the circle packing equations for
K
such that the curvatures
\{\kappa_{v}\}_{v\in V(K)}
determined by the
m_{\Delta,v}
satisfy
[\kappa_{v_{1}}:\cdots:\kappa_{v_{n}}]=[c_{1}:\cdots:c_{n}]
.

\hfill\square

Thus, for each fixed branch structure on
K
, the space of real positive solutions of the circle packing equations for
K
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}_{+}P^{n-1}\cong\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n-1}
, where
n
is the number of boundary vertices of
K
. The overall space of real positive solutions is isomorphic to a disjoint union of copies of
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n-1}
, one for each branch structure.

This means that the circle packing equations contain no redundancy; all the equations are independent. Indeed, letting
V=|V(K)|
,
E=|E(K)|
and
F=|F(K)|
, the number of variables is
3F
, the number of vertex equations is
|V_{int}(K)|=V-n
, the number of edge equations is
|E_{int}(K)|=E-n
, and the number of triangle equations is
F
. So the number of variables minus equations is

3F-\left(V+E+F-2n\right)=\left(-V+E-F\right)-2E+3F+2n=n-1,

the dimension of the space of solutions. The last equality uses Euler’s formula and the fact that, since each face of
K
is a triangle,
3F=2E-n
.

The circle packing equations are analogous to the Thurston gluing equations in several ways. Given an ideal triangulation of a hyperbolic 3-manifold, a cusp triangulation is a finite simplicial complex triangulating a torus. Thurston associates a variable to each corner of each triangle (e.g.
z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}
and
w_{1},w_{2},w_{3}
in the figure-8 knot complement example of
[
7
, ch. 4]
). The three variables in a triangle satisfy relations, and the variables around a vertex satisfy gluing equations multiplying to
1
, with an alternative “unbranched” logarithmic formulation. By Mostow rigidity, there is a unique solution. Solutions to the circle packing equations are similarly unique and rigid once boundary and branching conditions are specified. Similar relationships are also discussed in Thurston’s notes
[
7
, ch. 13]
.

1.5.
Structure of this paper

In Section
2
we discuss preliminary results from classical Euclidean triangle geometry, and the algebraic results we need relating angles and cotangents. In Section
3
we consider generalised Descartes circle theorems and prove Theorem

, using elementary and spinorial methods. In Section
4
we show that a circle packing satisfies the circle packing equations, and in Section

Lemma 2.2
.

\cos\theta_{A}=\frac{m_{A}^{2}-1}{m_{A}^{2}+1}

Of course, the

Proof.

By the cosine rule, using
\kappa_{\bullet}=1/r_{\bullet}
, and noting

m_{A}^{2}\pm 1=(L_{\Delta}\pm\kappa_{A}^{2})/\kappa_{A}^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\cos\theta_{A}

\displaystyle=\frac{(r_{A}+r_{B})^{2}+(r_{A}+r_{C})^{2}-(r_{B}+r_{C})^{2}}{2(r_{A}+r_{B})(r_{A}+r_{C})}=\frac{r_{A}^{2}+r_{A}r_{B}+r_{A}r_{C}-r_{B}r_{C}}{r_{A}^{2}+r_{A}r_{B}+r_{A}r_{C}+r_{B}r_{C}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa_{B}\kappa_{C}+\kappa_{A}\kappa_{C}+\kappa_{A}\kappa_{B}-\kappa_{A}^{2}}{\kappa_{B}\kappa_{C}+\kappa_{A}\kappa_{C}+\kappa_{A}\kappa_{B}+\kappa_{A}^{2}}=\frac{L_{\Delta}-\kappa_{A}^{2}}{L_{\Delta}+\kappa_{A}^{2}}=\frac{m_{A}^{2}-1}{m_{A}^{2}+1}.

\hfill\square

Various other calculations then follow straightforwardly.

Lemma 2.3
.

(1)

\sin\theta_{A}=\frac{2m_{A}}{m_{A}^{2}+1}

(2)

e^{i\theta_{A}}=\frac{m_{A}+i}{m_{A}-i}

(3)

\arg(m_{A}+i)=\theta_{A}/2
.

Proof.

For (1) we calculate

\sin^{2}\theta_{A}=1-\cos^{2}\theta_{A}=1-\frac{\left(m_{A}^{2}-1\right)^{2}}{\left(m_{A}^{2}+1\right)^{2}}=\frac{4m_{A}^{2}}{\left(m_{A}^{2}+1\right)^{2}}.

Since
m_{A}
and
\sin\theta_{A}
are both positive, taking a square root gives the desired equality. Then

e^{i\theta_{A}}=\cos\theta_{A}+i\sin\theta_{A}=\frac{m_{A}^{2}-1}{m_{A}^{2}+1}+\frac{2im_{A}}{m_{A}^{2}+1}=\frac{\left(m_{A}+i\right)^{2}}{\left(m_{A}-i\right)\left(m_{A}+i\right)}=\frac{m_{A}+i}{m_{A}-i},

giving (2). As
m_{A}+i
and
m_{A}-i

\arg(m_{A}-i)=-\arg(m_{A}+i)

Lemma 2.4

\cot\left(\frac{\theta_{A}}{2}\right)=m_{A}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\cot\left(\theta_{A}/2\right)}{\cot\left(\theta_{B}/2\right)}=\frac{m_{A}}{m_{B}}=\frac{\kappa_{B}}{\kappa_{A}}

In classic Euclidean geometry notation, with the side lengths of a triangle written as

Angle and cotangent identities

In this section we prove some elementary algebraic facts we need in the sequel. These all concern variables of the form
m_{\bullet}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and

Lemma 2.5
.

Suppose
m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3}\in(0,\pi)
satisfy
m_{j}=\cot(\theta_{j}/2)
for

. Then the following are equivalent.

(1)

\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}+\theta_{3}=\pi
.

(2)

m_{1}m_{2}m_{3}=m_{1}+m_{2}+m_{3}
.

Proof.

We have
\arg(m_{j}+i)=\theta_{j}/2
. So if (1) holds then
\arg(m_{1}+i)(m_{2}+i)(m_{3}+i)=\pi/2
, so its real part
m_{1}m_{2}m_{3}-m_{1}-m_{2}-m_{3}
must be zero. Conversely, if (2) holds then the real part of
(m_{1}+i)(m_{2}+i)(m_{3}+i)
is zero, so its argument
(\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}+\theta_{3})/2
is equal to
\pi/2
mod
\pi
. Hence
\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}+\theta_{3}
is equal to
\pi
mod
2\pi
. As each
\theta_{j}\in(0,\pi)
, we have
\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}+\theta_{3}=\pi
.

\hfill\square

Lemma 2.6
.

Suppose
m_{j}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
\theta_{j}\in(0,\pi)
satisfy

for

(1)

\sum_{j=1}^{d}\theta_{j}=2\pi(\beta+1)
for some integer

(2)

\sum_{I}(-1)^{\frac{d-|I|-1}{2}}\prod_{j\in I}m_{j}=0

Proof.

Again

\hfill\square

3.2.
Spinorial approach

We can also give a proof of Theorem

Under the (inverse) Cayley map

In this section we show that solutions to the circle packing equations describe circle packings. So let

Proof of Theorem
1.1
and Proposition
1.11
.

If
N=1
then
K
consists of a single triangle, so there are precisely
3
variables
m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}
and a single circle packing equation, that for a single triangle. Let
\{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}\}
be a solution with all
m_{j}>0
, and let
\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3}\in(0,\pi)
be defined by (
5.1
), i.e.
m_{j}=\cot(\theta_{j}/2)
. As
m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}
satisfy the triangle equation, by Lemma
2.5
then
\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}+\theta_{3}=\pi
. So
\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3}
are the angles of a Euclidean triangle. Let
\Phi\colon K\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
realise this triangle, and draw its Soddy circles, with curvatures
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2},\kappa_{3}
. By Lemma
2.4
, our solution
m_{\bullet}
coincides with the parameters
m_{\bullet}
as defined in Section
2.1
by
m_{j}=\sqrt{L_{\Delta}}/{\kappa_{j}}
where
L_{\Delta}=\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}+\kappa_{2}\kappa_{3}+\kappa_{3}\kappa_{1}
. Thus we have a circle packing as claimed, well defined up to similarity, and (
5.1
) holds. Indeed, any
\Phi
realising this solution must have angles
\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\theta_{3}
and so the realisation is unique up to similarity.

Now suppose
N\geq 2
, so Lemma
5.2
applies and we consider the two cases in turn. Let
\{m_{\Delta,v}\}_{(\Delta,v)\in C(K)}
be a solution to the circle packing equations for
K
with all
m_{\Delta,v}>0
, and let
\{\theta_{\Delta,v}\}_{(\Delta,v)\in C(K)}
be defined by (
5.1
), i.e.
m_{\Delta,v}=\cot(\theta_{\Delta,v}/2)
, with all
\theta_{\Delta,v}\in(0,\pi)
.

Suppose
K
is obtained from two smaller complexes
K_{1},K_{2}
, by gluing along an edge
e
. Then the circle packing equations for
K
consist of those for
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
, with no new variables, and one new edge equation. Letting the triangles adjacent to
e
in
K_{1},K_{2}
be
\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2}
, and the endpoints of
e
be
v_{1},v_{2}
, the situation is as in Figure
1
(centre) and the new edge equation is
m_{\Delta_{1},v_{1}}m_{\Delta_{2},v_{2}}=m_{\Delta_{1},v_{2}}m_{\Delta_{2},v_{1}}
. So the solution
\{m_{\Delta,v}\}_{(\Delta,v)\in C(K)}
of the circle packing equations for
K
contains solutions
\{m_{\Delta,v}\}_{(\Delta,v)\in C(K_{1})}
and
\{m_{\Delta,v}\}_{(\Delta,v)\in C(K_{2})}
of the circle packing equations for
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
. By induction, these yield realisations
\Phi_{1}\colon K_{1}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\Phi_{2}\colon K_{2}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
, unique up to similarity, with circle radii
\{r_{v}^{1}\}_{v\in V(K_{1})}
and
\{r_{v}^{2}\}_{v\in V(K_{2})}
and curvatures
\{\kappa_{v}^{1}\}_{v\in V(K_{1})}
and
\{\kappa_{v}^{2}\}_{v\in V(K_{2})}
respectively, such that the relations of (
5.1
) hold and the
\theta_{\Delta,v}
are realised as the angles arising in
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}
.

In
\Phi_{1}
, the circles corresponding to the endpoints
v_{1},v_{2}
of
e
have radii
r_{v_{1}}^{1},r_{v_{2}}^{1}
and curvatures
\kappa_{v_{1}}^{1},\kappa_{v_{2}}^{1}
respectively, so the length of
e
is
r_{v_{1}}^{1}+r_{v_{2}}^{1}
. Note also that by Lemma
2.4

\frac{m_{\Delta_{1},v_{1}}}{m_{\Delta_{1},v_{2}}}=\frac{\kappa_{v_{2}}^{1}}{\kappa_{v_{1}}^{1}}=\frac{r_{v_{1}}^{1}}{r_{v_{2}}^{1}}.

Similarly in
\Phi_{2}
, the circles corresponding to
v_{1},v_{2}
have radii
r_{v_{1}}^{2},r_{v_{2}}^{2}
and curvatures
\kappa_{v_{1}}^{2},\kappa_{v_{2}}^{2}
respectively, so the length of
e
is
r_{v_{1}}^{2}+r_{v_{2}}^{2}
, and

\frac{m_{\Delta_{2},v_{1}}}{m_{\Delta_{2},v_{2}}}=\frac{\kappa_{v_{2}}^{2}}{\kappa_{v_{1}}^{2}}=\frac{r_{v_{1}}^{2}}{r_{v_{2}}^{2}}.

By the new edge equation
m_{\Delta_{1},v_{1}}m_{\Delta_{2},v_{2}}=m_{\Delta_{1},v_{2}}m_{\Delta_{2},v_{1}}
we thus have

\frac{m_{\Delta_{1},v_{1}}}{m_{\Delta_{1},v_{2}}}=\frac{m_{\Delta_{2},v_{1}}}{m_{\Delta_{2},v_{2}}},\quad\text{i.e.}\quad\frac{\kappa_{v_{1}}^{1}}{\kappa_{v_{2}}^{1}}=\frac{\kappa_{v_{1}}^{2}}{\kappa_{v_{2}}^{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{r_{v_{1}}^{1}}{r_{v_{2}}^{1}}=\frac{r_{v_{1}}^{2}}{r_{v_{2}}^{2}}.

Thus the circles about vertices
v_{1},v_{2}
in
\Phi_{1}
and in
\Phi_{2}
are in the same ratio. Hence there is a unique dilation of
\Phi_{1}
after which their curvatures are equal,
\kappa_{v_{1}}^{1}=\kappa_{v_{1}}^{2}
and
\kappa_{v_{2}}^{1}=\kappa_{v_{2}}^{2}
, as are their radii,
r_{v_{1}}^{1}=r_{v_{1}}^{2}
and
r_{v_{2}}^{1}=r_{v_{2}}^{2}
. Then the side lengths of
e
in
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}
are equal,
r_{v_{1}}^{1}+r_{v_{2}}^{1}=r_{v_{1}}^{2}+r_{v_{2}}^{2}
. Adjusting
\Phi_{1}
by a further orientation-preserving isometry, we may then glue
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}
together along
e
to obtain an realisation
\Phi
of
K
. In this realisation, the circles at
v_{1},v_{2}
from
\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}
are identified. Since we only adjusted the radii and curvatures of
\Phi_{1}
by an overall dilation, the variables
\theta_{\Delta,v}
are still realised as angles. Since the ratios of curvatures in any individual triangle remain unchanged, the relations (
5.1
) continue to hold. Since
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}
are unique up to similarity, their gluing
\Phi
along
e
is also unique up to similarity.

Finally, suppose that
K
is obtained from a smaller complex
K^{\prime}
by gluing a triangle
T
along two consecutive boundary edges
e_{1},e_{2}
of
K^{\prime}
, with endpoints
u_{1},w
and
u_{2},w
respectively. The circle packing equations for
K
then consist of those for
K^{\prime}
and those for the single triangle
T
, with no new variables, one new vertex equation at
w
, and two new edge equations for
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
.
The solution
\{m_{\Delta,v}\}_{(\Delta,v)\in C(K)}
yields solutions
\{m_{\Delta,v}\}_{(\Delta,v)\in C(K^{\prime})}
and
\{m_{\Delta,v}\}_{(\Delta,v)\in C(T)}
of the circle packings for
K^{\prime}
and
T
. By induction, we obtain realisations
\Phi^{\prime}\colon K^{\prime}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\Phi^{T}\colon T\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
, unique up to similarity, with circle radii
\{r^{\prime}_{v}\}_{v\in V(K^{\prime})}
and
\{r^{T}_{v}\}_{v\in V(T)}
and curvatures
\{\kappa^{\prime}_{v}\}_{v\in V(K^{\prime})}
and
\{\kappa^{T}_{v}\}_{v\in V(T)}
, such that the relations of (
5.1
) hold and the
\theta_{\Delta,v}
are realised as angles.

At
w
, we have at least one corner of
K^{\prime}
, and the corner
(T,w)
of
T
. As the relations (
5.1
) hold, by Lemma
2.6
the vertex equation at
w
implies that
\theta_{T,w}
sums with the
\theta_{\Delta,w}
in corners of
K^{\prime}
at
w
to give a positive integer multiple of
2\pi
. As the
\theta_{\Delta,v}
are realised as angles in
\Phi^{\prime}
and
\Phi^{T}
, then
\theta_{T,w}
is just the right angle for
\Phi^{T}
to slot into
\Phi^{\prime}
at
w
.

In
\Phi^{\prime}
, the edges
e_{1},e_{2}
have lengths
r^{\prime}_{u_{1}}+r^{\prime}_{v}
and
r^{\prime}_{u_{2}}+r^{\prime}_{v}
respectively. As in the previous case, the edge equation for
e_{1}
implies that

\frac{\kappa^{\prime}_{u_{1}}}{\kappa^{\prime}_{w}}=\frac{\kappa^{T}_{u_{1}}}{\kappa^{T}_{w}}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{r^{\prime}_{u_{1}}}{r^{\prime}_{w}}=\frac{r^{T}_{u_{1}}}{r^{T}_{w}}.

Similarly, the edge equation for
e_{2}
implies that

\frac{\kappa^{\prime}_{u_{2}}}{\kappa^{\prime}_{w}}=\frac{\kappa^{T}_{u_{2}}}{\kappa^{T}_{w}}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{r^{\prime}_{u_{2}}}{r^{\prime}_{v}}=\frac{r^{T}_{u_{2}}}{r^{T}_{v}}.

Thus we have equalities of ratios

\left[\kappa^{\prime}_{u_{1}}:\kappa^{\prime}_{w}:\kappa^{\prime}_{u_{2}}\right]=\left[\kappa^{T}_{u_{1}}:\kappa^{T}_{w}:\kappa^{T}_{u_{2}}\right]\quad\text{and}\quad\left[r^{\prime}_{u_{1}}:r^{\prime}_{w}:r^{\prime}_{u_{2}}\right]=\left[r^{T}_{u_{1}}:r^{T}_{w}:r^{T}_{u_{2}}\right],

so the circles at
u_{1},w,u_{2}
in
\Phi^{\prime}
and
\Phi^{T}
are in the same ratio. As in the previous case, there is then a unique dilation of
\Phi^{T}
after which
(\kappa^{T}_{u_{1}},\kappa^{T}_{w},\kappa^{T}_{u_{2}})=(\kappa^{\prime}_{u_{1}},\kappa^{\prime}_{w},\kappa^{\prime}_{v_{2}})
and
(r^{T}_{u_{1}},r^{T}_{w},r^{T}_{u_{2}})=(r^{\prime}_{u_{1}},r^{\prime}_{w},r^{\prime}_{u_{2}})
. The side lengths of
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
then agree in
\Phi^{\prime}
and
\Phi^{T}
, so we may then glue
\Phi^{\prime}
and
\Phi^{T}
together to obtain a realisation
\Phi
of
K
, in which the circles at
u_{1},u_{2}
and
w
are identified. As in the previous case, the variables
\theta_{\Delta,v}
are still realised as angles, the relations (
5.1
) continue to hold, and
\Phi
is unique up to similarity.

\hfill\square

Proof of Theorem

The proof is identical to above, except that in the final case, when gluing around the vertex

, since

. Thus the angle sum around

, and
\Phi^{\prime}
and
