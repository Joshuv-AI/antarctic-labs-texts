Ectopic expression of maize gene ZmDUF1645 in rice affects rice grain traits and cold tolerance
Authors: Yulin Chen, Xingzhuo Sun, Peng Zhang, Yanting Li, Tongyue Ouyang, Lihua Li, Jianqing Zhu, Xiaomei Jia, Xiaoying Ye, Jun Zhu, Rongjun Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe DUF protein family is a class of functional proteins containing conserved structural domains that are widely involved in biological processes such as plant seed development, yield formation and response to adversity. Previous research has demonstrated that the heterologous expression of ZmDUF1645 from maize (Zea mays) in rice (Oryza sativa L.) markedly enhances grain yield and influences drought tolerance. However, its effects under other stress conditions remain poorly understood. This study aims to elucidate the role of ZmDUF1645 in rice grain traits and cold tolerance. To this end, the maize gene ZmDUF1645 was expressed in rice, resulting in transgenic plants that exhibited significantly increased yield but reduced grain quality. Further analysis revealed that ZmDUF1645, with functional conservation to its rice homolog OsSGL, exerts pleiotropic effects when ectopically overexpressed in rice. It enhances yield by upregulating GIF1 to promote panicle development, increase grains per panicle, and elevate thousand-grain weight. However, it impairs grain quality (reduced amylose, abnormal starch granules, higher chalkiness) via repressing GBSS1/AGPS2, and compromises cold tolerance by disrupting redox homeostasis, inhibiting superoxide dismutase – SOD; peroxidase - POD; proline - Pro, and downregulating genes related to starch synthesis/energy metabolism. These findings highlight the pleiotropic effects of ZmDUF1645 on rice yield, quality, and stress resistance, offering valuable insights for future crop genetic improvement.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07896-7.Keywords: Rice, Heterologous expression, Grain trait, Grain quality, Cold tolerance

Introduction

Rice (Oryza sativa L.) is the key food crop, providing the staple food for more than half of the world’s population [1, 2]. The demand for rice production and quality is increasing as the population increases and the standard of living improves [3].

Increasing yield has always been an important goal of crop breeding. Rice yield is mainly determined by the number of effective tillers, the number of grains per spike, grain weight and grain filling [4, 5]. The evaluation of rice quality is multifaceted, mainly including processing quality, appearance quality and flavor quality [6–9]. The appearance of good quality rice is full and crystal clear, and the taste of good rice is soft and delicate after cooking, and rich in flavor [10]. Rice with good appearance and flavor is preferred by consumers [11]. The processing quality of rice affects its efficiency and quality in production, such as brown rice rate, refined rice rate, head rice rate and other indicators [12, 13].

Heterologous expression of genes occupies a key position in modern life science research and biotechnology applications [14–16]. Heterologous expression technology plays an important role in plant genetic improvement and exploration of gene function [16, 17]. For example, heterologous expression of the TaPI-PLC1-2B gene enhanced drought and salt tolerance in transgenic rice seedlings [18]. The heterologous expression of AtPLC9 in rice greatly improved the heat tolerance of rice [19]. A miR395 gene was cloned from rice and heterologously expressed in tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum) to reveal the molecular mechanism of miR395 mediated plant sulfate metabolism [20]. Heterologous expression provides a theoretical basis for exploring gene function and analyzing the molecular mechanism of gene expression, and provides a new resource for genetic engineering technology to cultivate new crop varieties for stress resistance.

Proteins containing Domains of Unknown Function (DUFs) constitute a large and diverse group, many of whose biological roles remain uncharacterized. However, emerging evidence suggests their involvement in critical plant processes [21]. Certain DUF families have been specifically associated with abiotic stress responses and the regulation of agronomic traits [22]. For example, proteins with the DUF26 domain, such as OsRMC in rice, have been implicated in salt stress response and jasmonic acid (JA) mediated root development [23]. Similarly, OsSIDP366, a member of the DUF1644 gene family, may contribute to salt and drought resistance, likely through the regulation of stress granules [24]. These findings collectively highlight the potential roles of DUFs in environmental adaptation and yield formation. Of particular relevance to this study is the DUF1645 subfamily. Previous research has shown that overexpression of the rice gene OsSGL, which contains a DUF1645 domain, significantly enhances grain size and yield [25]. Despite its apparent importance in yield improvement, the mechanistic underpinnings of DUF1645 functionality—especially regarding the trade-offs between productivity, grain quality, and stress resilience—remain poorly understood. Furthermore, it is unclear whether DUF1645 orthologs from other species, such as maize, function similarly when expressed in rice. Given the documented role of DUFs in stress adaptation and the established effects of OsSGL on yield, we hypothesize that ZmDUF1645, a maize homolog, may pleiotropically influence key agronomic traits, potentially at the expense of stress tolerance or grain quality.

A recent study demonstrated that the ectopic expression of the maize gene ZmDUF1645 in rice significantly increases grain yield while concurrently reducing drought tolerance, thereby highlighting a trade-off between productivity and stress resilience [26]. However, the role of ZmDUF1645 in grain quality and cold stress response remains entirely unexplored.

Given this trade-off and the unresolved questions regarding its role in additional stress adaptations and grain quality, we hypothesized that ZmDUF1645 might involve multifaceted trade-offs in regulating rice yield. To test this hypothesis, we evaluated the effects of ZmDUF1645 overexpression on grain quality attributes and cold stress response in transgenic rice plants. Our study aims to provide a comprehensive functional assessment of ZmDUF1645, offering new insights into the potential of DUF1645-containing genes for molecular breeding programs that must balance productivity, quality, and environmental resilience.

Materials and methods

Plant material and growing conditions

Rice (O. sativa L. subsp. japonica cv. Nipponbare) was used as a wild type (WT) and material for genetic transformation. Seeds were soaked in 2% (v/v) NaClO for 30 min, rinsed thoroughly with sterile water, and then soaked in the dark at 37 °C for 2 day. The germinated seeds Primer Sequences were then incubated in a greenhouse with a Yoshida hydroponic solution in a cycle of 16 h of light at 28 °C and 8 h of darkness at 25 °C. Light intensity: 144 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹.

Construction of Transgenic plants

The full coding sequence of ZmDUF1645 was amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR). Expression vectors D-163 + 1300 (Derived from the vector pCAMBIA1300 harboring the enhanced CaMV 35 S promoter.) was double-digested with HindⅢ and BamHⅠ endonucleases, then ligated to construct ZmDUF1645 overexpression vectors. Using the O. sativa ssp. japonica cultivar Nipponbare as the genetic background, transgenic lines were generated via Agrobacterium-mediated transformation of callus tissue, and positive lines were validated by RT-qPCR [27]. A total of 15 independent overexpression lines were obtained after positive screening. For the present investigation, the two lines exhibiting the highest transgene expression (OE4 and OE5) were utilized for subsequent experiments. The relative expression levels of ZmDUF1645 in the OE4 and OE5 lines can be found in the Figure S1.

RT-qPCR analysis

The RT-qPCR was conducted using a Bio-Rad CFX96 Real-Time System coupled to a C1000 Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad, USA), with specific amplification systems and reaction programs detailed in Table S1 and Table S2. To accurately evaluate changes in expression of target genes, UBQ5 (LOC_Os01g22490) was used as an internal reference gene [28]. Normalized relative transcript levels were obtained by the 2−ΔΔCT method [29]. Three biological replicates were performed for each reaction using primers detailed in Table S3. All primers were designed using the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Primer-BLAST tool.

Plant growth status and trait determination

Field experiments were conducted on rice plants at the Wenjiang Experimental Base of Sichuan Agricultural University. Before transplanting the rice seedlings, 75 kg ha− 1 P2O5 as phosphorus fertilizer and 150 kg ha− 1 K2O as potash fertilizer were applied to experimental field. Nitrogen (N) fertilizer as urea (46.67% N) was applied with 150 kg ha− 1 N and was split into two applied percentages: (1) 70% for pre-transplanting (basal fertilizers) and 30% for the tillering stage (M1 = 7: 3); and (2) 30% for the pre-transplanting, 30% for the tillering stage, and 40% for the heading stage (M2 = 3: 3: 4). The plant density of rice was 1.8 × 105 plants ha− 1. plant distance × row space = 0.17 m×0.33 m [30]. Yield (kg/ha) = Panicles per m² × Grains per panicle × Percentage of Filled Grains × 1000-grain weight (g)/1,000,000 [31]. Four rice plants per row in the field were selected for measuring plant height and tiller number. Agronomic traits such as the number of grains per spike, thousand-grain weight (electronic balance NJNONITALAB, DNA233B, Nanjing, China), grain length, and grain width (automated rice seed analyzer JLM, mini1600, Shanghai, China) were counted after the rice grains were matured [32]. Detection of amylose content using iodine chromogenic method [33]. Appearance quality of rice was measured using Wanshen SC-E Rice Appearance Quality Test Analyzer. The taste value was determined using the Rice Taste Meter (Satake, Japan).

Observation by optical and scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The middle portion of the grain was gently tapped with the back of a knife to allow for natural breakage; the broken portion was then cut with a sharp blade to form a thin slice about 2–3 mm thick, and the chalky area was observed under a conventional light microscope [34]. Samples for SEM were transversely fractured with a sterile surgical blade to expose the internal structure of the endosperm. The fractured samples were then mounted on aluminum stubs using double-sided carbon adhesive tape. Critical point drying was performed using a Quorum K850 Critical Point Dryer (Quorum Technologies, UK). Following drying, the samples were sputter-coated with an approximately 10 nm layer of gold using a Hitachi E-1045 Ion Sputter Coater (Hitachi High-Tech, Japan) to enhance surface conductivity. SEM imaging was conducted with an FEI Inspect Scanning Electron Microscope (FEI, USA). Micrographs were obtained at magnifications ranging from 1000× to 3000× to thoroughly examine the morphology, packing density, and surface features of starch granules in both chalky and translucent regions of the endosperm.

Measurement of the physiological parameters

Crude enzyme extraction

To assess cold stress responses, two weeks old rice seedlings were subjected to 4 °C treatment for 24 h. Fresh leaf samples (0.2 g) were weighed and ground into a fine powder in liquid nitrogen using a pre-cooled mortar. The resulting powder was homogenized in 3 mL of ice-cold phosphate buffer (100 mmol/L, pH 7.8). The homogenate was transferred to a 5 mL centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was carefully collected and stored for subsequent enzymatic assays [35, 36].

Superoxide dismutase (SOD, EC1.15.1.1) activity assay

To prepare the SOD reaction premix for 30 reactions, 30 mL of 100 mmol/L phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.8) was combined with 0.6 mL of 1 mmol/L EDTA-Na2, 2 mL of 130 mmol/L methionine (Met), 2 mL of 750 µmol/L nitro blue tetrazolium (NBT), and 2 mL of 20 µmol/L riboflavin. A 50 µL aliquot of the crude enzyme extract was added to 1 mL of the SOD reaction premix. Control groups included Control I, which contained 50 µL of 100 mmol/L PBS (pH 7.8) incubated in complete darkness, and Control II, which contained 50 µL of 100 mmol/L PBS (pH 7.8) exposed to 4000 lx (lx) light. Test samples and Control II were illuminated (4000 lx) for 10–15 min, while Control I remained unexposed. Absorbance at 560 nm was measured under low-light conditions, using Control I as the blank reference [27].

Peroxidase (POD, EC 1.11.1.7) activity assay

To prepare the POD reaction premix for 50 reactions, 50 mL of 100 mmol/L PBS (pH 7.0) was supplemented with 28 µL of 0.2% (w/v) guaiacol, which was heated and stirred until fully dissolved. After cooling, 19 µL of 30% H₂O₂ was added. A reference cuvette was prepared with 50 µL of 100 mmol/L PBS (pH 7.8) and 1 mL of the reaction mixture. For test samples, 50 µL of crude enzyme extract was added to the cuvette, followed by 1 mL of the reaction mixture. Dynamic absorbance at 470 nm was recorded every 15 s for 1 min, and the average rate of change was calculated to determine POD activity [37].

Malondialdehyde (MDA) content assay

To assess MDA content, 100 µL of crude enzyme extract was added to a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube containing 1 mL of 0.25% (w/v) 2-thiobarbituric acid (TBA) solution. The control reaction was prepared by replacing the enzyme extract with 100 µL of 100 mmol/L PBS (pH 7.8). The mixtures were incubated in a boiling water bath for 15 min, cooled on ice for 5 min, and absorbance was measured at 532 nm and 600 nm [38].

Proline (Pro) content determination

Fresh leaf tissue (0.5 g) was homogenized in 5 mL of 3% (w/v) sulfosalicylic acid and boiled for 10 min. After cooling, the homogenate was filtered into a 5 mL centrifuge tube. A 2 mL aliquot of the filtrate was combined with 2 mL of glacial acetic acid and 2 mL of 2.5% (w/v) acidic ninhydrin solution. The mixture was incubated in a boiling water bath for 30 min and then allowed to cool. Subsequently, 5 mL of toluene was added, and the mixture was vortexed vigorously. After layering, the upper red layer was carefully collected, and its absorbance was measured at 520 nm. For the control, 2 mL of ddH₂O was used in place of the extract [39].

Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) accumulation assay

Leaf segments (1 cm) were immersed in a 1 mg/mL solution of 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) and incubated under light at 28 °C for 10 h. After incubation, chlorophyll was removed by boiling in absolute ethanol, and H₂O₂ accumulation was visualized using a Zeiss V20 stereomicroscope [40].

Superoxide radical (O₂⁻) detection

Leaf segments (1 cm) were incubated in a 6 mmol/L NBT solution under light at 28 °C for 10 h. Chlorophyll was removed using absolute ethanol, and O₂⁻ accumulation was observed under a Zeiss V20 stereomicroscope [41].

RNA Isolation, cDNA library construction and RNA-seq

The seedlings were cultivated under the same conditions as described in Sect. Plant growth status and trait determination until they reached two weeks of age. Leaves of two week old Nipponbare, OE4, and OE5 lines were sampled after 0, 2, 12, and 24 h of treatment at 4 °C [26]. A total of eight samples (4 time points × 2 rows, each consisting of plants from six biological replicates) were obtained for RNA-seq. Total RNA extraction was performed using the method described by Chomczynski et al. [42]. This process involves homogenizing samples in a monophasic solution containing guanidinium thiocyanate and acidic phenol, which denatures proteins and inactivates RNases. The subsequent addition of chloroform separates the mixture into aqueous and organic phases, allowing RNA to selectively partition into the upper aqueous phase. The RNA is then recovered by precipitation with isopropanol or ethanol, washed to eliminate contaminants, and finally dissolved in RNase-free water for downstream applications. The libraries were sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform and 150 base pairs (bp) paired-end reads were generated. About 24 M raw reads for each sample were generated. Raw reads of FASTQ format were firstly processed using FASTP and the low quality reads were removed to obtain the clean reads. Specific data can be found in the supplementary data. The whole experiment was done by OE Biotech, Inc., (Shanghai, China).

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed with SPSS 25.0 software. Data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 3 independent biological replicates). The normality of the data was confirmed using the Shapiro-Wilk test (P > 0.05). Statistical significance was determined by one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s post-hoc test for comparisons against the WT (Nipponbare) control. P < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

ZmDUF1645 affects rice production

To evaluate the effects of ZmDUF1645 overexpression in rice productivity, we evaluated yield-related agronomic traits in transgenic overexpression lines [26] (OE4, OE5) compared to WT plants. Phenotypic analysis revealed significant enhancement in three key yield components: panicle length (Fig. 1a, b), grain number per panicle (Fig. 1 d), and thousand-grain weight (Fig. 1e). As reported previously [26], ZmDUF1645 overexpression significantly enhanced panicle length and thousand-grain weight. However, there was no significant difference in the number of effective tillers among the lines (Fig. 1c), suggesting ZmDUF1645 specifically influences panicle architecture rather than tillering. These improvements collectively resulted in substantially increased grain yield per plant. Further molecular studies demonstrated that ZmDUF1645 overexpression significantly increased the transcript levels of the positive regulator of grain weight and yield, GIF1, which is consistent with the results described in previous report [26] (Fig. 1f). This aligns with the functional conservation of DUF1645-domain proteins between maize and rice. Specifically, our results indicate that ZmDUF1645 positively regulates rice yield by promoting panicle development and grain-related traits, which is consistent with the previously reported role of its rice homolog, OsSGL [43].

In this study, UBQ5 was selected as the reference gene for RT-qPCR analysis due to its well-documented stable expression in rice under both normal and stress conditions [44].

ZmDUF1645 affects rice grain shape

To investigate the role of ZmDUF1645 in grain morphology regulation, we conducted comprehensive phenotypic analyses comparing WT rice with two independent overexpression lines (OE4 and OE5) (Fig. 2a, d). Seed phenotype analysis shows that compared to the WT, the grain length of brown rice and dehulled rice in OE4 and OE5 lines increased, while the width decreased (Fig. 2e, f). Notably, the length-to-width ratios were substantially higher in both brown rice and milled rice (Fig. 2 g) indicating ZmDUF1645 overexpression promotes an elongated grain phenotype. At the molecular level, we observed coordinated regulation of key grain shape determinants: significant downregulation of GS3 (a negative regulator of grain length) and upregulation of GS5 (a positive regulator of grain size) in overexpression lines (Fig. 2 h, i), and the changes in the expression of these genes were consistent with the phenotypic observations, indicating that ZmDUF1645 has a regulatory role in rice grain morphogenesis. The observed effects of ZmDUF1645 overexpression on grain morphology align closely with the previously reported phenotype of its rice homolog OsSGL [43].

ZmDUF1645 affects rice quality

To elucidate the impact of ZmDUF1645 on rice quality, we conducted evaluations of appearance, processing, and flavour qualities in WT and two overexpression lines (OE4 and OE5), along with expression analysis of starch biosynthesis genes: GBSS1 (Granule-Bound Starch Synthase 1) and AGPS2 (Alkylglycerone Phosphate Synthase) [45, 46]. The results revealed that ZmDUF1645 overexpression reduced amylose content (Fig. 3a), taste quality was compromised in transgenic lines (Fig. 3 d). Additionally, chalkiness rate and degree were increased, and processing quality was altered—decreasing brown rice and refined rice rates while increasing head rice rate (Fig. 3e, f, g, h). SEM observations further showed that starch granules in the overexpression lines exhibited a looser and more irregular arrangement compared to the WT (Fig. 3i). At the molecular level, RT-qPCR confirmed marked downregulation of GBSS1 (to 60% in OE4 and 50% in OE5 of WT) and AGPS2 (to 10% in OE4 and 50% in OE5 of WT) (Fig. 3b, c), indicating disrupted starch biosynthesis. These findings demonstrate that ZmDUF1645 may negatively regulates rice quality by interfering with starch accumulation and endosperm development, similar to OsSGL [47]. Consumers often prefer rice that is less chalky and more translucent [48]. The significant increase in chalkiness can substantially reduce market value and consumer acceptance. The concurrent decline in amylose content and taste value further suggests poorer eating quality, which is undesirable in many major rice markets. From an industrial perspective, the reduced brown and refined rice rates imply lower efficiency, whereas the increased head rice rate may partially mitigate processing losses; nevertheless, the overall economic value is likely compromised by the high chalkiness and poor sensory attributes.

ZmDUF1645 reduces the cold stress tolerance in rice

To evaluate whether ZmDUF1645 overexpression alters the response to cold treatment, we subjected WT plants and two ZmDUF1645 overexpressing lines (OE4 and OE5) to 4 °C for 48 h followed by a 14 day recovery period under normal growth conditions. All genotypes exhibited pronounced growth inhibition following cold exposure, with overexpression lines displaying more severe symptoms. During the recovery phase, WT plants showed strong recovery ability, whereas both OE4 and OE5 showed significantly impaired recovery (Fig. 4a). Statistical analysis of the survival rates showed that the survival rates of WT, OE4 and OE5 were 58%, 31% and 10%, respectively, showing significant differences (Fig. 4b). These findings demonstrate that ZmDUF1645 overexpression substantially compromises both cold tolerance and post-stress recovery capacity in rice, suggesting this gene negatively regulates cold stress adaptation mechanisms.

Seedlings of ZmDUF1645 overexpression plants accumulate more ROS under cold stress

ROS play an important signaling role in plants and oxidative damage occurs when the balance between ROS production and elimination is disturbed [49–51]. Cellular damage is expressed as the degradation of biomolecules such as proteins and lipids, which ultimately leads to plant cell death [52]. To quantify H2O2 and O2- (major ROS components) accumulation in ZmDUF1645 transgenic seedlings under cold stress, we performed histochemical staining using DAB and NBT before and after 4 °C treatment. Before cold treatment, there was no significant difference between the leaves of all lines stained by DAB and NBT (Fig. 5a, c). While after 24 h of cold treatment, more brown and blue patches appeared on the surface of the leaves of the overexpression lines after staining, which indicated that the overexpression lines accumulated more ROS after cold treatment compared with the WT [53] (Fig. 5b, d).

ZmDUF1645 affects antioxidant capacity and pro accumulation to reduce cold stress tolerance in rice seedlings

Low-temperature stress induces excessive ROS generation in rice seedlings, resulting in membrane lipid peroxidation [54]. This process leads to the production of MDA, a widely recognized biomarker of oxidative damage [55]. Under normal conditions, no significant differences in MDA content were observed among the genotypes. However, following cold stress, ZmDUF1645 overexpressing plants exhibited significantly higher MDA levels compared to WT plants, indicating enhanced oxidative damage (Fig. 6a). To assess the capacity of the antioxidant system, we measured the activities of key ROS scavenging enzymes, SOD and POD. While enzyme activities were similar across all lines under control conditions, the overexpression lines demonstrated significantly reduced SOD and POD activities after cold stress compared to the WT (Fig. 6b, c). This suppression of key antioxidant enzymes likely contributes to the observed accumulation of ROS and subsequent oxidative damage, suggesting that ZmDUF1645 disrupts the redox homeostasis necessary for stress tolerance. We further investigated Pro accumulation, a key osmoprotectant that stabilizes cellular structures, maintains osmotic balance, and acts as a radical scavenger during abiotic stress [56]. After cold treatment, ZmDUF1645 overexpressing plants accumulated less Pro than WT controls (Fig. 6 d). This reduction is particularly noteworthy, as Pro biosynthesis in rice is regulated by cold-responsive signaling pathways, including those mediated by C-repeat binding factors (CBFs) and abscisic acid (ABA) [57]. The decreased Pro content suggests that ZmDUF1645 may interfere with these central regulatory networks, impairing both osmotic adjustment and the ability to neutralize ROS. In summary, these results demonstrate that ZmDUF1645 overexpression weakens the cold stress response on multiple fronts: by dampening the antioxidant enzyme system, leading to heightened oxidative stress, and by suppressing the accumulation of Pro.

Transcriptome analysis of ZmDUF1645 affecting cold tolerance in rice

To further elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying cold tolerance regulation mediated by ZmDUF1645, this study conducted RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis on WT and ZmDUF1645 overexpression (OE) seedlings under low-temperature stress conditions during the three-leaf stage. Compared to the wild type, a total of 177 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified after 4 °C treatment for 0 h, 2 h, 12 h, and 24 h, with 35 genes upregulated and 142 genes downregulated. Notably, the number of downregulated DEGs significantly increased to 122 after 12 h of cold treatment (Fig. 7a). Further analysis revealed that among these 122 downregulated genes, six have been annotated (Fig. 7b), specifically OsCrRLK1L4 (LOC_Os01g06280), OsSSIVa (LOC_Os01g52250), bHLH144 (LOC_Os04g35010), OsAPL4 (LOC_Os07g13980), and OsISA1 (LOC_Os08g40930). These genes primarily function in the biosynthesis of endosperm starch (including the accumulation and debranching of amylose and amylopectin) and the metabolism of storage substances. Their functional abnormalities can lead to agronomic traits defects such as chalkiness, saccharification, reduced grain weight, and deterioration of cooking quality [58–61]. These results indicate that ZmDUF1645 regulates cold tolerance under cold stress by downregulating the expression of starch biosynthesis genes. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis of the downregulated DEGs after 12 h of cold stress showed significant enrichment in pathways related to starch and sucrose metabolism, oxidative phosphorylation, biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids, and the interconversion of pentoses and glucuronic acid (Fig. 7c). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis indicated that DEGs are involved in starch biosynthesis, fatty acid metabolism, and influence pyruvate kinase activity (Fig. 7 d). Pyruvate kinase is a key rate-limiting enzyme in the glycolytic pathway, oxidative phosphorylation is the core pathway for ATP production in mitochondria, the interconversion pathway of pentoses and glucuronic acid belongs to the pentose phosphate pathway, which can provide NADPH, and fatty acid metabolism is closely related to the fluidity of cell membranes. Analysis of known related genes, such as the starch debranching enzyme OsISA1 (LOC_Os08g40930), has been reported to affect starch synthesis and endosperm development, with mutations leading to significant reductions in total starch, amylose, and amylopectin content in the endosperm, as well as changes in gelatinization temperature and starch chain length distribution [58]. The downregulation of this gene suggests a decline in starch synthesis capacity. The expression levels of six of these genes were validated by RT-qPCR before and after cold treatment (Fig. 7e, j). These results indicate that ZmDUF1645 influences cold tolerance in rice under cold stress by regulating starch synthesis genes and energy metabolism, thereby reducing cell membrane fluidity.

Discussion

The DUF family is involved in various biological processes, including plant seed development, yield formation, and stress responses [24, 62, 63]. For example, OsSIDP366 (DUF1644) overexpression enhances drought and salt tolerance while reducing water loss in rice [24]; OsSGL (DUF1645) overexpression increases grain size, weight, and overall yield [25]; and the genes S22B j, S22A j1, and S22A j3, encoding DUF1668 containing proteins, caused pollen sterility [63]. The aim of this study is to investigate the effect of the ZmDUF1645 overexpression in rice. Previous observations demonstrated that heterologous expression of ZmDUF1645 regulated grain morphology in rice, enhancing yield while reducing drought stress tolerance [26]. In this study, systematic phenotypic analysis of transgenic plants reveals that ZmDUF1645 heterologous expression significantly enhances rice yield but concurrently reduces grain quality and cold stress tolerance. Cold stress experiments demonstrated elevated ROS accumulation and reduced antioxidant enzyme activities in overexpression lines. Transcriptome analysis indicated that ZmDUF1645 mediated cold sensitivity involves alterations in plant hormone signaling and energy metabolism pathways. These findings reveal the multifaceted regulatory role of ZmDUF1645 and provide a theoretical basis for a balancing strategy in crop genetic improvement.

Notably, while crop yield is closely associated with multiple agronomic traits including grains per panicle, grain weight, and tillering [64–66], ZmDUF1645 specifically enhanced rice yield through increased panicle length, grains per panicle, and thousand-grain weight, without significantly affecting effective tiller number. This indicates that ZmDUF1645 primarily regulates panicle development rather than tiller formation. For example, the unknown protein encoded by YIGE1 influences the number of pistil florets to regulate ear length; Increasing the number of spikes per panicle by altering the determinacy of meristematic tissues and restoring lateral floret formation as a means of increasing yield [67, 68]. Although ZmDUF1645 overexpression increased yield, it significantly compromised grain quality. SEM revealed that starch granules in transgenic lines were more loosely arranged and irregularly shaped, contributing to increased chalkiness [9, 69, 70] (Fig. 3i). Additionally, grain morphology shifted toward an elongated shape, accompanied by elevated chalky white traits. We hypothesize that ZmDUF1645 alters grain morphology by disrupting endosperm cell proliferation and interfering with normal starch granule development and arrangement. This hypothesis is supported by the observed downregulation of key starch biosynthesis genes (including GBSS1, AGPS2) in ZmDUF1645 overexpressing lines, which aligns with the known function of its rice homolog, OsSGL, in inhibiting starch synthesis and endosperm development [47]. Consistent with these molecular changes, SEM revealed loosely packed and irregular starch granules in the transgenic grains (Fig. 3i), a phenotype commonly associated with altered starch metabolism. Furthermore, the significant reduction in amylose content and increase in chalkiness (Fig. 3a, f) provide physiological evidence of compromised starch accumulation and endosperm structure. We therefore suppose that ZmDUF1645 negatively regulates grain filling by interfering with the starch biosynthesis pathway, ultimately leading to the observed defects in grain morphology and quality. From a consumer perspective, high chalkiness is strongly associated with undesirable cooking properties—such as uneven texture and reduced transparency—and diminished visual appeal, greatly reducing market acceptance and commercial grade, especially in regions where grain appearance is a primary quality criterion. For the processing industry, the observed reduction in brown rice and milled rice rates suggests lower milling efficiency, which could elevate production costs. Although the higher head rice rate—potentially due to the presence of moderately hard, chalky cores (“heart whites”) that resist breakage—may partially mitigate losses, the overall economic value is compromised by the high chalkiness, which is typically penalized in market standards. Therefore, while ZmDUF1645 overexpression enhances yield, its negative impact on grain appearance, structure, and processing traits presents a major drawback for commercial deployment. These findings underscore the importance of comprehensive quality evaluation in molecular breeding and highlight a critical yield-quality trade-off that must be addressed—for instance, through targeted expression strategies or complementary genetic edits—to ensure that high-yielding varieties also meet consumer and industrial requirements.

Regarding stress responses, previous studies have shown that ZmDUF1645 expression in rice compromises drought tolerance [26]. This study further elucidates its role in cold stress susceptibility. Pyruvate kinase catalyzes the conversion of phosphoenolpyruvate to pyruvate and ATP in the presence of ADP, representing a key rate-limiting step in glycolysis that ultimately produces ATP. Pyruvate serves as a primary substrate for the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, continuously supplying carbon sources to the mitochondria, which in turn generate ATP through oxidative phosphorylation [71]. Under cold stress, the overexpression of ZmDUF1645 leads to the downregulation of pyruvate kinase-related gene expression, thereby inhibiting pyruvate kinase activity. This results in reduced pyruvate accumulation, insufficient carbon supply for the TCA cycle, decreased substrates for oxidative phosphorylation, and a concurrent downregulation of oxidative phosphorylation-related gene expression, collectively limiting ATP synthesis. Fatty acid metabolism influences membrane lipid saturation and unsaturation, promotes lipid remodeling, and regulates lipid signaling, thereby affecting membrane fluidity, membrane protein functionality, and the homeostasis of the electron transport chain. Cold stress can decrease membrane lipid fluidity, impair membrane protein function, and lead to electrolyte leakage [72]. Furthermore, the fatty acid composition of membrane lipids determines electron transport efficiency and ROS generation. Overly saturated fatty acid membranes are prone to structural and functional impairments at low temperatures, exacerbating oxidative damage [73]. Under cold stress, the downregulation of fatty acid metabolism genes and the inhibition of unsaturated fatty acid synthesis pathways may lead to reduced membrane lipid fluidity, which not only affects membrane protein activity but may also inhibit the efficiency of the electron transport chain, thereby increasing ROS accumulation. Starch is crucial for plants to cope with stress, maintain water balance, and stabilize cellular structures. A decrease in soluble sugar content impairs the plant cell’s capacity for osmotic regulation and ROS scavenging. This leads to the massive accumulation of ROS under cold stress, inducing oxidative damage such as membrane lipid peroxidation and reducing osmoprotectants, thereby significantly compromising cold tolerance [74]. Transcriptomic analysis indicates that ZmDUF1645 downregulates the expression of starch synthesis genes after 12 h of cold stress, which may lead to a decrease in soluble sugar content, increased cellular dehydration, and exacerbated ROS damage, ultimately reducing the cold tolerance of rice. In summary, we hypothesize that under cold stress, the overexpression of ZmDUF1645 leads to insufficient ATP supply by downregulating the expression of oxidative phosphorylation and glycolytic pyruvate kinase-related genes. On the other hand, the limitation of fatty acid synthesis results in decreased membrane lipid unsaturation, thereby reducing membrane fluidity and electron transport efficiency, which further exacerbates ROS accumulation. The resulting energy and substrate deficiencies ultimately inhibit the activity of starch synthase, diminishing the buffering capacity of starch synthesis and soluble sugars, thereby weakening the plant’s cold tolerance and leading to a cold-sensitive phenotype.

ZmDUF1645 exhibits pleiotropic effects, enhancing grain yield while simultaneously compromising grain quality and cold stress tolerance. This “trade-off” phenomenon underscores the complex regulatory mechanisms governing crop traits, where yield improvement may occur at the expense of quality and stress resistance, consistent with the well-documented “growth-defense trade-off” in plant physiology. For example, in cereals, similar trade-offs have been frequently reported. In rice, overexpression of certain yield-related genes, such as OsGRF4, can improve grain size but increase susceptibility to biotic stresses [75]. The well-documented example provided by Giolai and Laine illustrates how increased leucine‐rich repeat (NLR) gene density—while enhancing disease resistance—can constrain growth by reducing specific leaf area (SLA) [76].

Furthermore, recent study demonstrates how innovative approaches can potentially circumvent such trade-offs [77]. By co-expressing sensor and helper NLRs from Solanaceae in non-asterid crops (including rice, soybean, and Arabidopsis), they broadened the utility of resistance genes across species and mitigated the “restricted taxonomic functionality” (RTF) of NLRs. This strategy offers a promising breeding avenue—using heterologous expression and synthetic biology to bypass evolutionary constraints and decouple undesirable trait linkages. The case of ZmDUF1645 contributes to this important discourse by illustrating a yield-stress trade-off in a key cereal crop. From a breeding perspective, this implies that simply overexpressing ZmDUF1645 may not be a viable strategy without further fine-tuning—e.g., through tissue-specific promoters, stacking with cold-tolerant alleles, or genome editing to modulate its pleiotropic effects. Our findings emphasize the need for precision breeding approaches that balance high yield with resilience, thereby supporting sustainable agricultural productivity under changing climates.

This study provides important genetic resources for molecular design breeding of rice, which can be co-expressed with cold-resistant genes to balance yield and resistance in later applications, or co-delivery with helper NLRs may overcome potential heterologous expression inefficiencies. While this study demonstrates that ZmDUF1645 overexpression significantly alters yield, quality, and stress response phenotypes, we acknowledge that the use of constitutive overexpression may lead to pleiotropic or unintended effects that complicate the interpretation of gene function. Consequently, the present research represents a preliminary inquiry into the role of ZmDUF1645 in regulating cold stress tolerance in rice. A comprehensive elucidation of the mechanism governing its effects on yield, quality, and cold tolerance necessitates future studies.

It is important to note that the use of the constitutive 35 S promoter in this study, while effective for achieving high overexpression, may result in non-physiological expression patterns of ZmDUF1645 that do not mirror its endogenous spatiotemporal regulation. This ectopic expression could contribute to the observed pleiotropic effects on yield, quality, and stress tolerance. Future studies employing the native ZmDUF1645 promoter or tissue-specific promoters (e.g., for the endosperm or vascular tissue) would provide more biologically relevant insights into its precise functions and could help uncouple its beneficial and detrimental effects. Additionally, while UBQ5 is a widely accepted stable reference gene, the use of a single reference gene in our RT-qPCR analysis could introduce a certain degree of fragility to the gene expression data.

In the future, identifying protein interaction partners of ZmDUF1645 (e.g., through yeast two-hybrid screening or co-immunoprecipitation assays) and chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays to locate its genomic binding sites would help map the direct regulatory targets and pathways underlying its function. Incorporating these strategies in subsequent studies will be essential to distinguish the direct molecular functions of ZmDUF1645 from indirect compensatory responses and to validate its suitability as a target for precision molecular breeding.

Conclusion

The maize gene ZmDUF1645 acts as a pleiotropic regulator that enhances rice yield via GIF1-mediated spike development but concurrently compromises grain quality and cold tolerance. It disrupts starch synthesis by suppressing GBSS1 and AGPS2, leading to chalky grains, and impairs cold resilience by disturbing redox homeostasis and energy metabolism. These findings provide a genetic strategy for trait-balancing breeding, supporting the development of high-yield, climate-resilient rice varieties to address food security challenges under changing environments.
