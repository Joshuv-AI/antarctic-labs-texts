Evaluating Perfusion Quantification for Phase‐Resolved Functional Lung MRI Using Gaussian Filtering
Authors: Kirsten J. Schmidt, Andreas Voskrebenzev, Julian M. W. Glandorf, Marius M. Klein, Lea Behrendt, Frank Wacker, Jens Vogel‐Claussen, Filip Klimeš
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPhase‐resolved functional lung (PREFUL) MRI enables contrast‐free lung perfusion quantification (QQuant). Current quantification uses a single median value per slice of maximal blood exchange fraction (ΔS), which limits regional assessment, whereas regional ΔS causes artifacts. This retrospective study aims to optimize QQuant by applying Gaussian filtering to ΔS to improve intrascan/interscan repeatability in healthy volunteers, while evaluating whether the optimized QQuant retains agreement with dynamic contrast‐enhanced (DCE) MRI in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) patients and exhibits a physiologically plausible gravity‐dependent perfusion gradient. Analysis was performed on 29 healthy subjects (11 female, age: 19–33 years), 20 COPD patients (three female, age: 46–76 years) and 15 CTEPH patients (six female, age: 44–82 years), scanned at 1.5 T. Healthy subjects underwent four PREFUL scans in two sessions. Patients just underwent one PREFUL and DCE scan. QQuant maps were calculated while applying different Gaussian filter options to ΔS. In patients, PREFUL‐derived QQuant maps were coregistered to DCE‐derived pulmonary blood flow (PBF) maps for global and voxel‐wise correlations. In healthy subjects, the gravity‐dependent perfusion gradient was quantified by linear regression of slice‐wise QQuant across five coronal slices. Gaussian filtering ΔS with σ = 1.5 improved intrascan and interscan repeatability in healthy subjects (mean bias: ≤ 2.94 mL/100 mL; Cohen's d: 0.2) compared with the current slice median setting (mean bias: ≥ 3.75 mL/100 mL; Cohen's d: 0.68). Correlations with PBF remained stable after filtering (σ = 1.5) in COPD (filtered vs. default ΔS: global r = 0.57 vs. r = 0.59, voxel‐wise r = 0.32 vs. r = 0.32) and CTEPH (global r = 0.32 vs. r = 0.28, voxel‐wise r = 0.40 vs. r = 0.41), all significant except global CTEPH. The filtered QQuant maps showed a significant gravity‐dependent perfusion gradient (ΔS filtered σ = 1.5) of 1.47 mL/min/100 mL/cm. Gaussian filtering ΔS with σ = 1.5 improved QQuant repeatability while preserving DCE‐MRI agreement and is therefore recommended. The observed gravity‐dependent perfusion gradient confirms physiological plausibility, supporting potential in clinical use.

Introduction

Impaired pulmonary blood flow (PBF) is a hallmark of many chronic lung diseases, including the highly prevalent chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and the rarer chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH). Current guidelines [1, 2] recommend spirometry and ventilation‐perfusion single‐photon emission computed tomography (V/Q SPECT) measurements for assessment. Spirometry is noninvasive but provides only global lung parameters without spatial information. Established methods such as CT or SPECT can offer spatial resolution, but its utility in follow‐up examinations is limited by cumulative radiation exposure.

MRI enables regional, ionizing radiation‐free evaluation of pulmonary function and has emerged as a promising alternative. Both contrast‐enhanced techniques (e.g., dynamic contrast‐enhanced [DCE] MRI) and contrast‐free approaches (e.g., arterial spin labelling [ASL] and Fourier decomposition‐based methods like phase‐resolved functional lung [PREFUL] MRI) [3] are increasingly being explored [4].

DCE‐MRI is a clinically established method for quantitative pulmonary perfusion assessment [4]. It images the first pass of a gadolinium‐based contrast agent bolus during breath hold in a 3D volume with high temporal resolution and calculations of the signal evolution in T1‐weighted sequences [5, 6]. Despite its clinical utility, DCE‐MRI has limitations. It requires contrast agent administration, which may be contraindicated in certain patients and raises concerns regarding long‐term gadolinium deposition in brain tissue [7]. Additionally, it demands breath hold that certain patients cannot sustain for the entire measurement.

In contrast, PREFUL MRI enables contrast‐free, free‐breathing assessment of pulmonary perfusion by exploiting signal variations during the cardiac cycle. Acquisition uses a conventional spoiled gradient echo sequence. PREFUL‐derived quantification of perfusion (QQuant) relies on proton density and signal decay across multiple excitations towards steady state [8]. Using the signal (S) in parenchyma (par) and vessels (ves) at mid‐respiration position, at time = 0 (S0par/S0ves) and the median steady‐state parenchyma signal (S1par), the exchanged spin fraction per heartbeat and blood proportion per voxel are estimated. Subsequently, QQuant is calculated according to the following formula [8]: QQuant=exchange fraction·blood fraction·voxel volume·1T·100voxel volume, with the voxel volume cancelling out, # indicating global slice values, T the time between two heartbeats, and Q the signal amplitude during heart cycle resulting in: QQuant=Q(S0par−S1par)#·S0parS1ves#·1T·100,

The maximal blood exchange fraction can be shortened to ΔS=S0par−S1par. A more detailed explanation of all parameters can be found in the paper by Glandorf et al. [8].

A limitation of the current approach is that ΔS is estimated as a global slice‐wise median value (ΔSmedian). While this median approach improves robustness against noise, it may obscure regional heterogeneity in diseased lungs. Conversely, voxel‐wise estimation of ΔS can better capture spatial inhomogeneities but is highly susceptible to noise due to low signal‐to‐noise ratio [8].

Beyond these methodological issues, evaluation gaps remain. The repeatability and physiological validity of QQuant have not been systematically evaluated in healthy subjects. A perfusion gradient using PREFUL has only been assessed in perfusion‐weighted maps with arbitrary units [9].

Therefore, this study aims to (a) optimize ΔS estimation using Gaussian filtering to improve intrascan and interscan repeatability of QQuant in healthy volunteers; (b) evaluate the agreement of PREFUL‐derived perfusion with clinically established DCE‐MRI measurements in patients with COPD and CTEPH; and (c) assess the ability of QQuant to capture the physiologically plausible gravity‐dependent perfusion gradient [10], in healthy individuals.

Materials and Methods

Study Participants

This retrospective analysis was conducted under a protocol approved by the Institutional Review Board. All participants provided informed written consent participating in the study. The participants consisted of three cohorts: a healthy cohort for repeatability, and two disease cohorts, COPD and CTEPH, used for validation with DCE. The healthy repeatability cohort comprised 29 healthy subjects (11 female, age range: 19–33) from a previous study [11]. The validation cohorts comprised 20 patients with COPD (13 female, age range: 46–76), representing a subcohort of the CLAIM Study [12] and 15 patients with CTEPH (six female, age range: 44–82). Patients for the COPD cohort were eligible if they were aged ≥ 40 years, had a confirmed clinical diagnosis of COPD, a smoking history of at least 10 years, airflow limitation (baseline postbronchodilator forced expiratory volume in 1s (FEV1) of less than 80% predicted and a postbronchodilator FEV1‐to‐forced vital capacity (FVC) ratio of less than 0.7), hyperinflation (residual volume > 135% predicted), and stable cardiovascular function. Arrhythmia, heart failure (left ventricular ejection fraction < 40%), unstable ischaemic heart disease, and uncontrolled hypertension were exclusion criteria. Patients with CTEPH were eligible if they were aged ≥ 18 years and had pulmonary hypertension diagnosed by transthoracic echocardiography.

Exclusion criteria for both groups were incomplete MRI protocols and any contraindications to MRI or gadolinium‐based contrast agents.

To further characterize subjects, spirometry measurements were performed between repeatability scans in the healthy cohort and after the MRI scans of the COPD cohort. Outcomes were FEV1, FVC, and Tiffeneau index (FEV1/FVC), obtained according to guidelines [13]. In the CTEPH cohort, no spirometry measurements were performed.

Image Acquisition

Imaging of the healthy repeatability cohort was performed at 1.5 T (MAGNETOM Avanto, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). Subjects underwent two PREFUL MRI sessions (A and B), separated by ~1 h, acquiring the same five coronal slices (250 images each) twice (A1/2 and B1/2) with a 2D spoiled gradient echo sequence with the following settings: field of view (FOV): 500 × 500 mm2, matrix size: 128 × 128, slice thickness (ST): 15 mm, echo time (TE): 0.82 ms, repetition time (TR): 3 ms, flip angle (FA): 5°, and bandwidth (BW): 1500 Hz/px.

The PREFUL and DCE scans of the COPD/CTEPH validation cohort were performed at 1.5 T (MAGNETOM Avanto or Aera, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). For PREFUL, three coronal slices (200–250 images each, at tracheal bifurcation, anterior and posterior to the tracheal bifurcation) were acquired with the following settings: FOV: 340 × 340 to 500 × 500 mm2, matrix size: 128 × 96 or 128 × 128, ST: 15 mm, TE: 0.67–0.91 ms, TR: 3 ms, FA: 5°–6°, and BW: 1085–1502 Hz/px.

These PREFUL scans were directly followed by DCE examinations acquired in a single breath hold at end inspiration, using a bolus of 0.033–0.04‐mmol/kg bodyweight of gadolinium‐based contrast agents injected at a rate of 5 mL/s, using a 3D time‐resolved angiography with stochastic trajectories (TWIST) sequence with the following settings: FOV: 276 × 340 to 421 × 500 mm2, matrix size: 192 × 113 to 156 × 146, ST: 3–6 mm, TE: 0.7–0.89 ms, TR: 2.13–2.74 ms, and BW: 570–744 Hz/px. PREFUL MRI Postprocessing and Image Analysis

All acquired PREFUL images were registered towards a middle respiratory image using advanced normalization tools (ANTs) [14]. Subsequently, registered images were high‐pass (cutoff frequency of 0.75 Hz) filtered to maintain the perfusion information only. Using a piecewise sine‐fit in an automatically selected high perfused region of interest, e.g., aorta, images were sorted into one cardiac cycle. The detailed cardiac cycle reconstruction has been described previously [3, 15]. Lung parenchyma and, in a separate mask, vessels were segmented with convolutional neural networks [16] and subsequently verified and manually corrected if needed. The parameters of the QQuant formula, hence Q, S0par, S0ves, S1par, and heart frequency, were calculated as described [8]. Five QQuant maps were then calculated for each subject, each derived from a different version of ΔS (the signal decay in the parenchyma ROI). The first map used the current default approach, a single representative ΔS value per slice within the lung parenchyma (ΔSmedian). For the second map, a regional approach without any filtering was applied (ΔSregional). The remaining three maps were obtained by applying Gaussian filtering to ΔS maps with standard deviations of σ = 0.5, σ = 1.0, and σ = 1.5, denoted ΔSGF0.5, ΔSGF1, and ΔSGF1.5, respectively.

DCE‐MRI Postprocessing

Postprocessing of the DCE‐MRI began by identifying and selecting the perfusion weighted phase corresponding to the arrival of the contrast bolus in the aortic arch. The DCE maps were then denoised by applying a 3D Gaussian filter [15, 17]. Subsequently, DCE‐MRI derived PBF maps were calculated using a deconvolution algorithm based on the Volterra formula for discretization, with singular value decomposition applied as regularization [5, 6].

Intrascan and Interscan Repeatability of QQuant Under Varying ΔS Application in Healthy Volunteers

For the healthy repeatability cohort, all QQuant maps were normalized for heart frequency (QQuantnorm), to enable comparison of parameters independent of changing heart frequency (individual heart rates are provided in Table S2). The normalized formula being QQuant=Q(S0par−S1par)#xS0parS1ves#x100. Repeatability was assessed both within a single scan session (intrascan, A1 vs. A2) and between sessions separated by time outside the scanner (interscan, A1 vs. B1).

Comparison of PREFUL and DCE‐MRI

Compound DCE slices to match PREFUL ST were calculated by weighting them according to their overlap with the corresponding PREFUL slices and summed up as described previously [15]. PREFUL images were then registered onto the compound DCE slices using a combination of rigid and nonrigid ANTs transformations. The PREFUL parenchyma mask (excluding large vessels) was registered along to the DCE slice and corrected manually to be used for PREFUL and DCE. The corresponding PREFUL QQuant maps for all ΔS settings and DCE PBF maps were compared using median global patient values and voxel‐wise correlations. Voxel‐wise correlations were calculated separately for each patient, and the median correlation across all patients was reported.

Perfusion Gradient in Anterior–Posterior Direction

For perfusion gradient calculations, we used only the lung boundary segmentation from the PREFUL pipeline. This segmentation excludes only large pulmonary vessels. The additional PREFUL vessel segmentation was omitted to stay consistent with previous literature, where vessels either were not segmented inside the lung parenchyma [18] or sagittal images with less prone vessels were used [10, 19]. The anterior–posterior perfusion gradient was quantified in the A1 scans of the healthy repeatability cohort. For each healthy volunteer, we performed a linear regression of slice‐wise QQuant values. The median of all individual slopes was then taken as estimate of the overall gravity‐dependent gradient.

Statistical Analysis

Normality was evaluated by Shapiro–Wilk test and confirmed visually by histogram and Q–Q plot inspection.

As some of the repeatability datasets exhibited deviations from normal distribution, a nonparametric statistical approach was selected to ensure comparability across methods.

In the healthy volunteer's cohort, intrascan and interscan repeatability of QQuant maps with different ΔS settings was evaluated using coefficient of variation (CoV), intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), and Cohen's d. The differences between the repeated measurements were quantified by Bland–Altman analysis and tested for significance using Wilcoxon signed‐rank test. Alpha = 0.05 was chosen as significance level and Bonferroni adjusted due to the five ΔS methods to alpha = 0.01.

In the COPD and CTEPH cohorts, global and voxel‐wise relationship between DCE‐MRI PBF and PREFUL‐derived QQuant maps was assessed by Pearson correlation, and the agreement between their median values was quantified using Bland–Altman plots.

Statistical difference between posterior and anterior slice in gradient calculation was assessed by two‐sided paired t‐test.

Perfusion Gradient in Anterior–Posterior Direction

Given the previous results and comparable performance across ΔS filtering methods, the subsequent analysis of perfusion gradient in anterior–posterior direction is presented only for ΔSGF1.5. Results for the other approaches are provided in Table S1. All voxel QQuant values (per slice) of the overall dataset (representative also for individual subjects) are shown in Figure 5 (left). The most gravity‐dependent (posterior) slice consistently demonstrated the highest perfusion values, with both the highest individual QQuant values and the highest median values, with an overall median of 41 mL/min/100 mL, being significantly higher than the most anterior slice.

QQuant values using Gaussian filter with σ = 1.5 by slice location. Left: healthy subjects QQuant per voxel; right: median (IQR) values per slice including linear fit. General highest perfusion values were observed in the second posterior slice, highest median in the posterior slice. High outliers in some voxels likely originate from remaining vessel parts.

The distribution of overall slice‐wise median QQuant values and the corresponding median of linear fits per patient is presented in Figure 5 (right), resulting in an overall slope of 1.47 mL/min/100 mL/cm and a strength of the linear relationship with R 2 = 0.24, relative to the posterior slice, resulting in a change of 1.8%/cm.

Discussion

This study introduces an optimized PREFUL perfusion quantification method that filters ΔS values to preserve greater spatial information during the calculation. We validated the findings by improving intrascan and interscan repeatability in healthy subjects while maintaining global and voxel‐wise QQuant correlation to DCE PBF in similar range in COPD and CTEPH patients. However, PREFUL QQuant systematically underestimated absolute perfusion relative to DCE and also yielded lower values than other quantification methods in healthy subjects. Furthermore, we showed that with PREFUL QQuant the expected gravity‐dependent perfusion gradient can be observed in healthy volunteers, indicating reliability across slices and supporting physiologically plausible PREFUL perfusion.

Intrascan and Interscan Repeatability of QQuant Under Varying ΔS Conditions in Healthy Volunteers

Applying different filters to ΔS improved the repeatability of QQuant, with CoV values comparable to those reported in a previous study [20]. However, filtering settings below σ = 1.5 still produced image artifacts, even in healthy volunteers with expected homogeneous QQuant, hindering reliable quantification of perfusion. Differences between intrascan (identical slice positions) and interscan results underscore sensitivity of QQuant to minor variations in positioning or vessel segmentation. We also acknowledge that the heart frequency was omitted from the QQuant calculation for repeatability analysis because of its considerable variability across scans. This effect might be due to habituation effects, with healthy volunteers becoming more relaxed over the course of the repeatability scans [21].

Comparison of PREFUL and DCE‐MRI

The results of this study also demonstrate a moderate to strong positive correlation between the QQuant and DCE‐derived perfusion values across all filtering options, which are in agreement with previous DCE‐MRI correlations [8, 15]. QQuant values were consistently lower in the CTEPH cohort than in the COPD cohort, whereas DCE PBF showed the inverse pattern. These differences likely arise because the technique captures disease‐specific characteristics with varying effectiveness, as previously reported [8, 15]. In CTEPH, web‐like stenoses in the pulmonary arteries reduce blood flow to distal vessels leading to a reduced blood flow velocity. The result is a phase shift between obstructed and nonobstructed lung segments. As PREFUL QQuant relies only on one phase with the maximum flow‐related signal in the whole lung, this will likely result in an underestimation of perfusion in obstructed regions and in all lung areas exhibiting a phase delay relative to the selected phase. While previous studies have shown a moderate to strong correlation for perfusion defect percentage between DCE and PREFUL also in CTEPH patients [15, 22, 23], the overall underestimation of QQuant might lead to an increased divergence to DCE measurements, generally making PREFUL QQuant as a standalone value less reliable in patients with CTEPH than in those with other conditions, such as COPD.

QQuant values were generally lower than DCE‐derived PBF values. This difference, also reflected in the median values and in the mean bias, suggests a systematic underestimation of QQuant derived by PREFUL MRI. These findings align with other lung perfusion quantification studies, which consistently report higher average values than our results across both healthy cohorts and diseased patients. For instance, an alternative PREFUL MRI quantification approach yielded perfusion values > 86 mL/min/100 mL in healthy volunteers and in COPD patients [20], and ASL studies reported values exceeding 100 mL/min/100 mL in healthy and diseased lungs [24, 25]. Possible reasons for this underestimation include that PREFUL is prone to show more perfusion defects due to low signal‐to‐noise ratio and the intrinsic sensitivity only to flow that is orthogonal to the image slice, so perfusion flow predominantly in‐plane may be missed. Other errors may arise from suboptimal fitting of the signal decay during the ΔS calculation or from image registration inaccuracies, particularly in slices that contain a large portion of the heart.

Perfusion Gradient in Anterior–Posterior Direction

The observed slope of 1.8%/cm is within the range of previously reported lung perfusion changes values such as 3%/cm11 in sagittal slice, a slope of 0.447 between middle and posterior slice (meaning about 0.15%/cm) [18], or lower than observed 14%/cm in sagittal slices [19]. The differences might be due to the following reasons.

One might be due to incomplete coverage of the whole lung. In previous literature [10, 18, 26], mostly sagittal images spanning the full lung are used to assess gravitational gradients, making local effects more prone in our study. Differences in spatial resolution and blurring, together with greater vessel visibility in coronal compared to sagittal orientations, may result in more vessels being excluded in PREFUL images, potentially underestimating perfusion in some regions.

Additionally, inspiration status has been shown to influence the magnitude of the gravity‐dependent perfusion gradient, with lower values observed in expiration [18]. This effect cannot be accounted for in our study as all images were acquired in free breathing and registered towards a respective middle respiratory image.

The QQuant distribution over slice position also matches prior findings, where a strong linear correlation only occurred when calculating perfusion/density and the highest perfusion was observed a more intermediate than posterior part [10]. However, the middle slice exhibited fewer voxels with high QQuant values than expected, likely due to omission of large vessels. Although no additional vessel segmentation was performed, prominent vascular structures near the parenchymal borders were still inadvertently excluded from the analysis by the automated processing.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the number of acquired slices (3–5) for PREFUL MRI was relatively low, especially for assessing the gravity‐dependent perfusion gradient. While the acquired PREFUL slices are representative of the lung, future studies should consider acquiring additional coronal and preferably sagittal slices to capture the entire lung volume when assessing gravity‐induced perfusion gradients. This would enable a more comprehensive evaluation of the gradient and allow direct comparison with studies that assess gradients in the sagittal plane. Besides, sagittal slices would allow investigation of the underestimation of perfusion that may arise from predominantly in‐plane blood flow. Additionally, incorporating lung density corrections [26] could improve the accuracy of QQuant‐based gradient measurements.

The comparison between QQuant and DCE‐MRI is inherently limited by differences in spatial and temporal resolution, as PREFUL only acquired three slices, whereas DCE as a 3D sequence covers the whole lung, which can only be partially accounted for by using a compound DCE slice. DCE‐derived PBF values may also vary depending on the quantification method [27, 28, 29] and breathing position [30]. While the methods seem similar to a previous study, where almost no bias was detected [8], slight changes in the quantification algorithms might introduce a greater bias. Further errors might occur due to the registration of PREFUL images to DCE. Inaccurate registration might lead to artifacts, particularly problematic in the lower lung, where PREFUL images are acquired during tidal breathing, whereas DCE is performed during breath hold at end inspiration. Furthermore, DCE itself may be prone to errors in this region, as patients often struggle to maintain breath holds during acquisition. Further validation against gold‐standard methods, such as SPECT, would be beneficial, mirroring the approach taken in previous QQuant evaluations [15].

The selected patient groups limit the scope of the evaluation. The healthy volunteers were considerably younger than the diseased cohorts, and although CTEPH already exhibits distinct perfusion patterns compared with healthy and COPD lungs, the study did not include other clinically relevant pulmonary conditions. Although we expect the observed underestimation of perfusion to be specific to CTEPH, inclusion of a broader range of disease cohorts would be beneficial in future work.

While filtering ΔS improved regional information conservation without compromising correlation to DCE‐MRI, other parameters in the QQuant calculation (e.g., heart rate) remain potential sources of variability.

Beyond reducing ΔS‐related artifacts, further improvements could be achieved by refining the signal decay fitting and adopting a different phase‐selection strategy. For example, incorporating a broader range of cardiac phases, rather than the current single‐phase approach, might better capture perfusion in CTEPH patients, whose perfusion patterns are not accurately represented with the existing method.

Conclusion

Applying a Gaussian filter with σ = 1.5 to ΔS in QQuant calculation improves repeatability and maintains correlation to DCE. Consequently, this filtering approach is recommended to achieve more stable and robust quantification in PREFUL‐derived perfusion analysis. Furthermore, the gravity‐dependent perfusion gradient can be observed using PREFUL MRI in healthy volunteers, suggesting interslice consistency and physiological plausible results. While these findings represent a step towards reliable, quantified perfusion measurements, further improvements in CTEPH assessment and testing in larger, more diverse cohorts, preferably with extended lung coverage, are necessary to confirm its robustness across a broader range of clinical scenarios.
