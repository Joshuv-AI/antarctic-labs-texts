Delayed diagnosis of spinal tuberculosis mimicking spondyloarthritis: a case report
Authors: Yufan Chen, Huafeng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSpinal tuberculosis (TB) remains a significant health burden in endemic regions. Its diagnosis is frequently delayed due to non-specific early symptoms and radiological findings that can mimic other inflammatory spinal pathologies, leading to severe complications such as kyphosis and neurological deficit.Case presentationA 71-year-old woman from Southeast China presented with a 1-year history of progressive low back pain, initially misdiagnosed as axial spondyloarthritis. She experienced temporary relief with symptomatic treatment, including secukinumab. One year later, her symptoms recurred severely. Advanced imaging (computed tomography/magnetic resonance images) revealed destruction of the L1 and L2 vertebral bodies with a paravertebral abscess. Microbiological confirmation was obtained via a positive T-SPOT.TB test and metagenomic next-generation sequencing detecting Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex. The patient successfully underwent posterior debridement, spinal canal decompression, fusion, and instrumentation, followed by a standard anti-tuberculosis regimen. Her symptoms significantly improved, and she resumed return to work within 2 months.ConclusionThis case highlights the potential for spinal TB to masquerade as seronegative spondyloarthritis, leading to dangerous diagnostic delays. Clinicians should maintain a high suspicion for TB in endemic areas and employ specific diagnostic tests early. Timely surgical intervention for instability or neurological compromise, combined with appropriate chemotherapy, is crucial for optimal outcomes.Keywords: Spinal tuberculosis, Delayed diagnosis, Pott's disease, Spondyloarthritis, Case report

Introduction

Spinal tuberculosis (TB), one of the oldest diseases known to humankind, has been identified in Egyptian mummies dating back to 3300 bc [1] and is often referred to as Pott’s disease. In 1779, Percival Pott described paralysis resulting from anterior spinal destruction and progressive kyphosis [2, 3 ]. It is currently understood that spinal TB occurs when Mycobacterium tuberculosis spreads hematogenously via venous or arterial routes from primary foci in the lungs or genitourinary system to the cancellous bone of the vertebral bodies [4]. This hematogenous dissemination is facilitated by the rich local vascular network formed by the anterior and posterior spinal arteries—vascular arches that traverse the subchondral region of each vertebra, creating favorable conditions for bacterial seeding [5]. Due to the non-specific early symptoms and frequently normal findings on conventional radiography, the initial diagnosis of spinal TB is often elusive, leading to a high incidence of delayed diagnosis. Such delays can result in severe kyphosis and neurological impairment, which may disrupt spinal and bodily biomechanics, ultimately causing respiratory dysfunction, severe degenerative spinal stenosis, or even paraplegia [6]. Moreover, public health infrastructure primarily targets pulmonary TB, often overlooking screening for extrapulmonary forms. Research on the causes of diagnostic delays in spinal TB remains limited [7]. Given the progressive nature of the disease and the critical importance of early detection, identifying social, demographic, and biological factors contributing to such delays could help TB control programs reduce time to diagnosis. Undoubtedly, early diagnosis and appropriate treatment represent the best strategy to avoid the additional morbidity, healthcare costs, and risks associated with advanced spinal TB. This article presents a case initially diagnosed as inflammatory spondylitis with lumbar vertebral fracture, which was managed non-invasive therapy. Symptoms recurred and worsened after 1 year, leading to a final diagnosis of spinal TB, illustrating a notable instance of delayed diagnosis (Fig. 1).

Case presentation

Patient characteristics and past history

A 71-year-old female farmer from coastal Southeast China presented with low back pain. She had no history of tuberculosis exposure or prior TB infection. Her medical history was otherwise unremarkable except for osteopenia noted on bone densitometry, consistent with osteoporosis. She was a non-smoker and had no known comorbidities such as diabetes or immunosuppression. Before the onset of back pain, she was functionally independent and engaged in routine farming activities.

Investigations and diagnosis

The patient presented to a local hospital 1.5 years ago with a 1-month history of low back pain following a turning movement. The pain was described as persistent, sharp, worse at night and in the morning with stiffness, aggravated by movement, and radiating to the bilateral sacroiliac regions. On examination at the local hospital, the patient exhibited localized tenderness and percussion pain over the thoracolumbar spine, maximally at the L1–L2 level. Spinal range of motion was limited due to pain, particularly during flexion and extension. No spinal deformities or step-offs were palpable. Neurological examination of the lower limbs was unremarkable: motor strength was 5/5 in all major muscle groups (hip flexors, knee extensors, ankle dorsiflexors and plantarflexors), sensation was intact to light touch and pinprick in all dermatomes, and deep tendon reflexes (patellar and Achilles) were normal and symmetric. There were no signs of bowel or bladder dysfunction (red flags for cauda equina syndrome were absent). Lumbar magnetic resonance images (MRI) suggested inflammatory changes at L1/L2 (fracture not excluded), endplate inflammation at multiple levels, and disc herniation at L4/5 and L5/S1 (Fig. 2). Bone densitometry indicated osteopenia. After 1 month of symptomatic treatment (celecoxib and eperisone hydrochloride) with poor response, she was referred to orthopedics. Further imaging [X-ray and computed tomography (CT)] revealed abnormal L1 vertebral density (suggestive of inflammation), and secondary spinal canal stenosis at L4/5 (Figs. 3 and 4). With no clear surgical indication at that time, she was referred to rheumatology for further evaluation of suspected inflammatory spinal pathology.

Rationale for initial diagnosis of axial spondyloarthritis

The initial working diagnosis of axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA) was considered based on the following clinical picture: (1) inflammatory back pain features (nocturnal pain, morning stiffness, improvement with activity); (2) pain radiating to the sacroiliac regions; (3) absence of significant trauma; and (4) MRI evidence of inflammatory bone marrow edema at the L1/L2 vertebrae. However, definitive classification criteria for axSpA were not fully met. Notably, sacroiliac joint imaging was not performed, and HLA-B27 testing was negative (as reported in Table 1). There were no extra-articular manifestations such as uveitis, psoriasis, inflammatory bowel disease, or peripheral enthesitis.

Laboratory investigations, including complete blood count (CBC), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin (PCT), were within normal limits. And HLA-B27 negative, chest CT showed scattered inflammatory lesions in both lungs. A provisional diagnosis of "(1) Inflammatory spinal lesion (suspected axial spondyloarthritis?); (2) L2 vertebral fracture (with bone marrow edema)" was made.

Prior interventions

She received a 1-month diagnostic trial of secukinumab (300 mg subcutaneously once weekly), iguratimod (25 mg orally twice daily), and analgesics, which led to symptomatic improvement (Table 1).

The patient self-discontinued secukinumab after discharge and was lost to follow-up. One year later, her back pain recurred and progressively worsened, becoming refractory to oral medication. She developed painful flexion limitation and intermittent claudication, significantly impairing her daily activities. She was then referred to our institution's spine surgery department. Systemic review was notable only for fatigue, with no reported fever, night sweats, weight loss, or respiratory symptoms. Physical examination at our hospital revealed a VAS score of 7 (0–10 scale). Spinal curvature was straightened with loss of normal lumbar lordosis. Localized tenderness and percussion pain were elicited over the thoracolumbar spine, maximal at L1–L2. Bilateral paraspinal muscles were tender to palpation. Range of motion was severely limited in all planes due to pain and mechanical restriction. Neurological examination remained normal: motor strength was 4/5 in all lower extremity muscle groups, sensation was intact, and reflexes were symmetric. Straight leg raise test was negative bilaterally. No bowel or bladder dysfunction was reported. Investigations were repeated. Routine blood tests were unremarkable. However, T-Spot. TB was positive, and Mycobacterium tuberculosis DNA was detected in sputum. Spinal X-ray and CT demonstrated destruction of L1–L2 vertebral bodies, loss of the L1/2 disc height, and paravertebral soft tissue swelling/abscess, highly suggestive of infective spondylodiscitis (Fig. 4). Chest CT indicated possible old pulmonary tuberculosis. Lumbar MRI confirmed infectious involvement of L1/L2 with a paravertebral abscess (Fig. 2). A triple-phase bone scan supported the presence of infective lesions at L1/L2 (Table 1).

Treatment

Based on the clinical and radiological evidence, a diagnosis of L1-2 spinal infection (likely tuberculosis) with an epidural abscess was made. Given the progressive kyphotic deformity and significant bone destruction, surgical intervention was indicated according to Surgical treatment guidelines for spinal tuberculosis in China [8]. The patient underwent posterior L1/2 lesion debridement, epidural abscess excision, spinal canal decompression, iliac bone graft fusion, antibiotic-impregnated bone grafting, and T12-L3 internal fixation. Intraoperatively, severe edema, adhesion ventral to the cord, and a thickened posterior longitudinal ligament suggesting an epidural abscess were noted. The excised tissue had a caseous appearance. Pathological examination revealed inflammatory granulation tissue (acid-fast staining negative). Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) performed on the intraoperative tissue specimen detected the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (151 reads, 58.49% relative abundance). Xpert MTB/RIF on the same specimen was positive for M. tuberculosis complex and did not detect mutations associated with rifampicin resistance (a genotypic finding that should be interpreted in conjunction with clinical response and phenotypic susceptibility testing where available). Notably, routine bacterial culture of the surgical specimen grew Staphylococcus caprae, which was initially considered a possible contaminant given the overwhelming evidence of tuberculous infection and the absence of clinical signs suggestive of pyogenic spondylodiscitis (Table 1). However, subsequent antimicrobial susceptibility testing revealed that the isolate was sensitive to levofloxacin, an agent included in the planned anti-tuberculosis regimen. Therefore, no additional antibacterial therapy was required, as the existing regimen provided adequate coverage.

Postoperatively, in consultation with a tuberculosis specialist, a standardized anti-tuberculosis chemotherapy regimen was initiated. The patient received a 2-week induction phase with intravenous levofloxacin 0.5 g once daily, combined with oral isoniazid 0.3 g once daily, rifampin 0.6 g once daily, ethambutol 1.0 g once daily, and pyrazinamide 0.5 g three times daily. After 2 weeks, levofloxacin was discontinued, and the remaining four oral drugs (isoniazid, rifampin, ethambutol, pyrazinamide) were continued for a total planned duration of 12 months. During treatment, liver function, renal function, and visual acuity were monitored regularly to detect potential drug-induced hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, or optic neuritis. The patient experienced transient nausea and vomiting during the first week of anti-TB therapy, which resolved with symptomatic management and did not require regimen modification.

Outcome and follow-up

The patient's postoperative recovery was satisfactory. Postoperative back pain gradually improved and resolved within 3 weeks after surgery. Her VAS score decreased from 7 preoperatively to 2 at discharge, and further to 1 at the 1-month follow-up. Inflammatory markers normalized by the 1-month follow-up, with ESR decreasing from 45 mm/h preoperatively to 12 mm/h (reference range < 20 mm/h) and CRP from 28 to 3 mg/L (reference range < 5 mg/L).

After brief management of transient anti-TB drug-induced nausea and vomiting, she was mobilized with a brace on day 5 and discharged on day 9 following radiographic confirmation of satisfactory outcomes. She resumed light physical labor by the second month and discontinued the brace at 3 months.

Follow-up assessments were conducted at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months post-surgery, with planned annual follow-up thereafter. At each visit, clinical evaluation, inflammatory markers, liver and renal function tests, and visual acuity checks were performed. The 3-month postoperative CT scan (Fig. 4) demonstrated early evidence of bone fusion at the grafted site, with bridging trabeculae visible across the intervertebral space. The corrected kyphotic angle was maintained without loss of correction, and the internal fixation devices (pedicle screws and rods) remained in satisfactory position with no signs of loosening or migration.

Serial imaging at 6 months showed progressive consolidation of the bone graft and stable spinal alignment. No signs of recurrence—such as new osteolytic lesions, abscess formation, or hardware failure—were observed throughout the follow-up period. The patient successfully completed the 12-month anti-TB regimen and remained symptom-free, with full return to independent daily activities, at the final follow-up (Fig. 5).

Discussion

China bears a high burden of TB and latent TB infection (LTBI), a situation exacerbated by the COVID-19 pandemic [9, 10 ]. While extrapulmonary TB accounts for 10% of cases, spinal TB represents its most common musculoskeletal form [3]. A major challenge is the vast pool of approximately 250 million individuals with LTBI in China, who are potential future patients [11].

Delayed diagnosis in spinal TB is associated with an increased frequency of complications and a higher need for surgical intervention. A multicenter retrospective study from India reported a median diagnostic interval of 5 months for all spinal TB patients, with 25% experiencing extreme delays (10 months or longer). Notably, 34% of patients (54/160) already had radiologically severe disease at the time of diagnosis [7]. Those with extreme delayed diagnosis were at a higher risk of severe disease, underscoring that diagnostic delays lead to worse clinical outcomes [12]. This highlights the critical importance of early diagnosis, as a delay in initiating anti-TB treatment provides more time for the infection to destroy vertebral structures, ultimately leading to kyphotic deformity [3–6].

Diagnostic challenges and clinical reasoning pitfalls

This case illustrates several cognitive and diagnostic pitfalls that contributed to the delayed diagnosis. First, anchoring bias on a diagnosis of axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA) occurred early in the clinical course. The presence of inflammatory back pain features—nocturnal pain, morning stiffness, and sacroiliac region radiation—combined with MRI findings of bone marrow edema, led the initial care team to strongly favor an inflammatory rheumatologic condition. This initial diagnostic anchor persisted despite the absence of definitive axSpA criteria (negative HLA-B27, lack of sacroiliitis on imaging, no extra-articular manifestations) and the presence of atypical features such as focal vertebral destruction on CT (Fig. 4) and pulmonary inflammatory lesions on chest CT (Fig. 4).

Second, the transient symptomatic response to therapy further reinforced the incorrect diagnosis. The patient experienced temporary improvement with secukinumab, an IL-17A inhibitor, and analgesics. This response was misinterpreted as confirmation of an inflammatory/autoimmune process. However, it is now recognized that various non-specific back pain etiologies, including early infectious processes, can show temporary symptomatic improvement with anti-inflammatory or immunosuppressive agents due to non-specific modulation of pain and inflammation. The true underlying pathology—progressive tuberculous spondylodiscitis—continued unabated during this period, eventually manifesting with advanced vertebral destruction and paravertebral abscess formation.

Third, diagnostic limitations at the initial presentation played a role. Tuberculosis-specific tests (T-SPOT.TB, TB-DNA, mycobacterial culture) were not performed at the local hospital, and the significance of the chest CT findings was not fully appreciated in the context of spinal pathology. The lack of early image-guided biopsy for microbiological and histopathological diagnosis represented a missed opportunity for timely definitive diagnosis.

These challenges underscore the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis in patients presenting with back pain in TB-endemic regions, even when clinical features superficially suggest common inflammatory conditions. A transient response to therapy should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of a non-infectious etiology, and low-threshold utilization of TB-specific diagnostics is warranted when radiographic findings raise any suspicion of infection.

Reflecting on this case, the patient likely presented initially as a classic case of LTBI. The initial misdiagnosis of axial spondyloarthritis (SpA) at the local hospital was based on the absence of significant trauma, sacroiliac joint pain accompanying low back pain, morning stiffness, and nocturnal pain. Inflammatory markers were within normal limits, while MRI revealed inflammatory changes at the L1-2 vertebrae. Applying the Calin [13], Berlin [14], or Assessment of SpondyloArthritis international Society (ASAS) classification criteria [15] might seemingly justify a diagnosis of inflammatory back pain (IBP). However, IBP does not unequivocally indicate SpA. A 10-year follow-up study of 124 patients with new-onset IBP found that only 30% were ultimately diagnosed with SpA [16]. Furthermore, the transient symptomatic improvement with initial therapy obscured the correct diagnosis. Various causes of back pain can respond to biologics and NSAIDs; SpA-related pain often improves significantly within 24–48 h of treatment [17], potentially reinforcing the impression of a typical IBP case. However, retrospective review of the initial CT scans reveals overlooked clues: bony destruction at L1 and pulmonary inflammatory lesions (Fig. 4). Concurrently, the initial MRI demonstrated abnormal signal changes in the L1-2 disc space, warranting high suspicion for spinal infection (Fig. 2). Unfortunately, constrained by local diagnostic capabilities and clinical awareness, crucial tests like T-SPOT.TB, TB-DNA, and bone scans were not performed during the first admission. Following apparent initial "improvement," the patient's true pathology emerged: progressive bony destruction and paravertebral abscess formation. After 1 year of diagnostic delay, imaging showed significant anterior column destruction, with the spine attempting restabilization via Type B reconstruction (point contact of diseased vertebrae with facet joint dislocation) [6]. The sagittal deformity angle ratio (SDAR) progressed from 2.5° to −9.5° (Fig. 3), with a total vertebral body loss (TVBL) of 0.9 (Fig. 4), indicating "spinal at risk" radiological signs [18] (facet joint separation, posterior vertebral translation, lateral translation [6]). According to the spinal instability scoring system [18], the score of 4 indicated definite instability. Without intervention, S. Rajasekaran's predictive formula for TB kyphosis [6, 19 ] estimated progression to 33.0° in 5 years (Fig. 4), meeting Park et al.'s criteria for severe spinal TB [20]. Given this high risk of progression, surgical intervention was deemed necessary to prevent deformity advancement and enhance anti-TB treatment efficacy. Fortunately, despite severe radiographic involvement, the patient exhibited no significant neurological deficit, possibly due to the low-energy, insidious nature of tuberculous spinal cord compression compared to traumatic injury [3, 21 ]. However, delayed-onset paraplegia remains a risk from severe, persistent kyphosis causing spinal cord stretching and thinning at the apex, potentially leading to irreversible myelomalacia with poor neurological recovery even after late surgery [3]. Ultimately, thorough debridement, canal decompression, kyphosis correction, and standardized anti-TB therapy significantly alleviated the patient's symptoms and substantially reduced the risk of spinal cord injury (Table 2).

This case offers the following key insights:

① Diagnostic Challenges of Atypical Spinal TB: Early-stage spinal inflammatory lesions and minor fractures share nonspecific clinical presentations with atypical spinal TB (including disc-based, single vertebral body central, and skip lesion types). The lack of characteristic clinical history and imaging findings in such atypical cases makes misdiagnosis or missed diagnosis highly likely in the early stages, leading to delayed treatment. Multiple studies have confirmed that cases experiencing extreme diagnostic delays carry a significantly higher risk of poor clinical outcomes [3, 7 ].

② Judicious Use of Laboratory and Imaging Diagnostics: While elevated inflammatory markers can suggest TB, they can be negative in some patients. For suspected bony destruction, tests such as PPD, TB-DNA, T-SPOT, and whole-body bone imaging should be considered, along with monitoring changes on serial chest CT scans. Where feasible, obtaining valid specimens for mycobacterial culture or performing a vertebral biopsy is crucial for definitive diagnosis [8, 22 ]. An important imaging clue is the relative preservation of disc height even in advanced stages of spinal TB, which helps differentiate it from pyogenic spondylodiscitis—the latter typically shows early disc space narrowing [23].

③ Strict Adherence to Surgical Indications [8]: Surgical intervention should be reserved for clear indications, including: (1) Persistent local pain and poor quality of life despite standard anti-TB therapy; (2) Progressive enlargement of lesions or abscesses, or unremitting pain during treatment; (3) Failure of standard anti-TB treatment with ongoing spinal destruction; (4) Neurological deficits due to compression from abscesses, granulation tissue, caseous necrosis, or sequestra; (5) Spinal instability, progressive kyphotic deformity, and intractable pain; (6) Significant residual kyphosis post-healing, accompanied by pain or late-onset paralysis.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this case underscores the diagnostic challenges inherent in spinal tuberculosis, particularly when it presents with features mimicking more common conditions like axial spondyloarthritis. It highlights the critical importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for tuberculosis in endemic areas, even in the absence of classic systemic symptoms or markedly elevated inflammatory markers. Early and appropriate utilization of advanced microbiological (e.g., T-SPOT.TB, mNGS) and imaging modalities is essential to avoid detrimental diagnostic delays. Furthermore, the case illustrates that a favorable initial response to immunosuppressive or anti-inflammatory agents should not definitively rule out an underlying infectious process. For patients presenting with "at-risk" radiological signs or progressive deformity, timely surgical intervention combined with standardized anti-tuberculosis chemotherapy is pivotal for achieving spinal stability, preventing neurological sequelae, and ensuring a satisfactory clinical outcome. This report serves as a compelling reminder for clinicians to consider spinal TB in the differential diagnosis of inflammatory back pain, thereby facilitating earlier diagnosis and improving patient prognosis.
