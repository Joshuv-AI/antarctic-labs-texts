Visibility Stokes parameters as a foundation for quantum information science with undetected photons
Jaroslav Kysela, Markus Gräfe, Jorge Fuenzalida

Abstract
The framework of measurement operators plays a fundamental role in extracting information about quantum systems. Recently, techniques based on induced coherence have been developed to access the same information for undetected photons. However, there has been a lack of consistent reformulation of quantum operators for these techniques. In this work, we introduce a set of parameters that quantify the polarization of undetected photons based on measured visibilities. Given their similarity to classical counterparts, we refer to them as visibility Stokes parameters. We apply these parameters and the corresponding quantum operators to the problem of quantum state tomography, thoroughly analyzing the environment of undetected photons and its role in the reconstruction process. Because these parameters provide a more intuitive and consistent understanding of the measurement process, we believe that some established quantum information protocols could be adapted for undetected photons.

Introduction

Recent years have witnessed the advent of quantum technologies fueled by quantum information science that uses intrinsic quantum properties of systems such as photons
[
1
]

The development of optical coherence has inspired alternative ways to inspect the state of photons without detecting them through a quantum interference effect called induced coherence without induced emission

,

, the quantification of two-photon transverse momenta
[

, entanglement certification of a Bell state
[

, and the qubit quantum state tomography
[

. The same interference effect has also inspired several applications for probing objects with undetected light

[

,
12

, spectroscopy

, optical coherence tomography
[

, and imaging distillation
[

. Even though many of these techniques can be counted among the quantum information protocols, a universal recipe that would allow the translation of already established quantum protocols to the context of coherence-based configuration with undetected photons is missing.

In this work, we make a step towards filling this gap by delineating the relation between the standard quantum state tomography
[
18
]
and the tomography of undetected photons
[
9
]
. More specifically, we focus on the quantum state tomography of undetected qubits represented by the polarization of single photons. We reformulate this technique using a novel type of coherence-based parameters that we term

due to their similarity with the Stokes parameters
[
19
]
. But while the latter are based on intensity measurements, the former are extracted from visibilities. The resulting quantum state representation by means of visibility Stokes parameters is strikingly similar to the Bloch vector representation of quantum states in standard quantum tomography. This mutual relation between the two tomography techniques represents a stepping stone for adapting other quantum information techniques to the context of coherence-based quantum operations acting on undetected photons. As a part of our investigation, we present a general analysis of the coherence conditions of the state tomography setup. Moreover, we find how coherence and environment are intertwined when mixed states are considered.

The polarization of light and ways to quantify it has a long-standing history in optics

II.1
Stokes parameters

The Stokes parameters were introduced in 1852

\displaystyle\textup{S}_{x}=

\displaystyle|E_{D}|{{}^{2}}-|E_{A}|{{}^{2}},

(1)

\displaystyle\textup{S}_{y}=

\displaystyle|E_{L}|{{}^{2}}-|E_{R}|{{}^{2}},

(2)

\displaystyle\textup{S}_{z}=

\displaystyle|E_{H}|{{}^{2}}-|E_{V}|{{}^{2}}.

(3)

In these formulas,

stands for the field’s amplitude associated with the polarization mode

is either a linear polarization (horizontal
H

V
, diagonal

, and anti-diagonal

) or a circular polarization (left-circular
L
and right-circular

\textup{S}_{0}=|E_{D}|{{}^{2}}+|E_{A}|{{}^{2}}=|E_{L}|{{}^{2}}+|E_{R}|{{}^{2}}=|E_{H}|{{}^{2}}+|E_{V}|{{}^{2}}.

(4)

One can remove the dependence of the parameters
\textup{S}_{x}
,
\textup{S}_{y}
, and
\textup{S}_{z}
on the intensity by dividing them by

\vec{r}_{I}=(x,y,z)

x=\textup{S}_{x}/\textup{S}_{0},\quad y=\textup{S}_{y}/\textup{S}_{0},\quad z=\textup{S}_{z}/\textup{S}_{0}.

The quantum counterpart of these normalized parameters is given by the mean values of Pauli operators
[

,
1

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{x}=

\displaystyle|D\rangle\langle D|-|A\rangle\langle A|,

(6)

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{y}=

\displaystyle|L\rangle\langle L|-|R\rangle\langle R|,

(7)

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{z}=

\displaystyle|H\rangle\langle H|-|V\rangle\langle V|.

(8)

The mean values of Pauli operators for a given quantum state
\hat{\rho}_{I}
coincide with the Bloch vector components as

x=\Tr(\hat{\sigma}_{x}\,\hat{\rho}_{I}),\quad y=\Tr(\hat{\sigma}_{y}\,\hat{\rho}_{I}),\quad z=\Tr(\hat{\sigma}_{z}\,\hat{\rho}_{I}).

The polarization quantum state can then be reconstructed from the Bloch vector
\vec{r}_{I}

\hat{\rho}_{I}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbb{1}_{2}+\vec{r}_{I}\cdot\vec{\sigma}).

(10)

1

\hat{\rho}_{I}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha^{2}&\alpha\,\beta\,q\,e^{-i\xi}\\
\alpha\,\beta\,q\,e^{i\xi}&\beta^{2}\end{pmatrix},

(11)

where
\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}=1
,
0\leq q\leq 1
, and
\alpha,\beta\geq 0
,
\xi\in[0,2\pi)
. The Bloch vector coordinates then read explicitly

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\,\alpha\,\beta\,q\,\cos(\xi),

(12)

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\,\alpha\,\beta\,q\,\sin(\xi),

(13)

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}.

(14)

II.2
Quantum state tomography

The method that allows to determine the quantum state
\hat{\rho}_{I}
of a quantum system such as a photon is called quantum state tomography
[
18
,
23
]
. In the standard quantum tomography, one has an ensemble of identical systems in state
\hat{\rho}_{I}
and subjects each system to a measurement in a certain basis, cf. Fig.
1
(a). The measurement results provide sufficient information to reconstruct the state
\hat{\rho}_{I}
. For qubits, the measurement bases are usually given as the eigenbases of Pauli operators, and the state can be visually represented in the Bloch sphere depicted in Fig.
1
(b). If the ensemble is composed of photons, the measurement typically leads to the photons’ destruction.

However, the state reconstruction can also be carried out non-destructively. The quantum state tomography of undetected photons
[
9
]
, depicted in Fig.
1
(c), uses a pair of photons, for convenience referred to as the signal and the idler, which can be generated in a spontaneous parametric down-conversion process (SPDC). In this technique, the state
\hat{\rho}_{I}
of the idler photon is reconstructed by measuring its partner, the signal photon, while the idler photon remains undetected. The information about the quantum state is transferred from the idler to the signal via an induced coherence configuration. The state
\hat{\rho}_{I}
is then reconstructed from the interference patterns measured for the signal photon.

Figure 1:
Quantum state tomography of photonic qubits.
(a) An ensemble prepared in an unknown state
\hat{\rho}_{I}
can be reconstructed by measurements in different bases. This technique is known as the quantum state tomography. The state
\hat{\rho}_{I}
can be visualized as a point in the Bloch sphere depicted in (b). (c) The idler (top dashed line) and the signal (bottom solid line) photons form a photon pair (that can be produced via SPDC) and are employed for quantum state tomography of undetected photons. The idler is prepared in an initial state
\hat{\rho}_{I}
, which is transferred to the signal through induced coherence. The idler photon remains undetected. Single-photon interference patterns shown by the signal allow us to reconstruct the idler state
\hat{\rho}_{I}
.

For future reference and to emphasize the differences between the present work and Ref.
[
9
]
, we list below the individual steps that constitute the quantum state tomography of polarization of undetected photons:

1.

A non-linear interferometer (for details see Sec.
III
) with two sources of photons is employed to produce a pair of photons, the signal and the idler. The effect of induced coherence turns the state of these photons into a superposition of a reference state and the state
\hat{\rho}_{I}
that is to be reconstructed. This step, which is identical for both Ref.
[
9
]
and our work, is schematically represented by the two upper bubbles in Fig.
1
(c). Unlike in Ref.
[
9
]
, our approach considers the most general coherence conditions in the experimental setup.

2.

An identical ensemble of photon pairs is produced by the interferometer and subjected to several rounds of measurements. In each round, the signal photons’ state is locally adjusted to a particular form and the signal photons are subjected to measurements in a given polarization basis. This step is schematically represented by the two lower bubbles in Fig.
1
(c). While in Ref.
[
9
]
the measurement consists of two rounds and the signal photons are always measured in
H/V
basis, in our work the measurement comprises three rounds and in each round the signal photons are measured in one of Pauli eigenbases
H/V
,
D/A
, and
L/R
.

3.

For each round, the phase between the two photon sources is varied and the interference pattern in the rate of signal photons is recorded. The idler photons remain undetected and leave the interferometer, while the interference patterns for signal photon detections are post-processed and relevant quantities are extracted. In Ref.
[
9
]
, the visibilities and the relative phase shift between two interference patterns were used to reconstruct the polarization state
\hat{\rho}_{I}
. In our work, we instead need only the visibilities of the three interference patterns. This way, the dependence of the reconstructed state on the experimental phase backlash is removed.

Apart from the technical improvements listed above, there is a more conceptual and notable difference — we introduce visibility Stokes parameters that represent a convenient tool for analyzing quantum measurements based on coherence. These new parameters are determined by the visibilities of the recorded interference patterns and can be seen as counterparts of standard Stokes parameters that are based on intensities. The visibility Stokes parameters can be visualized in the form of the visibility Bloch vector and we show its connection to the standard Bloch representation of mixed states. In the upcoming sections we describe our technique in detail and perform a thorough theoretical analysis, first for pure states and then for mixed states.

Interference measurements

During the actual tomography measurement, the setup is kept fixed except for the interferometric phase

IV
Mixed states

So far, we have considered only pure polarization states of the idler photon. For our tomography technique to be applicable in a practical setting, we present in this section its generalization for mixed states. We again proceed in a stepwise manner. The procedure is essentially identical to that for pure states with the exception that the theoretical treatment is more involved and there are extra caveats that have to be considered:

1.

Idler initial state — The idler photon’s state is now a mixed state
\hat{\rho}_{I}
. In Sec.
IV.1
we show how we model this state using a larger pure state. The evolution of both signal and idler photons through the measurement setup is studied in Sec.
IV.2
.

2.

Signal bases and detection — The analytical formulas for detection probabilities in different measurement bases of the signal photon are derived in Sec.
IV.2
.

3.

Idler state reconstruction — The form of visibilities is found in Sec.
IV.3
. The visibility Stokes parameters for mixed states are defined in Sec.
IV.4
with the aim of being as close as possible to the standard Stokes parameters.

4.

Role of asymmetric coherence — We observe that when the environmental conditions are completely unknown, the uncertainty in the reconstruction of mixed states increases with the decoherence in the systems. We discuss what additional constraints have to be adopted to enable the mixed state reconstruction in Sec.
IV.5
.

Each of these steps is discussed in detail below.

IV.1
Modelling mixed states with pure states

In order to treat mixed idler states in our tomography setup we need to represent mixed states in the framework of induced coherence. We do so by modelling mixed states with pure states as explained below. Mixed states can be represented as pure states living in a larger Hilbert space using a mathematical trick known as
purification

[
1
]
, where a given mixed state
\hat{\rho}=\sum_{j}p_{j}|\varphi_{j}\rangle\langle\varphi_{j}|
is associated with a pure state
|\psi\rangle=\sum_{j}\sqrt{p_{j}}|\varphi_{j}\rangle|a_{j}\rangle
, while
\{|a_{j}\rangle\}_{j}
form an orthonormal basis of an extra ancillary Hilbert space. The partial trace over this extra space then gives the original mixed state as
\hat{\rho}=\mathrm{Tr}_{A}(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|)
. The choice of
\{|a_{j}\rangle\}_{j}
is arbitrary, and any orthonormal basis will do. In the following, we adopt a more physics-inspired approach that bears some similarity to the purification yet shows important differences.

Let us assume without loss of generality that the mixed state
\hat{\rho}_{I}
of the idler photon results from some decoherence processes in the state-preparation stage, which processes act unitarily on the composite pure state
(\alpha_{0}|H\rangle+\beta_{0}|V\rangle)\otimes|e\rangle
. The vector
|e\rangle
denotes the pure state of all the other degrees of freedom of a photon, which we from now on collectively refer to as the
environment
. The decoherence processes might be polarization-dependent, and so the final state of the idler photon just before entering Q2 attains the form

|\psi_{I}\rangle=\alpha|H_{b^{\prime}}\rangle\otimes|e_{H}\rangle+\beta e^{i\,\xi}|V_{b^{\prime}}\rangle\otimes|e_{V}\rangle,

(32)

where
|e_{H}\rangle
and
|e_{V}\rangle
are the final states of the environment for the
|H\rangle
and
|V\rangle
idler photon polarization mode, respectively. These states are not in general orthogonal and without loss of generality we can assume that
0\leq\langle e_{H}|e_{V}\rangle\leq 1
. If we make the following identification

q=\langle e_{H}|e_{V}\rangle

(33)

it is straightforward to verify that the partial trace over the environment returns the desired mixed state

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{I}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Tr_{e}(|\psi_{I}\rangle\langle\psi_{I}|).

(34)

The parameter
q
in the parametrization of
\hat{\rho}_{I}
in Eq. (
11
) quantifies the degree of coherence of the state in the
H/V
basis: for
q=1
, the state is pure, and for
q=0
it is completely dephased (in the
H/V
basis). Note that, unlike the standard purification procedure, here, the states
|e_{j}\rangle
are manifestly not orthogonal. However, there is still large freedom in their choice as
q=\langle e_{H}|e_{V}\rangle=\langle e_{H}|U^{\dagger}U|e_{V}\rangle
for any unitary
U
. We can thus model a mixed state
\hat{\rho}_{I}
by
|\psi_{I}\rangle
in Eq. (
32
) as long as the overlap of environmental states satisfies Eq. (
33
). Analogously, the composite state of the second source is now

|\Psi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|H_{b},H_{b}\rangle+|V_{b},V_{b}\rangle)\otimes|e_{\Psi}\rangle.

(35)

We assume that the
H
- and
V
-polarized terms in Bell state
|\Psi\rangle
are perfectly coherent and so they share the same environmental state
|e_{\Psi}\rangle
.

[
9
]
. However, here we consider the case of homogeneous losses for every polarization component.
The corresponding term we denote by
|\psi_{w}\rangle
. By
|\psi_{S}\rangle
we denote the state of the signal photon created in Q1 and propagating along path
a
after it traversed HWP
S
and QWP
S
, i.e., just before it enters the beam splitter BS. Similarly, by
|\psi_{I}\rangle
we denote the state of the idler photon created in Q1 and propagating along path
b^{\prime}
after it traversed HWP
I
and QWP
I
, which is now of the form of Eq. (
32
). This state propagates through the source Q2 unaffected and is reflected by the dichroic mirror DM
I
out of the setup. The state of signal and idler photons just before the projective measurement reads

|\psi\rangle=\mathcal{N}\cdot\mathrm{BS}\cdot\mathrm{DM}_{I}\cdot(Te^{i\phi}|\psi_{S}\rangle\otimes|\psi_{I}\rangle\,\\
+\sqrt{1-T^{2}}\,e^{i\phi}|\psi_{S}\rangle\otimes|\psi_{w}\rangle+P\,|\Psi\rangle),

(36)

where
\phi
is the relative phase between the two sources,
P
determines the relative pump power between the first and the second source as
P^{2}
, and
\mathcal{N}
is a normalization factor equal to
\mathcal{N}=1/\sqrt{1+P^{2}}
.

Note that the state of the idler photon has two terms. One term is
|\psi_{I}\rangle
coming from Q1, and the other term is included in
|\Psi\rangle
coming from Q2. Both terms are reflected off a dichroic mirror DM
I
into path
c
out of the setup. The dichroic mirror DM
I
acts only on the idler state, and the beam splitter BS acts only on the signal state. We can, therefore, rewrite the above state into

|\psi\rangle=\mathcal{N}\,\big(Te^{i\phi}|\psi^{\prime}_{S}\rangle\otimes|\psi^{\prime}_{I}\rangle\\
+\sqrt{1-T^{2}}\,e^{i\phi}|\psi^{\prime}_{S}\rangle\otimes|\psi_{w}\rangle+P\,|\Psi^{\prime}\rangle\big),

(37)

where we defined
|\psi^{\prime}_{S}\rangle=\mathrm{BS}\cdot|\psi_{S}\rangle
,
|\psi^{\prime}_{I}\rangle=\mathrm{DM}_{I}\cdot|\psi_{I}\rangle
and
|\Psi^{\prime}\rangle=\mathrm{BS}\cdot\mathrm{DM}_{I}\cdot|\Psi\rangle
. Remember that
\langle\psi_{w}|\psi^{\prime}_{I}\rangle=\langle\psi_{w}|\Psi^{\prime}\rangle=0
.

At this point, we subject the signal photon to projective measurements, embodied by projectors
\hat{\Pi}_{k}
and
\hat{\Pi}_{k}^{\perp}=\mathbb{1}-\hat{\Pi}_{k}
. For a given orthonormal basis
\{|k\rangle,|k^{\perp}\rangle\}
the two projectors are given by
\hat{\Pi}_{k}=|k_{S}\rangle\langle k_{S}|\otimes\mathbb{1}
and
\hat{\Pi}_{k}^{\perp}=|k_{S}^{\perp}\rangle\langle k_{S}^{\perp}|\otimes\mathbb{1}
, where the identity acts on the idler photon as well as the environment. The probability of measuring
\hat{\Pi}_{k}
for state in Eq. (
37
) is easily shown to be

\langle\psi|\hat{\Pi}_{k}\,|\psi\rangle=\mathcal{N}^{2}(c_{k}+2\,P\,T\,\mathrm{Re}(e^{-i\phi}z_{k})),

(38)

where we defined numerical quantities

\displaystyle c_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\psi^{\prime}_{S}|\hat{\Pi}_{k}|\psi^{\prime}_{S}\rangle+P^{2}\langle\Psi^{\prime}|\hat{\Pi}_{k}|\Psi^{\prime}\rangle,

(39)

\displaystyle z_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\psi^{\prime}_{S}|\otimes\langle\psi^{\prime}_{I}|\hat{\Pi}_{k}|\Psi^{\prime}\rangle.

(40)

Before we proceed to discuss the visibilities of these detection probabilities, let us emphasize that there is not a single term in Eq. (
38
) that would contain inner products of
|\psi^{\prime}_{I}\rangle
with itself or its projections onto some subspace. For that reason, there is no term that would contain the inner product
\langle e_{H}|e_{V}\rangle
(
33
), whose determination is necessary for the characterization of the mixed state
\hat{\rho}_{I}
. This observation is independent of particular forms of
|\psi^{\prime}_{S}\rangle
and
|\Psi^{\prime}\rangle
and is thus quite general. We, therefore, conclude with the important statement that the imbalanced ZWM interferometer in Fig.
2
does
not
allow for the perfect reconstruction of mixed states unless additional constraints are enforced. We come back to this problem in Sec.
IV.5
.

IV.3
Visibilities

In this section, we carry out the derivation of visibilities in different bases in a way analogous to that in Sec.
III.2
. When the signal photon’s polarization is measured in an orthonormal basis
\{|k\rangle,|k^{\perp}\rangle\}
, the probability of detecting
|k\rangle
is given in Eq. (
38
), which profile exhibits interference as the phase
\phi
is varied. The visibility of the emergent interference pattern is given by (see Appendix
B
)

\mathcal{V}_{k}=2\,P\,T\,\frac{|z_{k}|}{c_{k}},

(41)

where
c_{k}
and
z_{k}
are defined in Eqs. (
39
) and (
40
), respectively. This expression attains a conveniently simple form when we set
P=1
and choose the signal photon’s state
|\psi_{S}\rangle
to be unbiased with both
|k\rangle
and
|k^{\perp}\rangle
, i.e.,
|\langle\psi_{S}|k\rangle|=|\langle\psi_{S}|k^{\perp}\rangle|=1/\sqrt{2}
. It reduces to (see Appendix
C
)

\mathcal{V}_{k}=T|\langle\psi^{\prime}_{I}|k^{\ast}\rangle|e_{\Psi}\rangle|,

(42)

where
|k^{\ast}\rangle
is the “complex conjugate” of vector
|k\rangle
in the sense that if
|k\rangle=U|H\rangle
for some unitary
U
, then
|k^{\ast}\rangle=U^{\ast}|H\rangle
, where the star stands for the complex conjugation. The visibilities are thus equal to the overlap between the idler state (with its environment) and the complex conjugate basis state (with environment
|e_{\Psi}\rangle
).

For completeness, let us list the visibilities of detection probabilities for the typical choice of single-qubit bases
H/V
,
D/A
, and
L/R
. For these, we get
(k,k^{\ast})\in\{(H,H),(V,V),(D,D),(A,A),(L,R),(R,L)\}
and the visibilities explicitly read

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{H}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T\,\alpha\,|\langle e_{H}|e_{\Psi}\rangle|,\quad\mathcal{V}_{V}=T\,\beta\,|\langle e_{V}|e_{\Psi}\rangle|,

(43)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{D/A}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(T/\sqrt{2})|\alpha\langle e_{H}|e_{\Psi}\rangle\pm\beta e^{-i\xi}\langle e_{V}|e_{\Psi}\rangle|,

(44)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{L/R}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(T/\sqrt{2})|\alpha\langle e_{H}|e_{\Psi}\rangle\pm i\beta e^{-i\xi}\langle e_{V}|e_{\Psi}\rangle|.

(45)

Compare these expressions with Eqs. (
20
)–(
22
). The products of environmental states apparently play an important role and for convenience we introduce the following notation

\displaystyle m_{H}

\displaystyle=|\langle e_{H}|e_{\Psi}\rangle|,\quad m_{V}=|\langle e_{V}|e_{\Psi}\rangle|,

(46)

\displaystyle\Delta\varphi

\displaystyle=\arg\left(\langle e_{H}|e_{\Psi}\rangle\right)-\arg\left(\langle e_{V}|e_{\Psi}\rangle\right).

(47)

These parameters quantify the coherence of the
H
and
V
modes of the idler state with the second source Q2 and their properties are further studied in Sec.
IV.5
.

IV.4
Visibility Stokes parameters for mixed states

In the previous section, formulas (
43
)–(
45
) connect the form of environmental states and the visibilities of signal photon detection rates in individual Pauli bases. It is easy to verify from these expressions that

\mathcal{V}_{H}^{2}+\mathcal{V}_{V}^{2}=\mathcal{V}_{D}^{2}+\mathcal{V}_{A}^{2}=\mathcal{V}_{L}^{2}+\mathcal{V}_{R}^{2}=\mathcal{S}_{0},

(48)

where we defined

\mathcal{S}_{0}\equiv T^{2}(\alpha^{2}m_{H}^{2}+\beta^{2}m_{V}^{2}).

(49)

The sum of squares of visibilities in the two orthogonal states is thus constant. This constant quantifies the level of coherence between the first and the second source, and we refer to it as the
zeroth visibility Stokes parameter

\mathcal{S}_{0}
. It is easy to check that
0\leq\mathcal{S}_{0}\leq T^{2}\leq 1
(compare with Eq. (
28
)). In Appendix
D
we show that
\mathcal{V}_{k}^{2}+\mathcal{V}_{k^{\perp}}^{2}=\mathcal{S}_{0}
for any orthonormal basis
\{|k\rangle,|k^{\perp}\rangle\}
, not just the Pauli bases.

We can define the other visibility Stokes parameters for mixed states in the exact same way as we did for pure states in Eqs. (
29
)–(
31
) except that now the visibilities are given by Eq. (
42
). The visibility Stokes parameters then explicitly read

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T^{2}\,2\,\alpha\,\beta\,m_{H}\,m_{V}\,\cos(\xi+\Delta\varphi),

(50)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T^{2}\,2\,\alpha\,\beta\,m_{H}\,m_{V}\,\sin(\xi+\Delta\varphi),

(51)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T^{2}\,(\alpha^{2}\,m^{2}_{H}-\beta^{2}\,m^{2}_{V}).

(52)

The transmission coefficient
T
can be measured for a given experimental setup independently of a particular state of the idler photon, and one can thus adjust the formulas above by removing
T
. For this reason, from now on, we effectively set
T=1
.

There are a number of problems associated with these expressions. First, the coherence terms
m_{H}
and
m_{V}
always appear in the product with
\alpha
and
\beta
, respectively, which precludes the determination of the values of
\alpha
and
\beta
alone. Second, as discussed in Sec.
IV.1
, these formulas explicitly depend on the environment of the second source
|e_{\Psi}\rangle
, and there is no quantity
q
present in the formulas. These two issues together imply that a given triple of visibility Stokes parameters
(\mathcal{S}_{x},\mathcal{S}_{y},\mathcal{S}_{z})
is consistent with many states in the standard Bloch sphere. Moreover, the environment can have many degrees of freedom, and it can happen that states
|e_{H}\rangle
and
|e_{V}\rangle
turn out to be orthogonal to
|e_{\Psi}\rangle
, in which case all the visibility parameters are zero:
\mathcal{S}_{x}=\mathcal{S}_{y}=\mathcal{S}_{z}=0
. We would then misinterpret our results as corresponding to the maximally mixed state, whose Bloch vector is a zero vector.

In the next section, the aforementioned issues are discussed in detail, and possible solutions to the ambiguity of the visibility Stokes parameters are proposed.

IV.5
Role of asymmetric coherence

The quantities
q
,
m_{H}
,
m_{V}
, and
\Delta\varphi
defined in the previous sections are not completely independent of each other. As shown in Appendix
E
, they have to satisfy the inequality

1-\left(q^{2}+m_{H}^{2}+m_{V}^{2}\right)+2\,q\,m_{H}\,m_{V}\cos(\Delta\varphi)\geq 0,

(53)

where additionally
0\leq q\leq 1
,
0\leq m_{H}\leq 1
, and
0\leq m_{V}\leq 1
. The inequality (
53
) embodies a certain type of transitivity of coherence: when any two of the coherence parameters are close to unity, say
m_{H}
and
m_{V}
, the third parameter,
q
, has to be almost unity as well. In terms of polarization modes and the physical setup in Fig.
2
: when
H
polarization mode from Q1 is highly coherent with
H
produced by Q2 and also
V
mode from Q1 is highly coherent with
V
produced by Q2, then due to the fact that NL2 and NL3 in Q2 are highly coherent with each other, also the two polarization modes
H
and
V
from Q1 are highly coherent with each other. The region of valid values of
q
,
m_{H}
, and
m_{V}
while
\Delta\varphi=0
is plotted in Fig.
3
. It is obvious from Eq. (
53
) that the regions for nonzero
\Delta\varphi
are subsets of the region in Fig.
3
.

As is evident from Fig.
3
, for large enough values of
m_{H}
and
m_{V}
, only a subset of values of
q
can be attained. The whole range of
q
from 0 to 1 is accessible only when
0\leq m_{H}=m_{V}\leq 1/\sqrt{2}
. This subset of values is depicted as a dim gray dashed line in Fig.
3
. In all the other cases, the inequality (
53
) restricts the range of input states
\hat{\rho}_{I}
that are consistent with the measured visibilities. For example, when both
m_{H}
and
m_{V}
are equal to 1, the only allowed value for
q
is also 1. In such a case, only pure states of the idler photon can give rise to the measured visibilities.

with

. For such values, the inequality (

m_{V}=q
and
\Delta\varphi=0
. This situation is depicted as a red line in Fig.

. Assuming that

, the visibility Stokes parameters in Eqs. (

)–(

) reduce to

\displaystyle=

(56)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{0}

\displaystyle=

V
Algebraic properties of visibility Stokes parameters

In analogy to the standard Bloch representation, we can introduce the
visibility Bloch vector

\vec{\mathcal{S}}
as the triple

\displaystyle\vec{\mathcal{S}}=(\mathcal{S}_{x},\mathcal{S}_{y},\mathcal{S}_{z}).

(64)

It can be checked by the direct substitution of their explicit forms in Eqs. (
50
)–(
52
) that the three parameters satisfy

\mathcal{S}_{x}^{2}+\mathcal{S}_{y}^{2}+\mathcal{S}_{z}^{2}=\mathcal{S}_{0}^{2}

(65)

and so the norm of the visibility Bloch vector is equal to the zeroth parameter:
|\vec{\mathcal{S}}|=\mathcal{S}_{0}
. An important question is how close this visibility Bloch vector is to the actual Bloch vector
\vec{r}=(x,y,z)
. It is straightforward to show from the explicit expressions in Eqs. (
12
)–(
14
) and Eqs. (
50
)–(
52
) together with the inequality in Eq. (
53
), that

\displaystyle(\mathcal{S}_{x}-x)^{2}+(\mathcal{S}_{y}-y)^{2}+(\mathcal{S}_{z}-z)^{2}\leq(1-\mathcal{S}_{0})^{2}

(66)

and so the Euclidean distance between the two vectors is bounded from above by the
incoherence

1-\mathcal{S}_{0}
. All the Bloch vectors consistent with a given
\vec{\mathcal{S}}
thus form a ball centered in
\vec{\mathcal{S}}
with radius
1-\mathcal{S}_{0}
, as shown in Fig.
4
. The ball always touches the surface of the Bloch sphere at a point that corresponds to the pure state of the form
\alpha_{E}|H\rangle+\sqrt{1-\alpha_{E}^{2}}\exp(i\xi)|V\rangle
, where
\alpha_{E}=(1/\sqrt{2})\sqrt{1+\mathcal{S}_{z}/\mathcal{S}_{0}}
(cf. Appendix
D
).

Equation (
65
) resembles the relation
x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}=r^{2}
for standard Stokes parameters, where
r\equiv|\vec{r}|
is the norm of the Bloch vector. This norm relates to the
purity

\mathscr{P}
of the corresponding quantum state as
\mathscr{P}=(1+r^{2})/2
. For visibility Stokes parameters, such a relation does not hold. However, from Eqs. (
65
) and (
66
) one can easily show that

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}\leq(\vec{r}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{S}})+1-\mathcal{S}_{0}.

(67)

One can apply the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to the inner product in this formula to find the direct relation between the purity
\mathscr{P}
and the coherence
\mathcal{S}_{0}
. We arrive at the
lower
bound in the form:
\mathscr{P}\geq 1-2\mathcal{S}_{0}(1-\mathcal{S}_{0})
as long as
\mathcal{S}_{0}\geq 1/2
(for
\mathcal{S}_{0}<1/2
no special lower bound on
\mathscr{P}
applies). Analogously, one can derive that
\mathcal{S}_{0}\leq(1+r)/2
.

When the inequality (
67
) is understood as a constraint on varying visibility Stokes parameters while the Bloch vector
\vec{r}
is fixed, it turns out that this inequality represents a rotational ellipsoid, see Appendix
D
. The ellipsoid has its center in point
\vec{r}/2
, its two loci are located in the origin
(0,0,0)
and point
\vec{r}
, and its semiaxes read
1/2
and
\sqrt{1-r^{2}}/2
. Depending on coherence conditions one can thus for a given polarization state of a photon obtain many visibility Bloch vectors and all these vectors form an ellipsoid, see Fig.
5
.

Several other algebraic properties of the visibility Stokes parameters can be found in Appendix
D
.

VI
Measurement operators corresponding to visibility measurements

The standard Stokes parameters can be expressed as expectation values of measurement operators in a given quantum state. In this section, we analogously introduce the operators corresponding to the visibility Stokes parameters. At first, note that from Eq. (
42
) and the definition of
|\psi^{\prime}_{I}\rangle
it follows that

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{k}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T^{2}|\langle\psi^{\prime}_{I}|k^{\ast}\rangle|e_{\Psi}\rangle|^{2}

(68)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T^{2}\langle\psi^{\prime}_{I}|k^{\ast}\rangle|e_{\Psi}\rangle\langle e_{\Psi}|\langle k^{\ast}|\psi^{\prime}_{I}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{I}|\,\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{k}\,|\psi_{I}\rangle,

where we defined an operator

\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{k}=T^{2}(\mathrm{DM}_{I}^{\dagger}\cdot|k^{\ast}\rangle\langle k^{\ast}|\cdot\mathrm{DM}_{I})\otimes|e_{\Psi}\rangle\langle e_{\Psi}|.

(69)

One can therefore express the visibilities as expectation values of specific measurement operators. Following Eqs. (
29
)–(
31
), these operators can be used to introduce a triple of operators that correspond to the visibility Stokes parameters

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{x}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{D}-\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{A},

(70)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{y}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{L}-\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{R},

(71)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{z}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{H}-\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{V}.

(72)

All of these are Hermitian operators whose form is independent of the idler state
|\psi_{I}\rangle
. When expressed explicitly, their form turns out to be a tensor product of Pauli matrices and the projector on the state of the second source’s environment:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T^{2}(|D_{b^{\prime}}\rangle\langle D_{b^{\prime}}|-|A_{b^{\prime}}\rangle\langle A_{b^{\prime}}|)\otimes|e_{\Psi}\rangle\langle e_{\Psi}|,

(73)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T^{2}(|L_{b^{\prime}}\rangle\langle L_{b^{\prime}}|-|R_{b^{\prime}}\rangle\langle R_{b^{\prime}}|)\otimes|e_{\Psi}\rangle\langle e_{\Psi}|,

(74)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T^{2}(|H_{b^{\prime}}\rangle\langle H_{b^{\prime}}|-|V_{b^{\prime}}\rangle\langle V_{b^{\prime}}|)\otimes|e_{\Psi}\rangle\langle e_{\Psi}|.

(75)

These operators act on the idler polarization state together with its environment. From Eq. (
68
) it follows that the visibility Stokes parameters (cf. Eqs. (
29
)–(
31
)) coincide with the expectation values of the operators in Eqs. (
70
)–(
72
) as

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{I}|\ \hat{\mathcal{S}}_{x}\ |\psi_{I}\rangle,

(76)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{I}|\ \hat{\mathcal{S}}_{y}\ |\psi_{I}\rangle,

(77)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{I}|\ \hat{\mathcal{S}}_{z}\ |\psi_{I}\rangle.

(78)

We have just shown how the visibility measurements that are physically performed on signal photons are expressed as measurement operators acting on the undetected idler photon.
2
2

2

The visibility operators give information that is in actuality obtained from many copies of the idler photon state, each measured for a different interferometric phase
\phi
.
The fact that this is possible shows how information about the undetected idler photon is accessible without detecting it. The measurement operator associated with the zeroth visibility parameter
\mathcal{S}_{0}
reads

\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{0}=T^{2}(\mathbb{1}\otimes|e_{\Psi}\rangle\langle e_{\Psi}|).

(79)

In the standard tomography, this operator is equal to the identity and does not provide us with any additional information. In our case, though, the expectation value of this operator contains information about the coherence between the idler state and the state of the second source.

Even though the operators
\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{x}
,
\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{y}
, and
\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{z}
derived above resemble the Pauli operators in Eqs. (
6
)–(
8
), it is important to emphasize that they do not form a complete measurement in the usual sense. Formally speaking, each visibility Stokes operator
\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{k}
corresponds to two positive measurement operators
\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{k}
and
\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{k^{\perp}}
. These two operators nevertheless do not satisfy the completeness relation
\sum_{k}\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{k}=\mathbb{1}
. Instead, they sum up to
\mathcal{S}_{0}
, cf. Eq. (
48
). This can be remedied by expanding each set of visibility operators with an operator
\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathrm{incoh}}\equiv\mathbb{1}-\hat{\mathcal{S}}_{0}
, quantifying the incoherence between the two sources. Unlike the standard polarization measurement, where e.g.
\hat{\Pi}_{H}+\hat{\Pi}_{V}=\mathbb{1}
with
\hat{\Pi}_{k}=|k\rangle\langle k|
, here we have
\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{H}+\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{V}+\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathrm{incoh}}=\mathbb{1}
. Let us note that for
T=1
, the visibility operators, including
\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathrm{incoh}}
, are projectors.

VII
Post-measurement states

In this section, we briefly discuss the post-measurement state of the idler photon alone. Let us treat for simplicity only pure polarization states
|\psi_{Ib^{\prime}}\rangle
(
15
), in which case the pre-measurement state
|\psi\rangle
(
37
) can be rewritten into

\displaystyle|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle Te^{i\phi}(\mathrm{BS}|\psi_{Sa}\rangle)\otimes|\psi^{\prime}_{I}\rangle

(80)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sqrt{1-T^{2}}\,e^{i\phi}(\mathrm{BS}|\psi_{Sa}\rangle)\otimes|\psi_{w}\rangle

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{P}{\sqrt{2}}\,(\mathrm{BS}|H_{Sb}\rangle)\otimes(\mathrm{DM}_{I}|H_{I}\rangle)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{P}{\sqrt{2}}\,(\mathrm{BS}|V_{Sb}\rangle)\otimes(\mathrm{DM}_{I}|V_{I}\rangle).

Vectors associated with different paths,
a
and
b
, are orthogonal to each other and so
\langle\psi_{Sa}|H_{Sb}\rangle=\langle\psi_{Sa}|V_{Sb}\rangle=0
. Since both the beam splitter and the dichroic mirror are unitary operations, they do not change the orthogonality of vectors, and it is, therefore, not hard to perform a partial trace of the expression above to get the reduced state of the idler photon. We obtain

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{I}^{\mathrm{post}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Tr_{S}(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|)

(81)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{T^{2}}{1+P^{2}}|\psi^{\prime}_{I}\rangle\langle\psi^{\prime}_{I}|+\frac{P^{2}}{1+P^{2}}\left(\mathbb{1}/2\right)+W,

where

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1-T^{2}}{1+P^{2}}|\psi_{w}\rangle\langle\psi_{w}|

(82)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{T\sqrt{1-T^{2}}}{1+P^{2}}\left(|\psi^{\prime}_{I}\rangle\langle\psi_{w}|+|\psi_{w}\rangle\langle\psi^{\prime}_{I}|\right)

is that part of the state that ends up in path
w
. When the transmission of the setup is perfect, and
P=1
, the final state of the idler photon reads

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{I}^{\mathrm{post}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}|\psi^{\prime}_{I}\rangle\langle\psi^{\prime}_{I}|+\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbb{1}/2\right).

(83)

The final state is a uniform mixture of the maximally mixed state
\mathbb{1}/2
and the original pure state
|\psi^{\prime}_{I}\rangle
in path
c
(
15
). Evidently, the post-measurement state of the idler photon still contains some information about its original state of polarization.

VIII
Conclusion

We develop the formalism of visibility Stokes parameters that complements the standard Stokes parameters in the context of coherence-based quantum operations. Specifically, we focus on the technique of quantum state tomography of undetected photons introduced in Ref.
[
9
]
. The visibility Stokes parameters characterize a quantum system that is not directly measured but whose state information can be extracted via quantum interference using the effect of induced coherence without induced emission
[
2
]
. This methodology thus profoundly differs from the standard intensity measurements for state reconstruction. The visibility Stokes parameters and the corresponding visibility operators go beyond the problem of quantum state tomography and constitute a bedrock for other quantum information techniques. These operators could enable the translation of single-photon protocols such as the BB84
[
25
]
and the B92
[
26
]
to the realm of optical coherence.

The form of the visibility Stokes parameters is similar to the standard Stokes parameters. Still unlike the latter, which are based on intensity measurements, the former are determined through coherence measurements in the form of visibilities. For the general case of mixed states, there are several relations between the Stokes parameters and their visibility counterparts. In general, a given triple of visibility Stokes parameters can be consistent with many polarization states of the idler photon. Similarly, many triples of visibility Stokes parameters may represent a single polarization state. We discuss possible ways to eliminate this ambiguity and establish a one-to-one relation between the standard and the visibility Stokes parameters.

To analyze the dependence of visibility Stokes parameters on the input state and the physical setup, we thoroughly analyze the environment of the idler photon. The overlap of states of environment for the first and the second source in the setup models the mixedness of the idler photon’s state and the mutual coherence between the sources. In this discussion, we assume that the two crystals composing the second source are perfectly coherent and produce a Bell pair of polarization. One could expand this discussion and study the effects of partially coherent crystals. In addition, the concept of mutually unbiased bases is applied to find an efficient implementation of the quantum tomography of undetected photons. This way, not only the form of the resulting formulas is simplified, but one can also monitor counts simultaneously in both outputs in the actual physical setup, not just one as done in Ref.
[
9
]
.

In conclusion, we introduce a set of quantities akin to the standard Stokes parameters but for quantum systems composed of undetected photons. Whether there are other visibility parameters for other quantum entities remains open. The question of generalizing our results to higher dimensions is left open as well. With this, we expect to pave the way for adapting more state estimation techniques and quantum information protocols for undetected photonic systems.
