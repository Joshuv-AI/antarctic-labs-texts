A Host-Clinical Integrated Prediction Model for Early Sepsis Risk in Diabetic Patients with Acute Infections: Development and Internal Validation
Authors: Xilong Pan, Zhiyuan Xu, ChanJuan Zhuo, Li Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveDiabetic patients exhibit increased susceptibility to infections and a higher risk of progression to sepsis. This study aimed to develop a sepsis risk prediction model specifically for diabetic patients with acute infections.MethodsIn this retrospective, single-center study, we enrolled 604 hospitalized diabetic patients with acute infections. A propensity score-matched cohort (103 sepsis cases vs 412 non-sepsis controls) was constructed. A predictive model integrating clinical manifestations and laboratory parameters was developed by first pre-selecting variables via a bootstrap-based penalized regression process, followed by Firth penalized logistic regression. The model underwent internal validation and subgroup analysis, with its performance compared against the National Early Warning Score 2 (NEWS2), Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome (SIRS), and Quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) scoring systems in terms of discrimination, calibration, clinical utility, and cost-effectiveness.ResultsThe final model incorporated five predictors: temperature dysregulation, nausea or vomiting, fatigue, albumin, and procalcitonin. It demonstrated excellent predictive performance, with a corrected AUC of 0.915 and a calibration slope of 1.022. Subgroup analyses confirmed consistent performance across all subgroups, and the model significantly outperformed NEWS2, SIRS, and qSOFA in predictive accuracy, clinical applicability, and cost-effectiveness.ConclusionThe host-clinical integrated model developed in this study could provide a novel tool for early sepsis risk warning in diabetic patients with acute infections. This approach of integrating host characteristics with the pathophysiology of sepsis may offer new insights for sepsis risk early warning in specific populations. However, external validation in independent cohorts is needed before clinical implementation.Keywords: diabetes, acute infection, sepsis, risk prediction, host response

Introduction

Patients with diabetes are at an increased risk of infection and progression to sepsis due to hyperglycemia-induced impairments in immune, neurological, and vascular function. These impairments include defective neutrophil migration and chemotaxis, reduced complement activity, and altered cytokine responses, which collectively compromise the host’s ability to contain localized infections and facilitate sepsis progression.1–3 With increasing diabetes prevalence, sepsis incidence in this group is rising.3,4 A survey in the United States revealed that in 2011 alone, the total hospital costs for patients with diabetes complicated by infections exceeded $48 billion.5 Among sepsis-related hospitalizations, approximately 25% of patients had diabetes, and the in-hospital mortality rate for these individuals exceeded 30%.5 Multiple large-scale studies have consistently indicated and called for an urgent need to establish an integrated diabetes-infection management model. Strengthening the timely identification of sepsis in diabetic patients and implementing effective interventions are critical to alleviating the burden of this disease and reducing mortality rates.6–8 Several studies on sepsis risk identification have been reported for specific diabetic subpopulations, such as those with diabetic foot infections9 or urinary tract infections.10 However, to the best of our knowledge, studies specifically exploring sepsis risk identification tools for the entire adult diabetic population (covering all types of acute infections) remain limited. Current diagnostics are inadequate: The Third International Consensus Definitions for Sepsis and Septic Shock (Sepsis-3) criteria11 are too complex for primary care; other conventional scoring systems, such as the Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome (SIRS) criteria, the National Early Warning Score (NEWS), and the Quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA), suffer from inadequate sensitivity, are compromised by poor specificity, or are inherently limited in their comprehensive exclusion capacity;12–16 and single biomarkers perform poorly.17 As noted above, chronic hyperglycemia, low-grade inflammation, immune dysregulation, and autonomic neuropathy collectively alter how diabetic patients respond to an infectious challenge, making generic tools such as SIRS, NEWS, and qSOFA potentially inadequate for this specific population. Therefore, shifting from a “one-size-fits-all” generic screening strategy to a paradigm that considers host-specific features may offer advantages for this population. Previous literature has suggested that certain host-specific factors may be associated with infection outcomes in diabetic populations, including hypoalbuminemia,9,10,18 gastrointestinal symptoms such as nausea or vomiting,19–22 fatigue,1–3,23,24 and the inflammatory biomarker procalcitonin.25,26 Therefore, this study aimed to develop a composite sepsis risk prediction model for diabetic patients with acute infections based on routinely available clinical signs, symptoms, and laboratory parameters.

Patients and Methods

Study Population

This retrospective study consecutively enrolled adult (≥18 years) non-pregnant and non-postpartum hospitalized patients with diabetes and concomitant acute infections who were admitted to Zhongshan Dongsheng Hospital in Zhongshan City, Guangdong Province, China, between July 2019 and March 2025. The cohort was subsequently stratified into sepsis and non-sepsis groups.

The diagnosis of acute infection was established based on Chinese Expert Consensus on Early Prevention and Blocking of Sepsis,27 defined by the presence of at least two of the following three clinical criteria and concomitant receipt of antimicrobial therapy or microbiological testing: ① axillary temperature ≥37.3°C or ≤36.1°C within 72 hours; ② abnormal white blood cell count (>9.5×109/L or <3.5×109/L); ③ C-reactive protein >4.0 mg/L. Sepsis was diagnosed using the Sepsis-3 criteria,11 defined as confirmed infection with a ΔSOFA score ≥2. Based on WHO criteria, diabetes was diagnosed by meeting any of: fasting plasma glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L; 2- hour postprandial plasma glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L; random plasma glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L; or HbA1c ≥6.5%; or a documented history of diabetes. In this study, the diagnostic criteria for acute infection were redefined according to the Chinese Expert Consensus as described above. By contrast, the diagnostic criteria for sepsis consistently followed the Sepsis-3 criteria, and the diagnostic criteria for diabetes consistently followed the WHO criteria throughout the entire study period. Inclusion criteria were hospitalised patients meeting the acute infection diagnosis. Exclusion criteria included repeated admissions of the same patient, transferred patients with a confirmed diagnosis from other hospitals, patients who received albumin(ALB) biological agent therapy prior to a confirmed diagnosis, patients missing key outcomes or essential records (eg, due to discharge against medical advice/transfer).

Model Performance Comparison

Performance differences between model1 and comparators (model2-4) were evaluated across discrimination, calibration, clinical utility, and cost-effectiveness using 1000 bootstrap resamples.

(1)Quantitative metrics and visualisation: The differences in the Area Under the Curve (ΔAUC), Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI), Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI), Brier Score difference (ΔBrier Score), and net benefit difference (ΔNB) within the decision threshold range of 0.01–0.50 were calculated. Visualisation used ROC curves, calibration curves, decision curve analysis (DCA), and ΔNB curves. Under a predefined cost structure (FP:50, FN:1000, TP:-300, TN:-10), the decision cost advantage of the models was comprehensively evaluated by calculating the inter-model differences in the Area Under the Cost Curve (ΔAUCC) and the overall mean cost difference (ΔCost), along with the mean ΔCost at specific thresholds, supported by cost-curve visualization. This cost framework, hypothetically based on literature,28–30 simulates the clinical trade-offs in sepsis diagnosis and treatment regarding early intervention.

Subgroup Analysis

Prespecified subgroup analyses were conducted by sex (male/female), age (≤60/>60 years), and comorbidity history (yes/no). AUCs and 95% CIs for each subgroup were calculated using 1000 Bootstrap resamples, comparing differences with the modelling population. Due to sample size limitations, cross-stratified analysis was not performed; all results are considered exploratory.

Sensitivity Analysis in the Pre-Match Entire Cohort

The final model was validated on the pre-match entire cohort by calculating the AUC with 95% CI and comparing predictive performance stability against the modeling (post-PSM) population using DeLong’s test. Consistency in variable composition and stability in coefficient direction and significance (maintaining the P<0.10 criterion) were analyzed to evaluate the model’s applicability to the pre-match population.

Model Visualization and Clinical Translation

To facilitate clinical interpretation and bedside risk assessment, a nomogram was designed to convert the complex regression equation into a user-friendly visual tool. The points for each variable were planned to be calculated by linearly transforming the linear predictor (lp) to a 0–100 scale. The Points axis was planned to include minor grid lines (step = 1) to facilitate precise reading.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation; non-normally distributed data as median [interquartile range]. Categorical variables are described as frequency (percentage). All analyses were conducted using R 4.5.1 in strict accordance with the methodology detailed in Section 2.3. The significance level was set at α=0.05 (two-tailed), except for variable screening which used P<0.10.

Results

Patient Selection and Study Cohort Establishment

This retrospective cohort study initially included 3649 adult non-pregnant and non-postpartum diabetic inpatients (≥18 years) from Zhongshan Dongsheng Hospital (July 2019–March 2025). After sequential exclusions, 1486 patients with infection remained (excluded: 2163 without infection). Further exclusion of 822 non-acute infections yielded 664 acute infection cases. Subsequent exclusions comprised: transfers with prior external diagnosis (n=4), duplicate admissions (n=43), history of hypoalbuminemia (n=4), pre-diagnosis albumin-based therapy (n=0), and missing key outcomes/records (n=9). The final cohort (n=604) was stratified into sepsis (n=103) and non-sepsis (n=501) groups.

Missing laboratory values (LYM%, NEU%, CRP, PCT, ALB) ranged from 0.19–4.39% in non-sepsis and 2.91–4.85% in sepsis groups, and were handled by median imputation. Propensity score matching created the final cohort: 103 sepsis patients matched with 412 non-sepsis controls (Supplementary Figure S1). Baseline characteristics pre- and post-matching are shown in Supplementary Table S2.

Variable Selection and Model Construction

Penalized regression with bootstrap resampling initially identified six variables. After excluding LYM% (P>0.10) using multivariable Firth regression, an initial 5-variable model was constructed and demonstrated significant predictive performance (χ2=209.17, P<0.001). Temperature dysregulation (OR=2.314), nausea or vomiting (OR=2.480), fatigue (OR=1.817), and PCT (nonlinear; linear OR=3.204) were independent risk predictors, while ALB (OR=0.940) was a protective predictor (Supplementary Table S3 and Table 2).

Model Performance Comparison Results

Model 1 significantly outperformed models 2–4 across all four dimensions: Discrimination (AUC increased by 0.148–0.244, 95% CI >0; supported by IDI/NRI), Calibration (Brier score decreased by 0.046–0.058, 95% CI <0; calibration curve closest to the ideal), Clinical Utility (demonstrated not only a broader range (0.00 to >0.80) of non-negative net benefit than all comparators, but also significantly superior net benefit at thresholds ≥0.03, 95% CI >0), and Cost-Effectiveness (ΔAUCC: –33,176 to –41,130; overall ΔCost: –32,847 to –40,723) (Tables 4–5, Figures 2–5, and Supplementary Figure S5).

Subgroup Analysis Results

Model1 exhibited stable performance across subgroups (AUC range: 0.879–0.927); no significant AUC differences were observed between subgroups or against the modeling cohort (AUC=0.915) (Supplementary Table S6). As an exploratory analysis (the study was not designed or powered to detect subgroup differences), these results should be interpreted with caution and require validation in larger, prospective cohorts.

Sensitivity Analysis Results in the Full Pre-Matched Population

Model 1 remained robust in the full pre-matched population: AUC 0.911 (vs 0.915 post-matching, P=0.839), Brier score 0.088 (vs 0.092 post-matching). In the full pre-matched population, the OR for fatigue was 1.705 (95% CI: 0.982–2.961). Although the confidence interval included 1, the lower limit was very close to 1, and the point estimate was very close to and directionally consistent with the post-matching OR (1.817). Meanwhile, given that propensity score matching reduced confounding by balancing baseline characteristics between groups, thereby revealing its true effect, we considered the effect trend to be stable Except for fatigue, the coefficients of the other variables maintained consistent direction and significance (P<0.05) (see Supplementary Tables S7–S8).

Discussion

In clinical practice, a history of diabetes can be confirmed directly during patient assessment. For newly diagnosed cases, whether symptomatic or not, underlying diabetes can be identified promptly using the Medical Standards for Diabetes Care31 and its risk checklist, via systematic history-taking, physical examination, and point-of-care testing for blood glucose and glycated haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c). Diabetes is not merely a common comorbidity but an active biological driver that worsens infection severity and sepsis progression. This was starkly demonstrated during the COVID-19 pandemic, where a sharp rise in mortality among diabetic patients underscored their heightened vulnerability to infection.6–8

Facing the rising prevalence of diabetes and emerging infectious threats, integrating chronic disease management with sepsis prevention and health system resilience has become a vital proactive strategy.6–8 Accordingly, based on predictive modeling guidelines,32 this study developed and internally validated the first sepsis prediction model for the entire diabetic population using host-specific features, clinical characteristics and laboratory results from diabetic patients with acute infections. At the Youden index threshold, the model showed high sensitivity (0.922) and acceptable specificity (0.808). Its predictive performance surpassed NEWS2, SIRS, and qSOFA, and it is more widely applicable than SOFA, making it suitable for general wards, outpatient, and emergency departments. This aligns with the high risk of missed diagnosis in sepsis screening and supports infection management in diabetes.

This study shifts sepsis risk screening from generic scoring systems to host-specific pathophysiology, establishing a targeted risk stratification model for this population.

Unique Value of Model Variables: From Universal Parameters to Host-Specific Markers

Beyond traditional inflammatory markers,17,33 this study added the nutritional indicator ALB,34 which outperformed CRP, LYM%, and NEU% in prediction. This may be related to the increased risk of acute kidney injury and enhanced albumin leakage associated with diabetic patients during sepsis.18 Consistent with earlier studies in specific infected populations (diabetic foot patients9 and diabetic urinary tract infection patients10), our results support the significant predictive value of albumin for sepsis in diabetic patients with infection, and further indicate that its role in severe infection may extend beyond treatment and prognosis assessment.

Increased intestinal permeability in critically ill patients is associated with the development of systemic inflammatory response syndrome and multiple organ dysfunction syndrome.19 Numerous studies also confirm that the gastrointestinal tract is the initiating organ of sepsis-induced multiple organ dysfunction.20–22 Our finding that diabetic sepsis patients have a higher incidence of nausea or vomiting may be an intuitive reflection of this pathological process. One possible explanation could be that sepsis exerts a “second hit” on the already vulnerable gastrointestinal function1 of diabetic patients by amplifying systemic inflammation, disrupting the intestinal barrier, exacerbating dysbiosis, and inducing hemodynamic disturbance. This might ultimately contribute to gastrointestinal dysfunction and a higher incidence of nausea or vomiting. However, this interpretation is speculative and requires further extensive research validation.

Compared to general infections, the intense systemic inflammatory storm in sepsis23 and potential post-infection autoimmune activation,24 when superimposed upon the vulnerable baseline of diabetes marked by chronic low-grade inflammation, immune dysregulation, and underlying mitochondrial dysfunction,1–3 could theoretically lead to more severe energy production collapse and metabolic dormancy activation,23,24 which might contribute to the higher prevalence of fatigue. Although the confidence interval for fatigue included 1 in the full pre-match population, the point estimate remained consistent with the main model, supporting its inclusion as a meaningful predictor.

While fever is common in acute infection, hypothermia specifically marks host compensatory collapse and clinical deterioration,35 likely being more concentrated in septic patients. This makes the overall incidence of “Temperature dysregulation” significantly differ between groups, potentially making it a key factor for identifying critical risk.

Previous studies have established that PCT offers superior sepsis prediction compared to CRP, NEU%, and WBC in infected type 2 diabetes patients.25,26 In this broader cohort including both type 1 and type 2 diabetes, PCT similarly demonstrated significantly better predictive performance than these routine inflammatory markers, further supporting its general applicability across diabetic populations with infection. Although this study spanned the entire COVID-19 period, PCT maintained strong sepsis risk prediction, suggesting that sepsis in these patients likely remains primarily caused by bacterial or fungal infections, or secondary bacterial/fungal infections post-viral illness. Nevertheless, consistent with relevant reports,17,36 our findings indicate that PCT’s discriminative capacity remains limited and should complement other indicators to collectively improve early sepsis detection.

Of particular note, this study found that the symptom variables associated with sepsis-3 criteria11 — namely “tachypnea or dyspnea” (respiratory-related) and “altered mental status” (neurological-related) — did not demonstrate strong predictive value in the model. This may be explained by two reasons. Firstly, it may relate to the complex acute complications of diabetes itself, such as hypoglycaemia, hyperosmolar states, and ketoacidosis, all of which can independently lead to changes in mental status and/or respiration.37,38 When superimposed on non-severe infections, these conditions may obscure sepsis-specific neurological or respiratory manifestations. This observation further reflects the distinct host characteristics of the diabetic population. Secondly, for clinical practicality, these variables were defined as binary rather than ordinal, which may have limited their discriminative ability. Nonetheless, our findings suggest that when patients present with these symptoms, combined testing of PCT and ALB can serve as a useful adjunct for rapidly distinguishing whether diabetic patients with acute infection are progressing to sepsis, while also highlighting the complementary advantage of using multiple indicators.

Clear Value for Clinical Translation: From Predictive Performance to Decision-Making and Economic Benefit

In predictive performance, the developed model achieved an AUC of 0.915, significantly outperforming NEWS (0.764), SIRS (0.745), and qSOFA (0.668). Beyond discriminative ability, its sensitivity and specificity also surpassed these scores, partially addressing their common trade-off limitation.16 The Integrated Discrimination Improvement and Net Reclassification Improvement both confirmed its superior classification capability. Additionally, the model’s calibration Brier score was 0.092, lower than those of NEWS (0.137), SIRS (0.141), and qSOFA (0.149). This improvement in calibration (Brier score reduction: 0.046–0.058) indicates more accurate risk estimation, enhancing clinical credibility. By integrating diabetes-specific clinical and laboratory indicators, the model compensates for the limited discriminative power of general screening tools in diabetic patients, demonstrating the advantage of multi-indicator approaches over single biomarkers.17 Notably, our findings partially differ from general-population reports on traditional scores:39–41 (1) Overall performance: NEWS and SIRS were comparable and better than qSOFA,39,40 consistent with our results. (2) Sensitivity/specificity patterns: qSOFA’s high specificity/low sensitivity and NEWS’s balanced performance39–41 align with our study; however, SIRS showed high sensitivity/low specificity here,39–41 differing from literature—possibly due to diabetic subpopulation characteristics and study design. Nevertheless, our results support conclusions from report.16 Furthermore, to address the potential selection bias associated with using only “ antimicrobial therapy or microbiological testing” to define infection,42 this study employed a combined approach in establishing the acute infection control group. Specifically, the diagnostic criteria from the Chinese Expert Consensus on Early Prevention and Blocking of Sepsis27 were integrated with commonly used operational indicators, with the aim of minimizing research bias.

Clinical decision-making value: Decision curve analysis showed the developed model had a non-negative net benefit range (0.00–>80) twice that of traditional scores. Across a wider threshold range—including the most clinically relevant—it provided significantly higher net benefit. Using the model to guide decisions (eg, intensive monitoring or early empirical treatment) helps achieve better outcomes by balancing true-positive benefits against false-positive risks. This gives clinicians a precise tool to weigh “avoiding missed high-risk patients” against “preventing overtreatment of low-risk patients,” aligning with the personalised, precise intervention strategy in current sepsis guidelines.28 This also addresses known limitations of traditional scores:16,39–41 qSOFA’s low sensitivity raises missed-diagnosis risk; SIRS’s low specificity may lead to antibiotic overuse and resource waste; and NEWS’s insufficient sensitivity here could delay identifying high-risk patients.

Health economics perspective: This study innovatively incorporated cost-benefit analysis to validate the economic utility of the developed model. Based on a predefined cost framework, the analysis showed the model achieved a smaller area under the cost curve, lower overall mean cost, and reduced mean cost at specific thresholds compared to traditional scores. This economic advantage stems from two mechanisms: firstly, the model’s high sensitivity (92.2%) prevents high rescue costs arising from missed sepsis progression—studies indicate delayed sepsis diagnosis exponentially increases medical costs;29,30 secondly, its relatively high specificity (80.8%) saves resources by avoiding unnecessary antibiotics and tests. For the high-cost diabetic population,5 applying the model could significantly optimise resource allocation and yield substantial economic benefits, thereby improving patient outcomes and healthcare efficiency simultaneously. Although the cost structure used is hypothetical and differs from real-world costs, this does not undermine the comparative conclusion. In summary, the model’s superior predictive performance, clear clinical net benefit, wide decision-threshold applicability, and marked cost-saving advantages together provide strong support for its clinical adoption. They also highlight potential limitations of general scoring tools in this specific high-risk population.

Finally, we constructed a nomogram based on the final model (model 1), providing a user-friendly tool for bedside risk assessment.

Additionally, several sepsis prediction models have been developed for specific diabetic subpopulations. Lin et al9 constructed a nomogram for diabetic foot patients using six predictors (diabetes duration, Texas grade, WBC, HbA1c, CRP, and albumin), achieving an AUC of 0.919. Wang et al10 developed a logistic regression model for diabetic patients with urinary tract infections, identifying albumin, CRP, and WBC as independent risk factors (AUC 0.809). Compared with these models, our study has several distinctive features. First, our model covers the entire adult diabetic population with acute infections, rather than being confined to a single infection type or complication. Second, we incorporated host-specific symptom variables (temperature dysregulation, nausea/vomiting, and fatigue) that reflect the unique pathophysiological responses of diabetic patients to infection, which were not included in previous models. Third, we used restricted cubic splines to model the nonlinear relationship of procalcitonin with sepsis risk, providing more accurate risk estimation. Direct external validation against these models in the same cohort is needed to confirm relative performance, as differences in study populations, infection types, and predictor sets preclude direct comparisons.

Methodological Prudence and Model Robustness

During modeling, caliper-free propensity score matching enhanced causal inference robustness and potential real-world applicability. Firth penalized logistic regression addressed small-sample bias and complete separation.43,44 Key predictors were selected using penalized regression with Bootstrap resampling. During validation, robustness was verified through influential point identification and sensitivity analysis. Bootstrap-based internal validation demonstrated excellent discrimination and ideal calibration. Exploratory subgroup analysis confirmed stable performance across subgroups. Sensitivity analysis in the full pre-matched population further supported good generalizability. These methodological measures collectively ensured the final model’s favorable robustness.

In summary, this study demonstrates that incorporating quantified diabetes-specific host factors into a sepsis risk assessment framework alongside inflammatory markers can provide meaningful predictive benefits. However, given the lack of external validation, the clinical applicability of this model should not be overinterpreted. External validation in independent cohorts is essential before clinical implementation.

Limitations and Future Directions

Its single-center retrospective design requires future validation by prospective multicenter studies. The limited availability of point-of-care HbA1c and PCT testing in primary care also calls for developing resource-adapted model versions. Given the model’s high sensitivity but limited positive predictive value, it is most suitable for early screening. Future work should therefore focus on creating synergistic confirmatory models to establish a reliable “screening-confirmation” system.

Furthermore, symptoms such as fatigue, nausea, and vomiting were extracted from electronic medical records. Since sicker patients tend to receive more detailed documentation, this retrospective symptom collection may introduce information bias.

Conclusion

This study preliminarily developed a sepsis prediction model for acutely infected diabetic patients. By integrating clinical symptoms and biomarkers, it not only provides a tool with considerable efficacy for sepsis risk stratification in diabetic patients with acute infections, but more importantly, quantifies and incorporates diabetes-specific host factors into the sepsis risk assessment framework, thus underscoring the host-oriented concept of risk evaluation. This strategy may provide novel insights for developing population-specific specialised sepsis risk early warning systems.
