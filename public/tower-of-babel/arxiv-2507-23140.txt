Learning Smooth Populations of Parameters with Trial Heterogeneity
JungHo Lee, Valerio Baćak, Edward H. Kennedy

Abstract
We consider the classical problem of estimating the mixing distribution of binomial mixtures, but under trial heterogeneity and smoothness . This problem has been studied extensively when the trial parameter is homogeneous, but not under the more general scenario of heterogeneous trials, and only within a low smoothness regime, where the resulting rates are slow. Under the assumption that the density is s s -smooth, we derive fast error rates for the kernel density estimator under trial heterogeneity that depend on the harmonic mean of the trials. Importantly, even when reduced to the homogeneous case, our result improves on the state-of-the-art rate of Ye and Bickel, 2021 . We also study nonparametric estimation of the difference between two densities, which can be smoother than the individual densities, in both i.i.d. and binomial-mixture settings. Our work is motivated by an application in criminal justice: comparing conviction rates of indigent representation in Pennsylvania. We find that the estimated conviction rates for appointed counsel (court-appointed private attorneys) are generally higher than those for public defenders, potentially due to a confounding factor: appointed counsel are more likely to take on severe cases.



Keywords:
Binomial mixtures, mixing distribution, nonparametric density estimation, density difference, criminal justice

Introduction

The problem of estimating the mixing distribution under binomial mixtures has received longstanding attention in the statistics literature
(
Teicher, 1963
;
Lord, 1967
;
Lord and Cressie, 1975
;
Sivaganesan and Berger, 1993
;
Wood, 1999
;
Tian et al., 2017
;
Vinayak et al., 2019a
;
Vinayak et al., 2019b
;
Ye and Bickel, 2021
)
.
In this problem, we observe
n
binomial random variables, each with potentially unique probabilities and number of trials. Early works primarily addressed identifiability
(
Teicher, 1963
;
Wood, 1999
)
;
recent studies focus on optimal estimation under different regimes
(
Tian et al., 2017
;
Vinayak et al., 2019b
;
Ye and Bickel, 2021
)
as well as testing
(
Kania et al., 2025
)
.

Surprisingly, most prior work has focused on the special case in which the number of trials is homogeneous, even though heterogeneous trials are ubiquitous. For instance, in our application to estimating the conviction rates by attorney type in Pennsylvania, the number of cases vary significantly across counties.
Kline and Walters, 2021
, which develops a model for detecting employer discrimination, is a notable exception.

Even in contexts where the trial parameters are naturally heterogeneous, existing approaches largely remain ad hoc (e.g., trimming at the minimum number of trials, as in
Tian et al., 2017
), leaving much of the additional information from the trials untapped. Trial heterogeneity arises more frequently in the Bayesian literature where trials are random and treated as parameters of interest themselves
(
Royle, 2004
;
Wenger and Freeman, 2008
;
Dail and Madsen, 2011
;
Wu et al., 2015
)
. By contrast, the binomial mixing distribution literature regards the trial parameter as fixed. We likewise condition on the realized
t_{i}
’s, imposing a strictly weaker assumption and gaining robustness to any mis-specification of the trial distribution.

Moreover, the problem has largely been studied under minimal structural assumptions, such as low smoothness
(
Ye and Bickel, 2021
)
, which leads to slow rates.
In contrast, more structural assumptions, such as higher-order smoothness, could yield significantly faster rates and thus be valuable in addressing these measurement error problems where known rates are generally slow
(
Carroll and Hall, 1988
;
Fan and Truong, 1993
)
.

In this paper, we thus study nonparametric estimation of the binomial mixing density under
trial heterogeneity
and
smoothness
.
We derive improved error rates using a kernel density estimator under
s
-Hölder smoothness, a standard structural assumption in the nonparametric literature
(
Tsybakov, 2008
)
.
Notably, when reduced to trial homogeneity, our result improves upon the rates from
Ye and Bickel, 2021
where the density is assumed only to be Lipschitz (e.g.,
1
-smooth).

Further, we analyze the
density difference
. This quantity is potentially of broader interest and has found widespread applications in areas including, but not limited to, change point detection
(
Kawahara and Sugiyama, 2012
)
and feature selection
(
Torkkola, 2003
)
. We study nonparametric estimation of the density difference when it is
\gamma
-smooth, where
\gamma\geq s
. We demonstrate that one can leverage standard nonparametric tools to optimally estimate this quantity with proper tuning.

We illustrate our method in an application in criminal justice.
Specifically, we compare the conviction rates of different indigent representations – appointed counsel (court-appointed private attorneys) and public defenders – by estimating their densities.
Our data include the number of convictions (“successes”) and total cases (“trials”) for each county in Pennsylvania, which differ significantly across counties and attorney types and thus motivates the study of our problem.
We find that the estimated conviction rates for appointed counsel are generally higher than those for public defenders, potentially due to a confounding factor: appointed counsel are more likely to take on severe cases.
This suggests a need for further investigation into how appointed counsel are systemically assigned to cases in Pennsylvania.

Setup & Notation

We assume independent data
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
where
X_{i}\mid Q_{i}=q\sim\textnormal{Bin}(t_{i},q)
,
Q_{i}
is drawn from the mixing density
p
supported on
[0,1]
, and

is fixed and known, for
i=1,\ldots,n
. Under the classical setting with homogeneous trials, we would replace
t_{i}
with
t
.

Our goal is to study the estimation of
p
at a point
u\in(0,1)
with absolute pointwise bias
|\mathbb{E}\{\hat{p}(u)\}-p(u)|
and variance
\textnormal{var}\{\hat{p}(u)\}
.
We consider the canonical
kernel density estimator
(KDE)

\hat{p}_{h}(u)\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{h}K\bigg(\frac{X_{i}/t_{i}-u}{h}\bigg),

(1)

constructed from empirical proportions
X_{i}/t_{i}
,
for a valid kernel
K
and some bandwidth

.
At times, we write

K_{h}(X_{i}/t_{i})\coloneqq K\{(X_{i}/t_{i}-u)/h\}/h
following the notational convention.

Our key assumption is that
p
is
s
-smooth, i.e.,

belongs to a Hölder class with index
s
. More precisely, let
\lfloor s\rfloor
denote the largest integer strictly smaller than
s
. Then the Hölder class indexed by

\lfloor s\rfloor

|\partial^{\alpha}f(u)|\leq L\text{ for all }\alpha\leq\lfloor s\rfloor,\quad\text{and}\quad|\partial^{\lfloor s\rfloor}f(u)-\partial^{\lfloor s\rfloor}f(u^{\prime})|\leq L|u-u^{\prime}|^{s-\lfloor s\rfloor},

for some constant

and for all
u,u^{\prime}\in[0,1]
. For simplicity, we omit the constant
L
.

We use
\|p\|_{\infty}\coloneqq\sup_{u}|p(u)|
for the sup norm of a real-valued function

. For two real sequences

and

, we write
a_{n}\lesssim b_{n}

a_{n}\leq Cb_{n}
and similarly
a_{n}\gtrsim b_{n}
if
a_{n}\geq Cb_{n}
for some

.

Main Theoretical Results

2.1
Error Bounds for Smooth Mixing Densities

Central to our analysis is bounding the bias that arises from using empirical proportions
X_{i}/t_{i}
instead of true proportions
Q_{i}
. To do so, one needs to consider an error of the form

\mathcal{E}(f_{i})\coloneqq\int\bigg\{\sum_{x=0}^{t_{i}}f(x/t_{i})\binom{t_{i}}{x}q^{x}(1-q)^{t_{i}-x}-f(q)\bigg\}p(q)dq,

(2)

i.e.,
\int\big[\mathbb{E}\{f(X_{i}/t_{i})\mid Q_{i}=q\}-f(q)\big]p(q)dq
for some function
f
. More precisely, (
2
) is the
p
-weighted integrated error of a Bernstein polynomial approximation of
f
of degree
t_{i}
.
1
1

1

The subscript
i
on
f
in the right side of (
2
) is meant to highlight the dependence on
i
.

Although we are primarily interested in the case where
f=K_{h}
, we first prove a powerful lemma that controls the average of the Bernstein error (
2
) for a
generic

f
.

Lemma 1

(Bound on Bernstein approximation error)
.

Assume that

1.

p
is
s
-smooth,

2.

\left\lVert p\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq p_{max}
, and

3.

|p(u)-p(u^{\prime})|\leq L|u-u^{\prime}|^{\alpha}
for some
0<\alpha\leq 1
and for all
u,u^{\prime}\in[0,1]
.

Then

\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{E}(f_{i})\right|\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{g(t_{i},\alpha)\sum_{x=0}^{t_{i}}\frac{|f(x/t_{i})|}{t_{i}+1}+\mathcal{R}(f_{i}p)\right\},

(3)

where

g(t_{i},\alpha)\coloneqq L\left(\frac{1/4}{t_{i}+3}\right)^{\alpha/2}+L\left(\frac{1}{t_{i}+2}\right)^{\alpha}+\frac{p_{\max}}{t_{i}},

and

\mathcal{R}(f_{i}p)\coloneqq\left|\sum_{x=0}^{t_{i}}\frac{f(x/t_{i})p(x/t_{i})}{t_{i}}-\int f(q)p(q)dq\right|,

(4)

is a quasi-Riemann sum approximation error to
\int f(q)p(q)dq
.
2
2

2

We use the term “quasi-Riemann” since the sum is a left/right sum with one extra term.

The key idea behind the proof of the above is to observe that

\int\sum_{x=0}^{t_{i}}f(x/t_{i})\binom{t_{i}}{x}q^{x}(1-q)^{t_{i}-x}p(q)dq=\sum_{x=0}^{t_{i}}f(x/t_{i})\frac{\mathbb{E}\{p(B_{xt_{i}})\}}{t_{i}+1},

where
B_{xt_{i}}
is a Beta random variable with parameters
x+1
and
t_{i}-x+1
, and then decompose the Bernstein approximation error (
2
) as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(f_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{x=0}^{t_{i}}f(x/t_{i})\frac{\mathbb{E}\{p(B_{xt_{i}})\}-p\{\mathbb{E}(B_{xt_{i}})\}}{t_{i}+1}+\sum_{x=0}^{t_{i}}f(x/t_{i})\left\{\frac{p\{\mathbb{E}(B_{xt_{i}})\}}{t_{i}+1}-\frac{p(x/t_{i})}{t_{i}}\right\}

\displaystyle+\left\{\sum_{x=0}^{t_{i}}f(x/t_{i})\frac{p(x/t_{i})}{t_{i}}-\int f(q)p(q)dq\right\},

so that the last term corresponds to the quasi-Riemann sum approximation error.

Notably, the error bound (
3
) holds for any
f
and simply relies on its discrete
L_{1}
norm and the quasi-Riemann sum approximation error (
4
). On the other hand, classical Bernstein bounds
(
Kac, 1938
;
Lorentz, 1986
;
Lorentz, 2012
;
Bojanic and Cheng, 1989
;
Mathé, 1999
)
require smoothness of
f
and its derivatives. With
f=K_{h}
, such bounds result in undesirable
1/h
dependencies that lead to loose error bounds. Importantly, even under trial homogeneity with
f=K_{h}
, one can show that our Bernstein bound (
3
) reduces to
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{t}+1/ht)
, which improves upon the bound
\mathcal{O}(h+1/\sqrt{t}+1/ht)
in Theorem 8 of
Ye and Bickel, 2021
obtained using a histogram estimator.

Next, we present our first main theorem that characterizes upper bounds for pointwise bias and variance of the KDE (
1
) under trial heterogeneity and higher-order smoothness. The bias bound, in particular, consists of (1) the usual smoothing bias of order
h^{s}
and (2) additional terms stemming from the average Bernstein error bound (
3
) by setting
f=K_{h}
. Interestingly, the bounds depend on
harmonic mean
of the trial parameters.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\widetilde{t}\coloneqq n/\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}^{-1}
denote the harmonic mean of the trial parameters
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}
. Assume the conditions of Lemma
1

and additionally

1.

|K(v)|\leq K_{max}\mathbbm{1}(|v|\leq 1)
, and

2.

|K(v)-K(v^{\prime})|\leq M|v-v^{\prime}|^{\beta}
for some
0<\beta\leq 1
and for all
v,v^{\prime}\in[-1,1]
.

3.

\int K(u)du=1
,

4.

\int u^{j}K(u)du=0
for
j=1,\ldots,\lfloor s\rfloor
, and

5.

\int|u|^{s}|K(u)|du\leq B<\infty
.

Then, for
u\in(0,1)
, the pointwise bias of the kernel density estimator constructed from empirical proportions (
1
) is bounded as

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}\{\hat{p}_{h}(u)\}-p(u)|\leq\frac{LB}{\lfloor s\rfloor!}h^{s}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{g(t_{i},\alpha)\sum_{x=0}^{t_{i}}\frac{|K_{h}(x/t_{i})|}{t_{i}+1}+r(h,t_{i},\alpha,\beta)\right\},

where
r(h,t_{i},\alpha,\beta)
is an upper bound on the quasi-Riemann sum approximation error (
4
) defined as

r(h,t_{i},\alpha,\beta)\coloneqq\frac{K_{\max}p_{\max}}{ht_{i}}+\left(2+\frac{1}{ht_{i}}\right)\left\{LK_{\max}\left(\frac{1}{t_{i}}\right)^{\alpha}+2Mp_{\max}\left(\frac{1}{ht_{i}}\right)^{\beta}\right\}.

Furthermore, the pointwise variance is bounded as

\textnormal{var}\{\hat{p}_{h}(u)\}\leq\frac{K^{2}_{\max}p_{\max}}{nh}\bigg(2+\frac{1}{h\widetilde{t}}\bigg).

The assumptions we make in Theorem
1
are standard in the kernel density estimation literature. The first states that the kernel is bounded and supported on
[-1,1]
, and the second that the kernel is Hölder continuous with exponent
\beta
on the support. The rest of the assumptions define what is called a
higher-order kernel
to achieve the smoothing bias of order
h^{s}
. See
Tsybakov, 2008
for how to construct bounded kernels of arbitrary order using Legendre polynomials.

The following corollary gives a simplified result to ease interpretability.

Corollary 1
.

Assume the conditions of Theorem
1
and additionally

1.

p
and
K
are Lipschitz, and

2.

C/\widetilde{t}\leq h\leq C^{\prime}
for some
C,C^{\prime}>0
.

Then the bounds in Theorem
1
simplify to

|\mathbb{E}\{\hat{p}_{h}(u)\}-p(u)|\lesssim h^{s}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\widetilde{t}}}+\frac{1}{h\widetilde{t}}\quad\text{and}\quad\textnormal{var}\{\hat{p}_{h}(u)\}\lesssim\frac{1}{nh}.

The second condition constrains
h
to be in the “right window” and caps
1/h\widetilde{t}
in both the bias and variance terms by a fixed constant.

Corollary
1
encapsulates our key result: Under trial heterogeneity and
s
-smoothness, the pointwise bias and variance of the KDE (
1
) are of order
h^{s}+1/\sqrt{\widetilde{t}}+1/h\widetilde{t}
and
1/nh
, respectively. When reduced to homogeneity, the bias bound is retained by replacing the harmonic mean
\widetilde{t}
with some homogeneous trial parameter
t
. As such, this result directly improves the state-of-the-art bias of order
h+1/\sqrt{t}+1/ht
for a histogram estimator when the mixing distribution is assumed to be
1
-smooth
(
Ye and Bickel, 2021
, Theorem 8)
.

This contradicts the previous conjecture
(
Ye and Bickel, 2021
, pg. 9)
that a better rate than theirs would not be attainable even with a higher-order smoothness, which stems from their error analysis yielding the bound
\mathcal{O}(h+1/\sqrt{t}+1/ht)
that dominates the smoothing bias
\mathcal{O}(h^{s})
.
Our improvement is attributed to our novel Bernstein approximation error bound (
3
) in Lemma
1
that, with
f=K_{h}
, yields
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{t}+1/ht)
instead. See Appendix
A.3
for details.

Conditions for Oracle Minimax Estimation.

We now discuss conditions on the harmonic mean
\widetilde{t}
required for
oracle minimax estimation
, which we define as follows. In the canonical setting where we observe the i.i.d. true proportions, the minimax rate for estimating
p
, a 1-dimensional
s
-smooth density, is
n^{-1/(2+1/s)}

(
Van Der Vaart, 1998
;
Tsybakov, 2008
)
. We call this the
oracle
s
-smooth minimax density estimation rate
, or just the
oracle rate
for short. To the best of our knowledge, the minimax rates for the binomial mixing density estimation problem under smoothness and trial heterogeneity are unknown.
Tian et al., 2017
and
Vinayak et al., 2019b
derive minimax rates in the Wasserstein-1 metric under trial homogeneity.

To achieve this rate, we need the bias induced by empirical proportions to be dominated by the variance so that we can balance the usual smoothing bias and variance, i.e.,

1/\sqrt{nh}\gtrsim\max\{1/\sqrt{\widetilde{t}},/h\widetilde{t}\}
.
If
h\sim n^{-1/(2s+1)}
by the usual balancing so that the oracle rate can be achieved, this condition translates into

\widetilde{t}\gtrsim\begin{cases}n^{\frac{1+1/s}{2+1/s}},&s\leq 1,\\
n^{\frac{2}{2+1/s}},&s>1.\end{cases}

(5)

When
s=1
, this matches
t\gtrsim n^{2/3}
from
Ye and Bickel, 2021
. As
s\to\infty
, we face a steeper condition of
t\gtrsim n
, which stems from
1/\sqrt{nh}\gtrsim 1/\sqrt{t}
being the dominant requirement when
s>1
and, in turn,
t
having to grow to match the decreasing variance. However, once this is achieved, we can benefit from strictly faster rates from the smoothing bias
h^{s}
. In other words, exploiting
s
-smoothness of
p
using the KDE (
1
) does not lower the bar on the size of
t
, but significantly raises the payoff.

2.2
Tuning Parameter Selection

We now discuss tuning parameter selection for nonparametric density estimators constructed from empirical proportions using
Lepski’s method

(
Lepskii, 1991
)
. We consider a variant of the original approach proposed by
Spokoiny and Willrich, 2019
, which is succinctly described in
Chetverikov, 2024
. The notation and discussion of the method closely follow
Chetverikov, 2024
, but in the context of kernel density estimation, which is the main focus of this article.

The core idea of the method is simple: Start with a large value of
h
, and gradually reduce it until further reduction of
h
no longer significantly decreases the bias of
\hat{p}_{h}(u)
, where the “significance” is assessed in terms of variance. Formally, define

b_{h,h^{\prime}}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\{\hat{p}_{h^{\prime}}(u)-\hat{p}_{h}(u)\},\quad p_{h,h^{\prime}}\coloneqq\textnormal{var}\{\hat{p}_{h^{\prime}}(u)-\hat{p}_{h}(u)\},

for all
h,h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{H}^{-}(h)=\{h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}:h^{\prime}<h\}
. The goal is to test the null hypothesis

H_{h}:|b_{h,h^{\prime}}|^{2}\leq p_{h,h^{\prime}}\quad\text{for all }h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}^{-}(h),

for each
h\in\mathcal{H}
. Intuitively, if
H_{h}
is not rejected, there is no evidence that a smaller bandwidth than
h
would significantly reduce the bias. Now, a size
\alpha
test of
H_{h}
rejects if

\max_{h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}^{-}(h)}\frac{|\hat{p}_{h^{\prime}}(u)-\hat{p}_{h}(u)|}{\sqrt{p_{h,h^{\prime}}}}>1+c_{h}(\alpha)

where
c_{h}(\alpha)
is the
(1-\alpha)
quantile of

\max_{h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}^{-}(h)}\frac{|\hat{p}_{h^{\prime}}(u)-\mathbb{E}\{\hat{p}_{h^{\prime}}(u)\}-\hat{p}_{h}(u)+\mathbb{E}\{\hat{p}_{h^{\prime}}(u)\}|}{\sqrt{p_{h,h^{\prime}}}}.

To implement the method in practice, one can replace
p_{h,h^{\prime}}
with

\hat{p}_{h,h^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigg[\big\{K_{h^{\prime}}(X_{i}/t_{i})-K_{h}(X_{i}/t_{i})\big\}-\big\{\hat{p}_{h^{\prime}}(u)-\hat{p}_{h}(u)\big\}\bigg]^{2},

and estimate
c_{h}(\alpha)
using, e.g., multiplier bootstrap. For details, we point the reader to
Chetverikov, 2024
and
Spokoiny and Willrich, 2019
.

Denote
\hat{h}
as the tuning parameter selected by the procedure and
h^{*}
as the smallest value for which
H_{h}
holds, i.e., the
oracle
tuning parameter.
It follows that

|\hat{p}_{\hat{h}}(u)-\hat{p}_{h*}(u)|\leq\max_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{IN}}\sqrt{p_{h,h^{*}}}\{1+c_{h}(\alpha)\},

(6)

with probability at least
1-2\alpha
, where
\mathcal{H}_{IN}=\{h\in\mathcal{H}:h\geq h^{*}\}\backslash\mathcal{H}^{0}
and
\mathcal{H}^{0}=\{h\in\mathcal{H}^{+}(h^{*}):|b_{h,h^{*}}|^{2}>p_{h,h^{*}}\{c_{h}(\alpha)+c^{-}_{h^{*}}(\alpha)\}^{2}\}
.

The quantity on the right side of (
6
) is the
adaptation price
of the method, determined by (1)
c_{h}(\alpha)
of order
\sqrt{\log|\mathcal{H}|}\leq\sqrt{\log n}
, (2)
p_{h,h^{*}}
, and (3)
|\mathcal{H}_{IN}|
, the quantity referred to as the insensitivity region (IR)
(
Spokoiny and Willrich, 2019
)
. In our setting,
p_{h,h^{*}}
would be of order
(1/h^{*}-1/h)/n
under the conditions of Corollary
1
. It thus follows that

\max_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{IN}}\sqrt{p_{h,h^{*}}}\{1+c_{h}(\alpha)\}\lesssim\begin{cases}\sqrt{\log n/nh^{*}},&\text{large IR},\\
\sqrt{\log n/n},&\text{small IR},\end{cases}

which implies that, crucially, the overall adaptation price under empirical proportions can still be of
identical
order to that under true proportions.

Now, what remains is the comparison of the adaptation price with the oracle estimation error of order
h^{*s}+1/\sqrt{nh^{*}}+1/\sqrt{\widetilde{t}}+1/\widetilde{t}h^{*}
. If we let
1/\sqrt{nh^{*}}\gtrsim\max\{1/\sqrt{\widetilde{t}},1/\widetilde{t}h^{*}\}

so that the usual i.i.d. oracle error dominates, then we recover the typical story of Lepski’s method: The adaptation price is larger than the oracle error under a large IR and smaller under a small IR. In particular, if
h^{*}\sim n^{-1/(2s+1)}
, then we have

\widetilde{t}\gtrsim\begin{cases}n^{\frac{1+1/s}{2+1/s}},&s\leq 1,\\
n^{\frac{2}{2+1/s}},&s>1,\end{cases}

which is the same condition as in the oracle minimax density estimation (
5
). If
h^{*}
decreases at a slower rate, however, then we would be faced with a higher requirement.

Overall, the condition on
\widetilde{t}
to recover the “usual i.i.d. story” of Lepski’s method, in the best case scenario where the oracle bandwidth decreases at an optimal rate, can match that of minimax oracle density estimation.

Why not cross validation?

An alternative for tuning parameter selection is the omnipresent cross-validation with the pseudo-risk

\int\hat{p}_{h}(u)^{2}du-\frac{2}{n}\sum_{j\in D_{2}^{n}}\hat{p}_{h}(X_{j}/t_{j}),

and its minimizer
\hat{h}
. We do not consider this approach for two reasons. First, analyzing the adaptation price of cross-validation under empirical proportions can be nontrivial due to the random nature of
\hat{h}
. More importantly, the bar on
\widetilde{t}
for the usual i.i.d. cross-validation story (for details, see Chapter 8 of
Györfi et al., 2002
) to hold may be much higher than it is for Lepski’s method.

In particular, based on our theoretical results in Section
2.1
, we roughly expect the following for the KDE (
1
). Even in the “best case” scenario where both the data-driven and the oracle tuning parameters decrease at the optimal rate of
n^{-1/(2s+1)}
, the condition on
\widetilde{t}
might be

\widetilde{t}\gtrsim\begin{cases}n,&s\leq 1/2,\\
n^{4/(2+1/s)},&s>1/2,\end{cases}

which is already a more stringent condition than the one for minimax oracle density estimation (
5
), or even worse,

\widetilde{t}\gtrsim\begin{cases}n^{(1+1/s)/(2+1/s)},&s\leq 1/3,\\
n^{4/(2+1/s)},&1/3<s\leq 1/2.\end{cases}

That is, recovering the usual story is only feasible in a low-smoothness regime. We thus leave cross-validation under empirical proportions for future work.

2.3
Inference

One can adopt
undersmoothing

(
Hall, 1992
;
Chen and Qin, 2002
;
Chen, 2017
)
to construct an asymptotically valid
1-\alpha
confidence interval of the form

\hat{p}_{h}(u)\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\sqrt{\widehat{\textnormal{var}}\{\hat{p}_{h}(u)\}},\quad\widehat{\textnormal{var}}\{\hat{p}_{h}(u)\}=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\{K_{h}(X_{i}/t_{i})-\hat{p}_{h}(u)\}^{2}.

The following proposition establishes the asymptotic normality of the KDE (
1
) as well as the conditions for undersmoothing.

Proposition 1

(Inference via undersmoothing)
.

Assume the conditions of Theorem
1
and additionally

1.

h\to 0
and
nh\to\infty
,

2.

C/t_{i}\leq h\leq C^{\prime}
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
for some
C,C^{\prime}>0
,

3.

p
and
K
are Lipschitz, and

4.

nh^{s+1}\to\infty
,
\widetilde{t}\to\infty
,
(nh)^{-1}\widetilde{t}\to\infty
, and
(n/h)^{-1/2}\widetilde{t}\to\infty
.

It follows that

\sqrt{nh}\{\hat{p}_{h}(u)-p(u)\}\overset{d}{\to}N(0,\sigma^{2}),\quad\sigma^{2}=\lim_{n\to\infty}nh\textnormal{var}\{\hat{p}_{h}(u)\}.

Furthermore,
\hat{p}_{h}(u)\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\sqrt{\widehat{\textnormal{var}}\{\hat{p}_{h}(u)\}}
gives an asymptotically valid
1-\alpha
confidence interval for
p(u)
.

Condition
4
describes the rate assumptions necessary for undersmoothing.
If
h\sim n^{-\zeta}
and
\widetilde{t}\sim n^{\gamma}
for some
\zeta,\gamma>0
, we need

\max\bigg(\frac{1}{2s+1},1-\gamma\bigg)<\zeta<2\gamma-1,

where
\gamma>1/2
(otherwise
\zeta<0
). The first inequality arises from the intersection of the first and second rate conditions, and the upper bound arises from the third. Roughly speaking, the choice of the bandwidth should be smaller than the oracle choice
h\sim n^{-1/(2s+1)}
, but not too small with respect to how fast the harmonic mean
\widetilde{t}
grows.

3
Smooth Density Difference

Comparisons between two groups lie at the heart of statistics (e.g., two-sample testing) and science. In particular, we are often interested in the difference between two quantities, such as the difference in outcomes between the treated and control groups. To this end, we study nonparametric estimation of the
density difference
for both true proportions
Q_{i}
and empirical proportions
X_{i}/t_{i}
, assuming that each density is
s
-smooth. We first analyze the i.i.d. setting to show that the difference can be estimated optimally with standard non‑parametric techniques via proper tuning, and then turn to the binomial‑mixture setting, which is our main focus. The problem is analogous to the estimation of conditional average treatment effects in causal inference
(
Kennedy, 2023
;
Kennedy et al., 2024
)
, where one likewise seeks the difference in two regression functions.

True Proportions.

First, suppose that we have access to true proportions
Q_{i}
and observe i.i.d. data
(Q_{1},A_{1}),\ldots,(Q_{n},A_{n})
where
A_{i}
is a binary group indicator. The
density difference
is defined as

\tau(u)\coloneqq p_{1}(u)-p_{0}(u),

(7)

where
p_{a}(u)=p(u|A=a)
for
a=0,1
and
\int\tau(u)du=0
. Importantly,
\tau(u)
and
p_{a}(u)
are likely to have different complexities;
\tau
has to be at least as smooth as
p_{a}(u)
and is potentially much more structured.
Consider the following illustrative example adapted from
Kennedy, 2023
where the conditional densities are equal and given by

p_{a}(u)=\frac{1}{C}\begin{cases}2u^{2}+2u,&0\leq u\leq 0.25,\\
u,&0.25<u\leq 0.5,\\
-u^{2}+2u+0.2,&0.5<u\leq 0.75,\\
1.5u-1,&0.75<u\leq 1,\end{cases}

(8)

with
C\approx 0.508
denoting the normalizing constant. Clearly, each
p_{a}(u)
is non-smooth, but the difference
\tau(u)=0
is
\infty
-smooth (Figure
1
).

Figure 1
:
Plot of an illustrative example where the conditional densities individually are of lower smoothness (left), but their difference is of higher smoothness (right).

An intuitive approach to estimate the density difference (
7
) is to estimate each density separately and take the difference, e.g.,

\tilde{\tau}(u)\coloneqq\tilde{p}_{1}(u)-\tilde{p}_{0}(u)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{A_{i}}{\overline{A}}-\frac{1-A_{i}}{1-\overline{A}}\right)K_{h}(Q_{i}),

(9)

where
\overline{A}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}/n
. The following proposition shows that the kernel density difference estimator (
9
) achieves the optimal smoothing bias of order
h^{\gamma}
using an appropriate higher-order kernel.

Proposition 2
.

Assume that

1.

p_{a}
is
s
-smooth for
a=0,1
,

2.

(a)

\tau
is
\gamma
-smooth,

(b)

\left\lVert\tau\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\tau_{max}
,

(c)

|\tau(u)-\tau(u^{\prime})|\leq L_{\tau}|u-u^{\prime}|^{\alpha}
for some
0<\alpha<1
and for all
u,u^{\prime}\in[-1,1]
,

3.

\epsilon\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}/n\leq 1-\epsilon
for some
\epsilon>0
with probability 1,

4.

(a)

\int K(u)du=1
,

(b)

\int u^{j}K(u)du=0
for
j=1,\ldots,\lfloor\gamma\rfloor
,

(c)

\int|u|^{\gamma}|K(u)|du\leq B<\infty
, and

(d)

\int K(u)^{2}du\leq B^{\prime}<\infty
.

Then for
u\in(0,1)
, the pointwise bias and variance of the kernel density difference estimator (
9
) are bounded as

|\mathbb{E}\{\tilde{\tau}(u)\}-\tau(u)|\leq\frac{LB}{\lfloor\gamma\rfloor!}h^{\gamma}\quad\text{and}\quad\textnormal{var}\{\tilde{\tau}(u)\}\leq\frac{1}{nh}\bigg(\frac{p_{\max}B^{\prime}}{\epsilon^{2}}\bigg).

Similar results hold for other nonparametric methods. In the context of the orthogonal series estimator

b(u)^{\top}\int b(u)\tau(u)du\eqqcolon b(u)^{\top}\theta,

where
b(u)=\{b_{1}(u),\ldots,b_{k}(u)\}^{\top}
denotes an orthonormal basis of dimension
k
,
\theta
can be estimated unbiasedly with

\hat{\theta}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{A_{i}}{\overline{A}}-\frac{1-A_{i}}{1-\overline{A}}\right)b(Q_{i}).

Therefore, we would again obtain the optimal bias order
k^{-\gamma}
and the variance of order
k/n
under standard assumptions.

Tuning parameter selection.

In practice, performance depends critically on careful tuning. Tuning each density separately is sub-optimal, as will be shown in Section
4.2
. For simplicity, we consider cross-validation with two folds:
D_{1}^{n}
to estimate the density difference and
D_{2}^{n}
to choose the tuning parameter. For optimal performance, one should minimize the “joint” pseudo-risk
(
Wasserman, 2006
;
Györfi et al., 2002
)

\int\tilde{\tau}_{h}(u)^{2}du-\frac{2}{n}\sum_{j\in D_{2}^{n}}\left(\frac{A_{j}}{\overline{A}}-\frac{1-A_{j}}{1-\overline{A}}\right)\tilde{\tau}_{h}(Q_{j}),

(10)

where the average
\overline{A}
is taken over
D_{2}^{n}
. The joint pseudo‑risk arises from decomposing the integrated squared error of the kernel density difference estimator

\int\tilde{\tau}(u)^{2}du-2\int\tilde{\tau}(u)\tau(u)du+\int\tau(u)^{2}du,

and estimating the cross term
\int\tilde{\tau}(u)\tau(u)du
by averaging
\tilde{\tau}(Q_{j})
over the second fold and weighting by
A_{j}/\overline{A}-(1-A_{j})/(1-\overline{A})
, thereby turning the mixture average into the difference of group means. Hence, above pseudo-risk can be estimated unbiasedly and standard
L_{2}
cross-validation theory (e.g., Theorem 8.1 of
Györfi et al., 2002
) applies directly, subsequently yielding minimax optimal estimators. Thus, we would obtain optimal rates without
a priori
knowledge of the smoothness so long as the set of tuning parameters is sufficiently large. For example, in the context of kernel density estimators, the set of tuning parameters can consist of pairs
(h,\gamma)
of bandwidths
h
and kernel orders
\gamma
.

We emphasize that this idea of joint, or “single-shot”, tuning is well established in the literature
(
Hall and Wand, 1988
;
Sugiyama et al., 2013
;
Nguyen et al., 2014
)
. Our contribution is to clarify that optimal estimation of the density difference in both theory and practice using standard nonparametric methods is
feasible
, contrary to the conjecture that it may be difficult with
\gamma
being unknown
(
Sugiyama et al., 2013
, pg. 9)
.
3
3

3

Sugiyama et al., 2013
further discourages the use of higher-order kernels as they can be nonnegative, but negative density estimates can always be replaced with
0
and the risk will never become worse.

See Section
4.2
for a simulation study that demonstrates the performance of the estimator (
9
) tuned with the joint pseudo-risk (

).

Empirical proportions.

We now return to our main setting of interest where we observe independent data
(X_{1},t_{1},A_{1}),\ldots,(X_{n},t_{n},A_{n})
.
Consider

\hat{\tau}(u)\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{A_{i}}{\overline{A}}-\frac{1-A_{i}}{1-\overline{A}}\right)K_{h}(X_{i}/t_{i}),

(11)

i.e., the empirical proportions version of the kernel density difference estimator (
9

Corollary 2
.

Assume that

1.

p_{a}
is
s
-smooth for

2.

(a)

is
\gamma
-smooth,

(b)

\left\lVert\tau\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\tau_{max}
,

(c)

|\tau(u)-\tau(u^{\prime})|\leq L_{\tau}|u-u^{\prime}|^{\alpha}
for some

and for all
u,u^{\prime}\in[-1,1]

3.

\epsilon\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}/n\leq 1-\epsilon
for some
\epsilon>0
with probability 1,

4.

(a)

(b)

for some

(c)

\int K(u)du=1

where

g_{\tau}(t_{i},\alpha)\coloneqq L_{\tau}\left(\frac{1/4}{t_{i}+3}\right)^{\alpha/2}+L_{\tau}\left(\frac{1}{t_{i}+2}\right)^{\alpha}+\frac{\tau_{\max}}{t_{i}},

and

r_{\tau}(h,t_{i},\alpha,\beta)\coloneqq\frac{K_{\max}\tau_{\max}}{ht_{i}}+\left(2+\frac{1}{ht_{i}}\right)\left\{L_{\tau}K_{\max}\left(\frac{1}{t_{i}}\right)^{\alpha}+2M\tau_{\max}\left(\frac{1}{ht_{i}}\right)^{\beta}\right\}.

\textnormal{var}\{\hat{\tau}(u)\}\leq\frac{K^{2}_{\max}\tau_{\max}}{nh\epsilon^{2}}\bigg(2+\frac{1}{h\widetilde{t}}\bigg).

Interestingly, we obtain a bias bound almost identical to that of Theorem
1
, with the key difference that
\gamma
now replaces
s
. This is because the bounding of the additional bias from empirical proportions here concerns the
\tau
-weighted integrated Bernstein approximation error, for which we can directly leverage Theorem
1
and Proposition
2

1

|\mathbb{E}\{\hat{\tau}(u)\}-\tau(u)|\lesssim h^{\gamma}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\widetilde{t}}}+\frac{1}{h\widetilde{t}}\quad\text{and}\quad\textnormal{var}\{\hat{\tau}(u)\}\lesssim\frac{1}{nh},

which implies that the condition on
\widetilde{t}
to achieve the oracle rate of
n^{-1/(2+1/\gamma)}
is

\widetilde{t}\geq\begin{cases}n^{(1+1/\gamma)/(2+1/\gamma)},&\gamma<1,\\
n^{2/(2+1/\gamma)},&\gamma\geq 1.\end{cases}

For tuning parameter selection under empirical proportions, we can again exploit Lepski’s method, and analogous guarantees will hold.

5
Application: Conviction Rates by Attorney Type

Granting equal access to competent legal representation, as guaranteed by the Sixth Amendment, is central to achieving criminal justice. However, the American system for indigent representation often falls short, even though up to 80% of criminal defendants are assigned a public defender or a court-appointed private attorney, also known as an
appointed counsel
, because they cannot afford an attorney themselves
(
Pollitz Worden and Davies, 2014
)
. The case of Surrency
(
Bach, 2009
)
, an appointed counsel in Georgia known for his “efficient” approach of negotiating plea deals, well illustrates the problem – many of his clients, as a result of his tactic, plead guilty without realizing they could go to jail. In contrast, salaried public defenders are often better motivated and resourced, although they too must contend with excessively high caseloads
(
Baćak et al., 2024
;
Baćak et al., 2020
;
Welch, 2019
)
. Yet, defendants rarely know which system will handle their case, let alone have any choice in that matter.

Appointed counsel are typically assigned on an ad hoc, case-by-case basis, and usually paid under flat fee contracts
(
Spangenberg and Beeman, 1995
;
Neubauer and Fradella, 2015
)
. Accordingly, they come from diverse legal backgrounds and have an incentive to dispose of cases quickly, unlike salaried public defenders. As a result, assigned counsel systems “have been criticized for appointing attorneys with inadequate skills, experience, and qualifications to represent indigent defendants.”
(
Cohen, 2014
)
. Hence, a natural question that arises is:
Are appointed counsel associated with worse case outcomes than public defenders?

Relevant empirical literature
(
Anderson and Heaton, 2011
;
Cohen, 2014
;
Roach, 2014
)
suggest that appointed counsel are indeed associated with less favorable case outcomes, but their evidence is based on limited data. Many studies
(
Cohen, 2014
;
Roach, 2014
)
base their analyses on the State Court Processing Statistics (SCPS) from the Bureau of Justice Statistics, which only covers felony cases in 75 most populous counties in the U.S. Others not relying on SCPS
(
Anderson and Heaton, 2011
)
still primarily analyze data from large counties.

We therefore leverage novel data from the Measures for Justice (MFJ), a nonprofit whose mission is to tackle this “criminal justice data crisis”. Our analysis centers on Pennsylvania, where court-related decisions around indigent defense are decentralized and administered by the counties, unlike states that have a state-wide public defender office. Variation across counties is thus significant and meaningful, making counties as the most appropriate units of analysis. Whereas SCPS only covers Allegheny, Philadelphia, and Montgomery, MFJ provides data for all 67 counties and includes cases beyond felonies.

Specifically, MFJ supplies county-level counts of convictions and total cases by attorney type during 2009–2013. To address our question, we estimate the densities of conviction rates by attorney type, which is a binomial mixing density estimation problem where a conviction is treated as a “success”. The extreme variation in case counts by county and attorney type – ranging from 1 to 24,740 for appointed counsel, 154 to 117,282 for public defenders, and 54 to 39,295 for private attorneys – motivates studying the binomial mixing density estimation problem under trial heterogeneity.

. The vertical dashed lines mark the means. Estimated conviction rates for appointed counsel are generally highest (

). At first glance, the pattern reported in earlier SCPS-based studies appears to extend beyond the largest counties and felony cases.

Potential Confounding.

Are appointed counsel actually less effective at getting clients acquitted than public defenders? We note a potential source of
confounding
, which is that appointed counsel more often represent defendants facing more severe charges. Figure

ROR refers to cases in which the defendant is not required to post any cash bail, but must promise to attend all future court proceedings; it is therefore the least restrictive type of bail. While various factors determine the type of bail set, such as the defendant’s financial status and criminal record, the seriousness of the alleged offense is typically the most important deciding factor. Therefore, cases with ROR likely involve less severe cases than those requiring cash bail.

We observe that appointed counsel are more frequently involved in cases with cash bail for both misdemeanors and felonies, which could have contributed to the observed discrepancies in conviction rates. Therefore, counties should review and
standardize
the largely ad-hoc process of assigning appointed counsel, ensuring more serious cases are not systematically routed to them.

Future research in this direction should also examine several additional possibilities. First, appointed counsel may take on too many cases in response to the fee-based system, thus unable to devote adequate time and resources to cases. Second, they may also represent cases with higher cash bail if they see the client for the first time after the bail hearing has already been completed, or if “exogenous fluctuations in the local labor market induce relatively more low-quality attorneys to choose assigned counsel work”
(

)
. Finally, appointed counsel may prioritize non-indigent clients or lack administrative support to ensure they meet clients early in the case to represent them effectively
(

)
.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we have studied nonparametric estimation of
s
-smooth binomial mixing densities under trial heterogeneity. We have shown that the pointwise bias of the KDE is
\mathcal{O}(h^{s}+1/\sqrt{\widetilde{t}}+1/h\widetilde{t})
, where
\widetilde{t}
denotes the harmonic mean of the trial parameters. When reduced to the homogeneous case, this is faster than the rate in
Ye and Bickel, 2021
. Additionally, we have studied the difference between two
s
-smooth densities and have shown that one can exploit usual nonparametric density estimators and still achieve optimal performance. Density difference may also prove useful in contexts beyond those studied to date, e.g., in answering complex causal questions if comparing the difference in counterfactual densities
(
Kennedy et al., 2023
)
.

Empirically, we have examined the conviction rates by attorney type in Pennsylvania using novel criminal justice data with broader coverage. In particular, we note that the estimates are generally higher for appointed counsel than for public defenders, potentially because appointed counsel are taking on more severe cases at higher rates. As aggregate data are common in criminal justice, the KDE (
1
) can be usefully applied in other contexts, such as comparing private and public prisons
(
Baćak and Ridgeway, 2018
)
.

There are many intriguing directions for future research. The first is to investigate whether we can obtain better error rates by conducting a sharper analysis or using other estimators. In particular, we believe that the error rate of
h^{s}+1/\widetilde{t}+1/h\widetilde{t}
may be achievable. Under this rate, the requirement on
\widetilde{t}
for oracle minimax estimation becomes
\widetilde{t}\gtrsim n^{(1+1/s)/(2+1/s)}
for any
s>0
and
\widetilde{t}\geq n^{1/2}
as
s\to\infty
, which improves on
\widetilde{t}\geq n^{2/3}
.

The second question is uncertainty quantification. In our setting, a more informative interval than the one based in undersmoothing would explicitly reflect the number of trials. Intuitively, the interval should contract when the number of trials is large and expand when it is small. One option is a plug-in interval that uses the pointwise bias bound from Theorem
1
. However, the constants regarding
p
are difficult to estimate in practice.

Last but not least, one can incorporate covariates. For example, adopting the setup of
Ignatiadis and Wager, 2019
, we can posit the model

X_{i}\mid Q_{i}=q\sim\textnormal{Bin}(t_{i},q),\quad Q_{i}\mid C_{i}=c\sim\mathcal{N}(m(c),\sigma^{2}),

where
C_{i}
denotes the covariate vector for unit
i
. A natural question is whether we can estimate
Q_{i}
more accurately under assumptions on
m(\cdot)
.

The problem of binomial mixing density estimation clearly presents many captivating challenges.

Lemma 2

(A sufficient condition for Lindenberg’s condition)
.

Suppose
\{X_{ni},n\geq 1,1\leq i\leq i_{n}\}
is a triangular array such that, for each
n

X_{n1},\ldots,X_{ni_{n}}
are independent. Let
B_{n}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{i_{n}}\textnormal{var}(X_{ni})
. Assume there exists
\{L_{n},n\geq 1\}
satisfying

\max_{1\leq i\leq i_{n}}|X_{ni}|\leq L_{n},\quad\frac{L_{n}}{B_{n}}\to 0.

Then, Lindenberg’s condition holds, i.e., for any
\epsilon>0

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{B_{n}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{i_{n}}\mathbb{E}\big[\{X_{ni}-\mathbb{E}(X_{ni})\}^{2}\mathbbm{1}\{|X_{ni}-\mathbb{E}(X_{ni})|\geq\epsilon B_{n}\}\big]=0.

As a result,

\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{i_{n}}\{X_{ni}-\mathbb{E}(X_{ni})\}}{B_{n}}\overset{d}{\to}N(0,1).

We verify the sufficient condition for Lindenberg’s condition with
i_{n}=n
and

X_{ni}=\frac{1}{h}K\bigg(\frac{X_{i}/t_{i}-u}{h}\bigg).

We also know that

\textnormal{var}(X_{ni})\leq\frac{K_{\max}^{2}p_{\max}}{h^{2}t_{i}}(2ht_{i}+1)\lesssim\frac{1}{h},

where the first inequality holds under our pervious derivations and the second from condition
2
.
It follows that

\frac{L_{n}}{B_{n}}\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{nh}}\to 0,

and hence Lindenberg’s condition applies, implying the asymptotic normality of the first term.

C/\widetilde{t}\leq h\leq C^{\prime}

gives rise to the undersmoothing conditions.
