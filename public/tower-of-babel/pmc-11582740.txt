Impact of the Marangoni phenomenon on the different Enhanced Oil Recovery methods
Authors: Arash Ebrahimi, Yousef Kazemzadeh, Ali Akbari
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The occurrence of the Marangoni phenomenon is due to the surface tension gradient within a system, resulting in the creation of a convective flow from low surface tension to high surface tension points. This phenomenon arises from changes in solvent concentration, temperature and electric charge. In the context of the enhanced oil recovery (EOR) process, changing solvent concentration at the interface of two fluids in matrix fracturing systems caused by gas injection (in immiscible, immiscible and near-miscible conditions) and surfactant flooding, can enhance oil recovery by increasing the Marangoni effect. This article presents a comprehensive overview of the Marangoni phenomenon in oil recovery and the first investigation of this effect in conjunction with other related phenomena. The findings demonstrate that the Marangoni flow is not only present but also significantly impacts bypass oil recovery. The magnitude of the Marangoni effect is directly proportional to the absolute value of the temperature and concentration gradient at the interface. Despite being less recognized in petroleum engineering, this phenomenon has the potential to recover bypassed oil in both the matrix and low permeability zones. Experimental data indicates that under favorable conditions, the Marangoni phenomenon can increase the amount of EOR by approximately 35 %.

Introduction

Weber witnessed the Marangoni phenomenon while observing a glass of wine, wherein the wine flowed upwards on the glass wall as drops [10]. It was later referred to by Thomson as " Tears of Wine" (Fig. 1) [11]. Wine tears, are formed due to the Marangoni effect, which is the result of the difference in surface tension between water and alcohol in wine. As wine is swirled in a glass, the alcohol evaporates from the surface, leading to a decrease in surface tension. This creates a gradient in surface tension, causing the wine to flow down the sides of the glass in streaks or droplets, which are observed as wine tears. Additionally, evaporative cooling contributes to the flow responsible for wine tears, further enhancing their formation. He attributed the phenomenon to differences in surface tension at the air-wine interface caused by the concentration differential of alcohol evaporation. Marangoni first proposed a general formulation of this phenomenon in 1865, providing a foundation for its understanding. He established that in the presence of an interfacial tension (IFT) gradient between adjacent phases caused by variance in concentration, temperature, or electrical properties, liquid currents will transport from low IFT points to those on a medium or high IFT spectrum [12,13]. Thornton's research on solvent injection corroborates this phenomenon, as it demonstrates that if the solvent phase is disjunctive, the droplets will coalesce due to mass transfer, whereas if it remains commingled in the continuous phase, coalescence operations occur much later [14].Fig. 1Tears of wine and the marangoni effect.Fig. 1

Marangoni mechanism

The Marangoni mechanism is a fascinating field of study that involves the transfer of mass along an interface between two phases, most commonly liquids, caused by a gradient in surface tension. This gradient may arise from an array of factors, including differences in temperature, concentration, or the presence of surfactants. The Marangoni effect can occur in various contexts, ranging from the surface ripples of a hot beverage to the formation of complex microstructures during the fabrication of semiconductors. Temperature gradients in liquid systems can trigger changes in interfacial density, resulting in surface tension gradients that lead to Marangoni convection, a unique type of flow occurring at an interface and giving rise to a plethora of flow patterns, which can have significant impacts on mass transfer and mixing. Concentration gradients, on the other hand, often lead to concentration drift where solutes move from areas of high concentration to areas of lower concentration. This transport mechanism plays a vital role in mass transfer processes such as diffusion and governs the operation of surfactants.

The presence of surfactants introduces a Marangoni stress that results from the surface tension gradient along an interface. This stress can reduce IFT, promote mixing, and modify flow velocities, among other effects. In industrial applications, surfactants are commonly used to control the Marangoni effect to achieve desired system outcomes, such as improving the efficiency of printing and coating processes or enhancing the recovery of oil from reservoirs [15]. Despite extensive research investigating the Marangoni effect, many facets of this fascinating phenomenon remain unclear. Advances in technology and novel scientific methods continue to contribute to a deeper understanding of the mechanisms underlying this process. Consequently, there is still much to learn about the potential applications of the Marangoni mechanism across a variety of fields, including environmental science, food science, and reservoir engineering.

The role of Marangoni phenomenon in oil recovery

In 1960, Groothuis and Zuiderweg attributed Thornton's results to Marangoni, and for the first time hypothesized that Marangoni could contribute to oil production. They reported that Marangoni increases mass transfer by oscillating the interface of two phases, and depending on the direction of mass transfer, it can cause discontinuous phase continuity or further rupture. They also showed that by injecting a suitable solvent, trapped oil could be produced [16].

In 1983, Lam developed a visual model filled with synthetic grains in which trapped oil was produced by injecting water and normal propanol in equilibrium with oil. He calculated the critical capillary dimensionless number in the normally injected state and in the state where the amount of alcohol injected was slightly more than equilibrium. From their comparison, he concluded that the critical capillary number decreases when propanol is used more than equilibrium; as a result, more oil flows under normal conditions, which means more production. He also showed that as the concentration of alcohol increased, the volume of the oil increased (the oil swelled) and the tension between the steps decreased [17].

He used the results of the work of Sorensen [18] and Sterling [19] to separate the role of Marangoni from the mass transfer, stating that in certain conditions, the mass transfer on the interface can lead to turbulence and surface instabilities (Marangoni phenomenon). Therefore, Lam calculated the concentration gradient in displacement at different velocities at concentrations lower and higher than this and showed that the concentration gradient is very high in high-velocity injection (low alcohol concentrations). Therefore, by severe surface turbulency are applied to the system, mass transfer is accelerated several times, and as a result, due to the simultaneous transfer of mass and Marangoni in the production range, it is greatly affected by the difference between general and equilibrium concentrations.

However, when the displacement rate is low (at high alcohol concentration), the system concentration gradient is limited and therefore the surface turbulency are very low (Marangoni is low) and the distance between equilibrium and non-equilibrium is not large. He concludes by reporting that interface turbulency in field or core dimensions will be negligible. In a study of various alcoholic solvents, Lu et al. concluded that Marangoni increases mass transfer by 3–4 times [20].

Lyford continued Lam's work, creating a large vertical grain medium filled with hydrophilic granules. He saturated the micro model with water and then injected oil until it reached Swi. He injected oil in four different ways to production, and reported the results as saying that injecting slug was the best option, and that the system did not need to be mixed to increase alcohol consumption. Due to changes in the amount of IFT, it is likely that the cause of increasing production is interfacial turbulency (Marangoni). He suggested that it be better to repeat the analysis based on the calculation of the dimensionless number of Marangoni [21,22]. Lyford performed slug experiments with different types of alcohol and showed that injection of normal propanol produced the highest recovery. In his next study, he examined the IFT changes for the water and oil system in the presence of different percentages of different alcohols and showed that the IFT changes in the oil and propanol system are maximal.

In fluid injection projects, the injected fluids move in the reservoir in such a way that their flows do not necessarily come into contact with all of the oil in the reservoir. Residual oil in inaccessible pathways is called residual oil, and this phenomenon may often occur due to the heterogeneity of the reservoir rock and in microscopic or macroscopic dimensions; Common and insignificant heterogeneities in rocks (such as vug, sedimentary plates, and regional variations of rocks) or major heterogeneities (such as fractures, layers with different permeability, and shale layers). Under suitable conditions, various factors can contribute to residual oil production, including molecular diffusion and diffusion, cross-flow due to viscous force/capillary force/gravitational force, evaporation, and swelling. In this paper, it will be shown that Marangoni flow can also be one of the effective mechanisms for residual oil production.

During oil flow in heterogeneous reservoirs, the injected fluid flows along a path with higher permeability, leading to different capillary conditions in that specific path compared to adjacent ones. For example, during water injection in hydrophilic rock, the path with higher permeability will have increased water saturation, resulting in very low capillary pressure in that path. In petroleum engineering, Campbell [23], Mohanty [24], and Berger [25] observed similar phenomena in their micromodel experiments, attributing these effects to capillary forces.

In general, the Marangoni phenomenon refers to mass transfer due to the IFT gradient at the interface between two fluids. This gradient can arise from differences in solute concentration, electric charge, and surface temperature between the two phases. Convective flow occurs from points of low IFT to points of high IFT, accompanied by interfacial turbulence between the two fluids and liquid dispersion [26]. Additionally, if a temperature gradient exists, it can induce thermal-capillary convection, also known as Benard-Marangoni displacement. The Marangoni effect can occur in both gas-liquid and liquid-liquid systems, significantly increasing the mass transfer rate between the two phases [27].

In the oil industry, Groothuis and Zuiderweg claimed that the Marangoni phenomenon enhances oil recovery due to its influence on mass transfer rate and interfacial turbulence, a claim supported by many studies [16]. Numerous studies have also shown that unbalanced shear stresses at the interface of two fluids can induce convective flow [28,29]. The impact of Marangoni flow on the recovery of residual oil has often been reported in relation to capillary cross-flow. Wiley and Mohanty [24,30] and Sohrabi et al. [31] asserted that in areas with low permeability, diffusion and capillary flow can improve the production of residual oil.

One major challenge in fractured reservoirs is the production of trapped oil, which can be recovered through various mechanisms such as viscous cross-flows, diffusion, capillary force, and gravity force. It is important to note that the amount of oil produced depends on factors such as the presence of water, pore structure, and the type of injection regime. Gas injection is an effective method for oil recovery, used in both conventional and complex fractured reservoirs. Due to the high mobility of gas, it tends to flow through permeable paths during injection, potentially bypassing oil in less permeable areas. Parameters such as the type of injection (near-miscible, immiscible, or first-contact miscible), the presence of connate water, and dead-end pores can significantly influence the recovery process [32].

Gas injection under near-miscible conditions reduces the cost of gas compression and lowers the capillary pressure threshold, facilitating greater oil production from the matrix while minimizing costs and gravity head [33]. Morel et al. observed a cross-flow when saturating the matrix with a mixture of methane and pentane and injecting nitrogen into the fracture [34]. Romanser et al. conducted experiments using carbon dioxide (CO2) at low pressure, which were later improved by Berger, who examined the driving forces of gravity, viscosity, capillary forces, and penetration in their experiments [35].

Khosravi et al. built upon the work done in the Burger experiment to investigate the effect of Marangoni flow on the recovery of residual oil in the matrix block when CO2 is injected into the fracture under near-miscible conditions. They initially tested a two-component system that included decane-saturated matrix blocks and injected CO2 at various pressures. This process represented zero Marangoni flow because no Marangoni flow occurred in this system; in other words, the thermodynamic degree of freedom of the system remained zero in each experiment where CO2 was injected at different pressures. Since the surface tension gradient was constant, these experiments were conducted at the zero Marangoni level [1].

Khosravi et al. Performed further experiments by adding propane to their system to create a surface tension gradient by increasing the degree of freedom to one so that they could observe the Marangoni effect in their results. In the next step, they compared the results of the two-component system (zero Marangoni level) and the three-component system to observe the direct impact of the Marangoni phenomenon on oil recovery, which is proportional to the Marangoni number. It should be noted that this phenomenon is very weak in immiscible conditions and disappears in critical conditions, although it is very strong in near-miscible conditions. Khosravi et al. investigated the effect of IFT gradient in a one-dimensional matrix-fracture system when CO2 is injected in near-miscible conditions to produce residual oil in the reservoir. They showed that this phenomenon is at a maximum value in near miscible conditions, and depending on the type and composition of oil and how the surface tension changes, it affects the oil recovery within the matrix and can be up to about 35 % Increase oil recovery [36]. Also, Khosravi and Bahramian et al., According to the results of the Marangoni stream, show that this phenomenon can recycle bypassed oil. In 2015, Khosravi et al. conducted their experiments in a matrix system at different pressures (presence or absence of Marangoni phenomenon) in CO2 injection and found that this phenomenon could be delaying the breakthrough time [26]. Delay in breakthrough time also increases oil recovery, which is proportional to the CO2 injected pore volume.

Marangoni number

The Marangoni number is a non-dimensional constant that reflects the relative contribution of the displacements caused by Marangoni flow and diffusion within a system. Specifically, it represents the ratio of the mass transfer brought about by the underlying interfacial gradient of tension and the extent of displacement generated by molecular diffusion. The Marangoni phenomenon results from the presence of an IFT gradient present in the system, which ultimately underpins the pressure waves or irregularities in tension that drive movements in the mixed-phase fluids. In order to address the potentially significant implications of the Marangoni effect on fluid flow within a system, the precise mechanics of the IFT gradient warrant closer investigation [38]. Molecular diffusion generates differences in IFT that ultimately give rise to the Marangoni effect. Notably, as defined, Marangoni numbers can be regarded as a specific type of Peclet number. Peclet numbers are frequently used for quantifying the relative relation between the rate of advection and molecular diffusion. Given that Marangoni numbers adopt a similar formula to Peclet numbers, they are similarly well-suited to convey probabilities associated with mass migration resulting from combined effects of Marangoni phenomenon and molecular diffusion. Explicitly expressed, Marangoni numbers are derived as follows [39]:8Ma=amountofdisplacementbytheMarangoniflowamountofdisplacementbythediffusionphenomenon

These include heat transfer or temperature changes, increases or decreases in concentration (due to the presence of surfactants or other species), and variations in electrical charge. The Marangoni number can be quickly and easily calculated based on a handful of parameters, including changes in IFT, viscosity, diffusion coefficients, and flow lengths. In addition, Marangoni flow can manifest when temperature gradients exist across a fluid or medium. This is because IFT is a temperature-dependent property, and variations in temperature are consequently capable of altering this critical characteristic. As a result, minor temperature differentials throughout a system or medium can trigger variations in IFT, generating corresponding narrowing in the mobility of these mixed-phase fluids from sites of high temperature to those of lower heat. The intensity or extent of the Marangoni effect is determined by a dimensionless constant known as the Marangoni number. This phenomenon resulting from temperature gradients across a fluid is called the thermal Marangoni effect. In summary, small shifts in the temperature within a medium can act upon the IFT to generate Marangoni flows, which can prove to be significant factors impacting fluid dynamics in diverse fields and industries [42]. These observations demonstrate a clear correlation between the Marangoni number and the rate of oil/gas interface development, providing direct evidence of the contribution of the Marangoni effect to the overall recovery process.

However, it is important to note that in tests where CO2's ability to swell the oil phase and extract hydrocarbons plays a dominant role (tests 11 and 19), the Marangoni effect still contributes to the acceleration of the recovery process but is coupled with other mechanisms. This combination of effects highlights the complex interactions within the system, yet the proportionality between the ultimate recovery and the Marangoni number, as shown in Fig. 2, indicates that the Marangoni effect plays a significant role in enhancing oil recovery in matrix-fracture systems.

Surfactants in an aqueous solution exhibit a strong preference for positioning themselves atop the common surface that encompasses the liquid and gas phases. Whenever an external force creates a thin spot on the bubble, the resulting expansion of the bubble's surface area increases tension within, leading to reductions in the concentration of surfactants occupying the bubble surface. This decrease in surface concentration creates an interfacial chemical potential, generating net flux of surfactant molecules across interfacial boundaries towards regions where surfactant concentration is lower. As surfactants accumulate at regions with lower surface concentration, they will draw tiny drops with them from the bulk solution, leading to mass transfer and redistribution of the liquid contents and the Marangoni effect. Additionally, a decrease in surface tension will arise when surfactant concentration levels are below the critical micelle concentration (CMC); this generates a host of comprehensive and intricate outcomes. Broadly, Marangoni effect promulgates the most significant effects at concentration levels close to CMC and marginally beneath this critical threshold.

The performance of foam hinges on a multitude of factors, including the stability and elasticity of liquid films, the efficiency of surfactant placement, the viscosity of the liquid, and the charges emanating from the surfaces. When confronted with a foam system, a decrease in concentration occurs in the thin layer containing surfactant, corresponding to an increase in the surface area and a significant thinning of the liquid layer. With initial declines in surface surfactant concentrations, surface tension increases, subsequently resulting in the shrinking of the system's surface to achieve lower energies. As a result, the interfacial behavior of an optimal foam system is formulated by a range of complex, interacting forces that span fluid dynamics, molecular chemistry, and physical characteristics [47]. Intriguingly, the Marangoni effect precipitates fluid flow from areas of decreased surface tension to areas of relatively higher surface tension. Further insights illustrate that Marangoni flow conveniently forestalls thinning of the liquid film when impinging on foam. In other words, examination of this relationship suggests that Marangoni-mediated differences in surface tension critically support pluripotent cytotoxic treatments. Surface dynamics and elasticity represent fundamental phenomena that aptly evidence in situ micromolecules modulated through Marangoni mechanisms. Ultimately, modal codification reveals the supremacy of the Marangoni method in operations that rely upon surface separation of fluid phases, posing enhanced hydrodynamics and reduced surface exchanges as potential areas of exploration [48,49].

In general, the stability of foam is affected by three parameters: 1- Intermolecular forces of van der Waals, 2- Marangoni phenomenon 3- Electric double-layer which occurs due to the presence of dipolar surfactants in the system [50,51]. Rand and Kraynik investigated the effect of Marangoni on foam stability at high pressures in their experimental study. Their results showed that at high pressure, the size of the bubbles is uniform, which can reduce the movement of gas between the bubbles. This causes the liquid to move from the region with low surface tension to the region with high surface tension, increasing the stability of the foam. This phenomenon refers to the Marangoni effect on the stability of foam [52]. In 2019, Olayiwola and Dejam studied the effect of silica nanoparticles on oil production during alternating injection of sodium dodecyl sulfate surfactant and low-salt water. During this process, nanoparticles precipitate and reduce the permeability, and cause formation damage but due to the Marangoni flow, removes during the injection of the aqueous solution [53]. In 2019, Szabries et al. studied the stability of foam at high temperatures and pressures. Parameters that cause Marangoni Effect

The Marangoni effect arises from an intricate interplay of physical-chemical factors that operate in response to specific inducements and external stimuli. Magnitude and localization of temperature gradients at different fluid domains, concentrations of surfactants or charged compounds at the liquid and gas interface, disparities in wetting, and gradients in viscous stresses can promote the Marangoni effect.

These and other factors can instigate responsive adjustments to region-specific centers-of-mass within the system, leading to stress generation and IFT alteration. The coupling of these manifold components emblemizes the daunting incertitude of this enigmatic problem-space for physical-chemical analysts aiming to fully uncover the intricacies of Marangoni effects and potential futuristic technical applications that may arise from fundamental disclosures on surface tension physics.

The buoyancy convection flow decreases when dealing with the microgravity state, but the effect of Marangoni convection will still exist and can cause problems for the systems. Thermal Marangoni

The phenomenon of heat Marangoni was first observed by Henry Benard in the early 20th century. He designed a setup to study the behavior of a thin layer of white wax on a metal plate suspended above a boiling water bath. Benard noted the formation of hexagonal cells far from the edges of the vessel and correctly recognized that hot fluid flows toward the centers of these heat cells [57].

Lord Riley later analyzed linear stability, asserting that buoyancy forces produce convective cells. However, the system Riley had in mind was different from Benard's setup, and thus the results from the two could not be generalized to each other [58]. In 1956, Block experimentally demonstrated, and in 1958, Pearson theoretically proved that changes in IFT due to temperature variations at the interface of the liquid led to the formation of convection Benard cells.

In the 1960s, Groothuis and Zuiderweg were the first to propose the role of Marangoni in oil production, reporting that Marangoni effects could cause discontinuities in phase continuity or further ruptures, depending on the direction of mass transfer [16]. Fanton et al. conducted studies on a flat plate and found that the IFT gradient could cause the wetting phase to rise against gravity [59].

Nield utilized theoretical methods to investigate the relationship and interaction between Marangoni and Riley convection, demonstrating that the two convection types enhance one another. Lyford and Perth conducted experiments using aqueous solutions of normal heptanol and normal hexanol, showing that the direction of flow could not be detected by equilibrium IFT [60]. D'Aubeterre et al. designed two separate models for heat transfer and mass transfer experiments using aliphatic alcohols and CO2 [61]. They showed that more volatile substances evaporate more readily with temperature changes, leading to greater instabilities and, consequently, a more pronounced Marangoni effect.

Studies on the effect of thermal Marangoni on EOR processes have received relatively less attention, with limited research available on this topic. In 2016, Mohammad Moradi et al. simulated the SAGD process of thermal EOR using COMSOL software. In this study, they examined the flow of thermal Marangoni during the EOR process, concluding that this mechanism is highly effective in reducing the amount of residual oil [62].

Generally, a temperature difference at the interface of two fluids can create an IFT gradient. As a result of this gradient, convective flow is generated from regions of lower IFT to regions of higher IFT. This phenomenon, known as thermal Marangoni, can play a significant role in oil recovery during EOR processes.

In 2014, Alizadeh et al. numerically studied the effect of Marangoni convection on the porous structure of rock, focusing on EOR processes in petroleum engineering. They employed a simplified method called finite component analysis and examined both macro and micro-convection models. The Muhlenkamp model was utilized to evaluate micro-convections, which was validated by concentration maps. Alizadeh et al. used a specific concentration distribution as a boundary condition for micro-displacement to simulate conditions akin to EOR processes. They concluded that micro-convection does not significantly alter fluid flow in a porous medium. In the case of macro-convection, they demonstrated that damping forces can delay the onset of convection flow in a porous medium [63].

In 2018, Abdolhosseini et al. investigated the effect of thermal Marangoni on oil production during immiscible gas injection. They focused on the role of Marangoni in the EOR process, employing both analytical and experimental methods to explore this effect. Their results, derived from both isothermal and non-isothermal experiments, highlighted the direction of Marangoni flow. They found that the impact of the Marangoni phenomenon on oil production is significant, asserting that Marangoni flow is a crucial mechanism in oil production, with the amount of oil produced and the oil recovery factor being dependent on the temperature gradient (Fig. 5) [64].Fig. 5Effect of pressure and temperature on Marangoni effect [64].Fig. 5

Marangoni in the process of EOR

To summarize, the Marangoni effect can substantially boost the efficiency of chemical and gas injection EOR methods by inducing favorable fluid flow patterns and improving interfacial dynamics. EOR techniques encompass a wide range of procedures used to maximize the recovery of crude oil from oil reservoirs. The Marangoni effect has been identified as a crucial factor in the success of many EOR strategies, particularly those requiring reliable methods to control IFT and generate stable patterns of fluid movement. These methods are typically employed after primary and secondary recovery methods have been exhausted. The primary types of EOR methods include:

Thermal Recovery: This method involves the introduction of heat to the reservoir to reduce the viscosity of the oil, making it easier to extract. The most common thermal EOR techniques are steam injection and in-situ combustion. Thermal methods account for a significant portion of EOR production, especially in heavy oil reservoirs.

Chemical Injection: Chemical EOR involves injecting chemicals into the reservoir to increase oil recovery. The chemicals used can include polymers, surfactants, and alkali. These substances help to reduce the IFT between oil and water, increase the viscosity of the displacing water, and improve the mobility ratio, leading to more efficient oil displacement.

Gas Injection: This method involves injecting gases such as CO2, natural gas, or nitrogen into the reservoir. The gas helps to maintain reservoir pressure and can either dissolve in the oil to reduce its viscosity (miscible gas injection) or push the oil towards the production wells (immiscible gas injection). Gas injection is widely used and is particularly effective in light oil reservoirs.

Miscible and Immiscible Flooding: Miscible flooding involves injecting substances that mix completely with the oil, reducing its viscosity and improving flow. Immiscible flooding, on the other hand, involves injecting substances that do not mix with the oil but help to displace it by increasing reservoir pressure.

Among the mentioned methods, the Marangoni effect can be observed in the surfactant injection and gas injection methods. In the following, the laboratory results and then the simulation results of the Marangoni effect in the mentioned methods will be examined.

Difference between Marangoni Effect and capillary cross flow

Convection flow from points with less surface tension to points with more surface tension is called Marangoni flow. Unfortunately, this phenomenon is less known in petroleum engineering. However, the results of the studies show that under favorable conditions, Marangoni increases oil recovery by about 35 %. A review of previous studies shows that this phenomenon is sometimes observed in laboratory results but has been justified or modeled using the concept of capillary cross-flow. While cross-capillary flow and Marangoni are fundamentally different from each other [75]. The depicted Fig. 15 schematically portrays the diverse flow structures that are present within an evaporating droplet.Fig. 15Schematic of multiple flow facets within the framework of a droplet evaporation.Fig. 15

Schematic of multiple flow facets within the framework of a droplet evaporation.

In this section as seen in Fig. 15, a drop of water on a hydrophilic surface that is evaporating will be investigated. A drop of water on a hydrophilic surface exhibits specific characteristics due to the surface's affinity for water. On a hydrophilic surface, the water drops spread out rather than forming a bead. This spreading occurs because the surface energy of the hydrophilic material is high, allowing the water to wet the surface easily.

The contact angle, which is the angle formed between the tangent to the liquid surface and the solid surface at the contact point, is low on a hydrophilic surface. Typically, a contact angle of less than 90° indicates hydrophilicity. Evaporation of a droplet on a surface involves a complex interaction of physical processes and environmental conditions that together cause different flows in the droplet. These distinct fluidic motions arise due to the interplay between various underlying flow mechanisms such as capillary flow, Marangoni flow and interfacial flow, which all play pivotal roles during the droplet evaporation process. Notably, the precise trajectory of each flow feature is indicated through the orientation of directional flow arrows that accompany each respective flow mechanism within the diagram. This difference can be illustrated with an example, assuming a large container of water and this container has zero capillary forces due to the flat contact surface (large capillary radius). To observe the flow direction in the air-water interface, a minute quantity of pepper powder can be evenly dispersed on the water surface. A slightly soapy ear cleaner is then dipped into the center of this interface, detaching it from the walls of the container. It can be discerned that all particles are uniformly and radially dispersed from the gravitational center of the interface (a point with less IFT, resembling zero capillary force). In light of this topic, it can be inferred that the only active mechanism under near-critical conditions is the Marangoni flow, which causes the migration of particles from the center of the container towards its edges. Thus, at such specific volatile conditions, the capillary forces are profoundly inhibited and cannot be expected to contribute to any meaningful active cross-flow.

As previously established, in binary fluid systems, the IFT value is ascribed to the overall enrichment of CO2 in the fluid. The extent of liquid phase flow phenomena is directly proportional to the number of Marangoni counterparts produced owing to the coexistent IFT gradient.

The dynamic process observed in saturated environments indicates that the predominant force at play is the Marangoni flow, which induces particle migration from the fluid center to its peripheral edges. However, the assumption that capillary forces significantly weaken under neighboring near-critical conditions is not supported by actual oil displacement processes. Therefore, it is essential to quantitatively analyze the contribution of the Marangoni effect in this context.

Following the injection of CO2 into a three-component mixture, the IFT between oil and gas decreases with increasing concentrations of CO2, particularly relevant to near-fracture analysis. The histogram illustrates that higher CO2 concentrations are noticeably concentrated towards the fracture end. This clustering can be attributed to curvature anomalies and surface tension mechanics, leading to the establishment of a Marangoni flow gradient directed towards the fracture endpoint.

To quantitatively assess the contribution of the Marangoni effect, one can examine the relationship between the Marangoni number and the IFT gradient. This relationship can be expressed mathematically, allowing for the calculation of the Marangoni flow's influence on oil displacement. The constraining effect of the fluid environment significantly influences the magnitude of the Marangoni flow, and this can be analyzed through precise calculations that extrapolate the overall geometry of the IFT gradient alongside a formulated Marangoni number reflecting activity in the fluid phase.

As pressure and IFT increase under critical conditions, the Marangoni flow may become less significant. However, it is crucial to quantify this effect through experiments or simulations that measure the specific changes in IFT and the resultant flow patterns. In non-mixed phases, the Marangoni flow affects oil recovery; however, this recovery is often insufficient to counteract production declines associated with pressure drops. In nearby critical conditions, a robust Marangoni flow can be observed, independent of substantial surface tension shifts, favorably enhancing production in conjunction with decreasing pressure.

Experimental investigations into the IFT gradient phenomenon indicate that Marangoni flow is significantly decoupled from typical capillary cross-flow. This distinction is vital for a comprehensive understanding of the processes involved. Under mixed conditions, the Marangoni flow is minimal, while it exhibits only a slight influence in immiscible scenarios. Notably, exposure factors reveal that variations in strain coefficients relative to oil structures lead to significant increases in production.

Furthermore, it is anticipated that the magnitude of the Marangoni number, in conjunction with proximity to miscible restrictions, demonstrates a dramatic Marangoni flow. The type of oil must be accounted for in estimating production volumes, potentially reaching increases of up to 35 % in favorably obtained oil output [16].

People like Fayers [76], and Jamili [77] have modeled the capillary cross-flow by changing the capillary pressure graphs in terms of saturation. Since Marangoni is not caused by a change in the saturation of the wetting phase, it is possible from a modeling point of view to adapt the results to the experimental data by changing the capillary graphs. But this method is far from the reality and physics of the problem, and the convection flow due to the imbalance of shear stresses at the interface is a completely separate mechanism.

Conclusion and summery

This article encompasses an extensive analysis of the Marangoni effect in the EOR process, scrutinizing both its laboratory and simulation findings. Measuring and quantifying the exact extent and percentage of the influence of the Marangoni effect on EOR procedures at the reservoir level can pose significant challenges due to the intricate interplay of the various factors relevant to the process. The reviewed research and experiments demonstrated that the Marangoni effect can make a significant contribution to EOR and raise the oil recovery rate by a few percentage points. The impact of all variables affecting the Marangoni phenomenon was scrutinized, yielding the following outcomes.1.The presence of the surface tension gradient, due to the temperature difference, causes the creation of the thermal convective flow that can create the fluid flow from a region with a lower temperature to a region with a higher temperature.2.The magnitude of the Marangoni flow is directly related to the interfacial/surface tension gradient. As a result, with increasing temperature difference, the amount of interfacial/surface tension gradient increases and as a result, the effect on oil recovery increases.3.The calculated Marangoni number is consistent with the oil recovery difference between isothermal and non-isothermal experiments. This means that the oil recovery changes in each experiment are the result of a Marangoni flow.4.In a matrix-fracture system, a matrix which is a single-component saturated fluid, by injection of gas, the phenomenon of Marangoni does not occur (two-component system), because the degree of freedom is zero and the amount of IFT everywhere in the porous media and fracture, has a constant value and there is no IFT gradient. In this case, by adding another component to the system, the degree of freedom becomes 1, and the IFT changes in the matrix and the fracture are created, and the Marangoni phenomenon is activated, and due to the activation of this mechanism, the Marangoni phenomenon can have a positive and negative effect in the production of bypassed oil.5.The Marangoni phenomenon, in addition to increasing the production of bypassed oil, can delay the breakthrough time.6.In EOR operations, such as fluid injection processes, macro displacements are significantly more feasible compared to micro displacements. This distinction arises due to the fluctuations attendant upon the introduction of the injected fluid resulting from solution diffusion and oil evaporation at the interface. The Marangoni effect enhances the oil recovery process by augmenting mass transfer facilitated by concentration variegations encountered during macro displacement schemes.7.A comprehensive study of the Marangoni effect in this article highlights its significance and impact on the EOR process. However, limited studies and experiments exist in the fields of oil engineering and EOR, with many investigations failing to consider all phenomena that could interfere with the Marangoni effect. Consequently, this study serves as a foundation for conducting a thorough experimental investigation of Marangoni's work in the future. Suggested topics for future research are presented below.•Experiments to understand the effect of single Marangoni convection on multiphase flow in static geometries and micromodels.•Study of advanced hybrid EOR methods, incorporating insights from Marangoni's work to optimize sweep efficiency and improve oil recovery.•Study the dynamics of multiphase flow influenced by Marangoni phenomenon in various experimental setups to elucidate underlying mechanisms.
