Acellular fishbone scaffolds loaded with bone marrow mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes for bone defect repairing
Authors: Lei Zhu, Ji Wang, Zhengwei Liu, Kai Chen, Zhuhao Wu, Yongxiang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTissue engineering scaffolds remain pivotal in bone defect repair. Contemporary research in this field predominantly focuses on enhancing bone regeneration by optimizing scaffold composition and structure, and incorporating bioactive components. Herein, we developed a decellularized fish bone (DFB) scaffold integrated with hypoxia-osteogenic exosomes (HO-Exos), derived from rat bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) cultured under 5% oxygen tension with 7-day osteogenic priming, to promote osseous repair via their synergistic effect. The hierarchical porosity of DFB scaffolds creates a biomimetic microenvironment conducive to BMSC adhesion and osteogenic mineralization. Functionally, HO-Exos stimulate osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs via modulation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, enhance cellular migration, and promote tubulogenesis in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs). These vesicles synergistically potentiate vascularized bone regeneration in vivo through coordinated osteogenic-angiogenic regulation. To achieve sustained therapeutic delivery, HO-Exos were encapsulated within DFB scaffolds, leveraging their structural network for tailored release kinetics. In a rat model of critical-sized femoral metaphyseal defects, the designed scaffolds exhibited significant improvements in both neovascularization density and bone volume fraction compared to controls. Introduction

Bone defects, prevalent in orthopedic practice, primarily arise from trauma, infection, or neoplastic pathologies [1]. Critical-sized bone defects exhibit limited intrinsic regenerative capacity, significantly impairing treatment outcomes and presenting complex clinical challenges [2]. Recent bioengineering innovations have expanded strategies for mimicking native tissue microenvironment [3, 4]. Current interventions encompass autografts, allografts, and bone tissue engineering scaffolds [5]. However, the clinical application of autografts and allografts is restricted by donor tissue scarcity [6]. Consequently, bioactive scaffold-based strategies have emerged as a promising alternative, demonstrating therapeutic efficacy in preclinical and clinical settings [7–9]. While synthetic biomaterials dominate research, natural counterparts offer distinct advantages, including superior biocompatibility, biomimetic porosity, and sustainable sourcing [10–12]. Notably, xenogeneic grafts derived from bovine or porcine bone have achieved moderate clinical success when functionalized with growth factors [13, 14]. Nevertheless, their widespread translation remains hampered by multiple barriers, including risks of zoonotic pathogen transmission, limited availability due to reliance on livestock supplies, and ethical concerns in resource-limited regions [13]. Additionally, conventional monophasic growth factor delivery systems fail to adequately support the coupled angiogenesis-osteogenesis process essential for bone regeneration [15]. Innovations in multi-component system design provide new perspectives for addressing these limitations [16, 17]. Therefore, novel tissue-engineered scaffolds for bone defect repair remain a critical unmet need.

Here, we propose an innovative platform combining decellularized fish bone (DFB) with hypoxia-osteogenic exosomes (HO-Exos) to address critical-sized defects (Fig. 1). DFB overcomes mammalian xenograft limitations by eliminating zoonotic risks via evolutionary divergence, utilizing abundant aquaculture by-products for low-cost, sustainable sourcing, and exhibiting low immunogenicity due to reduced antigen homology [13, 14, 18–20]. Specifically, Salmon vertebrae were selected for their cancellous bone structure, which features inherent hierarchical porosity ideal for cell infiltration and exosome retention [21, 22]. As deep-sea fish, Salmon vertebrae may retain unique bioactive components (e.g., marine-specific collagens, mineral complexes) that support tissue repair, while their status as seafood processing waste enhances scalability and clinical translatability [18]. HO-Exos are derived from rat bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) cultured under 5% oxygen (O2), distinct from conventional normoxic (21% O2) or severe hypoxic (< 1% O2) conditions [23], and 7-day osteogenic priming, a timeframe balancing osteogenic factor accumulation with exosome secretion efficiency [24]. This dual-condition design mimics the physiological hypoxia of bone defects to amplify angiogenic-osteogenic cargo [25], such as vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA) [23], while optimizing osteogenic factor enrichment, including bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP-2) and BMP-4 [24]. This addresses the unmet need for coordinated angiogenic and osteogenic repair signals in single-stimulus exosome systems [24, 26]. These 30–150 nm extracellular vesicles are enriched with osteogenic microRNAs and bioactive proteins, demonstrating superior therapeutic potential compared to direct mesenchymal stem cell transplantation, exhibiting reduced immunogenicity and eliminating tumorigenic concerns [27–29]. Beyond osteoinductive capacity, HO-Exos enhance BMSC migration and promote human umbilical vein endothelial cell (HUVEC) vascular network formation, two mechanistically critical processes for scaffold-mediated defect repopulation [30–32]. This dual-functional system combines structural integrity with bioactive signaling, thereby demonstrating significant regenerative enhancement and clinical translation potential for bone reconstruction.

Schematic illustration of DFB scaffolds integrated with HO-Exos for enhanced bone regeneration. (a) Preparation workflow: Decellularization of Salmon vertebrae and HO-Exos conjugation. (b) Regenerative phases: Angiogenesis and bone regeneration in rat femoral defects

In this study, we developed a DFB scaffold integrated with HO-Exos to address critical-sized bone defects. The DFB scaffold was prepared through sequential decalcification and detergent-based decellularization of Salmon vertebrae, while HO-Exos were harvested via ultracentrifugation from supernatants of BMSCs subjected to 5% oxygen and 7-day osteogenic priming [33]. The scaffold provides a biomimetic three-dimensional (3D) microenvironment facilitating BMSC adhesion and matrix deposition, while HO-Exos act as bioactive cargo carriers, delivering osteogenic miRNAs and growth factors to modulate regenerative signaling. Functionalization of the DFB scaffold with HO-Exos was achieved through poly-D-lysine-conjugated immobilization, ensuring sustained exosome retention and localized bioactivity. In vitro, HO-Exos markedly promoted osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs, as demonstrated by elevated runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2) protein levels, enhanced alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity, and transcriptomic profiling that suggests involvement of Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathways. In vivo, the DFB/HO-Exos composite significantly accelerated bone formation and vascularization in rat femoral defects. Collectively, this dual-functional DFB scaffold presents a clinically translatable and biomimetic strategy for bone tissue engineering.

Materials and methods

Materials

Chemical reagents including Tris-hydrochloride (Tris-HCl, Phygene Biotechnology, China), deoxyribonuclease I (DNase I), Triton X-100 (New England Biolabs, Inc, USA), sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS, BioFroxx, Germany), ribonuclease A (RNase A, Biosharp, China), and ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA, Servicebio, China) were sourced from designated suppliers. Histological staining kits including hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) and Masson (Powerful Biology, China), as well as molecular biology reagents, were procured as specified. Cell culture components included Alpha Minimum Essential Medium (α-MEM), Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM, Gibco, USA), exosome-depleted fetal bovine serum (FBS, Vivacell, China), poly-D-lysine (PDL, Beyotime, China), and basement membrane matrix (MedChemExpress, China). Cellular assay kits including 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) dye solution, Calcein acetoxymethyl ester/propidium iodide (Calcein-AM/PI), Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8), ALP, 1,1’-Dioctadecyl-3,3,3’,3’-tetramethylindodicarbocyanine (DiD) membrane staining, and bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (Beyotime), as well as molecular probes SYBR Green quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) Mix (Beyotime), were obtained commercially. Antibodies targeting β-actin, Calnexin, HSP70, TSG101, CD63 (Abcam, UK), glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), CD68, CD90, VEGFA, platelet-derived growth factor BB (PDGF-BB), glycogen synthase kinase-3β (GSK-3β), phosphorylated GSK-3β (p-GSK-3β), β-catenin (Servicebio), and osteogenic markers including BMP-2, BMP-4, RUNX2, collagen type I alpha 1 (COL1A1), osteopontin (OPN), osteocalcin (OCN), ALP, and CD31 (Servicebio) were utilized. Immunoblotting supplies including polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes, enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate, and 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) developer (Servicebio), as well as genomic DNA extraction kits (Beyotime), complemented experimental workflows.

Preparation of DFB

DFB scaffolds were fabricated from Salmon vertebrae through sequential demineralization and decellularization. Fresh bones were dissected, mechanically debrided, and rinsed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove residual soft tissues. Demineralization was achieved through immersion in a decalcification solution composed of 0.5% (w/v) EDTA in 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), with continuous agitation at 120 rpm for 7 days. Cellular constituents were eliminated via three cycles of cryopreservation-thawing (alternating between − 196 °C and 37 °C), followed by successive treatments with 3% (v/v) Triton X-100 (5 days) and 2% (w/v) SDS (5 days), with intermittent PBS washes. Nucleic acid degradation was enzymatically mediated using a solution containing 100 units/mL DNase I and 50 µg/mL RNase A (incubated at 37 °C for 12 h), followed by thorough rinsing in deionized water for 72 h. Final scaffolds were sterilized in 75% ethanol (24 h), lyophilized, and cryopreserved at − 80 °C.

Characterization

Histomorphological evaluation (H&E, Masson staining) and nuclear residue assessment (DAPI staining) were performed according to manufacturers’ protocols to verify complete cellular component removal from fish bone. Residual DNA content was quantified using a genomic DNA extraction kit with spectrophotometric analysis (NanoDrop 2000, Thermo Fisher, USA; n = 3). The porosity of the fish bone was determined via the ethanol displacement method (n = 3), with values calculated as (V1-V3)/(V2-V3) × 100%. Here, V1, V2, and V3 denote the initial ethanol volume, total volume of the ethanol-scaffold system after immersion, and residual ethanol volume following scaffold removal, respectively. Additionally, the surface zeta (ζ) potential of DFB and PDL-coated DFB (PDL-DFB) was measured over a pH range of 2 to 10 using an Anton Paar Surpass 3 electrokinetic analyzer (Anton Paar GmbH, Switzerland; n = 3). Microstructural characterization of gold-sputtered native fish bone (NFB), DFB, and PDL-DFB scaffold surfaces was performed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, Hitachi TM-1000, Japan) operated at an accelerating voltage of 12 kV, enabling analysis of morphological features and pore interconnectivity. Finally, mechanical properties were evaluated using a materials testing system (MTS Standard 43) under uniaxial compression at a rate of 1 mm/min. Compressive strength was calculated from stress-strain curves (n = 3).

Cytotoxicity and proliferation

Cytocompatibility of DFB was evaluated via direct and indirect contact assays. For indirect cytotoxicity, BMSCs were cultured in α-MEM supplemented with DFB extracts for 2–4 days, followed by live/dead staining with Calcein-AM/PI (incubated at 37 °C for 30 min; Leica DMi8 fluorescence microscope, Germany). Direct cytocompatibility was assessed by seeding BMSCs on DFB surfaces (48 h culture) with subsequent SEM imaging of cell adhesion morphology. Proliferation kinetics were quantified using the CCK-8 assay: BMSCs were cultured in α-MEM supplemented with DFB extracts for 1, 3, or 5 days (n = 4), followed by incubation with CCK-8 solution at 37 °C for 2 h. Absorbance was subsequently measured at 450 nm using a microplate spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific Multiskan FC, USA).

Biocompatibility of DFB in vivo

DFB was subcutaneously implanted into the dorsal region of Sprague-Dawley rats (n = 4). After 3 weeks, the animals were euthanized, and subcutaneous tissues, whole blood, and visceral organs were harvested. Whole blood analysis was performed using an automated hematology analyzer (Mindray, BC-2800vet, China). Subcutaneous and visceral tissue specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by paraffin embedding and sectioning. Subsequently, tissue sections underwent H&E staining. For immunohistochemical staining, subcutaneous tissue specimens underwent thermal antigen retrieval in Tris-EDTA buffer (70 °C, 4 h), followed by quenching of endogenous peroxidase activity with 3% hydrogen peroxide (25 min) and blockade of nonspecific epitopes using 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA, 30 min). Tissue sections were subsequently incubated with a primary monoclonal antibody targeting CD68 (dilution 1:300, 4 °C overnight), followed by incubation with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody (1 h). Chromogenic visualization was performed using a DAB substrate kit; nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin, and digital image acquisition was conducted using a digital slide scanner (Olympus VS200, Japan).

Isolation and characterization of BMSC-derived exosomes

Following 7-day osteogenic priming under 5% oxygen, BMSCs were cultured in exosome-free differentiation medium supplemented with 10% exosome-depleted FBS for 48 h to promote exosome secretion. Conditioned media then underwent a series of centrifugation steps: initial centrifugation at 2,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove floating cells, followed by a secondary centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 30 min to eliminate cellular debris. The supernatants were subsequently filtered through 0.22 μm membranes and ultracentrifuged at 100,000 × g for 70 min to pellet exosomes. Purified exosomes were resuspended in PBS and comprehensively characterized. Size distribution profiles were quantified using the ZetaView® nanoparticle tracking analyzer (Particle Metrix PMX-110, Germany). Morphological integrity was validated by transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEOL JEM-1400Plus, Japan) operated at 100 kV with 2% uranyl acetate negative staining. Exosomal identity was further confirmed through Western blot analysis targeting TSG101, CD63, and Hsp70 (1:1000 dilution), while β-actin (1:5000) and Calnexin (1:1000) served as cytoplasmic and endoplasmic reticulum contamination controls, respectively.

To identify key bioactive components underlying the observed osteogenic and angiogenic activities, we compared the enrichment of osteogenic and angiogenic factors between normal (non-pretreated) BMSC-derived exosomes (N-Exos, control) and exosomes derived from BMSCs subjected to 7-day osteogenic priming under 5% oxygen (HO-Exos) via Western blot, as described below. Primary antibodies against BMP-4 (1:500 dilution), BMP-2, VEGFA, and PDGF-BB (1:1000 dilution) were used, with CD63 (1:1000 dilution) as the loading control.

Osteogenic protein expression by oxygen-varied exosomes

Three types of exosomes were isolated from BMSCs subjected to 7-day osteogenic priming under 1%, 5%, or 21% oxygen (designated as 1%-O2-Exos, 5%-O2-Exos, and 21%-O2-Exos, respectively). BMSCs were treated with these exosomes and induced for osteogenic differentiation for 14 days. Western blot was performed as described below to detect osteogenic proteins RUNX2, COL1A1, and OPN (1:1000 dilution).

Scratch assay

BMSC migration was evaluated using scratch assays. Cells were randomly configured as follows (n = 3 per group): Control (untreated cells in plates), HO-Exos (cells exposed to HO-Exos), DFB (cells supplemented with DFB extracts), and DFB-Exos (cells treated with DFB-Exos extracts). Uniform scratches were generated using a sterile pipette tip. Cells were stained with Calcein-AM at 0 h and 24 h post-scratch. Images were captured using a Leica DMi8 microscope. Migration rates were quantified as the percentage of scratch closure.

Endothelial tubulogenesis

HUVECs were resuspended in DMEM and allocated into the same four groups as described for the Scratch assay. HUVECs (5 × 104 cells/well) were plated onto 24-well plates pre-coated with basement membrane matrix and maintained in culture for 12 h. At the experimental endpoint, cells were stained with Calcein-AM at 37 °C for 30 min, and fluorescent images were captured using a fluorescence microscope to evaluate tubular network formation. Vascular tube density was determined as the number of lumens per square millimeter (n = 3).

Osteogenic differentiation analysis

BMSCs were suspended in α-MEM and randomly assigned to four experimental groups (n = 3 per group): Control (untreated cells), HO-Exos (cells treated with HO-Exos), DFB (cells seeded on DFB), and DFB-Exos (cells seeded on DFB with HO-Exos). Osteogenic differentiation was induced in specific media for designated durations.

Quantitative real-time PCR

After 7 days of osteogenic induction, total RNA was isolated from BMSCs using TRIzol reagent. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was generated via reverse transcription with the Evo M-MLV RT Mix Kit, and quantitative real-time PCR was conducted using SYBR Green Master Mix. Primer sequences are shown in Supplementary Table 1. mRNA expression levels were normalized to GAPDH using the comparative 2−ΔΔCt method for data analysis.

Western blotting

Following 14 days of induction, cells were lysed with Radioimmunoprecipitation Assay (RIPA) buffer. Proteins were separated by Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate–Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), transferred to PVDF membranes, and blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk. Membranes were probed overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting RUNX2, COL1A1, OPN, ALP (1:1000 dilution), GAPDH (1:5000 dilution; loading control), GSK-3β, p-GSK-3β, β-catenin (1:500 dilution), β-actin (1:5000 dilution; loading control). Subsequently, membranes were incubated for 1 h with corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein bands were detected with an ECL detection system and quantified using AIWBwell™ software.

ALP activity assay

On day 14, cells were harvested, lysed, and centrifuged. Supernatants were then incubated with para-nitrophenyl phosphate substrate at 37 °C for 10 min. Subsequently, absorbance was recorded at 405 nm using a microplate spectrophotometer (n = 3).

Mineralization assessment

After 21 days of induction, calcium deposition was analyzed via Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining. Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, stained with 2% ARS solution for 30 min, and visualized using a Leica DMi8 inverted microscope. Mineralized nodules (n = 3) were quantified using ImageJ software (v. 1.8.0_322; National Institutes of Health, USA).

Transcriptome sequencing and analysis

After 7 days of osteogenic induction with or without exosomal treatment, total RNA was isolated from BMSCs using TRIzol reagent. Genomic DNA contamination was eliminated by DNase I digestion (n = 3). RNA quality was verified using the A260/A280 ratio, LabChip GX Touch system, and Qubit quantification. Sequencing libraries were constructed with the KC™ mRNA Library Preparation Kit (Seqhealth Technology Co., Ltd, Wuhan, China) through size selection (200–500 bp). Paired-end 150-bp sequencing reads were produced on the DNBSEQ-T7 high-throughput sequencing platform. mRNA sequencing and data processing were conducted by Seqhealth Technology Co., Ltd. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified via the edgeR statistical package (v. 4.0.16) with thresholds of |fold change| > 2 and p < 0.05, followed by functional enrichment analysis using clusterProfiler.

Loading and release of exosomes on DFB

DFB scaffolds were coated with 0.1 mg/mL poly-D-lysine solution overnight at 4 °C and lyophilized. The scaffolds were then immersed in exosome solution (100 µg/mL protein concentration) at 4 °C for 12, 24, or 48 h. Exosome loading efficiency, defined as the percentage of protein adsorbed onto DFB relative to the initial protein content, was quantified using a BCA assay (n = 3). To visualize exosome loading, exosomes were fluorescently labeled with DiD using a cell membrane staining kit. Briefly, exosome pellets were resuspended in membrane staining working solution and incubated at 37 °C for 15 min. Exosome-depleted FBS was used to terminate the staining reaction. Labeled exosomes were then centrifuged, loaded onto DFB, and imaged by confocal microscopy (Zeiss LSM900, Germany). For SEM observation of exosome distribution on PDL-DFB surfaces, scaffolds were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde overnight at 4 °C, followed by gradient ethanol dehydration and subsequent critical point drying prior to SEM (ZEISS Sigma 360, Germany) imaging. For release profiling, exosome-loaded DFB scaffolds were incubated in PBS, and supernatant protein content at 0.5, 1, 2, 4, and 7 d was measured to calculate release efficiency (released protein/total loaded protein × 100%, n = 3).

Uptake of exosomes by BMSCs

BMSCs were co-cultured with DFB scaffolds loaded with DiD-labeled exosomes for 6 h. Exosome internalization by BMSCs was assessed via laser scanning confocal microscopy.

Femur defect model establishment

All in vivo experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Drum Tower Hospital, Nanjing University Medical School (2024AE02020). Male Sprague Dawley rats (12 weeks old, n = 72 in total, 18 per group) underwent a 3-week preoperative acclimation period. Under isoflurane anesthesia, a 1-cm longitudinal incision was created along the lateral aspect of the left knee. The distal femur was surgically exposed by blunt dissection of thigh musculature, followed by the creation of a circular bone defect (3.5 mm diameter, 4 mm depth) in the lateral metaphysis using a hollow trephine drill. Defects were implanted with DFB scaffolds (with or without HO-Exos). Control group rats underwent defect creation alone, while the exosome group received direct HO-Exos administration into the defect. All animals received standard postoperative analgesia and antibiotic prophylaxis.

Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) scan

Twenty-four rats (n = 6 per group) were sacrificed 8 weeks postoperatively for micro-CT analysis, and left femurs were harvested and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h. One specimen from the control group was excluded due to complete femoral fracture (final n = 5 for control group; n = 6 for other groups). Specimens were scanned using a high-resolution micro-CT system (NMC-200, Pingseng Scientific, China) with standardized parameters: 80 kV, 60 µA, and 35 μm isotropic resolution. 3D reconstructions were generated with the manufacturer-provided software. Bone morphometric parameters including bone volume to total volume ratio (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), trabecular number (Tb.N), and bone mineral density (BMD) were quantified using the Avatar Analysis Suite (n = 5 for control group; n = 6 for other groups).

Histological and Immunofluorescence analysis

At 7 days postoperatively, 12 rats (n = 3 per group) were euthanized for immunohistochemical staining of CD90 to evaluate the recruitment of endogenous BMSCs at the early stage of bone regeneration, with images acquired via a digital slide scanner. At 2 weeks postoperatively, another 12 rats (n = 3 per group) were euthanized for immunofluorescence staining of RUNX2 and CD31 to assess early-stage osteogenesis and angiogenesis. At 4 weeks postoperatively, 12 rats (n = 3 per group) were euthanized for RUNX2 and CD31 immunofluorescence staining to examine osteogenesis and angiogenesis at the intermediate stage. At 8 weeks postoperatively (defined as the mid-to-late stage of bone regeneration in this study), the 23 rats that had undergone micro-CT scanning (final n = 5 for control group; n = 6 for other groups) were processed for subsequent histological and immunofluorescence analysis. All tissue specimens from the 7-day, 2-week, 4-week, and 8-week time points were uniformly decalcified in 0.5 M EDTA solution and then embedded in paraffin wax. H&E and Masson’s trichrome staining were performed on the 8-week specimens using standardized protocols, with histomorphometric images acquired via a digital slide scanner. For immunofluorescence staining, which included RUNX2 (1:500 dilution)/CD31 (1:500 dilution) staining at 2 and 4 weeks as well as additional staining for OPN (1:500 dilution), OCN (1:1000 dilution), and CD31 (1:500 dilution) at 8 weeks, antigen retrieval was conducted in Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 8.0) at 95 °C for 25 min. Tissue sections were blocked with 3% BSA for 30 min, incubated overnight at 4 °C with the corresponding primary antibodies, followed by 1 h of incubation with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature, and counterstained with DAPI. All immunofluorescence images were captured using a confocal microscope, with n = 3 per group for the 2-week, and 4-week time points, and n = 5 for the control group and n = 6 for other groups at 8 weeks.

Mechanical evaluation of newly formed bone

At 8 weeks postoperatively, 12 rats (n = 3 per group) were euthanized to assess the mechanical properties of newly formed bone in the femoral defect. Bone specimens containing the defect were harvested, with test samples prepared by cutting 1 mm proximal to the superior border and 1 mm distal to the inferior border of the defect (preserving the intact medial condyle) using a low-speed diamond saw. Uniaxial compression tests were conducted with a materials testing system (MTS Standard 43) at a loading rate of 1 mm/min.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8 software. A two-tailed Student’s t-test was employed for pairwise comparisons. For multiple group comparisons (considering the inconsistent sample sizes across groups: n = 5 for the control group and n = 6 for other groups), Levene’s test was first used to verify variance homogeneity across groups. If variances were homogeneous, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was conducted; if heterogeneous, the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis H test with Dunn’s post hoc test was adopted to ensure robust results. Statistical significance was defined as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001.

Results and discussion

Characterization of DFB

Critical-sized bone defects present substantial clinical challenges due to their limited intrinsic repair capacity [5]. Against this backdrop, traditional scaffolds used for repairing such defects exhibit notable limitations that further hinder therapeutic success [34]. These include mismatched mechanical properties with native bone, resulting in premature failure or stress shielding that impairs regeneration [35, 36]; poor biocompatibility and failure to provide a biomimetic microenvironment for bone tissue regeneration [37]; limited raw material sources and complex fabrication processes that increase costs and hinder scalability [38]; and reliance on single bioactive factor delivery, failing to replicate the osteoangiogenic cascade of natural bone healing [39]. Together, these drawbacks underscore the need for advanced biomaterials that address both the structural and functional requirements of effective bone regeneration.

To address these challenges, we developed DFB scaffolds from NFB, specifically Salmon vertebrae. These scaffolds are designed to preserve structural integrity while eliminating immunogenic cellular components (Fig. 2a). Comprehensive characterization via H&E staining (Fig. 2b), DNA quantification (Fig. 2c), and nuclear staining (Fig. 2d) with DAPI confirmed the effective removal of cellular components, with residual DNA reduced to 16.5 ± 2.8 ng/mg (from 336.3 ± 36.8 ng/mg before decellularization). This meets the stringent clinical threshold (< 50 ng/mg) for low immunogenicity [40], directly enhancing biocompatibility. More importantly, histological evaluation with H&E (Fig. 2b) and Masson staining (Fig. 2e) demonstrated preservation of collagen fibers and extracellular matrix (ECM) architecture, which is key to constructing a biomimetic microenvironment that supports BMSC adhesion, osteogenic differentiation, and aligns with research showing biomimetic matrices drive cell-matrix crosstalk and osteogenic pathways [1], representing a critical improvement over traditional scaffolds [34].

(b-e) H&E staining (cell removal), DNA quantification (336.3 ± 36.8 ng/mg vs. 16.5 ± 2.8 ng/mg, n = 3), DAPI (nuclei clearance), and Masson’s trichrome (ECM retention). Scale bars: 200 μm (b, e), 100 μm (d). (f-h) Porosity (59.0% vs. 76.7%, n = 3) and SEM microarchitecture (dense NFB vs. hierarchical porous DFB). Scale bars: 100 μm (left), 10 μm (right). (i) Compressive stress-strain curves (13.1 MPa vs. 4.2 MPa at 50% strain, n = 3). ***p < 0.001

Quantitative porosity analysis revealed a 78.3% void fraction in DFB (Fig. 2f), significantly exceeding that of NFB. SEM imaging (Fig. 2g, h) delineated a hierarchical pore structure spanning submicron to macroscopic scales (i.e., hundreds of nanometers to hundreds of micrometers), a feature inherently conducive to exosome encapsulation [33]. To further amplify the scaffold’s biological functionality for exosome loading and release, we functionalized DFB with a cationic PDL coating to yield PDL-DFB scaffolds [33]. As shown in Fig. S1, SEM confirmed that while PDL coating induced modest morphological changes to DFB’s pore structure, PDL-DFB retained its critical hierarchical porous architecture. This structural preservation is essential, as it maintains the interconnected channels derived from the scaffold’s high porosity and hierarchical structure, which are required for efficient exosome retention and sustained release [33, 41]. Complementarily, zeta potential analysis (Fig. S2) revealed a measurable increase in PDL-DFB’s surface potential compared to pristine DFB, with the isoelectric point shifting from approximately pH 4.5 to approximately pH 8.6. This positive shift arises from PDL’s cationic amine groups, a property well-documented in biomaterials research to strengthen electrostatic interactions with negatively charged biological entities, specifically enhancing the adsorption of negatively charged exosomes through electrostatic attraction with their lipid bilayers [33, 42]. This multiscale porosity works synergistically with PDL’s surface chemistry to boost exosome retention and sustained release [33], while the encapsulated exosomes, which are rich in osteogenic growth factors and microRNAs [24], overcome the constraints of traditional single-factor delivery systems [15]. Finally, mechanical testing (Fig. 2i) showed a compressive strength of 4.2 ± 0.4 MPa at 50% strain, within the functional range of cancellous bone (2–20 MPa) [43]. This mechanical harmony with native tissue ensures DFB endures biomechanical stresses during early regeneration, a pivotal factor for clinical translation, and avoids the mismatch issues of traditional scaffolds [44].

Biocompatibility of DFB

Biocompatibility, encompassing cytocompatibility and immunological tolerance, is a pivotal prerequisite for scaffold-based bone tissue engineering [9]. Adverse host responses can disrupt regeneration and compromise long-term implant integration, making it a critical determinant of therapeutic success in orthopedics [45]. For such applications, materials must simultaneously support cellular activity and evade excessive inflammation; these dual criteria underpin clinical translation [40].

To evaluate DFB’s biocompatibility, rat BMSCs exhibited robust adhesion on DFB surfaces, with normal cellular morphology, as confirmed by SEM (Fig. 3a). This behavior reflects the synergistic effect of DFB’s preserved biomimetic ECM architecture. Calcein-AM/PI viability staining further demonstrated negligible apoptotic activity in BMSCs following 2- and 4-day co-culture with DFB extracts (Fig. 3b, c), while CCK-8 assays confirmed proliferation rates consistent with untreated controls (Fig. 3d). This absence of cytotoxicity underscores the efficacy of our decellularization protocol, which removes immunogenic components without releasing harmful byproducts, a critical safety feature for clinical translation [46].

(b, c) Cell viability of BMSCs cultured with or without DFB extracts for 2 and 4 days was evaluated using Calcein-AM/PI staining. Scale bar: 50 μm. (d) CCK-8 proliferation kinetics of BMSCs (Control vs. DFB, n = 4). (e, f) H&E staining and CD68+ macrophage infiltration in subcutaneous tissues at 3 weeks post-implantation (Sham vs. Implant). All scale bars were 400 μm. (g) Histopathology of major organs (Sham vs. Implant). Scale bar: 400 μm

Subcutaneous implantation in rats provided in vivo validation by showing that macrophage infiltration in DFB-implanted groups was comparable to sham controls, as evidenced by H&E staining (Fig. 3e) and CD68+ immunohistochemical analysis (Fig. 3f). This response likely stems from DFB’s low immunogenicity, attributed to residual DNA levels below 50 ng/mg, which avoids triggering destructive pro-inflammatory cascades [47]. Importantly, systemic safety was confirmed via unaltered organ histopathology (Fig. 3g) and whole-blood profiles (Fig. S3), ruling out evidence of off-target toxicity. Collectively, these data, which span cellular compatibility to in vivo macrophage infiltration comparable to sham controls, validate DFB as a biocompatible scaffold meeting the stringent requirements of long-term orthopedic applications. By supporting cellular activity while mitigating adverse host responses, DFB demonstrates strong translational potential for bone regeneration.

Multifunctional properties of BMSC-derived exosomes

Bone regeneration is a coordinated process requiring the synergistic interplay of osteogenesis, angiogenesis, and BMSC migration, key challenges that single-factor therapies often fail to address due to their inability to recapitulate the complex signaling networks of natural healing [37, 39]. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have long been recognized for their role in tissue repair, primarily through paracrine secretion [33]; however, emerging evidence highlights exosomes as their primary functional mediators, offering advantages over whole-cell transplantation by avoiding immunogenicity and tumorigenic risks while preserving bioactivity [28, 48]. This positions exosomes as promising cell-free therapeutics, particularly for regenerative medicine applications demanding multi-modal regulation [22, 33]. To define a suitable preconditioning protocol for BMSC-derived exosomes, we first selected the osteogenic priming duration based on relevant literature [24, 49, 50], then assessed the oxygen concentration using our preliminary experimental data. For the priming duration, multiple studies support 7-day osteogenic priming as a well-validated and effective protocol, as exosomes from 7-day primed BMSCs exhibit stronger osteogenic capacity than unprimed (0-day) counterparts [49, 50], and exhibit improved angiogenic potential relative to those from shorter or longer priming periods [50]. Additionally, hypoxic preconditioning is reported to enhance exosomal angiogenic efficacy [23, 51]. Based on this established 7-day priming protocol, we further compared exosomes from BMSCs subjected to 7-day osteogenic priming under 1%, 5%, or 21% (normoxic) oxygen. Western blotting (Fig. S4) showed that 5% oxygen-derived exosomes exhibited the strongest osteogenic-promoting effect among the assessed conditions, as indicated by higher expression of RUNX2, COL1A1, and OPN. Thus, we isolated exosomes from BMSCs with 7-day osteogenic priming under 5% oxygen (HO-Exos) via differential ultracentrifugation [49].

Structural characterization of HO-Exos confirmed their identity through three key analyses. TEM revealed the typical cup-shaped morphology of exosomes (Fig. 4a), while nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) further showed a mean diameter of 95 nm (Fig. 4b), which is consistent with the established exosomal size range (30–150 nm) [52]. Western blotting additionally validated the enrichment of canonical exosomal markers (HSP70, TSG101, CD63) and the absence of cellular (β-actin) or endoplasmic reticulum (Calnexin) contaminants (Fig. 4c), ensuring purity for subsequent functional assays [53]. Beyond structural validation, additional Western blotting analysis (Fig. S5) clarified HO-Exos’ bioactive cargo, showing enrichment in BMP-4, BMP-2, VEGFA, and PDGF-BB compared to exosomes from normal (non-pretreated) BMSCs (hereafter N-Exos). These factors are well-documented in bone regeneration, and among these, BMP-4 and BMP-2 drive osteogenic differentiation [54], while VEGFA promotes endothelial tube formation [55] and PDGF-BB enhances mesenchymal cell migration [55], collectively providing a molecular basis for HO-Exos’ potential multifunctionality.

Functionally, these enriched factors likely underpin HO-Exos’ multifunctional properties. HO-Exos significantly enhanced BMSC migration, with a 76.4% higher scratch closure rate relative to controls (p < 0.05; Fig. 4d, e), an effect aligning with the pro-migratory role of PDGF-BB enriched in HO-Exos and consistent with reported BMSC-derived exosome-mediated migration promotion [56]. They also exerted robust angiogenic effects, inducing a 3.6-fold increase in HUVEC tube density (p < 0.001; Fig. S6a, b), a response attributed to VEGFA, whose capacity to stimulate angiogenesis in bone repair is well-established [55], and critical for supplying nutrients and osteogenic factors to regenerating bone [25]. Concurrently, HO-Exos stimulated osteogenesis in naive BMSCs, as evidenced by transcriptional upregulation of runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2), collagen type I alpha 1 (Col1a1), secreted phosphoprotein 1 (Spp1), and alkaline phosphatase (Alpl) (Fig. S7), elevated protein expression of RUNX2, COL1A1, OPN, and ALP (Fig. 4f, g), and increased ALP activity and calcium nodule deposition (Fig. 4h, i; Fig. S8). This osteogenic effect is likely supported by BMP-4 and BMP-2, which are canonical inducers of osteoblast differentiation and mineralization [54]. Notably, while the present study confirms the efficacy of 5% oxygen and 7-day osteogenic priming as suitable and effective based on existing literature and our oxygen concentration experiments, future work could explore a broader range of osteogenic priming durations and more refined oxygen concentration gradients to fully validate the optimal preconditioning window for HO-Exos production. Collectively, these data establish HO-Exos as bioactive nanovesicles that integrate osteogenesis, angiogenesis, and BMSC migration, addressing the limitations of single-factor therapies to provide a synergistic strategy for repairing complex bone defects.

HO-Exos enhance osteogenesis via Wnt/β-catenin signaling modulation

Building on the observed osteogenic promotion by HO-Exos, we further explored the underlying pathways through transcriptomic sequencing. Profiling of rat BMSCs treated with HO-Exos revealed extensive reprogramming of osteogenic pathways, with normalized expression distributions (Fig. 5a) and volcano plot filtering (|log2 FC| > 1, p < 0.05; 1,161 upregulated, 790 downregulated genes; Fig. 5b) confirming robust and reliable gene expression shifts. This large-scale transcriptional change reflects exosomes’ ability to orchestrate multi-level osteogenic programs beyond the scope of single-factor interventions. Hierarchical clustering (Fig. 5c) highlighted pronounced induction of matrix-remodeling genes including Col1a1, periostin (Postn), and biglycan (Bgn), alongside suppression of the mineralization inhibitor matrix Gla protein (Mgp) [57]. The upregulation of collagen-related genes (Col1a1, Postn) correlates with the critical role of ECM remodeling in early osteogenesis, providing a scaffold for subsequent mineralization [57].

Gene Ontology (GO) analysis (Fig. 5d) underscored biological processes (BP) associated with osteogenesis, such as “collagen fibril organization”, cellular components (CC) including the “extracellular matrix”, and molecular functions (MF) like “Wnt-protein binding”. These enrichments were corroborated by upregulation of fibrillin 1 (Fbn1) and secreted protein acidic and cysteine rich (Sparc), key regulators of matrix assembly and bone development that stabilize collagen networks and promote mineral deposition [58]. The enrichment of “Wnt-protein binding” implies direct engagement of exosomal cargoes with Wnt signaling components, a hallmark of osteogenic induction [59]. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis (Fig. 5e) identified Wnt signaling and ECM-receptor interaction as top canonical pathways, whereas TGF-β signaling exhibited moderate activation. Western blot (Fig. 5f) revealed elevated β-catenin and phosphorylated GSK-3β in HO-Exos-treated cells, suggesting that exosomes may modulate Wnt/β-catenin signaling by suppressing GSK-3β-dependent β-catenin degradation, a key step in committing BMSCs to osteogenic lineages [60].

Upregulation of Col1a1 and Postn (key regulators of collagen matrix architecture) aligns with enriched “response to mechanical stimulus” terms, indicating exosomes prime BMSCs to sense and adapt to mechanical cues, a critical factor in bone remodeling under physiological loading where matrix stiffness and strain modulate osteoblast activity [61]. Suppression of follistatin (Fst), a known inhibitor of osteogenesis, further supports this pro-differentiation shift [62]. By neutralizing the soluble inhibitor (Fst), exosomes create a permissive microenvironment for osteoblast differentiation. While β-catenin accumulation and GSK-3β phosphorylation suggest the potential involvement of the Wnt pathway [63], the present study lacks gain- or loss-of-function experiments (e.g., β-catenin siRNA knockdown for loss-of-function, β-catenin overexpression plasmid for gain-of-function, or Wnt inhibitor DKK-1 treatment [63]) to directly establish a causal link between Wnt/β-catenin signaling and HO-Exos-mediated osteogenesis, and comprehensive mechanistic validation (e.g., TOPFlash luciferase assay to quantify Wnt pathway activity [64], or inhibitor rescue experiments to verify causality) is warranted in future work to confirm this connection. Mgp suppression and ECM-receptor interaction upregulation highlight exosomes’ ability to synchronize matrix synthesis and mineralization, a dual mechanism essential for forming structurally sound new bone [65]. Notably, exosomal induction of Sparc and plasminogen activator, urokinase (Plau), modulators of matrix mineralization, implies exosomes’ involvement in balancing matrix degradation and deposition during bone remodeling, a process often dysregulated in non-union fractures [66]. Enriched “basement membrane” (CC) and “laminin binding” (MF) terms suggest exosomes enhance cell-ECM adhesion through laminin-mediated interactions, critical for stable retention and functional integration of endogenous BMSC-derived osteogenic lineage cells (HO-Exos-induced) in load-bearing defects where mechanical integrity is paramount [67]. These findings position HO-Exos as a promising tool for bone regeneration, though further studies must delineate Wnt signaling’s precise contribution (via the aforementioned gain- or loss-of-function approaches) and explore exosomal cargoes (e.g., miRNAs targeting GSK-3β or Wnt regulators).

Exosome-engineered scaffolds promote osteogenesis and angiogenesis in vitro

Bone tissue engineering scaffolds are pivotal for defect repair, yet the clinical translation of conventional scaffolds is hindered by limitations such as poorly controlled bioactive signaling kinetics and poor synchronization with native regenerative cascades [68]. Leveraging the biocompatibility of decellularized scaffolds and the osteoinductive capacity of HO-Exos, we developed a composite scaffold through exosome integration into DFB. DiD-labeled exosomes incubated with DFB exhibited robust fluorescence localized to scaffold surfaces (Fig. 6a), and SEM images further confirmed successful exosome loading by visualizing exosomes attached to the DFB surface (Fig. S9). Subsequent co-culture with BMSCs revealed rapid exosome internalization, as evidenced by intracellular DiD fluorescence at 6 h (Fig. 6b). Loading dynamics showed 43.6% ± 3.9% exosome adhesion within 24 h, with efficiency declining sharply thereafter (Fig. 6c). Sustained release kinetics indicated 73.7% ± 6.9% cumulative exosome release over 7 days (Fig. 6d), with ~60% released within the first 3 days. This initial burst is supported by evidence to rapidly activate osteogenic signaling pathways [24, 28] and recruit endogenous BMSCs [36], which are key processes to prime the regenerative microenvironment and initiate bone repair cascades [69], while mitigating concerns about rapid exosome clearance via the scaffold’s sustained release of bioactive cargo over the subsequent period [33, 36]. This biphasic release profile aligns with the temporal demands of bone repair, where the burst release provides an immediate bioactive cue to drive early-stage cell recruitment and osteogenic commitment [36, 69], whereas sustained release maintains prolonged signaling to support subsequent cell differentiation and robust angiogenesis [37], consistent with the known requirement for multi-stage coordinated signaling in bone regeneration [28, 36, 37]. Scratch assays (Fig. S10) further demonstrated markedly enhanced BMSC migration in the DFB-Exos group (54.8%) versus the DFB group (31.1%; p < 0.001), attributed to the sustained release of exosomes. In parallel, HUVEC tubulogenesis assays showed a 3.1-fold elevation in vascular tube density within the DFB-Exos group relative to the DFB alone group (p < 0.01; Fig. 6e, f). These observations highlight the dual functionality of exosomes in enhancing both cellular migration, a prerequisite for defect infiltration, and endothelial network formation, which supports nutrient delivery to regenerating tissue [70]. Collectively, these data confirm efficient exosome loading, controlled release, and functional cellular uptake.

Functional characterization of the DFB/HO-Exos system revealed synergistic osteoinduction across molecular hierarchies. The observed angiogenic enhancement likely stems from exosomal delivery of key pro-angiogenic factors (e.g., VEGFA, PDGF-BB), which are well-documented to activate PI3K/Akt and ERK signaling pathways via binding to their respective receptors on endothelial cells, thereby promoting endothelial cell proliferation and tube formation [44]. Coordinated upregulation of osteogenic regulators occurred at transcriptional levels (Runx2, Col1a1, Spp1, Alpl) with corresponding protein-level amplification (RUNX2, COL1A1, OPN, ALP), significantly surpassing unmodified DFB controls (Fig. 6g, h; Fig. S11). This dual osteogenic-angiogenic activity aligns with the natural bone repair cascade, where vascular invasion is known to precede and support osteoblast recruitment, creating a nutrient-rich microenvironment that facilitates matrix deposition [71], consistent with our observations of enhanced endothelial tubulogenesis and BMSC migration. This molecular priming translated to functional superiority, as evidenced by elevated ALP activity and accelerated extracellular matrix mineralization compared to baseline scaffolds (Fig. S12, Fig. 6i, Fig. S13). By integrating exosomal cargo with scaffold mechanics, the DFB-Exos system offers distinct advantages over existing mammalian-derived scaffold strategies, addressing zoonotic pathogen transmission risks, limited availability, and ethical concerns inherent to mammalian xenografts [13, 14]. It further leverages exosomal multi-signal synergy to overcome the single-factor delivery shortfall of conventional mammalian-derived scaffold systems [13, 28, 36], synergistically enhancing osteogenesis and angiogenesis [36, 50]. These findings not only validate the in vitro functionality of the DFB-Exos system but also highlight its potential to outperform conventional mammalian-derived scaffolds, laying a solid foundation for subsequent in vivo bone repair evaluation. The bioactivity enhancement underscores DFB’s dual role as a structural template and exosome reservoir, where controlled liberation kinetics synergize with exosomal signaling to enhance osteogenic differentiation. These findings advance scaffold design paradigms by demonstrating the feasibility of engineering matrix-exosome hybrids for targeted bone regeneration, a strategy with potential to address the unmet need for therapies recapitulating the complexity of natural healing [44].

DFB scaffolds enriched with HO-Exos promote femur defect repair in vivo

Critical-sized femoral defects, characterized by their inability to self-heal due to insufficient endogenous regenerative capacity, remain a major challenge in orthopedic surgery, with existing scaffolds often failing to recapitulate the spatiotemporal complexity of natural bone repair [43]. Building on the observed osteogenic potential in vitro, we systematically evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of exosome-functionalized DFB scaffolds in a rat critical-sized femoral metaphyseal defect model, which is widely adopted and well-recognized in vascularized bone regeneration research [5, 35, 44, 72] owing to its cancellous bone properties that facilitate evaluating bone regeneration [72]. This model was selected for its clinical relevance to isolated femoral metaphyseal defects [5] and alignment with our focus on angiogenic enhancement, with analyses covering early repair initiation to mid-to-late-stage functional maturation. While this model exhibits richer vascularity than mid-diaphyseal defects [5, 25, 72], it enables validation of our angiogenic claims in a clinically relevant scenario. To further rigorously confirm the scaffold’s angiogenic potential under more challenging hypovascular conditions, future studies will employ mid-diaphyseal femoral defects as recommended.

To characterize the temporal progression of bone repair, we tracked early initiation and intermediate stage development. At 1 week post-operation, CD90+ immunohistochemical staining (Fig. S14) confirmed robust recruitment of endogenous BMSCs in the DFB-Exos group, a pivotal step providing the cellular foundation for subsequent angiogenesis and osteogenesis [69]. As a well-recognized marker for rat BMSCs, CD90 identifies cells whose early recruitment drives regeneration by differentiating into osteoblasts and regulating vascular ingrowth [69, 70]. By 2 weeks, the DFB-Exos group reached peak CD31+ microvessel density (Fig. S15), significantly higher than other groups, while its RUNX2+ signals (early osteogenesis, Fig. S16) showed moderate upregulation with markedly higher levels than the weak signals in other groups. At 4 weeks, the DFB-Exos group exhibited a mild reduction in CD31+ density from its 2-week peak (Fig. S17) yet remained significantly higher than other groups. In contrast, its RUNX2+ expression (Fig. S18) showed a notable increasing trend compared to its 2-week levels and was significantly higher than those of other groups, while other groups showed only mild RUNX2+ upregulation, reflecting the transition from vascular initiation to dominant matrix deposition [69]. These findings confirm angiogenesis precedes osteogenesis, consistent with vascular perfusion supporting nutrient exchange and osteogenic signaling in early bone formation [5, 37].

By 8 weeks post-operation, mid-to-late-stage structural repair was evident. Micro-CT analysis (Fig. 7a) revealed markedly improved bone repair in the DFB-Exos group, in contrast to limited defect closure in controls, with quantitative metrics (BV/TV, Tb.Th, Tb.N, BMD) confirming superior regenerative outcomes (Fig. 7b). These parameters, strongly linked to bone mechanical competence, indicate the scaffold’s ability to recapitulate the hierarchical complexity of native bone, from trabecular architecture to mineral density [5]. Histomorphometric analysis (H&E and Masson’s trichrome staining, Fig. 7c) further showed substantial new bone deposition in the defect areas of the DFB-Exos group, characterized by mature lamellar architecture and collagen alignment, features that are essential for restoring bone mechanical strength [1]. This contrasted with the limited bone matrix deposition and extensive fibrous connective tissue infiltration observed in control groups. Immunofluorescence analysis (Fig. 8a) revealed synergistic enhancement of the osteogenic markers OPN and OCN, as well as the angiogenesis marker CD31, in the DFB-Exos group. Compared to control, OPN and OCN fluorescence intensities increased by 20.5-fold (p < 0.001) and 10.6-fold (p < 0.001), respectively (Fig. 8b, c). The Exos group alone exhibited a 175.6% elevation in CD31⁺ microvessel density (MVD) versus control (p < 0.001), while the DFB-Exos group achieved a 419.2% increase (p < 0.001) (Fig. 8d), demonstrating that the scaffold acts as an effective delivery system. By protecting exosomes from rapid clearance and enabling sustained release, DFB prolongs paracrine signaling to endothelial cells, thereby amplifying angiogenic outcomes beyond those of free exosomes [36]. Functional validation via 8-week compressive strength testing (Fig. S19) further confirmed the DFB-Exos group achieved significantly higher maximum compressive stress (15.8 ± 0.9 MPa) compared to control groups (8.7 ± 0.8 MPa, p < 0.001), verifying that structural repairs translated into functional integrity, a key indicator of successful bone regeneration [72].

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the DFB-Exos system orchestrates spatiotemporal repair dynamics, from early BMSC recruitment and angiogenesis through osteogenic amplification at the intermediate stage to mid-to-late-stage structural and functional maturation, mirroring natural bone regeneration. This synchronization overcomes the limitations of single-modal therapies, underscoring its potential as a translatable clinical strategy.

Conclusion

This study establishes a biomimetic bone regenerative platform through the synergistic integration of DFB with HO-Exos. The DFB scaffold, characterized by hierarchical porosity, provides a structural framework supporting BMSC adhesion and mineralization, while serving as a controlled-release reservoir to prolong exosomal bioactivity and overcome the rapid clearance of free exosomes. HO-Exos orchestrate coupled repair by facilitating BMSC migration, activating osteogenic differentiation via Wnt/β-catenin signaling modulation, and promoting endothelial angiogenesis. Their multicomponent composition overcomes the limitations of conventional single-factor approaches, enabling spatiotemporally synchronized osteogenic-angiogenic coupling that mimics the natural bone repair cascade. In critical-sized defects, the exosome-integrated matrix significantly enhanced bone regeneration and vascular network formation, achieving functional bone restoration through synchronized structural reconstruction and bioactive signaling. These findings demonstrate a clinically translatable strategy for complex osseous defect reconstruction, bridging bioinspired design with functional tissue regeneration.

Despite these promising findings, this study has two key limitations. First, the regenerative efficacy of the DFB-Exos platform was validated exclusively in a rat model, and its translational potential warrants further validation in large animal models. Second, mechanistic insights into the specific exosomal cargos mediating osteogenic-angiogenic coupling remain to be elucidated. Future studies will focus on large animal experiments to confirm clinical translatability and explore the key exosomal components (e.g., miRNAs, proteins) driving the observed synergistic repair effects.
