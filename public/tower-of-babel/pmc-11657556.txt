Association of meeting 24-hour movement guidelines with anxiety and depressive symptoms in adults
Authors: William R Tebar, Beatriz A S Aguilar, Leandro D Delfino, Victor S Beretta, Javier Brazo-Sayavera, Danilo R P Silva, Claudiele C M Silva, Gerson Ferrari, André O Werneck, Diego G D Christofaro
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThere is growing evidence on the association between meeting the recommendations of isolated movement behaviors (physical activity, sedentary time, and sleep) with anxiety and depressive symptoms. However, the joint association of meeting the 24 h movement guidelines with anxiety and depressive symptoms is still unknown. The aim of this study was to analyze the association of meeting 24-hour movement guidelines with symptoms of anxiety and of depression in adults. The sample included 212 participants. Anxiety and depressive symptoms were evaluated using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), while physical activity and sedentary time were evaluated by accelerometry, and daily slept hours were self-reported. Age, sex, socioeconomic status, accelerometer wear time, and waist circumference were covariates. Poisson regression models (crude and adjusted by covariates) were used to analyze association between variables. Meeting isolate recommendations of 24-hour movement guidelines were not associated with symptoms of anxiety or depression. However, meeting two or three recommendations was inversely associated with symptoms of anxiety (β= -0.235; 95%CI= -0.447; -0.024); (β= -0.569; 95%CI= -0.853; -0.285) and of depression (β= -0.275; 95%CI= -0.509; -0.040); (β= -0.551; 95%CI= -0.877; -0.224), respectively. Intervention strategies for reducing anxiety and depressive symptoms should target the meeting of more than one movement guidelines, such as increasing physical activity, reducing sedentary time, and promoting adequate sleep time for adults.Keywords: Mental health, Epidemiology, Physical activity, Sedentary behavior, Sleep

Introduction

The prevalence of mental disorders has been increasing along the years, with an estimated increase of 35% in anxiety disorders and a 39% increase in depressive disorders between 1990 and 2019 [1], being recognized as an important public health problem of the 21st century, even before the covid-19 pandemic [2, 3].

Although enduring factors such as economic inequality and chronic stress have consistently contributed to mental disorders across the years [4–7], previous studies reported that increased treatment seeking may be one reason why these disorders appear more frequent, but this does not necessarily indicate an actual increase in prevalence [8]. Additionally, the increase in mental disorders between 1990 and 2019 may be partially explained by updates in diagnostic criteria, with broadened definitions especially in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders and in the International Classification of Diseases, as well as improved healthcare training and expanded access to services, leading to capture more cases [9, 10]. Nevertheless, more recent drivers of poor mental health such as digitalization and heightened social isolation contributed to enhance mental disorders in the years following 2019, exacerbated by the emergence of Covid-19 pandemic [7, 9, 10]. These factors reflect an interplay between genuine increases in mental disorders and evolving approaches to recognition and diagnosis over the years.

Anyway, the anxiety and depressive disorders were among the major causes of disability worldwide, with association with several non-communicable diseases, including cardiovascular [11–13]. Among the protective factors for anxiety and depression, previous studies have consistently reported that movement behaviors (i.e., physical activity, adequate sleep time, and lower sedentary time) are associated with lower anxiety and depression [14–18]. For instance, there is consistent evidence showing the role of meeting the World Health Organization’s recommendation of ≥ 150 min of moderate to vigorous physical activity (MVPA) in preventing depression [15], as well as anxiety [14]. In addition, sedentary time is the time an individual spends in sedentary behavior, characterized by activities that require 1.0 to 1.5 METS (metabolic equivalents) in a sitting or reclining posture [19], and is also consistently associated with depression and anxiety [16, 17]. Both short and long sleep duration is also prospectively associated with both anxiety and depression [18, 20].

Nevertheless, most of the previous studies analyzed each exposure related to movement behaviors separately or adjusting for the other movement behaviors. As the movement behaviors are cumulative, reducing time in one movement behavior means the increase in the others. In this line, the Canadian 24-hour movement behavior guidelines were the first to integrate recommendations on physical activity (PA), sedentary time and sleep [21]. According to the Canadian 24-hour movement behavior guidelines [21], a healthy routine for adults between 18 and 64 years of age includes at least 150 min of MVPA per week, having 7 to 9 h of quality sleep, and sedentary time of up to 8 h a day, limiting screen-time during leisure to a maximum of 3 h. Some systematic review and longitudinal studies have observed that children and adolescents who meet the 24-hour movement guidelines have better health indices such as lower depressive symptoms, less adiposity, better cardiorespiratory condition, quality of life, emotional and social health [22–24].

Recent studies analyzed the association of meeting the 24-hour movement guidelines and different mental health-related outcomes among adults. Adults who meet the 24-hour movement guidelines have better levels of emotional health, as well as lower levels of stress [24, 25]. In separate analyses, higher PA intensity, lower time sitting (< 8 h/day) during the day and sleep duration within the 7–9 h guidelines were positively associated with well-being in Australian adults [26]. In addition, a previous study found that meeting the 24-hour movement guideline from adolescence to adulthood was associated with lower incidence of depression [7]. However, no cross-sectional association between the 24-hour movement guideline (including MVPA, light physical activity, sedentary time and sleep) and symptoms of anxiety and depression was observed in working adults [27].

However, important gaps remain, as most of the previous evidence are composed of non-probabilistic samples from high-income countries, which received more treatment for mental health than low- and middle-income countries [28]. Studies of this nature in low- and middle-income countries, including Brazil, can contribute to the development of practical strategies in the prevention and treatment of mental disorders, as these countries face unique socioeconomic, cultural, and environmental factors that influence mental health and lifestyle habits like physical activity, sedentary behavior, and sleep. Differences in access to recreational facilities, healthcare, and mental health services could alter the relationship between movement behaviors and mental health. Additionally, residents of low- and middle-income countries often rely more on physical activity to cope with stress due to limited mental health care and may also experience more stressors, including poverty and violence, which could either weaken or strengthen the protective effects of these behaviors. It is important to highlight that most of the previous studies used self-reported assessments of PA and sedentary time, where device-based methods for movement assessment are still warranted to be better reliable to understand the association with health outcomes. Therefore, our aim was to analyze the association of meeting the 24-hour movement guidelines assessed by device-measured physical activity and sedentary behavior with symptoms of anxiety and depression among community-dwelling adults.

Methods

Study design

This is an epidemiological observational study with a cross-sectional design. Adults with 18 years and more were enrolled to participate. The sample was recruited in the city of Santo Anastacio, an inner city located in the Southeastern region of Brazil, with about 16,000 inhabitants with ≥ 18 years old. The study was previously approved by the ethics committee of São Paulo State University (protocol CAAE: 72191717.9.0000.5402) and the research protocol was prospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03986879, registered in 2019-06-11).

Sample

For this study, the sample calculation proposed by Miot [29] was performed, considering an r = 0.23 (Morres et al. 2019), a statistical power of 80% and an alpha error of 5%, resulting in a minimum number of 147 participants. Considering possible losses, 30% was added, resulting in minimum sample size of 192 participants.

A probabilistic sampling process was performed for participants recruitment. The city was divided into census tracts and later in neighborhoods, streets and households, which were all randomized to receive the visit of researchers and invitation to participate. From this, all adults with 18 years and more from the selected houses were considered eligible. Participants who agreed to participate provided a signed consent form. The following inclusion criteria were adopted: aged ≥ 18 years; agreeing to use the accelerometer and to answer the questionnaire, having no kind of cognitive impairment and not be pregnant. The following exclusion criteria were adopted: not using the accelerometer for at least 4 days, 10 h a day; not completing the body assessment; not fill the questionnaire, taking any antidepressant and/or anxiolytic medication. More detailed information about study protocol was previously published [30].

Physical activity and sedentary time

Physical activity and sedentary time were device-measured by Actigraph GT3X accelerometer (ActiGraph, LLC, Pensacola, FL, USA). Participants were asked to wear the device for seven consecutive days during the entire waking period, removing it only when in contact with water and for sleeping, except for daytime naps. An explanatory leaflet was demonstrated and delivered with the correct positioning of the device at the waistline and on the right side. A strategy used to minimize the insufficient use of the device was sending text messages to the participants in the morning, reminding them to use it during the scheduled days.

The period of minimum 4 days of device use with at least 10 h in a row was considered as sufficient device use for reliable field-based measurements of sedentary behavior and physical activity in populational studies [31, 32], being considered a 60-second epoch to provide output accelerometry data into minutes [33]. Periods with ≥ 60 min of consecutive zero counts were considered as non-wear time and excluded from total amount [34]. The cutoff point of less than 100 counts per minute was adopted to define sedentary time, as proposed by Troiano [35].

The physical activity was classified as light intensity if < 1952 counts per minute (< 3.00 METs), moderate intensity from 1952 to 5724 counts per minute (3.00-5.99 METs) and vigorous intensity if 5725 and more counts per minute (≥ 6.00 METs) [36].

Sleep

Sleep duration was assessed using the question “During the past month, how many hours of sleep did you get each night? from the Pittsburgh questionnaire [37], validated for Brazilian adults aged 18 to 65 years [38].

Compliance with the 24-hour movement guidelines

Participants were further classified according to each of the three items from the Canadian 24-hour movement guidelines (physical activity, sedentary time and sleep): (1) at least 150 min/week of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity; (2) Less than 8 h/day for sedentary time; (3) Sleep time between 7 and 9 h per night [21]. Cluster of meeting the 24-hour movement guidelines was created, where the participants were classified as not meeting any guideline, meeting one, meeting two or meeting the three guidelines. The Canadian 24-hour movement guidelines were chosen to be the first guideline to include time-specific recommendation simultaneously for the three behaviors (moderate-to-vigorous physical activity, sedentary behavior and sleep), which has been used worldwide, including in Brazil [39] and Latin-America [40–42].

Anxiety and depressive symptoms

The Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale – HADS [43] was used to assess anxiety and depressive symptoms in the sample. This instrument was validated for the Brazilian language and population by Botega [44]. The HADS questionnaire consists of 7 questions for Anxiety Symptoms (HADS-A) and 7 questions for Depression Symptoms (HADS-D), considering the last week for recall. The responses in a 4-point Likert scale ranged from 0 (best symptom) to 3 (worst symptom) [29]. This questionnaire was answered by the participants themselves in order to avoid discomfort between the participant and the evaluator. The score of each question was summed to obtain the total score of anxiety (HADS-A) and the total score of depression (HADS-D) separately.

Covariates

Sex, age, socioeconomic status, accelerometer wear time, and waist circumference were included as adjustment variables. Socioeconomic status was assessed using the Brazil Criteria for Economic Classification [45], which categorizes subjects into upper, middle and lower classes through questions about education level, consumer goods and number of rooms. Waist circumference was measured at the middle point between the last rib and iliac crest, with an inextensible tape for 3 times in a row by the same evaluator and the average of the three measurements was considered as the final value.

Statistical analysis

The characteristics of the sample were described using values of relative frequency, mean and standard deviation. Considering that both anxiety and depressive symptoms are count variables with skewed distribution, we used Poisson regression with log-link function to estimate the association of meeting the 24 h movement guidelines with the symptoms of anxiety and of depression. We created two models, one crude and another adjusting for sex, age, socioeconomic status, accelerometer wear time, and waist circumference. For the primary analysis, it was created a clustered model with the six following subgroups: (i) not meeting any guideline; (ii) meeting only the physical activity guideline; (iii) meeting only the sedentary behavior guideline; (iv) meeting only the sleep guideline; (v) meeting two of the three guidelines; and (vi) meeting all the three guidelines. In secondary analysis, we created specific combination models, by using the group who did not meet any guideline as reference and comparing against the three possible combinations of meeting guidelines: (i) meeting physical activity + sedentary behavior guidelines; (ii) meeting physical activity + sleep guidelines; and (iii) meeting sedentary behavior + sleep guidelines. The exponentiation of log-transformed beta coefficients was used to normal scale transformation. The analyzes were conducted using the software Stata 15.1.

Results

Our initial sample was composed of 262 adults. However, after exclusion for missing data of any included variable, the final sample of the present study was 212 adults, being 55.2% of men. Individually, the proportion of participants who met 24-hour movement guidelines was 44.8% for physical activity, 47.2% for sedentary behavior, and 53.3% for sleep. Discussion

Our main findings were that the isolate meeting of guidelines for PA, sedentary time or sleep time was not associated with anxiety and depressive symptoms. However, participants who met at least two out of the three Canadian 24-hour movement guidelines presented from 20.9 to 43.4% lower symptoms of anxiety and from 24.0 to 42.4% lower symptoms of depression, when compared with participants who did not meet any guideline. The specific combination analysis showed that meeting any combination of two guidelines (physical activity and sedentary behavior, physical activity and sleep, and sedentary behavior and sleep) was associated with at least 20.0% lower scores of anxiety and depressive symptoms. These findings indicate a possible additive interaction between movement behaviors in the association with symptoms of anxiety and of depression.

There are still no published studies investigating specifically the relationship of meeting the 24-hour movement guidelines with symptoms of anxiety and of depression in Brazilian adults. However, a previous cohort study found that meeting the 24-hour movement guidelines during adolescence was associated with lower incident suicidal ideation [46], which is a common symptom of mood disorders. Similarly, meeting two or three movement guidelines was associated with lower stress perception among adults [25], and lower depressive symptoms during the COVID-19 outbreak [47].

Our finding that meeting each movement behavior alone was not associated with anxiety and depression symptoms is in line with other studies analyzing the 24-hour movement guidelines worldwide [25, 46, 47]. However, previous studies have demonstrated the role of each movement behavior in relation to mental health, where physical activity has been associated to lower symptoms of anxiety and depression [48], contributing to improved mood and mental well-being [49]. In turn, high sedentary time has been associated with increased symptoms of anxiety and depression [50–52], as well as inadequate sleep duration was an independent predictor of developing mental disorders, particularly anxiety and depression [53, 54].

These divergent results may be related to the cumulative nature of movement behaviors and the possible synergistic association of them with mental health-related outcomes when analyzed in combination. For instance, Kandola et al. [55] observed that replacing 60 min of sedentary time with MVPA and sleep time was associated with lower symptoms of anxiety and of depression in adults. It is possible that there is a cumulative effect of the benefits of each healthy behavior, which would explain the significant effect found only with the clustered behaviors in the present study, meeting at least two from the three guidelines, by supporting the idea that movement behaviors are interrelated, and their combined association with mental health could be greater than the sum of their parts. For example, it has been reported that physical activity can counteract the negative effects of sedentary behavior on mental health through relatively complex ways, like the inhibition or stimulation of neurobiomolecules, enhancement of blood and cellular metabolic processes, and strengthened functional connectivity of the brain [56]. Werneck et al. [57] observed that physical activity plays a moderator role between device-measured sedentary time and depressive symptoms in adults. It is worth noting that both physical activity and sedentary behavior effects on mental health can be domain-dependent, while physical activity at leisure-time has been associated with lower depressive symptoms [48] the mentally passive sedentary behavior showed to be higher detrimental for psychological health [58].

Regarding the interrelationship between physical activity and sleep on mental disorders, Zhang et al. [59] reported that poor sleepers with moderate and high physical activity levels reported fewer negative emotions than those with low physical activity levels. Anxiety and depressive symptoms were serial mediators in the relationship between physical activity and sleep quality, highlighting the need to focus on both improving sleep and increasing physical activity for a multi-faceted approach to mental disorders [60], since physical activity and sedentary behavior were different behaviors that alternate throughout the day, so that high sedentary behavior levels can be presented even while meeting global physical activity levels [61].

The different behaviors from the 24-hour movement guidelines can share with each other the mechanisms linking to symptoms of anxiety and of depression. First, physical activity, sedentary time and adequate sleep are associated with neuroplasticity, through functional and structural changes in the hippocampus [62, 63], which may occur through changes in the metabolic profile and gut microbiota [64, 65]. Second, the components of movement guidelines have been associated with inflammation [66–68], which is associated with symptoms of anxiety and of depression [69, 70]. Third, the movement behaviors are associated with the regulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary adrenal axis, which is linked with immunity regulation and consequently, anxiety and depressive symptoms [68, 71]. The link of these mechanisms and the synergy of meeting the three 24-hour movement guidelines could be associated with the different mechanisms linking to anxiety and depressive symptoms.

As strengths of this study, we highlight the device-based measurement of PA and sedentary time, which precludes the memory bias for the estimation of duration and physical activity intensities, as well as the sedentary time amount. As main limitations, the present study has a cross-sectional design that prevents the analysis of causal relationships between the Canadian 24-hour movement guidelines and anxiety and depressive symptoms. The period in which the device was not worn could potentially skew the sedentary time and consequently the determination of meeting the sedentary behavior guideline. Another weakness of the study was the self-reported assessment of sleep time and the period of the last month for recall, compared to the last week period for physical activity and sedentary behavior assessment, precluding composition considerations. Lastly, the screening nature of the HADS, designed to identify participants at high risk for anxiety and depression, does not allow for definitive conclusions regarding a reduction in diagnoses. While lower HADS symptom scores may preliminarily suggest an improved clinical condition, further investigation is required to determine whether this correlates with a reduced risk of diagnosis.

In summary, participants who met two or all the three recommendations from the Canadian 24-hour movement guidelines had lower anxiety and depressive symptoms. Future studies with prospective design are warranted to confirm the longitudinal association of concurrently meeting the 24-hour movement guidelines with anxiety and depressive symptoms among adults, considering the 24-hour activity completely device-measured and preventing the accelerometer from measuring light physical activities as sedentary activities due to not consider body posture, by including complimentary devices like inclinometer.
