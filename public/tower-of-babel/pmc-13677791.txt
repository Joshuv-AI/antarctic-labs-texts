Paradoxical energetics in the polar diatom Fragilariopsis cylindrus exposed to extreme low light
Authors: Arthur Plassart, Nathalie Joli, Sneha Sivaram, Sébastien Guérin, Flavienne Bruyant, Marie-Hélène Forget, Chris Bowler, Marcel Babin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIt is generally considered that Arctic diatoms endure total darkness during polar winters by shifting to a hypometabolic state based on internal reserve mobilization. They can nonetheless rapidly initiate under-ice blooms at extreme low-light levels in early spring. The limits and mechanisms underlying this ability remain unknown. To explore the absolute minimum light required for photoautotrophic growth, we investigated the physiological and transcriptional state of the polar diatom Fragilariopsis cylindrus across a winter-to-spring light gradient. While cell division is arrested at 0.18 µmol photons m−2 s−1, photosynthetic electron transport remains operational down to 0.1 µmol photons m−2 s−1, which suggests an uncoupling between photosynthesis and biomass accumulation. Crucially, nondividing cells do not enter hypometabolism but instead maintain a functional photosynthetic metabolism without reserve consumption, redefining the lower limit of photoautotrophic life. While cells optimize photon capture efficiency between 3 and 15 µmol photons m−2 s−1 relative to 30 µmol photons m−2 s−1, this strategy collapses below 1 µmol photons m−2 s−1. In this dim-light regime, cells activate nonphotochemical quenching and a sustained xanthophyll cycle, which indicates a paradoxical requirement for energy dissipation. We propose that at these extremely low light levels, light absorption appears to exceed the minimum energy requirements for cell maintenance. To manage this active imbalance, cells dissipate excess energy as heat through active nonphotochemical quenching and possibly carbohydrate secretion, indicating a paradoxical requirement for energy dissipation despite extreme photon scarcity. This mechanism allows them to maintain a primed photosynthetic metabolism to facilitate rapid growth recovery upon the return of light after the winter solstice.

Introduction

The irradiance threshold at which photosynthetic reactions and carbon fixation exactly balance respiratory carbon losses is commonly known as the light compensation point and defines the absolute threshold beyond which net photoautotrophic growth becomes possible (Falkowski and Raven 2007). In aquatic ecology, this physiological threshold is of crucial importance, as it not only determines the depth-integrated zone able to support photosynthetic cell division, but also dictates the phenology of unicellular algal blooms, particularly in polar regions (Banse 2004; Marra 2004; Sverdrup 1953). While physiological strategies enabling optimal performance under over-saturating light conditions are now well documented, the fundamental cellular mechanisms at the other end of the irradiance range, near the suboptimal limit where light becomes very scarce, remain largely unexplored. Nevertheless, in the Arctic, recent field observations confirm net phototrophic accumulation under sea ice at irradiances far below the conventional paradigm, with division rates sustained between 0.04 and 0.8 µmol photons m−2 s−1 (Hancke et al. 2018; Kvernvik et al. 2018; Randelhoff et al. 2020; Hoppe et al. 2024). These findings reveal that polar phytoplankton reinitiate growth under conditions of near-total darkness, suggesting photosynthetic activity occurring close to the theoretical minimum quantum threshold required for oxygenic photosynthesis, modeled at 0.01 µmol of photons m−2 s−1 (Raven et al. 2000). Diatoms dominate these unicellular sympagic (ice-associated) and pelagic communities and play a central role in polar ecosystems through both their abundance and productivity (Lovejoy et al. 2002; Poulin et al. 2011; Alou-Font et al. 2013; Leu et al. 2015; Lafond et al. 2019; Ardyna and Arrigo 2020). Despite their ecological importance, the physiological limits of photosynthetic growth at extremely low irradiance remain unknown for these, and other, unicellular photoautotrophs found in Arctic ecosystems and more broadly in aquatic ecosystems.

Existing paradigms of photoacclimation derive largely from studies at moderate low-light irradiance in unicellular algae (typically ∼5 to 15 µmol photons m−2 s−1). In these regimes, unicellular algae such as diatoms maximize photon capture by increasing the cellular content of photosynthetic pigments, thereby improving photosynthetic efficiency (Falkowski and Owens 1980; Laws and Bannister 1980; Geider et al. 1985; Dubinsky et al. 1986; Geider et al. 1986; Quigg et al. 2006; Suggett et al. 2007; Arrigo et al. 2010; Halsey and Jones 2015; Croteau et al. 2021; Agarwal et al. 2023). Furthermore, they simultaneously downregulate respiratory costs to lower the compensation point for growth (Fisher et al. 1989; Quigg and Beardall 2003; Dubinsky and Stambler 2009; Halsey and Jones 2015). In sharp contrast, the response to complete darkness involves a fundamental shift toward hypometabolism, which relies on the consumption of endogenous reserves. Under these conditions, specifically during the polar night, Arctic diatoms arrest their cell cycle, degrade photosynthetic machinery, and actively catabolize intracellular reserves (such as lipids and proteins) via β-oxidation and autophagy to sustain essential housekeeping functions sustained by the Alternative Oxidase pathway (Schaub et al. 2017; Kennedy et al. 2019; Lacour et al. 2019; Morin et al. 2020; Sciandra et al. 2022; Juchem et al. 2023; Joli et al. 2024). Between these 2 extremes in light conditions (darkness, and low to high light), very low light remains a regime where photosynthesis is largely unexplored, yet it is highly relevant to the under-ice polar environment. Specifically, it remains unclear whether cells maintain phototrophic growth in a dim-light regime (below 5 µmol photons m−2 s−1) or if they perceive scarce photons as total darkness and thus trigger the dark-acclimation processes described above. As Arctic ice cover declines in extent, thickness and seasonal persistence due to climate change, light conditions will change significantly in the Arctic Ocean (Lewis et al. 2020; Castellani et al. 2022; Kristiansen et al. 2025). Consequently, an understanding of the physiological limits and responses of these key diatom producers is critical to predict future polar ecosystem dynamics.

To determine the absolute lower limit of photosynthetic growth, we investigate the steady-state physiological and molecular responses of the polar diatom Fragilariopsis cylindrus after full acclimation to a range of low light intensities. These intensities range from an extreme dim-light regime (0.1 to 1 µmol photons m−2 s−1) to low light (3 to 15 µmol photons m−2 s−1) and a moderate condition (30 µmol photons m−2 s−1), representative of under-ice light levels between the winter solstice and early spring conditions (Cohen et al. 2020; Massicotte et al. 2020). Through the integration of growth kinetics, photophysiology, and metabolic profiles, we designed our experiments specifically to capture the physiological threshold that separates light-limited growth from growth arrest. Our results reveal 2 divergent acclimation strategies. While cells optimize photon capture efficiency under low light, this classical photoacclimation response collapses in the dim-light regime. Indeed, under these conditions, cells neither divide nor enhance light harvesting. Rather, they activate nonphotochemical quenching (NPQ) and a sustained xanthophyll cycle. In the context of the extreme Arctic light cycle, this distinct dim-light strategy may be central to the ecological success of polar diatoms, allowing them to resume growth immediately once light levels exceed the compensation threshold and thereby preventing the lag phases associated with recovery from deep hypometabolism.

Results

Light below the compensation point maintains viability but induces cell cycle arrest

F. cylindrus cultures were beforehand acclimated to 6 continuous light levels ranging from 0.1 to 30 µmol photons m−2 s−1 for 21 d. During this period, we measured cell density, chlorophyll content and basic photophysiology parameters. The growth rate (μ) followed a typical logarithmic light-dependent response, peaking at a maximum rate (μmax) of 0.10 ± 0.01 d−1 at 30 µmol photons m−2 s−1 (Fig. 1a, Fig. S1 and Table S1). The light saturation parameter for growth (EK), which marks the transition between light-limited and saturating conditions, was estimated at 4.71 ± 1.74 µmol photons m−2 s−1 (Fig. S1 and Table S1). This EK value is low but typical of values reported for polar taxa (Lacour et al. 2017). The monitoring of cell density indicates that while cultures at 0.3 µmol photons m−2 s−1 maintained a modest but positive and significant growth, cell division ceased at 0.1 µmol photons m−2 s−1 (Fig. 1a and Table S2). Although a visual observation of the growth rate might suggest an apparent division rate slightly above zero (on average), which would scale about 3-fold light reduction from the 0.3 μmol photons m−2 s−1 treatment, this rate is not statistically different from zero (Table S1). Nevertheless, we cannot rule out the possibility that a residual, extremely low division rate exists at 0.1 μmol photons m−2 s−1, but remains below our statistical detection limit due to experimental constraints in replicate number and monitoring time relative to such prolonged cell cycles. We estimated, using a mathematical model, a light compensation point for growth (Ic) of 0.18 ± 0.11 µmol photons m−2 s−1. This theoretical threshold aligns closely with the observed experimental arrest of cell division (Fig. 1a). Despite the arrest of growth at the lowest irradiances, cell viability remained high (>90%) across all treatments (Fig. 1b). Furthermore, since the dark-acclimated quantum yield for photochemistry in PSII (Fv/Fm) did not show a drastic drop with decreasing light, these cells did not appear to be in a significant physiological stress (Fig. 1c). Morphologically, cell size progressively decreased with increasing irradiance from 0.1 to 15 µmol photons m−2 s−1, before increasing at the highest growth irradiance (30 µmol photons m−2 s−1) to reach values comparable to those under dim-light conditions (≤0.3 µmol photons m−2 s−1; Fig. 1d and Fig. S2a). Transcriptional profiles show that the machinery required for DNA replication, such as the origin recognition complex (ORC) and the proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA), was maintained even at the growth-arrest irradiance of 0.1 µmol photons m−2 s−1. Similarly, transcripts for general cell cycle regulators, including the Skp1/Cullin/F-box complex (SCF), structural maintenance of chromosomes (SMC) proteins, and the Anaphase-Promoting Complex core subunits (APC/C) with its co-activator CDH1, were also stable (Fig. S3a). In contrast, the transcript level of CDC20 genes was almost undetectable, which suggests that the specific absence of this component may contribute to the arrest of cell division in dim-light conditions (Fig. S3a). Interestingly, a similar observation can be made in cells of F. cylindrus acclimated to a prolonged period of darkness (Fig. S3b), based on data obtained from the study by Joli et al. (2024), where the cells entered a state of quiescence without cell division after 1 mo of complete darkness (D1M in Fig. S3b).

Dim light induces global metabolic slowdown and EPS production rather than metabolic reorganization and reserve consumption

Biovolume-normalized quotas of organic carbon (QVC) and nitrogen (QVN) remained stable across the light gradient (Fig. 2a and 2b), which indicates that cells maintained stable internal C and N organic pools even at growth-arrest irradiance. This observation argues against an endogenous catabolic shift driven by the degradation of organic reserves to sustain basal metabolism, a strategy previously reported in F. cylindrus after exposure to prolonged darkness (Kennedy et al. 2019; Morin et al. 2020; Sciandra et al. 2022; Joli et al. 2024). Although quantifying such low oxygen fluxes at 0 °C and in cells with very low metabolic activity pushes the detection limits of standard methods, the available measurements show that carbon-normalized respiration dropped ∼9-fold, from 0.36 ± 0.14 µmol O2 mg C−1 h−1 at 30 µmol photons m−2 s−1 to a minimal basal rate of 0.04 ± 0.01 µmol O2 mg C−1 h−1 at 0.1 µmol photons m−2 s−1 (Fig. S4b). Consistently, transcriptomic profiles indicated that the central metabolic architecture remained remarkably active at the transcriptional level under all light conditions (Fig. 3). Indeed, although a general decrease in the overall abundance of transcripts was observed at 0.1 µmol photons m−2 s−1 compared with higher-light, growth-supporting conditions (1 to 30 µmol photons m−2 s−1) (Z-score values in Fig. S5, and count in Figs. S6 and S7), the absolute expression levels of genes within the main metabolic pathways remained highly active and their distribution largely identical across the light gradient (Fig. 3 and bubble size in Fig. S5). This relative decrease in the quantity of transcripts, without the transcriptional silencing of specific pathways, is consistent with an overall metabolic slowdown rather than a qualitative reorganization of cellular metabolism. Notably, genes associated with the Calvin-Benson-Bassham (CBB) cycle, lipid biosynthesis, and mitochondrial β-oxidation remained actively transcribed even under the lowest light conditions despite their intensity downregulation (Fig. 3, Figs. S5 and S7). This suggests that central anabolic and catabolic pathways remain functional despite growth arrest in the dimmest light regime. Microscopy highlighted a distinct phenotypic shift at low light, characterized by increased cell aggregation (Fig. S2b and c). Accordingly, genes within Extracellular Polymeric Substance (EPS) biosynthesis pathways were upregulated in growth-arrested cells (Figs. S5 and S8). This response aligns with strategies commonly reported in sea ice diatoms under extreme environmental constraints (Underwood et al. 2010; Aslam et al. 2012, 2018). Collectively, these results indicate that F. cylindrus does not undergo a major metabolic reconfiguration under dim light but instead maintains its core metabolic machinery in a low-activity, homeostatic state.

Photosynthetic efficiency collapses under dim light despite the maintenance of core machinery

Photosynthetic pigments exhibited marked variations in response to changes in light availability. Quotas of chlorophyll a (Chl a) and accessory pigments (fucoxanthin (Fx) and chlorophyll c1 + c2) were approximately 2.4-fold higher in low-light acclimated cells (3 to 15 µmol photons m−2 s−1) compared with values at 30 µmol photons m−2 s−1 (Fig. 2c), which is a typical low-light photoacclimation strategy (Falkowski and Owens 1980; Quigg et al. 2006; Dubinsky and Stambler 2009; Kropuenske et al. 2009; Arrigo et al. 2010; Agarwal et al. 2023). This accumulation was accompanied by a shift in pigment stoichiometry, with a slight increase in Fx relative to Chl a (Fig. S9a). At the transcriptional level, this low-light response (3 and 1 µmol photons m−2 s−1) was associated with the specific induction of genes that encode lhcf25 (ProtID: 193422) and lhcf26 (ProtID: 195777) (Fig. 4a). These adjustments consistently correlated with the increase in the carbon-specific initial slope (αC) of the photosynthesis-irradiance response (PE) curve at 1 to 15 µmol photons m−2 s−1, a clear sign of optimization in light harvesting for photochemistry (Fig. 2f). This photoacclimation strategy was, however, not found in dim-light conditions. While pigment quotas at 0.1 µmol photons m−2 s−1 were similar to those at 30 µmol photons m−2 s−1 (Fig. 2c), the αC coefficient and the light-saturated carbon-specific photosynthetic rate (Pmc) declined dramatically (Figs. 2f and 5a and Fig. S10a and b). Whilst the maximum carbon-specific photosynthetic rate (PmC) reached the lowest values at 0.1 μmol photons m−2 s−1, a decrease in PmC was also observed at the highest light intensity (30 μmol photons m−2 s−1), even though the cultures reached their maximum growth rate (Fig. 1a, Figs. S1 and S10a). However, when normalized by chlorophyll a (Pmchla) or per cell (Pmcell), the maximum photosynthetic capacity remained constant across the entire range from 0.3 to 30 μmol photons m−2 s−1 (Fig. S10c and e). This result indicates, respectively, that the catalytic capacity per unit of light capture is maintained and the amount of Rubisco per cell also remains constant. However, it is important to note that the rapid metabolic turnover of a transiently fixed carbon pool shown in temperate species under nitrogen limitation could potentially lead to a slight underestimation of the absolute maximal carbon fixation rates in our conditions (Halsey et al. 2010, 2011, 2013, 2014). The linear relationship between αC and photosynthetic pigments (PS:C ratio) found at 3 to 15 µmol photons m−2 s−1 broke down at 0.1 µmol photons m−2 s−1 (Fig. 2f), with a loss of light use efficiency for photochemistry despite the maintenance of the pigment pool and core machinery transcripts. This physiological collapse contrasts with the transcriptomic stability of the core photosynthetic machinery. The nuclear genes encoding for reaction center cores (PSII and PSI), the oxygen-evolving complex (OEC), PSII repair and assembly mechanisms, and the entire thylakoid electron carriers displayed relatively comparable transcript levels across all light conditions (Fig. S11). This suggests that the photosynthetic machinery is maintained but functionally decoupled regarding the maximal fixation capacity observed (Pmc, Fig. S10). Additionally, genes for the different types of antennae showed a distinct signature under reduced light. The majority of lhcf genes (PSII antennae) were downregulated at 0.1 µmol photons m−2 s−1 while lhcr gene transcripts (PSI antennas) remained significantly more abundant than lhcf transcripts (Fig. 4a and Fig. S12).

Paradoxically active xanthophyll cycle and strong NPQ induction under dim light

The loss of photosynthetic efficiency at 0.1 µmol photons m−2 s−1 coincided with an activation of photoprotective mechanisms. While light-harvest pigment quotas increased with decreasing light intensity and remained stable in the dim-light condition, xanthophyll cycle pigments showed the opposite trend (Fig. 2d). The total pool of diadinoxanthin (Dd) and diatoxanthin (Dt) declined by ∼30% as irradiance decreased from 30 to 0.3 µmol photons m−2 s−1. In contrast, a specific de novo synthesis of xanthophylls was observed at 0.1 µmol photons m−2 s−1 (Fig. 2d). Additionally, while the de-epoxidation state (DES) dropped between 30 and 1 µmol photons m−2 s−1, it paradoxically increased significantly at the lowest irradiances (0.1 to 0.3 µmol photons m−2 s−1), indicating a reactivation of the xanthophyll cycle to dissipate light energy even at these very low light levels (Figs. 2d and 5b). This physiological reactivation of the xanthophyll cycle aligns with the transcriptomic results. In pennate diatoms and in polar specific diatoms, the thermal dissipation of light energy through NPQ relies on both xanthophyll pigment content and lhcx proteins (Bailleul et al. 2010; Croteau et al. 2025a). The transcriptomic profile revealed that widely expressed lhcx genes (specifically Lhcx4, -7, -8, and -10) maintained extremely high expression levels across all conditions, effectively dominating the transcript pool at 0.1 µmol photons m−2 s−1 by more than an order of magnitude over the diminished light-harvesting genes (Fig. 4b and c). Similarly, genes encoding for the xanthophyll cycle enzymes Violaxanthin De-Epoxidase (VDE) and Zeaxanthin Epoxidase (ZEP) also maintained high transcript levels in all conditions (Fig. S13a). In diatoms, these enzymes regulate the conversion of Dd into Dt to trigger energy quenching and the reverse reaction, respectively (Giossi et al. 2025a, 2025b). Notably, in complete darkness, F. cylindrus lacks expression of genes that encode VDE (Fig. S13b and see Joli et al. (2024). This constitutive investment in photoprotective machinery (lhcx and enzymes), combined with the re-accumulation of xanthophylls, appears to influence downstream photochemistry in PSII. Rapid Light Curves (RLC) revealed that, while cells in the photoacclimation window (1 to 15 µmol photons m−2 s−1) maintained low induction of NPQ, cells at 0.1 µmol photons m−2 s−1 exhibited a marked ∼6-fold increase in the quantum yield of nonphotochemical quenching (ϕNPQ) compared with cells acclimated to 1 to 15 µmol photons m−2 s−1 (Fig. 5b). This change indicates that the energy absorbed by the maintained antenna complexes was actively diverted from photochemistry (ϕPSII) to regulated heat dissipation upon the cessation of cell division. Furthermore, NPQ induction kinetics confirmed a faster response for nongrowing cells, showing NPQ activation at the lowest light levels of the RLC, well before photosynthesis estimated by carbon fixation reached saturation (Fig. 5a and b). This strong NPQ engagement provides a mechanistic explanation for the paradoxical loss of photosynthetic efficiency observed for nondividing cells (Fig. 2f).

Discussion

Through the integration of growth kinetics, photophysiology, and transcriptomic profiles of F. cylindrus across a light gradient representative of the sub-ice polar environment, this study defines the physiological thresholds and the photosynthetic and molecular strategies that support diatom survival at the edge of photoautotrophic life. The data suggest that while cells optimize light harvesting under low light, they transition to a distinct dim-light strategy below the growth compensation point of 0.18 µmol photons m−2 s−1. In this nondividing state, F. cylindrus paradoxically appears to activate photoprotective quenching based on xanthophyll cycle while the photosynthetic machinery remains in a state of functional readiness. This metabolic homeostasis is apparently distinct from the catabolic hypometabolic state characteristic of the polar night as previously reported (Joli et al. 2024). Our results provide a mechanistic framework for an understanding of how polar diatoms may exploit the earliest Arctic spring photon fluxes, and thus define the limits of photosynthetic growth in extreme environments.

The limits to growth

The present study identified a growth compensation point (Ic) of 0.18 ± 0.11 µmol photons m−2 s−1 for the polar diatom F. cylindrus. This value appears consistent with historical in situ observations from Arctic planktonic and sympagic natural assemblages which were based solely on changes in chlorophyll a concentrations or carbon acquisition and reported compensation thresholds between ∼0.17 and 0.8 µmol photons m−2 s−1 (Cota 1985; Palmisano et al. 1985; Suzuki et al. 1997; Mock and Gradinger 1999; Hancke et al. 2018; Kvernvik et al. 2018). While direct laboratory measurements of light compensation points for polar strains are lacking, a limited number of studies on temperate diatoms report compensation points of similar magnitude, in the range of ∼0.15 to 1 µmol photons m−2 s−1 (Falkowski and Owens 1980; Geider et al. 1985; Langdon 1987; Quigg et al. 2006). This similarity suggests a broadly conserved lower light boundary for growth across diatoms. It is important to note that these experiments were carried out in a simplified environment, ie, under continuous light, following acclimation to stable conditions, which is very different from the condition's algae experience in their natural habitat. While continuous illumination, used here to avoid confounding diurnal cell-cycle or photosynthesis variations (Croteau et al. 2021), can slightly modulate growth rates and photoprotective capacity in F. cylindrus (Kropuenske et al. 2009; Guérin et al. 2024) while altering carbon metabolism, leading to a nonoptimal efficiency of energy conversion into biomass (Wagner et al. 2006; Su et al. 2024). Consequently, this experimental approach might have resulted in a minor underestimate of our reported carbon assimilation and growth rates but also in use of organic carbon as energy source (Lacour et al. 2022). By contrast, a recent study documented positive biomass accumulation in the water column in the central Arctic basin at a daily irradiance of just 0.04 ± 0.02 µmol photons m−2 s−1, an order of magnitude below our estimate (Hoppe et al. 2024), although this assessment relied on chlorophyll concentration, an index that may not perfectly reflect changes in biomass. Furthermore, during the sampling period, the assemblage was dominated by flagellates and dinoflagellates rather than diatoms, which suggests that the observed biomass accumulation might result primarily from these other groups. Whether these taxa possess lower light requirements remains an open question, as, to our knowledge, no light compensation thresholds for growth have yet been reported in the literature for Arctic dinoflagellates, haptophytes, or other flagellate taxa. This highlights a critical knowledge gap in Arctic microalgal ecology. Our empirical threshold remains more than one order of magnitude above the theoretical minimum limit for net photosynthetic growth derived by Raven et al. (2000) of 0.01 µmol photons m−2 s−1. It should also be noted that, despite the arrest of cell division, the cell population at 0.1 µmol photons m−2 s−1 exhibited no particular signs of stress or senescence. Ultimately, this extreme low-light growth capability implies that unicellular diatoms in the Arctic could initiate exponential growth very early in the season under sea ice and provides a physiological basis for field observations of active winter growth such as those recently reported (Randelhoff et al. 2020; Hoppe et al. 2024).

A distinct metabolic state: autotrophy versus dark hypometabolism

The physiological state observed at 0.1 µmol photons m−2 s−1 appears to differ fundamentally from the hypometabolism response triggered by complete darkness recently reported by Joli et al. (2024). In previous studies, Arctic diatoms in prolonged darkness seem to undergo a switch to cell cycle arrest, the degradation of photosynthetic machinery (protein actors downstream of the PSII and/or the enzymatic pool involved in the CBB), and the catabolism of intracellular reserves through the β-oxidation of lipids and the recycling of proteins and amino acids to sustain housekeeping functions, reflected by a marked decrease in cellular carbon and nitrogen content (Kennedy et al. 2019; Morin et al. 2020; Sciandra et al. 2022; Joli et al. 2024). The same trends have been observed in the green microalga Koliella antartica and the haptophyte Phaeocystis antarctica, and appear to be common to various polar taxa exposed to 3 to 4 wk of total darkness (Baldisserotto et al. 2005; Van De Poll et al. 2020). In this dark-induced survival mode, F. cylindrus strongly upregulates alternative oxidase (AOX) gene expression (Joli et al. 2024). In this context, the AOX pathway could provide a simplified alternative pathway for minimal ATP production via the oxidation of organic matter, effectively bypassing the more conventional respiratory chain in order to minimize the formation of harmful reactive oxygen species (Murik et al. 2019; Joli et al. 2024). In sharp contrast, the current data suggest that cells grown at 0.1 µmol photons m−2 s−1 maintain stable organic carbon and nitrogen quotas without any apparent signs of chlorosis (Fig. 2a–c) and do not trigger this survival-driven AOX-dependent quiescent state (Table S3). Instead, core genes of the tricarboxylic acid cycle and the classical complexes of the mitochondrial electron transport chain remained stably expressed (Fig. 3). To confirm whether catabolic activity is maintained under these sub-growth irradiances, we measured dark oxygen consumption rates. Our results indicate that F. cylindrus does not enter metabolic arrest at 0.1 μmol photons m−2 s−1. Instead, we observed a progressive, logarithmic decline in dark respiration rates as a function of growth irradiance (Fig. S4). This controlled metabolic reduction, combined with no decrease in intracellular organic matter pools, indicates that even a minimal light input of 0.1 μmol of photons m−2 s−1 is sufficient to maintain basal functions via the classic electron transport chain, thereby bypassing the AOX-mediated survival mode likely triggered by total darkness (Joli et al. 2024). Transcriptomic profiles confirmed that central metabolic pathways remained actively transcribed, albeit at lower abundance (Fig. 3, Figs. S5 and S7). This suggests that F. cylindrus does not reconfigure its metabolism toward catabolism but rather maintains a fully functional autotrophic machinery to support basal energy requirements under sub-compensatory irradiance. This metabolic poise aligns with the same principle early observed in the chlorophyte Dunaliella tertiolecta (Hellebust and Terborgh 1967), where low photon fluxes seemed to sustain a reduced but functional protein pool, bypassing the degradative pathways triggered by total darkness. Nevertheless, the fundamental tradeoffs in protein allocation required to maintain such a state have remained elusive. In our case, the decrease in Pmc for nondividing cells indicates a drastic decrease in the CBB enzymatic pool (probably Rubisco) and/or protein actors involved in electron transport in the thylakoid photosynthetic chain (Fig. S10a). However, the maintenance of a positive light-saturated carbon-specific photosynthetic rate (Pmc) at 0.1 µmol photon m−2 s−1 supports the presence of a functional protein pool (Fig. 5a and Fig. S10a), which is not the case after 1 mo of full dark acclimation for F. cylindrus (Morin et al. 2020). A possible ecological benefit of this strategy is the potential for rapid growth recovery. Previous work on dinoflagellates has demonstrated that populations maintained at sub-compensatory light can recover maximum growth rates almost immediately upon re-illumination, whereas dark-acclimated cells exhibit apparent lag phases (Richardson and Fogg 1982). This active maintenance potentially confers a competitive advantage as it allows for immediate growth recovery once light levels rise above the compensation threshold and thus prevents the lag phases associated with exiting deep dark hypometabolism.

Bioenergetic reallocation at growth-saturating irradiance

At 30 μmol of photons m−2 s−1, the cultures reach their maximum growth rate, while we observe a simultaneous decrease in Pmc. Since the volumetric carbon quotas and the C:N ratio remained constant throughout the experiment (Fig. 2a and Fig. S14), this decrease cannot be attributed to an accumulation of intracellular carbon, as has been proposed for Skeletonema costatum (Anning et al. 2000; MacIntyre et al. 2002). Furthermore, the stability of Pmcell over the range of 0.3 to 30 μmol photons m−2 s−1 suggests that the pool of Rubisco per cell is maintained, in contrast to observations made in the polar diatom Chaetoceros neogracilis (Lacour et al. 2022). At this upper limit of acclimation, light is no longer the limiting factor, and growth is now constrained by the low temperature (0 °C), which greatly restricts the dark phase of photosynthesis (via the kcat of Rubisco and downstream CBB enzymes, Young et al. 2015). Investing in increased light-harvesting and carbon-fixation capacities in the presence of a thermal limitation would be inefficient and potentially damaging, risking an electronic bottleneck and severe oxidative stress. This excessive excitation pressure is clearly illustrated by a higher DES (Fig. 2e) and the early onset of NPQ at 30 μmol photons m−2 s−1, leading to a decline in photosynthetic efficiency (Fig. 2f and Fig. S9a, d and f). Consequently, the decrease in Pmc likely reflects a strategic metabolic reallocation of photoproducts (ATP and NADPH) away from the CBB and towards noncarbon energy pathways (Goldman et al. 2015; Lacour et al. 2022). To maintain high division rates and cope with increased maintenance costs, electrons can be diverted to other energy-intensive processes (eg repair of damages, ROS detoxification as well as nitrogen and sulfur assimilation and biosynthesis of new molecules). This increase in metabolic flux is corroborated by our measurements of respiration peaking at 30 μmol of photons m−2 s−1 (Fig. S4). To support these processes, a significant proportion of chloroplastic NADPH could be exported to fuel the mitochondrial respiratory chain, which supports the hypothesis that polar thermodynamic constraints favor alternative energy sinks at the expense of carbon fixation in limiting conditions (Goldman et al. 2015; Lacour et al. 2022).

Paradoxical maintenance of the proton gradient at extremely low irradiance

The presence of a significant basal de-epoxidation state (DES; Fig. 2e) under light growth conditions (30 μmol photons m−2 s−1), and its persistence at extremely low light intensities (0.1 to 0.3 μmol photons m−2 s−1), suggest that sufficient proton gradient (ΔpH) is maintained to activate VDE under these conditions. The generation of this proton motive force may rely on several alternative, nonmutually exclusive energetic pathways. In plant, cyclic electron flow (CEF) around PSI contribute to increasing the proton motive force in low light condition (Yamori et al. 2015). It has recently been shown in P. tricornutum that this is favored by a more oxidized state of the plastoquinone pool, induced by NPQ (Croteau et al. 2025b); and under our conditions of extremely low light, we hypothesize a weakly reduced plastoquinone pool. In diatoms, CEF is generally low under optimal conditions (Bailleul et al. 2015), but appears to be crucial during environmental stresses such as iron and nitrogen deficiency in P. tricornutum (Thamatrakoln et al. 2013; Wagner et al. 2016), anoxia in Thalassiosira pseudonana (Gain et al. 2023), low temperatures in F. cylindrus (Goldman et al. 2015), high-light stress (Croteau et al. 2025b), or acclimation to fluctuating light regimes in P. tricornutum (Doenier et al. 2026). The presence of a CEF under low-light conditions would be consistent with the stable expression of PGR5 observed at 30 and 0.1 µmol photons m−2 s−1 (Fig. S11) and the strong positive regulation (log2 FC ± 3 at 0.1 µmol photons m−2 s−1) of STROBE1 (Table S3), a CEF potentiator recently identified in P. tricornutum as essential for the generation of ΔpH (Doenier et al. 2026). Furthermore, diatoms are known to display a close coupling between chloroplasts and the mitochondria (Bailleul et al. 2015; Flori et al. 2017; Broddrick et al. 2019; Murik et al. 2019; Giustini et al. 2025; Rehder et al. 2025). This coupling could sustain the gradient by importing mitochondrial ATP into the stroma, followed by its hydrolysis by the reverse-operating chloroplastic ATP synthase, thereby allowing protons to be injected into the lumen (Bailleul et al. 2015; Buchert et al. 2021). Although this process represents an energy cost for a cell already limited by light at 0.1 µmol photon m−2 s−1, it proves essential for maintaining vital plastidial functions independent of photosynthesis, such as protein transport via the thylakoidal Tat pathway, which is strictly dependent on the pmf in the green lineage (Kohzuma et al. 2012). Finally, a low linear electron flux (LEF) disconnected from a slowed or inactive CBB cycle (represented by the drastic drop in Pmc to 0.1 µmol photon m−2 s−1) at these intensities could also generate a stable ΔpH, with proton efflux towards the stroma then being restricted by the low turnover of ATP synthase substrates (ADP and Pi). However, because the respective quantitative contributions of these processes, as well as the molecular mechanisms involved, remain a subject of debate in diatoms (Croteau et al. 2024), it is difficult to provide robust conclusions. These results therefore support the idea that elucidating parallel electron pathways represents the next frontier in understanding the plasticity and survival of polar diatoms in their environments.

Photoprotection at the lower limit of photosynthetic growth: the “energy surplus” hypothesis

The most striking feature of this sub-compensatory state represents an apparent paradox in photoacclimation. Typically, low-light acclimation involves the maximization of light harvest and quantum yield (Falkowski and Owens 1980; Laws and Bannister 1980; Geider et al. 1986; Geider et al. 1986; Quigg et al. 2006; Suggett et al. 2007; Arrigo et al. 2010; Halsey and Jones 2015; Agarwal et al. 2023). However, at 0.1 µmol photons m−2 s−1, we observed a collapse in both photosynthetic efficiency (αC) and photosynthetic maximum capacity (Pmc) which occurs in parallel with a strong upregulation of photoprotective mechanisms, including increased xanthophyll content (Dd + Dt) as well as the sustained expression of lhcx genes. We propose an “Energy Surplus” hypothesis to explain this response and NPQ induction. Cellular energy requirements can be partitioned into investment costs (to support cell division, DNA replication, and organelle duplication) and maintenance costs (which cover protein turnover, ROS detoxification, and homeostasis) (Raven and Beardal 2016; Bender et al. 2022). Investment in growth represents the primary energy sink for the cell. Under steady-state conditions at 0.1 µmol photons m−2 s−1, the arrest of cell division apparently eliminates this investment cost. Concurrently, cells appear to minimize maintenance costs through the downregulation of their enzymatic machinery, a classic low-light survival strategy (Fisher et al. 1989; Quigg and Beardall 2003; Halsey and Jones 2015), as evidenced here by the sharp decline in Pmc, which serves as a proxy for the active Rubisco pool. Furthermore, at 0 °C, the remaining basal metabolic rates are likely to be thermodynamically low. Due to the absence of the primary energy sink (cell division) and the reduction of enzymatic demand, even the minimal electron flow generated at 0.1 µmol photons m−2 s−1 may exceed the metabolically restricted capacity of the cell to utilize it. To manage this imbalance and help prevent photo-oxidative damage, cells appear to activate a specific dissipative sink. The upregulation of NPQ and xanthophyll synthesis acts as a safety valve for the dissipation of excess excitation energy upstream of the reaction centers, which reflects high-light acclimation response (Lavaud et al. 2002; Lavaud and Goss 2014; Lacour et al. 2018; Croteau et al. 2021, 2026). Additionally, the upregulation of EPS biosynthesis genes combined with observed cell aggregation (Figs. S2 and S7 respectively) raises the possibility that cells secrete excess carbon as extracellular polymeric substances. This could also provide a metabolic overflow mechanism, a strategy known to be employed by F. cylindrus in the stationary phase (Ugalde et al. 2013).

In conclusion, our study provides a mechanistic definition of the lower light limit of photosynthetic growth in the polar diatom F. cylindrus. The results demonstrate that the arrest of cell division at the light compensation point does not trigger metabolic collapse or a shift to dark-induced hypometabolism. Instead, cells enter a poised homeostatic state, where they actively manage relative energy surplus via photoprotective quenching while maintaining their photosynthetic machinery in functional readiness. These findings suggest that the minimum light requirement to ensure cell division is remarkably low, underscoring the necessity of determining absolute light thresholds across diverse photoautotrophs to accurately predict the timing of biological activities in polar oceanic regions. However, although the growth limit identified here marks the arrest of cell division at an irradiance still an order of magnitude above the theoretical minimum for maintenance (Raven et al. 2000), photosynthetic activity appears to remain functional. This decoupling between growth and photosynthesis raises a fundamental question about the precise irradiance threshold required for the photosynthetic machinery to truly cease to function. Future research could bridge this gap through the assessment of energy fluxes and acclimation kinetics in F. cylindrus and other dominant taxa, such as flagellates and dinoflagellates, during prolonged exposure to sub-compensatory and fluctuating dim-light regimes. Through the establishment of the first physiological bricks concerning the ultimate limits of photoautotrophy, this work helps refine our vision of Arctic microalgal physiology (Hoppe et al. 2024) and provides a framework for the exploration of the potential for photosynthetic life in extreme or extraterrestrial environments (Khuller et al. 2024).

Materials and methods

Culture conditions and experimental design

Axenic cultures of the polar diatom F. cylindrus (CCMP 3323) were maintained at 0 ± 1 °C in F/2 medium (Guillard 1975) prepared with prefiltered seawater collected from Baffin Bay and supplemented with antibiotics (0.1 g L−1 streptomycin, kanamycin, and antimycin; 0.05 g L−1 gentamicin). To evaluate the lower limits of phototrophy, triplicate cultures were acclimated for a minimum of 21 d to 6 constant irradiances (0.1, 0.3, 1, 3, 15, and 30 µmol photons m−2 s−1) which were provided by cool-white LEDs with spectral adjustments to mimic the under-ice light environment (Fig. S15). Scalar irradiance at the center of each cylindrical vessel directly in water was quantified using a QSL-2101 sensor (Biospherical Instruments, San Diego, CA, USA). To confirm measurement accuracy at the lowest irradiance threshold, the QSL-2101 was cross-calibrated down to 0.05 µmol photons m−2 s−1 against a high-sensitivity micro-radiometer (MPE, Biospherical Instruments), demonstrating high linearity (R2 = 0.9994) and a negligible systematic error of −0.017 μmol photons m−2 s−1 at 0.1 μmol photons m−2 s−1 (Table S4). Cultures were maintained in the exponential phase (∼1 × 106 cells ml−1) by semi-continuous dilution to minimize biomass-induced light attenuation.

Growth and cellular composition

Cell concentration and size were measured via a Beckman Coulter Multisizer 4 (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). While cultures were maintained in exponential growth, the specific growth rates (μ, d−1) were calculated via a linearized exponential growth equation, with growth parameters derived by least-squares regression: ln(N)=ln(N0)+μ⋅T (Wood et al. 2005). The relationship between μ and irradiance was modeled to derive the maximum growth rate (μmax), the saturation parameter (KE), and the compensation point for growth (I0) (MacIntyre et al. 2002). Cell viability was assessed via flow cytometry using SYTOX Green stain as describe in Sciandra et al. (2022). After acclimation, samples were harvested for the quantification of particulate organic carbon and nitrogen via elemental analysis (Costech ECS 8020, CA, USA), and pigment profiles via HPLC (Agilent 1260, CA, USA) following specific protocols detail in Guérin et al. (2022). Dark oxygen (O2) consumption rates were monitored at 0 ℃ for 4 h in hermetically sealed glass vials using fiber-optic oxygen sensors (FireSting-O2; Pyro Science GmbH, Aachen, Germany). Samples were gently stirred to prevent cell sedimentation. The respiration rate (μmol O2 L−1 h−1) was determined from the slope of the linear decrease in O2 concentration over time, and subsequently normalized to chlorophyll a content, particulate organic carbon and cell density.

Photosynthetic performance

Photophysiology was assessed using variable chlorophyll a fluorescence and 14C-uptake assays. PSII activity was assessed with a Phyto-PAM fluorometer (Phyto-ML, Heinz Walz GmbH, Germany). Samples were dark-adapted for 30 min before exposure to RLC. Actinic light was provided by a modulated blue LED source (450 ± 20 nm) across incremental 30 s steps from 0.3 to 163 µmol photons m−2 s−1. The quantum yields of the 3 energy dissipation pathways in PSII were determined by the complementary PSII quantum yields derivation: PSII photochemical conversion ϕPSII=(Fm′−Fs)/Fm′, NPQ regulated heat losses ϕNPQ=(Fs/Fm′)−(Fs/FM) and the nonregulated heat losses ϕNO=Fs/FM, the sum of which is always unity: Y(PSII) + Y(NPQ) + Y(NO) = 1 (Hendrickson et al. 2004; Klughammer and Schreiber 2008). Additionally, the functional absorption cross-section of PSII (σPSII) and the maximum quantum yield of PSII (FV/FM) from cells after 30 min of dark acclimation were determined with a Fluorescence Induction and Relaxation fluorometer (FIRe, Satlantic, Canada). This instrument applies a saturating, single turnover flash (STF, 120μs) of blue light (455 nm) at saturation. The analysis of raw curves was performed within MATLAB software via the FIReWORX script by Audrey Barnett (https://sourceforge.net/projects/fireworx/). Carbon fixation rates were measured via 20-min 14C-bicarbonate incubations across 28 light levels (0 to 250 µmol photons m−2 s−1) with an in-house incubator (Morin et al. 2020; Joli et al. 2024). A hyperbolic tangent model was fitted to the photosynthesis-irradiance data to derive the photosynthetic efficiency (α) and the maximum fixation rate (Pm) (Jassby and Platt 1976).

Transcriptomics (RNA sequencing)

Total RNA was extracted (Qiagen RNeasy) and DNAse-treated as described in Joli et al. (2024). Library preparation, which included poly-A selection, was performed using the Illumina TruSeq Stranded mRNA protocol. The generation of sequences was conducted and provided > 30 million paired-end reads (2 × 150 bp) per sample on an Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform. Read quality was verified using FastQC v0.11.9. Reads were aligned to the F. cylindrus reference genome (v1.0 (Mock et al. 2017)) using STAR v2.7.9a (Dobin et al. 2013). Raw read counts over exons were obtained with HTSeq-count v0.13.5 (Anders et al. 2015). Raw gene counts were normalized using the median of ratios method implemented in DESeq2 v1.26.0 (Love et al. 2014) to account for differences in sequencing depth and RNA composition, allowing for direct comparison of expression levels between samples (Love et al. 2014). The analysis targeted in-house manually annotated gene sets of 1,663 genes. Then, the genes were filtered at a minimum threshold of 30 counts in order to be considered actively transcribed in the cell. Regarding the additional figures comparison of gene expression profiles between this study and the study of F. cylindrus in prolonged darkness for 1-mo Joli et al. 2024, raw count data were downloaded from BioProject PRJNA902753 (GEO accession no. GSE218215). These raw data were then processed using the same bioinformatics pipeline described above.

Statistical analysis

Data was analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by a Tukey's HSD post-hoc test, or Kruskal–Wallis followed by a Dunn's test when assumptions of normality or homoscedasticity were not met. The significance threshold was defined at P-value <0.05.
