Diagnostic dilemma between probable cardiac sarcoidosis and non-granulomatous myocarditis: a grand round case report
Authors: Tulluru Ravi Sankar, M Ananthakrishna, A Arpudh Michael, P Sirish Chandra Srinath, T Viji Samuel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiac sarcoidosis (CS) can mimic other inflammatory cardiomyopathies, and true steroid resistance is uncommon. Distinguishing granulomatous myocardial involvement from active non-granulomatous myocarditis remains clinically challenging.Case summaryA 43-year-old woman with biopsy-proven systemic sarcoidosis and dilated cardiomyopathy presented with recurrent decompensated heart failure (HF). Fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography–computed tomography demonstrated diffuse myocardial uptake and was interpreted as probable CS. Despite guideline-directed HF therapy and immunosuppression, she developed progressive HF complicated by renal failure and refractory shock, culminating in death. Post-mortem myocardial biopsy revealed active non-granulomatous myocarditis without granulomas. Rare viral inclusion-like structures consistent with cytomegalovirus were identified in lung tissue, with elevated cytomegalovirus titres; however, immunohistochemistry was negative.DiscussionThis grand round highlights a diagnostic dilemma between probable CS and biopsy-demonstrated non-granulomatous myocarditis. While histology showed myocarditis, co-existing sarcoidosis cannot be definitively excluded due to post-mortem sampling limitations. This case underscores the limitations of imaging-based diagnosis, the potential risks of escalating immunosuppression in the setting of diagnostic uncertainty, and the importance of timely myocardial tissue reassessment, particularly when clinical deterioration occurs despite 2–4 weeks of intensive therapy, within a multidisciplinary framework.

Introduction

Cardiac sarcoidosis (CS) can closely mimic other inflammatory cardiomyopathies, posing substantial diagnostic and therapeutic challenges, particularly when overlapping clinical and imaging features occur in the setting of systemic illness and multiorgan dysfunction. Contemporary guidelines, including the 2024 clinical consensus statement on the management of CS from the Heart Failure Association, the European Association of Cardiovascular Imaging, the European Society of Cardiology Working Group on Myocardial and Pericardial Diseases, and the European Heart Rhythm Association, permit a diagnosis of probable CS in the presence of biopsy-proven extracardiac sarcoidosis with concordant cardiac imaging findings, even without myocardial histologic confirmation.1,2 Advanced imaging modalities such as fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography–computed tomography (FDG PET–CT) are central to current diagnostic algorithms but lack disease specificity. Moreover, myocardial histology has limited sensitivity because of the patchy nature of disease involvement.3,4

We present a Grand Round case illustrating a diagnostic dilemma between imaging-based probable CS and biopsy-demonstrated non-granulomatous myocarditis. In this patient, extracardiac granulomatous inflammation together with diffuse myocardial fluorodeoxyglucose uptake fulfilled contemporary diagnostic criteria for probable CS, thereby supporting initiation of immunosuppressive therapy under current diagnostic algorithms.1–4 This case highlights the limitations of imaging-led diagnosis, the risks of escalating immunosuppression in the setting of diagnostic uncertainty, and the importance of timely multidisciplinary reassessment, including the role of repeat tissue sampling, when clinical deterioration occurs despite guideline-directed therapy.

Case presentation

A 43-year-old woman with no cardiovascular risk factors presented with progressively worsening dyspnoea over 4 months. Three months earlier, she had been admitted to an outside hospital with acute decompensated heart failure (HF), where transthoracic echocardiography revealed severe left ventricular systolic dysfunction (LVSD) with moderate mitral regurgitation. She required intensive care unit (ICU) admission and intravenous dobutamine support. Renal dysfunction was noted (serum creatinine ∼2.0 mg/dL) and subsequent imaging revealed bilateral renal cortical scarring on ultrasonography, suggesting underlying chronic kidney disease, possibly related to reflux nephropathy or prior asymptomatic infections. She was advised for further evaluation at a tertiary-care centre.

Although she improved transiently, her symptoms progressed from New York Heart Association functional class II to IV over the subsequent two months, accompanied by orthopnoea and paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea. She denied chest pain, fever, haemoptysis, sputum, wheeze, photosensitivity, rash, haematuria, or frothy urine. There was no history of tobacco, alcohol, or nephrotoxic or cardiotoxic drug exposure. Her past medical history was otherwise unremarkable, with no diabetes mellitus, hypertension, coronary artery disease, tuberculosis, or prior cardiac disease. Surgical history included childhood appendicectomy, and obstetric history included one uncomplicated pregnancy with normal vaginal delivery 15 years earlier. Family history was non-contributory.

On presentation to our institution, she was dyspneic at rest. She measured 163 cm in height and weighed 58.6 kg (body mass index 22.1 kg/m2), with blood pressure 94/64 mmHg and heart rate 84 beats/min. Physical examination revealed elevated jugular venous pressure, bilateral basal crepitations, and bilateral 2+ pedal oedema. Cardiac auscultation revealed a soft first heart sound, a normal second heart sound, and a grade 2/6 holosystolic murmur at the apex. There was no rash, lymphadenopathy, cyanosis, or clubbing.

Given the presentation of severe LVSD, differential diagnoses included inflammatory and non-ischaemic cardiomyopathies.1–4 CS was strongly suspected given bilateral mediastinal lymphadenopathy (Figure 1A) and elevated serum angiotensin-converting enzyme levels. Active myocarditis, including viral or immune-mediated aetiologies, was considered because of the subacute course and systemic inflammation. Genetic dilated cardiomyopathy was considered and evaluated. Giant cell myocarditis was deemed less likely in the absence of fulminant conduction disease or eosinophilia. Ischaemic cardiomyopathy was unlikely given absent risk factors and non-ischaemic echocardiographic features (Figure 2). Infiltrative cardiomyopathies and stress-induced cardiomyopathy were considered less likely given imaging findings and the chronic progressive course. Concomitant renal dysfunction and systemic illness further complicated early diagnostic clarity.

Electrocardiography showed sinus rhythm with low-voltage QRS complexes and non-specific ST/T changes (Figure 3), with non-sustained ventricular tachycardia documented during ICU monitoring. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed severe LVSD with Left ventricular ejection fraction of 20%, global hypokinesia, grade III diastolic dysfunction, moderate secondary mitral regurgitation, mild tricuspid regurgitation, pulmonary hypertension (RVSP 55–57 mmHg), and borderline right ventricular dysfunction. Chest radiography demonstrated cardiomegaly, pulmonary congestion, and hilar lymphadenopathy, while serial imaging showed progressive bilateral infiltrates paralleling worsening hypoxaemia and clinical deterioration (Figure 1B). Computed tomography pulmonary angiography excluded pulmonary embolism but demonstrated upper-lobe–predominant perihilar consolidations and bilateral pleural effusions (Figure 4). Laboratory investigations revealed markedly elevated n-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide levels (>35 000 pg/mL), fluctuating renal dysfunction, progressive thrombocytopenia, anaemia, and intermittent transaminitis. During the ICU course, blood cultures grew Klebsiella species, coinciding with worsening cytopenias and haemodynamic instability. FDG PET–CT demonstrated diffuse myocardial FDG uptake with metabolically active mediastinal lymph nodes (Figure 5). Endobronchial ultrasound-guided lymph node biopsy revealed non-necrotizing granulomatous inflammation, confirming systemic sarcoidosis. Infectious and autoimmune evaluations were otherwise negative.

The patient was managed with a multidisciplinary approach involving cardiology, pulmonology, nephrology, infectious diseases, dermatology, and haematology teams. Acute HF was initially treated with intravenous loop diuretics and strict fluid balance. Guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) was limited by hypotension and renal dysfunction; β-blockers and vasodilators were trialled twice but discontinued, while Dapagliflozin was continued as tolerated. Inotropic support included a single course of dobutamine during the initial admission and two dopamine infusions and one course of levosimendan during the second admission, with only transient improvement. In view of biopsy-proven extracardiac sarcoidosis and FDG PET–CT evidence of myocardial inflammation, immunosuppression was initiated with oral prednisolone (0.5 mg/kg/day).3,4 Mycophenolate mofetil was added after normalization of liver function tests.5 Progressive clinical deterioration prompted escalation to pulse corticosteroid therapy with intravenous dexamethasone followed by methylprednisolone, with subsequent transition to high-dose oral steroids after multidisciplinary team discussion.6–8 Worsening renal failure and refractory congestion necessitated renal replacement therapy (RRT).

New-onset thrombocytopenia, likely secondary to sepsis, drug toxicity, or viral infection, resulted in petechiae and ecchymoses over the trunk and extremities and improved without transfusion. A skin biopsy of persistent papulonodular lesions showed non-specific inflammatory changes without granulomas, suggesting a reactive process to drugs or infection, rather than cutaneous sarcoidosis. No specific dermatologic intervention was advised by dermatologist. The patient subsequently developed oral candidiasis following corticosteroid therapy, which was treated with systemic fluconazole. During the late ICU course, she developed culture-proven Klebsiella sepsis, associated with further clinical deterioration. Despite broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy, only transient improvement was observed. Recurrent non-sustained ventricular arrhythmias were documented on continuous telemetry and were managed medically. Implantable cardioverter-defibrillator implantation was recommended but deferred due to sepsis, thrombocytopenia, and haemodynamic instability.

Despite aggressive HF management, escalating immunosuppression, RRT, and treatment of sepsis, the patient developed refractory cardiogenic and distributive shock with multiorgan failure. Pulmonary embolism was excluded during acute haemodynamic collapse. Despite maximal supportive care, including vasopressors, inotropes, ventilatory support, and dialysis, she died during the hospitalization. Post-mortem endomyocardial biopsy (EMB) revealed active non-granulomatous myocarditis with myocyte injury and interstitial fibrosis, without evidence of sarcoid granulomas. Rare cytomegalovirus-like inclusion bodies were identified in lung tissue, and subsequent testing demonstrated elevated circulating cytomegalovirus titres; although, immunohistochemistry for cytomegalovirus was negative (Figure 6). Clinical exome sequencing, sent during the ICU stay, was reported posthumously and showed no pathogenic variants associated with cardiomyopathy. Discussion

In this patient, the presence of non-necrotizing granulomas on extracardiac lymph node biopsy, together with diffuse myocardial fluorodeoxyglucose uptake, fulfilled contemporary consensus criteria for probable CS (Table 2). This diagnostic approach aligns with current recommendations, including recent consensus statements from the Heart Failure Association of the European Society of Cardiology and the American Heart Association.1–4,9 However, progressive clinical deterioration despite escalating immunosuppression highlighted the diagnostic uncertainty that may persist even when guideline-based criteria are fulfilled. Fluorodeoxyglucose uptake reflects inflammatory activity but lacks specificity and does not distinguish between granulomatous and non-granulomatous inflammation, which may lead to diagnostic overlap. This case illustrates how reliance on imaging-based diagnostic pathways, in the absence of definitive myocardial histology, may obscure alternative or concurrent inflammatory cardiomyopathies and complicate therapeutic decision-making.

Corticosteroids remain the first-line therapy for clinically manifest CS, with steroid-sparing agents reserved for refractory disease or intolerance. Although a ‘hit-hard and early’ therapeutic approach has been proposed for severe sarcoidosis, advocating earlier use of steroid-sparing agents or biologics to prevent irreversible organ damage, supporting evidence is largely observational, and inter-centre variability in immunosuppressive practice persists.5 True steroid resistance in CS is uncommon and may instead reflect irreversible myocardial injury, alternative inflammatory cardiomyopathies, or superimposed infection.7,8 In such scenarios, further escalation of immunosuppression may be ineffective or harmful. In the present case, immunosuppressive therapy was initiated for probable CS in accordance with contemporary HF association guidelines; however progressive deterioration despite treatment raised concern for alternative inflammatory aetiologies or superimposed infection rather than true steroid resistance.

EMB has limited sensitivity for CS because of sampling variability. Current guidelines recommend EMB in patients with new-onset cardiogenic shock of unclear aetiology, persistent clinical deterioration or when alternative diagnoses such as myocarditis are suspected. In this case, EMB was deferred during the index admission because of haemodynamic instability, thrombocytopenia, and evolving multiorgan dysfunction, which posed a high procedural risk. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging was similarly not feasible, underscoring the practical limitations of advanced imaging in critically ill patients. The post-mortem myocardial examination demonstrated active non-granulomatous myocarditis, highlighting potential discordance between imaging-based diagnosis and limited myocardial histologic sampling. It should be noted that post-mortem evaluation was limited to targeted myocardial sampling rather than comprehensive examination of the entire heart; therefore, the possibility of sampling error cannot be excluded, and focal granulomatous involvement consistent with CS may have been missed. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted with caution, and coexistence of CS and non-granulomatous myocarditis cannot be definitively excluded. These findings highlight the limitations of both imaging and tissue sampling in inflammatory cardiomyopathies, particularly in the context of patchy myocardial involvement. In retrospect, earlier consideration of EMB may have been appropriate after lack of clinical improvement despite 2–4 weeks of corticosteroid therapy. Although it remains uncertain whether earlier biopsy would have altered the clinical outcome, histologic clarification might have influenced therapeutic decision-making, including reconsideration of immunosuppressive escalation and evaluation for alternative aetiologies. This case underscores the importance of timely reassessment and individualized decision-making regarding EMB in critically ill patients.

Rare cytomegalovirus-like inclusions were identified in post-mortem lung tissue, together with elevated circulating cytomegalovirus titres despite negative immunohistochemistry. The discrepant cytopathic changes suggest possible systemic viral reactivation in the setting of immunosuppression, although alternative explanations such as infection with low viral load, focal sampling, other viral aetiologies with similar morphology, drug-related cytopathic changes, or post-mortem autolysis cannot be excluded.10–12 Although Cytomegalovirus infection has been associated with myocarditis and systemic manifestations such as cytopenias, renal dysfunction, a causal relationship with the observed myocardial inflammation cannot be established in this case. Accordingly, the pulmonary and serologic findings are best interpreted as incidental or contributory to systemic illness rather than a primary driver of myocardial pathology.

The interpretation of non-granulomatous myocarditis in this context warrants careful consideration. The observed myocardial inflammation may represent a primary inflammatory myocarditis underlying the initial presentation. Alternatively, it may reflect a secondary process related to prolonged immunosuppression, systemic infection, or critical illness, all of which may contribute to myocardial injury and inflammatory changes. A mixed process, including coexistence of CS with superimposed myocarditis, also remains plausible, given the limitations of myocardial sampling. In the absence of comprehensive myocardial evaluation and definitive etiologic markers, it is not possible to determine whether myocarditis was the primary driver of the clinical course or a secondary phenomenon. Although both CS and fulminant myocarditis may initially warrant immunosuppressive therapy, the intensity, duration, and adjunctive agents differ significantly. This distinction has important therapeutic implications, particularly in guiding escalation or de-escalation of immunosuppressive therapy. Given the substantial haematologic and renal toxicity of antiviral agents, empiric therapy was unlikely to confer meaningful benefit in the setting of advanced HF, multiorgan dysfunction, and diagnostic uncertainty.

Collectively, this case does not represent a deviation from established guidelines but rather highlights the diagnostic uncertainty that may persist despite adherence to consensus-based criteria and the complexity of managing steroid-refractory inflammatory cardiomyopathy. Early histologic clarification may influence the selection of second-line immunosuppressants, consideration of antiviral therapy, and candidacy for advanced HF therapies. It underscores the importance of timely reassessment, multidisciplinary decision-making, and consideration of myocardial tissue diagnosis in patients with suspected inflammatory cardiomyopathy who deteriorate despite guideline-directed therapy. Diagnosis and management in inflammatory cardiomyopathy should remain dynamic and responsive to evolving clinical data.

Patient’s perspective

Although a direct patient perspective was unavailable due to the patient’s clinical deterioration and subsequent death, her family remained closely engaged throughout the prolonged ICU course. They experienced significant emotional distress while witnessing her rapid decline despite escalation of guideline-directed therapies. This case highlights the importance of transparent communication with families regarding the uncertainties inherent in diagnosis, the limitations of imaging-based evaluation, and the potential risks of empiric immunosuppression, particularly in the setting of multiorgan dysfunction.
