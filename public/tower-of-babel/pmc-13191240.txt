Severe foreign body reaction with spontaneous extrusion of glue after cyanoacrylate closure of the great saphenous vein
Authors: Hitoshi Kusagawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Noninfectious inflammatory complications—such as phlebitis and systemic hypersensitivity reactions—can occur after cyanoacrylate closure for lower extremity varicose veins. In most cases, these reactions resolve within approximately 1 month, but in rare situations they may persist and become difficult to manage. This report describes a case of phlebitis after cyanoacrylate closure in which a severe foreign body reaction, rather than hypersensitivity or allergy, led to the formation of multiple fistulae. Through these fistulae, the cyanoacrylate polymer was eventually expelled, resulting in gradual healing. Understanding this clinical course and its underlying pathophysiology is important when providing preoperative explanations to patients.

Noninfectious inflammatory problems—such as phlebitis and systemic hypersensitivity reactions (HSRs)—develop in approximately 6% to 32% of patients after cyanoacrylate closure (CAC) for lower limb varicose veins.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 These reactions may appear around the glue injection site or as widespread skin symptoms.1, 2, 3, 4 This report describes a case in which the patient developed multiple fistulas and discharged glue from them, most likely owing to a strong foreign body reaction. The patient gave written informed consent for publication of her clinical details and imaging findings.

Case report

A 76-year-old woman with no history of allergies or autoimmune diseases presented with two ulcers on her left lower calf (Fig 1). The venous disorder was symptomatic C2 on the right and C6 on the left according to the CEAP classification. Ultrasound showed clear reflux >2 seconds in bilateral great saphenous veins (GSVs) that were not superficialized. The average diameter was greater in the left GSV. CAC of bilateral GSVs was performed as per standard procedure using the VenaSeal Closure System (Medtronic) (Fig 1). On postoperative day (POD) 7, uniform pain, induration, and swelling appeared on the right side, consistent with the GSV containing glue. Analgesics and topical steroids were started. On POD 14, the symptoms worsened, and oral steroids (prednisolone 10 mg/d) were started, but they were completely ineffective and discontinued after one week. Subsequently, symptoms improved gradually. From POD 28 to 56, fistulae appeared sequentially in two locations on the right thigh and three locations on the right calf along the course of the GSV. On POD 42, surgical excision of the glue and vein was suggested to the patient, but she preferred conservative treatment. Around POD 70, the glue that had solidified within the vein was sequentially expelled from each fistula (Fig 2). That the emitted substance was a polymer of cyanoacrylate was confirmed by the manufacturer. The lower extremity venous ultrasound findings at POD 70 are shown in Fig 3. The image on the left shows normal progression, and the image on the right shows that the diameter of the vein into which the glue was injected was thicker, and thick induration was observed around the vein, but there was no visible progression between POD 28 and POD 70. Meanwhile, the left leg had a good course, and the ulcer had completely healed by that time. After that, the symptoms in the glue expulsion areas improved dramatically. Symptoms persisted in the upper thigh where no fistulae had formed, so an incision and venous scar excision were performed; however, no glue was present (Fig 4). The symptoms subsequently improved although pigmentation remained in the fistula area and the excised area. Fig 5 shows the microscopic findings of the excised tissue, the vein (Figs 5, A-C), and the area surrounding the vein (Figs 5, D-F). Microscopically, small amounts of glue remained in the veins, surrounded by a variety of inflammatory cells, including foreign body giant cells, lymphocytes, and neutrophils. Proliferation of fibroblasts and collagen fibers, as well as areas of scarring, was also observed. Around the vein, there were mostly areas of fibroblast and collagen fiber proliferation and scarring, with scattered infiltration of various inflammatory cells such as lymphocytes and neutrophils in some areas. Discussion

The first feature of this case was that nonallergic pain and induration occurred evenly throughout the glue injection area, fistulas formed in multiple locations, and glue casts were expelled. The second feature was that this occurred on only one side of a bilaterally injected case.

When a generalized itchy rash occurs after CAC, abnormally sensitized immune responses owing to various allergies are thought to be involved. However, the clinical manifestations of noninfectious phlebitis localized around the cyanoacrylate-injected veins vary greatly from case to case. In addition to allergies, normal reactions such as foreign body reactions and thrombophlebitis may also be involved in the mechanism of inflammation. Nevertheless, the term hypersensitivity reactions tends to be used to include inflammatory reactions that are not considered to be allergic in its original sense.5,7,10,11 Since then, many terms have been reported to describe the lesion, including phlebitis-like abnormal reaction,1 complex hypersensitivity and irritation reaction,2 endovenous glue-induced hypersensitivity,3 and phlebitic reaction.6 However, whether these refer to the same entity is unclear.

In this article, the broad term phlebitis is used for the local symptoms, which includes hypersensitivity and a foreign body reaction, although they are difficult to define. In most cases of post-CAC phlebitis, symptoms disappear almost completely after approximately 1 month. However, in rare cases, the condition can be prolonged and become severe.

A case of a severe foreign body reaction after CAC, resulting in the spontaneous expulsion of the glue, was reported. This case is considered a rare, severe foreign body reaction after CAC; it is the only case among the 800 limbs treated with CAC at the author's clinic. Cases in which glue was expelled have been reported, although this phenomenon is extremely rare.11,12 Louden et al's11 second case was almost the same as the present case, but it was described as HSR; it seems more likely to be a foreign body reaction than HSR. Langridge et al's12 case was different in that the glue had extravasated.

In the present case, why the complication occurred only on the right side of bilateral procedure was inexplicable. The case was examined from various angles to determine whether there was any special circumstance that made the right leg's GSV particularly prone to foreign body reactions, but the reason remained unclear. There have been reports of phlebitis occurring on only one side of bilaterally treated patients,2,3,5,8 and conversely, there has been a report of phlebitis occurring on both sides of bilaterally treated patients.1 However, no explanations for these differences have been found.

Four cases have been reported in which phlebitis after CAC was treated with glue and full-length venous resection, but these cases were reminiscent of HSR.5,10,11,13 However, in the present case, the area around the vein where the glue was injected had extremely severe fibrosis, scarring, and adhesions, making it impossible to remove the entire vein. Histological findings around the vein did not show any glue or foreign body giant cells, but fibrotic scarring consistent with the surgical findings was observed. These perivenous findings differ from that of typical HSR. Intravenous histological findings showed infiltration of various inflammatory cells, including glue and foreign body giant cells, and fibrotic scarring, which is thought to be a reaction seen in all cases where CAC has been performed,14 although in severe cases, this inflammation is so strong that it may destroy the vein wall.

The basis and mechanism of the development of phlebitis after CAC remain unknown, and further research is needed. However, when talking to patients before surgery, severe foreign body reactions such as those in the present case in addition to HSR, must be explained.

Conclusions

A case in which glue was discharged through a fistula on one side after bilateral CAC for phlebitis, which is unlikely to be due to allergy, was reported. In such cases, fibrosis and scarring of the surrounding tissue are characteristic, and full-length resection may be difficult.
