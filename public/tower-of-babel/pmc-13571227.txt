Risk Scrutiny and Future Opportunities of Robot-Assisted Implant Surgery: A Narrative Review
Authors: Guanxun Wang, Yusen Lin, Xiao Zheng, Junyu Shi, Fei Huang, Yaqi Zhao, Shuyi Wen, Yujian Gan, Qing Wang, Jie Kong, Hongchang Lai, Mingdeng Rong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction and aimsRobot-assisted implant surgery (RAIS) signifies a major advancement in computer-guided implant technology. In comparison to traditional hand-guided placement, RAIS enhances the precision and standardization of implant positioning. However, the increased technical complexity introduces potential error sources at various stages of the digital workflow. This review aims to systematically evaluate the potential risks associated with RAIS and explore relevant management strategies and future technological advancements.MethodsThis review adhered to the Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles guidelines. A structured literature search was performed across the PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science databases, covering publications from January 2000 to May 2026. Studies reporting clinical outcomes, accuracy, technical performance, safety, complications, and workflow-related issues of RAIS were included. Evidence was narratively synthesized according to predefined thematic domains and appraised using Evidence-Based Medicine principles.ResultsRobot-assisted dental implant surgery demonstrates significantly enhanced implant placement precision compared to conventional freehand surgery and navigation-guided surgery. Nonetheless, risks remain throughout the workflow, including imaging and planning errors, registration inaccuracies, inappropriate patient selection, system failures, tracking interruptions, thermal and mechanical injury, patient movement, and limitations in complex clinical scenarios. Additional concerns include system compatibility and instrument-related infection risks. Implementation of standardized operational workflows, systematic training protocols, and technological optimizations may mitigate these risks to some extent.ConclusionDespite the considerable potential of RAIS to enhance surgical precision and standardize operations, it remains in a developmental stage with inherent limitations. Future efforts should prioritize technological advancements and rigorous clinical trials to establish long-term safety and efficacy.Clinical relevanceEstablishing the evidence base for potential risks associated with RAIS and its clinical relevance allows clinicians to optimize patient selection, improve operational workflow management, and develop targeted risk control strategies, thus enhancing the safety, predictability, and clinical value of this technology.Key words: Robot-assisted implant surgery, Dental implantation, Surgical risk, Complications, Risk management, Human-robot collaboration

Introduction

As a critical element of modern prosthetic dentistry, dental implantation significantly enhances masticatory function, aesthetics, and quality of life for patients experiencing tooth loss.1,2 However, conventional hand-guided placement is subject to notable limitations, including high operator dependency and precision control challenges, which can elevate the risk of surgical complications.3

To improve implant placement accuracy and predictability, computer-assisted implant surgery (CAIS) techniques have evolved rapidly, encompassing static surgical guides (static CAIS), dynamic navigation (dynamic CAIS), and robot-assisted implant surgery (RAIS) (robot-assisted CAIS).4 The latter integrates CBCT imaging, digital planning, real-time tracking, and robotic arm control to accurately translate preoperative plans into intraoperative execution. Despite this enhanced accuracy, not all clinical challenges in RAIS have been resolved. Current evidence indicates that robotic systems continue to encounter issues such as deviations in preoperative planning, registration errors, limitations in human-machine interaction, and insufficient capabilities for handling complex cases.11,12 (Figure 1)Notably, existing studies predominantly focus on validating the accuracy of robotic systems, while systematic reviews and critical evaluations of different risk factors, their underlying mechanisms, and corresponding risk management strategies remain relatively limited.

This review aims to provide a comprehensive analysis of potential risks associated with RAIS, assess their clinical impacts based on existing literature, explore underlying mechanisms of risk, and propose mitigation strategies. Additionally, it seeks to forecast future technological advancements. The goal is to offer reliable references for clinicians regarding the rational application of robot-assisted implantation technology and to guide future research efforts (Figure 2).

Material and methods

This narrative review synthesizes current evidence regarding risks, technical limitations, and future challenges in robot-assisted dental implant surgery. To ensure transparency and reproducibility, a structured literature search was performed across PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science databases, covering publications from January 2000 to May 2026.

The search strategy utilized both Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms related to RAIS and associated risk factors. Key search terms included: ‘robot-assisted implant surgery’, ‘robotic dental implant’, ‘dental implant robot’, ‘computer-assisted implant surgery’, ‘implant accuracy’, ‘surgical complication’, ‘risk factor’, ‘registration error’, ‘tracking error’, ‘thermal injury’, ‘learning curve’, ‘operator experience’, ‘patient movement’, ‘system failure’, and ‘surgical navigation’. Boolean logic operators (AND, OR) facilitated combinatorial searches in accordance with each database’s specific protocols. Additionally, manual back-tracking of reference lists from included articles and relevant reviews was performed to identify potentially significant studies that may have been initially omitted.

Inclusion criteria encompassed studies reporting clinical outcomes, technical performance, accuracy evaluations, safety assessments, adverse events, or operational workflows associated with robot-assisted dental implant surgery. Eligible study types included randomized controlled trials, prospective and retrospective clinical studies, in vitro experimental studies, technical validation studies, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses. Exclusion criteria encompassed studies unrelated to dental implant robots, conference papers that only presented abstracts without full texts, editorials, and critical reviews.

Screening of titles, abstracts, and full texts was conducted independently by two researchers, with final selections based on study relevance. Data extraction focused on study design, sample size, types of robotic systems, analysed risk factors, quantitative outcome measures, and primary findings. Evidence underwent narrative synthesis across predefined thematic domains, including imaging-related errors, preoperative planning deviations, registration and tracking inaccuracies, operator-related factors, risks of thermal damage, system failure incidents, patient-specific variables, and management of complex clinical cases.

This narrative review adheres strictly to Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles criteria to ensure methodological rigor and reporting transparency. To strengthen the interpretation of evidence, a comprehensive synthesis of literature was conducted based on evidence hierarchy principles commonly used in Evidence-Based Medicine. High-level studies, such as systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and randomized controlled trials, are regarded as having robust evidence bases, while prospective and retrospective clinical studies provide moderate evidence. In vitro studies, technical validation studies, and expert opinions are considered to have weaker evidence bases.

Comprehensive risk analysis in robot-assisted implant surgical workflow

Preoperative preparation phase: Issues and challenges

The success of RAIS is highly dependent on the accuracy and completeness of the preoperative digital workflow. Errors may be introduced at multiple stages, including image data acquisition, surgical planning, registration, patient selection, and operator training. These errors can propagate progressively through the digital chain, ultimately compromising surgical precision and treatment outcomes. Therefore, a systematic analysis of the sources, underlying mechanisms, and clinical relevance of potential risks in the preoperative phase is warranted.

Imaging data acquisition and processing errors

Similar to static guide and dynamic navigation-assisted implantation, imaging data accuracy is essential in RAIS, as any deviation can compromise the precision and success of subsequent procedures. CBCT is the primary method for acquiring three-dimensional images of the maxillofacial region, but its image quality is susceptible to metal artefacts, motion artefacts, and scanning parameter limitations.11,13 Metal restorations and implants often induce metal artefacts, obscuring critical anatomical structures such as bones, nerve canals, and blood vessels, which in turn compromises the accuracy of implant planning.12,14,15 The intensity of artefacts correlates with the metal content, and the direction of artefact distribution may lead to measurement deviations.16 Various metal artefact suppression reduction (MAR) algorithms have been implemented in clinical practice to mitigate these effects; however, their efficacy remains debated. Some studies report improvements in image quality, while others find limited enhancements in diagnostic accuracy or even reduced recognition capabilities for specific tasks.17,18 Motion artefacts, resulting from patient movements such as breathing and swallowing, cause image blurring and geometric distortion, negatively affecting the accuracy of 3D reconstructions and causing planned implant positions to deviate from the actual anatomical structures.19 Although motion correction techniques can alleviate motion-induced distortion from patient movement, they may compromise overall image quality.20

The selection of CBCT scanning parameters also significantly influences both image quality and the accuracy of subsequent digital planning. Key factors include voxel size, field of view, and exposure time.21,22 Conventional wisdom suggests that smaller voxels improve image resolution, thereby enhancing the recognition and measurement of anatomical structures. However, a systematic review by Fokas et al indicated substantial variability in reported measurement errors across studies. For implant planning, voxel sizes of 0.3 to 0.4 mm typically suffice for clinical applications, with no consistent evidence suggesting that further reduction enhances clinical measurement accuracy.23,24 Consequently, current research has shifted from merely pursuing higher resolution to optimizing the balance among image quality, radiation dose, and clinical requirements.

Current evidence regarding imaging data acquisition errors is primarily derived from studies focused on CBCT image quality, digitalized implant planning, and navigation-guided implantation, resulting in a moderate-strength evidence base. It consistently demonstrates that high-quality imaging data is a critical prerequisite for successful RAIS. However, rigorous clinical studies quantifying the specific impact of imaging quality on the final placement accuracy of robot-assisted implants remain lacking.

Surgical planning software failures

Advancements in digital implant technology have led to an increasing integration of computer-aided design, artificial intelligence (AI), and automated image analysis techniques within the surgical planning process.25 In contrast to conventional methods that rely heavily on operator experience for radiographic interpretation and implant planning, digital planning software enhances efficiency, improves anatomical structure recognition, and provides standardized preoperative designs for RAIS. Consequently, surgical planning software has become a critical link between patient imaging data and robotic execution systems, with its accuracy directly influencing the reliability of the final implantation plan.11,22

Errors during the planning stage may arise from both human factors and software limitations. Clinicians or technicians may introduce planning deviations due to operational inexperience, misidentification of anatomical structures, inappropriate implant parameter settings, or inadequate consideration of adjacent tooth relationships, occlusal relationships, and bone volume conditions.26 Concurrently, software algorithms can be affected by imaging artefacts, degraded image quality, anatomical variations, and the limitations of AI models, potentially leading to errors in anatomical structure recognition or inaccuracies in three-dimensional model reconstruction. Since robotic systems operate based on preoperative planning, any errors made during this phase can be directly transferred to surgical execution, compromising implant positioning and orientation.

Current studies indicate that while AI-assisted planning software improves efficiency and achieves high overall recognition accuracy in standard cases, its clinical reliability remains debated. Lawand et al27 highlight that current AI-guided implantation planning software often exhibits boundary errors, oversegmentation, undersegmentation, and anatomical structure misrecognition. Moreover, overall accuracy metrics frequently fail to adequately represent recognition quality in critical anatomical regions. For high-risk areas – such as the inferior alveolar nerve canal, maxillary sinus floor boundaries, and bone defect margins – even minor local errors can lead to serious consequences, including neural injury, maxillary sinus perforation, or deviations in implant positioning.28 Documented AI-driven planning errors include misidentification of bone augmentation areas as natural teeth, incorrect classification of non-neural structures as the inferior alveolar nerve canal, and overlooking impacted teeth or inadequately depicted mandibular bone structures.29 Such errors can lead to deviations in implant site design, increasing the risk of damage to adjacent critical anatomical structures.30,31

Overall, the current evidence regarding surgical planning software errors is primarily derived from software validation studies, technical verification studies, and research on digitalized implant planning, providing a moderate evidence base. While large-scale clinical studies directly linking software planning errors to serious adverse events in robot-assisted implantation are limited, the potential clinical implications necessitate further investigation.

Registration and alignment issues

Registration is a vital step in RAIS, ensuring precise alignment between patient anatomical structures and the robotic coordinate system; it also represents a significant source of error impacting ultimate placement accuracy. Multiple factors influence the registration process, including the methods used for positioning markers, marker stability, and image quality.11,32, 33, 34

Current research has quantitatively assessed registration and matching errors. In a study involving 40 robot-assisted implantation cases, Jin et al reported registration errors for fiducial markers averaging (0.0373 ± 0.0036) mm, with fusion matching errors between intraoral scans and CBCT at (0.157 ± 0.026) mm and (0.154 ± 0.033) mm, respectively. Platform deviation and apex deviation were (0.78 ± 0.41) mm and (0.89 ± 0.28) mm, with angular deviation measuring (1.24° ± 0.67°).35 These findings indicate that current robotic systems can control registration and matching errors to low levels under standardized workflows. However, minimal technical errors should not minimize their clinical implications, as registration initiates the entire navigational error chain. Minor inaccuracies at the registration stage may amplify during coordinate conversion and mechanical execution, ultimately compromising placement accuracy.36

Further research demonstrates that the configuration methodology for positioning markers is a critical determinant of registration precision. Zhao et al evaluated the influence of marker quantity and distribution patterns using edentulous mandible models in robot-assisted implantation. Increasing the number of markers from 3 to 6 reduced implant platform deviation from (0.73 ± 0.38 mm) to (0.53 ± 0.19 mm) and apex deviation from (0.80 ± 0.37 mm) to (0.59 ± 0.20 mm), indicating that higher marker density enhances registration stability and final placement precision. Conversely, the distribution of positioning markers, whether dispersed or centralized, did not significantly impact platform deviation, apex deviation, or angular deviation, suggesting that the quantity of markers is more critical than their spatial distribution for achieving precision. The authors also noted that displacement or instability of markers could increase registration errors, potentially undermining the reliability of navigational systems.37 This research indicates that optimizing marker design and fixation methods may offer greater clinical value in RAIS than merely adjusting marker distribution patterns.

In the context of registration, current studies primarily focus on optimizing the registration workflow while maintaining precision. Wang et al compared CBCT registration with intraoral scanning registration strategies, finding no significant differences in platform deviation, apex deviation, and angular deviation. This indicates that differing registration methods can meet the precision requirements for robot-assisted implantation. Intraoral scanning registration reduces radiation exposure from additional CBCT examinations and streamlines subsequent digital prosthetic design; however, its workflow complexity can make it more susceptible to factors such as scanner performance, lighting conditions, and tooth surface characteristics.38 Therefore, ongoing research is shifting from determining which registration approach offers higher accuracy to achieving an optimal balance among precision, safety, radiation exposure, and clinical efficiency.

Overall, evidence related to registration and matching errors primarily comes from in vitro experimental studies, observational clinical studies, and prospective technical validation studies, establishing a relatively robust evidence base. The existing evidence consistently identifies registration and matching as critical links in the error chain during RAIS. However, the stability of various registration and matching approaches in complex clinical scenarios and their influence on long-term clinical outcomes remain to be fully validated through high-quality prospective studies, highlighting the need for further investigation.

Inadequate patient selection and evaluation

Patient selection and preoperative assessment are fundamental prerequisites for the successful implementation of RAIS. While robotic systems enhance implant placement accuracy, they cannot substitute for clinical decision-making; thus, factors such as the patient’s systemic health, local anatomical conditions, and case complexity play critical roles in determining therapeutic efficacy. Inadequate preoperative assessment can lead to improper case selection, increasing surgical risks and the likelihood of adverse events.

First, the overall physical and psychological health of the patient can significantly impact the outcomes of RAIS. Systemic conditions such as diabetes and osteoporosis may hinder osseointegration and postoperative healing. Additionally, patients who experience anxiety, tension, or exhibit poor cooperation may be more prone to involuntary movements – including swallowing, head displacement, or abrupt jaw closure – that compromise navigation accuracy and surgical safety.34

Moreover, local anatomical conditions can limit the applicability of robotic systems. Restricted mouth opening is currently recognized as a prominent clinical constraint.39,40 Studies indicate that, in fully guided implant procedures within the posterior teeth region, approximately 17.5% of cases necessitated conversion to free-hand operation due to insufficient mouth opening for at least one implant.41 In particular, the posterior region, especially around the second molar, presents significant challenges for robotic arm positioning and drilling operations due to limited occlusal space and instrument maneuverability.

Robotic systems also face limitations in managing bone density, potentially causing displacement towards areas of lower bone density, leading to inaccuracies in implant positioning. Higher mandibular bone density, combined with excessive torque during implant insertion, may result in greater vertical deviations.34 Thicker palatal bone with increased resistance could lower implantation accuracy, especially in SRP Class I bone types.42 Mesiodistal bone quality may influence implant inclination more than mouth opening extent, though this hypothesis requires further validation.32,43

Evidence regarding the risks associated with inadequate patient selection and assessment primarily stems from prospective clinical studies, observational research, and technical validation studies, establishing a moderate evidence base. This evidence consistently demonstrates that restricted mouth opening, immediate implantation, and complex bone anatomy can diminish procedural predictability in robot-assisted implantation, indicating high clinical relevance. However, the effects of various patient factors on long-term clinical outcomes remain insufficiently validated by high-quality randomized controlled trials.

Efficiency challenges resulting from complex operational workflow

RAIS encompasses several steps, including preoperative image acquisition, spatial registration, robotic arm calibration, path planning, and intraoperative verification. As a result, its operational workflow is significantly more complex than that of conventional implant surgery, potentially extending procedure times and affecting patient experience – an issue particularly relevant in single-tooth implantation. Studies indicate that the average surgical duration for semiautonomous robot-assisted (Langyue, Level 2) single implant placement ranges from 20 to 25 minutes.44 A randomized controlled clinical trial by Wei et al45 revealed that the operative time for autonomous robot-assisted implantation (21.52 ± 9.81 min) was significantly longer compared to dynamic navigation systems (14.27 ± 3.57 min), suggesting substantial time costs associated with robotic systems in clinical settings. Xu et al46 further noted that preoperative preparation and intraoperative phases were elongated in autonomous robots (Yekebot, Level 2b) when examining various human-machine interaction modalities, with passive robotic (DentRobot, Level 1) surgery exhibiting the shortest duration. This efficiency variance primarily arises from the additional workflows inherent to autonomous systems, including spatial registration, robotic arm path planning, and system calibration, indicating that the efficiency of robotic platforms is significantly influenced by their design architecture and modes of collaborative interaction.

Current evidence on operative duration in RAIS is mainly sourced from randomized controlled trials, prospective clinical studies, and clinical case series, forming a robust evidence base. Existing research consistently demonstrates that RAIS incurs longer preoperative preparation and intraoperative durations compared to conventional digital surgical workflows. However, this time investment chiefly stems from system registration, calibration, and validation procedures rather than from the actual implantation phase. Innovations in automatic registration, intelligent calibration, and workflow optimization technologies are likely to further mitigate these clinical impacts.

Learning curve and operator experience dependence

Traditional views suggest that robot-assisted surgery entails a longer learning curve, especially for operators lacking prior digital surgical experience, with complex human-machine interactions potentially increasing training costs and affecting initial operational efficiency.47 However, recent research on the robotic implantation learning curve does not entirely support this perspective. A study by Zhuang et al revealed that while younger dentists without prior robotic experience exhibited a notable temporal learning effect during initial training – demonstrated by a decrease in operation time from 33.26 minutes on the first attempt to 30.47 minutes on the second (P < .001) – they quickly plateaued thereafter. Platform deviation, apex deviation, and angular deviation showed no statistically significant differences across training cohorts, indicating that the learning curve associated with robotic systems is more focused on familiarization with the operational workflow than on improvements in placement accuracy.48

Similar findings have been clinically validated in research settings. Wang et al’s retrospective analysis of 585 robot-assisted implants demonstrated that, following standardized robotic training, novice and experienced surgeons achieved comparable performance across most accuracy metrics, with statistically significant differences evident only in vertical deviation. Notably, intergroup disparities in vertical deviation were less than 0.5 mm, which the authors considered to have limited clinical significance. Additionally, both novice and experienced groups displayed an early plateau in the learning curve (P < .001), showing minimal improvement in placement accuracy with increased caseload.49 The study further illustrates that robot-assisted systems substantially reduce reliance on operator experience for achieving accuracy and help narrow the performance gap between practitioners of varying skill levels.

Current evidence concerning the learning curve in robot-assisted implantation primarily stems from model studies, small-sample clinical research, and retrospective analyses, establishing a moderate evidence base. This evidence indicates that RAIS does exhibit a learning curve related to time and workflow efficiency; however, its placement accuracy is markedly less dependent on operator expertise compared to conventional hand-guided techniques. As a result, the traditional notion that ‘robotic systems require extensive learning curves to ensure precision’ is being challenged. Nonetheless, as case complexity increases and diverse robotic platforms are adopted, further investigation into the long-term learning effects and their implications for clinical outcomes is warranted.

Potential risks and adverse events in the intraoperative phase

The intraoperative phase represents the stage with the highest concentration of risk in robot-assisted dental implant surgery, as it is influenced by multiple factors including the robotic system, human-machine interaction, patient-specific variables, and the intraoperative environment.

Robotic system failure

Robotic system failures, arising from hardware or software malfunctions, constitute a significant risk common to all types of robotic surgery. Such failures can lead to direct interruptions in procedures or potentially critical situations. Manifestations of system failure may include robotic arm malfunctions, instrument failures, console errors, software exceptions, or power/network disruptions, which can result in procedure suspension, loss of operational control, compromised navigation accuracy, or a shift to conventional surgical methods.

Current evidence regarding robotic system failures primarily derives from large-scale retrospective studies of multispecialty robotic surgery. An analysis of adverse events in 1.745 million robotic surgeries found 9377 incidents attributed to system or instrument failure, approximately 9.3% of which resulted in surgical conversion, interruption, or rescheduling.50,51 Another study involving 3935 robotic surgeries reported a 6.2% instrument failure rate, predominantly due to damage to instrument tips or joints (46.9%), cable failures (30.0%), control module malfunctions (5.3%), and abnormal drive shafts (3.2%).52 Additionally, research indicated that 34 system failures occurred during 138 robotic surgeries, comprising 61.8% robotic arm malfunctions, 26.4% instrument failures, and 11.8% console errors.53 These data demonstrate that while robotic surgery generally exhibits high safety, system failures remain a significant and non-negligible source of risk.

In the context of oral surgical robots, system failures may disrupt navigation or result in system shutdown, necessitating conversion to conventional hand-guided implant placement. In such circumstances, the surgeon must manually continue osteotomy preparation based on partially robot-drilled sites, which may lead to axial discrepancies between superior and inferior osteotomy segments, depth control deviations, or three-dimensional implant placement errors – factors that increase surgical complexity and patient risk.

Currently, direct evidence regarding the failure rates of oral surgical robots and their clinical consequences is limited, with most conclusions extrapolated from experiences in other robotic surgical domains. Compared to robotic systems in urological and general surgery, oral surgical robots present shorter operating times, more fixed operational pathways, and lower demands for degrees of freedom, suggesting potentially significant differences in failure patterns and occurrence rates. Therefore, establishing multicentre registration databases and standardized adverse event reporting systems is essential to accurately quantify the incidence of system failures, assess risk levels, and evaluate their impact on implant treatment outcomes.

Thermal damage during drilling

Drilling is a critical step in implant placement and represents a significant risk factor in RAIS. Effective coolant management is vital for maintaining bone tissue viability. When intraosseous temperatures exceed 47°C for more than 1 minute, it can lead to osteocyte necrosis and diminish the rate and quality of osteogenesis, resulting in irreversible injuries such as hyperaemia, fibrosis, osteocyte degeneration, and increased osteoclast activity.54,55

For robotic-assisted implant systems, the ability to perceive changes in bone tissue resistance remains limited. When the drill bit enters a region of high-density bone, insufficient chip evacuation, inadequate drill retraction, or reduced cooling efficiency may lead to heat accumulation, thereby increasing the risk of bone tissue overheating. In severe cases, this can result in bone burns, adversely affecting the smooth placement of the implant and subsequent osseointegration.34

However, recent research suggests that robot-assisted systems may offer innovative solutions for proactive thermal control. Zhao et al utilized an autonomous implant robot (Yekebot, Level 2b) to establish a strong correlation between drilling mechanical parameters and intraosseous temperature, subsequently developing a machine learning (ML)-based real-time temperature prediction system. This system predicts bone tissue temperature using mechanical signals collected during drilling and can automatically suspend the process and initiate cooling measures when preset temperature thresholds are reached, thereby mitigating potential risks of thermal damage.56 This advancement indicates that intelligent temperature monitoring and automated intervention technologies could become essential safety mechanisms in future robot-assisted implant systems.

Current evidence regarding thermal damage risks associated with RAIS primarily stems from ex vivo experiments and technical validation studies, resulting in a relatively limited evidence base. Zhao et al’s study is fundamentally a proof-of-concept investigation, utilizing an ex vivo rib model without validation in human clinical settings. As a result, although existing research has not identified an increased risk of thermal injury in robot-assisted implant systems, further prospective clinical studies are necessary to verify their clinical implications and long-term safety.

Mechanical damage during drilling

In addition to thermal damage, mechanical damage may occur during drilling. Excessive cutting forces or uneven bone density can cause drill bit slippage, compromising osteotomy preparation and triggering adverse histological outcomes. This risk is particularly heightened during immediate implantation or in cases of compromised alveolar bone with heterogeneous density distributions, where the drill may deviate towards areas of lower resistance, increasing the likelihood of positional implant deviations, soft tissue injuries, and instrument failures.12,34

A prospective clinical study by Liu et al involving a semiactive robotic system (Remebot) in 30 partially edentulous patients reported high overall implantation accuracy. However, significant lateral deviations were observed in cases involving immediate implantation and uneven bone surfaces, highlighting the potential for drill slippage under complex conditions. Factors such as restricted mouth opening, asymmetric extraction socket walls, and inadequate space for robotic arm movement were identified as key contributors to slippage occurrences.34

While robotic systems can substantially reduce deviations caused by hand tremors or fatigue, mechanical limitations persist in the context of immediate implantation or complex bone morphology in posterior regions. Therefore, the risk of drill bit slippage should not be overlooked. Additional protective measures during preoperative planning and intraoperative procedures are warranted, and further high-quality controlled studies are needed to quantify the incidence and clinical impact of drill slippage.

Restricted visual field and system blindness

The restricted surgical field presents a distinct risk associated with human-machine interaction in RAIS. The spatial requirements of robotic arms, positioning devices, and auxiliary equipment – especially in the posterior teeth region, in patients with restricted mouth opening, or in complex implant cases – can obstruct the surgeon’s direct line of sight. This obstruction hinders the observation of subtle variations in the surgical field and impairs real-time interventions, thereby elevating procedural demands and potential risks.12

In addition to impairing visual observation, obstruction of the visual field may lead to optical navigation system failures, a phenomenon known as ‘System Blindness’.1 Optical tracking systems are central to enabling high-precision navigation in RAIS. Their reliable performance requires continuous and stable line-of-sight connectivity between optical trackers and patient fiducial markers. If the surgeon’s head, hands, instruments, or robotic arm inadvertently enter the optical path, the system may temporarily lose its ability to track targets, potentially resulting in navigation disruptions, reduced positioning accuracy, or necessitating procedure suspension. In extreme cases, failure to promptly recognize tracking loss could increase the risk of positional deviations and related adverse events.39

Traditional perspectives assert that while optical navigation systems provide high positioning accuracy, their dependence on a continuous line of sight renders obstruction a significant risk. In contrast, electromagnetic navigation systems, which do not rely on visible light paths, are less susceptible to environmental obstructions and offer comparable positioning accuracy in specific surgical contexts, positioning them as viable alternatives.57

Recent research, however, suggests that the clinical impact of this collision risk may be diminishing. Various studies have enhanced system robustness through multicamera fusion, real-time visual optimization, and advanced tracking algorithms, maintaining high tracking precision even during partial marker occlusion.58 For instance, the integrated dual-arm high-precision oral implant navigation system developed by Tang et al2 features an Occluded Target Tracking Module, which automatically adjusts camera positions and viewing angles upon detecting marker occlusion, thus restoring target visibility while ensuring continuous tracking. In a preliminary clinical study involving six implant patients, this system achieved placement accuracy with an average angular deviation of 1.54° and an average implantation point error of 0.33 mm, suggesting that advanced tracking architectures can partially mitigate the effects of intraoperative visual obstruction.

Nevertheless, current evidence lacks high-quality comparative clinical studies to quantify the actual incidence and clinical consequences of visual obstruction and tracking failures. Additionally, variations in navigation technologies, optical tracking strategies, and operational workflows across different robotic platforms result in significant differences in their susceptibility to visual obstruction. Overall, limited visibility and System Blindness pose clinically relevant risks that remain significant, though technological advancements continue to diminish their effect on surgical precision and safety. Future studies must provide interpretations of their true clinical implications through rigorous high-quality research.

Challenges in handling complex situations

Although RAIS exhibits high precision in executing standardized osteotomy preparation and implant placement, its application in complex bone and soft tissue procedures continues to face significant limitations. While robotic systems excel at performing repetitive tasks along predefined paths, their autonomous processing capabilities are inadequate for complex surgical steps that require real-time judgment and flexible adaptation.

Current clinical studies on robot-assisted implantation primarily focus on flapless implantation and standardized implant placement protocols. Robotic systems have not yet demonstrated proficiency in executing procedures such as flap elevation, tooth extraction with socket preservation, autogenous soft tissue graft harvesting, and flap closure – tasks that depend heavily on the surgeon’s real-time perception of soft tissue tension and anatomical variations for continuous technique adaptation.3 In complex procedures like guided bone regeneration, maxillary sinus elevation, and sinus membrane grafting, robots struggle with tasks such as graft material placement, assessing sinus membrane integrity, and managing intraoperative adverse events. Consequently, both soft and hard tissue manipulation, as well as intraoperative decision-making in complex cases, remain largely reliant on experienced surgeons.4

Recent advancements in robotic technology, however, are increasingly challenging the perception that robots are only suitable for simple cases. Studies have documented robot-assisted procedures in complex scenarios, including immediate implantation, full-arch edentulous implantation, and zygomatic implantation, achieving promising preliminary accuracy outcomes.39,59, 60, 61 These findings suggest that robotic systems are expanding their application scope in complex anatomical environments, offering benefits that extend beyond standardized procedures to include precise drilling trajectories and implant positional control in challenging contexts.

Evidence regarding robotic systems’ proficiency in handling complex cases primarily derives from case reports, technical validation studies, and small-sample clinical studies, establishing a moderate evidence base. Current findings confirm that while robotic systems maintain high precision in standardized implant operations, they exhibit significant limitations in contexts involving complex bone augmentation, soft tissue reconstruction, and intraoperative decision-making. Therefore, clinically complex cases present a substantial challenge for the advancement of current robot-assisted implantation technology.

Patient movement and mouth closure

Unplanned intraoperative patient movement is identified as a significant risk factor in RAIS. Since the accuracy of robotic system path planning and navigation relies on stable correspondence between patient anatomical structures and the robotic coordinate system, any positional changes during surgery can adversely influence navigation accuracy.

Clinical observations confirm that patients may exhibit involuntary movements during implant procedures, including trembling, swallowing, head movement, or sudden jaw closure, often due to nervousness, anxiety, or discomfort.62,63 In such instances, surgeons typically must halt the procedure, reposition the drill, and stabilize the patient before resuming surgery. This indicates that patient movement can compromise navigation accuracy, potentially extend surgical duration, and increase the risk of intraoperative interruptions.

Advances in real-time optical tracking, dynamic registration, and motion compensation technologies have enabled modern robotic systems to detect and correct slow, low-amplitude head movements in real-time, thereby minimizing their impact on placement accuracy. However, limitations remain in current motion compensation techniques. Rapid, substantial, or abrupt head movements and sudden jaw closures can lead to inevitable time delays in detection and correction mechanisms.64 During this interval, the drill bit may deviate from its predetermined trajectory, increasing the risk of damaging anatomical structures. Unlike laparoscopic or orthopaedic robots, oral surgical robots operate within the highly mobile mandibular system, where the temporomandibular joint facilitates positional shifts during stress responses.59 In cases of significant movement or abrupt mouth closure, manual intervention may be necessary, requiring the surgeon to pause the procedure to address patient instability.

Currently, evidence regarding the risk of patient movement primarily originates from technical validation studies, observational studies, and expert experiences, constituting a moderately weak evidence base. Existing data support the assertion that patient movement poses a significant potential risk factor affecting the safety and precision of robot-assisted implantation. Nonetheless, contemporary tracking and compensation technologies have partially mitigated this influence, highlighting the need for further quantification of the true risk magnitude through high-quality clinical research.

Surgeon operation

In robot-assisted dental implant surgery, challenges confronting the surgeon remain substantial. While collaborative robotic systems aid surgeons in executing precision tasks, their accuracy is still influenced by the operator’s hand stability and the reliability of tactile feedback. Prolonged operation or high-pressure conditions can result in physiological hand tremors, while perceptual inaccuracies may lead to misjudgements in tissue resistance or instrument positioning. For collaborative robotic systems, such hand tremors and perceptual inaccuracies induce lateral deviations of 0.25 mm and angular deviations of 0.5°, potentially compromising implant placement accuracy.65

In Level 2 robotic systems, implant surgeons monitor screens rather than assessing actual intraoral conditions in real time. This limitation prevents prompt addressing of unexpected intraoperative incidents, thereby increasing the risk and severity of adverse events.40

Current findings demonstrate that robot-assisted implantation significantly reduces manual procedural errors and diminishes experience-based disparities affecting placement accuracy; however, the critical roles of surgeons in case assessment, intraoperative supervision, and risk control cannot be entirely replaced.

Other technology-specific dependencies and device-related risks

Adverse events resulting from system incompatibility

The success of RAIS relies heavily on the seamless compatibility of integrated system components, as any incompatibility can introduce novel adverse events.

Surgical planning instruments and drill sleeves are typically optimized for specific proprietary implant systems.36 In principle, deviating from these recommended implant specifications may compromise instrument fixation, create dimensional mismatches between drills and osteotomy sites, and lead to procedural inconsistencies. Such deviations not only jeopardize intraoperative accuracy but can also hinder complete abutment seating and cause screw misfits, resulting in mechanical and biological complications.66 Additionally, the heterogeneity of outcomes in dental implant rehabilitation clinical trials complicates comparative effectiveness research and may be attributed to data incompatibility issues across different systems.67

Direct clinical evidence addressing compatibility concerns in robot-assisted implant systems is relatively scarce. Most current studies utilize single robotic platforms with proprietary implant systems, lacking comparative investigations across various robotic platforms and implant systems. Insufficient evidence exists to confirm that system incompatibility significantly increases failure or adverse event rates in robot-assisted implantation. Consequently, current research on system compatibility risks remains predominantly theoretical and is not substantiated by high-quality clinical evidence, resulting in a limited evidence base. Future research focusing on compatibility among diverse robotic platforms, implant systems, and digital workflows is necessary to elucidate their actual clinical impact.

Instrument-related infection

Robot-assisted surgery presents novel challenges in infection control.68 Compared to conventional implant surgery, robotic systems introduce additional equipment, including robotic arms, tracking devices, and specialized instruments. The operational workflow necessitates frequent contact and movement of multiple components within and beyond the surgical site, theoretically increasing the risk of microbial contamination and cross-infection.

Existing studies indicate that infection risks in robot-assisted surgeries are primarily associated with biofilm formation on instrument surfaces and inadequate reprocessing of devices in RAIS. Biofilm formation on surgical instruments and implant surfaces is recognized as a significant source of postoperative infections, with common pathogens including Staphylococcus aureus, methicillin-resistant S. aureus, and coagulase-negative staphylococci.69,70 The insidious onset and atypical clinical presentations associated with biofilm-related infections complicate both diagnosis and source tracing.

Although current studies on robot-assisted implantation report no intraoperative infections, reusable robot-specific instruments remain a notable potential source of risk that requires careful management. For instance, drill shanks, instrument interfaces, and certain connecting components often feature complex geometries, confined cavities, and precision-fit joints that may retain microbial residues and promote biofilm colonization if manufacturer-recommended cleaning, disinfection, and sterilization protocols are not rigorously followed, thereby increasing the risk of delayed infections.71, 72, 73 Adhering to strict instrument reprocessing protocols and infection control procedures is essential to ensure the safety of robot-assisted implant surgeries.

Overall, the available evidence regarding infection risks in robot-assisted implantation is mainly derived from studies on robotic infection control in other surgical fields, instrument reprocessing research, and theoretical analyses. The evidence base remains limited, highlighting the need for future large-scale clinical studies to clarify actual risks.

Prevention and management strategies

As an advanced technology, robot-assisted dental implant surgery holds significant promise for enhancing surgical precision. However, developing strategies for the prevention and management of potential risks, as well as defining future trajectories, remains equally crucial. To minimize risks in robot-assisted dental implant surgery, comprehensive preventive and management strategies spanning the preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative phases are essential.

Preoperative optimization

Optimization of imaging data acquisition and processing

Similar to static guides and dynamic navigation systems, RAIS relies on high-quality CBCT data. In patients with metallic restorations, removable devices should be removed whenever possible; otherwise, optimized scanning protocols, metal artefact reduction (MAR) algorithms, and postprocessing techniques should be used to minimize artefacts.74 Adjustments to parameters such as voxel size, field of view, and kilovolt peak can enhance image quality, though this requires careful balancing of radiation dose against noise levels.75, 76, 77 Furthermore, motion artefacts may be reduced through patient immobilization and shorter scan times. Because RAIS is highly dependent on registration accuracy and trajectory execution, strict control of image acquisition and processing is essential to minimize error propagation.

Enhanced surgical planning and software management

Standardized software training is essential to ensure proficiency in surgical planning, anatomical identification, and software operation.78 For complex cases, multidisciplinary team planning is recommended. Regular software updates, clinical feedback, and algorithm optimization can further improve planning accuracy and system reliability.79

Enhancing registration accuracy

Positioning markers should exhibit good biocompatibility, stability, and radiographic visibility, and be securely fixed to the bone or surgical template to ensure reliable registration.80 Standardized registration protocols and operator training are essential for accurate navigation.

Previous studies in zygomatic implant surgery, endodontics, neurosurgery, and orthognathic navigation have shown that marker number, distribution, and position all influence registration accuracy81, 82, 83, 84 (Table 3). However, current evidence in RAIS suggests that marker quantity may have a greater impact on accuracy than spatial distribution – a finding that does not completely align with the established importance of spatial configuration in other navigated surgical disciplines.37 The specific mechanisms underlying the spatial distribution of markers and their clinical implications warrant further exploration in future studies.

Thorough patient selection and comprehensive assessment

Careful patient selection is essential for successful RAIS. Preoperative assessment should include systemic health, particularly uncontrolled diseases such as diabetes and osteoporosis, as well as local anatomical conditions including bone quality and critical structures evaluated by CBCT.89

Compared with conventional surgery, RAIS requires greater surgical space and more favourable anatomical conditions; therefore, patients with severe bone deficiency, complex sinus or nerve anatomy, high-risk esthetic sites, or limited mouth opening should be selected cautiously. Patients should also be informed of the benefits and limitations of RAIS to establish realistic expectations.34

Optimization of operational workflow and training system

Although the learning curve of RAIS mainly affects workflow efficiency rather than implant placement accuracy, registration, calibration, planning verification, and human–robot interaction may still challenge inexperienced operators. Therefore, standardized training programs remain essential for the safe implementation of RAIS.

Training should include software planning, registration, human–robot interaction, and complication management, supported by simulation and clinical practice. Emerging virtual reality (VR) and augmented reality (AR) technologies can further enhance procedural training in a risk-free environment.90,91 Moreover, experienced surgeons should mentor novice operators, and consistent academic exchange should be encouraged to elevate collective clinical proficiency.3

Intraoperative technical improvements

Robotic system safeguarding and failure response

Robotic system hardware and software should be continuously monitored during surgery, with immediate alerts and predefined emergency protocols in place to manage system failures. Backup instruments should be readily available to enable prompt conversion to conventional surgery when necessary, and regular emergency drills are recommended to ensure team preparedness.34

Workflow interruptions should be systematically documented, including their causes, manual interventions, postoperative outcomes, and patient prognosis. These records can support continuous improvement of system reliability, contingency planning, and surgical safety.34

Refined protocols during drilling procedures

Sharp drill bits, adequate saline irrigation, and appropriate drill selection based on bone density are essential to minimize thermal injury during robotic drilling.56,80 Robotic systems should incorporate overload protection to reduce the risk of drill fracture, and procedures should be suspended immediately if drill binding or fracture occurs, followed by radiographic evaluation and appropriate management.

When the surgical robot is not in use, it is crucial to ensure that no drill bit remains attached to the instrument tip and that the tip is positioned away from the patient to prevent accidental injury from unintended activation. Both the surgeon and the assistant must maintain constant vigilance over the foot-controlled panel to avert unintentional activations that may cause harm to the patient.

Optimization of surgical field and intraoperative system registration

Robotic arms should be compact, flexible, and designed to minimize obstruction of the surgical field, particularly in posterior regions with limited operating space.92

Strict adherence to optical tracking protocols is essential to maintain uninterrupted tracking throughout the procedure. If tracking is interrupted, the system should immediately alert the operator and suspend robotic motion until tracking is restored. Techniques such as multicamera fusion, real-time visual optimization, and real-time tracking algorithms have been effective in reducing the impact of obstructions while maintaining high-precision tracking during instrument movement or partial marker loss.93,94

Improvements in complex case handling capacity and patient mobility management

Complex procedures such as guided bone regeneration and maxillary sinus floor elevation still require human judgment, haptic feedback, and intraoperative decision-making, which current robotic systems cannot fully replicate.3 Future robotic platforms should improve tissue manipulation capabilities, integrate AI-assisted decision support, and expand their application to more complex implant procedures.34

To minimize the impact of patient movement, appropriate head stabilization and real-time motion tracking should be adopted during surgery. Future systems may further improve safety through AI-based motion prediction and adaptive control, enabling real-time compensation for unexpected patient movements.34,95

Other relevant risk management

System compatibility management

Clinicians should use implant systems and instruments that are recommended by or validated for compatibility with the robotic platform to ensure accurate drilling, stable implant placement, and prosthetic fit.96, 97, 98 Whenever possible, a unified digital workflow from preoperative planning to prosthetic restoration should be adopted to minimize compatibility-related errors and improve treatment outcomes.

Because interoperability among current robotic platforms remains limited, future development should prioritize open digital platforms and standardized data interfaces to facilitate data exchange and broader clinical adoption of RAIS.

Device-related infection prevention and management

Robot-specific reusable instruments should undergo standardized cleaning, disinfection, and sterilization after each use to prevent biofilm formation.99 Regular biofilm monitoring is recommended, and contaminated instruments should be promptly reprocessed or replaced to reduce the risk of delayed postoperative infections.100

Future prospects in RAIS

Notably, between 2024 and 2026, the focus of development in robot-assisted implantation technology has gradually shifted from merely improving placement accuracy to constructing a more intelligent digital ecosystem. Recent advances in multimodal AI, large vision models, human-robot collaborative decision-making, multisensor fusion, and closed-loop feedback control are driving the evolution of robotic systems from ‘executing pre-designed plans’ to integrated intelligent systems capable of ‘perception-decision-execution’. These advancements hold the potential not only to enhance placement accuracy but also to reshape conventional perceptions of established risks.4,25,101 However, most of these technologies remain in the early stages of clinical validation, and their long-term safety, reliability, and clinical benefits require further confirmation through ongoing research.

Deep integration of AI and ML

AI and ML are rapidly becoming integral components of digital oral healthcare workflows, with significant implications for the future of RAIS.102 In recent years, AI systems utilizing deep learning and computer vision techniques have been widely applied in various aspects of dentistry, such as oral imaging analysis, anatomical structure recognition, and treatment plan design.25,101,103,104 As AI technology evolves, research priorities are shifting from single-task recognition towards the development of multimodal AI and large vision models. In the future, robot-assisted implant systems are expected to not only automatically identify critical anatomical structures in CBCT but also integrate data from multiple sources – such as intraoral scans, facial imaging, periodontal parameters, and patient histories – to facilitate comprehensive and personalized treatment decisions.25

Moreover, AI models are anticipated to predict the risk of adverse events based on large-scale clinical data, aiding clinicians in identifying high-risk patients and formulating individualized prevention strategies, thereby enhancing the efficiency and accuracy of preoperative planning.105,106 The integration of AI within robotic systems could also pave the way for intraoperative real-time error monitoring and adaptive control mechanisms. By continuously analysing patient movements, drill trajectory deviations, and osseous tissue feedback, robotic systems could exhibit capabilities for dynamic path correction and risk notifications. Additionally, emerging AI algorithms, such as reinforcement learning, could enable robotic systems to develop continuous learning capabilities. This would allow for the dynamic optimization of path planning and execution strategies based on intraoperative feedback, ultimately enhancing placement precision and operational safety.104,107

Currently, robot-assisted implant systems predominantly belong to the task autonomy stage, with their operations heavily reliant on preoperative settings.4 Future AI advancements should focus not only on improving imaging recognition and planning efficiency but also on transitioning robotic systems from ‘task autonomy’ to higher levels of autonomy, such as ‘conditional autonomy’.108 By incorporating real-time bone density monitoring, patient motion tracking, and identification of critical anatomical structures, robotic systems could dynamically adjust surgical strategies in response to intraoperative environmental changes, achieving improved human-machine synergy and autonomous decision-making capabilities.

With ongoing advancements in standardized database development, multicentre validation studies, and AI technologies, the precision, individualization, and intelligence of RAIS are expected to improve significantly. This evolution will progressively enable a transition from traditional preoperative planning to comprehensive intelligent process management.

Integration of AR and VR

AR and VR technologies have been increasingly integrated into dental education, digital preoperative planning, and navigated surgery, emerging as significant complementary tools for future RAIS.109,110 During procedures, AR can superimpose real-time preoperative planning information onto patient-specific anatomical structures, helping surgeons understand the spatial positioning of the robotic arm and reducing spatial misjudgements caused by visual field obstructions.91,111 Meanwhile, VR systems offer high-fidelity simulation environments for procedural training in planning, registration, and drilling, thereby enhancing operational proficiency with the robotic system.112, 113, 114

Despite their promising applications, current clinical implementations of AR and VR in robot-assisted implantation remain largely exploratory. The effectiveness of AR systems is heavily reliant on accurate registration between virtual models and patient anatomies; however, challenges such as tracking latency, display resolution limitations, and ergonomic constraints may hinder their clinical adoption.91 Furthermore, there is insufficient evidence regarding whether the efficacy of VR training can fully translate to operative capabilities in real clinical environments.115,116

As such, future research should focus not only on enhancing the effectiveness of visual presentations but also on improving the accuracy of virtual-physical integration. It should also aim to optimize the human-machine interaction experience and validate the actual impact of these technologies on surgical precision, learning efficiency, and patient outcomes through high-quality clinical studies.

Novel sensors and feedback systems

While current robot-assisted implant systems provide precise position navigation and mechanical execution, their intraoperative tissue state sensing capabilities remain limited. Unlike experienced implant surgeons, existing robotic systems struggle to accurately perceive variations in bone density, drilling resistance, and subtle mechanical feedback during implant placement. Hence, these systems continue to depend on the surgeon’s experience and clinical judgment, particularly in complex anatomical structures, immediate implantation, and bone augmentation procedures.

To address these limitations, the development of novel sensor technologies and intelligent feedback systems is crucial for the advancement of RAIS.117,118 Advanced sensing technologies, such as piezoelectric sensors, optical fibre sensors, and torque sensors, show potential for real-time monitoring of force, torque, vibration, and temperature fluctuations during drilling. These systems can convert such data into visual or haptic feedback, thereby improving robotic systems’ perception of intraoperative conditions.46,119,120

Nevertheless, most studies related to these technologies remain at the laboratory validation and prototype development stages. A primary challenge lies in balancing sensor sensitivity with system stability while effectively converting complex biomechanical data into clinically actionable feedback signals. Additionally, the incorporation of additional sensor modules may increase system complexity and costs, and it is essential to verify whether the resulting clinical benefits would justify these additional expenditures.

Minimally invasive interventions for complex cases

Robot-assisted surgery procedures are inherently suited for minimally invasive approaches.121,122 Currently, the primary application of robot-assisted implantation is centred on standardized implant placement. However, its use in more complex cases, such as sinus augmentation and aesthetic zone reconstruction, remains relatively limited. These intricate procedures often require extensive soft tissue flap reflection and complex intraoperative maneuvers, which can lead to greater surgical trauma and heightened technical sensitivity.

In the future, advancements in AI, intelligent feedback systems, and robotic manipulation capabilities are expected to facilitate the expansion of robotic technology into more complex domains of implant surgery. By enabling more precise preoperative planning, stable trajectory control, and real-time avoidance of critical anatomical structures, robotic systems could potentially minimize unnecessary tissue damage while ensuring therapeutic efficacy.123 This progression may promote the development of minimally invasive treatment approaches for complex surgical cases.

However, high-quality clinical studies that validate the benefits of robotic systems in complex reconstructive procedures are still lacking. Therefore, future research should focus not only on enhancing the precision of robotic systems but, more importantly, on assessing whether these technologies genuinely reduce surgical trauma, shorten recovery times, and improve long-term clinical outcomes in complex cases.

Standardization and regulatory framework development

As robot-assisted implant technology continues to advance rapidly, its application is transitioning from a phase of technological validation to one of clinical dissemination. Nevertheless, significant variations exist among different robotic platforms regarding registration methods, tracking systems, operative workflows, human-machine interaction modes, and evaluation metrics for accuracy. These discrepancies contribute to heterogeneity in research outcomes and hinder direct comparisons and evidence-based evaluations of the clinical efficacy of RAIS.

Additionally, it is crucial to promote the refinement of relevant regulations and ethical guidelines.1 Issues related to data privacy protection, algorithmic transparency, accountability, and ethical considerations in AI-assisted clinical decision-making are emerging as new regulatory challenges. For example, when AI-planned outcomes conflict with clinical decisions, or unexpected incidents arise during robotic operations, there is often a lack of consensus on responsibility delineation.124,125

Thus, future development must prioritize not only the enhancement of robotic systems’ technical capabilities but also the establishment of comprehensive regulatory frameworks that encompass device research and development, clinical implementation, data governance, and AI-assisted decision support. By establishing unified international standards, quality control systems, and ethical guidelines, it will be possible to enhance comparative analysis and interoperability across platforms. This will ultimately facilitate the safe, standardized, and sustainable development of robot-assisted implantation technology.

Conclusion

Robot-assisted dental implant surgery represents a pivotal progression in oral implantology, offering substantial improvements in surgical precision, efficiency, and safety. However, existing research reveals important limitations, including insufficient long-term follow-up data, small sample sizes, and significant heterogeneity across studies, which necessitate careful interpretation of current findings. While numerous in vitro studies and pilot clinical trials have validated the enhanced precision of this surgical approach, large-scale, long-term clinical RCTs remain scarce.

Future advancements in RAIS will require interdisciplinary collaboration among experts in dental medicine, biomedical engineering, AI, materials science, and regulatory ethics. Through such multidisciplinary efforts, sustained innovation and comprehensive standardization within regulatory frameworks can address current challenges, thereby maximizing the potential of robotic technology in dental implantation and achieving more predictable therapeutic outcomes for patients.
