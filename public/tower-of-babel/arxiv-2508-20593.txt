On the extrema of the mean subtree order of graphs
Stijn Cambie, Jorik Jooken, Stephan Wagner

Abstract
It has been conjectured that the minimum and maximum of the mean subtree order among connected graphs of order n n are attained by the path P n P_{n} and clique K n K_{n} , respectively. Extending ideas due to Haslegrave and Vince, we confirm that the minimum is indeed attained by P n P_{n} . On the other hand, we discuss different approaches (both promising and flawed) that could lead to a proof of the extremality of K n K_{n} .

Let

\displaystyle\geq\frac{n+(\lvert T\rvert+1)}{2}\sum_{S\in\mathcal{T}}N(G,S)+\frac{(n-1-m)+\lvert T\rvert}{2}N(G_{1},T)

\displaystyle=\frac{n+\lvert T\rvert}{2}\left(\sum_{S\in\mathcal{T}}N(G,S)+N(G_{1},T)\right)+\frac{1}{2}\Big(\sum_{S\in\mathcal{T}}N(G,S)-(m+1)N(G_{1},T)\Big)

3
Counterexamples to the universal version of

In this section, we consider the change in the mean subtree order under adding or removing an edge. Specifically, in the first part we construct examples of graphs with the property that “many” potential edge additions reduce the mean subtree order, while in the second part we construct examples of graphs with the property that “many” potential edge removals increase the mean subtree order.

3.1
Proof of
1.3

Here we provide a construction of graphs
G
for which there is essentially only one edge satisfying
\mu(G+e)>\mu(G).

Let us first collect a few facts about subtrees of complete graphs that will be useful in the following.

•

The number of spanning trees of a complete graph
K_{n}
is well known to be
n^{n-2}
, and it was shown that the number of subtrees asymptotically only differs by a constant factor:
N(K_{n})\sim e^{1/e}n^{n-2}
(see
[
6
, Thm. 3.3]
as well as
[
5
]
).

•

By
[
16
, Cor. 2]
,
\mu(K_{n})=n-\frac{1}{e}+o(1)
.

•

The number of subtrees that contain a specific vertex
v
is

N(K_{n})-N(K_{n-1})\sim N(K_{n})\sim e^{1/e}n^{n-2}.

•

Let us fix a second vertex
w
. Among the subtrees that contain
v
, precisely
N(K_{n-1})-N(K_{n-2})\sim N(K_{n-1})\sim e^{1/e}(n-1)^{n-3}\sim e^{1/e-1}n^{n-3}
do not contain
w
. It follows that

\mu(K_{n},v)=n-(n-1)\frac{N(K_{n-1})-N(K_{n-2})}{N(K_{n})-N(K_{n-1})}=n-\frac{1}{e}+o(1).

•

Every edge
uv
is contained in the same number of subtrees. Since the average number of edges in a subtree is
\mu(K_{n})-1=n-O(1)
, it follows that the proportion of subtrees that contain
uv
is

\frac{n-O(1)}{\binom{n}{2}}=\frac{2}{n}+O\big(n^{-2}\big),

which means that the number of subtrees containing a fixed edge
uv
is asymptotically equal to
\frac{2}{n}N(K_{n})\sim 2e^{1/e}n^{n-3}
.

•

If we fix a third vertex
w\notin\{u,v\}
, there are (by the same argument applied to
K_{n-1}
) asymptotically
\frac{2}{n-1}N(K_{n-1})\sim 2e^{1/e-1}n^{n-4}
subtrees that contain
uv
, but not
w
. Hence we also have

n-\mu(K_{n},uv)\sim(n-2)\cdot\frac{\frac{2}{n-1}N(K_{n-1})}{\frac{2}{n}N(K_{n})}\sim\frac{1}{e}

or

\mu(K_{n},uv)=n-\frac{1}{e}+o(1).

Thus
\mu(K_{n})
,
\mu(K_{n},v)
and
\mu(K_{n},uv)
are equal up to
o(1)
, but as
4.7
in the following section shows (more generally for graphs that are both vertex- and edge-transitive), we have
\mu(K_{n})<\mu(K_{n},v)<\mu(K_{n},uv)
for all
n\geq 2
.

Now let
D_{n,w}
be a barbell of order
n
, which consists of two cliques of order
w
and a path that connects the two cliques (
depicts a concrete example, namely

). In the following, we show that barbells provide the desired examples for a suitable choice of

and

.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
G=D_{n,w}
with
w=o(n)=\omega(\log n)
be a barbell of sufficiently large order
n
.
There is essentially only one edge (out of the
2w-2
distinct choices up to isomorphism) whose addition increases the mean subtree order.

Proof.

Let the internal vertices (vertices of degree
2
) on the path be
v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n-2w}.
Let
v_{0}
and
v_{n-2w+1}
be the ends of the path, i.e., the two vertices of maximum degree in
G
.
Let
u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{w-1}
and
u^{\prime}_{1},u^{\prime}_{2},\ldots,u^{\prime}_{w-1}
be the other vertices of the two cliques, respectively.

For our choice of
w
,
w^{w-2}
grows faster than
n
(in fact faster than any power of
n
), which implies that almost all subtrees of
G
contain the entire path
v_{0}v_{1}\cdots v_{n-2w+1}
. All other subtrees are negligible. It follows from our observations on subtrees of complete graphs that
\mu(G)=n-\frac{2}{e}+o(1)
.

To prove the inequality
\mu(G+e)<\mu(G),
it is sufficient to show that
\mu(G+e,e)<\mu(G)
, since
\mu(G+e)
is a weighted average of
\mu(G+e,e)
and
\mu(G)
—note here that
\mu(G)
can be interpreted as the average subtree order of all subtrees in
G+e
that do not contain
e
.
We do so for all possibilities (up to relabelling, i.e., for a representative of each isomorphism class). Here the cases are ordered by the proportion of edges that belong to each case.

Case 1:

e=v_{i}v_{j}
with
\lvert i-j\rvert\geq 2.

It is still the case (by the same reasoning) that all but a negligible proportion of the subtrees of
G+e
that contain
e
also contain
v_{0}
and
v_{n-2w+1}
. The subtrees containing the edge
e=v_{i}v_{j}
are missing
\frac{\lvert i-j\rvert-1}{3}
of the vertices in
\{v_{i},v_{i+1},\ldots,v_{j}\}
on average. To see this, observe that the complement of a subtree that contains
e
necessarily induces a subpath between
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
.
This excluded subpath (which cannot be a single vertex) has an average length of
\frac{\lvert i-j\rvert+2}{3}
and thus an average number of internal vertices equal to
\frac{\lvert i-j\rvert-1}{3}
.

This implies that
\mu(G+e,e)=\mu(G)-\frac{\lvert i-j\rvert-1}{3}+o(1)
. If

is sufficiently large, we can conclude that
\mu(G+e,e)<\mu(G).

Case 2:

e=u_{1}v_{i}
with

In this case, we can use the same argument as before to show that all but a negligible proportion of the subtrees of
G+e
that contain

\frac{1}{e}+o(1)
of the vertices of the clique induced by the

. For the remaining part, we have the following three possibilities:

•

The subtree contains

, but not the entire path from

to

. By the inclusion-exclusion principle, there are

, and

•

The subtree contains the entire path from

to

\sim\binom{i+1}{2}\cdot e^{1/e}w^{w-2}

\mu(G+e,e)=n-\frac{i-1}{3}-\frac{2}{e}+o(1)=\mu(G)-\frac{i-1}{3}+o(1)<\mu(G)
in this case if

Case 3:

e=u_{1}u^{\prime}_{1}.

In this case, almost all subtrees containing

(the precise verification is similar to the previous two cases). It follows that
\mu(G+e,e)\sim\frac{2n}{3}
, which is smaller than
\mu(G)
.

Case 4:

e=u_{1}v_{1}.

This is the only case where

and thus
\mu(G+e)>\mu(G)
. To see this, we distinguish different types of subtrees of

and

•

Type A: subtrees of

that do not contain the edge
v_{0}v_{1}
,

•

Type B: subtrees of
G

v_{0}v_{1}

•

Type C: subtrees of
G+e

v_{0}v_{1}
,

•

Type D: subtrees of
G+e

Every subtree of type B is obtained by independently choosing two parts and combining them:

•

a subtree of the clique induced by the

that contains

•

a subtree of the graph induced by the

3.2
Deleting an edge can increase the mean subtree order

As we have seen that adding an edge can decrease the mean subtree order, deleting an edge can conversely increase it. Here we illustrate this with concrete examples of graphs that have many edges for which this is the case.

The join of two graphs
G
and
H
, written as
G\vee H
, is defined as the graph with vertex set
V(G)\cup V(H)
and edge set
E(G)\cup E(H)\cup\{xy\mid x\in V(G),y\in V(H)\}
. Let
G=K_{n}\vee mK_{1}
(equivalently,
K_{n+m}
with a
K_{m}
removed).
This is a complete multipartite graph with partition classes of size
n
and
m
times
1
.
Note that every induced subgraph
G^{\prime}
of
G
is itself of the form
K_{n^{\prime}}\vee m^{\prime}K_{1}.

Let
e
be an edge between two vertices of degree
n+m-1
in
G
.
Now
G\setminus e
is a complete multipartite graph with partition classes of size
n
,
2
and
m-2
times
1
.

The number of spanning subtrees of
G
and
G\setminus e
is given by
f(n,m)=(n+m)^{n-1}n^{m-1}
and

g(n,m)=(n+m-2)(n+m)^{n-2}n^{m-1}
, respectively. These formulas can be derived from Kirchhoff’s matrix-tree theorem, see
[
11
]
. Note in particular that
f(n,0)=n^{n-2}
is precisely Cayley’s formula.

Now the number of subtrees in

can be computed by taking the number of spanning trees over all subgraphs of the form

There are
\binom{n}{j}\binom{m}{i}

Here,

can be arbitrary nonnegative integers, except for the case

where the only subtrees are single vertices.
So the number of subtrees of

N(G)=m+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i=0}^{m}\binom{n}{j}\binom{m}{i}f(j,i),

and the total order of all subtrees of

R(G)=m+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i=0}^{m}\binom{n}{j}\binom{m}{i}(i+j)f(j,i).

The induced subgraphs of

or
(K_{j}\vee iK_{1})\setminus e.

There are
\binom{n-2}{j-2}\binom{m}{i}
possibilities for the latter.
In the same way as for

\displaystyle N(G\setminus e)

\displaystyle=m+\sum_{i=0}^{m}\binom{m}{i}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\left(\binom{n}{j}-\binom{n-2}{j-2}\right)f(j,i)+\binom{n-2}{j-2}g(j,i)\right)\mbox{ and }

\displaystyle R(G\setminus e)

\displaystyle=m+\sum_{i=0}^{m}\binom{m}{i}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(i+j)\left(\left(\binom{n}{j}-\binom{n-2}{j-2}\right)f(j,i)+\binom{n-2}{j-2}g(j,i)\right).

For many values of

\mu(G)=\frac{R(G)}{N(G)}<\frac{R(G\setminus e)}{N(G\setminus e)}=\mu(G\setminus e)
. Cameron and Mol
[

]
present the special case

and

. We verified by computer (see
https://github.com/JorikJooken/averageSubtreeOrderOfGraphs
, document DeletionEdge_mu_difference) that this also holds for all

that are multiples of

. For these examples with

of the edges have the property that

(restated below and with a short proof for completeness). We write
[n]=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
for the standard set of cardinality

for its power set.
For a family
\mathcal{A}\subset 2^{[n]}
, we write
\mathcal{A}_{i}=\{A\in\mathcal{A}\mid i\in A\}.

Lemma 4.4
.

For a non-empty family
\mathcal{A}\subset 2^{[n]}
, set

av(\mathcal{A})=\frac{1}{\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert}\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\lvert A\rvert
.
If
\mathcal{A}\subset 2^{[n]}
is a non-uniform family (i.e., not all cardinalities of the sets in
\mathcal{A}
are the same), then there exists an element
i\in[n]
such that

av(\mathcal{A}_{i})>av(\mathcal{A})
.

Proof.

We have

\max\{av(\mathcal{A}_{i})\mid i\in[n]\}\geq\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}_{i}}\lvert A\rvert}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lvert\mathcal{A}_{i}\rvert},

since a weighted average (with positive coefficients) is bounded by the maximum.
Writing the cardinalities of the sets
\{(A,i,j)\mid A\in\mathcal{A};i,j\in A\}
and
\{(A,i)\mid A\in\mathcal{A};i\in A\}
in two different ways, we get

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}_{i}}\lvert A\rvert=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\lvert A\rvert^{2}
and
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lvert\mathcal{A}_{i}\rvert=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\lvert A\rvert
. Finally,
\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\lvert A\rvert^{2}>\frac{1}{\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert}\big(\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\lvert A\rvert\big)^{2}
by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality (strict inequality holds by the assumption that the cardinalities of the sets in
\mathcal{A}
are not all the same). Putting these together, we obtain

\max\{av(\mathcal{A}_{i})\mid i\in[n]\}\geq\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}_{i}}\lvert A\rvert}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lvert\mathcal{A}_{i}\rvert}=\frac{\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\lvert A\rvert^{2}}{\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\lvert A\rvert}>\frac{\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\lvert A\rvert}{\lvert\mathcal{A}\rvert}=av(\mathcal{A}),

and the statement follows.
∎

Proposition 4.7

Recall that

\displaystyle\mu(G)

Lemma 5.7
.

For every graph
G
with
n
vertices and every positive integer
k<n

s_{k}(G)\geq\frac{s_{k}(K_{n})}{s_{k+1}(K_{n})}s_{k+1}(G).

Proof.

We have

s_{k}(G)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\subseteq V(G)\\
\lvert A\rvert=k\end{subarray}}s_{k}(G[A])=\frac{1}{n-k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\subseteq V(G)\\
\lvert B\rvert=k+1\end{subarray}}\sum_{v\in B}s_{k}(G[B-v])=\frac{1}{n-k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\subseteq V(G)\\
\lvert B\rvert=k+1\end{subarray}}s_{k}(G[B]).

To see why the second identity holds, note that every subset
A
of
k
vertices is contained in precisely
n-k
subsets

of
k+1
vertices and thus counted
n-k
times in the double sum. Now we can apply

s_{k}(G)\geq\frac{1}{n-k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\subseteq V(G)\\
\lvert B\rvert=k+1\end{subarray}}\frac{s_{k}(K_{k+1})}{s_{k+1}(K_{k+1})}s_{k+1}(G[B])=\frac{1}{n-k}\cdot\frac{s_{k}(K_{k+1})}{s_{k+1}(K_{k+1})}s_{k+1}(G).

Using the fact that every induced subgraph of a complete graph is again complete, one can apply the same reasoning as before in the opposite direction to show that

\frac{1}{n-k}\cdot\frac{s_{k}(K_{k+1})}{s_{k+1}(K_{k+1})}=\frac{s_{k}(K_{n})}{s_{k+1}(K_{n})}.

Alternatively, one can also use the explicit formula
