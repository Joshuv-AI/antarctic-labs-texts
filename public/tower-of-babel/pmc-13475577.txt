Wearable transparent OLED glasses for cognitive stimulation: Enhancing brain connectivity through gamma entrainment
Authors: Hyeonwook Chae, Yeseung Park, Yejung Kim, Euisuk Yoon, Ki Woong Kim, Seunghyup Yoo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gamma wave entrainment is a promising approach for improving cognitive function, particularly in the early stages of Alzheimer’s disease. However, for everyday treatment, a light source system that maintains visibility while delivering effective stimulation is essential for patient comfort and practicality. To address this need, we developed organic light-emitting diode (OLED) glasses with optimized large-area transparent OLEDs for gamma wave entrainment. The optimization involved all layers of the device, including encapsulation, to achieve high luminous transmittance (75%), uniformity (over 90%), and electrical performance, ensuring suitability for wearable applications. Under real-world conditions (see-through visibility and ambient light present), the transparent OLED glasses successfully induced gamma rhythm entrainment, enhancing brain connectivity between the occipital and frontal regions. They also notably reduced visual discomfort and showed a trend of enhanced connectivity compared to nontransparent OLEDs. These results highlight the potential of this noninvasive wearable technology for early cognitive intervention, providing comfortable therapeutic light stimulation.

INTRODUCTION

Organic light-emitting diodes (OLEDs) have revolutionized the field of optoelectronics, providing highly efficient, thin, and flexible light sources that are now integral to a wide range of applications including displays, lighting, and wearable optoelectronic sensors (1–6). In particular, transparent OLEDs have emerged as a promising technology, especially for interactive displays such as smart windows and wearable devices like smart eyewear (6–8). Their ability to emit light while maintaining transparency makes them ideal for integrating displays into everyday items such as glasses, where it is essential to maintain a clear field of vision when needed. Despite these advantages, transparent OLEDs face challenges related to performance stability and efficiency, particularly in large-scale applications (6–11). Balancing transparency with durability and light-emitting efficiency requires further optimization of device layers, such as the seed layer and top layers, to ensure that they are suitable for everyday use in wearable devices.

Reliable large-area transparent OLEDs open up opportunities for incorporating them into bioapplications, where the interface between light-emitting devices and biological systems could lead to innovative healthcare solutions. One such application is neural stimulation, where light patterns such as flickering light have been shown to entrain brain rhythms, particularly gamma oscillations, which are closely linked to cognitive functions (12–14). Preclinical studies have revealed that flickering light stimulation (FLS) at specific frequencies can entrain gamma rhythms, influencing neural processes involved in memory, attention, and sensory processing (12, 13). These studies have also demonstrated that FLS can modulate key factors related to Alzheimer’s disease (AD) such as amyloid-β protein, making it a promising tool for addressing conditions like mild cognitive impairment (MCI), a precursor to more severe cognitive decline and affecting brain activity in ways that could delay or mitigate cognitive deterioration (13, 15). At the same time, not all gamma-frequency stimulation paradigms appear to be equally effective: Some studies reported that 40-Hz light stimulation alone did not entrain native gamma oscillations in AD mouse models (16). Together, these findings suggest that the efficacy of gamma entrainment depends on the stimulation modality, frequency selection, and experimental context, underscoring the need to optimize stimulation parameters rather than assuming uniform benefit from a fixed 40-Hz paradigm.

Building on this principle, recent human studies identified age-dependent frequency ranges that produced stronger entrainment under controlled laboratory conditions using nontransparent light sources. In young healthy adults, stronger entrainment was observed at 34 to 38 Hz with 2-s stimulation blocks at 400 cd/m2 (17), whereas in older adults with preserved cognitive function, stronger gamma rhythms and connectivity were observed at 32 to 34 Hz with similar short-duration stimulation at 700 cd/m2 (18). These studies were conducted primarily under dark or low-ambient conditions to minimize external visual interference, and white matter tract microstructural integrity was also shown to influence the propagation and efficacy of entrainment (19). These findings collectively motivate the need to translate frequency-optimized gamma stimulation into wearable systems that remain effective under more natural viewing conditions.

In parallel with these clinical developments, transparent OLED-based devices offer a unique platform for incorporating FLS into wearable technologies, such as smart eyewear, that can be used in daily life. Transparent OLEDs, with their ability to emit light while maintaining visibility, are particularly suited for wearable applications where continuous interaction with the environment is essential. By overcoming the technical challenges of transparent OLEDs, particularly their stability and performance in large-area formats, these devices could serve as practical tools for delivering FLS in real-world settings. This would allow users to benefit from gamma entrainment and potential cognitive improvements during everyday activities.

This study focuses on two primary objectives: optimizing the performance of transparent OLEDs for large-scale wearable applications and validating their effectiveness in inducing gamma entrainment under real-world conditions. Through this research, we demonstrate the potential of transparent OLED-based smart eyewear as a noninvasive tool for cognitive enhancement, laying the groundwork for further development in practical bioapplications that are compatible with various lifestyles.

RESULTS

Overview of transparent OLED glasses and gamma entrainment

The proposed transparent OLED glasses were developed to deliver controlled gamma entrainment via visual stimuli. The setup for gamma entrainment involves a system comprising OLED glasses, a function generator, and a monitoring computer, as illustrated in Fig. 1A. This system was designed for human low-gamma FLS using five stimulation frequencies (32, 34, 36, 38, and 40 Hz), selected on the basis of prior human center-frequency studies (17, 18). In the electroencephalography (EEG) experiment, these frequencies were presented in randomized blocks across four viewing conditions to evaluate gamma entrainment under ambient and nonambient settings. This FLS is transmitted through neural pathways to the visual cortex, inducing synchronized gamma wave entrainment. The transparent OLED glasses provide a unique advantage by allowing the wearer to maintain a clear view of their surroundings while simultaneously receiving FLS. In the inactive (off) state, the wearer can see their surroundings without any obstruction, and even in the active (on) state, the glasses maintain see-through functionality around 1000 lux level, which is consistent with the illuminance of daylight or augmented reality (AR) displays, allowing the wearer to still perceive their environment, albeit with some reduction in clarity compared to the off state due to additive light from the OLED and being subject to individual variability in visual perception and adaptation. This feature is crucial for human clinical trials involving ambient light and demonstrates practicality in everyday environments. The simulated view through the glasses, as shown in Fig. 1C, demonstrates the device both in an inactive (off) and active (on) state, alongside a detailed cross-sectional schematic of the OLED device.

A photograph of the fabricated transparent OLED glasses in operation is presented in Fig. 1D. This illustrates their capacity to emit light while remaining visibly transparent. Spectral measurements of the emitted light from both the top and bottom emissions are shown in Fig. 1E, where the normalized spectral radiance was recorded at various angles. These results confirm that the emitted light is evenly distributed, with minimal spectral variation across different viewing angles, ensuring consistent gamma entrainment regardless of the user’s head orientation with respect to the OLED glasses.

Figure 1F shows the illuminance at the pupil, estimated from the luminance of the fabricated transparent OLEDs as a function of applied voltage, using the optical simulation software (LightTools, Synopsys Inc., Mountain View, CA, USA). Because transparent OLEDs emit light bidirectionally, the simulation carefully accounted for light directed toward the pupil. The experimental condition was set to deliver a pupil illuminance of 1158 lux, corresponding to an OLED luminance of 915 cd/m2. This intensity was chosen to approximate the saturation level observed in prior human studies (17, 18). In those studies with the previous lighting system, which had a smaller emitting area, entrainment strength increased from 662 lux (400 cd/m2) and plateaued at 1158 lux (700 cd/m2). Adverse effect analysis confirmed significantly reduced dazzling and asthenopia compared to nontransparent OLEDs (Table 1), enabling robust entrainment with improved tolerability under ambient conditions. This precise control over light intensity is crucial for ensuring the safety and efficacy of the FLS protocol used in gamma entrainment.

Electrical and optical design of the transparent OLED

Figure 2 provides an overview of the electrical and optical optimization strategies used to enhance both efficiency and uniformity while maintaining transparency. It is noted in Fig. 2A that the leakage current can be significant in large-area devices, especially with thinner hole transport layers (HTLs). As shown in the inset of Fig. 2A, the leakage current increases with a single thin HTL, which makes stability a challenge for larger devices. Although thicker HTL help suppress leakage current, using a single HTL structure with increased thickness could undermine the electrical characteristics. Therefore, in this study, a three-stacked HTL structure alternating N,N′-bis(1,4,5,8-naphthalenetetracarboxydiimide) (HATCN) and N,N′-bis(naphthalen-1-yl)-N,N′-bis(phenyl)benzidine (NPB) was used instead of a thick single HTL (20). It leverages discrete p-n-p interfaces to enable efficient nonradiative charge recombination, thereby achieving high hole current conduction without compromising device stability (21). The results show that this configuration maintains device performance, as evidenced by external quantum efficiency (EQE) measurements while also ensuring stability in larger-area devices. In addition, the thickness of the transport layers (electron and hole) was further optimized through the thin-film optical model following the framework of Chance, Prock, and Silbey (CPS) (22–24), to ensure that the design maximizes light entry through the pupil in the intended glasses platform, as illustrated in fig. S1.

The stability and carrier injection performance of the device are further analyzed in Fig. 2B, focusing on different types of seed layers used for transparent electrodes. Notably, alkali metal carbonate, such as cesium carbonate (Cs2CO3), can serve as both an injection and seed layer for thin-film Ag layers used as transparent top electrodes (25, 26). However, it significantly degrades vacuum conditions during processing, leading to poor uniformity and adhesion issues in larger cells, ultimately resulting in poor performance, as illustrated in Fig. 2B. On the other hand, aluminum-based seed layers (27, 28), while causing a slight reduction in transmittance, demonstrate high electron injection performance when used with 8-hydroxyquinolinolato lithium (Liq) and provide excellent wetting effects, resulting in superior stability and uniformity for large-area devices. In transparent OLEDs with an ultrathin Ag cathode, leakage current is particularly problematic due to increased susceptibility to pinhole/shunt defects, which further exacerbate nonuniform current distribution and localized heating and accelerated degradation (29, 30). Therefore, in this study, a Liq/Al-based seed layer was chosen for the ultrathin silver cathode (8 nm) (6) as this thickness represents the approximate percolation threshold for forming a continuous Ag film with a seed layer, achieving high transmittance while maintaining low sheet resistance (see fig. S2) to balance performance, transmittance, and stability.

The images of OLEDs shown in Fig. 2C demonstrate the effects of introducing appropriate HTL and seed layers: On the left, a single HTL paired with Cs2CO3 seed layer is shown; on the right, a stacked HTL combined with Al seed layer is depicted. This comparison reveals that combining stacked HTL with a pure metal seed layer improves the device optical characteristics significantly throughout the whole active area.

To ensure device stability and usability in ambient environments, it is crucial to provide OLEDs with an adequate encapsulation layer that does not damage the organic layers during the process. In this respect, one cannot overemphasize the importance of a capping layer on top of the Ag layer, which not only tunes the transmittance but also protects OLEDs from being damaged during the deposition of encapsulation layers, as illustrated in Fig. 2D. The atomic layer deposition (ALD) process was carried out for deposition of inorganic barrier layers after the capping layer was deposited, and thinner capping layers were found to cause more damage due to precursor exposure. Experimentally, it was confirmed that an inorganic thin film with a thickness of over 100 nm can effectively minimize damage from the ALD process, preserving both transparency and efficiency.

Although a thick capping layer can reduce damage from the ALD process, it turns out that simply increasing the thickness is not an ideal solution, especially in terms of optical transmittance. As shown in fig. S3, high-index capping layers achieve maximum transmittance at thinner layers (26, 31, 32), and transmittance significantly decreases with increased thickness beyond the first maximum point. To overcome this limitation, we introduced a dual capping layer consisting of high-index and low-index dielectric layers based on zinc sulfide (ZnS) and lithium fluoride (LiF), respectively, to allow the total capping layer thickness to be large while maintaining high transmittance (Fig. 2E).

The effectiveness of the proposed design is further supported by Fig. 2F, which compares the luminous transmittance of OLEDs depending on inorganic materials used for a 2.5 dyad multilayer encapsulation structure, wherein inorganic layers (Al2O3 or TiO2) are paired with polymeric layers of poly(1,3,5-trimethyl-1,3,5-trivinyl cyclotrisiloxane) (pV3D3) (33, 34). This structure highlights the importance of using a low-index material after initially suppressing reflectance with the high-index ZnS material at the capping stage. In our comparison, aluminum oxide (Al2O3) offered a broader transmittance window compared to titanium oxide (TiO2) while still providing adequate barrier properties based on water vapor transmission rate (WVTR) values (35–37). Therefore, Al2O3 was selected as the inorganic component in this 2.5 dyad encapsulation structure, effectively balancing barrier performance and optical transmittance for the transparent OLED.

Figure 2G presents the results of simulations alongside the measured transmittance, reflectance, and absorption for the fabricated transparent OLEDs, after the comprehensive optimization of the organic layer, seed, capping, and encapsulation layers for a large-area transparent OLED with an 8-nm silver cathode (6) (see the Materials and Methods section for details on the full stack geometry of the OLEDs). The transmittance is higher than 70% for the most visible spectral range with the peak of 83%, demonstrating the high luminous transmittance of 75%. The agreement between simulation and experimental data validates the design, with only slight deviations at shorter wavelengths. The photographs of the fabricated transparent OLED glasses in the turn-off (Fig. 2H) and turn-on (Fig. 2I) states demonstrate the visual transparency and uniform light emission characteristics of the device (see movie S1 for further insights into the top and bottom emissions of the fabricated transparent OLED), illustrating the effectiveness of the electrical and optical design strategies used, contributing to the development of transparent OLED glasses with optimized efficiency and uniformity suitable for gamma entrainment.

Characteristics and evaluation of the transparent OLED glasses

The developed transparent OLEDs were integrated into wearable glasses, fabricated using 3D printing, and their electrical, optical, and environmental characteristics were systematically assessed to establish their suitability and practicality for clinical studies and everyday use. The electrical and optical properties (J-V-L characteristics) of the transparent OLEDs presented in Fig. 3A show consistent current density and voltage characteristics for both top and bottom sides. It is noted that the devices exhibit low leakage current even with a large-area configuration, which is crucial for stability. The uniformity of the emitted light is further demonstrated in Fig. 3B, which depicts the distribution of peak wavelength, color coordinates, and luminance of large-sized transparent OLEDs (see fig. S4 for additional details.) This is partly due to the introduction of bus electrodes that reduce local voltage drop and thus ensure over 90% of the area to exhibit a deviation less than 5%, except for the top-left region, which showed slightly higher luminance (the detailed design of the bus electrode structure is illustrated in fig. S5). The excellent uniformity across the device area, for both spectrum and brightness, is crucial for user experience, eye safety, and comfort in wearable applications.

Of particular importance is the spectral composition of light reaching the retina, which directly influences the efficacy of FLS. Given the focus of previous studies on red cone cells (17, 18), we used a red emissive dopant for the transparent OLED. Although any color can be readily implemented in a transparent OLED format, a red-shifted spectrum was selected to align with the strong gamma entrainment observed with red or red-rich light in prior studies. Optical simulations using a human eye model in LightTools was performed to evaluate the effective retinal spectrum. This simulation incorporated the angle-dependent emission of the OLED with the nonuniform spatial distribution of cone cells; specifically, the incident irradiance on the retina (Fig. 3C) was weighted by the local cone cell density (see the Materials and Methods section). As shown in fig. S6, the high concentration of red cone cells within the −10° to 10° range implies that light emitted at lower angles is critical for effective stimulation. To further improve photon delivery into this critical ±10° cone region, we selected the emitter Ir(mphmq)2(tmd) with a high horizontal dipole ratio and optimized the full OLED stack using the CPS dipole model (22). To ensure high transparency, we avoided using a thicker metal electrode because it tends to reduce transmittance in visible spectrum although it could lead to stronger concentration of light emission along the normal direction. Owing to the minimal angular spectral shift of our transparent OLED, the emitted spectrum is nearly identical to the spectrum reaching the retina (fig. S7). This spectral consistency confirms that the proposed device is highly suitable for targeted red cone cell stimulation. Thus, the selected red emission spectrum was used as a biologically informed choice for wearable FLS delivery, whereas the entrainment efficacy itself was primarily determined by the optimized temporal stimulation parameters.

The operational lifetime of transparent OLEDs (Fig. 3D), evaluated under constant current conditions at an initial luminance level of 1000 cd/m2 (bottom emission), is found to be comparable to that of nontransparent OLEDs featuring a thick aluminum cathode, despite the higher current required to achieve the same luminance due to the lower reflectance of the transparent electrode compared to the thick aluminum cathode. Notably, the transparent OLEDs demonstrated an LT80 (time taken for luminance to drop to 80% of its initial value) exceeding 100 hours, exhibiting sufficient stability for our clinical trials. Furthermore, given the pulsed driving with 50% duty cycle in the clinical protocol—which is known to reduce the chance of electrochemical degradation compared to continuous operation—the effective operational lifetime is expected to be substantially extended (38). This stability could be further enhanced through the adoption of production-grade materials commonly used in the display industry, making these optimized transparent OLEDs highly suitable for prolonged real-world applications.

To assess their safety for human use, a thermal stability test was conducted, as shown in Fig. 3E. Because the human clinical trials are expected to last up to an hour, the thermal test was conducted under similar conditions to evaluate suitability within this timeframe. The temperature of the transparent OLED remained below 25°C even under prolonged operation at 1000 cd/m2, supporting their safe usage in wearable devices during typical use scenarios (39). This is due to the diffusive areal characteristics of OLEDs, in contrast with point-source-like characteristics of LEDs, which is likely to induce local hotspots to obtain the same effective light output.

Overall, the transparent OLED glasses were demonstrated to have the electrical and optical characteristics and reliability needed for clinical studies. Their see-through characteristics, high performance, “cold” operation without hotspots, and excellent uniformity make them a promising solution for wearable light sources for FLS while providing aesthetic appeal as well.

The experiment used four conditions (see Fig. 4, A and B): nontransparent OLED under the dark condition (NTOdark), nontransparent OLED with ambient light on (NTOlight), transparent OLED with ambient light off (TOdark), and transparent OLED with ambient light on (TOlight).

Figure 4 (C to F) illustrates the topographical distribution of gamma-band ERS during FLS across the four experimental conditions. In the present study, a desirable acute entrainment pattern was defined as strong gamma-band ERS during stimulation, particularly in posterior visual regions where visually driven responses are expected to emerge first, together with a broader anterior spread of ERS indicating more widespread cortical recruitment. When the nontransparent OLED glasses were worn, the ERS was shown to be predominantly localized in the occipital region with the ambient light deactivated (NTOdark) (Fig. 4C), whereas the ERS distribution was shown to be extended to the central region with the ambient light turned on (NTOlight), indicating a more extensive synchronization effect (Fig. 4D). When transparent OLED glasses were worn with the ambient light turned off (TOdark), a notable reduction in ERS intensity was observed, accompanied by a more pronounced synchronization effect in the occipital region (Fig. 4E). When transparent OLED glasses were worn with the ambient light turned on (TOlight), the ERS was observed in both the occipital and frontal regions, indicating the broadest synchronization (Fig. 4F). The topographical maps in Fig. 4 (C to F) are descriptive grand-average visualizations intended to illustrate spatial ERS patterns, whereas statistical inference was performed on the quantified ERS values shown in Fig. 4 (G to L) (see the Materials and Methods section for further details).

At the posterior region (Pz) (Fig. 4, G to I), the case with the transparent OLED glasses yielded a marginally higher ERS in comparison to the case with the opaque OLED glasses, suggesting that the transparent light source promoted gamma entrainment in the Pz. The presence of ambient light (on/off) had a significant effect on ERS in Pz, with synchronization increasing when ambient light was turned on [F(1,19) = 17.014, P = 0.001]. Furthermore, although no significant interaction effect was observed between the type of light source (transparent/nontransparent) and ambient light condition, the highest level of synchronization was observed when the transparent OLED glasses and the ambient light (TOlight) were both used.

Even at the anterior region (Fz) (Fig. 4, J to L), the transparent illuminant was associated with increased ERS, indicating a greater gamma-entrainment response of the frontal area. The ambient light condition (on/off) had a significant effect on ERS at the Fz as well, with an increase in synchronization observed when ambient light was turned on [F(1,19) = 20.610, P < 0.001]. Furthermore, although no significant interaction effect was observed between the type of light source (transparent/nontransparent) and ambient light condition, the highest level of synchronization was observed at the TOlight condition, similar to what was observed at the Pz. At both the Pz and the Fz, inspection of the immediate poststimulation windows (T9 to T10) further indicated that the gamma-band response declined rapidly after FLS offset and approached baseline within the subsequent poststimulation interval, suggesting that the observed entrainment was primarily acute.

Gamma rhythm connectivity in human clinical trials using transparent OLED glasses

As illustrated in Fig. 5 (A to D), the spectral Granger causality (sGC) of gamma rhythms exhibited a notable increase following FLS across parieto-occipito-frontotemporal and frontotemporal-parieto-occipito connection edges under all four conditions (P < 0.05). The number of the edges exhibiting augmented parieto-occipital-to-frontotemporal gamma rhythm connectivity following FLS ranged from 1753 (89.7% of 1953 edges; Fig. 5A) under the NTOdark condition to 1861 (95.3% of 1953 edges; Fig. 5D) under the TOlight condition. These findings suggest that gamma rhythm connectivity in the occipital region is more effectively established with transparent light sources than with opaque ones and in the presence of ambient light rather than in dark.

Furthermore, Fig. 5E illustrates that, although not statistically significant, the connectivity was stronger with the transparent OLED glasses than with the opaque glasses, based on the mean gamma rhythm connectivity strength. It can be proposed that the “see-through” capability of transparent light sources under ambient light induce gamma entrainment to a superior extent.

Reduced adverse effect of FLS with the transparent OLED glasses under ambient lighting

This study examined the self-reported adverse effects of FLS across four conditions: NTOdark, NTOlight, TOdark, and TOlight, as shown in Table 1.

The prevalence of dazzling and asthenopia was far lower under the TOlight condition in comparison to the other conditions. Specifically, the dazzling effect [F(1,19) = 5.842, P = 0.001] was significantly reduced under the TOlight condition compared to the NTOdark condition, indicating that wearing transparent OLED glasses with ambient lighting is effective in reducing the dazzling effect. Similarly, asthenopia [F(1,19) = 2.991, P = 0.038] was also significantly lower under the TOlight condition than under the NTOdark condition, indicating that the TOlight condition is effective in mitigating asthenopia.

In contrast, no significant differences were observed among the four conditions with respect to fatigue, headache, dizziness, or ocular pain. The P values for fatigue, headache, dizziness, and ocular pain exceeded 0.05, indicating that the type of OLED and the presence or absence of ambient lighting did not have a statistically significant impact on these symptoms. The findings in this study indicate that the use of a transparent OLED with ambient lighting may be an effective method for alleviating certain visual discomforts, such as dazzling and asthenopia, to a greater extent than nontransparent OLED configurations.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we successfully optimized large-area transparent OLEDs, integrating them into wearable glasses designed for FLS studies. By optimizing the device layers, including the HTL, seed, capping, and encapsulation layers, we demonstrated significant improvements in both electrical and optical performance of transparent OLEDs to a degree sufficient for preclinical studies. The results indicate that the transparent OLED glasses provide excellent optical uniformity, efficient emission characteristics, cold operation with no thermal hotspots, and operational stability—qualities critical for wearable applications, where consistent performance and visual comfort are necessary for practical daily use. Furthermore, their ability to maintain transparency while emitting light is an important feature as it allows users to see their surroundings and thus interact seamlessly with them.

It is noteworthy that transparent light sources can also be realized with micro-LEDs. In this case, a multitude of micro-LEDs are placed on a substrate in an array, with most of the region left free from light sources to achieve high effective transmittance. On the basis of inorganic crystalline III-V compound semiconductor technologies, micro-LEDs are known for their high durability and ability to achieve very high brightness. However, producing near-uniform emission would require a high-precision transfer process for a large number of microscale LEDs, which may not be cost-effective for the wearable applications under consideration. In contrast, transparent OLEDs offer inherent advantages in achieving uniform, transparent light sources with a straightforward thin-film deposition technique. Furthermore, the target luminance is achieved at the device operating luminance in OLEDs, whereas micro-LEDs must be driven at much higher luminance by a factor of Atotal/(NLED × ALED), where Atotal and ALED are the area of the whole substrate and a unit micro-LED, respectively, and NLED is the number of micro-LEDs. Such high-brightness operation can lead to local hotspots, which are undesirable for wearable applications. It may be noted that organic materials might undergo photoinduced degradation when used for an extended period of time in bright outdoor environment; whereas ultraviolet (UV) absorption of most glass or plastic substrates provide a protection against incidence of UV light through substrates into the transparent OLED, additional coating of TiO2 or replacement of Al2O3 therewith can offer further protection from the UV light.

In our study comparing transparent OLED glasses with opaque counterparts under various lighting conditions, gamma rhythm entrainment and connectivity were strongest when the transparent OLED glasses were worn with ambient light turned on (TOlight). The present EEG findings should therefore be interpreted primarily as acute stimulation-related responses, with only limited immediate carryover after stimulus offset. Enhanced occipital-frontal connectivity was observed under this condition, suggesting broader cortical recruitment during FLS. Notably, this condition also led to reduced visual discomfort, with lower levels of dazzling and asthenopia.

To interpret why the transparent OLED system may support stronger gamma responses, it is important to consider the visual context in which FLS is delivered. Gamma rhythms are known to facilitate efficient neural information transfer, and such processing is favored when competing visual interference is minimized (40). Unlike nontransparent OLED glasses, transparent OLED glasses deliver gamma-frequency flicker while preserving the wearer’s view of the surrounding environment, which may reduce visual strain and support more natural visual engagement during stimulation. In addition, a previous work has shown that gamma-frequency stimulation can enhance functional interactions between frontal and occipital regions while reducing cognitive burden (41). In this context, the stronger ERS and occipital-frontal connectivity observed in the present study with transparent OLED glasses suggest broader cortical recruitment during FLS, although this should be interpreted as a functional scalp-level pattern rather than direct evidence of whole-brain entrainment.

The effect of ambient light should be interpreted more cautiously as a contextual modulation of FLS-evoked responses rather than as an independent entraining stimulus. Brain oscillatory activity involved in selective attention and sensory suppression is known to be influenced by lighting conditions (42, 43), and gamma-band activity has been closely linked to attentional engagement (44). From this perspective, ambient light may alter the sensory context in which flickering stimulation is processed, thereby affecting the efficiency of entrainment. Prior studies have also suggested that sensory responsiveness, entrainment strength, and the temporal dynamics of neural processing can vary depending on visual and lighting conditions (45, 46). Accordingly, the stronger ERS and occipital-frontal connectivity observed under ambient light in the present study may reflect improved sensory engagement with FLS under a more natural viewing condition. At the same time, because the present study did not include a control condition with ambient light alone (without FLS), these findings should be interpreted as a modulation of neural responses to FLS under ambient viewing conditions, rather than as a direct EEG effect of ambient illumination itself.

In addition to the EEG findings, the reduced occurrence of dazzling and asthenopia further supports the practical value of transparent OLED glasses as a platform for FLS delivery. This is relevant because excessive visual stimulation can impair concentration and contribute to asthenopia and related visual discomfort (47). Compared with nontransparent OLED conditions, the transparent OLED system, particularly under ambient light, appears to reduce factors associated with visual fatigue while preserving effective stimulation delivery. This improved tolerability may help sustain attention to the flickering stimulus and thereby support acute gamma entrainment under more natural viewing conditions. Together, the present results suggest that transparent see-through stimulation under ambient light is associated with stronger entrainment/connectivity patterns and lower visual discomfort, although the precise mechanism linking these effects remains to be clarified in future studies.

This study lays the groundwork for future human clinical trials to assess the efficacy of the FLS based on transparent wearable illuminating glasses. Preliminary findings suggest that gamma wave entrainment and connectivity are stronger in the transparent system under ambient lighting and, at the same time, cause the least discomfort. The optimization of visual stimulus delivery made in this work can be regarded crucial for the induction of gamma rhythms and the promotion of neural connectivity, with a strong potential for the development of future brainwave entrainment devices based on gamma rhythm-based interventions, such as wearable light-stimulating devices that may be used toward reduction of AD symptoms.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Detailed transparent OLED glasses structure and fabrication

The transparent OLED structure used in this study was built on a glass substrate with dimensions of 40 mm by 40 mm and a thickness of 700 μm. The bottom anode consisted of a 150-nm layer of indium zinc oxide (IZO), chosen to achieve sufficiently low sheet resistance and thereby minimize resistive voltage drop. The organic layers were based on a three-stacked configuration, each consisting of an HTL, an emissive layer (EML), and an electron transport layer (ETL). The HTL was composed of HATCN (5 nm) and NPB (70 nm), optimized for efficient hole injection and transport. Above the HTL, an EML consisting of bis(10-hydroxybenzo[h]quinolinato)beryllium (Bebq2; 30 nm) as a host and bis(4-methyl-2-(3,5-dimethylphenyl)quinoline)Ir(III)(tetramethylheptadionate) [Ir(mphmq)2tmd; 5 wt %] as a dopant was added, followed by an ETL of Bebq2 (20 nm). The top cathode was designed as a multilayer structure of Liq (1 nm), aluminum (Al; 1 nm), and silver (Ag; 8 nm). For a dielectric capping layer, ZnS (22 nm) and LiF (118 nm) layers were used. Encapsulation was achieved using a combination of Al2O3 (30 nm) and pV3D3 (108 nm); 2.5 dyads of Al2O3/pV3D3 was used to ensure the stability and longevity of the OLED devices.

To accommodate the placement of both anode and cathode contacts along one edge of the substrate, a thick bus electrode (150 nm) was incorporated into the OLED structure. This design feature was implemented to mitigate potential IR drop issues, particularly in areas distant from the contact points. The bus electrode ensured uniform potential distribution across the entire OLED, facilitating consistent performance and large-area uniformity preferred for wearable applications. The detailed design of the bus electrode structure is illustrated in fig. S5.

The frame for the OLED glasses was fabricated using stereolithography (SLA) 3D printing technology, provided by HY3D Co. Ltd. An acrylonitrile butadiene styrene (ABS)-like resin was used because of its lightweight and durable properties, making it suitable for wearable applications. The frame design, as illustrated in fig. S8, allowed for easy insertion and removal of the transparent OLED. This feature ensured consistent experimental setups, facilitating reliable device characterization.

Evaluation of transparent OLED glasses

The electrical and optical characteristics of the transparent OLED glasses, including J-V-R characteristics and spectral data, were measured using a customized setup. This setup is composed of a source meter unit (model 2400, Keithley), a calibrated photodiode (FDS100, Thorlabs), and a fiber optic spectrometer (EPP2000, StellarNet) (48). All components were operated by a motorized goniometer to accurately capture angle-dependent data. In addition, given the bidirectional light emission characteristic of transparent OLEDs, light was blocked to isolate the emission from the other side, thereby minimizing interference from opposing emissions during characterization.

The uniformity of the light source was evaluated using a spectroradiometer (CS-2000, Konica Minolta). Luminance and spectral uniformity across the entire device were assessed by measuring 224 points at equal intervals.

Operational lifetime tests for both transparent and nontransparent OLEDs were conducted under constant current conditions at 1000 cd/m2, 30°C, and 50% relative humidity. To ensure the validity of comparative measurements, spectral differences between both light sources were assessed and found to be negligible, as illustrated in fig. S9. The high spectral consistency between the two light sources, along with the long-term operational stability ensured by their sufficient lifetimes, minimized potential systematic errors attributable to variations between the light sources. The operating temperature of the transparent OLED glasses was measured using an infrared thermometer (FLIR E6-XT, FLIR Systems). Measurements were conducted in an environment maintained at a constant temperature of 23°C to ensure consistency across all tests.

Optical simulation method

The OLED structure was optimized using a thin-film optical model based on the framework of CPS (22), which accounts for coherent wave interference and dipole emission near interfaces to maximize angular intensity and EQE while ensuring light entry through the pupil. LightTools (Synopsys Inc., Mountain View, CA, USA), was subsequently used for ray tracing simulation of pupil illuminance and spectrum as geometric optics is appropriate to describe poststack light propagation.

In detail, the optical simulation of light propagation for the transparent OLED glass–eye system was performed using LightTools, a simulation tool that uses ray tracing techniques combined with Monte Carlo simulations. The model incorporated the actual structure and dimensions of the transparent OLEDs, as illustrated in fig. S6.

For eyeball modeling, we used the default model provided by the simulation program. A spherical detector was positioned between −60° and 60° relative to the optical axis to capture the spectrum across the entire retina. As LightTools does not support direct input of angular spectrum, the simulation was conducted by incrementally adjusting the emission angles with corresponding spectra applied. The results of light emitted from each angle of OLEDs to retina are illustrated in unit-weighted spectra.

The conversion of unit-weighted spectra to cone-weighted spectra is illustrated in fig. S5. We multiply the cone cell distribution and the unit-weighted spectra to consider the effective light irradiated to red cone cell. In detail, we precisely subdivided the angle of the spherical detector (receiver) at the retina into 1° increments for angles below 15° and 5° increments for angles above 15°, due to the marked change in cone distribution at lower angles. Therefore, simulations were conducted at different receiver angles (0° to 1°, 1° to 2°, etc.) with varying radiant angles and corresponding spectra. In addition, input power was adjusted according to the angular intensity of the transparent OLEDs to account for changes in radiant power with angular distribution.

For each simulation, 4 billion rays were traced from the light source to accurately simulate light propagation. The results are presented in Fig. 3E.

Participants

We enrolled 20 young healthy volunteers (7 male and 13 female volunteers with the age of 28.3 ± 3.0 years). All participants had a normal or corrected-to-normal vision. None of them reported a previous history of major psychiatric or neurological disorders, including epilepsy. All participants provided a written informed consent. All procedures were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the institutional review board (IRB) of Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (IRB no. B-1904-534-303).

Experimental design

All participants were instructed to fast for at least 2 hours and not to drink alcohol 24 hours before the experiment. The main study then consisted of a 5-min resting phase for recording resting-state electroencephalography (rsEEG) and four experimental sessions for recording steady-state visual evoked potentials (ssVEPs) (Fig. 4A). To control the potential confounding effect of the experimental design, we randomized the order of FLS parameters to prevent habituation and set the interval between sessions as 3 min, which is long enough to wash out the effect of prior session. All intervals between the resting phase and the four experimental sessions were 3 min. One of the four conditions (NTOdark, nontransparent light with ambient light off; NTOlight, nontransparent light with ambient light on; TOdark, transparent light with ambient light off; and TOlight, transparent light with ambient light on) was assigned to each session. In the present design, ambient light served as a contextual factor within FLS sessions, rather than as an independently tested stimulus in the absence of FLS. A session consisted of 10 blocks. A 10-s break was placed before and after each block. Each block consisted of 10 FLS trials of a given condition and flickering frequency. One of the five gamma flickering frequencies (32, 34, 36, 38, and 40 Hz) was randomly assigned to each block in each session to ensure an even stimulation of brain activity within the low gamma band. Because each session consisted of 10 blocks, each flickering frequency was assigned to two blocks in each session. Each FLS was presented for 2 s, and the inter-FLS interval was randomly given from 3 to 6 s (4.5 ± 1.5 s). The flicker waveform was delivered as a square-wave stimulus with a 50% duty cycle. In all human EEG sessions, the transparent OLEDs were operated at an eye-directed luminance of ∼915 cd/m2, corresponding to ∼1158 lux at the pupil level estimated by optical simulation (same as the nontransparent OLED glasses), to match the effective stimulation range identified in our prior human studies (17, 18). In each experiment, rsEEG was recorded for 5 min with eyes closed. In each session, EEG was recorded for 11 min 55 s from 10 s before the first block. We instructed participants to keep their eyes open while FLS was performed. We obtained an informed consent to use the picture (Fig. 4B) from the volunteer for the publication. After each session, we checked the consistency of the luminance and spectrum of the light source and the participants were asked to rate the severity of six adverse effects of FLS (fatigue, headache, dizziness, dazzling, asthenopia, and ocular pain) using a seven-point Likert-type scale from 0 (not at all) to 6 (extremely severe).

EEG recording and preprocessing

EEG was recorded using 64 Ag-AgCl electrodes attached to an elastic cap (Easycap, EASYCAP GmbH, Munich, Germany) in accordance with the extended International 10–20 System. The reference electrode was FCz, whereas the ground electrode was positioned on the forehead. Additional electrodes were positioned below and above the left eye to record electromyogram activity. Throughout the recording process, the impedance of all electrodes was maintained at 10 kΩ or below. The EEG signal was amplified and stored using a 24-bit ActiCHamp dc amplifier and BrainVision Recorder (Brain Products GmbH, Gilching, Germany), with a sampling rate of 1000 Hz and no online filters applied. The synchronization of the stimulus markers with the recording was conducted through the FLS control system.

All preprocessing and analysis steps were conducted in MATLAB (The MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA, USA) using the EEGLAB (49) and BSMART (50) toolboxes. Subsequently, the signals underwent filtering with a 1-Hz high-pass finite impulse response filter and a 60-Hz notch filter, after which they were re-referenced to a common average. An independent component analysis was conducted to eliminate eye blinks and other ocular artifacts from the EEG data. The detailed step-by-step workflow of the experimental setup is illustrated in fig. S10.

Following the completion of the preprocessing stage, the 5-min rsEEG recordings were segmented into 1500-ms epochs. From this, 20 epochs devoid of artifacts were randomly selected. For the FLS sessions, 20 artifact-free epochs per frequency were extracted for each condition. These ERS values were first averaged within each subject and then entered into a 2 × 2 repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) with factors of light source transparency and ambient light presence. To perform the ERS analysis, each FLS session yielded 4000 ms epochs, extending from 1000 ms before FLS onset to 1000 ms subsequent to FLS offset, resulting in 20 epochs per frequency. To conduct an sGC analysis, the 4000-ms epochs were divided into two segments: the first comprising 501 to 1500 ms and the second comprising 1500 to 2000 ms.

Analyses

To ensure the light source transparency and ambient light presence of FLS for gamma rhythm entrainment in aging, we conducted an analysis of the spectral power changes induced by FLS using event-related spectral perturbation (ERSP) in each block. The time-frequency spectrum for each epoch was calculated and normalized by dividing by the average power of the prestimulus interval. The spectral power ratio of the electroencephalogram during the FLS period relative to the pre-FLS interval was calculated for each FLS. To obtain the normalized averaged spectral power change of FLS, ERD/ERS values were calculated by averaging the spectral power ratios from 20 trials at each FLS frequency within a session. In addition, time-associated power spectrum changes were evaluated by averaging the ERSP values across 11 nonoverlapping 250-ms subwindows, spanning from 250 ms before FLS onset to 2500 ms after onset in each block (T0 to T10). T0 (−250 to 0 ms) represents the time immediately preceding the onset of the FLS, whereas T9 (2000 to 2250 ms) and T10 (2250 to 2500 ms) represent the windows immediately following the offset of the FLS. The average ERS during FLS (T1 to T8) was used to examine the impact of light source transparency and ambient light on gamma entrainment. The Pz and Fz were selected as representative channels for the purpose of examining the generalized SSVEP response (51, 52). A 2 (transparency of light source: transparent, nontransparent) × 2 (ambient light: present, absent) design repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted to examine the effects of light source transparency and ambient light on gamma rhythm changes associated with FLS, with the Greenhouse-Geisser correction for nonsphericity and Bonferroni post hoc comparisons.

To check whether gamma rhythms entrained in the occipital cortex were transmitted to other brain regions, sGC values of gamma rhythms during FLS were compared with those in the resting state. sGC was calculated for all possible electrode pairs, with an average taken across each FLS frequency within a session. A time lag of 75 samples was used to calculate sGC (53). The mean sGC values of 20 1500-ms epochs from rsEEG were compared with those of 20 1500-ms epochs from SSVEP during FLS using paired t tests with false discovery rate (FDR)–corrected P values set at 0.05. Given the observation that significant electrode pair connections differed across FLS conditions, graph theory measures were used to quantitatively compare network structures across conditions of the light source transparency and ambient light presence (54, 55). Adjacency matrices were constructed for each FLS condition, namely, light source transparency and ambient light presence, based on edges exhibiting significant differences between rsEEG and SSVEP as determined by paired t tests. The number of edges exhibiting significantly enhanced sGC connectivity following FLS was contrasted between occipitoparietal-to-frontotemporal and frontotemporal-to-occipitoparietal connections. A 2 (light source transparency: transparent, nontransparent) × 2 (ambient light: present, absent) design repeated-measures ANOVA was used to investigate the effects of light source transparency and ambient light on connection strength during FLS, with Greenhouse-Geisser correction and Bonferroni post hoc comparisons.

To evaluate the extent of adverse effects across four FLS sessions (nontransparent OLED glasses with or without ambient light and transparent OLED glasses with or without ambient light), a 2 (light source transparency: transparent, nontransparent) × 2 (ambient light: present, absent) design repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted on six adverse effect measures (fatigue, headache, dizziness, dazzling, asthenopia, and ocular pain) with the Greenhouse-Geisser correction for nonsphericity and Bonferroni post hoc comparisons.
