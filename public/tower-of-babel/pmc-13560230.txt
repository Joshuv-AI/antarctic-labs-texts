Background

Candida albicans is a leading opportunistic fungal pathogen that causes a wide spectrum of infections ranging from superficial mucosal candidiasis to life-threatening invasive candidemia, particularly in immunocompromised individuals [1]. One of the most critical virulence attributes of C. albicans is its ability to form biofilms on both biotic and abiotic surfaces, including medical devices and host mucosal tissues [2]. Biofilm-embedded cells exhibit markedly enhanced tolerance to conventional antifungal agents and host immune defenses, contributing to persistent infections, treatment failures, and increased mortality rates [3]. The intrinsic resistance of biofilms to existing antifungal therapies poses a significant clinical challenge, necessitating the development of novel antibiofilm strategies [4].

Currently available antifungal agents, including azoles, polyenes, and echinocandins, remain the cornerstone for Candida infection management. However, the emergence of drug resistance, coupled with undesirable side effects such as nephrotoxicity and hepatotoxicity, has substantially limited their long-term efficacy [5]. Consequently, there is growing interest in exploring alternative or adjunctive therapeutic approaches, particularly natural bioactive compounds that can disrupt biofilm formation with favorable safety profiles [6].

Omega-3 PUFAs, particularly docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) and eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA), are essential long-chain PUFAs abundant in marine oils and widely recognized for their anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, and cardioprotective properties [7, 8]. Accumulating evidence suggests that omega-3 PUFAs also possess direct antimicrobial activities against various bacterial and fungal pathogens [9]. Previous studies have indicated that PUFAs can disrupt microbial membrane integrity, interfere with energy metabolism, and alter cellular redox homeostasis [10, 11].

Our group previously conducted a systematic investigation of six PUFAs, including α-linolenic acid (ALA), EPA, DHA, linoleic acid (LOA), γ-linolenic acid (GLA), and arachidonic acid (AA), against clinical C. albicans isolates from vulvovaginal candidiasis and candidemia. While the study identified DHA and EPA as effective inhibitors of biofilm formation [12], the molecular mechanisms remained elusive, and whether these structurally similar PUFAs act through common or distinct pathways was unknown. Building on these phenotypic observations, the present study aims to unravel the molecular mechanisms underlying the antibiofilm actions of DHA and EPA.

With the rapid advancement of high-throughput technologies, integrated multi‑omics approaches have become powerful tools for deciphering complex biological processes and drug mechanisms [13]. Transcriptomic profiling captures gene expression changes, while proteomic analysis reflects functional outputs and post-transcriptional regulation. Their integration provides a holistic view of cellular responses and reveals discrepancies between mRNA and protein levels, which is particularly valuable in microbial systems where such discordance is common [14].

In this study, we employed an integrated transcriptomic and proteomic strategy to investigate the antibiofilm activity of DHA and EPA against C. albicans, to explore the underlying molecular mechanisms, and identify novel therapeutic targets for combating biofilm-associated candidiasis.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

DHA and EPA were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). These two omega-3 PUFAs were prepared as 2 M stock solutions in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and stored at −80°C. Working solutions were prepared by diluting the stock with RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The final DMSO concentration in all cultures was ≤ 0.1%, which we previously confirmed not to affect C. albicans growth or biofilm formation [12].

Strain and growth conditions

C. albicans ATCC 90028 (American Type Culture Collection, USA) was used throughout this study. Frozen stock cultures (stored at −80 °C) were thawed and subcultured at least twice on Sabouraud Dextrose Agar (SDA; Dijing, Guangzhou, China) at 35 °C for 24 h before each experiment.

Minimum inhibitory concentration assay

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of nine antifungal agents (amphotericin B, fluconazole, voriconazole, itraconazole, posaconazole, caspofungin, micafungin, anidulafungin, and 5-flucytosine) against C. albicans was determined using the Sensititre YeastOne panel (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The MIC was defined as the lowest concentration that prevented color change from blue to pink (complete growth inhibition for amphotericin B, and ≥ 50% reduction for the other agents) following the CLSI M27 guidelines [15, 16].

The MIC of omega-3 PUFAs against C. albicans was determined as previously described [17]. Briefly, the initial C. albicans suspension was adjusted to 1 × 103 CFU/mL in RPMI 1640 medium. omega-3 PUFAs were serially diluted two-fold in RPMI 1640 to obtain final concentrations of 1, 0.5, 0.25, and 0.125 mM. RPMI 1640 supplemented with 0.1% DMSO served as the negative control. Following a 24 h incubation period at 35 °C, the MIC was visually defined as the lowest concentration causing a 50% reduction in planktonic growth, consistent with the CLSI endpoint for most antifungal agents.

Biofilm inhibition assay

The biofilm inhibition assay was performed as described in a previous study, with some modifications [12]. Briefly, the C. albicans suspension (~ 1 × 105 CFU/mL) was incubated with or without omega-3 PUFAs (0.125–1 mM) in sterile 96-well round-bottomed polystyrene plates (Corning, NY, USA). 200 µL of the mixed suspensions was added to each well of the microtiter plate, and the plates were incubated at 35 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere for 24 h. RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 0.1% DMSO was used as a negative control. All the experiments were performed in three independent biological replicates. After incubation, the wells were washed three times with pH 7.2 phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove planktonic cells.

To determine the biomass and metabolic activity of biofilms, the crystal violet (CV) and XTT [2,3-bis-(2-methoxy-4-nitro-5-sulfophenyl)−2H-tetrazolium-5-carboxanilide] reduction assays were used as previously described by Gulati et al. [18] and Manoharan et al. [19], respectively. For the CV assay, the biofilms were stained with 0.2% CV and dissolved in 33% acetic acid. The OD630 nm values were measured using a Multiskan EX microplate photometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, United States). For the XTT assay, an XTT Cell Proliferation Kit II (Roche, Mannheim, Germany) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The OD450 nm values were measured.

Transcriptomic analysis

C. albicans suspension was treated with 1 mM omega-3 PUFAs in sterile 6-well plates (Corning, NY, USA), while RPMI 1640 + 0.1% DMSO medium served as a control. After 24 h, the biofilm cells were collected by high-speed centrifugation, followed by rapid freezing in liquid nitrogen and storage at −80°C.

The RNA extraction of C. albicans biofilm was carried out using an RNAprep Pure Plant Kit (TIANGEN, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The concentration and integrity of extracted RNA were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA) and a Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA).

RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) and subsequent analyses were performed by Novogene Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). Briefly, total RNA was sequenced using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, USA) in paired-end mode and a read length of 150 bp. Approximately 6 GB of raw reads were generated for each sample. The raw reads were cleaned (removal of low-quality and adapter sequences) with the Fastp program under default parameters.

For RNA-seq analysis, clean reads were mapped to the genome of C. albicans SC5314, retrieved from the Candida Genome Database (CGD) (www.candidagenome.org) using Hisat2 (v2.2.1). Fragments per kilobase of transcript per million fragments mapped (FPKM) of each gene were calculated by FeatureCounts (v2.0.6). Gene expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 R package (v1.42.0). The threshold for significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between samples was the value of |log2 (fold change)|≥ 1 (corresponding to a twofold change) and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 (padj < 0.05), with log2 (fold change) ≥ 1 defined as up‑regulated and log2 (fold change) ≤ −1 as down‑regulated.

Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis of DEGs were performed using the ClusterProfiler (v4.8.1) package. The data represent three independent biological replicates for each condition.

Proteomic analysis

Biofilm cells for proteomic analysis were collected under the same growth and treatment conditions as those for transcriptomic analysis. All proteomic work, including sample preparation, mass spectrometry, and bioinformatic analysis, was performed by Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China).

Frozen cell pellets were ground in liquid nitrogen, lysed in SDT buffer (100 mM NaCl) containing DTT, and sonicated on ice for 5 min. After centrifugation (12,000 × g, 15 min, 4 °C), the supernatant was heated at 95 °C for 8–15 min, chilled, and alkylated with iodoacetamide in the dark for 1 h. Proteins were precipitated with four volumes of −20°C acetone (≥ 30 min, −20°C), pelleted by centrifugation (12,000 × g, 15 min, 4 °C), washed with acetone, air-dried, and redissolved in dissolution buffer (6 M urea, 100 mM TEAB, pH 8.5). Protein concentration was determined using the Bradford assay, and quality was verified by SDS-PAGE.

For digestion, 20 μg protein was adjusted to 100 μL with dissolution buffer and digested with trypsin at 37 °C for 4 h. The reaction was stopped with formic acid (pH < 3), and peptides were desalted on a C18 column, eluted with 0.1% formic acid in 70% acetonitrile, and lyophilized.

Dried peptides were dissolved in 0.1% formic acid and analyzed on a Thermo Orbitrap Astral mass spectrometer coupled to a nano-UHPLC system. Peptides were separated on a C18 column (150 μm × 15 cm, 2 μm) at 50 °C using a gradient of mobile phase B (80% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid). Data-independent acquisition (DIA) was performed with a full scan range of m/z 380–980 (resolution 240,000), 300 windows of 2-Th isolation width, and an MS/MS range of m/z 150–2000 (Astral resolution 80,000). Raw data were searched against the C. albicans protein database using DIA-NN. Cysteine carbamidomethylation was set as a fixed modification, and N-terminal methionine loss as a variable modification, allowing up to two missed cleavages. Peptide and protein identifications were filtered at Global Q-Value < 0.01 and PG Q-Value < 0.01. Global Q-value controls false discovery rate at the precursor level, while PG Q-value controls FDR at the protein group level. Differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were defined with |log2 (fold change)|≥ 1 and P < 0.05. Functional annotation was performed using InterProScan, and protein–protein interaction networks were predicted using the STRING database. The data represent three independent biological replicates for each condition.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 20.0 statistical software (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Data were expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD) of at least three independent experiments. For biofilm inhibition assays, statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s T3 test. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Transcriptome analysis

Transcriptional changes were determined by comparing the global transcriptomes of C. albicans ATCC 90028 with or without 1 mM omega-3 PUFAs. The 1 mM concentration was selected for multi-omics analysis based on dose–response biofilm inhibition assays, which demonstrated robust and statistically significant inhibition at this concentration while remaining below the MIC (> 1 mM), ensuring that observed molecular changes reflect specific antibiofilm mechanisms rather than general growth inhibition.

Comparing the DHA group with the control group (Fig. 2), it was found that DHA led to an up-regulated expression of 750 genes and a down-regulated expression of 1037 genes. The detailed DEGs between the two groups were depicted in a volcano plot (Fig. 2A). Functional analysis classified these DEGs into 24 significantly enriched GO terms, with 15 terms corresponding to biological processes (BP), 5 corresponding to cellular components (CC), and 4 corresponding to molecular functions (MF) (Fig. 2B). In the biological processes category, DHA treatment predominantly down-regulated genes involved in translation, peptide biosynthesis, ribosome biogenesis, and rRNA processing. At the CC level, genes annotated to the ribosome and ribonucleoprotein complex were almost exclusively down-regulated. At the MF level, structural constituents of the ribosome and electron transfer activity were entirely down-regulated, whereas oxidoreductase activity comprised both up‑ and down-regulated genes. In addition, KEGG pathway analysis revealed that DHA treatment profoundly suppressed multiple metabolic and biosynthetic pathways (Fig. 2C). The most affected pathway was the ribosome (cal03010), in which all 103 DEGs were down-regulated. Oxidative phosphorylation (cal00190) was also strongly suppressed, with 52 of 53 genes down-regulated. The biosynthesis of secondary metabolites pathway (cal01110) displayed a mixed pattern, comprising 49 up-regulated and 83 down-regulated genes.

Proteomic analysis

To further investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying the inhibitory effects of omega-3 PUFAs on C. albicans biofilm formation, we performed proteomic analysis comparing the protein expression profiles of biofilms treated with DHA or EPA vs. the control. The proteomic data complemented the transcriptomic findings and provided additional insights into post-transcriptional regulation.

Comparative proteomic analysis of the DHA‑treated group versus the control group revealed substantial changes in protein abundance. A total of 554 DEPs were identified, comprising 170 up-regulated and 384 down-regulated proteins (Fig. 4A). GO enrichment analysis revealed that the DEPs were predominantly involved in metabolic processes, single-organism metabolic processes, and oxidation–reduction processes (BP); membrane, integral membrane component, and mitochondrion-related compartments (CC); and oxidoreductase activity, transporter activity, and ion transmembrane transporter activity (MF) (Fig. 4B). Notably, a substantial proportion of mitochondrial proteins, including those associated with the mitochondrial inner membrane, respiratory chain complex, and oxidoreductase complexes, were markedly down-regulated. KEGG pathway analysis further demonstrated that oxidative phosphorylation was the most significantly enriched pathway, followed by metabolic pathways, biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, biosynthesis of antibiotics, carbon metabolism, pyruvate metabolism, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, the citrate cycle (TCA cycle), and fatty acid degradation (Fig. 4C). These findings suggest that DHA may affect pathways associated with energy production and mitochondrial function in C. albicans biofilms.

Similarly, proteomic comparison between the EPA‑treated group and the control group identified 259 DEPs under identical statistical thresholds, with 104 up-regulated and 155 down-regulated proteins (Fig. 5A). GO analysis showed that these DEPs were significantly enriched in single-organism processes, oxidation–reduction processes, transmembrane transport, carbohydrate metabolic processes, and lipid metabolic processes (BP); membrane and mitochondrial components (CC); and oxidoreductase activity, transporter activity, cofactor binding, and heme binding (MF) (Fig. 5B). KEGG pathway analysis again identified oxidative phosphorylation as the most significantly enriched pathway, together with metabolic pathways, carbon metabolism, the citrate cycle (TCA cycle), sulfur metabolism, fatty acid metabolism, pyruvate metabolism, peroxisome function, and 2-oxocarboxylic acid metabolism (Fig. 5C). Additionally, pathways related to the biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids, glutathione metabolism, and lysine biosynthesis were notably affected.

Both DHA and EPA profoundly altered the proteomic landscape of C. albicans biofilms, with oxidative phosphorylation and central carbon metabolism representing the core targeted pathways. However, DHA induced substantially more extensive proteomic alterations (554 DEPs) compared to EPA (259 DEPs), suggesting a more potent or broader mechanistic impact. The pronounced down-regulation of membrane and mitochondrial proteins, coupled with the impairment of oxidoreductase and transporter activities, is consistent with the hypothesis that omega-3 PUFAs may affect biofilm energy supply and membrane integrity, potentially contributing to their antibiofilm efficacy.

Integration of transcriptomic and proteomic data

To gain a comprehensive understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying the antibiofilm effects of omega-3 PUFAs, we integrated transcriptomic and proteomic datasets by comparing DEGs and DEPs between DHA- and EPA-treated C. albicans biofilms and controls.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the integrated differentially expressed molecules identified several core metabolic pathways commonly affected at both omics levels. In the DHA group, oxidative phosphorylation exhibited the largest enrichment with 132 molecules, followed by biosynthesis of secondary metabolites (Fig. 6A). Other significantly enriched pathways included carbon metabolism, pyruvate metabolism, the citrate cycle (TCA cycle), tyrosine metabolism, fatty acid degradation, 2-oxocarboxylic acid metabolism, sulfur metabolism, and peroxisome function. Notably, oxidative phosphorylation and carbon metabolism were enriched by both proteomic and transcriptomic data, suggesting that DHA may affect energy metabolism through coordinated transcriptional and translational changes. In the EPA group, integrated KEGG pathway analysis further highlighted oxidative phosphorylation as the most prominently enriched pathway, with 53 differentially expressed molecules (Fig. 6B). Other major pathways included carbon metabolism, the citrate cycle (TCA cycle), biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, pyruvate metabolism, sulfur metabolism, peroxisome function, fatty acid metabolism, glyoxylate and dicarboxylic acid metabolism, and 2-oxocarboxylic acid metabolism. Interestingly, the proteomic and transcriptomic contributions to these pathways were more balanced in the EPA group compared to the DHA group, particularly for oxidative phosphorylation and the citrate cycle, where both datasets showed comparable enrichment.

The integrated analysis revealed that both DHA and EPA converge on disrupting mitochondrial energy metabolism, particularly oxidative phosphorylation and the TCA cycle, despite differences in the degree of transcriptome-proteome correlation. The substantially higher number of integrated molecules enriched in oxidative phosphorylation in the DHA group (132 vs. 53 in EPA) is consistent with the more severe proteomic perturbation observed in DHA-treated biofilms. The limited direct overlap between DEGs and DEPs in both treatment groups underscores the importance of multi-omics integration, as relying solely on transcriptomic data would overlook critical post-transcriptional regulatory events. Collectively, these findings suggest that omega-3 PUFAs may affect C. albicans biofilm integrity primarily through multi-layered disruption of energy-producing pathways, with DHA exerting a more profound and widespread impact than EPA.

Oxidative phosphorylation

Given the prominent enrichment of oxidative phosphorylation identified in the integrated KEGG analysis, we further examined the expression patterns of individual molecules within this pathway. In DHA-treated biofilms, the majority of differentially expressed proteins associated with oxidative phosphorylation, including subunits of the mitochondrial respiratory chain complexes, were markedly down-regulated compared to controls (Fig. 7A). Concordantly, the corresponding genes encoding these respiratory chain components, such as NDH51 (complex I), SDH1, SDH2, and SDH4 (complex II), QCR2, QCR7, QCR8, and QCR9 (complex III), COX4, COX5, COX6, COX7, COX8, COX9, COX13, and COX15 (complex IV), and ATP19 and ATP20 (complex V), were also significantly down-regulated at the transcriptional level (Fig. 7B). This synchronous suppression at both omics layers indicates that DHA coordinately represses oxidative phosphorylation through extensive transcriptional and translational regulation.

In contrast, although EPA treatment also resulted in widespread down-regulation of oxidative phosphorylation-related proteins (Fig. 7C), the transcriptomic response was notably restricted. Only a small subset of genes, including COX15, YMX6, and CAALFM_C109440WA, exhibited altered expression, and these transcripts were up-regulated in EPA-treated biofilms compared to controls (Fig. 7D). This lack of concordance between the proteomic and transcriptomic data suggests that EPA may impair oxidative phosphorylation primarily through post-transcriptional mechanisms, such as enhanced protein degradation or translational repression, rather than through broad transcriptional down-regulation.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that both DHA and EPA compromise the oxidative phosphorylation capacity of C. albicans biofilms, yet through distinct regulatory modes: DHA exerts extensive dual-level suppression, whereas EPA induces protein-level inhibition with limited and selective transcriptional changes.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that the omega-3 PUFAs DHA and EPA effectively inhibit C. albicans biofilm formation at 1 mM, a concentration below the MIC, without significantly affecting planktonic growth. Using an integrated transcriptomic and proteomic approach, we uncovered distinct yet partially convergent molecular mechanisms underlying the antibiofilm activity of these two structurally related omega-3 PUFAs. DHA induced broad, synchronous suppression of oxidative phosphorylation, biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, and 2-oxocarboxylic acid metabolism at both the transcriptional and translational levels. In contrast, EPA primarily affected DNA replication and repair pathways at the transcriptional level while showing alterations in oxidative phosphorylation-related proteins predominantly through post-transcriptional mechanisms. These findings raise the possibility that omega-3 PUFAs suppress C. albicans biofilm formation through intricate, multi-layered regulatory perturbations that may affect the elevated metabolic activity required for biofilm development, suggesting a previously unappreciated complexity in their mechanism of action that could be addressed in future studies.

While our omics data reveal associations between omega-3 PUFAs treatment and altered expression of oxidative phosphorylation‑related genes and proteins, direct evidence of mitochondrial dysfunction is not provided. As such, oxidative phosphorylation represents a candidate pathway emerging from our analyses, and future studies could explore its functional relevance through approaches such as ATP measurement, membrane potential assays, or targeted genetic manipulation.

The observation that both DHA and EPA exhibited MIC values > 1 mM against planktonic C. albicans, yet significantly inhibited biofilm formation at the same concentration, confirms that their antibiofilm effects are not attributable to direct fungicidal or fungistatic activity against planktonic cells. This biofilm-selective inhibition is consistent with our previous findings [12] and reports from other groups demonstrating that PUFAs can inhibit microbial biofilms without affecting planktonic populations [20, 21]. Biofilm development is a highly regulated, energy-intensive process that requires coordinated expression of adhesion molecules, extracellular matrix production, metabolic reprogramming, and phenotypic switching between yeast and hyphal forms [22]. Biofilm maturation requires sustained ATP production for matrix synthesis, cell wall remodeling, and morphogenetic transitions, rendering biofilm cells particularly susceptible to disruptions in energy metabolism [23, 24]. Our multi-omics data are consistent with the hypothesis that DHA and EPA may inhibit biofilm formation through effects on bioenergetic pathways, potentially creating a cellular environment unfavorable for the establishment and maintenance.

The most striking transcriptomic finding was the coordinated down-regulation of the entire ribosomal machinery by DHA (Fig. 2). This transcriptional signature suggests that DHA suppresses cellular protein synthetic capacity, a function critical for biofilm development, given the extensive proteome remodeling required during maturation. The suppression of ribosomal protein genes may reflect activation of a stress-induced stringent response or nutrient starvation-like signaling, though the specific transcriptional regulators remain to be identified.

The pronounced proteomic alterations induced by DHA underscore its broad mechanistic impact. Significant enrichment of mitochondrial proteins, oxidoreductases, and transporters among the down-regulated DEPs indicates that DHA may affect membrane integrity and the electrochemical gradients essential for biofilm maintenance (Fig. 4). Notably, integrated KEGG analysis identified oxidative phosphorylation as the most significantly enriched pathway, substantially more molecules affected by DHA than by EPA (Fig. 6A and B). This quantitative divergence aligns with the more severe phenotypic inhibition observed for DHA in our biofilm assays, suggesting that DHA exerts a more potent and comprehensive metabolic impact than EPA.

At the molecular level, DHA down-regulated transcripts and proteins of multiple respiratory chain components, including NDH51 (complex I), SDH1/2/4 (complex II), QCR2/7/8/9 (complex III), multiple COX subunits (complex IV), and ATP19/20 (complex V) (Fig. 7A and B). This dual-level suppression is particularly significant because biofilm cells exhibit heightened respiratory activity relative to planktonic counterparts [23], and mitochondrial dysfunction has been implicated in biofilm dispersal and antifungal susceptibility [25, 26]. Whether DHA directly causes such dysfunction remains to be functionally validated.

In contrast to DHA's broad transcriptional suppression, EPA treatment predominantly down-regulated DNA replication and repair pathways. This transcriptional profile suggests that EPA may affect DNA replication capacity in biofilm cells, though it remains unclear whether EPA directly inhibits the replication machinery or induces secondary stress responses.

Notably, our group previously demonstrated that EPA disrupts mature C. albicans biofilms at concentrations that do not affect planktonic growth [17]. While that study established EPA's efficacy against established biofilms, the present work reveals distinct molecular mechanisms underlying its inhibition of biofilm formation. The previous report focused on phenotypic disruption of mature biofilm architecture, whereas the current multi-omics analysis uncovers that EPA suppresses DNA replication transcriptionally and impairs oxidative phosphorylation post-transcriptionally during initial biofilm development. These findings suggest that EPA may exert stage-specific effects, interfering with DNA replication and energy metabolism during biofilm establishment, while its activity against mature biofilms has been reported previously [17]. Whether these effects involve overlapping or temporally distinct mechanisms warrants further investigation.

The most intriguing aspect of EPA's mechanism is the marked discordance between its transcriptomic and proteomic effects on oxidative phosphorylation. While EPA caused widespread down-regulation of oxidative phosphorylation-related proteins, including subunits of respiratory chain complexes and mitochondrial membrane components, the corresponding transcripts were largely unchanged, and a subset of genes (COX15, YMX6, CAALFM_C109440WA) were actually up-regulated. This lack of mRNA-protein concordance indicates that EPA affects oxidative phosphorylation primarily through post-transcriptional mechanisms, such as enhanced protein degradation, translational repression, or impaired assembly stability of respiratory complexes. However, direct evidence for these mechanisms is currently lacking, and alternative explanations, including differential protein turnover, temporal response lags, and technical detection limits, cannot be excluded. The observation that EPA affected fewer total DEPs (259 vs. 554 for DHA) yet still showed substantial alterations in oxidative phosphorylation suggests that its post-transcriptional effects may be highly targeted.

The differential modes of action between DHA and EPA may be attributed to their distinct biophysical properties. DHA (22:6, omega-3) contains six double bonds and a 22-carbon chain, whereas EPA (20:5, omega-3) has five double bonds and 20 carbons [27]. The additional double bond and longer acyl chain of DHA confer greater membrane fluidity disruption potential and more extensive lipid raft perturbation [28, 29], which may explain why DHA coordinately represses gene expression at both transcriptional and protein levels, while EPA acts predominantly through post-transcriptional mechanisms.

Despite these mechanistic differences, both DHA and EPA converge on pathways linked to oxidative phosphorylation, underscoring the biological significance of this pathway in biofilm physiology. Oxidative phosphorylation is a central metabolic determinant and virulence factor in C. albicans biofilm formation [30]. Notably, PUFAs have been reported to induce apoptosis in Candida biofilms, as evidenced by reactive oxygen species accumulation, reduced mitochondrial membrane potential, and nuclear condensation[31]. Biofilm cells exhibit significantly elevated respiratory activity compared to planktonic counterparts, making oxidative phosphorylation a metabolic hallmark of the biofilm lifestyle [32]. This enhanced respiratory capacity supplies the ATP required for energy-intensive processes during biofilm development, including extracellular matrix synthesis, cell wall remodeling, and maintenance of three-dimensional architecture [25]. Moreover, oxidative phosphorylation supports biofilm-associated antifungal resistance by fueling drug efflux pumps; consequently, disruption of mitochondrial respiration increases susceptibility to conventional antifungals [33, 34]. Beyond energy provision, electron transport chain integrity influences morphogenetic transitions, particularly the yeast-to-hypha switch, and coordinates biofilm dispersal through reactive oxygen species and metabolic signaling [26].

Given these multifaceted roles, oxidative phosphorylation represents a validated therapeutic target for Candida biofilms. Recent studies have demonstrated that targeting mitochondrial complex I, alternative oxidase, or membrane potential effectively eradicates biofilms in vitro and in vivo [24, 35]. The convergent alterations in oxidative phosphorylation pathways by DHA and EPA identified in our study provide a compelling rationale for further exploring this bioenergetic dependency in next-generation antibiofilm strategies.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, our biofilm assays (CV and XTT) quantify biomass and metabolic activity but do not inform on biofilm architecture. Structural imaging techniques would be required to assess such effects in future studies. Second, our multi-omics analysis was performed using a single reference strain (C. albicans ATCC 90028); strain-dependent variation may occur, and validation in clinical isolates with diverse biofilm-forming capacities and antifungal resistance profiles is required to determine the broader applicability of our findings. Third, the upstream signaling mechanisms linking DHA and EPA exposure to the observed transcriptional and post-transcriptional reprogramming remain undefined. Fourth, while our multi-omics data identify oxidative phosphorylation as a candidate pathway, we did not perform functional validation to confirm the bioenergetic consequences. Future studies should therefore focus on validating the key findings of this work, including (i) confirming the causal role of oxidative phosphorylation disruption in omega-3 PUFA-mediated biofilm inhibition through genetic approaches, such as respiratory chain gene deletion or overexpression, as well as functional assays (e.g., ATP measurement or membrane potential assessment); (ii) elucidating the upstream sensors and signaling cascades that translate fatty acid exposure into the distinct transcriptional responses observed for DHA and EPA; and (iii) performing targeted validation of representative genes and proteins (e.g., via qPCR and Western blot) to further corroborate the key changes identified in our omics analyses.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our integrated transcriptomic and proteomic analysis suggests that DHA and EPA suppress C. albicans biofilm formation through partially overlapping yet mechanistically distinct pathways. DHA shows broad, dual-level down‑regulation of protein synthesis and oxidative phosphorylation via coordinated transcriptional and translational repression. In contrast, EPA primarily affects DNA replication at the transcriptional level and oxidative phosphorylation at the protein level with limited transcriptomic concordance. Both omega-3 PUFAs converge on pathways linked to mitochondrial energy metabolism, highlighting a critical bioenergetic vulnerability of biofilm cells that merits further functional exploration. These findings provide a comprehensive molecular framework for the antibiofilm activity of omega-3 PUFAs, highlight oxidative phosphorylation and the TCA cycle as promising therapeutic targets, and underscore the value of multi-omics integration in understanding complex drug mechanisms. Given the established safety profile and widespread availability of DHA and EPA, these omega-3 PUFAs represent attractive candidates for development as adjunctive agents against recalcitrant Candida biofilm infections, warranting further preclinical and clinical investigation.
