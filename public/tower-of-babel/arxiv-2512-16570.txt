Online Resource Allocation via Static Bundle Pricing
Dimitris Fotakis, Charalampos Platanos, Thanos Tolias

Abstract
Online Resource Allocation addresses the problem of efficiently allocating limited resources to buyers with incomplete knowledge of future requests. In our setting, buyers arrive sequentially demanding a set of items, each with a value drawn from a known distribution. We study environments where buyers’ valuations exhibit complementarities. In such settings, standard item-pricing mechanisms fail to leverage item multiplicities, while existing static bundle-pricing mechanisms rely on problem-specific arguments that do not generalize. We develop a unified technique for online resource allocation with complementarities for three domains: (i) single-minded combinatorial auctions with maximum bundle size d d , (ii) general single-minded combinatorial auctions, and (iii) a graph-based routing model in which buyers request to route a unit of flow from a source node s s to a target node t t in a capacitated graph. Our approach yields static and anonymous bundle-pricing mechanisms whose performance improves exponentially with item capacities. For the d d -single-minded setting with minimum item capacity B B , we obtain an O ⁡ ( d 1 / B ) O(d^{1/B}) -competitive mechanism, recovering the known O ⁡ ( d ) O(d) bound for unit capacities ( B = 1 B=1 ) and achieving exponentially better guarantees as capacities grow. For general single-minded combinatorial auctions and the graph-routing model, we obtain O ⁡ ( m 1 / ( B + 1 ) ) O(m^{1/(B+1)}) -competitive mechanisms, where m m is the number of items. We complement these results with information-theoretic lower bounds. We show that no online algorithm can achieve a competitive ratio better than Ω ⁡ ( ( m / ln ⁡ m ) 1 / ( B + 2 ) ) \Omega((m/\ln m)^{1/(B+2)}) in the general single-minded setting and Ω ⁡ ( ( d / ln ⁡ d ) 1 / ( B + 1 ) ) \Omega((d/\ln d)^{1/(B+1)}) in the d d -single-minded setting. In doing so, we reveal a deep connection to the classical extremal combinatorics problem of determining the maximum number of qualitatively independent partitions of a ground set.

1
Introduction

Online Resource Allocation is a central problem in Economics and Operations Research. A seller faces a sequence of buyers, each demanding a set of resources. The seller must make irrevocable allocation decisions with limited information about future arrivals. In this work, we study the setting in which the seller has access to the distribution of buyers’ valuations, placing the problem within the prophet-inequality framework.

The study of prophet inequalities originates in the classical single-item setting. Krengel, Sucheston, and Garling
[
22
]
and independently Samuel-Cahn
[
28
]
established the foundational results comparing online stopping rules to a prophet who observes all realizations in advance. Hajiaghayi et al.
[
18
]
later connected prophet inequalities to mechanism design, showing how prophet-style guarantees can be leveraged to design truthful posted-price mechanisms. Building on this connection, Chawla et al.
[
8
]
developed a theory of sequential posted pricing mechanisms.

Following these foundational results, research on prophet inequalities expanded rapidly, branching into richer allocation environments and more complex valuation models. In particular, combinatorial auctions emerged as a central and intensively studied setting within the prophet-inequality framework. For submodular and XOS valuations, Feldman et al.
[
15
]
gave a
2
-competitive posted-price mechanism. For subadditive valuations, Dütting et al.
[
14
]
obtained, via item-pricing, an
O(\log\log m)
-competitive mechanism (where
m
is the number of items), a guarantee that was recently improved by Correa and Cristi
[
12
]
, who achieved a
6
-competitive prophet inequality. For MPH-
k
valuations, Braun and Kesselheim
[
5
]
designed an item-pricing mechanism with competitive ratio
2k+\sqrt{2k(k-1)}-1
.

In this work, we focus on single-minded combinatorial auctions, a setting that captures strong complementarities: each buyer derives value only if they obtain their entire desired bundle. Single-minded combinatorial auctions capture the most extreme form of complementarities and arise naturally in applications where partial allocation provides no utility. Their simplicity makes them a fundamental testbed for understanding how complementarities interact with online decision-making and posted-price mechanisms. A canonical example is pricing goods in a grocery store. A shopper attempting to bake a cake gains value only if they obtain all necessary ingredients; eggs and milk provide zero utility if flour is missing.

Extending the grocery store analogy, many real systems feature multiple copies of each item rather than unique goods. A growing line of work examines how such multiplicities affect the performance of pricing mechanisms. Alaei
[
1
]
showed that, in the single-item setting with capacity
k
, one can achieve
1-O(1/\sqrt{k})
competitiveness. More recent results by Jiang et al.
[
19
]
and Chawla et al.
[
7
]
give matching upper and lower bounds for all values of
k
, while Chawla et al.
[
6
]
continued this line of work for XOS valuations. Recently, Ghuge et al.
[
17
]
studied online resource allocation with large capacities under stochastic arrivals using
dynamic
pricing mechanisms. They show that near-optimal welfare can be achieved using only a single sample from each distribution, highlighting that high capacities substantially mitigate uncertainty even with limited information.

In contrast, our work focuses on static mechanisms, in which the seller commits to a menu of prices before any buyer arrives and does not adjust them over time. Although such mechanisms are simple, transparent, and widely deployed in practice, they face significant limitations in the presence of complementarities. The seminal work of Feldman et al.
[
15
]
established strong impossibility results for item pricing in combinatorial auctions: when buyers’ valuations require complements, no item-pricing mechanism can be less than
\Omega(m)
-competitive. When parameterizing by the maximum bundle size
d
, Correa et al.
[
11
]
obtained a tight
(d+1)
-competitive guarantee.

Motivated by these challenges, Chawla et al.
[
9
]
initiated the study of bundle pricing in settings with complementarities, first in the context of online allocation of intervals and later for paths in trees. Their results demonstrated that richer pricing menus can circumvent the barriers inherent to item pricing. Moreover, their work highlighted that static pricing mechanisms admit progressively stronger guarantees as the number of available copies of each resource increases.

This raises two fundamental questions:

What static pricing mechanisms are appropriate for environments with complementarities?

How does the availability of multiple item copies affect the achievable performance of such mechanisms?

In this work, we develop a unified approach for prophet inequalities in the presence of complementarities. Our mechanisms are static (the corresponding menus are fully determined before any arrivals occur) and anonymous (buyers face the same menu). We show that this approach is applicable to the following domains:

1.

the
d
-single-minded setting, where each buyer demands a bundle of size at most
d

2.

general single-minded combinatorial auctions, with arbitrary bundle sizes

3.

the graph-routing setting, where buyers seek any feasible
s
-
t
path in a capacitated graph.

Across all of these environments, a pattern emerges:
bundle prices become exponentially more powerful as item capacities increase
. Even small increases in capacity can dramatically improve achievable competitive ratios, revealing a sharp structural transition in the difficulty of online allocation with complementarities.

At the same time, we complement our positive results with information-theoretic lower bounds for both the
d
-single-minded and the general single-minded settings. Our lower-bound construction is obtained by embedding the classical hard instance of Babaioff et al.
[
3
]
into the single-minded combinatorial auction framework. This embedding reveals an intriguing connection between our impossibility results and an extremal combinatorics problem: determining the maximum number of pairwise qualitatively independent partitions of a ground set, a question first studied by Rényi
[
26
]
. Together, these lower bounds show that the dependence of our guarantees on the number of items
m
and the minimum multiplicity
B
is essentially unavoidable.

1.1
Technical Overview and Contributions

We study online allocation problems with strong complementarities under distributional information, focusing on static and anonymous bundle-pricing mechanisms. All guarantees hold in expectation over both the randomness of the mechanism and buyers’ realized values, and the arrival order is chosen by an almighty adversary who observes the entire realization and the mechanism’s internal randomness.

Our starting point is the ex-ante linear relaxation, which upper bounds the expected welfare of the prophet, i.e. the expected value over the realizations welfare of the optimal allocation. Before solving this LP, we scale down all item capacities by a factor
\gamma
, creating a slack that later absorbs the fluctuations created by the randomness of the realization. This scaled relaxation guides the design of our pricing mechanism.

A notable feature of the optimal fractional solution is its simple and rigid structure. For any buyer, the LP allocates probability mass to values in an almost threshold-like form: below some cutoff the allocation is zero, above it the LP fully saturates the value distribution, and at most one
crucial
value may receive partial mass. When aggregated over all buyers who demand the same bundle
S
, these patterns define a canonical price level
w_{S}
that dictates how the menu treats
S
.

This structure directly informs our menu construction. The mechanism posts a static, anonymous menu containing multiple
copies
of each bundle
S
, priced either at the crucial value
w_{S}
or at the next higher value in the buyers’ value support,
w_{S}+1
. The guiding objective is to emulate the fractional solution as closely as possible: ideally, a buyer
b
with realized value
v
would find an available copy of their desired bundle with probability
x_{b,v}/q_{b,v}
, independently of other buyers, where
x_{b,v}
is the LP mass assigned to the buyer-value pair
(b,v)
and
q_{b,v}=\mathbb{P}\!\left[v_{b}=v\right]
.

Such independent acceptance probabilities, however, cannot be implemented directly using a static and anonymous menu. Instead, the mechanism must commit ex-ante to a fixed number of copies of each bundle at each price. Guided by the LP solution, we post no copies of bundle
S
at prices below
w_{S}
, and essentially unlimited copies at prices strictly above
w_{S}
, relying on the scarcity of high-value buyers to limit congestion. The number of copies offered exactly at price
w_{S}
is more delicate. Offering too many copies risks exhausting item capacities and blocking high-value buyers later in the sequence, while offering too few results in a significant loss relative to the fractional benchmark. To balance these effects, we randomize between offering
\lfloor x_{S,w_{S}}\rfloor
and
\lceil x_{S,w_{S}}\rceil
copies of bundle
S
at price
w_{S}
, where
x_{S,w_{S}}
denotes the total LP mass assigned to buyers demanding
S
with value
w_{S}
.

A desirable property of our menu is
price subadditivity
: for every bundle
S
, the posted price of
S
should be no greater than the total price of any collection of bundles whose union contains
S
. This guarantees that a buyer who desires
S
always prefers purchasing a copy of
S
rather than assembling a larger covering collection. Enforcing price subadditivity, however, is nontrivial. The prices suggested by the LP solution need not satisfy this property, and the buyers’ original value supports may not contain appropriate price levels that would permit a subadditive menu without sacrificing performance. To address this issue, we first augment each buyer’s value support with carefully chosen auxiliary price points. We then apply a post-processing step to the LP solution that enforces subadditivity before executing the menu-construction procedure described above.

To analyze the mechanism, we introduce an
unconstrained
algorithm: a hypothetical process that accepts every buyer who selects an available menu entry, ignoring item-capacity constraints. This auxiliary algorithm is easier to analyze and satisfies two key properties:

1.

Its expected welfare is a constant fraction of the scaled LP optimum, denoted by
\textnormal{{FracOpt}}_{\gamma}
throughout the paper.

2.

For every item
e
, the expected number of sold copies that use
e
is at most
c(e)/\gamma
.

The welfare of the actual mechanism equals the welfare of the unconstrained algorithm minus the value of
blocked
buyers-those accepted by the unconstrained algorithm but rejected when enforcing capacity constraints. Property (1) implies that it suffices to bound the expected welfare lost due to blocking as a constant fraction of the unconstrained value, while Property (2) allows us to control the expected contribution of blocked buyers.

d
-Single-Minded Setting.

In the
d
-single-minded setting, there is a set
\mathcal{M}
of
m
items, each with multiplicity specified by a capacity function
c:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
. Buyers arrive online, each drawing a value
v_{b}\sim\mathcal{D}_{b}
, and derive utility
v_{b}
if and only if they receive their entire desired bundle
S_{b}
, where
|S_{b}|\leq d
. Both the bundle
S_{b}
and the distribution
\mathcal{D}_{b}
are known in advance.

In this setting, we scale the item capacities in the LP relaxation by a factor
\gamma=e(10d)^{1/B}.
Using a concentration argument, we show that for any fixed item, the probability that it becomes congested is at most
1/(10d)
. Taking a union bound over the at most
d
items contained in a buyer’s desired bundle, we obtain that any bundle allocated by the unconstrained algorithm is congested with probability at most
0.1
. We leverage this bound to show that the expected value lost due to blocked buyers is at most a
1/10
fraction of the value of the scaled fractional optimum. Combined with the properties of the unconstrained algorithm, this yields the following guarantee.

Theorem 1.1
.

For the
d
-single-minded case with minimum item multiplicity
B
, our mechanism achieves an
O(d^{1/B})
-competitive static and anonymous posted-price mechanism.

General Single-Minded Setting.

We next consider the fully general single-minded setting, in which buyers may demand arbitrary bundles of items. Relative to the
d
-single-minded case, the absence of an a priori bound on bundle size requires more aggressive capacity scaling. Specifically, we scale down item capacities in the LP relaxation by a factor
\gamma=e(20m)^{1/(B+1)}.
The slack created by this scaling allows us to show that the expected cardinality of the set of blocked buyers is at most
1/20
.

A further complication arises from the possibility of unbounded valuations: even a single blocked buyer with a very large value could dominate the welfare loss, rendering bounds on the number of blocked buyers insufficient. To address this issue, we augment the main mechanism with a simple
small-market
component that, with a carefully chosen probability, posts a single bundle at an extremely high price. This auxiliary mechanism captures a constant fraction of the welfare contributed by very large values, ensuring that heavy-tailed realizations cannot overwhelm the performance guarantee of the LP-guided menu. The main mechanism handles the structured, high-probability portion of the market, while the small-market mechanism isolates and stabilizes the contribution of extreme values. Together, the combined mechanism yields the following guarantee.

Theorem 1.2
.

For general single-minded combinatorial auctions with minimum multiplicity
B
, our mechanism achieves an
O\!\left(m^{1/(B+1)}\right)
-competitive static and anonymous posted-price mechanism.

Graph Setting.

Finally, we extend our framework to the network-routing domain, in which each buyer seeks to route a unit of flow from a source node
s
to a target node
t
in a capacitated graph
G=(V,E)
. We view this setting as a natural generalization of the general single-minded case: rather than demanding a single bundle, each buyer can be satisfied by any feasible
s
-
t
path. The challenge arising by the extension is that the mechanism must select a specific routing for each buyer.

Once again, the structure of the LP relaxation plays a central role. We conceptually treat each source-target pair
(s,t)
as an abstract bundle and group buyers according to their requested pair. Applying the same menu-construction procedure as in the general single-minded setting determines how many copies are posted to serve each
(s,t)
pair, thereby inheriting the welfare guarantees of the unconstrained algorithm.

It remains to assign an explicit
s
-
t
path to each such copy. To this end, we independently sample a path for each copy according to a carefully chosen distribution derived from the LP solution. Using a roulette-style sampling argument, we show that this randomized routing preserves the expected load guarantees on every edge, matching those obtained in the single-minded analysis. This yields the following result.

Theorem 1.3
.

For the graph-routing setting with minimum edge capacity
B
, our mechanism achieves an
O\!\left(m^{1/(B+1)}\right)
-competitive static posted-price mechanism, where
m=|E|
.

Lower bounds.

Our impossibility results follow a standard lower-bound template for prophet inequalities (see, e.g.,
[
8
,
3
]
). In this template, buyers are partitioned into many
groups
of equal size
t
. Buyers arrive sequentially, each has value in
\{0,1\}
with
\mathbb{P}\!\left[v=1\right]=1/t
, and feasibility is engineered so that we can select buyers from at most one group. Since the expected number of buyers realized in a fixed group is
1
, after committing to a group, the online algorithm can extract only constant expected value from that group and thus gains at most
O(1)
value. In contrast, if we create about
t^{t}
groups, then with constant probability there exists a
fully active
group (all
t
buyers have value
1
), allowing the prophet to obtain welfare on the order of
t
. The main challenge is to
embed
this template into single-minded combinatorial auctions, where feasibility is induced by item capacities.

For unit capacities, we associate each group with a partition of the
m
items into
t
disjoint classes. The
t
buyers in the group are single-minded, with buyer
i
demanding exactly the
i
-th class. Disjointness within a partition guarantees that
within a group
all
t
buyers can simultaneously be served. To prevent serving buyers from
two different
groups, we choose a family of partitions that are
pairwise qualitatively independent (QI)
, a notion introduced by Rényi
[
26
]
, where every class of one partition intersects every class of another. Then any allocation that serves one buyer from each of two groups must allocate some common item twice, contradicting unit capacity. This realizes the "at most one group" constraint of the template using item feasibility.

With
B
copies per item, we generalize "at most one group" to "at most
B
groups". The right combinatorial notion is
(B\!+\!1)
-way qualitative independence
: for any
B\!+\!1
partitions and any choice of one class from each, their intersection is nonempty. Using such a family, any allocation that serves buyers from
B\!+\!1
distinct groups must allocate some item to
B\!+\!1
buyers, contradicting the
B
-copy constraint; meanwhile, any fixed set of
B
groups is simultaneously feasible because each item appears in at most one demanded class per group.

Poljak and Tuza
[
25
]
give the following lower bound on the number
r
-way qualitatively independent partitions of
m
items into
t
classes (denoted by
g(m,t,r)
):

g(m,t,r)\geq\frac{er}{t}\cdot\exp\left(\frac{m}{rt^{r}}\right)

thus, choosing

t\;=\;\left\lfloor\left(\frac{m}{2B\ln m}\right)^{1/(B+2)}\right\rfloor,

via simple algebra we can prove that there exists a
(B\!+\!1)
-way QI family of size at least
Bt^{t}
, thus we can instantiate
N=Bt^{t}
groups. In our instance, each group is fully active with probability
\left(1/t\right)^{t}
and if we let
X
be the number of fully active groups we get that
\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]=N\cdot\left(1/t\right)^{t}=B
, and with constant probability it holds
X\geq B
. The prophet then serves all
tB
active buyers, while any online algorithm is restricted to at most
B
groups and extracts only constant expected value per served group.

Theorem 1.4
.

For the general single-minded setting with
m
items and minimum item capacity
B\geq 1
,

every
online algorithm has competitive ratio,

\Omega\left(\left(\frac{m}{\ln m}\right)^{1/(B+2)}\right).

To obtain a lower bound in the
d
-single-minded setting, we additionally require every demanded bundle to have size at most
d
. This leads us to introduce the notion of
balanced
QI partitions, in which every class has size
\leq d
. We show that the probabilistic construction of
[
25
]
extends to this balanced setting (sampling uniformly from balanced labelings), yielding large
(B\!+\!1)
-way QI families with all classes of size about
m/t
. Setting
m=dt
and choosing

t\;=\;\left\lfloor\left(\frac{d}{2B\ln d}\right)^{1/(B+1)}\right\rfloor,

the same group-commitment argument goes through.

Theorem 1.5
.

For the
d
-single-minded setting with maximum desired bundle size
d
and minimum item capacity
B\geq 1
,

every
online algorithm has competitive ratio,

\Omega\left(\left(\frac{d}{\ln d}\right)^{1/(B+1)}\right).

For unit capacities, there exist stronger combinatorial constructions that improve the dependence on
m
beyond what the general probabilistic bound yields. Using the explicit construction of
[
25
]
, we build
t^{t}
pairwise QI
t
-partitions on
m=p^{3}
items with
t=p
, for some sufficiently large prime
p
, eliminating the
\ln m
loss.

Theorem 1.6
.

For the single-minded setting with
m
items and unit capacities,

every
online algorithm has competitive ratio,

\Omega\left(m^{1/3}\right).

Finally, we relate pairwise qualitative independence to the Alon-Alweiss measure (AAM) introduced by
[
2
,
29
]
, which yields even larger QI families on slightly fewer items and thus a mild exponent improvement.

Theorem 1.7
.

For the single-minded setting with
m
items and unit capacities,

every
online algorithm has competitive ratio,

\Omega\left(m^{\,1/3+c/\log\log m}\right)

for some absolute constant
c>0
.

1.2
Further Related Work

Online Contention Resolution Schemes.

We work with the ex-ante linear programming relaxation and interpret the fractional value assigned to each buyer-value pair as a target acceptance probability. The online realizations then play the role of a randomized rounding step. Our mechanism is responsible for enforcing feasibility ex-post, while preserving as much of the fractional optimum as possible.

This perspective is closely related to the framework of
contention resolution schemes (CRSs)
, introduced by Chekuri, Vondrák, and Zenklusen for submodular maximization under combinatorial constraints
[
10
]
. In this framework, a CRS is given a fractional point
x^{*}
in the relaxation polytope and samples a random set
R
according to the product distribution with marginals
x^{*}
. Since the resulting set
R
may violate feasibility constraints, the scheme subsequently discards selected elements to obtain a feasible subset
R^{\prime}\subseteq R
. Later, Feldman, Svensson, and Zenklusen
[
16
]
introduced the online counterpart of this framework, termed
online contention resolution schemes (OCRSs)
, which apply to a wide range of online settings, including prophet inequalities. In their work, they showed that the existence of a
c
-balanced OCRS implies a
c
-competitive prophet inequality. More recently, Lee and Singla
[
23
]
showed that a
c
-competitive prophet inequality also yields a
c
-competitive
ex-ante
OCRS.

Static versus Dynamic Pricing.

A related line of work is due to Rubinstein
[
27
]
, who studied prophet inequalities under general downward-closed feasibility constraints. He obtained an
O(\log n\cdot\log r)
-competitive algorithm, where
n
denotes the number of arriving elements (corresponding to buyers in our setting) and
r
the maximum cardinality of any feasible solution. Rubinstein’s algorithm observes each realized value upon arrival and accepts the element if doing so does not decrease a carefully designed dynamic potential function. Since this potential function depends on the current accepted set, the resulting acceptance rule is inherently dynamic. Recently, Banihashem et al.
[
4
]
showed that any prophet inequality has an implementation using a (sequential) posted price mechanism.

Lower Bounds.

The general
\Omega(\log n/\log\log n)
lower bound in prophet inequalities with
n
buyers that is used as a template for our main lower bound results has been introduced in other forms by Babaioff, Immorlica, and Kleinberg
[
3
]
and by Chawla et al.
[
8
]
. For the
d
-single-minded cases, lower bounds for prophet inequalities under the intersection of
q
partition matroids also imply hardness for
q
-single-minded prophet inequality instances: Kleinberg and Weinberg
[
21
]
gave a
\Omega(\sqrt{q})
lower bound and Saxena, Velusamy, and Weinberg
[
29
]
strengthened this to
q^{1/2+\Omega(1/\log\log q)}
using techniques of Alon and Alweiss
[
2
]
.

Preliminaries

Model.

The input consists of a set of
m
distinct items
\mathcal{M}
and copies per item specified by the function
c:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
. Let
B=\min_{e\in\mathcal{M}}c(e)
denote the minimum number of copies among all items
e\in\mathcal{M}
. We are also given a set
\mathcal{N}
of
n
buyers. We index buyers by
b\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Each buyer
b
is associated with a fixed set of items
S_{b}
known to the seller. In addition, each buyer
b
is associated with a valuation function
v^{(b)}:2^{\mathcal{M}}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that
v^{(b)}(S)=v_{b}
if
S_{b}\subseteq S
and
v^{(b)}(S)=0
otherwise. Therefore, buyers are single-minded and thus fully described by the value
v_{b}
and set
S_{b}
.

For the rest of the paper we will consider the Bayesian setting where
v_{b}
is drawn from a distribution
\mathcal{D}_{b}
that is known to the seller and independent across buyers. We will assume that the distributions are discrete with finite support
\mathcal{S}=\{0,\ldots,R\}
with
R\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, thus for each buyer
b
and each
v\in\mathcal{S}
we have
q_{b,v}>0
. In Appendix
A
we remove this assumption and we allow buyers to have different supports. Buyers arrive sequentially, each revealing their realized value upon arrival. We assume that the order of arrival is determined by an
almighty adversary
. Specifically, the almighty adversary can choose the arrival order after observing all realizations of the valuations and the random choices of the algorithm. Our objective is to maximize the social welfare, i.e., the sum of the values derived from the sets of items allocated to the buyers.

The class of mechanisms we consider is
static and anonymous bundle pricing mechanisms
. More specifically, before the arrival of the buyers, we offer a menu
M
that contains multiple entries of the form
(T_{j},p_{j},c_{j})
, with
T_{j}\in 2^{\mathcal{M}}
,
p_{j}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and
c_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
. In other words, we offer
c_{j}
copies of the bundle of items
T_{j}
at price
p_{j}
each. Upon arrival, each buyer
b
chooses a subset
J
of available menu entries that maximizes their utility, where their utility is
v_{b}
if
S_{b}\subseteq\bigcup_{j\in J}T_{j}
and
0
otherwise, minus the total payment
\sum_{j\in J}p_{j}
. Note that a menu entry
(T_{j},p_{j},c_{j})
can be chosen only if fewer than
c_{j}
copies of this entry have been sold so far and copies of each item in
T_{j}
are still available. The above class of mechanisms is static since it is determined upfront (before the realization of the buyers’ values) and does not change during the run of the algorithm. Also, it is anonymous because we offer the same menu to each buyer.

Notation.

We use the notation
[x]\coloneqq\{1,\ldots,x\}
for any
x\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
. For
v\in\mathcal{S}
define
q_{b,v}\coloneqq\mathbb{P}_{v_{b}\sim\mathcal{D}_{b}}\!\left[v_{b}=v\right]
and let
\mathcal{D}\coloneq\prod_{b\in[n]}\mathcal{D}_{b}=\mathcal{D}_{1}\times\ldots\times\mathcal{D}_{n}
. Let
I\sim\mathcal{D}
be an instance of buyers’ values. For each buyer
b
, we denote by
v_{b}
the random variable corresponding to its value. Note that, throughout the paper, whenever an index of the variables
x_{b,v}
where
b\in[n],\ v\in\mathcal{S}
is omitted, we implicitly assume summation over that index (unless stated otherwise). For example,
x_{b}=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{S}}x_{b,v}
and
x_{v}=\sum_{b\in[n]}x_{b,v}
. For an event
\mathcal{E}
we define
\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\mathcal{E}\right\}}
as its indicator function. Finally, let
\ell_{A}(e)
denote the load of item
e
due to buyers in
A
, i.e.

2.1
Linear Program

Like in many other works in the stochastic setting, we will compare against the expected offline optimal social welfare

\displaystyle\max\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{b\in[n]}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{S}}x_{b,v}\cdot v

s.t.

\displaystyle\sum_{b\in[n]:e\in S_{b}}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{S}}x_{b,v}\leq c(e),\qquad\forall e\in\mathcal{M}

\displaystyle 0\leq x_{b,v}\leq q_{b,v},\qquad\forall b\in[n],\ \forall v\in\mathcal{S}

Note that in the above LP, the capacity constraints are enforced only in expectation (ex-ante), so

represents a feasible ex-ante fractional allocation. Let

be the optimal value of the previous LP. Generally, fractional relaxations upper bound the optimal welfare

Lemma 2.1
.

\textnormal{{FracOpt}}\geq\textnormal{{Opt}}

2.2
Structure of the LP solution

Before analyzing our bundle pricing, we first establish several structural properties of the fractional optimal solution of the LP. The key conceptual tool for understanding this structure is the notion of
value exchange
.

Suppose for the moment that we ignore the constraints
x_{b,v}\leq q_{b,v}
. Consider two variables
x_{b^{\prime},v^{\prime}}>0
and
x_{b^{\prime\prime},v^{\prime\prime}}
with
S_{b^{\prime\prime}}\subseteq S_{b^{\prime}}
. If we decrease
x_{b^{\prime},v^{\prime}}
by a small amount (mass)
\delta>0
and increase
x_{b^{\prime\prime},v^{\prime\prime}}
by the same amount, then the capacity constraints remain satisfied, since allocating mass on a subset of a set consumes no more capacity than allocating the same mass on the original set. This exchange strictly increases the LP objective whenever
v^{\prime\prime}>v^{\prime}
, as we shift mass from a lower-value variable to a higher-value one without affecting feasibility. Thus, in an optimal LP solution
x
, no such improving exchange should be possible. The only way such an exchange can be blocked is by the constraints
x_{b^{\prime\prime},v^{\prime\prime}}\leq q_{b^{\prime\prime},v^{\prime\prime}}
. Consequently, for two variables
0<x_{b^{\prime},v^{\prime}}
and
x_{b^{\prime\prime},v^{\prime\prime}}
with
S_{b^{\prime\prime}}\subseteq S_{b^{\prime}}
, whenever
v^{\prime\prime}>v^{\prime}
, we must have
x_{b^{\prime\prime},v^{\prime\prime}}=q_{b^{\prime\prime},v^{\prime\prime}}
. In the following section, we formalize this intuition, first for each buyer individually and then for each set
S
.

Per Buyer.

Intuitively, our goal is to determine a price to offer each buyer. If we did not require the mechanism to be anonymous or static, a natural approach would be the following: upon the arrival of buyer
b
with realized value
v
, we accept them with probability
x_{b,v}/q_{b,v}
. Recall that
x_{b,v}
can be interpreted as the ex ante probability mass of allocating
S_{b}
to buyer
b
when
v_{b}=v
. Thus this acceptance rule ensures that buyer
b
is served with unconditional probability
q_{b,v}\cdot(x_{b,v}/q_{b,v})=x_{b,v}
, matching the LP’s marginal allocation in expectation. Of course, this does not yield a feasible mechanism, because the LP capacity constraints hold only in expectation and may be violated ex-post. The subsequent analysis will show how to overcome this limitation.

From this perspective, the LP solution guides our pricing decisions. Interpreting acceptance probabilities as price thresholds, the LP suggests which values should be priced out and which should be encouraged. If the LP assigns zero weight to a particular value, we should not accept buyers at that level, suggesting a higher price in a static-price mechanism. Conversely, if the LP assigns the maximum feasible weight to a value, this suggests that the corresponding price should not be set above that level. However, there may exist some intermediate
crucial
values where neither condition applies. Properly handling these crucial values is essential to the design of our mechanism.

Formally, fix a buyer
b
and consider the vector
X^{(b)}=[x_{b,v}]_{v\in\mathcal{S}}
, whose entries correspond to the optimal fractional LP allocation for buyer
b
, ordered in increasing order of

.

Definition 2.2
.

A value

is

if

, and

While identical pricing is crucial for maintaining anonymity, it must be combined with additional structural properties, such as subadditivity across sets, which we address in later sections. In this subsection, we focus on the implications of identical pricing. Specifically, anonymity allows us to treat all buyers interested in the same set

3.2

We now introduce an analytical tool, the Unconstrained Algorithm. This is a hypothetical version of our mechanism that completely ignores item capacities and only enforces the constraints imposed by the menu. In particular, the menu may induce total demand that exceeds the available copies of some items, and the Unconstrained Algorithm still serves all such requests. We use this algorithm purely for analysis: it is not required to be feasible, but it allows us to bound the performance of our actual (capacity-feasible) mechanism by comparing their expected welfare. General Single-Minded Setting

We now turn to the most general case, where buyers are single-minded over arbitrary bundles. We proceed to prove
Theorem

1.2
.

Approach.

Our algorithmic approach follows the same template as before: we solve the scaled-down LP (
Step 1
) with an appropriate choice of
\gamma
, construct the randomized menu (
Step 2
), and analyze the performance of the resulting posted-price mechanism.

In this setting, however, very large valuations can dominate the benchmark and invalidate the guarantees obtained by the LP-based menu alone. To handle this, we introduce an auxiliary mechanism that posts a single high-priced bundle; the final algorithm randomly mixes between these two mechanisms.

Analysis.

We select
\gamma\;=\;e(20m)^{1/(B+1)},
and let
\textnormal{{FracOpt}}_{\gamma}
be the value of the scaled LP. When a buyer arrives, they may desire a menu entry but fail to obtain it due to capacity conflicts with previously accepted buyers. As before, we define:

\textsc{Blocked}_{M,I}=\{b\in\mathcal{N}:b\in\textnormal{{UAlg}}_{M,I}\text{ and }b\notin\textnormal{{Alg}}_{M,I}\},

and
\textnormal{{Alg}}_{M,I}
is the total value obtained by our (constrained) algorithm. Using the unconstrained benchmark, we have the basic identity

\mathbb{E}_{M,I}\left[\textnormal{{Alg}}_{M,I}\right]\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{M,I}\left[\textnormal{{UAlg}}_{M,I}\right]\;-\;\mathbb{E}_{M,I}\left[\sum_{b\in\textsc{Blocked}_{M,I}}v_{b}\right].

We now bound the loss due to rejections. Partition buyers into those with
moderate
value and those with very large value:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{M,I}\left[\sum_{b\in\textsc{Blocked}_{M,I}}v_{b}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{M,I}\left[\sum_{b\in\textsc{Blocked}_{M,I}}v_{b}\,\mathbbm{1}{\left\{v_{b}<2\textnormal{{FracOpt}}_{\gamma}\right\}}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbb{E}_{M,I}\left[\sum_{b\in\textsc{Blocked}_{M,I}}v_{b}\,\mathbbm{1}{\left\{v_{b}\geq 2\textnormal{{FracOpt}}_{\gamma}\right\}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\textnormal{{FracOpt}}_{\gamma}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{M,I}\left[|\textsc{Blocked}_{M,I}|\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbb{E}_{M,I}\left[\sum_{b\in\mathcal{N}}v_{b}\,\mathbbm{1}{\left\{v_{b}\geq 2\textnormal{{FracOpt}}_{\gamma}\right\}}\right]

Bounding the number of rejected buyers.

We begin by analyzing the first term, bounding the expected number of blocked buyers.

Lemma 5.1
.

We have

\mathbb{E}_{M,I}\left[|\textsc{Blocked}_{M,I}|\right]\;\leq\;\frac{1}{20}.

Proof.

A buyer is rejected only if some item in their bundle is oversold. Thus

|\textsc{Blocked}_{M,I}|\;\leq\;\sum_{e\in\mathcal{M}}\sum_{k=c(e)+1}^{\infty}\mathbbm{1}{\left\{L_{M,I}(e)=k\right\}}\,(k-c(e)).

Taking expectations and using linearity,

\mathbb{E}_{M,I}\left[|\textsc{Blocked}_{M,I}|\right]\;\leq\;\sum_{e\in\mathcal{M}}\sum_{k=c(e)+1}^{\infty}(k-c(e))\,\mathbb{P}\!\left[L_{M,I}(e)=k\right].

By
Lemma

3.4
, the random load
L_{M,I}(e)
is upper bounded by a sum of negatively
associated Bernoulli variables with total mean

. A standard

\mathbb{P}\!\left[L_{M,I}(e)\geq k\right]\;\leq\;\mathbb{P}\!\left[Z_{M,I}(e)\geq k\right]\;\leq\;\left(\frac{e\,\mu_{e}}{k}\right)^{k}\;\leq\;\left(\frac{e\,c(e)}{\gamma\,k}\right)^{k}.

Let

with

. Using
(1+\tfrac{j}{c})^{c+j}\geq e^{j}
and our choice

\gamma

\left(\frac{e\,c(e)}{\gamma(c(e)+j)}\right)^{c(e)+j}\;\leq\;\left(\frac{e}{\gamma}\right)^{c(e)+j}e^{-j}\;\leq\;\frac{1}{20m}\,e^{-j}.

Summing over

gives

\sum_{k=c(e)+1}^{\infty}(k-c(e))\,\Pr[L_{M,I}(e)=k]\;\leq\;\frac{1}{20m}.

Summing over all

∎

Plugging
Lemma

5.1
into our decomposition and using

\mathbb{E}_{M,I}\left[\textnormal{{Alg}}_{M,I}\right]\;\geq\;\frac{1}{40}\,\textnormal{{FracOpt}}_{\gamma}-\sum_{b\in\mathcal{N}}\mathbb{E}_{M,I}\left[v_{b}\,\mathbbm{1}{\left\{v_{b}\geq 2\textnormal{{FracOpt}}_{\gamma}\right\}}\right].

Handling very large valuations.

Define an auxiliary mechanism

Lemma 5.2

\mathbb{P}\!\left[\exists b:v_{b}\geq 2\,\textnormal{{FracOpt}}_{\gamma}\right]\leq 1/2.

If instead

We now show that

Lemma 5.3
.

\mathbb{E}_{M,I}\left[\textnormal{{Alg}}_{2}\right]\;\geq\;\frac{1}{2}\,\sum_{b\in\mathcal{N}}\mathbb{E}_{M,I}\left[v_{b}\,\mathbbm{1}{\left\{v_{b}\geq 2\textnormal{{FracOpt}}_{\gamma}\right\}}\right].

Proof.

For fixed

\textnormal{{Alg}}_{2}=\sum_{b\in\mathcal{N}}v_{b}\,\mathbbm{1}{\left\{v_{b}\geq 2\textnormal{{FracOpt}}_{\gamma}\right\}}\,\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\text{the posted copy is still available for }b\right\}}.

Taking expectations and using independence of the

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{I}\left[\textnormal{{Alg}}_{2}\right]

\displaystyle\;\geq\;\sum_{b}\mathbb{E}_{I}\left[v_{b}\mathbbm{1}{\left\{v_{b}\geq 2\textnormal{{FracOpt}}_{\gamma}\right\}}\right]\,\mathbb{P}\!\left[\forall b^{\prime}:v(b^{\prime})<2\textnormal{{FracOpt}}_{\gamma}\right]

\displaystyle\;\geq\;\frac{1}{2}\sum_{b}\mathbb{E}_{I}\left[v_{b}\mathbbm{1}{\left\{v_{b}\geq 2\textnormal{{FracOpt}}_{\gamma}\right\}}\right],

where the last inequality applies

\textnormal{{Alg}}_{1}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\textnormal{{Alg}}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{3}\left(\frac{1}{40}\textnormal{{FracOpt}}_{\gamma}-\sum_{b}\mathbb{E}\left[v_{b}\mathbbm{1}{\left\{v_{b}\geq 2\textnormal{{FracOpt}}_{\gamma}\right\}}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{2}{3}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\sum_{b}\mathbb{E}\left[v_{b}\mathbbm{1}{\left\{v_{b}\geq 2\textnormal{{FracOpt}}_{\gamma}\right\}}\right]

In this section, we extend our framework to the setting where items correspond to edges and buyers correspond to routing requests in a general graph. Let

\displaystyle\max

\displaystyle\sum_{b\in[n]}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{b}}x_{b,v,p}\cdot v

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\sum_{b\in[n]}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in\mathcal{P}_{b}:\\

\displaystyle\forall e\in E,

\displaystyle\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{b}}x_{b,v,p}\;\leq\;q_{b,v},

\displaystyle\forall b\in[n],\,v\in\mathcal{S},

\displaystyle x_{b,v,p}\geq 0,

\displaystyle\forall b\in[n],\,v\in\mathcal{S},\,p\in\mathcal{P}_{b}.

Let

A key observation is that any

We introduce a generalized bundle

For the moment, assume that every buyer

Conclusion

We introduced a unified approach for online resource allocation with strong complementarities via
static
and
anonymous
bundle-pricing. Our mechanisms are guided by an ex-ante LP relaxation with scaled down capacities, and translate the fractional solution’s threshold structure into posted bundle prices. This yields improved guarantees as multiplicity grows: an
O(d^{1/B})
-competitive mechanism for the
d
-single-minded setting, and an
O(m^{1/(B+1)})
-competitive mechanism for general single-minded combinatorial auctions and for capacitated graph routing. We also prove information-theoretic lower bounds via an embedding of the classical prophet-inequality hard instance using qualitatively-independent partitions, showing that the dependence on
m
(or
d
) and
B
is essentially unavoidable. Two directions seem particularly interesting. First, closing the remaining gaps for both the general and
d
-single-minded setting. Second, sample-based implementations: our mechanism assumes that the value distributions of the buyers are explicitly given to solve the ex-ante LP and set prices. Can one obtain comparable guarantees using few samples?

Appendix A
Different Distribution Supports

Our results extend to the setting where each buyer
b
has a distribution
\mathcal{D}_{b}
over its own support
\mathcal{S}_{b}
and by a standard scaling argument we can assume that
\mathcal{S}_{b}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
without loss of generality. We extend each buyer-specific support
\mathcal{S}_{b}
to a common support
\mathcal{S}
, run our
c
-approximation algorithm in the resulting same-support instance, and post the induced randomized menu. We show that with the appropriate extension, this (randomized) menu yields a
4c/3
-approximation for the original problem with different supports.

Let
v_{\max}^{(b)}=\max_{v\in\mathcal{S}_{b}}v
and
v_{\max}=\max_{b}v_{\max}^{(b)}
. Define
\mathcal{S}=\{0,\ldots,v_{\max}\}\supseteq\cup_{b}\mathcal{S}_{b}
. To reduce to the equal-support case, we modify each distribution
\mathcal{D}_{b}
so that all of them share the same support
\mathcal{S}
. For each buyer
b
with
\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{b}\neq\emptyset
, define the modified distribution
\mathcal{D}_{b}^{\prime}
by

q^{\prime}_{b,v^{(b)}_{\max}}\;=\;q_{b,v^{(b)}_{\max}}-\varepsilon\cdot|\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{b}|,\qquad q^{\prime}_{b,v}=\varepsilon\quad\forall v\in\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{b},

and leave all other
q_{b,v}
unchanged. The parameter
\varepsilon>0
is chosen sufficiently small so that

0<q^{\prime}_{b,v^{(b)}_{\max}}\quad\text{i.e. }\quad\varepsilon\leq C_{1}\coloneqq\min_{b}\frac{q_{b,v^{(b)}_{\max}}}{2\,|\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{b}|}.

The modified distribution
\mathcal{D}_{b}^{\prime}
is a valid distribution, and all
\mathcal{D}_{b}^{\prime}
share common support
\mathcal{S}
. Define
\mathcal{D}\coloneqq\mathcal{D}_{1}\times\ldots\times\mathcal{D}_{n}
and
\mathcal{D^{\prime}}\coloneqq\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{1}\times\ldots\times\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{n}
.

Let
FracOpt
and
\textnormal{{FracOpt}}^{\prime}
be the fractional LP optima under the original and modified distributions, respectively. Now take an optimal LP solution
x
for
\mathcal{D}
and decrease
x_{b,v^{(b)}_{\max}}
by

\min\left\{x_{b,v^{(b)}_{\max}},\varepsilon|\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{b}|\right\}

for each buyer
b
; the resulting solution is feasible for
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
and loses at most
\varepsilon|\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{b}|\,v_{\max}^{(b)}
in value for each buyer
b
. Thus,

\textnormal{{FracOpt}}^{\prime}\geq\textnormal{{FracOpt}}\;-\;\varepsilon\sum_{b}|\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{b}|\,v^{(b)}_{\max}.

Thus, by choosing

\varepsilon\leq C_{2}\coloneqq\min\!\left\{C_{1},\;\frac{\textnormal{{FracOpt}}}{2\sum_{b}|\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{b}|\,v^{(b)}_{\max}}\right\},

we guarantee
\textnormal{{FracOpt}}^{\prime}\geq\frac{1}{2}\textnormal{{FracOpt}}
.

Let
I\sim\mathcal{D}
and
I^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
denote value realizations under the
original and modified distributions. For the total variation distance between
\mathcal{D}_{b},\mathcal{D}_{b}^{\prime}
for each buyer
b
we get by definition that
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal{D}_{b},\mathcal{D}_{b}^{\prime})\;=\;\varepsilon\,|\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{b}|
. Thus, since
\mathcal{D},\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
are product distributions we get

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{D}^{\prime})\;\leq\;\sum_{b}d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal{D}_{b},\mathcal{D}_{b}^{\prime})\;=\;\varepsilon\sum_{b}|\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{b}|.

For any fixed menu
M
and any realization
I
, the welfare of our algorithm is
bounded as

0\;\leq\;\textnormal{{Alg}}_{M,I}\;\leq\;\sum_{b\in\mathcal{N}}v_{\max}^{(b)}\;\eqqcolon\;V_{\max}^{\mathrm{tot}}.

Therefore, for any fixed
M
, using a standard property of the total variation distance (see Proposition 4.5 in
[
24
]
), we get

\Bigl|\mathbb{E}_{I}\left[\textnormal{{Alg}}_{M,I}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{I^{\prime}}\left[\textnormal{{Alg}}_{M,I^{\prime}}\right]\Bigr|\;\leq\;d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{D}^{\prime})\cdot V_{\max}^{\mathrm{tot}}\;\leq\;\varepsilon\!\left(\sum_{b}|\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{b}|\right)\!V_{\max}^{\mathrm{tot}}.

Taking expectation over the random menu
M
yields

\Bigl|\mathbb{E}_{M,I}\left[\textnormal{{Alg}}_{M,I}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{M,I^{\prime}}\left[\textnormal{{Alg}}_{M,I^{\prime}}\right]\Bigr|\;\leq\;\varepsilon\!\left(\sum_{b}|\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{b}|\right)\!V_{\max}^{\mathrm{tot}}.

Thus, by additionally choosing

\varepsilon\;\leq\;C_{3}\coloneqq\min\left\{C_{2},\frac{\textnormal{{FracOpt}}}{4c\,\left(\sum_{b}|\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{S}_{b}|\right)\!V_{\max}^{\mathrm{tot}}}\right\},

we get

\mathbb{E}_{M,I}\left[\textnormal{{Alg}}_{M,I}\right]\;\geq\;\mathbb{E}_{M,I^{\prime}}\left[\textnormal{{Alg}}_{M,I^{\prime}}\right]\;-\;\frac{\textnormal{{FracOpt}}}{4c}\;\geq\;\,\frac{\textnormal{{FracOpt}}^{\prime}}{c}\;-\;\frac{\textnormal{{FracOpt}}}{4c}.

Combining with
\textnormal{{FracOpt}}^{\prime}\geq\frac{1}{2}\textnormal{{FracOpt}}
gives

\mathbb{E}_{M,I}\left[\textnormal{{Alg}}_{M,I}\right]\;\geq\;\frac{3}{4c}\,\textnormal{{FracOpt}}.

Hence, a
c
-approximation algorithm for the equal-support case yields a
4c/3
-approximation algorithm for the general case, thus we can simply run our algorithm on the modified distributions
\mathcal{D}_{b}^{\prime}
and post the resulting randomized menu, justifying our assumption.

Appendix B
Price Subadditivity

We now relax the simplifying assumption that a buyer who desires a bundle
S
is only interested in
S
copies. In principle, a buyer could purchase several bundles
T_{1},\dots,T_{\ell}
from the posted menu such that
\bigcup_{i}T_{i}\supseteq S
. We show that after a simple normalization step applied to the fractional LP solution, no buyer can ever strictly prefer such a cover over purchasing
S
directly. In other words, the posted prices are subadditive.

Pre-processing.

After solving the scaled-down LP of
Step 1
, let
\mathcal{H}
denote the set of feasible fractional solutions
x
attaining value
\textnormal{{FracOpt}}_{\gamma}
. Among these, we select a canonical solution by solving the secondary LP

\min\;\Bigl(\textsc{FracWeight}(x)+\sum_{e\in\mathcal{M}}w_{e}(x)\Bigr)\qquad\text{s.t. }x\in\mathcal{H},

where

\textsc{FracWeight}(x)=\sum_{b}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{S}}x_{b,v},\qquad w_{e}(x)=\sum_{b:e\in S_{b}}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{S}}x_{b,v}.

We henceforth work only with optimal solutions of this normalization LP. We first prove the following lemma.

Lemma B.1
.

Fix a desired set
S
and a value
v
such that
x_{S,v}<q_{S,v}
.
Suppose there exist different desired sets
T_{1},\dots,T_{\ell}
such that
\bigcup_{i=1}^{\ell}T_{i}\supseteq S
.
If
x_{T_{i},v_{i}}>0
for all
i\in[\ell]
and some
v_{i}
, then

\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}v_{i}>v.

Proof.

Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that
\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}v_{i}\leq v
. First, if
v=0
the inequality trivially holds, thus we can assume that
v>0
. Then, if
\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}v_{i}<v
, since
v_{1},\dots,v_{\ell}
are crucial or tight values and
v
is non-tight we can transfer
\epsilon>0
mass (for sufficiently small
\epsilon
) from each
x_{b_{i},v_{i}}>0
to
x_{b_{j},v}
for some choice of buyers
b_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{T_{i}},\,b_{j}\in\mathcal{A}_{S}
for
i\in[\ell]
, and remain feasible while increasing the objective; contradiction.

Thus suppose that
\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}v_{i}=v
. We will get contradiction due to the objective of the pre-processing step. Indeed we can do the same transfer as before and get,

•

If
\ell=1
then
T_{1}\supsetneq S
, so the load
w_{e}(x)
decreases by
\epsilon
on every item
e\in T_{1}\setminus S
, while
\textsc{FracWeight}(x)
is preserved. Thus the total objective decreases, contradiction.

•

If
\ell>1
then the value shifted from
\ell
different buyers is consolidated into a single buyer, so
\textsc{FracWeight}(x)
decreases by
(\ell-1)\epsilon
, while each
w_{e}(x)
remains the same or decreases. Again, the total objective decreases, contradiction.

∎

Subadditivity.

Fix a buyer whose desired set is
S
. Suppose that in the posted menu they purchase bundles
T_{1},\dots,T_{\ell}
with and

\bigcup_{i}T_{i}\supseteq S\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}p(T_{i})<p(S)

Note that since we always offer
|\mathcal{A}_{S}|
copies of set
S
at some price, a copy of
S
will always be available. Indeed, for the buyer that arrives first this trivially holds and since we will prove that the menu is subadditive they will not purchase bundles of any other desired set, thus the
|\mathcal{A}_{S}|
are enough for each desired set
S
(they are dedicated to buyers that desire
S
).

First, if
p(S)=1
then
p(T_{i})=0
, contradiction. Now notice that
x_{T_{i},p(T_{i})}>0
and
x_{S,p(S)-1}
is not tight by the definition of our pricing rules. Thus, we can apply
Lemma

B.1
with
v_{i}=p(T_{i})
and
v=p(S)-1
and get

\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}p(T_{i})>p(S)-1,

that contradicts with (
B
).

Tie-Breaking.

We now show that no tie-breaking is needed by a small price perturbation. More specifically, instead of posting price
p
for a copy we post price
f(p)=(1-\delta)\cdot p
, for
\delta<1/(2R)
, where
R=v_{\max}
. Indeed
p-1<f(p)<p
thus
p
value buyers now gain positive utility by purchasing the copy, so no tie-breaking is needed, while buyers with value
<p
still do not afford buying the copy. Note that subadditivity of the menu still holds since the
(1-\delta)
factor cancels out.

D.2
Subadditivity

We now relax the simplifying assumption that a buyer who desires an
(s,t)
-path is only interested on
(s,t)
-path copies. The proof is similar to the single-minded case. Similarly, a buyer could purchase several paths

, that correspond to
(s_{1},t_{1}),\ldots,(s_{\ell},t_{\ell})
types respectively, from the posted menu such that
\bigcup_{i}p_{i}\supseteq p\in\mathcal{P}_{s,t}
. We will show that after applying essentially the same simple normalization step to the fractional LP solution as in the single-minded case, no buyer can ever strictly prefer such a cover over purchasing an
(s,t)
-path directly. In other words, the posted prices are subadditive.

Pre-processing.

For the graph setting, the objective of the pre-processing step becomes

\min\;\Bigl(\textsc{FracWeight}(x)+\sum_{e\in E}w_{e}(x)\Bigr)\qquad\text{s.t. }x\in\mathcal{H},

where

\textsc{FracWeight}(x)=\sum_{b}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{b}}x_{b,v,p}\quad\text{and}\quad w_{e}(x)=\sum_{b}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}_{b}\,:\,e\in p}x_{b,v,p}.

We now state the equivalent of
Lemma

B.1
for the graph setting.

Lemma D.1
.

Fix a desired
(s,t)
-path such that
x_{v}<q_{v}
.
Suppose there exist different
(s_{i},t_{i})
-paths

The proof proceeds exactly as in the single-minded case, except that we transfer mass from paths rather than from sets.

Subadditivity.

We slightly adjust our notation for prices, writing them as
r
rather than

Formally, consider a tree

\mathcal{D}_{b}=\mathrm{Be}(q_{b})

Approach.

The approach follows our general algorithmic framework and the analysis borrows the main ideas from
[
9
]
. First, solve the ex-ante linear relaxation with appropriately scaled capacities (
Step 1

. Next, post the randomized menu resulting from

We say that a buyer

and

. We partition blocked buyers according to where the blocking occurs:

\textsc{Peak}_{M,I}
is the set of buyers in

that are blocked on one of their peak edges,

•

\textsc{NonPeak}_{M,I}

Finally, let

Analysis.

Combining

For every edge

Moreover,

\mathbb{E}_{M,I}\left[\textnormal{{UAlg}}_{M,I}\right]\;\geq\;\frac{\textnormal{{FracOpt}}_{\gamma}}{8}\;\geq\;\frac{\textnormal{{FracOpt}}}{80}.

For a fixed

\displaystyle{}\textnormal{{Alg}}_{M,I}=\textnormal{{UAlg}}_{M,I}-\textsc{Blocked}_{M,I}=\textnormal{{UAlg}}_{M,I}-\textsc{Peak}_{M,I}-\textsc{NonPeak}_{M,I}

(3)

Lemma F.2

For every edge

\mathbb{E}_{M,I}\left[\ell_{\textnormal{{UAlg}}_{M,I}\setminus\{b\}}(e)\mid b\in\textnormal{{UAlg}}_{M,I}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{M,I}\left[\ell_{\textnormal{{UAlg}}_{M,I}}(e)\right]

Proof.

, a buyer
b

of buyers that desire the same set of edges,

. Define also

We now prove the following two lemmas.

Lemma F.3

\mathbb{E}_{M,I}\left[\textsc{NonPeak}_{M,I}\right]\leq 0.1\,\mathbb{E}_{M,I}\left[\textnormal{{UAlg}}_{M,I}\right]

It holds that:

\displaystyle\textsc{NonPeak}_{M,I}

\displaystyle=\sum_{b\in\textsc{Blocked}_{M,I}}\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\text{$b$ is blocked on a non-peak edge by a buyer in $\textnormal{{Alg}}_{M,I}$}\right\}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{M,I}\left[\textsc{NonPeak}_{M,I}\right]

\mathbb{E}_{M,I}\left[\textsc{Peak}_{M,I}\right]\leq 0.2\;\mathbb{E}_{M,I}\left[\textnormal{{UAlg}}_{M,I}\right]

Proof.

It holds that:

\displaystyle\textsc{Peak}_{M,I}

\displaystyle=\sum_{b\in\textsc{Blocked}_{M,I}}\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\text{$b$ is blocked on a peak edge by a buyer in $\textnormal{{Alg}}_{M,I}$}\right\}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{b\in\textnormal{{UAlg}}_{M,I}}\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\ell_{\textnormal{{Alg}}_{M,I}}(e_{b}^{1})>0\right\}}+\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\ell_{\textnormal{{Alg}}_{M,I}}(e_{b}^{2})>0\right\}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{b\in\mathcal{N}}\mathbbm{1}{\left\{b\in\textnormal{{UAlg}}_{M,I}\right\}}\left(\ell_{\textnormal{{UAlg}}_{M,I}\setminus\{b\}}(e_{b}^{1})+\ell_{\textnormal{{UAlg}}_{M,I}\setminus\{b\}}(e_{b}^{2})\right)

Taking expectations in both parts we get similarly to the non-peak case:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{M,I}\left[\textsc{Peak}_{M,I}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{b\in\mathcal{N}}\mathbb{P}_{M,I}\!\left[b\in\textnormal{{UAlg}}_{M,I}\right]\left(\mathbb{E}_{M,I}\left[\ell_{\textnormal{{UAlg}}_{M,I}}(e_{b}^{1})\right]+\mathbb{E}_{M,I}\left[\ell_{\textnormal{{UAlg}}_{M,I}}(e_{b}^{2})\right]\right)

\displaystyle=0.2\;\mathbb{E}_{M,I}\left[\textnormal{{UAlg}}_{M,I}\right]

Proof of Lemma
2.1

Proof.

Consider the optimal allocation for a fixed realization of buyers’ values.
For each buyer
b
and value
v\in\mathcal{S}
, let
y_{b,v}\in\{0,1\}
indicate whether in this realization buyer
b
is allocated bundle
S_{b}
when their value is
v
.
By feasibility of the offline solution, for every
e\in\mathcal{M}
we have

\sum_{b:e\in S_{b}}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{S}}y_{b,v}\leq c(e),

and moreover
y_{b,v}=0
unless buyer
b
realizes value
v
, implying
\mathbb{E}_{I}\left[y_{b,v}\right]\leq q_{b,v}
, where the expectation is over the randomness in the buyers’ realized values.
Define the fractional allocation

. By construction,
0\leq x_{b,v}\leq q_{b,v}
, so the box constraints of the LP are satisfied. Taking expectations of the offline capacity constraints yields, for every

\sum_{b:e\in S_{b}}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{S}}x_{b,v}=\mathbb{E}_{I}\left[\sum_{b:e\in S_{b}}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{S}}y_{b,v}\right]\leq c(e),

so the LP capacity constraints are also satisfied. The objective value of this feasible fractional solution is

\sum_{b\in[n]}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{S}}x_{b,v}\cdot v=\mathbb{E}_{I}\left[\sum_{b\in[n]}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{S}}y_{b,v}\cdot v\right]=\textnormal{{Opt}},

where the last equality holds because the offline allocation attains the optimal welfare for each realization. Since
FracOpt
is the optimal value of the LP and we constructed a feasible fractional solution with value

, we obtain
\textnormal{{FracOpt}}\geq\textnormal{{Opt}}
.
∎

G.2
Proof of Lemma
2.3

Proof.

Fix a buyer
b
and consider the vector
X^{(b)}=(x_{b,v})_{v\in\mathcal{S}}
, where values in
\mathcal{S}
are ordered increasingly. Recall the value-exchange argument from the main text: whenever
v^{\prime}>v
, we may shift an infinitesimal amount of mass from
x_{b,v}
to
x_{b,v^{\prime}}
without violating any LP constraints, unless one of the box constraints
0\leq x_{b,v}
or
x_{b,v^{\prime}}\leq q_{b,v^{\prime}}
becomes tight. Such a shift strictly increases the objective because
v^{\prime}>v
.

Case 1: A crucial value
w
exists.

Suppose there exists a value
v<w
with
x_{b,v}>0
. Since
v<w
and
x_{b,w}<q_{b,w}
, we may shift mass from
x_{b,v}
to
x_{b,w}
without violating feasibility, strictly improving the objective - a contradiction. Thus
x_{b,v}=0
for all
v<w
. Next, suppose there exists a value
u>w
with
x_{b,u}<q_{b,u}
. Since
x_{b,w}>0
, we may shift mass from
x_{b,w}
to
x_{b,u}
, again increasing the objective. Hence all
u>w
must satisfy
x_{b,u}=q_{b,u}
.

Case 2: No crucial value exists.

Here every
v
satisfies either
x_{b,v}=0
or
x_{b,v}=q_{b,v}
. Suppose there exist
u>v
with
x_{b,u}=0
and
x_{b,v}=q_{b,v}
. Since
u>v
and
x_{b,u}
is not tight, shifting mass from
v
to
u
preserves feasibility and increases the objective - a contradiction. Thus all zero entries occur at smaller values, and all tight entries occur at larger values, with no interleaving.
∎

Proof of Lemma

Proof.

Fix a bundle
S
and let
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{S}
and

for simplicity. Recall that

x_{S,v}\;=\;\sum_{b\in\mathcal{A}}x_{b,v}\qquad\text{and}\qquad q_{S,v}\;=\;\sum_{b\in\mathcal{A}}q_{b,v},

and we refer to

as tight if
x_{S,v}=q_{S,v}
and crucial if

. We begin with two structural claims.

Lemma G.1
.

If any buyer

has a crucial value, then all crucial values across buyers in
\mathcal{A}
occur at the same value. We call this value the

Suppose buyer

has crucial value

and buyer

has crucial value

. Because both buyers demand the same bundle

preserves all LP capacity constraints: both variables consume exactly the same set of items. This shift increases the objective because

can have crucial values at different levels, proving the claim.

Lemma G.2
.

If

x_{S,v}=0\;\text{ for all }v<w,\qquad\text{and}\qquad x_{S,v}=q_{S,v}\;\text{ for all }v>w.

. First suppose there exists

with

. Because

Now suppose there exists

G.6
Auxiliary Lemma

Lemma G.3
.

For a random variable
X
with mean
\mu
that is the sum of some independent random variables on
\{0,1\}
we get:

\mathbb{P}\!\left[X\geq\mu/2\right]\geq 0.5\cdot\min\{1,\mu\}

Proof.

Let
X=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
, where the
X_{i}
are independent
\{0,1\}
-valued
random variables with
\mathbb{P}\!\left[X_{i}=1\right]=p_{i}
. Then
\mu=\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}
.

Case 1:
0<\mu\leq 1
.

Here
\mu/2\in(0,1/2]
, hence (since
X
is integer-valued)
\{X\geq\mu/2\}=\{X\geq 1\}
. Thus

\mathbb{P}\!\left[X\geq\mu/2\right]=\mathbb{P}\!\left[X\geq 1\right]=1-\mathbb{P}\!\left[X=0\right]=1-\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-p_{i})\geq 1-\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\right)=1-e^{-\mu}.

using
1-x\leq e^{-x}
for
x\in[0,1]
. For
\mu\in[0,1]
, it holds that
1-e^{-\mu}\geq\mu/2
therefore we get the lemma.

Case 2:
\mu\geq 1
.

It is a standard fact for Poisson binomial random variables
(i.e., sums of independent Bernoulli variables) that any median
m
of
X
satisfies
\lfloor\mu\rfloor\leq m\leq\lceil\mu\rceil
(see for example Theorem 3.2 and Corollary 3.1 in
[
20
]
). In particular,
m\geq\lfloor\mu\rfloor
. Since
\mu\geq 1
implies
\lfloor\mu\rfloor\geq\mu/2
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}\!\left[X\geq\mu/2\right]\;\geq\;\mathbb{P}\!\left[X\geq m\right]\;\geq\;1/2.

Combining the two cases, we conclude that

\mathbb{P}\!\left[X\geq\mu/2\right]\geq 0.5\cdot\min\{1,\mu\}.

∎
