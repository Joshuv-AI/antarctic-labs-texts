Enhancing cardiovascular disease risk communication in diabetes care using Kern’s six-step model: development and pilot of a training program for general practitioners
Authors: Panpan Feng, Hongyan Song, Kai Lin, Zhijie Xu, Xiaoning Han, Xiting Wang, Mi Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular diseases (CVDs) caused by diabetes pose a significant health burden. CVD risk communication refers to the process of conveying CVD risk information to the public or patients at the individual and community levels and encouraging them to adopt effective preventive measures. However, Chinese general practitioners (GPs) do not adequately communicate CVD risks to their patients. Training may enhance the risk communication skills of GPs. Therefore, this pilot study aims to develop, implement, and preliminarily evaluate a tailored training program to enhance the skills of GPs in communicating cardiovascular risks to people with diabetes.MethodsThis is a pilot study employing a mixed-methods approach for evaluation of an educational intervention.The curriculum was designed via a literature review and discussions with experts from general practice, endocrinology, and cardiology. Following Kern’s six-step teaching model, the training was delivered through a 4-session (160-min) training program. GPs from local community health service centers were invited to participate in the training program. Participants’ learning outcomes were assessed using quantitative methods, while qualitative assessments focused on their experiences and perceptions of the learning process.ResultsOne male GP and nine female GPs from 10 different community health service centers participated in the training. Post-training knowledge test scores (71.50 ± 10.55) were significantly higher than pre-training scores (47.50 ± 9.20), with a mean difference of 24.00 (95% CI: 16.31, 31.69; P < 0.001). Post-training qualitative interviews revealed enhanced participant attitudes, knowledge, and skills, with a positive impact on clinical practice. Participants suggested further refining communication elements, incorporating video demonstrations, and revising assessment materials to optimize the training program.ConclusionThe training program developed in this study preliminarily demonstrates feasibility, effectiveness, and wide acceptance and offers an innovative educational framework for type 2 diabetes management. Despite the inherent limitations of the pilot study, such as its limited sample size and single-center design, this work establishes a crucial foundation for subsequent curriculum refinement, expansion of the training scale, and evaluation of long-term outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12875-026-03392-5.Keywords: Cardiovascular risk communication; Kern six-step model; Diabetes care, general practitioners, training program

Introduction

The incidence of diabetes, a prevalent and expensive chronic illness worldwide, is expected to increase to 578 million affected individuals by 2030 [1]. In particular, China has a severe burden of diabetes and its cardiovascular complications; 33.9% of people with diabetes develop CVDs, and 75% of people with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) ultimately die from such conditions [2]. Compared with people without diabetes, those with diabetes have a two-to-four-fold greater risk of developing coronary heart disease, stroke, heart failure, atrial fibrillation, and peripheral artery disease [3, 4]. However, the current treatment rate (32.2% of people with diabetes receiving treatment) and control rate (49.2% of those treated achieving glycemic control) remain low in China [5–7], with about 5.6% of people with diabetes achieving target levels for the critical cardiovascular risk factors for blood glucose, blood pressure, and lipids [8]. This highlights the crucial need for enhanced diabetes management and comprehensive care.

There must be effective communication between healthcare providers and people with diabetes regarding cardiovascular risks for patient empowerment and self-management [9, 10]. The European Society of Cardiology 2024 guidelines highlight elevated cardiovascular risk in people with diabetes and emphasize the importance of early risk assessment, underscoring the need for effective risk communication to support patient engagement and self-management [11]. Such communication improves patient understanding and adherence to treatment, and promotes self-management, leading to better health outcomes [12]. It aids both parties, increasing their satisfaction, adherence, and health status while reducing healthcare costs and waste [13].

General practitioners (GPs) are critical in managing diabetes, particularly in conveying CVD risk. The communication skills of GPs, as the first point of contact in the healthcare system, significantly influence patients’ disease awareness, adherence to treatment, and self-management.A focus group study revealed that Chinese GPs face challenges in communicating with people with diabetes [14]. Significant knowledge and communication gaps exist: patients often lack a systematic understanding of cardiovascular risks, while GPs struggle to achieve effective risk communication due to time constraints and inadequate training [14, 15].This communication barrier, compounded by a lack of training, creates a vicious cycle that prevents healthcare providers from conveying risk information effectively, thereby compromising patients’ risk perception and self-management.

Therefore, this study is designed to increase primary care GPs’ capacity to communicate effectively about diabetes and CVD risk. This study aims to provide a set of efficient, assessable, and continuously improvable training materials via a curriculum development approach based on Kern’s six-step method and an evaluation model based on Kirkpatrick’s levels of training effectiveness [16, 17]. The Kern six-step model demonstrates distinct advantages in developing training courses for cardiovascular risk communication skills, as evidenced by three key comparisons: (1) Traditional didactic training approaches lack the structured needs assessment and outcome-driven design emphasized in the Kern model; (2) The systematic framework of analysis, design, development, implementation, and evaluation ensures that training content is both evidence-based and learner-centered. By integrating needs assessment, objective formulation, and outcome evaluation into a cohesive process, the Kern model is particularly suitable for cultivating communication skills that depend on contextual adaptation and sustained behavioral change. This study developed and assessed a training program grounded in the Kern six-step model to improve the cardiovascular risk communication skills of GPs in diabetes care. This pilot study focused on evaluating the program’s feasibility, acceptability, and early indicators of behavioral change.

Methods

Study eligibility, human ethics, and consent to participate

This is a pilot study using a mixed-methods design to develop and evaluate a tailored training curriculum. Through a convenience sampling approach, the 10 GPs recruited for this study were all participants of the sixth session (March-September 2024) of the General Practitioner Teacher Training Program in Xicheng District, Beijing. The sample size was determined based on the inclusion of the complete cohort from the 2024 training program. Since 2016, the General Practitioner Faculty Training Program in Xicheng District, Beijing, has cumulatively trained 148 GPs and has been supported by the Xicheng District Health Commission of Beijing [18]. Beijing is the capital of China, and Xicheng District is a center of education, culture, and technology. By the end of 2022, the population of Xicheng District was close to 1.1 million, and there were 99 community health service centers (general practice clinics) with about 400 GPs [19]. The eligibility criteria were as follows: (1) being a registered GP with a minimum of one year of clinical experience in the community; (2) having completed a standardized residency or transition training program in general practice; (3) actively managing patient care through family doctor contracts, including the management of people with diabetes; and (4) the capacity to engage in the training program fully. Participation was entirely voluntary; GPs received no financial incentives, although continuing medical education credits were provided. No participant withdrew during the training. All study participants gave their informed consent to participate in the research. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Research Ethics Board of the Health Service Center of Peking University First Hospital Ethics Committee (No. 2024 Research 277–002).

Research design

The study comprises two steps: (1) developing a training course to enhance the cardiovascular risk communication skills of GPs for people with diabetes and (2) implementing the course to assess its feasibility, acceptability, and preliminary outcomes. The curriculum was designed through a literature review and expert consultations involving specialists in general practice, endocrinology, and cardiology. A training course on cardiovascular risk communication for diabetes patients was developed based on Kern’s six-step teaching model (Fig. 1) [16]. In this study, GPs from the community health service center were invited. Training effectiveness, based on learning and reaction levels, was assessed using the Kirkpatrick model [17]. The study used quantitative methods to evaluate the participants’ learning outcomes and qualitative methods to assess their perceptions of their learning experience.

Overall curriculum design for diabetes cardiovascular risk communication training based on Kern’s six-step approach

Step 1: problem identification and general needs assessment

Diabetes and its cardiovascular complications impose severe burdens [20, 21]. Effective communication between GPs and people with diabetes regarding CVD risk is essential for encouraging those with diabetes to participate in the self-management of CVD risk reduction [22]. Our previous research revealed that GPs often encounter people with diabetes with a lack of knowledge and misconceptions about cardiovascular risks associated with diabetes in their clinical practice [15]. Community people with diabetes reported that communication with GPs was too brief, lacking communication about the risks of diabetes complications and CVDs, and that GPs did not pay enough attention to their concerns [23]. Moreover, insufficient communication is a significant reason for the low level of trust that people with diabetes in the community have toward GPs [24]. Therefore, the ideal approach is to conduct training to improve general practitioners’ cardiovascular risk communication skills, increase awareness of cardiovascular risks in people with diabetes, encourage them to participate in self-management of cardiovascular risks actively, and ultimately improve related health outcomes.

Step 2: targeted needs assessment

As China has made substantial investments in the infrastructure and training of GPs, diabetes management has shifted from secondary to primary care settings [25, 26], where GPs are responsible for the day-to-day management of most people with diabetes. Our previous research revealed that [15], in terms of attitude, GPs are willing to enhance their communication skills through training, recognizing the importance of effective communication for improving patient health outcomes. However, they lack knowledge and skills in communicating about cardiovascular risks associated with diabetes. In practice, GPs may not fully consider patients’ emotional and psychosocial issues during communication, indicating a need for further training to improve their communication performance [27]. They prefer interactive learning methods, such as role-playing, interactive teaching, and sharing clinical cases, to enhance their communication skills. However, there is a lack of communication skills training courses for GPs in China, especially in diabetes management [28]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop training programs tailored to the needs of GPs to improve their communication ability.

Step 3: goals and objectives

The goal was to improve the communication skills of GPs regarding cardiovascular risk in people with diabetes. The specific objectives of this training are to understand the current status of T2DM and its cardiovascular complications; to recognize the necessity and significance of communicating cardiovascular disease risks to patients; to learn how to apply the cardiovascular risk assessmen tool to explain risk assessments to patients; and to master risk communication skills for application in clinical practice to improve the care of people with diabetes.

Step 4: educational strategies

This educational program was developed through an iterative process combining expert panel discussions (n = 5, including general practice, endocrinology, and cardiology specialists) and pilot testing with three general practitioners not involved in the main study. The core educational strategy is based on Kern’s six-step curriculum development model, ensuring a systematic approach from problem identification to evaluation. This study selected the “Prediction for Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease Risk in China” (China-PAR)model as the core communication aid, given its high authority and clinical recognition within the Chinese healthcare system, which facilitates rapid comprehension and adoption by general practitioners (GPs). China-PAR is a standardized risk assessment algorithm developed specifically for the Chinese population, functionally analogous to the Framingham Risk Calculator used in the United States. Its reliability is established on pooled data from four large-scale prospective Chinese cohorts (including InterASIA and China MUCA), covering 15 provinces and municipalities, over 40 collaborative sites, with a total sample size of approximately 127,000 participants and a maximum follow-up duration exceeding 23 years [29].The tool supports comprehensive cardiovascular risk assessment, providing both 10-year and lifetime risk estimates. As a publicly available and validated model, it is accessible via the official website (http://www.cvdrisk.com.cn). A screenshot of the tool interface, including the main input variables, is provided in Appendix 1 for reference. During the study design phase, we integrated the standard process of cardiovascular disease risk communication with the core content of this training. Through a literature review and expert discussions, we developed a cardiovascular risk communication feedback tool comprising 4 dimensions and 12 specific items. During the training’s skills simulation practice session, three instructors used this form of feedback to provide structured, item-by-item feedback immediately after each group’s role-play demonstration, while simultaneously documenting performance. This feedback tool was intended primarily to facilitate participants’ immediate reflection and improvement during the learning process rather than serve as a quantitative evaluation instrument for study outcomes. The detailed feedback records for each group have been compiled and included in Appendix 2 for further reference.

Step 5: implementation

The training was implemented by a professional training team from Peking University First Hospital. To meet the training requirements, the project invited three certified general practitioner training instructors, all of whom held qualifications in general practice education and had more than three years of teaching experience. These instructors not only possessed a deep understanding of diabetes and cardiovascular risk communication but also had extensive practical clinical experience. Their expertise in communication and teaching skills ensured the efficient transmission of relevant knowledge.

Before the pilot project commenced, to ensure teaching consistency and standardized implementation, all three instructors participated in a dedicated 2-h standardization training session led by the principal investigator. The content of this training included providing a detailed explanation of the course objectives and content, practicing the use of core communication elements for providing feedback, and standardizing operational methods for role-play debriefing sessions.

The training was conducted on June 2024 at Peking University First Hospital. It consisted of four structured components: pre-training preparation, core course delivery, post-training evaluation, and post-training qualitative interviews. To ensure the fidelity of intervention implementation and facilitate process evaluation, we implemented several quality assurance measures: (1) all the teaching materials (handouts, slides, case studies) were standardized; (2) the entire training process (with participant consent) was video-recorded for fidelity monitoring; and (3) immediately after the training, a brief debriefing meeting was held with the three instructors, and the research led to deviations during implementation and the collection of immediate feedback on the course flow and participant engagement.

During the skills practice component, participants were divided into five small groups for role-play exercises. The instructors rotated among the groups for guidance, maintaining an instructor-to-student ratio of about 1:3–4. This arrangement ensured that each participant received personalized observation and actionable feedback, thereby enhancing the learning outcomes. Step 6: evaluation and assessment

The quality of the medical education program and its ongoing refinement were assessed via the Kirkpatrick model [18]. This model is documented for its comprehensiveness, empirical support, adaptability, and decision-support capabilities. The assessment process comprises two primary components: reaction and learning level evaluations. Before the training, a questionnaire was used to assess participants’ understanding of diabetes risk communication and their past use of it (Appendix 3). A written examination was also used to assess their understanding of key theories and concepts (Appendix 4). At the learning level, upon completing the training, each participant is required to take a written test designed to assess improvement in theoretical knowledge. The participants’ genuine learning experiences at the reaction level are also considered important aspects reflecting the quality and effectiveness of the training program. Therefore, an anonymous survey was conducted to collect feedback on the course content, teaching methods, material quality, instructor performance, course organization, and overall experience (Appendix 5).

Following the course, qualitative research with 10 GPs who completed the training was conducted. To improve cardiovascular risk communication training for individuals with diabetes, focus group interviews were used to gather their experiences and suggestions. Two interviewers aided and documented the process, with one leading the discussion and the other assisting with recording and asking supplementary questions. The integrity and accuracy of the data were ensured by recording the interviews and transcribing them verbatim. The transcripts were reviewed and cross-checked by researchers. The participants were informed regarding the recording and assured of confidentiality; the data were anonymized, and all the participants provided informed consent for the study.

Data analysis

SPSS 21.0 software was used to analyze the data. Quantitative data are expressed as the means (x̄±s). Within-group comparisons (pre- and post-training scores) were analyzed using paired t tests, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. For the qualitative data, given the study’s focused scope and homogeneous sample, the Framework Method was selected for thematic analysis [30]. This approach provides a structured yet flexible way to organize and interpret qualitative data while maintaining contextual depth systematically. The total interview duration was 60 min, and transcription was completed within 24 h after the interviews. The interview guide was developed based on the research objectives, incorporating open-ended questions designed to explore participants’ overall experience, perceived changes in knowledge and skills, applicability of the training to practice, and suggestions for improvement. The preliminary guide was reviewed and refined by the research team, which includes experts in general practice and medical education. The complete interview guide is provided in Appendix 6. Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim, and two researchers (FPP and SHY) simultaneously checked the transcripts to correct any errors. The transcripts were subsequently returned to the participants for feedback to verify accuracy. The three members of the research team—FPP, SHY (with a master’s degree in general practice), and YM(with a doctoral degree in general practice)—all possess extensive experience in qualitative research. After the transcripts were imported into NVivo12 software, two researchers conducted independent open coding. Thematic analysis was performed using the framework method, with continuous iterative analysis informing subsequent data collection. During the initial stage of thematic analysis, the two researchers independently read the transcripts and field notes and conducted open coding of the data. Initial codes were generated by treating keywords and phrases as units of analysis. Meaningful units of text were extracted, and through discussion, they were summarized into contextual descriptions. In cases of disagreement, a third researcher (YM) was introduced as an arbitrator. The three researchers (FPP, SHY, and YM) engaged in in-depth discussions, jointly reviewing the transcripts and understanding the rationale behind each coding decision. The relevant sections of the interview transcripts were then revisited, and coding decisions were re-evaluated based on new insights gained from the discussions. Through this iterative process, all the perspectives were comprehensively considered, ultimately resulting in coding outcomes that were robustly supported by the data. Coding discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus, establishing a preliminary thematic framework. Sub-themes were derived by reviewing the matrix and linking codes within the dataset. Finally, core themes were identified from the sub-themes through inductive reasoning. The findings were shared with all the focus group participants for verification, and the content of their discussions was accurately reflected.

Results

A total of 10 GPs participated in the training. Six trainees, aged 31–40 years, had worked in primary care settings for more than 6 years. Seven trainees had tried communicating about the cardiovascular risk of diabetes; however, only two had used cardiovascular risk communication tools. Written test

The quantitative assessment outcomes were assembled, encompassing all participants’ pre- and post-training written test scores and their ability to evaluate cardiovascular risk communication. A total of 10 primary care physicians from community settings completed the assessments. In this pilot study, paired-samples t-test results indicated a statistically significant increase in participants’ knowledge test scores upon completion of diabetes cardiovascular risk communication training. The post-test mean score (71.50 ± 10.55) was significantly greater than the pre-test mean score (47.50 ± 9.20), resulting in a mean increase of 24.00 points (95% CI [16.31, 31.69]), t(9) = 7.06, p < 0.001. A large effect size was preliminarily calculated (Cohen’s d = 2.233, 95% CI [1.029, 3.405]).

The questionnaire of trainees’ experience for all participants

An anonymous survey was conducted post-training to assess the satisfaction and perceptions of the GPs who participated in the course. The training evaluation questionnaire was adapted from existing training assessment tools [31]. The survey sought feedback on various aspects, including content, practicality, comprehension, interactive communication, the training environment, case studies, engagement, and overall effectiveness. All 10 trainees completed the survey, and 90% provided affirmative responses to all questions. However, one participant remained neutral about the course’s ability to guide clinical practice in diabetes cardiovascular risk communication. The study results suggest that training grounded in Kern’s six-step approach offers an enhanced learning experience with satisfactory outcomes.

Themes

Post-training, qualitative research methods were applied to conduct focus group interviews with 10 participants. The interviews focused on their experiences and suggestions for the course. The following three overarching themes emerged from the feedback: course training experience; the impact of course training on cognition, skills, and practice; and course feedback. The course format was considered innovative, and its content was well aligned with the needs of GPs in clinical practice. The course benefits GPs by improving their understanding and skills in communicating cardiovascular risks and can substantially increase their communication proficiency through practical exercises. The role-play and communication scale-based teaching methods are considered highly effective by the GPs. Enhancements such as refining the communication scale content, incorporating more standard video demonstrations, and revising the written test materials to improve the course further are recommended. Discussion

This study developed a training framework for GPs in China based on Kern’s Six-Step Model, aiming to enhance their ability to communicate cardiovascular risk in the management of type 2 diabetes. This training provides innovative strategies and practical guidance for preventing diabetic cardiovascular disease. The program utilized a combination of teaching methods, including case analysis, collaborative learning, role-playing, peer assessment, and instructor feedback. The training effectiveness was evaluated using the Kirkpatrick Model, with a focus on participants’ reactions and knowledge acquisition. The results demonstrated a significant improvement in the ability of the GPs to convey cardiovascular risk, as evidenced by higher post-training test scores and positive feedback from the participants. In post-training qualitative interviews, the GPs highly commended the innovative design of the course and its alignment with the needs of clinical practice. They reported that the training significantly enhanced their communication skills, particularly emphasizing the importance of practical exercises and role-playing. Additionally, they suggested further refining the course by optimizing communication and feedback tools, incorporating more instructional videos, and updating assessment materials.

Role-playing, as an experiential teaching method, is crucial for skill development, as it enables participants to practice communication skills in simulated scenarios [32] effectively. Role-playing training that integrates peer assessment and instructor feedback facilitates the rapid translation of theoretical knowledge and skill enhancement [33, 34]. Post-training interviews and survey results confirm that this interactive teaching approach is innovative and effective, significantly improving learners’ motivation and engagement, thereby validating the course design and training methods. Additionally, role-playing provides a safe practice environment, allowing physicians to practice and reflect on communication behaviors without exposing real patients to risks [35]. This not only deepens their understanding of disease knowledge but also enhances empathy through emotional resonance, which is vital for establishing patient-centered communication. This method effectively stimulates intrinsic learning motivation and increases engagement [36]. In cardiovascular disease risk communication training, simulating scenarios of doctor-patient discussions about risk assessment and management strategies has unique educational value. Furthermore, peer assessment and instructor feedback enable immediate and specific performance evaluations, clarifying areas for improvement and accelerating skill enhancement [37–39]. In summary, role-playing offers multiple benefits in cardiovascular disease risk communication training: it provides physicians with a platform for practice and reflection, promotes the development and application of communication skills through multifaceted feedback mechanisms, and offers insights for continuous optimization of training programs. During implementation, ensuring that teaching activities are closely aligned with learners’ needs and educational objectives is essential.

Based on participant feedback, the training program has demonstrated potential for further refinement. Specific suggestions from learners offer valuable practical evidence for curriculum improvement, including streamlining items in the communication feedback tool, simplifying communication procedures to enhance clinical applicability, supplementing instructional content on communication styles, and developing pocket cards for clinical use. In terms of instructional format, participants recommended adding standardized demonstration videos of risk communication and appropriately extending the duration of group presentations and feedback sessions. With respect to the assessment component, learners suggested improving the consistency between written examination items and course content and introducing standardized patients into skills assessment to more objectively and realistically evaluate communication competence. This feedback provides valuable guidance for revising and improving the training program in the next stage. Accordingly, in combination with the program implementation process and qualitative findings, this study proposes the following systematic optimization strategies. First, the curriculum content is optimized according to adult learning principles to ensure that teaching materials are practical, targeted, and consistent with learners’ clinical experience and actual needs [40]. By focusing on core skills in cardiovascular risk communication, streamlining non-core modules, supplementing instruction on communication styles, and developing portable clinical tools, the program’s clinical operability and application value can be significantly enhanced [41]. Second, the teaching model will be innovated by introducing standardized video demonstrations to achieve audio-visual instruction, which not only accommodates the diverse learning preferences of adult learners but also fully embodies the educational advantages of Dale’s Cone of Experience [42]. Standardized videos can provide learners with standardized references, enabling them to observe, imitate and practice in a low-risk environment. Moreover, appropriately extending the time for group interaction and feedback further strengthens learning engagement and skill transformation [43, 44]. Third, an assessment system highly consistent with curriculum objectives will be established, reflecting the core elements of self-direction and timely feedback in Knowles’s adult learning theory [45]. The optimized assessment tools will be closely aligned with the instructional content, and standardized patient assessment will be adopted to enhance authenticity and objectivity. Feedback and self-assessment mechanisms will be strengthened to help learners identify strengths and areas for improvement. Throughout the curriculum design, the characteristics of adult learners—including strong autonomy and extensive practical experience—will be fully respected [40], promoting the organic integration of learning theories and teaching practices [46]. Through the above systematic improvements, training programs will become more targeted, feasible and effective, thereby better meeting the learning and clinical practice needs of general practitioners in diabetes and cardiovascular risk communication.

Although this pilot study demonstrated good feasibility, a critical challenge for the implementation of this program in real-world clinical settings remains how to sustain behavioral changes among healthcare professionals within time-constrained clinical environments. The long-term effectiveness of a single intensive training session is often limited. To further bridge the gap between training and clinical practice, the research team is developing a digital clinical support tool that integrates cardiovascular risk assessment models (such as the China Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease Risk Prediction Model, China-PAR) with standardized communication prompts. The tool aims to provide real-time guidance during patient consultations, reinforce the communication skills learned in training, and reduce the cognitive burden on clinicians regarding clinical decision-making and communication recall. In the future, we plan to conduct a randomized controlled trial that will incorporate the optimized training curriculum and follow the Kirkpatrick Training Evaluation Model to design the assessment framework.The evaluation will not only assess participants’ immediate feedback and knowledge acquisition (Levels 1–2) but also track the long-term impact of the training on clinical practice behaviors (Level 3) while laying the groundwork for exploring potential benefits for patient outcomes (Level 4). This will help precisely address the question of the long-term effectiveness of this intervention in real-world primary care settings [47].

Limitations

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to illustrate the development and implementation experience of a diabetes cardiovascular risk communication training course in China. This study has several limitations. First, as it employed a single-arm pre/post pilot design without a control group, the observed score increase, while a positive signal, cannot be definitively attributed to the intervention; its causal association requires further validation in future studies utilizing controlled designs (e.g., randomized controlled trials). The absence of a control group also limits the causal interpretation of the observed improvements, as the positive changes may be partially attributable to time effects or Hawthorne effects rather than the intervention itself. Second, the small sample size (n = 10) limits the statistical power of the quantitative analysis, and the participants were predominantly female and from a single geographic region. This may limit the representativeness of the sample, and the findings may not be directly generalizable to populations of general practitioners with a higher proportion of male practitioners or to those working in different regions, healthcare resource settings, or cultural contexts. Additionally, the use of a single convenience sampling method introduces potential selection bias. In this pilot feasibility study, although the observed large effect size suggests a meaningful change, future trials with larger sample sizes are still needed to confirm these preliminary findings with adequate statistical power. Future research should adopt a cluster randomized controlled trial (RCT) design to systematically address these potential confounding factors.However, there are certain advantages in smaller groups, including the ease of recruiting GPs, organizing and enabling, and less fragmentation of discussions, compared with larger groups. As a preliminary trial, it authenticates the effectiveness and feasibility of the study, providing valuable feedback and suggestions for improvement for subsequent research. In the future, to further augment the training experience and effectiveness, the course will be optimized based on the study results, integrating technological aids such as video demonstrations and visual communication tools.

Conclusion

A training program designed for Chinese GPs has been developed and piloted, demonstrating the potential to enhance their ability to communicate cardiovascular risks with people with diabetes. Role-playing and interactive learning have been implemented as effective instructional methods. Qualitative feedback has identified key directions for curriculum optimization, including the integration of video demonstrations and the refinement of assessment scales to support continuous improvement. Although the pilot study has inherent limitations, this research establishes a foundational, assessable, and continuously improvable curriculum framework for GPs. It provides essential preliminary groundwork and valuable insights for designing more rigorous large-scale trials to systematically evaluate the program’s impact on clinical practice and patient health.
