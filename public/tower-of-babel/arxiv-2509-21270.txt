Rings of non-commutative functions and their fields of fractions
Méric L. Augat, Robert T.W. Martin, Eli Shamovich

Abstract
Semi-free ideal rings, or semifirs , were introduced by Paul M. Cohn to study universal localizations in the non-commutative setting. We provide new examples of semifirs consisting of analytic functions in several non-commuting variables. These examples arise canonically in free analysis by completing the free algebra in the topology of “uniform convergence on operator-space balls” in the non-commutative universe of tuples of square matrices of any finite size. We show, in particular, that the ring of (uniformly) entire non-commutative (NC) functions in d ∈ ℕ d\in\mathbb{N} non-commuting variables, 𝒪 d \mathscr{O}_{d} , is a semifir. Every finitely–generated right (or left) ideal in 𝒪 d \mathscr{O}_{d} is closed, which yields an analytic extension of G. Bergman’s nullstellensatz for the free algebra. Any semifir admits a universal skew field of fractions; applying this to 𝒪 d \mathscr{O}_{d} yields the universal skew field of “NC meromorphic expressions”, ℳ d \mathscr{M}_{d} . We show that any f ∈ ℳ d f\in\mathscr{M}_{d} has a well-defined domain and evaluations in a large class of stably-finite topological algebras, including finite C ∗ C^{*} -algebras, extending a result of Cohn for NC rational functions. As an application, we extend the almost sure convergence result of Haagerup and Thorbjørnsen for free polynomials evaluated on tuples of random matrices to the setting of NC meromorphic expressions.



1
Introduction

This paper studies algebras of non-commutative functions and their ring-theoretic properties. The theory of non-commutative functions lies in the intersection of free analysis and pure algebra. This is exemplified in this paper in which we employ analytic techniques to prove algebraic results. Namely, we apply operator algebraic and functional analytic technologies, in particular the theory of non-commutative Hardy spaces of NC analytic functions, to prove that certain rings of non-commutative analytic functions presented as power series in several NC variables are semi-free ideal rings, a class of rings that admit universal localizations. We apply this to extend a result of Haagerup and Thorbjørnsen in random matrix and free probability theory.

2.1
Non-commutative functions

The complex

There are two natural operations on the NC universe (that can also be viewed through the lens of representation theory). We have the

: For

A non-commutative (NC) set is any subset

is an NC set, an

•

•

It is not hard to check that every free polynomial,

One can topologize

S(r)=\left\{\left.\sum_{\alpha}a_{\alpha}\mathfrak{z}^{\alpha}\right|\text{for all }1\leq j\leq d\text{ and }0<s_{j}<r_{j},\,\sum_{\alpha}|a_{\alpha}|s^{\alpha}<\infty\right\}.

We write for a word
\alpha
in the alphabet
\{1,\ldots,d\}
,
\mathfrak{z}^{\alpha}=\mathfrak{z}_{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\mathfrak{z}_{\alpha_{n}}
with the obvious evaluation on numbers and matrices. In this paper, we will show that in many regards the different mode of convergence introduced by Popescu
[
41
]
is more amenable to analysis than the one proposed by Taylor (see also
[
29
]
and
[
25
]
).

2.2
Bounded NC functions

The full Fock space over
\mathbb{C}^{d}
is the Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}=\bigoplus_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{C}^{d}\right)^{\otimes n}
. Equivalently,
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}
is the completion of the free algebra with respect to the inner product that makes the monomials an orthonormal basis.
That is, every element of
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}
can be viewed as a free formal power series with square–summable coefficients and hence
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}
is a natural non-commutative and multivariate extension of the classical Hardy space,
H^{2}
, of square–summable Taylor series at
0
. (Such power series necessarily have radius of convergence at least
1
and hence define analytic functions in the complex unit disk.)

The full Fock space is a
non-commutative reproducing kernel Hilbert space
(NC-RKHS) in the NC unit row-ball in the sense of Ball, Marx, and Vinnikov
[
7
]
. Namely, for any
Z\in\mathbb{B}^{(n\times n)\cdot d}
, the linear map
f\in\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\,\mapsto\,f(Z)\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
is a bounded linear map. The NC reproducing kernel of
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}
is a function from
\mathbb{B}^{(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N})\cdot d}\times\mathbb{B}^{(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N})\cdot d}
into completely bounded linear maps and is called the
NC Szegö kernel
: For any
Z\in\mathbb{B}^{(m\times m)\cdot d}
,
W\in\mathbb{B}^{(n\times n)\cdot d}
and
T\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
,

K(Z,W)(T):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{|\alpha|=n}Z^{\alpha}TW^{\alpha*}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n};\quad\quad\alpha\in\mathbb{W}_{d}.

We will write for every
Z\in\mathbb{B}^{(n\times n)\cdot d}
and every
y,v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
K\{Z,y,v\}
for the element of
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}
that satisfies for every
f\in\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}
,

\langle{K\{Z,y,v\}},{f}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}}=\langle{y},{f(Z)v}\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}=:y^{*}f(Z)v.

It is not hard to check that

K\{Z,y,v\}(\mathfrak{z})=\sum_{\omega\in\mathbb{W}_{d}}\overline{y^{*}Z^{\omega}v}\,\mathfrak{z}^{\omega}.

The vectors
K\{Z,y,v\}
are called
NC Szegö kernel vectors
, or
NC point evaluation vectors
. The left creation operators,
L_{i}\in\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d})
,
1\leq i\leq d
are the operators of left multiplication by the
d
independent variables,
L_{i}=M^{L}_{\mathfrak{z}_{i}}
. Similarly,
R_{i}
are the operators of right multiplication by the variables. The row
L=(L_{1},\cdots,L_{d})
is a
row isometry
,
i.e.
an isometry from
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d}
into
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}
, which further implies that each
L_{i}
is an isometry on
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}
so that the
L_{i}
have pairwise orthogonal ranges. The orthogonal complement of the range of
L
is spanned by the unit vector,
1
, also called the
vacuum vector
. As was observed by Bunce
[
8
]
, Frazho
[
16
]
, and Popescu
[
35
,
37
]
,
L
is a non-commutative analogue of the shift on the classical Hardy space of the disk and we will refer to the
L_{i}
as the
left free shifts
. Similarly, the
right free shift
,
R=(R_{1},\cdots,R_{d})
is a row isometry that is unitarily equivalent to
L
via the unitary transpose involution that sends the standard basis vector,
\mathfrak{z}^{\alpha}
, to
\mathfrak{z}^{\alpha^{\mathrm{t}}}
, where if
\alpha=i_{1}\cdots i_{n}\in\mathbb{W}_{d}
, then
\alpha^{\mathrm{t}}:=i_{n}\cdots i_{1}
.

The non-commutative Hardy algebra,
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}_{d}
, is the algebra of all uniformly bounded NC functions in the NC unit row-ball,
\mathbb{B}^{(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N})\cdot d}
. This algebra can be identified with the unital weak operator topology (WOT)–closed algebra generated by the
L_{1},\ldots,L_{d}

[
13
]
. Moreover,
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}_{d}
can be identified with the algebra of left multipliers of the NC-RKHS
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}

[
41
,
43
]
.

Fix
0<r<1
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
Z\in r\cdot\overline{\mathbb{B}^{(n\times n)\cdot d}}
, and unit vectors
v,y\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Write
\mathrm{Ad}_{Z}\colon\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
for the completely positive adjunction map,
\mathrm{Ad}_{Z}(T):=\sum_{j=1}^{d}Z_{j}TZ_{j}^{*}
. By our assumption,

\|\mathrm{Ad}_{Z}\|=\|\mathrm{Ad}_{Z}(I_{n})\|\leq r^{2}
. Then,

\displaystyle\|K\{Z,y,v\}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{|\alpha|=n}\left|y^{*}Z^{\alpha}v\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}y^{*}\mathrm{Ad}_{Z}^{\circ n}(vv^{*})y

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|y\|^{2}\|v\|^{2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}r^{2n}=\frac{\|y\|^{2}\|v\|^{2}}{1-r^{2}}.

This simple fact can be used to prove the following lemma,
[
24
, Lemma 4.5]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Any
f\in\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}
is bounded on
r\cdot\overline{\mathbb{B}^{(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N})\cdot d}}
for all
0<r<1
.

Proof.

Given any
Z\in r\cdot\overline{\mathbb{B}^{(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N})\cdot d}}
,

\displaystyle\|f(Z)\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup\left\{\left.|y^{*}f(Z)v|\ \right|\ \|v\|,\,\|y\|=1\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup\left\{\left.\langle{K\{Z,y,v\}},{f}\rangle\right|\ \|v\|,\,\|y\|=1\right\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\|f\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}}}{\sqrt{1-r^{2}}}.

∎

For any
f\in\mathbb{H}^{\infty}_{d}
we define
f(L):=M^{L}_{f}\in\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d})
as the bounded linear operator that acts as left multiplication by
f
. We note that for every
0<r<1
the operator
rL
is a strict row contraction. Hence, the bounded linear operator,
f(rL)
, is well-defined. The operator
f(rL)
corresponds to the restriction of
f
to
r\mathbb{B}^{(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N})\cdot d}
. More generally, this identification extends to
f\in\mathbb{H}^{\infty}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n\times k}
, for all sizes of matrices.

We will say that a function
f\in\mathbb{H}^{\infty}_{d}
and, more generally,
f\in\mathbb{H}^{\infty}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n\times k}
is
inner
, if the operator
f(L)
is an isometry. Similarly, we say that
f
is
outer
if
f(L)
has dense range. Every element
f\in\mathbb{H}^{\infty}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{k\times n}
admits an
inner–outer factorization
,
f=V\cdot T
, where
V
is inner and
T
is outer. This follows from the non-commutative extension of the classical Beurling theorem that was first discovered by Popescu in
[
34
,
36
]
and later proved independently by Davidson and Pitts in
[
13
]
. The proof of this NC Beurling theorem relies on the notion of wandering subspaces for row isometries. Namely, let
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a closed right shift-invariant subspace. Then, the
wandering subspace
of
\mathcal{H}
is defined as the orthogonal complement of the range of
R
restricted to
\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d}
,

\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{H}}:=\mathcal{H}\ominus R(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d}).

Elements of
\mathscr{W}_{\mathcal{H}}
are called
wandering vectors
. We will require several technical lemmas on inner and outer NC functions in the sequel.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
F\in\mathbb{H}^{\infty}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{k\times n}
be outer. Then, for every
0<r<1
,
F(rL)
is surjective. Moreover, there exists
H_{r}\in\mathbb{H}^{\infty}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n\times k}
such that
F(rL)H_{r}(L)=I_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}}\otimes I_{k}
.

Proof.

F(rL)
will be surjective if and only if
F(rL)^{*}
is bounded below. We first claim that
F(rL)^{*}
is bounded below if
F(Z)^{*}
is uniformly bounded below for all
Z\in r\cdot\mathbb{B}^{(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N})\cdot d}
.

First, since
F
is outer, we note that
F(Z):\mathbb{C}^{m}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}\twoheadrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{m}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{k}
is surjective onto
\mathbb{C}^{m}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{k}
for all
Z\in\mathbb{C}^{(m\times m)\times d}
. Indeed, if there exists a
y\in\mathbb{C}^{m}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{k}
and
Z\in\mathbb{C}^{(m\times m)\times d}
so that
y^{*}F(Z)=0
, then
K\{Z,y,v\}\in\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{k}
is orthogonal to
\mathrm{Ran}\,F(L)
for any
v\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
, a contradiction.

Suppose that
F(rL)^{*}
is not bounded below. Then, given any
\epsilon>0
there exists an
x\in\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{k}
so that
\|F(rL)^{*}x\|<\frac{\epsilon}{2}\|x\|
. Since
F(rL)\in\mathbb{A}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n\times k}
, it can be approximated in operator norm by its Cesàro sums. Hence there exists an
N\in\mathbb{N}
so that
\ell>N
implies that
\|F_{\ell}(rL)^{*}-F(rL)^{*}\|<\frac{\epsilon}{2}
, where
F_{\ell}(rL)
denotes the
\ell
-th Cesàro sum. Finally, we can approximate the “infinite–dimensional point”,
rL
, by finite–dimensional compressions,
Z^{(j)}\in r\cdot\mathbb{B}^{(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N})\cdot d}
, of size
j
, by compressing
rL
by the projection,
P_{j}
onto the first
j
standard basis vectors of
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}
. Then, since
F_{\ell}\in\mathbb{C}\langle\mathfrak{z}\rangle\otimes\mathbb{C}^{k\times n}
,
F_{\ell}(Z^{(j)})^{*}
converges, in the strong operator topology, to
F_{\ell}(rL)^{*}
. Hence, there is a
J\in\mathbb{N}
so that
j>J
implies that

\|F_{\ell}(Z^{(j)})^{*}P_{j}x-F(rL)^{*}x\|=\|(F_{\ell}(Z^{(j)})^{*}-F(rL)^{*})x\|<\epsilon\|x\|.

Since
P_{j}x\rightarrow x
this proves that
F(Z^{(j)})^{*}
is not uniformly bounded below, as
j\rightarrow\infty
. Hence
F(Z)^{*}
is not uniformly bounded below in
r\cdot\mathbb{B}^{(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N})\cdot d}
. We conclude that if
F(Z)^{*}
is uniformly bounded below in
r\cdot\mathbb{B}^{(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N})\cdot d}
, then
F(rL)^{*}
is bounded below, and hence
F(rL)
is surjective.

We now show that
F(Z)^{*}
is uniformly bounded below in
r\cdot\mathbb{B}^{(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N})\cdot d}
. If not, then there exist points
W^{(\ell)}
of size
m_{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}
in the row-ball of radius
r
and unit norm vectors
u^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{C}^{m_{\ell}}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{k}
so that
\|F(W^{(\ell)})^{*}u^{(\ell)}\|\rightarrow 0
.

Given any
h\in\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{k}
,
y=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}y_{1}\\
\vdots\\
y_{i}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathbb{C}^{m}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{k}
,
y_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
, and
v\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
, we define
K\{W,y,v\}\in\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{k}
by

\langle{K\{W,y,v\}},{h}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{k}}:=y^{*}h(W)v=(y_{1}^{*},\cdots,y_{k}^{*})\left(\begin{smallmatrix}h_{1}(W)v\\
\vdots\\
h_{k}(W)v\end{smallmatrix}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}y_{i}^{*}h_{i}(W)v.

Note that if
y=\sum_{i=1}^{k}y_{i}\otimes e_{i}
, that

K\{W,y,v\}=\begin{pmatrix}K\{W,y_{1},v\}\\
K\{W,y_{2},v\}\\
\vdots\\
K\{W,y_{k},v\}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{k}.

For any
1\leq i\leq k
, consider the sequence of vectors

h_{\ell}^{(i)}:=K\{W^{(\ell)},u^{(\ell)},u^{(\ell)}_{i}\}.

Here, we write
u^{(\ell)}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}u^{(\ell)}_{i}\otimes e_{i}
.

Then, it is clear that
K\{W^{(\ell)},u^{(\ell)},u^{(\ell)}_{i}\}
is a uniformly norm-bounded sequence in
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{k}
for each
i
since each
u^{(\ell)}
is a unit vector and
W^{(\ell)}\in r\cdot\mathbb{B}^{(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N})\cdot d}
. It follows that there is a weakly convergent subsequence
h_{\ell_{j}}^{(i)}:=K\{W^{(\ell_{j})},u^{(\ell_{j})},u^{(\ell_{j})}_{i}\}
, with a weak limit,
h^{(i)}\in\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{k}
. Note that

\hat{h}_{\emptyset}^{(i)}=\lim_{j}\begin{pmatrix}u^{(\ell_{j})*}_{1}u^{(\ell_{j})}_{i}\\
\vdots\\
u^{(\ell_{j})*}_{k}u^{(\ell_{j})}_{i}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{k},

so that

\|\hat{h}_{\emptyset}^{(i)}\|_{\mathbb{C}^{k}}\geq\lim_{j}u^{(\ell_{j})*}_{i}u^{(\ell_{j})}_{i}=\lim\|u^{(\ell_{j})}_{i}\|^{2}.

If
\hat{h}_{\emptyset}^{(i)}=0
for every
1\leq i\leq k
, this would imply that

0=\lim_{j}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\|u^{(\ell_{j})}_{i}\|^{2}=\lim_{j}\|u^{(\ell_{j})}\|^{2}=1,

since each
u^{(\ell_{j})}
is a unit vector. Hence at least one of the
h^{(i)}\neq 0
.

However, for any
x\in\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{k}
, and each
1\leq i\leq k
,

\displaystyle\langle{h^{(i)}},{F(L)x}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{j}u^{(\ell_{j})*}F(W)x(W)u^{(\ell_{j})}_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(F(W)^{*}u^{(\ell_{j})}\right)^{*}x(W)u^{(\ell_{j})}_{i}\rightarrow 0.

This proves that
F(L)
does not have a dense range, contradicting that
F
is outer.

The last part of the lemma follows from Popescu’s corona theorem
[
36
, Corollary 3.2]
. Since
T(rL)
is surjective,
T(rL)^{*}
is bounded below, and thus the corona theorem guarantees the existence of a right inverse.
∎

We note that if
f
is an NC entire function then the operator
f(rL)
makes sense for every
r>0
. Moreover, since the power series of
f
converges uniformly on any ball, we have
f(rL)\in\mathbb{A}_{d}
. Here,
\mathbb{A}_{d}
, the
NC
or
free disk algebra
of Popescu is the norm closure of the free algebra in
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}_{d}
. Equivalently,
\mathbb{A}_{d}
is completely isometrically isomorphic to the unital norm closed algebra generated by the left creation operators in
\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d})
. We will typically view elements of
\mathbb{A}_{d}
and
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}_{d}
as left multiplication operators on
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}
and given any
F\in\mathbb{H}^{\infty}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
we will sometimes write
F(L)
in place of
F
, to denote that
F
acts as left multiplication by
F
. Indeed, evaluating the free formal power series of
F
at
L=(L_{1},\cdots,L_{d})
yields the operator of left multiplication by
F
. (If
F\in\mathbb{A}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
the partial Cesàro sums of this power series converge in operator-norm while if
F\in\mathbb{H}^{\infty}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
these Cesàro sums and their adjoints converge in the strong operator topology.)

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
F\in\mathbb{H}^{\infty}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
. Then the wandering dimension of
\mathrm{Ran}\,F(L)^{-\|\cdot\|}
is at most
n
.

Proof.

The wandering space of
\mathscr{R}(F):=\mathrm{Ran}\,F(L)^{-\|\cdot\|}
is
\mathscr{W}(F):=\mathscr{R}(F)\ominus R\otimes I_{n}(\mathscr{R}(F)\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d})
, and the wandering dimension of
\mathscr{R}(F)
is the dimension of
\mathscr{W}(F)
. It is clear that the set
\{F(L)1\otimes e_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}\subseteq\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
is a cyclic set for the restricted row isometry
R\otimes I_{n}|_{\mathscr{R}(F)\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d}}
. We claim that if
P
is the orthogonal projection of
\mathscr{R}(F)
onto its wandering subspace, that the set of all
x_{j}:=P(F(L)1\otimes e_{j})
,
1\leq j\leq n
spans
\mathscr{W}(F)
. If not, suppose that there is an
x\in\mathscr{W}(F)
so that
x\perp\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
. Then, since
x\in\mathscr{W}(F)
, we have that

x\perp R^{\omega}\otimes I_{n}x_{j},\quad\quad\forall\ \omega\neq\emptyset,

and
1\leq j\leq n
, since
R^{\omega}\otimes I_{n}x_{j}\in\mathscr{R}(F)\ominus\mathscr{W}(F)
and
x\in\mathscr{W}(F)
. Moreover,
x\perp x_{j}
,
1\leq j\leq n
, by assumption. Hence,

0=\langle{R^{\omega}\otimes I_{n}x_{j}},{x}\rangle_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}},\quad\quad\forall\ \omega\in\mathbb{W}_{d}.

Since
\{x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
is an
R-
cyclic set, this implies that
x=0
, proving the claim.
∎

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\in\mathscr{O}_{d}(R)
,
R\in(0,+\infty]
. Let
F=(f_{1},\cdots,f_{n})
. For every
0<r<R
, let
F(rL)=V_{r}(L)T_{r}(L)
be the inner-outer decomposition. Then, there exists
0<\rho<R
and
k\leq n
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, such that for every
r>\rho
, the length of
V_{r}
is
k
. Moreover, if
\rho<r<s<R
, then
V_{s}(\tfrac{r}{s}L)
is injective and
\mathrm{Ker}\,F(rL)=\mathrm{Ker}\,T_{s}(\tfrac{r}{s}L)
.

In particular, if
F(rL)
is injective, then
T_{s}(\tfrac{r}{s}L)
is both injective and surjective, hence square and invertible.

Proof.

Let
0<r<s<R
, then

V_{r}(L)T_{r}(L)=F(rL)=V_{s}(\tfrac{r}{s}L)T_{s}(\tfrac{r}{s}L).

Let
w(t)
be the length of
V_{t}
, for
0<t<R
. Then
w(t)=\mathrm{dim}_{\mathscr{W}}\mathrm{Ran}\,F(tL)^{-\|\cdot\|}
is the wandering dimension of
\mathrm{Ran}\,F(tL)^{-\|\cdot\|}
. We first note that
w(t)\leq n
, by the previous lemma, since
F
is of length
n
. Moreover, since
T_{s}(\tfrac{r}{s}L)
is surjective by Lemma
2.3
, we have that

\mathrm{Ran}\,V_{r}(L)=\mathrm{Ran}\,F(rL)^{-\|\cdot\|}=\mathrm{Ran}\,V_{s}(\tfrac{r}{s}L)^{-\|\cdot\|}.

Since the dimension of the wandering subspace is, at most, the number of generators, we obtain that
w(r)\leq w(s)
. In other words, the function
w(t)
is monotonically increasing, bounded, and integer–valued. Therefore, there exists a
\rho>0
, such that for all
0<\rho<r<R
,
w(r)
is constant.

Now for
r>\rho
, we apply the inner-outer decomposition
V_{s}(\tfrac{r}{s}L)=VH_{s,r}
, where
H_{s,r}
is outer and square. Observe that any square outer is necessarily injective. Indeed, at every point
Z\in\mathbb{B}^{(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N})\cdot d}
, we have that
H_{s,r}(Z)
is surjective and, therefore, invertible. We conclude that any vector in the kernel of
H_{s,r}
must evaluate to
0
on
\mathbb{B}^{(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N})\cdot d}
and, thus, is zero. Therefore,
V_{s}(\tfrac{r}{s}L)
is injective.

The last statement then follows as if
V_{s}(\tfrac{r}{s}L)
is injective,
T_{s}(\tfrac{r}{s}L)
is surjective and
F(rL)=V_{s}(\tfrac{r}{s}L)T_{s}(\tfrac{r}{s}L)
is injective, we must also have that
T_{s}(\tfrac{r}{s}L)
is injective. By the open mapping theorem,
T_{s}(\tfrac{r}{s}L)
is then invertible. Any invertible
A(L)\in\mathbb{H}^{\infty}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
must be square,
i.e.

m=n
. Indeed,
A(L):\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{m}
is surjective. Hence
\{A(L)1\otimes e_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
is a
R\otimes I_{m}-
cyclic set for
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{m}
and hence
m\leq n
. Conversely,
A(L)^{-1}:\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
is also surjective, so
m=n
.
∎

Lemma 2.6
.

[
24
, Appendix]

Let
E\in\mathbb{H}^{\infty}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
be idempotent. Then there exists an
S\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{H}^{\infty}_{d})
and a
1\leq k\leq n
so that

E(L)=S(L)I_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}}\otimes\begin{pmatrix}I_{k}&0\\
0&0_{n-k}\end{pmatrix}S(L)^{-1}.

This lemma was established in
[
24
, Appendix]
, we include the proof for completeness.

Proof.

Consider the inner–outer factorization of
E
and of the complementary idempotent,
I-E
,
E(L)=V(L)F(L)
,
I-E(L)=W(L)G(L)
. Then,

\displaystyle I_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}}\otimes I_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E(L)+(I-E(L))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V(L)F(L)+W(L)G(L)=(V(L),W(L))\begin{pmatrix}F(L)\\
G(L)\end{pmatrix}.

Write
S(L):=(V(L),W(L))
. Since
E(L)^{2}=E(L)
, we get that
V(L)(I_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}}\otimes I_{k}-F(L)V(L))F(L)=0
. Since
V(L)
is injective and
F(L)
has dense range, we get that
F(L)V(L)=I_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}}\otimes I_{k}
. Similarly,
G(L)W(L)=I_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}}\otimes I_{j}
. Moreover, since
E(L)(I-E(L))=0=(I-E(L))E(L)
, we get that
F(L)W(L)=G(L)V(L)=0
. Hence,

is invertible with inverse

G(L)\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. In particular, both are square, i.e.,

It is now clear that

\displaystyle S(L)I_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}}\otimes\begin{pmatrix}I_{k}&0\\
0&0_{n-k}\end{pmatrix}S(L)^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(V(L),W(L))I_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}}\otimes\begin{pmatrix}I_{k}&0\\
0&0_{n-k}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}F(L)\\
G(L)\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V(L)F(L)+0=E(L).

∎

Remark 2.10
.

In
[
24
, Appendix]
, it is proved that
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}_{d}
is a projective free ring. However, it is also claimed in
[
24
, Appendix]
that

is a semifir. This fails even for

, as can be seen in
[
52
, Beispiel 2]
. To be more precise, if we take

and

, then by the corona theorem
f
and
g
do not generate the trivial ideal. Moreover, the ideal generated by the two is not principal. The reason is that if
h
divides both

and

, then we can write

, where
h_{i}
is inner and
h_{o}
is outer. Since

must be invertible since

has no outer part. On the other hand, since
g=hb
,
h_{i}=1
since
g
has no inner part. Hence,

is a unit, but this contradicts the fact that the ideal generated by
f
and
g
is not trivial.

As we will see, the obstruction that prevents

from being a semifir is that it is generally not possible to “trivialize” row outers that have dense range in the sense of
[
10
, Section 2.3 and Theorem 2.3.1]
. In this paper, we will see that for outers this problem can be overcome by increasing the radii of the balls. Namely, any such row outer on a row-ball of a given radius can always be trivialized on any ball of strictly smaller radius. Even though

is not a semifir, it would be interesting to determine whether

is a Sylvester domain, which is a larger class of rings than semifirs that also have universal skew fields of fractions

.

3
Topologies on rings of analytic NC functions

Classically, the ring of holomorphic functions on a domain has a natural Fréchet topology that arises from a compact exhaustion of the domain. Every two such exhaustions give rise to the same topology. Moreover, the resulting topological algebra is locally multiplicatively convex (LMC)
[
32
]
and nuclear. One can view such algebras as inverse limits of function algebras. The quantized version of a function algebra is an operator algebra and a theory of quantized topological vector spaces was explored by Effros and Webster in
[
15
,
53
]
. Later in Section
6
we will apply this theory of Effros and Webster to define a class of topological “local operator algebras” which admit well-defined evaluations of the universal skew field,
\mathscr{M}_{d}
, of
\mathscr{O}_{d}
.

Our algebras
\mathscr{O}_{d}
and
\mathscr{O}_{d}(R)
,
R>0
, admit a natural topology given by the family of semi-norms

\rho_{r}(f)=\sup_{0<r<R}\left\{\|f(X)\|\left|X\in r\cdot\overline{\mathbb{B}^{(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N})\cdot d}}\right.\right\}.

It is easily seen that the rings
\mathscr{O}_{d}(R)
,
R\in(0,+\infty]
, are complete with respect to this topology. We can view these rings as inverse limits of certain operator algebras. Namely, let us write
\mathbb{A}_{d}(r)
for the closure of the free algebra viewed as functions on
r\cdot\mathbb{B}^{(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N})\cdot d}
and equipped with the supremum (matrix) norm topology on this row-ball. (In the case where
r=1
this recovers the
non-commutative disk algebra
,
\mathbb{A}_{d}
, as introduced by Popescu and we will often call
\mathbb{A}_{d}(r)
the free disk algebra on the NC row-ball of radius
r
.) We also let
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}_{d}(r)
denote the ring of all NC functions that are uniformly bounded in
r\cdot\mathbb{B}^{(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N})\cdot d}
. This can also be viewed through the lens of operators. For every
r>0
, it is easy to see that
\rho_{r}(f)=\|f(rL)\|_{\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d})}
. Hence, the restriction maps are essentially given by evaluating the free FPS of
f
at the operator
d-
tuple
rL=(rL_{1},\cdots,rL_{d})
.

Let
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}(r)
be the
H^{2}
-space of the ball
r\cdot\mathbb{B}^{(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N})\cdot d}
. This is defined as the Hilbert space of all free FPS,

f(\mathfrak{z})=\sum_{\omega\in\mathbb{W}_{d}}\hat{f}_{\omega}\,\mathfrak{z}^{\omega}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\underbrace{\sum_{|\omega|=\ell}\hat{f}_{\omega}\,\mathfrak{z}^{\omega},}_{=:f_{\ell}(\mathfrak{z})}

with Hilbert space norm given by

\|f\|_{r}^{2}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}r^{2\ell}\|f_{\ell}\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}}^{2},

and where
\|f_{\ell}\|_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}}
is the norm of the
\ell-
th homogeneous component in
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}=\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}(1)
. For any
r>0
, the Hardy algebra,
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}_{d}(r)
, defined above, can be identified with the left multiplier algebra of
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}(r)
, and
\mathbb{A}_{d}(r)
can be equivalently defined as the operator-norm closure of the free algebra in
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}_{d}(r)
. The hardy algebra,
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}_{d}(r)
is then the weak operator topology (WOT) closure of
\mathbb{A}_{d}(r)
in
\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}(r))
. Also note that for any
0<r<s
, the re-scaling map,
F(Z)\mapsto F(\tfrac{r}{s}Z)
, defines an embedding of
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}_{d}(s)
into
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}_{d}(r)
and of
\mathbb{A}_{d}(s)
in
\mathbb{A}_{d}(r)
, both with dense ranges (albeit, in the former case, in the weak* topology of
\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}(r))
). In particular,
F\in\mathbb{H}^{\infty}_{d}(r)
if and only if
F(rL)\in\mathbb{H}^{\infty}_{d}=\mathbb{H}^{\infty}_{d}(1)
.

For
r<s
, there are natural restriction maps
\mathbb{A}_{d}(s)\hookrightarrow\mathbb{A}_{d}(r)
and
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}(s)\hookrightarrow\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}(r)
which we will denote by
\pi_{r,s}
. This gives two families of topological rings,
\{\mathbb{A}_{d}(r)|\ r\in(0,\infty)\}
, and
\{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}(r)|\ r\in(0,\infty)\}
equipped with continuous mappings
\pi_{r,s}
,
0<r<s<\infty
, given by restriction to the NC row-ball of radius
r<s
. For convenience, given any
R\in(0,+\infty]
, we fix a strictly increasing sequence
(r_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
so that
\lim_{n\uparrow\infty}r_{n}=R
. In the case where
R=+\infty
we will simply choose
r_{n}=n\in\mathbb{N}
. We will also write
\pi_{m,n}:=\pi_{r_{m},r_{n}}:\mathbb{A}_{d}(r_{n})\hookrightarrow\mathbb{A}_{d}(r_{m})
,
m<n
, for the continuous restriction map. Following
[
5
]
, the
inverse limit
of
\{\mathbb{A}_{d}(r_{n})\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is then a topological ring defined as follows. First, consider the Cartesian product space

\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{A}_{d}(r_{n}),

equipped with the product topology and component-wise multiplication making it a topological ring. The
inverse limit ring
,
\varprojlim\mathbb{A}_{d}(r_{m})
, of the family
\{\mathbb{A}_{d}(r_{n})\}
, is then defined as the topological sub-ring of
\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{A}_{d}(r_{n})
consisting of all sequences
(a_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
,
a_{n}\in\mathbb{A}_{d}(r_{n})
, obeying
a_{n}=\pi_{n,n+1}(a_{n+1})
and equipped with the subspace topology it inherits from the Cartesian product
\prod\mathbb{A}_{d}(r_{n})
. The inverse limit of the family
\{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}(r_{n})\}
is defined similarly. Observe that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the restriction map

is a completely contractive and unital homomorphism of operator algebras with dense range. Indeed, any
a\in\mathbb{A}_{d}(r)
is, by definition, the supremum-norm limit of free polynomials in
r\cdot\mathbb{B}^{(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N})\cdot d}
. Hence
\mathbb{C}\langle\mathfrak{z}\rangle
is dense in
\mathbb{A}_{d}(r)
for any

which implies that
\pi_{r,s}:\mathbb{A}_{d}(s)\hookrightarrow\mathbb{A}_{d}(r)
has dense range for any

. That is,
\varprojlim\mathbb{A}_{d}(r_{m})
is a

in the sense of Arens
[
5
]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

We have two presentations of
\mathscr{O}_{d}
as inverse limits

is any strictly increasing sequence with limit

.

The first presentation is clear since the topology on
\mathscr{O}_{d}
is given by the supremum norms on balls of increasing radius. For the second one, we note that if a function
f
extends analytically beyond the ball, then there is a bound on the
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}_{d}
norm by
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}

∎

Moreover, by the discussion above, we obtain the following:

Lemma 3.2
.

Given any
R\in(0,+\infty]
,
\mathscr{O}_{d}(R)
is a complete dense inverse limit topological ring.

Proof.

It is easily checked that
\mathscr{O}_{d}(R)
is complete with respect to the topology of uniform convergence on any NC sub-ball,

Each of the algebras,
\mathscr{O}_{d}(R)
, satisfy a version of the classical Montel theorem. Though several Montel-type theorems exist in non-commutative analysis (see, for example
[
3
, Theorem 13.14]
and
[
41
, Theorem 5.2]
), we provide a slightly different proof here for the sake of completeness. Recall from
[
50
, Definition 34.2]
that a topological vector space
\mathcal{E}
is called a
Montel space
, if it is Hausdorff, barrelled, and every closed bounded subset is compact. In particular, by
[
50
, Corollary 3 in Section 50]
, every nuclear Fréchet space is Montel. In a locally convex space, boundedness is equivalent to boundedness in each of the seminorms defining the topology. In particular, for a Fréchet space a bounded sequence has a convergent subsequence by metrizability. Thus, we obtain a template for Montel-type theorems.

Theorem 3.3
.

The space
\mathscr{O}_{d}
is a nuclear Fréchet space. In particular, if
f_{n}\in\mathscr{O}_{d}
is a sequence of entire NC functions that are bounded on NC row-balls of finite radii, then
f_{n}
has a convergent subsequence.

Proof.

By
[
46
, 7.3 Corollary 3]
, a Fréchet space is nuclear, if it is an inverse limit of Hilbert spaces with trace-class connecting maps. Therefore, it suffices to show that the restriction map
\Phi_{r}\colon\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\to\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}
is trace-class. We note that since
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}=\oplus_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{C}_{k}\langle\mathfrak{z}\rangle
, where
\mathbb{C}_{k}\langle\mathfrak{z}\rangle
is the space of homogeneous free polynomials of degree
k
. On each
\mathbb{C}_{k}\langle\mathfrak{z}\rangle
, the map
\Phi_{r}
acts as multiplication by
r^{k}
. Hence,
\Phi_{r}
is trace class, provided that
r<d
, since
\mathrm{dim}\,\mathbb{C}_{k}\langle\mathfrak{z}\rangle=d^{k}
. Choose a cofinal system of radii and hence a cofinal system of semi-norms associated to NC row-balls of those radii, such that the radii grow exponentially, with exponent
>d
. It follows that
\mathscr{O}_{d}
is the inverse limit of a system of Hilbert spaces with trace-class maps, as desired.
∎

Classically, the ring of entire functions is a Bézout domain. (And a commutative ring is a semifir if and only if it is a Bézout domain.) Moreover, every finitely generated ideal of the ring of entire functions (and even the ring of holomorphic functions on a domain in
\mathbb{C}
) is principal and closed. This result is called Helmer’s theorem according to
[
28
, Theorem 13.6]
. The converse also holds. Namely, if an ideal of the ring of entire functions is closed, then it is principal. The key tool in the proof of those results is the Weierstrass theorem that allows one to build entire functions with prescribed zeroes and multiplicities. In other words, the closed ideal of the ring of entire functions are described by analytic varieties (counting the multiplicity). This leads us to define varieties in the NC entire setting. Varieties of various types were studied both in the algebraic setting
[
21
]
and the setting of the NC unit row ball
[
24
,
43
]
. Let
S\subseteq\mathscr{O}_{d}
. Define the corresponding variety similarly to
[
24
]

\mathscr{Z}(S)=\bigsqcup_{n\leq\aleph_{0}}\left\{\left.(X,y)\in\mathbb{C}^{(n\times n)\cdot d}\times(\mathbb{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\})\right|\ y^{*}h(X)=0\ \,\forall h\in S\right\}.

Here,
\mathbb{C}^{\aleph_{0}}=\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})
and
\mathbb{C}^{(\infty\times\infty)\cdot d}:=\mathscr{B}(\ell^{2})
. Clearly, if
I
is the closed right ideal generated by
S
, then for every
g\in I
and every
(X,y)\in\mathscr{Z}(S)
, we have that
y\perp\mathrm{Ran}\,g(X)
. Therefore, the variety that we have defined corresponds to closed right ideals. Conversely, given
\mathscr{Z}\subseteq\bigsqcup_{n\leq\aleph_{0}}\mathbb{C}^{(n\times n)\cdot d}\times(\mathbb{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
, we can define the corresponding closed right ideal of entire functions. Namely,

I(\mathscr{Z})=\left\{f\in\mathscr{O}_{d}\left|\ y\perp\mathrm{Ran}\,f(X)\ \,\forall(X,y)\in\mathscr{Z}\right.\right\}.

We can also define corresponding varieties in the case of
\mathscr{O}_{d}(R)
,

Lemma 3.6

If

Let

The above lemma will later be used to prove a version of the Bergman Nullstellensatz for each

4
Algebraic properties of rings of analytic NC functions

In this section, we will prove two main results. First, we will prove that each
\mathscr{O}_{d}(R)
is a semifir and we will also show that every finitely–generated right ideal in
\mathscr{O}_{d}(R)
is closed. From the latter fact, we derive a version of the Bergman Nullstellensatz for each
\mathscr{O}_{d}(R)
,
R\in(0,+\infty]
. Recall from
[
10
]
that a ring,
\mathscr{R}
, is called a
semifir
or
semi-free ideal ring
if every finitely generated right (equivalently, left) ideal is free as a right (respectively, left) module. Alternative conditions for
\mathscr{R}
to be a semifir are provided in
[
10
, Theorem 2.3.1]
. In particular, we will employ the condition
[
10
, Theorem 2.3.1(a)]
. In order to state this condition, recall that for two
n
-tuples of elements
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\in\mathscr{R}
, if
\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}y_{j}=0
, we say that this equation is an
n-
term relation for
\mathscr{R}
. Such a relation is said to be
trivial
, if for every
1\leq i\leq n
, either
x_{i}=0
or
y_{i}=0
. Write
x=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x_{1}\\
\vdots\\
x_{n}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathscr{R}^{n}
and
y=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}y_{1}\\
\vdots\\
y_{n}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathscr{R}^{n}
. We say that an invertible matrix
S\in\mathscr{R}^{n\times n}

trivializes
the relation
x^{\mathrm{t}}y=0
, if the new relation
x^{{}^{\prime};\mathrm{t}}y^{\prime}:=(x^{\mathrm{t}}S)(S^{-1}y)=0
is trivial. We can now state the condition
[
10
, Theorem 2.3.1(a)]
; a ring,
\mathscr{R}
, is a semifir if and only if every finite relation,
\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}y_{j}=0
, can be trivialized by an invertible matrix in
\mathscr{R}^{n\times n}
. Since our goal is to prove that
\mathscr{O}_{d}(R)
is a semifir, for any
0<R\leq+\infty
, we will fix an arbitrary
n\in\mathbb{N}
and elements
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n},g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\in\mathscr{O}_{d}(R)
, such that
\sum_{j=1}^{n}f_{j}g_{j}=0
. We will write

F=\begin{pmatrix}f_{1}&\ldots&f_{n}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathscr{O}_{d}(R)^{1\times n}\text{ and }G=\begin{pmatrix}g_{1}\\
\vdots\\
g_{n}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathscr{O}_{d}(R)^{n}.

Then, our relation is
FG=0
. Our basic strategy will be to first show that given any such
n-
term relation for
\mathscr{O}_{d}(R)
, it can be trivialized in
\mathbb{A}_{d}(r)
for any
0<r<R
. We then construct an inverse limit of invertible
n\times n
matrices over
\mathbb{A}_{d}(r)
,
0<r<R
which trivialize our relation in
r\cdot\mathbb{B}^{(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N})\cdot d}
,
i.e.
in
\mathbb{A}_{d}(r)
, to obtain an invertible matrix which trivializes our original relation for the inverse limit ring,
\mathscr{O}_{d}(R)
.

For the remainder of this section, fix any
0<R\leq+\infty
, and a strictly increasing sequence of positive numbers,
(t_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}
, so that
t_{n}
converges to
R
. Hence, in the case where
R=+\infty
,
t_{j}\uparrow+\infty
diverges to
+\infty
and we will choose
t_{j}=j\in\mathbb{N}
, in this case.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\Phi\in\mathbb{H}^{\infty}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{1\times n}
. Then, there exists
S\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{H}^{\infty}_{d})
, such that
\Phi S=(\tilde{\varphi}_{1},\cdots,\tilde{\varphi}_{k},0,\cdots,0)
. The row,
\tilde{\Phi}=(\tilde{\varphi}_{1},\cdots,\tilde{\varphi}_{k})
, is injective if and only if there exists a decomposition
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}=\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathrm{Ker}\,\Phi
, where
\mathcal{H}
is a closed right shift-invariant subspace. Moreover, given any other
T\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{H}^{\infty}_{d})
, so that
\Phi T
has a decomposition with its first
k
-coordinates an injective row and the last
n-k
components are
0
, we have that
T
satisfies the same properties as
S
and

T=S\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\
B&C\end{pmatrix}.

Proof.

If there is such a decomposition, then we can choose
S=(V_{1},V_{2})
, where
V_{1}
is the inner with range
\mathcal{H}
and
V_{2}
is the inner with range
\mathrm{Ker}\,\Phi
. Conversely, if there is an
S
, such that
\Phi S
has the form above, then set
W=\mathrm{Span}\{e_{n-\ell},\ldots,e_{n}\}\otimes\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}
and note that
S^{-1}\mathrm{Ker}\,\Phi=W
. Therefore, if
\mathcal{H}=SW^{\perp}
, then
\mathcal{H}+\mathrm{Ker}\,\Phi=\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
. If
T
is another such invertible matrix then
T^{-1}SW=W
. Hence,
T^{-1}S
is lower-triangular and by a symmetric argument, so is
S^{-1}T
.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Given
F\in\mathscr{O}_{d}(R)^{1\times n}
,
G\in\mathscr{O}_{d}(R)^{n}
so that
F\cdot G\equiv 0
, then for any
0<r<R
, there exists
S\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{A}_{d}(r))
, such that

FS=\begin{pmatrix}\underbrace{\tilde{F}}_{1\times(n-\ell)}&\underbrace{0}_{1\times\ell}\end{pmatrix}\text{ and }S^{-1}G=\begin{pmatrix}\underbrace{0}_{(n-\ell)\times 1}\\
\underbrace{\tilde{G}}_{1\times\ell}\end{pmatrix},

in the row-ball of radius
r
. In other words, we can trivialize the relation
FG=0
in
\mathbb{A}_{d}(r)
. Moreover, there exists
0<r_{0}<R
, such that for
r>r_{0}
,
\tilde{F}
, is injective and
\ell
is independent of
r\in(r_{0},R)
.

Proof.

Choose any
r,s,t>0
so that
0<r<s<t<R
. Factor
F(tL)=V(L)T(L)
, where
V\in\mathbb{H}^{\infty}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{1\times k}
is inner and
T\in\mathbb{H}^{\infty}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{k\times n}
is outer (
k\leq n
). Now consider
T(\tfrac{s}{t}L)
(which corresponds to the restriction of
T
to the ball of smaller radius
s>0
). By Lemma
2.3
, there exists an
H_{s}\in\mathbb{H}^{\infty}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n\times k}
, such that
T(\tfrac{s}{t}L)H_{s}(L)=I_{\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}}\otimes I_{k}
. In particular, we have a (generally) non-orthogonal, topological direct sum,
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}=\mathrm{Ran}\,H_{s}(L)\dotplus\mathrm{Ker}\,T(\tfrac{s}{t}L)
. That is
\mathrm{Ran}\,H_{s}(L)
is a closed subspace, since
H_{s}(L)
is bounded below,
\mathrm{Ker}\,T(\tfrac{s}{t}L)
is also closed, and these two closed subspaces have trivial intersection. We use the notation
\dotplus
to denote such a topological, non-orthogonal direct sum.

Let
\Theta\colon\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n-\ell}\to\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
\Xi\colon\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
be inners, such that
\mathrm{Ran}\,\Theta=\mathrm{Ran}\,H_{s}(L)
and
\mathrm{Ran}\,\Xi=\mathrm{Ker}\,T(\tfrac{s}{t}L)
. (Both
\mathrm{Ran}\,H_{s}(L)
and
\mathrm{Ker}\,T(\tfrac{s}{t}L)
are closed
R\otimes I_{n}-
invariant subspaces so that the NC Beurling theorem applies.) Let
S(L)=(\Theta(L),\Xi(L))\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{H}^{\infty}_{d})
. Then,
S
is invertible (since the Friedrichs angle between the spaces is not
0
). Alternatively, since
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}=\mathrm{Ran}\,\Theta(L)\dotplus\mathrm{Ran}\,\Xi(L)
is a topological direct sum, there is a bounded, idempotent operator,
E\in\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n})
so that
\mathrm{Ran}\,E=\mathrm{Ran}\,\Theta(L)
and
\mathrm{Ran}\,I-E=\mathrm{Ran}\,\Xi(L)
. Then, for any
x\in\mathbb{H}^{2}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
1\leq j\leq d
,

(R_{j}\otimes I_{n})Ex=(R_{j}\otimes I_{n})E^{2}x=E(R_{j}\otimes I_{n})Ex,

since
E
is idempotent and
\mathrm{Ran}\,E=\mathrm{Ran}\,\Theta(L)
is
R\otimes I_{n}-
invariant. Similarly,

\displaystyle E(R_{j}\otimes I_{n})x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E(R_{j}\otimes I_{n})(E+I-E)x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E(R_{j}\otimes I_{n})Ex=(R_{j}\otimes I_{n})Ex,

and we conclude that
[R_{i}\otimes I_{n},E]=0
. By Davidson–Pitts
[
13
, Theorem 1.2]
, we conclude that
E=E(L)\in\mathbb{H}^{\infty}_{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, and Lemma
2.6
now implies that
S(L)=(\Theta(L),\Xi(L))
is invertible. We now have that,

T(\tfrac{s}{t}L)S(L)=\begin{pmatrix}\underbrace{\star}_{k\times(n-\ell)}&\underbrace{0}_{k\times\ell}\end{pmatrix}.

Restricting further to the ball of radius
r>0
, the above identity still holds for
T(\tfrac{r}{t}L)S(\tfrac{r}{t}L)
. Since
0=F(tL)G(tL)=V(L)T(L)G(tL)
, it follows that
T(L)G(tL)=0
and hence
T(\tfrac{s}{t}L)G(sL)=0
. That is, the range of
G(sL)
is contained in
\mathrm{Ker}\,T(\tfrac{s}{t}L)=\mathrm{Ran}\,\Xi(L)
. We conclude that
G(sL)=\Xi(L)G^{\prime}(L)
, for some matrix multiplier
G^{\prime}(L)
of the correct size. Therefore,

\displaystyle S(L)^{-1}G(sL)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S(L)^{-1}\Xi(L)G^{\prime}(L)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S(L)^{-1}(0_{n\times(n-\ell)},\Xi(L))G^{\prime}(L)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S(L)^{-1}S(L)\begin{pmatrix}0_{n-\ell}&0\\
0&I_{\ell}\end{pmatrix}G^{\prime}(L)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\underbrace{0}_{(n-\ell)\times 1}\\
\underbrace{\star}_{\ell\times 1}\end{pmatrix}.

Clearly, this relation holds in
\mathbb{A}_{d}(r)
,
i.e.
for
S^{-1}(\tfrac{r}{s}L)G(rL)
. Lastly,

\displaystyle F(rL)S(\tfrac{r}{s}L)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F\left(t(\tfrac{r}{t}L)\right)S(\tfrac{r}{s}L)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V(\tfrac{r}{t}L)T(\tfrac{r}{t}L)S(\tfrac{r}{s}L)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V(\tfrac{r}{t}L)T\left(\tfrac{s}{t}(\tfrac{r}{s}L)\right)\left(\Theta(\tfrac{r}{s}L),\Xi(\tfrac{r}{s}L)\right).

Since
\mathrm{Ran}\,\Xi(L)=\mathrm{Ker}\,T(\tfrac{s}{t}L)
it follows that
\mathrm{Ran}\,\Xi(\tfrac{r}{s}L)\subseteq\mathrm{Ker}\,T(\tfrac{r}{t}L)
so that the above equation becomes

F(rL)S(\tfrac{r}{s}L)=V(\tfrac{r}{t}L)\begin{pmatrix}\underbrace{\star}_{k\times(n-\ell)}&\underbrace{0}_{k\times\ell}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\underbrace{\star}_{1\times(n-\ell)}&\underbrace{0}_{1\times\ell}\end{pmatrix}.

In conclusion, we have trivialized our relation in
\mathbb{A}_{d}(r)
. Namely, if we restrict
F
to
r\cdot\mathbb{B}^{(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N})\cdot d}
, then for any
Z\in r\cdot\mathbb{B}^{(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N})\cdot d}
,
F(Z)=F(r(\tfrac{1}{r}Z))
, where
\tfrac{1}{r}Z\in\mathbb{B}^{(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N})\cdot d}
. It then follows, by the above equation, that

F(Z)S(\tfrac{1}{s}Z)=V(\tfrac{1}{t}Z)\begin{pmatrix}\underbrace{\star}_{k\times(n-\ell)}&\underbrace{0}_{k\times\ell}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\underbrace{\star}_{1\times(n-\ell)}&\underbrace{0}_{1\times\ell}\end{pmatrix},

for any
Z\in r\cdot\mathbb{B}^{(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N})\cdot d}
.

Assume now that
r>\rho
, where
\rho
is the constant obtained in Lemma
2.5
. By Lemma
2.5
,
\mathrm{Ker}\,F(rL)=\mathrm{Ker}\,T(\frac{r}{t}L)
and, by our construction,
T(\frac{r}{t}L)S(\frac{r}{s}L)=(\tilde{T},0)
. Note that
\tilde{T}
must be injective and, thus, so is
\tilde{F}=V(\frac{r}{t}L)\tilde{T}
.

It remains to show that for
0<r<R
sufficiently large,
\ell
, is independent of
r
. Let us write
\ell_{r}:=\ell
to emphasize this dependence. For
r>\rho
, we have that
\ell_{r}=\dim_{\mathscr{W}}\,\mathrm{Ker}\,F(rL)
, the wandering dimension of
\mathrm{Ker}\,F(rL)
. Clearly, for
r^{\prime}>r>\rho
, the restriction map induces an injective map from
\mathrm{Ker}\,F(r^{\prime}L)
to
\mathrm{Ker}\,F(rL)
. The closure of the image
\mathrm{Ker}\,F(r^{\prime}L)
is a closed right invariant subspace of
\mathrm{Ker}\,F(rL)
. Hence,
\ell_{r^{\prime}}\leq\ell_{r}
. Namely, the function
r\mapsto\ell_{r}
is monotonically decreasing, integer-valued, and bounded below by
1
. Hence, for
r
big enough, it is constant and we choose
0<\rho\leq r_{0}<R
so that
\ell_{r}
is constant for all
0<r_{0}<r<R
.
∎

The proof of the following theorem is inspired by the inverse limit results of
[
5
]
.

Theorem 4.3
.

The ring of uniformly analytic NC functions, with radius of convergence at least
R\in(0,+\infty]
,
\mathscr{O}_{d}(R)
,
d\in\mathbb{N}
, is a semi-free ideal ring. In particular, the ring of uniformly entire NC functions,
\mathscr{O}_{d}=\mathscr{O}_{d}(\infty)
, is a semifir.

Proof.

Let
F
and
G
be as above, and let
r_{0}
be the number obtained in the preceding lemma. Given the strictly increasing sequence
(t_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}
,
0<t_{m}<R
,
t_{m}\uparrow R
, fix
m\in\mathbb{N}
, such that
t_{m}>r_{0}
. Let
S\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{A}_{d}(t_{m+1}))
and
S^{\prime}\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{A}_{d}(t_{m}))
be such that

FS=\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{F}&0\end{pmatrix},\ FS^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{F}^{\prime}&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\mbox{and}\quad S^{-1}G=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\tilde{G}\end{pmatrix},\ (S^{\prime})^{-1}G=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\tilde{G}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix},

in the sense that the first two equations hold for all evaluations in
t_{m+1}\cdot\mathbb{B}^{(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N})\cdot d}
and the second two hold for all evaluations in
t_{m}\cdot\mathbb{B}^{(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N})\cdot d}
,
\widetilde{F}\in\mathbb{A}_{d}(t_{m+1})\otimes\mathbb{C}^{1\times n}
,
\widetilde{G}\in\mathbb{A}_{d}(t_{m+1})\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
\widetilde{F}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{A}_{d}(t_{m})\otimes\mathbb{C}^{1\times n}
and
\widetilde{G}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{A}_{d}(t_{m})\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

Such
S
and
S^{\prime}
exist by Lemma
4.2
. Moreover, by the aforementioned lemma,
\tilde{F}
and
\tilde{F^{\prime}}
are both injective. Let
\pi_{m+1,m}:\mathbb{A}_{d}(t_{m+1})\hookrightarrow\mathbb{A}_{d}(t_{m})
be the restriction map. By our assumption
\pi_{m+1,m}(S)
maps
W=\mathrm{Span}\{e_{n-\ell},\ldots,e_{n}\}\otimes\mathbb{H}^{2}(t_{m}\cdot\mathbb{B}^{(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N})\cdot d})
into the kernel of
F
so that
\pi_{m+1,m}(\tilde{F})
must be injective. To be more precise, if it was not injective, then
\mathrm{Ker}\,F(t_{m}L)
would be a right invariant closed subspace that would properly contain
W
. Therefore, it will have more wandering vectors than
\ell
. However, the wandering dimension of
\mathrm{Ker}\,F(t_{m}L)
, is
\ell
by the previous lemma, since we assume
t_{m}>r_{0}
. Let
m_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
be the smallest natural number so that
t_{m_{0}}>r_{0}
.

We conclude, from Lemma
4.1
, that there exists a lower triangular
T^{\prime}\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{A}_{d}(t_{m}))
, such that
S^{\prime}=\pi_{m+1,m}(S)T^{\prime}
. Now for every
\varepsilon>0
, we can choose
T\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{A}_{d}(t_{m+1}))
lower triangular, such that
\|\pi_{m+1,m}(T)-T^{\prime}\|<\frac{\varepsilon}{\|S\|}
. Indeed,

S^{\prime}=\pi_{m+1,m}(S)T^{\prime}\quad\Rightarrow\quad S^{\prime}T^{{}^{\prime};-1}=\pi_{m+1,m}(S).

Since both
S^{\prime}
and
\pi_{m+1,m}(S)
trivialize our relation
FG=0
on the row-ball of radius
t_{m}
, Lemma
4.1

implies that there is a block lower triangular
\widetilde{T}^{\prime}
so that
\pi_{m+1,m}(S)=S^{\prime}\widetilde{T}^{\prime}
. It follows that
\widetilde{T}^{\prime}=T^{{}^{\prime};-1}
, so that
T^{{}^{\prime};-1}
is also block lower triangular (with the same sized blocks). If

T^{\prime}=:\begin{pmatrix}A^{\prime}&0\\
B^{\prime}&C^{\prime}\end{pmatrix},

the fact that
T^{{}^{\prime};-1}
is also block lower triangular forces
the diagonal blocks,
A^{\prime}
and
C^{\prime}
to be invertible, so that

T^{{}^{\prime};-1}=\begin{pmatrix}A^{{}^{\prime};-1}&0\\
-C^{{}^{\prime};-1}B^{\prime}A^{{}^{\prime};-1}&C^{{}^{\prime};-1}\end{pmatrix}.

Note that the images of
\mathbb{A}_{d}(t_{m+1})^{n\times n}
and
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{A}_{d}(t_{m+1}))
in
\mathbb{A}_{d}(t_{m})^{n\times n}
and
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{A}_{d}(t_{m}))
, respectively, are dense. Indeed, this can be see from the fact any element of the unital operator algebra,
\mathbb{A}_{d}(t_{m})^{n\times n}
, is the supremum–norm limit of elements of
\mathbb{C}\langle\mathfrak{z}\rangle^{n\times n}
and the group of invertible elements in a unital Banach algebra is an open set. Hence, there exist matrices
A,B,C
over
\mathbb{A}_{d}(t_{m+1})
of appropriate sizes so that

A,C
are invertible and so that their images under the restriction map,
\pi_{m+1,m}
are arbitrarily close to
A^{\prime},B^{\prime},C^{\prime}
, respectively. That is, for any
\epsilon>0
, we can choose

T:=\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\
B&C\end{pmatrix}\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{A}_{d}(t_{m+1})),

so that
\|\pi_{m+1,m}(T)-T^{\prime}\|<\frac{\varepsilon}{\|S\|}
, and hence,

\|\pi^{(n)}_{m+1,m}(ST)-S^{\prime}\|\leq\|\pi^{(n)}_{m+1,m}(S)\|\|\pi_{m+1,m}(T)-T^{\prime}\|<\varepsilon.

In other words, we can replace
S
with a different trivializing matrix, such that its image will be close to the solution
T
within the desired arbitrary precision.

Given a complex, separable Hilbert space,
\mathcal{H}
and an invertible
B\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H})
, let
r(B):=\|B^{-1}\|^{-1}
. It is well known that the open ball of radius
r(B)
around
B
is contained in the group of invertible elements of
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H})
. If
B\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{A}_{d}(t_{m}))
, we will write
r_{m}(B)
in place of
r(B)
in what follows.

Using the above observations, we will inductively build sequences,
\varepsilon_{m}>0
, and
S_{m}\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{A}_{d}(t_{m+m_{0}}))
, for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
, with the following properties:

(i)

For all
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
F|_{m}S_{m}=(\tilde{F}_{m},0_{\ell})
with
\tilde{F}_{m}
injective, and where
F|_{m}
denotes the restriction of
F
to the row-ball of radius
t_{m}
,

(ii)

For all
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
\|\pi^{(n)}_{m+m_{0}+1,m+m_{0}}(S_{m+1})-S_{m}\|<\varepsilon_{m}
,

(iii)

\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon_{m}<\infty
,

(iv)

For every
m\in\mathbb{N}
and every
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\varepsilon_{m+k}\leq r_{m+m_{0}}(S_{m})/2^{m+k}
.

For each
m>m_{0}
, by Lemma
4.2
, we can find
T_{m}\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{A}_{d}(t_{m}))
, such that
\pi_{m}(F)T_{m}=(\tilde{F}_{m},0_{\ell})
, with
\tilde{F}^{\prime}_{m}
injective. Now for
m=1
, we set
S_{m}=T_{m_{0}+1}
and let
\varepsilon_{1}=\min\{1,r_{m_{0}+1}(S_{1})/2\}
. Now assume that
S_{1},\ldots,S_{m}
and
\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{m}
have been defined. By the argument above, an
S_{m+1}\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{A}_{d}(m_{0}+m+1))
exists, such that
\|\pi^{(n)}_{m_{0}+m+1,m_{0}+m}(S_{m+1})-S_{m}\|<\varepsilon_{m}
and
\pi_{m_{0}+m+1}(F)S_{m+1}=(\tilde{F}_{m},0_{\ell})
, with
\tilde{F}_{m+1}
injective. Now we set
\varepsilon_{m+1}=\frac{1}{2^{m}}\min\{1,r_{m_{0}+1}(S_{1})/2,\ldots,r_{m_{0}+m+1}(S_{m+1})/2\}
. The first and second properties are satisfied immediately from the construction. To check the last property, let us fix an arbitrary
m\in\mathbb{N}
. By construction,
\varepsilon_{m+k}
is a minimum taken over a finite set of numbers, one of which is
r_{m_{0}+m}(S_{m})/2
divided by
2^{m+k-1}
.

Having these sequences at hand, we fix
m\in\mathbb{N}
and set
S_{m,0}^{\prime}=S_{m}
. For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we set
S_{m,k}^{\prime}=\pi_{m+m_{0}+k,m+m_{0}}^{(n)}(S_{m+k})
. Since every
\pi_{m_{0}+m+1,m_{0}+m}
is a complete contraction, then so is
\pi_{m_{0}+m+k,m_{0}+m}
. We note now that

\displaystyle\|S_{m,k+1}^{\prime}-S_{m,k}^{\prime}\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\pi_{m_{0}+m+k+1,m_{0}+m}^{(n)}(S_{m+k+1})-\pi_{m_{0}+m+k,m+m_{0}}^{(n)}(S_{m+k})\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\pi_{m_{0}+m+k+1,m_{0}+m+k}^{(m)}(S_{m+k+1})-S_{m+k}\|<\varepsilon_{m+k}.

A standard argument now shows that
S_{m,k}^{\prime}
is a Cauchy sequence. Moreover,

\displaystyle\|S_{m,k}^{\prime}-S_{m}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}\|S_{m,k-j+1}-S_{m,k-j}\|<\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\varepsilon_{m+j}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\dfrac{r_{m+m_{0}}(S_{m})}{2^{m}}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\dfrac{1}{2^{j}}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\dfrac{r_{m+m_{0}}(S_{m})}{2^{m}}.

In particular, the Cauchy sequence stays in a closed ball of radius
\frac{r_{m+m_{0}}(S_{m})}{2^{m}}
around
S_{m}
and, thus, its limit is invertible. Let
S_{m}^{\prime}
be the limit. We now compute,

\displaystyle\pi_{m+m_{0}+1,m+m_{0}}^{(n)}(S_{m+1}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\pi_{m+m_{0}+1,m+m_{0}}^{(n)}(S_{m+1,k}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\mathscr{A}
,
a:=(a_{1},\cdots,a_{d})
belongs to the
\mathscr{A}-
domain of
f
,
\mathrm{Dom}_{\mathscr{A}}(f)
, if there exists: (i) a unital homomorphism
\varphi_{a}:\mathscr{O}_{d}\rightarrow\mathscr{A}
obeying
\varphi_{a}(\mathfrak{z}_{i})=a_{i}
for each
1\leq i\leq d
, and (ii),
a formal representation
f\sim(A,b,c;\vec{h})
so that the linear pencil
A\circ\vec{h}(a):=A\circ\varphi_{a}(\vec{h})
is invertible in
\mathscr{A}^{n\times n}=\mathscr{A}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
. One then defines

f(a):=1_{\mathscr{A}}\otimes b^{*}A(\vec{h}(a))^{-1}1_{\mathscr{A}}\otimes c.

Similarly, given a formal representation,
(A,b,c;\vec{h})\in\mathbb{C}^{(n\times n)\cdot(k+1)}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathscr{O}_{d}^{k}
, of an element of
\mathscr{M}_{d}
, we will say that the
d-
tuple,
a=(a_{1},\cdots,a_{d})\in\mathscr{A}^{1\times d}
belongs to
\mathscr{D}_{\mathscr{A}}(A;\vec{h})
, if there is a homomorphism,
\varphi_{a}
, obeying
\varphi_{a}(\mathfrak{z}_{i})=a_{i}
, as before, and if the affine linear pencil
A(\vec{h}(a))
is invertible in
\mathscr{A}^{n\times n}
.

Of course, there is a question whether the value of
f(a)
is well defined. Namely, it is not obvious whether or not this definition of
f(a)
, for
a\in\mathrm{Dom}_{\mathscr{A}}(f)
, is independent of the choice of formal representation for
f
. That this is indeed always well-defined is the essence of the following theorem.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
\mathscr{A}
be a stably–finite algebra over
\mathbb{C}
. Given
f\in\mathscr{M}_{d}
with formal representations
(A,b,c;\vec{h})
and
(A^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime};\vec{h}^{\prime})
, suppose that
a=(a_{1},\cdots,a_{d})\in\mathscr{A}^{1\times d}
belongs to
\mathscr{D}_{\mathscr{A}}(A;\vec{h})\cap\mathscr{D}_{\mathscr{A}}(A^{\prime};\vec{h}^{\prime})
. Then,

1_{\mathscr{A}}\otimes b^{*}A(\vec{h}(a))^{-1}1_{\mathscr{A}}\otimes c=1_{\mathscr{A}}\otimes b^{{}^{\prime}*}A^{\prime}(\vec{h}^{\prime}(a))^{-1}1_{\mathscr{A}}\otimes c^{\prime}.

That is,
f
is well-defined on
\mathrm{Dom}_{\mathscr{A}}(f)\subseteq\mathscr{A}^{1\times d}
.

The following argument is essentially the same as that used in the proof of
[
30
, Theorem 4.4]
.

Proof.

Suppose that
a=(a_{1},\cdots,a_{d})
belongs to the intersection of the
\mathscr{A}-
domains,
\mathscr{D}_{\mathscr{A}}(A;\vec{h})\cap\mathscr{D}_{\mathscr{A}}(A^{\prime};\vec{h}^{\prime})
, of the formal representations,
(A,b,c;\vec{h})\sim f\sim(A^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime};\vec{h}^{\prime})
of
f\in\mathscr{M}_{d}
, and yet that the evaluations of these representations at
a
do not agree,

F(a):=1_{\mathscr{A}}\otimes b^{*}A(\vec{h}(a))^{-1}1_{\mathscr{A}}\otimes c\neq 1_{\mathscr{A}}\otimes b^{{}^{\prime}*}A^{\prime}(\vec{h}^{\prime}(a))^{-1}1_{\mathscr{A}}\otimes c^{\prime}=:G(a).

Here, suppose that
A\in\mathbb{C}^{(n\times n)\cdot(k+1)}
and
A^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}^{(n^{\prime}\times n^{\prime})\cdot(k^{\prime}+1)}
and set
m:=n+n^{\prime}
. Then, consider the matrix

M(\mathfrak{z}):=\begin{pmatrix}0&b^{*}&b^{{}^{\prime}*}\\
c&A(\vec{h}(\mathfrak{z}))&0\\
-c^{\prime}&0&A^{\prime}(\vec{h}^{\prime}(\mathfrak{z}))\end{pmatrix}\in\mathscr{O}_{d}^{(m+1)\times(m+1)}.

By
[
10
, Proposition 5.4.6]
, if we assume that
F(a)\neq G(a)
, it follows that the inner rank of
M(a)=\varphi_{a}\circ M(\mathfrak{z})
is
m+1
over
\mathscr{A}
, as
\mathscr{A}
is stably finite.

On the other hand,
M(\mathfrak{z})\in\mathscr{O}_{d}^{(m+1)\times(m+1)}\subseteq\mathscr{M}_{d}^{(m+1)\times(m+1)}
. Since
\mathscr{M}_{d}
is a skew field, it is stably finite, and since
f(\mathfrak{z})=F(\mathfrak{z})=G(\mathfrak{z})\in\mathscr{M}_{d}
, we obtain that the inner rank of
M
over
\mathscr{M}_{d}
is
m
, again by
[
10
, Proposition 5.4.6]
. Since
\mathscr{O}_{d}
is a semifir and
\mathscr{M}_{d}
is its universal skew field of fractions, the embedding
\mathscr{O}_{d}\hookrightarrow\mathscr{M}_{d}
is honest and hence inner-rank preserving by
[
10
, Proposition 5.4.10]
. We conclude that
M
has inner rank
m
over
\mathscr{O}_{d}
.

If
M=BC
is an inner-rank factorization of
M
over
\mathscr{O}_{d}
, then
B\in\mathscr{O}_{d}^{(m+1)\times m}
and
C\in\mathscr{O}_{d}^{m\times(m+1)}
. But evaluation at
a\in\mathscr{A}^{1\times d}
then yields that
M(a)=B(a)C(a)\in\mathscr{A}^{(m+1)\times(m+1)}
has a rank factorization of inner rank

m<m+1
, a contradiction.
∎

The above result raises the natural question as to when the homomorphism
\varphi_{a}
,
a=(a_{1},\cdots,a_{d})\in\mathscr{A}^{1\times d}
is well-defined. In what follows, we construct a large class of stably-finite algebras,
\mathscr{A}
, including all finite
C^{*}-
algebras, which have the property that given any
d-
tuple of elements
a\in\mathscr{A}^{1\times d}
, the unital homomorphism
\varphi_{a}:\mathscr{O}_{d}\rightarrow\mathscr{A}
defined by
\varphi_{a}(\mathfrak{z}_{i})=a_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq d
, is well-defined.

Any uniformly entire NC function,
h\in\mathscr{O}_{d}
, can be identified with a free formal power series with infinite radius of convergence,

h(\zeta)=\sum_{\omega\in\mathbb{F}}\hat{h}_{\omega}\mathfrak{z}^{\omega};\quad\quad 0=R_{h}^{-1}=\lim\sqrt[2n]{\sum_{|\omega|=n}|\hat{h}_{\omega}|^{2}}.

In order to define evaluation of an entire
h\in\mathscr{O}_{d}
in a unital, stably-finite algebra,
\mathscr{A}
over
\mathbb{C}
, we can equip
\mathscr{A}
with a suitable topology so that this infinite power series converges to an element of
\mathscr{A}
. For example, if
\mathscr{A}
is a unital, separable and stably-finite
C^{*}-
algebra, then by the Gelfand–Naimark–Segal construction, we can assume, without loss of generality that
\mathscr{A}\subseteq\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H})
for a separable and complex Hilbert space,
\mathcal{H}
. In this case given
a_{1},\cdots,a_{d}\in\mathscr{A}\subseteq\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H})
, the partial sums of the above FPS of
h
, evaluated at
(a_{1},\cdots,a_{d})
converges absolutely and uniformly on any row-ball in
\mathscr{A}^{1\times d}\subseteq\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H})^{1\times d}
by
[
41
, Theorem 1.1]
. Namely, for any fixed
r>0
, setting

r\cdot\mathbb{B}^{d}(\mathcal{H}):=\left\{A\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H})^{1\times d}|\ \|(A_{1},\cdots,A_{d})\|_{\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d},\mathcal{H})}<r\right\},

the free polynomial partial sums,

h_{n}(\zeta):=\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\sum_{|\omega|=\ell}\hat{h}_{\omega}\mathfrak{z}^{\omega},

converge uniformly to
h(A)
when evaluated in
r\cdot\mathbb{B}^{d}(\mathcal{H})
, and they also converge absolutely and uniformly in
r\cdot\mathbb{B}^{d}(\mathcal{H})
the sense that

\sum_{\ell=n}^{\infty}\left\|\sum_{|\omega|=\ell}\hat{h}_{\omega}A^{\omega}\right\|_{\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H})}\rightarrow 0,

as
n\rightarrow\infty
, uniformly in
r\cdot\mathbb{B}^{d}(\mathcal{H})
. Since
\mathscr{A}
is closed in the operator-norm topology and any
a\in\mathscr{A}^{1\times d}
belongs to the row-ball of finite radius,
\|a\|_{\mathrm{row}}\cdot\mathbb{B}^{d}(\mathcal{H})
, it follows that
h(a)\in\mathscr{A}
for any
d-
tuple
a\in\mathscr{A}^{1\times d}
.

The proof of
[
41
, Theorem 1.1]
explicitly uses the
C^{*}-
identity for operators. Namely, given
A\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{J})
, where
\mathcal{H},\mathcal{J}
are complex Hilbert spaces,
\|A^{*}A\|=\|A\|^{2}
. Explicitly, if
h(\mathfrak{z})=\sum_{\omega\in\mathbb{W}_{d}}\hat{h}_{\omega}\mathfrak{z}^{\omega}\in\mathbb{C}\langle\!\langle\mathfrak{z}\rangle\!\rangle
has radius of convergence
R_{h}>0
, and
A\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H})^{1\times d}
, then one estimates:

\displaystyle\|h(A)\|_{\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\left\|\sum_{|\omega|=\ell}\hat{h}_{\omega}A^{\omega}\right\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\left\|\mathrm{row}(A^{\omega})_{|\omega|=\ell}\mathrm{col}(\hat{h}_{\omega})_{|\omega|=\ell}\right\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\left\|\mathrm{Ad}_{A,A^{*}}^{\circ\ell}(I_{\mathcal{H}})\right\|}\sqrt{\sum_{|\omega|=\ell}|\hat{h}_{\omega}|^{2}}.

In the above,
\mathrm{Ad}_{A,A^{*}}:\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H})\rightarrow\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H})
is the completely positive linear map of
adjunction
by
A
and
A^{*}
,

\mathrm{Ad}_{A,A^{*}}(T):=\sum_{j=1}^{d}A_{j}TA_{j}^{*}.

Given a
d-
tuple of bounded linear operators,
A\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H})^{1\times d}
one defines the
joint spectral radius
of
A
,
\rho_{\sigma}(A)
as the square root of the spectral radius of the bounded linear map,
\mathrm{Ad}_{A,A^{*}}
, viewed as an element of the unital Banach algebra
\mathscr{B}(X)
, where
X:=\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H})

[
39
]
. Namely,

\rho_{\sigma}(A)^{2}=\rho_{\sigma}(\mathrm{Ad}_{A,A^{*}})=\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt[\ell]{\left\|\mathrm{Ad}_{A,A^{*}}^{\circ\ell}(I_{\mathcal{H}})\right\|}.

Hence, since

\frac{1}{R_{h}}=\limsup_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt[2\ell]{\sum_{|\omega|=\ell}|\hat{h}_{\omega}|^{2}},

if the joint spectral radius of
A\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H})^{1\times d}
is less than
R_{h}
, we have absolute convergence of the power series
h(A)
(in operator norm).

The assumption that
\mathscr{A}
is a unital
C^{*}-
algebra is, however, a more restrictive assumption than necessary to make sense of evaluations of
h\in\mathscr{O}_{d}
in
\mathscr{A}
. To this end, we introduce a larger class of algebras,
local operator algebras
, based on the construction of
local operator spaces
in
[
15
,
53
]
. In particular, any inverse limit of operator algebras, such as
\mathscr{O}_{d}(R)
,
r>0
, will be a local operator algebra.

Definition 6.2
.

Let
\mathscr{A}
be a unital algebra over
\mathbb{C}
. A
matrix semi-norm
over
\mathscr{A}
is a collection of semi-norms,
\rho=(\rho_{n}:\mathscr{A}^{n\times n}\rightarrow[0,+\infty))_{n=1}^{\infty}
satisfying the axioms:

1.

Given any
a\in\mathscr{A}^{m\times m}
and
b\in\mathscr{A}^{n\times n}
,

\rho_{n+m}(a\oplus b)=\max\left\{\rho_{m}(a),\rho_{n}(b)\right\}.

2.

For any
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
,
\beta\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times m}
and
a\in\mathscr{A}^{n\times n}
,

\rho_{m}(\alpha a\beta)\leq\|\alpha\|\rho_{n}(a)\|\beta\|.

We will say that multiplication in
\mathscr{A}
is
\rho-
completely contractive
, or that
\rho
is an algebra matrix semi-norm if it further obeys a third axiom:

3.

For any
A,B\in\mathscr{A}^{n\times n}
,

\rho_{n}(AB)\leq\rho_{n}(A)\cdot\rho_{n}(B).

Given an algebra matrix semi-norm,
\rho
, on
\mathscr{A}
, let

\mathscr{N}_{\rho}:=\{a\in\mathscr{A}|\ \rho_{1}(a)=0\}.

This is clearly a two-sided ideal in
\mathscr{A}
, so that the unit,
1
, of
\mathscr{A}
belongs to
N_{\rho}
if and only if
\rho
is identically
0
. Hence, assuming
\rho\neq 0
,
A_{\rho}:=A/N_{\rho}
is again a unital and stably finite algebra with well-defined multiplication,

(a+N_{\rho})(b+N_{\rho})=ab+N_{\rho}.

Moreover, if we define

\|a+N_{\rho}\|_{\rho}:=\rho(a),

then
\|\cdot\|_{\rho}
is a matrix-norm on
\mathscr{A}_{\rho}
in the sense of Ruan, and moreover if multiplication in
\mathscr{A}
is completely
\rho-
contractive then for any
A,B\in\mathscr{A}_{\rho}^{n\times n}
,

\|A\cdot B\|_{\rho}\leq\|A\|_{\rho}\|B\|_{\rho}=\rho_{n}(A)\rho_{n}(B),

so that “multiplication is completely contractive” and
(A_{\rho},\|\cdot\|_{\rho})
is an abstract operator algebra in sense of Blecher, Ruan and Sinclair
[
33
, Corollary 16.7]
. We will further assume that our matrix semi-norms are ‘unital’ in the sense that
\rho(1)=1
. In this case
(A_{\rho},\|\cdot\|_{\rho})
is a unital abstract operator algebra, and hence is completely isometrically isomorphic to a unital algebra of operators on a Hilbert space by the Blecher–Ruan–Sinclair theorem.

Definition 6.3
.

A (unital)
local operator algebra
is a unital, separable, Hausdorff and complete topological algebra,
\mathscr{A}
, over
\mathbb{C}
, equipped with a family of unital and matrix-multiplicative semi-norms that generate the topology on
\mathscr{A}
.

The inverse limit of any family of unital operator algebras will be a local operator algebra. For example, each of the ball semifirs of uniformly analytic NC functions constructed in this paper,
\mathscr{O}_{d}(R)
,
R>0
, are local operator algebras. Conversely, any local operator algebra can be viewed as an inverse limit of abstract operator algebras
[
53
]
. If
\mathscr{A}
is a local operator algebra,
a=(a_{1},\cdots,a_{d})\in\mathscr{A}^{1\times d}
and
h\in\mathscr{O}_{d}
, the free polynomial partial sums,
h_{n}(a)
then converge to an element,
h(a)
, in
\mathscr{A}
. Indeed, to prove this, one need only show that given any
\rho\in\Lambda
, that the sequence
h_{n}(a+N_{\rho})
is Cauchy in the abstract, unital and separable operator algebra
A/N_{\rho}
, and this follows immediately from
[
41
, Theorem 1.1]
, since
h
has infinite Cauchy–Hadamard radius of convergence and we can view
A/N_{\rho}
as an algebra of operators on a separable, complex Hilbert space. That is, if
\mathscr{A}
is a unital local operator algebra, then for any
d-
tuple
a=(a_{1},\cdots,a_{d})\in\mathscr{A}^{1\times d}
, the unital homomorphism,
\varphi_{a}:\mathscr{O}_{d}\rightarrow\mathscr{A}
is well-defined. Hence, if a unital local operator algebra is also stably-finite, Theorem
6.1
applies and shows that we have well-defined
\mathscr{A}-
domains and evaluations for any
f\in\mathscr{M}_{d}
.

6.1
Strong convergence of meromorphic functions

Let
(\mathcal{A},\tau)
be a tracial
C^{*}-
algebra,
i.e.
a unital
C^{*}-
algebra equipped with a faithful and normalized (unital) trace,
\tau\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
. Recall from
[
20
]
that a tuple of elements
s_{1},\cdots,s_{d}\in\mathcal{A}
, is called
free
, if for every
p_{1},\cdots,p_{n}\in\mathbb{C}\langle\mathfrak{z}\rangle
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and every word
\omega=i_{1}\cdots i_{n}\in\mathbb{W}_{d}
, obeying
i_{k}\neq i_{k+1}
, the assumption that
\tau(p_{j}(s_{i_{j}}))=0
implies that
\tau(p_{1}(s_{i_{1}})p_{2}(s_{i_{2}})\cdots p_{n}(s_{i_{n}}))=0
. Such a free family,
s=(s_{1},\cdots,s_{d})
, is called a
free semicircular family
, if for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
and every
1\leq j\leq d
, we have both
s_{j}=s_{j}^{*}
and

\tau(s_{j}^{k})=\begin{cases}\frac{2}{k+2}\binom{k}{k/2},&k\text{ is even};\\
0,&k\text{ is odd}.\end{cases}

Let us fix a free semicircular tuple
s_{1},\ldots,s_{d}
. Let
\sgrm(n,\tfrac{1}{n})
denote the class of all
n\times n
self-adjoint random matrices on a probability space
\Omega
with independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) Gaussian entries with mean
0
and variance
\frac{1}{n}
. The normalization of the variance goes back to Voiculescu
[
51
]
. In
[
20
, Theorem A]
Haagerup and Thorbjørnsen proved that if we take a sequence
(X_{1}^{(n)},\cdots,X_{d}^{(n)})
of
d
-tuples of independent elements of
\sgrm(n,\tfrac{1}{n})
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then there exists a measure zero set
\Xi\subset\Omega
, such that for every
p\in\mathbb{C}\langle z\rangle
and every
\omega\in\Omega\setminus\Xi
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\|p(X_{1}^{(n)}(\omega),\cdots,X_{d}^{(n)}(\omega)\right\|=\|p(s_{1},\cdots,s_{d})\|.

This result was extended to matrix-valued polynomials by Haagerup, Schultz, and Thorbjørnsen in
[
19
]
. Using the terminology of
[
54
]
, we will say that the sequence
(X_{1}^{(n)},\cdots,X_{d}^{(n)})

converges strongly
to the free semicircular tuple
s_{1},\cdots,s_{n}
. In
[
54
, Corollary 1]
, Yin showed that if
\mathfrak{r}
is a rational expression with the tuple
(s_{1},\cdots,s_{d})
in its domain, then for
n
big enough and almost every
\omega\in\Omega
,
(X_{1}^{(n)}(\omega),\cdots,X_{d}^{(n)}(\omega)
lies in the domain of
\mathfrak{r}
and

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\|\mathfrak{r}(X_{1}^{(n)}(\omega),\cdots,X_{d}^{(n)}(\omega))\right\|=\|\mathfrak{r}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{d})\|.

It follows that,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{n}\tr(\mathfrak{r}(X_{1}^{(n)},\ldots,X_{d}^{(n)})\right)=\tau(\mathfrak{r}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})).

Here
\mathbb{E}
stands for the expectation of a random variable. This result extends the result of Haagerup and Thorbjørnsen to the free skew field. Our goal is to extend these results even further to the universal skew field of fractions,
\mathscr{M}_{d}
, of
\mathscr{O}_{d}
. Note that by the result of Haagerup and Thorbjørnsen, the row tuple,
(X_{1}^{(n)}(\omega),\ldots,X_{d}^{(n)}(\omega))
is, almost surely, in a row ball of bounded radius. We start with a simple lemma.

Lemma 6.4
.

Assume that
k=1
. Let
f\in\mathscr{O}_{d}^{k\times k}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, for almost every
\omega\in\Omega
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\|f(X_{1}^{(n)}(\omega),\cdots,X_{d}^{(n)}(\omega)\right\|=\|f(s_{1},\cdots,s_{d})\|.

Proof.

Any such
f
is given by a free FPS expansion of
f
about
0
,

f(\mathfrak{z})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{|\alpha|=k}\hat{f}_{\alpha}\mathfrak{z}^{\alpha}.

Let
p_{m}=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\sum_{|\alpha|=k}\hat{f}_{\alpha}\mathfrak{z}^{\alpha}
. Then, this sequence of free polynomial partial sums,
p_{m}
, converges uniformly to
f
on any NC row-ball of finite radii. Now by
[
20
, Theorem A]
, for almost all
\omega\in\Omega
and all
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we have that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\|p_{m}(X_{1}^{(n)}(\omega),\cdots,X_{d}^{(n)}(\omega))\right\|=\|p_{m}(s_{1},\cdots,s_{m})\|.

We see that

\displaystyle\left|\|f(X_{1}^{(n)}(\omega),\cdots,X_{d}^{(n)}(\omega))\|-\|f(s_{1},\cdots,s_{d})\|\right|\leq\left\|f(X_{1}^{(n)}(\omega),\ldots,X_{d}^{(n)}(\omega))-p_{m}(X_{1}^{(n)}(\omega),\cdots,X_{d}^{(n)}(\omega))\right\|

\displaystyle+\left|\|p_{m}(X_{1}^{(n)}(\omega),\cdots,X_{d}^{(n)}(\omega))\|-\|p_{m}(s_{1},\cdots,s_{d})\|\right|+\|p_{m}(s_{1},\cdots,s_{d})-f(s_{1},\cdots,s_{d})\|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m\rightarrow\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}0.

One argues similarly in the matrix-valued case.
∎

The following lemma is a slight modification of the corresponding result in
[
54
]
to the setting at hand.

Lemma 6.5
.

For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
and every full matrix
F\in\mathscr{O}_{d}^{k\times k}
, if
F(s_{1},\cdots,s_{d})
is invertible, then for almost every
\omega\in\Omega
for
n
sufficiently large,
F(X_{1}^{(n)}(\omega),\ldots,X_{d}^{(n)}(\omega))
is invertible.

Proof.

We note that there is a natural anti-holomorphic involution on the free algebra
\mathbb{C}\langle\mathfrak{z}\rangle
, such that the variables are self-adjoint (namely,
\mathfrak{z}_{j}^{*}=\mathfrak{z}_{j}
for all
1\leq j\leq d
.) The involution is given by transposing the monomials and taking the complex conjugate of the coefficients. This involution extends naturally to
\mathscr{O}_{d}
and to matrices over
\mathscr{O}_{d}
. Let
F\in\mathscr{O}_{d}^{k\times k}
, then
F^{*}F\in\mathscr{O}_{d}^{k\times k}
is self-adjoint. Moreover, for every tuple of self-adjoint elements
Y_{1},\cdots,Y_{d}\in B(\mathcal{H})
, we have that
(F^{*}F)(Y_{1},\ldots Y_{d})=F(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{d})^{*}F(Y_{1},\cdots,Y_{d})
. To simplify notations, let
T=F(s_{1},\cdots,s_{d})
and
T_{n}(\omega)=F(X_{1}^{(n)}(\omega),\ldots,X_{d}^{(n)}(\omega))
. We note that a self-adjoint operator
A\in B(\mathcal{H})
is invertible if and only if
A^{*}A
is invertible if and only if
\big\lVert\lVert A^{2}\rVert I-A^{*}A\big\rVert<\|A\|^{2}
. By the previous lemma
\|T_{n}(\omega)\|\to\|T\|
almost surely. Moreover, almost surely

\lim_{n\to\infty}\Big\lVert\lVert T\rVert^{2}I-T_{n}(\omega)^{*}T_{n}(\omega)\Big\rVert=\Big\lVert\lVert T\rVert^{2}I-T^{*}T\Big\rVert

Now

\displaystyle\Big\lVert\lVert T\rVert^{2}I-T_{n}(\omega)^{*}T_{n}(\omega)\Big\rVert

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Big\lvert\lVert T_{n}(\omega)\rVert^{2}-\lVert T\rVert^{2}\Big\rvert+\Big\lVert\lVert T\rVert^{2}I-T_{n}(\omega)^{*}T_{n}(\omega)\Big\rVert

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n\rightarrow\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}

\displaystyle\Big\lVert\lVert T\rVert^{2}I-T^{*}T\Big\rVert<\lVert T\rVert^{2}.

Therefore, for almost every
\omega
and
n
big enough,
\left\lVert\lVert T_{n}(\omega)\rVert^{2}I-T_{n}(\omega)^{*}T_{n}(\omega)\right\rVert<\lVert T_{n}\rVert^{2}
and
T_{n}
is invertible.
∎

The following lemma is an immediate consequence of the fact that the group of invertible operators on a complex Hilbert space is operator-norm open and the inversion map is uniformly continuous on this group.

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
F\in\mathscr{O}_{d}^{k\times k}
be a full matrix. Let
Q_{m}\in\mathbb{C}\langle\mathfrak{z}\rangle^{k\times k}
be the partial sums of the FPS expansion of
F
around the origin. Then, for every
X=(X_{1},\cdots,X_{d})\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H})^{d}
, such that
F(X)
is invertible,
Q_{m}(X)
is invertible for
m
big enough. Moreover, for every such
X
, there exists
r>0
, such that
Q_{m}^{-1}\to F^{-1}
uniformly on the closed uniform NC row-ball of radius
r
centered at
X
.

It follows immediately that if
f=d-b^{t}A^{-1}c
is a rational expression in
\mathscr{M}_{d}
, then there exists a sequence of rational expressions
\mathfrak{r}_{m}
, such that
\mathfrak{r}_{m}\to f
uniformly on uniform balls centered at points in the domain of the rational expression. Indeed, we can approximate
b
,
c
, and
d
uniformly on subballs by polynomial vectors and the previous lemma takes care of the rest.

Theorem 6.7
.

Let
f\in\mathscr{M}_{d}
and assume that
(s_{1},\cdots,s_{d})
are in the domain of
f
. Then, for almost every
\omega\in\Omega
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\|f(X_{1}^{(n)}(\omega),\ldots,X_{d}^{(n)}(\omega))\right\|=\|f(s_{1},\ldots,s_{d})\|.

Proof.

By Theorem
6.1
and its preceding discussion, we can choose a representation of
f=b^{*}A^{-1}c
, such that
b,c\in\mathbb{C}^{k}
,
A\in\mathscr{O}_{d}^{k\times k}
is full, and
A(s_{1},\ldots,s_{d})
is invertible. By Lemma
6.5
,
A(X_{1}^{(n)}(\omega),\cdots,X_{d}^{(n)}(\omega))
is invertible, for almost every
\omega
and
n
big enough. Now fix
\omega\in\Omega\setminus\Xi
, where
\Xi
is the measure
0
set that we must remove to ensure convergence. By the observation preceding the statement of the theorem, there exists a sequence of rationals
\mathfrak{r}_{m}
that converges to
f
uniformly on uniform balls centered at points in the domain of
f
. It remains to perform a calculation. For
m
big enough, the following inequality makes sense

\displaystyle\left\lvert\lVert f(s_{1},\cdots,s_{d})\rVert-\lVert f(X_{1}^{(n)}(\omega),\cdots,X_{d}^{(n)}(\omega))\rVert\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\lVert f(s_{1},\cdots,s_{d})-\mathfrak{r}_{m}(s_{1},\cdots,s_{d})\rVert

\displaystyle+\left\lvert\lVert\mathfrak{r}_{m}(s_{1},\cdots,s_{d})\rVert-\lVert\mathfrak{r}_{m}(X_{1}^{(n)}(\omega),\cdots,X_{d}^{(n)}(\omega))\rVert\right\rvert

\displaystyle+\lVert\mathfrak{r}_{m}(X_{1}^{(n)}(\omega),\cdots,X_{d}^{(n)}(\omega))-f(X_{1}^{(n)}(\omega),\cdots,X_{d}^{(n)}(\omega))\rVert.

Given
\varepsilon>0
, we can choose
m
big enough, so that

\left\lvert\|f(s_{1},\cdots,s_{d})\|-\|f(X_{1}^{(n)}(\omega),\cdots,X_{d}^{(n)}(\omega))\|\right\rvert\leq\varepsilon+\left\lvert\lVert\mathfrak{r}_{m}(s_{1},\cdots,s_{d})\rVert-\lVert\mathfrak{r}_{m}(X_{1}^{(n)}(\omega),\cdots,X_{d}^{(n)}(\omega))\rVert\right\rvert.

By the result of Yin
[
54
]
, letting
n
tend to infinity, we obtain

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\left\lvert\lVert f(s_{1},\ldots,s_{d})\rVert-\lVert f(X_{1}^{(n)}(\omega),\ldots,X_{d}^{(n)}(\omega))\rVert\right\rvert\leq\varepsilon.

Since this holds for every
\varepsilon>0
, the theorem is proven.
∎

As in
[
54
]
, this has the immediate consequence:

Corollary 6.8
.

Given any
f\in\mathscr{M}_{d}
so that
(s_{1},\cdots,s_{d})
are in the domain of
f
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{n}\tr\,f(X_{1}^{(n)},\cdots,X_{d}^{(n)})\right)=\tau\left(f(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})\right).

7
Outlook

We leave several interesting questions open. One natural question arises from the fact that the Fréchet topology on
\mathscr{O}_{d}
, viewed as an inverse limit of NC disk algebras, is independent of the chosen operator space structure on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. Here, we have chosen to work with the row operator space structure over
\mathbb{C}^{d}
to exploit non-commutative Hardy space techniques, since the free Hardy space can be viewed as a Hilbert space of uniformly analytic NC functions on the NC unit row-ball centred at
0\in\mathbb{C}^{1\times d}
. However, our methods depend crucially on the ‘row operator space structure’ that is implicit in the definition of Popescu’s NC disk algebra as NC functions in the NC unit row-ball. (The column operator space structure for
\mathbb{C}^{d}
would work equally well.) If
\mathcal{E}
is an operator space structure on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, then we denote by
\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{E}}^{d}
the NC set that is the open unit ball of
\mathcal{E}
. The algebras
\mathbb{H}^{\infty}(\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{E}}^{d})
and
\mathbb{A}_{d}(\mathcal{E})
were studied by Sampat and Shalit
[
44
,
45
]
. One can ask whether the algebras
\mathscr{O}(R\cdot\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{E}}^{d})
given by NC functions that are uniformly bounded on
r\cdot\overline{\mathbb{B}_{\mathcal{E}}^{d}}
for every
0<r<R
are semifirs that satisfy an analogue of the analytic Bergman Nullstelensatz.

A second natural question is whether every closed right (respectively, left) ideal in
\mathscr{O}_{d}
is free as a right (respectively, left)
\mathscr{O}_{d}
-module in the sense of completed tensor products as in
[
47
]
.

As described in Section
5
, one of the main problems we leave open is whether elements of the universal skew fields,
\mathscr{M}_{d}(R)
, of “NC meromorphic expressions”, can actually be identified with bona fide non-commutative functions. Namely, we have two natural questions.

Question 7.1
.

Recall that
\mathscr{O}_{d}(0)
is the semifir of uniformly analytic NC germs at
0
.
Is the natural embedding
\mathscr{O}_{d}(R)\hookrightarrow\mathscr{O}_{d}(r)
for
r<R\in[0,+\infty]
(totally inert, hence) honest?

Question 7.2
.

Do there exist non-trival “meromorphic identies” in
\mathscr{M}_{d}(R)
,
R\in(0,+\infty]
,
i.e.
non-zero elements of
\mathscr{M}_{d}(R)
which vanish on their domains in
\mathbb{C}^{(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N})\cdot d}
, or are undefined on any uniformly open subset of
R\cdot\mathbb{B}^{(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N})\cdot d}
?

In
[
6
]
, the authors have introduced a second skew field of NC meromorphic functions,
\mathscr{M}_{d}^{\mathscr{C}}
. This is the universal skew field of fractions of the semifir,
\mathscr{O}_{d}^{\mathscr{C}}
of all NC functions that admit a compact realization,
(A,b,c)\in\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{H})^{d}\times\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}
, where
\mathcal{H}
is a complex, separable Hilbert space and
\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{H})
denotes the compact linear operators on
\mathcal{H}
. That is, given any
h\in\mathscr{O}_{d}^{\mathscr{C}}
, given by the compact realization
(A,b,c)
, and any
Z\in\mathbb{C}^{(n\times n)\cdot d}
for which the monic, affine linear pencil,
L_{A}(Z):=I_{n}\otimes I_{\mathcal{H}}-\sum_{j=1}^{d}Z_{j}\otimes A_{j}
, is invertible,

h(Z)=I_{n}\otimes b^{*}L_{A}(Z)^{-1}I_{n}\otimes c.

By
[
6
, Theorem 4.5 and Corollary 4.4]
, any free formal power series,
f\in\mathbb{C}\langle\!\langle\mathfrak{z}\rangle\!\rangle
, is a uniformly entire NC function in
\mathscr{O}_{d}
if and only if it has a compact and jointly quasinilpotent realization,
(A,b,c)
. That is, each component operator,
A_{j}\in\mathscr{C}(\mathcal{H})
is compact and the completely positive map,
\mathrm{Ad}_{A,A^{*}}:\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H})\rightarrow\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H})
, of adjunction by the
A_{j}
and
A_{j}^{*}
, is quasinilpotent as an element of the unital Banach algebra,
\mathscr{B}(X)
, where
X:=\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H})
. In particular,
\mathscr{O}_{d}\subsetneqq\mathscr{O}_{d}^{\mathscr{C}}
. It then follows from the standard realization algorithm for sums, products and inverses of NC functions with realizations that a univariate formal power series is the Taylor series of a globally meromorphic function (which is analytic at
0
) if and only if it has a compact realization
[
6
, Theorem 5.1 and Theorem 5.3]
. This motivates the interpretation of
\mathscr{O}_{d}^{\mathscr{C}}
, the semifir of all NC functions with compact realizations, as the ring of globally meromorphic NC functions with analytic germs at
0
, as well as the interpretation of its universal skew field,
\mathscr{M}_{d}^{\mathscr{C}}
as the skew field of globally meromorphic NC functions. Here, elements of
\mathscr{M}_{d}^{\mathscr{C}}
can indeed be identified with bona fide uniformly analytic NC functions whose domains are uniformly open subsets that are level-wise analytic-Zariski dense at all sufficiently high levels in the NC universe, see
[
6
, Section 6.1 and Theorem 6.4]
.

In one-variable, it is not difficult to see, from the results of
[
6
]
, that these two definitions of the field of globally meromorphic functions are the same,
i.e.
that
\mathscr{M}_{1}=\mathscr{M}_{1}^{\mathscr{C}}
. Therefore, it would be very interesting to know whether these two skew fields of “NC meromorphic functions”,
\mathscr{M}_{d}
and
\mathscr{M}_{d}^{\mathscr{C}}
are actually the same field for any
d\in\mathbb{N}
. Since, as discussed above, any
h\in\mathscr{O}_{d}
has a compact and jointly quasinilpotent realization, we have an embedding
\mathscr{O}_{d}\hookrightarrow\mathscr{O}_{d}^{\mathscr{C}}
. If it could be proven that this embedding, or the embedding
\mathscr{O}_{d}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}\langle\!\langle\mathfrak{z}\rangle\!\rangle
is honest, it would follow that
\mathscr{M}_{d}\hookrightarrow\mathscr{M}_{d}^{\mathscr{C}}
, so that
\mathscr{M}_{d}
could be identified with a skew sub-field of
\mathscr{M}_{d}^{\mathscr{C}}
. A special case of a classical question posed by Henri Poincaré, and ultimately answered, in the affirmative by the positive solution to the Cousin problems, is whether any globally meromorphic function in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
is a ratio of entire functions
[
31
]
. Thus, we arrive at a “NC Poincaré problem:”

Question 7.3
.

For any
d\in\mathbb{N}
, is
\mathscr{M}_{d}=\mathscr{M}_{d}^{\mathscr{C}}
?
That is, is any uniformly meromorphic NC function in
\mathscr{M}_{d}^{\mathscr{C}}
equal to an NC rational expression in NC entire functions?
