Reconstruction of potential and damping coefficients in a semi-linear wave equation
Rahul Bhardwaj, Mandeep Kumar, Manmohan Vashisth

Abstract
. In this article, we investigate an inverse problem for a semi-linear wave equation posed in ℝ n + 1 \mathbb{R}^{n+1} , with n ≥ 2 n\geq 2 . Our primary objective is to reconstruct the damping coefficient, the linear potential, and the nonlinear potential from the associated Dirichlet-to-Neumann map. The analysis is based on a higher-order linearization method. As a key step, we establish the existence of suitable asymptotic solutions, crucial for reconstructing the nonlinear potential. In addition, we also provide a detailed study of the corresponding forward problem. Keywords. Wave equation, inverse problems, reconstruction, asymptotic solutions. Mathematics Subject Classification (2020) : 35R30, 35L05, 44A12



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Mathematical setup

Let
\Omega
be an open, connected and bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 2
, with a smooth boundary denoted by
\partial\Omega
. For
T>0
, we define the space-time domain
\Omega_{T}:=(0,T)\times\Omega
and denote its lateral boundary by
\Sigma:=(0,T)\times\partial\Omega
. Consider the following initial-boundary value problem (IBVP) for the semi-linear wave equation with lower order perturbations,

(1.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Box u(t,x)+a(x)\partial_{t}u(t,x)+b(x)u(t,x)+q(x)u^{2}(t,x)=0,&(t,x)\in\Omega_{T},\\
u(t,x)=f(t,x),&(t,x)\in\Sigma,\\
u(0,x)=0,~~\partial_{t}u(0,x)=0,&\quad x\in\Omega,\end{cases}

where
\Box
is the standard wave operator
\partial_{t}^{2}-\Delta_{x}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
,
a
represents the damping coefficient, and
b
and
q
respectively represent the linear and nonlinear potentials.

Our objective is to reconstruct the
coefficients
a
,
b
, and
q

from the boundary measurements given by the Dirichlet-to-Neumann (DN) map. The DN map, denoted by
\Lambda
, is defined by

(1.2)

\displaystyle\Lambda:E_{\delta}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow H^{m}(\Sigma),

(1.3)

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\longmapsto\partial_{\nu}u_{f}\big|_{\Sigma},

where
\partial_{\nu}u_{f}=\nu\cdot\nabla u_{f}
where
\nu
denotes the outward unit normal and
u_{f}
is the unique solution of IBVP (
1.1
) corresponding to Dirichlet data,
f
, on the lateral boundary
\Sigma
.

To ensure that the DN map is well-defined, we establish the well-posedness
of the (
1.1
) under suitable
compatibility conditions on the Dirichlet data
f
. More precisely, for
sufficiently small boundary data
f\in E_{\delta}
satisfying compatibility
conditions of order
m
, with
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
m>n+1
, we prove the
existence and uniqueness of a solution
u_{f}\in E_{m+1}
(see Theorem
2.3
in
Section
2.1
). Subsequently, we develop a reconstruction procedure that allows us to recover all these coefficients
a
,
b
, and
q
from the DN map.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded domain with smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
,
and let
T>\operatorname{diam}(\Omega)
.
Then the damping coefficient
a\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
and the potential terms
b,q\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
appearing in the semi-linear wave equation (
1.1
)
are uniquely reconstructed from knowledge of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map
\Lambda(f)
measured for all
f\in E_{\delta}
.

This result can be viewed as an extension of the reconstruction result established in
[
37
]
, where Lassas
et al.
investigated an IBVP for a semi-linear wave equation in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
,
n\geq 1
,
involving a time-dependent nonlinear potential without linear
potential and damping terms. Using knowledge of the DN map, they
established the reconstruction results for the nonlinear potential; however, due to the time dependence of the coefficient, the reconstruction was limited to a compact subset of the domain.

1.2.
Physical Significance and Motivation

Wave phenomena are ubiquitous in nature and occur across a wide range of physical systems, including acoustics, electromagnetism, elasticity, seismology, and fluid dynamics. The mathematical model considered in this work describes the essential features of wave propagation in a medium that can absorb energy, vary in space, and respond nonlinearly to wave amplitude.
Specifically, we study the real-valued function
u
governed by

(1.4)

\displaystyle\Box u(t,x)+a(x)\partial_{t}u(t,x)+b(x)u(t,x)+q(x)u^{2}(t,x)=0,\quad\text{ in}\qquad(0,T)\times\Omega

where the
\Box
operator plays a central role in wave propagation phenomena, as it naturally arises in the study of hyperbolic partial differential equations (PDEs) and their physical principles, such as causality and the finite speed of wave propagation. In addition, the coefficient functions
a
,
b
, and
q
describe the physical properties of the medium.

The term
a
represents a damping or attenuation effect, which can occur due to friction or absorption in the material.
The term
b
acts as a linear potential, describing a restoring or refractive effect arising from the resistance of the medium to deformation or its change in wave speed.
The coefficient
q
introduces a nonlinear potential, which allows the internal characteristics of the medium to change with position. This nonlinear term captures the amplitude-dependent behavior that arises in many physical systems. Equations (
1.4
) can be viewed as a generalized damped nonlinear
Klein–Gordon equation
(see
[
50
]
), which arises in quantum mechanics. These types of models have many applications. For example, in geophysics, it describes how seismic waves travel through the layers of the Earth, where stiffness and damping vary with depth. From the perspective of inverse problems, measurements of the boundary contain valuable information about the coefficients
a
,
b
, and
q
. Reconstructing these parameters from the observed data helps reveal the hidden internal structure of the medium.

1.3.
Existing articles

Let us first recall some key developments in the study of inverse problems for linear PDEs. In the elliptic setting, the foundational work of Calderón
[
4
]
introduced, now known as the Calderón inverse problem, which aims to determine the electrical conductivity of a medium from boundary measurements. The question of uniquely recovering a time-independent scalar potential associated with linear parabolic and hyperbolic PDEs from boundary measurements was first addressed by Bukhgeim and Klibanov in
[
2
]
.

Inspired by the pioneering ideas of Sylvester and Uhlmann
[
59
]
about the Calderón inverse problem, Rakesh and Symes
[

[
55
]
. In
[
19
]
, Isakov investigated the simultaneous recovery of a potential and damping coefficients in the time-independent case for the wave equation, and later Isakov and Sun extended the uniqueness result for the stability aspect; see
[
18
]
. In the general geometric setting, the uniqueness of inverse problems for hyperbolic equations with time-independent coefficients was established by Eskin
[
9
,
10
]
. We also refer to the book
[
21
]
, in which Isakov studied various types of inverse problems. Over the past few decades, several works have emerged on the recovery of various coefficients in the linear hyperbolic equation through boundary measurement; see, for example,
[

,

,

,

and the references therein. These contributions have significantly advanced our understanding of uniqueness, stability, and reconstruction techniques for inverse problems in linear PDEs across various geometric settings, e.g. the study of time-dependent coefficients, bounded domains, and the infinite waveguide.

Most studies on inverse problems for nonlinear equations employ the so-called
higher-order linearization
method, originally introduced by Isakov
[
20
]
. A remarkable development by Kurylev, Lassas and Uhlmann
[
32
]
revealed that the presence of nonlinearity in a wave equation can be advantageous for the associated inverse problem.
By introducing the

, they proved that the source-to-solution map uniquely determines the global topology, differentiable structure, and conformal class of the metric on a globally hyperbolic Lorentzian manifold of dimension
3+1
.
Later, Hintz and Uhlmann
[

extended these results and proved that the DN map allows the recovery of both the Lorentzian metric and the nonlinear coefficient. For more general classes of nonlinear operators, Oksanen et al.
[

established global uniqueness results to determine coefficients in nonlinear real principal-type equations from two different

In the Euclidean setting, Lassas et al.
[

established the stable recovery of a time-dependent nonlinear potential that appears in a semi-linear wave equation using the DN map, and showed that the potential can be recovered in a compact subset of the domain.
In another related contribution, Lin et al.
[

studied an inverse boundary problem for a time-dependent semi-linear hyperbolic equation, where both the nonlinear term and the source are unknown. More recently, Xiang et al.
[
53

investigated the simultaneous recovery of multiple unknown nonlinear coefficients together with the source term for semi-linear wave equations, based on the measurement of the DN map. Furthermore, nonlinear inverse problems arising in various physical models such as the Westervelt, Jordan-Moore-Gibson-Thompson (JMGT), nonlinear elastic wave, and nonlinear progressive wave equations have been extensively investigated in recent years; see, for example,

,

. These studies collectively demonstrate that nonlinear effects can encode rich geometric and physical information, enabling the recovery of quantities and structures that remain inaccessible in purely linear inverse problems. We also refer to several works on inverse problems for other kind of nonlinear that employ the
higher-order linearization
technique; see,
for instance,

Motivated by the studies mentioned above, this work focuses on an inverse problem for a semi-linear wave equation that accounts for both damping and potential effects.
Specifically, our objective is to reconstruct the damping coefficient, the linear and nonlinear potential terms from the associated DN map.
Our analysis is based on the

2.
Preliminaries and Well-posedness for the semi-linear wave equation

This section is devoted to establishing the well-posedness of the IBVP (
1.1
). First, we set the notation and provide the definitions required for subsequent analysis.
Let
1\leq p\leq\infty
, and
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{\infty\}
.
We write
L^{p}(\Omega)
for the usual Lebesgue space and
W^{k,p}(\Omega)
for the Sobolev space of order
k
.
For
1\leq p<\infty
, equipped these spaces with the norms

(2.1)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}:=\left(\int_{\Omega}|u(x)|^{p}\,dx\right)^{1/p},\qquad\|u\|_{W^{k,p}(\Omega)}:=\left(\sum_{|\alpha|\leq k}\|\partial^{\alpha}u\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{\,p}\right)^{1/p},

where
\alpha
is a multi-index and
\partial^{\alpha}u
denotes the weak derivative of
u
of order
\alpha
.
For
p=\infty
, we consider the norms

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}:=\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{x\in\Omega}|u(x)|,\qquad\|u\|_{W^{k,\infty}(\Omega)}:=\max_{|\alpha|\leq k}\|\partial^{\alpha}u\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}.

For
p=2
, we denote
H^{k}(\Omega):=W^{k,2}(\Omega)
.
It is a Hilbert space equipped with the inner product

(2.2)

\displaystyle\langle u,v\rangle_{H^{k}(\Omega)}:=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq k}\int_{\Omega}\partial^{\alpha}u(x)\,\overline{\partial^{\alpha}v(x)}\,dx,\quad\text{ for }\qquad u,v\in H^{k}(\Omega).

Moreover, we denote

\displaystyle H^{m}_{0}(\Omega):=\left\{f\in H^{m}(\Omega)\;:\;\partial^{\alpha}f=0\quad\text{on}\ \partial\Omega\ \text{ for }|\alpha|\leq m-1\right\}.

Next, we define time involved Banach spaces. Let
X
be a Banach space. Define

\displaystyle L^{p}\left(0,T;X\right):=\left\{f:[0,T]\to X\text{ strongly measurable }:\;\int_{0}^{T}\|f(t,\cdot)\|^{p}_{X}\,dt<\infty\right\}

equipped with the norm

\displaystyle\|f\|^{p}_{L^{p}(0,T;X)}:=\int_{0}^{T}\|f(t,\cdot)\|^{p}_{X}\,dt.

For an integer
m\geq 0
, define the Sobolev space

\displaystyle H^{m}\left(0,T;X\right):=\left\{f:[0,T]\to X\,:\,\partial_{t}^{j}f\in L^{2}(0,T;X)\text{ for all }j=0,1,\dots,m\right\}

with the norm

\displaystyle\|f\|_{H^{m}(0,T;X)}:=\left(\sum_{j=0}^{m}\|\partial_{t}^{j}f\|_{L^{2}(0,T;X)}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Following
[
45
, Chapter 1, Theorem 2.3]
, we have the continuous embedding

\displaystyle H^{m}(\Omega_{T})\hookrightarrow H^{m}\!\left(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)\right)\cap L^{2}\!\left(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega)\right),

and the equivalent norm for the space
H^{m}(\Omega_{T})
is given by

\displaystyle\|u\|_{H^{m}(\Omega_{T})}=\|u\|_{H^{m}\!\left(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)\right)}+\|u\|_{L^{2}\!\left(0,T;H^{m}(\Omega)\right)}.

Moreover, we denote

\displaystyle H^{m}_{0}(\Omega_{T})=\left\{f\in H^{m}(\Omega_{T})\;:\;\partial_{t}^{j}f\big|_{t=0}\in H_{0}^{m-j}(\Omega)\ \text{for }j=0,\dots,m-1\right\},

and the Sobolev space at the lateral boundary is given by

\displaystyle H^{m}(\Sigma):=H^{m}\left(0,T;L^{2}(\partial\Omega)\right)\cap L^{2}\left(0,T;H^{m}(\partial\Omega)\right).

In particular, when only time regularity is needed, we abbreviate

\displaystyle H^{m}_{0}\left(0,T;H^{k}(\Sigma)\right):=\{f\in H^{m}(0,T;H^{k}(\Sigma)):\partial_{t}^{j}f\big|_{t=0}=0\text{ in }H^{k}(\Sigma)\ \text{for }j=0,\dots,m-1\}.

Next, for integers
m\geq k\geq 0
, define the space

\displaystyle C^{k}\left([0,T];H^{m-k}(\Omega)\right):=\left\{\,u:[0,T]\to H^{m-k}(\Omega)\;:\;\partial_{t}^{j}u\in C\left([0,T];H^{m-k}(\Omega)\right)\ \text{for }j=0,\dots,k\right\},

with the standard norm

\displaystyle\|u\|_{C^{k}([0,T];H^{m-k})}:=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\ \sup_{t\in[0,T]}\,\|\partial_{t}^{\,j}u(t,\cdot)\|_{H^{m-k}(\Omega)},

and the energy space

(2.3)

\displaystyle E_{m}:=\bigcap_{k=0}^{m}C^{k}\left([0,T];H^{m-k}(\Omega)\right),

which is a Banach space with the norm

(2.4)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{E_{m}}^{2}:=\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\bigl\|\partial_{t}^{\,k}u(t,\cdot)\bigr\|_{H^{\,m-k}(\Omega)}^{2}.

For
m>n+1
, the Sobolev embedding implies that
E_{m}
becomes an algebra, see
[
3
, Definition 3.5]
. Moreover, it is continuously multiplicative in the sense that there exists
C_{m}>0
(constant depending on
m
only) such that

(2.5)

\displaystyle\|\Phi\Psi\|_{E_{m}}\leq C_{m}\|\Phi\|_{E_{m}}\,\|\Psi\|_{E_{m}},\quad\text{for all }\,\,\Phi,\Psi\in E_{m}.

For given
m\geq 0
and
M>0
, we introduce the admissible class for the damping and potential coefficients, denoted by
\mathcal{A}
, defined as follows

(2.6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(M,m):=\left\{h\in H^{m}(\Omega)\;:\;\|h\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}\leq M\right\}.

We require this admissible set to prove the estimate results in the forward problem, where a prior bound on the damping coefficient
a
and the potential coefficients
b
and
q
will be needed later.

Furthermore, for a positive integer
m
, we define the following function space

(2.7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{m+1}=\left\{f\in H^{m+1}(\Sigma)\;:\;f\in H_{0}^{m+1-k}(0,T;H^{k}(\Sigma)),\,\text{for}\,\,k=0,1,\dots,m\right\},

this is a boundary trace class compatible with
H^{m}
on
\Sigma
, and for a positive
\delta
, however small enough, define a set

(2.8)

\displaystyle E_{\delta}:=\left\{f\in\mathcal{N}_{m+1}\;:\;\|f\|_{H^{m+1}(\Sigma)}<\delta\right\}.

2.1.
Well-posedness of the semi-linear wave equation

In this subsection, we establish the well-posedness of the semi-linear wave equation described by IBVP (
1.1
). We start with recalling the well-posedness result for the following IBVP associated with the inhomogeneous linear wave equation with potential

(2.9)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Box v(t,x)+b(x)\,v(t,x)=F(t,x),&(t,x)\in\Omega_{T},\\
v(t,x)=f(t,x),&(t,x)\in\Sigma,\\
v(0,x)=\varphi(x),\quad\partial_{t}v(0,x)=\psi(x),&x\in\Omega.\end{cases}

Lemma 2.1

(Well-posedness for linear equations with potential (
[
42
]
Lemma 3.2))
.

Let
m>n+1
and
T>0
. Assume
\varphi\in H^{m+1}_{0}(\Omega)
,
\psi\in H^{m}_{0}(\Omega)
,
f\in\mathcal{N}_{m+1}
,
b\in\mathcal{A}(M,m)
, and
F\in E_{m}
with
\partial_{t}^{\,k}F(0,\cdot)\in H^{m-k}_{0}(\Omega)
for
k=0,1,\dots,m-2
. Then (
2.9
) admits a unique solution

\displaystyle v\in E_{m+1},\qquad\partial_{\nu}v\in H^{m}(\Sigma),

and there exists
C>0
(depending on
m
,
\Omega
,
M
) such that

(2.10)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\|v\|_{E_{m+1}}+\|\partial_{\nu}v\|_{H^{m}(\Sigma)}\\
&\quad\leq C\,e^{CT}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{m}\|\partial_{t}^{\,k}F\|_{L^{1}(0,T;H^{m-k}(\Omega))}+\|\varphi\|_{H^{m+1}(\Omega)}+\|\psi\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}+\|f\|_{H^{m+1}(\Sigma)}\right).\end{aligned}

Next, we establish the well-posedness of the linear wave equation in the presence of both a potential and a damping term. This result is stated in the following Lemma.

Lemma 2.2

(Well-posedness for linear equations with potential and damping terms)
.

Let
m>n+1
and
T>0
. Assume
f\in\mathcal{N}_{m+1}
,
a,b\in\mathcal{A}(M,m)
, and
F_{1}\in E_{m}
with
\partial_{t}^{\,k}F_{1}(0,\cdot)\in H^{m-k}_{0}(\Omega)
for
k=0,1,\dots,m-1
. Then the following IBVP

(2.11)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Box v(t,x)+a(x)\,\partial_{t}v(t,x)+b(x)\,v(t,x)=F_{1}(t,x),&(t,x)\in\Omega_{T},\\
v(t,x)=f(t,x),&(t,x)\in\Sigma,\\
v(0,x)=0,\quad\partial_{t}v(0,x)=0,&\quad x\in\Omega,\end{cases}

admits a unique solution such that

\displaystyle v\in E_{m+1}
with
\displaystyle\partial_{\nu}v\in H^{m}(\Sigma)

and there exists
C>0
(depending on
m
,
\Omega
,

M
) such that

(2.12)

\displaystyle\|v\|_{E_{m+1}}+\|\partial_{\nu}v\|_{H^{m}(\Sigma)}\leq C\,e^{CT}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{m}\|\partial_{t}^{\,k}F_{1}\|_{L^{1}(0,T;H^{m-k}(\Omega))}+\|f\|_{H^{m+1}(\Sigma)}\right).

Proof.

We start by proving the local existence of a solution via the Banach fixed-point theorem. Then, using the structure of the equation, we iteratively extended the local solution to cover the entire time interval and obtained the global solution. We divide the proof into two steps. In the first step, we prove the local existence, and in the next step, we show the global existence from the local existence.

Step:1

Let us first introduce the closed subspace
\mathcal{K}_{[0,T]}
of
E_{m+1}
defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{[0,T]}:=\left\{\,v\in E_{m+1}\;:\;\partial_{t}^{k}v(0,\cdot)\in H^{m+1-k}_{0}(\Omega)\ \text{ for }k=0,1,\dots,m\,\right\},

and equipped with the norm

(2.13)

\|v\|_{\mathcal{K}_{[0,T]}}:=\|v\|_{E_{m+1}}+\sum_{k=0}^{m}\big\|\partial_{t}^{k}v\big\|_{C([0,T];H^{m+1-k}(\Omega))},

which is equivalent to the norm
E_{m+1}
defined in (
2.4
). Therefore, without loss of generality, we will use the norm
E_{m+1}
instead of (
2.13
).
Suppose
v\in\mathcal{K}_{[0,T]}
, we have
F_{1}-a\,\partial_{t}v\in E_{m}
and

\partial_{t}^{k}\left(F_{1}-a\,\partial_{t}v\right)(0,\cdot)
belong to
H^{m-k}_{0}(\Omega)
for
k=0,1,\dots,m-1
. Using the Lemma
2.1
, we have the following IBVP

(2.14)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Box w(t,x)+b(x)\,w(t,x)=F_{1}(t,x)-a(x)\,\partial_{t}v(t,x),&(t,x)\in\Omega_{T},\\
w(t,x)=f(t,x),&(t,x)\in\Sigma,\\
w(0,x)=0,\quad\partial_{t}w(0,x)=0,&\quad x\in\Omega,\end{cases}

has a unique solution

\displaystyle w\in E_{m+1},\qquad\partial_{\nu}w\in H^{m}(\Sigma).

Moreover, it satisfies the following estimate

(2.15)

\displaystyle\quad\|w\|_{E_{m+1}}+\|\partial_{\nu}w\|_{H^{m}(\Sigma)}\leq\;Ce^{CT}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{m}\big\|\partial_{t}^{k}(F_{1}-a\,\partial_{t}v)\big\|_{L^{1}(0,T;H^{m-k}(\Omega))}+\|f\|_{H^{m+1}(\Sigma)}\right).

Now, we
define the operator

(2.16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}:\mathcal{K}_{[0,T]}\to E_{m},\qquad\mathcal{L}(v)=w,

where
w
solves the IBVP (
2.14
) corresponding to
v\in\mathcal{K}_{[0,T]}
. By Lemma
2.1
, the map (
2.16
) is well-defined. Since
\partial_{t}^{k}\left(F_{1}-a\,\partial_{t}v\right)(0,\cdot)\in H^{m-k}_{0}(\Omega)
for
k=0,1,\dots,m-1
, the compatibility conditions are satisfied, and hence
w\in\mathcal{K}_{[0,T]}
, allowing us to redefine the map (
2.16
) by

(2.17)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}:\mathcal{K}_{[0,T]}\to\mathcal{K}_{[0,T]},\qquad\mathcal{L}(v)=w.

Next, we show that
\mathcal{L}
defined by (
2.17
) is a contraction map for some
T_{0}
that will be specified later,

(2.18)

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{L}(v_{1})-\mathcal{L}(v_{2})\|_{E_{m+1}}=\|w_{1}-w_{2}\|_{E_{m+1}}.

Before proceeding further, observe that
w_{1}-w_{2}
solves

(2.19)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Box\left(w_{1}-w_{2}\right)(t,x)+b(x)\,\left(w_{1}-w_{2}\right)(t,x)=-a(x)\,\partial_{t}(v_{1}-v_{2})(t,x),&(t,x)\in\Omega_{T},\\
\left(w_{1}-w_{2}\right)(t,x)=0,&(t,x)\in\Sigma,\\
\left(w_{1}-w_{2}\right)(0,x)=0,\quad\partial_{t}\left(w_{1}-w_{2}\right)(0,x)=0,&\quad x\in\Omega.\end{cases}

Using equation (
2.15
), we obtain the following energy estimates for
w_{1}-w_{2}

(2.20)

\displaystyle\quad\|w_{1}-w_{2}\|_{E_{m+1}}\leq\;Ce^{CT}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\big\|\partial_{t}^{k}(-a\,\partial_{t}(v_{1}-v_{2}))\big\|_{L^{1}(0,T;H^{m-k}(\Omega))}.

Therefore, from equation (
2.18
) and estimate (
2.20
), we get

(2.21)

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{L}(v_{1})-\mathcal{L}(v_{2})\|_{E_{m+1}}

\displaystyle\leq\;Ce^{CT}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\left\|\partial_{t}^{k}\left(a\,\partial_{t}(v_{1}-v_{2})\right)\right\|_{L^{1}(0,T;H^{m-k}(\Omega))}.

Next, we will use the estimate that for any
p\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we have

\|f\|_{L^{1}(0,T;H^{p}(\Omega))}\leq T\,\|f\|_{C([0,T];H^{p}(\Omega))}
and combine with the fact that
E_{m}
is an algebra (
2.5
), we have

(2.22)

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{L}(v_{1})-\mathcal{L}(v_{2})\right\|_{E_{m+1}}

\displaystyle\leq\;CTe^{CT}\|a\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\left\|\,\partial_{t}^{k+1}(v_{1}-v_{2})\right\|_{C([0,T];H^{m-k}(\Omega))}

(2.23)

\displaystyle\leq CTe^{CT}\|a\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{E_{m+1}}.

For
\mathcal{L}
to be a contraction, we need
T
to be such that

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
m>n+1
,
M>0
and
T>0
. Assume
f\in E_{\delta}
,
a
,
b
and
q\in\mathcal{A}(M,m)
with
\partial_{t}^{k}f(0,\cdot)
belong to
H_{0}^{m-k}(\Omega)
for
k=0,1,\dots,m-1
.
Then IBVP (
1.1
) admits a unique solution in the ball

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{R}(0):=\left\{u\in E_{m+1}\;:\;\|u\|_{E_{m+1}}<R\right\},

for some
R>0
. Moreover, there exists
C>0
(depending on
m
,
\Omega
,
M
) such that

(2.50)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{E_{m+1}}+\|\partial_{\nu}u\|_{H^{m}(\Sigma)}\leq C\,e^{CT}\|f\|_{H^{m+1}(\Sigma)}.

Proof.

For
R>0
, define the closed subspace
\mathcal{K}^{[0,R]}
of
E_{m+1}
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{[0,R]}:=\Big\{u\in E_{m+1}\;:\;\|u\|_{E_{m+1}}\leq R,\;\partial_{t}^{k}u(0,\cdot)\in H_{0}^{m-k}(\Omega),\ k=0,1,\dots,m-1\Big\},

that is equipped with the norm of
E_{m+1}
.
Let
u\in\mathcal{K}^{[0,R]}
, consider the following linear IBVP

(2.51)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Box v+a(x)\partial_{t}v+b(x)v=-q(x)u^{2},&(t,x)\in\Omega_{T},\\
v=f,&(t,x)\in\Sigma,\\
v(0,x)=0,\quad\partial_{t}v(0,x)=0,&x\in\Omega.\end{cases}

Since
u\in\mathcal{K}^{[0,R]}
, using the fact that
E_{m}
is a Banach algebra, we get
-qu^{2}\in E_{m}
, and the compatibility condition is also satisfied by the product rule for Sobolev functions. Therefore, using the Lemma
2.2
, the IBVP
(
2.51
) admits a unique solution

\displaystyle v\in E_{m+1},\qquad\partial_{\nu}v\in H^{m}(\Sigma),

together with the estimate

(2.52)

\displaystyle\|v\|_{E_{m+1}}+\|\partial_{\nu}v\|_{H^{m}(\Sigma)}\leq Ce^{CT}\Bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{m}\|\partial_{t}^{k}(qu^{2})\|_{L^{1}(0,T;H^{m-k}(\Omega))}+\|f\|_{H^{m+1}(\Sigma)}\Bigg).

Now, we define the operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{R}:\mathcal{K}^{[0,R]}\longrightarrow E_{m+1},\qquad\mathcal{L}^{R}(u)=v,

where
v
is the solution of the IBVP
(
2.51
) corresponding to a given

u\in\mathcal{K}^{[0,R]}
.
By the estimate (
2.52
), we obtain

(2.53)

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{L}^{R}(u)\|_{E_{m+1}}=\|v\|_{E_{m+1}}\leq Ce^{CT}\Bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{m}\|\partial_{t}^{k}(qu^{2})\|_{L^{1}(0,T;H^{m-k}(\Omega))}+\|f\|_{H^{m+1}(\Sigma)}\Bigg).

Using the Banach algebra property of
E_{m}
, we have the following further estimate

(2.54)

\displaystyle\|qu^{2}\|_{E_{m}}\leq\|q\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}\,\|u\|_{E_{m}}^{2}\leq R\,\|q\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}\,\|u\|_{E_{m}},

for all
u\in\mathcal{K}^{[0,R]}
.

Since
f\in E_{\delta}
and
q\in\mathcal{A}(M,m)
, combining
(
2.53
) and (
2.54
) yields

(2.55)

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{L}^{R}(u)\|_{E_{m+1}}\leq Ce^{CT}\bigl(\delta+MR^{2}\bigr).

Finally, choosing
R,\delta>0
sufficiently small such that

(2.56)

\displaystyle R<\frac{1}{2Ce^{CT}M},\qquad\delta\leq\frac{R}{2Ce^{CT}},

ensures that
\|\mathcal{L}^{R}(u)\|_{E_{m+1}}<R
for all

u\in\mathcal{K}^{[0,R]}
. Hence,
\mathcal{L}^{R}
maps

\mathcal{K}^{[0,R]}
to itself.
Next, we show that the map
\mathcal{L}^{R}
is a contraction map.
Let
u_{1},u_{2}\in\mathcal{K}^{[0,R]}
, and set
v_{i}=\mathcal{L}^{R}(u_{i})
,
i=1,2
.
Then
w:=v_{1}-v_{2}
satisfies

(2.57)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Box w+a(x)\partial_{t}w+b(x)w=-q(x)(u_{1}^{2}-u_{2}^{2}),&(t,x)\in\Omega_{T},\\
w=0,&(t,x)\in\Sigma,\\
w(0,x)=0,\quad\partial_{t}w(0,x)=0,&x\in\Omega.\end{cases}

Applying the estimate (
2.52
) to

yields

\displaystyle\|w\|_{E_{m+1}}+\|\partial_{\nu}w\|_{H^{m}(\Sigma)}

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{CT}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\|\partial_{t}^{k}(q(u_{1}^{2}-u_{2}^{2}))\|_{L^{1}(0,T;H^{m-k}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{CT}\|q\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}\|u_{1}^{2}-u_{2}^{2}\|_{E_{m+1}}

\displaystyle\leq 2Ce^{CT}R\|q\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{E_{m+1}}.

Thus, we have the following estimate

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{L}^{R}(u_{1})-\mathcal{L}^{R}(u_{2})\|_{E_{m+1}}\leq 2Ce^{CT}R\|q\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{E_{m+1}}.

As previously, we have chosen
R>0
(see (
2.56
)), so the map
\mathcal{L}^{R}
becomes a contraction. Hence, using the Banach fixed-point theorem, there exists a unique

u\in\mathcal{K}^{[0,R]}
such that
\mathcal{L}^{R}(u)=u
, and this fixed point is the unique solution of the semi-linear problem
(
2.51
), and satisfies

\displaystyle u\in E_{m+1},\qquad\partial_{\nu}u\in H^{m}(\Sigma).

Finally, combining the estimate (
2.52
), we obtain

\displaystyle\|u\|_{E_{m+1}}+\|\partial_{\nu}u\|_{H^{m}(\Sigma)}

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{CT}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{m}\big\|\partial_{t}^{k}(qu^{2})\big\|_{L^{1}(0,T;H^{m-k}(\Omega))}+\|f\|_{H^{m+1}(\Sigma)}\right)

(2.58)

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{CT}\|qu^{2}\|_{E_{m}}+Ce^{CT}\|f\|_{H^{m+1}(\Sigma)}.

Using the Banach algebra property of
E_{m}
, we further estimate

\displaystyle\|qu^{2}\|_{E_{m}}\leq\|q\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}\,\|u\|_{E_{m}}^{2}\leq R\,\|q\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}\,\|u\|_{E_{m}}\leq R\,M\,\|u\|_{E_{m}},

where we have used
u\in\mathcal{K}^{[0,R]}
.
Now, using the value of
R
from (
2.56
), this nonlinear term is absorbed into the left-hand side and the desired estimate

\displaystyle\|u\|_{E_{m+1}}+\|\partial_{\nu}u\|_{H^{m}(\Sigma)}\leq Ce^{CT}\|f\|_{H^{m+1}(\Sigma)}.

This completes the proof.
∎

3.
Proof of Theorem

In this section, we provide a detailed proof of Theorem
1.1
. Our goal is to reconstruct the damping coefficient
a
, linear potential
b
, and nonlinear potential
q
, appearing in the IBVP (
1.1
) from the DN map defined by (
1.2
). Our analysis is based on the linearization technique, which has been used in prior works, see
[
20
,
36
,
35
,
26
,
22
]
and references therein. We first reconstruct the linear coefficients by using the first-order linearization technique. Subsequently, the second-order linearization technique is used to determine the nonlinear coefficient.

3.1.
Reconstruction of the linear coefficients

In this subsection, we derive reconstruction formulas for the damping coefficient
a
and the linear potential
b
using the first-order linearization technique. To this aim, suppose
\varepsilon=(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2})
, where

\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}>0
are sufficiently small parameters satisfying

\displaystyle|\varepsilon|:=|\varepsilon_{1}|+|\varepsilon_{2}|<\delta,

with
\delta>0
is same as we defined in the set
2.8
.
Let

and define the boundary data
\varepsilon f\in E_{\delta}
by

\displaystyle\varepsilon f:=\varepsilon_{1}f_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}f_{2}.

We denote
u(t,x):=u_{\varepsilon f}(t,x)\in E_{m+1}
,
which solves the following IBVP

(3.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Box u(t,x)+a(x)\partial_{t}u(t,x)+b(x)u(t,x)+q(x)u^{2}(t,x)=0,&(t,x)\in\Omega_{T},\\

Now, we define the associated DN map by

(3.2)

\displaystyle\Lambda:E_{\delta}

\displaystyle\rightarrow H^{m}(\Sigma),

(3.3)

\displaystyle\varepsilon f

\displaystyle\mapsto\partial_{\nu}u|_{\Sigma}.

Next, we differentiate (
3.1
) with respect to

for

, we obtain

(3.4)

\displaystyle\quad\begin{cases}\Box(\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}}u(t,x))+a(x)\partial_{t}(\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}}u(t,x))+b(x)\,\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}}u(t,x)+2q(x)\,u(t,x)\,\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}}u(t,x)=0,&(t,x)\in\Omega_{T},\\
\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}}u(t,x)=f_{k}(t,x),&(t,x)\in\Sigma,\\

The interchange of the derivative in the IBVP (

(3.5)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Box(\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}}u(t,x))\big|_{\varepsilon_{k}=0}+a(x)\partial_{t}(\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}}u(t,x))\big|_{\varepsilon_{k}=0}+b(x)(\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}}u(t,x))\big|_{\varepsilon_{k}=0}=0,&(t,x)\in\Omega_{T},\\
(\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}}u(t,x))\big|_{\varepsilon_{k}=0}=f_{k}(t,x),&(t,x)\in\Sigma,\\
(\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}}u)(0,x)\big|_{\varepsilon_{k}=0}=0,\quad\partial_{t}(\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}}u)(0,x)\big|_{\varepsilon_{k}=0}=0,&\quad x\in\Omega.\end{cases}

For convenience, we introduce the notation

(3.6)

v_{k}(t,x):=\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}}u(t,x)\big|_{\varepsilon_{k}=0}.

Then IBVP (

(3.7)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Box v_{k}(t,x)+a(x)\partial_{t}v_{k}(t,x)+b(x)v_{k}(t,x)=0,&(t,x)\in\Omega_{T},\\
v_{k}(t,x)=f_{k}(t,x),&(t,x)\in\Sigma,\\
v_{k}(0,x)=0,\quad\partial_{t}v_{k}(0,x)=0,&\quad x\in\Omega.\end{cases}

Since
u\in E_{m+1}
, and using an argument similar to the one above, the mixed derivatives
can be interchanged. Thus, for every
0\leq i\leq n
, we have

(3.8)

\displaystyle\partial_{\epsilon_{k}}\partial_{x_{i}}u(t,x,\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})=\partial_{x_{i}}\partial_{\epsilon_{k}}u(t,x,\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})\quad\text{for}\ (t,x)\in\Omega_{T}.

It follows that

(3.9)

\displaystyle\partial_{\epsilon_{k}}\partial_{\nu}u(t,x,\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})=\partial_{\nu}\partial_{\epsilon_{k}}u(t,x,\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})\quad\text{for}\ (t,x)\in\Omega_{T}.

Now restrict the above identity to
(t,x)\in\Sigma
and evaluate at
\epsilon=0
, then we obtain

(3.10)

\displaystyle\partial_{\nu}v_{k}(t,x)=\left.\partial_{\epsilon_{k}}\partial_{\nu}u(t,x,\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})\right|_{\Sigma,\,\epsilon=0}.

Thus, the DN map
associated with the IBVP
(
3.7
)
is given by

(3.11)

\displaystyle\Lambda^{(v_{k})}:\varepsilon f\longmapsto\left.\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}}\Lambda(\varepsilon_{1}f_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}f_{2})\right|_{\varepsilon_{k}=0}=\left.\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}}\partial_{\nu}u_{\varepsilon_{1}f_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}f_{2}}\right|_{\varepsilon_{k}=0,\,\Sigma}=\partial_{\nu}v_{k}\big|_{\Sigma},\qquad k=1,2.

Next, our aim is to establish the reconstruction formula for determining the coefficients
a
and
b
from the DN map (
3.11
).
For this purpose, we first multiply the IBVP (
3.7
)
by
v_{0}
, where
v_{0}
is a solution of the following backward problem

(3.12)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Box v_{0}(t,x)=0,&(t,x)\in\Omega_{T},\\
v_{0}(T,x)=0,\quad\partial_{t}v_{0}(T,x)=0,&\quad x\in\Omega,\end{cases}

and integrate over
\Omega_{T}
, we have

(3.13)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\Omega_{T}}\left(\Box\,v_{k}(t,x)+a(x)\partial_{t}\,v_{k}(t,x)+b(x)v_{k}(t,x)\right)v_{0}(t,x)\,dx\,dt=0.

Now, using integration by parts, we have

(3.14)

\displaystyle\quad\int\limits_{\Omega_{T}}\big[-a(x)\partial_{t}\,v_{k}(t,x)v_{0}(t,x)-b(x)v_{k}(t,x)v_{0}(t,x)\big]\,dx\,dt

(3.15)

\displaystyle\quad=\int\limits_{\Omega_{T}}v_{k}(t,x)\Box\,v_{0}(t,x)\,dx\,dt+\int\limits_{\Omega}\big[\partial_{t}v_{k}(T,x)v_{0}(T,x)-\partial_{t}v_{0}(T,x)v_{k}(T,x)\big]\,dx

(3.16)

\displaystyle\qquad-\int\limits_{\Omega}\big[\partial_{t}v_{k}(0,x)v_{0}(0,x)-\partial_{t}v_{0}(0,x)v_{k}(0,x)\big]\,dx-\int\limits_{\Sigma}v_{0}(t,x)\partial_{\nu}v_{k}(t,x)\,dS_{x}\,dt

(3.17)

\displaystyle\qquad+\int\limits_{\Sigma}v_{k}(t,x)\partial_{\nu}v_{0}(t,x)\,dS_{x}\,dt.

On the right-hand side of equation (
3.14
), the first three integrals vanish using the equations (
3.7
) and (
3.22
), and the last two integrals are known from the measurement using the DN map (
3.11
). Thus, we have the following integral identity

(3.18)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\Omega_{T}}\big[a(x)\partial_{t}\,v_{k}(t,x)v_{0}(t,x)+b(x)v_{k}(t,x)v_{0}(t,x)\big]\,dx\,dt=\text{known}.

In order to reconstruct the terms
a
and
b
, we will construct the geometric-optics solution for
v_{k}
and
v_{0}
which satisfies (
3.7
) and (
3.22
) respectively.

We now state a Lemma that provides the construction for the solution
v_{k}
,
k=1,2,
that satisfies IBVP (
3.7
). These solutions are also known as geometric-optics solutions.

Lemma 3.1
.

[
19
, Lemma 2]

Let
\Omega
be an open, connected, and bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 2
with a smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
and
T>0
. For any
(a,\varphi)\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)\times C_{c}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
such that
\supp(\varphi)\cap\Omega=\emptyset
and
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. For any
\tau>0
, there exists a solution of (
3.7
) having the form

(3.19)

\displaystyle\begin{split}v_{k}(t,x)=\varphi(x+t\omega)A^{+}(t,x)\exp(i\tau(x\cdot\omega+t))+R_{k}(t,x,\tau),\end{split}

where

\displaystyle A^{+}(t,x)=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}a(x+s\omega)\,ds\right),

and
R_{k}(t,x,\tau)
vanishes at initial time; that is,

(3.20)

\displaystyle R_{k}(0,x,\tau)=\partial_{t}R_{k}(0,x,\tau)=0\text{ in }\Omega.

Moreover,
R_{k}(t,x,\tau)
satisfies the following estimate

(3.21)

\displaystyle\tau\|R_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}+\|\partial_{t}R_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}\leq C\|\varphi\|_{H^{3}{({\mathbb{R}}^{n}})}.

Next, we state a Lemma that provides the construction for the solution
v
, which solves the following backward wave problem

(3.22)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Box v(t,x)-a(x)\,\partial_{t}v(t,x)=0,&(t,x)\in\Omega_{T},\\
v(T,x)=0,\quad\partial_{t}v(T,x)=0,&\quad x\in\Omega.\end{cases}

Lemma 3.2
.

[
19
, Lemma 3]

Let
\Omega
be an open, connected, and bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 2
with a smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
and
T>0
. For any
(a,\varphi)\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)\times C_{c}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
such that
\left(\supp(\varphi)\pm T\omega\right)\cap\Omega=\emptyset
and
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. For any
\tau>0
, there exists a solution of (
3.22
) having the form

(3.23)

\displaystyle v(t,x)=\varphi(x+t\omega)A^{-}(t,x)\exp(-i\tau(x\cdot\omega+t))+R(t,x,\tau),

where

\displaystyle A^{-}(t,x)=\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}a(x+s\omega)\,ds\right),

and
R(t,x,\tau)
vanishes at the final time; that is,

(3.24)

\displaystyle R(T,x,\tau)=\partial_{t}R(T,x,\tau)=0\text{ in }\Omega.

Moreover,
R(t,x,\tau)
satisfies the following estimate

(3.25)

\displaystyle\tau\|R\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}+\|\partial_{t}R\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}\leq C\|\varphi\|_{H^{3}{({\mathbb{R}}^{n}})}.

Now, we begin to reconstruct the damping coefficient
a
.
Substituting the geometric-optics solutions constructed in
Lemma
3.1
and Lemma
3.2
(taking
a\equiv 0
)
into the integral identity (
3.18
), we obtain

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\Omega_{T}}\Big[

\displaystyle a(x)\left(i\tau\,\varphi(x+t\omega)A^{+}(t,x)e^{i\tau(x\cdot\omega+t)}+R_{11}(t,x,\tau)\right)

\displaystyle\quad+b(x)\left(\varphi(x+t\omega)A^{+}(t,x)e^{i\tau(x\cdot\omega+t)}+R_{k}(t,x,\tau)\right)\Big]

(3.26)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\left(\varphi(x+t\omega)e^{-i\tau(x\cdot\omega+t)}+R(t,x,\tau)\right)\,dx\,dt=\text{known}.

Here, the correction term
R_{11}
is given by

(3.27)

\displaystyle\qquad R_{11}(t,x,\tau)=\;

\displaystyle\left(\omega\cdot\nabla\varphi(x+t\omega)\,A^{+}(t,x)+\varphi(x+t\omega)\,\partial_{t}A^{+}(t,x)\right)e^{i\tau(x\cdot\omega+t)}+\partial_{t}R_{k}(t,x,\tau).

Expanding the integrand in equation (
3.1
), we get

\displaystyle i\tau\int\limits_{\Omega_{T}}a(x)\,\varphi^{2}(x+t\omega)\,A^{+}(t,x)\,dx\,dt+i\tau\int\limits_{\Omega_{T}}a(x)\,\varphi(x+t\omega)\,A^{+}(t,x)e^{i\tau(x\cdot\omega+t)}R(t,x,\tau)\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle\qquad+\int\limits_{\Omega_{T}}a(x)\,\varphi(x+t\omega)e^{-i\tau(x\cdot\omega+t)}R_{11}(t,x,\tau)\,dx\,dt+\int\limits_{\Omega_{T}}a(x)\,R_{11}(t,x,\tau)R(t,x,\tau)\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle\qquad+\int\limits_{\Omega_{T}}b(x)\,\varphi^{2}(x+t\omega)\,A^{+}(t,x)\,dx\,dt+\int\limits_{\Omega_{T}}b(x)\,\varphi(x+t\omega)\,A^{+}(t,x)e^{i\tau(x\cdot\omega+t)}R(t,x,\tau)\,dx\,dt

(3.28)

\displaystyle\qquad+\int\limits_{\Omega_{T}}b(x)\,\varphi(x+t\omega)e^{-i\tau(x\cdot\omega+t)}R_{k}(t,x,\tau)\,dx\,dt+\int\limits_{\Omega_{T}}b(x)\,R_{k}(t,x,\tau)R(t,x,\tau)\,dx\,dt=\text{known}.

Dividing the above equation (
3.1
) by
i\tau
, we obtain

(3.29)

\displaystyle\text{known}=\;

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\Omega_{T}}a(x)\,\varphi^{2}(x+t\omega)\,A^{+}(t,x)\,dx\,dt+\sum_{j=2}^{8}I_{j},

where the
I_{j}
terms are defined as

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\Omega_{T}}a(x)\,\varphi(x+t\omega)\,A^{+}(t,x)e^{i\tau(x\cdot\omega+t)}R(t,x,\tau)\,dx\,dt,

\displaystyle I_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{i\tau}\int\limits_{\Omega_{T}}a(x)\,\varphi(x+t\omega)e^{-i\tau(x\cdot\omega+t)}R_{11}(t,x,\tau)\,dx\,dt,

\displaystyle I_{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{i\tau}\int\limits_{\Omega_{T}}a(x)\,R_{11}(t,x,\tau)R(t,x,\tau)\,dx\,dt,

\displaystyle I_{5}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{i\tau}\int\limits_{\Omega_{T}}b(x)\,\varphi^{2}(x+t\omega)\,A^{+}(t,x)\,dx\,dt,

\displaystyle I_{6}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{i\tau}\int\limits_{\Omega_{T}}b(x)\,\varphi(x+t\omega)\,A^{+}(t,x)e^{i\tau(x\cdot\omega+t)}R(t,x,\tau)\,dx\,dt,

\displaystyle I_{7}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{i\tau}\int\limits_{\Omega_{T}}b(x)\,\varphi(x+t\omega)e^{-i\tau(x\cdot\omega+t)}R_{k}(t,x,\tau)\,dx\,dt,

\displaystyle I_{8}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{i\tau}\int\limits_{\Omega_{T}}b(x)\,R_{k}(t,x,\tau)R(t,x,\tau)\,dx\,dt.

Using Hölder’s inequality, the estimates
(
3.21
), (
3.25
),
and the fact that
a,b\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
, we estimate each of the integrals

I_{2},\dots,I_{8}
appearing in (
3.29
). We write
\|v\|\lesssim\|w\|
to denote the inequality
\|v\|\leq c\,\|w\|
for some
constant
c>0
independent of
v
and
w
. This notation will be used throughout the rest of the article.

Estimate of
I_{2}
.

\displaystyle|I_{2}|

\displaystyle\leq\int\limits_{\Omega_{T}}\left|a(x)\varphi(x+t\omega)A^{+}(t,x)R(t,x,\tau)\right|\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\|R\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}\lesssim\frac{1}{\tau}\|\varphi\|_{H^{3}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}.

Estimate of
I_{3}
.

Recalling the expression of
R_{11}
in (
3.27
),
we write

\displaystyle|I_{3}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\tau}\int\limits_{\Omega_{T}}\left|a(x)\varphi(x+t\omega)R_{11}(t,x,\tau)\right|\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\tau}\int\limits_{\Omega_{T}}\left|a(x)\varphi(x+t\omega)\left(\omega\cdot\nabla\varphi(x+t\omega)A^{+}(t,x)\right)\right|\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{\tau}\int\limits_{\Omega_{T}}\left|a(x)\varphi^{2}(x+t\omega)\partial_{t}A^{+}(t,x)\right|\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{\tau}\int\limits_{\Omega_{T}}\left|a(x)\varphi(x+t\omega)\partial_{t}R_{k}(t,x,\tau)\right|\,dx\,dt\lesssim\frac{1}{\tau}\|\varphi\|_{H^{3}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}.

Estimate of
I_{4}
.

Similarly,

\displaystyle|I_{4}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\tau}\int\limits_{\Omega_{T}}\left|a(x)R_{11}(t,x,\tau)R(t,x,\tau)\right|\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\tau}\left(\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\|R\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}+\|R\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}+\|\partial_{t}R_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}\|R\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\tau}\|\varphi\|_{H^{3}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}.

Estimate of
I_{5}
.

\displaystyle|I_{5}|=\frac{1}{\tau}\int\limits_{\Omega_{T}}\left|b(x)\varphi^{2}(x+t\omega)A^{+}(t,x)\right|\,dx\,dt\lesssim\frac{1}{\tau}\|\varphi\|_{H^{3}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}.

Estimate of
I_{6}
.

\displaystyle|I_{6}|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tau}\int\limits_{\Omega_{T}}\left|b(x)\varphi(x+t\omega)A^{+}(t,x)e^{i\tau(x\cdot\omega+t)}R(t,x,\tau)\right|\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\tau}\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\|R\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}\lesssim\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\|\varphi\|_{H^{3}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}.

Estimate of
I_{7}
.

\displaystyle|I_{7}|\lesssim\frac{1}{\tau}\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\|R_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}\lesssim\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\|\varphi\|_{H^{3}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}.

Estimate of
I_{8}
.

\displaystyle|I_{8}|\lesssim\frac{1}{\tau}\|R_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}\|R\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{T})}\lesssim\frac{1}{\tau^{3}}\|\varphi\|_{H^{3}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}^{2}.

Combining the above estimates for each of the integrals

I_{2},\dots,I_{8}
in (
3.29
) and letting

\tau\to\infty
, we conclude that all contributions of these integrals vanish.
Consequently, the first term in (
3.29
) is given as

(3.30)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\Omega_{T}}a(x)\,\varphi^{2}(x+t\omega)\,A^{+}(t,x)\,dx\,dt=\text{known}.

Since
a\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
, we extend
a
by
0
outside of
\Omega
, so we have

\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{T}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}a(x)\varphi^{2}(x+t\omega)A^{+}(t,x)\,dx\,dt=\text{known}.

Now consider
y=x+t\omega
, this implies
x=y-t\omega
, and using this we have

\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{T}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}a(y-t\omega)\varphi^{2}(y)A^{+}(t,y-t\omega)\,dy\,dt=\text{known}.

Since the integrand is absolutely integrable (because
a
has compact support), using Fubini’s theorem, interchange the integrals

(3.31)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\underbrace{\left(\int\limits_{0}^{T}a(y-t\omega)A^{+}(t,y-t\omega)\,dt\right)}_{:=F(y)}\varphi^{2}(y)\,dy=\text{known}.

Define the operator

\displaystyle T(\varphi):=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}F(y)\varphi^{2}(y)\,dy,\quad\text{where}\quad F(y):=\int\limits_{0}^{T}a(y-t\omega)A^{+}(t,y-t\omega)\,dt.

Now we introduce a set

(3.32)

\displaystyle\Omega_{\varepsilon}:=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{\Omega}:\operatorname{dist}(x,\Omega)<\varepsilon\right\},\quad\text{where}\quad\varepsilon\in\left(0,\min\left\{1,\frac{T-\operatorname{diam}(\Omega)}{3}\right\}\right).

Given
y_{0}\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}
, we denote by
(\varphi_{\delta})_{\delta>0}\subset C_{c}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
the functions

(3.33)

\displaystyle\varphi_{\delta}(y)=\delta^{-\frac{n}{2}}\varphi\left(\frac{y-y_{0}}{\delta}\right),

where
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(B_{1}(0))
is the square root of the standard mollifiers in dimension
n
. By the construction of
\varphi_{\delta}
and
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
, we can observe the following

(3.34)

\displaystyle\supp(\varphi_{\delta})\subset\Omega_{\varepsilon}\text{ and }(\Omega_{\varepsilon}\pm T^{\prime}\omega)\cap\Omega=\emptyset\text{ for all }T^{\prime}\geq T,

for sufficiently small
\delta>0
. Thus, we have

known

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}F(y)\varphi^{2}_{\delta}(y)\,dy,

\displaystyle=\delta^{-n}\int\limits_{B(y_{0},\delta)}F(y)\varphi^{2}\left(\frac{y-y_{0}}{\delta}\right)\,dy.

Since
F
is continuous in
\Omega
, by the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem, we have

(3.35)

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}F(y)\,\varphi^{2}_{\delta}(y)\,dy=F(y_{0}).

Hence, taking the limit as
\delta\to 0
yields
F(y_{0})
as known for
y_{0}\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}
; that is,

\displaystyle\text{known}=F(y_{0})

\displaystyle:=\int\limits_{0}^{T}a(y_{0}-t\omega)A^{+}(t,y_{0}-t\omega)\,dt,

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{0}^{T}a(y_{0}-t\omega)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}a(y_{0}-\rho\omega)\,d\rho\right)\,dt,\quad\text{where}\quad\rho=t-s,

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{0}^{T}-2\,\partial_{t}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}a(y_{0}-\rho\omega)\,d\rho\right)\,dt,

\displaystyle=-2\left(\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}a(y_{0}-\rho\omega)\,d\rho\right)-1\right).

This implies

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}a(y_{0}-\rho\omega)\,d\rho\right)=\text{known}.

Using the invertibility of the
\exp
map from
{\mathbb{R}}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{0\}
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}a(y_{0}-\rho\omega)\,d\rho=\text{known}.

Since
a
is compactly supported in
\Omega
and
(\Omega_{\varepsilon}\pm T^{\prime}\omega)\cap\Omega=\emptyset\text{ for all }T^{\prime}\geq T
, combining the preceding facts gives

a(y_{0}-\rho\omega)=0
for
\rho\geq T
, thus we have

(3.36)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}a(y_{0}-\rho\omega)\,d\rho=\text{known},\qquad\text{ for }y_{0}\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}.

Next, we will show that the above integral holds (
3.36
) for any
y_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. The key observation is that if the line through a point
y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

in the direction
\omega
intersects the domain
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
,
then the integral (
3.36
) follows immediately
from a change of variables.

We first show that(
3.36
) holds for every

y_{0}\in\overline{\Omega}
. By the construction of
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
,
any point in
\overline{\Omega}
can be shifted along the direction
\omega

into
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
; that is,

\displaystyle y_{0}\in\overline{\Omega}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad y_{0}+t_{0}\omega\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}\quad\text{for some }t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}.

Hence, the estimate holds for all
y_{0}\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}\cup\overline{\Omega}
.

Next, consider
y_{0}\in(\Omega_{\varepsilon}\cup\overline{\Omega})^{c}
.
If the line
y_{0}+t\omega
intersects
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
, then, as before,
the estimate follows from the same change of variables argument.
If the line through
y_{0}
does not intersect
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
,
then it also does not intersect
\Omega
. Since
a\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
,
the integrand vanishes along this line and therefore the integral is zero. Thus, equation (
3.36
) holds for any
y_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
Since
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
and the domain is invariant under the transformation
\rho\mapsto-\rho
, the change of variables formula gives

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}a(y_{0}+\rho\omega)\,d\rho=\text{known}.

Thus, we obtain that the ray transform corresponding to the function
a
, denoted by
X_{a}
, is known for each
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. More precisely,

(3.37)

\displaystyle X_{a}(\omega,y):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}a(y+\rho\omega)\,d\rho=\text{known},\qquad(\omega,y)\in T\mathbb{S}^{n-1},

where
T\mathbb{S}^{n-1}:=\{\,(\omega,y)\,|\,\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1},y\in\omega^{\perp}\}
is the tangent bundle of the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. Using the Fourier slice theorem for the ray transform (see Theorem 1.1,
[
51
]
), we have

(3.38)

\displaystyle\widehat{a}(\xi)=\widehat{X_{a}}(\omega,\xi)=\text{known},\qquad\text{for every }\xi\in\omega^{\perp}.

Since for each
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
there exists some direction
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
with

\xi\in\omega^{\perp}
, this shows that
\widehat{a}(\xi)
is known for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Thus, applying the Fourier inversion formula yields the result that the function
a
is uniquely determined in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Finally, because the function
a
is compactly supported in
\Omega
, we conclude that

(3.39)

\displaystyle a(x)=\text{known },\quad\text{for all }x\in\Omega.

This completes the reconstruction of the damping coefficient
a
in
\Omega
.∎

Next, we will reconstruct the linear potential
b
. Since we have already reconstructed the damping coefficient
a
, therefore, we assume that
v_{a}
is the solution to the following backward wave problem

(3.40)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Box v_{a}(t,x)-a(x)\,\partial_{t}v_{a}(t,x)=0,&(t,x)\in\Omega_{T},\\
v_{a}(T,x)=0,\quad\partial_{t}v_{a}(T,x)=0,&\quad x\in\Omega.\end{cases}

Now, we multiply equation (
3.7
) by
v_{a}
, and integrate over
\Omega_{T}
, we have

(3.41)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\Omega_{T}}\left(\Box\,v_{k}(t,x)+a(x)\partial_{t}\,v_{k}(t,x)+b(x)v_{k}(t,x)\right)v_{a}(t,x)\,dx\,dt=0.

Now, using integration by parts, we have

(3.42)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\Omega_{T}}-b(x)v_{k}(t,x)v_{a}(t,x)\,dx\,dt

(3.43)

\displaystyle\quad=\int\limits_{\Omega_{T}}v_{k}(t,x)\left(\Box v_{a}(t,x)-a(x)\,\partial_{t}v_{a}(t,x)\right)\,dx\,dt+\int\limits_{\Omega}\big[\partial_{t}v_{k}(T,x)v_{a}(T,x)-\partial_{t}v_{a}(T,x)v_{k}(T,x)\big]\,dx

(3.44)

\displaystyle\qquad-\int\limits_{\Omega}\big[\partial_{t}v_{k}(0,x)v_{a}(0,x)-\partial_{t}v_{a}(0,x)v_{k}(0,x)\big]\,dx+\int\limits_{\Omega}a(x)\big[v_{k}(T,x)v_{a}(T,x)-v_{a}(0,x)v_{k}(0,x)\big]\,dx

(3.45)

\displaystyle\qquad-\int\limits_{\Sigma}v_{a}(t,x)\partial_{\nu}v_{k}(t,x)\,dS_{x}\,dt+\int\limits_{\Sigma}v_{k}(t,x)\partial_{\nu}v_{a}(t,x)\,dS_{x}\,dt.

On the right-hand side of equation (
3.42
), the first four terms vanish from (
3.7
), (
3.22
), (
3.40
) and the last two terms are known from the measurement using the DN map (
3.11
). Thus, we have

(3.46)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\Omega_{T}}b(x)v_{k}(t,x)v_{a}(t,x)\,dx\,dt=\text{known}.

Substituting the constructed geometrics solution for
v_{k}
and
v_{a}
from (
3.19
) and (
3.23
), in the integral identity described in (
3.46
), then we conclude

(3.47)

\displaystyle\text{known}=\int\limits_{\Omega_{T}}

\displaystyle\Big\{b(x)\left(\varphi(x+t\omega)\,A^{+}(t,x)\,e^{i\tau(x\cdot\omega+t)}+R_{1}(t,x,\tau)\right)

(3.48)

\displaystyle\qquad\left(\varphi(x+t\omega)\,A^{-}(t,x)\,e^{-i\tau(x\cdot\omega+t)}+R_{2}(t,x,\tau)\right)\Big\}\,dx\,dt.

Simplifying the above expression

(3.49)

\displaystyle\text{known}=

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\Omega_{T}}b(x)\varphi^{2}(x+t\omega)\,dx\,dt+\int\limits_{\Omega_{T}}b(x)\varphi(x+t\omega)A^{-}(t,x)R_{1}(t,x,\tau)e^{-i\tau(x\cdot\omega+t)}\,dx\,dt

(3.50)

\displaystyle+\int\limits_{\Omega_{T}}b(x)\varphi(x+t\omega)A^{+}(t,x)e^{i\tau(x\cdot\omega+t)}R_{2}(t,x,\tau)\,dx\,dt+\int\limits_{\Omega_{T}}b(x)R_{1}(t,x,\tau)R_{2}(t,x,\tau)\,dx\,dt,

(3.51)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\Omega_{T}}b(x)\varphi^{2}(x+t\omega)\,dx\,dt+J_{2}+J_{3}+J_{4}.

The integrals
J_{k}
,
k=2,3,4
, can be estimated in an analogous manner to

I_{j}
,
j=2,\dots,8
, using Hölder’s inequality together with the
estimates (
3.21
) and (
3.25
).
In particular, each
J_{k}
admits a bound of order
\mathcal{O}(\tau^{-1})
, so each
J_{k}
terms vanish as
\tau\to\infty
.
Thus, letting
\tau\to\infty
in the equation (
3.51
), we get the following identity

(3.52)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\Omega_{T}}b(x)\varphi^{2}(x+t\omega)\,dx\,dt=\text{known}.

Since
b\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
, we extend
b
by
0
outside of
\Omega
, so we have

\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{T}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}b(x)\varphi^{2}(x+t\omega)\,dx\,dt=\text{known}.

Now consider
y=x+t\omega
, this implies
x=y-t\omega
, and using this we have

\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{T}\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}b(y-t\omega)\varphi^{2}(y)\,dy\,dt=\text{known}.

Since the integrand is absolutely integrable (because
b
has compact support), using the Fubini theorem, we can interchange the integrals

(3.53)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\underbrace{\left(\int\limits_{0}^{T}b(y-t\omega)\,dt\right)}_{\Phi(y)}\varphi^{2}(y)\,dy=\text{known},

for all
\varphi\in{\mathbb{C}}_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\left(\supp(\varphi)\pm T\omega\right)\cap\Omega=\emptyset
. By the construction of the test function described in (
3.33
), we get

(3.54)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\underbrace{\left(\int\limits_{0}^{T}b(y-t\omega)\,dt\right)}_{:=\Phi(y)}\varphi_{\delta}^{2}(y)\,dy=\text{known}.

Define the operators

\displaystyle\widetilde{T}(\varphi):=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Phi(y)\varphi_{\delta}^{2}(y)\,dy,\quad\text{where}\quad\Phi(y):=\int\limits_{0}^{T}b(y-t\omega)\,dt.

By a similar analysis as we did earlier, we deduce that the function
\Phi
is known in
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
, where
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
is defined in equation (
3.32
). Thus, we have

\displaystyle\text{known}=\Phi(y_{0})

\displaystyle:=\int\limits_{0}^{T}b(y_{0}-t\omega)\,dt.

Since
b
is compactly supported in
\Omega
and
(\Omega_{\varepsilon}\pm T^{\prime}\omega)\cap\Omega=\emptyset\text{ for all }T^{\prime}\geq T
, combining the preceding facts gives

b(y_{0}-t\omega)=0
for
t\geq T
, we have

\displaystyle\Phi(y_{0})=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}b(y_{0}-t\omega)\,dt\,=\,\text{known},\qquad\text{ for }y_{0}\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}.

From this, we conclude that
b
is known for every
y_{0}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. This follows from the same arguments used to reconstruct the damping coefficient
a
after the step of equation (
3.36
). Finally, because the function
b
is compactly supported in
\Omega
, we conclude that

(3.55)

\displaystyle b(x)=\text{known },\quad\text{for all }x\in\Omega.

This completes the reconstruction of the linear potential
b
in
\Omega
.∎

3.2.
Reconstruction of the nonlinear coefficient

In the previous subsection, we used the first-order linearization
together with the DN map to reconstruct the coefficient
associated with the linear part of IBVP (
1.1
).
In the present subsection, we make use of the second-order
linearization of the DN map to reconstruct the
coefficient of the nonlinear term. Towards this objective, we differentiate IBVP (
3.4
) with respect to

\varepsilon_{m}
, where
m\in\{1,2\}
and
m\neq k
. This yields

(3.56)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Box\partial_{\varepsilon_{m}\varepsilon_{k}}^{2}u(t,x)+a(x)\partial_{t}\!\left(\partial_{\varepsilon_{m}\varepsilon_{k}}^{2}u(t,x)\right)+b(x)\partial_{\varepsilon_{m}\varepsilon_{k}}^{2}u(t,x)\\[2.0pt]
\qquad+2q(x)\,\partial_{\varepsilon_{m}}u(t,x)\,\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}}u(t,x)+2q(x)\,u(t,x)\,\partial_{\varepsilon_{m}\varepsilon_{k}}^{2}u(t,x)=0,&(t,x)\in\Omega_{T},\\[2.0pt]
\partial_{\varepsilon_{m}\varepsilon_{k}}^{2}u(t,x)=0,&(t,x)\in\Sigma,\\[2.0pt]
\partial_{\varepsilon_{m}\varepsilon_{k}}^{2}u(0,x)=0,\quad\partial_{t}\left(\partial_{\varepsilon_{m}\varepsilon_{k}}^{2}u(0,x)\right)=0,&\quad x\in\Omega.\end{cases}

Next, above IBVP (
3.56
) evaluating at
\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon_{2}=0
, and using the fact that

u(t,x)\big|_{\varepsilon=0}=0
, we obtain

(3.57)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Box\partial_{\varepsilon_{m}\varepsilon_{k}}^{2}u(t,x)\big|_{\varepsilon=0}+a(x)\partial_{t}\!\left(\partial_{\varepsilon_{m}\varepsilon_{k}}^{2}u(t,x)\right)\big|_{\varepsilon=0}+b(x)\partial_{\varepsilon_{m}\varepsilon_{k}}^{2}u(t,x)\big|_{\varepsilon=0}\\[2.0pt]
\qquad+2q(x)\,\left(\partial_{\varepsilon_{m}}u(t,x)\,\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}}u(t,x)\right)\big|_{\varepsilon=0}=0,&(t,x)\in\Omega_{T},\\[2.0pt]
\partial_{\varepsilon_{m}\varepsilon_{k}}^{2}u(t,x)\big|_{\varepsilon=0}=0,&(t,x)\in\Sigma,\\[2.0pt]
\partial_{\varepsilon_{m}\varepsilon_{k}}^{2}u(0,x)\big|_{\varepsilon=0}=0,\quad\partial_{t}\left(\partial_{\varepsilon_{m}\varepsilon_{k}}^{2}u(0,x)\right)\big|_{\varepsilon=0}=0,&\quad x\in\Omega.\end{cases}

For our convenience, we introduce the notation

(3.58)

w_{k,m}(t,x):=\partial_{\varepsilon_{m}\varepsilon_{k}}^{2}u(t,x)\big|_{\varepsilon=0}.

Using equation (
3.6
),
the IBVP (
3.57
) can be written as

(3.59)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Box w_{k,m}(t,x)+a(x)\partial_{t}w_{k,m}(t,x)+b(x)w_{k,m}(t,x)+2q(x)v_{k}(t,x)v_{m}(t,x)=0,&(t,x)\in\Omega_{T},\\
w_{k,m}(t,x)=0,&(t,x)\in\Sigma,\\
w_{k,m}(0,x)=0,\quad\partial_{t}w_{k,m}(0,x)=0,&\quad x\in\Omega.\end{cases}

Now, taking the mixed derivative of equation (
1.1
) with respect to the parameters
\varepsilon_{1}
and
\varepsilon_{2}
, and of the solution
u_{\varepsilon f}
, we see that

\displaystyle w:={\partial^{2}_{\varepsilon_{1}\varepsilon_{2}}}{\big|_{\varepsilon=0}}u_{\varepsilon f},

which solves the following IBVP

(3.60)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Box w(t,x)+a(x)\partial_{t}w(t,x)+b(x)w(t,x)=-2q(x)v_{1}(t,x)v_{2}(t,x),&(t,x)\in\Omega_{T},\\
w(t,x)=0,&(t,x)\in\Sigma,\\
w(0,x)=0,~~\partial_{t}w(0,x)=0,&\quad x\in\Omega.\end{cases}

Since the DN map (
1.2
) is assumed to be
known, the normal derivative of
w
on
\Sigma
is also known.
Using this observation, we define the DN map
associated with the solution
w
of the inhomogeneous boundary value
problem (
3.60
) by

(3.61)

\displaystyle\Lambda^{(w)}:\varepsilon f\longmapsto\left.\partial_{\varepsilon_{1}\varepsilon_{2}}^{2}\Lambda(\varepsilon_{1}f_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}f_{2})\right|_{\varepsilon=0}=\left.\partial_{\varepsilon_{1}\varepsilon_{2}}^{2}\partial_{\nu}u_{\varepsilon_{1}f_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}f_{2}}\right|_{\varepsilon=0,\,\Sigma}=\partial_{\nu}w\big|_{\Sigma}.

In this way, we have produced new linear equations from the nonlinear equation (
1.1
). Studying these new equations in inverse problems for nonlinear equations is known as the
higher-order linearization method
.

Next, our aim is to reconstruct the nonlinear potential
q
.
Due to nonlinearity, the correction terms cannot be estimated using standard geometric-optics solutions. To obtain an estimate of the correction term, higher regularity estimates will be required. For this purpose, we introduce asymptotic solutions, whose existence
and properties are established in
Proposition
3.3
.
We prove an auxiliary Lemma
3.4
, which will be used to prove the Proposition
3.3
of the current section.

Proposition 3.3

(Asymptotic solutions)
.

Let
a
,
b\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Consider the following IBVP

(3.62)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}_{a,b}v(t,x):=\left(\Box-a(x)\partial_{t}+b(x)\right)v(t,x)=0,&(t,x)\in\Omega_{T},\\
v(T,x)=0,~~\partial_{t}v(T,x)=0,&\quad x\in\Omega.\end{cases}

Then, there exists an asymptotic solution to the IBVP (
3.62

(3.63)

v(t,x)=e^{-2i\tau\,(t+x\cdot\omega)}\left(m_{0}(t,x)+\frac{m_{1}(t,x)}{\tau}+\frac{m_{2}(t,x)}{\tau^{2}}+\cdots+\frac{m_{N}(t,x)}{\tau^{N}}\right)+r(t,x,\tau),

where the functions
m_{j}
,
j=0,1,\dots,N
, satisfy the following transport systems:
The function
m_{0}
solves

(3.64)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}Lm_{0}(t,x):=\left(\partial_{t}-\omega\cdot\nabla_{x}+\frac{a(x)}{2}\right)m_{0}(t,x)=0,&(t,x)\in\Omega_{T},\\
m_{0}(T,x)=0,\quad\partial_{t}m_{0}(T,x)=0,&\quad x\in\Omega.\end{cases}

For
j=1,\dots,N
, the functions
m_{j}
solves

(3.65)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}4iLm_{j}(t,x)=\mathcal{L}_{a,b}m_{j-1}(t,x),&(t,x)\in\Omega_{T},\\
m_{j}(T,x)=0,\quad\partial_{t}m_{j}(T,x)=0,&\quad x\in\Omega.\end{cases}

The correction term
r(t,x,\tau)
satisfies the vanishing final-time conditions

(3.66)

\displaystyle r(T,x,\tau)=0,\qquad\partial_{t}r(T,x,\tau)=0,\quad x\in\Omega.

Moreover,

satisfies the following estimate

(3.67)

\displaystyle\|r\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{T})}\leq\frac{C}{\tau},

where the constant
C
is independent of
\tau
for any
\tau>1.

Before proving Proposition
3.3
, we state and prove a Lemma that gives the existence of
m_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,N
(solution of the transport system).

Lemma 3.4
.

For any integer
j=0,\dots,N
, there exist functions

m_{j}\in C^{\infty}(\Omega_{T})\cap C\left([0,T]\times\overline{\Omega}\right)

which solves the following system of transport equations.
The function
m_{0}
satisfies

(3.68)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}Lm_{0}(t,x)=0,&(t,x)\in\Omega_{T},\\
m_{0}(T,x)=0,\quad\partial_{t}m_{0}(T,x)=0,&\quad x\in\Omega,\end{cases}

and the functions
m_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,N
, solve

(3.69)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}4i\,Lm_{j}(t,x)=\mathcal{L}_{a,b}m_{j-1}(t,x),&(t,x)\in\Omega_{T},\\
m_{j}(T,x)=0,\quad\partial_{t}m_{j}(T,x)=0,&\quad x\in\Omega.\end{cases}

Proof.

Our strategy is to construct explicit solutions to the given transport equations.
We first reformulate the initial value problem by introducing a suitable change of variables

(3.70)

\widetilde{m}_{0}(t,x):=m_{0}(T-t,x).

By the chain rule,
\widetilde{m}_{0}
satisfies the following initial value problem

(3.71)

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\left(-\partial_{t}-\omega\cdot\nabla_{x}+\frac{a(x)}{2}\right)\widetilde{m}_{0}(t,x)=0,&(t,x)\in\Omega_{T},\\
\widetilde{m}_{0}(0,x)=0,\quad\partial_{t}\widetilde{m}_{0}(0,x)=0,&\quad x\in\Omega.\end{cases}

We now make another change of variable

(3.72)

\widetilde{v}_{0}(t,x):=-\widetilde{m}_{0}(t,x)\,\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{T-t}\frac{a(x+\tau\omega)}{2}\,d\tau\right).

This implies

(3.73)

\displaystyle\widetilde{m}_{0}(t,x)=-\widetilde{v}_{0}(t,x)\,\exp\left(\int_{0}^{T-t}\frac{a(x+\tau\omega)}{2}\,d\tau\right).

Next, we derive the equation satisfied by
\widetilde{v}_{0}
by computing the action of the operator

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}-\omega\cdot\nabla_{x}+\frac{a(x)}{2}

to
\widetilde{m}_{0}(t,x)

First, we compute the following term separately

(3.74)

\displaystyle\qquad\partial_{t}\widetilde{m}_{0}(t,x)

\displaystyle=-\exp\left(\int_{0}^{T-t}\frac{a(x+\tau\omega)}{2}\,d\tau\right)\left(\partial_{t}\widetilde{v}_{0}(t,x)-\frac{a\left(x+(T-t)\omega\right)}{2}\widetilde{v}_{0}(t,x)\right),

\displaystyle\omega\cdot\nabla_{x}\widetilde{m}_{0}(t,x)

\displaystyle=-\exp\left(\int_{0}^{T-t}\frac{a(x+\tau\omega)}{2}\,d\tau\right)\left(\omega\cdot\nabla_{x}\widetilde{v}_{0}(t,x)+\frac{\widetilde{v}_{0}(t,x)}{2}\int_{0}^{T-t}\omega\cdot\nabla a(x+\tau\omega)\,d\tau\right)

(3.75)

\displaystyle=-\exp\left(\int_{0}^{T-t}\frac{a(x+\tau\omega)}{2}\,d\tau\right)\left(\omega\cdot\nabla_{x}\widetilde{v}_{0}(t,x)+\frac{\widetilde{v}_{0}(t,x)}{2}\left(a\left(x+(T-t)\omega\right)-a(x)\right)\right),

where in the last step, we used the following relation, which follows from the chain rule and the fundamental theorem of calculus

\int_{0}^{T-t}\omega\cdot\nabla a(x+\tau\omega)\,d\tau=\int_{0}^{T-t}\partial_{\tau}a(x+\tau\omega)\,d\tau=a\left(x+(T-t)\omega\right)-a(x).

Combining (
3.74
) and (
3.75
), we obtain

(3.76)

\displaystyle\left(-\partial_{t}-\omega\cdot\nabla_{x}+\frac{a(x)}{2}\right)\widetilde{m}_{0}(t,x)=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{T-t}\frac{a(x+\tau\omega)}{2}\,d\tau\right)\left(\partial_{t}\widetilde{v}_{0}(t,x)+\omega\cdot\nabla_{x}\widetilde{v}_{0}(t,x)\right).

From (
3.71
), it follows that
\widetilde{v}_{0}
satisfies the initial value problem

(3.77)

\begin{cases}\exp\left(\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T-t}\frac{a(x+\tau\omega)}{2}\,d\tau\right)\left(\partial_{t}\widetilde{v}_{0}(t,x)+\omega\cdot\nabla_{x}\widetilde{v}_{0}(t,x)\right)=0,&(t,x)\in\Omega_{T},\\[6.0pt]
\widetilde{v}_{0}(0,x)=0,\quad\partial_{t}\widetilde{v}_{0}(0,x)=0,&\quad x\in\Omega.\end{cases}

Since the exponential factor is strictly positive, this reduces to

(3.78)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\widetilde{v}_{0}(t,x)+\omega\cdot\nabla_{x}\widetilde{v}_{0}(t,x)=0,&(t,x)\in\Omega_{T},\\
\widetilde{v}_{0}(0,x)=0,\quad\partial_{t}\widetilde{v}_{0}(0,x)=0,&\quad x\in\Omega.\end{cases}

We choose

\widetilde{v}_{0}(t,x):=-\varphi\left(x+\left(T-t\right)\omega\right),

where

satisfies

\partial_{t}^{\alpha}\varphi(x)=0,\quad x\in\overline{\Omega}\cup(\overline{\Omega}-T\omega)\quad\text{ for every }\alpha\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}.

With this choice,
v_{0}
solves (
3.78

From equation (
3.72
), we obtain

\widetilde{m}_{0}(t,x)=\varphi\left(x+\left(T-t\right)\omega\right)\exp\left(\int_{0}^{T-t}\frac{a(x+\tau\omega)}{2}\,d\tau\right).

Changing the original variables from (
3.70

m_{0}(t,x)=\widetilde{m}_{0}(T-t,x)=\varphi(x+t\omega)\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\frac{a(x+\tau\omega)}{2}\,d\tau\right).

Next, we construct an explicit solution for
m_{1}
, which satisfies the transport equation (
3.69
) for

, that is

(3.81)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}4iLm_{1}(t,x)=\mathcal{L}_{a,b}m_{0}(t,x),&(t,x)\in\Omega_{T},\\

Thus, it is sufficient to solve the inhomogeneous transport problem

From the definition of the operator

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\left(\partial_{t}-\omega\cdot\nabla_{x}+\dfrac{a(x)}{2}\right)m_{1}(t,x)=g(t,x),&(t,x)\in\Omega_{T},\\
m_{1}(T,x)=0,\quad\partial_{t}m_{1}(T,x)=0,&\quad x\in\Omega.\end{cases}

We proceed as in the construction of
m_{0}

Define the function
\widetilde{m}_{1}(t,x)
as follows

\widetilde{m}_{1}(t,x):=m_{1}(T-t,x).

Then
\widetilde{m}_{1}

(3.85)

\begin{cases}\left(-\partial_{t}-\omega\cdot\nabla_{x}+\dfrac{a(x)}{2}\right)\widetilde{m}_{1}(t,x)=g(T-t,x),&(t,x)\in\Omega_{T},\\
\widetilde{m}_{1}(0,x)=0,\quad\partial_{t}\widetilde{m}_{1}(0,x)=0,&\quad x\in\Omega.\end{cases}

Suppose that

(3.86)

\widetilde{m}_{1}(t,x)=-v(t,x)\exp\left(\int_{0}^{T-t}\frac{a(x+\tau\omega)}{2}\,d\tau\right).

Next, we derive the equation satisfied by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\widetilde{m}_{1}(t,x)

Consequently, we have

(3.89)

\displaystyle\left(-\partial_{t}-\omega\cdot\nabla_{x}+\frac{a(x)}{2}\right)\widetilde{m}_{1}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{T-t}\frac{a(x+\tau\omega)}{2}\,d\tau\right)\left(\partial_{t}v+\omega\cdot\nabla_{x}v\right).

From the IBVP (
3.85
), we obtain

(3.90)

\begin{cases}\exp\left(\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T-t}\frac{a(x+\tau\omega)}{2}\,d\tau\right)\left(\partial_{t}v(t,x)+\omega\cdot\nabla_{x}v(t,x)\right)=g(T-t,x),\\
v(0,x)=0,\quad\partial_{t}v(0,x)=0.\end{cases}

Multiplying both sides by the exponential factor yields the transport equation

(3.91)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}v(t,x)+\omega\cdot\nabla_{x}v(t,x)=g(T-t,x)\exp\left(-\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T-t}\frac{a(x+\tau\omega)}{2}\,d\tau\right),&(t,x)\in\Omega_{T},\\

To simplify the notation, we define

(3.92)

L(t,x):=g(T-t,x)\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{T-t}\frac{a(x+\tau\omega)}{2}\,d\tau\right).

In addition, define the function

z(s):=v(t+s,\,x+s\omega).

Then, using the chain rule

(3.93)

\frac{d}{ds}z(s)=\partial_{t}v(t+s,x+s\omega)+\omega\cdot\nabla v(t+s,x+s\omega)=L(t+s,x+s\omega).

Integrating with respect to

from
-t
to

, we obtain

(3.94)

\displaystyle\int_{-t}^{0}\frac{d}{ds}z(s)\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{-t}^{0}L(t+s,x+s\omega)\,ds,

(3.95)

\displaystyle z(0)-z(-t)

\displaystyle=\int_{-t}^{0}L(t+s,x+s\omega)\,ds.

Using the initial condition
v(0,x)=0
, we have

and hence

(3.96)

v(t,x)=\int_{-t}^{0}L(t+s,x+s\omega)\,ds.

From the definition of
L
from (
3.92
), we rewrite (

) as

(3.97)

\displaystyle v(t,x)

\displaystyle=\int_{-t}^{0}g(T-t-s,x+s\omega)\exp\left(\int_{0}^{T-(t+s)}\frac{a(x+(s+\tau)\omega)}{2}\,d\tau\right)\,ds.

By equations (
3.97
) and (

), we obtain

(3.98)

\displaystyle-m_{1}(T-t,x)\,\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{T-t}\frac{a(x+\tau\omega)}{2}\,d\tau\right)

(3.99)

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{-t}^{0}g(T-t-s,x+s\omega)\exp\left(\int_{0}^{T-(t+s)}\frac{a(x+(s+\tau)\omega)}{2}\,d\tau\right)\,ds.

Substituting

from (
3.83
), we get

(3.100)

\displaystyle-m_{1}(T-t,x)\,\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{T-t}\frac{a(x+\tau\omega)}{2}\,d\tau\right)

(3.101)

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{-t}^{0}\frac{1}{4i}\mathcal{L}_{a,b}m_{0}(T-t-s,x+s\omega)\exp\left(\int_{0}^{T-(t+s)}\frac{a(x+(s+\tau)\omega)}{2}\,d\tau\right)\,ds.

Finally, using the transformation

yields the explicit representation

(3.102)

\displaystyle m_{1}(t,x)

\displaystyle=-\int_{-(T-t)}^{0}\left(\mathcal{L}_{a,b}m_{0}(t-s,x+s\omega)\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t-s}\frac{a(x+(s+\tau)\omega)}{2}\,d\tau\right)\right)\,ds

(3.103)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\frac{a(x+\tau\omega)}{2}\,d\tau\right).

By construction, the function
m_{1}
satisfies the final-time condition; that is,

m_{1}(T,x)=0.

Next, we show that
m_{1}
also satisfies

\partial_{t}m_{1}(T,x)=0.

For notational convenience, we introduce

\displaystyle E(t,x)

\displaystyle:=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\frac{a(x+\tau\omega)}{2}\,d\tau\right),

\displaystyle E_{1}(t,s,x)

\displaystyle:=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t-s}\frac{a(x+(s+\tau)\omega)}{2}\,d\tau\right),

\displaystyle F(t,s,x)

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{L}_{a,b}m_{0}(t-s,x+s\omega).

With this notation, the function
m_{1}
can be written as

(3.104)

m_{1}(t,x)=-E(t,x)\int_{-(T-t)}^{0}F(t,s,x)\,E_{1}(t,s,x)\,ds.

Differentiating with respect to
t
and applying the product rule, we obtain

(3.105)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}m_{1}(t,x)

\displaystyle=-E(t,x)\,\partial_{t}\left(\int_{-(T-t)}^{0}F(t,s,x)\,E_{1}(t,s,x)\,ds\right)

(3.106)

\displaystyle\quad-(\partial_{t}E)(t,x)\int_{-(T-t)}^{0}F(t,s,x)\,E_{1}(t,s,x)\,ds.

Using the Leibniz rule, we get the following expression

\displaystyle\partial_{t}m_{1}(t,x)

\displaystyle=E(t,x)\,F(t,-(T-t),x)\,E_{1}(t,-(T-t),x)-E(t,x)\,\int_{-(T-t)}^{0}\partial_{t}\!\left(FE_{1}\right)(t,s,x)\,ds

(3.107)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-(\partial_{t}E)(t,x)\int_{-(T-t)}^{0}F(t,s,x)\,E_{1}(t,s,x)\,ds.

Evaluating the above expression at
t=T
, we obtain

(3.108)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}m_{1}(T,x)

\displaystyle=E(T,x)\,F(T,0,x)\,E_{1}(T,0,x).

From condition (
3.79
) and the explicit form of
m_{0}
given in (
3.80
), we conclude that

\partial_{t}^{\alpha}m_{0}(T,x)=\partial_{t}^{\alpha}m_{0}(0,x)=0,\qquad x\in\Omega.

Therefore, we have

\mathcal{L}_{a,b}m_{0}(T,x)=0,

and since
\mathcal{L}_{a,b}m_{0}(T,x)=F(T,0,x)
, it follows from (
3.108
) that

\partial_{t}m_{1}(T,x)=0.

Using calculations similar to those for
m_{1}
, we can define
m_{j}
,
j=2,\dots,N
, as follows

(3.109)

\displaystyle m_{j}(t,x)

\displaystyle=-\int_{-(T-t)}^{0}\Bigg(\mathcal{L}_{a,b}m_{j-1}(t-s,x+s\omega)\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t-s}\frac{a(x+(s+\tau)\omega)}{2}\,d\tau\right)\Bigg)\,ds

(3.110)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\frac{a(x+\tau\omega)}{2}\,d\tau\right).

By construction,
m_{j}
,
j=2,\dots,N
, satisfies the final-time condition

m_{j}(T,x)=0.

Moreover, the vanishing of the time derivative at the final time

\partial_{t}m_{j}(T,x)=0,\quad j=2,\dots,N,

followed by an argument similar to the one used to establish

\partial_{t}m_{1}(T,x)=0
.
∎

Next, we provide the proof of the Proposition
3.3
.

Proof of Proposition
3.3
Since
v
is of the form (
3.63
), the product rule is combined with
\Box(e^{-2i\tau(t+x\cdot\omega)})=0
, we have

(3.111)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{a,b}(v)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{a,b}\left(e^{-2i\tau(t+x\cdot\omega)}\sum_{j=0}^{N}\tau^{-j}m_{j}(t,x)+r(t,x,\tau)\right),

\displaystyle=e^{-2i\tau(t+x\cdot\omega)}\sum_{j=0}^{N}\tau^{-j}\mathcal{L}_{a,b}m_{j}-4i\tau e^{-2i\tau(t+x\cdot\omega)}\sum_{j=0}^{N}\tau^{-j}(\partial_{t}-\omega\cdot\nabla_{x})m_{j}

(3.112)

\displaystyle\quad+2i\tau a(x)\,e^{-2i\tau(t+x\cdot\omega)}\sum_{j=0}^{N}\tau^{-j}m_{j}+\mathcal{L}_{a,b}(r(t,x,\tau)).

Collecting terms with respect to the powers of
\tau
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{a,b}(v(t,x))

\displaystyle=e^{-2i\tau(t+x\cdot\omega)}\Bigg\{-4i\tau Lm_{0}(t,x)+\left(\mathcal{L}_{a,b}m_{0}(t,x)-4iLm_{1}(t,x)\right)+\frac{1}{\tau}\left(\mathcal{L}_{a,b}m_{1}(t,x)-4iLm_{2}(t,x)\right)

(3.113)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\left(\mathcal{L}_{a,b}m_{2}(t,x)-4iLm_{3}(t,x)\right)+\cdots+\frac{1}{\tau^{N}}\mathcal{L}_{a,b}m_{N}(t,x)\Bigg\}+\mathcal{L}_{a,b}(r(t,x,\tau)).

Using the transport equations (
3.65
), all terms of order

\tau^{k}
with
-N-1\leq k\leq 1
vanish. Consequently, we obtain

(3.114)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{a,b}(v(t,x))

\displaystyle=e^{-2i\tau(t+x\cdot\omega)}\frac{1}{\tau^{N}}\mathcal{L}_{a,b}m_{N}(t,x)+\mathcal{L}_{a,b}\left(r(t,x,\tau)\right).

Since
v
is assumed to satisfy
\mathcal{L}_{a,b}(v(t,x))=0,\quad(t,x)\in\Omega_{T}
, it follows that

(3.115)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{a,b}\left(r(t,x,\tau)\right)

\displaystyle=-\tau^{-N}e^{-2i\tau(t+x\cdot\omega)}\mathcal{L}_{a,b}m_{N}(t,x),\quad(t,x)\in\Omega_{T}.

Using the final-time conditions on
v
; that is,

v(T,x)=0,\qquad\partial_{t}v(T,x)=0\quad x\in\Omega,

together with the final-time conditions for the functions

m_{j}(T,x)=0,\qquad\partial_{t}m_{j}(T,x)=0,\quad x\in\Omega,\quad j=0,\dots,N.

We deduce that the correction term
r
satisfies the following inhomogeneous
IBVP

(3.116)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}_{a,b}r(t,x,\tau)=-\tau^{-N}e^{-2i\tau(t+x\cdot\omega)}\mathcal{L}_{a,b}m_{N}(t,x),&(t,x)\in\Omega_{T},\\
r(T,x,\tau)=0,\qquad\partial_{t}r(T,x,\tau)=0,&\quad x\in\Omega.\end{cases}

Using the change of variables
\tilde{t}=T-t
, the
correction term
r
satisfies the following IBVP

(3.117)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}_{-a,b}\,r(\tilde{t},x,\tau)=-\tau^{-N}e^{-2i\tau(\tilde{t}+x\cdot\omega)}\mathcal{L}_{-a,b}m_{N}(\tilde{t},x),&\text{in }\Omega_{T},\\
r(0,x,\tau)=0,\qquad\partial_{\tilde{t}}r(0,x,\tau)=0,&\text{in }\Omega.\end{cases}

Based on the well-posedness of IBVP
(
2.2
), the correction term
r
exists. Moreover,
standard energy estimates give the following estimate for the correction term

(3.118)

\displaystyle\|r\|_{H^{m}(\Omega_{T})}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|e^{-2i\tau(\tilde{t}+x\cdot\omega)}\tau^{-N}\mathcal{L}_{-a,b}m_{N}(\tilde{t},x)\right\|_{H^{m}(\Omega_{T})}\leq C\tau^{2m-N},

where the constant
C
is independent of
\tau
.
Since
m>n+1
, the Sobolev embedding theorem yields

(3.119)

\displaystyle\|r\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{T})}\leq\|r\|_{H^{m}(\Omega_{T})}\leq C\tau^{2m-N}.

Choosing
N
sufficiently large and for any
\tau>1
, we conclude that

(3.120)

\displaystyle\|r\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{T})}\leq C\tau^{-1}.

This completes the proof. ∎

Next, our aim is to reconstruct the nonlinear potential using the geometric-optics solutions and the asymptotic solution together with the DN map (
3.61
). To this end, we rewrite the following IBVP

(3.121)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Box w(t,x)+a(x)\,\partial_{t}w(t,x)+b(x)\,w(t,x)=-2q(x)v_{1}(t,x)v_{2}(t,x),&(t,x)\in\Omega_{T},\\
w=0,&(t,x)\in\Sigma,\\
w(0,x)=0,~~\partial_{t}w(0,x)=0,&\quad x\in\Omega,\end{cases}

and
v_{k}

w_{0}(T,x)=0,~~\partial_{t}w_{0}(T,x)=0,&\quad x\in\Omega.\end{cases}

Multiplying (
3.121

w_{0}

\Omega_{T}

.
To derive the reconstruction of the nonlinear potential
q
, first, we define the functions

(3.135)

\displaystyle G(t):=q(y-t\omega),\qquad H(t):=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}a(y-s\omega)\,ds\right),

so that

(3.136)

\displaystyle\Psi(y)=\int_{0}^{\infty}G(t)H(t)\,dt.

Let
U(\varepsilon):=\Psi(y-\varepsilon\omega)

(3.137)

\displaystyle-\omega\cdot\nabla_{y}\Psi(y)=\left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}U(\varepsilon)\right|_{\varepsilon=0}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\left[G_{\varepsilon}(t)H_{\varepsilon}(t)\right]\right|_{\varepsilon=0}dt,

\displaystyle G_{\varepsilon}(t):=q(y-(\varepsilon+t)\omega),\quad H_{\varepsilon}(t):=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}a(y-(\varepsilon+s)\omega)\,ds\right).

(3.139)

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{t}a(y-(\varepsilon+s)\omega)\,ds\right|_{\varepsilon=0}=a(y-t\omega)-a(y),

(3.140)

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}H_{\varepsilon}(t)\right|_{\varepsilon=0}=-\frac{1}{2}H(t)\left(a(y)-a(y-t\omega)\right),

and

(3.141)

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}G_{\varepsilon}(t)\right|_{\varepsilon=0}=-\omega\cdot\nabla_{y}q(y-t\omega)=-\frac{d}{dt}q(y-t\omega)=G^{\prime}(t).

Thus, we have

(3.142)

\displaystyle-\omega\cdot\nabla_{y}\Psi(y)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[G^{\prime}(t)H(t)-\frac{1}{2}G(t)\left(a(y)-a(y-t\omega)\right)H(t)\right]\,dt.

Integrating
G^{\prime}(t)H(t)
by parts, using
H^{\prime}(t)=-\frac{1}{2}a(y-t\omega)H(t)

(3.143)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}G^{\prime}(t)H(t)\,dt=\left[G(t)H(t)\right]_{t=0}^{t=\infty}-\int_{0}^{\infty}G(t)H^{\prime}(t)\,dt=q(y)-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}G(t)a(y-t\omega)H(t)\,dt.

Combining with (
3.136

3.142

(3.144)

\displaystyle-\omega\cdot\nabla_{y}\,\Psi(y)=q(y)-\frac{1}{2}\,a(y)\,\Psi(y).

Equivalently, we have

(3.145)

\displaystyle-\omega\cdot\nabla_{y}\,\Psi(y)+\frac{1}{2}\,a(y)\,\Psi(y)=q(y).

Hence,
q(y)
is known for all
y\in\Omega.

This completes the proof of the main theorem.∎
