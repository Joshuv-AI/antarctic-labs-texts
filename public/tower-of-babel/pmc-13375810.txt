Habitat Type and Locality Structure the Midgut Microbiota of Aedes albopictus
Authors: Chang-Hyun Kim, Arif Ciloglu, Jiayue Yan, Andrew Mackay, Kylee R Noel, Megan Cooper, Antony Oluoch, Thomas Canam, Christopher M Stone
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe mosquito midgut microbiome plays a crucial role in nutrition, reproduction, and immunity, yet how locality and urban development shape these communities and their potential relevance to mosquito–pathogen interactions remains poorly understood. Here, we investigated bacterial community composition and diversity in the midguts of adult female Aedes albopictus collected from residential and woodland habitats of Champaign-Urbana and Charleston in Illinois, USA. We sequenced the V4 region of the 16 S rRNA gene from 160 samples and analyzed the data using QIIME 2. After quality and feature filtering, 112 samples were retained, yielding 2,531 unique amplicon sequence variants assigned to 34 bacterial phyla, 246 families, and 404 genera. Because formal contaminant assessment was not possible, findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Woodland habitats showed significantly higher Shannon diversity, observed ASV richness, and phylogenetic diversity than residential habitats, with Charleston woodland samples exhibiting the highest richness and phylogenetic diversity. Factorial analyses showed significant associations of both city and habitat type with Shannon diversity, observed richness, and Faith’s phylogenetic diversity, with significant interaction terms also detected. Beta diversity analyses revealed distinct clustering of Charleston woodland samples, and factorial PERMANOVA indicated significant associations of both city and habitat type across all four beta diversity metrics, with the strongest interaction effect observed for unweighted UniFrac. In conclusion, these results show that habitat type and locality are strongly associated with the composition and diversity of the Ae. albopictus midgut microbiota, underscoring the importance of habitat-specific microbial patterns in mosquito biology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00248-026-02792-5.Keywords: Aedes albopictus, Gut microbiome, Woodland, Residential area, Urbanization

Introduction

Aedes albopictus is considered the most invasive mosquito species worldwide and has been identified as a vector of various zoonotic mosquito-borne pathogens, including dengue, chikungunya, and Zika viruses [1, 2]. In the United States, since their first introduction to Houston, Texas in 1985 [3], Ae. albopictus has expanded its range northward and is now established in Midwestern states such as Illinois, Indiana, Iowa, Michigan, and Wisconsin [4–6]. Their ability to outcompete other mosquito species through resource competition as larvae and mating interference as adults, along with their resistance to dryness and cold as eggs, is thought to facilitate their expansion [7, 8]. Their primary source of blood meal is mammals, although they occasionally feed on birds [9]. As a secondary vector following Aedes aegypti, the Asian tiger mosquito (Ae. albopictus) emerged as a major public health concern in the United States during the Zika outbreaks in the Americas between 2014 and 2017 [10, 11].

Mosquito microbiomes have significant implications for mosquito biology, such as behavior, development, and fecundity [12]. Moreover, the gut microbiomes of mosquitoes play a critical role in immunity, serving as the first line of defense against pathogens acquired from a blood meal [13]. Mosquito gut microbiota has often been implicated in blocking the transmission of several pathogens to subsequent blood hosts [14]. However, some bacteria such as Serratia odorifera, appear to enhance mosquito infectivity with arboviruses [15]. Components of the mosquito-associated microbiota, particularly the endosymbiont Wolbachia, can be exploited to control mosquito-borne disease transmission through mechanisms such as cytoplasmic incompatibility (CI) [16]. Recently, the utility of mosquito gut microbiome in paratransgenesis has been one of the major efforts to mitigate the spread of mosquito-borne pathogens [17].

Studies have shown that the mosquito microbiota is highly diverse, and its composition depends on numerous environmental factors such as temperature, habitat type, mosquito-host availability, resource availability, and land-use type [18]. Focusing on land-use type, human activities such as agriculture, industrialization, transportation, and construction have historically been major drivers of environmental change and urbanization worldwide [19]. Consequently, the transformation of natural landscapes has led to alterations and fragmentation of mosquito habitats, the introduction of invasive mosquito species, and increased resource competition due to reduced availability of natural ecosystems, all of which are thought to contribute to compositional changes in mosquito microbiomes [20].

Despite the significant implications of the microbiome for mosquito immunity and pathogen transmission [13], little is known about the impact of urban development on the mosquito gut microbiome. To address this gap, we hypothesized that habitat type influences the diversity and composition of the Ae. albopictus gut microbiome. We aimed to examine how urbanization shapes gut microbiome composition and diversity in Ae. albopictus by comparing individuals collected from two contrasting habitat types, woodlands and residential areas, across two geographically distinct regions in central Illinois, USA. We expected that the Ae. albopictus midgut microbiome would be more diverse and complex in woodlands than in residential areas, and in smaller cities than in more urbanized regions.

Methods

Mosquito Collection

Between May and October of 2017, mosquitoes were collected bi-weekly from multiple locations in both Champaign-Urbana (Fig. 1A) and Charleston (Fig. 1B) in central Illinois, USA using standard CDC light traps baited with dry ice. These locations were grouped into wooded and residential habitat types. Woodlands were selected using the following criteria: (1) trees were the dominant vegetation, (2) no houses existed within a 50 m radius, and (3) the location had a history of Ae. albopictus presence. Residential areas were selected if houses were located at least 50 m away from woodlands and had a history of Ae. albopictus presence. The 50 m radius limit was selected based on the relatively limited average dispersal distances of Ae. albopictus, as documented in multiple mark-release-recapture studies [21–23]. Specimens were transported to the laboratory and identified to species level using standard unique morphological characteristics on the dorsum of the thorax, abdomen, and legs [24]. After morphological identification, all adult female Ae. albopictus were stored at -80 °C until further processing.

DNA Extraction and 16 S rRNA Gene Library Preparation

Forty adult female Ae. albopictus were randomly selected from each of habitat types (residential and woodland) in both Champaign-Urbana and Charleston, for a total of 160 (Supplementary Table 1). The mosquitoes were surface-cleaned as previously described by Muturi et al. [25]. Briefly, each mosquito was rinsed three times in sterile water, surface disinfected in 70% ethanol for 5 min, washed in sterile Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS) solution (ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA, USA) for 5 min, and then subjected to five additional rinse cycles in sterile DPBS, with each cycle lasting 3 min. Each surface-cleaned Ae. albopictus was placed in a drop of sterile DPBS under a stereo dissecting microscope and the midgut was harvested. Midguts were individually suspended in 1.5-mL microcentrifuge tubes containing 50 µL of RNAlater solution (ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA, USA) and stored at -80 °C until DNA isolation. Total DNA from each midgut was extracted using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s recommendation, except that the volume of the final elution buffer was 50 µL. DNA was quantified using an Epoch spectrophotometer (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA) and its quality assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). For bacterial characterization, we targeted the V4 hypervariable region of the 16 S rRNA gene (292 base pairs) using the following: F515, (5`-GTGYCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3`) and R806, (5`-GGACTACNVGGGTWTCTAAT-3`) [26].

Library preparation and sequencing were performed at the Functional Genomics Unit of Roy J. Carver Biotechnology Center, University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign. DNA of each mosquito sample was amplified using the above primer set on the Fluidigm® microfluidics quantitative PCR platform with appropriate linkers and sample barcodes. The final Fluidigm® libraries were pooled in equimolar ratio for sequencing. The final denatured library pool was spiked with 10% non-indexed PhiX control library (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) and sequenced by 2 × 300 nt paired-end sequencing on the Illumina MiSeq V3 Bulk system (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).

16 S rRNA Sequences Processing

All microbiome data were processed using the QIIME 2 pipeline (v. 2024.10) [27]. Paired-end sequencing reads targeting the V4 region of the 16 S rRNA gene (515 F/806R primers) were imported into QIIME 2 using a manifest file. Sequence quality was assessed, and truncation parameters were selected based on quality profile plots (forward: 240 bp; reverse: 220 bp). Denoising, merging, and chimera filtering were conducted using DADA2 software [28] as integrated into QIIME 2 with the following parameters: --p-trim-left-f 0, --p-trim-left-r 0, --p-trunc-len-f 240, --p-trunc-len-r 220, and --p-min-overlap 12. Amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were then identified and output as a feature table and representative sequences.

Negative extraction and amplification controls were not available for formal contaminant assessment, and DNA concentration data generated by a spectrophotometer were not considered suitable for frequency-based contaminant identification using the decontam package [29]. Therefore, statistical identification of potential contaminants could not be performed. Instead, we applied a conservative filtering strategy based on read abundance and prevalence to reduce spurious ASVs and background noise; however, these filtering steps do not replace formal contaminant identification in low-biomass microbiome studies. Specifically, low-depth samples with fewer than 1,400 reads were excluded from downstream analyses to ensure sufficient sampling depth for diversity metrics [30]. A rarefaction depth of 1,400 reads was selected based on alpha rarefaction curves as a compromise between retaining sufficient sample numbers and capturing within-sample diversity (Supplementary Figure S1) [30]. In addition, ASVs with fewer than 10 total reads or detected in fewer than three samples were removed to minimize the influence of rare, potentially artifactual features [31–33].

Filtered ASVs were aligned using MAFFT [34] through the QIIME 2 alignment plugin q2-alignment. To construct a phylogenetic framework for downstream diversity analyses, a midpoint-rooted phylogenetic tree was generated with FastTree2 via q2-phylogeny [35]. Taxonomic assignment of ASVs was performed using the classify-sklearn method with a naive Bayes classifier trained on the SILVA rRNA database (version 138) [36–38], specifically trained for the 515 F/806R primer region using RESCRIPt [39]. To ensure compatibility with the classifier, the original RNA-based SILVA sequences were reverse transcribed into DNA prior to region-specific extraction. ASVs assigned to the genus Wolbachia were retained in the primary dataset used for the main diversity analyses because this endosymbiont represented a biologically relevant component of the bacterial community detected in the sequenced midgut samples. To evaluate the potential influence of this highly abundant endosymbiont on diversity estimates, we additionally performed a sensitivity analysis after excluding ASVs assigned to Wolbachia. Because removal of Wolbachia substantially reduced sequencing depth in several samples, the sensitivity analysis was conducted on a Wolbachia-excluded table rarefied to 500 reads per sample. Taxonomic profiles were collapsed to the genus level and subjected to filtration to exclude rare taxa with fewer than 10 total reads and presence in less than 3 samples as described by Aželytė et al. (2024) [40].

Statistical Analysis of Microbial Diversity and Abundance

To characterize midgut bacterial communities of Ae. albopictus across different habitat types and localities in central Illinois, all statistical analyses were conducted in R v. 4.2.3 [41] and QIIME 2 [27]. Prior to diversity analyses, we visualized taxonomic composition using a genus-level collapsed feature table and sample metadata.

Alpha and beta diversity metrics were computed through the q2-diversity plugin in QIIME 2 [27]. Within-sample diversity was quantified using four commonly used metrics calculated in QIIME 2 via the core-metrics-phylogenetic pipeline: (1) Shannon diversity index, a diversity metric that incorporates both richness and evenness [42], (2) Observed Features, a richness metric representing the number of unique ASVs [43], (3) Faith’s phylogenetic diversity, a richness-related metric that incorporates phylogenetic relationships among taxa [44], and (4) Pielou’s evenness, an evenness metric describing the relative distribution of taxa [45]. To reflect the factorial design of the study, alpha diversity metrics were analyzed in R using factorial models that included city, habitat type, and their interaction as explanatory terms. In addition, grouped comparisons among the four region classes were evaluated using Kruskal-Wallis tests [46]. When appropriate, Dunn’s post hoc tests [47] were performed in R using the FSA package [48] with Benjamini-Hochberg correction [49] to support interpretation of specific pairwise patterns among region groups. Significant pairwise contrasts (adjusted p < 0.05) were summarized across all four alpha diversity metrics. The same factorial alpha diversity framework was also applied in a sensitivity analysis after excluding Wolbachia ASVs to assess whether the main diversity patterns were robust to the dominance of this endosymbiont.

Between-sample microbial community dissimilarity was assessed using both phylogenetic (Weighted and Unweighted UniFrac) and non-phylogenetic (Bray-Curtis and Jaccard) beta diversity metrics implemented in QIIME 2 [27, 50, 51]. A rarefaction depth of 1,400 was applied prior to distance matrix construction. Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) was used to visualize sample clustering. To reflect the factorial structure of the design, beta diversity was evaluated in R using a factorial Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance (PERMANOVA) [52] model with city, habitat type, and their interaction included as explanatory terms (distance ~ city * habitat). In addition, pairwise PERMANOVA tests among the four region classes were retained to support descriptive comparisons among specific groups, with pairwise PERMANOVA tests among the four region classes were retained to support descriptive comparisons among specific groups, with Benjamini-Hochberg correction [49] applied for multiple comparisons. To assess within-group variability (i.e., dispersion), Permutational Analysis of Multivariate Dispersions (PERMDISP) tests were conducted using the same four distance metrics [53]. All statistical tests and ordination plots were performed and visualized using QIIME 2 and R with appropriate custom scripts.

Differential abundance testing across the four combined city × habitat groups was performed using the compositionally aware method ANCOM (Analysis of Composition of Microbiomes) [54], implemented within the QIIME 2 platform [27]. ANCOM was conducted on a genus-level collapsed feature table. Prior to analysis, low-abundance taxa with fewer than 10 total reads and present in fewer than 3 samples were removed, and a pseudocount was added to enable log-ratio transformation. Statistical comparisons were conducted based on the region metadata variable, which represented the four combined city × habitat groups. Taxonomic assignments were integrated with ANCOM output using custom scripts in Python (pandas library). Subsequent visualization of taxa identified by ANCOM at the genus level was performed in R using ggplot2, ggtext, and ggrepel. For interpretation, we focused on taxa flagged by ANCOM as rejecting the null hypothesis and reported their corresponding W statistics.

Results

Sequencing Output and Taxonomic Composition

We sequenced the V4 region of the 16 S rRNA gene from midgut samples of 160 Ae. albopictus females collected during the summer of 2017 from residential and woodlands in two urban centers in central Illinois (Champaign-Urbana and Charleston). Following quality filtering, denoising, chimera removal, and rarefaction to 1,400 reads per sample, 112 samples were retained for downstream analyses. The retained samples were distributed as follows: residential Champaign-Urbana, n = 23; woodland Champaign-Urbana, n = 27; residential Charleston, n = 29; and woodland Charleston, n = 33 (Supplementary Table 1). The rarefaction threshold was selected based on alpha rarefaction curves (Supplementary Figure S1) to balance diversity coverage with sample retention. At this depth, 112 samples were retained, whereas higher thresholds would have reduced the dataset substantially, to 97 samples at 2,000 reads, 90 at 2,200 reads, 81 at 3,000 reads, and 56 at 5,000 reads. Consistent with this rationale, observed feature richness at 1,112 reads captured on average 97.1% of the richness observed at 5,556 reads among samples retained at both depths. Low-depth samples, as well as ASVs with fewer than 10 total reads or detected in fewer than three samples, were excluded to minimize noise and spurious detections.

The final dataset comprised 2,531 unique ASVs, representing a total of 646,885 reads. Each sample yielded an average of 5,776 ± 4,460 reads (mean ± s.d.). Taxonomic classification assigned ASVs to 34 bacterial phyla, 246 families, and 404 genera. To enhance biological interpretation, poorly resolved taxa (e.g., unclassified or uncultured) were removed prior to genus-level comparisons. These poorly resolved assignments accounted for 10.21% of total reads in the final filtered dataset, with the highest proportion observed in woodland Charleston samples (14.18%), followed by woodland Champaign-Urbana (9.86%), residential Champaign-Urbana (6.24%), and residential Charleston (3.43%). We visualized taxonomic structure across ecological gradients using a stacked bar plot of the 20 most abundant named genera, grouped by region (city × habitat type) (Fig. 2). The genus Wolbachia was the most abundant taxon across all regions, particularly in residential habitats, where it accounted for over 50% of total reads (Fig. 2).

Alpha Diversity Metrics

To evaluate within-sample microbial diversity (alpha diversity), we calculated four complementary metrics: Shannon diversity, Faith’s phylogenetic diversity, observed ASVs, and Pielou’s evenness. Diversity estimates were computed from rarefied data (1,400 reads per sample) across 112 Ae. albopictus midgut samples stratified by region (i.e., city × habitat type). Factorial analyses showed significant main effects of both city and habitat type on Shannon diversity, observed ASV richness, and Faith’s phylogenetic diversity, with significant city × habitat interactions also detected for these three metrics; corresponding effect size estimates are provided in Supplementary Table 2. For Pielou’s evenness, the interaction term was significant, whereas the main effects of city and habitat type were not (Supplementary Table 2). Significant variation was detected across regions for three of the four metrics (Fig. 3). Specifically, woodland habitats exhibited significantly higher Shannon diversity, observed ASV richness, and phylogenetic diversity compared to residential habitats (Kruskal–Wallis p < 1 × 10⁻⁷ for all three metrics). Among regions, Charleston woodlands consistently harbored the highest microbial richness and phylogenetic diversity, whereas residential samples from both cities displayed comparatively lower diversity levels.

(A) Shannon Diversity, (B) Observed Features, (C) Faith’s phylogenetic diversity, and (D) Pielou’s evenness were calculated from samples collected in residential and woodland habitats in Champaign-Urbana and Charleston. Habitat type is shown along the x-axis (residential vs. woodland), while city identity (Champaign-Urbana vs. Charleston) is distinguished by color and shape. Boxplots display the median, interquartile ranges, and individual sample points. Statistical comparisons between groups were performed using Kruskal-Wallis tests, and corresponding p-values are indicated above each plot. A spacing between the residential and woodland groups was introduced to enhance visual separation

Shannon diversity was higher in woodland habitats, a pattern that appears to be driven primarily by greater taxonomic richness rather than by broad differences in evenness (Fig. 3A). Observed ASV richness (Fig. 3B) and Faith’s phylogenetic diversity (Fig. 3C) followed the same pattern, with the highest values observed in Charleston woodlands. In contrast, Pielou’s evenness did not differ significantly among the four region groups (Kruskal–Wallis p = 0.69; Fig. 3D), suggesting that while the number and phylogenetic breadth of taxa varied, the relative distribution of taxa remained broadly similar at the group level. However, the factorial model detected a modest but significant city × habitat interaction for evenness. A sensitivity analysis excluding ASVs assigned to Wolbachia showed that the main alpha diversity patterns were retained. After removal of Wolbachia and rarefaction to 500 reads per sample, 98 samples were retained for sensitivity analysis, including residential Champaign-Urbana (n = 17), woodland Champaign-Urbana (n = 26), residential Charleston (n = 22), and woodland Charleston (n = 33). Factorial analyses continued to detect significant effects of city, habitat type, and their interaction for Shannon diversity, observed richness, and Faith’s phylogenetic diversity, indicating that the primary diversity patterns were not driven solely by this dominant endosymbiont (Supplementary Table 3). For Pielou’s evenness, city and the interaction term remained significant, whereas habitat type was marginally non-significant in the Wolbachia-excluded dataset (Supplementary Table 3).

Post hoc Dunn’s tests with Benjamini-Hochberg correction (Supplementary Table 4) further confirmed that Charleston woodland samples exhibited significantly higher Shannon diversity, observed ASVs, and Faith’s phylogenetic diversity compared to all other region groups (adjusted p < 0.01). Additionally, Champaign-Urbana woodland samples differed significantly from both Charleston and Champaign-Urbana residential groups in observed richness and phylogenetic diversity.

Beta Diversity Metrics

To evaluate differences in Ae. albopictus midgut bacterial communities across distinct ecological settings, we employed both phylogenetic (Weighted and Unweighted UniFrac) and non-phylogenetic (Bray-Curtis and Jaccard) beta diversity metrics. PCoA based on Bray-Curtis distances revealed distinct clustering patterns by region, particularly for samples collected from woodland habitats in Charleston, suggesting region-specific microbial compositions (Fig. 4).

Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) plot based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarities of Aedes albopictus midgut bacterial communities. Samples are grouped by region (city × habitat type), with color and shape indicating geographic location and habitat type, respectively. Each point represents the bacterial community of an individual mosquito midgut. Ellipses indicate 95% confidence intervals for each group. The clustering pattern reveals clear separation among regions, particularly between woodland and residential habitats, with Charleston woodland forming the most distinct cluster

Factorial PERMANOVA analyses showed significant main effects of both city and habitat type across all four beta diversity metrics, with corresponding R² values reported in Supplementary Table 5. City was significant for Bray-Curtis, Jaccard, Unweighted UniFrac, and Weighted UniFrac, while habitat type was also significant for all four metrics. A significant city × habitat interaction was detected for Unweighted UniFrac, whereas interaction terms for Bray-Curtis, Jaccard, and Weighted UniFrac were not statistically significant or were only marginally significant (Supplementary Table 5). PERMANOVA analyses based on all four-distance metrics detected significant differences in beta diversity among the four regional groups (Bray-Curtis: pseudo-F = 1.89, p = 0.001; Jaccard: pseudo-F = 1.98, p = 0.001; Weighted UniFrac: pseudo-F = 4.98, p = 0.001; Unweighted UniFrac: pseudo-F = 6.12, p = 0.001). These grouped comparisons supported the region-level patterns observed in the factorial analysis.

Pairwise PERMANOVA comparisons further revealed that most region combinations exhibited statistically significant differences in beta diversity. For Bray-Curtis dissimilarities, five out of six pairwise comparisons were significant after multiple testing correction (q < 0.05; Supplementary Table 6), with the strongest divergence observed between residential Charleston and woodland Champaign-Urbana (pseudo-F = 2.53, q = 0.006). The only non-significant Bray-Curtis comparison was between residential Champaign-Urbana and residential Charleston (q = 0.069), suggesting a degree of similarity in urban microbiota between cities. A similar pattern was observed with Jaccard distances: five out of six regional pairs differed significantly (q < 0.01; Supplementary Table 7), whereas residential Champaign-Urbana and residential Charleston did not (q = 0.053), again pointing to limited divergence in taxonomic membership between urban environments.

Unweighted UniFrac distances, which incorporate phylogenetic presence/absence information, revealed significant differences across all six region pairs (q = 0.001; Supplementary Table 8), reflecting strong phylogenetic turnover. In contrast, Weighted UniFrac distances (which account for phylogenetic relationships weighted by abundance) yielded five significant comparisons (q < 0.01; Supplementary Table 9); the only non-significant result was again between residential Champaign-Urbana and residential Charleston (q = 0.419), indicating shared dominant lineages between urban samples.

To determine whether these compositional differences were accompanied by variation in within-group community heterogeneity, we conducted beta dispersion (PERMDISP) analyses using the same four metrics. Global dispersion differences were significant for Bray-Curtis (F = 2.56, p = 0.042) and Jaccard (F = 3.65, p = 0.006) distances. For Bray-Curtis, pairwise comparisons identified a single significant contrast: residential Champaign-Urbana vs. woodland Champaign-Urbana (F = 3.98, q = 0.036; Supplementary Table 10). This suggests that dispersion differences were primarily driven by increased variability within the Champaign-Urbana sites. Jaccard-based dispersion tests revealed broader variability, with significant differences in dispersion between multiple region pairs (e.g., residential Champaign-Urbana vs. woodland Charleston, q = 0.02; residential Charleston vs. woodland Charleston, q = 0.02; Supplementary Table 11), highlighting greater heterogeneity in woodland-associated communities.

In contrast, PERMDISP based on Unweighted UniFrac (F = 0.46, p = 0.726; Supplementary Table 12) and Weighted UniFrac (F = 1.69, p = 0.143; Supplementary Table 13) did not indicate significant differences in dispersion across regions. Most pairwise comparisons for these metrics were also non-significant (q > 0.8), except for a single significant comparison between woodland Champaign-Urbana and woodland Charleston (q = 0.03; Supplementary Table 13) in Weighted UniFrac, reflecting subtle phylogenetic heterogeneity between forested sites.

Differential Abundance

Differential abundance analysis using ANCOM on the genus-level table identified two genera that rejected the null hypothesis across the four combined city × habitat groups: Asinibacterium and Pseudomonas. These taxa showed the highest W statistics in the ANCOM output, with Asinibacterium represented by two genus-level features (W = 2525 and 2512) and Pseudomonas by one feature with W = 2464 (Supplementary Table 14).

Bar plot visualization (Fig. 5A) indicated that Asinibacterium predominantly associated with woodland habitats, particularly in Champaign-Urbana, whereas Pseudomonas showed higher mean abundance in residential sites, especially in Champaign-Urbana. These patterns were further supported by boxplots showing the distribution of raw abundances across regions (Fig. 5B), which highlighted a markedly higher abundance of Asinibacterium in samples from woodland areas in Champaign-Urbana and elevated Pseudomonas levels in residential samples.

A bubble plot depicting genus-level mean abundance by region (Fig. 5C) reinforced these habitat-specific trends, with the largest bubble for Asinibacterium in woodland Champaign-Urbana and for Pseudomonas in residential Champaign-Urbana. The overall distribution of W-statistics (Fig. 5D) showed that these genera were clearly separated from the bulk of taxa with low W values, consistent with their identification by ANCOM as differentially abundant features.

Discussion

The mosquito gut microbiome has a significant implication in shaping vector immunity, metabolism, and disease transmission, yet the extent to which environmental factors modulate these microbial communities remains poorly understood. To better understand how environmental context shapes mosquito-associated microbiota, we compared the gut microbiome of Ae. albopictus mosquitoes collected from residential and woodland habitats in two communities in central Illinois. Our findings demonstrate that both habitat type and geographic locality significantly influence the microbial diversity and community composition of Ae. albopictus midguts. In addition, the factorial analyses indicate that these effects are not entirely independent, as interaction terms were detected for multiple alpha diversity metrics and for unweighted UniFrac beta diversity, highlighting environmental context as a multidimensional driver of microbiome variability in this species.

The analyses of relative abundance and alpha diversity revealed that, at both locations, woodland samples exhibited greater bacterial diversity than residential samples. These pairwise differences emphasize the joint influence of habitat and geography on alpha diversity patterns in Ae. albopictus midguts. The factorial alpha diversity analyses confirmed these patterns, showing that city, habitat type, and their interaction significantly influenced Shannon diversity, observed richness, and Faith’s phylogenetic diversity, indicating that habitat-associated differences in microbial diversity were not constant across the two localities. Collectively, these findings highlight that woodland habitats, particularly in Charleston, were associated with more diverse and phylogenetically enriched midgut bacterial communities relative to residential settings. Consistent with this pattern, woodland groups also contained a higher proportion of poorly resolved reads than residential groups, with the highest proportion observed in woodland Charleston samples. This finding suggests that woodland habitats may include less characterized environmental microbial reservoirs that warrant further exploration. One plausible explanation for this pattern is the higher plant diversity typically found in woodland environments, which may promote increased diversity of bacterial genera compared to residential areas [55, 56]. Mosquitoes can acquire microbial organisms from their environment, both from aquatic habitats during juvenile developmental stages and from plants on which they feed for nectar during adulthood [57]. Aquatic mosquito habitats are believed to be enriched by detritus from surrounding plants [58]. Microbiota associated with specific plant species may be ingested by mosquito larvae, some of which could survive gut clearing during molting and persist into adulthood [59]. In adult mosquitoes, microorganisms may also be acquired during nectar consumption and subsequently colonize the gut [60]. Given that plant-associated microbiota are often species-specific [55, 61, 62], habitats with greater plant diversity are likely to support more diverse microbial communities, potentially resulting in higher bacterial diversity within adult mosquito guts. However, because plant diversity and associated environmental microbiota were not directly measured in the present study, this explanation remains a hypothesis requiring targeted environmental sampling, including vegetation, water, and plant-associated soil microbiomes, to test whether woodland habitats differ from more urbanized and potentially homogenized environments. Furthermore, while grouped comparisons did not indicate strong differences in evenness, the factorial analysis detected a modest interaction effect for Pielou’s evenness, suggesting that the relative balance of taxa may also respond to locality-specific habitat context, albeit more subtly than richness-based metrics.

Analyses of beta diversity indicated that the microbial communities in mosquito guts differed between the two urban centers and across the two habitat types. While residential samples from Champaign-Urbana and Charleston were only marginally distinct, the greatest divergence occurred between residential sites in Champaign-Urbana and woodland sites in Charleston. These findings demonstrate that, Ae. albopictus midgut bacterial communities exhibit significant compositional differences across ecological settings, with the most pronounced distinctions observed between woodland and residential habitats. These patterns were consistently detected across both phylogenetic and non-phylogenetic beta diversity metrics and were especially evident when comparing samples from Champaign-Urbana and Charleston. Factorial PERMANOVA further showed significant main effects of both city and habitat type across all four beta diversity metrics, whereas interaction effects were strongest for unweighted UniFrac and weaker or non-significant for the other metrics. This suggests that locality-dependent habitat effects were particularly evident for phylogenetic presence-absence structure, although interpretation of beta diversity differences should remain cautious for metrics in which dispersion heterogeneity was also detected. While PERMANOVA results support differences in community structure among groups, the significant PERMDISP results for Bray-Curtis and Jaccard indicate that heterogeneity in within-group dispersion may also have contributed to the observed beta diversity patterns. Therefore, these results should be interpreted as reflecting both differences in community composition and, for some metrics, differences in dispersion. These patterns suggest that both locality and habitat type play significant roles in shaping mosquito gut microbial communities, and their effects may be complex. Studies have shown that plant microbiomes may be influenced by soil microbiomes, which continuously interact with the surrounding environment, including plant root systems, abiotic factors such as pH, salinity, and microclimate, as well as animal activities and human practices [62–65]. It is likely that the mosquito gut microbiomes in the woodlands of both urban centers were shaped by plant microbiomes originating from the soil in the area, which is generally stable unless altered by human activity. In contrast, mosquito gut microbiomes in residential areas are likely influenced by plants that reside in materials used in development, which are products of human practices. Because man-made construction and landscaping materials lack plant root systems and contribute limited microbiota to the soil, plants in the residential areas may therefore support relatively simple microbial communities, which in turn may contribute to less diverse mosquito gut communities than those associated with woodland environments [66]. Furthermore, the use of similar construction and landscaping materials in modern residential development, combined with relatively simple built-environment features such as air circulation and water treatment systems and human occupancy may lead to reduced differentiation in microbial communities among residential areas [67]. Consistent with this interpretation, grouped comparisons showed limited separation between residential samples from the two cities in several beta diversity analyses, whereas stronger differences were observed when woodland-associated communities were involved.

The differential abundance analysis using ANCOM on the genus-level table identified Asinibacterium and Pseudomonas as differentially abundant across the four combined city × habitat groups. While Asinibacterium was abundant in woodland samples from Champaign-Urbana and Charleston, Pseudomonas was associated with both residential and woodland samples from Champaign-Urbana. These findings suggest that certain bacterial taxa may exhibit selective associations with specific habitat types or geographic contexts, consistent with previous studies demonstrating that the location of mosquito habitats can shape symbiotic microbial communities [68, 69]. For example, a variant of Asaia in Culex pipiens differed markedly in abundance between two nearby sampling sites in France, despite the mosquitoes belonging to the same species [70]. In a case study involving Ae. aegypti, mosquitoes collected from different habitat types exhibited bacterial genera that were differentially abundant depending on habitat type [71]. Asinibacterium spp. has previously been detected in a range of contexts, including donkey milk [72] and winter ticks, Dermacentor albipictus [73]. The bacterial genus is also found in environments contaminated with heavy metals including uranium [74] and has been described for dehalogenation capacities in breaking down halogenated organic pollutants in the environment [75, 76]. Likewise, species of Pseudomonas have been reported from heavy metal leachate-contaminated soils [77] and are increasingly recognized for roles in plant growth promotion and protection against plant pathogens [78]. In the context of the present study, these previously reported associations should be viewed as potential ecological hypotheses rather than direct explanations for the patterns observed here, because no environmental chemical or pollutant measurements were collected. Their presence in mosquito midguts may reflect environmental acquisition pathways linked to vegetation, nectar sources, or other habitat-associated microbial reservoirs.

Because Wolbachia was highly abundant in several samples, particularly in residential groups, we additionally performed a sensitivity analysis after excluding ASVs assigned to this genus. The main alpha diversity patterns were retained after Wolbachia removal, with significant effects of city, habitat type, and their interaction still detected for Shannon diversity, observed richness, and Faith’s phylogenetic diversity. These results suggest that the observed diversity differences among groups were not driven solely by Wolbachia dominance, although this endosymbiont may have contributed to some variation in taxon balance, particularly for evenness.

An additional limitation of this study is the absence of negative extraction and PCR amplification controls, which are strongly recommended for low-biomass microbiome studies because reagent- and laboratory-derived DNA can distort diversity estimates and community comparisons [79, 80]. Although we applied conservative prevalence- and abundance-based filtering, such steps do not substitute for formal contaminant identification approaches that leverage negative controls and/or DNA concentration-dependent patterns [29, 31]. Notably, several highly abundant genera observed here, including Pseudomonas, Acinetobacter, Sphingomonas, Brevundimonas, Corynebacterium, and Bradyrhizobium, overlap with genera reported from sequenced blank controls and commonly cited contaminant lists [79]. However, mosquito tissues can be low-biomass and susceptible to contamination, and some taxa frequently labeled as contaminants have also been reported as mosquito-associated bacteria, indicating that taxonomic identity alone may not be sufficient to distinguish contamination from true colonization [81]. Accordingly, these taxa, as well as low-abundance features more broadly, should be interpreted with appropriate caution in the absence of stepwise negative controls, and future work should incorporate extraction and PCR blanks together with supporting quantitative data to enable statistical contaminant identification [29, 31].

Data from the current study showed that the microbiomes of Ae. albopictus midguts vary by geographic location and habitat type, and the factorial analyses further suggest that these effects can interact. Because mosquitoes were collected between May and October, seasonal variation was not evaluated explicitly in the present study and may have contributed to some of the observed differences among groups. Although the present study was not designed to determine the environmental origins of these microbiotas, our findings suggest that habitat-specific environmental inputs may contribute to the observed patterns. To better identify the sources of microbiome variation, future studies would benefit from refining the criteria used to select sampling sites, with particular attention to habitat location and vegetation structure including plant abundance and diversity, as well as from incorporating parallel sampling of soil and plant-associated microbial communities and evaluating the temporal effects.

In conclusion, our results demonstrate that bacterial community composition and abundance in the midgut of Ae. albopictus differ significantly by habitat type and locality, and that specific taxa exhibit distinct habitat associations. Taken together with the factorial analyses, these findings indicate that urban locality and habitat context shape mosquito-associated microbiota through both independent and, in some cases, interacting effects. These findings underscore the importance of environmental context in shaping mosquito-associated microbiota and provide a foundation for future work exploring the ecological drivers of microbial variation in mosquito vectors.
