Targeted review of maximum residue levels (MRLs) for chlorfenapyr
Authors: European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), Giulia Bellisai, Giovanni Bernasconi, Marco Binaglia, Luis Carrasco Cabrera, Irene Castellan, Anna Federica Castoldi, Arianna Chiusolo, Katia Chukwubike, Federica Crivellente, Monica Del Aguila, Lucien Ferreira, German Giner Santonja, Luna Greco, Frederique Istace, Samira Jarrah, Anna Lanzoni, Renata Leuschner, Iris Mangas, Javier Martinez, Ileana Miron, Stefanie Nave, Martina Panzarea, Juan Manuel Parra Morte, Ragnor Pedersen, Hermine Reich, Silvia Ruocco, Miguel Santos, Alessia Pia Scarlato, Andrea Terron, Anne Theobald, Manuela Tiramani, Alessia Verani
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn accordance with Article 43 of Regulation (EC) 396/2005, EFSA received a request from the European Commission to review the existing maximum residue levels (MRLs) for the non‐approved active substance chlorfenapyr in view of the possible lowering of the MRL set for tea. This current EU MRL is based on an import tolerance established in 2007. EFSA reviewed the toxicological data assessed by other scientific bodies, proposing toxicological reference values to be used for an indicative risk assessment, noting that the values are affected by additional, non‐standard uncertainties. According to the indicative chronic and acute dietary risk assessment the existing MRL for tea does not pose an unacceptable risk for consumers. Further risk management discussions are required to decide which of the risk management options proposed by EFSA should be implemented in the EU MRL legislation.Keywords: chlorfenapyr, consumer risk assessment, MRL setting, non‐approved active substance, residue definitions, toxicological evaluation

SUMMARY

The European Commission submitted a request to EFSA for a targeted review of maximum residue limits (MRLs) for 10 active substances no longer approved in the EU, but for which MRLs greater than the limit of quantification (LOQ) are still in place and for which Member States have identified potential consumer health risks. Separate reasoned opinions should be provided in accordance with Article 43 of Regulation (EC) 396/2005, for each of the substances included in this mandate, one of them being chlorfenapyr.

For chlorfenapyr, the only EU MRL higher than the LOQ is the one for tea, set at the level of 50 mg/kg. In accordance with the terms of reference, EFSA investigated the origin of this MRL; it was found to be an import tolerance which was established in 2007 by Directive 2007/28, 1 following an assessment of a Japanese GAP by the competent authorities in the United Kingdom. The MRL was taken over in Regulation (EC) No 396/2005 in Annex II. Further risk management discussions are required to decide whether this existing EU MRL for tea is considered to be sufficiently substantiated, as the database supporting the MRL is not fully compliant with the current data requirements.

EFSA shared a draft reasoned opinion with Member States and the European Reference Laboratories (EURLs) for consultation via a written procedure. Comments received were considered during the finalisation of this reasoned opinion. The following conclusions are derived.

The metabolism of chlorfenapyr in plant and animal was previously investigated in the framework of the EU evaluation under Directive 91/414/EEC, as well as by JMPR. According to the results of the metabolism studies assessed, the residue for enforcement for plant and animal commodities was defined as chlorfenapyr in the EU. The same residue definition was derived by JMPR, which considered the residue as fat soluble. Since no MRLs for livestock are currently set in the EU regulation, the residue was not labelled as fat soluble, but it should be in case MRLs for animal commodities will be set at the LOQ. Regarding risk assessment, the EU residue definition was proposed as the sum of chlorfenapyr and 4‐bromo‐2‐(p‐chlorophenyl)‐5‐(trifluoromethyl)‐pyrrole‐3‐carbonitrile (tralopyril), expressed as tralopyril, for plants and animal commodities. However, this residue definition was not formally approved at EU level and there are no EU TRVs established for tralopyril. JMPR defined the residue for risk assessment as sum of chlorfenapyr and 10‐fold 4‐bromo‐2‐(p‐chlorophenyl)‐5‐(trifluoromethyl)‐pyrrole‐3‐carbonitrile (tralopyril); this residue definition acknowledges that tralopyril has – according to the JMPR assessment – a higher toxicity than parent chlorfenapyr. Hence, the residue definitions for risk assessment cover the same components, but they are not fully comparable.

Analytical methods are available for the enforcement of the currently applicable EU residue definition for enforcement in all four main plant matrices with an LOQ of 0.01 mg/kg and in tea with an LOQ of 0.8 mg/kg. Chlorfenapyr can be enforced in muscle, fat and milk with an LOQ of 0.01 mg/kg and in liver and kidney with LOQ of 0.05 mg/kg. According to the EURLs, multi‐residue analytical methods with an LOQ of 0.01 mg/kg are available for the routine analysis of chlorfenapyr in high‐water, high‐acid, high‐oil content and dry commodities, and a default LOQ of 0.01 mg/kg is also deemed achievable to monitor chlorfenapyr in all commodities of animal origin.

The TRVs derived at EU level and of those established by the JMPR were assessed against the current standards.

As critical issues were identified, a Member States experts' consultation took place. The experts concluded that an acceptable daily intake (ADI) of 0.028 mg/kg body weight (bw) per day and an acute reference dose (ARfD) of 0.028 mg/kg bw are appropriate for chlorfenapyr, noting a number of uncertainties as the data package did not fully comply with current scientific standards. With regards to the assessment of chlorfenapyr metabolite, tralopyril, approved as a biocidal active substance, and for which both the JMPR and the Biocidal Products Committee established a 10‐fold potency factor compared to chlorfenapyr, EFSA could not conclude on its toxicological profile while its genotoxicity potential is not clarified according to current standards. The following data would be required to address this concern:

It is highlighted that in line with the Terms of Reference, EFSA did not perform a full re‐evaluation of the toxicological data assessed by JMPR and within the framework of the biocide assessment; the fact that the relevant studies did not fully comply with the current requirements for reporting the study design and the results according to OECD test guidelines leads to additional uncertainties.

It is noted that, the TRVs proposed by the Rapporteur Member State in an assessment under Directive 91/414/EEC were not formally adopted by the European Commission. As these values are currently reported in the EU pesticides database and were used in previous EFSA assessment, risk managers should consider replacing them by the TRVs derived under the current assessment.

Indicative chronic and acute exposure calculations were performed using revision 3.1 of PRIMo, considering the relevant risk assessment values for the current MRL on tea. Since tralopyril was not analysed in the available trials, to account for the contribution of tralopyril to the overall toxicological burden, the conversion factor from enforcement to risk assessment (CF) of 1.17 as derived by the JMPR was applied. For all other commodities, the LOQ was used in the exposure calculation. The highest chronic exposure was calculated for FR adult (7% ADI), while the highest acute exposure was for tea accounting for 49% of the ARfD. When considering the lower EU TRVs currently reported in the EU pesticides database, chronic exposure represented 13% of the ADI (FR adult) and the highest acute exposure amounted to 91% of the ARfD.

It is underlined that exposure calculations are indicative because of additional non‐standard uncertainties related to the CF applied and derived from the metabolism studies on lettuces, which might not be representative for tea, the reduced number of trials available and missing toxicological data.

BACKGROUND

In March 2021, a Member State submitted to the European Commission the results of a screening performed on all maximum residue levels (MRLs) of active substances used in plant protection products that are not approved in the EU. The list contained 904 substances; for 297 of them, at least one MRL was set at a level above the limit of quantification (LOQ).

For 219 of these substances, the MRLs are not related to the uses of the substances in plant protection products (e.g. MRLs reflect the use of biocides or veterinary medical product, or MRLs are set to account for their occurrence in certain food due to environmental persistence, or their natural occurrence). For the other 78 substances, the MRLs were established either based on formerly approved uses in the EU, on import tolerance requests, or on Codex maximum residue limits (CXLs).

Some of these substances were never approved in the EU, or their approval was withdrawn before 2008, and therefore they did not fall within the scope of the systematic review of all existing MRLs under Article 12 of Regulation (EC) No 396/2005. 2

A second Member State conducted additional analysis, identifying potential consumer risk for some of the MRLs set for these active substances.

Based on these analyses, the European Commission conducted a prioritisation exercise to identify substances for which existing MRLs should be reviewed with high priority. The prioritisation was also discussed and agreed with Member States during several meetings of the Standing Committee on Plants, Animals, Food and Feed (SCoPAFF), section Phytopharmaceuticals – Pesticides residues (September 2021, 3 November 2021, 4 and February 2022 5 ). The SCoPAFF agreed that ten active substances, for which potential consumer risks were identified, should be assessed by EFSA as a priority. One of the substances identified for being assessed with high priority is chlorfenapyr.

The European Commission proposed to mandate EFSA to provide a targeted review of MRLs for the substances concerned without delay. Due to the urgency of the subject, EFSA was invited to consider, if appropriate, delivering a separate reasoned opinion for each of the substances included in this mandate, as to be able to start providing outcomes to the Commission as soon as possible and successively. In this reasoned opinion EFSA covered the targeted review of the MRLs for chlorfenapyr.

Comments received by 8 September 2023 were considered during the finalisation of this reasoned opinion (ToR 13).

Further supporting document to this reasoned opinion is the Member States consultation report (EFSA, 2023c). The exposure calculations for all crops reported in the framework of this review performed using the EFSA Pesticide Residues Intake Model (PRIMo) are also key supporting documents. Metabolism studies on oranges, tomatoes, potatoes, head lettuces and cottons were assessed in the framework of the EU evaluation (Spain, 1998) under Directive 91/414/EEC. In root crops (potatoes), chlorfenapyr does not translocate from treated plant parts to the tubers. In the other three categories of crops (fruits, cereals and oilseeds), unchanged parent chlorfenapyr was the predominant component of the residue. Two metabolites CL303268 (4‐bromo‐2‐(p‐chlorophenyl)‐5‐(trifluoromethyl)‐pyrrole‐3‐carbonitrile, named tralopyril) and CL325195 (oxidation product of tralopyril) were shown to be toxicologically relevant in rat. However, according to plant metabolism studies they are not expected to be quantified at levels above 0.01 mg/kg in the plant products. Therefore, the residue definition for monitoring and risk assessment in all plant commodities was initially proposed by the RMS as chlorfenapyr (Spain, 1998). During the peer review on the DAR, a concern was expressed about the commercial and in‐house storage period of the plant products and the possible appearance of the more acutely toxic metabolite CL303268 (tralopyril) which is the main photolysis degradation product. Therefore, the residue definition for risk assessment in plants was finally proposed as the sum of chlorfenapyr and tralopyril, expressed as tralopyril (ECCO, 1999a).

In addition to the metabolism studies assessed at EU level, JMPR assessed an additional study on tomatoes. This additional study showed similar results, with parent chlorfenapyr being the major compound identified (FAO and WHO, 2018a, 2018b). JMPR concluded the residue definition in plant for monitoring to be chlorfenapyr only. For risk assessment, JMPR noted that tralopyril, although present in low proportions in metabolism studies, up to 3.3% TRR), has the potential to contribute significantly to the overall toxicological burden due to its higher toxicological potency. For the residue definition for risk assessment a potency factor of 10 was included for tralopyril, compared to the parent compound. Consequently, the residue definition for risk assessment was agreed as the sum of chlorfenapyr and 10 times its metabolite tralopyril (FAO and WHO, 2018a, 2018b).

The nature of chlorfenapyr residues in livestock was investigated and assessed in the framework of the EU evaluation (Spain, 1998) under Directive 91/414/EEC. In the metabolism studies with goats and laying hens, chlorfenapyr was identified in significant proportions in all animal matrices. Additional metabolites considered toxicologically relevant, and a mixture of metabolites linked to peptides were also determined in some matrices. In the DAR, the RMS Spain proposed initially a residue definition for risk assessment for muscle and milk to be chlorfenapyr, for fat and eggs to be chlorfenapyr and metabolite CL303268 (tralopyril) and for liver and kidney to be chlorfenapyr and metabolites CL303268 (tralopyril) and CL325195. During Expert Meeting (ECCO, 1999a), a residue definition for risk assessment for all animal matrices was finally proposed as the sum of chlorfenapyr and tralopyril, expressed as tralopyril.

The nature of chlorfenapyr residues in livestock was also investigated and assessed by JMPR (FAO and WHO, 2012a, 2012b, 2018a, 2018b). Based on the same studies evaluated by Spain in the DAR, the JMPR proposed the residue definition for monitoring in all animal matrices as chlorfenapyr, and for risk assessment as the sum of chlorfenapyr and 10 times its metabolite tralopyril (FAO and WHO, 2018a, 2018b).

Analytical methods for MRLs enforcement

Analytical methods for the determination of chlorfenapyr residues were assessed in the framework of the EU evaluation (Spain, 1998) under Directive 91/414/EEC and in the framework of JMPR evaluation (FAO and WHO, 2012a, 2012b, 2018a, 2018b). According to the information assessed by the RMS and by the JMPR, analytical methods are available to enforce residues of chlorfenapyr in all four main plant matrices with an LOQ of 0.01 mg/kg. A specific method for the analysis in tea by gas chromatography with electron capture detector (GC‐ECD) with an LOQ of 0.8 mg/kg was evaluated by the JMPR (FAO and WHO, 2012a, 2012b).

Chlorfenapyr can be enforced in food of animal origin with an LOQ of 0.01 mg/kg in muscle, fat and milk and an LOQ of 0.05 mg/kg in kidney and liver (FAO and WHO, 2012a, 2012b; Spain, 1998). An analytical method for enforcement of residues in eggs was not available.

During the data collection, the EURLs provided information on a quick, easy, cheap, effective, rugged, and safe (QuEChERS) multi‐residue analytical method using gas chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry (GC–MS/MS) technique, with an LOQ of 0.01 mg/kg for the routine analysis of chlorfenapyr in high water, high acid, high oil and dry commodities. No data were provided regarding the possible enforcement of chlorfenapyr in complex matrices such as tea. According to the EURLs, chlorfenapyr can be monitored in commodities of animal origin (muscle, liver, milk and eggs) at the default LOQ of 0.01 mg/kg while, based on the experience gained with these matrices, an LOQ of 0.01 mg/kg is deemed achievable for animal fat and kidney (EURLs, 2022). The EURLs reported that the analytical standard for chlorfenapyr is commercially available (EURLs, 2022). It is concluded that analytical methods are available for the main plant and animal matrices and for tea.

Existing MRLs

The EU MRLs for chlorfenapyr are established in Annex II of Regulation (EC) No 396/2005. The only MRL higher than the LOQ is the MRL for tea, set at the level of 50 mg/kg.

It is noted that for a number of food products, codex maximum residue limits (CXLs) were established in 2019; however, these have not been taken over in the EU legislation since EU expressed reservations on all the proposed Codex MRLs in view of the approach taken by JMPR to derive input values for risk assessment using conversion factors derived from metabolism studies and because of an acute intake concern for tea, calculated with acute consumption data of Ireland implemented in EFSA PRIMo rev. 3.1 (CCPR, 2019). The summary of the toxicological studies reported in the DAR (Spain, 1998) is not detailed and does not meet the current standards. However, additional details, including tabulated results, are reported in the CAR and JMPR monograph, that mostly allow for an independent review of the conclusion reached by both authorities.

The genotoxicity data package for chlorfenapyr contains studies assessing the three endpoints, i.e. gene mutation in bacterial and mammalian cells (in vitro), clastogenicity (in vitro and in vivo) and aneugenicity (in vivo). 18 In addition, an in vitro unscheduled DNA synthesis (UDS) assay is reported whose TG (TG 482) was deleted in the meantime.

The in vitro studies on gene mutation showed negative results. Two in vitro chromosome aberration tests were also negative. These two latter tests investigated also polyploidy and one of them presented a single instance of statistically significant increase in polyploidy at the low dose level tested with metabolic activation and harvested after 24 h. Considering the lack of dose–response relationship, this finding was considered unlikely to be of biological significance. The in vivo micronucleus test was negative; however, it remains uncertain whether the bone marrow was properly exposed to confirm the results obtained. Overall, this deficiency was not regarded as critical to assess clastogenicity since the in vitro studies are negative, and it was concluded that chlorfenapyr is unlikely to be mutagenic or clastogenic. The aneugenic potential of chlorfenapyr has however not been fully investigated (polyploidy investigations in vitro are not sufficient to cover aneugenicity) and taking into considerations the limitations of the in vivo MN study, this remains as an uncertainty regarding the genotoxicity potential of chlorfenapyr. EFSA notes that the submission of an in vitro micronucleus test would address this uncertainty.

The critical no observed adverse effect levels (NOAELs) were identified in the long‐term studies. In mice, the NOAEL is 2.8 mg/kg bw per day based on reduced body weight (bw), body weight gain and vacuolation of the white matter of the brain observed at 16.6 mg/kg bw per day in an 18‐month carcinogenicity study. In rats, the NOAEL is 2.9 mg/kg bw per day based on reduced body weight, body weight gain, changes in clinical chemistry and haematological parameters, and increased liver weight with hepatocellular enlargement observed in the 2‐year toxicity and carcinogenicity study, supported by the NOAEL of 2.6 mg/kg bw per day for reduced body weight gain and feed efficiency, and neurohistopathological findings (vacuolar myelinopathy) seen at 13.6 mg/kg bw per day in a 1‐year neurotoxicity study in rats. A lower NOAEL of 2.1 mg/kg bw per day was proposed by the RMS based on an increased occurrence of lymphoid follicles in the stomach of dogs in a 1‐year toxicity study. This finding was also observed in control animals and corresponds to a well‐known background condition in dogs, possibly associated by the presence of Helicobacter in dog stomach and was therefore considered not treatment‐related by the CA and JMPR.

The use of an additional uncertainty factor (UF) was discussed, taking into account the data gaps and uncertainties listed above.

Chlorfenapyr is unlikely to be mutagenic or clastogenic. An uncertainty remains with regards to its aneugenic potential, however aneugenicity is recognised as relying on a threshold mode of action and therefore the uncertainty does not preclude the setting of TRVs. It was noted that the dataset is quite consistent with regards to species sensitivity and this may indicate that humans are less likely to be more sensitive than the tested species. The lack of an interspecies in vitro comparative metabolism study is therefore possibly of lower concern. Regarding the ED hazard potential, the data set does not show specific ED‐related effects, while neurotoxicity is of higher concern. The critical effects identified for chlorfenapyr are vacuolation of the white matter of the brain at 16.6 mg/kg bw per day in the long‐term study in mice (with a NOAEL of 2.8 mg/kg bw per day), multifocal vacuolation (minimal to moderate severity) of the white matter of the brain in rat offspring at 15 mg/kg bw per day in the DNT study (with NOAEL of 10 mg/kg bw per day), pup deaths and decreased motor activity in the same offspring at 10 mg/kg bw per day (with a NOAEL of 5 mg/kg bw per day) and neurohistopathological findings (vacuolar myelinopathy) at 13.6 mg/kg bw per day in the 1‐year neurotoxicity study in rats (with a NOAEL of 2.6 mg/kg bw per day). Clear NOAELs were identified for these effects and it was concluded that there is no need to increase the standard UF of 100.

The ADI proposed by the RMS and CA of 0.028 mg/kg bw per day based on the NOAEL of 2.8 mg/kg bw per day for reduced body weight, body weight gain and vacuolation of the white matter of the brain from the 18‐month study in mice and applying an UF of 100, was considered appropriate by all experts, even if uncertainties remain to comply fully with current standards. The ADI established by the JMPR relies on the same study and endpoints, but was rounded to 0.03 mg/kg bw per day.

Taking into account the neurotoxicity concern for chlorfenapyr, an ARfD needs to be established. The critical study for acute effects is a pharmacological study in mice used by the ECCO meeting and JMPR to derive the ARfD. This study from 1994 was performed on rats, mice and rabbits, to evaluate effects on the central nervous system, respiratory and cardiovascular systems, autonomic nervous system, gastrointestinal system, skeletal muscle and blood coagulation using three males per dose level. The acceptability of the study is limited considering the low number of animals used per group, behavioural data for control animals were not presented, behavioural evaluations were not conducted blindly and histopathological evaluation was not performed. Mice presented the lowest treatment‐related effects. The report for this study indicates behavioural changes in one out of three mice at 3 mg/kg bw (i.e. a slight depression in grooming and reactivity, a slight decrease in spontaneous motor activity and a slightly abnormal gait) while all animals were affected at 10 mg/kg bw and higher dose levels. On this basis, a NOAEL was established by the ECCO meeting experts and JMPR at 3 mg/kg bw in mice. Taking into account the limitations of the study, it was agreed that the setting of a NOAEL for the study is uncertain, however the study confirms that acute neurotoxicity is relevant to chlorfenapyr administration and the ARfD should be established at the same level as the ADI, i.e. based on the NOAEL of 2.8 mg/kg bw per day from the 18‐month study in mice, supported by the pharmacological study in mice and applying an UF of 100, resulting in an ARfD of 0.028 mg/kg bw. The JMPR established an ARfD at 0.03 mg/kg bw, based on the pharmacological study in mice and applying an UF of 100.

Detailed toxicological information on the metabolite tralopyril was retrieved from its evaluation by the CA (UK, 2014) and the Biocidal Products Committee (ECHA, 2014). The genotoxicity data package for tralopyril contains studies assessing the three endpoints, i.e. gene mutation in bacterial and mammalian cells (in vitro), clastogenicity and aneugenicity (in vivo). The in vitro studies on gene mutation showed negative and equivocal results. The in vivo study to address chromosome aberration and aneugenicity presents negative results. EFSA notes that additional information would be needed to confirm the conclusion reached by the JMPR and Biocidal Product Committee when applying current criteria to assess genotoxicity. Regarding gene mutation, additional details on the available studies would be needed to clarify the reliability of the negative and equivocal results obtained in bacterial and mammalian cells, and assess whether an in vivo study would be needed to clarify the mutagenic potential of tralopyril. With regards to clastogenicity and aneugenicity, the arguments given to assess bone marrow exposure in the in vivo mouse micronucleus study are not convincing against current criteria; an in vitro micronucleus assay would be the first choice to address these two endpoints. Accordingly, in EFSA's view, based on the available data package, the genotoxicity potential of tralopyril is inconclusive. With regards to the toxicological studies reported in the CA on tralopyril (UK, 2014), the critical effect is the presence of vacuolation of the white matter of the spinal cord and dorsal and lumbar root ganglia observed in a 90‐day dietary study in rats, for which no NOAEL, but a lowest observable adverse effect level (LOAEL) was identified at 5.2 mg/kg bw per day. This finding is the basis for the 10‐fold potency factor compared to chlorfenapyr, that presented a NOAEL for similar effects in a 1‐year neurotoxicity study in rats at 2.6 mg/kg bw per day and LOAEL at 13.6 mg/kg bw per day. However, while the genotoxicity potential of the metabolite is not clarified, EFSA cannot confirm this conclusion.

CONCLUSIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS

The metabolism of chlorfenapyr in plant and animal was previously investigated in the framework of the EU evaluation under Directive 91/414/EEC, as well as by JMPR. According to the results of the metabolism studies assessed, the residue for enforcement for plant and animal commodities was defined as chlorfenapyr in the EU. The same residue definition was derived by JMPR, which considered the residue as fat soluble.

Since no MRLs for livestock are currently set in the EU regulation, the residue was not labelled as fat soluble, but it should be in case MRLs for animal commodities will be set at the LOQ.

Regarding risk assessment, the EU residue definition was proposed as the sum of chlorfenapyr and 4‐bromo‐2‐(p‐chlorophenyl)‐5‐(trifluoromethyl)‐pyrrole‐3‐carbonitrile (tralopyril), expressed as tralopyril, for plants and animal commodities. However, this residue definition was not formally approved at EU level and there are no EU TRVs established for tralopyril.

JMPR defined the residue for risk assessment as sum of chlorfenapyr and 10‐fold 4‐bromo‐2‐(p‐chlorophenyl)‐5‐(trifluoromethyl)‐pyrrole‐3‐carbonitrile (tralopyril); this residue definition acknowledges that tralopyril has – according to JMPR assessment – a higher toxicity than parent chlorfenapyr.

Hence, the residue definitions for risk assessment cover the same components, but they are not fully comparable. Analytical methods are available for the enforcement of the currently applicable EU residue definition for enforcement in all four main plant matrices with an LOQ of 0.01 mg/kg and in tea with an LOQ of 0.8 mg/kg. Chlorfenapyr can be enforced in muscle, fat and milk with an LOQ of 0.01 mg/kg and in liver and kidney with LOQ of 0.05 mg/kg. According to the EURLs, multi‐residue analytical methods with an LOQ of 0.01 mg/kg are available for the routine analysis of chlorfenapyr in high water, high acid, high oil content and dry commodities, and a default LOQ of 0.01 mg/kg is also deemed achievable to monitor chlorfenapyr in all commodities of animal origin.

A screening of the quality of the TRVs derived at EU level and of those established by the JMPR was performed, and the set of toxicological studies used to derive these TRVs was assessed according to the current standards. As critical issues were identified, a Member States experts' consultation took place. The experts concluded that an ADI of 0.028 mg/kg bw per day and an ARfD of 0.028 mg/kg bw are appropriate for chlorfenapyr, noting a number of uncertainties as the data package did not fully comply with current scientific standards. In the meantime, acute consumption data for tea included in PRIMo 3.1. have been revised and the previously identified intake concern has been overcome. In the current assessment, EFSA agrees with the approach followed by JMPR for the correction factor, noting that this leads to additional non‐standard uncertainties. Further risk managers discussions are required for whether the existing MRL should be lowered to the LOQ or can be maintained. Rationale for alternative proposal: risk managers are invited to reconsider the previous EU reservation to the CXL proposal as the intake concern is no longer identified.
