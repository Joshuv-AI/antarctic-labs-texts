Natural compound triptolide induces caspase-3/GSDME-mediated pyroptosis by promoting ROS accumulation in small cell lung cancer
Authors: Yuheng Yan, Zhaolin Xu, Chengyan Wang, Ying Liu, Yaling Long, Xun Yuan, Qian Chu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSmall cell lung cancer (SCLC) is a highly aggressive malignancy with limited therapeutic options. Triptolide (TPL), a natural compound derived from the traditional Chinese herbal medicine Tripterygium wilfordii Hook F., exhibits broad antitumor effects. However, its role in SCLC remains unexplored.MethodsWe evaluated the effects of TPL on SCLC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion through in vitro assays, including Cell Counting Kit-8, clonogenic, 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine, cell cycle, and Transwell assays. To predict the potential antitumor targets of TPL against SCLC, we performed a network pharmacology analysis. Pyroptosis was examined by morphological observation, lactate dehydrogenase release assay, and Western blot analysis. The in vivo role of TPL was confirmed using a mouse xenograft model and immunohistochemical assays.ResultsTPL inhibited proliferation, triggered cell cycle arrest at the S-phase, and concomitantly produced a dose-dependent inhibition of SCLC cell invasion and migration. Network pharmacology analysis revealed that the antitumor effect of TPL in SCLC was associated with its potential to regulate inflammatory and apoptotic pathways, indicating the specific mode of cell death induced by TPL in SCLC cells. We confirmed that TPL induces caspase-3/GSDME-mediated pyroptosis in NCI-H1688 and NCI-H1339 cells. Furthermore, TPL induced pyroptosis by promoting reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, whereas ROS inhibition significantly abolished these pyroptotic effects. Additionally, TPL suppressed tumor growth in mice with SCLC xenografts and induced pyroptosis in vivo.ConclusionsThis study demonstrated the antitumor role of TPL in SCLC and its ability to trigger caspase-3/GSDME-mediated pyroptosis through ROS accumulation, providing new insights into SCLC treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-025-04071-8.

Background

Small cell lung cancer (SCLC), classified into limited stage (30%) and extensive stage (ES-SCLC; 70%), is a high-grade neuroendocrine carcinoma that accounts for 10%–15% of all diagnosed lung cancer instances [1]. It is characterized by early metastatic dissemination, leading to unfavorable clinical outcomes, as reflected by a 5-year survival rate of only 12% in ES-SCLC [1, 2]. Owing to advances in platinum-based chemotherapy and immunotherapy, the initial tumor shrinkage rate is 60%–70%. However, 60% of patients with SCLC experience rapid disease progression within 3 months, and the median overall survival for patients with ES-SCLC is approximately 12–13 months, highlighting the urgent need to develop novel therapeutic strategies [1, 3].

Traditional Chinese medicine, with its long history and extensive clinical experience, has gained increasing attention as an adjuvant therapy for lung cancer because of its potential to enhance therapeutic efficacy, reduce toxicity, and overcome drug resistance [4, 5]. Triptolide (TPL) is a diterpenoid tricyclic oxide derived from Tripterygium wilfordii Hook F. that exhibits broad therapeutic effects against autoimmune disorders, inflammatory diseases, and cancers, including pancreatic, breast, and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [6–10]. Minnelide, a water-soluble analog of TPL, has been clinically evaluated in solid tumors (NCT05166616 and NCT04896073) [11]. While the efficacy of TPL in NSCLC has been confirmed and evaluated in clinical trials (NCT05166616) [12], its effect on SCLC—a distinct type of lung cancer characterized by neuroendocrine features and limited therapeutic options—has not been elucidated.

The antitumor mechanisms of TPL are multifaceted and involve the induction of cell death (including apoptosis, autophagy, pyroptosis, ferroptosis, and cuproptosis) [13–15], inhibition of angiogenesis [16], and remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment [17]. Recent studies have revealed the role of TPL in inducing pyroptosis—an inflammatory form of programmed cell death mediated by gasdermin family proteins, including GSDMA, GSDMB, GSDMC, GSDMD, and GSDME [13, 18]. Intracellular pattern recognition receptors respond to external stimuli and initiate downstream signaling cascades that culminate in activation of caspase family proteins [19]. Chemotherapeutic drugs can trigger cancer cell pyroptosis through caspase-3 (CASP3)-mediated cleavage of GSDME, which represents another mechanism by which traditional Chinese medicine exerts tumor-suppressive effects [20]. However, whether TPL induces pyroptosis in SCLC remains unclear.

This study aimed to evaluate the antitumor role and underlying mechanism of action of TPL in SCLC. Through network pharmacology analysis and molecular and functional assays, this work sought to elucidate the therapeutic potential of TPL and provide mechanistic insights into its activity.

Methods

Cell culture

NCI-H1688, NCI-H82, and BEAS-2B cells were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (China), and NCI-H1339 cells were kindly provided by Dr. Hui Pan of the Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center. NCI-H1688, NCI-H82, and NCI-H1339 cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 culture medium (Procell, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; NEWZERUM, New Zealand), whereas BEAS-2B cells were cultured in DMEM medium (Procell, China). Cell cultures were kept in a 37 °C incubator providing a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO₂.

Reagents

TPL, Z-DEVD-FMK, and N-Acetyl-L-cysteine (NAC) were purchased from MedChemExpress (Shanghai, China). The Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8), Cell-Light 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) Apollo 567 In Vitro Imaging Kit, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) cytotoxicity assay kit, and Cell Cycle and Apoptosis Analysis Kit were purchased from Abbkine (China), RiboBio (China), DOJINDO (Japan), and Yeasen (China), respectively.

CCK-8 assay

Plating was carried out using 96-well plates with a seeding density of 5 × 10³ cells in each well. After cell adherence, the medium containing 0–200 nM TPL was added and incubated for 24, 48, or 72 h. Subsequently, the cells were treated with the CCK-8 reagent, and absorbance at 450 nm was measured.

Clonogenic assay

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10³ cells per well and treated with TPL for 48 h after adherence. The culture medium was then replaced with fresh medium, and the cells were incubated for another 14 d. After being fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, the cellular colonies were dyed with 0.1% crystal violet.

EDU assay

After seeding in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 10³ cells/well and following adhernce, the cells were treated with TPL for 24 h, followed by a 2 h incubation wiht 50 µM EdU. Next steps involved fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde, neutralization with 2 mg/mL glycine, and permeabilization with 0.5% Triton X-100. Subsequently, Apollo staining solution and Hoechst 33,342 were applied for staining. Cells were observed under a fluorescence microscope.

Cell migration and invasion assays

Cells were pretreated with varying concentrations of TPL, and then 5 × 10⁴ cells were seeded in the upper chamber of a Transwell insert (8 μm pore size, Corning Falcon) containing medium with 1% FBS. The lower chamber contained medium with 10% FBS. Following a 48 h incubation, non-migratory cells on the upper surface were scraped off. The migratory cells attached to the underside were then fixed and dyed with 0.5% crystal violet. Images were obtained using an inverted optical microscope. For the invasion assay, Transwell chambers were pre-coated with Matrigel (Corning Inc., USA) for 30 min before seeding.

Cell cycle and apoptosis analysis

Cells were treated with TPL for 48 h and harvested at a density of 1 × 10⁵–1 × 10⁶ cells per sample. After fixation with 70% ethanol at 4 °C overnight, the cells were incubated in staining solution containing propidium iodide (PI) and RNase for 30 min. For apoptosis analysis, the staining solution, constituted by 100 µL of binding buffer containing 2.5 µL Annexin V–FITC and 5 µL PI, was used to incubate the cells for 15 min in the dark. All samples were analyzed using a flow cytometer, and data were processed with FlowJo v10.8.1 software.

Network Pharmacology analysis

Potential therapeutic targets of TPL were identified using the Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology Database and Analysis Platform (TCMSP) (https://old.tcmsp-e.com/tcmsp.php) and GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/). SCLC-related targets were obtained from GeneCards and Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM) (https://omim.org/). The targets of TPL and SCLC were extracted and duplicate genes were removed. A Venn diagram (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny) was generated to identify overlapping genes representing potential therapeutic targets.

Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed using the DAVID platform (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/) to determine the biological processes and pathways involved in TPL’s activity against SCLC. The top 20 KEGG pathways and top 7 GO biological processes (BP, CC, and MF categories) with a false discovery rate < 0.05 were selected. Bubble plots and pathway–target networks were constructed to visualize the results.

The interactive genes were imported into the STRING v11.5 database (http://cn.string-db.org) to generate a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network and TSV dataset, which were analyzed and visualized using Cytoscape v3.9.0. Based on network topology analysis, genes with a degree ≥ 34 were identified as core targets of TPL.

Western blot

Cellular lysis was performed on ice for 30 min using RIPA buffer with PMSF and phosphatase inhibitors (Servicebio, China). The resulting lysates were subsequently sonicated, centrifuged, and combined with loading buffer. Protein quantification was carried out with a BCA assay kit (Beyotime, China). Equivalent protein quantities were resolved electrophoretically on 10% or 12% SDS-PAGE gels (Yeasen, China) and electrotransferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). Following a 1 h blockade with 5% BSA, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against GSDME (Abcam, ab215191), N-terminal fragment of GSDME (GSDME-N) (Abcam, ab222407), GSDMD (Abcam, ab219800), CASP3 (Santa Cruz, sc-56046), cleaved CASP3 (CST, 9661), CASP1 (Proteintech, 22915-1-AP), GAPDH (Proteintech, 60004-1-Ig), and α-tubulin (Proteintech, 1124-1-AP). After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies (HUABIO, HA1001 and HA1006) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using a Syngene G: BOX Chemi XT4 imaging system (UK).

LDH release assay

Following a 48-h TPL treatment of cells in 96-well plates, the supernatant was collected, mixed with 100 µL of working solution, and incubated for 30 min. The optical density value was then immediately measured at 490 nm.

Measurement of reactive oxygen species (ROS)

The quantification of intracellular ROS was performed using DCFH-DA (Beyotime, S0033S). Cells treated with TPL were harvested and stained with 10 µM DCFH-DA dissolved in serum-free medium for 30 min. The samples were then washed and immediately analyzed using a flow cytometer.

SiRNA transfection and quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

GSDME was transiently silenced in cells using siRNA (Sangon Biotech, China) transfection with jetPRIME reagent (Polyplus, France).

TRIzol reagent (TaKaRa, Shiga, Japan) was employed to isolate total RNA, which was purified with chloroform, isopropanol, and absolute ethyl alcohol. Subsequently, the RNA was reverse-transcribed by PrimeScript RT Master Mix (#RR036A, TaKaRa). TB Green Premix Ex Taq (#RR420A, TaKaRa) was added, and samples were analyzed on a Quantagene q225 real-time PCR platform (Kubo Technology, China). The 2⁻ΔΔCT method was used to compare relative mRNA expression levels. The primer sequences for GSDME were as follows: GSDME-F, CACACTGTGCCACTTGCTTC; and GSDME-R, GTCAGCTGAGGCAAACAAGC.

In vivo nude mouse xenograft study

A total of 1 × 10⁷ H1688 cells in 100 µL phosphate-buffered saline were injected into the right armpits of 4–6-week-old BALB/c male nude mice. Tumor length (L) and width (W) were measured regularly. When the average tumor volume exceeded 50 mm³ (V = L × W²/2), the mice were randomly divided into three groups: isotonic sodium chloride solution (control), 0.5 mg/kg TPL, and 1 mg/kg TPL. Drugs were administered intraperitoneally every other day. Body weight and tumor dimensions were evaluated every three days. Upon completion of the 12-day experimental period, the mice were sacrificed. Subsequently, tumors and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) were harvested and processed for embedding for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining, respectively.Primary antibodies used for IHC were Ki-67 (Abcam, ab16667), cleaved CASP3 (CST, 9661), and GSDME-N (Abcam, ab222407). All in vivo studies received approval from the Laboratory Animal Welfare & Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (TJH-202407045).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism 8, employing unpaired t-tests or one-way ANOVA for intergroup comparisons. All data were expressed as mean ± SD, with statistical significance thresholds defined as follows: NS, non-significant; *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001.

Results

TPL inhibits the malignant characteristics of SCLC cells

To explore the function of TPL (Fig. 1A) in SCLC cell lines, H1688, H1339, and H82 cells were treated with 0–200 nM TPL. The CCK-8 assay showed that TPL inhibited the viability of H1688, H1339, and H82 cells in a time- and dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1B). The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) values of TPL in H1688, H1339, and H82 cells at 48 h were 12.37 nM, 32.44 nM, and 12.54 nM, respectively. Notably, TPL also demonstrated cytotoxicity against the normal lung epithelial cell line BEAS-2B, with a 48 h IC₅₀ of 51.94 nM, which was higher than that in SCLC cell lines (Fig. S1A). To confirm these findings visually, clonogenic formation and EdU assays were performed to assess the effects of TPL on H1688 and H1339 cell survival and proliferation. Consistently, the number of colonies and EdU-positive cells significantly decreased following TPL treatment (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1C–F). Flow cytometry analysis showed that the proportion of H1688 and H1339 cells in the S phase markedly increased 48 h after TPL treatment (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1G and H), indicating that TPL induced cell cycle arrest in the S phase. Furthermore, Transwell assays were used to evaluate the effects of TPL on H1688 and H1339 cell migration and invasion. TPL significantly inhibited both migration and invasion in a dose-dependent manner (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1I and J). Collectively, these results indicate that TPL markedly suppresses the malignant characteristics of SCLC cells.

Potential targets of TPL in SCLC through network Pharmacology analysis

To investigate the potential antitumor mechanism of TPL in SCLC, we employed a network pharmacology approach to predict potential therapeutic targets of TPL. TCMSP and GeneCards databases were searched, yielding 287 targets related to TPL, and 3588 SCLC-associated targets were identified using the GeneCards and OMIM databases (Fig. 2A). A Venn diagram analysis revealed 166 overlapping targets between TPL and SCLC (Fig. 2A). KEGG and GO enrichment analyses were then performed to examine the functions and signaling pathways of these overlapping targets, which were notably enriched in inflammatory response and apoptotic processes (Fig. 2B and C). A PPI network comprising the 166 overlapping targets was constructed using the STRING 11.5 database and Cytoscape software (Fig. 2D). Nineteen targets with a degree ≥ 34 in the PPI network were identified as core targets, including apoptosis-related molecules, such as CASP3, CASP8, and BCL2, as well as inflammatory mediators IL-6 and TNF (Fig. 2E). The results suggested that TPL may act on both apoptotic processes and inflammatory response pathways.

TPL induces pyroptosis in SCLC cells

To explore the specific mode of cell death induced by TPL in SCLC cells, we conducted Annexin V/PI flow cytometric analysis. In addition to an observed increase in Annexin V⁺/PI⁻ cells (apoptotic cells) (Fig. 3A and B), a rise in Annexin V⁺/PI⁺ cells was also detected (Fig. 3A and C), representing cells undergoing pyroptosis, necroptosis, or necrosis [21]. The results of the network pharmacology analysis suggested that TPL may affect both apoptotic and inflammatory pathways (Fig. 2B and C). Because pyroptosis is a distinct form of programmed cell death associated with inflammatory activation, we hypothesized that TPL induces pyroptosis in SCLC cells. Morphological changes in TPL-treated cells were observed under a microscope. Exposure to TPL at its IC₅₀ for 48 h induced cellular swelling and the formation of bubble-like protrusions in both H1688 and H1339 cells (Fig. 3D). LDH, which is released following membrane disruption, serves as a characteristic marker of pyroptosis. Treatment with TPL at its IC₅₀ significantly increased LDH release into the supernatants of H1688 and H1339 cells at 24 and 48 h (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3E).

Pyroptosis is an inflammatory form of cell death mediated by members of the gasdermin protein family [22]. To elucidate the molecular mechanisms of TPL-induced pyroptosis, we examined the expression of two classical gasdermin molecules (GSDME and GSDMD) using Western blot analysis. The results showed that the release of the GSDME-N, but not of GSDMD, increased at 24 and 48 h following TPL treatment (Fig. 3F and G), indicating that TPL induced GSDME-dependent pyroptosis in SCLC cells. To further confirm the essential role of GSDME in TPL-induced pyroptosis, we silenced GSDME expression using siRNAs. qRT-PCR and Western blot analyses demonstrated that siRNA2 markedly reduced GSDME expression at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 3H and I). Inhibition of GSDME using siRNA2 suppressed TPL-induced pyroptosis in H1688 and H1339 cells, as evidenced by the decreased cellular swelling and bubble-like protrusions, as well as the reduced LDH levels (p < 0.05) observed in GSDME-silenced cells treated with TPL (Fig. 3J and K). These findings confirm that TPL induces GSDME-mediated pyroptosis in SCLC cells.

TPL-induced pyroptosis is triggered by CASP3-mediated cleavage of GSDME

GSDME can be cleaved by CASP3, one of the potential targets of TPL in SCLC (Fig. 2E), thereby switching apoptosis to pyroptosis [20]. Western blot was performed to investigate the mechanism by which TPL triggers GSDME-mediated pyroptosis. The results revealed that TPL treatment induced the cleavage of CASP3 but not CASP1 in H1688 and H1339 cells after 48 h of treatment (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4A and B). We confirmed that TPL increased LDH activity in BEAS-2B cells (Fig. S1B), as well as promoted the cleavage of both CASP3 and GSDME (Fig. S1C and D). Z-DEVD-FMK, a specific CASP3 inhibitor, significantly inhibited TPL-induced cellular swelling and bubble-like protrusions in H1688 and H1339 cells (Fig. 4C). It also reduced LDH activity (Fig. 4D) and the expression of cleaved CASP3 and GSDME-N (Fig. 4E–G). These results indicate that CASP3 activation is required for GSDME-mediated pyroptosis following TPL treatment.

TPL triggers CASP3/GSDME-induced pyroptosis by facilitating ROS accumulation

ROS generated from mitochondria during oxidative phosphorylation plays a crucial role in inducing pyroptosis [23]. To determine whether ROS are involved in TPL-induced pyroptosis, we conducted flow cytometric analysis of ROS levels. The results showed that TPL treatment caused a time-dependent increase in ROS levels in H1688 and H1339 cells (Fig. 5A and B), which was partially reversed by the ROS inhibitor NAC (Fig. 5C and D). In addition, NAC treatment prevented TPL-induced cellular swelling and bubble-like protrusions (Fig. 5E) and reduced LDH release (Fig. 5F). The expression levels of GSDME-N and cleaved CASP3 were also significantly decreased in NAC-treated cells following TPL administration (Fig. 5G–I), suggesting that TPL induces CASP3/GSDME-dependent pyroptosis by promoting ROS accumulation in SCLC cells.

Antitumor role of TPL in vivo

To further validate the antitumor effect of TPL in vivo, we established a xenograft mouse model by injecting H1688 cells into the subcutaneous tissue of nude mice. The method and timeline of TPL treatment are shown in Fig. 6A. Tumor imaging indicated that, compared with the isotonic sodium chloride solution group, intraperitoneal injection of TPL (both 0.5 and 1.0 mg/kg) significantly reduced the tumor burden in the SCLC mouse model, resulting in a significant reduction in tumor volume and weight (p < 0.05) (Fig. 6B–E). Administration of 1.0 mg/kg TPL every alternate day resulted in complete tumor regression in four of the five mice, demonstrating the favorable antitumor effect of TPL in SCLC. To assess the effects of TPL treatment on major organs, we conducted pathological examinations using H&E staining. The results showed no evidence of toxicity such as tissue damage or inflammation observed in mouse organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys, at administration concentrations of 0.5 and 1.0 mg/kg (Fig. S1E). In addition, the administered dosage levels were well tolerated, as supported by the absence of significant body weight variations across the experimental groups (Fig. 6F).

Further IHC analysis indicated that TPL treatment inhibited the expression of Ki-67 in tumor tissues, suggesting decreased tumor proliferative activity. To confirm the role of TPL in inducing pyroptosis in SCLC cells in vivo, we analyzed the expression of GSDME-N and cleaved CASP3 in the tumor tissues. Experimental data demonstrated that TPL administration produced a marked increase in the protein levels of both GSDME-N and cleaved CASP3, which are representative markers of pyroptosis in tumor tissues (Fig. 6G–H). Collectively, these results indicate that TPL can prevent the growth of SCLC tumors in vivo by inducing pyroptosis at concentrations of 0.5 and 1.0 mg/kg, which were biocompatible.

Discussion

Our study demonstrated that TPL exerts potent antitumor activity against SCLC by significantly inhibiting cell proliferation and inducing S-phase cell cycle arrest at low nanomolar concentrations. Malignant characteristics, including migration and invasion of SCLC cells, were also suppressed by TPL treatment. Through network pharmacology analysis and molecular and functional assays, our work demonstrated that TPL promoted SCLC cell pyroptosis via ROS/CASP3/GSDME signaling. TPL consistently suppressed tumor growth in SCLC-bearing mice and induced pyroptosis in SCLC cells in vivo.

We employed a systematic network pharmacology analysis to predict the potential targets and pathways of TPL in SCLC. This unbiased analysis specifically highlighted the critical role of TPL in regulating inflammatory response and apoptotic process, and CASP3/8, BCL2, as well as IL-6 and TNF were identified as core targets of TPL, highlighting the specific mode of cell death induced by TPL in SCLC cells. The use of network pharmacology provides a comprehensive theoretical foundation and mechanistic insight that was previously lacking, guiding our experimental design from a holistic perspective. As noted, Annexin V/PI flow cytometry revealed increases in both Annexin V⁺/PI⁻ (typically apoptotic) and Annexin V⁺/PI⁺ (which may include pyroptotic, necroptotic, or necrotic) populations. In addition, we have clearly demonstrated that TPL activates pyroptotic pathway in SCLC cells. There is an emerging concept of panoptosis—a coordinated form of cell death involving crosstalk among pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis [24]. In this framework, it is increasingly recognized that these pathways can be simultaneously engaged.

Pyroptosis is a proinflammatory form of immunogenic cell death triggered through caspase-1- and caspse-4/5/11-mediated cleavage of GSDMD, CASP3-mediated cleavage of GSDME, and other caspases-independent pathways, offering an opportunity to enhance systemic immunity and reduce immunosuppression during cancer treatment [25, 26]. Through systematic molecular and functional assays, we confirmed that TPL triggered pyroptosis in SCLC via the CASP3/GSDME pathway rather than the CASP1/GSDMD pathway. This finding aligns with a previous study, which demonstrated that TPL induced GSDME-mediated pyroptosis in head and neck cancer through mitochondrial hexokinase-II suppression and CASP3 cleavage [27]. Our work is the first to link TPL-induced pyroptosis to SCLC, a highly aggressive malignancy with limited therapeutic options. We provide comprehensive in vitro and in vivo evidence that TPL induces SCLC cell pyroptosis through CASP3/GSDME signaling. This focus addresses a specific and unmet clinical need.

ROS are critical inducers of pyroptosis [28, 29]; however, the mechanism remains unclear. Mitochondrial ROS generation leads to a decrease in mitochondrial membrane potential and triggers the opening of mitochondrial permeability transition pores. This results in the release of cytochrome c from mitochondria, which in turn activates CASP3/GSDME-induced pyroptosis [30]. A recent study revealed that ROS promote GSDMD palmitoylation, which is required for the formation of transmembrane pores and consequently causes cell pyroptosis [31]. Our data showed that TPL induced time-dependent ROS accumulation, which facilitated CASP3-mediated cleavage of GSDME. This cascade was abrogated by the ROS scavenger NAC, underscoring ROS as the upstream initiator of pyroptosis in SCLC. However, further research is required to elucidate the functional role of ROS accumulation in GSDME-mediated pyroptosis in SCLC.

A major barrier to the clinical application of TPL is its potential organ toxicity, particularly hepatotoxicity and nephrotoxicity [6]. However, no major organ toxicity related to TPL treatment was observed in our study based on H&E staining, indicating that administration doses of 0.5 to 1.0 mg/kg in mice were well tolerated. Nevertheless, we observed that TPL induced pyroptosis in the normal lung epithelial cell line BEAS-2B. Although the IC₅₀ of TPL at 48 h in BEAS-2B cells was 51.94 nM, which is higher than that in SCLC cell lines, these results indicate a nonspecific antitumor effect of TPL, which carries a risk of potential toxicity to normal cells. In recent years, various strategies have been investigated to mitigate the adverse effects of TPL, although most remain in preclinical stages. For example, an exosome-based TPL delivery system exhibited enhanced antitumor effects on ovarian cancer cells and reduced toxic effects on normal cells, with a high drug encapsulation rate [32]. Other strategies based on nanomaterials and novel drug delivery systems that aim to enhance efficacy and reduce toxicity have also been explored, providing new insights into TPL-based cancer therapies [18, 33–35].

Conclusions

Our study revealed a novel mechanism by which TPL eliminates SCLC cells through CASP3/GSDME-mediated pyroptosis by facilitating ROS accumulation. These findings suggest that TPL is a promising candidate for SCLC therapy and highlight pyroptosis induction as a viable strategy for combating this lethal disease. Future studies should focus on translating these insights into clinically applicable treatments while addressing pharmacological challenges.
