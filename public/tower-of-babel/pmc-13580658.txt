Readiness for Prenatal Genomic Medicine: A Cross-Sectional Survey of Obstetricians’ Competence and Attitudes Toward Whole-Exome Sequencing in Xinjiang, China
Authors: Yiqing Yu, Xiaoli Wang, Tingting Song, Na Hao, Yan Lü
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAs prenatal whole-exome sequencing (WES) becomes increasingly available, general obstetricians need to identify appropriate candidates for referral, while maternal–fetal medicine (MFM) specialists are involved in WES-related counseling, testing decisions, and pregnancy management. This study assessed obstetricians’ self-reported competence and attitudes toward prenatal WES and explored MFM specialists’ perspectives in Xinjiang, a geographically vast and resource-limited region in northwestern China.MethodsA two-stage cross-sectional survey was conducted between April 2025 and February 2026 using self-administered questionnaires. In the first-stage survey, 849 questionnaires were collected from obstetricians providing antenatal care across Xinjiang, of which 666 were valid and included. The second-stage survey was completed by 36 of 71 eligible MFM specialists. The surveys evaluated self-perceived competence, attitudes toward WES indications, and MFM specialists’ perspectives on testing strategies, timing, and result disclosure. Group comparisons used non-parametric tests, and multivariable binary logistic regression identified factors associated with self-reported competence.ResultsAmong 666 obstetricians, self-perceived competence was lowest for WES and single-gene disorders across different technologies and genetic disorders, respectively. MFM specialization was the factor most consistently associated with higher self-reported competence (OR range: 3.07–6.36; all P < 0.001). Most obstetricians supported WES for fetal structural anomalies (89.2%) and adverse reproductive or family history (89.3%). Among 36 MFM specialists, 44.4% had independently provided WES counseling, and 13.9% were very confident in interpreting WES results. Most favored individualized testing strategies (52.8%) and rejected strict gestational age limits (80.6%). Variants of uncertain significance and incidental findings were considered difficult to interpret, but most specialists supported disclosure in some form.ConclusionObstetricians in Xinjiang showed favorable attitudes toward prenatal WES but limited self-perceived competence in counseling and interpretation. These findings highlight a gap between the expanding use of genomic testing and clinicians’ perceived preparedness, emphasizing the need for targeted education and clearer clinical guidance.Keywords: prenatal whole-exome sequencing, obstetricians, genetic counseling, attitudes

Introduction

Whole-exome sequencing (WES) is a next-generation sequencing-based genomic diagnostic approach that primarily analyzes the exonic regions of the genome and has been increasingly used in prenatal diagnosis. In prenatal diagnosis, WES can improve the detection of monogenic disorders, particularly when fetal structural anomalies are present and conventional karyotyping or chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA) fails to provide a definitive diagnosis. Among fetuses with anomalies and normal cytogenetic results, WES increases the diagnostic yield by approximately 8.5–10%.1–5 Guidelines recommend prenatal WES in cases of fetal ultrasound anomalies when CMA and karyotyping are non-diagnostic.2,6,7 However, the clinical use of prenatal WES also introduces challenges related to variant interpretation, turnaround time, cost, and genetic counseling. Therefore, the implementation of prenatal WES depends not only on technological advances but also on clinicians’ preparedness toward genomic testing. Previous studies have shown that clinicians’ familiarity with genomic technologies and their confidence in variant interpretation significantly influence the acceptance and appropriate use of prenatal exome sequencing in clinical practice.1,3,8–11

Xinjiang is the largest province in China by geographical area, accounting for approximately one-sixth of the total land area in the country, and is characterized by multiethnic, multilingual, and multicultural communities, where healthcare resources are relatively limited. The region has reported approximately 236,000 annual deliveries,12 yet prenatal diagnostic resources remain relatively scarce, with only 10 accredited prenatal diagnosis centers13 and an estimated 115 certified maternal-fetal medicine (MFM) specialists serving the widely dispersed population. In the current healthcare system in China, general obstetricians are primarily responsible for routine antenatal care; when fetal abnormalities or other risk factors suggestive of genetic disorders arise, patients are referred to MFM specialists with certified prenatal diagnosis qualifications for further evaluation and management. Thus, general obstetricians’ preparedness toward prenatal WES may directly influence referral patterns and the appropriate use of this technology, while the perspectives of MFM specialists are more crucial, as they are directly involved in WES-related counseling and subsequent pregnancy management.

Although prenatal WES is increasingly being incorporated into clinical practice, limited data are available on obstetricians’ preparedness for its implementation in China, particularly in regions such as Xinjiang. In this context, self-reported competence may provide a practical measure of clinicians’ perceived readiness, confidence, and training needs. We hypothesized that obstetricians would generally support the clinical use of prenatal WES but might have limited competence in WES-related counseling, particularly for single-gene disorders and complex genomic results. To address this gap, we conducted a two-stage survey: first, to evaluate general obstetricians’ awareness and self-reported competence in prenatal WES and related counseling, and second, to assess clinical perspectives and practice-related preparedness among MFM specialists. Our findings aim to inform targeted training strategies and support the development of effective and consistent genetic counseling practices in the implementation of prenatal WES.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Participants

This two-stage, cross-sectional survey was conducted in Xinjiang from April 2025 to February 2026 using structured, self-administered questionnaires. The questionnaires were administered electronically and distributed uniformly to participating institutions through the Xinjiang Maternal and Child Health Administrative Authority to promote consistent dissemination across regions and healthcare settings. Participation was voluntary, and all responses were collected anonymously. Electronic informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to completion of the questionnaire survey. Questionnaires with incomplete demographic information or incomplete responses to key study items were excluded from the analysis. This study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Peking Union Medical College Hospital (Ethics Code: I-25PJ0321) and was reported in accordance with the STROBE Statement.

The first questionnaire was distributed to obstetricians providing antenatal care across the region. Because the survey link was disseminated through institutional coordinators rather than direct individual invitations, the exact number of obstetricians who received the questionnaire could not be determined; therefore, a conventional individual-level response rate could not be calculated. A total of 849 questionnaires were collected, and after data checking, 666 valid responses were included in the final analysis, yielding a valid questionnaire rate of 78.4%.

The second questionnaire was distributed to the 71 MFM specialists identified in the first-stage cohort, defined as obstetricians with advanced training and certified qualifications in prenatal diagnosis who also provide routine antenatal care. Of these, 36 completed the specialist questionnaire and were included in the final analysis, yielding a completion rate of 50.7%.

Questionnaires

Questionnaire items were developed by the study team based on a review of relevant guidelines, expert consensus, and key clinical issues in China. The preliminary versions were reviewed by a panel of senior specialists in obstetrics and prenatal diagnosis to ensure content validity, clarity, and clinical appropriateness. All experts involved in questionnaire review participated voluntarily and provided informed consent. Attitudinal items were assessed using five-point Likert-type scales, with response options tailored to the specific context of each question. The questionnaires were not formally validated in an independent population before use; therefore, the results should be interpreted as reflecting clinicians’ self-reported perceptions rather than objectively measured competence. The complete general questionnaire and MFM specialist questionnaire are provided as Questionnaire 1 and Questionnaire 2, respectively, in the Supplementary Material.

General Questionnaire

The first questionnaire, which targeted all obstetricians, focused on general perspectives toward prenatal screening and diagnosis and was structured into three sections. The first section collected demographic information, including age, sex, ethnicity, educational level, specialty (distinguishing between general obstetricians and MFM specialists), years of practice, professional rank, region of practice, and institutional characteristics–notably, whether the institution was a certified prenatal diagnosis center, as in China, only such centers are authorized to provide specialized prenatal genetic counseling, as well as to perform invasive prenatal diagnostic procedures and the corresponding laboratory tests. The second section assessed self-perceived counseling competence across a spectrum of prenatal screening and diagnostic technologies–ranging from maternal serum screening (MSS) to WES–and genetic disorders, from common trisomies to single-gene disorders. The third section explored attitudes toward indications for prenatal WES.

MFM Specialist Questionnaire

The second questionnaire, administered to MFM specialists, focused on clinical practice perspectives toward prenatal WES and was structured into three sections. The first section addressed self-reported experience, confidence, and application challenges. The second section explored attitudes toward testing sequence and timing, including preferences for parallel versus sequential testing and opinions on gestational age limits for WES. The third section examined approaches to managing challenging issues–specifically, variants of uncertain significance (VUSs) and incidental findings.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables are summarized as frequencies and percentages. Likert-scale items were treated as ordinal. For related ordinal variables, the Friedman test was used with Bonferroni‑corrected post hoc comparisons. For independent groups, the Mann–Whitney U-test (two groups) or Kruskal–Wallis test (≥3 groups) was applied, followed by Bonferroni adjustments.

To explore associated factors, ordinal logistic regression was initially considered for Likert outcomes, but the proportional odds assumption was violated (parallel lines test, P < 0.05). Therefore, Likert responses were dichotomized for binary logistic regression analyses. This dichotomization was used only for regression analyses, whereas descriptive analyses and group comparisons retained the ordinal nature of the Likert-scale responses. Variables with P < 0.05 in univariate analyses were entered into multivariable models.

All analyses were conducted using SPSS 29.0.1.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

In the first-stage survey, 849 questionnaires were collected from obstetricians providing antenatal care across Xinjiang, of which 666 were valid and included in the final analysis. In the second-stage survey, 36 of the 71 eligible MFM specialists completed the specialist questionnaire and were included in the analysis.

General Questionnaire

Demographics

As shown in Table S1 (Supplementary Material), most participants were female (98.2%) and aged 31–50 years (76.1%). Individuals of Han ethnicity accounted for 66.8% of the participants, followed by individuals of Uygur ethnicity (21.9%). The majority of participants held a bachelor’s degree (77.2%). Only 10.7% of the participants were MFM specialists, with the remainder being general obstetricians. Most participants (69.8%) had more than 10 years of clinical experience. The participants were relatively evenly distributed across professional ranks: 35.6% senior, 36.3% mid-level, and 28.1% junior.

Our survey covered the entire Xinjiang region (Table S2, Supplementary Material). Most participants were from secondary (49.4%) or tertiary hospitals (38.7%), while 11.9% were from primary hospital. With respect to institutional type, most participants worked in women and children’s hospitals (48.6%) or general hospitals (47.4%). Notably, only 26.3% of the participants were from centers with prenatal diagnosis qualifications.

Self-Reported Competence

Obstetricians reported varying levels of self-perceived counseling competence across prenatal screening and diagnostic technologies (Figure 1A). The greatest degree of competence was detected for maternal serum screening and non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT), followed by carrier screening and cytogenetic technologies, with the lowest level observed for WES. A Friedman test revealed significant overall differences among the six technologies (P < 0.001). Post hoc comparisons revealed that WES competence scores were lower than those for other technologies but did not differ significantly from those for CMA. A similar pattern was observed for genetic disorders (Figure 1B). Self-perceived competence was highest for common trisomies, followed by sex chromosome aneuploidies, and lowest for rare autosomal trisomies, microdeletion syndromes, and single-gene disorders. The Friedman test confirmed significant overall differences across disorder categories (P < 0.001). Detailed descriptive statistics are provided in Table S3 (Supplementary Material). Multivariable logistic regression analyses revealed that having certified qualifications in prenatal diagnosis (i.e., being an MFM specialist) was the most consistent factor associated with higher self-reported competence across different screening technologies, diagnostic technologies, and genetic disorders (all P < 0.001, with ORs ranging from 3.07–6.36 and reasonable 95% CIs). Obstetricians with prenatal diagnostic certification had significantly higher competence scores than general obstetricians did. Although several other demographic variables showed associations in some individual models, these effects were not consistent across technologies and conditions. The detailed regression results are presented in Tables S7 and S8 (Supplementary Material).

Obstetricians reported higher competence in prenatal screening methods, including maternal serum screening (MSS) and non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT), than in the use of genomic diagnostic technologies. Competence in whole-exome sequencing (WES) was the lowest among the evaluated methods. (B) Self-reported competence in counseling for different categories of fetal genetic abnormalities. Obstetricians reported higher competence in counseling for common trisomies (21, 18, and 13) and sex chromosome aneuploidies, whereas lower competence was observed for rare autosomal trisomies, microdeletion/microduplication syndromes, and monogenic diseases.

Attitudes Toward Indications for Prenatal WES

The majority of participants supported the use of prenatal WES for ultrasound structural anomalies (89.2%) and adverse reproductive or family history (89.3%), whereas support was significantly lower for patient requests with no other clinical indications (both adjusted P < 0.001; Figure 2 and Table S4, Supplementary Material). When MFM specialists were compared with other participants, stronger support was observed among MFM specialists for structural anomalies and adverse reproductive or family history (both P < 0.05), while no significant difference was found regarding patient requests (P = 0.623).

Attitudes toward indications for prenatal WES. Participants strongly supported the use of prenatal WES in cases in which ultrasonographic fetal structural anomalies were detected and unexplained stillbirth or fetal death had occurred. Support was significantly lower when WES was requested by patients without clear clinical indications.

Abbreviations: WES, whole-exome sequencing.

MFM Specialist Questionnaire

Among the 71 MFM specialists in the whole cohort, 36 completed the second questionnaire; the detailed responses are summarized in Table S5 (Supplementary Material).

Self-Reported Experience, Confidence, and Application Challenges

Fewer than half of the participants (44.4%, 16/36) reported having provided counseling independently, 12 (33.3%) had participated in relevant multidisciplinary team discussions, and the remaining eight (22.2%) had no prior experience. With respect to confidence, only five participants (13.9%) reported being very confident in interpreting WES results, 19 (52.8%) indicated that they required consultation with other genetic experts, and 12 (33.3%) reported being not confident.

Despite their limited experience and confidence, the participants identified several challenges in applying prenatal WES (Figure S1, Supplementary Material). The most frequently cited challenge was high cost (91.7%, 33/36), followed by insufficient counseling skills (80.6%, 29/36), difficulty in variant interpretation (77.8%, 28/36), and a lack of unified national guidelines or professional consensus (77.8%, 28/36). Additionally, 58.3% of the participants (21/36) reported that WES may increase the turnaround time and prolong the diagnostic process.

Attitudes Toward Testing Sequence and Timing

Testing sequence preferences varied among participants. Half of the participants (52.8%, 19/36) favored an individualized approach, with decisions made case by case on the basis of clinical indication, gestational age, or other factors; 11 (30.6%) preferred parallel testing (simultaneous CMA and WES), and 6 (16.7%) supported sequential testing (WES only after negative CMA results). With respect to the gestational age limits, most participants (80.6%, 29/36) believed that no upper limit should be set, whereas 7 (19.4%) recommended an upper limit.

Attitudes Toward Challenging WES Results

Among the different types of results that may be disclosed, participants perceived pathogenic variants and negative results as the easiest to interpret. In contrast, VUSs and incidental findings were rated as significantly more difficult (all adjusted P < 0.05). Detailed descriptive statistics are provided in Table S6 (Supplementary Material), and the distribution of difficulty scores is shown in Figure S2 (Supplementary Material).

With respect to VUSs reporting, only one participant preferred not to report; the remaining 35 favored reporting in some form–7 indicated that they would always report VUSs, 17 indicated that they would report VUSs only if multidisciplinary team (MDT) discussion suggested possible relevance, and 11 indicated that they preferred a patient-centered approach based on pre-test counseling.

For incidental findings, most participants (29/36, 80.6%) supported disclosure. Among the remaining seven, two considered that such findings should not be reported, and five preferred to report only those findings with early-onset implications. The distribution of responses is shown in Figure 3.

Discussion

Our survey revealed limited self-reported competence in WES and related counseling among obstetricians across Xinjiang. Attitudes toward indications were assessed among general obstetricians, whereas perspectives on testing sequence, timing, and challenging results were examined among MFM specialists. Overall, the findings suggest a gap between generally favorable attitudes toward prenatal WES and limited perceived preparedness for WES-related counseling and interpretation, while also indicating that MFM specialists tended to favor disclosure of complex genomic findings in some form.

Limited Self-Reported Preparedness for Prenatal Genomic Testing

Among all the participants, self-perceived competence in WES was among the lowest across prenatal screening and diagnostic technologies, while competence in single-gene disorders ranked the lowest among the genetic disorder categories. These findings may suggest a gap in clinicians’ preparedness for counseling related to WES and the genetic conditions it may uncover. As expected, prenatal diagnostic certification was one of the factors associated with higher self-reported competence scores across several technologies and genetic disorder categories, aligning with the tiered healthcare system in China and highlighting the importance of specialized training. Nevertheless, most MFM specialists lacked independent counseling experience and confidence in interpreting WES results, suggesting that perceived preparedness in prenatal WES remains limited even among specialists. Despite these limitations, the challenges identified–including difficulty in variant interpretation, high cost, and longer turnaround time–were largely consistent with those of previous reports, suggesting that such difficulties may be common across different clinical settings.8,9,14,15

Clinical Attitudes Toward WES Indications, Testing Sequence, and Timing

Beyond the well-recognized indication for prenatal WES–ultrasound anomalies–demand also exists for testing morphologically normal fetuses, with an additional diagnostic yield of approximately 2–3%.16,17 One study noted that a previous pregnancy affected by a monogenic disorder was the main indication for pursuing prenatal WES.16 In our study, the use of prenatal WES in the context of adverse reproductive or family history was supported by most participants. Prenatal diagnosis may be offered at parental request even without clear clinical indications;17 nearly half of the participants in our study supported such requests, although at a significantly lower rate than for parents with established indications. These findings may reflect a growing demand from families–particularly those with adverse reproductive or family histories–to seek more information to reduce the risk of having a child with a genetic disorder, a perspective that appeared to be shared by clinicians.

Attitudes toward testing sequence varied, with half of the practitioners favoring a flexible, individualized approach. This preference may reflect the fact that the diagnostic yield of WES differs across indications.18 Moreover, timely results in prenatal settings are important. Nevertheless, most participants opposed strict gestational age limits. This may be because additional genetic information may still be valuable for postnatal care or future reproductive planning–even if results are disclosed after delivery–and because there is no fixed gestational age cutoff for pregnancy management decisions in China.

Challenging Results: Between Information Sharing and Caution

Not surprisingly, VUSs were considered difficult to interpret. Professional bodies generally adopt a cautious stance, with some centers following a non-disclosure policy and others opting for selective reporting in specific clinical contexts.2,19 This caution reflects the dual nature of VUSs. On one hand, some VUSs may later be reclassified as pathogenic–particularly relevant in prenatal settings where fetal phenotypes are often limited and postnatal data may help clarify pathogenicity;20–24 on the other hand, uncertainty can cause parental anxiety and indecision.25–27 Despite these challenges, most participants in our study supported the reporting of VUSs in some form. Seven of 36 participants indicated that they would always report VUSs, while 11 favored a patient-centered approach on the basis of pre-test preference–which is consistent with the findings of previous studies.15 While respecting patient autonomy, clinicians should take responsibility for limiting unnecessary reporting through standardized pathways and MDT discussions.22 Nearly half of our participants (17/36) preferred reporting after MDT discussions and individualized case reviews.

Disclosing incidental findings requires balancing the benefits of more information sharing against the potential psychological burden of predicting diseases that remain unmanifested. Findings with early-onset implications should be prioritized for reporting; however, findings with late-onset implications that are actionable may also offer benefits for future healthcare management.28,29 In our cohort, most participants supported the disclosure of incidental findings. These findings reflect a generally proactive attitude toward information disclosure in prenatal genomic testing.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the use of convenience sampling in this cross-sectional survey may limit generalizability. Because the first questionnaire was distributed electronically through institutional coordinators rather than direct individual invitations, the exact number of obstetricians who received the questionnaire could not be determined, and a conventional individual-level response rate could not be calculated. However, the cohort covered all administrative regions of Xinjiang, and most participants were from tertiary or secondary hospitals, as well as from general or maternal and children’s hospitals, reflecting the local healthcare landscape. The proportion of MFM specialists (10.7%) aligns with the tiered healthcare system in China. According to publicly available data, there were 115 certified professionals for prenatal diagnosis in Xinjiang, although this number includes laboratory staff. Therefore, the 71 MFM specialists identified in our survey likely represent a substantial proportion of clinically active specialists in the region, supporting the representativeness of the cohort. Second, no formal sample-size or power calculation was performed because this was an exploratory regional survey aiming to include as many eligible obstetricians as possible during the study period. Therefore, analyses involving the MFM specialist subgroup should be interpreted cautiously. Although only 36 of the 71 MFM specialists completed the second questionnaire, no significant differences in demographic characteristics were observed between responders and non-responders, suggesting that the findings remain informative. Third, self-reported competence may not fully reflect actual clinical ability, potentially introducing response bias. In addition, although the questionnaire was developed based on relevant guidelines and reviewed by experts, it was not formally validated in an independent population, and some response options may have influenced participants’ answers or failed to capture all relevant perspectives. Fourth, dichotomization of Likert-scale responses for regression analyses may have reduced statistical efficiency and should be considered when interpreting the associated-factor analyses. Future studies using validated survey instruments, larger specialist cohorts, objective competence assessments, and qualitative interviews may help address these limitations. Nonetheless, as a preliminary study, these findings may provide useful preliminary evidence for understanding obstetricians’ perceived preparedness and training needs related to prenatal WES implementation.

Conclusions

This regional survey shows that Xinjiang obstetricians value prenatal WES for fetal anomalies and adverse histories, but feel unprepared in counseling and interpretation. MFM specialists differed on testing sequence and gestational limits, yet most supported disclosing VUSs and incidental findings despite interpretation challenges. These findings highlight a gap between favorable attitudes toward prenatal genomic testing and limited perceived preparedness to provide appropriate counseling. This gap underscores the need for targeted training, multidisciplinary support, and clearer practice guidance to promote consistent, effective, and ethically sound genetic counseling in the context of prenatal WES. Given the self-reported nature of the outcomes, the non-validated questionnaire, and the limited size of the MFM specialist subgroup, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary evidence of obstetricians’ perceived preparedness and training needs rather than objective clinical competence.
