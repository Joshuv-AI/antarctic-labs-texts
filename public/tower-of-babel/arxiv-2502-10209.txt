Abstract
This paper presents a comprehensive framework for holographic multiantenna communication, a paradigm that integrates both wide apertures and closely spaced antennas relative to the wavelength. The presented framework is physically grounded, enabling information-theoretic analyses that inherently incorporate correlation and mutual coupling among the antennas. This establishes the combined effects of correlation and coupling on the information-theoretic performance limits across SNR levels. Additionally, it reveals that, by suitably selecting the individual antenna patterns, mutual coupling can be harnessed to either reinforce or counter spatial correlations as appropriate for specific SNRs, thereby improving the performance.



I
Introduction

The progress of multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) communication entails expanding the array apertures relative to the wavelength, so as to augment the number of spatial dimensions
[
1
]
.
This is accomplished by physically enlarging the apertures and/or by increasing the carrier frequency, with antenna spacings at or above a half-wavelength as dictated by the channel’s scattering richness
[
2
]
and in line with the sampling theorem
[
3
]
.
However, both approaches face challenges and are ultimately curbed: exceedingly large apertures become difficult to implement and deploy, while escalating frequencies compromise the range.
Further progress may be possible by shrinking the antenna spacing below the Nyquist distance, an idea that defines the holographic MIMO
paradigm
[
4
]
.

Antennas are inherently exposed to their mutual radiations, with the intensity of the ensuing mutual coupling being determined by their radiation patterns and relative spacing
[
5
]
.
In particular, the coupling surges as the spacing falls below the Nyquist distance, which is precisely the defining attribute of holographic MIMO.
Therefore, while uncoupled formulations have been valuable in understanding fading correlation among the antennas
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
, ignoring coupling is ill-advised in holographic MIMO.
In recognition of this reality,
this paper introduces a unifying linear system-theoretic framework that intrinsically incorporates both correlation and coupling among the transmitting antennas.
Coupling among receiving antennas would enter the formulation similarly, as it stems from the same physical phenomenon.
A fundamental analogy is uncovered between fading correlation and coupling, both manifesting as linear filtering operations, but in stark contrast. To wit, i) coupling and fading behave oppositely, with fading mapping directly onto the channel response through a spatial convolution while coupling entails a deconvolution, and ii) the wavenumber response is dictated by scattering in the case of fading, while it is tied to the antenna patterns in the case of coupling.
The two phenomena, correlation and coupling, are subsumed in a cascaded filter response. The present study shows how coupling can be harnessed, by suitably altering the antenna pattern, to improve this cascaded filter response and thus the performance at every signal-to-noise-ratio (SNR).
Specifically matching/countering the antenna pattern to the fading spectrum whitens/colors the cascaded wavenumber response, leading to spatial decorrelation/correlation.
A reduced correlation is tantamount to a larger antenna spacing, effectively opening up additional spatial dimensions for multiplexing or diversity.
Antenna density is then dictated by the sampling theorem for the enlarged aperture, resulting in faster-than-Nyquist spatial sampling for the actual aperture.
The potential for such reconfigurability is backed by the latest advancements in antenna technology, including tightly coupled antennas
[
9
,
10
]
and metasurface antennas
[
11
]
.

II
Multiantenna Circuit Model

Fig. 1
:
Multiantenna circuit model. Each transmit antenna is driven by an ideal zero-impedance current source, which is the Norton equivalent of a source with an infinite impedance in parallel. In turn, each receive antenna is connected to an infinite-impedance voltmeter, such that no current is drawn from antennas.

II-A

Mutual Impedance

For narrowband communication at frequency
\omega
, each antenna is identified by a pair
of complex phasors
(j_{n},v_{n})
, i.e., a port, characterizing the instantaneous current and voltage via

\displaystyle j_{n}(t)=\Re\{j_{n}e^{-{\sf j}\omega t}\}\qquad v_{n}(t)=\Re\{v_{n}e^{-{\sf j}\omega t}\}.

(1)

Circuit theory inherently accounts for the interaction among antennas. Precisely, when a current is injected at one port, voltages appear across all ports.
This effect is measured by the mutual impedance
[
17
]

z_{nm}=\frac{v_{n}}{j_{m}}

(2)

defined as the ratio of the open-circuit voltage at a port
m
to the ideal current inserted at another port
n
, when all other ports are open-circuited (i.e., connected to an infinite load). The self-impedances correspond to
m=n
.

II-B

Discrete Multiport System Model

A system of

transmit and
N_{\text{r}}

(N_{\text{t}}+N_{\text{r}})

[
19
,
17
]

Transmit antenna ports are indexed by
1

{\bm{j}}_{\text{t}}=(j_{\text{t},1},\ldots,j_{\text{t},N_{\text{t}}})^{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}
as the transmit voltage and current vectors, and
{\bm{v}}_{\text{r}}=(v_{\text{r},1},\ldots,v_{\text{r},N_{\text{r}}})^{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}
and
{\bm{j}}_{\text{r}}=(j_{\text{r},1},\ldots,j_{\text{r},N_{\text{r}}})^{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}
as their receive counterparts,
the multiport model partitions as
[
19
,
17
]

\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{v}}_{\text{t}}\\
{\bm{v}}_{\text{r}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{Z}}_{\text{t,t}}&{\bm{Z}}_{\text{t,r}}\\
{\bm{H}}&{\bm{Z}}_{\text{r,r}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{j}}_{\text{t}}\\
{\bm{j}}_{\text{r}}\end{pmatrix}

(3)

where
{\bm{Z}}_{\text{t,t}}
and
{\bm{Z}}_{\text{r,r}}
are the transmit and receive impedance matrices, respectively, and
{\bm{Z}}_{\text{t,r}}
and
{\bm{H}}
the transimpedance matrices.
The latter depend on both the array geometries and the propagating medium. Due to reciprocity, a real symmetry occurs, namely
{\bm{Z}}_{\text{t,t}}={\bm{Z}}_{\text{t,t}}^{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}
,
{\bm{Z}}_{\text{r,r}}={\bm{Z}}_{\text{r,r}}^{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}
, and
{\bm{Z}}_{\text{t,r}}={\bm{H}}^{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}
.

As illustrated in Fig.
1
,
in its simplest form, (
3
) reduces to
[
29
]

\displaystyle{\bm{v}}_{\text{t}}

\displaystyle={\bm{Z}}_{\text{t,t}}{\bm{j}}_{\text{t}}

(4)

\displaystyle{\bm{v}}_{\text{r}}

\displaystyle={\bm{H}}{\bm{j}}_{\text{t}},

(5)

with
{\bm{H}}
the channel matrix.
The contribution to the transmit voltages of power reradiated back by the receive antennas is zero in (
4
); it
is negligible even with a finite-impedance receiver as per the
unilateral approximation

[
17
,
19
]
, which rests on the strong signal attenuation at radio frequencies.

Communication theorists tend to ignore the coupling among transmit antennas by setting
{\bm{Z}}_{\text{t,t}}={\bm{I}}_{N_{\text{t}}}

in (
4
), whereby
the transmit circuit power is
P_{\text{t}}=\|{\bm{j}}_{\text{t}}\|^{2}
.
Together with (
5
), this forms the (noiseless) uncoupled MIMO channel model. While exceedingly useful, this model is not physical.

II-C

Continuous Multiport System Model

Let
(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{\text{r}},\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{\text{r}},\hat{{\bm{z}}}_{\text{r}})
and
(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{\text{t}},\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{\text{t}},\hat{{\bm{z}}}_{\text{t}})
be orthonormal bases describing coordinate systems locally at receiver and transmitter, respectively. With respect to the former, a point in space is represented by the vector
r_{x}\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{\text{r}}+r_{y}\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{\text{r}}+r_{z}\hat{{\bm{z}}}_{\text{r}}
with coordinates
{\bm{{\sf r}}}=({\bm{r}},r_{z})
with
{\bm{r}}=(r_{x},r_{y})
; ditto with respect to the latter.

Letting the number of transmit antennas grow unboundedly within a compact space, what arises is a continuum of ports described by the current and voltage space-time fields specified by their complex phasors
j({\bm{{\sf r}}})
and
v({\bm{{\sf r}}})
.
The interactions between
{\bm{{\sf r}}}
and some other point
{\bm{{\sf s}}}
are described by a complex impedance kernel

z({\bm{{\sf r}}},{\bm{{\sf s}}})=\frac{v({\bm{{\sf r}}})}{j({\bm{{\sf s}}})},

(6)

which is the continuous counterpart to (
2
).
We differentiate between the transmit and receive impedance kernels,
z_{\text{t,t}}({\bm{{\sf r}}},{\bm{{\sf s}}})
and
z_{\text{r,r}}({\bm{{\sf r}}},{\bm{{\sf s}}})
, and the transimpedance kernel
h({\bm{{\sf r}}},{\bm{{\sf s}}})
.

For continuous apertures,
(
4
) morphs into the convolution

v_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf s}}})=(\mathcal{Z}_{\text{t,t}}j_{\text{t}})({\bm{{\sf s}}})=\iiint_{-\infty}^{\infty}z_{\text{t,t}}({\bm{{\sf s}}},{\bm{{\sf t}}})j_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf t}}})d{\bm{{\sf t}}}

(7)

with
\mathcal{Z}_{\text{t,t}}:L^{2}\to L^{2}
the operator analogue to
{\bm{Z}}_{\text{t,t}}:\mathbb{C}^{N_{\text{t}}}\to\mathbb{C}^{N_{\text{t}}}
. This operator is nonconjugate and self-adjoint, meaning that
z({\bm{{\sf r}}},{\bm{{\sf s}}})=z({\bm{{\sf s}}},{\bm{{\sf r}}})
, consistent with the physical reciprocity of the corresponding impedance matrices.

The transimpedance, in turn, depends on the propagation medium and is generally not self-adjoint.
From (
5
), it can be regarded as the impulse response of the uncoupled MIMO channel, being

v_{\text{r}}({\bm{{\sf r}}})=(\mathcal{H}j_{\text{t}})({\bm{{\sf r}}})=\iiint_{-\infty}^{\infty}h({\bm{{\sf r}}},{\bm{{\sf s}}})j_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf s}}})d{\bm{{\sf s}}}

(8)

with
\mathcal{H}:L^{2}\to L^{2}
the continuous analogue to
{\bm{H}}:\mathbb{C}^{N_{\text{t}}}\to\mathbb{C}^{N_{\text{r}}}
. Ignoring coupling at the transmitter entails
z_{\text{t,t}}({\bm{{\sf r}}})
being impulsive in (
7
), whereby the transmit circuit power is

P_{\text{t}}=\iiint_{-\infty}^{\infty}|j_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf s}}})|^{2}d{\bm{{\sf s}}}.

(9)

III
Transmit Coupling Kernel

for Punctiform Antennas

For a lossless system, conservation of energy requires the transmit circuit power to equal the power
of the produced
electromagnetic field. These powers, derived in Appendix A for punctiform antennas, are respectively given by

\displaystyle{\sf P}_{\text{c}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Re\left\{\iiint_{-\infty}^{\infty}d{\bm{{\sf r}}}\,\overline{j_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf r}}})}\iiint_{-\infty}^{\infty}d{\bm{{\sf s}}}\,z_{\text{t,t}}({\bm{{\sf r}}},{\bm{{\sf s}}})j_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf s}}})\right\}

(10)

and

\displaystyle{\sf P}_{\text{em}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Re\left\{-{\sf j}\kappa Z_{0}\iiint_{-\infty}^{\infty}d{\bm{{\sf r}}}\,\overline{j_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf r}}})}\iiint_{-\infty}^{\infty}d{\bm{{\sf s}}}\,g({\bm{{\sf r}}}-{\bm{{\sf s}}})j_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf s}}})\right\}

where
{Z_{0}\approx 120\pi}
is the wave impedance of free space while

(12)

III-A

Spectral Representation of the Transmit Impedance

Define
{\bm{{\sf v}}}=({\bm{v}},v_{z})
, with
{\bm{v}}={\bm{r}}-{\bm{s}}
and
v_{z}=r_{z}-s_{z}
, as the space-lag variable.
The equality between the circuit power in (
10
) and the electromagnetic power in (

\displaystyle z_{\text{t,t}}({\bm{{\sf v}}})

\displaystyle=-{\sf j}\kappa Z_{0}\,g({\bm{{\sf v}}})=-{\sf j}\kappa Z_{0}\frac{e^{{\sf j}\kappa\|{\bm{{\sf v}}}\|}}{4\pi\|{\bm{{\sf v}}}\|}

(13)

for
\|{\bm{{\sf v}}}\|>0

The transmit impedance kernel is space-invariant and takes the form of a spherical wave emanating from
{\bm{{\sf s}}}
to any other point
{\bm{{\sf r}}}
.

An exact Fourier representation of the above impedance kernel is derived next, using Weyl’s identity
[
30

\frac{e^{{\sf j}\kappa\|{\bm{{\sf v}}}\|}}{\|{\bm{{\sf v}}}\|}=\frac{{\sf j}}{2\pi}\iint_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{{\sf j}({\bm{\kappa}}^{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}{\bm{v}}+\gamma|v_{z}|)}}{\gamma({\bm{\kappa}})}d{\bm{\kappa}},

(14)

with

\gamma({\bm{\kappa}})=\begin{cases}\sqrt{\kappa^{2}-\|{\bm{\kappa}}\|^{2}}&\quad\|{\bm{\kappa}}\|\leq\kappa\\
{\sf j}\sqrt{\|{\bm{\kappa}}\|^{2}-\kappa^{2}}&\quad\|{\bm{\kappa}}\|>\kappa.\end{cases}

(15)

Plugging (
14
) into (
13
) yields

\displaystyle z_{\text{t,t}}({\bm{{\sf v}}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa Z_{0}}{2}\iint_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{{\sf j}\gamma|v_{z}|}}{\gamma({\bm{\kappa}})}e^{{\sf j}{\bm{\kappa}}^{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}{\bm{v}}}\frac{d{\bm{\kappa}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}.

(16)

The spectrum contributing to the impedance is the one lying on a wavenumber hemisphere of radius
\kappa=2\pi/\lambda
, either
\kappa_{z}=\gamma
or
\kappa_{z}=-\gamma
depending on the
location of
{\bm{{\sf r}}}
relative to
{\bm{{\sf s}}}
.

III-B

Transmit Coupling Kernel

Our attention now turns to the real part of the impedance, which is responsible for the transmit power. As shown in Appendix B, (

\displaystyle{\sf P}_{\text{t}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\iiint_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!d{\bm{{\sf r}}}\,\overline{j_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf r}}})}\iiint_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!d{\bm{{\sf s}}}\,\Re\{z_{\text{t,t}}({\bm{{\sf r}}}-{\bm{{\sf s}}})\}j_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf s}}}).

It is customary

\Re\{z_{\text{t,t}}({\bm{{\sf v}}})\}={\sf R}\,{\sf c}_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf v}}})

where
{\sf c}_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf v}}})
is the transmit coupling kernel and

With that, (

\displaystyle{\sf P}_{\text{t}}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\sf R}}{2}\iiint_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!d{\bm{{\sf r}}}\,\overline{j_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf r}}})}\iiint_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!d{\bm{{\sf s}}}\,{\sf c}_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf r}}}-{\bm{{\sf s}}})j_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf s}}}),

Precisely, applying Euler’s formula to (

\displaystyle{\sf c}_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf v}}})=\mathrm{sinc}\!\left(2\frac{\|{\bm{{\sf v}}}\|}{\lambda}\right).

The discretization with punctiform antennas amounts to an ideal spatial sampling of the continuous current (see Fig.

From (

An analogy can be established between (

the autocorrelation of an isotropic random channel

[
31
]
,
with uncoupled antennas being the analogue of antennas experiencing IID fading.
Indeed, as will be seen, coupling can be regarded as an additional spatial correlation—one that, unlike actual fading correlation, is not due to the angular spreading caused by scattering, but rather to the antenna structure.

Momentarily disregarding the

-component, which amounts to a translation,
for

But the Fourier transform of (

\displaystyle{\sf c}_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf v}}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\kappa}\iint_{\|{\bm{\kappa}}\|\leq\kappa}\frac{1}{\gamma({\bm{\kappa}})}\,e^{{\sf j}({\bm{\kappa}}^{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}{\bm{v}}+\gamma|v_{z}|)}d{\bm{\kappa}},

which implies the exclusion of evanescent waves; these do not contribute to the time-average transmit power

Consequently, the region of convergence of (

{\bm{{\sf v}}}
.
The above integration is intended on a

The upper and lower hemisphere supports in (

along the

The notion of causality applies here in the spatial domain and requires that

The coupling kernel

IV
Transmit Coupling Kernel

for Physical Antennas

Realizable antennas are non-infinitesimal, which corresponds to sampling with a non-impulsive response
[
33
]
.
This is a real function that physically describes the antenna skin whereon currents may exist. It specifies the directivity pattern, with narrower patterns requiring a larger antenna structure as per the uncertainty principle.
We henceforth assume all antennas are identical with space-invariant response
{\sf a}_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf v}}})\in L^{2}

and a corresponding 3D spectrum
{\sf A}_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf k}}})

Then, the complex current density with physical antennas is specified by the convolution

\displaystyle j^{\prime}_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf r}}})=\iiint_{-\infty}^{\infty}j_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf s}}})\,{\sf a}_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf r}}}-{\bm{{\sf s}}})\,d{\bm{{\sf s}}}

with

Replacing the current in (

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Re\Big\{-{\sf j}\kappa Z_{0}\iiint_{-\infty}^{\infty}d{\bm{{\sf r}}}\,\overline{j_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf r}}})}\iiint_{-\infty}^{\infty}d{\bm{{\sf s}}}\,j_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf s}}})

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt\cdot\iiint_{-\infty}^{\infty}d{\bm{{\sf p}}}\iiint_{-\infty}^{\infty}d{\bm{{\sf q}}}\,{\sf a}_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf p}}}-{\bm{{\sf r}}})g({\bm{{\sf p}}}-{\bm{{\sf q}}}){\sf a}_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf q}}}-{\bm{{\sf s}}})\Big\}.

Equated to the circuit power in (

takes the form of a superposition of spherical waves between any two points

Spectral Representation of the Transmit Impedance Kernel

Consider an antenna physically confined to a sphere of radius
r_{0}\geq 0
, as shown in Fig.
2
(bottom).
Then, centroids of adjacent antennas must be separated by at least
2r_{0}
as antennas cannot overlap spatially.
This inherent causality and the antenna directionality are captured by the Fourier representation of the transmit impedance kernel, derived in Appendix C as

\displaystyle z_{\text{t,t}}({\bm{{\sf v}}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa Z_{0}}{2}\iint_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d{\bm{\kappa}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\,\frac{e^{{\sf j}{\bm{\kappa}}^{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}{\bm{v}}}}{\gamma({\bm{\kappa}})}

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\cdot\begin{cases}\displaystyle|{\sf A}_{\text{t}}^{+}({\bm{\kappa}})|^{2}\,e^{{\sf j}\gamma v_{z}}&\quad v_{z}\geq 0\\
\displaystyle|{\sf A}_{\text{t}}^{-}({\bm{\kappa}})|^{2}\,e^{-{\sf j}\gamma v_{z}}&\quad v_{z}<0\end{cases}

(28)

for
\|{\bm{{\sf v}}}\|>2r_{0}
, where
{\sf A}_{\text{t}}^{\pm}({\bm{\kappa}})={\sf A}_{\text{t}}({\bm{\kappa}},\pm\gamma)
is the antenna pattern obtained evaluating the 3D spectrum at
\kappa_{z}=\pm\gamma
.
These patterns specify the non-isotropic coupling in the causal/anticausal direction.

For
r_{0}\to 0
, the antenna becomes infinitesimal and
{\sf a}_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf r}}})\to\delta({\bm{{\sf r}}})
whereby (
28
) correctly reduces to (
16
). As in (
16
), the symmetry of
z_{\text{t,t}}({\bm{{\sf v}}})
is established for any real-valued
{\sf a}_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf r}}})
, as
{\sf A}_{\text{t}}^{\pm}(-{\bm{\kappa}})=\overline{{\sf A}^{\pm}_{\text{t}}({\bm{\kappa}})}
due to Hermitian symmetry.

IV-B

Non-Isotropic Coupling

Excluding the evanescent portion of the spectrum from (
28

21

\displaystyle{\sf c}_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf v}}})

with

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi\kappa}\iint_{\|{\bm{\kappa}}\|\leq\kappa}\frac{|{\sf A}_{\text{t}}^{+}({\bm{\kappa}})|^{2}+|{\sf A}_{\text{t}}^{-}({\bm{\kappa}})|^{2}}{\gamma({\bm{\kappa}})}\,d{\bm{\kappa}},

(32)

which generalizes (

{\sf A}_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf k}}})=1

In the analogy between coupling and spatial correlation,
(

[

IV-C

Transmit Power Spectral Density

Plugging (
29
) into (

\displaystyle{\sf P}_{\text{t}}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\sf R}}{4\pi\kappa}\iint_{\|{\bm{\kappa}}\|\leq\kappa}\!\bigg(\frac{|J_{\text{t}}^{+}({\bm{\kappa}})|^{2}|{\sf A}_{\text{t}}^{+}({\bm{\kappa}})|^{2}}{\gamma({\bm{\kappa}})}

where
J_{\text{t}}^{\pm}({\bm{\kappa}})=J_{\text{t}}({\bm{\kappa}},\pm\gamma)

the 3D spectrum of the source.
Here,
|J_{\text{t}}^{+}({\bm{\kappa}})|^{2}
and
|J_{\text{t}}^{-}({\bm{\kappa}})|^{2}
are associated with the power flow along the
z
-axis, respectively causal and anticausal, measured on any
z
-plane.

By means of (

{\sf P}_{\text{t}}=\iiint_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\sf S}_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf k}}})\frac{d{\bm{{\sf k}}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}

given the power spectral density

\displaystyle{\sf S}_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf k}}})

\displaystyle=\frac{4\pi^{2}{\sf R}}{\kappa}\,|{\sf A}_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf k}}})|^{2}|J_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf k}}})|^{2}\delta(\|{\bm{{\sf k}}}\|^{2}-\kappa^{2})

\displaystyle={\sf R}\,|J_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf k}}})|^{2}{\sf C}_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf k}}}),

(37)

after substituting (
32
).
Rotationally symmetric sources, punctiform included, are indistinguishable in propagation as they generate the same power density.
Specifically, due to rotational symmetry,
{\sf a}_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf r}}})={\sf a}_{\text{t}}(\|{\bm{{\sf r}}}\|)

MIMO Model with Transmit Coupling

V-A

Composition of Channel and Coupling

Let us rewrite (
19
) as

\displaystyle{\sf P}_{\text{t}}=\langle\mathcal{C}j_{\text{t}},j_{\text{t}}\rangle

(38)

with
\mathcal{C}
the operator associated with the real kernel

\frac{{\sf R}}{2}{\sf c}_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf v}}})
given
{\sf c}_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf v}}})
in (
29
). By virtue of its positive-definiteness and symmetry,
(\mathcal{C}j_{\text{t}})({\bm{{\sf r}}})=(\mathcal{C}^{1/2}\mathcal{C}^{1/2}j_{\text{t}})({\bm{{\sf r}}})

for some other real symmetric and positive-definite operator
\mathcal{C}^{1/2}
with associated kernel
\sqrt{{\sf R}/2}\,{\sf c}^{1/2}_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf v}}})
, such that

\displaystyle{\sf c}_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf v}}})=\iiint_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!{\sf c}^{1/2}_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf t}}}){\sf c}^{1/2}_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf v}}}-{\bm{{\sf t}}})\,d{\bm{{\sf t}}}.

(39)

Then, (
38
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle{\sf P}_{\text{t}}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{C}^{1/2}\mathcal{C}^{1/2}j_{\text{t}},j_{\text{t}}\rangle

(40)

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{C}^{1/2}j_{\text{t}},\mathcal{C}^{1/2}j_{\text{t}}\rangle

(41)

\displaystyle=\|{\sf j}_{\text{t}}\|^{2},

(42)

where we exploited the symmetry of
\mathcal{C}^{1/2}
and defined as

{\sf j}_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf s}}})=(\mathcal{C}^{1/2}j_{\text{t}})({\bm{{\sf s}}})=\sqrt{\frac{{\sf R}}{2}}\iiint_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\sf c}^{1/2}_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf s}}}-{\bm{{\sf t}}})\,j_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf t}}})\,d{\bm{{\sf t}}}

(43)

the composition of current density and coupling.
Rewriting (
8
) in terms of (
43
) leads to

\displaystyle v_{\text{r}}({\bm{{\sf r}}})

\displaystyle=({\sf H}\,{\sf j}_{\text{t}})({\bm{{\sf r}}})=\iiint_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\sf h}({\bm{{\sf r}}},{\bm{{\sf s}}})\,{\sf j}_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf s}}})\,d{\bm{{\sf s}}},

(44)

where
{\sf H}=\mathcal{H}\mathcal{C}^{-1/2}
is the composition of the operator
modeling the uncoupled channel,

\mathcal{H}
, with
\mathcal{C}^{-1/2}
, positive-definite and associated with the kernel
\sqrt{2/{\sf R}}\,{\sf c}^{-1/2}_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf v}}})
.
The composition so defined associates with the space-variant kernel

\displaystyle{\sf h}({\bm{{\sf r}}},{\bm{{\sf s}}})

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{C}^{-1/2}h)({\bm{{\sf r}}},{\bm{{\sf s}}})

(45)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{{\sf R}}}\iiint_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\!h({\bm{{\sf r}}},{\bm{{\sf t}}})\,{\sf c}^{-1/2}_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf t}}}-{\bm{{\sf s}}})\,d{\bm{{\sf t}}}.

(46)

The invertibility of
\mathcal{C}^{1/2}
is proven in Appendix E;
it requires that
|{\sf A}_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf k}}})|
be strictly positive almost everywhere.

The model in (
44
) is equivalent to that of (
8
) in the sense that both provide the same output,
with
{\sf j}_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf r}}})
and
{\sf h}({\bm{{\sf r}}},{\bm{{\sf s}}})
providing an alternative embodiment of the MIMO formulation, in some ways advantageous relative to
j_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf s}}})
and
h({\bm{{\sf r}}},{\bm{{\sf s}}})
.

Composite MIMO Channel Matrix

Before proceeding, let us verify that sampling our continuous model at antenna locations yields established results for discrete arrays
[
19
]
.
Sampling the convolution in (

) yields the noiseless MIMO relationship inclusive of transmit coupling

and

These are obtainable from (

\displaystyle{\bm{{\sf H}}}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{{\sf R}}}\,{\bm{H}}{\bm{{\sf C}}}_{\text{t}}^{-1/2}

\displaystyle{\bm{{\sf j}}}_{\text{t}}=\sqrt{\frac{{\sf R}}{2}}\,{\bm{{\sf C}}}_{\text{t}}^{1/2}{\bm{j}}_{\text{t}}.

Expanding the space-lag variable in (

\displaystyle{\sf c}_{\text{t}}({\bm{{\sf r}}}-{\bm{{\sf s}}})

The composite current and composite channel coincide with the circuit-theoretic formulas
[

The lack of reciprocity in (

In turn, the factor
1/2

[
19
]
;
the baseband bandwidth is half the passband bandwidth.

V-C

Transmit Coupling Matrix

Changing the domain from wavenumber to spherical, it is found that

{\bm{\kappa}}(\theta,\phi)=\left(\kappa\sin\theta\cos\phi,\kappa\sin\theta\sin\phi\right),

(51)

and
\gamma(\theta)=\sqrt{\kappa^{2}-\|{\bm{\kappa}}\|^{2}}=\kappa\cos\theta

\frac{\partial{\bm{\kappa}}}{\partial(\theta,\phi)}=\kappa^{2}\cos\theta\sin\theta.

(52)

With these variable changes applied to (
29
),

\displaystyle[{\bm{{\sf C}}}_{\text{t}}]_{n,m}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|{\sf A}_{\text{t}}(\theta_{\text{t}},\phi_{\text{t}})|^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt\cdot a_{n}(\theta_{\text{t}},\phi_{\text{t}})\overline{a_{m}(\theta_{\text{t}},\phi_{\text{t}})}\sin\theta_{\text{t}}\,d\theta_{\text{t}}\,d\phi_{\text{t}},

(53)

where the array response vector is

\displaystyle a_{n}(\theta_{\text{t}},\phi_{\text{t}})

\displaystyle=e^{{\sf j}({\bm{\kappa}}^{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}(\theta_{\text{t}},\phi_{\text{t}}){\bm{r}}_{n}+\gamma(\theta_{\text{t}})r_{z,n})}

(54)

with
{\bm{\kappa}}(\cdot,\cdot)
as per (
51
) and with

{\sf A}_{\text{t}}(\cdot,\cdot)
being either
{\sf A}_{\text{t}}^{+}(\cdot,\cdot)
over the upper hemisphere,
\{\theta_{\text{t}}\in[0,\frac{\pi}{2}]\}
,
or
{\sf A}_{\text{t}}^{-}(\cdot,\cdot)
over the lower hemisphere,
\{\theta_{\text{t}}\in(\frac{\pi}{2},\pi]\}
.
From (
30
),

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|{\sf A}_{\text{t}}(\theta_{\text{t}},\phi_{\text{t}})|^{2}\,\sin\theta_{\text{t}}\,d\theta_{\text{t}}\,d\phi_{\text{t}}

(55)

with omnidirectional coupling, including punctiform antennas, arising for
{\sf A}_{\text{t}}(\theta_{\text{t}},\phi_{\text{t}})=1
. The function
|{\sf A}_{\text{t}}(\theta_{\text{t}},\phi_{\text{t}})|^{2}
is the power pattern of each antenna.

For a
z
-aligned array of punctiform antennas, (
53
) reduces to the impedance formula in
[
19
, Eq. 44]
.
And, for a uniform linear array (ULA) in particular,

\displaystyle[{\bm{{\sf C}}}_{\text{t}}]_{n,m}=\mathrm{sinc}\!\left(2\frac{\|{\bm{{\sf r}}}_{n}-{\bm{{\sf s}}}_{m}\|}{\lambda}\right),

(56)

consistent with (
20

, meaning transmit antennas that are infinitely apart from each other.
This is incongruent with the transmitter fitting in a certain form factor.

Fourier Model for MIMO Channels

Without Coupling

VI-A

Fourier Representation of Stationary Channels

The channel kernel in (
8
) comprises a causal
and an anticausal
component at each end of the link, resulting in four possible combinations.
The causal-causal component of a stationary Gaussian channel can be represented in Fourier form as
[
6
, Thm. 2]

\displaystyle h({\bm{{\sf r}}},{\bm{{\sf s}}})

\displaystyle=\iint_{\|{\bm{k}}\|\leq\kappa}\frac{d{\bm{k}}}{\sqrt{2\pi\kappa}}\iint_{\|{\bm{\kappa}}\|\leq\kappa}\frac{d{\bm{\kappa}}}{\sqrt{2\pi\kappa}}\,\frac{\tilde{H}^{++}({\bm{k}},{\bm{\kappa}})}{\sqrt{\gamma({\bm{k}})\gamma({\bm{\kappa}})}}

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\cdot e^{{\sf j}({\bm{k}}^{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}{\bm{r}}+\gamma({\bm{k}})r_{z})}e^{-{\sf j}({\bm{\kappa}}^{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}{\bm{s}}+\gamma({\bm{\kappa}})s_{z})}

(57)

with
\gamma({\bm{\cdot}})
in (
15
) and with
\tilde{H}^{++}({\bm{k}},{\bm{\kappa}})
the spectrum,
an independent complex Gaussian process.
The dependences on
r_{z}
and
s_{z}
are henceforth omitted as they can be absorbed into
\tilde{H}^{++}({\bm{k}},{\bm{\kappa}})
and are immaterial due to the statistical equivalence at different
z
-planes, namely

\tilde{H}^{++}({\bm{k}},{\bm{\kappa}})\sim e^{{\sf j}\gamma({\bm{k}})r_{z}}\tilde{H}^{++}({\bm{k}},{\bm{\kappa}})\,e^{-{\sf j}\gamma({\bm{\kappa}})s_{z}}.

(58)

We adhere to a separable model satisfying

\mathbb{E}\{|\tilde{H}^{++}({\bm{k}},{\bm{\kappa}})|^{2}\}=\mathbb{E}\{|\tilde{H}^{+}_{\text{r}}({\bm{k}})|^{2}\}\,\mathbb{E}\{|\tilde{H}^{+}_{\text{t}}({\bm{\kappa}})|^{2}\},

(59)

with
\mathbb{E}\{|\tilde{H}^{+}_{\text{r}}({\bm{k}})|^{2}\}
and
\mathbb{E}\{|\tilde{H}^{+}_{\text{t}}({\bm{\kappa}})|^{2}\}
the power angle spectra due to the separate scattering at receiver and transmitter, respectively.
These spectra are normalized so each can be interpreted as an angular distribution. For example,

\displaystyle 1=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathbb{E}\{|\tilde{H}^{+}_{\text{t}}(\theta_{\text{t}},\phi_{\text{t}})|^{2}\}\sin\theta_{\text{t}}\,d\theta_{\text{t}}d\phi_{\text{t}},

(60)

at the transmitter, with the variables change according to (
51
).
This normalization bounds the channel power, scaling with the product of the transmit and receive apertures rather than the antenna count, in the limit of antenna densification
[
34
]
.

VI-B

Karhunen–Loève Decomposition

The spatial limitation imposed by the array apertures implies that
h({\bm{{r}}},{\bm{{s}}})
can be approximated by a finite number of coefficients representing the channel’s spatial dimensionality at either end of the link.
For the sake of specificity, rectangular apertures of unnormalized dimensions
(L_{{\text{t}},x},L_{{\text{t}},y})
at the transmitter and
(L_{{\text{r}},x},L_{{\text{r}},y})
at the receiver are considered.
For later convenience, the shortest dimension aligns with the
x
-axis, whereby
L_{{\text{r}},x}\leq L_{{\text{r}},y}
and
L_{{\text{t}},x}\leq L_{{\text{t}},y}
.
Define

{\sf n}_{\text{r}}=\lceil\pi\det({\bm{D}}_{\text{r}})\rceil\quad\qquad{\sf n}_{\text{t}}=\lceil\pi\det({\bm{D}}_{\text{t}})\rceil

(61)

with
{\bm{D}}_{\text{r}}=\mathrm{diag}(L_{{\text{r}},x},L_{{\text{r}},y})/\lambda
and
{\bm{D}}_{\text{t}}=\mathrm{diag}(L_{{\text{t}},x},L_{{\text{t}},y})/\lambda
.
The number of spatial dimensions or degrees of freedom (DOF) is upper bounded by its value under isotropic scattering, given by
{\sf DOF}=\min({\sf DOF}_{\text{r}},{\sf DOF}_{\text{t}})
, with
[
34
]

{\sf DOF}_{\text{r}}={\sf n}_{\text{r}}+o\!\left({\sf n}_{\text{r}}\right)\qquad{\sf DOF}_{\text{t}}={\sf n}_{\text{t}}+o\!\left({\sf n}_{\text{t}}\right)

(62)

the cardinalities of the 2D lattices

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\text{r}}

\displaystyle=\left\{{\bm{i}}=(i_{x},i_{y})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:\|{\bm{D}}_{\text{r}}^{-1}{\bm{i}}\|\leq 1\right\}

(63)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\text{t}}

\displaystyle=\left\{{\bm{j}}=(j_{x},j_{y})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:\|{\bm{D}}_{\text{t}}^{-1}{\bm{j}}\|\leq 1\right\}.

(64)

The MIMO channel
[{\bm{H}}]_{n,m}=h({\bm{{r}}}_{n},{\bm{{s}}}_{m})
can then be approximated by the Karhunen–Loève expansion
[
34
,
35
]

\displaystyle{\bm{H}}

\displaystyle\approx{\bm{U}}\tilde{{\bm{H}}}{\bm{V}}^{\rm\scriptscriptstyle H}

(65)

where
{\bm{U}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{\text{r}}\times{\sf n}_{\text{r}}}
and
{\bm{V}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N_{\text{t}}\times{\sf n}_{\text{t}}}
are the isometry Fourier eigenvector matrices with column entries

\displaystyle[{\bm{u}}_{\bm{i}}]_{n}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{L_{{\text{r}},x}L_{{\text{r}},y}}{N_{\text{r}}}}u_{\bm{i}}({\bm{r}}_{n})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\text{r}}}}e^{{\sf j}2\pi({\bm{D}}_{\text{r}}^{-1}\bm{i})^{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}{\bm{r}}_{n}/\lambda}

(66)

\displaystyle[{\bm{v}}_{\bm{j}}]_{m}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{L_{{\text{t}},x}L_{{\text{t}},y}}{N_{\text{t}}}}v_{\bm{j}}({\bm{s}}_{m})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\text{t}}}}e^{{\sf j}2\pi({\bm{D}}_{\text{t}}^{-1}{\bm{j}})^{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}{\bm{s}}_{m}/\lambda}.

(67)

In turn,

{\bm{\tilde{H}}}={\bm{\Lambda}}^{1/2}_{\text{r}}{\bm{W}}{\bm{\Lambda}}_{\text{t}}^{1/2}

(68)

where
{\bm{W}}\in\mathbb{C}^{{\sf n}_{\text{r}}\times{\sf n}_{\text{t}}}
has IID standard complex Gaussian entries while
{\bm{\Lambda}}_{\text{r}}
and
{\bm{\Lambda}}_{\text{t}}
are diagonal with entries that depend on the power spectra at receiver and transmitter, respectively.
For example, at the transmitter,
[{\bm{\Lambda}}_{\text{t}}]_{j,j}=N_{\text{t}}\sigma^{2}_{{\bm{j}}}(\tilde{H}^{+}_{\text{t}})
for
j=1,\ldots,{\sf n}_{\text{t}}
an arbitrary ordering of the lattice points within
\Lambda_{\text{t}}
in (
64
)
[
34
]

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{{\bm{j}}}(\tilde{H}^{+}_{\text{t}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\iint_{\Omega^{+}_{{\bm{j}}}}\mathbb{E}\{|\tilde{H}^{+}_{\text{t}}(\theta_{\text{t}},\phi_{\text{t}})|^{2}\}\sin\theta_{\text{t}}\,d\theta_{\text{t}}d\phi_{\text{t}}

(69)

given
\Omega^{+}_{{\bm{j}}}
the spherical surface elements covering the entire upper hemisphere, each one centered at

\displaystyle\theta_{{\text{t}},{\bm{j}}}

\displaystyle=\sin^{-1}\!\left(\|{\bm{D}}_{\text{t}}^{-1}{\bm{j}}\|\right)\qquad\phi_{{\text{t}},{\bm{j}}}=\tan^{-1}\!\left({j_{y}}/{j_{x}}\right).

(70)

Under isotropic scattering,
\mathbb{E}\{|\tilde{H}^{+}_{\text{t}}(\theta_{\text{t}},\phi_{\text{t}})|^{2}\}=1
, and (
69
) reduces to the solid angle subtended by
\Omega^{+}_{{\bm{j}}}
,

\displaystyle|\Omega^{+}_{{\bm{j}}}|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\iint_{\Omega^{+}_{{\bm{j}}}}\sin\theta_{\text{t}}\,d\theta_{\text{t}}\,d\phi_{\text{t}}.

(71)

With that,
{\bm{\tilde{H}}}
specifies the angular coupling between resolvable transmit and receive directions, while the left and right multiplications by
{\bm{U}}
and
{\bm{V}}
change the domain from angular to spatial
[
34
,
36
]
.

As the apertures grow electrically large, the approximation in (
65
) sharpens
and, by virtue of Mercer’s theorem, converges in the mean-square sense to (
57
). Concurrently, the lower-order terms in (
62
) vanish as the number of DOF firms up.

VII-B

Coupling versus Correlation

Despite fading correlation and coupling having an isomorphic linear filtering nature, their effects appear respectively in the numerator and denominator of (

Correlation is associated with scattering and maps directly onto the channel, while coupling arises from the interactions among currents flowing into different antennas.
Putting them on an equal footing requires translating coupling to the channel through the reciprocal in (

This entails a deconvolution, as opposed to the convolution applied by scattering; while their functional dependence is isomorphic, therefore,
coupling effects run counter to those of scattering.

IX
Information-Theoretic Analysis

Let us now turn to the information-theoretic implications of mutual coupling at different SNR levels. To isolate its effect on the eigenvalue polarization from that of having extra DOF, the latter aspect is excluded in this section and the Fourier model is applied unless otherwise stated. The impact of the additional DOF could be separately studied by replacing the actual aperture by its coupling-expanded counterpart.

IX-A

Coupling as Physical Precoder

Communicating entails encoding independent unit-variance symbols

In turn,

This is in agreement with previous works on mutual coupling
[

The remaining terms in (

Expectedly, stronger correlation is beneficial at low SNR for a given number of uncoupled antennas, as preferred directions arise that beamforming can exploit

For large electrical apertures and a smooth fading spectrum,

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{{\bm{j}}}(\tilde{{H}}_{\text{t}}^{+})\approx|{\Omega}^{+}_{{\bm{j}}}|\,\mathbb{E}\{|H^{+}_{\text{t}}(\theta_{{\text{t}},{\bm{j}}},\phi_{{\text{t}},{\bm{j}}})|^{2}\}

•

Coupling can alternatively decorrelate antennas, diminishing the selectivity (see Figs.

The minimum of (

The highest value in (

For a fixed aperture, a trade-off arises between array gain, increasing with antenna densification, and antenna selectivity, requiring larger structures as dictated by the uncertainty principle.

•

As the number of antennas grows with the electrical aperture,

X
Conclusion

An informed holographic MIMO transmitter can optimize its channel capacity across varying SNR levels by accounting for correlation and mutual coupling in the precoder design. The configurability of the antenna patterns would add flexibility, allowing the transmitter to reinforce antenna correlations at low SNR and
tone them down at high SNR
so as to optimize the usability of the spatial dimensions provided by the scattering environment and array apertures. In essence, the adjustment of the antenna patterns mimics a reshaping of the environmental scattering.

Potential directions for future work include the following.

•

Accounting for mutual coupling at the receiver, due to finite load impedances at those antenna ports.

•

Incorporating matching networks into the circuit model.

•

Evaluating the impact of coupling on the power offset, the zero-order term of the capacity expansion at high SNR.

•

Identifying antenna patterns that maximize channel capacity across all SNR levels, generalizing the guidelines obtained for low and high SNR.

•

Determining the optimal antenna spacing for given losses, patterns, and scattering conditions.

•

Generalizing the framework to wideband channels
[
21
]
, addressing the superposition of time-harmonic currents and voltages across the communication bandwidth.

Also, an inherent assumption throughout the paper has been that all of the antennas within the transmit array
are identical.
By relaxing this condition, the impact of coupling could be extended to the realm of pattern and/or polarization diversity, in which antennas are purposely exposed to distinct portions of the channel spectrum and/or polarization as a mechanism to diminish their correlation
[
43
]
.
