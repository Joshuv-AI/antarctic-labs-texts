Alternative splicing and polyadenylation facilitate transcriptome diversity underlying high-altitude adaptation in pigs
Authors: Changyao Li, Pei Wang, Hailong Huo, Xia Zhang, Yiming Guo, Feidi Wen, Junlan Zhou, Yifan Niu, Guiying Zhao, Jinlong Huo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlternative splicing (AS) and alternative polyadenylation (APA) are critical post-transcriptional mechanisms that regulate gene expression and increase transcriptome and proteome diversity. The landscape and functional impact of AS and APA in pigs under high-altitude conditions remains poorly explored. Employing polyA-selected Iso-Seq, we obtained a high-quality transcriptome from the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney of low-altitude Diannan small-ear pigs (DSE, 500 m) and high-altitude Diqing Tibetan pigs (DT, 3200 m). Numerous unannotated transcripts were identified, including novel isoforms of both known and novel genes. AS- and APA-driven transcriptome diversity revealed distinct splicing and polyadenylation patterns, with marked differences in alternative major transcript (AMT) and alternative major polyadenylation (AMP) usage between low- and high-altitude pigs. Differentially regulated genes across five tissues were clearly enriched in high-altitude adaptation-related pathways, including heme binding, HIF-1, iron ion binding, oxidoreductase activity, and mitochondrial membrane components. Hub genes potentially associated with high-altitude adaptation were identified. RT-PCR and qPCR validation confirmed AS-driven transcriptomic diversity, highlighted the utility of Iso-Seq for AS profiling, and revealed transcripts and key genes expression differences underlying high-altitude adaptation in pigs. Collectively, our findings demonstrate that AS and APA play vital roles in shaping transcriptome diversity and may regulate genes involved in high-altitude adaptation in pigs.Subject terms: Animal breeding

Introduction

The Tibetan pig is a unique livestock breed native to high-altitude regions, serving as an indispensable source of high-quality protein for local residents. Having undergone long-term exposure to cold and hypoxic environments, Tibetan pigs have evolved remarkable physiological adaptations to harsh high-altitude conditions, including larger lungs (weight ratio of lung to body) and heart (weight ratio of heart to body), more denser arterioles, more developed capillaries, higher red blood cell (RBC) number and hemoglobin (HGB) concentration compared to lowland pigs1–4. Accumulating evidence indicates that genetic factors also play a crucial role in this acclimatization; genomic studies have identified several candidate genes and selection signatures, including HIF pathway genes such as EPAS15,6, HIF1A7, as well as metabolic regulators like PCK28 and CAPG9. Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying high-altitude adaptation in Tibetan pigs is essential for breeding strategies that integrate altitude resilience with desirable production traits.

Alternative splicing (AS) and alternative polyadenylation (APA) are widespread in eukaryotes and represent essential post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms10–12. Through the generation of distinct transcript isoforms via alternative splicing patterns and polyadenylation site selection, AS and APA substantially contribute to transcriptome diversity13,14. Numerous studies have shown that AS plays critical roles in the environmental adaptability of both plants and animals, such as in response to hypoxia15–17 and high salinity18–20. Although the role of APA in eukaryotic gene regulation has gained increasing attention, its contribution to hypoxia adaptation remains largely unexplored. To the best of our knowledge, this study presents the first systematic investigation of the potentially adaptive regulatory functions of APA in Tibetan pigs under high-altitude conditions.

Over the past decades, RNA-seq has been extensively applied to investigate gene expression differences of organisms under various environmental challenges; however, its limited read length hampers precise genome annotation21,22. Polyadenylation-selected long-read Iso-seq enables accurate capture of full-length transcripts23. This approach provides unique advantages for detecting AS events and APA sites, offering a powerful tool to profile their tissue-specific usage and to uncover their roles in transcriptome diversity and environmental adaptation14,24. Previous studies integrating Iso-seq with RNA-seq have successfully identified novel isoforms and described transcriptome landscapes across multiple species and tissues, including pigs25,26. Nevertheless, the landscapes of AS and APA in five major visceral tissues of low- and high-altitude pigs, as well as their potential regulatory roles in high-altitude adaptation, have yet to be systematically explored.

In this study, we employed polyA-selected Iso-Seq and RNA-seq to profile transcriptome diversity and investigate AS and APA differences between two pig breeds from distinct environments: the lowland heat-tolerant Diannan small-ear pig (DSE, 500 m) and the high-altitude Diqing Tibetan pig (DT, 3200 m) adapted to harsh plateau conditions. Iso-seq exhibited high sensitivity in detecting low-abundance and complex transcripts, and PCR validation further confirmed its advantage in identifying AS events and profiling transcriptome diversity. A substantial number of novel transcripts we detected refined the pig genome annotation. Beyond merely cataloging transcriptomic changes, our research characterizes the distinct AS and APA profiles associated with high-altitude adaptation. By providing a comprehensive resource of these post-transcriptional signatures in a physiologically relevant large animal model, this study offers a transcriptomic perspective on Tibetan pig hypoxia tolerance, providing a useful framework for future investigations into hypoxia-related pathologies.

Results

High-quality Iso-seq full-length transcriptome data

A total of 510.81 Gb subread data was obtained, including 4,394,647 circular consensus sequences (CCS) with an average of 51.10 Gb subread data and 1.37 Gb CCS data per sample from five visceral tissues in DSE and DT. On average, 431,522 full-length non-chimeric (FLNC) reads were obtained per sample, yielding 32,573.2 consensus isoforms and 3,241.8 isoforms that were successfully mapped to the reference genome, with an average mapping rate of 99.65%. After eliminating redundant isoforms and 5′ degraded sequences by Iso-Seq collapse and quality control by SQANTI3, a total of 131,539 unique isoforms were obtained and used for further analysis (Supplementary Table 1). The genome annotation files across five tissues in DSE and DT generated via the Iso-Seq workflow were given in Supplementary Data 1–10.

By integrating all FLNC sequences of five tissues in DSE and DT, we established a comprehensive transcriptome with the Iso-seq pipeline. The integrated genome annotation file was given in Supplementary Data 11. The comprehensive transcriptome comprised 63,181 transcripts corresponding to 14,930 gene loci, including 12,918 known loci (86.52%) and 2012 novel loci (13.48%) (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table 2). The majority of unique isoforms ranged from 2 to 3 kb in length, closely matching the distribution in the reference genome (Fig. 1b).

Genome-wide transcriptome profiling with Iso-seq facilitating the annotation of the pig reference genome

Remarkably, merely 15,522 transcripts (24.57%) were currently annotated in pig reference genome, while a remarkably large proportion of novel transcripts derived from annotated genes (45,282, 71.67%), along with 2377 novel transcripts from unannotated genes (3.76%), were detected across five visceral tissues of the two pig breeds, underscoring the incompleteness of the exiting pig genome annotation (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table 2). The Heat Shock Protein Family A (Hsp70) Member 8 (HSPA8) functions importantly under high-altitude hypoxic conditions27, producing a total of 41 transcripts, comprising 6 annotated and 35 novel isoforms. The muscleblind-like protein 1 (MBNL1) gene was linked to hypoxia and energy metabolism adaptations28, yielding 83 transcripts, with the vast majority being novel transcripts (74) and only a small fraction annotated transcripts (9). Myocyte enhancer factor 2C (MEF2C) plays an important role in the cardiovascular system29, generating 45 transcripts, including four known and 41 novel.

A total of 93.82% of known transcripts were predicted to be protein-coding, markedly higher than the 89.53% observed for novel transcripts, whereas a substantial difference was evident between novel and known genes, with 95.17% of annotated genes exhibiting coding potential compared to only 24.80% of novel genes (Supplementary Table 2). Of the 2012 novel genes identified, 499 were protein-coding genes (NCG) that produced 556 novel coding transcripts (NCG-cRNA) and 159 novel long noncoding RNAs (NCG-lncRNAs), whereas 1513 noncoding genes (NNG) generated 1662 novel lncRNA transcripts (NNG-lncRNA) (Fig. 1c). Compared with the annotated transcripts, the novel transcripts exhibit a more complex structure characterized by a longer transcript length (Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney, Z = −28.958, p < 2.2e-16, Fig. 1d) and an increased number of exons (Z = −31.167, p < 2.2e-16, Fig. 1e). Additionally, the novel transcripts display a lower expression level (Z = −61.362, p < 2.2e-16, Fig. 1f), the features that highlight the advantage of Iso-Seq in detecting longer and structurally diverse transcripts, while also sensitively detecting low-abundance transcripts. After processing with SQANTI3, a total of 63,181 transcripts were obtained, of which the most prevalent category was novel not in catalog (NNC, 46.60%), followed by novel in catalog (NIC, 21.90%). A total of 24.60% of transcripts were classified as annotated, comprising 11.20% complete full-splice matches (FSM) and 13.40% incomplete splice matches (ISM). A very small proportion of transcripts (3.10%) from known genes were identified as novel, including genic (1.00%) and fusion transcripts (2.10%). Other transcripts, including antisense, intronic, and intergenic transcripts, accounted for 3.80% in total (Fig. 1g, h and Supplementary Table 3). FSM, ISM, NNC, and NIC transcripts exhibited comparable ORF length distributions, whereas novel transcripts of novel genes displayed distinct length distribution patterns (Supplementary Fig. 1). Overall, our Iso-Seq data uncovered extensive transcript novelty, thereby refining and extending pig genome annotation.

Alternative splicing complexity and transcriptome diversity across distinct altitudes

Our investigation yielded an extensive catalog of 14,930 multi-exon genes in the pig visceral tissue transcriptome and 24,683 in the reference genome, with 67.07 and 43.01% undergoing AS, respectively. The average transcript number per gene was higher in our transcriptome (6.08) than in the reference (3.33), with a greater proportion of genes having multiple isoforms, particularly those with 2–10 and >10 transcripts (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Table 4). This highlights the superior sensitivity of Iso-seq in capturing transcriptomic diversity. Notably, the immunoglobulin heavy constant mu (IGHM) gene, associated with immune defense30, exhibited the highest transcript diversity, generating a total of 150 transcripts. To assess isoform divergence between DSE and DT pigs, we focused on genes with expressed transcripts (TPM >1) that exhibited inter-breed differences greater than three in transcript number across the five visceral tissues. Of particular significance, genes exhibiting significant isoform divergence in the heart were preferentially enriched in cellular energy metabolism, specifically those underpinning glycolysis and ATP/NADH turnover. Key enriched terms included pyruvate metabolic process, nucleoside phosphate metabolic process, and pyridine nucleotide metabolic process (p.adj <0.01) (Fig. 2b). This specific enrichment profile implied that adaptive metabolic reprogramming may occur in the heart of high-altitude DT pigs compared with low-altitude DSE pigs. Furthermore, in the kidney, isoform divergence genes were preferentially enriched in oxidative metabolism, specifically organic and carboxylic acid metabolic processes and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle. Notably, autophagy-related processes, such as autophagy and processes utilizing autophagic mechanisms, were also significantly enriched. (Supplementary Fig. 2).

The Spearman correlation analysis indicated a strong correlation (r = 0.657) between the number of exons and the number of transcripts (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Table 5), while the correlation (r = 0.367) between gene length and transcript number is relatively weak (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Table 5). A moderate correlation (r = 0.577) is observed between transcript expression level and transcript number (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Table 5). Alternative splicing analysis of five visceral tissues identified 36,776 unique alternative splicing (AS) events, including skipped exon (SE), retained intron (RI), alternative 3′ splice site (A3), alternative first exon (AF), alternative 5′ splice site (A5), alternative last exon (AL), and mutually exclusive exon (MX), which corresponded to 9170 AS-genes (Supplementary Table 6). Skipped exon (SE) was the most predominant alternative splicing event, accounting for 28.0% (n = 10,288; corresponding to 5469 AS-genes) among the five visceral tissues (Fig. 2f). Moreover, although the overall rates and patterns of individual AS event types were largely similar across tissues, we observed modest changes in specific AS proportions, notably an increased prevalence of retained intron (RI) events in the DT heart and a decreasing tendency in alternative first exon (AF) usage across most tissues except the lung (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Table 7). Among all AS genes, 40.73% (n = 3735) contained only one type of AS event, whereas 59.27% (n = 5435) harbored multiple AS event types (Fig. 2h).

A total of 35,610 transcripts were identified from AS genes. Among them, 16,940 (47.57%; 5962 AS genes) were breed- or tissue-specific transcripts. Notably, only 2232 transcripts (6.27%; 1,809 AS genes) were shared across all breeds and tissues (Supplementary Fig. 3). DT pigs exhibited the largest numbers of specific transcripts in the heart (3288, 9.23%) and lung (2232, 6.27%). Furthermore, Heart- and lung-specific AS-transcript genes in DT were significantly enriched in KEGG pathways such as adrenergic signaling in cardiomyocytes, cytoskeleton in muscle cells, citrate cycle (TCA cycle), MAPK signaling pathway, and spliceosome (Supplementary Fig. 4). These findings suggested that a large number of heart and lung-specific AS transcripts in DT pig cardiopulmonary tissues may contribute to the regulation of cardiopulmonary function, energy metabolism, and splicing processes, thereby potentially facilitating adaptation to high-altitude environments. KEGG enrichment analysis showed that the top 1000 isoformic genes were mainly enriched in pathways related to ferroptosis, fatty acid degradation, and fatty acid metabolism. Notably, several isoformic genes were also annotated to the HIF-1 signaling pathway, a vital regulator of cellular responses to hypoxia (Supplementary Fig. 5). To further explore its post-transcriptional regulation, we validated six core HIF-1 pathway genes (CAMK2G, VEGFA, HRAS, PGK1, MKNK1, and SCCPDH) involved in angiogenesis, metabolism, signaling, and translation by RT-PCR. Validation results confirmed consistent and significant alternative splicing patterns for these genes, confirming the reliability and accuracy of Iso-Seq in detecting AS events and profiling transcriptomic diversity. (Fig. 3a–f, Supplementary Fig. 6, and Supplementary Table 23).

RNA-seq revealed extensively differentially expressed genes between DSE and DT

We generated 392.45 Gb of RNA-Seq raw data, yielding gene expression profiles across five major tissues from two pig breeds (Supplementary Table 8), with a data summary presented in the Supplementary Table 9. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that PC1 and PC2 explained 30.5% and 22.1% of the total variance, respectively, with samples from the same tissue clustering tightly in the two-dimensional space (Fig. 4a). Across the five tissues, a total of 4669 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified, with the liver (n = 1561) showing the largest number (Fig. 4b, c and Supplementary Table 10). Most DEGs were tissue-specific (n = 3433; 73.53%), whereas only 40 DEGs (0.86%) were consistently differentially expressed across all five tissues (Supplementary Fig. 7). Integrated functional enrichment analysis revealed that DEGs across the five tissues were significantly enriched in GO terms such as heme binding, tetrapyrrole binding, and oxidoreductase activity (p.adj <0.01) (Supplementary Fig. 8), underscoring their roles in oxygen transport and redox regulation. Consistently, KEGG analysis highlighted pathways closely associated with high-altitude hypoxia adaptation, including the HIF-1, PI3K-Akt, AMPK, and calcium signaling pathways, as well as cytokine–cytokine receptor interactions (p.adj <0.01) (Fig. 4d), which collectively contribute to metabolic reprogramming, cardiopulmonary adaptation, and immune regulation under hypoxic conditions. Additionally, to identify key regulators of oxygen transport under high-altitude conditions, 28 heme-binding pathway genes enriched among RNA-seq DEGs were selected for qPCR validation. Notably, CYP7A1, CYP24A1, FMO1, and FMO3 were significantly upregulated, and four hemoglobin (HB) family genes (HBB, HBE1, HBM, and HBZ) exhibited coordinated upregulation, indicating their potential role as core regulators of oxygen transport and metabolism (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Table 23).

Extensive differentially alternative splicing events between DSE and DT

A total of 6275 differential alternative splicing (DAS) events were identified, corresponding to 2495 DAS genes. Among the five tissues, the heart exhibited the largest number of DAS events (n = 1409), with skipped exon (SE) and alternative first exon (AF) being the predominant AS events (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Table 11). Interestingly, the majority of DAS-genes carried only a single unique AS type (n = 1698, 68.33%), whereas a smaller fraction harbored two or more AS types (n = 787, 31.67%), with SE being the most frequently used AS type. (Fig. 5b). Most DAS genes displayed tissue specificity (n = 1540, 61.97%) (Supplementary Fig. 9). Functional annotation revealed that DAS genes were significantly enriched in RNA binding, oxidoreductase activity, and ATP hydrolysis activity, and mapped to key KEGG pathways, including Adrenergic signaling in cardiomyocytes, citrate cycle (TCA cycle), glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, fatty acid metabolism, and ferroptosis (Supplementary Figs. 10 and 11).

To ensure the robustness of the DAS genes identified by SUPPA2, we performed a cross-validation using rMATS, a count-based algorithm for DAS detection. rMATS identified 5910 DAS genes, covering 68.8% (1709/2,485) of the DAS genes detected by SUPPA2 (Fig. 5c). This high concordance confirms the reliability of our DAS detection. Among the 1,709 DAS genes, 226 were identified as differentially expressed (DE-DAS) genes across the five tissues (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Table 12). Functional annotation revealed that these genes were significantly enriched in key GO terms, including oxidoreductase activity, transition metal ion binding, iron ion binding, heme binding, and tetrapyrrole binding (Fig. 5e). These functional categories were closely associated with oxygen transport and redox homeostasis, suggesting a potential involvement of DE-DAS genes in high-altitude adaptation. Notably, many overlapping pathways were observed between DEGs and DE-DAS genes, particularly those associated with oxygen transport, energy metabolism, and redox homeostasis. Differential alternative splicing (DAS) analysis identified significant splicing events (p value <0.05, |ΔPSI| >0.1) in CTSV (ΔPSI = 0.638), OXNAD1 (ΔPSI = −0.295), and RAB2A (ΔPSI = −0.173), which exhibited distinct magnitudes of splicing change, with IGV sashimi plots showing highly consistent splicing pattern within individual from the same altitude group and clear structural differences between high- and low-altitude pigs. (Supplementary Fig. 12). To validate the reliability of the DAS results, we further performed experimental verification, and RT-PCR successfully confirmed clear exon usage differences in these genes between Tibetan pigs and low-altitude pigs, supporting the robustness and accuracy of our DAS analysis and highlighting their potential roles in metabolic regulation and cellular adaptation to hypoxia (Fig. 5f, Supplementary Fig. 13, and Supplementary Table 23). These findings suggest that alternative splicing, together with differential gene expression, may coordinately contribute to the transcriptomic regulatory landscape associated with high-altitude adaptation.

Abundant transcription factor motifs near differential alternative splicing sites

With skipped exon (SE) and alternative first exon (AF) being the predominant DAS types, we further investigated the upstream regulatory influences of the observed differentially alternative splicing (DAS) events. We performed motif enrichment analysis on the flanking sequences of SE and AF events, differential alternative splicing sites, identifying a large number of both known and novel motifs across the five tissues (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Table 13). KEGG enrichment analysis showed that many transcription factors (TFs) corresponding to the identified motifs near differential splice sites were significantly enriched in hypoxia adaptation-related pathways such as Th17 cell differentiation, the Hippo signaling pathway, the cGMP-PKG signaling pathway, and the TGF-β signaling pathway (Fig. 6b). These results suggest that the TFs may be involved in regulatory processes associated with high-altitude environments and may potentially reflect coordination between transcriptional programs and alternative splicing regulation. A total of 31 differentially expressed transcription factors (DE-TFs) were identified from the DEG set (Fig. 6c). All of these DE-TFs exhibited differential expression in at least one tissue (Fig. 6d). Notably, although these DE-TFs have not been recognized as canonical splicing factors, prior studies have indicated that they can link transcriptional programs with splicing regulation. For instance, E2F131,32, E2F333, SOX234, SOX935, MYC36, and FOXM137, have been reported to influence alternative splicing by regulating spliceosome components, including SRSF2, hnRNPK, SNRPN, and SF3A3, or by directly modulating gene splicing. Furthermore, GO enrichment analysis showed that these DE-TFs were significantly enriched in terms related to transcriptional regulation, including DNA-binding transcription factor activity, sequence-specific DNA binding, transcription regulatory region nucleic acid binding, and transcription cis-regulatory region binding (Fig. 6e). These findings suggest that these DE-TFs may play a potential regulatory role in the generation of SE and AF DAS events between high- and low-altitude pigs through a coordinated transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulatory network. qPCR validation of eight key DE-TFs yielded results in agreement with the RNA-Seq data (Fig. 6f and Supplementary Table 23), thereby corroborating their differential expression between low- and high-altitude pigs.

Widespread alternative major transcript (AMT) usage uncovered by combined Iso-seq and RNA-seq analysis

To further assess the functional consequences of AS at the gene level, we examined the most abundantly expressed transcript of each gene that exceeded the expression level of the secondary transcript by at least 10%, which was defined as the robust major transcript (MT). It was found that MT of the same gene exhibited transcript usage difference (|log2FC|>1) between low- and high-altitude pig breeds, and the phenomenon was named alternative major transcript (AMT) (Fig. 7a and Supplementary Table 14). AMT events were widespread across the five tissues in DSE and DT. Among 9170 AS genes, 1791 (19.53%) exhibited AMT, with the spleen showing the highest number of AMT genes (Supplementary Fig. 14 and Supplementary Table 14). Among the 1791 AMT genes, 788 were differentially expressed in at least one tissue, referred to as DE-AMT genes (Fig. 7b, c and Supplementary Table 14). These identified DE-AMT genes were significantly enriched in several high-altitude adaptation-related pathways, such as the PPAR signaling pathway, the AMPK signaling pathway and the Peroxisome pathway (Fig. 7d). These results suggest that DE-AMT genes may play a potential role in integrating systemic metabolic reprogramming and cellular structural remodeling in response to high-altitude stress.

PPI network analysis (score > 0.7) identified ALB as a central hub closely linked with PLG, F2, AHSG, and AMBP (Fig. 7e). qPCR validation of these hub genes showed expression changes between high- and low-altitude pig breeds that were highly concordant with the RNA-seq results, supporting the robustness of the data (Fig. 7f and Supplementary Table 23). Notably, these hub genes predominantly encode plasma proteins involved in the regulation of coagulation balance, molecular transport, antioxidant defense, and metabolic regulation. The functional interplay among these genes suggested a potential regulatory network that integrates hemostasis, metabolic transport, and oxidative stress defense, which may contribute to high-altitude adaptation.

PolyA-selected Iso-seq revealed the widespread utilization of alternative polyadenylation (APA) between DSE and DT

Alternative polyadenylation (APA) represents a pervasive layer of post-transcriptional regulation in eukaryotes, whereby selective usage of distinct polyadenylation sites (PAS) gives rise to isoforms with different 3′ ends. PolyA-selected Iso-seq coupled with PAS identification revealed extensive alternative polyadenylation (APA) events across five visceral tissues between low- and high-altitude pig breeds. In DSE, a total of 12,284 PAS were identified across 5662 genes, of which 2856 (50.44%) were classified as APA genes (≥2 PAS), supported with an average of 11.66 reads per PAS, while in DT pigs, 13,609 PAS were detected across 6109 genes, including 3054 (49.99%) APA genes, supported with an average of 9.05 reads per PAS. Overall, the number of APA genes was higher in DT than in DSE, with 2031 (52.40%) APA genes shared between the two breeds (Fig. 8a, b and Supplementary Table 15). The higher number of APA genes in DT compared with DSE may suggest enhanced post-transcriptional regulatory flexibility. NEBL, also known as LASP2, encodes a cytoskeletal structural protein38 and exhibits the highest PAS usage, with 33 sites detected. The length distribution of 3′UTRs in APA genes exhibited a similar pattern in both DSE and DT pigs, with the majority of APA transcripts clustering around 1000 bp (Fig. 8c, d and Supplementary Table 16).

To investigate PAS usage in the five visceral tissues of low- and high-altitude pigs, we calculated the expression ratio of distal PAS isoforms (D) to proximal PAS isoforms (P) for each gene. Across five tissues, the majority of genes (>75%) displayed a D/P ratio <1 (Pgenes), whereas only a smaller fraction exhibited D/P > 1 (Dgenes) (Fig. 8e and Supplementary Table 17). These results suggest that proximal PAS isoforms are generally more prevalent than distal PAS isoforms across tissues. Notably, we observed an increased usage of distal PAS (reflected by elevated D/P ratios) in the heart, lung, and kidney (Fig. 8f), which corresponded to 193 Dgenes (Fig. 8g). Further enrichment analysis revealed that Dgenes in heart, liver, spleen of DT pigs were significantly enriched in several critical pathways, including the cGMP-PKG signaling pathway associated with pulmonary arterial pressure regulation, the Apelin and Oxytocin signaling pathways related to vascular tone modulation, and the Mitophagy–animal and Glucagon signaling pathways implicated in mitochondrial function and energy metabolism (Fig. 8h). Subsequently, miRNA binding site scanning prediction analysis was performed on the extended 3’UTR sequences of Dgenes in the heart, lung, and kidney of DT, which correspond to genes exhibiting proximal polyadenylation site usage (Pgenes) in the DSE (Supplementary Fig. 15a). The prediction results revealed thousands of miRNA binding sites within the extended 3′UTRs of Dgenes, corresponding to 67 unique miRNAs (Supplementary Table 18 and Supplementary Fig. 15b, c). Notably, among the predicted miRNAs, we identified several that have been extensively reported to be involved in hypoxia-responsive processes, HIF-1 pathway regulation, cardiovascular function, and angiogenesis, such as miR-21, miR-145, miR-181, and miR-102 (Table 1).

Interestingly, we observed the opposite trend, with a marked decrease in the usage of distal PAS (reflected by reduced D/P ratios) in the liver of DT pigs. This trend indicates a global shortening of 3′ UTRs in the liver tissue. Given the liver’s central role in metabolism and its critical importance in responding to hypoxic stress, we performed GO enrichment analysis on genes with increased usage of proximal APA sites (Pgenes) in DT liver. The results showed that these Pgenes were significantly enriched in terms related to metabolism, including oxoacid metabolic process, oxidoreductase activity, organic acid metabolic process, and carboxylic acid metabolic process (Supplementary Fig. 16). Together, these findings suggest a model in which high-altitude DT pigs exhibited distinct post-transcriptional regulatory patterns, characterized by enhanced distal PAS usage in the heart, lung, and kidney, but increased proximal PAS usage in the liver. Notably, the extended 3′UTR exposed additional binding sites for key high-altitude related miRNAs (e.g., miR-21, miR-145, miR-181, and miR-214), thereby introducing a potential layer of miRNA-mediated post-transcriptional regulation. These APA shifts, coupled with the associated miRNA regulatory potential, were correlated with the modulation of signaling pathways related to vascular tone, pulmonary circulation homeostasis, and energy metabolism, potentially facilitating physiological acclimatization to the hypoxic environment.

Identification of core alternative major polyadenylation (AMP) genes underlying high-altitude adaptation

The PAS with the highest frequency of usage for each APA gene across the five visceral tissues in DSE and DT pigs was defined as the major PAS, and an alteration of the major PAS was referred to as an alternative major PAS (AMP). Across the five visceral tissues of DSE and DT, proximal PAS isoforms of APA genes exhibited significantly higher expression than distal PAS isoforms (Wilcoxon test, p value <0.01) (Fig. 9a and Supplementary Table 19). Transcripts with distal PASs are more likely to be targeted and degraded by microRNAs in than those with proximal PASs, which may contribute to the consistently lower expression of distal PAS isoforms relative to proximal PAS isoforms among fiver visceral tissues10,39,40. The result suggests that in the examined porcine tissues, the usage of proximal PAS likely serves to maximize the expression of key genes by bypassing post-transcriptional repression. In the five visceral tissues, the AMP of 1879 APA genes (70.27%) remained consistent between DSE and DT pigs, whereas 795 APA genes (29.73%) exhibited differential alternative major polyadenylation site (AM-PAS) usage between the two breeds. Notably, the heart and lung harbored the largest number of APA genes with altered AM-PAS usage between high-altitude (DT) and low-altitude (DSE) pigs (Fig. 9b and Supplementary Table 20). The result may suggest that APA regulation may be particularly important for cardiopulmonary adaptation to high-altitude hypoxia.

To further identify candidate genes potentially involved in high-altitude adaptation, we performed weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) on 759 AM-PAS genes, with the aim of detecting co-expression modules associated with altitude differences and identifying the most interconnected hub genes within these modules. WGCNA analysis identified a total of six co-expression gene modules (Fig. 9c and Supplementary Table 21), with the number of genes in each module shown in Fig. 9d. The module–trait relationship heatmap further revealed that the yellow module exhibited the strongest correlation with altitude (Supplementary Fig. 17). We subsequently concentrated on the yellow module and constructed a gene co-expression network, which was visualized in Cytoscape to further elucidate the interactions among its genes (Fig. 9e and Supplementary Table 22). From this network, five hub genes were identified, including DLAT, OMA1, AMN1, PREPL, and HADHA (Fig. 9f). Notably, these hub genes are predominantly involved in mitochondrial function, energy metabolism, and protein processing, suggesting that they may play key roles in the physiological adaptation of pigs to high-altitude hypoxia. These 61 MEyellow genes were significantly enriched in GO terms related to mitochondrial function, such as mitochondrial inner membrane, NADH dehydrogenase complex, and oxidoreductase complex (Fig. 9g). These hub genes were related to mitochondrial structure, respiratory chain function, and electron transport efficiency. The identification and enrichment of this co-expression module in high-altitude pigs suggest that modulating mitochondrial dynamics may be a vital strategy for sustaining energy metabolism and redox balance under high-altitude environment stress.

Discussion

The physiological and genetic mechanisms of high-altitude adaptation in plateau animals have received increasing research attention in recent years. As the world’s highest and largest plateau, the Tibetan Plateau has become a focal region for studying genetic adaptations to hypoxia in species such as the yak41, Tibetan antelope42, Tibetan Mastiff43, Tibetan chicken44, as well as Tibetan pig1. Yunnan Province, situated in southwestern China along the margins of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, features highly complex topography, steep altitudinal gradients, remarkable climatic heterogeneity, and extraordinary biodiversity, fostering the development of indigenous pig breeds adapted to distinct ecological niches. Here, we selected the DT (3200 m) pigs as a high-altitude model, with DSE (500 m) pigs as a low-altitude control. With a shared phylogeographic origin but long-term exposure to distinct altitudes, these two breeds constitute an ideal comparative model for high-altitude adaptation research. To our knowledge, this is the first study to present a comprehensive transcriptome landscape and provide a detailed characterization of AS and APA dynamics across five visceral tissues in pigs from contrasting altitudes.

Iso-seq overcomes the limitations of the short-read length of RNA-Seq, enabling more accurate and detailed annotation45, and has been widely employed to construct high-quality full-length transcriptomes across diverse species46–48, including pigs49. Here, we integrated Iso-Seq and RNA-Seq to construct a comprehensive transcriptome of five visceral tissues from low- and high-altitude pig breeds, thereby generating a detailed atlas of alternative splicing (AS) and alternative polyadenylation (APA). We identified a substantial number of novel transcripts as well as many previously unannotated genes, revealing the transcriptomic diversity of porcine visceral tissues and highlighting the current incompleteness of the pig reference genome annotation. Nevertheless, as only five tissues from two pig breeds were examined, likely, a considerable number of tissue-specific or breed-specific novel transcripts have yet to be identified. Moreover, the expression levels, exon numbers, and lengths of novel transcripts were significantly lower than those of known transcripts, underscoring the remarkable advantage of Iso-Seq in detecting low-abundance transcripts with complex structures.

AS is a crucial post-transcriptional regulatory mechanism in eukaryotes, essential for modulating gene expression and enhancing transcriptomic and proteomic diversity50. A single gene could produce multiple structurally distinct isoforms through various AS patterns, including SE, A5, A3, RI, MX, AF, AL, and other more complex patterns51. In this study, we identified 36,776 AS events from 9170 genes, generating 35,610 transcripts and contributing substantially to the transcriptomic diversity of five major visceral tissues. Skipping exon (SE) events were the predominant AS type, consistent with previous observations in pigs24,52. Notably, extensive differential AS events were detected between low- and high-altitude pig breeds across all five tissues, with SE remaining the most prevalent DAS type, echoing patterns previously reported in porcine placental tissues53. The DE-DAS genes were significantly enriched in pathways related to high-altitude hypoxia adaptation, including heme binding54, tetrapyrrole binding55, iron ion binding56, and oxidoreductase activity57. RNA-Seq results, further validated by qPCR, demonstrated the upregulation of three hemoglobin family genes (HBB, HBE1, HBM, and HBZ) involved in oxygen transport58,59 in high-altitude DT pigs. Collectively, these findings indicate that AS may modulate the expression of key genes, thereby contributing to DT pigs’ adaptation to high altitude.

While transcriptome diversity and AS complexity in pigs have been extensively explored24,60,61, investigations into APA remain limited. APA generates mRNAs with variable 3′ untranslated region (3′UTR) lengths62, thereby influencing mRNA stability, nuclear export, localization, and degradation, as well as post-transcriptional regulation by RNA-binding proteins and microRNAs63–65. In present study, we observed an increased usage of distal PAS (Dgenes) in the heart, lung, and kidney of DT, which correspond to genes exhibiting proximal polyadenylation site usage (Pgenes) in the DSE. Target prediction analysis revealed that these extended 3’UTR of these Dgenes harbored binding sites for a specific suite of miRNAs that were critically involved in hypoxia-responsive processes, angiogenesis and cardiovascular function. MiRNA-103/107 is involved in hypoxia-induced proliferation of pulmonary arterial smooth muscle cells by targeting HIF-1β66. Furthermore, previous studies have implicated miR-2167 and miR-21468 in the regulation of hypoxia-induced pulmonary vascular remodeling. MiR-181b69, miR-106a70, and miR-23a71 have been reported to be involved in the regulation of hypoxia-induced angiogenesis. Previous studies have shown that miR-181c protects cardiomyocytes from injury by inhibiting apoptosis via the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway72, while miR-19a and miR-29b have also been reported to protect cardiomyocytes against hypoxia-induced apoptosis73,74. Collectively, the recruitment of these functional miRNAs through 3’UTR lengthening in the heart, lung, and kidney likely provides a potential regulatory layer that facilitates the physiological adaptation of Tibetan pigs to high-altitude environments.

Our WGCNA and network analysis identified five hub genes—DLAT, HADHA, OMA1, PREPL, and AMN1, that likely occupy central regulatory positions in the response to high-altitude hypoxia. At the metabolic level, regarding metabolic reprogramming, DLAT (Dihydrolipoamide S-Acetyltransferase) is a core component of the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (PDC), which links glycolysis to the TCA cycle. Its regulation suggests an optimization of glucose oxidation efficiency, a critical trait for energy maintenance under hypoxia75. Concurrently, HADHA (Hydroxyacyl-CoA Dehydrogenase Trifunctional Multienzyme Complex Subunit Alpha) is pivotal for mitochondrial fatty acid β-oxidation76,77. The centrality of DLAT and HADHA implies a potential enhancement in metabolic flexibility. At the level of mitochondrial quality control, regarding mitochondrial quality control, OMA1 (overlapping with the m-AAA protease 1 homolog) serves as a stress-activated mitochondrial metalloprotease. It regulates mitochondrial dynamics by processing OPA1 (mammalian mitochondrial inner membrane fusion protein) in response to stress signals like hypoxia, thereby ensuring mitochondrial integrity and function78. Finally, PREPL and AMN1 are implicated in intracellular transport and cell cycle regulation, respectively, contributing to overall cellular homeostasis79,80. These hub genes highlight a coordinated adaptive strategy focusing on bioenergetic efficiency and mitochondrial maintenance, suggesting that their regulation via APA may represent an additional post-transcriptional mechanism potentially contributing to DT pigs’ adaptation to high-altitude conditions.

In conclusion, we characterized the comparative transcriptomic landscapes of five visceral tissues in low- and high-altitude pigs. By integrating RNA-Seq and polyA-selected Iso-Seq, we identified numerous novel transcripts and previously unannotated genes, thereby refining the porcine genome annotation and revealing extensive transcriptomic diversity, as well as the complexity and variation of AS and APA. Moreover, we characterized changes in AMT and AMP and identified hub genes (ALB, DLAT, OMA1, AMN1, PREPL, and HADHA) potentially involved in high-altitude adaptation. Our findings demonstrate the critical roles of AS and APA in shaping transcriptome diversity in pigs and regulating genes potentially involved in high-altitude adaptation. Collectively, these findings refine the porcine genome annotation and provide a new perspective on post-transcriptional regulation in the context of high-altitude hypoxia adaptation.

Methods

Animals and samples collection

Diannan small-ear pigs (DSE) and Diqing Tibetan pigs (DT), two indigenous breeds native to Yunnan Province, China, were used here. These two breeds share a close phylogeographic origin within the region but have inhabited distinct altitudes for generations, making them an ideal comparative model for high-altitude adaptation research. A total of 10 male 12-month-old pigs, with five individuals per breed, were raised under standardized husbandry conditions and the same feeding and management practices after birth and remained continuously at their respective experimental farms in Xishuangbanna (500 m) and Dingqing (3200 m), Yunnan Province, China, without relocation from weaning until slaughter. Following electrical stunning and slaughter, heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney tissues were immediately dissected, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C until further processing. All animal procedures were approved by the Life Science Ethics Committee of Yunnan Agricultural University (Approval No. 202403027). We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use.

RNA extraction and quality assessment

Total RNA was extracted from 50 samples (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) using the TRNzol Universal Reagent (TIANGEN, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration was determined with a Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and purity was assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). RNA integrity was evaluated on an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA), and only samples with an RNA integrity number (RIN) ≥7.0 were used for subsequent library construction and high-throughput sequencing.

Library construction and sequencing

Iso-Seq libraries were independently prepared for each breed and tissue type to ensure accurate representation of transcriptomic diversity. Poly(A)+ mRNA cDNA was synthesized using the SMARTer cDNA synthesis kit (Clontech, USA), and full-length cDNA was then amplified through PCR employing PrimeSTAR GXL DNA polymerase (Takara, China). Subsequently, SMRTbell libraries were constructed using the SMRTbell Template Prep Kit 1.0 (PacBio, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The qualified libraries were sequenced on the PacBio Sequel II platform at GrandOmics Biosciences Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). A total of ten libraries (five per tissue, across two breeds) were constructed and sequenced separately. Poly(A)+ mRNA was purified from total RNA using the Dynabeads mRNA Purification Kit (Invitrogen, USA). Sequencing libraries were prepared using the MGIEasy RNA Library Prep Kit V3.1 (MGI, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol and purified with MGIEasy DNA Clean Beads (MGI, China). The qualified libraries were subsequently subjected to paired-end sequencing on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform (MGI, China).

Iso-seq raw data processing

Raw subreads were processed using the SMRT Link v25.1 software suite (PacBio). Circular consensus sequences (CCS) were generated, and demultiplexing as well as barcode removal were performed with lima (v2.12.0) using the parameters “--isoseq --peek-guess --min-passes 3 --min-score 80”. Full-length non-chimeric (FLNC) reads were identified and poly(A) tails refined with isoseq refine (--require-polya).

Subsequently, transcripts were clustered using isoseq cluster2 (--use-qvs) to obtain high-quality consensus isoforms. The clustered isoforms were mapped to the reference genome (Sscrofa11.1) with pbmm2 align (--sort --preset ISOSEQ), a PacBio-optimized long-read aligner based on minimap2 (v2.26)81, and redundant isoforms were collapsed into a final non-redundant transcript set using isoseq collapse (--min-aln-identity 0.99 --max-5p-diff 1000). Furthermore, SQANTI3 (v5.5)82 was employed to characterize and annotate our transcriptome assemblies from the Iso-seq pipeline against the reference annotation file (Sus_scrofa.Sscrofa11.1.113.gtf). SQANTI3 classified isoforms, assessed protein-coding potential, and analyzed transcriptome structure and function, generating separate annotation files for each tissue of low- and high-altitude pigs. All FLNC sequences from all samples were merged using pbmerge (v3.4.0) to generate an integrated annotation file. A comprehensive transcriptome assembly was then constructed using the Iso-Seq pipeline, and isoforms were classified and annotated with SQANTI3.

RNA-seq raw data processing

Raw paired-end RNA-Seq reads were first quality-controlled using Fastp (v1.0.1)83 to remove adapters and low-quality reads. The reference genome (Sscrofa11.1) was indexed using HISAT2-build (v2.0.0beta)84, and clean reads were aligned to the genome with HISAT2 using default parameters. Transcriptome assembly for each sample was performed with StringTie (v2.1.7)85 with -G and -e optations, and all sample assemblies were subsequently merged using StringTie (--merge -G) to generate a unified transcript annotation. Gene- and transcript-level expression was then quantified in FPKM using StringTie (-e -G -A), producing normalized expression matrices for downstream analyses.

Differential expression analysis

Gene-level read counts were generated using featureCounts (Subread, v2.0.2)86 with the parameters “-p -t exon -g gene_id”. Differential expression analysis between low- and high-altitude breeds was conducted for each tissue separately using DESeq2 (v1.44.0). Genes with p-value < 0.05 and |log2(fold change)| >1 were considered differentially expressed. Functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was performed using the clusterProfiler R package (v4.12.0), with gene annotations provided by the org.Ss.eg.db database. Gene Ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways with p adj <0.01 were regarded as significantly enriched.

Alternative splicing analysis

Alternative splicing (AS) analysis was performed using SUPPA2 (v2.4)87. Transcript assemblies generated by StringTie were used to build a reference transcriptome annotation. Seven types of AS events were extracted with “suppa.py generateEvents”: skipped exon (SE), alternative 5′ splice site (A5SS), alternative 3′ splice site (A3SS), mutually exclusive exons (MX), retained intron (RI), alternative first exon (AF), and alternative last exon (AL). Percent spliced-in (PSI) values were quantified for each event using transcript abundance (FPKM) estimated by StringTie with “suppa.py psiPerEvent”. AS events with PSI values between 0.1 and 0.9 across biological replicates were retained and considered robust AS events for downstream comparative analyses. Differential alternative splicing (DAS) between low- and high-altitude breeds was evaluated with “suppa.py diffSplice” of SUPPA2 and “rmats.py” of rMATS88. Events with p value <0.05 and |ΔPSI|≥0.1 were considered significantly differentially spliced. PPI Protein–protein interaction networks were constructed using string (v12.0), and genes with a score >0.7 were visualized with Cytoscape (v3.10.3).

Transcription factor motif enrichment analysis

To investigate transcription factor (TF) binding potentially associated with differential alternative splicing, motif enrichment analysis was performed using HOMER (v5.1)89. For differential AS and AF events, the upstream and downstream 300 bp intronic sequences flanking splice sites were extracted as target regions. As background, 10,000 randomly selected intronic sequences of matched length were used. Motif enrichment was conducted with the “findMotifs.pl” command, and motifs with p value <0.05 were considered significantly enriched and annotated to corresponding TFs based on the HOMER motif database.

Alternative polyadenylation analysis

Alternative polyadenylation (APA) was characterized using the TAPIS pipeline (https://github.com/bio-comp/tapis/tree/master/scripts). Full-length non-chimeric (FLNC) sequences were aligned to the reference genome via gmap (v25.3.0)90 with “alignPacbio.py”, polyadenylation sites (PAS) for each gene were identified with “run_tapis.py”. To minimize false-positive PAS identification caused by internal priming at genomic A-rich regions, we extracted the 10-bp genomic sequences immediately downstream of each candidate PAS using bedtools getfasta. Candidate PAS whose downstream sequences contained continuous adenine stretches of ≥6 nucleotides were excluded from further analysis. Genes harboring more than two PAS were defined as APA genes. For each APA gene, we identified the genomic coordinates of the most upstream PAS (the absolute proximal site) and the most downstream PAS (the absolute distal site). The midpoint between the most proximal and most distal PAS was calculated as the fixed boundary to stratify transcripts, with those harboring PAS downstream of the midpoint classified as distal PAS transcripts (D), whereas transcripts with PAS upstream of the midpoint were considered proximal PAS transcripts (P). Transcript abundance ratios (D/P) were then computed for each APA gene, with D/P > 1 and D/P < 1 used to categorize genes as D and P types, respectively. To investigate the potential contribution of alternative major PAS (AMP) events of the APA gene to high-altitude adaptation in the DT pig breed, Gene co-expression network analysis was performed using the WGCNA R package (v1.72-1)91.

miRNA target prediction analysis of extended 3′UTRs

Target prediction analysis was performed to identify binding sites located in the extended 3′UTRs, defined as the regions between the most proximal and distal polyadenylation sites, of genes exhibiting distal polyadenylation site usage (Dgenes) in the heart, lung, and kidney of DT, which correspond to genes exhibiting proximal polyadenylation site usage (Pgenes) in the DSE. TargetScan (v7.0.2)39 and miRanda (v3.3a)92 were employed for miRNA target prediction, and to reduce false positives, only the overlapping miRNA interactions identified by both algorithms were retained.

RT-PCR and qRT-PCR validation

To further validate AS-driven transcriptomic diversity and transcript usage differences (DAS) under different altitude conditions, we performed independent RT-PCR validation using biological replicates for each gene. To verify the accuracy of the differentially expressed gene (DEG) selection, a subset of DEGs was subjected to qPCR validation.

Total RNA was extracted using RNAiso Plus (TaKaRa) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using PrimeScript™ IV 1st Strand Synthesis Mix. Conventional PCR and qRT-PCR were performed with standard reagents (Ex Taq or TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II, TaKaRa), using GAPDH as an internal control. Three biological replicates were included per assay, and relative transcript levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are listed in Table S23.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analyses and data visualization in this study were performed using R (version 4.4.0) and ggplot2 (version 3.5.1). Differences in transcript length, exon number, and FPKM between novel and known transcripts were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Correlations between transcript number and exon number, gene length, or transcript FPKM were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. qPCR validation of five hub genes is presented as mean ± s.d. (n = 5 independent samples per group). p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
