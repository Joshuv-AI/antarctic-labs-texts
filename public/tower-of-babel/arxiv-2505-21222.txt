Sylow subgroups for distinct primes and intersection of nilpotent subgroups
Francesca Lisi, Luca Sabatini

Abstract
. Let G G be a finite group, and let ( P i ) i = 1 n (P_{i})_{i=1}^{n} be Sylow subgroups for distinct primes p 1 , … , p n p_{1},\ldots,p_{n} . We conjecture that there exists x ∈ G x\in G such that P i ∩ P i x P_{i}\cap P_{i}^{x} is minimal in { P i ∩ P i g : g ∈ G } \{P_{i}\cap P_{i}^{g}:g\in G\} for all i i . We prove some weak forms of the conjecture, for example that a finite group cannot be covered by (proper) Sylow normalizers for distinct primes. Applications concerning the intersection of nilpotent subgroups are discussed.

1.
Introduction

In the literature of finite groups, there are not many results involving Sylow subgroups for distinct primes. One recent example is the article
[
DLMS25
]
which deals with commuting probabilities.
In this paper we study the action of a group on the totality of its Sylow subgroups, in a sense that we are going to describe.

For a finite group
G
and a prime
p
, let
\mathop{\mathrm{Syl}}_{p}(G)
be the set of the Sylow
p
-subgroups.
Moreover, let
\rho(G)
be the set of primes
p
where
G
has nonnormal Sylow
p
-subgroups, and let
r=|\rho(G)|
.
Then
G
acts on the cartesian product

\mathcal{SV}(G)\>=\>\bigtimes_{p\in\rho(G)}\mathop{\mathrm{Syl}_{p}}(G)

in the following natural way: If
P_{i}\in\mathop{\mathrm{Syl}}_{p_{i}}(G)
for some
p_{i}\in\rho(G)
, and
g\in G
,
then

(P_{1},\ldots,P_{r})^{g}\>=\>(P_{1}^{g},\ldots,P_{r}^{g}).

The kernel of this action is the intersection of all Sylow normalizers. By an old theorem of Baer
[
Bae53
]
this is the hypercenter, and the corresponding quotient is the permutation group on
\mathcal{SV}(G)
that we are interested in.

If
r=1
, then it follows from Sylow theorems that
the action of
G
on
\mathcal{SV}(G)
is transitive.
In general this is false, as for example it can happen that
N_{G}(P_{1})\subseteq N_{G}(P_{2})
, so there is no
g\in G
such that
(P_{1},P_{2})^{g}=(P_{1},\overline{P_{2}})
if
P_{2}\neq\overline{P_{2}}
.
The main point of this paper is that it is still possible to move the elements of
\mathcal{SV}(G)
significantly under the action of
G
.

We raise the following conjecture:

Conjecture A

(Main)
.

Let
G
be a finite group, and let
(P_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
be Sylow subgroups for distinct primes
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}
.
Then there exists
x\in G
such that
P_{i}\cap P_{i}^{x}
is minimal with respect to inclusion in
\{P_{i}\cap P_{i}^{g}:g\in G\}
,
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
.

This is a “synchronization” statement,
the crucial part being that
x
does not have to depend on
i
.
We observe that a minimal member (with respect to inclusion)
of
\{P_{i}\cap P_{i}^{g}:g\in G\}
is always close to
O_{p_{i}}(G)
in some sense

[
Isa08
, Theorem 1.38]
.
For groups of odd order, a theorem of Itô
[
Ito58
]
says that if
P
is a Sylow
p
-subgroup then there exists
g\in G
such that
P\cap P^{g}=O_{p}(G)
.
Therefore, Conjecture
A
takes a particularly elegant form:

Conjecture B

(Odd order)
.

Let
G
be a group of odd order, and let
(P_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
be Sylow subgroups for distinct primes
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}
.
Then there exists
x\in G
such that
P_{i}\cap P_{i}^{x}=O_{p_{i}}(G)
, for all
i=1,\ldots,n
.

The general situation is worse, the typical examples being
V\rtimes P
,
where
V=\mathbb{F}_{3}^{2}
and
P\cong D_{8}
or
P\cong SD_{16}
is a
2
-subgroup of
\mathop{\mathrm{GL}}_{2}(3)

(here
O_{2}(VP)=1
but
P\cap P^{v}\neq 1
for any
v\in V
).
The same examples show that the odd order assumption is necessary in many other parts of the paper.

One motivation for Conjectures
A
-
B
is their connection with
the study of intersections of general nilpotent subgroups (see Remark
2.7
).
For example, Conjecture
B
would imply the following neat fact,
where
F(G)
denotes the Fitting subgroup.

Conjecture C
.

Let
G
be a group of odd order and let
H\leqslant G
be nilpotent.
Then there exists
x\in G
such that
H\cap H^{x}\subseteq F(G)
.

Similar questions have been investigated by Mann
[
Man71
,
Man75
]
.
Conjecture
C
generalizes the aforementioned result of Itô,
as well as unifying remarkable theorems of Zenkov
[
Zen94
]
and Bialostocki
[
Bia75
]
for groups of odd order
(see Section
2.2
for a summary of the known results).

We believe that proving the Conjecture(s) above would be marvelous,
but we have not been able to achieve this task.
However, we have made some progress in understanding the action of
G
on
\mathcal{SV}(G)
,
implying in particular that potential counterexamples need to be quite elaborate.
First we observe that the action is even transitive when only two primes divide the cardinality of the group.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
G
be a finite group whose order has only two distinct prime divisors.
Then the action of
G
on
\mathcal{SV}(G)
is transitive.

This is false already with three primes,
as shown by examples of the type
V\rtimes K
where
V
is a faithful irreducible
K
-module
and
K
is nilpotent but not a
p
-group.
(Choosing two Sylow subgroups in the same complement
K
, their normalizers will be
K
.)

We move to a very general but weaker phenomenon:
The impossibility to cover any finite group with (proper) Sylow normalizers for distinct primes.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
G
be a finite group, and let
(P_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
be nonnormal Sylow subgroups for distinct primes.
Then
\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}N_{G}(P_{i})\neq G
.

The symmetric group on
4
elements shows that a group can be realized as the union of all its (proper) Sylow normalizers, so the hypothesis that the primes are distinct is critical.
The short proof of Theorem
1.2
is elementary and exploits a nice lemma of Bryce, Fedri and Serena
[
BFS95
]
.
It gives that for every vector
(P_{1},\ldots,P_{r})\in\mathcal{SV}(G)
there is some
x\in G
that moves all the coordinates,
so the following consequence is immediate:

Corollary 1.3
.

If
|P:O_{p}(G)|\leq p
for each prime
p
and
P\in\mathop{\mathrm{Syl}}_{p}(G)
,
then Conjecture
A
holds.

Finally, we settle a particular case of Conjecture
B
(and so of Conjecture
C
).
To use an inductive argument, we prove a stronger statement that is false in the general case.

Theorem 1.4
.

Conjecture
B
holds if
G
is metanilpotent.
In particular, there exists
x\in F(G)
that works.

This is interesting because stronger properties such as transitivity on
\mathcal{SV}(G)

are not enjoyed by metanilpotent groups.
The proof uses the existence of regular orbits in nilpotent linear groups of odd order
[
Har81
]
,
and other ideas that could be useful in understanding the general case.

2.
\mathcal{SV}(G)
and nilpotent subgroups

2.1.
The action on
\mathcal{SV}(G)

All groups in this paper are finite.
For a finite group
G
and a set of primes
\pi
,
O_{\pi}(G)
is the largest normal
\pi
-subgroup.
The Fitting subgroup
F(G)=\prod_{p}O_{p}(G)
is the largest nilpotent normal subgroup, and the hypercenter
\mathcal{H}(G)
is the last term of the upper central series of
G
. (Equivalently it is the smallest normal subgroup modulo which the center is trivial.)
It is clear that
\mathcal{H}(G)\subseteq F(G)
.

Theorem 2.1

(Baer)
.

Let
G
be a finite group. Then
\mathcal{H}(G)
coincides with

(i)

the intersection of the maximal nilpotent subgroups;

(ii)

the intersection of all the Sylow normalizers;

(iii)

the kernel of the action of
G
on
\mathcal{SV}(G)
.

Proof.

See
[
Bae53
, Corollaries 3-4]
.
Point (iii) follows from (ii).
∎

Baer proved other interesting properties of the hypercenter.
For example, he showed that a
p
-element lies in
\mathcal{H}(G)
if and only if it commutes with all the
p^{\prime}
-elements of
G

[
Bae53
, Theorem 1]
.
We remark the following:

Lemma 2.2
.

F(G/\mathcal{H}(G))=F(G)/\mathcal{H}(G)
.

Proof.

We first notice that
F(G/Z(G))=F(G)
.
In fact, if
F_{0}/Z(G)=F(G/Z(G))
,
then
Z(G)\subseteq F_{0}\cap C_{G}(F_{0})=Z(F_{0})
, and
F_{0}/Z(F_{0})
is nilpotent.
So
F_{0}
itself is nilpotent, and
F_{0}=F(G)
.
The proof follows by the definition of
\mathcal{H}(G)
and induction.
∎

In proving all the statements in this paper, we can always assume that we are working in
G/\mathcal{H}(G)
,
or equivalently that the center of the group
G
is trivial.

2.2.
Intersection of nilpotent subgroups

The study of intersections of Sylow subgroups and more in general of conjugate nilpotent subgroups is old and rich.
It relates with other topics as the existence of regular orbits in linear groups (see
[
Har81
]
for example), the reason being the following observation:

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
G=V\rtimes K
.
For each
v\in V
, we have
C_{K}(v)=K\cap K^{v}
.

Proof.

It is easy to see that
C_{K}(v)\subseteq K\cap K^{v}
.
Now suppose
a=b^{v}\in K\cap K^{v}
for some
a,b\in K
and
v\in V
.
Multiplying by
b^{-1}
we obtain
b^{-1}a=b^{-1}v^{-1}bv
.
The left side lies in
K
, while the right side lies in
V
, so
a=b\in C_{K}(v)
.
∎

We now report the theorem of Itô mentioned in the Introduction.

Theorem 2.4

(Itô)
.

Let
P\leqslant G
be a Sylow
p
-subgroup of the solvable group
G
.
Suppose one of the following holds:

•

|G|
is odd;

•

p
is an odd non-Mersenne prime;

•

p=2
, and
|G|
is not divisible by any Fermat prime, nor by any prime
q\equiv 3\pmod{4}
.

Then there exists
g\in G
such that
P\cap P^{g}=O_{p}(G)
.

Proof.

See
[
Ito58
]
.
∎

See also
[
Pas66
,
Man75
,
Rob84
]
for related work.
Moreover, Brodkey
[
Isa08
, Theorem 1.37]
showed that the conclusion of Theorem
2.4
holds whenever the Sylow
p
-subgroups are abelian.

We move to more general nilpotent subgroups.
Given a set of primes
\pi
and a
\pi
-subgroup
H
of
G
, we clearly have

Core_{G}(H)\subseteq O_{\pi}(G).

If
H
is nilpotent then

Core_{G}(H)\subseteq H\cap F(G)\subseteq O_{\pi}(F(G))\subseteq O_{\pi}(G).

If in addition
O_{\pi}(G)\subseteq H
, then

Core_{G}(H)=O_{\pi}(G)

and we have all equalities above.
The condition
O_{\pi}(G)\subseteq H
is assured if
\pi=\{p\}
and
H
is a Sylow
p
-subgroup, or if
G
is solvable and
H
is
\pi
-Hall nilpotent subgroup.
In fact, Bialostocki
[
Bia75
]
generalized Itô’s theorem to nilpotent Hall subgroups of groups of odd order:

Theorem 2.5

(Bialostocki)
.

Let
G
be a group of odd order.
If
H\leqslant G
is a nilpotent
\pi
-Hall subgroup, then there exists
x\in G
such that
H\cap H^{x}=O_{\pi}(G)
.

Proof.

This is
[
Bia75
, Theorem B.4]
.
∎

In general, even if
|G|
is odd, for a nilpotent subgroup
H
that is not a Hall subgroup, it is false that
H\cap H^{x}=Core_{G}(H)
for some
x\in G
.
Indeed the number of conjugates of
H
required to obtain the core can be arbitrarily large:
take
H_{0}\leqslant G_{0}
with
G_{0}
nilpotent,
G=G_{0}\wr_{n}C_{n}
,
and
H<G_{0}^{n}<G
the core-free subgroup such that
H\cong G_{0}^{n-1}\times H_{0}
.
There are more general results that only provide
H\cap H^{x}\subseteq F(G)
,
the most famous being Zenkov theorem on abelian subgroups of arbitrary finite groups.

Theorem 2.6

(Zenkov)
.

Let
G
be a finite group, and let
A\leqslant G
be abelian.
Then there exists
x\in G
such that
A\cap A^{x}\subseteq F(G)
.

Proof.

See
[
Zen94
]
, or
[
Isa08
, Theorem 2.18]
.
∎

This greatly generalizes Brodkey’s theorem.
It is worth mentioning another recent extension
[
Zen19
]
:
if
A\leqslant G
is abelian and
H\leqslant G
is nilpotent,
then there exists
x\in G
such that
A\cap H^{x}\subseteq F(G)
.
(This result relies on the Classification of Finite Simple Groups.)

As promised, there is a smooth connection between Conjectures
A
-
B
and results “à la Zenkov”:

Remark 2.7
.

Let
H
be a nilpotent subgroup of
G
.
Then
H=H_{p_{1}}\times\cdots\times H_{p_{n}}
, where
H_{p_{i}}
is a
p_{i}
-subgroup for each
i
. If
g\in G
, note that
H^{g}=H_{p_{1}}^{g}\times\cdots\times H_{p_{n}}^{g}
, and

H\cap H^{g}\>=\>(H_{p_{1}}\cap H_{p_{1}}^{g})\times\cdots\times(H_{p_{n}}\cap H_{p_{n}}^{g}).

In particular

H\cap H^{g}\subseteq F(G)
if and only if
H_{p_{i}}\cap H_{p_{i}}^{g}\subseteq O_{p_{i}}(G)
for each
i
.

We come to partial results towards Conjecture
C
.
The proof at the end of
[
Man75
]
contains some typos and is quite difficult to read,
so we rewrite it here for completeness.

Lemma 2.8

(Mann)
.

Let
G
be a group of odd order and let
H\leqslant G
be nilpotent.
If
F(G)\subseteq H
, then there exists
x\in G
such that
H\cap H^{x}=F(G)
.

Proof.

We can assume that
H
is a maximal nilpotent subgroup containing
F(G)
.
Then, by
[
Man71
, Theorem 1]
,
H
is a nilpotent injector of
G
(see
[
Man71
]
for the definition of injector).
It is well known that any two injectors are conjugate, so it is sufficient to provide two injectors intersecting in
F(G)
.
For any prime
p
, let
C_{p}=C_{G}(O_{p^{\prime}}(F(G)))
, and let
S_{p}\in\mathop{\mathrm{Syl}}_{p}(C_{p})
.
We have
O_{p}(C_{p})=O_{p}(G)
,
and it was proved in
[
Man71
]
that
S=\prod_{p}S_{p}
is a nilpotent injector of
G
.
For each
p
, by Itô’s theorem there exists
R_{p}\in\mathop{\mathrm{Syl}}_{p}(C_{p})
such that
S_{p}\cap R_{p}=O_{p}(G)
.
It follows that
R=\prod_{p}R_{p}
is also an injector of
G
and
S\cap R=F(G)
.
∎

We gain some more evidence towards Conjecture
C
.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
G
be a group of odd order and let
H<G
be nilpotent.

(i)

If
H
is maximal, then there exists
x\in G
such that
H\cap H^{x}=Core_{G}(H)
;

(ii)

if
(|F(G)|,|H|)=1
, then there exists
x\in G
such that
H\cap H^{x}=1
.

Proof.

(i) We can assume
Core_{G}(H)=1
. Let
V\lhd G
be a minimal normal subgroup, so that
V
is an elementary abelian
p
-group for some prime
p
.
It is easy to see that
G=V\rtimes H
and
C_{H}(V)=1
.
Then we can apply known results on regular orbits as in
[
Har81
]
.
(Also note that
H
is a Hall subgroup in this case.)

(ii) Let
G
be a minimal counterexample.
Let
N=F(G)
, and observe that
N\subseteq F(NH)
.
If
N\neq F(NH)
, then there exists a prime
p\nmid|N|
such that
O_{p}(NH)\neq 1
.
On the other hand we have

O_{p}(NH)=O_{p}(F(NH))\subseteq C_{G}(N)\subseteq N,

and this is clearly impossible.
So
N=F(NH)
. As a consequence, if
NH\neq G
, then
NH
gives a smaller counterexample, contradicting the minimality of
G
.
Otherwise
H
is a Hall subgroup of
G
, and we are done by Theorem
2.5
.
∎

3.
Proof of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2

The following fact is well known.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
H,K\leqslant G
. The following hold:

(i)

|HK|/|H|
is the number of right cosets of
H
intersected by
K
;

(ii)

if
H\cap Kg\neq\emptyset
for some
g\in G
,
then
H\cap Kg
is a right coset of
H\cap K
.

Proof.

Point (i) is clear. For (ii), suppose
h\in H\cap Kg
.
Then

H\cap Kg=H\cap Kh=(H\cap K)h.\qed

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
p_{1}\neq p_{2}
,
P_{i}\in\mathop{\mathrm{Syl}}_{p_{i}}(G)
, and suppose
G=N_{G}(P_{1})N_{G}(P_{2})
.
Then for every choice
Q_{i}\in\mathop{\mathrm{Syl}}_{p_{i}}(G)

(i=1,2)

there are at least
|N_{G}(P_{1})\cap N_{G}(P_{2})|
elements
x\in G
such that
P_{1}^{x}=Q_{1}
and
P_{2}^{x}=Q_{2}
.

Proof.

Let
I=N_{G}(P_{1})
,
J=N_{G}(P_{2})
,
and
P_{1}^{y}=Q_{1}
,
P_{2}^{z}=Q_{2}
for some
y,z\in G
.
Note that
(P_{1})^{gy}=Q_{1}
for every
g\in I
, and
(P_{2})^{gz}=Q_{2}
for every
g\in J
.
Then, all the elements of
Iy
provide the desired conjugate of
P_{1}
,
and all the elements of
Jz
provide the desired conjugate of
P_{2}
.
Now
|Iy\cap Jz|=|I\cap Jzy^{-1}|
.
By Lemma
3.1
and
G=JI
,
we have that
I
intersects every right coset of
J
, so this cardinality is nonzero.
In particular
Iy\cap Jz
is nonempty and has cardinality
|I\cap J|
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Immediate from Lemma
3.2
(note that
G=P_{1}P_{2}
).
∎

We move to Theorem
1.2
.
The next is
[
BFS95
, Lemma 2.2]
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
G=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}H_{i}
be an irredundant cover of subgroups,
in the sense that removing any of the
H_{i}
’s does not give a cover anymore.
If
p
is a prime with
p\geq n
, then
\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}H_{i}
contains all the
p
-elements of
G
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Let
G=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}N_{G}(P_{i})
be a minimal counterexample with respect to
|G|
and
n
.
Then
n\geq 2
and the cover is irredundant by the minimality of
n
.
(Note that in general not all the primes corresponding to nonnormal Sylow subgroups would appear in this union.)
If
p_{1}<\ldots<p_{n}
are the primes appearing, then it is clear that
p_{n}>n
.
By Lemma
3.3
, there exists a prime
q
(for example
q=p_{n}
) dividing
|G|
such that every
q
-element of
G
is contained in
N_{G}(P_{i})
for all
i
.
Let
K\lhd G
be generated by the
q
-elements, and let
\overline{G}=G/K
.
It is clear that
\overline{P_{i}}=KP_{i}/K\in\mathop{\mathrm{Syl}}_{p_{i}}(\overline{G})
.
As a consequence of the correspondence theorem, it is easy to see that

N_{\overline{G}}(\overline{P_{i}})=\frac{N_{G}(KP_{i})}{K}.

Moreover
N_{G}(P_{i})\subseteq N_{G}(KP_{i})
.
If
KP_{i}\lhd G
for some
i
, then
by the Frattini argument we would have
(KP_{i})N_{G}(P_{i})=G
.
Since
K\subseteq N_{G}(P_{i})
, the left side is
N_{G}(P_{i})
, so this is impossible by the choice of the
P_{i}
’s.
It follows that
N_{\overline{G}}(\overline{P_{i}})\neq\overline{G}
for each
i
,
so that
\overline{G}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}N_{\overline{G}}(\overline{P_{i}})
provides a smaller counterexample,
against the minimality of
G
.
∎

4.
Synchronization in metanilpotent groups

We will use the following observation without further mention:
If
G
is metanilpotent and
F(G)=O_{p}(G)
for some prime
p
,
then
p
does not divide
|G:F(G)|
.

The key idea in proving Theorem
1.4
is to achieve synchronization using a product of commuting elements.
We start with the following technical result.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
G
be a group of odd order where
F(G)
is a Hall subgroup and
G/F(G)
is nilpotent.
Let
(P_{i})_{i=1}^{r}
be nonnormal Sylow subgroups of
G
for distinct primes.
If
A\lhd G
is abelian and
AN_{G}(P_{i})=G
for all
i
,
then there exists
x\in A
such that
P_{i}\cap P_{i}^{x}=1
for all
i
.

Proof.

Let
F=F(G)
, and observe that
A\subseteq F
.
By Schur-Zassenhaus theorem, there exists
K<G
such that
G=F\rtimes K
.
We can suppose that
\pi(G/F)=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{r}\}
and that
P_{i}\in\mathop{\mathrm{Syl}}_{p_{i}}(G)
for each
i
.
For fixed
i
, the hypothesis
AN_{G}(P_{i})=G
means that
A
is transitive on
\mathop{\mathrm{Syl}}_{p_{i}}(G)
by conjugation.
We first use this fact to show that
F(AK)=A
.
Let
K=K_{1}\times\cdots\times K_{r}
where
K_{i}\in\mathop{\mathrm{Syl}}_{p_{i}}(K)\subseteq\mathop{\mathrm{Syl}}_{p_{i}}(G)
.
If
g\in F
, then

(AK)^{g}=AK^{g}=A(K_{1}^{g}\times\cdots\times K_{r}^{g}).

By the transitivity of
A
there exist
a_{1},\ldots,a_{r}\in A
such that

(AK)^{g}=A(K_{1}^{a_{1}}\times\cdots\times K_{r}^{a_{r}}).

Therefore
K_{i}^{a_{i}}\subseteq(AK)^{g}
for all
i
, and since
a_{i}\in A
,
also
K_{i}\subseteq(AK)^{g}
for all
i
.
This implies that
K\subseteq(AK)^{g}
and so
AK\lhd G
because
g
is arbitrary.
It follows that
F(AK)\subseteq AK\cap F=A
and equality holds.

By Theorem
2.5
