Molecular mechanisms and therapeutic targets in lung cancer–nerve crosstalk
Authors: Ziyang Xu, Shanshan Huang, Qian Chu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractReciprocal crosstalk between the nervous system and malignancies is increasingly recognized as a key regulator of tumor progression across solid cancers. Neuronal activity and nerve infiltration can shape tumor growth, immune tone, and metastatic behavior through paracrine signaling and, in selected contexts, synapse-like interactions. However, how neural circuits regulate lung cancer progression remains insufficiently defined. β-adrenergic blockade has been reported to be associated with reduced lung tumor growth and metastatic potential in multiple settings, highlighting the clinical relevance of innervation. Here we synthesize emerging evidence on lung cancer–nerve crosstalk from the perspective of cancer neuroscience, focusing on neural remodeling in the tumor microenvironment, mechanisms underlying tumorigenesis and metastasis, the neuro–immune–tumor axis, and translational opportunities for neuro-modulatory interventions. This review provides an integrated framework to inform future mechanistic studies and the development of therapeutically actionable targets.Keywords: Lung cancer, Cancer neuroscience, Neuro–tumor crosstalk, Metastasis, β-adrenergic signaling

Introduction

Cancer has traditionally been viewed as a disease driven by cell-intrinsic genetic alterations, accompanied by adaptations in proliferation, survival, metabolism, immune evasion, and metastatic competence.1, 2, 3 Accumulating evidence now indicates that the nervous system can actively modulate tumor behavior and reshape the tumor microenvironment (TME), while tumors can in turn remodel neural structure and signaling through neurotrophic cues, invasion-associated programs, and activity-dependent interactions.

Early studies reported that denervation could inhibit tumor progression; however, the underlying mechanisms remained unexplored.4 Landmark studies in prostate5 and gastric6 cancers established the critical role of nerves in tumor development and progression. Neuron–tumor co-culture experiments further demonstrated bidirectional crosstalk, in which tumor cells stimulate neuronal growth while nerves promote tumor invasiveness.7, 8, 9, 10 In colorectal cancer, neurogenesis has been identified as an independent risk factor for adverse clinical outcomes.11 Although these findings across solid tumors are highly informative, the lung represents a unique landscape for neuro-oncological research because of its dense innervation. The lung is extensively regulated by the autonomic nervous system (ANS), receiving reciprocal input from both parasympathetic and sympathetic pathways. Beyond local innervation, the lung is also highly susceptible to systemic neuroendocrine signaling. Chronic psychological stress activates the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis and the sympathetic nervous system (SNS), resulting in the release of catecholamines and glucocorticoids. As the lungs function as a primary interface with the external environment and are constantly regulated by neural reflexes (e.g., cough, bronchoconstriction), lung epithelial cells are constitutively exposed to neural signals, potentially rendering them primed for hijacking neural pathways during malignant transformation. Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality worldwide, and its progression is closely influenced by the complex regulatory network of the ANS.12, 13, 14, 15, 16

Based on histological characteristics of cancer cells, lung cancer is primarily classified into two major categories: small cell lung cancer (SCLC) and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Among these, NSCLC accounts for approximately 85% of all lung cancer types.17,18 In contrast, SCLC is a high-grade neuroendocrine carcinoma characterized by aggressive metastatic behavior and the highest burden of circulating tumor cells (CTCs) among solid tumors.19 Reflecting its neuroendocrine lineage, SCLC frequently expresses neuronal markers (e.g., neural cell adhesion molecule [NCAM], synaptophysin) and has been proposed to engage “neural mimicry”, including synapse-like coupling and autocrine neurotransmitter signaling in selected contexts. NSCLC encompasses histologically heterogeneous epithelial tumors in which perineural invasion (PNI) is frequently observed and is associated with adverse outcomes and symptom burden (e.g., pain and cough). Current anticancer strategies increasingly combine direct tumor targeting with modulation of non-malignant TME components, particularly angiogenesis and antitumor immunity.20, 21, 22 Nevertheless, therapeutic resistance and metastatic relapse remain common, and the neural dimension of the TME has been comparatively underappreciated. Emerging work suggests that nerves participate in a coordinated neuro–immune–tumor axis: neural signals can suppress antitumor immunity, influence myeloid polarization, and shape stromal programs, while neural invasion and remodeling contribute to metastatic routes and debilitating symptoms. Therefore, targeting neural components may offer a multifaceted opportunity to constrain tumor growth, modulate immunity, and improve quality of life. In this review, we synthesize recent advances in lung cancer–nerve crosstalk, highlighting neural remodeling in the TME, molecular mechanisms driving tumorigenesis and metastasis, neuro–immune interactions, and translational strategies for neuro-modulatory interventions.

Normal innervation of the lung

Lung cancer develops and progresses within a densely innervated organ, making neural inputs a plausible and recurrent regulator of both local symptoms and tumor biology. Functionally, three elements are particularly relevant. First, sensory afferents (including chemosensitive and mechanosensitive fibers) detect airway irritation or tissue injury and can trigger cough as well as neurogenic inflammation through neuropeptides and purinergic signaling. Second, autonomic efferents shape the airway microenvironment by regulating bronchomotor tone and glandular secretion. In particular, the parasympathetic cholinergic pathway and the sympathetic stress-related adrenergic axis represent key neurochemical routes frequently implicated in cancer biology and therapeutic modulation. Third, pulmonary neuroendocrine cells (PNECs) and neuroepithelial bodies form a specialized neuroepithelial interface that couples environmental sensing to local peptide signaling. Notably, PNECs are closely associated with neuroendocrine differentiation programs and with the pathogenesis of SCLC. Neural infiltration and remodeling in the lung tumor microenvironment

Increased neural density

Traditionally, tumors were considered "denervated" tissues. However, accumulating evidence has challenged this dogma, demonstrating that various solid tumors, particularly lung, pancreatic, prostate cancers, exhibit marked increases in neural density, a phenomenon termed "tumor innervation" or "tumor axonogenesis".5,23,24 In the context of lung cancer, gene expression profiling of primary and metastatic SCLC samples has revealed widespread upregulation of neuronal gene programs during tumor progression.25,26 Similarly, increased neural density has been observed in both overt tumors and pre-malignant lesions of NSCLC and SCLC.24 While concurrent increases in sympathetic and parasympathetic fibers have been documented in prostate5,7 and pancreatic cancers,27 lung adenocarcinoma displays a distinct distribution pattern. Sympathetic fibers preferentially infiltrate the peritumoral region, whereas parasympathetic fibers are largely confined to the intratumoral compartment.24 The molecular basis of this neural remodeling lies in the aberrant expression of neurotrophic factors (NTs). Tumor cells, together with stromal components, such as macrophages and cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), secrete NTs either autonomously or in response to host neurotransmitters, such as catecholamines. These NTs bind to receptors on adjacent nerve terminals, promoting neurite outgrowth and PNI.27 Specifically, lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) frequently overexpress nerve growth factor (NGF), while SCLC cells often overexpress brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), thereby promoting tumor innervation and growth.28 Clinically, intratumoral neural density has emerged as an independent indicator of poor prognosis across multiple cancer types. Tumors with higher neural density typically exhibit enhanced aggressiveness, increased metastatic risk, and reduced patient survival.29

In lung cancer specifically, elevated expression of β2-adrenergic receptors (β2-ARs) is associated with poor outcomes. Notably, the addition of β-blockers to standard oncological regimens has been reported to prolong overall and recurrence-free survival while reducing metastasis rates in some cohorts and settings.30

Finally, while previous research on neuro–immune and tumor–neural interactions focused predominantly on the primary tumor site,31,32 recent attention has shifted toward tumor-draining lymph nodes (tdLNs). Given the critical role of tdLNs in tumor immunity33, 34, 35, 36 and recent evidence demonstrating dense neural innervation within these nodes,37,38 substantial neuro–tumor–immune crosstalk is likely to occur within the tdLN microenvironment.39

Imbalance of neural subtypes and functional remodeling

Imbalance between sympathetic and parasympathetic innervation

Emerging evidence indicates that tumor progression within the TME is driven less by simple increases in neural density and more by functional remodeling of the nervous system.40 Under physiological conditions, the lung maintains homeostasis through a balanced antagonism between sympathetic and parasympathetic nerves. However, in the lung cancer microenvironment, this equilibrium becomes disrupted, shifting toward a state of sympathetic predominance.

Marked proliferation of sympathetic nerve fibers and excessive sympathetic signaling have been observed in SCLC, NSCLC, and other solid tumors, such as pancreatic and breast cancers.41,42 Infiltrating sympathetic terminals release norepinephrine (NE), which binds to β2-ARs upregulated on cancer cells. This interaction triggers downstream signaling pathways, including protein kinase A (PKA)/cAMP response element-binding protein (CREB) and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), and induces the release of NGF. Collectively, these stress-related signals drive a pro-tumorigenic program by promoting cellular proliferation, enhancing angiogenesis through vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) upregulation, fostering an immunosuppressive niche (e.g., by inhibiting T-cell activity and promoting M2 macrophage polarization), and further stimulating neural infiltration.27

Concurrently, the parasympathetic system may also be co-opted to support tumor progression. Acetylcholine (ACh) released from parasympathetic nerve terminals acts on muscarinic receptors (e.g., M3) expressed on tumor cells. In SCLC, as well as in gastric and colorectal cancers, ACh signaling has been shown to promote tumor growth.43 Furthermore, the vagus nerve’s physiological cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway may become ineffective within the TME. Chemical and surgical denervation studies have elucidated the distinct temporal roles of these repurposed neural pathways. Under normal physiological conditions, sympathetic and parasympathetic systems maintain a balance between growth-promoting and growth-inhibitory signals.44, 45, 46 In cancer, however, this regulatory mechanism is fundamentally disrupted. Sympathetic signaling appears to initiate early tumorigenesis, whereas the parasympathetic system—originally growth-inhibitory—is completely transformed into a critical driver of invasion and distant metastasis.5,47

Functional reorganization of sensory nerves

In the TME, sensory nerves undergo profound functional remodeling, the impact of which extends far beyond mere structural infiltration. This plasticity manifests across three distinct dimensions.

Neurochemical reprogramming and malignant loops: Sensory afferents are reprogrammed from passive signal transducers into active effectors. Tumor-derived NGF activates sensory terminals to release pro-tumorigenic neuropeptides such as substance P (SP) and calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), which in turn stimulate tumor growth and promote sustained NGF secretion, establishing a feed-forward malignant loop.

Peripheral sensitization and electrical excitability: The TME is rich in pain mediators, such as NGF, bradykinin, and protons, which sensitize C-fibers and increase the expression and sensitivity of transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) and transient receptor potential ankyrin 1 (TRPA1) channels in the lung cancer TME. This lowers the activation threshold, resulting in sustained pain signaling (hyperalgesia) and refractory cough.48, 49, 50, 51 Crucially, this electrical excitability is mirrored in SCLC cells, in which cholinergic input directly triggers membrane depolarization, driving tumor progression. Furthermore, these sensitized pathways transmit inflammatory signals to the spinal cord, inducing astrocyte activation and pathological alterations in the central nervous system.52

Functional dualism and heterogeneity: Contrary to the prevailing view that nerves are solely pro-tumorigenic, recent evidence has revealed a context-dependent functional dualism. In specific contexts, sensory nerves maintain the expression of protective genes (e.g., CASP7, ELK3); consequently, specific ablation of these nerves can paradoxically enhance metastatic potential.53,54 Thus, whether promoting or suppressing malignancy, the functional consequences of neural remodeling are highly contingent upon the specific neurotransmitter milieu and tumor type.5,55

PNI

PNI represents the most severe pathological manifestation of neural remodeling within the TME. It is defined as the active invasion of cancer cells along nerve fascicles—within, surrounding, or traversing the epineurium. Unlike tumor innervation, which refers to the growth of nerves into tumors, PNI describes the aggressive infiltration of tumor cells into neural structures. Although highly characteristic of malignancy, PNI is not exclusive to cancer and has also been reported in certain benign conditions.56, 57, 58 In NSCLC, the incidence of PNI ranges from 15–40%, with a significantly higher prevalence in adenocarcinoma than in squamous cell carcinoma.59 In SCLC, PNI is ubiquitous, attributed to the intrinsic neuroendocrine features of these cells and their high expression of neural adhesion molecules (e.g., NCAM), which facilitate neural adherence.60

Earlier views proposed that neuroinvasion occurred along paths of least resistance. However, recent studies demonstrate that diverse cell types within the perineural microenvironment actively contribute to this process. These cells secrete growth factors and chemokines that collectively support cancer cell migration along nerve axons.61,62 Lung cancer cells secrete neurotrophic factors (e.g., NGF, BDNF) and chemokines (e.g., chemokine (c-c motif) ligand 2 [CCL2], chemokine (c-x-c motif) ligand 12 [CXCL12]), establishing a high concentration gradient around nerves that chemoattracts cancer cells via specific receptors. Nerves and associated Schwann cells secrete glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF) and artemin (ARTN). Tumor cells responding via rearranged during transfection (RET)/GDNF family receptor alpha (GFRα) receptors exhibit enhanced adhesion and invasive capabilities. Activated Schwann cells are pivotal facilitators of PNI. They secrete transforming growth factor β (TGF-β) and autophagy-inducing factors to promote tumor survival and chemoresistance.8 Mechanistic insights from prostate cancer models further reveal that tumor-derived TGF-β1 can induce Schwann cells to downregulate caveolin-1 (Cav-1), thereby inhibiting apoptosis in invading cancer cells.63 Additionally, in vivo models implicate the galanin (GAL) signaling pathway as a further promoter of neuro–tumor crosstalk during PNI.64

By exploiting the perineural space as a low-resistance conduit, cancer cells can bypass conventional lymphatic and vascular barriers and disseminate over long distances, potentially reaching the CNS.52 Consequently, PNI serves as a robust negative prognostic indicator, closely associated with higher recurrence rates, reduced overall survival, and severe, intractable neuropathic pain (Fig. 2).54,58,65, 66, 67, 68

Effects of tumors on neurological function

Promoting neurogenesis

NTs: NTs constitute a family of soluble growth factors—including NGF, BDNF, neurotrophin-3 (NT-3), and neurotrophin-4 (NT-4)—that physiologically regulate synaptic function, plasticity, neuronal survival, lung innervation, and inflammatory responses.69, 70, 71, 72, 73 In healthy tissues, these factors tightly regulate neural homeostasis. In cancer, however, this machinery is effectively hijacked by malignancies. Through aberrant autocrine and paracrine signaling, tumors exploit NTs to activate tropomyosin receptor kinase (Trk) receptors and stimulate growth,74 downregulate p75 neurotrophin receptor (p75NTR) to evade apoptosis, and induce angiogenesis to sustain tumor vascularization.75 In lung cancer, NT receptor expression is widespread.28 In particular, SCLC exhibits significantly elevated levels of NGF and BDNF.76 Recent studies have identified NGF as a critical driver of neural infiltration within the NSCLC microenvironment, directly linking NT overexpression and aberrant innervation.77

The ability of tumor-derived NTs to induce neurogenesis and PNI has been widely documented across solid tumors.78, 79, 80 Notably, associations between circulating neurotrophins and cancer can be heterogeneous across tumor types; for example, plasma BDNF has been reported to be reduced in colorectal cancer cohorts compared with healthy controls.81,82

Overall, NT-driven neurogenesis may form a feed-forward loop in which tumor-derived NTs promote innervation, and neural neurotransmitters reciprocally support tumor programs.

Axon guidance molecules (AGMs): While NTs act as mitogens to fuel neural outgrowth, the spatial architecture of tumor innervation is shaped by AGMs. Lung cancer cells hijack developmental cues to construct a permissive microenvironment for neural cell infiltration.

Under physiological conditions, semaphorin 3A (SEMA3A) functions as a potent chemorepellent to restrict aberrant neural sprouting.83 However, in NSCLC, SEMA3A expression is frequently downregulated, effectively dismantling the inhibitory barrier and permitting the unrestricted ingress of sympathetic and parasympathetic fibers into the tumor bed.84

Concurrently, lung tumors upregulate attractive guidance cues. Netrin-1, which is overexpressed in a significant proportion of NSCLC cases, acts via deleted in colorectal carcinoma (DCC) and uncoordinated-5 (UNC5) receptors on nerve terminals.85,86 Netrin-1 can also enhance the invasion, migration, and angiogenesis of lung cancer through phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (AKT) and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) processes mediated by extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK).87 This signaling establishes a chemoattractive gradient that actively steers axonal pathfinding toward the tumor core.

Additionally, molecules of the slit guidance ligand (SLIT)/roundabout (ROBO) and Ephrin/Eph families, which are traditionally known to regulate midline crossing and topographic mapping, are repurposed in the TME to modulate neuronal branching and adhesion.88,89 Together with NTs, this dysregulated AGM network ensures that the tumor acquires a dense and functional neural supply to support its progression.

Cellular plasticity, microenvironmental reprogramming, and neuro mimicry: Lung cancer cells are active architects of the neural niche, exhibiting marked upregulation of transcriptional programs governing axonogenesis and neural migration. Key mediators include members of the SLIT/ROBO, Ephrin/Eph, and semaphorin families, alongside neural cell adhesion molecules, such as NCAM and L1 cell adhesion molecule (L1CAM).

In addition to recruiting nerves, lung cancer cells undergo neuron-like differentiation to acquire electrical excitability and release neurotransmitters. SCLC, originating from pulmonary neuroendocrine cells (PNECs), intrinsically expresses neuronal markers (neuron-specific enolase [NSE], synaptophysin) and possesses voltage-gated calcium channels (VGCCs) capable of generating action potentials.90,91 Recent breakthroughs reveal that SCLC cells can form functional synaptic structures with neurons, hijacking synaptic signals mediated by N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) and γ-aminobutyric acid type A (GABA(A)) receptors to fuel tumor progression.92 Given that synaptogenesis typically requires adhesion-mediated recognition and stabilization, immunoglobulin cell adhesion molecules (IgCAMs), such as NCAM and L1CAM, may contribute to tumor–neurite apposition and the assembly of synapse-like junctions, potentially facilitating the local recruitment of neurotransmission-related components.93 Importantly, this synaptic mimicry is likely to be spatiotemporally restricted and heterogeneous, potentially emerging in a subset of tumor cells at tumor–nerve interfaces and in plasticity-inducing contexts.92,94 Whether these states are reversible and how they evolve over time remain to be determined in lung cancer. This cellular plasticity is dynamic; co-culture with nerves can induce SCLC cells to adopt an astrocyte-like phenotype,95 while in NSCLC, therapeutic stress (e.g., epidermal growth factor receptor-tyrosine kinase inhibitor [EGFR-TKI] resistance) can trigger transdifferentiation into SCLC or neuroendocrine lineages.96

A novel mechanism of neurogenesis has been identified in the NSCLC microenvironment, particularly in older patients with adenocarcinoma. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) can directly promote innervation via macrophage-to-neuron transition (MNT), and inhibiting Smad3 signaling effectively blocks MNT in vivo, highlighting a potential therapeutic target to intercept tumor neurogenesis.97

Similar neuromodulatory phenomena are observed across various solid tumors, including secretory leukocyte protease inhibitor (SLPI)-driven nociceptor neurite outgrowth, sensitization, and CGRP release in melanoma, sensory neuron calcium oscillations and SP release in breast cancer, and semaphorin 4F (S4F)-dependent neurogenesis in prostate cancer.7,38,98

Induction of neuropathic cancer pain (NCP)

NCP represents one of the most debilitating complications of lung cancer, severely compromising a patient’s quality of life.99 Distinct from simple nociceptive pain caused by tissue injury or inflammatory pain mediated by TAMs, NCP arises from direct tumor damage or functional alteration of the somatosensory nervous system. Clinically, it is characterized by opioid resistance, often necessitating adjuvant therapies such as anticonvulsants (e.g., gabapentin) or antidepressants. The prevalence of NCP in lung cancer is approximately 30%, which significantly exceeds the pan-cancer average (20 %).100,101

Given the high propensity of lung cancer for spinal metastasis,103 tumors often destroy the vertebral body and extend into the spinal canal. This results in the compression of the spinal cord or nerve roots, causing radiculopathy, radiating pain, and paresthesia in the affected dermatomes.104

Beyond physical compression, lung cancer cells secrete an algogenic soup comprising NGF, prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), bradykinin, and protons H⁺. These inflammatory and acidic mediators within the TME may sensitize not only terminal nerve endings but also passage nerve trunks, leading to a profound amplification of nociceptive signaling.105

The functional consequences of tumor–neural interactions are context-dependent. In the CNS, malignancies, such as glioblastoma, induce aberrant synapse formation to increase neuronal excitability and precipitate seizures.106 Similarly, in the peripheral nervous system, tumor cells induce axonogenesis and PNI, resulting in circuit remodeling and chronic pain syndromes.107

Conversely, neural modulation does not manifest solely as pain generation; it can also involve sensory suppression, leading to a diagnostic delay. For instance, during the pre-malignant phase of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, cells release CXCL12 to recruit Schwann cells, which dampen pain perception.108 Thus, in lung cancer, NCP may reflect not only structural nerve damage but also active tumor–neural remodeling that shapes pain perception and clinical presentation.

Remodeling the microenvironment for neural growth

Tumors reconstruct their surroundings to establish a permissive microenvironment that favors neurogenesis and axonogenesis. This remodeling is orchestrated by the secretion of soluble factors, restructuring of the extracellular matrix (ECM), and recruitment of auxiliary cells.

As discussed above, tumor-derived neurotrophic and guidance programs can promote neurite outgrowth and directional ingrowth toward tumor regions. Concurrently, physical barriers are dismantled; tumor and stromal cells secrete matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) to degrade the dense collagen matrix, thereby eliminating mechanical resistance to nerve growth,109 whereas repulsive axon guidance cues, such as semaphorin 3A, are selectively downregulated.

Non-malignant stromal cells within the TME were also co-opted to support innervation. M2-polarized TAMs serve as critical reservoirs for NGF and BDNF, and are capable of guiding neurite outgrowth via direct physical contact. Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) secrete GDNF and CXCL12 while synthesizing a laminin-rich matrix that provides a scaffold for axonal adhesion and elongation.110 Furthermore, Schwann cells are activated to guide physical contact between cancer cells and nerves.8

In addition to local interactions, lung cancer cells exert long-range regulatory effects via extracellular vesicles. Tumor-derived exosomes carrying specific miRNAs (e.g., miR-34a and miR-1976) or proteins (e.g., ephrin type-A receptor 2, EphA2) are internalized by peripheral nerve terminals. These vesicles induce neuronal differentiation and axonal outgrowth, potentially conditioning the neural environment before tumor colonization.111

In SCLC, neuron co-culture studies further support a synapse-related remodeling program, with increased synaptic marker colocalization and elevated neuronal activity, consistent with functional coupling between neurons and tumor cells.95,112

Effects of nerves on tumor progression

Nerve-dependent regulation of lung cancer can be broadly mediated by two complementary routes: (1) sensory neuropeptide signaling that links tissue injury/irritation to local vascular and immune modulation, and (2) autonomic adrenergic and cholinergic signaling that reshapes tumor and stromal programs through systemic stress cues and local neurotransmission. To avoid redundancy, detailed mechanisms of neuro–immune suppression (e.g., effects on dendritic cell [DC]/T/natural killer [NK] cells and myeloid programs) are presented in Section “Neuro-immune-tumor Interactions”, and the therapeutic implications of these pathways are summarized in Section “Treatment Strategies”.

Neural activity promotes tumor growth

Direct interaction with lung cancer cells: Histopathological studies suggest that tumor innervation can be spatially heterogeneous, with nerve fibers detected either within tumor parenchyma or predominantly at the periphery, implying that direct neural modulation may depend on both cell–cell contact and short-range paracrine signaling (Table 2).5,11 In lung cancer, this heterogeneity is further shaped by histologic subtype (Table 3; SCLC vs. NSCLC). Where innervation is present, nerves traditionally regulate tumor growth via the paracrine release of neurotransmitters. In SCLC, sympathetic NE activates β2-adrenergic receptors (β2-AR) on tumor cells and engages PKA-linked programs associated with expansion.41 In parallel, nerve- and Schwann cell–derived GDNF can signal through RET to promote adhesion and migration; notably, in NSCLC with RET fusions, constitutive RET activation may reduce dependence on ligand availability.113 Beyond paracrine mechanisms, emerging evidence supports synapse-like neuron–tumor connectivity in SCLC, where inputs mediated by NMDA and GABA(A) receptors have been linked to proliferative advantages, and pharmacologic inhibition of these receptors suppresses SCLC growth in neuron co-culture models.92,95 Similar synapse-like interactions have been reported in other cancer contexts, including brain metastasis settings, supporting the broader relevance of activity-dependent signaling.114 The causal contribution of innervation is further supported by denervation studies, in which surgical or chemical sympathectomy has been associated with delayed tumor progression in experimental models.113,115, 116, 117, 118, 119, 120, 121, 122, 123 However, denervation effects can be context dependent, and paradoxical tumor acceleration has also been reported, underscoring the complexity of neural regulation.122,124,125

Inhibitory remodeling of the immune microenvironment: Neural signaling does not merely stimulate tumor cells directly; it also fundamentally reconstructs the immune landscape by shifting it from an active defense state to an immunosuppressive or "cold" phenotype. This remodeling is achieved through glial–immune crosstalk and chronic neuroinflammatory signaling.

In brain metastases of lung cancer, reactive astrocytes serve as key intermediaries of immune evasion. They do not act merely as structural support but actively disarm the adaptive immune response through specific molecular axes. In EGFR-mutant SCLC metastases, reactive astrocytes secrete high levels of interleukin-11 (IL-11), which activates AKT signaling in cancer cells via glycoprotein 130 (gp130) and EGFR-associated routes, leading to increased programed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression and T-cell apoptosis.126 Concurrently, phosphorylated signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (pSTAT3)+ astrocytes secrete tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinase 1 (TIMP1). This soluble factor acts directly on CD63+ and CD8+ T cells, abrogating their activation state and effectively neutralizing cytotoxic responses within the metastatic niche.127

In the peripheral TME, the physical interaction between nerves and cancer cells (PNI) initiates a distinct immunosuppressive cascade known as cancer-induced neural injury (CINI). As observed in cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma, melanoma, and gastric cancer, tumor invasion leads to myelin sheath degradation. As the burden of CINI increases, the initial acute neural inflammatory response transitions to a chronic inflammatory state. Paradoxically, this chronic environment suppresses anti-tumor immunity, shifting the global TME towards a permissive, immunosuppressive phenotype that favors tumor progression.128 These findings indicate that in lung cancer, neuroimmune remodeling may contribute to immune escape by suppressing cytotoxic T-cell activity and reinforcing an immunologically cold TME.

Systemic neuro–metabolic modulation and TME remodeling: Neural signaling fundamentally reshapes the metabolic and vascular landscapes of the TME. In prostate cancer, sympathetic nerves release NE, which reprograms endothelial cell metabolism towards aerobic glycolysis, thereby fueling angiogenesis.129 In highly innervated NSCLC cells, elevated secretion of serotonin (5-HT) activates the PI3K/Akt/mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway. This triggers metabolic reprogramming in tumor cells, which in turn exacerbates the immunosuppressive nature of the TME.77 In pancreatic cancer, sensory neuron-derived SP activates signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) signaling in neuroendocrine cells, eliciting trophic effects that support tumor growth.117 Since STAT3 is a key regulator of cancer metabolic reprogramming, this pathway may link sensory neural signaling to the metabolic support of tumor expansion by enhancing energy production and biosynthetic activity.

The nervous system is a key driver of cancer-related cachexia, a systemic wasting syndrome. Lung cancer-induced systemic inflammation (e.g., IL-6) activates the sympathetic nervous system. Sympathetic terminals release NE in adipose tissue, activating β3-adrenergic receptors (β3-ARs). This drives the "browning" of white adipose tissue and excessive thermogenesis, leading to rapid weight loss.130,131 Tumor-derived factors cross the blood–brain barrier or signal via vagal afferents to alter hypothalamic feeding centers. In cancerous states, neurons expressing orexigenic short neuropeptide F show significant upregulation of leucine-rich repeat containing G protein-coupled receptor 3 (Lgr3) and nucleobindin 1 (NUCB1). These proteins modulate feeding hormones via the Lgr3/leucine-rich repeat-containing G protein-coupled receptor 8 (Lgr8) receptor axis, thereby inducing cancer-associated anorexia.132

Chronic stress-induced immune suppression facilitates tumor escape. Mechanistically, this is mediated by the upregulation of the plasma neuropeptide kisspeptin and its receptor G protein-coupled receptor 54 (GPR54) in tumor-infiltrating T cells, which functionally suppress T-cell cytotoxicity.133 Sympathetic signaling constitutes a formidable barrier to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy. β1-adrenergic signaling not only upregulates PD-L1 expression but also physically excludes CD8+ T cells from the tumor parenchyma, rendering tumors resistant to anti-PD-1 therapy (primary resistance).134 Preclinical models of ICB-resistant pancreatic cancer demonstrate that combining β-blockers with ICB acts synergistically to restore CD8+ T cell responses and induce tissue-resident memory-like T cells, effectively converting "cold" tumors into "hot" ones and significantly improving therapeutic response rates.135 Rhythmic sympathetic signaling is essential for maintaining robust circadian rhythms.136 In lung cancer, circadian disruption leads to loss of the core tumor-suppressive clock genes period circadian regulator 2 (Per2) and brain and muscle ARNT-like 1 (Bmal1). This loss results in c-Myc upregulation, thereby enhancing its oncogenic potential and accelerating lung cancer progression.137

Neural regulation of the metastatic cascade

Driving local invasion, lympho-vascular intravasation, and circulatory survival: The nervous system drives cancer metastasis through diverse mechanisms that govern cell motility, structural invasion, and survival. In lung adenocarcinoma, the sympathetic release of NE activates β-ARs, initiating the EMT program via the TGF-β/Snail axis to enhance migration and invasion.138 Similarly, small cell lung cancer (SCLC) cells exhibit neural mimicry, forming axon-like protrusions and executing neuroblast-like tangential migration.139, 140, 141, 142 Anatomically, tumor cells exploit the subperineural space as a low-resistance channel to bypass barriers.61 In pancreatic cancer, nerve-secreted GDNF induces neural tracking along axons. In addition to direct cellular manipulation, neural signals actively remodel the metastatic niche to ensure cell survival. Stress-induced sympathetic activation upregulates vascular endothelial growth factor C (VEGF-C), promoting lymphangiogenesis and vessel dilation to accelerate nodal dissemination143,144—a factor correlated with poor prognosis in NSCLC.145 In the breast cancer microenvironment, sympathetic nerves facilitate the infiltration of CD11b(+)F4/80(+) macrophages and their differentiation into the M2 phenotype, thereby inducing a prometastatic gene expression profile.146 Finally, to ensure survival during hematogenous transit, stress hormones (particularly epinephrine and norepinephrine) activate the Src/focal adhesion kinase (FAK) pathway via β2-ARs; this signaling mimics integrin-mediated adhesion, protecting CTCs from anoikis (Fig. 4).147 Thus, in lung cancer, neural regulation may act across multiple steps of the metastatic cascade, from local invasion and lymphatic dissemination to hematogenous survival.

Systemic remodeling of the pre-metastatic niche (PMN): Nervous system activity serves as a critical upstream regulator of the pre-metastatic niche (PMN), initiating a cascade of immune remodeling events that favors colonization. Sympathetic nerve endings interact directly with macrophages to enhance S100A8 expression, thereby recruiting myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) to the pre-metastatic lung.148 Sympathetic-derived epinephrine upregulates CCL2 expression in pulmonary stromal cells and C-C chemokine receptor type 2 (CCR2) expression in monocytes, thereby facilitating macrophage infiltration via the CCL2/CCR2 axis.149 Accordingly, chemical sympathectomy has been shown to reduce lung metastases by disrupting sympathetic nerve–driven recruitment of immunosuppressive myeloid cells, particularly MDSCs, into the pre-metastatic lung niche.148 This neural regulation acts in concert with tumor-derived exosomes (TDEs) to entrench an immunosuppressive landscape. For instance, lung cancer-derived exosomes specifically induce pulmonary fibroblasts to secrete high levels of C-C motif chemokine ligand 1 (CCL1), which activates C-C chemokine receptor type 8 (CCR8) in T cells and drives the differentiation and accumulation of CD4+ forkhead box P3 (FoxP3)+ regulatory T cells (Tregs) to establish immunological tolerance.150 Concurrently, TDEs reprogram macrophages via nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-kB)-dependent glycolysis toward a PD-L1-high phenotype,151 whereas exosomal Cav-1 and specificity protein 1 (SP1) further modulate the niche by inhibiting the phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN)/CCL2/vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGF-A) pathway in macrophages and triggering neutrophil-mediated inflammation, respectively, thereby promoting a pro-metastatic lung microenvironment that facilitates tumor cell colonization and metastasis.152,153

In addition to these systemic neuroendocrine and vesicular mechanisms, Schwann cells, the principal glial cells of the peripheral nervous system, have emerged as key cellular modulators of the tumor microenvironment.154, 155, 156 Complementing the signaling role of neurons, Schwann cells recruited by tumor-secreted NGF undergo dedifferentiation during the precancerous phase and adopt a "repair-like" phenotype, as notably observed in SCLC.157, 158, 159 Leveraging these phenotypic changes, Schwann cells directly interact with tumor cells via NCAM1/L1CAM, guiding their directional migration along nerves to promote invasion.8,160 Schwann cells also actively enhance metastatic potential by inducing EMT. This is mediated through distinct pathways, such as the C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 5 (CXCL5)-activated PI3K/AKT/glycogen synthase kinase 3 beta (GSK-3β)/Snail-Twist axis in lung cancer or the BDNF/TrkB axis in pancreatic cancer.161,162 Furthermore, Schwann cells facilitate survival within the neural niche by inducing "Schwann cell-like differentiation" (S100 calcium-binding protein A4 [S100A4]/glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) expression) in tumor cells and secreting miRNA-containing exosomes,163 thereby acting as both structural guides and functional partners in the metastatic cascade.

Distant colonization and organ-specific metastasis

NSCLC is the most common cancer to metastasize to the brain, and the median survival of patients with brain metastasis is 6 months.164 The colonization of tumor cells in the brain requires overcoming intense selective pressure from the reactive stroma. Initially, the brain microenvironment defends against invasion by activating plasmin. Plasmin not only converts astrocyte-derived FasL into a paracrine death signal but also inactivates the axon guidance molecule L1CAM. However, brain-tropic lung cancer cells evade this defense by expressing high levels of anti-plasminogen activator (PA) serpins (anti-PA serpins such as neuroserpin and serpin B2).165 Once colonization is successful, metastatic cells co-opt reactive astrocytes by activating STAT3 signaling in astrocytes, thereby shifting the astrocytic phenotype from a hostile to a supportive state.166 Metastatic cells can also establish gap junctional communication with reactive astrocytes, which provides metabolic support and promotes chemotherapy resistance.167

In addition to utilizing glial cells, lung cancer cells exhibit "neural mimicry" to exploit neuronal activity. In SCLC, tumor cells intimately mingle with axons, and optogenetic experiments have confirmed that the activity of cortical neurons directly drives tumor proliferation and invasion.95 Mechanistically, tumor cells hijack synaptic signaling by forming "pseudo-tripartite synapses" with glutamatergic neurons and utilize NMDA receptors (NMDARs) to uptake glutamate to promote growth.114 This adaptation involves profound genetic reprogramming: after contacting neurons, cancer cells activate CREB, upregulate neurotransmitter receptors and synaptic mediators (e.g., synaptosome associated protein 25 [SNAP25] translocation to the cell membrane), and re-express the developmental gene Reelin (RELN) to adapt to the CNS microenvironment.168

The immune landscape of the brain also undergoes remodeling, which is favorable for metastasis. NSCLC cells release IL-6, which activates microglia via the Janus kinase 2 (JAK2)/STAT3 signaling in microglia and polarizes them toward an anti-inflammatory, tumor-supportive phenotype.169 This reflects not classical anti-tumor activation, but functional suppression of microglial immune surveillance. Tumor cells further utilize the macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF)–CD74 axis to maintain this immunosuppressive microglial state, while blockade of this axis restores the anti-tumor M1-like phenotype of microglia.170 Conversely, metastatic cells downregulate interferon-induced transmembrane protein 1 (IFITM1) to reduce complement C3 secretion, thereby avoiding microglial activation and CD8+ T cell-mediated cytotoxic killing.171

Neural signals also regulate tumor metastasis to the bone tissue. Stimulation of β2-ARs on osteoblasts can induce the expression of receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-b ligand (RANKL), promoting the migration of metastatic tumor cells to bone in vitro. The use of β-blockers or RANKL inhibitors (such as denosumab) can block this cycle of bone destruction and inhibit early colonization, providing a therapeutic strategy to improve patient survival.172

Finally, the temporal progression of metastasis is regulated by stress-mediated awakening of dormant cells. Many disseminated tumor cells (DTCs) enter dormant states that can persist for years. However, chronic stress and sympathetic activation trigger the release of catecholamines, which induce neutrophils to form neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs).173 These NETs remodel the extracellular matrix, trigger a signaling switch, and alter the p38/ERK ratio, thereby reawakening dormant cells and leading to overt metastasis years after the primary treatment.174

Neuro-immune-tumor interactions

The equilibrium of the immune system is paramount to human health, playing a critical role in identifying and eliminating foreign pathogens as well as in monitoring and clearing abnormal endogenous cells, such as cancer cells.175 Within the nervous system, both neurons and glial cells actively participate in regulating the tumor immune microenvironment, thereby reshaping the immune landscape and influencing therapeutic efficacy.37,176, 177, 178, 179, 180 In this section, we summarize representative neuro–immune mechanisms across key regulatory levels of the cancer immunity cycle, including priming, effector function, the establishment of suppressive niches, and context-dependent counter-regulatory pathways.

Priming-level suppression: inhibition of DCs

Neural suppression of immunity is initiated at the beginning of the cancer immune cycle. NE released by sympathetic nerves acts on β2-ARs expressed on the surface of DCs. This interaction elevates intracellular cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) levels and significantly inhibits the secretion of the critical cytokine interleukin-12 (IL-12).181 Given that IL-12 is required for inducing the differentiation of naïve T cells into cytotoxic T helper 1 (Th1)/CD8+ effector cells, this neural signal effectively blocks T cell priming at the source, preventing the generation of an adaptive immune response.177 Furthermore, sympathetic signals promote the migration of immature DCs to lymph nodes. Consequently, the accumulation of these immature DCs within the lymph nodes, which are incapable of effectively activating T cells, may paradoxically induce immunological tolerance rather than activation.182

Effector-stage impairment: reduced cytotoxic activity of T cells and NK cells

T cells

The nervous system imposes a dual blockade on T cells through distinct sympathetic and sensory mechanisms, and the sympathetic nervous system responds to systemic stress. Under physiological conditions, sympathetic signaling via β2-ARs regulates lymphocyte trafficking, retaining lymphocytes within lymph nodes to maintain their homeostatic distribution across lymphoid organs. However, in pathological states, hyperactivation of β2-ARs inhibits lymphocyte egress and recruitment to peripheral tissues.183 More critically, sympathetic signaling acts directly on surface β2-ARs of CD8+ T cells to suppress the expression of the glucose transporter type 1 (Glut1). This inhibition blocks glycolysis and impairs mitochondrial function, thereby preventing the metabolic reprogramming necessary for T-cell activation and diminishing antitumor cytotoxicity.134,184

The sensory nervous system responds to localized pain and tissue damage. In contrast to the systemic stress response mediated by catecholamines, sensory nerves (nociceptors) respond to local pain and tissue damage by releasing the CGRP. CGRP directly binds to the receptor activity modifying protein 1 (RAMP1) receptor on T cells. This binding inhibits the cells' ability to produce cytotoxic cytokines (such as interferon-γ [IFN-γ] and tumor necrosis factor-α [TNF-α]), leading to T cell desensitization to tumor antigens and a failure to execute killing functions.98

NK cells

As the first line of defense against circulating tumor cells and metastasis, NK cells are highly vulnerable to stress-induced sympathetic signaling. Under stress conditions, activation of β2-ARs on NK cells suppresses the expression of the NK-cell cytotoxic effector molecules perforin and granzyme B, thereby reducing NK-cell cytolytic activity. This impaired NK-cell surveillance allows tumor cells to survive in the bloodstream and facilitates metastatic dissemination.185

Reinforcement of a suppressive niche: expansion and functional polarization of MDSCs

Sympathetic signaling via β2-ARs on MDSCs promotes their survival and increases their frequency within tumors and tissues. Furthermore, it drives MDSCs to express higher levels of immunosuppressive molecules (e.g., arginase-1 and PD-L1), enabling them to more effectively stifle T cell proliferation and facilitate immune escape.186

Bidirectional crosstalk: immune-associated neural remodeling

The relationship between the nervous and immune systems is not unilateral; rather, it involves dynamic, bidirectional crosstalk in which immune cells reciprocally influence the neural structure. Previous studies in asthma models have shown that airway neurons release eotaxins to induce the perineural migration of eosinophils.187,188 The density of airway eosinophils correlates with increased epithelial sensory nerve density, and confocal microscopy has verified that eosinophils mediate sensory hyperinnervation in mice.189, 190, 191 These findings imply a parallel mechanism in the TME, where immune cells may feedback to promote neurogenesis.

Counter-regulatory modulation: parasympathetic signaling as a context-dependent brake

However, the neuroimmune impact on tumors is not unilaterally pro-tumorigenic; in certain contexts, the nervous system exerts oncostatic effects. In pancreatic cancer, surgical resection of the vagus nerve leads to a significant increase in TAMs and TNF-α levels, which subsequently accelerates tumor growth and metastasis.192 Subsequent research demonstrated that the vagus nerve inhibits tumor growth by acting on muscarinic acetylcholine receptor M1 (M1 mAChR) in tumor cells, thereby suppressing downstream MAPK/EGFR and PI3K/AKT proliferation pathways.55 Additionally, vagal signaling disrupts the malignant feedback loop between tumor cells and M2 macrophages, preventing the recruitment and polarization of M2 macrophages, which otherwise drives tumor proliferation.193

In summary, tumor immune evasion represents not merely an intrinsic dysregulation within the immune system but fundamentally results from the intrusion of aberrant neural signals. Tumor progression is largely contingent on the dynamic interplay between the pro-tumorigenic drive of sympathetic and sensory nerves and the oncostatic counterbalance provided by the parasympathetic nervous system. A deeper understanding of these neuro-immune molecular mechanisms, specifically through the inhibition of β-adrenergic or CGRP signaling, provides promising translational avenues to disrupt immunological tolerance and improve therapeutic outcomes in cancer immunotherapy.

Treatment strategies

Therapeutic strategies targeting neurobiology

Direct targeting of the nervous system

Given the critical role of the nervous system in tumor progression, targeting neural signaling and structures represents a promising therapeutic frontier. Representative neuro-targeting strategies, their proposed mechanisms, and levels of evidence are summarized in Table 4. A major strategy involves the repurposing of β-AR blockers. Retrospective studies have suggested that non-selective β-blockers, such as propranolol, significantly prolong progression-free survival in NSCLC. This potential benefit is supported by preclinical studies showing that blockade of β-ARs on tumor cells, or inhibition of the NGF–Trk axis on neurons, markedly improves survival in murine models. Consistent with these observations, clinical data from PDAC and other cohorts indicate that β-blocker use is associated with longer disease-free survival and reduced metastatic incidence.27,194, 195, 196

Furthermore, perioperative administration is emerging as an important consideration. Surgical stress induces a catecholamine surge that may promote metastasis from minimal residual disease, and this effect may be mitigated by short-term preoperative β-blockade.197 However, the efficacy of this approach remains controversial, as other studies have reported that perioperative β-blocker use does not necessarily improve recurrence-free or overall survival in patients undergoing NSCLC resection.198,199

Beyond receptor blockade, targeting neurogenesis and axon guidance provides another therapeutic avenue. Because tumors secrete NGF to recruit nerves, Trk inhibitors (e.g., entrectinib and larotrectinib) may serve a dual role: they target tumor cells harboring NTRK fusions and may also inhibit pathological tumor innervation. These agents have been approved as first-line or subsequent therapies for NTRK fusion-positive lung cancer and have demonstrated deep, durable systemic and CNS responses.200

Axon-guidance pathways such as semaphorins may influence tumor innervation and immune tone; for example, SEMA3A downregulation has been linked to hyperinnervation and poor prognosis, and epigenetic restoration has been proposed as a normalization strategy.201,202 Conversely, SEMA4D promotes axonal growth and is often upregulated in stressed infiltrating nerves within the TME. It acts through Plexin-B1 on immune cells (TAMs and MDSCs), guiding their migration and promoting M2 polarization. In NSCLC, blockade of SEMA4D with pepinemab in combination with anti-PD-L1 therapy has been shown to enhance T-cell infiltration, reverse immunosuppression, and improve therapeutic efficacy.203

Targeting central neural signaling may also have value, particularly in brain metastases. SCLC cells can hijack glutamatergic signaling through NMDA receptors. Accordingly, NMDA receptor antagonists have been shown to inhibit the growth of SCLC brain metastases as well as NSCLC progression.202,204

Denervation studies support a causal contribution of neural inputs in multiple tumor models, but effects can be context dependent and may trigger compensatory remodeling; therefore, denervation currently serves mainly as mechanistic validation rather than a broadly generalizable clinical approach.5,6,52,118,119

Finally, addressing CINI is important for overcoming immunotherapy resistance. Recent work links CINI to anti-PD-1 resistance, and mechanistic interventions (e.g., neuronal activating transcription factor 3 [Atf3] modulation or interleukin-6 receptor [IL-6R] co-blockade) have shown preclinical activity.128

Collectively, these multifaceted approaches highlight the therapeutic potential of targeting the neuro-neoplastic interface.

Immunomodulation via the nervous system

Reducing β-AR signaling has emerged as a robust strategy for shifting tumors toward an immune-active microenvironment characterized by increased CD8+ T-cell frequency and reduced PD-1 expression.134 Mechanistically, sympathetic activation of β2-ARs on CD8+ T cells suppresses glycolysis and impairs mitochondrial function, thereby disrupting the metabolic reprogramming required for effective T-cell activation.184 Preclinical and clinical studies across lung, breast, and pancreatic cancers indicate that β-blockers can reduce tumor burden and distant metastasis while prolonging survival.47,205, 206, 207, 208, 209 In addition, perioperative β-blocker administration in breast and ovarian cancers has been associated with reduced recurrence and prolonged disease-free survival, although the magnitude of benefit appears to vary across tumor types.198,199,210, 211, 212 These findings suggest that strategic use of β-blockers may improve response to immunotherapy and enhance overall antitumor efficacy. Because sympathetic signaling both upregulates PD-L1 and metabolically impairs T cells, combining β-blockers with immunotherapy may produce a synergistic effect.134 This rationale has prompted emerging clinical studies of β-blockers in combination with immunotherapy. However, findings in NSCLC and other solid tumors remain limited and inconsistent, and further confirmation is needed. Current clinical evidence regarding β-blockers is heterogeneous across treatment settings (Table 5). Meta-analyses and large registry-based cohorts have not consistently demonstrated an overall survival benefit in lung cancer, whereas several retrospective studies have reported signals of improved progression-related endpoints in selected settings, such as concomitant use with ICIs, EGFR-TKIs, chemoradiation, or chemotherapy. Importantly, null or even unfavorable results have also been reported, including cohorts in which baseline or pretreatment β-blocker exposure was not associated with improved outcomes or was linked to higher all-cause mortality. This variability likely reflects differences in β-blocker class, timing of exposure, patient selection, and disease stage. Accordingly, the current evidence supports at most a context-dependent association rather than a uniform class effect. Prospective studies and stratified analyses that clearly define β-blocker subtype, exposure window, and treatment backbone are needed to determine which patients, if any, derive meaningful benefit. Beyond the sympathetic axis, targeting the sensory–immune interface offers additional therapeutic opportunities. For example, CGRP receptor antagonists such as rimegepant have been shown to mitigate CD8+ T-cell exhaustion and delay tumor progression.98 Similarly, strategies that disrupt stress-induced structural changes—such as DNase I to degrade NETs or β2-AR blockers to prevent their formation—may inhibit awakening of dormant tumor cells and reduce metastasis.173

In brain metastasis settings, glial–tumor–immune signaling may represent actionable nodes for immunomodulation. In EGFR-mutant NSCLC brain metastases, reactive astrocyte-derived IL-11 can activate interleukin-11 receptor subunit alpha (IL-11Rα)/gp130–EGFR–AKT signaling in metastatic tumor cells, increase PD-L1 expression, and promote CD8⁺ T-cell apoptosis, suggesting that co-targeting IL-11/gp130 and EGFR-driven signaling may help disrupt astrocyte-mediated immune escape. In a distinct but related mechanism, TIMP1 produced by pSTAT3+ reactive astrocytes has been implicated in the local suppression of infiltrating CD8⁺ T cells. Together, these findings highlight astrocyte-derived immunosuppressive cues as potential targets for restoring antitumor immunity in selected brain-metastatic contexts.126 In NSCLC, microglial reprogramming driven by tumor-derived cytokines has also been linked to metastatic support, and interventions that restore microglial antitumor phenotypes, including blockade of the MIF–CD74 axis, potentially in combination with radiotherapy, warrant further evaluation.169,170

Finally, bioelectronic approaches provide a non-pharmacological avenue for immune modulation. Non-invasive vagus nerve stimulation (nVNS) can promote ACh release and engage α7-nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (α7nAChR) on macrophages, with downstream inhibition of NF-κB signaling and reduced pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6) in preclinical settings.213

Neuro-modulatory approaches are emerging as a complementary dimension of cancer therapy. Mechanism-informed combinations of immunotherapy with neuro-targeting strategies warrant prospective evaluation with careful attention to safety and patient stratification.

Other therapeutic strategies

Stress management and psychological intervention

Clinical studies indicate that approximately half of patients with lung cancer experience emotional distress, and about 31% exhibit depressive symptoms.214 Prospective and retrospective studies, together with mouse experiments, have shown that stress promotes tumor initiation and progression.215, 216, 217, 218, 219 Chronic psychological distress can activate both the HPA axis and the ANS through β2-AR signaling, thereby altering the local immune environment of the lung as well as the lung cancer TME.220 Stress management and psychological intervention for patients and their families are therefore necessary.221 In contrast, under chronic stress conditions, plasma kisspeptin levels and expression of its receptor Gpr54 are increased, leading to T-cell dysfunction and facilitating tumor immune escape.133 Chronic stress also induces tumor cells to release VEGF-C, which drives lymphatic remodeling and reorganizes the lymphatic network within and around the tumor, thereby creating routes for tumor cell dissemination.222 In addition, chronic stress raises serum norepinephrine levels and disrupts the balance between M1 and M2 polarization of TAMs within the TME, further promoting tumor progression.223

Because chronic stress can induce systemic changes that favor tumor progression, stress management may be considered an important component of comprehensive lung cancer care. The aim is to attenuate stress-related CNS signaling and reduce allostatic load. Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) has been reported to relieve psychological distress in patients with lung cancer, improve quality of life, and reduce circulating cortisol and catecholamine levels; these changes have been associated with lower inflammatory markers (e.g., IL-6) and, in some cohorts, improved clinical outcomes.217,224

A positive psychological state may also regulate the tumor immune environment through the brain’s reward system. By attenuating sympathetic activity, it suppresses MDSC function and enhances antitumor immune responses.225 Studies of the conserved transcriptional response to adversity, mediated through β-AR signaling, further suggest that positive psychosocial factors may reverse these effects by downregulating pro-inflammatory genes and upregulating antiviral and antibody-related genes, thereby modifying the tumor immune microenvironment.226

Psychosocial support is equally important for patients with lung cancer. Social isolation has been associated with elevated NE levels in patients with cancer, which may further promote tumor progression.

Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM)

TCM approaches have mainly been explored as adjunctive strategies relevant to lung cancer–nerve crosstalk through neuroautonomic and symptom-modulatory pathways. Mechanistically, electroacupuncture at acupoints such as ST36 can engage a somatosensory–autonomic reflex (the vagal–adrenal anti-inflammatory axis) via dorsal root ganglion circuits in animal studies, providing a plausible link to neuro–immune regulation.227 Clinically, acupuncture/electroacupuncture has been evaluated primarily for supportive outcomes, particularly chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy and pain, with recent evidence syntheses suggesting symptom improvement;228,229 however, study heterogeneity and the lack of large multicenter trials limit firm conclusions, and antitumor efficacy in lung cancer remains unproven. Other herbal compounds (e.g., ginsenosides or alkaloids) have reported neuroactive and anticancer effects in preclinical models, but direct evidence linking them to lung cancer–nerve crosstalk and clinically meaningful oncologic benefit is currently insufficient;230, 231, 232 therefore, they are best regarded as hypothesis-generating candidates for future validation.

Multimodal and mechanism-guided management of lung cancer-related pain

Lung cancer-associated pain is fundamentally distinct from nociceptive pain, which is caused by simple tumor compression or somatic tissue damage. Neuropathic pain arises from damage to the central or peripheral nervous system, such as peripheral sensitization within the TME and central sensitization in the dorsal horn neurons of the spinal cord.

To address this complexity, the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) Adult Cancer Pain Guidelines advocate a multimodal strategy as the standard of care. This approach integrates pharmacotherapy ranging from nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and adjuvant analgesics (such as antidepressants and anticonvulsants) to opioids, with physical, cognitive, and psychological interventions. When necessary, these are complemented by interventional techniques like nerve blocks or image-guided ablation, all aiming to achieve the "5A" goals of pain management: Analgesia, Activities, Adverse effects, Aberrant behavior, and Affect.233

Beyond these standard protocols, recent breakthroughs in the field of neurotumor interactions have unveiled precise molecular targets for refractory pain. For instance, the high expression of NGF in the lung environment, which drives perineural invasion and activates TrkA receptors, can be effectively counteracted by the monoclonal antibody tanezumab.

High NGF expression in the lung cancer microenvironment promotes PNI and activates TrkA receptors on CAFs and nerve terminals, leading to severe cancer pain.234 Consequently, the anti-NGF monoclonal antibody, tanezumab, has been shown to effectively alleviate cancer pain and improve patient function.235 Similarly, ketamine is frequently utilized as an adjuvant therapy for refractory cancer pain,236 mechanistically, as an NMDA receptor antagonist that inhibits central sensitization by acting on neuronal NMDA receptors, while simultaneously suppressing astrocyte activation, a process closely linked to neuropathic pain.237 Additionally, P2X3 receptors expressed at nerve terminals are activated by adenosine triphosphate (ATP) to induce severe pain. Specific inhibitors of P2X3 and P2X2/3 receptors have been found to significantly mitigate both cancer pain and cancer-associated cough.238 In bone metastasis, pain is often coupled to osteolytic remodeling and peripheral sensitization; accordingly, therapies that reduce osteoclast-driven bone destruction or dampen pro-nociceptive inflammatory signaling may help relieve symptoms in selected contexts.239,240 Finally, resiniferatoxin (RTX), utilized for selective “neuronal ablation”, can destroy specific nociceptive neurons to treat severe cancer pain without compromising normal neurological function.241

Conclusion and perspective

The comprehensive body of evidence synthesized in this review underscores a fundamental paradigm shift in our understanding of lung cancer pathogenesis. Lung cancer is not merely a localized accumulation of malignant cells, but systemic disease shaped by dynamic interactions among the nervous system, tumor cells, and the immune microenvironment. This crosstalk is bidirectional and mutually reinforcing. The nervous system acts as a master regulator, deploying a diverse range of neurotransmitters—including NE, 5-HT, and substance P—to reshape the tumor ecosystem. A central theme of this review is that lung cancer cells are not passive recipients in this process, but active participants. They secrete neurotrophic factors to promote neurite outgrowth and tumor innervation, thereby facilitating perineural invasion, and they also exhibit marked neural mimicry. Notably, recent breakthroughs have provided definitive electrophysiological and genetic evidence that SCLC cells establish functional synapses through NMDA and GABA(A) receptors. These findings confirm that such tumors actively hijack presynaptic signals to gain a distinct survival advantage, setting them apart from non-neuronal subtypes.

Despite rapid progress in cancer neuroscience, several important gaps remain in defining how the nervous system regulates lung cancer. First, the spatiotemporal dynamics of tumor innervation and neural signaling across disease evolution remain incompletely characterized, including when neural inputs exert their greatest influence, how these signals change from premalignant lesions to established primary tumors and treatment-adapted states, and whether similar programs persist during metastatic outgrowth. Second, systematic understanding of subtype-specific and rare-variant neuro-regulatory features remains limited. At present, rarer lung cancer variants are poorly characterized in this regard, and histology- and molecular subtype-stratified comparisons with functional validation are needed to determine whether distinct subtypes depend on different neural branches, receptors, or microenvironmental wiring. Third, circuit and node specificity across neural branches remains unresolved. Although sympathetic adrenergic signaling has been studied most extensively, the relative contributions of sympathetic, sensory, and parasympathetic pathways are likely to differ by context, and actionable driver nodes suitable for selective targeting remain poorly defined. Fourth, organ-specific neuro-regulation of metastasis and pre-metastatic niche conditioning, particularly in the brain and bone, is still not fully understood; paired primary–metastatic analyses and niche-focused spatial profiling may help identify site-specific vulnerabilities. Fifth, emerging neuron–tumor synapse-like interactions raise important therapeutic questions regarding the essential adhesion molecules and receptor complexes involved, the heterogeneity and reversibility of these features across tumor states, and their druggability. Systematic mapping of synaptic components in patient samples, followed by targeted perturbation studies, will be necessary to establish causality and therapeutic feasibility. Finally, a central translational challenge is achieving selective intervention with acceptable safety—that is, suppressing tumor-associated neural signaling without disrupting systemic neural function. Prospective, mechanism-informed study designs that incorporate biomarker-guided stratification, receptor-selective agents, and context-dependent delivery strategies will be critical for identifying the patients and treatment settings most likely to benefit. Ultimately, advancing neuro-immune-oncology will require integrative strategies that align mechanistic insight with patient stratification and safety-conscious intervention design. As the field moves from descriptive associations toward causal, circuit-resolved models, combining ICB with rational neuro-modulatory approaches may offer a path to mitigate neurally induced immune suppression while limiting off-target systemic effects, thereby improving both survival and quality of life in patients with lung cancer.

Building on these gaps, future work should prioritize spatially and temporally resolved analyses of lung cancer innervation. Spatial transcriptomics and three-dimensional imaging, applied to stage-stratified and paired primary–metastatic specimens, may clarify the anatomical heterogeneity of nerve fibers and their colocalization with immune subsets and tumor clones. In parallel, the confirmation of synapse-like signaling in SCLC warrants systematic mapping of synaptic components in patient samples, validation of receptor and adhesion modules as actionable dependencies, and assessment of whether comparable electrochemical programs emerge in NSCLC under defined microenvironmental or treatment-adapted conditions.

From a translational perspective, advancing neuro-immune-oncology will likely require mechanism-informed and safety-aware strategies that incorporate biomarker-guided stratification, clearly defined exposure timing, and receptor-selective modulation. Within this framework, combining ICB with rational neuro-modulatory approaches—such as selective β-adrenergic blockade, targeted neuropeptide pathway inhibition, or context-appropriate neuromodulation—may help mitigate neurally induced immune suppression while minimizing off-target systemic effects.
