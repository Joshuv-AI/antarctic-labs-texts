A Tsallis-Entropy Lens on Genetic Variation
Margarita Geleta, Daniel Mas Montserrat, Alexander G. Ioannidis

Abstract
We introduce an information-theoretic generalization of the fixation statistic, the Tsallis-order 𝒒 \bm{q} F-statistic, 𝑭 𝒒 \bm{F_{q}} , which measures the fraction of Tsallis 𝒒 \bm{q} -entropy lost within subpopulations relative to the pooled population. The family nests the classical variance-based fixation index 𝑭 ST \bm{F_{\textbf{ST}}} at 𝒒 = 𝟐 \bm{q{=}2} and a Shannon-entropy analogue at 𝒒 = 𝟏 \bm{q{=}1} , whose absolute form equals the mutual information between alleles and population labels. By varying 𝒒 \bm{q} , 𝑭 𝒒 \bm{F_{q}} acts as a spectral differentiator that up-weights rare variants at low 𝒒 \bm{q} , while 𝒒 > 𝟏 \bm{q{>}1} increasingly emphasizes common variants, providing a more fine-grained view of differentiation than 𝑭 ST \bm{F_{\textbf{ST}}} when allele-frequency spectra are skewed. On real data (865 Oceanian genomes with 1,823,000 sites) and controlled genealogical simulations (seeded from 1,432 founders from HGDP and 1000 Genomes panels, with 322,216 sites), we show that 𝑭 𝒒 \bm{F_{q}} in One-vs-Rest (OVR) and Leave-One-Out (LOO) modes provides clear attribution of which subpopulations drive regional structure, and sensitively timestamps isolation-migration events and founder effects. 𝑭 𝒒 \bm{F_{q}} serves as finer-resolution complement for simulation audits and population-structure summaries.



I
Introduction

When characterizing populations, demographies, or bottlenecks, or when simulating genotypes (i.e., single-nucleotide polymorphism sequences or SNPs)
[
8
,
22
,
1
,
34
,
19
]
, it is important to understand genetic differentiation
[
11
,
20
]
. SNP sequences are predominantly biallelic with each genetic marker able to be coded in most cases as having two possible states, often denoted as
reference
and
alternate
alleles or
ancestral
and
derived
. Several metrics derived from population genetics are commonly used to quantify differentiation or population structure; yet the field still relies primarily on statistics tied to variance in allele frequencies
[
18
]
—most prominently Wright’s F-statistics family
[
33
,
30
,
18
]
, in particular the fixation index
F_{\text{ST}}
, and related variants such as Nei’s
G_{\text{ST}}

[
21
]
and Hedrick’s
G^{\prime}_{\text{ST}}

[
9
]
. An alternative is Jost’s
D

[
13
]
, which derives from allelic diversity and measures differentiation independent of within-population diversity. These metrics emphasize common variants and can lose sensitivity when allele-frequency spectra are skewed
[
12
,
4
]
—situations increasingly common in whole-genome sequencing datasets.

We unify variance- and information-centric views by defining a
Tsallis-order
q
F-statistic
,
F_{q}
, which measures the fraction of Tsallis
q
-entropy lost within subpopulations relative to the pooled population. The family recovers the classical heterozygosity-based
F_{\text{ST}}
at
q{=}2
and a Shannon analogue at
q{=}1
, whose absolute form equals mutual information between allele and population labels.
Our empirical analyses of
F_{q}
patterns in Polynesia, Micronesia, Melanesia, and Southeast Asia accord with genomic reconstructions of settlement, drift, and admixture in Oceania.

II
Background and related work

II-A

Variance-Based Divergence

The genetic diversity of a population is often measured by the expected heterozygosity,
H_{e}
, which is the probability that two randomly drawn alleles differ or, alternatively, it is twice the variance of a Bernoulli-coded allele for a biallelic locus with allele frequency
p
:

H_{e}=1-p^{2}-(1-p)^{2}=2p(1-p).

(1)

Wright’s fixation index
F_{\text{ST}}
partitions diversity into within- and total components and remains foundational in population genetics
[
33
]
. A common biallelic form under Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium within subpopulations compares the heterozygosity of the total population,
H_{T}
, to the mean heterozygosity across subpopulations,
H_{S}

[
30
,
21
]
:

F_{\text{ST}}=\frac{H_{T}-H_{S}}{H_{T}}=\frac{\mathrm{Var}_{w}(p)}{\bar{p}(1-\bar{p})}

(2)

quantifying the fraction of genetic diversity lost due to population subdivision, where
\mathrm{Var}_{w}(p)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}(p_{k}-\bar{p})^{2}
is the weighted variance across
K
subpopulations.
Values near
0
indicate identical allele frequencies across subpopulations, whereas values approaching
1
require extreme conditions, such as complete fixation of alternate alleles in populations with close to null within-population diversity.
Although intuitive,
F_{\text{ST}}
emphasizes common alleles (a second-order statistic) and is a
relative
measure reporting the proportion of variance explained rather than the absolute magnitude of allele frequency differences.

Jost introduced
D
as an
absolute
measure of allelic differentiation, derived from effective numbers of alleles, and designed to decouple differentiation from within-population heterozygosity
[
13
]
.
Although
D
also ranges from 0 to 1, intermediate values lack a direct coalescent or migration-based interpretation, unlike
\text{F}_{\text{ST}}
which can, under strong assumptions, be an indicator of gene flow
[
31
]
.

II-B

Information-Theoretic Diversity Measures

Shannon entropy
H
quantifies the expected uncertainty of allele identity; its exponent
e^{H}
is widely used as a diversity index in ecology
[
5
,
24
]
. For a biallelic locus
X
with allele probabilities
p
and
1-p
; we define:

H_{X\sim\text{Bern}(p)}(X)=-p\log p-(1-p)\log(1-p)

(3)

which quantifies how uncertain (or
“surprising”
) an allele draw is on average. Unlike heterozygosity, which is quadratic in allele frequency, Shannon entropy is a first-order statistic; it downweights extremely common alleles and remains sensitive when the minor-allele spectrum is skewed. In population genetics,
Shannon differentiation
(normalized mutual information) formalizes a relative information-based divergence between subpopulations and the pool, with expectations under the finite-island model
[
4
]
.

Tsallis
q
-entropy
generalizes Shannon while remaining concave for
\forall q>0
, enabling Jensen-type decompositions across the entire
q
-range
[
29
]
and has been previously explored as a diversity measure on transcriptomic datasets
[
7
]
.

III
Theory – a Tsallis
q
-Entropy F-Statistic

III-A

Tsallis
q
-Entropy (Biallelic) and Notation

Let
Y\in\{1,...,K\}
be the population label with
P(Y{=}k)=w_{k}
, where
w_{k}>0
and
\sum_{k}w_{k}=1
. Let
X_{\ell}\in\{0,1\}
denote the allele at locus
\ell
. Conditional on
Y=k
, we write
X_{\ell}|Y{=}k\sim\text{Bern}(p_{k\ell})
, so marginally
X_{\ell}\sim\text{Bern}(\bar{p}_{\ell})
. For a random variable
X
with a Bernoulli probability mass function with parameter
p
, the Tsallis entropy of order
q
is defined as:

S_{q}(X)=\frac{1-\big(p^{q}+(1-p)^{q}\big)}{q-1},\quad q>0,\ q\neq 1,

(4)

with
S_{1}(X)=H(X)
(Equation
3
) by using l’Hôpital rule at the limit
q\rightarrow 1
. Define per-locus totals:

\displaystyle S_{q}^{\text{total}}(\ell)

\displaystyle\coloneqq S_{q}(X_{\ell})=S_{q}(\text{Bern}(\bar{p}_{\ell}))

\displaystyle S_{q}^{\text{within}}(\ell)

\displaystyle\coloneqq S_{q}(X_{\ell}|Y)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}\,S_{q}(\text{Bern}(p_{k\ell}))

III-B

Absolute and Relative Differentiation

Define the
absolute

q
-differentiation (Jensen–Tsallis gap):

\Delta_{q}(\ell)\coloneqq S_{q}^{\text{total}}(\ell)-S_{q}^{\text{within}}(\ell)\geq 0,

(5)

and the
relative

q
-statistic per locus:

F_{q}(\ell)\coloneqq\frac{\Delta_{q}(\ell)}{S_{q}^{\text{total}}(\ell)}\quad\text{(defined when }S_{q}^{\text{total}}(\ell)>0\text{)}.

(6)

III-C

Properties

Non-Negativity and Bounds

For
\forall q>0,\Delta_{q}(\ell)\geq 0
and
0\leq F_{q}(\ell)\leq 1
.

Proof
:
S_{q}
is concave for
q{>}0
; by Jensen,
S_{q}(\text{Bern}(\bar{p}_{\ell}))\geq\sum_{k}w_{k}S_{q}(\text{Bern}(p_{k\ell}))
, hence
\Delta_{q}(\ell)\geq 0
. Since
\sum_{k}w_{k}S_{q}(\text{Bern}(p_{k\ell}))\geq 0
and
S_{q}(\text{Bern}(\bar{p}_{\ell}))\geq\Delta_{q}(\ell)
, the ratio lies between
0
and
1
.

Reductions to Canonical Measures (
q=2
and
q=1
)

For
q=2
,
S_{2}(\text{Bern}(p))=2p(1-p)
, which equals
H_{e}
(Equation
1
). A direct computation yields:

\Delta_{2}(\ell)=2\bar{p}(1-\bar{p})-2\sum_{k}w_{k}p_{k\ell}(1-p_{k\ell})=2\,\mathrm{Var}_{w}(p_{k\ell}),

(7)

Hence
F_{2}(\ell)=(H_{T}-H_{S})/H_{T}=F_{\text{ST}}
via Equation
2
.

For
q=1
, recall the marginal distribution
P(X_{\ell})=\bar{p}_{\ell}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}p_{k\ell}
. Then:

\displaystyle\Delta_{1}(\ell)

\displaystyle=H(X_{\ell})-\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}H(X_{\ell}|Y=k)

(8)

\displaystyle=H(X_{\ell})-H(X_{\ell}|Y)=I(X_{\ell};Y),

the mutual information between the allele at locus
\ell
and the population label (equivalently, the weighted Jensen–Shannon divergence).
Thus,
F_{1}(\ell)=I(X_{\ell};Y)/H(X_{\ell})
is
Shannon differentiation
.

III-D

One-vs-Rest
F_{q}
(OVR): Per-Population Separation Within a Region

For a focal population
c
inside a macro-region
\mathcal{R}
, we define two groups at each locus
\ell
: group
A
collects the set of haplotypes from
c
, and group
B
represents the set of haplotypes pooled from
\mathcal{R}\setminus\{c\}
. We use equal group weights
w_{A}=w_{B}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
so populations contribute as units rather than by sample size (we down-sample large populations per bootstrap replicate to stabilize variance). With per-group allele frequencies
p_{A\ell},p_{B\ell}
and pooled
\bar{p}_{\ell}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}(p_{A\ell}+p_{B\ell})
, define:

\displaystyle S_{q}^{\text{total}}(\ell)

\displaystyle=S_{q}(\text{Bern}(\bar{p}_{\ell}))

(9)

\displaystyle S_{q}^{\text{within}}(\ell)

\displaystyle=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}S_{q}(\text{Bern}(p_{A\ell}))+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}S_{q}(\text{Bern}(p_{B\ell}))

and absolute and relative differentiation follow Equations
5
and
6
. Genome-wide micro-averages are
F_{q}^{\text{OVR}}=\sum_{\ell}\Delta_{q}(\ell)/\sum_{\ell}S_{q}^{\text{total}}(\ell)
. Analyzing
F_{q}^{\text{OVR}}
as a function of
q
gives a sensitivity spectrum:
q\approx 1
up-weights rarer alleles (drift/founder signals), and
q\gg 1
emphasizes common alleles (
q=2
recovers heterozygosity-based separation).

III-E

Leave-One-Out influence
\Delta F_{q}
(LOO): a Population’s Contribution to Regional Structure

Let
K_{\mathcal{R}}=|\mathcal{R}|
be the number of populations in region
\mathcal{R}
. Using equal population weights
w_{k}=1/K_{\mathcal{R}}
, define the regional statistic:

F_{q}^{(\mathcal{R})}=\frac{\sum_{\ell}\left(S_{q}(\text{Bern}(\bar{p}_{\ell}))-\frac{1}{K_{\mathcal{R}}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{R}}S_{q}(\text{Bern}(p_{k\ell}))\right)}{\sum_{\ell}S_{q}(\text{Bern}(\bar{p}_{\ell}))}

(10)

Define the LOO influence of population
c\in\mathcal{R}
as:

\Delta F_{q}(c)\coloneqq F_{q}^{(\mathcal{R})}-F_{q}^{(\mathcal{R}\setminus\{c\})}

(11)

with interpretation such that
\Delta F_{q}(c)>0
means removing
c
reduces between-population differentiation (so
c
is a driver of structure), and
\Delta F_{q}(c)<0
means
c
homogenizes the region.

IV
Experiments and Discussion

IV-A

Entropy Analysis of Pacific Islanders’ Haplotypes

We analyze Oceanian and Southeast Asian groups partitioned into Polynesia, Micronesia, Melanesia, and Southeast Asia (Figure
1
.C), using 865 samples from the Ocenian dataset
[
23
]
, each with 1,823,000 biallelic SNPs. We split diploid genomes into a resulting dataset of 1,730 haplotypes. Because sample sizes vary by population, we report equal-weight estimates by bootstrapping haplotypes within each population (100 resamples, with a per-population cap of 40) and aggregating across loci. This avoids confounding
F_{q}^{\text{OVR}}
and
\Delta F_{q}^{\text{LOO}}
signals with sample size.

By construction, note that higher
q
up-weights common variants, while lower
q
up-weights rarer variants. Thus, it is interesting to observe the slope of
F_{q}^{\text{OVR}}
as it decreases (as
q
increases): a steeper drop from
q=1
to
q=2
signals recent drift or serial founder effects, whereas a flatter
F_{q}^{\text{OVR}}
profile points to older structure dominated by common variants. This is exactly the tension we want to visualize across islands with different peopling histories.

IV-A
1
Polynesia

The consensus peopling model for East Polynesia (Cook Islands, French Polynesia) features rapid, late settlement following a serial founder expansion from West Polynesia (Samoa, Tonga)
[
10
,
32
]
. Cook Islands show the largest positive
\Delta F_{q}^{\text{LOO}}
across
q
(Figure
1
.A), marking them as especially differentiating within the Polynesian set (i.e., genetically distinct) likely to continental admixture. French Polynesia follows a similar pattern with high
q
as Samoa and Tonga, they are differentiated at rare marker variance (
q=1
); this may be one of the effects of French Polynesia having experienced more founder effects
[
10
]
.

IV-A
2
Micronesia

Micronesia’s history is complex and heterogeneous, with five migratory streams of ancestry and variable Near-Oceania (Papuan-related) contributions
[
16
]
, including Melanesian and Polynesian.
In our panel, Guam, Kiribati, and Palau rank consistently high in OVR and show clearly positive LOO influence, whereas Nauru has the lowest, with a small negative
\Delta F_{q}^{\text{LOO}}
(Figure
1
.B), acting as a homogenizer in the regional mixture. This reinforces
[
15
]
that found Nauru to be genetically clustered together with other Micronesian populations, while Guam and Palau lie further since they have been found to have more East-Asian like ancestry
[
16
]
.

IV-A
3
Melanesia

Within Melanesia we observe (Figure
1
.D) elevated
F_{q}^{\text{OVR}}
for Near-Oceanian islands (Papua New Guinea and the Solomon Islands), while Remote Oceania islands (Fiji and New Caledonia) show to have the lowest OVR. This gradient mirrors the findings by
[
6
,
26
]
that found Vanuatu and other Remote Oceanian populations have an admixed background from gene flow mainly coming from admixed Near Oceanian (Papua New Guinea and Solomon Islands) individuals.

IV-A
4
Southeast Asian Islanders and Neighboring Coastal Populations

Finally, in the Southeast Asia region, Andaman Islands appear high on both
q{=}1
and
q{=}2
relative to the mainland Southeast Asian populations (Figure
1
.E), in line with well-documented isolation and strong drift in these small island populations
[
25
,
27
]
. In contrast, mainland groups such as Myanmar, Laos, and Vietnam behave as regional intermediates, and contribute less to between-population variance with lower LOO influence in our set.

IV-B

Tsallis-Entropy Analysis of Simulated Haplotypes Across Generations

We evaluate
F_{q}
on controlled simulations seeded with real African whole genomes. Founders were drawn from high-coverage HGDP
[
3
]
and 1000 Genomes
[
28
]
datasets; variants were selected across autosomes and filtered to 322,216 biallelic SNPs. To avoid cryptic relatedness, we remove pairs up to third-degree using KING kinship inference software
[
17
]
. The final founder panel contains 1,432 unrelated individuals. Sex-specific recombination is modeled with refined male and female genetic maps
[
2
]
to better match crossover landscapes observed in human meiosis. Founders are clustered into three broad African demes by genetic and ethnolinguistic affinity, namely:
West Africa
(
WA
),
East or Horn and Great Lakes
(
EA
), and
Central, Southern, and Northern Africa
(
CSN
).

IV-B
1
Simulation Design

We simulate 17 generations with monogamous pairings. Candidate mates are pruned by sex and kinship rules: cousins closer than second degree are disallowed (including
removed
variants, e.g., 1C1R), and among first cousins only cross-cousin unions are permitted
[
14
]
. Offspring counts follow
\text{Poisson}(\lambda=3)
. Between-deme mating is governed by a
panmixia
parameter
\rho\in[0,1]
:
\rho=0
corresponds to strict endogamy and
\rho=1
to strictly exogamous random pairing across demes.

IV-B
2
Baseline Drift (Piecewise Panmixia)

We initialize our panmixia policy as
(\rho_{\textsf{WA}},\rho_{\textsf{EA}},\rho_{\textsf{CSN}})=(0.3,0.5,0.1)
. At generation 8, we isolate
WA
, increase
EA
mixing, and relax
CSN
, yielding
(0.1,0.6,0.3)
. Observe in Figure
2
(Column A) that
F_{q}^{\text{OVR}}
decays fast early as unrelated founders begin interbreeding.
\Delta F_{q}^{\text{LOO}}

pinpoints which deme carries the structure at each time: prior to gen-8,
EA
shows elevated
\Delta F_{q}^{\text{LOO}}
despite being the most panmictic:
EA
’s higher
\rho
turns it into a source of migrants, so removing
EA
measurably lowers between-deme differentiation. After gen-8,
WA
’s
\Delta F_{q}
rises while
EA
’s flattens—consistent with
WA
becoming more endogamous (
\rho=0.1
) and thus more distinctive, whereas
EA
’s extra mixing (
\rho=0.6
) dilutes its contrast, but is restored at gen-12 due to the source-sink dynamics. Overall, under gradual drift with changing mating regimes, OVR tracks the level of
differentiation
, while LOO attributes
responsibility
for structure.

IV-B
3
Isolation-Reconnection Pulse

We define a policy where we start from moderate flow for all demes,
(\rho_{\textsf{WA}},\rho_{\textsf{EA}},\rho_{\textsf{CSN}})=(0.5,0.5,0.5)
. At generation 8, we enforce near-isolation with panmixia set to
(0.05,0.05,0.05)
. At generation 14, we flip to strong exogamy
(0.9,0.9,0.9)
. Observe in Figure 2 (Column B) that after the beginning of isolation at gen-8, both
F_{q}^{\text{OVR}}
and
\Delta F_{q}^{\text{LOO}}
rise together in each deme, reflecting classic drift under isolation. Immediately after gen-14, once
\rho{=}0.9
reconnects demes with intensive migration, both curves drop sharply across demes as the pool re-homogenizes. As a practical implication, for simulator audits or real time-series, the combination of OVR (level) and LOO (attribution) distinguishes which population is structurally distinct, when isolation begins, and when contact resumes.

V
Conclusion

Variance-based fixation metrics—epitomized by
F_{\text{ST}}
, remain foundational but are most sensitive to common variants and can underweight drift signals when the spectrum is skewed. For simulation realism, this implies that simulators must reproduce not just variance-based divergence, but also rare-variant behavior.
F_{q}
reframes fixation through entropy:
q{=}1
(Shannon entropy) emphasizes low-frequency variants, while
q{=}2
matches standard heterozygosity
F_{\text{ST}}
. For practice, we propose reporting OVR and LOO: viewing OVR curves accross
q
yields a slope diagnostic, where a steep drop from
q=1
to
q=2
flags recent drift or serial founder effects, and a flat profile implies older structure supported by common variants or ongoing gene flow. This complements, rather than replaces,
F_{\text{ST}}
and related measures.
