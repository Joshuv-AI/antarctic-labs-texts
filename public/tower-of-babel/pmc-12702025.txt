Maternal milk-derived lipids dictate neonatal macrophage phenotype and function
Authors: Lunhua Liu, Jiyeon Yang, Jiro Sakai, Ranadhir Dey, Isabelle Coppens, Hira L. Nakhasi, Mustafa Akkoyunlu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryUnique properties of neonatal macrophages play an important role in tissue regeneration and susceptibility to infections. We discovered that exposure to neonatal mouse serum is sufficient to convert adult mouse macrophages into the neonatal phenotype. Macrophages incubated with neonatal serum accumulated lipid droplets due to the high lipid content in neonatal serum. We hypothesized that maternal milk derived lipids may be responsible for the immunomodulatory properties of neonatal serum. Supporting this hypothesis, sera from cow milk-fed adult mice converted adult macrophages into a neonatal macrophage phenotype. These macrophage phenotypic changes increased adult mouse susceptibility to cutaneous Leishmania major challenge, with skin macrophages manifesting an M2-like phenotype and CD4+ T cells mounting diminished Th1 responses. These results highlight the importance of maternal milk-derived serum lipids in the emergence of M2-like phenotype of neonatal macrophages and may have therapeutic implications by formulating a diet with tailored lipid content depending on host’s immunobiological needs.

Introduction

Infectious diseases continue to be an important cause of mortality during early life.1,2 Unique features of early age adaptive and innate immunity not only render them susceptible to infections but also prevent them from mounting an adult-like protective immune response to vaccines.3,4,5,6,7 Cells of the neonatal innate immune system are broadly characterized as immunosuppressive or anti-inflammatory.5,6 For example, myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) are abundant during the first week of murine life and appear to control inflammatory responses in the lungs and intestines elicited by rapid microbial colonization after birth.8 MDSC-mediated inhibition of T cell proliferation is a critical component of inflammatory response suppression by MDSCs. Inhibition of T cell responses is also a common feature for neonatal dendritic cells (DCs) and macrophages.6,9,10 Limited expression of MHCII, CD80, and CD86 on DCs and macrophages contributes to blunted CD4+ T cell responses.10,11 In adults, pathogen recognition and inflammatory cytokine responses are crucial steps in controlling infections. Contrasting the adult macrophage response, several studies have shown that microbial stimuli-induced inflammatory cytokine secretion is weaker in neonatal macrophages.4,7,12 In addition, neonatal macrophages exhibit impaired phagocytosis function.13,14,15 While the immunosuppressive phenotype of neonatal macrophages renders them susceptible to infections, emerging data indicate that the same phenotype is needed for tissue regeneration in the rapidly growing host.16,17 Thus, depending on the biological context, neonatal macrophage phenotype can prevent tissue damage as a result of a subdued response against an encounter with an abundance of microbial stimuli and also serve the needs of rapidly growing newborns.

Although epigenetic factors are also recognized,7 the suppressive nature of neonatal leukocytes is mainly attributed to environmental cues. For example, CD71+ erythroid cells, which are more abundant in neonatal mice than in adult mice, suppress inflammatory cytokine production and co-stimulatory molecule expression by macrophages through the production of arginase.18 Adenosine, a nucleoside that is more abundant in neonatal circulation than in adults, has been shown to specifically suppress TNF-α production in human monocytic cells and mouse macrophages.19,20 The suppressive phenotype of early age plasma extends to the inhibition of neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation by neutrophils. Higher concentrations of peptides that are termed “neonatal NET-inhibitory factor related peptides” in neonatal serum are responsible for the inhibition of NET formation.21 Another example of a neonatal anti-inflammatory feature is the presence of elevated levels of serum S100A8/A9 alarmins in newborn serum, which pre-activate the MyD88 pathway and trigger hyporesponsiveness in human and mouse monocytes/macrophages upon subsequent exposure to microbial products.22,23

In this study, we investigated phenotypic and functional changes in macrophages mediated by neonatal mouse serum. We found that neonatal serum induced a gene signature resembling the immune suppressive, M2-like tumor associated macrophage (TAM) phenotype, and this switch was accompanied by the massive accumulation of lipid droplets (LDs) in the cytoplasm. LD formation was necessary for the induction of the phenotype because delipidation of neonatal serum or blocking LD formation in macrophages by ablating hypoxia-inducible lipid droplet-associated (HILPDA) expression abolished the neonatal serum-induced M2-phenotype switch. The LD-inducing property of neonatal serum is likely dependent on maternal milk diet because adult mouse serum recapitulated most of the modulatory properties of neonatal mouse serum after feeding with pellets prepared from cow milk. Finally, underscoring the immunosuppressive phenotype of cow milk diet (MilkD)-fed mouse macrophages, MilkD-fed adult C57BL/6 mice were more susceptible to intradermal Leishmania major challenge compared to chow diet (ChowD)-fed adult mice.

Results

In vivo environment determines neonatal macrophage phenotype

Confirming previous reports characterizing neonatal macrophages as anti-inflammatory and manifesting an M2-like phenotype,5,9,11,24 we found higher CD206 and IL-4Rα on freshly isolated mouse neonatal CD11b+ peritoneal macrophages than on their adult counterparts (Figure 1A). To further characterize the macrophages harvested from neonatal and adult mouse peritoneum, we conducted quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (qPCR) analysis to assess the expression of genes previously reported to be uniquely downregulated in neonatal macrophages.11 Accordingly, we found that neonatal macrophages express significantly lower levels of the genes associated with MHC class II assembly (Cd74, H2-Ab1, Ctss) (Figure 1B). Similarly, lower expression of Cxcl13 in neonates was consistent with published reports.11 Earlier studies reported that hypoxia associated Hif-1α pathway is constitutively active in human cord blood cells as well as in neonatal MDSC.25,26 In line with these reports, we found higher expression of the hypoxia associated genes Il1rn and Eno2 in mouse neonatal macrophages than in adult macrophages.27,28 Except for Il10, the expression of genes associated with M1 as well as M2 phenotypes was generally higher in neonatal mouse macrophages than in adult mouse macrophages (Figure 1B). We next conducted RNA-seq analysis on isolated mouse peritoneal macrophages and assessed differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the two age groups. Interrogation of the RNA-seq data in single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) for transcriptional signatures associated with macrophage phenotypes confirmed the enrichment of M2 related genes over the M1 related genes (Figure S1A). Since the M2 phenotype is divided into subsets depending on the stimuli or environmental cues,29 we also interrogated RNA-seq data for gene sets representing TAM as well as IgG immune complex (IgG IC) and IL-4 stimulated macrophages. The gene set for LPS stimulated macrophages served as a control for M1 phenotype. Interestingly, GSEA for neonatal macrophages scored highest for TAM phenotype, while the IgG immune complex and IL-4 stimulated macrophage phenotypes were as distant as the LPS stimulated macrophage phenotype (Figure 1C). In addition to the distinct gene expression profile, neonatal macrophages are known to be impaired in antigen processing and presenting cell (APC) function11 as well as in phagocytic activity.13,14 The GSEA of DEG confirmed the negative association of neonatal macrophages with APC and phagocytosis pathways (Figures 1D and 1E). We hypothesized that the age-specific in vivo environment may be dictating the differences in the phenotype of freshly isolated macrophages from neonates versus adults. To assess this hypothesis, we performed adoptive transfer experiments by exposing mouse neonatal and adult peritoneal macrophages to an adult and neonatal mouse peritoneal environment, respectively. We adoptively transferred 1 x 106 neonatal peritoneal macrophages (CD45.2) into congenic CD45.1 adult mice and 0.1 x 106 adult peritoneal macrophages (CD45.1) into congenic CD45.2 neonatal mice. As controls, adult mice received adult macrophages and neonatal mice received neonatal macrophages. After 3 days, we harvested the peritoneal macrophages from the recipient mice and measured the levels of CD206 and IL-4Rα by flow cytometry. CD206 levels of adult cells were significantly increased following their transfer into neonatal peritoneum compared to freshly isolated adult macrophages, as well as for the adult cells that were transferred into adult peritoneum (Figures 1F and 1G). There was a reciprocal decrease in CD206 levels on neonatal cells transferred into adult mice, but this decrease did not reach statistical significance. Like the decrease in CD206 expression, neonatal cells harvested from adult peritoneum also had decreased IL-4Rα expression, and this decrease was statistically significantly different compared to freshly isolated neonatal cells and neonatal cells transferred into neonatal peritoneum (Figures 1F and 1H). The expression of IL-4Rα on adult cells transferred into neonatal peritoneum was also numerically higher than the adult cells transferred into adult peritoneum, but this increase was not statistically different. Taken together, the adoptive transfer experiments suggested an age-specific microenvironment influencing macrophage phenotype.

Serum factors determine neonatal macrophage phenotype

The adoptive transfer experiments suggested that the microenvironment may influence the phenotype of peritoneal macrophages in an age-dependent manner. Neonatal serum is a plausible candidate for the in vivo factor modulating host response, including the macrophage phenotype, based on its unique composition as compared to adult serum. For example, adenosine is known to promote IL-6 production while suppressing TNF-α secretion from rat peritoneal macrophages,30 and high adenosine levels in infant plasma selectively inhibit TLR-mediated TNF-α production but stimulate IL-6 secretion.20 Indeed, as shown previously,20,31 when we cultured macrophages in autologous mouse serum containing media, neonatal macrophages produced higher IL-6, but lower TNF-α compared to adult macrophages in response to CpG stimulation (Figures S2A and S2B). Moreover, when cultured in neonatal mouse serum, adult macrophages secreted significantly higher levels of IL-6 in response to CpG stimulation compared to those incubated in fetal bovine serum (FBS) or adult serum (Figure S2C), confirming that neonatal serum increases IL-6 production from innate cells, a phenomenon conserved in humans and mice.20,31 Other molecules that are found at a uniquely higher concentration in the serum of neonates than adult serum, with metabolic and innate immune functions, include FABP4, GDF-15, and S100A8/A9.23,32,33 Among these molecules FABP4 directly binds to lipids and plays role in immunometabolic events involving lipids,34 while GDF-15 is induced in response to increased fatty acid levels and inflammation primarily to maintain tissue tolerance and integrity during tissue stress.35 S100A8/A9 also maintains tissue tolerance during inflammation through NF-κB consumption and blunting of innate signaling.23 Confirming previous reports, we found higher levels of these molecules in neonatal mouse serum compared to adult mouse serum (Figure S3). We were also motivated to measure milk fat globule epidermal growth factor 8 (MFG-E8) in neonatal serum by the fact that MFG-E8 is rich in milk and presents with anti-inflammatory functions in neonates.36 Indeed, as expected, MFG-E8 levels were higher in neonatal mouse serum than adult mouse serum (Figure S3).

To further investigate the immunomodulatory effect of neonatal serum on macrophages and expand on the findings from serum transfer experiments, we utilized an in vitro mouse serum culture assay, in which adult peritoneal macrophages were cultured in media containing 20% serum from either adult or neonatal mice. To capture the overall influence of neonatal serum on steady state macrophage phenotype, we assessed global transcriptional regulation in cultured macrophages using RNA-seq analysis. We initially analyzed the expanded list of genes from the ex vivo analyzed macrophages shown in Figure 1B. The switch in the profile of genes expressed in adult macrophages following incubation in neonatal serum mostly resembled that of ex vivo analyzed neonatal macrophages. For example, hypoxia related genes Eno2 and Il1rn were both increased in neonatal serum-treated cells compared to adult serum-treated cells (Figure 2A). As detected in ex vivo examined neonatal macrophages, all the M1 phenotype associated genes were higher in neonatal mouse serum-treated adult cells. The hallmark M2 phenotype associated genes Arg1 and Arg2 were both increased after exposure to neonatal serum. Similar to the expression profile of ex vivo neonatal macrophages, neonatal serum-treated adult cells expressed reduced levels of the antigen presentation related genes Cd74, H2-Ab1, Ctss, as well as the chemokines Cxcl13 and Ccl22. However, the changes in the expression of the select list of genes did not immediately point to an M1 or M2 polarization of neonatal serum-treated macrophages. To further assess the phenotype of neonatal serum-treated macrophages, we subjected the RNA-seq data to GSEA with transcriptional signatures of mouse M1/M2 macrophages and found that the M1-macrophage transcriptional signature was suppressed while the M2-macrophage transcriptional signature was upregulated in neonatal serum-cultured macrophages (Figure 2B). Further analysis of DEG in single-sample GSEA with transcriptional signatures indicated that, as with freshly isolated neonatal macrophages, neonatal serum-cultured macrophages showed positive enrichment for the expression of TAM-associated genes, but not the genes for IgG IC and IL-4 stimulated macrophages (Figure 2C). GSEA data also indicated that neonatal serum was able to suppress the genes associated with APC function (Figure 2D). Like the freshly isolated neonatal macrophages (Figure S1), impaired phagocytosis is a characteristic of TAMs.37 Supporting a suppressed phagocytic capacity, RNA-seq data showed that neonatal serum-treated macrophages expressed lower levels of Mertk, Stab1, Timd4, Cd163, Fcgr1, Fcgr3, Scarb1, Marco, and Mfge8, all of which promote phagocytosis38,39 (Figure S4A). Confirming the lower expression of phagocytosis-related genes detected in RNA-seq analysis, GSEA revealed that genes enriched for phagocytosis were negatively regulated in neonatal serum-treated adult macrophages (Figure 2E). To examine whether neonatal mouse serum alters phagocytic capacity of macrophages, we incubated neonatal or adult serum-treated macrophages with fluorescently labeled microbeads to monitor microbead engulfment. Both microscopy (Figure S4B) and flow cytometry (Figure 2F) experiments demonstrated decreased phagocytosis of microbeads by adult macrophages following incubation with neonatal serum. Neonatal serum-treated macrophages switch to glycolysis

Unlike other M2-macrophage subsets that utilize fatty acid (FA) oxidation and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) as the main metabolic pathway, TAMs are known to use glycolysis to generate energy.40 Since the phenotypic changes in neonatal serum-treated adult macrophages mostly overlap with those of TAMs, we interrogated the RNA-seq data to determine if the phenotypic changes are also accompanied by a switch in metabolic pathways. Analysis of biological processes in neonatal mouse serum-cultured macrophages using the gene ontology (GO) annotation with GSEA suggested that neonatal serum treatment resulted in the preferential utilization of glycolysis and amino acid pathways over FA oxidation in macrophages because “Glucose metabolic process,” “Insulin receptor signaling,” and “Amino acid biosynthesis process” pathways were enriched while “FA β-oxidation” and “FA catabolic process” pathways scored low (Figure 3A). Based on the GSEA analysis results, we interrogated the RNA-seq data from neonatal and adult serum-treated macrophages for genes that are involved in glucose and lipid metabolism and found that most genes expressing glycolysis-associated proteins were expressed at higher levels in neonatal serum-treated macrophages than in adult serum treated cells (Figure 3B). Conversely, genes involved in FA synthesis, cholesterol synthesis, and β-oxidation were all lower in neonatal serum-exposed macrophages (Figure 3C). To assess glycolysis in neonatal and adult serum-treated macrophages, we measured glucose and lactate levels in culture supernatant during 48 h of incubation. We found elevated lactate production (Figure 3D) and increased glucose consumption (Figure 3E) in neonatal serum-treated macrophages, compared to adult serum-treated cells after 16 h of incubation. Lipids in neonatal serum dictate serum mediated modulation of macrophage phenotype

The suppression of genes involved in energy from FAs and β-oxidation was intriguing because the expression of genes that play a role in FA uptake, as well as the genes that participate in LD biogenesis, was increased in neonatal serum-treated macrophages compared to cells incubated with adult serum (Figure 4A). This suggested that after neonatal serum treatment, macrophages are actively transporting exogenously acquired FAs into the cytoplasm, and despite the increased uptake, FAs are likely stored as triacyl-glycerides (TAG) instead of being broken down to generate acetyl-CoA for the TCA cycle. Indeed, in addition to the robust phenotypic changes akin to TAMs and the switch to glycolysis, microscopy observations illustrated that both adult and neonatal macrophages incubated in neonatal serum containing media underwent morphological changes characterized by the appearance of large cytoplasmic vacuoles (Figure 4B), and this vacuolization phenotype increased proportionally to the percentage of neonatal serum added to the media (Figure S5). In contrast, macrophages incubated in adult serum or 10% FBS did not manifest the vacuolization phenotype (Figures 4B and S5). Staining of neonatal serum-incubated adult or neonatal macrophages with the lipid dye Oil Red O revealed the presence of supranumerous LDs, which were absent in adult serum-incubated adult macrophages and were minimal in adult serum-incubated neonatal macrophages, which was likely due to the presence of residual LDs in macrophages harvested from the neonatal environment (Figure 4C). Indeed, freshly isolated neonatal macrophages also contained LDs while adult macrophages did not (Figure 4D). We conducted electron microscopy (EM) studies to examine the ultrastructure of adult macrophages exposed to either adult or neonatal serum for 20 h, with special focus on LDs. Lipid droplets viewed by EM appeared as round structures with a homogenous electron-density (i.e., uniformly gray in the interior), without distinct internal structures and close to the endoplasmic reticulum tubules from where they bud (Figure 4E). While macrophages incubated in adult serum contained sparse LDs in the cytoplasm, macrophages exposed to neonatal serum showed abundant and enlarged LDs (up to 2.2 μm in diameter) (Figure 4E, compare cells in panels a and b at the same magnification), confirming fluorescence observations with Oil red staining (Figure 4D). These LDs were usually observed in large clusters and closely apposed with mitochondria and endoplasmic reticulum (Figure 4E, panel c), as expected for the normal life cycle of LDs and their diverse functions, such as lipid exchange. Elevated levels of lipids and lipid uptake molecules in neonatal serum

To test whether the detection of LDs in freshly isolated neonatal peritoneal macrophages and the overaccumulation of LDs in adult macrophages following their incubation in neonatal serum could be induced by increased lipid content in neonatal serum, we measured the levels of free fatty acids (FFAs), TAGs, total cholesterol, free cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) and low-density lipoprotein (LDL)/very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) in neonatal and adult serum. We found significantly higher concentrations of all the tested lipids and cholesterol-transporting lipoproteins in neonatal serum than in adult serum (Figure 5A). Removal of lipids from neonatal serum blunts the modulatory effect of serum on macrophages

To test whether the uptake of lipids from neonatal serum and the formation of LDs is mainly responsible for the unique phenotype observed in macrophages exposed to neonatal serum, we prepared delipidated neonatal serum using solvents (as described by Cham and Knowles42) for incubation with macrophages as compared to untreated serum. Confirming the ELISA measurements of lipid species (Figure 5A), the abundance of lipids in neonatal serum compared to adult serum was visually apparent as a white fraction after solvent treatment and centrifugation (Figure S7A). Removal of lipids was verified by measuring the FFA and TAG content before and after delipidation, showing complete absence of FFA and TAG in delipidated serum (Figure S7B). In support of the elevated lipid content in neonatal serum as the likely origin of the LDs in macrophages, delipidation of neonatal serum resulted in the abrogation of LD biogenesis in incubated macrophages (Figure 5B). Macrophages incubated in delipidated neonatal serum also manifested differences in the expression of genes compared to those incubated in unmanipulated neonatal serum. Generally, there was an increase in the expression of genes associated with the M1 phenotype and a decrease in the genes associated with the M2 phenotype (Figure 5C). Other notable changes were the decrease in genes associated with TAG synthesis, hypoxia, and glycolysis as well as an increase in FA synthesis. In addition to the decrease in M2-phenotype related genes, we found significantly lower percentages of CD206 and IL-4Rα positive cells in macrophages incubated with delipidated serum compared to those incubated in unmanipulated neonatal serum (Figure 5D). Finally, after incubation in delipidated neonatal serum, adult macrophages were able to phagocytose labeled beads as efficiently as the macrophages incubated in adult serum (Figure 5E). Thus, neonatal serum delipidation experiments demonstrated that the uptake of neonatal serum derived lipids by macrophages is responsible for the suppressive phenotype of neonatal serum.

Neonatal serum-mediated increase in hypoxia-inducible lipid droplet-associated expression dictates lipid droplet formation in macrophages

Environments with excess lipids, such as solid tumors and atherosclerotic plaques, trigger LD formation by upregulating HILPDA, which in turn inhibits TAG hydrolysis by suppressing adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL).43,44 Since the lipid content in neonatal serum appears to govern the formation of LDs in macrophages exposed to neonatal serum, we interrogated the RNA-seq data from neonatal and adult serum-treated macrophages. We found that the Hilpda gene was expressed significantly higher in neonatal serum-treated macrophages than in adult serum-treated macrophages (Figure 4A). Moreover, the increase in Hilpda was accompanied by a decrease in the ATGL gene (Pnpla2) in neonatal serum exposed macrophages. Analysis of macrophage lysates in Western blot assay 48 h after treatment with adult or neonatal serum confirmed the increase in HILPDA and the decrease in ATGL levels following exposure to neonatal serum (Figure 6A and S8A). Next, we sought to test whether silencing HILPDA or ATGL in macrophages would alter the LD formation induced by neonatal serum. We expected HILPDA inhibition to suppress and ATGL inhibition to promote LD formation.44 Evaluation of protein levels following the transfection of macrophages with small interfering (siRNA) for Hilpda or Pnpla2 (ATGL) indicated that the siRNAs efficiently ablated ATGL and HILPDA protein expression (Figure 6B and S8B). When the siRNA-silenced macrophages were incubated with neonatal serum, the BODIPY staining was reduced in HILPDA siRNA-transfected cells, whereas it was brighter in ATGL siRNA-transfected cells. Quantification of BODIPY mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) confirmed the decrease and increase in LD formation with silencing of HILPDA and ATGL, respectively (Figure 6C). Ablation of LD formation in Hilpda siRNA-treated cells was also accompanied by changes in gene expression. As seen in delipidated serum-treated macrophages, inhibition of HILPDA led to an increase in genes associated with the M1 phenotype, and a decrease in genes associated with glycolysis and TAG synthesis (Figure 6D). Other notable changes in Hilpda siRNA-transfected macrophages were the increase in genes related to antigen presentation, hypoxia, and cholesterol synthesis, and the decrease in genes involved in FA transport, glycolysis, and TAG synthesis. The phenotypic changes in Hilpda siRNA-transfected macrophages were also accompanied by functional changes. The inhibition of HILPDA improved the phagocytosis of microbeads by macrophages compared to mock and control siRNA-transfected cells when incubated in neonatal serum Figure S8 (Figure S8C). Sera of adult mice fed with milk diet manifest neonatal mouse sera-like macrophage modulatory properties

Our findings on serum-treated macrophages so far demonstrated that macrophages incubated in serum from 5- to 7-day-old (neonatal) mice accumulated LDs while serum from 6- to 14-week-old (adult) mice did not. We next assessed at which age neonatal mice lost the LD-inducing effect in macrophages by incubating adult macrophages with sera collected from mice aged 5 to 7 days, 2 weeks, and 3 weeks. We found that macrophages incubated with sera from 2-week-old mice had reduced LDs compared to macrophages incubated with sera from 5- to 7-day-old mice, and macrophages treated with sera from 3-week-old mice contained LDs almost as scarce as the macrophages incubated with adult mice sera (Figure S9A). In concert with the LD-inducing profile of sera from different ages of neonatal mice, we also measured gradually decreasing concentrations of FFA, TAG, total cholesterol, and LDL/VLDL after one week of age (Figure S9B). HDL levels in serum from 3-week-old mice were also significantly lower than in the serum of 1-week-old mice, but there was a significant increase in serum from 2-week-old mice compared to 1-week-old mice. Following the same pattern as serum lipid levels, the levels of serum proteins FABP4, GDF-15, S100A8/A9, and MFG-E8 gradually decreased in sera of 2- and 3-week-old mice as compared to sera of 1-week-old mice (Figure S9C). Coinciding with the changes in serum lipid and protein concentration after the first week of life, as well as the age-dependent pattern of LD-inducing property of neonatal serum, suckling neonatal mice gradually begin to eat their dam’s food pellets until they are weaned at week 3. This pattern prompted us to hypothesize that the LD-inducing property of neonatal/infant mouse sera may be proportional to the ratio of maternal milk in their total daily diet. To test this hypothesis, we prepared pellets from cow milk as described previously45 and fed 6-week-old adult mice with milk pellets for 2 weeks. We first measured FFA, TAG, total and free cholesterol, HDL, and LDL/VLDL levels in the sera of mice from ChowD and MilkD-fed mice. We found that the levels of all these lipids were significantly higher in MilkD-fed mouse sera (Figure 7A). Next, we assessed the lipid accumulation after the incubation of macrophages in ChowD-fed and MilkD-fed mouse sera by microscopy and found that macrophages incubated with MilkD-fed mouse sera contained LDs after 24-h of incubation, while those exposed to ChowD-fed mouse sera did not (Figure 7B). Confirming microscopy imaging, MFI of MilkD-fed mice sera treated macrophages was significantly higher than the ChowD-fed mouse sera-treated cells (Figure 7B). In neonatal serum, elevated lipid levels were accompanied by higher levels of lipid transfer molecules FABP4, GDF-15, and MFG-E8, as well as the alarmins S100A8/A9. We found statistically significantly higher GDF-15, MFG-E8 (Figure 7C), and S100A8/A9 (Figure S10) levels in MilkD-fed mouse serum compared to ChowD-fed mouse serum, while the level of FABP4 remained comparable in the sera of MilkD-fed and ChowD-fed mice (Figure 7C). Moreover, unlike the neonatal serum-exposed macrophages, levels of M2-macrophage markers CD206 and IL-4Rα were not higher in MilkD-fed mouse sera-incubated macrophages than ChowD-fed mouse sera-incubated macrophages (Figure 7D). In fact, IL-4Rα levels were statistically significantly lower after incubation in MilkD-fed mouse sera compared to those treated with ChowD-fed mouse sera. Nevertheless, despite the blunted expression of CD206 and IL-4Rα, a heatmap of select genes from qPCR data indicated that the expression of genes associated with the M2-phenotype was slightly higher in macrophages incubated in MilkD-fed mouse sera compared to those incubated in ChowD-fed mouse sera (Figure 7E). Importantly, as seen in neonatal sera-treated macrophages, further interrogation of RNA-seq data for DEGs in the GSEA indicated enrichment of genes for the M2 and TAM phenotypes in MilkD-fed mouse sera-exposed macrophages (Figure 7F). The heatmap of select genes indicated additional similarities between MilkD-fed adult and neonatal mouse sera-treated macrophages. For example, as with neonatal sera-treated macrophages, genes associated with hypoxia and glycolysis were increased, and antigen presentation was decreased in macrophages incubated in MilkD-fed mouse sera compared to ChowD-fed mouse sera (Figure 7E). GSEA also revealed further similarities between neonatal mouse sera and MilkD-fed adult mouse sera. Like the neonatal mouse sera-exposed macrophages, we found the suppressed expression of genes associated with phagocytosis and APC function (Figure 7F). Finally, in support of the suppressed phagocytosis-related genes, macrophages incubated with MilkD-fed mouse sera were not as efficient as macrophages exposed to ChowD-fed mouse sera in phagocytosing labeled beads (Figure 7G). Milk diet renders adult C57BL/6 mice susceptible to cutaneous Leishmania major infection

The recapitulation of phenotypic and functional properties of neonatal sera-treated macrophages exposed to sera from MilkD-fed adult mice prompted us to test the consequences of the switch in macrophage phenotype in MilkD-fed mice in an in vivo experimental infection model. We chose the cutaneous Leishmania major infection model in C57BL/6 mice because M1-polarized macrophages are essential for the resistance of C57BL/6 mice against L. major infection, whereas a switch to the M2-polarized macrophage phenotype is sufficient to render the mice susceptible to L. major-mediated pathology.2,46 After feeding adult mice with ChowD or MilkD for two weeks, mice were infected by intradermal (i.d.) inoculation of mCherry-expressing L. major parasites in the ear.2 Ear pathology and parasite burden were assessed over a 5-week period. Disease severity was determined by analysis of lesion sizes. Most of the ear pathology resolved in ChowD-fed mice by week 5. Compared to ChowD-fed mice, MilkD-fed mice manifested more severe gross pathological changes in their infected ear (Figure 8A). Consistent with the severity of the disease, 5 weeks after infection, there were significantly more infected ear macrophages harvested from the ear lesions of MilkD-fed mice compared to ChowD-fed mice (Figure 8B). Moreover, infected MilkD-fed mice macrophages harbored more parasites than those of ChowD-fed mice (Figure 8B). To determine whether a shift in macrophage phenotype accompanied the pronounced gross pathology and increased parasite load in MilkD-fed mice, we assessed M1-and M2-markers in macrophages isolated from the infected ear. Of the two co-stimulatory molecules, CD86 and CD80, that are known to represent the M1 phenotype and play a role in activating CD4+ T cells,47 the percentage and MFI of CD86 were significantly less in the ear macrophages from MilkD-fed mice compared to those harvested from ChowD-fed mice, whereas CD80 expression was comparable between the groups (Figures 8C and S11A). Conversely, the frequency of the M2-marker CD206 was significantly elevated in ear macrophages of MilkD-fed mice than those of ChowD-fed mice (Figure 8D). Discussion

Although neonatal macrophages are known to be anti-inflammatory and sometimes referred to as manifesting an M2-like phenotype,11 characterization of their exact biological attributes remains incomplete. Our study demonstrates that neonatal mouse peritoneal macrophages exhibit an M2 phenotype that is closest to that of TAMs, a phenotype considered to be a subtype of M2-macrophages.49,50 Notably, the treatment of adult macrophages with neonatal serum was sufficient to convert the phenotype of adult macrophages toward the neonatal macrophage phenotype. This shift was accompanied by the stimulation of LD biogenesis in macrophages, and the LDs induced by neonatal serum dictated the phenotype and function of neonatal macrophages. This conclusion stemmed from serum delipidation experiments and Hilpda inhibition experiments, both of which led to the prevention of LD formation as well as blunting the serum induced phenotypic and functional changes in macrophages. We also showed that maternal milk is likely responsible for the LD-inducing property of neonatal serum because sera of adult mice fed with MilkD also induced LD formation, accompanied by an M2-like phenotype in macrophages that resembled the neonatal serum-induced phenotype.

We began to investigate in vivo factors governing the phenotype of freshly isolated neonatal macrophages by adoptively transferring adult macrophages into neonatal peritoneum and neonatal macrophages into adult peritoneum. Analysis of changes in CD206 and IL-4Rα expression on donor macrophages after the transfer indicated that the adoptively transferred adult macrophages converted to an M2 phenotype after exposure to a neonatal peritoneal environment. These results prompted us to assess the influence of serum on dictating the neonatal macrophage phenotype as an in vivo environmental factor because neonatal serum is shown to contain elevated concentrations of immunomodulatory molecules such as S1008A/A9, lactoferrin, prostaglandin E2, and adenosine.23,30,51,52 In vitro incubation of adult macrophages with neonatal serum induced transcriptional changes that were consistent with neonatal macrophages and M2-like phenotype. Impaired phagocytosis and APC function are phenotypic features of both neonatal macrophages11,13,14 and TAMs.37,53 Indeed, our DEG analysis suggested a decrease in both phagocytosis and APC pathways in freshly isolated macrophages as well as in neonatal serum treated adult macrophages, and the phagocytosis assay confirmed the impaired phagocytosis in both groups.

By microscopy, we observed that both freshly isolated neonatal macrophages and the neonatal serum treated adult macrophages contained large cytoplasmic vacuoles, which were also reported by Winterberg and colleagues.11 However, unlike Winterberg and colleagues, who indicated that the vacuoles did not contain lipids, we were able to show that the vacuolized profiles were bona fide LDs based on electron microscopy images and fluorescence staining using various LD dyes (BODIPY, Oil Red O, and Nile Red). Notably, TAMs are also reported to contain LDs, and the immunosuppressive phenotype of TAMs is associated with LD formation.54,55 Another similarity between neonatal serum treated macrophages and TAMs is the switch from the TCA cycle to glycolysis as an energy source.40 Accompanying the switch to glycolysis in neonatal serum-treated macrophages, DEG analysis of RNA-seq data suggested a decrease in FA catabolism and β-oxidation. Interestingly, in support of a lack of de novo lipid synthesis, we found strong suppression of genes involved in FA and cholesterol synthesis. These data suggested that LD formation may be guided by substantial uptake of lipids from neonatal sera. Furthermore, the levels of FFA, TAG, total and free cholesterol, HDL, and LDL/VLDL, as well as molecules involved in lipid uptake, such as FABP4 and MFG-E834,41 were higher in neonatal serum than in adult serum. We employed two approaches to prevent the formation of LDs to assess the significance of LD formation on the emergence of the neonatal macrophage phenotype in neonatal serum-treated adult macrophages. In the first approach, we probed the effect of delipidated neonatal serum on adult macrophages and found that LD formation was abolished when neonatal serum lipids were removed. In the second approach, we examined the consequences of Hilpda and Pnpla2 (ATGL) silencing in macrophages. Indeed, we found diminished BODIPY staining in macrophages transfected with Hilpda siRNA after neonatal serum incubation. Conversely, transfection with Pnpla2 (ATGL) siRNA led to increased BODIPY staining. Although there were differences in the expression of some genes between delipidated serum treated and Hilpda siRNA transfected macrophages, in both groups, there was a pattern of inhibition of genes associated with the M2-phenotype and an increase in genes associated with the M1-phenotype. Other pathways that were suppressed in both groups were glycolysis and TAG synthesis. Notably, the switch from M2-to M1-polarization was accompanied by an improvement in phagocytic capacity in both groups. Thus, the LD inhibition experiments underscored the role of LD formation in steering the suppressive phenotype of macrophages following exposure to neonatal sera.

Suckling neonates start to consume solid food from dams’ ChowD at around day 12 before weaning at 3 weeks of age. Coinciding with this activity, we found that sera from 2-week-old-mice induced fewer LDs in macrophages compared to 1-week-old mice, and the LD-inducing capacity of sera from 3-week-old mice was indistinguishable from that of adult mice. Similarly, serum levels of lipids as well as lipid transfer molecules FABP4, GDF-15, and MFG-E8 decreased as neonates aged. Based on this pattern, we hypothesized that maternal MilkD could be responsible for the LD-inducing property of 1-week-old mice. We tested this hypothesis by feeding adult mice with cow milk pellets for two weeks and examined the immunomodulatory properties of their sera on macrophages. MilkD-fed adult mouse sera manifested most of the properties of neonatal mouse sera. For example, such as neonatal serum, MilkD-fed mouse serum contained higher lipids and lipoproteins as well as increased concentrations of MFG-E8, GDF-15, and S100A8/A9 compared to ChowD-fed mice sera. Importantly, just like neonatal mouse serum, MilkD-fed mouse serum induced LD formation in macrophages associated with higher expression of genes associated with glycolysis. Moreover, DEG analysis of MilkD-fed mouse serum revealed that macrophages adopted the neonatal phenotype with the diminished expression of phagocytosis and APC related genes, and macrophages manifested impaired phagocytic ability after incubation with MilkD-fed mouse serum.

Cutaneous L. major infection is an appropriate model to test the function of MilkD-induced conversion to neonatal macrophage phenotype, because whereas M1-polarization of macrophages and a Th1 response are protective in L. major-challenged C57BL/6 mice, mutant C57BL/6 mice with a default M2-macrophage response cannot mount a Th1 response and suffer from severe infection.2 Indeed, not only did the challenged ears of MilkD-fed mice harbor more L. major parasites but also presented with more severe pathology compared to infected ChowD-fed mice. As anticipated, increased susceptibility of the resistant C57BL/6 strain to L. major infection was accompanied by the inability of ear macrophages to mount an M1-polarized response. Macrophages isolated from the infected ears of MilkD-fed mice expressed higher CD206 (an M2-marker) and lower CD86 (an M1-marker) compared to those of ChowD-fed mice. There was also a trend in the lower expression of M1-phenotype related genes and higher expression of M2-related genes in ear macrophages from infected MilkD-fed mice. Also, glycolysis-related genes were expressed at higher levels in MilkD-fed mice compared to ChowD-fed mice. As expected, resistance of ChowD-fed mice to Leishmaniasis was accompanied by an IFN-γ-producing Th1-polarized CD4+ response, while CD4+ cells from MilkD-fed mice were unable to produce IFN-γ even after in vitro stimulation with Leishmania antigens.

In addition to containing essential nutrients, milk supports the neonatal immune system by transferring maternally derived immunoglobulins, complement factors, cytokines, and immune cells.56,57 The discovery of the importance of the intestinal microbiota in modulating the immune system led to the identification of bacteria such as Bifidobacteria that colonize the neonatal gut with the help of milk-derived oligosaccharides and support the neonatal immune system.58,59 Additionally, the alarmins S100A8/A9 in maternal milk are shown to increase neonatal serum alarmin levels directly following ingestion.23 Through a second mechanism, milk increases neonatal serum alarmins indirectly via lactoferrin, which stimulates the production of alarmins and prostaglandin E2 from MDSC.8 Our findings expand the immunomodulatory properties of maternal milk on the neonatal immune system and show that neonatal macrophages form LDs by acquiring milk-derived lipids from serum. Macrophages with LDs manifest an immunosuppressive phenotype that is closest to the phenotype of TAMs among M2 macrophage subsets. Both the neonatal serum delipidation and the MilkD experiments complement each other to further support the role of milk in enriching the serum lipid content, which drives the formation of LDs in macrophages. In addition to the in vitro phagocytosis experiments, L. major challenge experiments underscore the functional consequences of the immunosuppressive neonatal macrophage phenotype. Taken together, our current study shows that the well-recognized suppressive phenotype of neonatal macrophages is governed by the ingestion of lipids in maternal milk by suckling neonatal mice. These results also show that maternal milk, as the sole source of nutrition in newborns, appears to govern the function of macrophages in maintaining a delicate balance between preventing microbial environment initiated inflammatory damage to the host and the developmental requirements of the postnatal period, where neonatal macrophages are essential in tissue regeneration.16,17

How our mouse data translate into human milk mediated modulation of infant monocytes and macrophages remains to be explored. Although all mammalian infants rely on maternal milk and all mammalian milk is rich in lipids, results from our study may not be directly applicable to human infants due to the shorter suckling period in mouse pups and differences in lipid composition between mouse and human milk.60 In a recent study, Tan and colleagues reported that mothers’ own milk is more beneficial compared to donor milk in the NK cell development of preterm infants.61 A well-known benefit of maternal milk lipids on infant health is the use of FA as an energy source.62 However, the contribution of milk derived lipids to infants’ immune cell development is not reported. Nevertheless, studies suggested an immunomodulatory effect of maternally derived FA on infant innate and adaptive immune system based on correlations between serum FA content and immune mediated maladies such as allergic diseases.63 Future studies with human cells will help unveil whether maternal milk derived lipids play a role in the phenotype and function of monocytes and macrophages in infants. These studies can also be extended to assess whether milk lipids modulate the responses of other innate cells, such as dendritic cells, as well as lymphocytes. Fatty acid composition and content can change between mother’s milk, donor milk, and baby formula.62 Beneficial effects of mothers’ own milk over donor milk and baby formula on infants’ immune cell development are well recognized.62 Identification of lipid species in mothers’ own milk responsible for the phenotype and function of infant monocytes and macrophages may help in the informed design of infant formula lipid content. These lipid species can also be used as part of the diet in maladies where M2-like macrophages are needed for the control of inflammation and tissue repair.

Limitations of the study

This study provides insight into how maternal milk-derived lipids shape neonatal macrophage phenotype and function. The conclusions of our study derive from in vitro, ex vivo, and in vivo experiments using neonatal and adult mouse macrophages. Although all newborn mammals, including mouse and human neonates, rely on maternal milk for nutritional needs, the truncated neonatal period and other physiological attributes specific to mice prevent immediate translation of our findings to human neonates. Future studies using human macrophages/monocytes will help determine whether maternally derived milk lipids dictate neonatal macrophage-phenotype and function in humans, also. Our study clearly shows that elevated serum total lipid content, together with increased lipid uptake molecules, plays a critical role in inducing LD accumulation and phenotypic changes in neonatal macrophages. However, the specific lipid responsible for the macrophage phenotypic conversion remains unknown. Future studies incorporating lipid profile assessment using lipidomics and removal of candidate lipid species from neonatal serum, as well as the supplementation of the sera of chow-diet-fed adult mice with the candidate lipid species, would address this limitation.

Experimental model and study participant details

Mice and preparation of serum

C57BL/6J, CD45.2+ C57BL/6 mice and the CD45.1+ B6.SJL-Ptprca Pep3b/BoyJ were purchased from Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, Maine). All mice were maintained in pathogen-free animal facilities at FDA in accordance with FDA Center for Veterinary Medicine guidelines. Male or female neonatal (5- to 7-day-old) and female adult (6- to 14-week-old) mice were used in all experiments, including for the preparation of age-specific sera. Both male and female neonatal mice were used in neonatal mice experiments because physiologically they are not influenced by sex hormones during the first week of life. Serum was prepared from blood collected by cardiac puncture after induction of deep anesthesia using isoflurane. All animal procedures were conducted according to protocols #2002-31 and #2017-48, which were approved by FDA’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

Method details

MilkD feeding of adult mice

Six-week-old C57BL/6J female mice were fed with either regular chow diet (ChowD) or milk diet (MilkD) consisting of milk pellets. To prepare milk pellets, 5 g of whole milk powder (MP Biomedicals, Irvine, CA) was added to 0.5 mL of distilled water (Fisher Scientific, MA), and the mixture was mixed until a dough-like substance formed. This substance was allowed to dry to obtain the milk pellets. Experiments were performed on mice that were fed ChowD or MilkD for 2 to 4 weeks. ChowD and MilkD intake was monitored daily, and body weight was monitored twice weekly throughout the experiment.

Peritoneal macrophage isolation and cultivation

Peritoneal macrophages were obtained by lavage. Neonatal and adult cells were isolated by flushing the peritoneal cavity with 5 mL (adult mouse) or 1 mL (neonatal mouse) of ice-cold serum containing media using a 26G needle. Harvested peritoneal cells were washed, and 1 x 106 cells were suspended in 500 μL of DMEM/F12+GlutaMax containing D-glucose 3151 mg/mL (17.5 Mm), 10% heat inactivated FBS and penicillin/streptomycin. To enrich macrophages, 5 x 105 cells were added to 24-well plates in 500 μL of media, and cells were incubated to allow the adherence of macrophages. After 1-2 hours, unattached cells were flushed out using PBS until no floating cells were observed. For experiments with mouse serum culture, culture media was switched to the media containing adult or neonatal mouse serum.

Adoptive transfer of macrophages

Peritoneal macrophages of adult and neonatal mice were isolated by using CD11b magnetic beads (Miltenyi Biotec, San Diego, CA). Adult CD45.1+ B6.SJL-Ptprca Pep3b/BoyJ mice peritoneal macrophages (1 x 106) were transferred into the neonatal mouse peritoneum. Conversely, 0.1 x 106 peritoneal macrophages from neonatal CD45.2+ CD57BL/6 mice were transferred into the peritoneum of adult CD45.1+ B6.SJL-Ptprca Pep3b/BoyJ mice. Three days after macrophage transfer, phenotypes of peritoneal macrophages were analyzed by FACS.

Leishmania major infection of mice

Experiments were carried out using a stably transfected line of L. major FV1 promastigotes expressing a red fluorescent protein (mCherry-Leishmania).64 Parasites were grown at 26°C in medium 199 supplemented with 20% heat-inactivated newborn calf serum. Infective stage, metacyclic promastigotes of L. major were isolated from stationary cultures (4–5 days old) by negative selection using peanut agglutinin (PNA) (Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA). Mice were infected with the specified number of metacyclic promastigotes in the ear dermis by i.d. injection in a volume of 10 μL. Ear tissues were prepared as previously described.2 Briefly, the two sheets of infected ear dermis were separated and incubated in DMEM containing 0.2 mg/mL Liberase CI purified enzyme blend (Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, IN), and incubated for 1.5 hours at 37°C. Digested tissue was cut into small pieces and processed in a tissue homogenizer (Medimachine; BD Biosciences). Tissue homogenates were washed with fresh DMEM containing 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptavidin and filtered through a 70-mm cell strainer (Falcon Products, Corning, NY). Cells were then labeled, and infected dermal macrophages were analyzed with LSR Fortessa X20 or isolated using a FACSAriaTM III cell sorter (both from BD Biosciences). Infected dermal macrophages were processed for gene expression analysis using qPCR as described in the previous section.

Flow cytometry

Isolated peritoneal cells or ear macrophages were stained with fluorophore conjugated antibodies using FACS buffer containing 2% FBS, 0.5 M EDTA in PBS. Cells were stained with antibodies against CD11b (BV421 conjugated, clone M1/70, from BioLegend (San Diego, CA), IL-4Rα (Biotin conjugated, clone mIL4R-M1, from BD Biosciences (Franklin Lakes, NJ), CD206 (APC conjugated, clone C068C2, from BioLegend), CD86 (PE-Cy7 conjugated, clone GL-1, from BioLegend), and CD80 (FITC conjugated, clone 16-10A1, from BioLegend). Flow cytometry data were acquired on an LSR Fortessa flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) and analyzed using FlowJo software v10.7 (TreeStar Inc, Woodburn, OR).

TLR agonist stimulation

Isolated peritoneal macrophages were suspended in culture media containing 15% mouse serum and treated with CpG (100 ng/mL) for indicated durations. Cytokines were measured in supernatants by ELISA using the Mouse IL-6 ELISA Set, BD OptEIA (BD Biosciences) and Mouse TNF-α DuoSet ELISA (R&D system, Minneapolis, MN).

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells using the RNeasy Mini kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD). Complementary DNA was synthesized from the extracted RNA using TaqMan reverse transcript reagents (Applied Biosystems, Bedford, MA). Quantitative PCR was performed on the CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio Rad, Hercules, CA) and the QuantStudio 12K Flex Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystem) using TaqMan Gene Expression Master Mix (Applied Biosystem) and TaqMan probes (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Gene expression was determined by the quantification cycle difference (ΔCq) between the genes of interest and endogenous control mouse ACTB (actin, Beta) of the same sample. The ΔCq for each replicate was exponentially transformed to gene expression using the formula 2- ΔCq.

RNA sequencing analysis

Harvested peritoneal macrophages from adult and neonatal mice as well as in vitro cultures of adult peritoneal macrophages were subjected to RNA-seq analysis. For ex vivo RNA-seq analysis, lysis buffer was added to wells with attached macrophages that were harvested from peritoneum and total RNA was extracted. In two in vitro experiments, adult macrophages were subjected to RNA-seq analysis. In one experiment, adult macrophages were cultured in 20% neonatal or adult serum containing DMEM media for 16 hours. In the second experiment, adult macrophages were incubated in DMEM media containing 20% serum from adult mice fed with ChowD or MilkD for 16 hours. Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen). Samples were submitted to the FDA Biotechnology Resources Core Facility for quality control, library creation, and next-generation sequencing. The analysis of next-generation sequence data was performed by the FDA CBER High-Performance Integrated Virtual Environment.

Ex vivo and in vitro LD staining

Peritoneal cells were harvested and plated in 96-well plates for 1 hour. Suspended cells were removed by washing with PBS. Attached macrophages were stained with DAPI (20 ng/mL) (BioLegend) and BODIPY (1 μg/mL) (BODIPY™ 493/503 ThermoFisher Scientific, D3922). Images were taken using the EVOS cell imaging system (ThermoFisher Scientific) within 30 minutes after staining.

For in vitro staining, adult macrophages cultured under different conditions were washed with PBS at the end of incubation period. Cell monolayers were incubated with BODIPY (1 μg/mL) or Nile red (25 ng/mL) in PBS for 30 minutes. After washing with PBS three times, BODIPY stained cells were imaged with the EVOS cell imaging system and cell fluorescence intensity was quantified with ImageJ software (NIH Image J system, Bethesda, MD). For Oil red O staining, at the end of the incubation period, cells were fixed with 10% formalin for 30 minutes at ambient temperature followed by a rinse with water and permeabilization with 60% isopropanol for 5 minutes. Subsequently, cells were stained with Oil red O solution (1.4 mg/mL) for 5 minutes. Stained lipids were captured using light microscope DMi8 (Leica Microsystems, Durham, NC) and digital color camera DFC550 (Leica).

Serum delipidation

Lipids were removed from neonatal mouse serum as described by Cham and Knowles.42 Briefly, 1 mL of neonatal serum was mixed with 2 mL of di-isopropyl ether-Butanol mixture (40%:60%) (DIPE) and rotated at 30 rpm for 0.5 hours at room temperature. After centrifugation at 2000 rpm for 2 minutes, the lower aqueous phase was collected and transferred to a new 15 mL tube. Next, the aqueous serum was remixed with two volumes of DIPE and partitioned after centrifugation at 2000 rpm for 2 minutes. The lower delipidated serum fraction was transferred into a new tube and residual DIPE was removed after evaporation.

GSEA and GO enrichment analysis

GSEA V4.4.0 (Broad Institute) was performed using pre-racked gene lists derived from the DESeq2 DE values. Gene set enrichment statistics were calculated independently for each gene set collection in the Molecular Signatures Database Mouse MSigDB v2024.1. Analyses were performed with 1000 permutations, classic scoring schemes, and other default parameters. For correlation plots, GSEA mSigDatabases including curated Hallmarks, C2, C5 collections using standard setting (KEGG, Reactome) were compared. A normalized enrichment score (NES) >1 activator and NDS<1 suppressor with FDR of 0.25. GO enrichment analysis (http://www.geneontology.org) for differently expressed genes in different groups were performed using the Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery (DAVID) to find statistically significant biological functions and process.

Serum proteins, lipids, lactate, and glucose

ELISA kits for mouse GDF-15, MFG-E8, S100A8/A9 were purchased from R&D Systems and the ELISA kit for FABP4 was from Novus Biologicals (Centennial, CO). The Triglyceride assay Kit was purchased from Cayman Chemical (Ann Arbor, MI). Free Fatty Acid assay, Triglyceride assay kit, Cholesterol assay kit, L-Lactate Assay, and Glucose assay kit were purchased from Abcam (Waltham, MA). All quantification testing assays were performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

siRNA Knock-down

C57BL/6 mouse peritoneal macrophages were seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 3 to 5 x 105 cells/per well. After overnight incubation, macrophages were transiently transfected with 100 nM On-TARGETPlus SMARTpool siRNAs (Horizon Discovery, Cambridge, UK) using HiPerFect transfection agent (Qiagen) according to the manufacture’s protocol. Forty-eight hours later, the efficiency of RNA interference was determined by Western Blot analysis.

Western Blot analysis

Adult peritoneal macrophages were rinsed with ice-cold PBS twice and lysed with Laemmli Sample Buffer (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA) containing sample reducing agent (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA) and protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Cell signaling Technology, Danvers, MA). After denaturing at 95°C for 10 minutes, protein samples were separated by tris-glycine denaturing SDS-Page (Bio-Rad) and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes using iBlot 2 (Invitrogen). Membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk for 30 minutes, followed by incubation with primary antibodies against HILPDA (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA), ATGL (Cell signaling technology) and β-actin (Cell Signaling Technology) overnight at 4°C and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies against mouse and rabbit IgG (Cell Signaling Technology) for 1 hour at room temperature. Protein bands were imaged with FluorChem systems (Alpha Innotech, San Leandro, CA).

Phagocytosis assay

Adult peritoneal macrophages were incubated in 20% neonatal serum or adult serum in DMEM media for 16 hours in the presence of mTOR inhibitor (Rapamycin 100 nM), Myc inhibitor (10058-F4 50 μM), P53 inhibitor (GN25 20μM). For opsonization, 20 x 106 latex beads (1 μM size of amine-modified polystyrene-labeled with FITC, Invitrogen) were opsonized in 200 μL of adult mouse serum for 1 hour at 37°C in 5% CO2. In order to assess phagocytic function under the same conditions for both adult and neonatal serum-cultured macrophages, a phagocytosis assay was performed in 10% bead-opsonized adult mouse serum-containing media for 1 hour at 37°C in 5% CO2 (10:1 bead-to-macrophage ratio). Macrophages were detached after incubation with 10 mM EDTA/PBS for 10 min at 37°C and scraping for FACS staining, stained with efluor 780 fixable viability dye (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and anti-CD11b antibody.

Transmission electron microscopy

For ultrastructural observations of macrophages by thin section, samples were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 mM sodium cacodylate and processed as described previously.65 Ultrathin sections of infected cells were stained with osmium tetroxide before examination with Hitachi 7600 EM (Tokyo, Japan) under 80 kV.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Prism10 (GraphPad, San Diego, CA). Data plots were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Unpaired Mann-Whitney tests were used for comparisons between two groups and two-way ANOVA with Šídák multiple comparisons test were performed for multiple comparisons to determine the significance. P values of ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001, ∗∗∗P < 0.001, ∗∗P < 0.01, and ∗P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant and ns, not significant.
