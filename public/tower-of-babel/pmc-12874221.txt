Donor–Acceptor–Donor Small-Molecular Dots are Brighter than Polymer Dots of Similar Chromophore Units
Authors: Nirmal K. Das, Hubert Piwoński, Shuho Nozue, Manjusha Joshi, Kosuke Terayama, Tsuyoshi Michinobu, Satoshi Habuchi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Organic nanoparticles exhibiting bright fluorescence are promising for bioimaging applications. Both small-molecule organic fluorophores and conjugated polymers have been used to fabricate fluorescent nanoparticles. However, factors controlling the fluorescence characteristics of the two types of nanoparticles are not well understood. Here, we synthesized a series of donor–acceptor–donor (D-A-D) small molecules consisting of structural units similar to their polymer counterparts that showed fluorescence in the visible, near-infrared, and shortwave infrared spectral regions, and systematically characterized their spectroscopic properties in the solid state (i.e., nanodots). The nanodots consisting of the D-A-D small molecules consistently exhibited brighter fluorescence than the (D-A)n conjugated polymers. We separated the contributions of light absorption and emission to the fluorescence brightness using single-particle fluorescence microscopy, which revealed that the enhancement of both light absorption efficiency (i.e., molar extinction coefficient) and light-emitting efficiency (i.e., fluorescence quantum yield) resulted in the observed brighter fluorescence of the small-molecular dots compared with the polymer dots. Density functional theory (DFT) calculation and other spectroscopic studies suggested that a less twisted conformation (i.e., more planar conformation) of the molecules in the small-molecular dots directly caused the enhanced molar extinction coefficient. Steady-state and time-resolved fluorescence measurements suggested that this planarization upon particle formation indirectly resulted in the improved fluorescence quantum yield through the enhancement of the radiative rate. The single-particle excitation polarization modulation experiment demonstrated a more ordered overall orientation of the individual molecules in the small-molecular dots. This might explain their planar conformation and less efficient excited-state quenching due to intermolecular interactions. Our findings pave the way for the rational design and development of organic nanoparticles with bright fluorescence for bioimaging applications.

Introduction

Recent advancements in fluorescence microscopy techniques enable us to visualize biological structures and processes at the subcellular level with higher resolution. − Further improvements of the imaging techniques and their applicability require stable and biocompatible bright fluorophores. − Indeed, developing new fluorophores is one of the primary focuses in advancing fluorescence microscopy techniques. In particular, developing new fluorophores that emit fluorescence in near-infrared (NIR, 680–950 nm) and shortwave infrared (SWIR, 1000–1700 nm) is critical owing to the optical transparency of biological specimens in these spectral regions. ,,, The ideal fluorophores have a large molar extinction coefficient (ε), high fluorescence quantum yield (QY), enhanced photostability, water solubility, and small size. Researchers have been developing various bright NIR and SWIR emitting fluorophores, including semiconductor quantum dots (QDs), rare-earth-doped nanoparticles, dye-doped polymer dots, − carbon dots, metal nanoclusters (NCs), and single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWNTs). Among them, small organic molecules and conjugated polymers are two promising materials as they can exhibit bright fluorescence in the solid-state (i.e., small-molecular dots and polymer dots) in the NIR and SWIR spectrum regions. Furthermore, organic fluorophores and organic polymers are generally more biocompatible than inorganic probes or metal nanoparticles because of their lower cytotoxicity, which promotes their applications in bioimaging. Optical properties and size of the polymer dots and small-molecular dots can, in principle, be tuned by a proper molecular design and optimized fabrication protocol. Yet, the design of NIR- and SWIR-emitting fluorophores specifically tailored to form nanoparticles that retain prominent fluorescence brightness and physicochemical stability in an aqueous environment remains challenging.

The ultrasmall fluorescent nanoparticles consisting of donor–acceptor–donor (D-A-D) small molecules and D-A-type conjugated polymers ((D-A)n polymers) are two different classes of π-conjugated systems that have received immense interest due to their fluorescence brightness and photostability, which make them intriguing as probes for imaging and sensing applications. ,,,− The most common bottleneck in developing (D-A)n polymer dots is the generation of excited-state quenching sites through inter- and intrachain interactions inside nanoparticles. , As a consequence, they tend to exhibit relatively low QY or brightness. , This issue becomes more prominent in NIR and SWIR emitting polymer dots, where an extended π-conjugation system of the acceptor moieties, typically with a flat shape, leads to increased inter- and intrachain interactions inside the particle. , In addition, NIR- and SWIR-emitting fluorophores generally have lower QY due to the small energy gaps. The most frequently used approach to avoid excited-state quenching upon particle formation that leads to lower QY is to physically isolate fluorescent molecules (or fluorescent moieties) inside the particles, either by inserting bulky side groups into the molecules ,,, or by adding functional groups that cause aggregation-induced emission (AIE), − although these are done at the cost of the density of the fluorescent moieties inside the particles. An alternative approach is to use polymers with a bent and twisted conformation that effectively avoids inter- and intrachain interactions inside the particles without compromising the density of the fluorescent moieties. ,

The reduction of QY upon particle formation was largely avoided using small-molecule-based nanoparticles. ,, A similar bent and twisted approach has been used to develop nanoparticles consisting of D-A-D small molecules. ,, We previously showed a near-unity QY of Cz-based D-A-D small-molecular dots, while a decrease in QY was observed for the (D-A)n polymer dots that consisted of the same D–A unit. However, it is unclear whether this is the general trend. Researchers either developed brighter polymer dots ,,,− for various applications or engaged in generating stable and highly emissive small-molecular dots ,,,− for applying them in various interdisciplinary applications. No systematic investigation has been reported on the photophysical characteristics of the polymer-based and small-molecule-based nanoparticles, although such a comparative study would provide invaluable information about the development of brighter fluorescent nanoparticles.

Another seldom addressed issue in the development of fluorescent nanoparticles is the contribution of the light absorption efficiency to the overall fluorescence brightness. While the light-absorption efficiency (i.e., molar extinction coefficient) and light-emitting efficiency (i.e., QY) contribute equally to the overall fluorescence brightness, the primary focus in developing fluorescent nanoparticles has been on improving QY. The contribution of the light-absorption efficiency has largely been overlooked in the development of the bright fluorescent nanoparticles. This is probably due partly to the difficulty in experimentally quantifying the light-absorption efficiency of the nanoparticles. Clearly, the contribution of the light absorption efficiency to the overall fluorescence brightness needs to be quantitatively investigated to develop fluorescent nanoparticles further.

To address these issues, in this study, we synthesized a series of D-A-D small molecules consisting of Cz donor moiety and various acceptor moieties (Scheme ) that display fluorescence in the NIR and SWIR spectral regions and compared their photophysical characteristics with (D-A)n polymers that have similar structural units in the solution phase as well as in the solid state (i.e., nanodots). We experimentally determined the relative molar extinction coefficients of the small-molecular dots and polymer dots to separate the contributions of the light-absorption and light-emitting efficiencies to the overall fluorescence brightness of the dots. We further conducted DFT calculations, time-resolved fluorescence spectroscopy, and single-particle excitation polarization modulation experiments for a systematic comparative analysis of the factors governing the photophysical properties of the small-molecular dots and polymer dots.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Spectroscopic Characterizations of the Donor–Acceptor–Donor (D-A-D) Small Molecules and (D-A)n Polymers

To perform a systematic and comparative investigation, we rationally designed and synthesized a series of carbazole (Cz)-based (D-A)n type conjugated polymers and D-A-D small molecules with different acceptor moieties (namely, BT, BTT, DPP, IIG, NDI, and BBT, Scheme ). The detailed synthetic procedure for (D-A)n polymers was reported elsewhere. The general procedure for synthesizing D-A-D small molecules was mentioned in the Materials and Methods section. The choice of the bulky propeller-shaped donor (Cz-moiety) group with alkyl chains is aimed at our rational molecular design strategy to restrict π-stacking of the fluorophore (i.e., H-type aggregates) inside the nanoparticles, which further diminishes the fluorescence of the nanodots. , Further, the π-bridge, i.e., alkynyl linkage, directly connects the aromatic cores of various acceptors and the donors (Cz) to serve as the mode of electronic conjugation between donor and acceptor.

Steady-state absorption spectra of the D-A-D small molecules (CzBTCz, CzBTTCz, CzIIGCz, CzDPPCz, CzNDICz, and CzBBTCz) in THF (Figure S1) showed absorption bands in the visible to NIR spectral region, with peak wavelengths ranging from 492 to 807 nm (Table S1), which can be attributed to charge transfer (CT) absorption bands. The (D-A)n polymers ((CzBT) n , (CzBTT) n , (CzIIG) n , (CzDPP) n , (CzNDI) n , and (CzBBT) n ) in THF displayed absorption spectra similar to the corresponding D-A-D small molecules (Figure S1 and Table S2). The D-A-D small molecules displayed fluorescence in the visible to SWIR spectral region (Figure S1), with peak wavelengths ranging from 594–994 nm (Table S1), which are very similar to the fluorescence spectra of the (D-A)n polymers (Figure S1 and Table S2). Considering the number of repeating units in the (D-A)n polymers (3.5 – 5.8 units), these results suggest that the D-A-D small molecules and the (D-A)n polymers have similar spectroscopic units.

The fluorescence lifetime of the D-A-D small molecules and (D-A)n polymers exhibited a similar trend (Figures S2 and S3, and Tables S1 and S2), shorter fluorescence lifetimes for the molecules with longer absorption and fluorescence wavelengths. The radiative rate constants (k r) for the corresponding D-A-D small molecules and (D-A)n polymers determined from the lifetime measurements were very similar. Since k r is associated with the transition probability between the ground and excited electronic states, which is determined by the electronic structures in the ground state of the molecule, the observation suggests similar spectroscopic units for both D-A-D small molecules and (D-A)n polymers, which is consistent with the findings in the steady-state measurements. We observed distinct nonradiative rate constants (k nr) for the same pairs. The differences in the fluorescence lifetimes between the D-A-D small molecules and (D-A)n polymers are, thus, associated mainly with the differences in k nr caused by distinct excited-state dynamics. We also observed that the molecules exhibiting the absorption and fluorescence at the longer wavelengths showed larger k nr values, which is interpreted by the energy gap law.

The D-A-D small molecules and (D-A)n polymers exhibited the fluorescence quantum yields (QYs) in the range of 0.2–97% and 0.3–57%, respectively (Tables S1 and S2), and showed a similar trend (Tables S1 and S2), with smaller QYs for the molecules with longer absorption and fluorescence wavelengths. The D-A-D and (D-A)n pairs often showed distinct QYs, which is mainly associated with distinct k nr caused by excited-state dynamics (e.g., nonradiative deactivation paths related to molecular conformation and intramolecular interactions). The steady-state and time-resolved measurements suggest that the D-A-D small molecules and (D-A)n polymers in THF have similar ground-state electronic structures, resulting in similar spectroscopic units and k r values, whereas they have distinct excited-state dynamics that cause distinct QY and k nr values.

Fabrication and Photophysical Characterization of the Small-Molecular Dots and Polymer Dots

We fabricated the D-A-D small-molecular dots and (D-A)n polymer dots using the reprecipitation method (see Materials and Methods section for the fabrication details). ,, As reported previously, the reprecipitation protocol enabled the fabrication of ultrasmall (D-A)n polymer dots with diameters in the range of 2–5 nm, determined by atomic force microscopy (AFM) (Figure S4 and Table S3). The D-A-D small-molecular nanodots fabricated using a similar method showed the size distributions, determined by either AFM or transmission electron microscopy (TEM), similar to the (D-A)n polymer dots (Table , and Figures S5 and S6), although the size of the individual molecules inside the particles is significantly different. The AFM and TEM experiments demonstrated relatively narrow size distributions of the fabricated D-A-D small-molecular nanodots and (D-A)n polymer dots (Figure S4, S5, and S6). The zeta potential of the D-A-D small-molecular dots dispersed in water was in the range of −30 to −45 mV (Table ), indicating a good colloidal stability of the fabricated nanodots. These characterizations showed that the D-A-D small-molecular dots and their corresponding (D-A)n polymer dots have similar sizes and surface charges.

Spectroscopic and Physical Properties of the D-A-D Small-Molecular Dots in Water

Steady-state absorption spectra of the D-A-D small-molecular dots dispersed in water showed a systematic bathochromic (red) shift compared with the (D-A)n polymer dots (Figure , Table , and Table S4). This is in contrast to the absorption spectra of the molecules in THF, which did not show a systematic deviation between them (Figure S1). Given the similar particle sizes and surface charges, environmental factors (e.g., polarity) are unlikely to play a critical role in the observed difference. The finding rather indicates the involvement of intramolecular conformation (see the following section reporting DFT calculations for details). Steady-state fluorescence spectra of the D-A-D small-molecular dots dispersed in water mostly showed a systematic hypsochromic (blue) shift compared with the (D-A)n polymer dots (Figure , Table , and Table S3). This indicates that excited-state structural relaxation is less pronounced in the small-molecular dots compared with the polymer dots.

Steady-state absorption (solid lines) and fluorescence spectra (dashed lines) of the D-A-D small-molecular dots (red lines) and the (D-A)n polymer dots (blue lines) dispersed in water, namely (a) BT, (b) BTT, (c) IIG, (d) DPP, (e) NDI, and (f) BBT.

The fluorescence lifetime of the D-A-D small-molecular dots (Table and Figure S2) and (D-A)n polymer dots (Figure S3 and Table S3) dispersed in water showed an overall similar trend to the small-molecules and polymers in THF (Figures S2 and S3, and Tables S1 and S2). However, the k r values are consistently larger for the small-molecular dots than the polymer dots, which differs from the similar k r values observed for the D-A-D small molecules and (D-A)n polymers in THF, indicates the difference in the intramolecular twist between the D-A-D small molecules and (D-A)n polymers inside the particles (see below). The k nr values for the small-molecular dots and the corresponding polymer dots also differ, but unlike the k r values, we did not observe general trends between them. This indicates that the excited state photophysical dynamics that affect nonradiative deactivation paths in the small-molecular dots and polymer dots are governed by complicated factors.

The D-A-D small-molecular dots exhibited consistently larger QYs than (D-A)n polymer dots, except for BBT, in the range of 0.1–83% and 0.3–13%, respectively (Table and Table S3). In general, QY of organic small-molecule and polymer fluorophores decreases in their aggregated states in a solid (e.g., nanoparticles) compared with that in their isolated states in a solution because of intra- and interchain interactions that cause the generation of energy trap sites, leading to fluorescence quenching (i.e., larger k nr). ,− Indeed, the (D-A)n polymers showed a large decrease in QY upon particle formation, except for a slight increase in QY observed for IIG and NDI (Tables S2 and S3). In contrast, the D-A-D small molecules mostly maintained their QY upon particle formation (Table and Table S1), indicating less efficient generation of energy trap sites, leading to inefficient fluorescence quenching. Unlike the difference in QY observed for the D-A-D small molecules and (D-A)n polymers in THF, which is determined predominantly by k nr (i.e., light-emitting processes from the excited state), the steady-state and time-resolved measurements demonstrated that the difference in QY between the small-molecular dots and polymer dots in water is affected by both k r and k nr.

It should be mentioned that CzBTCz exhibited a distinct photophysical behavior upon particle formation (i.e., formation of highly fluorescent J-aggregates, with its spectral bandwidths significantly narrower than those of other small-molecular dots and very small Stokes shift), and, thus, its photophysical characteristics do not always match what would be systematically predicted from the behavior of other molecules.

Dissecting the Photophysical Characteristics: Fluorescence Brightness, Quantum Yield of Fluorescence, and Molar Extinction Coefficient

The light-emitting processes, particularly QY, have been investigated extensively to design and fabricate nanoparticles with bright fluorescence, the contribution of the light-absorption process to their fluorescence brightness has not been well studied, although they equally contribute to the fluorescence brightness of nanoparticles. To quantitatively evaluate the light-absorption process in the fluorescent brightness, we experimentally determined the relative molar extinction coefficient of the D-A-D small-molecular dots and (D-A)n polymer dots per unit volume. To that end, we measured fluorescence brightness of the individual nanodots under the identical imaging conditions (Figures and ), from which we calculated brightness per unit volume (cnts s–1/nm3/W cm–2). The relative extinction coefficient that quantifies the light-absorption efficiency of the particle per unit volume was estimated by dividing the fluorescence brightness per unit volume by QY.

The experimentally determined fluorescence brightness per unit volume (Figure b,e) clearly showed that the D-A-D small-molecular dots consistently exhibited brighter fluorescence than the corresponding (D-A)n polymer dots (Figure S7 and Tables S4 and S5). We note that we could not determine the fluorescence brightness of BBT small-molecular and polymer dots since their fluorescence is outside the wavelength range of the photodetector used in the experiment. The D-A-D small-molecular dots displayed 1.7 – 18-fold brighter fluorescence per unit volume than the (D-A)n polymer dots. This result clearly demonstrates that organic fluorescent nanoparticles consisting of small molecules and polymers with the same spectroscopic units could have more than an order of magnitude different brightness.

We observed up to 6-fold larger QY for the small-molecular dots than the polymer dots (Figure a,d). The observed systematic difference in the QY is not caused by the QY of the molecules in the isolated state, as there is no systematic difference in the QY between the small molecules and polymers in THF (Figure S8). The comparison of the QY of the small-molecular dots and polymer dots suggests that the difference in the fluorescence brightness between them partly arises from the difference in the QY.

The experimentally determined fluorescence brightnesses and QYs enabled us to calculate the relative peak molar extinction coefficient of the D-A-D small-molecular dots and (D-A)n polymer dots per unit volume (Figure c,f, and Tables S4 and S5). The D-A-D small-molecular dots consistently exhibit a larger molar extinction coefficient than the corresponding (D-A)n polymer dots. Figure c,f show that the small-molecular dots displayed 1.2–3.0-fold greater values than the (D-A)n polymer dots. This result suggests that the difference in the molar extinction coefficient (i.e., light-absorption efficiency) is another factor causing the observed difference in the fluorescence brightness between the small-molecular dots and polymer dots.

DFT Calculation: Factors Governing the Molar Extinction Coefficient

Using DFT and time-dependent DFT (TDDFT) quantum chemical calculations, we investigated the relationship between the intramolecular conformation and light-absorption efficiency in the D-A-D small-molecular dots and (D-A)n polymer dots, particularly the effect of intramolecular twist. , Optimum ground-state geometries, highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energies, and HOMO and LUMO molecular orbitals were obtained by DFT calculation using the B3LYP functional with the 6–31G+(d,p) basis set (see the Materials and Methods section for computational details, see Supporting Note 1). Absorption characteristics (i.e., UV–vis spectra and oscillator strengths) were obtained by TDDFT calculation (see Supporting Note 1). The alkyl chain on the Cz moieties of the small molecules was replaced by methyl groups for DFT and TDDFT calculations. The DFT and TDDFT calculations of the (D-A)n polymers were conducted using model systems consisting of D-A-D-A-D structures, in which all alkyl chains attached to the polymer molecules were replaced by methyl groups.

The DFT calculation suggests that the electron delocalizes along the D and A moieties in the HOMO level in both D-A-D small molecules (Figure ) and (D-A)n polymers (Figure S9), while the extent of the electron delocalization is slightly different in the small molecules and polymer molecules. A limited electron delocalization in the HOMO level has been reported for D-A-type conjugated polymers. In the LUMO level, the electron is localized to the A moieties in both D-A-D small molecules (Figure ) and (D-A)n polymers (Figure S9). These results clearly indicate the charge transfer (CT) nature of the HOMO–LUMO transition in all the small molecules and polymer molecules, consistent with the experimentally measured UV–vis absorption spectra of the molecules. The HOMO–LUMO energy gaps obtained for the D-A-D small molecules (Figure b) and the (D-A)n polymers (Figure d) by the DFT and TDDFT calculations agreed with the experimentally measured energy gaps of the D-A-D small molecules and (D-A)n polymers (Table S6 and S7). ,

The DFT calculation indicates that both D-A-D small molecules and (D-A)n polymers adopt a coplanar structure between the D and A moieties in the ground state, except for IIG (Figures and S9). The alkynyl linkage connecting the D and A moieties may alleviate steric hindrance that would otherwise prevent the coplanarity. It should be mentioned that the intramolecular conformation could deviate from the coplanar structure because the alkyl chains on the D moieties that might induce an intramolecular twist due to the steric hindrance are excluded in the DFT calculation. Indeed, a significant deviation from the coplanar structure was experimentally observed for individual (CzBT)n polymer molecules fixed in a polymer matrix. In addition, while the HOMO level energies increase with increasing the twist angle for all the D-A-D small molecules and (D-A)n polymers, the increase in the energy is within the thermal energy at room temperature (approximately 2.5 kJ mol–1) when the twist angle is up to 20–30° (Figures and S11), suggesting that the molecules in the ground state can adopt twisted conformation up to 20–30°. Together, the D-A-D small molecules and (D-A)n polymer molecules will likely have a broad distribution of the D–A twist angle, up to ± 20–30° in the solution.

The oscillator strengths (f) for the transitions from the ground state to the excited states obtained for the D-A-D small molecules and (D-A)n polymers displayed by the TDDFT calculation exhibited a similar trend , (Figure S12a, and Tables S6 and S7), but the D-A-D small molecules showed larger values except for DPP and NDI. Importantly, f is proportional to the spectrally integrated molar extinction coefficient (i.e., area under the UV–vis absorption spectrum) of the molecules. Thus, we compared the ratios of the calculated f of the D-A-D small molecules and the (D-A)n polymers (f sm/f p) with the ratios of the experimentally determined relative molar extinction coefficient (ε) of the D-A-D small-molecular dots and the (D-A)n polymer dots (ε sm‑dots/ε p‑dots) (Figure S12b). While we observed a similar trend for both, the experimentally determined ε sm‑dots/ε p‑dots showed larger values (Figure S12b).

The TDDFT calculation suggested that the twist angle also affects the energy gap and oscillator strength (Figures and S11). Intramolecular twist or torsion between D and A moieties in D–A type small molecules and conjugated polymers is known to inhibit electron delocalization across the entire molecule, , and hence, a gradual increment in the energy gap with an attenuation in oscillator strength is expected with the increase of the twist/torsional angle. Our TDDFT calculation on the D-A-D small molecules and (D-A)n polymers, indeed, suggested the attenuation of the oscillator strength and the increase in the energy gap with the increase of the twist angle (Figures and S11). Considering the broad D–A twist angle distribution (up to ± 20–30°) of the D-A-D small molecules and (D-A)n polymers in the solution, the larger ε sm‑dots/ε p‑dots values obtained experimentally compared with the f sm/f p values estimated by the TDDFT calculation could be interpreted by a more planar structure (i.e., planarization) of the D-A-D molecules in the small-molecular dots compared with the (D-A)n polymer in the polymer dots. The bathochromic shift of the absorption spectra (Figure ) of the D-A-D small-molecular dots compared with the (D-A)n polymer dots (i.e., smaller energy gap for the D-A-D small molecule dots) is also explained by the planarization of the molecules in the small-molecular dots, further supporting our interpretation. Together, the DFT and TDDFT calculations indicated that the larger light-absorption efficiency of the D-A-D small-molecular dots compared with the corresponding (D-A)n polymer dots is associated with the change in the intramolecular conformation (i.e., planarization) of the D-A-D small molecules upon particle formation.

Radiative and Nonradiative Processes: Factors Governing the Fluorescence Brightness and Quantum Yield

According to the Strickler–Berg equation, the oscillator strength (f) is described by 1 f∝kr The linear relationship between the f and k r suggests that the experimentally determined k r would reflect the intramolecular twist. Importantly, the intramolecular twist would also affect the light-emitting efficiency, as QY is described by 2 QY=krkr+knr We, thus, investigated how intramolecular twist affects the QY and fluorescence brightness of the D-A-D small-molecular nanodots and (D-A)n polymer dots. In addition, we also characterized the effect of k nr on their QY.

The effect of the planarization on the QY upon particle formation was quantified by plotting the ratio of the QY obtained for the nanodots dispersed in water and the small molecules and polymers dissolved in THF (QYdots/QYTHF) against the ratios of k r obtained for the nanodots and the small molecules and polymers (k r dots/k r THF) (Figure a). We found a general trend between them, a positive correlation between the k r dots/k r THF values and the QYdots/QYTHF values for both the D-A-D small molecules and (D-A)n polymers. Since the larger k r dots/k r THF values correspond to a more planar conformation of the molecule inside the nanodots than in the solution and vice versa, the result suggests that the change in twist angle of the molecules upon particle formation is one of the main factors determining the change in the QY upon particle formation for both the small molecules and the polymers. Notably, the D-A-D small molecules consistently exhibited larger k r dots/k r THF and QYdots/QYTHF values than the corresponding (D-A)n polymers, except BBT. The result strongly suggests that the D-A-D small molecules adopt a more planar conformation than the (D-A)n polymers inside the nanodots, resulting in the larger QYdots/QYTHF values (Figure a,d). These findings demonstrated that the intramolecular twist affects both the light-absorption and light-emitting efficiency. It should be mentioned that overall enhancement of the fluorescence brightness and QY of the small-molecule nanodots is governed by the enhanced radiative process of the molecules (see Supporting Note 2), which is largely unexplored in the context of fluorescent molecular aggregation engineering.

In contrast to the radiative rate constant, the ratio of the QY obtained for the nanodots dispersed in water and the small molecules and polymers dissolved in THF (QYdots/QYTHF) against the ratios of k nr obtained for the nanodots and the small molecules and polymers (k nr dots/k nr THF) exhibited a negative correlation (Figure b). Since the larger k nr dots/k nr THF values correspond to more efficient excited-state quenching, the observed general trend demonstrates that the excited-state quenching upon particle formation is another factor determining the change in the QY upon particle formation for both the small molecules and the polymers. The primary origin of the reduction in QY has been attributed to the generation of fluorescence quenching sites inside the particles owing to intra- and intermolecular interactions. ,,, The larger k nr dots/k nr THF values observed for DPP, BBT, and BTT in both small molecules and polymers may indicate more efficient excited-state quenching upon particle formation when thiophene groups are present in the acceptor moieties that induce weak interactions during the particle formation.

We did not observe any general trend between the k nr dots/k nr THF values obtained for the small molecules and polymers (Figure b). BT, IIG, and DPP showed larger k nr dots/k nr THF values for the small molecules, while BTT, NDI, and BBT displayed smaller k nr dots/k nr THF values for the small molecules. The lack of a general trend indicates that the twist angle between the D and A moieties does not affect the intra- and intermolecular interactions inside the particle that induce accelerated nonradiative quenching of the excited states. This finding rather indicates the contribution of multiple factors that govern the excited-state dynamics. The results of our quantitative analysis of the radiative and nonradiative rate constants strengthen the hypothesis that the small-molecular dots show brighter fluorescence compared with the polymer dots due to more efficient light absorption (i.e., larger molar extinction coefficient) and light emission (i.e., larger QY), both of which are governed by the intramolecular twist (see Supporting Note 3).

Single-Particle Excitation Polarization Modulation: Long-Range Molecular Packing and Photophysical Characteristics

To explore insights into the spatial packing of the small molecules and the polymers having the same repetitive fluorophore unit inside the particles, we conducted single-particle excitation polarization modulation experiments (Figure , see the Materials and Methods section for experimental details). The fluorescence intensities of individual particles recorded at varied excitation polarization angles were fitted to eq S1 (see Materials and Methods section) to determine the modulation depths (Figure b,d,f,h,j). We note that we could not determine the modulation depths of BBT small-molecular and polymer dots since their fluorescence is outside the wavelength range of the photodetector used in the experiment. We quantitatively evaluated the modulation depths obtained for the small-molecular dots and the polymer dots by the box plots of the modulation depths (Figure a,c,e,g,i). A large modulation depth, in principle, indicates an ordered packing of the molecules (i.e., spatially ordered transition dipoles), ,, while a small modulation depth corresponds to a random orientation of the molecules (see Supporting Note 4). ,,

Box plots of the excitation polarization modulation depths obtained for the small-molecular dots (red) and the polymer dots (blue); (a) BT, (c) BTT, (e) IIG, (g) DPP, (i) NDI. Typical examples of the polarization-angle-dependent fluorescence intensity obtained for the small-molecular dots (red) and the polymer dots (blue); (b) BT, (d) BTT, (f) IIG, (h) DPP, (j) NDI. The solid lines show the fitting of the data to eq S1.

We observed larger modulation depths for the small-molecular dots than the polymer dots for all the molecules except BT. IIG (mean depth of 0.38 and 0.1 for the small-molecular and polymer dots, respectively, Figure e) and NDI (mean depth of 0.63 and 0.34 for the small-molecular and polymer dots, respectively, Figure i) showed statistically significant differences. These results clearly demonstrate that the emitting sites inside the CzIIGCz and CzNDICz small-molecular dots are more spatially ordered than the (CzIIG) n and (CzNDI) n polymer dots. BTT (mean depth of 0.25 and 0.2 for the small-molecular and polymer dots, respectively, Figure c) and DPP (mean depth of 0.46 and 0.44 for the small-molecular and polymer dots, respectively, Figure g) also showed larger modulation depths for the small-molecular dots, but the differences are not statistically significant. Since the excitation polarization modulation experiment quantifies the long-range spatial order of the emitters, our findings suggest the correlation between the molecular-level conformation (i.e., planarity between the D and A moieties) and the particle-level spatial order of the molecules. The shorter length of the D-A-D molecules and, therefore, larger flexibility in the intra- and intermolecular interactions inside the particles may facilitate more spatially ordered molecular packing inside the particles, resulting in a more planar conformation of the molecules inside the particle compared with the (D-A)n polymers. BT displayed an opposite behavior (i.e., larger modulation depth for the polymer dots than the small-molecular dots, Figure a). We previously reported that CzBTCz forms J-aggregates inside the small-molecular dots, most likely consisting of multiple domains, which could lead to an overall smaller modulation depth than (CzBT) n polymer dots.

Conclusions

In summary, we systematically explored the photophysical characteristics of a series of NIR and SWIR-emitting D-A-D small-molecular dots and (D-A)n polymer dots. Although the small molecules and polymers formed nanoparticles with similar sizes (2–5 nm), their fluorescence properties differed extensively. We found that the D-A-D small-molecular dots consistently showed larger fluorescence brightness than the (D-A)n polymer dots because of the larger extinction coefficient and QY. Detailed spectroscopic characterization and DFT/TDDFT calculations suggested that a more planar conformation (i.e., less intramolecular twisted conformation) that the D-A-D small molecules adopt inside the particle than the (D-A)n polymers caused enhanced oscillator strength, which is proportional to the molar extinction coefficient and radiative rate constant (k r). Therefore, our findings strongly suggested that the radiative pathway was the primary factor for both enhanced molecular absorptivity and QY. The excitation polarization modulation experiment indicated that the long-range (i.e., particle level) spatially ordered packing of the small molecules inside the particles could be responsible for a more planar conformation of the small molecules than the polymers inside the particles. All these findings point to the possibility of developing bright fluorescent nanodots using an unconventional approach: molecular design and particle fabrication focusing on the radiative process of the D-A-D molecules rather than the conventional approach centered around the control of the excited-state quenching. Finally, given the emerging number of applications of the fluorescent nanodots made of D-A-type molecules owing to their wide fluorescence tunability, our findings provide a fundamental framework for designing and fabricating new ultrasmall small-molecular and polymer dots and pave an avenue for bioimaging applications in the NIR and SWIR wavelength region.

Fabrication Method of the Polymer Dots and Small-Molecular Dots

Conjugated polymer nanoparticles (polymer dots) in aqueous medium of BT, BTT, IIG, DPP, and NDI were fabricated using a reprecipitation method described elsewhere. All the small-molecules (including CzBTCz, which form stable J-dots) nanoparticles (small-molecular dots) were prepared using a slightly modified version of the reprecipitation method. A small amount of D-A-D molecules was dissolved in THF and briefly sonicated in an ultrasonic bath (Elmasonic P60H, Elma Schmidbauer GmbH), further filtered through a 0.2-μm PTFE syringe filter (Anatop 10, Whatman). Then, a ∼1.5% volume/volume D-A-D small-molecule in THF was rapidly injected into 12 mL of Milli-Q water (Millipore, Milli-Q Reference). The mixed solution was kept under sonication in an ultrasonic bath at 10 °C for 40 min. THF was removed from the mixed solution by evaporation under vacuum for 1 h, and a continuous flow of compressed dry air (1–2 h) concentrated further nanoparticle colloidal solutions. The resulting small-molecular dots suspension was filtered through a 0.2-μm membrane filter (Minisart, hydrophilic) for further study.

A circularly polarized excitation light was obtained using a quarter waveplate (Thorlabs, AQWP05M-600). Small-molecular dots and polymer dots were excited through an oil immersion objective (Olympus, TIRF UApo N, × 100, NA 1.49). The resulting fluorescence images were separated from the excitation laser by a combination of a dichroic beam splitter followed by an emission filter (Semrock, FF506-Di01 and Thorlabs, FELH0500) for 488 nm excitation, (Semrock, FF555-Di03 and Thorlabs, FELH0600) for 532 nm excitation, and (Semrock, Di01-R405/488/561/6535 and Thorlabs, FELH0650) for 640 nm excitation, respectively. The laser power density for the excitation was kept at 45, 11.5, 688, 200, and 580 Wcm–2 for BT, BTT, IIG, DPP, and NDI small-molecular dots, respectively, and 45, 55, 688, 200, and 580 Wcm–2 for the BT, BTT, IIG, DPP, and NDI polymer dots, respectively. The fluorescence images were collected by a high-speed EMCCD camera (iXon Ultra 897, Andor Technology). The fluorescence intensities of individual particles were calculated by integrating the intensities of each pixel in the fluorescence spots. Images were processed and analyzed using ImageJ, IgorPro, and OriginPro software.

DFT Calculations

All quantum mechanical calculations were carried out with the Gaussian16 software package. Ground-state (S0) geometry optimizations for all the small-molecules and polymers were performed by employing DFT calculations using the B3LYP functional and the 6–31G+(d,p) basis set. The alkyl substituent groups on the carbazole (Cz) units were replaced by methyl groups to reduce the computational cost. , Also, the polymeric (D-A)n molecules were modeled by a D-A-D-A-D structure. , Any alkyl substitution of (D-A)n polymer molecules was replaced by a methyl group to reduce the computational cost. Subsequently, frequency calculation was performed for the optimized structures of all molecules using the same level of theory to check if any imaginary frequency was involved in the optimized structures. The HOMO energy level of different molecules was estimated by energy calculation using the B3LYP functional and 6–31G+(d,p) basis set. To characterize the optical absorption features of the D-A-D small molecules and (D-A)n polymer molecules, molecular absorption spectra were estimated by calculating the lowest 10 singlet–singlet transitions for D-A-D small molecules and 15 singlet–singlet transitions for (D-A)n polymer molecules. The performance of four different functionals (B3LYP, CAM-B3LYP, ωB97X-D, and M062X) in combination with 6–31+G­(d,p) basis sets is explored on the ground state optimized molecular structures. Excitation energies and oscillator strength have been computed using SMD condition water as a medium to mimic the chemical structure of the molecules in aqueous conditions. The results showed that using the TDDFT method in computing the low-lying singlet excited state of different D-A-D systems allowed for obtaining absorbance data that compared well with the experimental values. In the best cases, the mean absolute error (MAE) was found to be 0.18 and 0.19 < 0.2 eV, respectively, for CAMB3LYP and M062X functional than the other functionals. , Therefore, we have employed those functionals for all other TDDFT calculations. As we obtained very similar TDDFT computational results from CAMB3LYP and M062X functional, we have displayed only the CAMB3LYP result in different figures in the paper. The transition (or excitation) energy and oscillator strength of the model systems at fixed twist angles from 0 to 90 deg (with 10-degree intervals) between the Cz (donor) and different acceptor units were also calculated using the same levels of theory.
