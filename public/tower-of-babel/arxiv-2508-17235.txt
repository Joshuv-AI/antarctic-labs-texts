On the relationship between the Wasserstein distance and differences in life expectancy at birth
Markus Sauerberg

Abstract
The Wasserstein distance is a metric for assessing distributional differences. The measure originates in optimal transport theory and can be interpreted as the minimal cost of transforming one distribution into another. In this paper, the Wasserstein distance is applied to life table age-at-death distributions. The main finding is that, under certain conditions, the Wasserstein distance between two age-at-death distributions equals the corresponding gap in life expectancy at birth ( e 0 e_{0} ). More specifically, the paper shows mathematically and empirically that this equivalence holds whenever the survivorship functions do not cross. For example, this applies when comparing mortality between women and men from 1990 to 2020 using data from the Human Mortality Database. In such cases, the gap in e 0 e_{0} reflects not only a difference in mean ages at death but can also be interpreted directly as a measure of distributional difference.



1
Introduction

The Wasserstein distance, also known as the Earth Mover’s distance, measures the distance between two probability distributions. The metric is often illustrated by imagining each distribution as a pile of dirt, where the Wasserstein distance corresponds to the minimal cost of transporting one pile into the other (see
docs.scipy.org
). The measure originates in optimal transport theory but is now widely applied in computer science, for example in image retrieval
(
Rubner et al. 2000
)
and machine learning
(
Peyré and Cuturi 2019
)
. A historical overview of the optimal transport problem can be found in
(
Santambrogio 2015
)
. In short, the problem of finding the optimal transport map to transform one distribution into another was first formulated in 1781 by the French mathematician Gaspard Monge. However, Monge’s original formulation remained mathematically intractable, since it was not clear whether a minimizer (an optimal transport map) always exists. In the 1940s, the Soviet mathematician Leonid Kantorovich reformulated the problem in terms of linear programming, which made it mathematically tractable and led to rigorous solutions. Since Monge and Kantorovich played central roles, the underlying problem is often referred to as the Monge–Kantorovich problem. The metric itself, however, is usually called the Wasserstein distance, after Leonid Vaserstein, who studied these distances in the 1960s
(
Santambrogio 2015
)
.

Using the Kantrovich formulation, the distance between two probability distributions
P
and
Q
can be defined as,

W_{p}(P,Q)=\left(\inf_{J\in\mathcal{J}(P,Q)}\int\|x-y\|^{p}dJ(x,y)\right)^{1/p},

(1)

where
\mathcal{J}(P,Q)
denotes a set of all couplings (also called transport plans) between
P
and
Q
. The
\inf_{J\in\mathcal{J}(P,Q)}
denotes the infimum, i.e., among all transport plans, we are looking for the one with the smallest cost. For each pair,
(x,y)
, the cost of moving mass from
x
to
y
is given by
\|x-y\|^{p}
. Accordingly,
\|x-y\|^{p}
defines how the distance between
x
and
y
is measured. If we set
p=1
, we are considering the average absolute distance moved. This is usually called the Earth Mover’s distance
(
Wasserman 2019
)
.

Further, it has been shown that in a special case - when considering distributions from a one-dimensional space and using the absolute difference,
p=1
, as the cost function - the Wasserstein distance simplifies to,

W_{1}(P,Q)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}|F-G|,

(2)

where
F
and
G
are the respective cumulative distribution functions (CDFs) of
P
and
Q
. Please refer to
Santambrogio 2015
, chapter 2 for more details on the equivalence of both equations.

The aim of this paper is calculating the Wasserstein distance for two life table age-at death distributions and comparing the value to the differences in life expectancy derived from the same life tables. The idea of using the Wasserstein distance to examine the similarity of life table age-at death distributions was introduced by
Cilek et al. 2023
. Their work, however, has mainly focused on applying the Wasserstein distance to two-dimensional distributions. That is, they compared cause-specific age-at-death distributions on the basis of the Wasserstein distance. This paper concentrates on the one-dimensional case that is shown in equation
2
. The following analysis aims to demonstrate the relationship between
W_{1}(P,Q)
and differences in life expectancy.

2
Methods

2.1
Life expectancy at birth

In demography, life expectancy at birth (
e_{0}
) measures the expected number of life years on the basis of a set of age-specific mortality rates.
e_{0}
is derived from a period life table - a procedure where age-specific mortality rates are first converted to probabilities of dying and then applied to an hypothetical birth cohort in order to calculate the number of persons alive and deceased in each age interval (see
Preston et al. 2001
). Life expectancy at birth can be defined as the mean age at death of the life table population,

e_{0}=\frac{\int_{0}^{\omega}x\cdot d(x)dx}{\int_{0}^{\omega}d(x)dx},

(3)

where
d(x)
denote the age-specific number of persons deceased in the life table and
\omega
defines the last open-age interval in the life table.

However, a more popular formula is to define
e_{0}
as the area under the survival curve,

e_{0}=\int_{0}^{\omega}l(x)dx,

(4)

where
l(x)
denotes the life table survivorship function.

Usually, the size of the initial life table birth cohort is set to 100 000 persons. The probability of dying at the oldest age is
1
, i.e., everyone dies in a life table and hence, the sum over all
dx
equals the initial life table population size. When setting the initial size to
1
, the
d(x)
function can be seen as a probability density function (PDF). Further, it is well-known in survival analysis that the PDF, CDF, and the survivorship function are directly linked,

\begin{split}S(x)&=1-\int_{0}^{x}f(u)du\\
&=1-F(x),\end{split}

(5)

where the CDF,
F(x)
, is obtained by integrating the PDF,
f(x)
, from
0
up to age (or time)
x
. The survivorship function is the complement of the CDF and vice versa,

F(x)=1-S(x).

(6)

2.2
Wasserstein distance and life table age-at-death distributions

Since age-at-death distributions are one-dimensional, we can compute their Wasserstein distance using their CDFs,

W_{1}(P_{A},P_{B})=\int_{0}^{\omega}|F_{A}(x)-F_{B}(x)|dx,

(7)

In this case, the
W_{1}
Wasserstein distance is simply the area between the respective CDFs of population
A
and
B
. Since the CDF is defined as
l(x)=1-F(x)
, we have

|F_{A}(x)-F_{B}(x)|=|l_{A}(x)-l_{B}(x)|,

(8)

so the area between the CDFs is equal to the area between the survivorship functions,

\begin{split}W_{1}(P_{A},P_{B})&=\int_{0}^{\omega}|F_{A}(x)-F_{B}(x)|dx\\
&=\int_{0}^{\omega}|l_{A}(x)-l_{B}(x)|dx.\end{split}

(9)

Here we are using the cost function
|x-y|
to measure transportation cost. Accordingly, the Wasserstein distance can be interpreted as the expected absolute difference in ages when the two distributions are matched optimally.

2.3
Wasserstein distance and the difference in life expectancy at birth

The difference in life expectancy at birth for populations
A
and
B
can be expressed as the net difference of their survivorship functions,

e_{0,A}-e_{0,B}=\int_{0}^{\omega}(l_{A}(x)-l_{B}(x))dx.

(10)

In contrast to the
W_{1}
Wasserstein distance, equation
10
does not reflect absolute differences in survivorship because positive and negative deviations in
l(x)
can cancel each other out. This is appropriate when the goal is to measure the difference in the expected number of life years. However, in empirical cases where one population has higher survivorship at all ages,
l_{A}(x)\geq l_{B}(x)
for all
x
, the difference in life expectancy equals the
W_{1}
Wasserstein distance.

3
Results

The following analysis uses period life tables from the
Human Mortality Database
. The data can be downloaded for free after creating an account. The Python code used for conducting the following analysis is available at
github.com/msauerberg
.

3.1
Sampling from the Human Mortality Database

The database includes period life tables for several countries in various years (sometimes going back to the 1800s). Each period life table is available for women, men, and for both sexes combined. It is computationally demanding to compare differences in
e_{0}
and
W_{1}
Wasserstein distance for all possible pairs in the dataset. For this reason, we focus on life tables for both sexes combined and sample 5 000 randomly selected country pairs from the subset. The cross-country comparison refers to one randomly select year, i.e., we do not compare period life tables over time. In addition to
W_{1}
and differences in
e_{0}
, we calculate the non-overlap index - also called Jaccard index -
(
Shi et al. 2022
)
and the Kullback-Leibler divergence for each pair. This allows comparing the
W_{1}
Wasserstein result to two alternatives measures for distributional similarity. One of the 5 000 sampled pairs is Denmark and Belgium in 1887. For this pair, the difference in
e_{0}
equals the
W_{1}
Wasserstein distance (
3.33
). The non-overlap index is
0.14
, while the Kullback-Leibler divergence is
0.02
. The results for all 5 000 pairs is depicted in Figure

4
Conclusion

When the survivorship functions of two populations do not cross, the
W_{1}
Wasserstein distance is equal to the difference in
e_{0}
. In such cases, the comparison of two age-at-death distributions is no longer simply a comparison of means, but rather the solution to an optimal transport problem. This perspective provides a novel interpretation of differences in life expectancy at birth.

The empirical analysis suggests that the discrepancy between the two measures is usually very small. Nevertheless, we do find cases where they diverge. For example, England & Wales and Iceland display very similar mean ages at death in 1849, yet the
W_{1}
Wasserstein distance indicates that their age-at-death distributions differ substantially. This arises because the
W_{1}
distance reflects the
absolute
differences between survivorship functions, whereas the difference in
e_{0}
reflects a
net difference
, where positive and negative deviations in
l(x)
can cancel each other out. The latter is meaningful when the focus is on expected life years, but it does not necessarily capture how different the underlying age-at-death distributions are. For this reason, it is worthwhile to calculate both measures.

In many empirical applications, we can expect survivorship functions not to crossover. For instance, when comparing women and men, or populations with high versus low socioeconomic status. In such cases, differences in
e_{0}
can be directly interpreted as distributional differences.

Finally, it is worth noting that other measures, such as the non-overlap index or the Kullback–Leibler divergence, can also be used to assess distributional differences. Our analysis shows that these measures are highly correlated with the
W_{1}
Wasserstein distance and yield broadly similar conclusions. However, the Wasserstein distance provides a particularly elegant and intuitive way to interpret differences in age-at-death distributions, linking them directly to the framework of optimal transport.
