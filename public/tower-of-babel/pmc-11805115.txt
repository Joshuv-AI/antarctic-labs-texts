Assessment of Condylar Position and Its Association with Unilateral TMD in Patients with Angle’s Class I, II, and III Malocclusions Using CBCT
Authors: Dollchi Mehta, Dhruv Swaminarayan, Nikita Patel, Noor Addeen Abo Arsheed, Narendra Prakash Rai, Ismat Fakhra
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction:With cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) as a diagnostic tool, we compare and analyze the condylar position and its relationship with unilateral temporomandibular disorder (TMD) in patients with Angle’s Class I, II, and III malocclusions.Materials and Methods:The association between TMD and age, gender, vertical condyle position, horizontal condyle position, and steepness of articular eminence in individuals with various malocclusions was examined using 30 CBCT images.Results:The asymptomatic group shared the concentric location of the condyle in their respective mandibular fossae, which is also a typical hallmark of Class II and Class III malocclusion on the left temporomandibular joint. Patients with TMD were more likely to be in the posterior condylar position. In all malocclusions and TMD cases, the condylar head’s vertical position was mostly normal and slightly elevated. All malocclusions and TMD patients had moderate to severe articular eminence steepness. In individuals with TMD and malocclusions, the mesiodistal breadth of was considerable on the left side.Conclusion:Future research should be done with a larger sample size and an emphasis on assessing how the condyle’s location will change after therapy for patients with TMD.

INTRODUCTION

The term “temporomandibular joint” (TMJ) refers to a ginglymoarthrodial joint made up of articular cartilage, a synovial cavity, and a capsule made of ligaments and synovial fluid that surrounds the joint. It is the mandibular condyle joined to the temporal bone cavity.[1] The TMJ complex is involved in a diverse range of musculoskeletal and neuromuscular problems together referred to as temporomandibular disorders (TMDs).[2] The genesis of TMDs is complex. Condylar position, or the area where the mandibular condyle and glenoid fossa meet at maximal intercuspation, is impacted by a variety of dynamic factors, including changes in occlusion, remodeling and growth, and physiological adaptations.[3]

There are several restrictions with the various imaging modalities utilized to assess the TMJ, including computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and conventional radiography. Cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) is a useful alternative imaging modality that provides effective three-dimensional joint imaging that may help with therapy. Compared to CT and MRI, it is less expensive, exposes patients to less radiation, is conveniently accessible, and is patient-compliant.[4]

MATERIALS AND METHODS

After receiving ethical approval from Sai Tirupati University, the research was carried out at the Department of Oral Medicine, Diagnosis and Radiology, Pacific Dental College and Hospital, Debari, Udaipur.

The study involved 30 CBCT scans taken between June 2022 and March 2024 on willing participants aged 16–60 years, with full FOV 17 × 11 (83 kV, 4.0 mA, 11.30 s) and a voxel size of 300 microns. All subjects were diagnosed with unilateral TMD and had Angle’s Class I, II, and III malocclusions. Conversely, participants with congenital abnormalities, radiation exposure contraindications, history of trauma to the maxillofacial region, systemic disorders that may affect the TMJ, severe attrition, and morphologically altered teeth were not allowed to participate in the study. The diagnostic technique that was used was the Diagnostic Criteria for Temporomandibular Disorders (DC/TMD).[5] For patients in the usual occlusion posture, a CBCT scan was performed. Carestream Image View was used to gather and analyze data.

RESEARCH PLAN

The research used a cross-sectional design. Three groups were created out of the whole sample pool.

Ten patients each from Angle’s Class I, Class II, and Class III with unilateral TMD were divided into three groups: Group A, Class I patients, Class II patients, and Class III patients.

For the secondary reconstruction, the axial image with the condylar head’s largest mesiodistal distance was selected as the reference view. The anterior TMJ space is the shortest path from the glenoid fossa’s anterior boundary to the condylar head’s anterior surface, whereas the posterior TMJ space is the shortest path from the glenoid fossa’s posterior border to the anterior surface of the condylar head.

The following formula was used to examine the condylar position: PULLINGER AND HOLLENDER’S

Condylar ratio is equal to P + A/P-A* × 100, where P and A represent the posterior and anterior joint spaces, respectively. The condyle’s position is regarded as follows:

Condylar ratio value Placement: ±12% Concentric, < -12% Lateral, > +12% Forward

The level of prominence in an area and steepness of the prominence were taken into consideration: 3–60°, moderate; 60–90°, severe; and 5–30°, mild.

DISCUSSION

The TMD group’s mean age in this research was found to be 33.7 ± 11.36 years. Two age peaks for patients seeking therapy for TMD were reported by Manfredini et al.[6]: 30–35 years old and 50–55 years old. However, in patients with unilateral TMD, we did not observe a gender link in the angle Class I, II, and III malocclusions. According to Marcelo Reis Fraga,[7] there are variations in the focal connection between normal occlusion and malocclusion types. In contrast to our investigation, we discovered that patients with right-sided TMD and male patients had larger posterior condylar positions in class III malocclusions.

Furthermore the distribution of centricity varies for both TMD sides on the right and left, with the left side having a larger percentage of posterior condylar ratio than the right. The comparatively large P values for both TMD sides and malocclusion kinds suggest that the condylar ratio and any of these variables may not be significantly correlated.

According to our research, there was a greater frequency of severe articular eminence steepness in cases of Class I malocclusion. There was a small increase in the incidence of severe articular eminence steepness in class II and III malocclusion. The majority of cases showed moderate to severe steepness. On both sides, there was a greater prevalence of moderate to severe steepness, with a slightly larger percentage on the left. These results provide credence to the theory that one etiologic factor contributing to the development of disk displacement is a steep articular eminence. In contrast, our research showed that patients with right-sided TMD had the maximum vertical distance in normal occlusion.

CONCLUSION

Therefore, further research with a larger sample size and several intuitions should be done to assess how the condyle’s location would alter after therapy for TMD patients.
