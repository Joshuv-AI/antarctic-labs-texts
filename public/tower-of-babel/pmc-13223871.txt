Effects of replacing soybean meal with water-soluble corn protein or cottonseed protein on growth performance, carcass traits, meat quality, serum biochemical indicators, and antioxidant capacity of broilers
Authors: Changzheng Guo, Shiyong Liao, Shourong Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The aim of this study was to evaluate the efficacy of a novel water-soluble corn protein (WCP) as a functional substitute for soybean meal (SBM) in broiler diets, using commercially mature cottonseed protein (CotP) as a reference alternative protein source. The WCP was produced from conventional corn steep liquor with an exceptionally high acid-soluble protein content (49.1%). A total of 504 one-day-old male Arbor Acres broilers were randomly assigned to three isonitrogenous and isocaloric dietary treatments: a basal diet control group (CON), a WCP group (5% of SBM replaced by WCP), and a CotP group (5% of SBM replaced by CotP). Over the 40-day experimental period, broilers fed the WCP diet had growth performance (final body weight, average daily gain, and feed-to-gain ratio), comparable to the CON group, and significantly outperformed the CotP group (P < 0.001). For carcass traits and meat quality, WCP inclusion significantly increased the dressing percentage (P = 0.042) and breast muscle yellowness (b* value, P = 0.004) compared to both CON and CotP groups. Serum biochemical analysis revealed that WCP supplementation significantly elevated the concentrations of total protein, globulin, and high-density lipoprotein (P < 0.05). Additionally, systemic antioxidant capacity was enhanced in the WCP group, evidenced by a significant increase in serum total superoxide dismutase activity (P = 0.049). These findings suggest that the bio-active peptides enriched in WCP during integrated processing provide physiological benefits beyond simple nitrogen supply. In conclusion, dietary replacement of 5% SBM with WCP maintains growth performance while enhancing slaughter yield and antioxidant capacity, demonstrating that WCP is an economically viable and functionally superior protein source for sustainable broiler production.

Introduction

Feed cost accounts for 70% of the total production cost in broiler operations (Mallick et al., 2020). With the ongoing intensification of global poultry production, stable high-quality protein supplies are critical to sustaining efficient industrial systems (Kim et al., 2019). Soybean meal (SBM), with its balanced amino acids and high digestibility, has long been indispensable in broiler nutrition (Ma et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025). However, in recent years, volatile SBM prices driven by global grain production flucuations, trade policy adjustments, and rising energy costs, have raised feeding costs and threatened the sustainability of the poultry industry (Swick, 2011; Zhuang et al., 2025). Consequently, identifying cost-effective, nutritionally adequate alternatives to SBM has become a research priority in animal nutrition (Abasubong et al., 2025).

Among the various alternative protein sources to SBM, cottonseed protein (CotP), a derivative of large-scale cottonseed processing, has emerged as one of the most widely adopted options in practical broiler production (Świątkiewicz et al., 2016; Yu et al., 2023), due to its abundant supply, lower cost, and comparable nutrition to SBM (e.g., high crude protein content). Notably, CotP can safely replace 10–15% of SBM in broiler diets as a cost-effective alternative (Świątkiewicz et al., 2016). However, naturally occurring free gossypol is a major anti-nutrient in cottonseed products, causing hepatotoxicity, impaired growth, and metabolic dysfunction in broilers (Świątkiewicz et al., 2016). Free gossypol levels vary widely (0.02–0.53%, 200–5300 mg/kg) by variety and processing (Nagalakshmi et al., 2007). We used a commercially detoxified CotP (685 mg/kg free gossypol, lower end of reported ranges), ensuring safety and commercial representativeness for broilers. However, detoxified CotP still has limitations, including an imbalanced amino acid profile vs. SBM, reducing nutrient utilization (Świątkiewicz et al., 2016).

Corn steep liquor (CSL), a wet-milling by-product rich in protein, small peptides, and free amino acids, is a promising alternative protein source (Zhang et al., 2021). Global annual CSL production is ∼4.3–4.8 million dry tons, based on starch output (∼88–98 million tons/year, 75% corn) and wet-milling mass balance (Vilpoux and Junior, 2023; Zhang et al., 2021). Importantly, CSL is low-cost, and its use reduces industrial waste environmental impact while providing an economical alternative to SBM.

Nevertheless, high conventional CSL inclusion often causes adverse physiological effects, limiting its practical use (Lee et al., 2025). Dietary inclusion exceeding 10% may suppress the development of immune and digestive organs (Yazdanyar et al., 2015), and induce crop mucosal hyperplasia, intestinal inflammation, and renal tubular necrosis (Ullah et al., 2018). These effects are primarily due to anti-nutrients and non-protein nitrogen-induced metabolic burden (Lee et al., 2025). CotP rather than raw CSL was selected as the comparator because raw CSL’s high moisture and poor nutritional balance make it unsuitable for equivalent substitution, and it is rarely used as a major protein feed in commercial broiler production.

To address these constraints, we developed a novel integrated CSL modification technology (core manufacturer steps: natural fermentation to degrade anti-nutrients, resin adsorption to remove phytic acid, ceramic membrane filtration to eliminate macromolecules, enzymatic hydrolysis, nanofiltration to enrich low-molecular-weight peptides, and concentration/drying). The resulting novel water-soluble corn protein‌ (WCP) is another cost-effective, resource-abundant alternative protein source, with its unit protein cost basically equivalent to that of commercial SBM in China. The proprietary multi-step purification process upgrades CSL nutritional quality, with standardized production, official safety certification and stable industrial scalability for feed application. Therefore, this study compared the effects of 5% WCP versus widely used low-gossypol CotP as partial SBM replacements on broiler growth, slaughter yield, meat quality, protein metabolism, and antioxidant capacity, and evaluated whether WCP offers superior nutritional value and application potential over CotP, providing scientific support for sustainable broiler production.

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

This study was approved by the institutional animal care and use committee of the Jiangsu Institute of Poultry Sciences (Permit Number: CNP20220323).

Animals, experimental design, and management

A total of 504 healthy 1-day-old male Arbor Acres broilers with similar initial body weight (40 ± 2 g) were randomly assigned to three treatments. Each treatment contained 7 independent biological replicates (pens), with 24 birds per pen. Each pen was regarded as one experimental unit. The control group (CON) was fed a standard corn-SBM basal diet. For the two experimental groups, SBM was replaced with either 5% WCP group or CotP group, respectively, while ensuring that all dietary treatments were isonitrogenous and isocaloric. The 5% inclusion level of WCP was selected based on our previous laying hen study, which verified the optimal efficacy of WCP at approximately 5% inclusion. The WCP used in this study was a commercial product provided by Zhucheng Haotian Pharm Co., Ltd., China. All broilers were reared in a floor-based housing system. The chicken house and associated equipment were thoroughly cleaned and disinfected one week prior to chick placement. During the initial rearing phase, the ambient temperature inside the house was maintained at approximately 34°C. From day 4 onwards, the temperature was reduced by 1°C every 48 h until a stable temperature of around 23°C was achieved. Throughout the experimental period, broilers had ad libitum access to feed and water. Routine immunization against diseases, sanitation cleaning, and disinfection protocols were strictly implemented, and all husbandry practices were performed in accordance with the standard broiler management guidelines.

Nutritional ingredients, pH and acid-binding capacity

The nutritional components of the feed ingredients, including SBM, WCP, and CotP, were determined according to the following standards: dry matter (GB/T 6435-2014) (General Administration of Quality Supervision, inspection and quarantine of the People’s Republic of China, 2014), crude protein (GB/T 6432-2018) (General Administration of Quality Supervision, inspection and quarantine of the People’s Republic of China, 2018a), crude ash (GB/T 6438-2007) (General Administration of Quality Supervision, inspection and quarantine of the People’s Republic of China, 2007), crude fiber (GB/T 6434-2022) (General Administration of Quality Supervision, inspection and quarantine of the People’s Republic of China, 2022), crude fat (GB/T 6433-2006) (General Administration of Quality Supervision, inspection and quarantine of the People’s Republic of China, 2006), acid-soluble protein (GB/T 22492-2008) (General Administration of Quality Supervision, inspection and quarantine of the People’s Republic of China, 2008), total phosphorus (GB/T 6437-2018) (General Administration of Quality Supervision, inspection and quarantine of the People’s Republic of China, 2018b), and amino acids (method 994.12, AOAC, 2005). Potassium, calcium, sodium, and magnesium were measured according to GB 5009.268—2016 (General Administration of Quality Supervision, inspection and quarantine of the People’s Republic of China, 2016).

The pH and acid-binding capacity at pH 3 (ABC‑3) of the diet was determined according to previous studies (Lawlor et al., 2005; Zhang et al., 2022). In brief, 20 g of the sample were mixed with 40 mL of deionized water, and stirring the mixture for 10 min at a constant speed using a magnetic stirrer (C-MAG HS 7 digital; IKA, Staufen, Germany). The pH value was then measured with a precision pH meter (MP 220 Mettler Toledo, Giessen, Germany). The ABC‑3 was assessed by weighing 100 g of air-dried diet into a 500 mL beaker and adding 200 mL of deionized water. The beaker was placed in a thermostatic water bath (Memmert WB 14, Schwabach, Germany) maintained at 37 ± 1 °C, and the temperature was monitored in real-time with a digital thermometer (TESTO 110, Testo AG, Lenzkirch, Germany). Under continuous magnetic stirring, the suspension was titrated with 1 mol/L hydrochloric acid standard solution until the pH stabilized at 3.0. All above measurements were conducted with three technical replicates.

Growth performance

Birds were accurately weighed and recorded on a replicate basis at 08:00 on day 1 and day 40 post-hatch, respectively. Morbidity and mortality rates of birds in each group were observed and documented daily throughout the experiment. Total feed supplied to each replicate of all groups was recorded from day 1 to day 40. In the event of bird mortality in a replicate, the weight of residual feed was promptly measured, and recording of subsequent feed supply was restarted to ensure calculation accuracy. Based on the above records, average daily gain (ADG), average daily feed intake (ADFI), feed-to-gain ratio (F/G), and mortality rate of birds in each group during the 1–40 d experimental period were calculated.

Carcass traits

All carcass-related measurements were conducted on day 40. Before sampling, all broilers were subjected to 12 h overnight fasting with free access to water. One bird per replicate (n = 7 per treatment) with body weight close to the average weight of the corresponding pen was selected as the sampled individual. All birds were handled under unified and quiet environmental conditions, and humanely euthanized by jugular vein exsanguination. After bleeding, live weight before slaughter, hot carcass weight, eviscerated carcass weight, semi-eviscerated carcass weight, abdominal fat weight, left breast muscle, left leg muscle and left wing muscle weight were measured sequentially. Corresponding percentage indicators were calculated based on live body weight.

Organ index

Organ indices were determined immediately after slaughter on day 40. The same sampled bird per replicate for carcass assessment was used for organ collection. The thymus, spleen, bursa of Fabricius, proventriculus, gizzard, heart and liver were rapidly isolated and weighed. The organ index was calculated as: Organ index (%) = [Organ weight (g) / Live body weight before slaughter (g)] × 100.

Relative intestinal length and weight

The entire small intestine was dissected immediately after slaughter, with pancreatic tissue and adipose tissue adherent to the intestinal wall removed. Each intestinal segment was fixed and measured for its length. Subsequently, the intestinal contents were scraped off with a glass slide, and the segments were weighed. The relative length and relative weight of the duodenum, jejunum and ileum were calculated separately, with the calculation formulas as follows: Relative length of intestinal segment (cm/kg) = Length of intestinal segment (cm) / Live body weight of bird (kg), Relative weight of intestinal segment (g/kg) = Weight of intestinal segment (g) / Live body weight of bird (kg).

All carcass, organ, and intestinal measurements were conducted blind to dietary treatments after random re‑coding of birds.

Meat quality

After slaughter and dissection, the left breast muscle was uniformly sampled for meat quality determination, with all sampling operations conducted under consistent experimental conditions to ensure uniformity. The lightness (L*), redness (a*), and yellowness (b*) values of breast muscle color were measured using an automatic colorimeter (Model SP62; 8 mm diameter aperture, Illuminant A, 10° observer; X-Rite, Inc., Grand Rapids, MI, USA). Drip loss was determined via the filter paper press method. For pH measurement, a small incision was made on the muscle surface with a scalpel, and a portable pH meter was used to determine the pH value of the breast muscle. Triplicate measurements were taken at three separate test points on each muscle sample, and the average value was calculated for statistical analysis. The specific determination methods for the above indices were carried out in accordance with the Chinese National Standard NY/T 1333-2017. The shear force of muscle was determined following the protocols specified in the Chinese National Standard NY/T 1180-2006.

Serum biochemical indices

Before slaughter on day 40, blood samples were collected from the brachial vein of the selected fasting birds. Serum was separated by centrifugation under standardized conditions and stored temporarily for subsequent detection. The concentrations of total protein (TP), globulin (GLO), albumin (ALB), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (T-CHO), glucose (GLU), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) in the serum were determined using an automatic biochemical analyzer.

Antioxidant capacity

Serum, liver and jejunal mucosa samples were collected immediately after slaughter. The activities of glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), total superoxide dismutase (T-SOD), and catalase (CAT), as well as the malondialdehyde (MDA) content and total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC) in serum, liver, and jejunum, were determined using commercial assay kits. All the assay kits used in the present study were purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute Co., Ltd.

Statistical analysis

Experimental data were collated using Microsoft Excel and subsequently subjected to statistical analysis with SPSS software (version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. All results are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), where P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant and P < 0.01 as highly significant.

Growth performance

The effects of dietary WCP as a SBM replacement on broiler growth performance are presented in the Table 5. Overall, WCP replacement did not adversely affect key growth performance parameters when compared with the CON group. At 40 d of age, the body weight of broilers in the WCP group was comparable to that of the CON group, and was higher than that of the CotP group (P < 0.001). During the 1–40 d experimental period, ADG and ADFI in the WCP group did not differ from the CON group, while being higher than those in the CotP group (P < 0.001). Discussion

Previous studies on CSL in broiler nutrition have been limited to the application of roughly processed CSL products (either dried concentrate or liquid raw CSL), which have been widely reported to have adverse effects at high inclusion levels (Lee et al., 2025). For instance, inclusion levels exceeding 10%–15% can induce hepatorenal toxicity, intestinal mucosal damage, and growth inhibition in broilers (Waldroup et al., 1970; Ullah et al., 2017), while liquid CSL can only be used as an acidic feed additive at an extremely low dosage (1–2 cc/kg diet) and cannot serve as a protein source to replace SBM (Hamidifard et al., 2023). These inherent defects and application limitations of conventional CSL severely restrict its large-scale application as a main protein feedstuff in broiler diets. The present study employed a novel CSL (WCP) instead of conventional CSL to evaluate its feasibility as a replacement for SBM and compare its performance with that of CotP.

In the present study, WCP showed distinct nutritional characteristics compared with traditional CSL (Ullah et al., 2018). Its amino acid profile was more balanced than that of traditional CSL, with higher contents of key essential amino acids such as lysine and sulfur-containing amino acids, and the proportion of each essential amino acid was more consistent with the nutritional requirements of broilers. The acid-soluble protein content of WCP reached 49.1%, which was considerably higher than that of SBM (1.47%) and CotP (3.96%). These components exist mainly in the form of small-molecular-weight peptides and free amino acids (Marais and Evanwell, 1983; Hou et al., 2022), which may improve nutrient digestibility and utilization in broilers (Hou et al., 2022). Given that the inherent pH of WCP was lower than that of SBM and CotP, when formulated into broiler diets, the dietary pH and ABC-3 of the WCP diet were significantly lower than those of the CON and CotP diets. These changes could help optimize gastrointestinal environmental conditions, which may be conducive to digestive enzyme activity in broilers (Abd El-Ghany, 2024; He et al., 2025).

The growth performance results indicated that replacing SBM with WCP did not compromise key growth parameters of broilers, with body weight, ADG, ADFI, F/G and EPI all comparable to those of the CON at 40 d of age. This outcome reflected the favorable nutritional profiles of WCP, which underpin efficient nutrient utilization to support broiler growth, an observation aligned with the findings of Russo et al. (Russo et al., 1960), who reported that fermented CSL improved feed efficiency relative to its unfermented counterpart, thus validating the value of modified CSL products in optimizing digestive conditions and growth performance in broilers (Lee et al., 2025). In contrast, the WCP group exhibited significantly superior growth performance to the CotP group, with higher body weight, ADG and EPI. This advantage confirms that processed WCP is a viable and effective alternative protein ingredient for broiler diets.

Carcass trait results demonstrated that replacement of SBM with WCP exerted no adverse effects on most slaughter parameters of 40-day-old broilers, yet significantly elevated the dressing percentage relative to the CON and CotP groups, which is consistent with the findings of relevant studies on modified protein feedstuffs (Chachaj et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2024). Both enzymatic hydrolysis and fermentation can degrade macromolecular proteins into absorbable small peptides and free amino acids (Eugenio et al., 2022; Malyar et al., 2024; Liao et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025), which may improve broiler dressing percentage by enhancing muscle protein synthesis and deposition (Chachaj et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2024). Similar to these modified protein sources, WCP is abundant in acid-soluble protein in the form of small peptides and free amino acids, which maybe facilitate nutrient absorption and muscle protein synthesis and deposition, thereby increasing the dressing percentage. Notably, no previous studies on conventional CSL have reported the beneficial effect of CSL-derived products on broiler carcass traits.

The meat quality results indicated that the only significant difference was the elevated yellowness value (b*) in the WCP group compared with the CON and CotP groups, which was presumably related to natural pigment substances derived from corn by-products. Similar phenomena have been reported in previous studies on corn processing materials (Rausch et al., 2006; Jiao et al., 2022), and this moderate increase in yellowness is acceptable and may provide certain commercial advantages for poultry meat products. Elevated yellowness enhances consumer appeal and marketability, particularly in Asia, Mexico and parts of the United States, where yellow skin is associated with healthier, higher‑quality and better‑tasting meat (Bilgili et al., 1998; Muñoz-Díaz et al., 2012; Wu et al., 2021). No negative changes in meat pH, water-holding capacity or shear force were observed, indicating that WCP addition had no detrimental impact on meat quality..

The serum biochemical results showed that, despite identical crude protein levels across the three diets, the WCP group had significantly higher serum TP, GLO and HDL than the CON group. The increased serum TP and GLO in the WCP group may be linked to the high digestibility of WCP-derived peptides and free amino acids, which could promote protein metabolism and humoral immune status (Eugenio et al., 2022).

Meanwhile, the elevated HDL level suggested mild beneficial regulation of lipid metabolism. Liver function-related indices in the WCP group remained within a normal physiological range and were comparable to the control, verifying the safety of 5% WCP supplementation with no obvious hepatic adverse effects. Regarding intestinal parameters, only the relative length of the jejunum in the WCP group was significantly lower than that in the CON and CotP groups, which is likely attributed to the numerically higher body weight of the WCP group. By contrast, the CotP group exhibited significantly different serum ALT, AST, and AST/ALT ratio values, which might be associated with anti-nutritional factors in cottonseed protein (Cao et al., 2025). This further confirms that the deep processing technology completely eliminates the toxic and side effects of conventional CSL, making WCP a safe and high-quality protein feedstuff for broilers.

The antioxidant capacity results showed that dietary WCP inclusion moderately increased plasma T-SOD activity. This beneficial change could be related to the unique protein composition and bioactive fractions of WCP (Lee et al., 2025). Combined with current data, WCP exerted a limited but positive effect on circulating antioxidant capacity. Nevertheless, the upstream regulatory pathways cannot be fully clarified based only on serum antioxidant indicators in the present study.

Conclusion

The WCP is rich in acid-soluble protein and may be associated with enhanced protein metabolism, as reflected by higher serum TP and GLO levels. Under the current experimental conditions, partial SBM replacement with WCP did not negatively affect growth performance, organ development or hepatic function in 40-day-old broilers, while increasing dressing percentage and moderately improving plasma antioxidant capacity. Nevertheless, this study only evaluated a single 5% inclusion level of WCP. The specific mechanisms responsible for improved serum protein indicators, carcass yield and antioxidant capacity remain unclear and require further investigation. Collectively, at the tested dosage, WCP shows promising potential as a feasible partial alternative protein source for broiler diets.
