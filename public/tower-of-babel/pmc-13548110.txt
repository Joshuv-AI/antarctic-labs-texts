Unobvious diagnosis: subcoracoid impingement. Current knowledge about this condition
Authors: Szymon Stupnicki, Mikołaj Zakryś, Jędrzej Jabłoński, Mikołaj Lewandowski, Jan Niziński, Przemysław Lubiatowski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Subcoracoid impingement (SCI) is one of the possible causes in anterior shoulder pain that can be misdiagnosed or missed. It results from a conflict between the coracoid process and the lesser tuberosity of the humeral head.The collision mainly affects the subscapularis tendon that is at risk of degeneration and tearing.The symptoms arise by positioning the shoulder in flexion, adduction and internal rotation, dynamically decreasing the coracohumeral distance, the most important diagnostic parameter in the SCI.The impingement has been associated with shoulder overuse that may occur in specific sport activities, such as freestyle swimming and overhead throwing.Ultrasound scan, MRI and arthroscopy may help in the identification of pathology. Although not suitable for soft tissue imaging, X-ray may be helpful in detecting bony factors contributing to SCI.The first and main line of treatment is usually conservative, but when it fails, bursectomy followed by mild coracoplasty is a safe and effective method. Patients may also need a repair of the subscapularis tears.

Introduction

We know very well that impingement around the shoulder area exists both externally (between coracoacromial arch and humeral tuberosities) and internally (between glenoid rim and proximal humerus segments). Yet, we do not clearly understand what the real clinical significance of these conditions is. It may well be a normal contact between the tissues or clear conflict of soft tissues against osseous structures that we could, for example, observe on dynamic ultrasound (US) imaging. In the latter scenario, patients may not express any symptoms. On the other hand patients have shoulder pain in different locations, and no clear pathology and obvious mechanical collision can be detected. Among different impingement syndromes around the shoulder, subcoracoid impingement (SCI) associated with anterior shoulder pain is probably the least known compared to subacromial impingement (SIS) and internal impingement of the shoulder, which are well-described and recognized pathologies (1). However, SCI poses a real issue in shoulder biomechanics. Since overlapping symptoms and general location is similar to other shoulder conditions, such as SIS, SCI can easily be overlooked or misdiagnosed. Therefore, these conditions should be carefully differentiated.

SCI was described for the first time by Goldthwait in 1909 (2). It is associated with anterior shoulder pain that results from excessive and repetitive impingement of the tissues between the lesser tuberosity of the humerus and the coracoid process (CP) (subcoracoid bursa, subscapularis tendon and long head of the biceps (LHB)). Both impingement and resulting symptoms are most prominently provoked by flexion, adduction and internal rotation of the shoulder (3). They have been associated with the overuse of shoulder in specific occupations or sports (swimmers and overhead throwers) (1, 4). Aging, overuse and/or possibly genetic or metabolic factors can be the causes of SCI; it may progress from no clear morphological changes, through tendinosis and inflammation, up to degenerative tendon failure and even static narrowing of coracohumeral distance (CHD). Coexisting tears of the LHB and subscapularis tendons (SSCs) have been described (5, 6, 7). It has been shown that the latter tendon is affected more frequently in the population than previously believed (8). In the absence of a noticeable acute injury, it is possible that SCI contributes to the degenerative rupture of SSC, thereby influencing the increased epidemiology of such tears. In light of the above, with a limited number of studies covering the topic of SCI, it is worth taking a close look at this pathology and summarizing the knowledge about it. Therefore, this study aimed to search the current literature concerning the topic of SCI and to perform a narrative review. For this purpose, we covered different subjects about this pathology, such as etiology, clinical diagnosis, diagnostic imaging features and treatment options.

Materials and methods

Several databases were searched (PubMed, ScienceDirect and BioMed Central) with the phrase ‘subcoracoid impingement’. As this study was designed as a narrative review, the literature search aimed to identify relevant publications rather than follow a strict systematic review protocol. Studies from the past 25 years (since 2000), in English, were included in this review, with emphasis on the more recent literature and studies providing clinically meaningful insights. The majority of them were published in the past 10 years (from 2015). We attempted to provide a comprehensive description with the current knowledge about SCI, starting from its origins and ending with treatment. Most of the studies we have found focused on imaging diagnostics and etiology. Few of them addressed the topic of treatment. We utilized studies of all levels of evidence; however, we primarily focused on level I and II studies (systematic reviews, RCTs and prospective cohort studies).

Etiology

SCI syndrome is considered a painful condition resulting from chronic and excessive contact of the SSC and the bursal structures between the CP and the lesser tuberosity (1, 3, 6, 9, 10). The so-called subcoracoid space is limited anteriorly by the posterior surface of the tip of CP with superior extension toward coracoacromial ligament and inferior extension into conjoined tendon and posteriorly by anterior part of proximal humerus (mostly lesser tuberosity). The space contains the humeral head articular cartilage, joint capsule, SSC, and subcoracoid bursa and must be sufficient to allow these structures to glide freely (3). The proximity of the LHB and its sheath cannot be overlooked. The dimension of this interval is defined by CHD, an important factor in SCI development as its narrowing is directly linked to the severity of SCI (3, 11). SCI produces pain in the anterior aspect of the shoulder, especially on flexion, adduction and internal rotation, that is, when the CHD is the narrowest (1, 4, 10). Narrowed CHD, due to either anatomical variations or repetitive microtrauma, leads to increased mechanical stress on the SSC, making it prone to degeneration and tearing (3, 12, 13).

There are several potential causes of the CHD restriction: age-related causes, causes associated with shoulder kinematics, CP morphology, previous degenerative tears, rotator cuff insufficiency and iatrogenic causes. In addition, SCI is provoked by postural habits and provocative positions causing anterior scapular tilt (10, 14).

SCI in athletes

The literature on the prevalence of SCI in athletes compared to SIS is limited. However, SCI is recognized as a cause of shoulder pain in overhead throwing activities and in freestyle swimmers (1, 4). Freestyle swimmers are particularly at risk of developing SCI because of their cyclic overhead motion of upper extremities provoking repetitive microtrauma and due to general hyperlaxity (1). Despite cautious literature review, we are not able to find any evidence of the prevalence of SCI in the weight lifting population, although it seems logical to occur in this group as is the experience of the senior author. However, the literature presents a case of a rock climber who developed an SCI due to hypertrophy of the SSC (15). Therefore, SCI in this population should also be taken into consideration in case of anterior shoulder pain.

Anatomical variations

Schulz et al. (13) concluded that the position of the CP tip may serve as a predictor of the rotator cuff tear site. They found that 78% of shoulders with high CP tip position (type II coracoid) had SSC tear (P < 0.0001) and 86% of shoulders with low CP tip position (type I coracoid) had supraspinatus (SSP) tendon tear (P = 0.0002). It may seem illogical at first but has a mechanical explanation. Inferiorly directed CP tip indicates a subacromial space restriction at the site of the greater tuberosity due to coracoacromial arch morphology alteration. In contrast, the superiorly positioned CP tip reduces the anterosuperior humeral head stability, thus inducing mechanical stress on the SSC, which is subjected to greater overload (13). The CP shows a variation in morphology and may present as flat and hooked and with osteophytes on the tip (3, 14). This anatomical diversity, especially hooked CP, which is more commonly present in older patients, decreases the CHD, thus aggravating SCI symptoms (3, 16). The most serious consequence of this CP morphologic type is SSC tear (6).

Biomechanical factors

A study by Gumina et al. identified three morphologic types of CP, and the most common type (type I, 45%, n = 204) was associated with the most significant restriction of CHD. However, they also stated that a narrowing of the CHD is not sufficient to diagnose SCI. The authors pointed out other factors contributing to the development of the impingement, such as the prominence of the lesser tuberosity, superior migration of the humeral head and calcifications in the SSC (17, 18). This theory can be supported by a cadaveric study by Radas and Pieper. According to the authors, SCI is not necessarily caused by morphologic changes of the CP but rather by a functional problem, such as anterior instability, which leads to functional CHD restriction (19). Some other researchers also found anterosuperior migration of the humeral head in rotator cuff-deficient shoulders, which may reduce the subcoracoid space, aggravate the symptoms of the SCI and predispose to SSC tear (4, 9). On the other hand, deficiencies in SSC, infraspinatus and teres minor create muscle imbalance, causing superior migration of the humeral head through the impairment of downward forces, stabilizing the shoulder girdle (20). We hypothesize that SCI and SIS create a mechanical sequence of events and coexist. By causing SSC degeneration or tears, SCI may predispose to SIS.

Overall, the CHD narrowing may be due to repetitive impingement that causes microtrauma and subsequent proliferation of the collagen fibers and result in soft tissue thickening. Prior shoulder injuries (especially SSP tears) or degenerative conditions may alter the shoulder kinematics, which lead to muscle imbalances, further destabilizing the shoulder (4).

It seems that there are two opposing theories on the etiology of the SCI: anatomic, comprising CP morphology and its direction and functional, which focuses on muscle imbalances; rotator cuff deficiency and anterior instability. On the other hand, these two theories may complement each other and coexist.

Iatrogenic SCI

The iatrogenic SCI should be taken into consideration when surgically manipulating around the shoulder. Gerber et al. emphasized that surgeons should respect the anatomical relationship between the humeral head and the CP so as to avoid iatrogenic SCI (14).

Clinical examination

It is worth noting that from 2015 to the current date, there have been no publications that focused on a clinical examination in the diagnosis of SCI. Therefore, we used previous literature that described this issue.

Combinations of tests are probably more reassuring, relying on some to provoke the symptoms and others to alleviate them. On top of clinical examination, imaging plays an important role for a full clinical picture. We do have in mind that existing tendon tears may not indicate the source of the patient’s symptoms. Nevertheless, a thorough physical examination of the patient’s shoulder and specific orthopedic tests are essential and fundamental in the diagnosis of SCI.

It is recognized that pain in this condition occurs when the SSC impinges between the coracoid and lesser tuberosity of the humerus. Typically, dull pain in the anterior portion of the shoulder with SCI is increased when the shoulder is in a forward flexion, adduction and internal rotation. We should ask the patient when the pain occurs or whether the patient’s daily activities involve repeated actions mentioned above. In addition, it is typical for pain to occur at the palpation of the soft tissues around the CP and between the CP and the lesser tuberosity (21). In order to avoid palpating the LHB, the shoulder is placed in a neutral or slightly external position to move LHB out of the coracoid area (Fig. 1). Modified Hawkins test, reflecting the pathomechanics of SCI, can be used for provocation of symptoms. The test is similar to the Kennedy–Hawkins impingement sign with some differences. In order to bring the lesser tuberosity in contact with the coracoid, the patient’s shoulder is placed in cross-arm adduction, forward elevation and internal rotation (Fig. 2) (21, 22, 23).

Palpation of the CP and nearby soft tissues. The shoulder is placed in different positions of rotation, aiming finally at a neutral or slightly external rotation.

Modified Hawkins test. The shoulder is placed in cross-arm adduction, forward elevation and internal rotation.

It is important to note that SSC tears or LHB pathology may be an accompanying issue with SCI. Therefore, specific tests, such as the bear hug, internal rotation lag sign, belly press and lift-off tests or LHB-related tests, should also be performed where there is suspicion of SCI. However, they should not be implemented individually but in combination as it enhances reliability (23).

The senior author uses local anesthetic injections under US guidance directly under CP into the bursal area with immediate pain relief confirming diagnosis (Figs 3, 4, 5). This method is supported by the literature as a diagnostic aid (14, 20, 24, 25). In the case of no relief, injection can be repeated into other locations (LHB sheath, subacromial space, and AC joint). Diagnosis of SCI is also supported by excluding other sources of pain using a standard battery of shoulder tests. Particular attention should be paid to scapular position and possible dyskinesia. This includes a thorough observation of scapular movement and scapular assistance tests (20). In the case of professional athletes, a thorough clinical and biomechanical assessment is necessary, including diagnosis of any other tissue conflicts, especially internal impermanence of rotational deficits (GIRD). Posterolateral internal impingement seems to be easily distinguished, for which the pain occurs mostly in late cocking position (abduction and external rotation) and is localized posteriorly. The real challenge is to differentiate the pain with anterior-superior impingement that has been described but is even less known. It is the one occurring on the gleno-humeral side (lesser tuberosity conflicting with anterosuperior glenoid) and probably leading to intra-articular damage (26).

Imaging

Imaging in SCI mainly involves taking basic plain radiographs, US and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Most of the current studies in this area aim to verify whether structural changes in the shoulder are considered to be the etiological factors of SCI. Studies focusing on the diagnosis of SCI often rely on differentiating parameters from the MRI and contrasting them retrospectively with videos of the arthroscopies. MRI studies evaluating SCI focused mainly on parameters such as CHD, coracoid overlap (CO), lesser tubercle cysts (LTCs), acromiohumeral distance (AHD), subscapularis tendon slip number (STSN), coracoid index (CI), height of the lesser tuberosity (HLT), coraco-glenoid angle (CGA), coracohumeral angle (CHA), width of the subscapularis tendon (WST), CP tip, coraco-glenoid distance (CGD), coraco-coracoid base angle (CBA) and coracoid obliquity (11, 16, 27, 28).

MRI may reveal lesions of SSC, inflammatory changes in bursa or LHB, but also osteoarthritic changes (cartilage loss and osteophytes) (Figs 6, 7, 8, 9). Porter et al. in their work demonstrated that an increased vertical distance of the CP tip below the level of the supraglenoid tubercle correlates with SSC tendinopathy. The researchers concluded that the vertical distance relating to CP tip position is more useful in the imaging diagnosis of anterior rotator cuff pathologies than axial CHD. In addition, it has been reported that the position of the CP tip affects the entire shape of the rotator cuff outlet, not just the narrowing of the subcoracoid outlet affecting only the SSC (29). The study by Yu et al. analyzed various diagnostic parameters of SCI, of which CHD showed the highest diagnostic accuracy. Combining CHD with HLT significantly improved the diagnostic performance, while adding CI to the combination did not significantly improve it (30).

Cetinkaya et al. presented a study on the evaluation of LTC of humeral heads assessed by MRI and juxtaposed them with SCI and SSC tears evident on arthroscopic surgery videos. A retrospective evaluation of the MRI focused on measuring LTC and CHD. The researchers found a strong association of LTCs with SSC tears, but did not show one between LTCs and the SCI (28). In their next publication, Cetinkaya et al. examined the STSN and CP morphology with MRI in patients with and without SSC tears. According to this study, CO was the most useful parameter predicting SCI. The CBA is the angle between the posteroinferior surface of the CP and the anterior surface of the base of the CP, while CGD is the shortest distance measured from the most inferior tip of the CP to the anterior rim of the glenoid. It has been found in the study that CGD and CBA reduce CHD, thus predicting SCI. However, the CHD measurements were found to be insignificant in SCI diagnosis, contrary to previous reports. CGD and CBA alone were found to be of marginal use in predicting SCI using MRI (27).

There are differences in the literature on whether CHD is a good prognostic indicator for SCI. In publications by Yu et al., researchers indicated that CHD has the highest diagnostic accuracy (30). However, in a publication by Cetinkaya et al., researchers concluded that CO is the most valuable parameter indicative of SCI (27). Nevertheless, in indicating SCI, CHD is too variable and too nonspecific to predict this condition on its own. Another significant problem was that it remains difficult to establish a reliable cutoff point for CHD. Cetinkaya et al. emphasized that CHD assessed by MRI is dependent on arm rotation and scapular position. Therefore, even a minimal rotation may alter the measurement. This results in high measurement variability and limited repeatability. However, in studies that consider CHD useful in diagnosis, this measurement was often analyzed in a provocative position (internal rotation) and was correlated with arthroscopy, sometimes in dynamic studies. In contrast, Cetinkaya et al. performed MRI in neutral rotation. Examination in a provocative position makes the differences greater and more legible, which translates into greater diagnostic value.

Another possible important reason why Cetinkaya et al. considered CHD to be of little use is that their study did not include a truly healthy control group. The study group consisted of patients with subscapularis tears, whereas the control group included patients with other shoulder pathologies, i.e. the control group also had impaired biomechanics (27). The more heterogeneous the population, the weaker the single geometric parameter. In contrast, in the study by Yu et al., the control group consisted of 100 healthy shoulders, which were compared with 47 shoulders of patients with SCI. In groups constructed in this way, the differences in SCI were more pronounced, and thus, the sensitivity and specificity of CHD were higher (30).

In a study by Kucukciloglu et al., glenoid version, CHD, CGA, CI, sagittal coracoid–glenoid tubercle distance and axial coracoacromial inclination–glenoid version differences were assessed in 298 patients. With increasing age, the CHD and CGA decreased and the incidence of SSC lesions increased. It was observed that the pathology of the SSC was associated with increasing age and male gender. MRI scans demonstrated an average CHD of 11 mm, the lowest recorded value being 4 mm in asymptomatic patients. In patients with shoulder pain and limited motion in this joint, the CHD was measured at an average of 5.5 mm (31).

US is a useful tool for evaluating shoulder impingement syndrome, especially in assessing the thickness of the SSC, coracohumeral ligament (CHL) and CHD. Tracy et al. confirmed that US effectively detects SCI, finding significantly narrower CHD in affected patients (P < 0.0001). The mean CHD in asymptomatic volunteers was 12 mm (32). Ting et al. found that patients with SCI had a thicker CHL and SSC and a higher SSC/CHD ratio compared to controls (9). These findings may help differentiate SCI from SIS. A significant advantage provided by US is the possibility of dynamic assessment and visualizing possible tissue conflicts.

Finally, it should be emphasized that in many cases with anterior shoulder pain, diagnosis of SCI is made entirely by clinical assessment with no particular pathological changes in shoulder imaging. It relies on history, clinical tests, local anesthetic injection and, partly, excluding other sources of pain or pathology. The paucity of recent high-quality studies on clinical examination underscores the need for validated, standardized clinical test clusters for SCI. Without such tools, diagnosis remains heavily reliant on imaging findings and interventional procedures rather than on reproducible bedside assessment. Future research should aim to establish reliable clinical criteria that integrate history, physical examination and selective imaging, thereby improving diagnostic efficiency and reducing unnecessary investigations.

Treatment

No particular and scientifically based treatment algorithm has been yet established with proven effectiveness for SCI. Both nonoperative and operative approaches have been described. As with many chronic nontraumatic shoulder conditions, SCI is addressed by conservative treatment in the vast majority of cases. Surgery (mostly arthroscopic) is indicated for those patients that fail conservative management and have persistent pain during daily activities, especially with advanced stages and more severe tissue pathology (1, 22, 33).

Conservative treatment

The aim of the treatment is to decrease the pain, provide a full, non-painful range of motion and get the patient back to normal daily activity. For the athletes and physically active patients, it usually starts with activity modification and active rest. This does not necessarily require complete cessation of sports activity, but is limited to those pain-provoking movements (especially flexion and internal rotation). No specific conservative therapy has clinical evidence of effectiveness or superiority over another. Yet, based on the lessons learned and basically agreed nonoperative treatment modalities, such as oral NSAIDs, activity modification and physical therapy should be considered first-line management for SCI. Corticosteroid injections provide therapeutic benefits, but can also be used as a diagnostic tool, to confirm or exclude the source of shoulder pain. Another option for pain modulation is locally applied transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) and pulsed US phonophoresis with NSAIDs or glucocorticosteroids (1, 22, 33, 34, 35). Roche et al. in their study based on 12 patients with SCI, of whom 11 were treated with corticosteroid injections in the subcoracoid region in combination with physiotherapy, demonstrated that every patient had symptomatic relief and nobody needed surgery after this type of management (36). In studies about, in some way homologous, SIS, corticosteroids were also shown to be effective, but they should not be used as the sole intervention, without physiotherapy (37, 38). We found no data regarding the utility of PRP in SCI, but in the case of SIS, it is a reasonable option, especially when there is SSP partial tear or tendinitis (39, 40). In the absence of large-scale studies on specific physiotherapy protocols, we referred to a recently published case report. Gamiel et al. in their study described specific therapeutic exercises and manual therapy interventions, which resulted in improvements in pain, function, performance and muscle strength in one-year follow-up. Manual therapy included mobilization of posterior and inferior glide, and thoracic mobilization. Myofascial release was focused on pectoralis major and minor, subscapularis, latissimus dorsi, levator scapulae and upper trapezius. In addition, posterior capsule stretching was performed. As specific exercises for rotator cuff, they implemented shoulder external and internal rotation exercises that were performed in two positions: standing using a theraband with a pillow under the elbow and standing at 90 degrees of abduction. Periscapular muscle strengthening exercises included shoulder extension with a theraband in supine and standing positions, seated row, shoulder extension in prone position and horizontal abduction with external rotation in prone position (35). In summary, physical therapy should focus on joint mobilization, periscapular and rotator cuff muscle stretching and strengthening and range-of-motion exercises if needed. Special attention is focused on scapular control and maintenance of correct posture. Again, in the absence of large-scale studies verifying conservative treatment of SCI, we can say that in case of SIS, the same multimodal physiotherapy approach (based on different types of stretching, strengthening of rotator cuff and scapular muscles and mobilization) is the best option (41, 42, 43, 44, 45). In addition, home exercises are as successful as supervised physiotherapy in treating SIS (42). This is promising as there may be the same potential in SCI treatment, although it should be verified.

As seen in the examples above, the approach to rehabilitation in SCI should be comprehensive. When conservative management fails, surgical management may be necessary (1, 22, 33).

Surgical treatment

Surgical treatment can be considered in patients that fail to respond to conservative interventions. No specific evidence-based treatment algorithm for SCI has been established, but typically as for other painful shoulder conditions, at least a 3- to 6-month period should be effectively devoted before surgery is considered (12, 46). Arthroscopic approach is then typically used. Several issues can be addressed depending on the severity of pathology (mostly based on specific lesions (SSC tears, LHB pathology, and labral tears)). Typically, in early stages when no pathology or tissue failure can be detected, arthroscopic debridement is recommended. Techniques of such interventions were described by Pearce et al. It is a simple and reproducible procedure. Initially, decompression with debridement of the space between the lesser tuberosity and the CP is performed by removing inflammatory tissue from the bursa, adhesions and thickened anterior capsule (Fig. 10). Subsequently, limited coracoplasty may be optionally performed, which includes skeletonizing the posterior surface of the CP using a radiofrequency wand, followed by the removal of a portion of the bone. The purpose of coracoplasty is to enlarge the subcoracoid space and eliminate mechanical compression of the SSC and adjacent structures. The commonly accepted goal is restoration of the CHD to at least 8 mm, which is generally considered adequate to relieve impingement while maintaining safe margins from nearby neurovascular structures (47, 48, 49).

Arthroscopic technique and the portals can be used at the discretion of the surgeon. The senior author usually addresses SCI after the subacromial space has been cleared from the bursa. Then, the camera can be moved to an anterolateral portal to improve visualization and a lower anterolateral portal is used for the shaver (tangentially to SSC). The subcoracoid space can be easily traced along the coracoacromial ligament. After sequential removal of bursa, conjoined tendon can be exposed. Then, the posterior surface of the CP tip is located using a shaver tip and coracoplasty is done if needed. During the procedure, the arm is held in internal rotation, which increases the working space for debridement.

In cases of tissue lesions detected by prior imaging or during the diagnostic part of arthroscopy, additional interventions can be performed. That includes repairing SSC or addressing LHB with tenodesis or tenotomy (50, 51). In cases when the repair of the SSC is needed, coracoplasty could be performed first. This facilitates manipulation around the SSC and prevents compression of the coracoid against the repaired SSC (52).

Based on the available studies on surgical interventions, it remains unclear whenever coracoplasty or rotator cuff repair plays a more significant role in treating SCI. Hodax et al. reported that a CHD of 13.7 mm was the mean value, based on measurements taken from 714 healthy, asymptomatic patients (53). In comparison, Reichel et al. found that a CHD threshold of 9.5 mm had an 84% sensitivity and specificity for predicting SSC tears in their retrospective study (7). Balke et al. mentioned that a CHD smaller than 6 mm is always linked with degenerative SSC tears (54). Based on these results, a reasonable approach would be to consider coracoplasty with subcoracoid decompression (with or without rotator cuff repair) when the CHD value is below the normal range (around 10 mm) in symptomatic patients and when conservative treatment has failed.

Postoperative protocol depends on the extent of the procedure and the underlying pathology addressed. For debridement only, immediate exercises are commenced postoperatively with gradual progression. No formal immobilization is used. However, in case of tissue repair, sling protection is indicated (e.g. SSC repair) (21, 47). It is also recommended to avoid impingement positions of the upper extremity for the first two weeks after the surgery (21). The aim is always regaining full ROM and strength without symptoms. Typical recovery time to return to sport following isolated debridement is 2–4 months based on our observations.

In a previous study evaluating this technique on 28 patients, favorable outcomes were reported with a minimum two-year follow-up. Single-anchor repair of the SSC with biceps tenodesis and coracoplasty (in most patients) resulted in American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) scores of 93.7 in Lafosse type II tears and 86.7 in Lafosse type I tears (50). Similarly, in a two-year follow-up study, Açan et al. evaluated multiple clinical and radiological outcomes before and after coracoplasty combined with SSP repair in 16 patients with SCI. Patients with SSC tears were excluded. The study showed that, postoperatively, the mean Hawkins test pain scores and CO decreased, while strength scores in the bear hug test, the belly press test and the lift-off test; ASES score; internal rotation; and CHD improved (55).

Limitations and future research directions

The main limitation of this study is that it does not adhere to the methodological rigor of systematic reviews, such as PRISMA guidelines. Therefore, a degree of subjectivity in the selection and interpretation of the literature cannot be excluded. In addition, since SCI remains a relatively underexplored clinical entity, the present review is primarily based on small cohort studies and case series. Moreover, several aspects of SCI lack robust evidence in the literature and are instead based on extrapolations from SIS protocols, single case reports and the clinical experience of the senior author. These gaps concern pathomechanisms, diagnosis and treatment, as most available studies focus on SIS rather than other shoulder impingement syndromes. Future research on SCI should include larger patient cohorts and be supported by higher-quality evidence to expand the current knowledge base. Therefore, further studies focusing on SCI, particularly in athletic populations, are strongly encouraged.

Conclusion

Although SCI is less frequently discussed in the literature than SIS, it may represent a significant cause of anterior shoulder pain, particularly in freestyle swimmers and athletes involved in overhead throwing sports. Moreover, it may be associated with SSC tears and the pathology of the LHB. Due to overlapping symptoms and similar anatomical location, as other shoulder conditions, SCI can be easily overlooked or misdiagnosed. Diagnosis is based mostly on clinical judgment with anterior tenderness in the area of coracoid and specific provocation with modified Hawkins test. Exclusion of other causes of shoulder pain is an essential part of the diagnostic workup. Imaging may be helpful, particularly in more advanced cases with reduced CHD and detectable intra-articular soft tissue lesions. Radiological modalities, including MRI, CT and US, remain useful; however, arthroscopy is considered the reference standard for definitive diagnosis. The mainstay of treatment is conservative, including activity modification, physiotherapy and injections. When conservative management fails, surgical treatment may be considered. Arthroscopic bursectomy, debridement and limited coracoplasty represent effective procedures for addressing anterior shoulder pain associated with SCI. These procedures may be combined with rotator cuff repair when indicated, but coracoplasty should be performed first.
