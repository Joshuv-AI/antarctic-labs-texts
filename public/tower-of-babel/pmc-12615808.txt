U shaped association between sleep duration and long term cognitive decline trajectories in a national cohort
Authors: Fei Feng, Zhilong Cai, Jianhua Chen, Diqiong Yang, Nanqu Huang, Yong Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe existing evidence concerning the association between sleep duration and long-term cognitive trajectories remains inconclusive. This cohort study utilized data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) spanning from 2011 to 2020, with biennial surveys conducted. Group-based trajectory modeling identified cognitive trajectories over a 9-year period. Multivariable logistic regression, incorporating multiple imputation techniques to address missing data, assessed the association between sleep duration and long-term cognitive function. The sample comprised 8668 participants with a mean age of 56.3 years, of whom 45.7% were female. Among them, 34.24% exhibited cognitive decline, while 65.76% maintained stable cognition. The odds ratios (OR) for incident cognitive decline were 1.17 (95% CI: 1.04–1.31) for individuals with short sleep duration (< 6 h) and 1.57 (95% CI: 1.31–1.89) for those with long sleep duration (> 8 h). Subsequent analysis revealed a U-shaped relationship between sleep duration and incident cognitive decline (nonlinear, p < 0.001). Specifically, when sleep duration was below 7.23 h, an increase in sleep duration significantly reduced the risk of cognitive decline (OR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.86–0.95). Conversely, when sleep duration equaled or exceeded 7.23 h, an increase in sleep duration significantly elevated the risk of cognitive decline (OR = 1.31, 95% CI: 1.17–1.46). These findings suggest that sleep duration is independently associated with long-term cognitive decline in a U-shaped manner, with a nadir around 7.23 h. Introduction

Global population aging represents one of the most significant demographic transformations of the twenty-first century, with profound implications for healthcare systems worldwide1. The Global Burden of Disease Study 2019 estimates that 57.4 million people worldwide were living with dementia in 2019, a number projected to increase to 152.8 million by 2050, representing a 166% increase2. In China, the proportion of individuals aged 60 and above is expected to reach 28% by 20403. Consequently, age-related cognitive impairment and dementia are rising substantially, contributing to higher rates of disability and mortality among older individuals and exacerbating the burden of long-term care on families and society4.

Sleep disturbances represent a multidimensional risk factor for dementia pathogenesis, with emerging evidence demonstrating that sleep fragmentation, circadian rhythm disruption, and altered sleep architecture significantly increase the risk of Alzheimer’s disease and vascular dementia through neuroinflammatory pathways, blood–brain barrier dysfunction, and impaired glymphatic clearance, while multidimensional sleep health assessments reveal distinct sleep phenotypes associated with differential dementia risk profiles5–7. Among sleep parameters, sleep duration has emerged as a particularly promising target, as it is fundamental to brain health yet remains amenable to intervention through lifestyle modifications8,9. Therefore, understanding the relationship between sleep duration and long-term cognitive trajectories in aging populations holds critical importance for preventing dementia and promoting healthy aging.

Given the absence of effective disease-modifying treatments for dementia, identifying modifiable risk factors has become paramount for prevention strategies10. The complex interplay between sleep duration and cognitive decline necessitates careful consideration of multiple confounding factors. Our analysis incorporated a comprehensive set of covariates, including sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, and health-related variables, to isolate the independent association between sleep duration and cognitive trajectories11,12. This approach is essential in view of their established associations with both sleep patterns and cognitive function in middle-aged and older adults13.

The relationship between sleep duration and cognitive decline remains contentious. While multiple prospective cohorts demonstrate a U-shaped association between sleep duration and cognitive decline, with both insufficient (≤ 4–6 h/night) and excessive sleep (≥ 8–10 h/night) increasing dementia risk compared to optimal 7-h duration9,14,15, conflicting evidence suggests that only prolonged sleep duration (≥ 7.95 h) may paradoxically increase cognitive decline risk in normal elderly, and objective sleep measures indicate that sleep disruption parameters rather than duration may be more predictive of cognitive trajectories, whereas self-reported sleep measures show little association with cognitive function16,17. These inconsistencies may be attributed to variations in study populations, methodological approaches, and follow-up durations18,19.Considering mild cognitive impairment (MCI) as a reversible state between normal cognitive function and dementia, which may revert to normal cognition20,21, underscores the importance of recruiting cognitively normal participants at baseline and utilizing long-term follow-up cohort joint group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) to provide a more accurate depiction of cognitive changes over time.

Therefore, this study investigates the association between baseline sleep duration and cognitive decline trajectories among cognitively normal participants from the nationally representative CHARLS cohort, employing GBTM over a 9-year follow-up period to minimize baseline confounding and accurately characterize heterogeneous cognitive change patterns. We predict that cognitive scores exhibit heterogeneous trajectories with varying rates of deterioration, and normal sleep duration will confer optimal long-term cognitive performance, while both insufficient sleep and prolonged sleep duration will increase the risk of long-term cognitive decline in middle-aged and older adults.

Methods

Study population

The China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) survey is a nationally representative longitudinal study conducted by Peking University among middle-aged and older adults (≥ 45 years) in China. The baseline survey (2011) covered 150 county-level units, 450 village-level units, and 17,708 individuals across 10,257 households in 28 provinces, municipalities, and autonomous regions, and these samples were tracked every 2 or 3 years. More details about CHARLS can be found in previous publications22. The data from 5 waves in 2011 (wave I), 2013 (wave II), 2015 (wave III), 2018 (wave IV) and 2020 (wave V) were used in this study. On the basis of our inclusion and exclusion criteria, We finally analyzed 8668 individuals who did not have cognition impairment (impairment was defined as 1 SD below the mean of cognition score) at baseline and were followed up in the subsequent waves23. Detailed process of participant selection is presented in Fig. 1.

Flowchart of sample selection.

The protocols of CHARLS have been approved by the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052-11,015). Prior to data collection, all participants provided informed consent by signing consent forms.

Measurement

Outcomes

This study evaluated cognitive performance across four domains—orientation, attention, episodic memory, and visuospatial abilities—using established assessment tools from the CHARLS in accordance with previous research methods24. The primary outcome measure was an overall cognition score (range: 0–21) that combined three validated instruments: the 10-item Telephone Interview of Cognitive Status (TICS-10) for orientation and attention, word recall tasks for episodic memory assessment, and figure drawing exercises for visuospatial function evaluation.

TICS‑10 in CHARLS comprises ten items: serial subtraction of 7 from 100 (up to five trials), date (month, day, year), day of the week, and season. The TICS‑10 score is the total number of correct responses and ranges from 0 to 1025,26. Episodic memory is assessed using immediate and delayed recall of a 10‑word list; the episodic memory score is the mean of the immediate and delayed recall counts (each 0–10), yielding a range of 0 to 1026,27. For visuospatial ability, respondents are shown two overlapping pentagons and asked to draw a similar figure; a correct drawing is scored 1, and an incorrect drawing is scored 0.

Exposure

Nighttime sleep duration was assessed by the following question, “During the past month, how many hours of actual sleep did you get at night (average hours for one night)?” Sleep duration was categorized into short (< 6 h/night), normal (6–8 h/night), and long (> 8 h/night)28.

Covariates

The study collected the baseline characteristics of participants from 2011. We assessed sociodemographic information including age, sex (male or female), marital status (married or others including separated, divorced, widowed, never married and cohabited), educational attainment (junior high school and below, high school and career training, higher education) and residence (urban or rural); lifestyle information such as smoking status (non-smoker, former or current), drinking status (non-drinker, former or current), social leisure activity, napping time and total household per capita consumption; and health-related information including BMI, self-reported health status (good or poor), self-reported chronic conditions contain hypertension, diabetes, cancer, chronic lung disease, heart disease, kidney disease, and depressive symptoms that was measured by the 10-item center for epidemiologic studies-depression (CESD-10) scale. The selection of covariates is based on previous literature and substantive reasoning.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were represented as proportions (%) while continuous variables were described by the mean (standard deviation, SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR), as appropriate.

GBTM assumes that the population is heterogeneous and composed of several classes of subjects characterized by a number of mean profiles of trajectories, which fundamental principle is consistent with that of previous similar studies29,30. We applied GBTM to identify subgroups of subjects sharing distinct trajectories of MMSE score within 9 years. To ensure the model’s applicability and simplicity, we construct a group trajectory model by combining indicators of model fitting effectiveness with professional interpretability. The fitting effect parameters include average posterior probability (AvePP), estimated probability (%), odds of correct classification (Occ), Bayesian information criteria (BIC), BIC difference between the two models (ΔBIC), and Relative entropy (Ek).

To compare the differences across groups, one-way analyses of variance (normal distribution), Kruskal–Wallis tests (skewed distribution), and chi-square tests (categorical variables) were under taken. Logistic regression models were used to estimate the odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) for the relationship between sleep duration and cognitive trajectories. Multiple imputation using the chained equations (MICE) algorithm was performed for covariates with missing data rates ≤ 15%31. In addition, restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression was performed with 4 knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of sleep duration to assess linearity and examine the dose–response curve between sleep duration and cognitive trajectories after adjusting variables. We used a two-piece-wise logistic regression model with smoothing to analyze the association threshold between sleep duration and cognitive trajectories after adjusting the variables. The likelihood-ratio test and the bootstrap resampling method were used to determine inflection points. Heterogeneity among subgroups was assessed by multivariate logistic regression, and interactions between subgroups and sleep duration were examined by likelihood ratio testing. We also performed sensitivity analyses using a complete-case analysis. Because the sample size was determined solely on the data provided, no a priori statistical power estimates were performed. All analyses were performed using the statistical software packages R 4.3.2 (http://www.R-project.org, The R Foundation, Shanghai, China), SAS Release 9.4 (SAS Institute) and Free Statistics software version 1.932. A descriptive study was conducted on all participants. By a two-tailed testing, a p-value of < 0.05 was declared significant.

Results

Study population

Initially, 17,708 participants were identified. Following the exclusion criteria, 8,668 patients were ultimately included in the study cohort (Fig. 1). Throughout the follow-up period, 6,930 participants completed the cognitive assessment in the 2013 survey, 6,984 in the 2015 survey, 5,651 in the 2018 survey, and 6,899 in the 2020 survey.

Models were tested from 1 trajectory classes to 4 trajectory classes (shapes from linear, quadratic, and cubic), and two distinct trajectory groups were ultimately identified by our model (Supplementary table s1). Longitudinal cognitive trajectories were progressively different within each group, group 1 (34.24%) represents people with significant cognitive decline, while group 2 (65.76%) showed a stable cognition function (Fig. 2).

The optimal trajectory class of cognition score (n = 8668).

Relationship between sleep duration and cognition

Compared with individuals with normal sleep duration (6–8 h), those with abnormal sleep patterns showed significantly increased risk for cognitive decline over the 9-year follow-up period. Short sleep duration (< 6 h) was associated with a 17% increased odds of cognitive decline (OR = 1.17, 95% CI: 1.04–1.31), while long sleep duration (> 8 h) showed an even stronger association with a 57% increased odds (OR = 1.57, 95% CI: 1.31–1.89) after adjusting for potential confounders (Table 2), respectively. These findings indicate detrimental effects of both insufficient and excessive sleep on cognitive outcomes.

Further analysis revealed a U-shaped relationship between sleep duration and incident cognitive decline (nonlinear, p < 0.001) in RCS (Fig. 3). In the threshold analysis, the OR of cognitive decline was 0.90 (95% CI: 0.86–0.95) in participants with sleep duration less than 7.23 h. This means that the risk of cognition decline is reduced by 10% with every 1 h increase in sleep duration. Conversely, beyond the 7.23-h threshold, longer sleep duration was associated with a 31% increased odds of cognitive decline per additional hour (OR = 1.31, 95% CI:1.17–1.46) (Table 3). The steep increase in risk with oversleeping compared to the moderate protection from adequate sleep suggests that excessive sleep may be particularly harmful to cognitive health.

Stratified analyses

Based on additional variables in several subgroups, stratified analysis was performed to assess potential effect modifications on the relationship between sleep duration and cognitive decline. No significant interactions were found in any subgroups after stratifying by age, sex, and residence (Supplementary Figure s1).

Sensitivity analysis

After excluding individuals with missing covariate data, 6366 individuals remained, and the association between sleep duration and cognitive decline remained stable. Compared to individuals with normal sleep duration, the adjusted OR values of cognitive decline in short and long sleep duration were 1.23 (95% CI:1.08–1.41, p = 0.002), and 1.47 (95% CI: 1.18–1.83, p < 0.001) (Supplementary Table s2), respectively.

Discussion

In this extensive population-based prospective cohort study, we have clearly demonstrated that both short sleep duration (< 6 h) and long sleep duration (> 8 h) are independently associated with a 17% and 57% increase in the risk of cognitive decline, respectively. Notably, analysis utilizing restricted cubic splines revealed a U-shaped relationship between sleep duration and the risk of cognitive decline (P nonlinearity < 0.05), with the nadir of risk at 7.23 h.

Our findings regarding sleep duration align with previous research33,14–16, consistently revealing a U-shaped association with cognitive decline within population-based contexts, even after adjusting for common sociodemographic, lifestyle and health-related information. Intriguingly, the observed effects of sleep duration on incident cognitive decline were modest yet consistent across subgroups categorized by age, race/ethnicity, and smoking status. Furthermore, our results remained generally robust across sensitivity analyses. It is worth noting that while not all studies support this conclusion, the majority of those that do have small sample sizes and relatively short follow-up periods16,17,34,35. Recently, Yanjun Ma et al. conducted an analysis using data from both the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA) and the CHARLS with a 4-year follow-up period9. They found that individuals with insufficient (≤ 4 h) or excessive (≥ 10 h) sleep duration experienced faster global cognitive decline, consistent with our findings.

The U-shaped relationship between sleep duration and cognitive decline can be explained through several interconnected biological mechanisms. Short sleep duration (< 6 h) compromises the brain’s glymphatic system, which is responsible for clearing metabolic waste products including amyloid-beta plaques and tau proteins during sleep36,37. This impaired clearance mechanism accelerates the accumulation of neurotoxic proteins associated with Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias. Additionally, sleep deprivation disrupts synaptic plasticity mechanisms essential for memory formation and consolidation, impairs long-term potentiation, and reduces dendritic spine density. Chronic sleep loss also triggers persistent neuroinflammatory responses, elevating pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6) that directly damage neural tissue and further impair cognitive function38.Beyond these direct neurobiological effects, sleep restriction interferes with the sleep-dependent replay of neural activity patterns necessary for memory consolidation, fundamentally disrupting the neural processes underlying learning and memory39.

Remarkably, prolonged sleep duration significantly increases the risk of cognitive decline, with a risk far greater than that observed in individuals with insufficient sleep40. This striking finding warrants critical examination of underlying mechanisms, as the pathways differ substantially from those associated with short sleep. The elevated risk associated with long sleep duration may primarily reflect undiagnosed sleep disorders, particularly obstructive sleep apnea syndrome (OSAS) and REM sleep behavior disorder (RBD)41–43. OSAS, affecting 15–30% of older adults, often manifests as prolonged sleep duration due to poor sleep quality and frequent arousals, while creating chronic intermittent hypoxemia that promotes neuroinflammation and directly damages hippocampal and prefrontal cortical regions critical for cognitive function7,44. Similarly, RBD, strongly associated with synucleinopathies including Parkinson’s disease and dementia with Lewy bodies, frequently presents with extended sleep duration as patients attempt to compensate for disrupted sleep architecture36,37. However, reverse causality represents an equally important consideration, wherein prolonged sleep duration may constitute an early manifestation of underlying neurodegeneration rather than a causal factor20. Pathological changes in hypothalamic nuclei controlling circadian rhythms, particularly the suprachiasmatic nucleus, occur early in Alzheimer’s disease progression and may manifest as altered sleep patterns years before overt cognitive symptoms45. Furthermore, long sleepers frequently exhibit clustering of comorbidities including depression, metabolic disorders, and cardiovascular disease that independently contribute to cognitive decline through shared inflammatory and vascular pathways11,46. The bidirectional relationship between depression and prolonged sleep creates a reinforcing cycle of cognitive decline acceleration, while socioeconomic factors such as lower educational attainment and reduced physical activity may partially confound the observed associations47.

Notably, the National Sleep Foundation recommends 7–9 h of sleep for adults aged 26–64 years and 7–8 h for those aged 65 years and older48, our finding of 7.23 h falls precisely within these recommended ranges validating both the biological appropriateness of these recommendations and providing a specific target for cognitive health interventions. This finding supports the integration of precision sleep optimization into routine clinical practice for cognitive decline prevention.

The steep risk gradient observed on either side of this threshold—with each hour below 7.23 h providing 10% risk reduction and each hour above increasing risk by 31%—underscores the narrow window for optimal cognitive protection and suggests that individualized sleep assessment and intervention strategies may be particularly valuable. Future mechanistic studies incorporating objective sleep measurement, comprehensive sleep disorder screening, and longitudinal biomarker assessment are needed to further disentangle causal relationships from reverse causality, particularly for the long sleep duration associations. The underlying mechanisms linking prolonged sleep to cognitive decline warrant continued investigation, as understanding these pathways could inform targeted interventions for this high-risk population. Our study possesses several key strengths. Firstly, considering the bidirectional relationship between sleep disorders and cognitive function in late life49,50, we addressed the risk of reverse causality by excluding patients with MCI at baseline. Secondly, given that a significant proportion of individuals with MCI may revert to normal cognition21,51, we employed a group-based trajectory model to develop a more accurate approach to grouping cognitive trajectories, thus enhancing the assessment of cognitive decline in the population. Thirdly, the wide range of covariates included is a key strength of this study. meanwhile, comorbidities play a crucial role in influencing sleep and cognitive function among older adults. Our study benefits from being based on a large population-based national cohort that was followed biennially for 9 years with minimal loss to follow-up. This comprehensive approach provides an extensive overview of common chronic conditions prevalent in this demographic, thereby contributing to a fuller understanding of the association between sleep duration and cognitive decline.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, the sleep variables were collected through self-reported data rather than objective measurements, which may introduce reporting bias and cannot capture the multidimensional nature of sleep health assessed by validated instruments like the PSQI. Secondly, despite adjusting for various potential confounders, there remains a possibility of residual confounding due to unmeasured covariates such as other sleep disturbances (e.g., Obstructive sleep apnea syndrome) and physical exercise. Thirdly, it is important to note that cognitive impairment encompasses various types (e.g., Alzheimer’s disease and dementia with Lewy bodies). However, due to insufficient information collected in this study, we only analyzed the influences of rapid cognitive decline and were unable to perform specific types of dementia analyses. Finally, our GBTM approach, while effective for identifying distinct sleep pattern subgroups, cannot examine individual-level sleep trajectories as comprehensively as linear mixed-effects models would allow.

Conclusion

Based on a 9-year follow-up of nationally representative data, we utilized GBTM to classify individuals into two distinct trajectory groups based on their cognitive function. Our findings suggest that sleep duration independently contributes to the initiation of long-term cognitive decline, with an optimal threshold of 7.23 h for cognitive protection. This emphasizes the clinical importance of sleep optimization in preventing cognitive decline. To advance this field, future research should incorporate objective sleep measurements (polysomnography, actigraphy) and conduct randomized controlled trials testing whether sleep duration interventions targeting the identified optimal range can reduce dementia risk and establish definitive causal relationships for clinical implementation.
