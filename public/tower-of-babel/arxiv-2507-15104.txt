AnalogFed: Federated Discovery of Analog Circuit Topologies with Generative AI
Qiufeng Li 1 , Shu Hong 1 , Jian Gao 2 , Xuan Zhang 2 , Tian Lan 1 , and Weidong Cao 1 1 George Washington University, 2 Northeastern University

Abstract
Recent breakthroughs in AI/ML offer exciting opportunities to revolutionize analog design automation through data-driven approaches. In particular, researchers are increasingly fascinated by harnessing the power of generative AI to automate the discovery of novel analog circuit topologies. Unlocking the full potential of generative AI in these data-driven discoveries requires access to large and diverse datasets. Yet, there is a significant barrier in the analog domain–Analog circuit design is inherently proprietary, involving not only confidential circuit structures but also the underlying commercial semiconductor processes. As a result, current generative AI research is largely confined to individual researchers who construct small, narrowly focused private datasets. This fragmentation severely limits collaborative innovation and impedes progress across the research community. To address these challenges, we propose AnalogFed, the first scalable and privacy-preserving framework for analog circuit topology design based on federated learning (FedL). AnalogFed enables collaborative topology discovery across decentralized clients (e.g., individual researchers or institutions) without requiring the sharing of raw private data. To make this vision practical, we introduce a suite of techniques tailored to the unique challenges of applying FedL in analog design–from generative model development and data heterogeneity handling to privacy-preserving strategies that ensure both flexibility and security for circuit designers and semiconductor manufacturers. Extensive experiments across varying client counts and dataset sizes demonstrate that AnalogFed achieves performance comparable to centralized baselines–while maintaining strict data privacy. Specifically, the generative AI model within AnalogFed achieves state-of-the-art efficiency and scalability in the design of analog circuit topologies. AnalogFed establishes a foundation for secure, collaborative, and scalable analog EDA, unlocking data-driven discovery in a privacy-constrained world.

Introduction

Electronic design automation (EDA) for analog circuits has been recognized as a longstanding challenge in the semiconductor field, due to their intricate design complexities and inherent difficulties associated with cross-level abstractions.
Excitingly, recent breakthroughs in AI/ML provide unprecedented opportunities to transform conventional manual analog design flows, i.e., using circuit data to train capable neural models for design automation

Remarkably, numerous learning methods, e.g., reinforcement learning and generative AI, have been explored to automate various analog design tasks, from circuit synthesis

and performance modeling
[

to topology discovery and physical design
[

.
Despite these promising developments, current learning-based analog EDA methods remain limited in scalability, largely due to their inability to fully utilize available circuit data, which is the key enabler for AI methods.
A fundamental barrier lies in the proprietary nature of analog circuit design, which often involves confidential intellectual property from not only the circuits themselves but also the commercial semiconductor processes used to implement circuits.
As such, individual researchers often construct small, private datasets narrowly tailored to specific design tasks.
This fragmentation significantly hinders the development of large-scale, shared datasets and prohibits collaborative innovations across the research community.
Thus, the full potential of data-driven analog EDA remains largely untapped.

The emerging application of generative AI to analog circuit topology design particularly suffers from this challenge, as it heavily relies on large and diverse datasets to enable data-driven discovery of novel topologies.
Historically, progress in analog circuit performance has been primarily driven by semiconductor process scaling, known as Moore’s law.
Yet, with the end of Moore’s law on the horizon
[
6
]
, continued performance improvements can no longer be expected from technology scaling alone.
Developing novel analog circuit topologies has thus arisen as a promising pathway to sustain and advance analog circuit performance in the post-Moore era.
Recent advances have demonstrated the significant capabilities of generative AI in this area.
For example, AnalogGenie
[
11
]
, a generative AI engine, has successfully discovered novel analog circuit topologies that match the performance of manually created designs.
However, the scalability of such approaches remains fundamentally limited to individuals who suffer from the scarcity of available datasets.
This challenge raises a pivotal research question:
How can we unify individually curated, private datasets without compromising confidentiality, in order to develop scalable, learning-based analog topology design methods that unlock AI’s full potential and foster broader accessibility and collaboration?

FedL

is a well-established privacy-preserving paradigm that enables the training of a powerful neural model to benefit all clients (e.g., researchers) without requiring direct access to the private datasets of individual clients, thereby achieving both data and model advantages over those of individual clients.
It has shown remarkable success in various domains involving confidential information, such as healthcare and medical research, financial services and fraud detection, and retail and personalized marketing

.
Inspired by these great successes, we develop AnalogFed, a domain-specific FedL framework for discovering analog topologies at scale.

To ensure effective deployment of AnalogFed, we address multiple domain-specific challenges when applying FedL to analog topology design.
First, we tailor a generative AI model to generate various analog circuit topologies using a cutting-edge graph modeling approach that is broadly applicable across circuit types.
To enhance the effectiveness of this approach, we introduce optimization techniques such as edge pruning and substructure reuse, which significantly improve the efficiency and scalability of topology generation.
These enhancements establish a state-of-the-art generative model that serves as a robust and efficient backbone for AnalogFed.
Second, we conduct a comprehensive analysis of analog topology datasets distributed across multiple clients to characterize the heterogeneous nature of individual private datasets.
This step is crucial to ensuring the quality of the global model without being adversely affected by non-independent and identically distributed (non-IID) data–a well-known challenge in real-world FedL applications.
Third, we propose a novel privacy-preserving strategy that maximizes confidentiality for both analog circuit designers and semiconductor manufacturers.
This strategy involves federated pre-training followed by decentralized fine-tuning.
Specifically, AnalogFed’s generative model is first pre-trained across clients to learn foundational knowledge of circuit connectivity across various types.
Evaluation and task-specific fine-tuning are then performed locally, allowing each client to customize the model using their own private datasets and proprietary semiconductor technologies–ensuring both data privacy and design relevance.

These techniques empower AnalogFed with exceptional performance, laying the groundwork for next-generation, scalable analog EDA research.
We believe this framework is not only well-suited for accelerating scientific discovery–by unifying individual private datasets from both industry and academia in a scalable and privacy-preserving manner–but also holds significant potential for enabling decentralized chip development.
By leveraging learning-driven analog EDA tools, AnalogFed can facilitate seamless collaboration among multiple companies while maintaining data confidentiality, marking a critical step toward the future of distributed semiconductor innovation.

Novel privacy-preserving learning for scalable Analog EDA:
This work innovatively harnesses FedL to address the scalability limitation of current analog EDA research, especially in topology design, by unifying multiple private datasets without breaching confidentiality.
This could promote collaborative innovations and foster broader accessibility in the research community.

•

Comprehensive tailored techniques to address domain-specific challenges:
We develop multiple techniques to address the specific challenges of applying FedL to discover analog circuit topologies, ranging from generative model development and data heterogeneity characterization to practical privacy-preserving strategies to ensure privacy and flexibility for both designers and manufacturers.

•

Impressive performance in data-driven scientific discovery:
We conduct extensive experiments to validate the scalability of AnalogFed with different federated settings and show its comparable performance in discovering novel circuit topologies to a centralized baseline.

Additionally, the generative AI model within AnalogFed achieves state-of-the-art performance in discovering analog circuit topologies.

II
Background and Related Work

II-A

Analog Circuits: Design Process and Automated Methods

II-A
1
Primary design process of analog circuits

The design of analog circuits begins with creating a circuit topology, which involves determining the device types (e.g., transistors, capacitors, resistors, and inductors) and the number of devices, and defining how they are interconnected.
Following this initial step, designers perform device sizing, i.e., optimizing the physical dimensions of devices to achieve the desired performance.
Finally, the physical layout is developed to prepare for manufacturing.
The topology design requires the most creative effort of all these stages, as it is conventionally conceptualized from scratch by human designers.
In addition, the topology primarily determines the performance, functionality, and efficiency of a circuit once a semiconductor process is finalized to implement it, much like a protein structure that dictates the generation of amino acids.
Given its importance, analogy circuit topology design is the primary focus of this work.

II-A
2
State-of-the-art learning methods for analog topology design

While significant progress has been made in automating device sizing and layout design
[
3
,
4
]
, the topology generation remains a challenging problem due to its abstract and complex nature.
Generative AI has demonstrated significant promise in tackling topology generation, efforts primarily centered around text-based or graph-based methods.
For instance, AnalogCoder
[
7
]
represents circuits as Python-style SPICE (PySpice) netlists, leveraging domain-specific prompt engineering with large language models (LLMs).
While this flexible representation supports diverse and scalable designs, its reliance on high-level text introduces challenges–particularly in generating valid circuits–due to the complexity of multi-token predictions required for each device and connection.
Artisan
[
8
]
sidesteps this complexity by focusing on topology selection rather than generation.
Using natural language to represent topology names, Artisan reuses existing topologies, avoiding the complexity of low-level design. However, this limits its ability to generate novel and custom
topologies.
Graph-based methods, such as CktGNN
[
9
]
, use adjacency matrices and a graph variational autoencoder (VAE) to generate topologies for specific analog ICs like operational-amplifiers (20 devices).
Similarly LaMAGIC
[
10
]
fine-tunes a masked language model (MLM) to generate fixed-node circuits, achieving high success rates on simpler designs, such as power converters with fewer than four devices.

Most recently, AnalogGenie
[
11
]
, a decoder-only transformer, has been proposed to generate various analog topologies by using a scalable sequence-based graph representation universal to analog circuits.
AnalogGenie demonstrates remarkable generation performance in broadening the variety of analog ICs, increasing the number of devices within a single design, and discovering previously unseen circuit topologies that far exceed any prior art.
This work will enhance the topology modeling used by AnalogGenie to develop an advanced generative AI model with significantly improved efficiency and scalability.

II-B

Federated Learning (FedL)

FedL is a distributed machine learning paradigm that enables multiple clients to collaboratively train a shared model without exposing their local data, as shown in Fig.
1
.
FedL addresses privacy and data governance concerns by keeping raw data locally and instead exchanging model updates, such as gradients or weights, between clients and a central server.
In a typical FedL workflow, each client performs a round of local training on its own dataset (
1
) and then sends the resulting model updates to a server (global aggregator,
2
).
The server combines these updates using an algorithm such as Federated Averaging (FedAvg,
3
), and redistributes the aggregated model to all clients for the next round (
4
).
This process continues iteratively until convergence.
FedL has been widely used in various domains, such as mobile NLP, computer vision, and healthcare, where data is sensitive, decentralized, and often non-independent and identically distributed (non-IID)
[
19
,
18
,
24
,
22
,
23
,
21
]
.

II-B
2
Challenges of applying FedL to analog circuit design

Despite its great effectiveness in privacy preservation, FedL suffers from performance degradation due to data heterogeneity
[
18
]
and various attack threats, including data poisoning attacks, membership attacks, and Byzantine attacks
[
29
]
.
These issues are especially critical for analog circuit designs, as they not only desire high performance but also involve extensive intellectual properties and confidential information.
This work will focus on demonstrating the feasibility of applying FedL to analog EDA.
We will investigate the impact of the data distribution of analog circuit topologies on the performance of our developed generative AI model.
We will also conduct experiments to demonstrate the vulnerabilities of AnalogFed under various representative attacks.
The defense strategies to enhance the robustness and security of AnalogFed are beyond the scope of this work and will be further studied in the future.
FedL could also require high communication cost
[
26
]
, arising from the repeated exchange of model updates between clients and the central server during training rounds, which can become significant, especially in large-scale or bandwidth-constrained environments.
There has been extensive work on improving the communication efficiency of FedL through various protocols.
In this work, we focus on developing an efficient backbone model to enhance the efficiency of AnalogFed.

It is worth noting that recent works have sought to address privacy concerns in the circuit domain.
For example, PRICING
[
16
]
tackles the data availability challenge in EDA by proposing a privacy-preserving data sharing framework.
It introduces a feature protector that transforms raw image-based layout data into obfuscated representations, allowing secure sharing for tasks such as lithographic hotspot detection.
However, PRICING is a specific solution for digital physical design and still relies on a centralized training setup.
A recent effort
[
17
]
also introduces FedL to model the performance of a specific type of circuit, i.e., microwave filters.
In contrast, our work harnesses FedL to discover novel and diverse analog circuit topologies in a scalable manner.

AnalogFed Framework

We will then address key domain-specific challenges when applying FedL to develop AnalogFed.

III-A

Overview and Domain-specific Challenges

As shown in Fig.
2
, AnalogFed is a scalable and privacy-preserving framework for analog topology design built on two essential components: a generative AI model and a FedL paradigm that enables the indirect access of private datasets to train the generative AI model.
The generative AI model is used as the backbone for topology discovery.
In our federated setup, each client independently trains the model on its private analog circuit dataset and periodically sends model updates (weights or gradients) to a central server.
The server performs Federated Averaging (FedAvg) to aggregate these updates and produce a new global model, which is then sent back to the clients.
With multiple such interactions, the final generative AI model will converge to achieve optimality.
Yet, it is non-trivial to apply the FedL paradigm to develop AnalogFed.
Several key challenges remain to be addressed.

First, to enable federated analog topology discovery, an efficient and scalable generative AI model for the design of analog circuit topologies is essential.
This model is also crucial for enhancing the communication efficiency of AnalogFed.
Yet, current generative AI models for analog topology design do not sufficiently show this capability.
We will develop an advanced generative AI model to achieve state-of-the-art efficiency and scalability, as outlined in Section
III-B
.
Second, analog circuit design involves multi-faceted privacy issues.
Clients must not only protect their IP (i.e., circuit topologies) but also safeguard the semiconductor fabrication technologies provided by manufacturers that are used to evaluate the performance of circuit topologies.
This complexity presents a significant challenge for the practical deployment of AnalogFed: how to deploy it in a way that maximizes the privacy of both IC designers and manufacturers.
We will address this challenge in Section
III-C
.
Lastly, practical analog design involves varying circuit types and dataset sizes across clients, i.e., non-IID data could be common in AnalogFed.
Such heterogeneity leads to weight variance between clients, which can cause the aggregated global model to drift or converge suboptimally.
We will investigate this issue in Section
III-D
.

III-B

Developing the Backbone Efficient Generative Model

To date, only two generative AI techniques have shown the capability to design various analog circuits: AnalogCoder
[
7
]
and AnalogGenie
[
11
]
.
Yet, AnalogCoder uses existing LLMs (e.g., GPT-4) as the backbone, which does not fit AnalogFed due to the billions of parameters of LLMs.
AnalogGenie
[
11
]
is a domain-specific decoder-only transformer to discover circuit topologies with graph modeling.
At its core is an
expressiveness-enhanced graph representation
, which models each device pin (e.g., gate, drain, source) as an individual graph node.
This pin-level granularity ensures that all electrical connections and inter-device interactions are explicitly represented, offering a cutting-edge, precise structural encoding.
A circuit graph is subsequently converted into an Eulerian circuit, which is a sequence of traversal paths of the circuit graph.
In this way, AnalogGenie converts a topology design task into a sequence generation problem by formulating a device-pin connection as a token and predicting the next device pin to connect (similar to the right subset in Fig.
2
).
Although it has only 11 million parameters and fits the FedL paradigm well, AnalogGenie does not sufficiently demonstrate generation efficiency and scalability.
As such, we develop an advanced version based on AnalogGenie by proposing several optimization techniques.

III-B
1
Precise and efficient graph modeling

Node pruning:
In the original AnalogGenie representation, each device (e.g., an NMOS transistor) is modeled using both its individual pins (e.g., NM1D, NM1G, NM1S) and a corresponding device node (e.g., NM1).
However, this device node primarily encodes trivial or redundant information, such as pin-to-pin connections within the same device or self-connections, which are unnecessary for generating the topology as shown in Fig.
3
(b).
In our approach, as shown in Fig.
3
(a), we remove all device-level nodes and instead directly connect the pins in a cycle to preserve structural relationships (e.g., NM1D
\leftrightarrow
NM1G
\leftrightarrow
NM1S
\leftrightarrow
NM1D).

Edge pruning:
When multiple device pins share the same net, prior work explicitly connects all pairs, resulting in
\frac{n(n-1)}{2}
edges for
n
pins. We instead simplify this by selecting a single anchor pin and connecting the remaining
n-1
pins to it–forming a star-shaped topology.
This reduces the complexity of the edges from quadratic to linear in
n
, improving the sparsity of the graph and the scalability of the model.

III-B
2
Subgraph mining and tokenization

AnalogGenie focuses solely on generating analog circuit topologies at the most fundamental device level, i.e., individual device pins, resulting in long, heavy-weight sequential representations.
To improve scalability and efficiency, we bridge this gap by enabling the simultaneous exploration of both custom topologies and reusable hierarchical substructures.
To support this, we incorporate a domain-specific tokenizer that encodes each token as either a device-level pin (e.g.,
NM1G
,
NM1D
,
NM1S
,
NM1B
) or a circuit-level terminal (e.g.,
VIN1
,
VOUT1
,
VDD
,
VSS
).
Additionally, we introduce a special token vocabulary to represent subcircuit-level pins such as
SG1VDD
,
SG1VOUT
, and
SG1termA
.
These tokens abstract away the internal structure of subcircuits, allowing the model to generate outer interfaces while omitting low-level internal details–enhancing abstraction and reusability.

To identify reusable substructures, we perform rigorous data mining over a curated database containing more than 3,000 real-world analog topologies spanning 11 circuit categories, provided by AnalogGenie.
We apply the
gSpan
algorithm
[
13
]
to mine frequent subgraphs.
Unlike earlier Apriori-based methods,
gSpan
efficiently discovers frequently connected substructures without explicitly generating candidate subgraphs, making it well-suited for graph-based data, as shown in Fig.
4
(a) (
1
).
Once frequent subgraphs are identified, we classify their nodes into two types:
isolated nodes
(connected only within the subgraph) and
non-isolated nodes
(connected to the rest of the circuit).
We prune isolated nodes and their associated edges to simplify the subgraph, then connect the remaining non-isolated nodes in a closed cycle Fig.
4
(a) (
2
).
The simplified subgraph is then replaced in the main graph with its corresponding subgraph token, resulting in a more compact, abstracted, and reusable representation, an example as shown in Fig.
4
(b).
This strategy significantly reduces sequence length while maintaining structural expressiveness, enabling the efficient and scalable generation of analog topologies.

III-B
3
Circuit sequence optimization via Eulerian traversal

The final key innovation in our modeling pipeline lies in constructing an efficient sequence representation from the circuit graph.
AnalogGenie uses non-optimal Eulerian circuits to model the graph.
Analog circuit topologies are typically sparse, with most devices connected only to their immediate neighbors.
To minimize the sequence length while preserving structural accuracy, we represent each circuit as a shortest closed path that visits every edge in the graph at least once.
This path is derived by solving the well-known
Chinese Postman Problem
(CPP).
When the circuit graph is Eulerian, i.e., all vertices have even degrees, the CPP solution corresponds exactly to an Eulerian circuit.
However, not all analog circuit graphs satisfy this condition.
In such cases, the task reduces to determining the minimum number of edge duplications needed to convert the graph into an Eulerian multigraph.

We employ a more principled approach by directly solving the CPP.
Given an unweighted graph
G=(V,E)
, we assign unit weights to all edges.
The algorithm first identifies vertices with odd degrees, then computes a minimum-cost matching among them using shortest-path distances.
The edges along the resulting paths are duplicated to ensure all nodes have even degree, thus transforming
G
into an Eulerian graph with minimal overhead, as shown in Fig.
5
.
This optimization significantly reduces the sequence length required for topology generation, resulting in a more compact and computationally efficient representation while faithfully preserving circuit connectivity.

III-C

Maximizing Deployment Privacy

As mentioned earlier, analog IC design involves multi-faceted privacy issues, i.e., IP within circuit topologies and confidential semiconductor processes.
To maximize the privacy for both IC designers and manufacturers, we propose leveraging federated pre-training and decentralized fine-tuning, as shown in Fig.
2
.
Specifically, within AnalogFed, the generative AI model will be used for pre-training to broadly learn foundational knowledge of circuit connections across various circuit types.
The evaluation process on the generated circuit topology will take place locally, allowing each client to fine-tune the pre-trained model for specific tasks on their own private dataset.
This strategy makes full sense according to the practice:
Individual clients can collaboratively pre-train a powerful generative model by indirectly accessing private topology datasets from all clients, thereby surpassing the capability of a model trained on a single client’s dataset.
Since each type of analog circuit has unique specifications for evaluation, it is more effective for clients to evaluate the performance of a generated circuit topology locally, ensuring the privacy of semiconductor processes.

III-C
1
Fine-tunning strategy

The pre-trained generative model in AnalogFed has acquired foundational knowledge of circuit connectivity across various analog circuit types, enabling it to achieve baseline discovery efficiency by generating valid circuit topologies.
To further enhance its ability to generate high-performance designs based on a (commercial) semiconductor technology within a limited number of attempts, clients can fine-tune this model to align with their specific preferences in both circuit type and performance objectives.
By building on the knowledge learned during pre-training, clients can guide the model toward high-quality designs using only a small set of performance-labeled topologies.
To enable this, we propose adopting Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
[
28
]
as the reinforcement learning with human feedback (RLHF) strategy, given its strong balance between performance and stability, as well as its widespread use in fine-tuning LLMs.
Clients can adapt existing PPO frameworks to effectively discover high-performance circuit topologies tailored to specific analog IC design tasks.

The PPO framework treats the pre-trained generative model as a policy network
\pi_{\theta}
, which generates sequences representing circuit topologies.
Specficially, each client can prepare a fine-tuning dataset–a limited subset of a private topology collection (e.g., Op-Amps)–and annotate it with performance metrics, such as the figure-of-merit (FoM).
Based on this annotated data, a reward model (Fig.
2
) is trained to serve as a proxy for human evaluation, guiding the generative process according to performance preferences.
The fine-tuning process begins with the generative model producing new circuit topologies, which are evaluated and labeled by the reward model.
Clients may use a Plackett-Luce ranking model
[
33
]
to assign sequence-level rewards by ranking generated samples across performance classes, as illustrated in Table
I
.
The reward model itself combines two components: a rule-based checker and a multiclass classifier.
The checker first verifies whether a generated circuit is valid (i.e., simulatable with default device sizing), while the classifier predicts the circuit’s type and estimated performance category.
To train the classifier, clients label a small set of circuit topologies with both type and performance annotations.
For example, if the goal is to discover high-performance Op-Amps, only Op-Amp circuits are treated as relevant, while others are excluded from the reward evaluation.
With the trained reward model in place, PPO is used to iteratively optimize the generative policy, following the procedure outlined in the original PPO framework.

III-D

Evaluating the Heterogeneity of Client Private Dataset

Data heterogeneity is a critical issue in federated settings, which can significantly impact the training performance of global models.
While the evaluation of data heterogeneity has been well studied in various domains, such as image and language processing
[
25
,
27
]
, circuit topology is a unique data modality that requires specific methods.
To facilitate the evaluation of data heterogeneity in AnalogFed, we propose to analyze the distribution of analog circuit datasets across clients after tokenization and embedding.
Specifically, for each client’s local dataset, we first tokenize all circuit graphs into sequences and convert the resulting token IDs into continuous-valued embeddings using the pretrained embedding layer.
We then compute statistical metrics over the embedding vectors, including the
mean
and
mean squared error (MSE)
, to quantify how embedding distributions vary from one client to another.
Additionally, we propose visualizing the value distribution of embedding activations for each client by plotting the probability density against the embedding values.
This helps to reveal whether any client exhibits significant divergence in feature space, which is critical for understanding the non-IID nature of the federated setup.
We provide an exemplary characterization in Section
IV-B1
.

These structural motifs are present across various circuit types and maintain a balanced ratio of device types, regardless of the functional complexity.
As a result, when analog circuits are encoded into token sequences–where each token corresponds to a device or connection–the overall token composition remains statistically similar across datasets.
This structural symmetry likely contributes to the low variance in embedding distributions, even when clients specialize in different types of circuits.
This discovery provides an important insight: analog circuit datasets are inherently well-suited for federated learning, due to their structural regularity and token-level distribution consistency.
This makes it feasible to leverage FedL to collaboratively train stronger and more generalizable generative models, without compromising data privacy.

Validity.
Ours method achieves a generation validity of 95%, significantly outperforming prior methods.

Artisan, which performs topology selection rather than generation, attains a higher validity of 82%, but cannot synthesize novel circuit structures.

Scalability.
Prior approaches such as CktGNN and LaMAGIC are constrained by the quadratic complexity of adjacency matrix representations, limiting them to circuits with at most 4 and 22 devices, respectively.
Template-based methods, such as AnalogCoder and Artisan, are similarly restricted by their reliance on fixed prompting formats, which generate circuits with a maximum of 10 and 18 devices.
The original AnalogGenie improves scalability up to 63 devices.
Building on this, our method further extends the scalability to 324 devices, thanks to its lightweight graph modeling and optimized tokenization.

Versatility.
Our method offers broader design versatility than prior methods.
Models such as Artisan, CktGNN, and LaMAGIC are constrained to a single circuit category, limiting their applicability in practical design scenarios.
While AnalogCoder supports up to seven circuit types, it is bound by a synthesis library that contains only 20 fixed topologies.
In contrast, our method supports a wide range of analog circuits and generates novel topologies without relying on pre-defined templates or libraries, demonstrating superior adaptability across diverse design domains.

Novelty.
Our method significantly outperforms previous methods in discovering novel circuit topologies.
AnalogCoder and Artisan primarily reuse pre-existing topologies or subblocks, offering limited creative potential.
LaMAGIC is confined to a narrow design space, restricting its ability to explore new configurations.

IV-B
5
Robustness against attacks via defense integration

In practical FedL deployments, it is often unrealistic to assume that all participating clients are fully trustworthy or that their datasets are uniformly high quality.
Given this, it is essential to evaluate the robustness of AnalogFed under common security threats, including
model poisoning attacks

[
32
]
and
data poisoning attacks

[
31
]
.
As our focus in this work is to demonstrate the feasibility of AnalogFed, we perform two exemplary attacks.
Comprehensive security issues and defensive techniques will be studied in the future.
For
model attacks
, we simulate a scenario in which a malicious client submits exaggerated updates to distort the global model.
Specifically, we select the 8-client configuration and randomly designate one Client as untrustworthy.
During each communication round, the Client sends scaled updates to the server, using either
10\times
or
5\times
magnification of its model delta, to mimic adversarial behavior that can destabilize or bias the aggregation process.
For
dataset attacks
, we explore a targeted form of poisoning known as token replacement attacks.
In this strategy, we corrupt a subset of training samples by randomly replacing certain tokens in the input sequence with incorrect ones, intentionally disrupting circuit connectivity or device consistency.
This simulates real-world label flipping or semantic tampering, which may arise from either faulty data curation or adversarial intent.

Our experiments reveal that adversarial behavior significantly degrades model performance. Under
model poisoning attacks
, where a malicious client submits amplified updates:
The
10\times
scaling attack increases the validation loss by
14.94
\times
.
The
5\times
scaling attack causes a
9.09
\times
increase in validation loss.
For
data poisoning attacks
, specifically through random token replacement, we observe a
3.57
\times
increase in validation loss.
These results underscore the importance of robust defense mechanisms.

To mitigate the impact of adversarial clients in federated settings, we incorporate a defense strategy inspired by
FLDetector

[
32
]
, which identifies malicious participants by monitoring the consistency of their model updates over time. FLDetector operates by predicting each client’s expected model update at every round using historical updates and compares it to the actual received update.

Our experiments demonstrate that
AnalogFed
, when equipped with the FLDetector-based defense mechanism, can effectively identify and isolate malicious clients under both
model poisoning
and
dataset poisoning
attacks.
Once the detected adversarial clients are removed, AnalogFed can recover and continue training an accurate and robust global model, restoring performance to levels comparable to those in clean, attack-free federated training scenarios.

V
Conclusion

This work introduces
AnalogFed
, the first FedL framework for scalable and privacy-preserving analog circuit topology generation.
By leveraging FedL, AnalogFed enables collaborative model training across decentralized datasets without sharing raw circuit data, addressing a key barrier in analog EDA.
We propose lightweight modeling optimizations to enhance generation efficiency, including graph simplification, subgraph tokenization, and sequence compression via Eulerian traversal.
Extensive experiments show that AnalogFed scales well with increasing client numbers and dataset sizes, and remains robust under common attack scenarios. Our findings demonstrate that AnalogFed not only matches centralized baselines in performance but also opens new opportunities for secure, collaborative analog design across organizations.
