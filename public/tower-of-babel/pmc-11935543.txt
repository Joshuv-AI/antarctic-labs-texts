Brachial‐Ankle Pulse Wave Velocity as a Predictor of Diabetes Development: Elevated Risk Within Normal Range Values in a Low‐Risk Population
Authors: Jeonggyu Kang, Ki‐Hyun Jeon, Kang‐Un Choi, Hyo‐In Choi, Ki‐Chul Sung
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRecent studies have suggested that increased brachial‐ankle pulse wave velocity (baPWV) is a risk factor for diabetes. Exploring its relationship with insulin resistance is of interest, necessitating further studies across different sexes and age groups.Methods and ResultsThis cohort study involved 119 170 Korean adults with an average age of 39.8 years, none of whom had diabetes at baseline. As part of a health screening, baPWV measurements were taken. Over a median follow‐up period of 5.6 years, fasting blood glucose, glycated hemoglobin, insulin levels, and questionnaire responses were collected. The risk of developing diabetes was evaluated using a flexible parametric proportional hazards model with data stratified by sex and age group (<40 versus ≥40 years). During the follow‐up period, diabetes was diagnosed in 5966 participants (5.0%). A fully adjusted model found that the hazard ratios for diabetes onset associated with baPWV quartiles Q2 (1171.0–1270.5 cm/s), Q3 (1271.0–1376.0 cm/s), and Q4 (≥1376.5 cm/s) compared with Q1 (<1171.0 cm/s) were 1.06 (95% CI, 0.96–1.17), 1.25 (1.14–1.38), and 1.48 (1.34–1.62), respectively (P for trend <0.001). A significant sex‐based interaction was noted in this association, with women showing a higher risk of diabetes development. Furthermore, higher baPWV quartiles were associated with an increased risk of developing insulin resistance, defined as the homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance.ConclusionsThese findings highlight the importance of arterial stiffness, as measured by elevated baPWV, in the development of diabetes and insulin resistance. Notably, this study highlighted a strong association, particularly among women.

Diabetes prevalence in adults is expected to rise from 10.5% in 2021 to 12.2% by 2045, affecting 783.2 million people globally. 1 Furthermore, the average age at which diabetes is diagnosed is decreasing, which can be attributed to rising obesity rates, sedentary lifestyles, increased urbanization, and higher sugar consumption. 2 Prolonged hyperglycemia can result in oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and chronic inflammation in the vascular system. 3 These disruptions can cause functional and structural changes, resulting in arterial stiffness and atherosclerosis, which can lead to cardiovascular disease (CVD). Among these vascular changes, surrogate markers for arterial stiffness are strong predictors of CVD and mortality in patients with diabetes. 4 , 5

Surprisingly, recent research has found that increased arterial stiffness is not only a result of diabetes but also a risk factor for it. Several population studies have found that increased arterial stiffness increases the risk of developing diabetes, regardless of the specific measurements used. For instance, the Framingham Heart Study discovered that a higher carotid‐femoral pulse wave velocity (cfPWV) was associated with various health outcomes, including the onset of diabetes. 6 Similarly, an analysis of data from the UK Biobank revealed a link between arterial stiffness, as measured by the pulse wave arterial stiffness index, and an increased diabetes risk. 7

However, the mechanisms underlying the association between arterial stiffness and risk of diabetes remain unknown. Furthermore, there is little evidence to support arterial stiffness measurements as reliable predictors of diabetes in diverse populations, particularly among younger low‐risk individuals with normal brachial‐ankle PWV (baPWV) ranges.

Given that the risk of diabetes varies with age, sex, and ethnicity, it is critical to investigate the association between arterial stiffness and diabetes across different demographics. Furthermore, the effect of arterial stiffness on glucose regulation requires further investigation. Therefore, exploring the relationship between arterial stiffness and insulin resistance (IR), as well as the risk of developing diabetes, in a large cohort remains a valuable research area.

In this context, our study aimed to assess the risk of developing diabetes based on baPWV, stratified by age and sex, and correlated with IR in a large cohort of young and middle‐aged Korean adults.

METHODS

Data Availability

The data are not publicly available because, when initially applying for institutional review board approval, guidelines regarding data distribution were not obtained. Although we cannot share the raw data, we are willing to provide detailed information about the data characteristics and analysis methods upon request.

Study Population

This study was performed as part of the Kangbuk Samsung Health Study, a cohort study of Korean adults aged 18 years or older who underwent annual or biennial health examinations in Seoul or Suwon, South Korea. Most (>80%) participants or their spouses worked for businesses and local government organizations. In South Korea, the Industrial Safety and Health Law mandates annual or biennial health screening examinations for all employees, which are provided free of charge. The remaining participants voluntarily underwent screening examinations at their own expense. Participants in this study were those who had a (baPWV) measurement as part of their health examination between 2007 and 2018 and who had at least one follow‐up until December 2019 (n=129 122).

We excluded 9952 participants who met 1 or more of the following exclusion criteria at baseline: 1796 with a history of CVD, 2978 with a history of malignancy, 5509 with diabetes, and 185 with missing variables during follow‐up. After these exclusions, 119 170 participants were included in the diabetes risk analysis based on baPWV (Figure 1). For the analyses regarding IR, after excluding those with baseline homeostatic model assessment of IR (HOMA‐IR) at or above the 90th percentile (≥2.43) and censoring visits where diabetes was diagnosed, the cohort size was reduced to 106 123. This study was approved by the institutional review board of Kangbuk Samsung Hospital (IRB No. 2021‐10‐005), which waived the requirement for informed consent, as we used only deidentified data routinely collected as part of the health screening examinations.

Flow chart of the study participants.

Measurements

Information on medical and family history, medication use, lifestyle factors, and education level was collected using a self‐reported questionnaire; anthropometric and serum biochemical parameters were measured by trained staff at baseline and follow‐up visits. Current smokers were defined as those who smoked at the time and reported having smoked >100 cigarettes in their lifetime. The average daily alcohol consumption was calculated using the frequency and amount of alcohol consumed per day. Physical activity levels were assessed using the validated Korean version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire Short Form and classified as inactive, active, or health‐enhancing. 8 , 9

Trained nurses measured blood pressure (BP) using an automated oscillometric device (53 000, Welch Allyn, Auburn, NY) with participants in a sitting position with the arm supported at the heart level. Three consecutive BP readings were acquired after the participants rested quietly in a sitting position for 5 minutes. The average of the second and third BP readings was used in the analysis.

The following blood parameters were measured after at least 10 hours of fasting: glucose, glycated hemoglobin, insulin, total cholesterol, triglycerides, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, and high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol. Diabetes was defined as fasting blood glucose (FBG) level≥126 mg/dL, hemoglobin A1c level≥6.5%, or current use of antidiabetic medications. We used the HOMA‐IR formula to assess IR: HOMA‐IR=[fasting insulin (μIU/mL)×FBG (mmol/L)]/22.5. 10 IR was defined using a threshold of 2.43 or higher, corresponding to the 90th percentile, which was a commonly used criterion in previous studies, in the baseline group with no previous history of diabetes or CVD. 11

baPWV Measurements

baPWV measurements were obtained with the participant in the supine position using a VP‐1000 vascular profiler (Omron Healthcare, Kyoto, Japan). This device recorded the pulse waveforms of the bilateral brachial and posterior tibial arteries using an oscillometric method. Electrocardiographic electrodes were attached to both wrists, and cuffs were wrapped around the upper arms (brachial region) and ankles. Concurrently, a phonocardiogram was used to record the acoustic signals of the heart, providing a reference for the start of the cardiac cycle. The path length from the suprasternal notch to the brachium (Lb) and that from the suprasternal notch to the ankle (La) was calculated using the participant's height. The baPWV was calculated using the following formula: baPWV=(La−Lb)/Tba, where Tba is the time interval between the wavefront of the brachial and ankle waveforms. Average values from the left and right sides were used in the analysis.

Statistical Analysis

The characteristics of the participants were summarized using descriptive statistics based on the quartiles of baseline baPWV.

The incidence of diabetes was the primary end point. Because all participants were free of diabetes at baseline, we could determine the first visit at which a participant showed diabetes, but we could not determine the precise time of outcome development (which occurred at some point between the first visit with diabetes and the previous visit). To address this type of interval censoring, we used a flexible parametric proportional hazards model implemented through the ‘stpm2’ command in STATA program. 12 , 13 This method was also applied to assess the development of IR.

To examine the continuous relationship between baPWV and outcomes, we calculated adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs using restricted cubic splines incorporated into the flexible parametric proportional hazards model. Knots were placed at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of the baPWV distribution, with the 25th percentile of baPWV serving as the reference point. This approach allowed us to capture potential nonlinear associations between baPWV and diabetes risk. Analyses were conducted for the total participant pool and stratified by sex and age groups (<40 years versus ≥40 years).

We also analyzed baPWV as a categorical variable by estimating HRs for quartiles 2 to 4, with quartile 1 as the reference. These analyses also were conducted for the total participant pool and stratified by sex and age groups. Due to the uneven distribution of higher baPWV values among men and participants aged >40 years, we conducted sensitivity analyses using sex‐ and age‐specific baPWV quartiles. To minimize reverse causation, we performed additional sensitivity analyses excluding participants who developed diabetes within the first year of follow‐up.

Statistical models assessing the risk of diabetes were adjusted for age, sex, total cholesterol, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, systolic BP, body mass index, medication for hypertension, medication for hyperlipidemia, health screening center, year of screening examination, alcohol consumption, smoking, physical activity, education level, family history of diabetes, and FBG. To evaluate IR risk, the fully adjusted model used HOMA‐IR as a covariate instead of FBG. The effects of the interactions between the subgroups and baPWV on incident diabetes or IR were investigated using a single model that included interaction terms for both baPWV and age group, as well as baPWV and sex. The effects of the interactions between the subgroups and baPWV categories on incident diabetes were investigated using likelihood ratio tests, which compared models with and without multiplicative interaction terms. To analyze the combined effects of age group and sex on the relationship between baPWV and both diabetes and IR, we evaluated sex‐specific interaction terms in age subgroups and age‐related interaction terms in each sex category.

To investigate changes in IR and fasting plasma insulin concentration, a multivariable‐adjusted linear regression model was used. As the follow‐up duration varied by individual, the changes in HOMA‐IR and fasting plasma insulin concentration were divided by the follow‐up duration. Using a multivariable linear regression model, changes in HOMA‐IR and fasting plasma insulin concentration across each baPWV quartile were estimated for both men and women, as well as different age groups. We also investigated the relationship between baPWV, a continuous variable, and the prevalence of diabetes. Finally, we conducted a cross‐lagged path analysis using structural equation modeling to examine bidirectional relationships baPWV and FBG among 41 507 participants who repeated measurements. The structural equation modeling included baPWV and FBG at 2 time points using the first (baPWV1, FBG1) and last measurements (baPWV2, FBG2), specifying auto‐regressive and cross‐lagged paths. We adjusted for aforementioned covariates and the time interval between measurements. The analysis was performed using the ‘sem’ command in STATA. Standardized correlation coefficients were reported to allow for the comparison of effect sizes across different paths in the model. We assessed statistical significance at the P<0.05 level and provided 95% CIs for the estimates. The cross‐lagged panel design employed in our study allows for the examination of the directionality of relationships between variables over time, which can provide evidence for potential causal pathways. By controlling for baseline levels of baPWV and FBG, as well as a comprehensive set of covariates, we tried to isolate the temporal effects of baPWV on changes and vice versa.

All statistical analyses were performed using STATA version 18.0 (Stata Corp LP, College Station, TX). All regression analyses were performed using robust standard errors to account for potential heteroscedasticity and to provide more conservative estimates of CIs and P values. The statistical significance level was set at P<0.05.

RESULTS

In a cohort of 119 170 participants, baseline characteristics were stratified based on baPWV quartiles (Table 1). As baPWV quartiles increased, there was a consistent trend of older age, a higher proportion of men, and a higher prevalence of obesity, alcohol consumption, regular exercise, hypertension medication, and hyperlipidemia medication. Clinical measurements showed that higher baPWV quartiles were positively associated with systolic and diastolic BP, FBG, glycated hemoglobin, total cholesterol, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, and triglyceride levels, and negatively associated with high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol levels.

Figure S1 shows that over an 8‐year follow‐up period, participants in higher baPWV quartiles had a higher cumulative probability of developing diabetes compared with those in lower quartiles. In the spline analyses, higher baPWV levels appeared to be associated with a progressively increased risk of diabetes in both the overall population and subgroups (Figure 2). When stratified by age and sex, the HRs visually rose more sharply with increasing baPWV in the younger age group (<40 years) and showed a greater reduction in HRs among women compared with men, particularly below the reference point. Similarly, higher baPWV levels appeared to be associated with an increased risk of IR (Figure 3), with similar patterns suggesting potentially stronger associations in women and younger participants.

Over a median follow‐up of 5.6 years, diabetes occurred in 5966 people (incidence rate of 9.1 per 1000 person‐years) (Table 2). The results showed that the incidence density of diabetes increased with higher baPWV quartiles across all sex and age groups (<40 and ≥40 years). This trend remained consistent in the models adjusted for multiple variables. Although higher HRs were noted in individuals aged <40 years than in those aged ≥40 years and in women than in men, statistically significant interactions were found only between sex and baPWV (P for trend=0.008). When analyzed by 4 groups based on age and sex (men aged <40 years, women aged <40 years, men aged ≥40 years, and women aged ≥40 years), the results indicated a monotonic increase in diabetes risk across baPWV quartiles in all subgroups (Table S1). In particular, interplay between the sexes was observed in those aged 40 years and above. Sensitivity analyses employing age‐ and sex‐specific baPWV quartiles revealed consistent results (Table S2). In this analysis, interactions by age group and sex were observed in the relationship between baPWV and the risk of diabetes onset. However, as the age‐specific baPWV quartiles still showed an imbalance in sex distribution (and vice versa for sex‐specific quartiles with respect to age group), it should be noted that the interaction terms were assessed separately.

In a subset of 106 123 participants observed over a median period of 4.52 years, 27 656 cases of IR developed. We noted a similar trend in the development of IR across baPWV quartiles, paralleling the observed diabetes risk trends (Table 3). Multivariable‐adjusted models showed that the risk of developing IR was dose‐responsively associated with the baPWV. Analysis of the 4 demographic groups, divided by age and sex, showed a progressive increase in IR as baPWV quartiles increased. However, no significant interactions observed in the association between baPWV and IR development across age groups in either men or women or between sexes in either age groups (Table S3). Sensitivity analyses using age‐ and sex‐specific baPWV quartiles supported these findings (Table S4). After excluding participants who developed diabetes and IR within the first year of follow‐up, 118 730 and 104 060 participants were analyzed for diabetes and IR risks, respectively. The analysis revealed that a higher baPWV is associated with increased risks of both diabetes and IR. Significant interactions were found between sex and age with baPWV in diabetes risk and between sex and baPWV in IR risk, as detailed in Tables S5 and S6. As shown in Table S7, the annual changes in HOMA‐IR and fasting plasma insulin concentration were positively associated with higher baseline baPWV quartiles. However, there were no significant interactions between age groups (<40 versus ≥40 years), sex, and baseline baPWV for changes in HOMA‐IR or fasting plasma insulin concentration. When the cross‐lagged paths were examined, with a mean follow‐up time of 3.81 years (interquartile range, 1.90–5.24 years), baPWV1 showed a small positive association with FBG2 (standardized correlation coefficient, 0.034 [95% CI, 0.024–0.044], P<0.001) (Figure S2). However, the relationship between FBG1 and baPWV2 was negligible and nonsignificant, indicating that initial FBG levels do not significantly predict future baPWV changes.

DISCUSSION

In this large cohort study involving young and middle‐aged Korean adults, we discovered a link between arterial stiffness, as measured by baPWV, and the risk of diabetes. A notable finding was the association between elevated baPWV and increased IR. Furthermore, although this relationship was evident across the entire study population, it was stronger in women than in men and more pronounced in those <40 years old than in the older cohort.

Recent studies have demonstrated that arterial stiffness can predict or even cause diabetes. In a population‐based study, 2450 participants with a mean age of 71.9 years who underwent cfPWV measurements were followed up for an average of 4.43 years. The HR for incident diabetes was 3.24 (95% CI, 1.51–6.97) when the highest tertiles of cfPWV were compared with the lowest. 14 In a study with a longer follow‐up period, the Framingham Heart Study, a higher cfPWV was linked with various long‐term health outcomes, including diabetes, hypertension, and CVD. 6 Furthermore, path analysis revealed that higher baseline arterial stiffness was associated with higher FBG levels at follow‐up, implying a potential causal link between arterial stiffness and diabetes risk. However, the initial FBG levels did not significantly affect arterial stiffness at follow‐up. This finding is consistent with a study on a younger cohort, which discovered that having a higher cfPWV during adolescence predisposes people to hyperinsulinemia and IR but not vice versa. 15 Three studies have found a link between baPWV and incident diabetes. 16 , 17 , 18 In a study involving 2429 hypertensive patients from the China Stroke Primary Prevention Trial, a connection was found between initial baPWV and the development of new diabetes over an average follow‐up of 4.5 years. Those with increased baPWV were consistently at a higher risk, regardless of their initial fasting glucose levels. 16 In a study of 11 156 Kailuan study participants, the relationship between hypertension, arterial stiffness, and diabetes was investigated. The findings after a median follow‐up of 6.16 years revealed that those with hypertension and elevated arterial stiffness had the highest diabetes risk, followed by those with normal BP but elevated arterial stiffness. Furthermore, arterial stiffness was a better predictor of diabetes than hypertension alone. 17 However, it is noteworthy that these 2 studies had only patients with hypertension. In another segment of the Kailuan study, which followed 14 159 participants who had baPWV measurements for a mean duration of 3.72 years, a higher risk of diabetes was observed with increasing baPWV levels. 18 Specifically, the risk for diabetes was 1.59 (95% CI, 1.34–1.88) in the borderline group (1400≤baPWV<1800 cm/s) and 2.11 (95% CI, 1.71–2.61) in the elevated group (≥1800 cm/s), when compared with the reference group (<1400 cm/s). Unlike previous research, this study uniquely showed that within the normal baPWV range defined by current diagnostic thresholds, a higher baPWV correlates with an increased risk of diabetes. Additionally, path analysis showed a significant relationship between baseline baPWV and subsequent FBG levels, with a regression coefficient of 0.09 (95% CI, 0.05–0.10). This indicates that higher initial baPWV predicts increased glucose levels, reinforcing baPWV's potential as a predictive marker for diabetes. The analysis also reported no significant effect of baseline FBG on future baPWV (β=0.00, 95% CI, −0.02 to 0.02), indicating that baseline FBG did not contribute to increased arterial stiffness. A recently published study investigated the joint effects of arterial stiffness and obesity on the incidence of diabetes and proposed the concept of the arterial stiffness obesity phenotype. 19 It showed that individuals with both elevated baPWV and obesity have the highest risk of developing diabetes. This study has significant clinical implications because it introduces the arterial stiffness obesity phenotype, which allows for a more effective risk stratification and management of diabetes.

Arterial stiffness, measured using methods other than PWV, has been consistently linked to the development of diabetes. A study on 173 patients with essential hypertension discovered that both central systolic BP and the augmentation index from radial artery tonometry were independent predictors of the onset of diabetes. 20 Furthermore, the UK Biobank study found a link between the pulse wave arterial stiffness index and the risk of incident diabetes, lending credence to the link between arterial stiffness and diabetes. 7 The Rotterdam study found that arterial stiffness indicators like the carotid distensibility coefficient and carotid artery intima‐media thickness were associated with an increased risk of diabetes. 21 In a study of 5676 Framingham Heart Study participants, researchers discovered that higher cfPWV and central pulse pressure were both associated with an increased risk of new‐onset diabetes. 22 These findings imply that arterial stiffness may play a role in glucose homeostasis and can be used to predict future diabetes risk.

Several underlying mechanisms can explain the link between arterial stiffness and an increased risk of diabetes development. First, endothelial dysfunction decreases arterial elasticity, resulting in arterial stiffness. In the context of glucose metabolism, impaired endothelial function leads to decreased capillary blood flow, which can affect insulin delivery and glucose uptake by tissues, playing a significant role in the pathophysiology of arterial stiffness and diabetes progression. 23 Endothelial dysfunction as a risk factor for diabetes is supported by a cohort study of 15 010 individuals from the Gutenberg Health Study. 24 The study measured endothelial function using the reactive hyperemia index and found that it was independently associated with hemoglobin A1c levels, increasing the risk of developing prediabetes and diabetes. Second, arterial stiffness has been implicated in liver dysfunction, potentially facilitating the development of IR, a key factor in the progression to diabetes. 25 The liver, with its high arterial flow and low vascular resistance, is particularly sensitive to arterial stiffness, which may disrupt the hepatic arterial buffer response. This disruption can affect the ability of the liver to regulate glucose and exacerbate insulin. Third, arterial stiffness impairs the pancreatic islet microvasculature, which is required for nutrient delivery and glucose sensing. 26 Reduced oxygen supply to islet cells complicates their endocrine function, leading to insufficient insulin secretion in response to glucose, resulting in hyperglycemia. Chronic activation of the sympathetic nervous system may have concurrent effects on these relationships. The sympathetic activity can alter vascular smooth muscle function and the intrinsic properties of the arterial wall, 27 and disturb glucose metabolism by increasing hepatic glucose production, modulating pancreatic insulin and glucagon levels, and impairing glucose uptake in skeletal muscles due to vasoconstriction of peripheral arterioles. 28

Several studies have found sex differences in diabetes risk factors and prognosis; however, research on the relationship between arterial stiffness and the risk of incident diabetes has been both limited and contradictory. In our study, a higher baPWV was associated with a higher risk of diabetes or IR in women than in men. This finding contradicts the findings of the Framingham Heart Study, which found no significant link between cfPWV and diabetes incidence in women. 6 Meanwhile, a large Chinese study discovered that elevated pulse pressure was associated with diabetes onset in women but not in men. 29 It is critical to note that differences in arterial stiffness measurements and study populations make drawing definitive conclusions about sex disparities in diabetes risk factors difficult. However, our findings should be interpreted considering various physiological differences, including hormonal variations between the sexes. Fluctuations in estrogen have a significant impact on vascular function and increase arterial stiffness, especially during menopause, increasing women's risk of IR and diabetes. 30 , 31 Conditions such as polycystic ovary syndrome, which is unique to women, increase this risk due to associated hormonal imbalances. 32 Given that women are more susceptible to microvascular dysfunction, these factors highlight the importance of sex‐specific research on diabetes development. 33 Because arterial stiffness affects the microvasculature, it may have a greater impact on organs such as the liver or muscles involved in glucose metabolism, leading to IR or increased susceptibility to diabetes. Therefore, further research is needed to investigate the links between arterial stiffness, IR, and diabetes, with a particular focus on sex differences.

Our study has several limitations that should be mentioned. First, our study population consisted of young to middle‐aged Korean adults, which raises questions regarding the applicability of our findings to other age groups and ethnicities. Second, our definition of diabetes was based solely on FBG and hemoglobin A1c levels. If the oral glucose tolerance test had not been included, we might have missed some patients with diabetes. Third, although baPWV measurements are more practical for broader use than cfPWV, our exclusive reliance on baPWV may limit the comparability of our findings to studies using cfPWV as a measure of arterial stiffness. 34 , 35 , 36 Other surrogate markers for arterial stiffness should have been considered to provide a more comprehensive understanding. Fourth, participants in this study underwent health checkups during which baPWV measurements were taken once per the program protocol. Consequently, the reproducibility of these measurements was not assessed in this study. Nonetheless, the baPWV test is widely recognized for its high reproducibility. 37 Lastly, although HOMA‐IR is used to evaluate IR, it may not effectively reflect IR in individuals with normal weight and primarily assesses hepatic rather than peripheral tissue resistance, limiting its comprehensiveness in evaluating overall IR. 38 , 39 Although our study extends previous epidemiological research to a younger Korean population, it still lacks causal mediation analysis or animal experimental studies, which are necessary to further elucidate the relationship between arterial stiffness and diabetes.

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, our findings highlight the importance of arterial stiffness monitoring as an early indicator of potential diabetes onset, with a particular emphasis on baPWV as a marker. The study, conducted on a large, low‐risk, and predominantly young cohort stratified by age and sex, found an increased risk of diabetes, even within the normal baPWV range. This finding suggests that normal baPWV values may still be predictive of IR and diabetes, which merits further investigation. Understanding these associations may be critical for developing diabetes prevention measures and treatment strategies.
