Unexpected Metal Ion Elevation and Adverse Tissue Reactions in Trident II Dual Mobility Constructs With Ceramic Heads
Authors: Henry Somerby, Carmelo Burgio, Gabrielle Dykhouse, Edward Grabov, Aarti Shenoy, Daniel Ramirez, Jonathan Vigdorchik, Gwo-Chin Lee, Peter Sculco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDual mobility (DM) constructs reduce dislocation risk in total hip arthroplasty. The Trident II system accepts a cobalt-chromium liner to create a DM bearing. However, dissimilar metal at the cup–liner interface raises concerns for corrosion. This case series describes six cementless primary Trident II DM total hip arthroplasty patients who experienced gradual onset of hip pain with low-grade inflammatory symptoms and elevated serum cobalt. All patients underwent revision surgery for metallic debris reaction. Preoperative magnetic resonance imaging revealed local tissue reaction, and intraoperative findings confirmed corrosion at the cup–liner interface. Surviellance through serum metal ion testing and magnetic resonance imaging may prevent soft tissue damage and facilitate timely revision. As DM systems evolve, further investigation is warranted to evaluate safety.Keywords: Case series, Metallosis, Dual mobility, Corrosion

Introduction

Total hip arthroplasty (THA) is a widely performed and cost-effective procedure for managing degenerative hip disease [1,2]. However, prosthetic dislocation remains a common cause for revision, accounting for 17.4% of all hip revision cases [3]. To address this complication, dual mobility (DM) constructs were developed and have seen increased use in both primary and revision THA due to their enhanced stability and reduced dislocation risk [4,5]. Modular DM designs incorporate 2 distinct articulations: a primary articulation between the femoral head and the polyethylene liner and a secondary articulation between the liner and the acetabular shell. A DM configuration increases the effective head size and jump distance, thereby enhancing range of motion and reducing risk of dislocation [6,7].

Modern DM systems, such as the Trident II modular construct (Stryker; Kalamazoo, MI), are distinctly different than monoblock DM designs and are more used in the United States as they offer greater intraoperative flexibility with multiple bearing options, screw options, and the ability to easily assess cup seating [8]. However, this modularity introduces new potential concerns as mixed-metal junctions create opportunities for mechanically assisted crevice corrosion (MACC), particularly at the liner–shell interface. Fretting and micromotion at this junction can release metal debris and metal ions, which may trigger adverse local tissue reactions (ALTRs) via immune mechanisms such as toll-like receptor 4-mediated inflammatory cascades [9,10]. Recent reports—including a notable case series by Abdelaal, Zachwieja, and Sharkey in 2021—have documented severe corrosion at the modular liner–shell interface in Trident II DM systems, associated with elevated blood metal ion levels and intraoperative findings of metallosis [[11], [12], [13]]. Additionally, malalignment or malseating of modular DM liners has been identified as a contributing factor in corrosion and metal ion release, particularly in the Trident II platform [14]. Previously, our group published a comparison between modular DM and anatomic DM constructs that found that the Trident I (Stryker; Kalamazoo, MI) cohort experienced no revisions for aseptic lymphocyte-dominant vasculitis associated lesions (ALVAL) at the 1 year timepoint [15]. Currently unpublished data from the same cohort at the 5 year timepoint demonstrates no sustained metal ion release in the Trident I model. Given these risks, ongoing clinical surveillance, radiographic assessment, and monitoring of serum metal ion levels are critical in patients with modular DM implants [15].

In this report, we present a case series of 6 patients from 5 surgeons within the same group at 1 institution who developed significant corrosion-related complications following implantation of the Trident II DM acetabular system. Each case was associated with elevated metal ion levels and symptoms requiring revision surgery. All patients had elevated cobalt metal ion testing, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings suggestive of metallosis or intraoperative visible pseudotumor or gross metal ion debris. These findings were uniquely associated with fretting and corrosion at the liner–shell interface and were noted not to be influenced by malseating. Intraoperative assessment and histopathology confirmed ALTRs and liner retrieval analysis demonstrated similar corrosion patterns in all cases. Each case received at least 6 months of follow-up. Additional case parameters and intraoperative findings were also noted (Tables 1 and 2). All patients provided informed written consent to participate in this series and had at least 11 months of follow up after their revision surgery. This study received institutional review board approval.

Case histories

Case 1

A 76-year-old Caucasian female, who provided written consent to be included in this case series, underwent cementless left THA with a Trident II DM liner in September 2019 for end-stage osteoarthritis. The patient received a cobalt chromium liner, a 52-mm acetabular shell, a cementless femoral stem, and a 28-mm −3 mm offset oxinium femoral head. Postoperatively, she was pain-free until approximately 12 months later, when she began experiencing groin, buttock, and lateral hip pain. The pain gradually progressed, requiring the use of a cane for ambulation. She denied systemic symptoms such as fever or malaise.

Laboratory workup included serum cobalt and chromium levels, with cobalt measuring 3.4 mcg/L (normal: ≤1.0 mcg/L) and chromium measuring <1.0 mcg/L (normal: ≤5.0 mcg/L). MRI of the left hip revealed marked synovial thickening and hyperintensity, with extracapsular fluid extending into the trochanteric bursa, consistent with an ALTR (Fig. 1a). Joint aspiration and synovial fluid cultures were negative for infection.

(a) Coronal MRI, showing thickening and hypersensitivity of the synovium lining the left hip joint. (b) Magnified image of explanted Trident II cobalt chromium liner. Arrows indicate deposits that appear green under light microscopy, indicating corrosion byproducts. (c) Anteroposterior (AP) radiographs 7 weeks post revision surgery. Radiographs show implant in place with no evidence of complication. (d) Anteroposterior radiographs 1-year postrevision surgery. Radiographs show implant in place with no evidence of complication.

The patient underwent revision surgery in November 2020. Intraoperatively, the oxinium femoral head and the titanium femoral trunnion appeared pristine; however, the backside of the DM liner showed peripheral black discoloration consistent with corrosion (Fig. 1b). The locking mechanism and acetabular shell were intact. A high crossed linked polyethylene liner and a 40-mm oxinium head with a titanium adapter sleeve were implanted. The hip was stable intraoperatively with no signs of impingement or instability.

On retrieval analysis, the liner taper was observed under digital optical microscopy, which showed burnishing, greenish deposits, likely corrosion by-products, and column damage [16,17] (Fig. 1b). Pathologic examination of the surgical tissues revealed histologic features consistent with ALTR/pseudotumor without corrosion products identified microscopically.

Postoperatively, radiographs showed good component positioning without fracture or dislocation (Fig. 1c). At 3 months, she reported satisfaction with her hip, minimal thigh soreness on stairs, and was ambulating independently. Serial radiographs showed stable implants throughout the follow-up period (Fig. 1d).

Case 2

A 61-year-old Caucasian male, who provided written consent to be included in this case series, underwent right cementless THA with a Trident II DM liner in April 2019 for osteoarthritis. The patient received a cobalt chromium DM liner, a 56-mm acetabular shell, a press-fit femoral stem and a 28-mm ceramic femoral head. His postoperative course was uneventful until June 2020, when he underwent a contralateral THA. At his 6-week left THA follow-up, he reported perceived leg length discrepancy and mild discomfort in the right hip. Radiographs of the right hip showed well-fixed components with no interval changes (Fig. 2a).

(a) Anteroposterior radiographs 6 weeks postoperatively from initial replacement surgery. Radiographs show new implant in place with no evidence of complication. (b and c) Axial (b) and Coronal (c) MRI findings 11 weeks after replacement surgery which demonstrate an adverse local tissue reaction with thickening synovium of the left hip joint. (d and e) Trident II acetabular liner under magnification. (d) Majority of the taper exhibits scratches and material loss. (e) (arrow) Liner shows extensive corrosion which appears to be chemically driven as evidence by blue-green markings in white box. Column damage is observed. (f) Anteroposterior radiographs 10 months postrevision surgery. Radiographs show implant in place with no evidence of complication.

Serum metal ion testing revealed elevated cobalt (19.4 mcg/L) and titanium (11 mcg/L), with normal chromium (3.2 mcg/L) in September 2020 (normal: ≤1.0 mcg/L for cobalt, ≤5.0 mcg/L for chromium, and ≤10.0 mcg/L for titanium). MRI of the right hip demonstrated hypertrophied synovium with signal abnormalities extending into the greater trochanteric bursa, concerning for ALTR (Fig. 2b and c). Aspiration and cultures were negative for periprosthetic joint infection.

In September 2020, he underwent revision THA and intraoperative findings included a pseudotumor with moderate joint fluid. The trunnion was not damaged, but the acetabular liner exhibited extensive corrosion (Fig. 2d and e). The revision involved exchanging the femoral head and acetabular component: a 36-mm ceramic head and a new highly cross-linked liner were implanted into a new 62-mm Trident shell.

On retrieval analysis, the liner taper exhibited burnishing and column damage, modes of corrosion which cause release of metal debris and ions into the periprosthetic joint space. Review of the surgical tissues revealed histologic findings consistent with ALTR/pseudotumor. No corrosion products were identified microscopically in the representatively submitted tissues.

Recovery was uncomplicated until 10 months postoperatively, when he presented with groin pain exacerbated by flexion; radiographs remained unchanged (Fig. 2f), and he was diagnosed with iliopsoas tendinitis, which was treated conservatively.

Case 3

A 68-year-old Caucasian male, who provided written consent to be included in this case series, underwent right cementless THA with a cobalt chromium Trident II DM liner, a 56-mm acetabular shell, a cementless stem, and a 28-mm −2.7 mm offset ceramic femoral head in March 2018 for end stage osteoarthritis. Six months postoperatively, following a right lateral unicompartmental knee replacement, he reported persistent groin and thigh pain. On examination, the hip was stable and the wound healed. Radiographs showed appropriate implant positioning (Fig. 3a). He was initially diagnosed with iliopsoas tendinitis and treated with non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and physical therapy.

(a) Anteroposterior radiographs 6 months postreplacement surgery. Radiographs show implant in place with no evidence of complication. (b and c) Coronal MRI, showing bone marrow edema around the acetabular cup and fluid build-up around the hip and greater trochanter. These findings were suggestive of an acetabular stress reaction, infection, metal allergy, or metallosis. (d) Trident II cobalt chromium liner magnified imaging. Burnishing exhibited on majority of taper. Likely column damage observed throughout, including noncontact areas (white box). (e) Anteroposterior radiographs 6 weeks postrevision surgery. Radiographs show right hip implant in place with no evidence of complication.

Symptoms persisted, and at 10 months postop he reported a deep ache when lying on his side and mild night pain. Repeat radiographs remained unchanged. A corticosteroid injection consisting of 1 mL of 1% lidocaine and 1 mL of dexamethasone (10 mg/mL) provided temporary relief. At 12 months, the pain recurred and radiographs showed no implant migration or loosening. MRI revealed fluid accumulation around the joint and greater trochanter, along with bone marrow edema surrounding the acetabular component (Fig. 3b and c). Labs revealed mildly elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and c-reactive protein (CRP). Aspiration showed white blood cells 1660 with 12% polymorphonuclear leukocytes—below infection thresholds. Cobalt and chromium levels were 6.1 mcg/L and 2.4 mcg/L, respectively.

In March 2019, he underwent revision surgery. Postrevision implant retrieval examination using high magnification microscopy showed the DM liner exhibited column damage over most of the taper surface area (Fig. 3d). The trunnion and femoral head appeared normal. The liner was replaced with a 40-mm ceramic head and titanium sleeve and a cross-linked polyethylene liner.

On retrieval analysis, the liner taper showed discontinuous bands of corrosion and regions of corrosion-driven material loss similar to patients 1 and 2. Histologic review of the tissues was consistent with ALTR/pseudotumor.

Postop cobalt/chromium levels trended downward (4.9/1.2 mcg/L at 6 weeks; 2.5/1.0 mcg/L by 3 months) and radiographs revealed no complications (Fig. 3e). He returned to skiing and daily activities uneventfully.

Case 4

A 57-year-old Caucasian male, who provided written consent to be included in this case series, underwent left cementless THA with a Trident II cobalt-chromium DM liner, a 56 mm Trident II acetabular cup, a 28-mm ceramic femoral head and a cementless femoral stem in October 2018 for severe osteoarthritis. Early postoperative course was uneventful, with return to function. At 2.5 years postop, he developed groin, buttock, and adductor pain without trauma. The pain was severe enough to interfere with ambulation and sleep. On examination, the incision was well healed, with no instability or infection signs. Radiographs showed no migration or component loosening (Fig. 4a).

(a) Anteroposterior radiographs 2.5 years postreplacement surgery. Radiographs show left hip implant in place with no evidence of complication. (b) Coronal MRI, showing fracture of the anterior column, poor integration, and a synovial expansion, that is, suggestive of severe metallosis. (c, d, and e) Trident II cobalt-chromium liner magnified imaging. (c) Bands of corrosion related material loss noted by the red arrow. (d) Damage seen continuing over the entire taper region. Material loss from this region can include debris particles from alloy, ions of cobalt and chromium, molybdenum rich alloy phases, and oxide particles. (e) High magnification imaging of material loss. (f) Anteroposterior radiographs 3 months postrevision surgery. Radiographs show left hip implant in place with no evidence of complication. (g) Anteroposterior radiographs 1 year post second revision surgery.

An MRI was obtained, revealing a fracture of the anterior acetabular column and synovial proliferation with large low-signal debris (Fig. 4b). ESR and CRP were slightly elevated (24 mm/h and 1.4 mg/dL). Aspiration showed no signs of infection. No metal ion testing was completed. Revision THA was performed in August 2021. Postrevision retrieval analysis findings included corrosion bands on the liner taper and shell interface (Fig. 4c, d, and e). A 64-mm Trident II multihole shell, elevated liner, and a-36 mm + 4 ceramic head were implanted. The cup was placed at 45° abduction and 20° anteversion.

On retrieval analysis, the liner taper showed extensive corrosion-driven material loss, burnishing, and column damage over the entire taper region. Histologic examination was consistent with an ALTR/pseudotumor (Fig. 5).

Hematoxylin and eosin stains of patient 4 pseudotumor. (a) 2.5x WSI photomicrograph demonstrates a low powered full thickness view through the wall of the pseudotumor. Circled is paucicellular densely hyalinized tissue, followed by a region with slightly increased cellularity consisting of lymphocytes, foamy histiocytes which are occasionally associated with implant-associated debris. (b) 10x WSI photomicrograph of the interface between the pseudotumor (upper) and surrounding cauterized soft tissue (lower). Circled are prominent aggregates of predominantly lymphocytic chronic inflammation, with scattered mononuclear histiocytes containing implant-associated debris particles. (c) 10x WSI photomicrograph of the inner lining of the pseudotumor. Acellular fibrinoid debris surfaces hyalinized and paucicellular soft tissue are marked by the arrow.

Initial recovery was favorable, though by 10 months postrevision, the patient reported recurring anterior pain. Imaging revealed poor bony ingrowth in the anterior column but no gross loosening (Fig. 4f). Due to persistent pain, acetabular nonunion, and pelvic discontinuity, a second revision was performed in November 2022. A custom triflange component was implanted with a-36 mm polyethylene liner. Intraoperatively, no metallosis was observed. At 6 weeks and 12 weeks, the patient was ambulating pain-free, and at 1 year, had resumed golfing and daily activities (Fig. 4g).

Case 5

A 46-year-old Asian female, who provided written consent to be included in this case series, underwent robotic-assisted left THA in June 2017 using the MAKO system for dysplastic hip osteoarthritis. Implants included a-52 mm Trident shell, 42-mm cobalt-chromium DM liner, and a DePuy S-ROM modular femoral stem with a Delta ceramic head, and acetabular screw fixation. Her initial postoperative course was uneventful, and she resumed all activities, including yoga. At 1 year, she reported groin tightness during single-leg poses. Radiographs demonstrated femoral neck notching without progressive change over several years (Fig. 6a).

(a) Progression of femoral neck notch from 1 year following primary THA to 1 month prior to revision hip arthroplasty. (b) Coronal and axial MRI images of pseudotumor extension. (c) Explanted pseudotumor with ruler for scale. (d) Explanted cobalt-chromium liner with corrosion present. (e) Anteroposterior radiograph following revision hip arthroplasty.

At 6 years, she presented with new-onset left thigh pain, and at 7 years 11 months, she developed buttock pain, subjective leg length discrepancy, and local warmth. Labs revealed elevated cobalt (2.7 mcg/L), chromium (1.2 mcg/L), ESR 24 mm/h, and normal CRP. No titanium levels were obtained. MRI using MAVRIC protocol showed a large pseudotumor extending into the iliopsoas and gluteal compartments with signal voids suggestive of metallic debris (Fig. 6b).

Revision THA in July 2025 involved excision of a 14-cm pseudotumor, removal of the well-fixed Sivash range of motion (S-ROM) stem but preservation of the ingrown sleeve, and exchange of the liner and head (Fig. 6c). The S-ROM stem was removed due to excessive notching, potentially compromising the implant. Intraoperative findings included corrosion at the liner–shell junction and notching at the neck–stem interface (Fig. 6d). A new S-ROM stem, 36 mm Delta ceramic head, and neutral polyethylene liner were implanted. She tolerated the procedure well and recovered without complications, regaining full range of motion and functional independence. Postoperative radiographs revealed no complications (Fig. 6e).

Histologic review demonstrated a centrally cystic reaction to implant debris with exuberant granulomatous features. Typical histologic features of ALTR were not identified in the representatively submitted tissues.

At 1.5 months, she reported continued swelling and presence of drainage but imaging showed stable components. In August 2025, a debridement, antibiotics, and implant retention procedure was performed for periprosthetic joint infection and in September 2025, an antibiotic spacer was inserted into the joint. In January 2026, the infection was found to be resolved and implants were reimplanted. The implants appear in good position with no signs of complications.

Case 6

A 77-year-old Caucasian female, who provided written consent to be included in this case series, underwent right cementless THA with a Trident II cobalt chromium DM liner, cementless 52 mm acetabular shell, cementless femoral stem, and a-28 mm ceramic head in April 2022. Initial recovery was uncomplicated. At 5 months, she reported deep hip pain and underwent spine MRI, which revealed spondylosis. A computed tomography (CT) scan showed a stress reaction along the medial acetabular wall (Fig. 7a). She received epidural steroid injections, which provided only temporary relief.

(a) CT scan showing a stress reaction along the medial acetabular wall (gray arrow). (b) Bone scan demonstrating radiolucency around the acetabular cup. (c) AP radiographs at 6 week postoperative revealing no complications. (d) AP radiographs at week 6 after secondary revision revealing no complications. (e) Liner taper with minor fretting scars, inflammation, with scattered mononuclear histiocytes containing implant-associated debris particles. (c) 10x WSI photomicrograph of the inner lining of the pseudotumor. Acellular fibrinoid debris surfaces hyalinized and paucicellular soft tissue are marked by the arrow.

A bone scan and CT in mid-2024 demonstrated radiolucency around the acetabular cup, suspicious for loosening (Fig. 7b). No cobalt or chromium testing was completed. In August 2024, revision THA was performed. Intraoperative findings included superiorly migrated, loose cup with metallosis and corrosion debris at the shell–liner interface. The cup was revised to a 62-mm multihole Trident II shell with morselized allograft. A 40-mm biolox ceramic head and X3 liner were used.

On retrieval analysis, the liner and shell both exhibited minor fretting scars and no significant corrosion damage (Fig. 7e). Histologic review showed features more consistent with traditional implant debris reactions, without features suggestive of ALTR/pseudotumor.

Initial postoperative course was uneventful and radiographs revealed no complication (Fig. 7c). In January 2025, she developed right lower extremity DVT. Imaging revealed acetabular cup impinging the external iliac vessel due to cup migration. A second revision with a custom triflange was performed in May 2025. Components were well placed, and the patient had no pain at 6 weeks postop (Fig. 7d).

Discussion

This case series describes 6 patients who underwent revision THA for metallosis and related complications after Trident II DM implant use. Metallosis and ALVAL is a rare but serious complication of THA, involving metal particle deposition in periprosthetic tissues leading to synovitis, pseudotumor formation, and soft tissue necrosis. Though traditionally associated with metal-on-metal bearings, recent evidence shows that MACC can also occur at modular junctions, such as cobalt chrome femoral heads on titanium stems or cobalt chromium liner in titanium shells in modular DM constructs utilizing dissimilar metals [18,19]. In our series, all patients had elevated cobalt levels, had MRI findings suggestive of atypical soft tissue reaction, or visible intraoperative metallosis and pseudotumor. These findings mirror previous studies. Civinini et al. found elevated cobalt or chromium levels in 29.7% of asymptomatic patients with DM implants, while Bonano et al. reported early MACC-related failure in 4 modular DM cases [20,21]. Additionally, the timeline of these complications aligns with other studies, such as McGrory et al. which found events of ALTR as early as 9 months postoperatively [22]. These studies, together with our results, support the use of serum cobalt/chromium testing and metal artifact–reduction MRI as first-line diagnostic tools in symptomatic patients with modular implants.

DM implants were developed to reduce dislocation risk by combining a polyethylene liner that articulates with both the femoral head and acetabular shell. This dual articulation increases jump distance and range of motion. However, the shift from monoblock to modular DM constructs, allowing for intraoperative flexibility, has introduced new interfaces prone to fretting and corrosion, particularly between cobalt-chromium liners and titanium shells. Emerging evidence has linked these mixed-metal junctions to ALTRs. Ozan et al. [23]documented 15 cases of metallosis in patients with non–metal-on-metal implants. Putman et al. [24]found higher rates of ALTR and corrosion in modular DM implants than monoblock systems. In our cohort, all patients received modular DM constructs and exhibited signs of corrosion despite the femoral head made of ceramic material, reinforcing the liner–shell junction as a critical site of potential failure.

The Trident II acetabular system, introduced in 2018, features a 3D-printed titanium shell, deeper screw pockets, and a modular design that accommodates cobalt-chromium DM liners via a taper junction. The previous iteration, The Trident I acetabular system was constructed using a 2-dimensional titanium printing system. The updated manufacturing system allows for a thinner shell and a deeper screw position [25]. All patients in this series were implanted with Trident II DM systems. None had other cobalt-chromium components, implicating the liner–shell taper as the likely source of ion release. Intraoperative findings frequently included gray synovial debris, backside liner corrosion, and in several cases, significant tissue and acetabular bone destruction requiring eventual custom triflange reconstruction in 3 of 6 cases (Table 2). Previous studies support these concerns. Romero et al. reported a 5% incidence of liner malseating in Trident I, Trident I PSL, and Trident II shells, potentially predisposing to micromotion and fretting [14]. Within this study, the rate of Trident II malseating was lower than Trident I and was comparable to Trident I PSL. Ulrich et al. also described poor screw engagement in the same system, suggesting mechanical vulnerabilities beyond the taper interface [25]. In contrast, a randomized control trial comparison of the DM and conventional G7 system (Zimmer Biomet; Warsaw, IN) did not reveal any ALTRs or other complications [26].

Our case series implant retrieval imaging demonstrated extensive corrosion and corrosion-driven material loss which likely led to debris particle and metal ion-mediated ALTRs and ultimately, necessitated revision surgery (Table 2). The causes for these adverse clinical outcomes might be exacerbated by malseating, which has been found to worsen corrosion severity. Similar corrosion features were previously described in non-DM, modular acetabular shell–liner junctions which represent a departure from classical micromotion-driven MACC corrosion and indicate a likelihood of more crevice corrosion-like processes. On radiographic examination before revision surgery, all cases were found to be well seated. While radiographic examination can help identify malseating, this method has limitations and is not always certain.

Given these findings, heightened surveillance is recommended in patients with Trident II modular DM constructs, especially those with new or unexplained hip symptoms. Until long-term data are available, careful patient selection and monitoring are essential, particularly in younger, active populations.

This case series presents 1 of the first detailed clinical reports linking metallosis and ALTRs specifically to the liner–shell interface of the Trident II modular DM system. A key strength of this study is the consistent presentation across all cases, with clinical symptoms, imaging findings, metal ion elevation, and intraoperative evidence of corrosion and implant retrieval analysis (Fig. 5). The inclusion of multirevision cases and varied implant configurations offers valuable insight into the progression of corrosion-related failure across different patient profiles. However, this study has limitations inherent to its retrospective design. The small sample size limits the generalizability of findings and prevents comparison with other implant types or systems. Not all patients had complete metal ion profiles before and after revision, and several lacked extended follow-up following their final surgery. Additionally, some of these cases involve the off-label use of implants, such as mixing implants from different manufacturers, which could impact development of metallosis. However, this practice is common in the United States and with DM constructs when the liner and head diameters are compatible. Finally, although intraoperative findings strongly suggested corrosion at the liner–shell taper, direct material, or tribological analysis of explanted components was not performed in all cases, leaving the precise mechanisms of failure inferential in some cases.

Summary

This case series demonstrates that Trident II modular DM liners can be susceptible to fretting and corrosion at the liner–shell interface, resulting in clinically significant metallosis and ALVAL necessitating revision surgery. These episodes of metallosis are distinct from instances of linear malseating and present a new complication with the Trident II DM system. Further study is required to better understand the differences between Trident I and Trident II acetabular shells and whether reduced thickness of the acetabular shell or varying tolerances of the cup–liner taper junction may have contributed to an increase in clinically significant corrosion. Routine surveillance with serum cobalt and chromium levels may aid in early detection of corrosion-related complications. As the use of Trident II implants continues, it is critical that orthopedic surgeons remain vigilant in identifying, investigating, and managing similar postoperative presentations to optimize patient outcomes.
