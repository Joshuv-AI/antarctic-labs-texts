Structure-Fair Quantum Circuit Complexity: An Information-Theoretic Lower Bound
HongZheng Liu 1,∗,† , YiNuo Tian 1 , Zhiyue Wu 1 1 Independent, China ∗ First author † Corresponding author: weiyouyeyu@foxmail.com

Abstract
Fairly quantifying the complexity of quantum states that possess intrinsic structures (such as symmetries or encodings) is a fundamental challenge for benchmarking quantum technologies. We introduce the "Reference-Contingent Complexity" (RCC). Its core idea is to utilize the quantum relative entropy to measure the extent to which a state deviates from its "structured vacuum" (the maximum entropy state within its constrained subspace), thereby only pricing the generation of non-trivial information. We establish a central theorem, establishing that the RCC is a rigorous information-theoretic lower bound on universal quantum circuit complexity, a bound that features a linear leading term, a universal logarithmic correction, and a precise physical correction for the non-uniformity of the spectral distribution. Furthermore, we establish an operational protocol based on quantum hypothesis testing, which connects this theoretical lower bound to experimentally accessible observables. This work provides a structurally-fair yardstick for quantum technologies and offers a new perspective for exploring the intrinsic connection between computational cost and the generation of non-trivial information.

Theoretical Framework: Reference-Contingent Complexity (RCC)

To establish a structurally fair theory of complexity, we must first precisely define the mathematical objects and physical setting that constitute its foundation. This chapter aims to systematically elaborate the core construction of Reference-Contingent Complexity (RCC). We begin from the setting of a universal quantum system, introduce the concept of the “reference set” as the cornerstone of the theory—a mathematical tool to formalize the system’s intrinsic structure as an a priori background—and ultimately, from first principles, provide a complexity measure rooted in information theory and possessing a clear physical meaning.

2.1
System Setting and Notational Conventions

We consider a finite-dimensional quantum system defined on a Hilbert space
H
of total dimension
d
. The dynamical evolution of the system is driven by a universal finite gate set
G\subseteq\mathrm{SU}(d)
with cardinality
g
. Our fundamental objective is to quantify the minimum resource cost required to prepare a target state
\rho
from a simple initial state (such as a product state) using a quantum circuit composed of this gate set. This minimum resource cost, the optimal circuit complexity
C^{*}_{\mathrm{opt}}(\epsilon)(\rho)
, is defined as the length of the shortest sequence of atomic instructions capable of preparing
\rho
within a precision
\epsilon
(as measured by the trace distance). To ensure that our complexity benchmark can be compared with concrete physical implementations, we must objectively and unambiguously calibrate the fundamental unit of computational operations. This leads to the core postulate of our framework:

Postulate A (Atomic Information Cost).
The fundamental computational cost of any quantum circuit is quantified by the total number of information bits required to describe the shortest corresponding serial sequence of atomic instructions,
C_{\mathrm{opt}}\cdot\log(\Gamma_{R})
, where:

1.

C_{\mathrm{opt}}
is the length of the shortest sequence of atomic instructions.

2.

\log(\Gamma_{R})
is the information cost of a single atomic instruction, i.e., the “information bandwidth.” This quantity is uniquely determined by the hardware’s capability, namely the size of the alphabet of atomic instructions,
\Gamma_{R}
.

The value of
\Gamma_{R}
is based on a rigorously formalized computational model that combines the gate set
g
with the hardware’s addressability
|S_{R}|
. A complete exposition of this model, including the precise definition of
\Gamma_{R}=g\cdot|S_{R}|
and the handling of parallel circuits and continuous-parameter gates, is axiomatically defined in detail in Appendix
A
. This postulate directly links abstract computational steps with the physical capabilities of the hardware (gate set size and addressing range), enabling our theory to capture the essential differences in computational power among different physical platforms. To ensure the self-consistency of the theory, we list here the key information-theoretic quantities that will be used in the subsequent derivations. The von Neumann entropy of a quantum state
\rho
is defined as
S(\rho)=-\mathrm{Tr}(\rho\cdot\log(\rho))
[
21
]
. The quantum relative entropy between two quantum states
\rho
and
\sigma
is
D(\rho||\sigma)=\mathrm{Tr}[\rho\cdot(\log(\rho)-\log(\sigma))]
;

]

2.2
Reference Set and Structured Priors

The realization of “structural fairness” depends on the precise mathematical characterization of the system’s intrinsic “structure.” The true cost of a logical operation on a fault-tolerant quantum computer should be independent of the vast number of physical qubits mobilized to maintain the error-correcting code; for a system with a conservation law, its dynamical evolution is restricted to specific symmetry sectors, and this constraint is “for free.” To systematically isolate these structures endowed by physical laws or deliberate encoding, we introduce the core construct of this paper.

Definition 2.1 (Constrained Reference Set).
A reference set
R
is a collection of mutually commuting constraint projectors
\{P_{i}\}
, whose common image space is defined by the total projector
\Pi_{R}=\prod_{i}P_{i}
, with the requirement that
\Pi_{R}\neq 0
. This total projector defines the structured subspace
H_{R}=\Pi_{R}\cdot H
, with dimension
d_{R}=\mathrm{Tr}(\Pi_{R})
.

(Note: For structures defined by mutually exclusive sectors (such as eigenspaces of different conserved charges), one should first select the total projector for a target sector,
P_{\mathrm{sector}}
, as
\Pi_{R}
(or as a member of
\{P_{i}\}
), and then define finer internal structures, so as to avoid the product of multiple mutually exclusive projectors being zero.)

The reference set
R
physically formalizes the “structured priors” of the system. In a system with a
U(1)
conserved charge
Q
, one should first fix the total projector for the target sector
P_{\mathrm{sector}}
(corresponding to a given charge value), and then select mutually commuting internal constraints
\{P_{i}\}
within that sector, such that
\Pi_{R}=P_{\mathrm{sector}}\cdot(\prod_{i}P_{i})\neq 0
; in a stabilizer error-correcting code,
\{P_{i}\}
would be the generators of the stabilizer group, and their common
+1
eigenspace is the code subspace. Once the reference set is given, we can define a state that is most disordered under this structural constraint.

Definition 2.2 (Reference State).
The reference state
\sigma_{R}
associated with a reference set
R
is defined as the maximally mixed state on the subspace
H_{R}
defined by the total projector
\Pi_{R}
. Its mathematical form is:

\sigma_{R}=\frac{\Pi_{R}}{d_{R}}

(2.1)

The reference state
\sigma_{R}
physically plays the role of a “zero point in the structured background.” It contains all the structural information encoded by the reference set
R
, but beyond that, it contains no additional quantum correlations or information that would require non-trivial dynamical evolution to generate. Therefore,
\sigma_{R}
can be regarded as a physically meaningful “structured vacuum” state. The information-theoretic “distance” of a quantum state relative to this vacuum naturally quantifies the non-trivial information content it embodies, which transcends the a priori structure. A detailed discussion on how to objectively select a reference set based on physical principles, and the intrinsic robustness of our framework with respect to this choice, is provided in Appendix
F.3
.

2.3
Definition of Reference-Contingent Complexity

Based on the foregoing preparations, we can now give the formal definition of Reference-Contingent Complexity. RCC aims to quantify the degree of information “excitation” of a quantum state
\rho
(whose support lies within the subspace
H_{R}
) relative to its structured background
\sigma_{R}
, normalized by the maximum amount of information that a single universal operation can provide.

Definition 2.3 (Reference-Contingent Complexity):
For a quantum state
\rho
whose support is contained within the subspace
H_{R}
, its Reference-Contingent Complexity
C_{R}(\rho)
with respect to the reference set
R
is defined as:

C_{R}(\rho)=\frac{1}{\log\Gamma_{R}}D(\rho||\sigma_{R})

(2.2)

where
\Gamma_{R}
is defined by Postulate A. This definition directly links complexity to the distinguishability of two quantum states. A highly complex state must be extremely easy to distinguish from its structured vacuum
\sigma_{R}
. By expanding the relative entropy
D(\rho||\sigma_{R})
and utilizing the property of the reference state
\sigma_{R}
as a maximally mixed state, we can obtain an equivalent expression with more physical intuition
1
1

1

It should be emphasized that the strict validity of this equivalence and the finiteness of the relative entropy
D(\rho\|\sigma_{R})
are conditional on the support of the quantum state being entirely contained within the subspace
H_{R}
, i.e.,
\mathrm{supp}(\rho)\subseteq H_{R}
. Considering that unavoidable state leakage in physical systems may cause this condition to be violated, we have discussed in detail in Appendix
B.1
how to handle this robustly using mathematical techniques such as projection or smoothing.
:

C_{R}(\rho)=\frac{1}{\log\Gamma_{R}}(\log d_{R}-S(\rho))

(2.3)

Eq. (
2.3

(\log d_{R}-S(\rho))
, represents the “information deficit” or “degree of order” of the quantum state
\rho
within its respective structured subspace
H_{R}
.
\log d_{R}
is the maximum Shannon information capacity of this subspace (i.e.,
S(\sigma_{R})
), while
S(\rho)
is the von Neumann entropy actually contained in the state
\rho

\log\Gamma_{R}
provides a precise calibration of the fundamental unit of computational resources. Therefore, the physical picture of RCC is: the structurally fair complexity of a quantum state is equal to the minimum amount of information required to be injected for its preparation, divided by the maximum information bandwidth that a single complete computational operation can provide. To specifically illustrate how RCC achieves structural fairness, we once again consider a stabilizer code that encodes
k
logical qubits into
N
physical qubits. The dimension of its code subspace
H_{R}
is
d_{R}=2^{k}
. For a logical pure state
\rho_{L}

C_{R}(\rho_{L})=k/\log(g_{L}\cdot k)

k

N
, while the denominator correctly reflects the cost of the logical operation set. This clearly demonstrates how RCC, by its very definition, automatically strips away the ‘free’ encoding structure and only prices the non-trivial logical information. In Appendix
C

3
Main Results: A Structurally Fair Lower Bound for Circuit Complexity

Having precisely constructed the theoretical framework for Reference-Contingent Complexity, we now present and prove the central theorem of this paper. This theorem, under a universal model of quantum computation, links a state complexity in an information-theoretic sense (RCC) with a process complexity in a computational science sense (the optimal number of atomic instruction steps) through a rigorous inequality. This not only endows RCC with a solid meaning in computational complexity theory but also provides a structurally fair lower bound for the resource consumption of quantum circuits, derived from fundamental physical principles.

3.1
Main Theorem

We first state the central theoretical result of this paper. The theorem, under ideal conditions, assumes that the support of the target state
\rho
is strictly contained within the subspace
H_{R}
, i.e.,
\mathrm{supp}(\rho)\subseteq H_{R}
. The rigorous proof of this theorem can be found in Appendix
A
. For the infinitesimal leakage that is unavoidable in physical reality, the theoretical framework remains robust. By applying projection and normalization to the state or smoothing to the reference state, the main theorem can be shown to still hold, with the addition of only controllable logarithmic correction terms, as detailed in Appendix
B.1
.

Theorem 3.1 (RCC Lower Bound on Circuit Complexity).
For a quantum computation model driven by a universal gate set
G
, any quantum circuit that can prepare a quantum state
\rho
(whose support is located within the subspace
H_{R}
defined by the reference set
R
) within a precision
\varepsilon
, its minimum number of atomic instruction steps
C_{\mathrm{opt}}^{(\varepsilon)}(\rho)
satisfies the following inequality:

C_{\mathrm{opt}}^{(\varepsilon)}(\rho)\geq\frac{1}{\log\Gamma_{R}}D(\rho||\sigma_{R})-\frac{c_{1}}{\log\Gamma_{R}}\left[\log(\max\{1,C_{\mathrm{opt}}^{(\varepsilon)}(\rho)\})+\log(1/\varepsilon)\right]+\frac{1}{\log\Gamma_{R}}\Delta_{\mathrm{spec}}(\rho)

(3.1)

where
c_{1}
is a system-independent universal constant of
O(1)
, and the spectral skew term
\Delta_{\mathrm{spec}}(\rho)
is defined as:

\Delta_{\mathrm{spec}}(\rho)=S(\rho)-H_{\mathrm{min}}(\rho)

(3.2)

where
S(\rho)
is the von Neumann entropy, and
H_{\mathrm{min}}(\rho)=-\log\lVert\rho\rVert_{\infty}
is the min-entropy, determined by the largest eigenvalue of
\rho
.Eq. (
3.1
) constitutes the theoretical core of this paper. The right-hand side of the inequality consists of three terms with clear physical roles:

1.

Linear Leading Term
:
\frac{1}{\log\Gamma_{R}}D(\rho||\sigma_{R})
, which is
C_{R}(\rho)
. It profoundly reveals that the minimum number of computational steps required to prepare a quantum state is, fundamentally, determined by the structurally fair complexity of that state. The more non-trivial information a state contains within its structured vacuum
\sigma_{R}
(the reference state), the higher the computational cost, in a linear relationship, that any algorithm preparing it must incur.

2.

Logarithmic Correction Term
:
-\frac{c_{1}}{\log\Gamma_{R}}[\dots]
. This term originates from the unavoidable information-theoretic overhead of describing the circuit itself (its length
C_{\mathrm{opt}}
) and the approximation precision (
\varepsilon
). In a prefix-free universal computation framework, the description of a process or an object requires the consumption of logarithmic information resources related to its own complexity or precision requirements. The growth of this term is much slower than that of the leading term and is secondary on a macroscopic scale, but its presence ensures the theoretical rigor of this lower bound.

3.

Spectral Skew Correction Term
:
+\frac{1}{\log\Gamma_{R}}\Delta_{\mathrm{spec}}(\rho)
. This is a key physical correction, with a coefficient of exactly
1
; this is an additive correction with a coefficient of 1 rigorously derived from an entropy identity (Proposition A.4) in Appendix
A
, rather than an order-of-magnitude estimate.
\Delta_{\mathrm{spec}}(\rho)
measures the non-uniformity of the quantum state’s spectral distribution. This term enters the inequality with a positive sign, implying that states with more non-uniform spectral distributions—for instance, a mixed state with a few large eigenvalues and a "sea" of small eigenvalues—conversely have a higher lower bound on their preparation complexity. This conclusion aligns with physical intuition: a state with highly concentrated information or a highly ordered structure (
H_{\mathrm{min}}(\rho)
is much smaller than
S(\rho)
), compared to a state with a more uniform spectral distribution, has a more special structure, and thus requires a more fine-tuned and complex dynamical evolution to be generated. In particular, for any pure state, we have
S(\rho)=H_{\mathrm{min}}(\rho)=0
, hence the spectral skew term
\Delta_{\mathrm{spec}}(\rho)
vanishes, making the lower bound expression for pure states more compact.

(Note: It is worth noting that Eq. (
3.1
) is a bootstrap inequality. Its non-recursive, explicit solution (a closed-form lower bound), which is convenient for direct use in data analysis, is derived in detail along with an implementation scheme in Appendix
C
.)

3.2
Proof Blueprint

The complete and rigorous mathematical proof of Theorem 3.1 is placed in Appendix

. Here, we only sketch the core logic of its proof. This proof chain systematically connects profound results from computational science, algorithmic information theory, and quantum information theory, and is divided into four steps:

1.

Encoding Argument (
C_{\mathrm{opt}}\to K_{\varepsilon}(\rho|R)
):
Any quantum circuit composed of
C_{\mathrm{opt}}
atomic instruction steps can itself be viewed as a computer program that describes
\rho
. Within a prefix-free description framework, the total number of information bits required to describe this circuit (upper-bounded by
C_{\mathrm{opt}}\cdot\log\Gamma_{R}
plus logarithmic corrections) necessarily constitutes an upper bound on the smoothed relative Kolmogorov complexity
K_{\varepsilon}(\rho|R)
of the generated state
\rho
.

2.

Algorithmic Information Theory Bound (
K_{\varepsilon}(\rho|R)\to D_{\max}^{\varepsilon}
):
A fundamental result in quantum algorithmic information theory is that the smoothed Kolmogorov complexity of a quantum state,
K_{\varepsilon}(\rho|R)
, is lower-bounded by its smoothed max-relative entropy
D_{\max}^{\varepsilon}(\rho||\sigma_{R})
with respect to the reference state
\sigma_{R}

[
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
]
. This step connects an uncomputable quantity,
K_{\varepsilon}
, with a rigorously defined information-theoretic quantity,
D_{\max}^{\varepsilon}
.
(Note: In particular, a key technical point is that the universal constant in this bound is independent of the system’s dimension, thereby ensuring the universality of our final lower bound. Its rigorous proof can be found in Appendix
B.2
.)

3.

Proposition 3.1 (Entropy Identity):
For a quantum state
\rho
with support contained in
H_{R}
, the following identity holds:

D_{\max}(\rho||\sigma_{R})-D(\rho||\sigma_{R})=S(\rho)-H_{\mathrm{min}}(\rho)=\Delta_{\mathrm{spec}}(\rho)

This identity precisely connects the language of algorithmic information theory with that of standard quantum information theory, with the difference being exactly equal to the spectral skew term.

4.

Combining and Rearranging:
By chaining together the inequalities and the identity from the three steps above, we obtain a complete logical chain from
C_{\mathrm{opt}}\cdot\log\Gamma_{R}
to
D(\rho||\sigma_{R})
. After algebraic rearrangement and separation of the leading and correction terms, the final form of Theorem 3.1 is obtained.

3.3
Smoothed Theorem and Operational Connection

Any computational process in physical reality is accompanied by a finite precision
\varepsilon
, and any statistical test operates under a finite confidence level
\eta
. To precisely capture this reality in theory and to establish a more direct connection with operational quantities, we introduce a “smoothed” version of the main theorem. 4
Operational Principles and Information Extraction for RCC

The vitality of any profound physical quantity lies not only in its theoretical self-consistency and elegance, but more so in its connection to observables. The preceding sections have established the theoretical framework of Reference-Contingent Complexity and have proven its central role as a lower bound on the optimal gate count. However, if RCC itself could not be probed by any physical means, this lower bound would lose its practical significance. This chapter aims to bridge this gap between theory and reality by systematically elaborating on several fundamental principles for extracting RCC information from a physical system, thereby transforming an abstract theoretical concept into a set of concrete physical measurement protocols with statistical confidence. This chapter will elaborate on these fundamental principles separately, while a practical protocol that integrates them into a unified operational workflow is provided in Appendix
B.3.2

On the other hand, this chapter will primarily discuss protocols in ideal finite-dimensional systems, whereas a rigorous framework for robust handling and conservative lower-bound schemes for the unavoidable energy or mode cutoffs when dealing with infinite-dimensional systems (such as quantum field theories) is detailed in Appendix

4.1
Principle A: Quantum Hypothesis Testing

From the fundamental perspective of information theory, the distinguishability between two quantum states is characterized by the theory of hypothesis testing. Theorem 3.2 has already revealed that the smoothed lower bound of RCC is directly related to the hypothesis testing relative entropy
D_{H}^{\eta}(\rho||\sigma_{R})
. This provides us with the first, and theoretically most fundamental, principle of information extraction. Its core task can be formulated as a binary quantum hypothesis testing problem: given a single-copy system, determine whether its state satisfies the null hypothesis
H_{0}:\sigma_{R}
or the alternative hypothesis
H_{1}:\rho
. Any given measurement (described by a POVM
\{T,I-T\}
) will yield a lower bound on
D_{H}^{\eta}(\rho||\sigma_{R})
, which alleviates the stringent requirement of finding the optimal measurement, making this principle feasible in practice. At the implementation level, let us assume
N
independent and repeated measurements are performed, from which the frequency of the type-II error (mistaking
\rho
for
\sigma_{R}
) is observed, and its probability
\beta
is thereby estimated. To ensure statistical rigor, we need to construct a confidence interval for
\beta
. Using standard statistical methods, such as the Hoeffding bound or the more precise Clopper-Pearson interval
[
28
,
29
]
, one can calculate the upper confidence endpoint of
\beta
, denoted
\beta_{U}(\delta)
, at a confidence level of
1-\delta
.
(Note: In the chosen binary test
\{T,I-T\}
, it is specified that observing
T
leads to the decision
H_{1}(\rho)
, while observing
I-T
leads to the decision
H_{0}(\sigma_{R})
. The type-I error is constrained as
\alpha=\mathrm{Tr}[T\cdot\sigma_{R}]\leq\eta
. With a finite sample size, the upper confidence endpoint of
\alpha
,
\alpha_{U}(\delta)
, can be obtained using the Clopper–Pearson (or Wilson) interval, and it should be ensured that
\alpha_{U}(\delta)\leq\eta
; under this constraint, the lower bound of
D_{H}^{\eta}(\rho||\sigma_{R})
is then assessed based on the upper confidence endpoint of
\beta
,
\beta_{U}(\delta)
.)
We then have a statistical lower bound for the leading term of RCC:

Proposition 4.1 (Hypothesis Testing Lower Bound):

D_{H}^{\eta}(\rho||\sigma_{R})\geq-\log\beta_{U}(\delta)

(4.1)

This inequality reveals a profound physical picture: the intrinsic complexity of a quantum state
\rho
is lower-bounded by our ability to confidently distinguish it from its “structured vacuum”
\sigma_{R}
in an experiment. A highly complex state must have extremely high distinguishability, corresponding to an extremely low type-II error rate, which in turn implies a large lower bound on its complexity. The complete experimental protocol, including the rigorous control of statistical errors, is detailed in Appendix
D.1
. Of particular importance is that the advantage of the present theoretical framework lies in the fact that this connection between “distinguishability” and “complexity” does not merely remain at the level of physical intuition. It is elevated by Theorem 3.2 to a mathematically provable relationship. It is this theorem, along with its complete derivation based on one-shot information theory in Appendix
A.6
, that builds the bridge from experimental data (the type-II error rate
\beta
) to the theoretical lower bound (
C_{\mathrm{opt}}
). This rigorous connection ensures that our proposed operational schemes are theoretically completely reliable and self-consistent, successfully transforming an abstract concept of complexity into a measurable physical reality.

4.2
Principle B: Projective Witnesses

The proof chain of the main theorem reveals a profound connection between RCC and the max-relative entropy,
D_{\max}
. We can probe this relationship by measuring a carefully chosen projection operator, thereby providing a rigorous lower bound for
D_{\max}

Lemma 4.1 (Projective Occupation Lower Bound):
Consider a projection operator
\Pi

H_{R}

D_{\max}(\rho||\sigma_{R})\geq\log(p\cdot d_{R}/r)

(4.2)

Proof.
If
\rho\leq 2^{\lambda}\cdot\sigma_{R}

\Pi
of rank
r

\mathrm{Tr}(\Pi\cdot\rho)\leq 2^{\lambda}\cdot\mathrm{Tr}(\Pi\cdot\sigma_{R})=2^{\lambda}\cdot r/d_{R}
. From
p=\mathrm{Tr}(\Pi\cdot\rho)
, one can solve for
\lambda\geq\log(p\cdot d_{R}/r)
. The lemma is thus proven. In experimental practice, we estimate the value of
p
through
N
measurements and calculate its lower confidence endpoint
p_{\mathrm{lower}}(\delta)
at a confidence level of
1-\delta
. Substituting this statistical lower bound into Eq. (
4.2

\rho

\Pi

much smaller than the dimension
d_{R}
of its containing structured subspace, then this serves as a proof that the information in this state is highly “concentrated” or “ordered.” This high concentration of information implies that the state
\rho
possesses a very large eigenvalue, and thus
H_{\mathrm{min}}(\rho)
is small and
D_{\max}
is large. Therefore, through a simple projective measurement, we have “witnessed” the non-triviality of the state, setting a rigorous lower bound on its complexity. The detailed implementation protocol for this principle, including the statistical estimation of occupation probabilities and confidence level corrections in scenarios with multiple witnesses, is formally articulated in Appendix

4.3
Principle C: Dephasing Information Bound

The third principle extracts information through a “classicization” process. To obtain a reliable lower bound, we employ the completely dephasing channel
\Delta_{R}
derived from the reference set
R
, which corresponds to performing a measurement in a basis of rank-1 projectors that simultaneously diagonalizes all projection operators
\{P_{i}\}
and then discarding the off-diagonal (coherent) information. Physically, this channel corresponds to the strongest decoherence process that leaves the structure
R
invariant.
[

According to the property of entropy increase in quantum information theory, the von Neumann entropy after the action of this channel is necessarily no less than before, i.e.,
S(\Delta_{R}(\rho))\geq S(\rho)
. The dephased state
\Delta_{R}(\rho)
is a classical probability distribution, and its Shannon entropy is denoted as
H=S(\Delta_{R}(\rho))
. Therefore, we have
H\geq S(\rho)
, which provides an operational and conservative lower bound for RCC:

Proposition 4.2 (Dephasing Lower Bound):

C_{R}(\rho)\geq\frac{(\log d_{R}-H)}{\log\Gamma_{R}}

(4.3)

Experimentally, this corresponds to performing measurements in a classical basis compatible with the reference set
R
, reconstructing the probability distribution, and estimating its Shannon entropy
H
. An upper confidence interval for

can be given through the DKW inequality or bootstrap methods,
[
30

thereby propagating the statistical uncertainty to the final lower bound of RCC. The detailed implementation protocol for this principle, particularly concerning how to rigorously estimate Shannon entropy from finite samples with bias correction, is presented with its complete statistical framework in Appendix
D.3

4.4
An Important Clarification: The Role of Purity

In complexity measures, the role played by purity,
P=\mathrm{Tr}(\rho^{2})
, is often misunderstood. A measurement of purity provides an upper bound on
S(\rho)
, not a lower bound, because the von Neumann entropy
S(\rho)
is always greater than or equal to the second-order Rényi entropy
S_{2}(\rho)=-\log P
. Therefore, substituting this relationship into the expression for RCC, Eq. (
2.3
), a purity measurement can only provide an upper bound on complexity:
C_{R}(\rho)\leq\frac{(\log d_{R}+\log P)}{\log\Gamma_{R}}
. It cannot be used to prove a lower bound on complexity; its more appropriate role is as a sanity check tool. A correct understanding is that “high purity” in itself does not guarantee a large RCC lower bound. A meaningful lower bound must originate from the distinguishability of the state
\rho
relative to the reference state
\sigma_{R}
. This distinguishability is quantified by the success rate of hypothesis testing, a high occupation probability on a low-rank projection, or a classical entropy that is far from the uniform distribution, rather than by purity itself. In contrast, the three operational principles proposed in this chapter can all provide rigorous lower bounds. Detailed protocols for the three principles mentioned above can be found in Appendix
D
, and their technical details and boundary conditions on issues such as multiple testing, statistical power control, and readout leakage propagation should be consulted in conjunction with Appendix
C
.

5
Properties and Application Interfaces of RCC

The preceding sections have established the definition of Reference-Contingent Complexity (RCC), its theorem as a lower bound for optimal gate count, and the operational principles for extracting its information. A well-behaved physical quantity should not only be operational but also adhere to a series of universal physical principles and be able to seamlessly interface with existing theoretical frameworks. This chapter aims to reveal that RCC indeed possesses such qualities. We will first systematically elaborate on the axiomatic properties that RCC satisfies as a well-behaved resource measure, and subsequently showcase its three key application interfaces: first, we will elucidate how the introduction of RCC deepens and unifies the performance frontier of quantum dynamics; second, how it provides a structurally fair calibration scheme for exploring fundamental questions such as the complexity-geometry duality; and finally, this paper will reveal its third application as a tool for quantifying thermodynamic order and constraining statistical process laws, demonstrating how this state function can provide rigorous lower bounds for the information processing work and evolution time in physical processes.

5.1
Axiomatic Properties as a Resource Measure

A measure for a physical resource should exhibit good and consistent behavior within the theoretical framework it defines. We will show that RCC satisfies a series of core axiomatic properties of a "well-defined" physical quantity, with most of these favorable properties being directly inherited from quantum relative entropy, which serves as its foundation.

6
Discussion

Having systematically constructed the framework of Reference-Contingent Complexity (RCC), proven its theoretical properties, and articulated its application interfaces in the preceding sections, we now place RCC within the broader landscape of theoretical physics for examination. This chapter aims to clarify the relationship between RCC and other relevant complexity measures in the field, frankly discuss the applicability boundaries and open questions of the current theoretical framework, and, based on this, provide an outlook on potential future research directions.

6.1
Comparison and Connection with Related Complexity Measures

RCC is not intended to replace existing complexity measures, but rather to provide a complementary tool with the advantage of structural fairness, which can serve as a universal benchmark. Its relationship with other major paradigms
is systematically compared in detail in Appendix
B.3
; here, we only briefly articulate the core logical relationships.

1.

Relationship with Nielsen’s Geometric Complexity:
Nielsen complexity
[
38
]
, from the perspective of differential geometry, defines complexity as the length of the geodesic connecting an initial state to a target state on the manifold of the unitary group, where penalty factors are used to suppress evolution in "difficult" directions. RCC forms a profound complementary relationship with it. Regardless of how the penalty factors are chosen, our main theorem, Theorem 3.1, can provide a rigorous lower-bound anchor point for any geometric path, derived from the fundamental principles of information theory. In other words, the length of any geometric path, after being converted into the number of atomic instruction steps, must be greater than or equal to the lower limit set by the RCC of the final state. This allows RCC to serve as a universal tool for the calibration and sanity-checking of geometric complexity.

2.

Relationship with Krylov Complexity:
Krylov complexity
[
39
]
measures complexity by analyzing the expansion of an operator in the Lanczos/Krylov basis generated by the Hamiltonian and the initial operator, and is closely related to quantum chaos and operator growth. The two focus on different physical aspects: Krylov complexity reflects the operator spreading and chaotic properties under continuous-time evolution, whereas RCC is rooted in the computational framework of discrete gate models and the system’s structural priors. An interesting research direction is to explore the quantitative relationship between RCC and Krylov complexity in the specific setting where the energy eigenbasis is taken as the reference set
R
. We conjecture that for certain chaotic systems, the two may exhibit a monotonic or order-preserving correspondence, which is worthy of systematic numerical and analytical verification in the future.

3.

Relationship with Resource Theories such as "Mana":
Resource theories represented by "mana"
[
40
]
aim to quantify non-stabilizer states, a key resource for achieving universal quantum computation. RCC and this type of specific resource theory form a complementary "cost-benefit" perspective: a high "mana" value is often associated with the ability to perform complex computational tasks, while our work shows that preparing such a high-"mana" state must itself incur a corresponding complexity cost in terms of RCC. RCC provides a unified, information-theory-based "manufacturing cost" pricing for all these specific quantum resources.

6.2
Limitations and Open Questions

The rigor of the RCC framework is built upon several key assumptions, which also define its applicability boundaries and the open questions that remain to be explored. It is worth emphasizing that, within a fixed subspace
H_{R}
, all pure states have the same leading term for RCC,
\log(d_{R})/\log(\Gamma_{R})
. As an information-theoretic lower bound, RCC aims to set a baseline for resource consumption, rather than to distinguish between quantum states that have the same spectral distribution but different geometric or entanglement structures—a common feature of all state functions based on von Neumann entropy. The most central theoretical assumption lies in the strict commutativity of the reference set
R
, which makes it naturally suited for systems defined by Abelian group symmetries or stabilizer subgroups. For systems defined by non-Abelian group symmetries, a possible path for generalization is as follows: technically, one can decompose the Hilbert space into a direct sum of irreducible representations (irreps) according to group representation theory, and use the normalized equally-mixed state of each irrep block (or its energy-truncated version) as the reference state; concurrently, define the CPTP maps that preserve this algebraic structure (such as the conditional expectation onto the center of the algebra) as free operations.
(Note: The complete formal construction of this generalization scheme, including the definition of its reference state and the preservation of core properties, has been detailed in Appendix
F.1
.)
Within this framework, the monotonicity of RCC is expected to be preserved based on the data processing inequality, but its proof as a lower bound on circuit complexity would need to be reconstructed. Another core open question is the near-tightness of RCC as a lower bound. In Eq. (
3.1
), we proved that RCC is a rigorous lower bound for
C_{\mathrm{opt}}
, but this does not answer whether
C_{\mathrm{opt}}
can also be bounded from above by RCC. If such an upper bound exists, it would imply that RCC is not just a theoretical baseline, but a benchmark with strong predictive power for the true complexity. We hereby formalize this as a testable core conjecture:

Conjecture 6.1 (Near-Tightness of RCC).
For a given hardware capability
\Gamma_{R}

and

satisfies the following upper bound:

C_{\mathrm{opt}}^{(\varepsilon)}(\rho)\leq a(\Gamma_{R})\cdot C_{R}(\rho)+b(\Gamma_{R})\cdot(\log d_{R}+\log(1/\varepsilon))

(6.1)

where the constants

and
b
are assumed to be independent of the system dimension
d_{R}
and the target state
\rho

A detailed discussion of this parameterization is provided in Appendix

.
. Validating or falsifying this conjecture would greatly deepen our understanding of the optimality of quantum circuit synthesis and could provide new information-theoretic guiding principles for the design of efficient quantum algorithms. Although proving this conjecture in a fully universal sense is challenging, we have found strong constructive support for its validity. Appendix

systematically proves that for three broad classes of "well-structured" state families prevalent in quantum information and physics, the upper bound on their optimal circuit complexity

(i)

(ii)

6.3
Resolving the Paradox of Pure State Complexity through an Observational Window

The core definition of the RCC framework,
C_{R}(\rho)=(\log d_{R}-S(\rho))/\log\Gamma_{R}
, while ensuring structural fairness, also introduces a profound paradox: from this global perspective, all pure states (
S(\rho)=0
)—whether they are simple product states or graph states with highly non-local entanglement—possess the same maximal leading term of complexity,
\log(d_{R})/\log(\Gamma_{R})
. This conclusion of "pure state degeneracy" severely contradicts both our physical intuition and practical computational experience, as it seems to completely erase the vastly different internal correlation structures of different quantum states, which are precisely what determine the true computational cost of preparing them. The resolution to this paradox is rooted in a more profound physical principle: complexity is not an intrinsic, absolute property of a quantum state, but rather a relational property that emerges from the interaction between an observer and the system. The structural complexity of a state is "latent" within its internal global correlations
[
41
]
;to quantify it, one must actively "reveal" it through a physical probe. This "physical probe" is precisely the "observational window"
E_{\Xi}
introduced for thermodynamic analysis in Sec.
5.4
. This mathematical tool formalizes a physically realistic observer with finite capabilities. Such an observer cannot perceive the entire global pure state of the system at once, but can only interact with the system through a specific perspective limited by their capabilities (e.g., being able to perform only local measurements). The core insight is that when such a constrained observer examines a pure state with a complex internal structure, the state will appear as a mixed state within their "window" due to the inability to resolve its delicate global correlations, thus giving rise to an "emergent" non-zero entropy
S(E_{\Xi}(\rho))>0
. Crucially, the magnitude of this emergent entropy is a direct "fingerprint" of how the internal correlation structure of the pure state responds to that specific mode of observation. A window that is highly "aligned" with the state’s structure will reveal its latent complexity to the greatest extent; whereas a simple product state will remain simple under any local window, and its emergent entropy will be minimal. Therefore, by shifting the theoretical framework from the global
C_{R}(\rho)
to the observation-window-dependent
C_{R,\Xi}(\rho)
, we not only solve the technical problem of pure state degeneracy but, more importantly, we elevate RCC into a more complete, dynamic theory. This theory acknowledges the central role of observation in defining physical reality
[
42
]
and provides a conceptually profound and practically feasible pathway for quantifying the structural complexity of different pure states. To this end, we further rigorize the concept of the observational window
E_{\Xi}
from
Sec. 5.4
, defining it as a conditional expectation acting on
H_{R}
:
E_{\Xi}
is a unital, completely positive and trace-preserving (CPTP), and idempotent quantum channel, corresponding to some von Neumann subalgebra
A_{\Xi}\subseteq\mathfrak{B}(H_{R})
, and satisfying the condition of compatibility with the reference vacuum,
E_{\Xi}(\sigma_{R})=\sigma_{R}
. This compatibility is an extension of the principle of structural fairness, ensuring that the observation process does not distort the zero point of complexity. Using the monotonicity of relative entropy,
D(\rho||\sigma_{R})\geq D(E_{\Xi}(\rho)||E_{\Xi}(\sigma_{R}))
, and the fact that
E_{\Xi}(\sigma_{R})=\sigma_{R}
, one can obtain
S(E_{\Xi}(\rho))\geq S(\rho)
, and thus
C_{R,\Xi}(\rho)\leq C_{R}(\rho)
. More importantly, this algebraic structure endows complexity with a hierarchical monotonicity: if the observational capability is enhanced, i.e.,
A_{\Xi_{1}}\subseteq A_{\Xi_{2}}
, then the idempotency of the conditional expectation ensures
E_{\Xi_{1}}\circ E_{\Xi_{2}}=E_{\Xi_{1}}
, which in turn implies
S(E_{\Xi_{1}}(\rho))=S(E_{\Xi_{1}}(E_{\Xi_{2}}(\rho)))\geq S(E_{\Xi_{2}}(\rho))
, thereby yielding a well-behaved complexity hierarchy
C_{R,\Xi_{1}}(\rho)\leq C_{R,\Xi_{2}}(\rho)\leq C_{R}(\rho)
. To connect this theoretical framework with physical practice, we provide an operational lower bound, which forms the basis for experimentally probing the structural complexity of pure states.

Proposition 6.2 (Windowed Pinching Lower Bound).
Let
\{P_{i}\}\subset A_{\Xi}
be a set of orthogonal projection operators forming a complete set on
H_{R}
(
\sum_{i}P_{i}=I_{H_{R}}

C_{R,\Xi}(\rho)\geq\frac{\ln d_{R}-H(\{p_{\Xi,i}\})-\sum_{i}p_{\Xi,i}\ln r_{i}}{\ln\Gamma_{R}}

In particular, when

C_{R,\Xi}(\rho)\geq\frac{\ln d_{R}-H(\{p_{\Xi,i}\})}{\ln\Gamma_{R}}

(6.3.2)

is
\ln(d_{R})/\ln(\Gamma_{R})

values. The significance of Eq. (

\{p_{\Xi,i}\}

This forms a key bridge connecting abstract theory with laboratory reality and gives rise to a powerful scheme for actively probing the latent complexity within pure states, which we term the "Experimental Squeeze of the Complexity Spectrum." Its core logic is that the choice of the measurement basis
\{P_{i}\}
reflects the experimenter’s "informed probing" of the system’s intrinsic structural complexity. For a highly structured pure state, such as a graph state defined by a stabilizer group
S
, if we "intelligently" choose a measurement basis that is "aligned" with its stabilizer generators, then according to stabilizer theory, the measurement outcomes will be highly deterministic (
\pm 1
). This will cause the probability distribution
\{p_{\Xi,i}\}
to become extremely sparse, with its Shannon entropy

approaching zero. According to Eq. (
6.3.2

C_{R,\Xi}(\rho)
to be "squeezed" upwards, approaching its theoretical upper limit
\ln(d_{R})/\ln(\Gamma_{R})
. Conversely, a simple product state, lacking intrinsic non-local correlations, will inevitably appear more random when probed with the same complex measurement basis, resulting in a larger
H
and a lower bound. This sharp contrast reveals a profound principle: the structural complexity of a state can only be most fully revealed when it is probed by an observer who "understands" its structural complexity. The significance of this "experimental squeeze" scheme is twofold. First, it constructs a feasible verification path for testing the core open question of this paper, Conjecture 6.1 (the near-tightness of RCC). By finding the optimal measurement bases that maximize the complexity lower bound for different classes of pure states (such as graph states, tensor network states, etc.), we can systematically establish "certificates of tightness" for their complexity. Second, it elevates the RCC framework from a static spectral measure to a complete, observation-dependent dynamical theoretical framework, in which the operational process of "finding the optimal measurement basis" is itself equivalent to the core task of "decoding the structural complexity of the quantum state."

6.4
Outlook for Future Work

The establishment of the RCC theoretical framework opens up avenues for future exploration in several directions. Firstly, the generalization from state complexity to the complexity of unitary operators/channels is a natural direction. By applying the idea of reference sets to Liouville space, we might be able to define an RCC for a unitary operator or a quantum channel, to quantify the intrinsic complexity of realizing a specific quantum operation compared to "free" operations (such as operations that preserve a specific subspace). Secondly, systematically connecting RCC with open quantum systems and quantum thermodynamics is another promising direction. For instance, by modeling processes that preserve energy eigen-populations or pinching processes of specific configurations as R-preserving channels, one can use the monotonicity of RCC, Eq. (
5.1
), to provide a non-trivial upper bound on the rate of information dissipation. Comparing this bound quantitatively with the change in thermodynamic quantities such as entropy production rate or non-equilibrium free energy is expected to lead to a series of testable inequalities connecting information-theoretic costs with thermodynamic prices. Finally, deepening the application of RCC in holographic duality will be an exciting long-term goal. As described in Sec.
5.3
, RCC can serve as a structured calibration benchmark to test the order-preserving capability and proportional stability of different geometric candidate quantities (such as the CV/CA conjectures) across multiple families of states. If inconsistencies are found, they may suggest the existence of structural biases in the duality proposal and provide clues for finding directions for correction.
(We provide a rigorous framework in Appendix
F.2
for calculating a controllable, conservative lower bound on complexity in the presence of energy or mode cutoffs.)

7
Conclusion

This paper has systematically proposed and constructed the theoretical framework of “Reference-Contingent Complexity” (
RCC
), aiming to provide a self-consistent and operational solution for addressing the long-standing “structural fairness problem” in quantum information science. Starting from first principles, we formalize the intrinsic structure of a system—whether it originates from symmetries, conservation laws, or deliberate encoding—as an a priori “reference set,” and, using this as a benchmark, we define complexity as the information gap of a quantum state relative to this structured background, a gap which can be measured by relative entropy. This construction fundamentally ensures that our measure only prices the non-trivial quantum information that must be generated at the cost of computational resources.
The core theoretical contribution of this paper is the proof of a main theorem (Theorem 3.1), which establishes that
RCC
is a rigorous, theorem-level lower bound on the optimal number of atomic instruction steps for any universal quantum circuit. This theorem is not only mathematically rigorous, but its physical meaning also reveals a profound connection between the information-theoretic properties of a state (the information gap
D(\rho||\sigma_{R})
and the spectral skew
\Delta_{\mathrm{spec}}
) and the computational cost of the dynamical process. To ensure the physical relevance of this theory, we have further proposed a set of operational principles based on quantum hypothesis testing, projective witnesses, and dephasing entropy bounds (detailed in Chapter
4
), demonstrating that the lower bound from
RCC
can be estimated through experiments and numerical simulations, thereby connecting an abstract theoretical concept with concretely measurable physical observables.
As a theoretical tool, the value of
RCC
is manifested in its broad application interfaces. We have shown the well-behaved mathematical properties it possesses as a proper resource measure, such as monotonicity under free operations (Sec.
5.1
). We have elucidated how it complements and deepens the theoretical framework for the performance frontier of quantum dynamics, elevating a process-dependent complexity measure to a state function determined solely by the final state, as shown in (Sec.
5.2
). Furthermore, we propose that the structural fairness of
RCC
makes it a promising candidate to play a key role as a calibration benchmark in the exploration of fundamental physical questions such as the “complexity-geometry” holographic duality (Sec.
5.3
).
In summary, Reference-Contingent Complexity provides a foundational and structurally fair theoretical tool for understanding and quantifying the physical costs of quantum computation. It offers a unified “meter stick” for us to compare and diagnose increasingly complex quantum algorithms and hardware platforms, and opens up a solid and operational new path for exploring the deep unification of information, dynamics, and spacetime geometry. We are well aware that the establishment of any theoretical framework is merely the beginning of an exploration, not its end. Many theoretical properties of
RCC
, such as the tightness of its lower bound (Conjecture 6.1), its generalization to systems with non-Abelian symmetries, and its quantitative connection to quantum chaos and thermodynamics, remain open questions full of opportunities. We hope that this work will inspire more theoretical and experimental explorations within the field, jointly advancing towards a deeper understanding of the fundamental concept of “complexity” in the quantum world.

holds. According to the definition of the smoothed max-relative entropy, this directly leads to:
D_{\max}^{\varepsilon}(\rho||M)\leq L=K_{\varepsilon}(\rho|R)
.

2.

\sigma
-padding and comparison of constant factors.
Take any
\alpha\in(0,1)
and define a new “padded” semi-density operator
M_{\alpha}=(1-\alpha)\cdot M+\alpha\cdot\sigma_{R}
. By construction, we have the evident operator orderings
\sigma_{R}\leq\alpha^{-1}\cdot M_{\alpha}
and
M\leq(1-\alpha)^{-1}\cdot M_{\alpha}
. Using the scaling comparison property of
D_{\max}
: if
X\leq kY
, then
D_{\max}(.||Y)\leq D_{\max}(.||X)+\log k
. These relationships lead to:
D_{\max}(\omega||\sigma_{R})\leq D_{\max}(\omega||M_{\alpha})+\log(1/\alpha)
and
D_{\max}(\omega||M_{\alpha})\leq D_{\max}(\omega||M)+\log(1/(1-\alpha))
. The inequalities remain valid after taking the infimum for
\varepsilon
-smoothing. Combining them yields
D_{\max}^{\varepsilon}(\rho||\sigma_{R})\leq D_{\max}^{\varepsilon}(\rho||M)+\log(1/(\alpha(1-\alpha)))
. Since
M_{\alpha}\geq\alpha\cdot\sigma_{R}
, the support
\mathrm{supp}(M_{\alpha})
contains
\mathrm{supp}(\sigma_{R})=H_{R}
, and thus all
D_{\max}(.||M_{\alpha})
are finite and well-defined (without needing to normalize
M_{\alpha}
).

3.

Choosing the constant and concluding.
Combining the two steps above, we obtain
D_{\max}^{\varepsilon}(\rho||\sigma_{R})\leq K_{\varepsilon}(\rho|R)+\log(1/(\alpha(1-\alpha)))
. To minimize the constant term, we choose
\alpha=1/2
, for which
\log(1/(\alpha(1-\alpha)))=\log(4)=2
(with logarithm base 2). Hence:
K_{\varepsilon}(\rho|R)\geq D_{\max}^{\varepsilon}(\rho||\sigma_{R})-2

By incorporating a fixed
O(1)
constant (denoted
c_{U}
) arising from the choice of the universal machine
U_{R}
itself into the right-hand side, we can set
c_{0}=c_{U}+2
. This constant is clearly independent of
d_{R}
. Q.E.D.

A.4
Entropy Identity

Proposition A.4 (Entropy Identity).
Under the premise that
\mathrm{supp}(\rho)\subseteq H_{R}
and
\sigma_{R}=\Pi_{R}/d_{R}
,

D_{\max}(\rho||\sigma_{R})-D(\rho||\sigma_{R})=S(\rho)-H_{\mathrm{min}}(\rho)=\Delta_{\mathrm{spec}}(\rho)

(A.4)

Proof.
On the subspace
H_{R}
, the operator
\log(\sigma_{R})
is equivalent to
-\log(d_{R})\cdot\Pi_{R}
. Since
\mathrm{supp}(\rho)\subseteq H_{R}
, we have
\mathrm{Tr}(\Pi_{R}\cdot\rho)=1
, and thus
\log(\sigma_{R})
acts well on
H_{R}
. Therefore, the standard relative entropy is
D(\rho||\sigma_{R})=\mathrm{Tr}[\rho(\log(\rho)-\log(\sigma_{R}))]=\log(d_{R})-S(\rho)
. Furthermore, from the definition of max-relative entropy, the inequality
\rho\leq 2^{\lambda}\cdot\sigma_{R}
is equivalent to
||\rho||_{\mathrm{\infty}}\leq 2^{\lambda}/d_{R}
, which implies
D_{\max}(\rho||\sigma_{R})=\log(d_{R})-H_{\mathrm{min}}(\rho)
. Subtracting the two expressions yields the conclusion. Q.E.D.

A.5
Combining and Rearranging the Inequality Chain

We now chain together all the preceding results to complete the proof of Theorem 3.1. The core of the argument is to combine the information-theoretic upper bound on circuit cost (Lemma A.2) with the lower bounds on information-theoretic measures (Lemma A.3 and subsequent relations). First, we introduce a standard smoothing continuity bound. According to one-shot information theory, there exists a function

\alpha(\varepsilon)=O(\log(1/\varepsilon))
that depends only on
\varepsilon
, such that:

D_{\max}^{\varepsilon}(\rho||\sigma_{R})\geq D_{\max}(\rho||\sigma_{R})-\alpha(\varepsilon)

(A.5)

Bounds of this type can be derived from the standard relationships between smoothed measures and the purified distance
[
43
]
. Second, by combining Lemma A.2 (Eq. (
A.2
)) and Lemma A.3 (Eq. (
A.3
)), we obtain the core inequality from physical cost to a smoothed information quantity. To ensure mathematical rigor, we uniformly use the form

\log(\max\{1,C_{\mathrm{opt}}^{(\varepsilon)}(\rho)\}):C_{\mathrm{opt}}^{(\varepsilon)}(\rho)\cdot\log\Gamma_{R}+c_{a}\log(\max\{1,C_{\mathrm{opt}}^{(\varepsilon)}(\rho)\})+c_{b}\log(1/\varepsilon)+O(1)\geq D_{\max}^{\varepsilon}(\rho||\sigma_{R})-c_{0}

(A.6)

Next, applying the aforementioned smoothing continuity bound (in the lower-bound direction) to the right-hand side and substituting the entropy identity proven in Proposition A.4 (Eq. (
A.4
)), we get:

C_{\mathrm{opt}}^{(\varepsilon)}(\rho)\cdot\log\Gamma_{R}\geq D(\rho||\sigma_{R})+\Delta_{\mathrm{spec}}(\rho)-\alpha(\varepsilon)-c_{0}-c_{a}\log(\max\{1,C_{\mathrm{opt}}^{(\varepsilon)}(\rho)\})-c_{b}\log(1/\varepsilon)-O(1)

(A.7)

Finally, we divide both sides by
\log\Gamma_{R}
and rearrange terms to obtain the final form. To consolidate all correction terms, we bound the universal constants: for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2]
, there exists a universal constant
\kappa=O(1)
such that
\alpha(\varepsilon)\leq\kappa\cdot\log(1/\varepsilon)
. Based on this, we can choose a sufficiently large
O(1)
constant
c_{1}
such that it satisfies
c_{1}\geq\max\{c_{a},c_{b}+\kappa\}
, and also absorb the remaining
O(1)
terms (such as
c_{0}
) into it. This yields the final form of the main theorem:

C_{\mathrm{opt}}^{(\varepsilon)}(\rho)\geq\frac{D(\rho||\sigma_{R})}{\log\Gamma_{R}}-\frac{c_{1}}{\log\Gamma_{R}}\left[\log(\max\{1,C_{\mathrm{opt}}^{(\varepsilon)}(\rho)\})+\log(1/\varepsilon)\right]+\frac{\Delta_{\mathrm{spec}}(\rho)}{\log\Gamma_{R}}

(A.8)

This expression is identical in form to Theorem 3.1 in the main text, where the coefficient of
\Delta_{\mathrm{spec}}(\rho)
is exactly
1
, and all constants are independent of
d_{R}
. When an explicit non-recursive lower bound is needed, the solution method derived in Appendix
C.2
can be applied to this bootstrap inequality.

A.6
Proof of the Smoothed Theorem and Tools from One-Shot Information Theory

This section aims to provide a self-contained and rigorous proof for Theorem 3.2 presented in the main text. This theorem connects the lower bound on circuit complexity with the hypothesis testing relative entropy
D_{H}^{\eta}
, which has a direct operational meaning. Our proof will be rooted in the modern framework of one-shot quantum information theory. This framework is specifically designed to handle information tasks in single, non-asymptotic systems. Its core tools are “smoothed” entropy quantities, which make the theoretical conclusions robust against the small perturbations that are unavoidable in physical systems.

A.6.1
Key One-Shot Information-Theoretic Quantities

We first precisely define the core information-theoretic quantities on which the proof in this section relies. By convention: all logarithms
\log
are base-2, errors are measured by the trace distance
(1/2)\cdot||.||_{1}
, and it is assumed by default that the support of all quantum states
\rho
is contained within the subspace
H_{R}
defined by the reference set
R
[
26
]
.

Definition A.6.1 (One-Shot Entropy Quantities).

1.

Hypothesis testing relative entropy:

D_{H}^{\eta}(\rho||\sigma)=-\log\min_{0\leq T\leq I,\mathrm{Tr}[T\sigma]\leq\eta}\mathrm{Tr}[(I-T)\rho]

(A.9)

This quantity physically quantifies the minimum exponential decay rate of the type-II error probability when distinguishing between
\rho
and
\sigma
with an optimal strategy, under the constraint that the type-I error probability does not exceed
\eta
.

2.

Max-relative entropy and its smoothed version:

D_{\max}(\rho||\sigma)=\inf\{\lambda:\rho\leq 2^{\lambda}\cdot\sigma\}

(A.10)

D_{\max}^{\varepsilon}(\rho||\sigma)=\min_{\rho^{\prime}:(1/2)||\rho^{\prime}-\rho||_{1}\leq\varepsilon}D_{\max}(\rho^{\prime}||\sigma)

(A.11)

A.6.2
Core Lemmas and Relations

Our proof relies on several standard results from one-shot information theory, which form the bridges between different entropy quantities.

Lemma A.6.1 (Data Processing Inequality).
For any CPTP map
E
, the following entropy quantities are monotonically non-increasing:

D_{H}^{\eta}(E(\rho)||E(\sigma))\leq D_{H}^{\eta}(\rho||\sigma)

(A.12)

D_{\max}^{\varepsilon}(E(\rho)||E(\sigma))\leq D_{\max}^{\varepsilon}(\rho||\sigma)

(A.13)

Lemma A.6.2 (One-Shot Entropy Bridging Inequality).
There exist system-independent universal constants
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}=O(1)
such that:

D_{H}^{\eta}(\rho||\sigma)\leq D_{\max}^{\varepsilon}(\rho||\sigma)+\kappa_{1}\cdot\log(1/\eta)+\kappa_{2}\cdot\log(1/\varepsilon)

(A.14)

Physical Interpretation.
This inequality is the core bridge connecting the operational quantity
D_{H}^{\eta}
with the more analytically tractable
D_{\max}^{\varepsilon}
. Its proof is rooted in the quantum Neyman-Pearson lemma and its smoothing techniques.

A.6.3
Upper Bound on Smoothed Max-Relative Entropy from Circuit Complexity

The first step of the proof is to establish a connection between the optimal number of circuit steps
C_{\mathrm{opt}}^{(\varepsilon)}(\rho)
and
D_{\max}^{\varepsilon}(\rho||\sigma_{R})
, which is in the same vein as the logic in
Section A.2
but employs a more self-contained proof technique.

Lemma A.6.3 (Description Length Upper Bound).
Let
L=C_{\mathrm{opt}}^{(\varepsilon)}(\rho)
. There exist universal constants
a,b=O(1)
that are independent of
d_{R}
, such that:

D_{\max}^{\varepsilon}(\rho||\sigma_{R})\leq L\cdot\log\Gamma_{R}+a\cdot\log(\max\{1,L\})+b\cdot\log(1/\varepsilon)

(A.15)

Proof.
Following the prefix-free encoding idea from
Section A.2
, we construct the universal semi-density operator
M=\sum_{p}2^{-|p|}\rho_{p}
. If there exists a circuit described by a program
p^{*}
that can
\varepsilon
-approximately prepare
\rho
(with output
\rho_{p^{*}}
), then by the definition of

, we have the operator inequality
M\geq 2^{-|p^{*}|}\cdot\rho_{p^{*}}
, which directly leads to
D_{\max}(\rho_{p^{*}}||M)\leq|p^{*}|
. To change the comparison object from
M
to
\sigma_{R}
, we introduce a mixed semi-density operator
M_{\alpha}=(1-\alpha)M+\alpha\sigma_{R}
, where
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Using the scaling comparison property of max-relative entropy, we get
D_{\max}(\rho_{p^{*}}||\sigma_{R})\leq D_{\max}(\rho_{p^{*}}||M)+\log(1/(\alpha(1-\alpha)))
. Choosing
\alpha=1/2
makes the constant term
\log(1/(\alpha(1-\alpha)))=\log(4)=2
. Combining this with the information-theoretic upper bound on
|p^{*}|
, which is
L\cdot\log\Gamma_{R}+O(\log L)

A.15
). This proof path, via the
\sigma
-padding technique, avoids any spectral constants with explicit dependence on
d_{R}
.

A.6.4
Proof of Theorem 3.2

We now have all the elements necessary to prove the smoothed theorem.

Theorem 3.2 (Restated).
Under the same conditions as in Theorem 3.1, there exist system-independent universal constants

C_{\mathrm{opt}}^{(\varepsilon)}(\rho)\geq\frac{1}{\log\Gamma_{R}}D_{H}^{\eta}(\rho||\sigma_{R})-\frac{c^{\prime}_{1}}{\log\Gamma_{R}}\left[\log(\max\{1,C_{\mathrm{opt}}^{(\varepsilon)}(\rho)\})+\log(1/\varepsilon)\right]-\frac{c^{\prime}_{2}}{\log\Gamma_{R}}\log(1/\eta)

Proof.
Let

. We chain together

and

. From Eq. (

D_{H}^{\eta}(\rho||\sigma_{R})\leq\left(L\cdot\log\Gamma_{R}+a\cdot\log(\max\{1,L\})+b\cdot\log(1/\varepsilon)\right)+\kappa_{1}\cdot\log(1/\eta)+\kappa_{2}\cdot\log(1/\varepsilon)

By algebraically rearranging the above expression to solve for a lower bound on

c^{\prime}_{1}=\max\{a,b+\kappa_{2}\}

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle B

This appendix aims to provide a series of key technical clarifications and supplementary materials for both theoretical and experimental researchers. Section

B.1
Robustness of the Theory to Support Set Leakage

The theoretical construction in the main text, particularly the main theorem, Theorem 3.1, under ideal conditions assumes that the support of the target state
\rho
lies strictly within the subspace
H_{R}
defined by the reference set
R
, i.e.,
\mathrm{supp}(\rho)\subseteq H_{R}
. However, in any physical experiment or numerical simulation, due to finite control precision or decoherence effects, the actually prepared state
\rho_{\mathrm{phys}}
will always unavoidably have a small component that leaks into the orthogonal complement space
H_{R}^{\perp}
. This leakage results in
\mathrm{Tr}(\Pi_{R}\cdot\rho_{\mathrm{phys}})=1-p_{\mathrm{leak}}<1
. If such a
\rho_{\mathrm{phys}}
were to be directly substituted into the definition of relative entropy
D(\rho_{\mathrm{phys}}||\sigma_{R})
, because the support of
\sigma_{R}
on
H_{R}^{\perp}
is null (its operator is the zero operator on this complement space), the relative entropy would strictly diverge. To ensure the applicability of this theoretical framework in realistic scenarios, we provide the following two standard schemes for handling this issue.

Scheme One: State Projection and Renormalization

The most direct way to handle this is to first project the physical state back into the target subspace and then proceed with the analysis.

Proposition B.1 (Construction of the Effective State):
Given a physical state
\rho_{\mathrm{phys}}
, we can define its effective state
\tilde{\rho}
within the structured subspace
H_{R}
as:

\tilde{\rho}=\frac{\Pi_{R}\cdot\rho_{\mathrm{phys}}\cdot\Pi_{R}}{\mathrm{Tr}(\Pi_{R}\cdot\rho_{\mathrm{phys}})}

(B.1)

All conclusions in the main theorem, Theorem 3.1, can be directly applied to this effective state
\tilde{\rho}
. The physical meaning of this scheme is that we focus our analysis on the part of the quantum state that was successfully prepared within the target structure, treating it as the true computational goal.

(Note: The above construction requires
q=\mathrm{Tr}(\Pi_{R}\rho_{\mathrm{phys}})>0
. When
q=0
(complete leakage), the smoothed reference state scheme (
B.2
) should be used instead.)

Scheme Two: Smoothing of the Reference State

Another, more general method in information theory is to construct a slightly perturbed “smoothed” reference state that has support over the entire space.

Proposition B.2 (Construction of the Smoothed Reference State):
For any small
\delta>0
, we can define a
\delta
-smoothed reference state
\tilde{\sigma}_{R}
as:

\tilde{\sigma}_{R}=(1-\delta)\cdot\sigma_{R}+\delta\cdot\frac{(I-\Pi_{R})}{(d-d_{R})}

(B.2)

where
I
is the identity operator on the total Hilbert space
H
. The relative entropy
D(\rho_{\mathrm{phys}}||\tilde{\sigma}_{R})
is now finite, and it can be shown that it provides a controllable approximation for
D(\tilde{\rho}||\sigma_{R})
. Their difference can be bounded by logarithmic terms related to the leakage probability
p_{\mathrm{leak}}
and the smoothing parameter
\delta
. In practical applications, as long as the leakage probability
p_{\mathrm{leak}}
is much smaller than the target precision
\varepsilon
, the corrections to the leading term of the complexity lower bound resulting from either scheme are secondary. Therefore, this theoretical framework is robust against the small state leakage that is physically unavoidable.

(Note: Here
I
is the identity operator on
H
, and
d=\dim H
. If
d_{R}=d
, then
\tilde{\sigma}_{R}=\sigma_{R}
, and no smoothing is necessary in this case.)

To apply RCC in experiments or numerical simulations, we propose a lower-bound estimation protocol consisting of three independent paths, complete with statistical confidence.

1.

Determine the Reference Set:

Execute a Measurement Path:

(a)

(b)

(c)

C.1
A Physics Commentary on the Definition of
C_{\mathrm{opt}}

To ensure the rigor of our theoretical derivations, in Appendix
A
, we directly define the optimal circuit complexity
C_{\mathrm{opt}}
as the minimum required number of serial atomic instruction steps. This definition is clear and unambiguous within the framework of algorithmic information theory. Here, we aim to provide a supplementary explanation from a physics perspective for this formal definition, to clarify its connection with the parallel quantum circuit models widely used in physical reality. In concrete hardware implementations, quantum circuits are typically executed in a parallel fashion. The computational resources of a parallel circuit are usually characterized by two dimensions: the depth of the circuit,
D
(i.e., the number of parallel time layers), and the degree of parallelism of each layer (i.e., the number of non-conflicting operations that can be performed simultaneously in that layer). The total computational workload, or total cost, of a circuit is physically proportional to the sum of all atomic operations performed across all parallel layers. Our definition of
C_{\mathrm{opt}}
is, conceptually, a direct quantification of this total physical cost. It is equivalent to the total length of an optimal parallel circuit after it has been "flattened" into an equivalent serial instruction sequence. This method of definition not only maintains a close connection to physical reality but also maximizes the simplicity and universality of the theoretical derivations.

C.2
Closed-Form Solution and Usable Approximations for the Bootstrap Inequality

The main theorem, Eq. (
3.1
), leads to a bootstrap-type lower bound
x\geq D-c\log(x)
(where
x>0
). This section provides its rigorous closed-form minimum solution, its monotonicity and uniqueness, and offers two types of implementable approximations for direct application in numerical or experimental reporting.

Lemma C.1 (Monotonicity and Unique Root).
Define the function
F(x)=x+c\log(x)-D
. For all
x>0

F^{\prime}(x)=1+c/(x\cdot\ln 2)>0
. Therefore,
F(x)
is strictly monotonically increasing on
(0,\infty)

has a unique solution on
(0,\infty)

is the minimum possible value that satisfies the original inequality.

Theorem C.2 (Tight Closed-Form Solution via Lambert-W).
Let

of the bootstrap inequality is given exactly by the principal branch
W_{0}
of the Lambert-W function:

x_{*}=\alpha\cdot W_{0}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\cdot\exp\left(\frac{D}{\alpha}\right)\right)

(C.1)

Proof.
The equation
x+c\log(x)=D
is equivalent to
x+\alpha\ln(x)=D
. Rearranging gives
(x/\alpha)\cdot\exp(x/\alpha)=(1/\alpha)\cdot\exp(D/\alpha)

u\exp(u)=z\iff u=W(z)

and

x/\alpha=W_{0}(z)
, where the principal branch
W_{0}
ensures the unique positive real solution. Q.E.D.

Proposition C.3 (Practical Approximation I: Leading Term for Large
D

When
D/\alpha
is sufficiently large, using the asymptotic expansion of

, which is
\ln(z)-\ln(\ln(z))+o(1)

x_{*}=D-c\log(D)+o(1)\quad\text{as }D\to\infty

(C.2)

Proposition C.4 (Practical Approximation II: Piecewise Conservative Lower Bound).
For any

\alpha>0

that can be computed without requiring a special function library:

x_{*}\geq\begin{cases}\max\{0,D/(1+\alpha)\}&\text{for }0<D\leq 1+\alpha\\
D-\alpha\ln(1+D)&\text{for }D>1+\alpha\end{cases}

(C.3)

Implementation Recommendation:
When performing numerical evaluations, the Lambert-W closed-form solution from Eq. (
C.1

C.2

C.3

D.1

The core idea of this protocol is to quantify the difficulty of distinguishing the target state

D_{H}^{\eta}(\rho||\sigma_{R})=-\log\left(\min_{0\leq T\leq I,\mathrm{Tr}(T\sigma_{R})\leq\eta}\mathrm{Tr}((I-T)\rho)\right)

Proposition D.1 (Confidence Lower Bound from Hypothesis Testing):
By estimating
\beta
through
N

\beta_{U}(\delta)

D_{H}^{\eta}(\rho||\sigma_{R})\geq-\log(\beta_{U}(\delta))

(D.2)

In the "zero-failure" case, a sufficient condition for the minimum number of samples

is:

N\geq 2^{L}\cdot\ln(1/\delta)

(D.3)

If
m
tests or thresholds are scanned in the experiment, a multiple testing correction should be applied (e.g., Bonferroni:
\delta\mapsto\delta/m
, or Benjamini-Hochberg to control the FDR), and this should be noted in the report.

D.2
Projective Witness Protocol

The physical basis of this protocol is to "witness" the degree of information concentration in a state by measuring the occupation probability of a specific projection.

Lemma D.1 (Projective Witness Lower Bound):
Let
\Pi
be a projection of rank
r

\mathrm{supp}(\Pi)\subseteq H_{R}
, and let
p=\mathrm{Tr}(\Pi\rho)
be its occupation probability. Then the max-relative entropy

satisfies:

D_{\max}(\rho||\sigma_{R})\geq\max\{0,\log(p\cdot d_{R}/r)\}

(D.4)

The maximum value of

Proposition D.2 (Confidence Lower Bound from Projective Witnessing):
By performing

D_{\max}(\rho||\sigma_{R})\geq\max\{0,\log(p_{L}(\delta)\cdot d_{R}/r)\}

N_{\min}\geq\frac{1}{2(p_{0}-p_{\mathrm{star}})^{2}}\cdot\ln(1/\delta)

(D.6)

If multiple sets of projections are scanned, a corresponding multiple testing correction must be performed.

D.3
Dephasing Entropy Bound Protocol

This protocol sets a conservative but robust bound on the complexity of a quantum state by measuring a "classicized" version of it. Consider the completely dephasing (pinching) channel
\Delta_{R}
in a classical basis compatible with the reference set
R

\Delta_{R}(\sigma_{R})=\sigma_{R}
. From the data processing inequality, we have:

D(\rho||\sigma_{R})\geq D(\Delta_{R}(\rho)||\sigma_{R})=\log(d_{R})-H(P)

(D.7)

where
P
is the classical probability distribution corresponding to the dephased state, and

is its Shannon entropy.

Proposition D.3 (Confidence Upper Bound for Entropy):
Let there be
M

calculated from the empirical distribution

obtained from
N

and

satisfies
||P_{\mathrm{hat}}-P||_{1}\leq\varepsilon_{N}(\delta)

\varepsilon_{N}(\delta)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{N}\cdot(M\ln 2+\ln(1/\delta))}

for

H_{U}(\delta)=\min\{\log M,H(P_{\mathrm{hat}})+v\log(M-1)+h_{2}(v)\}

D(\rho||\sigma_{R})\geq\log(d_{R})-H_{U}(\delta)

(D.10)

. The total projector
\Pi_{R}
of the non-Abelian reference set and its reference state
\sigma_{R}
are defined as:

\Pi_{R}=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in\Lambda}(I_{V_{\lambda}}\otimes Q_{\lambda})\\
d_{R}=\mathrm{Tr}(\Pi_{R})=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}d_{\lambda}\cdot r_{\lambda}\\
\sigma_{R}=\Pi_{R}/d_{R}

(F.1)

Lemma F.1 (Relative Entropy in the Non-Abelian Setting):
If
\mathrm{supp}(\rho)\subseteq\mathrm{supp}(\Pi_{R})
, then the expressions for
D(\rho||\sigma_{R})
and
D_{\max}(\rho||\sigma_{R})
are identical to the Abelian case:

D(\rho||\sigma_{R})=\log(d_{R})-S(\rho)

(F.2)

D_{\max}(\rho||\sigma_{R})=\log(d_{R})-H_{\min}(\rho)

(F.3)

Corollary F.1 (Entropy Identity and Monotonicity):
In the non-Abelian setting, the entropy identity
D_{\max}-D=S-H_{\min}
still holds. Concurrently, for any cluster-preserving channel
E
that leaves the reference state invariant (i.e., a CPTP map satisfying
E(\sigma_{R})=\sigma_{R}
), the monotonicity property
C_{R}(E(\rho))\leq C_{R}(\rho)
also holds.

F.2
Conservative Lower Bound under Energy or Mode Cutoff

When dealing with systems that have an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space, any numerical or experimental analysis must introduce an energy or mode cutoff
\Lambda
. We prove here that the RCC framework can provide a rigorous, conservative lower bound on complexity in this setting.
Let
\Pi_{[0,\Lambda]}
be the projection operator that preserves modes with energy below
\Lambda
. We define a truncated reference set
\Pi_{R}^{(\Lambda)}=\Pi_{[0,\Lambda]}\cdot\Pi_{R}
, with its corresponding reference state being
\sigma_{R}^{(\Lambda)}
. To avoid support issues, we employ a smoothed reference state
\tilde{\sigma}_{R}^{(\Lambda)}=(1-\delta)\sigma_{R}^{(\Lambda)}\oplus\delta\tau_{\perp}
, where
\tau_{\perp}
is the equally-mixed state on the orthogonal complement of
\Pi_{R}^{(\Lambda)}
. For a physical state
\rho_{\mathrm{phys}}
, its retention probability within the truncated subspace is
q=\mathrm{Tr}(\Pi_{R}^{(\Lambda)}\cdot\rho_{\mathrm{phys}})
, and the normalized truncated state is
\rho^{(\Lambda)}
.

Lemma F.2 (Truncation Error Bound):
For a "measure-and-prepare" channel that measures "whether the state is within
\Pi_{R}^{(\Lambda)}
", from the data processing inequality for relative entropy and its decomposition, we have:

D(\rho_{\mathrm{phys}}||\tilde{\sigma}_{R}^{(\Lambda)})\geq D_{\mathrm{Bern}}(q||1-\delta)+q\cdot D(\rho^{(\Lambda)}||\sigma_{R}^{(\Lambda)})

(F.4)

where
D_{\mathrm{Bern}}
is the classical Bernoulli relative entropy (base 2).

Corollary F.2 (Multiplicative Reduction Lower Bound):
By choosing the smoothing parameter
\delta=1-q
, the
D_{\mathrm{Bern}}
term becomes zero, and we obtain a concise multiplicative reduction bound:

D(\rho_{\mathrm{phys}}||\tilde{\sigma}_{R}^{(\Lambda)})\geq q\cdot D(\rho^{(\Lambda)}||\sigma_{R}^{(\Lambda)})

(F.5)

This expression clearly shows that when an energy cutoff or state leakage is present, the leading term of the measurable complexity lower bound is linearly reduced by the retention probability
q
of the state within the target subspace.

F.3
Acceptability and Objectivity of the Reference Set

The effectiveness of the RCC framework depends on the selection of a physically meaningful reference set
R
. To eliminate potential subjective arbitrariness in its selection, we propose the following criteria and robustness characterization. A physically acceptable reference set should satisfy the following criteria:

1.

Structural Invariance:
All dynamical processes in the system that are considered "free" or "zero-cost" should preserve the structure of the reference set.

2.

Measurability:
There must exist physical measurement schemes compatible with
R
that can effectively estimate any of the core information quantities in Appendix
D
.

3.

Principle of Minimal Sufficiency:
Among the candidates that satisfy the first two criteria, one should preferentially choose the "coarsest" structure (i.e., the one with the largest
d_{R}
), so as to avoid overestimating the complexity.

Proposition F.1 (Misspecification Robustness Bound):
Suppose a state
\rho
is supported by two different reference sets,
R
and
R^{\prime}
. The difference between their corresponding complexities,
C_{R}(\rho)
and
C_{R^{\prime}}(\rho)
, is explicitly constrained by the parameters of the reference sets themselves:

|C_{R}(\rho)-C_{R^{\prime}}(\rho)|\leq\left|\frac{\log(d_{R})}{\log(\Gamma_{R})}-\frac{\log(d_{R^{\prime}})}{\log(\Gamma_{R^{\prime}})}\right|+\left|\frac{1}{\log(\Gamma_{R})}-\frac{1}{\log(\Gamma_{R^{\prime}})}\right|\cdot S(\rho)

(F.6)

(This bound only uses
(d_{R},\Gamma_{R})
and
S(\rho)
; for the same
\rho
, this deviation is computable and controllable.)

In particular, if the hardware capability is unchanged (
\Gamma_{R}=\Gamma_{R^{\prime}}
) and
R^{\prime}
is a refinement of
R
(
d_{R^{\prime}}<d_{R}
), then there is an exact difference relation:

0\leq C_{R}(\rho)-C_{R^{\prime}}(\rho)=\frac{\log(d_{R})-\log(d_{R^{\prime}})}{\log(\Gamma_{R})}

(F.7)

This proposition transforms the effect of "subjective selection of a reference set" into a quantitatively computable and controllable deviation, thereby mathematically proving the robustness of the framework.

Appendix G
Near-Tightness of Reference-Contingent Complexity

This appendix aims to provide constructive, non-trivial, positive evidence for Conjecture 6.1 (the near-tightness of
RCC
), which was proposed in Section
6.2
of the main text. The conjecture posits that Reference-Contingent Complexity
C_{R}(\rho)
is not only a lower bound on the universal circuit complexity
C_{\mathrm{opt}}(\rho)
, but that for a class of well-structured families of states, it can also be bounded from above by
C_{R}(\rho)
. We will systematically corroborate this conjecture by constructing complexity upper bounds for three core families of states that are widely present in quantum information and physics: efficiently samplable classical diagonal states, low-rank states constructible via hierarchical projections, and efficiently encodable logical pure states with sparse generators. These constructive proofs collectively reveal that for these physically important states, the scaling behavior of
C_{\mathrm{opt}}(\rho)
is indeed closely related to that of
C_{R}(\rho)
, indicating that
C_{R}(\rho)
is not merely a theoretical baseline, but a physical quantity with strong predictive power for real computational resources.

G.1
Technical Conventions and Parameterization

To ensure the rigor of the arguments, we first unify the technical conventions of this appendix. All logarithms
\log
are taken to base
2
. The error metric used is the trace distance,
(1/2)\cdot||.||_{1}
. All core physical quantities in this paper, such as Reference-Contingent Complexity
C_{R}(\rho)
, the reference state
\sigma_{R}
, the subspace dimension
d_{R}
, and the information bandwidth parameter
\Gamma_{R}
, follow the definitions in Chapter 2 of the main text. According to the Stinespring dilation theorem
[
57
]
, any completely positive and trace-preserving (CPTP) map can be realized by a unitary evolution on a larger Hilbert space, followed by tracing out an ancillary system. This fundamental principle ensures that all seemingly non-unitary constructions in this appendix (such as measurements, random mixing) can be compiled into an equivalent, post-selection-free quantum circuit, with the constant factors in their costs being uniformly absorbed into the final upper bound constants.
A crucial clarification concerns the parameter dependence of the upper bound constants. To avoid circular logic, we must precisely define the scope of independence for these constants.

Definition G.1 (Parameterized Upper Bound Constants for Complexity).
In Conjecture 6.1 and all upper bound constructions in this appendix of the form
C_{\mathrm{opt}}\leq a\cdot C_{R}+b\cdot(\dots)
, the constants
a
and
b
are assumed to be
O(1)
quantities that are independent of the system size
d_{R}
and the specific target state
\rho
. However, they are allowed to depend on the information bandwidth parameter
\Gamma_{R}
, which is uniquely determined by the hardware architecture (i.e., the universal gate set
g
and the addressability
|S_{R}|
). Therefore, the rigorous form of the conjecture should be written as:

C_{\mathrm{opt}}^{(\varepsilon)}(\rho)\leq a(\Gamma_{R})\cdot C_{R}(\rho)+b(\Gamma_{R})\cdot(\log d_{R}+\log(1/\varepsilon))

(G.1)

This parameterization both ensures the validity of the core assertion (that the scaling behavior is independent of
d_{R}
) and aligns it with the intrinsic computational capabilities of different physical platforms.

G.2
Case One: Efficiently Samplable Classical Diagonal States

We first consider a class of states that are of paramount importance in statistical physics and machine learning: their density matrices are diagonal in a computational basis
\{|x\rangle\}
that is compatible with the reference set
R
, and thus are physically equivalent to a classical probability distribution.

Definition G.2 (Family of Classical Diagonal States).
A classical diagonal state
\rho
is a quantum state of the form
\rho=\sum_{x}p_{x}|x\rangle\langle x|
, where
p=\{p_{x}\}
is a probability distribution over
d_{R}=2^{n}
basis states, and
H(p)=-\sum_{x}p_{x}\log(p_{x})
is its Shannon entropy.

For an arbitrary probability distribution
p
, the cost of preparing the corresponding quantum state
\rho
can be extremely high. However, if the distribution satisfies certain conditions of efficient samplability, its complexity is greatly simplified.

Assumption G.1 (Efficient Amplitude Preparation).
A probability distribution
p
is considered efficiently preparable in amplitude if there exists a unitary circuit
U_{\mathrm{amp}}
, acting on
n
ancillary qubits, that can prepare its amplitude-encoded state
|\psi_{p}\rangle=\sum_{x}\sqrt{p_{x}}|x\rangle
within a precision
\varepsilon^{\prime}
in a number of atomic instruction steps bounded by
\mathrm{len}(U_{\mathrm{amp}})\leq c_{1}\cdot n+c_{2}\cdot\log(1/\varepsilon^{\prime})
. This assumption covers an important class of probability distributions in physics and algorithms, for which sufficient conditions include, but are not limited to:
(i)
the distribution can be described by an autoregressive model, and its conditional probabilities
p(x_{i}|x_{<i})
can be efficiently computed classically;
(ii)
the distribution comes from a probabilistic graphical model with low tree-width
[
58
,
59
,
60
,
61
]
.

Proposition G.1.
For a classical diagonal state
\rho
that satisfies Assumption G.1, its optimal circuit complexity
C_{\mathrm{opt}}^{(\varepsilon)}(\rho)
satisfies the following upper bound:

C_{\mathrm{opt}}^{(\varepsilon)}(\rho)\leq b(\Gamma_{R})\cdot(\log d_{R}+\log(1/\varepsilon))

(G.2)

Proof (Constructive).
We can prepare
\rho
via a three-step, post-selection-free unitary circuit. Consider a composite system consisting of a main system
S
(
n
-qubit) and an ancillary system
A
(
n
-qubit), with the initial state being
|0...0\rangle_{S}\otimes|0...0\rangle_{A}
.

1.

Amplitude Preparation:
On the ancillary system
A
, execute the unitary operation
U_{\mathrm{amp}}
to transform the system into
|0...0\rangle_{S}\otimes|\psi_{p}\rangle_{A}
. According to Assumption G.1, the cost of this step is
O(n)=O(\log d_{R})
.

2.

Controlled Copying:
Sequentially, for
i=1...n
, perform a CNOT operation with the
i
-th qubit of
A
as the control and the
i
-th qubit of
S
as the target. This copies the classical information from
|\psi_{p}\rangle_{A}
into
S
, resulting in the entangled state
\sum_{x}\sqrt{p_{x}}|x\rangle_{S}\otimes|x\rangle_{A}
. This step consists of
n
CNOT gates, and its serial cost is
O(n)=O(\log d_{R})
.

3.

Decoupling:
(Conceptually) Trace out the ancillary system
A
. Since
\mathrm{Tr}_{A}[(\sum_{x}\sqrt{p_{x}}|x\rangle_{S}\otimes|x\rangle_{A})(\sum_{y}\sqrt{p_{y}}\langle y|_{S}\otimes\langle y|_{A})]=\sum_{x}p_{x}|x\rangle_{S}\langle x|_{S}=\rho
, we have successfully prepared the target state
\rho
on the main system
S
.

The entire process is a deterministic CPTP map, and the total upper bound on the number of atomic instruction steps is
O(\log d_{R})+O(\log(1/\varepsilon))
, which proves Eq. (
G.2
). This result is in high agreement with Conjecture 6.1.
C_{R}(\rho)
is determined by
(\log d_{R}-H(p))
, while the upper bound on
C_{\mathrm{opt}}(\rho)
is dominated by
\log d_{R}
. The scaling behavior of the two is consistent when
H(p)
is much smaller than
\log d_{R}
, and when
H(p)
approaches
\log d_{R}
, the term
b\cdot\log d_{R}
provides the correct "fallback" upper bound.

G.3
Case Two: Low-Rank, High-Occupation States and Hierarchical Projection Construction

The second important family of states are those whose information is highly concentrated in a small-dimensional subspace of the Hilbert space.

Definition G.3 (Family of Low-Rank, High-Occupation States).
A low-rank, high-occupation state
\rho
is a quantum state of the form
\rho=p\cdot(\Pi/r)+(1-p)\cdot\tau
, where
\Pi
is a projection operator of rank
r\ll d_{R}
,
p
is a high occupation probability close to
1
, and
\tau
is a background state supported within
H_{R}
.

Assumption G.2 (Hierarchical Projectability).
A projector
\Pi
is considered hierarchically constructible if it can be defined by the product
\Pi=\prod_{j}P_{j}
of
m=O(\log(d_{R}/r))
mutually commuting projection operators
\{P_{j}\}
. This is a common structure in stabilizer codes and ground states of commuting local Hamiltonians.

Proposition G.2.
For a low-rank, high-occupation state
\rho
that satisfies Assumption G.2, its optimal circuit complexity
C_{\mathrm{opt}}^{(\varepsilon)}(\rho)
satisfies the following upper bound:

C_{\mathrm{opt}}^{(\varepsilon)}(\rho)\leq a(\Gamma_{R})\cdot C_{R}(\rho)+b(\Gamma_{R})\cdot(\log d_{R}+\log(1/\varepsilon))

(G.3)

Proof (Constructive).
Our core is to construct a CPTP channel
T_{\Pi}
that deterministically maps the maximally mixed reference state
\sigma_{R}
to the mixed state on the target subspace,
\Pi/r
.

Lemma G.2.1.
There exists a CPTP channel
T_{\Pi}
such that
T_{\Pi}(\sigma_{R})=\Pi/r
, and its circuit implementation cost is
O(m)=O(\log(d_{R}/r))
.

Proof of Lemma.
We can construct
T_{\Pi}
by concatenating
m
"measure-reprepare-depolarize" quantum instruments
\Phi^{(j)}
. For each
P_{j}
, define
\Phi^{(j)}(X)=P_{j}\cdot X\cdot P_{j}+\mathrm{Tr}[(I-P_{j})\cdot X]\cdot(P_{j}/\mathrm{Tr}(P_{j}))
. This is a post-selection-free CPTP map, which physically corresponds to measuring the eigenvalue of
P_{j}
: if the outcome is
+1
, the state is kept; if the outcome is
-1
, that part of the state is reinjected into the
+1
subspace of
P_{j}
. Since all
P_{j}
commute, these
m
channels can be applied sequentially, with each step compressing the state into a smaller subspace, ultimately mapping
\sigma_{R}
exactly to
\Pi/r
. The implementation cost of each step
\Phi^{(j)}
depends on the structure of
P_{j}
. If
P_{j}
acts on a local subsystem, this operation can be realized via a local
2
-design with
O(1)
cost
[
62
,
63
]
. If
P_{j}
is non-local, its implementation cost is absorbed as a constant factor into the total cost of
O(\log(d_{R}/r))
. Using this lemma, we can prepare
\rho
by introducing a control qubit: with probability
p
, apply the channel
T_{\Pi}
to
\sigma_{R}
, and with probability
1-p
, prepare the background state
\tau
. The total cost is dominated by
O(\log(d_{R}/r))
and the cost of preparing
\tau
, with an upper bound of
O(\log d_{R})
. On the other hand, from the convexity of relative entropy, the leading term of
C_{R}(\rho)
is determined by
p\cdot\log(d_{R}/r)
. Therefore, the upper bound on
C_{\mathrm{opt}}(\rho)
is consistent in scaling with the leading term of
C_{R}(\rho)
, thus verifying Eq. (
G.3
).

G.4
Case Three: Sparse Graph States and Efficiently Encodable Logical Pure States

The final class, and the one that most strongly demonstrates the near-tightness of
RCC
, comprises the core objects of quantum computation and error correction theory—logical pure states.

Definition G.4 (Family of Efficiently Encodable Logical Pure States).
A logical pure state
\rho=|\psi_{L}\rangle\langle\psi_{L}|
is a pure state located within a
d_{R}=2^{k}
-dimensional code subspace
H_{R}
. It is considered efficiently encodable if there exists an encoding unitary circuit
U_{\mathrm{enc}}
that can map a simple product state
|s_{0}\rangle
to a logical ground state
|0\rangle_{L}
within the code space, in a number of atomic instruction steps bounded by
\mathrm{len}(U_{\mathrm{enc}})\leq c\cdot k
. Logical operations can then be performed on this state. Typical representatives of this class of states are:
(i)
graph states with bounded vertex degree (
|E|=O(k)
);
(ii)
logical states of stabilizer codes that are geometrically sparse or satisfy LDPC conditions
[
15
,
64
]
.

Proposition G.3.
For a logical pure state
\rho
belonging to an efficiently encodable family, its optimal circuit complexity
C_{\mathrm{opt}}^{(\varepsilon)}(\rho)
is directly proportional to its Reference-Contingent Complexity
C_{R}(\rho)
:

C_{\mathrm{opt}}^{(\varepsilon)}(\rho)\leq a(\Gamma_{R})\cdot C_{R}(\rho)+b(\Gamma_{R})\cdot\log(1/\varepsilon)

(G.4)

Proof (Direct Construction).
For this class of states, the upper bound on
C_{\mathrm{opt}}(\rho)
is directly given by the most efficient encoding circuit, i.e.,
C_{\mathrm{opt}}(\rho)\leq O(k)=O(\log d_{R})
. On the other hand, since
\rho
is a pure state, its von Neumann entropy is
S(\rho)=0
. Therefore, its Reference-Contingent Complexity is:

C_{R}(\rho)=\frac{\log d_{R}-S(\rho)}{\log\Gamma_{R}}=\frac{k}{\log\Gamma_{R}}

(G.5)

Comparing the upper bound on
C_{\mathrm{opt}}(\rho)
, which is
O(k)
, with
C_{R}(\rho)=k/\log\Gamma_{R}
, we find that they differ only by a factor of
a(\Gamma_{R})=O(\log\Gamma_{R})
, and their scaling behaviors are identical. This directly proves Eq. (
G.4
), providing the strongest corroborating evidence for Conjecture 6.1.

Conclusion:
Through a constructive analysis of three families of states that are physically and computationally distinct but are all "well-structured," we have consistently found that the upper bound on their optimal preparation complexity,
C_{\mathrm{opt}}^{(\varepsilon)}(\rho)
, follows the form
C_{\mathrm{opt}}\leq a(\Gamma_{R})\cdot C_{R}(\rho)+b(\Gamma_{R})\cdot(\log d_{R}+\log(1/\varepsilon))
. This series of non-trivial pieces of evidence strongly supports the validity of Conjecture 6.1 proposed in the main text. This profoundly suggests that Reference-Contingent Complexity
C_{R}(\rho)
is not just an abstract, possibly loose, theoretical lower bound, but is, for a broad class of physically relevant states, a "near-tight" physical benchmark that can effectively estimate the true computational complexity.

Proposition 5.3

I.1
Core Setting

To proceed with the subsequent derivations, we first establish the core mathematical and physical setting, which specifically includes the following four aspects:

1.

System and Reference Set:
A finite-dimensional Hilbert space
H
. The reference set
R
is defined by a set of mutually commuting projectors
\{P_{i}\}

\Pi_{R}=\prod_{i}P_{i}\neq 0

H_{R}=\Pi_{R}H
(with dimension

\sigma_{R}=\Pi_{R}/d_{R}

2.

Windowed Coarse-Graining:
We adopt the conditional expectation maps
\{E_{\Xi}\}
(
\Xi\geq 0

[

\{A(\Xi)\}

E_{\Xi}(\sigma_{R})=\sigma_{R},\quad E_{\Xi}\circ E_{\Xi}=E_{\Xi},\quad\Xi_{1}\leq\Xi_{2}\Rightarrow A(\Xi_{1})\subseteq A(\Xi_{2})

(I.1.1)

The windowed entropy is defined as
S_{\Xi}(\rho)=S(E_{\Xi}(\rho))
, where
S
is the von Neumann entropy.

3.

CWT Thermodynamic Accounting:
The energy is
E=E(S_{\Xi},\Xi,\dots)
; its differential is
\mathrm{d}E=T_{\Xi}\mathrm{d}S_{\Xi}+\Pi_{\Xi}\mathrm{d}\Xi+\dots
, where
T_{\Xi}=(\partial E/\partial S_{\Xi})_{\Xi}
and
\Pi_{\Xi}=(\partial E/\partial\Xi)_{S_{\Xi}}

\Pi_{\Xi}=-T_{\Xi}\cdot(\partial S_{\Xi}/\partial\Xi)_{E}

4.

RCC Definitions:
The information bandwidth
\Gamma_{R}
is determined by
R
and the hardware addressability (see Sec. 2.1). The standard and windowed RCC are:

C_{R}(\rho)=\frac{\log d_{R}-S(\rho)}{\log\Gamma_{R}},\quad C_{R,\Xi}(\rho)=\frac{\log d_{R}-S_{\Xi}(\rho)}{\log\Gamma_{R}}

(I.1.2)

It is assumed by default that
\Gamma_{R}
is independent of
\Xi
; for a generalization, see
Sec. I.3.2
. Relevant functions are assumed to have sufficient smoothness.

I.2
Mathematical Properties of Windowed RCC

Based on the setting above, the windowed RCC (
C_{R,\Xi}
), as a core measure, exhibits the following key mathematical properties:

Proposition I.1 (Monotonicity and Limits).
Under the setting (
I.1.1
), for any state
\rho
:

(i)

If
\Xi_{1}\leq\Xi_{2}
, then
S_{\Xi_{1}}(\rho)\geq S_{\Xi_{2}}(\rho)
, and therefore
C_{R,\Xi}
is monotonically non-decreasing with
\Xi
;

(ii)

\lim_{\Xi\to\infty}S_{\Xi}(\rho)=S(\rho)
, hence
\lim_{\Xi\to\infty}C_{R,\Xi}(\rho)=C_{R}(\rho)
.

Proof.
From
S_{\Xi}(\rho)=\log d_{R}-D(E_{\Xi}(\rho)||\sigma_{R})
,
E_{\Xi_{1}}=E_{\Xi_{1}}\circ E_{\Xi_{2}}
, and
E_{\Xi}(\sigma_{R})=\sigma_{R}
, applying the data processing inequality to the relative entropy yields
D(E_{\Xi_{1}}(\rho)||\sigma_{R})\leq D(E_{\Xi_{2}}(\rho)||\sigma_{R})
; hence the conclusion holds.

I.3
The "Work–Complexity" Relation: From Identity to Boundary Cases

This section aims to establish a precise mathematical relationship between the generalized force (the differential form of work) and the change in complexity.

I.3.1
Identity and Work Accounting in the Standard Case

Theorem I.1 (Work–Complexity Identity).

\Pi_{\Xi}=T_{\Xi}\cdot\log(\Gamma_{R})\cdot\left(\frac{\partial C_{R,\Xi}}{\partial\Xi}\right)_{E}

(I.3.1)

Proof.
From (
I.1.2
), we have
(\partial C_{R,\Xi}/\partial\Xi)_{E}=-(1/\log\Gamma_{R})\cdot(\partial S_{\Xi}/\partial\Xi)_{E}
. Using
\Pi_{\Xi}=-T_{\Xi}\cdot(\partial S_{\Xi}/\partial\Xi)_{E}
then yields the result.

Corollary I.1 (Rigorous Work Accounting and Weighted Average Temperature).

W_{\mathrm{info}}=\int_{\Xi_{i}}^{\Xi_{f}}\Pi_{\Xi}\mathrm{d}\Xi=\log\Gamma_{R}\cdot\int_{C_{R,\Xi_{i}}}^{C_{R,\Xi_{f}}}T_{\Xi}\mathrm{d}C_{R,\Xi}=\log\Gamma_{R}\cdot\langle T_{\Xi}\rangle_{C}\cdot\Delta C_{R,\Xi}

(I.3.2)

where
\langle T_{\Xi}\rangle_{C}=\left(\int T_{\Xi}\mathrm{d}C_{R,\Xi}\right)/\Delta C_{R,\Xi}
.

I.3.2
Generalization: Variable Bandwidth
\Gamma_{R}(\Xi)

When
C_{R,\Xi}=(\log d_{R}-S_{\Xi})/\log\Gamma_{R}(\Xi)
, the chain rule gives

\left(\frac{\partial C_{R,\Xi}}{\partial\Xi}\right)_{E}=-\frac{1}{\log\Gamma_{R}}\cdot\left(\frac{\partial S_{\Xi}}{\partial\Xi}\right)_{E}-\frac{C_{R,\Xi}}{\log\Gamma_{R}}\cdot\left(\frac{\partial\log\Gamma_{R}}{\partial\Xi}\right)

(I.3.3)

Substituting this into the definition of
\Pi_{\Xi}
yields the generalized identity

\Pi_{\Xi}=T_{\Xi}\cdot\left[\log\Gamma_{R}\cdot\left(\frac{\partial C_{R,\Xi}}{\partial\Xi}\right)_{E}+C_{R,\Xi}\cdot\left(\frac{\partial\log\Gamma_{R}}{\partial\Xi}\right)\right]

(I.3.4)

I.4
The "Time–Complexity" Lower Bound: From Strict to Complete Form

This section derives the lower bound on the time required to complete a computational task, a bound determined by the change in complexity.

I.4.1
Strict Lower Bound and Physical Form

CWT provides an upper bound on the envelope rate,
\dot{C}^{*}(t)\leq(2/\hbar)\Pi_{\Xi}(t)
(almost everywhere). Integrating over time yields

C^{*}(t_{f})-C^{*}(0)\leq\frac{2}{\hbar}\cdot t_{f}\cdot\langle\Pi_{\Xi}\rangle_{t}

(I.4.1)

Combining this with the main theorem,
C^{*}(t_{f})\geq C_{R}(\rho_{f})
, gives

t_{f}\geq\frac{\hbar}{2\langle\Pi_{\Xi}\rangle_{t}}\cdot[C_{R}(\rho_{f})-C^{*}(0)]

(I.4.2)

Under the conditions of a
compact initial state
or a
non-retreating envelope
, this simplifies to

t_{f}\geq\frac{\hbar}{2\langle\Pi_{\Xi}\rangle_{t}}\cdot[C_{R}(\rho_{f})-C_{R}(\rho_{i})]

(I.4.3)

For an isothermal process and taking
\Pi_{\Xi}\lesssim\pi k_{B}T_{\Xi}
, we get

t_{f}\gtrsim\frac{\hbar}{2\pi k_{B}\bar{T}_{\Xi}}\cdot\Delta C_{R}

(I.4.4)

I.4.2
Lower Bound with Full Correction Terms

Substituting the full form of the main theorem, let

C_{R}^{\mathrm{full}}(\rho)=C_{R}(\rho)+\frac{\Delta_{\mathrm{spec}}(\rho)}{\log\Gamma_{R}}\\
C_{\log}(t,\varepsilon)=\frac{c_{1}}{\log\Gamma_{R}}\cdot\left[\log(\max\{1,C^{*}(t)\})+\log(1/\varepsilon)\right]

(I.4.5)

This gives

t_{f}\geq\frac{\hbar}{2\langle\Pi_{\Xi}\rangle_{t}}\cdot[C_{R}^{\mathrm{full}}(\rho_{f})-C_{R}^{\mathrm{full}}(\rho_{i})-\Delta C_{\log}^{\mathrm{upper}}]

(I.4.6)

where
\Delta C_{\log}^{\mathrm{upper}}=C_{\log}(t_{f},\varepsilon)-C_{\log}(0,\varepsilon)
is the difference in the logarithmic correction terms. If it is necessary to eliminate the bootstrap nature of this bound to obtain a closed-form lower bound, one can refer to the Lambert-W technique in Appendix
C.2
.

I.4.3
Boundary Case:
\Pi_{\Xi}
Changing Sign

If
\Pi_{\Xi}<0
during some part of the process, considering
\Pi_{\Xi}^{+}(t)=\max\{\Pi_{\Xi}(t),0\}
yields a sign-robust lower bound

t_{f}\geq\frac{\hbar}{2\langle\Pi_{\Xi}^{+}\rangle_{t}}\cdot[C_{R}(\rho_{f})-C^{*}(0)],\quad\langle\Pi_{\Xi}^{+}\rangle_{t}=\frac{1}{t_{f}}\int_{0}^{t_{f}}\Pi_{\Xi}^{+}(t)\mathrm{d}t

(I.4.7)

I.5
Robustness Analysis of the Framework

Finally, we analyze the robustness of this theoretical framework in the face of two typical practical challenges: leakage of the physical state and statistical uncertainty in the measures.

I.5.1
Handling of Support Set Leakage

If the physical state
\rho
has a small leakage
p_{\mathrm{leak}}
with respect to the target subspace
H_{R}
, the projected-and-renormalized state
\tilde{\rho}
has a trace distance

\delta=\frac{1}{2}||\tilde{\rho}-\rho||_{1}\leq 2p_{\mathrm{leak}}+2\sqrt{p_{\mathrm{leak}}(1-p_{\mathrm{leak}})}

(I.5.1)

By the Fannes–Audenaert continuity bound,

|S_{\Xi}(\tilde{\rho})-S_{\Xi}(\rho)|\leq\delta\log(d_{R}-1)+h_{2}(\delta)

(I.5.2)

which implies

|C_{R,\Xi}(\tilde{\rho})-C_{R,\Xi}(\rho)|\leq\frac{(\delta\log(d_{R}-1)+h_{2}(\delta))}{\log\Gamma_{R}}

(I.5.3)

When
p_{\mathrm{leak}}
is sufficiently small,
\delta=O(\sqrt{p_{\mathrm{leak}}})
, and the error is controllable.

I.5.2
Statistical Ledger for Operational Estimation

Following the protocol in Chapter
4
, one can obtain a lower bound
L_{H}
for
D_{H}^{\eta}(E_{\Xi}(\rho)||\sigma_{R})
at a confidence level of
1-\delta
, which in turn gives a statistical lower bound for the main term of
C_{R,\Xi}
,
C_{R,\Xi}^{\mathrm{(main)}}\geq L_{H}/\log\Gamma_{R}
. Substituting this into (
I.3.2
) and (
I.4.2
) allows for the estimation of confidence intervals for
W_{\mathrm{info}}
and
t_{f}
.
