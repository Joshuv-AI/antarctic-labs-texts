Biocompatibility of carbazochrome sodium sulfonate on human dental pulp stem cells: An in vitro investigation
Authors: M. R. Keerthika, Smita Surendran, Velmurugan Natanasabapathy, Sooriaprakas Chandrasekaran, Aishwarya Vasudevan, Kavya Baskar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:The effectiveness of vital pulp therapy (VPT) depends on achieving adequate hemostasis and preservation of pulp vitality. Carbazochrome sodium sulfonate (CSS), a synthetic hemostatic agent considered to be safe in systemic use; however, its local effect on human dental pulp stem cells (HDPSCs) remains unexplored. This preliminary in vitro study investigated the effects of various CSS concentrations and exposure durations on HDPSC behavior.Materials and Methods:HDPSCs were derived from healthy permanent teeth and maintained under standard culture conditions. The cells were distributed into control group (0.1% dimethyl sulfoxide serving as the vehicle) and experimental groups treated with CSS at 0.1 μM, 1 μM, 5 μM, and 10 μM. Cellular responses were assessed at 24, 48, and 72 h following exposure. The 3-[4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl]-2,5 diphenyl tetrazolium bromide assay was utilized to determine cell viability, and adhesion was calculated by measuring the number of nonadherent cells. Data were analyzed using the one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test.Results:Lower concentrations of CSS (0.1 μM and 1 μM) demonstrated viability and adhesion values similar to the control group. In contrast, higher concentrations (5 μM and 10 μM) showed a gradual decrease in both parameters over time (P < 0.05). The reduction was more evident with prolonged exposure.Conclusion:Based on the findings from this study, lower concentrations of CSS were well tolerated with HDPSCs, whereas higher concentrations negatively influenced both viability and adhesion. Hence, precise concentration control is essential for the use of CSS in VPT.

INTRODUCTION

Vital pulp therapy (VPT) has become a widely accepted treatment approach in conservative dentistry, especially for preserving dental pulp vitality in teeth with deep carious lesions or traumatic exposure.[12] Earlier pulp therapies concentrated mainly on controlling pain and associated symptoms. As understanding of pulp biology advances and superior biomaterials are developed, treatment options have progressively transitioned toward maintaining pulp vitality and enhancing the intrinsic healing capacity of pulp tissue. This reflects a shift toward minimally invasive, biologically driven approaches.[345]

The successful outcome of VPT is influenced by several biological events that occur immediately after pulp exposure. These include hemostasis, regulation of inflammatory response, protection of healthy pulp cells, and reparative dentin formation.[67] Among these, the management of hemorrhage is particularly important. Profuse bleeding can hinder the operating site, thereby making placement of the restorative material difficult. In addition, persistent bleeding may indicate underlying pulp inflammation.[7] Therefore, the success of VPT largely depends on achieving effective haemostasis, which is influenced by both clinical and biological factors.

Various hemostatic agents, such as sodium hypochlorite, ferric sulfate, aluminum chloride, and epinephrine-containing solutions, are used in dentistry to control pulpal bleeding during VPT. Among these, sodium hypochlorite is the most commonly used hemostatic agent as it disinfects the exposed area and helps achieve hemostasis during pulp treatments.[38] In clinical practice, this is often used as a key indicator of pulpal health when deciding whether to proceed with VPT.[9]

Although these agents effectively control bleeding, many studies have reported cytotoxic effects on pulp cells, especially at higher concentrations or with prolonged exposure.[10111213] While sodium hypochlorite is commonly used in clinical practice, laboratory studies have shown that it is highly toxic to pulp-derived cells.[14] Earlier studies have also reported that commonly used hemostatic and pulp management agents can adversely affect dental pulp stem cell viability and function in a concentration and time-dependent manner, indicating the need for more biologically compatible hemostatic agents.[1213]

Carbazochrome sodium sulfonate (CSS) is a hemostatic agent that improves capillary stability and reduces vascular permeability. It has long been used in medical practice for the control of hemorrhage.[15] More recently, its application has been explored in the surgical field, where its combination with tranexamic acid has been associated with reduced blood loss and postoperative inflammation without an increased risk of thrombotic events.[1617] However, information regarding its local effects on dental pulp tissues remains limited. As newer hemostatic agents are introduced, it is necessary to examine their biological effects at the cellular level before considering their use in VPT.

Human dental pulp stem cells (HDPSCs) are widely used as an in vitro model to evaluate cellular responses to dental materials.[1819] These cells are sensitive to chemical agents and early variations in viability or adhesion can reflect potential biological effects.[2021] Thus, assessing the HDPSCs’ response offers an initial understanding of the compatibility of hemostatic agents with pulp tissues.

With the increasing emphasis on biologically driven endodontic treatments, it is important to evaluate the interaction between CSS and HDPSCs before its use in VPT.[22] To date, there is limited evidence regarding the direct biological effects of CSS on HDPSCs. Considering the critical role of hemostasis in determining treatment outcomes, an ideal hemostatic agent should effectively control bleeding while maintaining pulp cell viability and function. Therefore, the aim of this Phase I in vitro study was to assess the effect of CSS on the viability and adhesion of HDPSCs at different concentrations and exposure periods. The null hypothesis was that CSS would not significantly influence HDPSC viability or adhesion under the tested conditions.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethical approval

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee (MADC 66/IC/2026). Informed consent was obtained from all the patients before tooth extraction.

Cell source and culture conditions

HDPSCs were isolated from healthy permanent third molars extracted for orthodontic reasons in patients aged 18–25 years (n = 30), following methods reported in earlier studies.[1819]

Following extraction, the pulp tissue was retrieved under sterile conditions and treated with collagenase type I and dispase for enzymatic digestion to obtain a single-cell suspension. The isolated cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Cultures were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 in a humidified atmosphere. Early passage cells were used for all experiments to preserve stem cell characteristics and ensure experimental consistency.

Preparation of carbazochrome sodium sulfonate solutions

(CSS; MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) solutions were made at varying concentrations by dissolving the agent in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). These solutions were further diluted in cell culture medium to prepare final CSS concentrations of 0.1 μM, 1 μM, 5 μM, and 10 μM. The control group comprised cells exposed only to the vehicle, containing 0.1% DMSO alone. To minimize solvent-related effects, the vehicle control was standardized across all groups.

Cell viability assay

HDPSCs were seeded in 96-well culture plates at a density of approximately 5 × 104 cells per well and incubated overnight to allow cell attachment. The cells were then allocated into five groups: A control group containing 0.1% DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) vehicle alone without CSS, and four experimental groups subjected to CSS at the concentrations of 0.1 μM, 1 μM, 5 μM, and 10 μM.

Cell viability was analyzed at 24, 48, and 72 h postexposure using the 3-[4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl]-2,5 diphenyl tetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay. At each time point, 20 μL of MTT reagent (5 mg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was dispensed into each well, and the cells were incubated at 37°C for 3 h. Following incubation, the culture medium was aspirated, and the resultant formazan crystals were dissolved in 100 μL DMSO. Absorbance readings were measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader. All experiments were performed in triplicate with three independent biological replicates [Figure 1].

Viability (%) of human dental pulp stem cells following carbazochrome sodium sulfonate exposure at varying concentrations measured at the specified time points, analyzed by the 3-[4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl]-2,5 diphenyl tetrazolium bromide method

Cell adhesion assay

To study adhesion, HDPSCs were seeded in six-well plates, at a density of approximately 1 × 105 cells per well. The cells were allowed to attach overnight. Afterward, the cells were grouped as described earlier.

The culture medium containing nonadherent cells was collected and centrifuged at 24, 48, and 72 h. The nonadherent cells were counted using a hemocytometer (Neubauer chamber; Marienfeld, Germany) under a light microscope. The number of adherent cells was then calculated by subtracting the nonadherent cells from the initial seeding density [Figure 2].

Human dental pulp stem cells adhesion after exposure to CSS across different concentrations over 24–72 h was evaluated based on the number of nonadherent cells

Sample size calculation

As this investigation represents a Phase I in vitro safety and biocompatibility study, sample size estimation was based on pilot viability data obtained at 72 h. Preliminary means (control ≈ 80.84; 0.1 μM ≈ 71.00; 1 μM ≈ 70.33; 5 μM ≈ 44.92; 10 μM ≈ 42.11) and a pooled standard deviation of approximately 10 absorbance units yielded an estimated effect size (Cohen’s f) of 1.54. Using an alpha level of 0.05 and a power of 0.80, power analysis indicated that a minimum of 11 biological replicates per group was required. To account for potential experimental loss, 12 independent biological replicates per group were included.

Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Intergroup comparisons were calculated using the one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA).

RESULTS

Effect of carbazochrome sodium sulfonate on human dental pulp stem cells viability

HDPSC viability was affected by both the concentration of CSS and the duration of exposure when compared with the vehicle control group (0.1% DMSO). After 24 h, the viability values for the lower CSS concentrations (0.1 μM and 1 μM) were similar to those of the vehicle control. After 48 h, the 1 μM group showed viability values comparable to, or slightly greater than, the control, while a decrease was seen in the 5 μM and 10 μM groups. By 72 h, a slight decrease in viability was observed in the low concentration groups; however, viability remained significantly higher than that of the 5 μM and 10 μM groups (P < 0.05).

A marked reduction (approximately 40%–50%) in cell viability was observed in the 10 μM group at 72 h compared to the control group. Overall, higher CSS concentrations demonstrated a clear time-dependent cytotoxic effect on HDPSCs [Table 1].

Effect of carbazochrome sodium sulfonate on human dental pulp stem cells adhesion

Cell adhesion varied with CSS concentration. The control and low CSS concentration groups showed a gradual increase in adherent cells over time, indicating stable adhesion and proliferation. In contrast, exposure to 5 μM and 10 μM CSS led to reduced adhesion at both 48 and 72 h when compared with the control and low concentration groups (P < 0.05).

The greatest reduction in adhesion (approximately 35%–45%) was observed in the 10 μM group at 72 h. These findings indicate a concentration- and time-dependent decline in HDPSC adhesion with increasing CSS concentrations [Table 2 and Figure 2]. DISCUSSION

In this study, CSS was examined as a potential hemostatic agent for VPT, given its ability to reduce microvascular bleeding and improve capillary stability.[151617] The results indicated that the response of HDPSCs showed variation with concentration and time. Lower concentrations (0.1 and 1 μM) showed stable cell response, whereas higher concentrations (5 and 10 μM) resulted in a decrease in both viability and adhesion. This helps to define a safe concentration range for CSS in the context of VPT.

HDPSCs exposed to lower concentrations should maintain metabolic activity similar to that of the vehicle control, especially at early time points. Preservation of early cell viability is important, as initial cellular injury following pulp exposure may influence subsequent healing events such as proliferation and differentiation.[1820] In contrast, the reduction in viability observed at higher concentrations became more evident with increasing exposure time, especially at 48 and 72 h. This suggests that the cytotoxic effect of CSS at higher concentrations may be cumulative rather than immediate. The concentration-dependent response observed in this study is consistent with the biological principle that increasing exposure to pharmacologically active compounds may alter cellular metabolism, membrane integrity, and adhesion signalling pathways.[1321]

The concentration range utilized in this study was selected based on pilot observations and previously reported pharmacological data.[151617] Lower concentrations (0.1–1 μM) were chosen to simulate minimal local exposure, whereas higher concentrations (5–10 μM) were included to evaluate concentration-dependent biological effects. The observed reduction in viability and adhesion at higher concentrations suggests a threshold beyond which cellular homeostasis may be affected.

Compared to sodium hypochlorite, which has been reported to exhibit significant cytotoxic effects on pulp-derived cells even at lower concentrations, CSS at lower concentrations in the present study demonstrated relatively favorable biocompatibility. A similar trend has been reported with other agents used during pulp therapy and restorative procedures, where increasing concentration and exposure duration can adversely affect cellular metabolism and integrity.[1112] In recent years, in vitro evaluation using HDPSCs has been widely recommended as an initial step before progressing to animal or clinical studies. For instance, calcium silicate-based materials such as Biodentine have demonstrated favorable effects on pulp cell viability, adhesion, and differentiation, which has supported their further validation in in vivo models.[23242526] A comparable stepwise approach was followed in this study to assess the biological response of HDPSCs to CSS before further investigation. Although the exact molecular mechanisms were not examined, the observed effects may be related to alterations in intracellular homeostasis with prolonged exposure.

Cell adhesion is another important factor to consider, as it plays a key role in maintaining cell structure and transmitting survival signals through cell–matrix interactions.[21] In this study, adhesion was maintained in the control and lower concentration groups, whereas higher concentrations clearly affected cell attachment. Reduced adhesion can increase susceptibility to detachment-induced apoptosis (anoikis), ultimately reducing the number of functional cells available for regeneration. The parallel reduction in both viability and adhesion at higher concentrations suggests that these processes may be closely interrelated.

From a regenerative point of view, reduced cell adhesion may impair reparative dentin formation. Pulpal healing depends on multiple cellular events, including attachment, migration, and differentiation. Earlier studies have reported that disturbances in adhesion can disrupt odontogenic differentiation and subsequent mineralized tissue formation.[2027] Even if early cytotoxicity is low, poor adhesion may alter the healing response. This highlights the importance of evaluating both viability and adhesion when assessing the biological effects of hemostatic agents.

The clinical relevance of these findings should be considered carefully. In vitro models lack the complexity of the in vivo pulp environment, including vascular supply, immune response, and inflammatory changes.[19] Clinically, hemostatic agents are applied for short durations of approximately 30–60 s, which may minimize cell damage compared to the prolonged exposure in experimental conditions. Despite these limitations, in vitro models remain useful for identifying safe concentration ranges and guiding further studies.[28]

Clinical evidence from surgical settings has shown favorable outcomes with CSS, including reduced blood loss and better postoperative recovery, without significant adverse effects.[1617] However, these findings focus on systemic use and do not provide insight into local tissue responses within the dental pulp. This study helps to address this gap by providing initial cellular-level effects relevant to VPT. Further studies under inflammatory conditions are needed, as they resemble actual clinical conditions.[19]

Based on the present findings, CSS was compatible with HDPSCs at lower concentrations, whereas higher concentrations reduced viability and adhesion. Future studies evaluating odontogenic differentiation markers and inflammatory cytokine expression, along with in vivo animal investigations, are necessary to validate further the biological safety and regenerative potential of CSS in VPT.

CONCLUSION

Within the limitations of this Phase I in vitro study, CSS was biocompatible at lower concentrations (0.1 and 1 μM), whereas higher concentrations (5 and 10 μM), adversely affected HDPSC viability and adhesion. These findings suggest that CSS could be used as a hemostatic agent in VPT when concentration and exposure duration are regulated. Further preclinical and clinical studies are required to better understand its effects on pulp healing and regeneration.
