On Siegel’s problem and Dwork’s conjecture for G G -functions
Javier Fresán, Yeuk Hay Joshua Lam, Yichen Qin

Abstract
. We answer in the negative Siegel’s problem for G G -functions, as formulated by Fischler and Rivoal. Roughly, we prove that there are G G -functions that cannot be written as polynomial expressions in algebraic pullbacks of hypergeometric functions; our examples satisfy differential equations of order two, which is the smallest possible. In fact, we construct infinitely many non-equivalent rank-two local systems of geometric origin which are not algebraic pullbacks of hypergeometric local systems, thereby providing further counterexamples to Dwork’s conjecture and answering a question by Krammer. The main ingredients of the proof are a Lie algebra version of Goursat’s lemma, the monodromy computations of hypergeometric local systems due to Beukers and Heckman, as well as results on invariant trace fields of Fuchsian groups.



1.
Introduction

In his 1929 memoir on applications of Diophantine approximation, Siegel
[
Sie29
]
introduced two classes of functions of a single variable
z
that he named
G
-functions and
E
-functions, as they were reminiscent of the geometric and the exponential series, respectively. The present work focuses on
G
-functions: roughly, these are power series
F(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}z^{n}
with algebraic coefficients
a_{n}\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
such that

(1)

F(z)
converges in a neighborhood of
0
and satisfies a linear differential equation with coefficients in
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}(z)
;

(2)

the sequence
d_{n}
of “common denominators” of
a_{0},\ldots,a_{n}
grows at most exponentially with
n
.

Siegel gave many examples of
G
-functions, with the primary ones being the so-called hypergeometric functions, defined explicitly as

(1.0.1)

_{p+1}F_{p}[a_{1},\dots,a_{p+1};b_{1},\dots,b_{p}\,|\,z]\vcentcolon=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a_{1})_{n}\cdots(a_{p+1})_{n}}{(b_{1})_{n}\cdots(b_{p})_{n}}\frac{z^{n}}{n!},

for parameters
a_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}
and
b_{j}\in\mathbb{Q}\setminus\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}
, where
(x)_{n}
is the Pochhammer symbol, given by
(x)_{0}=1
and
(x)_{n}=x(x+1)\cdots(x+n-1)
for
n\geq 1
. For example,
{}_{1}F_{0}[1;\emptyset|z]=\sum_{n\geq 0}z^{n}
is the usual geometric series.

Hypergeometric functions have a rich history. For
p=1
, their coefficients seem to have been first studied by Wallis in 1656
[
Wal56
, Scholium to Prop. 190]
, who coined the term
hypergeometric progression
in contrast to the usual geometric progressions. These functions were then studied by Stirling, Euler, Gauss, Riemann, and many others; we refer the reader to
[
Dut84
]
for the early history of hypergeometric functions. More recently, they were revisited by Katz in his influential works
[
Kat90
,
Kat96
]
on differential equations and rigid local systems on a punctured projective line.

Conjecturally,
G
-functions are precisely the elements of
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[\![z]\!]
satisfying differential equations
of geometric origin
, i.e., products of irreducible factors of Picard–Fuchs differential equations arising from one-parameter families of algebraic varieties over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
; see
[
And89
, Intro. Conj.]
. That solutions of Picard–Fuchs differential equations are indeed
G
-functions was proved by André
[
And89
, Intro. Thm. B]
, and the converse is the wide open Bombieri–Dwork conjecture. For this reason,
G
-functions have close ties to arithmetic geometry
[
And25
]
. The main result of this paper is a concrete statement about
G
-functions: we show the existence of a
G
-function that cannot be expressed as a polynomial in algebraic pullbacks of hypergeometric functions.

1.1.
Main results

The set of
G
-functions is closed under addition and multiplication, as well as algebraic substitutions of the variable. Therefore, starting from the hypergeometric
G
-functions (
1.0.1
), one can produce many new
G
-functions in this way. The analog of Siegel’s problem for
E
-functions, as stated by Fischler and Rivoal in
[
FR22
, Question 2]
, asks whether this produces all
G
-functions:

Question 1.1.1

(Siegel’s problem for
G
-functions)
.

Is it possible to write every
G
-function as a polynomial with coefficients in
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
in functions of the form

(1.1.1)

\mu(z)\cdot\ _{p+1}F_{p}[a_{1},\dots,a_{p+1};b_{1},\dots,b_{p}\,|\,\lambda(z)],

for integers
p\geq 0
, parameters
a_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}
and
b_{j}\in\mathbb{Q}\setminus\mathbb{Z}
, and algebraic functions
\mu(z)
and
\lambda(z)
such that
\lambda(z)
is holomorphic at
z=0
, and
\lambda(0)=0
?

The reason for the conditions on
\lambda
is that
G
-functions are, by definition, centered at zero. One can also work with a slightly more general notion that accommodates poles and log terms, but we will ignore this subtlety in the introduction.

Remark 1.1.2
.

This is almost the exact analog of Siegel’s original problem for
E
-functions, which was only recently answered in the negative by Fresán and Jossen
[
FJ21
]
. The main difference is that there is much more flexibility in the case of
G
-functions: while composing
G
-functions with algebraic functions
\lambda(z)
as above gives again
G
-functions, the same is seldom true for
E
-functions.

Fischler and Rivoal showed that
1.1.1
likely had a negative answer. In fact, combining
[
FR22
, Thm. 7.2]
with the argument on page 90 of
loc. cit.
due to André, the exponential Grothendieck period conjecture from
[
FJ24
, Conj. 1.3.2]
implies that there are counterexamples to a weaker version of
1.1.1
, assuming further that the algebraic functions
\lambda
have a common singularity at which they all tend to
\infty
.
Our main result provides unconditional and explicit counterexamples. We say that a
G
-function is
of differential order
k
if
k
is the minimal order of a non-zero differential operator with coefficients in
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}(z)
annihilating it.

Theorem 1.1.3
.

The answer to
1.1.1
is negative. More precisely, there exist
G
-functions of differential order
2
that cannot be written as a
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
-polynomial expression in functions of the form (
1.1.1
).

In fact, we prove that there are infinitely many such
G
-functions of differential order 2, even up to reparametrizations of the coordinate
z
; this is
5.3.1
, which answers a question of Daan Krammer’s.

As an explicit example, we show in
4.3.4
that the series expansion at
0
of all solutions in
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[\![z]\!]
to the differential equation

(1.1.2)

P(z)F^{\prime\prime}(z)+\frac{P^{\prime}(z)}{2}F^{\prime}+\frac{z-10}{18}F=0,

where
P(z)=(z-1)(z-2)(z-82)
, are counterexamples. In particular, one such solution is the
G
-function

F(z)=1-\frac{5}{2952}z^{2}-\frac{889}{726192}z^{3}-\frac{1851985}{2143718784}z^{4}-\frac{110984489}{175784940288}z^{5}-\cdots.

Remark 1.1.4
.

The André–Chunovsky–Katz theorem that the minimal differential equation of a
G
-function has regular singularities and rational exponents, as summarized in
[
And00
, §3]
, implies that every
G
-function of differential order
1
is algebraic, so the counterexamples to
1.1.1
in the above theorem have the smallest possible order. One of the main points of the present work is to provide examples of minimal order, which may moreover be explicitly computed as above.

1.2.
A sketch of proof

Our strategy to prove
1.1.3
begins the same way as that of Fresán and Jossen
[
FJ21
]
in the case of
E
-functions, in that we reformulate the problem in terms of Tannakian categories. More precisely, we want to construct two Tannakian categories
\mathbf{H}\subset\mathbf{G}
and reduce to proving that this inclusion is strict.

Following André
[
And89
]
, we define
\mathbf{G}
as the category of
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}(z)\langle\partial_{z}\rangle
-modules arising from algebraic geometry in
3.1.1
. The construction of
\mathbf{H}
is slightly trickier than that from
[
FJ21
]
, which is simply generated by the differential equations of hypergeometric
E
-functions, since we must consider
G
-functions of the form
F(\lambda(z))
where
F
are hypergeometric functions and
\lambda(z)
algebraic functions. Instead, we work with the Tannakian category
\mathbf{H}
in
3.2.1
generated by
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}(z)\langle\partial_{z}\rangle
-modules of the form
\pi_{2*}\pi_{1}^{*}\mathcal{H}
, built from hypergeometric connections with regular singularities
\mathcal{H}
using correspondences

(1.2.1)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{P}^{1}_{\lambda}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{P}^{1}_{z},}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{2}}

where
C
is a smooth projective curve and
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
are finite Galois covers. We deduce from a theorem by Katz
[
Kat90
, §5.4]
that
\mathbf{H}
is a full subcategory of
\mathbf{G}
.

At this point, our strategy for showing that
\mathbf{H}
is not the whole
\mathbf{G}
diverges from that of Fresán and Jossen. Roughly, they prove that the similar inclusion
\mathbf{H}\subset\mathbf{E}
is strict by an ingenious analysis of the geometry of the singularities of the
Fourier transforms
of objects of their category
\mathbf{H}
. Because of the algebraic pullbacks in our definition of
\mathbf{H}
, the singularities tell us almost nothing in the present situation.

Our strategy is then to find some other invariant of objects of
\mathbf{H}
. We do so by analyzing the
trace fields
of objects of
\mathbf{H}
(more precisely, their
adjoint trace fields
, which have better properties; this difference will be suppressed in the introduction). Under the Riemann–Hilbert correspondence, an object of
\mathbf{H}
corresponds to a local system on
\mathbb{P}^{1}\setminus S
for some finite subset
S\subset\mathbb{P}^{1}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
, and we may look at the field generated by traces of elements of
\pi_{1}(\mathbb{P}^{1}\setminus S)
acting on the associated representation.

We first observe that the work of Katz on rigid local systems easily implies that the trace field of a hypergeometric connection
\mathcal{H}
is abelian (
2.3.2
). Naively, one may think (as we did initially) that it is enough to find a rank-two connection
\mathcal{V}
of geometric origin on
\mathbb{P}^{1}\setminus S
whose trace field is not abelian. This does not work, since the objects of
\mathbf{H}
include arbitrary summands of tensor products of hypergeometric local systems, and it is not clear at all that the property of having abelian trace field is invariant under taking summands.

We get around this by applying a Lie algebra version of Goursat’s lemma due to Fresán and Jossen, and deduce that if a simple object
\mathcal{V}
of
\mathbf{G}
belongs to
\mathbf{H}
, then it is Lie-generated by a single hypergeometric connection
\mathcal{H}
, under a correspondence as in (
1.2.1
). We then apply the computation by Beukers and Heckman (quoted as
2.4.1
below) of the differential Galois groups of all regular-singular hypergeometric connections; this allows us to deduce that, if
\mathcal{V}
is of rank two with the maximal possible differential Galois group, then the same must be true of
\mathcal{H}
.

From here, we can apply the trace field argument as outlined above using certain techniques related to the adjoint trace fields of rank-two local systems and Fuchsian groups (
2.2.5
). Therefore, to conclude the proof of
1.1.3
, it suffices to find such a
\mathcal{V}
with non-abelian trace field. Indeed, one can find many examples coming from rational Shimura curves.

1.3.
Previous work

Siegel’s problem for
G
-functions is closely related to a conjecture by Dwork
[
Dwo90
, Conj. 7.4]
, which asked whether every
G
-function of order 2 with infinite monodromy may be written as

\mu_{1}(z)\cdot_{2}F_{1}[a_{1},a_{2};b_{1}\,|\,\lambda(z)]+\mu_{2}(z),

for some algebraic functions
\lambda(z),\mu_{1}(z),\mu_{2}(z)
. A counterexample to Dwork’s conjecture was first found by Krammer
[
Kra96
]
by considering a Shimura curve and crucially using Takeuchi’s classification
[
Tak77
, Table 1]
of arithmetic triangle groups. Several more counterexamples were then given by Bouw and Möller
[
BM10
]
, coming from rational Teichmüller curves. Our trace field criterion provides yet more counterexamples to Dwork’s conjecture. With our techniques, we can also prove that the examples of Krammer and Bouw and Möller negatively answer
1.1.1
.

As already pointed out by Krammer
[
Kra96
, §12]
in 1996, his work provides only finitely many counterexamples, up to reparametrizations of
\mathbb{P}^{1}
, to Dwork’s conjecture; the same is true of that of Bouw and Möller. In
Section

5
, we show that there are infinitely many “non-hypergeometric”
G
-functions (even up to reparametrizations of
\mathbb{P}^{1}
), thereby answering the question raised by Krammer. These are provided by local systems studied by Lam and Litt
[
LL23
]
, and applying the André–Pink–Zannier conjecture in certain cases that were proven by Richard and Yafaev
[
RY21
]
. In a recent work
[
vHvSZ23
]
, van Hoeij, van Straten, and Zudilin computed explicitly the
G
-function corresponding to one of these local systems, and also suggested that these give new counterexamples to Dwork’s conjecture.

As mentioned above, Fischler and Rivoal
[
FR22
]
provided a negative answer to a weaker version of
1.1.1
, assuming the Grothendieck period conjecture. While the present paper was in preparation, Dreyfus and Rivoal raised a generalization of
1.1.1
in
[
DR25
, Question 3]
, and gave negative answers to weaker versions of both the original and the generalized question. More precisely, for each integer
n
they construct a
G
-function that is not a polynomial expression with coefficients in
\overline{\mathbb{C}(x)}
of solutions of differential operators with
n
singularities, and from this they deduce counterexamples under the additional assumption that the
\lambda
’s are rational functions with denominators of uniformly bounded degree.

1.4.
Organization

This paper is organized as follows. In
Section

2
, we collect some basic properties of
G
-functions, trace fields, and hypergeometric connections. In
Section

3
, we construct the Tannakian categories
\mathbf{H}\subset\mathbf{G}
and reduce
1.1.1
to demonstrating that this inclusion is strict. Using the trace field argument,
1.1.3
is proved in
Section

4
, where we provide counterexamples derived from Shimura and Teichmüller curves. Finally, in
Section

5
, we construct infinitely many non-hypergeometric
G
-functions of order
2
using the generalized André–Pink–Zannier conjecture for Shimura varieties of abelian type from
[
RY21
]
. Given that this paper relies on results and techniques from various areas, we tried to include enough background material to make it as self-contained as possible.

2.
Preliminaries on hypergeometric connections

After briefly recalling the definitions of
G
-functions and trace fields, we gather in Proposition
2.3.2
and Theorem
2.4.1
the properties of hypergeometric connections and their associated local systems on which the proof of the main theorem relies.

2.1.
G
-functions and
G
-operators

Definition 2.1.1
.

A
G
-function
is a formal power series with algebraic coefficients

G(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n}\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[\![z]\!]

that is annihilated by a non-zero differential operator
L\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[z]\langle\partial_{z}\rangle
and satisfies the following growth conditions: for each
n\geq 1
, writing
d_{n}\geq 1
for the smallest integer such that
d_{n}a_{1},\dots,d_{n}a_{n}
are algebraic integers, there exists a real number
C>0
such that
|\sigma(a_{n})|\leq C^{n}
and
d_{n}\leq C^{n}
hold for all
\sigma\in{\rm Gal}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}/\mathbb{Q})
and all
n\geq 1
.

The set of
G
-functions is closed under addition and multiplication; this is more or less straightforward from the definition and was already observed in
[
Sie29
]
. It is also closed under the Hadamard product

(F\star G)(z):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}b_{n}z^{n}

for
F(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}z^{n}
and
G(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{n}z^{n}
, as proved in
[
And89
, Thm. D]
.

Definition 2.1.2
.

Let
\mathbb{C}\{\mskip-5.0mu\{z\}\mskip-5.0mu\}
denote the subring of
\mathbb{C}[\![z]\!]
consisting of power series with positive radius of convergence. Write
\mathcal{M}
for the differential algebra of
convergent Laurent series with monodromy

[
FJ21
, §1.1]
, defined as

\mathcal{M}:=\mathbb{C}\{\mskip-5.0mu\{z\}\mskip-5.0mu\}[(z^{a})_{a\in\mathbb{C}},\log(z)].

We denote by
\mathcal{G}
the differential subalgebra of
\mathcal{M}
consisting of complex linear combinations of elements of the form

z^{a_{i}}\log(z)^{b_{i}}F_{i}(z),

for rational numbers
a_{i}
, integers
b_{i}\geq 0
, and
G
-functions
F_{i}
.

Definition 2.1.3
.

A differential operator
L\in\mathbb{Q}(z)\langle\partial_{z}\rangle
is called a
G
-operator
if
L
admits a basis of solutions in the algebra
\mathcal{G}
.

See
[
FJ21
, §1.7]
for the equivalence of this definition with the more Diophantine one in terms of Galochkin’s condition which is usually found in the literature.

2.2.
Trace fields and adjoint trace fields

Definition 2.2.1
.

Let
\Gamma
be a finitely generated group and
\rho\colon\Gamma\rightarrow{\rm GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
a complex representation of
\Gamma
.

(1)

For any automorphism
\sigma\in{\rm Aut}(\mathbb{C})
, let
\sigma(\rho)
denote the composition

\Gamma\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rho}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\rm GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sigma}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\rm GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C}).

The
field of definition
of
\rho
is
k:=\mathbb{C}^{G}
, where
G\subset{\rm Aut}(\mathbb{C})
denotes the subgroup defined as
G:=\{\sigma\in{\rm Aut}(\mathbb{C})\,|\,\sigma(\rho)\simeq\rho\}
.

(2)

The
trace field
of
\rho
is the subfield of
\mathbb{C}
generated by the traces of
\rho(\gamma)
,
i.e.

\mathbb{Q}\big(\{{\rm tr}\,\rho(\gamma)\,|\,\gamma\in\Gamma\}\big)\subset\mathbb{C}.

We emphasize that, in our setup, the trace field is naturally a subfield of
\mathbb{C}
; in what follows, when we say that two trace fields are equal we mean that they are the same subfield of
\mathbb{C}
, and not just abstractly isomorphic.

(3)

The
adjoint trace field
of
\rho
is the trace field of the adjoint representation

{\rm ad}(\rho)\colon\Gamma\rightarrow{\rm GL}(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}).

Note that as a representation of
\Gamma
,
{\rm ad}(\rho)
decomposes as
\mathrm{triv}\oplus{\rm ad}^{0}(\rho)
, where
\rm{triv}
denotes the trivial representation and
{\rm ad}^{0}(\rho)
the representation of
\Gamma
on
\mathfrak{sl}_{n}\subset\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
, the subspace of traceless matrices. Then the adjoint trace field of
\rho
is also equal to the trace field of
{\rm ad}^{0}(\rho)
.

Remark 2.2.2
.

The adjoint trace field is contained in the trace field. Indeed, let
\gamma\in\Gamma
and suppose that
\rho(\gamma)
has generalized eigenvalues
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
. Then the endomorphism
{\rm ad}(\rho)(\gamma)
has trace

\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq n}\alpha_{i}\alpha^{-1}_{j}=(\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{n})(\alpha_{1}^{-1}+\cdots+\alpha_{n}^{-1})={\rm tr}\,\rho(\gamma)\cdot{\rm tr}\,\rho(\gamma^{-1}).

Remark 2.2.3
.

We highlight that, for
\Gamma
and
\rho
as above, there is another natural field which is the minimal field
\mathbb{K}\subset\mathbb{C}
such that
\rho
has a model over
\mathbb{K}
. This in general strictly contains the trace field of
\rho
—there is a Brauer obstruction class. There seems to be some discrepancies in the literature about the naming of these fields: we refer the reader to the discussion in
[
Cal24
]
. In any case, we will not use the auxiliary field
\mathbb{K}
in this paper, and hope that no confusions will be caused.

Lemma 2.2.4
.

The field of definition of a semisimple representation
\rho
coincides with its trace field.

Proof.

Let
K
and
F
be the trace field and field of definition of
\rho
, respectively. We keep the notation
G\subset{\rm Aut}(\mathbb{C})
from
2.2.1
. Since
\sigma(\rho)\simeq\rho
holds for
\sigma\in G
, we deduce
{\rm tr}\,\rho(\gamma)={\rm tr}\,\sigma(\rho)(\gamma)=\sigma({\rm tr}\,\rho(\gamma))
, and hence the inclusion
K\subset F
.

On the other hand, recall from
[
Bou12
, §20 6, VIII.376, Cor. (a)]
that two semisimple representations over a field of characteristic zero are isomorphic if and only if they have the same characters. This implies that
\tau(\rho)
is isomorphic to
\rho
for all
\tau\in{\rm Aut}(\mathbb{C}/K)
, and hence
{\rm Aut}(\mathbb{C}/K)\subset G
. Therefore,
K=F
, as required.
∎

Lemma 2.2.5

(Maclachan–Reid)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a finitely generated subgroup of
{\rm SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
, and
\rho\colon\Gamma\xhookrightarrow{}{\rm SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
the tautological representation. Suppose
\rho
is Zariski dense and not unitary. Then, the adjoint trace field of
\rho
is an invariant of the commensurability class of
\Gamma
, i.e. it is invariant under passing to finite index subgroups.

Proof.

This is essentially
[
MR03
, Lem. 3.5.6]
, as we now explicate. Our group
\Gamma
is non-elementary
[
MR03
, Def. 1.2.1]
,
i.e.
its image in
\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
does not have a finite orbit in its action on the extended hyperbolic
3
-space
\mathbb{H}^{3}\cup\hat{\mathbb{C}}
. Indeed, this amounts to saying that no finite index subgroup of
\Gamma
fixes a point in
\mathbb{H}^{3}\cup\hat{\mathbb{C}}
. For the sake of contradiction, suppose that a finite index subgroup
\Gamma^{\prime}\subset\Gamma
fixes a point
x\in\mathbb{H}^{3}\cup\hat{\mathbb{C}}
. Recall that, for the action of
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
on
\mathbb{H}^{3}
and
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
, the stabilizer of
x\in\mathbb{H}^{3}
is
\mathrm{SU}(2)
, and the stabilizer of
x\in\hat{\mathbb{C}}
is a Borel subgroup. The first case implies that
\rho
is unitary, and the second implies that
\Gamma
is not Zariski dense. As both of these contradict the assumptions,
\Gamma
is non-elementary.

It then follows from
[
MR03
, Thm. 3.3.4, Lem. 3.5.6]
that
\mathbb{Q}(\{{\rm tr}(\gamma^{2})\,|\,\gamma\in\Gamma\})
is an invariant of the commensurability class, which is called the
invariant trace field
in
[
MR03
, Def. 3.3.6]
. But for a subgroup of
{\rm SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
, the adjoint trace field of
\rho
is precisely this invariant trace field because of the relation
{\rm tr}(\gamma^{2})=-2+{\rm tr}(\mathrm{Ad}(\gamma))
.
∎

2.3.
Hypergeometric connections

We briefly review some facts and conventions regarding hypergeometric connections. Let
n\geq 1
be an integer and
a,b\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}
. The
hypergeometric differential operator
with parameters
a
and
b
is

\mathrm{Hyp}(a;b):=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(z\partial_{z}+b_{i}-1)-z\prod_{j=1}^{n}(z\partial_{z}+a_{j}).

The hypergeometric functions
{}_{p+1}F_{p}[a_{1},\dots,a_{p+1};b_{1},\dots,b_{p}\,|\,z]
from (
1.0.1
) in the introduction are annihilated by the operators
\mathrm{Hyp}(a_{1},\dots,a_{p+1};b_{1},\dots,b_{p},1)
. Thanks to the closure under Hadamard products, to show that hypergeometric functions are
G
-functions, it suffices to show that series of the form

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(a)_{n}}{(b)_{n}}z^{n}

are
G
-functions for
a\in\mathbb{Q}
and
b\in\mathbb{Q}\setminus\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}
. Following Siegel
[
Sie29
, Satz 5]
, this reduces to showing that the least common multiple of
1,2,\dots,n
is bounded by
C^{n}
for some constant
C
, which is a weak form of the prime number theorem.

Associated with the hypergeometric differential operator
\mathrm{Hyp}(a;b)
there is a connection
\mathcal{H}(a;b)
of rank
n
on
\mathbb{P}^{1}\backslash\{0,1,\infty\}
, called a
hypergeometric connection
. It has regular singularities at
0,1,\infty
, and is irreducible if and only if
a_{i}-b_{j}\not\in\mathbb{Z}
for all indices
i
and
j
by
[
Kat90
, Cor. 3.2.1]
; by the Riemann–Hilbert correspondence, there is an associated local system on
\mathbb{P}^{1}\setminus\{0,1,\infty\}
, which we denote by
\mathbb{H}(a;b)
.

Assume now that
n=p+1
for
p\geq 0
and
b_{p+1}=1
. We set
\alpha_{i}=\exp(2\pi\sqrt{-1}a_{i})
and
\beta_{i}=\exp(2\pi\sqrt{-1}b_{i})
, draw based loops around
0,1,\infty
as in
[
BH89
, p. 328]
, and denote by
g_{0},g_{1},g_{\infty}
the monodromy matrices of
\mathbb{H}(a;b)
along these loops. (Notice that the roles of
\alpha_{i},\beta_{j}
and
a_{i},b_{j}
are interchanged compared to the convention in
[
BH89
]
.)

Proposition 2.3.1

(
[
BH89
, Prop. 3.2, Thm. 3.5]
)
.

The elements
g_{0},g_{1},g_{\infty}
satisfy

\displaystyle det(t-g_{\infty})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(t-\alpha_{i}),

\displaystyle det(t-g_{0}^{-1})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(t-\beta_{i}),

\displaystyle\mathrm{rk}(g_{1}-\mathrm{Id})

\displaystyle=1,\ \det(g_{1})=\exp(2\pi\sqrt{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(b_{i}-a_{i})).

Moreover, for each generalized eigenvalue of
g_{\infty}
, there is a unique Jordan block with this generalized eigenvalue, and the same is true of
g_{0}
.

We prove a result on the adjoint trace fields of rank-two hypergeometric local systems for later use. We write
\mathbb{H}(a_{1},a_{2};b_{1},1)
for the rank-two local system
on
\mathbb{P}^{1}\setminus\{0,1,\infty\}
corresponding to the connection
\mathcal{H}(a_{1},a_{2};b_{1},1)
.

Proposition 2.3.2
.

The adjoint trace field
F
of an irreducible hypergeometric connection
\mathbb{H}(a_{1},a_{2};b_{1},1)
satisfies:

(1)

F
is contained in a cyclotomic field;

(2)

F
cannot be
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
for any odd squarefree integer
D\geq 7
.

Proof.

The first part follows from Katz’s theorem on rigid local systems, as we now explain. By
2.2.4
, the trace field coincides with the field of definition. In view of
2.2.2
, it then suffices to prove that the field of definition is contained in a cyclotomic field. For brevity, let us write
\mathbb{H}
for
\mathbb{H}(a_{1},a_{2};b_{1},1)
. Since the
a_{i}
and
b_{j}
are rational numbers, the eigenvalues of the local monodromies of
\mathbb{H}
around
0,1,\infty
are roots of unity, and hence there exists a torsion rank-one local system
\chi
such that
\mathbb{H}^{\prime}:=\mathbb{H}\otimes\chi
has trivial determinant. Since
\mathbb{H}
has traces in a cyclotomic field if and only if
\mathbb{H}^{\prime}
does, we are reduced to proving the statement for
\mathbb{H}^{\prime}
. Let
F\subset\mathbb{C}
be the field generated by the eigenvalues of the local monodromies of
\mathbb{H}^{\prime}
around
0,1,\infty
, which is evidently contained in a cyclotomic field. We claim that the field of definition of
\mathbb{H}^{\prime}
is contained in (and in fact equal to)
F
. Indeed, it suffices to show that
{\rm Aut}(\mathbb{C}/F)
is contained in

G^{\prime}:=\{\sigma\in{\rm Aut}(\mathbb{C})\,|\,\sigma(\mathbb{H}^{\prime})\simeq\mathbb{H}^{\prime}\}.

The key point is that
\mathbb{H}^{\prime}
is
physically rigid

[
Kat90
, Thm. 3.5.4]
,
i.e.
it is determined by its local monodromies. From this, we may now conclude: any
\sigma\in{\rm Aut}(\mathbb{C}/F)
fixes the conjugacy classes of local monodromies of
\mathbb{H}^{\prime}
, and hence
\mathbb{H}^{\prime}
itself.

We now prove the second part. Suppose that the adjoint trace field of
\mathbb{H}
is
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
for some odd squarefree integer
D\geq 7
. Write
\lambda=\alpha_{1}/\alpha_{2}
,
\mu=\beta_{1}
,
\nu=\beta_{1}/(\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2})
, which are roots of unity of orders
\ell,m,r
, respectively. Then the traces of
g_{\infty},g_{0},g_{1}
on the adjoint representation are given by

2+\lambda+\lambda^{-1},\ 2+\mu+\mu^{-1},\ 2+\nu+\nu^{-1},

respectively. Now
\lambda+\lambda^{-1}
has degree
\phi(\ell)/2
over
\mathbb{Q}
, and similarly for
\mu+\mu^{-1}
and
\nu+\nu^{-1}
. The assumption
D\geq 7
implies that
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
is different from
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta+\zeta^{-1})
for
any
root of unity
\zeta
, so
\lambda+\lambda^{-1},\mu+\mu^{-1},\nu+\nu^{-1}
must all be rational. In other words, each of the integers
\ell,m,r
is one of
1,2,3,4,6
.

As above, we twist
\mathbb{H}
by a character to obtain a local system
\mathbb{H}^{\prime}
with trivial determinant. Doing so, the local monodromies at
\infty,0,1
have eigenvalues

(2.3.1)

\{\alpha_{1}\beta_{1}^{1/2}\nu^{1/2},\alpha_{2}\beta_{1}^{-1/2}\nu^{1/2}\},\{\beta_{1}^{1/2},\beta_{1}^{-1/2}\},\{\nu^{1/2},\nu^{-1/2}\}

respectively, for some choices of square roots
\beta_{1}^{1/2},\nu^{1/2}
. Note that all the roots of unity appearing in (
2.3.1
) have orders dividing
24
. Since twisting by a character has no effect on the adjoint trace field, we must have that
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
is contained in the trace field of
\mathbb{H}^{\prime}
; by the above computation, we deduce that
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
is contained in

\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{24}+\zeta_{24}^{-1}),

where
\zeta_{24}
is a primitive
24
-th root of unity. Note that
[\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{24}+\zeta_{24}^{-1}):\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})]=2
, and by transitivity of the discriminant, we have

\mathrm{Disc}(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})/\mathbb{Q})^{2}\mid\mathrm{Disc}(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{24}+\zeta_{24}^{-1})/\mathbb{Q}).

Finally, one can check that the discriminant of
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{24}+\zeta_{24}^{-1})
is
2^{8}\cdot 3^{2}
. Since the discriminant of
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
is
D
when
D\equiv 1\,(\mathrm{mod}\,4)
or
4D
when
D\equiv 2,3\,(\mathrm{mod}\,4)
, we conclude that
D\leq 6
, which is a contradiction. Therefore, the adjoint trace field of
\mathbb{H}
cannot be such a
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
, as claimed.
∎

2.4.
Differential Galois groups

This subsection reviews some results related to the differential Galois groups of hypergeometric connections
\mathcal{H}(a;b)
, which are the Zariski closures of the monodromy groups of the corresponding local system
\mathbb{H}(a;b)
.

We call a hypergeometric connection
\mathcal{H}(a;b)

Kummer induced
(resp.
Belyi induced
,
inverse Belyi induced
) if it is the direct image of a hypergeometric connection along a Kummer covering (resp. a Belyi covering of type
(a,b)
, or an inverse Belyi covering of type
(a,b)
, for a pair of positive integers
(a,b)
) of
\mathbb{G}_{m}\backslash\{1\}
as in
[
Kat90
, (3.5)]
). We will sometimes simply say
induced
to include all these possibilities.

Theorem 2.4.1

(
[
BH89
, Thm. 6.5]
, also
[
Kat90
, Thm. 3.5.8]
)
.

Assume that the hypergeometric connection
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n};b_{1},\dots,b_{n-1},1)
is irreducible (i.e.
a_{i}-b_{j}\notin\mathbb{Z}
for all
i,j
) and is neither Kummer induced nor Belyi induced nor inverse Belyi induced. Let
G
be the differential Galois group of
\mathcal{H}
. Write
\gamma=\sum b_{j}-\sum a_{i}
.

(1)

The group
G
is reductive,
and
G^{\circ}=G^{\circ,\mathrm{der}}
.

(2)

The group
G^{\circ,\mathrm{der}}
is either the trivial group,
\mathrm{SL}_{n}
,
\mathrm{SO}_{n}
, or (if
n
is even)
\mathrm{Sp}_{n}
.

(3)

If
\exp(2\pi\sqrt{-1}\gamma)\neq\pm 1
, then
G^{\circ,\mathrm{der}}=\{1\}
or
\mathrm{SL}_{n}
.

(4)

If
\exp(2\pi\sqrt{-1}\gamma)=-1
, then
G^{\circ,\mathrm{der}}=\{1\}
or
\mathrm{SL}_{n}
or
\mathrm{SO}_{n}
.

(5)

If
\exp(2\pi\sqrt{-1}\gamma)=1
, then
G^{\circ,\mathrm{der}}=\mathrm{SL}_{n}
or (for
n
even)
\mathrm{Sp}_{n}
.

3.
Tannakian reformulation of Siegel’s problem

In this section, we introduce the Tannakian categories
\mathbf{H}\subset\mathbf{G}
and argue that the existence of an object of
\mathbf{G}
that does not belong to
\mathbf{H}
implies a negative answer to Siegel’s problem for
G
-functions (see
3.2.7
).

3.1.
The category
\mathbf{G}

For a Zariski open
U\subset\mathbb{P}^{1}
, let
\mathrm{Conn}_{\mathrm{rs}}(U)
denote the category of integrable connections on
U
with regular singularities along
\mathbb{P}^{1}\setminus U
. Let
\mathrm{Mod}_{\mathrm{rs}}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}(z)\langle\partial_{z}\rangle)
be the category obtained as the inductive limit of
\mathrm{Conn}_{\mathrm{rs}}(U)
, as
U
ranges over all Zariski open subsets of
U\subset\mathbb{P}^{1}
which are defined over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
, under the natural restriction functors.

Definition 3.1.1

(
[
And89
, II, §2.3]
)
.

We define
\mathbf{G}
as the smallest full subcategory of the abelian category
\mathrm{Mod}_{\mathrm{rs}}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}(z)\langle\partial_{z}\rangle)
containing all direct summands of
\mathrm{H}^{i}_{\mathrm{dR}}(X/S)
for
X
a smooth proper scheme over
S={\rm Spec}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}(z))
, as well as iterated extensions of such.

As mentioned in the introduction,
\mathbf{G}
is a subcategory of the category of
G
-operators discussed with the same notation in
[
FJ21
, Def. 2.9]
. In particular, objects of
\mathbf{G}
have a basis of solutions in the differential algebra
\mathcal{G}
from
2.1.2
. These two categories are conjecturally the same; see
[
And89
, Intro.]
.

Proposition 3.1.2
.

The categories
\mathrm{Mod}_{\mathrm{rs}}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}(z)\langle\partial_{z}\rangle)
and
\mathbf{G}
are Tannakian, and the Tannakian group of each object
M
of
\mathrm{Mod}_{\mathrm{rs}}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}(z)\langle\partial_{z}\rangle)
is isomorphic to the differential Galois group of
M
.

Proof.

The category
\mathbf{G}
is a tensor subcategory of
\mathrm{Mod}_{\mathrm{rs}}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}(z)\langle\partial_{z}\rangle)
, so it suffices to show that the latter is Tannakian. To do so, we need to construct a
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
-valued fiber functor
\omega\colon\mathrm{Mod}_{\mathrm{rs}}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}(z)\langle\partial_{z}\rangle)\to\mathrm{Vect}_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}
.

Recall that
\mathrm{Mod}_{\mathrm{rs}}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}(z)\langle\partial_{z}\rangle)
is the inductive limit of the categories
\mathrm{Conn}_{\mathrm{rs}}(U)
of integrable connections with regular singularities on Zariski open subsets
U
of
\mathbb{P}^{1}
defined over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
. On each
\mathrm{Conn}_{\mathrm{rs}}(U)
, the nearby cycle functor at
0
, denoted by
\Psi_{0}
, is a faithfully exact tensor functor,
i.e.
a
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
-valued fiber functor. Then, the nearby cycle functor
\Psi_{0}
induces a
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
-valued fiber functor on
\mathrm{Mod}_{\mathrm{rs}}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}(z)\langle\partial_{z}\rangle)
.

For the final claim, as explained in
[
Kat87
, §1.1]
, all fiber functors over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
are (non-canonically) isomorphic, and the corresponding Tannakian groups are the differential Galois groups.
∎

Remark 3.1.3
.

Any object

in

comes from some connection

on an open

,

Following

4.
Proofs of main results

The goal of this section is to prove
1.1.3
, and to do so it suffices, by
3.2.7
, to construct an object of
\mathbf{G}
which is not in
\mathbf{H}
. To this end, we first derive a key obstruction for a rank-two object of
\mathbf{G}
to belong to
\mathbf{H}
in Lemma
4.1.3
.

4.1.
A consequence of Goursat’s lemma

Let
\mathbf{T}
be a Tannakian category along with some choice of fiber functor
\omega
. For an object
A
of
\mathbf{T}
, we denote by
G_{A}
the Tannakian group of
A
, so that the functor
\omega
induces a tensor equivalence

\langle A\rangle^{\otimes}\cong{\rm Rep}(G_{A}).

The Lie algebra of the algebraic group
G_{A}
will be denoted by
\mathfrak{g}_{A}
; we may also view
\mathfrak{g}_{A}
as the adjoint representation of
G_{A}
, and going through the above equivalence we obtain an object of
\langle A\rangle^{\otimes}
, denoted by
\underline{\mathfrak{g}}_{A}.

Definition 4.1.1
.

For objects
A
and
B
of a Tannakian category
\mathbf{T}
, let
\mathfrak{g}_{B}
and
\mathfrak{g}_{A\oplus B}
denote the Lie algebras of the Galois groups of
B
and
A\oplus B
, respectively. We say that
A
is
Lie-generated
by
B
if the natural morphism of Lie algebras

\mathfrak{g}_{A\oplus B}\longrightarrow\mathfrak{g}_{B}

is an isomorphism. If
A
is Lie-generated by
B
, then there is a surjection
\mathfrak{g}_{B}\twoheadrightarrow\mathfrak{g}_{A}
.

We will make use of the following version of Goursat’s lemma for Lie algebras:

Lemma 4.1.2

(
[
FJ21
, Lem. 4.5]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{T}
be a Tannakian category generated by a family of objects
\mathcal{B}
. Let
A
be an object of
\mathbf{T}
such that
\mathfrak{g}_{A}
is simple and non-commutative. Then
A
is Lie-generated by a single object of the family
\mathcal{B}
.

We now come to the main criterion of our work.

Lemma 4.1.3

(Key criterion)
.

Let
A
be a rank-two object of
\mathbf{G}
with differential Galois group
\mathrm{SL}_{2}
. Suppose that
A
is Lie-generated by an object of the form
\pi_{2*}\pi_{1}^{*}\mathcal{H}
, where
\mathcal{H}
is an irreducible hypergeometric connection, which is not induced, and
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
are maps fitting into a diagram as in
3.2.1
. Then
\mathcal{H}
is of rank two with
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathcal{H}}\cong\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
.

Furthermore, the adjoint trace field of
A
, i.e. the trace field of
{\rm ad}^{0}(A)
,
is isomorphic to the adjoint trace field of
\mathcal{H}
. Here
{\rm ad}^{0}(A)
denotes the connection given by the traceless endomorphisms of
A
.

Proof.

By assumption,
A
is Lie-generated by
\pi_{2*}\pi_{1}^{*}\mathcal{H}
, and hence
\pi_{2}^{*}A
is Lie-generated by
\pi_{2}^{*}(\pi_{2*}\pi_{1}^{*}\mathcal{H})
, as objects of
\mathbf{G}
. Since
\pi_{2}
is a finite Galois cover, by Mackey’s induction-restriction formula we have

\pi_{2}^{*}(\pi_{2*}\pi_{1}^{*}\mathcal{H})=\bigoplus_{\sigma}(\pi_{1}^{*}\mathcal{H})^{\sigma},

where the sum is over automorphisms
\sigma\colon C\rightarrow C
covering
\pi_{2}
, and the superscript
σ
denotes the pullback under
\sigma
.

For brevity, let us write
\mathcal{V}\vcentcolon=\pi_{1}^{*}\mathcal{H}
. Then
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathcal{V}^{\sigma}}\cong\mathfrak{g}_{\mathcal{V}}\cong\mathfrak{g}_{\mathcal{H}}
, where the last isomorphism follows from
[
Kat87
, Prop. 1.4.4]
. On the other hand,
\mathfrak{g}_{A}\cong\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
is a quotient of
\bigoplus_{\sigma}\mathfrak{g}_{\mathcal{V}^{\sigma}}
. Since
\mathcal{H}
is irreducible,
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathcal{H}}
is a semisimple Lie algebra by
2.4.1
; as
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathcal{H}}
surjects to
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
, by inspection of the groups appearing in
2.4.1
we deduce that
\mathcal{H}
is of rank two with
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathcal{H}}\cong\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
.

It remains to prove the statement about trace fields, and for this, we may pass to a non-empty open subset
U\subset\mathbb{P}^{1}
such that
A
is smooth on
U
, and
\bigoplus_{\sigma}\mathcal{V}^{\sigma}
is smooth on
\pi_{2}^{-1}(U)\subset C
. Let
F_{\mathrm{ad}^{0}(\mathcal{H})}
be the adjoint trace field of
\mathcal{H}
, and similarly for
\pi_{1}^{*}\mathcal{H}
. So for any
\sigma
, the trace field of
\underline{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathcal{V}^{\sigma}}
is the same as
F_{\mathrm{ad}^{0}(\pi_{1}^{*}\mathcal{H})}
, which is a priori a subfield of
F_{\mathrm{ad}^{0}(\mathcal{H})}
. On the other hand, there is a surjective morphism

f\colon\bigoplus_{\sigma}\underline{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathcal{V}^{\sigma}}\longrightarrow\underline{\mathfrak{g}}_{\pi_{2}^{*}A},

where
\underline{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathcal{V}^{\sigma}}
and
\underline{\mathfrak{g}}_{\pi_{2}^{*}A}
are regarded as objects of the Tannakian subcategories
\langle\mathcal{V}^{\sigma}\rangle^{\otimes}
and
\langle\pi_{2}^{*}A\rangle^{\otimes}
of
\mathbf{G}
. Each
\underline{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathcal{V}^{\sigma}}
is simple and
f
is surjective, so
\underline{\mathfrak{g}}_{\pi_{2}^{*}A}
is isomorphic to a direct sum of some of the
\underline{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathcal{V}^{\sigma}}
. From this we deduce an isomorphism
\underline{\mathfrak{g}}_{\pi_{2}^{*}A}\cong\underline{\mathfrak{g}}_{\mathcal{V}^{\sigma}}
for one automorphism
\sigma
since both objects have the same rank. Therefore, the trace field of
\underline{\mathfrak{g}}_{\pi_{2}^{*}A}
is isomorphic to that of
F_{\mathrm{ad}^{0}(\pi_{1}^{*}\mathcal{H})}
.

Finally,
2.2.5
implies that the adjoint trace fields of
A
and
\mathcal{H}
are both invariant under passing to finite index subgroups, as we now explain. It suffices to show this for the Galois conjugates
\sigma(A)
and
\sigma(\mathcal{H})
for some choice of
\sigma\in{\rm Aut}(\mathbb{C})
; here, by Galois conjugates we mean the connections corresponding to
\sigma
applied to the local systems for
A
and
\mathcal{H}
, respectively. Since
A
has infinite monodromy, it has a Galois conjugate which is not unitary, and for ease of notation we assume this is
A
itself; then
\mathcal{H}
must also be non-unitary, as it Lie-generates
A
. Now both
A
and
\mathcal{H}
satisfy the assumptions of
2.2.5
, and so their adjoint trace fields are commensurability invariants.

In conclusion, we now have

F_{\mathrm{ad}^{0}(A)}=F_{\underline{\mathfrak{g}}_{A}}=F_{\underline{\mathfrak{g}}_{\pi_{2}^{*}A}}\simeq F_{\mathrm{ad}^{0}(\pi_{1}^{*}\mathcal{H})}=F_{\mathrm{ad}^{0}(\mathcal{H})},

as required.
∎

It remains to construct explicitly a rank-two object
A
of
\mathbf{G}
as in
4.1.3
that violates its conclusion,
i.e.
whose adjoint trace field cannot be isomorphic to that of a rank-two hypergeometric connection.

4.2.
Recollections on Shimura curves

We first collect some preliminaries regarding Shimura curves. We will try to take a down-to-earth approach here as that is all that is necessary for our applications. For more details on quaternion algebras, Shimura curves, and other things, we refer the reader to the excellent and comprehensive book of Voight’s
[
Voi21
, §38]
.

Let
F
be a totally real number field and
B
a quaternion algebra over
F
which is split at a unique infinite place
\sigma\colon F\xhookrightarrow{}\mathbb{R}
. There is, therefore, an isomorphism

B\otimes_{F,\sigma}\mathbb{R}\cong M_{2}(\mathbb{R}).

There is a unique involution
\bar{~}\colon B\rightarrow B
, known as the standard involution (see
[
Voi21
, §3.2]
), so that for any
\gamma\in B
, the
reduced norm

{\rm nrd}{\gamma}:=\gamma\bar{\gamma}
lies in
F
. The extension of scalars of
{\rm nrd}
to
B\otimes_{F,\sigma}\mathbb{R}=M_{2}(\mathbb{R})
is simply the determinant. For any order
\mathcal{O}\subset B
, let
\mathcal{O}_{1}\subset\mathcal{O}
denote the elements of reduced norm 1; there are maps

\mathcal{O}_{1}^{\times}\longrightarrow(B\otimes_{F,\sigma}\mathbb{R})^{\times}_{{\rm nrd}=1}={\rm SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\longrightarrow{\rm PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R}).

Let
\Gamma\subset{\rm PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
denote the image of this composition, so that the above map factors as
\mathcal{O}_{1}^{\times}\rightarrow\mathcal{O}_{1}^{\times}/\{\pm 1\}\simeq\Gamma\subset{\rm PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

Proposition 4.2.1
.

The quotient
\Gamma\backslash\mathfrak{H}
is in bijection with the complex points of a complex orbicurve
{\rm Sh}_{B,\mathcal{O}}
; moreover, the latter can be defined over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
.

Proof.

For a nice exposition, we refer the reader to
[
Mil03
, p. 36]
.
∎

Definition 4.2.2
.

We refer to
{\rm Sh}_{B,\mathcal{O}}
as the Shimura curve attached to
(B/F,\mathcal{O})
.

Proposition 4.2.3
.

Let
S\subset{\rm Sh}_{B,\mathcal{O}}
be the set of orbifold points, and
{\rm Sh}^{\circ}_{B,\mathcal{O}}
the complement of
S
. Consider the representation

\rho\colon\pi_{1}({\rm Sh}^{\circ}_{B,\mathcal{O}})\rightarrow{\rm PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\rightarrow{\rm GL}_{3}(\mathbb{C}),

with the last map being the adjoint representation of
{\rm PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Then

•

\rho
has trace field equal to
\sigma(F)
;

•

if
{\rm Sh}_{B,\mathcal{O}}^{\circ}
is rational, then
\rho
may be lifted to a representation

(4.2.1)

\tilde{\rho}\colon\pi_{1}({\rm Sh}_{B,\mathcal{O}}^{\circ})\longrightarrow{\rm SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R}).

Moreover, any such lift is of geometric origin.

Proof.

The first part follows from
[
MR03
, Thm. 8.3.9]
and Lemma 3.5.6 of
loc. cit.
.

For the second part, if
{\rm Sh}_{B,\mathcal{O}}^{\circ}
is rational, then its fundamental group is free, so one lifts
\rho
by choosing any lift to
{\rm SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
of the images by
\rho
of the generators of
\pi_{1}({\rm Sh}^{\circ}_{B,\mathcal{O}})
. For the claim that such a
\tilde{\rho}
is of geometric origin, see for example
[
CS08
, Thm. 9.3]
(where the authors treat the more general case of quaternionic Shimura varieties), combined with the fact that any polarized integral variation of Hodge structures of weight one comes from a family of abelian varieties.
∎

Definition 4.2.4
.

In the case when
{\rm Sh}_{B,\mathcal{O}}^{\circ}
is rational, we refer to any of the rank-two local systems (
4.2.1
) as a
uniformizing local system
.

4.3.
Non-hypergeometric
G
-functions from Shimura curves

We are now able to prove the main theorem from the introduction.

Proof of
1.1.3
.

By combining
4.1.2
and
4.1.3
with
2.3.2
, to prove
1.1.3
, it suffices to find a non-unitary local system of geometric origin with differential Galois group
{\rm SL}_{2}
and non-abelian adjoint trace field on a punctured projective line.

Claim 4.3.1
.

Suppose
{\rm Sh}_{B,\mathcal{O}}
is a Shimura curve which

•

is rational,

•

and is associated with a non-split quaternion algebra
B
over a totally real cubic number field
F
which is non-abelian (equivalently, the Galois closure of
F
has Galois group
S_{3}
over
\mathbb{Q}
).

Let
\mathbb{L}
be a uniformizing rank-two local system on
{\rm Sh}_{B,\mathcal{O}}^{\circ}
, as in
4.2.4
. Then the adjoint trace field of
\mathbb{L}
is
\sigma(F)\subset\mathbb{C}
for some embedding
\sigma\colon F\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}.
In particular, the adjoint trace field of
\mathbb{L}
is non-abelian. Moreover,
\mathbb{L}
has
{\rm SL}_{2}
-monodromy.

Proof of
4.3.1
.

The claim about trace fields is contained in
4.2.3
; for the second part, by construction
\mathbb{L}
is Zariski dense in
{\rm SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, as required.
∎

For explicit examples of Shimura curves satisfying the assumptions of
4.3.1
, we refer the reader to
[
Voi09
, Table 4.3]
; for example, we can take the first entry of the second column, where the cubic field
F
has discriminant
148
, and the signature of the Shimura curve is
(0;2^{3},3)
. The latter notation means that the Shimura curve is rational with four orbifold points with stabilizers
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}
, respectively. Note that

is a non-abelian extension since the discriminant

is not a square: indeed, the Galois closure of a cubic number field has Galois group either

or

.
∎

Remark 4.3.2
.

In fact, there is a plentiful supply of counterexamples coming from such Shimura curves. For example, just for Shimura curves attached to cubic fields
F
as above,
[
Voi09
, Table 4.3]
gives 45 examples; amongst these examples, the biggest discriminant of

is 1593.

Remark 4.3.3
.

As mentioned in the introduction, any uniformizing local system on a Shimura curve satisfying the hypotheses of
4.3.1
gives a counterexample to Dwork’s conjecture
[
Dwo90
, p. 784]
. In this way, we provide many new counterexamples to this conjecture.

Using
4.1.3
, we may rule out even more Shimura curve examples by arguing as Krammer does in
[
Kra96
, §11]
:

Proposition 4.3.4
.

Suppose
{\rm Sh}_{B,\mathcal{O}}
is a rational Shimura curve attached to a quaternion algebra
B
over
\mathbb{Q}
, whose discriminant is not
(1)
or
(2)(3)
. Then any of the uniformizing rank-two local systems on
{\rm Sh}_{B,\mathcal{O}}^{\circ}
is not an object of
\mathbf{H}
.

Note that we slightly abuse terminology and say that
“
\mathbb{L}
is not an object of
\mathbf{H}
”
to mean the same for the associated connection under Riemann–Hilbert.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathbb{L}
is a uniformizing rank-two local system on
{\rm Sh}_{B,\mathcal{O}}^{\circ}
, and that moreover, it is an object of
\mathbf{H}
. Note that, by the construction of Shimura curves, the image of the associated representation in
{\rm PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
is a discrete subgroup. By
4.1.3
, we have that
\mathbb{L}
is Lie-generated by
\pi_{2*}\pi_{1}^{*}\mathbb{H}
for some rank-two hypergeometric local system
\mathbb{H}
and covers
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
fitting into a correspondence as in

. This implies that the monodromy group
P
(
i.e.
the image of the associated representation, and not the Zariski closure) of
\mathbb{H}
is commensurable to that of
\mathbb{L}
. Since the hypergeometric monodromy groups that are Fuchsian are precisely the triangle groups, we deduce that
P
is

•

commensurable with a triangle group, and

•

arithmetic.

The arithmetic triangle groups were classified by Takeuchi
[
Tak77
]
, and the only ones associated with quaternion algebras over
\mathbb{Q}
are the ones with discriminants
(1)
and
(2)(3)
, which contradicts our assumption. Therefore,
\mathbb{L}
is not in
\mathbf{H}
.
∎

.

. In this case,

4.4.
Examples from Teichmüller curves

In this section, we give yet more examples of non-hypergeometric
G
-functions, this time coming from Teichmüller curves. Recall that a
Teichmüller curve
is a smooth complex algebraic curve
X
along with a morphism
X\rightarrow\mathcal{M}_{g}
for some
g\geq 2
such that
X
is geodesic for the Teichmüller metric on
\mathcal{M}_{g}
. These are very special curves in
\mathcal{M}_{g}
, and we refer the reader to
[
Möl06
, §1.3]
for other characterizations as well as detailed properties of such curves. Let
\pi\colon\mathscr{C}\rightarrow X
be the pullback of the universal family of curves over
\mathcal{M}_{g}
. The uniformization of
X
gives a representation
\pi_{1}(X)\rightarrow{\rm PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, which we refer to as the uniformizing representation.

Proposition 4.4.1

(
[
Möl06
, Prop. 2.4]
)
.

The local system
R^{1}\pi_{*}\mathbb{C}
has a rank-two local system
\mathbb{L}
, with trivial determinant, as a direct summand which is uniformizing in the following sense: if we write
\rho_{\mathbb{L}}\colon\pi_{1}(X)\rightarrow{\rm SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
for the representation corresponding to
\mathbb{L}
, then the composition

(4.4.1)

\pi_{1}(X)\rightarrow{\rm SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})\rightarrow{\rm PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})

is conjugate to the uniformizing representation of
X
.

We refer to such an
\mathbb{L}
as a tautological local system on
X
.

Proposition 4.4.2
.

Suppose
X
is a rational Teichmüller curve, and
\mathbb{L}
is one of its tautological local systems. If the adjoint trace field of
\mathbb{L}
is
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
for some squarefree integer
D\geq 7
, then
\mathbb{L}
does not correspond to an object of
\mathbf{H}
under the Riemann–Hilbert correspondence.

Proof.

As the tautological local system
\mathbb{L}
corresponds to the uniformizing representation (under the composition (
4.4.1
)), it in particular has Zariski-dense monodromy. Let
\mathcal{L}
denote the connection associated with
\mathbb{L}
, and assume that
\mathcal{L}
is an object of
\mathbf{H}
. Then
4.1.3
implies that
\mathcal{L}
is Lie-generated by an object of the form
\pi_{2*}\pi_{1}^{*}\mathcal{H}
(with notation as in the statement of the lemma), where
\mathcal{H}
is a rank-two hypergeometric connection with the same trace field as that of
\mathbb{L}
, namely
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
for some
D\geq 7
. On the other hand,
2.3.2
says that no such
\mathcal{H}
exists, and hence
\mathcal{L}
cannot be an object of
\mathbf{H}
, as required.
∎

Remark 4.4.3
.

The set of Teichmüller curves to which
4.4.2
applies is non-empty: examples were constructed by McMullen in
[
McM03
, Thm. 9.8]
. The required claim about the trace fields of these Teichmüller curves follows from the proof of Cor. 7.3 of
loc. cit.
. We refer the reader to
[
BM10
, §1]
for a summary of the properties of these Teichmüller curves.

5.
Infinitely many non-hypergeometric
G
-functions of order two

In this final section, we give infinitely many examples of non-hypergeometric
G
-functions of differential order
2
, thereby answering a question raised by Krammer in the context of his counterexample to Dwork’s conjecture; see
[
Kra96
, §12]
. We start with a very rough sketch of the argument to help orient the reader.

Let
\mathcal{M}_{0,4}
denote the moduli space of curves of genus
0
with
4
marked points, and
\pi\colon\mathcal{C}_{0,4}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}_{0,4}
the universal
4
-punctured genus
0
curve. The idea is to consider a local system
\mathbb{V}
on
\mathcal{C}_{0,4}
arising from a family of abelian varieties, which was constructed in a previous work of Lam and Litt, and to show that its restrictions to most fibers of
\pi\colon\mathcal{C}_{0,4}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}_{0,4}
do not come from algebraic pullbacks of hypergeometric local systems. Indeed, if the contrary holds, then our trace field argument implies that the restrictions of

5.2.
The André–Pink–Zannier conjecture

We will next show that the existence of only finitely many non-hypergeometric
G
-functions of order
2
would contradict the André–Pink–Zannier conjecture in the cases proven by Richard and Yafaev.

Theorem 5.2.1

(
[
RY21
, Thm. 1.3]
)
.

Let
s_{0}
be a point of a Shimura variety of abelian type
{\rm Sh}_{K}(G,X)
. Let
Z\subset{\rm Sh}_{K}(G,X)
be a subvariety whose intersection with the generalized Hecke orbit of
s_{0}
is Zariski dense. Then,
Z
is a finite union of weakly special subvarieties.

Let us briefly introduce the objects in the statement, referring the reader to
[
RY24
, §2]
for more details on generalized Hecke orbits. Recall that the Shimura variety
S={\rm Sh}_{K}(G,X)
is the double quotient
G(\mathbb{Q})\backslash(X\times G(\mathbb{A}_{f}))/\penalty K
, where
(G,X)
is a Shimura datum and
K
a compact open subgroup of
G(\mathbb{A}_{f})
. A point of
X
is given by a morphism of real algebraic groups
\mathbb{S}:=\mathrm{Res}_{\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{G}_{m})\to G_{\mathbb{R}}
, and its Mumford–Tate group is the smallest
\mathbb{Q}
-algebraic subgroup of
G
containing its image. We normalize
(G,X)
in such a way that

is the generic Mumford–Tate group of
X
. Each representation of
G
gives rise to a local system on

, and we refer to such a local system as

.

Let

be a point of

and let

be the Mumford-Tate group of
x_{0}
. The

of
s_{0}
is the set of
[\varphi\circ x_{0},g]
with

and

satisfying
\varphi\circ x_{0}\in X
.

Very roughly, if
S
parametrizes abelian varieties with extra Hodge tensors, one may think of two points being in the same generalized Hecke orbit if the corresponding abelian varieties are isogenous, with no extra constraints on the Hodge tensors. Along the same lines, we have the following:

Proposition 5.2.2
.
