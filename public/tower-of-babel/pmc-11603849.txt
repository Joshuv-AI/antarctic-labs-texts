The oncogenic role and prognostic value of PXDN in human stomach adenocarcinoma
Authors: Yuan Tian, Shenghui Qiu, Song Yang, Yuanjian Jiang, Hao Hu, Chengcheng Yang, Jinyong Cao, Shaomin Chen, Mingqing Hao, Hongling Li, Jianlin Zhu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD) is known for its high prevalence and poor prognosis, which underscores the need for novel therapeutic targets. Peroxidasin (PXDN), an enzyme with peroxidase activity, has been linked to cancer development in previous studies. However, its specific role in STAD is not well understood. In our study, we used public databases and clinical specimens to determine that PXDN expression is significantly elevated in STAD tissues and serves as an independent prognostic marker for patient outcomes. Our in vitro assays demonstrated that silencing PXDN significantly reduced STAD cell proliferation, invasion, and migration. Mechanistically, we found that PXDN promotes epithelial‒mesenchymal transition and angiogenesis in STAD cells and may be regulated by the PI3K/AKT pathway. Further analysis revealed that PXDN levels affect the sensitivity of STAD cells to various chemotherapeutic and small molecule drugs. Additionally, we observed a significant association between PXDN levels and the abundances of various immune cell types in patients with STAD. Our study highlighted a strong link between PXDN levels and the tumor immune microenvironment (TIM), suggesting that PXDN is a useful metric for evaluating the response to immune checkpoint inhibitors. Moreover, we found that PXDN is significantly associated with multiple immune checkpoints. In summary, our findings indicate that PXDN plays a critical role in STAD and that its level could serve as a potential prognostic biomarker. Thus, targeting PXDN may represent an effective treatment strategy for STAD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-024-13097-6.

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is a highly aggressive malignancy that poses a significant threat to human health and is the fourth leading cause of cancer-related deaths globally [1]. Stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD) is the predominant pathological type of GC, accounting for more than 95% of cases [2]. Helicobacter pylori infection, precancerous lesions, and genetic factors are widely recognized as high-risk factors for STAD [3]. Currently, surgery and adjuvant therapy remain the primary treatments for STAD [4]. Despite advancements in these treatments, poor prognosis and low 5-year survival rates remain, particularly for recurrent and metastatic STAD [5]. Given this challenge, there is an urgent need to explore additional therapies to improve patient outcomes. Rapid advances in molecular biology, sequencing, and bioinformatics have revealed the importance of genomic networks in cancer development [6]. Although certain gene sets have been identified as potential biomarkers for prognosis, most lack the necessary specificity and sensitivity [7, 8]. Therefore, it is critical to explore precise and reliable biomarkers for the diagnosis and prognostic management of STAD.

Over the past 20 years, immunotherapy based on immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has emerged as a promising research direction for the treatment of anti-solid malignancies, including STAD [9]. Currently, a variety of ICIs have been implemented in the treatment of patients with STAD, yielding surprisingly positive therapeutic effects [10, 11]. However, owing to the heterogeneity of tumors, only a minority of STAD patients benefit from ICI treatment, which significantly limits their broader applicability [12]. Numerous studies have shown that tumors can interact with and remodel the tumor immune microenvironment (TIM), leading to tumor immune evasion and metastasis [13]. The efficacy of immunotherapy closely correlates with immune cell infiltration in the TIM [14, 15]. Therefore, elucidating the specific mechanism by which STAD regulates the TIM and thus identifying immunotherapy targets is considered a promising strategy [14, 15]. Therefore, elucidating the specific mechanism by which STAD regulates the TIM and thus identifying immunotherapy targets is considered to be a promising strategy.

Peroxidasin (PXDN) is an extracellular matrix protein known for its diverse biological functions based on its peroxidase activity [16]. PXDN catalyzes bond formation in collagen to stabilize basement membranes [17]. The absence of PDXN destabilizes membranes, leading to tissue dysplasia [18, 19].Mutations in PXDN may induce certain vision disorders [20]. Recent studies revealed that the peroxidase activity of PXDN may promote tumor development. Zheng et al. demonstrated that PXDN enhances ovarian cancer growth, invasion, and migration via the PI3K/AKT pathway [21]. In oral cancer, PXDN is associated with lymph node metastasis, diffuse invasion, and Warburg-like effects, driving cancer progression [22]. These findings indicate that PXDN acts as an oncogene, promoting tumor growth and progression. However, the specific role and mechanism of PXDN in STAD remain poorly understood. Although bioinformatics analyses have linked high PXDN expression to poor prognosis in patients with STAD, in vivo and in vitro validation remains lacking [22].

Here, we integrated public databases, clinical samples, and in vitro assays to explore the role of PXDN in STAD. Our results demonstrated the potential of role PXDN in driving STAD progression, predicting risk, and forming an immunosuppressive TIM.

Materials and methods

Acquisition and analysis of STAD transcriptomic data

The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database is an open platform containing multi-omics data on various cancer types [23]. We obtained transcriptome data of STAD and its normal gastric tissue from TCGA (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) and processed it using the limma R package. The Wilcoxon test was applied to assessed the PXDN expression between the two groups. Differences were considered statistically significant when p < 0.05 (*p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ).

Cell culture

STAD cell lines AGS, HGC27, MKN74, and MKN45, along with gastric epithelial cells (GES1) and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), were sourced from the tumor cell bank of the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences. All STAD and GES1 cell lines were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). HUVECs were cultured in endothelial cell medium (ECM) supplemented with 5% FBS and endothelial growth factors. Cells were incubated at 37 °C in humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere.

Clinical patients and samples

We collected 90 pairs of STAD and corresponding normal tissue samples from patients who underwent surgical procedures at the First Affiliated Hospital of Jinan University. Each sample was confirmed as STAD through histopathological and clinical diagnosis. We excluded patients who received preoperative neoadjuvant chemotherapy for STAD from this study.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

STAD tissues and their adjacent counterparts were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and then embedded in paraffin. Paraffin-embedded sections were processed through dewaxing, rehydration, antigen retrieval, blocking with 10% goat serum, overnight incubation with anti-PXDN antibody at 4℃, and 30-minutes labeling with a secondary antibody at room temperature. Specimens were stained with DAB for 10 min at room temperature followed by counterstaining. Finally, images were captured for analysis. To estimate the cytoplasmic expression levels of PXDN, we employed the semi-quantitative immunoreactive score (IRS) method. The final estimated result is obtained by multiplying the reaction color intensity (1 - weak, 2 - moderate, 3 - strong) by the percentage of positive tumor cells (0 points for lack of expression, 1 point for > 1–10%, 2 points for > 10–50%, 3 points for > 50–80%, 4 points for > 80%).

siRNA transfection

PXDN-siRNA and NC-siRNA were acquired from Shanghai GenePharma Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China. MKN45 and HGC27 cells were seeded in 6-well plates. The following day, at 60–70% confluence, cells were transfected with 100 nM siRNA using TurboFect Transfection Reagent (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). RNA extraction was performed to assess the PXDN mRNA expression level. After 72 h, protein was extracted to evaluate the level of PXDN.

Colony formation assay

24–48 h post-transfection, cells were harvested and resuspended to create a single-cell suspension. Subsequently, cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 800 cells per well. The plate was gently agitated in a crosswise manner for even cell distribution, followed by incubation at 37 °C in 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere. Once colonies reached about 50 cells each, they were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for another 30 min. Post-staining, colonies were photographed and quantified using ImageJ software.

Analysis of immune cell infiltration in STAD

We utilized various databases to investigate the association between PXDN and immune cell infiltration, namely TISIDB and CIBERSORT (Cell-Type Identification by Estimating Relative Subsets of RNA Transcripts). TISIDB is an integrated online database platform encompassing a variety of functional modules pertinent to tumor immunology [24]. The immune cell infiltration module from TISIDB enables analysis of the interactions between tumor cells and immune cells. CIBERSORT is an advanced algorithm that utilizes the LM22 reference matrix to accurately decode gene expression profiles in transcriptomic data, pinpointing various immune cell types [25].

Transwell assays

The Transwell assay was employed to evaluate cell migration and invasion capabilities. Transwell chambers were seeded with 5 × 104 and 8 × 104 cells, respectively. Matrix gel was applied to the chambers 24 h prior to conducting the invasion assay. For both assays, serum-free base gel was added to the upper chamber, and the lower chamber was filled with a medium containing 20% serum. Following 24–48 h incubation, cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde, stained with crystal violet, and photographed.

Tube formation assay

The STAD cells (1.0 × 106) were cultured in 6-well plates overnight, after which the medium was switched to 2 mL of basal DMEM for each well. After 24 h, the conditioned medium (CM) was collected and centrifuged at 1500 g for 15 min. The harvested CM was then used for the tube formation assay. Subsequently, HUVECs were seeded at a density of 1.0 × 104 cells/well in 96-well plates pre-coated with 50 µL Matrigel matrix and incubated in CM for 4 hours at 37℃ in 5% CO2 atmosphere. Four random images were taken per well using microscopy and analyzed with Image J software.

Functional enrichment analysis

We conducted Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) analyses on PXDN to uncover its roles in biological processes, molecular functions, and pathway enrichment. We utilized the “ggplot2,” “enrichplot,” and “clusterProfiler” packages in R to carry out the GO and KEGG analyses. In addition, we applied GSEA (Gene set enrichment analyses) to determine the function of PXDN in STAD. The gene sets with normalized using an enrichment score of > 1, p < 0.05, which were regarded as significantly enriched gene sets.

Protein extraction and western blot

In this study, total protein was extracted from cells using RIPA (Servicebio, G2002) and quantified via the BCA method (Servicebio, G2026). We loaded 30 µg of the quantified protein onto a 10% SDS-PAGE gel for electrophoretic separation. Subsequently, we employed a wet transfer method to transfer the proteins onto a PVDF membrane (Millipore, WGPVDF45), followed by blocking with a 5% non-fat milk solution diluted in TBST for one hour. According to the molecular weight labeled by Marker(Elascience, E-BC-R273), PVDF was cut and hybridized with the primary antibody, and incubated in a refrigerator at 4℃ overnight to bind the membrane to the corresponding antibody. The following day, we washed off unbound primary antibodies from the membrane using TBST for 15 min. Next, we incubated the PVDF membrane with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for two hours and subsequently washed off excess secondary antibodies with TBST for another 15 min. Finally, protein expression was detected using ECL (Elascience, E-IR-R308) in an imaging system (Tanon Imaging System, 5200 S).

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from the STAD cells and gastric epithelial cells by TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Trizol). Total RNA concentrations were deter-mined using a NanoDrop and the absorbance ratio was measured at 260 nm. 2 µg of RNA were reverse transcribed using the High Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher, 4368814). Then equal amount of cDNA was then amplified with the SYBR Green PCR Amplification Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, 4913850001) on the CFX Connect Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA). GAPDH and β-Actin served as the internal control. The primers used are listed below.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 5.0 was utilized to analyze all the statistical data we obtained. Survival rates were calculated with the Kaplan-Meier method, and log-rank tests determined the significance of differences. Univariate and multivariate analyses were conducted using the Cox proportional hazards model with stepwise regression. Differences between the two groups were assessed using t-tests, Mann–Whitney U or Wilcoxon tests. All experiments were conducted in triplicate, the threshold of statistical significance was set as p < 0.05.

Results

High PXDN expression is associated with poor prognosis in patients with STAD

Given the dysregulation of PXDN in various cancer types, we aimed to determine its role in STAD. First, we examined PXDN expression in pancancer transcriptomic data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). Our analysis revealed significant increases in PXDN in several cancers, including breast, cholangiocarcinoma, colorectal, esophageal, glioblastoma, head and neck, kidney, lung, pheochromocytoma, prostate, and stomach cancers (Fig. 1A). Specifically, increased PXDN expression was noted in gastric cancer tissues than in normal gastric tissues (Fig. 1B), including paired normal samples (Fig. 1C). To validate these findings, we conducted immunohistochemical analysis of PXDN in 90 clinical STAD tissues and their matched normal samples. The analysis revealed a notable increase in PXDN levels in STAD tissues (Fig. 1D) and revealed that higher PXDN expression was correlated with poorer prognosis (Fig. 1E). Clinicopathological data analysis revealed that the PXDN level was correlated with the degree of differentiation and T stage (Table 1). Furthermore, univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses of 90 patients with STAD identified age and PXDN expression level as independent predictors of patient outcome (Table 2). Consistent with these findings, survival analysis of data from public databases revealed worse outcomes for STAD patients with high PXDN expression (Fig. 1F). ROC curve analysis demonstrated the predictive value of PXDN for the prognosis of patients with STAD at 1, 3, and 5 years (AUC = 0.605, 0.610, and 0.705, respectively; Fig. 1G). In summary, our results indicate significant PXDN upregulation in STAD and suggest its potential as a prognostic biomarker for patients.

Downregulation of PXDN reduces STAD cell proliferation, invasion, and migration

To determine the biological roles of PXDN in STAD, we stratified patients into high and low PXDN expression cohorts on the basis of the median expression level and extracted differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed that PXDN is involved in various biological functions related to STAD progression. The enriched molecular function (MF) terms included extracellular matrix components, glycosaminoglycan binding, sulfur compound binding, heparin binding, and cell‒cell adhesion. The enriched biological process (BP) terms included extracellular structure organization, muscle processes, ossification, and cell adhesion. The enriched CC terms linked PXDN to the extracellular matrix, endoplasmic reticulum, contractile fibers, and sarcomeres (Fig. 2A). To further elucidate the role of PXDN in STAD, we conducted a series of in vitro assays. We first examined PXDN expression in STAD cell lines and found that it was upregulated in most cells, particularly in HGC27 and MKN45 cells (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Material 1 A). These two cell lines were selected for PXDN knockdown via siRNA transfection, which was confirmed via western blotting and qRT‒PCR (Fig. 2C and Supplementary Material 1B). CCK8, colony formation, and Transwell assays demonstrated that PXDN downregulation significantly decreased STAD cell proliferation, invasion, and migration (Fig. 2D-F). Together, these data indicate that PXDN promotes tumor progression in STAD by functioning as an oncogene.

PXDN regulates STAD progression through the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway

Our preliminary data confirmed that PXDN regulates STAD cell proliferation, invasion, and migration, although the specific mechanisms involved remain unclear. To uncover potential mechanisms, we performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), which revealed that high PXDN expression was significantly enriched in the epithelial‒mesenchymal transition (EMT), angiogenesis, and inflammatory response pathways (Fig. 3A‒C). To validate these findings, our initial Western blot assays demonstrated that PXDN downregulation significantly reduced the expression of EMT-related proteins (Fig. 3D). We then conducted tube formation assays in which conditioned media (CM) from both PXDN-knockdown and control STAD cell lines were incubated with human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs). The results showed that PXDN knockdown diminished the angiogenic capabilities of STAD cells (Fig. 3E). Signaling pathway activation is crucial for tumor initiation and progression. To explore the potential pathways involved in STAD, we conducted Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis, which revealed significant enrichment of PXDN in the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway (Fig. 3F). Extensive research has demonstrated that the PI3K/AKT pathway regulates proliferation, invasion, migration, EMT, and angiogenesis [26, 27]. We hypothesized that PXDN may regulate STAD progression via the PI3K/AKT pathway. Consistent with our hypothesis, PXDN knockdown decreased PI3K and AKT phosphorylation levels (Fig. 3G). In summary, these findings strongly suggest that PXDN regulates STAD progression via the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway.

PXDN expression levels affect sensitivity to chemotherapy, small molecule drugs and immunotherapy in patients with STAD

Antitumor drug sensitivity is closely related to gene expression levels. We explored whether PXDN expression affects the sensitivity of STAD to chemotherapy and small molecule drugs. As shown in Fig. 4, patients with high PXDN expression had higher IC50 values for tipiarnib, TAK-715, QS11, pyrimethamine, PAC-1, mitomycin C, doxorubicin, 5-fluorouracil, and vinorelbine, indicating lower sensitivity to these drugs in patients with elevated PXDN levels.

Furthermore, our previous enrichment analysis suggested that PXDN might be involved in inflammatory responses, indicating a potential role in the immune function of STAD. Therefore, we further analyzed the correlations between PXDN and immune characteristics. Notably, patients with high PXDN expression presented significantly higher tumor microenvironment (TME) scores, including stromal, immune, and ESTIMATE scores (Fig. 5A). In contrast, STAD patients with high PXDN expression had lower tumor mutation burden (TMB) values (Fig. 5B). The immunophenotype score (IPS) is widely used to predict tumor responsiveness to immunotherapy, with higher IPS scores typically indicating a higher rate of response [28]. Our analysis revealed that patients with high PXDN expression had higher IPS scores regardless of PD1 or CTLA4 status (Fig. 5C-F). These findings suggest that PXDN expression not only influences drug sensitivity in STAD but also plays a significant role in modulating the TIM and responsiveness to immunotherapy.

PXDN is involved in the regulation of immune cell infiltration and immune checkpoints in STAD

Our previous research demonstrated that PXDN regulates inflammatory responses and impacts the immune score in patients with STAD. To validate this conclusion, we investigated the relationship between PXDN expression and immune cell infiltration in STAD. Using the CIBERSORT algorithm, we found that PXDN expression was inversely correlated with CD8 + T cells and CD4 + memory T cells and positively correlated with M2 macrophages (Fig. 6A). Additionally, data from the TISIDB database indicated that PXDN expression is negatively associated with CD8 + T cells and activated CD4 + T cells and positively associated with central memory T cells (Fig. 6B). To verify the above findings, we stratified STAD patients into high- and low-expression groups on the basis of the median PXDN expression value. We then labeled CD8 + T cells and M2 macrophages with CD8 and CD206, respectively, and performed immunohistochemistry to evaluate their infiltration and correlation with PXDN. The results revealed that in STAD, PXDN expression was negatively correlated with CD8 + T-cell infiltration and positively correlated with M2 macrophage infiltration (Fig. 6C). These findings strongly suggest that PXDN plays a critical role in modulating the TIM in STAD.

Immune checkpoints are instrumental in regulating immune cell functions and are known to depend on upstream genes [29]. Consequently, we investigated whether a mutual correlation exists between PXDN and various immune checkpoints. According to data from the TISIDB database, as depicted in Fig. 7A and B, PXDN is negatively correlated with various immunostimulators, including TNFSF13, TNFRSF25, and TNFRSF14, but positively correlated with immunoinhibitors, such as KDR and TGFB1. Interestingly, data analysis from TCGA revealed that PXDN is positively correlated with several immunoinhibitors, including KDR and PDCD1LG2, but negatively correlated with several immune stimulators, including ENTPD1, RAET1E and IL6R (Fig. 7C and D).

Discussion

STAD, which is prevalent and has a poor prognosis in Asian populations, requires accurate prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets to increase patient survival [30]. The complex biological functions of PXDN are indispensable for maintaining the normal physiological function of cells. Previous research has shown that PXDN promotes various tumors, including oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC), skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM), prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD), and ovarian cancer (OC) [19, 22, 31]. Although PXDN is highly expressed in STAD and associated with poor prognosis, its specific mechanism has not been thoroughly elucidated [32].

In this study, analysis of STAD datasets from public databases revealed that PXDN mRNA levels are significantly increased in tumors. However, as shown in Fig. 1C, we found that PXDN expression in some normal gastric epithelial tissues was higher than that in paired STAD samples, a phenomenon also observed in our clinical immunohistochemistry results. However, further statistical analysis revealed that PXDN expression in STAD tissues was still significantly upregulated compared with that in normal tissues. Previous studies have confirmed that specific genes can play completely opposite roles in different tumors [33]. We hypothesize that this may be due to the complex regulatory effects of gene networks, leading to tumor heterogeneity. Importantly, survival analysis of clinical samples and public data also revealed that patients with high PXDN expression have a poorer prognosis.

The TNM system is widely used and recognized for evaluating the prognosis of patients with STAD. In this study, univariate regression analysis of clinical samples revealed that age, stage, PXDN level, and T, N, and M stages were independent risk factors for the prognosis of patients with STAD. However, multivariate regression analysis revealed that stage and T, N, and M stages were not independent risk factors. We believe that this discrepancy may be due to the insufficient sample size, and we plan to increase the sample size in future studies to address this issue.

To explore the specific function of PXDN in STAD, we used GSEA for functional enrichment analysis. The results showed that STAD patients with high PXDN expression were significantly enriched in multiple functional signaling pathways, including EMT, angiogenesis, and the inflammatory response. EMT and angiogenesis are well known for their essential roles in tumor development and metastasis [34, 35]. To confirm our findings, we performed PXDN knockdown in STAD cells, followed by a series of in vitro experiments, including the detection of EMT markers via Western blotting and angiogenesis assays. The results confirmed that PXDN is involved in regulating EMT and angiogenesis. Further investigation using KEGG analysis revealed that PXDN plays a role in regulating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, which was confirmed by Western blotting. Previous studies have shown that the PI3K/AKT pathway regulates EMT and angiogenesis, suggesting that PXDN may influence these processes through the PI3K/AKT pathway [36–38]. However, our study lacks rescue experiments to definitively confirm this hypothesis, which we intend to address in future research. In summary, these results suggest that PXDN plays a critical role in maintaining tumor proliferation, invasion, migration, and angiogenesis. This evidence strongly supports the role of PXDN as an oncogene in STAD.

Although many studies have confirmed that PXDN plays an indispensable role in malignant tumors, its effect on the tumor immune microenvironment is poorly understood. Shi W et al. used data from a public database of malignant gliomas and reported that PXDN expression levels were positively correlated with the infiltration levels of resting NK cells, regulatory T cells, M0 macrophages, monocytes and eosinophils [26]. In addition, in melanoma, a public database was used to identify two immune molecular subtypes, identify the genes differentially expressed between them, and construct a risk prediction model based on six genes, in which PXDN was confirmed to be the key gene in the model [39]. These results strongly suggest that PXDN may be involved in the regulation of immune microenvironment function in malignant tumors. However, the above studies have never been verified in in vitro and clinical samples. In this study, to investigate whether PXDN participates in the regulation of immune cell infiltration in STAD, we applied the TISIDB database and CIBERSORT algorithm to analyze the correlation between PXDN and the abundance of various immune cells. The results revealed that PXDN expression was significantly negatively correlated with the abundance of CD8 + T cells and positively correlated with the number of M2 macrophages. CD8 + T cells are recognized as key players in antitumor immunity and are the primary immune cells responsible for tumor destruction, as established in prior research [40]. The infiltration and polarization of tumor macrophages are considered important events in carcinogenesis, and M2 polarization of macrophages has the ability to promote tumor progression [41]. To further validate these findings, we used immunohistochemistry to analyze the associations of PXDN with CD8 + T cells and M2 macrophages. These results are consistent with those of the database analyses, emphasizing that PXDN influences STAD progression not only by promoting tumor growth and metastasis but also by modulating immune cell infiltration and thus restructuring the TIM. However, a limitation of this study is that it does not elaborate on the specific mechanisms by which PXDN regulates the infiltration of CD8 + T cells and M2 macrophages. Further research is needed to elucidate these mechanisms.

Conclusion

To further validate these findings, we used immunohistochemistry to analyze the associations of PXDN with CD8 + T cells and M2 macrophages. These results are consistent with those of the database analyses, emphasizing that PXDN influences STAD progression not only by promoting tumor growth and metastasis but also by modulating immune cell infiltration and thus restructuring the TIM. However, a limitation of this study is that it does not elaborate on the specific mechanisms by which PXDN regulates the infiltration of CD8 + T cells and M2 macrophages. Further research is needed to elucidate these mechanisms.
