Canonical differential equations beyond polylogs
Fabian J. Wagner

Abstract
Feynman integrals whose associated geometries extend beyond the Riemann sphere, such as elliptic curves and Calabi–Yau varieties, are increasingly relevant in modern precision calculations. They arise not only in collider cross-section calculations, but also in the post-Minkowskian expansion of gravitational-wave scattering. A powerful approach to compute integrals of this type is via differential equations, particularly when cast in a canonical form, which simplifies their ε \varepsilon -expansion and makes analytic properties manifest. In these proceedings, we will present a method to systematically construct canonical differential equations even for integrals that evaluate beyond multiple polylogarithms. The discussion is kept as light as possible, focusing on the two-loop sunrise integral, deferring the technical details to the original publications.

1
Introduction

Scattering amplitudes are central quantities in Quantum Field Theory. They constitute the backbone for the calculation of most observables at particle
colliders.
Amplitudes are analytic functions of the kinematic invariants, whose non-analyticities are restricted to poles and branch cuts.
Singularities happen when particle states go on shell, thus
the singularity structure encodes a large amount of information about the particle content of the theory.
It is therefore crucial that the analytic expression of an amplitude compactly encodes the singularity structure.

In recent years, it has become evident that the computation of an amplitude is streamlined when the tensor structure is factored out, and the problem then reduces to computing scalar
Feynman integrals
.
In what follows, we focus exclusively on scalar integrals.
An extremely powerful technique to compute Feynman integrals is the differential equations method
[
41
,
49
,
33
]
, which in turn is based on the existence of
integration-by-parts identities

[
51
,
19
]
(IBPs) among Feynman integrals. IBPs allow one to reduce all Feynman integrals from a given family to a basis of so-called
master integrals
. Since Feynman integrals diverge, dimensional regularisation
[
52
,
12
]
can be employed to regularise them. Thus the integrals are functions of the kinematic invariants and the dimensional regulator
\varepsilon=(d-d_{0})/2
, with
d_{0}
an integer.

Decades of calculations have shown that the analytic expressions for most one-loop or massless amplitudes can be written as

\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{s})=\sum_{i}A_{i}(\boldsymbol{s})\int_{\gamma}\mathrm{d}\log{f_{n}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{s})\wedge\ldots\wedge\mathrm{d}\log{f_{1}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{s})\,,

(1)

where the
A_{i}
are algebraic functions, dependent on the kinematic invariants
\boldsymbol{s}
, that carry the information on the kinematic singularities, whereas the chain of integrations of logarithmic forms constitutes the transcendental part, carrying the information associated with poles and branch cuts. The appearance of logarithmic singularities is expected, as it is a consequence of the locality of the interactions.
Integrals, and amplitudes, with this property are called
pure

[
4
]
, term firstly introduced to describe amplitudes in
\mathcal{N}=4
super Yang-Mills theory.

Expressions as in eq. (
1
) are natural when computing one-loop integrals, as they are often associated to a (puctured) Riemann sphere, yielding only simple poles.
However, starting at the two-loop order, Feynman integrals are characterised by higher-dimensional geometries, and
\mathrm{d}\log
-forms are insufficient to span the space of possible solutions. It is then not clear if logarithmic singularities are enough to describe the amplitude.
In these proceedings, we describe how to maintain these desired properties even when the integrals evaluate to functions beyond polylogarithms.

2
Canonical differential equations and geometries

Let us start by reviewing the case of Feynman integrals that evaluate to polylogarithms and show what changes when going to a higher-dimensional underlying geometry.

Integrals that evaluate to multiple polylogarithms.

If the integral evaluates to iterated integrations of rational functions, then the only independent differential forms that may appear in the integrand are of the type

\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{x-a_{i}}\,,

(2)

with
a_{i}
being the singular points. Thus, the integral can be expressed in terms of iterated
\mathrm{d}\log
integrations

J(\boldsymbol{s})\sim\int_{\gamma}\mathrm{d}\log{f_{n}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{s})\wedge\ldots\wedge\mathrm{d}\log{f_{1}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{s})\,\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{s})^{k\,\varepsilon}\,,

(3)

often evaluating to
multiple polylogarithms
(MPL)
[
42
,
47
,
34
,
35
]
.
Integrals of this form are not only pure, but are in fact
canonical

[
38
]
, in the sense that they satisfy:

1.

\varepsilon
-factorised differential equations:
\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{J}=\varepsilon\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{s})\,\boldsymbol{J}
;

2.

The entries of the connection
\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{s})
have only simple poles;

3.

The differential forms in
\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{s})
are independent up to total derivatives.

This form is particularly convenient as it allows for a solution in terms of a path ordered exponential

\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{s},\varepsilon)=\mathbb{P}\exp\left[\varepsilon\int_{\gamma}\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{s^{\prime}})\right]\,.

(4)

Each order in the Laurent series in
\varepsilon
can be expressed in terms of Chen iterated integrals
[
18
]
over the differential forms in
\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{s})
.
Moreover, the integral
has uniform transcendental weight (UT)
[
40
]
.

Integrals that evaluate to special functions beyond MPLs.

For integration domains beyond the Riemann sphere, the differential forms in eq. (
2
) do not suffice to span the space of master integrands. Let us for now specialise to the simplest generalisation of the Riemann sphere: the elliptic curve.
On the elliptic curve, there are still forms that have logarithmic type singularities

\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{(x-a_{i})\,y}\,,

(5)

where
a_{i}
are the marked points and
y^{2}=P_{4}(x)
is the equation defining a quartic elliptic curve.
However, these forms do not suffice to span the cohomology, as a torus has at least two independent cycles beyond those encircling the punctures. The remaining ones are

\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{y}\,\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{y}\left(x^{2}-\frac{s_{1}\,x}{2}\right)\,,

(6)

where
s_{1}
is the first symmetric polynomial. The forms in eq. (
6
) have no pole and a double pole without residues, respectively.
In the rest of these proceedings we will show that, despite the different pole structure in the differential forms, integrals evaluating to MPLs and beyond MPLs do enjoy similar properties.

Let us start by considering the corner-integral,
I_{1}=I_{1,1,1,0,0}(\boldsymbol{s};d)

Either from a loop-by-loop approach

\begin{split}{\rm LS}\left(I_{1}\right)&\,\propto\oint\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{5}}{\sqrt{M^{2}+s+2x_{5}}\sqrt{M^{2}s-x_{5}^{2}}\sqrt{4m^{2}-M^{2}-s-2x_{5}}}=\oint\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{5}}{\sqrt{P_{4}(x_{5})}}\,,\end{split}

(8)

where
P_{4}(x_{5})
is the quartic polynomial, which, for generic

\begin{split}\mathcal{E}:\quad y^{2}=P_{4}(x_{5})&=(M^{2}+s+2x_{5})(M^{2}s-x_{5}^{2})(4m^{2}-M^{2}-s-2x_{5})\,,\\
&=4(x_{5}-a_{1})(x_{5}-a_{2})(x_{5}-a_{3})(x_{5}-a_{4})\,,\end{split}

(9)

where the four roots are given by

\displaystyle a_{1}=-\sqrt{M^{2}s}\,,\quad a_{2}=-\frac{M^{2}+s}{2}\,,\quad a_{3}=\frac{4m^{2}-M^{2}-s}{2}\,,\quad a_{4}=+\sqrt{M^{2}s}\,.

(10)

Thus, as long as the masses are non-zero, the geometry associated to the sunrise integral is the family of elliptic curves defined by eq. (
9
). If
M^{2}=0
, however, the elliptic curve may degenerate. Therefore, we discuss the two cases
M^{2}=0
and
M^{2}\neq 0
separately.

The polylogarithmic case:
M^{2}=0
.

From eq. (
9

M^{2}=0
two of the branch points coincide, and the elliptic curve degenerates into two copies of the Riemann sphere connected by the branch cut associated with the
remaining square root. The final geometry is then topologically
equivalent to a single Riemann sphere.
By solving the associated IBPs, we see that there are two independent master integrals,
which we can choose as

I_{1}=I_{1,1,1,0,0}(\boldsymbol{s};d)\textrm{~~~and~~~}I_{2}=I_{1,1,1,0,-1}(\boldsymbol{s};d)\,.

(11)

Assuming
m^{2}>0
and
s<0
for definiteness,
we see from eq. (
8
) that the leading singularities of the first integral in
d=2
take the form

{\rm LS}\left(I_{1}\right)\Big|_{M^{2}=0}\propto\oint\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{5}}{x_{5}\,\sqrt{s+2x_{5}}\sqrt{4m^{2}-s-2x_{5}}}\,.

(12)

The integrand of eq. (
12
) has a single pole at
x_{5}=0
and a branch cut between the two roots
x_{5,1}=-s/2
and
x_{5,2}=(4m^{2}-s)/2
. Correspondingly, we can consider two independent integration contours:
\mathcal{C}_{1}
is a small circle around the single pole, and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
encircles the branch cut (see

Since there is no pole at infinity, these two cycles cannot be independent.
One easily finds that, modulo overall prefactors and with
s\to s+i0

\displaystyle\oint_{\mathcal{C}_{1}}\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{5}}{x_{5}\,\sqrt{s+2x_{5}}\sqrt{4m^{2}-s-2x_{5}}}

\displaystyle\propto\oint_{\mathcal{C}_{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{5}}{x_{5}\,\sqrt{s+2x_{5}}\sqrt{4m^{2}-s-2x_{5}}}\propto\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{s(s-4m^{2})}}\,.

(13)

The result is a rational integrand with two poles, whose residues are equal and opposite due to the Global Residue Theorem
[
37
]
. Thus, we found a candidate with logarithmic singularities.

Let us repeat the same analysis for
I_{2}
. To obtain its integrand, we just need to
multiply the integrand of
I_{1}
by
(k_{2}\cdot p)=x_{5}

{\rm LS}\left(I_{2}\right)\Big|_{M^{2}=0}\propto\oint\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{5}\,}{\sqrt{s+2x_{5}}\sqrt{4m^{2}-s-2x_{5}}}\,.

(14)

The transformation
x_{5}\to 1/y_{5}
shows that the integrand has a single pole at
x_{5}=\infty
in addition to the branch cut. Again, the residue at the simple pole is related to the integral over
\mathcal{C}_{2}
, and we get

\displaystyle\oint_{\mathcal{C}_{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{5}}{\sqrt{s+2x_{5}}\sqrt{4m^{2}-s-2x_{5}}}\propto i\pi\,,

(15)

finding another integral with logarithmic singularities.

To obtain pure integrals, we multiply each integral by the inverse of its leading singularity, so that all algebraic prefactors are removed and the result has purely logarithmic singularities. Our basis of pure master integrals is given by

J_{1}=\sqrt{s(s-4m^{2})}I_{1}\,,\qquad J_{2}=I_{2}\,.

(16)

The analysis we have carried out so far assumed
\varepsilon=0
. Let us now restore the
\varepsilon
-dependence. This causes no obstruction for integrals of
\mathrm{d}\log
forms, as is apparent, for example, from the Baikov representation, where the
\varepsilon
-dependence enters as

J(\boldsymbol{s})\sim\int_{\gamma}\mathrm{d}\log{f_{n}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{s})\wedge\ldots\wedge\mathrm{d}\log{f_{1}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{s})\,\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{s})^{k\,\varepsilon}\,.

(17)

The Laurent-expansion around
\varepsilon=0
generates only additional logarithmic terms and therefore does not spoil the logarithmic singularity structure.

Lastly, we can compute the differential equation, e.g. in
s
, satisfied by the maximal cut of the basis in eq. (
16
). It reads

\displaystyle\partial_{s}\!\begin{pmatrix}J_{1}|_{\textrm{MC}}\\
J_{2}|_{\textrm{MC}}\end{pmatrix}=(d-2)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{2(s-m^{2})}{s(s-4m^{2})}&\frac{3}{\sqrt{s(s-4m^{2})}}\\

\end{array}\right)\begin{pmatrix}J_{1}|_{\textrm{MC}}\\
J_{2}|_{\textrm{MC}}\end{pmatrix}\,.

The entries of the differential equation matrix can be identified as dlog-forms. Equation (
3
) exactly satisfies our requirements of a canonical differential equation, as discussed in section

The elliptic case:
M^{2}\neq 0
.

Let us now see how the previous analysis changes if
M^{2}\neq 0
. Solving IBPs now exposes three independent master integrals, which fulfil a set of three coupled differential equations.
One possible choice is

\begin{split}I_{1}=I_{1,1,1,0,0}(\boldsymbol{s};d)\,,\qquad I_{2}=I_{1,1,1,0,-1}(\boldsymbol{s};d)\,,\qquad I_{3}=I_{1,1,1,0,-2}(\boldsymbol{s};d)\,.\end{split}

(20)

As before, let us study their leading singularities in
d=2
dimensions. We can write

{\rm LS}\left(I_{r}\right)\propto\oint\mathrm{d}x_{5}\frac{x_{5}^{r-1}}{\sqrt{P_{4}(x_{5})}}\,,\qquad r=1,2,3\,,

(21)

with
P_{4}
defined in eq. (
9
).
Let us repeat the same analysis as in the massless case.

For
r=1
, we reproduce eq. (
8

8

\mathcal{E}
in eq. (
9

Due to the existence of four distinct branch points, there are two
independent

\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}

\mathcal{E}

\psi_{0}=\oint_{\mathcal{C}_{1}}\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{5}}{\sqrt{P_{4}(x_{5})}}\,,\qquad\psi_{1}=\oint_{\mathcal{C}_{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{5}}{\sqrt{P_{4}(x_{5})}}\,,

(22)

evaluating to elliptic integrals of the first kind.
With an appropriate choice of cycles,
\psi_{0}
is holomorphic around the MUM-point (maximal unipotent monodromy point)
\boldsymbol{s}=0
and
\psi_{1}
has a logarithmic singularity. While
\psi_{1}
has non-trivial transcendental weight,
\psi_{0}
plays a similar role as the square root in eq. (
16

I_{1}
by
\psi_{0}
to get a pure integral.

Let us now consider
{\rm LS}\left(I_{2}\right)
.
With the transformation
x_{5}\to 1/y_{5}
, we get

{\rm LS}\left(I_{2}\right)\propto\oint\mathrm{d}y_{5}\left[\frac{1}{2y_{5}}-\frac{s+M^{2}-2m^{2}}{4}+\mathcal{O}(y_{5})\right]\,.

(23)

This exposes a single pole at
x_{5}=\infty
(or
y_{5}=0
). We may correspondingly consider a new independent contour
\mathcal{C}_{3}
, which encircles the pole at
x_{5}=\infty
. When evaluated on the contours
\mathcal{C}_{1}
or
\mathcal{C}_{2}
, we obtain an elliptic integral of the third kind. As the residue at
x_{5}=\infty
is constant, this
analysis singles out a second master integral, with similar properties as in the polylogarithmic case.

Finally, consider the leading singularities of the third master integral.
With the same change of variables as for
I_{2}
, we find

\displaystyle{\rm LS}\left(I_{3}\right)\propto\oint\mathrm{d}y_{5}

\displaystyle\left[\frac{1}{2y_{5}^{2}}-\frac{s+M^{2}-2m^{2}}{4y_{5}}\,+\mathcal{O}(y_{5}^{0})\right]\,.

(24)

The integrand has both a double and a simple pole at
x_{5}=\infty
.
The simple pole is proportional to the first symmetric polynomial
of the four roots
s_{1}=a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}+a_{4}=s+M^{2}-2m^{2}
, and so we may add the appropriate multiple of
I_{2}
to cancel the simple pole. As we have seen in eq. (
6
), this integral is independent from the previous ones, called integral of the second kind. Therefore, provides a third candidate master integral.
However, this integral does not appear to have purely logarithmic singularities.
Moreover, restoring the
\varepsilon
-dependence does not simply amount to adding logarithms. In the presence of higher poles, IBPs mix
\varepsilon
-powers with higher-pole contributions, thereby spoiling the logarithmic expansion. Next we describe how to resolve this issue.

4
The algorithm

In this section we summarise the algorithm of refs.
[
36
,
24
]
applied to the sunrise integral and we briefly discuss its general implementation.
We start by motivating a different choice for the integral with a double pole.
Suppose we found a canonical integral

J=c^{(0)}+\varepsilon\sum_{k}c_{k}^{(1)}\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(1)}+\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{k}c_{k}^{(2)}\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(2)}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{3})\,,

(25)

where
\mathcal{J}_{k}^{(i)}
are pure integrals and
c^{(i)}
are such that the integral is UT.
Taking a derivative with respect to any external kinematic

\partial J\sim\varepsilon\sum_{k}A_{k}\,c_{k}^{(1)}+\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{k}c_{k}^{(2)}\sum_{j}A_{j}\,\mathcal{J}_{k,j}^{(1)}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{3})\,,

(26)

yields still a UT integral, after appropriate multiplication by
\varepsilon^{-1}
. However, eq. (
26
) is not pure anymore, due to the (possibly transcendental) functions
A_{i}
, acting as leading singularities.
Therefore,
\partial J
has already some of the properties that are satisfied by a canonical integral.
To understand the leading singularity in this coupled elliptic case, let us introduce the
period matrix

\boldsymbol{W}
.
Briefly, the period matrix pairs the master integrands with the independent cycles and satisfies the same differential equation as the master integrals:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{I}(\boldsymbol{s},\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{s},\varepsilon)\boldsymbol{I}(\boldsymbol{s},\varepsilon)\,,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{W}(\boldsymbol{s},\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{s},\varepsilon)\boldsymbol{W}(\boldsymbol{s},\varepsilon)\,,

(27)

thereby, the entries of
\boldsymbol{W}
provide a minimal linearly-independent basis of solutions.
Let us now fix
d=d_{0}
.
In the polylogarithmic case (Riemann-sphere geometry), maximal cuts can be decoupled into
1\times 1
blocks.
This is, however, not the case for integrals associated with elliptic curves, where the cohomology of the maximal cut is two-dimensional.
Therefore, when generalising the notion of leading singularities to elliptic integrals, one must consider the full
2\times 2
period matrix.

Let us now go back to the sunrise family with master integrals in eq. (
20
), and let us focus on the coupled system formed by
\{I_{1},I_{3}\}
. Let us change basis to a derivative basis as motivated above. For
\boldsymbol{I}=\{I_{1},\partial_{s}I_{1}\}
, the period matrix, evaluated for
d=2
, reads

\boldsymbol{W}=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{0}&\psi_{1}\\
\partial_{s}\psi_{0}&\partial_{s}\psi_{1}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(28)

Analysing the series expansion of the entries of
\boldsymbol{W}
around a MUM-point, shows that they are neither pure nor have definite transcendental weight.
However, it is possible to split
\boldsymbol{W}
into its
semi-simple
and
unipotent
parts

\boldsymbol{W}=\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{0}&0\\
\partial_{s}\psi_{0}&\frac{\Delta}{\psi_{0}}\end{pmatrix}}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{\textrm{ss}}}\cdot\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}1&\frac{\psi_{1}}{\psi_{0}}\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}}_{\boldsymbol{W}_{\textrm{u}}}\,,

(29)

where
\Delta
is the determinant of
\boldsymbol{W}
, an algebraic function.

\boldsymbol{W}_{\textrm{ss}}
has transcendental weight zero
[
16
]
and can thus be interpreted as the generalisation of the algebraic leading singularities in the polylogarithmic case.
\boldsymbol{W}_{\textrm{u}}
is unipotent, and
\tau=\psi_{1}/\psi_{0}
is a pure function of transcendental weight one
1
1

1

The weight depends on the normalisation.
, thereby providing a generalisation of UT and pure functions beyond the polylogarithmic case.
Consequently, we multiply the system of
I_{1}
and its derivative by the inverse of the semi-simple part of their period matrix to have the correct transcendental weight at
\varepsilon=0
.

Let us now consider the Laurent expansion of the integrals for
\varepsilon\neq 0
.
A canonical integral as in eq. (
25
) is expected to have as coefficient of
\varepsilon^{k}
a pure function of uniform transcendental weight
k
.
However, while all the entries of
\boldsymbol{W}_{\textrm{u}}
are pure functions, the entries of the second column have different weights.
This can be remedied by a rescaling by powers of
\varepsilon
to ensure the expected correlation.
In the case at hand, as also suggested by the analysis of the weight of eq. (
26
), the goal is reached by rescaling
\partial_{s}I_{1}
by a relative factor of
\varepsilon^{-1}
.

Now that the transcendental weight is realigned and the integrals expand to pure functions, the last ingredient left to ensure is the
\varepsilon
-factorisation of the differential equation. It is possible to check (we refer to ref.
[
24
]
for the details) that
\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{I}=\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{B}}+\varepsilon\widetilde{\boldsymbol{A}}\right)\boldsymbol{I}
, with
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{B}}
a nilpotent matrix.
This implies that it can be removed by a unipotent matrix, with the non-constant entries defined by requiring the transformed basis
\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{s})=\boldsymbol{U}(\boldsymbol{s},\varepsilon)\boldsymbol{I}(\boldsymbol{s})
to satisfy

\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{s},\varepsilon)=\varepsilon\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{s})\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{s},\varepsilon)\,.

(30)

As a consequence the functions introduced to
\varepsilon
-factorise (
\varepsilon
-
functions
) satisfy first-order differential equations, which can be solved by series expansions.

Finally, locally close to singular points, we indeed get the properties wished for in section
2
. Furthermore, the limit
M^{2}\to 0
can be taken explicitly and indeed yields
\mathrm{d}\log
forms.

Beyond elliptic curves.

Going beyond the elliptic case requires no essential changes, as the algorithm outlined above is not tied to any specific geometry. It can be summarised in two steps:

1.

First step: we start by choosing a
good
initial basis
\boldsymbol{I}(\boldsymbol{s},\varepsilon)
. We propose to
choose a basis that is compatible with the mixed Hodge structure associated with the integral at
\varepsilon=0
. In practice, it is obtained by doing an integrand analysis in integer dimensions and choosing integrals aligned with the geometry underlying the maximal cuts at
\varepsilon=0
.

2.

Second step: we rotate the initial basis by a sequence of rotations:

\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{s},\varepsilon)=\boldsymbol{U}_{\mathrm{t}}(\boldsymbol{s},\varepsilon)\boldsymbol{U}_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon)\boldsymbol{U}_{\mathrm{ss}}(\boldsymbol{s})\boldsymbol{I}(\boldsymbol{s},\varepsilon)\,,

(31)

where,
\boldsymbol{U}_{\mathrm{ss}}(\boldsymbol{s})
is a matrix whose blocks are inverses of the semi-simple part of the respective period matrices.
\boldsymbol{U}_{\varepsilon}
is needed to re-align the transcendental weight, and finally
\boldsymbol{U}_{\mathrm{t}}(\boldsymbol{s},\varepsilon)
is the rotation defined such that the final differential equation matrix is
\varepsilon
-factorised.

5
Conclusions

In these proceedings, focusing on the elliptic two-loop sunrise integral, we have described an algorithm to bring integrals with any underlying geometry to canonical form.
So far, it has been applied to a range of cases, including but not limited to the following: elliptic amplitudes and correlators
[

,
30
,
45
,
9
,
20
,
10
]
, higher-genus curves
[

, black-hole scattering
[

,
21

22

, and integrals with a multi-scale Calabi-Yau geometry
[

.
In the future, it will be interesting to gain a deeper understanding of the
\varepsilon
-functions, a first step in this direction has been taken in

13

, to rigorously establish that it always yields a canonical form, and to assess its performance in the numerical evaluation of the special functions that appear in the differential equations.
