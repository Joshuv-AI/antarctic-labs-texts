Serial Donor-derived Cell-free DNA Monitoring in a Kidney Transplant Recipient With Adenovirus Nephritis
Authors: Eric J Xu, Samir Hirpara, Akil S Kavcar, Natalia Jucha, Nimisha Sulejmani, Martha Pavlakis, Seymour Rosen, Aditya S Pawar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOpportunistic infection is one of the most common complications in solid organ transplantation. In particular, viral infections of the renal allograft pose a diagnostic and management dilemma because the histopathologic differences between viral allograft nephritis and acute rejection can be subtle yet critical to recognize, given the fundamentally contradictory treatment strategies. Although noninvasive tests, such as viral polymerase chain reaction, can aid in the diagnosis of viral allograft involvement, a definitive diagnosis requires a kidney biopsy. Donor-derived cell-free DNA (dd-cfDNA) has been validated for detecting both acute cellular rejection and antibody-mediated rejection, but its utility as a test for renal allograft infection remains to be defined and has largely been restricted to cases of BK polyomavirus nephropathy. Here, we described a case of acute kidney injury that developed shortly after kidney transplantation. dd-cfDNA was elevated, and biopsy revealed findings characteristic of adenovirus infection. We examined the differential diagnosis of viral infections in renal allografts and explored the role of dd-cfDNA in managing these cases.

Case Presentation

A 55-year-old man with end-stage kidney disease from hypertension on intermittent hemodialysis underwent kidney transplantation from a donor after circulatory death. Pretransplant testing revealed a low-level donor-specific antibody (DSA) to human leukocyte antigen C7 (mean fluorescence intensity < 1,000) with negative physical crossmatches. He received induction with anti-thymocyte globulin (5 mg/kg) and was initiated on tacrolimus (goal 10-12 ng/mL), mycophenolic acid 720 mg twice daily, and steroids. Prophylaxis included valganciclovir, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, and nystatin. His course was notable for brief delayed graft function, requiring 1 session of hemodialysis on postoperative day (POD) 1. He required no further dialysis and was discharged on POD 7 with a serum creatinine level of 13.1 mg/dL. His renal function improved to a nadir serum creatinine level of 2.3 mg/dL on POD 17, then rose to 2.6 mg/dL on POD 21, for which oral hydration was encouraged.

He presented to the emergency room on POD 25 with 3 days of intermittent fever and 3 weeks of rhinorrhea, productive cough, and headaches. He was afebrile upon presentation with unremarkable vital signs. Laboratory investigation showed a serum creatinine level of 3.1 mg/dL and a white blood cell count of 2.3 K/uL. Tacrolimus levels were within the target range with stable hemoglobin concentration (10.3 g/dL) and platelet count (171 K/uL). The urine protein/creatinine level ratio was 0.5 g/g, and urinalysis revealed significant microscopic hematuria without pyuria. Renal ultrasound was unrevealing. Computed tomography demonstrated a subcutaneous seroma at the transplant site that was neither amenable to drainage nor suggestive of infection. A broad infectious respiratory viral panel, blood culture, and urine culture were negative. Serum creatinine levels remained elevated at 2.9-3.1 mg/dL despite intravenous fluids, which prompted dd-cfDNA (CareDx, AlloSure) and DSA testing on POD 29. Urine BK polymerase chain reaction and serum cytomegalovirus polymerase chain reaction were negative. He was discharged with plans for an outpatient kidney transplant biopsy. dd-cfDNA was elevated at 2.1%, and the DSA to C7 was detected at a mean fluorescence intensity of 1,200. Serum creatinine level and urine protein/creatinine ratio rose to 3.3 mg/dL and 1.4 g/g, respectively. Urinalysis showed persistent microscopic hematuria and new pyuria.

Multiple etiologies for the patient’s acute kidney injury were considered. BK polyomavirus nephropathy and cytomegalovirus nephritis were unlikely in the absence of viruria and viremia. Although the combination of elevated dd-cfDNA and a detectable DSA raised concern for rejection, the low immunogenicity of the human leukocyte antigen-C antigens and low-mean fluorescence intensity DSA made antibody-mediated rejection unlikely. A transplant biopsy was thus performed on POD 32 to evaluate for acute cellular rejection.

Results and Management

A tubulocentric necrotizing inflammatory process with interstitial nephritis and edema was noted on biopsy (Fig 1). Tubules demonstrated intense, histiocyte-predominant inflammation with frequent complete epithelial denudation and positive CD68 and CD4 staining. Viral inclusions were present in the tubules. C4d was negative. Fibrosis in the parenchyma, arterioles, and interlobular arteries was minimal, and larger arteries were unremarkable. Stains were negative for SV40, cytomegalovirus, and herpes simplex virus 1 and 2, whereas adenovirus staining was positive. Serum adenovirus polymerase chain reaction was subsequently checked and found to be elevated at 3.86 log copies/mL. Although the biopsy was most consistent with adenovirus-associated tubulointerstitial nephritis, the adenovirus stain was pending, and the degree of inflammation raised concern for possible concomitant cellular rejection. Therefore, the decision was made to cautiously reduce immunosuppression and monitor serial dd-cfDNA to further guide management. Tacrolimus was lowered to a goal of 8-10 ng/mL. Mycophenolic acid was lowered to 360 mg twice daily. Following these changes, prompt declines in dd-cfDNA levels, adenovirus viral loads, and serum creatinine levels supported the hypothesis that the biopsy findings reflected infection as the primary process (Fig 2). Cidofovir and intravenous immunoglobulin were deferred because of clinical improvement with immunosuppression reduction alone and absence of hypogammaglobulinemia (immunoglobulin G level 1,262 mg/dL).

Discussion

BK polyomavirus is the best-studied viral pathogen in kidney allografts, but infection from other viruses, such as adenovirus, has been reported. Adenovirus viremia is more common early after kidney transplantation and can present with a broad range of signs and symptoms—including fever, dysuria, hematuria, pyuria, acute kidney injury, lymphopenia, orchitis, and gastrointestinal distress—unlike the more limited presentation of BK.1,2 Although our patient exhibited several of these features, it was not clear that his renal impairment was because of adenovirus. The elevated dd-cfDNA, however, pointed toward an inflammatory process. To our knowledge, this is the first report of dd-cfDNA being used to detect adenovirus infection in a kidney allograft.

As initially reported by the diagnosing active rejection in kidney transplant recipients study, dd-cfDNA monitoring is based on the principle that allograft injury from cell death releases circulating donor DNA.3 Although dd-cfDNA has been validated as a noninvasive test for acute rejection, its role in renal allograft infection is less defined. Prior work by Kant et al4 showed that dd-cfDNA positively correlates with BK viral loads and polyomavirus nephropathy. A similar association may also exist for adenovirus, given the overlapping tropism of these viruses for the tubulointerstitium. Compared with BK, adenovirus nephritis usually causes more severe inflammation, with marked tubular injury, parenchymal edema, and minimal features of chronic injury. Tubulocentric necrotizing granulomas appear to be a distinguishing feature of adenovirus.5, 6, 7, 8

The marked inflammation seen in adenovirus may relate to differences in viral replication. BK relies on host cell endocycling, in which the cell re-enters the S phase from G2 without undergoing mitosis. This prolonged S phase supports viral replication and further endocycling, ultimately inducing tubular cell senescence and fibrosis.9 In contrast, adenovirus replication is lytic, using the adenovirus death protein to rupture the host cell membrane.10

No approved antiviral therapies exist for adenovirus. Cidofovir inhibits adenovirus replication in vitro and may reduce viral loads in transplant recipients, but evidence for its use is limited to observational studies.11, 12, 13 Furthermore, nephrotoxicity and myelosuppression often limit cidofovir use in kidney transplant recipients. Unlike cidofovir, brincidofovir contains a lipid conjugate that precludes its uptake by the organic anion transporter 1 in the proximal tubule.14 Phase 2 and 3 trials of brincidofovir for the treatment of adenovirus in solid organ and hematopoietic cell transplant recipients are underway, but early data suggest that the limited intrarenal accumulation of brincidofovir significantly attenuates its nephrotoxic potential.15, 16, 17 Hypogammaglobulinemia increases infection risk in solid organ transplant recipients.18 However, evidence for intravenous immunoglobulin efficacy is restricted to case reports and often confounded by cidofovir coadministration.11,19,20 It remains unclear whether intravenous immunoglobulin directly inhibits adenovirus or simply facilitates reduced immunosuppression through its immunomodulatory effects.

In the absence of approved antiviral therapies, the management of adenovirus infection in a kidney transplant recipient mirrors that of BK polyomavirus and relies heavily on immunosuppression reduction. Because this approach carries an inherent risk for rejection, close monitoring is essential to ensure that immunosuppression changes are appropriate. Because of its lytic propagation, dd-cfDNA may be an effective proxy for adenovirus nephropathy and particularly useful in navigating this management dilemma. In the aforementioned study of diagnosing active rejection in kidney transplant recipients, dd-cfDNA level was higher in antibody-mediated rejection (2.9%) than in acute cellular rejection (1.2% for grade 1B or greater, 0.2% for 1A). Interestingly, a retrospective analysis of the diagnosing active rejection in kidney transplant recipients study by Kant et al4 showed that dd-cfDNA levels in patients with grade 1A cellular rejection and co-existing BK polyomavirus nephropathy (2.84%) exceeded those with isolated acute cellular rejection and approximated those with isolated antibody-mediated rejection.3 The lytic replication profile of adenovirus provides a plausible explanation for why our patient’s dd-cfDNA level resembled those seen in antibody-mediated rejection. Because viral nephritis and cellular rejection can coexist and their features often overlap, a single biopsy or biomarker may be insufficient for distinguishing between these 2 entities. In cases where the histopathologic features are equivocal, serial dd-cfDNA monitoring levels may therefore serve as a dynamic adjunct to pathology, helping clinicians confirm that changes in immunosuppression are appropriately targeted to the primary disease.

In conclusion, we described the first use of serial dd-cfDNA monitoring to guide the management of adenovirus allograft nephropathy. Although biopsy remains the diagnostic gold standard, the intense inflammation associated with adenovirus may make dd-cfDNA particularly informative. Further studies are needed to determine whether dd-cfDNA can discriminate between adenovirus from rejection and BK polyomavirus and cost effectiveness of incorporating serial dd-cfDNA monitoring into routine practice.
