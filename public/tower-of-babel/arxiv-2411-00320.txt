A miscellanea of qualitative and symmetry properties of the solutions to the two-phase Serrin’s problem
Lorenzo Cavallina

Abstract
This paper investigates the solutions to the two-phase Serrin’s problem, an overdetermined boundary value problem motivated by shape optimization. Specifically, we study the torsional rigidity of composite beams, where two distinct materials interact, and examine the properties of the optimal configurations (critical shapes) under volume constraints. We first show that such a shape optimization problem admits no local minimizers. Then, using the method of moving planes, we show that the solutions exhibit no extended or narrow branches (“tentacles”) away from the core. We then show that the outer boundary of a solution cannot exhibit flat parts and that the only configuration whose outer boundary contains a portion of a sphere is the one given by concentric balls. Finally, we establish that concentric balls are the only admissible configurations that solve the two-phase Serrin’s problem for two distinct sets of conductivity values.



Key words.

two-phase, overdetermined problem, shape-optimization problem, symmetry, method of moving planes, qualitative properties of the solutions

AMS subject classifications.

35J15, 35N25, 35Q93.

Introduction

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
(
N\geq 2
) of class (at least)
C^{1,\gamma}
for
\gamma\in(0,1]
and let
D\subset\overline{D}\subset\Omega
be a nonempty bounded open set. Throughout this paper, we will be concerned with the following overdetermined problem (which is a generalization of the famous Serrin’s overdetermined problem
[
1
]
):

\begin{cases}-{\rm div}(\sigma\nabla u)=1\quad\text{in }\Omega,\\
u=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega,\\
\partial_{n}u=c\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

(1.1)

Here
\partial_{n}
denotes the normal derivative with respect to the outward unit normal vector
n\in C^{\gamma}(\partial\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})
,
c
is a given real constant, and
\sigma
is the piece-wise constant function defined as

\sigma(x):=\begin{cases}\sigma_{c}\quad\text{if }x\in D\;\text{ (core)},\\
1\quad\text{if }x\in\Omega\setminus D\;\text{ (shell)},\end{cases}

(1.2)

for some given positive constant
\sigma_{c}\neq 1
.
Notice that, problem (
1.1
) is
overdetermined
as we are imposing two distinct boundary conditions on
\partial\Omega
at the same time. As a result, problem (
1.1
) will only admit a solution
u
for some
suitable pairs

(D,\Omega)
. Throughout this paper, when no confusion arises, we will also refer to such pairs
(D,\Omega)
as the
solutions
to the overdetermined problem (
1.1
).

When
D
is just an open set, one can give meaning to problem (
1.1
) via the following weak formulation: we say that a function
u\in H_{0}^{1}
solves (
1.1
) if it satisfies

\int_{\Omega}\sigma\nabla u\cdot\nabla\varphi=\int_{\Omega}\varphi+c\int_{\partial\Omega}\varphi

(1.3)

for all
\varphi\in H^{1}(\Omega)
.
Even though less immediate to see at first glance, the above formulation still yields an overdetermined problem, as we have also imposed the value of the trace of
u
on the boundary
\partial\Omega
by requiring
u\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
.

Remark 1.1

(Reformulation as a transmission problem)
.

Independently of the regularity of the interface
\partial D
, equation
-{\rm{div}}(\sigma\nabla u)=1
holds in the classical sense point-wise away from the interface. That is to say,
u
satisfies
-\sigma_{c}\Delta u=1
in
D
and
-\Delta u=1
in
\Omega\setminus\overline{D}
(this follows by localizing the equation in each phase by choosing suitable test functions in the weak form (
1.3
)). Moreover, if
\partial D
is sufficiently smooth,
u
satisfies the following
transmission conditions
at the interface:

\left\llbracket u\right\rrbracket=0\quad\text{on }\partial D,\quad\left\llbracket\sigma\partial_{n}u\right\rrbracket=0\quad\text{on }\partial D,

where
\left\llbracket\cdot\right\rrbracket
denotes the difference between the traces on
\partial D
from the outside and the inside respectively, and
\partial_{n}
denotes the normal derivative on
\partial D
.

Remark 1.2

(On the regularity of
\partial\Omega
)
.

Throughout this paper, we assume that
\partial\Omega
is of class
C^{1,\gamma}
for some
\gamma\in(0,1]
. Thus, by the classical Schauder boundary estimates (see
[
2
]
), the solution
u
of the Dirichlet problem given by the first two lines of (
1.1
) is of class
C^{1,\gamma}
in a neighborhood of the boundary
\partial\Omega
. In particular, if such
u
also satisfies the Neumann boundary condition in (
1.1
) (that is, if
(D,\Omega)
solves the overdetermined problem (
1.1
)), then it does so in the classical (point-wise) sense. As a result, the local regularity result
[
3
, Theorem 2]
implies that
\partial\Omega
is an analytic surface.

Overdetermined problem (
1.1
) has many physical interpretations.
For instance, when Serrin originally introduced his overdetermined problem in
[
1
]
, corresponding to the case
\sigma\equiv 1
in (
1.1
), he gave the following fluid dynamical interpretation:

“
the tangential stress on the pipe wall is the same at all points of the wall if and only if the pipe has a circular cross section.
”

Another interpretation given in
[
1
]

pertains to the linear theory of elasticity (see
[
4
]
):

“
when a solid straight bar is subject to torsion, the magnitude of the resulting traction which occurs at the surface of the bar is independent
of position if and only if the bar has a circular cross section.
”

The latter interpretation readily generalizes to the case described in (
1.1
), where the bar is a “composite”, that is, made by two materials rigidly bonded together according to the distribution given by (
1.2
).

We remark that there is a natural reason for requiring the traction to be constant on the outer surface of the bar.
Indeed, as it will be explained in further detail in section
2
, such an overdetermined condition arises as a necessary condition for the shape optimization problem of maximizing the torsional rigidity functional
T_{D}(\cdot)
under a volume constraint. Here

T_{D}(\Omega):=\int_{\Omega}\sigma|\nabla u|^{2}=\int_{\Omega}u,

where
u
, the so-called “
Prandtl stress function
”, is defined as the solution to the boundary value problem

-{\rm div}(\sigma\nabla u)=1\quad\text{in }\Omega,\qquad u=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega,

that is,
u
the unique function in
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
satisfying (
1.3
) for all
\varphi\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
. We remark that such a simplified description of torsional rigidity via Prandtl stress function is physically accurate only if the bar is sufficiently long (compared to the dimension of its cross-section) and we refer the interested reader to
[
5
]
, where the authors fruitfully use this approach to study the multi-material composites found in the morphology of plant
stems.

Finally, we briefly mention some works where similar overdetermined problems have been considered in the context of two-phase thermal conductors
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
.

In what follows, we will introduce the main results of this paper.
As shown in
[
9
]
, the radial configurations
(D_{0},\Omega_{0})
given by concentric balls are
local maximizers
for
T_{D}
under a volume constraint if
\sigma_{c}>1
and are
saddle shapes
if
\sigma_{c}<1
. The following theorem concerns the non-existence of local minimizers.

Theorem I
.

Let
(D,\Omega)
be a solution to the overdetermined problem (
1.1
). Then,
(D,\Omega)
is not a local minimizer in the sense of Definition
2.2
.

The remaining theorems concern some qualitative properties of
\partial\Omega
. In particular, the following theorem formalizes the intuitive notion that “
\Omega
follows the shape of
D
without meandering too much”.

Theorem II
.

Let
(D,\Omega)
be a solution to the overdetermined problem (
1.1
). Then
\Omega
“
has no tentacles
”, in the sense of Definition
3.1
.

The following theorem shows that
\partial\Omega
generally exhibits a non-trivial shape.

Theorem III
.

Let
(D,\Omega)
be a solution to the overdetermined problem (
1.1
). Then
\partial\Omega
does not contain any flat part.
Moreover, if
\Omega\setminus\overline{D}
is connected and
D
is an open set of class
C^{2}
with finitely many connected components, then
\partial\Omega
contains a portion of a sphere if and only if
(D,\Omega)
are concentric balls.

Finally, the following theorem shows that the shape of the free boundary
\partial\Omega
depends on the choice of the constant
\sigma_{c}
unless
(D,\Omega)
are concentric balls.

Theorem IV
.

Let
\Omega\setminus\overline{D}
be connected and assume that the boundary of the open set
D
has zero Lebesgue measure and satisfies
\partial D=\partial\overline{D}
. Under these assumptions,
(D,\Omega)
is simultaneously a solution to the overdetermined problem (
1.1
) for two different values of
\sigma_{c}
if and only if
(D,\Omega)
are concentric balls.

This paper is organized as follows.
In section
2
, we give the precise definition of
local maximizers
,
local minimizers
, and
saddle shapes
and show Theorem
I
by making use of the asymptotic behavior of the eigenvalues of a two-phase Neumann-to-Dirichlet operator. In section
3
, we give the precise definition of “
having a tentacle
” and show Theorem
II
by adapting the classical proof of Serrin to the two-phase setting. In section
4
, we show Theorem
III
by employing the analyticity of the boundary
\partial\Omega
and a symmetry result for two-phase overdetermined elliptic problems by Sakaguchi
[
7
]
. Finally, section
5
is devoted to the proof of Theorem
IV
, where the desired symmetry is obtained by applying Serrin’s symmetry result
[
1
]
to a suitably defined auxiliary function.

2
Proof of Theorem
I

Let
(D,\Omega)
be a solution to (
1.1
). As mentioned in the introduction,
\Omega
is a critical shape for the torsional rigidity functional
T_{D}
under a volume constraint. In what follows, we will give the precise definition of the three types of critical shapes: “
local maximizer
”, “
local minimizer
”, and “
saddle shape
”.

For a given
\Omega
, let
\mathcal{A}
denote the space of admissible perturbations of
\Omega
that fix the volume of
\Omega
:

\mathcal{A}:=\left\{\Phi\in C^{2}\left([0,1],C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{N})\right)\;\middle|\;\Phi(0)={\rm Id},\quad|\Omega_{t}|=|\Omega|\quad\forall t\in[0,1]\right\},

(2.1)

where
\Omega_{t}:=\Phi(t)(\Omega)=\left\{\Phi(t)(x)\;\middle|\;x\in\Omega\right\}
.
For a given
\Phi\in\mathcal{A}
, we will use the following notation concerning its expansion:

\Phi(t)={\rm{Id}}+th+o(t)\quad\text{as }t\to 0^{+},

(2.2)

for some function
h\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{N})
, where the expansion holds in the
C^{2}
norm.
Notice that, since
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\frac{d}{dt}\vphantom{|}\right|_{t=0}}|\Omega_{t}|=\int_{\partial\Omega}h\cdot n
, the fact that
|\Omega_{t}|=|\Omega|
for all
t
leads to the following first-order volume preserving condition:

\int_{\partial\Omega}h\cdot n=0\quad\text{for all }\Phi\in\mathcal{A}.

(2.3)

Remark 2.1
.

Any function
h\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{N})
satisfying (
2.3
) can be “completed” to an element
\Phi\in\mathcal{A}
in the sense of (
2.2
) (see
[
10
, Remark 2.2]
for an explicit construction).

As shown in
[
9
, Theorem 3.3]
, for any
\Phi\in\mathcal{A}
, we have

{\left.\kern-1.2pt\frac{d}{dt}\vphantom{|}\right|_{t=0}}T_{D}(\Omega_{t})=\int_{\partial\Omega}(\partial_{n}u)^{2}\ (h\cdot n).

(2.4)

As a result,
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\frac{d}{dt}\vphantom{|}\right|_{t=0}}T_{D}(\Omega_{t})=0
holds true for all
\Phi\in\mathcal{A}
if and only if the function
(\partial_{n}u)^{2}
belongs to the orthogonal complement in
L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
of the subspace of functions with zero average. In other words,
\Omega
is a critical shape for
T_{D}
under a volume constraint if and only if
(\partial_{n}u)^{2}
is constant on
\partial\Omega
, which in turn holds if and only if
u
is a solution to the overdetermined problem (
1.1
) (as a consequence of Hopf’s boundary lemma).

Analogously, if
(D,\Omega)
is a solution to (
1.1
), by arguing as in
[
9
, Theorem 4.2]
, we get the following expression of the second order derivative:

{\left.\kern-1.2pt\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\vphantom{|}\right|_{t=0}}T_{D}(\Omega_{t})=2c\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{n}u^{\prime}\ (h\cdot n)+2c\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial^{2}_{nn}u\ (h\cdot n)^{2},

(2.5)

where
u^{\prime}
(the so-called
shape derivative of
u
) is the solution to the following boundary value problem:

\begin{cases}-{\rm{div}}(\sigma\nabla u^{\prime})=0\quad\text{in }\Omega,\\
u^{\prime}=-c\ (h\cdot n)\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

(2.6)

Based on (
2.5
), we give the following definitions:

Definition 2.2
.

Let
(D,\Omega)
be a solution to (
1.1
). We say that

•

(D,\Omega)
is a
local maximizer
if for all
\Phi\in\mathcal{A}
,
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\vphantom{|}\right|_{t=0}}T_{D}(\Omega_{t})\leq 0
,

•

(D,\Omega)
is a
local minimizer
if for all
\Phi\in\mathcal{A}
,
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\vphantom{|}\right|_{t=0}}T_{D}(\Omega_{t})\geq 0
,

•

(D,\Omega)
is a
saddle shape
if there exists two distinct perturbations in
\mathcal{A}
for which the quantity
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\vphantom{|}\right|_{t=0}}T_{D}(\Omega_{t})
has opposite signs.

Moreover, we will add the word “strict” to the terminologies above, whenever we need to emphasize that the inequalities above are satisfied in the strict sense.

It is known (see
[
9
, Theorem 4.5]
) that concentric balls are local maximizers if
\sigma_{c}>1
and saddle shapes if
\sigma_{c}<1
in the sense of Definition
2.2
. Moreover, by a perturbation analysis relying on the implicit function theorem for Banach spaces
[
11
,
12
]
and a bifurcation analysis by the Crandall–Rabinowitz theorem
[
13
]
, one can show the existence of many other nontrivial (non radially symmetric) local maximizers and saddle shapes
(D,\Omega)
.

In what follows, we will show that local minimizers in the sense of Definition
2.2
actually do not exist, proving Theorem
I
.

Proof of Theorem
I
.

Consider the following Neumann-to-Dirichlet operator:

\displaystyle\Lambda:

\displaystyle L_{*}^{2}(\partial\Omega)\to H_{*}^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)\hookrightarrow L_{*}^{2}(\partial\Omega)

\displaystyle\xi\longmapsto{\left.\kern-1.2ptv\vphantom{|}\right|_{\partial\Omega}},

where the subscript
*
indicates the subspace of functions with zero average on
\partial\Omega
and
v\in H^{1}(\Omega)
denotes the unique solution to the Neumann boundary value problem

\begin{cases}-{\rm{div}}(\sigma\nabla v)=0\quad\text{in }\Omega,\\
\partial_{n}v=\xi\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

(2.7)

normalized so that
\int_{\partial\Omega}v=0
. In other words,
v
is the unique function in
H_{*}^{1}(\Omega)
that satisfies the following weak formulation:

\int_{\Omega}\sigma\nabla v\cdot\nabla\varphi=\int_{\partial\Omega}\xi\varphi\quad\text{for all }\varphi\in H^{1}(\Omega).

(2.8)

By the trace theorem
[
14
, Theorem 1.5.1.3]
,
\Lambda
is a well-defined bounded operator from
L_{*}^{2}(\partial\Omega)
into
H_{*}^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
. Thus, by the compactness of the embedding
H_{*}^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)\hookrightarrow L_{*}^{2}(\partial\Omega)
,
\Lambda
becomes a compact operator from
L_{*}^{2}(\partial\Omega)
into itself.
Let us now show that
\Lambda:L_{*}^{2}(\partial\Omega)\to L_{*}^{2}(\partial\Omega)
is self-adjoint. To this end, take two arbitrary functions
\xi,\eta\in L_{*}^{2}(\partial\Omega)
and let
v,w\in H_{*}^{1}(\Omega)
be the corresponding normalized solutions to the Neumann boundary value problem (
2.8
). Taking
w
and
v
as test functions in the problems for
v
and
w
respectively yields

\langle\xi,\Lambda\eta\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}=\int_{\partial\Omega}\xi w=\int_{\Omega}\sigma\nabla v\cdot\nabla w=\int_{\partial\Omega}v\eta=\langle\Lambda\xi,\eta\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)},

whence
\Lambda
is a compact self-adjoint operator from
L_{*}^{2}(\partial\Omega)
into itself. As a result, by the spectral theorem for compact self-adjoint operators
[
15
, Theorem 6.11]
, there exists an orthonormal basis of eigenfunctions
\{\xi_{k}\}_{k}\subset L_{*}^{2}(\partial\Omega)
whose eigenvalues
(\lambda_{k})_{k}
converge to
0
as
k\to\infty
(see also
[
15
, Theorem 6.8]
).
If we set
v_{k}\in H_{*}^{1}(\Omega)
to be the solution to the Neumann boundary value problem (
2.8
) for
\xi=\xi_{k}
, we have:

\lambda_{k}\xi_{k}=\Lambda\xi_{k}={\left.\kern-1.2ptv_{k}\vphantom{|}\right|_{\partial\Omega}}.

(2.9)

It is easy to show that
\lambda_{k}>0
for all
k
. Indeed, be taking
v_{k}
as a test function in (
2.8
),

\lambda_{k}=\langle\Lambda\xi_{k},\xi_{k}\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}=\int_{\Omega}\sigma|\nabla v_{k}|^{2}\geq 0.

Now, if the right-hand side above were equal to zero, the function
v_{k}\in H_{*}^{1}(\Omega)
would be constant. As a result, (
2.8
) would imply that
\xi_{k}\equiv 0
, in contrast with
\xi_{k}
being an element of an orthonormal basis.

Finally, by following
[
14
, Theorem 2.5.1.1]
, one can bootstrap the regularity of the functions
v_{k}
to the class
W^{m,p}
near the boundary
\partial\Omega
for arbitrarily large
m
. As a result,
\xi_{k}\in C^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)
and, by Remark
2.1
, the function
h_{k}:=\xi_{k}n\in C^{\infty}(\partial\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})
can be “completed” to an element
\Phi_{k}\in\mathcal{A}
.

Consider now the solution
u^{\prime}_{k}
to (
2.6
) with respect to such
\Phi_{k}
. By comparing problems (
2.6
) and (
2.7
) with (
2.9
) at hand, we get:

u^{\prime}_{k}=-\frac{c}{\lambda_{k}}v_{k}\quad\text{in }\Omega.

Thus, by (
2.9
),

\partial_{n}u^{\prime}_{k}=-\frac{c}{\lambda_{k}}\partial_{n}v_{k}=-\frac{c}{\lambda_{k}}\xi_{k}\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega.

Finally, by (
2.5
), we can estimate the value of the second order derivative of
T_{D}(\Omega_{t})
corresponding to the perturbation
\Phi_{k}
as follows:

{\left.\kern-1.2pt\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\vphantom{|}\right|_{t=0}}T_{D}(\Omega_{t})=2c\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{-c}{\lambda_{k}}\xi_{k}^{2}+2c\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial^{2}_{nn}u\ \xi_{k}^{2}\leq-\frac{2c^{2}}{\lambda_{k}}+2c\min_{\partial\Omega}\partial^{2}_{nn}u,

where, in the last inequality, we have used the fact that
c<0
.
Notice that, for sufficiently large
k
, the right-hand side above becomes negative. Hence,
(D,\Omega)
cannot be a local minimizer in the sense of Definition
2.2
, concluding the proof of Theorem
I
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
II

In this section, we will show Theorem
II
. To this end, first, we will give the precise definition of “
having a tentacle
”.

Let
(D,\Omega)
be a solution to the overdetermined problem (
1.1
) and, (if necessary) up to a change of coordinates, let
\Pi_{\lambda}
denote the hyperplane

\Pi_{\lambda}:=\left\{(\lambda,x_{2},\dots,x_{N})\;\middle|\;(x_{2},\dots,x_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1}\right\}.

In what follows we are going to move the hyperplane
\Pi_{\lambda}
by slowly decreasing the parameter
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.
As a starting position, assume that
\lambda
is large enough so that
\overline{\Omega}
sits on the “left” of
\Pi_{\lambda}
, that is
\overline{\Omega}\subset\left\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\;\middle|\;x_{1}<\lambda\right\}
. Then it is possible to move
\Pi_{\lambda}
to the “left” by decreasing the value of
\lambda
until
\partial\Omega
and
\Pi_{\lambda}
first touch for some
\lambda=\lambda_{0}
. Now, for even smaller values of
\lambda
, we obtain a nonempty portion of
\Omega
lying on the “right” side of
\Pi_{\lambda}
. We are interested in the reflection of this portion with respect to the hyperplane
\Pi_{\lambda}
:

\Omega_{\lambda}:=\left\{x_{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\;\middle|\;x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\in\Omega,\quad x_{1}>\lambda\right\},

where
x_{\lambda}
denotes the reflection of the point
x
with respect to the hyperplane
\Pi_{\lambda}
:

x_{\lambda}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})_{\lambda}:=(2\lambda-x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{N}).

Notice that, since
\partial\Omega
is sufficiently smooth by Remark
1.2
, the set
\Omega_{\lambda}
lies inside
\Omega\setminus\overline{D}
if
\lambda
is smaller but sufficiently close to
\lambda_{0}
. One can keep decreasing the value of
\lambda
until (at least) one of the following happens:

(i)

\overline{\Omega_{\lambda}}
touches
\partial\overline{D}
at some point
P
.

(ii)

\overline{\Omega_{\lambda}}
touches
\partial\Omega
at some point
P\notin\Pi_{\lambda}
.

(iii)

\Pi_{\lambda}
meets
\partial\Omega
orthogonally at some point
P
.

Definition 3.1
.

We say that the pair
(D,\Omega)
“
has a tentacle
” if there exists some change of coordinates such that either case
(ii)
or case
(iii)
above occurs. On the other hand, if, for all changes of coordinates, cases
(ii)-(iii)
never occur, we say that the pair
(D,\Omega)
“
has no tentacles
”.

Proof of Theorem
II
.

By way of contradiction, suppose that there exists a change of coordinates for which either case
(ii)
or case
(iii)
happens.
In either case, let
\lambda
denote the exact value of the parameter at which either case
(ii)
or case
(iii)
happens and consider the following auxiliary function:

w_{\lambda}:=u-u_{\lambda}\quad\text{in }\Omega_{\lambda},

where

u_{\lambda}(x):=u(x_{\lambda}),\quad\text{for }x\in\Omega_{\lambda}.

By construction,
w_{\lambda}
solves the following boundary value problem:

\begin{cases}\Delta w_{\lambda}=0\quad\text{in }\Omega_{\lambda},\\
w_{\lambda}=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega_{\lambda}\cap\Pi_{\lambda},\\
w_{\lambda}=u\geq 0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega_{\lambda}\setminus\Pi_{\lambda}.\end{cases}

(3.1)

Moreover, notice that, since
D\neq\emptyset
,
\Omega
is not symmetric with respect to
\Pi_{\lambda}
, thus, there exists some point
Q\in\partial\Omega_{\lambda}\cap\Omega\setminus\Pi_{\lambda}
. In particular,
w_{\lambda}(Q)>0
and thus
w_{\lambda}
is strictly positive inside
\Omega_{\lambda}
.

First, let us assume that case
(ii)
happens.
In this case, the harmonic function
w_{\lambda}
attains its minimum at the boundary point
P
, where it vanishes. Thus, by Hopf’s boundary lemma,
\partial_{n}w_{\lambda}<0
at
P
. On the other hand, since
u
solves (
1.1
) by assumption, we have

\partial_{n}w_{\lambda}(P)=\partial_{n}u(P)-\partial_{n}u(P_{\lambda})=c-c=0,

leading to a contradiction.

Let now assume that case
(iii)
happens.
In this case, Hopf’s boundary lemma is insufficient and we will resort to Serrin’s corner Lemma
[
1
, Lemma 1]
. We will show that, if case
(iii)
happens, the point
P
is a second-order zero for
w
, that is,
w
and all its first and second-order derivatives vanish at
P
. If this is the case, then
[
1
, Lemma 1]
would imply
w\equiv 0
in
\Omega_{\lambda}
, leading to a contradiction.

We will now show that
P
is a second order zero for the function
w_{\lambda}
. To this end, let us consider a coordinate system
(\xi,\eta)=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{N-1},\eta)\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1}\times\mathbb{R}
with the origin at
P
, the
\eta
axis pointing in the direction
n
and the
\xi_{1}
axis orthogonal to
\Pi_{\lambda}
. In what follows, for any given scalar function
\varphi
, the “full” gradient and Hessian matrix with respect to the
N
variables
(\xi,\eta)=(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\dots,\xi_{N-1},\eta)
will be denoted by
\nabla\varphi
and
D^{2}\varphi
respectively, while
\nabla_{\xi}\varphi
and
D^{2}_{\xi}\varphi
will denote the following:

\nabla_{\xi}\varphi:=\left(\partial_{\xi_{1}}\varphi,\partial_{\xi_{2}}\varphi,\dots,\partial_{\xi_{N-1}}\varphi\right),\quad D_{\xi}^{2}\varphi:=\left(\partial^{2}_{\xi_{i},\xi_{j}}\varphi\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq N-1}.

There exists a smooth function
f:\mathbb{R}^{N-1}\to\mathbb{R}
such that a small portion of
\partial\Omega
near
P
can be locally expressed as the graph of
f
, as follows:

\left\{\big(\xi,f(\xi)\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1}\times\mathbb{R}\;\middle|\;|\xi\rvert<\varepsilon\right\}.

In particular, since we are assuming that
\Pi_{\lambda}
meets
\partial\Omega
orthogonally at
P
, we have

\nabla_{\xi}f(0)=0.

(3.2)

Using the local parametrization introduced above, the condition
u\equiv 0
on
\partial\Omega
can be rewritten as

u(\xi,f(\xi))=0,\quad\text{for }|\xi|<\varepsilon.

(3.3)

Now, differentiating (
3.3
) with respect to
\xi
yields

\nabla_{\xi}u(\xi,f(\xi))+\partial_{\eta}u(\xi,f(\xi))\nabla_{\xi}f(\xi)=0.

(3.4)

Computing (
3.4
)
at the point
P
with (
3.2
) at hand yields
\nabla_{\xi}u(P)=0
. This fact, combined with
c=\partial_{\eta}u(P)=\partial_{\eta}u_{\lambda}(P)
, ensures that all first derivatives of
u
and
u_{\lambda}
coincide at
P
, thus
\nabla w_{\lambda}(P)=0
, as claimed.

In what follows, we will show that also
D^{2}w(P)=0
. To this end, notice that in the new coordinate system, we have

u_{\lambda}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\dots,\xi_{N-1},\eta)=u(-\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\dots,\xi_{N-1},\eta).

In particular, this implies

\displaystyle\partial^{2}_{\ell\ell}u(P)=\partial^{2}_{\ell\ell}u_{\lambda}(P)\quad\text{for }\ell\in\{\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{N-1},\eta\},

\displaystyle\partial_{\xi_{1}}\partial_{\ell}u(P)=-\partial_{\xi_{1}}\partial_{\ell}u_{\lambda}(P)\quad\text{for }\ell\in\{\xi_{2},\dots,\xi_{N-1},\eta\}.

We are now left to show that all mixed derivatives with respect to
\xi_{1}
and
\ell\in\{\xi_{2},\dots,\xi_{N-1},\eta\}
of
u
at
P
vanish.
To this end, differentiate (
3.4
) with respect to
\xi
to get

D^{2}_{\xi}u+2\partial_{\eta}\nabla_{\xi}u\otimes\nabla_{\xi}f+\partial^{2}_{\eta\eta}u\nabla_{\xi}f\otimes\nabla_{\xi}=0.

Evaluating this at the point
P
with (
3.2
) at hand yields the desired

D^{2}_{\xi}u(P)=0.

This leaves us with just one equality to show, that is
\partial_{\xi_{1}}\partial_{\eta}u(P)=0
. To this end, notice that the outward normal
n
admits the local expression

n(\xi,f(\xi))=\frac{\left(-\nabla_{\xi}f(\xi),1\right)}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla_{\xi}f(\xi)|^{2}}},\quad\text{for }|\xi|<\varepsilon.

Therefore, the overdetermined condition
\partial_{n}u\equiv c
on
\partial\Omega
in local coordinates reads

-\nabla_{\xi}u(\xi,f(\xi))\cdot\nabla_{\xi}f(\xi)+\partial_{\eta}u(\xi,f(\xi))=c\sqrt{1+|\nabla_{\xi}f(\xi)|^{2}},\quad\text{for }|\xi|<\varepsilon.

(3.5)

Differentiating (
3.5
) with respect to
\xi
yields

-D^{2}_{\xi}u\nabla_{\xi}f-(\partial_{\eta}\nabla_{\xi}u\otimes\nabla_{\xi}f)\nabla_{\xi}f+\nabla_{\xi}\partial_{\eta}u+\partial^{2}_{\eta\eta}u\nabla_{\xi}f=\frac{cD^{2}_{\xi}f\nabla_{\xi}f}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla_{\xi}f|^{2}}}.

Finally, evaluating the above at
P
with (
3.2
) at hand yields
\nabla_{\xi}\partial_{\eta}u(P)=0
, whence, in particular, the desired
\partial_{\xi_{1}}\partial_{\eta}u(P)=0
.
To summarize, in the above, we have shown that all first and second-order derivatives of
u
and
u_{\lambda}
coincide at
P
, whence

\nabla w_{\lambda}(P),\quad w_{\lambda}(P)=0,\quad D^{2}w_{\lambda}(P)=0.

As remarked before, by Serrin’s corner Lemma
[
1
, Lemma 1]
, this is only possible if
w_{\lambda}\equiv 0
in
\Omega_{\lambda}
. As this contradicts the fact that
w_{\lambda}
is strictly positive inside
\Omega_{\lambda}
, the proof is completed.
∎

4
Proof of Theorem
III

In what follows, we will show Theorem
III
. Let
(D,\Omega)
be a solution to the overdetermined problem (
1.1
). Recall that, by Remark
1.2
,
\partial\Omega
is an analytic surface.

If
\partial\Omega
were to contain a flat part, then, by unique continuation,
\partial\Omega
would contain a hyperplane, contradicting the fact that
\Omega
is bounded.

Similarly, if
\partial\Omega
were to contain a portion of a sphere, then it would contain an entire sphere
S
as a subset (where both
u\equiv 0
and
\partial_{n}u\equiv c
hold). Now, by the Cauchy–Kovalevskaya theorem
[
16
, Subsection 4.6.3]
,
u
is radial (with respect to the center of
S
, which, without loss of generality, we will assume to be the origin) in a neighborhood of
S
. Then, since
u
is real analytic in the connected open set
\Omega\setminus\overline{D}
,
u
must be radial in the whole
\Omega\setminus\overline{D}
. As
\partial\Omega
is a level set of
u
, this leaves us with two possibilities:

(i)\quad\text{$\Omega$ is a ball $\{|x|<R\}$}\quad\text{or}\quad(ii)\quad\text{$\Omega$ is an annulus $\{R_{1}<|x|<R_{2}\}$.}

First, we will show that
(ii)
cannot occur. Indeed, if
(ii)
were true, the function
U(|x|):=u(x)
would solve

U^{\prime\prime}+\frac{N-1}{r}U^{\prime}=-1\quad\text{in }(R_{1},R_{2}),\quad U(R_{1})=U(R_{2})=0.

Thus, by elementary computations, for any
x\in\overline{\Omega}\setminus D
we would have:

u(x)=U(|x|)=\begin{cases}\frac{R_{1}^{2}-R_{2}^{2}}{2N(R_{1}^{2-N}-R_{2}^{2-N})}|x|^{2-N}+\frac{R_{1}^{2}R_{2}^{2}(R_{1}^{-N}-R_{2}^{-N})}{2N(R_{1}^{2-N}-R_{2}^{2-N})}-\frac{|x|^{2}}{2N}\quad&\text{if }N\geq 3,\\[7.0pt]
\frac{R_{1}^{2}R_{2}^{2}}{4\log\frac{R_{1}}{R_{2}}}\log|x|+\frac{R_{2}^{2}\log R_{1}-R_{1}^{2}\log R_{2}}{4\log\frac{R_{1}}{R_{2}}}-\frac{|x|^{2}}{4}\quad&\text{if }N=2.\end{cases}

Nevertheless, it is easy to check that the function above does not satisfy

{\left.\kern-1.2pt\partial_{n}u\vphantom{|}\right|_{|x|=R_{1}}}={\left.\kern-1.2pt\partial_{n}u\vphantom{|}\right|_{|x|=R_{2}}}

for any choice of
0<R_{1}<R_{2}
, contradicting the fact that
u
must solve (
1.1
).

As a result, the only possibility left is that
\Omega
is a ball, in which case we conclude by Sakaguchi’s symmetry result
[
7
, Theorem 5.1]
.

5
Proof of Theorem
IV

In what follows, we will show Theorem
IV
.
Assume that there exist two distinct positive constants
\alpha\neq\beta
such that the overdetermined problem (
1.1
) is solvable in the same pair
(D,\Omega)
for both values
\sigma_{c}=\alpha,\beta
. Let
u_{\alpha},u_{\beta}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
denote the respective solutions to the following.

\displaystyle\alpha\int_{D}\nabla u_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla\varphi+\int_{\Omega\setminus\overline{D}}\nabla u_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla\varphi=\int_{\Omega}\varphi\quad\text{for all }\varphi\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega),

(5.1)

\displaystyle\beta\int_{D}\nabla u_{\beta}\cdot\nabla\varphi+\int_{\Omega\setminus\overline{D}}\nabla u_{\beta}\cdot\nabla\varphi=\int_{\Omega}\varphi\quad\text{for all }\varphi\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega).

As (
1.1
) is solvable by assumption, both
u_{\alpha}
and
u_{\beta}
satisfy the overdetermined condition

\partial_{n}u_{\alpha}\equiv\partial_{n}u_{\beta}\equiv c\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega.

(5.2)

As a result of (
5.2
), since
\partial\Omega
is an analytic surface by Remark
1.2
, the Cauchy–Kovalevskaya theorem
[
16
, Subsection 4.6.3]
and the identity theorem for real analytic functions imply that
u_{\alpha}
and
u_{\beta}
coincide inside the connected set
\Omega\setminus\overline{D}
. In particular, since
u_{\alpha}
and
u_{\beta}
are continuous inside
\Omega
by
[
17
, Theorem 8.22]
, equality extends to their traces on
\partial(\Omega\setminus\overline{D})\cap\Omega=\partial\overline{D}=\partial D
:

{\left.\kern-1.2ptu_{\alpha}\vphantom{|}\right|_{\partial D}}={\left.\kern-1.2ptu_{\beta}\vphantom{|}\right|_{\partial D}}.

(5.3)

Now, consider the function
v:=\alpha u_{\alpha}-\beta u_{\beta}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
. By subtracting both rows in (
5.1
) and taking
\varphi=v
, we get

\int_{D}|\nabla v|^{2}=0,

whence
v
is locally constant in
D
.
Following the definition of
v
, we get

{\left.\kern-1.2ptv\vphantom{|}\right|_{\partial D}}={\left.\kern-1.2pt(\alpha u_{\alpha}-\beta u_{\beta})\vphantom{|}\right|_{\partial D}}=(\alpha-\beta){\left.\kern-1.2ptu_{\alpha}\vphantom{|}\right|_{\partial D}},

(5.4)

where we have used (
5.3
) in the last equality.

Consider now the function
w\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
defined as

w:=u_{\alpha}+(\alpha-1)E_{D}\left({\left.\kern-1.2ptu_{\alpha}\vphantom{|}\right|_{D}}-\frac{1}{\alpha-\beta}{\left.\kern-1.2ptv\vphantom{|}\right|_{D}}\right),

where
E_{D}:H_{0}^{1}(D)\hookrightarrow H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
denotes the extension-by-zero operator (see
[
18
, 3.26 and the subsequent results]
).
As
v
is locally constant in
D
, we have

\nabla w=\begin{cases}\alpha\nabla u_{\alpha}\quad\text{in }D,\\
\nabla u_{\alpha}\quad\text{in }\Omega\setminus\overline{D}.\end{cases}

Therefore, by the first identity in (
5.1
) it is immediate to see that

\int_{\Omega}\nabla w\cdot\nabla\varphi=\int_{\Omega}\varphi\quad\text{for all }\varphi\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega).

Also, since
w
coincides with
u_{\alpha}
in
\Omega\setminus\overline{D}
, we have
\partial_{n}w\equiv\partial_{n}u_{\alpha}\equiv c
on
\partial\Omega
. In other words,
\Omega
solves the one-phase Serrin’s overdetermined problem. As a result,
\Omega
is a ball (of radius
R=-cN
) and thus

u_{\alpha}(x)=u_{\beta}(x)=w(x)=\frac{R^{2}-|x|^{2}}{2N}\quad\text{for }x\in\Omega\setminus\overline{D}.

(5.5)

Moreover, by continuity, the expression in (
5.5
) holds true up to the boundary
\partial(\Omega\setminus\overline{D})=\partial\Omega\cup\partial\overline{D}=\partial\Omega\cup\partial D
.
Combining this with (
5.4
) yields:

v(x)=(\alpha-\beta)\frac{R^{2}-|x|^{2}}{2N}\quad\text{for }x\in\partial D.

(5.6)

Recall now that the continuous function
v
is locally constant in
D
, thus being constant on the boundary of each connected component of
D
. By (
5.6
), the boundary of any connected component of
D
lies inside some sphere concentric with
\partial\Omega
. We conclude that
D
must have exactly one connected component: a ball concentric with
\Omega
. This concludes the proof of the theorem.
