Popping Bubbles in Pangenome Graphs
Njagi Mwaniki, Erik Garrison, Nadia Pisanti

Abstract
In this paper we introduce flubbles , a new definition of "bubbles" that correspond to variants in a (pan)genome graph G G . We then show a characterization for flubbles in terms of equivalence classes regarding cycles in an intermediate data structure we built from the spanning tree of the G G , which leads us to a linear time and space solution for finding all flubbles. Furthermore, we show how a related characterization allows us to efficiently detect also what we define hairpin inversions : a cycle preceded and followed by the same path in the graph; being the latter necessarily traversed both ways, this structure corresponds to inversions. Finally, Inspired by the concept of Program Structure Tree introduced fifty years ago to represent the hierarchy of the control structure of a program, we define a tree representing the structure of G G in terms of flubbles, the flubble tree , which we also find in linear time. The hierarchy of variants introduced by the flubble tree paves the way to new investigations of (pan)genomes structures and their decomposition for practical analyses. We have implemented our methods into a prototype tool named povu which we tested on human and yeast data. We show that povu can find the flubbles, and also output the flubble tree, while being as fast (or faster than) well established tools that find bubbles such as vg and BubbleGun. Moreover, we show how, within the same time, povu can find hairpin inversions that, to the best of our knowledge, no other tool are able to find. Our tool is freely available at https://github.com/urbanslug/povu/ under the MIT License.

Introduction

A
pangenome
was defined in
[
9
]
as
any collection of genomic sequences to be analyzed jointly or to be used as a reference
in 2018.
Since then, computational pangenomics faces the algorithmic issues of the paradigm shift from linear reference genomes, to more complex graph-like structures required for pangenomes, with the suggestion of new pangenome representations and new tools to investigate them.
On the representation side, many graph-like structures that can be directed or not directed, labelled or not labelled, coloured or not coloured, and acyclic or not, have been suggested.
Furthermore, for each possible representation efficient tools to solve basic toolkit problems such as mapping a read therein, as well as more general tasks such as detecting variants, have also been suggested.

The challenge of a trade off emerges: on one hand the choice of complex structures that allow to represent more kinds of genomic variants at a cost of being hard to analyze (and to visualize), and on the other hand that of simpler linearized versions that fail to express structural variants but exhibit algorithmic advantage supporting fast search therein.

In any of the possible pangenome graph representation, a genomic variant is a so-called
bubble
: a set of distinct possible paths (the variants) that share the same contexts at their sides. The notion of bubble
[
13
]
has been extensively investigated in the literature even before computational pangenomics was conceived. Even for bubbles we have witnessed an explosion of possible definitions and of possible algorithms to detect them: superbubbles
[
26
]
, ultrabubbles
[
37
]
, mouths
[
31
]
, snarl
[
28
]
, if not more. The algorithms that have been suggested to find these structures are either quadratic or bound to work on acyclic graphs.

In this paper we provide an overview of some of the main notions of both (pan)genome graphs and bubbles. Then we choose
variation graphs
, that are (equivalent to one of) the most general pangenome graph representations, and we define therein a new notion of bubble, which we name
flubble
.
We thus describe an intermediate data structure that we build from the spanning tree of the variation graph, and we give a characterization for flubbles in terms of equivalence classes regarding cycles such data structure, which leads us to a linear time and space solution for finding all flubbles.
Furthermore, we show how a related characterization allows us to efficiently detect also what we defined
hairpin inversions
: a cycle preceded and followed by the same path in the graph; being the latter necessarily traversed both ways, and hence one forward and one reverse (one before and one after the cycle), this structure corresponds to inversions.
Finally, Inspired by the concept of Program Structure Tree introduced in
[
19
]
with the purpose of representing the hierarchy of the control structure of a program, we define a tree representing the structure of a variation graph in terms of flubbles, the
flubble tree
, which we also find in linear time. In our tree, nodes are flubbles, whereas edges are nesting relations among them.
By extending an idea of
[
28
]
for decomposing graphs in terms of bubbles, we propose the flubble tree to drive a progressive
popping
of flubbles to decompose complex pangenome graphs into simpler structures.
We have implemented our methods into a prototype tool named povu which we tested on human and yeast data. We show that povu can find the flubbles, and also output the flubble tree, while being as fast (or faster than) well established tools that find bubbles such as vg and BubbleGun. Moreover, we show how, within the same time, povu can find hairpin inversions that, to the best of our knowledge, no other tool is able to find.

In a

The set

As shown in

Conversely, to go from a biedged graph to a bidirected graph one would only need to collapse each pair of nodes

Bidirected and biedged graphs have been introduced with the aim of allowing both forward and reverse traversal of nodes according to whether we are considering the forward or reverse strand of DNA (in bidirected graph labels are associated to vertices, whereas in biedged graphs they are associated to black edges).

In this paper we refer to Variation Graphs

A

Bubbles

The concept of bubble in a graph was introduced in
[
13
]
as
"a region in which multiple paths exist" in a directed graph. In
[
28
]
a bubble is defined as a pair of paths that connect a common source node
s
to a common target node
t
that are otherwise disjoint (that is they do not share any node other than
s
and
t
).
Bubbles were also defined as

for the special case of SNPs in de Bruijn Graphs of order
k
as two distinct paths of length
k
from a common source vertex to a common target vertex
[
31
]
.
The concept was later extended to allow multiple paths from
s
to
t
, and/or the case
s=t
, and possibly nested structures
[
5
]
. An important step towards efficiency came in
[
26
]
with the definition of
superbubbles
as their properties restrict their number to
\mathcal{O}(n)
where
n=|V|
. Superbubbles are defined as follows in a directed graph where we say that a node
v
is
reachable
from a node
u
if there is a walk from
u
to
v
.
A
superbubble

[
26
]
is as any pair of distinct vertices
<\!s,t\!>
in a directed graph which satisfy the following properties:

•

reachable
:
t
is reachable from
s
,

•

matches
: the set of vertices
U
reachable from
s
without passing through
t
is equal to the set of vertices from which
t
is reachable without passing by
s
,

•

acyclic
: the subgraph induced by
U
is acyclic, and

•

minimal
: no vertex in
U
, other than
t
, forms a pair with
s
that satisfies the conditions above.

The definition of superbubbles was then extended to biedged graphs in
[
28
]
: (i) a
snarl
is defined as the minimal subgraph of a biedged graph with the
2BEC
property (that is, being
2-black-edges connected
), which means that it cannot be disconnected by removing less than
2
black edges (assuming grey edges cannot be removed), and (ii) an
ultrabubble
is tip-free acyclic snarl (a
tip
in a biedged graph is any node that is not incident with a grey edge
[
37
]
).

The motivation for all above mentioned notions of bubble that have been suggested, as well as for the many algorithms and tools that have been devised to enumerate them, is that they represent genomic variants as: (i) they correspond to subgraphs that are identified by a pair of nodes that correspond to the common left and right context of the variant, and (ii) the possible traversals of this subgraph spell distinct sequences that indeed represent the variants.

4
The Algorithm povu

Our algorithm povu takes in input a Variation Graph
G=(V,E)
. We assume without loss of generality that
G
is compact, that is there are no linear chains containing more than one black edge: should this be the case, they could be collapsed into a single black edge obtaining an equivalent graph.

As a first step, should
G
not be connected, povu decomposes it into connected components to be processed separately. Therefore, from now on we (can) assume that the graph
G
is connected.

As a second step, povu detects
tips
and creates as many dummy (grey) edges as the number of detected tips to connect each one of them to a suitably created dummy node
s
. Should
G
not have tips, let
s
be any node of
G
.

Third, a (pre-order) DFS spanning tree

is computed for

using

as a root.

Before describing our methods to detect hairpins and compute the flubble tree, we make the following observations and definitions:

•

The spanning tree

introduces a partial order

: we say that

if
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
belong to the same root-leaf path

and
v_{1}
is closer to
s
.

•

We remark that any edge

is such that
u\!\prec\!v
or
v\!\prec\!u
. We name these edges

, and we denote with
T^{\prime}_{s}
the tree

augmented with back-edges (that is,

is basically
G
with the partial order
\prec
).

•

A

of a tree edge
e\in T_{s}
is a back-edge of
T^{\prime}_{s}
connecting a descendant of
e
to an ancestor of
e
.

•

Two edges
e_{1},e_{2}\in T_{s}
are cycle equivalent in
T^{\prime}_{s}
if and only if they contain the same sets of brackets.

4.2
The Flubble Tree

Rather than just enumerating the flubbles, our algorithm implicitly lists them as nodes of a tree
T_{F}
, the
flubble tree
, which will additionally describe the nesting structure of the flubbles of
G
.

Flubbles are found by assigning cycle equivalence classes
2
2

2

A cycle equivalence class is a set where all elements are pairwise cycle equivalent. This is indeed an equivalence class.
to edges in
T^{\prime}_{s}
, and thus detecting a flubble per each equivalence class.
We will identify a flubble of
G
with a pair
<\!e_{1},e_{2}\!>
(named

) of distinct black edges of
T_{s}
(and hence of

) that are cycle equivalent in

The flubble boundaries are detected in linear time by traversing

This requirements implicitly ensures the following properties we desire for a flubble:

•

Being

a DFS spanning tree ensures that the back-edges will not overlap eachother, and hence edges will lie in cycle equivalence classes.

•

Being
T_{s}
a DFS spanning tree, and being
G
compact, these two edge belong to the same root-leaf path in
T_{s}
, and there are multiple paths connecting these two edges.

•

The pair
<\!e_{1},e_{2}\!>
make

More importantly, since we can prove that the number of back-edges is upper bounded by (and actually strictly less than) the number of edges in

Theorem 4.1

In a biedged graph

, there are at most a
|E|
flubbles.

Corollary 1

The flubble tree can be computed in linear time in the size of

5
Decomposing Pangenome Graphs via the Flubble Tree

The topological properties of
T_{F}
will necessarily reflect the (pan)genome that
G
represents. The leaves of
T_{F}
correspond to
minimal
flubbles, that is, those that do not contain any other flubble: these can be SNPs or INDELs or in general a list of alternative strings. Should there be a chain of them, these will be siblings leaves whose parent is a flubble that contains them. Hence, the wider the tree is, the more consecutive flubbles there are. Furthermore, the deeper
T_{F}
is, and the more nested bubbles there are. An interesting use of the flubble tree could be to use it as representing structural features of a (pan)genome.

Another possible use of the flubble tree could be to drive a sort of progressive decomposition of a (pan)genome graph by
popping
the flubbles via a post-order visit of
T_{F}
.
Indeed, computational pangenomics
[
9
]
is facing the challenge of finding the trade off between pangenomes representations that allow to highlight complex variants
[
15
,
33
,
24
,
7
]
but are computationally difficult to index or to search
[
11
]
, and others - such as (E)D strings
[
2
,
16
]
or founder graphs
[
32
]
- that have limited expressive power but allow fast pattern matching
[
22
,
6
,
3
]
(also in the presence of errors
[
4
,
23
,
22
]
), and/or indexing
[
32
]
. A post-order visit of the flubble tree could drive a processing of complex graphs that begin by collapsing (linear chains) of minimal flubbles into structures that are easier to handle (for example replacing the labels string in labels with ED strings), and then prune
T_{F}

popping
these leaves (and possibly iterate the process in a progressive manner) turning the graph into a hybrid representation that could combine the benefit of complex graphs and ED strings.
