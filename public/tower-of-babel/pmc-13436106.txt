Exploring sources of job satisfaction and work-related stressors experienced by organ and tissue donation coordinators in Canada: a qualitative analysis
Authors: Vanessa Silva e Silva, Ken Lotherington, Sonny Dhanani, Kendra-Lee Dupuis, Amina Regina Silva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo explore the sources of job satisfaction and work-related stressors experienced by organ and tissue donation coordinators in Canada.DesignA qualitative descriptive approach incorporating semi-structured virtual interviews conducted between March 2022 and April 2024. Inductive coding was used for this analysis.SettingCanadian organ donation organisations.ParticipantsCoordinators from across Canada working in deceased organ and tissue donation programmes.ResultsPerspectives from 39 coordinators from 10 organ donation organisations across Canada were explored in this analysis, of which most had a nursing degree (n=35, 89.75%) and a background in intensive care (n=32, 65.30%). Most participants were female (n=95, 81.9%), and the average age of participants was 41.8 (9.64). Satisfying aspects described by coordinators included interpersonal interactions, personal factors (such as a calling to the role) and work-related factors (such as the diverse work environment). Meanwhile, coordinators also highlighted work-related stressors experienced related to the outcomes, processes and structures of their roles.ConclusionsFindings from this analysis emphasise the complex work-related stressors and satisfying aspects of work experienced by coordinators in Canada in delivering life-saving donation care. Steps to enhance and increase the satisfying aspects of the role and to reduce the modifiable stressors are warranted to support coordinators’ critical work and their work-related well-being.Keywords: Burnout, Professional; Health Services; Health Workforce; Transplant medicine; Occupational Stress

Introduction

It is well known that the deceased organ donation process involves several intricate steps, from approaching the donor family for donation to donor maintenance and organ allocation and retrieval.1 It is also current common knowledge that an interdisciplinary approach is essential to ensure a seamless flow of the process, ensuring the quality of organs while also attending to the donor family’s needs, yet key to the success of deceased organ donation is the role of organ and tissue donation coordinators (coordinators).1 2 Coordinators work directly with the interdisciplinary team and donors’ families, providing support during each unique step along the way. This includes liaising with organisations to identify and screen potential donors, discussing consent with families, coordinating teams for the retrieval of donations and monitoring the donor throughout their maintenance care.1 Registered nurses (RNs) and other healthcare providers with critical care experience and specialised training in organ donation are often who fill the role of coordinators, delivering holistic care during these complex critical practices.1 3 4

The diverse range of activities undertaken by coordinators can have varying effects on their work-related well-being, ranging from positive impacts (eg, job satisfaction) to negative consequences (eg, moral distress), which can influence the quality of care they deliver.5 The demands of their roles, such as travel to work environments, managing care within time constraints, communication requirements, resource allocation and supporting family members, can expose coordinators to stressors and work-related issues like burnout and compassion fatigue. However, these challenges can be counterbalanced by rewards such as professional growth and personal fulfilment, highlighting the need for a balance between the different aspects of their work.6

This balance between negative and positive aspects is essential; if the stressors outweigh the satisfying elements, coordinators may feel compelled to resign and prematurely leave the profession.7 8 Therefore, it is crucial to understand both the challenges and stressors experienced by coordinators, and it is equally important to recognise the primary sources of satisfaction that coordinators experience so that efforts can be made to mitigate those negative effects and develop targeted interventions to improve/maintain their work-related well-being.

Most of the research exploring critical care providers’ perceptions of deceased organ donation has focused on their understanding and attitudes toward the practice, rather than delving into the specific factors that influence these processes,9 suggesting a gap in the literature on these key aspects identified. Therefore, the purpose of this research analysis is to explore the factors that influence the care provided by coordinators in Canada, specifically focusing on the stressors and challenges faced by coordinators and the motivators that contribute to their ongoing engagement and commitment to the profession.

Methods

Design

This paper is part of a collection of pragmatic work conducted through the Burnout and Resilience in Organ Donor Coordinators (BRiC) research programme with the Canadian Blood Services.6 The BRiC research programme is a national multi-phase programme of research focused on understanding work-related issues among Canadian organ and tissue donation coordinators. The overall aim of BRiC is to identify factors associated with burnout, compassion fatigue, moral distress and resilience among coordinators and to inform the development of interventions to support coordinators in their workplace. The programme includes three phases: (1) a scoping review of the international literature, (2) a national mixed-methods study consisting of a quantitative survey followed by qualitative interviews and (3) the development of intervention resources to support coordinators in practice. The present paper reports one qualitative analysis derived from the interview component of Phase II, which was designed to explore coordinators’ experiences of work-related well-being, including burnout, compassion fatigue, moral distress, resilience, stressors and professional experiences within organ donation practice. Given the breadth of topics covered, the interview dataset was intended to support multiple qualitative analyses addressing distinct but related research questions within the BRiC research programme.

We used a qualitative descriptive design approach for this analysis, as qualitative research within the health sciences allows for the exploration of individuals’ experiences with health, illness, clinical processes and other various health-related aspects.10 Meanwhile, a descriptive approach allows for a focus problem or phenomenon to be explored, which can be presented through a basic description and summary.11 Lastly, the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research checklist was used to guide reporting of the methods for this analysis.12

Ethical clearance was obtained from the Children’s Hospital of Eastern Ontario’s Research Ethics Board (#20210076). Additional approval from the specific organ donation organisations (ODOs) was obtained as required.

Study setting and recruitment

This was a multi-setting national analysis using a purposeful and convenience sampling technique to explore the perceptions of coordinators working across Canada. Representatives from all ODOs across Canada, which included the provinces of Alberta, British Columbia, Manitoba, New Brunswick, Newfoundland and Labrador, Nova Scotia, Ontario, Quebec and Saskatchewan, were contacted first. According to ODO reports, there were 175 coordinators available across Canada who could participate as they met the inclusion criteria (healthcare provider in the role of coordinator with experience in deceased organ donation). All potential participants ultimately received an e-mail invitation to take part in our BRiC study. All participants who responded to the survey and demonstrated interest in participating in the interviews were contacted by a member of our research team to schedule the interview and submitted a signed informed consent prior to interviews to participate.

Data collection

Interviews were conducted from March 2022 to April 2024 through an online video conference platform, Zoom. A semi-structured interview guide that was developed and pilot tested for this analysis was used to facilitate the interviews (online supplemental material 1). Since the interview guide was not significantly adjusted for phase II of the BRiC programme, the first five participants from the pilot test were included in the study sample. Individual interviews ranged from 29 to 146 min in duration. Interviews conducted in English were all completed by one female member of the research team (ARS), who is a PhD-prepared RN experienced in qualitative research. Interviews conducted in French were completed by a female researcher (JV), an RN with training in qualitative methodology. All interviews were video/audio recorded, and field notes were taken by the researchers throughout to record reflexive thoughts and observations. These notes were used to identify opportunities for refinement in interview questions when signs of confusion were identified or the interviewer felt uncomfortable with an approach to questioning. Participants were informed about the researcher’s affiliation with the organisation conducting the research and their role in the programme.

Data analysis

The complete set of interview transcripts was used for this analysis. The interviews addressed multiple aspects of coordinators’ professional experiences and generated a rich dataset suitable for addressing several related research questions within the BRiC programme. Full transcripts were analysed for each planned analysis. For the present analysis, coding and theme development focused specifically on participants’ descriptions of work-related stressors and satisfying aspects. Other analyses derived from the same interview dataset addressed different aspects of coordinators’ experiences using separate analytic questions and thematic interpretations.

A concurrent approach was used throughout the interview, transcription and data analysis process, which allowed for ongoing assessment of the interview guide for revision and need for additional interviews to obtain data saturation.13 First, individual interview audio/visual recordings were transcribed verbatim and de-identified after completion by a professional transcriptionist. Data from the interviews was then imported into NVivo, a qualitative data analysis software, where it was organised. Subsequently, content analysis was performed using an inductive approach by one member of the team (ARS) and peer-reviewed by two additional members (AR and VSS) to ensure consensus among the data. This involved highlighting and coding key words and phrases identified by participants, grouping those into common ideas, then mapping those against others’ ideas to identify themes, a process which was completed after each interview to allow for ongoing comparison and analysis. The analysis process was performed until data saturation was obtained, identified when there was repetition of participants’ responses and common ideas, suggesting thematic sufficiency. Transcripts were not returned to participants after completion, and they were not given copies of the analysis results.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Findings

A total of 39 coordinators were interviewed with representation from all ten Canadian ODOs in the sample. We have divided our findings into two main categories, each with three sub-categories, which emphasise the satisfying aspects of the job and work-related stressors as reported by coordinators in Canada. These results are summarised in the corresponding sections, following a description of participant characteristics.

Satisfying aspects of the job

Coordinators described several aspects of the job that they perceived as satisfying, which were categorised into interpersonal interactions, personal factors and work-specific factors, as one coordinator stated: “I love this job. I love this role. I love the autonomy … and I love bringing an organ case together … and I love the families.” (participant 26).

Interpersonal interactions

The collaborative nature of the role of coordinators was highlighted as a satisfying aspect by most participants. Many spoke to the dynamics of their team and working with their colleagues as being key to the role—“we’re very collaborative within our group and there’s constant communication” (participant 2); while others also appreciated their interactions with interdisciplinary team members from the hospital settings they support, from the ‘bedside staff’ (participant 3) to the ‘ICU team’ (participant 4). Similarly, several coordinators described their connection with families as being a monumental part of their role, as highlighted by a coordinator who indicated that they “met a wonderful family that filled up my cup again … made me remember what I loved about my job” (participant 4). Lastly, some coordinators described their passion for teaching and being “able to walk people through the process and what we’re going to do next” (participant 10), highlighting the satisfaction that comes when others understand the intricate processes involved in their care.

Personal factors

There were several personal factors described by coordinators in our analysis that contributed to their satisfaction. Overall, there was a love for the job expressed by coordinators, influenced in part by feeling ‘valued’ and the ‘sense of belonging’ and ‘growth’ (participant 4) they experience. Some even felt that the role was a ‘calling’ (participant 2) and that doing what they were ‘meant to do’ was satisfying in and of itself. Personality was identified as another factor for work satisfaction by numerous coordinators, with some alluding to their desire for ‘challenges’ (participant 7) and completing tasks, and others suggesting it is because they are ‘dedicated’ (participant 32) and like to know they have ‘pleased as many people’ (participant 23) as possible.

Work-specific factors

Coordinators found the meaning behind their work to be one of the more satisfying aspects of the job and part of the reason they continue providing care in these difficult circumstances. This was especially evident among cases where coordinators had the opportunity to share ‘positive outcomes’ (participant 2) with patients’ loved ones after their loss when organs were able to be retrieved or recipient outcomes were shared. Several coordinators expressed gratitude for hearing about recipient outcomes, to know that they are ‘saving all these lives’ (participant 31). It was also meaningful for coordinators to know that they can ‘honour somebody’s wish’ (participant 24) to donate their organs at the end of their life by engaging with this critical work.

The various responsibilities of coordinators offer a diverse work environment for these healthcare professionals, which many found to be a valuable component of their job. The mix of direct clinical care, teaching and administrative tasks was seen as both challenging and interesting, offering changing roles that keep them engaged. Furthermore, the diversity of roles provided opportunities for travelling, particularly to other ODOs, that one coordinator highlighted as being enjoyable (participant 27). Some, however, simply enjoyed the different clinical opportunities, such as being able to work ‘in the operating room’ and ‘eye banking’ (participant 12).

Work-related stressors

Although coordinators reported a deep meaning for the work they do, they still identified several stressful aspects of their work, ultimately contributing to levels of ‘dissatisfaction’. Prominent stressors were linked to the various outcomes, processes and structures impacting the specialised care delivered by these healthcare professionals.

Outcomes-related

Many coordinators described often only seeing one side of patient cases, suggesting that they ‘don’t see the good side’ because they ‘never see anyone get better’ (participant 29). In some instances, this is because they ‘don’t see the recipients’ (participant 29). Therefore, they do not get to see how the case turns out unless it results in death. Whereas, in other situations, the worst part is in having to “tell families that no organs have been accepted” (participant 1). Coordinators described sometimes having to obtain consent and perform testing, and then “it doesn’t work because the organs aren’t suitable for the transplant” (participant 38), and then they must share that information, ultimately “breaking the news of a second loss” (participant 38).

Alongside this, coordinators described the emotional demands of constantly being exposed to donation cases, especially when there is little time for recovery in between. As one participant stated, “it’s death and dying and we’re always dealing with grieving families, and that’s stressful” (participant 26). In some instances, these stressful factors contributed to physical impacts for coordinators, such as the physical characteristics of anxiety (ie, elevated heart rate) and the development of high blood pressure.

Process-related

Several processes involved with providing care as a coordinator were described as a source of stress by participants. The allocation of organs was reported as “probably the most stressful part that I find is basically getting everything done for the allocation. So doing all the workup and everything like that, making sure serology and everything is done.” (participant 22). In addition, the bridging role was also noted as an important source of stress, as one coordinator mentioned, “[…]the struggle that we feel as a coordinator, being the middleman essentially. Right? Because you are the person who needs to coordinate the family first and foremost and then the ICU team or the emerg team and the transplant teams and your team” (participant 2). The family commitment and conversations were also highlighted by coordinators who mentioned, “You’ve stepped in at the worst point of their lives predictably and you’ve asked them for something big and you feel a connection to them and a commitment to them.” (participant 2). In addition, being in the operating room, the introduction of new processes, pressures from management and institutions, time constraints, working with other members of the healthcare team and, overall, the unpredictability of the role were mentioned as added stressors, as noted by one participant: “So I think that’s kind of the most distressing part of the whole thing. It’s just not really knowing.” (Participant #20). As an example, with the potential for negatively impacting other people’s lives, whether that be the recipient or donor, a few coordinators expressed a “constant fear of making a mistake” (participant 39), leading to a significant source of stress for providing care. Concurrently, working alone also added to the stress of coordinators, as “there’s nobody just to cover for a couple hours while I go have a nap or anything like that … it just needs to be done” (participant 16).

Structure-related

Participants reported a variety of structural components within their roles as coordinators that contributed to workplace stress. One of the prominent stressors described was the administrative work required to provide donation care, including documentation, quality reviews and data collection and analysis. The ‘perfectionism’ and ‘minute details’ required in the administrative work was seen as ‘daunting’ and took away from their ability to focus on other key responsibilities. Additionally, some coordinators felt that there was a need for change within their work, suggesting that there are “not necessarily like best practices” (participant 30) for the administrative work and that things are done the same way they always have.

The lack of resources, staff and support were also highlighted as structural factors for some coordinators leading to workplace stress. These issues were often compounded by management, who expected a ‘perfect reality’ from these care providers, despite the complexity of the care provided during donation cases. As a result, some coordinators expressed feeling like leadership did not understand “how much [they’re] struggling” (participant 27). Insufficient training to manage some of the complexities of the role and the underlying structural issues added to the workplace stress.

Physical stressors emerged as well for coordinators within their positions. Inadequate office space, for example, was expressed by several participants, especially when having to travel to various hospital settings. Alternatively, one participant preferred not having to be in the office, stating, “I don’t want to be sitting in this office eight hours a day. I won’t” (participant 8). Lastly, the sheer workload required of coordinators was frequently identified as a stressor by participants, emphasising that “every case kind of wears on you in a different way” (participant 1). The demands of constantly being on call, long hours and their work schedule added to this stress. Yet, those who worked casually reported stressors as well, recounting needing to “sleuth out what said email means” (participant 10) because they missed a meeting or were not “at something because it’s not a workday” (participant 10).

On a higher level, frustration and confusion with the “hierarchy of how things, like the organization” (participant 30) work, was expressed by some coordinators. Some felt that the ‘policy piece’ and the ‘governance piece’ were the source of the dissatisfaction, while others found that because each province has their own ODO, they “all have their own little ways of working” (participant 22), making it difficult to provide care. ‘Roadblocks’ caused by the overarching system were also described as ‘challenging’ by one participant.

Discussion

This analysis offers a novel contribution to the literature by shifting the focus from attitudes of coordinators to actual concrete work-related factors applicable to practice. Focusing on these real-world work factors provides direction for practice and actionable items that can support coordinators in their roles. The inclusion of a large and diverse cohort of participants in interviews strengthened the applicability of the findings to the Canadian context.

Results from this analysis revealed the complex interplay between perceived satisfactions of the coordinator role and the various stressors that impact work-related well-being. Satisfying aspects ranged from interpersonal interactions to personal and work-specific factors, while stressors were based on the outcomes, processes and structures involved. These stressors, however, may be considered in relation to their ability to be modified for the workplace when deciding how to best support coordinators. Stressors that are considered modifiable are those that have the potential to be altered to reduce work-related stress and poor health outcomes.14 Clear work assignment and goals, knowing who makes decisions, work conflict and supervisory support were identified as some modifiable factors by Norful et al14 in a study among nursing professionals in 13 countries. Results from their study showed that those who reported experiencing conflict in the workplace and a lack of social support were at a higher risk of burnout. Burnout is a complicated condition characterised by “emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and decreased personal accomplishments”.15 Burnout is associated with patient safety issues, along with diminished quality of care and patient satisfaction and nurse commitment.15 Coordinators in Canada reported similar stressors, suggesting that these may be key work-related aspects to modify to reduce the incidences of stress among coordinators. These modifications are essential, then, not only for the well-being of nursing professionals but also for the outcomes of patients.

Alternatively, non-modifiable stressors may include factors such as personal attributes (gender, age), work location and current job, as studied by Islam et al16 when considering impacts of the COVID-19 on mental health issues. Coordinators in our analysis described several non-modifiable stressors, particularly related to the job responsibilities and nature of the specialty (ie, meeting families during a difficult time, liaising between teams and unpredictability). A prominent stressor highlighted by coordinators in this analysis, for instance, was not being able to know about recipients and outcomes. However, it is important to consider the ethics behind this knowledge, as there is a potential impact on individual intentions when one is made aware of this information, which may interfere with their duties as care givers.17

In addition, there are aspects that are inherent to the Canadian healthcare system’s structure, such as the universal healthcare system with independent provincial insurance plans, that reflect on the perceived variation of structures of programmes and processes as mentioned by participants.18 There is a need for governmental support to standardise programmes and ensure proper staff numbers to support not only increased organ donation numbers and support for family and hospitals during cases but also to ensure that coordinators' mental health is preserved with proper recovery time in between cases.

While many of these stressors cannot be controlled, there is an opportunity to support coordinators specifically related to these critical moments of stress. Coping strategies, for example, are described as healthy or unhealthy techniques that can be used to manage and mitigate stress from occupational demands.19 In a scoping review conducted by Silva et al19 it was found that acute and critical care nurses, who fill the majority of coordinator positions, could use support and education on incorporating stress-reducing techniques into their daily routine. This is especially required due to the reporting of unhealthy coping such as substance abuse among acute and critical care nurses.19 Supporting healthy coping strategies is a multilevel responsibility. While ODOs have historically supported coordinators by providing in-house counselling either through employee assistance programmes or by hiring someone for group support, there is still the need for higher-level interventions through the development of standardised education that also covers healthy coping strategies and governmental support to ensure proper staffing numbers across the provinces.

Fulfilling aspects can also support creating positive and meaningful interactions within the workplace, limiting stress. Coordinators reported fulfilling aspects such as interdisciplinary collaboration, personal growth and organisation of the donation process. Cantarelli, Vainieri and Seghieri20 found similar aspects of professional satisfaction in a large survey study across Italy, with improved planning, being part of a team and manager competence correlating with satisfaction in the workplace. In addition, communities of practice have been found to be beneficial to increase resilience levels in healthcare teams.21 To that end, Canadian Blood Services established in 2020 a community of practice called the Donor Coordinator Network, which focuses on fostering community building and national collaboration that enables clinical and social support among the community and also provides resources and opportunities to implement consistent clinical strategies nationally.22

Recommendations for future research and implications for practice

Future research projects may want to consider exploring the conflict experienced by coordinators in the workplace and how this impacts their ability to provide care during these critical moments since this is a prominent modifiable stressor. Further exploration of the roles and hierarchy in organ donation systems and expansion of analysis of communication patterns may provide key insights into how to enhance the work environment for coordinators. Managers may also wish to explore opportunities to incorporate education on coping strategies to employ while providing care during complex and emotionally charged situations. Interventions aimed at supporting coping may be warranted, such as mindfulness-based activities and others described in Silva et al’s coping review.19 Reflective and de-briefing practices may be considered, which could support coordinators' coping and social engagement.

Additionally, institutions may want to consider conducting an internal evaluation of processes to determine if there are any opportunities for change that could enhance the workplace for coordinators. Healthcare leaders may wish to explore organisational culture to support this process in promoting fulfilling aspects, which would require a comprehensive approach involving coordinators.23 In practice, this may look like the development of tailored education programmes, acknowledgment and reward of contributions, communication channels between employees and between leaders and managers, as well as opportunities for feedback.23

Strengths and limitations

Overall, this analysis demonstrates several important strengths that enhance its rigour. The data collection process, led by an experienced independent researcher using semi-structured interviews, supports credibility by fostering an environment in which participants could share detailed, experience-based insights in a flexible format. This interview style allowed coordinators to elaborate on their unique perspectives while ensuring that core topics were consistently explored, contributing not only to credibility through rich and authentic data but also to dependability by maintaining a relatively structured and systematic approach across interviews. Scheduling interviews according to participant availability may have further improved openness and comfort, potentially encouraging deeper reflection. In addition, the inclusion of a large proportion of coordinators from across Canada strengthened both credibility and transferability within the Canadian context by increasing the diversity of perspectives, improving the likelihood of thematic saturation and capturing experiences across varied geographical and healthcare settings. This broad representation helps ensure that findings more accurately reflect the realities of coordinators within Canada rather than a narrow subgroup. Confirmability is also supported using an independent interviewer, which may have minimised organisational influence or researcher bias during data collection, helping ensure that findings were grounded more strongly in participant experiences than in pre-existing assumptions.

Despite these strengths, some limitations affect the analysis’ overall trustworthiness. Response bias and social desirability bias may have reduced credibility and confirmability, as participants who used work-designated devices or feared potential employment repercussions may have hesitated to disclose negative experiences or criticisms fully. Even with privacy encouragement, the possibility of self-censorship could have influenced the authenticity and completeness of the data. Dependability may also be challenged by the contextual inconsistency introduced by conducting some interviews during the COVID-19 pandemic, as the extraordinary social, emotional and professional stressors of that period may have amplified participant perceptions in ways that differ from non-pandemic conditions. This temporal context could mean that participant responses were shaped not only by their coordinator role but also by heightened crisis-related pressures, potentially affecting the stability of findings over time. Transferability is similarly constrained because the coordinator role and healthcare delivery system in Canada may differ substantially from those in other countries. While the findings may be highly relevant to Canadian healthcare coordination, they may not be directly applicable to international settings where job responsibilities, organisational frameworks and healthcare infrastructures vary significantly. Furthermore, pandemic-era experiences may not fully transfer to future or more typical healthcare environments.

By transparently acknowledging these factors, the analysis strengthens its methodological integrity, enabling readers to more accurately interpret the reliability of the findings and determine the extent to which they may be applicable to other contexts.

Conclusion

This analysis offers a comprehensive understanding of the experiences of coordinators across Canada, highlighting both the stressors and the sources of satisfaction inherent in their roles. The presence of both modifiable and non-modifiable stressors underscores the importance of targeted education and interventions that can address the challenges experienced and can equip coordinators with the tools they need to manage the emotional demands of their work. The value of organisation and collaboration in the workplace was also evident, setting the stage for organisations to sustain the coordinator profession. These insights contribute to the growing body of literature of healthcare provider well-being and the complex roles of coordinators, emphasising how nurturing providers can also improve patient care and outcomes.
