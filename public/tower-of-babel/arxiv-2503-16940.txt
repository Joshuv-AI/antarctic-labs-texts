Magnetic ground states and the conformal class of a surface
Bruno Colbois, Luigi Provenzano, Alessandro Savo

Abstract




Abstract.
On a closed, orientable Riemannian surface
\Sigma_{g}
of arbitrary genus
g\geq 1
and Riemannian metric
h
we study the magnetic Laplacian with magnetic potential given by a harmonic
1
-form
A
. Its lowest eigenvalue (magnetic ground state energy) is positive, unless
A
represents an integral cohomology class. We isolate a countable set of ground state energies which we call
ground state spectrum
of the metric
h
. The main result of the paper is to show that the ground state spectrum determines the volume and the conformal class of the metric
h
. In particular, hyperbolic metrics are distinguished by their ground state spectrum.

We also compute the magnetic spectrum of flat tori and introduce some magnetic spectral invariants of
(\Sigma_{g},h)
which are conformal by definition and involve the geometry of what we call the Jacobian torus of
(\Sigma_{g},h)
(in Algebraic Geometry, the Jacobian variety of a Riemann surface).

Keywords:
Riemann surface, magnetic Laplacian, ground state energy

2020 Mathematics Subject Classification:
58J50, 58J53, 35P15, 53C18

1
Introduction

In this paper we will study the ground state energy of the magnetic Laplacian with zero magnetic field on
2
-dimensional compact surfaces with empty boundary. In this short introduction we define the magnetic Laplacian and introduce some of its fundamental properties. The main results will be discussed in Section
2
.

Let
(M,h)
be a compact Riemannian manifold without boundary; note that
h
denotes the metric (the notation
g
is reserved for the genus of a surface, considered below).

A
magnetic potential
is by definition a smooth real
1
-form
A
on
M
. It defines a
magnetic gradient

\nabla^{A}
acting on
C^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{C})
by the formula

\nabla^{A}u=\nabla u-iuA^{\sharp}

where
\nabla
denotes the gradient in the metric
h
and
A^{\sharp}
is the dual vector field of
A
(
vector potential
). Dually, one can define the
magnetic differential

of
u
as the complex
1
-form

d^{A}u=du-iuA.

The
magnetic Laplacian

\Delta_{A}
is given by
(\nabla^{A})^{*}\nabla^{A}
and may be written

\Delta_{A}u=\Delta u+2i\langle A,du\rangle_{h}+(|A|_{h}^{2}-i\delta A)u

(1)

where
\delta=d^{\star}
denotes the codifferential and
\Delta
the usual Laplacian; our sign convention is that, on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
:
\Delta u=-\sum_{k}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x_{k}^{2}}
. Equivalently,
\Delta_{A}=\delta^{A}d^{A}
, where
\delta^{A}
is the magnetic codifferential, acting on the
1
-form
\omega
by
\delta^{A}\omega=\delta\omega+i\langle{A},{\omega}\rangle_{h}
.

Since
\Delta_{A}
defines a compact self-adjoint operator with dense domain in
L^{2}(M,dv_{h})
, we have a non-negative, discrete spectrum

0\leq\lambda_{1}(M,h,A)\leq\lambda_{2}(M,h,A)\leq\dots\leq\lambda_{k}(M,h,A)\leq\dots

diverging to
+\infty
.
The
magnetic ground state energy
is the first eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}(M,h,A)
of
\Delta_{A}
; it has the usual variational characterization

\lambda_{1}(M,h,A)=\min_{0\neq u\in C^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{C})}R_{A}(u)

(2)

where
R_{A}
is the Rayleigh quotient associated with
\Delta_{A}
:

R_{A}(u)=\frac{\int_{M}|\nabla^{A}u|_{h}^{2}dv_{h}}{\int_{M}|u|^{2}dv_{h}}.

One sees immediately that
\Delta_{A}
reduces to the usual Laplace-Beltrami operator when
A=0
, giving rise to the same spectrum:

\lambda_{k}(M,h,0)=\lambda_{k}(M,h)

where on the right we have the
k
-th eigenvalue of the Laplace-Beltrami operator. Note that we number the eigenvalues so that
\lambda_{1}(M,h)=0
, hence, in this paper, the lowest positive eigenvalue of the Laplace-Beltrami operator is
\lambda_{2}(M,h)
.

A fundamental feature of the magnetic Laplacian is
gauge invariance
, expressed in the following lemma. Recall that the flux of a
1
-form
A
on the closed curve
c
is defined as
\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint_{c}A
.

\bullet\quad
We say that the closed
1
-form
A
is
integral
if the flux of
A
around every loop in
M
is an integer.

Note that exact
1
-forms have zero flux around any loop, hence they are trivially integral.

Lemma 1
.

Assume that
A
and
A^{\prime}
are closed potential
1
-forms such that
A-A^{\prime}
is integral. Then

\lambda_{k}(M,h,A)=\lambda_{k}(M,h,A^{\prime})
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

In fact, if
A-A^{\prime}
is exact:
A-A^{\prime}=d\phi
, the operators
\Delta_{A}
and
\Delta_{A}^{\prime}
are unitarily equivalent because of the formula

\Delta_{A}e^{i\phi}=e^{i\phi}\Delta_{A^{\prime}}

and then have the same spectrum. If more generally
A-A^{\prime}
is integral the above holds with

\phi(x)=\int_{x_{0}}^{x}(A-A^{\prime})\doteq\int_{\gamma}(A-A^{\prime})
, where
x_{0}
is any fixed point and the integral is taken along any curve
\gamma
joining
x_{0}
and
x
. Note that
\phi(x)
is multi-valued: if
\gamma
and
\tilde{\gamma}
are curves joining
x_{0}
and
x
then
\int_{\gamma}(A-A^{\prime})-\int_{\tilde{\gamma}}(A-A^{\prime})\in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}
by the integrality assumption, hence the function
e^{i\phi}
is well-defined and smooth.

Our main attention is given to
\lambda_{1}(M,h,A)
, the
ground state energy
of
(M,h)
for the potential
A
: it is important to know when it is strictly positive. The following fact is due to Shigekawa (for a proof, see
[
10
, Appendix 5]
).

Theorem 2
.

One has
\lambda_{1}(M,h,A)=0
if and only if
A
is closed and integral (that is, A has integral flux around every loop in
M
).

In this paper we will consider
closed
potentials
A
, so that the magnetic field
B
vanishes:
B\doteq dA=0
. According to Theorem
2
, the ground state energy could be strictly positive, precisely when the potential
A
has non-integral flux around some loop in
M
.
In the literature, this corresponds to a phenomenon in quantum mechanics called
Aharonov-Bohm effect
, observed in experimental physics: hence the condition
dA=0
is far from being trivial. This condition is very interesting also from the spectral geometric point of view, as we shall see very soon.

Let
{\rm Har}(M,h)
be the vector space of
h
-harmonic
1
-forms on
(M,h)
; by the Hodge-de Rham theorem, it is isomorphic to the first de Rham cohomology space
H^{1}(M,\mathbb{R})
. Observe that, if
A
is closed, we have by gauge invariance that
\lambda_{1}(M,h,A)=\lambda_{1}(M,h,\tilde{A})
, where
\tilde{A}
is the unique harmonic representative of
A
. Hence, we can restrict our attention to
harmonic
potential forms, and so we can view the ground state energy as a function

\lambda_{1}(M,h,\cdot):{\rm Har}(M,h)\to[0,\infty)

taking
A
to
\lambda_{1}(M,h,A)
. A simple but important result of this paper is the computation of the Hessian of this function at
A=0
(see Theorem
3
).

Next, let
\mathcal{L}^{*}
be the lattice in
{\rm Har}(M,h)
formed by the integral
1
-forms; as an abelian group, it is isomorphic to
H^{1}(M,\mathbb{Z})
. Note that Theorem
2
says precisely that

\bullet\quad
\lambda_{1}(M,h,A)=0
if and only if
A\in\mathcal{L}^{\star}
.

By gauge invariance, we conclude that the ground state energy is really a function on the quotient:

\lambda_{1}(M,h,\cdot):{\rm Har}(M,h)/{\mathcal{L}^{\star}}\to[0,\infty).

\bullet\quad
Suitably renormalized, the quotient torus
{\rm Har}(M,h)/{\mathcal{L}^{\star}}
will be called the
Jacobian torus
of
(M,h)
(see Subsection
2.4
).
When
M
is a Riemannian surface, it is a conformal invariant, known in Algebraic Geometry as the Jacobian variety of
(M,h)
.

We now recall a result from
[
10
]
which partly inspired the present paper. We endow
{\rm Har}(M,h)
with the
L^{2}
-inner product of forms, and define the distance of
A\in{\rm Har}(M,h)
to the lattice
\mathcal{L}^{*}
as follows:

d_{h}(A,\mathcal{L}^{*})^{2}=\min\{\|\omega-A\|^{2}_{L^{2}(h)};\ \omega\in\mathcal{L}^{*}\}.

(3)

Then we have the upper bound
[
10
, Theorem 3]
:

\lambda_{1}(M,h,A)\leq\frac{1}{{\rm vol}(M,h)}d_{h}(A,\mathcal{L}^{*})^{2}.

(4)

It also follows from
[
10
, Theorem 5]
that when
M
is a two-dimensional torus, we have equality in (
4
) if and only if
M
is flat.

1.1
Preliminary comments

The spectrum of the magnetic Laplacian has received great attention lately, also from the
purely mathematical point of view, on which we focus here. In the paper
[
10
]
a series of upper bounds have been proved, some of them generalizing the results and the methods employed in the classical non-magnetic case, i.e. the Laplace-Beltrami operator. For a general study of the magnetic Laplacian see for example
[
18
]
. A case which is widely studied is that of plane domains endowed with a constant magnetic field, see for instance
[
3
,
11
,
12
,
16
,
18
,
19
,
22
,
23
]
and the references therein. Aharonov-Bohm magnetic potentials play a role in the geometry of spectral minimal partitions of plane domains
[
21
]
; in two dimensions, isoperimetric inequalities for the lowest eigenvalue have been obtained in
[
13
]
, and Steklov eigenvalues estimates in the spirit of Fraser-Schoen have been studied for free boundary surfaces in the unit ball of
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
in
[
27
]
. Recently, magnetic spectral asymptotics for general magnetic potentials have been studied in
[
8
]
and, finally, we mention also
[
15
]
where the authors study the spectrum of the magnetic Hodge-Laplace operator acting on forms.

In the next section we state our main results. Briefly, we first show that the main inequality proven in
[
10
]
is sharp, asymptotically, as the potential form
A
tends to zero. We then focus on two dimensional closed, orientable Riemannian surfaces of arbitrary genus
g
endowed with a closed magnetic potential. This
“weak magnetic potential asymptotics”
will be used to isolate a countable family of ground state energies, called
ground state spectrum
, the knowledge of which, as shown in our main result, determines the volume and the conformal class of the surface; in particular, ground state-isospectral (in our sense) hyperbolic surfaces are isometric, a result which does not hold for the classical Laplace-Beltrami spectrum.

A concrete case of study will be flat tori, for which we compute in detail the magnetic spectrum and discuss optimization of the magnetic ground state energy under volume constraint. An important tool in our two-dimensional analysis is the conformal invariance of the magnetic energy, which holds in our Aharonov-Bohm magnetic situation. Thanks to this invariance, the above optimization extends to any surface of genus one.

We finally introduce some spectral invariants in the arbitrary genus
g
, which are conformal by definition and involve the geometry of the so-called Jacobian torus (or Jacobian variety) of the surface, which is (magnetic) spectrally determined and naturally arises from the lattice of integral
1
-forms on the surface. The Jacobian variety plays a crucial role in algebraic geometry
[
17
]
;
see
[
6
,
7
]
for a more differential geometric approach. See also the introduction of the Ph.D. thesis of B. Mützel
[
26
]
.

2
The results of the paper

2.1
Asymptotics of the ground state energy for weak magnetic potentials

Our first result is rather general and applies to any compact Riemannian manifold, complementing
[
10
, Theorem 2]
. We determine the asymptotics of
\lambda_{1}(M,h,A)
as
\lVert{A}\rVert_{L^{2}(h)}\to 0
, in the following precise sense.

Theorem 3
.

Let
(M,h)
be a compact Riemannian manifold and let
A
be a co-closed potential. Then, there exist constants
\rho>0
and
C>0
such that for all
r\in(0,\rho)
:

\frac{\|A\|_{L^{2}(h)}^{2}}{{{\rm vol}(M,h)}}r^{2}-Cr^{4}\leq\lambda_{1}(M,h,rA)\leq\frac{\|A\|_{L^{2}(h)}^{2}}{{\rm vol}(M,h)}r^{2}.

(5)

In particular, since for
r
small
d_{h}(rA,\mathcal{L}^{\star})^{2}=r^{2}\lVert{A}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(h)}
, it follows that

\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{{\rm vol}(M,h)\lambda_{1}(M,h,rA)}{d_{h}(rA,\mathcal{L}^{\star})^{2}}=1.

(6)

The limit (
6
) in Theorem
3
shows that the estimate (
4
) obtained in
[
10
]
is asymptotically exact as
\lVert{A}\rVert_{L^{2}(h)}\to 0
.

Note also that (
5
) can be re-written:

\lim_{r\to 0}\dfrac{{\rm vol}(M,h)\lambda_{1}(M,h,rA)}{r^{2}}=\lVert{A}\rVert_{L^{2}(h)}^{2}

(7)

showing that the knowledge of the lowest normalized eigenvalue
{\rm vol}(M,h)\lambda_{1}(M,h,rA)
for
r
in a small neighborhood of
0
(or on a sequence
r_{n}\to 0
) determines the
L^{2}
-norm of
A
. In dimension
2
it is known that the
L^{2}
-norm of a
1
-form is conformally invariant; thus, the limit in the left-hand side of (
7
) is a conformal invariant as well. This phenomenon was first observed in
[
27
]
in the context of the magnetic Laplacian on Riemannian cylinders with magnetic Steklov boundary conditions; in fact Theorem
3
can be extended (in arbitrary dimension) to manifolds with boundary endowed with magnetic Neumann (or Steklov) boundary conditions.

2.2
Main result: the magnetic ground state
energies determine the conformal class of a surface

We now focus on dimension
2
, and consider the (unique) compact, orientable, differentiable surface of genus
g\geq 1
, which we denote
\Sigma_{g}
. We fix the metric
h
on
\Sigma_{g}
and study the ground state energy

\lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{g},h,A)

where
A
is a closed
1
-form on
\Sigma_{g}
. We recall that this ground state energy is strictly positive, unless
A\in\mathcal{L}^{\star}
(i.e.
A
is integral), in which case it is zero. Here is the main result of this paper.

Theorem 4
.

Assume that the metrics
h
and
h_{0}
on
\Sigma_{g}
are such that:

\lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{g},h,A)=\lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{g},h_{0},A)\quad\text{for all closed $1$-forms $A$ on $\Sigma_{g}$}.

(8)

Then
(\Sigma_{g},h)
and
(\Sigma_{g},h_{0})
are conformal and have the same volume.

The proof uses Theorem
3
and a classical result in the theory of Riemann surfaces, the theorem of Torelli, to the effect that if two compact Riemann surfaces have the same Jacobian variety, they are conformal.
Now let
g\geq 2
. Since there is only one metric of constant curvature
-1
in any fixed conformal class, we have:

Corollary 5
.

Assume that
h
and
h_{0}
are hyperbolic metrics on
\Sigma_{g}
which satisfy (
8
). Then
h
and
h_{0}
are isometric.

Note that the family of eigenvalues in (
8
) needed to determine volume and conformal class consists only of lowest eigenvalues (ground state energies), but
corresponding to an infinite family of potential
1
-forms, i.e., closed potentials. By gauge invariance, we can restrict this family to that of harmonic
1
-forms, which form a vector space of dimension
2g
.
But indeed what we need is only the behavior of
\lambda_{1}
as
A\to 0
, roughly speaking, the asymptotics for
low energy
states; this allows to obtain the same results of Theorem
4
, by restricting (
8
) to a countable family of magnetic potentials, hence, to a countable family of eigenvalues, which we term
(magnetic) ground state spectrum
and which we define in the next subsection.

2.3
The (magnetic) ground state spectrum of a surface

We start by fixing a canonical homology basis
(\chi_{1},\dots,\chi_{2g})
(see Subsection
9.1
for the definition) and we let

(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{2g})
be its dual basis of closed forms in
H^{1}(\Sigma_{g},\mathbb{Z})
. Note that
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{2g})
depends only on the differentiable structure of
\Sigma_{g}
, and not on the metric. Then we define the following family of magnetic eigenvalues:

\mu_{jk,n}(\Sigma_{g},h)\doteq\lambda_{1}\left(\Sigma_{g},h,\frac{1}{n}(\alpha_{j}+\alpha_{k})\right)\quad 1\leq j\leq k\leq 2g,\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

(9)

We call
\{\mu_{jk,n}(\Sigma_{g},h)\}
the
(magnetic) ground state spectrum of the metric
h
.

Theorem 6
.

The volume and the conformal class of the metric
h
on
\Sigma_{g}
are determined by the ground state spectrum of
h
.

In fact, by Theorem
3
, the data above are enough to reconstruct the entries
\Gamma_{jk}
(rescaled by the volume) of the so-called
Gram matrix
(see Subsection
9.1
), a
2g\times 2g
matrix naturally associated to the conformal class of
h
, once a canonical homology basis of
H_{1}(\Sigma_{g},\mathbb{Z})
is fixed. That this matrix actually determines the conformal class of
h
follows from a deep theorem in the theory of Riemann surfaces, due to Torelli
[
30
]
, which we will use to conclude the proof.

Remark 7
.

Since what matters is only the behavior of the ground state when the potential form is small, we can replace the potential forms
\frac{1}{n}(\alpha_{j}+\alpha_{k})
in (
9
) by
c_{n}(\alpha_{j}+\alpha_{k})

where
\{c_{n}\}
is any sequence of positive numbers converging to zero as
n\to\infty
; the conclusion of Theorem
6
still holds. Also, volume and conformal class are determined by the knowledge of

\mu_{jk,n}(\Sigma_{g},h)
when
n\geq N_{0}
, where
N_{0}
is a fixed positive integer.

Corollary
5
takes the following form.

Corollary 8
.

Assume that
h
and
h_{0}
are hyperbolic metrics on
\Sigma_{g}
with the same ground state spectrum. Then
h
and
h_{0}
are isometric.

In what follows,
isospectral
refers to the classical notion in spectral geometry, namely, same Laplace-Beltrami spectrum. According to Wolpert
[
32
]
and Buser (see the introduction in
[
4
]
) Gelfand conjectured that two hyperbolic, isospectral surfaces are necessarily isometric.
This conjecture was first disproved by Vigneras in
[
31
]
for large genus and then by Buser
[
4
]
in genus 5. In particular, the Laplace-Beltrami spectrum does not determine the hyperbolic metric in the conformal class. Corollary
8
states that this happens in the magnetic sense, as defined above.

In genus one we have a similar phenomenon, in the sense that the flat metric is determined by the ground state spectrum. In fact, this is true in any dimension, see Theorem
29
.

Theorem 9
.

Two
d
-dimensional flat tori with the same ground state spectrum are necessarily isometric.

It is well known that this is not true for the Laplace-Beltrami operator, because, besides Milnor’s classical examples, there are flat tori in dimension four (and greater) which are isospectral but not isometric.

Given Theorems
6
and
9
one could ask:

\bullet\quad
Is it true that two surfaces
(\Sigma_{g},h)
and
(\Sigma_{g},h_{0})
which have the same ground state spectrum are actually isometric?

2.4
Some (conformal) spectral invariants

We just saw that the magnetic Laplacian associated to closed potentials has deep relations with the conformal structure of a surface. We introduce here some conformal invariants related to the magnetic ground state energy. To simplify the notation, through all the paper we shall denote by
|h|
the volume of the surface
\Sigma_{g}
for the metric
h
, namely
|h|:={{\rm vol}(\Sigma_{g},h)}
.

For a given metric
h
on
\Sigma_{g}
and a closed
1
-form
A
, define:

\Lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{g},[h],A)=\sup_{h^{\prime}\in[h]}\lvert{h^{\prime}}\rvert\lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{g},h^{\prime},A).

(10)

We remark that the quantity
\lvert{h}\rvert\lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{g},h,A)
is often called
normalized ground state energy
and it is easily seen to be scale invariant. The supremum is finite as we will see soon. By way of contrast, the infimum is zero, see Subsection
7.1
. We then define

\Lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{g},[h])=\sup_{A:dA=0}\Lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{g},[h],A).

(11)

Again note that the supremum can be taken over all
harmonic
potentials. Finally we define

\Lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{g})=\inf_{[h]}\Lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{g},[h]).

Note that this last invariant depends only on the genus, hence the topological structure of the surface. In the next section we will study and compute the above invariants when the genus is one.

The Jacobian torus.
All of the invariants in the previous section can be estimated from above by the geometry of a flat torus canonically associated to the surface: the
Jacobian torus
of
(\Sigma_{g},h)
; in algebraic geometry, it is known as the
Jacobian variety
of the given (compact, orientable) Riemann surface, and plays an important role in the theory of Riemann surfaces.

Let
(M,h)
be a compact,
d
-dimensional Riemannian manifold with first Betti number
b_{1}(M)\geq 1
, and let
V\doteq{\rm Har}(M,h)
be the vector space of
h
-harmonic
1
-forms endowed with the
L^{2}
-inner product:

\langle{\omega},{\phi}\rangle_{L^{2}(h)}=\int_{M}\langle{\omega},{\phi}\rangle_{h}dv_{h}.

As a vector space,
V
is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{R}}^{b_{1}(M)}
, by the Hodge theorem. We let
L^{\star}
be the lattice in
V
formed by those harmonic forms having
integral period

\oint_{c}\omega
around every loop
c
in
M
. In the previous section, we used the lattice
\mathcal{L}^{\star}
of forms with
integral flux

\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint_{c}\omega
; hence, here is the relation between the two lattices:

\mathcal{L}^{\star}=2\pi L^{\star}.

For us, it will be convenient to work with
L^{\star}
instead of
\mathcal{L}^{\star}
. We define the
Jacobian torus
of
(M,h)
as the flat torus

{\rm Jac}(M,h)={\rm Har}(M,h)/L^{\star}.

\bullet\quad
When
M=\Sigma_{g}
(the surface of genus
g
) the Jacobian torus is
2g
-dimensional; it is conformally invariant and has volume
1
for all
g
(see Theorem
36
).

We then focus on the surface
(\Sigma_{g},h)
. We have the following estimates from above. Given a harmonic
1
-form
A
, we denote
P_{A}=\frac{1}{2\pi}A
and let:

d_{h}(P_{A},L^{\star})^{2}=\min_{\omega\in L^{\star}}\lVert{P_{A}-\omega}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(h)}.

Note that
d_{h}(P_{A},L^{\star})
measures the minimum distance of the harmonic form
P_{A}
to the integral lattice
L^{\star}
, for the
L^{2}
metric. Consider the canonical projection

\pi:{\rm Har}(\Sigma_{g},h)\to{\rm{Jac}}(\Sigma_{g},h)

then, the image of
L^{\star}
under
\pi
is a distinguished point
\mathcal{O}\in\rm{Jac}(\Sigma_{g},h)
. As
\pi
is a local isometry, it is clear that

d_{h}(P_{A},L^{\star})=d_{h}(\pi(P_{A}),\mathcal{O}).

Next, we set

\mathcal{R}_{h}(L^{\star})^{2}\doteq{\rm max}_{A\in{\rm Har}(h)}d_{h}(P_{A},L^{\star})^{2}.

(12)

\mathcal{R}_{h}(L^{\star})
is the
inradius
of the lattice
L^{\star}
; that is, the radius of the largest open ball not intersecting
L^{\star}
. It measures how dense is
L^{\star}
inside
{\rm Har}(\Sigma_{g},h)
. Note that, given any two points on a flat torus, there exists an isometry (translation) taking one to the other. This immediately implies:

\mathcal{R}_{h}(L^{\star})={\rm diam}({\rm Jac}(\Sigma_{g},h))

the diameter of the associated Jacobian torus. The following estimate follows immediately from
[
10
, Theorem 3]
.

Theorem 10
.

One has:

\begin{aligned} &{\Lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{g},[h],A)\leq 4\pi^{2}d_{h}(P_{A},L^{\star})^{2}}\\
&{\Lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{g},[h])\leq 4\pi^{2}\mathcal{R}_{h}(L^{\star})^{2}=4\pi^{2}{\rm diam}({\rm Jac}(\Sigma_{g},h))^{2}}\\
&{\Lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{g})\leq 4\pi^{2}\inf_{[h]}{\rm diam}({\rm Jac}(\Sigma_{g},h))^{2}}\end{aligned}.

Some remarks.
The above inequalities show that the spectral invariants defined in this section admit upper bounds in terms of the geometry of the Jacobian torus of the given conformal class. In particular, the eigenvalue
\lvert{h}\rvert\lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{g},h,A)
is uniformly bounded above for
h
in any given conformal class. In Section
7
we will make a number of remarks:

1)

For arbitrary metrics,
\lvert{h}\rvert\lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{g},h,A)
can be arbitrarily large; in fact, we prove in Theorem
27
that there exist a closed potential form
A
and a sequence of metrics
h_{n}
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\lvert{h_{n}}\rvert\lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{g},h_{n},A)=+\infty.

2)

The normalized eigenvalue
\lvert{h}\rvert\lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{g},h,A)
can be arbitrarily small, even in a given conformal class. In fact, we prove in Theorem
25
:

\inf_{h^{\prime}\in[h]}\lvert{h^{\prime}}\rvert\lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{g},h^{\prime},A)=0.

3)

We will see in the next Subsection
2.5
that in genus one the supremum in the definition of
\Lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{g},[h])
is actually a maximum, and is achieved by the unique (up to homothety) flat metric in the conformal class
[h]
. Therefore it makes sense to ask the following question in genus
g\geq 2
:

\bullet\quad
Is the hyperbolic metric in each class
[h]
a maximiser for the invariant
\Lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{g},[h])
?

To that end, in Theorem
26
we prove the following universal upper bound: for any hyperbolic metric
h
on
\Sigma_{g}
and any harmonic potential
A
, one has:

\lvert{h}\rvert\lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{g},h,A)\leq C(g)

for a constant
C(g)
depending only on
g
. It is now clear that the hyperbolic metric in the conformal class of the metric
h_{n}
as in 1) cannot be a maximiser for large
n
. Hence, the question is more complicated.

4)

The invariant
\Lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{g})
is particularly interesting; in genus
1
it equals
8\pi^{2}/(3\sqrt{3})
(see Theorem
13
below) and is achieved by the regular hexagonal tiling (i.e. flat equilateral torus) in the plane. Note that the optimal conformal class
corresponds to a special Jacobian torus, hence to a lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{2g}
with specific geometric properties.
But the immediate question would be: is
\Lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{g})
positive for all
g
?

2.5
The magnetic spectrum of flat tori and the invariants in genus one

All the above invariants can actually be computed for
g=1
, that is when
\Sigma_{1}
is the
2
-torus. In that case the inequalities of Theorem
10
are actual equalities. This follows from a simple application of the conformal invariance of the magnetic energy of a function
u
:

\int_{\Sigma}\lvert{\nabla^{A}u}\rvert_{h}^{2}dv_{h},

which gives easily that, for any metric
h
on
\Sigma_{1}
:

Theorem 11
.

Given any metric
h
on the
2
-torus
\Sigma_{1}
one has

\lvert{h}\rvert\lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{1},h,A)\leq\lvert{\hat{h}}\rvert\lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{1},\hat{h},A)

where
\hat{h}
is the unique (up to homotheties) flat torus in the conformal class of
h
. Equality holds iff
h
and
\hat{h}
are homothetic. In particular:

\Lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{1},[h],A)=\lvert{\hat{h}}\rvert\lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{1},\hat{h},A).

The magnetic spectrum of a flat
d
-dimensional torus with a harmonic potential form
A
is then computed in Section
4
; we already remarked that, by gauge invariance, it reduces to the classical Laplace-Beltrami spectrum precisely when
A
has integral flux on every homology class, i.e. when
A\in 2\pi L^{\star}
; in that case
P_{A}=\frac{1}{2\pi}A
has integral periods and consequently:

d_{h}(P_{A},L^{\star})=0.

In general, the magnetic spectrum
{\rm spec}(\Sigma_{1},\hat{h},A)
is (up to a multiplicative constant of
4\pi^{2}
) the set of
the squares of all distances of
P_{A}
to the lattice points in
L^{\star}
(see
[
10
]
).
It is worth noticing that the family of eigenfunctions does not depend on
A
, and coincides, for all potentials
A
, with that of the usual Laplace-Beltrami operator; in particular, all eigenfunctions have constant modulus
\lvert{u}\rvert=C
on
\Sigma
.

Let then
(\Sigma_{1},\hat{h})
be a flat
2
-torus, identified with the quotient of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
by a lattice
L
. The dual lattice
L^{\star}
is identified precisely with the lattice of harmonic
1
-forms with integral periods. We note that in dimension
2
one always has that
L^{\star}
is homothetic to
L
(up to a congruence in
{\bf O}(2)
):

L^{\star}\sim\dfrac{1}{\lvert{\hat{h}}\rvert}L,

see Subsection
4.3
.

Remark 12
.

We recall that
L^{\star}
is naturally identified with the lattice of integral harmonic
1
-forms (see Lemma
14
). Since we are considering the
L^{2}
-norm on forms, which in this case corresponds to the usual Euclidean norm rescaled by
|\hat{h}|^{1/2}
(all harmonic forms on a flat torus have constant length) we deduce that the Jacobian torus of
(\Sigma_{1},\hat{h})
is a flat torus of volume
1
homothetic to
(\Sigma_{1},\hat{h})
.

It is a classical fact (see for example
[

, §B.I]
) that every flat torus

is homothetic to the one with lattice generated by the vectors:

w_{1}=(1,0),\quad w_{2}=(p,q)

with
p\in[0,\frac{1}{2}],q\geq p,p^{2}+q^{2}\geq 1
. We call
(p,q)
the

of

. It follows that the invariants above can all be expressed in terms of

. In particular:

Theorem 13
.

Let
(\Sigma_{1},h)
be a
2
-torus, and let
(\Sigma_{1},\hat{h})
be the unique flat torus conformal to
h
(up to homotheties). Assume that
(\Sigma_{1},\hat{h})
has parameters
(p,q)
. Then:

\Lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{1},[h])=\dfrac{\pi^{2}}{q^{3}}\left((p^{2}+q^{2}-p)^{2}+q^{2}\right).

Moreover,

\Lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{1})=\dfrac{8\pi^{2}}{3\sqrt{3}},

uniquely attained at the conformal class of the flat
60^{0}
-rhombic torus
(which has parameters
(p,q)=(\frac{1}{2},\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2})
).

3
Proof of Theorem
3
: asymptotics as
A\to 0

Let
(M,h)
be a
d
-dimensional, compact Riemannian manifold and let
A
be a co-closed
1
-form on it. Given
r>0
, we consider the ground state energy
\lambda_{1}(M,h,rA)
and compute its asymptotic behavior as
r\to 0
. The proof of Theorem
3
is divided in two steps: namely, we establish an upper and a lower bound for
\lambda_{1}(M,h,rA)
.

\bullet\quad
For simplicity of notation, as
(M,h)
is fixed, in what follows we will write

\lambda_{k}(rA)\doteq\lambda_{k}(M,h,rA).

Upper bound.
We simply choose as test function for
\lambda_{1}(rA)
in the min-max principle (
2
) the constant function
u=1
. As
\nabla^{rA}1=-irA^{\sharp}
(the dual vector field of
A
) we get immediately:

\lambda_{1}(rA)\leq r^{2}\frac{\|A\|^{2}_{L^{2}(h)}}{{\rm vol}(M,h)}.

Lower bound.
In the sequel, for smooth complex valued functions
u,v
, we write

q(u,v)\doteq\int_{M}\langle\nabla^{rA}u,\overline{\nabla^{rA}v}\rangle_{h}dv_{h}\,,\ \ \ q(v)\doteq q(v,v)=\int_{M}|\nabla^{rA}v|_{h}^{2}dv_{h}.

We write the constant function
1=u_{1}+v
where
u_{1}
is an eigenfunction for
\lambda_{1}(rA)
and
v
is orthogonal to
u_{1}
in
L^{2}(h):=L^{2}(M,dv_{h})
, namely,
\int_{M}u_{1}\bar{v}dv_{h}=0
. We recall that the second eigenvalue
\lambda_{2}(rA)
can be characterized as in (
2
), where the minimum is taken among all functions which are
L^{2}
-orthogonal to the first eigenfunction. Choosing
v
as test function we have

\frac{q(v)}{\|v\|_{L^{2}(h)}^{2}}\geq\lambda_{2}(rA).

(13)

By Green’ formula:

q(u_{1},v)=\int_{M}\langle\nabla^{rA}u_{1},\overline{\nabla^{rA}v}\rangle_{h}dv_{h}=\lambda_{1}(rA)\int_{M}u_{1}\bar{v}\,dv_{h}=0

This implies that, again by Green’s formula:

q(v)=q(v,v)=q(1,v)=\int_{M}\langle\nabla^{rA}1,\overline{\nabla^{rA}v}\rangle_{h}dv_{h}=\int_{M}\langle\Delta^{rA}1,\bar{v}\rangle_{h}dv_{h}=r^{2}\int_{M}|A|_{h}^{2}\bar{v}dv_{h},

note that the last equality follows from (
1
) and the hypothesis
\delta(rA)=r\delta A=0
, so that
\Delta^{rA}1=r^{2}\lvert{A}\rvert^{2}_{h}
. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality:

q(v)\leq r^{2}\left(\int_{M}|A|^{4}_{h}dv_{h}\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{M}|v|^{2}dv_{h}\right)^{1/2}.

(14)

By inequality (
13
), we have
\lVert{v}\rVert_{L^{2}(h)}\leq q(v)^{\frac{1}{2}}(\lambda_{2}(rA))^{-\frac{1}{2}}
hence, substituting in (
14
):

q(v)\leq\frac{\int_{M}|A|_{h}^{4}dv_{h}}{\lambda_{2}(rA)}\cdot r^{4}

(15)

As
1=u_{1}+v
and
q(u_{1},v)=0
:

\frac{q(1)}{\|1\|_{L^{2}(h)}^{2}}=\frac{q(u_{1})+q(v)}{\|u_{1}\|_{L^{2}(h)}^{2}+\|v\|^{2}_{L^{2}(h)}}=\frac{\lambda_{1}(rA)\|u_{1}\|_{L^{2}(h)}^{2}+q(v)}{\|u_{1}\|_{L^{2}(h)}^{2}+\|v\|_{L^{2}(h)}^{2}}

which we can rewrite

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(rA)\|u_{1}\|_{L^{2}(h)}^{2}=\frac{q(1)}{{\rm vol}(M,h)}(\|u_{1}\|_{L^{2}(h)}^{2}+\|v\|_{L^{2}(h)}^{2})-q(v).

(16)

Dividing both sides by
\lVert{u_{1}}\rVert_{L^{2}(h)}^{2}

we obtain the following lower bound for
\lambda_{1}(rA)
:

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(rA)

\displaystyle=\frac{q(1)}{{\rm vol}(M,h)}+\frac{q(1)}{{\rm vol}(M,h)}\frac{\|v\|_{L^{2}(h)}^{2}}{\|u_{1}\|_{L^{2}(h)}^{2}}-\frac{q(v)}{\|u_{1}\|_{L^{2}(h)}^{2}}

(17)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{q(1)}{{\rm vol}(M,h)}-\frac{q(v)}{\|u_{1}\|_{L^{2}(h)}^{2}}.

(18)

We now observe that, as
r\to 0
, we have
\lambda_{2}(rA)\to\lambda_{2}
, the second eigenvalue of the usual Laplace-Beltrami operator, which is positive. Then,
using (
13
), (
15
) and the identity
\lVert{u_{1}}\rVert_{L^{2}(h)}^{2}={\rm vol}(M,h)-\lVert{v}\rVert_{L^{2}(h)}^{2}
, we see that if
\rho>0
is sufficiently small there are positive constants
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},C
such that, for all
r<\rho
:

q(v)\leq c_{1}r^{4},\quad\lVert{v}\rVert_{L^{2}(h)}^{2}\leq c_{2}r^{4},\quad\lVert{u_{1}}\rVert_{L^{2}(h)}^{2}\geq c_{3},\quad\dfrac{q(v)}{\lVert{u_{1}}\rVert_{L^{2}(h)}^{2}}\leq Cr^{4}.

which implies that

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(rA)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{q(1)}{{\rm vol}(M,h)}-Cr^{4}

(19)

\displaystyle=\frac{\|A\|_{L^{2}(h)}^{2}}{{\rm vol}(M,h)}r^{2}-Cr^{4}

which proves the theorem. ∎

4
The spectrum of a flat torus

We study here in detail the spectrum of
\Sigma_{1}
with a flat metric. Often we will indicate the compact, orientable, differentiable surface of genus
1
by
T
(torus) instead of
\Sigma_{1}
.

4.1
Flat torus as a quotient

A flat torus is the quotient of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
by a lattice
L
with the inherited Euclidean metric. Precisely, if the lattice is generated by the vectors
w_{1}=(x_{1},y_{1}),w_{2}=(x_{2},y_{2})
, then

L=\{mw_{1}+nw_{2}:m,n\in\mathbb{Z}\}

and

(T,\hat{h})=({\mathbb{R}}^{2}/L,h_{E}),

where
h_{E}
is the Euclidean metric. In this section, we will drop the subscript
h_{E}
from the Euclidean scalar product and the Euclidean pointwise norm. The parallelogram generated by
(w_{1},w_{2})
:

\mathcal{P}=\{aw_{1}+bw_{2}:a,b\in[0,1]\}.

is a
fundamental domain
of
(T,\hat{g})
. We have then
|\hat{h}|=|\mathcal{P}|
, namely, the volume of
(T,\hat{h})
is the (Euclidean) volume of the fundamental domain.

A function
f:T\to\mathbb{C}
is obtained from a function
\tilde{f}:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}
which is invariant under the lattice, that is
\tilde{f}(x+p)=\tilde{f}(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
p\in L
. In the next subsection we describe
the set of eigenfunctions of
\lambda_{1}(T,\hat{h},A)
for every harmonic potential
A
. For that, introduce the
dual lattice

L^{\star}
of
L

defined as

L^{\star}=\{p^{\star}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}:\langle{p^{\star}},{w}\rangle\in\mathbb{Z}\quad\text{for all}\quad w\in L\}.

If
(w_{1},w_{2})
is a basis of
L
, the pair
(w_{1}^{\star},w_{2}^{\star})
defined by the conditions:

\langle{w_{i}^{\star}},{w_{j}}\rangle=\delta_{ij}

is a basis of
L^{\star}
called the
dual basis of
(w_{1},w_{2})
.

4.2
Eigenfunctions of the magnetic Laplacian

The family of eigenfunctions is the same as the one of the Laplace-Beltrami operator for any potential
A
. Given
p^{\star}\in L^{\star}
consider the function
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}
defined as

f(x)=e^{2\pi i\langle{x},{p^{\star}}\rangle}.

One sees immediately that
f
is invariant under translations defined by the lattice
L
, hence it defines a function on
T
(which we still denote by
f
). Writing
x=(x_{1},x_{2})
and
p^{\star}=(p_{1},p_{2})
we can write

f(x_{1},x_{2})=e^{2\pi i(p_{1}x_{1}+p_{2}x_{2})}

and one sees immediately that

\Delta f=4\pi^{2}(p_{1}^{2}+p_{2}^{2})f=4\pi^{2}\lvert{p^{\star}}\rvert^{2}f

which shows that
4\pi^{2}\lvert{p^{\star}}\rvert^{2}
is an eigenvalue of the usual Laplacian.

Introduce a magnetic potential
A
on
(T,\hat{h})
, which corresponds to a parallel
1
-form on the quotient
T
; its pull-back by the canonical projection
\pi:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to T

is then a parallel
1
-form on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
which can be written
\pi^{\star}A=2\pi(\alpha dx_{1}+\beta dx_{2})
for some
(\alpha,\beta)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and which, by a slight abuse of language, we still denote
A
:

A=2\pi(\alpha dx_{1}+\beta dx_{2}),

(20)

keeping in mind that, for different lattices
L_{1},L_{2}
, the differential form in (
20
) represents two different potentials on the respective quotient tori
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}/L_{1},{\mathbb{R}}^{2}/L_{2}
.

We denote by
P_{A}=(\alpha,\beta)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and call it the
position vector
associated with
A
. We observe:

Lemma 14
.

The potential
A
on
T={\mathbb{R}}^{2}/L
has integral flux along every loop in
T
if and only if
P_{A}
belongs to the dual lattice
L^{\star}
.

The proof is immediate from the observation that
A
has integral flux along the loop corresponding to
w\in L
if and only if
\langle{P_{A}},{w}\rangle\in\mathbb{Z}
.

We now compute the magnetic spectrum associated to
A
, starting from the definition (
1
):

\Delta_{A}f=\Delta f+|A|^{2}f+2i\langle A,df\rangle.

(21)

From (
20
) we have immediately

\lvert{A}\rvert^{2}=4\pi^{2}(\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}),

and, as
df=2\pi if(p_{1}dx_{1}+p_{2}dx_{2})
:

2i\langle{A},{df}\rangle=2i(4\pi^{2}if)(\alpha p_{1}+\beta p_{2})=-8\pi^{2}f(\alpha p_{1}+\beta p_{2}).

Therefore

\displaystyle\Delta_{A}f

\displaystyle=4\pi^{2}f\Big(p_{1}^{2}+p_{2}^{2}+\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}-2(\alpha p_{1}+\beta p_{2})\Big)

\displaystyle=4\pi^{2}f\lvert{P_{A}-p^{\star}}\rvert^{2}

which shows that, for any
p^{\star}\in L^{\star}
in the dual lattice, we get the magnetic eigenvalue

\lambda(p^{\star})=4\pi^{2}\lvert{P_{A}-p^{\star}}\rvert^{2}.

As the family of eigenfunctions thus obtained is a complete system in
L^{2}
(it is the same family of eigenfunctions of the usual Laplacian, see e.g.,
[
2
, §III.B]
), we obtain the following theorem.

Theorem 15
.

Assume that the flat torus
(T,\hat{h})
is the quotient of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
by the lattice
L
. Then a complete set of eigenfunctions of the magnetic Laplacian with potential
A=2\pi(\alpha dx_{1}+\beta dx_{2})
is indexed by
p^{\star}\in L^{\star}
as follows:

f_{p^{\star}}(x)=e^{2\pi i\langle{p^{\star}},{x}\rangle},

with associated eigenvalue

\lambda(p^{\star})=4\pi^{2}\lvert{P_{A}-p^{\star}}\rvert^{2},

where
P_{A}=(\alpha,\beta)
. The spectrum is therefore

{\rm spec}(T,\hat{h},A)=\{4\pi^{2}\lvert{P_{A}-p^{\star}}\rvert^{2}:p^{\star}\in L^{\star}\}.

As a consequence we get the following.

Corollary 16
.

For the flat torus
(T,\hat{h})
defined by the lattice
L
the lowest eigenvalue is

\lambda_{1}(T,\hat{h},A)=4\pi^{2}\inf_{p^{\star}\in L^{\star}}\lvert{P_{A}-p^{\star}}\rvert^{2}.

Figure 1:
The spectrum of a flat torus for a potential
A=2\pi(\alpha\,dx_{1}+\beta\,dx_{2})
is given by the squared distances of
P_{A}=(\alpha,\beta)
from the points of
L^{\star}
(i.e., the squared lengths of the segments in the picture), up to a factor
4\pi^{2}
. The first eigenvalue corresponds to the shortest distance, i.e., to the length of the bold segment in the picture.

Figure 2:
When
P_{A}\in L^{\star}
we get the usual Laplace-Beltrami spectrum.

We remark the following facts:

•

The eigenfunctions do not depend on the magnetic potential
A
: they are always the same.

•

The classical Laplacian spectrum is obtained precisely when
P_{A}\in L^{\star}
is a point of the dual lattice; in that case
\lambda_{1}=0
. In other words, the magnetic potential has integer fluxes and hence is gauge equivalent to
A=0
.

•

More generally, the spectrum does not change if we replace
P_{A}
with
P_{A}+p^{\star}
, where
p^{\star}
is any other point of

L^{\star}
. This gives a geometric meaning to the gauge invariance of the magnetic spectrum: if two potentials have fluxes which differ by integers, they give rise to the same magnetic spectrum.

•

Up to the multiplicative factor
4\pi^{2}
, the magnetic spectrum associated to the harmonic form
A
is given by the squares of the distances of
P_{A}
to the points of the dual lattice
\mathcal{L}^{\star}
. Seen on the quotient flat torus, the magnetic spectrum determines the set of the lengths of all geodesics joining the points
\pi(P_{0})
and
\pi(P_{A})
of
T
, where
\pi
is the projection onto
T
and
0
is the zero-form (this reduces to the marked length spectrum when
\pi(P_{A})=\pi(P_{0})
, which corresponds to the Laplace-Beltrami operator).

4.3
Moduli space of flat tori

The following fact is well-known, see e.g.,
[
2
, §III.B]
.

Lemma 17
.

Up to homotheties, congruences in
{\bf O}(2)
and change of basis in
{\bf SL}(2,{\mathbb{Z}})
, the lattice
L
admits a basis

w_{1}=(1,0),w_{2}=(p,q)
with
p\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]
,
q\geq p
and
p^{2}+q^{2}\geq 1
.

Figure 3:
Moduli space of flat tori.

Now, if
L
has basis
w_{1}=(1,0),w_{2}=(p,q)
, the dual basis
(w_{1}^{\star},w_{2}^{\star})
of
L^{\star}
defined in Subsection
4.1
is given by

w_{1}^{\star}=(1,-\frac{p}{q}),\quad w_{2}^{\star}=(0,\frac{1}{q}).

However it is convenient to use the basis of
L^{\star}
given by

v_{1}^{\star}=(1,\frac{1-p}{q}),\quad v_{2}^{\star}=(0,\frac{1}{q}).

(22)

It is indeed elementary to check that
(v_{1}^{\star},v_{2}^{\star})
is another basis of
L^{\star}
and that the parallelogram generated by
(w_{1},w_{2})
is homothetic to the one generated by
(v_{1}^{\star},v_{2}^{\star})
(up to a congruence in
{\bf O}(2)
): in fact, they have the same angles and proportional sides. Hence, the lattice
L^{\star}
is homothetic to
L
(up to a congruence in
{\bf O}(2)
). Moreover, the area of the parallelogram generated by
(w_{1},w_{2})
is
q
, while the area of the parallelogram generated by
(v_{1}^{\star},v_{2}^{\star})
is
\frac{1}{q}
. This shows that
L^{\star}\sim\frac{1}{|\hat{h}|}L
, where
|\hat{h}|=|\mathcal{P}|=q
is the volume of the fundamental domain of the torus generated by
L
.

Although we will not use it in the paper, it is perhaps interesting to express the spectrum of a flat torus in terms of the fluxes of the
1
-form
A
with respect to a homology basis (these fluxes in fact determine the
1
-form
A
). In particular, we will take the loops corresponding to the normal basis
(w_{1},w_{2})
, and we will call the corresponding fluxes
\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}
. We have

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\Phi_{1}=\langle(\alpha,\beta),(1,0)\rangle=\alpha\\
&\Phi_{2}=\langle(\alpha,\beta),(p,q)\rangle=\alpha p+\beta q\end{aligned}\right.

(23)

We can write
(\alpha,\beta)
in term of the fluxes
\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}
and of the parameters
p,q
of the flat torus in the moduli space

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\alpha=\Phi_{1}\\
&\beta=\langle(\alpha,\beta),(p,q)\rangle=-\frac{p}{q}\Phi_{1}+\frac{1}{q}\Phi_{2}\end{aligned}\right.\quad\text{and}\quad\left\{\begin{aligned} &\alpha=\Phi_{1}\\
&\beta=-\frac{p}{q}\Phi_{1}+\frac{1}{q}\Phi_{2}\end{aligned}\right..

We have the following.

Corollary 18
.

|\hat{h}|\lambda_{1}(T,\hat{h},A)=4\pi^{2}\min_{p^{\star}=(p_{1},p_{2})\in L^{\star}}d_{\hat{h}}(P_{A},L^{\star})^{2}=4\pi^{2}\min_{p^{\star}=(p_{1},p_{2})\in L^{\star}}\left(q(\Phi_{1}-p_{1})^{2}+\frac{(p\Phi_{1}-\Phi_{2}+qp_{2})^{2}}{q}\right).

We conclude this section by remarking that one can compute the magnetic spectrum of a
d
-dimensional flat torus
(T^{d},\hat{h})
: it is
\mathbb{R}^{d}/L
where
L
is a lattice of full rank. The dual lattice
L^{\star}
is defined exactly as for
d=2
and we have the analogous of Theorem
15
:

Theorem 19
.

Assume that the flat torus
(T^{d},\hat{h})
is the quotient of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by the lattice
L
. Then a complete set of eigenfunctions of the magnetic Laplacian with potential
A=2\pi(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}dx_{i})
is given by
\{e^{2\pi i\langle p^{\star},x\rangle}:p^{\star}\in L^{\star}\}
, and the spectrum is given by

{\rm spec}(T^{d},\hat{h},A)=\left\{4\pi^{2}|P_{A}-p^{\star}|^{2}:p^{\star}\in L^{\star}\right\},

where
P_{A}=(\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{d})
.

5
Optimization problems for the lowest eigenvalue of flat tori

Let
(T,\hat{h})
be a flat
2
-torus. Recall that, if
A=2\pi(\alpha dx+\beta dy)
and
P_{A}=(\alpha,\beta)
, then

\lambda_{1}(T,\hat{h},A)=4\pi^{2}\min_{p^{\star}\in L^{\star}}\lvert{P_{A}-p^{\star}}\rvert^{2}

where
P_{A}=\dfrac{1}{2\pi}A
. We identify

The conformal invariants in genus one

We now evaluate the conformal invariants introduced in Subsection
2.4
when the genus is
1
. Recall their definitions:

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{g},[h],A)

\displaystyle=\sup_{h^{\prime}\in[h]}\lvert{h^{\prime}}\rvert\lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{g},h^{\prime},A)

(24)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{g},[h])

\displaystyle=\sup_{A:dA=0}\Lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{g},[h],A)

(25)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{g})

\displaystyle=\inf_{[h]}\Lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{g},[h])

(26)

The following theorem shows that the supremum in the first invariant is attained at the flat metric in the conformal class.

Theorem 24
.

Given any metric
h
on the
2
-torus
\Sigma_{1}
one has

\lvert{h}\rvert\lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{1},h,A)\leq\lvert{\hat{h}}\rvert\lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{1},\hat{h},A)

where
\hat{h}
is the unique (up to homotheties) flat torus in the conformal class of
h
. Equality holds iff
h
and
\hat{h}
are homothetic. In particular:

\Lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{1},[h],A)=\lvert{\hat{h}}\rvert\lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{1},\hat{h},A).

Proof.

First observe that the metrics
h
and
\hat{h}
, being conformal, share the same harmonic
1
-forms. We then take an eigenfunction
u
associated to
\lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{1},\hat{h},A)
and use it as test-function for
\lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{1},h,A)
.
From the calculation in Section
4
it follows that all eigenfunctions of flat tori have constant modulus, hence we can take
u
so that
\lvert{u}\rvert=1
on
\Sigma_{1}
.
We now have:

\displaystyle\lvert{h}\rvert\lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{1},h,A)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{1},h,A)\int_{\Sigma_{1}}\lvert{u}\rvert^{2}dv_{h}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Sigma_{1}}\lvert{\nabla^{A}u}\rvert_{h}^{2}dv_{h}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma_{1}}\lvert{\nabla^{A}u}\rvert_{\hat{h}}^{2}dv_{\hat{h}}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{1},\hat{h},A)\int_{\Sigma_{1}}\lvert{u}\rvert^{2}dv_{\hat{h}}

\displaystyle=\lvert{\hat{h}}\rvert\lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{1},\hat{h},A)

where in the first inequality we used the min-max principle, and in the second equality the conformal invariance of the magnetic Dirichlet energy (see e.g.,
[
13
, Appendix C]
). The equality case follows from the expression of the magnetic Laplacian under conformal change. Assume that
h=\phi\hat{h}
for
\phi\in C^{\infty}(\Sigma)
, and let
\Delta_{h,A}
(resp.
\Delta_{\hat{h},A}
) be the respective magnetic Laplacians with the same potential form
A
. Then we have

\Delta_{h,A}=\dfrac{1}{\phi}\Delta_{\hat{h},A}.

If equality holds, then
u
(which is an eigenfunction of
\Delta_{\hat{h},A}
) must be an eigenfunction also of
\Delta_{h,A}
and this forces
\phi
to be constant, hence
h,\hat{h}
are homothetic. Conversely, if
h,\hat{h}
are homothetic it is immediate that the respective normalized eigenvalues are the same.

∎

,

and

7
Some remarks on the conformal invariants

7.1
The infimum in the conformal class is zero

Theorem 25
.

We have, for a given conformal class of metrics
[h]
on
\Sigma_{g}
and a given closed potential
A
:

\inf_{h^{\prime}\in[h]}\lvert{h^{\prime}}\rvert\lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{g},h^{\prime},A)=0.

Proof.

The proof amounts to construct a family of metrics
h_{\epsilon}
, conformal to
h
, such that the normalized ground state energy
\lvert{h_{\epsilon}}\rvert\lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{g},h_{\epsilon},A)\to 0
as
\epsilon\to 0
.
Pick a point
p\in\Sigma_{g}
and consider the ball
B(p,\epsilon)
of radius
\epsilon
. When
\epsilon\to 0
, the ball
B(p,\epsilon)
is quasi-isometric to a Euclidean ball of radius
\epsilon
(see
[
9
, Lemma 2.3]
), and one can show that, for our purposes, we can assume that being only quasi-isometric to a Euclidean ball does not affect the final statement (see
[
9
, p. 343]
).

Thus, we can assume that each such ball is Euclidean. By stereographic projection,
B(p,\epsilon)
is mapped conformally to the domain
\Omega_{\epsilon}\doteq\mathbb{S}^{2}\setminus B(\epsilon)
, the complement of a ball of radius
\epsilon
on the round sphere of radius
1
. We then replace
B(p,\epsilon)
with
\Omega_{\epsilon}
to obtain a metric
h_{\epsilon}
in the conformal class of
h
(see this construction in
[
9
, Par. 3]
). As
\Omega_{\epsilon}
is simply connected, the potential
A
is exact there, and up to a gauge transformation, the restriction of
A
to
\Omega_{\epsilon}
may then be supposed to be equal to
0
. Take
u
to be the first Dirichlet eigenfunction of
\Omega_{\epsilon}
; since it has support in
\Omega_{\epsilon}
, we see that
\Delta_{A}=\Delta u
. It is well known that the first eigenvalue of the Dirichlet problem on
\Omega_{\epsilon}
, say
\lambda(\epsilon)
, tends to
0
with
\epsilon
. We now extend
u
by zero outside
\Omega_{\epsilon}
and use this extension as test-function for
\lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{g},h_{\epsilon},A)
; we get

\lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{g},h_{\epsilon},A)\leq\lambda(\epsilon)

which tends to zero with
\epsilon
. Note that, in the conformal change, the volume of
h_{\epsilon}
is controlled from above:
\lvert{h_{\epsilon}}\rvert\leq c\lvert{h}\rvert
, hence the normalized eigenvalue tends to zero as well, which shows the assertion.
∎

7.2
A uniform bound for hyperbolic metrics

Theorem 26
.

There is a constant
C=C(g)<+\infty
depending only on the genus

, such that, for any hyperbolic metric

on
\Sigma_{g}
and any harmonic potential

, one has:

\lvert{h}\rvert\lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{g},h,A)\leq C(g).

Proof.

Every hyperbolic surface has a point

with injectivity radius

, where

is the Margulis constant (see
[

]
).
Then,

is an embedded hyperbolic disc of radius

; since it is simply connected,

is exact on

so that, up to a gauge transformation, one can assume that

is

.
As in Example 1, a universal upper bound of

is then given by the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of

, denoted

and not depending on

. As the area of a hyperbolic surface of genus

, there is a universal upper bound

and does not depend on the conformal class.

∎

Higher dimensional flat tori

A

\mathbb{R}^{d}
by a lattice

with the inherited Euclidean metric. Precisely, if the lattice is generated by

L=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}w_{i}\,:\,k_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}.

We also write
(T^{d},\hat{h})=(\mathbb{R}^{d}/L,h_{E})
, where

is the Euclidean metric. The spectrum of
d
-dimensional flat tori is computed as in Section
4
but we recall it here for completeness:

Theorem 28
.

Assume that the flat torus
(T^{d},\hat{h})
is the quotient of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by the lattice
L
. Then a complete set of eigenfunctions of the magnetic Laplacian with potential
A=2\pi(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}dx_{i})
is given by

, and the spectrum is given by

{\rm spec}(T^{d},\hat{h},A)=\left\{4\pi^{2}|P_{A}-p^{\star}|^{2}:p^{\star}\in L^{\star}\right\},

where
P_{A}=(\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{d})
.
The lowest eigenvalue is

\lambda_{1}(T^{d},\hat{h},A)=4\pi^{2}\inf_{p^{\star}\in L^{\star}}\lvert{P_{A}-p^{\star}}\rvert^{2}.

We prove that for flat
d
-dimensional tori, ground state-isospectrality implies isometry:

Theorem 29
.

Assume that the flat metrics
h,h_{0}
on
T^{d}
have the same ground state spectrum. Then
h_{0}
and
h
are isometric.

Proof.

Let
(T^{d},h)
be a flat torus. Its Jacobian torus is
{\rm Jac}(T^{d},h)={\rm Har}(h)/L^{\star}
, where now
L^{\star}
is generated by a dual basis
(\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{d})
of a given homology basis. We can identify the Jacobian torus of
(T^{d},h)
with
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}/\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with the metric

\Gamma_{jk}(h)=\langle{\alpha_{j}},{\alpha_{k}}\rangle_{L^{2}(h)},

where we take
\alpha_{j}=dx_{j}
,
j=1,...,d
.
In other words, the metric is
h=\sum_{j,k}\Gamma_{jk}dx_{j}dx_{k}
. The matrix
\Gamma(h)
with entries
\Gamma_{jk}
is the
Gram matrix
associated to the metric
h
(see also Subsection
9.1
).

Let
h_{0},h
be two flat metrics on
T^{d}
. It is clear that if
\Gamma(h)=c\Gamma(h_{0})
then the respective Jacobian tori are homothetic. Since
{\rm Jac}(T^{d},h)
is homothetic to the dual torus of
(T^{d},h)
, taking again duals, we conclude that
h
and
h_{0}
are homothetic:
h=bh_{0}
. Now observe that
\lambda_{1}
rescales as in the Laplace-Beltrami case, that is:

\lambda_{1}(T^{d},h,\frac{1}{n}(\alpha_{j}+\alpha_{k}))=\frac{1}{b^{2}}\lambda_{1}(T^{d},h_{0},\frac{1}{n}(\alpha_{j}+\alpha_{k}))

for all

.
Hence, by ground state-isospectrality we conclude that
b=1
, i.e.,
h
and
h_{0}
are isometric.

It remains to show that if
h_{0}
and
h
have the same ground state spectrum, then

\Gamma(h)=c\Gamma(h_{0}).

From the consequence (
7
) of Theorem
3
we have the asymptotics

\lim_{r\to 0}\dfrac{\lambda_{1}(T^{d},h,r(\alpha_{j}+\alpha_{k}))}{r^{2}}=\dfrac{1}{\lvert{h}\rvert}\lVert{\alpha_{j}+\alpha_{k}}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(h)}=\frac{\Gamma_{jj}(h)+\Gamma_{kk}(h)+2\Gamma_{jk}(h)}{\lvert{h}\rvert}.

We have the analogous limits for

. Taking

and passing to the limit as

we have, by our hypothesis:

\lvert{h_{0}}\rvert\left(\Gamma_{jj}(h)+\Gamma_{kk}(h)+2\Gamma_{jk}(h)\right)=\lvert{h}\rvert\left(\Gamma_{jj}(h_{0})+\Gamma_{kk}(h_{0})+2\Gamma_{jk}(h_{0})\right)

for all

. It is an easy exercise to conclude that

\Gamma_{jk}(h)=\dfrac{\lvert{h}\rvert}{\lvert{h_{0}}\rvert}\Gamma_{jk}(h_{0})

for all

. This concludes the proof.

On the other hand, the invariant
\Lambda_{1}(T^{d})
is a rather complicate object, already in three dimensions. This problem is related to the so-called
Delaunay triangulation
(see e.g.,
[
28
]
) of a point set.
Delaunay triangulations have been extensively studied in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and also in higher dimensions, and are a central topic in Computational Geometry as they enjoy several optimality properties. The interested reader may refer to
[
28
]
and references therein.

9
Proof of Theorem
6

We assume through this section that
\Sigma_{g}
is the (unique) differentiable compact, orientable surface of genus
g
. The pair
(\Sigma_{g},h)
denotes the surface
\Sigma_{g}
endowed with the Riemannian metric
h
. For simplicity of notation, as the genus is fixed in the discussion, we simply write

\lambda_{1}(h,A)\doteq\lambda_{1}(\Sigma_{g},h,A).

We prove the main theorem for an arbitrary genus
g
. When
g=1
we decided to provide, at the end of the paper, a simple, self-contained proof using only the uniformization theorem; this is done to illustrate the machinery needed in the general case in the simplest possible geometric situation. We mainly refer to the classical textbook by Farkas and Kra
[
17
]
.

9.1
Canonical homology basis and Gram matrix

We fix a so-called
canonical homology basis

\chi=(\chi_{1},\dots,\chi_{2g})
in
H_{1}(\Sigma,\mathbb{Z})
. This means that
\chi
is made up of two families of closed curves
a_{j},b_{j}
so that

(\chi_{1},\dots,\chi_{2g})\doteq(a_{1},\dots,a_{g},b_{1}\dots,b_{g})

and, if the dot denotes the oriented intersection number (the sum of the number of intersections, counted
+1
if the orientation of the intersection agrees with the orientation of
\Sigma
, and
-1
otherwise), we have the relations:

a_{j}\cdot a_{k}=0,\quad b_{j}\cdot b_{k}=0,\quad a_{j}\cdot b_{k}=\delta_{jk},\quad b_{j}\cdot a_{k}=-\delta_{jk}.

It follows that the
2g\times 2g
intersection matrix
J_{jk}=\chi_{j}\cdot\chi_{k}
of a canonical homology basis is

J=\begin{pmatrix}0&I_{g}\\
-I_{g}&0\\
\end{pmatrix}

Associated to
\chi
there is a dual basis of the integral cohomology space
H^{1}(\Sigma,\mathbb{Z})
which is made-up of closed
1
-forms

\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{2g})

so that, by definition,
\int_{\chi_{j}}\alpha_{k}=\delta_{jk}
. The dual basis is determined up to an exact
1
-form on each component. The cohomology class of
\alpha
is then a basis for the cohomology space

H^{1}(\Sigma_{g},\mathbb{Z})
and also of
H^{1}(\Sigma_{g},\mathbb{R})
(over
\mathbb{R}
). Of course, by the Hodge theorem, we can assume that
\alpha
consists of harmonic forms.

Recall from Subsection
2.4
that the dual basis
\alpha
generates a lattice in
{\rm Har}(h)
denoted
L^{\star}
. The quotient

{\rm Jac}(h)\doteq{\rm Har}(h)/L^{\star}

endowed with the
L^{2}(h)
metric, is a flat
2g
-dimensional torus, called
Jacobian torus
of
h
.

\bullet\quad
The Jacobian torus depends only on the conformal class of
h
.

It is clear that
{\rm Jac}(h)
is isometric with
{\mathbb{R}}^{2g}/\mathbb{Z}^{2g}
endowed with the metric
\Gamma_{jk}dx_{i}dx_{j}
where

\Gamma_{jk}=\langle{\alpha_{j}},{\alpha_{k}}\rangle_{L^{2}(h)}.

The matrix
\Gamma=\Gamma(h)
with entries
\Gamma_{jk}
is called the
Gram matrix
associated to the metric
h
; it is uniquely determined by the canonical homology basis we started with and the conformal class of
h
.

The proof of Theorem
4
is divided in two steps:

•

Step 1.
We first prove that if two metrics
h,h_{0}
have the same ground state spectrum, then they have the same volume and the same Gram matrix:
\Gamma(h)=\Gamma(h_{0})
.
In particular,
{\rm Jac}(h)={\rm Jac}(h_{0})
.

The proof uses the asymptotics of
\lambda_{1}(h,rA)
as
r\to 0
, proved in Section
3
, and the fact that the fixed homology basis is canonical, so that the associated Gram matrices have unit determinant (thanks to the so-called Riemann period relations).

•

Step 2.
We prove that if two metrics
h
and
h_{0}
satisfy
{\rm Jac}(h)={\rm Jac}(h_{0})
then they are conformal.

Here we use the conformal structure of
(\Sigma,h)
and a famous result in Riemann surface theory, the theorem of Torelli.

9.2
Proof of Step 1

We prove here the following theorem:

Theorem 31
.

Assume that the metrics
h
and
h_{0}
have the same ground state spectrum.
Then
h
and
h_{0}
have the same volume and
\Gamma(h)=\Gamma(h_{0})
.
In particular,
{\rm Jac}(h)={\rm Jac}(h_{0})
.

This is done in three steps: first we prove that the Gram matrices are homothetic; then we study in more detail the structure of the Gram matrix; finally we use this information to conclude.

9.2.1
The Gram matrices are homothetic

Theorem 32
.

Assume
h
and
h_{0}
have the same ground state spectrum, and let
\Gamma(h)
(resp.
\Gamma(h_{0})
) be the respective Gram matrices relative to the same canonical homology basis
\chi
. Then

\Gamma(h)=c\Gamma(h_{0}),\quad\text{with}\quad c=\frac{\lvert{h}\rvert}{\lvert{h_{0}}\rvert}.

Proof.

Given the basis
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{2g})
of
H^{1}(\Sigma_{g},\mathbb{R})
introduced above, we project this basis orthogonally on
{\rm Har}(h)
and obtain the basis

\alpha^{h}=(\alpha^{h}_{1},\dots,\alpha^{h}_{2g})

of
{\rm Har}(h)
. Since
\alpha^{h}_{k}
differ from
\alpha_{k}
by an exact form, we see:

\oint_{\chi_{j}}\alpha^{h}_{k}=\oint_{\chi_{j}}\alpha_{k}=\delta_{jk}

that is,
\alpha^{h}
is the unique basis of
{\rm Har}(h)
which is dual to
\chi
.
In a similar manner we obtain the basis

\alpha^{h_{0}}=(\alpha^{h_{0}}_{1},\dots,\alpha^{h_{0}}_{2g})

which is the unique basis of
{\rm Har}(h_{0})
dual to
\chi
. By gauge invariance and our hypothesis we have, for all
j,k,n
:

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(h,\frac{1}{n}(\alpha^{h}_{j}+\alpha^{h}_{k}))

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}(h,\frac{1}{n}(\alpha_{j}+\alpha_{k}))

(29)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}(h_{0},\frac{1}{n}(\alpha_{j}+\alpha_{k}))

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}(h_{0},\frac{1}{n}(\alpha^{h_{0}}_{j}+\alpha^{h_{0}}_{k}))

We now apply Theorem
3
to the
h
-harmonic form
\frac{1}{n}(\alpha^{h}_{j}+\alpha^{h}_{k}))
and get:

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{2}\lambda_{1}(h,\frac{1}{n}(\alpha^{h}_{j}+\alpha^{h}_{k}))

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\lvert{h}\rvert}\lVert{\alpha^{h}_{j}+\alpha^{h}_{k}}\rVert_{L^{2}(h)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\lvert{h}\rvert}\Big(\Gamma_{jj}(h)+\Gamma_{kk}(h)+2\Gamma_{jk}(h)\Big).

We apply Theorem
3
to the
h_{0}
-harmonic form
\frac{1}{n}(\alpha^{h_{0}}_{j}+\alpha^{h_{0}}_{k}))
and get the corresponding equality; taking into account the equality in (
29
), we have, for all
j,k

\dfrac{1}{\lvert{h}\rvert}\Big(\Gamma_{jj}(h)+\Gamma_{kk}(h)+2\Gamma_{jk}(h)\Big)=\dfrac{1}{\lvert{h_{0}}\rvert}\Big(\Gamma_{jj}(h_{0})+\Gamma_{kk}(h_{0})+2\Gamma_{jk}(h_{0})\Big)

and it is an easy exercise to conclude that

\Gamma_{jk}(h)=\dfrac{\lvert{h}\rvert}{\lvert{h_{0}}\rvert}\Gamma_{jk}(h_{0})

for all
j,k
.
∎

9.2.2
Riemann relations, the Hodge-star operator and the structure of the Gram matrix

Here we fix a generic metric
h
(and the canonical homology basis
\chi
) and write simply
\Gamma
for
\Gamma(h)
. In what follows, we omit the subscript
h
: therefore, it is tacitly assumed that the operator
\star
refers to
\star_{h}
, the Hodge-star operator for the metric
h
. Similarly, we denote
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{2g})
the basis of
{\rm Har}(h)
dual to
\chi
. From
[
17
, p. 58]
we see:

J_{kj}=-\langle{\alpha_{k}},{\star\alpha_{j}}\rangle.

The operator
\star
preserves harmonic forms. Let
G
be its matrix in the basis
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{2g})
. Writing the basis as a column vector (as in
[
17
]
), we see:

\begin{pmatrix}\star\alpha_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\star\alpha_{2g}\end{pmatrix}=G\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\alpha_{2g}\end{pmatrix}

which means

\star\alpha_{k}=\sum_{m=1}^{2g}G_{km}\alpha_{m}.

Now:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{kj}

\displaystyle=\langle{\alpha_{k}},{\alpha_{j}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle{\star\alpha_{k}},{\star\alpha_{j}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{2g}G_{km}\langle{\alpha_{m}},{\star\alpha_{j}}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\sum_{m=1}^{2g}G_{km}J_{mj}

that is

\Gamma=-GJ

and since
J^{2}=-I
we conclude:

Lemma 33
.

The matrices
G
and
\Gamma
are related by
G=\Gamma J
.

Now we write
\Gamma
as a block matrix

\Gamma=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\\
\end{pmatrix}.

Since
\Gamma
represents a Riemannian metric,
\Gamma
is symmetric and positive; therefore we have

A=A^{t},\quad D=D^{t},\quad C=B^{t},

moreover
A>0
and
D>0
. Hence we can write

\Gamma=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
B^{t}&D\\
\end{pmatrix}

(30)

which implies that

G=\Gamma J=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
B^{t}&D\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&I\\
-I&0\\
\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-B&A\\
-D&B^{t}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(31)

Knowing that
G^{2}=-I
we obtain the following identities:

AD-B^{2}=I,\quad DB=B^{t}D,\quad BA=AB^{t}.

We summarize as follows.

Theorem 34
.

Let
\Gamma=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
B^{t}&D\\
\end{pmatrix}
be the Gram matrix in a given metric
h
. Then:

i)

The matrix of the star operator
\star_{h}
is
G=\begin{pmatrix}-B&A\\
-D&B^{t}\\
\end{pmatrix}
;

ii)

We have the identities

AD-B^{2}=I,\quad DB=B^{t}D,\quad BA=AB^{t}.

Remark 35
.

Recall the definition of real symplectic matrices:

{\rm Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{R})=\{P\in M_{2g\times 2g}({\mathbb{R}}):P^{t}JP=J\}.

The Riemann period relations
ii)
above are equivalent to the identity

\Gamma J\Gamma=J

and since
\Gamma=\Gamma^{t}
and
\Gamma
is positive we have:

Theorem 36
.

The Gram matrix
\Gamma
is a (real) symplectic matrix of determinant
1
. In particular, the Jacobian torus of any Riemannian surface
(\Sigma,h)
is a flat
2g
-dimensional torus of unit volume.

To finish the remark, recall Siegel’s space (see
[
7
]
):

\mathcal{H}_{2g}=\{P\in\mathcal{P}_{2g}:PJP=J\}

where
\mathcal{P}_{2g}
(positive square matrices of determinant
1
) is acted upon by
{\rm Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z})
. The quotient

{\rm Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z})/\mathcal{H}_{2g}

parametrizes the principally polarized abelian varieties of complex dimension
g
. Note that the Gram matrix
\Gamma\in\mathcal{H}_{2g}
.

9.2.3
Conclusion of the proof of Theorem
31

From Theorem
32
we know that
\Gamma(h)=\frac{\lvert{h}\rvert}{\lvert{h_{0}}\rvert}\Gamma(h_{0})
. Since
\Gamma(h)
and
\Gamma(h_{0})
have both determinant
1
by Theorem
36
we must have
\lvert{h}\rvert=\lvert{h_{0}}\rvert
and then
\Gamma(h)=\Gamma(h_{0})
.
∎

9.3
Proof of Step 2: conformal structure and Torelli’s theorem

To finish the proof of the main result, we will prove the following:

Theorem 37
.

Assume that the metrics
h
and
h_{0}
satisfy
{\rm Jac}(h)={\rm Jac}(h_{0})
. Then
h
and
h_{0}
are conformal.

In fact, it is enough to show that
h
and
h_{0}
have the same Gram matrix with respect to the same fixed canonical homology basis
\chi
. The proof of Theorem
37
is divided into three steps. The main tool that we use is a fundamental result in Riemann surfaces theory, Torelli’s theorem, which relates the conformal structure of a Riemann surface with its normalized period matrix. Hence we will start by associating in a natural way a Riemann surface to a metric
h
; then we will describe the normalized period matrix of the Riemann surface with respect to a canonical homology basis; finally we prove that the Gram matrix determines the normalized period matrix and conclude.

9.3.1
A Riemann surface naturally associated to the metric
h

This subsection is quite standard, we add it for convenience of the reader. Again let
(\Sigma,h)
be an oriented Riemannian manifold of dimension
2
. There is a natural way to introduce an almost complex structure
J_{h}
on
\Sigma
, by setting

J_{h}\doteq\star_{h},

where
\star_{h}
is the Hodge-star operator induced by the Riemannian metric, which is globally defined because
\Sigma
is orientable.

Proposition 38
.

The almost complex structure defined above is integrable, and uniquely defines a Riemann surface, denoted
(\Sigma,J_{h})
.

Needless to say, the Hodge-star operator and the family of harmonic
1
-forms on
(\Sigma,h)
and its associated Riemann surface
(\Sigma,J_{h})
coincide.

Proof.

According to the Newlander-Nirenberg theorem,
J
is integrable if and only if the Nijenhuis tensor

N(X,Y)=[JX,JY]-J[JX,Y]-J[X,JY]-[X,Y]

acting on the vector fields
X,Y
vanishes identically. Introduce local isothermal coordinates in a neighborhood of any given point, so that the metric is expressed as
ds^{2}=\lambda(x,y)(dx^{2}+dy^{2})
. As the Hodge-star operator is conformally invariant, we see that

J\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}=\dfrac{\partial}{\partial y},\quad J\dfrac{\partial}{\partial y}=-\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x},

and
N
vanishes on any pair of coordinate fields, because on such fields the components of
J
are constant and all brackets vanish. Since
N
is a tensor, it must vanish on every pair of vector fields.
∎

Observe that
(\Sigma,J_{h})
and
(\Sigma,J_{h_{0}})
are biholomorphic if and only if the metrics
h
and
h_{0}
are conformal.

9.3.2
Normalized period matrix

We follow Farkas and Kra
[
17
, pp. 56-64]
. Fix a canonical homology basis
\chi=(\chi_{1},\dots,\chi_{2g})
of the Riemann surface
\Sigma
, and let
\omega
be a holomorphic differential. Then the
period vector
of
\omega
with respect to
\chi
is

(\chi,\omega)=\left(\oint_{\chi_{1}}\omega,\dots,\oint_{\chi_{2g}}\omega\right)\in\mathbb{C}^{2g}.

Let now
\omega=(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{g})
be a basis for the complex vector space
\mathcal{H}
of holomorphic differentials. The
period matrix
of
\omega
with respect to
\chi
is the
g\times 2g
matrix
(\chi,\omega)
having rows
(\chi,\omega_{1}),\dots,(\chi,\omega_{g})
. We can write it

(\chi,\omega)=(B,C)

where
B
and
C
are
g\times g
matrices. It is standard that
B
and
C
are non-singular
g\times g
. By a suitable change of basis in
\mathcal{H}
the right-hand side is written
(I,B^{-1}C)
. This fact, and the Riemann period relations, have the following consequence (see e.g.,
[
25
, pp. 3-5]
)

Theorem 39
.

There is a unique basis
\zeta=(\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{g})
of
\mathcal{H}
such that the period matrix of
\zeta
is of the form

(I,Z),

where
Z=X+iY
and
Y
are real symmetric
g\times g
matrices and
Y
is positive definite.

\bullet\quad
The matrix
(I,Z)
is as above is called the
normalized period matrix
.
It is uniquely determined by the choice of the canonical homology basis.

Now assume that we choose another canonical homology basis, say
\tilde{\chi}=(\tilde{\chi}_{1},\dots,\tilde{\chi}_{2g})
; then
\tilde{\chi}=\chi M
for a symplectic matrix
M\in{\rm Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z})
.
Then:

(I,Z)M=(B,C)

for some invertible
B,C
, and the normalized period matrix with respect to
\tilde{\chi}
and
(\tilde{\zeta}_{1},\dots,\tilde{\zeta}_{g})
will be
(I,Z^{\prime})
with
Z^{\prime}=B^{-1}C
. Therefore we say that

\bullet\quad
Two normalized period matrices
(I,Z)
and
(I,Z^{\prime})
are said to be
symplectically equivalent
if there is a symplectic matrix
M
such that
(I,Z)M=(B,C)
and
B^{-1}C=Z^{\prime}
.

9.3.3
Statement of Torelli’s theorem and conclusion of the proof

We state Torelli’s Theorem in the following form (see
[
24
,
25
]
).

Theorem 40
.

Let
\Sigma
and
\Sigma^{\prime}
be two compact Riemann surfaces of genus
g
and let
(I,Z)
and
(I,Z^{\prime})
be the normalized period matrices with respect to canonical homology bases
\chi
of
\Sigma
and
\chi^{\prime}
of
\Sigma^{\prime}
, respectively. If
(I,Z)
and
(I,Z^{\prime})
are symplectically equivalent, then
\Sigma
and
\Sigma^{\prime}
are conformally equivalent.

Now recall that there is a unique differentiable structure underlying any compact Riemann surface, and we can choose a common canonical homology basis
\chi
for
\Sigma
and
\Sigma^{\prime}
. We conclude that if the normalized period matrices of
\Sigma
and
\Sigma^{\prime}
with respect to
\chi
coincide, then
\Sigma
and
\Sigma^{\prime}
are conformally equivalent.

In order to conclude the proof of Theorem
37
it remains to prove that the Gram matrix determines the normalized period matrix.

We follow Farkas and Kra
[
17
, p. 56]
. We fix a canonical homology basis
(\chi_{1},\dots,\chi_{2g})
with dual basis of harmonic forms
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{2g})
. The Gram matrix
\Gamma
becomes a block matrix, each block being
g\times g
:

\Gamma=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\\
\end{pmatrix}.

Next, we introduce the matrix
G
representing the Hodge star operator
\star
of
H
with respect to the basis
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{2g})
. If

\mathcal{A}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{2g})^{t},

then

\star\mathcal{A}=G\mathcal{A}.

Further work shows that, if

G=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&\lambda_{2}\\
\lambda_{3}&\lambda_{4}\\
\end{pmatrix}

then

\Gamma=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{2}&-\lambda_{1}\\
\lambda_{4}&-\lambda_{3}\\
\end{pmatrix}

and we have the identities:

\lambda_{1}=-B,\quad\lambda_{2}=A,\quad\lambda_{3}=-D,\quad\lambda_{4}=B^{t}.

Jumping to (2.8.1) one sees that the normalized period matrix
(I,Z)
(in Farkas-Kra notation
Z
is denoted
\Pi
) is such that

Z=(-\lambda_{3})^{-1}\lambda_{1}^{t}+i(-\lambda_{3})^{-1}.

By the previous identifications:

Z=-D^{-1}B^{t}+iD^{-1}.

(32)

Then, the Gram matrix
\Gamma
uniquely determines the normalized period matrix through the identities (
32
). Viceversa, if
Z=X+iY
then using Riemann’s period relations one sees that the Gram matrix is:

\Gamma=\begin{pmatrix}Y+XY^{-1}X&-XY^{-1}\\
-Y^{-1}X&Y^{-1}\\
\end{pmatrix}

We summarize in the following theorem.

Theorem 41
.

The Gram matrix
\Gamma
uniquely determines the normalized period matrix
Z
, and viceversa (both matrices being associated to the same canonical homology basis).

Therefore, by Torelli’s theorem, if two Riemann surfaces have the same Gram matrix (with respect to a common canonical homology basis) then they are conformal. The proof of Theorem
37
is now complete.

9.4
A simple proof for tori

In the case of genus one surfaces, i.e., for tori, it is quite elementary to follow the lines of the proof of Theorem
4
. Indeed, the fact that two tori with the same ground state spectrum have the same volume and belong to the same conformal class follows by simple computations, which in turn allow a better understanding of the proof of Theorem
4
.

Let
T
be a genus one orientable surface, i.e., a torus. Consider
(T,h)
where
h
is a Riemannian metric on
T
. We will show how the Gram matrix
\Gamma=\Gamma(h)
determines the conformal class of
h
. Recall that the Gram matrix is a conformal invariant. Let then
(T,\hat{h})
be the unique (up to homotheties) flat torus conformal to
(T,h)
. We can identify it with the square torus
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}^{2}
endowed with the metric
\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
b&c\end{pmatrix}
, with
a,c>0
and
ac-b^{2}>0
. We consider now the canonical homology basis
(\chi_{1},\chi_{2})
corresponding to
e_{1}=(1,0)
and
e_{2}=(0,1)
. The dual basis of harmonic forms is then
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
, with
\alpha_{1}=dx
,
\alpha_{2}=dy
. We can now compute the Gram matrix

\Gamma=\begin{pmatrix}\|\alpha_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}&\langle\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\rangle_{L^{2}}\\
\langle\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\rangle_{L^{2}}&\|\alpha_{2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{ac-b^{2}}}\begin{pmatrix}c&{-b}\\
-b&a\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
B&D\end{pmatrix},

where
A=\frac{c}{\sqrt{ac-b^{2}}}
,
B=\frac{-b}{\sqrt{ac-b^{2}}}
,
D=\frac{a}{\sqrt{ac-b^{2}}}
. Here we are using the same notation of (
30
) .
As we have said, this is also the Gram matrix of
(T,h)
(with respect to the same basis of harmonic
1
-forms), since
h
and
\hat{h}
are conformal.

We observe immediately that
{\rm det}(\Gamma)=1
. It is trivial to see that if we know
\Gamma
, then we know
\hat{h}
up to homotheties, and hence the conformal class of
(T,h)
.

We now calculate the normalized period matrix of
(T,h)
for the genus
1
case.

Given the canonical homology basis
(\chi_{1},\chi_{2})
, the intersection matrix
J
is
J=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
{-1}&0\end{pmatrix}
. Next we have to compute the matrix
G
of
\star
with respect to the basis
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
of harmonic
1
-forms. To do so we compute

\star\alpha_{1}=\frac{bdx+cdy}{\sqrt{ac-b^{2}}}\,,\ \ \ \star\alpha_{2}=\frac{-adx-bdy}{\sqrt{ac-b^{2}}}

and hence

G=\frac{1}{\sqrt{ac-b^{2}}}\begin{pmatrix}b&c\\
-a&-b\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-B&A\\
-D&B\end{pmatrix},

see (
31
).
We immediately see the relation
G=\Gamma J
, and the Riemann relations, again, become trivial.

We take now any holomorphic differential, for example,
\alpha=\alpha_{1}+i\star\alpha_{1}=dx+i\frac{bdx+cdy}{\sqrt{ac-b^{2}}}
, and compute its period matrix with respect to the fixed canonical homology basis
\chi=(\chi_{1},\chi_{2})
:

(\chi,w)=\left(1+i\frac{b}{\sqrt{ac-b^{2}}},i\frac{c}{\sqrt{ac-b^{2}}}\right)=(1-iB,iA)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}.

We know that there is a unique holomorphic differential
\zeta
such that
(\chi,\zeta)=(1,X+iY)
where
X,Y\in\mathbb{R}
,
Y>0
. We can compute it explicitly. In fact,

(\chi,w)=\left(1+i\frac{b}{\sqrt{ac-b^{2}}},i\frac{c}{\sqrt{ac-b^{2}}}\right)=\eta\left(1,\frac{b}{a}+i\frac{\sqrt{ac-b^{2}}}{a}\right)=\eta\left(1,\frac{-B+i}{D}\right)\,,\ \ \ \eta=1+i\frac{b}{\sqrt{ac-b^{2}}}=1-iB.

So the normalized period matrix is

\left(1,\frac{b}{a}+i\frac{\sqrt{ac-b^{2}}}{a}\right)=\left(1,\frac{-B+i}{D}\right).

and
\zeta=\eta w
.

Again it is straightforward to see that the conformal class
[h]
determines the Gram matrix
\Gamma
, which in turn determines the normalized period matrix, and vice-versa. Note that we have used the uniformization theorem, which states that in a conformal class of metrics on
\Sigma
there is a unique flat metric (up to homotheties).

Now we go back to ground state isospectrality. If two metrics
h_{1},h_{2}
on
