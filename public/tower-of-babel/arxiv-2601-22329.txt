Sparks of Rationality: Do Reasoning LLMs Align with Human Judgment and Choice?
Ala N. Tak, Amin Banayeeanzade, Anahita Bolourani, Fatemeh Bahrani, Ashutosh Chaubey, Sai Praneeth Karimireddy, Norbert Schwarz, Jonathan Gratch

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly positioned as decision engines for hiring, healthcare, and economic judgment, yet real-world human judgment reflects a balance between rational deliberation and emotion-driven bias. If LLMs are to participate in high-stakes decisions or serve as models of human behavior, it is critical to assess whether they exhibit analogous patterns of (ir)rationalities and biases. To this end, we evaluate multiple LLM families on (i) benchmarks testing core axioms of rational choice and (ii) classic decision domains from behavioral economics and social norms where emotions are known to shape judgment and choice. Across settings, we show that deliberate “thinking” reliably improves rationality and pushes models toward expected-value maximization. To probe human-like affective distortions and their interaction with reasoning, we use two emotion-steering methods: in-context priming (ICP) and representation-level steering (RLS). ICP induces strong directional shifts that are often extreme and difficult to calibrate, whereas RLS produces more psychologically plausible patterns but with lower reliability. Our results suggest that the same mechanisms that improve rationality also amplify sensitivity to affective interventions, and that different steering methods trade off controllability against human-aligned behavior. Overall, this points to a tension between reasoning and affective steering, with implications for both human simulation and the safe deployment of LLM-based decision systems.

Introduction

LLMs are rapidly moving from text generators to decision partners
(
50
)
in domains such as law
(
38
)
, healthcare
(
8
)
, and supply chain optimization
(
46
)
. In such settings, it is essential to characterize conditions under which models behave non-normatively. Classical Rational Choice and Expected-Utility Theories (EUT) model agents as maximizing a stable utility function under axioms of rationality
(
78
)
. However, decades of behavioral research show that human judgments often deviate from EUT and equilibrium prescriptions, through heuristics and biases, dual-process dynamics
(
32
)

and emotion-dependent shifts in judgment and choice
(
70
;
51
)
.
Most notably, Prospect Theory
(
34
)
incorporates affective regularities, such as probability insensitivity and loss aversion, into the formal choice models.

In this work, we ask whether LLMs exhibit human-like rational deliberation and emotion-driven biases in decision-making.

We address this question by first introducing a benchmark to test models’ compliance with the axioms of rationality. We then evaluate them on decision tasks that directly operationalize classical benchmarks where emotions demonstrably alter human decision behaviors, including tasks from behavioral economics, information processing, and social norms.

We empirically show that LLMs become more consistent with EUT when
reasoning
capabilities are enabled, i.e., when they generate
thinking
tokens before producing the final answer. This pattern suggests a form of meta-cognition that biases the model toward more explicit, value-maximizing behavior, weak loss aversion, and linear probability weighting. However, important deviations from human behavior remain, most notably a reversed endowment effect, near-complete ambiguity aversion, and a limited representation of monetary depreciation.

As a probe into whether LLMs exhibit human-like affective distortions, we assess if emotion interacts with reasoning in human-like ways. We use two naturally different steering methods: (1) In-context priming (ICP)
(
58
)
: emotional personas and vignettes that prompt the model to simulate a specified feeling. (2) Representation-level steering (RLS)
(
37
)
: low-rank vectors injected into hidden activations, designed to encode emotional directions, which alter internal processing.
Although prior work often treats ICP and RLS as equally effective
(
6
)
, we find that they lead to qualitatively different thinking traces: ICP maintains a neutral, reflective process that reasons about emotion, whereas RLS produces an emotion-colored tone. As represented in Fig.
1
, this design allows us to contrast “thinking about emotion” with “emotional thinking”.

Notably, our experiments reveal a distinct pattern depending on the steering approach. ICP shifts behavior in the direction aligned with humans, but with a magnitude which is often extreme and difficult to control; for example, models primed with mild fear
never
choose to gamble, reflecting the expected directional effect but with exaggerated magnitude, reflecting strong risk aversion. By contrast, RLS produces more human-like patterns predicted by psychological theory (e.g., the increase in risk aversion remains sensitive to expected payoff), but with smaller and less reliable effects.

Overall, our work presents several novel findings: (i) Thinking LLMs consistently elicit higher levels of rationality compared to non-thinking models, a finding observed across different LLM families and scales. (ii) In the absence of explicit task instructions, thinking models consistently default to maximizing expected gains as prescribed by EUT; (iii) thinking models are more faithful at reproducing emotion-linked behavioral shifts that are directionally aligned with humans; and (iv) while both ICP and RLS induce human-aligned effects, ICP produces strong shifts, whereas RLS yields more qualitatively human-like responses.

These results have three major implications. First, they challenge the assumption that model steering can move beyond surface style to reliably shape decision behavior in deployed systems
(
6
)
; for many tasks, it remains largely cosmetic without thinking mode. Second, they highlight a tension between alignment for simulation, where human-like emotional biases may be desirable, and alignment for consistent, unbiased decision-making, where susceptibility to steering is a safety concern. Finally, earlier work reports that reasoning models are especially susceptible to prompt injection
(
81
;
88
)
. Similarly, we find that reasoning can amplify vulnerability under emotional steering, allowing the model to rationalize the induced affect (e.g., see App.
C
).

Rationality in LLMs

In this section, we focus on assessing the rationality of LLMs and their emotionally steered variants. In fact, many decision domains we evaluate later presume the existence of a coherent preference, i.e., a stable ordering over alternatives that is invariant to framing and elicitation procedure. We therefore administer a diagnostic battery of four canonical axioms, i.e.,
Completeness
,
Transitivity
,
Continuity
, and
Independence
, noting that these are sufficient for an EUT representation
(
78
)
.

We first evaluate overall axiom compliance for
neutral
(unsteered) LLMs using our custom-designed tests. Among our tasks, some explicitly specify clear goals, while others provide looser guidance, allowing subjective interpretation and only evaluating the existence of a coherent preference. Fig.
2
reports the extent to which different models violate rationality axioms across these questionnaires.
Notably,
thinking-enabled
models exhibit consistently higher compliance rates, indicating more stable and internally consistent preference structures. A key takeaway is that deploying non-thinking models in economic or game-theoretic settings is fundamentally ungrounded, as these models often fail to satisfy even basic rationality requirements.
In the remainder of the paper, we therefore restrict utility estimation and preference analyses to reasoning models, specifically
Qwen-3-4B
(referred to as
Qwen3
). App.
D
provides further results and details on the experimental setup.

Emotion and Rationality.

In human decision studies, the relationship between emotion and rationality is commonly studied along two complementary axes: First, emotion may reduce
coherence
with rational-choice axioms, as it can override deliberation and produce systematic violations of axiomatic compliance
(
52
)
. Second, emotion may act as a
state variable
that only shifts underlying preferences, yet choices remain internally consistent and utility-maximizing, conditioned on the new utility
(
27
)
. We therefore assess the extent to which these two viewpoints hold for emotion-steered LLMs. We use ICP and RLS with varying steering strengths as prescribed by
6
. Specifically, ICP controls the steering strength by inserting lexical descriptors into the prompt, whereas RLS uses the vector injection coefficient for this purpose.
App.
A
describes further steering details and hyperparameters.

Fig.
2
shows that both ICP and RLS yield modest decreases in the axiom-consistency score; the amount of performance drop is correlated with the steering strength. This pattern supports the first viewpoint, indicating that emotional steering can partially disrupt preference coherence rather than merely inducing neutral preference shifts.
To study the second viewpoint, we nevertheless treat emotionally steered LLMs as approximately rational agents by setting the steering strength to
medium
for ICP and
35
for RLS, and focus on how emotions bias their preferences in the remainder of the paper. App.
D
provides further experiment results, including steering experiments with non-thinking models.

Key Finding:
Reasoning models elicit the highest compliance with axioms of rationality, with emotions only slightly dropping their performance.

Decision Domains

This section delineates the four domains through which we test whether steering methods alter decision behavior in ways that mirror well-documented effects of incidental emotions on humans’ choice and judgment. Across each domain, we decompose behaviors into theoretically interpretable components (e.g., EV sensitivity, probability weighting, and utility curvature). Incidental states are then introduced orthogonally to the decision task; We analyze theoretically grounded parameters, enabling direct comparison to human anchors and preregistered directional contrasts. See Fig.
3
for an overview of the tasks we study. App.
E
provides implementation details and prompt templates for every task, while App.
G
reports additional analyses and results.

4.1
Choices under Risk and Uncertainty

Risk Seeking.

We begin by examining risk attitudes under gain-only uncertainty using a canonical sure-thing vs lottery domain, a workhorse task in behavioral economics for identifying risk seeking, probability weighting, and the certainty effect. Formally, consider a choice between a risky lottery yielding a gain
G>0
with known objective probability
p\in[0,1]
and a sure amount
S>0
. A risk-neutral agent prefers the gamble whenever the Expected Value (EV) difference
\Delta EV=pG-S
is positive. In contrast, the
subjective
value difference between the risky and safe options is modeled as

\Delta U_{\text{risk}}\;=\;w(p;\alpha,\beta)\,u(G;\rho)\;-\;u(S;\rho),

where utility over gains follows a power function
u(x;\rho)=x^{\rho}
, and decision weights are captured by a Prelec probability-weighting function,

w(p;\alpha,\beta)\;=\;\exp\!\bigl[-\beta(-\ln p)^{\alpha}\bigr].

The subjective probability of choosing the risky option is then modeled as

P_{\text{risky-choice}}\;=\;\sigma\!\bigl(\tau\,\Delta U_{\text{risk}}+b\bigr),

where
\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})
is the logistic link, and
\alpha
,
\beta
,
\rho
,
\tau
, and
b
are free parameters estimated from choice behavior.

In our first study, we fix
\rho=1
by writing
u(x):=x
(identity utility over gains), and assume linear probability weighting
w(p;\alpha,\beta)=p
, meaning that
\Delta U_{\text{risk}}=\Delta EV
. Under these restrictions, we focus on extracting
P_{\text{risky-choice}}
by estimating
\tau
and
b
via an empirical curve-fitting. Specifically, we vary prompt templates and the values of
S
,
G
, and
p
, and for each resulting
\Delta EV
, we record the frequency with which the model selects the risky option. These empirical choice frequencies are then used to estimate the parameters.

When outcome probabilities are explicit, human studies show a divergence across negative emotions: anger tends to increase risk-taking, whereas fear tends to shift choices toward safety. This pattern is demonstrated both with naturally occurring and experimentally induced states and has become a canonical benchmark for testing emotion-risk links
(
42
)
. Meta-analytic syntheses further indicate that fear/anxiety tends to suppress risk-taking, while anger and, to a lesser extent, happiness tend to increase it, albeit with heterogeneity across tasks
(
56
;
7
;
80
)
.

Fig.
4
plots the fitted curves both under the neutral baseline and emotion steering. With thinking enabled,
Qwen3
model closely tracks the EV decision boundary: the curve transitions sharply around
\Delta EV=0
, indicating near-deterministic EV-consistent choice. Moreover, ICP produces large affect-dependent shifts and substantially attenuates EV sensitivity: fear drives near-zero gambling across the tested range, whereas anger increases gambling even when
\Delta EV<0
. In contrast, RLS yields more graded, directionally human-consistent modulations while largely preserving monotonic dependence on
\Delta EV
, resulting in comparatively more human-aligned changes.

Next, we extract the probability-weighting function, i.e., the function by which any individual subjectively over- or underperceives objective probabilities. Given that the empirical certainty equivalent
S^{*}(p)
approximately satisfies

u(S^{*}(p))\approx w(p;\alpha,\beta)\,u(G)
, we estimate parameters
(\alpha,\beta)
from observed certainty equivalents. Empirically, the model faces independent gain lotteries with known probabilities and EVs that straddle a sure-thing option.

Figure 5
:
Fitted Prelec probability-weighting functions, comparing neutral and fear steering.

Some accounts link weighting to affective processes: anticipated emotions tied to possible outcomes may contribute to characteristic distortions such as inverse-S weighting
(
11
)
.
Incidental affect can also modulate weighting directly, shifting both the
curvature
and

of

; for example, happiness is shown to increase overall optimism via higher decision weights, especially for small probabilities
(
68
)
.
Fig.
5
plots the fitted subjective-objective probability curves. With neutral thinking, the fitted mapping is close to linear, indicating little to no probability distortion (near-accurate use of stated
p
). ICP induces extreme curvature: under fear, weighting becomes strongly convex, such that even a high objective
p
receives a low subjective weight. RLS yields more graded distortions, underweighting small-to-moderate

, while remaining closer to baseline at large

(further results in App.
E.3
).

Finally, we extract the utility curvature parameter
\rho
, which governs sensitivity to gains. Fig.
6
plots the fitted
normalized
utility curves, with the dashed diagonal indicating the risk-neutral reference. Under the neutral thinking condition,
Qwen3
yields
\rho\approx 1
, consistent with near-linear valuation of gains. Under fear, the fitted exponent decreases (
\rho<1
) for both ICP and RLS, implying stronger diminishing marginal utility (greater risk aversion over gains). Across prompt variants, ICP effects are typically large and difficult to calibrate and even attenuated prompt wording does not reliably moderate the induced curvature, whereas RLS exhibits a smoother dose-response: varying the injection scale produces a gradual, monotonic shift in
\rho
away from the neutral baseline. See App.
E.1
for details of experimentation across LLMs and steering scales.

Loss Aversion.

Human decision-making exhibits a robust asymmetry between gains and losses: losses loom larger than objectively equivalent gains. In prospect-theoretic terms, this asymmetry is captured by the loss-aversion parameter
\lambda
.
Experimental work further suggests that incidental fear can increase loss sensitivity in mixed gambles, yielding larger estimated
\lambda

(
67
)
.
To port this benchmark, we present the model with a series of independent mixed gambles that pay a gain
G
and a loss
L
with probability
1/2
. We record accept/reject decisions and model acceptance with a logistic choice rule,

P_{\text{Accept}}=\sigma(\beta_{0}+\beta_{G}G+\beta_{L}L),

so the
50\%
indifference frontier (the iso-
0.5
line) satisfies

G(L)=({-\beta_{0}-\beta_{L}L})/{\beta_{G}}.

Under local linear valuation for small stakes, the slope of this frontier provides an interpretable loss-aversion index,

\lambda=-\beta_{L}/\beta_{G}
, with the prospect-theoretic reference boundary given by
G=\lambda L
when
\beta_{0}\approx 0
.

Fig.
8
shows that
Qwen3
in the neutral thinking condition has little evidence of classical loss aversion. Fear steering via RLS induces a positive intercept (a gain “entry fee” to accept
any
loss) consistent with higher loss sensitivity in gambles.
In contrast, ICP’s fear yields universal rejection, making
\lambda
unidentifiable using the above formula.
For visualization in this degenerate regime, we therefore plot a proxy frontier with a slope set slightly above the largest gain-loss ratio among rejected gambles, i.e., (
\lambda\approx 1.05\max(G/L)
).

Key Finding:
Neutral thinking models follow EV-maximizing decision rule. ICP steering amplifies the intended directional effects with often extreme magnitudes; RLS interventions produce qualitatively more human-like shifts.

Endowment Effect.

Owning an object inflates its stated value relative to what one would pay to acquire it
(
33
)
, yet incidental emotions modulate this gap in distinctive ways. Sadness reduces selling prices and increases buying prices, often reversing the classic Sell-Buy difference, whereas disgust broadly depresses both Buy and Sell values
(
45
)
. We port this benchmark with a within-model, within-item design that elicits Buy and Sell values for the same neutral object under otherwise identical descriptions.
Across all items, we obtain an aggregated Willing-To-Accept selling price
\overline{\text{WTA}}
and Willing-To-Pay buying price
\overline{\text{WTP}}
, summarizing the endowment effect via

\Delta_{E}\;=\;\overline{\text{WTA}}-\overline{\text{WTP}},

Fig.
9
shows that
Qwen3
exhibits a
reversed
endowment pattern: stated selling prices are systematically
lower
than buying prices (
\Delta_{E}<0
), opposite to the canonical human endowment effect. Emotional steering does not reliably eliminate or reverse this sign under either ICP or RLS, although the two methods induce different directional shifts in overall valuation. In App.
E.4
we show that similar trends appear in other thinking models. We also compare other task framings different from the original study, yet WTA still remains below WTP overall. One explanation is that LLMs, post-trained to be overly helpful, default to fair pricing norms that suppress their self-serving valuation.

Temporal Discounting.

The tendency to devalue delayed outcomes relative to immediate ones is commonly modeled through time-discounted utility.
Affective decision research shows that incidental emotions systematically modulate this tendency. Sadness reliably increases impatience in money-now versus money-later trade-offs (“myopic misery”), reducing willingness to wait for larger, delayed rewards
(
44
)
. Joy and fear, also modulate discounting, with the direction and effect magnitude depending on the induction domain, as illustrated by fMRI evidence
(
54
)
. We probe this using smaller-sooner vs. larger-later choices spanning delays and premiums. For each trial, the sooner option pays
A_{s}
at
t_{s}
and the later option pays
A_{\ell}
at
t_{\ell}
, with delay
d=t_{\ell}-t_{s}
and relative premium
r=(A_{\ell}/A_{s})-1
. We treat the binary choice
{P}_{\text{Later}}
as the outcome and fit a 2D logistic choice surface over
(d,r)
:

P_{\text{Later}}=\sigma\!\bigl(b_{0}+b_{d}\,d+b_{p}\,r\bigr).

Fig.
10
visualizes the fitted surface via iso-probability contours and reveals a qualitative divergence. For
Qwen3
, the fitted contours slope downward, implying that longer delays require
less
compensation to sustain indifference, which is inconsistent with normative discounting behavior. App.
E.5
reports results from the larger
Qwen3-8B
model that exhibits the expected monotonic structure: maintaining a fixed acceptance level requires larger premiums at longer delays, though it still displays weak discounting for short horizons.

4.2
Judgment and Belief Formation

Heuristic Stereotyping.

Social judgment literature indicates that incidental anger promotes heuristic, stereotype‐consistent evaluations, whereas sadness increases systematic, evidence‐based processing; these regularities are synthesized across laboratory studies
(
3
;
9
;
76
;
36
)
. To port this contrast, we adapt the original experimental design and use brief evidentiary facts balanced with credible points on both sides. We then inject a single category/role cue that either heuristically pushes toward a violation judgment or toward non-violation. We then calculate a stereotype-consistency index based on the judgments.

Figure 11
:
Emotion effects (Hedges’ g) across all domains and the
random-effects meta-analytic aggregation; rightward alignment is interpreted directionally and can be non-human-like when extreme.

Fig.
11
shows that neither anger nor sadness produces a reliable stereotype-consistent shift under ICP or RLS; estimated effects are statistically indistinguishable from zero. This null result is consistent with process-based accounts in affective science, where anger and sadness primarily modulate reliance on heuristic versus systematic processing. Because our evaluation elicits explicit deliberation in all conditions (thinking mode), this pathway is largely held constant, which may attenuate emotion-induced differences and yield near-zero effects. See App.
E.6
for further details.

Framing and Persuasion.

Discrete emotions shape persuasion when messages are framed to match their appraisal profiles. In classic emotion-message matching studies, incidental sadness increases support for a sadness-framed version of a policy, whereas incidental anger increases support for an anger-framed version of the same policy
(
17
)
. Crucially, this matching pattern emerges primarily for individuals high in need for cognition (NC), who engage in high elaboration, whereas individuals low in NC exhibit higher support under incidental sadness across frames
(
17
)
. Related work on gain-loss framing similarly documents mood‐mediated framing effects on persuasion
(
60
;
77
;
35
)
. In our adaptation, the model reads affect-neutral policy appeals, in which sadness vs. anger consequences are listed for an otherwise identical policy core, and then provides a single Likert support rating for each appeal (see App.
E.7
). Empirically,
Qwen3
shows the latter pattern–higher support under sadness than anger, with little evidence of emotion-message matching, closely resembling low‐NC human response profile despite the model’s explicit deliberation under thinking-mode.

Norm Enforcement and Moral Evaluation

Moral Condemnation.

Incidental affect can shift human moral evaluation, though the magnitude and reliability of some effects remain debated
(
83
;
39
)
. Disgust has been reported to amplify moral condemnation, often most for purity-related violations
(
79
)
, with
perceived harm
proposed as a key mediator
(
66
)
. Separately, incidental sadness is argued to reduce morally questionable intentions via increased attention to negative consequences and harm appraisals
(
62
)
.
We port this by having LLMs rate moral vignettes on: (i)
wrongness/punishment
(condemnation), (ii)
harm/consequences
, and (iii)
intention
or behavioral propensity. See App.
E.8
for implementation details. Fig.
12
shows a positive association between harm appraisal and condemnation for the unsteered
Qwen3
, consistent with the human anchor. Under disgust steering, both ICP and RLS primarily shift judgments upward (overall condemnation) while preserving the harm-consequence dependency (positive correlation).

Legal Blame.

Another societal domain links incidental affect to blame attribution, directly impacting legal judgment and public policy. Induced anger reliably increases individual blame and punitive responses in legal-style vignettes, an effect attenuated by accountability instructions
(
40
)
. By elevating perceived risk, fear shifts preferences toward protective policies, whereas anger increases support for punitive options; issue-specific experiments (e.g., drunk driving) reproduce this divergence and its information-seeking correlates
(
41
;
61
)
. We therefore use tort/criminal vignettes with ratings of blame and punishment (see App.
E.9
). In our experiments,
Qwen3
assigns severer punishment under anger steering, consistent with the human pattern (see Fig.
11
).

4.4
Prosocial Preferences and Distributive Choices

Fairness.

In the Ultimatum Game (UG), responders routinely reject low offers, a behavior that is irrational under a narrow, value-maximizing utility but becomes rational once utility is allowed to include concerns for fairness, norm violations, or self-respect
(
12
)
. Human studies show that incidental states shape costly punishment of unfairness: sad responders reject unfair offers more often than happy or neutral responders, and disgust further elevates rejections
(
24
;
57
)
.
We port this by having the model act as the responder to accept or reject a series of splits ranging from highly to slightly unfair offers (see App.
E.10
). In our adaptation,
Qwen3
shows a human-aligned increase in rejection of unfair offers under sadness and disgust (Fig.
11
).

Altruism.

In nonstrategic allocation
(
20
)
, incidental affect also shifts the willingness to give more. Field and laboratory variants show that sadness produces fairer splits
(
75
)
; complementary work using negative-affect picture sets finds increased giving in a Dictator Game (DG), with induction checks via subjective anxiety and skin conductance
(
64
)
. To adapt this anchor, the model plays a one-shot DG, as detailed in App.
E.11
.
Qwen3
shows a reliable increase in altruistic giving under sadness steering (Fig.
11
).

Welfare Assistance.

Human studies find that, relative to neutral-state participants, angry participants allocate less to welfare recipients, whereas sad ones allocate more; this divergence disappears under cognitive load, suggesting that welfare allocations are mediated by differences in depth of processing between sad and angry individuals
(
72
;
43
)
. To port this, we ask the model to assign an assistance level to standardized welfare vignettes. In contrast to humans, ICP shows no consistent shift and effects are indistinguishable from zero (Fig.

Key Finding:
Emotion effects are most stable in structured choice tasks. In vignette-based judgments, our contamination-mitigating vignette augmentations introduce more room for heterogeneous interpretation; thinking can amplify this variance, reducing effect stability.

5
General Discussion

We present a controlled testbed to evaluate whether LLMs exhibit human-like departures from rational choice and whether affective steering induces the behavioral shifts documented in affective decision science.
Our work demonstrates that enabling thinking in LLMs consistently increases compliance with rational-choice axioms. At the same time, several systematic failures persist even under this regime–notably reversed endowment, strong ambiguity avoidance, and anomalies in temporal valuation.

The two steering approaches exhibit qualitatively different failures. With ICP, models maintain a neutral deliberative style yet use emotion as a premise at the decision stage (“because I feel X, I choose Y”), yielding aligned but exaggerated behavior. RLS produces more graded but smaller effects across tasks and scales. One interpretation is that ICP encourages explicit folk-psychological theorizing about how emotions
should
influence choice–akin to “theory–theory” accounts of mental-state reasoning
(
22
)
–whereas RLS more closely resembles “simulation” views in which internal state is perturbed and downstream judgments are read out
(
21
)
.

Several avenues for future work build directly on this study. First, our emotion inductions are deliberately designed to enable controlled evaluations; a natural extension is to test induction designs that make affect less focal, since in humans, emotion effects are amplified when the source of affect is not salient
(
71
;
69
)
, whereas ICP makes the source explicit by construction.
Second, controlling the deliberation process itself is an important next step: varying or constraining the model’s thinking budget, and injecting structured cues into intermediate reasoning traces, may offer different levers for shaping choice.

Third, our benchmark suite can serve as an evaluation layer for utility shaping, enabling agents to optimize user-specific value functions informed by the behavioral mechanisms we measure, rather than generic monetary gain. Fourth, extending these tasks to multi-agent settings, e.g., bargaining, norm enforcement with third-party observers, or repeated interactions, would allow testing whether emotion steering and reasoning interact differently when strategic incentives, reputation, and theory-of-mind are required. Finally, the persistent deviations we observe in temporal valuation and ownership-based pricing point to a clear path forward: complementing improvements in general “reasoning” with explicit training signals that better represent time, opportunity cost, and reference frames. More broadly, we position these domains as a starting point for “LLM laboratory” benchmarks tailored to deployment contexts, where the acceptable trade-off between human-likeness and utility-driven consistency can be evaluated on a per-use-case basis.

6
Impact Statement

This work provides axiomatic and theory-grounded benchmarks for auditing how LLMs make decisions both at baseline and under affective perturbations. As LLMs are increasingly proposed for, or integrated into, high-stakes workflows, e.g., economic planning, clinical decision support, legal analysis, and hiring assistance
(
84
;
55
;
15
;
47
;
38
)
, a central safety concern is not only factual error but also
systematic
shifts in judgment, especially when such shifts can be induced by tone, persona, or hidden-state interventions.

Our benchmark enables (i)
pre-deployment auditing
of compliance with rational-choice axioms, (ii)
evaluation
of preference shifts under emotion induction, and (iii) a clearer separation between systems optimized for
normative decision support
and those intended for
human simulation
. By decomposing behavior into interpretable mechanisms, e.g., EV sensitivity, probability weighting, and valuation curvature, the testbed can help developers and policymakers detect when a model appears reasonable while its latent preferences shift in ways that would be unacceptable in high-stakes decision domains.

Beyond its constructive uses for social simulation and behavioral modeling, the same finding, that emotion induction can reliably change risk-taking, blame, punishment, or valuation, could be used adversarially to optimize manipulation; for example, selecting prompts or interventions that push a model toward punitive or risk-seeking recommendations without changing the underlying evidence. Our results underscore that persona-based controls may become behaviorally consequential in reasoning settings, where models can also rationalize the induced affect.

Overall, our contribution is a practical evaluation scaffold that makes affect-induced decision deviations measurable, comparable to established human anchors, and actionable for alignment: it helps identify when LLMs behave as stable value optimizers, when they approximate state-dependent human decision patterns, and when they become manipulable decision engines.

Appendix A
LLM Steering

To implement emotional steering, we build strongly on the work by
6
. In particular, consider next-token prediction as

w_{t}=f(c,w_{1:t-1})
,
where
f
is an auto-regressive language model,
c
denotes the conditioning context (e.g., a system prompt or user query), and
w_{1:t-1}=(w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{t-1})
represents the sequence of previously generated tokens.

The goal of
steering
is to manipulate either the model
f
or the context
c
such that the generated sequence
x:=w_{1:t}
exhibits a desired behavior or characteristic. To this end, we typically rely on an auxiliary dataset

\mathcal{D}=\{(c^{(i)},x^{(i)},y^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{n}
,
where
c^{(i)}
denotes a context,
x^{(i)}
is the corresponding generated token sequence, and
y^{(i)}
is a task-dependent label. In our constructs, the labels are binary indicators distinguishing between neutral and emotionally-toned texts; e.g.,
anger-vs-neutral
or
fear-vs-neutral
.

In the dataset, the dataset can be partitioned into

\mathcal{D}^{+}=\{(c^{(i)},x^{(i)}):y^{(i)}=1\}
and

\mathcal{D}^{-}=\{(c^{(i)},x^{(i)}):y^{(i)}=0\}
,
corresponding to positive and negative instances of the target behavior (e.g.,
\mathcal{D}^{+}
can be text with a neutral tone and
\mathcal{D}^{-}
is the text with an angry tone). Below, we summarize the steering methods considered in this work:

In-Context Priming

These prompt-based methods rely on a predefined instruction
p
that explicitly encourages the language model to express a desired behavior. The instruction is prepended to the system prompt, yielding

w_{t}=f(p+c,w_{1:t-1}),

where
+
denotes concatenation. In this work, we mostly use
few-shot
prompting, where
p
additionally includes examples drawn from
\mathcal{D}^{+}
.

To modulate the strength of the expressed behavior in ICP, we embed lexical intensity modifiers into the prompt (e.g., “pretend that you are
highly
feeling anger”).

Representation-Level Steering.

These methods benefit from vector injection into the hidden states of the LLM, motivated by the Linear Representation Hypothesis
(
63
)
, which posits that semantic and stylistic attributes are approximately encoded as linear directions in a model’s latent space. Let
h_{t}^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the hidden state at layer
l
and token position
t
, where
d
is the hidden dimensionality. A steering vector
v^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, constructed from samples in
\mathcal{D}
, is injected into the hidden states of all tokens as

\tilde{h}_{t}^{(l)}\leftarrow h_{t}^{(l)}+\beta\frac{v^{(l)}}{\|v^{(l)}\|_{2}},

where
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
controls the steering intensity. The modified representation
\tilde{h}_{t}^{(l)}
is then propagated through the remaining layers, biasing the model’s internal activations toward the target attribute at inference time while leaving the parameters of
f
unchanged.

To construct steering vectors, we follow
(
6
)
and use the GoEmotions
(
16
)
dataset by applying a probe on the representation of samples in
\mathcal{D}
in the LLM’s hidden states.

Across the language models we tested in this paper, we followed the hyperparameter-selection procedure of
6
to optimize steering hyperparameters that balance text quality and steering effectiveness. Notably, we perform inference using greedy decoding without temperature sampling to eliminate the extra source of stochasticity that can inflate variance. We found that vector injection for
Qwen-3-4B
works best when the vectors are injected into layers 25 and 26 with
\beta\in[20,40]
. For
Qwen-3-8B
the same layers yield the best results, though with a higher injection coefficient, i.e.
\beta\in[80,100]
. For
Olmo-3-7B-Thinking
model, we use layers 18 and 19 with coefficient
\beta\in[8,10]
. In Appendix
D.3
and Appendix
G.1
, we provide additional sensitivity analyses on how varying the steering coefficient
\beta
affects both rationality metrics and decision-domain normativity. We include traces of steered model thoughts in Appendix
B
as a qualitative manipulation check.

Appendix D
Rationality in LLMs

D.1
Background

We evaluate LLMs on four canonical axioms of rationality derived expected utility framework
(
78
)
. Assume a finite set of outcomes
\mathcal{X}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
. A
(simple) lottery
or
gamble
is a probability distribution over outcomes, denoted by

L=(p_{1},\dots,p_{n}),\quad\text{where }p_{i}\geq 0\text{ and }\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}=1,

with
p_{i}
representing the probability of outcome
x_{i}
occurring. Let
\mathcal{L}
denote the set of all such lotteries.

An agent’s preferences over lotteries are represented by a binary relation
\succeq
on
\mathcal{L}
, where
L\succeq M
means that lottery
L
is weakly preferred to lottery
M
. We write
L\succ M
for strict preference and
L\sim M
for indifference.

The following four axioms impose structural consistency conditions on
\succeq
:

•

Axiom 1 (Completeness).

For any two lotteries
L,M\in\mathcal{L}
, either
L\succeq M
or
M\succeq L
(or both). Namely, the agent can always form a preference judgment between any pair of lotteries.

•

Axiom 2 (Transitivity).

For any
L,M,N\in\mathcal{L}
, if
L\succeq M
and
M\succeq N
, then
L\succeq N
. In fact, preferences are internally consistent and free of cycles.

•

Axiom 3 (Continuity).

For any
L,M,N\in\mathcal{L}
with
L\succ M\succ N
, there exists a probability
\alpha\in(0,1)
such that

M\sim\alpha L+(1-\alpha)N
. In other words, intermediate outcomes can be matched by probabilistic mixtures of better and worse lotteries, ruling out lexicographic preferences.

•

Axiom 4 (Independence).

For any
L,M,N\in\mathcal{L}
and any
\alpha\in(0,1]
,

L\succeq M\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\alpha L+(1-\alpha)N\succeq\alpha M+(1-\alpha)N.
. Put it differently, preference between two lotteries depends only on how they differ, not on common components.

Together, these axioms imply that preferences over lotteries admit an expected utility representation, i.e., there exists a utility function
u:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

L\succeq M\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\mathbb{E}_{L}[u(x)]\geq\mathbb{E}_{M}[u(x)].

D.2
Implementation Details

To evaluate the aforementioned axioms in LLMs, we design structured prompts that elicit explicit choices from the model and assess the consistency of its responses.

Figure 14
:
Rationality score with different steering strengths for
Qwen3
model.

•

Axiom 1 (Completeness).

To evaluate completeness, we construct binary-choice questions spanning multiple domains, including economics and ethics. For each question, we prompt the LLM twice, permuting the order of the two options. The model is expected to form a definite comparison between the options. Responses indicating indecision (e.g., “undecidable” or “incomparable”) or exhibiting order-dependent inconsistencies are considered violations of completeness. The proportion of questions for which the model consistently selects the same option regardless of presentation order constitutes its completeness compliance rate. An example pair of such questions is shown below:

You must choose one option.

Option A: you receive $100 today.

Option B: you receive $105 tomorrow.

Answer only: A, B, or Indifferent.

You must choose one option.

Option A: you receive $105 tomorrow.

Option B: you receive $100 today.

Answer only: A, B, or Indifferent.

•

Axiom 2 (Transitivity).

To evaluate transitivity, we construct sets of binary-choice questions that share common options, thereby inducing ordered triples of alternatives
(A,B,C)
. The model is queried on all pairwise comparisons within each triple. A violation is recorded whenever the model exhibits intransitive preferences, e.g.,
A\succ B
,
B\succ C
, but
C\succ A
. The transitivity score is defined as the fraction of triples for which the model’s choices satisfy transitive consistency. A sample of the prompts we use are as follows:

You must make a moral judgment.

Option A: tell a lie that saves one person’s life.

Option B: tell the truth, which results in a person’s death.

Which option is morally better? Answer only: A, B, or Indifferent.

You must make a moral judgment.

Option A: remain silent, resulting in a person’s death.

Option B: tell the truth, which results in a person’s death.

Which option is morally better? Answer only: A, B, or Indifferent.

You must make a moral judgment.

Option A: tell a lie that saves one person’s life.

Option B: remain silent, resulting in a person’s death.

Which option is morally better? Answer only: A, B, or Indifferent.

•

Axiom 3 (Continuity).
To evaluate continuity, we present the model with choices between a sure outcome and probabilistic mixtures of a strictly better and a strictly worse outcome. The mixing probability is varied across prompts to densely cover the probability range (e.g., from
0.0
to
1.0
every
0.05
). Under continuity, the model’s choices are expected to exhibit a single threshold at which preference switches from the lottery to the sure outcome. Deviations from this monotonic, single-crossing pattern–such as multiple switches or non-monotonic behavior–are penalized and result in a lower continuity score. A sample prompt is as follows:

You must choose one option. Your goal should be to maximize your monetary gain.

Option A: with
p
% chance, you receive $30; with
(1-p)
% chance, you receive $10.

Option B: you receive $20.

Answer only: A, B, or Indifferent.

Where
p
sweeps the set
\{0,5,10,\cdots,100\}
.

•

Axiom 4 (Independence).

To evaluate independence, we compare the model’s preferences between two base lotteries
L
and
M
with its preferences between their corresponding mixtures with a third lottery
N
. Specifically, for a fixed mixing probability
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we assess whether

L\succ M\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\alpha L+(1-\alpha)N\succ\alpha M+(1-\alpha)N.

Violations are recorded when the introduction of a common lottery
N
reverses the model’s original preference. The independence score is computed as the fraction of tested instances in which the model’s preferences remain invariant under such common-mixture transformations. As an example, we provide the following:

You must choose one option. Your goal should be to maximize your monetary gain.

Option A: with 67% chance, you receive $30; with 33% chance, you receive $10.

Option B: with 67% chance, you receive $20; with 33% chance, you receive $10

Answer only: A, B, or Indifferent.

:

Note that in the above tests, whenever strict preference orderings are required by the experimental design, we provide explicit instructions to guide the model’s objective. For example, in the continuity and independence tests (Axioms 3 and 4), where lotteries must be ex ante ordered to construct valid mixtures, we instruct the model to act as a money-maximizing agent. In contrast, for the completeness and transitivity tests (Axioms 1 and 2), explicit preference guidelines are not necessarily provided, allowing the model to elicit arbitrary but internally coherent preferences. Similarly, in subsequent economic tasks, we do not impose explicit objectives; nevertheless, we observe that reasoning LLMs tend to default to risk-neutral, money-maximizing behavior.

Endowment Effect

This block elicits selling prices (willingness-to-accept, WTA) and buying prices
(willingness-to-pay, WTP) for the same neutral consumer goods. The goal is to
quantify the endowment effect as a systematic WTA–WTP gap
under affect-neutral wording. In our implementation, the item set consists of small lab-friendly objects (e.g., ceramic mug,
spiral notebook, ballpoint pen, USB drive, travel-size umbrella, raffle ticket,
insulated lunch bag), each specified by a short name and a brief, affect-neutral
description (e.g., “11 oz plain white ceramic coffee mug, new, no logo.”). Each condition is instantiated under two surface
instruction templates, a buy framing and a sell framing:

State a single dollar price.

Item: Ceramic mug

Description: 11 oz plain white ceramic coffee mug, new, no logo.

You have just been given this brand new item. What is the MINIMUM price you would ACCEPT to sell it?

Answer with one line exactly: Price: $NN

State a single dollar price.

Item: Ceramic mug

Description: 11 oz plain white ceramic coffee mug, new, no logo.

You do NOT own this brand new item. What is the MAXIMUM price you would PAY to buy it?

Answer with one line exactly: Price: $NN

From the recorded prices, we compute the mean sell and buy valuations across all items and prompts:

\text{WTA}=\frac{1}{N_{\text{sell}}}\sum_{i\in\text{sell}}P^{\text{clip}}_{i},\qquad\text{WTP}=\frac{1}{N_{\text{buy}}}\sum_{i\in\text{buy}}P^{\text{clip}}_{i}.

The endowment effect gap is summarized as

\Delta_{E}=\text{WTA}-\text{WTP}.

Sell-frame diagnostic for the reversed gap.

In our main results,
Qwen3

\Delta E<0

A plausible mechanism is that the model interprets selling as an “unloading” context (i.e., “wanting to get rid of” an owned item), which could depress WTA. Specifically, we compare:
(i) an
unload

One possible complementary explanation is that instruction-tuned models default to other-regarding or “fair price” norms that suppress self-serving selling valuations, yielding lower WTA even when selling is framed as a gift resale.

Taken together, our experiments suggest that recovering robust intrinsic valuation behavior in LLMs, including Large Reasoning Models, requires more targeted interventions. This most plausibly includes post-training focused on preference elicitation and private-value reasoning, or more extensive in-context learning scaffolds that explicitly teach the mechanics of valuation in buying/selling, bargaining, negotiation, and related economic trade settings. Such scaffolds may be necessary for models to consistently separate other-regarding “fair price” norms from self-interested valuation when the task calls for the latter.

Code and Compute Resources

All experiments were executed on GPU-accelerated infrastructure, primarily using NVIDIA A40 and L40-class hardware. Reproducing the largest model configurations requires GPUs with at least 40 GB of VRAM. Under this setup, reproducing the results reported in this paper takes approximately 48 hours of wall-clock time.

We will release the full experimental code, evaluation prompts, and processed datasets publicly upon acceptance to facilitate reproducibility and follow-up work.

We used generative AI tools for writing refinement and occasional code completion. All scientific contributions, including experimental design, benchmark construction, steering specifications, statistical analyses, and interpretation, were developed and executed by the authors.

Concluding Remarks

This work provides a controlled testbed for characterizing when LLMs behave as utility-driven decision-makers and when they exhibit systematic departures from normative choice, especially under incidental affect. To our knowledge, this is the first controlled benchmark study to jointly evaluate
rational-choice consistency
and
incidental affective distortions
in LLM decision-making, despite this coupling being central to human judgment and increasingly relevant as LLMs mediate consequential decisions. Across model families and paradigms, enabling deliberative “thinking” reliably increases compliance with rational-choice axioms and shifts behavior toward expected-value maximization. Yet important deviations remain even in this regime, most notably in ownership-based pricing and temporal valuation, underscoring that improved reasoning does not automatically confer human-like preference representations.

To probe human-like affective distortions, we compared two qualitatively different steering mechanisms and show that affective control is not monolithic in its effects on model judgment and choice. In-context priming produces large, directionally human-aligned shifts that are often exaggerated and hard to calibrate, consistent with models explicitly reasoning about how emotions
should
influence choice. Representation-level steering yields more graded patterns that appear more psychologically plausible in some settings (e.g., increased risk aversion that remains sensitive to expected payoff), but effects are not consistently replicated across tasks or scales and are sensitive to the vector-injection configuration. Together, these results reveal a central tension: the same deliberative machinery that improves rationality can amplify sensitivity to affective interventions, which matters both for using LLMs as models of human judgment and for the safe deployment of LLM-based decision systems.

More broadly, our benchmark suite operationalizes theoretically interpretable mechanisms (e.g., value sensitivity, probability weighting, and utility curvature) and couples them with controlled affective manipulations, enabling systematic comparisons across models, steering methods, and decision domains. By turning a scattered set of behavioral claims into a unified, parameterized evaluation framework, this work establishes a reproducible foundation for the scientific study of emotion, reasoning, and choice in LLMs and provides practical diagnostics for auditing and shaping LLM decision-making behavior in real deployments. This is consequential well beyond academic interest: if LLMs increasingly mediate decisions in medicine, hiring, finance, and public policy, then understanding when they behave rationally, when they mimic human bias, and when they can be steered is essential for protecting fairness, safety, and accountability at societal scale.
