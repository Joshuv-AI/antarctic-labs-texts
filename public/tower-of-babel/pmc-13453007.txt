Second trimester serum lipidomics predicts large-for-gestational-age infants in apparently metabolically healthy pregnancies
Authors: Jing Wang, Yixin Gong, Tong Yue, Xianming Li, Qin Wang, Tian Wei, Likun Yang, Xueying Zheng, Sihui Luo, Yu Ding, Hongbo Chen, Jianping Weng, Yujie Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryLarge-for-gestational-age (LGA) births occur in many pregnancies with an apparently metabolically healthy phenotype, limiting risk identification based on conventional clinical characteristics alone. We explored the association between second-trimester maternal serum lipidomic profiles and LGA risk in this apparently healthy population using a nested case-control design within an ongoing prospective pregnancy cohort. The study included a derivation cohort of 135 participants and an independent temporal validation cohort of 66 participants. Lipidomic profiles were analyzed by using liquid chromatography and high-resolution mass spectrometry. Multivariate modeling identified 11 circulating lipid biomarkers (seven glycerophospholipids, two glycerolipids, and two sphingolipids) associated with LGA risk. Integrating these lipid biomarkers with routine clinical factors substantially improved predictive performance compared with clinical variables alone. These findings suggest that metabolic alterations are detectable before clinical manifestations become apparent and support serum lipidomic profiling as a complementary approach for early risk stratification in pregnancies traditionally considered low risk.

Introduction

Large-for-gestational-age (LGA) birth is associated with increased risks of cardiovascular disease persisting into adulthood, reflecting the enduring consequences of an adverse intrauterine metabolic environment on lifelong cardiometabolic health.1,2,3,4 From a life-course perspective, early identification and timely intervention during pregnancy, therefore, represent a critical window of opportunity to interrupt this intergenerational cycle of metabolic risk before it becomes established. Maternal hyperglycemia, pre-pregnancy obesity, and excessive gestational weight gain (GWG) are established risk factors for fetal overgrowth, primarily through enhanced transplacental transfer of glucose and free fatty acids.5,6,7 Nevertheless, LGA cases occur in up to 15% of pregnant women with normal weight and normal glucose metabolism,8,9 which represent an apparently metabolically healthy phenotype, suggesting that even apparently healthy women may harbor underlying metabolic abnormalities that drive excessive fetal growth through mechanisms not captured by conventional clinical screening.

Recent studies have proved the important role of maternal lipid metabolism in fetal growth. Mothers of LGA infants with obesity and/or hyperglycemia exhibit higher circulating levels of acylcarnitines and diacylglycerols along with reduced phospholipid species, including phosphatidylcholines (PCs), phosphatidylethanolamines (PEs), and phosphatidylinositols (PIs).10,11 Moreover, variations in acylcarnitines and branched-chain amino acids have been implicated in aberrant fetal growth trajectories.12,13 These lipid metabolites partly contribute to exacerbated insulin resistance.14,15 However, in apparently healthy women, the contribution of lipid metabolites to excessive fetal growth and their interactions with glucose regulation have not been fully clarified.

In this study, we explored the association between second-trimester serum lipidomic metabolites and LGA in normoglycaemic, normal-weight pregnant women. We further established a combined risk-prediction model for LGA, integrating lipidomic markers with clinical variables. These findings may provide insights into the second-trimester lipid metabolism profile in apparently metabolically healthy phenotype populations and pave the way for precision strategies for LGA.

Results

The baseline characteristics of the derivation and temporal validation cohorts are presented in Table 1. The two cohorts were generally comparable across most clinical parameters. Participants in the validation cohort were slightly older than those in the derivation cohort (32.56 ± 3.33 vs. 31.09 ± 3.81 years, p = 0.009), and had marginally lower high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) levels (2.01 ± 0.36 vs. 2.16 ± 0.39 mmol/L, p = 0.009). All other baseline characteristics were comparable between cohorts (all p > 0.05), supporting the suitability and representativeness of the validation cohort. Within the derivation cohort, LGA cases had significantly shorter gestational age at delivery (38.38 ± 2.08 vs. 39.36 ± 1.00 weeks, p < 0.001), higher rates of cesarean section (62.2% vs. 38.9%, p = 0.017), higher 2-h post-load glucose on oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) (6.64 ± 0.81 vs. 6.23 ± 0.85 mmol/L, p = 0.008), higher triglyceride (TG) levels (2.45 ± 1.12 vs. 1.91 ± 0.63 mmol/L, p = 0.001), and lower albumin levels (37.99 ± 2.40 vs. 38.79 ± 1.81 g/L, p = 0.034) compared with appropriate-for-gestational-age (AGA) controls. In the validation cohort, LGA cases showed higher GWG (14.07 ± 3.93 vs. 12.29 ± 4.40 kg, p = 0.034), higher rates of cesarean section (63.6% vs. 29.5%, p = 0.017), higher rates of excessive GWG (50.0% vs. 34.1%, p = 0.042), and lower HDL-C levels (1.85 ± 0.25 vs. 2.09 ± 0.38 mmol/L, p = 0.010) compared with AGA controls (Table S1). Lipid profiling and correlation analysis

Following comprehensive data preprocessing, a total of 830 lipid species were retained for final analysis. These lipids were classified into seven distinct lipid categories (glycerophospholipids [GP], glycerolipids [GL], sphingolipids [SP], sterol lipids [ST], saccharolipids [SL], fatty acyls [FA], and prenol lipids [PR]), which encompassed 32 lipid subclasses. Complete details of all analyzed lipid subclasses and their respective species counts are provided in Table S2. As shown in Figure S1, correlation analysis of subclass-summarized lipid concentrations revealed the strongest positive correlation between monoglyceride (MG) and ceramide phosphate (CerP) (r = 0.787 and p < 0.001), and the strongest negative correlation between monogalactosyldiacylglycerol (MGDG) and N-acetylhexosyl ceramide (CerG3GNAc1) (r = −0.385 and p < 0.001). In addition, total diglyceride (DG) concentrations demonstrated a significant positive association with LGA risk (false discovery rate [FDR] = 0.049) (Table S3).

Preliminary screening of differential serum lipids

Using orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), we first observed clear separations in serum lipidomic profiles between the LGA and AGA groups (Figure 1A). The permutation plot showed that the intercepts of R2 and Q2 were 0.347 and −0.208, respectively, supporting the stability of the OPLS-DA model and indicating that it was not overfit. The values of R2X(cum), R2Y(cum), and the Q2(cum) were 0.745, 0.615, and 0.429, respectively, indicating that the established OPLS-DA model had good adaptability and acceptable predictability (Figure S2). Independently, we conducted multivariable logistic regression analyses to account for potential confounding factors. As presented in Table S5, 51 lipid species remained significantly associated with LGA status. Of these, 27 lipid species overlapped with those identified by OPLS-DA, while the remaining 26 were exclusively identified through multivariable logistic regression, suggesting the complementary nature of the two screening approaches in capturing both multivariate discriminatory patterns and individually significant lipid-LGA associations independent of clinical confounders.

To ensure a comprehensive candidate set for model building while avoiding reliance on any single screening approach, we combined the lipids identified by either method (OPLS-DA/variable importance in projection [VIP] or multivariable logistic regression). A total of 67 unique lipid species were identified by at least one method and subsequently entered into the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) logistic regression for biomarker selection.

Pathway enrichment and functional annotation

To further elucidate the metabolic pathways most relevant to LGA, we performed pathway enrichment and topological pathway analyses by using MetaboAnalyst 6.0. Enrichment analysis revealed that these differential lipids were predominantly enriched in PI phosphate metabolism (p = 0.050), phospholipid biosynthesis (p = 0.084), and arachidonic acid metabolism (p = 0.188) (Figure 1C). Pathway topology analysis identified six perturbed Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) metabolic pathways, in which GP metabolism showed the most significant perturbation (p < 0.001), followed by linoleic acid metabolism (p = 0.013), alpha-linolenic acid metabolism (p = 0.032), and glycosyl PI (GPI)-anchor biosynthesis (p = 0.078) (Figure 1D).

Examination of the specific lipid species underlying these pathway perturbations revealed coordinated, class-level patterns. Within the GP metabolism pathway, 19 PC species, 8 diacyl PE species, and 7 DG species were elevated in the LGA group, reflecting broad remodeling of both membrane phospholipid composition and neutral lipid pools, including PC(32:3), PE(16:0/20:4), and DG(18:2/20:4). 14 TG species, PI(16:0/16:0), lyso-phosphatidylcholine (LPC)(18:3), and sphingomyelin (SM) (d35:3) were also elevated. In contrast, the depleted fraction was characterized by a coordinated loss of 12 ether-linked lipid species across PE and PC classes, including plasmalogen PE species (PE[18:1p/20:4], PE[18:0p/20:4], and PE[18:1p/22:6]), alkylacyl ether PE species (PE[16:0e/20:4] and PE[34:1e]), and plasmalogen PC species (PC[36:2p] and PC[37:7p]), alongside SM(d40:5) and cholesteryl ester (ChE)(20:4) (Tables S4 and S5). Figure 1E presents a schematic overview of glycerophospholipid biosynthetic and remodeling pathways, illustrating the positions and directional changes of the differentially expressed lipid species identified in this study within established metabolic networks, including the Kennedy pathway, the Lands’ cycle, phosphatidylethanolamine N-methyltransferase-mediated PE-PC interconversion, and the PI 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/Akt) signaling axis.

LASSO regression for lipid selection

To construct a parsimonious and robust predictive model for LGA, the 67 lipid species preliminarily identified as differentially expressed were entered into LASSO logistic regression (Figures 2A and 2B). 11 lipid species were identified as key biomarkers significantly associated with LGA risk. As shown in Figure 2C, the 11 selected lipid species demonstrated exceptional stability across 50 iterations of 10-fold cross-validation. Compared with the AGA group, the LGA group showed elevated levels of DG(18:2/20:4), PC(32:3), PC(34:3), PI(16:0/16:0), SM(d35:3), TG(16:0/12:0/14:0), and LPC(18:3) and reduced levels of PC(36:2p), PE(18:1p/20:4), SM(d40:5), and PE(34:1e). The concentration distributions of these lipids between the two groups are illustrated in Figure 3A (all FDR <0.005). To further evaluate the relationships between the 11 differential lipid species and LGA risk, we constructed separate multivariable logistic regression models for each lipid (Figure 3B). Module eigengenes were correlated with key clinical traits (Figure 4D). Three modules demonstrated statistically significant associations with LGA status: the turquoise module (r = 0.32 and p < 0.001) and brown module (r = 0.28 and p < 0.001) were positively associated, while the blue module was negatively associated (r = −0.27 and p = 0.002). Nine of the 11 LASSO-selected lipid biomarkers were located within these three LGA-associated modules (Figures 4E–4G), with module membership values ranging from 0.38 to 0.73 and gene significance values ranging from 0.22 to 0.33, indicating moderate to strong within-module co-expression. Specifically, PI(16:0/16:0), PC(32:3), PC(34:3), DG(18:2/20:4), and TG(16:0/12:0/14:0) were assigned to the turquoise module; LPC(18:3) to the brown module; and PE(34:1e), PC(36:2p), and PE(18:1p/20:4) to the blue module. Of the remaining two LASSO-selected lipids, SM(d40:5) was assigned to the yellow module and SM(d35:3) was unassigned to any co-expression module (gray module), suggesting that it may function as an independently dysregulated lipid species. These findings indicate that the majority of LASSO-selected biomarkers represent biologically coherent module-associated members within coordinated lipid co-expression communities, providing a systems-level rationale for their inclusion in the predictive model.

Beyond LGA status, several modules also showed significant associations with maternal glycemic parameters. The red module (r = 0.23 and p = 0.008) and green module (r = 0.21 and p = 0.016) were positively correlated with fasting plasma glucose (FPG) on OGTT, while the blue module was negatively correlated with FPG (r = −0.18 and p = 0.042). The brown module (r = 0.21 and p = 0.016) and red module (r = 0.19 and p = 0.025) were positively correlated with 2-h post-load glucose on OGTT. These associations suggest that the identified lipid co-expression communities are not only relevant to fetal overgrowth but may also reflect underlying maternal glycemic dysregulation.

Post hoc power calculation

To further evaluate the reliability of the key differential lipid findings, we conducted post hoc statistical power calculations for each lipid species. For these lipids, Cohen’s d effect sizes were calculated on the basis of mean differences between the LGA and AGA groups and pooled standard deviations. As shown in Table S6, the Cohen’s d values for the 11 selected lipid species ranged from approximately 0.52 to 0.77. With the current sample size, the estimated statistical power for detecting these effect sizes was consistently greater than 0.8 at an alpha level of 0.05 for all 11 lipid species. These results indicate that the prioritized lipid features have sufficient statistical sensitivity under the present study design, supporting the robustness of their observed group differences.

Construction and validation of the LGA risk prediction model

To evaluate the reproducibility of the identified lipidomic signature, OPLS-DA analysis and abundance distributions of all 11 LASSO-selected lipid species were assessed in the independent temporal validation cohort. OPLS-DA demonstrated partial separation between the LGA and AGA groups (Figure S3A), consistent with the pattern observed in the derivation cohort. All 11 lipid species exhibited complete directional consistency between the two cohorts, with 7 of 11 achieving statistical significance (FDR < 0.05) in the validation cohort (Figures S3B and S3C).

Prior to model development, collinearity diagnostics confirmed no evidence of high multicollinearity among clinical variables and the 11 lipid species, with all variance inflation factor values well below the threshold of 5 (Table S7). Four LGA risk prediction models were developed in the derivation cohort and evaluated via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis in both cohorts. Full performance metrics are presented in Table S8. In the derivation cohort (Figure 2D), model 1 demonstrated modest discriminative performance (areas under the curve [AUC] = 0.675, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.580–0.771). The addition of routine laboratory parameters in model 2 improved performance (AUC = 0.763, 95% CI: 0.679–0.848). Model 3, comprising the 11 lipid biomarkers alone, achieved substantially superior discrimination (AUC = 0.858, 95% CI: 0.793–0.923), outperforming both models 1 and 2. The comprehensive model 4, integrating lipid biomarkers with clinical and laboratory parameters, achieved the highest performance (AUC = 0.925, 95% CI: 0.879–0.972). In the temporal validation cohort (Figure 2E), all four models maintained consistent performance: model 1 (AUC = 0.632, 95% CI: 0.486–0.774), model 2 (AUC = 0.759, 95% CI: 0.639–0.868), model 3 (AUC = 0.819, 95% CI: 0.708–0.913), and model 4 (AUC = 0.883, 95% CI: 0.800–0.966). DeLong test comparisons confirmed no statistically significant difference in discriminatory performance between the two cohorts for any model (all p > 0.05; Figure 2F), supporting the generalizability of the developed predictive models.

Calibration curves were constructed for all four models in both cohorts (Figure 2G). All models demonstrated satisfactory calibration in the derivation cohort, with Hosmer-Lemeshow (HL) p values of 0.885, 0.505, 0.985, and 0.188 for models 1–4, respectively. In the validation cohort, good calibration was maintained across all models (HL p = 0.811, 0.757, 0.947, and 0.750, respectively), with calibration curves demonstrating reasonable agreement between predicted probabilities and observed event rates across the full probability range.

Discussion

In this nested case-control study, we investigated the association between second-trimester maternal serum lipidomic profiles and LGA risk specifically in normoglycaemic, normal-weight pregnant women. Through lipidomic profiling, we identified 11 lipid species comprising seven GPs, two GLs, and two SPs that served as predictive biomarkers for LGA in this apparently metabolically healthy population. Combining these lipids with conventional clinical risk factors significantly improved predictive performance, with AUC increasing from 0.675 to 0.925 in the derivation cohort and from 0.632 to 0.883 in the temporal validation cohort. Exploratory mediation analyses further identified SM(d35:3) and TG(16:0/12:0/14:0) as significant mediators linking subclinical postprandial glucose dysregulation to LGA risk. The integration of these lipid markers with conventional clinical risk factors substantially enhanced LGA prediction performance, with AUC values increasing from 0.67 to 0.92. These findings suggest that lipid metabolic perturbations may identify LGA risk in pregnant women with an apparently metabolically healthy phenotype that current screening approaches often miss.

The lipidomic profile associated with LGA exhibited coordinated alterations across multiple lipid classes, involving pathways related to lipid flux, membrane remodeling, intracellular signaling, and inflammatory-oxidative balance. Pathway topology analysis revealed that GP metabolism was the most significantly perturbed pathway with the highest biological impact, followed by linoleic acid and arachidonic acid metabolism. These patterns indicate that lipid dysregulation in LGA occurs at a systems level rather than as isolated species-level changes.

Alterations in lipid supply and flux were reflected by the accumulation of TGs and fatty acid-related species. TGs serve as primary fetal energy sources and exhibit progressive elevation throughout normal pregnancy.17 Multiple investigations have demonstrated that elevated TGs and free fatty acids, coupled with reduced HDL-C levels, are significantly associated with increased LGA risk in women with gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) or obesity,18,19,20 and even in metabolically healthy populations.21 In our study, TG(16:0/12:0/14:0) was identified as a key mediator linking maternal glucose to LGA. This species contains medium-chain fatty acids (lauric acid C12:0 and myristic acid C14:0), which can directly enter mitochondria independent of the carnitine shuttle and undergo rapid β-oxidation.22 Elevated levels of this TG may reflect enhanced placental lipid hydrolysis via lipoprotein lipase and endothelial lipase, facilitating excessive non-esterified fatty acid flux across the placenta and promoting fetal adipose tissue deposition.17 Consistent with this interpretation, mediation analysis indicated that TG species partially accounted for the association between post-load glucose and LGA, supporting a lipid-mediated nutrient oversupply pathway.

In addition to lipid oversupply, multiple lipid species involved in membrane structure and intracellular signaling were altered, suggesting disruption of placental metabolic regulation. We observed enrichment of several PC and PE species, the predominant phospholipids in syncytiotrophoblast membranes. These lipids are essential for maintaining membrane fluidity and facilitating fatty acid transport.23 Alterations in PC and PE composition may disrupt membrane microdomains and impair receptor-mediated signaling, a mechanism previously linked to placental insulin resistance.24,25 Furthermore, significant accumulation of DG species, including DG(18:2/20:4), was identified in the LGA group. DGs function as lipid second messengers that activate protein kinase C (PKC) isoforms, leading to inhibitory serine phosphorylation of insulin receptor substrate-1 and disruption of the PI3K/Akt.26,27 This mechanism may reduce placental insulin sensitivity and alter nutrient transporter activity. Consistent with prior findings, maternal DG species, such as DG(18:1/18:1), have been associated with LGA risk.11

We also observed increased levels of PI(16:0/16:0), a precursor of phosphoinositides involved in PI3K/Akt signaling.28 Dysregulation of PI metabolism may, therefore, further contribute to impaired insulin signaling and altered nutrient transport. Consistent with this notion, Chen et al.29 reported that PI metabolism may partially mediate the association between prenatal bisphenol exposure and increased infant skinfold thickness, suggesting a potential role of PI-related pathways in early growth regulation. In parallel, SM(d35:3) was elevated while SM(d40:5) was depleted, indicating bidirectional dysregulation of the ceramide-sphingomyelin axis. SM hydrolysis by sphingomyelinase generates ceramide, which activates protein phosphatase 2A and suppresses Akt phosphorylation,30 a mechanism that may similarly impair placental insulin responsiveness and contribute to altered glucose homeostasis, as observed in GDM placentas.31 Collectively, these findings indicate that membrane lipid remodeling and lipid-mediated signaling converge to impair placental insulin responsiveness.

Beyond structural and signaling roles, lipid species associated with inflammation and oxidative balance were also dysregulated. Several polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA)-containing lipids, including DG(18:2/20:4) and PE(18:1p/20:4), suggest redistribution of arachidonic acid pools. PUFAs serve as precursors for eicosanoids, which regulate inflammation and vascular function, and their dysregulation has been linked to chronic low-grade inflammation and metabolic dysfunction.32,33 LPCs can activate G protein-coupled receptors and Toll-like receptors, promoting pro-inflammatory cytokine production, oxidative stress, and insulin resistance.34 Specific LPC species, including LPC(18:3), have been reported to correlate with birth weight percentiles in cord blood, suggesting a potential signaling role in fetal growth regulation independent of maternal glycemia.35 Notably, we identified a coordinated depletion of ether-linked phospholipids, including multiple plasmalogen PE and PC species. Plasmalogens act as endogenous antioxidants by scavenging reactive oxygen species at the vinyl-ether bond.36 Their reduction suggests impaired antioxidant capacity and increased oxidative stress, which may compromise placental membrane integrity, trophoblast function, and vascular adaptation. The coordinated disruption of these interconnected metabolic pathways may therefore impair placental lipid handling and vascular adaptation, potentially contributing to an intrauterine metabolic environment that favors excessive fetal growth and increases the risk of LGA.

A multi-ethnic longitudinal lipidomic study demonstrated that alterations in certain PC, PE, and LPC levels significantly influenced neonatal birth weight and subcutaneous fat thickness,37 reflecting the coordinated role of these lipid classes in supporting energy supply and membrane biosynthesis during fetal development. Chagovets et al.38 identified significant associations between various PC and SM levels with macrosomia risk across different gestational periods. However, both studies lacked adequate control for metabolic confounding factors, limiting their applicability to metabolically healthy populations. A targeted lipidomic analysis of amniotic fluid from metabolically healthy mothers revealed that SM(14:0;O2/20:1) was significantly elevated in LGA cases at 15–20 weeks of gestation.39

Our findings support the presence of a coordinated lipid metabolic remodeling phenotype underlying LGA development in apparently metabolically healthy pregnancies. Collectively, the observed alterations across TG, DG, PC, PE, SM, and ether phospholipid pathways suggest that excessive fetal growth may arise from integrated disturbances in lipid transport, membrane remodeling, intracellular signaling, and inflammatory-oxidative balance rather than isolated lipid abnormalities. These interconnected alterations may converge on placental metabolic maladaptation, characterized by impaired insulin responsiveness, altered nutrient transport, oxidative stress, and disrupted vascular adaptation. Notably, exploratory mediation analyses further revealed that TG(16:0/12:0/14:0) and SM(d35:3) partially mediated the association between 2-h post-load glucose and LGA (20.8% and 22.9%, respectively), suggesting that subtle glycemic variation may represent one upstream contributor of this broader lipid-mediated cascade. Even within the normoglycaemic range, elevated 2-h post-load glucose may reflect subclinical postprandial insulin resistance associated with impaired hepatic lipid metabolism, increased very low-density lipoprotein-TG secretion,40 and enhanced placental lipoprotein lipase-mediated fatty acid transfer. The resultant excess non-esterified fatty acid flux across the syncytiotrophoblast may promote fetal lipid accumulation and excessive growth.41,42 In parallel, mild postprandial dysglycemia may increase saturated fatty acid availability and de novo ceramide synthesis via serine palmitoyltransferase,43 thereby exacerbating placental insulin resistance and impairing insulin-stimulated nutrient transporter trafficking.44 Taken together, these coordinated alterations suggest that excessive fetal growth may arise from subtle but systemic disturbances in maternal-placental lipid metabolism even in the absence of GDM.

LGA poses significant clinical risks, necessitating early identification and effective intervention approaches to reduce various adverse pregnancy outcomes and ensure maternal-fetal health. Multiple prospective studies have demonstrated dynamic changes in maternal lipid levels throughout pregnancy, characterized by limited lipid alterations in early pregnancy and marked increases during mid and late pregnancy.10,45 However, by late pregnancy, the fetus has already undergone rapid weight gain, and placental function begins to decline, making interventions less likely to reverse accumulated growth effects. Therefore, mid-pregnancy represents a favorable time frame for identifying differences and implementing early interventions.

Accurate LGA prediction during the second trimester remains challenging. Conventional clinical indicators, including maternal age, gestational glucose levels, pre-pregnancy BMI, and family history of diabetes, demonstrate limited predictive capacity for LGA risk, with AUC values typically ranging from 0.5 to 0.7.46,47 Although ultrasound examination provides the most direct method for estimating fetal size during pregnancy, mid-pregnancy ultrasound accuracy is limited by ultrasound parameters, prediction formulas, amniotic fluid volume, and fetal position.48 Even in late pregnancy, ultrasound-estimated fetal weight remains prone to considerable error compared with actual birth weight, with best-formula mean absolute percentage errors of 8.24% (singletons) and 11.11% (twins).49 Recent studies have incorporated lipid biomarkers into prediction models, achieving improved predictive performance. Chagovets et al.38 developed an OPLS-DA model based on second-trimester serum lipidomics for macrosomia prediction, demonstrating excellent discriminatory performance (AUC = 0.95) in both GDM and non-GDM subgroups. However, pre-pregnancy BMI was not included as an adjustment factor in their analysis. In our study, incorporation of conventional clinical factors, laboratory parameters, and lipid biomarkers significantly increased model AUC (model 4, AUC = 0.925 in the derivation cohort and 0.883 in the validation cohort), demonstrating significant improvement in predictive capacity through lipid biomarkers. Notably, the 11 LASSO-selected lipid biomarkers alone (model 3, AUC = 0.858 and 0.819, respectively) demonstrated substantially superior discriminative performance compared with conventional clinical risk factors (model 1, AUC = 0.675 and 0.632, respectively) and even outperformed the combination of conventional risk factors with standard laboratory parameters (model 2, AUC = 0.763 and 0.759, respectively). This observation suggests that specific circulating lipid species may capture metabolic alterations relevant to fetal overgrowth that are not fully reflected by standard clinical parameters. Specific lipid species may reflect both substrate availability for fetal adipose deposition and placental insulin signaling status, providing mechanistic insight into LGA pathophysiology. Incorporating lipidomic biomarkers into risk models could, therefore, improve early identification of pregnancies at risk for LGA, particularly among women who appear metabolically healthy by conventional criteria.

Our lipidomic findings highlight a panel of 11 maternal circulating lipid species associated with LGA risk, suggesting potential for a precision-medicine approach. We envision this lipid panel as a targeted second-tier screening tool for normoglycaemic, normal-weight women with borderline 2-h post-load glucose on routine OGTT (e.g., 6.7–8.4 mmol/L), who currently receive no additional metabolic surveillance despite elevated LGA risk. In practice, these biomarkers could be integrated into a multivariate risk index, combined with clinical parameters, to identify pregnancies at elevated risk for fetal overgrowth. This index could inform targeted interventions, such as individualized dietary or pharmacologic strategies, rather than general population screening, potentially improving efficiency and cost-effectiveness. Future studies should evaluate the predictive performance, cost-benefit, and feasibility of such a lipid-based risk tool in independent cohorts.

In this study, we identified multiple second-trimester lipid metabolites that significantly predict LGA risk in pregnant women with an apparently metabolically healthy phenotype. Lipid metabolic dysfunction may play an important role in fetal overgrowth mechanisms among apparently healthy pregnant women. Relying solely on conventional clinical risk factors may overlook a subset of high-risk individuals. Our findings provide evidence on the integration of lipidomic indices as complementary tools for identifying LGA in prenatal care. Clinical intervention strategies with regard to lipid metabolism management are potentially promising in preventing excessive fetal growth.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, this was a single-center exploratory discovery-stage study with a relatively modest sample size for lipidomic analysis, which may limit statistical power and the generalizability of the findings. Second, the study relied on single-time-point lipidomic data from mid-pregnancy, precluding assessment of dynamic lipid profile changes throughout gestation and their impact on LGA risk. Third, external validation in independent cohorts and direct functional validation of the identified lipid species and their effects on placental metabolic regulation were beyond the scope of the present exploratory study given practical constraints. Future multicenter longitudinal large-scale studies integrating serial lipidomic profiling with placental functional investigations will be essential to validate these findings and further clarify the underlying biological mechanisms.

Experimental model and study participant details

This study was nested within a prospective pregnancy cohort at the First Affiliated Hospital of the University of Science and Technology of China (USTC).50 The cohort is ongoing and prospectively recruits pregnant women at 4 weeks 0 days–27 weeks 6 days of gestation. Participants underwent follow-up visits at four predefined time-points: first trimester (≤13 weeks 6 days), second trimester (14 weeks 0 days–27 weeks 6 days), third trimester (28 weeks 0 days–33 weeks 6 days), and peri-delivery period (≥37 weeks 0 days). The study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of USTC (No. 2021-KY-LS [241]) and has been performed under the guidelines of the Helsinki Declaration. Written informed consent was obtained at the time of enrollment.

For this nested case-control analysis, we included Chinese women aged 18–45 years with normoglycaemia and normal weight (18.5 kg/m2 ≤ pre-pregnancy BMI <24 kg/m2) who delivered either an LGA or an AGA infant. Because all participants were pregnant women, sex was not considered a biological variable in the present study. Fetal size was categorized according to established birth weight percentiles relative to gestational age, using the race/ethnicity-specific standards developed by the Eunice Kennedy Shriver National Institute of Child Health and Human Development Fetal Growth Studies.51 LGA was defined as birth weight above the 90th percentile for gestational age, while AGA was defined as birth weight between the 10th and 90th percentiles for gestational age. We excluded women with GDM, pre-existing diabetes, chronic hypertension, hyperlipidemia, autoimmune diseases, or major organ dysfunction affecting the heart, liver, lungs, or kidneys. GDM was defined according to the International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Groups criteria, whereby a 75-g OGTT was considered diagnostic if any of the following plasma glucose thresholds were met: FPG ≥5.1 mmol/L, 1-h post-load glucose ≥10.0 mmol/L, or 2-h post-load glucose ≥8.5 mmol/L.52 Pre-existing diabetes was defined by a documented history of diabetes mellitus prior to pregnancy. Normoglycaemia was defined as the absence of GDM or pre-existing diabetes based on these standardized glucose measurements.

The study was conducted in two phases using a prospective temporal split. The derivation cohort comprised eligible participants recruited between October 1, 2021, and December 31, 2023. Of 524 eligible women, 45 delivered LGA infants. All 45 LGA cases were matched with 90 AGA controls in a 1:2 ratio based on maternal age and pre-pregnancy BMI (n = 135), and this cohort was used exclusively for lipidomic feature selection and predictive model development. The temporal validation cohort comprised eligible participants recruited between January 1, 2024, and July 1, 2024, from the same ongoing cohort under an identical recruitment protocol, eligibility criteria, case identification, and matching procedure, yielding 22 LGA cases and 44 matched AGA controls (n = 66). No data from the temporal validation cohort were used during model development or tuning (Figure S4).

Method details

Data collection and measurements

Sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle, and anthropometric measurements, including maternal age, educational level, parity, gravidity, height, pre-pregnancy weight, family history, obstetric history, medical history, smoking history, and alcohol consumption, were systematically collected at enrollment through face-to-face interviews. Delivery-related anthropometric parameters, including maternal pre-delivery weight, neonatal birth weight and length, infant sex, and pregnancy outcomes, were extracted from the electronic medical records of the First Affiliated Hospital of the USTC. GWG was classified according to Chinese population-specific reference ranges based on pre-pregnancy BMI.53 Adequate total GWG for women with normal weight was defined as 8–14 kg. GWG above this range was considered excessive, and GWG below this range was considered inadequate. Second-trimester fasting maternal venous blood samples were collected between 14 weeks 0 days and 27 weeks 6 days of gestation after an overnight fast of at least 8 h, processed within 4 h of collection, and stored at −80 °C until analysis. These samples were then used for the measurement of TG, total cholesterol (TC), HDL-C, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), insulin, C-peptide, and lipidomic profiling.

Lipid extraction

Thawing of frozen samples was carried out at 4°C, followed by the addition of 100 μL of two internal standard mixtures. Immediately, 300 μL of pre-cooled methanol and acetonitrile mixture (2:1, v/v) containing a mixture of internal standards [PC 15:0/18:1(d7), LysoPE 18:1-d7, Phosphatidylserine 15:0/18:1(d7)] was added to the samples for lipid extraction. The mixture underwent a 1-min vortex and a 20-min centrifugation at 4,000 rpm, followed by incubation for 2 h at −20°C. Subsequently, the supernatant was reconstituted in 150 μL of 50% methanol and centrifuged for 30 min. To minimize systematic bias and ensure reliable experimental results, samples were randomized and de-identified before analysis. The supernatant was transferred into autosampler vials for further analysis.

Lipidomics data acquisition

Lipidomic analysis was performed using an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography coupled to high-resolution tandem mass spectrometry platform to ensure robust detection and accurate quantification of serum lipids.

Chromatographic separation was achieved on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC I-Class Plus system (Waters, USA) equipped with a Waters Charged Surface Hybrid C18 column (1.7 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm). The column temperature was maintained at 55 °C. Mobile phase A consisted of 60% acetonitrile/40% water with 10 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid, and mobile phase B was 90% isopropanol/10% acetonitrile with 10 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid; the pH of the mobile phase was adjusted to 3.0 with ammonium formate and formic acid to achieve acidic conditions optimal for electrospray ionization (ESI). The gradient elution program was as follows: 0–2.0 min, 40–43% B; 2.0–2.1 min, 43–50% B; 2.1–7.0 min, 50–54% B; 7.0–7.1 min, 54–70% B; 7.1–13.0 min, 70–99% B; 13.0–13.1 min, 99-40% B; 13.1–15.0 min, re-equilibration at 40% B. The flow rate was 0.4 mL/min and the injection volume was 5 μL.

Mass spectrometric (MS) detection was performed on a Thermo Fisher Q Exactive high-resolution MS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) equipped with a heated ESI source operated in both positive and negative ion modes. Full-scan MS spectra were acquired over m/z 200–2000 with a resolution of 70,000 (at m/z 200), an automatic gain control target of 3 × 106, and a maximum injection time of 100 m s. Data-dependent MS/MS acquisition selected the top 3 most intense ions per scan for fragmentation, with MS/MS spectra collected at a resolution of 17,500, automatic gain control target of 1 × 105, and maximum injection time of 50 m s. Stepped normalized collision energies of 15, 30, and 45 eV were applied to ensure comprehensive fragmentation across lipid classes. ESI source parameters were set as follows: sheath gas flow rate = 40, auxiliary gas flow rate = 10, spray voltage = 3.80 kV in positive mode and −3.20 kV in negative mode, capillary temperature = 320 °C, and auxiliary gas heater temperature = 350 °C. Dynamic exclusion settings were applied to minimize repeated fragmentation of abundant ions. Mass calibration was performed prior to sample analysis following manufacturer protocols. All data were acquired using Thermo Xcalibur software (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Lipid identification

Raw data were processed using LipidSearch software (version 4.1, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for peak detection, lipid identification, alignment, and quantification. Lipid identification was performed using the Product ion search algorithm with the following parameters: precursor and fragment ion mass tolerance of 5 ppm, peak extraction tolerance of 5 ppm, fragment response threshold of 5.0%, M-score threshold of 5.0, and C-score threshold of 2.0. Retention time alignment was performed using a window of 0.1 min.

Adduct ions considered for identification included [M + H]+, [M + NH4]+, [M+Na]+ in positive ion mode and [M−H]-, [M−2H]-, and [M + HCOO]- in negative ion mode. Lipid annotations were assigned according to the confidence grading system implemented in LipidSearch (grades A-D), where grades A and B represent molecular-species level identifications and grades C and D correspond to lipid species level annotations based on MS/MS fragment ions.

Identified lipids were subsequently classified according to the LIPID MAPS classification system, and functional annotations were performed using the Human Metabolome Database and the KEGG databases.

Quality control and data quality assessment

Quality control (QC) samples were prepared by pooling equal aliquots from all study samples and were injected after every ten experimental samples throughout the analytical sequence to monitor instrument stability and data quality. The base peak chromatograms of QC samples in both positive and negative ion modes showed highly consistent retention times and peak intensities, indicating stable instrument performance. Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated among all QC samples based on lipid quantitative values. High inter-QC correlations indicated minimal systematic error and excellent analytical reproducibility across the batch. In addition, the coefficient of variation (CV) of each lipid feature across QC samples was calculated to assess analytical reproducibility. Lipid features with CV ≤ 30% were considered reproducible and retained for downstream analysis. In the present study, 82% of detected lipids exhibited CV values below 30%, indicating satisfactory analytical stability and data quality.

Data preprocessing and normalization

After lipid identification, the data matrix was further preprocessed before statistical analysis. Probabilistic quotient normalization was applied to normalize lipid intensities and obtain relative peak areas. Batch effects were corrected using quality control-based robust locally weighted scatterplot smoothing signal correction. Lipid molecules with a CV greater than 30% across QC samples were removed to ensure data reliability. These preprocessing procedures minimized systematic variation and improved the robustness of subsequent analyses.

Differential lipid screening and candidate feature selection

After comprehensive preprocessing and quality control filtering, a total of 830 lipid species were retained for downstream analysis from an initial set of 1012 detected lipids across second-trimester maternal serum samples. A dual-pathway screening strategy followed by regularized feature selection was employed to identify potential lipid metabolites associated with LGA risk in the derivation cohort (Figure S5).

OPLS-DA was applied to maximize discrimination between classification groups while performing dimensionality reduction and visualization of the 830 quantified lipid species. 7-fold cross-validation and 200-iteration permutation testing were used to verify model robustness and rule out overfitting. R2X(cum) and R2Y(cum) represent the model’s ability to explain variation in the X and Y matrices, while Q2 reflects predictive power under cross-validation.54 To address the multiple testing burden inherent in high-dimensional metabolomic analyses, the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR method was systematically applied, with adjusted p-values <0.05 considered statistically significant.55 Lipids meeting all of the following criteria were identified as differential through this pathway: (1) VIP values of the first two principal components >1.0; (2) fold change >1.2 or <0.83; and (3) FDR <0.05. In parallel, each of the 830 lipid species was individually evaluated using multivariable logistic regression models adjusted for maternal age, pre-pregnancy BMI, education level, parity, 2-h post-load glucose on OGTT, GWG, family history of diabetes, and PCOS. Lipids showing a significant independent association with LGA (FDR <0.05) after controlling for these clinical confounders were identified as differential through this pathway. Lipids identified as differential by either pathway (OPLS-DA or multivariable logistic regression) were combined to form a comprehensive candidate pool for subsequent feature selection. MetaboAnalyst 6.0 was used to annotate the signaling and biochemical metabolic pathways associated with these differential lipids.

LASSO regression

To identify the most informative lipid metabolites with optimal predictive capacity for LGA, we applied the LASSO regression to this candidate pool. This penalized regression method performs simultaneous variable selection and regularization, thereby enhancing model interpretability and predictive accuracy. To ensure the stability and reproducibility of feature selection, we performed 50 repetitions of 10-fold cross-validation and identified the optimal penalty parameter lambda based on the minimum mean cross-validation error criterion. Only lipid species selected in ≥90% of iterations (≥45/50) were retained as candidate biomarkers for model construction.

WGCNA of lipid profiles

To explore coordinated changes in maternal serum lipids and their relationship with clinical features, a weighted lipid co-expression network was constructed using the WGCNA R package. Prior to network construction, outlier samples were removed via hierarchical clustering of the samples. A soft-thresholding power of was selected based on the scale-free topology criterion (R2 > 0.8). Pairwise Pearson correlations between all lipids were computed to generate an adjacency matrix, which was subsequently transformed into a topological overlap matrix. Lipids were clustered based on topological overlap matrix dissimilarity, and modules of tightly connected lipids were identified using dynamic tree cut, with a minimum module size of 30 and a merge cut height of 0.25. Module-trait relationships were assessed by computing Pearson correlations between module eigengenes and key clinical traits, including age, pre-pregnancy BMI, birth weight, GWG, FPG, 1-h, and 2-h post-load glucose values on OGTT, TC, TG, HDL-C, LDL-C, and HOMA-IR. For each lipid species, module membership and gene significance were calculated.

Prediction model development and validation

To assess the predictive value of selected lipid species for LGA, we conducted the ROC curves and calculated the AUC with 95% CIs estimated by 1,000-iteration bootstrap resampling. Four predictive models were developed using the derivation cohort: (1) Model 1: adjusted for maternal age, pre-pregnancy BMI, systolic blood pressure, educational level, GWG, family history of diabetes, and PCOS; (2) Model 2: Model 1 plus additional routine metabolic parameters including 2-h post-load glucose levels on OGTT, HDL-C, LDL-C, TG, and TC; (3) Model 3: a multivariate model with 11 differential lipid species simultaneously entered, which were selected through LASSO regression analysis; and (4) Model 4: a comprehensive multivariate model simultaneously including indicators in Model 2 and Model 3.

All four models were applied to the temporal validation cohort using regression coefficients estimated exclusively from the derivation cohort. The optimal probability threshold for each model was determined in the derivation cohort using Youden’s index and applied directly to the temporal validation cohort without re-optimization. AUC values between the derivation and validation cohorts were formally compared using the DeLong test, with p > 0.05 indicating no statistically significant performance degradation. Additional performance metrics including sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, precision, and F1 score were calculated for both cohorts.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Maternal baseline characteristics were presented as mean (SD), median [IQR], or n (%), as appropriate. Group differences were assessed by Student’s t test, Mann-Whitney U test, chi-square, and Fisher’s exact test, where applicable. Pearson correlation analyses were conducted to explore associations between differential lipid metabolites and maternal clinical parameters. Post-hoc power calculation was performed for each lipid species using Cohen’s d effect sizes derived from group differences to assess the likelihood of detecting the observed effects given the current sample size. Multicollinearity among covariates was assessed by calculating variance inflation factors, with values <5 indicating no significant collinearity.56 In all analyses, n represents the number of individual participants unless otherwise specified. A two-sided p-value of less than 0.05 was established as the threshold for statistical significance. Detailed information regarding statistical tests, sample sizes, and significance levels is provided in the corresponding figure legends and Results section. All statistical analyses in this study were conducted using R software (version 4.4.2).
