Multi-dimensional joint resource scheduling for 5G eMBB by a PER-branch dual deep Q-network
Authors: Shujun Xu, Ying Li, Jianqin Guo, Xiaofei Mo, Guixin Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The large-scale deployment of fifth-generation (5G) networks has increased the complexity of resource management in enhanced mobile broadband (eMBB) scenarios. Network operators must jointly optimize throughput, energy efficiency, and user fairness. However, conventional scheduling algorithms often struggle to adapt to dynamic channel conditions and heterogeneous service requirements. To address these challenges, this study proposed a multidimensional resource scheduling framework based on a Prioritized Experience Replay Branching Double Deep Q-Network (PER-BDQ). The proposed model employed an action-branching architecture to independently optimize subcarrier allocation, discrete transmit power levels, and computational resource allocation. This decoupled decision mechanism reduced action-space complexity and improved training convergence. Experiments were conducted in a massive multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) multi-stream transmission environment. The proposed method achieved an aggregate system throughput of 11.34 Gbps and an energy efficiency of 53.9 bits/J, while maintaining stable learning behavior with a cumulative reward standard deviation of 22.3. Compared with baseline methods, the proposed framework consistently improved performance across simulation scenarios. Overall, the results demonstrated the effectiveness of multidimensional joint resource scheduling in simulated 5G eMBB environments and provided a useful reference for intelligent scheduling design.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-57050-y.

Introduction

Fifth-generation (5G) mobile communication technology, as a new generation of wireless communication infrastructure, was developed to satisfy rapidly increasing demands for high data rates, ultra-low latency, high reliability, and massive connectivity. Among its major application scenarios, enhanced Mobile Broadband (eMBB) supports bandwidth-intensive services such as high-definition video streaming, virtual reality, and augmented reality, which impose substantial challenges on network resource scheduling1–3. Traditional static or semi-static scheduling mechanisms, including Round Robin (RR), Maximum Carrier-to-Interference Ratio (Max C/I), and Proportional Fair (PF), have achieved satisfactory performance in fourth-generation (4G) Long-Term Evolution (LTE) systems. However, these methods were primarily designed under stationary channel assumptions and fixed service models. Consequently, they are not well suited to the highly dynamic user distributions, heterogeneous service requirements, and complex interference patterns of 5G networks. In dense small-cell deployments, rapid variations in Channel State Information (CSI) and increased user mobility further limit the ability of rule-based scheduling methods to capture the nonlinear relationship between system states and optimal decisions. This limitation constrains improvements in spectrum efficiency and Quality of Experience (QoE)4,5.

In recent years, Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) has emerged as an effective approach for sequential decision-making in complex and dynamic 5G environments. Through continuous interaction between an agent and its environment, DRL enables end-to-end optimization without requiring an explicit system model6–8. Compared with conventional resource scheduling methods based on analytical models or handcrafted rules, such as PF, Max C/I, convex optimization approaches, and Lyapunov-based queue scheduling algorithms, DRL exhibits stronger representation capability in modeling high-dimensional state spaces, multi-resource coupling relationships, and nonlinear constraints. These traditional methods can partially adapt to channel fluctuations and dynamic user behavior and have been widely applied in wireless resource management. However, their performance in 5G eMBB scenarios is often limited by model complexity and optimization scalability when spectrum, power, and computing resources are highly coupled. Therefore, DRL is more suitable for addressing the combinatorial complexity associated with multidimensional joint resource optimization. Existing studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of DRL in subproblems such as power control, user association, and channel allocation9,10. However, as networks evolve toward sixth-generation (6G) systems and artificial intelligence (AI)-native architectures, resource management has become increasingly complex and subject to stricter performance requirements11. Recent studies have extended DRL to Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) for unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) communications12, privacy-preserving Federated Reinforcement Learning (FRL) in wireless systems13, and cross-layer resource coordination in large-scale Mobile Edge Computing (MEC) environments14. Despite these advances, most existing DRL approaches still focus on single-dimensional decision-making and fail to fully exploit the coupling among spectrum, power, and computing resources in 5G New Radio (NR) systems15. In addition, multidimensional action spaces often cause combinatorial explosion, leading to slow convergence and unstable policies. These limitations reduce the effectiveness of DRL methods in eMBB scenarios, where both efficiency and reliability are critical.

To address these limitations, this study proposed an intelligent downlink resource scheduling method for 5G eMBB based on a Prioritized Experience Replay Branching Double Deep Q-Network (PER-BDQ). The core innovation of the proposed framework is the introduction of a branching action mechanism. Specifically, the framework employs a shared feature extraction layer with multiple parallel output branches, in which subcarrier allocation, discretized power control, and edge computing resource allocation are modeled as independent action branches. This design preserves the sample efficiency of value-based learning methods while mitigating the exponential growth of the action space. To improve training stability, a Double Deep Q-Network (Double DQN) architecture was adopted to reduce Q-value overestimation. In addition, Prioritized Experience Replay (PER) was incorporated to enhance the utilization of informative channel-state samples. Together, these components establish an end-to-end scheduling framework with strong physical feasibility for simulated 5G-Advanced and future 6G communication scenarios. The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:A branching architecture with a shared representation layer was designed to jointly schedule subcarriers, power, and computing resources, transforming the exponential action space into a linearly scalable structure and improving sample efficiency.A Double DQN framework combined with PER was adopted to mitigate Q-value overestimation and improve the utilization of high-priority transition samples, thereby enhancing training stability and policy robustness.An action-masking mechanism was introduced to enforce power and computing constraints, thereby improving the feasibility of the learned policies in practical systems.

Literature review

Research status of 6G networks and AI-native architectures

As mobile communication systems evolve toward 6G networks, AI-native architectures have emerged as a key enabler of pervasive intelligent connectivity. Wang et al. investigated a task-driven 6G AI-native architecture and emphasized the role of endogenous intelligence in supporting on-demand resource allocation16. Du et al. proposed an adaptive Federated Learning (FL) framework with Differential Privacy (DP) guarantees to enable secure data transmission in large-scale heterogeneous 6G environments. Their work provided a practical approach for privacy-preserving resource scheduling under distributed network conditions17. Juliy et al. developed a DRL-assisted channel decoding method for 5G and 6G systems, improving transmission robustness under severe channel conditions18.

Although substantial progress has been achieved in endogenous intelligence and privacy-preserving mechanisms, existing studies on 6G AI-native architectures have paid limited attention to practical scheduling constraints. In eMBB scenarios, high-frequency resource scheduling must balance computational overhead and signaling costs under massive access conditions. This trade-off remains insufficiently explored in the current literature.

Review of multi-dimensional heterogeneous network resource scheduling

In 5G multi-dimensional heterogeneous networks, most classical resource scheduling studies were based on deterministic optimization and heuristic algorithms. These approaches leveraged local CSI and system constraints to balance interpretability and computational complexity. They established a solid foundation for current research and served as baseline references for subsequent learning-based methods. Wu et al. proposed a two-stage heuristic PF resource allocation scheme for user-centric cell-free massive multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) networks. The method combined modularity-based grouping with a distributed PF allocation strategy. It utilized local CSI to enable cross-timeslot resource allocation, thereby improving network throughput while maintaining user fairness19. Du et al. combined the strengths of maximum weighted delay-first scheduling and PF algorithms. They proposed a dynamic weighted PF scheduling method, which improved resource allocation performance in heterogeneous networks integrating visible light communication and conventional wireless communication20. Noliya and Kumar evaluated multiple resource allocation algorithms in 5G NR networks. They formulated the resource allocation problem as a stochastic optimization task and proposed a hybrid approach that integrated learning-based and optimization-based methods21. Overall, these studies relied on PF scheduling, heuristic optimization, and stochastic optimization. They addressed multi-user resource allocation problems and achieved trade-offs among fairness, throughput, and latency. However, they typically depended on structural assumptions or predefined optimization criteria. Consequently, they faced challenges such as high computational complexity and limited capability to capture nonlinear coupling in dynamic environments, especially in high-dimensional joint resource allocation problems. These limitations motivated the adoption of data-driven DRL methods in complex 5G eMBB scenarios.

As fifth-generation advanced (5G-Advanced) and future network environments become increasingly complex, cross-domain resource coordination has emerged as a key research focus. Ivanova et al. jointly modeled the session layer and medium access control (MAC) layer using stochastic geometry and queueing theory to analyze traffic coexistence between ultra-reliable low-latency communication (URLLC) and eMBB services. Their results indicated that strict session-layer admission control could reduce packet loss probability, but might lead to latency violations for URLLC under high-load conditions, thereby requiring resource over-provisioning to guarantee quality of service (QoS)22. Parashar and Arya proposed a resource optimization framework for device-to-device (D2D)-assisted 5G Internet of Things (IoT) networks. By applying Lyapunov optimization for power control and dynamic resource allocation, their method enabled network slicing adaptation and effectively managed URLLC and eMBB traffic coexistence23. He et al. investigated adaptive multi-dimensional resource slicing in cognitive space–air–ground integrated vehicular networks and improved cross-domain transmission efficiency through dynamic spectrum and computing resource allocation24. Song et al. developed a multi-agent deep learning-based resource management framework for UAV communication networks, integrating backhaul traffic optimization and intelligent reflecting surface (IRS) techniques to enhance network throughput, energy efficiency, and reliability25. Mohajer et al. proposed FlexSlice, a dynamic offloading framework that combined sparse multi-head graph attention mechanisms with a twin delayed deep deterministic policy gradient (TD3) algorithm to improve resource allocation in mobile edge networks through accurate traffic prediction and adaptive decision-making26.

These studies cover a wide range of heterogeneous scenarios, including UAV-, satellite-, and space–air–ground-integrated networks. However, most existing approaches rely on a unified action space for decision-making and do not explicitly model the coupling among spectrum, power, and computing resources. As a result, decoupled low-latency scheduling frameworks remain insufficiently explored.

Advances in reinforcement learning optimization and efficiency

To improve sample efficiency and policy stability, recent studies have focused on refining DRL architectures. Doanis et al.27 proposed a sample-efficient multi-agent deep Q-network (DQN) for cross-domain slice orchestration in 5G + networks. Their method improved scalability by enhancing state representation in large-scale network slicing scenarios. Ali et al. developed a multi-agent double deep Q-learning (DDQN) framework for multi-dimensional resource allocation in cognitive radio networks28. Their decoupled Q-value estimation strategy effectively mitigated Q-value overestimation. Li et al. applied DQN-based methods to online monitoring and decision-making in high-dimensional data streams, demonstrating their capability in handling large-scale state spaces29. Lou et al. introduced a PER mechanism for off-policy reinforcement learning, which improved training efficiency and accelerated convergence30. Mohajer et al. proposed a multi-agent adaptive service offloading framework that integrated spatiotemporal graph attention, gated recurrent units (GRU), and a hybrid reward mechanism. The method enables service level agreement (SLA)-aware collaborative offloading and adaptive orchestration, achieving improved latency performance and service satisfaction under non-stationary edge traffic conditions31.

Despite these advances, a key limitation remains. Although PER improves sample efficiency, standard DDQN-based frameworks remain inefficient for multi-dimensional joint resource scheduling. As the number of decision variables increases, the action space grows exponentially, limiting real-time applicability in 5G eMBB scenarios. Therefore, a decoupled action branching architecture is required to enable scalable and efficient multi-resource decision-making.

Review of physical-layer transmission capacity and spectral efficiency limits

The physical feasibility of high system throughput depends on advances in multi-stream transmission at the physical layer. Li et al. investigated an enhanced multi-stream beamforming scheme for 5G NR Massive MIMO systems32. Their method incorporated accurate channel quality indicator (CQI) feedback to improve spatial multiplexing gain. The results indicated that advanced MIMO configurations, when combined with intelligent scheduling, can approach the theoretical upper bound of spectral efficiency. Building on these physical-layer gain models, this study introduced an action branching mechanism at the algorithmic decision level to align resource scheduling with underlying transmission constraints. This design ensures that resource allocation remains consistent with physical capacity limits while enabling multi-objective optimization. It also improves the physical interpretability and realism of simulation results.

Despite extensive research on 5G and emerging 6G resource scheduling, including UAV networks, multi-agent DRL, edge computing offloading, and multi-dimensional resource allocation, several limitations remain. Most existing studies focus on single- or two-dimensional optimization and lack explicit joint modeling of spectrum, power, and computing resources. In addition, action spaces are typically represented as unified vectors, which limits the scalability of low-latency scheduling frameworks. Furthermore, many studies rely on simplified simulation environments that do not fully capture practical constraints such as physical-layer limitations, CSI feedback delay, queue dynamics, and service level agreement (SLA) requirements. As a result, they struggle to meet the demands of high-throughput, multi-stream transmission and fairness guarantees in eMBB scenarios. In addition, the benefits of action-branching architectures and PER in multi-dimensional joint resource scheduling have not been systematically validated. To address these gaps, this study proposes a PER-BDQ-based joint resource scheduling framework. The proposed method enables parallel decision-making over subcarrier allocation, power control, and computing resource allocation through a branching action structure, while improving training stability and sample efficiency via Double DQN and PER. This provides a more scalable and physically consistent solution for multi-dimensional resource scheduling.

Research model

Markov decision process modeling for resource scheduling problems

In eMBB scenarios of 5G networks, resource scheduling must cope with highly dynamic user distributions, time-varying channel conditions, and heterogeneous service demands33. Traditional optimization methods typically rely on accurate system models and stationary assumptions, which limits their effectiveness under real-world network uncertainty. Reinforcement learning (RL) provides a data-driven framework for sequential decision-making in such environments, where the underlying problem is commonly formulated as a Markov decision process (MDP)34. This study develops a multi-cell downlink system model for 5G eMBB scenarios. The system consists of multiple base stations (gNodeBs) and user equipment (UEs). Each base station has access to quasi-real-time CSI, and orthogonal subcarrier allocation is assumed within each cell. Inter-cell interference is also considered in the system model. In addition, each user’s computation task can be offloaded to an edge server, and computational resource allocation is incorporated into the scheduling decision. Multi-objective reward function design and optimization objectives

In 5G eMBB scenarios, a single performance metric (such as throughput) cannot comprehensively reflect user experience. Therefore, scheduling strategies must balance system throughput, energy efficiency, and user fairness. As the “command stick” of reinforcement learning, the design of the reward function directly determines the convergence direction of the strategy. An ideal reward function should possess differentiability, interpretability, and multi-objective coordination capabilities36,37. In this study, the resource scheduling problem is formulated as a multi-objective optimization problem. A weighted-sum approach is adopted to approximate the Pareto optimal solution, enabling the reinforcement learning agent to make coordinated decisions among competing objectives.

Energy efficiency is further normalized with respect to its theoretical maximum value:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\widetilde{EE}_{t} = \frac{{EE_{t} }}{{EE_{{{\mathrm{max}}}} }}$$\end{document}

User fairness is evaluated using Jain’s fairness index, defined as:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$J_{t} = \frac{{\left( {\mathop \sum \nolimits_{k = 1}^{N} \overline{V}_{k,t} } \right)^{2} }}{{N\mathop \sum \nolimits_{k = 1}^{N} \overline{V}_{k,t}^{2} }}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\overline{V}_{k,t}$$\end{document} = \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{1}{t}\sum\nolimits_{\tau = 1}^{t} {V_{k,\tau } }$$\end{document} denotes the average data rate of user k, ensuring that the fairness metric lies within the range [0,1].

Based on the above metrics, the instantaneous reward function is defined as a weighted sum:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{t} = w_{1} \tilde{V}_{t} + w_{2} \widetilde{EE}_{t} + w_{3} J_{t} + w_{4} \frac{1}{N}\sum\nolimits_{k = 1}^{N} {U\left( {T_{k,t} } \right)}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${w}_{i}\ge 0$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sum}_{i=1}^{4}{w}_{i}=1$$\end{document}; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$U({T}_{k,t})$$\end{document} denotes a latency-related utility function that reflects fairness or task completion performance. The weight coefficients can be determined via Pareto sensitivity analysis.

The final objective is to maximize the cumulative discounted reward, which is formulated as a MDP optimization problem:12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pi^{*} = {\mathrm{arg}}\mathop {{\mathrm{max}}}\limits_{\pi } {\mathbb{E}}_{\pi } \left[ {\sum\nolimits_{t = 0}^{\infty } {\gamma^{t} R_{t} } } \right]$$\end{document}where π denotes the policy mapping function, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathbb{E}}_{\pi }[\bullet ]$$\end{document} represents the expectation under policy π. With this multi-objective reward design, the reinforcement learning agent can achieve coordinated optimization among throughput, energy efficiency, fairness, and task latency.

A DRL architecture based on PER-BDQ

To address the combinatorial explosion of the action space in conventional DQN for multi-dimensional joint resource scheduling38,39, as well as the mismatch between continuous action representations and value-based learning frameworks, a PER–based branched DQN (PER-BDQ) architecture is adopted in this study. The core idea is to decompose the high-dimensional joint action space into multiple independent branches, thereby reducing combinatorial complexity and improving training efficiency. The overall framework is illustrated in Fig. 2.Fig. 2PER-BDQ algorithm architecture.

PER-BDQ algorithm architecture.

The PER-BDQ framework consists of two main components: a shared feature extraction layer (state encoder) and a multi-branch action head. The state encoder maps the MDP state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${s}_{t}$$\end{document} into a unified latent representation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\phi ({s}_{t})$$\end{document}, which encodes CSI, queue backlogs, historical actions, fairness indicators, and computation task characteristics. The action head adopts a branching architecture, where the joint action space is decomposed into three independent branches corresponding to subcarrier allocation, power control, and computation resource allocation, respectively. The subcarrier allocation branch outputs Q-values \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${Q}_{1}({s}_{t},{a}_{1})$$\end{document} associated with user selection \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${u}_{r}$$\end{document} for each RB, defining the user–RB mapping. The power control branch produces Q-values \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${Q}_{2}({s}_{t},{a}_{2})$$\end{document} over discrete transmit power levels \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${p}_{r}$$\end{document}. The computation resource branch outputs Q-values \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${Q}_{3}({s}_{t},{a}_{3})$$\end{document} corresponding to discrete computation allocation ratios \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${f}_{r}$$\end{document}. Each branch produces a discrete Q-value distribution over its respective action dimension, directly aligned with the physical decision variables. To obtain the overall action value, a weighted aggregation mechanism is employed:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q_{{{\mathrm{total}}}} \left( {s_{t} ,a_{t} } \right) = \sum\nolimits_{d = 1}^{3} {\alpha_{d} Q_{d} \left( {s_{t} ,a_{d} } \right)}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${Q}_{total}({s}_{t},{a}_{t})$$\end{document} denotes the value of the joint action \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${a}_{t}=({a}_{1},{a}_{2},{a}_{3})$$\end{document} under state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${s}_{t}$$\end{document}; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${Q}_{d}({s}_{t},{a}_{d})$$\end{document} represents the Q-value of branch \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d\in \{\mathrm{1,2},3\}$$\end{document}; and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\alpha}_{d}\ge 0$$\end{document} are weighting coefficients that control the relative contribution of each decision dimension, which can be determined based on task priorities or sensitivity analysis. To stabilize learning, the target network parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\theta }^{-}$$\end{document} are periodically updated using a soft update mechanism.

Due to the multi-branch structure of BDQ, a conventional single TD (Temporal Difference)-error prioritization strategy is not directly applicable. Therefore, a weighted multi-branch temporal-difference (TD) error is defined as:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta_{t} = \sum\nolimits_{d = 1}^{3} {\beta_{d} \delta_{t}^{d} }$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\delta}_{t}^{d}$$\end{document} denotes the TD error of branch d at time step t, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\beta}_{d}\ge 0$$\end{document} satisfies \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sum}_{d=1}^{3}{\beta}_{d}=1$$\end{document}.

The priority of each transition is defined as:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_{t} = \left| {\delta_{t} } \right| + \epsilon$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P}_{t}$$\end{document} is the sampling priority in the replay buffer, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mid {\delta}_{t}\mid$$\end{document} measures the magnitude of the weighted TD error, and ε > 0 is a small constant to ensure non-zero sampling probability. The importance sampling weight is defined as:16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_{t} = \left( {\frac{1}{N} \cdot \frac{1}{{P_{t}^{\alpha } }}} \right)^{\beta }$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${w}_{t}$$\end{document} is the correction weight used in experience replay, N is the size of the replay buffer, α ∈ [0,1] controls prioritization strength, and β\betaβ is the annealing factor used to correct sampling bias and stabilize training. This mechanism increases the sampling frequency of transitions with higher learning significance across subcarrier allocation, power control, and computation resource scheduling, thereby improving sample efficiency and reducing instability in high-dimensional discrete action spaces.

The complete training process of PER-BDQ is summarized as follows:

Step 1: Initialize the main network parameters θ and target network parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta^{ - } = \theta$$\end{document}.

Step 2: Initialize the PER buffer.

Step 3: At each time step, feed state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${s}_{t}$$\end{document} into the shared encoder to obtain branch-wise Q-values, and select actions using an ϵ-greedy policy. Execute the joint action \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${a}_{d}$$\end{document}, observe reward \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R}_{t}$$\end{document} and next state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${s}_{t+1}$$\end{document}, and compute branch-wise TD errors \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\delta}_{t}^{d}$$\end{document}.

Step 4: Update transition priorities \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P}_{t}={\sum}_{d}{\beta}_{d}{\delta}_{t}^{d}+\epsilon$$\end{document}, sample mini-batches from the replay buffer, and compute weighted TD loss for gradient backpropagation to update θ.

Step 5: Perform soft target network update every K steps: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\theta }^{-}\leftarrow \tau \theta +(1-\tau ){\theta }^{-}$$\end{document}.

The proposed PER-BDQ framework enables efficient joint optimization of multi-dimensional resource allocation in large-scale action spaces. It improves training stability and sample efficiency while maintaining low-latency decision-making performance in dynamic 5G eMBB downlink scenarios.

Action representation discretization and physical constraint mapping

To align with the discrete decision mechanism of BDQ, continuous wireless and computational resources are discretized into resource-block-level sequential actions. Consider a transmission time interval (TTI) consisting of R RBs. The decision of the agent for the r-th RB at time t is defined as a triplet:17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_{t}^{\left( r \right)} = \left( {u_{t}^{\left( r \right)} ,\,p_{t}^{\left( r \right)} ,\,\eta_{t}^{\left( r \right)} } \right),r = 1,2, \ldots ,R$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${u}_{t}^{\left(r\right)}$$\end{document} denotes the selected user index, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${p}_{t}^{\left(r\right)}$$\end{document} represents the discrete transmit power level, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\eta}_{t}^{\left(r\right)}$$\end{document} denotes the discrete computation resource allocation level. The argmax operation is applied at the level of each individual RB rather than over the entire set of 1200 subcarriers simultaneously. The final scheduling decision within a TTI is obtained by sequentially or parallelly determining all resource-block-level actions and aggregating them into a complete scheduling solution. This formulation decomposes the original global combinatorial action space into a set of resource-block-level local decision spaces, thereby mitigating the curse of dimensionality and maintaining consistency with the branching structure of BDQ. For the subcarrier allocation branch, the network outputs a Q-value vector of dimension N, corresponding to all candidate users. Let the masked Q-value for user selection be denoted as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\widetilde{Q}}_{u}({s}_{t},k)$$\end{document}. The user assignment for RB r is given by:18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u_{t}^{\left( r \right)} = {\mathrm{arg}}\mathop {{\mathrm{max}}}\limits_{{k \in {\mathcal{U}}_{t}^{\left( r \right)} }} \tilde{Q}_{u} \left( {s_{t} ,k} \right)$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{U}}_{t}^{\left(r\right)}$$\end{document} denotes the feasible user set for RB r, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\widetilde{Q}}_{u}$$\end{document} is the masked Q-value after feasibility filtering. The user–resource allocation indicator is defined as:19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_{k,r,t} = \left\{ {\begin{array}{*{20}c} {1,} & {u_{t}^{\left( r \right)} = k} \\ {0,} & {{\mathrm{otherwise}}} \\ \end{array} } \right.$$\end{document}

Thus, the scheduling matrix at time t is represented as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${X}_{t}=[{x}_{k,r,t}{]}_{N\times R}$$\end{document}. Since each RB can be assigned to only one user, the following constraint holds:20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathop \sum \limits_{k = 1}^{N} x_{k,r,t} = 1,\forall r$$\end{document}

This formulation corresponds to a standard Orthogonal Frequency Division Multiple Access (OFDMA) scheduling model, where each RB serves only one user, while each user may occupy multiple RBs.

The maximum transmit power \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${P}_{\mathrm{m}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{x}}$$\end{document} is discretized into L levels. To ensure implementation feasibility and stable learning, a uniform discretization scheme is adopted:21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p^{\left( l \right)} = \frac{l - 1}{{L - 1}}P_{{{\mathrm{max}}}} ,l = 1,2, \ldots ,L$$\end{document}\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${p}^{\left(l\right)}$$\end{document} denotes the l-th discrete power level, and L represents the number of discrete power levels. The power selection for the r-th RB is given as:22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_{t}^{\left( r \right)} = {\mathrm{arg}}\mathop {{\mathrm{max}}}\limits_{{l \in {\mathcal{P}}_{t}^{\left( r \right)} }} \tilde{Q}_{p} \left( {s_{t} ,l} \right)$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{P}}_{t}^{\left(r\right)}$$\end{document} denotes the feasible power set under the remaining power budget, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\widetilde{Q}}_{p}$$\end{document} is the masked Q-value of the power branch. This discretization preserves monotonicity and enables stable exploration across different power regimes.

The total edge computing capacity is discretized into \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${M}_{c}$$\end{document} levels. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\eta }^{\left(m\right)}\in [\mathrm{0,1}]$$\end{document} denote the m-th computation resource level:23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eta^{\left( m \right)} = \frac{m - 1}{{M_{c} - 1}},m = 1,2, \ldots ,M_{c}$$\end{document}

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${M}_{c}$$\end{document} denotes the number of discrete computational resource levels. The computational resource selection for the r-th RB is given as:24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eta_{t}^{\left( r \right)} = {\mathrm{arg}}\mathop {{\mathrm{max}}}\limits_{{m \in {\mathcal{C}}_{t}^{\left( r \right)} }} \tilde{Q}_{c} \left( {s_{t} ,m} \right)$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{C}}_{t}^{\left(r\right)}$$\end{document} denotes the feasible set under the remaining computation budget, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\widetilde{Q}}_{c}$$\end{document} is the masked Q-value of the computation branch. The computation allocation at the user level is obtained by aggregation over RBs:25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eta_{k,t} = \mathop \sum \limits_{r = 1}^{R} x_{k,r,t} \,\eta_{t}^{\left( r \right)}$$\end{document}

This formulation explicitly couples communication scheduling and computation resource allocation at the action level.

To guarantee feasibility of the joint decision at each time step, a dynamic action masking mechanism is introduced. A value of 1 indicates that the corresponding action is feasible, while 0 indicates that the action violates the current system constraints. This masking mechanism ensures that the argmax operation is performed only over the physically feasible action set, thereby guaranteeing that the joint action inherently satisfies the total transmit power constraint, the total computational resource constraint, and the one-to-one user–RB assignment constraint. In other words, the multiple branches do not make independent and mutually unrelated decisions; instead, they perform coordinated decision-making within a shared dynamic feasible region. As a result, the resulting scheduling matrix, power allocation vector, and computational resource allocation vector are all strictly implementable.

Through the proposed discretization and constraint mapping strategy, continuous physical resources are transformed into resource-block-level discrete actions compatible with the PER-BDQ framework. This formulation preserves the physical interpretability of 5G eMBB systems while effectively avoiding the combinatorial explosion associated with global joint action spaces.

Experimental design and performance evaluation

Datasets collection

This study utilized the DeepMIMO dataset as the primary source of CSI. DeepMIMO is an open-source large-scale MIMO channel dataset released by the research team of IEEE Fellow Prof. H. Yanikomeroglu. It strictly follows the 3GPP TR 38.901 standard and supports multiple representative 5G scenarios, including Urban Micro (UMi). The dataset is publicly available at https://deepmimo.net. It generates high-precision channel responses using a ray-tracing engine, incorporating user locations, path loss, multipath components, and complex baseband channel coefficients, thereby ensuring full compliance with the standardized channel modeling framework. In this study, the “O1” scenario (urban street environment) was selected, with a base station height of 25 m and a user equipment (UE) height of 1.5 m, covering a 1 km × 1 km area. This scenario includes 10,000 UE location samples, each associated with frequency-domain CSI over 1200 subcarriers (subcarrier spacing: 30 kHz, total bandwidth: 36 MHz), together with multipath components and complex baseband channel coefficients. For service load and queue dynamics, the publicly available 5G-ERA traffic dataset (released by the EU 5G PPP project, https://5g-era.eu/data) was integrated. This dataset contains packet arrival timestamps and traffic volumes from real eMBB applications (e.g., YouTube streaming and Zoom conferencing), exhibiting statistical properties consistent with self-similarity and burstiness, making it suitable for buffer evolution modeling. By integrating these two publicly available datasets, end-to-end training samples were constructed.

The simulation scenario was defined as a 5G UMi downlink environment. Channel modeling strictly followed the 3GPP TR 38.901 specification. A massive MIMO configuration was adopted to enable high-throughput transmission. The simulation incorporated realistic channel impairments, including path loss, shadow fading, and multipath propagation. To further evaluate robustness, an inter-cell interference model compliant with 3GPP standards was introduced. The interference power was modeled as a log-normal random variable to emulate dense small-cell deployments.

As shown in Table 2, a 64 × 4 Massive MIMO configuration with 16 spatial streams was employed. Under 16 parallel layers, the equivalent spectral efficiency per stream reached approximately 19.6 bps/Hz. This value is consistent with the physical-layer limits assumed in 5G-Advanced standards. At the algorithmic level, a branching output architecture replaced the conventional single-index output. Separate branches were designed for subcarrier allocation, power levels (10 discrete levels), and computing resource levels (5 levels). This design transformed the action space structure from multiplicative growth to additive growth. As a result, the dimensionality problem inherent in standard DQN was fundamentally resolved. The exploration strategy adopted ε-greedy, with ε initialized at 1.0 and linearly decayed to 0.01. The target network was updated every 50 steps. The replay buffer was warmed up for 500 steps to avoid initial bias. The random seed was fixed at 42 to ensure consistency in network initialization, training data generation, and experimental reproducibility.

To strictly enforce the maximum transmit power (0.2 W) and bandwidth (36 MHz) constraints, an Action Masking mechanism was implemented. When the total allocated resources approached the predefined limits in Table 2, infeasible action branches were dynamically blocked.

To ensure the statistical reliability of the experimental results, all simulations were repeated over 10 independent runs. The mean values were reported as the final results, and both the mean and standard deviation were calculated to mitigate the impact of randomness.

Overall, the system throughput was determined jointly by channel conditions, spatial multiplexing gain, and MCS selection, ensuring physical implementability and alignment with practical 5G system constraints.

The DDQN variant with the Double mechanism effectively mitigates Q-value overestimation, leading to improvements in both throughput and energy efficiency, as well as enhanced training stability. With the addition of PER, sample efficiency is further improved, resulting in an 18.6% reduction in convergence time compared to DDQN, while also significantly enhancing performance for low-rate users. Finally, the complete PER-BDQ model achieves the best performance across all metrics, improving throughput by 4.2% over BDQ and reducing training variance. This demonstrates that PER and the branching architecture are complementary, where the former improves sample efficiency and the latter reduces action-space complexity.

Discussion

The proposed PER-BDQ resource scheduling method achieved a balanced trade-off among system throughput, energy efficiency, and user fairness. Compared with the Double DQN-based power allocation strategy reported by Ghomri et al.40, the primary advantage of the proposed approach lies in its more comprehensive multi-objective coordination capability. The method proposed by Ghomri et al. mainly focused on optimizing energy efficiency, which may reduce fairness for edge users under high traffic loads. In contrast, the composite reward function adopted in this study maintained the Jain’s Fairness Index above 0.92 while preserving relatively high aggregate throughput. These results suggest that the action-branching architecture can better support Pareto-efficient optimization across multiple resource dimensions and alleviate the imbalance commonly observed in single-objective optimization.

From an architectural perspective, the proposed framework differs fundamentally from the multi-connectivity network introduced by Fang et al.41. Their method addressed the coexistence of eMBB and Ultra-Reliable Low-Latency Communication (URLLC) in industrial automation scenarios through a multi-connectivity architecture integrating cellular and D2D links. The framework optimized power and spectrum allocation to improve eMBB capacity while satisfying URLLC QoS constraints. In contrast, the PER-BDQ framework employs parallel and decoupled action branches, in which subcarrier allocation, power control, and computational resource distribution are jointly optimized within a unified decision network. This design improved energy efficiency to 53.9 bits/J. In addition, a shared feature extraction layer was used to capture the spatiotemporal coupling between frequency-domain resources and computational workloads, thereby improving overall resource utilization.

In terms of training stability and convergence efficiency, comparisons were conducted with the standard DQN scheduler proposed by Zhang et al.42. When applied to large combinatorial action spaces, the standard DQN exhibited policy instability caused by Q-value overestimation and limited sample efficiency. By integrating PER with Double DQN, the proposed method reduced the standard deviation of cumulative rewards to 22.3 during the final training stage. The model converged within 1980 epochs, while inference latency remained below 1.7 ms. These findings indicate that the branching architecture effectively transforms exponential action growth into linear branch-wise decision-making, thereby reducing the computational burden of DRL in real-time 5G scheduling scenarios.

Under severe channel conditions, the proposed method maintained stable scheduling performance. Although the instantaneous channel gains of some users degraded significantly, the branching architecture and composite reward function ensured that edge users continued to receive priority in subcarrier allocation, power control, and computational resource assignment. Experimental results showed that the 5th-percentile user rate remained approximately 39.8 Mbps, while the Jain’s Fairness Index stayed above 0.92. These results indicate that the system preserved the QoS of disadvantaged users even under local channel degradation. Compared with conventional PF and Max C/I algorithms, the PER-BDQ framework achieved a better balance between throughput and fairness under low SINR conditions. These findings highlight the advantages of multi-objective optimization in dynamic and non-ideal channel environments and further validate the effectiveness of the action-masking mechanism in ensuring joint feasibility and policy robustness.

Conclusion

Research contributions

This study addressed the problem of multi-dimensional joint resource scheduling in 5G eMBB scenarios and validated the effectiveness of the proposed PER-BDQ framework. The main contributions are summarized as follows. First, subcarrier allocation, power control, and computational resource allocation were decoupled into independent yet synchronized decision branches, transforming the exponentially growing action space into a linearly scalable structure, thereby improving learning efficiency in high-dimensional discrete resource scheduling problems. Second, an action masking mechanism was introduced to ensure that decisions strictly satisfy physical constraints such as power and bandwidth limitations, improving the feasibility of scheduling policies in simulations and reducing the impact of infeasible actions during training. Third, under a large-scale MIMO multi-stream simulation environment, PER-BDQ achieved high throughput and energy efficiency while maintaining stable policy performance. The results demonstrate the effectiveness of the branching architecture in coordinating multiple resource dimensions.

Future work and limitations

Although the proposed framework achieves substantial performance gains, several limitations remain. A key direction for future research is to investigate the impact of imperfect CSI on scheduling robustness. In practical deployments, estimation errors and feedback delays may degrade decision optimality. As wireless networks evolve toward 6G AI-native architectures, privacy preservation and large-scale collaborative learning will become increasingly important. Future work will explore FRL for cross-base-station resource scheduling, aiming to achieve globally optimized resource allocation without exposing sensitive user data. In addition, transferability under highly dynamic scenarios—such as UAV-assisted networks and high-mobility vehicular communications—requires further investigation. Improving model adaptability in rapidly time-varying environments will be essential for practical large-scale deployment.
