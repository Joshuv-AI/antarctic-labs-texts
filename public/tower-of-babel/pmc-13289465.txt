The mediating effect of meaning in life on fear of death and attitudes toward caring for the dying among oncology nurses
Authors: Linlin Lai, Wenxia Wang, Juanjuan Zhao, Mingzhu Xin, Xue Fu, Meixian Chen, Huiying Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectivesCancer is one of the leading causes of death globally. Against the backdrop of inadequate life education and a deep-rooted death-avoidant culture in China, oncology nurses faced with multiple challenges in hospice care. The attitude of oncology nurses towards care of the dying is a crucial factor in improving the quality of palliative care. However, few studies have explored the attitude toward care of the dying of oncology nurses and their influencing mechanisms. This study aimed to explore the mediating effect of meaning in life on the fear of death and attitude toward care of the dying among oncology nurses in China.MethodsData were collected from July to August 2024 using Chinese versions of the Frommelt Attitude Toward Care of the Dying Scale, Form B (FATCOD-B-C), the Collett-Lester Fear of Death Scale (CLS), and Purpose in Life Test (CPLT-C). SPSS version 26.0 and AMOS version 24.0 were used for statistical analysis.FindingsThe mean total attitude score towards care of the dying was 95.52 ± 18.18. The fear of death was positively correlated with the meaning in life (r = 0.442, P < 0.01), and positively correlated with the attitude toward care of the dying (r = 0.340, P < 0.01). Meaning in life was positively correlated with attitude towards care of the dying (r = 0.474, P < 0.01). Meaning in life partially mediated the relationship between fear of death and attitudes toward care of the dying. The mediating effect (0.297) accounted for 63.19% of the total impact (0.479).ConclusionNurses’ fear of death is positively correlated with their attitude towards care of dying, and the meaning of life plays a partial mediating role in it. This discovery challenges the common view that “fear of death is necessarily detrimental to humanistic care”, highlighting the significant role of existentialist resources in shaping nurses’ professional attitudes. Based on this, intervention measures should focus on strengthening the intermediary resource of life meaning. Medical administrators, educators and policymakers can combine traditional training methods with emerging technologies to help nurses master palliative care knowledge and skills while cultivating and consolidating a sense of life meaning.Keywords: Mediating effect, Fear of death, Attitude toward care of the dying, Oncology nurses, Palliative care, Meaning in life

Introduction

Cancer is one of the leading causes of death, ranking second globally. It is estimated that nearly 0.61 million people died of cancer in the United States in 2024 [1]. In China, cancer is also a major public health problem, the total number of cancer deaths in China is 2.57 million [2], which has led to an urgent demand for palliative care to address the physical, psychological, social, and spiritual needs of patients and their families during the end-of-life stage.

In addition to clinical skills, one of the key points to offer high level of palliative care is to improve their attitude, which helps patients and their families to plan hospice care, manage concerns, and foster good interdisciplinary care [3, 4]. Current research has explored some of this, but the overall level about attitude toward care of the dying of nurses and is less than ideal [5]. This leads to negative consequences such as poor quality of care, burnout, emotional exhaustion, and even further leads to increased turnover rates. However, it is notable that there is a lack of research specifically targeting oncology nurses, a particular group with unique challenges and responsibilities. Therefore, against the background of the surging cancer patient mortality rate, it is essential to probe deeply into oncology nurses’ attitudes toward care of the dying and their underlying influencing mechanisms.

Thanatophobia or fear of death is humanity’s most fundamental fear. One definition is “Fear of death is a psychological feeling and emotional response, manifested as helplessness and anxiety when facing the uncertainty of death in terms of time while also knowing that it is inevitable” [6]. The death of others, the social culture that rejects death and extinction, and the metaphysical fear of facing “the death of oneself” are all sources of the fear of death and extinction [7]. Fear of death, was significant negative predictors influencing nursing students’ attitude towards the care of the dying [8]. Likewise, another study in China had similar results [9]. But the mediated model constructed by Yu et al. [10] shows the exact opposite result: fear of death was significantly and positively correlated with attitude towards caring for people who are dying.

Meaning in Life refers to the individual’s integrated perception of the meaning and purpose of self-existence [10]. The current scholarly consensus is that meaning in life is a subjective sense that consists of three primary components: comprehension (or coherence), purpose, and existential mattering (or significance) [11]. Researchers have found that individuals with a greater sense of meaning in life are more likely to accept death [12], and research also shows the link between the meaning in life and the attitude towards caring for the dying [10].

Within the framework of Viktor Frankl’s meaning-centered theory [13], human behavior is fundamentally driven by a “will to meaning,” an innate motivation to find purpose and significance in life, even in the face of suffering and mortality. Frankl articulated that meaning in life can be discovered through three distinct pathways: creative value, realized through work and action; experiential value, derived from love and relationships; and attitudinal value, which concerns the stance one takes toward unavoidable suffering, guilt, and death [14]. Unlike Freud and Adler, who focused more on the capacities for love and work, Frankl regarded the ability to find meaning in unavoidable suffering as a crucial therapeutic resource, one that enables individuals to transform adversity into personal achievement and attain self-transcendence [15].

Among these three pathways, attitudinal value offers a particularly relevant lens for understanding oncology nurses. Nurses cannot alter the fact that their patients will die, yet they retain the freedom to choose their stance toward this reality. When they consciously approach death with dignity, compassion, and professional commitment, they engage precisely in what Frankl identified as attitudinal value: discovering meaning in circumstances that cannot be changed. For oncology nurses who face heavy workloads and frequent encounters with death, this process of meaning-making through attitude may serve a crucial psychological function. Specifically, when confronted with death-related distress, a well-developed sense of meaning in life, especially meaning derived from how one faces mortality, may help nurses reinterpret their fear not as a threat but as an inherent component of a purposeful professional role. In this view, meaning in life operates as a mediating mechanism. It is the psychological resource through which fear of death is transformed into a positive and engaged attitude toward caring for dying patients.

The literature has identified a correlation between attitudes toward death, meaning in life, and attitude toward care of the dying [10, 16–18]. However, studies mainly focus on nursing students, and few studies pay attention to oncology nurses as a special group and use meaning-centered theory to test the relationship between these different variables. Based on the theoretical framework, the following hypotheses were proposed: (1) Fear of death and meaning in life are each associated with attitudes toward caring for the dying; (2) Meaning in life mediates the relationship between fear of death and attitudes toward caring for the dying. Drawing on prior research, we constructed a mediating model with a sample of Chinese oncology nurses to examine the relationships among fear of death, meaning in life, and attitudes toward caring for the dying.

Materials and setting

Study design and setting

The cross-sectional survey was conducted among nurses from a tertiary grade A specialized oncology hospital from a public cancer center in Guangdong Province, China, from July to August 2024 using convenience sampling.

Sampling and sample size

The study included nurses from intensive care units and general wards of cancer center. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years old; (2) nurses who have obtained a nurse practice certificate and are currently engaged in front-line clinical nursing work; (3) informed consent. Exclusion criteria were: (1)nurses with clinically diagnosed mental disorders or severe psychological problems. (2)nurses in a further study or internship, and on a leave of absence during the survey period were excluded. Referring to the standards of social science research, the following parameters were sited: significance level α = 0.05 (two-tailed), power (1-β) = 0.80, and medium effect size f²=0.15. Using G*Power 3.1 software, the minimum required effective sample size was calculated as 222 cases. Considering a potentially invalid questionnaire rate of 20%, the final sample size was adjusted and rounded up to 277 cases.

Data collection instruments

The online survey was conducted using Questionnaire Star, a popular Chinese - based online survey platform. A link to the questionnaire and a QR code were sent through WeChat, and nurses completed their responses online. Before commencing the questionnaire, the aim and significance of this research were briefly elucidated. All participants provided informed consent by choosing‘yes’or‘no’before completing the questionnaires. Those who consented were obliged to submit an electronic signature.

To control the quality of the data, non-target sample screening configurations were implemented. the questionnaire could only be answered once. Answering times that were abnormal (less than 1 min or greater than 10 min) were automatically flagged as invalid questionnaires. Additionally, only when the “agree” option was chosen for question No. 20 could the questionnaire be successfully submitted. Participants were reminded about the voluntary nature of the study and their freedom to withdraw from the study. Among them, all questions of 11 questionnaires were marked as “0” or “5,” which is considered invalid due to an obvious logical contradiction. Finally, a total of 277 questionnaires were included in this study and a response rate of 96.18%.

Demographic and background information questionnaire

This self-designed questionnaire includes 9 general demographic data based on literature review, including gender, whether being the only child, self-perceived mental health status, whether there is a direct relative with cancer, funeral participation experience, talking about death with family members, talking about death with colleagues, frequency of experiencing patient death, whether having been exposed to reports on death education in mass media.

Fear of death (CLS)

The Collett-Lester Fear of Death Scale (CLS) was jointly developed by Collett and Lester in 1969 and revised again in 2003 by Lester [19]. The Chinese version of the CLS was adapted by our research team following standard cross-cultural adaptation guidelines [20], including forward translation by two independent bilingual translators, synthesis by a third researcher, back translation by two additional translators blinded to the original version, expert review (six specialists), and psychometric evaluation with 282 physicians. This scale is used to measure the level of nurses’ fear of death and consists of four dimensions with 28 items, including fear of death of the self (7 items), fear of death of others (7 items), fear of dying of the self (7 items), and fear of dying of others (7 items). The items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not at all, 5 = very much). The total score ranges from 28 to 140, higher scores indicate greater fear of death, lower scores suggest less fear or discomfort with these topics. The Cronbach’s alpha in this study is 0.742.

Meaning in life (PIL)

Purpose in Life Test (PIL) was originally developed by American psychologists Crumbaugh and Maholick in 1964 [21], and later translated and revised into Chinese (C-PIL) by Shek in 1992 [22]. It is used to measure the degree to which an individual perceives the meaning and purpose of life, and the degree to which an individual acquires the meaning of life. The scale consists of four dimensions, a total of 20 items, including attitude to life (5 items), life value (6 items), life goals (7 items), and autonomy of life (2 items). The items are rated on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = not at all, 7 = very much). Total scores were calculated by summing all 20 items, yielding a possible range from 20 to 140, with higher scores indicating a stronger sense of meaning in life. Cronbach’s alpha in this study is 0.681.

Attitude toward care of the dying (FATCOD-B-C)

Frommelt Attitude Toward Care of the Dying Scale, Form B (FATCOD-B-C) was developed by Frommelt in 1991 [23], and used assess healthcare workers’ attitudes towards hospice care, including cognitive, emotional and behavioral intentions. Wang et al. [24] chinesize the scale to evaluate medical workers’ and medical students’ attitude to hospice care. It encompasses six dimensions and a total of 29 items, including attitude toward the interests of the dying person (6 items), attitude toward caring for the dying person (6 items), attitude toward the necessity of family support (5 items), attitude toward fear of caring for the dying person (5 items), attitude toward communication with the dying person (4 items), and attitude toward caring for the dying person’s family (3 items). The items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not at all, 5 = very much). Negatively worded items were reverse scored, and all items were summed to obtain a total score, with possible scores ranging from 30 to 150. Higher scores reflect more positive attitudes toward care of the dying, whereas lower scores indicate less favorable (i.e., more negative or fearful) attitudes, while lower scores indicate a weaker sense of meaning in life.

Cronbach’s alpha in this study is 0.694.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The participants’ sociodemographic data were described by frequency (percentage) for categorical variables, mean (standard deviation) for normally distributed continuous data, and median (interquartile range) for non-normally distributed continuous data. Pearson’s correlation was used to assess the correlation between nurses’ fear of death, meaning in life, and attitude toward care of the dying. The structural equation model was established by using AMOS 24.0 software to obtain path coefficients and model fitness indicators. To test the significance of the indirect effect, we employed a bootstrap procedure with 5,000 resamples to generate bias-corrected and accelerated 95% confidence intervals. A significant mediating effect is indicated if the confidence interval does not include zero.

Among the 277 participants surveyed, the gender distribution was as follows: 32 (11.6%) were male and 245 (88.4%) were female. In terms of family structure, 43 individuals (15.5%) were the sole child within their family unit. With respect to mental health self-assessment, 204 respondents (79.4%) reported a favorable psychological well-being status. Regarding the prevalence of cancer within their families, 205 participants (74.0%) indicated having direct relatives affected by the disease. Concerning funeral attendance, 173 individuals (62.5%) had partaken in such ceremonies on one to two occasions. When it came to the subject of death-related conversations, 152 respondents (54.9%) stated that they only engaged in discussions with their families when deemed necessary. Additionally, 157 participants (56.7%) perceived themselves as possessing a relatively liberal stance towards conversing about death with their colleagues. Over half of the surveyed nurses experienced the death of a patient while at work, and 12.6% have never experienced it. The structure equation model

A mediating effect analysis was performed, with fear of death serving as the predictor variable, meaning in life as the mediator, and attitude toward care of the dying as the outcome variable. For both the direct effect (the impact of fear of death on attitudes towards care of dying) and the mediating effect (the role of the meaning of life in it), the 95% confidence interval did not cross zero. This indicates that fear of death not only directly influences the attitude toward care of the dying but also exerts an indirect impact through the mediating role of meaning in life. The fitting indices of the modified model are as follows: X2/df = 2.317, RMSEA = 0.069, GFI = 0.920, AGFI = 0.885, CFI = 0.914, IFI = 0.915, TLI = 0.943. The direct effect (0.173) accounted for 36.81% of the total effect (0.479), while the mediating effect (0.297) constituted 63.19%. Detailed results are presented in Table 4. Discussion

This study examined the relationships among fear of death, meaning in life, and attitudes toward care for the dying in a sample of 277 Chinese oncology nurses. The findings revealed a positive association between fear of death and attitudes toward care for the dying, with meaning in life serving as a partial mediator. This result challenges the common assumption that fear of death is inherently detrimental to compassionate care and highlights the role of existential resources in shaping nurses’ professional attitudes.

Regarding the level of attitudes toward caring for the dying, the mean score among oncology nurses was 95.52 ± 18.18, accounting for roughly 65.88% of the total score. The scores obtained in the current study were lower than those reported in previous studies: specifically, slightly lower than second- or third-year students without clinical internship experience (98.08 ± 8.02) [10], and also lower than senior undergraduates students who had completed three years of on-site instruction and at least ten months of continuous clinical practice (104.72 ± 10.62) [25].

This paradox, where greater clinical experience appears to correlate with a decline in attitude scores, can be interpreted through the lens of professional factors and cultural context. Educational exposure, as evidenced by Alba Maestro-Gonzalez’s longitudinal study [26], effectively builds a positive foundation among students. Students typically learn about hospice care in structured [27], supportive environments where ideal scenarios are emphasized. Under the guidance of campus culture, undergraduate students are more likely to view hospice care care as a professional mission and a humanitarian responsibility. Traditional Chinese cultural elements such as grief and avoidance of death are present in their awareness but have not yet been strongly activated by clinical experiences. This may lead them to overestimate their preparedness for the emotional and ethical complexities of real-world practice. In contrast, practicing oncology nurses face the daily reality of caring for dying patients, with extensive clinical experience [28]. They confront family members’ intense emotional responses, including desperate pleas for curative treatment and difficulty accepting the reality of impending death. At the same time, they experience an internal conflict between their professional commitment to “healing the sick and saving lives” and the inevitable outcome of death. In this context, traditional cultural taboos surrounding death, such as avoiding discussions of mortality, can easily become sources of emotional distress [29]. Prolonged and emotionally intense contact leads to compassion fatigue, burnout, and emotional exhaustion [30].

The current study found that oncology nurses’fear of death was significantly and positively correlated with the attitude toward care of the dying, which were similar to the results of Yu et al. [10]. but more pronounced than those reported by Wang et al. [24]. Notably, higher scores on the attitude scale indicate more positive attitudes toward caring for the dying. On the surface, this seems counterintuitive, as fear is usually regarded as a negative emotion. From Frankel’s perspective, this may reflect the “activation conditions of the will to mean”. As discussed earlier, Frankl identified three pathways to meaning: creative, experiential, and attitudinal value. For oncology nurses, this theoretical framework finds support in the present findings. When facing the inevitability of patient death or their own fear of death, nurses can still discover meaning by consciously choosing their attitude: recognizing that accompanying patients through their final journey is itself valuable, reflecting experiential value; recognizing that alleviating patient suffering is their own contribution, reflecting creative value; and choosing to face death with dignity and compassion, reflecting attitudinal value. Oncology nurses face the death of patients every day. This continuous “death prompt” is not simply the accumulation of fear, but a persistent call upon the will to meaning. This kind of impact forces them to reflect, to construct and to confirm the meaning of their careers and lives. This repeated confrontation may be understood as a process akin to “death reflection” [31], in which existential fear becomes a source of meaning and connection.

Yet meaning-making does not fully account for the observed association. The partial mediation also suggests that fear of death exerts a direct influence on attitudes, independent of meaning. For oncology nurses, fear is not simply a negative emotion to be avoided [32]. It can also reflect a heightened awareness of life’s finitude. This awareness alone may prompt them to approach palliative care with greater seriousness, to be more present with patients, and to approach palliative care with greater conscientiousness. Fear can also sharpen vigilance and heighten accountability. When nurses worry about falling short, about failing to provide a peaceful death, that concern may drive them to be more meticulous and attentive. At the same time, fear can serve as a marker of professional commitment. Those who willingly work near death often feel honoured and professional [33]. A complete lack of fear may signal emotional numbness or burnout, whereas the presence of fear often indicates that they still care, still invest themselves. In these ways, fear of death in this context is not inherently detrimental; it can coexist with and even foster positive attitudes through existential awareness, heightened responsibility, and sustained professional commitment.

Within the Chinese cultural context, where direct discourse on death is often avoided as a means of bihui (averting misfortune). Nurses are compelled to navigate the tension between the cultural imperative to bihui and the professional duty to provide a “good death” [34]. It is precisely through navigating this tension that nurses engage in the meaning-making processes described earlier, developing a more grounded and resilient attitude over time. Consequently, the attitude measured in seasoned nurses is not the naive idealism of a student, but a hard-won professional virtue, forged in the continual negotiation between cultural taboos and the realities of life’s end.

Based on these findings, interventions for oncology nurses should move beyond simply reducing fear of death and instead focus on strengthening meaning in life as a mediating resource. Specifically, meaning-centered programs grounded in Frankl’s logotherapy, such as narrative sharing, reflective writing, and value clarification exercises, could help nurses recognize attitudinal value in their work and transform fear into compassionate attitudes toward care for the dying. Regular assessment of meaning in life may also be incorporated into mental health screening to identify nurses in need of early support. Organizational efforts to foster open discussion about death and existential reflection, such as Balint groups, meaning-focused workshops, or the creation of life story books, can help sustain meaning in life as a positive mediating mechanism. Such approaches may enhance nurses’ psychological resilience and ultimately improve the quality of care for dying patients.

Compared with previous studies, this study has several advantages. In this study, 277 nurses from the Cancer Center of Guangdong Provincial Public Hospital were taken as samples to comprehensively explore the relationship between the attitude of oncology nurses towards fear of death, meaning of life and their attitude toward care of the dying in the Chinese context. The results are relatively dialectical.

Research limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged.

Reliability of the measurement instruments

The Cronbach’s alpha values for the PIL (0.681) and FATCOD-B-C (0.694) were slightly below the conventional threshold of 0.70. This may be attributed to the multidimensional nature of both instruments, the cultural sensitivity of death-related topics in the Chinese context, and the heterogeneity of the oncology nurse sample. Nevertheless, both scales have been extensively validated in previous studies, item analysis confirmed that deletion of individual items did not meaningfully improve reliability, and the values obtained fall within a range often considered acceptable for cross-cultural research and for scales measuring complex psychosocial constructs. Future studies may consider further psychometric validation or the use of alternative instruments to confirm the findings.

Geographic representativeness

The geographical representativeness of the samples is limited. The study only included a sample of nurses from Guangdong Province. The economic and medical development level of this province ranks among the top in the country, and it is difficult to represent regions with relatively scarce resources such as the central and western regions. In an environment with tight resource allocation and higher workloads, nurses may lack the psychological space to reflect on the meaning of life, and their attitude towards care of dying may also be more inclined towards “task completion” rather than “humanistic care”. Furthermore, discussions on the topic of death in coastal areas are relatively open, while in regions where traditional concepts are deeply rooted, death remains a cultural taboo. This difference may significantly influence nurses’ attitudes towards death.

Institutional specificity

The institutional background has particularity. All the samples were from provincial tertiary cancer centers. Nurses working in top specialized hospitals have a stronger sense of professional identity and may tend to choose answers that conform to the “ideal professional image”, leading to a bias in social approval. Meanwhile, the specialized culture oriented towards “healing” may subconsciously regard death as a failure of treatment, and this cultural conflict may not be obvious in non-specialized hospitals. In addition, large medical centers usually have a relatively better ratio of nurses to patients, with dedicated palliative care teams or psychological support teams. The well-established support system itself constitutes a protective resource, which may buffer the negative impact of fear of death.

Sampling bias

The sampling method may lead to selection bias. Nurses who actively participate in surveys on the theme of “death” often have a strong concern for such issues themselves, while those who have avoided or experienced pain due to recent patient deaths are more likely to refuse to participate, resulting in self-selection bias. Furthermore, the sample only covered on-the-job nurses, and those who were exhausted or resigned due to hospice care stress were not included. This group is precisely the key subject for studying negative psychological outcomes.

These findings should be interpreted in light of the limitations noted above. Future research may benefit from multi-center stratified sampling across diverse geographic regions and institutional levels, as well as the incorporation of longitudinal designs and qualitative approaches.

Conclusion

This study examined the relationships among fear of death, meaning in life, and attitudes toward carefor the dying in a sample of Chinese oncology nurses. The results revealed a positive association between fear of death and attitudes toward care for the dying, with meaning in life serving as a partial mediator.These results challenge the assumption that fear of death is inherently detrimental to compassionate care and highlight the role of existential resources in shaping nurses’ professional attitudes.

Based on these findings, interventions should focus on strengthening meaning in life as a mediating resource. Future healthcare administrators, educators, and policymakers should target meaning in life alongside other key factors to develop interventions that enhance hospice care practices. For example, traditional educational methods such as workshops, case studies, and role-playing can be combined with emerging technologies such as virtual reality and artificial intelligence to help nurses cultivate meaning in life and master the theoretical knowledge and practical skills of hospice care.
