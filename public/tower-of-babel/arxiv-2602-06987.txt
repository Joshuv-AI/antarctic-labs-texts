diffpy.morph : Python tools for model independent comparisons between sets of 1D functions
Andrew Yang, Christopher L. Farrow, Pavol Juhás, Luis Kitsu Iglesias, Chia-Hao Liu, Samuel D. Marks, Vivian R. K. Wall, Joshua Safin, Sean M. Drewry, Caden Myers, Dillon F. Hanlon, Nicholas Leonard, Cedomir Petrovic, Ahhyun Jeong, Dmitri V. Talapin, Linda F. Nazar, Haidong Zhou, Samuel W. Teitelbaum, Tim B. van Driel, Soham Banerjee, Emil S. Bozin, Michael F. Toney, Katharine Page, Naomi S. Ginsberg, Simon J. L. Billinge

Abstract
diffpy.morph addresses a need to gain scientific insights from 1D scientific spectra in model independent ways. A powerful approach for this is to take differences between pairs of spectra and look for meaningful changes that might indicate underlying chemical, structural, or other modifications. The challenge is that the difference curve may contain uninteresting differences such as experimental inconsistencies and benign physical changes such as the effects of thermal expansion. diffpy.morph allows researchers to apply simple transformations, or “morphs”, to one of the datasets to remove the unwanted differences revealing, when they are present, non-trivial differences. diffpy.morph is an open-source Python package available on the Python Package Index and conda-forge. Here, we describe its functionality and apply it to solve a range of experimental challenges on diffraction and PDF data from x-rays and neutrons, though we note that it may be applied to any 1D function in principle.



I
Introduction

Neutron and X-ray powder diffraction (NXPD) and atomic pair distribution function (PDF) analysis are powerful tools for probing the local and average structure of materials
Dinnebier and Billinge 2008
;
Egami and Billinge 2012
.
They can provide many insights; for example, one can compare NXPD patterns and PDFs of samples measured at different temperatures to search for structural changes
Billinge and Jensen 2024
.
This may be done in a model independent way by comparing two patterns and looking for meaningful changes, for example, by plotting the difference curve between the patterns or computing similarity metrics such as the profile-weighted agreement factor (
R_{w}

Dinnebier and Billinge 2008
;
Egami and Billinge 2012
) or the Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC
Pearson and Galton 1895
).
This task is made more difficult in the presence of less interesting effects such as expansion/contraction due to temperature changes, thermal smearing due to increased thermal motion, and certain experimental artifacts.
These are effects that may not represent “interesting” structural changes, but can produce large contributions to the residual curves and
R_{w}
s, hiding more scientifically interesting structural changes.
A challenge is to remove the uninteresting changes to reveal scientifically interesting ones when they are present.

Here we present the Python program
diffpy.morph
that provides a solution to this problem by first applying simple transformations, or “morphs”, to spectra to partially or fully account for these non-interesting effects, allowing any interesting changes that can’t be removed by the morph to become apparent.

We note that this approach is completely general and may be applied beyond diffraction and atomic pair distribution function
Egami and Billinge 2012
data to any kinds of 1D spectra.
The approach is fast and model independent and is a powerful tool for interrogating data in real-time as it is being collected.

Here we describe the approach and test it on a number of different real-world examples from our own studies.
We show how it can be used to reveal when a structural phase transition occurs, and when it does not, in temperature series PDF data.
We then present an example where a thermodynamic parameter
\beta
, the (linear) thermal expansion coefficient, is extracted directly from temperature-dependent data without modeling,
and in contrast, where the temperature of a sample can be extracted given a known
\beta
, again in a model independent way.
We present an example where experimental inconsistencies could be removed between datasets allowing them to be compared despite the inconsistencies.
Finally, we also present some more advanced use-cases, where we use
diffpy.morph
to find optimal parameters in a data reduction workflow by morphing a low
Q_{\mathrm{max}}
high-throughput data to a reference high
Q_{\mathrm{max}}
target dataset.

diffpy.morph
is easy to use and flexible. It is open source (BSD license)
Regents of the University of California 1988
and available on PyPi
Yang et al. 2025a
and conda-forge
conda-forge community 2025
. It has a command-line interface and API allowing it to be integrated into users’ Python scripts. The program can read most column-style data files (e.g. ‘.xy’, ‘.dat’, ‘.chi’, ‘.gr’), with and without header information.
It also automatically handles comparison of two functions plotted on different grids by using linear interpolation.

Though this software was originally designed in the context of PDFs,
diffpy.morph
and its morphs can be easily applied to any general function.
For example, a neutron scattering experimentalist could subtract the hydrogen contribution to a density of states (DOS) by morphing a known hydrogen DOS to their measured DOS and taking the difference curve, or a computer vision scientist comparing two pictures taken at different brightnesses can give the (flattened) 2D images to
diffpy.morph
to correct for intensity differences.

II.2
Extracting Material Properties using Morph Comparisons

II.2.1
Thermal expansion and Debye temperature using stretch morph on temperature dependent x-ray data

Using a series of PDFs measured at different temperatures, one can estimate the linear thermal expansion coefficient of a material using the

In Fig.

\displaystyle\beta(T_{a})

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta(T_{b})-\eta(T_{a})}{(T_{b}-T_{a})(1+\eta(T_{a}))}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\eta(T_{b})-\eta(T_{a})}{T_{b}-T_{a}},

(2)

II.2.2
Using
diffpy.morph
for
in situ

The converse of finding the thermal expansion coefficient from temperature series data is using a known thermal expansion coefficient to estimate sample temperature in a model independent way.

There are few available techniques for precisely measuring the temperature of diffraction experiment samples heated to high temperatures. One such technique is pyrometry, but thermal gradients caused by uneven heating of the material and ablation in the scattering region can greatly limit the accuracy of the measurement
Ushakov et al. 2015
;
Aldebert 1984

II.2.3
Nanoparticle shape information from shape morphs

The shape and size of a nanoparticle can greatly affect its (electronic, optical, etc.) properties

;
Tolbert and Alivisatos 1994
.
If one has PDF measurements of a bulk and a nanoparticle of the same material, it is possible to use a nanoparticle “
shape

By way of example, we show how it is possible to determine the radius of a spherical nanoparticle using a
shape
morph.

This morph can be very useful if you have a PDF calculated from a bulk model but your data is from a nanocrystalline sample.

diffpy.morph
will then multiply the bulk PDF by the chosen nanoparticle characteristic function

;
Egami and Billinge 2012

\gamma_{0}(r)

If a good fit is obtained, the model choice and refined shape parameters can be considered useful, though other models should be tested to search for alternative possible candidates

;
Banerjee et al. 2019
;
Banerjee et al. 2020
;
Tao et al. 2024

Significant difference curve signals or

This approach has previously been used to estimate diameters of spherical CdSe nanoparticles consistent with those obtained from transmission electron microscopy, ultraviolet-visible spectroscopy, and photoluminescence measurements

Here, we have used this approach to estimate the size of PbS nanocrystals with thiostannate (Sn

II.3
High-Throughput Experiment Use Cases

diffpy.morph
has been utilized in high throughput experimental settings, such as synchrotron beam-times, for a variety of use-cases.
These include to expose sources of experimental error (e.g. incorrect sample-to-detector distance or misaligned beam center), to identify and remove unwanted signals such as laser induced heating, and to identify optimal processing parameters to compute reliable PDFs from measured
I(Q)
data.

A major advantage of using
diffpy.morph
in a high-throughput data setting is its speed and ease of use.
Rather than performing a lengthy structural refinement, one can obtain morphing parameters in seconds.

III
Morphing transformations

In this section we define the current set of morphs available in
diffpy.morph
and develop some theory behind some of the most widely used morph transformations.

In the general setup of morphing, there is a “target” signal and a “morphed” signal.
The target signal is unchanged, while the morphs are applied to the morphed signal.

The objective of the morphing process is to minimize the residual between the two signals by applying a series of simple “morphing” transformations.
This is like fitting, but it is model independent and it is designed simply to mimic physical effects, such as uniform lattice expansion or changes in thermal vibration, though it could be anything.

The signals could be any 1D functions or spectral signals such as a diffraction patterns, atomic pair distribution functions, but could also be Raman, NMR, or something else.

For simplicity we develop the theory with the spectra being PDFs with the understanding that the morphs can be applied to other signals.
Throughout this section, the unmorphed object PDF will be denoted
G(r)
, and the same PDF after morphing will be denoted as
G_{m}(r;\mathbf{p})
, where
\mathbf{p}
denotes the set of morphing parameters.

III.0.1
Scaling morph

Scaling morphs correct the intensity of the morphed PDF by a constant
s
. Scaling simply captures the fact that a measured signal might be on a different scale due to differences in the incident flux during measurements, instruments used, uncertainties in the data reduction process, or any factor that serves to change the scale of a signal.
The
scale
morph can be expressed simply as

\displaystyle G_{m}(r;s)=s\cdot G(r),

(10)

where
s
is the
scale
factor.

III.0.2
Stretching morph

Stretching morphs stretch the PDF linearly along the independent variable axis.
Stretching simulates the effect of a uniform lattice expansion of the structure when
\eta
is positive, while
-1<\eta<0
simulates a uniform lattice contraction.

The morph is done by linearly interpolating the
r
grid on to a new stretched
r
-grid,
r^{\prime}
.
We chose linear interpolation as most PDF data are stored on dense grids, so errors introduced are small and computational speed is a premium.

In detail, given a
stretch
factor
\eta
, the stretched distance,
r^{\prime}
is given by

r^{\prime}=\frac{r}{1+\eta}.

(11)

The resulting
stretch
morph is therefore given by

\displaystyle G_{m}(r;\eta)=G\left(\frac{r}{1+\eta}\right).

(12)

A real uniform lattice expansion with thermal strain
\epsilon
will also rescale the PDF on the
y
-axis by a factor
(1+\epsilon)^{-2}
(see Supplemental Material, Section
S.2
). Therefore, we recommend using a
scale
morph any time a
stretch
morph is used to account for intensity changes due to isotropic expansion in the physical system.

III.0.3
Smearing morph (general)

Smearing morphs apply a Gaussian broadening factor to broaden a signal. For example, this will mimic different levels of thermal motion in PDF data (Section
III.0.4
).
To accomplish this we carry out a convolution of the signal with a Gaussian function whose width can be varied.

The Gaussian broadening factor
T_{\sigma}(r)
has the form

\displaystyle T_{\sigma}(r)=\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{r-r_{c}}{\sigma}\right)^{2}\right\},

(13)

where
\sigma
is the
smear
factor, and
r_{c}=\frac{1}{2}(r_{max}+r_{min})
is the center of the
r
-grid.
The choice of
r_{c}
is arbitrary, but centering the convolution Gaussian in the middle of the
r
-grid minimizes edge effects in the numerics of the resulting convolution.

The
smear
morph then convolves the morphed signal with the broadening function
T_{\sigma}(r)
according to

\displaystyle R_{m}(r;\sigma)=\frac{(R\circledast T_{\sigma})(r+r_{c})}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}.

(14)

This serves to broaden all peaks in
R(r)
; specifically, the variance of each Gaussian peak is increased by
\sigma^{2}
after convolution.
The
r+r_{c}
shifts the smeared curve to ensure its centroid matches that of
R(r)
, and the normalization factor
1/\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}
ensures the two curves have the same integrated magnitudes. Proof that this operation indeed broadens the peaks of
R(r)
by
\sigma^{2}
while maintaining the centers and integrated magnitudes of each peak can be found in Supplemental Material, Section
S.4
.

Note that
T_{\sigma}(r)=T_{-\sigma}(r)
, so a
smear
parameter of
+\sigma
is equivalent to a
smear
parameter of
-\sigma
.

III.0.4
Smearing morph (PDF-specific)

The
smear
morph described above can be used to smear any signal.
When that signal is a measured
G(r)
function, it is preferred to convert
G(r)
to the radial distribution function (RDF
Egami and Billinge 2012
) before applying the
smear
. As such,
diffpy.morph
contains a modified version of the
smear
morph, called
smear-pdf
.
This is because atomic motion acts to broaden peaks in the RDF,
R(r)
, and not the PDF
G(r)
.

The differences are small and an approximately correct result can be obtained by smearing
G(r)
directly.
However, for more accurate results when dealing with PDFs, a user may use the
smear-pdf
morph instead of the
smear
morph. At the command line, this is done by using the
--smear-pdf
option instead of the
--smear
option. When using
diffpy.morph
in a Python script, you would use the
smear_pdf
parameter instead of
smear
. If both
smear-pdf
and
smear
options are used, the program ignores the
smear
input and applies the
smear-pdf
morph only.

For bulk materials, the RDF is related to the PDF by
Farrow and Billinge 2009

\displaystyle G(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{R(r)}{r}-4\pi r\rho_{0},

(15)

where
\rho_{0}
is the atomic density of the material, and
-4\pi\rho_{0}
may be referred to as the “baseline slope”.
When
smear-pdf
is used, the input functions are assumed to be PDFs. The program will first convert it to an RDF,
R(r)
, using either a user-given baseline slope or a best-fit slope (if the user is unable to provide one).
The smear morph then applies a Gaussian broadening factor
T_{\sigma}(r)
to
R(r)
.
The morphed
R_{m}(r)
is then converted back to a PDF which is then returned as the morphed PDF.

III.0.5
Shifting Morph

Shifting morphs shift the signal horizontally or vertically. They may be needed to account for constant backgrounds or calibration errors such as a shifted zero-point in a diffraction pattern. Shifting is also useful for data visualization techniques (e.g. waterfall plotting).

Given a user-specified horizontal
shift
parameter
\Delta_{h}
and vertical
shift
parameter
\Delta_{v}
, the
shift
morph is expressed simply as

\displaystyle G_{m}(r)

\displaystyle=G(r-\Delta_{h})+\Delta_{v}.

(16)

III.0.6
Nanoparticle shape morph in the PDF

PDFs measured from a nanoparticle and bulk sample of the same material can look quite different as the particle’s shape plays a role in shaping the pair distribution function. One can approximate the PDF of a nanoparticle
G_{nano}(r)
from the PDF of a bulk
G_{bulk}(r)
of the same material by the simple relation
Farrow and Billinge 2009

\displaystyle G_{nano}(r)

\displaystyle\approx\gamma_{0}(r)G_{bulk}(r).

(17)

The
shape
and inverse
shape
morphs in
diffpy.morph
were designed to compare bulk and nanoparticle PDFs using the nanoparticle characteristic function,
\gamma_{0}(r;\mathbf{p})
. This characteristic function depends on
r
and on some parameters
\mathbf{p}
.

The
shape
morphs take input
G(r)=G_{bulk}(r)
and simply multiply them by the characteristic function,
\gamma_{0}(r;\mathbf{p})
,

\displaystyle G_{m}(r;\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle=\gamma_{0}(r;\mathbf{p})\cdot G(r).

(18)

The inverse
shape
morphs take input
G(r)=G_{nano}(r)
and divide the characteristic function,

\displaystyle G_{m}(r)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{G(r)}{\gamma_{0}(r;\mathbf{p})}&\gamma_{0}(r;\mathbf{p})\neq 0\\
0&\gamma_{0}(r,\mathbf{p})=0\end{cases}.

(19)

Currently,
diffpy.morph
has nanoparticle characteristic functions for spheres
Kodama et al. 2006
and spheroids
Lei et al. 2009
. The spherical nanoparticle characteristic function,
\gamma_{0,sph}(r;s)
, refines the radius,
s
, of the nanoparticle. The spheroidal nanoparticle characteristic function,
\gamma_{0,srd}(r;s_{e},s_{p})
, refines the equatorial and polar radii,
s_{e}
and
s_{p}
, respectively.

III.0.7
General ordinate morph

In addition to the morphs introduced above, which are designed to account for specific physical effects,
funcy
provides a flexible interface for applying user-defined transformations to the
y
-axis data, enabling comparison of signals influenced by experiment- or system-specific distortions.
The definition of the
funcy
morph is,

\displaystyle G_{m}(r;f,\mathbf{p})=f(r,G(r);\mathbf{p}),

(20)

where
f
is a user-supplied function in the form of a Python function, and
\mathbf{p}
represents its parameter dictionary.
The user simply defines a Python function that takes the signal
r
and
G(r)
arrays (or equivalently, any
x
and
y
arrays) and a dictionary of parameters then returns the morphed
x
and
y
arrays.
This function is passed to the
funcy
interface along with the morphed and target functions and the parameters are refined to get a good agreement.

III.0.8
General grid (abscissa) morphs

Similarly it is possible to apply user-defined morphs that transform the
x
-axis of the function, generalizing the
stretch
morph. Below, we describe two morphs:
squeeze
and
funcx
. For reasons discussed at the end of this (sub)subsection,
squeeze
should be used when the expected change in the x-axis grid is small, while
funcx
could be used otherwise.

squeeze
applies a polynomial transformation to the
x
-axis, where the user can control the order of the polynomial. This offers a flexible approach for correcting non-linear distortions in signals.
A zero’th order polynomial is actually the
shift
morph and a first-order polynomial recovers the behavior of
shift
and
stretch
morphs applied together. But the
squeeze
interface generalizes this to higher orders.

These morphs have been found useful to account for geometric artifacts introduced by 2D detectors, such as module tilts, curved detection planes, or angle-dependent offsets, as well as structural effects intrinsic to the sample. Its use was introduced in Section
II.3.1
.

The transformation is defined as

\displaystyle\Delta r(r;a_{0},\cdots,a_{n})=a_{0}+a_{1}r+a_{2}r^{2}+\cdots+a_{n}r^{n},

(21)

where
\{a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\}
are the polynomial coefficients. The desired order,
n
, of the polynomial to use is a user defined input.
This is applied as

\displaystyle r^{\prime}(r;a_{0},\cdots,a_{n})=r+\Delta r(r;a_{0},\cdots,a_{n}),

(22)

resulting in a modified coordinate grid
with the morphed signal evaluated as

\displaystyle G_{m}(r^{\prime})=G(r).

(23)

Given a morph and target function,
diffpy.morph
will find the best set of
a_{0},\cdots,a_{n}
to apply in order to match the target.

To compare to previously-described morphs, if all
a_{i}=0
except
a_{0}\neq 0
,
squeeze
is equivalent to the horizontal
shift
morph with
\Delta_{h}=a_{0}
(Section
III.0.5
), and if instead all
a_{i}=0
except
a_{1}\neq 0
,
squeeze
is equivalent to a
stretch
morph with
\eta=a_{1}
(Section
III.0.2
).

The current implementation of the
squeeze
morph takes the original function as a two-column array
[[r],[G(r)]]
, where the notation
[\cdot]
indicates the quantity is an array. Then,
squeeze
computes
[r^{\prime}]
using Eq.
23
to get
[[r^{\prime}],[G_{m}(r^{\prime})]]
. Finally,
squeeze
reinterpolates
G_{m}(r^{\prime})
back onto the original grid
[r]
and returns the squeezed function interpolated on to the original grid, i.e.,
[[r],[G_{m}(r)]]
.
The reinterpolation is done for consistency and further processing.
This is computed using a cubic spline interpolation.
Extrapolation is allowed outside the original domain where needed (and the user will be notified if and where the extrapolation occurs). Care should be taken by the user if the morph results in a large extrapolation.

The
squeeze
morph will warn the user if the polynomial squeeze results in a modified grid,
[r^{\prime}(r)]
, that is not monotonically increasing.
Non-monotonicity of
r^{\prime}
poses a problem in our procedure above, as
G_{m}(r^{\prime})
may no longer be a single-valued function. This means there is no longer a unique way to interpolate
[[r^{\prime}],[G_{m}(r^{\prime})]]
onto the grid
[r]
. We resolve this by modifying
G_{m}(r^{\prime})
to be a single-valued function by first identifying all duplicated grid points
r^{\prime}_{dup}
in the array
[r^{\prime}(r)]
; i.e.
r^{\prime}_{1}=r^{\prime}_{2}=\cdots r^{\prime}_{s}=r^{\prime}_{dup}
for
s\geq 2
. We then set

\displaystyle G_{m}(r^{\prime}_{dup})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{s}G_{m}(r^{\prime}_{i}),

(24)

giving
G_{m}(r^{\prime})
a single unique value for each grid point
r^{\prime}_{dup}
where it may be multi-valued.
This procedure can result in a
[[r],[G_{m}(r)]
that looks vastly different than
[[r],[G(r)]]
if the function
G_{m}(r^{\prime})
is not smooth or the length of the non-monotonic intervals is large, but seems to work well for small extrapolations that are the most common.

In general, we advise users to avoid a non-monotonic
squeeze
. We have found success by (1) reducing the order of the polynomial and (2) first performing safe morphs like
shift
and
stretch
to get estimates of
a_{0}
and
a_{1}
, and giving those to
squeeze
as the initial parameter values.

While
squeeze
can only apply polynomials to the grid, the
funcx
, in analogy with
funcy
, morph allows arbitrary functions. The morphed signal is similarly given by Eq.
23
, though the new grid
r^{\prime}
is now given by

\displaystyle r^{\prime}(r;f,\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle=f(r,G(r);\mathbf{p}).

(25)

Here,
f
is a user-provided Python function, and
\mathbf{p}
is a dictionary of function parameters to refine over. The function
f
should take in both
r
and
G(r)
(equivalently,
x
and
y
) and return the morphed grid
r^{\prime}(r;\mathbf{p{}})
based on the parameters
\mathbf{p}
.

The implementation of the
funcx
morph is slightly different than that of
squeeze
. After taking in
[[r],[G(r)]
, we simply return
[[f(r,G(r);\mathbf{p}])],[G(r)]]
.
By design, no interpolation on to the original grid is done in this case. This choice allows arbitrary changes in the independent array, permitting, for example, functions that transform into different spaces, such as the
PDFGetter()
transform described in Section
II.3.2
.

The advantage to
funcx
is apparent when the morph and target function grids are extremely different as
squeeze
could result in strange and uncontrolled behavior.
However, this means the grid of the morphed function is constantly changing. This makes computing the residual between the morph and target functions difficult, and the calculation of the difference curve can involve varying amounts of interpolation error each iteration of the refinement. As such, we still recommend the
squeeze
morph for small changes to the grid as it operates on a single unchanging grid.

III.0.9
General function morph

While a user can use the
funcx
and
funcy
morphs together to change both the grid and data simultaneously, we have defined a
funcxy
morph for convenience. This morph is designed to be compatible with PDF calculators such as the
PDFGetter()
functor in
PDFgetX3

Juhas et al. 2013
, so one can refine the PDF calculation from a measured intensity function. This was described in Section
II.3.2
.

For this morph, the user supplies a single function
\mathbf{f}

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}(r,G(r);\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle=\left(f_{0}(r,G(r);\mathbf{p}),f_{1}(r,G(r);\mathbf{p})\right)

(26)

that takes in the arrays
r
and
G(r)
as well as a dictionary of parameters
\mathbf{p}
. The function must return two quantities: (a) the desired morph on the grid
f_{0}
and, (b) on the function values
f_{1}
. In Python, this can be done as a tuple
(f_{0},f_{1})
or as two comma-separated quantities
f_{0},f_{1}
.

The
funcxy
morph is then described by

\displaystyle G_{m}(r^{\prime};f_{1},\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle=f_{1}(r,G(r);\mathbf{p}),

(27)

where

\displaystyle r^{\prime}(r;f_{0},\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle=f_{0}(r,G(r);\mathbf{p}).

(28)

One can see the obvious parallels to Eqs.
20
,
25
.

III.0.10
Chaining together multiple morphs

All morphs (
scale
,
stretch
,
smear
,
smear-pdf
,
shape
,
squeeze
,
funcx
,
funcy
,
funcxy
, etc.) can be chained together with other morphs to incorporate multiple corrections. To do so, a user simply has to enable the morphs they want chained.

There are some limits to this and the user should use good sense. In some cases the code will prevent clashes, for example, only one of the
smear
and
smear-pdf
morphs can be used. If the user enables both on accident only the more specific
smear-pdf
morph will be done. Also, since a polynomial
squeeze
morph of order zero or higher includes the horizontal
shift
morph, the horizontal
shift
morph will be disabled when the
squeeze
morph is enabled (the vertical
shift
morph is not affected). For the same reason, if the polynomial
squeeze
is of order one or higher, the
stretch
is disabled.

Users enable morphs by specifying them and initializing the parameter values. Additionally, there are convenience commands to fix and free previously specified morphs. Detailed usage can be found in the documentation of the software.

When multiple morphs are specified they are applied a pre-defined order.
Since we found the optimizer to perform better by applying more complex morphs first, the default order in which enabled morphs are applied is (at the time or writing):
funcxy
,
funcx
,
funcy
,
squeeze
,
scale
,
stretch
,
smear
or
smear-pdf
,
shift
(horizontal and vertical are refined simultaneously), then
shape
. This order may change in the future.

III.0.11
Regression targets

For all morphs, the morphing process uses least squares regression to minimize the difference between the morphed and target signals by varying morphing parameters where
R_{w}
is used as the objective function.

If the morph and target functions are not on the same grid, the two functions are first interpolated (using linear interpolation) onto a common grid before computing
R_{w}
. The interval of this common grid is the intersection of the two original grid intervals (i.e. if the morphed grid is on the interval
[a,b]
and the target grid is on the interval
[c,d]
, then the new grid interval is
[\max\{a,c\},\min\{b,d\}]
). If the spacing (the distance between consecutive grid points) between the two grids is different, then the common grid uses the larger of the two spacings. If a grid has uneven spacing (the distance between consecutive grid points vary), then its spacing is defined as the average distance between consecutive grid points.

diffpy.morph
Software

diffpy.morph
is an open-source Python software package that runs on Windows, MacOS, and Linux operating systems as part of the
diffpy
project

DiffPy team 2025
.
It can be installed from
conda-forge

conda-forge community 2025
using conda (recommended) or from
PyPI

Yang et al. 2025a
using pip.
The source code is available on GitHub at
https://github.com/diffpy/diffpy.morph
.

The
diffpy.morph
program can be used both using a command line interface (e.g. a bash terminal) or in Python scripts directly.

The program takes in two input files: the file containing the function to be morphed and the file containing the target function. Readable files contain the data as a multi-column table, where one column contains the x-array and another column has the function values.
This uses the
loadData()
method in
diffpy.utils

Juhás et al. 2025
. By default when
loadData()
encounters two columns it assumes the first column is the
x
- and the second column is the
y

scale

The program then carries out the morphs described above, returning the optimized morphing parameters. It contains capabilities to plot the morphed function and the reference function with a difference function below, similar to figures in this paper. If requested, the program also returns the morphed function interpolated to the grid of the reference function.

The package also has an API allowing morphs to be called from within Python scripts.

Tutorials for the software and additional code documentation are available in the

documentation at

.

V
Conclusion

We demonstrate morphing as a model-independent tool for comparing 1D spectral data with worked examples that show how useful information may be extracted from diffraction and PDF data.
As
diffpy.morph
uses no model/structural refinement, it is fast, making it ideal for on-the-fly analysis in high-throughput data settings such as a synchrotron experiment.

While initially developed for x-ray PDFs, our examples show that the technique also works for 1D diffraction patterns and neutron diffraction data. Since

diffpy.morph
is model-independent, it can be used on other 1D functions (e.g. density of states, Raman spectra, flattened image data), though it has not been extensively tested beyond powder diffraction patterns and PDFs.

S.2
Derivation of the effects on the PDF of a uniform lattice expansion

To begin, we define the radial distribution function (RDF). For atom
i
, its partial radial distribution
R_{i}(r)
is defined such that
R_{i}(r)dr
gives the number of atoms that are a distance between
r
and
r+dr
away from
i
. The definition of
R_{i}(r)
excludes self-counting, so
R_{i}(0)=0
.

The total RDF,
R(r)
, is the stoichiometric average of the partial RDFs of each atom in the material, or

\displaystyle R(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\#\;atoms}\sum_{i=1}^{\#\;atoms}R_{i}(r).

(S2)

Thus,
\int_{a}^{b}R(r)dr
gives the number of atomic pairs (per atom) with a separation distance between
a
and
b
.

The RDF is related to the PDF
G(r)
by
Farrow and Billinge 2009

\displaystyle G(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{R(r)}{r}-4\pi r\rho_{0}\gamma_{0}(r),

(S3)

where
\rho_{0}
is the atomic number density, and
\gamma_{0}(r)
is the nanoparticle characteristic function (defined in Supplemental Material, Section
S.5
).

When a material expands isotropically with strain
\epsilon
, all distances between pairs of atoms increase by a factor of
1+\epsilon>0
(compression occurs for
-1<\epsilon<0
). Thus, the number of atomic pairs with separation distances between
a
and
b
before expansion occurs equals the number of atomic pairs with separation distances between
(1+\epsilon)a
and
(1+\epsilon)b
after. The total number of atoms in the material is unchanged by the expansion. Defining
R(r)
to be the RDF pre-expansion and
R^{\prime}(r)
to be that post-expansion,

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}R(r)dr

\displaystyle=\int_{(1+\epsilon)a}^{(1+\epsilon)b}R^{\prime}(r)dr.

(S4)

Applying a change of variables
r\mapsto r/(1+\epsilon)
to the left hand side of Eq.
S4
gives

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}R(r)dr

\displaystyle=\int_{(1+\epsilon)a}^{(1+\epsilon)b}\frac{R(r/(1+\epsilon))}{1+\epsilon}dr.

(S5)

Since the relations in Eq.
S4
and Eq.
S5
hold for all
0\leq a\leq b
,

\displaystyle R^{\prime}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{R(r/(1+\epsilon))}{1+\epsilon}

(S6)

for all
r\geq 0
.

The corresponding pre-expansion PDF is

\displaystyle G(r)=\frac{R(r)}{r}-4\pi r\rho_{0}\gamma_{0}(r),

(S7)

and the post-expansion PDF is

\displaystyle G^{\prime}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{R^{\prime}(r)}{r}-4\pi r\rho_{0}\gamma_{0}(r)

(S8)

\displaystyle=\frac{R(r/(1+\epsilon))}{(1+\epsilon)r}-4\pi r\rho^{\prime}_{0}\gamma^{\prime}_{0}(r).

(S9)

The
\rho_{0},\gamma_{0}(r)
are the pre-expansion atomic density and nanoparticle form function, and
\rho^{\prime}_{0},\gamma^{\prime}_{0}(r)
the same quantities post-expansion. Due to the expansion, the volume of the material increases by
(1+\epsilon)^{3}
, while the total number of atoms remains constant, so

\displaystyle\rho^{\prime}_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1+\epsilon)^{3}}\rho_{0}.

(S10)

The post-expansion nanoparticle characteristic function scales as

\displaystyle\gamma^{\prime}_{0}(r)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{0}\left(\frac{r}{1+\epsilon}\right).

(S11)

(See Supplemental Material, Section
S.5
for proof.)

Finally, we conclude that the PDF after expansion is

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;G^{\prime}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{R(r/(1+\epsilon))}{(1+\epsilon)r}-4\pi r\frac{\rho_{0}\gamma_{0}(r/(1+\epsilon))}{(1+\epsilon)^{3}}

(S12)

\displaystyle=\frac{G(r/(1+\epsilon))}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}.

(S13)

Therefore, applying a
stretch
parameter
\eta=\epsilon
and a
scale
parameter
s=(1+\epsilon)^{-2}
to
G(r)
models isotropic expansion with strain
\epsilon
.

It is important to note that an isotropic expansion (with strain
\epsilon)
generally denotes that time-averaged atomic distances are multiplied by a factor
1+\epsilon
. These time-averaged atomic distances generally correspond to peaks in the PDF. Therefore, we can still conclude from our derivation of Eq.
S13
that peaks in
G^{\prime}(r)
are located at the same
r
-positions as peaks in
G(r/(1+\epsilon))
.

We note that this treatment strictly holds only for a uniform expansion. This is unlikely to be a good approximation for structures with highly anisotropic structures or when there exist hierarchical bonding strengths; for example, molecular solids where a large lattice expansion will not be reflected in large changes in intra-molecular PDF.

It is not necessarily the case, however, that the relation Eq.
S13
holds for all parts of the PDF. Since atoms are not stationary, the RDF is a time-dependent function
R(r,t)
. Thus, so too is the PDF
G(r,t)
. The PDF
G(r)
we obtain from a material experimentally can be thought of as the time-averaged PDF of that material over the data collection period. The conditions causing a uniform lattice expansion (e.g. increasing temperature) can also change how atomic positions are distributed over time and affect the PDF in a manner distinct from Eq.
S13
. Other morphs are required to account for these changes. (For example, in Section
S.4
, we show how the smearing morph is used to correct for one such effect.)

With these caveats said, in our experience, the stretch morph can still be a useful approximate first step even when the conditions discussed above are not perfectly met.

S.3
Uniform Lattice Expansion Effects on Reciprocal Space Functions

We showed above how a uniform lattice expansion with strain
\epsilon
stretches the grid of the PDF by a factor
1+\epsilon
. Below we show that the same expansion instead stretches the reciprocal space (
Q
-space) function grid by a factor
(1+\epsilon)^{-1}
.

In particular, we derive the relations Eqs.
S17
,
S20
,
S58
, and
S56
in order to show that isotropic expansion of a material by strain
\epsilon
stretches the grid of the reciprocal space functions
I(Q)
,
S(Q)
, and
F(Q)
by
(1+\epsilon)^{-1}
.

While the change in
S(Q)
,
F(Q)
, and 1D neutron scattering intensities due to lattice expansion can be fully explained by the aforementioned grid stretch, this is not strictly the case for the 1D x-ray and electron scattering intensities. However, we have observed that in practice, for small
|\epsilon|<0.01
, it is still a very accurate approximation with little error. This error, we will show, grows linearly with
|\epsilon|
.

S.3.1
Effect on scattering intensities,
I(Q)

In a system with
N
atoms, the full 1D coherent scattering intensity function (of an isotropic sample) is given by by
Farrow and Billinge 2009

\displaystyle I_{c}(Q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}b_{j}^{*}b_{i}\frac{\sin(Qr_{ij})}{Qr_{ij}},

(S14)

where
r_{ij}
is the distance between atoms
i
and
j
. Removing the self-scattering gives the 1D discrete scattering intensity

\displaystyle I_{d}(Q)

\displaystyle=I_{c}(Q)-\sum_{i=1}^{N}b_{j}^{*}b_{i}.

(S15)

In the case of neutron scattering, the
b_{i}
denote the coherent neutron scattering lengths that are independent of
Q
. Therefore, upon an isotropic expansion with strain
\epsilon
, the interatomic distances
r_{ij}
become
(1+\epsilon)r_{ij}
. The coherent scattering intensity post-
\epsilon
-strain is then

\displaystyle I_{c,\;n^{0}}^{\epsilon}(Q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}b_{j}^{*}b_{i}\frac{\sin(Q(1+\epsilon)r_{ij})}{Q(1+\epsilon)r_{ij}}

(S16)

\displaystyle=I_{c,\;n^{0}}((1+\epsilon)Q),

(S17)

Thus, if a
stretch
morph is applied on

S.3.2
Analytically bounding the
I(Q)
error

In Table
S1
, it can be seen that the error (the
R_{w}
s) from applying Eq.
S17
seems to decrease linearly with
|\epsilon|
. This can be shown analytically.

We start by quantifying the error at each
Q
as

\displaystyle\text{err}(Q)

\displaystyle\equiv\mathinner{\!\left\lvert I_{c,\;strain,\;xray}(Q;\epsilon)-I_{c}((1+\epsilon)Q)\right\rvert}.

(S30)

We will show for x-ray scattering (within a
Q
-range of
0~\text{\AA}\leq Q\leq 25~\text{\AA}
),

\displaystyle\text{err}(Q)

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathcal{K}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\epsilon\right\rvert}

(S31)

for some constant
\mathcal{K}
independent of
Q
.
In words, we will show that the error is (approximately) linearly upper-bounded by the magnitude of the strain.

By applying Eqs.
S14
,
S28
, we see that the error in Eq.
S30
is

\displaystyle\text{err}(Q)

\displaystyle=\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\left(2\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{4}C_{i,j,k}h(Q;B_{j,k},\epsilon)+\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{4}A_{i,j,k,\ell}h(Q;B_{i,j,k,\ell},\epsilon)\right)\frac{\sin((1+\epsilon)Qr_{i,j})}{(1+\epsilon)Qr_{i,j}}\right\rvert}

(S32)

where

\displaystyle h(Q;B,\epsilon)

\displaystyle=e^{-BQ^{2}}-e^{-B((1+\epsilon)Q)^{2}},

(S33)

\displaystyle A_{i,j,k,\ell}

\displaystyle=a_{ik}a_{j\ell},

(S34)

\displaystyle B_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\frac{b_{i,j}}{16\pi^{2}},

(S35)

\displaystyle B_{i,j,k,\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{b_{i,k}+b_{j,\ell}}{16\pi^{2}},

(S36)

\displaystyle C_{i,j,k}

\displaystyle=c_{i}a_{jk}.

(S37)

For
\epsilon>0
,
0<h(Q;B,\epsilon)
, with maximum attained at

\displaystyle Q^{*}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2\ln(1+\epsilon)}{(2\epsilon+\epsilon^{2})B}}.

(S38)

For
\epsilon<0
,
h(Q;B,\epsilon)<0
, with minimum attained at
Q^{*}
. For both cases,

\displaystyle h(Q^{*};B,\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-\frac{2\ln(1+\epsilon)}{2\epsilon+\epsilon^{2}}\right\}\left(\frac{2\epsilon+\epsilon^{2}}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}}\right)

(S39)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{2\epsilon}{e},

(S40)

where this approximation holds for small
\epsilon<1
. For
\epsilon=0.01
, the approximation holds up to a relative error of
0.5\%
.
Then, as
|\text{sinc}(x)|\leq 1
, we can very loosely upper-bound the error

\displaystyle\text{err}(Q)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{4}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert C_{i,j,k}h(Q;B_{j,k},\epsilon)\right\rvert}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{4}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert A_{i,j,k,\ell}h(Q;B_{i,j,k,\ell},\epsilon)\right\rvert}

(S41)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{2|\epsilon|}{e}\left(2\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{4}|C_{i,j,k}|+\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{4}|A_{i,j,k,\ell}|\right).

(S42)

As such, we have demonstrated our desired relation Eq.
S31
.

A similar derivation can be done to show the error from applying Eq.
S20
,

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lvert I_{d,e,xray}(Q)-I_{d}((1+\epsilon)Q)\right\rvert},

(S43)

is also (approximately) linear in
|\epsilon|
.

S.3.3
Effect on scattering structure functions
S(Q)
and
F(Q)

In the process of computing the PDF
G(r)
from
I(Q)
, we compute two intermediate reciprocal-space functions
S(Q)
and
F(Q)
. We show below how these functions are defined and how an isotropic expansion with strain
\epsilon
also stretches their grid by
(1+\epsilon)^{-1}
. The equations below use the
Q
-independent coherent neutron scattering length
b_{i}
, but all derivations follow equivalently if one chooses to use the
Q
-dependent form factors
f_{i}(Q)
instead.

The total scattering structure function,
S(Q)
, is the discrete scattering intensity normalized by the average scattering factor
Farrow and Billinge 2009
:

\displaystyle S(Q)

\displaystyle=\frac{NI_{d}(Q)}{\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}b_{j}^{*}b_{i}}+1.

(S44)

For a finite number of scatterers,
N,I_{d}(Q)
are finite and the scattering intensities are non-zero, ensuring
S(Q)
is finite as long as the denominator of Eq.
S44
is non-zero. We will assume this is the case, as only very rare combinations of materials will cause the scattering length products to perfectly cancel.

The reduced total scattering structure function
F(Q)
is then obtained from
S(Q)
through

\displaystyle F(Q)

\displaystyle=Q(S(Q)-1).

(S45)

Since
S(Q)
and thus
S(0)
is finite,
F(0)=0
.

We finally obtain the PDF
G(r)
through the relation
Farrow and Billinge 2009

\displaystyle G(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}F(Q)\sin(Qr)dQ.

(S46)

To be pedantic, since we include the small angle scattering
Q
-values in the integral above, the result obtained is actually

\displaystyle\frac{R(r)}{r}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}F(Q)\sin(Qr)dQ,

(S47)

where
R(r)
is the radial distribution function explored in Section
S.2
. Our derivations below will use the more commonly used (albeit inaccurate) expression Eq.
S46
, but all results follow equivalently by substituting Eq.
S46
with Eq.
S47
and Eq.
S48
with Eq.
S6
.

Now, let us consider a PDF
G(r)
of a material and PDF
G_{e}(r)
of the same material having undergone uniform expansion with strain
\epsilon
.

\displaystyle G_{e}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{G(r/(1+\epsilon))}{(1+\epsilon)^{2}},

(S48)

Let
S(Q),F(Q)
be the functions used in the computation of
G(r)
and
S_{e}(Q),F_{e}(Q)
used in computing
G_{e}(r)
. To avoid dealing with
Q
-dependent form factors, we will work backward to obtain expressions for the functions post-uniform-expansion in terms of their original counterparts.

Combining Eq.
S46
and Eq.
S48
gives

\displaystyle G_{e}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{F(Q)}{1+\epsilon}\sin\left(\frac{Qr}{1+\epsilon}\right)\frac{dQ}{1+\epsilon}

(S49)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{F((1+\epsilon)Q^{\prime})}{1+\epsilon}\sin(Q^{\prime}r)dQ^{\prime},

(S50)

where we have applied a change of variables
Q^{\prime}=Q/(1+\epsilon)
on the last step.
From Eq.
S46
,

\displaystyle G_{e}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}F_{e}(Q)\sin(Qr)dQ,

(S51)

and subtracting Eq.
S50
gives

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(F_{e}(Q)-\frac{F((1+\epsilon)Q)}{1+\epsilon}\right)\sin(Qr)dQ.

(S52)

Since
F_{e}(0)=0
and
F(0)=0
,

\displaystyle F_{e}(0)-\frac{F(0)}{1+\epsilon}

\displaystyle=0,

(S53)

so by the inverse sine Fourier transform of an odd function
Boas 2006
,

\displaystyle F_{e}(Q)-\frac{F((1+\epsilon)Q)}{1+\epsilon}

\displaystyle\propto\int_{0}^{\infty}0\sin(Qr)dr

(S54)

\displaystyle=0.

(S55)

Therefore

\displaystyle F_{e}(Q)

\displaystyle=\frac{F((1+\epsilon)Q)}{1+\epsilon}.

(S56)

Then, from Eq.
S45
,

\displaystyle Q(S_{e}(Q)-1)

\displaystyle=Q(S((1+\epsilon)Q)-1).

(S57)

This means for all
Q>0
,

\displaystyle S_{e}(Q)

\displaystyle=S((1+\epsilon)Q).

(S58)

For continuous
S_{e}(Q),S(Q)
, this means
S_{e}(0)=S(0)
as well (and thus Eq.
S58
also holds at
Q=0
).

S.4
Modeling Thermal Smearing on the PDF

Peaks in the radial distribution function (see Supplemental Material, Section
S.2
for definition) obtained from measured PDFs have approximately Gaussian shapes due to Debye-Waller effects. The variance
\sigma_{p}^{2}
of each peak
p
is given by the atomic displacement parameters (ADP) of the atoms contributing to
p
.
The ADP can depend on dynamic (temperature-dependent) and static factors
Dinnebier and Billinge 2018
. Models, such as the Debye model, generally separate the two. Per this model, the mean square ADP, denoted
\overline{u^{2}}
, is given by

\displaystyle\overline{u^{2}}

\displaystyle=A_{i}(T)+A_{i,static},

(S59)

where
A_{i}(T)
increases with temperature. Specifically, the Debye model treats the temperature-dependent component as
Dinnebier and Billinge 2018

\displaystyle A_{i}(T)

\displaystyle=\frac{3h^{2}T^{2}}{4\pi^{2}M_{i}k_{B}\Theta_{D}^{3}}\int_{0}^{\Theta_{D}/T}\frac{x}{e^{x}-1}dx

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\frac{3h^{2}}{16\pi^{2}M_{i}k_{B}\Theta_{D}}

(S60)

where
M
is the mass of the atom
i
,
\Theta_{D}
is the Debye temperature of the (crystal) material,
h
is Planck’s constant, and
k_{B}
is Boltzmann’s constant.
Since

\displaystyle f(T)=T^{2}\int_{0}^{\Theta_{D}/T}\frac{x}{e^{x}-1}dx

(S61)

is a strictly increasing function of
T\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
for
\Theta_{D}>0
, increasing
T
causes
A_{i}(T)
to increase by some positive quantity
\sigma^{2}_{i}>0
. Increases in the ADP translate to broadening of each Guassian peak in the RDF.

Motivated by the Debye model, the
smear
morph broadens all Gaussian peaks in a radial distribution function by an increased variance of
\sigma^{2}
. As
\sigma^{2}\geq 0
, only thermal broadening due to increases in temperature can be captured, so it recommended to use
diffpy.morph
to morph a lower-temperature sample to match a target higher-temperature sample.

It is of note that the Debye model indicates that the temperature-dependent component of the ADP depends on the mass of the atom (see Eq.
S.4
). As such, increasing temperature will broaden each peak in the RDF differently.
diffpy.morph
only captures the “average” broadening, but still works well to reduce features in the difference due to thermal broadening. A user can also tell
diffpy.morph
to evaluate the smearing on an
r
interval capturing a single peak of interest.

The exact operation done by
diffpy.morph
is given in Eq.
14
. Below, we prove that this broadens all peaks in
R(r)
by an additional variance
\sigma^{2}
.

Assume our radial distribution function can be decomposed into a sum of Gaussians

\displaystyle R(r)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}I_{k}N(r;\mu_{k},\sigma_{k}^{2}),

(S62)

where each
I_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
is the amplitude multiplied to a normalized Gaussian
N(r;\mu_{k},\sigma_{k}^{2})
following

\displaystyle N(r;\mu_{k},\sigma_{k}^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{k}^{2}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{r-\mu_{k}}{\sigma_{k}}\right)^{2}\right\}

(S63)

with mean
\mu_{k}
and variance
\sigma_{k}^{2}
. Convolving the Gaussian broadening factor
T_{\sigma}(r)
from Eq.
13
with a single Gaussian
N_{k}(r)\equiv N(r;\mu_{k},\sigma_{k}^{2})
gives

\displaystyle(N_{k}\circledast T_{\sigma})(r)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}N_{k}(r^{\prime})\cdot T_{\sigma}(r-r^{\prime})dr^{\prime}

(S64)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{k}^{2}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{r^{\prime}-\mu_{k}}{\sigma_{k}}\right)^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\times\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{(r-r^{\prime})-r_{c}}{\sigma}\right)^{2}\right\}dr^{\prime}

(S65)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{k}^{2}}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left\{-\frac{f(r^{\prime})}{2\beta}+\alpha\right\}dr^{\prime},

(S66)

where we have defined

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{(r-(\mu_{k}+r_{c}))^{2}}{2(\sigma_{k}^{2}+\sigma^{2})},

(S67)

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\sigma^{2}\sigma_{k}^{2}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}+\sigma^{2}},

(S68)

\displaystyle f(r^{\prime})

\displaystyle\equiv\left(r^{\prime}-\frac{\sigma^{2}\mu_{k}+\sigma^{2}_{k}(r_{c}-r)}{\sigma_{k}^{2}+\sigma^{2}}\right)^{2}.

(S69)

Some rearrangement lets us evaluate the integral

\displaystyle(N_{k}\circledast T_{\sigma})(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\left\{-\alpha\right\}}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{k}^{2}}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left\{-\frac{f(r^{\prime})}{2\beta}\right\}dr^{\prime}

(S70)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\frac{(r-(\mu_{k}+r_{c}))^{2}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}+\sigma^{2}}\right\}

(S71)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}N(r;\mu_{k}+r_{c},\sigma_{k}^{2}+\sigma^{2}).

(S72)

As the convolution operator is linear, convolving
R(r)
from Eq.
S62
with
T_{\sigma}(r)
gives

\displaystyle(R\circledast T_{\sigma})(r)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}I_{k}\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}N(r;\mu_{k}+r_{c},\sigma_{k}^{2}+\sigma^{2}).

(S73)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{(R\circledast T_{\sigma})(r+r_{c})}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}I_{k}N(r;\mu_{k},\sigma_{k}^{2}+\sigma^{2}),

(S74)

which is
R(r)
, but with each Gaussian peak broadened by an additional variance
\sigma^{2}
.

S.5
Nanoparticle Characteristic Function

The shape of a nanoparticle plays a role in shaping the PDF through the nanoparticle characteristic function
\gamma_{0}(r)
(see Eq.
17
). In this section, we detail how the form factor is computed and prove the equality in Eq.
S11
.

To begin, define the shape function
s(\mathbf{r})
, which takes value
1
for
\mathbf{r}
within the nanoparticle and
0
for
\mathbf{r}
outside. The unaveraged particle form factor
\gamma_{0}(\mathbf{r})
is the autocorrelation of this shape function averaged over the nanoparticle volume
V
, computed as
Farrow and Billinge 2009

\displaystyle\gamma_{0}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V}\iiint s(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})s(\mathbf{r}^{\prime}+\mathbf{r})d\mathbf{r}^{\prime}.

(S75)

We refer to the angle-averaged version of this quantity, denoted
\gamma_{0}(r)
, as the nanoparticle characteristic function, which is given by
Farrow and Billinge 2009

\displaystyle\gamma_{0}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{\int d\phi\int d\theta\sin(\theta)r^{2}\gamma_{0}(\mathbf{r})}{\int d\phi\int d\theta r^{2}\sin(\theta)}.

(S76)

There is an important relationship between the nanoparticle characteristic function of a material before and after uniform expansion, used in Supplemental Material, Section
S.2
. Given a material has undergone isotropic lattice expansion with thermal strain
-1<\epsilon
, the new form factor
\gamma^{\prime}_{0}(r)
is a stretched version of the pre-expansion form factor:
\gamma^{\prime}_{0}(r)=\gamma_{0}(r/(1+\epsilon))
. To show this, we first note that expansion maps points
\mathbf{r}
in the original material to
\mathbf{r}^{\prime}=(1+\epsilon)\mathbf{r}
. Thus, the shape function post-expansion is related to that pre-expansion by

\displaystyle s^{\prime}((1+\epsilon)\mathbf{r})=s(\mathbf{r}).

(S77)

The volume of the nanoparticle is increased by a scale factor
(1+\epsilon)^{3}
due to expansion. Following Eq.
S75
, the post-expansion nanoparticle characteristic function follows

\displaystyle\gamma^{\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\kappa^{3}V}\iiint s^{\prime}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})s^{\prime}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime}+\mathbf{r})d\mathbf{r}^{\prime},

(S78)

where

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle\equiv 1+\epsilon

(S79)

is defined to condense the following equations. Applying a change of variables
\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\mapsto\mathbf{r}^{\prime}/\kappa
gives:

\displaystyle\gamma^{\prime}_{0}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\kappa^{3}V}\iiint s^{\prime}(\kappa\mathbf{r}^{\prime})s^{\prime}(\kappa\mathbf{r}^{\prime}+\mathbf{r})\kappa^{3}d\mathbf{r}^{\prime}

(S80)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V}\iiint s(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})s(\mathbf{r}^{\prime}+\mathbf{r}/\kappa)d\mathbf{r}^{\prime}

(S81)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{0}(\mathbf{r}/\kappa).

(S82)

Finally, taking the angle-averaged integrals from Eq.
S76
gives the desired relation in Eq.
S11
.

S.6
A theoretical motivation for the polynomial grid morph

A common setup for a diffraction experiment uses a flat detector and a (x-ray/neutron/electron) source beam perpendicular to the detector. Scattered rays travel off the sample to the detector some distance away. Let’s say that we measured a sample-to-detector distance of
D^{\prime}
distinct from the actual detector distance
D
. We will show that when the relative error is small (
|D^{\prime}-D|/D<0.1
), this results in a polynomial change to the grid of the measured 1D scattering intensity
I(Q)
. This motivates the morphs in Section
III.0.8
.

For elastic scattering in the setup above, a point on the detector that is a distance
d
from the detector center corresponds to a
Q
of
Dinnebier and Billinge 2008
;
Egami and Billinge 2012

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=\frac{4\pi\sin\left(\frac{1}{2}\arctan(d/D)\right)}{\lambda},

(S83)

but our computed
Q^{\prime}
using the incorrect
D^{\prime}
is

\displaystyle Q^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{4\pi\sin\left(\frac{1}{2}\arctan(d/D^{\prime})\right)}{\lambda}.

(S84)

There is no simple polynomial relation between
Q
and
Q^{\prime}
.

However, letting

\displaystyle\epsilon_{D}

\displaystyle=\frac{D^{\prime}-D}{D}

(S85)

be small (
|\epsilon_{D}|<0.1
), we can Taylor approximate to first order

\displaystyle Q^{\prime}

\displaystyle\approx Q-\frac{4\pi}{\lambda}\frac{(d/D)\cos\left(\frac{1}{2}\arctan(d/D)\right)}{2((d/D)^{2}+1)}\epsilon_{D}.

(S86)

Then, by using

\displaystyle\frac{d}{D}

\displaystyle=\tan\left(2\arcsin\left(\frac{\lambda}{4\pi}Q\right)\right)

(S87)

and some trigonometric identities (further below in this section), this simplifies to a polynomial action on
Q
per

\displaystyle Q^{\prime}

\displaystyle\approx Q-\epsilon_{D}\left(2\left(\frac{\lambda}{4\pi}\right)^{4}Q^{5}-3\left(\frac{\lambda}{4\pi}\right)^{2}Q^{3}+Q\right).

(S88)

For
|\epsilon_{D}|<0.1
, the relative error of the approximation is
\sim 1\%
for small
Q
, and this error decays to
0
for larger
Q
.
For
-40/41<\epsilon_{D}<1/2
,
Q^{\prime}
in Eq.
S88
is a monotonically increasing function of
Q
on the observable range
0\leq Q\leq 4\pi/\lambda
.

If we assume
Q<<\lambda/4\pi
, we can further approximate

\displaystyle Q^{\prime}

\displaystyle\approx(1-\epsilon_{D})Q.

(S89)

This shows that the
stretch
morph can account for this misalignment in low
Q
using
stretch
parameter
\eta=-\epsilon_{D}
.

Here at the end, we detail the full derivation of the polynomial approximation in Eq.
S88
from the Taylor approximation. For convenience, define

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\lambda}{4\pi}Q.

(S90)

We start by using the double angle formula on Eq.
S87
to get

\displaystyle\frac{d}{D}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\tan(\arcsin(q))}{1-\tan^{2}(\arcsin(q))}

(S91)

\displaystyle=\frac{2q\sqrt{1-q^{2}}}{1-2q^{2}},

(S92)

where we have used

\displaystyle\tan(\arcsin(x))

\displaystyle=\frac{x}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}.

(S93)

Then, we use the half angle formula on Eq.
S86
to get

\displaystyle\cos\left(\frac{1}{2}\arctan(d/D)\right)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1+\cos(\arctan(d/D))}{2}}

(S94)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+(d/D)^{2}}}}{2}},

(S95)

where we have used

\displaystyle\cos(\arctan(x))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}}.

(S96)

Plugging in Eq.
S92
into Eq.
S95
and simplifying gives

\displaystyle\cos\left(\frac{1}{2}\arctan(d/D)\right)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-q^{2}}.

(S97)

We can now use Eqs.
S92
,
S97
to obtain a polynomial relation

\displaystyle\frac{(d/D)\cos\left(\frac{1}{2}\arctan(d/D)\right)}{2((d/D)^{2}+1)}

\displaystyle=q(1-q^{2})(1-2q^{2}).

(S98)

Applying this to Eq.
S86
gives the desired Eq.
S88
.
