Interactions between the gut microbiome and DNA methylation patterns in blood and visceral adipose tissue in subjects with different metabolic characteristics
Authors: José Ignacio Martínez-Montoro, Gracia M Martín-Núñez, Andrés González-Jiménez, Lourdes Garrido-Sánchez, Isabel Moreno-Indias, Francisco J Tinahones
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe gut microbiome has been reported to induce epigenetic modifications in the host, which may be involved in the pathophysiology of metabolic diseases.ObjectiveTo evaluate the potential interactions between the gut microbiome and DNA methylome in subjects with different metabolic characteristics.MethodsSixty-four participants with different metabolic characteristics (i.e., participants without obesity -healthy controls-, and participants with obesity and normal insulin sensitivity/insulin resistance/ type 2 diabetes-T2DM-) were included in this study. A machine learning approach was performed in order to identify distinctive patterns in three omics (gut microbiome, blood DNA methylome, and visceral adipose tissue-VAT- DNA methylome) according to the different study groups.ResultsRobust distinctive distribution patterns of the three different omics were found between healthy controls and patients with obesity; participants with and without T2DM, and patients with obesity with and without insulin resistance. Importantly, strong correlations between the gut microbiome (including Odoribacteriaceae and Christensenllaceae families) and both blood and VAT DNA methylome were found. Moreover, in the entire study population, three main bacterial genera (Sutterella, Collinsella and Eubacterium) were related to the epigenetic regulation of different genes involved in distinct processes related to cellular metabolism and metabolic diseases, including small ubiquitin-related modifier (SUMO) transferase activity or lipid binding.ConclusionWe show that distinctive interactions between the gut microbiome and DNA methylome may occur in subjects with different metabolic characteristics. Further research is needed to elucidate the potential role of these interactions in the pathophysiology of obesity and related comorbidities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-024-05746-y.Keywords: Gut microbiota, Epigenetics, DNA methylation, Visceral adipose tissue, Obesity, Insulin resistance, Type 2 diabetes

Background

Obesity is a chronic disease with major repercussions on health, including mental disorders, musculoskeletal complications, cardiovascular disease, and different types of cancer [1]. It is also noteworthy that several metabolic disorders are closely related to obesity. Indeed, comorbidities linked to metabolic syndrome, such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, and insulin resistance/ type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) often coexist in the context of this disease [1].

In the last decades, the global prevalence of obesity has dramatically risen, and important health and socioeconomic consequences derived from this pandemic have emerged [2]. Despite the fact that several factors, such as unhealthy dietary habits and a sedentary lifestyle, have been identified as major determinants in the development of this condition, it is important to note that obesity is a complex multifactorial disease, and additional mechanisms may be involved the pathophysiology of obesity and associated metabolic complications [3]. In this regard, novel actors, such as the gut microbiome and the epigenome, have been postulated as potential key contributors in the development of obesity and related comorbidities, including T2DM [4, 5].

Interestingly, recent research has shown that the gut microbiome is able to induce epigenetic modifications in the host [6, 7]. Accordingly, it has been suggested that the gut microbiome can modify the epigenome via the synthesis of different metabolites that may serve as epigenetic substrates or regulators of enzymatic processes involved in epigenetic changes. Moreover, the gut microbiome has been demonstrated to directly influence DNA methylation [8], an epigenetic mechanism that consist of the covalent addition of a methyl group to cytosine by DNA methyltransferases [9]. Of note, the regulation of DNA methylation by the gut microbiota has been proposed to be implicated in the pathogenesis of several diseases [10, 11]. However, little is known about the potential relationship between gut microbiota composition and DNA methylation in individuals with obesity and/or other metabolic disturbances, such as T2DM.

Previously, in a genome-wide analysis of DNA methylation, we showed a different methylome pattern in subjects with obesity and a high Bacteroides-to-Firmicutes ratio (BFR) compared to those presenting a low BFR [12]. In line with this, Kumar et al., reported an association between gut microbiota composition and DNA methylation status of genes related to obesity in 8 pregnant women with different predominant microbiota [13]. Nevertheless, the hypothetical presence of distinctive gut microbiome-mediated DNA methylation patterns according to the metabolic status remains unexplored.

Hence, in this study, our aim was to analyze the potential interactions between the gut microbiome and the DNA methylome (measured in blood and visceral adipose tissue -VAT-) in subjects with different metabolic characteristics, including healthy controls without obesity and participants with obesity and distinct coexisting conditions (i.e., normal insulin sensitivity, insulin resistance and T2DM), using a machine learning approach to explore the presence of distinctive patterns among the different phenotypes, and the relationships between specific gut microbes and DNA methylation status.

Methods

Study population

From January 2020 to January 2021, 64 participants undergoing abdominal surgery at Virgen de la Victoria University Hospital (Málaga, Spain) were evaluated. Exclusion criteria comprised the presence of established cardiovascular disease, arthritis, acute inflammatory diseases, active cancer, infectious diseases or use of antibiotics/probiotics/ prebiotics 3 months prior to inclusion. All subjects were Caucasian.

The study design determined that, at least, 14 patients were necessary in each subgroup of study. After the performance of gut microbiota and epigenetic analyses of VAT and blood samples, no valid results for at least one of the main performed analyses were obtained from 3 participants, which resulted in the final inclusion of 61 participants. Therefore, 45 patients with severe obesity were included: 15 with low insulin resistance [homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance index (HOMA-IR) < 4.7] and 14 with high insulin resistance (HOMA-IR > 8) (this cut-off point was established by the mean ± 2SD of a healthy control population) [14], as well as 16 patients with T2DM. Participants with severe obesity underwent bariatric surgery. We also included 16 control subjects without obesity, and no alterations in glucose metabolism who underwent laparoscopic cholecystectomy.

Biological samples, biochemical determinations, and anthropometric measurements

Blood, VAT, and fecal samples were obtained from each participant. Blood samples were collected after an overnight fast of 12 hours from the antecubital vein and placed in EDTA treated vacutainer tubes (BD vacutainer, London, UK). Samples were processed and immediately frozen after their reception at Virgen de la Victoria University Hospital or Regional University Hospital of Málaga (Andalusian Public Health System Biobank). The serum was separated by centrifugation for 15 min at 4000 rpm and buffy coat was immediately frozen at −80 ºC until analysis. Serum was used for the determination of glucose, cholesterol, triglycerides, and high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol in a Dimension autoanalyzer (Dade Behring Inc., Deerfield, IL) by enzymatic methods (Randox Laboratories Ltd., UK). Low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol was calculated from the Friedewald equation [15]. Serum insulin was measured by immunoassay (ADVIA Centaur autoanalyzer, Siemens Healthcare Diagnostics). HOMA-IR was calculated from fasting insulin and glucose with the following equation: fasting insulin (mIU/mL) x fasting glucose (mmol/L)/22.5 [16]. VAT samples were obtained during surgery, were washed in a physiological saline solution, and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. These samples were stored at −80 ºC until analysis. Fecal samples were collected before surgery in sterile hermetic flasks and immediately stored at −80 ºC until analysis.

Weight, height, waist circumference, and blood pressure were measured by standard procedures. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters.

Gut microbiota analysis

DNA was extracted from 200 mg of stool samples with the QIAamp DNA stool Mini kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. A Nanodrop spectrophotometer (Nanodrop Technologies, Wilmington, DE, USA) was used for the measurement of the DNA concentration at 260 nm, and the A260/A280 ratio for purity verification.

Ribosomal 16S rRNA gene sequences were amplified from DNA using the Ion 16S Metagenomics Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Italy). The kit comprises two primer sets that amplify different hypervariable regions of the 16S region in bacteria: primer set V2–4–8 and primer set V3–6, 7–9. Libraries were created using the Ion Plus Fragment Library Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Each sample was marked by barcodes with the Ion Xpress Barcode Adapters kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Emulsion PCR and sequencing of the amplicon libraries were performed on an Ion 530 chip (Ion 530TM Chip Kit) using the Ion Torrent S5TM system and the Ion 510/520TM/530TM Kit-Chef (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After sequencing, the individual sequence reads were filtered using Ion Reporter Software V4.0 to remove low-quality and polyclonal sequences.

Quantitative Insights into Microbial Ecology (QIIME) 1.9.1 software was used to analyze the quality sequences. Briefly, operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were calculated by clustering sequences with a similarity of 97% with a closed-reference OTU picking approach. The representative sequences were submitted to the UCLUST algorithm to assign the taxonomy and the relative abundance of each OTU was determined using the Greengenes 16S rRNA gene database. Raw data can be found in the SRA database public repository from NCBI within the BioProject accession number PRJNA539905.

DNA methylation profiling

DNA was extracted from blood (EDTA buffy coat) and VAT using Zymo ZR 96 Quick gDNA kit (Zymo Research Corp., Irvine, CA, USA) following manufacturer’s instructions. After quantification and purity assessment, a total of 500 ng of genomic DNA was bisulfite treated using the ZymoResearch Infinitum HD FFPE Methylation kit (Zymo Research Corp, Irvine, CA, USA) and was purified by DNAClean-Up kit (Zymo Research Corp, Irvine, CA, USA). Over 850,000 methylation sites were interrogated with the Infinium Methylation EPIC Bead Chip Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) following the Infinium HD Assay Methylation protocol, and raw data (idat files) were obtained from iScan (Illumina) software. Raw data files (idat files) were processed to derive beta values after background correction and normalization by BMIQ [17]. Raw data can be found in the GEO database public repository from NCBI within the accession number GSE131461.

Data integration

Each dataset was pre-processed to filter outlier values. OTUs without data in more than 20% of patients were excluded; therefore, the total number of families was reduced from 182 to 32 in the final step.

Prior to integration, data were transformed. Specifically, blood and adipose tissue data were log-transformed, while microbiome data were transformed using centered log-ratios (CLR). Data were merged, and patients without complete information were filtered using the R environment RStudio build 492 and the R language 3.5.2.

Supervised multivariate models were developed using supervised sparse partial least squares discriminant analysis (sPLS-DA) with the mixOmics R package via the DIABLO framework [18]. Cross-validation (10 × fivefold) was used to determine the optimal model hyperparameters (number of components, number of features per component), as well as to provide an estimate of the ability of the model to generalize to new data. To construct the model, principal component analysis (PCA) techniques elaborate a covariance matrix with all possible pairs of the initial variables. Using these variances, the PCA construct each component as linear combinations of the initial variables in such manner that the first principal component accounts for the largest possible variance in the data set. The rest of components are calculated in the same way, with the condition that they are uncorrelated with the previous components and that they account for the next highest variance.

The plotIndiv() function was used to project the samples onto the PCA and sPLS components. The plotloading() function was used to visualize the loading vector of the sPLS analysis and represent the contribution of the variables kept for each component. The circosPlot() function was used to show the connections between selected features of different dataset (ρ < − 0.7 and ρ > 0.7, Spearman's correlation).

Then, we performed a functional enrichment analysis of data to obtain information about the pathways and mechanisms linked to the different expression between the groups. Functional enrichment was elaborated using three different sources, information about molecular function, biological process and cellular component of each gene were collected from Gene Ontology database. Information related to biological pathways were obtained from Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG). Both analyses were performed through clusterProfiler package of R Language.

Clustered image maps: discrimination of healthy controls and participants with obesity

A sPLS-DA study was performed in order to identify robust common distribution patterns of the different omics included in this study. Figure 2 displays the clustering heatmap of expression of different blood and VAT methylation patterns, along with the distinct OTUs (columns), and the study participants classified by their metabolic status (rows). Remarkably, patterns selected by component 1 of the PCA showed important differences between healthy controls and subjects with obesity. Accordingly, the most discriminative OTU families and DNA methylated regions were identified. Thus, a higher relative abundance of Odoribacteriaceae, Rikenellaceae and Christensenllaceae families was observed in the control group. Moreover, together with the aforementioned distinctive families, this group presented 18 CpG hypermethylated DNA regions (9 in blood and 9 in VAT), compared to subjects with obesity. On the other hand, participants with obesity exhibited a higher relative abundance of Planococcaceae and Veillonellaceae families, along with 2 CpG hypermethylated DNA regions (cg06936779 in blood and cg11419025 in VAT), which were observed generally hypomethylated in controls. Figure 3 shows the weight of each original variable's contribution to component 1, according to the different omics, evaluated in three distinct tissues. Considering those variables with values higher than 0.4, controls exhibited greater values of cg08485389 and cg07867325 in blood DNA methylation; cg09698465 in VAT DNA methylation, as well as Rikenellaceae and Chrinstensellaceae in gut microbiota. Interestingly, cg06936779 showed higher levels of DNA methylation in blood from participants with obesity and T2DM, while cg11419025 levels of DNA methylation were higher in participants with obesity without insulin resistance.

Very strong intercorrelation between the gut microbiome and gene methylation independent of the study subgroups

For a better understanding of the potential associations between the gut microbiome and different biological processes, we performed a correlation using a Spearman's test to evaluate the presence of potential microbiota-regulated genes with very strong correlation (R2 = 0.9) in our population regardless of previous metabolic groups. To further evaluate the biological significance of the genes encoded by sequences containing the 262 bacterial genera-associated CpGs, a GO analysis was performed, establishing several functional networks (Fig. 11). Accordingly, three main bacterial genera (Sutterella, Collinsella and Eubacterium) were associated with distinct biological process according to the gene ontogene (GO) analysis. In this regard, Sutterella was shown to be involved in the epigenetic regulation of genes involved in small ubiquitin-related modifier (SUMO) transferase activity, lipid binding, ribonucleic acid (RNA) gene silencing, negative regulation of cellular extravasation, and postsynaptic density. Also, Collinsella was linked to these same biological processes, except for SUMO transferase activity, through the epigenetic modification of the expression of different genes. On the other hand, epigenetic regulation by Eubacterium was related to pathways involved in lipid binding and SUMO transferase activity.

Discussion

In this multi-omics data integration approach, we showed distinctive gut microbiome signatures and CpG methylation patterns in subjects with different metabolic characteristics (i.e., participants with obesity versus controls without obesity, subjects with T2DM versus subjects without T2DM, and subjects with obesity and insulin resistance versus subjects with obesity but no insulin resistance). Moreover, specific associations between different gut bacteria and DNA methylation patterns in blood and VAT were observed, which may suggest a potential role of the gut microbiome in epigenetic regulation in metabolic diseases.

A growing body of evidence has revealed that the gut microbiome is able to mediate epigenetic modifications in the host, including DNA methylation, although previous works were mainly focused on histone modification [6, 19]. Remarkably, the role of the gut microbiome as an epigenetic modifier has been reported to have a major impact on the pathophysiology of several diseases [20], although the putative contribution of the interactions between gut microbiota and host epigenome to the development and progression of metabolic diseases is yet to be elucidated. Even though most of the available literature has been focused on histone modifications, in a previous study, we found that DNA methylation, including the presence of several candidate genes related to glucose metabolism and energy homeostasis, was associated with gut microbiota composition (according to FBR) in patients with obesity [12]. Also, the association of predominant bacterial phyla (i.e., Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes, and Proteobacteria) with differentially methylated gene promoters was described in 8 pregnant women [13]. Recently, a relationship between Ruminococcus abundance and MACROD2/SEL1L2 gene methylation in blood has been reported in obesity [21]. On the other hand, therapeutic interventions (i.e., dietary supplementation with probiotics) demonstrated changes in DNA methylation levels of obesity-related genes in both pregnant women and their offspring [22], whereas fecal microbiota transplant from healthy donors to subjects with metabolic syndrome showed several effects on the host epigenome [23]. Similar results have been observed in other metabolic diseases, such as metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease [24].

In the current study, we carried out for the first time machine learning approaches, such as PCA or sPLS, of gut microbiome and DNA methylation profile in VAT and blood to identify the most distinctive signatures among participants with different metabolic characteristics. Interestingly, we found a higher relative abundance of Odoribacteriaceae, Rikenellaceae and Christensenllaceae in the control group, compared to individuals with obesity. An increased abundance of these families has been previously associated with a healthy metabolic profile, and a decreased abundance may lead to the development of obesity [25–27]. On the other hand, an increased relative abundance of other OTUs, such as Veillonellaceae, was found in subjects with obesity, in line with previous works [28]. Of note, although the categorization of subjects of this study was mainly based on the presence/absence of obesity and insulin resistance or T2DM, other metabolic features, such as the lipid profile, may also have an influence on the gut microbiome. Together with these gut microbiome signatures, distinctive CpG methylation patterns were found between groups. Despite the fact that some of the involved genes have been previously reported to play a potential role in obesity (e.g., c8orf74, whose CpG region was hypermethylated in VAT in subjects with obesity, compared with controls in this study) [29], the role of other protein coding genes which were different between groups remains unclear. Interestingly, some of them have been associated with different metabolic and inflammatory processes (e.g., SIRT5, with differently methylated CpG regions of VAT in subjects with and without obesity in our study) [30]; however, further research is needed to clarify the clinical significance of these differences.

Remarkably, the main goal of the current study was to explore the relationships between gut microbiome and DNA methylation patterns. In this regard, it is noteworthy that some of the identified OTUs have been reported to induce epigenetic changes in the host in previous studies. Thus, Odoribacter has been demonstrated to be related to the methylation of lipid-regulated genes [31]. Also, Faecalibacterium could be involved in the methylation of the promoter region of free fatty acid receptor 3 (FFAR3) through the production of butyrate, with important implications in obesity/T2DM [32]. In this regard, the biosynthesis of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) has been suggested as a potential mechanism implicated in DNA methylation [19, 33]. Given the fact that some of the identified OTUs include SCFAs-producing bacteria (e.g., Odoribacteriaceae, Christensenllaceae, or Veillonellaceae) [34–36], it could be hypothesized that changes in DNA methylation patterns might be induced through this mechanism. Moreover, the generation of B vitamins by some gut bacterial families (e.g., Faecalibacterium, Coprococcus) [37, 38] may serve as donors of methyl groups for DNA methylation [6]. Although further research is warranted to unravel the mechanisms involved in the associations found in the current study, Odoribacteriaceae and Christensenllaceae were the two features with the most powerful associations with distinct methylation CpG patterns, mainly cg08485389, cg07867325, cg02738677, cg08485389 and cg07867325 in blood, as well as cg11419025 in VAT. However, no direct relationship has been established between the majority of these CpG islands and particular genes beyond DNAAF8 and cg08485389. Therefore, some of the relationships found between these gut microbiome features and CpGs deserve further investigation.

In the second component of the PCA, we found different patterns of DNA methylation between subjects with and without T2DM, together with different abundances of gut bacteria, mainly at the expense of Turicibacteriaceae. Intriguingly, little is known about this family. One of the multiple CpGs included in the discriminatory analysis in relation to T2DM was cg25996573, related to the gene RTN4. Reduction of RTN4 expression has been identified as a potential strategy for the treatment of high-carbohydrate diet-induced metabolic complications [39]. Thus, hypermethylation of cg25996573 in patients with T2DM could be associated with the improvement of insulin sensitivity. An additional family involved in the model in relation to T2DM was Clostridiaceae, although with much less weight. In this regard, when healthy controls and participants with T2DM were excluded from the analysis, Clostridiaceae was one of the main contributors to differences between subjects with obesity with and without insulin resistance. Therefore, subjects with insulin resistance presented an increased abundance in Clostridiaceae, which was in agreement with previous studies [40]. These differences in gut microbiota composition were accompanied by different DNA methylation patterns between subjects with and without insulin resistance, including several genes, such as SMYD3 and SOS1, both in adipose tissue, or NUP107 in blood. These genes have been previously related to obesity, insulin resistance or T2DM. Indeed, NUP107 has been associated with the regulation of body fat [41], whereas SOS1 has been related to the risk for gestational diabetes [42], and may have a role in the regulation of insulin sensitivity/resistance via protein phosphorylation [43]. Interestingly, SMYD3 is a lysine methyltransferase which methylates H3K4 and H4K5 [44], playing a role in chromatin regulation [45]. Thus, this gene has been previously linked to obesity and insulin resistance [46]. In this model, DNA methylome, both in blood and adipose tissue, demonstrated its discriminatory power to distinguish between patients with or without insulin resistance. Even though gut microbiota features contributed to the variables, the further correlation analysis did not include any gut microbiota component, a fact that may suggest a minor contribution in this regard.

However, in a final analysis attempt to find relationships between gut microbiome and DNA methylation patterns, we explored potential associations between the gut microbiome and distinct biological processes through epigenetic regulation in the entire study population. Accordingly, Sutterella, Eubacterium and Collinsella were the genera with the highest degree of interactions of different biological processes with demonstrated effects on cellular metabolism and metabolic disease [47]. It could be speculated that the aforementioned OTUs could be involved in these processes by the epigenetic regulation (DNA methylation) of specific genes. In line with this, it has been previously described that metabolite-producing bacteria, such as Eubacterium, may drive metabolic functions by the induction of epigenetic changes in the host [48]. Interestingly, another pathway of chromatin regulation, SUMOylation, has emerged. It is known that various bacteria interfere with host cell functions through the manipulation of host-protein post-translational modifications. Among these modifications, ubiquitins such as SUMO constitute key targets [49]. In fact, it has been suggested a crosstalk between methylation and SUMOylation as a general mode for regulation of chromatin function [50]. Therefore, Sutterella and Eubacterium could interact with their host through this novel mechanism.

This study has some limitations. First, PCA permits to summarize large multi-omic datasets into less complex and more interpretable data. However, it should be noted that PCA is a hypothesis-generating technique, and in-depth studies assessing specific interactions between the gut microbiome and DNA methylation patterns are needed to confirm these results. Also, due to the limited sample size included in this study, our results should be cautiously interpreted. Moreover, further investigation is warranted to unravel the potential mechanisms involved in DNA methylation by the described gut bacteria. On the other hand, due to the cross-sectional nature of this study, causality cannot be inferred, and given that our results are mainly based on associations and in silico analysis, specific studies are required. In fact, reciprocal interactions may be observed between the gut microbiome and the DNA methylome [19]. Therefore, further longitudinal studies assessing these interactions are needed. Moreover, as VAT microbiome may be another niche directly inducing epigenetic modifications in this tissue, this point also deserves further investigation.

In conclusion, in this multi-omics study, we found that distinctive gut microbiome signatures and related DNA methylation patterns were able to discriminate between different metabolic phenotypes. We also showed that distinctive interactions between the gut microbiome and DNA methylome may occur in subjects with different metabolic characteristics. Moreover, we have reported that some OTUs might be related to several biological processes by epigenetic regulation. Devoted in-depth studies are required to corroborate these hypotheses.
