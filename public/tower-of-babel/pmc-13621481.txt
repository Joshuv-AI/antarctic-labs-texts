Genome‐wide prediction of cloned disease resistance homologues across the Oryza genus
Authors: Sabrina Morrison, Jacqueline Batley, Ian Paul Navea, Van Schepler‐Luu, Robert J Henry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe wild relatives of rice (Oryza sativa L.) represent reservoirs of genetic diversity and potential sources of novel pest and disease resistance. The recent availability of high‐quality Oryza genome assemblies has enabled greater exploration of this diversity across the genus. This study aimed to identify and characterize novel disease resistance gene candidates across these wild species. A total of 24 assembled Oryza genomes and proteomes from both wild and cultivated species were analyzed. Putative homologues to functionally validated, cloned disease resistance (CDR) genes in rice were identified using basic local alignment searches with BLASTp and designated into classes with RGAugury. Homologue characterization analyses, including physical distribution, clustering, gene duplication, phylogenies, and domain structure for NBS‐LRR (NLR)‐like homologues, were also conducted. A total of 2460 homologues to CDR genes were identified. Homologue abundance was generally highest in cultivated rices, followed by wild AA species and non‐AA taxa, with no clear relationship to genome or chromosome size. In allotetraploids, CDR homologues were more abundant in CC sub‐genomes, consistent with broader patterns of preferential gene retention. Most homologues were designated as NLRs or receptor‐like kinases, which were also the gene classes most frequently associated with duplication. Atypical resistance genes were often designated as singletons or segmental genes. Nucleotide‐binding site domain analyses revealed conserved motifs across the genus. Discordance between gene phylogenies and the Oryza species tree suggests complex evolutionary histories across resistance‐associated gene families. Overall, this study provides an insight into disease resistance gene diversity across the Oryza genus and highlights loci that warrant further investigation for future rice breeding.

INTRODUCTION

Rice (Oryza sativa L.) is grown in over 100 countries as a staple food for an estimated 3.5 billion people (Hashim et al., 2024). Pests and diseases account for 30% of annual rice production losses, posing a significant threat to global food security (Savary et al., 2019). Introducing novel genetic resistance into cultivated lines remains the most economical and ecological approach for combating these biotic pressures in most rice production systems (Muduli et al., 2021; Ramalingam et al., 2020; Younas et al., 2023). Several hundred disease resistance genes have been identified in both cultivated and wild rice species, with around 50 of these genes cloned and functionally characterized (Sahu et al., 2022; Simon et al., 2023). The majority of these confer resistance against three major rice pests and diseases: rice blast, bacterial blight (BB), and brown planthopper (BPH) (Simon et al., 2023). Rice blast, caused by Magnaporthe oryzae, is among the most destructive diseases of rice and threatens production across nearly all major rice‐growing regions worldwide (F. Xu et al., 2026). BB, induced by the gram‐negative pathogen Xanthomonas oryzae pv. oryzae (Xoo), is the most important bacterial disease of rice and can result in yield reductions of up to 70% under favorable conditions (Lore et al., 2025). Finally, BPH [Nilaparvata lugens (Stål)] is a major sap‐feeding insect pest across Asia, where outbreaks can cause between 20% and 80% yield loss each year (Muduli et al., 2021). Sap‐feeding and chewing insects are also vectors for several important viral diseases of rice, including rice tungro disease, caused by rice tungro spherical virus (RTSV) and rice tungro bacilliform virus (Amelia et al., 2023), rice stripe virus (Y. Zhang et al., 2024), and rice yellow mottle virus (Ndikumana et al., 2025). Despite the success of deploying major disease resistance (R) genes into rice breeding programs, resistance breakdown remains an ongoing challenge due to the rapid evolution of pathogen populations and the tendency for many R genes to confer race‐specific resistance (Fukuoka & Okuno, 2019; Mundt, 2014; S. Wang et al., 2020).

The genus Oryza comprises 27 recognized species, of which only two, Asian O. sativa L. and African Oryza glaberrima Steud., were domesticated independently to give rise to cultivated rice (Jacquemin et al., 2014; Stein et al., 2018). The remaining 25 wild relatives span 11 distinct genome groups, including six diploid groups (n = 12; AA, BB, CC, EE, FF, and GG) and five allotetraploid groups (n = 24; BBCC, CCDD, HHJJ, HHKK, and KKLL) (Stein et al., 2018). These wild Oryza taxa are distributed across diverse ecological regions and exhibit extensive morphological and genetic variation, including natural resistance against a broad range of pathogens (Henry et al., 2010; Stein et al., 2018; Tirnaz et al., 2022). These species therefore represent largely untapped sources of genetic disease resistance that may have use in cultivated rice breeding programs (Stein et al., 2018; Tirnaz et al., 2022).

Allelic mining of putative disease resistance genes from cultivated rice lines has been widely used to identify novel sources of resistance for rice breeding (Ashkani et al., 2015). This approach has been applied to a lesser extent in wild rice, with most work being confined to the AA genome group; however, there are some studies that have examined specific resistance gene classes, usually NBS‐LRRs (NLRs), across multiple cultivated and wild rice genomes (Long et al., 2024; Stein et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2008). Cloned disease resistance (CDR) homologues are genes that share ancestry with a previously characterized functional disease resistance gene (Cantila et al., 2022). As such, they provide a useful entry point for identifying novel sources of resistance since they are expected to retain structural features associated with disease resistance function while also harboring sequence variation that may confer novel phenotypes (Cantila et al., 2022; Stein et al., 2018; Tirnaz et al., 2022). Homology‐guided mining of resistance loci has been effective in multiple crop species, including wheat, barley, and several Brassica species (P. C. Bailey et al., 2018; Cantila et al., 2022; Dolatabadian et al., 2025; Steuernagel et al., 2016). In rice, several wild homologues to known disease resistance genes have been functionally validated and deployed into rice lines, such as Pi9 from Oryza minuta, the Pi54 variant alleles against rice blast, Pi54rh and Pi54of from Oryza rhizomatis and Oryza officinalis, respectively, and the BPH18 variant conferring resistance against BPH, introgressed from Oryza australiensis (Das et al., 2012; Devanna et al., 2014; Jena et al., 2006). However, systematic characterization of CDR homologues (CDRHs) across wild species remains limited. Here, we perform a genome‐wide, cross‐species prediction of homologues to almost all CDR genes in rice using protein sequence alignment searches against 24 Oryza genomes (Simon et al., 2023). In doing so, we aim to broaden understanding of genetic diversity across the genus and expand the catalogue of putative resistance‐associated candidate alleles for future functional investigation and potential application in cultivated rice breeding.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Acquisition of Oryza genomes and CDR genes

A total of 24 genomes from 21 Oryza species were downloaded from publicly available databases or repositories linked to the original source publications for CDRH identification (Table 1). Included were genomes for both O. sativa subsp. indica and japonica, two assemblies from geographically distinct Oryza rufipogon accessions, and both a diploid and tetraploid assembly for Oryza punctata. The tetraploid O. punctata is also referred to as O. punctata (schweinfurthiana) in other studies but is maintained as O. punctata in the study from which the genome assembly was retrieved. The tetraploid species name was therefore kept as O. punctata in the study at hand and differentiated from the diploid species when mentioned. The genome for Leersia perrieri was also included as an outgroup for analyses. All assemblies had been sequenced and assembled using long‐read assembly technologies (ONT or PacBio). The General Feature Format (GFF) annotation files for the genomes were filtered for the longest isomer, and corresponding protein sequences were extracted from the genome assemblies using Another Gtf/Gff Analysis Toolkit (AGAT) (Dainat, 2022). Proteomes were clustered using OrthoFinder (v2.5.5) (Emms & Kelly, 2019) and the resulting phylogenetic tree was visualized within the Interactive Tree of Life (iTOL) (Letunic & Bork, 2021) (v7.2.2) and re‐rooted using Leersia perrieri as the outgroup.

Protein sequences for 56 cloned rice disease resistance genes (independent loci) conferring resistance to blast, BB, BPH, and viral pathogens were compiled from the literature (Simon et al., 2023) and public databases (Table 2). These proteins served as the reference dataset for identifying putative CDRHs across the Oryza genus. Where available, the original CDR gene sequences were retrieved from GenBank (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genbank/) or UniProtKB (https://www.uniprot.org/). When the original gene sequence was not available, the corresponding locus from the Nipponbare IRGSP1.0 reference genome was retrieved from the Rice Annotation Project (Ohyanagi et al., 2006) and MSU Rice Genome Annotation Project (RGAP) databases (Hamilton et al., 2024; Kawahara et al., 2013). In cases where multiple cloned genes mapped to the same locus, a single representative gene was used to minimize redundancy. Some additional considerations were made for certain genes. The NLR‐like gene corresponding to the Os12g37290 locus in the Nipponbare IRGSP1.0 reference is truncated, as Nipponbare possesses the susceptible haplotype at this locus. Consequently, a resistant allelic variant from cultivated O. sativa subsp. indica, BPH26, and another variant introgressed from wild rice O. australiensis, BPH18, were used instead. The resistance allele for Xa47 (802 aa) was retrieved and translated from the source paper (Xing et al., 2021). The NLR gene Pb2 is split into two loci due to a retrotransposon (Y. Yu et al., 2022) (Os11g45610) in the Nipponbare reference. The two loci (Os11g45600 and Os11g45620) corresponding to the split Pb2 domains were extracted from the MSU RGAP database, concatenated, and run through the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) for protein sequences, BLASTp, to identify intact Pb2 variants in other cultivated lines. An O. sativa subsp. indica gene (BBD82501.1) of 1033 residues was identified as a likely homologue (query cover: 99%, E‐value: 0.0, percent identity: 94.51%) and was reverse BLASTed to ensure homology to the same Pb2 loci tags in Nipponbare (ABA95282.1 and ABA95284.1). This sequence was used to identify other homologues to Pb2 in the wild species.

Identification of putative CDRHs

The Oryza proteomes were queried against the CDR protein set using BLASTp within BLAST+ (v2.11.0) (Camacho et al., 2009; Z. Zhang et al., 2000). Genes were retained as putative CDRHs based on BLAST hit thresholds used in previous studies (e‐value ≤ 1× 10−45, query coverage ≥ 80%, similarity (percent positives) ≥ 60%) (Cantila et al., 2022; Dolatabadian et al., 2025). For very small peptide genes (<150 aa), the query coverage threshold was higher (≥90%). Orthogroups across the Oryza genomes were inferred using OrthoFinder v2.5.5 (Emms & Kelly, 2019) under the default parameters with the annotated proteome for each species. CDRH candidates occurring in orthogroups containing the corresponding CDR gene from the reference O. sativa proteome were recorded as having additional orthology support.

RGAugury (P. Li et al., 2016) and integrated tools BLAST (Z. Zhang et al., 2000), nCoils (Lupas et al., 1991), PfamScan (Finn et al., 2010), Phobius (Käll et al., 2004), and InterProScan (Quevillon et al., 2005) were used to predict the resistance gene analogue (RGA) class of CDRHs. Genes were classified as either NLR‐like, receptor‐like kinases (RLKs), receptor‐like proteins (RLPs), transmembrane (TM)–coiled‐coils (CCs), or “non‐RGA” proteins. NLR‐like genes were further classified based on the presence of the following domain types: nucleotide‐binding site (NBS), CC, Toll/interleukin‐1 receptor (TIR), Resistance to Powdery Mildew 8 (RPW8), or leucine‐rich repeats (LRRs). Results were visualized with iTOL (Letunic & Bork, 2021) (v7.2.2) and circos figures were made with Circa 2.0 (OMGenomics Labs, https://circa.omgenomics.com).

Multi‐sequence alignments (MSAs) for CDRHs were generated using MUSCLE (v3.8) (Edgar, 2004) and trimmed using TrimAl (v1.5.0) (Capella‐Gutiérrez et al., 2009). Phylogenetic relationships were inferred by maximum likelihood using IQ‐TREE (v2.4.0) with ultrafast bootstrap parameters (bootstrap replicates: 1000) (Hoang et al., 2018; Nguyen et al., 2015).

Duplication events and physical clustering of homologues

The physical locations of CDRH candidates were analyzed for genomes with chromosome‐level assemblies. Gene coordinates were extracted from GFF annotation files and then filtered for CDRHs using Galaxy (v1.0.4) (https://usegalaxy.org.au/). Chromosomal positions were visualized using MG2C (v2.1) (Chao et al., 2021). Physical clustering analyses were conducted by ordering gene coordinates of CDRHs and calculating the distance between homologues. A gene cluster was defined as two or more CDRHs located within 200 kb of each other on the same chromosome (Cantila et al., 2022; Jupe et al., 2012). Clusters were further classified as homogenous if the CDRHs within the cluster were homologous to the same CDR gene. Clusters with CDRHs to different genes were designated as heterogenous.

Tandem, proximal, and dispersed duplicated genes were identified with MCScanX (Y. Wang et al., 2012) using the longest isomer protein sequence files and corresponding GFF annotation files. Tetraploid assemblies were separated into sub‐genomes to avoid artificial inflation of whole‐genome duplicated homologues. The protein sequence files for each species were self‐BLASTed using BLASTp (Z. Zhang et al., 2000) within DIAMOND (v2.1.7.161) (Buchfink et al., 2021). The following parameters were specified: –more‐sensitive, –max‐target‐seqs 5, and –e value 1e‐45. A 30% query coverage threshold was additionally applied to exclude short local matches while retaining more divergent duplicated genes. The BLAST outputs and GFF files were run through duplicate_gene_classifier within MCScanX (Y. Wang et al., 2012). MCScanX classified CDRHs as either: 0: singletons with no detected paralogues, 1: dispersed, where paralogues are not syntenic and are distant (e.g., located on different chromosomes), 2: proximal, where paralogues appear on the same chromosome and fall within a proximal window (within 20 genes of each other), 3: tandem, where paralogues are adjacent, and 4: whole genome duplication (WGD) or segmental events (Y. Wang et al., 2012).

Analysis of NBS domains

Homologues that were classified as NBS‐encoding in RGAugury, together with homologues to CDR genes previously reported to be NLR‐like, were analyzed using InterProScan (Galaxy Version 5.59‐91.0) (Quevillon et al., 2005) to identify CDRHs with NB‐ARC (nucleotide‐binding adaptor shared by APAF‐1, R Proteins, and CED‐4) domains. NB‐ARC domains were detected using the Pfam HMM PF00931 and extracted from protein sequences with seqtk_subseq (v1.5) within the seqtk toolkit (Mistry et al., 2020; Shen et al., 2016; Sonnhammer et al., 1997). The extracted NB‐ARC regions were used for downstream alignment, motif, and phylogenetic analyses. Domains were aligned using MAFFT (v7.511) (Katoh et al., 2019; Kuraku et al., 2013), and multiple sequence alignments were trimmed using TrimAl (v1.5.0) with –automated1 parameter settings (Capella‐Gutiérrez et al., 2009). Aligned NBS domains were assessed for key conserved motifs using the Simple, Thorough, Rapid, Enriched Motif Elicitation (STREME) tool (Galaxy Version 5.5.8) (T. L. Bailey, 2011; The Galaxy Community, 2024), and fragmented or truncated domains were identified. An NB‐ARC domain was considered intact if it possessed Walker A/P‐loop, Walker B, RNBS‐B, and GLPL motifs. Phylogenetic trees were then inferred by maximum likelihood using IQ‐TREE (v2.4.0) with ultrafast bootstrap parameters (bootstrap replicates: 1000) (Nguyen et al., 2015). NB‐ARC conserved motifs were visualized with WebLogo3 (v3.7.9) (Crooks et al., 2004; Schneider & Stephens, 1990) and phylogenetic tree visualizations were made with iTOL (Letunic & Bork, 2021) (v7.2.2).

Alignment and phylogenetic analysis of Pi‐ta2 homologues

Phylogenetic analyses were conducted on the whole sequences and armadillo (ARM)‐repeat domains of the atypical disease resistance gene Pi‐ta2 and its associated homologues. The amino acid sequences of known allelic variants of Pi‐ta2 were sourced from a previous study (Greenwood et al., 2024) for whole gene sequence alignment. Homologues to Pi‐ta2 were run through InterProScan (Galaxy Version 5.59‐91.0) (Quevillon et al., 2005) using SUPERFAMILY (Gough et al., 2001; Madera et al., 2004), SMART (Schultz et al., 1998), Gene3d (Lees et al., 2010), and PANTHER (Thomas et al., 2003) annotation databases/tools. ARM repeat domains were called using the following key terms and accession IDs: SSF48371, ARM, armadillo, and PF00514. ARM domains were extracted from protein sequences using seqtk_subseq (v1.5) within the seqtk toolkit (Shen et al., 2016). Both the ARM domains and whole genes were aligned using MUSCLE (v3.8) (Edgar, 2004), and phylogenetic relationships were inferred by maximum likelihood using IQ‐TREE (v2.4.0) with ultrafast bootstrap parameters (bootstrap replicates: 1000) (Nguyen et al., 2015). The secondary structures of homologues to Pi‐ta2 were predicted using the PSIPRED workbench (v4.0) (Buchan et al., 2024; Jones, 1999) with default parameters. Predicted α‐helices, β‐strands, and coil regions were compared against homologues to assess structural conservation within the ARM‐repeat domain.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using RStudio with R v4.5.1. Pearson's chi‐square analyses of independence were conducted to test the association between duplication mode of CDRHs and predicted RGA classification. Adjusted standardized Pearson residuals were examined to identify cells contributing to differences in the overall distribution, with positive and negative residuals indicating overrepresentation and underrepresentation, respectively. Residuals with absolute values > 1.96 were considered to indicate deviations from expected frequencies. Statistical analyses for CDRH abundance and cluster distribution across chromosomes were performed using nonparametric Friedman tests. Where significant differences were detected, two‐sided paired Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests were used to compare counts between specific chromosomes, with post hoc p‐values adjusted using Bonferroni correction. A two‐sided paired Wilcoxon signed‐rank test was similarly used to compare CDRH abundance between sub‐genomes in allotetraploid species. A one‐sided Jonckheere–Terpstra test (clinfun package v1.1.5) was used to assess whether CDRH abundance followed an ordered trend across non‐AA wild species, wild AA‐genome species, and cultivated rice. Group order reflected increasing genetic affinity to cultivated rice (Harlan & de Wet, 1971; Solis et al., 2020), and p‐values were estimated using 100,000 permutations. For all analyses, statistical significance was assessed at p < 0.05.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Results

The wild Oryza taxa contain diverse resistance‐associated gene homologues

A total of 2460 genes across the Oryza genomes were identified as candidate homologues (CDRHs) to 56 known CDR genes (Supporting Information Data 1). From the wild Oryza genomes, the tetraploid Oryza punctata (BBCC) had the highest number of CDRHs (208 homologues, 168 genes), followed by O. minuta (BBCC) (160 homologues, 133 genes) and Oryza malampuzhaensis (BBCC) (152 homologues, 126 genes) (Figure 1). The cultivated rice genomes generally possessed a higher number of homologues to cloned resistance genes relative to wild diploid Oryza species. Oryza sativa subsp. japonica, O. sativa subsp. indica, and O. glaberrima possessed 173, 176, and 132 homologues, representing 128, 130, and 100 genes, respectively. Wild AA‐genome species also generally contained greater numbers of CDRHs than non‐AA wild species; for example, Oryza nivara (AA) contained 159 homologues (125 genes) and Oryza barthii (AA)—the putative ancestor of O. glaberrima—possessed 157 homologues (119 genes) (Figure 1). Consistent with this pattern, CDRH abundance demonstrated a significant ordered increase from non‐AA wild species to wild AA species and cultivated rice (Jonckheere–Terpstra test, JT = 72.5, p = 0.007). The most evolutionarily distant AA genome species, Oryza meridionalis, had the lowest number of homologues (55 homologues, 48 genes), followed by O. australiensis (EE) (69 homologues, 54 genes) and Oryza coarctata (KKLL) (82 homologues, 60 genes).

Orthology analyses were conducted using OrthoFinder (Emms & Kelly, 2019) to provide additional support for CDRH assignments. Of the identified CDRHs, 872 (35.4%) were assigned to 42 orthogroups containing the corresponding 56 CDR protein sequences. Ten orthogroups contained more than one CDR gene. Orthogroup assignments are provided in Supporting Information Data 1.

Homologues were characterized into resistance gene class using RGAugury. NBS‐encoding CDRHs were the most common (1355 homologues, 43%), with most of these (1324) possessing one or more LRR domains (NL RGA class), and 725 further possessing a CC domain at the N‐terminal (CC‐NBS‐LRR [CNL] class) (Figure 1). No TIR‐NL/TIR‐NBS‐LRR (TNL) homologues were identified. There were 891 (29%) RLK homologues identified across the genomes, seven RLPs, and two TM–CC proteins. There were 867 (28%) CDRHs that were identified as “non‐RGAs,” meaning that they lacked typical conserved structures associated with RGA classes as specified by RGAugury. Homologue classification by RGAugury largely coincided with expectations based on known CDR gene structure. Of the 1364 homologues to cloned genes with known NBS‐encoding regions, 1340 were predicted to also be NBS‐encoding by RGAugury (Supporting Information Data 1). Similarly, 891 of the 907 homologues to cloned RLK resistance genes were also predicted to encode for RLKs, while 830 of the 851 homologues to nonconventional resistance genes were classified as non‐RGAs (i.e., without NBS, RLK, RLP, TM–CC, or RPW8 domains).

Of the 56 CDR genes analyzed in this study, 28 (50%) are known to confer resistance to rice blast, 13 (23%) are for BB resistance, 10 (18%) are against BPH, and 5 (9%) confer resistance against viral diseases. This was generally consistent with the proportions of homologues to blast (51%), BB (26%), BPH (16%), and viral (7%) resistance genes identified within the domesticated species of rice (O. sativa subsp. japonica and indica and O. glaberrima) (Figure 1). Within the wild AA Oryza species (847 total CDRHs), the proportion of homologues to genes associated with BB resistance increased (30% of CDRHs), while the proportion of homologues to BPH genes was slightly lower (13% of CDRHs). This proportional increase in BB resistance homologues was even more pronounced in the non‐AA diploid species (221/671 CDRHS, 33%). Homologues to BPH resistance genes remained at 13% of CDRHs within the non‐AA diploid group, while homologues to blast resistance genes were proportionally lower (42%). The proportion of CDRHs to viral disease resistance genes increased to 12% in the non‐AA diploid genome group, although the average number of these genes per species did not change significantly relative to cultivated rice.

The Xoo LRR‐RLK disease resistance gene, Xa21, had the highest number of homologues across the genus (308 CDRHs), followed by two other LRR‐RLK‐type genes: the blast resistance gene Pi65 (179 CDRHs) and another Xoo resistance gene, Xa26 (174 CDRHs) (Table 3). An executor R gene, Xa27, had the lowest number of homologues (5 CDRHs), followed by an atypical resistance gene consisting of a short consensus repeat domain, BPH32 (9 CDRHs), and another executor gene, Xa23 (10 genes) (Table 3).

Phylogenetic analyses of the CDRHs revealed several instances where individual gene trees were discordant with the overall Oryza species phylogeny, specifically where homologues in the CC genomes clustered more closely with the cloned resistance gene than expected. The unconventional rice tungro resistance gene tsv1 (eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4G [eIF4G]) was represented by 32 homologues across the Oryza genus (Table 3), corresponding to a single homologue in each diploid genome and tetraploid sub‐genome. Phylogenetic analysis of these tsv1‐like homologues largely reflected the expected species relationships, with the exception of the CC genome homologues, which clustered more closely with the AA genome group and cloned tsv1 reference than the BB genome homologues (Figure S1). Similarly, both the cloned reference sequence for Pb1 and its corresponding locus in the Nipponbare reference were phylogenetically closer to homologues in the CC genomes than to other AA genome homologues (Figure S2).

Additional analyses of tsv1 homologue MSAs were conducted to identify potential resistance‐associated alleles. Within the alignment, the putative resistance region identified by J.‐H. Lee et al. (2010) was examined in greater detail (Figure S1). There were nine homologues that deviated from the known susceptible allele sequence (AGKSYVVDHP). Both O. minuta and O. australiensis possessed deletions at positions 1014 and 1018, respectively, with O. australiensis additionally possessing an H→L substitution at position 1017. Homologues from the CC diploid genomes and the CC sub‐genomes of BBCC tetraploids possessed an A→D substitution at position 1009, while O. coarctata possessed an A→T substitution at the same position. Additional variation was also observed in adjacent residues, including a conserved Y→F substitution at position 1000 and an A→T substitution at position 1028 in all non‐AA Oryza species, as well as an L→P substitution at position 1006 in BB genomes/sub‐genomes.

Another notable example of phylogenetic discordance was observed for the BB resistance gene Xa47. Xa47, which co‐localizes with the Pik locus on chromosome 11 in the Nipponbare genome (Os11g46200) (Simon et al., 2023), shared all but one homologue with the blast resistance gene, Pik‐1 (Table 1). Despite this shared homologue set, phylogenetic analyses resolved the sequences into two distinct clades corresponding to Xa47 and Pik‐1 (Figure S3). Homologues from Oryza glumaepatula, O. nivara, O. rufipogon, and O. sativa subsp. japonica clustered with the Xa47 reference, while homologues from Oryza longistaminata, O. barthii, and O. sativa subsp. indica grouped with Pik‐1, resulting in the division of the AA genome homologues between the two clades.

The NLR genes Pib (chromosome 2) and BPH18/26 (chromosome 12) also shared a largely overlapping set of homologues, which resolved into three distinct phylogenetic clades (Figure S4). The largest clade corresponded to Pib‐like homologues and included CDRHs from the AA, BB, CC, and tetraploid genome groups, all of which were located on chromosome 2 of their respective genomes. A second clade comprised BPH18/26‐like homologues from O. sativa subsp. indica, O. punctata, and Oryza latifolia genomes, all located on chromosome 12. The third clade, distinct from both reference genes, was comprised of homologues from the AA, BB, and CC genomes/tetraploid sub‐genomes and located on chromosome 8. A BLAST analysis of the O. sativa Nipponbare locus Os08g42670, which fell within this third clade, identified similarity to a Pik‐2‐like disease resistance protein. This differed from the canonical Pik‐2 locus (Os11g46210), suggesting that this clade represents a distinct group of CDRHs related to Pik‐2‐like disease resistance proteins.

Nonrandom chromosome distribution of disease resistance homologues

The distributions of CDRHs along the wild Oryza chromosomes were visualized in genomes with chromosome‐level assemblies. The O. sativa Nipponbare and O. glaberrima genomes were also included in analyses for comparative purposes. A total of 2069 homologous genes were able to be mapped to chromosomes across the genomes. CDRH abundance was observed to vary greatly between chromosomes (Friedman test, χ 2(11) = 202.3, p < 0.0001). Chromosome 11 possessed the greatest total number of homologous genes across the analyzed genomes (688 genes), with the long end of chromosome 11 observed to be a gene‐rich region for disease resistance candidates in most of the Oryza genomes (Figure 2). Post hoc paired Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests confirmed that chromosome 11 contained significantly more CDRHs relative to each of the other chromosomes (all Bonferroni adjusted p = 0.0005). Chromosome 2 (240 genes) and chromosome 6 (213 genes) possessed the second and third greatest numbers of CDRHs, respectively. Chromosomes 3, 5, and 7 had the lowest number of homologous genes, with 80, 38, and 50 genes across the species, respectively.

Chromosome 2, which only has two of the O. sativa CDR genes used in this analysis (RLS1 and Pib), possessed the second highest number of homologues across the wild species. In addition to RLS1 and Pib, most of these were also homologues to Xa21 (LRR‐RLK) and xa41 (Sugars Will Eventually be Exported Transporter [SWEET]) (Figure 2). The SWEET gene xa41 was additionally found on chromosomes 3, 8, and 12 in several species, while homologues to Xa21 were widespread along most chromosomes.

Several CDR genes possessed putative paralogue copies that were consistently identified on chromosomes differing from their canonical locations in O. sativa: Pi63 (NLR, chr4) was found on the distal ends of chromosome 2 in several species, and RYMV1 (eIF4G‐1, chr4) and RYMV2 (constitutive expressor of pathogenesis‐related genes 5 [CPR5], chr1) also had copies on chromosome 2, xa5 (IIA gamma subunit, chr5) homologues were found on chromosomes 1 and 8, and STV11 (sulfotransferase, chr11) homologues were consistently located on chromosome 9.

There were similar patterns of CDRH chromosomal distribution observed between species; however, CDRH number itself did not seem to correlate with genome or chromosome length. For example, O. australiensis (EE) and Oryza meyeriana (GG), two species with a markedly longer chromosome 6 compared to other genomes, were sparse in CDRHs compared to the shorter AA chromosomes (Figure S5).

Clustering and duplication analyses reveal potential disease resistance gene expansions in wild Oryza

Physical clustering and duplication analyses were conducted on 2372 CDRHs across chromosome‐level genome assemblies. Tetraploid assemblies were separated into sub‐genomes to avoid overestimation of WGD‐designated homologues. There were 141 CDRHs identified as singletons, with the remaining 2231 homologues designated as either dispersed (951 CDRHs), proximal (606 CDRHs), tandem (492 CDRHs), or WGD/segmental (186 CDRHs) duplications (Supporting Information Data 1). The duplication category was found to differ significantly among RGA classes (Pearson's χ 2(12) = 694.8, p < 0.0001). Examination of adjusted standardized residuals (ASRs) indicated that nonconventional resistance genes (i.e., those without NLR, RLK, or LRR domains) were overrepresented among singletons (121 CDRHs, ASR = 18.29). This included homologues to the atypical genes RLS1 (NBS‐ARM, 27 singletons), pi21 (proline‐rich protein, 24 singletons), and BPH29 (B3 domain, 21 singletons) (Table S1). Homologues to executor R genes, namely, Xa10 (nine genes), Xa23 (seven genes), and Xa27 (four genes), were also common among the singleton‐designated group.

The tandem, proximal, and dispersed duplicate groups consisted predominantly of NLR‐like (50%) and RLK‐like (29%) homologues. NLR‐like homologues were enriched in the dispersed duplicate (459 CDRHs, ASR = 3.24) and proximal duplicate groups (322 CDRHs, ASR = 5.30), while RLKs were overrepresented in the proximal (219 CDRHs, ASR = 6.04) and tandem (157 CDRHs, ASR = 2.79) duplicate categories. Homologues to the RLK gene Xa21 comprised the largest group of tandem (49 CDRHs), proximal (130 CDRHs), and dispersed (82 CDRHs) duplicates. Other common homologues in these groups were Xa26/Pi65‐like genes (RLK, dispersed duplicates: 78, proximal duplicates: 38, and tandem duplicates: 43), NLS1 (NLR, dispersed duplicates: 58, proximal duplicates: 31, and tandem duplicates: 46), and Pb3 (NLR, dispersed duplicates: 35, proximal duplicates: 31, and tandem duplicates: 39). Homologues to nonconventional CDR genes were underrepresented within the dispersed and proximal categories (ASR = ‐5.53 and −8.67, respectively), although a handful of CDRH classes showed recurrent duplication across genomes. These included homologues of bsr‐d1 (C2H2‐type zinc finger, tandem duplicates: 48), STV11 (sulfotransferase, dispersed duplicates: 37, proximal duplicates: 37, and tandem duplicates: 37), and RYMV2 (CPR5, dispersed duplicates: 53). Homologues to SWEET‐type genes were enriched in the dispersed duplicate (103 CDRHs, ASR = 8.43) and WGD/segmental duplicate categories (34 CDRHs, ASR = 7.51), but underrepresented in the proximal (0 CDRHs, ASR = −7.09) and tandem duplicated groups (2 CDRHs, ASR = ‐5.78). Among the WGD/segmental‐designated duplicates, RYMV1 (eIF4G‐1) had the highest number of homologues (60 CDRHs), followed by xa5 (IIA gamma subunit, 38 CDRHs) and xa13 (SWEET‐type, 34 CDRHs). NLR‐ and RLK‐like homologues were underrepresented in the singleton (ASR = −9.15 [NLR] and −5.48 [RLK]) and WGD/segmental duplicate categories (ASR = −8.81 [NLR] and −3.98 [RLK].).

Oryza nivara, O. barthii, and O. longistaminata, all belonging to the AA genome group, possessed the highest number of dispersed duplicate CDRHs, with 62, 52, and 42 dispersed duplicates, respectively (Figure 3). For proximal duplications, cultivated O. sativa subsp. indica (44 CDRHs) and O. sativa subsp. japonica (38 CDRHs) had the highest number of duplicates. The CC sub‐genome of the tetraploid O. punctata (35 CDRHs), O. rhizomatis (CC genome, 32 CDRHs), and O. officinalis (CC genome, 32 CDRHs) possessed the highest number of proximal duplicates within the wild species. Oryza barthii, O. longistaminata, and the Australian O. rufipogon possessed the highest numbers of tandem duplications of the wild species, with 37, 34, and 33 duplicates, respectively.

The physical distributions of CDRHs were used for determining heterogenous and homogenous clusters of CDRHs. There were 1074 homologues forming 378 clusters of two or more genes across the genomes (Figure 3). Of these, 236 clusters (603 CDRHs) were homogenous (consisting of homologues to the same disease resistance gene/s), and 142 (471 CDRHs) were heterogenous (possessing at least one homologue to a different gene). Homologues to Xa21 (RLK, 137 CDRHs), Pb3 (NLR, 60 CDRHs), and bsr‐d1 (C2H2‐type zinc finger, 52 CDRHs) were most frequently associated with homogenous gene clusters, while Xa26 and Pi65 (RLKs, 70 CDRHs), Xa4 (wall‐associated kinase, 42 CDRHs), and Pia‐RGA4/RGA5 (NLRs, 37 and 29 CDRHs) were most frequently occurring in heterogenous clusters (Table S2 and Supporting Information Data 1). The BBCC tetraploid O. punctata possessed the highest number of clusters (25; 15 homogenous), split between its BB (10 clusters) and CC (15 clusters) sub‐genomes (Figure 3). Of the wild diploid species, O. barthii (AA) possessed the highest number of clusters (24; 16 homogenous), followed by O. longistaminata (AA, 21 clusters; 15 homogenous), and O. nivara (AA, 20 clusters; 13 homogenous). Oryza australiensis (EE genome, five clusters) and O. meyeriana (GG genome, seven clusters) had the lowest number of CDRH clusters of the diploid genomes. CDRH cluster abundance differed significantly between chromosomes across the genus (Friedman test, χ 2(11) = 180.4, p < 0.0001). Chromosome 11 possessed a significantly higher number of clusters across all the genomes (158 clusters, two‐sided Wilcoxon signed‐rank test, Bonferroni‐adjusted p < 0.0001 for all comparisons), followed by chromosome 6 (40 clusters) and chromosome 4 (38 clusters). There were no clusters of CDRHs identified in chromosome 5 across all the genomes, and only one cluster was found on chromosome 7 in the diploid O. punctata assembly.

Sub‐genome retention of CDRH copies appears nonrandom in Oryza allotetraploids

The seven Oryza allotetraploid genomes possessed 1041 of the 3122 total CDRHs identified across the genus. Higher numbers of homologues were generally observed within tetraploid genomes compared to diploid species. Within this group, O. punctata (BBCC) carried the highest number of homologues (208 CDRHs), while O. coarctata (KKLL) possessed the lowest number (82 CDRHs) (Figure 1; Table 3). The most common CDRHs in the Oryza tetraploid group were those associated with resistance against blast disease (379 CDRHs), followed by BB (303 CDRHs), viruses (128 CDRHs), and BPH (108 CDRHs) (Figure 4).

Ocoa_21 and Ocoa_22 refer to the sub‐genomes in O. coarctata chromosome 11; however, these are not designated into genome groups.

Analyses of CDRH distribution across the six allotetraploids with BB, CC, and DD sub‐genomes demonstrated that homologue numbers in the CC sub‐genomes were significantly higher than their BB/DD sub‐genome counterparts (exact paired Wilcoxin signed‐rank test, V = 21, p = 0.036) (Figure 4). The largest difference in sub‐genome CDRH retention was observed in O. latifolia, which possessed an additional 34 homologues in the CC (83 CDRHs) sub‐genome compared to the DD (49 CDRHs) sub‐genome. The O. punctata BB and CC sub‐genomes were closest in CDRH number, possessing 102 and 106 homologues, respectively. Preferential retention within CC sub‐genomes was particularly evident among NLR‐like homologues, including Pi‐ta2, RYMV3, and the gene pair Pia‐1/Pia‐2 (RGA4/RGA5), which were identified exclusively on CC sub‐genomes on chromosomes 12, 2, and 11, respectively (Figure 4 and Figure S6). Conversely, homologues to several non‐NLR genes, including Xa21, RYMV2, and STV11, were often observed distributed across both sub‐genomes (Figure 4 and Figure S7).

Analysis of the NB‐ARC domains in CDRHs

InterProScan identified a total of 3222 structural domains across the 1138 CDR genes with known NLR‐like domains (29 genes) and their associated homologues. Filtering for the NB‐ARC domain using Pfam HMM PF00931 reduced this down to 1286 candidate domains representing 1109 homologues across the genomes. Alignment of these NBS domains revealed highly conserved motifs commonly expected within NB‐ARC proteins, including the Walker A and B kinases, RNBS‐A and ‐B motifs, and GLPL region (Figure 5; Table 4) (Takken et al., 2006). Analysis of these domains across the Oryza genus identified an RNBS‐A consensus motif of QFDCRAWVCVSQKPD (Table 4). Most homologues possessed the typical Walker A/P‐loop motif GxxxxGK[S/T] (Takken et al., 2006), although only RLS1 and its homologues possessed the GxxxxGKS variant. There were 16 homologues to Pish, Pi37, and Pi64 that carried an unconventional Walker A (AxxxxGKS). In the Walker B consensus sequence, which follows the typical consensus hhhhD[D/E] (Takken et al., 2006), the variant motif in the Oryza NBS domains was almost always a second aspartate (D). Pi‐ta homologues possessed an unconventional Walker B motif, with a glutamate (E) taking the place of the invariant aspartate (hhhhED). The GLPL/GxPL motif showed greater variation across the NBS domains, with variants including GxPI, HHPL, RLPL, and RSPI, although the proline residue (P) remained strongly conserved across homologues.

Phylogenetic analyses were conducted on the NBS sequences (Figure 5). Domains were additionally assessed for fragmentation, where a domain was considered intact if it possessed at least Walker A and B motifs, an RNBS‐B motif, and a GLPL motif. Genes with multiple NBS domains were also recorded, including those with multiple fragmented or multiple intact domains. The BPH resistance gene BPH18 and blast resistance gene Pib were found to reside within the same phylogenetic clade and largely overlapped in homologues across species. Homologues to these genes were observed to possess two NLR‐like domains, one of which was an intact NB‐ARC domain and the other was truncated. Several homologues to Pi36 and NLS1 possessed more than one intact NBS domain, while Pik‐2 homologues possessed multiple truncated domains. The reference gene for Pi54, along with most of its homologues, lacked a detectable NBS domain base on InterProScan analysis. However, a subset of homologues to this gene exhibited a truncated NBS region, which corresponded to the clade of Pi63‐like sequences. Similarly, homologues to the blast resistance genes Pb1 did not possess a detectable NBS domain, although InterProScan identified an N‐terminal domain in these sequences.

Natural variation within the Pi‐ta2 homologues

There were 15 homologues to Pi‐ta2 identified across the Oryza species, all of which were located on chromosome 12. Interestingly, homologues to Pi‐ta2 were only identified in species with AA and CC genomes, including the diploid CC species O. rhizomatis (Figure S8), as well as in CC sub‐genomes of both BBCC and CCDD tetraploid species, with the exception of O. latifolia (Figure 6A). Phylogenetic analyses of the Pi‐ta2 ARM domains showed that homologues from the AA species formed a distinct clade from the CC genomes (Figure 6). Within the AA Pi‐ta2 ARM domains, wild species O. barthii, O. glumaepatula, and O. meridionalis formed a clade with cultivated O. sativa subsp. japonica and O. glaberrima, while O. longistaminata, O. nivara, and O. rufipogon were grouped with O. sativa subsp. indica. Homologues from the AA genomes possessed a 56 aa insertion at position 361 aa to 416 aa of the ARM domain alignment that was absent in the CC genome group (Figure 6 and Figure S9). The AA species O. meridionalis possessed an additional 53 aa insertion between positions 427 aa and 479 aa of the alignment that was not found in any other species. Structural prediction by PSIPRED indicated the presence of α‐helical‐like elements interspersed with coil elements in both of these regions, consistent with the presence of an additional ARM‐repeat domain (Coates, 2003).

Previously validated allelic variants of Pi‐ta2 conferring varying levels of blast resistance were obtained from a prior study (Greenwood et al., 2024) and aligned with the Pi‐ta2 homologues. Phylogenetic analyses grouped homologues from O. longistaminata, O. nivara, O. rufipogon, and O. sativa subsp. indica into a clade with the Pi‐ta2‐B variants (Figure 6C and Figure S10). Oryza glaberrima, O. barthii, O. glumaepatula, and O. meridionalis were grouped within the broader clade comprising the Pi‐ta2‐A, Pi‐ta2‐C, Pi‐ta2‐D, and Pi‐ta2‐E alleles but did not form a distinct sub‐clade with any of these variants. The O. sativa subsp. japonica Pi‐ta2 homologue formed a clade with the Pi‐ta2‐D alleles. Homologues from the CC genomes formed a separate clade and were phylogenetically distinct from the other variants.

Residues were largely conserved among AA‐genome alleles across the first 500 alignment positions (Figure S10). Beyond this region, homologues within the Pi‐ta2‐B clade showed increased polymorphism relative to the O. sativa Nipponbare Pi‐ta2‐D allele. Residue variations between Pi‐ta2 homologues were frequent in the C‐terminal region, particularly between alignment positions 1131 and 1167 (Figure 6), and were most common among the CC‐genome homologues.

None of the wild Oryza alleles contained the characteristic Pi‐ta2‐A deletion (Greenwood et al., 2024) spanning alignment positions 1134–1140. However, the CC‐genome homologues possessed a distinct 5 aa gap further toward the terminal region at residue position 1152, and the tetraploid O. punctata Pi‐ta2 homologue additionally harbored a 4 aa deletion at position 1131. The O. rufipogon allele contained a terminal deletion resulting in truncation of the sequence at position 1167.

Interestingly, the duplicate gene classification analysis from MCScanX indicated the presence of additional Pi‐ta2‐like homologues across several genomes, suggesting ancestral duplication events within the broader chromosome 12 Pi‐ta2 locus (Table S1). Re‐analysis of these genomes using the more relaxed thresholds applied in the MCScanX workflow identified multiple additional BLAST hits, including homologues within DD sub‐genomes, with most located on chromosome 12 of their respective genomes. The exception was O. nivara, where three homologues were identified on chromosome 7. Phylogenetic analysis showed that these lower‐similarity homologues were placed outside the primary clade containing the conserved AA and CC Pi‐ta2 orthologues, instead forming several deeper paralogous branches (Figure S11). Re‐analysis of the diploid O. punctata (BB) genome (Figure S11) and closely related outgroup species Leersia perrieri (data not shown) using these relaxed parameters also identified several distantly related Pi‐ta2‐like homologues on chromosome 12; however, none were placed within the highly conserved Pi‐ta2 clade.

Discussion

Durable, broad‐spectrum genetic resistance remains a key strategy for managing biotic pressures in rice production systems. The crop wild relatives of rice represent a reservoir of genetic diversity from which novel sources of disease resistance can be identified and integrated into rice breeding programs. In this study, we implemented a set of previously characterized CDR genes to identify homologous candidates across wild and cultivated Oryza species, providing an insight into disease resistance potential within the genus.

We identified 2460 homologues to CDR genes across the Oryza genomes. Within the diploid genomes, the cultivated rices and closely related AA genome species tended to possess higher numbers of CDRHs compared to more distantly related wild species, coinciding with previous suggestions that cultivated rices possess greater numbers of disease resistance genes relative to other species due to artificial selection for pathogen resistance during domestication (Long et al., 2024; Stein et al., 2018). The most evolutionarily distant member of the AA genome group, O. meridionalis, possessed a much lower number of CDRHs relative to other species, potentially reflecting its genetic distance and prolonged geographic isolation from other wild AA taxa (Abdullah et al., 2025; Henry et al., 2010; T. Zhu et al., 2014). It is also possible that differences in CDRH abundance may be influenced by variation in genome assembly and annotation quality, which may affect homologue recovery and subsequent downstream analyses. Notably, annotation pipelines and gene‐prediction approaches differed among the source genome studies (Abdullah et al., 2025; Long et al., 2024; Stein et al., 2018). Nevertheless, both the genome assembly and annotated gene set for O. meridionalis exhibited very high completeness, with BUSCO scores of 99.1% and 98.3%, respectively, suggesting that annotation incompleteness is unlikely to explain its comparatively low CDRH abundance (Abdullah et al., 2025). Additionally, the BLAST thresholds applied in this study were implemented to prioritize high‐confidence, broadly full‐length matches and may therefore have excluded some highly divergent homologues, particularly in the more distantly related species.

Allotetraploid genomes generally possessed higher numbers of CRDHs compared to diploid genomes, reflecting the duplication of gene content following polyploidization (X. Wang et al., 2025). The CC sub‐genomes of allotetraploid species were observed to possess a higher number of CDRHs relative to their BB/DD sub‐genome counterparts. The exception to this was O. punctata, which possessed similar CDRHs across both sub‐genomes, suggesting neoallopolyploidy of the species, as most tetraploid genomes tend to diploidize over time (Feldman et al., 2012; Schiavinato et al., 2021; X. Wang et al., 2025). Previous studies also support O. punctata as the most recently evolved allotetraploid, forming around ∼0.19–0.46 Mya (Zou et al., 2015). Genomic asymmetry is commonly observed in allopolyploid species and the duplicate elimination of certain gene classes, including disease resistance genes, has been observed in other allopolyploid crops (Feldman et al., 2012). The favoring of one particular genome for retention of certain genes is also common; for example, the wheat BB genome harbors almost double the number of RGA loci compared to the AA and DD sub‐genomes (Feldman et al., 2012). In the Oryza tetraploid species, homologues to the dominant genes RYMV3 and Pi‐ta2, conferring resistance against rice yellow mottle virus and rice blast, respectively, were only found on the CC sub‐genomes. Additionally, homologues of the Pi resistance gene pair Pia‐RGA4 and Pia‐RGA5, both of which are required to elicit an immune response (Cesari et al., 2013), were only retained together on the tetraploid CC sub‐genomes. While there is no extant diploid member of the Oryza DD genome group, comparisons between the diploid BB (O. punctata) and CC (O. officinalis, O. rhizomatis, and Oryza eichingeri) genomes indicated no great differences in CDRH types or numbers, duplication patterns, or cluster abundance. This supports the likelihood that disease resistance gene duplicates have been lost from BB/DD sub‐genomes and non‐randomly retained on CC sub‐genomes in the Oryza tetraploids. Previous analyses of broader gene retention across several tetraploid Oryza species have similarly identified greater gene representation within the CC sub‐genomes, indicating that this pattern extends across multiple gene families and is not specific to CDRHs or resistance‐associated genes (Fornasiero et al., 2025).

The majority of CDRHs possessed protein structures representative of the NLR (NBS‐LRR) disease resistance gene class. RLKs (LRR‐RLKs) comprised the second‐most common class, despite accounting for only five of the 56 CDR genes from the original gene set. Homologues to Xa21, Pi65, and Xa26—three RLK genes—were the most commonly identified candidates across the genomes. The prevalence of RLK‐like CDRHs across the genomes was expected, given that these are the largest receptor class in plants and often possess broad and multifunctional biological roles outside of biotic stress response (Belkhadir et al., 2014). A number of RLK disease resistance genes have also been linked to pathways that influence drought tolerance (Shamsunnaher et al., 2020), growth signaling (X. Chen, Zuo, et al., 2014), and cell‐wall strength and integrity (Hu et al., 2017). No homologues were classed as TNLs, aligning with previous suggestions that the TNL gene class was discarded during evolution of the monocot lineage (Akita & Valkonen, 2002; Bai et al., 2002; Cannon et al., 2002; P. Li et al., 2016; Meyers et al., 1999).

There was no apparent correlation between chromosome or genome length and CDRH number. The two largest diploid genomes, O. australiensis and O. meyeriana, possessed comparatively low numbers of CDRHs, consistent with previous findings that their increased genome sizes are primarily driven by transposable element expansion, rather than gene family proliferation (Long et al., 2024; Morrison et al., 2025). Chromosomes 3, 5, and 7 were low in both CDRH numbers and clusters. These chromosomes are generally considered sparse in disease resistance genes compared to other chromosomes (Ning et al., 2020; Pedrozo et al., 2025). Consistent with previous studies, the distal region of chromosome 11 was observed to be a gene‐rich hotspot for disease resistance candidates, harboring the highest numbers of CDRHs, clusters, and duplicated genes across the genomes (J. Liu et al., 2010; J. M. Song et al., 2021). Chromosome 6 also possessed a high number of CDRHs and homologue clusters, which also aligned with expectations from previous findings (J. Liu et al., 2010). Chromosome 2, which harbors the Pib and RLS1 loci in the O. sativa Nipponbare reference, contained the second highest number of CDRHs across the genomes, largely driven by an apparent expansion of Xa21‐like genes located in proximity to RLS1 homologues.

The duplication and subsequent diversification of disease resistance genes in plants can lead to neofunctionalization and expand pathogen recognition, forming a core component of plant immune defense (Leister, 2004). Additionally, duplicated homologues located at different genomic loci may represent valuable candidates for resistance pyramiding, enabling the combination of multiple resistance alleles within a single breeding line. Duplication analyses of CDRHs across the Oryza genome demonstrated that NLR‐like homologues were enriched within the proximal duplicate category, consistent with local duplication events that drive diversification and birth–death evolution in NLR‐like gene families (Leister, 2004; Y. Wang et al., 2011). These duplication processes have also likely facilitated the formation of clustered NLR loci observed across the genomes (Barragan & Weigel, 2021). Homologues to RLK CDR genes were also overrepresented among the tandem and proximal duplicate groups and were often located within homogenous clusters, supporting the role of tandem duplication as a primary driver of expansion in RLK gene families (Shiu & Bleecker, 2003). NLR‐like homologues were also commonly identified as dispersed duplicates, suggesting that transposition or large‐scale genomic rearrangements may have played a role in the expansion of this gene family (Y. Wang et al., 2011). Segmental duplication/WGD events were underrepresented among the NLR‐like homologues, occurring primarily in homologues to nonconventional resistance gene classes that possess additional functions outside of disease resistance, such as SWEET genes (Cannon et al., 2004; L.‐Q. Chen et al., 2010; Z. Chen, Jolley, et al., 2014; Gallino et al., 2018). There was little evidence to suggest that segmental duplication was a primary driver of expansion in RLK‐like CDRHs, despite reports of RLK gene family expansion via this mechanism in Arabidopsis (Shiu & Bleecker, 2003). Singleton CDRHs were predominantly associated with unconventional resistance gene types, with executor R genes being particularly prevalent. Executor R genes do not undergo the same expansion patterns as typical R gene families because pathogen specificity is primarily determined by variation in the promoter region rather than through the gene coding sequence itself (Gu et al., 2005; C. Wang, Zhang, et al., 2015; J. Zhang et al., 2015). Consistent with this, these gene classes were not generally observed within CDRH clusters across Oryza chromosomes either.

Panicle blast represents one of the most severe forms of rice blast disease (X. Xu et al., 2026); however, only a handful of cloned blast resistance genes confer resistance against this phenotype, with the majority characterized for leaf blast (Y. Yu et al., 2022). Consequently, there are ongoing efforts to identify novel resistance genes that target this disease stage. The Pb genes were some of the first panicle blast resistance genes to be cloned (N. Hayashi, Inoue, et al., 2010; Ma et al., 2022; Y. Yu et al., 2022). Analysis of homologues to these CDR genes pointed to several potential sources of allelic variation for resistance to panicle blast. Notably, a putative expansion of Pb3 was identified in the Australian O. rufipogon genome on chromosome 11, where 15 homologues were detected in tandem duplication. Additionally, intact homologues to Pb2, which is truncated and nonfunctional in the O. sativa Nipponbare genome, were detected in several Oryza genomes, which may suggest the presence of multiple functional variants of this gene in wild species.

Analysis of the NBS regions of NLR‐like homologues identified conserved motif sequences and structures across all the domains that coincided with motif order observed in other plant species (Takken et al., 2006; Wan et al., 2013; X. Yu et al., 2021). Previous studies have shown that the RNBS‐A domain in TIR‐encoding NLRs differs from those found in other types of NLR gene (Wan et al., 2013). Indeed, analysis of the NBS domains across the Oryza genus demonstrated an RNBS‐A consensus that aligned more closely to the non‐TIR consensus, xFdxxiwVcVSxxFdxxxIlx, than the TIR‐associated motif, gLvxLQxxLLxxilx (Wan et al., 2013). This aligns with the lack of TNL genes observed during resistance gene classification with RGAugury. Most homologues possessed the typical Walker A/P‐loop motif GxxxxGK[S/T] (Takken et al., 2006), although there were 16 homologues to Pish, Pi37, and Pi64—all closely linked genes found on chromosome 1—that carried an unconventional Walker A that was similar to atypical motifs found in Arabidopsis (Bonardi et al., 2012). In the Walker B consensus sequence, which follows the typical consensus hhhhD[D/E] (Takken et al., 2006), the invariant aspartate residue was almost always conserved, except for in Pi‐ta homologues, which possessed an hhhhED Walker B motif. This atypical variant was similarly identified in the original Pi‐ta gene (Bryan et al., 2000). Pb1 and its associated homologues completely lacked a Walker A/P‐loop motif (Bonardi et al., 2012; N. Hayashi, Inoue, et al., 2010). The loss of the Walker A motif suggests an impairment in nucleotide binding, yet Pb1 is known to confer durable blast resistance (N. Hayashi, Inoue, et al., 2010) and Pb1 homologues were prevalent within the disease resistance hotspot region of chromosome 11 across ten wild Oryza species. This implies that Pb1‐like genes likely do not follow classical activation modes or that nucleotide binding is achieved via alternate mechanisms (Bonardi et al., 2012; N. Hayashi, Inoue, et al., 2010). Collectively, these patterns demonstrate the general conservation of core NBS domain architecture across Oryza NLR homologues, while the presence of atypical motif variants and structures may suggest potential functional diversification within specific gene lineages.

The blast resistance gene Pi‐ta2 was examined in greater detail as a representative unconventional resistance gene, characterized by its ARM‐repeat architecture and atypical mechanism of action (Meng et al., 2020). Allelic variation of Pi‐ta2 is important for its function in resistance against rice blast (Navea et al., 2026). Recently, an allelic variant, Pi‐ta2‐A, has been identified as a board spectrum resistance allele against neck blast (Navea et al., 2026). Here, we evaluated homologues across the Oryza species to identify potential new Pi‐ta2 variants. Homologues to Pi‐ta2 were only identified in AA and CC diploid genomes or tetraploid sub‐genomes. Phylogenetic analyses clustered AA genome homologues with known allelic profiles from cultivated lines (Greenwood et al., 2024), while CC genome homologues formed a separate clade. None of the wild homologues formed a clade with the susceptible Pi‐ta2‐D allele, nor did any form a clade with the highly resistant Pi‐ta2‐A allelic profile (Greenwood et al., 2024; Navea et al., 2026). Three of the wild species and O. sativa subsp. indica formed a clade with the Pi‐ta2‐B allele conferring resistance against isolates Mo15‐23 and Mo15‐24 (Greenwood et al., 2024). While none of the wild alleles carried the deletion characteristic of the resistant Pi‐ta2‐A allele, homologues exhibited increasing polymorphism toward the C‐terminal end of the gene—a region known to influence resistance specificity (Greenwood et al., 2024)—with CC‐genome homologues additionally harboring a 5‐aa gap in this region.

Interestingly, phylogenetic analysis placed the Pi‐ta2 homologue from O. meridionalis, the most distantly diverged member of the AA genome group, in a clade with the cultivated O. sativa subsp. japonica Pi‐ta2 locus, while O. rufipogon, O. longistaminata, and O. nivara formed a distinct, independent clade. The Pi‐ta2 gene phylogeny was therefore discordant with the established Oryza species tree. This coincides with previous phylogenetic analyses on the Pi‐ta2 allele, which similarly designated O. longistaminata, O. nivara, and O. rufipogon into a separate clade from O. meridionalis and most cultivated japonica alleles (H. Zhao et al., 2018). This may be indicative of incomplete lineage sorting of ancestral Pi‐ta2 alleles during early divergence of the AA genome group, historical introgression of alleles between species, or other evolutionary processes, including gene conversion or ancient duplication followed by differential gene loss (Degnan & Rosenberg, 2006; Wambugu et al., 2015).

Re‐analysis of Pi‐ta2 homologues using lowered BLAST homology thresholds identified more divergent Pi‐ta2‐like hits across the genomes, most of which were located proximally to the conserved Pi‐ta2 homologues on chromosome 12. Phylogenetic analysis resolved these sequences into several clades outside of the primary conserved Pi‐ta2 clade, suggesting that they represent deeper paralogues derived from ancestral duplication events (Meyers et al., 2005). The identification of more distantly related homologues in the related grass genus Leersia (Stein et al., 2018) further implies that the origin of the ancestral Pi‐ta2‐like ARM‐repeat gene predates divergence of the Oryza lineage. Notably, while the diploid O. punctata (BB) genome retained several of these distant paralogues, none were placed within the highly conserved Pi‐ta2 clade, suggesting that the closest Pi‐ta2 orthologue was either lost following divergence of the BB lineage, has diverged substantially beyond recognition as a conserved orthologue, or remains unresolved in genome annotations.

In addition to Pi‐ta2, several CDRH groups produced gene phylogenies that were discordant with the expected species relationships. Notably, Pik‐1 and Xa47, two independently identified NLR genes that co‐localize to the same genomic locus, shared nearly identical sets of homologues across the Oryza genus. However, phylogenetic analysis resolved these homologues into distinct clades corresponding to Xa47 and Pik‐1, where members of the AA group were split between two clades: O. rufipogon, O. nivara, and O. glumaepatula grouped with Xa47, while O. barthii and O. longistaminata clustered with Pik‐1. Given the geographic distributions of these species—O. barthii and O. longistaminata in Africa, O. rufipogon and O. nivara in South and Southeast Asia, and O. glumaepatula in the Americas (Long et al., 2024; Stein et al., 2018)—this observed clustering may be consistent with geographically derived evolutionary processes, including potential introgression among regional lineages. However, such processes were not formally tested here, and other explanations for the discordant phylogenetic pattern remain possible. The geographic separation of these lineages also coincides with regional differences in historical prevalence of major rice diseases, with BB particularly prevalent in South and Southeast Asia and rice blast representing a major constraint in West and sub‐Saharan Africa (Quibod et al., 2026; Mutiga et al., 2021). Whether differential pathogen pressure has contributed to this observed phylogenetic pattern remains unclear. The allelic variations observed between the two clades may also warrant further investigation to determine whether they contribute to differential recognition of M. oryzae and Xoo effectors.

Similar instances of phylogenetic discordance were observed in other CDRH groups. For example, homologues to the rice tungro disease resistance gene tsv1 from the CC genome group clustered more closely with AA genome homologues than the BB genome clade. A comparable pattern was also observed in homologues to the blast resistance gene Pb1, where CC genome homologues were more closely related to the reference gene than those from the AA group, despite Pb1 itself being derived from an indica line. These patterns demonstrate that gene phylogenies for several CDR gene classes do not consistently reflect the expected species relationships, with CC genome homologues frequently clustering closer to reference sequences than BB or AA homologues. This discordance could be indicative of a number of evolutionary processes, including potential introgression between and within genome groups, gene conversion or recombination, incomplete lineage sorting, or ancestral duplication followed by differential retention (Degnan & Rosenberg, 2006; Wambugu et al., 2015). Historical introgression represents a plausible explanation, particularly given previous evidence for gene flow among wild AA species and between wild and cultivated rices (Abdullah et al., 2025; Koyanagi et al., 2026). However, it is also possible that some of the observed relationships may also reflect methodological artifacts, including annotation errors and assembly discrepancies.

The resistance conferred by tsv1 against RTSV is believed to be associated with in‐frame mutations within the putative eIF4G susceptibility motif region (AGKSYVVDHP) (Ambita et al., 2026; J.‐H. Lee et al., 2010; Macovei et al., 2018). For this reason, further analyses of tsv1 homologues were conducted to identify potential resistance‐associated alleles. Lee et al. (2010) first demonstrated that missense mutations or deletions affecting the YVV motif within this region conferred resistance to RTSV. This was expanded upon by Macovei et al. (2018), who showed that in‐frame mutations in adjacent amino acids, particularly within the NL motif immediately upstream of the susceptible motif region, could also confer resistance against rice tungro disease. In this study, no tsv1 homologues possessed mutations within the NL motif, however, a homologue identified in O. minuta contained a seven‐residue deletion encompassing the two valines of the YVV motif. Additionally, several CC genome homologues possessed an Ala→Asp substitution at position 1009, while BB genomes/sub‐genomes possessed a Pro→Leu substitution further upstream at position 1006. These previously unreported substitutions represent potential candidates for further investigation, although experimental validation will be required to determine whether they influence RTSV resistance.

Overall, the AA genome group emerged as a promising source of CDRHs, characterized by high homologue abundance and close evolutionary relatedness to cultivated rice. Wild AA rice species belong to the primary gene pool of cultivated rice, meaning that traits can be introgressed relatively easily through conventional breeding approaches (Jena, 2010). CDRHs from these species may therefore represent preferred targets for further investigation. Homologues occurring at independent genomic loci may be particularly useful in the context of gene pyramiding and may also warrant greater attention when co‐localized with established resistance‐associated QTLs. Additional consideration of gene structure, expression, and copy‐number variation could further inform candidate prioritization. The CC‐genome and tetraploid species were also identified as notable potential sources of resistance gene diversity, although introgression from non‐AA genome groups is generally more challenging because of reduced cross‐compatibility, hybrid sterility, and linkage drag (Jena, 2010). This can hinder the stable transfer of desirable alleles into cultivated rice and increase the time and breeding effort required. Nevertheless, several notable disease resistance genes have been successfully integrated from non‐AA wild Oryza, including Pi9 from the tetraploid O. minuta (BBCC) (Qu et al., 2006), BPH18 from O. australiensis (EE) (Jena et al., 2006), and Pi54of (Devanna et al., 2014) and Pi54rh (Das et al., 2012) variants of the Pi54 disease resistance gene from O. officinalis and O. rhizomatis (CC), respectively. Transformation and gene‐editing approaches may provide additional routes for evaluating and introducing promising alleles from more divergent taxa.

CONCLUSION

This study provides an insight into disease resistance gene diversity across the Oryza genus and presents a catalogue of candidate genes for future investigation. It should be noted that sequence similarity to a characterized resistance gene does not equate to function or expression. This is a particularly relevant consideration for rapidly evolving gene families such as NLRs, which frequently undergo duplication, loss, pseudogenization, and functional divergence (Leister, 2004). Consequently, functional validation will be required to determine whether these genes retain relevant molecular functions and confer resistance phenotypes. Nonetheless, the homologues identified in this study provide a useful candidate set for future functional validation and potential application in rice breeding.
