Abstract
This paper deals with the regularization of the sum of functions defined on a locally convex space through their closed-convex hulls in the bidual space. Different conditions guaranteeing that the closed-convex hull of the sum is the sum of the corresponding closed-convex hulls are provided. These conditions are expressed in terms of some ε \varepsilon -subdifferential calculus rules for the sum. The case of convex functions is also studied, and exact calculus rules are given under additional continuity/qualifications conditions. As an illustration, a variant of the proof of the classical Rockafellar theorem on convex integration is proposed. Key words. Biconjugate function, bidual space, regularization of the sum, ε \varepsilon -subdifferential calculus. Mathematics Subject Classification (2020) : 49J45, 49J52, 49J53, 49N15

Introduction

Convexification processes are crucial in optimization. For instance, if we
consider the optimization problem

(\mathtt{P}_{f})\quad\mathop{\mathrm{M}in}f(x)\text{\quad}\mathrm{s.t.}\text{\quad}x\in F,

with objective function
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R\cup\{+\infty\}}
,
X

being a (real) locally convex space, and feasible set
F
, we can think on
the convenience of replacing this problem by an associated
convex
relaxed problem

(\mathtt{P}_{g})\quad\mathop{\mathrm{M}in}g(x)\text{\quad}\mathrm{s.t.}\text{\quad}x\in G,

where
g:\mathbb{R\cup\{+\infty\}}
is a proper convex function and
G
is
a convex set that somehow approximate
f
and
F,
respectively. For
instance,
g
can be the convex or closed convex hull of
f,
and
G
is the
convex or closed convex hull of
F
. This strategy makes sense, both
theoretically and numerically, and one is interested in conditions under
which the optimal values of both problems,
v(\mathtt{P}_{f})
and
v(\mathtt{P}_{g}),
coincide. More precisely, it is a well-known fact (e.g.

[
6
, Lemma 8.3.1]
) that

v(\mathtt{P}_{f})=\mathop{\mathrm{M}in}_{X}(f+\mathrm{I}_{F})=\mathop{\mathrm{M}in}_{X}\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}(f+\mathrm{I}_{F}),

and the desired conditions should guarantee the equality

\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}(f+\mathrm{I}_{F})=(\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}f)+\mathrm{I}_{\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}F},

(1)

which, generally, can be expressed using the biconjugate function (the
definition is given latter):

(f+\mathrm{I}_{F})^{\ast\ast}=f^{\ast\ast}+(\mathrm{I}_{F})^{\ast\ast}.

Doing so, it is also interesting to consider this relaxation in the
possibly larger bidual space
X^{\ast\ast}.
This problem is also
meaningful even when
f
and
F
are convex-lsc and closed convex,
respectively. This interest is justified by the fact that the original
problem may not have solutions, but the relaxed one can be solved in
X^{\ast\ast}
where the bounded sets are relatively weak*-compact (thanks
to the Alaoglu-Bourbaki theorem). This process gives rise to the
so-called generalized solutions; see, for instance,
[
12
]
, which
includes important examples where the closed convex hulls of
f
and
F

coincide with the their closed hulls,
\bar{f}
and
\overline{F}
. As some
authors (e.g.,
[
3
]
,
[
9
]
,
[
20
]
) recognize in their
works the interest of using the biconjugate of functions which are

obtained by several operations which preserve convexity. The authors of

[
1
]
(and references therein) also work in the latter framework, and
establish formulae for the biconjugates of some functions that appear often
in convex optimization, under suitable regularity conditions.

This article is part of the methodology described above for regularizing the
sum of functions defined in locally convex spaces. To this aim we provide different criteria to ensure the equalities

\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}(f+g)=(\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}f)+(\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}g)\text{ and }(f+g)^{\ast\ast}=f^{\ast\ast}+g^{\ast\ast},

(2)

by means of
\varepsilon
-subdifferential calculus rules for the sum.

In the Banach spaces setting, other criteria expressed in terms of exact
and fuzzy subdifferential calculus are also provided. In particular, we
extend some sequential subdifferential calculus rules for the sum of convex
functions introduced in
[
17
,
18
]
for reflexive Banach spaces.

The structure of the paper is as follows. After a section devoted to
notation and preliminaries, Theorem
3
in section
3

establishes different conditions for the fulfillment of (
2
). In
section
4
conditions are given to ensure the validity of (
6
) when both functions are convex but using, this time, exact
calculus rule as in
[
21
, Theorem 2.8.7]
and
[
10
]
. Namely,
Theorem
5
provides some sequential subdifferential calculus
rules for the sum of convex functions in Banach spaces which are not
necessarily reflexive. The last section
5
provides an illustration
of the main results of this work.

2
Notations and preliminary results

Let
X
be a (real) locally convex space (lcs, in brief) and let
X^{\ast}

be its topological dual space. We consider the bidual of
X,
written
X^{\ast\ast},
which is the topological dual of
X^{\ast}
when the
latter is endowed with any locally convex topology
\tau
such that

\sigma(X^{\ast},X)\subset\tau\subset\beta(X^{\ast},X),

(3)

where
\beta(X^{\ast},X)
is the strong topology (for instance, the dual
norm topology in
X^{\ast}
when
X
is a normed space), and
\sigma(X^{\ast},X)
is the
w^{\ast}
-topology. Since
\sigma(X^{\ast},X)\subset\tau
(possibly, strict), we have
X=(X^{\ast},\sigma(X^{\ast},X))^{\ast}\subset(X^{\ast},\tau)^{\ast}=X^{\ast\ast}
, and we can
identify
X
with a linear subspace of
X^{\ast\ast}.
Moreover, we
consider in
X^{\ast\ast}
the topology
\sigma(X^{\ast\ast},X^{\ast}),
also denoted
w^{\ast\ast}
for which
X
is dense in
X^{\ast\ast}.
The topology
\sigma(X^{\ast\ast},X^{\ast})
induces in
X
the weak
topology
\sigma(X,X^{\ast}).
In particular, if
\tau=\sigma(X^{\ast},X),
then
X^{\ast\ast}=X
and
w^{\ast\ast}=\sigma(X,X^{\ast}).
If

X
is a normed space, we denote by
B_{r}(x)
the closed ball centered at
x\in X
with radius
r>0,
and
B_{X}:=B_{1}(\theta).

Given any locally convex topology
\mathfrak{T}
on
X
(or
X^{\ast}
,
X^{\ast\ast},

X
),
\mathcal{N}_{X}(\mathfrak{T})
denotes the basis of
neighborhoods of the origin
\theta
that are convex,
\mathfrak{T}
-closed
and balanced. Given a set
A\subset X
(or
X^{\ast},

X^{\ast\ast}),

by
\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}A
and
\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}^{\mathfrak{T}}A
we denote
the convex and the closed-convex hulls of
A
, respectively, whereas
\mathop{\mathrm{c}l}^{\mathfrak{T}}A
and
\mathop{\mathrm{i}nt}^{\mathfrak{T}}A
are the
respective closure and interior of
A
(we omit the superscript
\mathfrak{T}
when no confusion is possible). The indicator of the set
A
is the
function
\mathrm{I}_{A},
which is
0
on
A
and
+\infty
elsewhere.

We denote
\overline{\mathbb{R}}:=\mathbb{R\cup}\{\pm\infty\}
and
\mathbb{R}_{\infty}:=\mathbb{R\cup}\{+\infty\},
and use the convention
+\infty+(-\infty)=+\infty
and
0(+\infty)=+\infty.
Given a function
f:X\rightarrow\overline{\mathbb{R}},
we define its (effective) domain
\mathop{\mathrm{d}om}f:=\{x\in X:f(x)<+\infty\}
and epigraph
\mathop{\mathrm{e}pi}f:=\{(x,\lambda)\in X\times\mathbb{R}:f(x)\leq\lambda\}.
We say that
f

is proper if
f>-\infty
and
\mathop{\mathrm{d}om}f\neq\emptyset,

lower-semicontinuous (lsc, in brief) if
\mathop{\mathrm{e}pi}f
is closed, and
convex if
\mathop{\mathrm{e}pi}f
is convex. The closed and the closed convex
hulls of
f
are the functions
\mathop{\mathrm{c}l}^{\mathfrak{T}}f
and
\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}^{\mathfrak{T}}f
whose epigraphs are
\mathop{\mathrm{c}l}^{\mathfrak{T}}(\mathop{\mathrm{e}pi}f)
and
\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}^{\mathfrak{T}}(\mathop{\mathrm{e}pi}f),
respectively. We denote by
\Gamma_{0}(X)
the family of proper lsc
convex functions. The conjugate and biconjugate functions of
f
are,
respectively, the functions
f^{\ast}:X^{\ast}\rightarrow\overline{\mathbb{R}}
and
f^{\ast\ast}:X^{\ast\ast}\rightarrow\overline{\mathbb{R}}
defined by

f^{\ast}(x^{\ast}):=\sup_{x\in X}\{\left\langle x^{\ast},x\right\rangle-f(x)\},\text{ }x^{\ast}\in X^{\ast},

and

f^{\ast\ast}(x^{\ast\ast}):=\sup_{x^{\ast}\in X^{\ast}}\{\left\langle x^{\ast\ast},x^{\ast}\right\rangle-f^{\ast}(x^{\ast})\},\text{ }x^{\ast\ast}\in X^{\ast\ast}.

Given
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}
, the
\varepsilon
-subdifferential of
f
at
x\in X
is the set

\partial_{\varepsilon}f(x):=\{x^{\ast}\in X^{\ast}:f^{\ast}(x^{\ast})+f(x)\leq\left\langle x^{\ast},x\right\rangle+\varepsilon\},

with the convention that
\partial_{\varepsilon}f(x)=\emptyset
if
f(x)\notin\mathbb{R}
or
\varepsilon<0.
If
\varepsilon=0
, we recover
the usual (Fenchel) subdifferential of
f
,
\partial f:=\partial_{0}f.
We
also use the notation
\mathrm{N}_{A}(x):=\partial\mathrm{I}_{A}(x)
for
the normal cone to a set
A\subset X
at
x\in X.

The inf-convolution of two functions
f,

g:X\rightarrow\overline{\mathbb{R}}\
is the function
f\square g:X\rightarrow\overline{\mathbb{R}}

defined as

(f\square g)(x):=\inf\{f(x_{1})+g(x_{2}):x_{1}+x_{2}=x\}.

We always have that

\left(f\square g\right)^{\ast}=f^{\ast}+g^{\ast}.

Additionally, if
f,

g\in\Gamma_{0}(X)
are such that
\mathop{\mathrm{d}om}f\cap\mathop{\mathrm{d}om}g\neq\emptyset,
then

(f+g)^{\ast}=\overline{f^{\ast}\square g^{\ast}}^{\sigma(X^{\ast},X)}.

(4)

This fact comes from the Fenchel-Moreau-Rockafellar theorem which states
that, for every function
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\infty},
we have

(f^{\ast\ast})_{\mid X}=\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}f,

provided that
\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}f
is proper and where
(f^{\ast\ast})_{\mid X}
is the restriction of
f^{\ast\ast}
to
X.

For any real extended-valued function
f:X\rightarrow\overline{\mathbb{R}},
we define its extension to
X^{\ast\ast}
by

\hat{f}(x):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}f(x),&\text{if }x\in X,\\
+\infty,&\text{if }x\in X^{\ast\ast}\setminus X.\end{array}\right.

For simplicity, the extension
\hat{f}
is usually denoted as the original
function
f
when no confusion occurs. It is easy to see that
(\hat{f})^{\ast}=f^{\ast}
and, for every
x\in X
and
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}
,

\partial_{\varepsilon}\hat{f}(x)=\partial_{\varepsilon}f(x).

By
\overline{f}^{w^{\ast\ast}}:X^{\ast\ast}\rightarrow\overline{\mathbb{R}}
we denote the
w^{\ast\ast}
-closed hull of (the extension
of)
f:X\rightarrow\overline{\mathbb{R}};
equivalently,

\overline{f}^{w^{\ast\ast}}(x)=\liminf_{y\overset{w^{\ast\ast}}{\rightarrow}x,\text{ }y\in X}f(y):=\sup_{U\in\mathcal{N}}\inf_{y\in x+U}f(y),\text{ }x\in X^{\ast\ast},

(5)

where
\mathcal{N}:=\mathcal{N}_{X^{\ast\ast}}(w^{\ast\ast})
and
y\overset{w^{\ast\ast}}{\rightarrow}x
(usually also written
y\rightharpoonup x
) refers to the convergence in
(X^{\ast\ast},w^{\ast\ast}).
Moreover, the
w^{\ast\ast}
-closed convex hull of (the
extension of)
f
is the function
\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}^{w^{\ast\ast}}f:=\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}^{w^{\ast\ast}}(\hat{f}).
The functions
\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}^{w^{\ast\ast}}f
and
\overline{f}^{w^{\ast\ast}}\
are the supremum of all the lsc convex and all the lsc minorants of
f,

respectively. It is also worth observing that

(\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}^{w^{\ast\ast}}f)^{\ast}=(\overline{f}^{w^{\ast\ast}})^{\ast}=f^{\ast}.

When
f\in\Gamma_{0}(X)
and
\tau=\sigma(X^{\ast},X),
we have
\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}^{w^{\ast\ast}}f=\overline{f}^{w^{\ast\ast}}=\overline{f}^{\sigma(X,X^{\ast})}=f,
due to Mazur’s theorem. If
f
is
proper, the function
\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}^{w^{\ast\ast}}f
is
proper if and only if
f
has a continuous affine minorant. Indeed,
\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}^{w^{\ast\ast}}f
is proper if and only if it has a
continuous affine minorant (see, e.g.,
[
6
, Proposition 3.1.4]
), so
if and only if
f
also has.

3
Regularization of the sum

In this section, given functions
f
and
g
defined on the lcs
X,
we
establish different conditions to guarantee the equality

\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}^{w^{\ast\ast}}(f+g)=(\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}^{w^{\ast\ast}}f)+(\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}^{w^{\ast\ast}}g).

(6)

These conditions rely on the validity of appropriate
\varepsilon
-subdifferential calculus rules for the sum
f+g.
A first condition has
been introduced in
[
3
, Proposition 5.5]
, and uses the following
calculus rule, for
x\in\mathop{\mathrm{d}om}f\cap\mathop{\mathrm{d}om}g
and
\varepsilon>0,

\partial_{\varepsilon}(f+g)(x)=\mathop{\textstyle\bigcap}_{\alpha>\varepsilon}\mathop{\mathrm{c}l}\nolimits^{\tau}\left(\mathop{\textstyle\bigcup}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}=\alpha\\
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}\geq 0\end{subarray}}\partial_{\varepsilon_{1}}f(x)+\partial_{\varepsilon_{2}}g(x)\right).

(7)

Observe that the set between parenthesis is always convex and, so, instead
of
\tau
one can use any other compatible topology for the pair
(X^{\ast},X^{\ast\ast}),
in particular the topology
\sigma(X^{\ast},X^{\ast\ast})
.

In the present work, we provide an alternative condition by means of the
sharper formula,

\partial_{\varepsilon}(f+g)(x)=\mathop{\mathrm{c}l}\nolimits^{\tau}\left(\mathop{\textstyle\bigcup}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}=\varepsilon\\
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}\geq 0\end{subarray}}\partial_{\varepsilon_{1}}f(x)+\partial_{\varepsilon_{2}}g(x)\right).

(8)

Notice that (
8
) reduces to the well-known Hiriart-Urruty &
Phelps formula in the framework of convex analysis (
[
14
]
; see,
also,
[
6
]
,
[
5
]
, and
[
13
]
), provided that
f,

g\in\Gamma_{0}(X)
and
\tau=\sigma(X^{\ast},X)
(in this case
X^{\ast\ast}=X
). Our characterizations will also involve the following inclusion,
for
x\in X
and
\varepsilon>0,

\partial_{\varepsilon}(f+g)(x)\subset\mathop{\mathrm{c}l}\nolimits^{\tau}(\partial_{2\varepsilon}f(x)+\partial_{2\varepsilon}g(x)).

(9)

We first state a useful equivalence of (
6
) with a conjugation
sum rule. This result, which is used later in the proof of Theorem
3
, is a nonconvex counterpart of the corresponding property in
[
2
, Theorem 2.5]
.

Proposition 1

Let
f,

g:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\infty}
be functions
such that
\mathop{\mathrm{d}om}f\cap\mathop{\mathrm{d}om}g\neq\emptyset
and each one
has a continuous affine minorant. Then (
6
) is equivalent to

(f+g)^{\ast}=\overline{f^{\ast}\square g^{\ast}}^{\tau}.

(10)

Proof.
If (
6
) holds, then (
4
) entails

\displaystyle(f+g)^{\ast}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}^{w^{\ast\ast}}(f+g))^{\ast}=((\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}^{w^{\ast\ast}}f)+(\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}^{w^{\ast\ast}}g))^{\ast}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\overline{(\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}^{w^{\ast\ast}}f)^{\ast}\square(\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}^{w^{\ast\ast}}g)^{\ast}}^{\tau}=\overline{f^{\ast}\square g^{\ast}}^{\tau},

and (
10
) follows. Conversely, observe that

\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}^{w^{\ast\ast}}(f+g)\geq\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}^{w^{\ast\ast}}(\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}^{w^{\ast\ast}}f+\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}^{w^{\ast\ast}}g)=\left(\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}^{w^{\ast\ast}}f\right)+\left(\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}^{w^{\ast\ast}}g\right)>-\infty,

and
\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}^{w^{\ast\ast}}(f+g)\leq f+g
, so
\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}^{w^{\ast\ast}}(f+g)
is proper. Then, taking the conjugate
in (
10
) and applying twice Moreau’s theorem in
(X^{\ast},X^{\ast\ast}),
we obtain

\displaystyle\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}^{w^{\ast\ast}}(f+g)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(f+g)^{\ast\ast}=\left(\overline{f^{\ast}\square g^{\ast}}^{\tau}\right)^{\ast}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(f^{\ast}\square g^{\ast}\right)^{\ast}=f^{\ast\ast}+g^{\ast\ast}=(\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}^{w^{\ast\ast}}f)+(\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}^{w^{\ast\ast}}g).

Next, we introduce the following quantity (possibly,
+\infty
) associated
with a function
f:X\rightarrow\overline{\mathbb{R}}
and
x\in\mathop{\mathrm{d}om}f,

\varepsilon_{f}(x):=\inf\{\alpha>0:\partial_{\alpha}f(x)\neq\emptyset\}.

(11)

The following lemma provides some information on this quantity.

Lemma 2

The following assertions hold, for all function
f:X\rightarrow\overline{\mathbb{R}}
and all
x\in\mathop{\mathrm{d}om}f.

(i)
If
f\in\Gamma_{0}(X),
then
\varepsilon_{f}(x)=0.

(ii)
If
f^{\ast}
is proper, then
\varepsilon_{f}(x)<+\infty.

(iii)
If
\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}^{w^{\ast\ast}}f
is proper, then

\inf_{z\in\mathop{\mathrm{d}om}f}\varepsilon_{f}(z)=0.

Proof.

(i)
If
f\in\Gamma_{0}(X),
then
\partial_{\alpha}f(x)\neq\emptyset

for all
\alpha>0.

(ii)
If
f^{\ast}
is proper, then
f
is also proper. Moreover, for
every
x\in\mathop{\mathrm{d}om}f
and
x^{\ast}\in\mathop{\mathrm{d}om}f^{\ast}
, we
have
f^{\ast}(x^{\ast})+f(x)-\left\langle x^{\ast},x\right\rangle\geq 0.
So, every scalar
\alpha
such that
\alpha>f^{\ast}(x^{\ast})+f(x)-\left\langle x^{\ast},x\right\rangle
satisfies
x^{\ast}\in\partial_{\alpha}f(x),
and we deduce
\varepsilon_{f}(x)\leq\alpha.

(iii)
Since
\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}^{w^{\ast\ast}}f
is proper, its
conjugate
f^{\ast}=(\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}^{w^{\ast\ast}}f)^{\ast}
is
also proper (e.g.,
[
6
, Proposition 3.1.4]
). Take
x^{\ast}\in\mathop{\mathrm{d}om}f^{\ast}
and pick any
\rho>0.
Then there exists some
x_{\rho}\in\mathop{\mathrm{d}om}f
such that

f^{\ast}(x^{\ast})+f(x_{\rho})-\left\langle x^{\ast},x_{\rho}\right\rangle\leq\rho;

that is,
x^{\ast}\in\partial_{\rho}f(x_{\rho})
and

\inf_{z\in\mathop{\mathrm{d}om}f}\varepsilon_{f}(z)\leq\varepsilon_{f}(x_{\rho})\leq\rho.

Hence,
\inf_{z\in\mathop{\mathrm{d}om}f}\varepsilon_{f}(z)=0
by the
arbitrariness of
\rho>0.

The following result provides the announced characterizations of (
6
).

Theorem 3

For every functions
f
and
g
as in Lemma
1
, the
following statements are equivalent:

(i)

\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}^{w^{\ast\ast}}(f+g)=(\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}^{w^{\ast\ast}}f)+(\overline{\mathop{\mathrm{c}o}}^{w^{\ast\ast}}g).\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt

(ii)
Formula (
8
) holds, for all
x\in\mathop{\mathrm{d}om}f\cap\mathop{\mathrm{d}om}g
and
\varepsilon>\varepsilon_{f+g}(x).\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt

(iii)
Formula (
7
) holds, for all
x\in\mathop{\mathrm{d}om}f\cap\mathop{\mathrm{d}om}g
and
\varepsilon>0.\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt

(iv)
The inclusion (
9
) holds, for all
x\in X
and
\varepsilon>0.

Proof.
The equivalence between
(i)
and
(iii)
is established in
[
3
, Proposition 5.5]
, and we prove next that
(i)\Longleftrightarrow(ii)
,
(iv)\implies(i)
and
(iii)\implies(iv).

(i)\implies(ii).
Assume that
(i)
holds or, equivalently according to
Lemma
1
,

(f+g)^{\ast}=\overline{f^{\ast}\square g^{\ast}}^{\tau}.

(12)

Fix
x\in\mathop{\mathrm{d}om}f\cap\mathop{\mathrm{d}om}g
and
\varepsilon>\varepsilon_{f+g}(x)
(remember that
\varepsilon_{f+g}(x)<\infty
by Lemma

(ii)
). Take
x^{\ast}\in\partial_{\varepsilon}(f+g)(x)
and a
\theta
-neighborhood
U
in
(X^{\ast},\tau).
Since
\varepsilon>\varepsilon_{f+g}(x)
, there exists some
0<\alpha<\varepsilon
such
that
\partial_{\alpha}(f+g)(x)\neq\emptyset;
that is,
\cup_{0<\delta<\varepsilon}\partial_{\varepsilon-\delta}(f+g)(x)\neq\emptyset.
So,
by applying
[
6
, Proposition 4.1.9]
to the function
\hat{f}+\hat{g}

defined on
X^{\ast\ast}
, we obtain

\displaystyle x^{\ast}

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle\partial_{\varepsilon}(f+g)(x)=\partial_{\varepsilon}(\hat{f}+\hat{g})(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{c}l}\nolimits^{\tau}\left(\mathop{\textstyle\bigcup}_{0<\delta<\varepsilon}\partial_{\varepsilon-\delta}(\hat{f}+\hat{g})(x)\right)=\mathop{\mathrm{c}l}\nolimits^{\tau}\left(\mathop{\textstyle\bigcup}_{0<\delta<\varepsilon}\partial_{\varepsilon-\delta}(f+g)(x)\right).

Hence, we find
\delta\in(0,\varepsilon)
and
y^{\ast}\in\partial_{\varepsilon-\delta}(f+g)(x)
such that
x^{\ast}-y^{\ast}\in U.

Moreover, since (remember (
12
))

\displaystyle(f+g)(x)+\overline{f^{\ast}\square g^{\ast}}^{\tau}(y^{\ast})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(f+g)(x)+(f+g)^{\ast}(y^{\ast})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\langle y^{\ast},x\right\rangle+\varepsilon-\delta,

we find some
z^{\ast}\in y^{\ast}+U
such that

(f+g)(x)+\left(f^{\ast}\square g^{\ast}\right)(z^{\ast})\leq\left\langle z^{\ast},x\right\rangle+\varepsilon-\delta/2<\left\langle z^{\ast},x\right\rangle+\varepsilon.

In other words, there are
z_{1}^{\ast},

z_{2}^{\ast}\in X^{\ast}

such that
z_{1}^{\ast}+z_{2}^{\ast}=z^{\ast}
and

(f+g)(x)+f^{\ast}(z_{1}^{\ast})+g^{\ast}(z_{2}^{\ast})\leq\left\langle z_{1}^{\ast}+z_{2}^{\ast},x\right\rangle+\varepsilon;

that is, rearranging these terms,

The following
corollary shows that the relation above also holds for the
w^{\ast\ast}
-closure in
X^{\ast\ast}
, under the same interiority condition verified in
X
.

Corollary 7

Let
A,

B
be nonempty convex sets such that
A\cap(\mathop{\mathrm{i}nt}B)\neq\emptyset.
Then

\overline{A\cap B}^{w^{\ast\ast}}=\overline{A}^{w^{\ast\ast}}\cap\overline{B}^{w^{\ast\ast}}.

Proof.
According to Corollary
6
, we have that

\mathrm{I}_{\overline{A\cap B}^{w^{\ast\ast}}}=\overline{\mathrm{I}_{A\cap B}}^{w^{\ast\ast}}=\overline{\mathrm{I}_{A}+\mathrm{I}_{B}}^{w^{\ast\ast}}=\mathrm{I}_{\overline{A}^{w^{\ast\ast}}}+\mathrm{I}_{\overline{B}^{w^{\ast\ast}}}=\mathrm{I}_{\overline{A}^{w^{\ast\ast}}\cap\overline{B}^{w^{\ast\ast}}}.

We give a functional counterpart of the previous observation, showing that
the usual interiority conditions applied to the original functions also give
rise to exact subdifferential calculus rules for the regularized functions.

Theorem 8

Let
f,

g:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\infty}
be convex
functions with continuous affine minorants. If
f
is continuous somewhere
in
\mathop{\mathrm{d}om}g
, then, for all
x\in X^{\ast\ast}
and
\varepsilon\geq 0,

\displaystyle\partial_{\varepsilon}\left(\overline{f+g}^{w^{\ast\ast}}\right)(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{\varepsilon}\left(\bar{f}^{w^{\ast\ast}}+\bar{g}^{w^{\ast\ast}}\right)(x)

(24)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigcup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}\geq 0\\
\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}=\varepsilon\end{subarray}}\left(\partial_{\varepsilon_{1}}\bar{f}^{w^{\ast\ast}}(x)+\partial_{\varepsilon_{2}}\bar{g}^{w^{\ast\ast}}(x)\right).

Proof.
The first equality is an immediate consequence of Corollary
6
.
To prove the second one we observe first that
\overline{f}^{w^{\ast\ast}},

\overline{g}^{w^{\ast\ast}}\in\Gamma_{0}(X^{\ast\ast}),
due to
existence of continuous affine minorants for
f
and
g
in
X.
Then,
thanks to Theorem
3
, the functions
\overline{f}^{w^{\ast\ast}}

and
\overline{g}^{w^{\ast\ast}}
satisfy (
7
); i.e., for all
x\in X^{\ast\ast}
and
\varepsilon\geq 0,

\partial_{\varepsilon}\left(\overline{f}^{w^{\ast\ast}}+\overline{g}^{w^{\ast\ast}}\right)(x)=\mathop{\textstyle\bigcap}_{\alpha>\varepsilon}\mathop{\mathrm{c}l}\nolimits^{\tau}\left(A_{\alpha}\right),

(25)

where

A_{\alpha}:=\bigcup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}\geq 0\\
\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}=\alpha\end{subarray}}\left(\partial_{\varepsilon_{1}}\overline{f}^{w^{\ast\ast}}(x)+\partial_{\varepsilon_{2}}\overline{g}^{w^{\ast\ast}}(x)\right).

Take
x^{\ast}\in\partial_{\varepsilon}\left(\overline{f}^{w^{\ast\ast}}+\overline{g}^{w^{\ast\ast}}\right)(x)\
and consider any net
(\alpha_{V})_{V\in\mathcal{N}}\subset]0,1[
such that
\alpha_{V}\downarrow\varepsilon
(here,
\mathcal{N}:=\mathcal{N}_{X^{\ast}}(\tau)
is
partially-ordered by descending inclusions). Hence, for all
V\in\mathcal{N},
(
25
) implies that
x^{\ast}\in\mathop{\mathrm{c}l}\nolimits^{\tau}\left(A_{\alpha_{V}}\right)
and we can find
x_{V}^{\ast}\in A_{\alpha_{V}}
such that
x_{V}^{\ast}\in x^{\ast}+V.
Given any
W\in\mathcal{N},
by taking
V_{0}=W
we see that

x_{V}^{\ast}\in x^{\ast}+V\subset x^{\ast}+W,\text{ for all }V\subset V_{0};

that is,
(x_{V}^{\ast})
is
\tau
-convergent to
x^{\ast}.
Moreover,
for each
V\in\mathcal{N}
there are
\varepsilon_{V,1},

\varepsilon_{V,2}\geq 0,

y_{V}^{\ast}\in\partial_{\varepsilon_{V,1}}\overline{f}^{w^{\ast\ast}}(x)
and
z_{V}^{\ast}\in\partial_{\varepsilon_{V,2}}\overline{g}^{w^{\ast\ast}}(x)
such that
\varepsilon_{V,1}+\varepsilon_{V,2}=\alpha_{V}
and
x_{V}^{\ast}=y_{V}^{\ast}+z_{V}^{\ast}.
By the
current continuity hypothesis, we choose
x_{0}\in\mathop{\mathrm{d}om}g
and
\theta
-neighborhood
U_{0}\subset X
such that

\overline{f}^{w^{\ast\ast}}(x_{0}+u)\leq f(x_{0}+u)\leq f(x_{0})+1,\text{
for all }u\in U_{0}.

Then, for all
u\in U_{0}
(
\subset X
),

\displaystyle\left\langle y_{V}^{\ast},x_{0}-x+u\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\overline{f}^{w^{\ast\ast}}(x_{0}+u)-\overline{f}^{w^{\ast\ast}}(x)+\varepsilon_{V,1}

(26)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle f(x_{0})-\overline{f}^{w^{\ast\ast}}(x)+2.

At the same time, there exists some
M\geq 0
such that

\left\langle y_{V}^{\ast},x_{0}-x\right\rangle\geq-M,\text{ for all }V;

otherwise, since
\left\langle x_{V}^{\ast},x_{0}-x\right\rangle=\left\langle y_{V}^{\ast}+z_{V}^{\ast},x_{0}-x\right\rangle
converges
to
\left\langle x^{\ast},x_{0}-x\right\rangle
and
z_{V}^{\ast}\in\partial_{\varepsilon_{V,2}}\overline{g}^{w^{\ast\ast}}(x),
we get a
contradiction with

\left\langle z_{V}^{\ast},x_{0}-x\right\rangle\leq\overline{g}^{w^{\ast\ast}}(x_{0})-\overline{g}^{w^{\ast\ast}}(x)+1,\text{ for all }V.

Therefore (
26
) reads

\left\langle y_{V}^{\ast},u\right\rangle\leq f(x_{0})-\overline{f}^{w^{\ast\ast}}(x)+M+2,\text{ for all }u\in U_{0},

and, thanks to the Alaoglu-Bourbaki theorem, we may assume that
(y_{V}^{\ast})_{V}
is
\sigma(X^{\ast},X)
-convergent to some
y^{\ast}\in X^{\ast};
hence,
(z_{V}^{\ast})_{V}
is
\sigma(X^{\ast},X)
-convergent to
x^{\ast}-y^{\ast}.
We can also assume that
\varepsilon_{V,1}\downarrow\varepsilon_{1}
and
\varepsilon_{V,2}\downarrow\varepsilon_{2}
with
\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}=\varepsilon.

More precisely, since for all
y\in X
and
V\in\mathcal{N}
we have

\left\langle y_{V}^{\ast},y-x\right\rangle\leq\overline{f}^{w^{\ast\ast}}(y)-\overline{f}^{w^{\ast\ast}}(x)+\varepsilon_{V,1}\leq f(y)-\overline{f}^{w^{\ast\ast}}(x)+\varepsilon_{V,1},

by taking the limit on
V
we infer that

\left\langle y^{\ast},y-x\right\rangle\leq f(y)-\overline{f}^{w^{\ast\ast}}(x)+\varepsilon_{1}.

Thus, knowing that
X
is
\sigma(X^{\ast\ast},X^{\ast})
-dense in
X^{\ast\ast},
we deduce that

\left\langle y^{\ast},y-x\right\rangle\leq\overline{f}^{w^{\ast\ast}}(y)-\overline{f}^{w^{\ast\ast}}(x)+\varepsilon_{1},\text{ for all }y\in X^{\ast\ast};

that is,
y^{\ast}\in\partial_{\varepsilon_{1}}\overline{f}^{w^{\ast\ast}}(x).
Similarly, we show that
x^{\ast}-y^{\ast}\in\partial_{\varepsilon_{2}}\overline{g}^{w^{\ast\ast}}(x)
, and we conclude that

x^{\ast}=y^{\ast}+(x^{\ast}-y^{\ast})\in\partial_{\varepsilon_{1}}\overline{f}^{w^{\ast\ast}}(x)+\partial_{\varepsilon_{2}}\overline{g}^{w^{\ast\ast}}(x).

Thus, we are done because the inclusion “
\supset
” in (
24
) is straightforward.

5
Illustration

To illustrate our previous results, we give the following proposition which
offers an alternative proof of the well-known integration result of the
Fenchel subdifferential of convex functions. Our proof relies on Corollary
8
and Lemma
4
, which were both established
independently of the present integration result. It is worth noting that
while this result was used in the proof of Theorem
5
, the latter
does not enter into the following proof.

Proposition 9

Let
f,

g\in\Gamma_{0}(X)
be such that

\partial f(x)\subset\partial g(x),\text{ for all }x\in X.

Then
f
and
g
coincide up to some additive constant, provided that one of
the following conditions holds:

(i)

f
is continuous somewhere in
X.

(ii)

X
is Banach.

Proof.

(i)
Assume that
f
is continuous at
x_{0}\in\mathop{\mathrm{d}om}f.
Given
x\in X
, we introduce the functions
\varphi,

\psi\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R})
defined as

\varphi(s):=f(x_{0}+s(x-x_{0})),\text{ }\psi(s):=g(x_{0}+s(x-x_{0}),\text{
}s\in\mathbb{R}.

Since
0\in\mathop{\mathrm{i}nt}(\mathop{\mathrm{d}om}\psi),

[
6
, Lemma 4.4.2]

entails

\varphi(s)-\psi(s)=f(x_{0})-g(x_{0})=:c_{0},\text{ for all }s\in\mathbb{R},

which in turn implies
f(x)=g(x)+c_{0}.

(ii)
Assume that
X
is Banach and fix
x_{0}\in\mathop{\mathrm{d}om}f\cap\mathop{\mathrm{d}om}g
together with
r>\left\|x_{0}\right\|.
Given
z\in X^{\ast\ast},
by Theorem
8
(applied twice) and Lemma
4
, we have that

\displaystyle\partial(\bar{f}^{w^{\ast\ast}}+\mathrm{I}_{rB_{X^{\ast\ast}}})(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial(\overline{f+\mathrm{I}_{rB_{X}}}^{w^{\ast\ast}})(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{\textstyle\bigcap}_{U\in\mathcal{N}}\mathop{\mathrm{c}l}\nolimits^{\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\ast}}\left(\mathop{\textstyle\bigcup}_{x\in z+U}\partial(f+\mathrm{I}_{rB_{X}})(x)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{\textstyle\bigcap}_{U\in\mathcal{N}}\mathop{\mathrm{c}l}\nolimits^{\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\ast}}\left(\mathop{\textstyle\bigcup}_{x\in z+U}\left(\partial f(x)+\mathrm{N}_{rB_{X}}(x)\right)\right),

where
\mathcal{N}:=\mathcal{N}_{X^{\ast\ast}}(w^{\ast\ast}).
Hence,
using the current assumption,

\partial(\bar{f}^{w^{\ast\ast}}+\mathrm{I}_{rB_{X^{\ast\ast}}})(z)\subset\mathop{\textstyle\bigcap}_{U\in\mathcal{N}}\mathop{\mathrm{c}l}\nolimits^{\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\ast}}\left(\mathop{\textstyle\bigcup}_{x\in z+U}\left(\partial g(x)+\mathrm{N}_{rB_{X}}(x)\right)\right),

and, proceeding as above, we conclude that

\partial(\bar{f}^{w^{\ast\ast}}+\mathrm{I}_{rB_{X^{\ast\ast}}})(z)\subset\partial(\bar{g}^{w^{\ast\ast}}+\mathrm{I}_{rB_{X^{\ast\ast}}})(z),\text{ for all }z\in X^{\ast\ast}.

Moreover, using Moreau’s theorem, we have that

(\bar{f}^{w^{\ast\ast}}+\mathrm{I}_{rB_{X^{\ast\ast}}})^{\ast}=(\overline{f+\mathrm{I}_{rB_{X}}}^{w^{\ast\ast}})^{\ast}=(f+\mathrm{I}_{rB_{X}})^{\ast}=f^{\ast}\square(r\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\ast})=:f_{r},

and similarly
(\bar{g}^{w^{\ast\ast}}+\mathrm{I}_{rB_{X^{\ast\ast}}})^{\ast}=g^{\ast}\square(r\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\ast})=:g_{r};
hence, for all
x^{\ast}\in X^{\ast},

\partial f_{r}(x^{\ast})=(\partial(\bar{f}^{w^{\ast\ast}}+\mathrm{I}_{B_{X^{\ast\ast}}}))^{-1}(x^{\ast})\subset(\partial(\bar{g}^{w^{\ast\ast}}+\mathrm{I}_{B_{X^{\ast\ast}}}))^{-1}(x^{\ast})=\partial g_{r}(x^{\ast}).

Therefore, since
f_{r}
is (norm-)continuous, by
(i)
there exists some
c_{r}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

f_{r}=g_{r}-c_{r}.

So, taking the conjugate with respect to the pair
(X,X^{\ast})
and using
Moreau’s theorem, we obtain

f+\mathrm{I}_{rB_{X}}=g+\mathrm{I}_{rB_{X}}+c_{r}.

In particular,
c_{r}=f(x_{0})-g(x_{0})=:c_{0}
and we deduce that
f+\mathrm{I}_{rB_{X}}=g+\mathrm{I}_{rB_{X}}+c_{0},
for all sufficiently large

r;
that is,
f=g+c_{0}.

Remark 2

It is worth noting that statement
(i)
in Proposition
9
can also
be obtained from
(ii)
. Indeed, given any finite-dimensional linear
subspace
L\subset X
that intersects the set of continuity points of
f,

we obtain, for all
x\in X,

\partial(f+\mathrm{I}_{L})(x)=\partial f(x)+\partial\mathrm{I}_{L}(x)\subset\partial g(x)+\partial\mathrm{I}_{L}(x)\subset\partial(g+\mathrm{I}_{L})(x).

So, it suffices to apply statement
(ii)
in Proposition
9
to the
restriction of the function
f+\mathrm{I}_{L}
to
L
(which is obviously
Banach).
