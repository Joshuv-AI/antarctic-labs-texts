Tail-robust estimation of factor-adjusted vector autoregressive models for high-dimensional time series
Dylan Dijk, Haeran Cho

Abstract
We study the problem of modelling high-dimensional, heavy-tailed time series data via a factor-adjusted vector autoregressive (VAR) model, which simultaneously accounts for pervasive co-movements of the variables by a handful of factors, as well as their remaining interconnectedness using a sparse VAR model. To accommodate heavy tails, we adopt an element-wise truncation step followed by a two-stage estimation procedure for estimating the latent factors and the VAR parameter matrices. Assuming the existence of the ( 2 + 2 ​ ϵ ) (2+2\epsilon) -th moment only for some ϵ ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \epsilon\in(0,1) , we derive the rates of estimation that make explicit the effect of heavy tails through ϵ \epsilon . Simulation studies and an application in macroeconomics demonstrate the competitive performance of the proposed estimators.

Introduction

High-dimensional time series are routinely collected in many areas such as finance, economics, medicine, engineering, natural and social sciences and for their modelling, vector autoregressive (VAR) models
(
Lütkepohl 2005
)
are popularly adopted
(
Diebold & Yilmaz 2008
;
Gorrostieta et al. 2012
;
Bay et al. 2004
)
. As the dimension of the time series grows, the number of parameters to estimate in a VAR model grows quadratically, and hence estimation quickly becomes a high-dimensional problem.
Therefore, the assumption of sparsity is often made in conjunction with
\ell_{1}
-type regularisation-based methods in the literature
(
Basu & Michailidis 2015
)
.

The sparsity assumption, however, may not be suitable for datasets typically observed in economics and finance, which exhibit strong cross-sectional correlations that cannot be accounted for by a small number of non-zero VAR coefficient values. In addition, theoretical investigation into the consistency of the VAR estimation often assumes that the largest eigenvalue of the spectral density matrix is uniformly bounded across the frequency
(
Basu & Michailidis 2015
)
, which is also at odds with the phenomenon observed in some real data applications;
see Figure
1
plotting the largest eigenvalues of the sample covariance matrices of increasing dimensions obtained from the macroeconomic data example in Section
6
.
Here, as the number of cross-sections increases, the largest eigenvalue of the thus-obtained sample covariance matrix grows linearly with the dimensionality.
Consequently, when modelling the dynamic dependence among a large number of time series, an increasingly popular approach is to adjust for the presence of latent factors driving the strong cross-sectional correlations, before a sparse model is fitted that lends interpretability
(
Fan et al. 2020
;
Fan et al. 2023
;
Fan et al. 2024
;
Barigozzi et al. 2024
;
Krampe & Margaritella 2025
)
.

Figure 1
:
The largest eigenvalue (
y
-axis) of the covariance matrix estimated from
the US macroeconomic dataset analysed in
Section

6
(February 1960 to November 2023,
n=767
) with subsets of
cross-sections randomly sampled 100 times for each given dimension
p\in\{5,\dots,108\}
(
x
-axis).

As well as strong cross-sectional correlations, another common characteristic of high-dimensional time series is heavy-tailedness
(
Fan et al. 2021
)
. It is well-documented that the performance of popular
\ell_{1}
-regularisation methods such as the Lasso, deteriorates in the presence of heavy tails, see e.g.
Wu & Wu 2016
. Similarly, principal component analysis, which is commonly adopted for time series factor modelling, is known to be sensitive to the presence of extreme observations due to heavy-tailedness
(
Kristensen 2014
)
.

To handle the issues arising from heavy tails, tail-robust estimation methods have been studied in the literature on factor modelling
(
He et al. 2022
;
He et al. 2023
;
He et al. 2025
)
, and sparse high-dimensional linear models.
In the latter setting, the Huber loss has been shown to be robust against both heavy-tailedness and outliers
(
Li et al. 2011
;
Fan et al. 2017
;
Wang et al. 2021
)
.
For sparse VAR estimation,
Barbaglia et al. 2020
investigate the maximum likelihood estimator assuming that the innovations are
t
-distributed, while
Qiu et al. 2015
develop a quantile-based robust estimator under the assumption of an elliptical VAR model.
There are also methods based on data truncation, which has been utilised to mitigate the effects of heavy tails in high-dimensional covariance estimation
(
Ke et al. 2019
)
, trace regression under the low-rank assumption
(
Fan et al. 2021
)
or generalised linear modelling
(
Han et al. 2023
)
.
In the context of VAR modelling,
Liu & Zhang 2025
and
Wang & Tsay 2023
investigate the truncation-based estimators via Lasso and constrained
\ell_{1}
-minimisation (a.k.a. Dantzig selector), under a weak assumption requiring the existence of
\nu
-th moment for some
\nu>2
.

In this paper, simultaneously addressing the strong cross-sectional dependence and heavy-tailedness, we study the problem of factor-adjusted VAR modelling under a week moment condition.
We combine the two-stage estimation procedure of
Barigozzi et al. 2024
with the data truncation step, which first obtains the principal component estimator of the factor structure, followed by the Lasso-type estimation of the VAR coefficients.
Assuming the existence of
(2+2\epsilon)
-th moment for some
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, our theoretical investigation fully characterises the performance of the resultant estimators and the choice of the truncation parameter in terms of
\epsilon
; this in particular reveals that as
\epsilon\to 1
, our rates are comparable to those attained in light-tailed situations.
To the best of our knowledge, the existing work on factor-adjusted modelling assume at least the existence of the
4
-th moment or higher.
Additionally, our investigation into the second-step Lasso estimation of the VAR model is of interest on its own;
in the special case of no factors, we derive a rate for the tail-robust
\ell_{1}
-regularised estimator which is shown to be sharper than those derived in the existing literature
(
Wang & Tsay 2023
;
Liu & Zhang 2025
)
.

2
Factor-adjusted vector autoregressive model

A zero-mean,
p
-variate process
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
follows a VAR(
d
) model if it
satisfies

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}=\mathbf{A}_{1}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t-1}+\ldots+\mathbf{A}_{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t-d}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}\,,

(1)

where
\mathbf{A}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
,
1\leq\ell\leq d
, determine how the values of the vector time series depend on its past. The innovations
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}
are modelled as a zero-mean white noise process so that
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}]=\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{s}^{\top}]=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\cdot\mathbb{I}_{\{s=t\}}
for some positive definite matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
.
As the number of parameters in the VAR model grows quadratically with
p
, the assumption of sparsity is commonly found in the literature on high-dimensional VAR modelling.
As noted in the Introduction, however, such an assumption may be inadequate in modelling large time series data exhibiting pervasive cross-sectional correlations, and thus limit the applicability of the model to large time series data arising in economic and financial applications.

Several approaches have emerged where strong cross-sectional and/or serial correlations are captured by a handful of latent factors, a.k.a. factor-adjusted VAR modelling.
In this framework, we observe
\mathbf{X}_{t}=(X_{1t},\dots,X_{pt})^{\top}
,
1\leq t\leq n
, that admits the following decomposition

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{F}_{t}+\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\chi}_{t}+\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t},

(2)

where
\boldsymbol{\chi}_{t}
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}
are latent with
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}
following the VAR model as in (
1
) and
\boldsymbol{\chi}_{t}
is a factor-driven component.
Following
Fan et al. 2023
and
Krampe & Margaritella 2025
, we adopt a static factor modelling approach where
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r}

denotes the matrix of factor loadings, and
\{\mathbf{F}_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
an
r
-variate vector time series of latent factors which is not necessarily second-order stationary.
We mention that
Barigozzi et al. 2024
explore factor-adjusted VAR modelling based on the generalised dynamic factor model (Forni et al.
Forni et al. 2000
,
Forni et al. 2015
).
Throughout we assume that the number of factors,
r
, is fixed, and that the VAR process
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
is stable, i.e. all the solutions of the equation
|\mathbf{\mathcal{A}}(z)|=0
are outside the unit circle where
\mathbf{\mathcal{A}}(z)=\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathbf{A}_{1}z-\cdots-\mathbf{A}_{d}z^{d}
is referred to as the AR matrix polynomial in
z

(
Lütkepohl 2005
)
.

The loading matrix and factors are generally not identifiable since for any invertible matrix
\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
, we have
\boldsymbol{\chi}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{F}_{t}
.
Also, neither
\boldsymbol{\chi}_{t}
nor
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}
are observable, which calls for a condition that ensures their (asymptotic) identifiability.
To this end, we make the following assumptions.

Assumption 1
.

(i)

For all
p\geq 1
,
p^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
is a diagonal matrix with positive and distinct diagonal entries, and
\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf{F}_{t})=\mathbf{I}
for all
t
.

(ii)

There exists a constant
M>0
such that
|\boldsymbol{\Lambda}|_{\infty}\leq M
.

1

(i)
implies that the eigenvalues of
\mathrm{Cov}(\boldsymbol{\chi}_{t})
diverge linearly in
p
, which in turn indicates that the factors are pervasive across the cross-sections of
\mathbf{X}_{t}
.
The identification condition is comparable to that found in
Fan et al. 2013
, and is slightly stronger than those found in
Bai 2003
or
Stock & Watson 2002
.
1

(ii)
is a standard condition, see Assumption B in
Bai 2003
.

Assumption 2
.

(i)

The smallest and largest eigenvalues of
\mathrm{Cov}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t})=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}
are bounded as
0<\Lambda_{\min}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}})\leq\Lambda_{\max}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}})<\infty
.

(ii)

Denoting
\mu_{\min}(\mathbf{\mathcal{A}})=\min_{|z|=1}\Lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{\mathcal{A}}^{*}(z)\mathbf{\mathcal{A}}(z))
and
\mu_{\max}(\mathbf{\mathcal{A}})=\max_{|z|=1}\Lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{\mathcal{A}}^{*}(z)\mathbf{\mathcal{A}}(z))
, we have

0<\mu_{\min}(\mathbf{\mathcal{A}})\leq\mu_{\max}(\mathbf{\mathcal{A}})<\infty
.

Basu & Michailidis 2015
provide lower and upper bounds for
\mu_{\min}(\mathbf{\mathcal{A}})
and
\mu_{\max}(\mathbf{\mathcal{A}})
in terms of the coefficients matrices
\mathbf{A}_{\ell}
’s.

2
ensures that the eigenvalues of the spectral density matrix of
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
are bounded away from zero and finite uniformly in the frequency.
Specifically, let us denote the spectral density matrix of
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
at some frequency
\omega\in[-\pi,\pi]
, by
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(\omega)
, which satisfies

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(\omega)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{h\in\mathbb{Z}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(h)e^{\iota\omega h}

(3)

where
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(h)=\mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t-h}^{\top})
denotes the autocovariance matrix of
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
at some lag
h\in\mathbb{Z}
with
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(-h)=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(h)^{\top}
, and
\iota=\sqrt{-1}
.
Further, define the smallest and largest eigenvalues across all frequencies as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\right)=\underset{\omega\in[-\pi,\pi]}{\operatorname{essinf}}\Lambda_{\min}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(\omega)\right)\text{ \ and \ }\mathcal{M}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}\right)=\underset{\omega\in[-\pi,\pi]}{\operatorname{esssup}}\Lambda_{\max}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(\omega)\right)\,.

Then,
2
implies that there exists some constants
0<m_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\leq M_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
such that

\displaystyle m_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{\Lambda_{\min}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon}\right)}{\mu_{\max}(\mathcal{A})}\leq\mathfrak{m}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right)\leq\mathcal{M}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{\Lambda_{\max}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon}\right)}{\mu_{\min}(\mathcal{A})}\leq M_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\,,

(4)

see
Basu & Michailidis 2015
.
In particular, this ensures that there exists a constant
C>0
satisfying

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(h)\|_{2}\leq C\text{ \ for all \ }h\in\mathbb{Z}.

(5)

Altogether, jointly under
1
and
2
, the leading
r
eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{x}}=n^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}_{t}\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\top}]
,
are diverging linearly in
p
, while the remaining eigenvalues are bounded for all
p\geq 1
.
Consequently,
\boldsymbol{\chi}_{t}
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}
are asymptotically identifiable as
p\to\infty
, through the presence of a gap in the eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{x}}
.
Then, imposing the sparsity on the VAR coefficients is justifiable under the proposed model (
2
), since pervasive cross-sectional correlations are accounted for by the factors and only the remaining idiosyncratic dynamic dependence is captured by the non-zero coefficients of
\mathbf{A}_{\ell},\,1\leq\ell\leq d
.

For the investigation of the tail-robust estimation method to be proposed in the next section, we make the following condition:

Assumption 3
.

There exist some constants
\epsilon\in(0,1)
and
M_{2+2\epsilon}>0
such that

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq t\leq n}\left\{\max_{1\leq i\leq p}\mathbb{E}[|\xi_{it}|^{2+2\epsilon}],\,\max_{1\leq j\leq r}\mathbb{E}[|F_{jt}|^{2+2\epsilon}]\right\}\leq M_{2+2\epsilon}.

Together with
Assumption
1

(ii)
, Assumption
3
implies that
\mathbb{E}[|X_{it}|^{2+2\epsilon}]\lesssim M_{2+2\epsilon}
with
2+2\epsilon\in(2,4)
.
This permits the data to be considerably heavier-tailed compared to the commonly found conditions in the factor modelling literature, which require the existence of the fourth moment
(
Bai 2003
)
or even sub-Weibull-type tail behaviour
(
Fan et al. 2013
)
. In the high-dimensional VAR modelling literature, Gaussianity of the time series is often assumed
(
Basu & Michailidis 2015
;
Han et al. 2015
)
, with the exception of
Wang & Tsay 2023
and
Liu & Zhang 2025
that make conditions analogous to Assumption
3
.

3
Methodology

Under the factor-adjusted VAR model in (
2
), to estimate the coefficient matrices
\mathbf{A}_{\ell},\,1\leq\ell\leq d
, driving the dynamics in the VAR process
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}\}_{t\mathbb{Z}}
, we first estimate the factor-driven common component
\boldsymbol{\chi}_{t},\,1\leq t\leq n
.
To handle heavy tails, we propose to truncate the data prior to carrying out the principal component-based estimation of the factor structure.
The estimated common component is then subtracted from the truncated data to yield the estimator of
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t},\,1\leq t\leq n
, from which the VAR coefficients are estimated via Lasso.
Sections
3.1
and
3.2
describe these steps while regarding the number of factors
r
and the VAR order
d
as known, and Section
3.3
discusses the selection of
r
,
d
and other tuning parameters.

3.1
Data truncation and factor structure estimation

Given a truncation parameter
\tau>0
, we perform the element-wise truncation to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}_{t}(\tau)=(X_{1t}(\tau),\dots,X_{pt}(\tau))^{\top},\text{ \ where \ }X_{it}(\tau)=\text{sign}(X_{it})\cdot(\tau\wedge|X_{it}|),

with which we estimate
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{x}}=n^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}_{t}\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\top}]
by

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{\mathbf{x}}(\tau)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbf{X}_{t}(\tau)\mathbf{X}_{t}(\tau)^{\top}\,.

We discuss the selection of
\tau
via cross validation in
Section

3.3
. Next, to estimate the loading matrix
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
and the factor time series
\mathbf{F}_{t}
under the identifiability condition made in
1

(i)
, we adopt the popularly adopted principal component estimators, as

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}(\tau)=\widehat{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{x}}(\tau)\widehat{\mathbf{M}}_{\mathbf{x}}^{1/2}(\tau)\text{ \ and \ }\widehat{\mathbf{F}}_{t}(\tau)=\widehat{\mathbf{M}}_{\mathbf{x}}^{-1/2}(\tau)(\widehat{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{x}}(\tau))^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{t}(\tau),

(6)

where

\widehat{\mathbf{M}}_{\mathbf{x}}(\tau)
denotes the diagonal matrix containing the leading
r
eigenvalues of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{\mathbf{x}}(\tau)
, and
\widehat{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{x}}(\tau)
the matrix containing the corresponding eigenvectors.
Then we estimate the common component by

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{t}(\tau)=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}(\tau)\widehat{\mathbf{F}}_{t}(\tau)=\widehat{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{x}}(\tau)(\widehat{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathbf{x}}(\tau))^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{t}(\tau),

from which an estimator of the idiosyncratic component is obtained as
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t}(\tau)=\mathbf{X}_{t}(\tau)-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{t}(\tau)
.

3.2
VAR parameter estimation

Let us denote the matrix collecting all the VAR coefficients for the idiosyncratic VAR process, by
\mathbb{A}=[\mathbf{A}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{A}_{d}]\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times pd}
.
We can re-write the VAR model in (
1
) as

\displaystyle\underbrace{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\top}_{n}\\
\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\top}_{d+1}\end{array}\right]}_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\top}_{n-1}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\top}_{n-d}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\top}_{d}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\top}_{1}\end{array}\right]}_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}\underbrace{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{A}_{1}^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{A}_{d}^{\top}\end{array}\right]}_{\mathbb{A}^{\top}}+\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\top}_{n}\\
\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\top}_{d}\end{array}\right].

While
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}
are not observed, with the latent idiosyncratic component estimated by
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t}(\tau)
as described in Section
3.1
, we derive their estimated counterparts as

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}}(\tau)
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}(\tau)
, respectively.

Then, we propose to estimate
\mathbb{A}
via
\ell_{1}
-penalised least squares estimation as

\displaystyle(\widehat{\mathbb{A}}(\tau))^{\top}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{pd\times p}}\frac{1}{N}\left|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}}(\tau)-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}(\tau)\mathbf{M}\right|_{2}^{2}+\lambda|\mathbf{M}|_{1}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{pd\times p}}\text{Tr}\left(\mathbf{M}^{\top}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}(\tau)\mathbf{M}-2\mathbf{M}^{\top}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\tau)\right)+\lambda|\mathbf{M}|_{1}

(19)

where
\lambda>0
is a penalty parameter,
N=n-d
, and the matrices
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}(\tau)
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\tau)
are defined as

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}(\tau)=\frac{1}{N}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}(\tau)^{\top}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}(\tau)\text{ \ and \ }\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\tau)=\frac{1}{N}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}(\tau)^{\top}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Y}}}(\tau)\,.

(20)

The matrices
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}(\tau)
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\tau)
can be regarded as block matrices composed of sample autocovariance matrices of the estimated idiosyncratic process. We denote their respective estimands by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Gamma}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(0)&\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(1)&\dots&\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}(d-1)\\
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}(1)^{\top}&\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}(0)&\dots&\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}(d-2)\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}(d-1)^{\top}&\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}(d-2)^{\top}&\dots&\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}(0)\end{bmatrix}\text{ \ and \ }\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}(1)\\
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}(2)\\
\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}(d)\end{bmatrix}\,.

(21)

The computation of (
19
) can be facilitated by solving the
p
separate sub-problems in parallel, as

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbb{A}}(\tau)=\left[\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{1}(\tau),\dots,\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{p}(\tau)\right]^{\top}\text{ \ with \ }\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{i}(\tau)=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{pd}}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}(\tau)\boldsymbol{\beta}-2\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\tau)_{(i)}+\lambda|\boldsymbol{\beta}|_{1},\,1\leq i\leq p\,.

3.3
Tuning parameter selection

We propose to perform a cross validation (CV) procedure to select the truncation parameter
\tau
.
We denote the set of equi-distanced candidate
values by
\mathcal{G}=\{\tau_{j},\,1\leq j\leq J:\,\tau_{1}<\ldots<\tau_{J}\}
, where
\tau_{1}=\text{median}_{i,t}|X_{it}/\widehat{\sigma}_{i}|
, and
\tau_{J}=\max_{i,t}|X_{it}/\widehat{\sigma}_{i}|
, and
\widehat{\sigma}_{i}
denotes the median absolute deviation of
\{X_{it}\}_{t=1}^{n}
adopted for variable standardisation.
For the numerical results in this paper we set
J=60
.
We split the data into two folds, the first and second half, with their sets of indices denoted by
\mathcal{I}_{1}=\{1,\dots,\lfloor n/2\rfloor\}
, and
\mathcal{I}_{2}=\{\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1,\dots,n\}
. Then, for each
\tau\in\mathcal{G}
, the data are truncated based on a scaled version of
\tau

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}_{t}(\tau)=(X_{1t}(\tau_{1}),\dots,X_{pt}(\tau_{p}))^{\top}\text{ \ where \ }\tau_{i}=\widehat{\sigma}_{i}\cdot\tau\,,

and the following cross validation score is obtained

\displaystyle\text{CV}(\tau)=\max_{0\leq h\leq d}\left\{\left|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{\mathbf{x},\mathcal{I}_{1}}(\tau,h)-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{\mathbf{x},\mathcal{I}_{2}}(\infty,h)\right|_{\infty}\,+\,\left|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{\mathbf{x},\mathcal{I}_{2}}(\tau,h)-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{\mathbf{x},\mathcal{I}_{1}}(\infty,h)\right|_{\infty}\right\}\,,

(22)

where
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{\mathbf{x},\mathcal{I}}(\tau,h)
denotes the sample autocovariance matrix at lag
h
obtained from
\{\mathbf{X}_{t}(\tau),\,t\in\mathcal{I}\}
, with
\tau=\infty
indicating that no truncation is performed.
As shown in
Section

4
, the theoretical properties of the proposed estimator
\widehat{\mathbb{A}}(\tau)
are inherited from those of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{\mathbf{x}}(\tau,h)
, which motivates the CV measure in (
22
).
The final choice of
\tau
is then given by the minimiser of the cross validation measure score, as
\widehat{\tau}=\argmin_{\tau\in\mathcal{G}}\text{CV}(\tau)
.

When
p
is finite, the Akaike information criterion
(
Akaike 1998
)
and Bayesian information criterion
(
Schwarz 1978
)
are frequently adopted for the selection of the VAR order
d
, and there also exists likelihood ratio tests
(
Tiao & Box 1981
)
.
The extended Bayesian information criterion of
Chen & Chen 2008
explores the applicability of an information criterion to high dimensions where
p\to\infty
.
In the context of factor-adjusted VAR modelling,
Barigozzi et al. 2024
and
Krampe & Margaritella 2025
propose to adopt CV for joint selection of the number of factors, together with the VAR order.
While these approaches have achieved some success, their theoretical and numerical performance is still to be fully understood in high dimensions.
In our simulation studies we assume that the VAR order
d
is known, focusing on the investigation into the proposed tail-robust estimation strategy.
In our data application in
Section

6
, we consider a range of VAR order when performing a forecasting exercise and investigate the impact of its choice.

For the selection of the factor number, the information criterion-based approach of
Bai & Ng 2002
is popularly adopted.
We also refer to
Onatski 2010
,
Alessi et al. 2010
,
Ahn & Horenstein 2013
and
Fan et al. 2022
for alternative factor number estimators.
In Section
5
, we treat
r
as known, to separate the issues arising from the factor number selection from the investigation into the usefulness of the proposed truncation-based method. In a forecasting exercise performed on macroeconomic data in
Section

6
, we utilise the estimator of
Bai & Ng 2002
following the practice of
McCracken & Ng 2016
analysing the same dataset.

For the selection of
\lambda
in (
19
), we use the CV-based approach which is implemented in the R package
glmnet

(
Friedman et al. 2010
)
.

4
Theoretical results

In addition to the assumptions made in
Section

2
for model identifiability and characterising the tail behaviour, we make the following assumptions on the serial dependence in
\{\mathbf{X}_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
.
For any stochastic process
\left\{\mathbf{Y}_{t}\right\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
, we denote the
\alpha
-mixing coefficient as

\displaystyle\alpha^{\mspace{-1mu}\mathbf{Y}}\mspace{-2mu}(\ell)=\sup_{s\in\mathbb{Z}}\,\alpha\left(\left\{\mathbf{Y}_{t}\right\}_{t=-\infty}^{s},\left\{\mathbf{Y}_{t}\right\}_{t=s+\ell}^{\infty}\right),\,\text{where}

\displaystyle\alpha\left(\left\{\mathbf{Y}_{t}\right\}_{t=-\infty}^{s},\left\{\mathbf{Y}_{t}\right\}_{t=r}^{\infty}\right)=\sup|\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)-\mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}(B)|,

with the supremum taken over all events
A\in\sigma\left(\left\{\mathbf{Y}_{t}\right\}_{t=-\infty}^{s}\right)
and
B\in\sigma\left(\left\{\mathbf{Y}_{t}\right\}_{t=r}^{\infty}\right)
, and
\sigma(\cdot)
is the sigma field generated by the process.

Assumption 4
.

There exists some constant
c\in(0,\infty)
such that:

(i)

\{\mathbf{F}_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
is
\alpha
-mixing with the coefficients decaying as
\alpha^{\mspace{-1mu}\mathbf{F}}\mspace{-2mu}(m)\leq\exp(-2cm)
.

(ii)

For any
1\leq i,j\leq p
, the bivariate process
\{(\xi_{it},\xi_{jt})\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
is
\alpha
-mixing with the coefficients decaying as
\max_{1\leq i,j\leq p}\alpha^{\mspace{-1mu}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}_{ij}\mspace{-2mu}(m)\leq\exp(-2cm)
.

Mixing-type short range dependence conditions such as
4
are found both in the factor modelling
(
Fan et al. 2013
)
and the tail-robust VAR modelling
(
Wang & Tsay 2023
)
literature.

4

(ii)
places the
\alpha
-mixing condition on the bivariate process
\{(\xi_{it},\xi_{jt})\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
, which follows if
\alpha
-mixing is assumed on the entire vector process
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
.
We take the former approach as the latter requires care in high dimensions with
p\to\infty

(
Han & Wu 2023
)
.
Finally, we assume:

Assumption 5
.

The processes
\{\mathbf{F}_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
and
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
are independent.

Jointly,
4
and
5
imply that the bivariate processes formed by truncated data,
\{(X_{it}(\tau),X_{ij}(\tau))\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
, are also strongly mixing.

Proposition

1
and
Proposition

2
show that the (lagged) second-moment matrix of
\mathbf{X}_{t}
estimated with the element-wise data truncation and consequently, the estimator of the autocovariance matrix of
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}
, are consistent even in ultra-high dimensions, under the weak conditions requiring the existence of the
(2+2\epsilon)
-th moment only (
3
). Let us write for some
h\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{x}}(h)=\frac{1}{n-h}\sum_{t=h+1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbf{X}_{t}\mathbf{X}_{t-h}^{\top}\right)\text{ \ and \ }\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{\mathbf{x}}(\tau,h)=\frac{1}{n-h}\sum_{t=h+1}^{n}\mathbf{X}_{t}(\tau)\mathbf{X}_{t-h}^{\top}(\tau)\,.

Proposition 1
.

Suppose that
1
,
3
,
4
and
5
, hold, and let us set

\displaystyle\tau\asymp\left[\frac{n\widetilde{M}_{2+2\epsilon}}{\log(p)\log^{2}(n)}\right]^{\frac{1}{2+2\epsilon}}\,

(23)

with
\widetilde{M}_{2+2\epsilon}\in(0,\infty)
that depends only on
r
,
M
and
M_{2+2\epsilon}
, where
M
and
M_{2+2\epsilon}
are defined in
1

(ii)
and
3
, respectively.
Then for any fixed
h\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{\mathbf{x}}(\tau,h)-\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{x}}(h)\right|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=O_{p}\left(\left(\frac{\log(p)\log^{2}(n)\widetilde{M}_{2+2\epsilon}^{\frac{1}{\epsilon}}}{n}\right)^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}\right).

Proposition 2
.

Suppose that
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
and
5
hold, and
recall
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}(\tau)
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\tau)
in (
20
) and their population counterparts
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
in (
21
).
Then,
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{n,p})\rightarrow 1
as
n,p\rightarrow\infty
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{n,p}=\left\{\max\left\{\left|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}(\tau)-\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\right|_{\infty},\left|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\tau)-\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right|_{\infty}\right\}\leq C\left(\left(\frac{\log(p)\log^{2}(n)\widetilde{M}_{2+2\epsilon}^{\frac{1}{\epsilon}}}{n}\right)^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}\,\vee\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\right)\right\}\;,

for some large enough constant
C>0
, and with
\tau
chosen as in (
23
).

Let us measure the sparsity of
\mathbb{A}=[\mathbf{A}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{A}_{d}]\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times pd}
with
s_{0,j}=\left|\mathbb{A}_{j}\right|_{0}
,
s_{\text{max}}=\max_{1\leq j\leq p}s_{0,j}
and
s_{0}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}s_{0,j}
, each representing the number of non-zero coefficients in the
j
-th row of
\mathbb{A}
, their maximum over

and the total number of non-zero coefficients in
\mathbb{A}
, respectively.
Together with
Proposition

2
, the following

3
establishes the consistency of
\widehat{\mathbb{A}}(\tau)
in (

).

Proposition 3
.

Suppose that
2
holds. In addition, assume that

\displaystyle Cs_{\text{max}}\left\{\left(\frac{\log(p)\log^{2}(n)\,\widetilde{M}_{2+2\epsilon}^{\frac{1}{\epsilon}}}{n}\right)^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}\vee\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\right\}\leq\frac{\pi m_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}{16}\,,

(24)

where
C
is the same constant as in
\mathcal{E}_{n,p}
in
Proposition

, and
m_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
is defined in (

Let us set

\lambda\asymp(\|\mathbb{A}\|_{\infty}+1)\left(\left(\frac{\log(p)\log^{2}(n)\,\widetilde{M}_{2+2\epsilon}^{\frac{1}{\epsilon}}}{n}\right)^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}\vee\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\right),

where
\|\mathbb{A}\|_{\infty}=\max_{1\leq i\leq p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}|A_{\ell,ij}|
.
Then on
\mathcal{E}_{n,p}
, the following statements hold deterministically:

\displaystyle|\widehat{\mathbb{A}}(\tau)-\mathbb{A}|_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{s_{0}}(\|\mathbb{A}\|_{\infty}+1)\left(\left(\frac{\log(p)\log^{2}(n)\widetilde{M}_{2+2\epsilon}^{\frac{1}{\epsilon}}}{n}\right)^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}\,\vee\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\right)\,,

(25)

\displaystyle|\widehat{\mathbb{A}}(\tau)-\mathbb{A}|_{1}

\displaystyle\lesssim s_{0}(\|\mathbb{A}\|_{\infty}+1)\left(\left(\frac{\log(p)\log^{2}(n)\widetilde{M}_{2+2\epsilon}^{\frac{1}{\epsilon}}}{n}\right)^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}\,\vee\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\right)\,,

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\mathbb{A}}(\tau)-\mathbb{A}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\lesssim s_{\text{max}}(\|\mathbb{A}\|_{\infty}+1)\left(\left(\frac{\log(p)\log^{2}(n)\widetilde{M}_{2+2\epsilon}^{\frac{1}{\epsilon}}}{n}\right)^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}\,\vee\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\right)\,.

The proof of
Proposition

3
proceeds by showing that on the event
\mathcal{E}_{n,p}
, the matrix
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}(\tau)
in (
20
)
satisfies the so-called restricted eigenvalue condition under (
24
), along with a deterministic bound on the deviation
|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\tau)_{(j)}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}(\tau)\mathbb{A}_{j}|_{\infty}
for all
1\leq j\leq p
.
The condition (
24
) on the sparsity requires that for each row equation in (
1
), the number of non-zero coefficients is bounded as
O\big((\tfrac{n}{\log(p)\log^{2}(n)})^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}\wedge\sqrt{p}\big)
.

Remark 4.1
.

Under the model (
2
), if there are no factors in the model, i.e.
\boldsymbol{\chi}_{t}=\textbf{0}
, then the rate in
Proposition

2
improves from
\max\big\{\big(\log(p)\log^{2}(n)\widetilde{M}_{2+2\epsilon}^{1/\epsilon}/n\big)^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}},p^{-1/2}\big\}
to
\big(\log(p)\log^{2}(n)\widetilde{M}_{2+2\epsilon}^{1/\epsilon}/n\big)^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}
. Consequently, the rate of estimation for
\widehat{\mathbb{A}}(\tau)
reported in
Proposition

3
, also improves analogously, e.g. the
\ell_{2}
-estimation error in (
25
) becomes

|\widehat{\mathbb{A}}(\tau)-\mathbb{A}|_{2}\lesssim\sqrt{s_{0}}(\|\mathbb{A}\|_{\infty}+1)\left(\frac{\log(p)\log^{2}(n)\widetilde{M}_{2+2\epsilon}^{\frac{1}{\epsilon}}}{n}\right)^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}

which, as
\epsilon\to 1
, becomes comparable (up to log factors) to the rate available under Gaussianity, see e.g.
Basu & Michailidis 2015
and
Han et al. 2015
.
Combined with data truncation, the constrained
\ell_{1}
-minimisation estimator of
Wang & Tsay 2023
attains the rate

\sqrt{s_{0}}\|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\|_{\infty}\big(\log(p)\log^{2}(n)M_{2+2\epsilon}^{1/\epsilon}/n\big)^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}
, under a comparable moment condition while assuming the boundedness of
\|\mathbb{A}\|_{\infty}
and the strong mixingness imposed on the
p
-variate process
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
.
We note that recalling the matrix
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{pd\times pd}
defined in (
21
), the term
\|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{-1}\|_{\infty}
may grow with
p
.
Taking an alternative approach to characterising the decay of the serial dependence via a spectral decay index,
Liu & Zhang 2025
derive the rate

\sqrt{s_{0}}(M_{2+2\epsilon})^{\frac{1}{2+2\epsilon}}(\|\mathbb{A}\|_{\infty}+1)\big(\log(p)/n\big)^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+2\epsilon}}
;
by comparison, our analysis returns an improved rate of estimation in its dependence on the index
\epsilon
.

5
Simulation studies

5.1
Set-up

We consider the following factor-adjusted VAR process of order
1
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{F}_{t}+\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}\text{ \ with \ }\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}=\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t-1}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}\,.

For each of the settings described below,
we generate 200 realisations and vary the dimensions of the data as
(n,p)\in\{(50,100),(50,200),(100,200),(200,50),(200,100)\}
.

Generation of
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}
.

We consider the following settings for the VAR coefficients
\mathbf{A}=[A_{ij}]
.

(V1)

Banded.

A_{ij}=0.5\cdot\mathbb{I}_{\{i=j\}}+\text{sign}(i-j)\cdot 0.4\cdot\mathbb{I}_{\{|i-j|=1\}}
. Taken from
Wang & Tsay 2023
.

(V2)

Erdös-Rényi random graph.
We generate
\mathcal{E}
as the edge set of a randomly generated directed graph with the link probability
p^{-1}
, and set
\mathbf{A}_{0}=[A_{0,ij}]
with
A_{0,ij}=0.275\cdot\mathbb{I}_{\{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}\}}
.
Then we obtain
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{A}_{0}/\|\mathbf{A}_{0}\|_{2}
.
Taken from
Barigozzi et al. 2024
.

For the innovation, we set
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{1/2}\mathbf{v}_{t}
where the entries of
\mathbf{v}_{t}=(v_{1t},\ldots,v_{pt})^{\top}
are i.i.d. random variables with zero mean and unit variance generated as below.

(I1)

v_{it}\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

(I2)

\sqrt{\tfrac{\nu}{\nu-2}}\cdot v_{it}\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}t_{\nu}
with
\nu\in\{2.1,3,4\}
.

(I3)

\sqrt{\exp(2)-\exp(1)}\cdot(v_{it}+\exp(1/2))\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}\text{lognormal}(0,1)

With
(I2)
and
(I3)
, we explore the non-Gaussian scenarios.
Under
(I2)
, the random variables
v_{it}
are symmetric heavy tailed random variables with finite
k
-th moments only for
k<\nu
, and hence do not have a finite fourth moment.
In the main text, we focus on the case of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}=\mathbf{I}
while in Appendix
A
, we consider alternative scenarios.

Generation of
\boldsymbol{\chi}_{t}
.

We consider the following settings:

(F1)

Setting
r=3
, the loadings are generated as
\lambda_{ij}\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, where
\lambda_{ij}
denotes the
(i,j)
-th entry of the loading matrix
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
.
The factor time series is generated as a VAR(
1
) process
\mathbf{F}_{t}=\mathbf{D}\mathbf{F}_{t-1}+\mathbf{u}_{t}
, with
\mathbf{D}=0.7\cdot\mathbf{D}_{0}/\Lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{D}_{0})
where the diagonal entries of
\mathbf{D}_{0}
are i.i.d.
\mathcal{U}[0.5,0.8]
, and the off-diagonal ones are from
\mathcal{U}[0,0.3]
.
The distribution of
\mathbf{u}_{t}
follows that of
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}
in
(I1)
–
(I3)
.
Once
\chi^{0}_{it}=\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{F}_{t}
is generated, we re-scale it such that empirical variance of
\chi_{it}
matches that of
\xi_{it}
.
Motivated by
Fan et al. 2013
and
Barigozzi et al. 2024
.

(F2)

No common component.

\boldsymbol{\chi}_{t}=\mathbf{0}
.

As noted in Section
3.3
, we assume that the true number of factors
r
and the VAR order
d
are known when estimating both the factor model and the transition matrix. This allows us to separate issues of factor and order selection from the investigation into the performance of the proposed tail-robust estimation method in combination with the CV-based selection of
\tau
.

6
Real data application

We use the proposed tail-robust estimation method for the factor-adjusted VAR model, to forecast a large set of major US macroeconomic variables taken from the Federal Reserve Economic Data monthly database (FRED-MD).
FRED-MD is maintained by the St. Louis Federal Reserve Bank, with monthly updates of the data released; we refer to
McCracken & Ng 2016
for further description of the dataset including the suggested variable-specific transformations for stationarity.
We consider the variables with no missing values and standardise each series using its mean absolute deviation. In the end, the dataset contains the observations from 108 variables (
p=108
) between 1960-02 and 2023-11 (
n=767
).
FRED-MD has popularly been analysed in the factor modelling literature as a benchmark dataset, and the presence of a handful of factors driving the pervasive cross-sectional correlations is evident from
Figure

1
.
Also, noticing that outliers have been manually removed from the dataset prior to further analysis in the literature
(
Kock et al. 2025
)
, the use of our tail-robust method is well-motivated.
Upon truncating the data, with
\tau
selected as described in
Section

3.3
, we select the number of factors
r
using the three information criteria of
Bai & Ng 2002
, all of which return
7
as the factor number.

We perform a rolling window-based forecasting exercise with the window size
T=120
(corresponding to
10
years of data).
At each
t\geq T
, we estimate the model in (
2
) using the preceding
T
observations as described in Section
3
where, for the VAR order, we consider
d\in\{1,\ldots,5\}
; we denote all such estimators with the subscripts
t
and
T
.
Then, we generate the
h
-step ahead forecast of
\mathbf{X}_{t+h}
as described in
Barigozzi et al. 2024
.
Specifically, for the common component, we estimate the best linear predictor of
\boldsymbol{\chi}_{t+h}
given the preceding
T
observations, as

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\chi}}}_{t+h\mid T}(\tau)=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{\boldsymbol{\chi},t\mid T}(\tau,h)\widehat{\mathbf{E}}_{t\mid T}(\tau)(\widehat{\mathbf{M}}_{t\mid T}(\tau))^{-1}(\widehat{\mathbf{E}}_{t\mid T}(\tau))^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{t}(\tau)\,,

where
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{\boldsymbol{\chi},t\mid T}(\tau,h)=T^{-1}\sum_{u=t-T+h+1}^{t}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{u,t\mid T}(\tau)\widehat{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{u-h,t\mid T}(\tau)^{\top}
denotes an estimator of the autocovariance of
\boldsymbol{\chi}_{t}
at lag
h
, the matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}_{t\mid T}(\tau)
contains the leading
r
eigenvalues of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{\mathbf{x},t\mid T}(\tau,0)
on its diagonal,
\widehat{\mathbf{E}}_{t\mid T}(\tau)
contains the corresponding eigenvectors, and

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{u,t\mid T}(\tau)=\widehat{\mathbf{E}}_{t\mid T}(\tau)(\widehat{\mathbf{E}}_{t\mid T}(\tau))^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{t}(\tau)
for
u\in\{t-T+1,\ldots,t\}
.
The forecast of the idiosyncratic component is computed as

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+h\mid T}(\tau)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{\ell,t\mid T}(\tau)\widehat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+h-\ell,t\mid T}(\tau),

where
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+h-\ell,t\mid T}(\tau)=\mathbf{X}_{t+h-\ell}(\tau)-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{t+h-\ell,t\mid T}(\tau)
for
\ell\geq h
, and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+h-\ell,t\mid T}(\tau)=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+h-\ell\mid T}(\tau)
otherwise.
Then, the combined forecast is given by

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{t+h\mid T}(\tau)=\widehat{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\chi}}}_{t+h\mid T}(\tau)+\widehat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+h\mid T}(\tau)\,.

We carry out the exercise with the forecasting horizon
h=1
, and compute the absolute error of the forecasts for each individual series.
For comparison, we consider the standard approach without any truncation (i.e.
\tau=\infty
). Denoting the forecast errors for the
i
-th series from the two approaches, by

\displaystyle\text{FE}_{it}(\tau)=\left|\widehat{X}_{i,t+1\mid T}(\tau)-X_{i,t+1}\right|\text{ \ and \ }\text{FE}_{it}(\infty)=\left|\widehat{X}_{i,t+1\mid T}(\infty)-X_{i,t+1}\right|\,,

respectively,
we investigate whether they perform significantly differently at any point by means of the fluctuation test of
Giacomini & Rossi 2010
(implemented in the
murphydiagram
package,
Jordan & Krueger 2019
,
Jordan & Krueger 2019
). This test addresses the fact that one forecasting method may outperform another only on certain intervals, which global performance measures can miss. Formally, it is a two-sided test with the null hypothesis that the expected losses of the competing forecasts are equal on all of the windows. A series of test statistics is computed as a scaled rolling average of the process
\text{FE}_{it}(\tau)-\text{FE}_{it}(\infty)
over the window of length
\lfloor\mu(n-T)\rfloor
for some
\mu\in(0,1)
.
Following the authors’ recommendation of using
\mu\geq 0.2
, we set
\mu=0.3
.

We observe that irrespective of the VAR order number
d
, significant improvements are observed for a large number of variables when the data are suitably truncated;
for simplicity, we present the results for
d=1
.
Then, for the 54 out of the 108 variables, the fluctuation test favours the forecasting performance from the proposed tail-robust approach, while for three variables, the opposite is the case; for the remaining variables, no significant difference is observed.

:
Time path of the fluctuation test statistics for six variables, which are scaled rolling averages of

; we set the parameter
\mu=0.3
which determines the window. The large negative values correspond to when the proposed truncation-based method outperforms the standard method without any truncation. The horizontal lines are the critical values.

In
Figure

, we visualise the outputs from the fluctuation test for a selection of six variables. These six variables are chosen as they display the differing relative forecasting performance between the two approaches over three distinct periods. The first row of
Figure

5
shows the results from the federal funds rate (FEDFUNDS) and the yield for a 3-month treasury bill (TB3MS), both of which are the measures of short-term interest rates. The two plots show that the tail-robust estimator is to be favoured in the earlier period of the dataset, where the Fedfunds reached a historic high in the 1980s to combat inflation
(
Federal Reserve History 2013
)
. The second row presents the results for Moody’s Seasoned Baa Corporate Bond Yield (BAA), which is the average yield of lower investment-grade bonds, and the REALLN variable which measures the amount of loans that commercial banks are lending for real estate purposes. These variables changed drastically during the 2007–09 financial crisis, coinciding with the periods over which the truncation-based forecasts are favoured against the non-robust counterparts. The final row of plots considers the unemployment rate (UNRATE) and the M1 money supply (M1SL), both of which had sharp increases in response to the COVID-19 pandemic.
The truncation-based method is successful in minimising the effect of these extreme events on the forecasting model estimation.

7
Conclusions

We propose a tail-robust estimation method for factor-adjusted VAR modelling of high-dimensional time series, which simultaneously accounts for pervasive correlations in the data as well as idiosyncratic interconnectivity between the variables.
We utilise data truncation for achieving tail-robustness, and show its efficacy theoretically with the rates of estimation explicitly dependent on the moment condition.
In the special case of no factors present, our estimator of the VAR coefficients is shown to perform favourably compared to the state-of-the art.
Simulation studies and a forecasting exercise on the real data confirm that the good performance of the proposed estimators. All of the R scripts used to generate the numerical results are provided in the following GitHub repository:
https://github.com/DylanDijk/truncFVAR
.

Proofs

We define here notation that is used within the proofs.
Throughout, we denote the autocovariance matrix of lag
h
,
h\geq 0
, of the common component
\boldsymbol{\chi}_{t}
, and truncated data
\mathbf{X}_{t}(\tau)
as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\chi}}(h)=\frac{1}{n-h}\sum_{t=h+1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left(\boldsymbol{\chi}_{t}\boldsymbol{\chi}_{t-h}^{\top}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{x}}(\tau,h)=\frac{1}{n-h}\sum_{t=h+1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbf{X}_{t}(\tau)\mathbf{X}_{t-h}^{\top}(\tau)\right)\,.

Furthermore, for the estimated common component
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{t}
and estimated idiosyncratic component
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t}
we denote their sample autocovariance matrices by

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{\boldsymbol{\chi}}(h)=\frac{1}{n-h}\sum_{t=h+1}^{n}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{t}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\chi}}_{t-h}^{\top}\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(h)=\frac{1}{n-h}\sum_{t=h+1}^{n}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t-h}^{\top}\,.

We also make use of big
O
and little
o
notation, which we define here. Given two sequences
\{a_{n}\}
and
\{b_{n}\}
, we write
a_{n}=O(b_{n})
