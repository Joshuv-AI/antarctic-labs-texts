OpenAI’s HealthBench in Action: Evaluating an LLM-Based Medical Assistant on Realistic Clinical Queries
Sandhanakrishnan Ravichandran, Shivesh Kumar, Rogério Corga Da Silva, Miguel Romano, Reinhard Berkels, Michiel van der Heijden, Olivier Fail, Valentine Emmanuel Gnanapragasam

Abstract
Evaluating large language models (LLMs) on their ability to generate high-quality, accurate, situationally aware answers to clinical questions requires going beyond conventional benchmarks to assess how these systems behave in complex, high-stake clincal scenarios. Traditional evaluations are often limited to multiple-choice questions that fail to capture essential competencies such as contextual reasoning, awareness and uncertainty handling etc. To address these limitations, we evaluate our agentic, RAG-based clinical support assistant, DR.INFO , using HealthBench , a rubric-driven benchmark composed of open-ended, expert-annotated health conversations. On the Hard subset of 1,000 challenging examples, DR.INFO achieves a HealthBench score of 0.51 , substantially outperforming leading frontier LLMs (GPT-5, o3, Grok 3, GPT-4, Gemini 2.5, etc.) across all behavioral axes (accuracy, completeness, instruction following, etc.). In a separate 100-sample evaluation against similar agentic RAG assistants (OpenEvidence, Pathway.md), it maintains a performance lead with a health-bench score of 0.54 . These results highlight DR.INFO ’s strengths in communication, instruction following, and accuracy, while also revealing areas for improvement in context awareness and completeness of a response. Overall, the findings underscore the utility of behavior-level, rubric-based evaluation for building a reliable and trustworthy AI-enabled clinical support assistant.

Dataset Overview

HealthBench is a large-scale, physician-curated benchmark comprising 5,000 clinically realistic examples designed to evaluate the performance of large language models in realistic healthcare scenarios. Each example contains a conversation and a set of rubric criteria tailored to that conversation
[
13
]
.
Conversations in HealthBench may be either:

•

Single-turn:
A single user message to which the model must respond.

•

Multi-turn:
Alternating user and model messages, ending with a user prompt.

2.1
HealthBench Consensus Subset

HealthBench Consensus

[
13
]
is a filtered subset of the dataset, containing only those 3,671 examples that include one or more positive consensus criteria. This subset is particularly useful for studying high-confidence failure cases and for validating model behavior against physician consensus.

2.2
HealthBench Hard Subset

HealthBench Hard

[
13
]
is a challenge subset consisting of 1,000 examples identified as especially difficult for current frontier models. It includes both positive and negative scoring criteria and captures cases with high clinical complexity and ambiguity.

In our evaluation, we focus exclusively on the Hard subset due to its relevance for high-stakes scenarios and its utility in assessing model behavior under complexity and inference cost constraints.

3
Rubrics

In HealthBench,
rubrics
are structured evaluation criteria that define what constitutes a high-quality response in a clinical context. Each rubric targets a specific expectation—such as factual accuracy, appropriate escalation, or instruction compliance—and reflects standards of safe and effective medical communication.

Each rubric includes a clear criterion, a point value (positive or negative), and an annotation indicating whether it was met in a specific model response. These rubrics are applied by expert reviewers to evaluate the quality, safety, and completeness of LLM outputs. They serve as the backbone of the HealthBench evaluation framework, enabling both human- and model-based judgments to be transparent, reproducible, and clinically grounded.

This rubric-based evaluation approach aligns with emerging efforts in the broader LLM community to move beyond generic metrics. Benchmarks such as VISTA
[
17
]
, PaperBench
[
18
]
, WildBench
[
19
]
, AMEGA
[
20
]
, and MultiChallenge
[
21
]
similarly apply structured criteria to assess complex model behavior, but HealthBench uniquely scales this to thousands of real-world medical conversations.

4
Themes and Axes in HealthBench

To capture the diverse challenges of real-world healthcare interactions, HealthBench organizes its evaluation into two key dimensions:
themes
and
axes
. These allow for detailed and meaningful assessment of model behavior across various use cases and behavioral attributes
[
13
]
.

4.1
Themes

HealthBench dataset is grouped into one of seven high-level
themes
, which represent common categories of health-related tasks encountered in practice. These themes help evaluate how well a model performs in specific real-world scenarios. Below is an overview:

•

Emergency referrals:
Assesses whether the model can recognize urgent or life-threatening situations and advise seeking appropriate care. Mistakes here—either failing to escalate or escalating unnecessarily—can lead to harm or burden the health system.

•

Context-seeking:
Evaluates whether the model can detect when important clinical details are missing and actively ask for them. Real users often provide incomplete information, so this skill is essential for safe responses.

•

Global health:
Tests the model’s ability to adapt advice to different healthcare settings around the world, especially where resources, norms, and diseases differ. This is critical for broad accessibility and equity.

•

Health data tasks:
Measures the model’s skill in structured health applications—such as summarizing notes, interpreting labs, or drafting documents. High accuracy is vital here since mistakes can propagate through a patient’s care.

•

Expertise-tailored communication:
Checks whether the model tailors its language and explanation depth depending on whether the user is a layperson or a healthcare professional.

•

Responding under uncertainty (Hedging):
Evaluates if the model appropriately expresses uncertainty when the clinical situation is ambiguous or when medical evidence is weak. Overconfident answers can mislead users.

•

Response depth:
Assesses whether the model provides an appropriate level of detail—avoiding both overly brief and excessively verbose replies—based on the task complexity and user need.

These themes help researchers and practitioners understand not just how well a model performs overall, but where its strengths and weaknesses lie across types of real-world healthcare interactions.

4.2
Behavioral Axes

Each individual rubric criterion is categorized by the following
behavioral axes
.

•

Accuracy:
Determines whether the response is factually correct and consistent with current clinical knowledge. This axis also includes appropriately signaling uncertainty when evidence is unclear or evolving.

•

Completeness:
Assesses whether all essential and relevant information is included in the response. Even an accurate answer may be inadequate if it leaves out key steps, risks, or next actions.

•

Context awareness:
Measures how well the model responds based on contextual cues such as user role, regional constraints, or prior conversation. It also covers whether the model asks clarifying questions when necessary.

•

Communication quality:
Evaluates whether the information is clearly and effectively presented—well-structured, appropriately technical, and easy to understand for the intended user.

•

Instruction following:
Tests whether the model complies with specific user instructions, such as responding in a requested format or addressing a specific part of the input, without compromising medical safety.

Each criterion in HealthBench is tagged with one of these axes, allowing performance to be analyzed not only by conversation topic (theme) but also by the underlying behavioral skill being assessed.

Discussion

The evaluation of our agentic RAG-based clinical support assistant,
DR.INFO

[
14
]
, using the HealthBench framework reveals valuable insights into its capabilities and limitations across real-world medical conversations. Through both large-scale evaluation on the HealthBench Hard subset (
n=1000
) and a controlled comparison against similar systems on a smaller representative sample (
n=100
), we observed consistent strengths in core clinical behaviors such as communication, instruction following, and factual accuracy.

On the 1000-sample HealthBench Hard subset, the recently reported GPT-5
[
22
]
achieved an overall HealthBench score of 0.46 in its “thinking” mode and 0.26 in non-thinking mode,

To better understand performance relative to similar architectures, we conducted an additional 100-sample evaluation comparing

To quantify the robustness of these results given the limited sample size, we conducted a bootstrapped resampling analysis on the axis-level scores (n=100). The bootstrapped mean and confidence intervals (90% and 95%) for each system’s HealthBench score are shown in Table

DR.INFO

DR.INFO
is likely to outperform the compared systems even under variability, though a larger evaluation would help increase confidence.

While this bootstrapped comparison was restricted to a 100-sample representative subset due to rate limits and manual evaluation constraints, we acknowledge that more definitive comparisons would require large-scale scoring across the full HealthBench Hard set. Expanding this analysis to the full 1000-sample dataset remains an important direction for future work, and would enable higher-confidence statistical conclusions about system-level differences.

While HealthBench provides a rigorous framework for evaluating behavioral competencies in clinical dialogue, it has limitations. Rubric-based assessments, though authored by physicians, may reflect subjective interpretations. Additionally, HealthBench is restricted to text-based interactions; it does not evaluate performance on other critical modalities such as medical imaging, genomics, structured data interpretation, or multimodal diagnostic tasks. A comprehensive assessment of clinical readiness would require benchmarking across these diverse modalities to reflect the full spectrum of real-world medical decision-making.

8
Conclusion

This article presents a comprehensive evaluation of
DR.INFO

[
14
]
, an agentic retrieval-augmented clinical support assistant, using the HealthBench framework. Unlike conventional benchmarks such as the USMLE or MedQA, HealthBench enables a multi-dimensional evaluation of real-world clinical conversations across axes that matter for safe and effective deployment.

DR.INFO
demonstrated consistent strengths across challenging scenarios achieving a HealthBench score of 0.51 on the Hard subset and outperforming previously reported baselines. Its high scores in communication, instruction-following, and factual accuracy suggest that retrieval-augmented, agentic strategies can support more aligned and clinically effective responses. Notably, the system maintained robust performance even in ambiguous and information-sparse scenarios, where conventional LLMs often fail. The rubric-based evaluation further enabled fine-grained behavioral analysis, surfacing areas for targeted refinement, particularly in context awareness and response completeness. These findings underscore the value of behavior-level, clinically grounded benchmarks like HealthBench for assessing real-world readiness in medical AI systems.

To validate the robustness of observed gains, we applied bootstrapped resampling on a representative subset of the dataset. The results showed that
DR.INFO
consistently outperformed comparable clinical assistants with statistical significance under a 90% confidence threshold. While the stricter 95% threshold was not met, this analysis provides supportive evidence of performance advantages, especially given evaluation constraints. Extending this analysis to larger evaluation sets remains a key area for future work.

Together, these findings establish
DR.INFO
as a competitive, real-world-ready clinical assistant and reinforce the utility of behavior-level evaluation frameworks such as HealthBench in guiding the development and safe integration of LLM-based systems into healthcare. While an healthbench score of 0.51 on hard dataset indicates substantial room for improvement, it currently represents state-of-the-art performance on the HealthBench Hard subset outpacing both frontier LLMs and similarly designed agentic systems. This underscores the broader challenge of clinical deployment and the importance of continued benchmarking and iteration.
