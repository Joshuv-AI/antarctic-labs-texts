The novel thioredoxin reductase inhibitor butaselen suppresses lung cancer by inducing oxidative stress
Authors: Yifan Chen, Guangliang Qiang, Liang Jin, Yan Sun, Guozhou Zhang, Fengling Hu, Jinhui Feng, Feng Wei, Shengxin Zeng, Lixiang Xue, Huihui Zeng, Shaohua Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundLung cancer is one of the leading causes of cancer-related deaths worldwide with limited treatment options available. The anti-tumor effects of the TrxR inhibitor Butaselen (BS/BS1801) on lung cancer and its underlying mechanisms remain unknown.MethodsThis study utilized lung cancer cell lines, LLC1-bearing mice models, and organoids to detect the inhibitory effects of BS on lung cancer. The ROS-induction and apoptotic role of BS on lung cancer cells and molecular mechanisms were assessed with flow cytometry, western blot, Co-IP, real-time PCR, ChIP, reporter gene assay, ELISA, and bisulfite pyrosequencing.ResultsBS can effectively inhibit lung cancer both in vitro and in vivo, by triggering ROS-induced apoptosis. The inactivation of NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways, along with the activation of PI3K-Akt and HBP1 signaling pathways, are involved in BS's suppression of lung cancer. HBP1 is a novel downstream target of the Trx system. The activation of HBP1 by BS is dependent on ROS accumulation and further leads to the transcriptional inhibition of DNMT1 and the demethylation of the whole genome, as well as the promoters of p21 and HOXA9.ConclusionThe TrxR/Trx inhibitor butaselen suppresses lung cancer by triggering ROS-induced apoptosis. This study provides a novel and effective regimen for treating lung cancer.KEYWORDS: Lung cancer, oxidative stress, thioredoxin reductase, butaselen, apoptosis, high-mobility group box-containing protein 1 (HBP1), ubiquitination, DNA methylation

Introduction

Lung cancer accounts for approximately 20% of cancer-related deaths worldwide [1]. Although novel treatments for lung cancer, such as molecular-targeted therapy, immunotherapy, and surgery, have been rapidly developing in recent years, the average 5-year survival rate for lung cancer is less than 20% [2]. Therefore, innovative and effective treatments for lung cancer are still urgently needed.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are crucial for cell signaling and the survival of living cells. However, increased ROS generation contributes to tumor promotion and progression due to an elevated metabolic rate and DNA mutations. Excessive ROS production can trigger programmed cell death in tumor cells. Therefore, inducing excessive ROS production to target cancer cells is a promising strategy for cancer treatment, as tumor cells typically contain higher levels of ROS than normal cells [3,4]. One of the key cellular antioxidant systems, the Trx system, is overexpressed in malignant cancers to tolerate high levels of ROS and serves as an important therapeutic target for cancer treatment [5]. Trx and TrxR are the major components of the Trx system. TrxR is an FAD-containing pyridine nucleotide disulfide oxidoreductase that maintains Trx in its reduced state by utilizing nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) as the proton donor, thereby preserving the balance of the redox state in biosynthetic pathways within living cells [6,7]. Targeting TrxR/Trx with inhibitors such as auranofin and TRi-1/−2 induces oxidative stress and leads to apoptotic cell death, thereby halting cancer development [8,9]. However, the underlying mechanisms still require further elucidation.

We previously developed an organic selenium compound butaselen (BS/BS1801, 2-bis[2-(1,2-benzisothiazol-2(2H)-ketone)] butane) as an inhibitor targeting the Trx system [10]. BS is modified from the structure of ebselen [11] and BBSKE [12] and was specifically designed to target the C-terminal active site [10]. Our previous studies have demonstrated that BS can suppress tumor progression by not only directly killing cancer cells, but also enhancing tumor cell immune. On one hand, BS inhibits liver cancer cells by inducing apoptosis through inhibiting TrxR activity and downregulating Bcl-2/Bax [13]. BS inhibits chronic hepatic inflammation, fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) by suppressing TrxR/Ref-1 signaling, the NF-κB pathway, and pro-inflammatory factors, resulting in ROS generation, G2/M arrest, and apoptosis [14]. On the other hand, BS could promote CD4−CD8+ T lymphocytes by reducing PD-L1 expression via the STAT3 pathway [15], and enhance NK and T cell activity and infiltration by upregulating the expression of CXCR3/NKG2D [16], thereby inhibiting tumor cell immune escape and suppressing tumorigenesis. TrxR contains selenocysteine (Sec) in the C-terminus active site and exhibits broad substrate specificity beyond Trx, including glutaredoxin, protein disulfide isomerase, selenite, 9,10-PQ, toxoflavin, and juglone [17-19]. Therefore, the downstream effects of BS need to be thoroughly elucidated for a complete understanding of these TrxR inhibitors.

In the present study, we further explored the effects and mechanisms of BS on lung cancer and elucidated the novel mechanisms involved in BS’s anti-cancer effects. Our results demonstrate that BS significantly impairs the viability of various lung cancer cell lines, including A549 and H1299 cells. BS is shown to suppress lung cancer in vivo, resulting in a deceleration of tumor growth and a notable reduction in tumor volume in LLC1-bearing models. Additionally, BS inhibits the growth of human lung cancer organoids. BS decreases the activity of TrxR, induces oxidative stress by promoting cellular ROS accumulation, and ultimately triggers apoptosis. The activation of JNK signaling and the inactivation of NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways are implicated in BS’s anti-tumor effects. Although p53 is not directly activated by BS treatment, the signaling pathway involving high-mobility group box-containing protein 1 (HBP1)/p21/Bax/DNMT1 is activated instead. Further mechanistic studies reveal the regulatory mechanism of the tumor suppressor HBP1 by BS/TrxR/Trx. The accumulation of ROS induced by BS elevates the expression of HBP1 by inhibiting its proteasomal degradation. HBP1 is a novel downstream target of the Trx1 system. In its reduced form, Trx1 interacts with HBP1 and promotes its proteasomal degradation, a process that can be inhibited by treatment with BS. The activation of HBP1 by BS further leads to the transcriptional inhibition of DNMT1. The downregulation of DNMT1 results in the demethylation of the whole genome as well as the promoters of p21 and HOXA9, leading to increased expression of both p21 and HOXA9. In conclusion, this study reveals the overall impact of the TrxR/Trx inhibitor BS on oxidative stress and related signaling pathways in lung cancer. BS suppresses lung cancer by triggering ROS-induced apoptosis, accompanied by the inactivation of the NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways, and activation of the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway and the tumor suppressor HBP1, a novel downstream target of the Trx system. Collectively, our results provide a novel and effective regimen for treating lung cancer.

Cell viability assay and colony formation

Cell viability was measured using Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) (Dojindo Laboratories) as previously described [20]. Cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2000 cell/well. After culturing for 24, 48, or 72 h, 10 μl of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, followed by further incubation at 37°C for 1∼4 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was read using the microplate reader (Tecan). The CCK-8 assay was performed in three biological replicates. The IC50 of BS was calculated with Origin 2018.

Colony formation assay was performed as previously described [21]. A549 and H1299 cells were seeded in six-well plates and treated with BS for 24∼48 h. After incubation for 7–14 days, the surviving cells were stained with crystal violet, and colonies containing more than 50 cells were counted by ImageJ. The colony formation assay was performed in three biological replicates.

Animals and treatments

C57BL/6J mice (eight-week-old female, 18∼22 g) were purchased from the Animal Center, Peking University Health Science Center. All experimental studies were performed in accordance with the animal protocol approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Peking University Health Science Center (approval number: PUIRB-LA2023330). Mice were kept under standard environmental conditions and weighed every 2∼3 days. Water and food were readily available. After two days of adaptation to the environment, 1 × 106 of LLC1 cells were suspended in 100 μl of PBS and subcutaneously injected into the right hind leg of mice. One week after injection, the mice were randomly divided into the Control group and the BS group. BS (360 mg/kg) was administered daily via gavage, and the same volume of saline solution (5 ml/kg) was administered daily via gavage in the control group. The daily activities, mental state, diet, drinking status, and their body weight, were recorded at a fixed time every 2∼5 days until the mice were euthanized by spinal cord dislocation method. Fifteen days after BS treatment, the mice were euthanized, the tumors were weighed and the size was measured. The organs including spleen, liver, kidney, heart, and lungs were weighed.

Thioredoxin reductase, glutathione reductase (GR), and glutathione peroxidase (GPx) activity assay

The intracellular and tissue TrxR activity was determined with Thioredoxin Reductase Assay Kit (Sigma-Aldrich) using DTNB as the substrate, as instructed by the manufacturer and described previously [22], with a total of 25–30 μg whole cell lysis. The intracellular GR and GPx activity were conducted as described previously [14].

Western blot

The experiment was performed as described previously [23]. The cells and tissues were collected and lysed in RIPA buffer (Thermo Scientific) containing Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (AMRESCO) and Phosphatase Inhibitor (Bimake), and protein concentrations were measured by the BCA Protein Assay kit (Thermo Scientific). A total of 20–60 μg of protein was separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore). The primary antibodies used for immunoblotting analysis are as follows: TrxR1 (Proteintech, 11117-1-AP), Trx1 (Proteintech, 14999-1-AP), HBP1 (Proteintech, 11746-1-AP), DNMT1 (Proteintech, 24206-1-AP), Bcl-2 (Proteintech, 12789-1-AP), Bax (Proteintech, 50599-2-Ig), β-actin (Bioss, bs-0061R), Flag (Sigma-Aldrich, F1804), HA (Covance, MMS-101P), p53 (Santa, sc-126), p21 (MBL, K0081-3), p27 (MBL, K0082), γ-H2AX (CST, 9718), NF-κB (Abcam, ab32536), p-NF-κB(CST, 3033), p38 (Santa, sc-7972), p-p38 (Santa, sc-101759), JNK (MCE, HY-P80728), p-JNK (Immunoway, YP0157), Akt (Santa, sc-5298), phospho-Akt (CST, 4060). Horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (ZSGB-Bio, 1: 2000) were applied for incubation with membranes at room temperature for 1–1.5 h before visualized by ECL kit (Advansta) and analyzed by Tanon. The quantification of proteins was determined using ImageJ.

Organoid culture and CellTiter-Glo analysis of the human organoids

To establish organoids derived from human metastatic lung cancer specimens, human lung cancer with spine metastases tissues were mechanically minced into 1 mm3 pieces and digested in 50 ug of Dnase I (SIGMA) and 1 mg of Collagenase type 4 (Roche) prepared in basal medium for 1 h at 37 °C. After filtering through a 70 μm cell filter, the cell suspension was centrifuged at 500 g for 10 min. The cell pellet was washed with PBS. After another centrifuge at 500 g for 5 min, the supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellets were mixed with Matrigel, seeded at 10 µl per drop in preheated six-well plates, solidified at 37 °C for 30 min, and cultured in Lung Carcinoma Organoid Medium (Precedo) with 1% penicillin–streptomycin. The medium was changed every 3–4 days. Passages were made every 1–3 weeks, depending on the density and size of the organoids.

To establish organoids derived from human lung cancer pleural effusion specimens, the samples were centrifuged at 400 g for 10 min at room temperature. Then the cell pellets were mixed with Matrigel, seeded at 10 µl per drop in preheated six-well plates, solidified at 37 °C for 30 min, and cultured in Lung Carcinoma Organoid Medium (Precedo) with 1% penicillin–streptomycin. The medium was changed every 3–4 days. Passages were made every 1–3 weeks, depending on the density and size of the organoids.

Cytotoxicity of BS on human organoid was assessed using the CellTiter-Glo® 3D Cell Viability Assay (Promega), a method based upon detection of ATP content. The CellTiter-Glo assays were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions. On day zero, fully matured organoids were harvested and a single-cell suspension was subsequently obtained by incubating the organoids for 5 min with Trypsin/EDTA (0.25%/0.02% (w/v) in PBS) solution at 37 °C. Following this, a cell density of 5 × 10^3 cells was mixed with 15 µl of Matrigel and plated in 96-well plates using the Lung Carcinoma Organoid Medium (Precedo). After 24 h, serial dilutions of BS were prepared in the organoid medium and added to the matured organoids. Following stimulation with either BS or DMSO for 24 h, endpoint ATP levels were measured by adding 100 µl of CellTiter-Glo reagent to each well. Subsequently, the plate was mixed for 2 min, followed by a 10 min incubation at room temperature in the dark to ensure complete organoid lysis. The acquired luminescence intensity was then measured using a Spark Multimode Microplate Reader (Tecan).

Intracellular and mitochondria ROS detection

Intracellular ROS detection was measured by staining cells with the fluorescent probe 2’, 7’-dichloro fluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) [14]. Mitochondria ROS detection was measured by staining cells with the MitoSOX Red (Thermo Scientific). A549 and H1299 cells were treated with BS or NAC of different concentrations for 24 h. After staining with 10 µM DCFH-DA or 1 µM MitoSOX Red at 37 °C for 30 min, the cells were washed with PBS three times and the fluorescence intensity of the cells was measured using the flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter). The data were analyzed with CytExpert and FlowJo software.

Assay of glutathione (GSH) and glutathione disulfide (GSSG) levels

A549 and H1299 cells were exposed to BS for 24 h before protein extraction with NP-40 lysate added with sulfosalicylic acid to prevent loss of GSH. The assay measured GSH and GSSG were conducted as previously reported [24].

Apoptosis assay

The cells were collected, and the apoptosis assay was conducted using the Annexin V-FITC/ propidium iodide (PI) Dual Staining Kit (KeyGEN) as instructed by the manufacturer and described previously [25]. Briefly, after treatment, the cells were trypsinized and resuspended in 500 µl of 1× Binding Buffer and incubated with Annexin V-FITC for 15 min, followed by incubation of PI for another 5 min in the dark. After staining, the cells were analyzed using the flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter). The apoptosis assay was measured at least three times with independent samples. The data were analyzed with CytExpert software.

Real-time PCR

The cells were collected, and total RNA was extracted with RNAisoPlus kit (BioTeke) as instructed by the manufacturer. RNA was then processed for Real-time PCR. The cDNA was synthesized using ReverAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Scientific) and then analyzed by Real-time PCR analysis with Maxima SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Thermo Scientific) [23]. The DNA sequences of the primers are listed in Table 1. β-actin was applied as an internal control for normalizing the Real-time PCR results.

Immunoprecipitation

Cells for immunoprecipitation assay were lysed in IP lysis buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 1 mM EDTA, 5% glycerol) containing Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Sigma). The cell extracts were incubated with specific antibodies or control IgG and Protein A Sepharose (GE) overnight at 4°C with constant rotation. The immunocomplexes were then washed three times with IP lysis buffer, and subsequently resolved by SDS-PAGE followed by western blot analysis [23].

In vivo ubiquitination assay

For the detection of HBP1 ubiquitination levels, HEK-293 T cells were co-transfected with various plasmids as indicated in individual experiment. 18 h after transfection, cells were treated with 10 μM MG132 (Merck Millipore) for 6 h and the whole cell lysates prepared by Flag-lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.8, 137 mM NaCl, 10 mM NaF, 1 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, 0.2% Sarkosyl, 1 mM DTT, 10% glycerol and fresh protease inhibitors) were immunoprecipitated with anti-Flag antibody and then resolved by SDS-PAGE followed by western blot analysis [23].

Protein half-life assay

A549 cells were transfected with plasmids as indicated in individual experiment. After BS treatment of 24 h, 100 μg/ml of cycloheximide (CHX) was added to the dishes, and the CHX treatment was terminated at indicated time points. The whole cell lysates were prepared and 25 μg to 80 μg of total protein from each sample was analyzed by western blot with anti-HBP1 antibody. Quantification of HBP1 protein level was determined using ImageJ, normalized to β-actin.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)

The ChIP assay was performed using Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP) Assay Kit (Millipore) following the manufacturer’s instructions using 2 µg of either normal IgG or antibodies against HBP1. Real-time PCR was performed as described above at least three times with independent DNA samples. The reported data represent Real-time PCR values normalized to input DNAs and to the values obtained with normal IgG, which were set as one unit in each calculation. Data are presented as fold differences relative to input and values obtained by normal IgG with the formula 2((Ct IgG − Ct Input) − (Ct Ab – Ct Input)), where Ct is the threshold cycles, IgG is the normal IgG, Ab is the specific antibody, and Input is the input genomic DNA. For each antibody, at least three independent ChIP experiments were performed [26]. The DNA sequences of the primers are listed in Table 1.

Reporter gene assay

Cells were transfected with plasmids as indicated in individual experiment. Cell lysates were prepared with the Dual Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (Promega) as the manufacturer’s instruction at 48 h post-transfection. The Firefly Luciferase activity measurements were normalized to Renilla Luciferase activity for the same sample. The luciferase assay was performed in three biological replicates and each replicate was measured at least three times [26].

ELISA detection of the global DNA methylation

The global DNA methylation of mouse DNA was conducted with the MethylFlash™ Global DNA Methylation 5mC ELISA Easy Kit (Epigentek). 100 ng of sample DNA (2 μl), PC, or NC were added into 100 μl of BS solution in the 96 well plate. Mix and incubate the 96 well plate at 37°C for 60 min. Remove the BS solution and wash with 150 μl of the Diluted WB for three times. Add 50 μl of the 5-mC Detection Complex Solution and incubate at room temperature for 50 min. Remove the 5-mC Detection Complex Solution and wash with 150 μl of the Diluted WB for five times. Add 100 μl of DS, gently shake the plate against a flat surface for 5–10 s and incubate at room temperature for 3-4 min. When the color in the 5% PC wells turns deep blue, stop the enzyme reaction by adding 100 μl of SS solution. After an incubation of 1-2 min, the absorbance was read on a microplate reader at 450 nm. Calculate the percentage of methylated DNA (5-mC) in total DNA using the following formula: 5-mC% = (Sample OD-NC OD)/ (Slope × S) × 100%. S is the amount of input sample DNA in ng.

Genomic DNA of the mice tumor tissues were isolated using the QIAamp DNA Mini kit (Qiagen). One microgram of DNA was bisulfite-treated using the EZ DNA methylation Gold kit (Zymo Research). PCR was conducted with EpiArt HS Taq Master Mix (Vazyme) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Bisulfite pyrosequencing was conducted with Pyromark Q48 Autoprep (Qiagen). Successful analysis showed that the percentage of samples with good quality was all 100%. The primers for bisulfite PCR were reported before [27] or designed by PyroMark Assay Design 2.0 and were listed in Table 1. The length of the target sequence is 164 bp (HOXA9) and 179 bp (p21), and the annealing temperature is 41°C (HOXA9) and 55°C (p21).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed by GraphPad Prism 9.3.0 statistical software. n> = 3 independent experiments, except for cell death inhibitors screening, which are technical replicates. For each data set, Anderson-Darlin, D’Agostino-Pearson, Shapiro–Wilk, or Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests were used to assess the normal distribution of data within groups and to select the appropriate parametric or non-parametric test. For normally distributed data, differences between two groups were analyzed using the two-tailed unpaired Student’s t test. Statistical differences in experiments with multiple groups were assessed using the One-way ANOVA followed by Newman–Keuls or Tukey’s post hoc test analysis. The data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (std). For comparisons between normal and tumor tissues, paired t-tests were used to assess differences between adjacent normal tissue and tumor tissue. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and the statistical details are shown in the figures and figure legends.

Results

Butaselen suppresses lung cancer both in vitro and in vivo

In our previous studies, we demonstrated that butaselen (BS) effectively inhibits hepatocarcinogenesis [14,16]. To extend this discovery, we assessed the activity of BS on lung cancer. We first employed the CCK-8 assay to evaluate the cytotoxicity of BS on seven lung cancer cell lines (A549, H1299, HCC827, H1650, H1666, H1975, and SPC-A1). The cells were exposed to BS concentrations ranging from 5 μM to 40 μM for 24 h, and cell viability was determined (Supplementary Fig. 1A). As shown in Figure 1(b), BS inhibited the growth of lung cancer cell lines, with an average IC50 of less than 20 μM. Four non-transformed cell lines, HFL-1 (normal lung embryonic fibroblast cell), IOSE-80 (normal breast cell), HEK-293 (normal kidney cell), and HL-7702 (normal liver cell), were estimated to investigate the cytotoxicity of BS on normal cells, which is an important factor limiting the clinical practice of chemotherapy. It was shown that BS showed less toxicity to four noncancerous cell lines, with an average IC50 of more than 20 μM. This suggests that BS inhibited the viability of lung cancer cells and has higher selectivity for lung cancer cells than normal cells.

It has been reported that several lung cancer cell lines carry gene mutations, including A549 (KRAS G12S), H1299 (p53 deletion), HCC827 (EGFR deletion 746-A750), H1650 (EGFR deletion 746-A750), H1666 (B-RAF G465 V), and H1975 (EGFR L858R/T790M) [28,29]. Notably, there was no significant difference in the sensitivity of the seven lung cancer cell lines to BS treatment, suggesting that BS likely does not exhibit a preference for specific genetic alterations in lung cancer cells and demonstrates a broad-spectrum inhibitory effect on lung cancer.

We selected two cell lines, A549 and H1299, based on their IC50 values following BS treatment, for further investigations into the mechanism by which BS inhibits lung cancer. The viability of these two lung cancer cell lines decreased in a dose – and time-dependent manner after BS treatment (Figure 1(c), Table 2). To validate the results obtained from the CCK-8 assay, we employed a colony formation assay to assess cell viability. Following 24 h of treatment with varying concentrations of BS, the cells were cultured for 7–14 days and subsequently stained with crystal violet. The cells were then counted, and the colony formation assay produced consistent results, further confirming the cytotoxic effects of BS on A549 and H1299 (Figure 1(d)).

After demonstrating remarkable efficacy in vitro, we designed in vivo experiments to evaluate the effectiveness of BS using a murine model with LLC1 xenografts. Following treatment with BS, the mice exhibited a significant reduction in tumor volume (Figure 1(e)) and tumor weight (Figure 1(f)) compared to the control group. Importantly, there was no significant loss of body weight (Supplementary Fig. 2), and no changes were observed in the relative weights of the kidneys, heart, and lungs (Supplementary Table) in the treated group. These results suggest that BS exerts an anti-tumor effect in vivo without causing significant toxicity to organs such as the kidneys, heart, and lungs.

Since BS was designed as a TrxR inhibitor [10], the expression and activity of TrxR were detected in lung cancer cells and tumors to further validate the function of BS. BS inhibited the activity of TrxR in both lung cancer cells (Figure 1(g)) and tumor tissues (Figure 1(i)). Immunoblots further indicated that BS reduced the protein levels of TrxR1 and Trx1 in vitro (Figure 1(h)) and in vivo (Figure 1(j)). This suggests that BS inhibits TrxR/Trx in lung cancer.

Finally, we established five organoid models derived from patients with lung cancer and lung cancer with spinal metastases. We tested the effect of BS on the growth of these organoids by conducting a CellTiter-Glo analysis. The results demonstrated that BS significantly inhibited the viability of lung cancer organoids (Figure 1(k, l)). Collectively, these data suggest that butaselen, as a TrxR inhibitor, suppresses the growth, proliferation, and tumorigenicity of lung cancer both in vitro and in vivo.

Butaselen induces oxidative stress in lung cancer cells

TrxR is a crucial component of the cellular antioxidant system. Inhibiting TrxR activity can disrupt cellular redox homeostasis, leading to oxidative stress, which is characterized by excessive production of ROS. Numerous TrxR inhibitors, such as alantolactone and plumbagin, have been shown to induce oxidative stress [30]. To determine whether BS exhibited a similar effect, we measured the ROS levels in cells following BS treatment. First, we assessed the total intracellular ROS content using dichloro-dihydro-fluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA), a general probe for detecting intracellular ROS production. Treatment of A549 and H1299 cells with BS resulted in a significant increase in intracellular ROS levels in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 2(a)).

Excessive production of ROS further damages mitochondria, which are major producers of ROS. We then applied the mitochondrial-specific ROS dye MitoSOX to assess the levels of mitochondrial ROS. As shown in Figure 2(b), MitoSOX staining revealed an elevated signal following BS insult, indicating an increase in mitochondrial ROS. Immunoblot analysis demonstrated that the ratio of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 to the pro-apoptotic marker Bax was downregulated by BS (Figure 2(c)). Additionally, the ratio of intracellular GSH to oxidized GSSG, another marker of ROS production, was significantly decreased (Figure 2(d)). Finally, we assessed the effects of BS on glutathione peroxidase (GPx) and glutathione reductase (GR) at the cellular level. Both enzymes are components of the glutathione reductase system, another critical intracellular redox regulatory pathway. Since TrxR and glutathione peroxidases contain reactive thiols that can sometimes be inhibited by the same small molecules, we evaluated whether BS affects their activity. Our results show that BS treatment does not inhibit GPx or GR activities within cells (Figure 2(e, f)). Collectively, these findings suggest that BS promotes oxidative stress in lung cancer without affecting the activities of GPx or GR.

Butaselen induces ROS-dependent apoptosis in lung cancer cells

The excessive accumulation of ROS can exert toxic effects and lead to oxidative stress, damaging essential cellular components such as DNA, lipids, and proteins, ultimately resulting in cell death through mechanisms including apoptosis, autophagy, and ferroptosis [31,32]. TrxR is a critical component of the cellular antioxidant system; inhibiting its activity can disrupt cellular redox homeostasis, thereby inducing cancer cell death. Consequently, we investigated the specific mechanisms by which BS induces cell death. We employed various cell death inhibitors to examine the effects of BS on lung cancer cells. The results indicated that only Z-VAD-FMK and Z-DEVD-FMK, two apoptotic inhibitors, along with the antioxidant NAC, impaired the growth inhibition of BS on A549 and H1299 cells. In contrast, ferroptosis, autophagy-induced death, and necrosis were not involved in BS-induced lung cancer cell death (Figure 3(a)). Further Annexin V staining assays demonstrated a significant dose – and time-dependent induction of early, late, and total apoptosis in A549 and H1299 cells treated with BS (Figure 3(b)). Consequently, the induction of apoptosis is implicated in the anti-tumor effects of BS.

To further investigate whether BS-induced apoptosis is related to changes in the intracellular redox environment, we examined intracellular ROS production in BS-treated lung cancer cells following pre-treatment with NAC, a ROS scavenger. Given that 10 μM of butaselen resulted in a statistically significant increase in ROS levels with a slight inhibition of cell viability, we selected 10 μM of butaselen as the dosage for subsequent experiments. As shown in Figure 3(c), the accumulation of ROS in A549 cells induced by BS was mitigated by NAC. Immunoblot analysis showed that NAC could reverse the downregulation of the Bcl-2/Bax ratio induced by BS (Figure 3(d)). The apoptosis induced by BS could be reversed by co-treatment with NAC (Figure 3(e)). Collectively, the results suggest that ROS accumulation-induced cell apoptosis plays a significant role in the inhibitory effects of BS on lung cancer.

NF-κB, p38/JNK, and PI3K-Akt signaling pathways are involved in BS’s suppression on lung cancer

It has been reported that the inhibition of TrxR/Trx induces the generation of ROS, which can affect several signaling pathways and subsequently lead to apoptosis. These pathways include the p38/JNK signaling pathway, the NF-kappa B signaling pathway, the p53 signaling pathway, and the PI3 K/Akt signaling pathway, etc. [33,34]. Therefore, we investigated whether these signaling pathways were involved in BS's suppression of lung cancer.

In response to ROS, the oxidized form of Trx is released, activating apoptosis signal-regulating kinase 1 (ASK1) to mediate apoptosis through the p38-MAPK and c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) signaling pathways [35]. We investigated the effect of BS on the p38/JNK pathway in A549 and H1299 cells. Immunoblot analysis revealed that BS increases the levels of phosphorylated p38 and phosphorylated JNK, indicating the activation of the p38/JNK signaling pathway (Figure 4(a)).

BS affects NF-κB, p38/JNK, and PI3K-Akt signaling pathways in lung cancer. (a–d) A549 and H1299 cells were treated with BS (0, 5, 10 μM) for 24 h, followed by western blot. Error bars are means ± std. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, compared with the control group by One-way ANOVA (n = 3).

NF-κB has been reported to be directly regulated by Trx. In the nucleus, NF-κB undergoes several post-translational modifications, including the reduction of cysteine residues by Trx-1, which facilitates its binding to DNA and the transcription of various proteins [36,37]. Immunoblot analysis revealed that BS decreased phospho-NF-κB levels, indicating an inactivation of NF-κB signaling (Figure 4(b)).

The PI3 K/AKT pathway is one of the most well-known pathways regulated by ROS and can be activated by Trx, thereby inhibiting apoptosis in cancer cells. Following treatment with BS, the expression of phospho-AKT decreased, while the total expression of AKT remained nearly unchanged. These results indicate that BS inactivates the PI3 K/AKT pathway in lung cancer cells (Figure 4(c)).

p53 can be released from MDM2-mediated proteasomal degradation and activated by oxidative stress, thereby promoting the expression of several apoptosis-inducing proteins. Trx/TrxR maintains p53 in a redox-active state and enhances its DNA-binding activity. We investigated the effects of BS on p53 signaling. Immunoblots revealed that the expression of p53 remained unchanged, while the targets of p53, including p21, p27, and γ-H2AX, were upregulated following BS treatment (Figure 4(d)). This accompanied by the fact that the absence of p53 expression in H1299 cells suggests that p53 itself was not activated by BS. Instead, the activation of p21, p27, and γ-H2AX by BS occurs independently of p53. This indicates the possibility of an alternative pathway, which, while similar to p53 signaling, operates independently and can be activated by BS to induce apoptosis.

Taken together, BS inhibits the NF-kB and PI3K-Akt pathways while activating the p38/JNK signaling pathway in lung cancer.

Activation of HBP1 plays an important role in BS-induced apoptosis of lung cancer

Subsequently, we sought to identify the specific signaling pathway activated by BS independently of p53. Previous reports indicate that elevated levels of ROS associated with the induction of apoptosis are linked to various downstream mechanisms, including the activation of high-mobility group box-containing protein 1 (HBP1), a well-established tumor suppressor and senescence-promoting factor identified in our earlier studies. HBP1 can be induced by hydrogen peroxide [25] or act as a cellular pro-oxidant by sensitizing tumor cells to ferroptosis [38]. More importantly, HBP1 exerts regulatory roles on the targets of p53 (p21, p27, and γ-H2AX, etc) in an independent manner of p53 activity [23]. Hence, we sought to determine whether BS-induced elevated ROS generation further activated HBP1 in lung cancer. Immunoblots indicated that the expression of HBP1 was upregulated by BS treatment both in vitro and in vivo (Figure 5(a, b)). It has been reported that HBP1 is a negative regulator of DNMT1 [26] and a positive regulator of p21 [23] and Bax [25]. Immunoblots showed that BS dramatically decreased the protein level of DNMT1, and increased the levels of p21 and Bax (Figure 5(c)), suggesting an activation of HBP1 signaling. Then by using NAC, immunoblots revealed that NAC could reverse the upregulation of HBP1, p21, and Bax, and the decrease of DNMT1 by BS treatment (Figure 5(c)), indicating that the activation of HBP1 signaling by BS was dependent on ROS accumulation.

To further validate the role of HBP1 in BS’s inhibition on lung cancer, we conducted rescue assays with siRNA targeting HBP1. It was shown that the regulation of DNMT1, p21, and Bax by BS treatment were partially rescued by the co-treatment of siHBP1 (Figure 5(d)). Further annexin V staining assay showed that the induction of BS on the apoptotic death of lung cancer cell was partially rescued by the co-treatment of siHBP1 (Figure 5(e)). Hence, activation of HBP1 plays an important role in BS-induced apoptosis of lung cancer cells.

The Trx system inversely associates with HBP1 in lung cancer

Since HBP1 was activated by BS, and BS is a specific inhibitor of TrxR/Trx, we aimed to determine whether the activation of HBP1 by BS was a result of TrxR/Trx inhibition. We initially investigated the relationship between the expression of TrxR1 (encoded by the gene TXNRD1), Trx1 (encoded by the gene TXN), TXNIP (an endogenous inhibitor of Trx1 encoded by the gene TXNIP), and HBP1 in lung cancer using the GEPIA database [39]. Data analysis revealed that the mRNA levels of TrxR1 and Trx1 in tumor tissues were higher than those in the normal adjacent tissues of lung cancer patients (Figure 6(a, b)), while the mRNA levels of TXNIP and HBP1 in tumor tissues were lower than those in the normal adjacent tissues (Figure 6(c, d)). Using normal adjacent tissues and tumor tissues isolated from patients diagnosed with lung cancer before any treatment (Table 4), we conducted immunoblots and thioredoxin reductase activity assay. The results indicated that the protein levels (Figure 6(e)) and activities (Figure 6(f)) of TrxR were elevated in lung cancer. Additionally, the protein levels of Trx1 were increased, while the protein levels of TXNIP and HBP1 were decreased in lung cancer (Figure 6(e)). Further univariate Kaplan-Meier survival analysis [40] revealed a significant correlation between the expression of TrxR1, Trx1, TXNIP, and HBP1 and the clinical outcomes of patients with lung cancer. Lower expression levels of TrxR1 (Figure 6(g)) and Trx1 (Figure 6(h)) were associated with longer survival times, while higher expression levels of TXNIP (Figure 6(i)) and HBP1 (Figure 6(j)) were also associated with longer survival times. Finally, correlation analysis indicated a weak inverse association between the mRNA levels of Trx1 and HBP1 in lung cancer (Figure 6(k)), while a positive association was observed between the mRNA levels of TXNIP and HBP1 (Figure 6(l)). Therefore, it can be concluded that there may be a negative regulatory relationship between the Trx system and HBP1 in lung cancer.

TrxR1/Trx1 inhibition leads to reduced proteasomal degradation of HBP1

We next tried to investigate whether HBP1 was regulated by TrxR/Trx and the relative mechanism. Knocking down TrxR1 with siRNA had minimal impact on the expression of HBP1 (Supplementary Fig. 3), which aligns with our previous research [41] and other studies indicating that modulating TrxR1 with siRNA may not elicit downstream effects. This is likely because Trx1 can also remain reduced by the cytosolic form of glutaredoxin [7,42]. Therefore, we focused on the regulation of Trx1 on HBP1 in the following studies. We first conducted a flow cytometry assay and confirmed that the knockdown of Trx1 with siRNA (siTrx1) could promote the generation of ROS similar to BS treatment. Additionally, co-treatment of siTrx1 and BS synergistically promoted the generation of ROS, further validating that the effects of BS on ROS generation was mediated by targeting Trx1 (Figure 7(a)). Consequently, the expression of HBP1 was also elevated following Trx1 knockdown, and the combined treatment of siTrx1 and BS could synergistically enhance the expression of HBP1 (Figure 7(b)). Additionally, the expression of the targets of HBP1, including DNMT1, p21, and Bax were also regulated by the treatment of siTrx1, BS, or siTrx1 + BS (Figure 7(c)). Together, these results further validated that Trx was the target of BS and Trx exerted negative regulation on the expression of HBP1 and its downstream pathways.

Subsequently, we tried to elucidate the mechanisms of Trx’s regulation on HBP1. Since the mRNA levels of HBP1 remained unchanged following siTrx1 or BS treatment (Figure 7(c)), we hypothesized that BS and Trx1 might affect the stability of HBP1 protein. First, the half-life of the HBP1 protein was measured, and the results indicated that BS extended the half-life of HBP1 (Figure 7(d)). Based on the experiment involving the treatment of MG132, a proteasome inhibitor, we found that the upregulation of HBP1 by BS depended on proteasomal degradation (Figure 7(e)). Our previous studies demonstrated that the degradation of HBP1 protein can be mediated by ubiquitination [43], so we conducted an in vivo ubiquitination assay to detect the ubiquitination of HBP1. The results indicated that both BS and Trx1 knockdown could decrease the ubiquitination of HBP1, and the combined treatment of siTrx1 and BS exerted a synergistic effect in reducing the ubiquitination of HBP1 (Figure 7(f)). NAC could reverse the inhibition of BS on the ubiquitination of HBP1. As Trx1 exerts regulatory roles on its targets through direct protein interaction [44], we conducted a co-immunoprecipitation assay to determine whether there is an interaction between Trx1 and HBP1. The results indicated that HBP1 interacted with Trx1 in vivo (Figure 7(g)), and this interaction was inhibited by BS treatment, which could be further restored by NAC (Figure 7(h)). Furthermore, co-treatment with siTrx1 and BS + siTrx1 could also diminish the interaction between Trx1 and HBP1 (Figure 7(h)). Taken together, these results suggest that BS can enhance the stability of HBP1 by reducing its proteasomal degradation while targeting Trx1.

TrxR/Trx inhibition enhances the transcriptional repression activity of HBP1 on DNMT1

Since BS activates the expression of HBP1 and subsequently influences its downstream targets, we aimed to determine the effects of BS and Trx1 on the transcriptional activity of HBP1, particularly regarding DNMT1 and the DNA methylation mechanism, which had not been clarified in previous studies on TrxR inhibitors. The mRNA levels of DNMT1 decreased in response to BS treatment, as well as in the presence of siTrx1 or the combined treatment of BS + siTrx1 (Figure 8(a)). Then with luciferase reporter assay by utilizing two DNMT1 promoter-luciferase reporters, one containing the native DNMT1 segment (Luc-DNMT1), and the other with a deletion that eliminates the HBP1 affinity site (Luc-ΔDNMT1) [26], it was observed that siTrx1 or BS treatment could activate the DNMT1 promoter (Figure 8(b)). Either the siTrx1 or BS treatment had no impact on the DNMT1 promoter without the HBP1 affinity site (Figure 8(b)). Furthermore, using the ChIP assay, we found that both BS and siTrx1 promoted the binding of HBP1 to the DNMT1 promoter (Figure 8(c)). Additionally, co-treatment with BS and siTrx1 synergistically activated the DNMT1 promoter and enhanced the binding of HBP1 to the DNMT1 promoter (Figure 8(b, c)). Thus, inhibition of Trx1 by siRNA and BS treatment can activate HBP1's negative regulation of DNMT1 at the transcriptional level.

Furthermore, since the repression of HBP1 on DNMT1 can lead to the demethylation and upregulation of several tumor suppressor genes [26], we also investigated the effects of BS on the methylation of genes regulated by DNMT1. The methylation level of the whole genome was detected by ELISA, and the results showed that BS could significantly decrease the methylation of the genome DNA (Figure 8(d)). We further observed a decrease in the methylation of the CG sites on the promoter region of p21 (Figure 8(e)) and HOXA9 (Figure 8(f)), two of the known targets of DNMT1, as a result of Trx1 inhibition. The decreased methylation of the p21 and HOXA9 promoter led to the upregulation of p21 and HOXA9 mRNA level by BS or siTrx1, respectively (Figure 8(g, h)). Taken together, these results suggest that TrxR1/Trx1 inhibition can activate the transcriptional repression activity of HBP1 on DNMT1 and lead to the demethylation of whole genome and tumor suppressor genes, such as p21 and HOXA9.

Discussion

The high death rates of lung cancer make it urgent to explore the mechanisms and develop effective therapeutics for lung cancer [2,45]. In the present study, we demonstrated that butaselen (BS), an organic selenium compound targeting the TrxR/Trx system, can inhibit lung cancer by inducing ROS-dependent apoptosis (Figure 9). The inactivation of the NF-κB and PI3K-Akt signaling pathways, along with the activation of the p38/JNK signaling pathway, contributes to the anti-cancer effects of BS on lung cancer. Although p53 itself is not activated by BS, the HBP1/DNMT1/p21/γ-H2AX/Bcl-2/Bax signaling pathway is activated by BS and contributes to its tumor-inhibitory role. Further mechanistic studies revealed HBP1 as a novel target of the Trx system. The Trx system inversely associates with HBP1 in lung cancer and regulates HBP1 expression at the post-translational level. Under normal conditions, TrxR1 catalyzes the reduction of Trx1 by utilizing NADPH. In its reduced form, Trx1 interacts with HBP1, promoting the ubiquitination of HBP1, which leads to its proteasomal degradation and maintains a low level of HBP1 within cancer cells. Treatment with butaselen inhibits the activity of TrxR1 in lung cancer cells, resulting in the oxidation of Trx1 and the subsequent excessive generation of ROS. HBP1 is activated after being released by the oxidized Trx1 and escaping proteasomal degradation. The activated HBP1 inhibits the expression of DNMT1 and elevates Bax. The decreased DNMT1 further results in the demethylation of the whole genome as well as the promoters of p21 and HOXA9. Ultimately, the upregulation of p21 and γ-H2AX, along with the downregulation of DNMT1 and Bcl-2/Bax, contributes to the apoptosis of lung cancer cells induced by BS. Taken together, the TrxR/Trx inhibitor butaselen inhibits lung cancer by promoting ROS-induced apoptosis through the NF-κB, PI3K-Akt, p38/JNK, and HBP1/DNMT1 signaling pathways.

Some drugs commonly used in clinical practice, such as cisplatin [46], arsenic trioxide [47], and auranofin [48], have been identified as inhibitors of TrxR/Trx. Novel TrxR/Trx inhibitors, including current compounds such as butaselen and ethaselen [12], Tri-1/−2 [9], Curcuminoid WZ26 [49], have been investigated. Although TrxR/Trx also express in normal cells as the major antioxidant system within the living cells, some TrxR inhibitors, including BS, have been proven to exert much less toxicity in normal cells than in cancer cells [50,51], which enhances the therapeutic potential of these TrxR inhibitors. In addition, the Trx system also plays an important role in the activation and function of immune cells [52,53]. Hence, the mechanisms of the anti-tumor effect of TrxR inhibitors might be quite complicated, considering the complexity of the immune microenvironment in vivo [7], which needs further elucidation. Many of these compounds are still in the preclinical stage of research. BS is currently in a Phase I clinical trial (CTR20234234) to evaluate its inhibitory effects on high-grade gliomas. Recently, BS has been approved for clinical investigation under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FDCA) to evaluate its inhibitory effects on primary liver carcinoma /cholangiocarcinoma (IND166278).

Our previous studies indicated that BS suppresses multiple types of cancer. BS could prevent hepatocarcinogenesis by inhibiting chronic inflammation, cirrhosis, and tumor progression through the inhibition of TrxR activity, promotion of ROS elevation, G2/M arrest, and apoptosis [14]. BS could inhibit the development of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) by decreasing PD-L1 expression through the STAT3 pathway and enhancing the immune response [15]. A combination of BS with cisplatin or an anti-PD-1 antibody could synergistically inhibit the development of liver cancer, lung cancer, or colorectal cancer [13,16]. The present study demonstrated that BS inhibits lung cancer by promoting ROS-induced apoptosis and several ROS-related pathways. In addition to its inhibitory effects on malignant cancers, our recent studies have revealed that BS may also be a potential drug for treating fibrotic diseases [41,50]. Since cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) also play important roles in the development and treatment of cancer [54,55], these studies also support the anti-tumor effects of BS. In addition, the absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion of BS have been elucidated in humans and rats [56,57]. The drug safety of BS has been proven through physical examinations, clinical laboratory tests, monitoring vital signs, and conducting 12-lead electrocardiograms (ECGs) on the volunteers in our clinical trials, which was further demonstrated by good drug tolerance in our previous [41] and current studies evidenced by the fact that the body weights and organ relative weights remained almost unchanged in the BS group compared with the control group. Therefore, based on these studies, we conclude that BS has the potential to be a drug for the treatment of malignant cancer such as lung cancer.

Previous studies have shown that BS can inhibit NF-κB, Ref-1, etc., which have been identified as downstream targets of the Trx system by reducing protein disulfides. Trx acts as a small carrier protein for reductive potential in oxidation/reduction cycles. It circulates between ROS and reductive TrxR, donating a proton and forming an intramolecular disulfide bond, and then it is reactivated by TrxR [34]. The activity of transcription factors, including Ref-1, NF-κB, P53, and HIF1α, and signaling factors such as ASK1, is mediated by thioredoxin through direct interactions. For instance, in its reduced form, Trx inhibits ASK1. As reactive ROS accumulate, the oxidized Trx loses its inhibitory effect on ASK1, leading to the initiation of apoptotic cell death [35]. In the present study, we validated the anti-tumor effect of BS on lung cancer through TrxR/Trx inhibition and ROS accumulation. By demonstrating the activation of HBP1 during BS treatment, we have identified a novel oxidative sensor of BS-induced oxidative stress. This expanded our understanding of the mechanisms by which TrxR inhibitors inhibit tumorigenesis.

The limitation of this study is that we have not yet fully excluded the possibility that BS may have other unknown cellular targets. Potential off-target effects have long been a concern among enzymatic inhibitors/kinases [58-61] including auranofin, P2Y12 receptor inhibitors, MEK inhibitors, and in this case BS. Given the lethal consequences of TrxR knockout in animal models and the significant phenotypic changes observed at the cellular level [62,63], applying TrxR inhibitors to TrxR knockout models may complicate the interpretation of whether the loss of inhibitory function results from the absence of TrxR itself or from the lack of the inhibitor’s target. Although currently lacking ideal approaches to definitively rule out the possibility of BS’s off-target effects in vivo, we have conducted several experiments indicating that the primary target of BS is likely TrxR. For example, we used Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR) technology to determine that the affinity constant KD value of BS (BS1801)/TrxR1 is 2.44 μM. And notably, BS exhibits more than twice the binding affinity for TrxR1 compared to auranofin, a well-known TrxR1 inhibitor, indicating that BS binds more strongly to TrxR1 than auranofin [22]. Additionally, we assessed the effects of BS on GPx and GR, structurally similar enzymes to TrxR and components of another critical intracellular redox regulatory system. The results show that BS treatment does not inhibit GPx or GR activities within cells (Figure 2(e, f)). Furthermore, in our previous studies, BS did not exhibit a significant inhibitory effect on GR and GPx activities in liver cancer [14]. Finally, NAC treatment reversed the oxidative stress and apoptosis induced by BS, indicating that oxidative stress is an essential mechanism by which BS inhibits lung cancer. Given that the TrxR/Trx system is one of the primary regulators of intracellular oxidative stress and based on current in vivo and in vitro experimental evidence, TrxR is a primary functional target of BS. Inhibiting TrxR is the central mechanism through which BS exerts its biological effects. The multiple pathway alterations induced by BS treatment are partly related to ROS accumulation and may also be associated with the broad substrate specificity of TrxR itself. While we cannot definitively conclude that all the biological effects observed in this study are solely attributable to TrxR inhibition, future research will aim to employ more comprehensive technical approaches to identify potential off-target proteins of BS, thereby providing a more complete understanding of its mechanism.

In summary, our study demonstrates that the TrxR1/Trx1 inhibitor butaselen can inhibit lung cancer through suppressing NF-κB and PI3K-Akt signaling pathways, activating p38/JNK and HBP1/DNMT1 signaling pathways, and promoting ROS-induced apoptosis. This research establishes a theoretical foundation for future clinical studies on the use of butaselen in treating cancer patients.
