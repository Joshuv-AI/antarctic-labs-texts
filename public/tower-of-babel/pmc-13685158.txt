Clinical impact of PSMA PET/CT on the management of biochemical recurrence in prostate cancer
Authors: Sohyun Park, Tae-Sung Kim, Sung Uk Lee, Nhu Quang Tran, Jae Young Joung, Seok-ki Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGa-68 prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA) positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) improves lesion detection in patients with biochemical recurrence (BCR) of prostate cancer. However, real-world evidence linking imaging-driven management changes to early biochemical outcomes remains limited, particularly in Korean populations. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of Ga-68 PSMA PET/CT on clinical management and its association with early biochemical response.MethodsThis study included retrospectively registered 75 patients who underwent Ga-68 PSMA PET/CT for suspected BCR after radical prostatectomy between September 2022 and February 2024. Intended management at referral and final management after PET were compared. Biochemical outcomes were assessed based on follow-up prostate-specific antigen (PSA) levels.ResultsSeventy-five patients were analyzed (median age, 71 years; median PSA level, 0.30 ng/mL). PSMA PET/CT demonstrated positive findings in 28 patients (37.3%), including equivocal finding. Overall, intended management was changed in 19 patients (25.3%) following PET/CT. At after 2 years of follow-up, 49 patients achieved PSA ≤ 0.20 ng/mL, and 36 achieved PSA ≤ 0.03 ng/mL.ConclusionsIn prostate cancer patients with suspected BCR, Ga-68 PSMA PET/CT led to clinically meaningful changes in management and influenced treatment selection in a Korean cohort. Larger prospective studies are warranted to further define its incremental clinical value.Keywords: Prostate cancer, Biochemical recurrence, Ga-68 PSMA PET/CT

Background

Prostate cancer represents an increasing global health burden, driven by rising incidence and mortality rates worldwide [1, 2]. For curative treatment, radical prostatectomy or radiotherapy is commonly performed; however, approximately one-third of patients experience biochemical recurrence (BCR) following definitive treatment [3]. According to recent guidelines, salvage therapy is recommended at the lowest possible PSA level, emphasizing early intervention in the course of recurrence [4]. In patients treated with radical prostatectomy, salvage external beam radiotherapy, with or without androgen deprivation therapy (ADT), is an established treatment option with favorable recurrence-free survival outcomes [3]. Factors such as a short PSA doubling time, high Gleason score, and elevated PSA level are associated with high-risk BCR and may necessitate more aggressive therapeutic strategies [3, 5].

Prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA) is a transmembrane enzyme encoded by the FOLH1 gene and is expressed at moderate to high levels in prostate cancer [6]. Owing to these characteristics, Ga-68 PSMA positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) has been widely adopted for staging, detection of recurrence, and assessment of treatment response. Although its diagnostic performance varies depending on disease burden and PSA levels, Ga-68 PSMA PET/CT demonstrates high lesion detection rates, particularly in patients with high-risk biochemical recurrence [7, 8]. The clinical impact of Ga-68 PSMA PET/CT in patients with suspected BCR is increasingly recognized, both in terms of survival outcomes [9] and treatment decision-making [10, 11]. In particular, Ga-68 PSMA PET/CT has been increasingly associated with changes in therapeutic intent, including increased pelvic irradiation and the addition of concurrent ADT [12].

Prostate cancer has historically had a lower incidence in Asian populations. But its incidence and mortality have recently increased markedly, likely due to westernized lifestyles and population aging [13, 14]. Asian patients tend to present with more aggressive disease, along with distinct genomic profiles and a higher risk of treatment-related myelosuppression, all of which may influence therapeutic strategies [15]. Although PSMA overexpression has been demonstrated in previous studies in the Korean prostate cancer population [16, 17], its clinical implications have not been fully elucidated. Moreover, whether these biological characteristics translate into differences in imaging performance and clinical outcomes remains unclear. In the present study, we evaluated the clinical management change after Ga-68 PSMA PET/CT in patients with suspected BCR of prostate cancer and assessed early clinical outcomes in a Korean cohort.

Methods

Patient selection and study design

This single-center retrospective study included 75 patients with prostate cancer who underwent PSMA PET/CT between September 2022 and February 2024 for suspected BCR after radical prostatectomy. This study was approved by the institutional review board (NCC2026-0077). Changes in clinical management were assessed through retrospective chart review and categorized as major, moderate, or no change based on their clinical impact, according to the methodology described by van Leeuwen et al., with further modifications [12, 18]. In brief, no impact was defined as no change in management intent or treatment plan. Initiation of ADT that had already been planned before imaging was not classified as a management change. Moderate impact referred to changes in RT delivery without alteration of treatment intent, including modifications in RT volume or dose, addition of elective pelvic nodal RT, nodal boost to PSMA-positive pelvic nodes, or addition of ADT to salvage RT. Major impact was defined as a substantial change in treatment intent, such as identification of metastatic disease leading to a shift toward systemic treatment, omission of salvage RT, or initiation of metastasis-directed therapy for oligometastatic disease, including stereotactic RT. Biochemical clinical outcomes were evaluated with a minimum follow-up duration of 2 years after PET imaging, with longer follow-up available for some patients.

Image acquisition and analysis

Ga-68 PSMA-11 was synthesized in accordance with regulatory approval as a hospital-compounded radiopharmaceutical, authorized by the regional public health authority. Quality assurance procedures followed the European Pharmacopoeia guidelines. Briefly, ⁶⁸GaCl₃ eluate was incubated with PSMA-11 in HEPES buffer and heated at 100 °C for 10 min, followed by purification and sterile filtration (BIK Therapeutics, Korea). The radiochemical yield exceeded 70%, with purity > 95%.

For PET/CT, Ga-68 PSMA-11 was administered intravenously at 3.0–3.7 MBq/kg. Patients were instructed to drink 500 mL of water prior to imaging and were encouraged to void before image acquisition. Scans were acquired 60 min after injection using digital PET/CT systems (Discovery MI and OMNI Legend, GE Healthcare, USA). Whole-body imaging from the vertex to the upper thighs was performed. CT was used for attenuation correction and anatomical localization. PET data were acquired in 3D mode with acquisition times of 3 min 30 s (OMNI Legend) or 2 min 30 s (Discovery MI) per bed position. Images were reconstructed using the QCHD (VUE Point HD & Q.clear) method (5.0 mm Filter cutoff, 3 iterations, 22 subsets, 256 × 256 matrix, / GE OMNI Legend, GE Healthcare, USA) and QCFX(VUE Point FX & Q.clear) method (5.0 mm Filter cutoff, 3 iterations, 16 subsets, 256 × 256 matrix, / GE Discovery MI, GE Healthcare, USA).

Qualitative visual analysis of PET/CT images was performed, and focal PSMA uptake exceeding surrounding background activity was considered positive. Equivocal findings were defined according to PSMA-RADS category 3 [19]. Two nuclear medicine specialists (S.P. and T.K.) independently reviewed the images, and discrepancies were resolved by a third reader (S.K.).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.0.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Austria). Continuous variables are expressed as median (range). Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value < 0.05.

Results

Between September 2022 and February 2024, a total of 75 patients were retrospectively enrolled. The baseline characteristics of the study population are summarized in Table 1. The median age was 71 years, and the median PSA level prior to Ga-68 PSMA PET/CT was 0.3 ng/mL (0.1–1.6). Among the study population, 13 patients had PSA levels < 0.2 ng/mL, 41 had PSA levels of 0.2–0.5 ng/mL, and 21 had PSA levels ≥ 0.5 ng/mL. Ga-68 PSMA PET/CT demonstrated positive findings in 21 patients (28.0%), with an additional 7 patients (9.3%) showing equivocal findings, for a combined positivity rate of 37.3%. According to PSA level, PSMA PET/CT positivity was observed in 3 of 13 patients (23.1%) with PSA levels < 0.2 ng/mL, 13 of 41 patients (31.7%) with PSA levels of 0.2–0.5 ng/mL, and 12 of 21 patients (57.1%) with PSA levels ≥ 0.5 ng/mL. All of equivocal findings were located in the ribs or femur. At the time of radical prostatectomy, 41 patients (54.7%) had an International Society of Urological Pathology (ISUP) grade group ≥ 3. Excluding equivocal findings, positive Ga-68 PSMA PET/CT findings were observed in 15 patients (36.6%) with an ISUP grade group ≥ 3, compared with 6 patients (17.6%) with an ISUP grade group < 3 (Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.08).

Clinical management change

The initial management plans prior to PSMA PET/CT included salvage radiotherapy (sRT), ADT, combined sRT and ADT, sRT with elective pelvic nodal radiation, and observation. Following Ga-68 PSMA PET/CT, changes in clinical management occurred in 19 patients (25.3%), including moderate changes in 10 patients and major changes in 9 patients (Fig. 1). Moderate changes included the addition of elective pelvic nodal radiation, nodal boost to pelvic nodes (Fig. 2), and the addition of ADT. Major changes included stereotactic ablative radiotherapy (SABR) to distant metastatic lesions (Fig. 3), pelvic lymph node dissection, and initiation of ADT instead of sRT. In the remaining patients, no change in the original treatment intent was observed. A total of 36 patients underwent sRT to the prostate bed, 11 were managed with observation, 7 underwent sRT with ADT, 1 underwent sRT with elective pelvic radiation, and 1 received ADT.

Follow-up of patients

With a minimum follow-up of 2 years, data were available for 69 patients. Among these, 49 patients (71.0%) had PSA levels ≤ 0.20 ng/mL, including 36 patients (52.2%) with undetectable PSA (less than 0.03) and 13 patients (18.8%) with PSA levels ≤ 0.20 ng/mL. Among patients with PSA levels > 0.20 ng/mL, 4 developed distant metastases during follow-up.

Discussion

In this retrospective study of patients with suspected BCR, Ga-68 PSMA PET/CT demonstrated a detection rate of 28%, with an additional 9.3% of patients showing equivocal findings predominantly in the ribs and femur. Higher detection rates were observed in patients with ISUP grade group ≥ 3, although the difference did not reach statistical significance. It should be noted that detection rate reflects the diagnostic performance of Ga-68 PSMA PET/CT, whereas management change reflects its downstream clinical utility; these two metrics capture distinct aspects of diagnostic performance and downstream clinical impact. In this context, Ga-68 PSMA PET/CT was associated with changes in clinical management in 25.3% of patients, including both moderate and major modifications. At a minimum follow-up of 2 years, the majority of patients (71%) had PSA levels ≤ 0.20 ng/mL, while a small subset developed distant metastases during follow-up.

The sensitivity of Ga-68 PSMA PET/CT in BCR is well known to be closely related to PSA levels. Meta-analyses and large patient cohorts have reported detection rates of approximately 30% at PSA levels < 0.2 ng/mL and 50–65% at PSA levels of 0.5–1.0 ng/mL [20, 21]. In contrast, studies including patients with higher PSA levels typically report detection rates ≥ 50–70%. For example, Burgard et al. (n = 321, all PSA ≤ 0.2 ng/mL) reported an overall detection rate of 29.6%, with no significant difference between Ga-68 PSMA-11 and other tracers [22]. In mixed-PSA cohorts, detection rates often exceed 70%, likely reflecting the inclusion of patients with higher PSA levels [23]. In this context, the overall detection rate of 28% (21/75), excluding equivocal findings, observed in our study is relatively low but consistent with the expected range for cohorts with low PSA levels (median 0.3 ng/mL). In addition to PSA, other factors such as higher ISUP grade, shorter PSA doubling time, and the use of advanced PET technology may further influence detection and should be considered when interpreting these findings.

Although Ga-68 PSMA PET/CT demonstrates excellent sensitivity for detecting recurrence [24, 25], false-positive uptake, particularly in isolated bone lesions or benign soft-tissue sites, may lead to misinterpretation and inappropriate management [26]. Various benign conditions, including degenerative changes, fractures, fibrous dysplasia, and Paget’s disease, are known to show increased PSMA uptake [27, 28]. In our cohort, 7 patients (9.3%) had equivocal bone lesions, all located in the ribs (Fig. 4) or femur, none of which resulted in a change in clinical management. Five patients underwent salvage radiotherapy, while two were managed with observation based on patient preference. With the exception of one patient who had a PSA level of 0.04 ng/mL after sRT, all others achieved undetectable PSA levels, supporting the likely benign nature of these equivocal findings. Importantly, uncritical interpretation of such lesions as metastatic disease may lead to overtreatment, including unnecessary radiotherapy, pelvic lymph node dissection. Although no large-scale studies have systematically quantified the harm associated with false-positive PSMA findings, prior reports have highlighted their potential clinical consequences [26, 29]. To mitigate this risk, careful interpretation of equivocal PSMA findings is essential. Correlation of PSMA-avid bone lesions with MRI or CT, particularly when lesions are solitary or atypical, short-interval follow-up imaging or PSA monitoring to assess persistence, consideration of biopsy for accessible lesions when uncertainty remains, and multidisciplinary review may help guide appropriate management. In addition, structured interpretation systems such as PSMA-RADS may aid in identifying low-confidence lesions [19, 30]. Awareness of tracer-specific pitfalls is also important, as F-18 PSMA-1007 has been associated with a higher frequency of nonspecific bone uptake compared with Ga-68 tracers [31].

Beyond imaging-related factors, underlying biological differences might also influence PSMA expression and imaging performance. A limited body of literature has proposed that ethnic differences in prostate cancer biology could be relevant to the interpretation of PSMA PET/CT in Asian populations [15, 32], although this remains an area of active investigation rather than an established conclusion. Specifically, some reports describe that prostate cancer in Asian patients exhibits a distinct molecular landscape, characterized by a relative enrichment of ETS-related gene (ERG)-negative, androgen receptor (AR)-driven subtypes, lower frequencies of PTEN alterations, and a higher prevalence of FOXA1 alterations [33, 34]. These population-specific genomic features might influence AR-related tumor biology; however, whether they are associated with differences in PSMA expression or PSMA PET/CT performance has not been established. With respect to our clinical findings, the detection rate in patients with suspected BCR was comparable to those reported internationally, suggesting that the diagnostic performance of Ga-68 PSMA PET/CT in our population was broadly consistent with global experience. The rate of management change was also broadly in line with that reported in previous studies [12, 18], although this measure reflects downstream clinical utility rather than diagnostic performance. Any potential relationship between these biological features and our findings requires further investigation in larger prospective studies. Given the single-center retrospective design and relatively small sample size of the present study, selection bias cannot be excluded and the generalizability of the findings may be limited. It should also be noted that our statistical analysis was limited to univariable comparisons, and multivariable analysis was not performed. Consequently, potential confounding factors that may influence both PSMA PET/CT positivity and subsequent management decisions could be influenced. A better understanding of the relevant biological and clinical characteristics, together with multivariable analyses, may help identify independent factors associated with PSMA PET/CT positivity and management changes. This may ultimately help clarify the variability in PSMA PET/CT detection performance and clinical utility.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the detection rate of Ga-68 PSMA PET/CT in this Korean cohort was comparable with that reported in the international literature, indicating detection performance consistent with that observed in similar clinical settings. Imaging findings were associated with management changes in approximately one quarter of patients, indicating the potential downstream clinical utility of Ga-68 PSMA PET/CT in informing treatment decisions for suspected BCR. Taken together, our findings support the role of Ga-68 PSMA PET/CT as a valuable decision-making tool in early BCR, while emphasizing that imaging findings should be interpreted in conjunction with clinical parameters and, when necessary, corroborated with additional imaging modalities or follow-up to optimize patient management.
