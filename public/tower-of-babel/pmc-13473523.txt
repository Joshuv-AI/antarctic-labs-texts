Adaptive spatiotemporal graph learning for multi-horizon probabilistic wind power forecasting
Authors: Dong Hua, Geyu Huang, Peiyi Cui, Suisheng Liu, Bojun Deng, Yuanting Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate and reliable forecasting of wind power generation is a cornerstone for ensuring the operational flexibility, economic efficiency, and grid stability of modern power systems with high renewable penetration. While recent advances in deep learning have improved point and probabilistic forecasting, most existing methods still emphasize error minimization at isolated horizons or locations, neglecting the spatiotemporal dependencies, uncertainty calibration, and temporal coherence that are critical in real-world decision-making contexts. In this study, we propose a novel adaptive spatiotemporal graph neural network (ST-GNN) framework for multi-horizon probabilistic wind power forecasting, designed to jointly optimize deterministic accuracy, distributional sharpness, and temporal stability. The framework integrates site-specific meteorological inputs and SCADA data into a dynamically evolving graph structure, where edges are adaptively reweighted to capture changing inter-site correlations driven by wind climatology. A unified multi-objective loss function is formulated, combining mean error minimization with probabilistic metrics such as the Continuous Ranked Probability Score (CRPS) and a temporal smoothness regularizer that mitigates spurious fluctuations across consecutive horizons. Comprehensive case studies are conducted using two years of high-resolution data from a wind farm cluster consisting of twelve geographically diverse sites. Results demonstrate that the proposed model achieves up to 13% reduction in RMSE at 1–4 hour horizons and maintains improvements above 5% at 24-hour horizons when compared with state-of-the-art baselines. Probabilistic evaluations indicate a reduction in calibration error, with empirical coverage deviations generally within 3% across central quantiles and uncertainty estimates that adapt to different meteorological regimes. Moreover, the temporal rank-order consistency of predictions remains significantly higher than existing models, reducing dispatch-related instabilities and improving multi-site coordination. These findings suggest that the integration of spatiotemporal graph structures with probabilistic training objectives can provide a flexible and potentially generalizable paradigm.Subject terms: Climate sciences, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The integration of large-scale wind power into modern power systems has emerged as both a technical opportunity and an operational challenge. As decarbonization targets tighten and renewable portfolio standards expand, wind generation is expected to play a dominant role in meeting global energy demand1,2. However, the inherently intermittent and non-stationary nature of wind resources introduces substantial uncertainty into short- and medium-term power system operations. Accurate forecasting of wind power is therefore a cornerstone of secure, economic, and reliable grid management, influencing a broad spectrum of decision-making processes including unit commitment, reserve scheduling, congestion management, and market bidding3–5. Traditional forecasting approaches, ranging from statistical time series models to deep learning architectures, have delivered steady progress in predictive accuracy. Yet, most existing methods treat each wind farm as an independent forecasting task or assume that spatial correlations can be adequately captured through simple feature concatenation, overlooking the dynamic and heterogeneous nature of spatiotemporal dependencies between geographically distributed sites6,7.

In practice, wind farms do not operate in isolation. Their generation patterns are often correlated due to shared meteorological drivers, terrain-induced flow effects, and atmospheric phenomena such as wake propagation or weather front movement. These spatial dependencies evolve over time, influenced by changing wind directions, pressure gradients, and mesoscale climatic conditions. Temporal dependencies, on the other hand, arise from the autocorrelated nature of wind speed and power output, as well as from the persistence of weather regimes. Existing forecasting frameworks generally lack a unified mechanism to model such dynamic spatiotemporal interactions explicitly and adaptively, especially under multi-horizon prediction requirements where both accuracy and reliability need to be sustained across several hours ahead. Without capturing these dependencies, forecast outputs risk being spatially incoherent or temporally unstable, which can lead to inefficient or even risky operational decisions8–10.

The objective of this research is to develop a novel forecasting methodology that explicitly models and leverages both the spatial and temporal relationships inherent in multi-site wind power data, while simultaneously providing calibrated probabilistic forecasts. This work proposes an adaptive spatiotemporal graph neural network (ST-GNN) framework capable of learning time-varying inter-farm relationships from heterogeneous data sources, integrating them with temporal sequence modeling to produce multi-horizon predictions. The model is designed to operate on a dynamically evolving graph where nodes represent wind farms and edges encode learned measures of spatial influence that adapt to meteorological regimes. By embedding physical insights into the graph construction process–such as gating edge strengths by wind direction and imposing constraints derived from turbine characteristics–the proposed framework remains both data-driven and physically interpretable. The proposed approach follows a modular pipeline. First, multi-source inputs are encoded, combining historical SCADA records, high-resolution numerical weather predictions, and geospatial attributes. A dynamic adjacency matrix is then computed to reflect the current and anticipated connectivity between sites, capturing both static factors like distance and dynamic factors like directional wind alignment. Graph neural network layers propagate information across this adaptive topology, enabling each site’s forecast to benefit from relevant upstream conditions at other locations. Temporal dependencies are modeled using recurrent neural units that operate on the graph-refined embeddings, ensuring that sequence patterns are preserved and multi-horizon consistency is achieved. The prediction head produces both point forecasts and probabilistic outputs, the latter expressed in quantiles and parametric distributional forms, ensuring that uncertainty is communicated alongside central estimates. The model is trained end-to-end under a unified objective that jointly minimizes deterministic errors, probabilistic scoring rules, and structural regularizers that enforce sparsity, smoothness, and temporal stability in the learned graph.

What distinguishes this work from previous efforts is its combination of three elements rarely addressed together: a truly adaptive spatial graph that reconfigures with weather regimes, a temporal learning module that directly consumes these dynamic spatial encodings, and a dual-output probabilistic structure that offers both quantile-based and distributional forecasts. This triad is further strengthened by a set of rigorously defined constraints that inject domain knowledge into the learning process, ensuring that the forecasts adhere to turbine operational limits, quantile ordering, and empirical coverage targets. The resulting model not only improves forecast accuracy in conventional metrics but also delivers spatially coherent and temporally reliable predictions–attributes essential for operational scenarios such as regional reserve allocation or coordinated dispatch.

The methodological novelty extends beyond model architecture to the training process, which incorporates a unified loss function blending multiple objectives with physical and statistical regularization terms. This design ensures that improvements in one aspect of performance do not come at the expense of others, yielding a balanced and operationally robust forecasting tool. Additionally, the framework’s flexibility allows it to ingest new data sources, adapt to new wind farm configurations, and be deployed in markets or grid regions with varying meteorological characteristics. In summary, this paper makes four main contributions. First, it introduces an adaptive spatiotemporal graph neural network for wind power forecasting that learns and updates inter-farm dependencies in response to evolving meteorological conditions. Second, it embeds physical constraints and domain knowledge into both the model architecture and training process, ensuring operational feasibility and interpretability. Third, it integrates deterministic and probabilistic forecasting in a single end-to-end framework, providing both central estimates and well-calibrated uncertainty measures for multi-horizon predictions. Fourth, it establishes a comprehensive empirical evaluation framework on real-world multi-site datasets, demonstrating consistent improvements over state-of-the-art baselines in both accuracy and reliability, while maintaining stability in the face of high-variability events. Through this combination of adaptive spatial modeling, temporal reasoning, probabilistic output, and physical realism, the proposed work advances the frontier of wind power forecasting toward methods that are not only more accurate but also more aligned with the operational needs of future renewable-rich power systems.

Literature review

The forecasting of wind power has evolved through several distinct methodological eras, reflecting advances in data availability, computational capacity, and the growing operational importance of renewable generation. Early studies were dominated by statistical time series models such as autoregressive moving average (ARMA) and its variants, including ARIMA and seasonal ARIMA, which sought to capture autocorrelation structures in wind speed or power output. These approaches often relied on stationarity assumptions and linear relationships, limiting their ability to adapt to the non-linear and non-stationary nature of wind processes11–13. Efforts to extend these models incorporated exogenous variables such as temperature, pressure, and upstream wind measurements, leading to ARIMAX and transfer function frameworks. While these enhancements improved short-term accuracy, the linear modeling structure still constrained the capture of complex interactions between meteorological drivers and turbine performance14,15.

The emergence of numerical weather prediction (NWP) models provided a richer set of predictors for wind power forecasting. Integrating NWP outputs with statistical post-processing techniques, such as model output statistics (MOS) or bias correction, became standard practice. Studies demonstrated that blending site-specific measurements with mesoscale weather forecasts improved performance for horizons beyond a few hours. However, such integrations often treated each wind farm independently, failing to account for spatial dependencies between geographically distributed sites. This omission is significant, given that weather systems, especially frontal passages and low-pressure systems, influence multiple wind farms in correlated patterns that evolve over time16–18. Machine learning methods began to reshape the field by offering greater flexibility in capturing non-linear relationships. Support vector regression (SVR), decision trees, and ensemble methods such as random forests and gradient boosting were widely explored. These approaches handled high-dimensional feature sets more effectively than classical statistical methods and showed improved performance when combined with meteorological inputs. Nevertheless, they typically relied on handcrafted features and static learning structures, which did not explicitly model temporal dynamics beyond short lags, nor did they integrate spatial correlations in a principled way19,20.

Adaptive and regime-aware forecasting under non-stationarity

Recent studies have increasingly recognized that wind power generation exhibits strong non-stationary behavior driven by evolving meteorological regimes, seasonal transitions, and mesoscale atmospheric dynamics. To address this challenge, a growing body of work has focused on adaptive and regime-aware forecasting frameworks that explicitly model time-varying system dynamics. One line of research leverages regime-switching mechanisms, such as hidden Markov models or clustering-based approaches, to partition wind patterns into distinct states and train specialized predictors for each regime. While these methods improve local accuracy, they often rely on predefined regime definitions and lack the flexibility to adapt continuously to evolving conditions. Another direction explores adaptive learning strategies, including online learning, attention mechanisms, and dynamic parameter updating, enabling models to adjust their behavior in response to changing data distributions. In particular, attention-based temporal models and adaptive weighting schemes have demonstrated improved responsiveness to non-stationary inputs, though many of these approaches focus primarily on temporal dynamics without explicitly capturing spatial interdependencies. More recently, regime-aware spatiotemporal models have emerged, integrating graph-based representations with adaptive mechanisms to account for evolving spatial relationships. These models attempt to reflect how wind propagation patterns and inter-site correlations shift under different meteorological conditions. However, existing approaches often employ either static graph structures or partially adaptive mechanisms, and typically lack a unified treatment of spatial adaptation, temporal consistency, and probabilistic uncertainty within a single framework. Compared with these recent developments, the proposed framework advances the state of the art by introducing a fully adaptive spatiotemporal graph structure that evolves continuously with meteorological conditions, while simultaneously incorporating temporal regularization and probabilistic calibration. This integrated design enables the model to capture non-stationary dynamics more effectively, maintain consistency across forecasting horizons, and produce reliable uncertainty estimates, addressing key limitations observed in prior adaptive and regime-aware forecasting methods.

The advent of deep learning brought a new wave of architectures tailored to sequential data. Recurrent neural networks (RNNs), and particularly long short-term memory (LSTM) networks and gated recurrent units (GRUs), became popular for their ability to capture long-range temporal dependencies in wind speed and power series. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and temporal convolutional networks (TCNs) were also introduced, offering parallelizable and stable alternatives for sequence modeling. Studies comparing LSTM, GRU, and TCN approaches reported that deep architectures could outperform traditional models, particularly when trained on large volumes of high-resolution SCADA and meteorological data21,22. Still, most applications focused on single-site forecasting or treated spatial effects in an ad hoc manner, such as through concatenation of features from nearby sites without explicit modeling of their interdependencies. Spatiotemporal modeling has emerged as a promising direction for wind power forecasting, drawing inspiration from research in traffic flow prediction, climate modeling, and sensor networks. Graph-based approaches, in particular, have gained traction for their ability to represent irregular spatial topologies. Graph convolutional networks (GCNs) extend convolution operations to non-Euclidean domains, enabling the aggregation of information from neighboring nodes in a network. Initial applications in renewable forecasting have used static adjacency matrices based on geographical distance or correlation coefficients, applying GCN layers in combination with temporal models like LSTM to create spatiotemporal hybrid architectures. These models have shown that explicitly encoding the spatial structure can improve forecast coherence and stability across multiple sites. However, static graphs fail to capture the dynamic nature of wind farm relationships, which vary with changing meteorological conditions23,24.

Adaptive graph learning represents a more recent evolution, in which the adjacency structure itself becomes a learnable and time-varying component of the model. In the context of wind forecasting, this allows the network to adjust spatial connectivity in response to evolving weather regimes, wind direction shifts, and localized turbulence25,26. Some studies have incorporated attention mechanisms into the graph structure, enabling the model to weight neighbors according to their current relevance rather than fixed proximity measures. While these adaptive mechanisms have demonstrated improvements in capturing spatiotemporal dependencies, many implementations have not integrated physical constraints or domain-specific priors, potentially limiting interpretability and operational acceptance. Probabilistic forecasting has developed in parallel as a means to quantify uncertainty in wind power predictions. Approaches such as quantile regression, Bayesian neural networks, and ensemble learning have been used to generate prediction intervals or full probabilistic distributions. Scoring rules like the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS) and pinball loss have provided rigorous evaluation metrics for these probabilistic outputs. Combining probabilistic forecasting with spatiotemporal models remains relatively underexplored, with most work focusing on either deterministic spatiotemporal architectures or probabilistic models that operate at single sites. Hybrid approaches that integrate physical modeling with machine learning have also gained interest. Coupling outputs from physical models like the Weather Research and Forecasting (WRF) system with data-driven post-processing allows the exploitation of both meteorological theory and statistical learning. Some works have incorporated turbine power curve physics directly into the loss function or network architecture, constraining predictions to feasible ranges. Others have integrated wake modeling or turbulence intensity estimates into feature engineering. These hybrid strategies aim to balance predictive performance with physical realism, a balance that is crucial for operational deployment. Regularization and constraint enforcement in deep learning-based forecasting have been recognized as important for achieving stable and interpretable models. Graph Laplacian regularization, sparsity constraints, and temporal smoothness penalties have been employed to promote coherence and avoid overfitting. In probabilistic settings, quantile crossing penalties and coverage calibration constraints have been used to ensure the validity of uncertainty estimates. However, the systematic integration of such constraints into a unified end-to-end framework, especially one that addresses both spatial and temporal aspects adaptively, has been rare. The literature on multi-horizon forecasting emphasizes the challenge of maintaining accuracy and calibration across increasing lead times. Sequence-to-sequence architectures, multi-output regression, and recursive prediction strategies have been compared, each with trade-offs in error propagation and temporal consistency. Recent work has explored multi-task learning setups in which forecasts for different horizons are learned jointly, sharing intermediate representations to improve generalization. Yet, in the domain of wind power, these strategies have often been implemented without an explicit graph-based spatiotemporal backbone or with only static spatial encodings24,27.

In addition, recent advances have explored robust and adaptive forecasting under distribution shifts, which are particularly relevant in renewable energy systems where data distributions evolve due to changing weather regimes and system conditions. Zhao et al.28 proposed a causal self-supervised graph learning framework that enhances robustness against distribution shifts by leveraging invariant causal representations across varying conditions. This approach improves generalization by reducing reliance on spurious correlations in historical data. In a related line of work, Zhao et al.29 introduced a missing data-aware spatiotemporal graph network with a hierarchical downsampling–upsampling structure, which explicitly addresses data incompleteness and enhances robustness in real-world forecasting scenarios. Their method demonstrates improved resilience to both missing data and structural variability in load patterns. Furthermore, Zhao et al.30 developed a spatiotemporal pyramidal graph modeling approach for short-term load forecasting, which captures multi-scale temporal dependencies and hierarchical spatial features. This pyramidal design enables the model to better adapt to complex and evolving demand patterns across different temporal resolutions. While these studies provide important advances in handling distribution shifts, missing data, and multi-scale dynamics, they primarily focus on either robustness or representation learning aspects. In contrast, the proposed framework aims to complement these directions by jointly addressing adaptive spatial dependency learning, temporal consistency across forecasting horizons, and probabilistic uncertainty calibration within a unified model, thereby providing a more comprehensive solution for non-stationary wind power forecasting. In sum, the trajectory of wind power forecasting research reflects a gradual but incomplete shift from independent-site, point-based predictions toward integrated, spatiotemporal, and probabilistic modeling. While advances in deep learning, graph neural networks, and adaptive modeling have laid a foundation for capturing the complex dynamics of wind generation, the combination of dynamically learned spatial structures, temporally aware sequence modeling, and physically constrained probabilistic outputs remains a frontier that has only begun to be addressed in the literature.

Results

The empirical evaluation of the proposed adaptive spatiotemporal graph neural network (ST-GNN) framework is conducted using a comprehensive dataset collected from a real-world wind farm cluster located in an inland–coastal transitional region of eastern China. The cluster comprises 12 utility-scale wind farms, each equipped with between 20 and 36 turbines rated at 2.5 MW, yielding an aggregate installed capacity of approximately 810 MW. Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA) data were acquired at a 10-minute resolution over a continuous 24-month period spanning January 2022 to December 2023, resulting in 105,120 timestamped records per site. Each record contains turbine-level and farm-level variables, including active power output (MW), rotor speed (rpm), nacelle wind speed (m/s), wind direction (degrees), and ambient temperature (C). In addition to SCADA data, high-resolution Numerical Weather Prediction (NWP) forecasts from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) Integrated Forecasting System were retrieved at a 3 km spatial resolution and 1-hour temporal resolution, providing meteorological predictors such as multi-height wind speed (10 m, 80 m, 100 m), air pressure (hPa), relative humidity (%), and temperature (C). Geospatial features, including farm altitude (m), terrain roughness (dimensionless), and relative position vectors between wind farms, were extracted from a digital elevation model with 30 m resolution.

Prior to model training, the dataset underwent a rigorous preprocessing pipeline. Missing or corrupted SCADA entries, accounting for approximately 0.8% of total observations, were imputed using a k-nearest neighbor approach constrained by physical plausibility limits derived from turbine specifications. NWP forecasts were temporally aligned to SCADA timestamps via cubic spline interpolation and spatially collocated to each farm centroid. All continuous variables were normalized using z-score scaling computed from the training set, while categorical features, such as wind sector classifications, were one-hot encoded. To assess generalization across varying meteorological regimes, the dataset was split chronologically into 70% for training (January 2022 to August 2023), 15% for validation (September 2023 to November 2023), and 15% for testing (December 2023 to February 2024). The test set was deliberately chosen to include both winter monsoon and transitional season conditions, which are known to exhibit rapid wind variability and directional shifts. The forecasting task targeted lead times of 1 to 24 hours ahead in hourly increments, yielding 24 separate prediction horizons per input timestamp.

The computational experiments were executed on a high-performance workstation equipped with two NVIDIA A100 GPUs (40 GB each), dual AMD EPYC 7742 CPUs clocked at 2.25 GHz, and 512 GB of DDR4 RAM, running Ubuntu 22.04 LTS. All models were implemented in Python 3.10, leveraging PyTorch 2.0 for neural network components and DGL 1.1 for graph construction and message passing operations. Data preprocessing and feature engineering pipelines were managed through Pandas 2.1 and NumPy 1.26, with visualization support from Matplotlib 3.8 and Seaborn 0.13. Training was performed using the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of , decayed by a factor of 0.7 upon validation loss plateau, and a batch size of 256 time steps per GPU. Early stopping with a patience of 15 epochs was employed to prevent overfitting. On average, the proposed ST-GNN model required approximately 9 hours to converge for the full 24-horizon joint training, while baseline models completed within 3–6 hours. All experiments were repeated over three random initialization seeds to ensure robustness, and results are reported as averages across these runs.

This adaptive sparsification behavior allows the model to shift between more centralized topologies during convective events and more distributed structures during stable regimes. The interpretability of WFII values supports physically grounded analysis of the learned graph, particularly when tracking how individual sites transition between influencing and receiving roles across weather conditions.

Conclusion

This study proposes a unified adaptive spatiotemporal graph neural network (ST-GNN) framework for multi-horizon probabilistic wind power forecasting. By integrating dynamic graph learning, temporal sequence modeling, and probabilistic prediction within a single end-to-end architecture, the proposed method addresses key limitations of existing approaches, particularly the lack of adaptive spatial modeling, temporal inconsistency across forecasting horizons, and insufficient uncertainty calibration. The main contribution of this work lies in three aspects. First, an adaptive graph construction mechanism is developed to capture time-varying inter-site dependencies driven by meteorological conditions, enabling the model to dynamically adjust spatial relationships rather than relying on static connectivity assumptions. Second, a unified multi-objective learning framework is formulated, jointly optimizing point prediction accuracy, probabilistic reliability, and temporal smoothness, which ensures both numerical precision and operational consistency across multiple horizons. Third, the integration of physics-informed constraints and probabilistic calibration techniques improves the interpretability and practical reliability of the forecasting outputs, making them more suitable for real-world grid operation and decision-making. Extensive experiments conducted on real-world multi-site wind farm data demonstrate that the proposed framework consistently outperforms a range of benchmark models across both deterministic and probabilistic metrics. The results show significant improvements in short-term prediction accuracy, stable performance across extended horizons, and well-calibrated uncertainty estimates, suggesting the effectiveness and robustness of the proposed approach. Despite these promising results, several directions for future research remain. First, the current framework can be extended to incorporate additional exogenous information sources, such as satellite observations or large-scale atmospheric circulation indicators, to further enhance predictive capability under extreme weather conditions. Second, the scalability of the dynamic graph learning mechanism could be explored for larger regional or national wind farm networks with higher spatial complexity. Third, future work may investigate the integration of advanced architectures, such as transformer-based temporal encoders or foundation models, to further improve long-horizon forecasting performance. Finally, the deployment of the proposed model in real-time operational environments, including its interaction with power system dispatch and market mechanisms, remains an important avenue for practical validation. Overall, this work provides a systematic and extensible framework for spatiotemporal probabilistic wind power forecasting, bridging the gap between data-driven learning, physical interpretability, and operational applicability in modern renewable energy systems.
