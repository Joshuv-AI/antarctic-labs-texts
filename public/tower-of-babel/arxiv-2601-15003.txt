Note on the multiplicity of solutions for nonlinear scalar field equations with a critical inverse-square potential
Bartosz Bieganowski, Daniel Strzelecki

Abstract
. We are interested in the multiplicity of solutions to the following scalar field equation − Δ ​ u − ( N − 2 ) 2 4 ​ | x | 2 ​ u = g ⁡ ( u ) , in ​ ℝ N ∖ { 0 } . -\Delta u-\frac{(N-2)^{2}}{4|x|^{2}}u=g(u),\quad\mbox{in }\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\}. We establish the existence of infinitely many radial and non-radial solutions. Keywords: variational methods, singular potential, scalar field equation, critical Hardy potential AMS Subject Classification: 35Q99, 35J10, 35J20, 58E99



1.
Introduction

We study the following scalar field equation

(1.1)

-\Delta u-\frac{(N-2)^{2}}{4|x|^{2}}u=g(u),\quad\mbox{in }\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\},

where
N\geq 3
, and we are interested in the multiplicity of solutions.
Equation (
1.1
) involves the inverse-square Hardy potential with the optimal constant, which places the problem at the critical threshold of the Hardy inequality and leads to substantial analytical difficulties.

The nonlinear scalar field equation

-\Delta u=g(u)

is well understood, starting from the seminal works of Berestycki and Lions
[
1
,
2
]
, who established existence results under general assumptions on
g
, including the celebrated Berestycki-Lions conditions.
Further developments addressed qualitative properties of solutions as well as symmetry and uniqueness issues.
Later, the existence and multiplicity of nonradial solutions were obtained in
[
12
]
and
[
16
]
, among others.

The literature is far more sparse regarding the critical inverse-square Hardy potential

-\frac{(N-2)^{2}}{4|x|^{2}}
is present with the optimal constant, usually referred to as the
critical Hardy potential
.
To the best of our knowledge, the first contribution addressing the existence of solutions for the pure power nonlinearity
g(u)=|u|^{p-2}u
,
2<p<2^{*}:=\frac{2N}{N-2}
, is due to Mukherjee et al.
[
17
]
, who obtained existence results by exploiting a Hardy-Gagliardo-Nirenberg inequality and minimizing the associated Weinstein functional. The inverse-square potential
V(x)=-\frac{\mu}{|x|^{2}}
falls into the class of the so-called
singular transitional potentials

[
9
]
and it is studied by many authors from the mathematical and physical point of view
[
5
,
24
,
15
,
10
,
4
,
18
,
6
,
13
]
.

One of the main difficulties in studying (
1.1
) is the lack of coercivity of the quadratic form on
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
generated by the operator

-\Delta-\frac{(N-2)^{2}}{4|x|^{2}}
.
As a consequence, the natural energy space associated with (
1.1
) is strictly larger than
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and cannot be characterized as a standard Sobolev space.
Such a space was introduced independently in
[
17
,
22
,
23
]
, where some of its fundamental properties were established.

A major additional difficulty is that this energy space is not translation invariant, which renders the classical concentration-compactness principle of Lions inapplicable in a direct way.
In a recent contribution
[
3
]
, the functional framework was refined and a suitable version of the concentration-compactness approach was developed, leading to the existence of a nontrivial weak solution to (
1.1
).
It is also shown therein that working in the enlarged energy space is essential: if a solution is sufficiently regular (for instance, nonnegative and of class
{\mathcal{C}}^{2}
), then it does not belong to
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, cf.
[
21
]
.

The solution constructed in
[
3
]
is obtained as a minimizer of the energy functional on the Pohožaev manifold and is radial provided that
g
is odd.
However, due to the lack of full regularity theory in the critical Hardy setting, it is not known whether all weak solutions satisfy the Pohožaev identity.
It is also shown in
[
3
]
that equation (
1.1
) admits at least one nonradial solution.

In the present paper, we establish the multiplicity of both radial and nonradial solutions to (
1.1
).
Our approach builds upon the functional framework developed in
[
3
]
and exploits the additional symmetry induced by the oddness of the nonlinearity, by the application of the abstract critical point theorem due to Ikoma
[
11
]
.
Accordingly, we work under the same assumptions as in
[
3
]
, with the additional requirement that the right-hand side is odd.

We impose the following general conditions on the nonlinearity
g
, already introduced in
[
3
]
:

(G1)

g:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is continuous and odd;

(G2)

-\infty<\liminf_{s\to 0}\frac{g(s)}{s}\leq\limsup_{s\to 0}\frac{g(s)}{s}=-\omega<0
;

(G3)

\lim_{|s|\to\infty}\frac{g(s)}{|s|^{p-1}}=0
and
\lim_{|s|\to\infty}g(s)s\geq 0
for some
p\in\left(2,2^{*}\right)
;

(G4)

there exists
\zeta_{0}>0
such that
G(\zeta_{0})>0
, where
G(s):=\int_{0}^{s}g(\tau)\,d\tau
;

(G5)

there exist
\nu>2
and
\gamma_{0}<0
such that

G(s)-\frac{1}{\nu}g(s)s\leq\gamma_{0}s^{2}\quad\text{for all }s\in\mathbb{R}.

Under these assumptions, the solutions of (
1.1
) can be interpreted as the so-called
standing waves

\Psi(t,x)=e^{i\omega t}u(x)
of the nonlinear Schrödinger equation with a critical inverse-square potential

i\partial_{t}\Psi=-\Delta\Psi-\frac{(N-2)^{2}}{4|x|^{2}}\Psi-f(|\Psi|)\Psi,\quad(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{N},

where
g(u)=\omega u+f(|u|)u
. The critical constant
\mu_{c}:=\frac{(N-2)^{2}}{4}
represents the threshold for the coerciveness of the quadratic form associated with the Hamiltonian
{\mathcal{H}}_{\mu}:=-\Delta-\frac{\mu}{|x|^{2}}
. More precisely, if
\mu<\mu_{c}
, the corresponding quadratic form is coercive on the Sobolev space
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and power functions
\psi_{\pm}=|x|^{\frac{-(N-2)\pm\sqrt{(N-2)^{2}-4\mu}}{2}}
belonging to
L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
satisfy
{\mathcal{H}}_{\mu}\psi_{\pm}=0
. If
\mu=\mu_{c}
, we have
{\mathcal{H}}_{\mu_{c}}\left(|x|^{-\frac{N-2}{2}}\right)=0
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{\mu_{c}}\left(|x|^{-\frac{N-2}{2}}\ln|x|\right)=0
. In contrast, when
\mu>\mu_{c}
, coerciveness is lost,
\sqrt{(N-2)^{2}-4\mu}
becomes imaginary and the system exhibits the so-called
fall to the center
phenomenon, describing a quantum-mechanical instability driven by the strong singular attraction at the origin (see e.g.
[
20
,
7
]
).

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the functional and variational framework, including the definition of the energy space and a discussion of its main properties, following
[
3
]
and the abstract critical point theory developed in
[
11
]
. In Section
3
, we state the main result and provide its proof.

2.
Functional and variational setting

2.1.
The
X^{1}
space

Recall the classical Hardy inequality

(2.1)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{u^{2}}{|x|^{2}}\,dx\leq\frac{4}{(N-2)^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx,\quad u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N}).

This inequality implies that the functional

(2.2)

\|u\|^{2}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx-\frac{(N-2)^{2}}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{u^{2}}{|x|^{2}}\,dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u^{2}\,dx,\quad u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N}),

defines a norm on
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. However, due to the optimality of the constant
\frac{(N-2)^{2}}{4}
and the fact that minimizers of (
2.1
) do not belong to
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, this norm is not complete in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. To work within a Hilbert space framework, we consider
X^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
as the completion of
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
with respect to (
2.2
). This space has been investigated in
[
22
,
17
,
23
]
.
According to
[
8
, Theorem 1.2]
(see also
[
22
]
), we have the continuous embeddings

(2.3)

H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\subset X^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\subset H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

for any
s\in[0,1)
, where
H^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N}):=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Consequently,

(2.4)

X^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\subset L^{t}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

is continuous for
t\in[2,2^{*})
and locally compact (specifically, the embedding
X^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\subset L^{t}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
is compact).

For functions
u\in X^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\setminus H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, the first two integrals in (
2.2
) are not well-defined. As discussed in
[
23
,
25
]
, the inner product structure on
X^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
requires a generalized characterization. We introduce the singular bilinear form
\xi(\cdot,\cdot)
defined by

(2.5)

\xi(u,v):=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0+}\left(\int_{|x|>\varepsilon}\nabla u\nabla v\,dx-\frac{(N-2)^{2}}{4}\int_{|x|>\varepsilon}\frac{uv}{|x|^{2}}\,dx-\frac{N-2}{2}\varepsilon^{-1}\int_{|x|=\varepsilon}uv\,dS\right).

We also denote
\xi(u):=\xi(u,u)
. The scalar product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and the norm on
X^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
are then given by

(2.6)

\langle u,v\rangle=\xi(u,v)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}uv\,dx,\quad\|u\|^{2}=\xi(u)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u^{2}\,dx.

Note that for
u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, (
2.6
) reduces to the standard formula (
2.2
).

2.2.
Symmetry classes.

To study nonradial solutions, we assume
N\geq 4
and
N\neq 5
. Let
2\leq M\leq\frac{N}{2}
such that
N-2M\neq 1
. We decompose
\mathbb{R}^{N}=\mathbb{R}^{M}\times\mathbb{R}^{M}\times\mathbb{R}^{N-2M}
and write points as
x=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
with
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
and
x_{3}\in\mathbb{R}^{N-2M}
.
We introduce the involution
\tau:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
given by

\tau(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}):=(x_{2},x_{1},x_{3}),

and the corresponding subspace of antisymmetric functions

(2.7)

X_{\tau}:=\left\{u\in X^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N}):u(x)=-u(\tau x)\text{ for a.e. }x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\right\}.

Observe that
X_{\tau}
contains no nontrivial radially symmetric functions.
Additionally, let
\mathcal{O}_{2}:=\mathcal{O}(M)\times\mathcal{O}(M)\times\mathcal{O}(N-2M)\subset\mathcal{O}(N)
and let
X^{1}_{\mathcal{O}_{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\subset X^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
denote the subspace of
\mathcal{O}_{2}
-invariant functions.

To look for radially symmetric solutions, we define
X^{1}_{\mathrm{rad}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\subset X^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
as the subspace of radially symmetric functions, as long as
N\geq 3
.

In what follows, we define the working space
X
as

X:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}X^{1}_{\mathrm{rad}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})&\text{(radial case: $N\geq 3$),}\\
X^{1}_{\mathcal{O}_{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap X_{\tau}&\text{(nonradial case: $N\geq 4$, $N\neq 5$).}\end{array}\right.

Combining the continuous embeddings (
2.3
) with compactness results from
[
14
, Theorems II.1 and III.3]
, we conclude that the embedding

X\subset L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N}),\quad p\in(2,2^{*})

is compact.

2.3.
Variational setting

We define the energy functional
J:X^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

J(u)=\frac{1}{2}\xi(u)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}G(u)\,dx.

Under assumptions (G1)–(G3) and in view of the embeddings (
2.4
), the functional
J
is of class
{\mathcal{C}}^{1}
and its derivative is given by

J^{\prime}(u)(v)=\xi(u,v)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}g(u)v\,dx,\quad u,v\in X^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N}).

It is established that any critical point
u\in X^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
of
J
is a weak solution to (
1.1
) on
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\}
(see
[
3
]
).

We introduce the Pohožaev functional
P:X^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined as

P(u)=\xi(u)-2^{*}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}G(u)\,dx.

Due to the presence of singular terms in
\xi(u)
, it remains an open question whether every critical point
u\in X^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
of
J
satisfies the identity
P(u)=0
. Nevertheless, we will search specifically for solutions that satisfy this condition.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
c\in\mathbb{R}
. We say that
J:X\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies
(\mathrm{PPS})_{c}
if every sequence
(u_{n})\subset X
such that

J(u_{n})\to c,\quad J^{\prime}(u_{n})\to 0,\quad P(u_{n})\to 0,

has a convergent subsequence.

Let us define the scaling action
\Phi:\mathbb{R}\times X^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\rightarrow X^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
by

\Phi(t,u)(x):=u(e^{-t}x).

A direct computation shows that

J(\Phi(t,u))=\frac{e^{(N-2)t}}{2}\xi(u)-e^{Nt}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}G(u)\,dx,

and the map
J\circ\Phi
is of class
{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}\times X^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N}))
.

In this framework, the abstract critical point theorem by Ikoma
[
11
, Theorem 2.4]
takes the following form.

Theorem 2.2
.

Suppose that
J_{\mid X}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{1}(X,\mathbb{R})
is an even functional, i.e.,
J_{\mid X}(-u)=J_{\mid X}(u)
for all
u\in X
. Assume further that:

(i)

J_{\mid X}
satisfies the
(\mathrm{PPS})_{c}
condition for each
c>0
;

(ii)

there exists
\rho_{0}>0
such that
\inf_{\|u\|=\rho_{0}}J_{\mid X}(u)>0=J(0)
;

(iii)

for each
k\geq 1
, there exists an odd map
\gamma_{k}\in{\mathcal{C}}(S^{k-1},X)
such that

\max_{\sigma\in S^{k-1}}J(\gamma_{k}(\sigma))<0\quad\text{and}\quad\gamma_{k}(S^{k-1})\subset X\setminus\overline{B_{\rho_{0}}(0)},

where
S^{k-1}:=\{\sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{k}:|\sigma|=1\}
.

Then there exists a sequence
(u_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}\subset X
such that
(J_{\mid X})^{\prime}(u_{j})=0
,
P(u_{j})=0
, and
J(u_{j})=c_{j}\to\infty
as
j\to\infty
.

Although the theorem provides critical points of the restricted functional
J_{\mid X}
, by the principle of symmetric criticality (see
[
19
]
), these points are also critical points of the unconstrained functional
J
.

3.
Main result

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose that (G1)–(G5) hold. Then,

(i)

if
N\geq 3
, there exists a sequence of radial weak solutions
(u_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}\subset X
of (
1.1
),

(ii)

if
N=4
or
N\geq 6
, there exists a sequence of non-radial weak solutions
(u_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}\subset X
of (
1.1
).

Moreover, in both cases,
u_{j}\in C^{1,\alpha}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\})
for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
P(u_{j})=0
and
J(u_{j})\to\infty
as
j\to\infty
. If, in addition,
g
is Hölder continuous,
u_{j}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\})
.

Proof.

It is enough to verify assumptions (i)–(iii) of Theorem
2.2
, while the regularity of solutions follows directly from
[
3
, Theorem 1.2 (c),(d)]
.

To show (i) take
(u_{n})\subset X
such that
J(u_{n})\to c>0,\,J^{\prime}(u_{n})\to 0
, and
P(u_{n})\to 0
. From
[
3
, Lemma 5.1]
,
(u_{n})
is bounded in
X^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Then
u_{n}\rightharpoonup u_{0}
in
X^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, up to a subsequence. By the compactness of embeddings
u_{n}\to u_{0}
in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
,
p\in(2,2^{*})
. Since
J
is weak-to-weak* continuous,
J^{\prime}(u_{0})=0
. Then

\displaystyle o(1)

\displaystyle=J^{\prime}(u_{n})(u_{n}-u_{0})-J^{\prime}(u_{0})(u_{n}-u_{0})=\xi(u_{n}-u_{0})-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(g(u_{n})-g(u_{0})\right)(u_{n}-u_{0})\,dx

\displaystyle=\xi(u_{n}-u_{0})+\omega\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(u_{n}-u_{0})^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(g(u_{n})+\omega u_{n}\right)(u_{n}-u_{0})\,dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(g(u_{0})+\omega u_{0}\right)(u_{n}-u_{0})\,dx.

Observe that, by (G1)–(G3), for every
\varepsilon>0
there is
C_{\varepsilon}>0
such that

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(g(u_{n})+\omega u_{n}\right)(u_{n}-u_{0})\,dx\right|

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|u_{n}||u_{n}-u_{0}|\,dx+C_{\varepsilon}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|u_{n}|^{p-1}|u_{n}-u_{0}|\,dx

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon+C_{\varepsilon}\|u_{n}-u_{0}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})},

where we used that
(u_{n})
is bounded in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Since
\|u_{n}-u_{0}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\to 0
and
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, we obtain that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(g(u_{n})+\omega u_{n}\right)(u_{n}-u_{0})\,dx\to 0.

Similarly

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(g(u_{0})+\omega u_{0}\right)(u_{n}-u_{0})\,dx\to 0.

Hence
\xi(u_{n}-u_{0})+\omega\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}(u_{n}-u_{0})^{2}\,dx\to 0
, namely
u_{n}\to u_{0}
in
X^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Hence,
(\mathrm{PPS})_{c}
holds.

Condition (ii) follows directly from
[
3
, Lemma 3.1]
, and
\rho_{0}
can be chosen arbitrarily small.

To show (iii), we claim that for every
k\geq 1
there are a radius
R_{k}>0
and an odd map
\overline{\gamma}_{k}\in{\mathcal{C}}(S^{k-1},X)
such that
\mathrm{supp}\,(\overline{\gamma}_{k}(\sigma))\subset B(0,R_{k})
,
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}G\left(\overline{\gamma}_{k}(\sigma)\right)\,dx\geq 1
. In the radial setting it follows from
[
2
, Theorem 10]
, while for the nonradial setting it is a consequence of
[
12
, Lemma 4.2]
. Then, we define
\gamma_{k}
as
\gamma_{k}(\sigma)(x)=\overline{\gamma}_{k}(\sigma)(e^{-\theta_{k}}x)
, where
\theta_{k}>0
is to be chosen. Note that, then

\displaystyle J(\gamma_{k}(\sigma))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\xi(\gamma_{k}(\sigma))-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}G(\gamma_{k}(\sigma))\,dx=\frac{1}{2}e^{(N-2)\theta_{k}}\xi(\overline{\gamma}_{k}(\sigma))-e^{N\theta_{k}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}G(\overline{\gamma}_{k}(\sigma))\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}e^{(N-2)\theta_{k}}\xi(\overline{\gamma}_{k}(\sigma))-e^{N\theta_{k}}.

Hence, for sufficiently large
\theta_{k}>0
, we get
\max_{\sigma\in S^{k-1}}J(\gamma_{k}(\sigma))<0
. Choosing sufficiently small
\rho_{0}
in (ii), we obtain also that
\gamma_{k}(S^{k-1})\subset X\setminus\overline{B_{\rho_{0}}(0)}
and (iii) is satisfied.
∎
