Distributed Triangle Enumeration in Hypergraphs
Duncan Adamson, Will Rosenbaum, Paul G. Spirakis

Abstract
In the last decade, subgraph detection and enumeration have emerged as a central problem in distributed graph algorithms. This is largely due to the theoretical challenges and practical applications of these problems. In this paper, we initiate the systematic study of distributed sub-hypergraph enumeration in hypergraphs. To this end, we (1) introduce several computational models for hypergraphs that generalize the CONGEST model for graphs and evaluate their relative computational power, (2) devise algorithms for distributed triangle enumeration in our computational models and prove their optimality in two such models, (3) introduce classes of sparse and “everywhere sparse” hypergraphs and describe efficient distributed algorithms for triangle enumeration in these classes, and (4) describe general techniques that we believe to be useful for designing efficient algorithms in our hypergraph models.



1
Introduction

In distributed graph algorithms, a network is represented by a graph
G=(V,E)
. The set
V
of vertices represents computing entities (nodes) and edges
e\in E
represent communication links between vertices. Initially, vertices have little or no global information about the network
G
. Yet the goal is to find an efficient procedure for vertices to exchange messages and perform local computations such that the respective local outputs of the vertices satisfy some global specification. Some well-studied problems in this area include local symmetry breaking (e.g., finding maximal independent sets, maximal matchings, and proper colorings), global optimization problems (e.g., minimum spanning trees), identifying local structures (e.g. triangle/clique detection and enumeration), and approximate global optimization (e.g., maximum matchings and stable matchings).

Fundamental to the study of these networks is the
communication model
, the restrictions under which information may be shared between nodes. Our focus is on synchronous models, where executions proceed in synchronous rounds. In each round, vertices may perform unbounded local computation, send messages to neighboring vertices, and receive messages sent from neighboring nodes. The main measure of complexity is the number of rounds necessary and sufficient to perform a given task.
Three well-studied communication models introduced by
[
32
,
33
,
38
]
are:

LOCAL

[
32
]
no restriction on message sizes, but communication is only between adjacent vertices

CONGEST

[
38
]
adjacent pairs of vertices can exchange messages of length
B
bits (typically
B=O(\log n)
, where
n=\abs{V}
is the number of vertices in the graph)

CLIQUE

[
33
]
(or CONGESTED CLIQUE) and the related massively parallel computation (MPC) model
[
24
]
, where each vertex can exchange short (
B
-bit) messages with all other vertices in the network.

These models each emphasize one or more “dimensions” of computational complexity for distributed systems, while abstracting away details such as local computational cost. Complexity in the LOCAL model measures how far in the local network each vertex must see in order to produce its local output. In contrast, complexity in the CLIQUE model measures how many bandwidth-restricted communication rounds are required to perform the task, independent of locality in the underlying graph
G
. Finally, complexity in the CONGEST model gives hybrid measure of complexity with restricted communication and locality in
G
. Thus, for a given task, understanding its computational complexity in these different models provides contrasting views on the aspects of distributed computing that make the problem easy or hard to solve.

The computational models above, however, abstract away many features of distributed systems that may be relevant to understanding the computational power of real-world systems. Two such shortcomings of these models is that they are homogenous (all vertices and edges have precisely the same computational power) and they do not model “higher order” interactions that involve more than two vertices. Indeed, many large-scale networks are hybrid or hierarchical in nature. The internet is best viewed as a “network of networks” with low-latency, high bandwidth connections between devices on a local area network, and potentially costlier connectivity between devices in different local networks
[
4
,
39
]
; data centers consist of thousands of servers, each with dozens or hundreds of cores on a single device
[
12
]
; social networks consist of many individuals that share different communication channels for different social groups to which each individual might belong
[
25
]
. In all of these settings, both the structure and the computational capabilities of the system is, arguably, better modelled as a hypergraph rather than a traditional graph
[
9
,
11
,
13
]
.

A
hypergraph

H=(V,E)
consists of a set,
V
, of vertices and a set,
E
, of edges that model connections between vertices. Unlike traditional graphs, each edge
e\in E
may contain more than two vertices, thereby modelling higher order interactions between vertices. Hypergraphs offer greater descriptive power in defining and/or analyzing network structure compared to graphs. For example, specifying that a set of 5 devices share the same local network is more informative than saying that the 5 devices can communicate pair-wise with each other. Similarly, utilizing a shared email list or group chat is potentially both more efficient for planning social events, and the structure of such groups is more informative about the structure of a social network than pair-wise connectivity alone.

Given the generality and flexibility of hypergraphs in modelling networks, they have become an increasingly popular model for complex networks in data mining and machine learning
[
3
,
21
,
30
]
. Yet most of the work in this area focuses on centralized and parallel algorithms applied to hypergraphs, rather than modelling the underlying computational system as a hypergraph.

We believe that viewing underlying computational network as a hypergraph is illuminating in many cases. Indeed, such a view is natural for hybrid networks as described above, where the inclusion of hyperedges is used to model faster or more efficient communication between tightly-connected local networks, and the large-scale network structure is defined by the hypergraph. Our main motivation in this work is to initiate the systematic investigation of distributed hypergraph algorithms with congestion by analogy with the CLIQUE and CONGEST models for graphs. We focus attention on a generalization of the triangle enumeration problem to hypergraphs. Our reasons for considering this problem are twofold. On the theoretical side, triangle enumeration is in some sense the ultimate problem for understanding congestion in a network, as the problem requires a polynomial number of rounds the the CLIQUE and CONGEST models
[
14
,
16
,
17
,
22
,
37
]
while it can trivially be solved in a single round of the LOCAL model. On the applied side, the distribution of triangles (in both graphs and hypergraphs) is a well-studied parameter that can reveal significant structural information about the network
[
10
,
23
,
26
,
29
,
42
,
43
]
.

1.1
Our Contributions

Our main contributions in this paper are to build a formal and conceptual framework for distributed algorithms in hypergraphs under congestion, and to analyse the complexity of the distributed triangle enumeration task within this framework. The primary contributions and results are as follows.

In Section
2.2
, we introduce six different models of for synchronous distributed computation that are all generalizations of the CONGEST model for graphs. Very briefly, these models are

•

PRIMAL CONGEST (PC) which is equivalent to the CONGEST model applied to the “primal graph” (i.e., the graph obtained by connecting each pair of vertices in each hyperedge with an edge)

•

EDGE CLIQUE (EC) in which each hyperedge can simulate a single round of the CLIQUE model in each round

•

EDGE BROADCAST (EB) in which each vertex in each hyperedge can broadcast a single message to all other vertices in the hyperedge

•

EDGE UNICAST (EU) in which each vertex can send a single message to one neighboring vertex in each incident edge in each round

•

EDGE SOLOCAST (ES) in which each hyperedge specifies a single vertex to broadcast a message to an edge each round, and

•

EDGE PAIRCAST (EP) in which each hyperedge specifies a single pair of incident vertices that can exchange messages each round.

We provide simulation results that characterize the relative power of these models (Propositions
2.2
and
2.4
).

In Section
3
, we analyze the complexity of triangle enumeration in hypergraphs in the CLIQUE, PC, and EC models. In the triangle enumeration problem, each vertex should output a set of triangles (i.e., simple 3-cycles in
H
) such that every triangle in
H
is output by precisely one vertex. Recall that the
rank
of a hypergraph
H=(V,E)
is the largest cardinality of any hyperedge
e\in E
. We establish the following results.

Theorem 1.1

(Informal)
.

Consider the triangle enumeration task in hypergraphs of rank
r
. Then:

•

Triangle enumeration can be performed in
O(n^{r-5/3}/\log n)
rounds in the CLIQUE model.

•

Triangle enumeration requires
\Omega(n^{r-5/3}/\log n)
rounds in the CLIQUE and PC models, and
\Omega(n^{1/3}/\log n)
in the EC model.

•

Triangle enumeration can be performed in
\widetilde{O}(n^{r-5/3})
rounds in expectation in the PC and EC models.

The upper bounds of Theorem
1.1
follow from natural generalizations the algorithms of Dolev, Lenzen, and Peled
[
17
]
(for the CLIQUE model) and Chang et al.
[
16
]
for the PC and EC models. Our lower bound generalizes the argument of Izumi and Le Gall
[
22
]
. The main technical contribution in this section is the derivation of a combinatorial bound on the minimum number of (hyper)edges required to support a fixed number of triangles in hypergraphs (Theorem
3.10
), which we believe to be novel.

While the upper and lower bounds of Theorem
1.1
are tight in the CLIQUE and PC models, the exponential dependence on
r
shows that these results may be impractical for hypergraphs with large rank. Thus, in Section
4
we turn our focus to families of sparse hypergraphs. We define two notions of sparseness. The first is “global sparseness” in which the sum of degrees of the vertices is
O(n)
. The second notion is “everywhere sparseness” in which the density of every sub-hypergraph is bounded by a parameter
\mu
. This notion generalizes the (asymptotically equivalent) notions of bounded arboricity and bounded degeneracy in graphs, though we believe its definition for hypergraphs to be novel. For sparse and everywhere sparse hypergraphs, we prove the following result.

Theorem 1.2

(Informal)
.

The following bounds hold for triangle enumeration in sparse hypergraphs.

•

Triangle enumeration is sparse hypergraphs on
n
vertices can be performed in
O(n)
rounds of the PC and EB models, and for every
\varepsilon>0
,
\Omega(n^{1-\varepsilon})
rounds are required for the PC model.

•

In hypergraphs with maximum density
\mu
, triangle enumeration can be performed in
O(\mu)
rounds in the PC and EB models, and the value of
\mu
need not be known in advance. For every
\varepsilon>0
,
\Omega(\mu^{1-\varepsilon})
rounds are required for triangle enumeration in the PC model on hypergraphs with maximum density
\mu
.

In particular, we believe the maximum density parameter
\mu
and associated algorithmic techniques in Section
4.3
to be of interest for other distributed computational problems on hypergraphs.

Finally, in Section
5
, we discuss other computational problems in the models introduced in Section
2.2
. We provide some generic simulation techniques that we believe will be helpful in generalizing CONGEST algorithms to hypergraphs, especially in the weaker hypergraph models, such as EDGE UNICAST.

1.2
Related Work

The models we introduce in Section
2.2
are generalizations of the CONGEST model, formalized by Peleg
[
38
]
. In addition to formalizing the CONGEST model, Peleg’s text describes fundamental techniques and solutions to classic problems in distributed computing. The related CLIQUE (a.k.a. CONGESTED CLIQUE) model was introduced by Lotker et al. in
[
33
]
.

Dolev, Lenzen and Peled
[
17
]
devised an algorithm for triangle enumeration in graphs in the CLIQUE model in
O(n^{1/3}/\log n)
rounds. Our CLIQUE algorithm for this problem in Section
3.1
(Theorem
3.1
) is a generalization of their algorithm. A matching lower bound of
\Omega(n^{1/3}/\log n)
for triangle enumeration in CLIQUE (and hence CONGEST as well) was independently found by Izumi and Le Gall
[
22
]
and Pandurangan, Robinson, and Scquizzato
[
37
]
. Our lower bound for the CLIQUE and PC models generalizes the argument of Izumi and Le Gall, which employs an information theoretic framework to bound the communication complexity of triangle enumeration. Izumi and Le Gall’s argument employs a combinatorial lemma due to Rivin
[
40
]
that provides a lower bound on the number of edges in a graph containing a given number of triangles. This combinatorial lemma does not generalize to hypergraphs in the “obvious” way, but we employ Rivin’s techniques to obtain a suitable generalization (Theorem
3.10
) sufficient to derive our main CLIQUE lower bound (Theorem
3.3
).

A nearly optimal algorithm for triangle enumeration in graphs in the CONGEST model was first described by Chang et al.
[
16
]
. This result was achieved by combining two main components. The first component is to show that triangle enumeration can be performed in expected time
\widetilde{O}(n^{1/3}\tau_{\mathrm{mix}})
, where
\tau_{\mathrm{mix}}
is the mixing time of the graph. The main idea here is to simulate an analogue of Dolev et al.’s algorithm for the CLIQUE model using an expander routing technique developed by Ghaffari, Kuhn and Su
[
18
]
and Ghaffari and Li
[
19
]
. The second component of Chang et al.’s algorithm is to develop an efficient distributed procedure for expander decomposition, whereby the graph is partitioned into components, each of which is an expander graph, by removing a small fraction of edges. Triangle enumeration can be performed efficiently on each component of the decomposition. The overall algorithm works by then recursively performing triangle enumeration on the remaining edges in the graph. Subsequent work has generalized and improved these results across several dimensions. We refer the reader to the survey of Censor-Hillel
[
14
]
for recent developments on subgraph detection and enumeration. Our general upper bound for triangle enumeration in hypergraphs in the EC and PC models (Theorem
3.27
) follows by showing that the techniques of Chang et al. generalize to our setting in a straightforward manner.

Our definition of the “maximum density” of a hypergraph (Definition
4.11
) is a hypergraph analogue of the characterization of graph arboricity given by Nash-Williams
[
35
]
. For graphs, Barenboim and Elkin
[
7
,
8
]
showed that the vertices of graphs of bounded arboricity can be partitioned into
O(\log n)
layers where each vertex has few neighbors in higher layers. They then use this result to derive efficient distributed algorithms for several fundamental problems in distributed computing. In Section
4.3
we generalize Barenboim and Elkin’s decomposition techniques to hypergraphs, and show that they can be applied to obtain an algorithm for hypergraph triangle enumeration parameterized by the hypergraph’s maximum density (Theorem
4.20
).

The central role of triangles in the (graph) structure of social networks was first popularized by the work of Granovetter
[
20
]
. The distribution of triangles and other “motifs” in networks has subsequently become foundational to network science
[
34
,
36
,
44
]
. Hypergraphs are becoming an increasingly popular tool for modeling complex networks. We refer the reader to the text of Bretto
[
13
]
for general background and applications, as well as recent surveys by Lee et al.
[
30
]
and Antelmi et al.
[
3
]
for applications to data mining and machine learning (respectively). The distribution of triangles in hypergraphs modelling real-world networks has been studied, for example, by Benson et al.
[
11
]
and LaRock and Lambiotte
[
29
]
.

In the other direction, distributed algorithms for hypergraphs have become an increasingly well-studied area, particularly concerning
symmetry breaking

[
1
,
2
,
6
,
28
,
27
]
, a set of problems concerned with ensuring more even access to resources by splitting vertices either into colour classes
[
1
,
28
]
or by assigning a local leader in the form of maximal independent sets
[
2
,
6
,
27
]
. We note that in the above examples, communication is most commonly done in the LOCAL or CONGEST models on the primal graph. Indeed, it is open as to how the round complexity for undertaking such symmetry breaking under our more restrictive communication models, such as EDGE SOLOCAST or EDGE PAIRCAST.

1.3
Technical Overview

Here, we provide a more detailed overview of our main results and techniques. Again, our main algorithmic problem is the triangle enumeration task. In a hypergraph
H=(V,E)
, we define a triangle to be a closed path of length
3
, i.e., a sequence of the form
v_{0},e_{0},v_{1},e_{1},v_{2},e_{2}
where
v_{0},v_{1},v_{2}\in V
,
e_{0},e_{1},e_{2}\in E
and for
i=0,1,2
, we have
v_{i}\in e_{i-1}\cap e_{i}
where indices are computed modulo
3
. Such a triangle is
simple
if
v_{0},v_{1},v_{2}
and
e_{0},e_{1},e_{2}
are pair-wise distinct, and
induced
(a.k.a.
open
) if
v_{i}\notin e_{i+1}
for
i=1,2,3
. For the
triangle enumeration task
, each vertex
v\in V
must output a set
\mathcal{T}_{v}
of triangles such that the
\mathcal{T}_{v}
are pair-wise disjoint and
\mathcal{T}=\bigcup_{v\in V}\mathcal{T}_{v}
contains all triangles in
H
. The algorithms we define can be adapted to output either all simple triangles or all induced (open) triangles in the same running time, so we do not specify if
\mathcal{T}
should be all simple triangles or all induced triangles in the task definition. Further, our lower bounds hold for both variants of the triangle enumeration task.

1.3.1
Triangle Enumeration in CLIQUE, PC and EC

Our first main result (Theorem
3.1
) shows that triangle enumeration in the CLIQUE model can be performed in
O(n^{r-5/3}/\log n)
rounds. This result generalizes the result of Dolev, Lenzen and Peled
[
17
]
, and our algorithm is a straightforward adaptation of their algorithm. Without loss of generality, we take the vertex set
V=\left\{1,2,\ldots,n\right\}
, and we assume
H
is
r
-uniform (i.e., every edge has rank exactly
r
). We first partition
V
into
s=n^{1/3}
sets
S_{1},S_{2},\ldots,S_{s}
each of size
n^{2/3}
, and assign each vertex
v
a set of triples
T_{v}=S_{i_{0}}\times S_{i_{1}}\times S_{i_{2}}
. The vertex
v
is then responsible for outputting all triangles
v_{0}e_{0}v_{1}e_{1}v_{2}e_{2}
where
(v_{0},v_{1},v_{2})\in S_{i_{0}}\times S_{i_{1}}\times S_{i_{2}}
.

In order for
v
to output all such triangles, each vertex
u\in S_{i_{j}}
(
j=0,1,2
) should send
v
all of its incident edges
e
that also contain another vertex
w\in S_{i_{j+1}}\cup S_{i_{j-1}}
. To this end, for each such
w
,
u
can enumerate all of the
\binom{n-2}{r-2}
possible such edges form a characteristic binary vector
e_{u,w}
that encodes which possible edges are present. The vertex
u
then sends this
O(n^{r-2})
-bit vector to
v
. Repeating this procedure for every possible choice of
v
,
u
, and
w
, we find that every vertex is the source and destination of
O(n^{4/3})
messages, each of length
O(n^{r-2})
. Applying the deterministic routing scheme of Lenzen
[
31
]
, all of the messages can be routed in
O(n^{r-5/3}/\log n)
rounds.

For triangle enumeration in the PC and EC models, we first observe that the EC model can trivially simulate the the PC model, hence it suffices to solve triangle enumeration in the PC model. In the PC model, our algorithm is a straightforward adaption of Chang et al.’s algorithm for triangle enumeration on graphs in the CONGEST model
[
16
]
. The high level strategy of Chang et al. is to use a distributed expander decomposition to decompose the network into components, each of which has large conductance, by removing only a small fraction of edges. Then within each component, triangle enumeration is performed using a procedure that exploits the rapid mixing of high conductance graphs. Remaining triangles consisting only of edges between components are then dealt with recursively. For the rapidly mixing components of the expander decomposition, Chang et al. generalize the algorithmic techniques of Dolev et al.
[
17
]
. Specifically, they partition vertices according to their degree, and compute a derived partition of possible triangles (i.e., triples of vertices) such that each vertex is responsible for outputting all triangles consisting of triples of vertices assigned to it. This assignment is chosen such that each vertex must receive a number of edges proportional to its degree. Similarly, each vertex must send a number of messages (corresponding to its incident edges) that is proportional to its degree as well. The messages are then routed using an adaptation of the expander routing scheme of Ghaffari et al.
[
18
,
19
]
. Under Chang et al.’s partition of triangles, each vertex
v
is the source and destination of
O(n^{1/3}\deg(v))
messages, and the message routing is performed in
\widetilde{O}(n^{1/3})
rounds.

In order to adapt this procedure to hypergraph triangle enumeration in the PC model, we observe that the PC model is precisely the CONGEST model for the primal graph of
H=(V,E)
—i.e., the graph
G=(V,E^{\prime})
where
\left\{u,v\right\}\in E^{\prime}
if and only if there exists an edge
e\in E
such that
u,v\in E
. Thus, the different components of Chang et al.’s algorithm on
G
can be simulated in
H
. In order to do this, we observe that hypergraph triangle enumeration in
H
is equivalent to triangle enumeration in a multigraph
G_{\left\{H\right\}}
that includes one copy of each edge
\left\{u,v\right\}\in E^{\prime}
for each (hyper)edge
e\in E
that contains
u
and
v
. Thus, the machinery of Chang et al. can be applied to
G
with a simple adaptation: if
u
sends an incident edge
\left\{u,v\right\}
to a vertex
w
in Chang et al.’s algorithm applied to
G
,
u
instead sends all hyperedges
e
containing both
u
and
v
to
w
in our simulation. Since the number of such hyperedges is at most
\binom{n-2}{r-2}=O(n^{r-2})
, the message length and running time grows by a factor of
O(n^{r-2})
compared to that of Chang et al. applied to
G
. Thus, the overall running time of our simulation is
\widetilde{O}(n^{1/3}\cdot n^{r-2})=\widetilde{O}(n^{r-5/3})
.

1.3.2
Lower Bounds

In Section
3.2
, we prove that triangle enumeration in the CLIQUE model (and hence the PC model as well) requires
\Omega(n^{r-5/3}/\log n)
rounds, thereby showing the algorithms above are optimal. Our argument generalizes the argument of Izumi and Le Gall
[
22
]
, who prove the analogous lower bound for triangle enumeration in graphs. Specifically, we consider hypergraphs sampled from
G(n,r,1/2)
, which consists of
r
-uniform hypergraphs where each possible edge (i.e., subset of
r
vertices) is included independently with probability
1/2
. We show that any algorithm that performs triangle enumeration for graphs sampled from this distribution with sufficiently high probability requires
\Omega(n^{r-5/3}/\log n)
rounds in expectation.

Our lower bound argument follows the information theoretic framework developed by Izumi and Le Gall. The basic idea is that in order for a vertex
v
to output a set
\mathcal{T}_{v}
of triangles, the information content of the messages received by
v
must be at least the information content of the (existence of) the edges contained in the triangles in the triangles in
\mathcal{T}_{v}
. This statement is formalized in Lemma
3.25
, and its proof is identical to that of the analogous statement in
[
22
]
. We can then obtain round lower bounds by observing that the the total message length received must be at least the expected entropy of the messages received. Since each vertex can receive only
O(n\log n)
bits each round, an entropy lower bound of
h
yields a round lower bound of
h/n\log n
.

In order to prove a lower bound on the maximum entropy of messages received by some vertex
v
, we argue as follows. For each
v\in V
, let
\mathcal{T}_{v}
denote the set of triangles output by
v
, and take
\mathcal{T}=\bigcup\mathcal{T}_{v}
be the set of all triangles in
H
sampled from
G(n,r,1/2)
. We first show that with large constant probability,
\abs{\mathcal{T}}=\Omega(n^{3r-3})
(for fixed constant
r
). Let
\mathbf{w}=\arg\max_{v}\abs{\mathcal{T}_{v}}
be the vertex that outputs the most triangles. By the pigeonhole principle,
\abs{\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{w}}}=\Omega(n^{3r-4})
with constant probability.

Next, we show that the triangles in
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{w}}
must contain many edges. To this end, we establish the following bound (cf. Theorem
3.10
): if
H
is an
r
-uniform hypergraph with
n
vertices and
m
edges that contains
t
triangles, then

t\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{3}\left(m\binom{n-3}{r-2}\binom{r}{2}\right)^{3/2}.

(1)

Taking
t=\Omega(n^{3r-4})
and solving for
m
gives
m=\Omega{n^{r-2/3}}
for constant
r
. Thus in outputting
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{w}}
,
\mathbf{w}
must witness
\Omega(n^{r-2/3})
edges.

The bound (
1
) generalizes a bound of Rivin
[
40
]
, which corresponds to (
1
) in the case of graphs (i.e.,
r=2
). Our proof of (
1
) essentially follows the argument of Rivin, where the crucial insight again is to associate triangles in a hypergraph
H
with triangles in the multigraph
G_{\left\{H\right\}}
introduced above. In
G_{\left\{H\right\}}
, the maximum multiplicity of an edge is
\mu\leq\binom{n-2}{r-2}=O(n^{r-2})
. By applying standard tools in algebraic graph theory, we show that for such a multigraph with
t
triangles and
m
edges, the number of triangles satisfies
(6t)^{2}\leq(2\mu m)^{3}
, whence (
1
) follows.

Finally, we conclude our proof of the main triangle counting lower bound by following the argument of Izumi and Le Gall. We take
\mathcal{E}=(\mathbf{e}_{1},\mathbf{e}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{m})
to be the indicator vector for the existence of each of the
m=\binom{n}{r}
possible edges in
H
, and we let
\pi_{\mathbf{w}}
denote the transcript of messages received by
\mathbf{w}
. By applying standard tools from information theory, along with Lemma
3.25
, we argue that the entropy of
\mathbf{w}
satisfies
H(\pi_{\mathbf{w}})\geq\mathbb{E}[\abs{E(\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{w}})}]-H(\rho_{\mathbf{w}})
, where
E(\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{w}})
is the set of edges contained in the triangles output by
\mathbf{w}
and
H(\rho_{\mathbf{w}})
is the entropy of the initial state of
\mathbf{w}
. The main lower bound then follows by applying the bounds above for
\abs{E(\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{w}})}
along with the observation that
H(\rho_{\mathbf{w}})=O(n^{r-2})
.

1.3.3
Sparse Hypergraphs

We say that a hypergraph
H=(V,E)
is
sparse
if
\sum_{e\in E}\abs{e}=\sum_{v\in V}\deg(v)=O(n)
. We show that if
H
has maximum degree
\Delta
, then triangle enumeration can be performed in
O(\Delta)
rounds of the EDGE BROADCAST (EB) and PRIMAL CONGEST (PC) models (Theorem
4.3
), and in particular, for sparse hypergraphs, this running time is
O(n)
. The algorithm is simple: we simply compute unique edge IDs, and each vertex broadcasts the IDs of its incident edges to its neighbors. It is straightforward to show that this is sufficient to solve triangle enumeration. To compute short (
O(\log n)
bit) unique IDs, we take the ID of an edge
e
to be the concatenation of the smallest vertex ID of a vertex
v
in
e
concatenated with
v
’s port number associated to the edge
e
.

We show that this
O(n)
upper bound for sparse graphs is essentially tight in the PC model by reduction to the CLIQUE lower bound (Theorem
4.6
). Specifically, for any
\varepsilon>0
, we can find a rank
r
and integer
n^{\prime}\leq n
such that the CLIQUE lower bound of Theorem

1.3.4
Everywhere Sparse Hypergraphs

For a hypergraph
H=(V,E)
, we define the

of vertices to be

\mu_{H}(U)=\frac{1}{\abs{U}}\sum_{e\in E[U]}\abs{e},

where
E[U]=\left\{e\cap U\,\middle|\,e\in E,\abs{e\cap U}\geq 2\right\}
is the set of induced edges

of

\mu(H)=\max_{U}\mu_{H}(U)
. We show that maximum density has properties analogous to that of arboricity and degeneracy of graphs. Specifically, we show that if

can be partitioned into
\ell=O(\log n)
layers

such that each vertex

has degree at most

in

. In Section

[
7
,
8
]
on arboricity bounded graphs.

We first define a distributed algorithm
\textnormal{{Peel}}(\alpha)
for the EB model that takes an estimate
\alpha
of the maximum density as input. If the maximum density of
H
is at most
\alpha
, then the algorithm produces a layered decomposition of the vertices as above, where each vertex
v\in V
learns which layer

in which it is contained. The algorithm follows a simple greedy approach. All vertices are initially active. In the first round, all vertices with degree at most
2\alpha
become inactive, join
L_{1}
, and broadcast this fact to their neighbors. In subsequent rounds, each vertex keeps track of its incident edges still containing active vertices. If in round

2\alpha
edges containing active vertices, then
v
becomes inactive and joins
L_{i}
. Thus, if each vertex is given a correct upper bound
\alpha
on the maximum density, the vertices can compute a layered decomposition in
O(\log n)
rounds of the EB model. Conversely, we show that if some vertex

fails to become inactive after
\log n
rounds, then the maximum density of

is at least
\alpha/2
.

Given layered decomposition

with
\ell=O(\log n)
, let
V_{i}=L_{i}\cup L_{i+1}\cup\cdots\cup L_{\ell}
. We can perform triangle enumeration as follows. In rounds

, each vertex
v\in L_{i}
broadcasts each nonempty

for each of

2\alpha
edges, each containing at most
r

The approach above assumes that the vertices are given some

\alpha
that are sufficient to solve triangle enumeration in time

without the assumption that a faithful estimate
\alpha\geq\mu(H)
is given, so long as some

is known to all vertices. The procedure works as follows. Each vertex runs the “peeling” procedure for computing a layered decomposition described above for geometrically increasing estimates
\alpha=2,4,8,\ldots,M

bit messages. Each vertex

for which

terminated after at most

is a constant factor approximation of the true maximum density

. Further, we show that if each vertex sets

to be the maximum value of

Finally, we show that the dependence on

which require

2.2
Computational Models

We view each hypergraph as a communication network by analogy with distributed graph algorithms. For a hypergraph
H=(V,E)
, we assume that each vertex
v\in V
has a unique ID of
O(\log n)
bits, where
n=\abs{V}
is the number of vertices in the hypergraph. We assume that each vertex’s ID is initially known to that vertex, and possibly its neighboring vertices as well.

We focus on synchronous message passing models that are analogous to the (congested) CLIQUE, LOCAL, and CONGEST models. Throughout our discussion of computational models, we use the parameter
B
as the number of bits communicated per interaction per communication round. Traditionally (e.g., for the CONGEST model), we take
B=O(\log n)
, but for the sake of generality we treat
B
as a parameter in defining our models. We assume that initially each vertex knows its own ID, degree, as well as a list of neighboring vertices in each incident edge. Further, we assume that each vertex initially holds a port numbering of its incident edges so that it can refer unambiguously to its first, second, etc., incident edges.

One approach to generalizing the LOCAL and CONGEST models to hypergraphs is simply to use the primal graph
G(H)
as the underlying communication network. In each case, we assume that the input of each vertex contains an ordered list of its adjacent edges. We formalize the communication restrictions in these models as follows:

CLIQUE

each round, each vertex
v\in V
can send (distinct)
B
-bit messages to all other vertices
w\in V
. This is equivalent to the “congested clique” model for standard graphs.

PRIMAL CONGEST

(PC) each round, each vertex
v\in V
can send (distinct)
B
-bit messages to each adjacent vertex
w\in N(v)
.

PRIMAL LOCAL

(PL) each round, each vertex
v\in V
can send (distinct) messages of unbounded size to each adjacent vertex
w\in N(v)
.

Note that the PC and PL models are equivalent to the CONGEST and LOCAL models on the primal graph
G(H)
.

While the PC model may be a “natural” generalization of the CONGEST model for hypergraphs in some contexts, it ignores the structure of (hyper)edges in the network, and reduces all communication to pair-wise adjacency. Depending on what the edges in the hypergraph are meant to model, this may afford either too much or too little computational power to the model. In order scenarios in which each (hyper)edge represents a shared communication channel, we define five generalizations of the CONGEST model to hypergraphs. The power of these models all differ as a function of the rank
r
of the hypergraph, though for fixed constant rank they are equivalent to the (PRIMAL) CONGEST model.

We define our distributed hypergraph models as follows:

EDGE CLIQUE

(EC) each round, for each vertex
v\in V
and each incident edge
e\ni v
,
v
can send (distinct)
B
bit messages to each other vertex
w\in e
. This is equivalent to each (hyper)edge acting as in the congested clique model for standard graphs.

EDGE BROADCAST

(EB) each round, for each vertex
v
and incident edge
e\ni v
,
v
can broadcast a single
B
-bit message to all vertices
w\in e
. Each
v
may broadcast different messages to different incident edges. This is equivalent to each (hyper)edge acting as in the “broadcast congested clique” model for standard graphs.

EDGE UNICAST

(EU) each round, for each vertex
v
and incident edge
e\ni v

v

B

B

w\in e

3.2
Triangle Enumeration Lower Bound

In this section, we prove our main lower bound for the triangle enumeration problem. The primary result generalizes the CLIQUE lower bound of Izumi and Le Gall
[
22
]
to hypergraphs.

Theorem 3.3
.

For any constant
r
there exists a distribution over
r
-uniform hypergraphs such that any (randomized or deterministic) algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that solves the triangle listing problem in the CLIQUE model with sufficiently large constant probability requires
\Omega(n^{r-5/3}/\log n)
rounds. This bound applies
a fortiori
to the PRIMAL CONGEST (PC) model as well.

Applying Theorem
3.3
along with Corollary
2.5
and Proposition
2.2
, we obtain the following corollary for triangle enumeration in the various models introduced in Section
2.2
.

Corollary 3.4
.

Solving triangle enumeration with sufficiently large probability in the EC, EB, EU, ES, and EP models in hypergraphs with
n
vertices requires
\Omega(n^{1/3}/\log n)
rounds.

Our proof of Theorem
3.3
generalizes the lower bound argument of Izumi and Le Gall to hypergraphs. The idea of Izumi and Le Gall’s proof is to consider a random input drawn from
G(n,1/2)
, where each pair of vertices forms an edge independently with probability
1/2
. A graph
G
sampled from
G(n,1/2)
contains
\Omega(n^{3})
triangles in expectation. Thus, in order to solve the triangle enumeration problem, some vertex must output
\Omega(n^{2})
triangles (with constant probability).

Izumi and Le Gall’s argument relies upon two main lemmas. The first (Lemma 4.3 in [CITE]) gives a lower bound on the mutual information between the set of triangles output by a vertex and the edges of the graph by the number of edges contained in the triangles output by the vertex. The second lemma (Lemma 4.2 in [CITE]) gives a lower bound on the number of edges contained in
t
triangle, namely any set of
t
triangles contains at most
t^{2/3}
edges.

Izumi and Le Gall’s main argument then proceeds as follows. For
G\sim G(n,1/2)
, some vertex
v
outputs
\Omega(n^{2})
triangles with constant probability. By the first lemma (Lemma 4.3), the mutual information between the edge set of
G
and the set
T
of triangles is at least the number of edges contained in some triangle of
T
. This quantity is
\Omega(n^{4/3})
by the second lemma (Lemma 4.2). The lower bound then follows by arguing that mutual information between
E
and
T
is a lower bound for the number of bits received by
v
. Since
v
can receive at most
n\log n
bits per round, the lower bound
n^{1/3}/\log n
follows.

To prove Theorem
3.3
, we generalize each of the parts of Izumi and Le Gall’s argument. Specifically, our argument proceeds as follows:

1.

We introduce the random hypergraph model
H(n,r,p)
that generalizes the random graph model
G(n,p)
, and we show that
H\sim H(n,r,1/2)
contains
\Theta(n^{3r-3})
triangles with constant probability. Hence, in order to solve the triangle enumeration problem, some vertex must output
\Theta(n^{3r-4})
triangles with constant probability.

2.

Next we show that any set set of
m
edges in an
r
-uniform hypergraph on
n

t
triangles must satisfy
m=\Omega(t^{2/3}/n^{r-2})
. This result generalizes the result of Rivlin
[
40
]
relating the minimum number of edges needed to produce a given number of triangles in a graph.

3.

Finally, we apply the information theoretic framework of Izumi and Le Gall
[
22
]
to infer the lower bound of Theorem
3.3

To this end, first consider the random

Solving for

In order to complete our proof of Theorem

, we employ the information theoretic framework of Izumi and Le Gall
[

H\sim H(n,r,1/2)
, then for any algorithm

that solves the triangle listing problem, there is some vertex

\Omega(n^{3r-4})
triangles with constant probability (by Corollaries

edges. That is,

bits of entropy, as the (non)existence of each edge in

has
1

3.3
follows from the fact that

can receive at most

bits each round.

In order to formalize this approach, we introduce some notation and recall some fundamental definitions and results from information theory. Fix the vertex set
V=\left\{[n]\right\}=\left\{1,2,\ldots,n\right\}
and set

Fix a triangle enumeration algorithm
\mathcal{A}
. Let

be the random variable consisting of the initial state of
v

H

v

We note that in order to show Theorem

We recall that for for a random variable

Lemma 3.23
.

Suppose

•

•

For any random variables

\mathbf{Y}

The following lemma, due to Izumi and Le Gall

For any vertex

I(\mathcal{E};\mathcal{T}_{v})\geq\mathbb{E}\left[E(\abs{\mathcal{T}_{v}})\right]

(24)

We now have all the pieces in place to prove Theorem

(Proof of Theorem

3.3
Triangle Listing in General Hypergraphs

The matching upper and lower bounds of Sections
3.1
and
3.2

2.2

to suppress factors that are polylogarithmic in the function’s argument.

Theorem 3.27
.

Triangle enumeration can be performed in rank
r
hypergraphs in expected
\widetilde{O}(n^{1/3}\min\left\{r\Delta_{2},n^{r-2}\right\})
rounds in the EDGE CLIQUE (EC) and PRIMAL CONGEST (PC) models, where
\Delta_{2}
is the pair-degree of
H
.

By Proposition
2.2
, the result of Theorem
3.27
holds for all of the models defined in Section
2.2
up to factors that are polynomial in

.

Remark 3.28
.

While the upper bound of Theorem
3.27
matches the (tight) upper bound of Theorem
3.1
for the CLIQUE model, the computational power of CLIQUE and EC are not comparable when
\Delta_{2}
is super-constant. To see why, observe that if
u
and
v
share
\deg(u,v)
incident edges in
H
, then in a single round of EC,
u
and
v
can exchange
O(\deg(u,v)\log n)
bits of communication (corresponding to
O(\log n)
bits per shared edge). Thus, the lower bound of Theorem
3.3
does generally apply to the EC model. On the other hand, a single round of the EC model can be simulated in
\Delta_{2}
rounds of the CLIQUE model, hence the upper bound of Theorem
3.27
is tight (up to
\poly r\log n
factors) when
\Delta_{2}=O(1)
.

The proof Theorem
3.27
applies the techniques developed by Chang et al.
[
16
]
for solving (graph) triangle enumeration in the CONGEST model. Chang et al.’s solution develops two main tools to perform triangle enumeration:

1.

computing an expander decompositions in which the graph
G=(V,E)
is partitioned into components such that (a) each component is an expander graph, and (b) only
\varepsilon\abs{E}
edges are not contained within some component, and

2.

computing efficient message routing within expander graphs.

With these two tools, Chang et al. perform triangle enumeration in the CONGEST model by first computing an expander decomposition of the graph. Then within each component of the decomposition, the efficient message routing procedure is applied to simulate the CLIQUE triangle enumeration algorithm of Dolev et al.
[
17
]
within each component (with only
\poly\log n
overhead) to enumerate all triangles with at least two vertices in the component. This two step procedure is then applied recursively on the subgraph of inter-cluster edges (of which there are at most
\varepsilon\abs{E}
).

Our approach is to simulate both steps of Chang et al. in the EC model. There are two main observations that allow us to perform this simulation. First, we observe that if
G=G(H)
is the primal graph of
H
, then a single round of CONGEST in
G
can be simulated by a single round of computation in the EC model. Thus, any CONGEST algorithm on
G
can be simulated in
H
in the EC model without additional computational overhead. Second, we note that triangle enumeration in a hypergraph
H
is equivalent to triangle enumeration in the multigraph
G_{[H]}
in which the multiplicity of each pair
\left\{u,v\right\}
is equal to the number of edges
e\in E
such that
u,v\in e
. Using these two observations we perform triangle enumeration in
H
as follows:

1.

Compute an expander decomposition of
G=G(H)
with resulting components
G_{1},G_{2},\ldots
.

2.

Within each component
G_{i}
perform triangle enumeration, listing triangles of the form
T=v_{1}e_{1}v_{2}e_{2}v_{3}e_{3}
such that at least two of the
v_{i}
are in
G_{i}
as follows:

(a)

simulate computation of the message the routing of
[
16
]
for triangle enumeration in
G_{i}
,

(b)

if
u
sends the edge
e^{\prime}=\left\{u,v\right\}
to a vertex
w
in the simulation, then
u
sends to
w
all of its incident hyperedges
e
with
u,v\in e
.

3.

Recursively enumerate triangles containing only inter-cluster edges in
G
.

The simulation of Steps 1, 2b, and 3 are straightforward given the our observation that CONGEST computation in
G(H)
can be simulated in
H
in the EC model. To perform Step 2b, we note that the size of the message sent from
u
to
w
can be made to be
O(\min\left\{r\Delta_{2},n^{r-2}+\log n\right\})
where
r
is the rank of
H
. The first term can be achieved by each vertex sending the list of neighboring vertices in each incident edge, while the second is achieved by sending the identities of
u
and
v
followed by a binary vector of length
n^{r-2}
where the indices enumerate all of the possible
O(n^{r-2})
edges that contain
u
and
v
. Clearly, these longer messages can be routed to their respective destinations with a multiplicative overhead of
O(\min\left\{r\Delta,n^{r-2}/\log n+1\right\})
rounds compared to the algorithm of
[
16
]
.

4
Triangle Enumeration in Sparse Hypergraphs

Our results in Section
3
provide existentially optimal algorithms for triangle listing in the CLIQUE and PRIMAL CONGEST (PC) models parameterized by the rank of a hypergraph. The upper bound may have limited practical application, however, because the bound is exponential in the rank
r
of the hypergraph. Many natural hypergraphs (such as those representing social networks, for example) may have very large rank, making this algorithm unsuitable. On the other hand, the lower bound construction in Section
3
contains a number of edges that is exponential in
r
. Again this is a feature that many naturally occurring hypergraphs do not possess.

In this section, we consider families of sparse hypergraphs that generalize sparse graphs. We define two notions of sparseness: global sparseness and everywhere sparseness. A (globally) sparse hypergraph is a hypergraph in which the sum of the degrees of vertices (equivalently the sum of the cardinalities of edges) is linear in the number of vertices. This definition is a direct generalization of a sparse graph. An
everywhere sparse
hypergraph is a hypergraph in which every sub-hypergraph is (globally) sparse. We give a formal definition of this notion in Section
4.3
. Everywhere sparseness generalizes the asymptotically equivalent notions of degeneracy and arboricity of a graph. Just as many naturally occurring graphs have bounded degeneracy (and arboricity), we expect that many natural families of hypergraphs are everywhere sparse.

In this section we give upper bounds for triangle enumeration in sparse and everywhere sparse hypergraphs. These algorithms can be implemented in the EDGE BROADCAST (EB) model. We complement our upper bounds with lower bounds as well, though these bounds are not tight. We leave it as an open question whether the algorithms or lower bounds can be improved.

4.1
Low Degree Triangles

Let
H=(V,E)
be a hypergraph of rank
r
. We say that a vertex
v\in V
is
\delta
-light
if
\deg(v)\leq\delta
. We call a triangle
T=v_{1}e_{1}v_{2}e_{2}v_{3}e_{3}

\delta
-light if at least one of
v_{1},v_{2}
and
v_{3}
is
\delta
-light. The following result shows that we can efficiently enumerate all
\delta
-light triangles in

Proposition 4.1
.

Suppose
H=(V,E)
is a hypergraph of rank
r
, and let
\delta>0
be a parameter. Then all
\delta
-light triangles in

can be listed in
O(r\delta)
rounds of the EDGE BROADCAST (EB) and PRIMAL CONGEST (PC) models. Moreover, each
\delta
-light triangle is listed by exactly one vertex in
H
.

Proof 4.2
.

Consider the following procedure: each
\delta
-light vertex

broadcasts its

,

broadcasts

rounds in the EB model, as each
v
sends
\deg(v)\leq\delta

r

Now suppose
w
is a neighbor of

where

and

e

v

w
knows every triangle

containing the

v

Finally, to ensure that each

•

If

\delta

•

If

\delta

Note that each vertex can determine for itself if it should output

4.2
Sparse hypergraphs

A hypergraph
H=(V,E)
is

if
\sum_{e\in E}\abs{e}=O(n)

n=\abs{V}
. Observe that
\sum_{e\in E}\abs{e}=\sum_{v\in V}\deg(d)
. Also, if
H
is sparse, the maximal degree
\Delta
of a vertex is
O(n)
. In this section, we will show that triangle enumeration in sparse hypergraphs can be performed in
O(n)

\Omega(n^{1-\varepsilon})
rounds in sparse hypergraphs for every
\varepsilon>0

Theorem 4.3
.

Suppose
H=(V,E)
has maximum degree
\Delta
. Then triangle enumeration can be performed in
O(\Delta)
rounds of the EDGE BROADCAST (EB) and PRIMAL CONGEST (PC) models.

Proof 4.4
.

We assume that each edge
e
has a unique
O(\log n)
-bit ID. In the EB model with the
\mathrm{KT}_{1}

O(\Delta)

and
v_{2}

v_{0},v_{1}\in e_{0}

v_{1},v_{2}\in e_{1}

1.

v_{0},v_{2}\in e_{2}

2.

3.

4.

Note that Condition 1 holds if and only if

By symmetry,

The following corollary follows immediately from the fact that sparse hypergraphs have maximum degree satisfying

Corollary 4.5
.

Suppose

is a sparse hypergraph. Then triangle enumeration can be performed in

We now prove a nearly matching lower bound for sparse hypergraphs for PC model.

Theorem 4.6
.

For every
\varepsilon>0

\Omega(n^{1-\varepsilon})
rounds in the PRIMAL CONGEST (PC) model.

Proof 4.7
.

The idea of the proof is to construct a distribution of sparse hypergraphs that contain smaller dense sub-hypergraphs to which we can apply our general CLIQUE lower bound (Theorem

r=\lceil{2/\varepsilon}\rceil
, and for any

n^{\prime}=\lceil{n^{1/r}}\rceil

H

V=\left\{1,2,\ldots,n\right\}

•

For vertices in

independently with probability

•

For each

•

Add a single hyperedge
\left\{n^{\prime}+1,n^{\prime}+2,\ldots,n\right\}

Note that the edges in Item 1 are distributed according to

First, we give an upper bound on the number and size of edges in

r

\sum_{e\in E}\abs{e}\leq r(n^{\prime})^{r}+n^{\prime}+(n-n^{\prime})=r(n^{1/r})^{r}+n=O(rn).

Thus, for constant

is sparse.

Next, we argue that each round of PC can be simulated by

, the restriction of

to vertices in
[n^{\prime}]

can be simulated by a single round of CLIQUE on

can broadcast the message it sent to the vertex

n^{\prime}+1

can simulate the states of all vertices

in the simulated EC round, as the initial states and edges incident to these vertices are known to all vertices. Thus, 2 rounds of CLIQUE on

are sufficient to simulate a single round of EC on

We next apply the lower bound of Theorem

\Omega((n^{\prime})^{r-5/3})

(n^{\prime})^{r-5/3}=(n^{1/r})^{r-5/3}=n^{1-5/3r}\geq n^{1-\varepsilon}.

While Theorem

\Delta_{2}=\Theta(n^{1-2/r})

\Omega(n^{1/3r})

Corollary 4.8
.

Triangle enumeration in sparse hypergraphs requires
\Omega(n^{1/6})
rounds in the EC, EB, EU, ES, and EP models.

While this bound is far from tight, it shows that a polynomial number of rounds is required for triangle enumeration in sparse hypergraphs, even in the the strong EDGE CLIQUE (EC) model.

4.3
Everywhere Sparse Hypergraphs

The
\Omega(n^{1-\varepsilon})
lower bound of Theorem
4.6
crucially employs hypergraphs with small but dense subsets of vertices (namely, the set
[n^{\prime}]
in the construction). Thus, the hypergraphs are globally sparse, having
O(n)
edges, but locally dense, as the average degree in
H^{\prime}=H\left[[n^{\prime}]\right]
is
\Theta(n^{1-1/r})
in expectation. On the other hand, the upper bound of Theorem
4.3
implies that if
all
degrees are bounded, then triangle enumeration can be performed efficiently. In this section, we consider more refined hypergraph parameter that measures the maximum density of sub-hypergraphs of
H
.

In the case of
graphs
, the idea of “maximum density subgraph” is captured by the graph’s arboricity. Here the

\alpha(G)
, of a graph

is defined to be the minimum number of forests into which
G

Theorem 4.9

(Nash-Williams CITE)
.

Suppose

is a graph. Then

\alpha(G)=\max_{U\subseteq V}\frac{\abs{E[U]}}{\abs{U}-1},

(27)

where
E[U]
denotes the set of edges of
G
within the set
U
.

For hypergraphs, we define an analogous measure of maximum sub-hypergraph density. Before introducing this measure, we define the restriction of a hypergraph
H=(V,E)
to a subset
U

Definition 4.10

Let

5
Discussion and Future Work

In this paper, we have initiated the systematic study of distributed sub-hypergraph enumeration in hypergraphs. To this end, we (1) introduced several computational models for hypergraphs that generalize the CONGEST model for graphs and evaluate their relative computational power, (2) provided algorithms for distributed triangle enumeration in our computational models and proved their optimality in two such models, (3) introduced classes of sparse and “everywhere sparse” hypergraphs and described efficient distributed algorithms for triangle enumeration in these classes.

While we proved that our triangle enumeration algorithms are optimal or near-optimal for the CLIQUE and PRIMAL CONGEST (PC) models, we do not prove optimality for the EDGE CLIQUE (EC) or weaker models. The reason is that these models provide potentially more computational power by communicating across parallel edges in a hypergraph. For example, consider the distribution of hypergraphs
G(n,r,1/2)
used to prove the
\Omega(n^{r-5/3}/\log n)
lower bound for triangle enumeration in the CLIQUE and PC models. Observe that if
H=(V,E)\sim G(n,r,1/2)
, then for every pair of vertices
u,v\in V
, there are
\frac{1}{2}\binom{n-2}{r-2}
edges
e
containing both
u
and
v
in expectation. A simple application of Chernoff bounds shows that for constant
r
, every pair of vertices
u
,
v
is contained in
\Omega(n^{r-2})
edges with high probability. Thus, in the EC model, this pair can exchange
\Omega(n^{r-2}\log n)
bits each round. As a result, the messages from our adaptation of Dolev et al.’s algorithm
[
17
]
for triangle enumeration in the CLIQUE model can be simulated using only
O(n^{1/3}/\log n)
communication rounds (with high probability over selection of
H\sim G(n,r,1/2)
). We suspect that, at least for constant rank hypergraphs, this upper bound is achievable for all hypergraphs in the EC model.

Question 5.1
.

It is possible to perform triangle enumeration in
\widetilde{O}(n^{1/3})
rounds of the EC model in hypergraphs of bounded rank.

We suspect an affirmative answer to Question
5.1
. Towards proving such an upper bound, it seems possible that the techniques of Chang et al.
[
16
]
could still be applied, albeit with more significant modifications than our discussion in Section
3.3
. Indeed, the main ingredients of Chang et al.—triangle enumeration in rapidly mixing (hyper)graphs and expander decompositions—would both need to be generalized in a nontrivial way. Yet some parts of the machinery employed by Chang et al. does generalize in a natural way to the EC model in such a way that parallel edges can be exploited for more efficient message routing. We conclude the paper by describing such techniques that we believe will be useful in addressing triangle enumeration and other problems in hypergraphs. We focus on making algorithms efficient in the EDGE UNICAST (EU) model, which is more restrictive (and likely more realistic) than the EC model in hypergraphs with unbounded rank.

One basic idea for generalizing CONGEST algorithms to the EU model is to simulate the execution of a CONGEST algorithm on a graph embedded in a hypergraph network. One way to an embedded graph in a hypergraph
H=(V,E)
is by forming a graph
G=(V,E^{\prime})
where each edge

is replaced by graph with vertex set

Definition 5.2

Let

be a hypergraph and let

For suitably chosen families

For efficient message routing in hypergraphs, we can employ the framework of Ghaffari et al.

19

Using transition probabilities (

has conductance
\Phi
and the graph family
\mathcal{G}
is a family of
k
-regular expander graphs with expansion
\varepsilon
, then the graph reduction
G_{H}
has conductance at least
\frac{\varepsilon}{k}\Phi
. Thus, if
H
is rapidly mixing, then so is the graph reduction
G_{H}
. We can then simulate the message routing procedures of Ghaffari et al.
[
18
,
19
]
in
G_{H}
to efficiently route messages in the EC model in
H
.

Applying this approach for message routing, we could then try to generalize the triangle enumeration procedure of Chang et al.
[
16
]
to perform triangle enumeration in
\widetilde{O}(n^{1/3})
rounds of EC. Indeed, a natural generalization of the partition of (possible) triangles used by Chang et al. ensures that each vertex
v
is the source of
O(n^{1/3}\deg(v))
messages. But in order to route all messages in
\widetilde{O}(n^{1/3}\tau_{\mathrm{mix}})
rounds, each vertex must also be the destination of
O(\deg(v)n^{1/3})
, which does not seem to be guaranteed by Chang et al.’s partition for hypergraphs. Finding a suitable modification of this method seems to be a promising approach to an improved upper bound for triangle enumeration in rapidly mixing hypergraphs in the EC model. Generalizing expander decompositions to hypergraphs could then yield efficient algorithms for arbitrary hypergraphs as well.

5.1
Further Directions

Beyond triangle enumeration, we hope that the definitions and techniques presented in this paper prove valuable in solving other distributed problems in hypergraphs. One natural extension of triangle enumeration is to consider more general sub-hypergraph enumeration, such as cliques or other motifs. While our definition of triangles as 3-cycles in hypergraphs is natural, simplices in hypergraphs can also be viewed as generalizations of triangles. Simplex counting was recently studied by Chakrabarti and Haris
[
15
]
in the streaming setting, but we are unaware of any work that address simplex enumeration in the distributed setting.
