Orthodontic Rubber Band Traction Improves Trainees' Learning Curve of Colorectal Endoscopic Submucosal Dissection: A Prospective Randomized Study
Authors: Linfu Zheng, Longping Chen, Binbin Xu, Baoxiang Luo, Fuqiang Wang, Zhilin Liu, Xingjie Gao, Linxin Zhou, Jiawei Chen, Longke Xie, Yaping Hou, Dazhou Li, Wen Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
INTRODUCTION:Colorectal endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) is a technically demanding operation with a long learning curve. The aim of this study was to determine whether orthodontic rubber band (ORB)-assisted colorectal ESD could improve the trainees' learning curve as it was shown to shorten colorectal ESD procedure time in trainees and experts.METHODS:This prospective, randomized, controlled clinical study involved 178 patients who underwent colorectal ESD, randomly divided into trainee ORB-assisted ESD (Trainee ORB-ESD; n = 60), trainee conventional ESD (Trainee C-ESD; n = 57), and expert conventional ESD (Expert C-ESD; n = 61) groups. The main outcome was procedure time.RESULTS:Per Protocol analysis showed a similar operative time in the trainee ORB-ESD and expert C-ESD groups, which was shorter than in the trainee C-ESD group [28.0 (21.0–35.0) and 25.0 (15.0–35.0) vs 41.0 (31.0–52.5) min; (P < 0.001)]. The trainee ORB-ESD, trainee C-ESD, and expert C-ESD groups differed significantly in resection speed (11.35, 9.07, and 12.56 mm2/min, respectively), good visual field exposure rate (96.7%, 80.7%, and 93.4%), and muscle injury rate (5.0%, 17.5%, and 6.6%). However, the trainee ORB-ESD and expert C-ESD groups were similar in these measures, and all 3 groups were similar in adverse events, en bloc resection, R0 resection, and pathology rates. The cumulative sum suggested that the learning inflection point of the trainee ORB-ESD group was earlier than that of the trainee C-ESD group.DISCUSSION:ORB-ESD shortened colorectal ESD procedure time and improved efficiency in trainees to an expert level. ORB-ESD could shorten the learning curve, justifying its widespread application.

INTRODUCTION

Increasing numbers of early colorectal cancer and precancerous lesions are found due to improvements in health awareness in the population and popularization of colonoscopy screening (1). Consequently, the demand for the corresponding endoscopic treatment of colorectal lesions, such as endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD), has also increased. However, unlike ESD in other organs, such as the stomach, ESD in the intestinal tract has relatively poor manipulation freedom due to the thin intestinal wall and many folds, particularly when the lesions are near the right half of the colon. Therefore, colorectal ESD is more difficult to perform than gastric ESD and its learning curve is longer (2,3). Shortening the trainee colorectal ESD learning curve would have great clinical and social value.

Our team previously reported that the ESD operation efficiency could be improved, and operating time by trainees and experts could be shortened when using an oral orthodontic rubber band (ORB) traction (4,5). However, the previous report was a retrospective study, and reports on whether ORB-assisted ESD could improve the colorectal ESD learning curve are lacking.

We conducted this prospective randomized study to compare the procedure time and efficiency between the trainee groups performing colorectal ESD with or without ORB and an expert group performing traditional ESD (without ORB). We hypothesized that the prospective clinical study would confirm the practicability of ORB traction so that more endoscopists would become familiar with and master this simple, economical, convenient, and effective traction method. We strive to make colorectal ESD safer, more efficient, popular, and more suitable for fast endoscopy personnel training.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

This prospective, randomized, controlled clinical study randomly divided the patients into 3 groups: the trainee ORB-ESD group, in which trainees performed the ORB-assisted colorectal ESD procedure; the trainee C-ESD group, in which trainees performed the conventional ESD procedure; and the expert C-ESD group, in which experts performed the conventional ESD procedure. Trainees were defined as those who performed fewer than 50 colorectal ESD procedures. They had to fulfill the following conditions before the start of the study: (i) complete over 1,000 colorectal procedures and (ii) complete 10 gastric and colorectal ESD procedures under expert supervision (n = 6). Experts were those who performed more than 300 colorectal ESD procedures (n = 2) (6). Trainees were supervised by expert when performing ESD. The study included patients who underwent ESD for colorectal lesions at the Digestive Endoscopy Center of the 900th Hospital of the People's Liberation Army between August 2022 and December 2023. Only one lesion was included in the study if an ESD procedure treated multiple colorectal lesions. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the 900th Hospital of the People's Liberation Army and registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (registration number ChiCTR2200062387). All patients signed informed consent forms.

Sample size calculation and randomization

In our previous study, the mean procedure times were 41.6 ± 20.6 minutes for the trainee ORB-ESD group, 65.1 ± 24.6 minutes for the trainee C-ESD group, and 54.4 ± 32.5 minutes for the expert C-ESD group. A significance level of 5% was assumed. The statistical power was 90%, and the grouping ratio was 1:1:1. Calculations with the Power Analysis and Sample Size software (version 16.0) indicated that 50 samples per group were required. At an expected shedding rate of 20%, 63 cases per group were required, 189 in total. The patients and the doctors were assigned to the 3 groups (n = 63 each) using numerical randomization generated by Microsoft Excel 2010 (Microsoft, Redmond, WA).

ESD procedure

The conventional ESD procedure entailed submucosal injection of methylene blue and sodium hyaluronate. Peripheral mucosa incision and submucosal dissection were performed with a golden knife [MK-T-2-195, Micro-tech (Nanjing) Co., Nanjing, China]. During the ORB-ESD procedure, the lesion peripheral mucosa was incised. A clip holding an ORB (Fox rubber band) of 6.35 mm inner diameter was introduced into the bowel through the working channel and clamped the anal aspect of the incised mucosa. Another clip was then used to pull the opposite side. The ESD procedure was then completed (Figure 1).

Procedure of ORB-endoscopic mucosal dissection. (a) Transverse colon, a 10 × 25 mm lesion; (b) Periannular incision; (c) ORB combined clip traction; (d) The submucosa was well exposed; (e) Close the wound; (f) Complete resection of the lesion. ORB, orthodontic rubber band.

The following cases were considered surgical patient shedding events: (i) the trainee could not complete the operation independently, requiring an expert to take over; (ii) traction such as ORB was used in the trainee or expert C-ESD group due to poor surgical field exposure or other reasons.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Intention-to-treat (ITT) and per-protocol (PP) analyses were used to compare the procedure time in the 3 groups. Continuous variables are expressed as means ± SDs, while categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages. Measurement data were compared using 1-way analysis of variance or the Kruskal-Wallis test. Counting data were compared using the χ2 or Fisher exact probability test. When the Bonferroni method was used to correct for multiple pairwise comparisons, used the corrected statistical P-values. Significance was set at P < 0.05.

Cumulative sum (CUSUM) is commonly used to analyze learning curves, as the difference in lesion sizes can lead to large differences in procedure times (7). CUSUM was used to calculate the resection speed (8,9) to compare the learning curves of the trainee ORB-ESD and trainee C-ESD groups. The resection speed CUSUM value was calculated chronologically for both patient groups, and the mean resection speed (RSmean) was taken as the target value. The CUSUM value was calculated as CUSUMn = (RSn–RSmean) + CUSUM(n-1), and the accumulation continued following this rule for all cases (i.e., n values). The last case had a CUSUM value of 0. The surgical case numbers were inserted horizontally in Excel and the CUSUM values vertically. The learning curve in IBM SPSS, Version 25.0, was used for learning curve fitting and goodness testing. Curve fitting was successful if P < 0.05 and the closer R2 was to 1, the higher the goodness of curve fitting. The peak of the fitted curve was the learning inflection point. The smaller the learning inflection point, the fewer operations required, indicating better training.

RESULTS

Colorectal ESD was performed in 293 patients between August 2022 and December 2023. There were 41 patients with a lesion diameter greater than 30 mm, 36 with submucosal tumors (including neuroendocrine tumors), 18 with residual additional ESD after EMR, and 9 with a JNET3 type lesion or suspected submucosal deep infiltration before surgery. A total of 189 patients met the inclusion criteria and were assigned equally to the 3 groups (Figure 2). The number of colorectal ESD cases completed by the 6 trainees was 126, with a mean of 21 cases per trainee (ITT analysis).

Three procedures in the trainee ORB-ESD group could not be completed independently by the trainee and were taken over by the expert, leaving 60 procedures in this group. In the trainee C-ESD group, 4 procedures were taken over by an expert, and in 2, the ORB traction method was used, leaving 57 patients in this group. The ORB traction was used in 2 of the expert C-ESD group's procedures, leaving 61 procedures in this group (Figure 1).

ITT analysis showed that the median operative times were 28 min ([interquartile range, IQR], 21.0, 35.0) in the trainee ORB-ESD group, 39 minutes ([IQR], 30.0, 52.0) in the trainee C-ESD group, and 25 minutes ([IQR], 16.0, 35.0) in the expert C-ESD group and differed between the groups (P < 0.001; Table 2). The respective medians in the PP analysis were 28 ([IQR], 21.0, 35.0), 41([IQR], 31.0, 52.5), and 25([IQR], 15.0, 35.0), min (P < 0.001; Table 3). Pairwise comparisons showed that the operative times in the trainee ORB-ESD and expert C-ESD groups were statistically similar and shorter than that in the trainee C-ESD group (P < 0.001; Figure 3).

The resection speeds in the trainee ORB-ESD, trainee C-ESD, and expert C-ESD groups were 11.16, 9.07, and 13.16 mm2/min by ITT analysis (P = 0.002; Table 2) and 11.35, 9.07, and 12.56 mm2/min by PP analysis (P = 0.005; Table 3). Comparison of submucosal visual field exposure among 3 groups (PP analysis)

In the trainee ORB-ESD group, 58 cases (96.7%) had good submucosal visual field and 2 (3.3%) had average submucosal visual field. In the trainee C-ESD group, 46 cases (80.7%) had good submucosal visual field, 10 (17.5%) had average submucosal visual field, and one (1.8%) had poor submucosal visual field. In the expert C-ESD group, 57 cases (93.4%) had good submucosal visual field and 4 (6.6%) had average submucosal visual field. The groups differed significantly in the submucosal visual field quality (P = 0.020; Table 3). Pairwise comparisons showed that the good visual field rate in the trainee ORB-ESD group was higher than in the trainee C-ESD group (P < 0.05). The good submucosal visual field rate in the expert C-ESD group was similar to that of both trainee groups (P > 0.05).

Comparison of adverse events, en bloc resection, R0 resection, and pathology among the 3 groups (PP analysis)

Muscle injury occurred in 3 cases (5%) in the trainee ORB-ESD group, 10 (17.5%) in the trainee C-ESD group, and 4 (6.6%) in the expert C-ESD group. The rate of muscle injury differed among the 3 groups (P = 0.043). Although the trainee C-ESD group had numerically the highest rate, pairwise analysis indicated that the 3 groups were statistically similar (P > 0.05). The trainee ORB-ESD group had 2 cases of delayed bleeding (3.3%), one of intraoperative perforation (1.7%), and one of PECS (1.7%). The trainee C-ESD group had 3 cases of delayed bleeding (5.3%) and 2 of intraoperative perforation (3.5%). The expert C-ESD group had 2 cases of delayed bleeding (3.3%) and 2 of intraoperative perforation (3.3%). The adverse events rate was statistically similar among the 3 groups (P = 0.931). All postoperative bleeding events were controlled after the endoscopic treatment. Intraoperative perforations were mended with endoscopic hemostatic clips. The PECS in the trainee ORB-ESD group was relieved by antibiotic therapy (Table 3).

The en bloc resection rate was 100% in the trainee ORB-ESD and expert C-ESD groups and 98.2% in the trainee C-ESD group. The trainee ORB-ESD group had one horizontal margin positive and one vertical margin positive, so its R0 removal rate was 96.5%. The expert C-ESD group had one horizontal margin positive and one vertical margin positive, so its R0 removal rate was 96.7%. The 3 groups had similar en bloc and R0 resection rates and similar postoperative pathology (P > 0.05; Table 3). The patients with a positive vertical margin were reoperated.

The average ORB placement time was 1.82 ± 0.69 minutes. The trainees believed that the ORB traction method was simple to implement, had a satisfactory traction effect, and helped improve their confidence (Figure 5). ORB detachment during traction occurred in 2 cases due to poor occlusal tissue and excessive aeration. The subsequent traction process was completed after clip replacement. In 15 patients treated with ORB, some colonic mucosa damage was caused by the mechanical pulling force applied to the clip fixed to the traction side of the lesion. However, no bleeding or perforation was caused by the ORB.

DISCUSSION

To learn to perform ESD, it is necessary to accumulate theoretical and systematic knowledge, including basic knowledge of the digestive system, ESD operating procedures, indications and contraindications related to ESD, and knowledge of related equipment accessories (10). After completing the first training stage, which includes clinical observations and participation in ESD procedures, ESD trainees start performing ESD in the lower third of the stomach under the guidance of an ESD expert and gradually transition to the esophagus and the colon (11). However, not all endoscopic centers have ESD expert guidance at all times, and the small number of ESD procedures for early gastric cancer in Western countries is small, making this training mode unsuitable in such cases (12,13). Many in vitro animal models (14,15), live animals (16,17), and a multipurpose ESD operating model made primarily of konjac powder (18) are now available for training ESD beginners. Training on these models can shorten the ESD learning curve and improve the trainees' confidence. Improvements and innovations related to ESD devices (19,20) and application of traction methods (6,21) could reduce the difficulty of performing ESD and shorten the learning period in ESD trainees. However, an international consensus on how to shorten the ESD learning curve is lacking.

Our previous retrospective study found that ORB traction was cheap [100 ORBs cost only 21 cents, while an S-O clip costs about $50 (22)] and clinically accessible, and its use in colorectal ESD could shorten the procedure time difference between trainees and experts. Reports on the value of ORB traction in colorectal ESD training are few. ITT and PP analyses in this prospective randomized controlled study showed that ORB traction use in the trainee ORB-ESD group resulted in a significantly shorter operative time and faster resection speed than in the trainee C-ESD group. PP analysis also showed that the trainee ORB-ESD and expert C-ESD groups were similar in their operative times (28.0 [21.0–35.0] vs 25.0 [15.0–35.0] minutes) and resection speed (11.35 [8.13–15.99] vs 12.56 [7.45–18.67] mm2/min). Ichijima et al (23). reported that traction-assisted colorectal ESD could reduce the operative time compared with conventional ESD when performed by nonexpert endoscopists (P = 0.07). However, the traction method used during each procedure in their study (e.g., S-O clip, traction-assisted method, or modified dental floss clip method) was selected based on operator preferences. Similarly, Tamaru et al reported using various traction methods and compared them with conventional ESD, confirming that the traction methods helped improve the efficiency of colorectal ESD (24). However, the range of traction methods could introduce confounding factors that could affect the results. Our study used only the ORB and clips traction method, introducing fewer confounding factors.

Good submucosal exposure is a prerequisite for a smooth ESD operation. Dobashi et al (25) reported using an internal traction device with a small magnetic loop in a pig large intestine in vitro model for colorectal ESD training. Their results showed that magnetic traction could better expose the submucosa and shorten the procedure time in colorectal ESD trainees. We found that submucosal visualization could also be improved by ORB traction. The trainee ORB-ESD group had a good visual field exposure rate of 96.7%, higher than the 80.7% in the trainee C-ESD group. Moreover, the anatomical level of the submucosa is clear, and the rate of ESD-related muscle damage is reduced when using ORB traction, further improving the confidence of colorectal ESD trainees. In our study, the trainee ORB-ESD group had a lower muscle injury rate than the expert C-ESD and trainee C-ESD groups (5.0% vs 6.6% vs 17.5%). However, a pairwise comparison found no difference in muscle injury between the groups. The groups were also similar in the postoperative bleeding, perforation, and PECS rates, possibly because the sample in this study was relatively small.

For ESD trainees, confidence in ESD procedure is critical, and the results of our questionnaire showed that the ORB combined clip traction was considered useful and helped to improve the confidence of trainees. Meanwhile, en bloc resection is an important factor affecting the operator's confidence. Yang et al (26) used a new type of claw traction tool developed by Boston company in an isolated animal model, showing that although the device could improve the cutting efficiency for endoscopy trainees, the en bloc resection rate was lower than in the conventional ESD group (60% vs 70%). The en bloc resection rates in the trainee ORB-ESD, trainee C-ESD, and expert C-ESD groups were higher than the above values, and similar among our study groups (100%, 98.2%, and 100%). This difference could be because all the trainees in our study had a certain ESD foundation, while trainees enrolled in Yang et al’s study used the grasping and pulling equipment for the first time, so they were unfamiliar with the instruments, resulting in a low en bloc resection rate. The R0 removal rate in the trainee ORB-ESD group in our study (98.3%) was consistent with that in previous reports (27). Furthermore, the CUSUM analysis suggested that the learning curve of the trainee ORB-ESD group was shorter than that of the trainee C-ESD group. This suggests that it was easier for trainees to master the ORB-assisted colorectal ESD.

In this study, we found that the clips fixed on the normal mucosal side caused mechanical injury to the intestinal mucosa when removed; however, no delayed bleeding or perforation events occurred. ORB with detachable clips or improved ORB traction technology could help avoid similar situations in the future. This study had certain limitations. First, this was a single-center randomized controlled trial with a small sample. Future multicenter large-sample studies on ORB-assisted ESD training are needed to provide further support to our findings. Second, the lesions included in this study were small. We limited the maximum lesion diameter to 30 mm as it was reported that trainees showed a high incomplete resection rate using ESD for larger lesions (11). However, the lesions were around 20–30 mm in some areas, and the EMR treatment was used. Whether ORB-ESD is also suitable for training the resection of lesions larger than 30 mm will be assessed in future studies. Third, whether ORB traction is also suitable when training for ESD operation of other parts of the digestive system, such as the stomach and duodenum, could be confirmed by relevant future studies.

ORB-ESD could shorten the gaps between trainees and experts in their ESD procedure time and resection speed, improve surgical field exposure, and reduce the muscle damage rate. It could also improve the confidence and shorten the learning curve of colorectal ESD trainees, making it worthy of clinical promotion and application.
