Regulatory role of rhizosphere microbial structure and function in yield formation of ratooning season rice
Authors: Zhaochen Wang, Lu Bai, Shule Dong, Dandan Yang, Zhaolong Tong, Fangfang Zhong, Hongjuan Zi, Yuhang Zeng, Mengying Chen, Hailong Xu, Bianhong Zhang, Wenxiong Lin, Zhixing Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn ratoon rice cultivation, the formation of tillers from stubble following the harvest of the main crop rice is crucial for achieving high and stable yields during the ratooning season. This study employed two rice varieties, YongYou1540 (YY1540) and JinHui809 (JH809), which exhibited comparable yields in the main crop season but markedly different yields in the ratooning season. We systematically compared changes in the physicochemical properties of the rhizosphere soil, as well as the microbial community structure and functional characteristics, between the two varieties at different growth stages during their ratooning seasons (0, 10, and 20 days after harvest). Our results revealed that microbial nutrient-acquiring enzyme activities were significantly higher in YY1540 compared to JH809. Analysis of rhizosphere microbial communities indicated that community diversity (e.g., Chao1) and network properties (e.g., robustness) had limited effects on the yield of ratooning season rice. In contrast, the functional features of the microbiome—particularly the abundances of carbon fixation genes (e.g., acsA, acsB), carbon degradation genes (e.g., malQ, pulA), and phosphorus cycling genes (e.g., pstS, ppx)—were significantly higher in YY1540. Furthermore, Mantel tests indicated that nutrient-cycling functional genes in the rhizosphere microbiome were significantly associated with soil nutrient indices, such as available phosphorus (AP), extractable organic carbon (EOC), and dissolved organic carbon (DOC), as well as the status of microbial nutrient limitation (e.g., VA) and yield of ratooning season rice. Notably, functional genes involved in carbon and phosphorus cycling exhibited particularly strong associations with AP, EOC, and yield of ratooning season rice. Collectively, the increased abundances of key functional genes (e.g., acsA and pstS), along with elevated activities of β-1,4-glucosidase and acid phosphatase, alleviated carbon and phosphorus limitations, improved nutrient supply efficiency during the regrowth stage, and ultimately enabled YY1540 to achieve higher yields in ratooning season rice. This study provides initial evidence for the crucial role of rhizosphere microbial functions in the yield formation of ratooning season rice. Introduction

In the context of global climate change, continuous population growth, and increasingly limited arable land, global rice demand is projected to rise by approximately 30% by 2050, posing a significant challenge to food security [1, 2]. Ratoon rice exploits the regeneration of axillary buds on stubble left after the initial harvest, enabling a ‘one sowing, two harvests’ system. This approach substantially increases the multiple cropping index and grain yield per unit area while simultaneously reducing labor requirements, improving land-use efficiency, and enhancing overall agricultural productivity [3, 4]. Compared with double-cropping rice or rice–wheat rotation systems, ratoon rice demonstrates a superior ecological footprint, with lower carbon emissions, reduced nitrogen losses, and higher water use efficiency [5–7]. Although the yield of the ratooning season is 45.9% lower than that of the main crop, its daily yield is higher, and energy input and economic costs are reduced by 57.3% and 71.0%, respectively, significantly enhancing both energy-use efficiency and economic returns [8]. Currently, countries such as China, India, and Brazil are actively promoting ratoon rice cultivation. In China, the planting area has recently expanded to 1.2 million hectares, spanning 11 provinces and municipalities [4, 9]. Maxent model predictions indicate that approximately 5 million hectares of arable land in southern China are suitable for ratoon rice cultivation [10]. With ongoing advances in varietal breeding and cultivation management, this ‘one sowing, two harvests’ system is expected to play an increasingly crucial role in national food security strategies [11].

However, despite the promising development trajectory of the ratoon rice industry, it continues to face numerous challenges in achieving stable yield improvements and broad regional adaptability. In theory, the ratooning season rice has a yield potential that can surpass that of the main crop; yet in practice, yields are highly variable and strongly influenced by climatic conditions and management practices. Only in certain well-established demonstration areas, such as Fujian, do the yields of ratooning season rice approach or match those of the main crop rice [12, 13]. Reported yields of ratooning season rice in China range from 2.79 to 8.74 t ha⁻¹, with a mean of only 3.64 t ha⁻¹, which corresponds to a mere 41.8% of the potential yield [14, 15]. Therefore, identifying and overcoming the key constraints limiting yield improvement of ratooning season rice represents a critical bottleneck for the wider adoption and improved performance of ratoon rice technologies.

To address these challenges, agricultural research has increasingly focused on identifying the key regulatory factors that govern yield enhancement in ratooning season rice. Extensive field experiments indicate that optimizing water and fertilizer management, as well as tillage practices following the harvest of main crop rice, can significantly improve the establishment of ratooning season rice and increase yields [16, 17]. Similarly, higher planting density [18], appropriate stubble height [19], and the use of varieties with strong ratooning ability [20] have also led to yield increases across various agroecological regions, demonstrating the substantial potential of agronomic management to enhance the productivity of ratooning season rice. However, most of these studies have been confined to specific agroecological zones or single management regimes, and their conclusions exhibit considerable variability in applicability and stability across different climates, soils, and production contexts [21]. This limitation suggests that relying solely on surface-level agronomic practices cannot fully explain yield fluctuations in ratooning season rice. In fact, the intrinsic responsiveness of agroecosystems and their internal processes—particularly the underlying mechanisms related to soil physical structure, nutrient cycling, and microbial community dynamics—also play decisive roles in yield formation. Notably, most existing studies focus on how agronomic practices affect the yield of ratooning season rice, whereas the nutrient-supply mechanisms mediated by cultivar-specific differences in rhizosphere microbial functional genes (e.g., genes involved in carbon fixation and phosphate transport) remain insufficiently and unsystematically elucidated. Therefore, a more systematic, multi-level, and multi-perspective analysis of the mechanisms underlying yield formation in ratooning season rice is warranted to provide a robust theoretical foundation for breakthroughs in key technologies and their broad, environment-adaptive deployment.

Soil serves as the fundamental environmental substrate for crop production, with its structural properties and ecological functions directly influencing resource-use efficiency and yield formation. Soil microorganisms, often referred to as “remodelers of ecological rules,” are key drivers of cropland ecological processes and play crucial roles in nutrient cycling, disease suppression, and the mitigation of abiotic stresses [22, 23]. In recent years, system-level manipulation of the plant microbiome has emerged as a pivotal strategy to enhance both crop productivity and the resilience of agricultural systems [24, 25]. Among microbial management pathways, the rhizosphere — the critical interface between plant and soil — is the primary target for precise microbiome manipulation. A large body of research indicates that the structural and functional characteristics of rhizosphere microbial communities are intrinsic determinants of crop performance [26]. For ratooning season rice, the unique axillary-bud regeneration mechanism makes yield formation more dependent on the coordinated regulation of soil physical structure, nutrient transformation efficiency, and microbial activity, resulting in a more systemic and complex ecological response. Preliminary evidence supports this hypothesis: Guo et al. (2024) discovered that reductions in yield of ratooning season rice were closely linked to plant senescence, which was induced by diminished rhizosphere microbial activity [27]. Additionally, Li et al. (2025) reported that the enrichment of rhizosphere autotrophic microbes enhances energy-use efficiency and lowers the carbon footprint, representing a critical biological mechanism underlying high yield in ratooning season rice [28, 29]. Collectively, these findings point to a central proposition: rhizosphere microorganisms play a pivotal regulatory role in stabilizing the yield of ratooning season rice.

During the assembly of the rhizosphere microbiome, host genotype is widely recognized as a primary driver of both structural and functional differentiation within microbial communities. Previous studies have shown that differences in microbiomes between crop varieties substantially exceed the variations observed within a single variety, and that distinct genotypes maintain considerable structural divergence in their microbiomes even under identical soil and management conditions [30]. Yue et al. (2024) found that the enrichment of specific rhizosphere fungi can activate drought-response pathways in wheat, thereby enhancing its drought tolerance [31]. Similarly, Zhou et al. (2022) reported that rhizosphere microbes can suppress Fusarium wilt by activating host immune pathways, thereby improving disease resistance in tomatoes [32]. These studies have expanded the theoretical framework for microbiome-mediated control of crop traits and have stimulated deeper investigations into genotype-specific microbial assemblages across various crops. In the case of ratoon rice, previous research indicates that several varieties produce comparable yields in the main crop season but diverge significantly in yield during the ratooning season [33]. This suggests that, starting from similar baseline conditions, the regulation of the rhizosphere microbiome may play a major role in yield formation during the ratooning season. Hence, we propose the hypothesis that, within a ratoon cropping system, high-yielding rice varieties selectively enrich genotype-specific microbial communities during the yield formation process. Furthermore, these microbial communities are posited to more effectively enhance host traits that are critical for determining yield.

In this context, we selected two rice varieties that produced comparable yields during the main crop season but exhibited pronounced yield divergence during the ratooning season. We systematically analyzed their rhizosphere microbial community structures and functional attributes to identify the key ecological mechanisms by which rhizosphere microorganisms shape yield formation during the ratooning season. By linking microbial community features with varietal yield performance, this study provides a theoretical foundation for enhancing yield stability in ratoon rice and offers guidance for the development of microbiome-based precision management strategies.

Materials and methods

Experimental site and materials

The experiment was conducted in 2022 and 2023 at the experimental station of Fujian Agriculture and Forestry University, located in Baisha Town, Minhou County, Fuzhou, Fujian Province (119.280°E, 26.080°N). This site has been under continuous ratoon rice cultivation for many years. This region is characterized by a typical subtropical monsoon climate. During the 2022 growing season, the average temperature was recorded at 23.51 °C, with cumulative rainfall totaling 1,433.77 mm. In 2023, the average temperature increased slightly to 23.83 °C, while cumulative rainfall decreased to 1,230.44 mm (Fig. S1). The surface soil at the experimental site was classified as sandy loam. The plough layer exhibited a pH of 6.18, an organic matter content of 2.12%, total nitrogen (TN) of 1.64 g·kg⁻¹, available phosphorus (AP) of 12.35 mg·kg⁻¹, and available potassium (AK) of 90.1 mg·kg⁻¹. We selected rice varieties that exhibit yields comparable to those of main crop rice, yet demonstrate significantly different yields in ratooning season rice. Specifically, we focused on the indica–japonica hybrid rice with a high yield in ratooning season rice, YongYou1540 (purchased from Ningbo Seed Co., Ltd., Ningbo, China), and the indica inbred rice with a low yield in ratooning season rice, JinHui809 (kindly provided by the College of Agriculture, Fujian Agriculture and Forestry University, Fuzhou, China).

Experimental design

Field experiments were established using a randomized complete block design, with each plot measuring 20 m² and consisting of four replicates. Small ridges were constructed between the plots, and drainage ditches were installed around the experimental area to ensure that each plot could be irrigated and drained independently. The transplanting density was set at 20 cm × 20 cm, with a single seedling transplanted per hill. The main crop rice was harvested on August 21, 2022, and August 19, 2023, respectively. Fertilization for both the main crop rice and ratooning season rice adhered to local high-yield cultivation practices. For the main crop rice, pure nitrogen was applied at a rate of 225 kg·ha⁻¹, divided among basal, tillering, panicle-initiation, and grain-filling applications in a ratio of 4:2:3:1. The total nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium (N:P:K) application ratio was 1:0.5:0.8; phosphorus fertilizer (containing 12% P₂O₅) was applied entirely as a basal dressing, while potassium fertilizer (containing 60% K₂O) was split evenly (1:1) between basal and panicle-initiation dressings. For the ratooning season rice, a total of 120 kg·ha⁻¹ of pure nitrogen was applied in two dressings—at the start of the ratoon tillering period (day 0) and at the uniform-heading stage—in a 3:1 ratio. At the harvest of the main crop rice, stubble height was maintained at 25 cm for all varieties. Pest and disease control, along with other field management practices, were consistently applied across treatments.

Sampling and measurements

Yield and yield components

At maturity, each plot was harvested in its entirety. After removing unfilled grains and impurities, the grain moisture content was standardized to 14%, allowing for the determination of the actual yield. Grain moisture content was measured using a moisture meter (GAC 2100 AGRI; DICKEY-john, Minneapolis, MN, USA). Additionally, based on the average tiller number of each plot, five representative hills per plot were selected to assess spikelets per panicle, seed-setting rate, and panicle density (panicles·m⁻2). Following hand threshing, grains were immersed in tap water to separate filled grains, which were then oven-dried at 70 °C until reaching a constant mass for grain weight determination.

Measurements of tiller dynamics, dry matter, and non-structural carbohydrates

The day of the main crop rice harvest was designated as day 0 of the ratooning season rice. Subsequently, every five days, 20 plants were randomly sampled from each plot using the five-point sampling method to determine the number of ratoon tillers.

After oven-drying and weighing the ratooning season rice stubble, the samples were ground and passed through a 100-mesh sieve. A 0.1 g subsample was utilized to determine the contents of soluble sugars and starch, following the anthrone colorimetric method described by Zou [34]. Soluble sugars were extracted in a boiling water bath, while starch was extracted from the remaining residue using 9.2 mol·L⁻¹ HClO₄ (perchloric acid). Both extracts were reacted with anthrone reagent, and absorbance was measured at 630 nm. A standard curve relating absorbance to sugar concentration was established using sucrose standards, from which the concentrations of soluble sugars and starch in the samples were calculated and expressed as mg·g⁻¹ DW. Non-structural carbohydrates (NSC) were defined as the sum of soluble sugars and starch.

Soil sample collection, soil physicochemical properties, soil enzyme activities, and microbial nutrient limitation characteristics

At 0, 10, and 20 days following the initiation of the ratooning season rice, five uniformly growing representative plants per cultivar were selected from each plot, and the entire root systems were excavated. After promptly removing debris from the root surfaces, rhizosphere soil tightly adhering to fine roots (with a root diameter of < 1 mm) was collected. Rhizosphere soils from the four plots of the same cultivar were thoroughly homogenized and immediately aliquoted into sterile sampling bags. One aliquot was flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and transferred to a − 80 °C freezer for soil enzyme activity assays and DNA extraction, while the other aliquot was air-dried and passed through a 2-mm sieve to remove debris for soil nutrient analyses.

Total nitrogen (TN) and total phosphorus (TP) were quantified using the HClO₄–H₂SO₄ digestion method with a Smartchem 2000 automatic discrete chemical analyzer (Alliance Instruments, Italy). Ammonium nitrogen (NH₄⁺-N) was assessed via the indophenol blue colorimetric method, while nitrate nitrogen (NO₃⁻-N) was evaluated using a dual-wavelength spectrophotometric method. Available phosphorus (AP) was determined through the molybdenum-antimony colorimetric method [35]. Soil organic carbon (SOC) was measured using the external heating potassium dichromate oxidation method, and easily oxidizable organic carbon (EOC) was assessed via the KMnO₄ oxidation method [36]. Dissolved organic carbon (DOC) was extracted using cold water [37]. Microbial biomass carbon (MBC) was determined through the chloroform fumigation–K₂SO₄ extraction method, while microbial biomass nitrogen (MBN) and microbial biomass phosphorus (MBP) were both evaluated using the chloroform fumigation–extraction method [38].

The activities of β−1,4-glucosidase (BG), β-D-cellobiohydrolase (CBH), β−1,4-N-acetylglucosaminidase (NAG), L-leucine aminopeptidase (LAP), and acid phosphatase (ACP) were determined using a microplate fluorometric assay [39].

The microbial nutrient limitation status was evaluated using the vector length (VL) and vector angle (VA) derived from unimodal enzymatic stoichiometry. Specifically, VL indicates the relative degree of carbon limitation, while VA distinguishes between nitrogen and phosphorus limitations: VA >45° indicates phosphorus limitation, VA < 45° indicates nitrogen limitation, and the greater the deviation from 45°, the stronger the corresponding nitrogen or phosphorus limitation [40]. Extraction of rhizosphere soil bacterial DNA and high-throughput sequencing

DNA extraction, PCR amplification, and sequencing of soil samples were performed by Beijing Aoweisen Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Soil genomic DNA was extracted using the E.Z.N.A. Soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Inc., USA). The quality and concentration of DNA were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., USA) and 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. DNA samples were stored at − 20 °C for subsequent analyses. The extracted total soil DNA was amplified for the V4 region of the 16 S rDNA gene using barcoded primers GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA and GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT. Libraries were constructed with the TruSeq® DNA PCR-Free Sample Preparation Kit and sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq platform. Raw sequencing image data were processed using the RDP Classifier algorithm with the Silva database Release 119 to cluster reads with > 97.0% similarity into operational taxonomic units (OTUs). Representative OTU sequences were aligned and taxonomically annotated. The OTU abundances in each sample were then normalized for downstream diversity and differential community analyses.

Structure and characteristics of community co-occurrence networks

Microbial co-occurrence networks were independently constructed for each treatment using rarefied OTU tables. The “reshape2” and “WGCNA” packages [41, 42] were employed to develop microbial network matrices, while network visualization was conducted using Gephi 0.10.1, retaining edges with Spearman’s correlation coefficients |r| >0.8 and P < 0.01. To assess the complexity of microbial networks and the interactions among taxa, we calculated key network properties, including the number of nodes, number of edges, average degree, graph density, modularity index, and the ratio of positive to negative interactions. These metrics were computed utilizing the “igraph” package [43].

Cohesion serves as an index to quantify microbial interactions [44]. In this study, bj represents the relative abundance of species j, while sij denotes the interaction strength between species i and j, quantified using the Pearson correlation coefficient, hence sij=sji. Upon the removal of selected nodes from the multilayer ecological network (MEN), a node i is deemed extinct or isolated and subsequently removed from the network if wMISi = 0 (indicating a complete loss of connectivity) or wMISi < 0 (indicating insufficient mutualistic interactions to sustain its presence). This process is repeated iteratively until all remaining species exhibit positive wMIS values. The proportion of surviving nodes serves as an indicator of network robustness. Network robustness was evaluated under two scenarios: the random removal of 50% of nodes and the targeted removal of five module hub nodes [43].

Data analysis

After normalizing the operational taxonomic unit (OTU) abundances across samples, we conducted diversity and differential analyses. The alpha diversity of the microbial communities was computed using the diversity function from the R package ‘vegan’ [47]. Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) based on Bray–Curtis distances was performed utilizing the corresponding functions in the ‘vegan’ package [47]. We predicted key microbial indicators influencing the yield of ratooning season rice by employing a random forest regression model constructed with the R package ‘randomForest’ [48]. Mantel’s r was calculated utilizing the ‘mantel_test’ function from the R package ‘linkET’. The P-values were derived via permutation testing, with statistical significance determined at a threshold of P < 0.05. Additionally, pairwise relationships among environmental variables were characterized using Pearson correlation coefficients and visualized as a correlation heatmap, with significance annotated at P < 0.05, 0.01, and 0.001. Figures were generated using Microsoft Excel 2019, Origin 2024b, and R version 4.2.9, while statistical significance was assessed with SPSS version 22.0 through Duncan’s multiple range test (α = 0.05).

Results

Yield and ratooning performance

Based on two consecutive years of field experiments, this study compared the yield components and growth characteristics of two varieties, YY1540 and JH809, under the ratoon cropping system. The results showed that the dynamics of tiller number, stump dry matter, and stump non-structural carbohydrate content exhibited similar patterns in both varieties during the ratooning season, with most changes occurring within the first 20 days after the harvest of main crop rice (Fig. 1A–C). These findings suggest that the initial 20 days following the harvest constitute a critical period for tiller formation in ratooning season rice. According to the yield statistics presented in Table S1, the combined yield of the main crop rice and ratooning season rice for YY1540 in 2022 and 2023 was higher than that of JH809. This advantage was particularly pronounced in the yield of ratooning season rice, with YY1540 producing 21.17% and 24.07% more than JH809 in 2022 and 2023, respectively. Furthermore, the yield ratio of ratooning season rice to main crop rice was significantly higher in YY1540, exceeding that of JH809 by 12.86% and 17.15% in the two years (Fig. 1D), indicating a superior ratooning capacity for YY1540.

As shown in Fig. 2, no significant differences were detected between YY1540 and JH809 in TN, TP, or SOC contents. However, regarding available nutrients, YY1540 exhibited significantly higher levels of NO₃⁻-N, NH₄⁺-N, AP, EOC, DOC, MBC, MBN, and MBP compared with JH809, with increases of 7.73%, 4.95%, 13.76%, 14.47%, 4.54%, 9.59%, 11.26%, and 32.94%, respectively. For rhizosphere enzyme activities associated with microbial nutrient acquisition, the activities of BG, CBH, NAG, LAP, and ACP were all significantly higher in YY1540 than in JH809 (Fig. 3A–E), showing increases of 19.50%, 18.66%, 87.41%, 13.95%, and 47.38%, respectively.

Analysis of microbial nutrient limitation characteristics using the enzymatic vector model indicated that the VA values for both YY1540 and JH809 exceeded 45°, suggesting that rhizosphere microorganisms were primarily phosphorus-limited during the ratooning season. Moreover, the VA of JH809 was significantly higher than that of YY1540 (Fig. 3H), indicating that the rhizosphere microorganisms of the lower-yielding variety JH809 experienced a greater degree of phosphorus limitation. Similarly, the VL of JH809 was significantly higher than that of YY1540 (Fig. 3G), implying that its microbial community was subjected to stronger carbon limitation. Taken together, these results suggest that the rhizosphere microbial community of the higher-yielding variety YY1540 possesses a relative advantage in nutrient mobilization and acquisition.

Characteristics of rhizosphere bacterial community diversity and composition in ratooning season rice

As shown in Fig. S2A, the rarefaction curves approached saturation at 97% sequence similarity, indicating that the sequencing depth sufficiently covered the bacterial community diversity and that the data were reliable for downstream analyses. Results of community diversity analysis (Table S2) revealed that the diversity indices of JH809 were significantly higher at 10 and 20 days after harvest than at day 0, suggesting an increase in rhizosphere bacterial diversity during regeneration in this variety. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis distances was further employed to quantitatively assess differences in community structure between varieties (Fig. S2B). The results indicated that in JH809, samples at day 0 were distinctly separated from those at days 10 and 20 along the PCoA1 and PCoA2 axes, indicating substantial shifts in rhizosphere microbial community structure during regeneration. In contrast, YY1540 exhibited no obvious separation among time points, suggesting a relatively stable community structure.

Further comparisons were conducted on the top ten dominant bacterial phyla and genera based on their relative abundance (Fig. S3). The findings demonstrated that in JH809, the abundances of dominant phyla, including Proteobacteria, Cyanobacteria, and Actinobacteriota, experienced a significant decline during the regrowth phase (P < 0.05), as illustrated in Fig. S3C. At the genus level, as depicted in Fig. S3D, the top ten dominant genera present at day 0 showed significant reductions during regeneration. Notably, genera such as Chitinophaga, Novosphingobium, and Rhizobacter also exhibited marked decreases as regrowth advanced (P < 0.05). In contrast, in YY1540, the dominant phylum Acidobacteriota experienced a significant increase in abundance, while the dominant genera Bathyarchaeia and Candidatus_Nitrosotalea showed a marked decline (Fig. S3A–B; P < 0.05). In summary, rice varieties with differing yields during the ratooning season exhibited notable differences in rhizosphere bacterial diversity and community succession, with high-yield varieties demonstrating a relatively stable microbial community structure.

Analysis of rhizosphere bacterial co-occurrence network mechanisms in ratooning season rice

To evaluate the complexity of microbial networks and interspecies interactions, we constructed rhizosphere bacterial co-occurrence networks for the rice varieties JH809 and YY1540 at 0, 10, and 20 days after harvest (Fig. 4A). The network analysis included structural metrics such as the number of nodes, the proportion of positively correlated edges, modularity, positive cohesion, overall cohesion, and robustness as a measure of community resistance to perturbation (Fig. 4E). The results indicated that the number of network nodes in JH809 increased significantly and remained elevated; both the proportion of positive edges and the modularity index exhibited a rise-and-fall trajectory, mirroring the trends in positive cohesion and overall cohesion. In contrast, YY1540 maintained a consistently high node count from the outset. Its proportion of positive edges also experienced an increase followed by a decline, while its modularity index demonstrated a general upward trend. Robustness analysis revealed a significant decline in community robustness for JH809, whereas YY1540 showed no significant change (Fig. 4E), suggesting that the rhizosphere microbiome of YY1540 was more resistant to disturbance. We infer that YY1540, with its higher network modularity index and a greater proportion of positive edges, enhanced metabolic complementarity among microbes, thereby sustaining community robustness and ensuring a continuous nutrient supply.

Analysis based on a random forest regression model (Fig. 5) demonstrated a strong predictive ability for both the yield of ratooning season rice and the yield ratio of ratooning season rice to main crop rice, with corresponding R² values of 0.7648 and 0.6479, respectively. Variable-importance profiles indicated that the structural features of the rhizosphere microbial community outperformed alpha diversity metrics overall. Specifically, network robustness was identified as the primary predictor in both tasks, followed by network cohesion (including total and negative cohesion). In contrast, phylogenetic diversity (PD_whole_tree) and alpha diversity indices (e.g., Shannon and Chao1) contributed less and exhibited inconsistent effects. Taken together, while shifts in the bacterial community help explain the variation in the yield of ratooning season rice and the yield ratio of ratooning season rice to main crop rice, the underlying mechanisms remain to be elucidated. Therefore, further investigation into the effects of key bacterial taxa on the yield of ratooning season rice is warranted.

Changes in the abundance of functional genes involved in C, N, and P cycling in rhizosphere bacterial communities of ratooning season rice

In order to elucidate variety-specific differences in nutrient-cycling traits during regeneration and their potential effects on plant growth, this study further compared the abundances of rhizosphere microbial functional genes involved in carbon (C), nitrogen (N), and phosphorus (P) cycling between YY1540 and JH809 during the ratooning season. In the carbon cycle (Fig. 6), YY1540 exhibited significantly higher abundances of carbon-fixation-associated genes (e.g., acsA, acsB, korA, and accA) compared with JH809; genes involved in carbon degradation (malQ, pulA, and malL) were likewise more abundant in YY1540. These results indicate that the rhizosphere microbiome of YY1540 exhibits a greater capacity for both carbon fixation and carbon decomposition, potentially providing the plant with enhanced pools of available carbon. In YY1540, the rhizosphere abundance of acsA, a carbon fixation gene, was higher, which may enhance microbial carbon-fixation efficiency. This increase could lead to a higher content of EOC in the soil, thereby providing nutritional support for the accumulation of non-structural carbohydrates in the rice stubble (Fig. 1C). In terms of nitrogen cycling (Fig. 7), YY1540 contained significantly higher abundances of the nitrogen-fixation gene nifH and nitrification genes amoA and hao compared to JH809, while denitrification genes norB and nosZ were significantly less abundant in YY1540. These findings suggest that the YY1540 rhizosphere microbiome has a stronger nitrogen-fixing potential and greater N-transformation efficiency, which may reduce nitrogen losses and improve plant nitrogen availability. Regarding the phosphorus cycle (Fig. 8), YY1540 also showed significantly higher abundances of genes related to high-affinity inorganic phosphate transport (pstS), organic P uptake (phnD), and polyphosphate degradation (ppx) than JH809, reflecting a greater microbial potential for phosphorus acquisition and mobilization.

In summary, YY1540 and JH809 exhibit significant differences in the abundances of rhizosphere microbial functional genes involved in carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus cycling in ratooning season rice. YY1540 demonstrates dual advantages in carbon fixation and degradation, enhanced nitrogen fixation and N transformation efficiency, as well as more effective phosphorus uptake and utilization. Collectively, these traits contribute to YY1540’s superior nutrient acquisition and growth performance in ratooning season rice.

Relationships between the abundance of functional genes involved in nutrient cycling, rhizosphere nutrient status, microbial nutrient limitation characteristics, yield, and ratooning performance

Mantel tests combined with correlation analyses further revealed the relationships between the abundance of rhizosphere microbial functional genes involved in C, N, and P cycling, and rhizosphere nutrient status, microbial nutrient limitation characteristics, the yield of ratooning season rice, and the yield ratio of ratooning season rice to main crop rice (Fig. 9). The results showed that on days 0, 10, and 20, microbial functional genes exhibited varying degrees of significant correlations with the aforementioned agronomic and soil physicochemical indicators. Specifically, carbon-cycling genes were significantly correlated with NO₃⁻-N, AP, EOC, MBC, MBP, VA, VL, the yield of ratooning season rice, and the yield ratio of ratooning season rice to main crop rice; nitrogen-cycling genes were significantly correlated with NH₄⁺-N, NO₃⁻-N, EOC, MBC, MBP, VA, VL, the yield of ratooning season rice, and the yield ratio of ratooning season rice to main crop rice; phosphorus-cycling genes were significantly correlated with NH₄⁺-N, NO₃⁻-N, AP, EOC, DOC, MBC, MBN, MBP, VA, VL, the yield of ratooning season rice, and the yield ratio of ratooning season rice to main crop rice. Among these, VA and VL were negatively correlated to varying degrees with the ratoon-season yield and the yield ratio of ratooning season rice to main crop rice. Collectively, these findings indicate that changes in rhizosphere microbial C, N, and P cycling functional genes are closely linked to soil nutrient status, microbial nutrient limitation, and the yield of ratooning season rice, potentially playing a key role in driving yield differentiation in the ratooning season rice.

Discussion

Ratoon rice is cultivated under a “one sowing, two harvests” system, which has significant potential to ensure food security and improve resource use efficiency. However, its yield during the ratooning season generally exhibits high variability, representing a major bottleneck that limits the large-scale adoption of this cultivation mode. Rhizosphere microorganisms, as key drivers of soil nutrient cycling and critical ecological processes, are closely associated with crop yield formation. Previous studies have indicated that the first 20 days of the ratooning season rice constitute a critical period for tiller formation, during which the number of effective panicles and the yield of the ratooning season rice are determined [4, 49, 50]. In this context, this study selected two rice varieties with similar yields of main crop rice but contrasting yields in ratooning season rice: a high-yield variety, YY1540, and a low-yield variety, JH809. We systematically compared the dynamic changes in soil nutrient status and rhizosphere microbial communities during the early growth stages of ratooning season rice (at 0, 10, and 20 days) to provide theoretical support for rhizosphere-based ecological regulation and cultivation management, aimed at achieving high and stable yields in ratoon rice.

Reduced nutrient limitations in the rhizosphere of high-yield varieties during the ratooning season may result from functionally enhanced microbial communities

As the material foundation for crop growth and yield formation, the availability of soil nutrients directly influences actual productivity. Rhizosphere microorganisms enhance this availability by secreting extracellular enzymes that promote organic matter decomposition and nutrient release, making them key regulators of nutrient effectiveness [51–54]. In this study, we conducted a systematic comparison of the nutrient content present in rhizosphere soils and the nutrient acquisition traits of rhizosphere microorganisms between a high-yield rice variety (YY1540) and a low-yield rice variety (JH809) within a ratoon cropping system. The results showed that during the early growth stage of ratoon rice, although the rhizosphere soils of the two varieties did not differ significantly in TN, TP, or SOC contents, the concentrations of available nutrients (NO₃⁻-N, NH₄⁺-N, AP, EOC, and DOC) were significantly lower in JH809 than in YY1540 (Fig. 1). This indicates that, under identical cultivation management, the rhizosphere of JH809 provided relatively fewer nutrients readily available to crops and microorganisms. Previous studies have shown that low nutrient availability often drives microorganisms into nutrient-limited states [55]. Analysis of rhizosphere enzyme activities further revealed differences in microbial nutrient acquisition between the two varieties: the activities of extracellular enzymes associated with carbon (C), nitrogen (N), and phosphorus (P) acquisition were significantly higher in YY1540 than in JH809 (Fig. 2A–E), indicating a stronger nutrient acquisition potential of its rhizosphere microbial community. Vector analysis of enzyme stoichiometry (Fig. 2G–H) showed that the vector length (VL) of JH809 was significantly greater than that of YY1540, indicating a stronger carbon limitation in its microbial community. Both varieties exhibited vector angles (VA) greater than 45°, suggesting overall phosphorus limitation; however, JH809 had a significantly larger VA than YY1540, implying a higher degree of phosphorus limitation and greater relative demand for phosphorus. This finding not only aligns with the perspective of Cui et al. (2022) regarding the relationship between labile nutrient availability and microbial nutrient limitation [55], but it also supports our correlation analysis: VA was significantly and negatively correlated with AP and with yield of ratooning season rice (Fig. 9). Therefore, under identical management regimes, variations in available phosphorus are likely to influence the yield formation of ratooning season rice by modulating the state of microbial nutrient limitation, thereby shaping rhizosphere microbial nutrient-acquisition strategies. By maintaining higher AP and lower VA, YY1540 demonstrates a more favorable microbial nutrient-constraint profile, providing a potential advantage in nutrient acquisition. The VA values of both varieties did not reach the nitrogen limitation threshold, indicating that rhizosphere microbial communities were not nitrogen-limited in ratooning season rice. This may be attributed to conventional nitrogen fertilization, which supplies adequate nitrogen to crops and microorganisms during the early growth stage of ratooning season rice. Collectively, these results provide preliminary evidence that high-yield varieties during the ratooning season may enrich rhizosphere microbial communities with enhanced functional capacities, thereby improving nutrient availability and promoting yield formation in ratooning season rice.

A highly stable microbial community structure could better support rice plants in coping with nutrient-limited conditions during the ratooning season

To further elucidate the potential mechanisms by which rhizosphere microorganisms influence the supply of available nutrients and the yield of ratooning season rice through functional communities, we compared the rhizosphere microbial community structures of the varieties YY1540 and JH809. The results indicated that JH809 exhibited significantly higher bacterial α-diversity and a markedly altered community composition, whereas YY1540 showed no significant changes in either α-diversity or community composition (Table S2). This observation contrasts with the common notion that varieties with superior or more stable traits are typically associated with higher microbial diversity [56–58]. This phenomenon may be explained by reduced nutrient availability or carbon–phosphorus limitation, which suppresses the proliferation of dominant bacterial taxa, thereby creating more ecological niches for coexisting groups and markedly increasing bacterial α-diversity [59]. The shifts in dominant bacterial genera in JH809 further support this hypothesis: the top 10 most abundant genera in its rhizosphere microbiome all exhibited significant declines (Fig. S3). Moreover, ecological interactions within microbial communities play crucial roles in community assembly and functional expression [60]. In the early growth stage of ratoon rice, although JH809 exhibited a greater number of network nodes, the proportion of positive edges, community cohesion, and network robustness all declined significantly (Fig. 4). This suggests that nutrient scarcity or resource limitation may intensify microbial competition and antagonistic interactions, thereby weakening metabolic complementarity and cross-feeding, which reduces positive correlations within the community and compromises overall stability [61, 62]. In contrast, higher modularity in microbial networks is known to support community stability and sustain key ecological functions such as nutrient cycling and matter transformation [61]. This pattern is consistent with the increased modularity and enhanced community stability observed in the rhizosphere microbiome of YY1540. However, random forest regression analysis (Fig. 5) indicated that microbial diversity, community cohesion, and robustness possessed some predictive power for the yield of ratooning season rice, with a total explanatory rate exceeding 50%. Nevertheless, the number of core predictors was limited, suggesting that while community structural traits can reflect differences in ratoon yield and the yield ratio of ratooning season rice to main crop rice, they are not the primary drivers. This highlights the need for further investigation into rhizosphere microbial functions related to nutrient cycling, particularly carbon and phosphorus cycling.

Enhanced nutrient cycling by rhizosphere microorganisms—especially carbon and phosphorus cycling—could improve the availability of essential nutrients and positively impact the yield of ratooning season rice

Building on these results, we further analyzed the abundance of functional genes related to carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus cycling in the rhizosphere microbiomes of YY1540 and JH809. Carbon cycling is a major pathway through which soil microorganisms acquire energy and nutrients [63]. In this study, the abundances of carbon-degrading genes (malQ, pulA, malL) and carbon-fixation genes (acsA, acsB, korA, accA) in the rhizosphere microbiome of YY1540 were significantly higher than those in JH809. This difference may be attributed to the higher concentrations of DOC and EOC in the rhizosphere of YY1540, which provided sufficient substrates for carbon degradation, promoted organic carbon mineralization and CO₂ release, and subsequently supported the increase in abundance of carbon fixation genes in autotrophic and facultative autotrophic microorganisms via carbon fixation pathways [64–66]. Moreover, increases in carbon-fixation and carbon-degradation genes are generally accompanied by enhanced microbial metabolic activity and nutrient demand, which, in turn, drive the upregulation of nutrient-acquisition enzymes for nitrogen and phosphorus, maintaining the elemental balance required for cell growth [67, 68]. This explains why nutrient acquisition enzyme activities were significantly higher in the rhizosphere of YY1540 than in JH809. In nitrogen cycling pathways, the abundances of nitrogen-fixation genes (nifH) and nitrification-related genes (amoA, hao) were also significantly higher in YY1540 compared to JH809. Since microbial nitrogen fixation typically requires high energy input [69], the increase in nitrogen-fixation gene abundance is likely associated with the enrichment of DOC and EOC [70]. The enhanced nitrogen-fixation capacity and increased abundance of nitrification-related genes may explain why NH₄⁺-N concentrations were significantly higher in the rhizosphere soil of YY1540 compared with JH809 [71]. In ratoon rice production, phosphorus fertilizer is typically not reapplied in ratooning season rice because superphosphate applied during the main crop rice mineralizes slowly and is thought to supply phosphorus throughout the ratooning season rice. However, the slow mineralization of phosphorus fertilizer is largely due to the limited decomposition efficiency of rhizosphere microorganisms. Most soil phosphorus exists in insoluble inorganic or organic forms and must be mobilized by the microbial secretion of functional enzymes, such as phosphatases, to become available to plants. Therefore, phosphorus deficiency in ratooning season rice arises not only from the lack of external supplementation but also from the limited phosphorus-activation capacity of rhizosphere microorganisms. Our results indicated that both YY1540 and JH809 were subjected to phosphorus-limited conditions (Fig. 2H). Under low inorganic phosphorus conditions, YY1540 exhibited relatively higher abundances of inorganic phosphorus transport system genes (pstS), which maximized phosphate uptake efficiency. Simultaneously, its higher abundance of organic phosphorus transport genes (phnD) enabled more effective utilization of organic phosphorus. Additionally, the abundance of polyphosphate-degrading enzyme genes (ppx) was significantly greater in YY1540, facilitating the mobilization of intracellular phosphorus reserves and thus providing available inorganic phosphorus under fluctuating supply–demand conditions (Fig. 8). Notably, microbial carbon metabolism and phosphorus activation are bidirectionally regulated; carbon metabolism supplies the energy required for phosphorus activation, while phosphorus availability can, in turn, regulate the synthesis of carbon-degrading enzymes [72]. In the JH809 rhizosphere, lower concentrations of DOC and EOC constrained the energy supply necessary for phosphorus activation, resulting in reduced AP and exacerbating phosphorus limitation [73–75]. In contrast, the rhizosphere of YY1540 exhibited an enrichment trend for the key carbon degradation gene malQ, along with phosphorus mobilization-related genes ppx, pstS, and phnD. This finding offers a potential mechanistic explanation for the stronger carbon and phosphorus limitations, as well as the lower levels of AP observed in the JH809 rhizosphere system.

Further correlation analysis revealed significant associations between the abundances of functional genes involved in carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus cycling and soil nutrient status, as well as microbial nutrient limitation indices (VL and VA), the yield of ratooning season rice, and the yield ratio of ratooning season rice to main crop rice. Notably, the yield of ratooning season rice was significantly negatively correlated with microbial nutrient limitation (Fig. 9). In summary, enhanced nutrient cycling functions of rhizosphere microorganisms—particularly those related to carbon and phosphorus cycling—can improve soil nutrient availability and increase microbial metabolic activity, thereby jointly promoting nutrient uptake and yield formation in ratooning season rice. Among varieties exhibiting pronounced yield divergence in ratooning season rice, such microbial functional differences may represent a key ecological mechanism driving yield variation.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that, under the ratoon cropping system, compared to low-yield rice variety (JinHui809), high-yield rice variety (YongYou1540) exhibits greater rhizosphere microbial activities associated with carbon (C), nitrogen (N), and phosphorus (P) cycling, particularly in the carbon and phosphorus pathways. These enhanced functions confer a stronger capacity for nutrient mobilization and acquisition in the rhizosphere, as evidenced by significantly higher concentrations of key readily available nutrients—including available phosphorus (AP), dissolved organic carbon (DOC), ammonium nitrogen (NH₄⁺-N), nitrate nitrogen (NO₃⁻-N), and easily oxidizable organic carbon (EOC)—in the rhizosphere soil, alongside elevated activities of microbial nutrient-acquiring enzymes. Consequently, these processes effectively alleviate carbon and phosphorus limitations in the rhizosphere microenvironment, ensuring a sustained nutrient supply and ultimately promoting yield enhancement in ratooning season rice. Collectively, our findings provide theoretical support for elucidating the role of rhizosphere microbe–nutrient interactions in the yield formation of ratooning season rice and establish a foundation for developing yield-improvement strategies based on microbial–nutrient regulation. Additionally, the results suggest that future research should implement targeted microbial inoculation trials. Emphasis could be placed on isolating and applying elite strains or consortia that either harbor or highly express key functional genes such as acsA and pstS. Concurrently, phosphorus management strategies for the ratooning season rice, such as the co-application of microbial inoculants with organic carbon amendments, should be integrated to validate both the efficacy and the mechanistic pathways through which C–P cycling microorganisms enhance the yield stability and productivity of ratooning season rice. These efforts would establish a technical foundation for integrated microbe–nutrient management.
