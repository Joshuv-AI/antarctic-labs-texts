A fossil humerus of Desmostylus highlights the spatio-temporal distributional changes of its genus on both sides of the Pacific coastlines
Authors: Kumiko Matsui, Marin Mitoh, Takehisa Tsubamoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Desmostylus is an extinct genus of large, quadrupedal, herbivorous marine mammals from the Oligocene to Miocene strata of the North Pacific. Despite abundant fossil discoveries, the taxonomic, ontogenetic, and ecological aspects of this genus remain insufficiently understood. In this study, we reexamined a previously misidentified specimen (NMNS-PV 44111) from the Miocene Chikubetsu Formation in Hokkaido, Japan. Archival records rediscovered in 2024 enabled precise documentation of the locality and its stratigraphic context. Comparative anatomical analysis of the humerus revealed that the specimen represented a juvenile Desmostylus, with a body size larger than that of early desmostylians but smaller than that of fully mature Desmostylus individuals. The growth stage suggests a rapid increase in body size during the early ontogenetic phases. Stratigraphic and paleontological evidence places the specimen within the Middle Miocene Climatic Optimum (MMCO), a period linked to marine transgression and warm conditions that may have facilitated a northward range expansion of this genus. Our findings underscore the importance of reassessing legacy specimens using updated stratigraphic and comparative frameworks to better understand the evolutionary history of marine mammals in the North Pacific.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46323-1.

Introduction

Desmostylia is an enigmatic, extinct group of quadrupedal, herbivorous marine mammals known from both coasts of the North Pacific Ocean1–7. Numerous fossil records, particularly from the Middle Miocene marine strata, suggest that desmostylians are key taxa for understanding Miocene marine paleoecology. Their fossil records range from the Eocene/Oligocene boundary8 to the Late Miocene9–11.

Desmostylia is a monophyletic group, but its phylogenetic position remains debated, with hypotheses placing it within either Afrotheria (Tethytheria:2 or Perissodactyla (Laurasiatheria:12,13. Desmostylia comprises two monophyletic clades: Desmostylidae and Paleoparadoxiidae. The former is uniquely characterized by seven or more major cusps on the third upper molar (M3)14. The Desmostylidae family includes four genera and six species. Among them, Desmostylus is notable for its abundant fossil records, particularly from middle Miocene marine strata (e.g.5,15,

Despite extensive fossil records of Desmostylus, much of its biology, ontogeny, and evolutionary history remain poorly understood. Desmostylians were among the North Pacific’s large, metabolically active marine vertebrates, and their temporal changes in body size are thought to reflect coastal productivity16. Pyenson and Vermeij16 documented the body size evolution of marine consumers. They showed that Desmostylus was the most extensive marine herbivorous consumer from the Oligocene to the Middle Miocene in the North Pacific.

Previous studies have examined the age and distribution of Desmostylus fossils using paleoenvironmental analyses of fossil-bearing strata in Japan17,18. Both authors noted that Desmostylus fossils were predominantly recovered from the Middle Miocene Climatic Optimum (MMCO), a period of global warmth and high marine productivity. However, Ogasawara18 further proposed that the genus inhabited cool-temperate zones and became extinct as a result of the Late Miocene Global Cooling around 11 million years ago (Ma), likely associated with polar-region cooling events.

While body size evolution has been described in Paleoparadoxiidae19,20, further evidence is required to evaluate such trends in Desmostylus. For example, the large mandibles of Desmostylus were found in the late Middle to late Miocene of the Puente Formation in Orange County, California9; however, further evidence is required to understand their potential body size evolution.

In this study, we describe a newly reexamined Desmostylus specimen discovered in Hokkaido, Japan, and discuss its taxonomic and evolutionary significance. In July 2024, KM rediscovered administrative documents from Obira Town, Hokkaido, including excavation plans, locality information, and photographs taken during the excavation. These records enabled the reassessment of the specimens. In addition to describing this material, we compiled the spatiotemporal distribution of Desmostylus along the northwestern Pacific coastline and compared it with that of the northeastern Pacific to gain insights into their evolutionary history and extinction patterns.

Research the history of NMNS-PV 44,111

This specimen was initially reported by Nakaya et al.21. According to Nakaya et al.21, the specimen originated from the distribution area of the Tappu Formation in the geological map of Tsushima et al.22, but they identified that this fossil was found in the previously undiscovered allochthonous block of the Chikubetsu Formation based on its lithofacies and molluscan fossils (see p. 20 line 15–17 Nakaya et al.21 and Supplemental File 1). Nakaya et al.21 identified the specimen as Behemotops, a stem genus of Desmostylia.

According to an administrative document housed in the office of Obira Town, this specimen was discovered in Obira Town, Rumoi District, Hokkaido, Japan, during the construction of the Batta-no-sawa forestry road in 1988 (See Supplementary Material 2 and Fig. 1). The Obira Education Board received the report from a construction team member and visited the site on October 6, 1989. The education board initially identified it as a well-preserved large Eocene mammal fossil, as the fossil site probably lies within the distribution area of the Eocene Tappu Formation, as described by Tsushima et al.22. To locate other parts of the specimen, the education board organized a research team comprising researchers from the Historical Museum of Hokkaido (now the Hokkaido Museum), Hokkaido University, the Hokkaido University of Education, and Kagawa University, from July 25 to 28, 1990. The team discovered a natural mold of a humerus in sandstone, which is associated with the same specimen initially identified by the education board. In the short research report in the office of Obira Town’s administrative documents, the specimen was identified as a humerus of the primitive Desmostylia, possibly Behemotops, and found from the Miocene Chikubetsu Formation because the locality is originally within the distribution area of the Chikubetsu Formation in Tsushima et al.22(Fig. 1). The administrative document also includes locality maps and photos (Supplementary Material 1). The administration documents reached the same conclusion as those in Nakaya et al.21.

Geological setting

Invertebrate fossils (for example, Glycymeris sp., Macoma cfr. nasuta) were collected from the same horizon as the specimens at the time of excavation in 1990 (Table 1), and these findings are consistent with previous studies on the Chikubetsu Formation and its associated molluscan fauna22,23.

The locality map of the administrative document indicates that the specimen is within the distribution range of the Chikubetsu Formation22. Previous reports incorrectly identified the specimen locality as part of the distribution area of the Eocene Tappu Formation; however, they suggested that the specimen originated from a previously undiscovered block of the Chikubetsu Formation, a unit that occurs sporadically owing to its complex geological structure21. Figure 1 shows the revised locality and geological maps.

Formation and age

The specimen was found in sandstone near the Taki-no-sawa (or Tengu-no-sawa) River, within the distribution area of the Chikubetsu Formation (Fig. 1). This formation is widely distributed across northwestern Hokkaido. The Neogene geology of the Obira region is quite complex, with the Cretaceous Upper Yezo Group underlying the Neogene layers. These layers trend N-S to NNE-SSW and dip towards the west. In ascending order, they are classified as the Jugosenzawa, Arakizawa, Chikubetsu, and Kotambetsu Formations. The Chikubetsu Formation overlies the Arakizawa Formation, probably with an unconformity, and is conformably overlain by the Kotambetsu Formation23.

The Chikubetsu Formation comprises two sections: an upper siltstone and a lower sandstone (e.g.23, . According to photographs in the administrative documents of Obira Town at the time of discovery (Fig. 2), the specimen was found in sandstone, indicating that it was located in the lower section of the Chikubetsu Formation, because the middle to upper Chikubetsu Formation is composed of mudstone and an interbedded alternation of sandstone and mudstone. Diatom fossils from the Chikubetsu Formation indicate that the age of formation ranges from the late Early Miocene to the Middle Miocene (e.g.23,24, . Motoyama25 estimated a more precise age for the Chikubetsu Formation of 16.8–15.5 Ma. The depositional conditions of the lower part of the Chikubetsu Formation ranged from the intertidal zone at the earliest stage to the euneritic and/or mesoneritic zones. At the same time, molluscan fossils from the formation suggest that its paleoclimate was characterized by cool to temperate marine conditions. The molluscan fauna of the Chikubetsu Formation suggests that they migrated from the south during the Mid-Miocene Climatic Optimum (MMCO)23.

Description and comparative anatomy

NMNS-PV 44,111 contains three parts of the humerus (Figs. 3 and 4): a well-preserved distal end, a poorly preserved humeral head, and a cast of the natural mold of the humeral shaft. All measurements are listed in Table 2.

The surface of the humeral head is rough, and the original surface is lost (Fig. 3A). The left side of the isolated humeral head is missing. The specimen lacks sharp surfaces, indicating that the humeral head was isolated from its original position and that its surface dissolved during the taphonomic process. The humeral head has a maximum width of 11.8 cm and a maximum height of 5.8 cm. The articular facet of the humeral head is very wide compared to potential outgroups (e.g., perissodactyls, proboscideans, and sirenians), but similar to other desmostylians. The humeral head is oval and slightly convex distally, like other desmostylians. Unfortunately, it is difficult to make further comparisons because of poor preservation.

The cast of the natural mold of the humeral shaft includes the left proximal side of the humerus (Fig. 3B). The shaft measured a maximum length of 32.1 cm, with its thickest part at 8.14 cm. The proximal end retains the epiphyseal line of the humerus. While the distal side appears oval and convex, it does not align with the humeral head because of preservation issues. The natural mold of the shaft is thick and straight, similar to that of other desmostylians. The lesser tubercles are unossified and displaced from the epiphysis, indicating that the ontogenetic stage of the specimen is significantly younger than that of sub-adult desmostylians (UHR-18466, NMNS PV-5601; Fig. 5) and closer to that of juvenile desmostylians (GSJ-F7743, LACM 150150; Fig. 5). The lesser tubercle does not develop to the same size as that of paleoparadoxiids. Additionally, the crest of the lesser tubercle does not develop like that of sub-adult to adult D. hesperus (UHR-18466 and OME-U-0170) or Ashoroa.

The distal part of the humerus has a well-preserved surface but shows some damage, possibly occurring during the excavation process (Fig. 4). All measurements are listed in Table 2. This part is well ossified, unlike juvenile desmostylians (GSJ-F7743, LACM 150150; Fig. 5). The distal part of the humerus is substantial. The cross-section is more quadrangular than that of Neoparadoxia, Ashoroa, or D. hesperus specimens. The humeral condyle is prominent, unlike that of potential relatives. This specimen features a hole connecting the olecranon fossa and coronoid fossa, but it is a secondary hole drilled during preparation. The olecranon fossa is elongated and deep in the proximal-distal direction. This fossa is narrower and longer than that in D. hesperus and paleoparadoxiids but resembles that in Ashoroa. The coronoid fossa is broad and long, like those of juvenile (GSJ F07743) and adult (OME-U-0170) D. hesperus. Subadult D. hesperus (UHR-18466), Ashoroa, and paleoparadoxiids exhibit narrower and shorter coronoid fossae than those of the specimen. The lateral epicondyle is well-developed distally compared to that of P. tabatai and Ashoroa. The distal end of the lateral epicondyle was damaged but is broader than that of juvenile D. hesperus (GSJ F07743). The epicondyle crest is not as well defined as that in adult D. hesperus (OME-U-0170) or N. repenningi (UCMP 81302). However, it is developed like that of juvenile N. cecilialina (LACM 150150) or Ashoroa. The medial epicondyle is also well developed but flat, similar to that of Ashoroa. Compared to paleoparadoxiids, the medial epicondyle is small, not sloping towards the inside, but well-developed compared to D. hesperus. However, compared to other desmostylians, the medial condyle slope of this specimen is almost right-angled. The craniocaudal width of the trochlea is also wide, like that of other Desmostyloidea. Compared to the size of the trochlear and craniocaudal diameters of the medial or lateral condyle, the specimen is almost the same size. Still, the medial condyle is slightly wider than the lateral condyle (medial vs. lateral condyle = 1.15:1). D. hesperus also has similar patterns, but P. tabatai (1.27:1) and N. cecilialina (1.44:1) have wider medial condyles than this specimen. The trochlea is wide, accounting for 78% of the distal end. Behemotops has a narrower trochlea than the specimen, unlike other desmostyloideans (D. hesperus GSJ F07743 76%, OME-U-0170 86%, UHR18466 77%; Ashoroa AMP21 64%; P. tabatai NMNS PV-5601 62%; N. repenningi UCMP 81302 81%; N. cecilialina LACM150150 79%). The trochlear groove is deep and clear, similar to that of sub-adult to adult D. hesperus and Ashoroa. Juvenile D. hesperus (GSJ F07743) and paleoparadoxiids have shallower trochlear grooves than those of the specimen.

Discussions

Previous desmostylian discoveries around the locality

Matsumoto30 documented the discovery of a Desmostylus molar near the locality of the specimen; however, detailed information on this discovery remains unavailable. There is insufficient evidence to accurately locate the specimen from the Chikubetsu Formation or adjacent formations, although it may have belonged to the Chikubetsu Formation. Kimura and Takahisa31 reported the potential discovery of a Desmostylus molar within the Chikubetsu Formation. This specimen was found on the riverbank of the Sankebetsu River, located approximately 30 km north of the locality of NMNS-PV 44,111. Their specimen, which was found as a boulder in the Upper Yezo Group distribution area dating to the Late Cretaceous, was determined to originate from the Chikubetsu Formation because of the broad distribution of fossiliferous lower Chikubetsu Formation upstream of the Sankebetsu River. NMNS-PV 44,111 is the first discovery with accurate locality, horizon, and age information from the Chikubetsu Formation and the surrounding region.

The ontogenetic stage of NMNS-PV 44,111

Desmostylia dental ontogeny is like the delayed molar eruption of afrotherians in that the eruption of the final set of molars is delayed until after the mandible has reached its maximum9. Santos et al.9 proposed five ontogenetic stages of Desmostylus: two juvenile stages, the subadult stage, the adult stage, and the advanced-age stage, based on a very old Desmostylus mandible (OCPC 22650) from Orange County, CA, USA. According to their definition, the first juvenile stage includes p1 and p2; the second juvenile stage includes p3-4 and m1; the subadult stage includes p4 and m1-2; and the adult stage includes m1-3 in the Desmostylus mandible. In the advanced age stage, Desmostylus lost all postcanine teeth in its mandible. In this study, we compared Desmostylus with other desmostylian taxa, including paleoparadoxiids. These taxa retain their premolars even after the third molars (m3) are heavily worn (e.g., Archaeoparadoxia weltoni32. Therefore, the ontogenetic stage is defined here based on the sequence of molar eruption and the functional molar position involved in mastication (i.e., m1, m2, or m3).

The specimen has an ossified distal end but an unossified humeral head and lesser tubercle epiphysis. The comparative materials used in this study indicate that our specimen is older than the m1 stage (D. hesperus; GSJ F07743) or the m1/m2 stage (N. cecilialina; LACM 150150) but younger than the m2 stage, with an almost completed but not yet erupted M3 (D. hesperus; UHR18466) (Fig. 5). It was almost the same ontogenetic stage as the holotype specimen of D. japonicus, with an erupted and incomplete M3 (NMNS PV-5600;33.

Body size of NMNS-PV 44,111 and Desmostylus growth speed

The humeral measurements of the desmostylians are listed in Table 2. The humerus size of NMNS-PV 44,111 is smaller than that of UHR18466 and OME-U-0170 and larger than that of GSJ F07743 in Desmostylus. More basal Desmostylidae, Ashoroa (AMP21), is much smaller than NMNS-PV 44,111. Compared to Paleoparadoxiidae, NMNS-PV 44,111 is larger than UCMP114285 (Archaeoparadoxia) and NMNS PV-5601 (Paleoparadoxia) and is almost the same size as LACM 150,150 (N. cecilialina). Barnes20 reconstructed the body size of LACM 150,150, which was estimated to be approximately 2.44 m long. The specimen could also be estimated to be almost the same size as that of juvenile N. cecilialina, LACM 150,150. NMNS-PV 44,111 was identified as a young Desmostylus specimen, but older than LACM 150,150 (N. cecilialina). This specimen was not as large as an adult Neoparadoxia spp. AMP 21 (Ashoroa), UCMP114285 (Archaeoparadoxia), and NMNS PV-5601 (Paleoparadoxia) were older than NMNS-PV 44,111 but had smaller humeri. This suggests that NMNS-PV 44,111 was larger than Ashoroa, Archaeoparadoxia, or Paleoparadoxia. Therefore, the body size of NMNS-PV 44,111 falls within the Desmostylus range.

Inuzuka19 reconstructed the body sizes of desmostylian specimens, indicating that the body size of UHR18466 was 2.71 m and that of GSJ F07743 was 1.68 m long. NMNS-PV 44,111 was located between UHR18466 and GSJ F07743. The specimens in order of growth stage are GSJ F07743 (m1), NMNS-PV 44,111 (m1/2), and UHR18466 (m2). Figure 6 shows the estimated body size and mandibles of these specimens. Compared with NMNS-PV 44,111 and other specimens, the body size growth rate significantly changed from the m1 stage to the m1/2 stage and from the m1/2 stage to the m2 stage within the m1 to m2 stage (Fig. 6). The Desmostylus body length was 1.45 times larger from m1 to m1/2, whereas the volume was three times larger, the length of the body increased by a factor of 1.1, and the volume increased by 1.3 times from m1/2 to m2. In Desmostylus, the growth rate is thought to have been rapid until approximately the time of m2 eruption, after which it slowed significantly. This is potentially related to reproductive age.

In Perissodactyls, Lu et al.34 described a female fossil rhino, Plesiaceratherium gracile, with a fetus; the specimen was also in the m1/2 stage. This coincided with a period of reduced Desmostylus growth rates. In contrast, in Tethytheria, Perry35 reported an African elephant specimen with an 8-month-old fetus, which used mm4/M1. To compare these two studies, the sexual maturation of Desmostylus is estimated to occur around the time when m2 begins to be used, similar to that of perissodactyls. However, more skeletons must be discovered and studied to facilitate further discussion and clarify this issue.

Spatiotemporal expansion and extinction patterns of Desmostylus in the Northwestern Pacific

In this section, we discuss the spatiotemporal distribution and extinction patterns of Desmostylus based on updated fossil records and their paleoenvironmental context in the northwestern Pacific. Many Desmostylus fossils have been found in the North Pacific Miocene marine strata (e.g.3,5, . In the northwestern Pacific region, the specimen UHR 07428 from Sakhalin Island is dated to the Aquitanian age (22.06–21.06 Ma). Additionally, another fossil, previously housed in the Karafuto Agency Museum, which ceased operations in 1945, is believed to have been discovered in a geological formation spanning from the Aquitanian to the Burdigalian (21.4–16.9 Ma). As the museum is no longer operational and the original report lacks photographic documentation of the specimens, it is currently impossible to reexamine these fossil records. Nevertheless, these two specimens indicate that, at least during the Aquitanian stage, Desmostylus may have extended its range along the northwestern Pacific coast. However, it had not reached the mid-latitude areas of the northwest Pacific. During the Aquitanian, the Sea of Japan began to expand36, and it is believed that there were few environments suitable for Desmostylus to inhabit at that time. At the early Burdigalian stage (18 − 17 Ma), Desmostylus localities were limited in number and were only found in central Japan (Fig. 7; Supplementary Material 3).

In contrast, throughout the Burdigalian stage, the distribution area of Desmostylus expanded from Sakhalin Island to the central part of Japan (Fig. 7). At this stage, most of the present-day Honshu Island in Japan was submerged under the sea and was composed of many small islands rather than one large island37–41. This suggests that the fragmented landmass of Honshu provided favorable shallow marine environments for Desmostylus. The distribution area of Desmostylus in the Langhian and Serravallian remained essentially unchanged (Fig. 7). In the Tortonian stage, several Desmostylus were reported42–44, and the last age of Desmostylus on the northwestern side was approximately 11 Ma. These geographic and paleoenvironmental changes likely set the stage for the subsequent northward expansion of Desmostylus.

NMNS-PV 44,111 is from 16.8 to 15.5 Ma and is one of the earliest Desmostylus found on Hokkaido Island (Supplementary Material 2). Most Desmostylus specimens from before and around this age were either already lost, not pictured in the reports, or their locations were poorly documented, making these records impossible to verify and likely containing inaccurate information. This specimen, the most age-controlled, demonstrated a real northward extension of the Desmostylus range. Two additional specimens from nearly the same time (approximately 17–15.5 Ma) were found only in the Okhotsk Seaside and the inland area of Hokkaido. These fossils show that Desmostylus suddenly expanded its habitat on Hokkaido Island. This means that in the early stages of its expansion, they already lived on both the Okhotsk and Sea of Japan sides.

The age of NMNS-PV 44,111 is consistent with the Middle Miocene Climatic Optimum (MMCO)45. Chinzei17 and Ogasawara18 noted that most Desmostylian fossils from Japan were found in strata dated after the N8 foraminiferal zone, although its base age was not well constrained at the time. Recent studies have refined the base of the N8 zone to 16.8 Ma46, and our fossil data (Supplementary Material 2) fall entirely after this boundary, supporting their hypothesis with updated chronostratigraphic evidence. This alignment further emphasizes the significant role of mid-Miocene climatic conditions in shaping the Desmostylus distribution. These findings support the idea that the MMCO provided favorable environmental conditions, such as the expansion of shallow seas, which facilitated the northward spread of Desmostylus. This timing coincides with the MMCO (16.9–14.7 Ma)45, a global warming event that paralleled the expansion of the Sea of Japan36. The concentration of Desmostylus fossils during this interval suggests that warming conditions may have facilitated their ecological success in the northwestern Pacific Ocean. Ogasawara18 questioned extinction scenarios that attribute the demise of Desmostylus solely to global climate change.

Following the MMCO, the global cooling event known as the Middle Miocene Climate Transition (MMCT) commenced, and Earth continued to cool after the MMCT45,47,48. In the northwestern Pacific, it caused sea level regression and the initiation of the Tsushima Current49, followed by current closure and an influx of colder waters into the Sea of Japan50. Although these environmental changes affected many marine animals48,51, they did not coincide with the disappearance of Desmostylus. The fossil record shows that Desmostylus persisted in Honshu and Hokkaido until the late Miocene, indicating resilience to climatic disruptions. Desmostylus disappeared from the Northeastern Pacific during the early Tortonian (Fig. 7 and Supplementary Material 2). At the same time, the diversification event of Odobenidae, a clade of pinnipeds, is known in this region52. This suggests that it is worthwhile to explore other possible factors contributing to the extinction of Desmostylus.

Because climatic factors alone do not explain the extinction of Desmostylus, potential ecological competition must be considered. One potential factor is niche replacement. Desmostylus is an herbivorous marine mammal, and the number of herbivorous marine mammals is limited. However, during the Miocene, the distribution area of Desmostylia overlapped with that of Sirenia53; Fig. 7). Not only the geographic ranges, but also the maximum body sizes of both taxa are comparable16. In addition, Domning54,55 suggested that both Sirenia (Dusisiren and Hydrodamalis) and Desmostylia were kelp feeders. Based on analyses of carbon isotopes, Desmostylus exhibited high δ13C values, suggesting that Desmostylus foraged on seagrasses7, similar to modern sirenians. These results show that Desmostylia and North Pacific Sirenia used the same resources. In the Northwestern Pacific, the earliest record of Sirenia is from the Oligocene Sakamizu Formation in Kitakyushu, Japan56; Fig. 7). This specimen is the earliest sirenian record from the Pacific. However, there is a huge age gap (approximately 20 Ma) between the first and second sirenian fossils from the Northwestern Pacific, and the second record is Dusisiren dewana from the late Miocene Hongo Formation57 or an undescribed sirenian fossil from Kinkomanai Formation58. Both ages are approximately 11 Ma59, which slightly overlaps with the last Desmostylus record (Supplementary Material 3). This means that in the western Pacific, the first Sirenia lineage was once extinct in the Northwestern Pacific, and the second-oldest sirenian lineage (Hydrodamalinae) migrated into the Northwestern Pacific around 11 Ma and survived until very recently. The replacement of seagrass-eating desmostylians by sirenians in the North Pacific suggests the potential for competitive interactions among these taxa60,61.

Despite the numerous fossils found in the northwestern Pacific, Desmostylus’s primary habitat and evolutionary center were likely in North America. Matsui & Pyenson62 reported that the oldest of Desmostylus specimen from Skooner Gulch Formation, the middle of California, which has clear dating, was dated between 23.03 and 22 Ma, suggesting that Desmostylus originated in western North America. In the northwestern Pacific, Desmostylus existed at least during the Aquitanian stage, indicating that it expanded its distribution area on both sides of the Pacific coast. No Desmostylus fossils have been found in the northwestern Pacific after approximately 11 Ma (Fig. 7; Supplementary Material 3). However, on the other side of the Pacific, multiple fossil records exist from possibly younger ages9,11,63. Desmostylus first appeared and lived for a long time in the Northeastern Pacific. However, it is believed that its primary habitat and center of evolution were in North America.

In the northernmost area of the western Pacific, near Sakhalin or Kamchatka, many Desmostylus fossils have been reported to date64–67. However, most specimens were discovered before World War II, and some have since been lost. The discovery of further specimens from these regions would be extremely important for elucidating the spatiotemporal distribution of Desmostylus and understanding the differences between the two sides of the Pacific Ocean. These historical gaps highlight the need for future discoveries to better define the spatial and temporal distribution of Desmostylus in this region and gain a comprehensive understanding of its paleoecology and extinction.

These findings not only provide new insights into Desmostylus evolution but also highlight the importance of continued paleontological exploration in the underrepresented regions of the northwestern Pacific.

Conclusion

This study presents a reexamination of a Desmostylus humerus (NMNS-PV 44111) from the Chikubetsu Formation in Hokkaido, Japan, with detailed stratigraphic, morphological, and ontogenetic analyses. The specimen, now identified as a juvenile Desmostylus, fills a temporal and geographical gap in the known distribution of the genus and provides new insights into desmostylian growth and body-size dynamics. Our updated synthesis of fossil occurrences demonstrates that Desmostylus expanded its range across Hokkaido Island during the Middle Miocene Climatic Optimum (MMCO), likely in response to warming seas and the emergence of newly shallow marine habitats. The genus persisted in the northwestern Pacific until approximately 11 million years ago (Ma), after which a combination of global cooling, regional oceanographic changes, and ecological competition with Sirenians may have driven its extinction. The rediscovery and analysis of this specimen highlight the importance of archival and geological contexts in reevaluating historical fossil finds and contribute to refining our understanding of Miocene marine mammal paleobiogeography.

Materials and methods

Paleontological ethics statement

The locality of NMNS-PV 44,111 is within a national forest. NMNS-PV 44,111 was collected under the authority of Obira Town, Rumoi District, Geological Survey of Hokkaido (current Hokkaido Research Organization), and the District Forest Office for Tappu (current the District Forest Office for the Southern Part of Rumoi), Hokkaido Regional Forest Office, Japan’s Forest Agency during 1989–1990, and has been housed in the collections at NMNS.
