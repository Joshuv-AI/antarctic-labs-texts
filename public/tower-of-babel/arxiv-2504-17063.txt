Port-Hamiltonian modeling of rigid multibody systems
Thomas Berger, René Hochdahl, Timo Reis, Robert Seifried

Abstract
We employ a port-Hamiltonian approach to model nonlinear rigid multibody systems subject to both position and velocity constraints. Our formulation accommodates Cartesian and redundant coordinates, respectively, and captures kinematic as well as gyroscopic effects. The resulting equations take the form of nonlinear differential-algebraic equations that inherently preserve an energy balance. We show that the proposed class is closed under interconnection, and we provide several examples to illustrate the theory.

Introduction

Port-Hamiltonian system models encompass a broad class of nonlinear physical systems
Jeltsema and van der Schaft (2014)
;
van der Schaft (2017)
and originate from port-based network modeling of complex dynamical systems in various physical domains, such as mechanical and electrical systems
Jeltsema and van der Schaft (2014)
;
van der Schaft (2013)
. Modeling with port-Hamiltonian systems has garnered significant attention, and considerable progress has recently been made in port-Hamiltonian modeling of constrained dynamical systems, leading to differential-algebraic equations (DAEs)
van der Schaft (2010)
;
Beattie et al. (2018)
;
Mehl et al. (2018)
;
Maschke and van der Schaft (2018)
;
Maschke and van der Schaft (2020)
;
van der Schaft (2013)
;
Venkatraman and van der Schaft (2010)
;
Gernandt et al. (2020)
.
In particular, the extension of the port-Hamiltonian framework to implicit energy storage
Maschke and van der Schaft (2018)
;
Maschke and van der Schaft (2020)
;
Mehrmann and van der Schaft (2023a)
;
Mehrmann and van der Schaft (2023b)
enables the modeling of a significantly broader range of physical systems with constraints, such as electrical circuits
Gernandt et al. (2020)
.

An inherent feature of port-Hamiltonian systems, in addition to their ability to maintain an energy balance, is the incorporation of a modular principle. This principle is built upon an elegant theory of power-preserving coupling, resulting once more in a port-Hamiltonian system
Jeltsema and van der Schaft (2014)
;
Cervera et al. (2007)
;
Behrndt et al. (2010)
;
Skrepek et al. (2023)
. Historically, the theory of port-Hamiltonian systems has been developed based on the observation that principles from rational mechanics (see e.g.
Arnold (1989)
) apply to various physical domains. Over time, however, the theory has advanced well beyond its mechanical origins and has been further developed as an independent framework. In this article, we take a step back and analyze rigid multibody systems through the lens of port-Hamiltonian theory.

Multibody systems
Schiehlen and Eberhard (2014)
;
Woernle (2024)
;
Shabana (2020)
constitute a modern computer-oriented modeling approach for mechanical systems that undergo large rigid body translational and rotational motion. This approach is widely used in fields such as machine dynamics, vehicle dynamics, robotics or biomechanics. Multibody systems consist of rigid bodies which are connected by joints and bearings as well as coupling elements such as springs and dampers. Due to occurring finite rotational motions, the systems are inherently nonlinear. As a special case, multibody systems also include vibration systems of rigid bodies, which typically undergo only small linear motions.

The Hamiltonian approach is typically formulated in terms of state variables consisting of position and momentum of each body. However, in the literature on mechanical systems, it is more common to use velocity – a so-called co-energy variable – instead of momentum. To bridge this gap, we employ the theory presented in
Maschke and van der Schaft (2020)
, which addresses implicit energy storage. This approach also facilitates the incorporation of both position and velocity constraints.

We begin by formulating translational motion in the port-Hamiltonian framework, considering systems of point masses with positions and velocities expressed in Cartesian coordinates. While this approach does not require much conceptual effort, it is limited from a practical perspective: when spatially expanded (in particular rotating) rigid bodies are involved, their orientation must also be incorporated into the model.
In this case, the nonlinear relationship between angular velocity and the rotational parameters that determine the body’s orientation must be taken into account. This introduces an additional layer of complexity compared to purely translational motion, which is also explored in this article within the port-Hamiltonian framework.

This article is structured as follows: After establishing basic notations, we provide a concise introduction to port-Hamiltonian systems in Section
2
. Subsequently, we consider multibody systems within this framework: point masses in Cartesian coordinates are discussed in Section
3
. Multibody systems in redundant coordinates are considered in Section
4
. The interconnection of two systems via their ports is introduced in Section
5
and it is shown that the class of port-Hamiltonian systems is closed under interconnection. A selection of illustrative examples are discussed in Section
6
and Conclusions are given in Section
7
. Some auxiliary results on Dirac structures and Lagrangian submanifolds are collected in Appendix
8
.

1.1
Notation

Throughout this article, the space
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
will be identified with matrices of size
n\times 1
. In particular, the Euclidean inner product of
z_{1},z_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is given by
z_{1}^{\top}z_{2}
.

The vector space framework is often insufficient for describing physical systems, particularly those involving ideal constraints. Instead, the state evolution is represented within a
manifold
, a topological space that, at least locally, resembles the structure of a real vector space. This concept is further discussed in
Reis (2021)
in conjunction with port-Hamiltonian systems. For our purposes, it is adequate to consider the slightly simpler notion of submanifolds of a finite dimensional space
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

Definition 1

(Submanifold of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a subset. Then

\mathcal{M}
is called differentiable submanifold of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
(in this article just submanifold of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
for sake of brevity), if for all
x\in\mathcal{M}
, there exists a neighborhood
U_{x}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
of
x
, a number
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and a continuously differentiable mapping
f_{x}:U_{x}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
, such that
f_{x}^{\prime}(x)
is surjective, and

\mathcal{M}\cap U_{x}=\left\{\,y\in U_{x}\,\left|\vphantom{y\in U_{x}}\,f_{x}(y)=0\,\right.\right\}.

For a submanifold
\mathcal{M}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
x\in\mathcal{M}
, the implicit function theorem implies the existence of a number
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, a neighborhood
V_{x}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
of zero, and a continuously differentiable mapping
g_{x}:V_{x}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
satisfying the conditions
g_{x}(0)=x
,
g_{x}(V_{x})\subset\mathcal{M}
and
g_{x}^{\prime}(0)
has a trivial kernel. Such a mapping
g_{x}
possessing these properties is termed a
chart of
\mathcal{M}
at
x
.

Next we introduce the concept of the tangent space. To this end, we utilize continuously differentiable curves on
\mathcal{M}
, i.e., continuously differentiable mappings
x:(-1,1)\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with
x(t)\in\mathcal{M}
for all
t\in(-1,1)
. Defining the concept of the tangent space can be quite abstract in a general differential geometric context. However, the situation becomes significantly simpler when dealing with submanifolds of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
Loosely speaking, the tangent space
T_{x_{0}}\mathcal{M}
at
x_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
consists of all vectors that can be obtained by taking derivatives of smooth curves within
\mathcal{M}
that pass through
x_{0}
.

Definition 2

(Tangent space of a submanifold)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a submanifold of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
The tangent space of
\mathcal{M}
at
x_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
is defined by

T_{x_{0}}\mathcal{M}=\left\{\,v\in\mathcal{W}\,\left|\vphantom{v\in\mathcal{W}}\,{\parbox{256.0748pt}{there exists a continuously differen\-tiable curve $x:(-1,1)\to\mathcal{M}$ with $x(0)=x_{0}$ and $\dot{x}(0)=v$}}\,\right.\right\}.

The elements of
T_{x_{0}}\mathcal{M}
are called tangent vectors of
\mathcal{M}
at
x_{0}
.

It can be observed from the definition of the tangent space that, for a chart
g_{x}:V_{x}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
of
\mathcal{M}
at
x
, we obtain
\im g_{x}^{\prime}(0)=T_{x}\mathcal{M}
. In particular,
T_{x}\mathcal{M}
is a subspace.

2
Port-Hamiltonian systems

We recall some fundamental concepts in port-Hamiltonian systems from
Maschke and van der Schaft (2018)
;
Maschke and van der Schaft (2020)
. An essential concept to grasp is the notion of a
Dirac structure
, which describes the power-preserving energy routing within the system.

Definition 3

(Dirac structure)
.

A subspace
\mathcal{D}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is called a
Dirac structure
, if
for all
f,e\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, we have

(f,e)\in\mathcal{D}\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\forall\,(\hat{f},\hat{e})\in\mathcal{D}:\;\hat{f}^{\top}e+f^{\top}\hat{e}=0.

For
(f,e)\in\mathcal{D}
, we call
e
an
effort
and
f
is termed a
flow
. By equipping
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with the indefinite inner product

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\!\rangle\;:

\displaystyle\big({\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\big)\times\big({\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\big)

\displaystyle\to{\mathbb{R}},

\displaystyle\big((f_{1},e_{1}),(f_{2},e_{2})\big)

\displaystyle\mapsto\langle{f}_{1},e_{2}\rangle+\langle f_{2},{e}_{1}\rangle,

we can conclude that
\mathcal{D}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is a Dirac structure if, and only if,
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}^{\bot\!\!\!\bot}
, where the latter denotes the orthogonal complement of
\mathcal{D}
with respect to
\langle\!\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\!\rangle
.

Any Dirac structure
\mathcal{D}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is
n
-dimensional. Furthermore, for matrices
K,L\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
,
\mathcal{D}=\im\begin{bmatrix}K&L\end{bmatrix}
is a Dirac structure if, and only if,
\rank\begin{bmatrix}K&L\end{bmatrix}=n
and
KL^{\top}+LK^{\top}=0
, see
(
Courant, 1990
, Prop. 1.1.5)
.

Dirac structures are oftentimes insufficent for being a component in port-Hamiltonian modeling of rigid multibody systems. Therefore, we require the more comprehensive concept of a
modulated Dirac structure
which is, loosely speaking, a family of Dirac structures depending on a parameter.

In a more general setting, a modulated Dirac structure on a manifold

\mathcal{M}
is defined as a certain subbundle of
T\mathcal{M}\oplus T^{\prime}\mathcal{M}
(representing the sum of the tangent bundle and cotangent bundle of
\mathcal{M}
)
(
Courant, 1990
, Def. 2.2.1)
, as discussed in
Jeltsema and van der Schaft (2014)
. This general manifold setup is not necessary for the class of multibody systems discussed in the present article.

Definition 4

(Modulated Dirac structure)
.

Let
U\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
be open. A family
(\mathcal{D}_{x})_{x\in U}
of subspaces of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is called a
modulated Dirac structure
, if the following holds for all
x\in U
:

(a)

\mathcal{D}_{x}
is a Dirac structure.

(b)

There exists a neighborhood
U_{x}\subset U
of
x
and a family
(T_{y})_{y\in U_{x}}
of linear and bijective mappings
T_{y}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to\mathcal{D}_{y}
, such that, for all
z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, the mapping

y\mapsto T_{y}z

is continuous from
U_{x}
to
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

The conditions (
a
) and (
b
) outlined above imply that a modulated Dirac structure aligns with the definition of a vector bundle, as described in
(
Lang, 1999
, Chap. III, § 1)
, where (
b
) is referred to as
local trivialization
.

Next, we introduce a relation that describes the energy storage of the system, known as a
Lagrangian submanifold
. We note that the general definition of this concept, as found in
(
Lee, 2012
, p. 568)
, is not required for the systems considered in this work. Instead of dealing with submanifolds of general manifolds, it suffices to consider submanifolds of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Typically, these manifolds are assumed to be smooth in the sense that the charts are infinitely often differentiable. However, for our purposes, we can relax this assumption and consider less smooth submanifolds.

Definition 5

(Lagrangian submanifold)
.

A submanifold
\mathcal{L}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is called
Lagrangian submanifold
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, if for all
z\in\mathcal{L}
and
{(v_{1},v_{2})}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
we have

(v_{1},v_{2})\in T_{z}\mathcal{L}\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\forall\,(w_{1},w_{2})\in T_{z}\mathcal{L}:\ v_{1}^{\top}w_{2}-w_{1}^{\top}v_{2}=0.

(1)

In
(
Lee, 2012
, Prop. 22.12)
, it is shown that for a smooth function
H:U\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
defined on a simply connected domain
U\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, the set defined as

\mathcal{L}\coloneqq\left\{\,(x,H(x))\,\left|\vphantom{(x,H(x))}\,x\in U\,\right.\right\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

is a Lagrangian submanifold if, and only if,
H
is a gradient field. That is,
\nabla\mathcal{H}=H
for some smooth function
\mathcal{H}:U\to{\mathbb{R}}
. In Appendix
8
, we present a generalization of this result, which will be used in the context of position constraints in mechanical systems. Additionally, Appendix
8
includes some results on special sets that form a modulated Dirac structure.

Another essential concept for port-Hamiltonian systems is that of a
(modulated) resistive relation
, which characterizes the internal energy dissipation within the system. This relation is defined on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. The components of
(f_{\mathcal{R}},e_{\mathcal{R}})\in\mathcal{R}
are called
resistive flows

f_{\mathcal{R}}
and
resistive efforts

e_{\mathcal{R}}
, resp.
(
Jeltsema and van der Schaft, 2014
, Sec. 2.4)
.

Definition 6

((Modulated) resistive relation)
.

A relation
\mathcal{R}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is called
resistive
, if

\forall\,(f_{\mathcal{R}},e_{\mathcal{R}})\in\mathcal{R}:\ f_{\mathcal{R}}^{\top}e_{\mathcal{R}}\geq 0.

Let
U\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
be open. A family
(\mathcal{R}_{x})_{x\in U}
is called modulated resistive relation, if
\mathcal{R}_{x}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is a resistive relation for all
x\in U
.

Having established the definitions of (modulated) Dirac structures, Lagrangian submanifolds, and (modulated) resistive relations, we are now prepared to introduce port-Hamiltonian systems, cf. also Fig.
1
. Again, we note that this class can be defined in a more general setting involving manifolds
Maschke and van der Schaft (2020)
;
Jeltsema and van der Schaft (2014)
. However, we simplify this to the specific setup required for our class of rigid multibody systems.

Definition 7

(Port-Hamiltonian system)
.

A
port-Hamiltonian (pH) system
is a differential inclusion

\left(\begin{pmatrix}\dot{x}(t)\\
f_{\mathcal{R}}(t)\\
f_{\mathcal{P}}(t)\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}e_{\mathcal{L}}(t)\\
e_{\mathcal{R}}(t)\\
e_{\mathcal{P}}(t)\end{pmatrix}\right)\in\mathcal{D}_{x(t)},\quad\big(x(t),e_{\mathcal{L}}(t)\big)\in\mathcal{L},\quad(f_{\mathcal{R}}(t),e_{\mathcal{R}}(t))\in\mathcal{R}_{x(t)},

where, for
U\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\mathcal{L}}}
being open,

•

\mathcal{D}=(\mathcal{D}_{x})_{x\in U}
with
\mathcal{D}_{x}\subset({\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\mathcal{L}}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\mathcal{R}}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\mathcal{P}}})\times({\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\mathcal{L}}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\mathcal{R}}}\times\mathcal{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n_{\mathcal{P}}})
is a modulated Dirac structure (see Definition
3
),

•

\mathcal{L}\subset\mathcal{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n_{\mathcal{L}}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\mathcal{L}}}
is a Lagrangian submanifold (see Definition
5
), and

•

\mathcal{R}=(\mathcal{\mathcal{R}}_{x})_{x\in U}
with
\mathcal{\mathcal{R}}_{x}\subset\mathcal{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n_{\mathcal{R}}}\times\mathcal{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n_{\mathcal{R}}}
is a modulated resistive relation (see Definition
6
).

The elements of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\mathcal{L}}}
,
{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\mathcal{R}}}
,
{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\mathcal{P}}}
are, accordingly, called the
energy-storing flows/efforts
,
resistive flows/efforts
and
external flows/efforts
.

Remark 1
.

It should be noted that our definition of port-Hamiltonian systems slightly differs from the one e.g. in
Jeltsema and van der Schaft (2014)
, where the negative of
\dot{x}
enters the Dirac structure, and the resistive relation fulfills
f_{\mathcal{R}}^{\top}e_{\mathcal{R}}\leq 0
for all
(f_{\mathcal{R}},e_{\mathcal{R}})\in\mathcal{R}_{x}
,
x\in U
. By substituting the flow with its negative, it is straightforward to establish a one-to-one correspondence between these two definitions. In practical terms, one approach views the system from the perspective of a consumer, while the other adopts a producer-centric viewpoint.

3
Newtownian mechanical systems: Point masses in Cartesian coordinates

In this section, we consider mechanical systems consisting of point masses, with their positions expressed in conventional Cartesian coordinates, such as in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
or
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
. Loosely speaking, we consider multibody systems in which each rigid body has a negligible spatial expansion and possesses both potential and kinetic energy. The position and velocity of all masses are given by the vector-valued functions
\bm{{r}},\bm{v}:I\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, where
I\subset{\mathbb{R}}
denotes the considered time interval. The functions
\bm{{r}}
and
\bm{v}
collectively encode the position coordinates and velocities, respectively, of all point masses in the system. Specifically, for a system with
p
point masses in three-dimensional space, we have
n=3p
, meaning each mass contributes three coordinates to the overall position vector.

We first present the modulated Dirac structure for this kind of systems. It contains velocity and force balances, as well as constraints formulated on velocity level. These velocity constraints are described by an equation of the form

A(\bm{{r}}(t))\bm{v}(t)=0,

where
A
is a continuous matrix-valued function with constant rank. Note that all or some of these constraints may be
holonomic
– that is, they represent position constraints differentiated with respect to time – if they satisfy an integrability condition. Otherwise, they are referred to as
nonholonomic
.

Let
U_{\mathrm{pos}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be an open set, representing the admissible positions of the point masses.
We consider the family that is modulated by the positions, namely
(\mathcal{D}_{\bm{{r}}})_{{\bm{{r}}}\in U_{\mathrm{pos}}}
with

\mathcal{D}_{\bm{{r}}}:=\left\{\,\begin{pmatrix}\bm{v}_{\mathcal{L},f}\\
\bm{F}_{\mathcal{L},f}\\
\bm{v}_{\mathcal{R}}\\
\bm{v}_{\ext}\\
\bm{F}_{\mathcal{L},e}\\
\bm{v}_{\mathcal{L},e}\\
\bm{F}_{\mathcal{R}}\\
\bm{F}_{\ext}\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{6n+2m}\,\left|\vphantom{\begin{pmatrix}\bm{v}_{\mathcal{L},f}\\
\bm{F}_{\mathcal{L},f}\\
\bm{v}_{\mathcal{R}}\\
\bm{v}_{\ext}\\
\bm{F}_{\mathcal{L},e}\\
\bm{v}_{\mathcal{L},e}\\
\bm{F}_{\mathcal{R}}\\
\bm{F}_{\ext}\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{6n+2m}}\,\,{\parbox{199.16928pt}{$\bm{v}_{\mathcal{L},f}=\bm{v}_{\mathcal{L},e}=\bm{v}_{\mathcal{R}},\ A(\bm{{r}})\bm{v}_{\mathcal{L},e}=0$,
\\
$\bm{v}_{\ext}=B(\bm{{r}})^{\top}\bm{v}_{\mathcal{L},e}$,\\
$\exists\,\bm{\mu}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}:$\\
$\bm{F}_{\mathcal{L},f}+\bm{F}_{\mathcal{L},e}+\bm{F}_{\mathcal{R}}+B(\bm{{r}})\bm{F}_{\ext}+A(\bm{{r}})^{\top}\bm{\mu}=0$}}\!\!\,\right.\right\}.

(2)

Before proving that this constitutes a modulated Dirac structure, let us first discuss the practical meaning of the involved vectors and matrices.
The identical vectors
\bm{v}_{\mathcal{L},f},\bm{v}_{\mathcal{L},e},\bm{v}_{\mathcal{R},e}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
represent the velocities of the point masses. One might wonder why three copies of the velocity appear in the Dirac structure. The reason is that velocity is needed in conjunction with potential energy, kinetic energy, and damping.
Furthermore,
\bm{F}_{\mathcal{L},f}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
represents the inertial force,
\bm{F}_{\mathcal{R}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
corresponds to the damping force, and
\bm{F}_{\mathcal{L},e}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
denotes a restoring force. The vectors
\bm{F}_{\ext},\bm{v}_{\ext}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
collect the forces and velocities at the external ports, respectively.
Here, the matrix
B:U_{\mathrm{pos}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times m}
describes the direction of the external forces. Moreover, as mentioned above,
A:U_{\mathrm{pos}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell\times n}
encodes the velocity constraints. The term
A(\bm{{r}})^{\top}\bm{\mu}
represents the forces that ensure the fulfillment of the velocity constraints.

The following result states that
(\mathcal{D}_{\bm{{r}}})_{{\bm{{r}}}\in U_{\mathrm{pos}}}
is a modulated Dirac structure. We do not present a proof at this point, but instead refer to Proposition
2
, which establishes a more general statement that encompasses the one below; we emphasize that Proposition
2
does not rely on any results from this section.

Proposition 1
.

Let
U_{\mathrm{pos}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be open, let
A:U_{\mathrm{pos}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell\times n}
,

B:U_{\mathrm{pos}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times m}
be continuous, and assume that
A
has constant rank on
U_{\mathrm{pos}}
. Then the family
(\mathcal{D}_{\bm{{r}}})_{\bm{{r}}\in U_{\mathrm{pos}}}
with
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{{r}}}
as in (
2
) is a modulated Dirac structure.

The damping elements are modeled using a modulated resistive structure. In the most general case, damping is described by a dissipative relation between the damping force and velocity. A typical application scenario is the consideration of kinetic friction (see
Armstrong-Hélouvry et al. (1994)
;
Leine and Nijmeijer (2004)
).
A dependence of this relation on the position vector
\bm{{r}}
may arise in non-homogeneous setups. For example, this can occur when a car moves on a road with varying surface properties.

Let us assume a damping model that is slightly simpler than the relational setup described above:
Specifically, for
U_{\rm d}\subset U_{\mathrm{pos}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, we consider a function
\bm{F}_{\rm d}:U_{\rm d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with

\forall\,(\bm{{r}},\bm{v})\in U_{\rm d}:\quad\bm{v}^{\top}\bm{F}_{\rm d}(\bm{{r}},\bm{v})\geq 0.

Then it follows immediately that
\mathcal{R}=(\mathcal{R}_{\bm{{r}}})_{\bm{{r}}\in U_{\mathrm{pos}}}
with

\mathcal{R}_{\bm{{r}}}:=\left\{\,\begin{pmatrix}\bm{F}\\
\bm{v}\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2{n}}\,\left|\vphantom{\begin{pmatrix}\bm{F}\\
\bm{v}\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2{n}}}\,\bm{F}=\bm{F}_{\rm d}(\bm{{r}},\bm{v})\,\right.\right\}

(3)

is a modulated resistive relation.

The remaining key component is the Lagrangian submanifold, which is, in the context of mechanical systems, responsible for storage of kinetic and potential energy. In particular, it encodes the restoring and inertial forces.
Let
M\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
be a symmetric and positive definite matrix (representing the mass matrix),

\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{pot}}:U_{\mathrm{pos}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
a twice continuously differentiable mapping (representing the potential energy),
and
c:U_{\mathrm{pos}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
a twice continuously differentiable function (representing the holonomic constraints on the position
{r}
),
which vanishes on a non-empty subset of
U_{\mathrm{pos}}
and whose Jacobian satisfies
\rank c^{\prime}(\bm{{r}})=k
for all
\bm{{r}}\in U_{\mathrm{pos}}
.
We then define

\mathcal{L}:=\left\{\,\!\begin{pmatrix}\bm{{r}}\\
\bm{p}\\
\bm{F}\\
\bm{v}\end{pmatrix}\in U_{\mathrm{pos}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3n}\,\left|\vphantom{\!\begin{pmatrix}\bm{{r}}\\
\bm{p}\\
\bm{F}\\
\bm{v}\end{pmatrix}\in U_{\mathrm{pos}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3n}}\,\;\parbox{156.49014pt}{$M\bm{v}=\bm{p},\,c(\bm{{r}})=0,\,$\\
$\exists\,\bm{\lambda}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k}:\ \bm{F}=\nabla\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{pot}}(\bm{{r}})+c^{\prime}(\bm{{r}})^{\top}\bm{\lambda}\!\!$}\,\right.\right\}.

(4)

Here,
\bm{p}
stands for the momenta and
\nabla\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{pot}}(\bm{{r}})
is the restoring force caused by potential energy storage.
The term
c^{\prime}(\bm{{r}})^{\top}\bm{\lambda}
represents the forces that ensure the fulfillment of the position constraints.

We can conclude from Proposition
8.11
in Appendix
8
(by setting
\mathcal{H}(\bm{{r}},\bm{p})=\tfrac{1}{2}\bm{p}^{\top}M^{-1}\bm{p}+\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{pot}}(\bm{{r}})
and
d(\bm{{r}},\bm{p})=c(\bm{{r}})
) that
\mathcal{L}
as in (
4
) is a Lagrangian submanifold.
Indeed, it is even a Lagrangian submanifold, if
M
is only positive semi-definite. This follows by an application of
Proposition

4
Multibody systems: Redundant coordinates

In this section, we consider a much more general class of multibody systems. In these general multibody systems, rigid bodies are considered. In contrast to point masses, a complete description of a rigid body includes not only its position but also its orientation, with the center of gravity often chosen as the reference point for the position. Accordingly, the Cartesian coordinates (three per body in space) must be extended by rotational parameters (at least three per body in space).

Different parameterizations such as unit-quaternions or Euler angles are possible within the port-Hamiltonian framework and have different advantages
Fujimoto et al. (2015)
. In the following, we prefer to use Euler angles, as they require only three parameters and their associated singularity in spatial representation coincides with physical limitations of the systems discussed in Section
6
. Thus, six spatial coordinates are necessary for each rigid body (three in planar motion). It should be noted that these coordinates may depend on each other if position constraints are present. Since there are more coordinates available than necessary to describe the kinematics uniquely, these coordinates are called
redundant coordinates
. In contrast, generalized coordinates are the minimal number (corresponding to the degree of freedom) of coordinates to uniquely describe the kinematics
Schiehlen and Eberhard (2014)
. Generalized coordinates are used e.g. in the Newton-Euler formalism or the Lagrangian equation of 2nd order, yielding the equations of motion as an ordinary differential equation. In contrast, the use of redundant coordinates yield the equation of motion as a differential algebraic equation. Both approaches have case-dependent advantages and disadvantages.

In the following, redundant coordinates are used to describe rigid multibody systems as port-Hamiltonian systems. With this approach, the kinematic relations between angular velocity and rotational parameters and the appearance of gyroscopic forces must be considered. The latter appear when the translational and angular velocities are defined within a body-fixed coordinate frame. This approach is used to maintain a constant mass matrix. Although this framework is more general than the one in the previous section and encompasses far more multibody systems than the previous approach, the class introduced in the following does not claim to cover all multibody systems with rigid components.

Subsequently, we use the following notation:

\bm{\zeta}
:

global positions, redundant coordinates

\bm{\Gamma}
:

global momenta,

\bm{\varpi}
:

global velocities, velocity coordinates,

\bm{\tau}
:

global forces.

Note that there is no universally agreed-upon standard notation for this in the literature. The global positions, i.e. the redundant coordinates of all bodies, collect all positions (as discussed in the previous section) and orientation parameters of all rigid bodies. Consequently,
\bm{\Gamma}
may encompass both linear and angular momenta of all bodies,
\bm{\varpi}
may consist of both linear and angular velocities of all bodies, and
\bm{\tau}
may include both forces and torques. The collection of the quantities of all bodies is reflected by the term

Again, we start with a function

representing the potential energy, where

is open. It is reasonable to assume that, in general, the number

of variables determining the potential energy may differ from the number

of variables governing the kinetic energy. This is evident in spring-mass-damper systems

, where each spring serves as a potential energy storage element, while each mass acts as a kinetic energy storage element. The kinetic energy is given by the term

being symmetric. This matrix incorporates inertia effects of all bodies, which is why it is referred to as the

As in the case of systems in Cartesian coordinates, assuming positive semi-definiteness is reasonable from a practical perspective, although it does not mathematically contribute to the findings of this article. Note that positive semi-definiteness becomes relevant when studying the existence of global solutions, which is not addressed here.

5
Interconnection

One of the biggest advantages of port-Hamiltonian models is their modular structure. That is, the class of port-Hamiltonian systems is closed under a certain type of interconnection
Cervera et al. (2007)
. This type of interconnection is based on a splitting of the external ports of two systems into those to be linked and those that are ‘truly external’, that is

f_{\mathcal{P}i}(t)=\begin{pmatrix}f_{\mathcal{P}{\rm c},i}(t)\\
f_{\mathcal{P}{\ext},i}(t)\end{pmatrix},\,e_{\mathcal{P}i}(t)=\begin{pmatrix}e_{\mathcal{P}{\rm c},i}(t)\\
e_{\mathcal{P}{\ext},i}(t)\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{\rm c}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{\ext,i}},\quad i=1,2.

The coupling relations are

f_{\mathcal{P}{\rm c},1}(t)=f_{\mathcal{P}{\rm c},2}(t),\quad e_{\mathcal{P}{\rm c},1}(t)=-e_{\mathcal{P}{\rm c},2}(t),

(9)

and the resulting external ports of the interconnected system are given by

f_{\mathcal{P}}(t)=\begin{pmatrix}f_{\mathcal{P}\ext,1}(t)\\
f_{\mathcal{P}{\ext},2}(t)\end{pmatrix},\,e_{\mathcal{P}}(t)=\begin{pmatrix}e_{\mathcal{P}{\ext},1}(t)\\
e_{\mathcal{P}{\ext},2}(t)\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{\ext,1}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{\ext,2}}.

For completeness, note that
Cervera et al. (2007)
uses the interconnection rules
f_{\mathcal{P}{\rm c},1}=-f_{\mathcal{P}{\rm c},2}
and
e_{\mathcal{P}{\rm c},1}=e_{\mathcal{P}{\rm c},2}
, which are equivalent to ours. However, we prefer the coupling relations in (
9
), as they are more natural in the context of the class of multibody systems studied in this article.

For constant (i.e., unmodulated) Dirac structures, the coupling (
9
) again results in a Dirac structure, as shown in
Cervera et al. (2007)
.
For modulated Dirac structures, it remains unknown whether this type of coupling still results in a modulated Dirac structure, as it is unclear whether the local trivialization property holds for the interconnected system. In
Jacobs and Yoshimura (2011)
;
Jacobs et al. (2010)
, additional conditions on the involved modulated Dirac structures have been imposed to ensure that the interconnection preserves the modulated Dirac structure property.

Our situation is somewhat more specific, as we focus on a subclass of port-Hamiltonian systems – namely, multibody systems as described in Section
4
. We demonstrate that, under a rather mild additional assumption on the matrices
A
and
B
, this class remains closed under interconnection via (
9
).

More precisely, we consider two multibody systems of type (
8
), where all involved matrices, functions, and variables are indexed by
i=1,2
, depending on the respective system. We further partition the matrices
B_{i}
by

B_{i}(\bm{\zeta}_{i})=[B_{{\rm c},i}(\bm{\zeta}_{i}),B_{{\ext},i}(\bm{\zeta}_{i})],\quad\;B_{{\rm c},i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\mathrm{kin},i}\times m_{\rm c}},\;B_{{\ext},i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\mathrm{kin},i}\times m_{\ext,i}},\quad i=1,2,

and the external forces and velocities by

\bm{\tau}_{\ext,i}(t)=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\tau}_{{\rm c},i}(t)\\
\bm{\tau}_{\widetilde{\ext},i}(t)\end{pmatrix},\,\bm{\varpi}_{\ext,i}(t)=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\varpi}_{{\rm c},i}(t)\\
\bm{\varpi}_{\widetilde{\ext},i}(t)\end{pmatrix},\\
\bm{\tau}_{{\rm c},i}(t),\bm{\varpi}_{{\rm c},i}(t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{\rm c}},\;\bm{\tau}_{\widetilde{\ext},i}(t),\bm{\varpi}_{\widetilde{\ext},i}(t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{\ext,i}}.

We show that under the assumption that

\rank\begin{bmatrix}A_{1}(\bm{\zeta}_{1})&0\\
0&A_{2}(\bm{\zeta}_{2})\\
B_{{\rm c},1}(\bm{\zeta}_{1})^{\top}&-B_{{\rm c},2}(\bm{\zeta}_{2})^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\equiv\text{const}

(10)

on
U_{\mathrm{pos},1}\times U_{\mathrm{pos},2}
, the coupling

\bm{\tau}_{{\rm c},1}(t)=-\bm{\tau}_{{\rm c},2}(t),\quad\bm{\varpi}_{{\rm c},1}(t)=\bm{\varpi}_{{\rm c},2}(t),

which is induced by condition (
9
), leads to an interconnected system which is again port-Hamiltonian. Note that the coupling corresponds to a connection between the two systems. More precisely, the velocities of the connected ports are equal, while the force on one side of the link acts as the counterforce on the other side. Altogether, this gives rise to the system

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{\zeta}}_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=Z_{1}({\bm{\zeta}}_{1}(t))\bm{\varpi}_{1}(t),

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{\zeta}}_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=Z_{2}({\bm{\zeta}}_{2}(t))\bm{\varpi}_{2}(t),

\displaystyle M_{1}\dot{\bm{\varpi}}_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=-Z_{1}({\bm{\zeta}_{1}}(t))^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{pot},1}(\bm{\zeta}_{1}(t))

\displaystyle\quad\,-Z_{1}({\bm{\zeta}}_{1}(t))^{\top}c_{1}^{\prime}(\bm{\zeta}_{1}(t))^{\top}\bm{\lambda}_{1}(t)-\bm{\tau}_{{\rm d},1}(\bm{\zeta}_{1}(t),{\bm{\varpi}_{1}(t)})

\displaystyle\quad\,-G_{1}(M_{1}\bm{\varpi}_{1}(t))\bm{\varpi}_{1}(t)-A_{1}(\bm{\zeta}_{1}(t))^{\top}\bm{\mu}_{1}(t)

\displaystyle\quad\,-B_{{\rm c},1}(\bm{\zeta}_{1}(t))\bm{\tau}_{{\rm c},1}(t)-B_{\ext,1}(\bm{\zeta}_{1}(t))\bm{\tau}_{\widetilde{\ext},1}(t),

\displaystyle M_{2}\dot{\bm{\varpi}}_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=-Z_{2}({\bm{\zeta}_{2}}(t))^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{pot},2}(\bm{\zeta}_{2}(t))

\displaystyle\quad\,-Z_{2}({\bm{\zeta}}_{2}(t))^{\top}c_{2}^{\prime}(\bm{\zeta}_{2}(t))^{\top}\bm{\lambda}_{2}(t)+\bm{\tau}_{{\rm d},2}(\bm{\zeta}_{2}(t),{\bm{\varpi}_{2}(t)})

\displaystyle\quad\,-G_{2}(M_{1}(\bm{\zeta}_{2}(t))\bm{\varpi}_{2}(t))\bm{\varpi}_{2}(t)-A_{2}(\bm{\zeta}_{2}(t))^{\top}\bm{\mu}_{2}(t)

\displaystyle\quad\,+B_{{\rm c},2}(\bm{\zeta}_{2}(t))\bm{\tau}_{{\rm c},1}(t)-B_{\ext,2}(\bm{\zeta}_{2}(t))\bm{\tau}_{\widetilde{\ext},2}(t),

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=c_{1}(\bm{\zeta}_{1}(t)),

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=c_{2}(\bm{\zeta}_{2}(t)),

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=A_{1}(\bm{\zeta}_{1}(t)){\bm{\varpi}}_{1}(t),

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=A_{2}(\bm{\zeta}_{2}(t)){\bm{\varpi}}_{2}(t),

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=B_{{\rm c},1}(\bm{\zeta}_{1}(t))^{\top}{\bm{\varpi}}_{1}(t)-B_{{\rm c},2}(\bm{\zeta}_{2}(t))^{\top}{\bm{\varpi}}_{2}(t),

\displaystyle\bm{\varpi}_{\widetilde{\ext},1}(t)

\displaystyle=B_{\ext,1}(\bm{\zeta}_{1}(t))^{\top}{\bm{\varpi}}_{1}(t),

\displaystyle\bm{\varpi}_{\widetilde{\ext},2}(t)

\displaystyle=B_{\ext,2}(\bm{\zeta}_{2}(t))^{\top}{\bm{\varpi}}_{2}(t).

It may appear quite complex at first glance, yet the system has an inherent structure. Specifically, it is of the form of (
8
) with

\displaystyle n_{\mathrm{pot}}

\displaystyle=n_{\mathrm{pot},1}+n_{\mathrm{pot},2},\quad n_{\mathrm{kin}}=n_{\mathrm{kin},1}+n_{\mathrm{kin},2},\quad m=m_{\ext,1}+m_{\ext,2},

\displaystyle\bm{\zeta}(t)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{\zeta}}_{1}\\
{\bm{\zeta}}_{2}\end{pmatrix},\;\bm{\varpi}=\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{\varpi}}_{1}\\
{\bm{\varpi}}_{2}\end{pmatrix},\;\bm{\Gamma}=\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{\Gamma}}_{1}\\
{\bm{\Gamma}}_{2}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\bm{\varpi}_{\ext}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\varpi}_{\widetilde{\ext},1}\\
\bm{\varpi}_{\widetilde{\ext},2}\end{pmatrix},\;\bm{\tau}_{\ext}(t)=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\tau}_{\widetilde{\ext},1}\\
\bm{\tau}_{\widetilde{\ext},2}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{pot}}(\bm{\zeta})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{pot},1}(\bm{\zeta}_{1})+\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{pot},2}(\bm{\zeta}_{2}),

\displaystyle Z(\bm{\zeta})

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}Z_{1}({\bm{\zeta}}_{1})&0\\
0&Z_{2}({\bm{\zeta}}_{2})\end{bmatrix},\;\;M=\begin{bmatrix}M_{1}&0\\
0&M_{2}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle G(\bm{\Gamma})

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}G_{1}({\bm{\Gamma}}_{1})&0\\
0&G_{2}({\bm{\Gamma}}_{2})\end{bmatrix},\;\bm{\tau}_{\rm d}(\bm{\zeta},\bm{\varpi})=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\tau}_{{\rm d},1}(\bm{\zeta}_{1},\bm{\varpi}_{1})\\
\bm{\tau}_{{\rm d},2}(\bm{\zeta}_{2},\bm{\varpi}_{2})\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}(\bm{\zeta}_{1})\\

0&A_{2}(\bm{\zeta}_{2})\\

\displaystyle B(\bm{\zeta})

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}B_{\ext,1}(\bm{\zeta}_{1})&0\\
0&B_{\ext,2}(\bm{\zeta}_{2})\end{bmatrix}.

This means that the interconnection rules cause for an additional velocity constraint
B_{{\rm c},1}(\bm{\zeta}_{1}(t))^{\top}{\bm{\varpi}}_{1}(t)-B_{{\rm c},2}(\bm{\zeta}_{2}(t))^{\top}{\bm{\varpi}}_{2}(t)=0
and the corresponding Lagrange multiplier

By condition (

) the matrix

as above has constant

, hence it follows from the results in Section

that the interconnected system is again port-Hamiltonian.

Finally, we note that the interconnection of more than two multibody systems can be reduced to the interconnection of two systems by an inductive approach.

Remark 6.5
.

In the presented theory of interconnection for port-Hamiltonian systems, some velocities of the subsystems are used for interconnection.
However, in the case of the slider-crank mechanism, an interconnection based on position level would be more appropriate.
We note that coupling via velocities, together with a suitable choice of initial conditions, implicitly yields the desired interconnection on position level.

At the modeling level, interconnection based on positions requires a modification of the standard port concept and calls for a theoretical framework involving the interconnection of Lagrangian submanifolds rather than Dirac structures.
An appropriate adaptation of the port concept has already been proposed through the notion of ‘energy ports’
Krhač et al. (2024)
.

7
Conclusion

In this article, we have presented a port-Hamiltonian formulation of a class of multibody systems with rigid components.
This includes, in particular, the formulation of suitable Dirac structures and Lagrangian submanifolds that allow for the incorporation of kinematic relations, position and velocity constraints, gyroscopic forces, and restoring forces arising from potential energy.
Furthermore, we have shown that the port-Hamiltonian interconnection of such systems again yields a system of the same structural form.
In this context, the interconnection relations behave like velocity constraints.
Our theoretical developments are illustrated by three examples.

We begin with a preparatory lemma on local image representations of continuous matrix-valued functions with pointwise full row rank.

Lemma 8.6
.

Let
U\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
be an open set, and let
E:U\to{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell\times n}
be a continuous function. Assume that
E
has constant rank
r\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, i.e.,
\rank E(x)=r
for all
x\in U
. Then, for every
x\in U
, there exists a neighborhood
U_{x}\subset U
of
x
and a continuous function
J:U_{x}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times(n-r)}
, such that
\im J(y)=\ker E(y)
for all
y\in U_{x}
.

Proof 8.7
.

Fix
x\in U
. Without loss of generality, we assume that
E(x)
admits a partition

E(y)=\begin{bmatrix}E_{11}(y)&E_{12}(y)\\
E_{21}(y)&E_{22}(y)\end{bmatrix},\quad y\in U,

such that
E_{11}(x)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r\times r}
is invertible (otherwise, rearrange the rows and columns of
E(x)
accordingly). Since
E
is continuous and
E_{11}(x)
is invertibile, there exists a neighborhood
U_{x}\subset U
of
x
such that
E_{11}(y)
is invertible for all
y\in U_{x}
. As a consequence, since
\rank E(y)=r
for all
y\in U_{x}
, there exists
R:U_{x}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{(\ell-r)\times r}
such that
[E_{21}(y),E_{22}(y)]=R(y)[E_{11}(y),E_{12}(y)]
for all
y\in U_{x}
. Then the function
J:U_{x}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times(n-r)}
with

J(y)=\begin{bmatrix}-E_{11}(y)^{-1}E_{12}(y)\\
I_{n-r}\end{bmatrix}

has the desired properties.

Next we investigate a special modulated Dirac structure that is derived from another one by incorporating an additional type of constraint. This is used in our analysis of mechanical systems to account for velocity constraints.

Proposition 8.8
.

Let
U\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
be open and
(\mathcal{D}_{x})_{x\in U}
be a modulated Dirac structure with
\mathcal{D}_{x}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
for all
x\in U
. Further, let
E:U\to{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell\times n}
be continuous and assume that the dimension of the space

\mathcal{D}_{x}\cap\big({\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times\ker E(x)\big)

(14)

is independent of
x\in U
.
Then for

\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{x}:=\left\{\,\big({f},{e}\big)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,\left|\vphantom{\big({f},{e}\big)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\,E(x)e=0,\;\exists\,\mu\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}:\ \big(f+E(x)^{\top}\mu,e\big)\in\mathcal{D}_{x}\,\right.\right\},\quad x\in U,

we have that
(\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{x})_{x\in U}
is a modulated Dirac structure.

Proof 8.9
.

Fix
x\in U
.

Step 1: We show that
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{x}
is a Dirac structure. A straighforward calculation yields that

\forall\,(f,e),(\hat{f},\hat{e})\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{x}:\quad\hat{f}^{\top}e+f^{\top}\hat{e}=0.

Next assume that
(f,e)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
fulfills
\hat{f}^{\top}e+f^{\top}\hat{e}=0
for all
(\hat{f},\hat{e})\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{x}
.
We use the orthogonal decomposition
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}=\im E(x)^{\top}+\ker E(x)
(that is,
(\ker E(x))^{\perp}=\im E(x)^{\top}
) to obtain a decomposition
f=f_{1}+f_{2}
,
e=e_{1}+e_{2}
with
f_{1},e_{1}\in\im E(x)^{\top}
,
f_{2},e_{2}\in\ker E(x)
.
Then, for all
(\hat{f}_{1},\hat{e})\in\mathcal{D}_{x}
with
E(x)\hat{e}=0
,
\hat{\mu}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}
, we have that
(\hat{f}_{1}+E(x)^{\top}\hat{\mu},\hat{e})\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{x}
and hence

0=(\hat{f}_{1}+E(x)^{\top}\hat{\mu})^{\top}e+f^{\top}\hat{e}=\big(\hat{f}_{1}+E(x)^{\top}\hat{\mu})^{\top}(e_{1}+e_{2})+(f_{1}+f_{2})^{\top}\underbrace{\hat{e}}_{\in\ker E(x)}\quad\\
=\hat{f}_{1}^{\top}(e_{1}+e_{2})+\hat{\mu}^{\top}E(x)e_{1}+f_{2}^{\top}{\hat{e}}.

This gives, by setting
(\hat{f}_{1},\hat{e})=(0,0)
and
\hat{\mu}=0
one after the other, that
e_{1}\in\ker E(x)
and

\forall\,(\hat{f}_{1},\hat{e})\in\mathcal{D}_{x}:\quad 0=\hat{f}_{1}^{\top}(e_{1}+e_{2})+f_{2}^{\top}{\hat{e}}.

Using
e_{1}\in\ker E(x)
together with
e_{1}\in\im E(x)^{\top}
, we obtain that
e_{1}=0
, and thus

\forall\,(\hat{f}_{1},\hat{e})\in\mathcal{D}_{x}:\quad 0=\hat{f}_{1}^{\top}e_{2}+f_{2}^{\top}{\hat{e}}.

Since
\mathcal{D}_{x}
is a Dirac structure, this implies that
(f_{2},e_{2})\in\mathcal{D}_{x}
. Now
e_{1}=0
gives
e=e_{2}
, whence
(f_{2},e)\in\mathcal{D}_{x}
.
By
f_{1}\in\im E(x)^{\top}
there exists
\mu\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}
such that
f_{1}=E(x)^{\top}\mu
, and thus

(f,e)=(f_{2}+f_{1},e)=(f_{2}+E(x)^{\top}\mu,e)\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{x}.

Step 2: We show that there exists
a neighborhood
U_{x}\subset U
of
x
and a family
(T_{y})_{y\in U_{x}}
of linear and bijective mappings
T_{y}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to\mathcal{D}_{y}
, such that, for all
z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, the mapping

y\mapsto T_{y}z

is continuous from
U_{x}
to
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

The definition of a modulated Dirac structure yields that there exists a neighborhood
U_{1,x}\subset U
of
x
, and continuous
K,L:U_{1,x}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
, such that

\forall\,y\in U_{1,x}:\quad\mathcal{D}_{y}=\im\left[\begin{smallmatrix}K(y)\\
L(y)\end{smallmatrix}\right].

Then

\forall\,y\in U_{1,x}:\quad\mathcal{D}_{y}\cap\big({\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times\ker E(y)\big)=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}K(y)\\
L(y)\end{smallmatrix}\right]\ker E(y)L(y).

(15)

Since
\dim\mathcal{D}_{y}=n
, the matrix
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}K(y)\\
L(y)\end{smallmatrix}\right]
has full column rank.
Hence, a combination of (
15
) with the rank-nullity theorem yields that

\displaystyle\rank E(y)L(y)

\displaystyle=n-\dim\ker E(y)L(y)

\displaystyle=n-\dim\left(\left[\begin{smallmatrix}K(y)\\
L(y)\end{smallmatrix}\right]\ker E(y)L(y)\right)=n-\dim\Big(\mathcal{D}_{y}\cap\big({\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times\ker E(y)\big)\Big),

which is independent of
y
by the assumption that the last term is constant. Consequently,
E(y)L(y)
has constant rank, which we denote by
r_{1}
. Then, by Lemma
8.6
, there exists a neighborhood
U_{2,x}\subset U_{1,x}
of
x
and some continuous
J_{1}:U_{2,x}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times(n-r_{1})}
, such that
\im J_{1}(y)=\ker E(y)L(y)
for all
y\in U_{2,x}
. The definition of
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{y}
yields that

\forall\,y\in U_{2,x}:\quad\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{y}=\im\underbrace{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}K(y)J_{1}(y)&E(y)^{\top}\\
L(y)J_{1}(y)&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]}_{=:D(y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n\times(r_{1}+\ell)}},

which can be seen as follows: If
(f,e)\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{y}
, then
e\in\ker E(y)
and
(f+E(y)^{\top}\mu,e)\in\mathcal{D}_{y}
for some
\mu\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}
, that is

\begin{pmatrix}f+E(y)^{\top}\mu\\

L(y)\end{smallmatrix}\right]\ker E(y)L(y)\quad\implies\quad\begin{pmatrix}f\\

L(y)J_{1}(y)&0\end{smallmatrix}\right].

On the other hand, let
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}f\\
e\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\im\left[\begin{smallmatrix}K(y)J_{1}(y)&E(y)^{\top}\\
L(y)J_{1}(y)&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]
, then

\begin{pmatrix}f\\
e\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{f}\\
\tilde{e}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}E(y)^{\top}\\
0\end{bmatrix}\mu,\quad\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{f}\\
\tilde{e}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathcal{D}_{y}\cap\big({\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times\ker E(y)\big),\ \mu\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell},

from which it follows that

and
(f-E(y)^{\top}\mu,e)\in\mathcal{D}_{y}
, thus
(f,e)\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{y}
.

Since
\dim\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{y}=n
, we have that
\rank D(y)=n
for all
y\in U_{2,x}
. Consequently, again using Lemma
8.6
, there exists a neighborhood
U_{3,x}\subset U_{2,x}
of
x
, and some continuous
J_{2}:U_{3,x}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{(r_{1}+\ell)\times(r_{1}+\ell-n)}
, such that
\im J_{2}(y)=\ker D(y)
for all
y\in U_{3,x}
. Consequently,
J_{2}
has pointwise full column rank, and another use of Lemma
8.6
yields that there exists a neighborhood
U_{4,x}\subset U_{3,x}
of
x
, and some continuous
J_{3}:U_{4,x}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{(r_{1}+\ell)\times n}
, such that
\im J_{3}(y)=\ker J_{2}(y)^{\top}
for all
y\in U_{3,x}
. Using that
\im J_{2}(y)
is the orthogonal complement of
\im J_{3}(y)=\ker J_{2}(y)^{\top}
, we have that
[J_{2}(y),J_{3}(y)]
is an invertible matrix for all

. Hence, for all

,

\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{y}=\im D(y)=\im D(y)[J_{2}(y),J_{3}(y)]=\im D(y)J_{3}(y).

Since
D(y)J_{3}(y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n\times n}
has full column rank, the desired result holds for
U_{x}=U_{4,x}
and
T_{y}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to\mathcal{D}_{y}
with

.

Remark 8.10
.

In Proposition
8.8
, the assumption that the rank of the space
\mathcal{D}_{x}\cap\big({\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times\ker E(x)\big)
is constant is essential for

to be a modulated Dirac structure.
As a counterexample, consider the (constant) Dirac structure

\mathcal{D}=\im\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\\
0&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right],

and the matrix-valued function

. Then the subspaces
(\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{x})_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}}
, as constructed in Proposition
8.8
, satisfy

and

\forall\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{0\}:\quad\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{x}={\mathbb{R}}^{2}\times\{0\}.

In particular, for any neighborhood
U_{0}\subset{\mathbb{R}}
of zero and any family
(T_{y})_{y\in U_{0}}
of linear bijective mappings

, we find that for

with

do not hold in this case, since

It was mentioned in Section
2
that the graphs of gradient fields are Lagrangian submanifolds.
In the following, we present a generalization of this result, where a gradient field is considered alongside a certain type of restriction. This generalization is used in this article to appropriately formulate position constraints.
The following result can essentially be deduced from the findings in
(
Maschke and van der Schaft, 2020
, Sec. 4.2)
on so-called
Morse families
; see also
Barbero-Liñán et al. (2019)
;
Castrillón López and Ratiu (2016)
.
However, in these references, the result is embedded within rather abstract differential geometric concepts.
For this reason, we have chosen to present an elementary proof here.

Proposition 8.11
.

Let
U\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be open, and let
\mathcal{H}:U\to{\mathbb{R}}
and
d:U\to{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
,
k,n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, be twice continuously differentiable. Further assume that
d^{\prime}(x)
has full row rank for all
x\in U
, and the set
\left\{\,x\in U\,\left|\vphantom{x\in U}\,d(x)=0\,\right.\right\}
is non-empty. Then

\mathcal{L}\coloneqq\left\{\,(x,\nabla\mathcal{H}(x)+d^{\prime}(x)^{\top}\lambda)\,\left|\vphantom{(x,\nabla\mathcal{H}(x)+d^{\prime}(x)^{\top}\lambda)}\,x\in U\text{ with }d(x)=0,\;\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k}\,\right.\right\}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

is a Lagrangian submanifold of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

Proof 8.12
.

It can be seen that
\mathcal{L}
is a differentiable submanifold of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. In the sequel we show that it is Lagrangian.

Step 1
: We calculate
T_{z}\mathcal{L}
for some fixed
z\in\mathcal{L}
. First observe that by the assumption that
d^{\prime}
has full row rank everywhere it is a submersion, cf.
(
Lee, 2012
, Ch. 4)
. Then the submersion theorem (see e.g.
(
Lee, 2012
, Cor. 5.13)
) implies that the non-empty set
M:=\left\{\,x\in U\,\left|\vphantom{x\in U}\,d(x)=0\,\right.\right\}
is a submanifold with dimension
\dim M=n-k
. Partitioning
z=(z_{1},z_{2})
, then
z_{1}\in M
and there exists a neighborhood
V\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n-k}
of zero and a chart
g_{1}:V\to U
of
M
at
z_{1}
, i.e.,
g_{1}(0)=z_{1}
and
\ker g_{1}^{\prime}(0)=\{0\}
as well as
\im g_{1}^{\prime}(0)=T_{z_{1}}M
. Furthermore,
since

for all

, by differentiation we obtain that

d^{\prime}(g_{1}(v))g_{1}^{\prime}(v)=0.

Since
\dim\ker d^{\prime}(x)=n-k
for all
x\in U
by assumption,
g_{1}(0)=z_{1}
and
\ker g_{1}^{\prime}(0)=\{0\}
we find that

\ker d^{\prime}(z_{1})=\im g_{1}^{\prime}(0)=T_{z_{1}}M.

(16)

Set

and define

g:W\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n},\ (w_{1},w_{2})\mapsto\big(g_{1}(w_{1}),\nabla\mathcal{H}(g_{1}(w_{1}))-d^{\prime}(g_{1}(w_{1}))^{\top}(\hat{z}+w_{2})\big),

Then

is a chart of
\mathcal{L}
at
z
, hence

T_{z}\mathcal{L}=\im g^{\prime}(0).

We have that

g^{\prime}(w_{1},w_{2})=\begin{bmatrix}g_{1}^{\prime}(w_{1})&0\\
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{H}(g_{1}(w_{1}))g_{1}^{\prime}(w_{1})-D(g_{1}(w_{1}))(\hat{z}+w_{2})g_{1}^{\prime}(w_{1})&-d^{\prime}(g_{1}(w_{1}))^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n\times n},

where
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{H}
is the Hessian of
\mathcal{H}
and
D=((d^{\prime})^{\top})^{\prime}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to L({\mathbb{R}}^{k},{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n})
, where the latter denotes the space of linear mappings from
{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
to
{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
. Then we find that

T_{(z_{1},z_{2})}\mathcal{L}=\left\{\,(v_{1},v_{2})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\,\left|\vphantom{(v_{1},v_{2})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\,\begin{array}[]{l}v_{1}\in\im g_{1}^{\prime}(0),\\
v_{2}-\Big(\nabla^{2}\mathcal{H}(z_{1})-D(z_{1})\hat{z}\Big)v_{1}\in\im d^{\prime}(z_{1})^{\top}\end{array}\!\!\,\right.\right\}.

By the symmetry of second derivatives we have that

for any

, and hence

is symmetric. Furthermore, the Hessian

\displaystyle=v_{1}^{\top}M(z_{1},z_{2})w_{1}+v_{1}^{\top}y-w_{1}^{\top}M(z_{1},z_{2})v_{1}-w_{1}^{\top}x

\displaystyle=v_{1}^{\top}M(z_{1},z_{2})w_{1}-w_{1}^{\top}M(z_{1},z_{2})v_{1}=0,

where the last equality follows from symmetry of

\Leftarrow

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=v_{1}^{\top}w_{2}-w_{1}^{\top}v_{2}

\displaystyle=v_{1}^{\top}M(z_{1},z_{2})w_{1}+v_{1}^{\top}y-w_{1}^{\top}v_{2}

\displaystyle=\big(M(z_{1},z_{2})v_{1}-v_{2}\big)^{\top}w_{1}+v_{1}^{\top}y.

Since
w_{1}
and
y
are arbitrary we obtain that
M(z_{1},z_{2})v_{1}-v_{2}\in\left(\ker d^{\prime}(z_{1})\right)^{\perp}
and
v_{1}\in\ker d^{\prime}(z_{1})
, thus
(v_{1},v_{2})\in T_{z}\mathcal{L}
. This completes the proof.
