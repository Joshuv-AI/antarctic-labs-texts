Spectral Graph Clustering under Differential Privacy: Balancing Privacy, Accuracy, and Efficiency
Mohamed Seif ∗, Antti Koskela ∗, H. Vincent Poor, Andrea J. Goldsmith

Abstract
We study the problem of spectral graph clustering under edge differential privacy (DP). Specifically, we develop three mechanisms: (i) graph perturbation via randomized edge flipping combined with adjacency matrix shuffling, which enforces edge privacy while preserving key spectral properties of the graph. Importantly, shuffling considerably amplifies the guarantees: whereas flipping edges with a fixed probability alone provides only a constant ε \varepsilon edge DP guarantee as the number of nodes grows, the shuffled mechanism achieves ( ε , δ ) (\varepsilon,\delta) edge DP with parameters that tend to zero as the number of nodes increases; (ii) private graph projection with additive Gaussian noise in a lower-dimensional space to reduce dimensionality and computational complexity; and (iii) noisy power method that distributes Gaussian noise across iterations to ensure edge DP while maintaining convergence. Our analysis provides rigorous privacy guarantees and a precise characterization of the misclassification error rate. Experiments on synthetic and real-world networks validate our theoretical analysis and illustrate the practical privacy–utility trade-offs.

Introduction

Network data, such as the connections found in social networks, often contain sensitive information. Therefore, protecting individual privacy during data analysis is essential. Differential Privacy (DP)
dwork2014algorithmic
has become the standard method for providing strong privacy guarantees. DP ensures that the inclusion or exclusion of any single user’s data in a dataset has only a minimal effect on the results of statistical queries.

In analyzing network and graph data, two primary privacy notions have been introduced
karwa2011private
;
karwa2014private
: edge DP, which protects individual relationships (edges) by ensuring that the inclusion or removal of any single edge has limited influence on the output, and node DP, which protects an entire node and all of its incident edges. For clustering and community detection, edge DP is generally more appropriate since relationships are central to defining node labels
chen2023private
;
seif2025differentially
;
suppakitpaisarn2025locally
.

Beyond clustering (which is the focus of this paper), DP has become the dominant framework for private graph analysis, offering rigorous guarantees without assumptions on the adversary. DP algorithms have been developed for a broad range of tasks, including subgraph counting
khayatian2025differentially
;
roy2025robust
(stars, triangles, cuts, and dense subgraphs), community detection, personalized PageRank
wang2013differential
;
epasto2022differentially
, and synthetic graph generation
nguyen2016detecting
;
qin2017generating
;
imola2021locally
;
blocki:itcs13
;
hehir2021consistency
;
cohen2022scalable
;
mohamed2022differentially
.

Related Work.
Spectral clustering
ng2001spectral
;
von2007tutorial
is known to be a computationally efficient algorithm for finding clustering in graph datasets. This algorithm has long been studied as a reliable method for community detection, with consistency guarantees established under various random graph models. Foundational results show that the leading eigenvectors of the adjacency or Laplacian matrices recover latent communities when the graph is sufficiently well-separated
mcsherry2001spectral
;
lei2015consistency
;
abbe2017community
. Extensions have further analyzed sparse and degree-heterogeneous settings
qin2013regularized
;
le2017concentration
;
abbe2020entrywise
. However, despite this rich line of work, there remains no clear understanding of spectral clustering when subject to privacy constraints.

Recently, several efforts have investigated spectral clustering under privacy. Hehir
et al.

hehir2021consistency
analyzed graph perturbation using randomized response and showed that accurate recovery is guaranteed only when the average degree scales as
\Theta(\sqrt{n})
, a condition much stronger than those typically required in sparse or heterogeneous graphs. Suppakitpaisarn
et al.

suppakitpaisarn2025locally
considered the local model, where users randomize their adjacency lists before aggregation, but their analysis is restricted to this particular mechanism and the special case of two communities.

In contrast, our work develops a unified framework of spectral mechanisms under edge-DP, deriving explicit misclassification error bounds and examining the scaling laws of the proposed private mechanisms. Unlike subspace clustering methods
ng2001spectral
;
wang2015differentially
, which partition high-dimensional data points lying near multiple low-dimensional subspaces, our focus is on graph clustering, where the structure is defined by pairwise relationships among nodes. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first systematic study of the fundamental trade-offs among privacy, accuracy, and efficiency in spectral clustering without restrictive assumptions on the underlying graph model.

Our Contributions.
We make the following key contributions to privacy-preserving spectral clustering under edge DP:

1.

Matrix Shuffling Mechanism
: We employ randomized response to perturb the adjacency matrix before applying spectral clustering. In addition, we incorporate
matrix shuffling
, which preserves spectral properties and thus does not affect the clustering guarantees. The resulting mechanism achieves
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-edge differential privacy for any
\varepsilon>0
, and we provide error bounds that characterize how perturbation and shuffling jointly impact recovery accuracy.

2.

Projected Gaussian Mechanism
: Building on ideas from
kenthapadi2012privacy
, we design a projection-based noise addition scheme that perturbs the graph representation in a lower-dimensional subspace. This mechanism satisfies
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-edge DP and requires less noise than direct perturbation. We analyze how the projection dimension controls the trade-off between noise level and clustering accuracy.

3.

Noisy Power Method Mechanism
: We adapt the classical noisy power method by injecting Gaussian noise at each matrix–vector multiplication. This mechanism satisfies
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-edge DP under iterative compositions. We derive bounds on the eigenspace perturbation error and show how the number of iterations and noise variance govern the clustering accuracy.

Problem Statement & Preliminaries

We consider an undirected graph
G=(V,E)
consisting of
n
vertices,
where
V=\{1,\dots,n\}
is the vertex set and
E\subseteq\{\{i,j\}:i,j\in V,\,i\neq j\}

is the edge set.
The vertices are partitioned into
k
disjoint clusters

C_{1},\dots,C_{k}
such that
V=\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{k}C_{\ell}
.
The adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
is defined by

A_{ij}=\mathbf{1}\{\{i,j\}\in E\},\quad A_{ij}=A_{ji},\quad A_{ii}=0.

The eigenvalues of the adjacency
\mathbf{A}
are given as
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A})\geq\lambda_{2}(\mathbf{A})\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}(\mathbf{A})\geq 0
.

We now introduce the metric that quantifies the accuracy of the recovered partitions.

Definition 1

(
(\beta,\eta)
-Accurate Recovery)
.

Let
\bm{\sigma}^{*}=\{\sigma^{*}_{1},\sigma^{*}_{2},\ldots,\sigma^{*}_{n}\}
denote the ground-truth cluster assignment of
n
nodes into
k
(unknown) clusters, where
\sigma^{*}_{i}\in[k]
.
A recovery algorithm outputs an estimated assignment
\hat{\bm{\sigma}}(G)=\{\hat{\sigma}_{1},\hat{\sigma}_{2},\ldots,\hat{\sigma}_{n}\}
with
\hat{\sigma}_{i}\in[k]
.

We say that a clustering algorithm
\hat{\bm{\sigma}}
achieves
(\beta,\eta)
-accurate recovery
(up to a global permutation of clusters labels) if

\displaystyle\Pr\Bigl(\operatorname{err}\operatorname{rate}\bigl(\hat{\bm{\sigma}}(G),\bm{\sigma}^{*}\bigr)\leq\beta\Bigr)\;\geq\;1-\eta,

(1)

where the probability is taken over the randomness of the clustering algorithm.

Here, the error rate is defined by the normalized Hamming distance with optimal label assignment:

\displaystyle\operatorname{err}\operatorname{rate}\bigl(\hat{\bm{\sigma}}(G),\bm{\sigma}^{*}\bigr):=\frac{1}{n}\cdot\min_{\pi\in S_{k}}\operatorname{Ham}\bigl(\pi(\hat{\bm{\sigma}}(G)),\,\bm{\sigma}^{*}\bigr),

where
S_{k}
is the set of all permutations of
[k]
, and
\pi(\hat{\bm{\sigma}}(G))
denotes relabeling the estimated clusters according to
\pi
.

Differential Privacy on Graphs.
We focus on the notion of
edge
privacy
blocki2013differentially
, where two graphs
G
and
G^{\prime}
are said to be neighbors, denoted
G\sim G^{\prime}
, if they share the same vertex set but differ in exactly one edge. The formal definition is given next.

Definition 2

(
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-edge DP)
.

A (randomized) clustering algorithm
\hat{\bm{\sigma}}
as a function of a graph
G
satisfies
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-edge DP for some
\varepsilon\in\mathds{R}^{+}
and
\delta\in[0,1]
, if for all pairs of adjacency matrices
G
and
G^{\prime}
that differ in
one
edge, and any measurable subset
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\operatorname{Range}(\hat{\bm{\sigma}})
, we have

\displaystyle{\operatorname{Pr}(\hat{\bm{\sigma}}(G)\in\mathcal{S}})\leq e^{\varepsilon}{\operatorname{Pr}(\hat{\bm{\sigma}}(G^{\prime})\in\mathcal{S}})+\delta.

The setting when
\delta=0
is referred as
\varepsilon
-edge DP.

Note that DP guarantees can alternatively be described using the hockey-stick divergence. For
\alpha>0
, the hockey-stick divergence
H_{\alpha}
from a distribution
P
to a distribution
Q
is defined as

H_{\alpha}(P||Q)=\int\big[P(t)-\alpha\cdot Q(t)\big]_{+}\,dt.

where for
t\in\mathbb{R}
,
[t]_{+}=\max\{0,t\}
.
Tight
({\varepsilon},\delta)
-bounds using the hockey-stick divergence can be characterized as follows (see Lemma 5 in
zhu2022optimal
for more details).

Lemma 2.1
.

For a given
{\varepsilon}\geq 0
,
tight
\delta({\varepsilon})
is given by the expression

\delta({\varepsilon})=\max_{G\sim G^{\prime}}H_{e^{\varepsilon}}\big(\hat{\bm{\sigma}}(G^{\prime})||\hat{\bm{\sigma}}(G)\big)
, where
G\sim G^{\prime}
denotes that
G
and
G^{\prime}
differ in one edge.

By Lemma
2.1
, for obtaining the edge-DP guarantees, it is sufficient to bound the hockey-stick divergence for all neighboring graphs.

3
Main Results & Discussions

In this section, we introduce three spectral clustering mechanisms that operate under edge DP. The proposed mechanisms achieve privacy amplification and provide stronger utility guarantees compared to direct perturbation methods.
In particular, we consider three design approaches:
(i) matrix perturbation and shuffling of the adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}
, which also applies randomized edge subsampling to further reduce sensitivity and enhance privacy guarantees (more details in Appendix
A.6
);
(ii) matrix projection, which employs dimensionality reduction through random projections of the adjacency
\mathbf{A}
to preserve spectral properties while reducing noise; and
(iii) noisy power method, which integrate carefully calibrated noise into noisy power iterations with
\mathbf{A}
, ensuring privacy at each step while maintaining convergence to informative eigenvectors.
Using directly the adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}
is natural for the chosen methods since they are designed to approximate leading eigenvectors or singular vectors, and choosing
\mathbf{A}
results in clean sensitivity bounds.

3.1
Approach
1
: Matrix Shuffling

First, we would like to make an observation that the graph structure is invariant to permutations, so instead of releasing the adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}
, releasing
\mathbf{P}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}^{\top}
with any permutation matrix
\mathbf{P}
will give the same information about the graph structure. With this insight, we consider releasing
\mathbf{P}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}^{\top}
with a random permutation and show how to use the so-called shuffling amplification results
feldman2022hiding
to amplify the DP guarantees induced by the graph perturbation mechanism which is defined next.

Definition 3

(Graph Perturbation Mechanism)
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be the adjacency matrix of the graph.
Under Warner’s randomized response (RR) mechanism
warner1965randomized
with perturbation parameter

\mu=1/(e^{\varepsilon_{0}}+1)
, we generate a privatized adjacency matrix

\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{E},

(2)

where
\mathbf{E}
is a symmetric random perturbation matrix.
For each off-diagonal entry
(i,j)
with
i<j
, the perturbation is defined as

E_{ij}=\begin{cases}0,&\text{with probability }1-\mu,\\
1-2A_{ij},&\text{with probability }\mu,\end{cases}

(3)

and we enforce
E_{ji}=E_{ij}
to ensure symmetry, while setting
\widetilde{A}_{ii}=0
.
By construction, the entries satisfy

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[E_{ij}]=\mu\,\bigl(1-2A_{ij}\bigr),\quad\operatorname{Var}(E_{ij})=\mu(1-\mu)\,\bigl(1-2A_{ij}\bigr)^{2}.

Privacy Amplification via Matrix Shuffling.
The setting in the existing results is slightly different from ours as they consider a random permutation of
n
local DP mechanisms, however we are able to get a amplification result for the random permutation of a randomized response perturbed adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}
. Moreover, in the shuffling amplification results of
feldman2022hiding
, one considers
n
binary data points
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
and applying randomized response to each of them (with
{\varepsilon}_{0}
-DP) and then shuffling randomly. The random permutation of these local DP results is
({\varepsilon},\delta)
-DP where

{\varepsilon}=O(n^{-1/2}\sqrt{e^{{\varepsilon}_{0}}\log 1/\delta})

and there are explicit upper bounds for
{\varepsilon}
that can be used for amplifying the existing randomized response results.
In particular, the analysis is based on decomposing individual local DP contributions to mixtures of data dependent part and noise, which leads to finding
({\varepsilon},\delta)
-bound for certain 2-dimensional discrete-valued distributions. We next present our privacy amplification result in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{A}^{\prime}
be two symmetric adjacency matrices differing in the
(i,j)
th
element.
Define the mechanism
\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{A})
as:

\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{A})=\mathbf{P}\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{P}^{\top},

where
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}
is the adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}
perturbed using
{\varepsilon}_{0}
-DP randomized response (i.e., strictly upper triangular elements of
\mathbf{A}
are perturbed with
{\varepsilon}_{0}
-DP randomized response and mirrored to the lower triangular part) and
\mathbf{P}
is a permutation matrix corresponding to a randomly drawn permutation
\pi
of
[n]
, i.e.,

\mathbf{P}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{e}_{\pi(1)}&\ldots&\mathbf{e}_{\pi(n)}\end{bmatrix}^{\top},

where
\mathbf{e}_{j}
,
j\in[n]
, denotes the
j
th
unit vector.
Then, for any
\alpha\geq 0
, we have:

\displaystyle H_{\alpha}\left(\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{A}),\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{A}^{\prime})\right)\leq H_{\alpha}\left(P_{0}\left({\varepsilon}_{0}\right),P_{1}\left({\varepsilon}_{0}\right)\right),

(4)

where
P_{0}({\varepsilon}_{0})
and
P_{1}({\varepsilon}_{0})
are given as follows:

\displaystyle P_{0}({\varepsilon}_{0})=(A+\Delta,C-A+1-\Delta),

\displaystyle P_{1}({\varepsilon}_{0})=(A+1-\Delta,C-A+\Delta),

where,

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{Bin}\left(n-2,\frac{2}{{\varepsilon}_{0}+1}\right),\quad A\sim\mathrm{Bin}(C,\tfrac{1}{2}),

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{Bern}\left(\frac{e^{{\varepsilon}_{0}}}{{\varepsilon}_{0}+1}\right),\quad n\in\mathbb{N},\quad p\in[0,1].

(5)

To summarize, Theorem
3.1
says that the privacy profile of the randomly permuted randomized response-perturbed adjacency graph is dominated by the privacy profile determined by the privacy profile

h(\alpha):=H_{\alpha}\left(P_{0}\left({\varepsilon}_{0}\right),P_{1}\left({\varepsilon}_{0}\right)\right).

Setting
\alpha=e^{{\varepsilon}_{0}}
and deriving upper bounds for

gives the following (see Thm. 3.2 of

). We refer the reader to Appendix

for further discussion and details.

Corollary 3.1
.

For any
\delta\in(0,1]
and
{\varepsilon}_{0}\leq\log\left(\frac{n}{8\log(2\delta)}-1\right)
, the mechanism
\mathcal{M}(A)
is
({\varepsilon},\delta)
-DP for

{\varepsilon}\leq\log\left(1+(e^{{\varepsilon}_{0}}-1)\left(\frac{4\sqrt{2\log(4/\delta)}}{\sqrt{(e^{{\varepsilon}_{0}}+1)n}}+\frac{4}{n}\right)\right).

(6)

Corollary

provides a simplified analytical upper bound on the hockey-stick divergence given in Thm.
3.1
, with the constraint
{\varepsilon}_{0}\leq\log(n/(8\log(2/\delta))-1)
arising purely from analytical tractability. However, the bound in Eqn. (

An important implication of Cor.

is that the shuffled mechanism achieves

We next analyze the accuracy of our proposed mechanism. To this end, we first focus on the error induced by the randomized response perturbation and establish a set of intermediate lemmas that will serve as building blocks for proving the final guarantee on the misclassification error rate.

Lemma 3.1

(Matrix Representation for
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}
)
.

Let
\mathbf{J}=\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}
. Under symmetric RR with edge flip probability
\mu\in[0,\tfrac{1}{2})
, the perturbed adjacency matrix can be represented as

\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}\;=\;c\,\mathbf{A}\;+\;\mu(\mathbf{J}-\mathbf{I})\;+\;\mathbf{Z},\quad c:=1-2\mu,

where
\mathbf{Z}:=\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}-\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}\mid\mathbf{A}]
has independent mean-zero entries, bounded by

v_{\max}:=\mu(1-\mu)
.

We next present an upper bound on the spectral norm of the matrix
\mathbf{Z}

Lemma 3.2

(Bounding the Spectral Norm of
\mathbf{Z}
)
.

Let
\mathbf{Z}
be defined as above. Then, for any

\|\mathbf{Z}\|_{2}\;\leq\;\sqrt{\,2(n-1)v_{\max}\,\log\frac{2n}{\eta}\,}\;+\;\frac{1}{3}\,\log\frac{2n}{\eta}.

We next introduce two standard lemmas that will be useful in our analysis.
The first lemma, a variant of the classical Davis–Kahan theorem,
controls the deviation between the true eigenspace and the perturbed eigenspace
in terms of the spectral gap and the perturbation magnitude.

Lemma 3.3

(Davis–Kahan)
.

Let
\mathbf{U}=[\mathbf{u}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{u}_{k}]

\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}
collect the top-

\mathbf{A}

\operatorname{dist}(\mathbf{U},\widetilde{\mathbf{U}})\;\leq\;2\cdot\frac{\|\mathbf{Z}\|_{2}}{\Delta_{k}}.

The second lemma, a Procrustes alignment bound, shows that after an appropriate orthogonal rotation,
the Frobenius error between the two embeddings can be controlled by the eigenspace distance.

Lemma 3.4

(Procrustes alignment)
.

There exists an orthonormal matrix
\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{O}_{k}
such that

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\,\|\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}-\mathbf{U}\mathbf{R}\|_{F}\;\leq\;\sqrt{2k}\,\cdot\operatorname{dist}(\mathbf{U},\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}).

We next introduce a result that bridges the spectral embedding perturbation error with the final clustering performance.
In particular, it connects the misclassification rate of
k
-means clustering with the classification margin,
captured by the separation between cluster centers

, and the within-cluster radius

Lemma 3.5

(Error Rate Analysis via Classification Margin)
.

Let

\displaystyle:=\min_{r\neq s}\|c_{r}-c_{s}\|.

\displaystyle\mathrm{err\,rate}(\hat{\bm{\sigma}},\bm{\sigma}^{\ast})\;\leq\;\frac{\|\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}-\mathbf{U}\mathbf{R}\|^{2}_{F}}{n\cdot\bigl(\tfrac{\Delta_{\star}}{2}-r_{\star}\bigr)^{2}}.

5
Conclusion and Future Work

In this paper, we studied the problem of spectral clustering for graphs under the notion of edge DP. We analyzed three different classes of privacy-preserving mechanisms, namely, graph perturbation, projected Gaussian mechanism, and edge DP noisy power method. We showed fundamental trade-offs between privacy, accuracy, and efficiency (in terms of computational and space complexity), and established theoretical guarantees on clustering performance. We also presented simulation results on both synthetic and real-world graphs to validate our theoretical findings. An important direction for future work is to extend these results to
attributed graphs
, where node labels are multidimensional and introduce additional sensitivity challenges.

A.1
Brief Refresher on Spectral Clustering for Graphs

Spectral clustering partitions the vertices of a graph
G
into communities by leveraging the spectral properties of its adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}
.
This is achieved by performing an eigen decomposition of
\mathbf{A}
, obtaining its eigenvalues

\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}
and the corresponding eigenvectors
\mathbf{u}_{1},\mathbf{u}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{n}
.
For the case of two communities, clustering is based on the leading eigenvector
\mathbf{u}_{1}
, which captures the dominant block structure of the graph.
More generally, for
k
communities, the top
k
eigenvectors of
\mathbf{A}
are assembled into an
n\times k
embedding, and applying a clustering algorithm such as
k
-means to the rows of this embedding recovers the community structure under standard separation conditions
von2007tutorial
.

A.2
Privacy Threat Model

In the context of graph clustering, our threat model is based on edge DP in a centralized setting, where a curator has access to the entire graph, including all nodes and their connections. The primary sensitivity lies in the relationships between nodes rather than in the nodes themselves. This sensitivity arises because exposing how individuals or entities are connected can reveal undisclosed or sensitive information. We assume an adversary may already possess partial knowledge of the graph’s structure and attempt to infer additional undisclosed relationships. Our approach therefore ensures that clustering can be performed without compromising the privacy of individual relationships, while still enabling meaningful analysis of the overall graph structure.

Proof of Lemma
3.1

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
be the adjacency matrix of an undirected graph
G
. Let
\mathbf{J}=\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}
and define
c:=1-2\mu
, where
\mu\in[0,\tfrac{1}{2})
is the flipping probability of the randomized response (RR) mechanism.

Under symmetric RR, for each off-diagonal pair
i<j
,

\widetilde{A}_{ij}\;=\;\begin{cases}A_{ij},&\text{w.p. }1-\mu,\\
1-A_{ij},&\text{w.p. }\mu,\end{cases}\qquad\text{and set}\quad\widetilde{A}_{ji}=\widetilde{A}_{ij},\;\;\widetilde{A}_{ii}=0.

Thus, conditionally on
\mathbf{A}
,

\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{A}_{ij}\mid\mathbf{A}]=(1-\mu)A_{ij}+\mu(1-A_{ij})=c\,A_{ij}+\mu,\quad\text{for }i\neq j,\qquad\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{A}_{ii}\mid\mathbf{A}]=0.

Matrix-wise, this gives

\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}\mid\mathbf{A}]=c\,\mathbf{A}+\mu\,(\mathbf{J}-\mathbf{I}),

since
\mathbf{J}-\mathbf{I}
has ones off-diagonal and zeros on the diagonal.

Define the zero-mean (conditional) noise matrix

\mathbf{Z}\;:=\;\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}-\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}\mid\mathbf{A}],\qquad\text{so that}\qquad\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Z}\mid\mathbf{A}]=\mathbf{0}.

Hence the exact decomposition

\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}=c\,\mathbf{A}+\mu(\mathbf{J}-\mathbf{I})+\mathbf{Z}.

Let

\mathbf{S}\;:=\;c\,\mathbf{A}+\mu(\mathbf{J}-\mathbf{I}).

Since
\mathbf{J}
has eigenvalue
n
on
\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{1}\}
and
0
on
\mathbf{1}^{\perp}
, we have for any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{1}^{\perp}
,

\mathbf{S}\mathbf{x}\;=\;c\,\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mu\,\mathbf{x}.

Thus, on
\mathbf{1}^{\perp}
,
\mathbf{S}=c\,\mathbf{A}-\mu\,\mathbf{I}
, a shifted–scaled copy of
\mathbf{A}
. Hence
\mathbf{S}
and
\mathbf{A}
share eigenvectors on
\mathbf{1}^{\perp}
. Only the fluctuation
\mathbf{Z}
can rotate the informative eigenspace. This proves the lemma.

. To address this, we first subsample edges with probability
q_{s}
, which preserves sparsity, reduces complexity to
O(q_{s}n^{2})
, and amplifies privacy. We then randomly permute the adjacency matrix to obfuscate node identities. This subsample–perturb–shuffle mechanism balances accuracy, efficiency, and privacy, leading to the following corollary.

Corollary A.1

(Spectral
k
-means under Subsample–Perturb–Shuffle)
.

Work under the setting of Theorem
3.2
.
Construct
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}
by:

(i) subsampling each off-diagonal
(i,j)
independently with probability
q_{s}
,
(ii) applying randomized response with symmetric flip probability
\mu\in[0,\tfrac{1}{2})
to each retained entry, and (iii) permuting rows and columns by an independent random permutation matrix
\mathbf{P}
. Let
\hat{\bm{\sigma}}
be the (global optimum)
k
-means clustering of the top-
k
eigenvectors of
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}
. Then, with probability at least
1-\eta
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{err\,rate}(\hat{\bm{\sigma}},\bm{\sigma}^{\ast})\;\leq\;\frac{\Bigl[\displaystyle\frac{2\sqrt{k}}{q_{s}c\,\Delta_{k}}\,\sqrt{\,2(n-1)\,v_{\max}^{(q_{s})}\,\log\frac{4n}{\eta}\,}\;+\;\frac{\sqrt{2k}}{3\,q_{s}c\,\Delta_{k}}\,\log\frac{4n}{\eta}\Bigr]^{2}}{\bigl(\tfrac{\Delta_{\star}}{2}-r_{\star}\bigr)^{2}},

where
\Delta_{k}=\gamma_{k}(\mathbf{A})-\gamma_{k+1}(\mathbf{A})
,
c=1-2\mu
, and

v_{\max}^{(q_{s})}\;:=\;\max\Bigl\{\,q_{s}\,\mu\bigl(1-q_{s}\mu\bigr),\;\;q_{s}(1-\mu)\bigl(1-q_{s}(1-\mu)\bigr)\Bigr\}.

Proof sketch.

Let
Y_{ij}=\mathrm{RR}_{\mu}(A_{ij})
and
X_{ij}=B_{ij}Y_{ij}
, with
B_{ij}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(q_{s})
. Then
\mathbb{E}[X_{ij}\mid A_{ij}=a]=q_{s}\bigl((1-2\mu)a+\mu\bigr)
and
\mathrm{Var}(X_{ij}\mid A_{ij}=a)=q_{s}p_{a}\bigl(1-q_{s}p_{a}\bigr)
with
p_{a}=(1-2\mu)a+\mu
. Thus

\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}\mid\mathbf{A}]\;=\;q_{s}\Bigl(c\,\mathbf{A}+\mu(\mathbf{11}^{\top}-\mathbf{I})\Bigr).

On
\mathbf{1}^{\perp}
, the structured part equals
q_{s}c\,\mathbf{A}-q_{s}\mu\,\mathbf{I}
, so it shares eigenvectors with
\mathbf{A}
there, and the informative eigengap scales as
\Delta_{k}\bigl(\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}]\bigr)=q_{s}c\,\Delta_{k}(\mathbf{A})
.
For the fluctuation
\mathbf{Z}:=\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}-\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}]
, matrix Bernstein bounds its spectral norm by the stated term with probability
\geq 1-\eta
. Davis–Kahan with gap
q_{s}c\,\Delta_{k}
controls the subspace error, and the same
k
-means misclustering lemma as in Theorem
3.2
gives the bound. The random permutation
\mathbf{P}
leaves eigenvalues invariant and only relabels nodes, hence it does not affect the spectral error but amplifies privacy.
∎

A.7
Exact Recovery Condition under Randomized Response Perturbation

We provide a sufficient condition under which spectral
k
-means achieves exact recovery (up to permutation) when the
adjacency
matrix is perturbed by the RR mechanism.

Let
\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
denote the clean spectral embedding formed by the top-
k
eigenvectors of the (unperturbed) adjacency
\mathbf{A}
, where the
i
th row corresponds to the embedding vector
\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\top}
with
\mathbf{u}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
.
Similarly, let
\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
be the perturbed embedding from
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}
, with rows
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i}^{\top}
and embedding vectors
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
.

To compare
\mathbf{U}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}
, we align them via an orthogonal matrix

\mathbf{R}\;=\;\arg\min_{\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{O}_{k}}\|\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}-\mathbf{U}\mathbf{Q}\|_{F}.

Define the worst-case per-node perturbation

e_{\infty}\;:=\;\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\,\bigl\|\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i}-\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{u}_{i}\bigr\|.

Voronoi argument.

Suppose nodes are partitioned into
k
clusters with population centers

\mathbf{c}_{r}=|C_{r}|^{-1}\sum_{i\in C_{r}}\mathbf{u}_{i}
,
minimum separation

\Delta_{\star}:=\min_{r\neq s}\|\mathbf{c}_{r}-\mathbf{c}_{s}\|
,
and within-cluster radius

r_{\star}:=\max_{r}|C_{r}|^{-1/2}\!\left(\sum_{i\in C_{r}}\|\mathbf{u}_{i}-\mathbf{c}_{r}\|^{2}\right)^{1/2}
.
If

e_{\infty}+r_{\star}\;<\;\tfrac{\Delta_{\star}}{2},

then every perturbed point
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i}
remains closer to its true center
\mathbf{c}_{r}

than to any other
\mathbf{c}_{s}
. Hence
k
-means recovers the ground-truth partition exactly
(up to label permutation).

By Procrustes alignment and Davis–Kahan for adjacency eigenvectors,

e_{\infty}\;\leq\;\frac{\sqrt{2k}}{\Delta_{k}}\cdot\|\mathbf{Z}\|_{2},

where
\Delta_{k}=\gamma_{k}(\mathbf{A})-\gamma_{k+1}(\mathbf{A})
is the adjacency eigengap, and
\mathbf{Z}:=\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}-\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}\mid\mathbf{A}]
is the zero-mean RR noise. Matrix Bernstein yields the high-probability bound

\|\mathbf{Z}\|_{2}\;\leq\;2\sqrt{\,2(n-1)v_{\max}\,\log\tfrac{4n}{\eta}\,}\;+\;\tfrac{2}{3}\,\log\tfrac{4n}{\eta},\qquad v_{\max}=\mu(1-\mu).

Exact recovery condition.

A sufficient condition for exact recovery is therefore

\frac{2\sqrt{k}}{\Delta_{k}}\,\sqrt{\,2(n-1)v_{\max}\,\log\tfrac{4n}{\eta}\,}\;+\;\frac{\sqrt{2k}}{3\,\Delta_{k}}\,\log\tfrac{4n}{\eta}\;\leq\;\frac{\Delta_{\star}}{4}-r_{\star}.

Scaling.

In a balanced
k
-SBM with parameters
(p,q)
, one has

\Delta_{k}\asymp\tfrac{n}{k}(p-q)
,
r_{\star}=0
, and
\Delta_{\star}\asymp\sqrt{k/n}
.
Substituting these values shows that the misclassification rate decays on the order of

\operatorname{err\,rate}\;\lesssim\;\frac{k}{(p-q)^{2}\,n},

so for fixed
k
and constant separation
p-q>0
, the error vanishes at the rate
O(1/n)
.

Corollary A.2

(Two-community case)
.

For
k=2
, the spectral embedding can be taken as one-dimensional (the principal direction).
In this case the cluster centers are antipodal on the real line,
so
r_{\star}=0
and
\Delta_{\star}\asymp n^{-1/2}
.
The eigengap is
\Delta_{1}=\gamma_{1}(\mathbf{A})-\gamma_{2}(\mathbf{A})
.
A sufficient condition for exact recovery under RR is

\frac{2}{\Delta_{1}}\,\sqrt{\,2(n-1)v_{\max}\,\log\tfrac{4n}{\eta}\,}\;+\;\frac{\sqrt{2}}{3\,\Delta_{1}}\,\log\tfrac{4n}{\eta}\;\leq\;\frac{\Delta_{\star}}{4}.

In a balanced two-block SBM with parameters
(p,q)
,
this simplifies to the requirement

(p-q)\;\gtrsim\;\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n}},

and the misclassification rate satisfies
\operatorname{err\,rate}\lesssim 1/n
.

Insights and discussion.

The error bound in Theorem
3.2
has three interacting drivers:

(i) Cluster geometry (margin).

The denominator involves the margin
\bar{m}:=\tfrac{\Delta_{\star}}{2}-r_{\star}
.
Large inter-center separation
\Delta_{\star}
and small within-cluster spread
r_{\star}
make
\bar{m}
larger,
so the same spectral error translates to fewer label mistakes.

(ii) Spectral stability (eigen-gap).

The prefactor
\Delta_{k}^{-1}
shows that the adjacency eigenspace is
Lipschitz
in the perturbation
with modulus inversely proportional to the gap
\Delta_{k}
.
When
\Delta_{k}
is small, the top-
k
eigenspace is fragile and
k
-means inherits that fragility.
In planted models (e.g., SBM),
\Delta_{k}
grows with signal strength (roughly with
n(p-q)
),
so larger graphs and stronger communities stabilize the embedding.

(iii) Privacy noise (RR parameters).

Randomized response (flip probability
\mu
) both attenuates the signal and injects noise.
Two scalars capture this:

c=1-2\mu,\qquad v_{\max}=\mu(1-\mu)=\frac{e^{\varepsilon}}{(e^{\varepsilon}+1)^{2}}.

For small
\varepsilon
,
c\approx\varepsilon/2
and
v_{\max}\approx 1/4
, so the bound scales roughly like
1/\Delta_{k}^{2}
with a larger constant;
for larger
\varepsilon
,
c\uparrow 1
and
v_{\max}\downarrow 0
, recovering near-nonprivate behavior.

Heuristically (dropping logs and constants), the average embedding error scales as

E\;\approx\;\frac{\sqrt{k}}{\Delta_{k}}\,\sqrt{n\,v_{\max}},

and the misclassification rate is about
(E/\bar{m})^{2}
.
In an SBM,
\Delta_{k}=\Theta\!\big(n(p-q)\big)
, hence

E\;=\;\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\Big(\frac{\sqrt{k\,v_{\max}}}{(p-q)\,\sqrt{n}}\Big),\qquad\mathrm{err\,rate}\;\approx\;\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\Big(\frac{k\,v_{\max}}{(p-q)^{2}\,\bar{m}^{2}\,n}\Big),

so accuracy improves with
n
and the signal gap
(p-q)
, and degrades with privacy (via
v_{\max}
), number of clusters
k
, and smaller margin
\bar{m}
.

Proof of Lemma
3.7

We bound the difference between the leading
k
‐dimensional singular subspaces of the “unperturbed” matrix
\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{Q}
and its noisy version
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{Q}+\mathbf{E}
. Write the compact singular‐value decompositions

\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{U}\,\mathbf{\Sigma}\,\mathbf{V}^{\top},\quad\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}=\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\,\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\,\widetilde{\mathbf{V}}^{\top},

where
\mathbf{U},\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}\in\mathds{R}^{n\times k}
contain the top
k
left singular vectors. Let

\sigma_{k}=\sigma_{k}(\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{Q}),\quad\sigma_{k+1}=\sigma_{k+1}(\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{Q}),

and define the singular‐value gap
\Delta_{k}^{(Q)}=\sigma_{k}-\sigma_{k+1}
.

Next, we have the following set of steps. Wedin’s theorem (a refinement of Davis–Kahan for singular subspaces) states that

\bigl\|\sin\Theta(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}},\mathbf{U})\bigr\|_{\operatorname{F}}\;\leq\;\sqrt{2}\cdot\frac{\|\mathbf{E}\|_{2}}{\Delta_{k}^{(Q)}}\;\leq\;2\cdot\frac{\|\mathbf{E}\|_{2}}{\Delta_{k}}\,,

where
\sin\Theta(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}},\mathbf{U})
is the matrix of principal angles between the two subspaces. Equivalently, there exists an orthonormal matrix
\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{O}_{k}
such that

\|\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}-\mathbf{U}\,\mathbf{R}\|_{\operatorname{F}}\;\leq\;\frac{2\sqrt{k}}{\Delta_{k}}\cdot\|\mathbf{E}\|_{2}.

The extra factor
\sqrt{k}
comes from relating the Frobenius norm of the angle matrix to that of the difference of orthonormal bases.

The perturbation matrix
\mathbf{E}\in\mathds{R}^{n\times m}
has i.i.d. entries
E_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\bar{\sigma}^{2})
. By classical random‐matrix concentration (e.g. Theorem 4.4.5 in
vershynin2018high
), with probability at least
1-\eta
,

\|\mathbf{E}\|_{2}\;\leq\;\bar{\sigma}\cdot\bigl(\sqrt{n}+\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{\log(1/\eta)}\bigr).

Substituting into the Wedin bound yields

\|\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}-\mathbf{U}\,\mathbf{R}\|_{F}\;=\;O\Bigl(\frac{\sqrt{k}}{\Delta_{k}}\cdot\bar{\sigma}\cdot(\sqrt{n}+\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{\log(1/\eta)})\Bigr).

Denote the
i
th rows of
\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}
and
\mathbf{U}
by
\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{i}^{\top}
and
\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\top}
, respectively. Then writing

\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}-\mathbf{U}\,\mathbf{R}=\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\top}-\mathbf{u}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{R}\\[4.0pt]
\vdots\\[2.0pt]
\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}_{n}^{\top}-\mathbf{u}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{R}\end{bmatrix}\;=\;\begin{bmatrix}\delta_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\delta_{n}\end{bmatrix},

we have

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\delta_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\;=\;\|\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}-\mathbf{U}\,\mathbf{R}\|^{2}_{\operatorname{F}}.

In particular each row

\|\delta_{i}\|_{2}\leq\|\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}-\mathbf{U}\,\mathbf{R}\|_{\operatorname{F}}
,
so

\|\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}_{i}-\mathbf{u}^{\top}_{i}\mathbf{R}\|_{2}\;\leq\;O\Bigl(\frac{\sqrt{k}}{\gamma}\cdot\sigma\cdot(\sqrt{n}+\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{\log(1/\eta)})\Bigr),

as claimed.

Corollary A.3

(Johnson–Lindenstrauss Dimension for Projected Adjacency Matrix)
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be the adjacency matrix and

\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
be a random Gaussian projection with entries

Q_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1/m)
.
For any
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
and failure probability
\delta\in(0,1)
, if

m\;\geq\;\frac{4\log(n/\delta)}{\varepsilon^{2}/2-\varepsilon^{3}/3},

then with probability at least
1-\delta
the projection preserves all pairwise Euclidean distances
between rows of
\mathbf{A}
up to
(1\pm\varepsilon)
distortion:

(1-\varepsilon)\,\|\mathbf{A}_{i,:}-\mathbf{A}_{j,:}\|_{2}^{2}\;\leq\;\|(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q})_{i,:}-(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Q})_{j,:}\|_{2}^{2}\;\leq\;(1+\varepsilon)\,\|\mathbf{A}_{i,:}-\mathbf{A}_{j,:}\|_{2}^{2}

for all
i,j\in[n]
.
In particular, the embedding dimension can be taken as
m=O(\varepsilon^{-2}\log(n/\delta))
.

Theorem A.1

(Converse for balanced
k
-SBM under edge
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP)
.

Let
G\sim\mathrm{SBM}(\bm{\sigma}^{\star};n,k,p,q)
be a balanced
k
-block model on
n
nodes
(within-community probability
p
, across-community probability
q
, with
p\neq q
).
Let
\mathcal{M}
be an edge
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP mechanism that outputs
\mathbf{Y}=\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{A})
.
Suppose a decoder
\widehat{\bm{\sigma}}(\mathbf{Y})
achieves
(\beta,\eta)
-accuracy:

\Pr\!\Big(\min_{\pi\in S_{k}}\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{\widehat{\sigma}_{i}\neq\pi(\sigma_{i}^{\star})\}\leq\beta\Big)\geq 1-\eta.

(21)

In particular:

•

For pure DP
(\delta=0)
and

\varepsilon\;\geq\;C\,\sqrt{\frac{\log(k-1)}{|p-q|\,n}\cdot\frac{1}{\,1-\tfrac{1}{k}-\beta\,}}\quad\text{(ignoring the $\log(1/\eta)$ term),}

•

For
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP with

Proof.

We proceed with following set of steps:

Packing.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a Varshamov–Gilbert (GV) packing of balanced

-partitions of

with pairwise misclassification distance at least

after optimal relabeling.

for an absolute constant

CoverThomas2006
.

Flip-count combinatorics.

For a labeling

W_{\bm{\sigma}}(i,j)=\mathbf{1}\{\sigma_{i}=\sigma_{j}\}

\Xi_{k}(\bm{\sigma},\bm{\tau})\;:=\;\#\big\{\{i,j\}:W_{\bm{\sigma}}(i,j)\neq W_{\bm{\tau}}(i,j)\big\}.

If the Hamming distance between
\bm{\sigma}

\bm{\tau}

(after the best permutation),

\Xi_{k}(\bm{\sigma},\bm{\tau})\;\geq\;c_{1}\,\beta\Bigl(1-\tfrac{1}{k}-\beta\Bigr)\,n^{2},

for an absolute

Canonical coupling.

Couple

Hence

\mathbb{E}\big[d_{\mathrm{edge}}(G,G^{\prime})\big]\;=\;|p-q|\cdot\Xi_{k}(\bm{\sigma},\bm{\tau}).

(24)

DP
\Rightarrow
KL contraction.

Let
P_{\bm{\sigma}}
denote the law of
\mathcal{M}(G)
when

By joint convexity of KL, we have

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{\bm{\sigma}}\,\|\,P_{\bm{\tau}})\;\leq\;\mathbb{E}\Big[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\mathcal{M}(G)\,\big\|\,\mathcal{M}(G^{\prime})\big)\Big].

(25)

Fix a realization

with

differing edges.
Traverse a random path that flips these

By joint convexity,

is bounded by the average KL between outputs of

and
(\varepsilon,\delta)

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mu_{r-1}\,\|\,\mu_{r})\;\leq\;\kappa(\varepsilon,\delta),

where

is defined in (

).
For

and then

(

(

Summing over

C.1
Permutation-Invariant Error Rate

We first describe the formula for obtaining the error rate that is used in the experiments.
Recall from Section
2
that the error rate is defined as a minimum over all permutations of the labels:

\displaystyle\operatorname{err}\operatorname{rate}\bigl(\hat{\bm{\sigma}}(G),\bm{\sigma}^{*}\bigr):=\frac{1}{n}\cdot\min_{\pi\in S_{k}}\operatorname{Ham}\bigl(\pi(\hat{\bm{\sigma}}(G)),\,\bm{\sigma}^{*}\bigr),

Suppose we have
k
classes and
n
samples. Denote as

\bm{t}=(t_{1},\dots,t_{k}),\quad\sum_{i=1}^{k}t_{i}=n,

the true class counts we get from
\bm{\sigma}^{*}
, and

\bm{p}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{k}),\quad\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}=n,

the predicted class counts we get from
\hat{\bm{\sigma}}(G)
.

Define the sorted vectors

\hat{\bm{t}}=\operatorname{sort}(\bm{t},\downarrow),\qquad\hat{\bm{p}}=\operatorname{sort}(\bm{p},\downarrow),

where
\hat{\bm{t}}=\operatorname{sort}(\bm{t},\downarrow)
is a reordering of
\bm{t}
such that
\hat{t}_{1}\geq\ldots\geq\hat{t}_{k}
and similarly
\hat{\bm{p}}=\operatorname{sort}(\bm{p},\downarrow)
is a reordering of
\bm{p}
such that
\hat{p}_{1}\leq\ldots\leq\hat{p}_{k}
.

Then, clearly, the permutation-invariant error is obtained via the smallest possible sum of class count errors as

\min_{\pi\in S_{k}}\operatorname{Ham}\bigl(\pi(\hat{\bm{\sigma}}(G)),\,\bm{\sigma}^{*}\bigr)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lvert\hat{p}_{i}-\hat{t}_{i}\rvert,

and therefore, the error rate is given by the expression

\displaystyle\operatorname{err}\operatorname{rate}\bigl(\hat{\bm{\sigma}}(G),\bm{\sigma}^{*}\bigr)=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lvert\hat{p}_{i}-\hat{t}_{i}\rvert=1-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\min(\hat{p}_{i},\hat{t}_{i}).
