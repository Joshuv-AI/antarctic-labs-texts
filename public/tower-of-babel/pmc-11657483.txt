Preoperative versus postoperative radiotherapy for localized soft tissue sarcoma treated with curative intent in a French tertiary center “SARCLOC”
Authors: Maxime Noeuveglise, Williams Tessier, Maël Barthoulot, Gauthier Decanter, Antoine Cayeux, Helene Marin, Pauline Lemoine-Gobert, Estelle Aymes, Sophie Taieb, Cindy Fayard, Juliette Beaujot, Yves-Marie Robin, Eric F. Lartigau, Nicolas Penel, Abel Cordoba
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSoft tissue sarcomas of the extremities (E-STS) are rare and heterogeneous. Treatment combines surgery with preoperative or postoperative radiotherapy (RT) for deep, large, or high-grade tumors. We evaluate the effectiveness and toxicity in E-STS patients treated at our institution from 2015 to 2021.Materials and methodsThis retrospective, single-center study, compared clinical and therapeutic characteristics, toxicities, and surgical complications by radiotherapy type. Local recurrence-free survival (LRFS), overall survival (OS), and recurrence-free survival (RFS) were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method.ResultsWe included 158 patients; 124 (78%) received preoperative RT. The two groups (preoperative RT vs. postoperative RT) differed significantly in terms of median tumor size (111 vs. 67.5 mm, p < 0.01), perivascular involvement (51% vs. 29%, p = 0.03), and bone contact (32% vs. 12%, p = 0.02), but not in grade (grade 2 or 3: 65% vs. 74%, p = 0.72). Median follow-up was 2.9 vs. 5.1 years. Two-year LRFS was 82% [95%CI: 74–88] for both groups; OS was 87% [95%CI: 80–92] vs. 88% [95%CI: 71–95]; and RFS was 72% [95%CI: 63–79] vs. 70% [95%CI: 52–83]. Postoperative complications were 53% vs. 35%, with wound disruption at 39% vs. 15% and infections at 29% vs. 6%. Grade 2 or higher dermatitis was 16% vs. 29%, and RT discontinuation rates were 4% vs. 12% for the two groups, respectively.ConclusionDespite poorer prognostic factors in the preoperative RT group, LRFS and OS were comparable to those in the postoperative RT group. Postoperative complications were more frequent with preoperative RT, and treatment discontinuation was more common postoperatively.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-024-13243-0.

Background

Soft tissue sarcoma (STS) represents a heterogeneous group of malignancies, accounting for less than 1% of all cancer diagnoses [1]. While it can occur at any site in the body, 40% of STS cases occur in the limbs [2]. Expert care at specialized centers has been shown to improve outcomes for patients diagnosed with or suspected of having STS [3–6].

Surgery is the primary STS treatment, particularly for localized extremity soft tissue sarcomas (E-STS). Conservative surgery combined with either preoperative or postoperative radiotherapy (RT) is the standard care regimen, showing comparable efficacy to amputation in terms of local control [7, 8]. Advancements in surgical techniques and RT methods have improved tumor local control while minimizing adverse effects.

For superficial, low-grade E-STS tumors smaller than 5 cm or if compartmental resection is possible, surgery alone may be considered after a multidisciplinary review. Postoperative RT should be considered for grade 2 and 3 E-STS, tumors exceeding 5 cm, or cases with positive surgical margins where re-excision is not possible. Preoperative RT is recommended for locally advanced tumors with anticipated inadequate surgical margins or proximity to neurovascular structures [9, 10]. While perioperative RT improves local control in high-grade E-STS, its effect on metastasis-free and overall survival remains unclear [11]. The optimal timing for RT remains controversial. While both preoperative and postoperative treatments yield comparable local control rates, their toxicity differs. Preoperative RT tends to cause wound disruption, which is generally reversible, whereas postoperative RT is associated with longer-term side effects, such as irreversible joint stiffness, edema, and fibrosis [12, 13].

We aimed to examine oncological outcomes and toxicity levels in patients undergoing preoperative or postoperative RT combined with surgery for localized E-STS.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

In this retrospective, single-center cohort study based on population data, we included all consecutive patients with E-STS who underwent RT with curative intent, between 01/2015, and 12/2021.

Eligible participants were aged > 18 years with a new, histologically confirmed diagnosis of E-STS. Patients with pre-existing distant metastases, concurrent systemic therapy, or those treated only by surgery or RT were excluded. We also excluded cases where postoperative RT was indicated but not administered due to surgical complication.

Two groups were defined: a preoperative RT group, consisting of patients who underwent RT before surgery or had a whoops surgery (unplanned sarcoma excision) followed by RT and re-excision; and a postoperative radiotherapy group, which included patients who received RT after surgery or had a whoops surgery, re-excision, and subsequent RT.

Endpoints

The primary endpoint was local recurrence-free survival, which spanned the time from diagnosis to either local recurrence, as reported by the investigator, or death from any cause. Secondary endpoints included recurrence-free survival, metastasis-free survival, and overall survival (all measured from the date of diagnosis to the respective events or death from any cause). Observations were censored at the date of the last follow-up for patients not experiencing the event.

Adverse events were reported according to the CTCAE v5.0 [14]. Acute pain was assessed by changes in analgesic prescription during RT. Surgical complications were recorded and described, including wound disruption, infection, surgical revision, and local care-related hospitalization, along with rehospitalization duration.

Tumor grades were extracted from surgical pathology reports and classified per the FNCLCC system. They were referred to as low if they were grade 1, or high if they were grade 2 or 3.

Statistical analysis

Local recurrence-free survival (LRFS), recurrence-free survival (RFS), metastasis-free survival (MFS), and overall survival (OS) were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, separately for the pre- and postoperative groups. Survival rates, along with their 95% confidence intervals (CI), were calculated at 1, 2, 3, and 5 years as well as their medians. The median follow-up was estimated via the reverse Kaplan–Meier method (Schemper), extending from the date of diagnosis to the date of the last follow-up for patients alive at that time.

The cumulative incidence of local recurrence was estimated using the Kalbfleisch and Prentice model, accounting for competitive events such as regional recurrence, metastasis, or death without prior local recurrence. Observations were censored at the date of a competitive event’s occurrence. Sensitivity analyses were conducted, where RFS and OS were estimated after excluding whoops surgeries to assess the outcomes for patients who received optimal care. An exploratory analysis outlined histological differences between biopsy and post-surgery.

Descriptive analyses (frequency and percentages for categorical variables; mean, standard deviation, median and range for quantitative variables) were performed for each group (pre- and postoperative radiotherapy treatment) to describe patients’ characteristics, pathological findings, treatments, efficacy, and RT-related toxicities. Comparative analyses of clinical, therapeutic, and toxicity characteristics were made based on the radiotherapy type using Fisher's exact tests or Student's t-tests. The count of missing data (MD) was indicated for each variable. The data was analyzed using STATA software version 17.0.

Regulatory framework

Data were extracted from the PMSI database. All data were collected from hospital patient files. Non-opposition to anonymously report patient data was verified for every patient alive at the time of inclusion. The study is compliant with the MR004 methodology adopted by the French Data Protection Authority. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Oscar Lambret Center (N° CEC-2023–003).

Patients and primary tumor characteristics at initial diagnosis

Tumors in the preoperative group were larger (median size 111 mm, range 17.0–320) compared to those in the postoperative group (median size 67.5 mm, range 8.0–308; p = 0.002). Additionally, tumors in the preoperative group were more likely to be deep (82.3% vs 61.8%, p = 0.011), perivascular (50.8% vs 29.4%, p = 0.027), or in contact with bone (32.3% vs 11.8%, p = 0.018), as shown in Table 1.

The postoperative group had a higher frequency of whoops surgeries (38.2% vs 7.3%, p < 0.001), but these were less often classified as R2 resection at diagnosis (46.2% vs 85.7%, p = 0.158).

The most frequent histological subtypes were liposarcoma, (38%) including well-differentiated, dedifferentiated, myxoid, and pleomorphic liposarcomas, and undifferentiated sarcoma, (34.2%) including undifferentiated pleomorphic, spindle cell, epithelioid sarcomas, and undifferentiated sarcoma NOS.

High-grade tumors accounted for 64.5% of the preoperative group and 73.5% in the postoperative group (p = 0.721).

Age and tumor size are described using median [interquartile range, IQR], comparison between groups was performed using Wilcoxon-Mann–Whitney tests. Qualitative variables are described using number (percentage), comparison between groups was performed using Pearson χ2 tests or Fisher exact test.

Characteristics of treatment

Conservative surgery was achieved in 97.6% and 100% of the preoperative and postoperative groups, respectively (Supplementary Table 1, annex). The surgical margins were similar, with a majority of R0 resections (75.4% in the preoperative group and 76.5% in the postoperative group), while R1 resections comprised 23.0% and 23.5%, respectively.

IMRT was utilized for 95.2% of the preoperative group and 79.4% of the postoperative group (p = 0.008). In the preoperative group, 96% completed treatment versus 88.2% in the postoperative group (Supplementary Table 2, annex). Discontinuation factors in the preoperative group included skin toxicity in one patient, local progression in two patients, digestive occlusion and unrelated surgery with failed final session in one patient, and amputation due to severe unrelieved pain in one patient; in the postoperative group four patients discontinued treatment following skin toxicity.

The median dose for both groups was 50.4 Gy. The median time interval between the end of preoperative RT and surgery was 52 days (range, 14–98 days); between surgery and the start of postoperative RT, the median interval was 83 days (range, 14–199 days).

Oncological outcomes

The median follow-up was 2.9 years (range 0.5–7.9 years) for the preoperative group and 5.1 years (range 1.1–8 years) for the postoperative group. Survival data are shown in Fig. 2. In the preoperative group, 30 patients had an event affecting LRFS: 4 had local recurrence and were alive at last follow-up, 4 had local recurrence and died, and among the 22 who died without local recurrence, 14 died from sarcoma disease progression. The LRFS rates were 96.0% [95%CI = 90.6; 98.3] at 1 year, 82.1% [95%CI = 73.6; 88.2] at 2 years, and 67.5% [95%CI = 54.4; 77.6] at 5 years. Considering LRFS in the postoperative group, 11 patients had an event: 2 had local recurrence and were alive at last follow-up, 2 had a local recurrence alone and died, and among the seven patients who died without local recurrence, six died from disease progression. The LRFS was 100% at 1 year, 81.9% [95%CI = 64.1; 91.4] at 2 years, and 67.8% [95%CI = 48.2; 81.4] at 5 years. Among the patients who discontinued RT, one patient had a local relapse.

The cumulative incidence of local recurrence was 1.6% (95%CI = 0.3–5.2) at one year and 6.4% (95%CI = 2.8–12.1) at two years for the preoperative group. For the postoperative group, these figures were 3.1% (95%CI = 0.2–13.5) at one year, 6.1% (95%CI = 1.1–17.7) at two years, and 9.3% (95%CI = 2.4–22.1) at three years (Supplementary Fig. 1).

RFS and OS were similar between the preoperative and postoperative groups: one-year RFS was 87.1% [95%CI = 79.8; 91.9] vs. 85.3% [95%CI = 68.2; 93.6]; two-year RFS was 71.9% [95%CI = 62.7; 79.2] vs 70.3% [95%CI = 51.7; 82.8]; five-year RFS was 61.2% [95%CI = 49.1; 71.3] vs 59.3% [95%CI = 40.0; 74.2]; one-year OS was 98.4% [95%CI = 93.7; 99.6] vs 100%, two-year OS was 87.4% [95%CI = 79.5; 92.4] vs 88.0% [95%CI = 71.0; 95.3], and five-year OS was 69.9% [95%CI = 56.2; 80.0] vs 76.8% [95%CI = 57.1; 88.3], respectively (Fig. 2). MRFS was similar between groups as shown in Supplementary Table 3.

Regarding treatment for recurrence, 60.9% of patients received systemic therapy (58.8% in the preoperative group and 66.7% in the postoperative group), RT was administered to 32.6% of patients (35.5% vs 25% in the postoperative group), surgery was performed in 28.3% (23.5% vs 41.7% in the postoperative group), and palliative care was opted for in 13.3% (15.2% vs 8.3% in the postoperative group). Systemic treatment was combined with RT and/or surgery in 24% of patients.

Neither preoperative nor postoperative RT appeared to be correlated with the risk of local recurrence or death after excluding those who underwent whoops surgery (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Radiotherapy toxicities and surgical complications

Toxicities secondary to RT are detailed in Supplementary Table 2. In the preoperative RT group, 85 patients (68.5%) experienced at least one form of toxicity, compared to 29 patients (85.3%) in the postoperative RT group. Grade 2 dermatitis or higher was reported more frequently in the postoperative group (17,6% grade 2; 11,8% grade 3) than in the preoperative group (12,9% grade 2; 3,2% grade 3). However this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.080). Late functional disorders were equivalent in both groups: 7.3% in the preoperative group and 5.9% in the postoperative group (p = 1).

Wound management presented more challenges in the preoperative RT group: 11 out of 123 patients (8.1%, MD = 1) had wounds that did not completely heal at the last follow-up, compared to just 1 out of 34 patients (2.9%) in the postoperative group (p = 0.458). Additionally, 13 out of 124 patients (10.7%) in the preoperative group required hospitalization for localized care, compared to none in the postoperative group). The median time to complete wound healing was notably longer in the preoperative group: 78.0 days (range: 12.0; 812.0) vs 48.5 days (range: 10.0; 547) in the postoperative group (p = 0.048).

Patients treated with preoperative RT had more postoperative complications: 39,3% vs 14,7% had wound disruption (p = 0.007), and 28,5% vs 5,9% had infection (p = 0.006). The surgical revision rate was similar between the groups (p = 0.492): 9,8% vs 14,7% had a single surgical revision and 3,3% vs none had multiple surgical revisions.

Hospital stay duration was marginally longer in the preoperative group with a median of 8.0 days (range 1.0;62.0) vs. 7.0 days (range 2.0;24.0) in the postoperative group (p = 0.188), with a marginally higher rate of rehospitalization (17,4% vs 15,2% in the postoperative group, p = 0.765). Rehospitalizations lasted longer in the preoperative group, median time: 11 days (range 1.0;40.0) vs. 6.0 days (range 3.0;11.0) in the postoperative group (p = 0.161). Among all patients, 19 had discordant histology between the biopsy and surgical specimens.

Discussion

This single-center retrospective study involving 158 patients who received perioperative RT and surgery for E-STS revealed comparable LRFS, RFS, and OS rates in both preoperative and postoperative settings. Care should be taken when retrospectively comparing patients treated with either preoperative or postoperative RT, as patient selection for these different therapies is inherently biased. However, since the baseline tumor characteristics detailed in Table 1 were more adverse in the preoperative group, with a similar local control, our findings suggest that preoperative RT might have improved the patients’ prognosis.

Fourquet et al. [15] reported a five-year LRFS of 70.9% in a cohort treated postoperatively. O’Sullivan’s 2013 phase 2 study [16] assessing preoperative RT showed a five-year LRFS of 88.2%, with a comparable local recurrence rate of 6.8% versus 6.4% in our study. Dapper et al.'s 2023 study reported a 70% five-year LRFS rate in patients treated with either preoperative or postoperative RT, without distinction [17]. A summary of the main results from recent series is included in Supplementary Table 5.

Patients treated with preoperative RT experienced more surgical complications. This observation aligns with O’Sullivan et al. [12], who found twice as many wound complications in the preoperative group, 35% vs 17%) in the postoperative group. Despite the increased incidence of surgical complications in the preoperative RT group, these complications did not prevent patients from completing the entire treatment sequence. Surgical complications did, however, prevent the delivery of adjuvant RT in six cases in our study, meaning those patients did not receive optimal care. The RT discontinuation rate was lower in the pre-operative setting.

R0 resection rates in our study were 75.4% in the preoperative group and 76.5% in the postoperative group. O’Sullivan et al. reported R0 rates of 83% and 85% in the preoperative and postoperative groups, respectively [12]. Gingrich’s 2017 NCBD analysis of 27,969 patients showed R0 rates of 90% and 75%, respectively [18]. Wang et al. reported a 76% R0 rate in the preoperative setting [19]. Almost one-third of our patients had contact with bone, and nearly half were close to critical structures. A higher R1 resection rate in our study does not necessarily signify a diminished chance of successful treatment, as preoperative RT for E-STS improves local control even in instances of planned positive margins [20].

Sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the risk of local recurrence and death in both groups after excluding patients who underwent whoops surgery. Although Kaplan–Meier curves appeared to show better outcomes in the preoperative group, the results were not statistically significant. This may be due to the low rate of postoperative RT without whoops surgery, leading to a lack of statistical power.

To the best of our knowledge, this series represents the most extensive French retrospective analysis focused on the oncological outcomes and adverse events of newly diagnosed E-STS treated with both preoperative and postoperative RT. A key strength of our study, is the utilization of the PMSI database and the systematic record review, allowing for an exhaustive analysis of patients treated during the study period. This study provides modern data regarding the oncological outcomes of E-STS treated with RT, both pre- and postoperatively.

The findings should be interpreted cautiously due to the retrospective nature of this study. Certain variables, such as functional disorders or late pain, may be underestimated or lack standardized assessment. The fibrosis rate appeared unusually low, likely because it was under-reported in medical records. Radiological and histological assessments for evaluating the response to preoperative radiotherapy were not standardized, rendering them unsuitable for prognostic analysis.

Another limitation is the use of a uniform radiation regiment of approximatively 50 Gy in both groups, as only 8,8% of patients in the post operative group received a boost. The main explanation for the absence of radiation boost was the lack of preoperative MRI imaging and treatment at external facilities, which hindered the accurate localization of the at-risk areas for a radiation boost. Increasing the dose to the entire CTV was a concern for toxicity.

Despite recent technological advancements, the prognosis for E-STS remains poor. Combining preoperative RT with sensitizing agents offers promise for improved local control and reduced treatment-associated morbidities [21]. Overall survival correlates with the disease's metastatic progression. Emerging E-STS treatment combinations could lead to improved outcomes. Ongoing trials, such as RT-Immune (NCT03474094), aim to evaluate the clinical efficacy of integrating immunotherapy with RT and surgery. The benefits of supplementing targeted drug therapies with additional RT for E-STS remain unconfirmed and warrant future [22].

Conclusion

Our retrospective study, involving 158 patients treated with perioperative radiotherapy and surgery for localized E-STS, revealed similar local control and overall survival rates between those treated with preoperative and postoperative radiotherapy. This was despite the preoperative group having more pejorative characteristics such as larger and deeper tumors, and proximity to critical structures. In accordance with existing literature, we found that postoperative complications were more frequent in the preoperative radiotherapy group. However, this group also had better treatment compliance. It should be noted that postoperative complications can occur independently of radiotherapy and may preclude its use in cases of impaired wound healing, preventing patients from receiving the full course of treatment.

Given that survival rates are largely influenced by the metastatic progression of STS, further studies are necessary to improve these patients' prognosis. For instance, ongoing trials are exploring the inclusion of immunotherapy or targeted therapy alongside radiotherapy and surgery.
