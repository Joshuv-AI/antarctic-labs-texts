Patients before paperwork: a feasibility study evaluating digital and human scribes in psychiatric clinical settings – a randomised controlled trial protocol
Authors: Jessica Dyck, Essence Perera, Braidin Filbert, Antonio Paletta, Sarvesh Logsetty, Rae Spiwak, Christopher Classen, Cassy Joaquin, Jitender Sareen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThere are national calls for reducing physician administrative burden. While interest in digital scribes (DS) is growing, there is limited evidence evaluating their safety and effectiveness. In contrast, human scribes (HS) have demonstrated significant benefits in multiple controlled studies. To our knowledge, no prior study has compared the utility of HS versus DS in improving clinical efficiency in psychiatric clinical settings. We hypothesise that HS and DS will reduce administrative burden for psychiatrists compared with no scribes.Methods and analysisFollowing institutional ethics board approvals, we will conduct a randomised controlled crossover study with 10 Manitoba psychiatrists. Study phases will include 1 month periods for baseline, HS and DS for a total of 3 months. Measures will include physician characteristics, practice patterns, administrative hours and patient satisfaction. Depending on distributional characteristics, data will be analysed using parametric or non-parametric tests to compare scribed and non-scribed periods as well as HS versus DS.Ethics and disseminationEthics approval has been obtained from the Committee for Harmonized Health Impact, Privacy, and Ethics Review (CHIPER) Health Research Ethics Board, which is the ethics board for medical research at the University of Manitoba (HS26944 (H2025:154)). The results of this trial will be disseminated through a peer-reviewed journal and provincial and national conference presentations. Integrated knowledge translation will be conducted throughout the duration of the research process with key stakeholders including medical organisations, such as the provincial health organisation.Protocol version1.2, 9 April 2026

Introduction

Physician administrative burden is a critical issue in healthcare, with substantial evidence linking excessive documentation and clerical tasks to physician burnout.1 Burnout, characterised by emotional exhaustion, depersonalisation and a reduced sense of personal accomplishment, negatively affects physician well-being, patient care and healthcare system efficiency.1 Recent Canadian reports, including findings from Manitoba, highlight that physicians often spend over 10–15 hours per week on documentation outside of clinical hours, significantly contributing to burnout.2 3 Addressing this issue has become a priority for improving physician wellness, reducing attrition and enhancing healthcare outcomes.3

Psychiatrists, in particular, are vulnerable to experiencing burnout for various reasons, including high work demands without adequate resources and emotional stressors that are unique to the specialty.4 Survey results published by Woolhandler and Himmelstein demonstrate that psychiatrists spend 20.3% of their working hours performing administrative tasks; compare this to the 16.6% of working hours that the average physician spends on administrative tasks.5 Psychiatrists largely spend this time on the documentation of extended consults for electronic medical records (EMRs). The field of psychiatry is an opportune setting to test technology that may optimise the completion of administrative tasks.

In response to administrative burden challenges, interest in digital scribes (DS) and ambient scribes, which use voice recognition and artificial intelligence (AI) technologies, has grown exponentially. DS can reduce physician workload by transcribing clinical encounters directly into EMRs, while ambient scribes go further by using AI-powered systems to autonomously generate clinical notes based on conversations during patient visits.6 Tierney et al reported the implementation of an ambient scribe in a large US healthcare system with over 3400 physicians.6 Nearly 1000 physicians used the AI scribe, and the physicians reported more personal, meaningful and effective patient interactions and a reduction in after-hours clerical work. Although these are promising early results, challenges such as software integration, note accuracy and provider acceptance remain significant barriers to widespread adoption.6 7

In contrast, multiple controlled studies have shown that human scribes (HS) decrease documentation time, improve workflow efficiency and enhance patient care by increasing ‘face time’ during clinical encounters.8 9 Furthermore, studies report improved provider satisfaction, reduced burnout and higher productivity, with physicians seeing more patients during a set time-period when using human scribes.1012 For example, Graves et al reported that using scribes in Canadian emergency departments significantly improved physician efficiency without compromising patient interaction.13 Despite these benefits, challenges such as training costs, ongoing operational expenses and privacy concerns limit the scalability of human scribes, particularly in resource-limited settings.14

We chose to model our study after a high-impact study by Mishra et al, which studied 18 primary care physicians and used a crossover design which demonstrated reduction in administrative burden and increased patient interaction time during scribed periods compared with non-scribed periods.9 A six-question physician survey measured administrative burden and a three-question patient survey measured patient experience.9

While DS offer a promising alternative with scalability and lower long-term costs, they lack the robust evidence base of HS. To our knowledge, few studies directly compare these modalities, leaving a critical gap in the literature. This study aims to evaluate the feasibility of implementing and comparing HS and DS in clinical workflows to reduce physician administrative burden and improve clinical efficiency. Both physician and patient experiences are affected by the introduction of scribes into clinical settings, therefore this study also aims to examine the impact of HS and DS on physicians’ self-reported work satisfaction and visit quality as well as patient perceptions of the impact of scribes on their physician interactions. We aim to assess the practicality and workflow integration of medical scribes within clinical settings primarily through the lens of physicians with weekly surveys and bidirectional feedback. The specialty of psychiatry has been chosen as an initial setting in which to test the feasibility of the study design before initiating a large-scale trial.

Methods and analysis

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

For participants to be included in this study, they must be (1) licensed psychiatrists in good standing with the College of Physicians and Surgeons of Manitoba (CPSM); (2) certified as specialists in psychiatry by the Royal College of Physicians and Surgeons of Canada; and (3) be willing to integrate scribes into their clinical workflow and complete study-related surveys.

Exclusion criteria

Participants will be excluded from this study if they (1) are unable or unwilling to consent to participation and (2) are currently under investigation by the CPSM, not in good standing with the CPSM or facing a by-law concern.

Recruitment

The psychiatrist sample (N=10) will be recruited through advertisements distributed via professional networks and medical organisations. A formal invitation to participate will prompt physicians to complete a screener questionnaire in order to qualify themselves for the study. The email will include the details of the study and a generic link to a screening survey followed by a consent form. A study email address will be provided for any participant queries throughout the recruitment process. We will measure the rate of recruitment of psychiatrists.

Study design

This is a pilot randomised controlled non-inferiority crossover trial designed to evaluate the feasibility of comparing HS and DS in reducing administrative burden and improving clinical efficiency (figure 1). The study includes three 1 month phases: baseline (no scribes), HS and DS. Individual physicians will be randomised into two groups in equal proportions (1:1) to determine the order of HS and DS phases:

Design of the trial. Physician participants (N=10) are randomised (1:1) into either group A (baseline → HS → DS) or group B (baseline → DS → HS). Physician surveys are sent out weekly.

Sample size

A sample of 10 psychiatrists was deemed an adequate sample size as this is a pilot crossover trial. Five psychiatrists will be randomised to each of the two groups. All 10 psychiatrists will undergo both interventions (HS and DS), only in different sequences depending on their randomisation. A sample size of 10 will result in statistically significant feasibility results as per Teresi et al, since we expect a large effect size across parameters.15 Furthermore, the within-subject statistics of a crossover trial increase the study’s power, as each participant serves as their own control,16 further substantiating a sample size of 10.

Randomisation

The psychiatrists will be randomised using a Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) randomisation sequence. This involves importing a randomisation table from Excel, which will be ‘randomized’ by a member of the research team after participant eligibility has been verified. A simple randomisation allocation without stratification will divide the 10 psychiatrists into two groups (1:1).

The need to manually schedule HS shifts, the nature of the implementation of a scribe and the statistical analysis of questionnaire data means that we are unable to blind either the investigators, the participants, or the data analysts.

Procedures

All participants will begin the study by completing a 1-month baseline period, where they will not use a scribe (HS or DS), regardless of whether a scribe was a part of their practice prior to the study. The same 6-item survey that will be disseminated to the physicians during the interventions will also be disseminated during the baseline period.

Physicians in the HS period of the study will be provided with an HS by the research team. They are encouraged to use this HS in whichever way makes the most sense for their practice. This could include synchronous scribing, asynchronous scribing or remote scribing. Their preference in the utilisation of the HS will be measured in the weekly surveys, and this preference may change from week to week.

We will hire five scribes that we will train in documentation, medical terminology and privacy compliance under the Personal Health Information Act (PHIA). Training will consist of a 2-day standardised programme. Scribes will provide synchronous or asynchronous documentation services in accordance with physician preferences, which will be recorded. The research team will schedule HS shifts to ensure physicians in the HS period consistently work with the same HS during clinical encounters.

When the recruited physicians enter the DS period of the study, they will be given the choice of DS that they would prefer to use, but two products will be recommended to physicians: Scribeberry (synchronous scribing during the patient encounter) and Scribe4All (a chatbot that can collect patient information asynchronously). Both Scribeberry and Scribe4All comply with Canadian privacy standards, including the Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act (PIPEDA). There are a variety of DS tools available. The scribe software chosen by physicians must comply with Canadian privacy standards and PIPEDA. The physician preference of synchronous versus asynchronous DS in the pilot study will guide future larger trials.

Data collection

Data collection will occur through the University of Manitoba’s REDCap system, a secure, web-based application for building and disseminating online surveys and storing data. The physician REDCap surveys will be disseminated to physicians via links emailed weekly. The patient REDCap survey will be disseminated to patients via a paper-copy invitation with a QR code after in-person consults or via a link emailed to patients after either in-person consults or virtual consults. The administrative staff of the physician participants themselves will be responsible for the dissemination of the patient survey links.

Participants will be required to complete the questionnaires online using the University of Manitoba’s REDCap survey system. All existing patient confidentiality regulations will be respected and adhered to, and all computer database materials will be devoid of unique patient identifiers. The team members involved in the data collection phase include research staff and students, all of whom have completed a PHIA pledge and Course on Research Ethics (CORE) training. As this is a 3-month feasibility study with no direct impact on patient care, the trial poses minimal risk to patient or provider safety and a Data Monitoring Committee was deemed unnecessary.

Consent process

Physician consent will be obtained through REDCap once, after the completion of the screening survey and prior to the commencement of the trial. Physician consent will be collected through a separate project file on REDCap in order to retain anonymity as the survey data is collected. Patient consent will be obtained prior to the completion of the patient survey, that is, after their consult with a participating physician.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics (means, SD, medians and proportions, as appropriate) will summarise all primary feasibility outcomes, including the rates of physician recruitment, retention across all study phases and completion of study-related surveys by physicians and patients. Feasibility of integrating HS and DS into clinical workflows will also be reported descriptively by examining the proportion of weeks in which each scribe type was successfully used.

For quantitative secondary outcomes collected during the baseline, HS and DS phases including physician satisfaction, administrative burden, documentation time and burnout data will first be assessed for normality. Depending on distributional characteristics, parametric tests (paired t-tests or repeated-measures analysis of variance) or non-parametric tests (Friedman tests and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests) will be applied. The Friedman test will be used when outcomes are non-normally distributed, and significant overall findings will be followed by Wilcoxon signed-rank tests to evaluate pairwise differences between HS and DS. Descriptive statistics will also summarise outcomes such as the median hours spent on documentation and percentage of time directly interacting with patients across the three phases. Potential period or carryover effects related to the crossover design (ie, HS→DS vs DS→HS) will be explored descriptively.

Exploratory sensitivity analyses will examine whether baseline levels of administrative burden or burnout are associated with participant withdrawal or reduced completion of study procedures, if such patterns arise during the trial.

Patient survey responses (3-item survey) will be summarised using descriptive statistics of central tendency and proportions to evaluate feasibility of patient data collection and to assess patient-reported experience with scribes.

All randomised participants will be included in analyses without subgroup stratification. The amount and pattern of missing data will be examined to inform appropriate analytical considerations for the large-scale trial. Effect sizes calculated from phase-level comparisons will inform sample size and power estimates for the future large-scale RCT.

Patient and public involvement

Patients are involved in the conduct and dissemination planning of this feasibility study. Patient feedback on clinician-patient interactions in the presence of scribes will be collected through periodic surveys. Participants may pause or discontinue scribe recording during encounters to preserve autonomy and minimise discomfort. Findings from patient feedback will inform the design and implementation of a future larger trial, in which patient and public involvement will be further integrated. Plans for dissemination include the development of lay summaries and a webinar to share findings with patient and public audiences.

Ethics and dissemination

As the intervention of the study has no direct impact on patient care, the trial poses minimal risk to patient or provider safety. Psychiatrists are free to withdraw from the study should they find that their safety or well-being, or their patients’ safety or well-being, is at risk.

The presence of scribes, human or digital, may influence clinician–patient interactions. This study incorporates measures to monitor patient perspectives through periodic surveys. Participants will have the option to pause or discontinue scribe recording during the encounter, preserving patient autonomy and minimising discomfort. Patient feedback collected in this feasibility phase will inform the design and implementation of a future larger trial.

Ethics approval has been obtained from the Committee for Harmonized Health Impact, Privacy, and Ethics Review (CHIPER) Health Research Ethics Board, which is the ethics board for medical research at the University of Manitoba (HS26944 (H2025:154)). Official ethics amendments will be made should the protocol change prior to the start date. Integrated knowledge translation will be conducted before, during and after the trial with key stakeholders including medical organisations, such as the provincial health organisation.

The protocol as well as physician screener, physician survey and patient survey will be published in peer-reviewed journals. Once the study has been completed, the manuscript will also be published in a peer-reviewed journal. Student researchers will present the results of the trial at various local conferences. We will generate lay summaries and organise a webinar to communicate key insights to broader healthcare and patient communities, including patients and the public.

De-identified data will be made on request to the corresponding author.
