Thermodynamic Uncertainty Relation in Hybrid Normal-Superconducting Systems: The Role of superconducting coherence
Franco Mayo, Nahual Sobrino, Rosario Fazio, Fabio Taddei, Michele Governale

Abstract
We study the violation of thermodynamic uncertainty relations (TURs) in hybrid superconducting systems consisting of one superconducting and two normal leads coupled to a central region containing localized levels, focusing on the role of quantum coherence. We first study the simplest setup yielding TUR violations, where the central region is a single-level quantum dot and only one normal lead is connected. In addition to the classical TUR, we consider an alternative formulation recently derived for coherent conductors. We find even the latter TUR is violated (although by a smaller extent) as a result of the macroscopic coherence related to the superconducting condensate. To support this conclusion, we connect the second normal lead as a dephasing probe, demonstrating that the violation is directly correlated with the superconducting coherence, as measured by the dot’s pair amplitude. When the central region is a Cooper-pair splitter, crossed Andreev processes introduce nonlocal superconducting correlations that further enhance the quantum-TUR violation.



Introduction—

Stability and efficiency are mutually exclusive in a thermodynamic process, e.g. in a thermal machine. Any effort to reduce the fluctuations of a certain output quantity is necessarily accompanied by an increase of entropy production, therefore lowering its efficiency. This interplay is beautifully captured by the so called Thermodynamic Uncertainty Relations (TURs) which set a lower bound on the relative uncertainty of a current for a given rate of entropy production. For classical systems governed by Markovian dynamics, the TUR can be expressed as the inequality

An interesting question now arises if the presence of

The aim of this Letter is to answer this question by studying hybrid superconductor-normal systems. Surprisingly, we find that while single particle coherence does not lead to any violation of the quantum TUR, macroscopic coherence does.
We will consider a system consisting of a superconducting lead and two normal leads coupled to a region containing localized levels, as shown in Fig.
1
(a).
By using a Green’s functions approach, we look for violation of both forms of TUR in the case where the central region is a single QD and the lead R is disconnected and then
assess the effect of dephasing introduced by connecting lead R and using it as a voltage probe.
We find that the violation of the quantum TUR weakens by increasing the coupling to the probe, i.e., by decreasing the superconducting coherence, as measured by the superconducting pair amplitude of the QD.
We also find the maximal value of the coupling to the probe for which the two forms of the TUR are still violated, finding that the quantum TUR is roughly 10 times more sensitive to decoherence with respect to the classical TUR.
Acknowledging that hybrid systems allow a richer phenomenology, including the non-locality represented by the crossed Andreev reflection, we assess this effect on TUR violation by considering a Cooper pair splitter (CPS) which is implemented when the central region consists of two QDs, as shown in Fig.

Model and Formalism—
Let us consider a central region containing localized levels (QDs), coupled to one superconducting (S) lead and two normal leads
\eta={\rm L,R}
, as shown in Fig.
1
.
The normal leads are described by the Hamiltonians

H_{\eta}=\sum_{k,\sigma}\epsilon_{k}\,c^{\dagger}_{\eta k\sigma}\,c_{\eta k\sigma}
,
where
c_{\eta k\sigma}
is the annihilator operator for an electron with momentum
k
and spin
\sigma
in lead
\eta
. The single-particle energies in the leads are denoted by
\epsilon_{k}
and we assume that are the same for both leads and independent of spin.
The leads are coupled to the central region by means of the standard tunneling Hamiltonians:

H_{{\rm tunn},\eta}=\sum_{k,\sigma,i}V_{\eta,i}\left(c^{\dagger}_{\eta k\sigma}d_{i,\sigma}+\text{H.c.}\right)
,
where the label
i
denotes a localized state in the central region and will be omitted for the case of only one quantum dot,
d_{i\sigma}
is the annihilation operator for an electron in level
i
, and without loss of generality, we take
V_{\eta,i}
to be real.
Both normal leads are kept at temperature
T
with chemical potentials
\mu_{\rm L}
and
\mu_{\rm R}
, respectively.
The superconducting lead is grounded and we assume the gap
\Delta
of the superconductor to be the largest energy scale (
|\Delta|\rightarrow\infty
limit). In this limit, the quasi-particles in the superconductor do not contribute and the Andreev reflection processes between the superconductor and the central region can be included as an effective pairing term in the Hamiltonian of the central region
Rozhkov and Arovas 2000
;
Meng et al. 2009
;
Eldridge et al. 2010
.

The current
J_{\eta}
flowing from lead
\eta
into the central region can be expressed in terms of the full Green’s functions of the central region in Nambu representation and the Fermi function of lead
\eta

Pala et al. 2007
.
The other ingredient needed for the TUR is the zero-frequency noise in lead S, which is defined as
\mathcal{S}=\frac{1}{2}\,\int dt\,\langle J_{\rm S}(t)J_{\rm S}(0)+J_{\rm S}(0)J_{\rm S}(t)\rangle-\langle J_{\rm S}\rangle\langle J_{\rm S}\rangle
.
If the interactions in the central region can be treated at the level of mean-field, also the zero-frequency noise can be written in terms of Green’s function of the central region in Nambu representation and the Fermi function of lead
\eta
. The expressions for the current, the noise and the Green’s functions of the central region can be found in the Supplemental Material.
Since the normal currents
J_{\eta}
are the only dissipative currents in the system, the entropy-production rate is simply given by
\sigma=-\sum_{\eta}\mu_{\eta}J_{\eta}/(eT)
, where
-\mu_{\eta}/e
is the voltage applied to each normal lead.

Effect of Macroscopic coherence on TUR—
The effects of macroscopic quantum coherence can be explored by modeling the central region in its minimal form as a single QD.
The right normal lead will be used as a dephasing probe (R=P) by setting its chemical potential
\mu_{\rm R}=\mu_{\rm P}
in such a way to nullify the current flowing through it. The left normal lead (L=N) is kept at a chemical potential
\mu_{\rm L}=\mu_{\rm N}
. We denote the tunneling rates between the leads P and N and the QD, as
\Gamma_{P}
and
\Gamma_{N}
, respectively. These rates are calculated in second-order in
H_{{\rm tunn},\eta}
in the wide-band limit.

In the
|\Delta|\rightarrow\infty
limit,
one can write an effective Hamiltonian for the QD that takes into account the coupling to the superconductor
Rozhkov and Arovas 2000
;
Meng et al. 2009
;
Eldridge et al. 2010
:

H_{\rm QD}=\epsilon\sum_{\sigma}d^{\dagger}_{\sigma}d_{\sigma}-\frac{\Gamma_{\rm S}}{2}\,\left(d^{\dagger}_{\uparrow}d^{\dagger}_{\downarrow}+\,d_{\downarrow}d_{\uparrow}\right)\>.

Here,
\epsilon
represents the energy level of the QD.
We start with the ideal system, where decoherence is absent, by setting
\Gamma_{\rm P}=0
.

The exact expressions for the current
J
flowing from the lead N into the QD and its zero-frequency noise are provided in the Supplemental Material.

Here, we examine the limit where
\Gamma_{\rm N}\ll k_{B}T
, but making no assumptions on
\epsilon
,
\Gamma_{\rm S}
and
\mu_{\rm N}
.
This is in contrast to standard perturbative expansions in
\Gamma_{N}
, as e.g. in
Braggio et al. 2011
, where it is also assumed that
\Gamma_{N}
is much smaller than the splitting of the Andreev bound states,
\epsilon_{A}=\sqrt{\epsilon^{2}+\frac{\Gamma_{\rm S}^{2}}{4}}
, and therefore of
\Gamma_{S}
. The standard approach leads to expressions that are not valid in the region of parameter space where TUR violations occur.
In the case considered here, the
current and noise can be written as (hereafter
\hbar=1
)

\displaystyle J=

\displaystyle-e\,\frac{\Gamma_{\rm N}\frac{\Gamma_{\rm S}^{2}}{4}}{\bar{\Omega}^{2}}\Tr\left[\bm{\tau_{3}F^{+}_{\rm N}}(\epsilon_{A})\right],

(2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=

\displaystyle e^{2}\,\frac{\Gamma_{\rm N}\frac{\Gamma_{\rm S}^{2}}{4}}{\bar{\Omega}^{3}}\left\{\left(\bar{\Omega}^{2}+\Gamma_{\rm N}^{2}\right)\frac{\Gamma_{\rm S}^{2}}{4}\Tr\left[\bm{F^{+}_{\rm N}}(\epsilon_{A})\bm{F^{-}_{\rm N}}(-\epsilon_{A})\right]\right.

\displaystyle+\left.\left[2\bar{\Omega}^{4}-\left(\bar{\Omega}^{2}+\Gamma_{\rm N}^{2}\right)\frac{\Gamma_{\rm S}^{2}}{4}\right]\Tr\left[\bm{F^{+}_{\rm N}}(\epsilon_{A})\bm{F^{+}_{\rm N}}(-\epsilon_{A})\right]\right\},

(3)

where
\bar{\Omega}^{2}=\epsilon_{A}^{2}+\Gamma_{\rm N}^{2}/4
and
\bm{\tau_{3}}
is the

The symbol
\bm{F^{\pm}_{\eta}(\omega)}
denotes the

\bm{F^{\pm}_{\eta}(\omega)}=\text{Diag}[f^{\pm}(\omega-\mu_{\eta}),f^{\mp}(-\omega-\mu_{\eta})]
,
where

In this limit, the Fano factor is minimized for

\displaystyle\epsilon=0\quad\text{and}\quad\Gamma_{\rm S}=\sqrt{5/3}\,\Gamma_{\rm N},

taking a very simple form

\displaystyle\frac{9}{16}\left[1-2f(-\mu_{\rm N})\right]-\frac{1}{1-2f(-\mu_{\rm N})},

Physically, the violation of the quantum bound can be attributed to the presence of macroscopic quantum coherence due to the presence of the superconducting condensate. The superconducting correlations in the QD can be quantified by the pair amplitude

|\langle d_{\downarrow}d_{\uparrow}\rangle|=\left|\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\left(\mathbf{G}^{<}(\omega)\right)_{1,2}\right|
.
From a mathematical point of view, the violation of the quantum bound in our system is not unexpected, as it was originally derived based on currents and noise expressions for a normal system.
In contrast, in our case the mathematical expressions for current and noise have a different structure due to the coupling between electrons and holes induced by superconducting correlations.

To corroborate the fact that the violation of the quantum bound is due to the presence of macroscopic quantum coherence, we introduce a source of decoherence by adding a fictitious voltage probe, which reduces the presence of superconducting correlations as measured by the pair amplitude. The probe consists of a normal lead (denoted by P in Fig.
1
) whose voltage is adjusted to make the current flowing through the lead vanish. To do so we calculate the current in the probe
J_{\rm P}
numerically for different values of
\mu_{\rm P}
and find the one that nullifies the current. The coupling strength of the probe is set by its tunneling rate
\Gamma_{\rm P}

\Gamma_{\rm P}\ll\Gamma_{\rm S}
the pairing parameter is reduced by the noise as

|\langle d_{\downarrow}d_{\uparrow}\rangle|=|\langle d_{\downarrow}d_{\uparrow}\rangle|\evaluated{}_{\Gamma_{\rm P} = 0}-\alpha\frac{\Gamma_{\rm P}}{\Gamma_{\rm S}},

where
\alpha>0

\Gamma_{\rm N}
,
\epsilon

\mu_{\rm N}
.
For the parameters values used for the Figures, we have found
\alpha\approx 0.05

As the probe introduces noise in the system we find that both the quantum and classical TUR stop being violated. We define

\bar{\Gamma}_{\rm P}^{\rm cl}

Nonlocal correlation effects on the TUR—

In order to investigate the effect of non-local correlations on the TUR, we model the central region as a Cooper-pair splitter (CPS), as shown in Fig.
1
(b).
The system consists of a double quantum dot symmetrically coupled to two normal leads (
\Gamma_{\rm L}=\Gamma_{\rm R}\equiv\Gamma_{\rm N}
) and one superconducting lead. In the
|\Delta|\rightarrow\infty
limit, the effective Hamiltonian takes the form

\displaystyle H_{\rm CPS}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\eta,\sigma}\epsilon_{\eta}d^{\dagger}_{\eta,\sigma}d_{\eta,\sigma}-\frac{\Gamma_{\rm S}}{2}\sum_{\eta}\,\left(d^{\dagger}_{\eta,\uparrow}d^{\dagger}_{\eta,\downarrow}+{\rm H.c.}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{\Gamma_{\rm C}}{2}\,\left(d^{\dagger}_{R,\uparrow}d^{\dagger}_{L,\downarrow}+d^{\dagger}_{R,\downarrow}d^{\dagger}_{L,\uparrow}+{\rm H.c.}\right)\>,

where
\eta={\rm L,R}
refers to the dots index. The last term describes crossed Andreev reflection, in which a Cooper pair is coherently split between the two dots, generating non-local superconducting correlations characterized by the rate
\Gamma_{\rm C}
.

The combined influence of local (

) tunneling processes on the quantum TUR for the CPS system is studied numerically for the current

For

Conclusions and Outlook—
In this Letter, we demonstrate that the macroscopic coherence arising from superconducting correlations in hybrid systems causes a violation of the recently formulated quantum TUR
Brandner and Saito 2025
.
The classical TUR is also violated, and to a greater extent, even in the weak coupling regime, in contrast to what happens in normal (non-superconducting) systems.
We do this by calculating the Fano factor, using a Green’s functions method, for the minimal system consisting of a QD coupled to a normal and a superconducting lead.
We find that the quantum TUR is violated in a smaller region of parameter space compared to the classical TUR, and it is more sensitive to decoherence.
To understand the role of superconducting correlations, we introduce a decoherence probe. We find that this probe reduces the pairing amplitude in the QD, and for sufficiently strong decoherence, the TUR is no longer violated.

Macroscopic quantum coherence can also introduce non-local correlations. We find that in a Cooper pair splitter, in which two QDs are coupled to the superconducting lead, these correlations can lead to a stronger violation of the quantum TUR.
These findings suggest the importance of formulating a TUR that accounts for superconducting correlations.

Current and Noise for Hybrid Superconducting systems

In this Supplemental Material we present all the necessary technical details on the derivations of the quantities needed for calculating the TUR in a quantum dot coupled to a superconducting lead and two normal leads, and in the Cooper pair splitter coupled to a superconducting lead and two normal leads.

Single Quantum Dot

For the system with a single quantum dot, we need to calculate the current leaving the normal lead as well as its noise. The current flowing from a normal lead
\mathrm{N}
into the dot is defined as
J_{\rm N}=-e\langle\frac{dN_{\rm N}}{dt}\rangle=ie\left[N_{\rm N},H_{\rm tunn,N}\right]
, where

H_{\rm tunn,N}=V_{\rm N}\sum_{k,\sigma}\left(c^{\dagger}_{\rm{N}k\sigma}d_{\sigma}+d^{\dagger}_{\sigma}c_{\rm{N}k\sigma}\right),

and
N=\sum_{k}\bm{\Psi^{\dagger}_{\rm{N}k}\tau_{3}\Psi_{\rm{N}k}}
is written in Nambu representation, where
\bm{\Psi_{{\rm N}k}}=(c_{{\rm N}k\uparrow},c^{\dagger}_{{\rm N}-k\downarrow})^{\intercal}
and
\bm{\Phi}=(d_{\uparrow},d^{\dagger}_{\downarrow})^{\intercal}
. The expression for the current leaving the normal lead is therefore (hereafter
\hbar=1
):

J_{\rm N}(t)=-e\sum_{k}i\,\bm{\Psi^{\dagger}_{{\rm N}k}(t)\tau_{3}\mathbf{V}_{\rm N}\Phi(t)}+\mathrm{H.c.},

(S1)

and
\mathbf{V}_{\rm N}=\mathrm{diag}(V_{\rm N},-V^{*}_{\rm N})
is a matrix that contains the coupling strength between the normal lead and the QD.

Defining
(\bm{G^{<}_{D,\rm{N}k}(\omega)})_{nm}
as the Fourier transform of
i\langle\bm{\Psi^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{N}km}(0)\Phi_{n}(t)}\rangle
we can rewrite the current as

J_{\rm N}=-2\,e\,\mathrm{Re}\left\{\sum_{k}\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\tau_{3}\,\bm{V_{\rm N}}\bm{G^{<}_{D,{\rm N}k}(\omega)}\right]\right\}.

Using a Dyson equation we can rewrite
\bm{G^{<}_{D,\rm{N}k}(\omega)})
as

\bm{G^{<}_{D,{\rm N}k}(\omega)}=\bm{G^{R}_{D}(\omega)V^{\dagger}_{\eta}g^{<}_{\mathrm{N}k}(\omega)}+\bm{G^{<}_{D}(\omega)V^{\dagger}_{\eta}g^{A}_{\mathrm{N}k}(\omega)},

where
\bm{G_{D}(\omega)}
refers to the full dot’s Green’s function (GF) and
\bm{g_{\mathrm{N}k}(\omega)}
to the free lead GF.
Therefore, the current leaving the normal lead can be expressed in terms of the full GF of the QD
as
Meir and Wingreen 1992
;
Pala et al. 2007
:

J_{\rm N}=-e\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\Gamma_{\rm N}\mathrm{Im}\left\{\mathrm{Tr}\left[\tau_{3}\left(2\bm{F^{+}_{\rm N}}(\omega)\bm{G^{R}_{D}(\omega)}+\bm{G^{<}_{D}(\omega)}\right)\right]\right\},

(S2)

where
\bm{G^{R}_{D}(\omega)}
and
\bm{G^{<}_{D}(\omega)}
are the retarded and lesser full GFs of the QD in Nambu representation
Pala et al. 2007
.
The matrices
\bm{F^{\pm}_{{\rm N}}(\omega)}
are defined in terms of the Fermi function of the leads as

\bm{F^{\pm}_{{\rm N}}(\omega)}=\begin{bmatrix}f^{\pm}(\omega-\mu_{\rm N})&0\\
0&f^{\mp}(-\omega-\mu_{\rm N})\end{bmatrix},

where
f^{+}(\omega)\equiv f(\omega)
and
f^{-}(\omega)\equiv 1-f(\omega)
with
f(\omega)
being the Fermi function at temperature
T
.

The other ingredient needed for the TUR is the zero-frequency noise in lead N, which is defined as
foo

\mathcal{S}_{\rm{NN}}=\frac{1}{2}\,\int dt\,\langle J_{\rm N}(t)J_{\rm N}(0)+J_{\rm N}(0)J_{\rm N}(t)\rangle-\langle J_{\rm N}\rangle\langle J_{\rm N}\rangle,

(S3)

To obtain the zero frequency noise, we start with the general formula at frequency
\omega
, as it makes the calculation simpler:

\mathcal{S}_{\rm NN}(\omega)=\frac{1}{2}\,\int dt\,e^{i\omega t}\langle J_{\rm N}(t)J_{\rm N}+J_{\rm N}J_{\rm N}(t)\rangle-\langle J_{\rm N}\rangle\langle J_{\rm N}\rangle.

(S4)

Using expression (
S2
) for the current it is easy to show that

\mathcal{S}_{\rm NN}(\omega)=\,\int dt\,e^{i\omega t}\mathrm{Re}\left\{\langle\mathrm{T}\left\{J_{\rm N}(t)J_{\rm N}\right\}\rangle\right\}-\langle J_{\rm N}\rangle\langle J_{\rm N}\rangle.

(S5)

Applying Wick’s theorem and rearranging, we arrive at:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\left\{\langle J_{\rm N}(t)J_{{\rm N}}\rangle\right\}

\displaystyle=e^{2}\,V_{\rm N}\,V_{\rm N}\mathrm{Re}\sum_{k,k^{\prime}}\sum_{i,j}\{-\langle{\Psi^{\dagger}_{{\rm N}ki}(t)\Phi_{i}(t)}\rangle\langle\mathrm{T}\left\{\Psi^{\dagger}_{{\rm N}k^{\prime}j}\Phi_{j}\right\}\rangle-\langle\mathrm{T}\left\{\Psi^{\dagger}_{{\rm N}ki}(t)\Phi_{j}\right\}\rangle\langle\mathrm{T}\left\{\Phi_{i}(t)\Psi^{\dagger}_{{\rm N}k^{\prime}j}\right\}\rangle

\displaystyle+\langle\Psi^{\dagger}_{{\rm N}ki}(t)\Phi_{i}(t)\rangle\langle\Phi^{\dagger}_{j}\Psi_{{\rm N}k^{\prime}j}\rangle+\langle\mathrm{T}\left\{\Psi^{\dagger}_{{\rm N}ki}(t)\Psi_{{\rm N}k^{\prime}j}\right\}\rangle\langle\mathrm{T}\left\{\Phi_{i}(t)\Phi^{\dagger}_{j}\right\}\rangle\}.

The first and third term, the ones in which the brackets only contain operators at equal times, combined with the factors outside of the sums give rise to a term that is the product of the currents, so we get:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\left\{\langle J_{\rm N}(t)J_{{\rm N}}\rangle\right\}

\displaystyle=\langle J_{\rm N}(t)\rangle\langle J_{{\rm N}}\rangle+e^{2}\,V_{\rm N}\,V_{{\rm N}}\mathrm{Re}\sum_{k,k^{\prime}}\sum_{i,j}\{-\langle\mathrm{T}\left\{\Psi^{\dagger}_{{\rm N}ki}(t)\Phi_{j}\right\}\rangle\langle\mathrm{T}\left\{\Psi^{\dagger}_{{\rm N}k^{\prime}j}\Phi_{i}(t)\right\}\rangle

\displaystyle+\langle\mathrm{T}\left\{\Psi^{\dagger}_{{\rm N}ki}(t)\Psi_{{\rm N}k^{\prime}j}\right\}\rangle\langle\mathrm{T}\left\{\Phi_{i}(t)\Phi^{\dagger}_{j}\right\}\rangle\}.

In order to get rid of the time ordering, we expand all terms as

\mathrm{T}\left\{A(t)B\right\}=\Theta(t)A(t)B-\Theta(-t)BA(t),

and after regrouping we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\left\{\langle J_{\rm N}(t)J_{{\rm N}}\rangle\right\}

\displaystyle=\langle J_{\rm N}(t)\rangle\langle J_{{\rm N}}\rangle+e^{2}\,V_{\rm N}\,V_{{\rm N}}\mathrm{Re}\sum_{k,k^{\prime}}\sum_{i,j}\{-\langle{\Psi^{\dagger}_{{\rm N}ki}(t)\Phi_{j}}\rangle\langle{\Phi_{i}(t)}\Psi^{\dagger}_{{\rm N}k^{\prime}j}\rangle

\displaystyle-\langle{\Psi_{{\rm N}ki}(t)\Phi^{\dagger}_{j}}\rangle\langle{\Phi^{\dagger}_{i}(t)\Psi_{{\rm N}k^{\prime}j}}\rangle+\langle{\Psi_{{\rm N}ki}(t)\Psi^{\dagger}_{{\rm N}k^{\prime}j}}\rangle\langle{\Phi^{\dagger}_{i}(t)\Phi_{j}}\rangle

\displaystyle+\langle{\Psi^{\dagger}_{{\rm N}k^{\prime}j}\Psi_{{\rm N}ki}(t)}\rangle\langle{\Phi_{j}\Phi^{\dagger}_{i}(t)}\rangle\}.

The remaining terms can be expressed as products of GFs, as:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\left\{\langle J_{\rm N}(t)J_{{\rm N}}\rangle\right\}

\displaystyle=\langle J_{\rm N}(t)\rangle\langle J_{{\rm N}}\rangle+\,e^{2}\mathrm{Re}\sum_{k,k^{\prime}}\{-2\Tr{\boldsymbol{\tau_3\,\mathbf{V}_{\rm N}\,G^<_{D,{\rm N} k}(-t)\,\tau_3\,\mathbf{V}_{{\rm N}}\,G^>_{D,{\rm N} k'}(t)}}

\displaystyle+\Tr{\boldsymbol{\tau_3\,\mathbf{V}_{\rm N}\,G^>_{{\rm N} k',{\rm N} }(-t)\,\tau_3\,\mathbf{V}_{{\rm N}}\,G^<_{D}(t)}}+\Tr{\boldsymbol{\tau_3\,\mathbf{V}_{\rm N}\,G^<_{{\rm N} k,{\rm N}k'}(-t)\,\tau_3\,\mathbf{V}_{{\rm N}}\,G^>_{D}(t)}}\},

where we have used that
(\bm{G^{<}_{D,{\rm N}k}}(t))_{ij}=i\langle\Psi^{\dagger}_{j}\Phi_{i}(t)\rangle
,
(\bm{G^{<}_{D}}(t))_{ij}=-i\langle\Phi^{\dagger}_{i}\Phi_{j}(t)\rangle
and
(\bm{G^{<}_{{\rm N}k,{\rm N}k^{\prime}}}(t))_{ij}=i\langle\Psi^{\dagger}_{j}\Psi_{i}(t)\rangle
.
And therefore the noise spectral density is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{{\rm N}{\rm N}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\int dt\,e^{i\omega t}\,e^{2}\mathrm{Re}\sum_{k,k^{\prime}}\{-2\Tr{\boldsymbol{\tau_3\,\mathbf{V}_{\rm N}\,G^<_{D,{\rm N} k}(-t)\,\tau_3\,\mathbf{V}_{{\rm N}}\,G^>_{D,{\rm N} k'}(t)}}

(S6)

\displaystyle+\Tr{\boldsymbol{\tau_3\,\mathbf{V}_{\rm N}\,G^>_{{\rm N} k',{\rm N} k}(-t)\,\tau_3\,\mathbf{V}_{{\rm N}}\,G^<_{D}(t)}}+\Tr{\boldsymbol{\tau_3\,\mathbf{V}_{\rm N}\,G^<_{{\rm N} k,{\rm N}k'}(-t)\,\tau_3\,\mathbf{V}_{{\rm N}}\,G^>_{D}(t)}}\}.

Rewriting the GFs in terms of their Fourier transforms we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{{\rm N}{\rm N}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}\,e^{2}\mathrm{Re}\{\sum_{k,k^{\prime}}-2\Tr{\boldsymbol{\tau_3\,\mathbf{V}_{\rm N}\,G^<_{D,{\rm N} k}(\omega')\,\tau_3\,\mathbf{V}_{{\rm N}}\,G^>_{D,{\rm N} k'}(\omega'+\omega)}}

(S7)

\displaystyle+\Tr{\boldsymbol{\tau_3\,\mathbf{V}_{\rm N}\,G^>_{{\rm N} k,{\rm N} k'}(\omega')\,\tau_3\,\mathbf{V}_{{\rm N}}\,G^<_{D}(\omega'+\omega)}}+\Tr{\boldsymbol{\tau_3\,\mathbf{V}_{\rm N}\,G^<_{{\rm N} k,{\rm N}k'}(\omega')\,\tau_3\,\mathbf{V}_{{\rm N}}\,G^>_{D}(\omega'+\omega)}}\}.

We now use Dyson equations to separate the GFs into full dot GF and free normal lead GF. And then we perform the summation over
k
and
k^{\prime}
by assuming a constant normal density of states and replacing the sum with an integral, obtaining the following formula for the zero frequency noise:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{NN}(0)=

\displaystyle e^{2}\Gamma_{N}\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\mathrm{Re}\left\{\Tr{i\boldsymbol{F^+(\omega)G_D^>(\omega)} - i\boldsymbol{F^-(\omega)G_D^<(\omega)}}\right\}

\displaystyle+e^{2}\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\frac{\Gamma_{N}^{2}}{4}\mathrm{Re}\left\{\Tr{\boldsymbol{\tau_3} \left[2\boldsymbol{G_D^R(\omega)F^+(\omega)} - 2\boldsymbol{F^+(\omega)G_D^A(\omega)} + \boldsymbol{G^<_D(\omega)}\right]\boldsymbol{\tau_3}\boldsymbol{G_D^>(\omega)}}\right\}

\displaystyle-e^{2}\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\frac{\Gamma_{N}^{2}}{4}\mathrm{Re}\left\{\Tr{\boldsymbol{\tau_3} \left[2\boldsymbol{G_D^R(\omega)F^-(\omega)} - 2\boldsymbol{F^-(\omega)G_D^A(\omega)} - \boldsymbol{G^>_D(\omega)}\right]\boldsymbol{\tau_3}\boldsymbol{G_D^<(\omega)}}\right\}

\displaystyle-2e^{2}\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\frac{\Gamma_{N}^{2}}{4}\mathrm{Re}\left\{\Tr{\boldsymbol{\tau_3} \left[2\boldsymbol{G_D^R(\omega)F^+(\omega)} + \boldsymbol{G^<_D(\omega)}\right]\boldsymbol{\tau_3}\left[2\boldsymbol{G_D^R(\omega)F^-(\omega)} - \boldsymbol{G^>_D(\omega)}\right]}\right\}.

(S8)

In order to obtain the current and noise we need the GF of the QD. Here we provide the expressions for the case when the system is also coupled to a probe, i.e. a second normal lead with tunneling rate
\Gamma_{\rm P}
(the S-QD-N case is obtained by setting
\Gamma_{\rm P}=0
). The retarded and advanced GF can be written as:

\bm{G^{R/A}_{D}(\omega)}=\frac{1}{(\omega\pm i\frac{\tilde{\Gamma}}{2})^{2}-\epsilon_{A}^{2}}\begin{bmatrix}\omega+\epsilon\pm i\frac{\tilde{\Gamma}}{2}&-\frac{\Gamma_{\rm S}}{2}\\
-\frac{\Gamma_{\rm S}}{2}&\omega-\epsilon\pm i\frac{\tilde{\Gamma}}{2}\end{bmatrix}\,,

where
\tilde{\Gamma}=\Gamma_{\rm N}+\Gamma_{\rm P}
is the sum of tunneling rates for the normal lead and probe and
\epsilon_{A}=\sqrt{\epsilon^{2}+\frac{\Gamma_{\rm S}^{2}}{4}}
. The lesser and greater GF can be obtained by means of the Dyson equations
Haug and Jauho 2008

\bm{G^{\lessgtr}}=(1+\bm{\Sigma^{R}\,G^{R}})\,\bm{G^{\lessgtr}_{0}}\,(1+\bm{G^{A}\,\Sigma^{A}})+\bm{G^{R}\Sigma^{\lessgtr}G^{A}},

where the self-energies are:
\bm{\Sigma^{<}(\omega)}=i\Gamma_{\rm N}\,\bm{F_{\rm N}^{+}(\omega)}+i\Gamma_{\rm P}\,\bm{F_{\rm P}^{+}(\omega)}
,
\bm{\Sigma^{>}(\omega)}=-i\Gamma_{\rm N}\,\bm{F_{\rm N}^{-}(\omega)}-i\Gamma_{\rm P}\,\bm{F_{\rm P}^{-}(\omega)}
,
\bm{\Sigma^{R}}=-i\tilde{\Gamma}/2
and
\bm{\Sigma^{A}}=i\tilde{\Gamma}/2
.
Replacing this GFs in Eqs. (
S2
) and
S8
we can obtain the following expressions for the current leaving the normal lead and its noise respectively:

\displaystyle J\equiv J_{\rm N}=-2e\,\int\,\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\,R_{A}(\omega)\,\Tr\left[\bm{\tau_{3}F^{+}_{\rm N}(\omega)}\right]\,

(S9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{\rm NN}=4e^{2}\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\,\frac{R_{A}^{2}(\omega)}{\Gamma_{\rm N}^{2}\,\frac{\Gamma_{\rm S}^{2}}{4}}\,\bigg[\Tr\left[\bm{F^{+}_{\rm N}}(\omega)\bm{F^{-}_{\rm N}}(\omega)\right]\Gamma_{\rm N}^{2}\frac{\Gamma_{\rm S}^{2}}{4}

\displaystyle+\Tr\left[\bm{F^{+}_{\rm N}}(\omega)\bm{F^{+}_{\rm N}}(-\omega)\right]\left(\left(\omega^{2}-\epsilon_{A}^{2}+\frac{\Gamma_{\rm N}^{2}}{4}\right)^{2}+\Gamma_{\rm N}^{2}\epsilon^{2}\right)\bigg]\,,

(S10)

with
R_{A}(\omega)
being the Andreev reflection probability
Splettstoesser et al. 2007
:

R_{A}(\omega)=\pi\,\frac{\Gamma_{\rm N}\,\frac{\Gamma_{\rm S}^{2}}{4}}{\omega^{2}+\epsilon_{A}^{2}+\frac{\Gamma_{\rm N}^{2}}{4}}\left[L_{\Gamma_{\rm N}}(\omega-\epsilon_{A})+L_{\Gamma_{\rm N}}(\omega+\epsilon_{A})\right],

where
L_{\Gamma}(x)=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{\Gamma/2}{x^{2}+\Gamma^{2}/4}
and
\epsilon_{A}=\sqrt{\epsilon^{2}+\frac{\Gamma_{\rm S}^{2}}{4}}
.

Since the normal current
J_{\rm N}
is the only dissipative current in the system, the entropy-production rate is simply given by
\sigma=-\mu_{\rm N}J_{\rm N}/(eT)
, where
-\mu_{\rm N}/e
is the voltage applied to the normal lead.

Cooper Pair Splitter

For the Cooper pair splitter system, the retarded and advanced GFs can be written as:

\bm{G^{R/A}_{\rm CPS}(\omega)}=\begin{bmatrix}\omega-\epsilon_{L}\pm i\frac{\Gamma_{\rm L}}{2}&\frac{\Gamma_{\rm S}}{2}&0&\frac{\Gamma_{\rm C}}{2}\\
\frac{\Gamma_{\rm S}}{2}&\omega+\epsilon_{L}\pm i\frac{\Gamma_{\rm L}}{2}&\frac{\Gamma_{\rm C}}{2}&0\\
0&\frac{\Gamma_{\rm C}}{2}&\omega-\epsilon_{R}\pm i\frac{\Gamma_{\rm R}}{2}&\frac{\Gamma_{\rm S}}{2}\\
\frac{\Gamma_{\rm C}}{2}&0&\frac{\Gamma_{\rm S}}{2}&\omega+\epsilon_{R}\pm i\frac{\Gamma_{\rm R}}{2}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\,.

(S11)

S2

S8

\eta

where

are the scattering amplitudes for particle type
n
in lead
\beta
to scatter into lead
\alpha
as type
m
.
