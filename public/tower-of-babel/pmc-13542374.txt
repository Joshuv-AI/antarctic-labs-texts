Ethical considerations for consent for publication
Authors: Marc Cornock
Published: 2026

Consent is a vital ethical and legal aspect of the research process. Research organisations and bodies which specialise in clinical research, such as the UK NHS Health Research Authority [1] and the World Health Organization [2], clearly outline the standard that is expected of clinical researchers with regard to the principles of consent and the requirement to obtain consent from participants in clinical research.

The importance of consent in research cannot be overstated. Participation in research should be voluntary unless the individual is incapable of making decisions, as stated in the Declaration of Helsinki [3]. Recognising that participation is voluntary upholds and safeguards the ethical principles of the autonomy of individuals to participate or not, their dignity and their rights. Having a consent process as part of the research ensures that participants in the research know what their participation will involve, what the research entails, any potential risks and benefits, and that their data may be used in a publication or other form of dissemination.

Before a publisher is able to accept a research paper for publication they have to be assured that the researchers/authors have adhered to the appropriate research ethics procedures, which will include consent. A failure to meet the ethical requirements by authors can lead to the paper being rejected on ethical grounds, regardless of the merit of the paper.

The key to consent is clear communication, that is, presenting information to potential participants in a way that is understandable by them. This generally means using simple, jargon-free language. The information that is presented should explain why the research is needed and/or being undertaken, how long the individual will be expected to participate, what participation means, for instance what procedures may be used and/or what the participant will be expected to do, and any foreseen risks. Any benefits of participation should also be explained. Individuals being approached to participate should have it made very clear that they do not have to participate, that doing so is voluntary, and that there will be no repercussions for not participating. Details about withdrawing from the research, and any time constraints for doing so, should also be explained.

Where there is an intent to publish the results of the research, potential participants also need to be informed that their data may be used in the resultant publications or conference presentations. The extent and form of that data should be made clear: for example, case details including test results and outcomes, photographs, quotations from the participants.

Consent to participate in research has to be given voluntarily. This means that it cannot be given under any duress, manipulation, undue influence or coercion. Vulnerable groups of individuals, such as children and those lacking capacity to make decisions, may be particularly at risk of being manipulated or coerced. For individuals in these groups, additional safeguards may exist in the country in which the research is being conducted. For example, in England and Wales the Mental Capacity Act 2005 [4] has a section which deals specifically with the requirements for undertaking research with adults who lack capacity; the section even deals with the situation where a person has consented to participate but subsequently loses capacity to be able to consent to their continued participation. Where the research is being conducted with child participants, it may be that consent has to be obtained from a parent or legal guardian, either instead of or along with any consent obtained from the child.

There is a difference between an individual giving consent for a medical procedure, consent to participate in research and consent for their data to be in a publication. If the patient has given their consent to a medical procedure, consent would separately need to be obtained for participation in a research project and this should also include consent for publication [5].

Although written consent is generally preferred, it is not the only way that consent can be obtained for participation in research [6]. Participants who may be illiterate or paraplegic or otherwise unable to record their written consent can give consent verbally. It is important to note that if verbal consent is obtained, this needs to be adequately recorded, and best practice is that this is witnessed.

Only when an individual is fully informed and gives their consent voluntarily can their consent to participate be said to be legal valid.

Consent provides an important function in research in protecting and upholding the rights of participants and also ensuring the credibility of researchers and the integrity of the research. When research is published, readers can be assured that ethical safeguards have been maintained and that the results of the research are not at the expense of the participant's autonomy.
