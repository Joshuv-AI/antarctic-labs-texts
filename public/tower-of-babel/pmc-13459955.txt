Bridging the gap in pre-hospital maternity care: emergency personnel’s knowledge and clinical skills in managing obstetric emergencies
Authors: Shirin Shahbazi Sighaldeh, Zeinab Norollahpour, Maryam Nekoolaltak, Shahla Khosravi, Peyman Saberian, Abbas Rahimi Foroushani, Fatemeh Shateranni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPre-hospital obstetric emergencies are unpredictable and time-sensitive, requiring competent and well-trained emergency personnel. Limited preparedness in managing such events can lead to preventable maternal and neonatal complications. This study aimed to investigate the knowledge and clinical skills of pre-hospital emergency personnel in providing obstetric services, offering an integrated overview of the current state at the Tehran Emergency Center.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted in March 2022 at the Tehran Emergency Center. A total of 97 pre-hospital emergency personnel were selected through simple random sampling from the operational staff list. Data were collected using a self-reporting knowledge and clinical skill (SRKCS) questionnaire, a knowledge test, and four objective structured clinical examination (OSCE) checklists. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26 with Pearson’s correlation, independent t-test, repeated measures, and Kruskal-Wallis tests.ResultsParticipants demonstrated moderate knowledge (mean ± SD = 46.6 ± 10.9%) and clinical skill levels (mean ± SD = 58.4 ± 8.7%). Among the OSCE stations, personnel performed best in managing cardiac arrest in pregnant women (mean ± SD = 83.2 ± 11.1%) and weakest in handling childbirth and postpartum hemorrhage (mean ± SD = 33.3 ± 14.0%). A significant positive correlation (P < 0.05) was observed between OSCE performance and both actual and self-reported knowledge and skill levels.ConclusionThe findings highlight critical gaps in pre-hospital readiness for obstetric emergencies, particularly in childbirth and postpartum bleeding management. Incorporating structured, simulation-based training and interdisciplinary collaboration with midwifery educators into emergency personnel education programs could substantially enhance preparedness and improve maternal outcomes in pre-hospital settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09636-3.

Introduction

Medical emergencies are a leading cause of mortality and morbidity globally, with a significant proportion of emergency-related deaths occurring prior to hospital admission [1, 2]. It is estimated that 45% of deaths and 35% of disability adjusted life years could be solved by improving the emergency system in low- and middle-income countries. In these settings, an effective ambulance-based system may save approximately 700 lives annually, including about 200 from ischemic heart disease, 200 from obstetric emergencies, and 300 from trauma [3].

Obstetric or midwifery emergencies are known as acute and unpredictable emergencies that can happen at any time during pregnancy [4]. Although these emergencies are rare and only affect approximately 15% of women during pregnancy, childbirth or postpartum, not managing these cases leads to serious consequences for mother and baby [5, 6]. The most common encountering obstetric emergencies in under-resourced countries include bleeding, prolonged labor, sepsis, eclampsia, ruptured uterus, abortion and its complications [7]. In the United States, during 2008–2009, 47.6% of all emergency calls made by women were related to obstetric emergencies, with the most common cases involving preterm birth, low birth weight delivery, childbirth during transfer to the hospital, and subsequent bleeding [8].

The widely recognized “Three Delays Model” proposed by Thaddeus and Maine highlights delays in seeking care, reaching a health facility, and receiving adequate treatment as major contributors to maternal mortality [9]. Within this framework, pre‑hospital emergency services play an important role in reducing delays in reaching appropriate care. In this context, the proficiency of Pre‑Hospital Emergency Personnel (P‑HEP), who are formally trained paramedics and emergency medical technicians responding to out‑of‑hospital emergencies, in managing obstetric emergencies before hospital arrival is essential for ensuring positive maternal and neonatal outcomes [10].

Training programs for P-HEP often focus on trauma and cardiac emergencies while obstetric emergencies are often neglected [11]. This gap is particularly critical in under-resourced countries, since the quality of providing obstetrics and gynecology services is poor and the problem in transferring the women to the hospital is much more common [12]. Therefore, enhancing the knowledge, skills, and attitudes of P-HEP in such settings can greatly influence performance indicators and elevate the quality of emergency care [13].

In many countries, the incidence of births before arriving to the hospital (BBA) is increasing [14], and these high-risk emergency deliveries are often attended by individuals who are not trained midwives [15]. There is little information regarding the incidence of BBA and how they are managed by paramedics and nurses as the first professionals who face these emergencies [16]. Complications associated with BBA include perinatal morbidities like hemorrhage due to retained placenta, genital tears, neonatal infections, hypothermia, hypoglycemia, and subsequent admission to neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) [15, 17, 18]. Having an appropriate level of knowledge, skill and ability in providing obstetric services, as one of the ethical requirements, is directly correlated with patient safety and quality of care [19].

Research indicates that the responsibility, along with insufficient knowledge and skills makes P-HEP vulnerable and stressed when attending to BBA [10]. Although these issues are increasingly acknowledged, few studies have evaluated P-HEP’s knowledge and skills in obstetric emergencies [20–22]. Furthermore, there is currently no available report on the state of P-HEP in addressing obstetric emergencies within Iran. Thus, this study aims to assess the competence of P-HEP as a key determinant of outcomes and the quality of emergency midwifery services by evaluating their knowledge and clinical skills in this domain. This approach provides an integrated overview of the current situation at the Tehran Emergency Center (TEC).

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

The present descriptive-analytical study was conducted in March 2022 at the Tehran Emergency Center (TEC), also known as Emergency 115, which is a regional operational branch of Iran’s national Emergency Medical Services [23] Organization. TEC is an ambulance-based prehospital emergency service and the primary EMS provider in Tehran, the capital city and most populous metropolitan area of Iran, responsible for dispatching and managing emergency medical responses across Tehran Province. It provides prehospital care for a wide range of medical and traumatic emergencies, including obstetric emergencies. In Iran, prehospital emergency care is delivered through the EMS Organization of Iran, which operates nationwide via the 115 dispatch number under the supervision of the Ministry of Health and Medical Education. Therefore, operational protocols, recruitment processes, and in-service training programs are largely standardized across EMS centers nationwide, which may reduce substantial organizational variation in personnel competency assessment. Emergency responses are provided free of charge by ambulance crews, typically consisting of emergency medical technicians and nurses, operating under standardized protocols and centralized dispatch decisions.

Participants and sample size

The study population included all personnel working in the operational units of TEC (n = 1,600), including emergency medical assistants, emergency medical technicians, nurses, and anesthetists. This wide range of personnel was included in order to capture a comprehensive assessment of knowledge and clinical skills across all staff involved in prehospital obstetric emergencies. The sample size was determined using the formula for Pearson’s correlation coefficient. A correlation coefficient of 0.3 or higher was considered statistically significant at a 95% confidence level, with a test power of 80%. The inclusion criteria were employment in the operational units of the TEC as an emergency medical assistant, emergency medical technician, nurse, or anesthetist; at least six months of work experience in the emergency center, and an educational level ranging from a high school diploma to a doctoral degree. Personnel from non-operational units or other professional categories were excluded from participation.

The exclusion criteria included unwillingness to continue participation in the study, failure to complete more than 10% of the self-report questionnaire items, and non-participation in any of the four Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE) stations or failure to complete either the researcher-developed knowledge questionnaire or the self-reported knowledge and clinical skills questionnaire.

A complete list of operational personnel at the TEC (N = 1600) was obtained, with each staff member identified by a unique operational code. Participants were selected using simple random sampling through a computer-generated randomization process (www.random.org). Initially, 106 eligible personnel were enrolled based on the calculated sample size. However, 9 participants were excluded because they were unable to complete all four OSCE stations due to urgent operational responsibilities during the assessment sessions. Therefore, the final sample consisted of 97 participants (Fig. 1).

Although the sample size was relatively limited, this was primarily due to the inherent challenges of recruiting P-HEP, including irregular work schedules, high workload, and continuous field deployment. Consequently, the final sample represents the most feasible and ethically obtainable group within the study period and provides valuable insights into a hard-to-reach and understudied professional population.

Instrument development and validation

In this study, three instruments have been used. Data were collected using a self-reporting knowledge and clinical skill (SRKCS) questionnaire, along with a knowledge questionnaire and four clinical skill checklists from the OSCE [24]. All questionnaires and checklists were designed and developed by the researcher in Persian language (the official language of the study setting) and prepared based on a thorough and comprehensive literature review including numerous scientific articles, textbooks, national and international guidelines, and expert consultations.

Self-reporting questionnaire

The SRKCS questionnaire comprised of three sections. The first section focused on demographic information, including age, marital status, education level, field of study, type of employment, work experience, participation in obstetric emergency services, and previous training in this field. The second part of the questionnaire consisted of 25 items each requiring participants to rate their level of knowledge (self-reported knowledge) using a 3-point Likert scale ranging from low (score 1), medium (score 2), and good (score 3). Self-reported knowledge represents a broader cognitive perception and was therefore measured using a three-point Likert scale to simplify responses and reduce respondent burden. Finally, the participants’ total scores were calculated as percentages using Eq. 1, and classified into three levels: weak (0-57.31), average (57.32–77.33), and good (77.34–100), based on the first and third quartiles. The third section consisted of 27 items that required participants to report their level of clinical skill (self-reported clinical skill) using a 5-point Likert scale: very low (score 1), low (score 2), moderate (score 3), high (score 4), and excellent (score 5). Self-reported clinical skills reflect a more nuanced and experience-based construct; thus, a five-point Likert scale was employed to allow greater sensitivity and discrimination across skill levels Finally, the participants’ total scores were calculated as percentages using Eq. 2, and classified into three levels: weak (0 to 54.06), average (54.07 to 69.26), and good (69.27 to 100), based on the first and third quartiles. Participants who left more than 10% of the questionnaire unanswered were excluded from the study.

Knowledge test

The knowledge questionnaire consisted of 25 multiple-choice questions designed to evaluate participants’ knowledge of obstetric emergency assessment, obstetric care and interventions, and the management of pre-hospital obstetric emergencies. The questions were designed based on the emergency personnel job description and were compatible with the concepts and content found in prehospital care literature, which was made accessible to all TEC operational personnel. Participants were given 20 min to answer the questions. Each correct answer received a score of 1, while incorrect answers were scored as 0. The participants’ knowledge levels were calculated as percentages Eq. 3 based on their total scores. These scores were then classified into three levels using the first and third quartiles: weak (0–35), average (36–56), and good (57–100).

OSCE checklists

The OSCE was used to evaluate participants’ clinical skill in managing obstetric emergencies. The OSCE had four stations and participant performance were evaluated using checklists for each stations. The first station focused on history taking and maternal risk assessment (HTMRA) and its scenario was “A pregnant woman complains of headache and blurred vision. What questions do you ask her and what initial steps do you take in your self-assessment?”. The checklist for this station included items related to personal protection, systematic history taking, identification of obstetric warning signs, assessment of maternal vital signs, and initiation of appropriate initial management steps. The second station covered managing a complicated child delivery (CCD) with a nuchal cord and postpartum bleeding and its scenario was “A pregnant woman called the emergency department due to severe abdominal contractions and vaginal discharge. The checklist for this station included items related to preparation for delivery, safe management of childbirth, recognition and management of a nuchal cord, newborn assessment, and initial management of postpartum hemorrhage. The third and fourth stations assessed skills in newborn (NR) and pregnant women’s (PR) resuscitation, respectively. The third station scenario was: “Imagine that you performed a delivery in an ambulance on a woman at 37 weeks and 5 days whose amniotic fluid was stained with thick meconium. In total, 15 seconds have passed since the birth of a baby who is not breathing and is cyanotic but has pulse. What actions do you take?”. The checklist for this station included items related to neonatal resuscitation procedures such as airway positioning, stimulation, positive‑pressure ventilation, corrective ventilation steps (MRSOPA), advanced airway management, chest compressions, medication administration, and post‑resuscitation care. And the fourth station scenario was: “A 28-year-old pregnant woman called the emergency department due to shortness of breath, palpitations, chest pain, and fatigue. You have arrived at the patient’s location. What actions do you take?”. The checklist for this station included items related to cardiopulmonary resuscitation in pregnant women, including maternal positioning, high‑quality CPR, defibrillation, airway management, medication administration, and post‑resuscitation monitoring.

A pilot test was conducted one week prior to the OSCE to determine the required time for each station. Based on the results, the estimated time for each station was set at 7 min. A separate scenario was created by the researcher for each station.

Participants were evaluated by a skilled and trained examiner using the following scoring system: ‘not done’ (score 0), ‘partially done’ (score 1), and ‘fully completed’ (score 2). The total score for each skill was calculated by summing the scores of checklist items in each station. Based on the number of items in each station (31 in the first, 54 in the second, 29 in the third, and 35 in the fourth), the possible total scores for the respective skills were 0 to 62, 0 to 108, 0 to 58, and 0 to 70. Participants’ clinical skill levels were calculated as a percentage using Eq. 4 based on the total score for all four stations (0-298). These scores were then classified into three levels: weak (0-52.51), average (52.52–63.09), and good (63.10–100), using the first and third quartiles.

Both qualitative and quantitative face and content validity methods were employed. Ten experts from midwifery and emergency medicine fields assessed face validity by reviewing the appearance and clarity of the tools. For qualitative content validity, they provided feedback on grammar, wording, relevance, and item placement. Quantitative content validity was established through the Content Validity Ratio (CVR), where items with a CVR above 0.62 were retained, and the Content Validity Index (CVI), where items scoring above 0.79 were accepted, those between 0.70 and 0.79 were revised, and items below 0.70 were removed. Following expert recommendations and revisions, the final tools were validated and approved. The reliability of the SRKCS questionnaire was confirmed with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.98. The reliability of the knowledge questionnaire was assessed using the split‑half method. The 25 items were randomly divided into two sets of odd and even items (13 and 12 items, respectively). After entering the scores into SPSS version 26, the correlation between the two halves was calculated. The Spearman–Brown coefficient was 0.59, indicating a moderate and acceptable level of correlation between the two item sets. In addition, the Guttman split‑half coefficient was calculated as 0.58, which further supported the approximate reliability of the measurement tool.

In this study, since evaluators were considered part of the assessment tools, the researcher also examined the validity and reliability of the OSCE they conducted. For content validity, evaluators received clear instructions before the exam on how to conduct the OSCE and score each station objectively, with reminders not to offer any guidance or teaching during the assessment. Evaluators for Stations 1, 2, and 4 were selected from faculty members and experienced labor ward staff, while Station 3 was assessed by a seasoned emergency department staff member with prior OSCE experience. Regarding reliability, because all evaluators were experts familiar with the OSCE process and the same evaluator was assigned to each station on both exam days, calculating inter-rater reliability was not deemed necessary.

Data collection

The study was conducted over two days at the crisis management building in Tehran’s municipal districts 5 and 13. To carry out the research, participants first completed the SRKCS questionnaire, as well as the knowledge questionnaire, in the meeting hall on the second floor of the building, under the supervision of two research team members. Subsequently, they proceeded to the first floor in groups of four to participate in the OSCE, which was organized into four separate stations. At each station, an examiner evaluated each participant on a specific clinical skill. Following the evaluation at each station, the examiner directed the participant to the next station. After completing all the stations, participants exited the building through the designated exit door, allowing the next group of four to enter through the entrance door.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS statistical software version 26, employing both descriptive and inferential statistics. To compare the levels of knowledge, clinical skills and demographic variables, the Pearson correlation test, independent group t-test, and Kruskal-Wallis test were utilized. Pearson correlation test was also used for determining the significance relationship between self-reported knowledge, self- reported clinical skill, knowledge and skill. Results

Table 1 presents the demographic characteristics of the participants, consisting of 97 personnel from TEC. The majority was married (62.9%) and belonged to the 20–30 year age group (55.7%). Most participants (60.9%) had an associate degree (a two‑year post‑secondary academic program) and were emergency medical assistants. A significant portion (47.4%) had less than 5 years of work experience. Although 55.7% reported having had more than 10 obstetric emergency missions during their careers, the majority (53.6%) had not participated in any training courses related to obstetric emergencies.

According to the results shown in Table 2, the mean score of participants’ self-reported knowledge in managing pre-hospital obstetric emergencies was 67.6%. Overall, 22.7% of the personnel reported a poor level of knowledge, 52.6% demonstrated a moderate level, and 24.7% showed a good level of knowledge.

The mean score of participants’ self-reported clinical skills was 59.8%. Based on the first and third quartiles, 23.7% of participants reported a poor level of clinical skills, 51.5% reported a moderate level, and 24.7% reported a good level.

According to the findings, among the four evaluated skills in the OSCE, participants achieved the highest mean score in PR (83.2%), followed by NR (80.5%). The lowest mean scores were observed in managing CCD (33.3%) and HTMRA (53.5%).

Discussion

This study was conducted to evaluate the competence of P-HEP as the primary determinant of the outcomes and quality of emergency obstetric services by assessing P-HEP’s knowledge and clinical skills in this domain in Iran.

The findings of the present study indicated that more than half of the participants self-reported an average level of knowledge. The highest reported mean scores, in ascending order, were related to familiarity with the tools inside the delivery set, recognizing signs of impending delivery, identifying mothers who have lost a significant amount of blood, familiarity with the steps of NR, and familiarity with the steps of normal delivery. However, in the educational needs assessment conducted by Wehbi et al., one of the educational gaps identified by P-HEP was in performing child delivery [25].

In a study by Persson et al., ambulance nurses in Sweden reported low levels of preparation and knowledge regarding out‑of‑hospital childbirth and expressed a need for additional training in this area [26]. These findings are consistent with the study results of Vagle et al. [27].

The study by Leonardsen et al. in Norway indicated that 96.6% of ambulance staff reported needing more training in the field of prehospital postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) management [28]. In our study, almost half of the participants (45.4%) self-reported a good level of knowledge and 27.9% self-reported high to excellent level of clinical skills in recognizing and managing PPH.

While these figures appear relatively higher than anticipated, they should be interpreted cautiously given the inherent limitations of self-assessment tools, which are known to overestimate actual competence. This concern is supported by the findings of Govender et al. [29], who conducted an objective KAP assessment among 417 emergency care providers in KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa, and found that despite 68.6% of participants expressing confidence in their skills, 95.7% demonstrated poor knowledge and 96.6% demonstrated poor practice in managing obstetric hemorrhage. The authors described this as a misalignment between self-rated and actual competence, potentially leading to substandard clinical care. A similar pattern may underlie the relatively higher self-reported competence in our study, where objective performance may fall below perceived ability. In this regard, some studies employing more objective measures have reported that P‑HEPs’ knowledge of obstetric emergencies is 60% and 72.7% [30, 31], which are still higher than the mean score observed in our study, further underscoring the persistent knowledge gaps in this domain.

Several factors likely contribute to the suboptimal performance in PPH management observed among P-HEP. Most notably, the high-acuity but low-frequency nature of these obstetric emergencies means that, without regular and targeted training, both knowledge and clinical skills are prone to rapid decay [32]. Furthermore, high workload, stress and poor coordination between P-HEP and maternity care facilities, may reduce preparedness and effective emergency response.

Erkek’s study in Turkey revealed that 68.5% of emergency personnel did not have sufficient knowledge about emergency interventions required during childbirth, and their level of knowledge in this field was 37.1% [33]. Although this mean score is close to the mean score of emergency personnel’s overall knowledge in our study (46.6%), emergency interventions in childbirth were not the only issues addressed in the present study.

These differences may be related to variations in EMS systems, training programs, and the scope of practice of prehospital personnel across countries. For instance, the high proportion of ambulance staff needing more training in Leonardsen et al.‘s study in Norway may indicate greater awareness of educational needs. Conversely, the higher knowledge levels reported in other studies may be associated with differences in training curricula or exposure to obstetric emergencies. Therefore, such contextual differences should be considered when comparing findings across studies.

Moreover, the convergence of low knowledge scores across diverse economic contexts including high-income (Norway) and upper-middle-income settings (South Africa, Turkey, and Iran) suggests that the inadequacy of obstetric emergency preparedness in P-HEP is a structural rather than a resource-dependent phenomenon. This finding highlights the critical need to integrate standardized, competency-based obstetric training into EMS curricula.

Given that only 24.7% of the personnel in our study reported satisfactory knowledge of managing obstetric emergencies, and the mean knowledge score was below 50% (46.6%). These findings indicate a clear need for additional in‑service training for Iranian P‑HEP.

Furthermore, 51.5% of participants reported an average level of clinical skill. The highest mean scores, in ascending order, were related to PR, mother care after a normal delivery, and managing and performing all steps of a normal delivery on the scene or in an ambulance when necessary. The lowest mean scores were for managing or performing a child delivery with dystocia on the scene or in an ambulance, managing or performing a breech delivery on the scene or in an ambulance, and carrying out special care and considerations for premature babies.

This hierarchy of competence closely mirrors the frequency with which each scenario is encountered in practice. Flanagan et al. [32], in a cross-sectional KAP study of 264 paramedics in Australia, similarly identified complex intrapartum complications as the most prominent self-reported areas of weakness, while routine delivery tasks were comparatively better managed.

The results of the OSCE in the present study also highlighted the low mean score of P-HEP in managing CCD on the scene or in an ambulance, as well as their high mean score in PR. One reason for the high mean score in PR may be the similarity between many steps of this procedure and standard resuscitation for non‑pregnant women. The resuscitation procedure is a routine task for P‑HEP in their daily work. Another assumption is that, compared to performing child delivery, resuscitation is a more frequent and less specialized skill within the field of obstetric emergencies for P-HEP. Previous evidence has also emphasized that obstetric emergencies occur too infrequently for providers to maintain competency without ongoing simulation-based education [34]. Together, these findings suggest that the skill profile of P-HEP is shaped more by training frequency than by the clinical severity of the scenario.

It is worth noting that in real-life situations, participants’ actual scores for all skills, especially childbirth, might be lower than their obtained scores in the OSCE due to increased stress and reduced self-efficacy. However, the study by Khazaei et al. indicated that the most stressful emergency cases for P-HEP are cardiac emergencies, followed by gynecologic emergencies [35].

In conclusion, despite the good skills of TEC staff in PR, continuous in-service training is recommended to maintain their high-level skills and increase their professional confidence. The study by Papi et al. (2020) in Khuzestan province of Iran showed that 42.7% of P-HEP had poor skills in performing basic life support for non-pregnant women, and 71% did not use the automated external defibrillator device correctly [36]. Whereas our findings showed that 56.7% of P‑HEP had a moderate level of skill in performing PR. Their mean score for maintaining basic life support was 79.3%.

Previous studies have shown that simulation-based interventions can significantly improve clinical competence, teamwork, and decision-making in obstetric emergencies. For instanc, Fransen et al. reported that obstetric team simulation training improved skills in emergency management, particularly communication and coordinated response during critical maternal events [37]. Moreover, regular simulation-based in-service training allows P-HEP to practice technical and communication skills in realistic scenarios without compromising patient safety. Such training can help maintain competency in obstetric emergencies that are rarely encountered in routine prehospital practice. Therefore, integrating ongoing simulation-based obstetric emergency training into continuing professional education programs may enhance the preparedness and performance of P-HEP.

In assessing the relationship between self-reported knowledge and clinical skill, and knowledge and clinical skill in OSCE, the results showed a relationship between self-reported knowledge and both self-reported clinical skill and clinical skill in OSCE. Additionally, there was a relationship between knowledge and clinical skill in OSCE, and clinical skill in OSCE had a positive relationship with all three variables: self-reported knowledge, self-reported clinical skill, and knowledge.

Despite our findings, the study by Papi et al. showed no significant relationship (p = 0.298) between the knowledge and clinical skill of P-HEP in terms of basic life support [36]. In contrast, Malekzadeh et al. (2015) found a significant relationship between the knowledge and overall performance of nursing and midwifery students in NR (r = 0.012) [38]. Additionally, Henderson’s study showed a direct and positive relationship between the perceived competence of emergency personnel and their clinical skill [39].

Increased clinical knowledge appears to enhance clinical reasoning, accelerate decision-making, and improve protocol adherence during obstetric emergencies, which may translate into improved practical performance. The discrepancy between our findings and those of Papi et al. may be related to differences in the clinical context, the emergency skills assessed, participant demographics, or the methods used to evaluate competence and performance.

In this study, no significant relationship was found between work experience, field of study, or level of education and the total scores of knowledge and clinical skill in OSCE. The only significant difference was observed in the skill of managing CCD. In this skill, participants with a bachelor’s degree or higher scored significantly higher than those with an associate degree. Additionally, work experience had a significant relationship with the skills of managing CCD, NR, and PR. This suggests that increased work experience in performing out-of-hospital childbirth enhances these skills. Conversely, individuals with less work experience demonstrated higher levels of skill in NR and PR.

These differing findings suggest that obstetric emergency competence may be influenced by factors beyond educational level or years of experience alone. Variables such as recent participation in training programs, exposure to real clinical cases, and involvement in emergency simulations may play an important role in maintaining and improving clinical skills over time. Therefore, future research should use longitudinal or multivariate approaches to better examine the independent effects of academic education, clinical experience, and ongoing training on obstetric emergency competency.

In addition, the absence of a significant association between mean scores of knowledge (self-reported and evaluated) and self-reported clinical skill across different fields of study may be attributed to the standardized structure of pre‑hospital emergency services. Personnel in pre-hospital settings are usually required to operate under the same clinical protocols, guidelines and scope of practice. Our results also demonstrated that medical emergency technicians and emergency medical assistants had higher PR skills than anesthetists. This difference may reflect the more frequent direct involvement of emergency medical personnel in out‑of‑hospital obstetric emergencies and cardiopulmonary resuscitation. Conversely, the study by Abate et al. in Ethiopia revealed a significant relationship (p < 0.05) between nurses’ work experience and level of education, and their knowledge and skill in pre-hospital emergency care [40]. These findings highlight the need for more research on the effects of two main factors: “work experience” and “number of obstetric emergency missions,” on the skill of P-HEP in managing prehospital obstetric emergencies.

Strength & Limitations

One of the major strengths of this study is that it was conducted at the TEC, which covers the entire pre-hospital emergency system of Tehran. This broad coverage enhances the representativeness of the sample and allows the findings to be generalized to a reasonable extent, to P-HEP working in similar urban emergency systems across Iran. In addition, the use of simple random sampling minimized selection bias and further strengthened the representativeness of the study population.

Another important strength was the comprehensive assessment strategy employed in this study. Participants’ competencies were evaluated not only through self-reported questionnaires and knowledge tests, but also through OSCE stations, which enabled a more objective and practical assessment of clinical skills in managing obstetric emergencies. To the best of our knowledge, this is among the first studies in Iran to assess P-HEP’s competencies in obstetric emergencies using an OSCE-based methodology.

Furthermore, conducting this study among P-HEP posed considerable logistical challenges because of the unpredictable and mission-based nature of their work. Despite these difficulties, participants were successfully gathered in person and assessed under standardized conditions, which improved the consistency and quality of data collection.

Despite these strengths, several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged when interpreting the findings.

Because the OSCE stations and knowledge questionnaire items were identical on both days of the study, there was a potential risk of information exchange between participants. To mitigate this, participants were randomly selected from different emergency bases across the north, south, east, and west of Tehran each day. Strict procedures were implemented: participants entered and exited through separate doors, were instructed not to share test-related information with those who had not yet participated, and were prohibited from using any communication devices, including mobile phones, during or after the assessments. Compliance was monitored by a supervisor, and examiners did not provide any guidance or feedback on answers or performance to prevent bias or prior knowledge influence.

Another limitation of this study is that clinical skills were assessed using OSCE stations in a controlled environment, which may not fully reflect the stressful and unpredictable conditions of real prehospital missions. Therefore, participants’ OSCE scores may overestimate their actual clinical performance in the field.

Given that part of the findings relied on participants’ self-reported data, there is a possibility that they may have overestimated or underestimated their knowledge and skills. In this study, participants’ scores, both self-reported and objectively obtained, were classified into three levels (weak, average, and good) using the first and third quartiles. Consequently, the cut-off points for these classifications were based on participants’ own performance, which constitutes an important limitation of the study. Furthermore, the statistical analyses in the present study were primarily based on bivariate comparisons and did not control for potential confounding variables. Therefore, the observed associations should be interpreted with caution, and future studies using larger sample sizes and multivariable analytical approaches are recommended to better determine independent predictors of knowledge and clinical skill in obstetric emergencies.

Conclusion

This study revealed that the majority of P-HEP both reported and demonstrated a moderate level of knowledge and clinical skill in managing pre-hospital obstetric emergencies. Moreover, P-HEP ‘s clinical skills in performing childbirth and managing postpartum bleeding were found to be poor. This underscores the need for targeted interventions to enhance the knowledge and clinical skills of emergency personnel in performing both normal and complicated child deliveries.

Future studies should investigate the effectiveness of interventions such as increasing clinical internships for emergency personnel and implementing well‑designed simulation‑based retraining and in‑service education programs. In addition, improving communication between P‑HEP and midwives and providing opportunities for midwives to be present in pre‑hospital emergency centers may further enhance the quality of care. These measures aim to improve the quality of pre-hospital emergency obstetric care, maintain the health of mothers in the community, and reduce maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality rates.
