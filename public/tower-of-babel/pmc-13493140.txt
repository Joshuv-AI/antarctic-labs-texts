Experiences of culturally Deaf people with health care access and navigation in Ontario, Canada: a qualitative co-design study
Authors: Ben B Levy, Koren Teo, Abby Kapsack, Allia Karim, Kelly MacKenzie, Rose Etheridge, Laurie Legere, Tamara Harth, Csilla Kalocsai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Equitable access to care is a cornerstone of inclusive and fair societies, yet Deaf patients continue to encounter entrenched barriers in Canadian health care systems that compromise safety, trust, and health outcomes. We aimed to characterize the experiences of culturally Deaf community members with accessing and navigating health care, and to identify priorities for creating equitable and culturally safe care.Methods:We conducted a qualitative co-design study grounded in a critical–interpretive paradigm in partnership with Deaf Services Canada, a nonprofit organization that provides services to members of the Deaf community. We recruited participants from multiple regions across Ontario, Canada, including urban centres, rural communities, and northern Ontario. We recruited Deaf adults, family members, interpreters, and advocates, and conducted 8 focus groups using American Sign Language (ASL)–English interpreters. We rooted reflexive thematic analysis in the sociocultural model of deafness. We carried out coding and theme development collaboratively through iterative discussions, ensuring that the analytic process reflected diverse perspectives, reinforced trustworthiness, and remained accountable to experiences of the Deaf community.Results:We included 23 participants. A total of 14 identified as culturally Deaf and 9 identified as hearing, with overlapping roles as family members (n = 7), interpreters (n = 5), and advocates (n = 7). Five intersecting themes were generated: limited access to care is embedded in a system that privileges hearing and spoken language; structural barriers in appointment-booking processes reinforce exclusion; interpreting services are inconsistently valued and implemented, compromising communication; communication roadblocks within the medical team compromise equitable care; and agency and adaptive strategies persist despite systemic barriers.Interpretation:Deaf community experiences highlight how the privileging of spoken language and hearing norms operates both structurally and interpersonally to shape and constrain health care access and navigation. Embedding Deaf cultural responsivity in health-professional education, standardizing provision of interpreting services, and integrating culturally safe communication pathways are critical first steps toward advancing health equity for this underrecognized group.

In Canada, approximately 357 000 people identify as culturally Deaf, which is a distinct cultural and linguistic identity rather than a deficit requiring correction.1,2 Deaf patients communicate via sign language, and face structural and interpersonal barriers in health care settings, including inaccessible administrative processes, 3,4 limited access to interpreters,5 negative provider attitudes, 6 and inadequate accommodations.7–9 These barriers all contribute to lower health literacy10–12 and worse health outcomes in the Deaf population.13 Whereas Deaf individuals use primary care services more frequently than the general population, they encounter increased difficulty finding providers who are willing to accept them or arrange interpreting services.14

Deaf people’s rights to accessible health care communication are nevertheless grounded in Canadian law. In Eldridge v. British Columbia (Attorney General), the Supreme Court of Canada ruled that failure to ensure access to sign-language interpretation in publicly funded health care constitutes discrimination under section 15(1) of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms.15–17 Following this landmark decision in 1997, sign-language interpretation in Canadian health care is understood as a legally required component of equitable care. However, its implementation and service organization are largely determined at the provincial and territorial levels, leading to considerable variations in practice.18 Whereas British Columbia has developed a centralized, integrated framework for providing interpretation,19–21 jurisdictions such as Ontario and Quebec rely largely on community- and institution-level arrangements, resulting in variable service provision.22,23

Previous international studies have documented barriers to health care access for Deaf populations;24,25 however, little is known about the health care experiences of Deaf individuals in Canada. Much of the existing literature is framed through a medicalized view of deafness that does not recognize Deaf cultural identity and its implications for care.2 Barriers to care are often discussed without specifically examining the strategies Deaf communities use to navigate systems and structures that privilege hearing and spoken language. Moreover, guidance for health care providers in Canada remains sparse, and structural policy solutions are underdeveloped. Our study addressed these gaps by working in partnership with Deaf community members to examine their health care experiences in Ontario, Canada.

We sought to better understand Deaf individuals’ experiences of seeking and receiving health care; identify factors that contribute to their marginalization as an underrecognized group; and inform future efforts to improve cultural safety. Understanding the experiences of Deaf people in health care is essential to advancing health equity and improving health outcomes for and with the Deaf community.

Methods

We conducted a qualitative co-design study of culturally Deaf people’s experiences accessing health care services in Ontario.26 The co-design was operationalized as a multiphase, collaborative methodology that engaged individuals from diverse backgrounds in joint inquiry and problem exploration, with the aim of informing subsequent development of practical solutions.27 We conceptualized and conducted the study in partnership with Deaf Services Canada (formerly Silent Voice Canada), a nonprofit organization that provides services to members of the Deaf community. We employed a critical–interpretive paradigm, which views knowledge and reality as socially constructed and shaped by power relations, and applies social theories as analytic lenses to contextualize participants’ lived experiences and examine how meaning is produced from different sociocultural positions. 28,29 Our analytic approach was informed by the sociocultural model of deafness, which views deafness as a socially constructed and context-dependent experience shaped by structural inequities, systemic barriers, and power dynamics.30,31 Our reporting was guided by the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research.32

Study design

We recruited participants from 4 categories: adult individuals (age ≥ 18 yr) with congenital or early-acquired deafness (i.e., before first-language acquisition or prelingual) who identify as culturally Deaf, family members of culturally Deaf individuals, American Sign Language (ASL)–English and Deaf interpreters, and self-identified advocates for the culturally Deaf community (including leaders of advocacy organizations). Eligible participants had to be able to communicate in either ASL or English; ASL is a distinct visual–spatial language, independent of spoken English, with its own structure, grammar rules, and syntax, and uses facial expressions, body postures, hand shapes, and movements to convey meaning. We did not include participants who identified as hard-of-hearing or who had acquired hearing loss after first-language acquisition. Participants were prospectively recruited from Deaf Services Canada’s membership network and partnering agencies in Ontario, including the Ontario Association of the Deaf, Canadian Association of Sign Language Interpreters, Bob Rumball Canadian Centre of Excellence for the Deaf, PAH! Mental Health Services, and the Canadian Cultural Society of the Deaf. Recruitment was not contingent on participants’ health status or the nature of their prior health care experiences. The executive director (K.M.) emailed participants and obtained consent; potential participants were not employees of Deaf Services Canada, and no prior employment or supervisory relationship existed between K.M. and study participants. Participation was voluntary and uncompensated.

Demographic characteristics were collected via an online survey hosted on REDCap. We developed a semistructured interview guide (Appendix 1, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.251945/tab-related-content) that explored participants’ experiences with accessing and navigating the health care system either as culturally Deaf patients or as family members, sign-language interpreters, or community advocates. The guide was created based on a literature review and discussions with Deaf Services Canada representatives (K.M. and R.E.). We pilot tested the guide with representatives from each participant category to ensure clarity, relevance, and cultural appropriateness.

Our core team (B.B.L., K.T., and A.K.) conducted virtual focus groups, with 2 to 3 participants per group, from May to August 2023. Each participant took part in a single focus group, and all participants within a focus group belonged to the same participant category. We intentionally kept focus groups small, as the use of sign language requires additional time for communication. Smaller groups also allowed for in-depth exploration of shared and contrasting experiences and captured nuanced individual perspectives.33 Two certified ASL interpreters who did not participate in the study provided ASL–English interpretation and cross-checked interpretation accuracy. Author K.M. attended the focus groups to provide support with interpreter coordination and ASL-accessible consent processes as part of a trauma-informed approach, but did not participate in the questioning or discussions. All sessions were timed and were 60 minutes in length. Focus groups were video recorded and transcribed verbatim by an independent third-party transcription service. The core team manually reviewed transcripts against the original video recordings to ensure accuracy and completeness.

We recorded field notes during and immediately after each focus group to document contextual observations and early interpretive reflections. Data collection and analysis occurred iteratively. Thematic saturation was achieved when additional focus groups no longer contributed substantive new insights relevant to the study aims, but instead reinforced and deepened existing themes.34

Setting

We conducted the study in Ontario. We recruited participants from multiple regions across the province, including urban centres, rural communities, and northern Ontario. Geographic data were not collected. We used video conferencing software (Zoom) to facilitate participation across geographically diverse locations and support accessibility. All authors were based in Toronto, Ontario.

Data analysis

Authors B.B.L., K.T., A. Kapsack, A. Karim, and C.K. reviewed transcripts for conceptual coding. Using a reflexive approach to thematic analysis,35 we developed line-by-line data analysis and subsequent codes, categories, and themes. We generated a comprehensive codebook with themes and subthemes to organize the analysis while reflecting on new and recurring ideas gleaned from the data. We discussed diverging and converging ideas to come to an agreement on codes and themes. We ensured trustworthiness and rigour by inviting individuals with lived experiences to participate in the analysis, reviewing memos, and reflecting on our assumptions and biases. Our team reflected on our heterogeneous positionalities in the analytical discussions to ensure the findings authentically reflected Deaf experiences. We used NVivo 12 for data management.

Team composition and reflexivity

Our core team consisted of 3 resident physicians (B.B.L. [man], K.T. [woman], and A. Kapsack [woman]) who were medical students at the time of data collection. The core team completed a longitudinal placement with Deaf Services Canada and received extensive background training on Deaf culture and respectful communication practices. In addition to K.M., the extended team included a director of equity and social accountability at an academic tertiary care hospital (L.L.), the director of resource development services at Deaf Services Canada (R.E.), and 2 qualitative researchers (A. Karim and C.K.). Author R.E. identifies as culturally Deaf, and the other members of our team identify as hearing. We approached the project with a strong commitment to respectful, accountable, and collaborative engagement with the Deaf community.

Ethics approval

The Sunnybrook Health Sciences Centre Research Ethics Board approved this study (no. 5297).

Results

We held 8 virtual focus groups from May to August 2023. A total of 23 individuals volunteered, and all of them were included. Of the participants, 14 identified as culturally Deaf and 9 identified as hearing. Several participants had overlapping roles as family members of culturally Deaf individuals (n = 7), ASL–English interpreters (n = 5), and advocates for the culturally Deaf community (n = 7). The median age of participants was 41 (interquartile range 36 to 49) years, most identified as women (n = 15), and 9 identified as Black or as a person of colour. Nine participants used ASL exclusively, as identified on the demographic survey. Participants were recruited from multiple regions. Five interrelated themes were generated to reflect the various dimensions of Deaf patients’ journeys through the health care system (Table 3). Barriers unfolded across multiple points of contact and reflected how audism (i.e., a form of structural stigma rooted in the privileging of hearing and spoken language) can operate both interpersonally and structurally to shape and constrain access, communication, navigation, and patient experiences in health care.

Limited access to care is embedded in a system that privileges hearing and spoken language

For many participants, the first point of exclusion occurred before even entering the health care system. Members of the Deaf community struggled to find health care providers willing to care for them. Some experienced explicit refusals of care, which they attributed to providers’ discomfort or unwillingness to accommodate their communication needs. Deaf participants developed strategies to “screen” potential providers, reflecting how the burden of system navigation frequently shifted onto patients.

Mistrust was compounded by negative experiences during initial encounters, particularly when clinicians made assumptions or judgments about deafness as pathological. Participants noted that trust developed only over time and often depended on stable primary care relationships. Short-term encounters with specialists and in the emergency setting left little room to establish rapport, intensifying feelings of exclusion.

Structural barriers in appointment-booking processes reinforce exclusion

The appointment-booking process was a point of challenge. Most administrative systems are designed around phone-based communication, which excludes Deaf patients from making or modifying appointments independently. Many participants reported encounters with administrative staff who were unwilling or unable to book appointments via email or video interpretation. Participants described having to repeatedly explain how interpretation works and why they required alternative communication methods. Participants reported that some clinics were unwilling to provide interpreting services or accommodate longer appointments to support interpretation. These interactions were described as frustrating and underscored a lack of accessibility protocols at clinics and hospitals. Participants perceived that time pressures, lack of reimbursement mechanisms for physicians, and administrative burden led providers to deprioritize Deaf patients.

Interpreting services are inconsistently valued and implemented, compromising communication

Access to qualified interpreting services was inconsistent and unreliable, with interpretation frequently treated as a courtesy rather than a right. Participants described repeatedly reminding staff to book an interpreter and arriving to appointments without one present. Interpreter availability and logistics posed further challenges. Participants described limited interpreter supply, cancellations due to scheduling conflicts, and service backlogs due to delayed or lengthy appointments. One participant shared a positive experience within a city-based hospital network where the presence of in-house interpreters was perceived as making access more consistent; however, in emergency situations, the inability to access interpreters in a timely manner was seen as a detriment to communication.

Interpreters also varied in their medical-language proficiency. Participants shared that interpreters sometimes relied on on-the-job learning, which increased miscommunication, especially for patients using region-specific sign language. Clinicians sometimes defaulted to using family members as interpreters, compromising information accuracy, privacy, and patient autonomy.

Communication roadblocks with the medical team compromise equitable care

Participants reported encountering multiple communication barriers in clinical settings. Some were left in waiting rooms unaware their names had been called, as staff relied exclusively on verbal announcements. Others described frustration from administrative staff who did not know how to engage with Deaf patients.

One participant described a positive experience in which her limited availability as an interpreter was recognized, enabling an appointment to proceed in a timely manner. However, most clinical encounters reflected health care providers’ limited awareness regarding the variable health literacy and cultural and linguistic diversity among Deaf patients. Participants shared that newcomers to Canada who use different sign languages or come from non-English-speaking countries found health information difficult to access. Participants acknowledged that interim communication tools such as written notes, text-based communication, and text-to-speech applications were more accessible and often used in constrained circumstances when interpreters were unavailable. Nevertheless, these approaches were consistently described as inadequate and inappropriate substitutes for sign-language interpretation. Reliance on written communication was perceived to introduce risk of misunderstanding and exacerbate inequities for Deaf patients with lower English literacy or for whom English was not a first language.

Participants described that technology introduced new challenges. Participants reported inadequate hospital infrastructure, logistical delays, and unreliable Internet connections. Some lacked access to the necessary devices or data, and interpreters reported that remote platforms compromised the nuances of medical communication.

Agency and adaptive strategies persist despite systemic barriers

Deaf participants actively adapted to systemic exclusions. They reported strategic behaviours such as prescreening providers, educating administrative staff and clinicians, advocating for their rights to interpreters, and developing personal networks of accessible care providers to navigate existing health care structures. A small number of participants, particularly advocates for the culturally Deaf community, demonstrated awareness of relevant legal protections. Many described becoming experts in navigating systems not designed for them. While participants often had to advocate for their communication needs, some accounts illustrated how such advocacy could lead to meaningful clinician learning and changes in practice.

While acts of self-advocacy reflect resilience and community strength, participants recognized that individual efforts alone cannot overcome systemic inequities, highlighting the limits of personal agency in the absence of institutional accountability, robust accessibility policies and practices, and provider education.

Interpretation

We conducted our qualitative co-design study of culturally Deaf people’s health care access and navigation experiences in partnership with a nonprofit agency that provides services to the Deaf community in Canada. The 5 interrelated themes that we derived from our research suggest that systemic audism can influence Deaf individuals’ health care experiences from the initial point of access to the clinical encounter itself. Our findings emphasize the importance of Deaf cultural identity and demonstrate how both interpersonal and structural audism can intersect to produce inequitable experiences. We also documented the strategies that Deaf individuals use to navigate barriers, underscoring both agency and its limits within health care systems. These strategies reflect the resilience of Deaf participants but also reveal how the responsibility for access has been displaced onto patients, beyond the level of engagement typically expected within person-centred, team-based models of care. Systemic solutions must therefore involve Deaf communities to address current gaps in policy, service implementation, and communication infrastructure.

Framing deafness as a deficit or impairment can obscure its cultural dimensions.36,37 Our findings align with Deaf scholars’ arguments that Deaf people should be recognized as a cultural–linguistic minority rather than as disabled patients requiring accommodation.31,38 This perspective shifts the responsibility from individuals and emphasizes the responsibility of institutional systems and structures. Inequities seem inevitable when health care systems view communication access as an accommodation rather than a right. Thus, medical education and health policy should incorporate education on Deaf cultural safety to help address systemic inequities in care. A survey of physicians and medical students found that, although awareness of potential communication barriers exists, exposure to Deaf individuals and culture is lacking.39 Indeed, our participants shared that many negative encounters stemmed from providers’ lack of cultural knowledge and humility, which demonstrates the necessity for training.

Many studies of Deaf experiences in health care have represented inequities as communication gaps.5,9,40–42 Participants described a system where spoken language was the default mode of interaction; structures such as inaccessible booking systems and interpreter scarcity positioned Deaf individuals’ communication needs as exceptional, rather than fundamental to equitable care. Our findings build on prior work by suggesting that inequities may also be shaped by broader institutional norms and structures that privilege hearing and spoken language. Provider avoidance and discomfort may reflect not only knowledge gaps, but also the normalization of values and practices that exclude Deaf patients. Recognizing audism as a health-equity issue is therefore a necessary step to improve accessibility and health care delivery. Moreover, access challenges for Deaf individuals may be magnified by other forms of structural and interpersonal stigma. Accounts describing reliance on written English communication or digital technology when interpreters were unavailable suggest that Deaf identity may intersect with English literacy level, immigration status, or geographic location in shaping health care access. This highlights the importance of future research to better understand the intersecting stigmas that affect Deaf patients’ health care experiences.

Our findings can be compared with the challenges faced by others who may prefer or need to communicate in a language other than English (including immigrants, refugees, and Indigenous people), as these groups all contend with access and communication barriers that can compromise health care experiences and outcomes.43–45 Qualitative work with immigrants in Canada with limited English proficiency highlights overlapping challenges, including delays in accessing health care services, difficulty forming therapeutic relationships, lack of effective communication, and problematic involvement of ad hoc interpreters (e.g., family members).43 Consistent with data demonstrating worse health outcomes among Deaf populations,13 linked census data from the Canadian Institute for Health Information demonstrate that people in Canada who do not speak English or French have a 30% higher rate of harmful events during hospital admission than those who speak an official language. 46 Retrospective data from Ontario further show that these patients have significantly higher risks of emergency department visits, hospital admissions, and death when receiving language-discordant primary care.47 Whereas these challenges and their consequences are shared, our study highlights several barriers specific to Deaf patients. The pathologization of deafness reinforced expectations that Deaf people should adapt to a health care system that privileges hearing and spoken language, including through the normalization of inadequate substitutes for sign-language interpretation that fail to meet Deaf patients’ needs. Effective communication for Deaf patients depends on access to visual language modalities such as in-person sign-language interpretation, which cannot be substituted by the telephone-based services often used for spoken language. Whereas spoken-language interpretation is an unregulated industry with heterogeneous standards across Canada,44 sign-language interpreters are required to complete interpreter education programs recognized by the Canadian Association of Sign Language Interpreters.48

Our findings suggest that the delivery of sign-language interpretation is inconsistent within Ontario. In many settings, culturally Deaf patients experience variable access to interpretation services that do not reliably meet their communication needs or provide culturally safe care. Despite national legal precedents such as Eldridge v. British Columbia (Attorney General),15 current provision of medical interpreting services varies substantially across Canadian provinces, reflecting differences in funding models and availability.44 With no province-wide coverage in Ontario and Quebec, individual hospitals and institutions develop and fund interpreting services through their own operating budgets, with local health authorities assuming this responsibility in some jurisdictions.44 In contrast, British Columbia has a centralized framework for providing interpretation via Provincial Language Services, which offers sign language and Deaf interpreters across multiple settings.19,49 Manitoba similarly operates centralized Language Access Interpreter Services via its provincial health authority.50 Other provinces, including Alberta, New Brunswick, and Nova Scotia, support access to sign-language interpretation via health authority–coordinated or regionally delivered models.51–53

Suggestions for improving health care access and navigation for and with the Deaf community

Addressing gaps and barriers will require organization-led knowledge translation and co-design processes in partnership with Deaf communities. We propose 3 initial steps: integrating Deaf cultural-safety training into health-professional education, strengthening and standardizing interpreting services, and adopting inclusive communication technologies and practices.

Integrating Deaf cultural-safety training into health-professional education

Audism awareness, the sociocultural model of deafness, and effective communication with Deaf patients should be included as standard components of curricula for health professionals. Such education should be co-produced with and involve Deaf community members. Training may include practical instruction on protocols for scheduling interpreters and respectful communication strategies. Training should extend beyond clinicians to include front-line staff, including those responsible for patient registration and appointment scheduling. Such education can serve to increase knowledge and critical reflection, reduce attitudes and practices that perpetuate audism, and foster more culturally safe clinical encounters. Building familiarity with best practices can minimize reliance on ad hoc workarounds for Deaf patients.

Strengthening and standardizing interpreting services

Reliable access to qualified interpreters is essential to equitable and culturally safe care for the Deaf community. Health care organizations should work together with Deaf community members to create policies that mirror language-access standards applied to other linguistic minority groups, with Deaf patients offered a choice in interpretation modality. Organizations should also co-produce and implement clear protocols to ensure interpreters are consistently available for both scheduled and unscheduled encounters. This includes mechanisms for advanced booking for routine visits, as well as rapid deployment for urgent and emergent care. Investments in expanding the pool of qualified sign-language interpreters may also help reduce cancellations from scheduling conflicts and prevent service backlogs. Costs of interpreting services should be included in operational budgets, rather than treated as ancillary expenditures to be covered by patients or community agencies.

Adopting inclusive communication technologies and practices

Administrative processes such as appointment booking and patient inquiries should include accessible alternatives to telephone-based systems, including secure email and text messaging options. Although available technologies, including teletypewriter services and speech-to-text applications, may offer temporary stop-gap measures for Deaf patients in exceptional circumstances, they should not be thought of as substitutes for qualified sign-language interpreters. Within clinical settings, visual alert or haptic feedback systems should be implemented to notify Deaf patients when they are called in waiting areas. Patient education materials and informed consent documentation should be made available in ASL in view of English health literacy barriers faced by the Deaf community. Importantly, Deaf people should be meaningfully engaged in the design and evaluation of patient-facing digital tools and education materials to ensure accessibility measures reflect lived experience and community priorities.

Limitations

Our study reflects the perspectives of culturally Deaf community members in Ontario; however, it may not capture the full diversity of all Deaf populations or be generalizable to health care systems in other provinces and territories. As our study included only participants who were able to communicate in ASL or English, we did not capture the perspectives of francophone Deaf individuals and users of Quebec Sign Language or other signed languages. Deaf newcomers to Canada who use different sign languages or come from non-English-speaking countries were also less likely to be represented in our sample. Deaf individuals who face challenges with technology access or unreliable Internet connectivity may have also been less likely to participate. Although recruitment was not contingent on participants’ health status or the nature of their prior health care experiences, it is conceivable that individuals with negative health care experiences or who were already engaged in advocacy efforts were more inclined to participate. Recruitment via Deaf Services Canada community networks may have introduced selection bias. However, a balance of perspectives and data saturation were achieved through broad recruitment from multiple independent Deaf-serving agencies across Ontario and wide sociodemographic diversity among our participants. Our generated themes are grounded in the lived experiences of diverse key partners within the Deaf community, and biases were mitigated via reflexivity and the use of robust co-design methodology.

Conclusion

Our qualitative co-design study of the experiences of culturally Deaf people with health care access and navigation in Ontario demonstrates how the privileging of hearing and spoken language can shape Deaf individuals’ access, communication, and experiences across multiple stages of their health care journey. Five themes highlight an environment that may lead to misunderstanding of Deaf patients’ rights to accessible and equitable health care, resulting in substandard accommodations and perpetuating harms and inequities that may compromise care. Deaf participants’ strategies of adaptation demonstrated individual and community strength and the limits of individual agency. Advancing health equity and outcomes among Deaf people will require embracing a sociocultural approach that embeds linguistic accessibility as a core standard of equitable, culturally safe, and patient-centred care.
