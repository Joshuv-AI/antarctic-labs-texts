Min-Max Optimization with Dual-Linear Coupling
Ronak Mehta, Jelena Diakonikolas, Zaid Harchaoui

Abstract
We study a class of convex-concave min-max problems in which the coupled component of the objective is linear in at least one of the two decision vectors. We identify such problem structure as interpolating between the bilinearly and nonbilinearly coupled problems, motivated by key applications in areas such as distributionally robust optimization and convex optimization with functional constraints. Leveraging the considered nonlinear-linear coupling of the primal and the dual decision vectors, we develop a general algorithmic framework leading to fine-grained complexity bounds exploiting separability properties of the problem, whenever present. The obtained complexity bounds offer potential improvements over state-of-the-art scaling with n \sqrt{n} or n n in some of the considered problem settings, which even include bilinearly coupled problems, where n n is the dimension of the dual decision vector. On the algorithmic front, our work provides novel strategies for combining randomization with extrapolation and multi-point anchoring in the mirror descent-style updates in the primal and the dual, which we hope will find further applications in addressing related optimization problems.

Min-max (a.k.a. saddle point) optimization represents a fundamental class of problems with long history in optimization and game theory, spanning a plethora of applications in machine learning and operations research

In their general form, min-max optimization problems ask for solving

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\max_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\ c(\bm{x},\bm{y})-\psi(\bm{y})+\phi(\bm{x}),

(1)

General convex-concave problems of the form (

However, the broad applicability of these results comes at the cost of possibly missed opportunities for further speed improvements when the coupled component

On the other side of the spectrum, splitting primal-dual methods specialized to problems with a bilinearly coupled component such as the primal-dual hybrid gradient (PDHG) method of

Applegate et al. 2025

. The question that then arises is whether the success of splitting primal-dual methods can be transferred to objectives with more general primal-dual coupling.

The starting point of our work is the observation that in many applications of core practical interest, the coupled component of the objective is neither bilinear nor fully nonbilinear. In particular, a wide range of problems studied within the framework of distributionally robust optimization (DRO), both for supervised learning

1.1
Technical Motivation

To motivate the usefulness of specialized algorithms for (
2
), consider treating it generically as a nonbilinearly-coupled saddle-point problem (or more generally still as a variational inequality (VI) problem). To quantify the resulting complexity, let
\lambda
be the Lipschitz parameter of
(\bm{x},\bm{y})\mapsto(\nabla_{\bm{x}}\mathcal{L},-\nabla_{\bm{y}}\mathcal{L})
, when it exists. Classical approaches such as
(
Korpelevich 1976
;
Popov. 1980
;
Nemirovski 2004
;
Nesterov 2007a
)
, and their linearly convergent variants
(
Nesterov and Scrimali 2006
;
Marcotte 1991
;
Tseng 1995
;
Mokhtari et al. 2020
;
Le Thi Thanh Hai and Vuong 2025
)
will achieve a total arithmetic complexity of

\displaystyle\tilde{O}\left(nd\min\left\{\tfrac{\lambda}{\varepsilon},\tfrac{\lambda}{\mu\wedge\nu}\ln\left(\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)\right\}\right),

to achieve a primal-dual gap bounded above by
\varepsilon>0
(see (
8
),
Section

3
), where we assume that the per-iteration complexity is
\tilde{O}(nd)
and we interpret
\lambda/0:=+\infty
.

Furthermore, using generic acceleration Catalyst schemes
(
Lin et al. 2018
)
adapted for min-max problems, improved complexity bounds can be obtained.
Yang et al. 2020
achieve an
\tilde{O}(nd\lambda/\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon})
runtime in the strongly convex-concave regime.
Recently
Lan and Li 2023
obtained a complexity of
\tilde{O}(nd\lambda/\sqrt{\mu\nu}\ln\left(\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\right))
for strongly convex-strongly concave problems.
A disadvantage of classical approaches that is overcome by the Catalyst approach is the dependence on the minimum of the two strong convexity constants. In the examples above,
\psi
often plays the role of a strongly convex smoothing penalty whose strong convexity constant may be near-zero (e.g.,
O(\varepsilon)
for some applications). Thus, guarantees depending on
(\mu\wedge\nu)
are undesirable.
A second disadvantage incurred by all of the approaches above is the dependence on a coarse Lipschitz constant
\lambda
for the entire vector field. When it is only known that each component function
f_{j}
is
G_{\max{}}
-Lipschitz continuous and
M_{\max{}}
-smooth, and that
\left\lvert y_{j}\right\rvert\leq D_{\max{}}
for any
\bm{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\in\mathcal{Y}
, then
\lambda
can be estimated as
n(G_{\max{}}+D_{\max{}}M_{\max{}})
. Combined with the order-
nd
per-iteration cost, the total arithmetic complexity can have an
O(n^{2}d)
dependence on the dimensions
(n,d)
.
These two issues highlight the price to pay when using generic acceleration schemes: the loss of adaptation to the structure of the problem and the related constants. In contrast, by leveraging non-uniformity in the various Lipschitz/smoothness constants for each
f_{j}
and randomized updates, we may achieve complexities that are linear in
(n+d)
.

Moreover, the Lipschitz continuity and smoothness parameters of the component functions represent quantities of practical interest in applications, which we review via the examples in
Section

2
. In
Example

1
, if we assume that
f_{j}(\bm{x})=\ell(\bm{z}_{j}^{\top}\bm{x})
for a univariate loss function
\ell
and data instance
\bm{z}_{j}
, then under appropriate conditions on
\ell
, the continuity properties of
f_{j}
will depend on the norm of
\bm{z}_{j}
. In
Example

2
, we have that
f(\bm{x})=(h(\bm{x}),\bm{x})
for some
\mathbb{R}^{n-d}
-valued function
h
so that
f_{n-d+1},\ldots,f_{n}
are
1
-Lipschitz continuous and
0
-smooth; a complexity result that only depends on the
n
times the maxima of these constants over the components of
f
could be overly pessimistic.
In our analysis, we handle every combination of
\mu=0
versus
\mu>0
and
\nu=0
versus
\nu>0
to achieve dependences on
\varepsilon
in a unified way, with complexity bounds than enjoy a transparent dependence on these component-wise Lipschitz and smoothness constants.
The dependence on component-wise problem constants has been shown in recent works on stochastic variance-reduced methods for variational inequality problems
(
Alacaoglu and Malitsky 2022
;
Cai et al. 2024
;
Pichugin et al. 2024
;
Alizadeh et al. 2024
;
Diakonikolas 2025
)
, for which we offer a detailed technical comparison in
Section

6
.

1.2
Contributions

We study the dual linear min-max problem (
2
) as an intermediary between bilinearly and nonbilinearly coupled objectives. We provide a general template for a first-order proximal gradient algorithm motivated by the analysis and instantiate it in three forms: a full vector update variant, a stochastic variant for generic objectives, and a variant that utilizes “separable” structure in the objective and feasible set.
We prove convergence rates for algorithms for this problem class, with a transparent dependence on all individual problem parameters, including individual smoothness and strong convexity constants.
With regards to the suboptimality parameter
\varepsilon>0
, we achieve the standard
O(1/\varepsilon)
and
O(\ln(1/\varepsilon))
complexities for the convex-concave and strongly convex-strongly concave settings, and
O(1/\sqrt{\varepsilon})
in the strongly convex-concave setting.
Regarding the dimension dependence, we achieve up to an
\sqrt{n}
factor improvement over the recent methods of
Alacaoglu and Malitsky 2022
and
Cai et al. 2024

by leveraging additional problem structure in the convex-concave case.
Furthermore, while motivated to solve problems more general than bilinear coupled min-max problems, our algorithm achieves complexity improvements in more specific classes, including matrix games. For non-separable matrix games (see
Definition

1
), we may improve over state-of-the-art methods by a
\sqrt{n}
-factor, and an up to
n
improvement in separable cases when the coupling matrix is dense and square.
The main technical ingredient of the algorithm is a combination of non-uniform sampling for randomized extrapolation and non-uniform weighting of multiple prox anchors/centers (which we refer to as “historical regularization”) within mirror descent-style updates.
The randomized extrapolation component can also be interpreted as a variance reduction method for objectives with sum-decomposable coupled components. Notably, the historical regularization component, outlined in the upcoming
Algorithm

1
, overcomes a barrier that was previously not handled by non-uniform sampling alone
(
Chambolle et al. 2018
;
Song et al. 2021
;
Alacaoglu et al. 2022a
)
.

1.3
Related Work

Among methods similar to ours for general nonbilinearly-coupled objectives,
Hamedani and Aybat 2021
achieve an
O(1/\varepsilon)
iteration complexity for the convex-concave case using a similar extrapolation term to ours with full vector updates, which can be seen as a generalization of iterate extrapolation in Chambolle-Pock-style algorithms
(
Chambolle and Pock 2011
;
Chambolle and Pock 2016
)
. Interestingly, they also consider the dual linear case in which
\phi
is strongly convex, matching our
O(1/\sqrt{\varepsilon})
complexity in this case. Beyond the dependence on the error parameter
\varepsilon
, a key focus of our work is achieving improved, fine-grained, complexity with stochastic algorithms, dependent on Lipschitz continuity and smoothness constants of individual component functions. When the coupled term is expressible as a finite sum of component functions (as in (
2
)),
Hamedani and Aybat 2021
also apply an SVRG-style variance reduction scheme
(
Johnson and Zhang 2013
)
to achieve an
O(n\varepsilon^{-1/2}+\varepsilon^{-3/2})
iteration complexity. By leveraging non-uniformity of the various Lipschitz/smoothness constants of the component functions, our
O(1/\varepsilon)
iteration complexity (independent of
n
) in the convex-concave regime improves over these results. One challenge of our approach in more specialized problem classes, such as
Example

3
, is the possible unboundedness of the dual domain in the non-strongly concave regime. Issues such as these have been addressed by line search/adaptive step size methods, which were implemented in
Hamedani and Aybat 2021
as well as in randomized block coordinate-wise algorithms in
Hamedani et al. 2023
. Further discussions on both classical and recent works are provided in
Section

6
.

We also comment that a subset of technical ideas we develop in this manuscript appeared in a preliminary NeurIPS conference paper written by the same authors
(
Mehta et al. 2024a
)
. Compared to this prior work, the common theme consists in using a primal-dual method featuring a multiple prox-center scheme (i.e., historical regularization) and a randomized updating scheme akin to SAGA-style variance reduction
(
Defazio et al. 2014
)
. In contrast, the algorithmic framework from this work allows for importance-based sampling and employs randomized (instead of cyclic) updates to gradient and function value tables common to SAGA-style variance-reduction approaches, further allowing for more general choices of prox-centers. These properties of our new algorithmic framework are crucial for establishing more fine-grained complexity bounds, leading to the aforementioned complexity improvements over state of the art (including our aforementioned past work).

\displaystyle=m(\bm{z}^{+})+\Delta_{h}(\bm{u},\bm{z}^{+})+a\Delta_{g}(\bm{u},\bm{z}^{+})+\frac{A\gamma+\gamma_{0}}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{r}w_{i}\Delta_{\Delta_{\varphi}(\cdot,\bm{z}_{i})}(\bm{u},\bm{z}^{+})

\displaystyle=m(\bm{z}^{+})+\Delta_{h}(\bm{u},\bm{z}^{+})+a\Delta_{g}(\bm{u},\bm{z}^{+})+\frac{A\gamma+\gamma_{0}}{2}\Delta_{\varphi}(\bm{u},\bm{z}^{+}).

Use
\Delta_{h}(\bm{u},\bm{z}^{+})\geq 0
and then relative strong convexity
a\Delta_{g}(\bm{u},\bm{z}^{+})\geq a\gamma\Delta_{\varphi}(\bm{u},\bm{z}^{+})
to prove the desired result.
∎

Henceforth, we use the notation
\Delta_{\mathcal{X}}(\cdot,\cdot)
to denote the Bregman divergence on
\mathcal{X}

\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}
and that satisfies

(i.e.,

\Delta_{\mathcal{X}}
). As an example, this is satisfied by
\Delta_{\mathcal{X}}:=\Delta_{\phi/\mu}
for
\mu>0

\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}(\cdot,\cdot)
analogously.
As a technical consideration, we also assume that the unique solution of (

\operatorname{ri}(\operatorname*{dom}(\varphi))
, which is satisfied for common choices of the generator
\varphi
.
Finally, we introduce additional structure on the problem (

) which can be exploited when available.

Definition 1
.

We call
\mathcal{L}
a
dual-separable objective
if the dual component decomposes as
\psi(\bm{y})=\sum_{J=1}^{N}\psi_{J}(\bm{y}_{J})
where
\bm{y}_{J}
denotes the components of
\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
corresponding to the indices in block
B_{J}
. We call
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
a
dual-separable feasible set
if
\mathcal{Y}=\mathcal{Y}_{1}\times\ldots\times\mathcal{Y}_{N}
and
\bm{y}_{J}\in\mathcal{Y}_{J}
for
J=1,\ldots,N
. We call the problem (
2
) a
dual-separable problem
if its objective and feasible set are both dual-separable.

Dual-separability of the objective is commonly satisfied, such as when
\psi
represents an
\ell_{2}
or negative entropy penalty.
In this case, we have that
\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}(\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime}):=\sum_{J=1}^{N}\Delta_{J}(\bm{y}_{J},\bm{y}^{\prime}_{J})
, where each
\Delta_{J}(\cdot,\cdot)
is a Bregman divergence on
\operatorname*{dom}(\psi_{J})\times\operatorname{ri}(\operatorname*{dom}(\psi_{J}))
. As before,
\psi
is relatively
\nu
-strongly convex with respect to
\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
.
In later sections, we may also use the subscript on
\bm{y}_{k}\in\mathcal{Y}
to denote a particular time index of an algorithm, as opposed to the block index
J
on
\bm{y}_{J}\in\mathcal{Y}_{J}
; the difference will be clear from context. Dual-separability of the feasible set is a less common assumption. It is not satisfied, for instance, on simplicial domains such as the one in Example 1. Based on this observation,
Section

4
and
Section

5
are dedicated to the proposed algorithms for non-separable and separable problems, respectively.

We conslude this section with a discussion of example problems that can be addressed in the considered framework of dual linear min-max optimization problems.

Example 1

(Distributionally robust optimization (DRO))
.

The DRO problem can be written as

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{y}\geq 0\\
\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{y}=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}y_{j}f_{j}(\bm{x})-\psi(\bm{y})+\phi(\bm{x}),

where
\bm{x}
represents the parameters of a predictive model, each
f_{j}
represents the loss incurred on training example
j
, and
\phi
is a regularizer (e.g., the
\ell_{2}
-norm). The dual vector
\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
denotes a collection of weights in the
(n-1)
-probability simplex and
\psi(\bm{y})
penalizes
\bm{y}
from shifting too far from the original uniform weights
\bm{1}/n=(1/n,\ldots,1/n)
over the training set. This can either be a soft penalty (e.g.,
\psi(\bm{y})
is the Kullback-Leibler divergence between
\bm{y}
and
\bm{1}/n
) or a hard penalty that further constrains the dual feasible set.

Example 2

(Fully composite optimization)
.

The fully composite optimization problem is defined by

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}F(h(\bm{x}),\bm{x}),

where
h:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n-d}
is component-wise convex and
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is closed and convex. It is assumed that
h
is smooth and “hard” to compute, whereas
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
may be non-differentiable but “easy” to compute. This formulation can be viewed as a generalization of the typical additive composite problem in which
n-d=1
and
F(h(\bm{x}),\bm{x})=h(\bm{x})+g(\bm{x})
for a smooth component
h
and non-smooth component
g
. By taking the Fenchel conjugate
F^{*}(\bm{y}):=\sup_{\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathcal{X}}\left\langle\bm{z},\bm{y}\right\rangle-F(\bm{z})
of
F
, we achieve the formulation (
2
) with
f(\bm{x}):=(h(\bm{x}),\bm{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\psi(\bm{y})=F^{*}(\bm{y})
, and
\phi(\bm{x})=0
. To ensure that the overall problem is convex-concave we also assume that
F(\cdot,\bm{x})
is monotone in that
\bm{u}\leq\bm{v}\text{ element-wise }\implies F(\bm{u},\bm{x})\leq F(\bm{v},\bm{x})
.

Example 3

(Problems with functional constraints)
.

Consider the classical convex minimization problem with constraints defined by sublevel sets of convex functions

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\phi(\bm{x})\text{ s.t. }f_{j}(\bm{x})\leq 0\text{ for all }j=1,\ldots,n.

Then, the Lagrangian formulation yields the expression (
2
) by letting
\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
denote the Lagrange multipliers and setting
\psi\equiv 0
. The objective (
2
) also encompasses the related setting of “soft” functional constraints, where we set
\psi
as any
\nu
-strongly convex function
\psi
with
\nu>0
to produce a faster convergence rate at an approximation cost governed by the parameter
\nu
.
In this case, the primal solution resulting from this smoothed problem may only approximately satisfy the functional constraints.

Example 4

(Maximal eigenvalue minimization)
.

Given a collection of
d
symmetric matrices
\bm{A}_{1},\ldots,\bm{A}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
, the classical problem of minimizing the maximal eigenvalue
\lambda_{\max{}}(\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}\bm{A}_{i})
in
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be formulated
(
Nesterov 2007b
;
Baes et al. 2013
)
as the saddle-point problem

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\max_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\operatorname{tr}\left((\bm{A}_{i}x_{i})\bm{y}\right),

where
\mathcal{Y}
is the set of positive semi-definite matrices satisfying
\operatorname{tr}(\bm{y})=1
and
\mathcal{X}
is any convex, compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Here,
n=m^{2}
depends quadratically on the height/width of the matrices
(\bm{A}_{i})_{i=1}^{d}
, and blocks may naturally correspond to matrix structure, such as rows or columns. This constitutes a non-separable matrix game, a specific problem class discussed in
Section

6
.

3
Our Method and a General Analysis Template

We consider an algorithm to be a sequence of primal-dual iterates
(\bm{x}_{k},\bm{y}_{k})_{k\geq 0}
,
with fixed initial point
(\bm{x}_{0},\bm{y}_{0})
. This sequence may be random, in which case the relevant probabilistic information is introduced when we analyze stochastic algorithms. As is standard for primal-dual algorithm of the same type
(
Chambolle and Pock 2011
;
Alacaoglu et al. 2020
;
Song et al. 2021
)
, the analysis tracks the
primal-dual gap
function at fixed
\bm{u}\in\mathcal{X}
and
\bm{v}\in\mathcal{Y}
:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Gap}^{\bm{u},\bm{v}}(\bm{x},\bm{y}):=\mathcal{L}(\bm{x},\bm{v})-\mathcal{L}(\bm{u},\bm{y}).

(8)

To measure suboptimality in the strongly convex case (when
\mu>0
and
\nu>0
), we set
\bm{u}=\bm{x}_{\star}
and
\bm{v}=\bm{y}_{\star}
, where
(\bm{x}_{\star},\bm{y}_{\star})
denotes the unique saddle point of (
2
), satisfying

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\bm{x}_{\star},\bm{y})+\frac{\nu}{2}\left\|{\bm{y}}-{\bm{y}}_{\star}\right\|_{\mathcal{Y}}\leq\mathcal{L}(\bm{x}_{\star},\bm{y}_{\star})\leq\mathcal{L}(\bm{x},\bm{y}_{\star})-\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|{\bm{x}}-{\bm{x}}_{\star}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}

for all
(\bm{x},\bm{y})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
. When
\mu=\nu=0
, we measure suboptmality using the supremum of (possibly the expectation of) (
8
) over
\bm{u}\in\mathcal{U}
and
\bm{v}\in\mathcal{V}
for compact sets
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
. When
\mu=0
or
\nu=0
exclusively, we naturally take the supremum over
\bm{u}
or
\bm{v}
exclusively.

We first fix
\bm{u},\bm{v}
and aim to show that
\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}\operatorname{Gap}^{\bm{u},\bm{v}}(\bm{x}_{t},\bm{y}_{t})\leq 0
(possibly in expectation), with a convergence rate in terms of the problem constants from
Section

2
and iteration count
t\geq 0
. To this end, we introduce an averaging sequence
(a_{k})_{k\geq 1}
of positive constants with
a_{0}=0
, and their aggregation
A_{t}:=\sum_{k=0}^{t}a_{k}
, and then pursue an upper bound of the form

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{t}a_{k}\operatorname{Gap}^{\bm{u},\bm{v}}(\bm{x}_{k},\bm{y}_{k})\leq G_{0}(\bm{u},\bm{v}),

(9)

where
G_{0}(\bm{u},\bm{v})
is a constant independent of
t
, so that when dividing by
A_{t}
, the average expected gap decays at rate
A_{t}^{-1}
. Accordingly, we wish for
A_{t}
to grow as fast as possible with
t
. By way of convexity, we have that
\operatorname{Gap}^{\bm{u},\bm{v}}(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t},\tilde{\bm{y}}_{t})\leq G_{0}(\bm{u},\bm{v})/A_{t}
for
(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t},\tilde{\bm{y}}_{t}):=A_{t}^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{t}a_{k}(\bm{x}_{k},\bm{y}_{k})
, which can be returned by the algorithm to realize the gap bound (
9
). We may conclude by taking the supremum of
G_{0}(\bm{u},\bm{v})
. In the randomized scenarios, we also discuss in
Section

6
how the analysis can be adapted to an even stronger convergence criterion for which the supremum is taken prior to the expectation. Because many of the technical ideas remain similar when proving convergence with respect to this stronger criterion, we describe only the parts that change in
Section

6
.

It is the combination of

For the separable case, our proof technique relies on an auxiliary sequence of dual variables that offers an elegant extension of previous meta-analyses when using coordinate-wise updates.

3.1
Algorithm Template and a First Gap Bound

We fix the convention that the update for
\bm{x}_{k}
occurs before the update for
\bm{y}_{k}
. Thus, we require that only information available up to and including time
k-1
is used in the update. In the stochastic setting, this requirement will be formalized in the language of measurability. Both the primal and dual updates will resemble those of a proximal gradient-type algorithm, wherein
\bm{x}_{k}
and
\bm{y}_{k}
are defined by minimizing or maximizing an approximation of (
2
) with a proximity term (i.e. a Bregman divergence). In the primal update, the proximity term promotes
\bm{x}_{k}
being close to not only
\bm{x}_{k-1}
, but several additional to-be-specified comparison points
\hat{\bm{x}}_{k-1,1},\ldots,\hat{\bm{x}}_{k-1,N}
. Similarly,
\bm{y}_{k}
will be made close to
\bm{y}_{k-1}
along with a single comparison point
\hat{\bm{y}}_{k-1}
. The use of multiple comparison points is in fact the motivation for the modified three-point inequality (
Lemma

1
). The remaining components to specify are
\bar{\bm{g}}_{k-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, a vector which will be used to linearize the objective (
2
) in the primal update, and
\bar{\bm{f}}_{k-1/2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, an analogous vector used in the dual update. The subscript
k-1/2
indicates that “half” of the information in iteration
k
(namely, the value of
\bm{x}_{k}
) can be used in the update. The components introduced so far generate a template algorithm which can combine them in various ways; the pseudocode for this method is shown in
Algorithm

1
, in which the algorithm-specific content is abstracted into three subroutines.

The hyperparameters are shown explicitly to better accompany the theoretical analysis. They are set to specific values over the course of the proofs.
We motivate the updates (
10
) and (
11
) using a lower bound on
a_{k}\mathcal{L}(\bm{u},\bm{y}_{k})
and an upper bound on
a_{k}\mathcal{L}(\bm{x}_{k},\bm{v})
. Several terms will appear that either telescope when summed or are used to cancel errors incurred at each iteration. For the reader’s convenience, we summarize this notation below.

Notation:
Throughout the analyses for each algorithm, the primal and dual “distance-to-opt” terms will be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{P}}_{k}:=(A_{k}\mu+\mu_{0})\Delta_{\mathcal{X}}(\bm{u},\bm{x}_{k}),\quad\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{D}}_{k}:=(A_{k}\nu+\nu_{0})\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}(\bm{u},\bm{y}_{k}),

(12)

indicating that they telescope when summed.
The bounds also produce the negation of the terms

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{P}}_{k}:=(A_{k-1}\mu+\mu_{0})\Delta_{\mathcal{X}}(\bm{x}_{k},\bm{x}_{k-1}),\quad\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{D}}_{k}:=(A_{k-1}\nu+\nu_{0})\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}(\bm{y}_{k},\bm{y}_{k-1}),

(13)

which appear in the gap function bound and are used for canceling errors that appear when controlling the primal-dual gap. Analogous terms appear based on the comparison points instead of the iterates. That is, consider for each
I\in[N]

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{\mathrm{P}}_{k,I}:=(A_{k}\mu+\mu_{0})\Delta_{\mathcal{X}}(\bm{u},\hat{\bm{x}}_{k,I}),\quad\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{\mathrm{D}}_{k}:=(A_{k}\nu+\nu_{0})\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}(\bm{v},\hat{\bm{y}}_{k}),

(14)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathrm{P}}_{k,I}:=(A_{k-1}\mu+\mu_{0})\Delta_{\mathcal{X}}(\bm{x}_{k},\hat{\bm{x}}_{k-1,I}),\quad\hat{\mathcal{C}}^{\mathrm{D}}_{k}:=(A_{k-1}\nu+\nu_{0})\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}(\bm{y}_{k},\hat{\bm{y}}_{k-1}).

(15)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{P}}_{k}=a_{k}\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{k})^{\top}\bm{y}_{k}-\bar{\bm{g}}_{k-1},\bm{u}-\bm{x}_{k}\right\rangle,\quad\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{D}}_{k}=a_{k}\left\langle f(\bm{x}_{k})-\bar{\bm{f}}_{k-1/2},\bm{v}-\bm{y}_{k}\right\rangle,

(16)

When applying

6
Discussion

Our discussion covers internal comparisons between full vector methods and stochastic methods, as well as external comparisons to contemporary methods for solving saddle point and variational inequality problems. We briefly comment on our chosen convergence criterion, as it may differ slightly from those used in comparisons.

6.1
Stronger Convergence Criteria

The results discussed in this section are expressed in terms of
global complexity
or
arithmetic complexity
, which is computed by multiplying the number of iterations shown in
Theorems

1
to
5
by
\tilde{O}(nd)
for full vector update methods;
for block-wise methods with
N

\tilde{O}(n(d/N+1))
for full updates of

and
\tilde{O}(n(d/N))

2

Importantly, in the case of randomized algorithms, the complexity in terms of the number of iterations is itself determined by the number
t
such that the algorithm may output a point
(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t},\tilde{\bm{y}}_{t})
satisfying
\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Gap}^{\bm{u},\bm{v}}(\tilde{\bm{x}}_{t},\tilde{\bm{y}}_{t})]\leq\varepsilon
, where the expectation is taken over all algorithm randomness with
\bm{u}\in\mathcal{X}
and
\bm{v}\in\mathcal{Y}
fixed (i.e.,
(\bm{u},\bm{v})
is independent of the algorithm randomness). When
\mu>0
, the criterion is made meaningful by setting
\bm{u}=\bm{x}_{\star}
as the unique minimizer of the strongly convex objective

. Otherwise, we choose a compact set

and consider

\bm{v}

\bm{y}_{\star}
when

or another supremum is taken over
\bm{v}\in\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
for
\mathcal{V}
compact otherwise. As described in
Alacaoglu et al. 2022a
, the “supremum of expected gap” criterion is weaker than the “expected supremum of gap” criterion, as algorithms with divergent behavior can still converge according to the first criterion. We render guarantees for the stronger criterion as a technical detail, as in light of previous work, largely similar steps can be applied to achieve the same complexity guarantee for the expected supremum of gap. To not overcomplicate the proofs, we only highlight the parts of the analysis that change. Consider the argument used to derive (
40

\displaystyle a_{k}\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{k})^{\top}\bm{y}_{k}-\bar{\bm{g}}_{k-1}

\displaystyle,\bm{u}-\bm{x}_{k}\rangle=a_{k}\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}_{k})^{\top}\bm{y}_{k}-\hat{\bm{g}}_{k-1}^{\top}\hat{\bm{y}}_{k-1},\bm{u}-\bm{x}_{k}\right\rangle

O\left(nd+{d\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\|\bm{A}_{i\cdot}\right\|_{2}}/{\varepsilon}\right)
. It is relevant to note that our improvement relies heavily on the non-uniform sampling and non-uniform historical regularization applied in
Section

4.1
and
Section

5.1
. Non-uniform sampling has been applied in the works above as well as earlier works such as
Alacaoglu et al. 2017
and
Chambolle et al. 2018
, but our findings show that sampling strategies may not be sufficient to remove the extraneous dimension factors in the iteration complexity.

We also consider a particular application to (non-separably constrained) matrix games, that is, the problem

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{x}\in\Delta^{d-1}}\max_{\bm{y}\in\Delta^{n-1}}\left[\mathcal{L}(\bm{x},\bm{y}):=\bm{y}^{\top}\bm{A}\bm{x}\right],

for a matrix
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
and probability simplices
\Delta^{d-1}
and
\Delta^{n-1}
, meaning that
\phi\equiv 0
and
\psi\equiv 0
. Let
A_{ij}
denote the element
(i,j)
-th entry of
\bm{A}
and let
\operatorname{nnz}(\bm{A})
be the number of such entries that are non-zero. For this example problem, the results of
Carmon et al. 2019
and
Alacaoglu and Malitsky 2022
achieve a global complexity of

\displaystyle\tilde{O}\left(\operatorname{nnz}(\bm{A})+\sqrt{\operatorname{nnz}(\bm{A})\cdot(n+d)}\tfrac{\max_{i,j}\left\lvert A_{ij}\right\rvert}{\varepsilon}\right)

(95)

whereas the method of
Diakonikolas 2025
achieves

\displaystyle\tilde{O}\left(\operatorname{nnz}(\bm{A})+(n+d)\tfrac{\left(\sum_{i}\left\|\bm{A}_{i\cdot}\right\|_{\infty}^{2/3}+\sum_{j}\left\|\bm{A}_{\cdot j}\right\|_{\infty}^{2/3}\right)^{3/2}}{\varepsilon}\right),

(96)

where
\bm{A}_{\cdot j}
is the
j
-th column of
\bm{A}
and
\bm{A}_{i\cdot}
is defined as in (
93
).
Due to the non-separability of
\Delta^{n-1}
, we may apply the result of
Theorem

2
with
N=n
and
G_{i}=\left\|\bm{A}_{i\cdot}\right\|_{\infty}
to achieve a global complexity of

\displaystyle\tilde{O}\left(\operatorname{nnz}(\bm{A})+\frac{(n+d)\sum_{i}\left\|\bm{A}_{i\cdot}\right\|_{\infty}}{\varepsilon}\right).

Notice that this is a direct improvement over (
96
) by replacing the
\ell_{2/3}
-norms with the
\ell_{1}
-norm. On the other hand, for cases when
\bm{A}
is dense but if the infinity norms of the rows are highly non-uniform, the complexity above will improve over (
95
) by a factor of
\sqrt{\operatorname{nnz}(\bm{A})/(n+d)}
(which is order
\sqrt{n}
for dense square matrices). If these row norms are not highly non-uniform, but the matrix is still dense, then our complexity will be worse than (
95
) by an
n\cdot\sqrt{(n+d)/\operatorname{nnz}(\bm{A})}
-factor (which is also order-
\sqrt{n}
for dense square matrices).

6.5
Conclusion

In this paper, we provided a class of algorithms for dual-linear min-max problems under separability and non-separability assumptions.
A primary direction for further investigation would be establishing lower bounds for both the full vector and stochastic case, following recent trends in the saddle-point optimization literature
(
Zhang et al. 2022
)
. In particular, achieving lower bounds that 1) account for non-smooth uncoupled components
\phi
and
\psi
and 2) depend on the individual Lipschitz and smoothness parameters of
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
, would be of interest. This contrasts current results, which depend on the smoothness and strong convexity constants of the entire objective
(\bm{x},\bm{y})\mapsto\mathcal{L}(\bm{x},\bm{y})
jointly in both the primal and dual vectors. On the side of upper bounds, one may pursue complexity guarantees that fully decouple the Lipschitz and smoothness constants of each objective. For instance,
Theorem

2
has a “partially decoupled” complexity which depends on the constant
\left\|\bm{\lambda}\right\|_{1}^{1/2}(\left\|\bm{\lambda}\right\|_{1}^{1/2}+\left\|\bm{G}\right\|_{1}^{1/2})
, which is upper bounded by
\left\|\bm{\lambda}\right\|_{1}
(the constant associated with a generic stochastic variational inequality method) and lower bounded by
\left\|\bm{L}\right\|_{1}+\left\|\bm{G}\right\|_{1}
. Accordingly, we ask whether a complexity dependent on
\left\|\bm{L}\right\|_{1}+\left\|\bm{G}\right\|_{1}
can be achieved, with analogous results for strongly convex-strongly concave settings.

On a practical note, we also raise the possibility of
extending our algorithm class to parameter-free or adaptive variants. For instance, inspired by the golden ratio algorithm of
Malitsky 2020
, an “auto-conditioned” variant of PDHG was recently proposed
(
Lan and Li 2024
)
. This approach employs not only extrapolated gradient estimates, but also an additional sequence of extrapolated prox-centers; these alternative prox-centers may bear similarity to the historical regularization technique described in
Section

4.1
and
Section

5.1
. These techniques, developed primarily for bilinearly coupled min-max problems, avoid computation of the spectral norm of the coupling matrix
\bm{A}
(analogous to adaptively estimating
\bm{G}_{1},\ldots,\bm{G}_{N}
in our setup).
Fercoq 2024
also proposes techniques for adaptive step size selection in PDHG, using a spectral norm estimation technique. Given that PGHG is a conceptual predecessor of our algorithm, we hypothesize that similar techniques may be employed in the dual-linear setting. We leave these directions as interesting avenues for future research.
