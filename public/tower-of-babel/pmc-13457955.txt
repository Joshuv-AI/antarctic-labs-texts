Performance analysis of electrostatic and transport characteristics of underlap-engineered gate-all-around carbon nanotube (CNT) FETs for nanoelectronics circuitry applications
Authors: Shuvra Jyoti Bose, Rudra Sankar Dhar, Mousa I Hussein, Falah Awwad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA cylindrical gate-all-around (GAA) carbon nanotube field-effect transistor (CNT FET) utilizing a semiconducting zig-zag (19,0) nanotube channel is developed for sub-10-nm technology applications. The device incorporates a conformal high-k lanthanum oxide gate dielectric forming a wrap-gate architecture, enabling strong electrostatic confinement under aggressive device scaling. To regulate carrier transport and mitigate short-channel effects, underlap engineering is systematically introduced with lengths ranging from 0 to 5 nm, resulting in six device configurations (Devices A–F). Self-consistent quantum transport simulations based on the non-equilibrium Green’s function (NEGF) formalism reveal a distinct trade-off between drive capability and electrostatic integrity within the device. Performance evaluation is conducted using key electrostatic and carrier transport metrics. Compared with the non-underlap configuration (Device A), the optimized 3 nm underlap device (Device D) exhibits a 50.7% reduction in off-state leakage current, a 23.7% improvement in subthreshold swing, and a 26.5% suppression in drain-induced barrier lowering, while enhancing the switching ratio by 84.5% with minimal degradation in drive current. Furthermore, linearity and harmonic distortion analysis indicates reduced higher-order components, confirming improved analog stability under RF operating conditions for advanced nanoelectronic applications.Keywords: Gate all around, Carbon nanotube, FET, Carrier transport, Underlap engineeringSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology, Physics

Introduction

The relentless scaling of metal–oxide–semiconductor field-effect transistors (MOSFETs) have driven unprecedented progress in enriching drive current performance over the decades. However, as device dimensions approach the sub-10 nm technology node, planar MOSFETs or even FinFETs face critical electrostatic and physical limitations hindering further miniaturization1. Short-channel effects (SCEs), including drain-induced barrier lowering (DIBL), threshold voltage roll-off, quantum tunnelling, and increased leakage currents are the consequences that lead to degradation of switching performance and elevate static power dissipation2. Consequently, achieving high on-state current (Ion) while maintaining low off-state current (Ioff) becomes increasingly difficult, threatening the continued applicability of Moore’s Law to traditional CMOS technology and beyond3. To sustain device performance beyond these scaling limits, attention has long shifted toward innovative transistor architectures and alternative channel materials. Among structural innovations, three-dimensional (3D) transistor geometries, with FinFET already in use, have proven to be particularly effective in suppressing electrostatic degradation4,5. Gate-All-Around (GAA) FETs—where the gate fully encapsulates the channel—offer superior electrostatic control, minimal leakage, and strong immunity to SCEs compared to planar and FinFET architectures6. Silicon nanowire and nanosheet GAA transistors are already being integrated into advanced technology nodes (5 nm and 3 nm), underscoring the architecture’s scalability and effectiveness7. Nevertheless, silicon’s intrinsic limitations—such as moderate carrier mobility, high phonon scattering, and limited thermal conductivity at nanoscale dimensions—curb its performance potential in these ultra-scaled regimes8, hence alternative channel materials are actively being explored.

Carbon nanotubes (CNTs), by contrast, exhibit exceptional material and transport properties that make them compelling candidates for next-generation transistor channels. Their quasi-one-dimensional (1D) structure allows near-ballistic transport, high intrinsic carrier mobility, excellent thermal conductivity, and chemical stability, enabling possible higher transconductance, reduced leakage, and superior energy efficiency compared to silicon material9,10. A defining characteristic of a CNT is its chirality, represented by the integers (n, m), which determines both its diameter and its metallic or semiconducting behaviour11. For transistor applications, semiconducting CNTs are indispensable for their finite bandgaps enabling effective switching12. The bandgap is expected to vary inversely with the CNT diameter, creating a critical trade-off between drive current and leakage current such that smaller diameters enhance leakage suppression but limits current13. Hence, incorporating CNT as a material in FET architectures may be an alternative for improved performance and related applications.

Recent theoretical and computational studies systematically explored CNT FET performance across various chiralities. Dixit and Gupta8, compared CNTs having chiralities (10,0), (13,0), (16,0), (19,0), and (25,0) demonstrating that the semiconducting (19,0) configuration achieves an optimal balance between transconductance, threshold voltage, and leakage current control. This (19,0) CNT offers moderate diameter and bandgap values supporting higher on-state current and favourable on/off current ratios better than the other chiralities preserving manageable subthreshold characteristics. Furthermore, larger gate capacitance at moderate gate voltage enhances gate–channel coupling and switching efficiency, making (19,0) CNTs highly suitable for integration in FETs8–13. Though these properties show improvements but, CNT FETs are still at a developing stage and far from being incorporated in regular CMOS technology with enriched performance and be able to compete with the existing technologies. Hence, further research leading to the development of CNT FET for performance enhancement optimizations and probable applications are required.

Recent progress in conformal gate deposition and dielectric integration advances the practical realization of GAA FETs with CNT. Using ALD enables uniform high-k dielectric coatings over the cylindrical CNT surface, improving electrostatic control and reducing subthreshold swing close to the thermionic limits (≈ 70–80 mV/decade) are also achieved14–16, making CNT FETs an alternative choice. Moreover, the superior thermal conductivity of carbon allows efficient heat dissipation in CNT GAA structures, mitigating self-heating effects and enhancing reliability at high power densities17,18, a much needed option in Nanoscale FETs. Despite these advantages, challenges remain in achieving scalable, chirality-controlled synthesis and integration of CNTs. Recent improvements in gas flow-directed CVDs using non-metallic catalysts, such as nanodiamonds, demonstrates highly aligned, defect-free CNTs with reduced metallic impurities, enabling better control over structural and electronic properties19. Still the CNT FETs developed so far are not suitable for use in regular CMOS technology based applications because of their higher leakages and degraded switching speed. Therefore, developing CNT FETs for performance enrichment is extremely desired.

Semiconducting (19,0) CNTs integrated into GAA architectures emerge as a promising solution for extending device scaling and performance for the future. Their diameter-dependent bandgap provides an ideal trade-off between leakage suppression and current drive, while the cylindrical GAA ensures a robust gate control and efficient coupling with high-k dielectrics20–22. However, despite these advantages, conventional CNT FETs still face several key challenges that limit their widespread implementation especially in the CMOS technology23. Although GAA based CNT FETs exhibit better carrier transport, their performance at nano-dimensions are constrained by strong drain-to-channel coupling and increased leakages, which limit both the achievable Ion and the Ion/Ioff ratio23,24. Introducing controlled gate to source/drain underlap regions within a Gate-All-Around CNT FET architecture provides an effective electrostatic engineering approach to possibly overcome these limitations25,26. From a device-physics perspective, these limitations originate from insufficient gate control over the channel potential in the presence of strong drain fields. The underlap region is expected to suppress parasitic drain influence, fringing fields, strengthen gate control and minimize off-state leakage, and possibly enable enhancement in drive current and switching ratio for the device26,27. Thus by exploiting underlap-induced modulation of the channel potential profile and contact electrostatics, the present paper focuses to establish an optimized GAA CNT FET architecture capable of simultaneously enhancing drive current and switching performance to be at par with the present CMOS technology. Therefore, the analysis will further highlight the necessity of underlap-engineered GAA configurations for improved electrostatics and transport characteristics essential for next-generation nanoelectronic devices.

Device structure

A three-dimensional semiconducting single-walled carbon nanotube (CNT) is employed as the channel material to realize a gate-all-around (GAA) CNT field-effect transistor (FET). The baseline configuration is referred to as Device A, consisting of a semiconducting zig-zag (19,0) CNT fully surrounded by a conformal high-k lanthanum oxide (La₂O₃) dielectric layer serving as the gate oxide and wrapped by a metallic gate electrode, as shown in Fig. 1a. The device channel length (Lch) is set to 10 nm, flanked symmetrically by 2 nm heavily doped source and drain, thereby forming a compact GAA transistor geometry. A 1 nm thick La₂O₃ dielectric layer (k ≈ 30, bandgap ≈ 5.5 eV) is employed providing favourable band offsets that are expected to effectively suppress gate tunneling to ensure high interface quality with reduced trap density. Its ability to form stable capping layers contributes to threshold voltage stability and mitigates low-frequency (1/f) noise—attributes critical for enhanced performance in GAA transistors. This compact coaxial configuration is shown in Fig. 1a. Device A contains no gate underlap and thus forms the baseline CNT FET device. Figure 1b presents the radial 2D cross-sectional view of the CNT FET to illustrate the concentric alignment of the CNT core, dielectric shell, and the gate metal, which is expected to provide strong electrostatic confinement and minimal fringing field induced degradation.

The active transport channel of the CNT FET device developed here and denoted as Device A utilizes a (19,0) CNT to achieve an optimal balance between high drive current and effective leakage suppression. According to the bandgap–diameter relationship (Eg ∝ 1/d), the (19,0) tube possesses a diameter of 1.49 nm, which minimizes contact resistance and phonon scattering while maintaining a bandgap of 0.43 eV—ideal for achieving low off-state leakage at nanoscale channel lengths8. Fixing the chirality ensures consistent band structure and minimizes variability arising from mixed CNT ensembles, thereby providing a controlled platform for assessing electrostatic and material effects. Enclosing the CNT with a cylindrical gate through a uniform La₂O₃ dielectric ensures strong electrostatic coupling thereby establishes a uniform radial electric field distribution around the channel. Such geometry is essential to achieve sub-70 mV/dec subthreshold slope and robust short-channel control. The gate work function is tuned close to the CNT midgap to ensure symmetric conduction characteristics and reduce ambipolar behaviour. This adjustment is implemented using dipole-forming interfacial layers, consistent with modern high-k/metal gate integration techniques.

To explore electrostatic design optimization, further geometrical modifications are introduced to Device A by incorporating gate-underlap (LUL) regions—defined as the ungated space between the gate edge and the source/drain contacts. Thus, the gate-all-around (GAA) CNT FET is developed with the central region corresponding to the gated CNT channel, followed by the underlap segments on either side and then the source/drain contacts are placed. Five additional devices are hence developed and labelled as Device B to Device F, by varying this underlap length from 1 nm to 5 nm. Underlap engineering serves as an important design parameter for nanoscale FETs; it effectively expands the depletion region near the drain, reducing drain-induced barrier lowering (DIBL), gate-induced drain leakage (GIDL), and band-to-band tunnelling currents in the off-state while also subdues the fringing field effects. Although it slightly reduces on-current due to series resistance, optimized underlap configurations are known to significantly enhance subthreshold characteristics and leakage suppression without major current degradation—an effect previously observed in advanced GAA nanowire devices1,14. The source and drain contacts are defined at the ends of the CNT channel, while the conformal gate geometry suppresses short-channel effects and enhances gate–channel coupling as shown in Fig. 1d. The device variants are developed here using the QuantumATK28, which offers an atomistic, first-principles-based device simulation environment appropriate for low-dimensional nanotransistors. The device here is developed here from molecular structure being gathered step by step to form a device in the bottom-up approach system. The transport characteristics are obtained by solving the non-equilibrium Green’s function (NEGF) formalism self-consistently with the Poisson equation, ensuring accurate modelling of quantum confinement, carrier injection, tunneling effects, and drain-induced perturbations. The NEGF formalism enables accurate assessment of quantum transport, potential distribution, and carrier scattering under nanoscale regime. This framework inherently captures key quantum mechanical effects such as carrier confinement, tunnelling, interface traps, and phase-coherent transport. As the devices developed here are less than 30 nm in channel length, ballistic transport is assumed by the NEGF as transport is governed by the quantum transmission effects rather than drift–diffusion models. This assumption is justified due to the short channel length of ~ 10 nm, which is comparable to the electron mean free path in carbon nanotubes, making ballistic transport a reasonable approximation28. The CNT is represented at atomic resolution using parameters derived from density-functional-theory-informed models, while the gate metal and dielectric layers are modelled using realistic material constants in the software. A semi-empirical Local Density Approximation (LDA) based calculator is employed for electronic structure calculations with open boundary conditions at the contacts and Dirichlet boundary conditions for electrostatics under applied bias. A density mesh cut-off energy of 15 Hartree is used for real-space integration, and electronic occupation is modelled using the Fermi–Dirac distribution with a thermal broadening of 1000 K to aid numerical convergence28. The source/drain doping is implemented by introducing appropriate charge modulation within the electrode regions, consistent with the atomistic framework used in Quantum ATK28. All simulations are performed at an ambient temperature of 300 K under steady-state conditions with appropriate k-point sampling along the transport direction. Devices A through F are constructed in a controlled and systematic manner to isolate the effect of gate underlap on electrostatic behaviour and transport performance of the devices.

Results and discussion

Electrical analysis

The semiconducting cylindrical gate-all-around (GAA) carbon nanotube (CNT) FET with chirality (19,0), and a channel length of 10 nm is employed to systematically investigate the electrical performance. As discussed in the previous section the newly developed Devices A to F with varying underlap length of 0 to 5 nm, respectively, are analysed. Figures 3a and b present the transfer characteristics in linear and log scale, respectively, for all the 10 nm gate length cylindrical GAA CNT FET under varying gate underlap lengths. The results show a systematic variation in the drain current as the underlap length is increased from 0 to 5 nm, while the channel length, source/drain length, and doping profiles are kept constant. Increasing the underlap length alters electrostatic coupling between the gate and the ungated access regions, which in turn affects carrier injection and access resistance, leading to a subtle change in the drain current. Since the source and drain regions are formed by heavy doping rather than metal contacts, the transport characteristics are dominated by gate-controlled electrostatics and junction access resistance, rather than by Schottky barrier effects at the contacts. The linear-scale ID-VGS characteristics in Fig. 3a reveal a gradual reduction in the peak drain current with increasing underlap length. This behaviour is attributed to underlap CNT regions that increases series resistance and reduces carrier injection efficiency into the cylindrical channel. Importantly, the reduction in on-state current remains progressive rather than abrupt, indicating that the cylindrical GAA geometry maintains strong gate control over the channel even when partial underlap is introduced. This highlights the robustness of the GAA configuration against electrostatic degradation at a 10 nm gate length. In the logarithmic IDS–VGS characteristics (Fig. 3b), all devices exhibit a well-defined off-state region with a pronounced current minimum near the charge neutrality point or Dirac point that is observed to increase slightly with increase in the underlap length of the device with VGS=0.348 V for Device A. The deeper current minimum observed for larger underlap devices confirms improved control of the channel potential and reduced drain-induced barrier thinning due to lessened parasitic based electrostatic couplings at nanoscale dimensions.

The threshold voltage (Vth) values for all the developed devices are acquired and plotted in Figure. 3c. A gradual increase in the Vth trend is observed with increasing gate underlap length from 0.348 V (Device A) to 0.379 V (Device F), attributing to the reduced gate influence over the source-side carrier injection region as the underlap length increases. In contrast, the previously reported Device G8 exhibits a much higher Vth of 0.40 V, despite having a longer gate length of 30 nm, indicating weaker electrostatic efficiency. The smooth and monotonic variation across Devices A–F confirms stable device operation and demonstrates that underlap provides a predictable means of threshold voltage tuning, which is further detailed in Table 2. Figure 3d presents on-state current for all the developed devices and are also compared to the existing devices. Device A delivers the highest Ion of 44.3 µA/µm, owing to strong gate–source coupling and minimal access resistance. With increasing underlap, Ion decreases gradually to 36.9 µA/µm for Device F, representing a controlled reduction of ~ 16.7% from 0 to 5 nm underlap length of the devices (A-F). This decrease arises from the presence of underlap region in the CNT FET that makes the source and drain regions increasingly away from the channel regions thereby having a larger carrier travelling path. Nevertheless, the developed devices significantly outperform previously reported CNT FETs of Device G8 and I27 that exhibit Ion of 10 µA/µm and also Device H26 which is having Ion of only 18.14 µA/µm providing a much lower current than Device D with 3 nm underlap based cylindrical CNT GAA FET that retains a comparatively higher Ion of 40.3 µA/µm indicating a considerable enrichment. A systematic reduction in off-state current, Ioff, with increase in underlap length is clearly illustrated in Fig. 3e for devices A to F mainly due to the parasitic series resistance that is being inducted by the underlaps. Ioff decreases to 24.6 nA/µm for Device D, reflecting effective suppression of leakage paths with increased underlap of 3 nm. The absence of abrupt current degradation within the device suggests that carrier transport remains smooth throughout the channel region and is not contact-limited. This behaviour confirms the effectiveness of the doped source/drain regions and the cylindrical GAA configuration in maintaining stable carrier injection even in the presence of underlap. The switching ratio (ION/IOFF) reflects the switching capability based on the speed of operation of the device is observed to improve from 0.711 × 10³ for Device A to 1.962 × 10³ for Device F, corresponding to an enhancement of ~ 2.75 times with increasing underlap length, as shown in Fig. 3f and further summarized in Table I. This improvement is primarily attributed to the suppression of mainly IOFF current in the newly developed devices, which is directly dependent on the introduction of the underlap region and its increase in length from Device B to Device F creating an ungated extension between the gate and source/drain. This effectively increases the barrier width for carrier transport as well as the carrier transport length, thereby limits carrier injection inducing parasitic resistances. As a result, the OFF-state current is significantly reduced, while the ON-state current remains nearly unaffected, leading to the observed enhancement in the switching ratio. In comparison, Device G8 exhibits a much lower switching ratio of 0.65 × 10³ (highest among the existing devices), indicating inferior leakage control.

Devices E and F exhibit lower Ioff and provide better Ion/Ioff but, they suffer from a pronounced degradation in on-state current (Ion), which drops to 38.6 and 36.9 µA/µm, respectively, due to increased parasitic resistance introduced by longer underlap regions in the CNT FET. In contrast, Device D retains a comparatively higher Ion of 40.3 µA/µm while the leakage current (Ioff) is not as high as in case of Device A or so hence, provides a more balanced operation for the device. This indicates that Device D effectively mitigates short-channel effects without excessively weakening gate-to-source coupling, making it electrostatically superior to the other developed devices. Device D, therefore, provides a better trade-off between drive current and switching performance, making it a more practical configuration from a circuit-level perspective.

The extracted subthreshold swing (SS) for the devices is obtained using Eq. (1)30 and further plotted in Fig. 3g and summarized in Table 2. Following a similar trend, SS also decreases with increasing underlap length mainly due to leakage reduction resulting from reduced fringing effects from the gate-source and gate-drain terminals induced in the device, which enhances gate control over the channel region, thereby providing SS of ~ 54.63 mV/dec for Device D, approaching the ideal limit. Although, further increase in the underlap length for Devices E and F yields reduced SS, but only marginally indicating a near saturation hence may not be further considered. Despite partial gate decoupling near the source, the cylindrical GAA architecture effectively preserves subthreshold behavior. In comparison, Device H26 reports a substantially higher SS of 149 mV/dec, indicative of stronger short-channel effects. Thus, Device D retaining a comparatively higher Ion providing an enriched switching speed simultaneously achieve a significant reduction in SS can therefore be considered as a better performing device.

Drain-induced barrier lowering (DIBL) expressed by Eq. (2)30 and presented in Fig. 3h, showcases a monotonic decrease among the developed devices with Device D reaching below 100 mV/V, indicates improved electrostatic screening of the channel. In contrast, Device H26 exhibits a much higher DIBL of 127.5 mV/V despite its larger gate length, underscoring the effectiveness of underlap engineering combined with a cylindrical GAA geometry in suppressing short-channel effects as observed in Table 2. This indicates that Device D with DIBL of 97 mV/V, effectively mitigates short-channel effects without excessively weakening gate-to-source coupling, making it electrostatically superior in contrast to the other developed devices.

Overall, the quantitative trends in threshold voltage, drive current, switching ratio, subthreshold swing, and DIBL—explicitly supported by Table 2—demonstrate that the newly developed GAA CNT FET achieves a favourable balance between performance with enriched electrostatic control and reduced fringing effects. Hence, Device D can be considered as an improved device with 3 nm underlap length offering substantial leakage suppression and improved switching behaviour without severe consequences in current drive, establishing the Device D based architecture as superior to previously reported CNT FET designs to probably meet the future generation requirements.

The transmission spectrum analysis reveals that increasing underlap length significantly suppresses transmission near the Fermi level due to the extension of the effective potential barrier, thereby reducing source–drain coupling and leakage pathways. Additionally, the widening of the transport gap indicates improved electrostatic control through mitigation of drain-induced barrier modulation. However, a concurrent reduction in transmission amplitude at higher energies suggests degraded carrier injection efficiency, highlighting a fundamental trade-off between leakage suppression and current drive capability. This energy-resolved transport behavior provides a physical basis for the observed trends in SS, DIBL, and ON-current across different underlap configurations. In an underlap device, the effective tunnelling barrier increases and introduces additional series resistance, weakening electrostatic coupling and reducing the number of propagating modes within the bias window. Consequently, the transmission spectrum is suppressed near the Fermi conduction region, leading to a lower ON-state current. In contrast, the non-underlap structure provides stronger source–channel coupling and a shorter effective barrier, enabling higher transmission and enhanced carrier injection, which is consistent with its larger drain current and improved conduction characteristics. Thus the underlap structure based CNT FETs developed here introduces resistive regions on either side of the channel at source/channel and drain/channel interfaces weakening electrostatic coupling and hence reduces carrier injection efficiency. This manifests the transmission spectrum as shown in Fig. 4 to suppress peaks near the Fermi conduction window presenting a smoother onset of propagating modes, and a wider low-transmission region for Device D compared to the Device A. Unlike absolute transconductance (gm), which increases with bias current, TGF provides a bias-independent metric reflecting the intrinsic capability of the device to generate transconductance under RF operation. A higher TGF indicates improved carrier transport efficiency and enhanced gate control over the channel, which are critical for achieving higher gain in the RF circuits. Therefore, analyzing TGF allows a fair comparison of different underlap configurations by revealing how structural based underlap engineering impacts RF efficiency beyond mere current scaling. Thus, zero-underlap device (Device A) exhibits stronger gate coupling, while underlap devices demonstrate smoother and more stable TGF behaviour over a wider bias range, which is advantageous for bias-tolerant analog and RF circuit operation. Hence, giving a better performance for Device D as observed and provides an enriched linearity and stability in contrast to Device A.

Figure 5f presents the IDS–VDS characteristics of a 10-nm gate-length based GAA CNT FET for varying underlap lengths, ranging from 0 nm (Device A) to 5 nm (Device F). A clear and systematic dependence of the drain current on underlap length is observed, validating the physical correctness of the devices developed here. The output performance of all the devices are distinctively observed to vary with change in underlap length throughout the region of operation while right shift of the device performance is revealed with increase in underlap length from Device A to F as evident due to increase in the parasitic.

Thus, the results observed demonstrate that introducing the underlap region effectively though enhances output resistance and suppresses output conductance but it stabilizes Early voltage, improves intrinsic gain particularly in the low-to-moderate inversion region of operation and provides an enriched output performance. These findings ascertain that underlap engineering is a viable and efficient strategy for optimizing analog performance without significantly compromising bias scalability.

Linearity analysis

The linearity performance analysis of the CNT FET based developed devices (Device A, B and D) are further analyzed for stability and viability of the devices for applications. The second-order transconductance (gm2) characteristics reflecting the nonlinear behavior of the developed devices is revealed in Fig. 6a. The non-underlap device (Device A) exhibits a high peak gm2 of approximately 1.7 mA/V², indicating strong second-order nonlinearity near threshold operation. As the underlap is introduced gm2 is significantly suppressed, reducing the peak to about 0.55 mA/V² for Device D, Table IV details the comparison among the devices. This trend confirms that underlap engineering smooths the channel potential profile, thereby mitigating abrupt carrier modulation responsible for second-order distortion causing disruption in the linearity of the devices. The third-order transconductance (gm3) exhibits quite a similar trend with Device A demonstrating a larger gm3 excursions with peak magnitudes of 4.6 mA/V³, while Devices D shows progressively reduced values of about 3.7 mA/V³ as observed from Fig. 6b and thereafter presented for detailed understanding and comparison in Table 3 along with results from existing device of Ahmad30. The reduced and smoother gm3 response for Device D with the addition of underlap region indicates an effective suppression of higher-order nonlinearities, providing a critical view for minimizing harmonic and intermodulation distortion in analog and RF applications, thereby instituting Device D to be a superior device with enriched performance.

Harmonic distortion analysis

Harmonic distortion analysis represents a frequency-domain manifestation of device nonlinearity indicating critical performance metrics for evaluating the distortion analysis of nanoscale field-effect transistors intended for analog and radio-frequency applications. Considering the adverse effects of device nonlinearity, gain compression, and harmonic distortion on analog and RF performance, second-order (HD2), third-order (HD3), and intermodulation distortion (IMD3) are analyzed as a function of gate voltage for the 10 nm channel length GAA CNT FET devices developed here and further they are compared with the existing Graphene FET of Ahmad30. This analysis enables a comprehensive understanding of the underlap engineering which influences device linearity and distortion suppression are essential for optimizing CNT-based transistors for low-noise analog devices and circuits.

Figure 7a illustrates the variation of third-order intermodulation distortion (IMD3) as a function of gate bias (VGS) as expressed by Eq. (15)35 for the novel 10 nm GAA CNT FET with varied source–drain underlap lengths. A pronounced IMD3 minimum is observed around VGS ≈ 0.18–0.2 V for all devices, indicating the bias point of enhanced third-order linearity. Among the three configurations, Device D (3 nm underlap) exhibits the lowest IMD3 magnitude, reaching approximately − 155 dBm at the distortion null, followed by Device B (1 nm) and Device A (0 nm), as summarized and further compared in Table 4 with results of existing device from Ahmad30. The improved IMD3 suppression with increasing underlap is attributed to reduced electric-field crowding near the source/channel junction, which moderates higher-order transconductance components (gm3) and suppresses nonlinear current modulation. At higher gate biases (VGS > 0.6 V), IMD3 gradually increases and converges for all devices due to strong inversion and velocity saturation effects dominating the transport behavior.

It is therefore observed that introducing a controlled underlap in the 10 nm GAA CNT FET significantly improves linearity as well as the harmonic distortion effects by the parameters gm₂, gm₃, VIP2, VIP3, IIP3, THD, and IMD3 in the device performance. This improvement arises as the underlap region weakens the direct electrostatic coupling between the gate and the source/drain junctions, thereby suppressing abrupt carrier injection and reducing peak transconductance nonlinearity. Since higher-order linearity parameters are computed from successive derivatives of the IDS–VGS characteristics, their accuracy is fundamentally governed by the fidelity of the simulated carrier transport and electrostatics. As a result, the channel potential varies more gradually with gate bias, leading to a smoother current modulation and reduced higher-order harmonic distortion. Additionally, the underlap mitigates short-channel effects and parasitic tunneling near the contacts, which further stabilizes the transconductance profile and enhances linear operation.

Conclusion

Cylindrical gate-all-around (GAA) CNT FETs employing a semiconducting zig-zag (19,0) carbon nanotube channel with a 10 nm gate length and high-k gate dielectric are developed and analyzed. Underlap engineering is systematically introduced (0–5 nm) to investigate the trade-off between drive capability and electrostatic control under aggressive scaling. The results demonstrate that increasing underlap length slightly reduces the on-state current but significantly suppresses off-state leakage, yielding an overall improvement in switching performance. In particular, Device D (3 nm underlap) achieves nearly a 1.84 times enhancement in switching ratio (1.312 × 10³) and a 51% reduction in leakage current compared to the baseline device, while maintaining over 91% of the original drive current. Moreover, Device D exhibits improved electrostatic characteristics, including a subthreshold swing of 54.6 mV/dec and reduced DIBL of 97 mV/V, along with high transconductance (125.6 µS), indicating a balance between device performance and SCE control. Harmonic and linearity analysis further confirm its superiority, with reductions of 24.4% and 23.8% in HD2 and HD3, respectively, and a 14.8% improvement in IMD3. For Device D, improved linearity is observed with VIP2 = − 5 V, VIP3 = 6.8 V, and IIP3 = − 0.017 dBm, along with enhanced 1 dB compression behavior, indicating reduced distortion under large-signal operation. Despite these promising results, a key limitation of this CNT as a material is the reliance on chirality-specific semiconducting property, particularly the (19,0) configuration, which remains challenging to realize in large-scale fabrication due to the inherent production of mixed chiralities in conventional synthesis techniques. Overall, the optimized GAA CNT FET demonstrates superior switching, electrostatic stability, and linearity, making it a promising candidate for low-power digital and analog/RF applications. The combined effect of chirality control, high-k dielectric integration, and optimized underlap design provides an effective pathway for extending device performance beyond conventional silicon technologies, supported by NEGF-based transport insights into carrier modulation and leakage suppression.
