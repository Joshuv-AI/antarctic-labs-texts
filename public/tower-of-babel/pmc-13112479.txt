Iranian traffic sign dataset: A step towards understanding smart cities
Authors: Iman Zarei, Abdolhossein Fathi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
With the rapid expansion of smart cities and the growing need for accurate and real-time analysis of road infrastructure, the development of artificial intelligence systems capable of perceiving, analyzing, and recording environmental information has become increasingly vital. In this context, the present study introduces a novel system for the detection of Iranian traffic signs, making a significant contribution not only in the data collection domain but also in the detection of visual data. A comprehensive and unique dataset is constructed, consisting of 14,111 images and 19,000 traffic signs across 118 distinct classes, has been collected over a two-year period under diverse temporal conditions (morning, noon, dusk, and night) and throughout all four seasons, covering urban, rural, and intercity areas across the country. The image annotation process was conducted with high precision using the MakeSense tool in two standard formats: YOLO (.txt) and Pascal VOC (.xml). Subsequently, an automated detection system was developed based on the advanced deep neural network model YOLOv12, enabling precise identification of traffic signs. The model’s performance, evaluated through 6-Fold Cross Validation, demonstrated outstanding accuracy achieving an mAP@50 of 96%. These results highlight the model’s remarkable efficiency in real-world scenarios and its superiority over previous YOLO versions. Beyond detection capabilities, the proposed system can be employed for the extraction of digital traffic sign maps, serving as a foundational tool for navigation systems, intelligent vehicles, spatial analytics, and the development of a national traffic sign map of Iran and other similar countries. By integrating cutting-edge technologies, data-driven localization, and state-of-the-art deep learning architectures, this research represents a significant step toward a smarter and safer future for the nation’s road networks.

Background

In recent years, with the rapid expansion of smart cities and the advancement of autonomous transportation systems, the need for precise visual understanding of road environments, especially traffic sign detection, has become essential. At the global level, datasets such as GTSRB [1], BelgiumTS [2], and TT100K [3] have been widely used as benchmark resources for training and evaluating traffic sign detection models.

However, these datasets exhibit limited generalizability to Iranian road conditions due to significant cultural, linguistic, and graphical differences. For instance, the presence of Persian text, distinct color, and unique shapes of some traffic signs in Iran make it challenging for models trained on foreign datasets to perform effectively in real-world Iranian scenarios.

On the other hand, the absence of a comprehensive, indigenous, and standardized dataset of Iranian traffic signs is a fundamental challenge for researchers in the fields of computer-vision and artificial-intelligence in Iran. Although existing datasets such as PTTSD [4] and PTSD [5] represent initial steps in this regard, they possess significant limitations in terms of the total number of classes, and geographical diversity. The objective of this study is not to establish a standardized dataset, but to provide a foundation for the development of intelligent transportation systems in Iran.

Data Description

Given the absence of a comprehensive and locally relevant dataset of Iranian traffic signs, as well as the incompatibility of existing foreign datasets with the visual and linguistic characteristics of Iranian roads, this study introduces a dedicated dataset entitled Iranian Traffic Sign Dataset (ITSD). The primary goal of this dataset is to provide a localized benchmark for research in the detection, recognition, and tracking of traffic signs within real-world driving environments across Iran. The ITSD dataset contains 14,111 high-resolution images collected from various regions of the country under diverse lighting, seasonal, and weather conditions. In total, 19,718 traffic signs have been manually annotated and categorized into 118 distinct classes. To promote reproducibility and open research, the proposed dataset has been made publicly available and can be accessed through the following link: (https://figshare.com/articles/dataset/Iran_Traffic_Sign_Dataset_ITSD_/28943957) [6].

One of the major challenges in applying traffic sign detection models trained on international datasets lies in the significant differences in visual characteristics, graphic design, and textual representation of traffic signs in Iran. Iranian traffic signs not only differ in language and typography, but also in aspects such as font style, line thickness, background color, aspect ratio, and even the degree of physical wear and fading. For instance, in Fig. 1 the “Speed Limit 60” sign in Iran appears in multiple formats, such as or even a bilingual combination of Persian and English. In contrast, datasets like GTSRB or TT100K represent such signs in a single numeric format with uniform European fonts, which fails to capture the linguistic and stylistic diversity observed in Iranian road environments. This issue is well illustrated in Fig. 1.Fig. 1Examples of visual and textual differences of Iranian traffic signs compared to their counterparts in international datasets.Fig 1 dummy alt text

Examples of visual and textual differences of Iranian traffic signs compared to their counterparts in international datasets.

Moreover, some Iranian traffic signs include additional text such as road names, directions, or regional labels, which increases visual diversity and consequently makes automatic recognition more challenging. In addition, environmental variations including lighting conditions, surface wear, color changes due to weather, and camera viewing angles further reduce the accuracy and robustness of models trained on foreign datasets when applied to Iranian signs. Due to these fundamental differences in the visual and contextual structure of the signs, collecting and carefully annotating a localized dataset of Iranian traffic signs is essential. Such a dataset, encompassing a wide range of lighting conditions, viewing angles, and text styles, can serve as a precise foundation for training, evaluating, and optimizing traffic sign detection and tracking models in real-world conditions in Iran.

The data collection process was carried out over a two-year period across different seasons to ensure comprehensive coverage of various environmental conditions, including strong sunlight, rain, fog, dusk, and nighttime. Data were captured using a range of smartphones securely mounted on the vehicle dashboard, and recording was performed along urban, intercity, and rural routes at varying speeds up to 110 km/h, with frame rates between 30 and 60 frames per second. Subsequently, suitable frames were extracted and selected from the recorded videos. Examples of these images are presented in Fig. 2. To prevent redundancy, images captured from the same location or with similar viewpoints were removed. Additionally, very low-quality, blurry, or poorly exposed images were excluded.Fig. 2Examples of raw images collected from urban and intercity environments in Iran.Fig 2 dummy alt text

Examples of raw images collected from urban and intercity environments in Iran.

The classification and labelling of traffic signs were done based on the official reference of the Iranian Traffic Regulation Handbook. The ITSD encompasses 118 active classes, comprising those signs that were empirically collected and documented on Iranian roadways. The ITSD dataset divided to six folds and each data fold contains three folders: the images folder containing the pictures, the labels folder containing annotation files in YOLO .txt format, and the annotations folder containing annotation files in VOC .xml format. Furthermore, the images were selected at intervals of 50 to 100 frames to prevent the inclusion of duplicate or excessively similar frames. To preserve privacy, all identifiable faces and vehicle license plates have been obfuscated. Annotation Tool: The data labelling was performed using the Make-Sense annotation tool, an open-source and user-friendly solution for image annotation that operates locally without requiring data upload to cloud servers, thereby ensuring data security. The annotations have been stored in both YOLO (.txt) and Pascal VOC (.xml) formats to facilitate the use of this dataset across a wide range of deep learning models and frameworks.

Image Specifications: All images were captured in JPG format at resolutions of 1080×1920 or 936×1920 pixels. The dataset encompasses images captured under diverse weather conditions -including sunny, rainy, snowy, and foggy environments- as well as at different times of day. Such diversity ensures that deep learning models trained on this dataset possess enhanced generalization capabilities for real-world applications.

Data Augmentation Techniques: To address the class imbalance, particularly for classes with a limited number of samples, data augmentation methods were employed. However, since certain signs possess specific orientations (e.g., ``No Right Turn'' versus ``No Left Turn'' in Fig. 4), applying automatic augmentation techniques such as flipping during training could introduce label errors. Consequently, data augmentation was performed offline. New and valid samples for underrepresented classes were systematically generated, carefully re-annotated, and added to the dataset to enhance and help balance the limited classes.Fig. 4Illustration of the offline data flipping methodology applied to increase sample diversity.Fig 4 dummy alt text

Illustration of the offline data flipping methodology applied to increase sample diversity.

Data Partitioning: To ensure a fair and reliable evaluation of the models, the collected data was partitioned using a six-fold cross-validation methodology. In this approach, the data was divided into six equally sized subsets. In each iteration, one subset was held out as the test set, while the remaining five were used for training. This partitioning was implemented with a fixed random seed to guarantee the reproducibility and comparability of all experimental results. The primary objective of evaluating the proposed dataset is to assess its quality, effectiveness, and generalization capability in training and inference of deep learning models. Given the direct impact of data quality on the performance of computer vision systems, the collected dataset was used to train and evaluate several versions of the YOLO family (v5–v12) [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13]] as well as RT-DETR [14]. This evaluation aimed to examine the dataset’s ability to represent Iranian traffic signs effectively and its compatibility with different model architectures. Evaluation and analysis of the ITSD dataset

Given the data-centric nature of this study, the primary objective of evaluating and analysing the results is to assess the quality, diversity, and efficacy of the proposed dataset in enhancing the performance of traffic sign detection models. To this end, a series of experiments were conducted using various deep learning models to examine the extent to which the data characteristics influence model accuracy and robustness.

In assessing the performance of computer vision models, metrics such as detection accuracy, inference time, and computational efficiency play a pivotal role; these indicators reveal the balance between accuracy and speed and form the basis for a qualitative comparison of models in real-world conditions.

In this study, the performance of the models is evaluated using Precision, Recall, and F1-Score metrics to measure the accuracy and correct identification of instances.(1)Recall=TPTP+FN=TP#groundtruths(2)Precision=TPTP+FP=TP#predictions(3)F1_Score=2×Precision×RecallPrecision+Recall

Mean Average Precision (mAP) is the primary metric for evaluating object detection of models. This metric represents the mean of the Average Precision (AP) calculated across all object categories and specified Intersection over Union (IoU) thresholds. This study reports two common variants: mAP@0.5, which computes the AP at a single, fixed IoU threshold of 0.5, and mAP@0.5:0.95, which averages the AP across multiple IoU thresholds from 0.5 to 0.95 in steps of 0.05. The IoU quantifies the overlap between the predicted bounding boxes and the ground-truth boxes. Higher mAP values indicate superior detection accuracy, as this metric provides a comprehensive assessment of both localization and classification performance.(4)IoU=AreaofOverlapAreaofUnion

In addition to accuracy metrics, temporal metrics were also examined to assess model efficiency. These metrics include pre-processing time, inference time, and post-processing time, which represent the duration required for input preparation, model execution for prediction, and the application of post-detection operations such as suppressing overlapping regions using Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS), respectively. Evaluating these metrics enables a simultaneous analysis of model accuracy and speed, playing a decisive role in selecting the optimal model for real-time applications.

The YOLO model simultaneously performs three critical tasks: predicting object location, detecting object presence, and classifying the object. A single loss function is insufficient to optimize all these components; therefore, distinct loss functions are responsible for optimizing each specific aspect. Consequently, during model training, these three loss functions are weighted and combined to enable the model to learn more accurately and detect objects more effectively. The figures below illustrate the behavior of these loss values throughout the training epochs, as well as the change in the mAP50 metric, averaged across the six folds for the different models. YOLOv10 exhibits the highest loss during training. YOLO employs a CNN-based architecture that directly predicts bounding boxes from extracted image features. This approach may yield lower accuracy and consequently higher loss when processing small objects, densely packed objects, or objects with diverse shapes. In contrast, the RT-DETR model utilizes a self-attention mechanism, enabling it to better comprehend long-range dependencies between different image features. This capability allows for more precise localization of objects and bounding box predictions, thereby resulting in lower training loss.

As illustrated in Fig. 8, the objectness loss gradually decreases throughout the training process, as the models become more optimized and demonstrate improved performance in distinguishing objects with increased exposure to data. Initially, YOLOv10 exhibits a higher loss compared to other YOLO variants and the RT-DETR model, which may be attributed to its greater architectural complexity, differences in initial configurations, or heightened sensitivity to varying data types.Fig. 8Comparison chart of object detection loss values throughout the training process.Fig 8 dummy alt text

Effectively, the initial disparities in learning rates and feature extraction methodologies diminish in influence, allowing the models to approach a common upper accuracy limit where no significant difference in their respective loss values is observed.

Furthermore, the utilization of an L1 Loss function, as opposed to DFL, contributes to more stable and improved convergence, thereby enhancing its predictive accuracy. Consequently, this model exhibits superior performance throughout the entire training trajectory and achieves the lowest final loss value.

The ITSD dataset, with its precise annotations, provides a comprehensive and indigenous benchmark for the development and evaluation of traffic sign detection models. By encompassing a wide geographic coverage, diverse lighting and environmental conditions, and imagery captured with various cameras at different speeds, it facilitates the training of highly generalizable models. However, certain limitations persist, including incomplete coverage of some rare sign classes, minor visual variations among similar signs, and the exclusion of very small or distant signs. Furthermore, the imbalanced distribution of samples and the complexity of some scenes can pose challenges for model learning. Despite these limitations, the ITSD dataset is recognized as a credible and practical resource for scientific research and the evaluation of advanced traffic sign detection models in Iran.

Limitations

Some of the traffic signs existing in the Iranian Traffic Regulation Handbook are not in the collected dataset, because they are rarely used in the roads. Future directions can include expanding the number of classes and instances to achieve comprehensive coverage of all signs stipulated in the Iranian Traffic Regulations, collecting imagery under more adverse lighting and environmental conditions such as dense fog and nighttime, and developing multi-task models capable of not only detection but also tracking and projecting sign positions onto digital maps. Furthermore, exploring the use of federated learning could enhance centralized models without the need for direct data sharing. This research demonstrates that combining a rich, indigenous dataset with advanced deep learning architectures can establish a robust foundation for developing intelligent traffic systems and enhancing road safety and efficiency.
