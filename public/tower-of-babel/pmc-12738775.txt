From prediction to sustainability: AI for smart energy management in wastewater treatment plants
Authors: Saeed Hamood Alsamhi, Ammar Hawbani, Mohammed A A Al-qaness, Niall O’Brolchain, Liang Zhao, Ahmed Al-Dubai, Mamoona Asghar, Redhwan Algabri, Mohsen Guizani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs), accurate energy forecasting is crucial for optimizing operations, promoting self- sufficiency, and ensuring sustainability. We compare and evaluate the performance of Machine Learning (ML) techniques for energy self-consumption (i.e., long-term memory (LSTM), support vector machines (SVM), recurring neural networks (RNN), gated recurrent units (GRU), and XGBoost), to forecast energy generation (EG) and energy consumption (EC) in WWTPs. The performance of models is evaluated using metrics (i.e., mean absolute error (MAE), mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), and root mean square error (RMSE)), based on a solid dataset of daily operating records. The findings show that GRU achieves the highest performance, with an RMSE of 0.102, MAE of 0.085, and R² of 0.978, followed by LSTM, GRU, and RNN. The models demonstrate temporal prediction capabilities, as well as driving energy efficiency and reducing operational costs in WWTPs. This paper offers insights into implementing ML for sustainable energy management in WWTPs to energy forecasting, enhancing energy self-consumption, and boosting operational efficiency and environmental sustainability.Keywords: Energy generation, Energy consumption, Self-consumption, Wastewater treatment plants, Sustainability, Transforming energy forecasting, Smart energy, Machine learningSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Environmental sciences, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Wastewater Treatment Plants (WWTPs) play a vital role in environmental protection and public health by removing contaminants from wastewater. The process of wastewater involves several energy-intensive stages, i.e., preliminary screening, primary sedimentation, biological treatment, nutrient removal, and sludge dewatering (as shown in Fig. 1), resulting in significant increase source of operating expenses and greenhouse gas emissions1–5. WWTPs are under pressure to improve efficiency while reducing energy consumption (EC) as urbanization increases and environmental restrictions tighten. About 3% of the world’s power consumption is attributable to WWTPs, predicted to increase as the population and industrial demand expand6,7. Furthermore, aeration in the biological treatment stage accounts for up to 60% of total EC and demand, such as pumps, mixers, and UV disinfection units. The energy demand in WWTPs is dynamic, affected by seasonal load variation, rainfall infil- tration, and variable influent characteristics (organic load, flow rate, etc.). Achieving sustainability goals requires maximizing energy use while preserving treatment effectiveness8. The challenges underscore the need for smart energy management to minimizes EC, optimizes self-consumption—using on-site renewable energy (e.g., solar or biogas). Therefore, effective energy forecasting and optimization are key role for achieving the demand. However, traditional static control systems cannot adapt in real-time to fluctuations in energy supply and demand.

Energy flow in WWTP.

Recently, advances in machine learning (ML) have created a transformative opportunity to address the limitations of traditional methods. ML models are designed for time-series forecasting, learned from historical operational data and dynamically making the right predictions. Random Forests, Support Vector Machines (SVM), XGBoost, and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks have demonstrated encouraging outcomes9. Because LSTM models can capture temporal dependencies and trends, LSTM is well-suited for time-series data10. Additionally, a novel method to improve the prediction accuracy is the use of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) for energy generation (EG) forecasting from renewable sources, including solar power11. The authors of12 applied CNNs’ prowess in spotting local patterns in temporal data to enhance solar energy output predictions, which was essential for efficient energy management in renewable systems. Therefore, ML allows WWTPs to plan better, shift loads, reduce reliance on the grid, and improve sustainability.

This paper focuses on applying ML models (i.e., LSTM, RNN, GRU, SVM, XGBoost, and RGU)- to forecast EC and EG in WWTPs. The proposed ML models are defined by the operational characteristics and constraints of a real plant, highlighting the most suitable models for capturing the temporal dynamics of EC and EG. The study compares ML models (i.e., SVM, XGBoost, LSTM, RNN, and GRU) for energy optimization in WWTPs. We evalute the model performance using (i.e., root mean square error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE) metrics, and mean absolute percentage error (MAPE)), to support sustainable WWTP management. The findings provide WWTP operators advice on model selection for incorporating predictive analytics into energy management systems.

Methodology

In this section, we employ ML approaches to forecast EC and generation in WWTPs to achieve energy self-consumption. The methodology consists of data preprocessing, model selection, model architecture design, and training strategies. WWTPs consist of multiple interconnected units, including preliminary treatment, primary clarification, biological treatment, secondary clarification, and tertiary treatment, which transform raw sewage into environmentally safe effluent. The thickening, digestion, and dewatering procedures are used to treat the resulting sludge. Every step involves operational setups for the removal of chemical, biological, and physical contaminants. Because blowers supply oxygen for microbial activity run continuously, the biological treatment stage (also known as the aeration system) uses the most energy.

Dataset description

“Dillman Road Wastewater Treatment Plant Electricity Consumption and Influent Volume”28. The facility locates in Monroe County, Indiana (USA), humid continental climate and characterizes (i.e., cold winters, hot summers, and variable solar radiation levels ). The seasonal data relevant for modeling solar EG and daily load behavior in WWTPs operations. The Dillman Road WWTP serves approximately 85,000 residents and treats an average of 12–15 million gallons per day (MGD) of wastewater28. The facility equips with on-site PV systems, enabling studies focused on energy self-consumption and forecasting.

The dataset records from January 1, 2013 to May 31, 2021 in daily-resolution records. The main variables pro- vided are billed_kWh (Daily billed grid electricity consumption (kWh)), solar_kWh (Daily on-site solar EG (kWh)), total_kWh (Total energy used from grid and solar (kWh)), kW (Peak daily demand (kW)) and MG (Influent wastewater volume treated daily (million gallons)). The dataset includes features (i.e., billed_kWh (energy billed to consumers), solar_kWh (energy generated from solar panels), total_kWh (overall EC), kW (power usage), and MG (Relevant to energy management)). The data spans allow for the capture of seasonal variations and operational changes28. The combination of grid consumption, solar generation, and influent volume enables both short-term forecasting and long-term performance evaluation using ML techniques.

Data prepossessing

Imputation techniques are used to identify and fix missing data points in the dataset.

Handling missing values

Here, the data preprocessing pipeline handles missing values in the dataset. We employ a Random Forest (RF) imputation technique to address the issue. Selection of RF imputation is motivated by robustness and the ability to model complex, non-linear relationships within the data, making it superior to simple mean or median imputation for this type of operational dataset. Missing values are filled using the median of features to create a complete, baseline dataset. We perform iterative imputation. For each feature with missing values, a Random Forest model was trained using all other features as predictors. The model was used to predict the missing values for the target feature. The procedure cycles features with missing data several times (we set the maximum number of iterations at 10). As the imputed values stabilize in between rounds, the process converges. The algorithm 1 converges when the maximum change in imputed values between iterations falls below the threshold of 0.001.

GRU

For time-series forecasting tasks like EG and EC prediction, the GRU, a potent variation of the RNN, GRU, is useful because it effectively captures long-term relationships in sequential data. GRU consists of a reset gate (rt) and the update gate (zt), which regulate the information flow, and are the gating mechanisms that GRUs use to solve the vanishing gradient issue in contrast to conventional RNNs. The update gate strikes a balance between integrating new input and preserving previously concealed states, while the reset gate decides how much of the past should be forgotten. The final hidden state (ht) uses a weighted update to merge the new and retained information after a candidate hidden state (h˜t ) selectively uses previous knowledge. With fewer parameters than LSTMs, this simplified design allows GRUs to learn more quickly without sacrificing prediction accuracy.

The GRU is well-suited for time-series data with temporal dependencies because of its design, which skillfully strikes a balance between integrating fresh input and preserving old knowledge. Statistical testing

We perform a paired t-test on the absolute prediction errors over the test set for both EC and EG to determine whether the performance difference between the GRU and RNN models is statistically significant. According to the null hypothesis, the two models’ prediction accuracy is not significantly different from one another. The test’s p-value for EC was p = 0.012, meaning that, at the 95% confidence level, GRU’s smaller error than RNN is statistically significant. Similarly, the test yielded p =. 0.038 for EG, confirming GRU’s statistically significant advantage. The findings support the superiority of GRU over RNN in WWTPs and verify that the observed variations in MAE and RMSE are not the result of chance.

Discussion of model performance

The results demonstrate the performance of ML models used to predict EG and EC in WWTPs to achieve energy self- consumption. Lower RMSE, MAE, and MAPE values show that the LSTM model is more accurate than the SVM and XGBoost models. The performance of LSTM is a good fit for time-series forecasting and captures the temporal dependencies included in the data on energy usage well. Therefore, LSTM is a strong for energy management applications because it learns from sequential data and adjust to changes in energy patterns. However, trade-off between interpretability and accuracy is crucial when choosing a model. Despite the superior prediction capabilities of the LSTM model, it might be a challenge to decipher the underlying correlations in the data. XGBoost provides more interpretability, which provides information on the importance of characteristics and decision-making procedures for stakeholders looking to comprehend the variables that influence the patterns of EG and consumption.

The results underscore the importance of interpretability and validation in predictive modeling, with a focus on LSTM for time-series forecasting in energy management. Energy forecasting using machine learning must transform operational processes, inform energy policy, and support broader sustainability initiatives. WWTPs enhance their contribution to climate change mitigation, energy efficiency, and sustainable resource management by integrating state-of-the-art techniques into their daily operations.

Due to its incapacity to directly model temporal dependencies, XGBoost performed noticeably worse in the experiments than GRU and RNN. XGBoost processes data as independent rows without regard to time order or sequence. Because GRU and LSTM are made to remember previous inputs, they work exceptionally well with sequential data, like time-series energy patterns in WWTPs. The GRU and LSTM models account for cyclical behavior, delayed effects, and nonlinear relationships over time to accurately forecast EC and EG based on operational schedules, weather, and inflow patterns. Additionally, XGBoost cannot automatically model the energy data’s temporal autocorrelation and seasonal fluctuations without the provision of extensive preprocessing and engineered lag features. This method is not used in this baseline model for consistency. Consequently, XGBoost is less appropriate for the problem structure than recurrent neural networks, even though it is helpful as a benchmark. The predictive results achieved by the GRU model MAE of 86.27 kWh for EC and 12.96 kWh for solar generation, translate into actionable insights for WWTP operators.

The ML models use to predict energy values in isolation and design to support data-driven decision-making within the energy management in WWTPs.Plant operators plan ahead for periods of high load and maximize the scheduling of high-demand equipment including centrifuges, aerators, and pumps using accurate EC forecasts. Predicting EG makes it easier to prepare for self-consumption, when operating requirements and available solar power are in line, lowering reliance on the electrical grid. The predicted insights assist balance supply and demand by offering useful input to energy storage systems or hybrid energy management platforms. Reduced operating costs, increased energy autonomy, and higher environmental sustainability are the results of integrating ML projections into WWTP.

Limitations and future directions

Limitations

First, a dataset from a single WWTP in a particular climatic zone is used to create and evaluate the models. The models’ proven ability to be applied to other plants with varying capacities, operating characteristics, or geographic locations is limited. Second, the forecasting models operate in an offline manner and have not been integrated into a live energy management system. Consequently, the challenges and benefits of a real-time deployment and closed-loop control remain unquantified. Third, the current framework relies on point forecasts and does not provide probabilistic uncertainty estimates, which are crucial for risk-aware operational decision-making. Forth, in order to improve the model’s contextual sensitivity and causal comprehension, the study only uses a core set of operational data, leaving out granular process variables and external meteorological information.

Conclusion

This paper has investigated the use of ML models for adaptive energy management in WWTPs to improve operational efficiency, optimize EG and EC, achieve self-consumption, and improve sustainability. It was concluded that GRU and RNN models performed the best to predict EG and EC, with the lowest RMSE and MAE, according to the comparison of the performance of different ML models. GRU became the standard for energy forecasting in dynamic systems such as WWTPs with an RMSE of 0.102, MAE of 0.085, and an excellent R² of 0.978. The study provided a performance comparison of the models, highlighting the advantages of RNN and GRU in spotting trends in time-series data. As a consequence, the RGU model prediction increases the energy self-consumption, optimizes the resource allocation, and enhances sustainability. Furthermore, the findings demonstrate that ML models support operational enhancements and reduce energy expenses in WWTPs, ultimately leading to more sustainable wastewater treatment. Future plans is to extend this work to include real-time deployment using multi-modal data.
