GraphChIAr: genome-wide super-resolution reconstruction of protein-mediated remote chromatin interactions by augmenting hi-C interaction maps with multiple ChIP-seq profiles
Authors: Hao Dong, Guo-Zheng Rao, Hao-Yu Wang, Xin-Ran Wu, Tong Xian, Bo-Qiang Wang, Pu-Feng Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProtein-mediated chromatin interactions are fundamental to gene regulation. However, experimental approaches such as Chromatin Interaction Analysis by Paired-End Tag sequencing are limited by data scarcity and high cost, while existing computational models are constrained by limited resolution and challenges in effectively integrating heterogeneous genomic data. To address these issues, we propose GraphChIAr, a regression-based deep learning framework that estimates chromatin interaction strength by augmenting Hi-C contact maps with various ChIP-seq profiles and genomic sequence information. A key advantage of GraphChIAr is its super-resolution capability, enabling accurate estimations of chromatin interactions from conventional resolutions down to ultra-high near-nucleosome resolution (e.g. 200 bp). By introducing genomic shift distance in GraphChIAr, we enabled it to predict remote interactions between distant genomic loci at a genome-wide scale. Cross-referencing results demonstrate high predictive accuracy for key mediating proteins such as CTCF, highlighting the benefits of integrating complementary genomic features. Together, GraphChIAr provides an effective computational tool to augment experimental data and advance the study of 3D genome organization. The source code of GraphChIAr is available at (https://github.com/don194/GraphChIAr).Keywords: chromatin interaction, ChIA-PET, ChIP-seq, super-resolution

Introduction

Chromatin is intricately organized within the nucleus in a 3D structure that governs essential cellular processes [1, 2]. The spatial organization of the genome facilitates interactions between distal regulatory elements and their target genes, which is crucial for proper gene expression regulation [3–6]. Understanding these complex 3D interactions is essential for deciphering gene regulation mechanisms and their implications in development and disease [7].

Over the past decade, several experimental techniques have been developed to investigate 3D genome organization. Hi-C [8, 9] and the higher-resolution Micro-C [10] have enabled genome-wide mapping of chromatin contacts, revealing hierarchical structures like compartments [11] and topologically associating domains (TADs) [12–14]. Chromatin Interaction Analysis by Paired-End Tag sequencing (ChIA-PET) [15], on the other hand, captures chromatin interactions mediated by specific proteins of interest, such as CTCF, RAD21, or RNA Polymerase II. In contrast to Hi-C, where a comprehensive view of all potential interactions is offered, ChIA-PET offers valuable insights into protein-specific chromatin loops that often have functional significance in gene regulation [16].

ChIP-Seq (Chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by high-throughput sequencing) is widely used for analyzing interactions between DNA and chromatin-associated proteins, providing crucial information about protein binding sites [17–22]. In general, ChIA-PET can be viewed as a hybrid of Hi-C and ChIP-seq [23]. However, ChIA-PET experiments are labor-intensive, time-consuming, and expensive [24]. The scarcity of publicly available ChIA-PET datasets is evident in the NCBI GEO [25] database. There are only 999 ChIA-PET experimental sets in the NCBI GEO database as of March, 2025. However, there are 10 128 Hi-C and 105 544 ChIP-seq sets at the same time. Therefore, artificially synthesizing ChIA-PET interactions from discrete Hi-C and ChIP-Seq data offers a promising strategy to expand the available ChIA-PET datasets [23].

Numerous computational methods have been developed to predict CTCF-mediated chromatin loops using different types of features, including DNA sequence-based approaches such as CLNN-loop [26], CTCF-MP [27], and DeepMILO [28], methods integrating genomic and epigenomic features such as Lollipop [29], and approaches combining CTCF binding and Hi-C data, such as LOOPbit [30]. However, these methods are not designed to learn and predict ChIA-PET interactions at a genome-wide level.

DeepChIA-PET [23] partially addressed this limitation by integrating Hi-C and ChIP-seq data to predict ChIA-PET interactions. Nevertheless, it formulates the task as a pixel-wise binary classification problem on a contact matrix, which limits its ability to capture the continuous nature of chromatin interaction strengths. ChIPr [31], on the other hand, models interaction prediction as a regression task to estimate interaction strength. However, it operates on predefined pairs of genomic regions, making it difficult to generalize to genome-wide prediction.

Recent advances in Hi-C prediction have demonstrated the effectiveness of integrating multi-modal data and modeling chromatin interactions across multiple genomic scales [32–41]. However, these approaches primarily focus on aggregate chromatin contacts and do not explicitly model protein-mediated interactions. These observations highlight the need for a computational framework that, similar to Hi-C prediction methods, integrates diverse sequence and epigenomic features to predict protein-specific ChIA-PET interaction strengths across the genome in a regression-based manner.

To this end, we present GraphChIAr, a novel regression model that integrates genomic sequence features, ChIP-seq profiles, and Hi-C contact maps to synthesize ChIA-PET interaction maps digitally. Our approach constructs a genome interaction network that aggregates multi-modal features across the genome to estimate ChIA-PET interaction strength values. Our GraphChIAr significantly elevated the resolution of a contact map from conventional resolution to nucleosome-level resolution. Our method provides a valuable alternative approach to investigate high-resolution genome 3D conformation in the context that experimental ChIA-PET profiles are limited or unavailable. This also enables scientists to acquire high-resolution chromatin interaction maps with limited resources.

Materials and methods

Data preprocessing

Hi-C and micro-C data for multiple cell types (GM12878, IMR90, and H1-hESC) were downloaded from the 4DN data portal [42, 43]. ChIA-PET datasets were downloaded from the ENCODE project [44, 45]. HiChIP datasets were downloaded from the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database [25]. For the 200 bp resolution data, BAM files were downloaded and processed using the bam2pairs and juicer tools [46] to generate Hi-C contact matrix files in hic format. The complete list of all Hi-C, ChIA-PET, and micro-C datasets in this study is provided in Table S1 in the supplementary, with their sources and accession numbers.

We extracted Hi-C contact matrices using the hic-straw [47] library. Four resolutions were tested in this study, including 10 kb, 5 kb, 1 kb, and 200 bp. Hi-C, micro-C, and ChIA-PET samples were extracted along the diagonal of a 2D contact matrix using a sliding window approach.

For the 10 kb resolution, a window size of 200 × 200 bins with a step size of 50 bins was used. At 5 kb resolution, the window size was increased to 400 × 400 bins, with a step size of 100 bins. To accommodate GPU memory constraints while maintaining high resolution, a window size of 500 × 500 bins with a step size of 250 bins was used during training at the 1 kb resolution. During testing, the step size was reduced to 125 bins for more granular predictions. For the ultra-high 200 bp resolution, which is also computationally intensive, we used a 250 kb window (1250 × 1250 bins) with a 500 kb step. Unlike the conventional-resolution settings, the 200 bp prediction was formulated as a super-resolution task, in which 1 kb Hi-C data were used as input to predict chromatin interactions at 200 bp resolution. This design enables GraphChIAr to generate near-nucleosome-level interaction maps from lower-resolution Hi-C data.

Epigenomic data, including ChIP-seq profiles for various histone modifications and transcription factors, were obtained from the ENCODE project [44, 45]. All datasets were downloaded as BAM files. The replicates for each epigenomic track were merged using pysam (https://github.com/pysam-developers/pysam), a Python wrapper for samtools [48]. The merged BAM files were then converted to BigWig format using bamCoverage from the deepTools [49] suite with a bin size of 10 bp and reads per genomic content (RPGC) normalization. A log(x + 1) transformation was applied on all normalized values in BigWig files before these values were used as inputs to the model. The complete list of ChIP-seq datasets used in this study is provided in Table S2 in the supplementary.

Model architecture

GraphChIAr is implemented in PyTorch and consists of four main components, as illustrated in Fig. 1, a feature extraction module, a DIFFormer aggregation module, a spatial pattern extraction module, and a prediction module. The architecture is designed to flexibly handle two distinct prediction tasks: standard intra-region prediction (within a single genomic window) and long-range inter-region prediction (between two distinct genomic regions, separated by a genomic distance). To enable genome-wide predictions, we introduce the offset mechanism, which allows the model to predict interactions between non-overlapping genomic regions separated by a specified genomic distance. Unlike traditional methods that focus only on intra-region interactions along the main diagonal, the offset mechanism shifts the model’s focus to other diagonals of the interaction matrix, enabling the prediction of long-range interactions. This approach enables the prediction of chromatin interactions across the entire genome at multiple resolutions.

The architecture of the GraphChIAr model for ChIA-PET prediction. The model consists of four main components: (1) feature extraction module that processes DNA sequence and ChIP-seq data through convolutional layers with PE; (2) spatial pattern extraction module that captures chromatin interaction patterns from hi-C matrices; (3) graph node aggregation module using DIFFormer to aggregate node features across genomic bins while leveraging hi-C interaction data as the graph structure; and (4) prediction module that combines these features to generate ChIA-PET interaction matrices. This module employs sequential 2D residual blocks with dilation rates of (r1, r2, r3) = (1, 2, 4) to expand the receptive field. For the ultra-high resolution (200 bp) experiments, these dilated blocks were omitted to reduce computational complexity and memory usage. The DIFFormer component integrates graph convolutional networks with global attention mechanisms to capture both local and long-range genomic dependencies. Feature extraction

The model inputs consist of a matrix of c epigenomic signals, Xepi∈Rl × c, which is defined over a genomic window of length l. Depending on the specific configuration (e.g. for CTCF prediction), a one-hot encoded DNA sequence (Xseq∈Rl × 5) may also be included. These available features are concatenated along the feature axis and transposed to form the input tensor X(0), where the feature dimension reflects the number of included data tracks (i.e. c or c + 5).

This tensor is processed by a series of 1D Residual Blocks to learn genomic bin representations. Prediction

The prediction head is responsible for integrating the graph-derived and spatial features to produce the final interaction map.

First, the pairwise node representation P∈Rn × n × 2h and the spatial features Fspatial∈Rn × n × h are concatenated along the channel dimension, resulting in a combined feature tensor Min∈Rn × n × 3h. Subsequently, a linear transformation, implemented as a 1 × 1 convolution, is applied to this tensor. This step serves to both fuse the features from the distinct pathways and adjust the channel dimension from 3 h back to the model’s core dimension h. The resulting tensor, which we denote as M(0)∈Rn × n × h, is then passed to the subsequent residual blocks.

A dropout rate of 0.1 is applied throughout the network for regularization. The core hidden dimension for the entire model is h = 32.

All training procedures for GraphChIAr and DeepChIA-PET were performed on a workstation equipped with a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4060 Ti GPU with 16 GB memory. C.Origami and HiCDiffusion were trained on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5090 GPU due to their higher computational power requirements.

We trained GraphChIAr using a chromosome-based data partitioning strategy, as in previous studies [35]. Specifically, the training set comprised all chromosomes except chromosomes 5, 10, and Y, with chromosomes 5 and 10 held out for validation and testing, respectively [35]. To accommodate different data resolutions, we used resolution-specific batch sizes: 32 for 10 kb, 16 for 5 kb and 1 kb, and 4 for 200 bp resolution data. Model training was performed using the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 10−4, coupled with a learning rate scheduler that applied linear warmup followed by cosine annealing. We employed early stopping with a patience of 10 epochs, and training was capped at a maximum of 80 epochs. The model checkpoint with the lowest validation loss was retained for inference. During prediction, the model processes the test chromosomes using the same sliding window approach as in training. This generates multiple overlapping prediction matrices for the targeted regions. To construct a single, coherent map for evaluation, the final value for any bin pair located in an overlapping area is calculated as the mean of all predictions covering that pair. All performance metrics were then computed by comparing this final, averaged prediction map against the corresponding regions of the ground truth experimental matrix. For cross-cell-type evaluation, models trained on one cell line were applied to data from other cell lines to assess the model’s capacity to capture both conserved and cell-type-specific chromatin interaction patterns.

Baseline comparisons with existing methods

To comprehensively evaluate the performance of our model, we compared GraphChIAr against several representative baseline methods: DeepChIA-PET [23], CAESAR [40], HiCDiffusion [41], and C.Origami [35], all of which have been used to predict chromatin interactions. For DeepChIA-PET, a state-of-the-art method for ChIA-PET interaction prediction, we adapted its architecture to perform regression rather than binary classification by replacing the final classification layer with a regression output and changing the loss function from binary cross-entropy to MSE. Following its original design, DeepChIA-PET utilized Hi-C contact maps and ChIP-seq signals as input features. To ensure a fair comparison, we maintained the original residual network structure while adjusting the number of hidden units according to the resolution: 32 units for the 10 kb resolution and 16 units for the more memory-intensive 5 kb resolution. We also matched training conditions to those used in GraphChIAr, with batch sizes of 16 and 8 for 10 kb and 5 kb data, respectively.

Furthermore, we evaluated two additional state-of-the-art models originally developed for predicting Hi-C contact maps: C.Origami and HiCDiffusion. C.Origami is an integrative framework that utilizes multiple genomic signals as inputs; in our study, it was configured with the same set of genomic features as GraphChIAr. In contrast, HiCDiffusion is a diffusion-based generative model designed as a sequence-to-map framework and was thus trained using pure DNA sequence data as its primary input according to its original design. To ensure a fair and computationally efficient comparison, both models were implemented using their official source code with a consistent hidden layer dimension of 256. For CAESAR, since the original implementation only supports 200 bp resolution, we reimplemented the contact profile model in PyTorch to accommodate our 1 kb resolution data. Our reimplementation follows the architectural principles described in the original publication, incorporating one-dimensional convolutional layers to extract local epigenomic features along the chromatin sequence and graph convolutional layers to model spatial interactions over the chromatin contact graph. For both CAESAR and our model, we used the same set of epigenomic features corresponding to each specific task as input signals. Specifically, our CAESAR implementation included two graph convolutional layers and two 1D convolutional layers, each with 32 hidden units. This adapted model serves as a baseline to assess the high-resolution predictive capability of GraphChIAr.

Aggregate peak analysis

Aggregate Peak Analysis (APA) was performed using juicer tools [46] to evaluate the focal enrichment of chromatin interactions. To obtain high-resolution anchor centers, the pre-called loop lists were filtered to retain only those interactions whose anchors overlapped with ChIP-seq summits, ensuring spatial alignment at a fine scale. The resulting aggregated matrices were used to calculate APA scores, including the Peak-to-Lower-Left (P2LL) ratio, to quantify the signal enrichment at the center of the loops relative to the background regions.

Results

GraphChIAr accurately predicts chromatin interactions across conventional resolutions

We benchmarked GraphChIAr on the GM12878 data against DeepChIA-PET, HiCDiffusion, and C.Origami. All models were trained on the GM12878 dataset with input features configured according to the architecture of each method (detailed in Section 2.5). For GraphChIAr, CTCF predictions utilized sequence information and CTCF ChIP-seq signals, Cohesin-associated interactions used RAD21 and SMC3 data, and transcription-associated interactions combined POLR2A ChIP-seq with H3K27ac and H3K4me3 signals.

We assessed performance using Pearson correlation, Non-zero Pearson correlation, and stratum-adjusted Pearson correlation coefficients (SCC). As in Fig. 2, GraphChIAr consistently demonstrated superior predictive accuracy. In direct comparisons of correlation coefficients for CTCF, RAD21, and POLR2A at both 10 and 5 kb resolutions, GraphChIAr outperformed DeepChIA-PET, C.Origami, and HiCDiffusion (Fig. 2A). Moreover, for all tested proteins, GraphChIAr consistently achieved higher stratum-adjusted Pearson correlation coefficients across genomic distances compared to the baselines (Fig. 2B). The high predictive performance indicated by these metrics is visually corroborated by the strong agreement between GraphChIAr’s predictions and the experimental data (Fig. 2C and Fig. S1). To further evaluate generalizability, we performed cross-protocol validation on HiChIP datasets. GraphChIAr yielded consistent and robust predictive results, demonstrating its reliability across different 3D genomic experimental platforms (Table S3 and Fig. S2). Taken together, these results indicate that GraphChIAr provides accurate predictions of chromatin interactions across different target factors and resolutions. The consistent performance improvements observed for CTCF, RAD21, and POLR2A highlight the robustness and flexibility of the framework.

Integration of complementary features improves predictive performance

To assess the contribution of complementary data sources beyond the primary target protein’s binding signal, we conducted ablation experiments for CTCF, RAD21, and POLR2A ChIA-PET predictions at both 10 and 5 kb resolutions (Fig. 3). We compared a baseline ablated model, which relied solely on the target protein’s ChIP-seq signal, with the full model, which additionally incorporated complementary features: DNA sequence information for CTCF, SMC3 ChIP-seq data for RAD21, and H3K27ac/H3K4me3 histone modification signals for POLR2A.

Across all settings, integration of complementary features resulted in measurable performance gains. As shown in Fig. 3A (bottom), the full model consistently achieved a higher Non-zero Pearson correlation than the ablated model. Prediction quality was also evaluated using stratum-adjusted Pearson correlation coefficients (SCC), which showed the full model’s performance was strong and often moderately improved across genomic distances (Fig. 3B).

To better understand the improved performance of the full model, we performed a Shapley-value-based analysis to estimate the contribution of each input type (Fig. 3C). The results showed that the primary target signal remained the main contributor for CTCF and RAD21 prediction, while complementary inputs also made substantial contributions. In contrast, POLR2A prediction relied more heavily on complementary epigenetic features, with histone marks contributing nearly half of the total importance. These results indicate that the improved performance of the full model arises from integrating multiple genomic signals rather than relying on a single input source.

In summary, the ablation and interpretability analyses show that integration of complementary features enhances GraphChIAr’s predictive performance. The improved concordance with experimental data and the substantial contributions from complementary inputs highlight the importance of incorporating diverse genomic signals in chromatin interaction prediction.

GraphChIAr enables cross-cell-type chromatin interaction prediction

To evaluate GraphChIAr’s ability to predict chromatin interactions across different cell types, we conducted cross-cell-type evaluation experiments between GM12878 and IMR90 cells. This experiment tests whether our model can capture both conserved features and cell-type-specific chromatin organization patterns.

As shown in Figs 4A and 4C, metrics demonstrate the robust generalization of GraphChIAr. The Pearson correlation coefficients for cross-cell-line predictions closely track the intra-cell-line benchmarks across genomic distances. For both IMR90 and GM12878, the cross-cell-line performance remains highly consistent with the results obtained from models trained and tested on the same cell type. The Nonzero Pearson Correlation similarly shows minimal performance decay in cross-cell-line tasks, indicating that the model effectively identifies true interaction regions even in a different cellular context.

Visual analysis further confirms these findings. As shown in Fig. 4B, we analyzed a genomic region on chromosome 10 spanning from 15.5 to 17.5 Mb. In GM12878 cells, our model successfully captured key interaction hotspots that match the experimental ChIA-PET data. Similarly, in IMR90 cells, GraphChIAr accurately predicted cell-type-specific interaction patterns. These findings indicate that GraphChIAr demonstrates robust performance in cross-cell-type prediction tasks. The model can generalize from training data to capture both conserved and cell-type-specific chromatin interaction patterns in a different cell line. This capability establishes GraphChIAr as a robust tool for predicting interactions in cell lines where experimental data are lacking.

We also examined the model’s performance in predicting ChIA-PET interactions at 1 kb resolution in GM12878 cells. Visually, GraphChIAr successfully recovered the key interaction patterns for CTCF (Fig. 5C, middle panel). However, the stratum-adjusted Pearson correlation (Fig. 5B, right panel) shows moderate concordance, due to the increased noise and data sparsity inherent to experimental ChIA-PET data at this high resolution.

We further explored GraphChIAr’s capability to model chromatin interactions at ultra-high resolution (200 bp), approaching a near-nucleosome precision. To accommodate the increased computational load and memory usage at this resolution, we optimized our GraphChIAr by removing the dilated residual blocks from the prediction head. To generate 200 bp resolution interaction maps, GraphChIAr interpolates the 1 kb resolution Hi-C data to construct a graph-based representation. As shown in the multi-resolution zoom-in process for CTCF (Fig. 5C), the experimental data at this ultra-high resolution appear sparse and visually noisy. In contrast, GraphChIAr provides a sharper contact map visually, with more defined interaction hotspots that align closely with the corresponding protein binding sites (Fig. 5C, bottom track). The corresponding multi-resolution predictions for RAD21 and POLR2A, which show similar patterns, are provided in Fig. S3 in the supplementary. The SCC analyses for the 200 bp resolution are also included in Fig. S4 in the supplementary.

To further evaluate the biological relevance of the predictions, we performed APA across multiple proteins and resolutions (Fig. 6). At both 1 kb and 200 bp resolutions, GraphChIAr predictions exhibit clear focal enrichment centered at loop anchors, forming the characteristic cross-shaped interaction patterns observed in experimental ChIA-PET data. In contrast, Hi-C signals alone show weaker and more diffuse enrichment. The P2LL scores of GraphChIAr are substantially higher than Hi-C and approach those of experimental ChIA-PET data across CTCF, RAD21, and POLR2A, indicating that the model effectively reconstructs biologically meaningful protein-mediated interactions at fine scale.

As a locus-level application example, we further examined the DPPA2–DPPA4 locus, a development- [51] and cancer-related [52] region previously reported to exhibit cell-type-specific RAD21-mediated (cohesin-associated) chromatin loops. Previous RAD21 ChIA-PET analyses showed that this locus is embedded in prominent loops in stem cells, whereas these interactions are absent in GM12878 and attenuated in K562 [53]. Using the GM12878-trained RAD21 model with cell-type-specific Hi-C and RAD21 ChIP-seq inputs, GraphChIAr predicted few candidate RAD21-mediated interactions in GM12878, prominent interactions in H1-hESC, and reduced interactions in K562 (Fig. 6B). This example illustrates how GraphChIAr can prioritize candidate protein-mediated interactions at loci lacking matched ChIA-PET data.

Overall, these results demonstrate that GraphChIAr effectively enhances chromatin interaction prediction from conventional Hi-C data to kilobase and sub-kilobase resolution, while preserving biologically meaningful loop enrichment signals.

Genome-wide estimation of remote chromatin interactions

To extend the utility of GraphChIAr from intra-window predictions to a genome-wide scale, we introduced an ‘offset’ mechanism. This mechanism enables the model to predict interactions between two non-overlapping genomic regions separated by a specified genomic distance (the offset). This is a critical step toward genome-wide scale analysis.

We evaluated this functionality at both 10 kb and 200 bp resolutions. It is well-established that due to the physical properties of chromatin, most significant interactions occur within a limited distance, making long-range interactions inherently sparse. Our model accurately captures this biological reality. As shown in Fig. 7B (right panel), at 10 kb resolution, the model’s predicted inter-region contact map (showing interactions between the 21–23 and 23–25 Mb regions) exhibits a largely sparse background, which is highly consistent with experimental observations. Crucially, the model does not merely predict a flat background; instead, it successfully and precisely identifies true, CTCF-mediated long-range chromatin loops, which are visible as concentrated signals in the bottom-left corner of the map.

Left: Standard intra-window (on-diagonal) prediction for the 21–25 Mb region. Right: Inter-region (offset) prediction, showing interactions between the 21–23 and 23–25 Mb windows. The concentration of predicted signals in the bottom-left corner accurately reflects the distance-decay properties of chromatin interactions, confirming that most contacts occur within ~2 Mb. Furthermore, the offset mechanism is indispensable for ultra-high-resolution (200 bp) analysis. Due to computational constraints, the manageable window size at 200 bp resolution is limited and cannot directly span long-range interactions. By introducing the offset mechanism, we can systematically predict fine-scale interactions across the genome at a feasible computational cost. As illustrated in the 200 bp offset prediction (Fig. 7D, right panel), the model utilizes this mechanism to accurately predict fine-scale chromatin structures that span the window boundaries at a 200 bp resolution.

In summary, the offset mechanism is a core function that successfully extends GraphChIAr’s capabilities to genome-wide prediction, enabling the systematic analysis of long-range interactions at both conventional and ultra-high resolutions.

Conclusion

We present GraphChIAr, a deep learning framework designed to estimate protein-mediated chromatin interactions by integrating Hi-C maps, ChIP-seq signals, and DNA sequence data. By utilizing graph neural networks, GraphChIAr effectively captures not only local chromatin interactions but also remote chromatin dependencies, enabling accurate modeling of chromatin conformation at a genome-wide scale. The model’s ability to integrate complementary features, such as DNA sequences and epigenomic profiles, significantly enhances its predictive performance. One of GraphChIAr’s key advantages is its multi-resolution capability, allowing for reliable interaction predictions down to an ultra-high 200 bp resolution. At this scale, the model effectively reduces noise in experimental data, revealing detailed chromatin structures that align with specific protein binding sites. GraphChIAr serves as a useful tool for studying chromatin interactions, especially in cases where experimental data are limited. This framework contributes to advancing our understanding of 3D genome organization and provides a means for exploring gene regulatory mechanisms in diverse biological contexts.

Despite its strong performance, GraphChIAr has several limitations. The model relies on the availability of protein-mediated chromatin interaction data for training, which restricts its applicability to proteins and conditions with sufficient public datasets. In addition, the diversity of datasets explored in this study remains limited, and further validation across broader experimental platforms would be beneficial. A possible future direction is to extend GraphChIAr toward a unified multi-modal screening framework by integrating Hi-C with multiple ChIP-seq profiles to identify protein-specific 3D chromatin structures. In this way, the model could serve as both a predictor and a computational filter, and incorporating more diverse epigenomic features may further improve its robustness and applicability.
