Senescent SPP1+ macrophages remodel the tumor microenvironment and promote the progression of early-stage lung adenocarcinoma featured with mixed ground glass nodule
Authors: Hong Zheng, Yan-qi Li, Xiao Lu, Jiao Zhang, Sha-sha Yu, Xu-feng Deng, Xiao-bing Liu, Man-yuan Li, Yi Cao, Qian Chen, Yuan Qiu, Quan-xing Liu, Dong Zhou, Ji-gang Dai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Most persistent ground glass nodules (GGNs) are eventually diagnosed as early-stage lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD). Delving into the molecular underpinnings of malignant transformation of GGNs will aid in the development of preventive and therapeutic strategies to interrupt the occurrence and progression of early-stage LUAD. Macrophages (Macs) are critical in the formation of immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME). However, its role in triggering the advancement of early-stage LUAD with mixed ground glass nodule (mGGN) is yet to be clarified. Utilizing scRNA-seq analysis on normal lung tissues, ground glass regions, and solid regions of mGGNs, complemented by multicolor immunohistochemistry (mIHC) and flow cytometry, we found an increase and peri-tumoral aggregation of immunosuppressive SPP1+ alveolar Macs and monocyte-derived Macs (Mo-Macs), with a particular emphasis on the Mo-Macs. This accumulation at the tumor margin could obstruct the penetration of immune cells into the tumor’s core, thereby promoting the malignant transformation of GGNs. SPP1+ Macs not only display a senescent phenotype but also harbor the potential capacity to foster tumor metastasis and angiogenesis. Clinical data from LUAD tissue array and TCGA-LUAD database revealed a positive association between the tumoral SPP1+ Macs percentage and poor prognosis. Furthermore, SPP1+ Macs could reshape the TME into an immunosuppressive state through interactions with other immune cells. In vitro and in vivo assays revealed SPP1 knockout inhibited the immunosuppressive polarization and senescence of Macs, reversed the immunosuppressive status of TME and reduced the growth of LUAD xenograft tumors. Our findings propose an emerging therapeutic strategy aimed at suppressing SPP1+ Macs, which could potentially decelerate or halt the malignant conversion of GGNs to early-stage LUAD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12943-025-02497-2.

Introduction

With the application of low-dose computed tomography (LD-CT) in lung cancer screening, an increasing number of patients with lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) are being diagnosed based on the imaging characteristics of pulmonary nodules (PNs) [1, 2]. Among all types of PNs, pure ground-glass nodule (pGGN) is a special subset with indolent tumor characteristics and a better prognosis [3]. However, most persistent pGGNs will progress to mixed ground-glass nodules (mGGNs), even solid pulmonary nodules (SPNs), and ultimately are diagnosed as early-stage LUAD [4, 5]. Nevertheless, due to the incomplete elucidation of the specific cellular and molecular mechanisms involved in this process, there currently exist no effective clinical strategies to prevent the malignant transformation of pulmonary nodules. Notably, mGGN concurrently contains both pGGN and solid components, providing a valuable research model for elucidating the malignant transformation of nodules and the initiation and progression of early-stage LUAD [6]. Although previous studies have analyzed the single-cell atlas of PNs [7, 8], they have not provided a deep exploration into the formation of the tumor microenvironment (TME) and “pro-tumorigenic niches” in mGGN.

Tumor associated macrophages (TAMs) with immunosuppressive characteristics were shown to be largely present immune cells in tumor niche. Evidence from the last decade suggests that these highly plastic macrophages (Macs) are vital to the occurrence, malignant development, and invasion of cancer [9]. In general, Macs come from two sources: monocytes derived from bone marrow, and tissue-resident Macs derived from embryonic tissues such as fetal liver and yolk sac [10]. For non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), investigations revealed Macs pools dynamically change throughout cancer progression, where alveolar Macs (AMs) decrease with malignant progression, while monocyte-derived Macs (Mo-Macs) increase [11]. Specifically, AMs phenotypically reprogrammed and spatially redistributed in the occurrence of lung cancer. These AMs trigger subsequent Mo-Mac recruitment [12, 13], which together leads to the activation and formation of the pro-tumorigenic niches [14]. Therefore, deciphering the unique changes in critical Mac subpopulations during the mGGN malignant progression and attempting to intervene these Macs may be a potential strategy for preventing the formation of the suppressive TME and malignant transformation from pGGN to early LUAD. However, the present research on Macs in lung cancer is predominantly focused on the non-nodular stage [15–17], whereas previous investigations into Macs within early-stage LUAD featured with PNs have been limited to the analysis of single-cell sequencing data [8, 18, 19], with no validation of the findings utilizing cellular or animal models.

Here, we comprehensively dissected and characterized the changes in the Mac subpopulations and formation in immunosuppressive barriers of early LUAD, by comparing single-cell transcriptome data from paired samples derived from mGGN patients and further validating these findings utilizing multicolor immunohistochemistry (mIHC), flow cytometry, and SPP1-knockout mice.

Methods

Human specimens

This investigation has been reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Xinqiao Hospital of Army (Third Military) Medical University (No. 145-01), and all processes follow the priciples of Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent were obtained from all patients involved. Four mGGN patients were enrolled with the following inclusion criteria: (1) CT imaging features of mixed nodules and diameter >2 cm; (2) pathologically diagnosed with LUAD; (3) previously untreated; (4) no other neoplastic diseases; (6) receive surgery; (7) provided informed consent. Exclusion criteria: (1) patients with severe COPD, coronary heart disease, and cerebrovascular diseases who are unable to undergo surgery. Paired ground glass regions (GGRs), solid regions (SRs), and normal pulmonary tissues were collected from each patient (12 samples total) after surgery according to the difference between the ground glass area and the solid area of mGGN in imaging and histology [20]. Normal pulmonary tissue was surgically removed 5 cm away from the malignant mGGN region. An additional 15 mGGN samples were collected for flow cytometry analysis according to the criteria described above. The LUAD tissue array including 98 patients and 180 samples was provided by Outdo Biotech (Shanghai, China).

Sample preparation and single-cell RNA-sequencing

After resection, each sample was minced and transferred to DMEM (ThermoFisher) containing 0.5% collagenase I/IV (Roche, Basel, Switzerland), 20U/ul DNAse I (Sigma), and 1 mg/ml neutral protease (Invitrogen). Incubate for 15–30 min at 37 °C, mix at 5-minute intervals. Next, add pH 7.4 PBS, and filter with Scienceware Flowmi 70-µm cell strainers. Following centrifugation (300g, 4 °C, 5 min), resuspend using 1 mL red blood cell lysis buffer (Sigma). Incubate at 25 °C, 2 min, cold centrifuged (300g) again, resuspended 400 µg/1mL BSAPBS solution, and screen with Scienceware Flowmi 40-µm cell strainers (VWR) incorporating 1 mL low-retention filter tips (Mettler-Toledo). Next, determine the live cell concentration by using TC20™ Automated Cell Counter (Bio-Rad). Tissue samples were processed within 3 h from surgical removal and profiled using the using manufacture-provided protocol (10X Genomics). Raw sequence were generated using the NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina) by Gene Denovo Biotechnology (Guangzhou, China). Raw count matrix was obtained for samples utilizing CellRanger 3.1.0, then mapped in R 3.5.3 with GRCh38 as reference, and read with Seurat v3.0.1.

Unsupervised dimensional reduction and clustering of single cells

Seurat R 3.0.1 was used to explore QC metrics and filter cells. Gene expression count data for each sample was normalized with the “NormalizedData” function, followed by scaling to regress unique molecular identified (UMIs) and mitochondrial content using “ScaleData” function (negative binomial model). Cells that had either more than 60,000 UMIs, over 7,000 or below 200 expressed genes, or over 20%/50% UMIs derived from mitochondrial genome, were removed. Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA) was applied to correct for batch effects, ensuring data consistency across different batches. The “RunTSNE” functions were used to identify the deviations among cell types (i.e., cells clustering and different cell subtypes clustering) with different resolutions (0.5 for global cells, ACs, FBs, and myeloid cells; 0.6 for T cells; 1.5 for ECs and B cells). Next, DEGs were determined using the Wilcoxon test implemented in the “FindAllMarkers” function, considered significant with an average natural logarithm (fold change) of at least 0.25, and a Bonferroni-adjusted P < 0.01. Clusters were validated by mIHC and flow cytometry using mGGNs samples.

Gene ontology and KEGG enrichment analysis

For cluster marker genes, group DEGs, and group stem genes, the Gene Ontology database (RRID: SCR_002811) and KEGG databases (RRID: SCR_018145) were used for functional annotation, whereas R 3.5.3 was utilized for enrichment analysis. The calculated FDR-corrected P-value was obtained FDR < 0.05 as enriched pathways.

Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

GSVA or GSEA was carried out using R package GSVA 1.34.0 (RRID: SCR_021058) or GSEA 4.0.3 (RRID: SCR_003199), respectively, on all the MsigDB database gene sets with default parameters. To evaluate variations in pathway activities between cell groups, we compared the activity scores for individual cells using a generalized linear model, and visualized using with R. Signifiacantly altered pathways in any cell set were identified. For GSEA, default parameters were used for latter analyses.

Contribution evaluation across subsets

Each cell subset’s contribution score was assessed using cell population and transcriptional difference, as described previously [21]. First, signature genes of alveolar, T, B, SPP1+ Macs, and FABP4+ Macs were determined by the top up-regulated genes from Normal to GGR, and GGR to SR group, by analyzing the bulk differential gene expression. Second, differential genes (DEG) of Normal and GGR groups or GGR and SR groups in all cell subsets were identified respectively. These genes belonging to the top-100 DEG set were considered as corresponding the signature genes. Third, the contribution score for each cell subset was determined as signature genes’ average FC score in this cluster.

Survival analysis

Transcription and TCGA clinical phenotype data of LUAD was retrieved via UCSC. A total of 510 patients with LUAD and 284 patients with LUAD stage I were included for survival analysis. Survival curves were generated using the R package survival and depicted with the “survminer::ggsurvplot” function. Log-Rank Test was performed to determine whether two survival curves were statistically significantly different.

mIHC

We enrolled formalin-fixed/paraffin-embedded samples via our hospital. LUAD tissue array was purchased from Shanghai Outdo Biotech. The antibodies utilized were anti-CD68 (M0876, Dako, RRID: AB_2074844), anti-CD163 (ab74604, Abcam, RRID: AB_1280790), anti-SPP1 (MA5-17180, Invitrogen, RRID: AB_2538651), anti-CD3 (IR503, Agilent), anti-CD8 (MA5-14548, Invitrogen, RRID: AB_10984334), anti-CD56 (MA5-11563, Invitrogen, RRID: AB_10985168), anti-FOXP3 (14–4776−82, Invitrogen, RRID: AB_467554), anti-SFTPC (ab90716, Abcam, RRID: AB_10674024), and anti-CD20 (PA5-16701, Invitrogen, RRID: AB_10980806). Antigenic binding sites were detected with the Opal 7-Color Manual IHC Kit (PerkinElmer, NEL811001KT) using provided manual. The slides were mounted with Prolong Gold Antifade. Multicolor immunohistochemistry intensity was generated with Mantra Quantitative Pathology Workstation (PerkinElmer). The TissueFAXS system (TissueGnostics Asia Pacific Limited, Austria) and StrataQuest (v7.0.1.165) was used for single-channel fluorescence signal information collection. The DAPI channel was utilized to verify cell nuclei. Based on these valid nuclei, thresholds were established according to the staining characteristics of each protein to delineate the cell populations expressing the protein positively. Counts and percentages of these populations were then calculated. Furthermore, the numbers and percentages of cells that were CD68+, CD68+SPP1+, CD68+CD163+, and CD68+CD163+SPP1+ cells were calculated relative to the total number of nucleated cells.

Trajectory inference of alveolar and CD8+T cell States

To generate a trajectory, a matrix of cells and gene expressions of four CD8+T cell clusters from Seurat R 3.0.1 were used as input. Single-cell trajectory was analyzed through the R package Monocle 2.6.4, and transcriptional trajectory was inferred after dimension reduction and cell ordering with parameters (sigma = 0.001, lambda = NULL, param.gamma = 10, tol = 0.001). Critical genes for differentiation were retrieved (FDR < 1e-5). These genes were catogorized according to expression pattern.

Gene regulatory network inference

To carry out transcription factor (TF) network inference, analysis was performed on the SCENIC R 1.1.2. TF binding motifs (TFBS) overrepresentation and network inference were determined with package Rcis-Target (v1.6.0) and GENIE3 (v1.8.0). In this process, 20-thousand motifs were employed. Then, a regulon activity score (RAS) for every single cell was obtained via the AUC scores using AUCell R 1.12.0 and the results were visualized using a heatmap with hierarchical clustering.

Tissue distribution analysis

To assess the tissue preference of SPP1+ Macs, FABP4+ Macs, and major cell subsets, the observed-to-predicted cell number (Ro/e) ratios were calculated for all cell types in normal and GGR [22]. Chi-square test was utilized for cell numbers eprediction.

Cell-cell interaction network construction

We ultized Cellphone DB to analyze the potential cell-cell interaction. Receptors and ligands (L/R) detected in over 25% cells within a given cell subset were included in further analysis. Circular plot and heatmap was used for cell-cell L/R interaction visualization, displaying the interaction L/R pairs and the predicted interaction strength. Based on the analysis of the significant enriched L/R pairs among cell types, the p-value was then plotted with pheatmap::pheatmap function 1.0.12 (RRID: SCR_016418).

Preparation and bone marrow-derived Macs (BMDM) culture

SPP1-knockout (C57BL/6NCya-Spp1em1/Cya) mice were purchased from Cyagen Biosciences (Suzhou, China), and wildtype (WT) littermate mice were used as control. The femur and tibia of mice was obtained and the epiphyses was removed to 15 ml tubes. Cells were sedimented for 15 min for removing large tissue mass, and bone marrow cells (BMs) were collected after centrifuge. 1 × 107 BMs were seeded in 10 cm dishes, cultured with RPMI 1640 medium with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, A5669701, Gibco), 1% penicillin-streptomycin (2587131, Gibco), stimulated by 0.02 mg/mL macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF, 315-02, PeproTech). After 7 consecutive days of differentiation, the morphology of the BMDM cells was observed. Then BMDMs were digested, counted, and prepared for in vitro stimulation and in vivo injection steps. The knockout of SPP1 gene in BMDMs was identified by western blotting (anti-SPP1/Osteopontin, 6696-RBM3-P1, NeoBiotechnologies).

Flow cytometry analysis

For human tissues, fresh tumor and corresponding paracancerous tissues were processed as before. The dissociated cells were stained using corresponding antibodies at the manufacturers’ recommended concentrations, 4 °C, 45 min, subsequently washed with PBS. Stained Macs with Invitrogen’s CD68-FITC, CD68-PE, IL-6-BV480, IL-10-PE/Cyanine7, TNF-α-BV421, Arg-1-PerCp, PD-L1-APC/Cyanine7, CD163-PE, SPP1-APC, and FABP4-FITC. For phagocytosis detection, Macs were co-cultured with pHrodo Green Dextran (P35368, Invitrogen) in vitro for for 20 min, and stained with CD68-PE/Cyanine7, SPP1-APC, FABP4-APC. Phagocytic Capacity was evaluated by detecting the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of FITC and the proportion of positive cells. For NK and regulatory T cells, cells were stained with BioLegend’s CD3-PerCP, CD56-APC, CD4-PE, and CD25-BV421. To examine the cytotoxic and exhausted T cells, we stained surface markers with the following BioLegend antibodies: CD3-PerCP, CD4-PE/Cyanine7, CD8-FITC, PD-1-PE, or LAG3-APC.

For xenograft tumors, the tumors were finely ground through a 200-mesh nylon screen to generate a single-cell suspension. Following this, red blood cells within the cell suspension were lysed using Red Blood Cell Lysis Solution (130-094−183, Miltenyi). Suspend with flow cytometry wash buffer and centrifuge 2000 rpm for 5 min. Blocked using CD16/32, and stain with CD45-FITC, F4/80-PE, CD11b-PerCP-5.5, and CD206-APC for detection of Macs. Cells were stained with CD3-PE-Cy7, CD4-PE, CD8α-FITC, and CD279-APC for detection of PD1+CD8+T, stained with CD4-PE and Foxp3-APCfor detection of Treg.

For BMDMs, treat using 20ng/mL IL-4 (214 − 14, PeproTech) and IL-13 (210 − 13, PeproTech), 20ng/mL IFN-gamma (315-05, PeproTech) and 100 ng/mL LPS (tlrl-pglps, Invivogren), or CMT64-CM (CM: fresh medium = 1:1) for 48 h. Then, cells were stained with CD45-FITC, F4/80-PE, CD11b-PerCP-5.5, CD86-PE-Cy7, CD206-APC, IL-10-FITC, and Arg-1-APC.

Mentioned samples were analyzed with FACS CantoII (BD) and Flow Jo v10.8.1 (RRID: SCR_008520). All antibodies used for flow cytometry detection was listed in Table S1.

Reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Treat 106 WT or SPP1-/- BMDMs (seeded in 6-well) with 20ng/mL IL-4 and IL-13 for 48 h. Total RNA was isolated using Trizol (15596026CN, Invitrogen) and then reverse transcribed into cDNA (RR047A, TAKARA). mRNA abundance of immunosuppressive marker genes were detected with TB Green® (RR820A, TAKARA). Primers used in this study were listed in Table S2.

β-Galactosidase (β-Gal) staining

For detecting senescence Macs percentage, β-Galactosidase Staining kit (9860, Cell Signaling Technology) was used according operation manual. Briefly, 1 × 105 WT or SPP1-/- BMDMs were seed in 12-well and treated with 20ng/mL IL-4 and IL-13 for 48 h. Fix and treat with β-Galactosidase Staining Solution overnight at 37℃. Photograph and analyze positive cells percentage.

In vitro co-culture experiments

Mouse LUAD CMT64 cells (RRID: CVCL_2406) were purchased from Fenghui Biotechnology (Human, China). For cell viability and proliferation detection, treat WT or SPP-/- BMDMs with 20ng/mL IL-4 and IL-13 and seeded in upper cell chamber. Seed CMT64 cells in the lower culture pore with standard medium (following manusfacture’s manual), 1 × 104 cells for viability and 5 × 104 cells for proliferation in 24-well plate. After co-culturing for 1, 3, 5 days, quantify cell viability of CMT64 cells with Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8, C0037, Beyotime Biotechnology) and cell number was counted, respectively.

For colony formation experiment, 1 × 103 CMT64 cells were seeded in 6-well plate, 4 × 105 IL-4 and IL-13 treated WT or SPP-/- BMDMs were seeded in upper cell chamber. Fix and stain post 7 days co-culture, count and analyze the clones.

For detection of migration ability, 1 × 105 IL-4 and IL-13 treated WT or SPP-/- BMDMs were seeded in 24-well plate, and 5 × 104 CM64 cells were seeded in the upper cell chamber and cultured with FBS-free DMEM. IL-4 and IL-13 treated WT or SPP-/- BMDMs were seeded in the upper cell chamber and cultured for 24 h. The cells were fixed and stained post 24 h co-culture, and cells on outside of chamber were photographed 20 field randomly and counted.

In vivo experiments

CMT64-luciferase (CMT64-luc) cells were established by infecting CMT64 cells with the luciferase overexpression lentivirus (pLV-CMV-LUC, Fenghui Biotechnology, MOI value: 100) and then selected with 3ug/ml of puromycin. 3 × 105 CMT64-luc cells were subcutaneously injected alone or co-injected with 6 × 105 IL-4 and IL-13 treated WT or SPP1-/- BMDMs into the inguinal region of C57BL/6 N mice. Ten days post injection, the tumor volume began to be measured every other day. Twenty-five days after inoculation, the mice were photographed and the average fluorescence intensity of the xenograft tumor was detected using IVISbrite™ D-Luciferin (122799, PerkinElmer) and living imaging system (Newton FT500 18.16a, Vilber Bio Imaging). Mice were euthanized following standard guidelines. The xenograft tumors were separated, photographed, and collected to assess the tumor weight. Subsequently, the xenograft tumor was digested into single-cell suspension for flow cytometric analysis of the immune cell status within the tumor. The Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethieseommittee of Army Medical University approved all animal experiment protocols (AMUWEC20234511).

For in vivo antagonism of CD8 and CD44, 200 µg of anti-CD8α (A2102, RRID: AB_3099521, Selleck) and anti-CD44 (BE0445, RRID: AB_3696168, Bioxell) antagonistic antibodies were administered intraperitoneally one day before cell injection, and subsequently administered every other day after injection.

Statistical analyses

SPSS 23.0 (IBM, USA, RRID: SCR_002865) and Graphpad Prism 8.0.1 (GraphPad, USA, RRID: SCR_002798) were utilized for the statistical analyses and preparation of graphs. Student t-test or Mann-Whitney U test were used according to the homogeneous of the variance. When comparing multiple datasets, one-way ANOVA with post hoc pairwise comparisons was employed, followed by Tukey (variance was homogeneous) or Dunnett’s T3 (variance was not homogeneous) for post hoc analysis. The Chi-square test or Pearson precision test was employed to compare the counting data. Survival analysis was performed using the KM method. Statistical significance was defined by a p < 0.05.

Results

Leukocyte cell subtypes are dynamically changing among SR, GGR, and normal pulmonary tissues

To investigate the cellular dynamics in mGGN progression, a total of 12 samples including paired SR, GGR, and normal pulmonary tissues were sequenced using scRNA-seq technology and a total of 122,720 cells were acquired (Fig. 1A, Figure S1A-1B, Table S3). Clustering analysis revealed that these cells could be grouped into 25 clusters and annotated as seven distinct cell lineages of alveolar cells (ACs), endothelial cells (ECs), epithelial cells, fibroblasts (FBs), and immune cells (myeloid, T-, and B cells) according to known cell subtype markers [23, 24] (Fig. 1B, C). Overall, GGR, SR, and normal pulmonary cells exhibited distinct expression profiles based on the UMI value (Fig. 1C, D), and the feature markers with enriched expression were identified in each subtype (Fig. 1E). From normal to GGR to SR, the cell ratios were sequentially increased for ACs (Chi-Square test, p < 0.001), as well as stromal cells of ECs and FBs, suggesting that the formation of tumor and TME was accompanied by cellular remodeling of normal lung tissue. However, in three different tissues, leukocytes constantly occupied the largest proportion and number, suggesting that the immune microenvironment might be crucial in mGGN malignant development. Based on the expression abundance and intensity of the marker genes, these 96,095 immune cells were divided into 22 groups (Figure S1C, D). Myeloid-derived cell subsets create a strong inhibitory TME in mGGN progression

Given the importance of Macs in the early-stage pro-tumorigenic niche, we analyzed the features of myeloid-derived cell subtypes in mGGN progression. From nine cell subsets, 53,231 myeloid-derived cells were included, and 21 clusters were identified. Overall, AMs were the main myeloid-derived cell population and detected in all samples, whereas Mo-Macs were mainly present in GGR and SR (Fig. 2A, B). The proportions of AMs and monocytes were dramatically reduced across normal, GGR, and SR tissues, but the Mo-Mac and dendritic cell (DC) proportions were markedly increased (Fig. 2C) (Chi-Square test, p = 0.003). Surprisingly, all AMs highly expressed anti-inflammatory markers (Fig. 2B), and enrichment analysis confirmed a strong reduction of GSVA score in IFN-α/γ response, reactive oxygen species producing and cholesterol homeostasis (specific function of AM) [25] pathways in GGR- and SR-derived AMs (Fig. 2D) and Mo-Macs (Fig. 2E). Pseudotime analysis further revealed a distinct trajectory from monocytes to Mo-Macs, and the expression of immune-suppressive Mac markers was significantly elevated along pseudotime (Fig. 2F, G). Thus, these two types of Macs may exhibit a predominant anti-inflammatory polarization in the early-stage of lung nodule development. Elevated expression of IL-1β and CXCL8 (a pro-inflammation marker) indicated that granulocytes may mediate active inflammatory response in mGGN. CD1c+ DCs (Langerhans cells, LCs), CD207+CD1a+ LCs, CCR7+LAMP3+ DCs (activated DCs), CD141+XCR1+ DCs (cross-presentation DCs), and plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) constitute the DC population [26] in mGGN (Figure S2A, B). Conversely, pDCs represented an immunosuppressive phenotype with elevated leukocyte immunoglobulin-like receptor (LILR) family genes [27] (Figure S2D).

Immunosuppressive SPP1+ Macs promote pro-tumorigenic niche production

We next sought to reveal the role of Mac subsets (AM and Mo-Mac) in the construction of the early-stage pro-tumorigenic niche. Cellphone DB analysis demonstrated Mac subsets consistently occupied the central position of cell-cell communication in different tissue types (Fig. 3A, B, Figure S3A). Notably, based on the the interaction strength between ligand-receptor pairs among cells, three SPP1-related ligand-receptor pairs appeared in both of the two Mac subsets during the mGGN malignant progression (Fig. 3C). Among them, the SPP1/PTGER4 pathway was shown to have a major impact on the immunosuppressive barrier and pro-tumorigenic niche for intestinal adenoma [16, 28]. Based on a recent publication showing that the main Mac subset was FABP4+ Macs in LUAD [16], we included the SPP1 and FABP4 genes for subsequent analysis. In both Mac subsets, as mGGN malignancy progresses, FABP4 expression decreases, while SPP1 expression increases (Fig. 3D, Figure S3B). We further subdivided the two Mac subsets into SPP1+ and FABP4+ Macs (Fig. 3E), and analyzed their dynamic changes with the progression of tissue malignancy (Fig. 3F). Consistent with the expression analysis, although FABP4+ AMs were the most abundant, they significantly “collapsed” with the progression of tissue malignancy. SPP1+ Mo-Macs significantly increased with the progression of malignancy (Chi-Square test, p = 0.048). However, the absolute number of SPP1+ Mo-Macs was not high in SR, which may be due to the increased difficulty in infiltration of SR. Further, tissue flow cytometry of fresh mGGN surgical resection specimens verified the accuracy of the results of our analysis (Fig. 3G). Additionally, we investigated the spatial distribution of SPP1+ Macs in mGGN and their prognostic value for lung cancer patients. Because of the evident anti-inflammatory characteristics of Macs (Fig. 2B), we utilized CD68, CD163, and SPP1 for multicolor IHC analysis of mGGN tissue. Compared to paired normal tissue, Macs in mGGN displayed clear redistribution (Fig. 4A). Surprisingly, the SPP1+ Mac subset exhibited significant spatial heterogeneity, with the majority of SPP1+CD163+ Macs surrounding the cancer nest, while only a few SPP1+CD163- Macs were found within the nest. This also further demonstrated that the SPP1 Macs subset with anti-inflammatory characteristics promotes the construction of the early-stage immunosuppressive barrier. We also performed the same mIHC analysis on a cohort of 98 LUAD patients (Figure S3D, Table S4). A cohort of multiple advanced LUAD patients revealed a statistically significant increase in the proportion of CD68+SPP1+ Macs and CD68+CD163+SPP1+ Macs, although the increase in CD68+CD163+SPP1+ Macs was relatively low (Fig. 4B, Table S5). Prognostic analysis showed that the ratio of CD68+CD163+SPP1+ Macs was a significant prognostic indicator, where patients with a high ratio have a poor overall survival (p = 0.013). A similar trend was seen among patients with stage I LUAD although the trend was not statistically significant (Fig. 4C). Dual factor prognostic analysis using TCGA-LUAD data showed that patients with high SPP1 expression and high M2 Mac infiltration had the worst prognosis, although it is not statistically significant (Fig. 4D). LUAD samples characterized by high percentage of CD68+CD163+SPP1+ Macs have higher T, N, and TNM stage (Table S6). Pan-cancer data from TISCH database showed that SPP1 was specifically expressed in monocyte Mac cells in a variety of human tumors (Figure S3E-G). SPP1+ Macs exhibit a senescent phenotype and reshape the TME during malignant progression of mGGN

We next explored the functional differences among the three Mac types (FABP4+ AM, SPP1+ AM, and SPP1+ Mo-Mac). Differential analysis of functional genes revealed that SPP1+ Macs highly expressed senescence-associated genes, and exhibited a pro-metastatic and pro-angiogenic capacity. SPP1+ Mo-Macs expressed more M2 markers and immune suppressive genes as compared to the other two Macs, indicating a key contributing role in immunosuppressive TME (Fig. 4E). We also performed functional enrichment based on the differentially expressed genes between FABP4+ and SPP1+ Macs in AMs. Similar to previous results in mGGN malignant progression, the GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed a higher enrichment of immune response-related regulatory pathways in SPP1+ AMs (Figure S4A, B). GSVA further supports that SPP1+ Macs exhibit higher scores in immunosuppressive pathways compared to FABP4+ Macs (Figure S4C). This suggests that SPP1+ AMs may have a more potent immune negative regulatory function [29].

Subsequently, flow cytometry analysis of Macs in mixed GGN tissue samples also confirmed the above findings: The proportion of Arg-1+/PD-L1+ (immunosuppressive) cells was significantly higher in SPP1+ Macs than FABP4+ Macs, while that of TNF-α+/IL-6+ (immunostimulatory) cells was significantly lower (Fig. 4F). SPP1+ Macs also had slightly more IL-10+ cells, though not statistically significant (Figure S4D). Meanwhile, SPP1+ Macs also exhibited weaker phagocytic capacity (Lower pHrodo Green+ percentage and mean fluorescence intensity) (Fig. 4G and Figure S4E). Interestingly, the PD-L1 protein level in LUAD patients positively correlated with the number of infiltrating CD68+CD163+SPP1+ Macs (Fig. 4F), and Ro/e analysis also showed distribution of SPP1+ Macs inversely correlated with T cells during Normal to GGR progression, the first step of malignant evolution (Fig. 4G). These findings indicate that SPP1+ Macs are the key contributors in reshaping the TME during malignant progression of mGGN.

To better understand the differences in CD8+ T cells among normal, GGR, and SR, we inferred the functional changes across the four clusters of CD8+ T cells using unsupervised trajectory analysis. Tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells showed a dynamic functional spectrum of three different states according to the deferentially expressed genes (Fig. 5C, Figure S5B, C, Table S7). Specifically, clusters 1 and 8 consisted mostly of CD8+ T cells from the normal tissue and State 3, whereas clusters 2 and 4 occupied a large proportion of SR and GGR CD8+ T cells that mostly derived from State 1 (Fig. 5D, Figure S5D). GSVA between State 3 and State 1 branches revealed a significantly high enrichment score of cytotoxic T lymphocyte immunology pathways in State 3, such as antigen recognition, T cell receptor signaling, and effector functions (Figure S5E). Moreover, the exhaustion markers of LAG3 and PDCD1 (PD-1) were highly expressed in clusters 2 and 4 (State 1), whereas clusters 1 and 8 (State 3) showed high expression of granzyme and IFN-γ secretion (Figure S5A). The results indicated that CD8+ T cells in State 1 inclined toward transition (supported by the expression of GZMK) and pre-exhaustion [30]. Additionally, CD8+ T cells in State 1 from GGR and SR tissues, expressed more inhibitory/transitional molecules, but fewer effector markers than normal tissue. We also observed that the cytotoxic CD8+ T cell population gradually decreased and converted into exhausted cells with the progression of mGGN. The expression of CD28 and PD-1 was markedly increased in CD8+ T cells of State 1 in SR, indicating that PD-1 inhibition therapy may be an effective treatment regimen for SPN [31, 32]. In contrast, CD8+ T cells of State 1 in GGR highly expressed LAG3 and CTLA4 (Fig. 5E, Figure S5F). GRN analysis with SCENIC revealed that the regulons of various effector maintenance-related TFs, such as KLF2, KLF3, BATF, and TBX21 (T-bet), have higher activity score in State 3, whereas the activity scores of regulons for FOXO1, TCF7 (naïve and memory-related TF), IRF4, and EOMES (exhaustion promotion) [33] were elevated in State 1. Activation of AP-1 (FOS and JUN), NFKB2, and RUNX3 in State 1 was presumably associated with the remaining IL-2 production in cluster 4 (Fig. 5F, Figure S5G, H). However, the increased proliferation-relevant gene expression suggests that the CD8+ T cells in State 1 could be in the early stage of dysfunction and differentiated from cytotoxic cells [30, 34].

NK (cluster 5) and NKT cells (cluster 3) exhibited a significantly increased capacity of IFN-γ and granzyme secretion (Figure S5A). Thus, infiltrating NK and NKT cells may be essential to oppose the growth of malignant cells in early LUAD. STEM analysis further confirmed that these two types of cells progressively lost their effector functions in GGR and SR. The expression of heat shock proteins [35, 36], which associated with the activation and maintenance of function of NK/NKT, was progressively decreased across normal, GGR, and SR tissues, whereas the expression of the negative immune regulator METRNL was significantly increased, indicating the loss of effector function (Figure S5I). As shown in Fig. 5G and H and Figure S5J, flow cytometry and multicolor immunohistochemistry also illustrated that CD8+ T and NK/NKT cells gradually decreased and regulatory T cells (Treg) increased in mGGN progression. Analysis of cell-cell interactions further supported that SPP1+ Macs strongly interact with these T/NK cells (Fig. 5I, Figure S5K).

GSVA revealed that follicular B (FB) cells in GGR and SR have a low enrichment score in cell proliferation and differentiation (MYC, mTOR, IL-2, and IL-6 pathways) and inflammatory response (IFN and complement pathways) (Fig. 6D), suggesting that FB cells become exhausted despite an increase in the FB cell population. Moreover, feature genes of C6 closely correlated with immune response and activation, including PKM, CSK, ENO1, PSME2, LAT2, PGLS, CD40, and CXXC5 (Fig. 6E), and increased mRNA abundance of these genes were obnserved in C6 of GGR and SR compared to that of normal (Fig. 6F). Consistent with follicular B cells, GCB cells in SR have a lower GSVA score in proliferation and differentiation capacity pathways, whereas GCB cells in GGR were similar to those in normal tissue with potential anti-tumor effects during early tumor initiation (Fig. 6G). C14 and C13 were mostly derived from GGR and SR, and located in the light zone of GCB cells, they expressed low MKI67, implying a progressive change to low proliferation in neoplastic tissue. Cell-to-cell communication analysis showed that SPP1+ Macs have a close connection with GCBs and pDCs (Fig. 6H, Figure S6D). SPP1 knockout impedes the polarization of BMDM towards an immunosuppressive and pro-tumorigenic phenotype

To further verify the pro-tumorigenic role of SPP1+ Mac on the malignant progression of GGN, BMDM was isolated and generated from bone marrow of wildtype or SPP1-knockout mice, and depletion of SPP1 was confirmed by western blotting (Figure S8A). Morphologically, SPP1 knockout did not affect the morphology and generation of BMDM 7 days post M-CSF treatment (Fig. 8A, Figure S8B). However, the absence of SPP1 significantly limited the immunosuppressive polarization of BMDM stimulated by IL-4 and IL-13 (Fig. 8B) without affecting the activation induced by IFN-γ and LPS (Figure S8C) Furthermore, depletion of SPP1 also inhibited the CMT64-CM induced immunosuppressive polarization of BMDM (Fig. 8C). These results were supported by the RT-qPCR and flow cytometry, classic immunosuppresive markers, including IL-10, Arg-1, and Mrc-1, were elevated in BMDM after IL-4 and IL-13 exposure, but this trend was observably diminished when SPP1 was knocked out (Fig. 8D and E). Transwell assays demonstrated that SPP1 knockout significantly impaired IL-4 and IL-13-induced BMDM migration (Fig. 8F). In previous articles, the senescence of Macs has been associated with its immunosuppressive function and tumor-promoting effect, and SPP1 is considered to be one of the markers of senescent Macs [40]. Our results also suggest that SPP1+ Macs in mixed pulmonary nodules may exhibit senescence and promote nodular malignancy (Figs. 3 and 4). Next, we explored the effect of SPP1 knockout on the senescence status of BMDM. As we expected, SPP1 knockout significantly reduced the percentage of β-Gal positive BMDMs after IL-4 and IL-13 treatment (Fig. 8G). Combining these results, we speculate that Macs may lose their tumor-promoting function after SPP1 knockout. To confirm this hypothesis, we examined the effect of SPP1-/- BMDM on the tumor characteristics of mouse LUAD cell CMT64 (Fig. 8H). After immunosuppressive polarization of BMDM stimulated by IL-4 and IL-13, WT BMDM can promote the cell viability and proliferation of CMT64 cells, but SPP1-/- BMDM cannot (Fig. 8I). Similarly, IL-4 and IL-13 treated WT BMDM exhibited a significant increase in clone number (Fig. 8J) and improved migration capacity (Fig. 8K) of CMT64 cells, but these enhancements were also abolished after SPP1 knockout. All these findings demonstrate that SPP1 is critical for immunosuppressive polarization of BMDM, and SPP1 knockout inhibited the promotion role of BMDM in the proliferation and migration of CMT64.

SPP1-/- BMDM reverses the immune-suppressive state of TME and suppresses the growth of CMT64 subcutaneous tumor in vivo

Macs play a crucial role as a key component of the TME, actively involved in regulating the status of immune cells within this complex system [9]. To validate the regulatory effects of SPP1 Macs on the TME in early LUAD, we utilized a in vivo model in which WT or SPP1-/- BMDM and CMT64-luc cells were co-injected into the inguinal region of mice in a ratio of 2:1 (Fig. 9A). Day 25 after injection, living imaging results demonstrated the mean fluorescence intensity of the co-injection group of CMT64 and WT BMDM was significantly higher compared to CMT64 group. In contrast, the mean fluorescence intensity in the co-injection group of CMT64 and SPP1-/- BMDM was significantly lower compare to other groups (Fig. 9B and C). In addition, the data of in vivo growth curve and tumor weight also revealed that WT BMDM significantly promoted the growth of CMT64 cells, whereas SPP1-/- BMDM inhibited their proliferation rate in vivo (Fig. 9D and F). Considering that the knockout of SPP1 will significantly restrict the polarization of BMDM towards an immunosuppressive phenotype induced by cytokines or tumor cell conditioned medium (Fig. 8B and C), we speculate that the TME in xenograft of co-injection SPP1-/- BMDM might not be in a markedly immunosuppressive state compared to the co-injection of WT BMDM. Subsequently, the immune cell composition within the subcutaneously implanted tumors was characterized using flow cytometry. Analysis results (Fig. 9G and H) demonstrated varying degrees of boost in the percentage of PD-1+CD8+T cells and Tregs in xenograft generated from co-injection of WT BMDM, however, this increase trend did not appear in the xenografts generated from co-injection of SPP1-/- BMDM, where the percentage of PD-1+CD8+T cells and Tregs decreased significantly comparing other two groups. Furthermore, we also measured the proportion of immunosuppressive Mac cells within the xenograft tumors on day 25 post-injection. As a result, the proportion of CD206+Mac cells in the xenograft co-infection with SPP1-/- BMDM was significantly decrased compared to other groups (Fig. 9I). Detection of pro-inflammatory (IL-1β/2/6, IFN-γ, TNF-α, MCP-1, and MIP-1α) and anti-inflammatory (IL-4/10 and TGF-β) factor expression at both mRNA and protein levels in xenograft tumor tissues revealed that the expression of pro-inflammatory factors were markedly upregulated following SPP1 knockout as compared with WT BMDM or CMT64 group (Figure S9). These findings indicate that SPP1-/- BMDMs are unable to induce immunosuppression in the microenvironment; instead, they could activate anti-tumor immune responses by upregulating the levels of pro-inflammatory factors in the microenvironment.

To exclude the influence of other macrophages on the tumor-promoting effect of SPP1+Macs, we pre-treated mice with clodronate liposomes (CL) to deplete non-target macrophages before tumor cell inoculation. First, we generated RAW264.7 cells with SPP1 overexpression (OE-spp1) and observed that SPP1 overexpression enhanced their responsiveness to IL-4 and IL-13-induced immunosuppressive polarization (Figure S10A-10D). As expected, following CL-mediated macrophage depletion, OE-spp1 RAW264.7 significantly accelerated CMT64 tumor growth in mice at an early stage (Figure S10E-10I). After blocking CD8+ T cells using a CD8α-antagonistic antibody (Figure S11), we observed that the inhibitory effect on tumor growth in the SPP1-/- group was abolished. This indicates that the tumor-suppressive effect of SPP1-/- Macs is dependent on CD8+T cells (Fig. 9J and M). In vitro co-culture experiments confirmed that SPP1+ Macs significantly suppressed CD8+T cell proliferation and IFN-γ secretion, while SPP1-/- Macs lost this inhibitory capacity (Figure S12). Subsequently, in vivo CD44 antagonism experiments were conducted to validate whether the regulation of CD8+T cells by SPP1+Macs is mediated by the SPP1/CD44 axis. The tumor growth curve demonstrated that the promoting effect of WT BMDMs on CMT64 tumor growth was abolished following CD44 antagonism, with the growth rate and weight of tumors in the antagonism group significantly lower than those in the isotype control group (Fig. 9N and Q). Furthermore, SPP1-PTGER4 signal was also investigated using a specific inhibitor E7046. In vitro co-culture experiments of macrophages and CD8+ T cells showed that E7046 could attenuate the inhibitory effect of WT Macs stimulated by IL-4 + IL-13 on CD8+ T cells, restoring the proliferative capacity (CFSE) and IFN-γ secretion capacity of CD8+ T cells (Figure S13). These results show that the inhibition of CMT64 tumor growth after SPP1 knockout in BMDM is CD8+ T cell-dependent, and SPP1+Macs suppress the function of CD8+ T cells via the SPP1/CD44 and SPP1/PTGER4 signal.

Discussion

Based on screening results from the United States National Lung Screening Trial (NLST) and the Chinese population, GGN accounts for 84.87% of lung nodules ultimately diagnosed as early lung cancer. Furthermore, early lung cancer have a 5-year survival rate exceeding 85%, and timely appropriate treatment can reduce cancer-related mortality in these patients by 20% [41–43]. Understanding the key mechanisms driving the malignant progression of pGGN and targeting them in the management and treatment of patients with pulmonary nodules is necessary for advancing the lung cancer diagnosis and therapy.

Compared to previous studies on pulmonary nodules [44], here we focused on the role of highly plastic TAMs in promoting formation of pro-tumorigenic niches during the malignant transformation of pGGN, and attempted to identify key Mac populations therein. In solid stage lung cancer, molecular crosstalk between cancer cells and TAMs has been widely reported to facilitate cancer growth and invasion [45–47]. Furthermore, reprogramming TAMs through intervention has shown great potential in the treatment of lung cancer [48, 49], and has achieved near-radical results in the experimental stage [50]. However, studies investigating key Mac populations in the progression of pGGN are scarce, and the findings from solid lung cancer may not be entirely applicable to patients with pulmonary nodules.

Several recent studies emphasized the important role of SPP1+ Macs population in cancer progression [17, 51]. In hepatocellular carcinoma, SPP1+ Macs form an immunosuppressive barrier at the border, thus limiting immune infiltration into the tumor core [52]. We further analyzed the role of SPP1+ Macs in promoting pro-tumorigenic niches formation in mGGN, and our mIHC results similarly showed that SPP1+ Macs with anti-inflammatory characteristics only appear around the cancer nest. Our data also revealed that SPP1+ Macs exhibit a senescent phenotype, which is consistent with recent papers [40, 53] that senescent AM accumulate early in neoplasia of mice and human lung cancer tissues, with property of promoting tumorigenesis by adjusting the antitumor T cell response. Meanwhile, SPP1+ Mac was also recognized as tumor-promoting Macs that contribute to development of smoke-related lung cancer [54]. This indicates that targeted clearance of SPP1+ Macs might be an effective strategy to reverse mGGN malignancy. Different from exited view that SPP1+ Mac was only derived from monocyte, our results uncovered that two distinct derived Macs, SPP1+ AM and Mo-Mac, stand out as a pivot in the malignant development of mGGN which finally diagnosed as early LUAD. SPP1+ Macs exhibit different functional role from FABP4+ Macs and could play a regulatory core in recruiting and transforming immune cells in TME. Although our results indicate that SPP1+ Mac exhibits a more notable immunosuppressive effect compared to FABP4+ Mac, FABP4+ Mac cells may also possess a certain degree of microenvironment regulatory function due to their high proportion in malignant lung nodule tissues. Interestingly, existing evidence suggests that FABP4+ Mac cells have multiple pro-inflammatory effects. For instance, FABP4+ Mac promotes the production of pro-inflammatory factors, thereby enhancing anti-tumor immunity in NSCLC [55]; the secreted FABP4 protein can promote the polarization of Mac towards the M1 phenotype [56, 57]. Additionally, a significant decrease in the proportion of FABP4+ Macs was observed in brain metastases from early and advanced primary tumors as well as in lung cancer, compared to normal tissues [29]. Our flow cytometry analysis on human pulmonary nodule tissue samples also confirmed that FABP4+ Macs express higher levels of IL-6 and TNF-α, lower levels of Arg-1 and PD-L1, and exhibit stronger phagocytic capacity compared to SPP1+ Macs. These results suggest that FABP4+ Macs could not drive immunosuppression in the TME, and there may be a negative correlation between them and the progression of lung cancer.

According to one perspective, in cases where the tissue is exposed to external stimuli such as cancer, certain Mo-Macs undergo a gradual transformation into more plastic tissue-resident Macs, particularly when the severity and duration of the injury are significant [13]. Considering the obvious “collapse” phenomenon of AMs in the mGGN malignant progression, and the proven important role of AMs in the formation of pro-tumorigenic niches in early-stage lung cancer [11, 58], we speculate that in the early-stage lung cancer, some monocyte and AMs together constitute the anti-inflammatory CD163+SPP1+ Mac population, and are located at the cancer boundary, forming an immunosuppressive barrier through cross-linking with multiple immune cells. Our results innovatively suggest that SPP1+ Macs are largely monocyte-derived, implying that SPP1+ Macs could be recruited and differentiated by signal from tumor cells or other cells within the microenvironment. However, the mechanisms involved in this process have not yet been unraveled, which constitutes a limitation of the present study.

Besides, we also found the raised exhausted CD8+ T cell populations, Tregs, contribute to the formation of immunosuppressive barrier in mGGN progression. Interestingly, exhausted CD8+ T cells in mGGN exhibited a “transient state” phenotype with the marker expression of GZMK and IL-2 [30, 34]. Moreover, PD-1 and LAG3 are the principal inhibitory molecules expressed by CD8+ T in mGGN. However, PD-1 and CD28 are highly expressed in CD8+ T-SR compared with CD8+ T-GGR, indicating that the patients with SPN, not pGGN, could benefit from PD-1 inhibition. We did not observe classic tertiary lymphoid structures in mGGN which also have the potential to result in ineffective PD-1 blockade therapy for patients with pGGN or mGGN [37].

Our analysis results and published paper [59] show that SPP1+ Macs have the capacity to promote the formation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment and progression of human tumors. Here, we first conducted in vitro and in vivo experiments using SPP1-/- BMDM to evidence the significance of SPP1+ Macs in the occurrence and progression of early-stage LUAD. Compared to WT BMDM, SPP1-/- BMDM demonstrate deficiencies in responding to the immunosuppressive polarization, aging and migration stimulated by IL-4 and IL-13, which further supports those previous findings regarding the association between Mac senescence and immunosuppressive function [40, 53]. In vitro co-culture experiments revealed that SPP1-/- BMDM did not significantly promote the CMT64 cells’ proliferation and migration capabilities than WT BMDM. Additionally, the SPP1 knockout failed to inhibit the proliferation and IFN-γ secretion of CD8+ T cells as effectively as WT BMDMs. The in vivo tumor-bearing experiments results revealed that while WT BMDM significantly promoted the growth of CMT64 cells, the co-injection of SPP1-/- BMDM actually resulted in a mild inhibition of CMT64 cell proliferation, with growth rates marginally lower than those observed in the CMT64 group. This opposite effect may be attributed to the fact that SPP1 knockout primarily impairs immune-suppressive polarization without affecting the immune-activating of Macs. Consequently, the SPP1-/- BMDM injected may be preferentially activated in the early TME to adopt a more anti-tumorigenic phenotype. This hypothesis was further confirmed by flow cytometry results of immune cell populations within the xenograft tumors generated from co-injection of SPP1-knockout BMDM, the PD-1+CD8+ T cells, Tregs, and CD206+ Macs percentage were all lower compared to the other two groups. In vivo antagonism experiments of CD8α and CD44 demonstrated that SPP1+Macs suppressed CD8+T cell function via the SPP1/CD44 signaling pathway, thereby promoting tumor growth. In contrast, co-culturing SPP1-deficient BMDMs with CMT64 cells significantly inhibited tumor progression in vivo, which may be attributed to the increased infiltration of effector CD8+T cells in the tumor microenvironment following SPP1 knockout. This indicated that targeted inhibition or knockout of SPP1 in Macs may potentially reverse the tumor immune microenvironment and thereby inhibit tumor growth.

However, there may be some divergent views regarding the role of SPP1+ Macs in tumors. An integrated analysis [60] of multiple tumor datasets has indicated that SPP1+ Macs may exhibit enhanced phagocytic function, capable of ingesting a higher number of tumor cells. Concurrently, the level of infiltration of SPP1+ Macs positively correlates with a favorable prognosis following immune checkpoint blockade therapy [61, 62]. These findings are not in alignment with our own research, which may be attributed to the differences in experimental models. Notably, the potential mechanisms of SPP1 in regulating macrophage function and its differential roles across tumors or models merit further exploration in future studies.

Conclusions

Different from exited view that SPP1+ Mac was only derived from monocyte, our results uncovered that two distinct derived Macs, SPP1+ alveolar Mac and Mo-Mac, stand out as a pivot in the malignant development of mGGN which finally diagnosed as early LUAD. SPP1+ Macs exhibit different functional role from FABP4+ Macs and play a regulatory core in recruiting and transforming immune cells in TME. Furthermore, in vitro and in vivo experiments confirmed the necessity of SPP1 for the immunosuppressive polarization of Mac. SPP1 knockout in BMDM reverses the immunosuppressive state of nitch and reduces the growth of LUAD xenograft tumors in mice. These findings shed us a new slight that SPP1+ Macs could be a promising target for preventing the initiation and progression of early LUAD.
