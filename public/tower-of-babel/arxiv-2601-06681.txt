Extinction and persistence criteria in non-local Klausmeier model of vegetation dynamics on flat landscapes
Maciej Tadej, Ricardo Martinez-Garcia, Michael Hecht

Abstract
This paper investigates the dynamics of vegetation patterns in water-limited ecosystems using a generalized Klausmeier model that incorporates non-local plant dispersal within a finite habitat. We establish the well-posedness of the system and provide a rigorous analysis of the conditions required for vegetation survival. Our results identify a critical patch size governed by the trade-off between local growth and boundary losses; habitats smaller than this threshold lead to inevitable extinction. Furthermore, we derive a critical maximal biomass density below which the population collapses to a desert state, regardless of the domain size. We determine stability criteria for stationary solutions and describe the emergence of stable, non-trivial biomass distributions. Numerical experiments comparing sub-Gaussian and super-Gaussian kernels confirm that non-local dispersal mechanisms, particularly those with fat tails, enhance ecosystem resilience by allowing vegetation to persist in smaller, fragmented habitats than predicted by classical local diffusion models.

Introduction

Water-limited ecosystems often exhibit a variety of regular vegetation patterns

. Due to the long time scales at which vegetation patterns form and change, mathematical modeling has been an essential tool to better understand how patterns form and how they affect ecosystem dynamics

Different modeling efforts have, for example, suggested that pattern shapes could be used as early indicators of impending desertification processes, as water-stressed vegetation consistently forms hexagonally distributed spots in hyper-arid conditions

,

,

,

. More recent results indicate that patterns could alternatively enhance ecosystem resilience by allowing ecosystems to avoid sudden collapses through spatial reshaping of the vegetation cover
[

]
. Mathematical modeling has thus made key contributions to our understanding of self-organization in water-limited ecosystems. However, most of these models are formulated in ideal conditions, assuming infinite domains, and thus neglecting how more realistic boundary conditions could change both spatial patterns and vegetation survival (but see, e.g.,
[
9
,
29
,
30

).

In less applied scenarios, researchers have studied population dynamics in bounded domains, mainly using extensions of the Fisher–KPP equation
[
4
,
15
,
10
,
11
]
known as KISS models in the mathematical biology literature
[

,
18
]
. KISS models show that population survival in confined domains—or habitats in ecological terms—requires that the domain is large enough so that reproduction within the domain balances population loss due to density fluxes through the domain edges
[
5
]
. For domains smaller than this critical size, population extinction is the only stable solution. A key difference between these studies and vegetation-pattern models is that the latter often present non-local terms that account for long-range processes responsible for vegetation growth (e.g., resource intake via roots or sibling dispersal
[
23
,
12
]
). Therefore, models for vegetation pattern formation provide an ideal scenario to extend KISS models to cases in which population dynamics are spatially non-local.

We investigate the relationship between non-local population dynamics in finite habitats and population survival. To establish this connection, we use a generalized formulation of Klausmeier’s model for vegetation pattern formation in flat landscapes that accounts for non-local plant dispersal
[
12
,
19
]
. We extend this formulation to consider the more realistic case in which vegetation can only grow in a finite region that is always surrounded by desert.

Our approach is novel, but some progress has already been made. For instance, in
[
17
,
6
]
the authors constructed weak, local-in-time solutions. In a similar framework, though not involving the water density effects directly, the authors have shown several important properties such as large-time asymptotics, existence of non-trivial stable patterns, and a critical domain size, see
[

,

,

,

]
and references therein. When the local diffusion of water is replaced by a non-local operator it is known that there is a unique, global-in-time solution

1.1
The non-local Klausmeier model

The classic Klausmeier model for vegetation patterns describes the coupled dynamics of the vegetation biomass
v:=v(x,t)
and the water concentration
w:=w(x,t)
as a reaction-diffusion system.
Here, we extend the dimensionless form
[
12
]
to the finite habitat case:

\displaystyle v_{t}=d_{v}\mathcal{L}v+v^{2}w-Bv

(x,t)\in\Omega\times[0,T],

(1.1)

\displaystyle w_{t}=d_{w}\Delta w-v^{2}w-w+A

(x,t)\in\Omega\times[0,T],

(1.2)

\displaystyle v=0

(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega\times[0,T],

(1.3)

\displaystyle w=0

(x,t)\in\partial\Omega\times[0,T],

(1.4)

\displaystyle v(0)=v_{0}

x\in\Omega,

(1.5)

\displaystyle w(0)=w_{0}

x\in\Omega\,,

(1.6)

where
T>0
is the evolution time,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
an open and bounded domain,

d_{v}>0
models plant dispersal,
d_{w}>0
the water diffusion,
A>0
the average rainfall, and
B>0
the mortality.

In order to render the model more realistic we consider a non-local dispersal operator
\mathcal{L}
, given as

\mathcal{L}v(x)=\mathds{1}_{\Omega}(x)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J(x-y)\left(v(y)-v(x)\right)\>dy\,.

(1.7)

Here,
\mathds{1}_{\Omega}
is the characteristic function of the domain
\Omega
and
J:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is an integral kernel fulfilling the assumptions listed below.

Assumption 1.1
.

We assume the integral kernel
J\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
satisfies the following properties:

(J1)

Positivity:

J(z)\geq 0
for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
J(0)>0
.

(J2)

Symmetry:

J(z)=J(-z)
for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

(J3)

Decay:

J(z)\to 0
as
|z|\to\infty
.

(J4)

Finite second moment:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J(z)\,|z|^{2}\,dz<\infty.

(J5)

Normalization:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J(z)\,dz=1.

Assumption
(J5)
and the non-local Dirichlet boundary condition (
1.3
) allow us to rewrite (
1.7
) as

\mathcal{L}v(x)=\mathds{1}_{\Omega}(x)\left(\int_{\Omega}J(x-y)v(y)\>dy-v(x)\right).

(1.8)

We furthermore impose the following constraints on the domain
\Omega
.

Assumption 1.2
.

We assume that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is bounded, open and satisfies the following properties:

(O1)

Uniform estimates:

Let
\{\phi_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
be the eigenfunctions of
\Delta
with Dirichlet boundary conditions. There exists a constant
C:=C(\Omega)>0
such that
\sup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\|\phi_{j}\|_{\infty}\leq C
.

(O2)

Boundary regularity:

The boundary of
\Omega
is sufficiently regular, i.e.
\partial\Omega\in C^{k}
with suitable
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

For the convenience of the reader, we also provide a summary of the notation used throughout this article.

Contribution 1

(Critical Patch Size and Kernel Shape)
.

We derive an analytical criterion for vegetation persistence based on the spectral properties of the dispersal operator. Specifically, as shown in Lemma
3.4
, extinction is guaranteed if the dispersal rate
d_{v}
exceeds a threshold
M/\beta_{1}
, where
M
is related to the maximum growth rate and
\beta_{1}
is the principal eigenvalue of the operator
-\mathcal{L}
on
\Omega
. Since
\beta_{1}
scales inversely with the domain size (see Remark
3.1
), this establishes a critical patch size for persistence.

Crucially, our numerical analysis reveals that this critical size is sensitive to the shape of the dispersal kernel. We show that non-local kernels with heavier tails (i.e., higher kurtosis) significantly reduce the critical patch size compared to the classical Laplacian diffusion (see Figure
1
, left panel). This implies that long-range dispersal mechanisms enhance ecosystem resilience in fragmented landscapes.

Contribution 2

(Minimum Biomass Threshold for Persistence)
.

We identify a critical threshold for the initial biomass density required to prevent ecosystem collapse. Our stability analysis, formalized in Theorem
4.1
, demonstrates that if the maximal biomass density drops below a critical level, approximately scaled by the ratio of mortality to rainfall,
\|V\|_{\infty}\sim B/A
, then the system is attracted to the trivial desert state regardless of the habitat size. This theoretical lower bound is numerically confirmed by the bifurcation diagrams in Figures
2
and
3
, which show that no stable non-trivial solutions exist below this biomass threshold.

Contribution 3

(Existence and Stability of Non-Uniform Patterns)
.

We prove the existence of stable, non-trivial stationary solutions
V(x)
that bifurcate from the spatially uniform states of the kinetic system.

•

Existence Condition:
Non-deserted states emerge only when the environmental stress is low enough, specifically when rainfall
A
and mortality
B
satisfy
A>2B
. Under this condition, and ensuring the reaction kinetics satisfy specific bounds (established in Proposition
3.2
), Theorem
3.1
guarantees the existence of a stationary solution
V
in the neighborhood of the uniform state
v_{*}
.

•

Stability Regime:
By analyzing the linearized operator in Theorem
4.2
, we show that these spatially structured solutions are linearly exponentially stable, provided that the transport parameters
d_{v}
and
d_{w}
are sufficiently small.

Unlike the classical reaction-diffusion model, which predicts smooth profiles, the non-local model supports stable solutions characterized by sharp biomass gradients at the habitat boundaries (as visualized in the right panel of Figure
1
), offering a distinct morphological signature of non-local interactions.

1.2
Structure

The remainder of this article is organized as follows.
In Section 2, we establish the well-posedness of the non-local Klausmeier model by proving the local-in-time existence and uniqueness of solutions to the problem (1.1)–(1.6). We also demonstrate fundamental properties such as the non-negativity of solutions and the existence of an invariant region.
Section 3 contains the core mathematical analysis of this paper, where we provide a rigorous proof for the existence of non-trivial stationary solutions.
In Section 4, we investigate the long-term behavior of these solutions by deriving criteria for both the extinction of vegetation and the persistence of these equilibria through a stability analysis.
Section 5 is devoted to numerical experiments designed to validate and illustrate our theoretical findings. We present two main computational studies: the first investigates the critical patch size for different dispersal kernels, while the second explores the system’s bifurcation structure.
Finally, in Section 5.1, we summarize our main conclusions and outline potential directions for future research.

2.1
Local-in-time solutions

We denote with

C_{0,\Omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\left\{v\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):v\in C^{0}(\Omega),\>v=0\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega\right\},

(2.1)

the space of functions vanishing outside of
\Omega
, which becomes a Banach space when equipped with the norm
\|v\|=\|v\|_{C^{0}(\Omega)}+\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega)}
. We study the linear part in the vegetation equation (
1.1
).

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{L}:C_{0,\Omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to C_{0,\Omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be the non-local dispersal operator from equation (
1.7
),
B>0
.
Consider the linear operator
\mathcal{A}:C_{0,\Omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to C_{0,\Omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}v(x)=\begin{cases}d_{v}\mathcal{L}v-Bv&x\in\Omega,\\
0&x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega,\end{cases}\quad d_{v}>0\,.

(2.2)

We denote with
\mathcal{T}(t):C_{0,\Omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to C_{0,\Omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
t\geq 0
, the solution operator of the linear problem

\frac{d}{dt}v(t)=\mathcal{A}v(t)\,,\quad t>0\,,\quad v(0)=v_{0}\in C_{0,\Omega}.

(2.3)

Then
\mathcal{T}(t)=e^{t\mathcal{A}}
is well defined and the family
\{\mathcal{T}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
is a uniformly continuous semigroup of contractions.

Proof.

First, observe that by definition of
\mathcal{L}
, the operator
\mathcal{A}
is bounded and linear. For each
v\in C_{0,\Omega}
the solution of the problem (
2.3
) exists for all
t\geq 0
, it is unique and given explicitly by
u(t)=\mathcal{T}(t)u(0)
, with
\mathcal{T}(t)=e^{t\mathcal{A}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathcal{A}^{n}t^{n}}{n!}
. Hence,
\mathcal{T}(t)
is well defined and
\{\mathcal{T}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
is a uniformly continuous semigroup, see
[
7
, Proposition 1.2.2]
and
[
28
, Theorem 1.2]
. Thanks to
[
8
, Proposition 3.2]
and
[
37
, Theorem 2.1]
, the spectrum of the operator
\mathcal{L}
is discrete and negative. Thus, for each
t\geq 0
,
\mathcal{T}(t):C_{0,\Omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow C_{0,\Omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a contraction
[
13
, Proposition V.1.7]
.
∎

). Denote by

the Banach space of functions continuous in

Lemma 2.2
.

Consider the linear operator

given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}w(x)=\begin{cases}d_{w}\Delta w-w&x\in\Omega,\\

where the domain

(2.5)

Then the solution operator
\mathcal{T}(t):D(\mathcal{B})\to C_{0}(\Omega)
given by

Proof.

The proof can be found in

We use the ingredients above to construct the local-in-time solutions of the Klausmeier system.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
(v_{0},w_{0})\in C_{0,\Omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times C_{0}(\Omega)
. Then there exists
0<T:=T(v_{0},w_{0})

v\in C\left([0,T],C_{0,\Omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\right)\cap C^{1}\left([0,T),C_{0,\Omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\right),

w\in C\left([0,T],C_{0}(\Omega)\right)\cap C^{1}\left([0,T),C_{0}(\Omega)\right).

(2.7)

Proof.

Given

(2.8)

Next, denote the vectors

. Using this notation we rewrite the Klausmeier system (

(2.9)

where

is given by

\displaystyle F(u)=F(v,w)=\begin{cases}(v^{2}w,-v^{2}w+A)&x\in\Omega,\\
(0,0)&x\notin\Omega\end{cases}.

(2.10)

Lemma

of contractions on

(2.11)

Since

such that (

) to find that

u(t)=u_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}(\mathcal{C}u(s)+F(u(s)))\>ds.

Hence, the continuity of

implies differentiability of

in the time interval
[0,T)
.
∎

2.2
Non-negative and bounded solutions

We start by showing the non-negativity and the existence of an invariant region
\Gamma
for the system (
1.1
)–(
1.6
).

Theorem 2.2
.

Let the initial datum
(v_{0},w_{0})\in C_{0,\Omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times C_{0}(\overline{\Omega})
be non-negative. Then the solution
(v,w)
ensured by Theorem
2.1
is non-negative and for all
(x,t)\in\Omega\times[0,T]
it holds

w(x,t)\leq\max\{\|w_{0}\|_{\infty},A\}=:R_{1}>0\,.

(2.13)

Moreover, let
\Gamma=[0,B/R_{1}]
. If
v_{0}(x)\in\Gamma
for all
x\in\Omega
then for all
(x,t)\in\Omega\times[0,T]
the solution
v(x,t)\in\Gamma
.

Proof.

It follows from the parabolic maximum principle that for all
(x,t)\in\Omega\times[0,T)
we have the estimate of the form

0\leq w(x,t)\leq\max\{\|w_{0}\|_{\infty},A\}=:R_{1}.

(2.14)

Next, we define the point
\xi:=\xi(t)\in\Omega
and evaluation
\mu:=\mu(t)
such that

\mu(t):=v(\xi(t),t)=\min_{x\in\Omega}v(x,t).

Without loss of generality, we take arbitrary
0<s<t<T
and use the
Lipschitz continuity of
\mu
to estimate

\left|\mu(t)-\mu(s)\right|\leq\left|v(\xi(s),t)-v(\xi(s),s)\right|\leq\max_{(x,r)\in\Omega\times[0,T]}|v(x,r)|\,|t-s|.

It follows from the Radamacher Theorem
[
14
, Theorem 3.2]
that
\mu
is differentiable almost everywhere in
[0,T]
. Next, we estimate the difference quotients by applying the mean value theorem

\displaystyle\frac{\mu(t+h)-\mu(t)}{h}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{v(\xi(t+h),t+h)-v(\xi(t+h),t)}{h}=v_{t}(\xi(t+h),c(t,h)),

\displaystyle\frac{\mu(t+h)-\mu(t)}{h}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{v(\xi(t),t+h)-v(\xi(t),t)}{h}=v_{t}(\xi(t),c(t,h)),

where
c(t,h)\in[t,t+h]
is an intermediate point. Passing with
h\rightarrow 0
, estimating
\mathcal{L}
in a minimum point and using non-negativity of
w
, we obtain the differential inequality

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mu^{\prime}(t)&=d_{v}\mathcal{L}v(\xi(t),t)+\mu^{2}(t)w(\xi(t),t)-B\mu(t)\geq-B\mu(t),\\
\mu(0)&=\min_{x\in\Omega}v_{0}(x)\geq 0.\end{split}

(2.15)

When solved, inequality (
2.15
) yields the following lower bound

v(x,t)\geq\mu(t)\geq\mu(0)e^{-Bt}\geq 0.

Now, we define the point
\zeta:=\zeta(t)\in\Omega
and evaluation
\nu:=\nu(t)
such that

\nu(t):=v(\zeta(t),t)=\max_{x\in\Omega}v(x,t).

Proceeding as previously, we arrive at the following differential inequality

\displaystyle\begin{split}\nu^{\prime}(t)&=d_{v}\mathcal{L}v(\zeta(t),t)+\nu^{2}(t)w(\zeta(t),t)-B\nu(t)\leq R_{1}\nu^{2}(t)-B\nu(t),\\
\nu(0)&=\max_{x\in\Omega}v_{0}(x)\leq B/R_{1}.\end{split}

(2.16)

When solved, inequality (
2.16
) yields the following upper bound

v(x,t)\leq\nu(t)\leq\frac{B\nu(0)}{R_{1}\nu(0)+(B-R_{1}\nu(0))e^{Bt}}\leq B/R_{1}.

(2.17)

Hence,
\Gamma=[0,B/R_{1}]
is an invariant region.

Lemma 3.1

Let

Next we show that the mapping

Lemma 3.2

Let

\displaystyle\begin{cases}d_{w}\Delta u-(v_{1}^{2}+1)W(v_{1})+(v_{2}^{2}+1)W(v_{2})=0,&\quad x\in\Omega,\\

\displaystyle\begin{cases}d_{w}\Delta u-(v_{1}^{2}+1)u=W(v_{2})(v_{1}^{2}-v_{2}^{2}),&\quad x\in\Omega,\\

We estimate the supremum norm of the right-hand side of the first equation in (

\|W(v_{2})(v_{1}^{2}-v_{2}^{2})\|_{\infty}\leq 2AR\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{\infty}.

Lemma 3.3

Let

3.11
). Denote by
W(v_{*})
the solution of equations (
3.2
) and (
3.4
). Then for all
\epsilon>0
there exists a diffusion rate
d_{w}>0
, sufficiently small, such that

\|W(v_{*})-w_{*}\|_{\infty}<\epsilon.

Proof.

We denote with
\{\phi_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\subset L^{2}(\Omega)
the set of eigenfunctions of
\Delta
with Dirichlet boundary condition. Next,
we expand a constant function
A=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{j}\phi_{j}
. Since
W(v_{*})\in L^{2}(\Omega)
we can expand it in the eigenfunction basis as well, namely
W(v_{*})=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}c_{j}\phi_{j}
. Plugging this into equation (
3.2
) we get that

d_{w}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}c_{j}\lambda_{j}\phi_{j}-(v_{*}^{2}+1)\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}c_{j}\phi_{j}+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{j}\phi_{j}=0.

(3.12)

We multiply equation (
3.12
) by each
\phi_{j}
and then integrate. From orthogonality, we get that the following equation holds for all
j\in\mathbb{N}

-d_{w}c_{j}\lambda_{j}-(v_{*}^{2}+1)c_{j}+a_{j}=0.

(3.13)

Now, solving equation (
3.13
) for the coefficients
c_{j}
we obtain

c_{j}=\frac{a_{j}}{v_{*}^{2}+1+d_{w}\lambda_{j}}.

It follows that

W(v_{*})=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{j}}{v_{*}^{2}+1+d_{w}\lambda_{j}}\phi_{j}.

Next, we plug the eigenfunction expansion of the constant function
A
into the definition of the steady state
w_{*}=\frac{A}{v_{*}^{2}+1}
. Observe that we assumed that
\Omega
is such that
\{\phi_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is uniformly bounded by some number
C>0
. This allows us to estimate

\displaystyle\|W(v_{*})-w_{*}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{j}}{v_{*}^{2}+1+d_{w}\lambda_{j}}\phi_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{j}}{v_{*}^{2}+1}\phi_{j}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{v_{*}^{2}+1}-\frac{1}{v_{*}^{2}+1+d_{w}\lambda_{j}}\right)a_{j}\phi_{j}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left|\frac{d_{w}\lambda_{j}a_{j}}{(v_{*}^{2}+1)(v_{*}^{2}+1+d_{w}\lambda_{j})}\right|

\displaystyle=C\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left|\frac{d_{w}\lambda_{j}}{v_{*}^{2}+1+d_{w}\lambda_{j}}\right|\left|\frac{a_{j}}{v_{*}^{2}+1}\right|+C\sum_{j=N+1}^{\infty}\left|\frac{d_{w}\lambda_{j}}{v_{*}^{2}+1+d_{w}\lambda_{j}}\right|\left|\frac{a_{j}}{v_{*}^{2}+1}\right|.

For any fixed
\epsilon>0
there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\sum_{j=N+1}^{\infty}|a_{j}|<\frac{\epsilon}{2C}
. Hence, provided that
d_{w}<\frac{\epsilon}{2C}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{|\lambda_{j}a_{j}|}{(v_{*}^{2}+1)^{2}}\right)^{-1}
we obtain the estimate

\|W(v_{*})-w_{*}\|_{\infty}<\epsilon.

(3.14)

∎

Having that, we continue our analysis of stationary solutions by analyzing the existence of stationary vegetation solutions.

3.2
Existence of non-constant vegetation profiles

We use Lemmas
3.1
and
3.2
to reduce the system (
3.1
)–(
3.4
) to the following non-local and non-linear problem

\displaystyle 0=d_{v}\mathcal{L}v+f(v)

x\in\Omega,

(3.15)

\displaystyle v=0

x\in\partial\Omega,

(3.16)

where we denoted
f(v)=v^{2}W(v)-Bv
. Note that
W(v)
is the stationary solution obtained by Lemma
3.1
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose that
f(0)=0
and
f
is Lipschitz continuous with constant
M
. Denote by
\beta_{1}
the principal eigenvalue of
-\mathcal{L}
. If

d_{v}>\frac{M}{\beta_{1}},

(3.17)

then there exists only one stationary solution
v\in L^{2}(\Omega)
of the system (
3.15
)–(
3.16
), namely
v\equiv 0
.

Proof.

Let
v\in L^{2}(\Omega)
be a non-zero solution of (
3.15
). We multiply equation (
3.15
) with
v
and integrate. Applying the mean value theorem we estimate

0=d_{v}\langle\mathcal{L}v,v\rangle+\langle f(v),v\rangle=d_{v}\frac{\langle\mathcal{L}v,v\rangle}{\|v\|_{2}^{2}}\|v\|_{2}^{2}+\langle f(v)-f(0),v\rangle\leq(-d_{v}\beta_{1}+M)\|v\|_{2}^{2}.

Since
-d_{v}\beta_{1}+M<0
it follows that
\|v\|_{2}=0
.
∎

Remark 3.1
.

From Lemma
3.4
, we can draw the following conclusions:

•

For a fixed patch
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, if the dispersal rate
d_{v}>0
is sufficiently large, the vegetation will go extinct.

•

For a fixed dispersal rate
d_{v}>0
the principal eigenvalues of
\mathcal{L}
on any patches

\Omega_{1}\subset\Omega_{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfy

\beta_{1}(\Omega_{2})<\beta_{1}(\Omega_{1})
.
Hence, there exists a sufficiently large patch
\Omega
such that vegetation will persist.

Theorem 3.1
.

Denote by
(v_{*},w_{*})
a non-zero solution of the system (
3.11
). Let
f:C_{0,\Omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow C_{0,\Omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be twice Fréchet differentiable mapping. Assume that there exist sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
and numbers
M>0,m>0
such that

\|f(v_{*})\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon,\quad f^{\prime}(v_{*})<-m\quad\text{ for all }x\in\Omega\quad\text{ and }\quad\|f^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}<M.

(3.18)

Then there exist
R_{*}>0
and
D_{v}>0
such that for all
d_{v}<D_{v}
there exists a unique, non-zero solution
V
of the problem (
3.15
)–(
3.16
), satisfying
\|V-v_{*}\|_{\infty}\leq R_{*}
.

Proof.

First, let us denote the closed ball
B_{R}=\{\varphi\in C_{0,\Omega}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\leq R\}
.
Let
V
be a function defined by

V(x)=v_{*}\mathds{1}_{\Omega}(x)+\varphi(x),

(3.19)

where
\varphi\in B_{R}
. Using the Taylor expansion for the nonlinearity
f
, we can write

f(V)=f(v_{*})+f^{\prime}(v_{*})\varphi+\mathcal{N}(\varphi),

(3.20)

where
\mathcal{N}(\varphi)
represents the higher-order remainder term. Assuming the second derivative is bounded by
M
, we have the estimate
\|\mathcal{N}(\varphi)\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{M}{2}\|\varphi\|_{\infty}^{2}
. For simplicity, let us denote
C_{2}=\frac{M}{2}
.

Since
f^{\prime}(v_{*})<-m<0
and
\mathcal{L}
is negative definite, the linear operator
-d_{v}\mathcal{L}-f^{\prime}(v_{*})\mathrm{Id}
is invertible, with the norm of the inverse bounded by
(m-\beta_{1}d_{v})^{-1}
. Plugging (
3.20
) into the system (
3.15
)–(
3.16
) allows us to reformulate the problem as a fixed point equation

\varphi=\left(-d_{v}\mathcal{L}-f^{\prime}(v_{*})\mathrm{Id}\right)^{-1}\left(d_{v}v_{*}\mathcal{L}\mathds{1}_{\Omega}+f(v_{*})+\mathcal{N}(\varphi)\right)=:\mathcal{F}(\varphi).

(3.21)

We first show that
\mathcal{F}:B_{R}\to B_{R}
. Note that we assumed (
3.18
), hence estimating the supremum norm of (
3.21
), we obtain

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{F}(\varphi)\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{d_{v}v_{*}\|\mathcal{L}\mathds{1}_{\Omega}\|_{\infty}+\epsilon+C_{2}R^{2}}{m-\beta_{1}d_{v}}.

(3.22)

Furthermore, we demand
\|\mathcal{F}(\varphi)\|_{\infty}\leq R
. The minimal radius
R_{*}
satisfying this quadratic inequality corresponds to the smaller root:

R_{*}=\frac{m-\beta_{1}d_{v}}{2C_{2}}\left(1-\sqrt{1-\frac{4C_{2}(d_{v}v_{*}\|\mathcal{L}\mathds{1}_{\Omega}\|_{\infty}+\epsilon)}{(m-\beta_{1}d_{v})^{2}}}\right).

(3.23)

From Lemma
3.3
, the term under the square root is positive and close to 1 for sufficiently small
d_{v},d_{w}
, ensuring
R_{*}
is well-defined and positive. Furthermore,
R_{*}\to 0
as the parameters vanish, ensuring
R_{*}<v_{*}
so that
V
remains non-negative.

Secondly, we verify that
\mathcal{F}
is a contraction. Let
\varphi,\psi\in B_{R_{*}}
. We start by estimating the difference of the images:

\|\mathcal{F}(\varphi)-\mathcal{F}(\psi)\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{m-\beta_{1}d_{v}}\|\mathcal{N}(\varphi)-\mathcal{N}(\psi)\|_{\infty}.

(3.24)

To bound the difference of the nonlinear remainders, we re-write equation (
3.20
) and use the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{N}(\varphi)-\mathcal{N}(\psi)&=\left(f(v_{*}+\varphi)-f(v_{*}+\psi)\right)-f^{\prime}(v_{*})(\varphi-\psi)\\
&=\int_{0}^{1}\left(f^{\prime}(v_{*}+\psi+t(\varphi-\psi))-f^{\prime}(v_{*})\right)(\varphi-\psi)\,dt.\end{split}

(3.25)

Using the assumption that
\|f^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq M
, we can bound the term in the integrand as
\|f^{\prime}(v_{*}+\eta)-f^{\prime}(v_{*})\|_{\infty}\leq M\|\eta\|_{\infty}
. Applying this along with the triangle inequality for the convex combination
\|\psi+t(\varphi-\psi)\|_{\infty}\leq(1-t)\|\psi\|_{\infty}+t\|\varphi\|_{\infty}
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{N}(\varphi)-\mathcal{N}(\psi)\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq M\|\varphi-\psi\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\left((1-t)\|\psi\|_{\infty}+t\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\right)dt

(3.26)

\displaystyle=\frac{M}{2}(\|\varphi\|_{\infty}+\|\psi\|_{\infty})\|\varphi-\psi\|_{\infty}.

(3.27)

Recalling that
C_{2}=M/2
and
\varphi,\psi\in B_{R_{*}}
, we arrive at the estimate:

\|\mathcal{N}(\varphi)-\mathcal{N}(\psi)\|_{\infty}\leq 2C_{2}R_{*}\|\varphi-\psi\|_{\infty}.

(3.28)

Thus, the Lipschitz constant
k
for the operator
\mathcal{F}
is given by:

k=\frac{2C_{2}R_{*}}{m-\beta_{1}d_{v}}.

(3.29)

Substituting the explicit formula for
R_{*}
from (
3.23
) into (
3.29
), we find:

k=1-\sqrt{1-\frac{4C_{2}(d_{v}v_{*}\|\mathcal{L}\mathds{1}_{\Omega}\|_{\infty}+\epsilon)}{(m-\beta_{1}d_{v})^{2}}}.

(3.30)

Since the discriminant is positive, the square root is real and positive, guaranteeing
k<1
. Thus,
\mathcal{F}
is a contraction on
B_{R_{*}}
. By the Banach Fixed Point Theorem, there exists a unique solution
\varphi\in B_{R_{*}}
, completing the proof.
∎

Now, we show that the assumptions of Theorem
3.1
hold true in the case of the Klausmeier model, i.e.
f(v)=v^{2}W(v)-Bv
. We start with a detailed investigation of the uniform solutions
(v_{*},w_{*})
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
A>0
and
B>0
. The following statements hold true.

•

If
A\geq 2B
then there exist three or two real solutions of (
3.11
).

•

If
A<2B
then there exists only one real solution of (
3.11
).

Proof.

From the second equation in (
3.11
), we get that
w=\frac{A}{v^{2}+1}
. Thus the first equation becomes

-Bv^{3}+Av^{2}-Bv=0.

(3.31)

For all parameters
A>0,B>0
we can find the solution of the following form

\displaystyle\left(v_{*,1},w_{*,1}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(0,A\right).

(3.32)

Provided that
A>2B
, the equation (
3.31
) admits two more solutions

\displaystyle\left(v_{*,2},w_{*,2}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{A-\sqrt{A^{2}-4B^{2}}}{2B},\frac{2B^{2}}{A-\sqrt{A^{2}-4B^{2}}}\right),

(3.33)

\displaystyle\left(v_{*,3},w_{*,3}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{A+\sqrt{A^{2}-4B^{2}}}{2B},\frac{2B^{2}}{A+\sqrt{A^{2}-4B^{2}}}\right).

(3.34)

Finally, if
A=2B
, then the solutions (
3.33
) and (
3.34
) merge into

\displaystyle\left(v_{*,4},w_{*,4}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(1,B\right).

(3.35)

For
A<2B
there are no other real solutions of (
3.31
) than (
3.32
).
∎

Now we conclude that there is only one uniform solution
(v_{*},w_{*})
which satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
3.1
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
A>2B
, and
W(v_{*})
be a stationary solution of the problem (
3.2
), (
3.4
), with
v_{*}=v_{*,3}>1
being a non-zero solution given by Proposition
3.1
. Denote the non-linear function
f(v)=v^{2}W(v)-Bv
. Then for every
\epsilon>0
there exists
d_{w}>0
, sufficiently small, and numbers
m>0,M>0
such that

\|f(v_{*})\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon,\quad f^{\prime}(v_{*})<-m\quad\text{ for all }x\in\Omega,\quad\|f^{\prime\prime}(v_{*})\|_{\infty}<M.

(3.36)

Proof.

The first inequality,
\|f(v_{*})\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon
, follows directly from Lemma
3.3
and the fact that the constant steady states satisfy
v_{*}^{2}w_{*}-Bv_{*}=0
. We have:

\|f(v_{*})\|_{\infty}=\|v_{*}^{2}W(v_{*})-Bv_{*}-(v_{*}^{2}w_{*}-Bv_{*})\|_{\infty}=v_{*}^{2}\|W(v_{*})-w_{*}\|_{\infty}.

By Lemma
3.3
, for any
\epsilon^{\prime}>0
, we can choose
d_{w}>0
small enough such that
\|W(v_{*})-w_{*}\|_{\infty}<\epsilon^{\prime}
. Setting
\epsilon^{\prime}=\epsilon/v_{*}^{2}
yields the desired bound.

Next, we analyze the Fréchet derivative of
f
. Since
v_{*}w_{*}=B
, the derivative evaluated at the constant function
v_{*}
is given by

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(v_{*})

\displaystyle=2v_{*}W(v_{*})+v_{*}^{2}W^{\prime}(v_{*})-B

\displaystyle=2v_{*}(W(v_{*})-w_{*})+2v_{*}w_{*}+v_{*}^{2}W^{\prime}(v_{*})-B

\displaystyle=2v_{*}(W(v_{*})-w_{*})+v_{*}^{2}W^{\prime}(v_{*})+B.

We analyze the terms in the limit as
d_{w}\to 0
. The first term vanishes, since
\|W(v_{*})-w_{*}\|_{\infty}\to 0
. The operator
W^{\prime}(v_{*})
is the Fréchet derivative of the map
v\mapsto W(v)
, which is the solution operator of the water equation
d_{w}\Delta W-(v^{2}+1)W+A=0
. By differentiating this equation with respect to
v
in the direction of a perturbation
h
, we find that
W^{\prime}(v_{*})h
is the solution to

(d_{w}\Delta-(v_{*}^{2}+1)\mathrm{Id})(W^{\prime}(v_{*})h)=2v_{*}W(v_{*})h.

In the limit
d_{w}\to 0
, this becomes an algebraic relation:
-(v_{*}^{2}+1)W^{\prime}(v_{*})h=2v_{*}w_{*}h
. Thus, the operator
W^{\prime}(v_{*})
converges in the operator norm to multiplication by the constant
-\frac{2v_{*}w_{*}}{v_{*}^{2}+1}=-\frac{2B}{v_{*}^{2}+1}
.

Consequently, the derivative
f^{\prime}(v_{*})
converges to a constant value as
d_{w}\to 0
:

\lim_{d_{w}\to 0}f^{\prime}(v_{*})=0+v_{*}^{2}\left(-\frac{2B}{v_{*}^{2}+1}\right)+B=B\left(1-\frac{2v_{*}^{2}}{v_{*}^{2}+1}\right)=B\frac{1-v_{*}^{2}}{1+v_{*}^{2}}.

As stated in the proposition, we consider the steady state
v_{*}=v_{*,3}>1
, which implies
1-v_{*}^{2}<0
. Therefore, the limit of
f^{\prime}(v_{*})
is a strictly negative constant. Let this limit be
L<0
. By the continuity of the derivative with respect to
d_{w}
, for any small
\delta>0
, we can choose
d_{w}
sufficiently small such that
f^{\prime}(v_{*})<L+\delta
. By choosing
\delta<-L/2
, we ensure that
f^{\prime}(v_{*})<L/2<0
. We can thus define a positive constant
m=-L/2
, satisfying
f^{\prime}(v_{*})<-m<0
for all
x\in\Omega
.

Finally, we verify that the second Fréchet derivative of
f
is bounded. The second derivative,
f^{\prime\prime}(v_{*})
, is a bilinear operator that acts on a pair of perturbations
(h,k)
. Differentiating
f^{\prime}(v)[k]
with respect to
v
in the direction
h
, and evaluating at
v=v_{*}
, yields:

f^{\prime\prime}(v_{*})[h,k]=2W(v_{*})hk+2v_{*}\left((W^{\prime}(v_{*})[h])k+(W^{\prime}(v_{*})[k])h\right)+v_{*}^{2}(W^{\prime\prime}(v_{*})[h,k]).

To find
W^{\prime\prime}(v_{*})
, we differentiate the equation for
W^{\prime}(v)[k]
once more:

(d_{w}\Delta-(v_{*}^{2}+1)\mathrm{Id})(W^{\prime\prime}(v_{*})[h,k])=2W(v_{*})hk+2v_{*}\left((W^{\prime}(v_{*})[h])k+(W^{\prime}(v_{*})[k])h\right).

Since the operator on the left-hand side is invertible and its inverse is bounded,
W^{\prime\prime}(v_{*})
is a bounded bilinear operator. As
\|W(v_{*})\|_{\infty}
and
\|W^{\prime}(v_{*})\|_{\infty}
are also bounded, it follows from the triangle inequality that
\|f^{\prime\prime}(v_{*})\|_{\infty}
is bounded. We can therefore define a constant
M
as

M:=2\|W(v_{*})\|_{\infty}+4v_{*}\|W^{\prime}(v_{*})\|_{\infty}+v_{*}^{2}\|W^{\prime\prime}(v_{*})\|_{\infty},

which is finite and positive. This completes the proof.
∎

We continue by analyzing stability of the stationary solutions obtained in this section.

4
Stability analysis

We derive the conditions under which the vegetation will survive or go extinct.

4.1
Extinction of vegetation

We show that a sufficiently small initial density concentration leads to the extinction of vegetation.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
M=\max\{\|v_{0}\|_{\infty},A\}
. Denote an interval
\Gamma=\left[0,\frac{B}{M}\right]
. Suppose that
v_{0}(x)\in\Gamma
for all
x\in\Omega
. Then the solution
(v,w)
of the problem (
1.1
)–(
1.6
) satisfies

v(x,t)\xrightarrow{t\rightarrow\infty}0,\quad w(x,t)\xrightarrow{t\rightarrow\infty}W_{0}(x),

(4.1)

where we denoted the stationary water profile
W_{0}:=W(0)
from Lemma
3.1
.

Proof.

The first limit is a consequence of the bound (
2.17
). Since
(B-M\nu(0))\geq 0
we get that for all
x\in\Omega

v(x,t)\leq\frac{B\nu(0)}{M\nu(0)+(B-M\nu(0))e^{Bt}}\rightarrow 0\quad\text{as}\quad t\rightarrow\infty.

Now, let us replace the quadratic term in equation (
3.2
) with zero to get

d_{w}\Delta W-W+A=0,\quad x\in\Omega,\quad W=0,\quad x\in\partial\Omega.

(4.2)

Recall that Lemma
3.1
yields existence of a non-constant solution
W_{0}
of the problem (
4.2
). Denote
\psi(x,t)=w(x,t)-W_{0}(x)
. One can easily verify that it satisfies the following partial differential equation.

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\psi_{t}=d_{w}\Delta\psi-\psi-v^{2}w&\quad t>0,x\in\Omega,\\
\psi=0&\quad t>0,x\in\partial\Omega,\\
\psi(0)=w_{0}-W_{0}&\quad x\in\Omega.\end{cases}

(4.3)

Denote by
e^{t(d_{w}\Delta-\mathrm{Id})}
the heat semigroup, then apply the Duhamel principle to equation (
4.3
)

\psi(t)=e^{t(d_{w}\Delta-\mathrm{Id})}\psi(0)-\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)(d_{w}\Delta-\mathrm{Id})}v^{2}(s)w(s)\>ds.

(4.4)

Equation (
4.4
) allows us to estimate the
L^{\infty}
norm of
u
.

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|\psi(t)\|_{\infty}&\leq e^{-t}\|\psi(0)\|_{\infty}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(t-s)}\|v^{2}(s)\|_{\infty}\|w(s)\|_{\infty}\>ds\\
&\leq e^{-t}\|\psi(0)\|_{\infty}+M\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(t-s)}\|v^{2}(s)\|_{\infty}\>ds.\end{split}

(4.5)

Now, we use inequality (
2.17
) to estimate the integral term as follows

\displaystyle\begin{split}M\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(t-s)}\|v^{2}(s)\|_{\infty}\>ds&\leq\frac{MB\nu(0)}{B-M\nu(0)}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(t-s)}e^{-Bs}\>ds\\
&=\frac{MB\nu(0)}{(B-M\nu(0))(B-1)}e^{-t}-\frac{MB\nu(0)}{(B-M\nu(0))(B-1)}e^{-Bt}.\end{split}

(4.6)

Combining inequalities (
4.5
) and (
4.6
) yields

\displaystyle\|\psi(t)\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\|\psi(0)\|_{\infty}+\frac{MB\nu(0)}{(B-M\nu(0))(B-1)}\right)e^{-t}

\displaystyle-\frac{MB\nu(0)}{(B-M\nu(0))(B-1)}e^{-Bt}.

Thus, it follows that
\|\psi(t)\|_{\infty}\rightarrow 0
as
t\rightarrow\infty
.
∎

Roughly speaking Theorem
4.1
states that zero vegetation is a stable solution, while non-zero vegetation is unstable for too small initial vegetation. We continue by addressing the question under which conditions the vegetation can persist.

4.2
Survival of vegetation

We show the asymptotic stability of the non-zero stationary solutions, ensuring the survival of vegetation.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let the parameters
A>0,B>0
satisfy
A>2B
. Let
(v_{*},w_{*})
be the constant solutions from Proposition
3.1
, satisfying
v_{*}>1
. We denote by
(V,W)
the stationary solution contained in a neighborhood
B_{R}(v_{*},w_{*})
of radius
R>0
, given by Theorem
3.1
. Then, whenever the dispersal and diffusion rates
d_{v},d_{w}
are sufficiently small, the solution
(V,W)
is linearly, exponentially stable in
L^{2}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

Let
(V,W)
be a solution constructed in Theorem
3.1
. We set
f(v)=v^{2}W(v)-Bv
as in Proposition
3.2
. Then, we linearize the vegetation problem (
1.1
), (
1.3
) and (
1.5
) around
V
and introduce a peturbation
\varphi(x,t)=V(x)-v(x,t)
. This new variable satisfies the following linearized problem

\displaystyle\varphi_{t}=d_{v}\mathcal{L}\varphi+f^{\prime}(V)\varphi

(x,t)\in\Omega\times[0,\infty],

(4.7)

\displaystyle\varphi=0

(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega\times[0,\infty],

(4.8)

\displaystyle\varphi(0)=V-v_{0}

x\in\Omega.

(4.9)

Let
\beta_{1}>0
be the principle eigenvalue of
-\mathcal{L}
, applying the standard energy method, we get that

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\varphi^{2}\>dx\leq-d_{v}\beta_{1}\int_{\Omega}\varphi^{2}\>dx+\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(V)\varphi^{2}\>dx.

(4.10)

Assuming that
d_{w}
is sufficiently small, we deduce by continuity argument and Proposition
3.2
that the second integral is negative, hence inequality (
4.10
) yields

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\varphi^{2}\>dx\leq-d_{v}\beta_{1}\int_{\Omega}\varphi^{2}\>dx.

(4.11)

The differential inequality (
4.11
), when solved gives the estimate

\int_{\Omega}\varphi^{2}\>dx\leq\int_{\Omega}(V-v_{0})^{2}\>dx\>e^{-2d_{v}\beta_{1}t}=:M_{0}e^{-2d_{v}\beta_{1}t}.

(4.12)

Hence
V
is linearly exponentially stable. Now, consider a perturbation of the solution
W
, given by
\psi(x,t)=W(x)-w(x,t)
. The corresponding evolution problem is given by

\displaystyle\psi_{t}=d_{w}\Delta\psi-\psi-(V^{2}W-v^{2}w)

(x,t)\in\Omega\times[0,\infty],

(4.13)

\displaystyle\psi=0

(x,t)\in\partial\Omega\times[0,\infty],

(4.14)

\displaystyle\psi(0)=W-w_{0}

x\in\Omega.

(4.15)

First, let us re-write the non-linear term appearing in equation (
4.13
)

V^{2}W-v^{2}w=(V-v)(V+v)W+v^{2}(W-w)=\varphi(V+v)W+v^{2}\psi.

(4.16)

Let
\lambda_{1}>0
be the principle eigenvalue of
-\Delta
, the energy method yields the following estimate

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\psi^{2}\>dx\leq-d_{w}\lambda_{1}\int_{\Omega}\psi^{2}\>dx-\int_{\Omega}(1+v^{2})\psi^{2}\>dx-\int_{\Omega}W(V+v)\varphi\psi\>dx

(4.17)

Since
v,V,W\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and
v
is global solution, we set
C=\sup\{\|W(V+v)\|_{\infty}:t>0\}
. Applying
\epsilon
-Cauchy inequality
ab\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}a^{2}+\frac{1}{2\epsilon}b^{2}
to the last term we obtain and estimating with inequality (
4.12
) we obtain

\int_{\Omega}W(V+v)\varphi\psi\>dx\leq\frac{C\epsilon}{2}\int_{\Omega}\psi^{2}\>dx+\frac{C}{2\epsilon}\int_{\Omega}\varphi^{2}\>dx\leq\frac{C\epsilon}{2}\int_{\Omega}\psi^{2}\>dx+\frac{CM_{0}}{2\epsilon}e^{-2d_{v}\beta_{1}t}

(4.18)

Combining inequalities (
4.17
) and (
4.18
) we get the differential inequality

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\psi^{2}\>dx\leq\left(-d_{w}\lambda_{1}-1+\frac{C\epsilon}{2}\right)\int_{\Omega}\psi^{2}\>dx+\frac{CM_{0}}{2\epsilon}e^{-2d_{v}\beta_{1}t}

(4.19)

Solving differential inequality (
4.19
) we obtain

\int_{\Omega}\psi^{2}\>dx\leq\left(\int_{\Omega}(W-w_{0})^{2}\>dx\right)e^{-\mu t}+\frac{CM_{0}}{\epsilon(\mu-2d_{v}\beta_{1})}\left(e^{-2d_{v}\beta_{1}t}-e^{-\mu t}\right),

(4.20)

where we denoted constant
\mu=2(d_{w}\lambda_{1}+1)-C\epsilon
, which is positive provided that
\epsilon<\frac{2(d_{w}\lambda_{1}+1)}{C}
. Since choice of
\epsilon>0
was arbitrary, we proved that
W
is exponentially stable.

∎

Thus far, we have derived the asymptotic behavior through theoretical analysis. We now proceed to validate and extend these results using numerical experiments.

Experiment 5.1
.

We design a numerical experiment to investigate the existence of a critical patch size for vegetation survival, a concept discussed in our theoretical analysis (see Remark
3.1
). The experiment’s primary goal is to compare the critical patch size of the classical local (reaction-diffusion) model against two distinct non-local models. These non-local models employ dispersal kernels with “thin tails” (super-Gaussian) and “fat tails” (sub-Gaussian), respectively. Crucially, both non-local kernels are calibrated to have the same variance (second moment), ensuring that their leading-order Taylor approximations are equivalent to the same Laplacian operator. This setup allows us to directly isolate and observe the influence of higher-order terms, which represent the shape of the dispersal kernel, on population persistence.

The simulation framework is configured as follows:

•

By Proposition
3.1
, we assume that
A>2B
to guarantee the existence of non-zero, spatially uniform vegetation and water solutions
(v_{*},w_{*})
of the system (
3.11
). In particular, we take mortality
B=0.45
and rainfall
A=1.8
. For this choice of parameters we get that
(v_{*,3},w_{*,3})
satisfies (A3).

•

We consider fixed dispersal rate
d_{v}=2.0
and diffusion rate
d_{w}=0.1
.

•

We choose the range of patch half-widths
L\in[10^{0},10^{2}]
. We split this interval into
50
points equally distributed along the logarithmic scale.

For each value of
L
, we find the stationary solution for all three models by simulating the system’s dynamics. The simulation is performed using a forward Euler time-stepping scheme with a time step of
h_{t}=10^{-4}
. The system is considered to have reached a steady state when the
\ell^{2}
-norm of the difference between consecutive time steps falls below a tolerance of
\epsilon_{\text{tol}}=10^{-5}
. To encourage convergence to non-trivial solutions, the initial condition for each simulation is given by the spatially uniform steady state
(v_{*,3},w_{*,3})
, which is then perturbed by a small cosine profile. The spatial domain is discretized using a number of equidistant nodes
N
that increases proportionally with
L
to maintain adequate numerical resolution across all scales.

To quantify the results, we record the average biomass density of the final stationary solution for each
L
. The critical patch size,
L_{\text{crit}}
, is then numerically determined for each model as the largest patch half-width for which the average biomass falls below a small threshold of
0.1
.

5.1
Final conclusions and future work

In this work, we have established a rigorous mathematical framework for the Klausmeier model with non-local dispersal (
1.1
)–(
1.6
) on finite flat landscapes, proving the existence and stability of non-zero stationary solutions (Theorems
3.1
and
4.2
) and deriving conditions for vegetation extinction (Theorem
4.1
).

Unexpectedly, our numerical experiments revealed that non-local models can sustain vegetation on significantly smaller habitats compared to the classical reaction-diffusion counterpart (
5.4
)–(
5.9
), as illustrated in Figure
1
.
This finding challenges the intuitive logic that non-local dispersal, by facilitating long-range transport, should increase the flux of biomass through the boundaries into the uninhabitable desert, thereby making extinction more likely.

We attempted to numerically investigate the underlying causes of this enhanced resilience using the theoretical criteria derived in this paper, specifically the spectral condition in Lemma
3.4
(discussed in Remark
3.1
) and the expression for the critical radius given by formula (
3.23
).
However, due to the highly implicit nature of these conditions—particularly the complex dependence of the principal eigenvalue and the perturbation radius on the specific kernel shape—we were unable to isolate a simple analytical reason for why the critical patch size decreases for more non-local operators.

In future work, we intend to investigate this problem much further through both extensive numerical experimentation and theoretical analysis.
This will also include providing a precise proof for the existence of non-constant, patterned solutions in the regime
A<2B
, where our numerical results (see Figure
3
) suggest vegetation persists even after the uniform solution has collapsed.
