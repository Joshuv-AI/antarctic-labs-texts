The role of arbuscular mycorrhizal symbiosis in maintaining potassium uptake in bean under drought and ABA stress
Authors: Sahar Panahi-Moghaddam, Parviz Heidari, Hamid Reza Asghari, Abdullah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Potassium, as a key element, plays a prominent role in regulating cellular activities and stress responses in plants. However, its availability to plants is limited due to strong adsorption by soil aggregates. In this study, the effects of symbiosis with mycorrhizal fungi, Funneliformis mosseae and Rhizophagus irregularis on potassium uptake and the expression of AKT and HKT genes under drought stress and ABA application in bean were investigated. The results revealed that the concentration of potassium in root and shoot tissues increased in response to symbiosis with arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMFs). Additionally, under both control and drought conditions, AMF-colonized plants maintained higher potassium concentrations compared to non-symbiotic plants. Cellular location prediction revealed that most PvAKT, except PvAKT9 and PvAKT14, were localized to the plasma membrane. RNA-seq data analysis showed that the PvAKT and PvHKT genes exhibited differential expression in response to salt stress. Furthermore, qPCR results indicated that drought conditions and AMF symbiosis enhanced the expression of PvAKT genes. The expression profile of PvHKT genes indicated that symbiotic relationships significantly enhanced their transcriptional activity in root tissues. These findings indicate that PvAKT and PvHKT genes respond to AMF symbiosis and abiotic treatments and are associated with potassium transport in bean roots. Overall, our results suggest that some PvAKT genes respond to ABA treatment, although their direct involvement in ABA signaling pathways requires further functional validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48871-y.

Introduction

In natural environments, plants are constantly exposed to adverse environmental conditions from both biotic and abiotic sources, including pathogens, extreme temperatures, nutrient imbalances, salinity, and drought, all of which negatively affect plant survival, growth, and productivity1. In recent years, the harmful effects of water-related stresses, including salinity and drought, have been increasing at an alarming rate. Drought is considered the most important abiotic factor limiting plant growth and performance in many regions2. Various plant responses to environmental stresses are regulated by complex interactions between phytohormones and signaling molecules3. Abscisic acid (ABA), as a phytohormone, plays important role in plant responses to abiotic stresses, especially water-related stresses4. ABA is known as the “key stress hormone” because its biosynthesis is rapidly induced under diverse stress conditions. ABA functions not only in the regulation of growth and development but also in enhancing plant defense responses5,6.

In addition to innate defense systems against environmental stresses, plants can establish beneficial associations with rhizospheric microorganisms that help alleviate stress symptoms7. One of the most studied and widespread plant-microorganism interactions is the symbiosis with arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF). Approximately 80% of terrestrial plants, including most agricultural and horticultural species, can establish this type of symbiosis with fungi of the phylum Glomeromycota8. Through this mutually beneficial association, AMF fungi obtains photosynthetic products from the host plant to complete its life cycle, while facilitating host acquisition of water and mineral nutrientss9. This enhanced nutrient availability strengthens stress resistance, and AMF symbiosis mitigates stress-induced damage7,10,11.

There are several mechanisms by which AMF enhance plant drought tolerance, including direct uptake of water and minerals by extra-radical hyphae, improved osmotic and hormonal regulation, activation of antioxidant defense systems, etc12. However, few studies have investigated these mechanisms at the molecular level. Interaction of plants with AMF enhances the solubility of minerals such as phosphorus and potassium in the rhizosphere, thereby improving root nutrient uptake7,13. Potassium transporters, which are present in all tissues, play an important role in the uptake and transport of potassium ions7,14. AMF symbiosis with Lycium barbarum has been shown to enhance potassium accumulation15.

Potassium transporters have been classified into several classes based on their molecular structure and function, with the major groups being HKT (high-affinity K+ transporter), AKT (Arabidopsis K+ transporter) and HAK/KUP (high-affinity K+ transporter/K+ uptake permeases)16. Despite this diversity and the critical role of potassium in plant stress responses, no comprehensive study has examined the combined effects of ABA and drought stress under AMF symbiosis on potassium uptake and transporter gene activity in beans. In this study, we investigated the effects of Phaseolus vulgaris symbiosis with Funneliformis mosseae and Rhizophagus irregularis on potassium uptake and the activity of PvHKT and PvAKT genes under drought stress and exogenous ABA application. Our objectives were to elucidate the effects of AMFs symbiosis and ABA on intra-tissue potassium content under drought stress conditions and to characterize the effect of symbiosis on potassium transporters expression.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and treatments

Common bean seeds, Kermanshah cultivar, were sterilized using 5% sodium hypochlorite for 3 min. Seeds were then washed several times with sterile water and sown in pots containing soil (collected from the field of Faculty of Agriculture, Shahrood University of Technology) mixed with cocopeat at a 4:1 ratio. The soil samples were autoclaved before potting, and 40 g of mycorrhizal inoculum, consisting of colonized root fragments of Trifolium alexandrinum L. and soil/sand containing spores and external hyphae, was added to designated pots according to the experimental design. The experiment followed a completely randomized design with three biological replicates. The experimental treatments included the type of mycorrhizal fungi (Funneliformis mosseae and Rhizophagus irregularis), abscisic acid application (0 and 100 µM), and drought stress.

After sowing the seeds, the pots were irrigated and grown at 25 ± 3 °C with a 14 h light period. After four weeks, symbiosis of fungi with bean roots was examined. The Phillips and Hayman staining method17 was used to confirm AMF colonization in the roots (Supplementary Fig. 1). According to the experimental plan, half of the pots containing five-week-old seedlings were sprayed with 100 µM abscisic acid, while the remaining pots were sprayed with sterilized water. Three days after ABA pretreatment, half of the pots was imposed to drought stress by adjusting irrigation intervals to maintain 35% of field capacity. After 24 h of applying drought stress, the roots and shoots of the seedlings were harvested and stored in a -70 freezer.

Assessment of potassium levels in plant tissues

To determine potassium content in shoot and root tissues, samples were oven-dried at 42 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, 2 g of each tissue sample was finely ground and exposed to a temperature of 550 °C for a period of 5 h. Then, 20 ml of 6% hydrochloric acid was added to each sample, followed by incubation in a Bain-Marie at 80 °C for 20 min. The resulting solution was transferred to a volumetric flask and diluted to 100 ml with distilled water. Potassium concentration in both standards and experimental samples was quantified using a flame photometer at 766.5 nm.

Characterization of PvAKT and PvHKT family members

In the present study, the protein sequence of PvAKT and PvHKT family members was downloaded from the Ensembl Plants database18. Subcellular localization of PvAKTs and PvHKTs was predicted using the Wolf Psort tool (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp/). To construct the phylogenetic tree, the protein sequence of PvAKTs with their homologs in Pisum sativum was aligned by Clustal Omega19. The aligned file was introduced to IQTREE website20 to illustrate a phylogenetic tree based on Maximum Likelihood method with 1000 Bootstrap.

Gene expression analysis

After collecting the samples, total RNA was extracted from root and shoot tissues using the hot phenol method, and DNA contamination was removed with DNase enzyme (Yekta Tajhiz Azma Co., Tehran, Iran) was used to remove DNA contamination. High-quality RNA samples were selected and converted to cDNA using an Amplicon kit (Ampliqon, Stenhuggervej, Denmark). Specific primers for AKT and HKT genes (Supplementary Table 1) were designed by Primer3 online software and validated with Primer BLAST tool. In the present study, both PvHKT genes and four PvAKT genes were selected representing different phylogenetic groups: PvAKT3, PvAKT5, PvAKT6, and PvAKT11, with one representative from each of the four main phylogenetic clusters (Groups 1–4) to capture functional diversity across the family. Additionally, PvActin was used as a reference gene to normalize the data. Relative expression pattern of genes was determined using the Syber Green Amplicon kit (Ampliqon, Stenhuggervej, Denmark) and Real-Time PCR analysis. Finally, raw data were analyzed by Delta Delta CT method21.

RNA-seq data analysis

In this study, publicly available RNA-seq data related to the Phaseolus vulgaris genome under abiotic and biotic stress conditions were analyzed. The first dataset, PRJNA574280, associated with fungal (Sclerotinia sclerotiorum) infection, was downloaded from the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA). In addition, the dataset GSE156113, comprising RNA-seq data from leaf and root tissues of the salt-susceptible cultivar TR43477 and the salt-tolerant cultivar Ispir subjected to salinity stress (125 mM NaCl)22, was retrieved from the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO).

Raw sequencing reads were quality-checked using FastQC, and adapter sequences were removed using Cutadapt23. Cleaned reads were mapped to the P. vulgaris reference genome using STAR24, and gene-level read counts were obtained using featureCounts25. Normalization of read counts was performed using the DESeq2 package26. Normalized expression values of PvAKT and PvHKT genes were extracted for downstream analysis and visualization. Heatmaps were generated to illustrate relative expression patterns across tissues and treatments based on log2 fold-change (salt stress/control). No fold-change thresholds or adjusted p-value cutoffs were applied for the salt-stress dataset, as the analysis was intended for descriptive visualization rather than for identification of statistically defined differentially expressed genes.

Interaction network analysis

An interaction network of PvAKTs and PvHKTs based on available data from homologous proteins in the dicot model plant, Arabidopsis thaliana, was constructed. Initially, sequences of PvAKTs and PvHKTs were submitted to the String database27, and Arabidopsis thaliana genome was selected. Then, to expand the interaction network, 20 nodes were included in the first layer and 5 in the second layer. Additionally, gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed based on the biological process and cellular component terms.

Results

Potassium concentration in plant organs

The results showed that potassium concentration increased significantly in the roots and shoots of P. vulgaris under symbiosis with AMFs (Fig. 1). The highest potassium concentration was recorded in the roots of seedlings in symbiosis with R. irregularis under non-stress conditions (control). Overall, root tissues had higher potassium concentrations than shoots. The application of drought stress, reduced potassium concentrations in both root and shoot tissues compared to non-stress conditions. However, under symbiosis with AMFs, the potassium concentrations in the tissues increased, indicating that AMFs improved potassium uptake even under stress conditions. The maintenance of significantly higher tissue K+ levels in mycorrhizal plants under stress indicates a key functional benefit of the symbiosis, potentially through enhanced root exploration or direct fungal transport mechanisms, thereby improving plant osmotic adjustment and stress resilience. Overall, no significant difference (p-value < 0.05) was observed between the types of AMF used and both mycorrhizal fungi showed similar effects on potassium uptake. This functional equivalence suggests that the ability to improve K+ nutrition under both control and stress conditions may be a conserved trait among these AMF species in symbiosis with P. vulgaris.

Characterization of PvAKTs and PvHKTs

Previously, we identified and characterized nineteen PvAKT and two PvHKT genes (Table 1) in the genome of common bean7. The protein length of PvHKTs ranged from 508 to 519 amino acid (aa), and PvAKTs ranged from 309 aa (PvAKT17) to 875 aa (PvAKT6). Based on phylogenetic analysis, PvAKTs were classified into four main groups (Fig. 2), and one gene from each group was selected for qPCR analysis. Members of group one showed greater genetic distance than other groups, indicating diversity within them. Results revealed that all PvAKTs, except PvAKT9 and PvAKT14, and both PvHKTs were localized to the plasma membrane (Table 1). PvAKT14 was predicted to localize to the chloroplast.

Expression profile of PvAKT and PvHKT genes based on RNA-seq data

In response to salinity treatment, PvAKT and PvHKT genes displayed diverse tissue- and cultivar-dependent expression patterns (Fig. 3). In the salt-sensitive cultivar TR43477, PvAKT genes generally showed higher expression levels in root tissues than in leaves. Notably, PvAKT8 and PvAKT2 exhibited higher expression in roots and reduced expression in leaves under salinity stress. PvAKT13 and PvAKT18 were predominantly expressed in root tissues, whereas PvAKT7 showed relatively higher expression in leaf tissues. In the salt-tolerant cultivar Ispir, PvAKT18, PvAKT15, and PvAKT13 also exhibited distinct expression patterns between root and leaf tissues under salinity treatment. PvHKT genes consistently showed higher expression levels in root tissues in both cultivars. Collectively, these observations indicate clear tissue- and cultivar-specific variation in PvAKT and PvHKT gene expression under salinity stress. However, these patterns are presented descriptively and are not intended to represent statistically defined differential expression or to infer functional dominance of specific genes.

Interaction network of PvAKTs and PvHKT

To gain insight into the potential functional partnerships of the HKT and AKT protein families, we constructed a protein-protein interaction network. This analysis revealed that HKT and AKT proteins primarily interact with members of key transporter families involved in nitrate (SLAH), potassium (POT, AKT), and sodium/potassium pH regulation (NHX, KEA) (Fig. 4a). Notably, we observed several high-confidence interactions that suggest specific regulatory nodes: HKTs showed strong interactions with the sodium/proton exchangers NHX1, NHX4, and NHX7, as well as with the potassium transporters AKT2, AKT11, and POT1. Furthermore, a dense web of strong interactions among AKT proteins themselves suggests potential heteromerization or functional co-regulation within this family. GO enrichment analysis showed that most nodes in the networks were localized to the plasma membrane and vacuolar membrane. GO enrichment analysis further revealed that biological process terms including potassium ion transport, metal ion transport, inorganic cation transmembrane transport, ion transport, inorganic cation import across plasma membrane, regulation of membrane potential, stabilization of membrane potential, regulation of ion transmembrane transport, potassium ion homeostasis, and potassium ion import across plasma membrane were associated with HKT and AKT interactions (Fig. 4b).

Expression profile of PvAKT genes in response to ABA application and drought stress under symbiosis conditions

The expression level of PvAKT3 in was upregulated in response to symbiosis with the fungi R. irregularis and F. mosseae under normal conditions. In contrast, its expression was downregulated under drought stress in both tissues, root and shoot (Fig. 5a). In shoot and root tissues, expression levels of PvAKT3 were not affected by ABA treatment. Symbiosis with the fungus R. irregularis showed a significant effect on PvAKT3 expression in root tissues. Besides, results revealed that the interaction between drought stress and symbiosis with AMFs, as well as the interaction among drought stress, ABA application and symbiosis with AMFs increased transcription levels of PvAKT5 in shoot tissues (Fig. 5b). In root tissues, the expression level of PvAKT5 was more induced in response to symbiosis with fungus R. irregularis than F. mosseae compared with shoot tissues. Expression levels of PvAKT6 were sharply downregulated under symbiosis with AMFs in shoot tissues under control condition, while its expression increased in root tissues (Fig. 5c). Drought stress in shoot and root tissues and ABA application in root tissues increased the expression levels of PvAKT6. The highest expression level of PvAKT6 was observed in root tissues under drought stress. The expression profile of PvAKT11 showed that its expression levels were higher in shoot tissues than in roots (Fig. 5d). Additionally, under symbiosis with AMFs, high expression levels of PvAKT11 were recorded in shoot tissues. The interaction among drought stress, ABA application, and symbiosis with AMFs had a significant effect on the expression levels of PvAKT11.

Expression profile of PvHKT genes in response to ABA application and drought stress under symbiosis condition

The expression pattern of PvHKT genes in various organs of P. vulgaris revealed that these genes are expressed in root and shoot tissues (Fig. 6). Under conditions of symbiosis with AMFs and without stress, the expression of PvHKT1 gene in roots showed an increase, while in shoots it showed a decrease/no induction (Fig. 6a). Under drought stress (both alone and in combination with ABA), the expression pattern of PvHKT1 showed a significant increase compared to the control. The expression of the PvHKT2 gene was similar to that of the PvHKT1 gene, although differences were also observed. For example, in response to ABA application, the PvHKT1 gene was slightly upregulated in shoot tissues, while PvHKT2 gene was upregulated in root tissues (Fig. 6b). For both PvHKT genes, the highest expression was observed in root tissues under conditions of symbiosis with R. irregularis and in response to a combination of ABA treatment and drought stress.

Discussion

Potassium is an essential macronutrient that plays an important role in regulating plant growth and development28. The symbiosis of plant roots with AMF significantly enhances potassium solubility and availability, thereby substantially improving nutrient acquisition7,29. Although AMF symbiosis enhances potassium uptake in roots, its effects under stress conditions and with exogenous phytohormones remain poorly understood. In the current study, we found that potassium concentrations were increased significantly in the roots and shoots of P. vulgaris under symbiosis with AMFs. Potassium concentrations were significantly increased under symbiosis with R. irregularis in the root tissues under the control condition. In response to stress conditions (drought stress and drought + ABA application), symbiosis with AMFs also affected the K+ uptake. ABA regulates potassium transport and accumulation in plant roots30. External K+ changes can be perceived by plant cells through physical and chemical signals, which then regulate K+ transporters31. Abscisic acid is known as a stress hormone and acts through the transmembrane signaling pathways. Under stress, ABA appears to activate plant stress tolerance responses, including the increased uptake of potassium made available by AMF.

In the genome of P. vulgaris, nineteen PvAKT and two PvHKT genes were previously identified that showed diverse physiochemical properties7. Prediction of subcellular localization revealed that both PvHKTs and all PvAKTs, except PvAKT9 and PvAKT14, were localized to the plasma membrane. Their main role appears to involve transporting potassium across the plasma membrane into the cytosol, although some AKTs likely also function in organelle membranes to distribute and accumulate potassium. According to RNA-seq datasets, PvAKT and PvHKT genes have diverse functions in response to stresses. For example, in response to salt stress, PvAKT genes were more highly expressed in root tissues, and PvAKT18, PvAKT15, and PvAKT13 genes showed differential expression between root and leaf tissues in the salt-tolerant cultivar. Additionally, PvHKT1 was downregulated under salt stress in the leaf tissues of the salt-sensitive cultivar. Previous studies have reported that poplar HKT genes are more highly expressed in root tissues in response to salinity stress32. HKT plays a prominent role in regulating the balance of sodium and potassium ions within the cell and helps plants tolerate salt stress33–35. Besides, interaction network analysis showed that PvAKTs and PvHKTs interact with proteins involved in metal ion transport and inorganic cation transmembrane transport. These findings suggest that PvAKTs and PvHKTs cooperate with other membrane transporters to maintain cellular ion homeostasis.

Our results revealed that PvAKT3, PvAKT5 and PvAKT11 are induced under symbiosis with fungi R. irregularis and F. mosseae in shoot tissues. However, PvAKT3, PvAKT5 and PvAKT6 were more expressed in root tissues. In previous studies, it was stated that K+ transporters are induced in response to symbiosis with AMF7,29,36. We suggest to produce the knockout/knockdown lines of the key candidate transporter genes (e.g., PvAKT3, PvAKT5 and PvAKT6) in future studied to better understanding of their function under AMF symbiosis. The AKT transporters play a significant role in potassium ion (K+) uptake under varying concentrations of K+, exhibiting heightened activity specifically within the root systems and their associated tissues37. AKT genes have diverse functions and are expressed in different tissues. For example, AKT1 in Arabidopsis and rice is more expresses in root epidermal cells14,38.

Under ABA application, PvAKT11 was just induced in both root and shoot tissues. This suggests that not all PvAKTs participate in ABA-dependent signaling pathways. The expression levels of PvAKT5, PvAKT6 and PvAKT11 genes increased in root tissues in response to drought stress; however, symbiosis with AMFs somewhat reduced the expression of these genes compared to stress conditions without symbiosis. Under conditions without symbiosis, the amount of available potassium is lower, and plant roots require more potassium under stress, which probably caused the increased expression of PvAKT genes. Our results revealed that drought and symbiosis with AMFs could induce PvAKT genes.

The expression pattern of PvHKT genes showed that symbiosis was able to increase the transcription of these genes in root tissues. HKT plays a prominent role in ionic balance in response to stresses and potassium ion transport7,39. Our results also revealed that this PvHKT1 gene is upregulated in response to drought stress and ABA treatment under drought stress. Also, the highest expression level for both PvHKT genes under drought stress with ABA application and symbiosis with R. irregularis was observed in the root. These results show that PvHKT genes display treatment- and tissue-dependent expression patterns and are associated with potassium transport in root tissues. Overexpression of ABI4, as a transcription factor involved in the abscisic acid signal transduction pathways, reduced HKT1;1 expression and sensitivity to salt stress in Arabidosis40. It seems that PvAKTs are involved in ABA signaling pathways, and they are a part of cellular responses to drought stress.

Conclusion and future perspectives

This study demonstrates that arbuscular mycorrhizal symbiosis is associated with increased potassium concentrations and altered expression patterns of AKT and HKT genes in common bean. These effects were observed under both control and abiotic treatment conditions, including drought and ABA application. While AMF colonization was consistently associated with higher potassium levels that were maintained under stress, the present results do not provide statistical evidence for a drought-specific or causal role of potassium transport modulation in AMF-mediated drought tolerance. Further functional and physiological studies will be required to clarify the mechanistic contributions of potassium transporters to AMF-associated stress responses.
