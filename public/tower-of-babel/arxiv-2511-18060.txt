An operator splitting analysis of Wasserstein–Fisher–Rao gradient flows
Francesca Romana Crucinio, Sahani Pathiraja

Abstract
Wasserstein-Fisher-Rao (WFR) gradient flows have been recently proposed as a powerful sampling tool that combines the advantages of pure Wasserstein (W) and pure Fisher-Rao (FR) gradient flows. Existing algorithmic developments implicitly make use of operator splitting techniques to numerically approximate the WFR partial differential equation, whereby the W flow is evaluated over a given step size and then the FR flow (or vice versa). This works investigates the impact of the order in which the W and FR operator are evaluated and aims to provide a quantitative analysis. Somewhat surprisingly, we show that with a judicious choice of step size and operator ordering, the split scheme can converge to the target distribution faster than the exact WFR flow (in terms of model time). We obtain variational formulae describing the evolution over one time step of both sequential splitting schemes and investigate in which settings the W-FR split should be preferred to the FR-W split. As a step towards this goal we show that the WFR gradient flow preserves log-concavity and obtain the first sharp decay bound for WFR.

Introduction

We consider the task of generating samples from a target probability distribution with density
\pi(x)\propto e^{-V_{\pi}(x)},\enskip x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
known up to a normalisation constant. Despite the conceptual simplicity of this task, its efficient implementation when
V_{\pi}
is multi-modal, the underlying space is high dimensional and/or modes are separated by large distances remains challenging. Given the broad application of sampling to (Bayesian) statistics and statistical machine learning, several avenues have been investigated to derive efficient and scalable algorithms.

A natural way to formulate this task is via gradient flows
[
3
]
, which can be seen as optimisation of a functional measuring the dissimilarity to
\pi
, typically the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence
[
47
]
.
This formulation yields considerable freedom in the design of sampling algorithms; arguably the most well-known being the so-called Wasserstein Gradient flows, hereafter W flows.
It is well-known that when
\pi
satisfies a Log-Sobolev Inequality (LSI), the W flow converges exponentially fast to
\pi
, with rate depending on the Log-Sobolev constant. This highlights an inherent limitation of W flows, namely that when the LSI constant is large (e.g. as is typically the case in multi-modal densities with well separated modes), the convergence rate can be prohibitively slow
[
44
]
.

Recent research efforts have instead considered gradient flows in the Fisher-Rao geometry (FR flow). These are well known in the biological literature as describing the macroscopic properties of a population with varying traits or species, and is sometimes referred to as birth-death or replicator dynamics
[
25
,
45
,
13
]
. It is now known that FR flows are intimately connected to mirror descent
[
12
]
, stochastic filtering
[
2
,
23
,
39
,
16
]
and Sequential Monte Carlo
[
15
]
. The have also recently been exploited to develop sampling algorithms
[
37
,
34
,
46
,
9
,
33
,
32
]
, most notably due to the fact that it is possible to achieve convergence rates independent of the properties of
V_{\pi}

[
7
,
32
]
. However, the development of stable and efficient numerical approximations of pure FR flows remains a challenge.

In this work, we consider the so-called Wasserstein–Fisher–Rao (WFR) gradient flow, wherein the metric is given by the direct sum of the Wasserstein and Fisher-Rao metrics.
We refer the reader to
[
30
]
for a rigorous treatment of this metric and its corresponding gradient flow.
WFR gradient flows combine the diffusive behaviour of W flows with the birth-death or reactive properties of FR flows and enjoy better convergence properties than both W and FR alone
[
32
,
9
]
. They have a natural interpretation as combining ‘exploration’ or ‘mutation’ to provide new particles (W flow) with ‘selection’ where particles that are a poor fit to the target are killed
[
39
]
. The convergence properties of WFR flows have been theorised to improve on both W and FR flows, yet the best known results are obtained under strong conditions on the ration between
\pi
and the initial distribution
\mu_{0}

[
32
, Appendix B]
and
[
33
, Remark 2.6]
and, as we show later, are not sharp. Research into WFR flows is active; several open questions and areas of investigation remain both in terms of algorithmic development and theoretical investigation.

The structure of WFR gradient flows naturally lends itself to numerical methods built on
operator splitting

[
24
]
. Splitting of the WFR PDE has been considered in several papers when deriving algorithms
[
15
,
33
,
32
]
but without a precise characterisation of the effect of the splitting choice.

Operator splitting has been previously exploited to build splitting schemes for the kinetic Langevin SDE arising from the W flow to obtain algorithms with no time discretisation bias
[
8
]
and methods with higher accuracy for fixed computational cost
[
28
,
6
]
.
Our work aims to show that splitting schemes can exhibit faster convergence to the target than the exact dynamics and thus a wise choice of splitting can reduce computational cost.

In this work we focus on exact splitting, i.e.
we do not consider numerical schemes to approximately solve the W and FR flow and their numerical errors but rather focus on the numerical error introduced by time discretisations via operator splitting.
We demonstrate that numerical error introduced by operator splitting can be exploited to accelerate convergence to equilibrium. A particular advantage of this observation is that ordering of the operators does not affect computational cost, and thus speed up can be obtained at no additional cost and without introducing additional numerical approximations.

This is particularly relevant when the computational budget is limited, as the speed up due to splitting is mostly observed in the early iterations. We thus claim that the continuous time process need not be the target of our numerical approximations but rather the splitting scheme which gives us the fastest convergence given
\pi,\mu_{0}
.
This then paves the way for the development of practical numerical algorithms (i.e. space-time discretisations) that approximate the operator split WFR flow rather than the fully coupled WFR gradient flow.
There are a myriad schemes for numerically approximating both W
[
42
,
41
,
35
]
and FR
[
36
,
34
,
46
,
27
,
9
]
flows and we leave their detailed investigation to future work.

Our main contributions are as follows:

•

We derive variational formulae (PDEs) that quantify the impact of operator splitting as function of the step size (Section
2
) and demonstrate that a carefully chosen operator splitting method can yield theoretical convergence rates that are faster than the fully continuous time WFR flow (Section
6
) without increasing the computational cost.

•

To do so, we obtain the first convergence result for the continuous time WFR flow which clearly shows that the rate of convergence is the sum of the rate of the W flow and that of the FR flow (Section
5
). This result is obtained after establishing that the WFR preserves log-concavity thanks to the strong regularising properties of the FR flow (Section
4
).

•

We provide as motivation a precise quantitative characterisation in the multivariate Gaussian case (Section
3
) which allows us to gain some intuition on general guidelines on the choice of initial distribution
\mu_{0}
to obtain faster convergence (Section
6
).

2
Operator Splitting for the WFR PDE

The gradient flow of
\mathrm{KL}(\mu||\pi)
w.r.t. the geometry induced by the Wasserstein–Fisher–Rao distance is given by the following PDE
[
32
, Theorem 3.1]

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu_{t}=f_{\textrm{W}}(\mu_{t})+f_{\textrm{FR}}(\mu_{t}),

(2.1)

where

\displaystyle f_{\textrm{W}}(\mu)

\displaystyle:=\nabla\cdot(\mu\nabla g(\mu)),

(2.2)

\displaystyle f_{\textrm{FR}}(\mu)

\displaystyle:=-\mu\left(g(\mu)-\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[g(\mu)\right]\right),

(2.3)

\displaystyle g(\mu)

\displaystyle:=\log\frac{\mu}{\pi}.

(2.4)

and
f_{\textrm{W}}
and
f_{\textrm{FR}}
correspond to the Wasserstein and Fisher–Rao operators respectively, and takes the form of a reaction-diffusion equation.
This PDE is a combination of the Wasserstein (W) flow and of the Fisher–Rao (FR) flow and enjoys better convergence properties than both W and FR alone
[
32
,
9
]
.

The WFR PDE (
2.1
) corresponds to the underlying continuous time dynamics of many recent sampling methods as highlighted in
[
15
]
. Recent methods involve using a variety of numerical schemes e.g. interacting particle based methods
[
32
,
33
,
9
]
and kernel based methods
[
34
,
37
,
46
]
. A well-known approach to discretising PDEs involving sums of operators is operator splitting
[
24
,
4
]
which has been applied to the W flow to split advection and diffusion and led to a vast number of numerical approximations of Langevin dynamics
[
1
,
19
,
8
,
28
,
6
]
. Such operator splitting is particularly natural for the WFR PDE, where the W flow is characterised by advection and diffusion operators whilst the FR flow is characterised by reaction dynamics. Splitting of the WFR PDE has been considered in several papers when deriving algorithms
[
15
,
33
,
32
]
but without a precise characterisation of the effect of the splitting choice.

Throughout this manuscript, we focus on time discretisation of (
2.1
) via operator splitting under the assumption that the operators are evaluated exactly over a single time step, i.e. without further discretisations using either determinstic or Monte Carlo type approximations. Our motivation for doing so is to quantify biases due solely to splitting error, thereby giving rise to a new PDE which can potentially yield faster (continuous time) convergence rates than the original WFR PDE. This opens the door for the development and study of numerical schemes whose underlying dynamics correspond to the split WFR flow rather than its fully continuous time counterpart.
In particular, we assume that the solution operator of the W flow for a time step
\gamma
,
S_{\textrm{W}}(\gamma,v)(x)
,
can be evaluated exactly.
Similarly, we assume the solution operator of the FR flow given by (e.g.
[
9
, Appendix B.1]
)

\displaystyle S_{\textrm{FR}}(\gamma,v)(x)\propto\pi(x)^{1-e^{-\gamma}}v(x)^{e^{-\gamma}},

(2.5)

can also be evaluated exactly.
A commonly used first order accurate splitting method is sequential splitting or Lie-Trotter splitting (see, e.g.,
[
4
]
for a recent review), where each operator is solved over a step size in an alternating manner. More specifically, consider a time-discretisation
t_{0}=0<t_{1}<t_{2}\cdots<t_{M}=T
with
t_{n+1}-t_{n}=\gamma\enskip\forall\enskip i=1,2,\dots M
. A sequential splitting applied to (
2.1
) takes the form

\displaystyle\hat{\nu}_{1}(x;\gamma)

\displaystyle=S_{\textrm{W}}(\gamma,\mu_{0})(x)

(2.6)

\displaystyle\nu_{1}(x;\gamma)

\displaystyle=S_{\textrm{FR}}(\gamma,\hat{\nu}_{1})(x)

\displaystyle\hat{\nu}_{2}(x;\gamma)

\displaystyle=S_{\textrm{W}}(\gamma,\nu_{1})(x)

\displaystyle\nu_{2}(x;\gamma)

\displaystyle=S_{\textrm{FR}}(\gamma,\hat{\nu}_{2})(x)

where
S_{\textrm{FR}}(\gamma,v)
and
S_{\textrm{W}}(\gamma,v)
denote the solution operators corresponding to
f_{\textrm{FR}}(\mu)
and
f_{\textrm{W}}(\mu)
acting on
v
over time interval of size
\gamma
, respectively. We refer to (
2.6
) as Wasserstein then Fisher–Rao, or W-FR for short.
It is known that the
n
th iterate of the splitting scheme (
2.6
),
\nu_{n}
, converges to the solution of (
2.1
) at time
t=n\gamma
,
\mu_{n\gamma}
, as
\gamma\rightarrow 0
(at rate
\mathcal{O}(\gamma)
) under mild conditions on
f_{\textrm{FR}}
,
f_{\textrm{W}}
and

(see e.g.
[
24
]
).

The splitting scheme in (
2.6
) corresponds to evaluating the W solution operator first. A valid alternative sequential splitting scheme to (
2.6
) involves instead evaluating the FR solution operator first, i.e.

\displaystyle\hat{\eta}_{1}(x;\gamma)

\displaystyle=S_{\textrm{FR}}(\gamma,\mu_{0})(x)

(2.7)

\displaystyle\eta_{1}(x;\gamma)

\displaystyle=S_{\textrm{W}}(\gamma,\hat{\eta}_{1})(x)

\displaystyle\hat{\eta}_{2}(x;\gamma)

\displaystyle=S_{\textrm{FR}}(\gamma,\eta_{1})(x)

\displaystyle\eta_{2}(x;\gamma)

\displaystyle=S_{\textrm{W}}(\gamma,\hat{\eta}_{2})(x)

We refer to (
2.7
) as Fisher–Rao then Wasserstein, or FR-W for short.
When the operators
S_{\textrm{FR}}
and
S_{\textrm{W}}
do not commute, it is known that
\eta_{i}(x;\gamma)\neq\nu_{i}(x;\gamma)
for

and

]

Variational Formula: W-FR

We begin by analysing the evolution obtained by using the splitting scheme in (

The advantage of this characterisation is that we can identify a perturbation to the exact dynamics and more easily characterise how splitting biases modify convergence speed. The traditional route to analysing decay of

.

\displaystyle f_{\textrm{G}}(\mu):=\mu\log\frac{\pi}{\mu}.

\displaystyle\rho_{\gamma}=S_{\textrm{G}}(\gamma,S_{\textrm{W}}(\gamma,\mu_{0})),

Using the chain rule,

\displaystyle\partial_{\gamma}\rho_{\gamma}=f_{\textrm{G}}(\rho_{\gamma})+\partial_{2}S_{\textrm{G}}(\gamma,v_{\gamma})f_{\textrm{W}}(v_{\gamma}),

\displaystyle\partial_{2}S_{\textrm{G}}(\gamma,v)h=\left.\frac{d}{d\epsilon}S_{\textrm{G}}(\gamma,v+\epsilon h)\right|_{\epsilon=0}

\displaystyle\partial_{\gamma}\rho_{\gamma}=f_{\textrm{G}}(\rho_{\gamma})+e^{-\gamma}S_{\textrm{G}}(\gamma,v_{\gamma})f_{\textrm{W}}(v_{\gamma})v_{\gamma}^{-1}.

\displaystyle f_{\textrm{W}}(v_{\gamma})

Convergence speed of Exact WFR: log-concave case

The rate of decay of
\mathrm{KL}
for the WFR flow is obtained considering the time derivative of
\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{t}||\pi)
along the WFR flow

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{t}||\pi)

\displaystyle=\int\log\frac{\mu_{t}}{\pi}\partial_{t}\mu_{t}=-\int\mu_{t}|\nabla\log\frac{\mu_{t}}{\pi}|^{2}-\text{Var}_{\mu_{t}}\left[\log\frac{\mu_{t}}{\pi}\right].

(5.1)

As observed in
[

, the first term corresponds to the negative gradient of
\mathrm{KL}
w.r.t. the Wasserstein-2 metric, while the remaining terms give the negative gradient of
\mathrm{KL}
w.r.t. the Fisher–Rao geometry, implying that the dissipation for the WFR metric is the sum of the W and the FR dissipation.
As a consequence, the convergence rate of WFR gradient flow (

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{t}||\pi)\leq\min\left\{\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{t}^{\textrm{FR}}|\pi),\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{t}^{\textrm{W}}|\pi)\right\},

(5.2)

where
\mu_{t}^{\textrm{FR}},\mu_{t}^{\textrm{W}}
denote the solutions of the FR and W flow PDEs respectively.
A bound on the decay of KL along the W flow can be obtained if
\pi
satifies an LSI (
2.8
) (e.g.,
[
11
]
).
Decay of KL along the FR flow at rate
\mathcal{O}(e^{-t})
is guaranteed under boundedness conditions on the score
\log\pi/\mu_{0}
(
[
9
, Theorem 4.1]
and
[
33
, Proposition 2.4]
).

A sharper rate of converge for WFR can be obtained assuming
\log\nicefrac{{\pi(x)}}{{\mu_{0}(x)}}\geq M
and a warm-start condition
[

, Appendix B]

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{t}||\pi)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\left(2\lambda_{\pi}^{-1}+(2-3\delta)\right)(t-t_{0})}\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{0}||\pi),

(5.3)

for all
t\geq t_{0}:=\log(M/\delta^{3})
and

.

We point out that neither (

which makes (

In Proposition
5.1

The symmetrised
\mathrm{KL}
divergence, or Jeffrey’s divergence

\displaystyle J(\mu,\pi):=\mathrm{KL}(\mu||\pi)+\mathrm{KL}(\pi||\mu),

for any

and also
\pi\ll\mu

[

[

On the other hand, Jeffrey’s divergence satisfies a gradient dominance condition

Proposition 5.1
.

Let Assumption
2

\mu_{t}
the solution of the WFR PDE (

\displaystyle J(\mu_{t},\pi)\leq J(\mu_{0},\pi)e^{-t\kappa},

where

Proof.

Assuming valid interchange of differentiation and integration,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}J(\mu_{t},\pi)

\displaystyle=\int\left(\log\frac{\mu_{t}}{\pi}-\frac{\pi}{\mu_{t}}\right)(x)\partial_{t}\mu_{t}(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int\left(\log\frac{\mu_{t}}{\pi}-\frac{\pi}{\mu_{t}}\right)(x)\left(f_{\textrm{W}}(\mu_{t}(x))+f_{\textrm{FR}}(\mu_{t}(x))\right)dx.

(5.4)

Begin with the first term, by a standard integration by parts argument,

\displaystyle\int\left(\log\frac{\mu_{t}}{\pi}-\frac{\pi}{\mu_{t}}\right)f_{\textrm{W}}(\mu_{t})dx

\displaystyle=-\int\left(\log\frac{\mu_{t}(x)}{\pi(x)}-\frac{\pi(x)}{\mu_{t}(x)}\right)\nabla\cdot\left(\mu_{t}(x)\nabla\log\frac{\mu_{t}}{\pi}(x)\right)(x)dx

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{t}}\left[\left|\nabla\log\frac{\mu_{t}}{\pi}\right|^{2}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\nabla\log\frac{\mu_{t}}{\pi}\cdot\nabla\log\frac{\mu_{t}}{\pi}\right]

where

is the relative Fisher information between
\mu_{t}
and
\pi

Then for the second term in (

\displaystyle-\int\frac{\pi}{\mu_{t}}f_{\textrm{FR}}(\mu_{t})dx\leq-J(\mu_{t},\pi)l,

6
Splitting & convergence speed

Existing
\mathrm{KL}
decay results for the W flow and FR flow developed in the literature cannot be straightforwardly utilised to characterise differences in convergence speed between the W-FR and FR-W scheme. In particular, combining existing exponential decay bounds for the KL divergence for the W part and the FR part would not allow us to isolate changes to convergence speed depending on the order as the exponents simply add (and commute). Hence, the first variation results developed in Proposition
2.2
and
2.3
will be critical in characterising how convergence speed is affected due to splitting, and the ordering of operators.

We utilise the variational formulae (
2.9
) and (
2.14
) to tighten the upper bound on the KL. We focus on one single split step of size
\gamma>0
and consider the classical KL divergence
\mathrm{KL}(\mu||\pi)
, as a first step.

6.1
Convergence speed: W-FR

We first focus on the PDE (
2.9
) due to its easier form. It is possible to obtain a KL decay ODE by a straightforward calculation using (
2.9
), integration by parts, the sufficient tail decay of
\nu_{\gamma}
and
\pi

1
and assuming sufficient regularity to interchange differentiation and integration.

Consider one step of the W-FR splitting (
2.6

\gamma>0
.
Recall the split W-FR PDE in Proposition
2.2

\displaystyle\partial_{\gamma}\nu_{\gamma}=f_{\textrm{W}}(\nu_{\gamma})+f_{\textrm{FR}}(\nu_{\gamma})+(e^{\gamma}-1)f_{\textrm{P}}(\nu_{\gamma}),

where

Then, we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\gamma}\mathrm{KL}(\nu_{\gamma}||\pi)

\displaystyle=\int\log\frac{\nu_{\gamma}(x)}{\pi(x)}\left(f_{\textrm{W}}(\nu_{\gamma}(x))+f_{\textrm{FR}}(\nu_{\gamma}(x))+(e^{\gamma}-1)f_{\text{P}}(\nu_{\gamma}(x))\right)dx

It is clear that the behaviour of KL decay for the W-FR split will depend on the perturbation term

This quantity measures the covariation between the log density ratio (when it is pointwise positive, it means that the current density is larger than the target density) and the squared score function (or magnitude of the score function), which is large in regions where the ratio
\frac{\nu_{\gamma}(x)}{\pi(x)}
varies rapidly. In fact, if the log-ratio and the score are strongly negatively correlated, it is this negative correlation that contributes to

being negative and driving the speed-up.
In the below Lemma, we list conditions under which this covariance is negative. We emphasise that these conditions are sufficient, and establishing similar results for more relaxed conditions is left for future work. Given that a similar speed-up can be achieved for a 1D Gaussian mixture target (see Figure 1 (right) in
[

However, we emphasise that negativity of the perturbation term is not sufficient to guarantee a speed-up, uniformly in time. A more precise characterisation, e.g. via comparison theorems, would require a lower-bound on the decay rate of the
\mathrm{KL}

The following results hold.

(i)

7
Conclusion & Future Work

Operator splitting methods lie at the heart of many gradient flow based algorithms, in particular, for discretisations of the Wasserstein–Fisher–Rao gradient flow. We examine errors introduced by splitting schemes whereby the Wasserstein and Fisher–Rao operators are solved separately (and exactly). We develop variational formulae for the split schemes and identify a perturbation which modifies the dynamics. It is demonstrated that the order in which these operators are evaluated can have a considerable impact on convergence speed, without affecting
\pi
-invariance. This impact is unsurprisingly more pronounced when the chosen step size is large, but not so large that it is nearly equivalent to evaluating either the Wasserstein or Fisher-Rao flow alone. In practical terms, this means that a sequence of alternating operators with a fixed step size
\gamma
can be re-arranged (e.g. such that
m
consecutive steps of size
\gamma
of the same operator are evaluated) in order to reduce time to convergence, with no extra computational burden.

We focus primarily on characterising this behaviour in the Gaussian case and then more broadly in the strongly log-concave case, showing that although the exact Wasserstein–Fisher–Rao flow preserves log-concavity uniformly in time, under some conditions. Decay rates of the exact WFR flow are then characterised in terms of the symmetrised
\mathrm{KL}
, using the derived log-concavity constants and new functional inequalities for Fisher–Rao flows in
[
7
]
.

There are several avenues for future work, including (but not limited to) 1) quantifying the impact of further space and time discretisations of the W and FR flows, which are currently assumed to be evaluated exactly; 2) using the derived results to develop adaptive step size splitting schemes and to determine which ordering is preferrable and finally 3) in more precisely quantifying the acceleration/deceleration of symmetrised
\mathrm{KL}
due to splitting.

and thus

\displaystyle f^{(1)}(s)

\displaystyle=-e^{(\gamma-s)f_{\textrm{W}}}\mathcal{G}^{2}\eta_{s}

using the fact that
f_{\textrm{W}}
and
e^{tf_{\textrm{W}}}
commute for any
t>0
.

For
f^{(2)}
we have

\displaystyle f^{(2)}(s)

\displaystyle=f_{\textrm{W}}e^{(\gamma-s)f_{W}}\mathcal{G}^{2}\eta_{s}-e^{(\gamma-s)f_{W}}\partial_{s}\mathcal{G}^{2}\eta_{s}.

Recalling that

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{2}v=[f_{\textrm{W}},[f_{\textrm{W}},f_{\textrm{FR}}]](v)=f_{\textrm{W}}(\mathcal{G}^{1}v)-(\mathcal{G}^{1})^{\prime}(v)[f_{\textrm{W}}(v)],

we find

\displaystyle\partial_{s}f_{\textrm{W}}(\mathcal{G}^{1}\eta_{s})

\displaystyle=f_{\textrm{W}}(\mathcal{G}^{1}\eta_{s})[\partial_{s}\mathcal{G}^{1}\eta_{s}]=f_{\textrm{W}}(\mathcal{G}^{1}\eta_{s})\left([f_{\textrm{W}},f_{\textrm{FR}}]^{\prime}(\eta_{s})[f_{\textrm{W}}(\eta_{s})]\right)

\displaystyle\partial_{s}(\mathcal{G}^{1})^{\prime}(\eta_{s})[f_{\textrm{W}}(\eta_{s})]

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{G}^{1})^{\prime\prime}(\eta_{s})(f_{\textrm{W}}(\eta_{s}),f_{\textrm{W}}(\eta_{s}))+(\mathcal{G}^{1})^{\prime}(\eta_{s})[f_{\textrm{W}}^{2}(\eta_{s})].

Therefore

\displaystyle\partial_{s}\mathcal{G}^{2}\eta_{s}

\displaystyle=f_{\textrm{W}}(\mathcal{G}^{1}\eta_{s})\left([f_{\textrm{W}},f_{\textrm{FR}}]^{\prime}(\eta_{s})[f_{\textrm{W}}(\eta_{s})]\right)-(\mathcal{G}^{1})^{\prime\prime}(\eta_{s})(f_{\textrm{W}}(\eta_{s}),f_{\textrm{W}}(\eta_{s}))+(\mathcal{G}^{1})^{\prime}(\eta_{s})[f_{\textrm{W}}^{2}(\eta_{s})]

\displaystyle=f_{\textrm{W}}(\mathcal{G}^{1}\eta_{s})\left([f_{\textrm{W}},f_{\textrm{FR}}]^{\prime}(\eta_{s})[f_{\textrm{W}}(\eta_{s})]\right)-\left((\mathcal{G}^{1})^{\prime}(\eta_{s})f_{\textrm{W}}(\eta_{s})\right)^{\prime}[f_{\textrm{W}}(\eta_{s})]

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{G}^{2})^{\prime}(\eta_{s})[f_{\textrm{W}}(\eta_{s})]

using the chain rule for operators.

Following the same steps as before we have

\partial_{s}\mathcal{G}^{2}\eta_{s}-f_{\textrm{W}}(\mathcal{G}^{2}\eta_{s})=-\mathcal{G}^{3}\eta_{s}

and thus

f^{(2)}(s)=e^{(\gamma-s)f_{W}}\mathcal{G}^{3}\eta_{s}
.
A recursive argument then gives

\displaystyle f^{(k)}(s)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}e^{(\gamma-s)f_{W}}\mathcal{G}^{k+1}\eta_{s},

from which follows

\displaystyle e^{(\gamma-\tau)f_{\textrm{W}}}\mathcal{G}^{1}\eta_{\tau}=\mathcal{G}^{1}\eta_{\gamma}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}(\tau-\gamma)^{k}\mathcal{G}^{k+1}\eta_{\gamma}.

Integrating both sides w.r.t.
\tau

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\gamma}e^{(\gamma-\tau)f_{\textrm{W}}}\mathcal{G}^{1}\eta_{\tau}d\tau

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\gamma}\mathcal{G}^{1}\eta_{\gamma}d\tau+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathcal{G}^{k+1}\eta_{\gamma}}{k!}\int_{0}^{\gamma}(\gamma-\tau)^{k}d\tau

\displaystyle=\gamma\mathcal{G}^{1}\eta_{\gamma}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathcal{G}^{k+1}\eta_{\gamma}}{k!}\frac{\gamma^{k+1}}{k+1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\gamma^{k}}{k!}\mathcal{G}^{k}(\eta_{\gamma}).

∎

Before stating our results we recall the following properties of matrix exponentials: for

\displaystyle e^{X}e^{Y}

\displaystyle e^{YXY^{-1}}

\displaystyle=Ye^{X}Y^{-1}\quad Y\enskip\text{invertible}

\displaystyle g_{M}(\mu_{t})

\displaystyle=\nabla g(\mu_{t})^{\top}M_{t}\nabla g(\mu_{t})

(B.2)

Lemma B.1
.

Moment ODEs, Multivariate Gaussian.
Consider (
B.1
) with the aforementioned conditions. Then
m_{t},C_{t}
satisfy for all
t>0
,

\displaystyle\frac{dC_{t}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-C_{t}C_{\pi}^{-1}(I-2M_{t}C_{\pi}^{-1})C_{t}-\left(I-\tfrac{1}{2}C_{\pi}+2M_{t}\right)C_{\pi}^{-1}C_{t}-C_{t}C_{\pi}^{-1}(I-\tfrac{1}{2}C_{\pi}+2M_{t})+2(I+M_{t})

(B.3)

\displaystyle\frac{dm_{t}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-(C_{t}C_{\pi}^{-1}(I-2M_{t}C_{\pi}^{-1})+(I+2M_{t})C_{\pi}^{-1})(m_{t}-m_{\pi})

(B.4)

Proof.

By straightforward integration and assuming interchange of derivatives and integrals,

\displaystyle\frac{dC_{t}}{dt}=\int(x-m_{t})(x-m_{t})^{\top}\partial_{t}\mu_{t}(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int(x-m_{t})(x-m_{t})^{\top}(f_{\textrm{W}}(\mu_{t})+f_{\textrm{FR}}(\mu_{t}))dx

(B.5)

\displaystyle+\int(x-m_{t})(x-m_{t})^{\top}(g_{M}(\mu_{t})-\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{t}}[g_{M}])\mu_{t}(x)dx.

The first term on the rhs is obtained directly from the covariance ODE for the exact WFR, see
[
15
, Appendix D]
for a derivation,

\displaystyle\int(x-m_{t})(x-m_{t})^{\top}(f_{\textrm{W}}(\mu_{t})+f_{\textrm{FR}}(\mu_{t}))dx=-C_{t}C_{\pi}^{-1}C_{t}+C_{t}-C_{\pi}^{-1}C_{t}-C_{t}C_{\pi}^{-1}+2I.

(B.6)

The remaining term is straightforwardly evaluated using that for

\displaystyle g_{M}(\mu_{t})=(x-m_{t}+m_{t}-\tilde{m}_{t})^{\top}\tilde{C}_{t}^{-1}M_{t}\tilde{C}_{t}^{-1}(x-m_{t}+m_{t}-\tilde{m}_{t})

(B.7)

where

and
\tilde{m}_{t}=\tilde{C}_{t}(C_{\pi}^{-1}m_{\pi}-C_{t}^{-1}m_{t})
. Then,

\displaystyle\int(x-m_{t})(x-m_{t})^{\top}g_{M}(\mu_{t})\mu_{t}(x)dx=\int(x-m_{t})(x-m_{t})^{\top}(x-m_{t})^{\top}\tilde{C}_{t}^{-1}M_{t}\tilde{C}_{t}^{-1}(x-m_{t})\mu_{t}(x)dx

\displaystyle+2\int(x-m_{t})(x-m_{t})^{\top}(x-m_{t})^{\top}\mu_{t}(x)dx\,\tilde{C}_{t}^{-1}M_{t}\tilde{C}_{t}^{-1}(m_{t}-\tilde{m}_{t})

\displaystyle+C_{t}(m_{t}-\tilde{m}_{t})^{\top}\tilde{C}_{t}^{-1}M_{t}\tilde{C}_{t}^{-1}(m_{t}-\tilde{m}_{t})

\displaystyle=2C_{t}\tilde{C}_{t}^{-1}M_{t}\tilde{C}_{t}^{-1}C_{t}+C_{t}Tr[C_{t}\tilde{C}_{t}^{-1}M_{t}\tilde{C}_{t}^{-1}]+C_{t}(m_{t}-\tilde{m}_{t})^{\top}\tilde{C}_{t}^{-1}M_{t}\tilde{C}_{t}^{-1}(m_{t}-\tilde{m}_{t})

\displaystyle=2(C_{t}C_{\pi}^{-1}M_{t}C_{\pi}^{-1}C_{t}-C_{t}C_{\pi}^{-1}M_{t}-M_{t}C_{\pi}^{-1}C_{t}+M_{t})+C_{t}Tr[C_{t}\tilde{C}_{t}^{-1}M_{t}\tilde{C}_{t}^{-1}]

\displaystyle+C_{t}(m_{t}-\tilde{m}_{\gamma})^{\top}\tilde{C}_{t}^{-1}M_{t}\tilde{C}_{t}^{-1}(m_{t}-\tilde{m}_{t})

(B.8)

where in the second last line we have used that for a random variable

,

and any constant matrix
A

\mathbb{E}[XX^{\top}X^{\top}AX]=\mathbb{E}[XX^{T}AXX^{T}]=\Sigma(A+A^{\top})\Sigma+\Sigma Tr[\Sigma A]
. Also,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{t}}[g_{M}]\int(x-m_{t})(x-m_{t})^{\top}\mu_{t}(x)dx=C_{t}\left(Tr[C_{t}\tilde{C}_{t}^{-1}M_{t}\tilde{C}_{t}^{-1}]+(m_{t}-\tilde{m}_{t})^{\top}\tilde{C}_{t}^{-1}M_{t}\tilde{C}_{t}^{-1}(m_{t}-\tilde{m}_{t})\right)

(B.9)

Substituting (
B.8
) and (
B.9
) into (
B.5
) and combining with (
B.6
) yields the covariance ODE (
B.3

The mean ODE is obtained in a similar way

\displaystyle\frac{dm_{t}}{dt}=\int x\partial_{t}\mu_{t}(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int x(f_{\textrm{W}}(\mu_{t})+f_{\textrm{FR}}(\mu_{t}))dx+\int x(g_{M}(\mu_{t})-\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{t}}[g_{M}])\mu_{t}(x)dx,

where the first term on the rhs is obtained directly from the mean ODE for the exact WFR, see
[

\displaystyle\int x(f_{\textrm{W}}(\mu_{t})+f_{\textrm{FR}}(\mu_{t}))dx=-(C_{t}+I)C_{\pi}^{-1}(m_{t}-m_{\pi})

(B.10)

and for the remaining term, using (

\displaystyle\int x(g_{M}(\mu_{t})-\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{t}}[g_{M}])\mu_{t}(x)dx

Appendix C
Preservation of log-concavity

Our proof strategy involves starting with a sequential splitting of the WFR flow and using Girsanov theorem to characterise the intermediate density due to the W flow.
This is based on a similar proof strategy used to show that the filtering density satisfies a Poincaré inequality (see Lemma 5.1 in
[
38
]
). In Appendix

, we show that when the initial density is “sufficiently” strongly log-concave (to be made precise), there exists a finite time interval over which the W flow maintains log-concavity. Although the W flow cannot be expected to preserve log-concavity uniformly in time (unless
\pi
is Gaussian), the properties of the FR flow can be exploited to maintain log-concavity uniformly in time. The next lemma shows that the FR does exactly this, and then the proof in Appendix

combines the results.

\delta_{0}
in the first step, as this will be used to define the sequence of step sizes. Then

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}(\mathcal{F}_{1}-\tfrac{1+\delta_{1}}{2}V_{\pi})

\displaystyle=\nabla^{2}\left(\left(1-\tfrac{1+\delta_{1}}{2}\right)V_{\pi}+e^{-\tau_{1}}(\mathcal{G}_{1}-\tfrac{1}{2}V_{\pi})\right),

\displaystyle=\nabla^{2}\left(\left(\tfrac{1-\delta_{1}-e^{-\tau_{1}}}{2}\right)V_{\pi}+e^{-\tau_{1}}\mathcal{G}_{1}\right)

\displaystyle\succeq\left(\left(\tfrac{1-\delta_{1}-e^{-\tau_{1}}}{2}\right)\alpha_{\pi}+e^{-\tau_{1}}\widehat{c}_{1}\right)I

\displaystyle=:\alpha_{d,1}I

where
\alpha_{d,1}
denotes the concavity parameter required for Assumption
3
(a)
in Lemma
4.1
. A sufficient condition for strict positivity of
\alpha_{d,1}
is to ensure
0<\delta_{1}<1-e^{-\tau_{1}}
. Then
\widehat{\nu}_{2}
is again strongly convex by Lemma
4.1
with potential
\mathcal{E}_{2}=\frac{1}{2}V_{\pi}+\mathcal{G}_{2}
,
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{G}_{2}\succeq\widehat{c}_{2}I
and

\displaystyle\widehat{c}_{2}

\displaystyle=b\tan\left(\tan^{-1}\left(\tfrac{c_{1}}{b}\right)-b\tau_{2}\right)

\displaystyle c_{1}

\displaystyle=\left(\tfrac{1-\delta_{1}-e^{-\tau_{1}}}{2}\right)\alpha_{\pi}+e^{-\tau_{1}}\widehat{c}_{1}+\tfrac{\delta_{1}}{2}\alpha_{\pi}

\displaystyle=\left(\tfrac{1-e^{-\tau_{1}}}{2}\right)\alpha_{\pi}+e^{-\tau_{1}}\widehat{c}_{1}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\alpha_{\pi}+e^{-\tau_{1}}(\widehat{c}_{1}-\tfrac{\alpha_{\pi}}{2})

and
c_{1}>0
since
\tau_{1}
is chosen such that
\widehat{c}_{1}>0
. Once again,
\tau_{2}
must be chosen such that
\widehat{c}_{2}>0
. Then once again for the FR step, we have
\nu_{2}\propto\exp(-\mathcal{F}_{2})
with

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{2}

\displaystyle=(1-e^{-\tau_{2}})V_{\pi}+e^{-\tau_{2}}\mathcal{E}_{2}

\displaystyle=V_{\pi}+e^{-\tau_{2}}(\mathcal{E}_{2}-V_{\pi})

\displaystyle=V_{\pi}+e^{-\tau_{2}}(\mathcal{G}_{2}-\frac{1}{2}V_{\pi})

and
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{F}_{2}\succ\alpha_{2}I
where

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}=\left(1-\frac{1}{2}e^{-\tau_{2}}\right)\alpha_{\pi}+e^{-\tau_{2}}\widehat{c}_{2}.

Proceeding inductively for
i=3,4,\dots
, in a similar way with required conditions on
\tau_{i}
and
\delta_{i}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\tfrac{1}{2}e^{-\tau_{i}}\right)\alpha_{\pi}+e^{-\tau_{i}}\widehat{c}_{i}=c_{i}+\frac{\alpha_{\pi}}{2},\quad i=1,2,3,\dots

(C.13)

where
\widehat{c}_{{i}}
and
c_{i}
satisfy the recursion for
i=1,2,3,\dots

\displaystyle\widehat{c}_{i}

\displaystyle=b\tan\left(\tan^{-1}\left(\tfrac{c_{i-1}}{b}\right)-b\tau_{i}\right)

(C.14)

\displaystyle c_{i}

\displaystyle=(1-e^{-\tau_{i}})\frac{\alpha_{\pi}}{2}+e^{-\tau_{i}}\widehat{c}_{i}

(C.15)

\displaystyle c_{0}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{d}+\frac{\delta_{0}}{2}\alpha_{\pi}.

(C.16)

Similarly to the limiting analysis in the proof of Lemma
4.1
, we have that (
C.14
)-(
C.15
) corresponds to a splitting scheme of the ODE

\displaystyle\dot{c}_{t}=-c_{t}^{2}-c_{t}-b^{2}+\frac{\alpha_{\pi}}{2},

(C.17)

whereby for the
i
th iteration, the ODE
\dot{c}_{t}=-c_{t}^{2}-b^{2}
, initialised at
c_{{i-1}}
is solved over time
\tau_{i}
yielding
\widehat{c}_{i}
and then the ODE
\dot{c}_{t}=-c_{t}+\alpha_{\pi}
initialised at
\widehat{c}_{i}
is solved over time
\tau_{i}
yielding
c_{i}
.
Finally, due to (
C.13
),
\alpha_{t}=c_{t}+\frac{\alpha_{\pi}}{2}
, is the log-concavity constant of
\mu_{t}
, the solution of the exact WFR PDE (
2.1
) at time
t
.

We now make precise the conditions on
\tau_{i}
which must be satisfied in order for the limit (
C.17
) to hold as
\max_{i}\tau_{i}\rightarrow 0
. Recall that
\tau_{i}
must be chosen such that
\widehat{c}_{i}>0
, which using (
C.12
), is possible if
0<\tau_{i}<\tau_{i}^{\ast}
where

\displaystyle\tau_{i}^{\ast}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{b}\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{c_{i-1}}{b}\right),\quad i=1,2,3,\dots

(C.18)

Notice that there exists a
\tau_{1}^{\ast}>0
whenever
\alpha_{d},\delta_{0}>0
, which implies
\widehat{c}_{1}>0
and
c_{1}>\left(1-e^{-\tau_{1}}\right)\frac{\alpha_{\pi}}{2}
and
\tau_{2}^{\ast}>\frac{1}{b}\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{(1-e^{-\tau_{1}})\alpha_{\pi}}{2b}\right)>0
. This then implies
\widehat{c}_{2}>0
and once again
c_{2}>\left(1-e^{-\tau_{2}}\right)\frac{\alpha_{\pi}}{2}
and
\tau_{3}^{\ast}>\frac{1}{b}\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{(1-e^{-\tau_{2}})\alpha_{\pi}}{2b}\right)>0
. By induction,
\tau_{i}^{\ast}>\frac{1}{b}\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{(1-e^{-\tau_{i-1}})\alpha_{\pi}}{2b}\right)>0
and since
\tau_{i-1}<\tau_{i-1}^{\ast}
, we can instead consider the recursion

\displaystyle\tau^{\ast}_{i}=\frac{1}{b}\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{(1-e^{-\tau_{i-1}^{\ast}})\alpha_{\pi}}{2b}\right),\enskip i=1,2,3,\dots

(C.19)

It is not difficult to see that
\tau_{i}^{\ast}>0
for all
i=1,2,3,\dots
. Its limit,
\tau_{\infty}^{\ast}
can be found from the fixed point equation
\tau^{\ast}_{\infty}=g(\tau_{\infty}^{\ast})
where
g(\tau):=\frac{1}{b}\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{(1-e^{-\tau})\alpha_{\pi}}{2b}\right)
, for which
t_{\infty}^{\ast}=0
is a valid (but not unique) solution. Notice also that
0<\frac{dg(\tau)}{d\tau}=\frac{\alpha_{\pi}}{2b^{2}}\frac{e^{-\tau}}{1+\left(\frac{(1-e^{-\tau})\alpha_{\pi}}{2b}\right)^{2}}<\frac{\alpha_{\pi}}{2b^{2}}
for all
\tau>0
. Under the assumed condition (
4.4
),
\frac{dg(0)}{d\tau}=\frac{\alpha_{\pi}}{2b^{2}}>0
,
\tau=0
is an unstable fixed point, so that
\tau_{\infty}^{\ast}\neq 0
since
\tau_{1}^{\ast}\neq 0
. To show that there exists at least one other
0<\tau_{\infty}^{\ast}<\infty
, consider
h(\tau):=g(\tau)-\tau
. First note that
h(\tau)\rightarrow-\infty
as
\tau\rightarrow\infty
since
g(\tau)<\frac{1}{b}\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\alpha_{\pi}}{2b}\right)
for all
\tau\geq 0
. Additionally,
h(0)=0
and
\frac{dh(0)}{d\tau}>0
and
\frac{dh(\tau)}{d\tau}\rightarrow-1
as
\tau\rightarrow\infty
monotonically.

Then by continuity of
h
and
g
, there must exist a
\tau>0
such that
h(\tau)=0
, i.e. there exists a
\tau_{\infty}^{\ast}>0
. Therefore, we may construct a sequence of step sizes
\{\tau_{i}\}_{i=1,2,3,}
with
\tau_{i}<\min(\tau_{1}^{\ast},\tau_{\infty}^{\ast}\neq 0)
for all
i=1,2,3,\dots
as a discretisation of the interval
[0,T]
for all
T>0
, for which the recursion (
C.14
)-(
C.15
) converges to the continuous time ODE (
C.17
) as
\max_{i}\tau_{i}\rightarrow 0
.

As a further confirmation of the need for condition (
4.4
), consider the case
\frac{\alpha_{\pi}}{2b^{2}}<1
. The recursion (
C.19
) is a decreasing sequence with
\tau_{\infty}^{\ast}=0
by a standard fixed point argument. Furthermore, the bound on
\frac{dg(\tau)}{d\tau}
implies
g
is Lipschitz and so
|\tau_{i+1}^{\ast}-\tau_{i}^{\ast}|=|g(\tau_{i}^{\ast})-g(\tau_{i-1}^{\ast})|\leq\frac{\alpha_{\pi}}{2b^{2}}|\tau_{i}^{\ast}-\tau_{i-1}^{\ast}|
. Then since
g(0)=0
, it holds that
\tau_{i}^{\ast}\leq\left(\frac{\alpha_{\pi}}{2b^{2}}\right)^{i}\tau_{0}^{\ast}
. Since
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\alpha_{\pi}}{2b^{2}}\right)^{i}=\frac{1}{1-\frac{\alpha_{\pi}}{2b^{2}}}
whenever
\frac{\alpha_{\pi}}{2b^{2}}<1
, it holds that
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\tau_{i}<\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\tau_{i}^{\ast}\leq\frac{1}{1-\frac{\alpha_{\pi}}{2b^{2}}}
, so that a uniform in time control does not follow from the above sequence of thresholds
\{\tau_{i}^{\ast}\}
.

Finally, to obtain a uniform in time log-concavity constant, consider the fixed point of (
C.17
),

\displaystyle c_{\infty}=\frac{-1\pm\sqrt{1+4(\frac{\alpha_{\pi}}{2}-b^{2})}}{2},

(C.20)

which has one real positive solution under condition (
4.4
). Note also that
c_{t}
approaches
c_{\infty}
monotonically, as is true for scalar, autonomous Riccati ODEs. Its explicit solution is trivially found by solving the separable ODE, which we include here for completeness. Denote by
c_{\infty,1}
and
c_{\infty,2}
, the positive and negative solutions of (
C.20
), respectively. Then,

\displaystyle\int_{c_{0}}^{c_{T}}\frac{1}{(c_{t}-c_{\infty,1})(c_{t}-c_{\infty,2})}dc_{t}

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{T}dt

\displaystyle\frac{(c_{T}-c_{\infty,1})(c_{0}-c_{\infty,2})}{(c_{T}-c_{\infty,2})(c_{0}-c_{\infty,1})}

\displaystyle=e^{-T(c_{\infty,1}-c_{\infty,2})}

\displaystyle(c_{T}-c_{\infty,1})

\displaystyle=l_{0}e^{-T(c_{\infty,1}-c_{\infty,2})}(c_{T}-c_{\infty,2})

\displaystyle c_{T}

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{\infty,1}-c_{\infty,2}l_{0}e^{-T(c_{\infty,1}-c_{\infty,2})}}{1-l_{0}e^{-T(c_{\infty,1}-c_{\infty,2})}}

where
l_{0}=\frac{(c_{0}-c_{\infty,1})}{(c_{0}-c_{\infty,2})}
. Finally, noting that
\alpha_{T}=c_{T}+\frac{\alpha_{\pi}}{2}
yields the result.
