Knowledge, Attitude and Practice of Dairy Farmers Regarding Antibiotic Use and Resistance in Selected Regions of Eritrea
Authors: Redie Kidane Ghebrehawariat, Derrick Noah Sentamu, KhairElsid Ali Abdella, Zerai Woldehiwet, Michael Kahsay Ghebremariam, Gabriel Oluga Aboge, Joshua Orungo Onono
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIrrational use of antibiotics in livestock and human, especially in low- and middle-income countries poses public health threat. Despite the extensive use of antibiotics in dairy farms in Eritrea, so far there is no published report on use of antibiotics and other related health challenges.MethodologyA cross-sectional survey was undertaken between July to October, 2023 to assess the dairy farmers’ knowledge, attitude and practice regarding antibiotic use and antibiotic resistance through face-to-face interviews using semi-structured questionnaires.ResultsAll dairy farmers 246 (100%) reported using antibiotics, yet 160 (65%) could not identify which infections antibiotics are intended to treat, and 151 (61.4%) had no knowledge of antibiotic resistance. Majority of dairy farmers 207 (84.1%) kept no treatment records, and 144 (58.5%) did not observe withdrawal periods for antibiotics before milk consumption. Overall, 209 (89%), 196 (79.7%) and 200 (81.3%) of the farmers possess inadequate knowledge, unfavorable attitude, and poor practice, respectively. Dairy farmers in Maekel region had favorable attitudes (OR = 3.8, p = 0.037), while farmers in Debub (OR = 13, p = 0.022) and Maekel (OR = 14, p = 0.018) regions showed better practices than Anseba region. And, university educated respondents showed markedly better practices (OR = 35.4, p = 0.002) on antibiotic usage as compared to non-graduates.ConclusionThe low level of farmer’s Knowledge, attitude and practice in our study underscore the need for a tailored intervention to improve the awareness of farmers on antibiotic use and resistance in order to mitigate the public health risk.Keywords: knowledge, attitude, practice, antibiotic use, resistance, dairy farmers, regions, eritrea

Introduction

Antimicrobials play an indispensable role in the treatment of livestock diseases, preventing infections, and enhancing productivity.1,2 Nonetheless, the expanding demand for animal products in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC) has led to their excessive use.3 This overuse may accelerate the emergence of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), as bacteria naturally evolve resistance when repeatedly exposed to antibiotics.4,5 Currently, AMR is globally a serious public health threat, causing considerable mortality and financial strain. It is estimated that the human death toll attributed to AMR will be 10 million per annum by 2050, accompanied by a financial loss of USD 100 trillion.6 Hence, the World Health Organization (WHO) categorized antibiotic resistance among the ten leading threats to health globally.7

Two-thirds of the antibiotics produced globally are consumed by livestock reared for food production.8 It has been reported that the use of antibiotics in livestock is among the many other factors that play a role in the occurrence of AMR in humans.1,2 In particular, the widespread use of antibiotics in the dairy sector, where 68% of antibiotics is used for treating mastitis in dairy cattle, along with the restricted invention of new antimicrobials is greatly contributing to the occurrence of AMR is concerning.9,10

Farmers’ knowledge, attitude and practice (KAP) are key factors determining antimicrobial use (AMU) practices and ultimately leading to the emergence and spread of AMR.11 Geta et al12 stated that the injudicious use of antibiotics in animal and human health facilitates the development and spread of antibiotic-resistant bacteria, thereby substantially endangering human health. Among farmers, inappropriate use of antibiotics, such as incorrect dosage, purchasing antibiotics without a prescription, and non-compliance with the withdrawal period are attributed to the farmers’ poor knowledge, unfavorable attitude, and suboptimal practices.13,14 For instance, a study conducted in Ethiopia reported that inappropriate use of antimicrobials in animals is strongly associated with multidrug resistance (MDR) in the study area.15 Furthermore, the intense use of antimicrobials may accelerate the selection and persistence of resistance genes in bacteria, and if such bacteria is of zoonotic importance, may transmit to humans through direct contact, food or environment.16 Checcussi et al17 reported that using animal manure in soils can enhance the proliferation and spread of antibiotic residue, antibiotic resistant bacteria, and resistance genes in the environment (soil-water).

The lack of adequate veterinary services, poor regulations, and limited awareness of livestock farmers in LMICs including Africa exacerbated the condition of AMR. Reports from across Africa continuously indicated that low knowledge levels regarding AMU and AMR, along with limited attitude and inappropriate practices of livestock farmers, play a role in the misuse and overuse of antimicrobial agents and the development of AMR.18–20

Eritrea, an East African country, is bestowed with indigenous livestock of various types, mainly small ruminants accounting for up to 67% of the total, followed by cattle (19%), chickens (11%), and camels (3%). Apart from the local cattle breeds, the country also has more than 20,000 heads of exotic and crossbreeds, mainly raised for milk production.21 The latest Joint External Evaluation report on International Health Regulations (IHR) Core Capacities of the State of Eritrea recommended the need to increase surveillance of AMR pathogens in both human and animal health sectors.22 With these recommendations, Eritrea has developed a national action plan for combating AMR. One of the four strategic objectives of the national action plan is to raise awareness through education and training.23 Although AMR is initially considered a public health issue, the emerging crisis requires a holistic (One Health) approach that incorporates humans, animals and the environment.24

Assessing the farmers’ KAP on antibiotic use (ABU) and antibiotic resistance (ABR) is fundamental in order to minimize the spread of antibiotic resistance originating from the dairy cows.25 Even though there is existing evidence of poor knowledge and inappropriate use of antibiotics in many countries, there is a paucity of nationwide data in Eritrea. There is very little published information about the prevalence of AMR in bacteria isolated from humans or animals in Eritrea. However, a recent study conducted in one of the regions (Anseba) showed that all S. aureus isolates from milk of cows with mastitis were resistant to nearly all the antimicrobial agents widely used in the country.26 To our knowledge, no study has been conducted in Eritrea to determine the dairy farmers’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding the judicious use of antibiotic agents and ABR. Establishing baseline data on the level of awareness of AMR among farmers is regarded as one of the first necessary steps towards establishing a sound AMR policy in developing countries.22,27

The main objective of the present study is to determine the knowledge, attitude, and practice of dairy farmers regarding the use of antibiotics. This information will be useful for policymakers to design strategies that raise awareness about AMR in the animal and human sectors and to make informed decisions on the distribution and control of antibiotics and other antimicrobial agents.

Methodology

Study Area and Sampling Design

The study was conducted in a northeast African country, Eritrea, located between 12°22′N and 18°02′ N latitude and 36°26’E and 43°13’E longitude. The country is bordered by Sudan to the West, Ethiopia to the South, the Red Sea to the East, and Djibouti to the Southeast. Administratively, Eritrea is divided into six regions: Maekel, Anseba, Debub, Gash Barka, Northern Red Sea, and Southern Red Sea.28 Of these, the three major milk producing regions Maekel, Debub and Anseba were selected as study sites (Figure 1). Maekel and Debub are located in the highland with a mean annual temperature ranging from 16 °C to 18 °C, while Anseba region which is located in the lowland reaches up to 28°C.29,30 The total dairy cow population in the country is estimated at 22350, with 1901, 6624, and 4870 cows in the Anseba, Debub, and Maekel regions, respectively.

A map showing the study areas.

The study population constituted middle and large-scale dairy farm owners in the respective study areas. Only one person per farm was interviewed.

Study Design

A cross-sectional study was carried out between July and October, 2023, to assess the knowledge, attitude and practice of dairy farmer in Eritrea regarding antibiotic use and resistance. The KAP studies have been proved useful for generating information on farmers’ knowledge about antibiotic use and resistance, their attitudes towards antibiotic use and their practices.13,27,31 Such information is helpful in designing strategies to optimize and maintain the therapeutic value of antibiotics by the responsible bodies.31

Sample Size Determination and Sampling Procedure

The sample size was calculated based on the formula,32 assuming a 95% confidence interval, 50% expected prevalence level, and an absolute error of 5%. Resulting in a sample size of 384. The estimated population with medium and large-scale dairy farms was small (N=431) (Registry of the regional animal resource offices of the respective study areas, 2023). Hence, the sample size was adjusted to 202 using the finite population correction factor.33 To increase the precision, the sample size was increased to 246.

Purposive sampling was used. The regions were selected due to their high density of registered dairy farms and significant contribution to the milk production. We only recruited the middle and large-scale dairy farms based on accessibility, feasibility.

Data Collection

Data was collected through a face-to-face interview of a person per farm using a semi-structured questionnaire containing both pre-defined closed-ended questions and open-ended questions. Each interview took up to 15–20 minutes. The questionnaire was developed based on previously published studies with a slight modification to suit the local context.11,13,27,34–41 It was prepared in English and then translated into a local language (Tigrigna) for a better understanding by the respondents. The instrument contained a total of 38 questions in four main parts: socio-demography (N=4), knowledge of ABU and ABR (N=13), attitude (N=7), and practice (N=14), with various formats of questions such as multiple-choice, open-ended, binary responses (yes/no), and a three-point Likert scale (Agree, Disagree, and not know) (Supplementary Material S1).

Prior to the interview, the questionnaire was reviewed by veterinary epidemiology and public health experts for clarity, relevance and reliability.

Data Analysis

The data obtained from the interview were entered into Microsoft Excel, cleaned, coded, and analyzed using the R software version 4.2.2.42 Descriptive statistics were computed to summarize the data and presented using percentages and frequencies in tables and graphs.

Univariate analysis was conducted; a chi-square test was used to determine the association between the dependent variables (knowledge, attitude, and practice) and the independent variables (socio-demographic factors) stratified by region. A confidence level set at 95% and results with a p-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The “Arsenal” package was used for this analysis.43

For inferential analysis, the KAP’s responses were assigned equal weights that totaled 100. A threshold of ≥75% was set to create bivariate variables from all responses constituting each of the KAP. Hence, respondents scored ≥ 75% were considered knowledgeable, have favorable Attitudes and good Practices.

Multivariable logistic regression analysis was carried out to explore the associations between socio-demographic characteristics and KAP variable. The outcomes were reported as Odds Ratios (OR) and confidence intervals (95%). The association between the bivariate KAP variables was analyzed using a chi-square test with p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant from the “stats” package. Anseba region was selected as the reference region as previous research conducted there provided relevant epidemiological evidence on bovine mastitis, Staphylococcus aureus, and antimicrobial resistance, making it an appropriate baseline for comparison with Debub and Maekel.26

Ethical Statement

Ethical clearance was obtained from the joint Ethics Committee of the Ministry of Agriculture (MOA), National Agricultural Research Institution (NARI), Hamelmalo Agricultural College (HAC); and the University of Nairobi (UoN). A written request was sent to the regional representatives of the Ministry of Agriculture of the three regions, and permission was sought and granted. A written consent form was prepared in English and the local language, and only volunteers who signed the written consent were recruited for the study.

Before signing the consent form, participants were fully informed about the research’s objectives and benefits.

Results

Socio-Demographic Characteristics of Participants

From the total number of respondents, 223 (90.7%) were male, and the remaining 23 (9.3%) were female. The majority of the participants, 38.2% (n=94) were aged 60 years and above, followed by 24.8% (n=61) 41–50 years, while 24.0% (n=59), 9.8% (n=24) and 3.3% (n=8) were between the ages of 51–60, 31–40, and 21–30, respectively (Table 1). The educational levels of the participants were high school (42.7%; n=105), Junior (19.9%; n=49), Illiterate (9.3%; n=23), University/College (12.2%; n=30), and Primary (15.9%; n=39). The majority of the respondents, 34.1% (n=84), had engaged in dairy farming for 11–20 years, while 4.5% (n=11) had the least experience of 0–5 years (Table 1).

Knowledge of Dairy Farmers Regarding Antibiotic Use

More than three-fourths of the respondents, 81.7% (n=201), had heard about antibiotics and could correctly describe and explain them. Knowledge of antibiotics significantly differed by region (p = 0.006) (Table 2). The majority of participants in the three study regions, 78.0% (n=192), indicated that antibiotics should be used to treat infections, followed by 12.2% (n=30) for prevention and treatment (p = 0.742) (Table 2). Only 35.0% (n=86) of the respondents correctly knew that antibiotics are used to treat infections caused by bacteria, while 28.9% (n=71) of the respondents said they did not know against which causative agent antibiotics are used; this was significantly different (P = 0.024). The average number of days of antibiotic administration was (3.4 SD: ±1.3, range 1–8) and did not significantly differ by region (p = 0.915). Only 44.3% (n=109) of the respondents knew about the withdrawal period; the rest, more than half, 55.7% (n=137), did not know. Of the three regions, Anseba had the highest percentage of respondents, 85.7% (n=30) who “didn’t know” what the withdrawal period is, followed by the Maekel region, 57.1% (n=76); these numbers significantly differed by region (p < 0.001) (Table 2). Overall, only less than half of the 43.9% (n=108), knew that administering overdoses or underdoses of antibiotics constitute antibiotic abuse. A significant difference was observed across regions (p = 0.008). The highest proportion of respondents, 38.2% (n=94), did not know the consequences of antibiotic misuse, whereas 30.9% (n=76) reported that persistence of infection despite treatment and extra cost of treatments could arise as consequences. These responses differed significantly by region (p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Knowledge of Dairy Farmers Regarding Antibiotic Resistance (ABR)

The majority of dairy farmers/workers, 61.4% (n=151), did not know about antibiotic resistance (Table 3). Almost half of the respondents, 113 (45.9%), knew that bacteria can develop resistance to antibiotics, while 96 (39.0%) did not know; there was a significant difference by region (p = 0.005). Of all the respondents, only 30 (12.2%) knew all the routes by which antibiotic resistance can be passed from dairy cows to humans; 45 (18.3%) did not know any of these routes, while the rest could only mention some but not all the routes, these responses significantly differed by region (p < 0.001). Less than half of the respondents,102 (41.5%) could correctly highlight all the effects of antibiotic resistance on humans, while the majority 144 (58.5%) could mention partially or had no knowledge at all. Almost half of the respondents, 120 (48.8%), believed that improper waste management had no role in spreading antibiotic resistance. A less than half of respondents, 117 (47.6%), agreed that vaccination can limit the occurrence of antibiotic resistance (Table 3).

Dairy Farmers’ Attitude Regarding Antibiotic Use and Resistance

Most dairy farmers/workers, 235 (95.5%), agreed that proper farm management (good hygiene, proper feeding, and good biosecurity practices) has a role in reducing antibiotic use and thereby the occurrence of antibiotic resistance (Table 4). Almost half of the respondents, 112 (45.5%), agreed that misuse and overuse of antibiotics in lactating dairy cows can lead to antibiotic resistance, while 101 (41.1%) did not know about that (p = 0.006). A higher percentage of respondents 107 (43.5%) agreed that using the same antibiotic for a long time could lead to antibiotic resistance, and a significant number of respondents, 93 (37.8%), had no knowledge about it (p = 0.039) (Table 4). Nearly three-fourths, 173 (70.3%), of the respondents disagreed that if one animal in the herd is sick, the whole herd should be treated with antibiotics to prevent them from infection; this response significantly differed by region (p = 0.017). Over two-thirds of the respondents, 166 (67.5%), disagreed with increasing the dose and frequency of antibiotics in the absence of recovery (p < 0.001). The majority of the dairy farmers/workers, 191 (77.6%) disagreed that using leftover antibiotics even after a long time is okay. About half of the respondents, 112 (45.5%), had no knowledge that consuming milk before the withdrawal period can expose humans to antibiotic resistance; this response significantly differed by region (p = 0.022) (Table 4).

Practice of Dairy Farmers on Antibiotic Use and Resistance

Antibiotics were mostly purchased from veterinary clinics (36.6%; n=90), other sources of the drugs included veterinary drug shops and hawkers, and these significantly differed by region (p < 0.001) (Table 5). The majority of the respondents, 176 (71.5%), always purchased antibiotics according to prescriptions (p < 0.001). Most prescriptions, 193 (78.5%), were by animal health professionals, followed by both animal health professionals and farmers themselves, 46 (18.7%), and only farmers themselves, 7 (2.8%), and these differed by region (p < 0.001) (Table 5). Almost half of the respondents, 112 (45.5%), said that animal health workers administer antibiotics. However, a significant number of the participants, 94 (38.2%) indicated that they administer the antibiotics themselves. Almost half of the dairy farmers 116 (47.2%) who administer antibiotics by themselves, read the safety and dosage instructions. Of the respondents who administer antibiotics by themselves, 90 (36.6%) determined the dose based on body weight, and another 47 (19.1%) decided the dose based on the instructions on the label (p = 0.003) (Table 5). More than half of the participants, 167 (67.9%) store the antibiotics in a living room; others store them in the storeroom, 56 (22.8%), and the cowshed, 23 (9.3%) (Table 5). The highest percentage of respondents, 207 (84.1%), did not keep a record of treatment (p = 0.008). The majority of the respondents, 225 (91.6%), follow the antibiotic course as prescribed (p < 0.001). Three-fourths of the participants, 192 (78.0%), did not use expired drugs (p < 0.001). A significant percentage of the dairy farmers, 212 (86.2%), put effectiveness as a sole criterion for selecting/purchasing antibiotics; other criteria considered included effectiveness and easily accessible, 11 (4.5%), and 8 (3.3%) of said they put easy accessibility as a criterion. More than half of the respondents, 144 (58.5%), did not stick to the withdrawal period to use the milk for consumption. It was found that farmers hold milk to be used by consumers for an average number of days (1.955 SD: ± 2.6, range 0–10); there was no significant difference by region (p = 0.058) (Table 5).

Discussion

Documenting farmers’ KAP regarding antibiotic use and antibiotic resistance is crucial for designing strategies that ensure a proper use of antibiotics and thereby minimize the public health risk. The present questionnaire-based cross-sectional study investigated aspects of farmers’ knowledge, attitude and practice regarding ABU and their awareness of ABR in accordance with studies carried out in other low-income countries.44,45 To our knowledge, the current study is the first to investigate dairy farmers KAP regarding ABU and ABR in Eritrea.

All farmers (100%) acknowledged the use of antibiotics in their farms. Of all the antibiotics used, penicillin and oxytetracycline were the most used mainly because they are readily available and accessible and the broad-spectrum nature of the later.46 Due to their use in human medicine, inappropriate use in farm animals may present a potential risk to the public.47

Large percentages of the dairy farmers had no knowledge of ABU (87%) and ABR (89%). Likewise, 79.7% and 81.3% of the respondents exhibited negative attitudes and poor practices, respectively. Some of the inappropriate practices are: wrong dosage, poor storage, absence of record for previous treatments, and non-compliance with withdrawal periods. Similar findings were reported by studies conducted in Vietnam and Ethiopia.11,44 On the contrary, our findings contradict the reports of the studies from Adis Ababa, Ethiopia and Gweru district, Zimbabwe who showed an improved of levels of farmers’ KAP.27,48 Low levels of KAP may lead to misuse of antibiotics and emergence of resistance.49

More than three-fourths (81.7%) of the respondents had heard of antibiotics, and our finding is in line with previous studies.14,49 The knowledge level significantly differed by region (p<0.006) (Table 2). The farmers from Maekel region exhibited higher level of antibiotic knowledge and this can be due to variation in access to veterinary extension services and sources of information on antibiotic use. The findings indicate the need for region wise interventions to improve the farmers’ knowledge regarding ABU and ABR. Majority of the farmers used antibiotics to treat infections (78%), yet 65% could not identify which infections antibiotics are intended to treat; a finding lower than that reported in Ethiopia.31 Less than half of the respondents (44.3%) knew what withdrawal period is, which is consistent with the findings of an Indian study,50 but higher than the proportion reported from Ethiopia.31,51 Consuming milk with antibiotic residue can put the public at risk of acquiring ABR. Hence, improving dairy farmers’ knowledge in this regard is crucial. In the current study, only 43.9% of the respondents recognized that wrong dosage can play a part in the emergence of ABR. Our finding agrees with the findings from Ethiopia and Bangladesh.27,40 Inappropriate dosage is often attached to the emergence and spread of resistant bacteria and may contribute to the ABR in humans.52 Furthermore, our findings revealed that there was low knowledge level (30.9%) of the consequences following antibiotic misuse in dairy cows such as treatment failure and increased cost of treatment, consistent with findings of a previous study.12

More than half of the respondents (61.4%) had not heard of ABR, aligned with a similar finding from Indonesia.39 However, it is much lower than the reports from Vietnam and Amhara region, Ethiopia.11,12 Similar to the findings of a study from Rwanda13 who reported a knowledge level of 52.8%, in the current study 54.1% of the respondents had no prior knowledge if bacteria can develop resistance to antibiotics or not. Furthermore, only 41.5% of the respondents could correctly describe the impact of antibiotic resistance in humans almost similar with the findings from Ethiopia.12 On the other hand, only 28% of the respondents recognized that improper waste management can play a part on the of spread of ABR. Our findings also revealed that only 47.6% of the respondents acknowledged that vaccine can minimize the use of antibiotics and thereby reduce the emergence of ABR, in accordance with.40 All the discrepancies on the knowledge of antimicrobial resistance in our study could be due to information deficit which may encourage imprudent use of antibiotics. This can lead to the emergence of antibiotic-resistant bacteria and at same time promoting spread resistant bacteria to humans, especially those who work in close contact with the cows and those who consume their products. The findings of the current study indicate the need for targeted awareness creation trainings and educational campaigns to reinforce the farmers knowledge on antibiotics, rational use of antibiotics and resistance by the veterinary body under the Ministry of Agriculture in collaboration with other stake holders through seminars, posters, newspapers, radios etc.

Majority dairy farmers (95%), agree that good hygiene and biosecurity measures can reduce the use of antibiotics. Such attitude should be promoted as it can play a crucial role in controlling ABR. In the current study, less than half of the respondents acknowledged that inappropriate use of antibiotics (45.5%) and prolonged use of the same antibiotics (43.5%) in lactating dairy cows can lead to antibiotic resistance. Our finding is lower than the studies conducted in Rwanda and Amhara region, Ethiopia.12,13 This is worrisome because implementing safe ABU is impossible without a better awareness on the consequence of inappropriate ABU. Higher proportion of the respondents disagreed on treating the whole herd once one animal is found sick (70.3%), consistent with the report of a previous study.53 Furthermore, a greater fraction of the farmers disagreed on the increase of dose frequency in absence of recovery signs (67.5%), lower than the finding of a similar study conducted in Kenya.14 Unlike the findings of the study conducted in Ethiopia,31 in which 78.3% of the farmers admitted that they used leftover antibiotics after long time of storage, three fourths of the respondents (77.6%) in our study did not use long stored leftover antibiotics for treating sick animals. Only 35.8% of the farmers acknowledged that consuming milk before the withdrawal period can expose humans to antibiotic resistance. Much lower than the finding of a study conducted in Nakuru, Kenya where 82.5% of the farmers recognized that consuming milk from cows treated with antibiotics before withdrawal period is unsafe.45 It has been indicated that poor attitudes towards antibiotic use have been linked to development of antibiotic resistance in dairy cows.54 Therefore, efforts should be made to improve the attitudes of the dairy farmers towards antibiotic use and resistance.

Three-fourths of the farmers (74%) purchase the antibiotics from a proper source with a prescription from an animal health worker (71.5%), consistent with reports of previous studies.31,46,55 Nonetheless, only 45.5% of respondents in the current study admitted that antibiotics are administered by animal health workers, consistent with reports of previous studies from Rwanda in which 45.1% and 50.1, of the respondents indicated that antibiotics are administered by animal health officers.14,56 It has been indicated that farmers treat their animals by themselves to escape the cost of veterinary services.2,12 Such improper use of antibiotics may promote occurrence of ABR, put the consumers at risk through time by ingesting the antibiotic residues in milk. Of all the farmers who self-administer antibiotics, 47.2% read the safety and dosage instructions on the leaflet. Furthermore, those who administer antibiotics by themselves, 36.6% determined the dose solely based on the body weight, 19.1% based on the label and 13% based on the body weight and the label. Failure to read the safety and dosage instruction on the label can lead to emergence and spread of ABR among the livestock and probably to humans. Furthermore, majority of the respondents (84.1%), did not keep record of the antibiotics used at their farm, in line with study from Vietnam that reported 81.5% absence of record of treatment.11 Absence record can lead to poor monitoring of previously used antibiotics and failure of completing the treatment fully, hence, increasing the risk of development of resistance bacteria. A standardized treatment record should be introduced at the farms by the responsible body and farmers should be trained on record keeping and its use. Our findings revealed that more than three fourths of the farmers (78.0%) did not use expired antibiotics to treat their cows, similar to the report from Ethiopia.57 Furthermore 58.5% of the respondents did not adhere to the withdrawal period before selling or using the milk for human consumption, a study from Adis Ababa, Ethiopia reported a similar finding.27 Failure to adhere to the withdrawal period may result in selection of resistant bacteria due to the antibiotic residues in the consumed milk putting the public at risk of acquiring antibiotic resistance,58 compromising food safety and exposing consumers to carcinogenicity.59 Therefore, farmers should be trained on strict adherence to the withdrawal periods. Furthermore, a strong regulation should be put in place to control antibiotic dispensing, as sources like unlicensed hawkers can be a source of low-grade antibiotics and predisposing factors to antibiotic resistance, and antibiotics should be made available for farmers all the time.

Findings of various studies from Rwanda and Ethiopia, respectively, reported the association of demographic variables with all the KAP of farmers with antibiotic use and resistance.13,57 In contrast, in our study, only geographical location and education level of respondents were significantly associated with attitude and practice of dairy farmers (P<0.05). Respondents from Maekel region were 3.8 times more likely to express a favorable attitude towards ABU and ABR (OR = 3.8, 95% CI = 1.2–15.6). Likewise, respondents from Debub (OR = 13, 95% CI = 2.1–256.3) and Maekel (OR = 14.0, 95% CI = 2.3–278.4) exhibited a greater likelihood of using antibiotics appropriately than the reference region (Anseba). These findings are similar with reports of a previous study.60 The discrepancy on the association of regions to attitude and practice of respondents regarding antibiotic use and resistance inform that responsible bodies should put targeted intervention measures. Our findings further revealed that, education was also significantly associated with practices of farmers. Respondents with higher level of education (university) demonstrated higher likelihood of implementing suitable practices (OR = 35.4, 95% CI = 5.2–725.1) and is in accordance with the findings from Ouagadougou, Burkina Faso.61 Hence, it is imperative to strengthen educational and awareness campaigns for those with lower level of formal educational.

A strong association was exhibited among some KAP variables. Respondents with favorable attitude towards ABU were 4.82 times more likely to have good knowledge of ABU (OR = 4.82, 95% CI = 2.18–10.65, p < 0.0001). Similarly, respondents with favorable attitude were 4.36 times more likely to demonstrate a better practice on appropriate use of antibiotics (OR = 4.36, 95% CI = 2.16–8.78, p < 0.0001). Furthermore, respondents with a better knowledge of ABR were 4.18 times more likely to demonstrate an appropriate antibiotic use practice (OR = 4.18, 95% CI = 1.75–9.95, p = 0.002). Nationwide, large-scale studies should be conducted to assess the KAP of antibiotic use and resistance and ABR among farmers keeping different livestock species.

Strengths and Limitations

Our study had numerous strengths and limitations. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to assess knowledge, attitude and practice of dairy farmers regarding antibiotic use and resistance in selected regions of Eritrea. The study compared the current findings with findings of previous studies, which may provide directions for further studies. The study included the three major dairy producing regions which can adequately represent the country and we were able to compare the KAP levels among the regions. The findings provide base line information on farmers’ KAP regarding ABU and ABR which will help in designing targeted interventions on antibiotic use and farmers’ education.

In the contrary, the study had also limitations. There was a variation in the sample size among the three regions which could have effect on the meaningful associations between demography and the KAP as shown in (Table 6). Our study was a cross-sectional survey, hence, like various other similar studies the cause-and-effect relationship of the predictor variables and the dependent variables (KAP) of the dairy farmers/workers can be affected. Furthermore, our study did not assess factors other than the KAP such as access to veterinary services, farm management conditions, and economic constraints which may also influence the antibiotic use practices and play a role in development of ABR.

Conclusion

There were significant gaps on dairy farmers’ KAP regarding ABU and ABR in the study areas, accompanied by inappropriate practices such as poor knowledge on antibiotic resistance, Poor treatment record keeping, and failure to adhere to withdrawal periods. Dairy farmers’ KAP varied by region and educational level. As a first study in Eritrea, assessing dairy farmers’ KAP on antibiotic use and resistance, this work provided baseline information that can guide authorities in designing effective intervention strategies and policies to prevent and reduce antibiotic resistance.
