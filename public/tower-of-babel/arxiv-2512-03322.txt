SSLfmm: An R Package for Semi-Supervised Learning with a Mixed-Missingness Mechanism in Finite Mixture Models
Geoffrey J. McLachlan, Jinran Wu

Abstract
Semi-supervised learning (SSL) constructs classifiers from datasets in which only a subset of observations is labelled, a situation that naturally arises because obtaining labels often requires expert judgement or costly manual effort. This motivates methods that integrate labelled and unlabelled data within a learning framework. Most SSL approaches assume that label absence is harmless, typically treated as missing completely at random or ignored, but in practice, the missingness process can be informative, as the chances of an observation being unlabelled may depend on the ambiguity of its feature vector. In such cases, the missingness indicators themselves provide additional information that, if properly modelled, may improve estimation efficiency. The SSLfmm package for R is designed to capture this behaviour by estimating the Bayes’ classifier under a finite mixture model in which each component corresponding to a class follows a multivariate normal distribution. It incorporates a mixed-missingness mechanism that combines a missing completely at random (MCAR) component with a (non-ignorable) missing at random (MAR) component, the latter modelling the probability of label missingness as a logistic function of the entropy based on the features. Parameters are estimated via an Expectation–Conditional Maximisation algorithm. In the two-class Gaussian setting with arbitrary covariance matrices, the resulting classifier trained on partially labelled data may, in some cases, achieve a lower misclassification rate than the supervised version in the case where all the labels are known. The package includes a practical tool for modelling and illustrates its performance through simulated examples.

Introduction

In classification problems, the construction of classifiers typically assumes access to fully labelled training data. In many real-world settings, however, complete annotation is difficult to obtain: expert labelling may be costly, time-consuming, or reliant on diagnostic procedures that cannot be applied in every instance. Consequently, datasets frequently contain feature vectors for which the corresponding class labels are missing. Such omissions may result from routine factors, limited time, incomplete documentation, or technical interruptions during data collection, but may also arise from more systematic reasons. In areas such as medicine or defence, for example, ambiguous or borderline cases are often left unlabelled when the available evidence does not support a confident decision, or when further diagnostic procedures are constrained by ethical or logistical considerations. As noted by
24
, the mechanisms that lead to missing labels can be subtle. In practice, they typically combine incidental omissions with deliberate, feature-dependent decisions, making the development of classifiers substantially more challenging.

Semi-supervised learning (SSL) provides a principled framework for combining labelled and unlabelled data, making it well suited to applications where complete annotation is difficult to obtain
(
23
;
25
;
7
)
. A wide range of SSL methodologies has been developed, including generative approaches
(
16
;
26
)
, graph-based algorithms
(
6
;
27
)
, and semi-supervised extensions of support vector machines
(
5
;
8
)
. Within this landscape, finite mixture models (FMMs) offer a probabilistic representation for partially labelled data, which can be fitted by the expectation–maximisation (EM) framework
(
11
;
20
;
13
)
; see
22
and
21
. FMM-based SSL methods are widely used
(
15
;
28
)
, but they typically adopt the assumption that unlabelled observations arise for reasons unrelated to the data-generating process. When the missingness depends on the observed features or latent class structure, this assumption is violated, potentially leading to biased inference. Recent work has reframed unlabelled observations explicitly as a missing-data problem, showing that accounting for the missing-label mechanism can yield gains in statistical efficiency. For example,
1
showed that partially labelled data can outperform completely labelled data in two-component normal models, with related extensions in
2
. These developments suggest that a careful treatment of the missing-label mechanism can broaden the scope and effectiveness of SSL methods.

A range of software tools now support SSL within both discriminative and generative modelling frameworks. On the discriminant side, packages such as RSSL
(
17
)
, S2Net
(
10
)
, LSX
(
30
)
, LFDA
(
29
)
, and SAMGEP
(
3
)
extend established classification and representation-learning methods by allowing unlabelled data to contribute through regularisation, graph-based constraints, or surrogate modelling. Methods such as SMMAL
(
14
)
apply similar ideas in causal inference, using unlabelled data to stabilise outcome and propensity estimation. Generative approaches, implemented in toolkits including mclust
(
12
)
, flexmix, mixtools
(
4
)
, Rmixmod
(
18
)
, and more recently batchmix
(
9
)
, make use of partially labelled data through likelihood-based or Bayesian mixture-model estimation, where unlabelled observations help refine component densities and classification boundaries. Although these packages differ in purpose and modelling strategy, most of them adopt the assumption that unlabelled observations arise for reasons unrelated to the features or underlying class structure. In applications where labels are withheld for systematic or case-specific reasons, this assumption is unlikely to hold. Hence, it would appear that existing software incorporates unlabelled data without addressing the mechanism by which labels become unavailable, leaving room for methods that explicitly account for the missing-label process within the SSL framework.

In this work, we introduce an open-source R package
SSLfmm
(semi-supervised learning under a mixed-missingness mechanism in finite mixture models), now available at
https://github.com/wujrtudou/SSLfmm.git
. The package implements a likelihood framework that jointly models class membership and the label-missingness process, explicitly accommodating both MCAR and entropy-based MAR mechanisms. Estimation is carried out via an Expectation–-Conditional Maximisation (ECM) algorithm, which alternates between computing soft label assignments and missingness indicators, updating logistic regression parameters through standard
glm

nlminb

SSLfmm
provides a unified and practical solution for semi-supervised mixture modelling in domains where a mixed-missingness mechanism is present.

6

FMM under a mixed-missingness mechanism

2.1
FMM

Let
\mathbf{Y}
be a
p
-dimensional random feature vector on an entity belonging to one of
g
predefined classes
C_{1},\dots,C_{g}
, which occur in proportions
\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{g}
satisfying
\sum_{i=1}^{g}\pi_{i}=1
. Conditional on membership to class
C_{i}
, the random vector
\mathbf{Y}
corresponding to
\mathbf{y}
has probability density function,

f_{i}(\mathbf{y};\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i})=\phi(\mathbf{y};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{i})

with mean
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i}
and covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{i}
. The class-specific parameter vector is

\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i}
containing the elements of
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i}
and the distinct elements of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{i}
, (
i=1,...,g
). The full parameter vector is
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{g-1},\boldsymbol{\omega}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}},\dots,\boldsymbol{\omega}_{g}^{\mathsf{T}})^{\mathsf{T}}
. We define the complete observation vector as

\mathbf{x}_{j}=(\mathbf{y}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}},\,\mathbf{z}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}})^{\mathsf{T}},

where the class-indicator vector

\mathbf{z}_{j}=(z_{1j},\dots,z_{gj})^{\mathsf{T}}
is given by

z_{ij}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }\mathbf{y}_{j}\in C_{i},\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\qquad i=1,\ldots,g;\quad j=1,\ldots,n.

In semi-supervised learning, some labels
\mathbf{z}_{j}
are missing.
Define

m_{j}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }\mathbf{z}_{j}\text{ is missing},\\
0,&\text{if }\mathbf{z}_{j}\text{ is observed}.\end{cases}\qquad j=1,\ldots,n.

For observations with missing labels, the contribution of the feature vector
\mathbf{y}_{j}
to the likelihood function is,

f(\mathbf{y}_{j};\boldsymbol{\theta})=\sum_{i=1}^{g}\pi_{i}\phi(\mathbf{y}_{j};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{i})\quad(i=1,...,n).

The posterior probability that an entity with feature vector
\mathbf{y}
belongs to the
i
th class is given by,

\tau_{i}(\mathbf{y};\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{\pi_{i}f_{i}(\mathbf{y};\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i})}{\sum_{h=1}^{g}\pi_{h}f_{h}(\mathbf{y};\boldsymbol{\omega}_{h})}\quad(i=1,...,g).

The Bayes’ classifier assigns an entity with feature vector
\mathbf{y}
to the class to which it has the highest posterior probability of belonging.

The log likelihood
\log L_{C}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
based on the labelled features is given by

\log L_{C}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(1-m_{j})\sum_{i=1}^{g}z_{ij}\log\{\pi_{i}f_{i}(\mathbf{y}_{j};\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i})\},

(a)

while the log likelihood
\log L_{UC}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
based on the unlabelled features is

\log L_{UC}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}m_{j}\log\!\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{g}\pi_{i}f_{i}(\mathbf{y}_{j};\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i})\right\}.

(b)

From (
a
) and (
b
), under the assumption of ignorable missingness, the log likelihood for a partially classified data set is

\log L_{PC}^{(\mathrm{ig})}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\log L_{C}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\log L_{UC}(\boldsymbol{\theta}).

(1)

If all the labels are observed (
m_{j}=0
for all
j
), then this reduces to

\log L_{CC}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{g}z_{ij}\log\{\pi_{i}f_{i}(\mathbf{y}_{j};\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i})\}.

2.2
A mixed-missingness mechanism for class labels

The missing mechanism can be ignored in likelihood-based inference if the missing labels satisfy the MCAR assumption, for which missingness does not depend on the feature vector or its class label. More generally, feature-truncated missingness, where labels are absent whenever a predictor falls below a threshold, belongs to the ignorable MAR case, and so can be ignored when forming the likelihood
(
19
)
. By contrast, in the entropy-based MAR setting considered here, the probability of missingness depends on the class entropy, which itself involves the distribution parameters. Because the parameter-distinctness condition is violated, this mechanism is non-ignorable and needs to be explicitly modelled. Accounting for it can also improve classification performance.
1
further noted that in practice, unlabelled samples (i.e., observations with missing class labels) often lie in ambiguous regions of the feature space, where class overlap is substantial. Such samples provide valuable information that can enhance the efficiency of parameter estimation for
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. The uncertainty in class membership for a feature vector
\boldsymbol{y}_{j}
in these regions can be quantified using Shannon entropy,

e(\boldsymbol{y}_{j};\boldsymbol{\theta})=-\sum_{i=1}^{g}\tau_{i}(\boldsymbol{y}_{j};\boldsymbol{\theta})\log\tau_{i}(\boldsymbol{y}_{j};\boldsymbol{\theta}),

where
\tau_{i}(\boldsymbol{y}_{j};\boldsymbol{\theta})
is the posterior probability that an entity with feature vector
\boldsymbol{y}_{j}
belongs to class
C_{i}
,
(i=1,...,g)
.

Building on this idea, we extend the framework of
1
to allow for a mixed-missingness mechanism that accommodates MCAR and entropy-based MAR. Specifically, we introduce binary indicators
m_{1j}
and
m_{2j}
. For observation
\boldsymbol{y}_{j}
, we set
m_{1j}=1
if its label is missing due to MCAR, and
m_{2j}=1
if the missingness arises from entropy-based MAR. When the label is observed, both indicators take the value zero, that is,
m_{1j}=m_{2j}=0
, implying
m_{j}=0
. The MCAR mechanism is modelled as

\text{Pr}\{M_{1j}=1\mid\boldsymbol{y}_{j},\mathbf{z}_{j}\}=\text{Pr}\{M_{1j}=1\mid\boldsymbol{y}_{j}\}=\alpha,

where
\alpha
is the probability that a label is MCAR. Next, for the MAR element, we model the probability of entropy-based missingness as

\text{Pr}\{M_{2j}=1\mid\boldsymbol{y}_{j},\mathbf{z}_{j}\}=\text{Pr}\{M_{2j}=1\mid\boldsymbol{y}_{j}\}=(1-\alpha)q(\boldsymbol{y}_{j};\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\xi}),

where
\boldsymbol{\xi}=(\xi_{0},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}^{T})^{T}
parameterises the entropy-based MAR mechanism and is distinct from the model parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. The function
q(\boldsymbol{y}_{j};\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\xi})
is defined as a logistic function of the log-entropy

q(\boldsymbol{y}_{j};\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\xi})=\frac{\exp\{\xi_{0}+\xi_{1}\log e(\boldsymbol{y}_{j};\boldsymbol{\theta})\}}{1+\exp\{\xi_{0}+\xi_{1}\log e(\boldsymbol{y}_{j};\boldsymbol{\theta})\}}.

In the special case of
g=2
normal components with equal covariance matrices, the log entropy can be replaced by the negative of the square of the discriminant function
(
1
)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\Psi}=(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{T},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{T})^{T}
. Define
\boldsymbol{m}_{1}=(m_{11},\ldots,m_{1n})^{T}
, and
\boldsymbol{m}_{2}=(m_{21},\ldots,m_{2n})^{T}
, and let
f
denote a generic density or probability function. To construct the full likelihood function
L_{\text{PC}}^{(\text{full})}(\boldsymbol{\Psi})
for the partially classified dataset, we require the following components

f(\boldsymbol{y}_{j},\mathbf{z}_{j},m_{j}=0),\quad f(\boldsymbol{y}_{j},m_{1j}=1),\quad f(\boldsymbol{y}_{j},m_{2j}=1),

which correspond to the contributions from classified, MCAR-unclassified, and MAR-unclassified observations, respectively.

Therefore, for a classified feature vector
\boldsymbol{y}_{j}
, the contribution is

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{y}_{j},\mathbf{z}_{j},M_{j}=0)

\displaystyle=f(\mathbf{z}_{j})f(\boldsymbol{y}_{j}\mid\mathbf{z}_{j})\text{pr}\{M_{j}=0\mid\boldsymbol{y}_{j},\mathbf{z}_{j}\}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{g}\left\{\pi_{i}f_{i}(\boldsymbol{y}_{j};\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i})\right\}^{z_{ij}}\left\{(1-\alpha)(1-q(\boldsymbol{y}_{j};\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\xi}))\right\}.

For an unclassified MCAR feature
\boldsymbol{y}_{j}

f(\boldsymbol{y}_{j},m_{1j}=1)=f(\boldsymbol{y}_{j})\text{pr}\{m_{1j}=1\mid\boldsymbol{y}_{j}\}=\sum_{i=1}^{g}\pi_{i}f_{i}(\boldsymbol{y}_{j};\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i})\alpha.

For an unclassified MAR feature
\boldsymbol{y}_{j}

f(\boldsymbol{y}_{j},m_{2j}=1)=f(\boldsymbol{y}_{j})\text{pr}\{m_{2j}=1\mid\boldsymbol{y}_{j}\}=\sum_{i=1}^{g}\pi_{i}f_{i}(\boldsymbol{y}_{j};\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i})(1-\alpha)q(\boldsymbol{y}_{j};\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\xi}).

Let
\mathcal{C}
denote the set of classified observations, that is, those with observed labels (i.e.,
m_{j}=0
). Let
\mathcal{U}_{\text{MCAR}}
represent the set of unclassified observations whose labels are missing due to an MCAR mechanism (i.e.,
m_{1j}=1
). Finally, let
\mathcal{U}_{\text{MAR}}
denote the set of unclassified observations with label missingness arising from an entropy-based MAR mechanism (i.e.,
m_{2j}=1
). The full likelihood function is

\displaystyle L_{\text{PC}}^{(\text{full})}(\boldsymbol{\Psi})=\prod_{j\in\mathcal{C}}f(\boldsymbol{y}_{j},z_{j},m_{j}=0)\quad\times\prod_{j\in\mathcal{U}_{\text{MCAR}}}f(\boldsymbol{y}_{j},m_{1j}=1)\quad\times\prod_{j\in\mathcal{U}_{\text{MAR}}}f(\boldsymbol{y}_{j},m_{2j}=1).

(2)

Taking the logarithm, the full log likelihood can thus be decomposed as:

\log L_{\text{PC}}^{(\text{full})}(\boldsymbol{\Psi})=\log L_{\text{PC}}^{(\text{ig})}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\log L_{\text{PC}}^{(\text{miss})}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}),

where
\log L_{\text{PC}}^{(\text{ig})}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is the log likelihood based on partially classified data, which is defined by (
1
) of Section
2.1
, and

\displaystyle\log L_{\text{PC}}^{(\text{miss})}(\boldsymbol{\Psi})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Big[

\displaystyle m_{1j}\log\alpha+(1-m_{1j})\log(1-\alpha)

(3)

\displaystyle+m_{2j}\log q(\boldsymbol{y}_{j};\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\xi})+(1-m_{2j})\log\left(1-q(\boldsymbol{y}_{j};\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\xi})\right)\Big].

3
Estimation via an ECM Algorithm

This section outlines the estimation of the parameters in the finite mixture model with a mixed-missingness mechanism. Two levels of incompleteness are present: (i) missing class labels
for a subset of observations, and (ii) latent indicators specifying whether each missing label
arises from MCAR or entropy-based MAR. The overall parameter vector is

\boldsymbol{\Psi}=(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\top},\alpha)^{\top}.

The ECM algorithm alternates between two expectation steps and two conditional maximisation steps.

E-step 1: Posterior probability of class membership

Given current estimates
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}
, the posterior probability that observation
j
belongs
to class
C_{i}
is

\tau_{ij}^{(k)}=\frac{\pi_{i}^{(k)}\,\phi(\mathbf{y}_{j};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{i}^{(k)})}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{g}\pi_{\ell}^{(k)}\,\phi(\mathbf{y}_{j};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\ell}^{(k)},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\ell}^{(k)})}.

The estimate of the entropy after the
(k+1)
th iteration of the ECM algorithm,

e_{j}^{(k+1)}=-\sum_{i=1}^{g}\tau_{ij}^{(k)}\log\tau_{ij}^{(k)},\qquad d_{j}^{(k)}=\log e_{j}^{(k)},

is then obtained for use in modelling entropy-based missingness.

E-step 2: Missingness indicator estimation

For labelled observations (
m_{j}=0
), the missingness indicators satisfy
m_{1j}=m_{2j}=0
.
For unlabelled observations (
m_{j}=1
), the probability that a missing label arises from MCAR is

m_{1j}^{(k)}=\frac{\alpha^{(k)}}{\alpha^{(k)}+(1-\alpha^{(k)})q_{j}^{(k)}},

and the probability that it arises from entropy-based missingness is

m_{2j}^{(k)}=\frac{(1-\alpha^{(k)})q_{j}^{(k)}}{\alpha^{(k)}+(1-\alpha^{(k)})q_{j}^{(k)}},

where

q_{j}^{(k)}=\mathrm{logit}^{-1}(\xi_{0}^{(k)}+\xi_{1}^{(k)}d_{j}^{(k)}).

These provide the soft assignment of each missing label to the MCAR or MAR mechanism.

CM-step 1: Update of
\alpha
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}

The updated MCAR proportion is updated by averaging the soft MCAR assignments to give

\alpha^{(k+1)}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}m_{1j}^{(k)}.

The entropy-based missingness parameters are updated by maximising the weighted logistic likelihood,

\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(k+1)}=\arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left[m_{2j}^{(k)}\log q_{j}(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(k)})+(1-m_{2j}^{(k)})\log(1-q_{j}(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(k)}))\right],

where
q_{j}(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(k)})=\mathrm{logit}^{-1}(\xi_{0}^{(k)}+\xi_{1}^{(k)}d_{j}^{(k)})
.
This corresponds to a weighted logistic regression of
m_{j}
on
d_{j}^{(k)}
.

CM-step 2: Update of the mixture model parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}

Given the current missingness parameters
(\alpha^{(k)},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(k)})
and the posterior class probabilities
\{\tau_{ij}^{(k)}\}
from E-step 1, the mixture model parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is updated by maximising the expected complete-data log likelihood with respect to
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. Because the MCAR component of the missingness likelihood does not depend on
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, it is constant in this step and is therefore omitted. The resulting
Q
-function for updating
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is

\displaystyle Q_{\theta}^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{g}\Big[(1-m_{j})\,z_{ij}+m_{j}\,\tau_{ij}^{(k)}\Big]\Big[\log\pi_{i}+\log\phi(\mathbf{y}_{j}\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{i})\Big]

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Big[m_{2j}^{(k)}\log q_{j}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(k)})+\big(1-m_{2j}^{(k)}\big)\log\!\big(1-q_{j}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(k)})\big)\Big].

Maximisation of
Q_{\theta}^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is computed numerically (e.g., via
nlminb
) under the specified covariance structure.

Remark

Initial mixture parameters are obtained from the labelled subset. Posterior probabilities and entropies are computed in E-step 1, followed by a weighted logistic fit for the missingness mechanism in CM-step 1. Upon convergence, the algorithm outputs
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(k+1)}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{(k+1)}
.

4
Main functions

The
SSLfmm
package provides a set of functions for simulating data, initialising parameters, performing semi-supervised ECM estimation, making predictions, and computing theoretical error rates under a mixed-missingness mechanism that combines MCAR and entropy-based MAR processes. This package is designed for the Gaussian mixture setting (
g=2
), where partial class labels and structured missingness must be jointly modelled to improve classification performance. The workflow begins with
simulate_mixed_missingness()
to generate synthetic datasets, proceeds to
EM_FMM_SemiSupervised_Complete_Initial()
for robust parameter initialisation, and then applies
EM_FMM_SemiSupervised()
for iterative estimation of both mixture and missingness parameters. The package also includes
bayesclassifier()
for Bayes-optimal classification and
error_beta_classification ()
for computing the theoretical Bayes’ error rate in equal-covariance cases, allowing for both simulation studies and methodological evaluation within a consistent framework.

4.1
Data simulation

The function
simulate_mixed_missingness()
generates artificial datasets from a two-component FMM under a mixed-missingness process that combines MCAR and MAR mechanisms. The model is specified with
g=2
mixture components having mixing proportions
\boldsymbol{\pi}=(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})
, a component mean matrix
\boldsymbol{\mu}
of dimension
p\times 2
, and a component covariance array
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
of dimension
p\times p\times 2
. Complete observations are first simulated using the
rmix()
function. For each simulated observation, entropy is calculated via
get_entropy()
, and a logistic regression model with intercept
\xi_{0}
and slope
\xi_{1}
is applied to the logarithm of this entropy to yield MAR probabilities. Observations are first subjected to MCAR missingness by randomly selecting an
\alpha
proportion of samples from the entire dataset and treating their labels as missing. Among the remaining complete observations, we then impose entropy-based MAR missingness by drawing Bernoulli indicators from the corresponding logistic probabilities.

The function accepts as inputs the sample size
n
, the mixing proportions
(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})
, a
p\times 2
matrix of component means, and a
p\times p\times 2
array of component covariance matrices. The entropy-based MAR mechanism is governed by the logistic parameters
\xi_{0}
(intercept) and
\xi_{1}
(slope), while the parameter
\alpha\in[0,1]
specifies the proportion of MCAR cases introduced across the full dataset. A random seed can also be supplied for reproducibility. The output is a
data.frame
containing the simulated feature vectors
y_{1},\dots,y_{p}
, the entropy values, a missingness indicator taking values 0 (observed), 1 (MCAR), and 2 (MAR), the observed label, and the true underlying class label.

4.2
Parameter initialisation

The function
EM_FMM_SemiSupervised_Complete_Initial()
provides a unified initialisation routine for semi-supervised Gaussian mixture modelling with a mixed-missingness mechanism (MCAR + MAR). It constructs stable, data-informed starting values for both the mixture parameters and the missingness mechanism by leveraging both labelled and unlabelled observations. The procedure operates in two stages: (i) mixture-parameter estimation from the labelled subset, yielding initial
\boldsymbol{\pi}
,
\boldsymbol{\mu}
, and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
under either a shared (equal) or class-specific (unequal) covariance structure as specified by
ncov
; and (ii) a short heuristic warm-up/calibration step that incorporates unlabelled data via soft responsibilities, evaluates entropy-based classification uncertainty, and calibrates the missingness parameters by updating the MCAR proportion
\alpha
and fitting a logistic model for the MAR component.

The function takes as input a data frame whose first
p
columns contain numeric feature variables, followed by two required columns:
missing
, indicating whether an observation is labelled (
0
) or unlabelled (nonzero), and
obs
, storing the observed class labels
1{:}g
for the labelled rows. The value of
p
is inferred as
ncol(data) -- 2
under this structure. Users may specify the number of mixture components
g
(default
2
) and the covariance structure via
ncov
, where
1
assumes a shared covariance matrix and
2
allows class-specific covariance matrices (default
1
). Additional tuning parameters include an initial MCAR proportion
alpha_init
in
(0,1)
for the warm-up calibration step (default
0.01
), the number of warm-up EM iterations
warm_up_iter
(default
20
), and a convergence tolerance
tol
(default
1e-6
). The function outputs a list containing the initialised mixture model parameters: the mixing proportions
\boldsymbol{\pi}
; the component mean vectors
\{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i}\}
; the covariance matrices, returned either as a single shared
p\times p
matrix or as class-specific matrices depending on
ncov
; the calibrated MCAR proportion
alpha
; and the logistic MAR parameters
\boldsymbol{\xi}=(\xi_{0},\xi_{1})
.

4.3
Estimation routine

The function
EM_FMM_SemiSupervised()
carries out the full semi-supervised ECM estimation for the FMM with the mixed-missingness mechanism (MCAR + entropy-based MAR) described in the preceding subsection. It takes stable, data-informed starting values from
EM_FMM_SemiSupervised_Complete_Initial()
and iteratively refines both the mixture parameters and the missingness mechanism parameters until convergence.

At each iteration, the algorithm begins with an E-step that computes component responsibilities
\tau_{ij}
for all observations, setting
\tau_{ij}=\mathbb{I}(z_{ij}=i)
for labelled cases. From these responsibilities, it calculates the entropy-based uncertainty measure
e_{j}
and its log-transform
d_{j}=\log(e_{j})
for each observation. Using the current missingness coefficients
\boldsymbol{\xi}=(\xi_{0},\xi_{1})
, the MAR link is evaluated as
q_{j}=\mathrm{logit}^{-1}(\xi_{0}+\xi_{1}d_{j})
. For unlabelled observations, the posterior channel weights for the MCAR and MAR components are then computed as
m_{1j}=\alpha/\{\alpha+(1-\alpha)q_{j}\}
and
m_{2j}=(1-\alpha)q_{j}/\{\alpha+(1-\alpha)q_{j}\}
, respectively, while both weights are set to zero for labelled cases. In the M-step for the missingness mechanism, the MCAR proportion
\alpha
is updated as the mean of
m_{1j}
across the whole dataset, and the MAR coefficients
\boldsymbol{\xi}
are updated by fitting a weighted logistic regression of
m_{2j}
on
d_{j}
, using weights of one for all cases. The subsequent M-step for the mixture parameters re-estimates
\boldsymbol{\theta}
by maximising the mixture log likelihood, treating
\tau_{ij}
for labelled cases as fixed and for unlabelled cases as the current responsibilities. This maximisation is performed numerically using
nlminb
applied to a packed parameter vector from
pack_theta()
, which contains
(g-1)
logits for
\boldsymbol{\pi}
(with the last component as baseline), all component means in column-major order, and the lower-triangular elements (including diagonal) of the covariance matrix or matrices. The covariance structure is determined by the
ncov
argument: when
ncov = 1
, a single
p\times p
shared covariance matrix is estimated and replicated internally; when
ncov = 2
,
g
distinct covariance matrices are estimated separately.

The function takes as input a data frame in which the first
p
columns contain the numeric feature variables, followed by the columns
missing
(coded
0
for labelled and nonzero for unlabelled observations) and
obs
(recording the observed class labels
1{:}g
for the labelled cases). Users may specify the number of mixture components
g
(default
2
), provide initial parameter values through the list
init_res
(containing
pi
,
mu
,
Sigma
,
alpha
, and
xi
), and control the ECM optimisation through the maximum number of iterations
max_iter
(default
5
) and the convergence tolerance
tol
(default
1e-6
). The covariance structure is governed by the flag
ncov
, where
1
specifies a shared covariance matrix and
2
allows component-specific covariances (default
1
). The function outputs a list containing the estimated mixing proportions, the component mean vectors, the covariance matrix or matrices (depending on
ncov
), the estimated MCAR proportion
alpha
, the MAR logistic coefficients
\boldsymbol{\xi}
, and the maximised total log likelihood.

4.4
Prediction

The function
bayesclassifier()
implements a Bayes’ classifier for a two-component Gaussian mixture model. Given
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
, the function assigns each observation to the component that maximises its posterior log-probability

\hat{z}_{ij}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }i=\displaystyle\arg\max_{h}\left\{\log\pi_{h}+\log\phi(\mathbf{y}_{j};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{h})\right\},\\[10.0pt]
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\qquad h=1,2.

The classifier supports both shared covariance matrices (
p\times p
) and component-specific covariance arrays (
p\times p\times 2
), and it can handle either a single observation vector or an
n\times p
data matrix.

The function accepts as input a numeric data object
dat
, provided either as an
n\times p
matrix or a length-
p
vector, together with the dimension
p
, the mixing proportions
\pi=(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})
satisfying
\pi_{1}+\pi_{2}=1
, a
p\times 2
matrix of component means, and either a shared
p\times p
covariance matrix or a
p\times p\times 2
array of component-specific covariance matrices. Optionally, a list
paralist
containing
pi
,
mu
, and
sigma
may be supplied, in which case these entries override the corresponding individual arguments. The function returns an integer vector of length
n
containing the predicted class labels in
\{1,2\}
.

4.5
Error rate computation

The function
error_beta_classification()
computes the theoretical Bayes classification error rate for a two-component GMM under the assumption of a common (equal) covariance structure across components. It requires the estimated mixture parameters: the component means
\boldsymbol{\mu}
, the shared covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
, and the mixing proportions
\boldsymbol{\pi}=(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})
. The function accepts
\boldsymbol{\mu}
either as a
p\times 2
numeric matrix, where each column is a component mean vector, or as a list of two numeric mean vectors, which is internally converted to matrix form.

The classification rule is derived from the linear discriminant function

z_{h}=1\quad\text{if }h=\arg\max_{h}\left\{\log\pi_{h}+\log\phi(\mathbf{y};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\right\},\quad\text{else }0,\quad(h=1,2)

where
\phi(\cdot;\boldsymbol{\mu}_{h},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
denotes the
p
-variate Normal density with the same covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
for both components. The discriminant coefficients are given by

\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}),\quad\beta_{0}=-\tfrac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2})+\log\left(\frac{\pi_{1}}{\pi_{2}}\right),

and the Bayes’ error rate is computed as

\mathrm{Err}=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\pi_{i}\,\Phi\left(\frac{(-1)^{i}(\beta_{0}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i})}{\lVert\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}\rVert}\right),

where
\Phi(\cdot)
is the standard normal cumulative distribution function and
\rVert\cdot\rVert
denotes the Euclidean norm.

The function takes as input the estimated component means, provided either as a
p\times 2
numeric matrix or as a list of two
p
-dimensional vectors; a shared
p\times p
covariance matrix
\Sigma_{\text{hat}}
, which must be identical for both mixture components; and the estimated mixing proportions
\pi_{\text{hat}}=(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})
satisfying
\pi_{1}+\pi_{2}=1
. Given these quantities, the function returns a single numeric value corresponding to the theoretical Bayes classification error rate.

Summary and discussion

This work introduces
SSLfmm
, an
R
package for semi-supervised finite mixture modelling under a mixed-missingness mechanism. Unlike conventional approaches that assume MCAR labels,
SSLfmm
jointly models both MCAR and entropy-based MAR mechanisms within a unified likelihood. Estimation is carried out via an ECM algorithm, complemented by robust initialisation routines for numerical stability. The package provides an end-to-end workflow that includes data simulation, parameter estimation, classification, and theoretical error rate computation. Simulation studies confirm that explicitly accounting for a mixed-missingness mechanism improves classification accuracy over benchmarks that are completely supervised. Several directions remain open for future development. The current implementation is limited to binary mixtures, with potential scalability challenges in high-dimensional or large-sample settings. Extensions to multiclass mixtures, integration of regularisation methods, and incorporation of alternative missingness mechanisms beyond entropy-based MAR would broaden applicability. Despite these limitations,
SSLfmm
delivers both methodological novelty and practical utility. It unifies MCAR and entropy-based MAR processes into a coherent semi-supervised framework and operationalises this advance in a reproducible software package, offering a principled tool for analysing partially labelled data in realistic settings where a mixed-missingness mechanism is prevalent.
