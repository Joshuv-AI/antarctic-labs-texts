Late-onset severe axonal polyneuropathy in mut0 methylmalonic acidemia after liver-kidney transplantation: a genotype-informed case report
Authors: Mariapia Griffo, Francesca Magri, Francesca Furlan, Elisabetta Bernardi, Francesca Menni, Francesco Tagliaferri, Sara Testa, Dario Ronchi, Sara Petrillo, Fiorella Piemonte, Delia Gagliardi, Mosè Parisi, Samuel Padelli, Daniele Velardo, Nicola Molitierno, Stefania Corti, Giacomo Pietro Comi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLiver-kidney transplantation in methylmalonic acidemia (MMA) improves metabolic control but does not eliminate neurological risk. Peripheral neuropathy is increasingly recognized in transplanted patients, yet its pathophysiology and surveillance strategies remain poorly defined.Case presentationWe describe a severe axonal sensorimotor polyneuropathy that occurred 12 years after combined liver and kidney transplantation in a 20-year-old patient with mut0 methylmalonic acidemia (MMA), compound heterozygous for MMUT gene mutations and lacking fibroblast MCM enzymatic activity.Clinical, electrophysiological, and biochemical investigations were performed, including cerebrospinal fluid analysis, anti-ganglioside antibody testing, and measurement of circulating biomarkers of neuroaxonal injury (NfL) and mitochondrial dysfunction (FGF21, GDF15).Electrophysiological studies demonstrated a purely axonal process with active denervation. Cerebrospinal fluid protein and cell count were normal, anti-ganglioside antibodies were negative, and neuroimaging was unremarkable, excluding Guillain-Barré syndrome variants. Plasma NfL was markedly elevated (4083 pg/mL, 204× ULN), exceeding levels reported in hereditary and acquired neuropathies. FGF21 (1682 pg/mL) and GDF15 (1438 pg/mL) indicated mitochondrial stress.ConclusionsThis case demonstrates that neurological stability is not guaranteed in mut0 MMA even 12 years post-transplantation. Management included switching from tacrolimus to everolimus and optimizing vitamin B12 supplementation.We propose NfL, FGF21, and GDF15 as monitoring tools for MMA transplant recipients. Keywords: Methylmalonic acidemia, Axonal neuropathy, MMUT gene, Mut0 genotype, Genotype-phenotype correlation, Liver-kidney transplant, Tacrolimus neurotoxicity, Neurofilament light chain, Calcineurin inhibitor neuropathy

Introduction

Methylmalonic acidemia (MMA) is an autosomal recessive disorder of methylmalonate metabolism due to deficiency of methylmalonyl-CoA mutase, encoded by the MMUT gene (6p12.3). This enzyme catalyzes the conversion of methylmalonyl-CoA to succinyl-CoA; its deficiency impairs oxidation of isoleucine, valine, methionine, threonine, odd-chain fatty acids, and cholesterol side chain, leading to the accumulation of methylmalonic acid, propionic acid, and propionylcarnitine [1, 2].

More than 200 different mutations of the MMUT gene have been identified. There are two forms of enzyme deficiency: mut0, where the enzyme has no activity, and mut-, where the enzyme has residual activity. There is a genotype-phenotype correlation, with mut0 patients presenting with earlier age of onset, severe phenotypes, poor outcome, compared with mut- patients who have milder phenotypes [3]. Most of the mut0 alleles contain truncating mutations, compared with missense mutations of the mut- alleles, which affect the C-terminal adenosylcobalamin-binding domain.

Clinical manifestations are caused by the accumulation of toxic metabolites and include feeding difficulties, developmental delay, and multiorgan failure [3, 4].

Chronic kidney disease occurs early in the course of the disease, typically before the age of 2 years, in patients with the mut0 subtype. The liver-kidney transplant has been proposed as a therapeutic option for patients with severe forms of this disease. Although this approach has been successful, it has not been able to correct serum metabolites completely. In addition, although plasma methylmalonic acid levels decrease considerably, it has been noted that cerebrospinal fluid levels remain increased, indicating that the central nervous system is still being challenged with toxic metabolites.

In addition to toxic metabolites accumulation, recent evidence shows that post-translational modifications such as methylmalonylation, propionylation, and lactylation could play a role in MMA pathogenesis with detrimental effects and toxic consequences [5].

Peripheral neuropathy is an uncommon but increasingly recognized complication of MMA, particularly in transplant patients. However, the pathogenesis of this complication is poorly understood. The factors that contribute to this neuropathy include direct effects of toxic metabolites, functional cobalamin deficiency, and drug-induced neurotoxicity [2, 6, 7]. It is of interest that patients with mut0 genotypes are at greatest risk of developing this complication. The neurological outcome in terms of genotypes is poorly understood.

We describe a 20-year-old male with mut0 MMA who developed subacute severe axonal polyneuropathy 12 years after combined liver-kidney transplantation, with emphasis on neurophysiological differential diagnosis and multifactorial pathogenesis.

Case report

A 20-year-old male was admitted with subacute lower limb weakness and ascending paresthesia evolving over 3–5 weeks. He had been previously diagnosed with MMA at 10 months of age following severe metabolic acidosis during a febrile illness. Compound heterozygosity in the MMUT gene was detected by molecular genetic analysis at disease onset, with a nonsense mutation c.1420 C > T p.(Arg474*) and a missense mutation c.1073T > C p.(Leu358Pro). Fibroblast assays showed severely reduced [1-¹⁴C]-propionate incorporation (7.7% of controls) and absent MCM enzymatic activity.

Renal function progressively deteriorated from 5 years of age; end-stage renal disease requiring peritoneal dialysis developed at 7 years old, followed by liver-kidney transplantation one year later. Post-transplant immunosuppressive therapy included mycophenolate mofetil, low-dose prednisone, and cyclosporin, switched to tacrolimus at the age of 17 years to reduce the cyclosporin-related adverse effects. Concomitant medications included rabeprazole, darbepoetin alfa, calcitriol, atenolol, sertraline, and diazepam; none of these are independently known to cause axonal neuropathy at therapeutic doses.

Metabolic control improved substantially post-transplantation: plasma methylmalonic acid decreased from 1557 µmol/L to 143 µmol/L 5 years post-transplantation, although not within the normal range. He subsequently developed chronic kidney disease in the transplanted kidney.

The patient had a history of chronic fatigue and weakness of the distal upper limbs, with impaired fine motor skills. Ambulation had been independent, with assistive devices only during periods of increased physical exertion. This history of symptoms is suggestive of a subclinical neuropathy, which had a chronic course, and then an acute episode of neuropathy manifested on top of it, an acute-on-chronic neuropathy.

The patient had maintained a protein-restricted diet (25–28 gprotein/day) with stable metabolic parameters since transplantation. However, university-related stress had recently led todietary non-compliance with skipped meals and reduced caloric intake, a common phenomenon in the transition of patients with inherited metabolic disorders from paediatric to adult care, which is now increasingly recognised. In MMA patients, caloric restriction is known to increase endogenous protein breakdown, thereby increasing the burden of propionogenic substrates and potentially causing metabolic decompensation even in transplanted patients with previously stable metabolic parameters.

Clinical findings

The patient had flaccid quadriparesis, predominantly at the lower limbs, with stocking-distribution paresthesias, (Table 1) and areflexia. Cognition, cranial nerves, and respiratory function were preserved. Weakness had progressed to inability to walk. Serum creatine kinase was normal, making a myopathic cause unlikely.

Electrodiagnostic and imaging findings

The patient presented with a clinical picture of severe axonal sensorimotor polyneuropathy confirmed on electrodiagnostic testing (Table 2). Results showed normal conduction velocities with severely reduced CMAP amplitudes, absent conduction blocks, normal F-wave latencies, and active denervation potential. These findings excluded AIDP. Cerebrospinal fluid showed normal protein and cell count without albuminocytologic dissociation. Anti-ganglioside antibodies (anti-GM1, anti-GD1a, anti-GD1b, anti-GQ1b) were negative, excluding axonal GBS variants (AMAN/AMSAN).

Neurofilament light chain (NfL), a biomarker of axonal damage measured on Ella device (ProteinSimple, CA, USA) using a Human Simple Plex assay kit (ProteinSimple, CA, USA), as previously described in pediatric neurological cohorts [8], was dramatically elevated. Plasma NfL was 4083 pg/mL, 204-fold above the upper limit of normal (ULN), assuming an age-adjusted ULN of approximately 20 pg/mL. CSF NfL was 5983 pg/mL, 12-fold above ULN, based on a CSF cut-off of approximately 500 pg/mL. These values quantitatively establish severe ongoing neuroaxonal damage. To contextualize, plasma NfL levels in hereditary neuropathies (CMT) have a reported median of approximately 26 pg/mL [9], in chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (CIDP) approximately 30 pg/mL [10], while in Guillain-Barré syndrome the median at admission is approximately 55 pg/mL for the overall cohort and approximately 190 pg/mL for the AMAN subtype, with values never reported to exceed 1000 pg/mL even in the most severe cases with inexcitable nerves at presentation [11, 12]. The 4083 pg/mL observed in our patient vastly exceeds values reported in these conditions and is likely a cumulative effect of several simultaneous neurotoxic insults superimposed on chronic subclinical neuroaxonal injury. FGF21 was elevated at 1682 pg/mL (ULN < 200 pg/mL), much higher than the 1500 pg/mL threshold associated with complications in organic acidemias (positive predictive value 0.83) [13]. GDF15 was elevated at 1438 pg/mL (reference 200–1000 pg/mL). Tacrolimus trough level was 4.1 µg/L. Plasma NfL levels can be elevated 2–3-fold in CKD stages 3–4 due to reduced renal clearance [14]. Even accounting for this, the residual NfL elevation (approximately 68-fold above ULN after CKD adjustment) far exceeds impaired renal clearance alone. CSF NfL, independent of renal clearance, provides additional confirmation of genuine neuroaxonal injury. The relative increase of NfL above the upper limit of normal was significantly higher in the blood than in the cerebrospinal fluid, suggesting a peripheral source of neurofilament release. Similarly, GDF15 may be elevated in CKD, and its interpretation requires caution.

Comparison with other metabolic and toxic neuropathies

Differential diagnosis includes a number of potential causes, which may be present either singly or in combination and may act synergistically.

Chronic idiopathic axonal polyneuropathy (CIAP): the diagnosis of CIAP, a sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy of unknown aetiology defined by exclusion, was also considered. Although a normal CSF protein value does not per se rule out CIAP (typically associated with normal or mildly elevated CSF protein), several elements argue against this diagnosis: the clinical course was subacute rather than insidious and slowly progressive over years; the patient presents a clearly identifiable metabolic and pharmacological context (mut0 MMA with persistently elevated methylmalonic acid, CKD stage 3b, prolonged calcineurin inhibitor exposure, dietary non-compliance with increased endogenous protein catabolism), each independently capable of causing axonal injury; plasma and CSF NfL levels markedly exceeded age-adjusted reference values [15, 16] and those reported in slowly progressive axonal polyneuropathies of hereditary [9] or metabolic origin [17], pointing to acute/subacute severe neuroaxonal damage rather than chronic indolent axonopathy, although dedicated NfL data in CIAP are lacking [18]; finally, FGF21 and GDF15 elevations indicate an active mitochondrial-stress component not typical of CIAP. The neuropathy was therefore not classified as idiopathic.

Cobalamin C Disease Neuropathy: In late-onset CblC, there may be an acute or subacute progressive neuropathy, and there may be associated neurological symptoms [19–22]. The possibility of cobalamin C neuropathy was ruled out due to normal levels of homocysteine (8.2 µmol/L), which confirms isolated MCM deficiency. The patient has neuropathy of different aetiology, given the different enzymatic defect (MCM vs. methionine synthase/MMACHC). In CblC disease, the MMACHC defect impairs intracellular processing of cobalamin and affects both cobalamin-dependent enzymes (methionine synthase and methylmalonyl-CoA mutase), producing the combined biochemical phenotype of elevated homocysteine and elevated methylmalonic acid. In our patient, the isolated MCM defect selectively impairs adenosylcobalamin utilisation at the mitochondrial enzyme step, while methionine synthase activity and the methylcobalamin-dependent remethylation pathway are preserved, as reflected by the normal homocysteine value. The term “functional cobalamin deficiency” is therefore used here in a restricted sense, referring to non-utilisation of adenosylcobalamin as an enzymatic cofactor by the defective MCM, and not to a systemic vitamin B12 deficiency state. Intramuscular cyanocobalamin (1000 µg weekly) was administered as standard supportive therapy in MMA to maximise any residual cofactor responsiveness and to ensure adequate substrate for methionine synthase, and not as a treatment targeting CblC neuropathy. With regard to the electrodiagnostic pattern, classical vitamin B12 deficiency is typically associated with a myelopathy and with predominantly demyelinating features on nerve conduction studies; the purely axonal pattern observed in our patient is therefore not consistent with a primary B12-deficiency neuropathy and supports a multifactorial axonal process driven by toxic-metabolic and drug-related mechanisms rather than by demyelination.

Uremic Neuropathy: The patient has CKD Stage 3b with markedly increased cystatin C levels. Uremic neuropathy is a chronic slowly progressive neuropathy that improves with a transplant, but there has been a worsening of renal function in the transplant kidney. This can increase the level of neurotoxic metabolites because of the reduced clearance.

Calcineurin inhibitor neuropathy: Tacrolimus neurotoxicity is a significant condition that needs to be excluded. Neuropathy has been described with calcineurin inhibitor therapy even at therapeutic blood levels. Calcineurin inhibitor-induced neuropathy appears to be more common in patients with MMA than in other transplant recipients [2, 6]. Patients with MMA are particularly susceptible to tacrolimus-induced neurotoxicity because the mitochondrial dysfunction in these patients acts as a “second hit” that reduces the patient’s neurotoxicity threshold. The patient has been on calcineurin inhibitors for a total of 12 years (cyclosporin for 9 years and tacrolimus for 3 years) with low trough levels below the standard therapeutic range of 5–15 µg/L (4.1 µg/L). It is not possible to exclude the effect of cumulative toxicity, as calcineurin inhibitor neurotoxicity is not strictly dose-dependent and can occur via mechanisms that are independent of trough levels. Given this background, calcineurin inhibitor therapy was withdrawn and replaced with everolimus; the favourable clinical course subsequently observed is consistent with, although not specifically demonstrating, a contribution of CNI-related neurotoxicity to the neuropathy.

Direct MMA neurotoxicity: It has been established that methylmalonic acid indeed has neurotoxic effects and can inhibit the mitochondrial respiratory chain enzymes. Even after the transplant, the level of methylmalonic acid was found to be significantly elevated at 349 µmol/L. This means the patient has been under the effects of a known neurotoxic metabolite. Additionally, the block at the MCM step creates a state of functional cobalamin deficiency. Although the level of cobalamin was found to be elevated in the serum due to supplementation, the intracellular level of adenosylcobalamin as an enzymatic cofactor would be affected due to the complete absence of functional MCM activity, creating a state of functional intramitochondrial cobalamin deficiency at the MCM step.

Mitochondrial dysfunction: FGF21 and GDF15, biomarkers of mitochondrial dysfunction, were markedly elevated [23–26]. FGF21 was 1682 pg/mL (normal < 200 pg/mL). GDF15 was 1438 pg/mL (normal 200–1000 pg/mL); interpretation requires caution given concurrent CKD stage 3b [25, 27]. CSF concentrations of FGF21 (12 pg/mL) and GDF15 (219 pg/mL) were within the normal range, indicating a steep gradient between plasma and CSF levels and suggesting a peripheral origin of mitochondrial stress. These findings indicate mitochondrial dysfunction, contributing to neuropathic injury [13, 28]. The “second hit” theory, whereby mitochondrial dysfunction can lower the threshold for neurotoxic effects from other causes, could have a role in this multifactorial scenario.

Plasma FGF21 and GDF15 were found increased in mut- and mut0 patients compared to age-matched controls. This trend reached a statistical significance for mut0 but not for mut- patients, suggesting a positive correlation between the reduction of MCM activity and mitochondrial dysfunction in patients [29]. Liver transplantation is associated with a massive build-up of serum FGF21 and FGF19 levels 24 h after surgery. These values tend to reduce in the following two weeks in patients undergoing liver transplantation due to hepatocellular carcinoma, cirrhosis caused by hepatitis B or C and primary biliary cholangitis. Conversely, transplanted patients with alcoholic liver disease or autoimmune hepatitis exhibit stably increased FGF21 two weeks after liver transplantation [30]. In mut0 patients undergoing liver or combined liver-kidney transplantation (but not isolated kidney transplantations), the correction of FGF21 and GDF15 levels towards normal range soon after liver surgery has been reported [29, 31]. The long-term follow up of a cohort of mut0 transplanted patients documented, after an apparent period of FGF21 stability, a significant increase of FGF21 levels starting from 10 years after liver transplantation. This finding, interpreted by some authors as the clue of a “hormetic” response to chronic mitochondrial stress [32], can be on the other hand considered the biomarker of progressive mitochondrial damage contributing to multisystem clinical symptoms even in patients with optimal response to liver transplantation. Long-term neurological follow up and serial sampling for serum biomarkers in mut0 patients surviving liver transplantation will help to clarify the correlation between increased serum FGF21/GDF15 levels and clinical onset of neurological symptoms. In a recent study, intellectual disability and neurological involvement were observed in > 40% of patients with > 10 years of follow-up [33].

Therapeutic management

Therapeutic management for each of these aetiological factors was also provided (Table 4). The patient was taken off tacrolimus and commenced on everolimus, considering expert opinion and the potential for neurotoxicity with CNIs in patients with MMA and emerging evidence for the excellent neurological safety profile of mTOR inhibitors [2]. Vitamins were also provided. Vitamin B complex, cyanocobalamin given intramuscularly once a week at a dose of 1000 µg, ascorbic acid, folic acid, and ubidecarenone were also given.

Carglumic acid (N-carbamylglutamate, 600 mg three times a day) was used as supportive therapy to prevent intercurrent episodes of hyperammonemia during this period of metabolic instability; ammonia levels were within the normal range at presentation. This synthetic analogue of N-acetylglutamate activates carbamoyl phosphate synthetase 1, optimizing nitrogen metabolism in organic acidurias [20, 34]. Carglumic acid is currently approved for use in N-acetylglutamate synthase deficiency and organic acidurias and has also been used for urea cycle disorders and for treating hyperammonemia. Although combined liver-kidney transplantation substantially reduces the risk of hyperammonemic crises, it does not fully abolish the underlying enzymatic defect in extrahepatic tissues, and transplanted MMA patients can still show secondary disturbances of nitrogen handling under conditions of metabolic stress, such as the dietary non-compliance and increased endogenous protein catabolism observed in our patient. Given this clinical picture, carglumic acid was prescribed prophylactically to support ureagenesis and limit any rise in ammonia during the acute illness, consistent with its approved indication in organic acidurias.

The patient also underwent an intensive inpatient rehabilitation program. During the stay in the hospital, the patient showed improvement in upper extremity strength, gradually regaining antigravity strength in the wrist extensors and hand grip. The patient also showed the ability to have trace voluntary movement in the toes. However, the weakness of the lower extremities was severe, as shown in Table 1. The patient was discharged and sent to a rehabilitation facility.

At three-month follow-up, neurophysiological testing revealed mild improvement, while clinical improvement was even more pronounced (Tables 5 and 6). At six-month follow-up, further clinical improvement was observed, and electrodiagnostic studies showed essentially unchanged neurophysiological findings compared with the 3-month assessment (Tables 5 and 6). Serial plasma levels of NfL and FGF21, along with neurophysiological studies, are being carried out for this patient at 3 months and 6 months after initiation of treatment. At the time of submission, only clinical and electrophysiological follow-up had been completed; plasma NfL, FGF21 and GDF15 measurements at six months are ongoing; the corresponding values are not yet available, and will be reported in a subsequent dedicated longitudinal analysis.

Discussion

This case report has highlighted several points concerning neurological complications of MMA.

This case confirms that prolonged post-transplant stability does not preclude severe peripheral neuropathy. The mut0 variant carries the highest complication risk [2, 35]. This genotype-phenotype correlation underscores that mut0 patients require closer neurological monitoring than mut- patients, even after successful transplantation. Despite metabolic improvement post-transplantation, plasma methylmalonic acid levels do not normalize, and CSF metabolites remain elevated [36]. The pre-existing symptoms of upper extremity weakness and reduced dexterity suggest subclinical neuropathy predating acute decompensation. While this cannot be confirmed without pre-existing neurophysiological data, the possible pre-existing damage to the peripheral nervous system from years of metabolic vulnerability supports the rationale for neurophysiologic studies on transplanted mut0 patients, even without neurologic complaints. This case reinforces the recommendation for regular neurophysiological monitoring of transplanted mut0 patients even during clinical stability.

Notably, our patient harbors a missense mutation in the N-terminal substrate-binding domain rather than the C-terminal adenosylcobalamin-binding domain typically affected in mut- alleles. The p.Leu358Pro mutation affects a highly conserved residue, and based on the structural location alone, this mutation would have been predicted to result in a mut- rather than mut0 phenotype. In such cases, enzymatic assays were therefore critical to correctly classify the enzymatic subtype. Indeed, the compound heterozygous status of the p.Leu358Pro variant with the p.Ala191Glu allele was detected in a mut0 patient with undetectable residual enzymatic activity [37] suggesting a major detrimental effect of this missense variant on protein stability and/or activity. In support of this, enzymatic assay in our patient confirmed complete absence of MCM activity, consistent with the mut0 subtype.

Secondly, this case serves to highlight the value of electrodiagnosis in differentiating between treatable and non-treatable causes of weakness. As mentioned above, investigations had already pointed towards axonal neuropathy through neurophysiologic investigations and thus AIDP was ruled out because this condition is immunomodulatory therapy-responsive. In addition, the lack of albuminocytological dissociation and negative anti-ganglioside antibody tests ruled out axonal variants of GBS like AMAN and AMSAN because these conditions also manifest with axonal neuropathy on neurophysiologic tests but have different treatment modalities.

Thirdly, this particular case serves to highlight the intricacy of attributing neurological manifestations to a specific aetiology in a patient with multiple aetiologies that may be acting synergistically. The patient had multiple aetiologies for metabolic toxicity with elevated MMA and functional vitamin B12 deficiency, chronic kidney disease with uremic toxicity, calcineurin inhibitor toxicity with a long duration of treatment, and a change in diet regimen that could have resulted in a rapid change in energy metabolism [38]. The rationale for treating all aetiologies simultaneously instead of sequentially was based on the severity of aetiologies and the difficulty in quantifying the relative contribution of each aetiology.

The switch from tacrolimus to everolimus deserves special mention. CNI neurotoxicity in the context of MMA is underappreciated. The development of neurological symptoms despite subtherapeutic tacrolimus levels underscores that trough concentrations alone are insufficient for assessing cumulative neurotoxicity. Expert opinion now favors mTOR inhibitors when neurological symptoms develop in MMA patients. It should be emphasised, however, that the clinical and electrophysiological improvement observed at three months cannot be attributed specifically to the calcineurin inhibitor switch. The patient simultaneously received parenteral vitamin B12, levocarnitine, ubidecarenone, ascorbic acid and folic acid, dietary optimisation, and an intensive rehabilitation programme, and the available follow-up is too short to disentangle the independent contribution of each of these concurrent interventions. The improvement should therefore be interpreted as the combined effect of multimodal management, rather than as evidence of a specific therapeutic effect of mTOR inhibitor substitution. The marked NfL elevation (204-fold in plasma and 12-fold in CSF) quantitatively reflects severe axonal damage, exceeding levels reported in other neuropathies [9]. NfL is emerging as an important tool for measuring disease activity in organic acidemias [29, 32]. Longitudinal NfL monitoring may also have utility as a treatment response biomarker for organic acidemias with neurological manifestations. It remains to be seen how NfL responds to treatment. Plasma NfL can be elevated 2–3-fold in CKD stages 3–4, but even with this adjustment, levels remain approximately 68-fold above ULN [29, 39]. CSF NfL, unaffected by renal clearance, confirms genuine neuroaxonal damage. The elevated FGF21 (1682 pg/mL) in a post-transplant patient aligns with the persistent hormetic response described by Manoli et al. [32]. This level exceeds the 1500 pg/mL threshold associated with long-term complications in organic acidemias. FGF21 reflects not only hepatic but also muscular mitochondrial dysfunction in this clinical context. FGF21 is particularly useful in MMA patients where creatinine is unreliable [29, 32].

Limitations

There are several limitations that should be noted. The most important methodological limitation of this report is the absence of baseline neurophysiological data acquired before the onset of symptoms. Such information could have been obtained by regular surveillance and could have allowed subclinical abnormalities to have been detected at an earlier time. In the absence of pre-symptomatic nerve conduction studies, the chronicity of the underlying neuropathic process and the precise temporal relationship between metabolic, drug-related and nutritional triggers and the onset of axonal injury can only be inferred from the clinical history and from the markedly elevated NfL values, and not directly demonstrated. The follow-up period of six months is also short and does not allow full conclusions about the durability of the observed improvement. The influence of CKD stage 3b on plasma NfL levels cannot be accurately assessed in an individual patient. Although the rise is clearly disproportionate to impaired clearance alone, and CSF NfL levels confirm neuroaxonal damage, in the future it would be ideal to use NfL reference ranges based on eGFR. A nerve biopsy was not performed, although in the absence of any ambiguity in the results obtained by electrophysiological studies, it is unlikely that it would have changed the management plan. The relative contribution of each of the factors cannot be determined with certainty, and the response of the patient to the withdrawal of tacrolimus has not been established in the long term. The role of carglumic acid in this scenario has also not been validated. The inability to determine the relative contribution of each of the factors is an inherent limitation of a multifactorial disease process. A comparison with mut– patients is also not possible and is beyond the scope of a single case report.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the 12-year latency between organ transplantation and neurological decompensation demonstrates that long-lasting stability does not equal safety in patients with mut0 MMA. In transplanted patients with MMA, the development of new neurological symptoms must prompt an investigation that should include electrophysiological assessment of neuropathy type, cerebrospinal fluid analysis, anti-ganglioside antibodies to rule out immune mechanisms, and measurement of circulating biomarkers. Our case supports the need for genotype-dependent neurological surveillance of transplanted patients with MMA, including regular nerve conduction studies, neuro-tolerant immunosuppressive regimens favouring mTOR over calcineurin inhibitor-based therapy, and regular monitoring of NfL for neuroaxonal damage and FGF21/GDF15 for mitochondrial damage. We recommend that a normal neurological examination following organ transplantation in a patient with mut0 MMA is by no means reassuring: in the present case, subclinical neuroaxonal damage was present, which was expressed abruptly after cumulative stressors reached a tipping point (Figs. 1 and 2).
