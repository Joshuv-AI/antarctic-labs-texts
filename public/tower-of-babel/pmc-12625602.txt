Understanding the heterogeneity in healthcare expenditure in India
Authors: Srikanth Reddy Umenthala, Udaya Shankar Mishra, KS James
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHealth expenditure is indicative of the financial burden of health care and serves as a yardstick of health system performance. However, health expenditure may be shaped by multiple factors such as prevalence of morbidity, income inequality and above all, unobserved heterogeneity such as disease severity. This study uses finite mixture models (FMM) to analyze health expenditure distribution based on a National Sample Survey (NSS) which is a nationally representative dataset. This exercise identifies three different class of health care users, acknowledging the heterogeneity within the expenditure distribution. The classes demonstrate variations in spending behavior and associated characteristics. It is observed that health spending is influenced by disease severity, age, gender, education, social group, and economic status. Notably, health expenditure for similar diseases varies significantly across three classes, with the highest expenditure observed in the third latent class. It also reaffirms the gender disparities in health spending irrespective of the class. Additionally, socio-economic status consistently affects health expenditure across classes. These findings underscore the importance of recognizing unobserved heterogeneity in health expenditure for the design of effective healthcare policies. In conclusion, there is a need to recognize the unobserved heterogeneity in health expenditure data and such a recognition that distinct classes within may have greater significance in designing better health care policies. Beyond health expenditure, this analytical framework can be adopted to other medical and public health research to identify the latent classes, thus offering a broader methodological value.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12874-025-02695-y.

Background

Reported health care expenditure distribution indicates the nature and extent of variation in the cost of health care across different groups or individuals. Recognition of this inherent spread in reported health expenditure is vital to understand its implied financial burden on different income groups and evaluate the performance of health systems. Several factors influence the distribution of health expenditure, including income inequality, the prevalence of morbidity, and the health care market as a whole . A broad literature observes the distribution of health expenditure to be often regressive, with the burden falling disproportionately on low-income households. In addition to this, there are varied systems of health service provision that raise policy concerns regarding financing given that policies suitable for one category may not necessarily be ideal for another [1]. Understanding the drivers of rising health care expenditure is essential for forecasting future healthcare requirements and improving efficiency in healthcare spending [2]. Overall, the distribution of reported health care expenditure is complex and requires careful consideration of all its underlying attributes.

The distribution of reported healthcare expenditure typically exhibits two distinct features. Firstly, these distributions are commonly positively skewed depicting empirical density with long, thin right tails. Secondly, these distributions commonly contain a substantial point mass at zero [3]. Given this common feature of the distribution, it does not qualify for the use of linear regression in its analysis . A number of statistical remedies have been developed to address this concern. Prominent among them is the two-part model which has been widely adopted to study health care expenditure by decomposing the distribution into two parts, one part that generates zeros and second having nonzero positive spending. Modelling the positive expenditure (second part) is critically important, as different models may yield different results [3]. The two-part model outperforms single-equation models when distinguishing between users and non-users, however, struggles with extreme distributions [4]. Thus, generalized linear models were proposed as alternative models to accommodate the skewness of the data in a more natural way and to fit the expenditure distribution well [5].

There are a number of reasons to believe that the marginal effect of various covariates on total spending at various levels of expenditure is not constant. For example, a unit change in health status may have a smaller marginal effect on health expenditure for a person with good health status. However, the same may not be true for a person in poor health. Similarly, the effect of a unit change across multiple socioeconomic factors, as well as unit change in health, may not have a consistent bearing on the distribution of health expenditures. From a statistical standpoint, when a linear regression is fitted, it assumes that the covariates are constant across all values of the population [6]. When a researcher tries to explore the heterogeneity of covariates in relation to the dependent variable by specifying quadratic terms and different interactions, there is a risk of false findings [6]. There are strong reasons to believe that effects are heterogeneous along different dimensions of the outcome variable, which cannot easily be characterized by parametric functional forms or by interactions of covariates as they are typically specified. Ignoring such heterogeneity may also lead to false conclusions [6]. In this analysis we account for the inherent heterogeneity in the outcome of the distribution that is health expenditure and length of stay.

Estimates of health care demand have often been shown to depend on empirical evidence for better policy measures and effective implementation. Measuring such health care needs in association with financial dependency requires clear conceptual specification and statistical assumptions. If such specifications overlook the underlying behavior, then estimates may be misleading [4]. This paper emphasizes the distinction between different classes of health care users by adopting an innovative specification: the Finite Mixture Models (FMM). The FMM specification offers numerous advantages over the commonly adopted standard specifications, by providing a more accurate measure of average expenditure by different subgroups [4]. For low-and middle-income countries (LMICs), these precise and nuanced estimates are crucial for allocating limited resources efficiently, ensuring equitable access to healthcare, and formulating policies that address the specific needs of diverse population segments.

This paper uses nationally representative data from India and applies FMM methods to measure the healthcare demand in an economic framework. In this paper, we analyze the relative importance of disease severity in understanding the direct medical expenses and quantify the indirect losses of income to individuals due to disease in India. We also aim to differentiate between latent classes present within the entire spectrum of healthcare users. Ultimately our results demonstrate the significance of disease severity in shaping the expenditure outcomes, yet this factor is not prominent within the UHC agenda, particularly with reference to indirect losses of wage among the poorer households in India.

Indian context

With India’s rapid demographic and epidemiological transition, understanding the rise in healthcare expenditure and its characteristics is significant for better policy designs. Concerns over escalating healthcare expenditure are heightened by the high out-of-pocket (OOP) spending and underdeveloped health insurance markets [7]. The economic burden of health spending in developing countries, including India, has been well documented [8–12]. However, only fewstudies have considered the distribution of healthcare expenditure.

Studies have documented the financial consequences of healthcare expenditure in India. According to Pandey et al. (2012), the proportion of households experiencing catastrophic healthcare expenditure increased 1.47 times between 1993 and 1994 and 2011–2012 [13]. During thisperiod, annual per capita consumption expenditure increased by 36%, whereas annual per capita healthcare expenditure increased by 96%, underscoring the disproportionate rise in health-related costs. This increase is strongly linked to income loss due to illness and additional medical expenses. The adverse impact of rising healthcare costs, including impoverishment and associated coping strategies, have been widely examined in the Indian context [8–12]. For instance, to cope with such high expenditures, 60% of rural and 40% of urban households resort to borrowing, selling assets, and seeking contributions from friends and relatives when faced with hospitalization [11].

Healthcare expenditure systematically correlates with socioeconomic status, healthcare facility choice, and disease type. Evidence suggests that in India, a single hospital stay for cancer or heart disease in a private facility costs 80–90% of annual per capita income, compared to 40–50% for treatment in public hospitals [8]. Other studies link variations in healthcare spending to disease category [12], patient age [13], and region [10]. However, previous research has not accounted for differences in disease severity or the intensity of healthcare-seeking behaviour.

While studies on catastrophic health expenditure and impoverishment offer insights into the long-term effects on different groups, this study aims to identify the heterogeneity in healthcare expenditure and to categorize different sub-groups of healthcare users.

The variation in healthcare spending has mostly been attributed to disease-specific [12], age-specific [13], and regional factors [10]. Empirical studies suggest that except in case of severe illness, household members generally recover from the financial impact of healthcare costs [14]. Thus, addressing the heterogeneity in healthcare expenditure is essential for effective policy formulation, especially in a context like India where out-of-pocket (OOP) spending is predominant.A key consequence of this rise in OOP spending relates to income loss of the diseased along with the additional burden in terms of medical expenditure [15]. The disproportionate or catastrophic nature of healthcare expenditure, its role in induced impoverishment, and coping strategies have been widely examined in the Indian context.

Data sources and method

Data sources

The present study utilized data on hospitalization and morbidity from the “Social Consumption in India: Health” survey. This survey was conducted with the aim of generating insights into India’s healthcare sector. Employing a stratified multi-stage sampling technique, the 75th round of this survey was carried out from July 2017 to June 2018, encompassing the entirety of the Indian Union. The data was collected from a substantial 555,114 households spanning 8,077 randomly selected villages and 6,181 randomly selected urban blocks. We considered individuals who reported being hospitalised in the last one year, resulting in a sample of 63,785 individuals. The survey data included self-reported causes of morbidity and hospitalization over the past 365 days, along with details such as the duration of hospitalization, length of illness (both before and after hospitalization), and medical expenditure. However, for individuals who did not seek treatment or were not hospitalized, no information on cause of death was available in the survey. Additionally, the survey encompassed post-natal care details and information pertaining to childbirth. However, for the purposes of this study, expenditure and information related to childbirth were excluded from consideration. The main outcome variable for the study is the reported health expenditure for inpatients due to any ailment during last one year.

The healthcare expenditure variable is constructed by summing components such as doctors’ or surgeons’ fees, medicines, diagnostic tests, bed charges, other medical expenses, transport costs, and additional non-medical expenses incurred by the household.

Method

Various remedial approaches have been adopted to address the skewed feature of this particular variable and the most common among them is the adoption of non-linear models. Health expenditure is a count variable. The following section discusses the characteristics of health expenditure distribution, and the models used to study it.

The model assumes that class probabilities are constant across the outcome distribution, we estimates the posterior probabilities of each classes after the model parameters has been fitted. Further, we can interpret these probabilities to classify the observations in each class. In practice the number of classes are unknown priori. To determine the number of classes we use Akaike information criterion (AIC) and Bayesian information criterion (BIC) as tools for models’ selection. Additional models are fit until model selection criteria (AIC and BIC) show no further improvements. Results

A simple representation of reported health expenditure in India (See Fig. 1) highlights its right-skewed nature, where the mean is greater than the median value. The severe right skew of this distribution makes it ineligible for standard descriptive analysis and the application of ordinary linear regression. Any analysis that overlooks this characteristic may yield biased results. To address this limitation, this paper applies a finite mixture model to study the underlying features of the distribution.

Discussion

Evidence from the majority of countries has indicated that healthcare burden is posing well-being adversity among households [16]. A considerable engagement has been on account of analysing the catastrophic consequences of disproportionate health spending on one hand and its implications for impoverishment and distressed financing [11, 12, 17].

The study highlights significant heterogeneity in the characteristics of different latent classes of healthcare users in India. We identified at least three distinct classes, with the highest-spending class having a lower probability of incurring expenses but a significantly higher expenditure when they do. Our analysis finds that class III healthcare users bear disproportionately high expenditure regardless of their socio-economic background and tend to consume more healthcare resources, as reflected in their longer hospital stays. Our estimates also suggest that marginal effect of various socio-economic variables on healthcare expenditure differs across classes, possibly due to disease severity or other unobserved heterogeneity in the data.

The results within each class align with the previous results. Healthcare expenditure increases with age, and spending on females remains consistently lower across all the classes. Additionally, the propensity of healthcare expenditure varies with class transitions, indicating distinct spending behaviors. In sum, defining these latent classes within the expenditure distribution provides a deeper understanding of health care spending and their broader implications. Recognizing this heterogeneity is crucial for interpreting the consequences of healthcare expenditure and for designing targeted interventions to mitigate financial burdens. By accounting for multi-dimensional influencing healthcare expenditure patterns, policy makers can develop more equitable and effective healthcare strategies.

These findings underscore the importance of recognizing distinct expenditure patterns across latent classes when assessing the impact of health expenditures on household well-being. Households in Class III, experiencing higher health expenditures, likely face greater consumption suppression to manage ill-health, whereas those in Classes I and II face relatively lower financial strain and compromise less on overall consumption.

Our analysis also reveals significant differences in average health expenditure by disease across different classes. This variation, despite similar diagnoses, highlights the role of unobserved heterogeneity in health expenditure distribution. Thus, variation might be due to choice of provider, with low-income groups being more price-sensitive [18].

Healthcare access and provider choice in India are shaped by socioeconomic status, public spending, and structural inequalities [18]. Public health spending has not influenced the decision to seek care but has reduced reliance on private providers, particularly among lower-income groups [18]. Socioeconomic disparities also determine the quality of care, as wealthier individuals access more knowledgeable providers, while the poor, especially in low-income communities, receive lower-quality care [19]. Hospital service utilization reflects inequities, with private healthcare being pro-rich, while public hospitals serve the poor more frequently, particularly in urban areas [19]. However, high out-of-pocket costs remain a barrier to healthcare access for low-income populations, with substantial variations across states [19]. To address issues of accessibility, affordability, and availability, the Government of India introduced the Ayushman Bharat program. However, its impact remains under evaluation. Preliminary studies in states like Chhattisgarh have shown no significant effect, while a similar initiative in Andhra Pradesh, Aarogyasri, has demonstrated positive outcomes, including a reduction in catastrophic health expenditures and an increase in hospital admissions among the poor [20, 21]. These disparities are further compounded by evidence from numerous countries indicating that the burden of healthcare poses significant challenges to household well-being [16]. Extensive research has documented the catastrophic financial consequences of disproportionate health spending and its role in driving impoverishment and distressed financing [11, 16, 17]. Despite these initiatives, significant disparities persist, underscoring the need for stronger policy interventions to improve the affordability, accessibility, and quality of public healthcare services.

The study has various policy implications. First, disparities in spending based on gender, education, and residence emphasize the need for inclusive health policies that address these inequities. These disparities persist across classes, and become more pronounced as we move from one class to another. Therefore, interventions should focus on reducing gender-based disparities and ensuring equitable access to healthcare services across different educational levels and geographic locations. Similar conclusions were drawn by Ladusingh and Pandey (2013) and Sangar et al. (2018), who found that the intensity of the burden of out-of-pocket health expenditure (OOPHE) is influenced by urban-rural differentials in India [22, 23]. These insights highlight the importance of targeted policies to alleviate the financial strain on vulnerable populations and promote health equity across the country.

Second, recognizing the distinct expenditure patterns across latent classes can guide the design of health financing strategies. For instance, enhancing insurance coverage and providing subsidies for high-cost treatments can reduce the financial burden on households in Class III. In countries like India, studies indicate that inadequate health insurance coverage leads to heavy reliance on out-of-pocket (OOP) health expenditure [24]. Additionally, policies promoting preventive care and early intervention could mitigate the progression of diseases and reduce overall health expenditures.

Previous research indicates that socially and economically underprivileged Indians face faster increases in OOPHE compared to wealthier groups [24, 25]. However, our study reveals significant differences in spending habits even among those with the same disease, highlighting the complex nature of health expenditure in India. This variation aligns with Grossman’s human-capital framework, which suggests that individuals invest in health for both consumption and production benefits [26]s. Additionally, theories of healthcare utilization, such as Andersen’s Behavioral Model, help explain variations in expenditure across socio-economic groups. The finite mixture model further accounts for unobserved heterogeneity, recognizing distinct health-seeking behaviors and financial constraints. These findings underscore the need for nuanced health policies that address these disparities by ensuring equitable access to health services, particularly for socially and economically disadvantaged groups. Implementing such policies could mitigate the financial burden of health expenses on underprivileged populations.

These findings not only identify the classes of health expenditure but have potential implications when studying the impoverishment or catastrophic health expenditures. The majority of studies report impoverishment or catastrophe due to health expenditure at an aggregate level, or disaggregated level by various socio-economic indicators, but none of them have considered the heterogenous distribution of health care expenditure. Identifying such classes will further improve the scope in targeted policy intervention for better and effective health care protection.

Importantly, the finite mixture approach demonstrated here is not confined to expenditure studies. Similar latent-class structures are encountered in diverse medical fields, such as studying the risk disparities alcohol use [27, 28], school absenteeism [29], and various other public health fields [30–34].

The study has limitations. Firstly, while we have accounted for unobserved heterogeneity through our statistical model and identified different healthcare user classes based on the given data, these latent classes may vary with different datasets. Therefore, identifying a uniform group of classes may be challenging.
