Prevalence of bacterial ear infections and multidrug resistance patterns among ear infection suspected patients in Ethiopia: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Mihret Tilahun, Agumas Shibabaw, Ermiyas Alemayehu, Zewudu Mulatie, Alemu Gedefie, Tesfaye Gesese, Mesfin Fiseha, Selamyhun Tadesse, Bekele Sharew, Abdurehman Eshete Mohammed, Habtu Debash, Melaku Ashagrie Belete
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOtitis media is an inflammation of the middle ear that affects both adults and children, leading to hearing loss and impacting intellectual performance, especially in developing countries. Key factors contributing to its development include lack of vaccination, inadequate breastfeeding, overcrowding, and malnutrition. Major bacterial pathogens involved are Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Staphylococcus aureus, Proteus mirabilis, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Streptococcus pyogenes, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, and Moraxella catarrhalis.This systematic review and meta-analysis revealed the pooled prevalence of bacterial ear infections and multidrug resistance in Ethiopia.MethodsThorough searches were conducted across electronic databases and grey literature using precise entry terms or phrases. Eligible studies were compiled into MS Excel and imported into STATA version 14 software for statistical analysis. The collective prevalence of bacterial ear infections and multidrug resistance patterns were calculated using a random-effect model, with heterogeneity assessed via the I2 value. Publication bias was examined using a funnel plot and Egger’s test. Sensitivity analysis was performed to gauge the impact of individual studies on the overall effect size.ResultSeventy studies met the criteria for qualitative analysis, with 14 selected for the meta-analysis. These studies reported a total of 3,667 bacterial isolates. The prevalence of bacterial ear infections varied significantly, ranging from 28.2% to 98.2% across individual studies. In this systematic review and meta-analysis, the pooled prevalence of bacterial ear infections in Ethiopia was found to be 73.39% (95% CI: 65.15–81.63), demonstrating substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 98.5%, p < 0.001). Additionally, the pooled prevalence of multidrug resistance among bacterial ear infections was 72.45% (95% CI: 66.86–78.04). The most frequently identified isolate was Staphylococcus aureus, with a pooled prevalence of 68.87% (95% CI: 13.94–123.79), followed closely by Proteus spp. at 68.55% (95% CI: 3.15–140.26), Pseudomonas aeruginosa at 48.15% (95% CI: 1.84–94.45), and Escherichia coli at 37.15% (95% CI: 14.03–88.34).ConclusionThe pooled prevalence of bacterial ear infections in Ethiopia is alarmingly high, accompanied by significant levels of multidrug resistance. These findings highlight the urgent need to reevaluate empirical treatment strategies due to the rising antimicrobial resistance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-024-10231-4.

Introduction

Otitis media (OM) is an inflammatory illness that results temporary or permanent hearing impairment due to fluid effusion or pathological alterations in the tympanic membrane of the middle ear [1]. An estimated 1.23 billion individuals are affected with bacterial ear infections worldwide, contributing to morbidity, mortality, and preasure on healthcare systems [2]. Globally, among 709 million patients with AOM, 51% were under-five children. On the other hand, AOM is responsible for approximately 25% of antibiotic prescriptions among children [3–5]. The peak prevalence of OM among children is mainly attributed to immature immune status, the shorter and more horizontal anatomy of the Eustachian tubethe, frequent exposure to upper respiratory infections, and malnutrition.

Bacteria are the most common agents for OM. The most common causative agents are Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa), Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), Proteus mirabilis (P. mirabilis), Klebsiella pneumoniae (K. pneumoniae), Streptococcus pyogenes (S. pyogenes), Streptococcus pneumoniae ( S. pneumoniae), Haemophilus influenzae (H. influenzae) and Moraxella catarrhalis (M. catarrhalis) [3, 4]. Variation on the prevalence of bacterial ear infections were reported worldwide, for example, 78.8% (63) of bacterial isolates with a ratio of GNB to GPB (57.1% to 42.9%) were reported [5]. In sub-Saharan Africa and other developing nations including Ethiopia, OM is increasingly prevalent and the most predominant pathogenic bacteria associated with these infections include weree S. aureus, P. mirabilis, P. aeruginosa, Klebsiella spp. and H. influenzae [6]. On the other hand, according to triple studies conducted in Ethiopia, the most frequent bacterial isolates were Proteus spp, S. aureus, and Pseudomonas spp [7–9].

The World Health Organization (WHO) report highlights a significant global health crisis attributed to drug-resistant pathogens. With an estimated 25,000 deaths annually and 1.5 billion people affected, the economic and healthcare burdens are profound. [10, 11]. As a This resistance leads to increased healthcare costs, loss of productivity, result of excessive and indiscriminate antibiotic use, resistance pathogen and spread of resistant bacteria are increasing and poses a global public health treat [12]. Limited laboratory resources in developing nations often compel physicians to treat patients empirically with broad-spectrum antibiotics, leading to the emergence of drug-resistant bacterial strains. This shift has affected the management of otitis media and its complications. The rise in antibiotic resistance is closely linked to factors such as dosing and administration practices. Understanding the transmission and resistance genes of bacterial species is vital for effective antibiotic selection. In low-income countries, otitis media treatment frequently relies on clinical symptoms without laboratory testing or drug susceptibility assessments, heightening the risk of resistant infections and leading to preventable complications like deafness and meningitis..

In Ethiopia, S. aureus isolates displayed high resistance levels to penicillin, tetracycline, and oxacillin, respectively [13]. P. aeruginosa, Proteus spp., E. coli, and K. pneumoniae demonstrated resistance to amoxicillin/clavulanic acid and ampicillin. However, ceftazidime, piperacillin, ceftriaxone, gentamicin, amikacin, ciprofloxacin, chloramphenicol, and cotrimoxazole were effective antibiotics against most Gram-negative bacterial isolates [14].

Bacterial ear infections, particularly OM, are significantly influenced by socio-environmental factors such as lack of vaccination, inadequate breastfeeding, overcrowding, and malnutrition. Vaccination against pathogens like Streptococcus pneumoniae and Haemophilus influenzae is crucial for reducing infection rates, especially in vulnerable children [15]. In Ethiopia, the high prevalence of bacterial ear infections presents a serious health risk across all age groups, often leading to complications like hearing impairment. The rise of multidrug-resistant bacterial pathogens raises significant concern within the healthcare community, limiting treatment options. This systematic review and meta-analysis aim to fill this knowledge gap by consolidating data from relevant studies conducted in Ethiopia. By integrating findings from various sources, we investigate the pooled prevalence of bacterial ear infections and patterns of multidrug resistance (MDR) among patients suspected of ear infections. This research aims to inform the development of targeted and effective management strategies.

Methods

Design and protocol registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis sought to establish the collective occurrence of bacterial ear infections and the trends in multidrug resistance among patients suspected of ear infections in Ethiopia. The study adhered to the guidelines outlined in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols) [16]. The review protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Review (PROSPERO) under registration number CRD42023439437.

Data source and search strategy

The review includes articles published from January 1, 2010, to April 16, 2023, focusing on studies related to bacterial isolates and multidrug-resistant patterns in patients with ear infections in Ethiopia. A thorough search was conducted across several electronic databases, including PubMed, Google Scholar, Scopus, ScienceDirect, African Index Medicus, African Journal Online (AJOL), Ethiopian Journals, and the WHO Afro Library, between April 6 and April 16, 2023. Additionally, a supplementary search of reference lists from relevant articles was performed to ensure comprehensive coverage.

The search was guided by the CoCoPop framework, leading to the development of targeted search terms such as “prevalence,” “epidemiology,” “magnitude,” “bacterial ear infections,” “antimicrobial resistance,” “antibiotic resistance,” “antibiotic susceptibility,” “otitis externa and otitis media,” “ear inflammation,” and “Ethiopia.” These terms were systematically combined using “OR” and “AND” to enhance article retrieval efficiency.

Formulation of research question and objectives

This systematic review and meta-analysis was to determine the prevalence of bacterial ear infections among patients suspected of having ear infections in Ethiopia. The primary objective was to assess the pooled prevalence of bacterial profiles, while the secondary objective focused on analyzing the multidrug resistance patterns of pathogens among these Ethiopian patients.

Study selection criteria

Before selecting pertinent full-text articles published in English from both local and international journals, a checklist was developed to evaluate study eligibility. The inclusion criteria focused on articles that addressed bacterial ear infections and their antimicrobial resistance profiles across all age groups, specifically targeting laboratory-based observational studies, such as cross-sectional and retrospective studies. Conversely, the exclusion criteria eliminated articles lacking information on bacterial isolates, as well as qualitative inquiries, review articles, commentaries, case series, case reports, conference proceedings, and abstracts This systematic approach ensured that only relevant studies were included, thereby enhancing the understanding of bacterial ear infections and their associated antimicrobial resistance patterns.

Data extraction

Independent reviewers (AG, ZM, AE, MT, MAB, SA, and MF) conducted the data extraction using a standardized format based on the Joanna Briggs Foundation (JBI) data extraction template [17], which was validated by an additional group of reviewers (HD, MT, OM, and HE). The extracted data were meticulously documented in a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. Each article was independently assessed by two reviewers to ensure a comprehensive and unbiased evaluation. The extracted information included the first author’s name and publication year, study period and design, geographical location of the study, total sample size and isolates, diagnostic criteria for bacterial ear infections, counts and proportions of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, and prevalence of multidrug resistance. This rigorous approach enhances the reliability and validity of our findings..

Quality assessment

The quality of the articles was methodologically assessed by five authors (MT, TG, AS, MAB, and MA) using the JBI quality appraisal tool. The full texts of the articles were reviewed to ensure they met the selection criteria and to identify any concerns regarding their eligibility. Using the critical appraisal checklists, studies that scored between 50 and 75% were deemed of good quality, while those scoring above 75% were classified as high quality. Consequently, articles meeting either the good or high-quality criteria were included in the analysis of bacterial profiles and the prevalence of multidrug resistance [17] (Supplementary Table 1).

Data synthesis and meta-analysis

Data analysis was conducted using STATA statistical software version 14.0. The pooled estimates and multidrug resistance (MDR) of the isolates were calculated using a random-effects model. Additionally, subgroup and meta-regression analyses were performed to identify potential sources of heterogeneity. Heterogeneity among studies was evaluated using Cochran’s Q test and I2 statistics, with a p-value of < 0.05 considered indicative of significant heterogeneity [18] and the Der Simonian-Laired random-effects model was used [19]. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on region, study design, sample size, and population type. The results were presented in a table and a forest plot. Publication bias was assessed by examining the symmetry of the funnel plot and utilizing Egger’s test statistics. For asymmetrical funnel plots, trim-and-fill analysis was applied to account for missing studies and estimate an adjusted effect size. Additionally, meta-regression was used to further explore the sources of heterogeneity..

Result

Selection and identification of studies

The initial search across the databases yielded a total of 1,220 articles. After removing 470 duplicates, approximately 750 articles remained. A preliminary review of titles, abstracts, and study objectives led to the exclusion of 680 articles. Ultimately, 70 full-length articles were rigorously assessed against the established eligibility criteria, resulting in the inclusion of 14 studies in the final meta-analysis [6, 7, 9, 14, 20–29] (Fig. 1).Fig. 1PRISMA flow diagram illustrating the process of selecting eligible studies for the systematic review and meta-analysis

Pooled estimate of significant bacterial ear infection

A total of 14 studies were selected, which yielded 3,667 bacterial isolates from 4,566 samples collected from patients suspected of having bacterial ear infections at healthcare facilities.. This systematic review and meta-analysis found that the pooled prevalence of bacterial ear infections in Ethiopia was 73.391% (95% CI 65.148–81.634), indicating significant heterogeneity (I2 = 98.5%, p < 0.001) among studies (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Forest plot showing the pooled prevalence of bacterial ear infections in Ethiopia, 2023

Forest plot showing the pooled prevalence of bacterial ear infections in Ethiopia, 2023

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses indicated that the prevalence of bacterial ear infections was 73.18% (95% CI: 64.32–82.05, I2 = 97.6%, p < 0.001) in studies conducted in the Amhara region, and 51.95% (95% CI: 5.50–98.41, I2 = 98.7%, p < 0.001) in the South nationality. In contrast, Oromia showed a significantly higher prevalence of 94.46% (95% CI: 37.26–43.66, I2 = 0%, p = 0.764) (Fig. 3). Further subgroup analysis by study populations revealed a prevalence of 71% (95% CI: 57.18–84.81, I2 = 98.9%, p < 0.001) among all age groups, and 73.45% (95% CI: 45.04–101.88, I2 = 98.9%, p < 0.001) specifically in children, with heterogeneity between groups at p = 0.243 (Fig. 4). Additionally, analysis based on study design showed that retrospective studies had a prevalence of 8.18% (95% CI: 78.72–78.84, I2 = 83.0%, p < 0.001), while cross-sectional studies reported a prevalence of 68.22% (95% CI: 52.69–83.76, I2 = 99.1%, p < 0.001), with heterogeneity between groups at p = 0.086 (Fig. 5).Fig. 3Subgroup analysis by region in EthiopiaFig. 4Subgroup analysis by category of ages in EthiopiaFig. 5Subgroup analysis by study design in Ethiopia

Pooled prevalence of multidrug resistance (MDR)

The prevalence of multidrug resistance (MDR) among bacterial ear infections ranged from 32.4% to 97%. The pooled prevalence of MDR in Ethiopia was found to be 72.45% (95% CI: 66.86–78.04), demonstrating a high degree of heterogeneity across studies (I2 = 96.8%, p < 0.001) (Fig. 6). Subgroup analysis of MDR across all age groups indicated a prevalence of 69.64% (95% CI: 59.62–79.65, I2 = 97.9%, p < 0.001), while the prevalence among children was higher at 80.09% (95% CI: 76.35–83.84, I2 = 35.3%, p = 0.213). Notably, significant heterogeneity was observed between the groups (p = 0.008) (Fig. 7).Fig. 6Forest plot showing the overall pooled prevalence of MDR in EthiopiaFig. 7Forest plot showing subgroup analysis of MDR in Ethiopia

Pooled prevalence of bacterial isolates causing ear infection

Table 2 summarizes 12 different types of bacterial isolates identified in the studies. Gram-negative bacteria were the most prevalent, comprising 65.91% of the pooled prevalence. The most frequently identified isolate was S. aureus, with an overall prevalence of 68.87% (95% CI: 13.94–123.79), followed closely by Proteus spp. at 68.55% (95% CI: 3.15–140.26), P. aeruginosa at 48.15% (95% CI: 1.84–94.45), and E. coli at 37.15% (95% CI: 14.03–88.34). The least prevalent isolate was Acinetobacter species, with an overall prevalence of 8.63% (95% CI: 0.25–17.01) (Table 2). Sensitivity analysis

The results of our sensitivity analysis indicated that excluding any single study did not significantly alter the pooled estimate of the proportion, confirming a robust aggregate outcome. When individual studies were removed, the pooled effect size consistently remained within the 95% confidence interval of the overall pooled effect size. This demonstrates that no single study had a substantial impact on the overall prevalence of bacterial ear infections in Ethiopia (Table 3). Publication bias

The funnel plot was utilized to evaluate the impact of small-study effects and publication bias on the estimated pooled prevalence. In this analysis, the asymmetry observed in the funnel plot suggested the presence of publication bias, as over 64.3% of the studies clustered on the right side of the triangular zone (Fig. 8). Meta-regression

Meta-regression was performed to explore potential sources of heterogeneity among the studies included in the meta-analysis. Continuous study characteristics, such as publication year, sample size, and number of bacterial isolates, were analyzed as covariates. However, no significant variables were identified that could explain the observed heterogeneity among the studies in this investigation (P > 0.05) (Table 7).

Discussions

Bacterial ear infections pose a significant public health threat in developing countries, largely due to the rise of antibiotic resistance. These infections are a major concern, leading to hearing loss, severe disability, and even death [30]. Thus, this meta-analysis determines the burden and etiology of bacterial ear infections and multidrug resistance patterns in Ethiopia.The pooled prevalence of bacterial ear infections in Ethiopia was 73.39% (95% CI: 65.148–81.634) with substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 98.5%, p < 0.001) across the studies. This finding is comparable with a previous review reported as the pooled prevalence of pediatric with AOM was 67% [31] and it is lower than the meta-analysis study in sub-Saharan among adult patients with a pooled prevalence of 92% [32]. This result is consistent with findings separately reported in Ethiopia, including 80.4% of Bahir-Dar [33], 76.7% in Gondar [26], 75.6% in Hawassa [29] and other countries like Yemen (78.8%), Saudi Arabia (75%), and Nepal (82.13%) [34]. However, it was less than what was reported from Mekelle (98.2%) [32], Dessie 83.6% [8], Nigeria (96.5%) [29], and the Western Pacific (90%) [6]. The current result was slightly higher than South Western Nigeria’s reported result of 60% [30]. The numerous factors such as study design, sample size, geographical variation, and study participants may have an impact on the differences in culture positivity rates.

In this meta-analysis, Gram-negative bacteria accounted for 65.91% of culture-positive samples. Similarly, in a review article on bacterial infections in patients, the majority of studies identified Gram-negative bacteria as the most common type. Ethiopia (74.2%) and Dessie (66.7%) had comparable prevalence rates, while Nigeria had a close prevalence of 66.7% [35, 36]. It is higher than the study conducted in Gondar, which accounted for 59.1% [22], and Bangladesh (55%) [37]. The notably high prevalence of Gram-negative bacteria across various studies can be attributed to variations in sample size, geographic location, study duration, study participants, and potential contamination of respiratory flora specimens.

Regarding the specific bacteria identified, the most common bacterial isolate of bacterial ear infections was S. aureus, with total bacteria isolates with a pooled prevalence of 68.87 (95% CI 13.94–123.79), followed by Proteus spp. 68.55 (95% CI −3.15-140.26), P. aeruginosa 48.15 (95% CI 1.84–94.45), and E. coli 37.15. The lowest bacteria with Acinetobacter species pooled prevalence was 8.63 (95% CI 0.25–17.01). In contrast, a systematic review and meta-analysis of pediatric acute otitis media found that the pooled prevalence of streptococcus pneumoniae was 30%, followed by Haemophilus influenza at 23% and Moraxella catarrhalis at 5% [31]. Similarly, systematic review and meta-analysis done in sub-Saharan the commonest isolate was P. aeruginosa followed by S. aureus, P. mirabilis, and Klebsiella species [31]. A single Study in, Nigeria showed P. aeruginosa (21.2%), S. aureus (14.6%) and P. vulgarise (13.9 %) were predominate bacteria [38] and Saudi Arabia [39], Similarly, Proteus spp. followed by S. aureus and Pseudomonas spp. were the predominant isolates [8, 28, 29], and apart from Ethiopia, Bangladesh reported S. aureus (37%) and Pseudomonas species (31.5%) were the leading isolates [37]. Different kinds of literature also mentioned S. aureus, P. aeruginosa, and Proteus species as the most common isolates of bacterial ear infection [30, 40]. Since the natural habitats of most of these bacteria can be skin, environment, and soil, ear infection, particularly from these isolates is usually common [5, 30, 35, 41].

Furthermore, the pooled prevalence of multi-drug resistant bacterial ear infections was 72.45% (66.86–78.04) with a high level of heterogeneity (I2 = 96.8%, p < 0.001) across the studies. In other single studies conducted in Dessie 72.6% [7], Dessie area 74% [9], Gondar 76% [22] supported this study. However, this systematic review and meta-analysis are lower than a single study conducted by Hawassa 97% [20] and it is higher than studies conducted in Bahr-Dar 32.4% [21] and Debremarkos 59.2% [24]. This phenomenon can be attributed to Several factors contribute to the high rates of MDR in these infections, including the prescription of antibiotics without laboratory guidance, self-medication through unregulated pharmacy purchases, and misuse in animal husbandry. Inappropriate prescribing practices and an aggressive approach to treating infections with antibacterial agents further exacerbate the issue.Moreover, the biofilm-forming capabilities of common bacterial isolates complicate treatment efforts, while inadequate bacterial culture facilities and poor infection prevention and control measures in healthcare settings significantly contribute to the development of MDR. Incorporating these considerations into the results section clarifies the complexity of the issue and contextualizes the observed heterogeneity in MDR rates across studies.

The prevalence of MDR pathogens in bacterial ear infections is significantly influenced by patient treatment history. Specifically, the proportion of MDR pathogens from antibiotic-naive patients versus those from previously treated patients is a critical confounding variable. Understanding this distinction is vital as it underscores the impact of prior antibiotic exposure on resistance patterns [42].

The current study revealed significant heterogeneity (I2 = 97.6%, p < 0.001). The study’s significant heterogeneity is most likely due to methodological differences such as sample size, target population categories, and underlying patient circumstances, rather than publication bias. The diversity of the target groups from which samples were collected, as well as the ongoing antibiotic resistance crisis, could also be factors to consider. Antibiotics gradually lose their effectiveness over time due to the emergence and spread of resistance among bacterial pathogens, which is primarily driven by antibiotic overuse and improper utilization, as well as their widespread use in agriculture and the food industry.

While antibiotic resistance is an inherent phenomenon in bacteria that cannot be eliminated, various measures can be implemented to slow its development and develop more effective strategies for controlling its spread. Given the clinical significance of ear infections caused by multidrug-resistant bacteria, we provide a comprehensive review of the main findings concerning the epidemiology, diagnosis, and clinical implications of such infections.

Sensitivity analysis, subgroup analysis, and meta-regression were used to identify potential sources of heterogeneity. The sensitivity analysis revealed that no single study significantly influenced the pooled effect size. The sequential omission of each study resulted in pooled prevalence estimates that were within the 95% confidence interval. Furthermore, publication bias was assessed using a funnel plot and Egger’s test statistics, which indicated the presence of publication bias, even though the graph appeared symmetrical, indicating the lack of publication bias. Following trim and fill analysis, the pooled prevalence of bacterial ear infections in Ethiopia was found to be 73.392% (95% confidence interval: 65.148–81.635).

Meta-regression analysis revealed that the total number of pathogen isolates and sample size did not significantly contribute to heterogeneity in the prevalence of bacterial ear infections, and publication year was not found to be a significant factor.

This study is notable for its comprehensive approach, as it is the first of its kind to conduct a thorough examination of bacterial ear infections and multidrug resistance patterns in Ethiopia. It provides a comprehensive overview by incorporating a wide range of studies conducted across multiple regions and cities in the country. Furthermore, the study includes several investigations involving different target populations and clinical specimens, providing a comprehensive picture of bacterial ear infections and multidrug resistance in the country. However, the results should be interpreted with caution due to the high heterogeneity observed among the reviewed studies.

Discrepancies were found in terms of prevalence, etiology, study designs, participants, disease conditions, clinical specimens, sample sizes, and antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST). To address this heterogeneity, the meta-analyses used Der Simonian and Laird’s random-effects model. Subgroup analyses, sensitivity analyses, and meta-regression were also performed to better understand and mitigate the effects of heterogeneity on the finding.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis reveal a concerning prevalence of bacterial ear infections in Ethiopia, accompanied by a high rate of multidrug resistance (MDR). Staphylococcus aureus was identified as the most frequently encountered pathogen, followed by Proteus spp., Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Escherichia coli, based on data from multiple studies. The prevalence of MDR among bacterial ear infections shows significant variability, indicating a pressing public health concern. These findings call for concerted efforts from international, continental, and national health authorities to develop effective prevention and control measures for both hospital-acquired and community-acquired infections. Furthermore, the implementation of robust infection prevention and antibiotic stewardship programs is essential to address the challenges posed by biofilm-producing bacterial infections. This data serves as a critical resource for guiding future research and facilitating evidence-based decision-making in clinical and public health contexts.
