Knowledge, Attitudes, and Perceptions Regarding Mind Mapping Among Undergraduate Respiratory Therapy Students in Saudi Arabia: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Faisal A Turkestani, Jameel Hakeem, Norah Abdullah Alharthi, Rema Alharthi, Ziyad F Al Nufaiei, Raid M Al Zhranei, Rana M Altabee, Shada Alodaini, Alaa Bugis, Saleh Alselemi, Nowaf Y Alobaidi, Ayedh D Alahmari, Genevieve P Zipp
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeMind mapping is an active learning strategy that supports information organization and integration. Evidence regarding Saudi respiratory therapy students’ knowledge, attitudes, and perceptions regarding mind mapping remains scarce. This study assessed these domains among this population.MethodsThis observational, descriptive, cross-sectional survey included 405 undergraduate respiratory therapy students from 13 Saudi universities, recruited through convenience and snowball sampling. A researcher-developed questionnaire was used to evaluate the content validity, face validity, and internal consistency. Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 22. Nonparametric statistical tests were applied, with statistical significance set at p <0.05.ResultsThe overall mean knowledge score was 4.70 of 10 (SD = 1.54). Using modified Bloom cutoffs, 277 students (68.4%) had low knowledge. The mean attitude and perception scores were 3.66 (SD = 0.58) and 3.64 out of 5 (SD = 0.66), respectively, indicating generally favorable responses. Knowledge and attitude scores did not differ significantly by sex, academic level, or geographic region. Attitude scores showed a significant increasing trend across the grade point average (GPA) categories (p <0.001). Female students had a higher overall perception score than males (3.70 vs 3.58; p =0.022). Perception scores did not differ significantly by academic level or geographic region, but differed across universities (p <0.001), with means ranging from 3.36 to 4.35.ConclusionRespiratory therapy students demonstrated limited knowledge but generally favorable attitudes and perceptions regarding mind mapping. Attitude scores increased across the GPA categories, whereas perception scores varied by sex and participating university. These findings provide multi-institutional baseline evidence that may inform future educational planning and evaluations of mind mapping in respiratory therapy education.

Introduction

Education for health professions increasingly emphasizes learner-centered approaches that promote active participation, knowledge integration, critical thinking, and lifelong learning.1 Active learning strategies involve students analyzing, organizing, discussing, and applying information, rather than passively receiving it. These strategies are particularly relevant in healthcare education, where learners must connect scientific knowledge with clinical assessments and decision-making.1,2

This educational emphasis is particularly important in respiratory therapy (RT). Respiratory therapists must integrate respiratory physiology, patient assessments, diagnostic information, and therapeutic interventions when making clinical decisions in complex patient care situations. Therefore, critical thinking is a fundamental competency in respiratory care because it enables practitioners to interpret information, evaluate alternatives, solve clinical problems, and make appropriate decisions.3 Previous research has found that respiratory care students demonstrate only moderate overall critical thinking, reinforcing the need for educational strategies that support the development of higher-order cognitive skills.4 Recent literature on respiratory care has similarly emphasized the value of active learning approaches for preparing students to apply knowledge in clinical practice.3,5

Mind mapping (MM) is a visual active learning strategy in which information is organized around a central topic and represented through interconnected branches.6 This nonlinear structure allows learners to identify relationships among concepts, organize complex information, and connect new knowledge with their existing understanding. MM may also function as a metacognitive and self-directed learning tool, because constructing a map requires students to select relevant information, establish conceptual relationships, and represent their understanding visually.2

Evidence from the education of health professionals supports several potential applications of MM. In medical diagnostic curricula, implementing MM in small-group learning has been associated with greater student engagement and improvements in physical examinations, electrocardiogram interpretation, history taking, and clinical reasoning activities.2 A systematic review of randomized studies involving undergraduate medical students found that mind and concept maps were generally associated with improved knowledge retention, comprehension, and academic performance, although the findings were not consistent.7 Among clinical nursing students, MM training improved critical thinking disposition, and students reported benefits related to memory and the organization of information.8 A randomized study involving physician assistant students also reported improvements in critical thinking skills among students who used MM compared to those who used conventional note-taking strategies.9 In medical education, MM has additionally been associated with better perceived understanding and improved examination performance in pharmacology,10 while a randomized crossover study found greater knowledge acquisition in anatomy following MM compared to didactic lectures.11 Collectively, this evidence suggests that MM may support the organization, integration, and application of complex information across different healthcare disciplines.

Respiratory care education in Saudi Arabia has expanded considerably; however, differences remain in the geographical distribution, institutional resources, faculty availability, and educational development of RT programs. A national survey identified 23 respiratory care programs and reported several barriers to their development, including faculty shortages, limited research activities, and ineffective communication between institutions.12 Variations in the adoption and implementation of active-learning methods have also been reported. For example, a national survey of simulation use found that approximately 75% of the responding Saudi RT programs used simulation-based learning, but its implementation differed across institutions, with shortages of trained faculty and staff identified as important barriers.13 These findings illustrate the diversity of the educational environments in which Saudi RT students are trained and emphasize the need to examine students’ experiences across multiple institutions.

Despite growing evidence from medical, nursing, and other health professional programs, research examining MM, specifically in RT education, remains scarce. One study conducted among students from 2 Saudi RT programs demonstrated a significantly greater improvement in critical thinking scores following a 15-week MM intervention compared to standard note-taking, providing initial evidence of MM’s relevance to RT education.14 However, that study evaluated the effectiveness of a structured intervention and did not examine RT students’ broader knowledge, attitudes, or perceptions regarding MM. To the best of our knowledge, these outcomes have not previously been assessed across multiple RT programs and geographical regions in Saudi Arabia. Understanding students’ knowledge, attitudes, and perceptions is important because awareness and acceptance of a learning strategy may influence their willingness to use it when incorporated into educational practice. Thus, this study aimed to assess knowledge, attitudes, and perceptions regarding MM among undergraduate RT students across multiple universities in Saudi Arabia.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

A multi-institutional, descriptive, cross-sectional survey was conducted to assess knowledge, attitudes, and perceptions regarding MM among RT students in Saudi Arabia. Data were collected from 13 participating institutions between November 20, 2023, and February 20, 2024. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of King Abdullah International Medical Research Center (IRB Protocol No. SP23J/116/07).

Participants and Sampling

Students enrolled in RT programs at the participating Saudi universities were recruited using non-probability convenience sampling supplemented by snowball sampling. Convenience sampling was selected because RT students were distributed across multiple universities, and no national sampling frame was available to support probability-based recruitment. Snowball sampling was used to broaden recruitment across geographically dispersed institutions.

The 13 participating institutions were anonymized using alphabetical codes (A–M) to maintain institutional confidentiality and avoid attributing findings to identifiable individual programs. The institutions were grouped into four geographical regions: central (I, J, and K), western (A, B, G, H, L, and M), eastern (C and D), and southern (E and F).

Eligible participants were RT students in their second, third, or fourth academic year, as well as internship students who were enrolled at the participating institutions during the study period and voluntarily agreed to participate. Interns were included because they remained a part of the RT educational program and continued to receive academic and clinical supervision during their internship. Students without prior exposure to MM were excluded because basic familiarity with the technique was required to assess their knowledge, attitudes, and perceptions concerning its use. The potential selection bias resulting from this criterion is acknowledged as a limitation.

Sample Size Calculation

The target population was estimated to include 1297 undergraduate RT students enrolled in Saudi RT programs, based on a national survey of respiratory care education.12 Using a 95% confidence level, 5% margin of error, and 50% response distribution, the Raosoft sample size calculator indicated a minimum required sample of 297 participants. Recruitment continued until 405 completed questionnaires were obtained, which exceeded the calculated minimum. Nevertheless, as convenience and snowball sampling were used, the calculated sample size did not establish national representativeness.

Data Collection Procedures

Data were collected electronically using a self-administered questionnaire hosted on Google Forms. The survey link was distributed through university representatives, email, WhatsApp, and other communication platforms commonly used by RT students. Participants were also encouraged to share the link with eligible students at their own or other participating institutions, thereby facilitating snowball recruitment. Each participant completed the questionnaire once and no longitudinal follow-up or repeated assessments were conducted.

Questionnaire and Scoring

The questionnaire consisted of 29 items divided into 4 sections: sociodemographic characteristics, knowledge, attitudes, and perceptions. The sociodemographic section collected information on sex, academic level, grade point average (GPA) category, geographical region, and prior exposure to MM. The complete study questionnaire is provided in Supplementary File 1.

The knowledge domain contained 10 dichotomously scored items assessing students’ understanding of the definition, purposes, and educational applications of MM. One point was assigned to each correct response and zero to each incorrect response, producing a total score ranging from 0 to 10. Based on the modified Bloom cutoff criteria, knowledge was categorized as low for scores below 60% (0–5 points), moderate for scores of 60–79% (6–7 points), and high for scores of 80–100% (8–10 points).15 These categories were used as descriptive thresholds and were not specifically validated for the present MM knowledge instrument.

The attitude domain contained six items assessing students’ evaluations of MM and their behavioral intentions regarding its use. These items addressed its perceived value as a learning and productivity tool, usefulness for organizing information and supporting understanding and memorization, willingness to recommend it to peers, active engagement in creating mind maps, and motivation to use it in academic development. Each item was rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The overall attitude score was calculated by averaging the six items, with higher scores indicating more favorable attitudes toward MM.

The perception domain contained five items assessing students’ perceptions and use of MM, including its usefulness in understanding complicated topics, summarizing information, and preparing for lectures and examinations, and its use as a learning strategy within the RT program. Each item was rated on a five-point scale ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (always). The overall perception score was calculated by averaging the five items. For descriptive interpretation, mean scores were classified using equal intervals of 0.80: 1.00–1.79 (Never), 1.80–2.59 (Rarely), 2.60–3.39 (Sometimes), 3.40–4.19 (Often), and 4.20–5.00 (Always).

Validity and Reliability

The research team developed a questionnaire based on a knowledge, attitude, and perception framework. Content validity was assessed by a panel of experts in medical education and educational psychology with experience in active-learning strategies. A pilot study involving 25 participants was conducted to assess face validity, including the clarity and comprehensibility of the questionnaire items. Minor revisions were made based on expert feedback and the pilot study findings before the questionnaire was administered to the main study sample.

Internal consistency was assessed separately for each domain. The Kuder–Richardson Formula 20 was used for the 10 dichotomously scored knowledge items, whereas Cronbach’s alpha was used for the 6 attitude and 5 perception items. The coefficients were 0.72 for knowledge, 0.76 for attitude, and 0.75 for perception. The reliability coefficients were calculated using data from the final study sample.

Data Management and Analysis

Completed online responses were retrieved from Google Forms and imported into IBM SPSS Statistics version 22 (IBM Corp.). The data were checked for completeness and accuracy before analysis. Categorical variables are summarized as frequencies and percentages. Continuous and scale-based variables are summarized using means and standard deviations, with medians and interquartile ranges reported where appropriate.

Score distributions were assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests. As the knowledge, attitude, and perception scores were not normally distributed, nonparametric statistical methods were used. The Mann–Whitney U-test was used to compare knowledge, attitude, and perception scores between male and female participants. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used to compare scores across academic levels, geographic regions, and participating universities. Separate Kruskal–Wallis analyses were also conducted for individual perception items across academic levels. When the omnibus Kruskal–Wallis test was statistically significant, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed with Holm adjustment for multiple comparisons.

The Jonckheere–Terpstra test was used to assess the ordered trend in attitude scores across the four GPA categories: 3.00–3.49, 3.50–3.99, 4.00–4.49, and 4.50–5.00. Mean ranks are reported for non-parametric group comparisons. All statistical tests were 2-sided, and statistical significance was set at p <0.05.

Attitude Scores in Relation to GPA

Discussion

This multicenter study examined knowledge, attitudes, and perceptions regarding MM among RT students across 13 universities in Saudi Arabia. The principal findings were generally a low level of knowledge and favorable attitudes and perceptions regarding MM. Knowledge scores did not differ significantly according to sex, academic level, or geographical region. Attitude scores were also similar across these groups, although they demonstrated a statistically significant increasing trend across the ordered GPA categories. Female students had significantly higher overall perception scores than males, whereas perception scores did not differ significantly across academic levels or geographic regions. However, perception scores varied significantly among the participating universities.

Knowledge of Mind Mapping

A notable finding was the low knowledge of MM. The mean knowledge score was 4.70 out of 10, and more than two-thirds of participants were classified as having low knowledge. All participants reported creating at least one mind map per academic year, indicating exposure to MM. However, the study did not examine the relationship between frequency of use and knowledge or assess the context and quality of students’ MM experiences. The knowledge scores did not differ significantly by sex, academic level, or geographic region, indicating that limited knowledge was broadly observed across participant groups. A direct comparison with previous studies is difficult because much of the available literature examines the effectiveness of structured MM interventions rather than students’ baseline knowledge of the strategy. Nevertheless, evidence suggests that MM can support learning outcomes when students receive structured guidance in its use.7,14 These findings provide a rationale for evaluating whether explicit instruction in constructing and applying mind maps can strengthen students’ knowledge of the strategy.

Attitudes Toward Mind Mapping by Sex

Students generally demonstrated positive attitudes toward MM, with the overall mean score falling within the “Agree” category. This finding is consistent with previous research in which students viewed MM positively as a method for summarizing and organizing information.16 Similarly, a Saudi study reported that students were generally satisfied with MM and commonly identified organization and planning among the skills gained through its use.17 A recent national study of RT students in Saudi Arabia found that female students had significantly more positive attitudes toward simulation-based education than males and identified sex as an independent predictor of the overall attitude score.18 Although simulation and MM are distinct active learning strategies, this finding indicates that sex may be associated with attitudes toward some educational approaches within RT education. However, in the present study, the observed numerical difference did not reach statistical significance and should not be interpreted as evidence of a sex-based difference in attitudes toward MM. Both male and female students generally demonstrated positive attitudes toward its use.

Attitude by Academic Level and Geographic Region

Positive attitudes toward MM were observed across academic levels and geographic regions, with all group means falling within the “Agree” category. Although third-year students and students from the western region had the highest mean scores, the differences across academic levels and geographic regions were not statistically significant. Thus, the findings provide no evidence that attitudes toward MM differ according to students’ educational stage or geographic location.

Attitude in Relation to GPA

Attitude scores demonstrated a statistically significant increasing trend across the ordered GPA categories, indicating that students in the higher GPA categories tended to report more favorable attitudes toward MM. Evidence specifically examining the relationship between academic performance and attitudes toward MM in RT education remains limited. A previous RT study demonstrated that structured use of MM improved students’ critical thinking skills, but it focused on an educational outcome rather than the relationship between attitude and GPA.14 More broadly, a narrative review of active learning in respiratory care education reported that strategies such as flipped classrooms, simulation, problem-based learning, and team-based learning can enhance student engagement, knowledge retention, and clinical decision-making.5 Studies of attitudes toward other educational approaches have produced mixed findings. Sezer19 found that medical students’ attitudes toward e-learning differed significantly according to their academic achievement, whereas Daryazadeh et al20 found no significant correlation between e-learning attitudes and GPA. Although these educational approaches are not directly equivalent to MM, they demonstrate that the relationship between attitudes toward learning strategies and academic performance may depend on educational context. Because the present study was cross-sectional, it cannot be determined whether favorable attitudes toward MM contribute to academic performance, whether higher-performing students are more likely to value the strategy, or whether other factors explain the observed trend.

Perceptions of Mind Mapping by Sex

Female students had significantly higher overall perception scores for MM than males. Female students also had higher mean scores across all five perception items, although none of the individual item-level differences were statistically significant. Therefore, the significant overall result reflects a consistent cumulative pattern across the perception scale, rather than a difference attributable to a single statement. Related research suggests that sex may influence how students engage in and respond to MM. Abdolahi et al21 reported that female medical students achieved higher anatomy scores following mind map-based instruction, whereas Merchie and Van Keer22 found that sex, appreciation of MM, and self-efficacy influenced students’ spontaneous use of mind maps, with females using this strategy more frequently. However, these studies examined performance and spontaneous use rather than perception. More recently, an orthodontic education study found that perceptions of MM were generally similar by sex, although one item showed a significant difference favoring male students.23 Taken together, the available evidence suggests that sex-related responses to MM may vary according to the educational context and outcome assessed. The present study provides RT-specific evidence that female students may have more favorable overall perceptions of MM, although further studies are needed to examine the educational and experiential factors underlying this difference.

Perceptions by Academic Level

Students across all academic levels reported favorable perceptions of MM, with every group mean falling within the “Often” category. Although second-year students had the highest mean perception score and fourth-year students the lowest, the difference across academic levels was not statistically significant. All participants reported using mind maps, most commonly 1–5 times per academic year (58.3%), followed by 6–10 times (28.6%); however, these data describe frequency rather than the nature or quality of use. D’Antoni et al24 suggested that students may require multiple MM sessions and repeated exposure to develop proficiency and proposed that MM could be introduced early in their education. Favorable responses have also been reported among new-entry medical students,16 whereas He et al2 reported high acceptance following the integration of MM into medical diagnostic workshops for junior undergraduates. A systematic review also found that mind and concept maps can support knowledge retention and comprehension in preclinical medical education.7 This evidence provides a rationale for prospectively evaluating the introduction of MM during the earlier academic years and its reinforcement throughout the curriculum.

Perceptions by Region and University

Perception scores did not differ significantly across geographic regions but varied significantly between individual universities, demonstrating university-level variation that was not captured by broader regional groupings. Previous studies offer a possible context for this finding. Zipp and Maher25 found that only 10.9% of the surveyed physical therapy programs incorporated MM into their curricula and identified limited faculty awareness as the principal barrier to its use. Agou23 reported more favorable perceptions among students who received structured orientation, guided instruction, and individualized feedback than among those who completed MM activities independently. Similarly, Yan et al26 reported higher self-perceived motivation, knowledge framework development, and teamwork when MM was integrated with problem-based learning. These studies suggest that curricular exposure, instructional design, and faculty guidance are relevant to students’ perceptions of MM. Because these factors were not examined in the present study, they cannot be used to explain the observed university-level differences but represent specific areas for future investigation.

Implications for Respiratory Therapy Education

These findings support further evaluation of how RT students are taught to construct and apply mind maps. RT programs may consider providing structured instruction and guided practice beginning during earlier academic years and continuing through progressively more complex theoretical and clinical content.23,24 The university-level variation in perception scores also indicates that the educational context of individual programs should be considered, including existing curricular exposure and faculty guidance. Prospective multicenter studies are needed to determine whether structured and repeated MM instruction improves students’ knowledge, proficiency, engagement, and academic and clinical performance.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths. It included a relatively large sample of 405 RT students from 13 universities across all 4 geographical regions of Saudi Arabia. The participants represented different academic levels, including interns, and the nearly equal distribution of male and female students supported sex-based comparisons. The simultaneous assessment of knowledge, attitudes, and perceptions provides a multidimensional description of the three domains of MM. This multicenter design extends the available evidence beyond a single institution and addresses an important gap in the literature on RT education.

The findings should be interpreted in consideration of the study’s limitations. The cross-sectional design prevents conclusions from being drawn regarding temporal or causal relationships. The use of convenience and snowball sampling, and an online self-administered questionnaire, may have introduced selection and response biases. Representation was also uneven across regions and universities, with relatively small samples from some institutions, which may have reduced the precision of the subgroup comparisons and limited the generalizability of the findings. Although the questionnaire underwent expert review, pilot testing, and internal consistency assessment, a comprehensive psychometric validation was not conducted. Furthermore, the knowledge-level categories were based on conventional modified Bloom percentage cutoffs rather than thresholds validated specifically for the present instrument and should, therefore, be interpreted descriptively. The study also did not assess the extent of formal MM instruction, curricular integration, or faculty guidance at each university, limiting its ability to explain the observed institutional variation. Finally, as the data were collected over several months, students’ exposure to MM and related educational experiences may have changed during the study period.

Conclusion

This multicenter study provides an overview of knowledge, attitudes, and perceptions regarding MM among RT students across 13 universities in Saudi Arabia. Students demonstrated limited knowledge but generally favorable attitudes and perceptions. These patterns were broadly consistent across academic levels and geographic regions, whereas attitude scores increased across GPA categories and perception scores varied by sex and university. The findings indicate that favorable responses to MM are generally present across participating programs, whereas students’ knowledge and university-level variation in perceptions remain areas for educational attention. Therefore, RT programs may consider reviewing how MM is introduced, taught, and reinforced within their curricula, while prospective studies should evaluate the effects of structured instruction on students’ knowledge and educational outcomes.
