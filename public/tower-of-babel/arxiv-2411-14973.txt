Mean Value for Random Ideal Lattices
Nihar Gargava, Maryna Viazovska

Abstract
We investigate the average number of lattice points within a ball for the n n th cyclotomic number field, where the lattice is chosen at random from the set of unit determinant ideal lattices of the field. We show that this average is nearly identical to the average number of lattice points in a ball among all unit determinant random lattices of the same dimension. To establish this result, we apply the Hecke integration formula and subconvexity bounds on Dedekind zeta functions of cyclotomic fields. The symmetries arising from the roots of unity in an ideal lattice allow us to prove the existence of ideal lattice packings of dimension φ ⁡ ( n ) \varphi(n) and density n ⋅ 2 − φ ⁡ ( n ) ​ ( 1 + o ⁡ ( 1 ) ) n\cdot 2^{-\varphi(n)}(1+o(1)) as n n goes to infinity.



Introduction

The following is a classical fact due to Siegel
[
18
]
that is often referred to as the Siegel mean value theorem. If
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a ball of volume
V
, then for any
d\geq 2

\mathbb{E}(\card B\cap\Lambda)=1+V,{\text{ for a Haar-random unit covolume lattice }\Lambda\in\SL_{d}(\mathbb{R})/\SL_{d}(\mathbb{Z})}.

(2)

The
1
on the right side is the contribution of
0\in\Lambda
, which lies in each lattice. It is worth noticing here that the expected value does not depend on
d
. This means that as long as the volume of the ball is fixed, the expected number of lattice points in a ball cannot tell us which dimension our lattices live in.
The goal of this paper is to have a version of
Equation

2

for an ensemble of special lattices that have an arithmetic structure. Let us first set up some notation.

Let
K
be a number field of degree
d

\mathcal{O}_{K}
be the ring of integers
K
.
We define an ideal lattice to be a

\mathcal{O}_{K}
-module in
K\otimes\mathbb{R}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, normalized to have covolume 1.
On

K
(see Equation (
15

The collection of all such lattices is a compact abelian Lie group denoted as
\Ar(K)
, called the Arakelov class group
[
2
]
of the number field
K
.
Naturally, we can embed
\Ar(K)\subseteq\SL_{d}(\mathbb{R})/\SL_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
.
One can then try to compute
\mathbb{E}_{\Lambda\in\Ar(K)}(\card B\cap\Lambda)
for a number field
K
.
See

The main theorem that we present is the following.

Theorem 1

Let

\mathbb{E}(\card B\cap\Lambda)=1+V+\varepsilon(V,K)\text{ for a Haar-random unit covolume }\Lambda\in\Ar(K).

(3)

where
\varepsilon(V,K)
is an error term
1
1

1

The

in the error term agrees with the results of Södergren-Strömbergsson

who show that for a Haar random lattice

the random variable

\frac{(\card B\cap\Lambda)-(1+V)}{\sqrt{2V}}\rightarrow\mathcal{N}(0,1)\text{ in distribution as }d\to\infty,

that satisfies

|\varepsilon(V,K)|\leq c\cdot\sqrt{V}\Delta_{K}^{{-\eta}}.

(5)

\eta

Here
\Delta_{K}
is the absolute value of the discriminant of the number field

The discriminant

So actually, if one follows through the proof in Section

The best value of

Motivation

Sphere packings

Our main motivation here is that using an argument exactly like Venkatesh
[
22
]
, it is possible to improve the lower bound on the lattice packing constant by a factor of
2
.
It is possible to conclude the following from Theorem
1
.

Corollary 2
.

Let

c_{d}=\sup\{\vol(B)\mid\exists g\in\SL_{d}(\mathbb{R}),g\mathbb{Z}^{d}\cap B=\{0\}\}.

(6)

Then
for
n
large enough and some constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
independent of
n

c_{\varphi(n)}\geq n-c_{1}e^{-c_{2}\varphi(n)\log n}.

(7)

In particular, there exist infinitely many dimensions
d
such that

c_{d}\geq d\log\log d-c_{1}e^{-c_{2}d\log d}.

(8)

Proof.

(of Corollary
2
)

Let
K=\mathbb{Q}(\mu_{n})
, where
\mu_{n}\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
is a primitive
n
th root of unity.

Observe that both the ball
B
, due to the definition of the trace form in Equation (
15
), and the lattice
\Lambda
, being an ideal lattice, are invariant under the action of the roots of unity
\mu_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}
.
Hence, we obtain that
\card(B\cap\Lambda)\in 1+n\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. Hence, if
V<n-O(e^{-c\varphi(n)\log n})
, we know that
B\cap\Lambda=\{0\}
.

Then, using the choice of

as the product of first

primes like in
[
22
]

and letting

, we maximize the ratio of

and

to obtain the sequence of dimensions.
∎

In general, it is an open problem to exhibit an “explicitly” defined unit-covolume lattice

for which

, where

is an origin-centered ball whose volume is at least a constant.
The work of Terras

proves using the non-vanishing of Dedekind zeta functions

Theorem

Back when Venkatesh’s result had been published, it was not just the best sphere packing bound in high dimensions.
That has now been surpassed by the work of Klartag
[
12

shows the existence of unit covolume lattices that prove

d\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
.

Nonetheless, the case of ideal lattices for sphere packing still remains interesting because of its connections to number theory.

Lattice-based cryptography

Ideal lattices over cyclotomic fields are of independent interest

2

.
Probabilistic results about behaviour of short vectors in ideal lattices are interesting from this point of view.
To this end, Theorem

.
This has attracted some interest from the cryptography community.
For example, a chapter in the doctoral thesis by H. Bambury

General number fields

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a set of number fields up to isomorphism.
Then one can conclude that
\mathcal{S}
contains finitely many number fields with bounded discriminant. Indeed,
for any
T>0
, it is a known fact that there are finitely many number fields
K
with
\Delta_{K}\leq T
.

For a set
\mathcal{S}
, the following are the hypotheses that interest us.

1.

Subconvexity

The following is the version of subconvexity hypothesis required for our purpose.

Hypothesis 3
.

For some
1/4\geq\eta_{0},\eta_{2}>0
and some
\frac{1}{2}\geq\eta_{1}>0
, there exists a universal constant
C(\eta_{0},\eta_{1},\eta_{2})>0
,
such that for all number fields
K\in\mathcal{S}
and all
t\in\mathbb{R}
one has

|\zeta_{K}(\tfrac{1}{2}+it)|\leq C(\eta_{0},\eta_{1},\eta_{2})^{\deg K}\cdot\left(\Delta_{K}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}-\eta_{0}}\cdot\left((1+|t|)^{r_{1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}-\eta_{1}}\cdot\left((1+|t|)^{2r_{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}-\eta_{2}}.

(9)

Here
(r_{1},r_{2})
is the signature of the number field
K
so that
\deg K=r_{1}+2r_{2}
. The constants
\eta_{0},\eta_{1},\eta_{2}
are required to be independent of
r_{1},r_{2}
and
K
.

When
\tfrac{1}{2}-\eta_{1}=\tfrac{1}{4}-\eta_{2}<\frac{1}{4}
,
this type of bound on
\zeta_{K}(\tfrac{1}{2}+it)

is called a “subconvexity bound” with respect to the analytic conductor
\Delta_{K}\cdot(1+|t|)^{\deg K}
.
The corresponding “convexity bound” would correspond to the statement with
\eta_{0},\eta_{2}=-\varepsilon
and
\eta_{1}=\tfrac{1}{4}+\varepsilon
for some arbitrarily small
\varepsilon>0

[
10
]
.

Remark 4
.

It would also be pertinent to stress that weak subconvexity may not suffice when
\deg K\xrightarrow{}\infty
.
This is because we need the constant
C^{\deg K}
in front of
Equation

9

to be absorbed in
\Delta_{K}^{-\eta_{0}}
to make our error term disappear (c.f.
Hypothesis

5
).
It is expected that a constant
C^{\deg K}
would be present with any subconvexity bound of the style in
Equation

9
.

2.

Lower bound on the discriminant

One also needs the following hypothesis for dealing with the exponential factors
C(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})

that appear in
Hypothesis

3
.

Hypothesis 5
.

\text{ For $K\in\mathcal{S}$, }\frac{\deg K}{\log\Delta_{K}}\rightarrow 0\text{ as }\Delta_{K}\rightarrow\infty.

(10)

It is known that counterexamples to
Hypothesis

5
exist. This is due to Golod and Shafarevich’s work on infinite class field towers of ramified extensions constructed using class field theory
[
8
]
.

Odlyzko proved that
\deg K/\log\Delta_{K}
is universally bounded
[
16
]
.
A bound
\deg K/\log\Delta_{K}<c
may also suffice if
c>0
is small enough relative to
\eta_{0}
and
\log C(\eta_{0},\eta_{1},\eta_{2})
from
Equation

9
; see
Equation

114
.
It is not clear what the interest in such a scenario may be.

3.

Lower bound on residues of Dedekind zeta function

We will also need to assume the following hypothesis.

Hypothesis 6
.

One has for
K\in\mathcal{S}

\frac{\log|\Res_{s=1}\zeta_{K}(s)|}{\log\Delta_{K}}\rightarrow 0\text{ as }\Delta_{K}\xrightarrow{}\infty.

(11)

This hypothesis is actually implied by
Hypothesis

5
if one assumes that all the number fields
K\in\mathcal{S}
are Galois, or if one assumes the Generalized Riemann hypothesis (GRH).
This implication is due to the Brauer-Siegel theorem (c.f.
[
19
]
).

In fact, for the case of normal number fields, Stark
[
19
]
proves a much stronger result. We will discuss this in
Section

4
.

One can then state the theorem
Theorem

1
in the following generality.

Theorem 7

Let
\mathcal{S}
be set of number fields satisfying
Hypothesis

3
,
5
and

Related work and proof techniques

In terms of technique, this work draws inspiration from
[
3
, Proposition 12.5]
which is related to proving Duke’s theorem for cubic fields. In their paper, Einsiedler-Lindenstrauss-Michel-Venkatesh showed a version of Theorem
1
when
K
varies over cubic fields of growing discriminant by using Hecke integration formula and subconvexity, just like we use it in this paper.
Combined with dynamical ideas, for
\SL_{3}(\mathbb{R})/\SL_{3}(\mathbb{Z})
this was sufficient to prove equidistribution of measures supported on these ideal lattices as
\Delta_{K}\rightarrow\infty
.
Since in our case the degree of the number field
K
may go to infinity, it is no longer meaningful to talk about equidistribution.
In the situation with growing degree, Theorem
1
is a variant of Duke’s theorem for cyclotomic fields.

Here is a rough outline of the proof of
Theorem

1
. The main input is the Hecke integration formula given in
Theorem

17
which lets us average Epstein zeta functions on ideal lattices. One can then use this formula to count the average number of lattice points using an inverse Mellin transform.
This is done in
Corollary

19
using a contour shifting argument which allows writing the average as a residue term and a certain contour integral involving the Dedekind zeta function of the number field
K
. The former is our main term and the latter is the error term.
There is a small obstruction in contour shifting, which is that the decay rate of the integrand may not be sufficient to allow moving the vertical contour line.
So in
Section

2.3
we show that when
\deg K
is large enough, ratios of gamma functions decay fast enough to allow contour shifting.
Finally, one used the hypotheses stated earlier to hammer the nail,
which allows
one to show that the error term in
Theorem

1
vanishes as
\deg K\rightarrow\infty
.

We prove that the Hypotheses
3
,
5
and
11

are satisfied for cyclotomic number fields using two deep theorems,
namely subconvexity bounds by Petrow-Young
[
17
]
and the other being an effective version of the Brauer-Siegel theorem due to Stark
[
19
]
.

Organization of the paper

This paper has been written for an interdisciplinary audience and we have tried to make it self-contained. In particular, we spend a considerable effort in reintroducing the classical work of Hecke which is well-known in analytic number theory and geometry of numbers.

A reader familiar with these concepts can freely skip this section.
Section

Finally, Section

Hypothesis

3
,
Hypothesis

5
and
Equation

11
for the case of cyclotomic fields.

Preliminaries

Let
K
be a number field of degree
d=r_{1}+2r_{2}
where
r_{1}
is the number of real embeddings and
r_{2}
is the number of pairs of complex embeddings, paired under the complex conjugation.
We denote by
x\mapsto\overline{x}
the complex conjugation on each of the
r_{2}
pairs of complex embeddings
K\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
.
Let
\Delta_{K}
be the absolute value of the discriminant of
K
.
Let
K_{\mathbb{R}}=K\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbb{R}
.
We endow
K_{\mathbb{R}}
with the positive-definite quadratic form

x\mapsto\Tr(x\overline{x}).

(15)

We denote by
\mathcal{O}_{K}
the ring of integers of
K
. In the geometry of numbers, it is a classical fact that
\Delta_{K}
is the square of the covolume of
\mathcal{O}_{K}\subseteq K_{\mathbb{R}}
with respect to this quadratic form. That is

\sqrt{\Delta_{K}}=\vol(K_{\mathbb{R}}/\mathcal{O}_{K}).

(16)

We see every ideal
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}
as a lattice in
K_{\mathbb{R}}
. With respect to this, the ideal
\mathcal{I}
has

\vol(K_{\mathbb{R}}/\mathcal{I})=\covol(\mathcal{I})=\N(\mathcal{I})\sqrt{\Delta_{K}},

(17)

where
\N(\mathcal{I})=\card\mathcal{O}_{K}/\mathcal{I}
, which is known to be finite.
See
[
15
]
for more background information.

Remark 8
.

With a different choice of the norm in Equation (
15
), it is common to have a factor of
2^{-r_{2}}
in Equation (
17
). See
[
15
, Chapter I-§5]
.

Remark 9
.

To get an idea of the quadratic form in
Equation

15
, observe that
the region
[-1,1]^{r_{1}}\times\{x\in\mathbb{C}\mid|x|\leq 1\}^{r_{2}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{r_{1}}\times\mathbb{C}^{r_{2}}\simeq K_{\mathbb{R}}
should have a volume of
2^{r_{1}}(2\pi)^{r_{2}}
(and not
2^{r_{1}}\pi^{r_{2}}
).

For example, with
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-1})
,

is at a distance
\sqrt{2}
from the origin.

1.1
Arakelov class group

Let
\Ar(K)
be the Arakelov class group of
K
, also known as the extended class group in
[
13
]
.
The Arakelov class group
\Ar(K)

is defined as the set of ideal lattices
(g,\mathcal{I})
where
\mathcal{I}\subseteq K
is a non-zero finitely generated
\mathcal{O}_{K}
-module
and
g\in K_{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}
. We identify two ideal lattices as per the rule

(g_{1},\mathcal{I}_{1})=(g_{2},\mathcal{I}_{2})\Leftrightarrow g_{1}\mathcal{I}_{1}=c\cdot g_{2}\mathcal{I}_{2}\text{ for some }c>0.

(18)

Here the equality on the right is the equality of sublattices in
K_{\mathbb{R}}
.

Observe that the following sequence of topological abelian groups is exact.

\{1\}\rightarrow K_{\mathbb{R}}^{(1)}/\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}\rightarrow\Ar(K)\rightarrow\Cl(K)\rightarrow\{1\}.

(19)

Here
K_{\mathbb{R}}^{(1)}
is the group

K_{\mathbb{R}}^{(1)}=\{a\in K_{\mathbb{R}}\mid|\N(a)|=1\}.

(20)

The map
\N:K_{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the norm map. Dirichlet’s unit theorem tells us that
K_{\mathbb{R}}^{(1)}/\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}
is compact.

One can renormalize every ideal lattice to be of unit determinant by identifying it with the following sublattice of
K_{\mathbb{R}}
:

(g,\mathcal{I})\mapsto\left(\sqrt{\Delta_{K}}\cdot|\N(g)|\cdot\N(\mathcal{I})\right)^{-\frac{1}{d}}\cdot g\mathcal{I}.

(21)

Hence, from now on, when we say
\Lambda\in\Ar(K)
is an ideal lattice, we mean a unit covolume lattice.

1.1.1
Haar measure on the Arakelov Class group

Since
\Ar(K)
is a locally compact topological group, it admits a Haar measure. If we fix a Haar measure
\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}^{\times}\hskip-2.70004pta
on
K_{\mathbb{R}}^{(1)}
,
we get a natural measure on
\Ar(K)
as
\card\Cl(K)
number of copies of
K_{\mathbb{R}}^{(1)}/\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}
. The most natural choice of a Haar measure on
K_{\mathbb{R}}^{(1)}
is

\int_{A}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}^{\times}\hskip-2.70004pta=\int_{A\cdot(0,1]\subseteq K_{\mathbb{R}}}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}\hskip 1.30005ptx,\text{ for any measurable }A\subseteq K_{\mathbb{R}}^{(1)},

(22)

where
\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}\hskip 1.30005ptx
is the Lebesgue measure on
K_{\mathbb{R}}
with respect to the inner product given in Equation (
15
).
Throughout the paper, we will assume
K_{\mathbb{R}}^{(1)}
has this measure.

Remark 10
.

To see how the measure
\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}\hskip 1.30005ptx
on
K_{\mathbb{R}}
and
\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}^{\times}\hskip-2.70004pta
on
K_{\mathbb{R}}^{(1)}
are related, we observe that for
A\subseteq K_{\mathbb{R}}^{(1)}
and

, we have for

\int_{A\cdot(T,T+\varepsilon)}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}\hskip 1.30005ptx=\varepsilon\,(dT^{d-1})\,\int_{A}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}^{\times}\hskip-2.70004pta+O(\varepsilon^{2}).

(23)

1.2
Dedekind zeta function

Recall that the Dedekind zeta function of a number field is the function

\zeta_{K}(s)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}\\
\mathcal{I}\text{ ideal}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\N(\mathcal{I})^{s}}\text{ for }\Re(s)>1.

(24)

This function can get meromorphically continued to the complex plane with a simple pole at
s=1
. The residue at
s=1
is the one that will concern us the most.

1.2.1
Residue at 1

Using a standard argument, one observes readily that

\Res_{s=1}\zeta_{K}(s)=\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\card\{\text{Ideals }\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}\mid\N(\mathcal{I})\leq T\}}{T}.

(25)

By a classical
geometry of numbers argument, one can precisely evaluate this residue. First we need the following lemma which will also be of use eventually.

Lemma 11
.

Let
\mathcal{I}\subseteq K
be a fractional ideal.
There is a bijection between
w\in(\mathcal{I}\setminus\{0\})/\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}
and the ideals in the ideal class
[\mathcal{I}^{-1}]\in\Cl(K)
given by

w\mapsto w\mathcal{I}^{-1}.

(26)

Proof.

If
w\in\mathcal{I}
then the
\mathcal{O}_{K}
-module
\langle w\rangle
generated by
w
lies in
\mathcal{I}
.
Hence, there exists a fractional ideal
\mathcal{J}\subseteq K
such that
\langle w\rangle=\mathcal{J}\mathcal{I}
.
One can then check that
\mathcal{J}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}
and
\mathcal{J}
is actually an ideal. Clearly
[\mathcal{J}]=[\mathcal{I}^{-1}]

On the other hand, suppose we are given an ideal
\mathcal{J}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}
in the ideal class of
[\mathcal{I}^{-1}]
. Then, we must have that
\mathcal{J}\mathcal{I}=\langle w\rangle
for some
w\in K^{\times}
. Since
\langle w\rangle\subseteq\mathcal{I}
, this uniquely identifies
w\in(\mathcal{I}\setminus\{0\})/\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}
.
∎

We are now ready to prove the following classical result, known as analytic class number formula.

Lemma 12
.

\Res_{s=1}\zeta_{K}(s)=\frac{\vol(\Ar(K))}{\sqrt{\Delta_{K}}}.

(27)

Proof.

We will only sketch the proof.
Let us fix an ideal class
[\mathcal{I}^{-1}]\in\Cl(K)
. Then, we get from
Lemma
11
that the set of ideals
\mathcal{J}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}
in this ideal class are in bijection with
the set
(\mathcal{I}\setminus\{0\})/\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}
. So we get that

\card\{\text{ Ideals }\mathcal{J}\in[\mathcal{I}^{-1}]\mid\N(\mathcal{J})\leq T\}=\card\{w\in(\mathcal{I}\setminus\{0\})/\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}\mid\N(w)\leq T\N(\mathcal{I})\}

(28)

This is the same thing as counting lattice points in a domain in
K_{\mathbb{R}}
. That is, the count equals

\card\left(\{x\in K_{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}\mid\N(x)\leq T\N(\mathcal{I})\}\cap\mathcal{I}\right)/\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}.

(29)

One can then observe that

\vol(K_{\mathbb{R}}/\mathcal{I})=\N(\mathcal{I})\sqrt{\Delta_{K}}.

(30)

And so the point count is asymptotically (for large
T
)

\card\{\text{ Ideals }\mathcal{J}\in[\mathcal{I}^{-1}]\mid\N(\mathcal{J})\leq T\}\sim\frac{\vol(K_{\mathbb{R}}^{(1)}/\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times})}{\sqrt{\Delta_{K}}\N(\mathcal{I})}\cdot(T\N(\mathcal{I})),

(31)

where the volume on
K_{\mathbb{R}}^{(1)}
is taken with respect to the Haar measure defined in Section
1.1.1
. Doing this for each ideal class and summing up gives the required statement.
∎

1.3
Mellin transform

Let
g:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be a compactly supported smooth function (so the support is bounded away from
0
). We define the Mellin transform of
g
to be

\left[\mathcal{M}g\right](s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}g(t)t^{s-1}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}\hskip 1.30005ptt.

(32)

This is a holomorphic function on all of the complex plane. One can recover the function
g
using the inverse Mellin transform as

g(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\sigma-i\infty}^{\sigma+i\infty}t^{-s}\left[\mathcal{M}g\right](s)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}\hskip 1.30005pts,

(33)

where we are free to choose any
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
.

However, our main interest is in using the theory of the Mellin transform for the function
\ind_{[0,R]}
, which is the indicator function of the interval
[0,R]\subseteq\mathbb{R}
for some
R>0
. For this situation, observe that

\left[\mathcal{M}\ind_{[0,R]}\right](s)=\tfrac{1}{s}R^{s}{\text{ for }s\neq 0,}

(34)

which is a holomorphic function on
\Re(s)>0
.
In this case, one can still nearly recover the function
\ind_{[0,R]}
on
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
using an improper integral.

Lemma 13
.

Suppose that
t\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and
t\neq R
. Then

\ind_{[0,R]}(t)=\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\sigma-iT}^{\sigma+iT}t^{-s}\frac{R^{s}}{s}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}\hskip 1.30005pt{s}\text{ for any $\sigma>0$}.

(35)

For
t=R
, the right hand side is equal to
1/2
.

Proof.

Follows from residue calculations.
See
[
14
, (5.3),(5.5)]
for instance.
∎

Therefore, to avoid worrying too much about
t=R
, from henceforth we will assume that
\ind_{[0,R]}(R)=\tfrac{1}{2}
.

2
Hecke integration formula

Let
f_{\rad}:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be a compactly supported smooth function.
One can then use this to define a compactly supported radial function

f:K_{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by taking

f(x)=f_{\rad}(\|x\|),

(36)

where
\|x\|
is as defined in Equation (
15
).
We can then recover the function
f(x)
by Equation (
34
) as

f(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\sigma-i\infty}^{\sigma+i\infty}\|x\|^{-s}\left[\mathcal{M}f_{\rad}\right](s)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}\hskip 1.30005pts\text{ for any }\sigma>0.

(37)

Given an ideal lattice
\Lambda\subseteq K_{\mathbb{R}}
,
one can consider the sum given by

\sum_{v\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}}f(v)={\sum_{v\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\sigma-i\infty}^{\sigma+i\infty}\|v\|^{-s}\left[\mathcal{M}f_{\rad}\right](s)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}\hskip 1.30005pts.}

(38)

We would like to pull out the integral outside the sum. However, to have that

\sum_{v\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}}\|v\|^{-s}
is absolutely convergent, we assume that
\sigma>d
due to the following lemma.

Lemma 14
.

Given a lattice
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a suitable inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the following sum is absolutely convergent and defines a holomorphic function on the half-plane
\Re(s)>d
:

\mathscr{E}(\Lambda,s)=\sum_{v\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}}\|v\|^{-s}.

(39)

It admits an analytic continuation to
\mathbb{C}\setminus\{d\}
with a pole at
s=d
and residue:

\Res_{s=d}\mathscr{E}(\Lambda,s)=d\cdot\Res_{s=1}\mathscr{E}(\Lambda,sd)=d\cdot\frac{\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}}{\Gamma(\tfrac{d}{2}+1)}\cdot\frac{1}{\covol(\Lambda)}.

(40)

The completed Epstein zeta function satisfies the following functional equation when
\covol(\Lambda)=1
.

\displaystyle\mathscr{E}^{*}(\Lambda,s)

\displaystyle={\pi^{-\frac{s}{2}}}{\Gamma(\tfrac{s}{2})}\mathscr{E}(\Lambda,s),

(41)

\displaystyle\mathscr{E}^{*}(\Lambda,s)

\displaystyle=\mathscr{E}^{*}(\Lambda^{*},d-s).

(42)

Here
\Lambda^{*}=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid\langle y,\Lambda\rangle\subseteq\mathbb{Z}\}
is the dual lattice of
\Lambda
.

Proof.

Absolute convergence and the value of the residue can be shown by counting lattice points
x\in\Lambda
such that
\|x\|\leq R
as
R\to\infty
. This is roughly the volume of a ball of radius
R
normalized by the size of a fundamental domain of
\mathbb{R}^{d}/\Lambda
.

Analytic continuation and the functional equation follows from the Poisson summation formula.
One can see all details worked out in Neukirch
[
15
]
.
∎

The function
\mathscr{E}(\Lambda,s)
is called the Epstein zeta function of the lattice
\Lambda
.
One can then get the following lemma.

Lemma 15
.

Let
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a unit covolume lattice and
f(x)=f_{\rad}(\|x\|)
be a radial function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for a compactly supported smooth function
f_{\rad}:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Then

\sum_{v\in\Lambda}f(v)=\frac{d}{2\pi i}\int_{\sigma-i\infty}^{\sigma+i\infty}\mathscr{E}(\Lambda,sd)\left[\mathcal{M}f_{\rad}\right](sd)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}\hskip 1.30005pts\text{ for $\sigma>1$}.

(43)

Proof.

Follows from absolute convergence of
\mathscr{E}(\Lambda,s)
for
\Re(s)>d
and Equation (
38
). Note the change of variable from
s
to
sd
.
∎

Before moving to the main theorem in this section, let us reestablish the so-called
“Hecke’s trick”
[
11
, Lemma 3.5]
.

Lemma 16
.

For
\Re(s)>1
we have that

\displaystyle\int_{a\in K_{\mathbb{R}}^{(1)}}\|av\|^{-sd}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}^{\times}\hskip-2.70004pta

\displaystyle=|\N(v)|^{-s}\cdot{\frac{(2\pi)^{r_{2}}\Gamma(\tfrac{s}{2})^{r_{1}}\Gamma(s)^{r_{2}}}{2^{sr_{2}}\cdot\frac{d}{2}\Gamma(\frac{sd}{2})}}.

(44)

Proof.

Let us prove it for
v=1
.
Consider the integral

\int_{x\in K_{\mathbb{R}}}e^{-\|x\|^{2}}|\N(x)|^{s-1}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}\hskip 1.30005ptx=\int_{x\in K_{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}}e^{-\|x\|^{2}}|\N(x)|^{s}|\N(x)|^{-1}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}\hskip 1.30005ptx.

(45)

We can evaluate it in two ways.
One way is to write the Haar measure on
|\N(x)|^{-1}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}\hskip 1.30005ptx
on
K_{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}
by writing (see Remark
10
)

\displaystyle K_{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}

\displaystyle\simeq\mathbb{R}_{>0}\times K_{\mathbb{R}}^{(1)}

(46)

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=t\cdot a

(47)

\displaystyle|\N(x)|^{-1}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}\hskip 1.30005ptx

\displaystyle=\deg K\cdot\left(\tfrac{1}{t}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}\hskip 1.30005ptt\right)\cdot\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}^{\times}\hskip-2.70004pta.

(48)

Observe then that for
d=\deg K
we have
\N(at)=t^{d}\N(a)
. Hence,

\displaystyle\int_{x\in K_{\mathbb{R}}}e^{-\|x\|^{2}}|\N(x)|^{s-1}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}\hskip 1.30005ptx

\displaystyle=\int_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}}\int_{a\in K_{\mathbb{R}}^{(1)}}e^{-t^{2}\|a\|^{2}}dt^{sd-1}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}^{\times}\hskip-2.70004pta\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}\hskip 1.30005ptt.

(49)

\displaystyle=\int_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}}e^{-t^{2}}dt^{sd-1}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}\hskip 1.30005ptt\int_{a\in K_{\mathbb{R}}^{(1)}}\|a\|^{-sd}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}^{\times}\hskip-2.70004pta

(50)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{d}{2}\Gamma(\tfrac{sd}{2})\int_{a\in K_{\mathbb{R}}^{(1)}}\|a\|^{-sd}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}^{\times}\hskip-2.70004pta.

(51)

On the other hand, one can also write
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{r_{1}+r_{2}})\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{1}}\times\mathbb{C}^{r_{2}}
.

\displaystyle\int_{x\in K_{\mathbb{R}}}

\displaystyle e^{-\|x\|^{2}}|\N(x)|^{s-1}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}\hskip 1.30005ptx

(52)

\displaystyle=\int_{x\in K_{\mathbb{R}}}e^{-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r_{1}}|x_{i}|^{2}+2\sum_{i=r_{1}+1}^{r_{2}}|x_{i}|^{2}\right)}|x_{1}x_{2}\dots x_{r_{1}}|^{s-1}|x_{r_{1}+1}\dots x_{r_{1}+r_{2}}|^{2(s-1)}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}\hskip 1.30005ptx

(53)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{r_{1}}\left(2\int_{x\in{\mathbb{R}_{>0}}}e^{-x^{2}}x^{s-1}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}\hskip 1.30005ptx\right)\prod_{i=r_{1}+1}^{r_{1}+r_{2}}\left(2\cdot 2\pi\int_{x\in{\mathbb{R}_{>0}}}e^{-2x^{2}}x^{2(s-1)}x\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}\hskip 1.30005ptx\right)

(54)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{r_{2}}2^{-sr_{2}}\Gamma(\tfrac{s}{2})^{r_{1}}\Gamma(s)^{r_{2}}.

(55)

The factor of
2
appearing before
2\pi
is a bit subtle. See Remark
9
.

For
v\neq 1
, replace
x
with
xv
in the integral in Equation (
45
) and follow the same process.

∎

Suppose now if we equip
\Ar(K)
with a Haar-probability measure, then one can ask what is the value of

\int_{\Lambda\in\Ar(K)}\left(\sum_{v\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}}f(v)\right)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}\hskip 1.30005pt\Lambda.

(56)

The answer can now be given by the classical theorem of Hecke.
Hecke originally evaluated this integral for the case of
f(v)=\|v\|_{2}^{-s}
to prove the meromorphic continuation of the Dedekind zeta function
[
9
]
.

Theorem 17
.

(Hecke, 1918)

Suppose
\Ar(K)
is given the Haar-probability measure
\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}\hskip 1.30005pt\Lambda

and suppose that each
\Lambda\in\Ar(K)
is normalized as a unit covolume lattice in
K_{\mathbb{R}}
.
Then, for
\Re(s)>1
we have that

\int_{\Lambda\in\Ar(K)}\mathscr{E}(\Lambda,ds)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}\hskip 1.30005pt\Lambda=\frac{\zeta_{K}({s})}{\vol(\Ar(K))}\cdot\frac{\pi^{\frac{sd}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{sd}{2})\cdot\frac{d}{2}}\cdot\frac{\Delta_{K}^{\frac{s}{2}}\left(\pi^{-\frac{s}{2}}\Gamma(\tfrac{s}{2})\right)^{r_{1}}\left((2\pi)^{-s}\Gamma(s)\right)^{r_{2}}}{(2\pi)^{-r_{2}}}.

(57)

Remark 18
.

One way to sanity check that the above expression is correct is to know that the completed Dedekind zeta function

\zeta^{*}_{K}(s)={\Delta_{K}^{\frac{s}{2}}\left(\pi^{-\frac{s}{2}}\Gamma(\tfrac{s}{2})\right)^{r_{1}}\left((2\pi)^{-s}\Gamma(s)\right)^{r_{2}}}\zeta_{K}(s)

(58)

must satisfy the functional equation
\zeta_{K}^{*}(1-s)=\zeta_{K}^{*}(s)
. Then, using (
42
), we know that the function
\zeta_{K}^{*}(s)^{-1}\int_{\Lambda\in\Ar(K)}\mathscr{E}^{*}(\Lambda,ds)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}\hskip 1.30005pt\Lambda

must be free of Gamma factors since
\Ar(K)
is symmetric with respect to
\Lambda\mapsto\Lambda^{*}
. Hence, it is likely to be a constant and we can then chase this constant by taking
s\rightarrow 1
.

Proof.

(of
Theorem

17
)

Even though we had the Haar-random measure on
\Ar(K)
, for the proof
we will work with the natural measure on
\Ar(K)
as described in Section
1.1.1
. Also, we will assume that
\Re(s)>1
since this assumption is sufficient to show the equality.
Let
V_{C}=\vol(\Ar(K))
for notational clarity.

Denote by
\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}^{\times}\hskip-2.70004pta
the measure on
K_{\mathbb{R}}^{(1)}
given in Equation (
22
).
Let
\Lambda\in\Ar(K)
be an ideal lattice.
Then
K_{\mathbb{R}}^{(1)}\cdot\Lambda

is one complete fiber of the map
\Ar(K)\rightarrow\Cl(K)
containing
\Lambda
.
The stabilizer of the
K_{\mathbb{R}}^{(1)}
-action is exactly

\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}\subseteq K_{\mathbb{R}}^{(1)}
.
So, if
h=\card\Cl(K)
and if
\Lambda_{1},\dots,\Lambda_{h}
is a set of ideal lattices, one from each ideal class, then after some simple folding-unfolding

\displaystyle\int_{\Lambda\in\Ar(K)}\mathscr{E}(\Lambda,sd)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}\hskip 1.30005pt\Lambda

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V_{C}}\sum_{i=1}^{h}\int_{a\in K_{\mathbb{R}}^{(1)}/\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}}\left(\sum_{v\in\Lambda_{i}\setminus\{0\}}\|av\|^{-sd}\right)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}^{\times}\hskip-2.70004pta

(59)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V_{C}}\sum_{i=1}^{h}\int_{a\in K_{\mathbb{R}}^{(1)}/\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}}\left(\sum_{u\in\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}}\sum_{v\in(\Lambda_{i}\setminus\{0\})/\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}}\|auv\|^{-sd}\right)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}^{\times}\hskip-2.70004pta

(60)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V_{C}}\sum_{i=1}^{h}\int_{a\in K_{\mathbb{R}}^{(1)}}\left(\sum_{v\in(\Lambda_{i}\setminus\{0\})/\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}}\|av\|^{-sd}\right)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}^{\times}\hskip-2.70004pta.

(61)

Then, we can bring in
Hecke’s trick from Lemma
16

and get that if
v\in K_{\mathbb{R}}^{\times}
then

\displaystyle\int_{a\in K_{\mathbb{R}}^{(1)}}\|av\|^{-sd}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}^{\times}\hskip-2.70004pta

\displaystyle=|\N(v)|^{-s}\cdot{\frac{(2\pi)^{r_{2}}\Gamma(\tfrac{s}{2})^{r_{1}}\Gamma(s)^{r_{2}}}{2^{sr_{2}}\cdot\frac{d}{2}\Gamma(\frac{sd}{2})}}.

(62)

Now without loss of generality, one can assume that
\Lambda_{i}\subseteq K
is a fractional ideal.
This means that
v\in\Lambda_{i}\setminus\{0\}\Rightarrow|\N(v)|\neq 0
. Note that
|\N(v)|=|\N(uv)|
for any
u\in\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}
so it is well defined as a function on
(\Lambda_{i}\setminus\{0\})/\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}
. Hence, we can write that

\int_{\Lambda\in\Ar(K)}\mathscr{E}(\Lambda,sd)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}\hskip 1.30005pt\Lambda=\frac{1}{V_{C}}\sum_{i=1}^{h}\left(\sum_{v\in(\Lambda_{i}\setminus\{0\})/\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}}|\N(v)|^{-s}\right)\cdot{\frac{(2\pi)^{r_{2}}\Gamma(\tfrac{s}{2})^{r_{1}}\Gamma(s)^{r_{2}}}{2^{sr_{2}}\frac{d}{2}\Gamma(\frac{sd}{2})}}.

(63)

Observe that

\sum_{i=1}^{h}\sum_{v\in(\Lambda_{i}\setminus\{0\})/\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}}|\N(v)|^{-s}=\Delta_{K}^{\frac{s}{2}}\zeta_{K}({s}).

(64)

Indeed, we have that for some ideal
\mathcal{I}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}
,

\Lambda_{i}=\Delta_{K}^{-\frac{1}{2d}}\N(\mathcal{I}_{i})^{-\frac{1}{d}}\mathcal{I}_{i}.

(65)

So, for some
v\in\Lambda_{i}
, we have some
w\in\mathcal{I}_{i}
such that

v=\Delta_{K}^{-\frac{1}{2d}}\N(\mathcal{I}_{i})^{-\frac{1}{d}}w\Rightarrow|\N(v)|=|\N(w)|\cdot\Delta_{K}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\cdot\N(\mathcal{I}_{i})^{-1}.

Then, observe that as
w
varies through the different representatives of
\mathcal{I}_{i}/\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}
, we must have that
\N(w)/\N(\mathcal{I}_{i})
goes through norms of different ideals lying in the ideal class of
\mathcal{I}_{i}^{-1}
.
This was shown in Lemma
11
.

∎

After this, we would like to use Lemma
15
to average
\sum_{v\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}}f(v)
. This gives a completely explicit formula.

Corollary 19
.

Consider the same setting as Theorem
17
. Let
f_{\rad}:\mathbb{R}_{>0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a compactly supported smooth function and
f:K_{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be
f(x)=f_{\rad}(\|x\|)
as before. Then

\int_{\Lambda\in\Ar(K)}\left(\sum_{v\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}}f(v)\right)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}\hskip 1.30005pt\Lambda=\int_{x\in K_{\mathbb{R}}}f(x){\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}\hskip 1.30005ptx}+\varepsilon(K,f),

(66)

where

\varepsilon(K,f)=\frac{(2\pi)^{r_{2}}}{\vol(\Ar(K))\pi i}\int_{\frac{1}{2}-i\infty}^{\frac{1}{2}+i\infty}\Delta_{K}^{\frac{s}{2}}\zeta_{K}(s)\left[\mathcal{M}f_{\rad}\right](sd)\left(\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2})^{r_{1}}\Gamma(s)^{r_{2}}}{2^{sr_{2}}\Gamma(\frac{sd}{2})}\right)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}\hskip 1.30005pts

(67)

Remark 20
.

There is perhaps a more elegant but less explicit version of the error term
\varepsilon(K,f)
. First, define the “Arakelov-Mellin transform” of
f:K_{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
to be

\left[\mathcal{M}_{K}f\right](s)=\int_{K_{\mathbb{R}}}f(x)|\N(x)|^{s-1}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}\hskip 1.30005ptx.

(68)

Then, it turns out that for
f
considered in Theorem
17
and Corollary
19
, we have

\left[\mathcal{M}_{K}f\right](s)=\left[\mathcal{M}f_{\rad}\right](sd)\cdot\left(2(2\pi)^{r_{2}}\frac{\Gamma(\tfrac{s}{2})^{r_{1}}\Gamma(s)^{r_{2}}}{2^{sr_{2}}\Gamma(\tfrac{sd}{2})}\right).

(69)

One can then write that

\varepsilon(K,f)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\frac{1}{2}-i\infty}^{\frac{1}{2}+i\infty}\Delta_{K}^{\frac{s}{2}}\zeta_{K}(s)\left[\mathcal{M}_{K}f\right](s)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}\hskip 1.30005pts.

(70)

Proof.

(of
Corollary

19
)

Using
Theorem

17
and
Lemma

15
, we can write

\int_{\Lambda\in\Ar(K)}\left(\sum_{v\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}}f(v)\right){\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}\hskip 1.30005pt\Lambda}

to be the following for

\frac{(2\pi)^{r_{2}}}{\vol(\Ar(K))\cdot\frac{d}{2}}\frac{d}{2\pi i}\int_{\sigma-i\infty}^{\sigma+i\infty}\Delta_{K}^{\frac{s}{2}}\zeta_{K}(s)\left[\mathcal{M}f_{\rad}\right](sd)\left(\frac{\Gamma(\frac{s}{2})^{r_{1}}\Gamma(s)^{r_{2}}}{2^{sr_{2}}\Gamma(\frac{sd}{2})}\right)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}\hskip 1.30005pts.

(71)

Note that here we interchanged the contour integration with the integration over
\Ar(K)
which is allowed since
\Ar(K)
is compact. Now observe that
f_{r}
is a smooth function and therefore the Mellin transform
\left[\mathcal{M}f_{r}\right]
has excellent decay along the imaginary axis whenever
\sigma

Since the integrand on the right hand side analytically continues to

.

Using Lemma

, we can get the value of this residue to be

\frac{(2\pi)^{r_{2}}}{\frac{d}{2}}\frac{d\pi^{\frac{r_{1}}{2}}}{2^{r_{2}}\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}\left[\mathcal{M}f_{\rad}\right](d)=\frac{d\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+1)}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{>0}}f_{\rad}(t)t^{d-1}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}\hskip 1.30005ptt=\int_{K_{\mathbb{R}}}f(x)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}\hskip 1.30005ptx.

(72)

Here we use the fact that the surface volume of a unit sphere in

d\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}/\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}+1)

2.1
Estimates for gamma factors on the critical line

Define

G(s)=\frac{\Gamma(\tfrac{1}{2}s)^{r_{1}}\Gamma(s)^{r_{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{1}{2}ds)}

(73)

We will prove the following lemma.

Lemma 21
.

For some constant
c_{1}>0
which does not depend on
r_{1},r_{2}
and
t
, we have

\log\left|{G(\tfrac{1}{2}+it)}\right|\leq-\left(\tfrac{1}{4}d\log d-c_{1}d\right)-\tfrac{r_{1}+r_{2}-1}{4}\log\left(t^{2}+\tfrac{1}{4}\right)

(74)

Proof.

We begin with the exact Stirling approximation formula. For
|\arg(s)|<\pi-\varepsilon
, where
\varepsilon>0
, it states

\log\Gamma(s)=\left(s-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\log s-s+\tfrac{1}{2}\log(2\pi)+E(s)

(75)

where

E(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{2\arctan(\frac{x}{s})}{e^{2\pi x}-1}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\hskip-1.49994pt\bar{}\hskip 1.30005ptx.

(76)

Our main interest is in estimating for
s=1/2+it

\Delta(s)=\Re(r_{1}E(s/2)+r_{2}E(s)-E(ds/2)),

(77)

since the real part alone contributes to the absolute value of
G(s)
.

We know that

4
Properties of cyclotomic fields

As promised before, we will derive
Hypothesis

3
,

5
and
11
for the case of
K
being a cyclotomic number field.

Proposition 27
.

The statements of
Hypothesis

3
,
Hypothesis

5
and
Equation

11
are true for
K
varying in the set of cyclotomic number fields.

Proof.

1.

Subconvexity

Since
r_{1}=0
for cyclotomic fields,
\eta_{1}
does not matter.
The following theorem
from
[
17
]
is the best possible known result toward the required subconvexity
according to the knowledge of the authors.
It shows that
\eta_{0},\eta_{2}
arbitrarily close to
\tfrac{1}{12}
are possible.

Theorem 28
.

(Petrow-Young, 2023)

Let
L(s,\chi)
be the Dirichlet
L
-function of primitive character
\chi
with conductor
q=q(\chi)
.
For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a universal constant
C(\varepsilon)>0
independent of
q
or
\chi

such that for every
\chi
and every
t\in\mathbb{R}
one has

|L(\tfrac{1}{2}+it,\chi)|\leq C(\varepsilon)\cdot q^{\frac{1}{6}+\varepsilon}(|t|+1)^{\frac{1}{6}+\varepsilon}.

(115)

Recall the following equalities for an Abelian number field
K
should give us a subconvexity bound for
\zeta_{K}
.

\displaystyle\zeta_{K}(s)=\prod_{\chi:\Gal(K)\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{\times}}L(s,\chi),

(116)

\displaystyle|\Delta_{K}|=\prod_{\chi:\Gal(K)\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{\times}}q(\chi).

(117)

So to get the estimate in Hypothesis
3
, we just multiply
Equation (
115
) across all characters of
\Gal(K)
.

2.

Lower bound on the discriminant

This follows from the following explicit formula for the cyclotomic number field
[
23
]
.

|\Delta_{K}|^{\frac{1}{\varphi(n)}}=\frac{n}{\prod_{p\mid n}p^{\frac{1}{p-1}}}.

(118)

Let
\nu_{p}(n)
be the
p
-adic valuation of
n
.
Then, the left hand side of
Hypothesis

5
follows immediately whereas the right side follows by observing

\displaystyle\log n-\sum_{p\mid n}\frac{\log p}{p-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p\mid n}\left(\nu_{p}(n)-\frac{1}{p-1}\right)\log p.

(119)

We observe that

\nu_{p}(n)-\frac{1}{p-1}\geq\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}\nu_{p}(n)&\text{if }p\neq 2\\
\frac{1}{2}\nu_{p}(n)-1&\text{if }p=2,\end{cases}

(120)

which gives the lower bounds

\sum_{p\mid n}\left(\nu_{p}(n)-\frac{1}{p-1}\right)\log p\geq\frac{1}{2}\log n-\log 2\gg\log n.

(121)

Hence, we establish that

\frac{\log\Delta_{K}}{\deg K}\rightarrow\infty

(122)

as required.

3.

Lower bound on residues of Dedekind zeta function

Since cyclotomic number fields are normal (Galois, in particular), one can use the Brauer-Siegel theorem to justify that

Equation

11
is true. In fact, one can make more precise asymptotics due to the effective version of the Brauer-Siegel theorem due to Stark
[
19
]
.

Theorem 29
.

(Stark, 1974)

Let
K
be a normal extension of
\mathbb{Q}
. Then

\Res_{s=1}\zeta_{K}(s)\geq\frac{c_{1}}{\deg K\cdot|\Delta_{K}|^{\frac{1}{\deg K}}}\text{ for some }c_{1}>0.

(123)

In
[
5
]
, the authors report that
c_{1}=0.001448
seems to work for Equation (
123
).
We then simply combine
Theorem

29
with the estimate in
Hypothesis

5
to get Hypothesis
11

using the bound

\Res_{s=1}\zeta_{K}(s)\geq c_{1}\Delta_{K}^{-c/\deg K}

for any fixed
c>2
and all sufficiently large
K
.

Since the hypotheses of

Corollary 30

Theorem
