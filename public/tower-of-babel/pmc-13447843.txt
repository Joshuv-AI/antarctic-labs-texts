An uncertainty-aware evaluation framework based on hierarchical vision transformers for robust cross-domain plant leaf disease classification
Authors: Tripti Shrivastava, Ayush Kumar Agrawal, Abhinav Shukla, Parul Dubey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlant leaf disease detection is a critical task in precision agriculture, where reliable diagnosis under real-world conditions is essential for reducing crop losses and supporting timely intervention. Although deep learning models have achieved high classification accuracy, their performance often degrades under domain shift between controlled laboratory datasets and real-field environments, while predictive uncertainty and confidence calibration remain largely unaddressed.This study presents an uncertainty-aware cross-domain evaluation framework based on a Hierarchical Vision Transformer (HViT) for plant leaf disease classification. The framework integrates multi-scale feature learning with Monte Carlo Dropout-based predictive uncertainty estimation and temperature-based calibration to systematically analyze model behavior in terms of accuracy, reliability, and robustness. Experiments were conducted on two complementary datasets: the New Plant Diseases Dataset (controlled conditions) and the PlantDoc dataset (field conditions), enabling bidirectional cross-domain evaluation. Results demonstrate that the proposed framework achieves superior performance, attaining 97.8% accuracy on controlled data and 93.6% on field data, while significantly improving calibration with lower Expected Calibration Error (ECE = 0.032 / 0.041), reduced Negative Log-Likelihood, and lower Brier score compared to baseline CNN and transformer models. Furthermore, the framework exhibits improved robustness under domain shift, with reduced performance degradation and stable uncertainty behavior. Overall, this study highlights the importance of integrating uncertainty estimation and calibration within a hierarchical transformer-based framework, providing a more reliable and deployment-ready solution for real-world agricultural disease diagnosis.Keywords: Hierarchical vision transformer, Bayesian predictive calibration, Plant leaf disease classification, Uncertainty estimation, Cross-domain generalizationSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Agriculture contributes nearly 4% of global GDP and supports the livelihoods of more than 2.5 billion people worldwide, while crop diseases are estimated to cause 20–40% annual yield losses, translating into economic damages exceeding USD 220 billion per year1,2. Leaf-based plant diseases are among the most visually detectable and economically destructive threats in crop production systems3. Automated image-based diagnosis has therefore emerged as a statistically scalable solution, where deep learning models attempt to approximate a function , mapping high-dimensional image input to discrete disease class labels . However, conventional deterministic classifiers provide only point estimates , without quantifying predictive uncertainty , which is critical in high-stakes agricultural decision-making. Furthermore, empirical studies show that classification accuracy can decline by 10–25% under domain shift, particularly when models trained on controlled laboratory images are deployed in real-field environments4,5.

In this study, we adopt a framework-oriented approach, where a hierarchical vision transformer backbone is used within an uncertainty-aware evaluation pipeline to analyze performance, calibration, and robustness under domain shift. In recent years, with the rapid development of deep learning techniques, plant disease classification accuracy has been greatly improved3. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) achieve favorable performance on widely-used benchmark dataset, surpassing 95% accuracy in the controlled setting6. Nevertheless, CNN structures are locally receptive fields designed based, making use of the convolutional kernels for obtaining spatially-limited features. Although these architectures have shown excellent capability for texture-based disease identification, they might perform poorly in modeling long-range dependencies and global leaf deformations based on the level of diseases. Hierarchical Vision Transformers (ViTs), such as Swin Transformers, mitigate this limitation by learning self-attention that captures global semantic relationships among patches residing on all levels of different scales in an image. Hierarchical Vision Transformers additionally improve upon this by hierarchically aggregating patches, such that the model can learn across multi-scale representations – a necessary characteristic to be capable of modelling both localized lesion characteristics and large scale morphological deformations.

However, two bottleneck problems are still open in plant disease classification. First, the vast majority of existing works are trained on single domain databases captured under controlled illumination and background clutter and perform poorly when tested on field-acquired images experiencing different lighting conditions, occlusions and background clutters. Second, the existing models work in a deterministic setting with high-confidence predictions for obscure or out-of-distribution samples. Overconfidence of this kind may result in wrong agronomic operations, economic loss and to the lack of trust for AI-based tools working inside agricultural systems. Thus, strong cross-domain generalization and uncertainty-aware prediction capabilities are crucial for real-world deployment.

To this end, we utilize a Hierarchical Vision Transformer integrated with Bayesian predictive calibration within a unified evaluation framework. The model is based on a hierarchical transformer backbone to extract multi-scale features, and a Bayesian predictive layer for the epistemic uncertainty estimation based on the stochastic inference. The central problem addressed in this study is not only to improve classification accuracy, but to evaluate how models behave under domain shift, particularly in terms of prediction confidence, calibration, and uncertainty. This requires a framework that simultaneously analyzes performance, reliability, and robustness across controlled and real-field datasets. Despite significant progress in plant disease classification, existing studies typically address only isolated aspects such as classification accuracy, transformer-based representation learning, or uncertainty estimation. The cross-domain generalization, predictive uncertainty quantification, and confidence calibration have been barely considered simultaneously, especially in the context of hierarchical transformer-based models. This lack of integration restricts the applicability of models to the real-field context where domain shift and decision uncertainty are relevant factors.

Firstly, in contrast to earlier research that mostly emphasized the enhancement of classification accuracy in a single-domain setting, this paper is framed as a cross-domain assessment framework of plant disease classification. The goal is not to suggest a new learning paradigm, but to explore hierarchical transformer-based representations, predictive uncertainty estimation, and calibration mechanisms in a systematic study and how they all make robust model behavior when shifting to a different domain. Combining these elements into a single experimental framework, this study will focus on robustness, reliability, and deployment readiness, which have not been explored in the agricultural image classification literature. Instead of suggesting a radically novel deep learning architecture, this study aims to develop a unified evaluation system to jointly learn hierarchical features, estimate uncertainty, and analyze calibration to achieve strong cross-domain performance evaluation. Literature review

The study of plant disease classification has evolved significantly with advancements in deep learning and computer vision. Recent approaches have focused on improving model accuracy, but challenges remain in handling domain shift, uncertainty, and calibration for reliable field deployment.

Plant leaf disease classification in precision agriculture

Automated identification of plant leaf disease is promising in precision agriculture and can response effectively, efficiently and scalably to the large scale monitoring of crops7. The AI-based diagnostic system is able to provide quick and real-time diagnosis, and it can effectively help stop the spread of diseases while also reducing crop losses8 since that the traditional methods requiring visual inspection by practicians or a lab test take time being subjective errors. Deep learning models (e.g., Convolutional Neural Networks as CNNs and attention-based transformers) have shown superiority in classification by utilizing discriminative patterns learned from large-scale corpus9. A maximum yield loss of 40% has been estimated for all major crops and result in enormous economic losses, >$220 billion per annum worldwide, and food security represents a huge risk with the increase in global population coupled with increasing demand for human food10. As a result, automated detection systems contribute to improve productivity and promote sustainable agriculture by allowing intervention in time and reducing chemical overdosage11. Paradigm shift of these human-eye-based-to AI-driven systems, computer vision techniques and data driven decisions could help in large-scale control, better reliability and improved robustness of new generation agriculture.

Traditional machine learning approaches for plant leaf disease prediction

Conventional modes of plant leaf disease prediction based on machine learning are mainly based on handcrafted feature extraction methods in conjunction with the classical classifiers12,13. The features related to the image are calculated for the demonstration of the patterns of disease, such as color histogram, statistical color texture (GLCM, SIFT, etc.), and shape, and so on. Classifiers such as Support Vector Machines (SVM), Random Forest (RF), and k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN) use these handcrafted features14,15. Due to high reliability of a multiparametric space and its ability to hard classify, support vector machine (SVM) models have been widely adopted. The latter case is the Random Forest model that is more generalized whereas k-NN is a simple but relatively good classifier in smaller datasets.

These techniques are promising in controlled laboratory, but they are cripplingly limited when used in the field16. The varying lighting, background-disorder, occlusions and leaf-orientation affect the strength of features and model-dependability. The class-imbalance of field data also bias predictions towards large classes. Moreover, manual engineered features are not dynamic to the complicated and heterogeneous disease patterns, which limits cross-domain generalization. Consequently, there is often a decrease in performance of classical machine learning approaches when applied to the real world of agriculture. Yet, these methods can only be used in a controlled setting and cannot be used to handle cross-domain variability, predictive uncertainty and confidence calibration so they are less applicable to real-field applications.

Deep learning with convolutional neural networks

Deep learning with Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs)17 has been a significant step forward in plant leaf disease detection. It has been demonstrated that networks such as ResNet, EfficientNet and DenseNet, have good classification performance due to the deep hierarchical feature extracting process18. Using the method of residual connections ResNet solves the gradient vanishing issues to allow deeper and more stable dense networks19. Applying the search with evolutionary neural architecture and network design that is efficient to optimize balance between the depth, width, resolution and performance of the network20. The dense connectivity of DenseNet enables better feature reuse while it solves the vanishing-gradient issue which affects image classification performance.

The CNN-based models demonstrated accuracy rates exceeding 98 to 99% when tested on the PlantVillage benchmark datasets which assess their performance under controlled testing conditions21. The two architectures enable us to achieve strong feature learning while our transfer learning and data augmentation efforts permit us to obtain good generalization results. The CNN models exhibit excessive confidence in their predictions when they encounter clean lab images and dogs from field images. The agricultural application of real-world systems faces two challenges because of domain shift which creates performance degradation and requires advanced computing power while small datasets provide unreliable results. The controlled datasets show high accuracy for CNN-based models but these systems display overconfidence with no ability to estimate uncertainty while their performance drops during domain shifts which makes them unsuitable for real-world farming operations.

Vision transformers in agricultural image analysis

Plant leaf disease prediction shows high popularity for Vision Transformers because of their advanced self-attention system which extracts complete image information. ViTs use a different approach than local convolutional filter based CNNs because they divide an image into patches and examine all connections between patches together with their joint examination22,23. The self-attention mechanism enables the system to understand both global long-distance dependencies and complex spatial patterns which are necessary to identify the subtle disease patterns on leaf surfaces24. The system represents features in a global manner that enables it to identify visually similar disease categories25.

The initial ViT system requires quadratic time consumption because it processes image patches which makes it impossible to use with high-resolution farming photos26,27. Hierarchical transformer models, e.g. to counter this limitation. Swin Transformer implements window-based attention through its shifted window system28. This method enables effective descent which retains both local and global aspects of data according to research findings in29. The hierarchical architecture enables extraction of multiple feature scales which proves essential for modeling plant leaf lesions that exhibit various size and texture characteristics according to research from30.

The empirical research shows that transformer-based models exceed CNNs in term of cross-domain generalization because they show greater strength in handling different tasks31. The technology functions properly with plant disease detection because it combines global attention with multi-scale representation through its aggregation capabilities. Transformer-based models create better global context understanding which improves generalization but existing methods still function as fixed systems which lack uncertainty estimation and calibration capabilities and they have not been tested for cross-domain performance.

Base paper review

The base paper introduces a deep learning framework which classifies plant leaf diseases through a deterministic hierarchical vision architecture that operates on controlled image datasets32. The study aims to increase classification accuracy by using better feature extraction methods together with improved network architecture design. The model functions under deterministic conditions because it excludes Bayesian uncertainty estimation and it does not use calibration metrics like Expected Calibration Error (ECE) or Negative Log-Likelihood (NLL) or Brier Score to assess prediction confidence. Furthermore, the study does not investigate model robustness under domain shift between controlled and real-field conditions.

In contrast, the present work extends beyond architectural design by introducing an uncertainty-aware evaluation framework that systematically analyzes cross-domain performance, predictive uncertainty, and calibration. This not only makes the study an advancement with regard to evaluation and reliability analysis but also not just a suggestion of a different model architecture. Nevertheless, it does not collectively look at cross-domain robustness, uncertainty estimation, and calibration, which are paramount to real-world deployment. Dataset description

We experimentally verify our hierarchical vision transformer with Bayesian predictive calibration framework on two publicly available datasets of plant leaf diseases both in a controlled laboratory and in the real-world scenario.

New plant diseases dataset (controlled environment dataset)

New Plant Diseases Dataset is a publicly available benchmark dataset on Kaggle, which is a large-scale dataset extensively utilized in plant leaf disease classification research33. It contains approximately 87,000 RGB images that are annotated by 38 disease classes in various crop species including tomato, potato, corn, grape and apple. The images were collected in a lab setup, with constant background, same lighting over the spread and low noise. There is a label for each image at the class level and the images are resized to a standardized size for deep learning training. The dataset is not heavily biased across classes and offers a stable training distribution and thus is suitable for supervised representation learning. Nevertheless, because it is collected in a controlled acquisition setup, the domain variability of it is small, and therefore can decrease its generalization performance when testing on real-field agricultural applications.

PlantDoc dataset (field condition dataset)

The PlantDoc dataset is a real natural field-based plant disease dataset34. It is an image-based single plant diseased dataset which consists of about 2500 RGB images for 17 plant species and covers altogether with 27 disease categories as well as multiple types of tomato leaf diseases. As opposed to lab-based collect images of PlantDoc contain complex backgrounds, natural illumination changes, occlusions and scale variation as well as camera viewpoint variations. Annotations are done at the image level, and certain versions of this dataset al.so include annotations of bounding boxes for disease localisation tasks. Due to its high intra-class variability and environmental noise, PlantDoc represents a domain distribution significantly different from controlled datasets. This renders it exceptionally appropriate in the context of assessing cross-domain generalization, robustness, and predictive calibration when applied in actual agricultural scenarios.

This emphasises the need to integrate uncertainty estimation and calibration mechanisms since it will enable the model to capture more of the prediction confidence, and remain reliable in real-life situations involving agriculture.

Uncertainty-aware evaluation framework

This part will present the recommended uncertainty-conscious evaluation framework of the cross-domain plant leaf disease classification. The framework combines a hierarchical vision transformer backbone with predictive uncertainty estimation and calibration mechanisms, which allow one to systematically analyse the behaviour of the model in the case of domain shift. It is concerned with assessing the role of these components in accuracy, reliability, and robustness under realistic agricultural conditions. Figure 3 depicts the whole cross-domain learning process that involves the data set preparation (controlled and field images), pre-processing and augmentation, hierarchical vision transformer-based features, Bayesian inference with MC Dropout to estimate uncertainty, predictive calibration to enable credible decision-making in domain shift settings.

Framework for uncertainty-aware cross-domain evaluation using hierarchical vision transformers.

The suggested scheme adheres to the systematic flow beginning with the input preprocessing up to the final prediction. The input leaf plant image is initially resized and normalized and then subdivided into fixed-size patches and encoded into feature representations. Such embeddings undergo hierarchical transformer layers to learn multi-scale features. In inference, dropout is used to support stochastic forward passes, which can be used to approximate the posterior predictive distribution. To determine the probability of classes and uncertainty, predictive mean and variance are calculated. This is then followed by temperature scaling to scale confidence scores and finally a prediction and uncertainty estimates are generated to be used to do trustful cross-domain evaluation.

In this work, Monte Carlo Dropout is chosen to be used as a computationally efficient practical way to approximate the Bayesian inference in deep neural networks. MC Dropout has much lower computational complexity than other uncertainty estimation methods (including deep ensembles, variational inference, and fully Bayesian neural networks), and can be trained without structural changes to existing transformer-based systems. This renders it especially applicable to agricultural data, where computational power can be constrained and training large-scale ensembles can be prohibitive. Moreover, MC Dropout allows to make assumptions on epistemic uncertainty using repeated stochastic forward passes, which is a trustworthy approximation of model confidence, yet remains scaleable. Even more advanced methods of Bayesian are theoretically better guaranteed, but were not used in this work because of more training complexity, higher memory demands and lower applicability to real-world deployment situations.

Experimental setup

This experimental setup is sound to examine the performance of the proposed Hierarchical Vision Transformer within the proposed evaluation framework under both in-domain and cross-domain settings. Two types of public datasets collected from controlled laboratory and real-field agricultural environments were used for the diagnosis of domain shift robustness. All images were rescaled to 224 × 224 × 3, normalized with ImageNet statistics, and augmented with controlled geometric as well as photometric transformations for better generalizability. Stratified sampling was implemented and used to maintain the class distribution among training, validation, and testing sets. Domain robustness was assessed through a bidirectional cross-domain protocol, i.e., training on controlled and testing on field data as well as the reverse.The backbone of the hierarchical vision transformer (Swin-T variant) was initialized by ImageNet-pretrained weights. Bayesian predictive modelling was done using Monte Carlo dropout at inference time by conducting stochastic forward passes per sample to approximate the posterior predictive distribution. Final classification was performed using predictive mean and epistemic uncertainty with predictive variance. We measured the reliability of calibration using ECE, NLL, and the Brier score. The model was optimized with the AdamW optimizer with cosine annealing learning rate scheduling. Overfitting was prevented by early stopping based on the validation loss. All experiments were implemented in a GPU environment to make sure the efficiency of computations. Results and discussion

In this section, we present a detailed analysis of the proposed HVT-BPC. The ablation studies are organized in the form of processing diagnostics, in-domain performance, cross-domain robustness, ablation comparison analysis, statistical validation study and comparative benchmarking and comparisons with paper.

Preprocessing diagnostics, outlier detection, and model fit

The quality and distributional properties of the data were pre-assessed prior to training. Outliers were observed in feature space using Mahalanobis distance, and model fit was confirmed via the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). Limitations and future work

Though the performance of the proposed framework is satisfactory and substantially robust to cross-domain evaluation, it is evaluated on two publicly accessible datasets and still has its space that may be validated in a large-scale and more diverse real-field agricultural scenario. In the future, we will consider more lightweight model optimization, such as multimodal fusion (e.g., environmental data and soil data) and large-scale field deployment, to further improve the generalized practicability of our method.

Limitations

Despite the competitive performance of the Bayesian HViT on controlled and field datasets, it is limited to public benchmark datasets for evaluation. Nevertheless, these datasets cannot satisfy all the real-world variations, such as strong lighting variations, occlusions, mixed infections, and complex background noise that may appear in practical agricultural use cases. Although Monte Carlo Dropout improves uncertainty estimation, it adds computational load on inference that may limit deployment in resource-constrained (low-power) edge devices. In addition, the presented work is primarily dedicated to image-based diagnosis and does not integrate contextual agricultural indicators like weather conditions, soil type or crop developmental stage. Class imbalance in the rare disease categories is still an issue despite better calibration.

Future work

Future studies will focus on a large-scale field validation in different environments in order to increase generalizability. Efficient Bayesian approximations and low-cost model compression methods (e.g. pruning, quantization, knowledge distillation) will be also investigated to make possible real-time edge deployment. The incorporation of explainable AI techniques will contribute to increasing interpretability for agronomists and farmers. Furthermore, multimodal learning techniques integrating visual, weather and sensor data will be explored in order to increase robustness. Semi-supervised and incremental learning method will be employed as well to make the model generalize for unseen plant diseases.

Real-world implications

The proposed model has significant real-world applications in precision agriculture. By enabling smartphone-based diagnosis, it offers farmers a powerful decision support tool for early disease detection, potentially improving crop yield and reducing pesticide use. The model’s ability to provide uncertainty estimates ensures safer AI deployment in agriculture, allowing farmers to make informed decisions. When the model’s confidence is low, uncertainty triggers an expert review, preventing incorrect diagnoses that could lead to poor decision-making. This feature enhances the model’s reliability and promotes its safe integration into fieldwork. Furthermore, the ability to reject low-confidence predictions ensures that the system only provides actionable insights, reducing the risks of false positives in critical agricultural contexts.

Conclusion

We presented a Bayesian predictive calibration Hierarchical Vision Transformer for cross-domain plant leaf disease classification in this paper. It can well capture both local and global visual patterns by the use of hierarchical attention, and incorporate uncertainty estimation for improving its reliability in real-world situations. Experiments on Controlled and Field datasets show that our approach leads to better accuracy, calibration (lower ECE, NLL, Brier scores), and consistent uncertainty behavior than the typical CNNs and vanilla ViT baselines. This study does not aim to introduce a fundamentally new deep learning architecture, but rather demonstrates the importance of combining hierarchical representation learning with uncertainty estimation and calibration within a cross-domain evaluation framework. The results show that such integration significantly improves model reliability and robustness, which are critical for real-world agricultural deployment.

The experimental results (qualitative analysis) also verify that the presented method can be applied in-field under various backgrounds and illumination, which makes the proposed approach practical for precision agricultural applications. Integrating transformer-based feature representation with uncertain probability estimation, the suggested model increases the prediction accuracy of crop classification and the confidence of farmers. Overall, we present a new deep learning model of plant disease diagnosis with any-device-anywhere and time-of-arrival completion that may be utilized to complement the future generation of intelligent crop monitoring systems. This paper discusses how it is crucial to consider deep learning models not only by their accuracy but also by their uncertainty and calibration in a single cross-domain context to enable real-world use of AI in agriculture.
