MiRNA-based drugs: challenges and delivery strategies
Authors: Ziyu Wang, Yingyuan Peng, Haifeng Zhou, Min Zhang, Dianwen Ju, Zhilong Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe miRNA-based therapeutics held great promise for the treatment of diseases associated with aberrant gene expression. However, the development of miRNA-based drugs still faces many obstacles, including stability, targetability, tissue penetration, and induction of immune responses. To overcome these challenges, researchers developed various miRNA modification methods and miRNA-based delivery systems, which can protect miRNA from degradation and facilitate their transport across biological barriers. Here, we give an overview of the latest advancements in a variety of delivery systems, including virus, lipid, polymer, inorganic, and exosome-based nanosystems, which have been proved as versatile and valuable carriers for miRNA. Moreover, the use of stimuli-responsive materials allows for the controlled release of oligonucleotides in response to specific triggers. Importantly, the current miRNA-based therapeutics in clinical trials are summarized in this review. The combination of these advancements has the potential to promote more effective and safer treatment designs for a wide range of diseases. This review will contribute to developing more precise targeted delivery systems of miRNA, ultimately facilitating its clinical development.Graphical Abstract Keywords: MicroRNA-based therapy, Delivery systems, Stimuli-responsive materials, Chemical modification

Introduction

MicroRNA (miRNA), a class of single-stranded non-coding RNA molecules, has garnered great attention in the field of genetic therapy nowadays. Its ability to fine-tune gene expression makes it a promising candidate for therapeutic interventions in a variety of diseases, including immune disorders, cardiovascular diseases, and cancers (Moraes et al. 2021). In 1993, Ambros and his colleagues made a groundbreaking discovery by identifying the first miRNA gene, lin-4, in Caenorhabditis elegans, which provided more therapeutic opportunities for nucleotides (Lee et al. 1993).

MiRNA and siRNA represent two major classes of RNAs that are extensively used for gene silencing. These two types of RNAs share similarities in their physicochemical properties, yet they exhibit distinct functions and mechanisms of action. SiRNA is highly specific and only targets a single mRNA; however, miRNA targets and regulates multiple mRNAs. To initiate RNA interference, siRNA should be fully complementary to its target mRNA, while miRNA binds to multiple mRNAs with partial complementarity to suppress the expression (Kleaveland 2023; Zare M. et al. 2022). Additionally, the differences between siRNA and miRNA in mechanisms are as follows: siRNA effectively cleaves mRNA, whereas miRNA suppresses or replaces the translation of mRNA. As a result, their design requirements and therapeutic applications differ significantly. SiRNA works through antisense therapy, whereas miRNA is employed for therapeutic applications in two ways: antisense therapy and replacement therapy (Huang et al. 2021). Antisense therapy is utilized when the target genes are overexpressed. In contrast, when miRNA is downregulated or deficient, miRNA (called miRNA mimics) can be introduced into the diseased cells to restore expression levels similar to those observed in healthy cells and mimic the function of target miRNA (Quan et al. 2018; Song et al. 2019).

When it comes to oncology, MRX34, the first miRNA-based therapy approach, has been under clinical testing since 2013. MRX34 formulated by liposomes can restore the activity of depleted miR-34 on the p53/wnt cellular pathways (Kelnar et al. 2014). The therapeutic miRNA agents have not yet been applied to clinical practice, but some have entered the clinical research stage. For example, a study was conducted to assess the safety and tolerability of multiple doses of Miravirsen (miR-122) in patients with chronic hepatitis C infection in September 2010. The current miRNA-based therapeutics in clinical trials are summarized in this review in Table 1. The biggest challenge for miRNA agents entering clinical practice is that naked miRNAs cannot be delivered to specific and targeted tissues without causing harm to the body. This review will summarize miRNA-based therapeutics in clinical trials and highlight the recent advances of miRNA mechanisms, delivery challenges, categories of delivery systems, and stimuli-responsive materials.

Mechanism of miRNA

MiRNA biogenesis is a complex process which begins with the transcription of miRNA genes by RNA polymerase II. The initial miRNA transcript undergoes processing by Drosha and DGCR8 to produce pre-miRNA, which will convert to mature miRNA duplex in the cytoplasm (Wu 2020; Zhao et al. 2022). The duplex then interacts with the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC), where one strand is selected as the guide strand to target mRNA for degradation (Jeong et al. 2023). MiRNA regulates gene expression by binding to complementary sequences in the 3′untranslated region of target mRNA (Qin et al. 2016). MiRNA plays important roles in diverse biological processes, and aberrant miRNA expression has been associated with a variety of diseases, including immune disorders, cardiovascular diseases, and cancers. Therefore, understanding the mechanisms of miRNA biogenesis and function is crucial for developing effective therapeutic strategies for these diseases.

Challenges in miRNA-based drug delivery

Despite the remarkable advantages of miRNA in cancer treatment, it is essential to acknowledge that the clinical application of miRNA has encountered some obstacles that need to be overcome.

Instability of the naked miRNA

The inherent instability of miRNA in blood is a barrier to miRNA-based treatment. Nucleases, particularly ribonucleases (RNase), quickly break down unmodified miRNA with an unaltered 2′OH. Moreover, due to fast renal excretion clearance, the half-life of naked miRNA is relatively short in circulation, lasting for several minutes to one hour (Xu et al. 2021). To overcome these obstacles, various chemical modifications are introduced to increase the stability and functionality of miRNA.

One approach involves the modification of the 2′ hydroxyl group. 2′O-Methyl and 2′O-(2-methoxyethyl) modifications significantly increased miRNA stability by preventing RNase A degradation (Lennox and Behlke 2011; Li S et al. 2023). Additionally, locked nucleic acid (LNA) has been developed to improve the binding affinity of miRNA and RISC (Kumar et al. 2014). These chemical modifications not only enhance the stability of miRNA but also improve their pharmacokinetic properties, thereby enhancing the therapeutic potential of miRNA.

Poor tissue penetration

The primary hurdle in miRNA therapy lies in achieving precise targeted delivery with effective penetration, which hinges on the capability to traverse cell membranes. This challenge arises due to the hydrophilic nature and negative charge of miRNAs, resulting in charge repulsion and limited permeability (Ban et al. 2019). Protective nanocarriers enable precise delivery of therapeutic cargo to the target organs with valid diffusion.

Hurdle of the escape of miRNA from endosomes

The endocytic route serves as the primary uptake mechanism for miRNA. MiRNA becomes trapped within endosomes and is degraded by a variety of nucleases in the lysosome, where the pH ranges from 5 to 6 (Wang Y et al. 2020). Consequently, a crucial aspect of achieving a successful miRNA-based therapy lies in promoting endosomal escape and ensuring the cytosolic delivery of therapeutics. The viruses and synthetic biomimetic peptides, which utilize different mechanisms to penetrate the membranes of their target cells, facilitate the endosomal escape and exhibit minimal levels of pathogenicity and toxicity.

Induction of innate immune responses by miRNA

The host system can recognize single-stranded RNAs as pathogens, which activate the innate immune system (Zhang Z et al. 2021). Following miRNA injection into the body, significant production of inflammatory cytokines may occur, along with the activation of the type I interferon (IFN) pathway via Toll-like receptors (TLRs) (Sharma et al. 2022). Another factor to take into account is the toxicity associated with delivery carriers that possess extremely positive charges. When nanoparticles enter the body, they are recognized as foreign entities and can trigger immune responses. One approach to reducing immune reactions is to modify the surface of the nanoparticles to make them less immunogenic (Bronevetsky and Ansel 2013). This can be achieved through coating the particles with biocompatible materials such as polymers or lipids. The combination of miRNA-based therapies with nanotechnology offers great promise for the treatment of various diseases. Nevertheless, more research is required to fully understand interactions between nanoparticles and the immune system, as well as to optimize delivery methods for maximum therapeutic efficacy with minimal side effects.

Off-target effect of miRNA

MiRNA regulates gene expression through binding to the 3′untranslated region (3′UTR) of target mRNA under normal conditions. However, it has been found that miRNA can also bind to the 5′untranslated region (5′UTR) or coding sequence (CDS), resulting in off-target effects (Diener et al. 2022). This can result in various unwanted biological outcomes, including the regulation of cell proliferation, apoptosis, differentiation, and other processes (Singh et al. 2011). The mechanism of miRNA off-target effects is not fully understood; however, it is believed that miRNA can bind to similar sequences in the 3′UTR, 5′UTR, or CDS of target mRNA, leading to the regulation of these genes (Ishida and Selaru 2013). The identification and characterization of miRNA off-target effects are important for the recognition of the biological functions of miRNA as well as their potential therapeutic applications. However, it is also challenging because miRNA regulates a great number of genes simultaneously, and the biological consequences of miRNA off-target effects can be subtle and difficult to detect.

Delivery strategies of miRNA-based drugs

Chemically modified miRNA

Although the chemical modifications do not belong to delivery vehicles of miRNA, they do act a crucial role in strengthening its own inherent properties of miRNA. The stability, circulation half-life, biodistribution profile, and cellular uptake can be modulated through the application of suitable chemical modifications. These modifications not only enhance the delivery efficiency but also minimize the potential toxicity associated with miRNA. Thus, by understanding these modifications, we can better design miRNA delivery vectors that can effectively deliver miRNA to target cells, leading to more effective gene silencing and therapeutic outcomes.

In general, there are several types of chemical modifications commonly used in miRNA drug development: sugar ring modifications, 3′ modifications, terminal modifications, and non-nucleotide modifications (Jeyaram et al. 2020). Up to now, sugar ring modifications, including 2′-O-methyl (2′-OMe), 2′-O-methoxyethyl (2′-MOE), 2′-fluoro, and locked nucleic acid (LNA), have been widely applied (Lennox et al. 2017). Several commonly used methods for sugar ring modifications are shown in Fig. 1. The modification of the sugar ring can enhance anti-miRNA oligonucleotides (AMO) specificity, achieve high binding affinity, and superior stability. MiRNA 221 and miRNA 222, chemically modified by 2′-OMe-phosphorothioate, have been demonstrated to be efficient inhibitors of KIT gene expression, exhibiting superior binding affinity and cellular uptake potency, which increased stability in rat plasma (Di Martino et al. 2016). In comparison to 2′-OMe oligonucleotides, the anti-miRNA activity of 2′-MOE oligonucleotides, which provide miRNA-based drugs with enhanced nuclease resistance and binding affinity, is significantly superior (Li X et al. 2022; Paloviita et al. 2021). While medical modification enhances the effectiveness of miRNA delivery, it should be noted that the nucleotides produced by the degradation of naked miRNA are identical to those intrinsic to the human body. In contrast, many of the chemical modifiers used are not native to the human system.

Viral-based vectors

Viral delivery systems have emerged as powerful tools for the efficient and precise delivery of miRNA drugs offering improved therapeutic outcomes. There are several types of viral vectors, including retroviral vector, adenoviruses vector, and adeno-associated viral vector.

The majority of retroviral vectors have primarily concentrated on gamma-retroviruses and lentiviruses. Gamma-retroviral vectors cannot be transduced in non-dividing cells, which limits their application in miRNA-targeted delivery to some extent (Savenkova et al. 2022). However, lentiviral vectors can address this challenge as they have the ability to transduce both dividing and non-dividing cells. Lentiviral vector is developed from human immunodeficiency virus type 1 (HIV-1), which is safe for the human body, owing to the genetic engineering techniques (Herrera-Carrillo et al. 2017). The lentiviral expression vector deletes the majority of HIV virus genes responsible for virus replication. Thus, lentiviral carriers retain functional capability and lose replicative ability, rendering their low immunogenicity (Reid et al. 2017; Sun et al. 2014). Li and colleagues successfully transferred miR-519a in glioblastoma employing lentiviral vectors to treat a xenograft model (Li H et al. 2018).

Adenoviruses are outstanding carriers of miRNA therapy due to their non-pathogenicity, minimal immunogenicity, high transduction efficiency, and broad tropism. MiR-34a with adenoviruses impairs CD15 +/CD133 + tumor progression as well as metastasis, and supports neural differentiation in medulloblastoma (De Antonellis et al. 2011). Nevertheless, adenovirus vectors face a problem as humans constantly encounter various new serotypes of adeno-associated viruses, producing antibodies to eliminate these vectors (Yao et al. 2014). To solve this problem, the immunogenic epitopes of adenovirus vectors can be modified, or more rare capsid serotypes can be used.

Despite their promise, viral delivery systems for miRNA drugs face challenges such as immune responses and potential toxicity. Ongoing research aims to improve vector safety and specificity while reducing immunogenicity.

Lipid-based vectors

Lipid-based miRNA delivery systems include solid lipid nanoparticles (SLN) and liposomes, including anionic and cationic liposomes. It is essential to consider the biodistribution of lipids following systemic administration. The liver is a major organ for the clearance of circulating lipids, as it is a highly perfused organ with abundant Kupffer cells that phagocytose foreign particles (Guilliams and Scott 2022). Therefore, liver accumulation of lipid-based SLN and liposomes is expected. However, through precise control of particle size, surface charge, and coating ligands, it may be possible to optimize the pharmacokinetics of lipid-based miRNA delivery systems to achieve preferential accumulation in tumor tissues while minimizing off-target effects (Zhang W et al. 2015).

SLN have such advantages as the simplicity of passing through cell membranes, compatibility, and high transfection efficiency. It is worth noting that one siRNA drug approved by the FDA for the therapy of Ebola is delivered by SLN. Experiments showed that the addition of oleic acid to SLN carriers remarkably improved the delivery potency of miR-122, which is a liver-specific microRNA (Wang X et al. 2013). Moreover, the researchers developed a novel dual-drug delivery system for microRNA-34a and paclitaxel, which were loaded into SLN. The dual drug delivery can better target lung cancer and inhibit tumor growth in vivo (Shi et al. 2014).

Liposomes offer tunable drug release properties that can be tailored to specific applications. The miRNA can be loaded into the liposomes in a stable form and released in a controlled manner upon reaching the target site. This allows for spatial, temporal, or pH control over miRNA delivery, ensuring that the maximum therapeutic effect is achieved with minimal side effects. The researchers encapsulated 2′-OMe and phosphorothioate modified miR-122 with a pH-sensitive lipid, which exhibited superior activity in liver cancer cells compared to Lipofectamine™ 2000 due to its efficient endosomal escape mechanism (Hatakeyama et al. 2014). To solve the problem of targeted delivery, a novel liposome reformed with an aptamer was introduced for the delivery of miR-29b targeted to ovarian cancer, which showed a potential new platform to improve the therapeutic outcome in ovarian cancers (Jiang et al. 2020).

Polymer-based vectors

The cationic polymer-based nanoparticles are one of the most extensively utilized non-viral gene delivery systems, primarily because of the simplicity in synthesis, high biocompatibility, and amenability to tunable physicochemical modifications to meet therapeutic requirements (Ahmadi et al. 2023). These polymers form complexes with miRNA through ionic interactions, protecting them from degradation and enabling efficient cellular uptake. Cationic polymers are divided into natural polymers (peptides, proteins, and polysaccharides) and synthetic forms (polyethyleneimines (PEI), Poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA), and PAMAM dendrimers) (Mehta et al. 2021; Rahbek et al. 2010).

The PEI has emerged as a promising candidate due to its high cationic charge density and ability to form stable complexes with nucleic acids. The miRNA-145 acts as a tumor suppressor, suppressing the proto-oncogene Pim-1. PEI/miR-145 complexes were injected into mice and resulted in a significant reduction in tumor growth (50%) (Ibrahim et al. 2011). However, PEI-based vectors have also been associated with some toxic effects and limited transfection efficiency in certain cell types. Therefore, further research is needed to optimize PEI-based vectors and improve their safety and efficiency for gene delivery applications. The PEGylation of PEI not only reduces its toxicity but also enhances its solubility and stability. The chitosan-PEI conjugate combines the advantages of PEI (gene-transfection ability) and chitosan (biocompatibility), making it an ideal gene vector for gene delivery (Shao et al. 2017).

PLGA can effectively shield miRNA from degrading by nucleases, ensuring a sustained release of the cargo (Shams et al. 2022). PLGA-based miRNA delivery systems have several advantages, which render them particularly well suited for therapeutic applications. Firstly, the products of PLGA are lactic acid and glycolic acid, which means that PLGA is biocompatible, so it can be safely used in the body without causing adverse reactions (Chaichian et al. 2022). Secondly, PLGA has a tunable degradation profile, which allows for the adjustment of the release rate of the encapsulated miRNA (Kasina et al. 2023). This is important to achieve optimal therapeutic effects, as the right release rate can ensure that the miRNA is present at the site of action for a long enough time to effectively regulate gene expression. The biocompatibility, tunable release profile, nuclease protection, and targeting capabilities of PLGA make it a versatile and effective tool for gene regulation therapy. Ananta et al. used PLGA nanoparticles to deliver antisense miR-21 and antisense miR-10b to glioblastoma, which is more effective than temozolomide therapy in vivo (Ananta et al. 2015; 2016). Researchers constructed mPEG-PLGA-VEGFab nanoparticles as vectors to recover miR-99a levels to inhibit the progression of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). These carriers showed higher delivery efficiency than the conventional transfection agent Lipofectamine™ 2000 and revealed that the construct selectively delivered miR-99a to HCC (Cai et al. 2017). With further research and development, PLGA-based miRNA delivery systems could potentially be employed in a variety of applications, including cancer treatment, neurodegenerative disease therapy, and tissue engineering.

Chitosan, a natural polymer, whose positive charge enables it to form electrostatic complexes with negatively charged oligonucleotides. Researchers have successfully engineered chitosan nanoparticles for the targeted delivery of miR-33 mimic to macrophages. The construct mentioned above was highly effective in enhancing the stability and delivery efficiency of miR-33, thus offering promising therapeutic outcomes in macrophage-related diseases (Nguyen et al. 2019).

Inorganic-based vectors

Inorganic nanomaterials, such as gold nanoparticles, mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNPs), carbon nanotubes (CNTs), and quantum dots, offer promising new avenues for miRNA delivery (Deka Dey et al. 2023). Inorganic nanomaterials have a high loading capacity for oligonucleotides, which allows for higher dose delivery of miRNA than what can be achieved with organic vectors.

Gold, an inert material, does not interact with most chemicals, which renders it beneficial for employment in living organisms. In addition to their inertness and ease of synthesis, gold nanoparticles have been found to exhibit anti-angiogenic effects (Shetty et al. 2022; Sousa and Conde 2022). In a previous study, gold nanoparticles were demonstrated to suppress the multiplication of vascular endothelial cells triggered by VEGF165, a protein that promotes angiogenesis (Pan et al. 2014). The unique properties of gold nanoparticles, including their inertness, ease of synthesis, ability to carry oligonucleotides, and anti-angiogenic effects, make them a highly versatile and promising tool for miRNA delivery. For example, by using these gold-liposomal nanoparticles, anti-miR-92b can be specifically delivered to the brain tumor cells. The gold liposome-ApoE nanoparticles were found to be more effective than traditional liposome-based delivery systems in terms of the reduction of miR-92b expression and blastoma cell viability. What is more, these nanoparticles accumulated in glioblastoma in vivo (Grafals-Ruiz et al. 2020).

The formation of complexes between CNTs and miRNA could be achieved by either non-covalent or covalent bonding, depending on the type of miRNA (Zare H. et al. 2021). The unique properties of CNTs (stability, high surface-to-volume ratios, easy surface functionalization) make them a promising delivery carrier for miRNA. Researchers found that functionalized CNTs conjugated with miR-503, which is essential for regulating a key gene of cell proliferation and angiogenesis (CDC25A), effectively stabilized against nucleolytic degradation (Masotti et al. 2016).

MSNPs have held great promise for miRNA delivery due to their powerful loading capacity (Hosseinpour et al. 2021). The photosensitizer indocyanine green was incorporated into the delivery system. After light irradiation, the nanosystem showed superior delivery efficiency and significant suppression of primary cancer metastasis in vivo (De Jong et al. 2019). Researchers have functionalized mesoporous silica with CD44, which was loaded with dual miRNA (antisense-miR-10b and miR-34a-mimic) to treat triple-negative breast cancer. This study demonstrated that the tailored nanoparticles show high specificity and a high tumor suppressive effect, which affirmed the clinical application potential of MSNPs in the miRNA delivery system (Ahir et al. 2020).

Exosome-based vectors

Exosomes, natural carriers, deliver medicine in a cell type-specific manner, inherently generated, which significantly reduces their detriment and immunogenicity. Researchers have investigated the function of exosomes in delivering miRNA for targeted cells. They observed that functionalized exosomes loaded with miR-317b-5b were internalized in the targeted site of osteosarcoma tumor-bearing mice, simultaneously exhibiting anti-tumor activity in vivo (Xue et al. 2021). Moreover, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) were used as bio-factories of exosome generation. These exosomes were demonstrated to suppress the growth of intracranial GSC xenograft tumors in mice (Lang et al. 2018). Yang and his colleagues found that exosome-based vectors loaded with miR-155 antisense significantly reduced miR-155 expression in obese mice, contributing to the reduction of obesity-related inflammation (Wei et al. 2020).

Stimuli-responsive delivery systems for miRNA-based therapy

The delivery efficiency of miRNA to the target tissues and cells is vital for therapeutic outcomes. The drug release behavior of miRNA delivery systems can be designed to achieve sustained miRNA release over extended periods of time, which can improve therapeutic outcomes. The miRNA-derivative nucleotide drug delivery systems can be designed to release the miRNA in response to changes in pH, temperature, enzyme levels, redox potential, or specific substances in the target tissue (Fig. 3). This targeted release mechanism allows for more controlled delivery and release of miRNA, ensuring that they are mostly released when and where they are needed, optimizing the therapeutic response, and minimizing side effects (Zhang F et al. 2023; Zhang P et al. 2021).

Schematic illustration of stimuli-responsive delivery systems for miRNA-based therapy

PH-responsive delivery systems

The low pH level within the tumor microenvironment offers an advantage for the development of miRNA delivery systems which utilize polyelectrolytes, ester, and acetate bounds (Lin F et al. 2019). The release of the cargo at the therapeutic spot triggered tunable and sustained delivery when the pH of the environment changes. The key to pH-responsive controlled release of drugs is the selection of appropriate materials and the conception of delivery systems (Zang et al. 2019). Juang and his colleagues constructed pH-sensitive and peptide-modified nanoparticles encapsulated with irinotecan and miR-200. pH-sensitive PEG-lipid derivatives were used to coat the functional peptides with an imine bond. On the nanoparticles, there are three types of peptides: cell-penetrating peptide, neo-vasculature-targeting peptide, and mitochondria-targeting peptide. The intelligently designed nanoparticles displayed pH-responsive properties in terms of release and pharmacokinetics in colon cancer, and demonstrated excellent treatment outcomes by suppressing the growth of tumors and minimizing systemic toxicity (Juang et al. 2019).

Thermo-responsive delivery systems

The high temperature at the tumor site may be caused by factors such as tumor heat and inflammatory stimulation. Thus, temperature can serve as an internal or external stimulus for nanoparticles-controlled release (Borghei and Hosseini 2018). The thermo-sensitive polymers have a unique property that allows them to undergo a phase transition in response to changes in temperature. This phase transition is characterized by a low critical solubility temperature (LCST). Under LCST, the polymers are soluble in water and form hydrogen bonds, creating a swollen state. When temperature rises above LCST, the polymers become insoluble due to the break of hydrogen bonds. In a study, temperature- and pH-responsive dual delivery carriers of gemcitabine and miR-345 were developed for treating pancreatic cancer, which showed inhibited tumor growth and less metastasis in distant tissues (Uz et al. 2019).

Light-responsive delivery systems

Light stimuli-responsive nanoparticles, such as gold nanoparticles, release the cargo after non-ionizing radiation. The gold nanoparticles can bind double-stranded oligonucleotides, including one strand of sensitizing miRNA and one strand of active miRNA. After applying near-infrared irradiation, the sensitizing strand disassociates from the active strand, resulting in the release of miRNA content within the cell (Qiao et al. 2017). To achieve sustained and controlled release in targeted organs, researchers developed intelligent nanocarriers that incorporate stimuli-sensitive gold nanoparticles and temperature-responsive polymers. The nanocarriers remain stealth when entering circulation, which protects the cargo from nuclease digestion. However, when exposed to near-infrared irradiation within the tumor site, the surface switches to another state, facilitating endocytosis by targeted cells. Moreover, the delivery system for miRNA, even at low dosages, can synergistically inhibit tumor growth and trigger cell death under mild near-infrared irradiation. Notably, they cause minimal harm to normal organs (Zhang P et al. 2016).

Enzyme-responsive delivery systems

Enzyme-responsive delivery systems utilize enzymes as triggers to release the miRNA payload in a controlled manner. The delivery vector is designed to be stable in the bloodstream but sensitive to the enzyme present at the target site. Once the vector reaches the target tissue, the enzyme catalyzes a chemical reaction and leads to the release of the miRNA (Wang X et al. 2019). The choice of enzyme is crucial for the success of these systems. Enzymes that are overexpressed in diseased tissues or present at the target site can be leveraged to achieve precise delivery. For example, proteases are overexpressed in many cancers and can be used to trigger the release of miRNA in tumor cells. Wang et al. showed the controlled release of miR-21 was achieved by enzymatic cleavage of the chitosan/hyaluronic acid nanoparticle, leading to more active osseointegration (Wang Z et al. 2015). Guo and his coworkers constructed a glutathione-responsive polymer to protect the nanocarriers of miR-21 antisense from degradation by Dicer or other RNase in normal cells (Guo et al. 2019).

Redox-responsive delivery systems

Based on the redox reaction, redox-responsive delivery systems offer a promising solution, leveraging the natural redox environment of cells to control the release of miRNA (Yu et al. 2017). A self-assembling peptide-polysaccharide carrier was loaded with indocyanine green and miR-34a. When the conjugate entered cancer cells, the intracellular redox potential of glutathione tripeptide caused its disulfide bond to break. This disruption leads to the release of miR-34a, which can then carry out its therapeutic function (Deng et al. 2018). Another type of redox-responsive carrier was designed to deliver miR-129 and Dox for treating breast cancer. DL-dithiothreitol could cause carriers to swell, resulting in the break of the disulfide bond. This allowed the two therapeutics to be simultaneously released from the carriers, which showed a promising combination of effective drug and miRNA therapy (Yi et al. 2016).

Summary and outlook

Since miRNA-based drugs are still in the early stages of development, further preclinical and clinical studies will provide abundant approaches to improve their safety and delivery efficacy. The development of miRNA-based therapeutics requires a deep understanding of the complex networks involving miRNA and their target genes. Identifying the precise miRNA that regulates the expression of disease-causing genes is crucial for the successful development of therapeutic interventions. Moreover, choosing an appropriate miRNA nano-delivery design is a very challenging task due to the diversity of barriers encountered within the organism. This must be tightly controlled for avoiding off-target effects as well as ensuring optimal therapeutic outcomes.

The combination of miRNA with other therapeutic strategies, such as immune modulation or stem cell therapy, is opening up new avenues for disease therapy. By manipulating the expression of specific miRNA, it may be possible to treat a variety of diseases, such as tumors, neurodegenerative disorders, and autoimmune diseases. There is no denying that a few investigations have been halted because of related immune side effects, which could be attributed to the miRNA and its delivery system. The ideal vectors of miRNA should have the following characteristics: (1) keeping the stability of miRNA in the circulatory system and extending its half-life; (2) enhancing their distribution in target tissues; (3) reducing the toxicity and off-target effects associated with miRNA drugs; and (4) promoting transfection efficiency for drug delivery of miRNA, conquering physiological barriers within the body. Recent advances in nanotechnology will pave the way for the clinical translation of the miRNA-based drugs in the upcoming years.

What is worth expecting is that miRNA-based therapeutics will obtain approval in the few years to come. The development of new technologies and methods for manipulating miRNA function will further enhance the ability to treat diseases effectively. By harnessing the unique properties of nanoparticles, such as the ability to cross biological barriers and target specific organs, it may be possible to design more effective delivery systems for oligonucleotides. Besides, modification of miRNA-based drugs and better delivery systems will enhance targetability and stability, and improve the controlled release of oligonucleotides. The advancements of stimuli-responsive modes will simultaneously improve the pharmacodynamics of oligonucleotides, allowing for less frequent dosing and reducing side effects. Rigorous clinical trials will be essential to assess the safety and efficacy of these agents in treating human diseases, as well as to determine their optimal dosing and treatment schedules. By addressing these challenges mentioned above, the field of miRNA-based therapeutics has the potential to make a transformative impact on human health in the future.
