Integrated Transcriptomics of Human and Canine Osteosarcoma Reveals Species Stemness and Trabectedin Sensitivity
Authors: Kenta Kono, Yomogi Shiota, Ting‐Ting Shu, Hayato Shibahara, Daiki Kato, Takayuki Nakagawa, Seiji Ohtori, Tadashi Kondo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCanines share close environmental and physiological homologies with humans, making them essential large‐animal models in preclinical drug discovery. However, species‐specific discrepancies in therapeutic responsiveness often limit the direct extrapolation of canine data to human clinical scenarios. Although osteosarcoma (OS) spontaneously occurs in large‐breed dogs at an incidence 30‐fold higher than in humans, the underlying interspecies molecular differences governing tumor behavior and drug sensitivity remain poorly understood. This study aimed to systematically evaluate the translational reliability and limitations of the canine OS model by comparing human and canine malignant architectures at baseline and during anti‐cancer drug exposure. By integrating single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq) from treatment‐naive patient specimens with multi‐conditional temporal bulk RNA‐seq of representative cell lines, we interrogated their dynamic transcriptomic responses to Trabectedin. Integrated single‐cell analysis revealed that human OS possesses a highly primitive, high‐stemness subpopulation hierarchy and a more primed tumor‐infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) profile than canine OS. Pharmacogenomic assays demonstrated that these distinct cellular architectures dictate therapeutic vulnerabilities: The rigid, high‐stemness hierarchy of human OS facilitates transcriptomic robustness and adaptive drug resistance. In contrast, the fluid, less primitive network of canine OS renders its tumor cells prone to immediate, stochastically driven functional network collapse and superior Trabectedin sensitivity. Collectively, our study provides a comprehensive comparative molecular framework for human and canine OS. These findings emphasize the necessity of accounting for species‐specific stemness and immune landscapes, ultimately accelerating the precision development of next‐generation OS therapeutics.

Introduction

The domestic dog ( Canis lupus familiaris ) shares a close evolutionary history and a shared environment with humans, resulting in remarkable physiological and pathological similarities between the two species [1]. Consequently, canines serve as a critical preclinical large‐animal model in modern drug discovery, extensively utilized for evaluating therapeutic efficacy, pharmacokinetics, and toxicological profiles [2, 3, 4]. However, substantial species‐specific variations in drug sensitivity frequently emerge, indicating that therapeutic outcomes in canine models cannot always be seamlessly extrapolated to human clinical scenarios [5]. Understanding the molecular underpinnings of these interspecies discrepancies remains a fundamental challenge in translational oncology.

Osteosarcoma (OS) is the most prevalent primary malignant bone tumor in both humans and canines. The two species share remarkably parallel clinical presentations, including a predilection for the long bones of the extremities—particularly the distal femur—as well as highly congruent histopathological features, a high incidence of occult metastasis at initial diagnosis, and a distinct tropism for distant metastasis, predominantly to the lungs [6, 7, 8, 9]. Consequently, the spontaneous canine model is widely recognized as an ideal translationally relevant model for human OS, prompting extensive cross‐species comparative research. Paradoxically, the translational fidelity of this model faces inherent epidemiological and pharmacological constraints. While large‐breed dogs exhibit an overall incidence of OS exceeding 30‐fold that of the human population, their age‐associated disease kinetics diverge significantly; human OS characteristically displays a bimodal age distribution with a pronounced peak in young children and adolescents [10, 11], whereas canine OS predominantly manifests as an age‐related malignancy with high incidence rates in older dogs [8, 9, 12]. Furthermore, while some data demonstrate similar outcomes following anticancer drug exposure [13], substantial species‐specific variations in chemosensitivity frequently emerge, highlighting the fundamental limitations of direct interspecies extrapolation [13].

Recent advances in scRNA‐seq have revolutionized our capability to dissect the tumor microenvironment and malignant cell states at a granular, molecular pathway level [14, 15]. While single‐cell transcriptomics has intensified our understanding of distinct patient cohorts within a single species, comprehensive integrated analyses bridging human and canine datasets from a comparative oncological perspective remain remarkably scarce. Previous cross‐species studies have predominantly focused on identifying simple genomic overlaps or conserved cell‐type markers; however, their ability to elucidate how species‐specific organizational principles and cellular hierarchies dictate dynamic drug responsiveness has remained limited [16, 17, 18, 19].

To address these critical gaps, the objective of this study was to systematically evaluate the translational reliability and inherent limitations of the canine OS model by comparing human and canine malignant architectures at baseline and during anti‐cancer drug exposure. By integrating scRNA‐seq from treatment‐naive patients with multi‐conditional bulk RNA‐seq of representative cell lines, we interrogated the distinct ways in which these two species respond to Trabectedin, a DNA minor‐groove binder [20]. Our findings reveal a fundamental divergence in tumor organization: The rigid, orchestrated hierarchy of human OS facilitates transcriptomic robustness and adaptive resistance, whereas the fluid and vulnerable networks of canine OS are prone to stochastically driven functional collapse. By defining these species‐specific boundaries of interspecies translation, this study provides a crucial framework for optimizing the use of spontaneous canine models and accelerating the precision development of next‐generation therapeutics for OS.

Materials and Methods

Cell Lines and Cell Culture

The human OS cell lines NCC‐OS1‐X2‐C1 (OS‐1) and NCC‐OS2‐C1 (OS2) were previously established in our laboratory [21, 22]. U‐2 OS and MG‐63 were purchased from the Japanese Collection of Research Bioresources Cell Bank, Osaka, Japan. The canine OS cell lines POS, HMPOS, OOS, and D17 were kindly provided by the Laboratory of Veterinary Surgery, the University of Tokyo [23, 24, 25]. All cells were cultured using DMEM/F‐12 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with GlutaMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 5% heat‐inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific). To maintain optimal growth conditions, the medium was further supplemented with 10 μM Y‐27632 (Selleck Chemicals, Houston, TX, USA), 10 ng/mL basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF; Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), 5 ng/mL epidermal growth factor (EGF; Sigma‐Aldrich), 5 μg/mL insulin (Sigma‐Aldrich), 0.4 μg/mL hydrocortisone (Sigma‐Aldrich), and 100 μg/mL each of penicillin and streptomycin (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan). All cultures were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Drug Treatment and Cell Viability Assay

Drug treatment and cell viability assays were performed as previously described [26, 27]. Briefly, cells seeded in 96‐well plates (Nunc; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) were treated with various concentrations of Trabectedin (MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) for 72 h, and cell viability was quantified using Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK‐8; Dojindo Molecular Technologies Inc., Kumamoto, Japan).

RNA Extraction and RNA‐Seq Library Preparation

Human and canine OS cell lines were seeded in flat‐bottom 96‐well plates at a density of 1.5 × 104 cells/well and cultured at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. After 24 h of stabilization, cells were treated with Trabectedin at concentrations corresponding to their respective IC50 and 1/2 IC50 values, or maintained with DMSO as a vehicle control. Following incubation for 0, 3, 8, and 24 h, cells were washed with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS [−]; Nacalai Tesque Inc., Kyoto, Japan) to remove residual medium. Total RNA extraction and purification were performed by Zeon BioSolutions Inc. (Tokyo, Japan) using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's instructions. RNA concentration and purity were evaluated by spectrophotometric measurement of the A260/280 ratio, and RNA integrity was assessed using the Agilent Bioanalyzer or TapeStation system (Agilent Technologies). Only samples with an RNA Integrity Number (RIN) greater than 8.0 were used for subsequent library preparation. RNA‐seq libraries were prepared using 20 ng of total RNA per sample as input for the Quartz‐Seq2 protocol, a high‐sensitivity transcriptomic profiling method [28]. Notably, neither ribosomal RNA depletion nor poly(A) selection was performed, as per the Quartz‐Seq2 standard procedure. Libraries were multiplexed and sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA) with 101 bp paired‐end reads. The sequencing achieved a mean depth of 2.16 ± 1.83 million reads per sample (mean ± SD).

Bulk RNA‐Seq Data Analysis and Interspecies Batch Correction

Raw reads were aligned to the respective reference genomes (GRCh38 for human and ROS_Cfam_1.0 for canine). To enable direct interspecies comparison, canine gene IDs were mapped to their human 1:1 orthologs using the orthogene and babelgene R packages, resulting in a conserved gene matrix. Raw counts were normalized via variance stabilizing transformation (VST) using DESeq2. Interspecies batch effects were corrected using the removeBatchEffect function in limma, incorporating time points and drug doses into the design matrix. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) against MSigDB Hallmark gene sets were performed using the batch‐corrected VST matrix, with gene ranking based on expression changes relative to the vehicle control.

scRNA‐Seq Integrated Analysis

Integrated scRNA‐Seq Analysis of Human and Canine Osteosarcoma

Publicly available human (GSE252470, n = 6) [17] and canine (GSE162454, n = 6) [16] scRNA‐seq datasets were integrated using the Seurat (version 5.0.1) framework. All pipelines were implemented using RStudio (version 2025.09.0 + 387, Posit Software, PBC, Boston, MA, USA).

Ortholog Mapping and Mitochondrial Gene Rescue

For interspecies comparison, canine gene symbols were mapped to their human 1:1 orthologs using the orthogene and babelgene packages. To ensure accurate quality control across species, we implemented a custom “mitochondrial gene rescue” pipeline: Canine‐specific mitochondrial gene nomenclature (e.g., mt‐Nd1) was manually standardized to the human convention (e.g., MT‐ND1) prior to ortholog conversion, preventing the loss of critical metabolic indicators during integration.

Data Preprocessing and Quality Control

The integrated raw count matrix was subjected to stringent quality control (QC) filtering. Cells were retained if they met the following criteria: (i) number of detected features (nFeature_RNA) between 500 and 7,500, and (ii) mitochondrial gene content (percent.mt) less than 15%. After stringent quality control filtering, a total of 71,127 high‐quality cells were retained for downstream analysis, comprising 30,605 canine cells and 40,522 human cells. Data were normalized and variance‐stabilized using the SCTransform algorithm, regressing out the effects of mitochondrial content.

Integration and Dimensionality Reduction

To mitigate technical batch effects and inter‐individual variation while preserving biological signals, the Harmony integration algorithm was employed. Harmony embeddings were computed based on the top 30 principal components (PCs), using subject_id and species as batch variables. Non‐linear dimensionality reduction was performed using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP). Unsupervised clustering was executed using the shared nearest neighbor (SNN) modularity optimization with a resolution of 0.5.

Cell Type Annotation and Marker Identification

Cell clusters were annotated based on the expression of canonical markers: CD3E/CD5 (T cells), CTSK/ACP5 (Osteoclasts), CD68/MRC1 (Macrophages), COL1A1/ALPL (Osteosarcoma/Fibroblasts), and TOP2A/MKI67 (Cycling cells), in accordance with the criteria established in the original reports [16, 17]. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between species within specific clusters were identified using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test or the MAST (Model‐based Analysis of Single‐cell Transcriptomics) GLM framework, with a log2 fold‐change threshold of 0.25 and an adjusted p‐value < 0.05.

Quantification of Developmental Potential and High‐Stemness Subpopulation Identification

The developmental potential of individual tumor cells and stemness status in the OS cell lines were objectively quantified using the CytoTRACE (version 0.3.3) R package [29]. First, for scRNA‐seq datasets, CytoTRACE scores were calculated independently for each sample/patient to assess intra‐tumoral cellular hierarchy based on transcriptional diversity (number of expressed genes per cell), independent of species‐specific marker genes. To identify the most primitive subpopulations, peak stemness cells were defined as those within the top 1st percentile of CytoTRACE scores within each species group. Second, to evaluate the baseline developmental potential across cell line models, CytoTRACE scores were calculated from untreated control (0 h) bulk RNA‐seq expression profiles using the single‐cell‐derived framework adapted for bulk samples. Samples were rank‐normalized to a scale ranging from 0.0 (lowest potential) to 1.0 (highest potential) to facilitate direct cross‐species comparisons between human and canine cell lines.

Pathway Enrichment Analysis

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using the fgsea and clusterProfiler packages against the MSigDB Hallmark gene sets [30]. For GO enrichment analysis, the enrichGO function was applied to differentially expressed or conserved gene sets to identify overrepresented Biological Processes (BP).

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R. Comparison of stemness and drug sensitivity to compare the baseline CytoTRACE scores and Trabectedin IC50 values between human and canine groups; the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test (Mann–Whitney U test) was employed.

Results

Integrated Single‐Cell Transcriptomics Reveals Conserved Cellular Landscapes in Human and Canine Osteosarcoma

While the high prevalence of OS in dogs is well‐documented, it remains unclear whether this clinical disparity is attributed to fundamental differences in cellular architecture. To address this, we first compared the cellular composition of treatment‐naive OS tissues across species using integrated scRNA‐seq analysis. Publicly available scRNA‐seq datasets for both human (n = 6) and canine (n = 6) OS were obtained, and canine gene symbols were mapped to their corresponding human 1:1 orthologs. For cross‐species integration, we employed the SCTransform and Harmony algorithms to mitigate technical batch effects and normalize the data. Stringent quality control (QC) ensured comparable data quality between species, with 21,052 ± 486 genes (average ± standard deviation) and 6,754 ± 1,725 cells detected per human sample, vs. 12,004 ± 398 genes and 5,101 ± 2,916 cells per canine sample (Figure S1).

Integrated UMAP projection revealed a highly comparable cellular landscape between human and canine OS tissues (Figure 1a). Cell type annotation, based on canonical lineage markers, allowed for a precise quantification of the tumor microenvironment (TME) components. Comparative analysis of cell proportions indicated that the overall TME structure is largely conserved between species. However, specific differences were observed: The proportions of macrophages and osteoclasts were higher in human OS, whereas neutrophils, T cells, and dendritic cells (DCs) showed a relative enrichment in canine samples compared to human counterparts (Figure 1b).

Conserved Transcriptional Programs and Species‐Specific Stemness Architectures in Human and Canine Osteosarcoma Cells

Given that Figure 1 revealed no obvious disparities in cellular composition, we hypothesized that intrinsic transcriptomic differences within the malignant cells themselves contribute to the species‐specific clinical characteristics of OS. Re‐clustering of the malignant compartment identified a conserved transcriptomic core, characterized by the expression of canonical OS markers such as COL1A1, COL1A2, and VIM (Figure 2a and Table S1). However, volcano plot analysis highlighted significant species‐specific gene expression: Human OS cells were enriched in genes such as SNRPG, BANF1, and ROMO1, while canine cells showed higher expression of BBLN, TOMM6, and AMDHD1 (Figure 2b, Tables S2 and S3).

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) of these differentially expressed genes (DEGs) revealed unexpected functional profiles (Figure 2c). Canine OS cells exhibited a broad upregulation of TNF‐alpha signaling, the p53 pathway, and oxidative phosphorylation, suggesting a highly active metabolic state coupled with active tumor‐suppressive checkpoints at the cluster level. In contrast, human OS cells showed relative enrichment in E2F targets and general metabolic signaling.

Considering the pivotal role of cancer stem cells in tumorigenesis, we next quantified the developmental potential within each species using CytoTRACE. We identified the peak stemness population, representing the top 1st percentile of cells with the highest CytoTRACE scores (Figure 2d). Notably, human peak stemness cells exhibited significantly higher CytoTRACE scores compared to their canine counterparts, indicating a more primitive, undifferentiated state in the human high‐stemness subpopulation (Figure 2e). Furthermore, GSEA focused specifically on this high‐stemness subpopulation revealed that E2F targets, DNA repair, and MYC targets were significantly and exclusively upregulated in the human high‐stemness subpopulation (Figure 2f). In addition, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis confirmed that the top 1st percentile of CytoTRACE‐high cells significantly enriched stem cell‐ and development‐related pathways compared to the remaining 99% population (Figure S4).

Collectively, these results demonstrate a striking divergence in tumor architecture: While canine OS exhibits higher overall proliferative and metabolic signaling across the tumor bulk, human OS is characterized by a more distinct hierarchy, where a highly undifferentiated and molecularly potent high‐stemness subpopulation drives tumor maintenance.

Comparative Profiling of Tumor‐Infiltrating T Cells Reveals Enhanced Anti‐Tumor Transcriptomic Signatures in Human Vs. Canine Osteosarcoma

Given that tumor‐infiltrating T cells play a pivotal role in suppressing tumor progression, we next characterized the functional states of the T‐cell compartment across species. Integrated scRNA‐seq analysis clearly partitioned the T‐cell population into distinct CD4+ and CD8+ subsets (Figure 3a,b).

Comparative Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed a striking functional divergence between human and canine TILs. In both CD4+ and CD8+ T‐cell subsets, human cells were significantly enriched in biological processes related to T‐cell activation, the regulation of lymphocyte differentiation, and leukocyte cell–cell adhesion (Figure 3c–f).

These signatures suggest that human TILs exist in a more primed and functionally active state, potentially as a coordinated response to the highly undifferentiated and aggressive tumor hierarchy observed in human OS. In contrast, canine T cells were predominantly characterized by metabolic and biosynthetic processes, such as cytoplasmic translation and cellular respiration, with a relative lack of canonical activation markers. These results indicate that while T cells are present in the canine tumor microenvironment, they lack the robust anti‐tumor transcriptomic programs found in their human counterparts.

Distinct Stemness Hierarchies in Human and Canine OS Underlie Divergent Pharmacological Vulnerabilities and Adaptive Landscapes

Based on our finding that human OS possesses a more primitive and molecularly potent high‐stemness subpopulation hierarchy, we hypothesized that these species‐specific stemness architectures influence therapeutic vulnerability. Prior to cross‐species comparison, human and canine cell line transcriptomes were integrated by mapping to 1‐to‐1 orthologous genes, followed by batch‐effect correction and rigorous quality control, which confirmed consistent library sizes and normalized expression distributions across all time points and treatment conditions (Figure S2a,b). On this standardized platform, we first confirmed that the baseline CytoTRACE scores were significantly higher in human OS cell lines compared to canine cell lines, reflecting the more undifferentiated state of human models (Figure 4a). Given that the human high‐stemness subpopulation exhibited an enrichment of DNA repair pathways (as shown in Figure 2f), we evaluated the efficacy of Trabectedin—a minor‐groove DNA binder [31] that induces apoptosis—across species. Consistent with the lower stemness scores in canine models, Trabectedin exhibited significantly more potent anti‐tumor activity in canine cell lines, characterized by lower IC50 values compared to human counterparts (Figure 4b–d).

To elucidate the transcriptomic mechanisms underlying this differential sensitivity, we performed bulk RNA‐seq under multiple conditions, varying both Trabectedin concentration (0 nM, 1/2 IC50 nM, and IC50 nM) and exposure time (0, 3, 8, and 24 h). After accounting for species‐specific batch effects, PCA revealed a profound divergence in transcriptional trajectories (Figure 5a). Human OS cell lines exhibited dynamic and directional transcriptomic shifts, whereas canine cell lines maintained relatively stationary states on the global PCA map upon Trabectedin exposure (Figure 5b,c). Intriguingly, despite this lack of global movement in PCA space, canine OS cell lines demonstrated a far more intense functional response at the pathway level. As shown in the GSEA hallmark profile (Figure 5d), canine OS exhibited rapid and robust transcriptional remodeling, characterized by the induction of stress‐responsive and apoptotic signaling (e.g., p53, TNFalpha, and Apoptosis) and the pervasive suppression of key survival and proliferation circuits (e.g., DNA repair, Oxidative Phosphorylation, MYC targets, PI3K/AKT/mTOR, and E2F targets) compared to the attenuated responses in human counterparts. These data suggest that while human OS cells undergo an organized and adaptive state transition, canine OS cells lack the capacity for systematic remodeling, instead experiencing an immediate collapse of functional networks within their baseline transcriptomic state. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that human OS harbors a rigid high‐stemness subpopulation hierarchy that drives adaptive drug resistance, whereas canine OS possesses a fluid molecular network that undergoes rapid functional collapse upon Trabectedin exposure (Figure 6).

Discussion

The objective of this study was to systematically evaluate the translational utility and inherent limitations of the canine model for human OS. Through scRNA‐seq, we successfully delineated the comprehensive organizational landscape of the tumor microenvironment across both species. While the core malignant features of OS remain largely conserved between humans and canines, distinct species‐specific variations emerged within the high‐stemness subpopulation hierarchy; human OS exhibited a significantly less differentiated, more primitive stemness profile characterized by the marked elevation of E2F targets, DNA repair, and MYC signaling pathways. Furthermore, we observed pronounced differences in the tumor‐infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) compartment, wherein human TILs demonstrated superior functional priming compared to their canine counterparts, suggesting a potentially enhanced capacity to suppress tumor progression within the human microenvironment. Driven by the single‐cell transcriptomic insight that human OS and high‐stemness subpopulation exhibit a marked upregulation of DNA repair machinery and E2F target pathways, we rationalized that targeting this network could expose species‐specific therapeutic vulnerabilities. Trabectedin, a unique DNA minor‐groove binder, exerts its cytotoxic effects by binding covalently to guanines in the minor groove, which traps transcription factors and induces lethal double‐strand breaks that uniquely depend on and disrupt active transcription‐coupled nucleotide excision repair (TC‐NER) and homologous recombination (HR) systems [32]. Multi‐conditional bulk RNA‐seq transcriptomic analysis of representative human and canine OS cell lines confirmed that this underlying stemness architecture is far more robustly preserved and sustained in humans, ultimately dictating therapeutic outcomes and conferring superior resistance to Trabectedin in human OS compared to the fluid, more vulnerable canine networks.

Previous cross‐species transcriptomic studies have predominantly operated under a conservation‐centric paradigm, focusing almost exclusively on the degree of molecular similarity between animal models and humans [16]. Consequently, comparative approaches dedicated to systematically delineating interspecies divergence remain remarkably scarce. Given that OS is characterized by an exceptionally high degree of both intratumoral and intertumoral heterogeneity [33], merely identifying conserved markers is insufficient for predicting clinical outcomes. Instead, clarifying explicit species‐specific discrepancies is an absolute prerequisite for rigorously defining both the translational utility and the inherent boundaries of the spontaneous canine model—a system extensively utilized in preclinical drug development. The high‐stemness subpopulation represents a critical subpopulation within malignant tumors, characterized by its unique capacities for self‐renewal, multilineage differentiation, and intrinsic resistance to conventional chemotherapy [34]. Therefore, the functional and transcriptomic divergence within the high‐stemness subpopulation hierarchy identified in this study provides a mechanistically grounded explanation for the observed discrepancies in therapeutic vulnerability. Our findings suggest that these species‐specific stemness architectures fundamentally dictate the differential dynamics of chemoresistance between human and canine malignancies.

Despite the insights gained from our comparative analysis, there are several limitations. First, although we implemented stringent quality control ensuring comparable data quality, a disparity in the total number of detected genes per subject remained, with 21,052 ± 486 genes in humans vs. 12,004 ± 398 genes in canines (Figure S1). This discrepancy is largely attributed to the constraints of one‐to‐one ortholog mapping, which filters out species‐specific genomic elements to facilitate cross‐species integration. Consequently, some specialized transcriptomic features unique to either species may have been overlooked in the integrated analysis [35, 36]. Second, while the use of four human and four canine cell lines provided a foundational understanding of species‐specific vulnerabilities, OS is characterized by profound intratumoral and inter‐individual heterogeneity [37]. As shown in Figure S5 and Table S4, the selected cell lines exhibited genomic and baseline expression heterogeneity in key driver genes (e.g., TP53, RB1, and MYC) [21, 22, 38, 39]. Nevertheless, our batch‐corrected principal component analysis (PCA) and global transcriptomic profiling demonstrated that species‐specific differences remained the predominant driver of variance separating human and canine models. Furthermore, trabectedin response profiles exhibited consistent intra‐species trends despite these differences in individual driver gene statuses. Given this inherent complexity, the findings derived from these representative models may not fully encompass the entire clinical spectrum of the disease. Therefore, further validation using a larger cohort of primary patient‐derived samples and clinical trials will be essential to confirm the direct clinical applicability of these results.

In conclusion, our study establishes a comprehensive molecular framework for understanding the functional divergence between human and canine OS. We have demonstrated that species‐specific architectures within cancer stem‐like cell populations serve as a primary determinant of drug resistance, particularly in the context of DNA‐targeted therapies. Furthermore, the distinct profiles of tumor‐infiltrating lymphocytes further substantiate the fundamental species differences within the tumor microenvironment. These findings emphasize that while the dog remains an invaluable spontaneous model for human OS, researchers must rigorously account for these inherent disparities in stemness and immune landscapes when translating therapeutic findings across species.
