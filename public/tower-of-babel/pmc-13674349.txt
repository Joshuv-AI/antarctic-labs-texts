The modified Norwood/Sano in the settings of dextrocardia and situs solitus.

Central Message

Norwood palliation in HLHS with dextrocardia is complex due to distorted great arterial anatomy, aberrant pulmonary artery position, and arch vessel anomalies impacting surgical and perfusion plans.

When performing the Norwood procedure in hypoplastic left heart syndrome (HLHS) with dextrocardia and situs solitus, the surgeon is faced with unique surgical challenges as the result of the altered spatial anatomy. The rightward displacement of the heart changes the orientation of the great arteries, complicating the aortic arch reconstruction and Damus-Kaye-Stansel (DKS) anastomosis. In addition, the abnormal position of the right pulmonary artery (PA) and variations in arch branches demand modified approaches to shunt placement and cerebral perfusion. Successfully navigating these anatomical complexities requires careful intraoperative adaptation and innovative surgical strategies to achieve optimal outcomes in this rare patient population.

Methods

Institutional review board approval was not required because of the case report nature of the current manuscript; however, parental consent was obtained.

Patient Presentation

A 2.6-kg neonate presented with a fetal diagnosis of HLHS secondary to aortic and mitral valvular atresia. After initial stabilization and confirmation of the anatomy with both echocardiography and computed tomography, the decision was made to proceed to the operating room on day of life 1.

Surgical Technique

The Norwood procedure included several technical modifications (Video 1). The right common carotid artery was used for the arterial inflow of the cardiopulmonary bypass, and selective antegrade cerebral perfusion was performed at 28 °C via a 3.5-mm polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) graft (Gore-Tex; W. L. Gore & Associates, Inc) that was sewn to the right common carotid artery. The aortic arch and the ascending aorta were augmented with a decellularized pulmonary homograft patch, followed by implantation of the main PA into the augmented aorta, rather than performing the traditional DKS.

The disconnected pulmonary arterial confluence was augmented with a decellularized pulmonary homograft patch, and pulmonary blood flow was established via a modified Sano conduit. A 6-mm cryopreserved valved aortic homograft was secured to the free wall of the systemic right ventricle and was connected to a 5-mm ringed Gore-Tex graft, which was connected to the pulmonary arterial confluence through the homograft patch. The aortic crossclamp, selective antegrade cerebral perfusion, and cardiopulmonary bypass times were 103, 75, and 235 minutes, respectively.

Outcome

The postoperative course was uneventful. The neonate underwent delayed chest closure 48 hours later, and he was extubated on postoperative day 5. He continued to do well during his follow-up and is currently being prepared for stage II palliation.

Discussion

Performing the Norwood procedure in neonates with HLHS complicated by dextrocardia and situs solitus presents formidable anatomical challenges that require innovative surgical adaptations. Although a hybrid strategy1 may be chosen in the current scenario and the Norwood deferred later,2 in our opinion, this will introduce more challenges. The “double-barrel technique” of DKS reconstruction has been used in certain anatomies involving transposed great vessels3; however, we believe it would have been quite challenging to perform it in the setting of dextrocardia with a very small ascending aorta that is posteriorly located (measured 2 mm due to underlying aortic valvular atresia), as in the current case.

We believe the following 3 key factors supported the favorable outcome of our modified approach in this patient. (1) Avoidance of DKS anastomotic tension and kinking: the patient's markedly hypoplastic ascending aorta and posterior arch orientation, combined with the large, transversely oriented PA (Figure 1, B), rendered the classical DKS prone to distortion. Our technique directly implanted the PA into an augmented aortic arch patch, circumstantially avoiding tension or kinking inherent in the traditional DKS repair in this anatomy.

(2) Preservation of aberrant vascular anatomy and customized arch reconstruction to accommodate distorted anatomy: the patient's unique spatial orientation attributable to dextrocardia and situs solitus caused the great vessels and aortic arch to have an unusual course and position. Our technique of augmenting the hypoplastic aortic arch with a decellularized pulmonary homograft patch created a larger, more pliable neoaortic pathway. This tailored reconstruction minimized tension and distortion, providing a stable and durable systemic outflow tract better suited to this patient's anatomy. The presence of an aberrant right subclavian artery, left intact in our approach, maintained vital cerebral and right arm perfusion without additional dissection-related risks. This is consistent with previous reports that highlight the complexity of arch branch anomalies in this patient population.

(3) Our use of a valved Sano conduit constructed from a 5-mm Gore-Tex graft connected to a 6-mm valved aortic homograft represents another important innovation.4 We have been using this composite construct (PTFE to valved homograft) for all our Norwood procedures, but the valved portion is sewn distally to the pulmonary arterial confluence. We modified this in the current case to avoid any possible distortion or compression on the valve as a result of patient's current challenging anatomy. We chose to suture the homograft to the free wall of the systemic right ventricle (RV) instead. This had to be placed around the Gore-Tex ring, which created a stented, flow-limiting orifice that maintains valve competence while preventing conduit distortion and preserving anatomical alignment (Figure 1, C). Positioning the valved Sano conduit to the left of the main PA was critical to avoid compression of the diminutive ascending aorta and reduce the risk of hemodynamic compromise postoperatively. On the basis of availability, we use aortic or pulmonary homografts to provide the valved segment in our composite Sano conduit, typically sized between 6 and 8 mm to minimize size mismatch with the 5-mm ePTFE graft. Published long-term data5 demonstrate that although some catheter-based interventions occur, the rate is not significantly different compared with nonvalved conduits. We believe this valved conduit offers early postoperative benefits, especially valuable in lower-volume centers, by facilitating a smoother recovery.

Although femoral vein homografts have been used in the Sano conduits, we generally avoid this approach because of the longer conduit length required, which increases risk of compression and demands positioning behind the sternum on the right side. Our composite homograft/PTFE conduit is shorter, placed easily on the left side of the neoaorta, and better suited for off-pump second-stage Glenn procedures, as it maintains antegrade pulmonary flow and remains well away from surgical fields.

The use of modified Blalock-Taussig-Thomas shunt as in the standard Norwood would have been extremely difficult and probably risky in such anatomy because of the deeply located right PA. In fact, in the case described by Munshi and colleagues,2 the authors had to switch from a Blalock-Taussig-Thomas shunt to a Sano because of inadequate saturation of the patient coming off bypass, which most likely was related to compression of the shunt by the reconstructed aorta.

Outcomes for Norwood palliation in patients with HLHS and dextrocardia remain limited in the literature, with available data suggesting increased perioperative risk and poorer prognosis compared with typical left-sided cardiac anatomy. The altered spatial relations complicate arch reconstruction and cerebral perfusion strategies, and the rarity of these cases limits the surgeon's experience and standardized approaches. Nevertheless, our successful application of a tailored surgical technique with careful attention to anatomical challenges supports the feasibility of Norwood palliation in this population when modifications are appropriately applied.

In conclusion, Norwood palliation in HLHS with dextrocardia and situs solitus demands individualized surgical planning. Modifying the arch reconstruction to avoid DKS distortion, preserving arch vessel anomalies judiciously, and using a valved Sano conduit positioned to minimize compression can contribute to favorable outcomes. Continued reporting and analysis of these rare cases are vital to refining strategies and improving prognoses.
