Incidence and determinants for non-suicidal self-injury in an Italian prison in Milan (Feb–Oct 2023): a retrospective cohort analysis
Authors: Cristina Cairone, Sara Mazzilli, Calogero Carlino, Virginia Casigliani, Serena Fondelli, Maha Aon, Ruggero Giuliani, Filipa Alves da Costa, Roberto Ranieri, Lara Tavoschi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNon-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) is a complex and often overlooked public health concern in prison settings, particularly among marginalized populations. This retrospective cohort study investigated the incidence and determinants of NSSI among people living in prison (PLP) at San Vittore, a pre-trial detention facility in Milan, Italy, between February and June 2023. Data were collected and updated until October 1, 2023. Among 712 incarcerated men, 8% engaged in NSSI during their detention. Nearly 90% of all NSSI episodes involved individuals from North Africa. In this subgroup, NSSI was frequently motivated by requests to change cell location or to access medication, underscoring its communicative function within the prison environment. Multivariable analysis showed that, among North African PLP, younger age and a prior history of NSSI were significantly associated with increased risk of self-injury. Among individuals from other regions, psychiatric illness, previous NSSI, and previous suicide attempts were key risk factors, while having children and higher educational attainment were protective. These findings suggest that NSSI reflects not only psychological distress but also structural barriers to care and communication. The study highlights the urgent need for culturally competent, age-sensitive mental health interventions and systemic reforms. Introduction

People entering the prison system often present with significant health vulnerabilities, including high rates of mental illness, substance use disorders, and chronic physical conditions1,2. The prison environment, characterized by psychological stress, social isolation, limited access to quality healthcare, and exposure to violence, often exacerbates these conditions and contributes to the development or intensification of self-injurious behaviors and suicidal ideation3,4.

Data on the prevalence of self-harm in prisons is limited and often inconsistent, largely due to differences in definitions, reporting standards, and study designs. However, available evidence consistently shows markedly elevated rates of self-harm among people living in prison (PLP) compared to the general population.

In Ireland, between 2017 and 2019, the annual prevalence of non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) was 3.1% among incarcerated males and 18.4% among females5, compared to 0.2% in the general population in 20216. A systematic review by Favril et al. encompassing 35 studies from 20 countries reported a pooled prevalence of 3.8% for self-harm among incarcerated individuals, although none of the included studies were from low- or middle-income countries7.

The burden of suicide in Italian prisons is also alarming. The incidence of these behaviors among PLP is estimated to be 9–21 times higher than in the general population, with suicide being the leading cause of death in custody8. In 2023, at least 70 suicides were recorded in Italian prisons, increasing to 91 in 20249,10.

In Italy, research on NSSI within correctional settings is limited. Mucci et al. found that 17.7% of PLP reported lifetime deliberate self-harm, with 62.4% of these acts occurring during incarceration, and significant associations with psychotic disorders, borderline personality disorder, affective disorders, and polysubstance misuse11. Regional initiatives, such as the Emilia-Romagna suicide prevention program, emphasized situational stressors like solitary confinement and judicial hearings as major contributors12. According to the most recent 2025 Antigone Observatory Report, self-harm episodes in 58 Italian prisons increased by 40% between 2023–2024 and 2024–2025. This rise is disproportionate to the 9% increase in the overall incarcerated population, suggesting a worsening mental health crisis within Italian prisons10. This reports also provide concerning insights identifying a strong association between prison overcrowding and elevated rates of self-harm10. As of April 30, 2025, Italian prisons held 62,445 individuals in 190 facilities, including both pre-trial detention centres and long-term institutions, designed for 51,29213. Pre-trial status is a significant feature of the Italian prison system: approximately 15% of detainees nationwide are awaiting their first hearing, with an additional 9% pending appeal or serving non-final sentences13. Taken together, nearly one-fourth of the prison population in Italy does not have a definitive sentence, a status that is more common among foreign nationals (27.8%) than Italians (22.3%)10.

Foreign nationals constitute a substantial proportion of the incarcerated population, averaging 31.6% of adult detainees, although this varies widely across regions and types of institution. Facilities with the highest proportion of foreign nationals—often exceeding 50%—are predominantly pre-trial detention centres in northern Italy and Sardinia10. Among foreign nationals, the most represented nationalities are Moroccan, Romanian, Tunisian, Albanian, and Nigerian13. The Antigone Report also highlights that the increase in NSSI rates is particularly pronounced in pre-trial detention facilities10.

Common forms of NSSI in prisons include cutting and scratching, though self-injurious behaviors span a wide spectrum varying in method, lethality, and psychological drivers1,5,14. This heterogeneity reflects a complex and multifaceted behavioral landscape that poses significant challenges to standardized assessment and prevention strategies. The risk factors associated with NSSI in prison populations are similarly complex. The most consistently identified predictors of NSSI include current or past suicidal ideation, previous self-harm, major depressive disorder, borderline personality disorder, and polysubstance use7,15. Sex differences are also evident: studies from England and Wales, Germany, and Morocco have reported significantly higher rates of NSSI among incarcerated women16–18

Healthcare in Italian prisons has been formally integrated into the regional health system since the 2008 penitentiary healthcare reform. Under this system, health services—including mental health care—are provided by local health authorities and governed in the same way as community-based care19. Despite this integration, significant gaps remain in service delivery, particularly in psychiatric care, due to regional funding disparities, staff shortages, and infrastructural limitations10.

In Italy, psychological distress among PLP is pervasive. According to the Antigone Observatory, nearly 12% of the prison population has a diagnosed severe psychiatric disorder, and 20% receive regular prescriptions for mood stabilizers, antipsychotics, or antidepressants—compared to only 7% antidepressant use and under 2% antipsychotic use in the general population. Sedative use is similarly disproportionate: 40% of PLP receive hypnotics or sedatives versus just 5–7% of the general public9,20.

Environmental and systemic conditions within prisons further exacerbate risk of NSSI. Overcrowding, solitary confinement, disciplinary sanctions, disrupted interpersonal relationships due to frequent cellmate changes, and exposure to physical or sexual violence are all recognized contributors to NSSI21–24 Many PLP enter incarceration already burdened by adverse social determinants—poverty, trauma, unstable housing, and limited education—that compound once inside the carceral system7,25–27.

The consequences of self-harm extend beyond the individuals directly affected, significantly impacting prison staff and healthcare workers through increased occupational stress, emotional exhaustion, and burnout, with potential implications for institutional safety and the quality of care28.

Despite the use of multiple screening tools (e.g., CORE-OM, PriSnQuest, BSL-23, SHI, PHQ-9), existing literature suggests that none are sufficiently predictive of NSSI within prison settings. Most available instruments show limited accuracy in identifying individuals at risk of self-injury29. Moreover, only a small number of studies have assessed the predictive validity of screening approaches specifically in incarcerated populations. This limitation is particularly critical in high-turnover, pre-trial institutions, where structural, legal, and cultural stressors may heighten psychological vulnerability but are rarely captured by standard assessments. In the Italian context, although national and regional reports provide valuable insights, empirical research remains scarce. This gap underscores the need for robust, data-driven approaches to prevention, tailored to the unique needs and vulnerabilities of incarcerated populations.

This study aims to examine the incidence, characteristics, and key determinants of NSSI among PLP in Italy, with a focus on identifying modifiable risk factors that could inform more effective prevention strategies.

Methods

Study setting and population

This retrospective longitudinal study was conducted at San Vittore, a pre-trial detention facility located in Milan, Italy. The prison has a nominal capacity of approximately 700 individuals but frequently accommodates up to 1,100 people due to chronic overcrowding30. Although the institution includes both male and female units, these are completely separate in terms of management, staff, and daily operations. During the study period, data collection was authorised only for the male section, which also accounted for more than 90% of the total PLP. More than 60% of PLP at San Vittore were foreign nationals, predominantly from North Africa, consistent with trends observed in other urban detention centres in northern Italy10. San Vittore receives an average of 200 new admissions per month, with a mean detention duration of approximately four months. The institution is characterized by outdated infrastructure and limited access to essential services, including healthcare.

The study cohort included all male individuals whose incarceration at San Vittore began between February 1 and June 6, 2023. Data were collected and updated until October 1, 2023. Clinical and sociodemographic data were retrospectively extracted from routine prison medical records. Only individuals with complete medical records available during the period of interest were included in the analysis.

Informed consent was sought from all participants who remained in custody at the time of data extraction. For those who had been released, up to three contact attempts were made. Individuals who could not be reached were provided with a study information sheet posted on the official website of ASST Santi Paolo e Carlo, including contact details allowing them to request exclusion from the study.

Procedures

Standard medical assessment

Healthcare services within San Vittore Prison are provided by the San Paolo University Hospital (ASST Santi Paolo e Carlo, Milan), under the coordination of the Regional Department of Public Health of Lombardy. The multidisciplinary health team includes general practitioners, nurses, health care assistants, psychiatrists, and clinical psychologists. A dedicated on-site emergency medical unit, operating 24 h a day, ensures timely response to urgent health issues. During daytime hours (07:00–20:00), the facility is staffed by two physicians and two nurses; overnight (20:00–07:00), one physician and one nurse are present. Mental health services are available daily from 09:00 to 17:00, with a team composed of two clinical psychologists and three psychiatrists.

Upon entry to the facility, and in accordance with international guidelines31, all individuals undergo a standardized medical assessment conducted by a general practitioner within the emergency unit. This initial evaluation includes a full physical examination, comprehensive clinical history, and administration of a structured questionnaire to assess suicide risk. In cases where language barriers exist, a cultural mediator is involved to ensure equitable access to care. Following the initial assessment, all newly admitted individuals receive a secondary psychological evaluation conducted by a clinical psychologist. This assessment serves to confirm the individual’s suicide risk status and to further explore psychological vulnerabilities. Based on the outcomes of both evaluations, referrals are made as needed to specialized services, including psychiatric care. As part of the standard admission protocol, a biological sample (hair) is collected to conduct toxicological screening for recent or ongoing substance use, including alcohol and cannabis. Individuals diagnosed with a substance use disorder are offered access to medical and psychosocial support through the harm reduction program. Participation in all procedures is voluntary, and individuals have the right to decline any support or treatment offered.

Management of non-suicidal self-injury

In the event of a NSSI, the individual is promptly referred to the on-site emergency medical unit, where a general practitioner assesses the injury and provides initial medical care. When advanced or specialized treatment is required, the person is transferred to an external hospital.

Following medical stabilization, the individual undergoes a psychological evaluation conducted by the prison mental health team to assess the risk of recurrence or progression to suicidal behavior. Based on the clinical assessment, the individual may be temporarily relocated to a secure area with enhanced surveillance. Referral to psychiatric services or enrollment in a multidisciplinary mental health care plan—coordinated by psychologists and psychiatrists—may also be initiated as appropriate.

The mental health team schedules ongoing follow-up evaluations to monitor the individual’s psychological status and determine the appropriate timing for reintegration into the general prison population.

Data collection and variables

Data were obtained through retrospective medical record review. For each individual, information was extracted from the date of incarceration until release or October 1, 2023, whichever occurred first. This design allowed for a minimum follow-up period of four months, exceeding the average length of stay at the facility. Data extraction was conducted using REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture), a secure, web-based platform designed to ensure data integrity and confidentiality.

The following data were collected: (i) demographics: age, nationality, residence prior to incarceration, education level, social support network; (ii) legal status: type of crime, date of incarceration and release; (iii) health status: substance use, chronic or psychiatric conditions, suicidal ideation and previous NSSI; (iv) NSSI episodes: date, method, severity, and reported motivations.

NSSI is commonly defined as the deliberate, direct destruction of one’s own body tissue without suicidal intent32,33. While this definition traditionally excludes behaviors such as the ingestion of foreign objects, an emerging body of literature advocates for a broader, function-based conceptualization of NSSI. In this view, the underlying psychological purpose, such as emotional regulation, dissociation relief, or self-punishment, takes precedence over the specific method used33,34. In line with this functional perspective, and consistent with clinical observations in high-risk populations, this study adopted an expanded definition of NSSI, which includes repetitive, intentional ingestion of objects when reported as clearly non-suicidal in nature. The severity of NSSI was categorised into three levels: low requiring nursing-level care only; moderate—requiring medical treatment by a physician on-site; high—requiring transfer to an external hospital for advanced care.

Data analysis

Descriptive analysis

Descriptive statistics were first used to summarize the characteristics of the study population. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. Differences between groups were assessed using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact test.

Statistical analysis

We employed a combination of statistical models to investigate individual-level risk factors associated with the occurrence, timing, and frequency of NSSI during incarceration.

To assess the association between covariates and the risk of experiencing at least one NSSI episode (binary outcome) during the observation period, we used univariable and multivariable Poisson regression models with robust standard errors to estimate risk ratios (RR) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The binary outcome was defined as the presence (1) or absence (0) of any NSSI event during the observation period. Variables with clinical relevance or statistical significance in univariable analysis (p < 0.05) were considered for inclusion in the multivariable model. A bidirectional stepwise selection procedure based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) was applied to identify the most parsimonious model.

Given that the vast majority of NSSI episodes occurred among individuals of North African origin, region of origin was explored as a potential effect modifier and stratified analyses were conducted by region of origin (North Africa vs other). However, because the number of NSSI cases among non–North African individuals was very small and the data were characterized by a large imbalance between cases and non-cases, the risk factor analysis was deemed to have insufficient statistical power. Therefore, all analytical analyses were restricted to the North African subgroup to ensure adequate statistical power and reliability.

Time to first NSSI episode was analyzed using Cox proportional hazards regression. Univariable and multivariable models were fitted, and hazard ratios with 95% CIs were reported. Multivariable model selection was performed using a bidirectional stepwise approach based on AIC, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

To evaluate factors associated with the number of NSSI episodes per individual over time, we used a zero-inflated negative binomial (ZINB) regression model, which accounts for overdispersion and excess zero counts in the outcome distribution. In the first step, univariable ZINB models were fitted for each covariate individually, specifying a constant-only zero-inflation component. Variables that were statistically significant in univariable models were included in a multivariable ZINB model, with backward stepwise selection based on AIC. The ZINB model has two components: the count model estimates the association between covariates and the number of NSSI episodes among individuals at risk; the zero-inflation component models the probability of being in the “structural zero” group—those with no likelihood of engaging in NSSI.

All analyses were performed using the R statistical environment (version 4.3.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Data security and ethical approval

This study was submitted to the Ethics Review Board of ASST Santi Paolo e Carlo (Comitato Etico Territoriale Lombardia 1) (N: CET 258-2025, July 7, 2025). The study fully respected the principles laid out in the Helsinki declaration, namely in what concerns autonomy.

Data management complied with the European Union General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR 2016/679) as well as relevant Italian legislation, including Legislative Decree No. 196/2003 (Italian Data Protection Code) as amended by Legislative Decree No. 101/2018.

Results

Descriptive analysis

Between February 1 and June 6, 2023, a total of 966 new admissions were recorded at San Vittore prison. We were able to recruit 712 individuals for the study, including 8 individuals who had two separate admissions, resulting in a total of 720 prison entries. The median length of stay in prison was 49 days (interquartile range (IQR): 18–188). The largest group was Italians (249; 35.0%), followed by individuals from North Africa (245; 34.4%). European non-Italians were 72 (10.1%), individuals from the Americas 54 (7.6%), Sub-Saharan Africa 48 (6.7%), Asia 17 (2.4%), other countries 15 (2.1%), and the Middle East/Caucasus 7 (1.0%).

Out of 712 individuals, 57 (8.0%) reported at least one episode of self-injury, of whom 42 (73.7%) engaged in NSSI more than once (Fig. S1, supplemental material). The median number of NSSIs per individual was 3 (Range: 1–26; IQR: 1–6) (Fig. 1). Among individuals who engaged in NSSI, the median number of days between prison entry and the first episode was 26 (IQR 9–45) (Fig. 2). PLP with self-injury episodes had a lower median age (23 years, IQR 21–28) compared to those without (36 years, IQR 28–46). Among those under 26 years old, 38 (27.5%) reported self-injury, while in the oldest age group (> 45 years), no episodes were reported. In terms of nationality, the proportion of individuals with self-injury episodes was higher among those from North Africa (48 out of 245; 19.6%) and Sub-Saharan Africa (2 out of 48; 4.2%). Among individuals from Italy, 5 out of 254 (2.0%) reported self-injury. A single episode was reported among European (non-Italians) individuals from Eastern Europe (1/71; 1.4%). No episodes were observed among individuals from Central/South America (0/54), Asia (0/17), or the Middle East (0/7). (Table S1, supplemental material).

Statistical analysis

Univariable and multivariable Poisson regression analyses

In the univariable analysis, older age was significantly associated with a lower risk of self-injury. Compared with individuals younger than 26 years, those aged 25–35 years had a RR of 0.28 (95 CI 0.14–0.56; p < 0.001), and those older than 35 years had an even lower risk (RR 0.03, 95 CI 0.00–0.23; p < 0.001). Having children was also associated with a reduced risk (RR 0.41, 95 CI 0.18–0.91; p = 0.03), as was being in a relationship (RR for single status 3.42, 95 CI 1.35–8.64; p = 0.01). Individuals charged with drug-related offenses had a significantly lower risk of self-injury compared with those charged with property crimes (RR 0.34, 95 CI 0.13–0.87; p = 0.02). Poor knowledge of the Italian language was associated with increased risk (RR 4.03, 95 CI 1.17–13.83; p = 0.03), while other levels of language knowledge were not significantly associated. Previous NSSI was a strong predictor of current self-injury (RR 3.17, 95 CI 1.71–5.86; p < 0.001). Other factors, including first incarceration, education level, occupational status, and psychiatric or organic disease, did not show significant associations in univariable models.

In the multivariable model, age remained a significant protective factor. Individuals aged 25–35 years had a significantly lower risk of self-injury (adjusted RR 0.22, 95 CI 0.10–0.50; p < 0.001), as did those over 35 years (adjusted RR 0.04, 95 CI 0.01–0.32; p = 0.002). Previous self-injury remained a strong predictor (adjusted RR 2.79, 95 CI 1.47–5.29; p = 0.002). In the North Africa subgroup, results from a Cox proportional hazards model (available in the supplemental material, Table S2) showed that the same covariates were significantly associated with NSSI (Table 4).

Univariable and multivariable ZINB regression analyses

In the univariable analysis permanent residence was associated with a higher number of events (Incidence Rate Ratio (IRR) = 2.68, p = 0.008), as was substance use (IRR = 3.50, p = 0.007) and a history of self-harming behaviors (IRR = 4.30, p = 0.002). Marital status also was associated, with being single linked to increased event counts (IRR = 5.08, p = 0.002). The presence of organic disorders was significantly associated with a lower number of events (IRR = 0.036, p = 0.005), as was having committed drug-related offenses (IRR = 0.13, p < 0.001). Age category also showed a significant association: compared to the youngest group, individuals aged 26–35 and over 35 had notably lower event counts (IRR = 0.19, p < 0.001, and IRR = 0.005, p < 0.001, respectively) (Table S3, supplemental material).

In the multivariable ZINB model, permanent residence, substance use, and a history of self-harming behaviors were significantly associated with an increased number of events. Specifically, individuals with substance use had more than six times the rate (IRR = 6.20, p < 0.001), and individuals with prior self-harm had about twice the rate (IRR = 2.07, p = 0.02). Age also played a role: individuals aged 26–35 showed a significantly lower event rate compared to younger PLP (IRR = 0.33, p = 0.006), while the effect for those over 35 was not statistically significant. Permanent residence was associated with a higher number of events (IRR = 3.38, p < 0.001). In the zero-inflation component, individuals aged 26–35 were significantly more likely to belong to the zero-inflated group (Odds ratio (OR) = 8.51, p < 0.001), suggesting they had higher odds of not engaging in any events at all. Conversely, a history of self-harm was associated with lower odds of zero inflation (OR = 0.23, p = 0.005), indicating that these individuals were less likely to have zero event counts. No significant effects were observed for organic disorders or substance use in the zero-inflation part of the model (Table 5). Variance inflation factors were all below 1.1, indicating no multicollinearity issues.

Discussion

This study provides novel evidence on the burden and determinants of NSSI in an Italian custodial setting, with an overall incidence of 8.0% during the study period. This rate is more than twice the average incidence reported in international prison cohorts (typically around 3–4)7 and conceals substantial heterogeneity. NSSI was reported by nearly one in five individuals from North Africa (19.4%) compared with approximately 2% among individuals from Italy or other regions. These findings point to a disproportionate burden in specific subgroups and underscore the need for culturally sensitive and targeted prevention strategies within prison health systems.

Nearly 90% of all NSSI episodes involved individuals from North Africa. While the literature on NSSI in North African prison populations is extremely limited18, available data suggest that individuals from this region constitute a significant portion of the foreign prison population in Italy, about 38%, with regional variation9. In two facilities with the highest NSSI rates, including San Vittore, foreign-born individuals represent over 60% of PLP.

The high proportion of individuals from North Africa may be influenced by structural factors such as precarious legal status, unstable housing or employment, and disproportionate policing in marginalised urban areas. In Northern Italy, where recent migration flows have been concentrated and prison infrastructure is more extensive, these dynamics may be further amplified. The Antigone Report also highlights a disproportionate use of pre-trial detention for foreign nationals and a more frequent application of preventive custody, particularly among those lacking a fixed domicile or community ties. Collectively, these factors contribute to higher incarceration rates among North African individuals, even when charges involve lower-severity or non-violent offences10.

This pattern suggests that the demographic composition of the prison population may partly explain the observed rates. It is plausible that certain self-injurious behaviours are culturally embedded; shaped by beliefs or ritual practices that render them socially meaningful or acceptable within specific groups. In addition, people with a migration background often face a complex interplay of stressors, including linguistic challenges, cultural dislocation, and separation from family networks35. Those arriving through irregular routes may also carry trauma linked to transit experiences or asylum processes36. In a general population studies conducted in Germany, migration has been associated with higher risk of suicidal ideation and NSSI, particularly when compounded by school exclusion or discrimination37.

In custodial settings, foreign-born individuals are frequently exposed to structural disadvantages. United Kingdom’s data show elevated levels of psychological distress compared to native-born people living in prison, alongside reduced access to appropriate care and social contact38. Foreign-born individuals have specific cultural and health-care needs that require tailored management and rehabilitation programmes39. These conditions may exacerbate feelings of isolation and emotional dysregulation, contributing to self-injurious behaviour.

Among individuals from North Africa, younger age emerged as the strongest predictor of NSSI, with markedly elevated risk among those under 26 years. This finding is particularly relevant in light of the demographic composition of the prison population: North African persons PLP tend to be significantly younger than their counterparts from other nationalities. In our sample, the median age among North African individuals was 28, compared to 39 among PLP from other regions. This age gap may partially account for the disproportionate NSSI burden observed in this subgroup and suggests youth-specific vulnerabilities. This trend is consistent with meta-analytic evidence indicating that NSSI is most prevalent in adolescents and young adults, with lifetime prevalence rates up to 18 among adolescents compared to under 4 among adults40. Additional reviews have confirmed that the behavior tends to onset in mid-adolescence and declines significantly by early adulthood41. Importantly, no individuals over the age of 45 in our sample reported engagement in NSSI, aligning with evidence from population-based studies that document a sharp age-related decline in NSSI prevalence. Two large studies conducted in Germany42 and in England43, found that lifetime NSSI rates dropped significantly with age, suggesting that self-injurious behaviours are predominantly concentrated in adolescence and early adulthood, with markedly lower occurrence in mid-to-late adulthood. This reinforces the importance of age-sensitive screening strategies, especially in high-risk developmental windows.

The medical management of NSSI events places a significant burden on both healthcare personnel and custodial staff. In San Vittore prison, each NSSI episode requires an estimated 6 h and 30 min of medical staff time, including approximately 90 min for immediate clinical care and initial psychological support, followed by an additional 5 h over the subsequent month for wound care and weekly psychological support sessions. Beyond the logistical demands, NSSI incidents exert a considerable emotional toll on healthcare providers and custodial staff, particularly those who are the first to respond28. This emotional burden may affect coping strategies and compromise the overall quality of care. Prison staff are pivotal in both the prevention and management of NSSI. However, evidence highlights a degree of role tension: while some officers adopt empathetic and proactive approaches, others may respond with scepticism or punitive attitudes. This variation is influenced by several factors, including insufficient training, high workloads, and unsupportive institutional culture. The establishment of supportive, trusting relationships between officers and PLP has been associated with a reduction in self-harm risk. In contrast, neglect or punitive responses have been shown to exacerbate it. Custodial staff frequently report feelings of inadequacy and a lack of support in managing self-harm incidents, underlining the urgent need for targeted training and organisational support44.

Our study corroborates existing literature by identifying a prior history of NSSI or suicide attempts as a consistent risk factor across groups. This finding aligns with research indicating that NSSI is often a chronic, recurrent behavior associated with emotion dysregulation and limited access to mental health care. Incarcerated individuals may experience heightened emotional distress due to the restrictive environment, leading to increased instances of NSSI as a coping mechanism.

Importantly, our data suggest that NSSI serves not only as an emotional outlet but also as a form of institutional communication—a way for individuals to express distress, negotiate their conditions, or protest unmet needs. This dual function has been described in other forensic contexts: for example, Dixon-Gordon et al.45 found that incarcerated individuals often self-injure both to regulate emotions and to convey suffering or urgency to prison staff. In Moroccan prisons, self-harm has even been described as a “normalized language” of distress and resistance18. Addressing both the emotional and communicative dimensions of NSSI is therefore essential to designing effective clinical and systemic interventions.

This communicative function of NSSI takes on particular significance in the context of prison overcrowding, where reduced staff time and overstretched resources make it more difficult for incarcerated individuals to access care or have their needs acknowledged. When health professionals are less available and system navigation is more difficult, more visible or extreme behaviours, such as NSSI, may be used as a means of being seen or heard.

According to the XXI Antigone Report on Detention Conditions, the incidence of self-harm in Italian prisons has increased significantly from 2024 to 2025, alongside a marked rise in overcrowding rates10. This trend has accelerated following the implementation of stricter immigration and sentencing policies in Italy in 2023 (Decreto Cutro46) which has contributed to a substantial increase in the number of PLP. These developments underscore the urgency of the issue: conditions are deteriorating, and the risk environment for self-injury and suicide is intensifying.

Given this context, we strongly recommend further research to explore the cultural, structural, and psychosocial determinants of NSSI in prison settings. Qualitative studies are needed to better understand the subjective and symbolic meanings of self-harm, especially in overrepresented groups such as North African PLP. Such research should also extend to other prison systems to assess whether similar patterns emerge elsewhere. With rising incarceration rates and mounting evidence of self-injurious and suicidal behaviour, the need for action is increasingly urgent.

This study has several limitations. First, the analysis was restricted to male PLP, limiting the generalizability of the findings; therefore, they should not be directly extrapolated to female populations. Evidence indicates that self-harm rates in prison are higher among women than men, and risk profiles may differ5. Second, incomplete follow-up due to transfers or alternative custodial measures may have led to underestimation of NSSI incidence. Third, reliance on routinely collected clinical records may have resulted in underreporting of minor or unreported NSSI episodes. Moreover, the medical records did not report the instruments used to inflict NSSI. As a result, we were unable to analyse or characterise the methods of self-injury in detail. Detailed information on the means of self-harm could provide valuable insights for risk assessment, prevention strategies, and clinical management, and future studies should aim to include this level of detail. Fourth, the single-site design and short observation window may limit external validity, as prison environments vary in prison population composition, crowding, staffing, and access to mental health services. The short follow-up period also prevents any possible seasonal effect on the frequency of self-harm events or the identification of periods of increased risk in times of the year not included in the study. Addressing these limitations in future prospective, multi-site studies with comprehensive data collection will strengthen the evidence base on NSSI risk in correctional settings.

Conclusions

These findings highlight the urgent need for differentiated approaches to self-harm prevention in custodial settings, particularly among young, foreign-born individuals from North Africa. To address the outstanding disparities in NSSI incidence, the implementation of culturally competent care models, with healthcare and custodial staff receiving training in cross-cultural communication, trauma-informed care, and the identification of psychological distress in diverse populations may be endorsed. Tailored psychosocial support programmes—delivered in PLP native languages and sensitive to their sociocultural backgrounds—could significantly improve engagement, reduce isolation, and prevent escalation to acute self-injury. In parallel, age-sensitive interventions are warranted, given the elevated risk of NSSI among younger PLP.

Importantly, the growing burden of NSSI places additional pressure on an already strained prison healthcare system. Without proactive and preventive strategies, repeated self-injury episodes risk overwhelming limited clinical resources and exacerbating existing gaps in care delivery. From both clinical and economic perspectives, investing in targeted, evidence-based interventions is critical to improving outcomes and reducing long-term costs associated with unmanaged mental health crises.

Furthermore, structural reforms are urgently needed to address institutional drivers of vulnerability, including limited healthcare access, overcrowding, insufficient educational and rehabilitative opportunities, and the often punitive culture of prison environments. Enhancing access to social support, reducing procedural isolation, and fostering trusting relationships between PLP and staff are critical components of a comprehensive and effective prevention framework.

Finally, these findings call for enhanced surveillance systems to monitor self-injury trends across subpopulations, and for inter-sectoral coordination between prison authorities, public health services, and migrant health specialists. Future research should adopt longitudinal and participatory approaches to deepen understanding of lived experiences and inform the co-design of interventions that are both evidence-based and context-specific.

Without targeted and systemic efforts, NSSI will remain an under-addressed indicator of suffering and inequity within correctional health systems, reflecting broader failures to uphold the dignity and mental wellbeing of all individuals in custody.
