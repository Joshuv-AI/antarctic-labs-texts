Relationships of motivation, self-efficacy and social support with healthy eating behaviours among adolescents
Authors: Natalia Fraczek, Lorraine B Robbins, Hesam A Varpaei, Kimberly Arcoleo, Jiying Ling
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposePsychosocial factors contributing to the continued failure of adolescents to follow the US Dietary Guidelines for Americans (2020–2025) remain poorly understood. This study investigated the relationships of adolescents’ autonomous motivation, self-efficacy and social support with the healthy eating index (HEI) total (primary outcome), whole fruit, total fruit, vegetable, sodium, added sugar and caloric intake (secondary outcomes).MethodsA secondary analysis of baseline data from a randomised controlled trial was conducted. A total of 935 5th–8th graders, aged 10–14 years, were enrolled. Generalised linear models and structural equation models examined relationships of motivation, social support and self-efficacy for healthy eating with healthy eating behaviours.ResultsIn this sample (51% female, mean age=12.08±0.99), 48.24% were black/African American, and 48.2% were overweight/obese. In generalised linear models, family social support was significantly associated with HEI fruit (β=0.16, p=0.02) and higher caloric intake (β=44.09, p=0.03). Additionally, friend social support was significantly associated with HEI sodium (lower sodium intake; β=0.23, p=0.01). Structural equation models showed that through motivation, social support indirectly improved HEI total (β=0.09, p=0.006) and whole fruit intake (β=0.07, p=0.04) and increased HEI added sugar (lower added sugar intake; β=0.07, p=0.04). Through self-efficacy, social support was indirectly associated with lower HEI sodium (higher sodium intake; β=–0.04, p=0.04).DiscussionThese findings add to the growing body of research on adolescent nutrition by emphasising the complex interplay of psychosocial factors in shaping specific dietary behaviours.Trial registration numberNCT04213014.

Introduction

Although meeting the 2020–2025 Dietary Guidelines for Americans issued by the US Departments of Agriculture and Health and Human Services1 is imperative for reducing the risk of obesity and cardiovascular disease,1 2 many adolescents do not meet these recommendations. In the USA, the average daily intake of vegetables and fruits by adolescents, aged 9 to 13, is below the recommended amounts.1 Close to 80% of US adolescents in this age range exceed recommendations for added sugar. Interestingly, studies worldwide have also shown that adolescents are nutritionally deficient.3 These concerning findings clearly indicate an urgent need to understand factors contributing to the problem.

Age, sex at birth, race/ethnicity, body mass index (BMI) and family income shape how adolescents adhere to recommended dietary patterns. Healthy Eating Index (HEI) is a measure of how closely an individual’s diet aligns with the 2020–2025 Dietary Guidelines for Americans.1 HEI scores are typically consistent between males and females.4 However, one study found that adolescent females tend to have higher HEI scores for fruits and vegetables compared with males.5 Across all age groups, individuals who identify as black or African American consume fewer vegetables than those who are white.6 Interestingly, among 12–18-year-old adolescents with obesity, those classified as metabolically healthy obese (eg, lower incidence of cardiovascular disease and type II diabetes) have been shown to have higher HEI total scores than those who have metabolically abnormal obesity.7 A systematic review focusing on the USA, Europe, Canada, Australia and New Zealand showed that better dietary patterns among adolescents were associated with higher parental socioeconomic status.8 While international research on adolescent HEI provides valuable insights into global dietary trends, further investigation is needed to understand how demographics, BMI, parent income and other factors influence HEI among young adolescents in the USA.

One framework that focuses on motivation and helps in understanding factors that influence healthy eating is the self-determination theory (SDT). According to SDT, three basic psychological needs, including autonomy, competence (self-efficacy) and relatedness (social support), guide an individual’s behaviours and choices, including their dietary habits.9 Autonomous motivation, which is characterised by enjoyment of the behaviour (intrinsic motivation) and/or the personal importance attached to an outcome (identified regulation),10 has been associated with healthier eating.1113

Self-efficacy, or the belief in one’s ability to succeed or competence in specific tasks, influences dietary behaviours. Across both younger and older adolescent age groups, those with higher healthy eating self-efficacy have been found to be able to resist unhealthy foods and make informed healthy dietary choices.14 15 Conversely, low self-efficacy can lead to unhealthy eating behaviours, as individuals may feel powerless to make lasting changes.16 Research has shown that racial and ethnic differences affect adolescents’ self-efficacy in maintaining a healthy diet, with some groups (eg, based on gender or geographical location) perceiving more barriers to healthy eating than others.16 17 Across various ages, higher self-efficacy is linked to healthier eating.12 16 Understanding self-efficacy in relation to healthy eating and how demographic factors play a role can help with identifying strategies that empower young adolescents to make healthy choices.

The presence of a supportive social network is also associated with improved dietary outcomes.18 19 Support from friends and family can influence adolescent eating behaviours, but the nature of this support may differ.20 For example, one study examining diet quality and eating behaviour among minority youth found that African American youth perceived that support from friends reinforced unhealthy eating behaviours and support from parents reinforced healthy eating behaviours.20

Despite several studies examining HEI in adolescents,4 6 several gaps remain in the literature. While some studies have investigated groups of adolescents whose ages were similar to those included in the current study,6 17 others looked at older age groups12 or had sample sizes that were small; or a comprehensive understanding of the psychosocial factors influencing dietary habits was not evident. Previous research has also explored factors related to dietary quality such as parental income, sex at birth and BMI, but often in specific regions or populations outside the USA.5 21 Other studies have focused on demographic factors or specific food groups (eg, vegetables, sugar intake) but did not examine the interplay between motivation, self-efficacy and social support in shaping healthy eating behaviours.4 22

Therefore, the current study purpose was to: (1) investigate the relationships of young adolescents’ autonomous motivation, self-efficacy and social support for healthy eating with healthy eating behaviours (measured via HEI) and whole fruit, total fruit, vegetable, sodium, added sugar and caloric intake. Findings can be used to inform the design of interventions for increasing healthy eating among diverse young adolescent populations.

Method

Participants and procedures

This study is a secondary (cross-sectional) analysis of baseline data from an individual randomised controlled trial called Guys/Girls Opt for Activities for Life (GOAL).23 The ongoing trial’s main objective is to evaluate the effectiveness of the GOAL physical activity and nutrition intervention on improving adolescents’ per cent body fat, BMI, cardiorespiratory fitness, physical activity, diet quality and quality of life. Another aim is to determine the effect of the intervention on adolescents’ perceived social support, self-efficacy and autonomous motivation for healthy eating and physical activity. The study is being conducted in 14 schools predominately located in urban areas in the Midwestern USA.23 A total of 935 adolescents in grades 5 through 8 are enrolled in the trial. Baseline data were collected in the fall of 2022 (four schools), 2023 (five schools) and 2024 (five schools). The Michigan State University Biomedical and Health Institutional Review Board provided approval to conduct the study (ID 202500240).

To participate, adolescents had to meet the following inclusion criteria: age 10–14 years old; capable of attending a GOAL after-school physical activity and healthy eating/cooking programme offered 2 days a week for 13 weeks if randomly assigned to the intervention; available for a follow-up assessment 9 months after the programme concluded and proficient in English (or have access to an interpreter). Exclusion criteria included any mental or physical health conditions that could hinder safe participation in the intervention; use of medications that affect appetite, weight or growth; and academic or behavioural issues identified by school authorities as problematic.23

Recruitment

Adolescents were recruited at school assemblies, lunches, study or library periods, open houses and other school events as well as in classrooms. After school administrators’ approval, information about the study was shared with parents and adolescents via emails, text messages and/or the school’s social media site. Adolescents or their parents received a flyer with details and a QR code linking to the consent and assent forms. Adolescents were instructed to share the flyer with their parents before completing any forms. Once consent was obtained, parents completed a brief demographic survey. After adolescents assented, they completed an online eligibility screening and baseline survey.23

Measures

Demographics

Data were collected on adolescents’ age, date of birth, sex at birth, academic grade, race, ethnicity, socioeconomic status and BMI. To determine socioeconomic status, parents were asked on the consent form to report the family’s annual income, parents’ educational level and any participation by their adolescent in the free/reduced price lunch programme at school.

Body mass index

BMI was estimated by dividing adolescents’ body weight (in kilograms) by the square of their height (in metres). Based on BMI for age and sex, adolescents’ weight status was categorised as underweight (<5th percentile), healthy weight (5th to <85th percentile), overweight (85th to <95th percentile), obese (≥95th percentile) or severely obese (≥120% of the 95th percentile or BMI≥ 35).24

Motivation for healthy eating

Motivation was evaluated using the Healthy Eating Behavior Scale, derived from the original reliable and valid 24-item scale.25 Some scale items were adapted to increase clarity and relevance for young adolescents. To assess autonomous motivation for healthy eating, 8 items associated with the identified regulation (four items) and intrinsic (four items) motivation subscales were used. Cronbach’s alpha (α) for the eight items was acceptable at 0.94 (identified; α=0.82, intrinsic; α=0.86). Five response choices were (0) not very true for me, (1) rarely true for me, (2) sometimes true for me, (3) usually true for me and (4) very true for me. Higher scores reflected greater autonomous motivation for healthy eating.

Self-efficacy for healthy eating

Self-efficacy for healthy eating was measured using a reliable 9-item scale developed for adolescents.26 This scale assessed self-confidence in making healthy food choices with friends and family (Cronbach’s α=0.86). Response choices included (1) not at all sure, (2) not very sure, (3) somewhat sure, (4) very sure. Higher scores indicated greater self-efficacy.

Social support for healthy eating

Social support for healthy eating was measured using the Family Support for Eating and Friend Support for Eating Scales.27 Adolescents reported the support received during the past 3 months. These scales demonstrated acceptable reliability and validity.27 Both scales were slightly modified to reduce the response burden, omit double negatives and increase clarity. Social support received was assessed using eight items (family, 4 items; friends, 4 items). Response choices ranged from never (1) to very often (5), and a higher score reflected greater support. The adapted scale had a Cronbach’s α=0.88 (family; α=0.83, friend; α=0.92).

Diet quality

Within a 2-week period at baseline, each adolescent completed two 24-hour dietary recalls conducted one-on-one with a trained research assistant. The University of Minnesota Nutrition Data System for Research (NDSR; University of Minnesota Nutrition Coordinating Center, Minneapolis, Minnesota) was used to capture these data. The 24-hour recall method has been validated with adolescents.28 Healthy eating was assessed using the HEI, which is a measure that evaluates how an individual’s food intake aligns with the 2020–2025 Dietary Guidelines for Americans’1 recommendations. According to the NDSR, the HEI score is composed of 13 components that reflect various food groups and dietary elements. In the current study, HEI total and five components (three adequacy components, including foods or dietary elements that are encouraged: fruits, whole fruits and vegetables; and two moderation components, including foods or dietary elements for which consumption limits are recommended: sodium and added sugar), in addition to caloric intake, were each examined separately. Scores ranged from 0 to 100. For the adequacy components, higher scores indicated a healthier diet. For the moderation components, higher scores reflected a lower intake.

Data analysis

SAS V.9.4 (copyright 2017, SAS Institute) was used for the descriptive analyses, and MPlus V.8.11 was used for the structural equation model (SEM) analyses. Descriptive statistics were conducted (ie, mean, SD, frequency, percentage; table 1). Generalised linear models (GLM) were used to assess the relationship of motivation, social support and self-efficacy for healthy eating (independent variables) with HEI total (primary outcome (dependent variable)) and fruit, whole fruit, vegetable, sodium, added sugar and caloric intake (secondary outcomes). All GLMs were conducted as unadjusted crude univariate analysis and as multivariate (adjusted for biological sex, age, parental annual household income, race/ethnicity and BMI z-score24). Separate SEMs were used to examine the direct and indirect effects of social support, motivation and self-efficacy for healthy eating on the HEI total score, each of the five components, and caloric intake. A latent variable for motivation was comprised of the observed variables for identified regulation and intrinsic motivation to specifically reflect autonomous motivation. Exogenous variables for the direct effects on the total social support and HEI variables were child’s sex, race/ethnicity, BMI and family income.

Results

Descriptives

A total of 935 adolescents were enrolled (51% female, mean age=12.08±0.99), of whom 48.24% were black/African American, and nearly half of the adolescents in the sample were overweight or obese (48.2%; table 1). In univariate Pearson correlation analyses (online supplemental table 1), perceived self-efficacy was positively associated with HEI whole fruit intake (r=0.08, p=0.01). Family social support showed positive associations with HEI total score (r=0.09, p=0.007), HEI fruit (r=0.10, p=0.002), HEI whole fruit (r=0.11, p=0.001) and HEI added sugar (r=0.10, p=0.002). Friend social support was also positively correlated with HEI total (r=0.09, p=0.010) and HEI added sugar (r=0.08, p=0.013). BMI was positively associated with HEI vegetables (r=0.07, p=0.030) and HEI added sugar (r=0.13, p<0.001). Additionally, age was positively associated with HEI added sugar (r=0.07, p=0.039).

Regression analysis (GLM models)

As depicted in table 2, the results of the multivariate GLM-adjusted models showed that no significant psychosocial factors were related to HEI total score. Although the GLM models reached statistical significance for several outcomes, the explained variance was modest (R²=0.02–0.04), which implies that psychosocial predictors accounted for only a small proportion of variability in dietary behaviours. Family social support was significantly associated with HEI fruit (β=0.16, p=0.02) and higher caloric intake (β=44.09, p=0.03). Additionally, friend social support was significantly associated with HEI sodium (lower sodium intake; β=0.23, p=0.01) after adjustment for the aforementioned covariates. While the effect size for caloric intake corresponds to an increase of approximately 44 kcal, this occurrence represents a relatively small change compared with the average baseline intake (>2000 kcal/day) and may have limited clinical significance despite being statistically significant. Higher perceived self-efficacy for healthy eating was inversely associated with HEI sodium (β= –0.42, p=0.02), which means that adolescents with greater confidence in making healthy food choices consumed more sodium.

SEM results

Across all outcome variables, higher social support for healthy eating led to significantly increased motivation and self-efficacy for healthy eating (table 3 and online supplemental table 2). It should be noted that the model R² values were low (0.01–0.04), indicating that these psychosocial variables accounted for only a small proportion of the variance in dietary outcomes. Higher autonomous motivation was significantly associated with greater HEI total (β=0.14, p=0.005) and higher HEI added sugar score (lower added sugar intake; β=0.10, p=0.04). The only significant direct effect observed for self-efficacy was for HEI sodium score (β=−0.10, p=0.04). Aligned with findings from the GLM-adjusted model, greater self-efficacy was associated with higher sodium intake so adolescents with higher self-efficacy consumed more sodium. In contrast, several indirect effects were observed through mediation pathways. Social support influenced diet quality via two key psychosocial mediators: through motivation, social support indirectly improved HEI total (β=0.09, p=0.006) and whole fruit intake (β=0.07, p=0.04) and increased HEI added sugar (lower added sugar intake; β=0.07, p=0.04). Through self-efficacy, social support indirectly affected HEI sodium (β=–0.04, p=0.04), such that greater social support increased self-efficacy, which in turn was associated with a lower HEI sodium score (higher sodium intake). Collectively, these findings indicate that while motivation and self-efficacy exert both direct and mediated influences, the primary effects of social support on dietary behaviours operate indirectly through these psychosocial mechanisms.

Discussion

This study examined the associations of psychosocial factors (motivation, self-efficacy and social support) with various healthy eating behaviours in young adolescents. The finding that autonomous motivation may enhance diet quality aligns with prior research suggesting that autonomous motivation leads to better dietary choices.11 13 30 After adjusting for the covariates, higher autonomous motivation for healthy eating was also significantly associated with lower added sugar intake (as indicated by a higher HEI added sugar score). These results are particularly important given the association between high adolescent sugar intake and the increasing prevalence of overweight and obesity noted during this developmental stage.31 Considering that nearly half of the adolescents in the sample were overweight or obese, researchers may want to consider this psychosocial factor, which can potentially shape dietary behaviours, in subsequent dietary studies including this population.

Higher self-efficacy was significantly associated with higher sodium intake (as indicated by a lower HEI sodium score). Adolescents with greater confidence in their ability to make healthy food choices consumed more sodium. Previous studies demonstrate the importance of self-efficacy in making healthy food choices15 16 as well as its role in enhancing motivation to influence changes in eating habits.12 However, our results suggest that while the adolescents in our sample may feel confident in their ability to make healthy dietary choices, they may be unaware of or unable to recognise their high sodium consumption. No significant contributions were found between self-efficacy and HEI total or other dietary outcomes in the univariate or multivariate models. The lack of significance on overall healthy eating in the current study could be attributed to the generalised nature of the self-efficacy measure, which assesses confidence across a range of healthy eating behaviours rather than focusing on specific food categories or eating contexts. The 9-item scale may benefit from further refinement or dimensional reduction to focus on more targeted areas of self-efficacy relevant to specific dietary behaviours with a goal of showing a stronger relationship with overall healthy eating and other dietary outcomes. However, there is also a lack of comparison studies examining the relationship between self-efficacy and HEI total scores in the US adolescent population, which may limit interpretability.

Social support for healthy eating, which is another important psychosocial factor, made a significant contribution to whole fruit. Prior research found that social support is associated with healthy eating, specifically with fruit and vegetable consumption.19 32 However, the current study measured overall social support for healthy eating and not explicitly for fruit and vegetable intake. This broader measure may not have captured the specific types of support or encouragement necessary to boost overall fruit or vegetable consumption. A positive association between family social support and caloric intake was also found, which aligns with research suggesting that parental involvement and encouragement in food-related decisions significantly contribute to adolescents’ eating habits.19 While the increase in caloric intake was statistically significant, the actual change in intake was relatively small and not clinically significant. However, this information is important to consider because a high caloric intake in adolescent populations is alarming,33 and parental education in this area may be needed. However, despite achieving statistical significance for many outcomes, the explained variation was limited, suggesting that the psychosocial predictors contributed only a small fraction of the variability in dietary behaviours.

The SEM analysis provided a novel approach to the current literature and a holistic understanding of the relationships between social support, motivation and self-efficacy for healthy eating and several eating behavioural outcomes. Understanding whether psychosocial factors directly or indirectly affect healthy eating among adolescents is crucial. Psychosocial variables were significant; however, the amount of variance explained was relatively small, indicating that its practical impact on dietary patterns may be limited. Among these factors, social support was found to have a significant positive impact on motivation and self-efficacy for healthy eating. Social support can provide motivation and strengthen self-efficacy for healthy eating, but it is possible that for autonomously motivated individuals, social support has less of an impact because these individuals are already internally motivated and may not need external encouragement. Higher autonomous motivation was associated with improved overall diet quality and lower added sugar intake, as indicated by a higher HEI added sugar score. These findings show the importance of autonomous motivation in leading to overall healthier eating habits and reducing consumption of added sugar in this sample.

Some limitations in other studies included focusing on older adolescent populations22 or not examining all three psychosocial variables comprehensively. In addition, some studies were conducted outside the USA,5 12 which limited generalisability to young adolescents in the USA. Furthermore, the finding in the current study that adolescents who perceive higher social support may feel more motivated to make healthier food choices, and that motivation is associated with a higher HEI total, lends support for motivation being crucial in behaviour change models such as SDT.34 The current study builds on prior research by examining all components of the HEI that were not previously addressed.11 12 32

The finding from the SEM analysis showing that a lower family income is associated with less social support for healthy eating among adolescents has important implications for many HEI outcomes (total HEI, fruit, whole fruit, vegetables, sodium and added sugar) because lower social support can potentially hinder the enactment of healthy dietary behaviours in this age group. While literature already links lower socioeconomic status to poorer dietary behaviours,8 this study uniquely examined the associations between these variables comprehensively through SEM analysis. This approach allowed for a more holistic understanding of how multiple factors interact to influence specific dietary outcomes. Adolescents from low-income families who lack social support may have fewer resources (eg, less family encouragement and peer influence) to make healthier food choices. Addressing socioeconomic barriers, such as increasing access to affordable, healthy foods and supportive community groups, is essential for improving dietary behaviours among adolescents in low-income families.

Limitations

Some study limitations must be acknowledged. First, the use of self-reported data for dietary intake and psychosocial measures introduces the possibility of recall and social desirability bias,16 as participants may overestimate healthy behaviours or under-report unhealthy behaviours. Future studies may need to incorporate objective measures of dietary intake, such as biomarker assessments, to enhance the validity of the findings. Second, the study relied on a single measure of self-efficacy, motivation and social support, which may not capture the full complexity of these constructs. Future research may benefit from using more specific measures to assess different domains of eating behaviour and the related psychosocial factors. Additionally, while a broad range of demographic factors was examined, other variables, such as school environment and neighbourhood/local food environments, were not included in the analysis. Future research exploring these contextual factors is recommended to further increase understanding of the complex dynamics that shape adolescent eating behaviours.

Conclusion

Multivariate GLM-adjusted models showed that family social support made a positive contribution to fruit intake; and friend social support contributed to a lower sodium intake. Additionally, SEM analysis showed that higher social support for healthy eating led to significantly increased motivation and self-efficacy for healthy eating. Higher autonomous motivation was significantly associated with greater HEI total, whole fruit and added sugar score (indicating lower sugar intake). Finally, both GLM and SEM models showed that greater self-efficacy was associated with higher sodium intake (indicated by a lower HEI sodium score). These findings contribute to the expanding body of literature on adolescent nutrition by highlighting the complex interaction between psychosocial factors in influencing specific dietary behaviours.
