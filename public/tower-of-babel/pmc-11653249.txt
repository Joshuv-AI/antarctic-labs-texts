Syntheses and Crystal Structures of Alkaline Earth Metal Hydrogen Acetylides AE(C2H)2 with AE = Ca, Sr, Ba
Authors: Lisa Körtgen, Uwe Ruschewitz
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCrystalline Ba(C2H)2 was obtained by the reaction of elemental barium dissolved in liquid ammonia, forming a blue electride, and acetylene (C2H2) injected into the reaction vial with the electride solution. From the colorless precipitate that was obtained after evaporation of the ammonia, the crystal structure of Ba(C2H)2 was solved and refined using synchrotron powder diffraction data. It crystallizes in the trigonal space group P3̅m1 (no. 164) with Z = 1, all HC2– anions are aligned along [001]. It is the first crystal structure of an alkaline earth metal hydrogen acetylide published up to now, showing a close similarity with the brucite (Mg(OH)2) and CdI2-type structures. For Sr(C2H)2, a product with a significantly reduced crystallinity was obtained, but its powder diffraction pattern makes an isotypic crystal structure very likely. IR/Raman spectroscopic investigations as well as GC analysis of the gases released upon hydrolysis unambiguously confirm the existence of HC2– anions in these compounds. Similar results were obtained for Ca(C2H)2, however this compound is almost completely amorphous. Upon heating Ba(C2H)2 with additional elemental barium to 200 °C in vacuum, highly crystalline BaC2 (I4/mmm, Z = 2) was obtained.

Introduction

CaC2 is an important feedstock for calcium cyanamide (CaCN2) and acetylene (C2H2) production, the latter is an indispensable starting material for many chemical raw materials and products.1 CaC2 is produced industrially from readily available CaO (lime) and coke at high temperatures of well over 2000 °C in an electric arc furnace. The very high energy consumption and the release of CO2 during the calcination of limestone or chalk (CaCO3) to obtain CaO gives the process a high carbon footprint.2 It was proposed to use biomass resources to reduce the carbon footprint,3−5 but basically, the CaC2 process is mainly unchanged since its invention in the late 19th century. In 2022, China produced almost 28 Mt CaC2 using this process, which accounts for more than 95% of the calcium acetylide production worldwide.6 Its advantage is certainly the coal-based synthesis of acetylene, which reduces the dependence on crude oil.

In 2023, Li et al. presented an interesting new approach for the acetylide-based production of acetylene.7 CaC2 was replaced by BaC2 to establish a more recyclable process. It was shown that in contrast to CaCO3, BaCO3 reacts with carbon up to 950 °C to yield BaO and CO, i.e. no CO2 is released. In a subsequent reaction up to 1300 °C, BaO reacts with further carbon to form BaC2, which is then used to produce acetylene upon hydrolysis. The resulting Ba(OH)2 may capture CO2 thus forming BaCO3 and starting the cycle again, which leads to a much lower overall carbon emission. This interesting finding by Li et al. shows nicely that the simple replacement of calcium with barium leads to a surprising and unexpected new chemistry of this approximately 150 years old industrial process. For us, this is a clear justification for ongoing basic research in the field of acetylides paving the way to new and sometimes even unexpected reactions in the field.

On a laboratory scale, highly pure alkaline earth metal acetylides AEC2 (AE = Ca, Sr, Ba) may be obtained via a reaction of the elements, i.e. the respective alkaline earth metals and graphite, using temperatures starting at ∼800 °C.8−10 Li2C2 is the only alkali metal acetylide that can be synthesized using a similar procedure.11 MgC2 is accessible by reaction of elemental magnesium with acetylene (C2H2) at moderate heating (∼500 °C) leading to a product of only low purity,12 whereas the synthesis and characterization of BeC2 remains to be a tantalizing mystery.13 The chemistry of the heavier alkali metal acetylides appears to be completely different. As mentioned, only Li2C2 can be synthesized directly from the elements upon heating at approximately 800 °C.11 For all other acetylides A2C2 with A = Na, K, Rb, Cs, a typically two-step process is used.14,15 In a first step, the respective alkali metal is dissolved in liquid ammonia forming a deep blue electride,16 which reacts with gaseous acetylene to form AC2H

In a second step, this hydrogen acetylide AC2H is either heated in vacuum directly (for A = Na) or with a stoichiometric amount of A (for A = K, Rb, Cs) to yield the respective acetylides

or

The alkaline earth metals AE = Ca, Sr, Ba also form electrides and, in principle, a similar synthetic route should be feasible. However, the information in the literature about these reactions is very sparse. Based on elemental analysis, it was reported that hydrogen acetylides AE(C2H)2 might be obtained as ammoniates in liquid ammonia, however the reports about their composition are inconclusive.17,18 In other work, these hydrogen acetylides AE(C2H)2 were prepared in situ and used as intermediates for subsequent organic reactions.19,20 To the best of our knowledge, no reliable isolation of any alkaline earth metal hydrogen acetylide has been reported so far and no structural or spectroscopic details of these compounds are given anywhere. In the following, we will present a first reproducible synthesis of crystalline Ba(C2H)2 and its crystal structure along with some spectroscopic (IR/Raman) properties. Furthermore, the lighter congeners Ca(C2H)2 and Sr(C2H)2 are discussed.

Methods

Caution

Acetylides may react sensitively to impacts. Although no severe explosions occurred in our experiments, lighter detonations were observed frequently for some samples. Therefore, appropriate safety precautions must be taken, including synthesizing only small sample amounts!

Synthesis of Ba(C2H)2

In a glovebox (argon atmosphere), 0.1557 g (1.134 mmol) elemental barium (99.9%, Alfa Aeser) was weighted into a Schlenk tube. Outside the glovebox, approximately 25 mL NH3, dried with elemental sodium for at least 1 h prior to the reaction, were condensed onto this solid using an isopropanol/dry ice bath (ca. −78 °C) for cooling. Then, acetylene gas (dried over activated charcoal and a molecular sieve) was injected into the reaction vial, until the blue electride solution decolorized and a colorless precipitate formed (Figure S1, Supporting Information). After that, the cooling bath was removed to evaporate the ammonia. Finally, a dynamic vacuum (ca. 1 × 10–3 mbar) was applied for 30 min at room temperature. The product was obtained as a colorless, crystalline powder. Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns confirmed the purity of the sample (Figure 1). In general, the solubility of the compounds AE(C2H)2 in liquid ammonia is very low and thus significantly lower than the solubility of the respective alkali metal compounds AC2H.21,22

Synthesis of Sr(C2H)2

In an analogous synthesis using 0.1542 g (3.848 mmol) elemental strontium (99.95%, Thermo Scientific) in approximately 25 mL NH3, Sr(C2H)2 was obtained as a colorless, crystalline powder (Figure S1, Supporting Information). PXRD patterns reveal additional reflections pointing to at least one unknown impurity (Figure S2, Supporting Information).

Synthesis of Ca(C2H)2

In an analogous synthesis using 0.1653 g (4.124 mmol) elemental calcium (99.99%, Sigma-Aldrich) in approximately 25 mL NH3, Ca(C2H)2 was obtained as a colorless powder (Figure S1, Supporting Information). PXRD patterns reveal the formation of a mainly amorphous product (Figure S3, Supporting Information).

PXRD

Data were collected at room temperature on a Stoe Stadi P powder diffractometer (Mo-Kα1 radiation, Ge(111) monochromator, Mythen detector). Samples were measured in sealed glass capillaries (Ø = 0.5 mm, argon atmosphere). Typical recording times were 30 min. The WinXPow software package23 was used for data handling, simulation of theoretical patterns and visualization of the PXRD patterns.

Synchrotron Powder Diffraction

High-resolution synchrotron powder diffraction data were recorded at the beamline BL09 of the Center for Synchrotron Radiation (DELTA, Dortmund/Germany). The radiation was adjusted to the desired wavelength of λ = 0.45927 Å using a silicium (311) double crystal monochromator. The diffractometer is equipped with a PILATUS 100 K detector and the photon source consists of a superconducting asymmetric wiggler (SAW2). For the experiments, samples filled in glass capillaries (Ø = 0.5 mm) and sealed in an argon atmosphere were used. The capillaries were mounted horizontally on a spinning goniometer.

Crystal Structure Solution

Within the WinXPOW software system23 using DICVOL,24 the reflections of the X-ray powder diffraction pattern of Ba(C2H)2 were indexed in a primitive hexagonal/trigonal unit cell with a ≈ 4.55 Å, c ≈ 7.42 Å and V ≈ 133.3 Å3. No systematic absences of reflections were found leading to eight possible trigonal and eight possible hexagonal space groups. A Le Bail fit within JANA200625 supported the choice of unit cell and reflection conditions. Estimation of the volume of Ba(C2H)2 using known volume increments26 gave Z = 1. As IR/Raman data confirmed the existence of HC2– anions, it became evident that they need to be aligned along [001]. With these considerations, a structural model was developed in P3̅m1 (no. 164) by hand. Ba can only occupy a 1a site (or 1b), whereas C1, C2 and H1 must occupy sites with a multiplicity of 2. Several orientations of the HC2– anion were tested (there are not many!), always taking reasonable distances between the anions, but also between Ba2+ and the anion into account. The structural model was finally confirmed by subsequent Rietveld refinements in P3̅m1 (no. 164).

Rietveld Refinement

Rietveld refinements were carried out with the GSAS-II package.27 The unit cell obtained with JANA200625 and the positional parameters of the raw model obtained by hand, were used as a starting point for the refinement: Ba was placed on the 1a site (000) and C1, C2 and H1 on the 2d site (1/3 2/3 z) with z values chosen that reasonable interatomic distances resulted for the starting model. The H–C≡C– anion was fixed using the soft constraints C–C = 1.20(1) Å and C–H = 1.06(1) Å. The refinement was complicated by the following aspects: (1) preferred orientation along [001], (2) anisotropic peak broadening (this is a well-known phenomenon for acetylides9), (3) two broad background features at low diffraction angles underneath the first reflections, eventually due to an amorphous byproduct. To obtain a reasonable profile fit, the Rietveld refinement had to account for all these features. The preferred orientation was refined using a March-Dollase correction28 ([001] as preferred axis) and the anisotropic peak broadening using an uniaxial mustrain model within GSAS-II.27 More details have been provided in the deposited cif file (see below). In the final refinement cycles, all atoms were refined with isotropic temperature factors (Uiso), but the Uisos of the two carbon atoms and the hydrogen atom were constrained to one value. Nonetheless, large Uisos resulted. To support this finding, analogous refinements were conducted with the TOPAS software.29 The results are essentially the same—including large Uisos for C1, C2, H1—with a somewhat worse profile fit so that in the following only the results of the GSAS-II refinement are presented (Figure 1). IR Spectroscopy

IR spectra were recorded with a Bruker ALPHA FT-IR spectrometer using the ATR sample technique (with a diamond ATR crystal). The spectrometer was housed in a glovebox (argon atmosphere) to provide inert conditions.

Raman Spectroscopy

Raman spectra were recorded with a Renishaw InVia Qontor Raman microscope using a 457 nm laser (laser power 1%, exposure time: 10 s), which was focused on the sample with a ×100 objective (grating: 1800 lines per mm). The spectrometer was equipped with a Centrus 05TJ CCD detector. Before measurements, the instrument was calibrated with an internal Si standard. Samples were measured in sealed glass capillaries (Ø = 0.5 mm, argon atmosphere).

Thermoanalytical Investigations

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) and thermogravimetric (TGA) investigations were performed with a STARe System from METTLER TOLEDO TGA/DSC 1 in Al2O3 containers with a heating rate of 10 K·min–1 in an argon atmosphere (sample mass: ca. 12 mg).

GC

To analyze the gas phase after hydrolysis of the acetylides, a home-built gas chromatograph was used.31 Pure hydrogen gas (99.95%) and the hydrolysis products of KC2H32 were used as references.

Elemental Analyses (CHNS)

Elemental analysis of carbon, nitrogen and hydrogen were conducted with an in-house HEKAtech GmbH EuroEA 3000 Analyzer. Approx. two mg of each compound were filled into a tin cartridge (together with V2O5 for an efficient combustion). For each sample, three measurements were carried out, from which a mean value was calculated.

Results and Discussion

Barium dissolved in liquid ammonia, formed a deep blue electride solution, which after injection of an appropriate amount of gaseous acetylene (C2H2) into the reaction vial decolorized forming a suspension and a colorless precipitate (Figure S1, Supporting Information). Evaporation of the ammonia by heating up the solution to room temperature, led to the formation of a white crystalline powder. After removing adherent ammonia in a dynamic vacuum, the PXRD pattern revealed a material with a good crystallinity (Figure 1). Similar observations were made with strontium and calcium (Figure S1, Supporting Information). However, the crystallinity decreased substantially, when going from the heavier to the lighter alkaline earth metals, i.e. the precipitate of the reaction with calcium was mainly amorphous with only few very broad reflections (Figure S3, Supporting Information). Elemental analyses (CHNS) of the respective precipitates gave the results summarized in Table 1.

The results in Table 1 illustrate that a correct determination of the elemental composition of such compounds is difficult or even impossible due to their high sensitivity against oxidation and hydrolysis. We assume that when transferring the sample from the Schlenk tube to the instrument (in air!) the hydrogen acetylides were partially hydrolyzed to form the respective hydroxides. To corroborate this, we calculated the carbon and hydrogen contents for a sample consisting of a mixture of Ba(C2H)2 and Ba(OH)2 in a 1:1 ratio resulting in w(C) = 13.39% and w(H) = 1.12%. These values are much closer to the experimentally determined values given in Table 1. Large discrepancies of the values obtained for different samples and in different measurements, support the assumption that hydrolysis takes place during preparation for the measurements. Most notably, the results obtained for nitrogen given in Table 1 are illustrative, as they clearly show that the barium compound does not contain any (almost) ammonia, i.e. the formation of an ammoniate as postulated in the literature can be excluded.17,18 Minor nitrogen amounts are found for the strontium compound and larger nitrogen amounts for the calcium compound. The postulated formation of thermally labile ammoniates might hinder the crystallization of the hydrogen acetylide, which leads to PXRD patterns of modest or even “amorphous” quality (Figure S2 and S3, Supporting Information). Nonetheless, as will be shown later on, IR/Raman data clearly showed that also in the precipitates of the reactions with calcium and strontium HC2– anions had been formed (Figure 4).

As the PXRD pattern of potential Ba(C2H)2 showed the best quality, it was attempted to solve its crystal structure from powder diffraction data. As a matter of fact, already the X-ray powder diffraction data recorded at our lab instrument were easily indexed in a small hexagonal/trigonal unit cell, for which Z = 1 was estimated. As elemental analysis excluded NH3 and IR/Raman spectra confirmed the presence of HC2– anions (vide infra), a structural model was developed by hand in space group P3̅m1 (no. 164). To obtain more reliable refinement data, a synchrotron powder diffraction pattern was recorded at beamline BL9 of the Dortmund synchrotron facility DELTA (for details on structure solution and refinement see Methods Section). In Figure 1, the final Rietveld fit is shown. Preferred orientation, anisotropic peak broadening and two broad background features below 10°/2θ resulted in an only modest profile fit. Few reflections with very low intensities (e.g., at 8.12°/2θ) indicate minor amounts of an unknown impurity. These reflections could not be assigned to BaO, Ba(OH)2, Ba(NH2)2, BaC2, or elemental Barium. In Table 2, some essential structure parameters of Ba(C2H)2 are listed. More detailed information is given in Tables S1 and S2 (Supporting Information), the latter lists the atomic coordinates.

In Figure 2, two views of the crystal structure of Ba(C2H)2 are depicted. The HC2– anions are aligned along [001] with the hydrogen atoms pointing in opposite directions in different layers. The shortest H···H contact is 2.718 Å, which is similar to that found in NaC2H (2.777 Å).32,33 Ba···C distances were determined to 2.852(4) Å (6x) and 3.496(7) Å (6x). For BaC2, Ba···C distances of 2.970(7) Å (2x) and 3.163(1) Å (8x) were reported.10 In the latter, Ba2+ is coordinated to two C22– dumbbells in an end-on fashion (shorter contacts) and four C22– dumbbells in a side-on coordination (longer distances). In Ba(C2H)2, Ba2+ is also coordinated to six HC2– anions, however the coordination mode cannot be described as a clear end-on or side-on coordination (cp. Figure 2, left). Accordingly, the crystal structure and the determined interatomic distances of Ba(C2H)2 fit to the reported data of other acetylides. To the best of our knowledge, Ba(C2H)2 crystallizes in a new structure type, which shows a close similarity to the brucite-type structure (Mg(OH)2)34 with HC2– anions replacing HO– anions.

In Figure 3 (left column), the coordination spheres around Ba2+ and HC2– in Ba(C2H)2 are shown. As mentioned, Ba2+ is surrounded by six HC2– dumbbells in-between a side-on and an end-on coordination mode. Conversely, each HC2– dumbbell is coordinated by three Ba2+ cations summing up to a coordination number ratio of 6:3 as expected for an AB2 structure. Figure 3 compares these coordination spheres with those found in NaC2H (right column).32,33 Na+ is surrounded by five HC2– dumbbells, one in an end-on and four in a more less side-on coordination. The HC2– dumbbells on the other hand, are surrounded by five Na+ cations leading to a coordination number ratio of 5:5 in accordance with an AB structure.

With the knowledge of the crystal structure of Ba(C2H)2 and the IR/Raman spectra of the product of the reaction of strontium with C2H2 in liquid ammonia confirming the existence of HC2– anions (Figure 4), we inspected the PXRD pattern of the resulting precipitate (Figure S2, Supporting Information). Some of the reflections of this pattern are assignable to a potential compound Sr(C2H)2. The refined lattice parameters are given in Table 2 leading to the expected decrease of the unit cell volume when going from a barium to a strontium compound. Using the volume increments given for Sr2+ and Ba2+,26 a unit cell volume of 124.6 Å3 is estimated for Sr(C2H)2, which is a little bit larger than 115.6 Å3, as found from the refinement of the assigned reflections. Nonetheless, the IR/Raman spectra strongly support the formation of Sr(C2H)2. The PXRD pattern shown in Figure S2 (Supporting Information) exhibits several additional reflections. One weak reflection at 1/d ≈ 3.038 Å–1 points to elemental strontium (vide infra). The other reflections could not be assigned to any possible byproduct or impurity so that we assume that they might belong to a potential ammoniate of Sr(C2H)2. The IR/Raman data of the precipitate formed by the reaction of calcium with C2H2 in liquid ammonia also support the existence of HC2– anions (Figure 4). However, the PXRD pattern of the precipitate (Figure S3, Supporting Information) shows only amorphous features, i.e. few very broad reflections, which could not be assigned to any possible impurity or byproduct. Again, they might point to a potential ammoniate of Ca(C2H)2.

It was shown that IR and Raman spectroscopy is a helpful tool to analyze acetylides in solid-state materials.35 Whereas the C≡C stretching vibration in unsymmetrical HC2– anions is visible in both IR and Raman spectra, a symmetrically surrounded C22– dumbbell is only visible in its Raman spectrum. The results summarized in Table 3 reveal several trends for acetylides and hydrogen acetylides. With increasing atomic numbers the wave numbers of the C≡C stretching vibrations decrease, e.g. for CaC2 to BaC2 from 1858 to 1833 cm–1 and for NaC2H to CsC2H from 1871 to 1839 cm–1. This trend is also found in the IR spectra of AE(C2H)2 from AE = Ca (1896 cm–1) to AE = Ba (1876 cm–1). As a second trend, the frequencies of the C≡C stretching vibrations of the acetylides are always smaller than those of the respective hydrogen acetylides. This trend is also observed for all pairs AEC2/AE(C2H)2 with AE = Ca, Sr, Ba. However, it should be noted that the wave numbers of the C≡C stretching vibrations observed with IR and Raman spectroscopy are typically identical. Here, especially Ca(C2H)2 and Sr(C2H)2 show significantly differing values. This may be partly attributed to the modest quality of some spectra (e.g., Figures S4/S7, Supporting Information; IR spectrum of Ca(C2H)2 and Raman spectra in general), but the main reason is unclear at the moment. One may speculate that the existence of NH3 in the samples of Ca(C2H)2 and Sr(C2H)2 (vide supra, Table 1) has an influence on this finding. The wave numbers of the C–H vibrations seem to be unaffected by possible NH3 impurities, ranging from 3220 cm–1 for Sr(C2H)2 to 3236 cm–1 for Ca(C2H)2. A close inspection of the IR spectrum of Ca(C2H)2 (Figure S4, Supporting Information) reveals some additional signals at 1146 and 1589 cm–1, which can be assigned to coordinating ammonia molecules.37

To have a second method confirming the existence of HC2– anions in the newly synthesized compounds AE(C2H)2 with AE = Ca, Sr, Ba, we analyzed the gas phase after hydrolysis using a home-built gas chromatograph (GC, see Methods Section).31 For the measurement, the powder of the respective analyte was quickly transferred to a small test tube in air without using inert conditions (e.g., argon atmosphere), as this gas may influence the results of the GC measurement. One mL of deionized water was added to the powder and the test tube was sealed with a septum. After approximately 3 min, 2 mL of the evolved gas was removed using a syringe and inserted into the GC. Pure hydrogen (H2) from a gas cylinder and the acetylene (C2H2) evolved from the hydrolysis of KC2H were used as references. In Figure 5, the results of these measurements are summarized. For all four compounds KC2H and AE(C2H)2 with AE = Ca, Sr, Ba, the evolution of C2H2 was clearly detected. Even the evolution of hydrogen for Sr(C2H)2 was observed confirming the results of the PXRD data (vide supra). Those revealed a weak reflection assigned to elemental (unreacted) strontium, which reacts with water to form H2.

As shown in eqs 4 and 5, only NaC2H can be transformed to its acetylide Na2C2 by heating in vacuum. For the heavier alkali metals, the respective alkali metal in its elemental form has to be added to the reaction mixture. Therefore, we heated Ba(C2H)2 (0.0411 g, 0.219 mmol) with elemental barium (0.0301 g, 0.219 mmol). To ensure good mixing of the reactants, they were first slurried in liquid ammonia. The residue, after the ammonia had been evaporated, was then heated in vacuum (∼1 × 10–3 mbar) at 200 °C for 5 h resulting in a brownish powder. The PXRD pattern of this powder is shown in Figure 7. It confirms the formation of crystalline BaC2 in its tetragonal modification (I4/mmm, Z = 2). Some weak additional reflections (2θ ≈ 12.72° (shoulder), 14.67, 20.86 or 24.5°) can be assigned to BaO. However, the stronger additional reflection at 2θ ≈ 11.85° could not be assigned to either the monoclinic modification of BaC2, Ba(C2H)2 or elemental barium so that its origin is unknown at the moment.

Conclusions and Outlook

By the reaction of elemental barium dissolved in liquid ammonia, thus forming a blue electride solution, with gaseous acetylene (C2H2) the precipitation of crystalline Ba(C2H)2 was observed. Its crystal structure, which represents the very first crystal structure of an alkaline earth metal hydrogen acetylide, was solved and refined from powder diffraction data (P3̅m1 (no. 164) with Z = 1). All HC2– anions are aligned along [001], the structure shows similarities with the brucite-type structure (Mg(OH)2), which on the other hand is closely related to the CdI2-type structure. The crystallinity decreases going to the lighter alkaline earth metals, the precipitate of potential Ca(C2H)2 is almost completely amorphous. It is likely that they contain adherent ammonia, which could not be removed upon heating or in vacuum. Nonetheless, IR/Raman spectra as well as GC analysis of the gas phase obtained upon hydrolysis clearly confirm the existence of HC2– anions in all compounds AE(C2H)2 with AE = Ca, Sr, Ba.

Upon heating Ba(C2H)2 at 250 °C in vacuum, it is transformed to poorly crystalline BaC2. Adding elemental barium to this reaction mixture, heating in vacuum at 200 °C leads to BaC2 with a much higher crystallinity. These results are in perfect agreement with those obtained for AC2H with A = K, Rb, Cs. Experiments to improve the crystallinity of the respective calcium and strontium congeners are under way. For the formation of Na2C2 from NaC2H38 or Cs2Cd(C2H)2(C2) from Cs2Cd(C2H)4,39 we developed PXRD techniques to follow the release of C2H2 and the formation of the products in situ, although the starting materials as well as the products are sensitive to air and moisture. Up to now, these techniques were unsuccessful to follow the formation of BaC2 from Ba(C2H)2, as the latter seems to be more sensitive to hydrolysis and oxidation. Even a slight shock sensitivity was observed in some experiments, e.g. under pressure. Obviously, our setup for in situ investigations of these AE(C2H)2 compounds has to be improved to address their increased sensitivity.
