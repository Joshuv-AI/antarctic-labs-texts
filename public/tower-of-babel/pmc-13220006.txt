Association of General Personality Traits with Outcomes in Total Hip Arthroplasty: A Multicenter, Cross-Sectional, Prospective Trial
Authors: Felix Wunderlich, Philipp Schippers, Thomas Klonschinski, Yama Afghanyar, Frank Traub, Philipp Drees, Lukas Eckhard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe influence of psychological traits such as depression on postoperative outcomes after total hip arthroplasty (THA) is known, but data on general personality traits, such as optimism and pessimism, remain scarce. Understanding the impact of optimism and pessimism on postoperative outcomes in THA patients could provide valuable insights for preoperative patient assessments and postoperative care strategies. We hypothesized that patients with higher levels of optimism would demonstrate superior functional outcomes and that the opposite applies to pessimistic patients.MethodsA prospective, multicenter, cross-sectoral study assessed the association of personality traits with preoperative and 3, 6, and 12-month postoperative functional scores (12-item Hip Disability and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score [HOOS-12]). Optimism and pessimism were measured preoperatively via the revised Life Orientation Test (LOT-R). Linear regression models were fitted, and coefficients of determination (R2) and t-tests were obtained to reveal the correlation and statistical significance of the associations. The study was recorded in the German Clinical Trials Register (DRKS00013972).ResultsEight hundred forty-six patients were analyzed. Optimism was significantly associated with HOOS-12 scores over the first postoperative year, whereas pessimism was significantly associated for 6- and 12-month follow-up (preoperative: optimistic r = 0.11, p = 0.018; pessimistic r = 0.07, p = 0.099; postoperative optimistic: 3 months r = 0.143, p = 0.005; 6 months r = 0.148, p = 0.013; 12 months r = 0.121, p = 0.049; postoperative pessimistic: 3 months r = 0.090, p = 0.070; 6 months r = 0.155, p = 0.010; 12 months r = 0.165, p = 0.008).ConclusionsOptimism and pessimism are opposing and significantly associated with postoperative outcomes after THA, although not reaching the minimal clinically important change of the HOOS-12 score. If patients scheduled for THA seem prone to pessimistic behavior, further screening of the patient’s personality traits and other risk factors should be conducted. The outcomes of these patients could be improved through psychoeducation or cognitive behavioral interventions.

Total hip arthroplasty (THA) remains one of the most successful and frequently performed procedures in orthopedic practice. It provides pain relief and functional improvement for patients suffering from debilitating hip conditions (i.e., osteoarthritis or avascular necrosis). Despite the high success rates of THA, patient outcomes can vary widely and are influenced by numerous factors, including surgical technique, prosthetic design, patient comorbidities, and psychological factors.

Recent attention has focused on psychological factors, such as dispositional optimism or pessimism, associated with recovery and functional outcomes in THA and total knee arthroplasty (TKA).1) Dispositional optimism, a general expectation that good things will happen in the future, has been associated with better health outcomes in various medical contexts.234) Conversely, dispositional pessimism, the tendency to expect negative outcomes, may hinder recovery and negatively impact patient satisfaction and functional outcomes.56) Optimistic individuals tend to engage more actively in their recovery processes, adhere better to rehabilitation protocols, and report higher levels of satisfaction postoperatively,7) whereas pessimistic individuals may experience increased stress, poorer adherence to rehabilitation, and slower recovery.8)

Understanding the impact of dispositional optimism and pessimism on postoperative outcomes in THA patients could provide valuable insights for preoperative patient assessments and postoperative care strategies. This study aimed to explore the relationships between dispositional optimism and pessimism and postoperative outcomes in THA patients. We hypothesized that patients with higher levels of dispositional optimism would demonstrate superior functional outcomes. Conversely, we expected patients with higher levels of dispositional pessimism to experience poorer functional outcomes.

This study focused on preoperative evaluation processes by elucidating the influence of psychological factors such as optimism and pessimism on surgical outcomes, leading to more personalized and holistic approaches in orthopedic care.

METHODS

The study was registered in the German Clinical Trials Register (DRKS00013972). Informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study. Institutional Review Board approval was granted in all participating states (No. 837.533.17 (11367), Ethics Committee at the State Chambers of Physicians of Rhineland-Palatinate; No. B-F-2018-042, Ethics Committee at the State Chambers of Physicians of Baden-Wuerttemberg; No. MC 84/2018, Ethics Committee at the State Chambers of Physicians of Hesse).

Data were obtained via the PROMISE (Prozessoptimierung durch interdisziplinare, sektorenubergreifende Versorgung am Beispiel von Huft- und Kniearthrosen) trial, a study with a multicenter, cross-sectoral, prospective healthcare research approach. It was conducted in 3 different German hospitals, representing all levels of care. The sample size was not specified a priori. A detailed description of the methods used is displayed in the published study protocol.9)

Criteria for Inclusion and Exclusion

Patients over 18 years who were scheduled for primary THA due to end-stage osteoarthritis were included in the study. The exclusion criteria were a German language barrier, noncompliance with signing a written consent, a life expectancy of less than 1 year, and health factors precluding elective surgery. Patients suffering from depression were excluded from the analyses. Written informed consent was mandatory.

Data Collection

Follow-up was 12 months postoperatively in all study sites. Inclusion was carried out between May 2018 and January 2021. A total of 1,871 patients were included in the PROMISE trial, of which 846 patients were eligible for analysis in this study. Baseline data were collected at the time of informed consent. Patients’ personality dimensions were examined preoperatively via the German revised Life Orientation Test (LOT-R) (Fig. 1).10) The score consists of 10 items: 3 items (1, 4, and 10) assess optimism, and 3 items (3, 7, and 9) assess pessimism. Four further items are defined as filler items. Patients answer on a 5-point Likert scale, and the response categories range from strongly agree to strongly disagree. The score’s reliability and validity have been established,11) and norm values have been determined.12) The test measures dispositional optimism and pessimism with high test-retest reliability.7) For regression analyses, the LOT-R score variable was used as a separate, continuous, bidimensional variable in both personality traits. This is the preferred application of this test.1112) High scores on the scales indicate “high optimism” or “low pessimism.” Concerning descriptive statistics, the LOT-R was categorized into 3 groups per variable: “Optimistic”: 0–6 points, not optimistic; 7–9 points, somewhat optimistic; 10–12 points, very optimistic; “Pessimistic”: 0–6 points, very pessimistic; 7–9 points, somewhat pessimistic; 10–12 points, not pessimistic.

The 12-item Hip Disability and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (HOOS-12) was used to measure functional outcomes preoperatively and at 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively. The HOOS-12 score evaluates symptoms and function via items from 4 subscales: pain, function, daily living, and quality of life. The items are scored on a 4-point Likert scale. The score ranges from 0 to 100. Higher scores indicate better results. The German score has been validated and checked for reliability,1314) and the minimal clinically important change (MCIC) ranges from 15.7 to 19.2 points.15)

Both aforementioned surveys were handed to the included participants during the last visit at the outpatient clinic before surgery (range, 1–8 weeks) and after surgery at the above-described follow-ups. The pseudonymized collected data were stored in an electronic database.

Statistical Analysis

The distribution of baseline characteristics was compared across very optimistic/pessimistic, somewhat optimistic/pessimistic, and not optimistic/pessimistic patients. To test for significant differences in distributions, the Kruskal-Wallis test was used for continuous variables and Pearson’s chi-square test for categorical variables.

The distributions of LOT-R optimism and pessimism were summarized with means and standard deviations (SDs). Hexagonal binning plots were used to visualize the relationships between baseline HOOS-12 scores and baseline LOT-R optimism and pessimism, respectively. To compare HOOS-12 score distributions between more and less optimistic/pessimistic patients across the 4 time points, grouped boxplots were used for visual comparison, and the corresponding means and SDs were presented side by side in a cross-table.

Correlations between baseline LOT-R optimism and HOOS-12 scores at baseline and 3, 6, and 12 months postoperative were calculated, including 95% CIs and p-values. To assess the expected change in HOOS-12 scores per 1-unit increase in optimism, we performed linear regressions and reported the corresponding coefficients with 95% CIs. To evaluate whether these associations persisted after adjusting for potential confounders, we computed partial correlations between baseline optimism and baseline HOOS-12 scores and at 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively. The following variables were considered as potential confounders: age, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score, body mass index (BMI), preoperative HOOS-12 score, EuroQol 5-Dimension (EQ-5D) score, Hospital for Special Surgery (HSS) expectation score, educational background (low vs. high), and sex. All analyses were conducted using multiply imputed datasets, with predictive mean matching as the imputation method, and were repeated using baseline LOT-R pessimism as the independent variable. Analyses were performed in R version 3.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing), with the level of significance set at 0.05.

RESULTS

Patient Demographics

The data of 846 patients were included in the study. Patients suffering from depression were excluded from the cohort. Table 1 shows the baseline characteristics of the overall cohort. The distributions of age, sex, BMI, marital status, and education level did not differ significantly across levels of optimism. In contrast, length of stay (LOS) varied significantly by optimism group: very optimistic patients were, on average, discharged earlier than somewhat optimistic and non-optimistic patients. Additionally, the proportion of patients with ASA levels III and IV was substantially higher among somewhat optimistic and non-optimistic patients compared to very optimistic patients (Tables 2 and 3).

Dispositional Optimism and Pessimism

Most patients were optimistic, with over half achieving the maximum score of 12 (Fig. 2), while fewer than 7% scored 6 or below, yielding a mean optimism score of 10.5 (SD, 2.1). Regarding pessimism (Fig. 3), although the proportion of patients reporting a relatively pessimistic mindset (score ≤ 6) was higher (17.6%) than the proportion reporting low optimism, the majority were not pessimistic. More than half scored 10 or higher, resulting in a mean pessimism score of 9.4 (SD, 2.0).

Functional Scores

HOOS-12 scores improved substantially throughout the first postoperative year (Tables 4 and 5, Figs. 4 and 5), starting with a preoperative mean of 37.2 (SD, 14.8) and reaching 84.8 (SD, 16.7) at 1 year postoperative.

Associations between Functional Scores and Dispositional Optimism/Pessimism

Baseline optimism was positively associated with HOOS-12 scores at baseline (r = 0.17; 95% CI, 0.15–0.18; p = 0.001) (Fig. 6) as well as at 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively (Table 6). Regressing HOOS-12 scores on LOT-R optimism yielded slopes between 1.23 and 1.58, indicating a 1.2- to 1.6-point increase in HOOS-12 per unit increase in optimism. After adjusting for confounders, optimism remained positively associated with HOOS-12 at all time points, though partial correlations were slightly weaker (e.g., partial r = 0.108 at baseline, p = 0.018) (Table 7).

Lower baseline pessimism was similarly associated with higher HOOS-12 scores (r = 0.21; 95% CI, 0.19–0.23; p < 0.001) (Table 6, Fig. 7), with slope estimates ranging from 0.98 to 1.44. After adjustment, the associations weakened and were no longer statistically significant at baseline and 3 months (e.g., partial r = 0.074 at baseline, p = 0.099) (Table 7). Further information about the regression coefficients is shown in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2.

DISCUSSION

The most important finding of this study was the significant association between the psychological variables (optimism and pessimism) and the postoperative functional outcomes of THA. The analyses revealed significantly increased HOOS-12 scores with increased optimism, whereas the opposite was true for pessimism scores, which corresponds with previous findings of postoperative recovery in optimists and pessimists.138) A bias through depression and anxiety, as outcome-modifying variables after THA,16) were excluded, as patients suffering from depression were excluded from the analyses. Analysis with preoperative scores as a covariate eliminated the potential bias of higher preoperative scores in optimistic patients than in their pessimistic counterparts, as patients with less preoperative impairment benefit more from the procedure.17) As optimism is understood as a general attitude that good things in life will happen to the individual, Scheier and Bridges18) found optimists to stay engaged in their goal to overcome confronting problems, while people who expected to be unsuccessful (i.e., pessimists) disengaged from their goal-directed efforts. Rasmussen et al.19) revealed optimism as a codetermining variable in coping strategies. Applied to rehabilitation after THA, this construct could presuppose that optimists put more effort into reaching their preset goals (i.e., improvement of joint function, improvement in activities in daily living) than their pessimistic counterparts do, thus generating higher functional scores through more ambitious training. Enhancing this effect could be the overall greater social support and treatment offers that optimists receive through society20) and their ability to discover positive aspects in their situations.21)

This study uses the 12-item short-form variant of the HOOS, which assesses items from multiple subcategories, such as pain, daily living, and quality of life. On the basis of its broad approach to measuring postoperative outcomes, the authors believe that the HOOS-12 is the ideal patient-reported outcome measure (PROM) when the influence of psychological variables is analyzed. Although PROMs seem to be the ultimate tool for measuring patient satisfaction, their status when analyzing psychological variables remains questionable. Recent data from China22) revealed that individuals with better mental well-being were more likely to report better PROM results. Claessen et al.23) reported on the influence of priming patients’ mindsets on the reported PROM outcome, showing that positive priming could enhance PROM scores in patients in an orthopedic hand and upper extremity hospital. When considering the clinical relevance of the study results, the MCIC in the HOOS-12 score of 15.7 to 19.2 points15) was not reached in the intergroup comparison of the optimism and pessimism subgroups, although there was an increasing difference in the HOOS score with increasing optimism or pessimism. Nevertheless, the authors interpret the change in scores as sufficient to justify examining personality traits in patients scheduled for THA and probably consider a preoperative behavioral intervention if risks such as considerable pessimism are detected.

The study design has several limitations. Patients were not chosen at random, as only patients already scheduled for THA were included in the study. Other potentially biasing comorbidities, such as alcoholism, could not be taken into account, as they were not identified in the database. Nevertheless, the strengths of a large, multicenter cohort, standardized treatment pathway, and postoperative rehabilitation, and the use of a validated scoring system outweigh these limitations in the analyses of stable traits such as optimism/pessimism.7)

Optimism and pessimism are opposing and significantly associated with postoperative outcomes after THA, although not reaching the MCIC of the HOOS-12 score. If patients scheduled for THA seem prone to pessimistic behavior, further screening of the patient’s personality traits and other risk factors should be conducted. The outcomes of these patients could be improved through psychoeducation or cognitive behavioral interventions.
