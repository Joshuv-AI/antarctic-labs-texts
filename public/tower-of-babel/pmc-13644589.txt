Gut bacteria and their metabolites may influence our decisions, intelligence, cognition, and consciousness
Authors: Leon M T Dicks, Alfred Botha, Carolina Pohl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe human gut hosts approximately 38 trillion bacteria, representing 400 to 500 species. Gut bacteria are in constant communication with the central nervous system (CNS) via the enteric nervous system (ENS), the peripheral nervous system (PNS), the autonomic nervous system (ANS), the immune system, the enteroendocrine system (EES), and the vagus nerve (VN). Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), especially butyrate, strengthen the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and modulate inflammatory signaling, thereby affecting brain function and cognitive performance. Acetate enhances the synthesis of lipid-rich myelin that protects neurons. Signaling molecules that cross the gut wall may optimize synaptic signaling in the frontal cortex. Bacterial metabolites that stimulate inflammatory responses activate the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, thereby increasing glucocorticoid production. This may reduce activity in the prefrontal cortex and suppress higher-order thinking and decision-making. We discuss how signaling molecules produced by gut bacteria influence the limbic system (thalamus, amygdala, hypothalamus, and hippocampus), primarily involved in emotions, memory, and motivation. The HPA axis and the gut microbiota–oligodendrocyte axis (GMOA) are discussed in more detail. We ask whether interactions between signaling molecules produced by gut bacteria and the limbic system can affect human intelligence, cognition, consciousness, and decision-making, coordinated by the cerebrum (cerebral cortex), prefrontal cortex, and parietal lobe.Keywords: Gut bacteria, metabolites, intelligence, cognition, consciousness

Introduction

The human gut hosts approximately 38 trillion bacteria, representing 400 to 500 species. 1 This is equivalent to the estimated three trillion (3.0 × 1013) cells in a person weighing 70 kg. 1 According to Tierney et al. 2 gut bacteria have approximately 46 million genes and, based on metagenomic studies, contribute over 99% of the genetic material in the human body. 1 This gives bacteria a far-reaching genetic “networking” capacity that may influence our intellect, emotions, cognition, consciousness, and decision-making processes. It is thus not surprising that the role of gut microbiota on intestinal homeostasis, immunity, brain development, and mental health is well documented, 3 and that the impact of diet, medication (especially antibiotics), age, lifestyle, and geography on the gut microbiome is covered in several reports. 4 , 5 A number of studies investigated the influence of microbial metabolites on neurotransmitter dysfunction, inconsistent ATP production, and cognitive impairment. 6-8 Evidence of a clear link between microbial dysbiosis and neurodevelopmental, neurodegenerative and psychiatric disorders such as autistic spectrum disorder (ASD), schizophrenia, Alzheimer’s disease (AD), major depressive disorder (MDD) and Parkinson’s disease (PD) prompted researchers to have a closer look at the gut-brain axis (GBA) and bidirectional communications between the CNS and the digestive system, neural, hormonal, immune, and metabolic pathways. 9

Bonham et al. 10 were the first to show that gut microbiota and their genes are associated with cognitive performance and brain structure. A total of 381 healthy children between 40 days and 10 years of age were enrolled in the study. Using a combination of multivariate linear models and machine learning (ML), the authors found large variations in microbial species in children older than 18 months. In this group of children, significant variations in cognitive function score (R2 = 0.0099, q < 0.01) and microbial gene functions (R2 = 0.0115, q < 0.001) were noted. Less variation in microbial taxa and genes, and less variation in cognitive functions, was observed in children younger than 6 months. Children older than 18 months and with higher cognitive function scores had higher cell numbers of Alistipes obesi, Asaccharobacter celatus (also known as Adlercreutzia equolifaciens subsp. celatus), Gordonibacter pamelaeae, and SCFA-producing probiotic species such as Eubacterium eligens and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii. 10 A. equolifaciens produces phytoestrogen-derived equol, 11 a selective estrogen receptor beta (ERβ) agonist and a powerful antioxidant that modulates metabolic and hormonal processes 12 and enhances the transcription of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ). 13 Studies conducted on mice 14 have shown that the activation of PPARγ resulted in a decline of Alzheimer's disease (AD), suggesting that it may prevent memory loss. PPARγ may thus support cognition by protecting nerve cells and reducing brain inflammation. G. pamelaeae produces the neuroprotective metabolite urolithin, 15 , 16 which may, hypothetically, account for the higher cognitive scores in children older than 18 months.

Several metabolic pathways were either significantly enriched or depleted in children with higher cognitive ability scores. 10 Genes encoding the degradation of acetate and propionate were significantly depleted in children younger than 6 months and with higher cognitive abilities. However, in children older than 18 months and with higher cognitive scores, an increase in acetate synthesis and propionate degradation was noted. These findings are noteworthy, as SCFAs play an important role in regulating the immune system and brain functions. 17 Fewer genes encoding the synthesis of menaquinone (vitamin K) were observed in cognitively compromised children. 10 Lower levels of menaquinone 4 (MK-4) were reported in children diagnosed with ASD. MK-4 is known for its neuroprotective abilities and may thus account for the increase in cognitive functions. 10

An increase in genes encoding the synthesis of the amino acids glutamate and tryptophan was noted in children 18 months and older with higher cognitive scores. 10 Glutamate regulates neuronal excitatory/inhibitory signaling. 18 , 19 A decrease in Trp metabolism, normally associated with gut dysbiosis, and systemic inflammation, led to an increase in indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO1) production, 20 associated with the production of neurotoxins, variations in serotonin production, and impaired intestinal barrier function, 21 suggesting that the Trp-GBA may influence cognitive states and possibly also behavior and decision-making.

Bagheri et al. 22 observed a notable correlation between plasma metabolites (signaling molecules) and the speed at which neurons process information. This may affect short-term and long-term cognitive functions, as shown in an experiment inoculating germ-free mice with human gut microbiota. 23 Although the gut microbiome may contribute to cognitive functions and learning via the GBA, 9 there is no direct evidence for this, and the concept of linking the gut microbiome to human intelligence, cognition, consciousness, and decision-making remains controversial. Human intelligence is a complex process that combines learning from experience, the ability to think abstractly, solve problems, and adapt to changing environments. Cognition is the collection of mental operations, perceptions, attention, memory, language, and reasoning, which are used to acquire, store, transform, and apply knowledge. 24 Consciousness is a multifaceted and dynamic state, shifting between subjective states, internal experiences, feelings, or awareness of the self and the surrounding world. 25 Decision-making is the cognitive process of evaluating options and selecting a specific course of action from alternatives to achieve a goal. 25

Many studies linking the gut microbiome to human intelligence, cognition, consciousness, and decision-making are flawed because they are based on conflicting findings stemming from methodological mistakes, individual variability, and the challenge of proving direct causality versus correlation in cognitive and neurological studies. Observational studies often link specific microbial profiles to conditions such as autism, depression, or AD, but suffer from statistical and conceptual limitations that fail to establish direct causation 26 , 27 Findings from murine studies often do not correlate with findings from clinical studies, mainly due to fundamental immune and metabolic differences, 28 and the inconsistent use of diagnostic biomarkers and profiling methods. 29 This emphasizes the need for human studies and more in-depth studies on microbial interactions, host metabolism, and neural networks, supported by multi-omics and neuroimaging.

In the present review, we discuss how signaling molecules produced by gut bacteria may influence our decisions, intelligence, cognition, and consciousness. We focus on the limbic system, specifically the thalamus, amygdala, hypothalamus, and hippocampus, which are primarily involved in emotions, memory, and motivation. 30 The hippocampus, which plays an important role in the formation of short-term and long-term memory, learning, and spatial orientation, is particularly exposed to signaling molecules produced by gut bacteria. 31 , 32 and informs the amygdala of physiological changes. 33 The thalamus sorts sensory information, analyses changes in physiological conditions, and alerts the amygdala, which activates the sympathetic nervous system and the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis. The latter governs the body’s hormonal stress response. We acknowledge that studies linking the gut microbiome to human cognition and decision-making have major limitations, including reliance on correlation rather than causation, small human sample sizes, and heavy dependence on animal models. We also understand that, due to the complexity of the human brain, concepts such as consciousness and intelligence are difficult to measure in the laboratory. Although human brain behavior cannot be attributed to a specific group of gut bacteria, it is important to examine how they communicate with the central nervous system via the complex network of neural, hormonal, immune, and metabolic pathways. Conclusions drawn from experiments conducted on laboratory animals are carefully evaluated.

Major axes transferring signals of gut bacteria to the brain

The gut-brain axis (GBA)

Gut bacteria are in constant bidirectional communication with the CNS via the enteric nervous system (ENS) and VN, collectively referred to as the GBA, reviewed by Dicks et al. 34 The major signaling molecules produced by gut microbiota, and their communication with the CNS, are shown in Figure 1. Microbial signals may act through intestinal epithelial cells (IECs), enteroendocrine cells (EECs), immune cells, the VN, hepatic metabolism, and circulating mediators. Although all these routes are summarised in one figure, they act independently from each other. Signaling molecules that cross the GBB are transferred to the circulatory system via the hepatic portal vein (Figure 1). Signals reach the brain via the VN and enter the brain stem (Figure 1), from where they synapse onto elongated nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS) neurons A2, Prlh+, Glp1+, and Cck+. 35 Although gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), serotonin (5-HT), dopamine (DA), and norepinephrine (NE) in Figure 1 are shown as neurotransmitters produced in the gut and crossing the intestinal barrier, it is important to note that they remain trapped in the peripheral circulation and cannot pass through the blood-brain barrier (BBB). Instead, they influence the brain indirectly (such as through the VN).

Signaling molecules produced by gut bacteria also regulate immune responses and stimulate oligodendrocytes to produce a multilayered lipid membrane (myelin) sheath that protects neurons. 36 The effects of signaling molecules on hormone production and neuronal myelination are covered in the following sections. All these systems need to be in perfect balance to control metabolism and maintain the gut microbiota in a balanced (homeostatic) state to regulate memory, learning, stress, and mood.

Experimental evidence supporting the impact of gut bacteria on neurogenesis and cognitive functions is discussed in several papers. Carlson et al. 37 showed that there may be a link between gut bacteria and cognitive development. Certain gut bacteria, notably bifidobacteria, may improve our ability to recognize and discriminate between objects and modulate our cognitive processes, as shown with studies conducted on mice. 38 Changes in the gut microbiome of mice influenced their social development, brain development, repetitive behavior, and cognitive functions. 39 None of these physiological and neurological changes were observed in germ-free mice. 39 The authors noted that similar impaired neurodevelopmental disorders were observed in patients suffering from schizophrenia and autism and hypothesized that these deficits may be associated with modulation of the immune system, specifically the release of cytokines, and changes in VN activity and neuroendocrine functions. Desbonet et al. 39 have shown that treatment of mice with antibiotics following weaning increased anxiety, altered cognitive behaviors, and modulated neurological development in manners similar to those observed in germ-free mice. The authors concluded that dysregulation of the GBA in the post-weaning period may contribute to these disorders in adulthood, and attributed it to changes in neuromodulators such as noradrenaline, Trp, and the neuropeptides oxytocin and vasopressin. As mentioned elsewhere in this review, care should be taken not to make direct comparisons between animal studies and humans.

Studies conducted on human volunteers suffering from depression and anxiety 40 have shown that changes in the gut microbiome cause systemic inflammation and alterations in SCFA production, leading to altered cognitive function. Foster et al. 41 reported that cognitive functions in humans are dependent on the immune system and the production of neurotransmitters by gut bacteria. In another study, Dinan and Cryan 42 showed that probiotics reduced inflammation and improved cognitive functions. Studies on rat PC12 cells showed that SCFAs can modulate genes encoding the cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) response element-binding (CREB) protein, which regulates the synthesis of catecholamine neurotransmitters, such as DA. 43 , 44 Studies with rats showed that the conversion of DA to NE is initiated by SCFAs that activate the expression of tyrosine hydroxylase, the first reaction in the production of DA. This decreases the expression of DA-β-hydroxylase (DBH; EC 1.14.17.1), and thus the conversion of DA to NE. 45 , 46 The hippocampus and striatum are especially susceptible to DA, which triggers a “neurotransmitter reward” response, and thus a change in mood. 47 The rate at which DA is synthesized, including its conversion to NE, needs to be tightly regulated. A deficiency in DBH (thus elevated DA levels) may have a serious negative effect on the autonomic nervous system (ANS), which controls the regulation of blood pressure and body temperature. 48

SCFAs regulate several other physiological functions in the human body, e.g., the maturation and functioning of microglia in the CNS, 49 and the transmission of signals generated by 5-HT, GABA and DA to neurons. 50 , 51 In EECs, SCFAs stimulate the release of gut hormones. 52

Bile salts, combined with SCFAs, play an integral part in enterohepatic circulation, but are equally important in the regulation of neuronal pathways and signaling to the CNS. 53 Studies with rats showed that free-fatty acid receptors FFAR2 and FFAR3 are expressed in the ENS, portal nerve and sensory ganglia, 54 , 55 suggesting that SCFAs regulate the nervous system. This is supported by the induction of the nuclear phosphoprotein Fos in the dorsal vagal complex of the brainstem, the hypothalamus, and spinal cord. 54 Fos expression is induced by serum, growth factors, tumor promoters, and cytokines. For more information on gut bacteria and neurotransmitters, the reader is referred to Dicks. 56

Glutamate (Glu), released from presynaptic nerve terminals, is the principal excitatory neurotransmitter in the brain and plays a key role in memory storage. 57 The adherence of Glu to ionotropic glutamate receptors (iGluRs), located on postsynaptic terminals, increases the transfer of Ca2+ through voltage-activated calcium channels (VACCs), and activates calcium calmodulin-dependent kinase (CaMK; EC 2.7.11.17). Glu also activates extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK, EC 2.7.11.24) and the CREB protein, all crucial for protein synthesis and maintenance of synaptic density. Excess Glu is taken up by glial cells via the EAAT1 and EAAT2 transporters. Glu is then converted to glutamine, which is transported back to presynaptic nerve terminals and converted to Glu by glutaminase (EC 3.5.1.2). The over-excitation of Glu (glutamate excitotoxicity) accelerates the progression of AD. 58 For more information on glutamate excitotoxicity, the reader is referred to the review by Esposito et al. 59

GABA is found in many brain regions and is released into the synaptic cleft upon depolarization of presynaptic neurons. 60 GABA is also produced in glia (astrocytes) connected with pre- and post-synaptic neurons 61 but different metabolic pathways. 62 In GABA-expressing neurons, α-ketoglutarate, synthesized in the Krebs cycle, is converted to L-Glu by GABA α-oxoglutarate transaminase (GABA-T, EC 2.6.1.19) and decarboxylated to GABA by glutamate decarboxylase (GAD). In glial cells, Glu is not decarboxylated to GABA, as these cells do not express GAD. Instead, GABA is synthesized from N-acetylputrescine with monoamine oxidase B (MAOB, EC 1.4.3.4) as a key enzyme. 62 GABA in glia is converted to succinic semialdehyde by GABA-T but also to glutamine, which is deaminated to glutamate before it re-enters the GABA shunt. 60

In germ-free mice, significant neuronal development in the dorsal hippocampal region (essential for spatial learning and memory) was noted. 63 In another study with infant germ-free mice, 64 significant morphological changes of the amygdala and hippocampus were noted. The decline in neurogenesis could not be reversed by recolonizing the mice with gut bacteria, 65 suggesting that neurogenesis is an early event and is controlled by the gut microbiome. In another study on mice, 66 a decrease in hippocampal neurogenesis was prevented by pre-treatment with a combination of Lactobacillus helveticus and Bifidobacterium longum. Rolls et al. 67 noted that mice deficient in TLR2 showed a reduction in neurogenesis and hippocampal volume, whilst an improvement in neurogenesis was observed in mice lacking TLR4 (genetic knockouts). However, mice lacking TLR4 showed a decrease in learning ability. 68 The binding of lipopolysaccharides to TLR4 on neural progenitor cells (NPCs) inhibits proliferation and neuronal differentiation through MyD88 signaling. 67 MyD88 is a myeloid differentiation primary response protein in humans that acts as a central communication hub for the innate immune system. 67

Stimulation of the VN affects various cognitive functions, including learning and attention, verbal, working, and short-term memory, and decision-making. 69 Electrical stimulation of the VN in mice led to improved memory, enhanced learning, and the recall of information. 70 On a molecular level, VN stimulation is associated with changes in neurotransmitters such as GABA, Glu, 5-HT, DA, and NE. 69 An increase in GABA led to an increase in synaptic connections between neurons in the brain and synaptic plasticity, both essential for learning and memory. Memory enhancement, accompanied by the stimulation of the VN, was noted when the levels of GABA and NE were increased. 69 , 71 In mice, the vagal afferent nerves were activated when the diet was supplemented with GABA. 72 Activation of the VN leads to an increase in the release of DA in areas such as the ventral tegmental area (VTA) and the substantia nigra, which are essential for neural plasticity related to reward and learning processes. 69 , 72-75 A decrease in the population and diversity of gut bacteria (referred to as dysbiosis) alters VN stimulation and increases the number of pathogenic bacteria that produce signaling molecules that may lead to inflammation, oxidative stress, an imbalance in energy supply, increased cellular degeneration, and brain dysfunction. 76 Both of the latter phenomena are instigated by an increase in the permeability of the intestinal mucosal barrier and the BBB. 77

The hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis

The hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis is the body's primary stress response system. The arcuate nucleus (ARC) of the hypothalamus and forebrain receives signals via the NTS (Figure 2, bottom) in the lower part of the brain stem, the medulla. Connection of the NTS with the neuromodulator nuclei is shown in Figure 2 (rectangle). The NTS connects with the nucleus basalis of Meynert in the basal forebrain, raphe nuclei in the midbrain, pons, and medulla; and the locus coeruleus in the pons. These nuclei regulate vital homeostatic functions such as feeding behavior, appetite, 78 the release of hormones (e.g., cortisol, oxytocin, and vasopressin), and the production of glutamate and GABA, acetylcholine, NE, and 5-HT. They also distribute the hormones to the cortex, subcortical nuclei, and spine. 35 Although Gruber et al. 35 did not find evidence of a link between HPA dysfunction and cognition, they concluded that a more responsive HPA axis may be associated with an increase in cognitive abilities, especially at old age. Sharan and Vellapandian, 79 however, found that chronic HPA axis dysregulation and an overproduction of cortisol can impair memory, decision-making, and long-term cognitive functions.

The hippocampus (a seahorse-shaped structure located deep within the brain’s temporal lobe, Figure 2, right side) is highly sensitive to changes in the HPA axis, due to a high density of glucocorticoid (cortisol) receptors (GRs) in this region. The expression of GRs is, however, reduced during dysbiosis, increasing the abundance of pathogenic bacteria. 80 Patients suffering from acute Crohn’s disease (chronic inflammation) may have a dysfunctional HPA, impairing the body's ability to regulate inflammation and stress responses. 81 An overactive HPA (increased cortisol production) becomes resistant to glucocorticoids and loses the ability to inhibit inflammation. The release of corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) increases intestinal permeability and may lead to leaky gut syndrome, thereby amplifying inflammatory responses. 82 An increase in interleukin-17A (IL-17A) from T helper 17 (Th17) cells in the lamina propria leads to an increase in the release of aged neutrophils into the bloodstream. Mice subjected to chronic stress showed a decrease in beneficial bacteria such as Lactobacillus, and an increase in kynurenine. Treatment with Lactobacillus reuteri controlled kynurenine levels, normalised (balanced) the gut microbiome, and ameliorated behavioral abnormalities. 83 Repressing the production of glucocorticoid and blocking of IL-17A alleviated stress and improved learning. 84

Germ-free mice with an impaired HPA displayed changes in the prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, and amygdala. 85 Long-term exposure to high levels of glucocorticoids leads to decreased activity in the prefrontal cortex, and thus to lower-order thinking and decision-making. At the same time, activity in the amygdala (which regulates emotional responses and survival instincts) increases. 86 For further information on the HPA axis, the reader is referred to the review by Alavian and Safaeian. 87

The gut microbiota–oligodendrocyte axis (GMOA)

The gut microbiota–oligodendrocyte axis (GMOA, Figure 3) refers to the association between gut microbiota and the multilayered lipid membrane myelin produced by oligodendrocytes in the CNS. The sheathing of nerve axons allows for the leaping and sudden propagation of action potentials 88 and increases nerve impulse velocity as much as 100-fold. 89 Myelin is produced by oligodendrocytes in the CNS and Schwann cells in the PNS. 90

A summary of the gut microbiota–oligodendrocyte axis (GMOA), also known as the gut-myelin axis. Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), especially acetate, produced by gut bacteria from the metabolism of dietary fiber, cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB). Acetyl-CoA synthetase in oligodendrocytes (located in the central nervous system, CNS), and Schwann cells in the peripheral nervous system (PNS), converts acetate into acetyl-CoA (ac-CoA), which is used in lipid biosynthesis pathways to form and maintain the myelin sheath that surrounds neurons. Ac-CoA provides acetyl groups for histone acetyltransferases (HATs) and inhibits histone deacetylase (HDAC). Acetylation of histone proteins relaxes the chromatin structure and activates the transcription of genes encoding myelin production. Indole-3-propionic acid (IPA) and indole-3-aldehyde (I3A), produced by gut bacteria, cross the BBB. IPA, a potent antioxidant and a strong ligand for the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR), reduces neuroinflammation, lowers amyloid-beta aggregation, and increases neuroprotective metabolites such as kynurenic acid. I3A acts as an agonist for the AhR and activates the AhR pathway. I3A thus regulates immune responses and neuroinflammation, which helps to suppress systemic and central nervous system autoimmunity. I3A binds to the vitamin D receptor (VDR) to regulate mucosal immunity, intestinal homeostasis, and epithelial barrier integrity. Tryptophan, an essential amino acid, is absorbed by enterocytes in the small intestine, from where it enters the bloodstream. Colonic bacteria convert tryptophan into indole and its derivatives (IPA and tryptamine). Tryptophan prevents the destruction of myelin, thus preventing neuroinflammation. Tryptamine binds to trace amine-associated receptors (TAARs) and serotonin (5-HT) receptors, and functions as a neuromodulator of the CNS. SCFAs, especially acetate, produced by gut bacteria from the metabolism of dietary fiber, cross the BBB. Ac-CoA synthetase in oligodendrocytes (located in the CNS), and Schwann cells in the PNS, converts acetate into ac-CoA, which feeds directly into the lipid biosynthesis pathways required to form and maintain the myelin sheath. 88 , 90 The acetyl groups from ac-CoA serve as substrates for histone acetyltransferases (HATs). Acetylation of histone proteins relaxes the chromatin structure and activates the transcription of genes encoding myelin production. The myelin (myelin sheath) provides structural support to axons. 90

An increase in acetate, produced by gut bacteria, enhanced the synthesis of myelin lipids in a rodent model. 91 Dysbiosis related to the depletion of acetate-producing bacteria may thus lead to reduced myelin production and dysfunctional neurons. 88 Changes in the myelination of neurons in areas of the brain involved with memory and learning will thus affect cognitive functions, social behavior, memory, and decision-making. Humans experience late myelination of frontal cortex axons in their third decade of life, making them vulnerable to external factors such as microbial metabolites. 92 Recent research on the GBA indicated that demyelination may be reversed by increasing the population of certain gut bacteria. Further research on the GMOA may shed more light on multiple sclerosis (MS), which affects approximately 2.8 million people worldwide. 93

IPA and I3A, produced by gut bacteria, cross the BBB. IPA is a potent antioxidant and a strong ligand for the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) and reduces neuroinflammation, lowers amyloid-beta aggregation, and increases neuroprotective metabolites such as kynurenic acid. 94 I3A, synthesized by gut bacteria, acts as an agonist for the AhR. By activating the AhR pathway, I3A regulates immune responses and neuroinflammation and plays a critical role in suppressing systemic and central nervous system autoimmunity. 95 The interaction between I3A and the vitamin D receptor (VDR) regulates mucosal immunity, intestinal homeostasis, and epithelial barrier integrity. 96

Tryptophan is an essential amino acid (not synthesized by the human body) and is supplied from dietary sources such as meats, eggs, and seeds. 97 Specialized intestinal epithelial cells (enterocytes) in the small intestine absorb tryptophan and transport it to the bloodstream. 97 Tryptophan that is not absorbed reaches the colon, where it is co-metabolised by gut bacteria and the host. Gut bacteria convert tryptophan into indole, IPA, and tryptamine. Between 1 and 2% of the tryptophan is converted into serotonin by enterochromaffin cells. Most of the tryptophan is metabolized via the kynurenine pathway, from where it regulates the immune system and prevents the destruction of myelin (thus preventing neuroinflammation). 98 Tryptamine binds to trace amine-associated receptors (TAARs) and serotonin (5-HT) receptors to regulate dopaminergic, serotonergic, and glutamatergic systems. Tryptamine, thus, functions as a neuromodulator of the CNS. For further information on MS and GMOA, the reader is referred to the review by Torres et al. 88 and Tang et al. 99

Hoban et al. 100 discovered that certain gut bacteria stimulate the myelination of nerves in the prefrontal cortex (PFC) and stated that “appropriate cortical myelination relies on the presence of a functional microbiota during critical windows of neurodevelopment”. We know gut bacteria stimulate synaptic growth and plasticity, as clearly evident in patients with neurological disorders such as PD and AD. 101

How does bacterial “intelligence” influence our intelligence?

Bacterial “intelligence” may be defined as the ability to adapt to external factors and practise competitive coherence. The latter refers to connectivity-based competition between a current (developing) phenotypic and genotypic state and subsequent states. For example, a cell that expresses a set of genes encoding, e.g., heat shock and cold shock proteins, 102 is likely to be outcompeted by a next generation of cells that requires only one of the two gene sets. Thus, for bacteria to maintain a competitive coherent state, the cell must adapt to internal and external conditions and overcome selective pressure. If cells make the wrong decision (imposed upon by, e.g., a sudden change in molecular stimuli), they will not evolve and survive. Bacterial sentience is defined as “feelings” or behavior based on “experiences,” and cognition as the ability to receive, process (learn), store, and act (decide) based on observations (e.g., of the environment) and memory. 103 Biofilm formation is a good example displaying intelligence, sentience, and cognition. In a biofilm, genetically related cells exposed to the same environment develop new phenotypic characteristics (known as bet-hedging), alter their phenotypic characteristics to explore multiple environmental niches (referred to as stochastic switching), and display individualistic nutrient uptake. 104 Collective decision-making is a coordinated, social process, often driven by changes in chemical signals that trigger quorum sensing (QS) at a specific cell density. Gram-negative bacteria may produce several forms of AHLs, 105 each detected by specific LuxR (luciferase regulator) receptors 106 , 107 AHLs pass through the cell membrane and bind directly to soluble receptor proteins. The SdiA (suppressor of division inhibition) QS receptor, found in Escherichia coli and Salmonella spp., activates transcription of the ftsQAZ gene cluster, thereby increasing production of the cell-division protein FtsZ. LuxS (S-ribosylhomocysteine lyase) cleaves the thioether bond of S-ribosylhomocysteine (SRH) to produce 4,5-dihydroxy-2,3-pentanedione (DPD), which, due to its instability, immediately cyclizes and rearranges into various AI-2 derivatives. 108 AI-3, combined with Epi and NE, forms the AI-3/Epi/NE pathway, which coordinates gene expression, motility, and virulence. 109 Enterohemorrhagic E. coli (EHEC) uses the AI-3/Epi/NE pathway to upregulate the locus of enterocyte effacement (LEE) pathogenicity island, promoting the formation of lesions in IECs 110 For further information, the reader is referred to the review by Dicks. 111 and Dicks et al. 108

Bacteria developed a complex “toolkit” by assessing the significance of the signals they receive, evaluating each signal, and responding in their best interest. 112 The spectrum of molecular signals bacteria respond to, and the machinery used to process signals and respond to each, differ for each species and strain. Key signaling mechanisms of bacteria are quorum sensing (QS), two-component systems (TCSs), and mechanosensing (e.g., the triggering of cyclic diguanylate, c-di-GMP). 113 Memory-based decisions are not made on changes in individual molecules but on persistent changes in bacterial cells, e.g., DNA methylation, histone modifications, and changes in glycolysis. 114 , 115

The survival of bacteria (living cells in general) depends on the ability to synchronize gene transcription and translation (mRNA, tRNA), express polyamines, functional proteins and enzymes, produce macromolecules such as fatty acids, adenosine triphosphate (ATP), cytidine triposphate (CTP), cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP), and regulate the flow of micro molecules, such as calcium, iron, zinc, etc. Cells “learn” from DNA regions transcribed upstream, gene activators and repressors, mRNA terminators, and post-translational modifications to avoid next-generation mistakes. The adaptation and reaction of bacteria to the gastrointestinal environment is far more complex than an “action-reaction” process. This is especially evident with cells grouped in a colony or biofilm. Grouped cells self-/re-organize according to their nutritional requirements and form “hyper structures” such as cytoskeletal filaments; elongation factor Tu (EF-Tu); CTP synthase; RNases; proteinaceous organelles (large, polyhedral, protein-based assemblies) that sequester metabolic intermediates; chemoreceptors; lipid rafts; acidocalcisomes; polyphosphate granules; E1 proteins that unwind DNA ahead of the replication fork; enzyme complexes, e.g., involved in phosphorylation; membrane proteins; and proteins involved in DNA replication, transcription, transfer of signaling molecules across cell membranes, and bacteriophage adhesion. 116

The “intelligence” of gut bacteria is perhaps best understood by analysing the genetic abilities of a simple (but intelligent) bacterium such as Escherichia coli. The cell’s growth, metabolism, colonisation of the gut wall (intestinal epithelial cells, IECs, and mucus layers), replication, and survival are controlled by more than 300 transcriptional regulators. 117 This allows E. coli to degrade different sources of carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, and sulphur, withstand changes in oxygen tension, temperature, osmotic pressure, and pH, and develop resistance to, e.g., heavy metals, free radicals, and antibiotics. 117

Bacteria transfer molecular signals using one-, two-, and multi-component systems located in the cell membrane. TCSs are often referred to as a “nano-brain”, thereby implying that they represent neuronal networks of higher life forms. Neuronal connections are limited to synapses on dendrites. In bacteria, these connections are represented by nanotubes and nanowires. 116 With such a complex signal-transducing network, metabolically active bacteria are constantly in a state of competitive coherence and relate current phenotypic and genetic states to previously encountered scenarios. 116 Taking all these functions into consideration and keeping in mind that they are on average 1-2 μm in length, one may argue that the genetic capabilities of bacteria are superior to those of humans.

There is a close relationship between gut bacteria and brain development, particularly concerning learning, attention, and memory. 118 However, research linking human intelligence, cognition, consciousness, and decision-making to the gut microbiome is complex (Figure 4) and often borders on assumptions rather than experimental facts pointing to specific agents or signalling molecules. Tillisch et al. 119 claimed that probiotic bacteria alter human cognitive behavior. Although a valuable observation, the reasons and mechanisms involved have not been clarified. Studies on patients with irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), which we know is caused by many factors, including an imbalance in gut microbiota, revealed evidence of a stress-related impairment in visuospatial memory, 120 defined as a cognitive system responsible for temporarily storing, maintaining, and manipulating visual and spatial information, such as the shapes of objects, their colors, and their locations in an environment. 121 Research approaches to study the influence of gut microbiota on human intelligence, cognition, consciousness, and decision-making are listed in Table 1.

Gut bacteria regulate hippocampal neurogenesis and may thereby affect learning and memory. 65 Disturbances in the microbiota led to abnormal neurogenesis and cognitive functions, as shown with a significant increase in dorsal hippocampal neurons 63 and morphological changes in the amygdala and hippocampus of infant mice. The recolonization of mice after infancy did not stimulate neurogenesis, suggesting that the regulation of neurogenesis by the gut microbiota must occur early in life. 65 A decrease in neurogenesis, due to stress, could be prevented by pretreatment with probiotic strains of Lactobacillus helveticus and Bifidobacterium longum. 66 A deficiency in TLR2 reduced neurogenesis and hippocampal volume, whereas TLR4 knockout mice showed an increase in neurogenesis, 67 accompanied by a decrease in learning ability. 68 The reason for these changes was attributed to the disruption of a MyD88-dependent Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) signaling cascade when MyD88 was inhibited. The blocking of MyD88 disrupts the binding of LPS to TLR4 on neural progenitor cells (NPCs) and deactivates NF-κB. 67 This prevents the downstream transcriptional programs that normally protect cells or drive inflammation, resulting in reduced inflammatory signaling and altered neurodevelopment. These studies demonstrate the role of bacteria and their metabolites on neurodevelopment and their potential impact on the regulation of cognitive function. It is, however, interesting to note that when the developing of neurons in the hippocampus is suppressed during early life, communication between the existing neurons increase to retain or improve memory. 149-151

Most studies failed to identify specific bacterial molecules or chemical reactions that impact learning and memory. The first breakthrough came with the report from Kunze et al. 152 showing that Lactobacillus reuteri repressed the opening of calcium-dependent potassium channels and, by doing so, decreased the hyperpolarization in sensory colonic AH (after hyperpolarization) neurons in the ENS. AH neurons experience a prolonged drop in membrane potential for up to 20 secs, due to changes in calcium-dependent potassium channels. 153 They act as intrinsic primary afferent neurons (IPANs), focused on detecting physical and chemical changes in the intestinal tract, e.g., the expansion or contraction of the gut wall, nutrient levels, and changes in pH. 153 In humans, serotonin- and GABA-producing gut microbiota regulate the maturation of the ENS and relays vital sensory signals back to the CNS via the ENS, PNS, and VN. 154 O'Mahony et al. 155 claimed that altering the gut microbiota of rats early in life alleviated visceral pain in fully grown rats, but did not affect cognitive or anxiety-related behaviors. In a subsequent study, 156 it was shown that the response to pain was due to an increase in activity-related transcriptional pathways in the amygdala. Dendrites in the amygdala and hippocampus of mice with an imbalanced gut microbiome were notably different from those in the control group, 64 suggesting that the functioning of neurons in these areas of the brain depends on a balanced gut microbiome.

Neurotransmitters such as GABA, DA and tryptophan derivatives (e.g., serotonin) enter the bloodstream directly, bypassing neurons of the ENS/PNS and communicate directly with distant organs. 157 These metabolites reach the liver through the portal vein and interact directly with receptors on cells in the pancreas, kidneys, lungs, and heart. 158 Cytokines, released from immune cells that have been stimulated by bacterial signaling molecules, activate microglia of the CNS. 159 This “bacteria-mind continuity” supports the idea of an endless cooperation between biological and mind processes, suggesting that consciousness may be deeply rooted in the physiology of bacteria and humans, supporting the theory of a “holobiont mind” 160 (Figure 5), which re-defines human intelligence, cognition, and consciousness. Mark Rowlands 161 referred to this hypothetical extracranial process as “4E cognition”, with reference to the four “Es” in cognition, i.e., “embodied”, “embedded”, “enacted”, and “extended”). Embodied cognition defines our ability to think, learn, and remember based on how we interact with the physical world. Embedded cognition refers to the use of tools and social structures to support cognitive functions. Enacted cognition is defined as information gained via dynamic interactions (e.g., the human body and its environment). The GBA, HPA, and GMOA fall within this definition. Enacted cognition is often referred to as enactivism, which relates to embodied cognition, combined with a sense-making process that involving brain, body, environment, and subjective experience. Extended cognition is more of a philosophical concept and is described as functions of the mind and cognitive processes spilling over to the environment. This suggests that physical objects, sensory experiences, environments, and people influence our thinking, learning, and memory. This implies that the brain is a computational organ influenced by many factors. Not addressed in this hypothesis is the influence of the gut microbiome and the signaling molecules they produce on thinking, learning, and memory. This suggests that gut bacteria may have a more profound influence over our intelligence, cognition, consciousness, and decision-making than originally anticipated.

Conclusions

Human intelligence, cognition, consciousness, and decision-making are not solely “brain-bound”. The signaling molecules and neurotransmitters produced by gut bacteria, e.g., 5-HT, DA, and GABA may affect our responses to internal and external stimuli. Gut bacteria may play a crucial role in neurological development, regulating brain chemistry, mood, social behavior, and stress responses. Bacterial interactions with the CNS are driven by the GBA, HPA, and GMOA. Gut bacteria act as an “organ” in a complex, symbiotic ecosystem, participating in the regulation of metabolism, biochemical pathways, immune functions, and neurological processes. Gut bacteria and their metabolites may modulate specific aspects of brain function through immune, endocrine, metabolic, neural, and myelin-related mechanisms, while direct evidence for effects on human intelligence and consciousness remains limited. The HPA axis and the gut microbiota-oligodendrocyte axis form a bidirectional communication network that may shape brain structure and function. The HPA axis governs the body’s hormonal stress response, while the GMOA controls myelin production. Together, they influence cognition, learning, and mental health, though direct links to raw human intelligence or core consciousness remain theoretical. Since the hippocampus plays an important role in learning and stress regulation, it is important to study the role of gut microbiota on the limbic system and other brain centers. We need to adopt a holistic approach in examining the role gut bacteria play in brain functions. We need to reconsider how we study interspecies quorum sensing (QS), gut-bacteria-generated signaling molecules, bacterial communication with IECs, the ENS and CNS, and immune reactions. The relatively recent “4E cognition” concept is aimed at improving our understanding of how we think, learn, and remember. Intelligence, cognition, and consciousness are a holobiontic product in which gut bacteria are part of a larger network (Figure 5).
