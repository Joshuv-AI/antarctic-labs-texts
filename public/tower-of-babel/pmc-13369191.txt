Asymmetric pathways for lithium extraction and recovery based on the two-phase equilibrium of layered oxides
Authors: Grant T. Hill, Raphael Stone, Yu Han, Jiadong Liu, Siqi Zou, Hua Zhou, Chong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Electrochemical intercalation offers a promising platform for Li+ extraction. However, only limited types of electrode materials have been investigated. The challenge to broaden and tailor materials for electrochemical intercalation-based Li+ extraction lies in the lack of understanding of material’s response upon co-intercalation of multiple ions, therefore, paired process design to enable reversible Li+ extraction and recovery. Here, we showcase the design of asymmetric ion pathways for Li+ extraction and recovery for host material with complex Li+ and Na+ interaction using layered cobalt oxide as a model material. The two-phase equilibrium of Na0.48CoO2 and Li0.94CoO2 governs Li+ selectivity when a high depth of intercalation is achieved (low vacancy level). We show that the relative rate between ion exchange and intercalation is critical to determine the ion pathways. The relationship can be quantitatively compared using the average pseudo ion exchange rate (CpseudoIX) and the intercalation rate (Cinter). The ion pathways at the three regimes with CpseudoIX > Cinter, CpseudoIX ~ Cinter, and CpseudoIX <Cinter are constructed. By selecting the optimized ion pathway and particle size, we demonstrate 9.7×104 Li+ selectivity with 99% purity Li+ recovery from an initial 1:1000 Li: Na molar ratio solution using 115 mAh/g specific capacity.

Introduction

Projected lithium supply will not meet half the expected demand by 2040, required by the renewable energy technology deployment for climate change mitigation goals1. Extracting Li+ from unconventional sources is critical to overcoming the expected Li+ supply shortage2–4. Even though feasible, Li+ extraction from unconventional sources remains challenging due to competing ions such as Na+ being 74 (Salton Sea, California) to 20,000 (seawater) times greater in abundance3. Additionally, the common soda-lime evaporation method causes significant depletions of water, which amplifies water scarcity issues5,6. Electrochemically driven ion intercalation into the interstitial sites of electrode material is an attractive solution for Li+ extraction by being environmentally friendly, highly Li+ selective, and energy efficient7–12. The high Li+ over Na+ selectivity can be achieved via the proper selection and tailoring of electrode materials to have preferable charge transfer and mass transport behaviors8–11,13. The competition from divalent ions (such as Mg2+) is greatly reduced owing to the undesirable intercalation because of the high desolvation energies, structural instability, and high migration energies in the lattice3,14. However, the development of tailored materials for Li+ extraction is still a nascent field. This is because no mature Li+ extraction system exists yet. The solution environments that the host electrode must go through repeatedly for extraction and recovery are largely undecided and host material dependent. The materials' operation conditions are also undetermined. Therefore, understanding and predicting host material response in complex solution environments is critical to define the working conditions and material challenges for Li+ extraction and recovery, as well as to identifying solutions for achieving high Li+ selectivity, fast extraction, and durability that are required for technology deployment.

The layered transition metal oxides emerge as a promising family of host materials for Li+ extraction owing to their thermodynamic favorability of Li+ to Na+ and the ability to form phase-separated structures to stabilize Li+ and Na+ co-intercalation9,15,16. In particular, Li parent material Li1-vCoO2 (v represents vacancy level) has been shown to undergo spontaneous, non-Faradaic ion exchange at room temperature in Na+ containing aqueous electrolyte when 1-v < 0.94 to form a phase equilibrium between Na0.51CoO2 and Li0.94CoO29. This leads to mixed (NaLi)1-vCoO2 that has phase separations in terms of the spatial distribution of alkali ions and cobalt valence states (Co3+/4+), and variations of layer spacing throughout the particle. For Na parent material Na1-vCoO2 with 0.48 < 1-v < 0.94, the phase equilibrium is between Na0.48CoO2 and Li0.94CoO2. The slight difference in the Na phase could be due to particle property difference16. This prior work primarily focused on materials synthesis, which revealed the strong potential of layered cobalt oxide as an effective electrode for lithium extraction. When biphasic equilibrium occurs between Na0.48CoO2 and Li0.94CoO2, it indicates that the mobile ions will be swinging between Na+ and Li+ with host vacancy level v changing between 0.06 and 0.52 (Fig. 1). The vacancy dependent composition indicates a theoretically high Li+ to Na+ selectivity since the Li+ contents increase as the vacancy level decreases and a pure Li phase can be achieved with high degree of intercalation with <0.06 vacancy level. More than four orders of magnitude Li+ to Na+ selectivity has been hinted at for (NaLi)1-vCoO2 in our previous work9,16. However, the design of paired intercalation and recovery processes to enable reversible cycling has not been addressed for the layered cobalt oxide electrode. In this work, using a model layered cobalt oxide, we investigated the electrochemical co-intercalation ion pathways and unveiled the importance of the particle size for reversible electrochemical ion exchange and the relative kinetics between intercalation and ion exchange for Li+ extraction capacity, selectivity, and reversibility. The two-phase equilibrium between Li0.94CoO2 and Na0.48CoO2 divided the materials space into Li-rich and Na-rich regions when driven out of equilibrium with v between 0.06 and 0.52 (Fig. 1a). To quantitatively compare the kinetics, we define an average ion exchange rate as CpseudoIX and categorize the different intercalation behaviors into three characteristic regimes. Using both in situ and ex situ composition and structural characterization, we constructed the three different ion pathways that depend on the relationship between CpseudoIX and intercalation rate (Cinter). This relationship is predictive of the behavior when intercalating from Li0.48CoO2 as the extraction starting material. At CpseudoIX < Cinter (Regime 3), the intercalation quickly brings the structure into the Na-rich out-of-equilibrium region, with the following intercalation resembling the Na+ intercalation features. In this case, ion exchange is needed after intercalation to bring the structure back to equilibrium to restore the Li+ content, and if ion exchange cannot be completed, both Li+ selectivity and recovered Li+ purity will decrease. We also show that particle morphology is important, and smaller particles can promote faster and more reversible ion exchange. At CpseudoIX > Cinter (Regime 1), coupled intercalation and ion exchange maintain the structure in the Li-rich region. A high depth of intercalation and high Li+ selectivity can be achieved in a single step of intercalation without additional ion exchange. When CpseudoIX is close to Cinter (Regime 2), the ion pathway is a combination of the previous two, with a longer period of intercalation maintained in the Li-rich region compared with Regime 3; however, the structure still enters the Na-rich region at the end. Enabled by the understanding of the ion pathway, we designed the Li+ extraction and recovery processes. The extracted Li is recovered in a pure Li+ solution and is not C-rate dependent, which makes the extraction and recovery processes asymmetric. We achieved 9.7 × 104 Li+ selectivity during extraction from an initial solution with a 1:1000 Li: Na molar ratio and ~ 99% purity during Li+ recovery with 115 mAh/g specific capacity. A good reversibility was also demonstrated with the optimized Li+ extraction and recovery path.

Results

The possible paths for Li+ extraction and recovery using model layered cobalt oxide are illustrated in Fig. 1. Figure 1a shows the phase diagram of CoO2-LiCoO2-NaCoO2 with the phase equilibrium between Na0.48CoO2 and Li0.94CoO2 shown as the purple line. Regions out of equilibrium are shown in blue and red triangles as Li-rich and Na-rich regions for Li+ and Na+ dominated structures, respectively16. When the host composition is within either the blue or the red region, the host prefers to ion exchange with solution Na+ (blue) or Li+ (red), to reestablish the phase equilibrium. The composition can be electrochemically controlled by modulating the vacancy and therefore the phase volume of the two phases that coexist in the host material (Fig. 1b). Figure 1c shows different hypothetical paths of Li+ extraction and recovery considering the Faradaic Efficiency (FE) and purity of Li+. FE describes the amount of Li+ intercalated per e− added. Optimizing Li+ extraction selectivity requires pushing the host to as close as Li0.94CoO2 at the end of the intercalation to minimize the content of Na+ in the structure, which is predicted by the two-phase equilibrium between Na0.48CoO2 and Li0.94CoO2 (Fig. 1d). If a high depth of intercalation (1-v = 0.94) is not achieved, the purity of Li+ will be sacrificed. There are two extreme cases that need to be discussed in determining the extraction and recovery paths. In the first case, the FE is maximized. The extraction and recovery are cycled between Na0.48CoO2 and Li0.94CoO2, which are both the equilibrium phases of the host. For this path, the FE can reach ~200% (46% charge capacity for 0.94 Li+ composition), which is attractive from an energy perspective. In the second case, the Li+ purity is maximized. The extraction and recovery are cycled between Li0.48CoO2 and Li0.94CoO2. For this work, we prioritized Li+ purity with the first case for maximizing FE, remaining attractive for future directions. However, there is still a key question that remains unaddressed. As the end phase after recovery, Li0.48CoO2 could automatically convert to Na0.48CoO2 (thermodynamically stable phase) in the extraction condition (1:1000 Li: Na) by ion exchange. It is unknown when starting from Li0.48CoO2, how the host structure will transform in response to the concurrent and competitive intercalation and ion exchange; therefore, how to design the pathway of Li+ extraction to enable a reversible loop to Li0.94CoO2. In the following sections, we will introduce the critical roles of particle morphology and the relative kinetics between ion exchange and intercalation in enabling the closed-loop extraction and recovery of Li+.

The non-Faradaic ion exchange kinetics were tested in a 1:1000 (1 mM: 1 M) Li: Na acetonitrile (ACN) solution using a setup like our group’s previous work (Fig. 2d–f and Supplementary Fig. 2)8,9. ACN was chosen due to the fast ion exchange kinetics for NCO that is measurable by the change in the open circuit potential (OCP) curve (Supplementary Fig. 3). This OCP curve change resembles the charging curve of Na0.67CoO2 to Na0.48CoO216,22. For comparing the different materials at the same starting state, both LCO and sLCO were deintercalated in pure Li+ electrolyte to 33% vacancy to match the vacancy of NCO material and then soaked in 1 M NaClO4 ACN solution to reach two-phase equilibrium (Supplementary Fig. 4). For NCO particles, ion exchange occurred in 1: 1000 Li: Na solution (Supplementary Fig. 3). The compositions after ion exchange were Na0.32Li0.35CoO2 for LCO parent (Li0.94CoO2 and Na0.51CoO2 equilibrium), Na0.28Li0.39CoO2 for sLCO parent (Na0.48CoO2 and Li0.94CoO2 equilibrium), and Na0.28Li0.39CoO2 for NCO parent (Na0.48CoO2 and Li0.94CoO2 equilibrium), measured by XRD (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. 4) and inductively coupled mass spectroscopy (ICP-MS) confirming two-phase equilibrium. The biphasic equilibrium seems to consist of slight variations in the Na phase, which may be related to variation between electrode properties9,16.

Ion-exchanged LCO, sLCO, and NCO materials are then intercalated in the 1:1000 Li: Na solution to cutoff voltages of 0.1 V or −0.2 V vs Ag/Ag+ and given sufficient time for ion exchange. The ion exchange OCP curves reveal a substantial difference in the time to equilibrium. Na0.32Li0.35CoO2 (from LCO) (Fig. 2d) could not ion exchange to reach OCP of ~ 0.45 V (two-phase equilibrium) after intercalation to both voltage cutoffs of 0.1 and −0.2 V. The XRD spectra showed co-existence of a Li0.94CoO2 phase and a Na0.67CoO2 phase for the −0.2 V cutoff and a (001)Na phase peak between Na0.67CoO2 and Na0.48CoO2 for the 0.1 V cutoff indicating deviation from two-phase equilibrium (Supplementary Fig. 5). Both Na0.28Li0.39CoO2 (from sLCO) and Na0.28Li0.39CoO2 (from NCO) reached equilibrium with intercalation at a C/10 rate (27.4 mA/g for sLCO and 25.2 mA/g for NCO), which were shown by OCP curves and confirmed by ICP-MS and XRD (Fig. 2e–f and Supplementary Fig. 6–7). It is worth mentioning that the ion exchange process resembles pseudo Na1-vCoO2 charging, which can be enhanced to less than 2 h by using a more dilute slurry with about half the mass loading for electrodes (Supplementary Fig. 8)20,22. Furthermore, the ion exchange for NCO retains faster rates (time to reach the final plateau) if the Na1-vCoO2 phase retains a high vacancy level (Fig. 2h and Supplementary Table 2). The rate becomes much slower when v < 0.33 after intercalation, which highlights the importance of Na phase vacancy, therefore, the cutoff voltage, on the ion exchange kinetics. From the SEM images of the three particle types after intercalation to −0.2 V, the smaller sLCO and NCO particles show no cracking, unlike the larger LCO, which may be due to the increasing energy required for fracture with decreasing particle size (Supplementary Fig. 9)23. Additionally, scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) revealed cation disorder as a form of degradation for LCO (Supplementary Fig. 10). The smaller particle sizes can manage the larger stress from both the Li phase and Na phase separation in a single particle. The large mechanical stress in big particles could be a key hindrance to the ion exchange. Based on the ion exchange kinetics and particle fracture resilience, NCO particles were selected for further investigations, and a cutoff voltage of −0.2 V was selected to ensure fast ion exchange to maintain two-phase equilibrium and large intercalation capacity.

To reach as close as Li0.94CoO2, the electrochemical intercalation and ion exchange processes can be repeated for NCO particles over a voltage window and then allowed to relax finally back to Na0.48CoO2 (Fig. 2i). The electrochemical curve shape was maintained for all intercalation pulses with a decreasing intercalation capacity with each subsequent pulse (Supplementary Fig. 11). The XRD reveals an almost complete transformation of the original Na0.67CoO2 to Li0.94CoO2 with only a small residual (001)Na peak at 1.13 Å−1 from the Na0.48CoO2 phase (Fig. 2g). The final composition Na0.08Li0.86CoO2 contains an increased alkali ion content than expected (v = 0.13) based on the intercalated capacity. Based on the equilibrium between Li0.94CoO2 and Na0.48CoO2, the final Na+ content almost matches the expected 0.07 value; however, the Li+ content is higher than the expected 0.80 value. This suggests that the additional capacity may come from additional intercalation via side reaction or Li salt precipitation, but not carry over via non-specific adsorption, which would favor the higher concentration of Na+. The recovery of this excess Li+ will be discussed later.

Kinetic pathway for optimizing Li extraction from Na0.48CoO2 to Li0.94CoO2

Firstly, the ion pathway from thermodynamically stable end phase Na0.48CoO2 to Li0.94CoO2 in the two-phase equilibrium was investigated. It becomes clear that when the cutoff voltage is set for NCO particles, the capacity per intercalation is determined by C-rate, which affects the following ion exchange time, the final depth of intercalation, and Li+ selectivity. To understand the phase transformation during the repetitive intercalation and ion exchange, in situ synchrotron XRD was conducted during the initial ion exchange with OCP monitored. Starting material of Na0.67CoO2 was selected. A 1:100 1 M Li:Na solution was used to avoid Li transport issues in the in situ cell (Supplementary Fig 12). During the ion exchange (Fig. 3a), the (001)Na peak (1.14 Å−1) and (002)Na peak (2.29 Å−1) of the Na0.67CoO2 parent material are left shifting to lower Q values, indicating the formation of Na phase with larger layer spacings and vacancy. At OCP of −0.05 V, a small peak at 1.32 Å−1 emerges that continually right shifts and grows in intensity until reaching 1.34 Å−1. This indicates the formation of the Li0.94CoO2 phase and suggests that it is facilitated by the formation of the high vacancy Li phase. At ~0.06 V, the appearance of the (003)Li peak is also coupled with abrupt emergence and increase in intensity of other Li phase peaks and decrease of the Na phase peaks that indicates a major change in the lattice to a predominant R-3m phase (Li0.94CoO2 phase) from the C2/m phase (Na0.48CoO2–Na0.67CoO2 phase) despite only a small amount of Li+ present in the material. The (001)Na and (002)Na peaks continue to left shift until reaching the position of Na0.48CoO2 at 0.5 V. During the ion exchange process, the (001)Na peak intensity continues to diminish while the (003)Li peak intensity grows. At 0.5 V, the two-phase equilibrium between Li0.94CoO2 and Na0.48CoO2 was established, resulting in Na0.28Li0.39CoO2. A following intercalation in the same solution was characterized at C/10. The electrochemical response resembles the Na1-vCoO2 discharging (Na+ intercalation) behavior. The (001)Na and (002)Na peaks right shift while the Li phase peaks maintain their position. Additionally, the intensity of the Li phase continues to grow while that of the Na phase decreases. This suggests that during the Na phase transformation (intercalation), ion exchange with solution Li+ occurs, resulting in an increase in the volume fraction of the Li phase. Due to the lack of sites for intercalation into the Li0.94CoO2 phase, as well as the insulating nature, the Li phase is kinetically unfavorable for intercalation. This results in direct Na intercalation into the Na0.48CoO2 phase that results in its phase transformation (Supplementary Fig. 14). Based on the above phase transformation, the kinetics-dependent ion pathways from Na0.48CoO2 to Li0.94CoO2 can be clearly drawn on the CoO2-LiCoO2-NaCoO2 phase diagram. As shown in Fig. 3b red line i, when starting from any point on the Na0.48CoO2-Li0.94CoO2 equilibrium line (such as from the above example Na0.28Li0.39CoO2 or Na0.48CoO2 as the extreme case), if the intercalation C-rate is fast, the line showing the intercalation path quickly intersects with the Na0.67CoO2-Li0.94CoO2 co-existence line with limited cation content increase. In this case, Na+ intercalation dominates, and it takes a long time for ion exchange with solution Li+ to bring the structure back to the equilibrium line (horizontal path line). Also, it requires many cycles of intercalation and ion exchange to push the host to Li0.94CoO2. On the other hand, when the intercalation C-rate is slower (red line ii), the intercalation line tilts to the right towards Li+ intercalation and intersects with the Na0.67CoO2-Li0.94CoO2 co-existence line at a lower vacancy level. Therefore, more Li+ is extracted via concurrent ion exchange during the intercalation. Less ion exchange events are needed after the voltage cutoff to bring the structure back to the equilibrium line, and less cycles of intercalation and ion exchange are needed. This trend points to the possibility that when the C-rate is even slower, the intercalation line could overlap with the Na0.48CoO2-Li0.94CoO2 equilibrium line (purple line) and no ion exchange will be needed after intercalation. As experimental evidence, with Na0.28Li0.39CoO2 starting material at a C/50 rate (5.04 mA/g), a large, sloped plateau suggests a competitive ion exchange with the direct Na intercalation that would occur when intercalating from Na0.48CoO2 and Li0.94CoO2 along the equilibrium line path (Supplementary Fig. 15, purple line on Fig. 3b).

Kinetic pathway for optimizing Li extraction from Li0.48CoO2 to Li0.94CoO2

To prioritize Li+ purity in recovery, ideally. Li0.94CoO2 will be delithiated to Li0.48CoO2 in a pure Li+ solution. The delithiation showed Li1-vCoO2 charging type curve (Supplementary Fig. 16)24. With no doubt, the C-rate of the following extraction is still critical in determining the ion pathway from Li0.48CoO2 to Li0.94CoO2. We found that the intercalation behavior differs significantly at different rates, and it correlates with the relative rates of intercalation and ion exchange. Therefore, to be quantitative, we define CpseudoIX to characterize the average rate of the initial ion exchange starting from NCO so that CpseudoIX = (yinitial – yfinal)/\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau$$\end{document}τ, where yinitial and yfinal is the Na+ content in NayCoO2 phases before and after ion exchange, respectively (Supplementary Table 2). The yfinal is determined by comparing the plateau voltage to the Na1-vCoO2 charging curve16. The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau$$\end{document}τ is the time of ion exchange between yinitial and yfinal (Supplementary Fig. 8). The kinetics of ion exchange is affected by many factors, such as particle morphology, electrolyte transport, and especially particle packing and electrode porosity. A detailed investigation on these effects is beyond the scope of this work; however, the use of CpseudoIX offers a simplified metric to compare the ion exchange kinetics with intercalation kinetics, the applied C-rate (Cinter).

Three regimes with different reaction rates are defined: Regime 1, CpseudoIX > Cinter; Regime 2, CpseudoIX~Cinter; Regime 3 CpseudoIX < Cinter (Fig. 3c). Regime 1 is defined as CpseudoIX > Cinter (Cinter is at least 3.5 times slower than CpseudoIX), and its key features are the largest intercalation capacity (1-v changes from 0.50 to 0.92), highest intercalation potential, and no Na+ intercalation feature on the electrochemical curve. Regime 2 is defined CpseudoIX~Cinter, and its key features are a smaller intercalation capacity than Regime 1, a more sloped plateau that occurs at a lower potential than Regime 1, which could describe the same reaction. For Regime 2, there is a clear transition to Na+ intercalation behavior below 0.3 V, which could be due to larger Na phase domains in Regime 2 than in Regime 1. Finally, Regime 3 is defined as CpseudoIX <Cinter (Cinter is at least 3.5 times faster than CpseudoIX), and its key features are a stretched Na+ intercalation curve and the smallest capacity, which suggests the Na+ intercalation dominates. These regimes were found to be general not only to the NCO-based particles but also for other synthesized LiCoO2 over a range of sizes (Supplementary Figs. 17–20). It is interesting to see that in Regime 1, a single step of intercalation can achieve the targeted depth of intercalation (v ~ 0.06) with minimal ion exchange needed, which is confirmed by ICP-MS. The Na+ content only decreases from 0.07 to 0.04 from −0.2 V to 0.1 V in OCP during ion exchange. To explain the significant differences among the three regimes, the reaction for the long plateau in Regime 1 needs to be identified, and the reaction pathways for all regimes need to be unveiled.

The phase evolution of the intercalation process in Regime 2 was characterized using in situ synchrotron XRD (Fig. 3d) in the interest of time, since it has a similar plateau to that in Regime 1. The sample was prepared by a 1:1000 solution at C/50 (5.04 mA/g) intercalation outside the flow cell, followed by deintercalation in Li+ solution inside the flow cell (Supplementary Fig. 21). The in situ intercalation was carried out after switching the solution to 1:100 Li: Na at C/10 (25.2 mA/g) starting from Li1-vCoO2. The regimes were found to exist in 1: 100 Li: Na molar ratio solutions, which also exhibit the two-phase equilibrium behavior (Supplementary Figs. 22 and 23)16. The electrochemical curve was confirmed to resemble the Regime 2 extraction process. The increased potential of the plateau could be due to higher shift in potential with 1:100 solution compared to 1:1000 solution (Supplementary Fig. 22). At the beginning of intercalation on the plateau at ~0.60 V, peaks of Li phase right shifted, and no Na phase was observed (Fig. 3d). As the (003)Li peak shifts to 1.32 Å−1 corresponding to the approximately the Li0.66CoO2 phase, Na0.48CoO2 phase emerged as indicated by the (001)Na peak (Supplementary Fig. 24). The emergence of Na phase could be due to either Na+ intercalation or concurrent ion exchange. The Na0.48CoO2 peak becomes slightly more intense until the Li phase reaches Li0.94CoO2. Once the Li phase finally transforms into the Li0.94CoO2 phase at ~0.42 V, the electrochemical curve transitions to a Na1-vCoO2 discharging type, and the (001)Na peak begins to right shift until the potential cutoff (Supplementary Fig. 24). The Na phase peak intensity is greater for high C-rate intercalation, which promotes larger Na phases than low C-rate intercalation. (Supplementary Figs. 25 and 26). The following second deintercalation showed the same behavior as the first deintercalation (Supplementary Fig. 27). The in situ XRD provides critical information that the plateau ~0.55 V (~0.40 V in 1:1000 solution, Supplementary Fig. 22) corresponds to Li+ intercalation-like behavior. With only a slight ~50 mV difference between the two plateaus in Regime 1 and 2, the long plateau in Regime 1 could also correspond to Li+ like intercalation. The existence of a large fraction of Li phase and Li+ dominated intercalation is not intuitive, since the thermodynamically stable phase at high vacancy is Na0.48CoO2, and the electrode was in contact with Na-rich solution for a long time.

To confirm this and resolve the ion pathway of Regime 1 in a 1:1000 solution, electrode composition from starting Li0.50CoO2 was characterized in situ by measuring the change of solution ion concentration for Regime 1 intercalation. The electrode composition changed within the first minute of intercalation, where the Na+ composition increased from 0.02 to 0.11 and reached its maximum of 0.26 by the first change in the intercalation curve slope at ~0.60 V (Supplementary Fig. 28). A second electrode revealed the same trend but with less ion exchange. The variability in ion exchange may be due to differences in contact time without current (intercalation start time). This composition change provides important information about the ion pathway. During the first minute, there is barely any increase in the total cation content in the host material, so that the Na+ content increase should come from ion exchange. Based on our previous work, ion exchange prefers to establish equal potential phase equilibrium, therefore, for charge conservation, the vacancy level of the Li phase will decrease, and a high vacancy Na phase (v > 0.52) will be preferred to form16. This matches the fact that the Na+ content kept increasing during intercalation from 0.8 to 0.6 V. This first segment of the electrochemical curve resembles the transformation of Na0.37CoO2 to Na0.48CoO216. The Na+ composition decreased after passing 0.60 V and was 0.15 (first attempt) and 0.05 (second attempt) by the time the major plateau at 0.50 V was reached.

The evidence of the decrease of Na+ content is critical for resolving the Regime 1 pathway (Supplementary Fig. 28). This indicates that at high vacancy level (v ~ 0.52), even though Na0.48CoO2 is the preferred phase based on the two-phase equilibrium, it is suppressed during intercalation in Regime 1 (Supplementary Fig. 29). Moreover, as the high vacancy Na phase (formed via ion exchange before intercalation started) was transformed to Na0.48CoO2 (0.8–0.6 V), its corresponding equal potential Li0.94CoO2 phase should prefer to form. If so, ion exchange of solution Li+ with Na phase could occur and result in the decrease of Na+ content as the Li0.94CoO2 phase is formed16. Since the high vacancy Li phase still exists in the same particle, the high Li+ content phase (Li0.94CoO2) could disappear via merging with the existing Li phase. Therefore, the long plateau could be an equivalent Li+ intercalation plateau (resembling Li0.75CoO2 and Li0.94CoO2 two-phase equilibrium) with complicated Li+ and Na+ interactions involving high vacancy Na phase formation, transition to Na0.48CoO2, and Li0.94CoO2 phase formation and merging with the existing Li phase. Finally, this Regime 1 ion pathway can be achieved since the high vacancy Li phase has available sites for Li+ intercalation, and adequate time is given for Li+ diffusion both from the solution and in the solid (Supplementary Fig. 14)25–27. With the commercial and synthesized Li0.67CoO2 without Na ion exchange before intercalation, the pure Li+ like intercalation behaviors could also be observed (Supplementary Figs. 30 and 31).

Therefore, the differences in the intercalation behavior in Regime 1, 2, and 3 are due to the limit of Li+ mass transfer in solution and solid diffusion. For Regime 3, large quantities of Na+ will be inserted into the host structure due to both ion exchange and electrochemical intercalation, which squeeze the Li phase and force the quick formation of Li0.94CoO2 and Na0.48CoO2 phase equilibrium which end up on the equilibrium line (Supplementary Fig. 32). After that, the process will be similar to that from Na0.48CoO2 to Li0.94CoO2 as described above (a transition to red path i or ii lines on the phase diagram). The intercalation curve looks like Na+ discharge, and ion exchange is needed after each intercalation step to drive the host back to two-phase equilibrium. The ion pathway of Regime 2 is the combination of Regime 1 and Regime 3, where at the beginning, during the plateau of ~0.45 V, Li+ like intercalation is dominant. From ~ 0.3 V, a two-phase equilibrium was established, and the host switched to Na+ intercalation and requires ion exchange to increase the Li+ content in the structure. This switch in intercalation behavior could be caused by solid diffusion limitation, which means ion exchange cannot catch up with intercalation as the vacancy level decreases. Additionally, the higher currents in Regime 2 than in Regime 1 promote more Na phase growth during the beginning of the intercalation process (Supplementary Fig. 33). The three types of ion pathways can also be clearly explained on the CoO2-LiCoO2-NaCoO2 phase diagram, as shown using the blue lines (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 14).

The Li and Na phase element distribution was characterized on a single particle level for Regimes 1 and 3 (Supplementary Figs. 34–37). For Regime 3, the particle selected was intercalated to (NaLi)0.80CoO2. The electrode was pulled out of the solution immediately to limit ion exchange. The STEM images with electron energy-loss spectroscopy (EELS) mapping of the Li K edge reveal nonuniformity for Regime 3 (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 35). Furthermore, the energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) signal was used to quantify the Na+ content in each of the particles, which confirms the large particle-to-particle heterogeneity again. Phase separation heterogeneity has been well characterized for insertion materials from the single particle level to the electrode level28–33. The effects of particle characteristics and processing conditions can affect the phase separation morphology and ability of phase domains to coarsen during intercalation34–36. For Regime 3, the heterogeneity could be caused by the fast intercalation rate. The sample selected in Regime 1 was in the middle of the plateau at v = 0.3 (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Fig. 36). Based on our assignment of the ion pathway in Regime 1, the particle should have a minimal level of Na+ content with uniform Li+ distribution. Based on the EELS and EDS mapping, this is true for some of the particles, but not for all. There is still a large heterogeneity for the Na+ content and elemental distribution. But this could be caused by the ion exchange after the current stops, as mentioned in the ex situ XRD characterization, which cannot be avoided for ex situ samples.

Demonstration of reversible Li extraction and recovery

For Li+ extraction, NCO was selected as the parent material due to its high capacity in Regime 1 and the ability to predict the Regime 1 transition using the CpseudoIX value from the pristine material (Supplementary Fig. 18). Operating in Regime 1 (Cinter < CpseudoIX) offers the highest capacity in a single intercalation pass and requires minimal ion exchange (Fig. 4) The Li+ extraction results for Regime 2 (Cinter~CpseudoIX) and Regime 3 (Cinter > CpseudoIX) are summarized in Supplementary Figs. 38–41. The main findings are that Regime 3 requires multiple pulses to increase the capacity and approach high Li+ selectivity; both Regime 2 and Regime 3 require ion exchange after intercalation, which begins to degrade the CpseudoIX rate with each extraction sequence. The effect of long term solvent exposure on degradation will need to be explored in future work (Supplementary Fig. 42). Even though operating at Regimes 2 and 3 can lead to reasonably good depth of intercalation and they have faster Cinter rates (Regime 3), the total time to the final depth of intercalation to ensure a good Li+ selectivity is longer than Regime 1 due to their increasingly long ion exchange times after each extraction sequence. Therefore, Regime 1 is selected as a better condition for Li+ extraction. Moreover, it is worth mentioning that autointeraction was observed for the Li+ extraction process, which results in increased Li+ content than predicted by the electrochemical capacity (FE > 100%). A spontaneous side reaction involving the (LiNa)1-vCoO2 was observed, which allows for the reduction of the Co4+ that promotes Li+ intercalation (Supplementary Figs. 43–46). In one case, Li0.48CoO2, formed via delithiation in 1 M NaClO4 solution, can transform into Li0.90Na0.05CoO2 without any use of electricity over 90 hours. The reactivity of the ion-exchanged sample could be related to the high strain at the interface, as the solution should be stable in this voltage window37–39. Additionally, this excess Li+ can be reversibly recovered during the recovery. The electrochemical behavior is asymmetric in that the deintercalation in the pure Li+ recovery solution does not involve an ion exchange process. By operating in Regime 1, the vacancy can approach 0.06 so that most of the capacity is used for Li+ extraction (Fig. 4a). By using the extended deintercalation to a voltage cutoff, additional Li+ is recovered from the Li+ intercalated from the autointercalation reaction and not the applied C-rate. The OCP curve quickly changes to a positive potential after intercalation and does not become more sluggish with additional Li+ recovery sequences since minimal to no ion exchange is needed (Fig. 4b). The Na+ composition does not change anymore after the first extraction sequence, and less than 5% available sites hold Na+ in the structure. Therefore, we can achieve 99% Li+ purity on the 3rd extraction sequence with FE of 120% during intercalation (Fig. 4c). The extraction sequence has increasing purity after each extraction sequence, approaching levels appropriate for battery grade precursors (Supplementary Tables 3 and 4). A Li+ recovery with 99% purity is achieved while using ~40% of the theoretical capacity of the (NaLi)1-vCoO2 material without large variability in the post intercalation equilibrium kinetics or large loss of capacity between extraction sequences. The calculated specific energy consumption of the loop for Regime 1 is 4.6 kWh per kg Li recovered, similar to other values in the literature (Fig. 4a)40. Additional energy savings can be achieved with optimized system design.

Discussion

We revealed the complicated and intriguing Li+ and Na+ co-intercalation and ion exchange behavior in layered oxides. The two-phase equilibrium between Li0.94CoO2 and Na0.48CoO2 can be harnessed to guide the understanding of the ion intercalation pathways. We unveiled that the relative kinetics of intercalation and ion exchange is crucial to determine the Li+ extraction capacity, selectivity, and reversibility. We define an average ion exchange rate CpseudoIX to quantitatively compare the kinetics and divide the different intercalation behaviors into three regimes with CpseudoIX > Cinter (Regime 1), CpseudoIX~Cinter (Regime 2), and CpseudoIX < Cinter (Regime 3). The ion pathways for different regimes are constructed and illustrated clearly using the phase diagram of CoO2-LiCoO2-NaCoO2 by correlating the electrochemical response with in situ XRD and composition data. The CpseudoIX can be enhanced by optimizing the particle morphology and electrode porosity. In Regime 1, when CpseudoIX > Cinter, high vacancy Li phases are maintained, and a great portion of the ion pathway can be maintained for direct Li+ intercalation to achieve the large intercalation capacity and high Li+ selectivity without the assistance of ion exchange. By operating in Regime 1, the layered cobalt oxide demonstrated a 9.7 × 104 Li+ selectivity and produced a recovery solution with 99% Li+ purity using 115 mAh/g specific capacity per recovery sequence and 4.6 kWh per kg Li recovered per cycle with good reversibility. Its specific capacity is comparable to many other sodium layered oxides used for Na battery; further particle and electrode optimization could allow improved rate performance and stability as well (Supplementary Table 5). Interestingly, (LiNa)1-vCoO2 can benefit from an autointercalation phenomenon that favors Li+ over Na+ to enable a Faradaic efficiency greater than 100%.

Our work provides a guide for the design of Li extraction and recovery using layered cobalt oxide as a model material. We showcase that the understanding of materials response in complex solution environments is critical to define the working conditions and material challenges, as well as identify solutions for Li+ extraction and recovery. For the future deployment, Co-free electrode materials are necessary due to cost and supply chain instability41. The Li+/ Na+ ion exchange behavior has been found to exist in other Mn-rich layered positive electrodes, which will likely demonstrate high Li+ selectivity16. The use of organic extraction solution has potential applications in the processing of electronic waste recovery42,43, a hybrid electrochemical cell with organic electrolyte separated from Li ion-containing aqueous source by membrane44, and extraction from organic electrolyte used for solvent extraction from aqueous source as well45,46. Another key aspect for the deployment is the lifetime. Future investigations into the degradation mechanism of the electrode materials are needed, as well as a paired strategy to extend the lifetime. Overall, our results show promise to enable a large class of layered oxides for Li+ extraction to solve the lithium supply chain concerns.

Methods

Sample preparation

LiCoO2 (LCO) was purchased from MTI corporation. The smaller LiCoO2 (sLCO) was synthesized by a standard solid-state method47. Li2CO3 and Co3O4 (Alfa, 99.7%) were mixed in a stoichiometric ratio of Li:Co = 1:1 with 5% weight excess Li2CO3 in a SPEX 8000 M Mixer, a higher energy ball mill with a steel container and balls for 30 min. The mass ratio of materials to beads is 1:37. The product was ground again and sintered at 900 °C for 10 h. Na0.67CoO2 was synthesized by a standard solid-state method20. Na2O2 (Alfa, 95%) and Co3O4 (Alfa, 99.7%) were transferred in a stoichiometric ratio of Na:Co = 0.68:1 with 5% weight excess Na2O2 into a stainless-steel container with balls while in an Ar glovebox. The powders were mixed for 30 min in the high-energy ball mill with the same mass ratio of materials to beads. The powder was formed into pellets in the Ar glovebox and then sintered in air at 535 °C for 16 h. The pellet was moved back into the glovebox after cooling down to 300 °C. Electrodes were prepared by casting a slurry of active material, Super P carbon black (MTI), and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF, MTI) with a mass ratio of 8:1:1 in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (Sigma-Aldrich). The slurry is made with a 1:5 weight ratio of active material: NMP. The electrode slurry was drop cast on a ~ 0.8 cm2 geometrical surface of a 1 ×5 cm2 rectangular carbon cloth (ELAT-H, Fuel Cell Etc, 406 µm in thickness, 80% porosity) current collector and dried on a hotplate at 100 °C overnight. The mass loading of active material ranged from 0.8–3.0 mg/cm2 per electrode. The differences between the dense NCO and dilute NCO were the drop casting method. Dense NCO electrodes were prepared by drop casting the slurry using a 1 mL pipette (Thermo Scientific Finntip Filtered Pipette Tips), and the dilute NCO electrodes were prepared by drop casting the slurry using a 3 mL pipette (Heathrow Scientific Transfer Pipette).

The additional synthesis for LiCoO2 materials listed in Supplementary Fig. 17a–f was synthesized according to the literature. LCO(a) was prepared using a molten salt synthesis of KCl with CoO: LiOH·H2O:KCl mol ratio 1:1:4 at 850 °C for 8 h48; LCO(b) was prepared by mixing Co oxalate precursor with 5% weight excess LiOH·H2O in Agate mortar, followed by calcination at 500 °C and 900 °C. The Co oxalate precursor was prepared by the precipitation method by slowly adding a stoichiometric equivalent of H2C2O4·2H2O in ethanol to 0.2 M Co(CH3COO)2 in ethanol. The recovered Co oxalate precursor was centrifuged and washed three times in water and twice in ethanol49. LCO(c) was prepared using a molten salt synthesis of KCl with CoO: LiOH·H2O:KCl mol ratio 1:1:1 mol ratio 1:1 at 850 °C for 8 h48; LCO(d) was prepared by mixing Li2CoO3 and Co3O4 (Alfa, 99.7%) in Agate mortar followed by sintering at 1000 °C and 900 °C47; LCO(e) was prepared using a molten salt synthesis of KNO3 with CoO:LiOH·H2O:KNO3 mol ratio 1:1:3 at 800 °C for 8 h50; and LCO(f) was prepared by high energy ball milling LCO (MTI) for 1 h in steel container with steel balls (1:37 materials: bead mass ratio) followed by 10 h sintering at 550 °C. A heating rate of 5 °C/min was used for all sintering.

Counter electrodes were prepared by dipping carbon felt (Alfa Aesar) disks (0.9525 cm diameter × 3.18 mm thickness, around 240 g/m2 in areal weight) in a slurry of 8:1:1 LiFePO4 (MTI):Super P: PVDF in NMP solvent and drying at 100 °C overnight, similar to previous work9. The mass loading of LiFePO4 was about 110 mg/cm2 per electrode. For layered oxide intercalation experiments, the LiFePO4 was used as a counter to maintain a close to 1:1000 1 M Li:Na ratio in the solution. For layered oxide deintercalation experiments, the counter was first prepared by the deintercalation of LiFePO4 in 1 M LiCl (aq) solution at C/5 (32 mA/g based on 160 mAh/g theoretical specific capacity) to 0.4 V vs Ag/AgCl to make FePO4.

Electrochemistry

Electrochemical experiments were performed in a three-neck round-bottom flask using a Biologic VMP-3 potentiostat. The batch cell is a three-neck round-bottom flask containing a working electrode, reference electrode, and counter electrode. The steps of extraction and recovery are shown in Supplementary Fig. 2. The working electrodes were the active material on carbon cloth held by a Pt clamp, the counter electrode was the LiFePO4/FePO4 electrodes on carbon felt held by Ti wire, and the reference electrode was a commercial non-aqueous Ag/Ag+ reference electrode (CH Instrument Inc.) consisting of silver nitrate and tetrabutylammonium perchlorate in acetonitrile. N2 was bubbled into the flask to displace dissolved O2 and H2O. The open circuit potential and intercalation experiments were done in 80 mL 1 mM LiClO4 and 1 M NaClO4 acetonitrile solutions with a stir bar mixing the solution. The salts were stored in an Ar-filled glovebox. The deintercalation experiments were done in 40 mL of 0.1 M LiClO4 acetonitrile solution. LCO and sLCO soaking experiments were done in 40 mL of 1 M NaClO4 acetonitrile solution after deintercalation in 0.1 M LiClO4 acetonitrile solution. The electrodes were rinsed three times in pure acetonitrile when switching solutions to remove residual Na and Li salts. C-rates and expected changes in the capacity after (de)intercalation were defined based on theoretical specific capacities of 252.028 mAh/g for Na0.67CoO2 materials and 273.838 mAh/g for LiCoO2.

X-ray diffraction

Synchrotron XRD measurements were conducted at the 13-BM-C (X-ray wavelength 0.4335 Å) and 33-BM-C (X-ray wavelength 0.6199 Å) beamlines of the Advanced Photon Source (APS) at Argonne National Laboratory and 11-3 (X-ray wavelength 0.9763 Å) beamline of the Stanford Synchrotron Radiation Lightsource (SLAC)51. In situ experiments were performed by the transmission of a monochromatic X-ray incident beam through a custom-designed electrochemical flow cell (Supplementary Fig. 12). The cell was identical to that used by Zhang et al.52. The sample cell frame and all components were made of corrosion-resistant polymer Kel-F. The carbon cloth electrode was placed within the central position of the cell between two sealed Kapton windows. The cell body can hold roughly 5 mL solution around the center to make sure the electrode is always in contact with the solution. This solution was continuously refreshed by a peristaltic pump operating at 10 RPM. The reference electrode was the same commercial, non-aqueous Ag/Ag+ described above, and the counter electrode was Pt wire. The electrochemical measurements were performed with a Biologic SP-50 potentiostat. All synchrotron XRD measurements were performed at 13-BM-C (APS) except for Supplementary Figs. 25 and 26, which were performed at 33-BM-C (APS) and Supplementary Fig. 29 at 11-3 (SLAC) using the same setup. Ex situ samples were covered in Kapton film and transported in a vacuum-sealed container to protect samples from ambient conditions. Pristine powder samples (Supplementary Fig. 1 and 17) were collected by using a Rigaku MiniFlex 600 with a Cu Kα source. The tube voltage and the current used were 40 kV and 15 mA, and the step size was 0.02° step width and a 10°/min speed. Carbon cloth electrode samples measured using in-house XRD were performed with a Rigaku SmartLab multipurpose diffractometer, using Cu Kα radiation. The tube voltage and the current used were 40 kV and 40 mA, and the step size was 0.02° step width and a 3°/min speed.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Scanning electron micrographs were obtained on a Zeiss Merlin scanning electron microscope using a 20 kV accelerating voltage and 300 pA. Particle sizes were determined by measuring diameter and thickness using imageJ53. At least 10 particles were used for each average and standard deviation.

Scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM)

STEM images were acquired using JEOL ARM 200 F equipped with a cold field emission source at 200 kV. The HAADF detector angle was 90–270 mrad and the LAADF detector angle ranged between 30 and 120 mrad. EELS spectra were acquired using a Gatan GIF Continuum ER with a dwell time of 0.03 s per pixel. The Li K signal was assigned between 60.3 and 62.8 eV for mapping. EDS spectra imaging was acquired using an Oxford X-Max 100TLE windowless SDD detector with a dwell time of 6.14 ms.
