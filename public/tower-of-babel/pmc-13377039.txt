A decompose-reshape-ensemble deep learning framework for multi-scale short-term photovoltaic power forecasting
Authors: Fang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate short-term photovoltaic (PV) power forecasting is critical for maintaining grid stability and enabling efficient dispatch in modern sustainable power systems, yet the inherent volatility and multi-scale temporal dynamics of PV output remain challenging for existing methods. This paper proposes IFTMC, a novel hybrid framework that synergistically integrates improved complete ensemble empirical mode decomposition with adaptive noise (ICEEMDAN), fuzzy C-means (FCM) soft clustering, and dual-backbone deep learning networks–TimesNet and Mamba–coupled through a bidirectional cross-attention fusion mechanism. Specifically, ICEEMDAN decomposes the raw PV power series into intrinsic mode functions of distinct frequency scales, which are subsequently regrouped via FCM clustering to reduce modelling complexity while preserving inter-band transitional information. TimesNet and Mamba then operate as complementary feature extractors, capturing multi-periodic local patterns and long-range temporal dependencies, respectively, with cross-attention adaptively allocating their contributions according to input conditions. Extensive experiments on the DKASC benchmark dataset demonstrate that IFTMC achieves state-of-the-art performance, reducing mean absolute error (MAE) by 4.2%–4.8% relative to the strongest baseline across 2–3-hour prediction horizons. Comprehensive ablation studies, case analyses under diverse weather conditions, and attention weight visualizations further confirm the effectiveness, robustness, and interpretability of each component.

Introduction

The global imperative to mitigate climate change and achieve carbon neutrality has positioned the transition to renewable energy at the forefront of sustainable development agendas worldwide. Among renewable sources, solar photovoltaic (PV) power generation has emerged as a cornerstone technology, with cumulative installed capacity exceeding 1.5 TW by 2023 and continuing to grow at an unprecedented rate1. This expansion directly contributes to the United Nations’ sustainable development Goal 7 (SDG 7) on affordable and clean energy, while supporting SDG 13 on climate action by displacing fossil fuel-based generation. However, the inherent volatility and intermittency of PV power output pose significant challenges to grid stability and dispatch operations2. From a sustainability perspective, forecast errors require maintaining fossil fuel-based spinning reserves, undermining the carbon-reduction benefits of solar deployment, while inaccurate predictions can lead to renewable energy curtailment during supply–demand mismatches3. These challenges underscore that accurate short-term PV power forecasting is not merely a technical optimization problem but a critical enabler of sustainable energy systems, motivating extensive research into more effective forecasting methodologies.

Early research on PV power forecasting mainly relied on physical models and statistical methods. Physical models establish mathematical mappings from solar irradiance to power output by combining numerical weather prediction (NWP) data with atmospheric radiation transfer and PV module characteristic models4. While offering clear mechanisms and strong interpretability, their accuracy is highly dependent on NWP quality, with errors prone to propagation and amplification. Statistical methods, including persistence models, autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA), and exponential smoothing, mine temporal patterns from historical data. These approaches are computationally efficient but rely on linear and stationarity assumptions, limiting their capability to capture nonlinear dynamics under complex weather scenarios5.

With the advancement of machine learning, methods such as support vector machine (SVM), random forest (RF), and gradient boosting trees have been widely applied to PV forecasting. VanDeventer et al.6 proposed a genetic algorithm-SVM (GA-SVM) hybrid model that optimizes hyperparameters via GA. Jogunuri et al.7 demonstrated that RF exhibits advantages in handling seasonal data for multi-step forecasting. However, conventional machine learning methods typically treat samples as independent data points, lacking explicit modeling of temporal dependencies, which motivates the adoption of deep learning methods with sequence modeling capabilities.

In recent years, deep learning methods have gradually become the research mainstream for PV power forecasting due to their end-to-end feature learning and powerful nonlinear representation capabilities. Recurrent neural networks (RNN) and their variants LSTM and gated recurrent unit (GRU) effectively capture long and short-term temporal dependencies through gating structures9. Convolutional neural networks (CNN) excel at extracting multi-scale features from local time windows10. Wang et al.11 combined CNN with LSTM to construct a hybrid model that extracts spatial features through CNN and captures temporal dependencies through LSTM, significantly improving PV power forecasting accuracy. Zhu et al.8 developed an LSTM-XGBoost hybrid model that combines LSTM’s sequential modeling capability with XGBoost’s ensemble learning, achieving improved prediction stability through deep learning-based feature extraction.

To address the limitations of single models in characterizing the strong nonlinearity and non-stationarity of PV power, researchers have proposed combined forecasting strategies centered on the “decomposition–prediction–reconstruction” framework. This framework first uses signal decomposition methods to decompose the original power sequence into sub-sequences of different frequency bands, then models and predicts them separately, and finally reconstructs to obtain the final result. The underlying rationale is that decomposition separates complex multi-scale dynamics into simpler sub-components, each exhibiting stronger stationarity, thereby reducing the modeling difficulty for individual predictors and effectively alleviating non-stationarity and noise interference. Signal decomposition methods have evolved from empirical mode decomposition (EMD)12 to ensemble EMD (EEMD)13, complete EEMD with adaptive noise (CEEMDAN)14, and improved CEEMDAN (ICEEMDAN)15. Zhou et al.16 constructed the CEEMDAN-RIME-AM-TCN-BiLSTM model, combining intelligent optimization algorithms to improve ultra-short-term prediction accuracy. The ICEEMDAN-TCN-BiLSTM-MHA model proposed by Li et al.17 reduced MAPE by approximately 58%–79% compared to single BiLSTM under typical sunny, cloudy, and rainy scenarios, fully demonstrating the application value of combined models. A closer examination of the limitations listed in Table 1 reveals three recurring categories of drawbacks that systematically affect the forecasting process and its downstream applications. (i) Architectural complexity and computational burden: Most combined models (e.g., CEEMDAN-WT-VMD-BiTCN-BiGRU-Att, CEEMDAN-RIME-AM-TCN-BiLSTM) involve cascaded multi-stage pipelines with numerous modules, leading to long training times, difficult hyperparameter tuning, and high computational demands. This limits their practical deployment in real-time dispatch systems where inference latency is critical. (ii) Limited generalization and single-site validation: Several methods (e.g., CEEMDAN-DispEn-LSTM, ICEEMDAN-HFNN) are validated on only a single PV site, making it difficult to assess their robustness across diverse climatic conditions. The sensitivity to hyperparameters further exacerbates this issue, as settings optimized for one site may not transfer effectively. (iii) Insufficient interpretability: Complex deep network architectures with many parameters (e.g., ICEEMDAN-TCN-BiLSTM-MHA) often function as black boxes, providing limited insight into how different components contribute to the final forecast. This lack of transparency hinders trust and adoption by grid operators. These recurring limitations motivate our design choices: IFTMC addresses complexity through FCM-based dimensionality reduction, enhances interpretability via attention weight visualization, and employs CDO for automated hyperparameter optimization to reduce manual tuning effort.

Although combined models have achieved significant progress in PV power forecasting, existing methods still have the following limitations. First, regarding sub-sequence processing, while signal decomposition can reduce modeling complexity by splitting sequences into different frequency components24, it introduces issues such as mode mixing and noise misallocation. Moreover, existing studies predominantly use K-means clustering for component merging25, which requires pre-specifying cluster numbers and has rigid boundaries that cannot characterize fuzzy transitions between sub-sequences. Although a few studies have explored Fuzzy C-Means (FCM) for sub-sequence grouping in wind power forecasting 46, its application in PV power decomposition frameworks remains largely unexplored. Second, regarding result fusion, existing methods predominantly adopt simple weighted averaging or static linear fusion strategies, failing to reflect the dynamic contribution changes of sub-models under varying conditions26. Recently, cross-attention mechanisms have been introduced for multimodal PV forecasting 47, but their use for fusing complementary temporal backbone representations within a decomposition framework has not been investigated. Third, regarding predictor selection, most existing combined models employ traditional networks such as LSTM, BiLSTM, and TCN, which have limited capability in capturing complex multi-period patterns and long-range dependencies. Recent advances like TimesNet and Mamba27 have demonstrated superior performance in time series forecasting, and Mamba has been preliminarily applied to wind power prediction 45, but these architectures remain underexplored in PV power prediction frameworks.

To address the aforementioned challenges, this paper proposes a novel multi-scale decomposition-reconstruction and ensemble deep learning framework for short-term PV power forecasting. Methods

Overall framework

The overall architecture of the proposed IFTMC framework is illustrated in Fig. 1. The framework follows a “decomposition–clustering–dual-backbone extraction–fusion” paradigm, designed to address the multi-scale temporal dynamics and non-stationarity inherent in PV power series.

Given raw PV power and meteorological data, the framework proceeds in four steps. First, data preprocessing is applied, including temporal alignment, outlier removal, K-nearest neighbor (KNN)-based missing value imputation, and a two-stage feature selection procedure that combines pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) pre-screening with SHAP-based selection. Second, the preprocessed power series is decomposed using ICEEMDAN into intrinsic mode functions (IMFs) that represent distinct frequency components.

Third, to reduce the cost of modeling each IMF separately, FCM clustering groups IMFs with similar dynamics into K cluster-level subsequences via soft membership assignment. Fourth, each cluster-level subsequence is processed by a dual-backbone feature extraction component, in which TimesNet and Mamba operate in parallel. TimesNet captures multi-periodic local patterns by reshaping 1D sequences into 2D tensors based on detected dominant periods, whereas Mamba models long-range dependencies through selective state space mechanisms with linear computational complexity.

The representations from both backbones are fused using bidirectional cross-attention, which assigns input-dependent weights to each branch. The fused features are then passed to a prediction head to generate multi-step forecasts. CDO is used for global hyperparameter tuning.Fig. 1Overall architecture of the proposed IFTMC framework. The framework integrates ICEEMDAN decomposition, FCM clustering, dual-backbone feature extraction (TimesNet and Mamba), and cross-attention fusion for short-term PV power forecasting.

ICEEMDAN-based adaptive decomposition

Affected by cloud occlusion, abrupt irradiance variations, and temperature changes, PV power time series typically comprise multi-scale components: intra-day periodicity and trend (low frequency), weather-induced fluctuations (mid frequency), and stochastic spiky noise (high frequency), thereby exhibiting strong non-stationarity and nonlinearity. Directly modeling the raw series requires a predictor to fit dynamics across multiple temporal scales simultaneously, which often leads to insufficient tracking of abrupt local changes or drift in long-term trends.

To address this issue, ICEEMDAN is adopted to decompose the PV power series into a set of quasi-narrowband IMFs and a residual term. Compared with traditional EMD and its variants, ICEEMDAN effectively alleviates mode mixing and residual noise issues through adaptive noise injection and ensemble-mean estimation mechanisms, yielding more stable and reproducible decompositions30. Stationarity analysis of decomposed components

The stationarity property is of primary importance for the effectiveness of a time-series forecasting model, as non-stationary characteristics are mainly responsible for performance degradation and forecasting unreliability 43,44. To rigorously examine whether the input series are suitable for training the proposed deep learning framework, we adopt the joint ADF–KPSS testing strategy introduced by Livieris 43. The Augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF) test examines the null hypothesis \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_0$$\end{document}: “the series possesses a unit root” against \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_1$$\end{document}: “no unit root”, while the Kwiatkowski–Phillips–Schmidt–Shin (KPSS) test examines \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_0$$\end{document}: “the series is stationary around a deterministic trend” against \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_1$$\end{document}: “the series possesses a unit root”. By combining both tests, four diagnostic cases arise 43: Case I (ADF: fail to reject; KPSS: reject)—the series possesses a unit root; Case II (ADF: fail to reject; KPSS: fail to reject)—the series is trend stationary; Case III (ADF: reject; KPSS: reject)—the series is difference stationary; and Case IV (ADF: reject; KPSS: fail to reject)—the series is stationary.

FCM-based intelligent clustering

The multi-scale IMF components obtained after ICEEMDAN decomposition are typically numerous and differ substantially in frequency bands, fluctuation intensity, and complexity. Modeling each IMF individually may lead to inflated model size, increased training cost, and accumulation of redundant forecasting errors. To address this issue, Fuzzy C-Means (FCM) clustering is introduced as an “intelligent clustering” module to adaptively merge IMF components with similar dynamic characteristics into a small number of cluster-level subsequences, enabling effective dimensionality reduction and structured modeling31. Unlike conventional K-means hard clustering, FCM allows each IMF to belong to multiple clusters with different membership degrees. This soft partitioning mechanism is particularly suitable for PV power decomposition where frequency-band overlap and ambiguous boundaries (e.g., mixtures of mid–high-frequency disturbances and noise) are common, helping reduce information fragmentation and misallocation risks induced by hard clustering.

IMF Feature Construction. To characterize the dynamic properties of each IMF component, a multi-dimensional feature vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{g}_i$$\end{document} is extracted for each \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c_i(t)$$\end{document}. The feature selection rationale is as follows: sample entropy \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{SE}_i$$\end{document} measures sequence complexity (and thus predictability), energy \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_i$$\end{document} reflects overall component intensity, center frequency \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_i$$\end{document} characterizes the dominant oscillation period, and standard deviation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _i$$\end{document} represents fluctuation amplitude:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} E_i&= \sum _{t} c_i^2(t), \quad f_i = \arg \max _{f}\left| \mathscr {F}\left\{ c_i(t)\right\} \right| , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{g}_i&= \left[ \textrm{SE}_i,\; This feature set comprehensively characterizes IMF properties from complexity, energy, and frequency dimensions, providing discriminative inputs for subsequent clustering.

FCM Objective Function and Iterative Updates. Let K be the number of clusters, m be the fuzzifier (set to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m=2$$\end{document} in this study), \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{v}_k$$\end{document} be the k-th cluster center, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u_{ik}$$\end{document} be the membership degree of IMF i to cluster k. The FCM objective function is formulated as:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \min _{\textbf{U},\textbf{V}} J = \sum _{i=1}^{M}\sum _{k=1}^{K} u_{ik}^{m}\left\| \textbf{g}_i-\textbf{v}_k\right\| _2^{2}, \quad \text {s.t.} \quad \sum _{k=1}^{K}u_{ik} = 1, \; \forall i, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where M is the total number of IMFs. The iterative update formulas are derived through Lagrangian optimization:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{v}_k = \frac{\sum _{i=1}^{M}u_{ik}^{m}\textbf{g}_i}{\sum _{i=1}^{M}u_{ik}^{m}}, \quad u_{ik} = \frac{1}{\sum _{j=1}^{K}\left( \frac{\left\| \textbf{g}_i-\textbf{v}_k\right\| _2}{\left\| \textbf{g}_i-\textbf{v}_j\right\| _2}\right) ^{\frac{2}{m-1}}}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The selection of cluster number K has significant impact on model performance. Cluster-Level Soft Reconstruction. After clustering, cluster-level subsequences are constructed by aggregating IMFs weighted by their memberships:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} s_k(t) = \sum _{i=1}^{M}u_{ik}\,c_i(t), \quad k = 1,\ldots ,K. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The residual \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_M(t)$$\end{document} can be merged into the low-frequency cluster or treated as an independent cluster. Soft reconstruction preserves each IMF’s contribution to different frequency-band prototypes, avoiding abrupt information loss caused by hard assignment.

TimesNet for multi-periodic local pattern extraction

PV power time series exhibit pronounced intra-day periodicity (e.g., the diurnal irradiance cycle) while being superimposed with non-stationary disturbances induced by cloud occlusion and temperature variations. This periodic structure is characterized by “multi-period coexistence” and “time-varying intra-period morphologies”, placing stringent requirements on the model’s ability to capture periodicity.

TimesNet serves as the periodic feature extraction module, whose core idea is to transform periodic modeling in time series into a pattern-recognition problem on 2D representations32. The specific mechanism is as follows: first, dominant periods in the sequence are adaptively detected via Fast Fourier Transform (FFT); then, the 1D sequence is folded into a 2D tensor according to detected periods; next, 2D convolutions are applied to simultaneously capture inter-period variations (e.g., trend evolution across days) and intra-period variations (e.g., power curve shapes within a day); finally, multi-period features are fused back into 1D temporal representations. Mamba for efficient long-range dependency modeling

Although short-term PV power series are dominated by intra-day fluctuations, their variations are still influenced by longer time-scale factors, such as persistent evolution of cloud systems, weather regime transitions, seasonal changes in irradiance, and slow temperature drift. Therefore, the forecasting model should capture long-range dependencies and global trends under relatively long input windows.

Mamba builds a sequence-modeling module with approximately linear complexity based on the Selective State Space Model (SSM), substantially reducing computation and memory overhead while maintaining strong long-sequence modeling capacity33. The core recurrence formulas of Selective SSM are:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{h}_{t+1}^{(k)} = \textbf{A}_t \textbf{h}_t^{(k)} + \textbf{B}_t \textbf{u}_t^{(k)}, \quad \textbf{H}_k^{M}(t) = \textbf{C}_t \textbf{h}_t^{(k)} + \textbf{D}_t \textbf{u}_t^{(k)}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{u}_t^{(k)}$$\end{document} is the input embedding for the k-th FCM cluster-level subsequence (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k = 1, \ldots , K$$\end{document}), \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{h}_t^{(k)}$$\end{document} is the hidden state (hidden dimension set to 64 in this study), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{H}_k^{M}(t) \in \mathbb {R}^{d}$$\end{document} denotes the Mamba branch output representation at time step t for the k-th cluster, where the superscript M indicates the Mamba branch. Unlike traditional SSMs with fixed parameters, Mamba’s key innovation lies in its selective mechanism: the state-transition matrices \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{A}_t$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{B}_t$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{C}_t$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{D}_t$$\end{document} are dynamically generated from the input via a gating function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g(\textbf{u}_t^{(k)})$$\end{document}. Specifically, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g(\cdot )$$\end{document} is implemented as a SiLU (Sigmoid Linear Unit) activation, i.e., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$g(x) = x \cdot \sigma (x)$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma (\cdot )$$\end{document} is the sigmoid function. This gating function controls which information is passed to the state-space computation, enabling content-aware filtering. This input-dependent parameterization enables the model to adaptively decide what to retain and what to discard based on the current content, yielding content-aware long-term memory updates. Computational efficiency is achieved through a parallel-scan algorithm that reduces recurrence complexity to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {O}(L)$$\end{document}.

It is worth noting that the original Mamba architecture (Mamba-1) 27 employs a sequential selective scan mechanism, while the subsequent Mamba-2 reformulates the selective SSM as a structured state space duality (SSD) framework that enables more efficient hardware-aware parallelization. This study adopts the Mamba-1 architecture due to its straightforward integration with cross-attention fusion and its sufficient modeling capacity for the sequence lengths considered in short-term PV forecasting.

For PV power forecasting, the advantages of Mamba lie in: (1) the selective mechanism can filter out time-point information most relevant to current prediction from long historical windows while suppressing irrelevant noise; (2) linear complexity enables the model to process longer input sequences (e.g., 7-day historical data), capturing long-range dependencies such as weather system evolution; (3) it complements TimesNet’s periodic local modeling, jointly covering multi-scale temporal dynamics.

Cross-attention-based adaptive fusion mechanism

TimesNet and Mamba excel at capturing multi-periodic local patterns and long-range global dependencies respectively, making them highly complementary at the feature extraction level34. However, simple concatenation or fixed-weight fusion fails to adaptively allocate the importance of these two types of representations under different weather conditions, time periods, and volatility levels, potentially causing high-frequency abrupt changes to be “drowned” by trend information or long-range dependencies to be “interfered” with by local fluctuations.

To address this issue, cross-attention is introduced to construct a feature-level adaptive fusion module35. Let the temporal hidden representation from the TimesNet branch be \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{H}^{T} \in \mathbb {R}^{L \times d}$$\end{document} and that from the Mamba branch be \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{H}^{M} \in \mathbb {R}^{L \times d}$$\end{document}. Cross-attention takes one branch as the query side and the other as the key-value side:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{Q}&= \textbf{H}^{T}\textbf{W}_{Q}, \quad \textbf{K} = \textbf{H}^{M}\textbf{W}_{K}, \quad \textbf{V} = \textbf{H}^{M}\textbf{W}_{V}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{W}_{Q}, \textbf{W}_{K}, \textbf{W}_{V} \in \mathbb {R}^{d \times d_k}$$\end{document} are learnable projection matrices, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{V} \in \mathbb {R}^{L \times d_k}$$\end{document} is the value matrix that carries the information content from the Mamba branch to be aggregated, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} This process can be interpreted as follows: at each time step, TimesNet forms a query and retrieves the most relevant information from Mamba’s global representations, thereby aggregating long-range dependency information for the current prediction.

The adaptive fusion advantages of cross-attention are threefold: attention weights vary dynamically with input, enabling the model to automatically adjust dual-branch contributions between clear-sky (stronger periodicity) and cloudy/abrupt-change (stronger non-stationary disturbances) scenarios; cross-scale alignment between local fluctuations and global background states is achieved through \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{Q}\textbf{K}^{\top }$$\end{document} matching36; when high-frequency disturbances are pronounced, the attention mechanism tends to focus on key time points, reducing accumulation and propagation of irrelevant noise in multi-step forecasting.

This study adopts a bidirectional cross-attention strategy: TimesNet serves as the query to retrieve Mamba’s global dependency information, while Mamba serves as the query to retrieve TimesNet’s periodic local information, enabling bidirectional local–global alignment37. The fused representation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{\textbf{F}}$$\end{document} undergoes further nonlinear transformation through a feed-forward network (FFN) before being fed into the prediction head for multi-step forecasting outputs.

Hyperparameter optimization via cloud drift optimization

The proposed IFTMC framework involves multiple key hyperparameters, including: ICEEMDAN noise coefficient \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} and ensemble size N, FCM cluster number K and fuzzifier m, TimesNet period detection number J, Mamba hidden state dimension \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_h$$\end{document}, as well as training parameters such as learning rate and batch size. Manual tuning is time-consuming and prone to local optima; therefore, CDO is employed for global hyperparameter optimization38.

CDO is a novel metaheuristic optimization algorithm that treats each candidate solution as a cloud particle and achieves stable convergence in complex nonconvex search spaces through a two-phase exploration-exploitation drift strategy, as illustrated in Fig. 6. Evaluation metrics

To comprehensively evaluate prediction accuracy and goodness-of-fit, four evaluation metrics are employed: mean absolute error (MAE), root mean squared error (RMSE), mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), and coefficient of determination (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document}). Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_t$$\end{document} be the ground-truth power, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{P}_t$$\end{document} be the predicted power, and N be the number of test samples. The metrics are defined as:19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{MAE}&= \frac{1}{N}\sum _{t=1}^{N}\left| P_t - \hat{P}_t\right| , \quad \textrm{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum _{t=1}^{N}\left( P_t - \hat{P}_t\right) ^2}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{MAPE}&= \frac{100\%}{N}\sum _{t=1}^{N}\left| \frac{P_t - \hat{P}_t}{P_t + \varepsilon }\right| , \quad R^{2} = 1 - \frac{\sum _{t=1}^{N}\left( P_t - \hat{P}_t\right) ^2}{\sum _{t=1}^{N}\left( P_t - \bar{P}\right) ^2}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varepsilon$$\end{document} is a small positive constant to avoid division by zero and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar{P}$$\end{document} is the mean of ground-truth values. MAE and MAPE reflect overall error magnitude and relative deviation, RMSE is more sensitive to large errors (e.g., cloud-induced abrupt changes), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document} evaluates goodness-of-fit from the perspective of explained variance. This study adopts “lower error metrics and higher \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document}” as the comprehensive evaluation criterion.

Results

Dataset and experimental setup

Dataset. The proposed framework is evaluated on the DKASC (Desert Knowledge Australia Solar Centre) dataset, a publicly available benchmark collected from a grid-connected PV system in Alice Springs, Australia (23.7°S, 133.9°E). The PV system has a nominal capacity of 6.3 kW (STC-rated). This location features high solar irradiance and diverse weather conditions, including clear skies, dust storms, and monsoon-influenced cloud cover. The dataset spans January 2019 to December 2020 with an original recording interval of 5 minutes. To balance information granularity and computational cost, and to align with the typical temporal resolution used in grid dispatch scheduling, the data are resampled to 15-minute intervals by averaging consecutive 5-minute records. This resolution has been widely adopted in short-term PV forecasting studies and is consistent with the standard settlement period in many electricity markets. Input features include PV power output, global horizontal irradiance (GHI), direct normal irradiance (DNI), ambient temperature, module temperature, wind speed, and relative humidity. Data preprocessing includes removing nighttime records (power \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$< 0.01$$\end{document} kW) and imputing missing values via KNN. The data are split chronologically into training (70%, January 2019 – August 2020), validation (15%, September 2020 – mid-November 2020), and test (15%, mid-November 2020 – December 2020) sets.

Input feature temporal scope. To ensure a strictly causal forecasting setup, all input features used by the model are restricted to time steps \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t - L + 1$$\end{document} through t (i.e., the historical window). No meteorological or power observations from the future horizon \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t+1$$\end{document} through \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t+H$$\end{document} are used as model inputs during either training or inference. The meteorological features (GHI, DNI, temperature, wind speed, humidity) correspond to historical measured values co-located with the PV system, not numerical weather prediction (NWP) forecasts. Consequently, the near-zero time lag observed between predicted and actual power curves in Fig. 9 is attributable to the model’s effective extraction of temporal patterns from the historical window rather than to any use of contemporaneous future information. To further quantify this, we computed the cross-correlation function between the predicted and actual power series: the peak correlation occurs at lag 0 with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r = 0.967$$\end{document} for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H=8$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r = 0.941$$\end{document} for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H=12$$\end{document}, with no significant lead component, confirming the absence of look-ahead bias.

Weather condition statistics. Based on the clearness index classification, the dataset contains 36.0% clear-sky days (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_t \ge 0.7$$\end{document}), 42.0% partly cloudy days (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.4 \le K_t < 0.7$$\end{document}), and 22.0% overcast days (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_t < 0.4$$\end{document}). The predominance of partly cloudy conditions, combined with the arid climate’s characteristic rapid cloud transient events, makes this dataset particularly challenging for short-term forecasting. Clear-sky days exhibit peak power outputs of approximately 5.2–5.8 kW with smooth bell-shaped diurnal curves, partly cloudy days show highly irregular fluctuations between 0.5–5.0 kW with frequent ramp events, and overcast days are characterized by dampened output levels generally below 2.5 kW.

Seasonal split considerations. The 70-15-15 chronological split results in the training set predominantly covering January 2019 through August 2020 (spanning all seasons including two full summers), the validation set covering September–November 2020 (spring), and the test set covering November–December 2020 (late spring to early summer). Since Alice Springs is located in Australia’s arid interior, the inter-seasonal variation in solar irradiance patterns is less pronounced than in temperate climates: the region experiences relatively consistent high irradiance year-round, with the primary variability arising from cloud cover and dust events rather than seasonal daylight changes. Furthermore, the training set encompasses two full annual cycles, ensuring exposure to the seasonal patterns present in the test period. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that the validation and test sets are biased toward the spring–summer transition, and future work should consider cross-validation or rolling-origin evaluation to provide a more comprehensive temporal assessment.

Baseline methods

The proposed framework is compared with 17 baseline methods spanning five categories: (1) traditional statistical methods (Persistence and ARIMA); (2) machine learning methods (SVR, RF, and XGBoost); (3) deep learning methods (LSTM, GRU, TCN, and Transformer); (4) advanced time series models (Informer39, Autoformer40, TimesNet41, and Mamba27); and (5) decomposition-based methods (CEEMDAN-LSTM42, ICEEMDAN-Bagging-XGBoost20, CEEMDAN-RIME-AM-TCN-BiLSTM16, and ICEEMDAN-TCN-BiLSTM-MHA17). To ensure a fair comparison, all baseline methods are tuned on the same validation set using grid search over their key hyperparameters. Specifically, for deep learning baselines, we search over learning rate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\in \{0.0001, 0.0005, 0.001, 0.005\}$$\end{document}, hidden dimension \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\in \{32, 64, 128, 256\}$$\end{document}, number of layers \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\in \{1, 2, 3\}$$\end{document}, and batch size \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\in \{16, 32, 64\}$$\end{document}. For machine learning baselines (SVR, RF, XGBoost), we tune regularization strength, tree depth, and number of estimators via grid search. For decomposition-based methods, the decomposition parameters follow their original papers while the prediction network hyperparameters are re-tuned on our dataset. The configuration yielding the lowest validation MAE is selected for each baseline. All deep learning models share the same early stopping criterion (patience = 15 based on validation MAE) and the same training/validation/test split to eliminate confounding factors.

Forecasting reliability analysis

Beyond prediction accuracy, the reliability of a forecasting model must be evaluated by examining whether its residuals are free of autocorrelation 43,44. A model whose residuals exhibit significant autocorrelation has failed to capture all temporal dependencies in the data, rendering its forecasts potentially unreliable even if the point-prediction metrics appear satisfactory. Discussion

Methodological implications

The experimental results show that the proposed IFTMC framework improves short-term PV power forecasting, supporting the effectiveness of the “decomposition–clustering–dual-backbone–fusion” paradigm. The observed gains from FCM-based soft clustering (relative to K-means hard partitioning) suggest that frequency-band boundaries in decomposed PV signals can be ambiguous, and that membership-weighted reconstruction may help preserve transitional information.

The complementary behavior of TimesNet and Mamba also motivates the dual-backbone design: TimesNet emphasizes multi-periodic intra-day patterns, whereas Mamba emphasizes longer-range dependencies. The cross-attention module then provides input-dependent fusion, which is particularly relevant under partly cloudy conditions where both local fluctuations and broader temporal context affect power output.

From a practical standpoint, the framework offers tangible benefits that extend beyond technical metrics to support broader sustainability objectives. Accurate 2–3-hour-ahead forecasts enable grid operators to make more informed dispatch decisions, potentially reducing reliance on fossil fuel-based spinning reserves and associated carbon emissions. The demonstrated MAE reduction of 4.2%–4.8% translates to improved renewable energy utilization by minimizing curtailment during supply–demand mismatches. Moreover, the modest inference latency (12.3 ms) supports real-time operation, facilitating integration into existing grid management systems and supporting higher renewable penetration.

Limitations and future directions

Despite these promising results, several limitations motivate future research. First, the fixed cluster number (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K=3$$\end{document}) was determined through validation-based selection; developing adaptive clustering mechanisms that automatically adjust to data characteristics could enhance generalization across different PV installations and climatic regions, thereby supporting global-scale renewable energy deployment. Second, while the DKASC dataset provides a valuable benchmark, evaluation on geographically diverse datasets would strengthen claims of broad applicability and better assess robustness across climatic conditions. Third, extending the framework from deterministic point forecasts to probabilistic forecasting with uncertainty quantification would provide richer decision support for risk-sensitive applications, enabling more robust grid planning under increasing climate variability. Fourth, incorporating additional data modalities such as satellite imagery and sky camera feeds could further improve performance during rapid cloud transient events. Finally, exploring transfer learning strategies to adapt pre-trained models to new PV sites with limited historical data represents an important avenue for reducing deployment barriers, particularly in developing regions where accelerating renewable adoption is critical for sustainable development.

Conclusion

This paper proposes a novel hybrid framework integrating ICEEMDAN decomposition, FCM soft clustering, dual-backbone deep learning networks (TimesNet and Mamba), and cross-attention fusion for short-term PV power forecasting. Extensive experiments on the DKASC dataset demonstrate that the proposed method achieves state-of-the-art performance, with MAE reduced by 4.2%–4.8% compared with the best baseline across prediction horizons of 2–3 hours ahead. Ablation studies confirm the contribution of each component: ICEEMDAN effectively separates multi-scale dynamics, FCM preserves frequency-band transitional information, the dual-backbone architecture captures complementary patterns, and cross-attention enables adaptive fusion. The framework exhibits particular robustness under partly cloudy conditions where rapid irradiance fluctuations pose the greatest challenge. By improving forecasting accuracy and reliability, this work contributes to the broader goal of sustainable energy systems, enabling higher penetration of renewables while maintaining grid stability. Future research will focus on adaptive clustering, probabilistic forecasting, and transfer learning to further extend the framework’s applicability across diverse geographical and climatic contexts.
