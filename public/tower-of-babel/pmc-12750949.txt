Emergency surgical conditions in geriatric patients: current research landscape
Authors: Dezhuang Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe global population is aging rapidly, leading to a significant increase in geriatric patients requiring emergency surgical intervention. This demographic presents unique challenges due to high frailty, multisystem comorbidities, and atypical disease presentations, which contribute to elevated surgical risks and poorer outcomes compared to younger populations.ObjectiveThis review aims to synthesize the current research landscape regarding emergency general surgical conditions in older adults. It focuses on the common disease spectrum, evolving management strategies, and critical prognostic determinants to inform and enhance evidence-based clinical practice.MethodsA comprehensive literature review was conducted to delineate the etiology of acute abdomen in the elderly, evaluate the utility of preoperative assessment tools and surgical approaches, and identify key factors influencing patient outcomes, including mortality and complication rates.ResultsAcute appendicitis, intestinal obstruction, gastrointestinal perforation, and biliary diseases represent the most prevalent conditions. Precise preoperative risk stratification using scoring systems like POSSUM is crucial. For hemodynamically stable patients, a laparoscopic-first approach is associated with reduced complications and shorter hospital stays. Prognosis is critically influenced not just by age, but more significantly by frailty, multimorbidity, and psychosocial factors. The integration of Multidisciplinary Team (MDT) models and rehabilitation medicine principles is pivotal for optimizing comprehensive care.ConclusionsThe management of geriatric emergency surgical patients necessitates a tailored, multidimensional strategy. Future efforts to improve outcomes should focus on refined preoperative optimization, the promotion of minimally invasive techniques, and the systematic implementation of multidisciplinary collaboration and early rehabilitation.

Introduction

Global demographic shifts have precipitated continuous growth in elderly populations across both developed and developing nations. In May 2020, the United Nations General Assembly declared 2021–2030 the “Decade of Healthy Aging,” emphasizing policymakers’ imperative to prioritize quality-of-life improvements for current and future elderly populations [1]. The global population aged ≥ 65 years is projected to increase from 524 million in 2010 to 1.5 billion by 2050 [2]. In 2021, 14.2% of China’s population exceeded 65 years of age, with average life expectancy rising to 78.2 years [3]. By 2065, China will become the most aged nation among the world’s 20 most populous countries [4].

Age-related organ functional decline and immunosenescence heighten susceptibility to environmental stressors, resulting in higher disease prevalence and increased presentation of critically ill elderly patients in emergency departments. These patients exhibit distinct clinical characteristics that profoundly influence diagnostic and therapeutic decisions, ultimately affecting survival quality and clinical outcomes. With the advancing population, elderly patients now constitute over 30% of all emergency general surgery cases [5]. Critically, emergency general surgeries carry fivefold higher mortality and triple the complication rates of elective procedures [6]. Consequently, optimizing management strategies for geriatric emergency surgical patients represents a critical and urgent healthcare priority. However, current research addressing this population remains limited. This review comprehensively synthesizes the disease spectrum distribution, treatment strategies, and prognostic determinants for elderly emergency general surgical patients to inform evidence-based clinical practice.

Disease spectrum distribution

Elderly emergency general surgical patients most commonly present with acute abdominal pain. Acute abdomen, characterized by sudden-onset abdominal pain requiring prompt diagnosis and intervention, progresses rapidly with variable manifestations and represents a major cause of mortality in geriatric populations. Age-related neurological degeneration, reduced skin elasticity, and diminished abdominal muscle tone attenuate pain perception in the elderly, allowing pathological processes to advance to critical stages before symptom manifestation. Consequently, acute abdominal pain in geriatric patients poses significant diagnostic challenges for emergency physicians. A retrospective study of 1,500 elderly patients with acute abdominal pain revealed the following etiological distribution: acute appendicitis (28.5%), intestinal obstruction (22.4%), gastrointestinal perforation (19.8%), incarcerated hernia (8.5%), biliary diseases (6.3%), followed by gastrointestinal bleeding, soft tissue infections, and ischemic conditions [7].

Acute appendicitis

Acute appendicitis has become the leading cause of acute abdominal pain in the elderly. With the progressing aging of the population, its incidence rate is approximately 5–10%, with a mortality rate of about 8%, both showing an increasing trend annually [8–10]. Appendiceal calculi-associated acute appendicitis represents one of the most common abdominal emergencies in the elderly. Diminished physiological responses and impaired immunity frequently cause delayed symptom presentation, reduced diagnostic accuracy [11, 12], and inconclusive laboratory and physical findings [11, 13]. Concomitant appendiceal atherosclerosis further predisposes elderly patients to appendiceal ischemia and necrosis during acute episodes [14]. Regarding the diagnosis of acute appendicitis, computed tomography (CT), capable of accurately revealing appendiceal wall thickening, intraluminal fluid accumulation, and the extent of increased density in the surrounding adipose tissue, serves as the preferred diagnostic technique [15]. Furthermore, studies indicate that elevated total bilirubin levels, acute abdominal pain, and higher body temperature can predict complicated appendicitis [16].

Acute intestinal obstruction

Acute intestinal obstruction primarily denotes mechanical blockage caused by intra- or extra-luminal factors [17], with mortality approximating 20%. Elderly patients constitute the majority of fatal cases [18, 19]. Obstruction subtypes include mechanical and paralytic forms. Geriatric patients typically present with abdominal pain, vomiting, distension, and dehydration. Malignancies, adhesions, and incarcerated hernias represent predominant etiologies [20]. Contributing factors include extended lifespan, alcohol consumption, high-fat diets, advancements in diagnostic imaging increasing tumor detection rates, and higher prevalence of prior abdominal surgeries. Emergency CT with multiplanar reconstruction provides panoramic visualization, offering critical value for determining obstruction etiology, localization, and strangulation [21].

Gastrointestinal perforation

Upper gastrointestinal perforation in the elderly constitutes a severe complication of gastroduodenal diseases with high mortality [22, 23]. While peritoneal irritation signs from digestive fluids are pronounced, comorbidities such as diabetes, coronary artery disease, and hypertension increase pain tolerance, masking disease severity [24]. Research indicates degenerative and atrophic gastroduodenal mucosa with impaired self-repair mechanisms in elderly patients [25]. Consequently, perforations often manifest as large, deep defects, elevating risks of peritoneal contamination and infectious complications. When perforation is clinically suspected, abdominal plain radiography remains the initial imaging choice. However, its sensitivity and positive predictive value decline significantly for small perforations with minimal free air. Evidence demonstrates CT’s superiority in detecting free intraperitoneal gas while accurately identifying perforation sites and underlying causes [26].

Acute biliary diseases

Acute biliary diseases in the elderly primarily encompass acute cholecystitis and acute cholangitis. Gallstones cause over 50–70% of acute cholecystitis cases. Acute cholangitis typically follows biliary obstruction from choledocholithiasis, tumors, or pancreatitis, leading to elevated ductal pressure, bacterial/endotoxin translocation, and severe systemic infection [27]. Elderly patients typically present with atypical clinical features. While symptoms and signs (e.g., abdominal pain, peritonitis) are often mild or inconspicuous and may not reflect the underlying pathological severity, the systemic consequences are disproportionately severe. They are susceptible to early toxic shock, a high risk of secondary multiple organ dysfunction, and consequently, a markedly elevated mortality rate. Gallbladder torsion warrants consideration in differential diagnoses. Thapar et al. recently reported an 86-year-old female presenting with classic acute cholecystitis symptoms (vomiting, right upper quadrant pain). Imaging revealed gallbladder distension, marked wall thickening, and biliary duct dilation, suggesting torsion-induced obstruction. Emergency laparoscopy confirmed gallbladder torsion with gangrene and intramural hemorrhage [28]. Ultrasound (88.6% utilization) remains the most frequently selected imaging modality for biliary diseases in emergency settings [15].

Inguinal hernia

Inguinal hernia denotes protrusion of abdominal contents through weakened musculofascial layers of the lower abdominal wall, predominantly affecting infants and the elderly [29]. Muscle atrophy, attenuated abdominal wall integrity, and comorbidities elevating intra-abdominal pressure contribute to higher incidence in geriatric populations [30, 31], reaching 11.6‰ among those >60 years [32]. Clinical features include abdominal heaviness, pain, reducible masses, and strangulation, potentially accompanied by nausea, vomiting, or constipation. Incarceration occurs in 40% of cases, risking necrosis and perforation [33]. Rapid progression to ischemic necrosis of herniated contents poses severe consequences in elderly incarcerated inguinal hernias. Additionally, underweight elderly females exhibit higher susceptibility to obturator hernia, protrusion through the obturator foramen into the femoral triangle, accounting for 0.05–1.4% of abdominal wall hernias [34]. Ultrasound serves as the primary imaging modality due to its non-invasiveness, dynamic assessment capability, and clear demonstration of hernia sac dimensions, contents, and adjacent structures. CT offers superior diagnostic value for complex inguinal hernias.

Acute ischemic bowel disease

Ischemic bowel disease encompasses conditions involving insufficient arterial perfusion or impaired venous drainage leading to intestinal wall injury, including acute mesenteric ischemia (AMI), chronic mesenteric ischemia, and ischemic colitis. This uncommon entity predominantly affects the elderly [35], with AMI incidence reaching 10% in those >70 years [36]. Common etiologies include mesenteric embolism, thrombosis, vasospasm, or hypoperfusion [37]. Increased vascular tortuosity and resistance in the elderly heighten susceptibility. Patients may exhibit severe abdominal pain discordant with physical signs, accompanied by nausea, vomiting, distension, hematochezia, or diarrhea [36]. High misdiagnosis rates prevail. Imaging characteristics primarily demonstrate mesenteric vascular insufficiency and intestinal ischemic injury [38]. Current definitive diagnostic modalities include computed tomography angiography (CTA) and digital subtraction angiography [39].

Surgical management

Rapid global population aging has transformed emergency abdominal surgery for elderly acute abdominal conditions into a worldwide challenge. Substantial evidence confirms unique physiological and clinical features of abdominal diseases in the elderly: diminished organ functional reserve and higher comorbidity prevalence collectively elevate risks of severe postoperative complications and mortality following emergency general surgery compared to younger populations. Reported in-hospital mortality reaches 21.4% for emergency laparotomy patients >70 years and escalates to 44% for octogenarians [40]. Significant challenges persist in improving outcomes for geriatric emergency surgery [41]. Consequently, optimizing preoperative assessment, surgical approach selection, and postoperative complication evaluation is paramount.

Preoperative assessment

Frailty, immunocompromise, and multiple chronic comorbidities often render elderly patients suboptimal surgical candidates [42]. Postoperative recovery typically involves greater complexity and prolonged hospitalization in this demographic [40]. However, when conservative management fails for abdominal emergencies, accurate preoperative assessment establishes the foundation for therapeutic efficacy and prognosis. Key assessment elements include comorbidities, vital signs, and laboratory parameters. Among scoring systems, the Physiological and Operative Severity Score for the enumeration of Mortality and Morbidity (POSSUM) provides critical perioperative risk stratification [43], incorporating 12 preoperative physiological indicators and 6 surgical severity parameters [44] to evaluate abdominal surgery risks and complications in elderly acute abdomen patients. Studies confirm significantly higher POSSUM scores in patients developing complications versus complication-free groups, with escalating scores correlating with worse Clavien-Dindo complication grades [44]. Recently, Xu et al. developed the Geriatric Emergency Surgery score—derived from 1,500 elderly acute abdomen patients—to preoperatively predict postoperative mortality. Independent predictors included age >80 years, COPD history, ventilator requirement within 48 h preoperatively, Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index < 82, GFR ≤ 44 ml/min/1.73 m², pleural effusion, intra-abdominal free air, and serum aspartate aminotransferase >40 U/L [7].

For acute intestinal obstruction, management strategies center on conservative versus surgical approaches [45]. Conservative management applies to incomplete obstruction without strangulation, adhesive obstruction, or paralytic ileus. Surgery is indicated for malignancy-induced obstruction, strangulated obstruction, or failure of rigorous conservative management [46, 47]. A study of 312 elderly intestinal obstruction patients identified abdominal tender mass, peritonitis signs, high fever, irregular bowel wall thickening, progressive abdominal pain, non-operative treatment duration, ascites, preoperative white blood cells >10 × 10⁹/L, fecal impaction, prior abdominal surgery, and elevated lactate dehydrogenase as key determinants for surgical intervention [20].

Regarding appendicitis complication prediction, preoperative maximum temperature ≥ 38 °C, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, fibrinogen level, appendiceal fecalith, and periappendiceal relationships (fluid collection, ileocecal thickening) independently correlate with gangrenous perforation [48].

Surgical approach selection

Age-related vulnerabilities increase risks of diffuse peritonitis, multiorgan dysfunction, and impaired adaptation/compensation, culminating in high surgical risks, frequent complications, and elevated mortality. Therefore, managing primary pathologies requires selecting appropriate surgical techniques based on systemic and local conditions, prioritizing simplicity, safety, efficacy, and expediency. Evidence-based medicine indicates that for hemodynamically stable patients, laparoscopic surgery can avoid non-therapeutic laparotomy, reduce postoperative complications, shorten hospital stay [49], and decrease the incidence of incisional hernias or adhesions [50]. Recent Cesena guidelines propose that in hemodynamically stable conditions, laparoscopic surgery is the preferred approach recommended by the international WSES expert panel for emergency abdominal surgery in general surgical emergencies and abdominal trauma [51]. Consequently, due to its higher safety profile and minimal invasiveness compared to open surgery, laparoscopic surgery has been established as the optimal intervention for acute appendicitis, acute cholecystitis, and most gynecological emergencies. An Italian multicenter study of nearly 2,000 elderly acute abdomen patients reported 32.6% overall complication rates. Open surgery exhibited significantly higher complications than laparoscopy. Independent predictors for complications included elevated creatinine/glucose, low hemoglobin, oral anticoagulation, acute respiratory failure, malignancy, systemic inflammatory response syndrome, and open surgical approach [52].

For acute appendicitis, the 2020 WSES Jerusalem guidelines recommend laparoscopic appendectomy as the preferred approach over open appendectomy for both uncomplicated and complicated cases [53]. Laparoscopic surgery demonstrates significantly lower complication rates, reduced mortality, shorter average hospital stays, and lower mean total hospitalization costs in both non-perforated and perforated appendicitis [54]. A Zhongshan Hospital (Fudan University) study of 122 octogenarians undergoing emergency appendectomy revealed that over 80% received laparoscopic appendectomy, with 12% requiring conversion to open surgery. Approximately 40% had appendicoliths, which significantly increased the risks of gangrene and perforation [55]. Additionally, Fournier’s gangrene secondary to appendiceal perforation necessitates vigilance for intra-abdominal infections, particularly in elderly comorbid patients with atypical appendicitis symptoms, underscoring the importance of early surgical intervention [56].

For elderly acute intestinal obstruction, clinical decisions must balance disease severity against surgical tolerance [57]. Research involving 72 elderly patients demonstrated significantly higher intervention efficacy, lower bowel necrosis rates, and reduced mortality in surgical versus conservative groups [58]. Given disease characteristics, management requires initial imaging to localize pathology, formulation of lesion-specific surgical plans, adoption of efficient and simplified techniques to minimize operative duration, and prompt postoperative rehabilitation to restore gastrointestinal and systemic function, ultimately improving quality of life [58, 59].

For gastrointestinal perforation, surgical intervention remains primary. Traditional open repair constitutes the historical gold standard but yields suboptimal outcomes due to large incisions, substantial blood loss, and high rates of postoperative ileus and wound infection [60–63]. Conversely, laparoscopic repair minimizes tissue trauma through smaller incisions, reduces vascular injury, provides diagnostic capability, eliminates open surgery’s exploratory nature, attenuates systemic stress responses, preserves intestinal barrier function, improves prognosis, and accelerates recovery [64].

For acute cholecystitis, multiple comorbidities elevate surgical risks in the elderly, establishing endoscopic therapy as the preferred approach [65]. While emergency laparoscopic cholecystectomy was historically common, high complication rates prompted the development of percutaneous transhepatic gallbladder drainage (PTGBD) followed by interval cholecystectomy after stabilization, achieving significant success. A Shanxi study demonstrated lower complication rates with combined PTGBD-cholecystectomy versus direct laparoscopy [66]. Similarly, Sichuan research showed significantly higher cure rates and lower complications with endoscopic sphincterotomy plus nasobiliary drainage/stenting versus open cholecystectomy and common bile duct exploration, affirming enhanced safety, reduced trauma, fewer complications, and shorter hospitalization [67].

For an acute incarcerated inguinal hernia, surgery remains the sole effective intervention. Literature reports indicate incarcerated hernias constitute 5–15% of all inguinal hernia operations [68, 69]. An Anhui study demonstrated superior clinical symptom resolution, earlier ambulation, shorter hospitalization and gastrointestinal function recovery times, reduced intraoperative bleeding, and lower complications with laparoscopy versus open repair [70].

Prognostic determinants

A large observational study of over 150,000 acute appendicitis patients identified age >65 years as a significant mortality risk factor in multivariate analysis [71]. Beyond age, prognostic factors for elderly acute abdomen include comorbidities, frailty, iatrogenic variables, and psychological elements.

Multimorbidity is more prevalent among older adults and has emerged as a major health concern. A substantial body of evidence indicates that comorbidities, rather than chronological age itself, represent the primary risk factor for poor surgical outcomes in the elderly population [72]. A meta-analysis of 1,330 elderly emergency laparotomy patients revealed 21% overall mortality, 83.6% comorbidity prevalence, and 89.2% comorbidity-attributable deaths [73]. A Finnish retrospective analysis of 430 geriatric emergency laparotomies identified age, atrial fibrillation, low BMI, open surgery, ASA class ≥ III, and prior malignancy as mortality predictors [74]. Furthermore, comorbidities, surgical magnitude, and physiological reserve increase perioperative complications [75].

Frailty, a multidimensional dynamic state, represents a prevalent geriatric syndrome [76] reflecting reduced physiological reserve to buffer stressors due to system deterioration. This predisposes to cumulative multisystem decline, correlating with heightened risks of falls, disability, hospitalization, systemic illness, and all-cause mortality [77, 78]. Estimated overall frailty prevalence in elderly patients reach 47.4% [79]. Frailty severity is commonly quantified via Clinical Frailty Scale scoring [80]. Meta-analyses identify frailty correlates including advanced age, low BMI, female sex, solitary living, low physical activity, polypharmacy, smoking, alcohol use, vitamin D deficiency, malnutrition, diabetes, hearing impairment, cognitive dysfunction, poor sleep, fall history, pain, and depression [81]. In addition, frailty is a key factor in the poor prognosis of elderly patients. A Belgian prospective study linked frailty to higher comorbidity rates, falls, hearing impairment, incontinence, 30-day mortality, 90-day mortality prediction [82], and increased 20-day readmission risk [83].

Iatrogenic and psychological factors significantly influence prognosis. Among elderly emergency surgery patients, multiple studies identify age ≥ 65 years, anemia, hospitalization >1 week, operative duration >2 h, catheterization >5 days, malnutrition, ASA class ≥ IV, and Class III incisions as predictors for higher nosocomial infection rates [84–86]. Beyond physiological vulnerability from aging, depression increasingly affects the elderly. Solitary-living elders exhibit heightened physical and psychosocial vulnerability. Compared to cohabiting counterparts, they demonstrate lower socioeconomic status, higher chronic disease prevalence, multimorbidity, depressive symptoms, and elevated adverse event rates (falls, abuse). Complex etiologies and comorbidities of somatic diseases complicate diagnosis. Therapeutic neglect and symptom disregard by patients and relatives may even precipitate suicidal behavior [87].

Discussion

Geriatric patients frequently confront multidimensional medical, psychosocial, and functional challenges necessitating multidisciplinary solutions. Mounting evidence underscores the Multidisciplinary Team (MDT) model’s value in assessing medical and social factors influencing therapeutic outcomes [88]. MDTs typically integrate attending physicians, charge nurses, radiologists, and relevant subspecialists to optimize care through interdisciplinary expertise. Current MDT formats vary across settings, with adaptations for acute scenarios. Core elements of geriatric MDT meetings encompass information synthesis across critical domains: medical, psychiatric/cognitive, social, and functional [89]. Effective MDT principles incorporate skills, processes, and core values. Reports indicate significantly shortened hospital stays for MDT-managed orthopedic, urologic, and vascular surgery patients over the past decade [90]. Consequently, implementing efficient MDTs in geriatric emergency general surgery holds critical promise and may emerge as a primary prognostic determinant.

Rehabilitation medicine, a discipline centered on optimizing function and reducing disability through targeted interventions [91], addresses the unique needs of aging populations. Healthy aging is defined as “the process of developing and maintaining functional ability enabling well-being in older age” [92], comprising intrinsic capacity (individuals’ composite physical/mental capabilities) and functional ability (outcome of complex interactions between health status and physical/interpersonal/social environments) [93]. Common rehabilitation interventions include personalized goal assessment, therapeutic exercise, education, and self-management guidance. Kalisch et al. demonstrated that increased in-hospital physical activity improves physical function, emotional status, and social well-being [94], potentially reducing fall risks [95]. Furthermore, multidimensional interventions, such as resource augmentation, designated walking paths, or functional monitoring, positively influence daily step counts [96, 97] and accelerate functional recovery [98].

In conclusion, Geriatric emergency general surgical conditions—predominantly acute appendicitis, gastrointestinal perforation, and biliary diseases—demand individualized preoperative optimization and minimally invasive surgical approaches. Comorbidities, frailty status, and psychosocial elements critically impact prognosis. Future integration of multidisciplinary collaborative models and rehabilitation medicine paradigms will redefine standards of care for this vulnerable population.
