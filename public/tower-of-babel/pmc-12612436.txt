Proteome-wide mendelian randomization identifies FCRL3 and LY9 as potential therapeutic targets for lymphoma
Authors: Qianqian Guo, Juanjuan Shang, Bingyu Liu, Mei Ding, Yang Han, Yingshu Luo, Xin Wang, Shunfeng Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLymphoma is a common hematologic malignancy with limited treatment options. The research objective was to ascertain plasma proteins that could be targeted for lymphoma treatment.MethodsWe conducted proteome-wide mendelian randomization (PW-MR) analyses on blood proteins from the deCODE (N = 355,559) and Fenland (N = 10,708) studies. Protein-protein interaction (PPI), sensitivity analysis and external validation were used to identify potential drug targets. We analyzed enrichment analysis, single cell sequencing, drug prediction, molecular docking, mouse genome informatics (MGI), qRT-PCR and CCK-8 assays.ResultsPW-MR analyses predicted 37 proteins associated with lymphoma. Therein, 8 proteins, including APOBEC3G, TNFAIP6, LY75, MAPK3, SERPING1, CSF2RB, FCRL3, and LY9, interacted with known lymphoma drug targets. Through enrichment analysis, these 8 proteins were found to be linked to organism’s response to stimuli and the proliferation of leukocytes. PheWAS identified LY75, SERPING1 and CSF2RB as associated with hematologic abnormalities and immune disorders. Single cell analysis revealed the expression enrichment of FCRL3 and LY9 in B cells. The affinity between 5 potential drug targets and predicted lymphoma drugs was identified by molecular docking, and the association of LY75, MAPK3, CSF2RB, LY9 with abnormalities in the hematopoietic system was confirmed in MGI. Finally, qRT-PCR showed that FCRL3 and LY9 mRNA levels were significantly down-regulated in diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) cells. In CCK-8 assay, predicted drugs amiodarone and chrysin inhibited DLBCL cell proliferation in dose-dependent and time-dependent manner.ConclusionsOur study identified 37 potential lymphoma drug targets, emphasizing FCRL3 and LY9 as valuable therapeutic candidates, and amiodarone and chrysin in clinical translational application.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03931-z.Keywords: Mendelian randomization, Lymphoma, Proteome, Therapeutic targets

Introduction

The lymphomas are a heterogeneous group of lymphoid malignancies classified as Hodgkin lymphomas (HL) and non-Hodgkin lymphomas (NHL) [1]. It was characterized by high heterogeneity, strong aggressiveness, and poor prognosis. In 2024, the number of America lymphoma cases accounts for approximately 4.5% of all cancer incidences, while the mortality rate accounts for about 23.6% [2]. Currently, the combination of antibody-based immunotherapy, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy is the standard of care for NHL patients [3]. Despite this, approximately one third of patients still experience relapsed or refractory diseases, leading to eventual progression and mortality [4, 5]. Further evidence is urgently needed to develop novel therapeutic targets for lymphoma.

The utilization of proteomics in drug development has garnered significant attention in the development of anti-tumor drugs [6], and several key proteins have been identified as drug targets for tumor treatment, such as CD20 [7]. As a result of cellular leakage and active secretion, proteins in blood circulation provide valuable information about tumor development, biomarkers and drug targets [8]. The likelihood of a protein drug target gaining market approval is doubled when its association with disease is supported by genetic evidence [9]. The causal impact of plasma proteins on lymphoma, however, remains to be determined.

Mendelian randomization (MR), a genetic epidemiology study design, offers a robust approach to investigate causal relationships between traits and diseases [10]. By utilizing genetic variants as instrumental variables for exposure, such as circulating proteins, MR enhances the ability to draw causal inferences. MR offers a reduced susceptibility to confounding as a result of the random assortment of genetic variants at conception, thereby minimizing associations with environmental or self-selection factors. MR has been widely used to establish causal relationships between diseases and exposures, including the link between circulating proteins and cancer. Recent studies showed that circulating proteins played a causal role in carcinogenesis [11], providing strong evidence for their pathogenic role in lymphoma. This supports the application of MR to identify potential drug targets, further substantiate the causal relationship between circulating proteins and lymphoma, and open new avenues for therapeutic target discovery.

In this study, we systematically identified circulating protein biomarkers associated with lymphoma risk using proteome-wide MR (PW-MR). To improve the credibility of results, the subsequent protein–protein interaction (PPI), gene-based association and pathway analyses, Phenotype-wide association analysis (PheWAS) analysis, and querying the mouse genome informatics (MGI) were performed. Using single cell analysis, the enriched cell types of identified genes in lymphoma tissues were identified, and the druggability was evaluated to determine their potential as therapeutic targets. Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) and CCK-8 assays were performed to evaluate the expression of identified molecules and the proliferation inhibitory of potential targeted drugs.

Methods

Data sources of plasma protein

The present study utilized two proteomic investigations, namely deCODE and Fenland. Using the SomaScan Multiple Aptamer Assay (version 4) in 35,559 Icelandic individuals with genotypic and phenotypic data, the deCODE study measured plasma protein levels [12], including 4,907 aptamers and 4,719 proteins. Non-human aptamers or aptamers mapping to multiple genes were excluded. Fenland, a proteome-wide proteome association study of 10,708 participants of European ancestry, used the SomaScan v4 assay with 4979 single-stranded oligonucleotides (aptamers) targeting 4775 unique proteins [13]. Therefore, they used these 4979 SOMAmers for association studies.

Data sources of lymphoma

The proteome-wide association study (GWAS) database for malignant lymphoma is available from the open GWAS website (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/). The dataset (ukb-d-C_LYMPHOMA) used in this study includes 359,442 controls and 1,752 lymphoma patients, with a total sample size of 361,194. All participants were of European ancestry. There was no overlap between the exposure and outcome samples. All GWAS datasets can be accessed by anyone.

Cell lines

OCI-LY1, OCI-LY3, OCI-LY8, OCI-LY10, VAL, and U2932 cells were purchased from ATCC, cultured in IMDM (Gibco, CA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Cellbox), 1% penicillin/streptomycin mixture, and 2 mM glutamine, and incubated at 37 °C in humidified air containing 5% CO2. All cells were examined for short tandem repeat (STR) and mycoplasma infection periodically.

PW-MR analysis

In this study, a two-sample MR study was conducted using plasma proteins and lymphoma. Figure 1 showed the overall study design. A set of stringent criteria was followed when selecting pQTLs: (I) proteome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10− 8); (II) genes corresponding to protein-coding genes located within 10 MB; (III) we filtered variants to exclude those with significant linkage disequilibrium (LD). Variants with r² values greater than 0.3 were removed to ensure that the genetic instruments used in our analysis were independent; (IV) being cis-acting variants; and (V) F-statistic >10 to reduce bias [14]. Two types of pQTLs were identified: cis-pQTLs located near genes encoding proteins, and trans-pQTLs found on different chromosomes. Trans-pQTLs were excluded to reduce false positives, due to difficulties in distinguishing pleiotropic pathways.

We utilized the “TwoSampleMR” package from R program for IVW or Wald’s ratio PW-MR separately for each protein in the deCODE study (https://github.com/MRCIEU/TwoSampleMR) [15]. Multiple testing was corrected with a false discovery rate (FDR) of 5%. Pleiotropy was assessed using Egger regression with a p-value >0.05, indicating no significant pleiotropy. Heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s Q test with a p-value >0.05, indicating no significant heterogeneity [16, 17]. The Fenland protein analysis was performed as an external validation to confirm the findings from the deCODE study. Proteins that showed consistent results across both studies were considered significant in the context of protein research.

PPI network

To elucidate the interactions between the candidate proteins and anti-lymphoma drug targets, we retrieved information of the targets of existing cancer therapeutic drugs from the DrugBank database (https://www.drugbank.ca) [18]. Moreover, we explored potential interactions among the candidate proteins. PPI networks were constructed using these data and version 11.5 of the Search Tool for Retrieval of Interacting Genes (STRING) database (https://string-db.org/) [19]. We set a minimum interaction score threshold of 0.7, and Cytoscape (V3.7.0) was used to visualize PPI results [20]. To prioritize potential therapeutic targets, we classified the identified causal proteins into three levels based on their PW-MR evidence strength and known interactions with approved lymphoma drug targets. First-level targets were defined as proteins that showed strong causal effects in both PW-MR and sensitivity analyses and had known functional relevance. Second-level targets included proteins that passed MR or sensitivity analyses but did not interact with any known lymphoma drug targets. The remaining proteins, which only passed a single MR test or lacked drug-target interaction evidence, were designated as third-level targets.

Gene-based association and pathway analyses

The FUMA GWAS website [21] was used to explore the extent of enrichment of protein-coding genes identified by PW-MR methods using the GENE2FUNC tool. In GO bioprocesses, the significance of enrichment was based on exceeding the 5% FDR p-value threshold and having at least 2 genes overlapping in the gene set. We assessed gene expression levels in 54 GTEx v8 general tissues. We then calculated the log2 fold-change expression for each gene. Subsequently, we investigated the differentially expressed genes in each of the 54 GTEx v8 general tissues vs all the remaining tissues, and the results were presented as the log2 transformed average expression of the 8 first-level targets in each tissue. The height of the bar charts represented the log2 transformed average expression values of the 8 first-level target genes across 54 GTEx v8 general tissues. These values were calculated by comparing the expression levels of each gene in each tissue against all other tissues, with the log2 fold-change expression for each gene being determined.

PheWAS

The AstraZeneca PheWAS Portal [22] (https://azphewas.com/) was used to perform a PheWAS to assess horizontal pleiotropy and potential side effects of prospective drug targets. Using exome sequencing data from 269,171 British Biobank participants, 17,361 binary phenotypes and 1,419 quantitative phenotypes were examined [22, 23]. Multiple corrections were applied with a threshold of 2 × 10− 9 set (following the default setting in the AstraZeneca PheWAS portal) to reduce possible false positives.

Single cell expression analysis

Single cell RNA-seq data from the GEO database (GSE182434) was used to assess the cell-type-specific expression of the first-level targets [24]. The database included single cell sequencing of 14 pairs of B cell and non-B cell samples derived from 7 tumor tissue, including activated B cell diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (ABC-DLBCL) (n = 3), germinal center B-DLBCL (GCB-DLBCL) (n = 1), FL tumors (n = 3), and 3 patients with tonsillitis. We initially used the ‘Seurat’ software package [25] to preprocess and convert raw single-cell RNA-seq data, followed by cell type annotation with the ‘SingleR’ package [26]. The average expression values were computed by normalizing the expression of each gene in every cell using the Seurat package, ensuring comparability across different cells. The violin plots were then generated using the normalized expression values to visualize gene expression across different cell populations.

Drug candidate prediction

We utilized the Drug Signature Database [27] (DSigDB, http://dsigdb.tanlab.org/DSigDBv1.0/) to accomplish the drug candidate prediction. By uploading identified target genes into DSigDB, we could predict drug candidates and evaluate the pharmacological activity of the intended targets.

Molecular Docking

Autodocksuite 4.2.6 [28] was employed for protein-ligand docking. Drug structure data were obtained from PubChem Compound Database [29] (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), while protein structure data were downloaded from Protein Data Bank (PDB, http://www.rcsb.org/). Using a deep learning technique, we predicted the structure of proteins without a known molecular structure. The specific open-source protein structural modeling code is available at https://github.com/deepmind/alphafold. Final visualization was performed using pymol (https://github.com/schrodinger/pymol-open-source).

Querying the MGI database

We utilized the MGI (http://www.informatics.jax.org/) database to retrieve genes identified as potential causal candidates through our MR approach. MGI aggregated information from primary literature, and we systematically extracted reported genetic associations and phenotypic abnormalities related to the queried genes.

Cell proliferation assay

The DLBCL cells were seeded into a 96-well plate at a volume of 100 µL per well of cell suspension and subsequently exposed to the drugs for 24 to 48 h. After the treatment, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 1 to 4 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was quantified using a microplate reader. The drug inhibition rate was determined by comparing the absorbance values of the treated cells against those of the untreated control cells.

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR

The DLBCL cells were lysed using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen) to obtain total RNA. This RNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the HiScript III RT SuperMix (Vazyme). The cDNA was then quantified in triplicate using a SYBR Green master mix on a Roche LightCycler 480 real-time PCR system. GAPDH served as an endogenous control to normalize the expression levels. The relative mRNA expression of the target gene was calculated using the 2 − ΔΔCt method, which accounted for variations in the initial amount of RNA and efficiency of the reverse transcription reaction.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.2.0; R Core Team) and RStudio (version 2023.06.2 Build 561; Posit PBC, Boston, MA) software.

Results

PW-MR identified 37 Circulating proteins for lymphoma

To explore potential drug targets for lymphoma using proteomics, we investigated the association between plasma protein and lymphomas using summary statistics of genetic associations in the cohort of 35,559 Icelanders from deCODE database and 10,708 Europeans from Fenland database. In the discovery phase (deCODE study), a total of 113 proteins were found to have a causal association with lymphoma risk (FDR threshold of 0.05 to correct for multiple testing) (Supplementary Table 1). In the replication phase (Fenland study), a total of 143 proteins were identified as having a causal association with lymphoma risk (FDR threshold of 0.05 to correct for multiple testing) (Supplementary Table 2). Among these proteins, 52 overlapping proteins were significantly associated with lymphomagenesis. Differences in sample populations between the two cohorts may lead to population-specific genetic variations affecting protein expression or lymphoma susceptibility. Methodological differences in protein quantification and genetic studies, such as the distinct proteomic platform used in the deCODE study compared to the Fenland cohort, could introduce measurement bias and sensitivity differences. Biological heterogeneity, like variations in immune responses and lymphoma subtypes, might also cause inconsistent observations. The sensitivity analysis aimed to exclud proteins with heterogeneity or pleiotropy to ensure robust findings. The horizontal pleiotropy test (P < 0.05, Supplementary Table 3) identified AMY2A, FCGR2B, GATM, MBL2, MICB, OLFM2, SAT2, TDGF1 and VIT as proteins that failed the test and were subsequently excluded from further analyses. Additionally, the heterogeneity test (Supplementary Table 3) revealed heterogeneity among SNPs in ANGPTL1, BTN3A3, C2, CRELD1, ECM1, MANBA and MICB leading to their exclusion. As a result, 37 specific plasma proteins were significantly associated with lymphoma (Fig. 2).

The PPI study identified 8 first-level targets

To demonstrate the feasibility of 37 proteins as potential drug targets, we performed a comprehensive analysis of lymphoma-related drug targets in DrugBank and constructed a PPI network using STRING to show the links between candidate proteins and disease-related drug targets (Supplementary Table 4). PPI network revealed interactions between 37 proteins and lymphoma drug targets (Fig. 3, Supplementary Table 5). These 8 proteins (APOBEC3G, TNFAIP6, LY75, MAPK3, SERPING1, CSF2RB, FCRL3 and LY9) were shown to exhibit a strong causal relationship with lymphoma, which were considered as first-level targets. In addition, 29 proteins did not interact with known lymphoma drug targets, while they were categorized as second-level targets. The remaining proteins (n = 176) were classified as third-level targets that, due to various factors, only passed a single PW-MR or sensitivity analysis and did not exhibit interactions with known drug targets for lymphoma.

Gene-based association and pathway analyses

We used the GENE2FUNC tool of the FUMA web application programming interface to investigate the enrichment analysis and tissue expression of proteins associated with lymphoma. These 8 proteins in the first-level target were significantly enriched in the organism’s response to stimuli and the proliferation of leukocytes (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, the expression levels of first-level targets in the spleen, whole blood, and lymphocytes were significantly elevated. Second-level targets also showed high expression, suggesting their potential targeting capabilities (Fig. 4B). Interestingly, 8 proteins among the first-level targets exhibited significant enrichment in lymphocytes and the small intestine (Fig. 4C), which may be due to the presence of an abundant lymphatic system in the small intestine.

Phenotype-wide association analysis

To further assess the potential impact of the 8 first-level targets on other traits and to explore pleiotropic effects that were not considered in the MR-Egger intercept test, we analyzed phenotypes from 17,361 dichotomous and 1,419 quantitative traits. It was possible to identify a correlation between genetically determined protein expression and specific diseases or traits using the AstraZeneca PheWAS Portal database. Except for LY75, SERPING1 and CSF2RB, no significant associations of APOBEC3G, TNFAIP6, MAPK3, FCRL3, and LY9 with other traits were observed at the gene level (P < 5 × 10− 8 for genomic association), suggesting that the potential side-effects and horizontal pleiotropy of drugs targeting these genes are likely to be small (Tables 1 and 2; Supplementary Figures S1-8). In contrast, LY75, CSF2RB, and SERPING1 were found to be associated with multiple clinical indicators and immune disorders, suggesting that drugs targeting these 3 proteins may have a side effect on various clinical indicators and immune disorders. However, the PW-MR of LY75, CSF2RB, and SERPING1 proteins may have a pleiotropic effect on the results.

Single cell combined PW-MR revealed 8 first-level targets with remarkable differences in various cell types

To explore whether the genes encoding these 8 circulating proteins were specifically enriched in lymphoma cells (B cells), we also used GEO (GSE182434, DLBCL, n = 4; FL, n = 3) to analyze single cell RNA-seq data. The cells were divided into 31 clusters and further categorized into 11 cell types (B cells, CD4 + T cells, CD8 + T cells, DC, endothelial cells, erythrocytes, fibroblasts, HSC, macrophages, monocytes, and NK cells) (Fig. 5A). 7 protein-coding genes showed significant enrichment in different cell types, identified through differential gene expression analysis on clustered single cell data from the GEO database, with a significance threshold of P < 0.05. LY9 and FCRL3 were mainly enriched in B cells, TNFAIP6 and CSF2RB were enriched in macrophages, LY75, SERPING1, APOBEC3G were enriched in DCs, fibroblasts and NK cells, respectively (Fig. 5B-D). Therefore, LY9 and FCRL3 were identified as significant genes for subsequent analysis in B cell lymphoma. We performed KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of FCRL3- and LY9-related genes using the Enrichr platform (https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr/), which revealed their significant enrichment in cell adhesion and cell differentiation pathways, indicating that they are involved in biologically relevant processes in tumorigenesis and immune regulation (Supplementary Figures S9-10).

Molecular docking

In order to better understand how drug candidates interact with target proteins and how these targets are druggable, we performed molecular docking at the atomic level and evaluated the binding energies and interaction patterns. We aimed to identify drug targets for further experimental validation and optimize potential drug candidates by identifying ligands with high affinity and good interaction patterns. Molecular docking evaluates binding capacity based on the binding energy or affinities between ligands and receptors, and lower binding energies indicate more stable conformations. We set the binding energies/affinities below − 5 kcal/mol as good compatibility. Therefore, we proceeded molecular docking of the 8 proteins in the first-level targets with their corresponding predicted drugs, and visually represented the drugs exhibiting the highest affinity. The first step involved docking 11 drugs (Table 3) in combination with target proteins (FCRL3, LY9) and ascertaining their corresponding affinities (Table 4). Subsequently, amiodarone and chrysin with the highest protein affinity were selected for visual presentation (Fig. 6). In the second step, we also docked the relative affinities between the predicted 10 potential drugs for 6 proteins (Supplementary Table 6) and the target proteins (Supplementary Table 7), including APOBEC3G, TNFAIP6, LY75, MAPK3, SERPING1 and CSF2RB, and the 3 drugs with the highest affinities were selected for visualization (Supplementary Figure S11). The drug candidates were connected to their protein targets through visible hydrogen bonds and robust electrostatic interactions, suggesting a stable binding.

Querying the MGI database

By searching the MGI database for knock-out mouse models, we examined whether knock-out of a target causes a phenotype relevant to lymphoma. In the 8 genes identified by MR, we retrieved evidence of gene knockout associated with hematopoietic abnormalities. Knockout models of LY75, MAPK3, CSF2RB, and LY9 exhibited abnormal CD8-positive T cell expression, reduced T cell expression, and decreased eosinophil levels, indicating their intrinsic involvement in the hematopoietic abnormalities (Table 5).

To elucidate the expression level of FCRL3 and LY9 in DLBCL, results of qRT-PCR showed that the mRNA expression level of FCRL3 and LY9 were down-regulated in DLBCL cells in comparison with normal B cells (Fig. 7). We conducted protein expression analyses using the Human Protein Atlas database. The results showed that FCRL3 protein was not expressed in lymphoma tissues (n = 12) (Supplementary Figure S12), while LY9 protein showed only weak cytoplasmic positivity in lymphoma tissues (n = 12) (Supplementary Figure S13).

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to investigate the causal relationship between plasma proteins and lymphoma risk in a comprehensive manner. PW-MR, sensitivity analysis, and external validation combined identified 37 proteins. PPI analysis highlighted 8 proteins that were significantly associated with lymphoma. Collectively, we identified 8 proteins (APOBEC3G, TNFAIP6, LY75, MAPK3, SERPING1, CSF2RB, FCRL3, and LY9) with the most convincing evidence (first-level targets), 29 proteins with convincing evidence (second-level targets), and 176 proteins with middle convincing evidence (third-level targets). Through phenome-wide association analysis and MGI querying, we identified pleiotropic effects of the target proteins, and revealed potential side effects of the targeted drugs. Enrichment analysis and tissue expression profiling provided insights into the biological roles of these drug targets, and revealed their involvement in critical pathways and specific tissues. Furthermore, molecular docking predicted several promising drug candidates for these targets, which underscored their druggable potential and offering new therapeutic avenues. qRT-PCR and cell proliferation assays were performed to evaluate the expression and druggablility of identified molecules in lymphoma.

The priority proteins among the first-level targets were FCRL3 and LY9. FCRL3, a type I transmembrane glycoprotein, displayed slightly increased expression levels in splenic naive and memory B cells [30], which possesses both an immunoreceptor tyrosine activation motif (ITAM) and an immunoreceptor tyrosine inhibitory motif (ITIM) within its cytoplasmic structural domain. FCRL3, a member of the immunoglobulin receptor superfamily, is expressed in B lymphocytes and has priority in B cells with dual signal conduction ability, which aligns with our findings [31]. LY9, a member of the SLAM family of immunomodulatory receptors, is primarily expressed on B and T cells. It functions as a signaling molecule, crucial for regulating lymphocyte homeostasis and activation [32]. A key regulator of marginal zone B cells, as well as innate-like T and B lymphocytes, it plays an important role in the development of these cells [33]. Recent studies have not only identified LY9 as a valuable disease biomarker but also highlighted its potential as an attractive therapeutic target in B cell malignancies and multiple myeloma [34]. The observed reduction in T cell proliferation and aberrant morphology of T-helper 2 cells in LY9 knockout mice [35]. In our study, the mRNA expression level of FCRL3 and LY9 was down-regulated in DLBCL cells. Further studies are needed to explore the oncogenic role and regulatory network of LY9 and FCRL3 in the development of lymphoma.

To evaluate the druggability of identified molecules, further drug prediction and molecular docking analysis showed that amiodarone formed stable bonds with FCRL3. Amiodarone is a pharmaceutical agent utilized primarily for the management of severe structural heart disease, particularly ventricular tachyarrhythmias [36]. It exhibits diverse pharmacological effects encompassing sodium channel blockade, potassium channel blockade, calcium channel blockade, and β-receptor antagonism [37]. While its primary application lies in cardiovascular therapy, its multifaceted pharmacological profile may potentially impact cancer cells [36, 38]. However, its clinical use is limited by notable adverse effects, such as cardiotoxicity and pulmonary fibrosis, which have been reported in long-term administration [39]. These side effects must be carefully considered when repurposing amiodarone for oncological indications. Nevertheless, there are currently no ongoing clinical trials comparing amiodarone with other anticancer treatments. Consequently, direct implementation of this drug in lymphoma treatment poses challenges. Drug prediction and molecular docking analysis revealed a stable binding between chrysin and LY9, suggesting its potential as an additional therapeutic agent for lymphoma treatment. Chrysin exhibits antioxidant properties and confers protective effects on the brain, heart, and lung [40]. The antiproliferative and pro-apoptotic properties of chrysin have been demonstrated in several cancers, including leukemia, cervical cancer, esophageal cancer, prostate cancer, thyroid cancer, and breast cancer [41–44], while chrysin suffers from poor oral bioavailability and uncharacterized pharmacokinetics in humans [45]. Although our in-vitro data demonstrated the anti-proliferative effects of amiodarone and chrysin in DLBCL cell lines and implicated FCRL3 and LY9 as potential targets, the absence of in-vivo validation and patient-derived models limits immediate translational applicability. The observed lack of FCRL3 and LY9 protein expression in clinical lymphoma samples warrants further investigation into their context-dependent roles. Notably, amiodarone and chrysin were employed here primarily as mechanistic probes to dissect FCRL3 and LY9 function in DLBCL pathogenesis. Future efforts should prioritize developing analogs with improved safety profiles and identifying novel compounds targeting FCRL3 and LY9 with druggable properties. Future studies using murine xenografts, genetically engineered models, and primary patient specimens are essential to confirm the therapeutic potential of these targets and compounds.

We have also identified several other primary targets, including APOBEC3G, TNFAIP6, MAPK3, LY75, SERPING1 and CSF2RB. Additionally, APOBEC3G, TNFAIP6, MAPK3 have been recognized as promising targets for lymphoma drug development. In mitogen-activated lymphocytes, APOBEC3G has been shown to play a dual role in promoting lymphoma cell survival. It promotes DSB repair after genotoxic treatment and prevents cell death, while it also drive tumor progression by promoting a mutant phenotype [46]. In addition to belonging to the MAP kinase family, MAPK3 is also known as extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK). These kinases play an essential role in signaling cascades, regulating diverse cellular processes such as proliferation, differentiation, and cell cycle progression in response to various extracellular signals [47]. The overexpression of MAPK3 has been extensively documented in B cell tumors, including DLBCL [48]. Isoorientin is a natural flavonoid that has various functions including anticancer, antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities [49–51]. The compound isoorientin has the potential to modulate the MAPK and PI3K/AKT pathways as well as oxidative stress [52], thereby influencing cancer occurrence and progression through alternative mechanisms. For instance, it can enhance ferroptosis by activating the SIRT6/Nrf2/GPX4 signaling pathway to overcome drug resistance in lung cancer [53]. Consequently, it holds promise as a prospective therapeutic agent for lymphoma treatment. In our study, it was predicted to have a strong affinity with APOBEC3G and MAPK3. TNFAIP6 plays a crucial role in regulating extracellular matrix organization during tissue remodeling, primarily through the AKT signaling pathway and innate immune system [54]. In contrast, luronit with the highest predicted affinity for TNFAIP6 have not been studied in tumors.

This study systematically examined the associations between plasma protein biomarkers and lymphoma risk using a two-stage PW-MR design, benefiting from large sample sizes, extensive proteome coverage and confounding bias. The robustness of the study findings was confirmed by multiple rigorous analyses. PheWas, single cell expression analysis, PPI, druggability evaluation, and querying MGI further identified druggable targets and provided insights into potential pathogenic effects of candidate proteins. Although the lack of drug information of several proteins (LY75, SERPING1 and CSF2RB), these proteins still deserve to be a promising new therapeutic target for lymphoma.

Although our initial PW-MR focused on lymphoma as a single disease entity, we fully acknowledged the marked heterogeneity among lymphoma subtypes. To address this, we performed stratified MR analyses across major B-cell lymphoma subtypes (Supplementary Figure S14). These analyses revealed important subtype-specific patterns in different lymphoma subtypes: FCRL3 demonstrated consistent risk effects for DLBCL, HL, and mantle cell lymphoma (MCL), whereas LY9 showed predominant associations with follicular lymphoma (FL) and marginal zone B-cell lymphoma. Notably, the divergent effect directions observed for these genes in MCL further supported subtype-specific pathogenic mechanisms. Our experimental validation focused on DLBCL cell lines based on several considerations: [1] DLBCL represents the most common aggressive B-cell lymphoma with urgent clinical needs; [2] our MR analysis showed the strongest association signals for FCRL3 in DLBCL; and [3] the lack of unified experimental models encompassing all subtypes. Therefore, these findings are most applicable to B-cell lymphomas, particularly DLBCL, and may not generalize to T-cell lymphomas or other rare subtypes. Future studies should validate their functions in other lymphoma models.

While PW-MR provides preliminary evidence for the importance of FCRL3 and LY9 in lymphoma development, direct validation through gene-specific knockdown or overexpression is needed. Future studies will quantify the impact of these genes on lymphoma proliferation, apoptosis, and drug response to establish causal mechanisms. The study is subject to several limitations that warrant consideration. Firstly, this approach integrateed protein data from diverse ethnic groups and populations. Genetic variations among these groups can affect protein expression or function. Different experimental techniques used across datasets may also lead to discrepancies in protein performance, which may introduce bias. Secondly, these findings are applicable solely to European populations, while caution must be exercised when extrapolating them to other racial or ethnic groups due to potential biases arising from differences in population background, genetic makeup, and linkage disequilibrium patterns. Lastly, molecular docking assays heavily rely on protein structure and ligand mass for enhanced accuracy. Therefore, further experiments and clinical trials are necessary to ensure the efficacy of potential drug targets in a clinical setting.

Conclusions

Based on our integrative analysis, we conclude that genetically determined levels of circulating APOBEC3G, TNFAIP6, LY75, MAPK3, SERPING1, CSF2RB, FCRL3, and LY9 are causally associated with lymphoma risk. We highlighted the great value of FCRL3 and LY9, and the predictive drug amiodarone and chrysin in clinical translational application. Further research is required to investigate their roles and molecular mechanism in lymphoma development.
