What Are Computer‐Assisted Methods Achieving in Fine‐Needle Aspiration Cytology of the Pancreas? A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Al‐Amaan Mohamed Mirzan, Roberto Dina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivePancreatic malignancies present major diagnostic challenges. The gold standard for diagnosis is endoscopic ultrasound‐guided fine‐needle aspiration (FNA) with cytopathological assessment. Workforce shortages have driven interest in computer‐assisted diagnosis (CAD) to improve efficiency. However, its overall diagnostic impact in pancreatic cytology remains unclear. We evaluated the accuracy of CAD in this setting.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review and meta‐analysis. Five databases were searched (January 2010–May 2025) for studies applying CAD to pancreatic FNA cytology. Two reviewers independently screened studies, with risk of bias assessed using QUADAS‐2. Random‐effects bivariate models generated pooled sensitivity, specificity, and summary receiver‐operating‐characteristic (SROC) curves. Studies were analysed according to the unit of analysis: image‐level studies generated predictions from individual cytopathological images, whereas patient/case‐level studies integrated multiple images per case to produce a single diagnostic output.ResultsTen studies met eligibility criteria and provided quantitative data. At the image‐level, pooled sensitivity and specificity were 91% [95% confidence interval: 86–94] and 92% [87–96], respectively, with a SROC area under the curve (AUC) of 0.962. At the patient/case‐level, pooled sensitivity and specificity were 85% [69–93] and 91% [73–97], respectively, with an SROC AUC of 0.919. Heterogeneity was high (I 2 = 65%–93%), driven by retrospective designs and variable reference standards.ConclusionCAD tools achieve near‐expert accuracy for definitive diagnoses, reducing routine screening burdens and accelerating surgical planning. However, selection bias, single‐centre training and inconsistent thresholds limit generalisability. Prospective multi‐centre validation using whole‐slide workflows is warranted, and telepathology integration could expand low‐resource access.Print Synopsis (For ‘Inside This Month's Cytopathology’)This systematic review and meta‐analysis shows that computer‐assisted diagnosis for pancreatic EUS‐FNA cytology achieves high accuracy in distinguishing benign from malignant lesions, with pooled sensitivity up to 91% and specificity up to 92%. Current evidence is dominated by retrospective and curated image‐level studies, highlighting the need for prospective multicentre validation using whole‐slide workflows before routine clinical adoption.Tweet‐Length SynopsisComputer‐assisted diagnosis for pancreatic EUS‐FNA cytology shows promising accuracy in this systematic review and meta‐analysis, but current evidence is still limited by retrospective, curated datasets. Considerable prospective multicentre validation in real‐world whole‐slide workflows is needed before clinical adoption.Keywords: computer‐assisted diagnosis, deep learning, diagnostic accuracy, image analysis, machine learning, meta‐analysis, pancreatic fine‐needle aspiration cytology, systematic review

Introduction

Pancreatic malignancies remain among the deadliest solid tumours worldwide. GLOBOCAN 2020 estimated 496,000 new cases and 466,000 deaths, placing pancreatic cancer (PC) seventh for cancer‐related mortality despite its comparatively lower incidence [1]. Five‐year overall survival for all pancreatic neoplasms is only 1–12%, with minimal improvement over three decades [2]. Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) accounts for > 90% of cases, other types include cystic and endocrine tumours. Early treatment is critical: Stage IA cancers show 31.7% 5‐year survival versus < 1% in Stage IV [3]. However, vague symptoms such as abdominal discomfort, back pain, weight loss mean that fewer than 15% of patients present with resectable Stage I disease, while over 50% already have metastases at diagnosis [4]. An aging population and rising incidence could nearly double PC's absolute burden by 2040 [5]. Given the strong association between survival and stage at diagnosis, alongside the projected rise in disease burden, improving diagnostic accuracy and timeliness is of critical importance.

Increasing demand on diagnostic services, particularly cytopathology, has intensified the need for efficient and scalable tools. Following detection of a suspicious pancreatic lesion on CT or MRI, endoscopic ultrasound‐guided tissue acquisition, including EUS‐FNA, is a key component of pathological diagnosis [6]. EUS‐FNA is minimally invasive and achieves high sensitivity with few complications [7, 8]. However, diagnostic accuracy is limited by atypical cytological findings [9], tumour heterogeneity and a global shortage of cytopathologists [10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. These pressures are driving a shift to digital pathology, enabling remote interpretation and integration of tools such as digital image analysis (DIA) and artificial intelligence (AI).

While EUS‐FNA remains a cornerstone of pancreatic cancer diagnosis, its reliance on expert cytopathologist interpretation introduces variability that AI‐based systems are now positioned to address. AI and DIA systems learn cytological patterns to identify malignant features and highlight regions of interest for expert review, improving diagnostic speed, consistency and accessibility. This systematic review and meta‐analysis evaluated the current application of machine learning in pancreatic cytopathology, identified methodological gaps and proposed recommendations to accelerate clinical adoption of AI‐based diagnostic tools.

Methods

Our systematic review and meta‐analysis were conducted in line with PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses) guidelines and were registered under PROSPERO (ID: CRD420251014289). Our search was comprehensive and covered five databases: MEDLINE, EMBASE, Web of Science, Scopus and IEEE Xplore. The initial search strategy focused on AI applied to FNA cytology of PanNETs; however, preliminary screening showed that eligible studies were insufficient for robust meta‐analysis. Therefore, the scope was broadened to include all pancreatic lesions and computer‐assisted techniques, including DIA, WSI and other morphometric or machine‐learning methods. The original search was conducted up to 31‐03‐25, and a secondary search reflecting the revised inclusion criteria was carried out up to 07‐05‐25.

We used terminology based on computer‐assisted methods (including AI, ML, DL, DIA and neural networks), fine‐needle aspiration/biopsy cytology and morphology and pancreatic lesions (including pancreatic malignancy and benign conditions). There was no restriction on the specific computer‐assisted modality or type of cytology preparation (smear, cell block, fluid). Our full search strategy, designed to capture studies assessing the performance of these methods in detecting malignancy in pancreatic cytology, is outlined in Appendix A. We limited the search to English‐language human studies and used Covidence [15] to structure the screening process. Given the limited availability of studies, we did not restrict by report status and included conference abstracts.

Data Collection and Extraction

From each included study, we extracted key data. Extracted items included study design (retrospective or prospective), sample size and characteristics, types of pancreatic lesions evaluated (e.g., pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), cystic neoplasms, PanNETs), details of the AI technique (algorithm type, features used, training method), reference standard for diagnosis (e.g., surgical histopathology or clinical follow‐up) and outcome measures (sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, area under the ROC curve). When not provided, 2 × 2 contingency data were derived from reported metrics. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and source review. For Yucel et al. [16], grade‐specific cell counts and performance metrics were taken directly from their published tables.

Our primary outcome was overall diagnostic accuracy (the proportion correctly classified as benign vs. malignant). Secondary outcomes were sensitivity and specificity. When authors reported sensitivity/specificity without raw counts, we back‐calculated counts where possible.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were completed in R [17] using R Studio [18] with the meta [19], mada [20], metafor [21], readxl [22], tidyverse [23] and caTools [24] packages. Diagnostic 2 × 2 tables (True Positive, False Positive, False Negative, True Negative) were extracted or backcalculated for every study; when any cell contained a zero, a uniform continuity correction of +0.5 was applied to all four cells to permit logit transformation. Studies were categorised based on the unit of analysis. Image‐level studies generated predictions from individual cytopathological images or fields of view. Patient/case‐level studies generated a single diagnostic output per case by integrating information from multiple images, fields or specimens. Sensitivity, specificity and overall accuracy were pooled using a PLOGIT generalised‐linear mixed model (metaprop), stratified by unit of analysis (image‐level vs. patient/case‐level). Each subgroup yielded inverse‐variance weighted proportions with 95% CI plus heterogeneity (I 2, τ 2); between‐group differences were tested with χ 2 (Q M , p < 0.05). A Reitsma bivariate random‐effects model (mada::reitsma) incorporated the unit‐of‐analysis moderator, and Wald tests assessed coefficient significance. Subgroup SROC curves (mada::sroc) with AUCs (mada::AUC, caTools::trapz) were plotted in ggplot2. Accuracy was pooled using the same framework. Sensitivity analyses excluded conference abstracts and removed continuity corrections.

Results

Our search identified 3701 records. After removal of 1306 duplicates, 2395 records were screened and 2302 were excluded at title and abstract level. 93 full texts were assessed for eligibility, of which 72 were excluded due to wrong study design, wrong setting or wrong intervention, leaving 21 studies included in the systematic review (Figure 1). These studies evaluated various computational methods, including artificial intelligence (AI) models, DIA and mathematical algorithms, applied to EUS‐FNA samples for tasks related to pancreatic pathology. Two reviewers independently identified relevant studies. Any discrepancies were resolved by author consensus.

Seventeen studies were retrospective cohorts, two were prospective clinical trials and two were technical feasibility papers. Most investigations originated from East Asia or North America, with additional single centre contributions from France and Iran. Sample sizes and units of analysis varied. Patient/case‐level cohorts ranged from 9 to 194 individuals, while image‐level datasets spanned 55 to more than 21,652 digitised cytology fields; the largest external test set contained 3213 images from 194 patients [25]. Reported mean ages clustered in the late 50s to early 60s, and the male‐to‐female ratio was roughly balanced across studies. All studies analysed material obtained by EUS‐FNA and fine‐needle biopsy cell blocks.

Of the 21 studies included in the systematic review, 10 provided sufficient quantitative data for differentiating malignant (primarily PDAC) versus benign solid pancreatic lesions using computational analysis on EUS‐FNA derived cytology samples. These were included in the meta‐analysis and stratified by unit of analysis into patient/case‐level (n = 5 datasets: Khozeymeh et al. 2023 [30], Kong et al. 2019 [31], Momeni‐Boroujeni et al. 2017 [33], Yamada et al. 2022 [36], Zhang et al. 2022 [25]) and image‐level (n = 5 datasets: Fang et al. 2025 [42], Lin et al. 2023 [32], Qin et al. 2023 [34], Fuji et al. 2024 [27], Zhang et al. 2023 [37]). The full true‐positive, false‐positive, false‐negative and true‐negative dataset is available in Table S1.

Upon Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies 2 (QUADAS‐2) assessment, applicability concerns were high or unclear for patient selection and the index test in most studies. Although the overall clinical question aligned, AI workflows deviated from routine practice, with most models analysing curated, human‐selected ‘hot spots’ rather than autonomous whole‐slide images. Patient selection was similarly affected by dataset simplification, as several studies excluded diagnostically challenging or blood‐obscured cases.

Risk of bias varied across studies. Patient selection (D1) was the most frequent source of bias, with nine studies rated high risk due to retrospective or case‐control designs without consecutive enrolment. The index test (D2) showed high or unclear risk in three studies due to post‐hoc threshold optimisation or exploratory modelling. The reference standard (D3) was low risk in eight studies using histology or clinical follow‐up, but high risk in two relying on cytology alone. Flow and timing (D4) was high risk in four studies due to post‐hoc exclusion of non‐diagnostic or low‐quality samples. These findings are summarised in the QUADAS‐2 traffic light plot (Figure 2).

Sensitivity Analysis

Patient/case‐level studies achieved a pooled sensitivity of 0.85 (95% CI 0.69–0.93), whereas image‐level studies achieved 0.91 (95% CI 0.86–0.94); the subgroup difference was not statistically significant (χ 2 = 1.20, p = 0.27). Substantial heterogeneity was present in both subgroups (I 2 = 80.4% and 77.6%, respectively).

Specificity Analysis

Patient/case‐level studies achieved a pooled specificity of 0.91 (95% CI 0.73–0.97), whereas image‐level studies achieved 0.92 (95% CI 0.86–0.96); the subgroup difference was not statistically significant (χ 2 = 0.09, p = 0.76). Heterogeneity was substantial, particularly in the image‐level subgroup (I 2 = 65.2% and 90.0%, respectively).

Accuracy

Reported study‐level accuracy ranged from 0.71 to 0.96 (Table 2). Image‐level algorithms demonstrated a higher median accuracy (≈0.90) compared to patient/case‐level models (≈0.82), although this difference was not clearly distinct across studies. Given that accuracy is influenced by the malignant‐to‐benign ratio within each dataset, pooled estimates were not calculated. Instead, sensitivity and specificity (Figures 3 and 4), which are independent of disease prevalence, were used for cross‐study comparison. overall diagnostic performance was further assessed using summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC) curves.

The remaining studies could not be incorporated into a single meta‐analysis because they addressed highly variable tasks, employed different targets and used bespoke performance metrics. Some focused on automated grading (e.g., Ki‐67 hotspot quantification versus dual‐path CNN cytology grading), others on slide‐adequacy triage (flagging inadequate FNA smears) or AI‐assisted rapid on‐site evaluation (ROSE) with unique sensitivity, specificity and accuracy thresholds. Given this diversity in clinical questions and outcomes, a pooled effect estimate would be misleading. Instead, we present each study narratively to illustrate the range of AI approaches explored in pancreatic cytopathology.

Discussion

Our findings indicate that computer‐assisted diagnostic (CAD) methods achieve high accuracy in distinguishing benign from malignant pancreatic FNA cytology. Both patient/case‐level and image‐level analyses demonstrated strong diagnostic capability, with pooled sensitivities of 0.85 and 0.91 and specificities of 0.91 and 0.92, respectively. Importantly, subgroup analyses revealed no statistically significant differences in diagnostic performance between the image‐level and patient/case‐level models for either sensitivity (χ 2 = 1.20, p = 0.27) or specificity (χ 2 = 0.09, p = 0.76). The SROC curves supported this, with AUC values of 0.919 and 0.962, indicating strong threshold‐independent discrimination, particularly among image‐level models. However, these findings should not be taken as evidence that CAD can replace expert cytopathologists. The true value of the cytopathologist lies not in binary classification alone, but in integrating morphologic findings with clinical and radiologic context, assessing specimen adequacy and navigating diagnostic uncertainty. That multimodal interpretive role remains beyond the reach of current algorithms.

Our review nevertheless suggests that some state‐of‐the‐art systems are beginning to approximate expert‐level performance in tightly defined classification tasks. In the largest multi‐centre study to date [42], a semi‐supervised convolutional neural network achieved an external validation test accuracy of approximately 94% with an AUC of 0.99. This performance, alongside strong concordance with senior pathologists, suggests that CAD may improve consistency and reduce inter‐observer variability in high‐volume settings. However, strong performance on preselected cytological images is not the same as full clinical equivalence, particularly when algorithms are evaluated on constrained datasets rather than routine diagnostic workflows. This consistency may reduce oversight errors and improve reliability, particularly when used as a ‘second reader’ in high‐volume settings.

AI's quantitative precision may be particularly useful for narrow, well‐defined tasks such as neuroendocrine tumour grading. Shaker et al. [40] demonstrated that automated Ki‐67 index calculation on cell block images achieved almost perfect agreement with manual counting (r = 0.968, κ = 0.86) and correctly reproduced the pathologist's Ki‐67 grade in 92% of cases. The authors concluded that the algorithm performed best as an adjunct rather than a replacement, enhancing objectivity and efficiency while preserving the pathologist's interpretive role. This reflects a broader theme across the literature; CAD appears strongest when deployed to enhance objectivity, standardisation and workflow efficiency within bounded subtasks, while the pathologist retains responsibility for higher‐order interpretation and final diagnosis.

Despite these encouraging results, our review highlights major limitations that temper enthusiasm for clinical deployment. The most important, as reflected by the high risk of bias in our QUADAS‐2 assessment (Figure 2), is that real‐world pancreatic cytopathology is rarely binary. Many included algorithms were trained and tested on retrospectively collected datasets enriched for definitive benign and malignant cases, creating a risk of spectrum bias and potentially inflating performance estimates. In several studies, indeterminate categories were excluded, relabelled or underrepresented, even though atypical and suspicious diagnoses are among the most clinically consequential outputs of pancreatic FNA. This matters because these ‘grey zone’ cases are precisely where human expertise is most valuable. When AI models were challenged with such cases, performance dropped. Fang et al. reported accuracy of 78.79% in atypical cytology, while Momeni‐Boroujeni et al. reported only 77% accuracy in an atypical dataset, compared with perfect performance in definitive benign and malignant cases. These findings suggest that current CAD systems perform best in morphologically clear‐cut cases, but remain less reliable in equivocal lesions where the distinction between benign and malignant cytology is less definite.

A second limitation is that the performance of many models depended on upstream human curation. Most studies did not evaluate autonomous whole‐slide workflows, but instead relied on pathologist‐selected hot spots or representative regions of interest for image capture. In some cases, only diagnostically representative cell clusters were retained. As a result, these systems often analysed enriched fields rather than the full complexity of routine slides, likely inflating performance and limiting generalisability. This was further constrained by small, homogeneous datasets, often from single centres using one stain, preparation method or imaging system. External validation was uncommon, and where performed, performance often declined. For example, Lin et al.'s ROSE‐AI model showed a drop in positive predictive value from 71.7% internally to 60.7% externally [32]. Together, these findings suggest that current CAD systems remain vulnerable to variation in case mix, slide preparation and institutional workflow.

Another limitation is the narrow pathological spectrum represented in current training sets. Most studies focused predominantly on common pancreatic malignancies, particularly PDAC, while rare entities such as acinar cell carcinoma, lymphoma, metastatic lesions or mixed neoplasms were seldom represented or were explicitly excluded. A system trained largely on a benign‐versus‐PDAC binary may perform unpredictably when confronted with rare lesions or unusual mimics, especially when those entities share only partial morphological overlap with the patterns learned during training. Future datasets must therefore expand not only in size but also in pathological breadth if CAD is to support real diagnostic practice rather than idealised binary classification.

This adjunctive role may be particularly valuable in rapid on‐site evaluation (ROSE), where time pressure and workforce limitations are pronounced. Several groups explored AI‐assisted ROSE in EUS‐FNA workflows, a clinically important development because ROSE can improve adequacy and diagnostic yield but is often unavailable outside tertiary centres due to limited on‐site cytopathology support. Lin et al. developed a Diff‐Quik‐based ROSE‐AI model and reported strong external accuracy [32], while Thosani et al. prospectively evaluated ROSE‐AId against in‐person cytopathology [43]. Zhang et al. further combined convolutional and transformer‐based architectures to analyse ROSE images with state‐of‐the‐art performance [37]. Taken together, these studies suggest that AI may have genuine service‐level utility in supporting adequacy assessment and rapid slide triage during procedures. However, even in this setting, the most defensible role is supplementation rather than substitution, particularly when diagnostically equivocal material or uncommon tumour types are encountered.

Despite promising pooled accuracy, clinical translation remains limited by poor handling of indeterminate and non‐diagnostic cases and, more broadly, by poor workflow fit. Future studies should prospectively evaluate CAD within the full EUS‐FNA pathway using unselected whole‐slide datasets, including specimen adequacy assessment, standardised image acquisition and case‐level aggregation without manual ‘hot‐spot’ selection. Reporting should explicitly state how non‐diagnostic specimens and indeterminate categories (atypical/suspicious) are handled, since these drive the most clinically consequential decisions and may depress apparent performance compared with curated binary datasets. Pre‐specifying decision thresholds linked to actions, such as repeat sampling, ancillary testing, MDT discussion or surgical referral, would also improve interpretability and comparability across studies.

Conclusion

Computer‐assisted methods in pancreatic FNA cytology show considerable promise, with evidence of high diagnostic accuracy. These tools can achieve strong performance in defined classification tasks and offer advantages in speed, consistency and quantitative analysis. AI has demonstrated value as an assistive technology, from supporting ROSE and preliminary diagnosis to objectively grading proliferation markers in PanNETs, with potential to improve workflow efficiency and diagnostic confidence.

Nevertheless, most current models have been developed in controlled settings and frequently simplify diagnosis to binary outcomes, which do not reflect the full complexity of cytopathology. Real‐world deployment will require careful attention to bias, generalisability and clinical integration. Algorithms should be trained on diverse datasets that include atypical, indeterminate and rare lesions, and undergo robust external validation across multiple centres before routine clinical adoption. Future research should prioritise prospective multicentre studies that evaluate AI within real‐time diagnostic workflows and compare AI‐assisted practice directly with standard care. Transparent reporting and adherence to diagnostic accuracy standards will also be essential to support reproducibility and meaningful comparison across studies. Close collaboration between developers, pathologists and initiatives such as the National Pathology Imaging Co‐operative will be important to refine these tools, support implementation and build user trust. With continued validation, AI‐driven image analysis may become a reliable adjunct in pancreatic cytology, improving efficiency and supporting diagnosis under expert human oversight.
