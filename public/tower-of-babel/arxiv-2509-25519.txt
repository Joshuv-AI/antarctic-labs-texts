Flow Matching with Semidiscrete Couplings
Alireza Mousavi-Hosseini, Stephen Y. Zhang, Michal Klein, Marco Cuturi

Abstract
Flow models parameterized as time-dependent velocity fields can generate data from noise by integrating an ODE. These models are often trained using flow matching, i.e. by sampling random pairs of noise and target points ( 𝐱 0 , 𝐱 1 ) (\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{x}_{1}) and ensuring that the velocity field is aligned, on average, with 𝐱 1 − 𝐱 0 \mathbf{x}_{1}-\mathbf{x}_{0} when evaluated along a time-indexed segment linking 𝐱 0 \mathbf{x}_{0} to 𝐱 1 \mathbf{x}_{1} . While these noise/data pairs are sampled independently by default, they can also be selected more carefully by matching batches of n n noise to n n target points using an optimal transport (OT) solver. Although promising in theory, the OT flow matching (OT-FM) approach ( Pooladian et al. 2023 ; Tong et al. 2024 ) is not widely used in practice. Zhang et al. 2025 pointed out recently that OT-FM truly starts paying off when the batch size n n grows significantly, which only a multi-GPU implementation of the Sinkhorn 1964 algorithm can handle. Unfortunately, the pre-compute costs of running Sinkhorn 1964 can quickly balloon, requiring O ⁡ ( n 2 / ε 2 ) O(n^{2}/\varepsilon^{2}) operations for every n n pairs used to fit the velocity field, where ε \varepsilon is a regularization parameter that should be typically small to yield better results. To fulfill the theoretical promises of OT-FM, we propose to move away from batch-OT and rely instead on a semidiscrete formulation that can leverage the fact that the target dataset distribution is usually of finite size N N . The SD-OT problem is solved by estimating a dual potential vector of size N N using SGD; using that vector, freshly sampled noise vectors at train time can then be matched with data points at the cost of a maximum inner product search (MIPS) over the dataset. Semidiscrete FM (SD-FM) removes the quadratic dependency on n / ε n/\varepsilon that bottlenecks OT-FM. SD-FM beats both FM and OT-FM on all training metrics and inference budget constraints, across multiple datasets, on unconditional/conditional generation, or when using mean-flow models.

Introduction

Flow-based generative models
(
Rezende and Mohamed 2015
;
Dinh et al. 2016
;
Kingma and Dhariwal 2018
;
Grathwohl et al. 2018
)
can gradually transform noise vectors into structured data.
Flow models parameterized as time-dependent velocity fields
(
Chen et al. 2018
)

\bm{v}_{\theta}(t,\mathbf{x})
can be efficiently trained using flow matching (FM)
(
Lipman et al. 2023
;
Peluchetti 2022
;
Albergo et al. 2023
)
to yield state-of-the-art performance
(
Zheng et al. 2024
;
Esser et al. 2024
)
. The FM training approach proposes to sidestep the need to differentiate through a costly numerical ODE integration at training time by instead minimizing velocity fields locally through a regression loss. In a nutshell, the loss is formed by sampling a pair of noise/data points
(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{x}_{1})
and a random time
t\in[0,1]
to evaluate
\|\mathbf{x}_{1}-\mathbf{x}_{0}-\bm{v}_{\theta}(t,\mathbf{x}_{t})\|^{2}
, where
\mathbf{x}_{t}:=(1-t)\mathbf{x}_{0}+t\,\mathbf{x}_{1}
is a barycenter in the
[\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{x}_{1}]
segment.

Coupling Noise
\times
Data.

A crucial ingredient in the FM methodology lies in choosing the
coupling
of noise/data used to sample pairs. Indeed, given a source and target distribution, there are infinitely many probability paths that can interpolate between them; choosing a different coupling yields a different loss and hence a different flow
(
Albergo et al. 2023
;
Liu 2022
)
.
The default formulation of FM
(
Lipman et al. 2024
)
relies on the
independent
coupling, where noise and data are sampled independently. While simple and cheap, this approach is known to produce ODE paths with high curvature, which requires increased compute at inference time. In practice, the quality of samples is highly dependent on the number of function evaluations (NFEs) used to integrate the ODE.

Optimal Transport Flow Matching.

Tong et al. 2024
and
Pooladian et al. 2023
have proposed using an optimal transport (OT) coupling to select noise/data pairs motivated by the dynamic least-action principle of
Benamou and Brenier 2000
. Both works advocate sampling
n
noise and data points, compute an optimal
n
-permutation (or
n\times n
coupling matrix, from which pairs of indices are sampled from), as illustrated in the middle-left plot of Figure
1
.
While appealing in theory, OT-FM yields limited improvements for an additional precompute cost.
In a follow-up work,
Davtyan et al. 2025
have proposed to keep in memory the pairings returned by multiple Hungarian solvers run along training iterations, but this approach is bottlenecked by a price of
O(n^{3})
at each batch.

Zhang et al. 2025
hypothesize that the mitigated results of OT-FM are due to the choice of a small
n
: Indeed, optimal matchings on small sample sizes are inherently unstable and cannot reliably approximate matches that would appear for far larger
n
due to the curse of dimensionality
(
Hütter and Rigollet 2021
;
Chewi et al. 2024
)
.

Zhang et al. 2025
propose to instead use the
Sinkhorn 1964
algorithm with significantly larger
n
(from
256
used originally to
\approx 10^{6}
) using a multi-GPU-node implementation, carefully ablating the role of
\varepsilon
regularization.
They argue that these computations are
precompute
costs, happening independently of FM training, and therefore can be cached beforehand. However, that cost grows as
O(n^{2}/\varepsilon^{2})

(
Dvurechensky et al. 2018
;
Lin et al. 2019
)
for every
n
pairs fed to FM training. Their finding that larger
n
/ smaller
\varepsilon
yield even better results hinders the adoption of OT-FM as currently implemented (see also our discussion in Table
1
).

The Semidiscrete Approach.

Our work proposes an alternative route to OT-guided FM that does go to the costly process of optimally pairing batches of noises to data.
We leverage the entropy regularized
semidiscrete
(SD) formulation of OT
(
Oliker and Prussner 1989
;
Mérigot 2011
;
Cuturi and Peyré 2018
;
An et al. 2020
)
, which studies OT from a
continuous
(noise) to a
discrete
(data) distribution. SD-OT relies on a potential vector of size
N
of the target, fitted using SGD
(
Genevay et al. 2016
)
. At FM train time, freshly sampled noise is paired to a point in the dataset in
O(N)
time, as summarized in Figure
1
. Following the presentation of background material in
Section

2
,

•

We propose in
Section

3
a new convergence criterion and convergence analysis of SGD for regularized SD-OT that is applicable to both the
\varepsilon=0
and
\varepsilon>0
cases.

•

We introduce in
Section

4
our semidiscrete FM (SD-FM) method, comparing it to FM and OT-FM in terms of memory and compute. We propose a generalization of the Tweedie formula
(
Robbins 1956
)
for flows trained using SD-FM, with a correction
\bm{\delta}_{\varepsilon}
that vanishes when
\varepsilon\approx 0
or
\varepsilon\approx\infty
. We use it to sample from a geometric mixture of distributions using their velocity flows.

•

We illustrate in
Section

5
in a varied set of (un)conditional generative tasks the advantages of SD-FM over OT-FM and FM. We show that SD-FM results in a better pairing of noise to data (as seen in better metrics) for a negligible computational overhead relative to the cost of FM training.

Background and related work

Stochastic interpolants and flow matching.

For two probability distributions
\mu,\nu\in{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, let
\Gamma(\mu,\nu)\subset{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
be the set of couplings between
\mu
and
\nu
, i.e. all joint distributions having
\mu
as
\nu
as their first and second marginal, respectively.
Consider a source and target pair of distributions
\mu_{0},\mu_{1}
and
\pi\in\Gamma(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
a prescribed coupling between the two.
For an interpolant function
(t,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\mapsto\varphi_{t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\varphi_{0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{x}
and
\varphi_{1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{y}
,
Albergo et al. 2023
prove that to a coupling
\pi
and an interpolant function
\varphi_{t}
corresponds a flow generating a continuous
probability path

\rho_{t}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
where
\rho_{t}:=\varphi_{t}\#\pi
, where
\#
is the pushforward operator, and
\rho_{0}=\mu_{0},\,\rho_{1}=\mu_{1}
.
That interpolant can be written equivalently as a
probability flow ODE
with a random initial condition, for a suitable time-parameterized velocity field
\bm{v}_{t}
:

{\mathrm{d}}\bm{X}_{t}=\bm{v}_{t}(\bm{X}_{t}){\mathrm{d}}t,\quad\bm{X}_{0}\sim\mu_{0}.

(2.1)

Access to
\bm{v}_{t}
then allows for samples
\bm{X}_{0}\sim\mu_{0}
to be transformed into samples
\bm{X}_{1}\sim\mu_{1}
through evolution of equation
2.1
.
Training a neural approximation
\bm{v}_{\theta}
for the flow field
\bm{v}
can be done by sampling
(\bm{X}_{0},\bm{X}_{1})\sim\pi
and
t\in[0,1]
, setting
\bm{X}_{t}:=\varphi_{t}(\bm{X}_{0},\bm{X}_{1})
and minimizing

\min_{\theta}\>{\mathbb{E}}_{t,(\bm{X}_{0},\bm{X}_{1})\sim\pi}\>\mathcal{L}(\bm{X}_{1}-\bm{X}_{0},\bm{v}_{\theta}(t,\bm{X}_{t})).

(2.2)

The
linear
interpolant
\varphi_{t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}):=(1-t)\mathbf{x}+t\mathbf{y}
and the squared Euclidean distance for
\mathcal{L}
are commonly used
(
Lipman et al. 2024
, Eq. 2.3)
.
Albergo et al. 2023
prove that the global minimum in equation
2.2
recovers
\bm{v}_{t}
from equation
2.1
. Other interpolants or loss functions
(
Song and Dhariwal 2024
;
Kim et al. 2024
)

as well as penalizations facilitating one-step generation
(
Boffi et al. 2025
, and references therein)

have also been proposed.

Straighter flows…

While the independent product coupling
\pi_{I}:=\mu_{0}\otimes\mu_{1}
is the most widely used in practice
(
Lipman et al. 2024
, §4.8.3)
, it may not be efficient in the sense that it induces
curved
flows. This means that adaptive ODE solvers consuming many neural function evaluations (NFEs) must be used to generate data at inference time.
This is hardly surprising from an OT perspective
(
Santambrogio 2015
, §4)
, because the independent coupling
\pi_{I}
is known to incur a high transportation cost, where for any valid coupling
\pi\in\Gamma(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})
that cost is usually defined as
\mathcal{C}(\pi):=\mathbb{E}_{(\bm{X}_{0},\bm{X}_{1})\sim\pi_{I}}\|\bm{X}_{0}-\bm{X}_{1}\|^{2}
.
Concretely, although the flow
\bm{v}_{t}
learned from
\pi_{I}
might validly link
\mu_{0}
to
\mu_{1}
, its
kinetic energy

\smallint_{0}^{1}\|\bm{v}_{t}\|_{L^{2}(\rho_{t})}^{2}{\mathrm{d}}t
is high.

Benamou and Brenier 2000
show how optimal transport arises naturally as the least-action dynamics that transports
\mu_{0}
to
\mu_{1}
. That map is exactly the
Monge 1781
map, and the corresponding optimal flow paths are straight lines that would be trivial to integrate. To benefit from this insight, two families of works have emerged.

… using Reflow.

Liu 2022
proposes to straighten a
pretrained
flow model
(
Liu et al. 2022
)
using
Reflow
, a method that forms noise/generated-data pairs, used subsequently to improve that model using FM. For Reflow to work, the pretrained flow model must be good enough to generate approximately the target distribution, and significant compute must be spent on generating at each reflow step (through ODEs) a large number of “virtual” data points. While successful
(
Liu et al. 2024
)
, Reflow is a costly recursive
post-training
procedure that uses FM training as an
inner
iteration.

… using pairs sampled from OT couplings.

Pooladian et al. 2023
and
Tong et al. 2024
(see also discussion in
Lipman et al. 2024
) propose OT-FM, a much lighter approach that simply replaces
\pi_{I}
by a coupling
\pi
that should, ideally, approximate the OT coupling
\pi^{\star}=\arg\min_{\pi\in\Gamma(\mu_{0},\mu_{1})}\mathcal{C}(\pi)
.
Compared to Reflow, which uses FM as an
inner
step, OT-FM adds a
pre-processing
effort to FM training, to select better noise/data pairs. Because the ground-truth OT coupling
\pi^{\star}
is never known, however, they use OT
matrices
: sampling
n\approx 256
points from both
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
, they match them using an OT solver (e.g. the Hungarian algorithm,
Kuhn 1955
) and feed these pairs to the flow loss equation
2.2
.

Davtyan et al. 2025
proposed then LOOM-CFM, a hybrid approach that stores buffers of paired noise/data samples (using the Hungarian algorithm).
Unfortunately, OT-FM yields only modest gains.
Zhang et al. 2025
posit that this is due to the well-known statistical bias arising when approximating continuous OT couplings using samples
(
Hütter and Rigollet 2021
)
. To reduce the effect of this bias, they use significantly higher
n
handled with a multi-GPU, multi-node implementation of the
Sinkhorn 1964
algorithm, carefully ablating the role of the
\varepsilon
regularization parameter. While encouraging, their results show better performance as
n
grows and
\varepsilon
shrinks to 0, an explosive cocktail of hyperparameters since the cost of running
Sinkhorn 1964
grows as
O(n^{2}/\varepsilon^{2})
.
Fundamentally, these approaches are bottlenecked by their reliance on the
discrete
view of OT problems – that is, they rely repeatedly on matching
n
i.i.d. samples of noise and data.

Conditional generation using OT.

Chemseddine et al. 2024
;
Kerrigan et al. 2024
;
Hosseini et al. 2025
;
Baptista et al. 2024
provided a noteworthy extension of OT-FM in the context of
conditional
generation, where each data point
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is paired with a corresponding condition
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. The
data
is now an
augmented
random variable
(\bm{X}_{1},\bm{Z}_{1})\sim\tilde{\mu}_{1}\in\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{R}}^{d+p})
, with a marginal distribution
\tilde{\mu}_{1,\bm{Z}}
over
conditions
. The goal remains to generate samples from noise in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, but conditioned on a vector
\mathbf{z}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
of interest.
OT-FM has a natural generalization to this setting, using the notion of condition-preserving
triangular
maps
T:(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\mapsto(T_{\mathbf{z}}(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{z})
that couples conditional noise distribution
\tilde{\mu}_{0}:=\mathcal{N}(0,\Id)\otimes\tilde{\mu}_{1,\bm{Z}}\in\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{R}}^{d+p})
with augmented data in
\tilde{\mu}_{1}
.

Kerrigan et al. 2024
(see also
Baptista et al. 2024
) state that such triangular maps
can be reduced to transport of distributions supported on the product space
\mathbb{R}^{d+p}
following exactly OT-FM principles, implemented as ODE trajectories using a
conditional
vector field
\dot{\bm{X}}_{t}=\bm{v}(t,\bm{X}_{t}|\mathbf{z})
. OT-FM then consists in sampling
n
noise and condition vectors, along with
n
data points and their known condition, which are then optimally paired using an
augmented
cost:

c((\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}),(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{z}^{\prime}))=c_{\mathcal{X}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})+\beta c_{\mathcal{Z}}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{\prime}),\quad\beta>0\,.

(2.3)

Semidiscrete optimal transport.

In the scenarios envisioned in this work, the noise measure
\mu_{0}
is continuous, while the target measure
\mu_{1}
of data is finite.
This setting fits the stochastic optimization approach outlined in
(
Genevay et al. 2016
, §2)
, defined for two probability measures
\mu,\nu\in{\mathcal{P}}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, a cost function
c:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and entropic regularization
\varepsilon\geq 0
as:

\min_{\pi\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\mathcal{C}_{\varepsilon}(\pi):={\mathbb{E}}_{(\bm{X},\bm{Y})\sim\pi}\left[c(\bm{X},\bm{Y})\right]+\varepsilon\mathrm{KL}(\pi|\mu\otimes\nu),

(
\mathrm{P}_{\varepsilon}
)

This problem can be written in a
semidual
form
(
Cuturi and Peyré 2018
)
, leveraging the fact that
\nu
is a finite measure,
\nu:=\sum_{j=1}^{N}b_{j}\delta_{\mathbf{y}_{j}}
where
\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},\dots,b_{N})
is a probability vector. For a potential vector
\mathbf{g}=(g_{1},\dots,g_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, we define the soft-
c
transform
(
Peyré and Cuturi 2019
, §5.3)
as:

f_{\mathbf{g},\varepsilon}:\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\begin{cases}-\varepsilon\log\left[\sum_{j=1}^{N}b_{j}\exp\left(\frac{g_{j}-c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j})}{\varepsilon}\right)\right],&\varepsilon>0\\
-\max_{j=1}^{N}\left[g_{j}-c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j})\right],&\varepsilon=0.\end{cases}\in{\mathbb{R}}

(2.4)

One can solve equation

P
ε

by solving the
concave maximization
semidual problem parameterized entirely by
\mathbf{g}
, that
Genevay et al. 2016
proposed to solve using averaged SGD (our
Algorithm

1
)

\max_{\mathbf{g}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}F_{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{g}})\coloneqq{\mathbb{E}}_{\bm{X}\sim\mu}[f_{\mathbf{g},\varepsilon}(\bm{X})]+\langle\mathbf{b},\mathbf{g}\rangle\,.

(
\mathrm{S}_{\varepsilon}
)

For any
\varepsilon\geq 0
, the values of equation

P
ε

and equation

S
ε

are equal. Moreover for
\varepsilon>0
, given a maximizing potential
\mathbf{g}^{\star}=(g^{\star}_{1},\dots,g^{\star}_{N})
of equation

S
ε

, one can recover a minimizer
\pi^{\star}_{\varepsilon}
of equation

P
ε

that has density

\mathrm{d}\pi^{\star}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j})=\exp\left(\frac{f_{\mathbf{g},\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})+g^{\star}_{j}-c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j})}{\varepsilon}\right)b_{j}\>\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{x}).

(2.5)

Improved Optimization for Semidiscrete Optimal Transport

We start from the formulation presented by
Genevay et al. 2016
and adapt it to the scales relevant to our work. We present new practical recommendations as well as a new theoretical analysis to inform the computation of SD-OT, with (or without) entropic regularization.

Marginal Estimation for Semidiscrete Couplings.

A crucial aspect of the primal-dual relationship in equation
2.5
is that it holds at optimality, but can also be used to create a coupling: one can plug a vector
\mathbf{g}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
into equation
2.5
to define a joint probability that only satisfies the first marginal constraint. The exponentiation in equation
2.5
can be rewritten using the softmax operator mapping a vector
\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\dots,z_{N})
to a probability vector in the simplex
\Delta^{N}
,

\bm{\sigma}_{\mathbf{b},\varepsilon}(\mathbf{z})=\begin{cases}\left[\frac{b_{j}\exp(z_{j}/\varepsilon)}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}b_{k}\exp(z_{k}/\varepsilon)}\right]_{j},\>\>\>\varepsilon>0,\qquad\left[\frac{\mathds{1}[j\in\argmax_{\ell}z_{\ell}]b_{j}}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathds{1}[k\in\argmax_{\ell}z_{\ell}]b_{k}}\right]_{j},\>\>\>\varepsilon=0.\end{cases}

(3.1)

This helps to define a coupling
\pi_{\varepsilon,{\mathbf{g}}}
that arises from a partial optimization of the dual, as

\mathrm{d}\pi_{\varepsilon,{\mathbf{g}}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j})\>\coloneqq[s_{\varepsilon,{\mathbf{g}}}(\mathbf{x})]_{j}\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{x})\,,\text{ where }\mathbf{s}_{\varepsilon,{\mathbf{g}}}(\mathbf{x}):=\bm{\sigma}_{\mathbf{b},\varepsilon}([g_{j}-c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j})]_{j})\in\Delta^{N}.

(3.2)

By construction, the first marginal of
\pi_{\varepsilon,{\mathbf{g}}}
is
\mu
: Indeed, the measure
\pi_{\varepsilon,{\mathbf{g}}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j})
in equation
3.2
sums to
\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{x})
when integrated against the data for a fixed
\mathbf{x}
, since summing w.r.t. index
j
is akin to summing a weighted soft-max distribution.
On the other end,
\pi_{\varepsilon,{\mathbf{g}}}
would be an OT coupling if and only if its
second marginal, defined as

\mathbf{m}({\mathbf{g}}):=[\pi_{\varepsilon,{\mathbf{g}}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d},\mathbf{y}_{j})]_{j}\!=\!\int_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\mathbf{s}_{\varepsilon,{\mathbf{g}}}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{x})\in\Delta^{N}

(3.3)

coincided with
\mathbf{b}
, by analogy to the
Sinkhorn 1964
algorithm
(
Peyré and Cuturi 2019
, §4.14)
. Note that
\pi_{\varepsilon,{\mathbf{g}}}
solves equation

P
ε

if
{\mathbf{g}}
is a maximizer of equation

S
ε

.

Convergence Criterion.

To our knowledge, no convergence criterion has been considered in the stochastic SD-OT literature
(
An et al. 2020
, Alg.1)
,
(
Genevay et al. 2016
, Alg.2)
. Recall that
{\mathbf{g}}
is the solution to the dual problem if and only if
\mathbf{m}({\mathbf{g}})=\mathbf{b}
, i.e. the
\nu
-marginal constraint is satisfied.

A good criterion for convergence could be given by a distance between
\mathbf{m}({\mathbf{g}})
and
\mathbf{b}
. A natural candidate could be total variation,

\mathrm{TV}(\mathbf{m}({\mathbf{g}}),\mathbf{b})=\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{m}({\mathbf{g}})-\mathbf{b}\|_{1}
, as often used to track the convergence of the
Sinkhorn 1964
algorithm.
Unfortunately for continuous
\mu
, the expectation used in
\mathbf{m}(\mathbf{g})
would need to be replaced with an empirical mean, leading to a biased estimate, because that expectation would be inside the norm. Reducing that bias would require a number of samples scaling linearly with
N
, making it prohibitively large. Fortunately, one can efficiently obtain an unbiased estimator for the
\chi^{2}
divergence, defined for two vectors
\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}\in\Delta^{N}
as
\chi^{2}(\mathbf{p}\|\mathbf{q})=\sum_{j}(p_{j}/q_{j})^{2}q_{j}-1
. That
\chi^{2}
divergence can be formulated as an expectation, as highlighted in the fact below, proved in
A.1
:

\textsf{{ Fact 1}}:\chi^{2}(\mathbf{m}({\mathbf{g}})\,\|\,\mathbf{b})=\iint_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\sum_{j}\tfrac{1}{b_{j}}[s_{\varepsilon,{\mathbf{g}}}(\mathbf{x})]_{j}[s_{\varepsilon,{\mathbf{g}}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})]_{j}\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})-1.

(3.4)

When
\nu
is uniform, by
Lemma

7
, the above
\chi^{2}
divergence is also equal to the rescaled squared norm of the semidual gradient.
From equation
3.4
, we propose in equation
3.5
an unbiased estimator using a batch of i.i.d. samples
\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{B}\sim\mu
that can be computed in
O(NB)
time.

shows that we are able to effectively track
{\widehat{\chi}}^{2}
as it decays towards zero along SGD updates.

Convergence Analysis.

Studying the case
\varepsilon=0

Assumption 1
.

Suppose
c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=-\mathbf{x}^{T}\mathbf{y}
,
\delta\coloneqq\min_{j\neq j^{\prime}}\|\mathbf{y}_{j}-\mathbf{y}_{j^{\prime}}\|>0
, and
\mu

Score Estimation and Guidance

A key property that connects flow matching and diffusion models is that learning the marginal velocity field under independent coupling and Gaussian noise is equivalent to estimating the marginal score along the probability path. Namely, let
\rho_{t}
denote the law of
\bm{X}_{t}=(1-t)\bm{X}_{0}+t\bm{X}_{1}
, where
\bm{X}_{0}\sim\rho_{0}=\mu
and
\bm{X}_{1}\sim\rho_{1}=\nu
independently. Recall that,

\nabla\log\rho_{t}(\mathbf{x})={\mathbb{E}}[\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{t}}\log\rho_{t|1}(\bm{X}_{t}\,|\,\bm{X}_{1})\,|\,\bm{X}_{t}=\mathbf{x}]=\tfrac{t{\bm{v}}_{t}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{x}}{1-t}.

(4.1)

Note that the above is Tweedie’s formula since the flow matching velocity that transports
\rho_{0}
to
\rho_{1}
satisfies
(1-t){\bm{v}}_{t}(\mathbf{x})={\mathbb{E}}[\bm{X}_{1}-\bm{X}_{t}\,|\,\bm{X}_{t}=\mathbf{x}]
. While equation
4.1
relates score and velocity, the second equality above relies on the independence of
\bm{X}_{0}
and
\bm{X}_{1}
; it may no longer hold under general couplings.
Surprisingly, we find that for SD-OT couplings equation
4.1
still holds approximately for
\varepsilon\approx 0
.

Proposition 3

(Generalized Tweedie’s Formula)
.

For any
{\mathbf{g}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
, let
\bm{X}_{0},\bm{X}_{1}\sim\pi_{\varepsilon,{\mathbf{g}}}
, let
\rho_{t}
denote the density of
\bm{X}_{t}=(1-t)\bm{X}_{0}+t\bm{X}_{1}
for
t<1
, and define
{\bm{v}}_{t}(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{X}_{1}-\bm{X}_{0}\,|\,\bm{X}_{t}=\mathbf{x}]
.
Suppose
\rho_{0}=\mathcal{N}(0,{\bm{I}}_{d})
. Then,

\nabla\log\rho_{t}(\mathbf{x})=\tfrac{t{\bm{v}}_{t}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{x}+(1-t)\bm{\delta}_{\varepsilon}}{(1-t)^{2}}

holds for any
\varepsilon\geq 0
, where

\bm{\delta}_{\varepsilon}=\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}{\mathbb{E}}\left[-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{0}}c(\bm{X}_{0},\bm{X}_{1})+{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{0}}c(\bm{X}_{0},\bm{X}_{1})\,|\,\bm{X}_{0}]\,|\,\bm{X}_{t}=\mathbf{x}\right]

for
\varepsilon>0
,
and
\bm{\delta}_{0}={\bm{0}}
.

For small
\varepsilon

\varepsilon\rightarrow 0

becomes increasingly determined by

Correcting the Guidance.

To see a direct application of Proposition

(\rho_{1,t})_{t=0}^{1}

Proposition 4

(Informal)
.

Let
(\bm{X}^{(i)}_{0})_{i=1}^{r}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\rho_{0}

\mathrm{d}\bm{X}^{(\gamma,i)}_{t}=\{\gamma{\bm{v}}_{1,t}(\bm{X}^{(\gamma,i)}_{t})+(1-\gamma){\bm{v}}_{2,t}(\bm{X}^{(\gamma,i)}_{t})\}\mathrm{d}t,\quad\forall i\in[r].

\operatorname{Law}(\bm{X}_{t})\to\rho^{(\gamma)}_{t}

Limitations and Discussion

Some aspects of the SD-FM pipeline presented here leave room for improvement.
Figure
2
shows that computing the SD-OT potential to a reasonable degree of precision impacts the performance of SD-FM.

Figure 6
:
FID on ImgN-256 vs. NFE for mean-flows.

That SD-OT preprocessing cost occurs only once prior to FM training; it only depends on the noise distribution and data, and is typically much cheaper than the FM training cost itself.
However, if SD-FM were to be applied to datasets of billions
N
of points, this could become a challenge (though still less so than FM training).
Because SD-OT is a concave maximization problem, one could leverage recipes such as batching, momentum or
\varepsilon
-tempering.
For
\varepsilon>0
, categorical sampling over a
N
-sized softmax vector would be costly, which is why we highlighted the
\varepsilon=0
case that can leverage MIPS and fast retrieval tools (both to train
\mathbf{g}
and to pair fresh noise).
Finally, having in mind dataloader efficiency, one may want to flip the tables in our sampling approach: Start from the
j
-th data point in memory, and match it with a Gaussian noise restricted
(
Wu and Gardner 2024
)
to be in its Laguerre cell
\mathcal{L}_{j}=\{\mathbf{x}:\mathbf{x}^{T}(\mathbf{x}^{(j)}_{1}-\mathbf{x}_{1}^{(k)})+g_{j}-g_{k}\geq 0,\forall k\neq i\}
.
While we have shown successful applications of the SD coupling preprocessing approach to extended FM methods such as mean-flow or guidance, SD couplings could be of course used
within
even more complex approaches building upon FM, such as Reflow. Our focus was on ablating the impact of the SD coupling over the independent coupling in the most widely used FM setup. Assessing how SD couplings interact with more advanced or orthogonal methods is left for future work.

Conclusion

OT-FM can been used to guide the training of flow models by optimally pairing batches of
n
noise
n
data points, with results only truly paying off for very large
n
. We proposed the more cost-efficient semidiscrete OT paradigm, building on a two-step approach (fit potential vector as a one-off cost, assign noise when training through a MIPS) that is not bottlenecked by a batch size
n
. Our experiments in various settings, both in unconditioned and conditioned scenarios, show much improved metrics over I-FM and orders of magnitude cheaper compute compared to OT-FM.

\displaystyle=\int\nabla\log\rho_{t|1}(\mathbf{x}\,|\,\mathbf{x}_{1})\rho_{1|t}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_{1}\,|\,\mathbf{x}).

Now, using the definition
\bm{X}_{t}\coloneqq(1-t)\bm{X}_{0}+t\bm{X}_{1}
and the change of variables formula, we have

\rho_{t|1}(\mathbf{x}\,|\,\mathbf{x}_{1})=\frac{1}{(1-t)^{d}}\rho_{0|1}\left(\frac{\mathbf{x}-t\mathbf{x}_{1}}{1-t}\,|\,\mathbf{x}_{1}\right).

As a result,

\nabla\log\rho_{t|1}(\mathbf{x}\,|\,\mathbf{x}_{1})=\frac{1}{1-t}\nabla\log\rho_{0|1}\left(\frac{\mathbf{x}-t\mathbf{x}_{1}}{1-t}\,|\,\mathbf{x}_{1}\right).

Consequently,

x\nabla\log\rho_{t}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{1-t}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{0}}\log\rho_{0|1}(\bm{X}_{0}\,|\,\bm{X}_{1})\,|\,\bm{X}_{t}=\mathbf{x}\right].

Let us define
\nu(\mathbf{X}_{1})=b_{I}
and
g(\mathbf{X}_{1})=g_{I}
where
I
is the index of the random vector
\mathbf{X}_{1}
, i.e.
\mathbf{X}_{1}=\mathbf{x}^{(I)}_{1}
.
Recall that for
\varepsilon>0
, the coupling is given by

\rho_{0,1}(\bm{X}_{0},\bm{X}_{1})=\frac{e^{\frac{g(\bm{X}_{1})-c(\bm{X}_{0},\bm{X}_{1})}{\varepsilon}}}{\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{1}}e^{\frac{g(\mathbf{x}_{1})-c(\bm{X}_{0},\mathbf{x}_{1})}{\varepsilon}}\nu(\mathbf{x}_{1})}\mu(\bm{X}_{0})\nu(\bm{X}_{1}).

Thus, for
\mu=\mathcal{N}(0,{\bm{I}}_{d})
, we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{0}}\log\rho_{0|1}(\bm{X}_{0}\,|\,\bm{X}_{1})

\displaystyle=-\bm{X}_{0}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{0}}c(\bm{X}_{0},\bm{X}_{1})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\cdot\sum_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{1}}\frac{\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{0}}c(\bm{X}_{0},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{1})e^{\frac{g(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{1})-c(\bm{X}_{0},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{1})}{\varepsilon}}\nu(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{1})}{\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{1}}e^{\frac{g(\mathbf{x}_{1})-c(\bm{X}_{0},\mathbf{x}_{1})}{\varepsilon}}\nu(\mathbf{x}_{1})}

\displaystyle=-\bm{X}_{0}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{0}}c(\bm{X}_{0},\bm{X}_{1})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{0}}c(\bm{X}_{0},\bm{X}_{1})\,|\,\bm{X}_{0}\right].

Plugging this back into equation
A.1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\nabla\log\rho_{t}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=-\frac{{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{X}_{0}\,|\,\bm{X}_{t}=\mathbf{x}]}{1-t}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon(1-t)}{\mathbb{E}}\left[-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{0}}c(\bm{X}_{0},\bm{X}_{1})+{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_{0}}c(\bm{X}_{0},\bm{X}_{1})\,|\,\bm{X}_{0}]\,|\,\bm{X}_{t}=\mathbf{x}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{-{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{X}_{0}\,|\,\bm{X}_{t}=\mathbf{x}]+\bm{\delta}_{\varepsilon}}{1-t}.

Moreover, since the optimal velocity field in flow matching is given by the expectation of the conditional velocity field
(
Lipman et al. 2023
)
, we have

{\bm{v}}_{t}(\mathbf{x})={\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{\bm{X}_{t}-\bm{X}_{0}}{t}\,|\,\bm{X}_{t}=\mathbf{x}\right]=\frac{\mathbf{x}-{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{X}_{0}\,|\,\bm{X}_{t}=\mathbf{x}]}{t}.

Combining the two identities above finishes the proof for the
\varepsilon>0
case.

For the
\varepsilon=0
case, we define
\bm{T}_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{0})=\argmax_{\mathbf{x}_{1}\in\{\mathbf{x}^{(i)}_{1}\}_{i=1}^{N}}g(\mathbf{x}_{1})-c(\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{x}_{1})
, where we can arbitrarily break ties, by e.g. picking the maximizer with the smallest index. Then, with probability 1 over the draw of
\bm{X}_{0}
, the samples
\bm{X}_{0},\bm{X}_{1}\sim\rho_{0,1}
are given by
\bm{X}_{1}=\bm{T}_{1}(\bm{X}_{0})
.
As a result, we can write

\bm{X}_{t}=\bm{T}_{t}(\bm{X}_{0})\coloneqq(1-t)\bm{X}_{0}+t\bm{T}_{1}(\bm{X}_{0}).

Recall that by Brenier’s theorem,
\bm{T}_{1}
can be written as a gradient of a convex function, therefore
\bm{T}_{t}
is strictly monotone and we can write
\bm{X}_{0}=\bm{T}_{t}^{-1}(\bm{X}_{t})
for all
t<1
. Further, we observe that
\bm{T}_{1}(\bm{X}_{0})
is a piecewise constant function, thus its Jacobian is
{\bm{0}}
almost everywhere, i.e. we have
D\bm{T}_{t}(\bm{X}_{0})=(1-t){\bm{I}}
almost surely, where
D
denotes Jacobian.

For any
\mathbf{x}_{t}
, define
\mathbf{x}_{0}=\bm{T}_{t}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_{t})
. We can now use the change of variables formula

\rho_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})=\frac{\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{0})}{\left\lvert\det D\bm{T}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\right\rvert}=\frac{\rho_{0}(\bm{T}_{t}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_{t}))}{(1-t)^{d}}.

As a result,

\displaystyle\nabla\log\rho_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})=D\bm{T}_{t}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_{t})\nabla\log\rho_{0}(\bm{T}_{t}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_{t}))

\displaystyle=(D\bm{T}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{0}))^{-1}\nabla\log\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-t}\nabla\log\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathbf{x}_{0}}{1-t},

where we plugged in
\rho_{0}=\mu=\mathcal{N}(0,{\bm{I}}_{d})
in the last step. Furthermore, since
\bm{X}_{t}
is
\bm{X}_{0}
-measurable, we have

{\bm{v}}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{t})={\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{\bm{X}_{t}-\bm{X}_{0}}{t}\,|\,\bm{X}_{t}=\mathbf{x}_{t}\right]=\frac{\mathbf{x}_{t}-\bm{T}_{t}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_{t})}{t}=\frac{\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mathbf{x}_{0}}{t}.

Combining the above equations completes the proof.
∎

The following is the formal statement of
Proposition

4
.

Proposition 5
.

Suppose
{\bm{v}}_{1,t}
and
{\bm{v}}_{2,t}
generate the probability paths
\rho_{1,t}
and
\rho_{2,t}
respectively. Let

{\bm{v}}^{(\gamma)}_{1,t}(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\gamma{\bm{v}}_{1,t}(\mathbf{x})+(1-\gamma){\bm{v}}_{2,t}(\mathbf{x}),

and define
\psi^{(\gamma)}_{t}(\mathbf{x})
as the solution of the ODE
\frac{\mathrm{d}\psi^{(\gamma)}_{t}(\mathbf{x})}{\mathrm{d}t}={\bm{v}}^{(\gamma)}_{t}(\mathbf{x})
with the initial condition
\psi^{(\gamma)}_{0}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}
, i.e.
\psi^{(\gamma)}_{t}(\mathbf{x})
is the position of a particle initialized from
\mathbf{x}_{0}=\mathbf{x}
, moving along the velocity field
{\bm{v}}^{(\gamma)}_{t}
. Define the weight

w_{t}(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\gamma(\gamma-1)\int_{s=0}^{t}\big({\bm{v}}_{1,s}(\psi^{(\gamma)}_{s}(\mathbf{x}))-{\bm{v}}_{2,t}(\psi^{(\gamma)}_{s}(\mathbf{x}))\big)^{T}\big(\nabla\log\rho_{1,s}(\psi^{(\gamma)}_{s}(\mathbf{x}))-\nabla\log\rho_{2,s}(\psi^{(\gamma)}_{s}(\mathbf{x}))\big)\mathrm{d}s.

Then, for any test function
\phi:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
,

\int\phi(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\rho^{(\gamma)}_{t}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\int e^{w_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\phi(\psi^{(\gamma)}_{t}(\mathbf{x}))\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})}{\int e^{w_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})}.

Before presenting the proof of
Proposition

5
, we comment on its informal interpretation,
Proposition

4
. Let
\rho^{(r)}_{0}
denote the stochastic empirical law of
k
i.i.d. random vectors
(\mathbf{X}^{(i)}_{0})_{i=1}^{r}
from
\rho_{0}
.
Then, if
\rho_{0}
is sufficiently concentrated, we have almost surely for all test functions

\frac{\int e^{w_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\phi(\psi^{(\gamma)}_{t}(\mathbf{x}))\mathrm{d}\rho^{(r)}_{0}(\mathbf{x})}{\int e^{w_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\mathrm{d}\rho^{(r)}_{0}(\mathbf{x})}\to\frac{\int e^{w_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\phi(\psi^{(\gamma)}_{t}(\mathbf{x}))\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})}{\int e^{w_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})}

(A.2)

as
r\to\infty
. Define
\rho^{(r,\gamma)}_{t}
as the measure that satisfies

\int\phi(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\rho^{(r,\gamma)}_{t}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\int e^{w_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\phi(\psi^{(\gamma)}_{t}(\mathbf{x}))\mathrm{d}\rho^{(r)}_{0}(\mathbf{x})}{\int e^{w_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\mathrm{d}\rho^{(r)}_{0}(\mathbf{x})},

for all test functions
\phi
. Then, sampling from
\rho^{(r,\gamma)}_{t}
is exactly equivalent to drawing from
(\psi^{(\gamma)}_{t}(\mathbf{X}^{(i)}_{0}))_{i=1}^{r}
according to (unnormalized) weights
e^{w_{t}(\mathbf{X}^{(i)}_{0})}
.
Finally, as
r\to\infty
, we have

\int\phi(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\rho^{(r,\gamma)}_{t}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\int e^{w_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\phi(\psi^{(\gamma)}_{t}(\mathbf{x}))\mathrm{d}\rho^{(r)}_{0}(\mathbf{x})}{\int e^{w_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\mathrm{d}\rho^{(r)}_{0}(\mathbf{x})}\to\frac{\int e^{w_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\phi(\psi^{(\gamma)}_{t}(\mathbf{x}))\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})}{\int e^{w_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})}=\int\phi(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\rho^{(\gamma)}_{t}(\mathbf{x})

as
r\to\infty
. Therefore,
\rho^{(r,\gamma)}_{t}
converges almost surely, weakly to
\rho^{(\gamma)}_{t}
. This is the sense in which the convergence of
Proposition

4
holds. We keep the argument informal since we do not study the exact concentration properties required for equation
A.2
to hold.

To prove
Proposition

5
, we recall this result about the solution of an advection-reaction PDE.

Lemma 6
.

Suppose
(\rho_{t})_{t\geq 0}
satisfies

\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=-\nabla\cdot(\rho_{t}{\bm{v}}_{t})+\rho_{t}(\alpha_{t}-\int\alpha_{t}\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}),

(A.3)

where
{\bm{v}}_{t}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
\alpha_{t}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
. Let
\psi_{t}(\mathbf{x})
be the solution to the ODE
\frac{\mathrm{d}\psi_{t}(\mathbf{x})}{\mathrm{d}t}={\bm{v}}_{t}(\psi_{t}(\mathbf{x}))
with initial condition
\psi_{0}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}
. Further define

w_{t}(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\int_{s=0}^{t}\alpha_{s}(\psi_{s}(\mathbf{x}))\mathrm{d}s.

(A.4)

Then, for any
t\geq 0
and any test function
\phi:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
,

\int\phi(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\int e^{w_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\phi(\psi_{t}(\mathbf{x}))\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})}{\int e^{w_{t}}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})}.

(A.5)

Proof.

We proceed by differentiating equation
A.5
against time to obtain equation
A.3
. We have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\int\phi(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(\mathbf{x})=

\displaystyle\frac{\int e^{w_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\langle\nabla\phi(\psi_{t}(\mathbf{x})),{\bm{v}}_{t}(\psi_{t}(\mathbf{x}))\rangle\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})}{\int e^{w_{t}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})}}+\frac{\int e^{w_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\alpha_{t}(\psi_{t}(\mathbf{x}))\phi(\psi_{t}(\mathbf{x}))}{\int e^{w_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})}

\displaystyle-\frac{\int e^{w_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\phi(\psi_{t}(\mathbf{x}))\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})}{\int e^{w_{t}}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})}\cdot\frac{\int e^{w_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\alpha_{t}(\psi_{t}(\mathbf{x}))\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})}{\int e^{w_{t}(\mathbf{x})}\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\langle\nabla\phi(\mathbf{x}),{\bm{v}}_{t}(\mathbf{x})\rangle\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(\mathbf{x})+\int\phi(\mathbf{x})\left(\alpha_{t}(\mathbf{x})-\int\alpha_{t}\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}\right)\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(\mathbf{x}),

where the last equality holds by the change of variables formula. This is exactly the weak sense of equation
A.3
.
∎

As a result, to prove
Proposition

5
, we only need to show that
\rho^{(\gamma)}_{t}
satisfies a PDE of the form equation
A.3
.

Proof of
Proposition

5
.

Let
\tilde{\rho}^{(\gamma)}_{t}\coloneqq\rho_{1,t}^{\gamma}\rho_{2,t}^{1-\gamma}
be the unnormalized measure, and recall
Z^{(\gamma)}_{t}=\int\tilde{\rho}^{(\gamma)}_{t}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}
is the normalizing constant. We have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\tilde{\rho}^{(\gamma)}_{t}=

\displaystyle\gamma\rho_{1,t}^{\gamma-1}\rho_{2,t}^{1-\gamma}\frac{\partial_{t}\rho_{1,t}}{\partial t}+(1-\gamma)\rho^{\gamma}_{1,t}\rho_{2,t}^{-\gamma}\frac{\partial\rho_{2,t}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\gamma\rho_{1,t}^{\gamma-1}\rho_{2,t}^{1-\gamma}\nabla\cdot(\rho_{1,t}{\bm{v}}_{1,t})-(1-\gamma)\rho_{1,t}^{\gamma}\rho_{2,t}^{-\gamma}\nabla\cdot(\rho_{2,t}{\bm{v}}_{2,t})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot(\tilde{\rho}^{(\gamma)}_{t}{\bm{v}}^{(\gamma)}_{t})+\gamma(\gamma-1)\tilde{\rho}^{(\gamma)}_{t}\langle{\bm{v}}_{1,t}-{\bm{v}}_{2,t},\nabla\log\rho_{1,t}-\nabla\log\rho_{2,t}\rangle,

where we recall
{\bm{v}}^{(\gamma)}_{t}=\gamma{\bm{v}}_{1,t}+(1-\gamma){\bm{v}}_{2,t}
, and the third identity follows from the product rule
\nabla\cdot(m{\bm{v}})=\nabla m\cdot{\bm{v}}+m\nabla\cdot{\bm{v}}
. Define

\alpha_{t}(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\gamma(\gamma-1)\langle{\bm{v}}_{1,t}(\mathbf{x})-{\bm{v}}_{2,t}(\mathbf{x}),\nabla\log\rho_{1,t}(\mathbf{x})-\nabla\log\rho_{2,t}(\mathbf{x})\rangle.

Then,

\partial_{t}\rho^{(\gamma)}_{t}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{Z^{(\gamma)}_{t}}\partial_{t}\tilde{\rho}^{(\gamma)}_{t}-\frac{\rho^{(\gamma)}_{t}}{Z^{(\gamma)}_{t}}\partial_{t}Z^{(\gamma)}_{t}.

Note that
Z^{(\gamma)}_{t}
is constant in
\mathbf{x}
, and
\partial_{t}\int\rho^{(\gamma)}_{t}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=0
. Therefore,

\partial_{t}Z^{(\gamma)}_{t}=\partial_{t}\int\tilde{\rho}^{(\gamma)}_{t}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=Z^{(\gamma)}_{t}\int\alpha_{t}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(\mathbf{x}).

Therefore,

\partial_{t}\rho^{(\gamma)}_{t}=-\nabla\cdot(\rho^{(\gamma)}_{t}{\bm{v}}^{(\gamma)}_{t})+\rho^{(\gamma)}_{t}\left(\alpha_{t}-\int\alpha_{t}\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}\right).

We have obtained our desired PDE, which concludes the proof.
∎

A.2
Properties of the Semidual Loss

Since we are interested in the semidiscrete case, the optimal transport plan always admits a density with respect to the product measure
\mu\otimes\nu
, regardless of entropic regularization.
Therefore, we restrict the optimization problem to those that admit a density, and abuse the notation by using
\pi
as the density of a coupling with respect to
\mu\otimes\nu
.
Recall
\mathrm{d}\pi_{\varepsilon,{\mathbf{g}}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j})=s_{\varepsilon,{\mathbf{g}}}(\mathbf{x})_{j}
. Therefore, we can rewrite
{\mathcal{C}}_{c,\varepsilon}(\pi)
as

{\mathcal{C}}_{\varepsilon}(\pi_{\varepsilon,{\mathbf{g}}})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j})s_{\varepsilon,\mathbf{g}}(\mathbf{x})_{j}\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{x})+\varepsilon\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int s_{\varepsilon,{\mathbf{g}}}(\mathbf{x})_{j}\log\left(\frac{s_{\varepsilon,{\mathbf{g}}}(\mathbf{x})_{j}}{b_{j}}\right)\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{x}).

We first relate the dual objective
F_{\varepsilon}
to the distance between
\nu_{\varepsilon}
and
\nu
.

Lemma 7
.

For any
{\mathbf{g}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
and
\varepsilon\geq 0
, we have
\nabla F_{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{g}})=\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{m}({\mathbf{g}})
(we use subgradient for
\varepsilon=0
). As a result,

\chi^{2}(\mathbf{m}({\mathbf{g}})\,\|\,\mathbf{b})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{b_{j}}(\partial_{g_{j}}F_{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{g}}))^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{TV}(\mathbf{m}({\mathbf{g}}),\nu)=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\nabla F_{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{g}})\right\rVert_{1}.

In particular, for
\mathbf{b}={\bm{1}}_{N}/N
we have
\chi^{2}(\nu_{\varepsilon}[{\mathbf{g}}]\,\|\,\mathbf{b})=N\|\nabla F_{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{g}})\|^{2}.

Recall the Pinsker inequality
\mathrm{TV}\leq\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{KL}}
and the fact that
\mathrm{KL}\leq\log(1+\chi^{2})
. Thus we can always upper bound total variation using the chi-squared divergence.

Proof of
Lemma

7
.

Note that the Chi-Squared and total variation bounds follow immediately from

\partial_{g_{j}}F_{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{g}})=b_{j}-m({\mathbf{g}})_{j},

(A.6)

since then

\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{b_{j}}(\partial_{g_{j}}F_{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{g}}))^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\frac{m({\mathbf{g}})_{j}}{b_{j}}\right)^{2}b_{j}-1=\chi^{2}(\mathbf{m}({\mathbf{g}})\,\|\,\mathbf{b}),

and

\left\lVert\nabla F_{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{g}})\right\rVert_{1}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\lvert m({\mathbf{g}})_{j}-b_{j}\right\rvert=2\mathrm{TV}(\mathbf{m}({\mathbf{g}}),\mathbf{b}).

Thus, we turn to showing equation
A.6
. For
\varepsilon>0
,

\partial_{g_{j}}F_{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{g}})=b_{j}-\frac{e^{\frac{g_{j}-c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j})}{\varepsilon}}b_{j}}{\sum_{k}e^{\frac{g_{k}-c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{k})}{\varepsilon}b_{k}}}=b_{j}-\int s_{\varepsilon,{\mathbf{g}}}(\mathbf{x})_{j}\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{x})=b_{j}-m({\mathbf{g}})_{j}.

In fact, one could show the above identity directly using duality and the envelope theorem, as
\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{m}({\mathbf{g}})={\bm{0}}
is a constraint of the minimization problem.

For
\varepsilon=0
, we similarly have

\partial_{g_{j}}F_{0}({\mathbf{g}})=b_{j}-\int\frac{\mathds{1}[j\in\argmax_{l}g_{l}-c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{l})]}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathds{1}[k\in\argmax_{l}g_{l}-c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{l})]}\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{x})=b_{j}-\int s_{0,{\mathbf{g}}}(\mathbf{x})_{j}\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{x}),

which completes the proof.

∎

The following allows us to turn an approximate stationary point (maximizer) of the dual into an approximate optimal transport plan.

Lemma 8
.

For any
{\mathbf{g}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
we have
{\mathcal{C}}_{\varepsilon}(\pi_{\varepsilon,{\mathbf{g}}})\leq{\mathcal{C}}^{*}_{\varepsilon}+\left\lVert{\mathbf{g}}\right\rVert\left\lVert\nabla F_{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{g}})\right\rVert
.

Proof.

Note that since
\nu
is a discrete measure, any coupling with the correct marginals admits a density with respect to
\mu\otimes\nu
. Hence, we can use the change of variables
\mathrm{d}\pi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{x})
. Then, we can write the OT cost as

{\mathcal{C}}^{*}_{c,\varepsilon}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{x})\geq{\bm{0}}\\
\int\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{b}\\
\sum_{j=1}^{N}s(\mathbf{x})_{j}=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int s(\mathbf{x})_{j}\left[c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j})+\varepsilon\log\left(\frac{s(\mathbf{x})_{j}}{b_{j}}\right)-\varepsilon\right]\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{x})+\varepsilon,

.

When
\varepsilon>0
, we can drop the constraint
\pi\geq 0
as it is satisfied by entropy regularization. The Lagrangian is then given by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{s},f,{\mathbf{g}})\coloneqq

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int s(\mathbf{x})_{j}\left[c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j})+\varepsilon\log\left(\frac{s(\mathbf{x})_{j}}{b_{j}}\right)-\varepsilon\right]\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{x})+\varepsilon

\displaystyle+\int f(\mathbf{x})\Big(1-\sum_{j=1}^{N}s(\mathbf{x})_{j}\Big)\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{x})+\Big\langle{\mathbf{g}},\mathbf{b}-\int\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{x})\Big\rangle.

Maximizing over
\mathbf{s}
yields
s(\mathbf{x})_{j}=e^{(f(\mathbf{x})+g_{j}-c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j}))/\varepsilon}
, and leads to the following dual problem

\max_{f,g}{\mathcal{D}}_{\varepsilon}(f,{\mathbf{g}})\coloneqq\int f(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{x})+\langle{\mathbf{g}},\mathbf{b}\rangle-\varepsilon\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int\exp\Big(\frac{f(\mathbf{x})+g_{j}-c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j})}{\varepsilon}\Big)b_{j}\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{x})+\varepsilon,

This dual is maximized by

f_{\mathbf{g},\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}_{0})=-\varepsilon\log\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp\Big({\frac{g_{j}-c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j})}{\varepsilon}}\Big)b_{j}.

Note that at
f=f_{\mathbf{g},\varepsilon}
,
s_{f,{\mathbf{g}},{\mathbf{g}}}
coincides with
s_{\varepsilon,{\mathbf{g}}}
of equation
3.2
.
The semidual is defined as
F_{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{g}})\coloneqq\max_{f}{\mathcal{D}}_{\varepsilon}(f,{\mathbf{g}})
, and given by

F_{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{g}})=\langle{\mathbf{g}},\mathbf{b}\rangle-\varepsilon\int\Big[\log\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp\Big(\frac{g_{j}-c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j})}{\varepsilon}\Big)b_{j}\Big]\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{x}).

for
\varepsilon>0
. Thus, we derived the semidual equation

S
ε

for
\varepsilon>0
.
Importantly, one can verify

F_{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{g}})={\mathcal{L}}_{c,\varepsilon}(\mathbf{s}_{\varepsilon,{\mathbf{g}}},f_{\mathbf{g},\varepsilon},{\mathbf{g}})={\mathcal{C}}_{\varepsilon}(\pi_{\varepsilon,{\mathbf{g}}})+\Big\langle{\mathbf{g}},\mathbf{b}-\int\mathbf{s}_{\varepsilon,{\mathbf{g}}}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{x})\Big\rangle.

(A.7)

We now turn to the case of
\varepsilon=0
. The Kantorovich dual in this case is given by

\max_{\{f,g\,:\,f\oplus g\leq c\}}{\mathcal{D}}_{0}(f,g)\coloneqq\int f(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{x})+\langle{\mathbf{g}},\mathbf{b}\rangle

when
\varepsilon=0
, where
f\oplus g\leq c
means
f(\mathbf{x})+g_{j}\leq c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j})
for
(\mu\otimes\nu)
-a.e.
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j})
. This is achieved by

f_{\mathbf{g},0}(\mathbf{x})=\min_{j}c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j})-g_{j}.

This yields the semidual

F_{0}({\mathbf{g}})=\langle{\mathbf{g}},\mathbf{b}\rangle+\int[\min_{j}c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j})-g_{j}]\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{x}).

We once again derived the semidual equation

S
ε

.
Using the definition of
\pi_{0,{\mathbf{g}}}
, we have

\displaystyle F_{0}({\mathbf{g}})

\displaystyle=\langle{\mathbf{g}},\mathbf{b}\rangle+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int[c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j})-g_{j}]s_{0,{\mathbf{g}}}(\mathbf{x})_{j}\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j})s_{0,{\mathbf{g}}}(\mathbf{x})_{j}\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{x})+\Big\langle\mathbf{g},\mathbf{b}-\int\mathbf{s}_{0,{\mathbf{g}}}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{x})\Big\rangle.

Once again, we obtain

F_{0}({\mathbf{g}})={\mathcal{C}}_{0}(\pi_{0,{\mathbf{g}}})+\Big\langle{\mathbf{g}},\mathbf{b}-\int\mathbf{s}_{\varepsilon,{\mathbf{g}}}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{x})\Big\rangle,

i.e. we can extend equation
A.7
to
\varepsilon=0
. By
Lemma

7
, for any
\varepsilon\geq 0
we have

\nabla F_{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{g}})=\mathbf{b}-\int\mathbf{s}_{\varepsilon,{\mathbf{g}}}\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{x}_{0}).

The above formula directly implies the total variation bound.
Therefore,

F_{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{g}})={\mathcal{C}}_{\varepsilon}(\pi_{\varepsilon,{\mathbf{g}}})+\langle{\mathbf{g}},\nabla F_{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{g}})\rangle.

By duality, we additionally have
F_{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{g}})\leq{\mathcal{C}}^{*}_{\varepsilon}
. Combined with the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, we obtain

{\mathcal{C}}_{\varepsilon}(\pi_{\varepsilon,{\mathbf{g}}})\leq{\mathcal{C}}^{*}_{\varepsilon}+\left\lVert{\mathbf{g}}\right\rVert\left\lVert\nabla F_{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{g}})\right\rVert
, completing the proof.
∎

A.3
Convergence of SGD

We begin by recalling the following classical result from convex optimization, and we include its proof for completeness.

Proposition 9
.

Suppose
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a stochastic convex function with subgradient
\nabla f
, and there exists a constant
G
such that
{\mathbb{E}}[\|\nabla f({\mathbf{g}})\|^{2}]\leq G^{2}
for all
{\mathbf{g}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
.
Define
F:{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\to{\mathbb{R}}
via
F(\cdot)={\mathbb{E}}[f(\cdot)]
, where expectation is over the stochasticity of
f
.
Let
{\mathbf{g}}^{*}
denote any minimizer of
F
, and
F^{*}=F({\mathbf{g}}^{*})
.
Let
\{{\mathbf{g}}_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T-1}
denote the iterates of SGD with the sequence of step size
\{\eta_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T-1}
. Define
\bar{{\mathbf{g}}}_{T}=\frac{\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_{t}{\mathbf{g}}_{t}}{\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_{t}}
. Then,

{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\mathbf{g}}_{t}-{\mathbf{g}}^{*}\|^{2}]\leq\|{\mathbf{g}}_{0}-{\mathbf{g}}^{*}\|^{2}+G^{2}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_{t}^{2},\quad\forall t\in[T].

If additionally
F
is
L
-smooth, i.e.
\|\nabla^{2}F\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq L
. Then

\frac{\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_{t}{\mathbb{E}}[\|\nabla F({{\mathbf{g}}}_{t})\|^{2}]}{\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_{t}}\leq\frac{F({\mathbf{g}}_{0})-F^{*}}{\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_{t}}+\frac{\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_{t}^{2}LG^{2}}{2\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_{t}}.

Proof.

By the convexity of
F
, for any
{\mathbf{g}}
we have
F^{*}\geq F({\mathbf{g}})+\nabla F({\mathbf{g}})^{T}({\mathbf{g}}^{*}-{\mathbf{g}})
. With this property, for any
l
we can write

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\mathbf{g}}_{l+1}-{\mathbf{g}}^{*}\|^{2}\,|\,{\mathbf{g}}_{t}]

\displaystyle=\|{\mathbf{g}}_{t}-{\mathbf{g}}^{*}\|^{2}-2\eta_{l}{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla f({\mathbf{g}}_{l})\,|\,{\mathbf{g}}_{l}]^{T}({\mathbf{g}}_{l}-{\mathbf{g}}^{*})+\eta_{t}^{2}{\mathbb{E}}[\|\nabla f({\mathbf{g}}_{l})\|^{2}\,|\,{\mathbf{g}}_{l}]

\displaystyle=\|{\mathbf{g}}_{l}-{\mathbf{g}}^{*}\|^{2}-2\eta_{l}\nabla F({\mathbf{g}}_{l})^{T}({\mathbf{g}}_{l}-{\mathbf{g}}^{*})+\eta_{l}^{2}{\mathbb{E}}[\|\nabla f({\mathbf{g}}_{l})\|^{2}\,|\,{\mathbf{g}}_{t}]

\displaystyle\leq\|{\mathbf{g}}_{l}-{\mathbf{g}}^{*}\|^{2}+2\eta_{l}(F^{*}-F({\mathbf{g}}_{l}))+\eta_{l}^{2}{\mathbb{E}}[\|\nabla f({\mathbf{g}}_{l})\|^{2}\,|\,{\mathbf{g}}_{l}].

Taking an expectation over
{\mathbf{g}}_{l}
, we obtain

{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\mathbf{g}}_{l+1}-{\mathbf{g}}^{*}\|^{2}]\leq{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\mathbf{g}}_{l}-{\mathbf{g}}^{*}\|^{2}]-2\eta_{l}{\mathbb{E}}[F^{*}-F({\mathbf{g}}_{l})]+\eta_{l}^{2}G^{2}.

By summing both sides from
l=0
to
t-1
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\mathbf{g}}_{t+1}-{\mathbf{g}}^{*}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\|{\mathbf{g}}_{0}-{\mathbf{g}}^{*}\|^{2}+\sum_{l=0}^{t-1}\eta_{l}{\mathbb{E}}[F^{*}-F({\mathbf{g}}_{l})]+G^{2}\sum_{l=0}^{t-1}\eta_{l}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|{\mathbf{g}}_{0}-{\mathbf{g}}^{*}\|^{2}+G^{2}\sum_{l=0}^{T-1}\eta_{l}^{2},

which proves the first inequality.

For the second inequality, we start with the smoothness lemma

F({\mathbf{g}}_{t+1})\leq F({\mathbf{g}}_{t})+\nabla F({\mathbf{g}}_{t})^{T}({\mathbf{g}}_{t+1}-{\mathbf{g}}_{t})+\frac{L}{2}\left\lVert{\mathbf{g}}_{t+1}-{\mathbf{g}}_{t}\right\rVert^{2}.

Taking expectation conditioned on
{\mathbf{g}}_{t}
, we obtain

{\mathbb{E}}[F({\mathbf{g}}_{t+1})\,|\,{\mathbf{g}}_{t}]\leq F({\mathbf{g}}_{t})-\nabla\left\lVert\nabla F({\mathbf{g}}_{t})\right\rVert^{2}+\frac{\eta_{t}^{2}L}{2}{\mathbb{E}}[\left\lVert\nabla f({\mathbf{g}}_{t})\right\rVert^{2}\,|\,{\mathbf{g}}_{t}].

Taking another expectation, we obtain

{\mathbb{E}}[F({\mathbf{g}}_{t+1})]-{\mathbb{E}}[F({\mathbf{g}}_{t})]\leq-\eta_{t}{\mathbb{E}}[\left\lVert\nabla F({\mathbf{g}}_{t})\right\rVert]^{2}+\frac{\eta_{t}^{2}LG^{2}}{2}.

By rearranging the terms, summing over
t
from
0
to
T-1
, and noticing that
F^{*}\leq{\mathbb{E}}[F({\mathbf{g}}_{T})]
, we obtain

\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_{t}{\mathbb{E}}[\left\lVert\nabla F({\mathbf{g}}_{t})\right\rVert^{2}]\leq F({\mathbf{g}}_{0})-F^{*}+\frac{1}{2}LG^{2}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_{t}^{2},

which completes the proof.
∎

To apply
Proposition

9
, we need to estimate the smoothness constant of the dual, achieved by the following lemma.

Lemma 10
.

Consider the semidual
F_{\varepsilon}
defined in equation

S
ε

.
For any cost
c:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and any
\varepsilon>0
, we have
\left\lVert\nabla^{2}F_{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 1/\varepsilon
.
Furthermore, if
c(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=-\mathbf{x}^{T}\mathbf{y}
and
\mu
admits a density, then for
\varepsilon=0
we have
\left\lVert\nabla^{2}F_{0}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 2C^{\mathrm{max}}_{\mu}/\delta
, where
\delta=\min_{i\neq j}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}^{(i)}_{1}-\mathbf{x}^{(j)}_{1}\right\rVert
, and
C^{\mathrm{max}}_{\mu}
is the maximum
\mu
-surface area of any convex set in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

7
, we have

\nabla F_{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{g}})=\mathbf{b}-\int\mathbf{s}_{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{g}},\mathbf{x}_{0})\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{x}_{0}),

where we notice that for
\varepsilon=0
we can write

s_{0,{\mathbf{g}}}(\mathbf{x}_{0})_{i}=\mathds{1}[g_{i}+\mathbf{x}^{T}\mathbf{y}_{i}\geq g_{j}+\mathbf{x}^{T}\mathbf{y}_{j},\,\forall j],

since
\mu
admits density, and consequently, ties in the maximum occur with probability zero.
Thus, for
\varepsilon>0
, by a direct calculation we obtain

\nabla^{2}F_{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{g}})=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int[\mathbf{s}_{\varepsilon,{\mathbf{g}}}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{s}_{\varepsilon,{\mathbf{g}}}(\mathbf{x})^{T}-\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{s}_{\varepsilon,{\mathbf{g}}}(\mathbf{x}))]\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{x}).

Using this calculation, one immediately obtains
\left\lVert\nabla^{2}F_{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
, which in fact does not depend on the cost function (the argument above holds for any other cost
c
).

For
\varepsilon=0
, by
Kitagawa et al. 2019
, we have

\nabla^{2}F_{0}({\mathbf{g}})={\bm{W}}-\mathrm{diag}({\bm{d}}),

where
d_{i}=\sum_{j\neq i}W_{ij}

W_{ii}=0,\quad\mathrm{and},\quad W_{ij}=\frac{1}{\left\lVert\mathbf{y}_{i}-\mathbf{y}_{j}\right\rVert}\int_{A({\mathbf{g}},i)\cap A({\mathbf{g}},j)}\mu(\mathbf{x}_{0})\mathrm{d}S(\mathbf{x}),\forall i\neq j

where
A({\mathbf{g}},i)=\{\mathbf{x}:g_{i}+\mathbf{x}^{T}\mathbf{y}_{i}\geq g_{j}+\mathbf{x}^{T}\mathbf{y}_{j},\forall j\}
and
\mathrm{d}S
denotes the
d-1
-dimensional surface measure in a Euclidean space. Note that
-\nabla^{2}F_{0}
is the Laplacian of a weighted graph with weights
W_{ij}\geq 0
, therefore
\left\lVert\nabla^{2}F_{0}({\mathbf{g}})\right\rVert\leq\max_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j\neq i}W_{ij}
.
Further,

\sum_{j\neq i}W_{ij}=\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{1}{\left\lVert\mathbf{y}_{i}-\mathbf{y}_{j}\right\rVert}\int_{A({\mathbf{g}},i)\cap A({\mathbf{g}},j)}\mu(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}S(\mathbf{x})\leq\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{\partial A({\mathbf{g}},i)}\mu(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}S(\mathbf{x})\leq\frac{C^{\mathrm{max}}_{\mu}}{\delta},

which finishes the proof.
∎

.

Appendix C
Experiment details

C.1
Semidiscrete optimal transport

To solve for the semidiscrete dual potential
\mathbf{g}
in practice, we implement Algorithm
1
using JAX and utilize built-in data parallelism to scale to handle large datasets over multiple GPUs and nodes. Since the problem equation

S
ε

is concave (and strictly, for
\varepsilon>0
)
one can use a variety of different optimizers and obtain a suitable convergence behavior.
We use the dot-product cost and scale
\varepsilon
with the standard deviation of the cost, as proposed by
Zhang et al. 2025
. In the class-conditional setting, we choose
\beta=10^{2}
for ImageNet-32 and
\beta=2\times 10^{2}
for ImageNet-64 and PetFace.

In all cases, we opt to use AdaGrad with a constant learning rate for
200\,000
iterations followed by inverse-square root decay for an additional
100\,000
iterations and perform iterate averaging over the final
50\,000
iterates. To choose the learning rate, we notice that typically
\left\lVert{\mathbf{g}}\right\rVert
is of order
\sqrt{N}
while
\left\lVert\nabla F_{\varepsilon}({\mathbf{g}})\right\rVert
is of order
1/\sqrt{N}
. Thus, we expect
L
, Lipschitz constant of the gradient, to be of the order
1/N
, which is smaller than the conservative estimation
L_{\varepsilon}
used in
Theorem

2
.
As
Theorem

2
suggests a learning rate that scales with
\sqrt{1/L}
(the typical scaling under bounded gradient norm) , we find
\sqrt{N}
to be a suitable heuristic for setting the learning rate. As
N
for our datasets is of order
10^{6}
, we use
10^{3}
as the initial learning rate to optimize the potential.
Once computed, the optimal dual potential
\mathbf{g}^{\star}
can be cached and reused. Optimal dual potentials for our ImageNet and PetFace experiments will be made public upon publication.

To estimate
\chi^{2}(\mathbf{m}({\mathbf{g}})\,\|\,\mathbf{b})
, we use a total of
2^{20}
noise samples, which we divide into batches of size
2^{13}
that can further be sharded along multiple devices, and average equation
3.5
over these batches. For the ImageNet-32x32 dataset, this calculation takes less than 2 minutes on a node of 8 H100 GPUs.
