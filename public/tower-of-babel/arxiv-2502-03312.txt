An ‘Experimental Mathematics’ Approach to Stolarsky Interspersions via Automata Theory
Jeffrey Shallit

Abstract
We look at the Stolarsky interspersions (such as the Wythoff array) one more time, this time using tools from automata theory. These tools allow easy verification of many of the published results on these arrays, as well as proofs of new results.



1
Introduction

Let the Fibonacci numbers be denoted, as usual, by

F_{1}=1,\ F_{2}=1,\ \text{and $F_{n}=F_{n-1}+F_{n-2}$ for $n\geq 3$}.

We can generalize this sequence by
allowing two arbitrary starting values, to get the
generalized Fibonacci numbers:

F^{a,b}_{1}=a,\ F^{a,b}_{2}=b,\ \text{ and
$F^{a,b}_{n}=F^{a,b}_{n-1}+F^{a,b}_{n-2}$ for $n\geq 3$.}

(1)

These sequences have been studied for at least 120 years
[
23
]
and
probably longer. As Tagiuri observed, a simple induction gives a
useful formula for the
n
’th term of a generalized Fibonacci sequence
in terms of the ordinary Fibonacci sequence.

F^{a,b}(n)=aF_{n-2}+bF_{n-1}

(2)

for
n\geq 3
.

Several authors have studied
Stolarsky interspersions
,
which are two-dimensional arrays

{\bf A}=(A_{i,j})_{i,j\geq 1}

of positive integers, strictly increasing across rows and down
columns. The
first row consists of the Fibonacci numbers
(F_{n})_{n\geq 1}
,
and each succeeding row is a generalized Fibonacci sequence. The
term
A_{i,1}
in the first column is defined to be
the smallest positive integer that appeared in no preceding row.
More formally, for a set
S\subsetneq\mathbb{N}
, we define
the
\mex
function (
m
inimal
ex
cluded element)

\mex(S)=\min\{x\,:\,x\not\in S\};

then

A_{i,1}:=\mex(\{A_{i^{\prime},j}\,:\,1\leq i^{\prime}<i\text{ and }j\geq 1\}).

(3)

It remains to define the elements in the second column. In the most
general case,
A_{i,2}
is a function of
i
and
A_{i,1}
:

A_{i,2}=f(i,A_{i,1})
. For the time being, we will consider the simpler case where

f
does not depend on
i
, and write instead
A_{i,2}=f(A_{i,1})

for all
i\geq 1
. We will return to the more general case
in Section
8
.
All successive terms in the

i
’th row follow the generalized Fibonacci sequence rule:

A_{i,n}=A_{i,n-1}+A_{i,n-2}
for
n\geq 3
. Thus the arrays
we study are completely determined by specifying the function
f
.

We use the notation
\alpha=(1+\sqrt{5})/2
and

\beta=(1-\sqrt{5})/2
.
Arrays fitting this description include

(a)

the Wythoff array, where
f(n)=\lfloor\alpha n-\beta\rfloor
;

(b)

the Stolarsky array, where
f(n)=\lfloor\alpha n+{1\over 2}\rfloor
; and

(c)

the dual Wythoff array, where
f(n)=\lfloor\alpha n+\beta^{2}\rfloor
.

These arrays can have many interesting properties,
and are the subject of many papers;
for example, see

[
1
,
2
,
4
,
5
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
15
,
22
]
.

Proofs of these properties, however, are often somewhat tedious, involving
complicated arguments
and/or lengthy inductions. In this paper I consider the kinds of things
that can be proven using a more ‘experimental mathematics’ approach.
In this approach we “guess” finite automata that can compute the
arrays, and then
rigorously
verify their correctness using
a decision procedure from first-order logic,
as implemented in the free software
Walnut
. Indeed, it often
suffices to merely state what it is you want to prove, and
Walnut

can produce a rigorous proof or disproof in a matter of seconds.
With this technique we can often, with very little work,
find explicit formulas that required
rather involved arguments. We also solve an open question
of Kimberling from 1994, in Theorem
8
.

2
Zeckendorf representation

Recall that every non-negative integer
n
has a unique representation as
a sum of distinct Fibonacci numbers, subject to the constraint that
no two consecutive Fibonacci numbers are used.
This is called the Fibonacci or Zeckendorf representation

[
14
,
24
]
. We can associate
a representation

n=\sum_{1\leq i\leq t}a_{i}F_{t+2-i}

with a binary string
a_{1}\cdots a_{t}
. Provided the leading
digit
a_{1}
equals
1
, and
(a_{i},a_{i+1})\not=(1,1)
for

1\leq i<t
, this gives a unique way to associate positive
integers with binary strings. We write
(n)_{F}=a_{1}\cdots a_{t}

for this unique binary string
a_{1}\cdots a_{t}
, and the inverse map

[a_{1}\cdots a_{t}]_{F}=\sum_{1\leq i\leq t}a_{i}F_{t+2-i}
.
The representation for
0
is
\epsilon
, the empty string.
Sometimes it is useful to allow leading zeros in these binary strings;

101,0101,00101,\ldots
, etc., are all considered to be the same
representation for the integer
4
.

3
Automata, logic, and
Walnut

A finite automaton is a simple model of a computer. It consists of a finite
set of states, a distinguished initial state, a set of final (or accepting)
states, and labeled transitions between states. An input string
w
is
said to be
accepted
if reading the symbols of
w
, starting in the
initial state, leads to a final state; otherwise it is
rejected
.
For more information about this model, see, for example,

[
6
]
. Finite automata are often depicted
as diagrams where states are represented by circles, transition by
labeled arrows, and final states by double circles.

A finite automaton
M
defines a subset
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}
by letting

S
consist of the integers whose Zeckendorf representation
is accepted by
M
. For example, Figure
1
depicts an automaton
that accepts the set of odd natural numbers.

Figure 1:
Automaton accepting the set of odd natural numbers.

Finite automata can also be used to compute relations.
The way to do this is to allow transitions labeled with pairs of letters,
instead of just single letters. Thus an input can be thought of as
representing a pair of natural numbers
(x,y)
being read
in parallel by the automaton, where the Zeckendorf
representation of
x
corresponds to the first components of the input
and that of
u
to the second components. Since we are reading

x
and
y
in parallel, we may have to allow leading zeros so that
both inputs are of the same length. If for each
x
there is exactly
one
y
such that
(x,y)
is accepted, then we can think of the automaton
as (implicitly) computing a function
f
defined by
f(x)=y
.
For example, Figure
2
depicts an automaton computing
the function
f(x)=2x+1
.

Figure 2:
Automaton for the function
f(x)=2x+1
.

In the analogous fashion we can define a function of two variables,

z=f(x,y)
, with triples of inputs, etc.

Now we turn to introducing
Walnut
. This is a free
open-source software package that can rigorously prove or disprove
first-order logical statements about integer sequences and sets generated
by finite automata. The domain of variables is assumed to be

\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
, the non-negative integers.

Walnut
uses a shorthand which is easy, but takes some getting
used to. First, the universal quantifier
\forall
is written

A
and the existential quantifier
\exists
is written

E
. Logical AND is written
&
; logical OR is written

|
; logical NOT is written
~
; logical
implication is written
=>
; logical IFF is written

<=>
.

The command
def
defines an automaton for later use, while

eval
evaluates
a first-order expression with no free variables and returns

TRUE
or
FALSE
.
The command
reg
allows the user to specify an automaton through a regular
expression. (For more about regular expressions, see

[
6
]
.)

For example, here is the
Walnut
command that
computes the automaton in Figure
1
.

def odd "?msd_fib Ek n=2*k+1":

For the automaton in Figure
2
, we use the command

def double1 "?msd_fib z=2*n+1":

Notice that this expression
z=2*n+1
has two free variables,

z
and
n
, so the produced automaton has transitions on pairs
of letters. The order of inputs is alphabetical order on the
variable names when the command is defined; in this example,

n
is spelled out by the first coordinates of inputs and

z
is spelled out by the second coordinates.

Lemma 1
.

Suppose
(a)_{F}=x
and
(b)_{F}=x0
. Then
(a+b)_{F}=x00
.

Proof.

We first build a ‘shift’ automaton that accepts inputs
x
and

y
iff
y=x0
. We can do this in
Walnut
with a regular
expression:

reg shift msd_fib msd_fib "([0,0]|[0,1][1,1]*[1,0])*":

This produces the two-state automaton in Figure
3
.

Figure 3:
Automaton for the shift.

Now we verify that if
y=x0
and
z=y0
then
z=x+y
.

eval lem4 "?msd_fib Ax,y,z ($shift(x,y) & $shift(y,z)) => z=x+y":

And
Walnut
returns
TRUE
. The theorem is proved.
∎

Lemma 2
.

Let
a,b
be integers with
a\geq 0
and
b=\lfloor\alpha a\rfloor+\epsilon

for
\epsilon\in\{0,1\}
. Define the generalized Fibonacci
sequence
F^{a,b}
by (
1
). Then

(F^{a,b}(n))_{F}=(F^{a,b}(3))_{F}\,0^{n-3}

(4)

for
n\geq 3
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that
(F^{a,b}(4))_{F}=(F^{a,b}(3))_{F}\,0
, because then
(
4
) follows by induction from Lemma
1
.
We define
b=\lfloor\alpha a\rfloor+\epsilon
,

c=a+b
, and
d=b+c
.
Then we need to show that
(d)_{F}=(c)_{F}\,0
.

eval fab "?msd_fib Aa,b,c,d,x ($phin(a,x) & (b=x|b=x+1) & c=a+b
& d=b+c) => $shift(c,d)":

And
Walnut
returns
TRUE
.
Here
phin
is an automaton of two inputs
n
and
x
that
accepts if and only if
x=\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor
.
It can be found, for example, in
[
19
, p. 278]
.
∎

It would be very nice if there were automata taking three inputs

i,j,x
in parallel for which
A_{i,j}=x
for the arrays
\bf A

we discuss here. (Indeed, this nice property is true for the arrays in

[
20
]
.) Unfortunately, for the arrays we discuss
in this paper, this is not possible, because
while columns grow linearly, rows grow exponentially, and this violates
a growth rate condition on synchronized sequences

[
18
]
. Depending on the choice of
f
, we can (in some cases)
find automata computing the
i
’th element of any individual column

A_{i,n}
. For the arrays we discuss in this paper, it turns out
that this is enough!
Lemma
2
ensures that there is an
an integer
n_{0}
such that the set
S
of all
values appearing in columns
\geq n_{0}
are expressible in a simple
way from those in column
n_{0}
, and hence (depending on
f
) there can
be an automaton accepting
S
. A similar result holds
for all columns of even (resp., odd) index.
As we will see, this suffices to prove
many existing theorems about the arrays
\bf A
.

For more about
Walnut
, see
[
16
,
19
]
and
the web page

https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~shallit/walnut.html
.

4
The experimental approach to Stolarsky interspersions

The experimental approach to Stolarsky interspersions involves seven steps.

1.

Compute many terms of the first column of the array
{\bf A}=(A_{i,j})_{i,j\geq 1}

from its definition. In practice, for the arrays we discuss here,
500 to 5000 terms seem sufficient.

2.

Use a procedure based on a variation of the Myhill-Nerode theorem to
“guess” an automaton
M
computing a function
i\rightarrow A^{\prime}_{i,1}

matching the known data; see
[
19
, §5.6]
. We now hope
that
A^{\prime}_{i,1}=A_{i,1}
, but of course this has to be rigorously verified!

3.

Verify, using
Walnut
,
that
M
truly does compute a function
A^{\prime}_{i,1}
(that is, for every
i\geq 1

the automaton accepts exactly one input of the form
(i,x)
).

4.

Verify that
A^{\prime}_{i,1}
is strictly increasing as a function of
i
.

5.

Compute, using
Walnut
,
an automaton for
A^{\prime}_{i,2}=f(A^{\prime}_{i,1})
and

A^{\prime}_{i,3}=A^{\prime}_{i,1}+A^{\prime}_{i,2}
.

6.

From this, using
Lemma
2
, compute an automaton accepting the Zeckendorf representations
of the set
S:=\{A^{\prime}_{i,j}\,:\,i\geq 1,j\geq 3\}
.

7.

Verify inductively that
M
is correct by proving that

A^{\prime}_{i,1}=\mex(\{A^{\prime}_{i^{\prime},j}\,:\,i^{\prime}<i,j\geq 1\})
.
Then
A^{\prime}_{i,n}=A_{i,n}
for all
i
and
n=1,2
.

Let us now carry out the seven steps of the experimental approach.

Step 1: We compute the first 500 terms of column 1.

Step 2: These suffice to guess a 10-state automaton

w1

that we hope computes
W_{n,1}
for
n\geq 1
. It is depicted in
Figure
4
.

Figure 4:
Guessed automaton for the function
n\rightarrow W_{n,1}
.

Step 3: We verify that
w1
actually computes a function.
We do this as follows:

eval w1_func1 "?msd_fib An (n>=1) => Ex $w1(n,x)":
eval w1_func2 "?msd_fib ~En,x,y n>=1 & x!=y & $w1(n,x) & $w1(n,y)":

And both return
TRUE
.

Step 4: We verify that
w1
computes a strictly increasing
function. We do this with

eval increasing_w "?msd_fib An,x,y (n>=1 & $w1(n,x) & $w1(n+1,y)) => x<y":

Step 5: We find automata for computing columns 2 and 3 from the definitions:

def fw "?msd_fib Ey $phin(n+1,y) & z+1=y":
def w2 "?msd_fib Ex $w1(i,x) & $fw(x,z)":
def w3 "?msd_fib Ex,y $w1(i,x) & $w2(i,y) & z=x+y":

These have 13 and 18 states, respectively.
The automaton
w2
is depicted in Figure
5
.

Figure 5:
Automaton computing the function
n\rightarrow W_{n,2}
.

Step 6: We compute an automaton testing membership in

S=\{W_{i,j}\,:\,i\geq 1,j\geq 2\}
. To do so, we use
the fact that
(W_{i,j})_{F}=(W_{i,3})_{F}0^{j-3}
. So we first
make an automaton accepting the
set
\{W_{i,3}\,:\,i\geq 1\}
, and then, from that
automaton, we concatenate the language
0^{*}
on its right using
the
concat
command of
Walnut
. The
alphabet

command forces the correct numeration system to be attached
to the result.

def wc3_mem "?msd_fib Ei i>=1 & $w3(i,n)":
reg all0 msd_fib "0*":
concat wcols3 wc3_mem all0:
alphabet wcols msd_fib $wcols3:

Then we form the automaton for
S
by also including the values
of
\{W_{i,2}\,:\,i\geq 1\}
.

def sw "?msd_fib (Ei i>=1 & $w2(i,n)) | $wcols(n)":

Step 7:
Now we are poised to prove that the first column computed by
w1
is
correct, by induction on
i
. The base case,
i=1
, is clear.
We use
Walnut
to check if the “mex” property holds
for rows
1,2,\ldots,i-1
,
then it also holds for row
i
.

It suffices to show
that
W_{i,1}
is the least number not in

T_{i}:=S\,\cup\,\{W_{i^{\prime},1}\,:\,1\leq i^{\prime}<i\}
.

def chk1 "?msd_fib (~$sw(n)) & Aip,x (ip<i & $w1(ip,x)) => n!=x":

def mex "?msd_fib $chk1(i,x) & Ay (y>=1 & $chk1(i,y)) => y>=x":
# check if x is the smallest of all of these
eval chk "?msd_fib Ai,x (i>=2 & $mex(i,x)) => $w1(i,x)":

And
Walnut
returns
TRUE
.
We have now (almost) completed the induction proof that
w1
correctly
computes the array’s first column.
However, there is one small technical point.
The astute reader will notice that the “mex” result proved her is
that
W_{i,1}
is the least number not in

T^{\prime}_{i}:=\{W_{i^{\prime},1}\,:\,1\leq i^{\prime}<i\}\,\cup\,\{W_{j,k}\,:\,j\geq 1,\ k\geq 2\}
, which is slightly different from the definition
(
3
)
mentioned earlier: the set
T^{\prime}_{i}
includes
all
of the elements
in columns
3
and higher, not just those in rows
i^{\prime}<i
. Clearly

T_{i}\subseteq T^{\prime}_{i}
. However, we
now argue this makes no difference. For if
W_{i,1}
does not occur
in
T_{i}
and
does occur in
T^{\prime}_{i}
,
then we would have
W_{i,1}=W_{j,k}

for some
j\geq i
and
k\geq 2
. But this is impossible since
the array
\bf W
is strictly increasing across rows and down columns.
Now the proof is complete.

[

defined the first two columns of the

the definition

We can prove this is equivalent to ours, as follows:

def w1 "?msd_fib Ey $phin(n,y) & $phin(y,z)":

eval check_m1 "?msd_fib An,z1,z2 (n>=1 & $w1(n,z1) & $w2(n,z2))
=> $fw(z1,z2)":

TRUE
.

We can also prove closed forms for columns 1 and 2.

Proposition 3
.

(a)

W_{i,1}=\lfloor\alpha^{2}i\rfloor-1
for
i\geq 1
.

(b)

W_{i,2}=2\lfloor\alpha i\rfloor+i-1
for

.

Proof.

eval prop5a "?msd_fib Ai,x,y (i>=1 & $w1(i,x) & $phi2n(i,y)) => x+1=y":
eval prop5b "?msd_fib Ai,x,y (i>=1 & $w2(i,x) & $phin(i,y)) => x+1=2*y+i":

Another property of this array was proven by Morrison
[
15
]
,
although its statement as written was imprecise.
More precisely stated, it is the following:

Theorem 4
.

\{W_{i,2}-W_{i,1}\,:\,i\geq 1\}=\{W_{j,2k+1}\,:\,j\geq 1\text{ and }k\geq 0\}.

Proof.

To prove the theorem, we construct automata testing membership in
each set, and then check that they accept exactly the same sets.

An automaton for the set on the left is easy to construct:

def left "?msd_fib Ei,x,y $w1(i,x) & $w2(i,y) & z+x=y":

For the set on the right, we use the fact that

(W_{j,\ell})_{F}=(W_{j,3})_{F}0^{\ell-3}
for

\ell\geq 3
. Thus we can construct the set

\{W_{j,2k+1}\,:\,j\geq 1\text{ and }k\geq 0\}

as follows:

def wc3_memb "?msd_fib Ei i>=1 & $w3(i,n)":
reg even0 msd_fib "(00)*":
concat w3even wc3_memb even0:
alphabet w3e msd_fib $w3even:
def right "?msd_fib (Ej j>=1 & $w1(j,n)) | $w3e(n)":

And now we check equality:

eval checkeq "?msd_fib An (n>=1) => ($left(n) <=> $right(n))":

And
Walnut
returns
TRUE
.
∎

Final remarks

In principle,
one can use this method to compute automata and prove theorems about
Stolarsky interspersions based on other functions
f
and other
classification sequences. However, currently we have no proof that,
for example, there will always be an automaton for the first
column of a Stolarsky interspersion with
periodic classification sequence
\delta_{i}
, nor a direct way to
compute the automaton from
\delta_{i}
without “guessing”.
We also do not know how the number of states
grows with the period of the periodic sequence.
It would be interesting to try to prove something about this.

For example, for the classification sequence
defined by
\delta_{i}=1
if
i\equiv 1
(mod
3
), and

0
otherwise, we can get an automaton
k100
for the
first column sequence

1,4,7,9,12,14,17,20,23,25,27,30,33,35,38,40,44,46,49,\ldots

with 87 states. With our method we can prove, for example,
the following result:

One thing I do not currently know how to do is use these kinds of methods
to discuss the so-called “row sequence”, which is sequence

r_{n}
defined by the row number in which
n
in appears, say, in the
Wythoff array. I do not see a way to study this sequence using automata
theory.

One can object that, for example, for the
ESC
array, we have only
verified that the second column is always even, but we have not explained
why. This raises the question of what would be considered a satisfying
answer. Evidently a simple formula of the form
\lfloor\gamma n+\rho\rfloor
for some irrationals
\gamma,\rho
would be acceptable, but
subword complexity shows that
no such formula can work here. We would need something more complicated.
Perhaps the 39-state automaton for
esc1
is going to be the simplest
“formula” we can find. We note that Behrend did not prove any kind
of simple formula for the second column of
ESC
, either.
