Reducing the oil absorption and oil deterioration in fried apple slices by ultrasound integrated in infrared frying
Authors: Ya Su, Shuyi Li, Tiantian Hu, Jieru Zhang, SM Roknul Azam, Aiqing Ren
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe effects of integrated ultrasonic infrared frying (USIF) on the oil absorption of apple slices and the oil deterioration were studied with frequency of 28 and 40 kHz, respectively. Results showed that the heat transfer and moisture migration was accelerated by the integrated ultrasound in IF. The soluble Gal-A content and esterification degree of pectin was increased, the damages of pectin crystal structure and chemical structure in side chain was aggravated. These damages to pectin were intensified with higher frequency (40 kHz) of ultrasound. Lower retention of phenols was found in USIF apple slices, but the flavonoids content had no significant change compared to CF samples. USIF samples showed a smoother morphology, and the pore volume and porosity were reduced by ultrasonication applied with 28 kHz but increased with 40 kHz. The largest volume fraction of pores was changed from 100-250 μm in IF to 0.02–10 μm and 10–100 μm by the integrated ultrasound at 28 kHz and 40 kHz samples, respectively. The total oil uptake in USIF samples was reduced by 24.9 %–33.2 % compared to the conventional fried (CF) samples, and achieved the lowest with the frequency of 40 kHz. The surficial and structural oil were also decreased by 39.2 %-51.3 % and 20.9 %–32.3 %, respectively. The peroxide value, acid value, carbonyl value, polar component, and the saturated fatty acids ratio of oil in repeated frying for 16 h was reduced in USIF, especially with ultrasonication 40 kHz. These results indicate that USIF is a promising method for producing novel low-oil apple fries.Keywords: Ultrasound, Infrared frying, Pectin, Crust, Porous characteristics, Fatty acids composition

Introduction

Frying is one of the most domestic, economical, and convenient cooking methods to produce ready-to-eat fruits and vegetables snacks [1]. During frying process, serious physicochemical reactions occurred in the food material and oil medium to form the attractive appearance, aroma, flavor and texture (crispness), including heat transfer, moisture removal, oil absorption, Maillard reaction, crust formation, texture changes, and lipid oxidation [2]. Nonetheless, excessive fat intake has been reported to increase the risk of chronic disease, which severely interfering with the consumption of fried foods [3]. Therefore, reducing the oil content and caloric density of fried fruits and vegetables is still an urgent need for commercial application of innovative technologies and methods [4]. Infrared frying (IF) is a new frying technique that uses infrared radiation as a heating resource with increased thermal efficiency and improved quality characteristics compared to conventional frying (CF) [5]. However, considering the limitation of the penetration depth of infrared radiation, the crust formation enhances at early stage of frying would hinder the migration of internal water and increase the internal pressure gradient, and even cause large holes and cracks in the surface, which would lead to an increase of oil absorption and reduction of frying efficiency [6]. Therefore, improving the frying efficiency and quality while reducing the oil absorption has become a challenge for the application of infrared frying in high efficiency and low-fat fried fruits and vegetables in industrial settings.

Ultrasonics (20 kHz-10 MHz) are mechanical waves that can cause vibration, cavitation, and mechanical effects in the medium when passing through food materials, creating intercellular spaces, micro-channels, and micro-jets on the cell structure, thus improving processing efficiency and quality of food [7], [8]. Recently, ultrasound has attracted the top interest as it has the potential to significantly increase evaporation efficiency and reduce oil absorption in fried tissue applied either in pre-treatment or in frying process as an external physical field [9], [10]. In the current literatures, it has been widely believed that the mechanism of reducing oil absorption by ultrasound is the formation of intercellular spaces or microchannels in the cell wall, however, the effect of ultrasound on the crust or porous structures has not been quantitatively measured and has not been adequately analyzed [11]. Pectin polysaccharides (pectin) are one of the major components found in the cell wall middle lamella of fruits and vegetables, which are inter- and intra- molecularly crosslinked to maintain the integrity of the cell wall matrix [12]. Additionally, nutrients such as polyphenols in plant cells are also connected to pectin through chemical bonding, so pectin is an critical substance that affects the cells structural integrity and the physicochemical properties of fruit and vegetable, especially in fruits such as apples with pronounced cellular structures [12], [13]. In view of the oil absorption mechanism of fried food, the characteristics of porous structures, including morphology and pore distribution, was considered as decisive factors in oil absorption [14].The integrity of the pectin structure in the cell wall plays an important role in the formation and characteristics of porous structure of fried fruits and vegetables, as well as the hydrolysis and oxidation of oil at high temperature with the dissolution of moisture and component from the cell tissue [13]. Therefore, the influence of integrated ultrasound on the oil absorption and oil deterioration in infrared fried apple slices can be revealed considering changes in pectin structures.

Ultrasonic frequency and power are two key parameters to measure its performance. From a macro point of view, the power density determines the intensity and penetration capacity of ultrasound, while the frequency determines the penetration depth of the ultrasonic wave and the destructive effect on the cell structure [15]. A large number of studies have evaluated the influence of ultrasonic power on the quality of fried products, however, the application effect of different ultrasonic frequencies in the frying process has not been reported. In this study, the effects of integrated ultrasonic on the oil absorption and oil deterioration in infrared fried apple slices were investigated with a respective ultrasonic frequency of 28 and 40 kHz, since this is the frequency of commercial applications in the food industry. The damages in cell structure in USIF apple slice were comparatively analyzed with pectin structure changes and the heat transfer and moisture removal process. The total content of phenols and flavonoid in fried apple slices was determined to confirm the destructions on pectin structure. The oil absorption was quantitatively measured and qualitatively characterized with the determination of both total oil content and oil fractions. The modifications in crust characteristics of were described to reveal the oil absorption. In addition, the oil deterioration was evaluated with lipid oxidation index and fatty acid composition.

Materials and methods

Raw material

Fresh apples (Malus domestica Borkh. cv. Red Fuji) with the same ripeness level (diameter between 70 and 75 mm) were purchased from Shaanxi province, China, and were stored in the refrigerator at 4 °C prior to the frying experiment (up to 4 days). The total soluble solids of apples were 12.87 ± 0.23 %, the total acid content (in malic acid) was 4.16 ± 0.12 g/L, and the pH was 4.12. The palm oil used in this study was purchase from Yihai Kerry Company, Shanghai, China. The Nile Red fluoresce was sourced from Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd., Japan. The Folin-Ciocalteu’s phenolic reagent and standard rutin were supplied by Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China. The Sudan I and other reagents (analytical grade) used for the index determinations were obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The fatty acid standards used for the gas chromatography analysis were provided by Millipore Sigma, St. Louis, MO.

Fresh apples were sliced with a thickness of 6.0 ± 0.2 mm and an average weight of 4.2 g. Before frying, the slices were immersed in 5 % citric acid for 5 s and blanched in deionized water at 90 °C for 1 min to inactive the catalase [16]. Then, the slices were cooled using tap water and blot the surface before the frying test.

Frying method

An innovatively designed ultrasonic infrared fryer (USIF) was used for the frying experiment with the schematic diagram specified in Fig. 1(a). The fryer has a tank (280 × 280 × 280 mm) with a volume capacity of 12 L. Two sets of ultrasound transducers (Tianhua Ultrasonic Electronic Instrument Co., Ltd, Jining, China) equipped with a respectively frequency of 28 and 40 kHz and adjustable power levels ranging from 0 to 1000 W, were attached uniformly to the bottom of the frying chamber. The infrared radiation heater was set at 3000 W (220 V) in this study according to our preliminary results. Comparison experiments were conducted using a conventional fryer with a resistance heater (2500 W, 220 V) and a smaller tank size of 150 × 250 × 300 mm for control purposes.

Changes of surface temperature (c), core temperature (d), and moisture content (e) in apple slices during different frying process with the time interval of 2 min. CF: conventional frying. IF: Infrared frying. USIF: Ultrasonic infrared frying.

The palm oil, 7.5 L in total, was heated to 160 °C before frying. Each type of frying method (CF, IF, USIF-28 kHz, and USIF-40 kHz) was tested using identical 50 g apple slices for 2 min. After frying, the samples were cooled on an iron basket sealed in a desiccator at room temperature (25 °C) for later analysis. The oil used for frying was collected every 5 batches (60 min) for a total of 16 h without adding fresh oil, and stored in a refrigerator at 4 °C for analysis of deterioration.

Analysis methods

Temperature changes in oil and samples

The temperature changes of oil heated by each frying method was determined using both the temperature sensor equipped to the fryer and a T-type thermocouple (UT325, UNI-T group Co., Ltd, Guangdong, China). The temperature changes in both the core and surface of apple slices during the frying process was recorded with a T-type thermocouple, following the methodology detailed in our previous research [5]. The heat distribution in the surface of apple slices during different frying procedure was captured every minute with infrared thermal imager (UTi165A, UNI-T group co., Ltd, Guangdong, China).

Moisture content

The moisture content in raw or fried apple slices was determined using the oven-drying method at 102 ± 1°C until constant mass [17]. The moisture content was expressed in g/100 g in de-oiled dry basis.

Extraction and characterization of pectin in fried apple slices

The pectin in raw and fried apple slices was extracted using the acid-soluble method [12]. Briefly, raw apple samples were blanched in boiling water for 4 min to inactive pectinase and fried samples were completely de-oiled with the petroleum ether. After crushing, the pomace was washed twice with 70 % ethanol to remove interfering soluble sugars, organic acid, and partial pigments, and then oven-dried at 50°C. Approximately 4.0 g of the dried sample was re-dissolved in 1 % citric acid at pH 2.3 (w/v = 1/6) with stirring at 50 °C for 2 h. After cooling in an ice bath and centrifugation at 10,733 g for 30 min, the sediment was precipitated in 95 % ethanol and vacuum filtered with 400 mL deionized water, then vacuum-dried until the weight remained constant.

Galacturonic acid (Gal-A) content

The galacturonic acid (Gal-A) content was determined using the carbazole colorimetric method [18]. Briefly, 1.0 mL of aqueous pectin solution (100 mg/ L) was mixed with 6 mL of H2SO4 solution (0.5 M) and incubated in a water bath at 75 ℃ for 20 min. After cooling to room temperature, the mixture was treated with 0.2 mL of carbazole ethanol solution (0.15 %), vortexed, and incubated in the dark for 2 h. The Gal-A content (%) was calculated by spectrophotometry at absorbance 525 nm referring to the calibration curve (y = 0.0040 A + 0.0901, R2 = 0.9952) established using standard Gal-A (0–120 mg/L) [18].

Degree of esterification (DE)

The DE content of pectin was detected by the potentiometric-titration method as specified by Zhong et al. [19].

Crystalline structure analysis

The crystalline structure of pectin was evaluated with a powder X-ray diffractometer (D/max-r C, Rigaku, Japan) with Cu-Ka radiation at 30 mA and 40 kV [19]. XRD scanning was performed with a diffraction angle (2θ) of 5-60° at 6°/min and a scan step of 0.02°.

Fourier transform-infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy analysis

The chemical structural changes in pectin were analyzed by Fourier transform-infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR, Vertex 70, Bruker Banner Lane, Coventry, Germany) from 400 to 4000 cm−1 with a resolution of 4 cm−1 of 64 scans [20].

Total phenolic and flavonoid contents

Firstly, the flavonoids and phenols in the fried apple slices were extracted using 80 % ethanol with a volume ratio of 1:100 (w/v) after de-oiled with petroleum ether, then homogenized and filtered with nylon (0.45 μm) [16]. Briefly, extractions (200 μL) were incubated with 400 μL Folin-Ciocalteu’s phenolic reagent and 4 mL distilled water at 25 °C for 3 min. Then, the solution was stand for 1 h in the dark after adding with 2 mL of 5 M Na2CO3 solution. The total phenolic content (TPC) (mg GAE/100 g dry basis of de-oiled samples) was spectrophotometric determined at 765 nm with a standard curve (y = 12.0410 A + 0.0154, R2 = 0.9996) established with gallic acid hydrate [21].

To determine the total flavonoid content (TFC), 2.0 mL of the extraction was treated with 0.12 mL of NaNO2 (1 M) for 6 min, followed by the addition of 0.12 mL of AlCl3 (2 M) and 1.5 mL of NaOH (1 M). The mixture was then diluted to a final volume of 7.5 mL with distilled water and incubated in the dark for 15 min. The TFC was calculated using spectrophotometric analysis at 510 nm with a standard curve (y = 1.8315 A-0.0164, R2 = 0.9988) generated from freshly prepared rutin solutions [21].

Pore properties

The pore properties of fried apple slices were analyzed using mercury intrusion porosimetry (Poremaster GT-60, Quantachrome Instruments, USA) [22]. Fried samples were sliced into small tube that were less than 10 mm in length and width following the removal of oil and water, and subjected to poremaster with pressures ranging from 0.006 to 69.431 MPa.

Oil absorption and oil fractions

The total oil content (TOC) in fried apple slices was determined using the Soxhlet extraction method with petroleum ether (30-60°) as solvent [23]. The oil fractions, including surface oil (SUOC) and structural oil (STOC) were analyzed using staining method with Sudan I fluoresce according to the report of Zhang et al. [22].

Oil distribution (CLSM)

The oil distribution in fried apple slices was visualized using Ar laser (λ = 488 nm) with an inverted confocal laser scanning microscope (Ti-E-A1R, Nikon Instruments Inc., Japan) [24]. Apple slices were fried in pre-dyed oil with fluorescent probe Nile Red (0.0192 mg/mL) for 12 min by different frying method. The distribution of total oil content was observed in CLSM directly while the distribution of structural oil fractions was captured after being washed in petroleum ether for 1 s. Thirty optical sections were scanned along the axial direction (z-axis) with a step size of 3.4 μm using a 10 × objective (0.45NA). 3D reconstruction images for the oil distribution were composited using the Carl Zeiss LSM software (NIS Elements Viewer 4.2.0 Software, Nikon Instruments Inc.) using the layer scanning pictures, and the relative mean fluorescence intensity (RMFI) in the 3D images of TOC and STOC was quantitatively determined at their respective transmittance.

Microstructure observation（SEM）

The microstructure of fried apple slices was observed and visualized with a scanning electron microscope (SEM, JSM-5610LV, JEOL Corporation, Japan). Fried apple slices were firstly de-oiled and fixed with 2.5 % glutaraldehyde, then spraying coated with gold. Representative photograph was captured with 50 × -200 × magnification using acceleration voltage of 15 kV [5].

Oil oxidation index

The oxidation index of oil, such as peroxide value (POV), acid value (AV), carbonyl value (CV), and polar component (PC) were determined according to National Standard of the People's Republic of China (CNS, GB 5009.168–2016).

Fatty acid composition (Gas chromatography)

Fatty acid composition was analyzed as fatty acid methyl esters according to the AOCS Official Method (external standard method) by gas chromatography (GC-2010 Plus, Shimadzu, Japan) equipped with a polydicyanopropyl siloxane strongly polar stationary phase capillary column (Rt-2560 Capillary Column; 100 m × 250 μm, 0.20 μm, Restek) and a flame ionization detector. Fatty acids were methyl esterified (FAMEs) following the guidelines of the Chinese National Standard (CNS, GB 5009.168–2016). In details, 200 μL of oil was introduced into a test tube with screw top. Then 2 mL of 0.5 M methanolic sodium hydroxide was added, and the mixture was vigorously mixed for 30 min in a water bath at 65 °C·BF3-methanol (v/v, 1:3, 2 mL) reagent was added into the mixture. After being mixed for 10 min in a water bath at 70 °C, 2 mL of hexane was added. Then screw on the top and shake well for 3 min, centrifuge, and remove the upper layer to new tube for further analysis. Every 1.0 μL of FAMEs and mixed standard sample was injected to the gas chromatograph, and the GC analysis parameters included a temperature ramp of the column from 100 to 185 °C at 10 °C/min for 10 min, followed by a ramp to 200 °C at 1 °C/min for 20 min, and a final increase to 230 °C at 4 °C/min for 10.5 min [5]. The detection was performed with the inlet and detector temperatures of 270 °C and 280 °C, respectively, in pure nitrogen flow 1 mL/min and the split ratio was 1:100. The fatty acid methyl esters were identified using their respective standard, and quantitative analyses were presented as relative percentage.

Statistical analysis

Each experiment was performed at least in triplicate, and the data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS for Windows (Version 26.0 SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). Significant variances were determined using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan multiple-range tests at a 95 % confidence level (p < 0.05) for multiple mean comparisons.

Results and Discussion

Heat transfer and moisture removal

To determine the role of ultrasound in the heat transfer and moisture removal during frying process, the temperature changes both in oil and apple samples was monitored. As observed in Fig. 1(b), the utilization of infrared radiation heating significantly accelerated the rising rate of oil temperature. Compared with conventional electric heating, the time for oil to reach the target temperature (160°C) was shortened from 22 min to 16 min, showing a reduction of 28 %. Compared with IF, the synergy of ultrasonic and infrared heating increased the oil temperature by 2-4°C at the same heating time and shortened the time for frying oil to reach the target temperature by 13.3 %. However, the application of ultrasonication at different frequencies has no significant difference in the heating process of frying oil, and the time of oil temperature reaching the target temperature has no significant difference. It indicated that the heating effect of ultrasonic on oil temperature is much smaller compared with that of infrared radiation. The temperature changes in apple slice during frying was presented in Fig. 1(c, d). The temperature in both the surface and core of apple slices rise rapidly at the beginning (within 1 min) of frying due to the heat transfer from oil and additional physical field (infrared radiation and ultrasound) [1]. Specifically, the rising rate of surface temperature in USIF samples showed no significant change compared to that in IF samples, while the rising rate of core temperature in the IF sample was significantly facilitated by the ultrasound, especially at the frequency of 40 kHz. In addition, the heat distribution in USIF apple slices from 2 to 12 min was more uniform than that of CF and IF samples as observed in the infrared thermograms (Figure S1). For the moisture evaporation as observed in Fig. 1(e), the moisture content in apple slices decreased rapidly at the beginning of frying (2 min) and the moisture loss rate gradually decreased with the time, indicating a falling rate dehydration process [25]. The moisture removal of apple slices was facilitated in IF compared to that of CF due to the unique heating efficiency of infrared radiation. The moisture loss rate of USIF samples showed a slight higher than that of IF samples with the application of ultrasound at 28 kHz, however, the moisture loss rate of USIF samples was significantly accelerated when the ultrasonication was applied at 40 kHz. Combining the results of oil temperature, sample temperature, and moisture removal, it can be concluded that the ‘‘vibration effect” or “cavitation effect” is greater than the “heating effect” of the ultrasound contributed most to enhancing the heat transfer and internal moisture migration of apple slices during infrared frying [6].

Structural changes in pectin

Gal-A content and DE of pectin

As observed in Fig. 2 (a), the Gal-A content was significant reduced in each fried sample due to thermal decomposition of pectin [19]. However, the Gal-A content in USIF apple slices obtained a higher retention than that in IF samples, and the USIF-28 kHz samples achieved the highest value. The esterification degree (DE) of apple pectin was determined to be 77.6 %, indicating that it is a high-methoxyl pectin [Fig. 2(b)]. Specifically, the DE of IF apple pectin was significantly (p < 0.05) increased by the assist of ultrasound, and the USIF-28 kHz samples obtained the highest DE value. The cavitation effect of ultrasound would induce micro-channels and destructions on the cell wall, accelerate mass transfer, thus increasing pectin solubilization and Gal-A content [19]. In addition, it has been reported that the breakage of pectin side-chain is promoted by sonication, resulting in an increase in Gal-A-rich homogalacturonan main chains [18]. Meanwhile, the mechanical damages caused by ultrasonication would expose more methyl groups of pectin in heat and oxygen environments, resulting in a higher esterification degree of pectin [26]. The higher Gal-A content and DE value of pectin in USIF samples helped to explain the destructions on cell structure by the assist of ultrasound in frying process from the perspective of chemical structure, which will also lead to modification of porous characteristics after frying.

Crystal structure changes (XRD) of pectin

As illustrated in Fig. 2(c), apple pectin displayed features of A-type crystallinity with a weak characteristic peak at around 14° and strong characteristic peaks located around 18° and 21° in the patterns of X-ray diffraction [12]. While the diffraction peak at 21° in the fried samples weakened and widened, suggesting that the frying process damaged the long-range ordered structure and caused scission of the carbohydrate chain in the pectin [20]. Specifically, the strength of the peaks at 18° in the USIF pectin samples decreased, and the peak patterns became narrower and combined into one peak when compared to the X-ray diffraction patterns of pectin in CF and IF samples. This result revealed that the crystal structure of pectin was further damaged by the integrated ultrasound during the IF process [12]. The larger amorphous pectin area and enhanced solubility also contributed to the smoother appearance of the crust and the greater presence of small pores in the USIF sample.

Chemical structure changes of pectin

As illustrated in Fig. 2(d) and Table S1, a strong and broad absorption band at approximately 3440 cm−1 was observed in FT-IR spectra of all samples, refer to the stretching vibration of intramolecular or intermolecular hydrogen bonds (–OH) of pectin [18]. Compared to raw samples, this absorption band shifted to the right and the absorptance was reduce in all fried samples, suggesting that the pectin’s intramolecular or intermolecular hydrogen bonds were damaged during frying. The characteristic absorption peaks at approximately 2932 cm−1, which assigned to the –CH2- and –CH stretching in the pectin carbohydrate chain, weakened after frying, suggesting that the pectin carbohydrate chain integrity was destroyed by the thermal effect of frying [20], [19]. The absorption peaks at about 1749 and 1631 cm−1 that related to the stretching vibration of esterified (COO-CH3) and free carboxyl (COO–) groups, respectively, weakened in the fried samples due to the methylation of pectin [27]. The peak at around 1440 cm−1 corresponded to the bending vibration of C-OH groups, while signals at approximately 800–1200 cm−1 representing pyranose characteristics with the presence of C-O-C and C-O-H bonds [27]. The majority of the absorption peaks in the FT-IR spectrum of pectin samples are consistent, however, slight variations can be observed in terms of wavenumber and transmittance, indicating the ultrasonication at varying frequency modes has minimal impact on the main chain of pectin, but does affect the side chain [18]. In particular, the highest transmittance of characteristic absorption peaks was observed in the pectin sample fried using USIF-28 kHz, while USIF-40 kHz samples showed lower transmittance compared to samples fried using CF and IF (Table S1). It was suggested that the degree of esterification in the side chain of pectin is higher at ultrasonic frequency 28 kHz, which also agrees with the chemical analysis results.

Total phenolic and flavonoid contents

As can be seen in Fig. 2 (e, f), the TPC of CF apple slices increased by about 15 % compared to the raw samples, while the residual TPC was reduced by approximately 9 %, 3 %, and 5 %, in apple slices fried by IF, USIF-28 kHz, and USIF-40 kHz, respectively. The TFC in apple slices reduced after each frying session, the retention of TFC in IF samples were significantly (p < 0.05) lower than that of CF samples due to accelerated heat transfer. However, the TFC in USIF samples was slightly higher than that of IF samples, showing a reduction by about 43 % compared to the raw apple slices with ultrasonication both at 28 kHz and 40 kHz. The frequency of the ultrasound applied in the IF process had no significant difference in TPC and TFC in this test conditions. During thermal processing of plant food, the phenols and flavonoids would be detached and degraded from the cell matrix accompanied by cell wall destruction and thermal reaction due to the co-exist and interact of polysaccharides and polyphenols [21]. The pectin in and between cell wall was more readily to dissolve and precipitate from the cellular structure with the assist of ultrasound during frying process, which facilitating the disentanglement of the phenolic compounds from the copolymerized state [28], [29]. Besides, the higher heat transfer could compromise the cell integrity of apple slices and inactive presenting catalase, which release and retain more flavonoids from the apple cell matrix [30]. In addition, it was demonstrated that the ultrasonic effects did not cause any significant changes to the phenol structure [31]. Similar results were found in our previous studies that the total anthocyanin content had a higher retention in vacuum fried purple-fleshed potato chips with the assistance of ultrasound [9].

Pore properties

Table 1 indicates that the pore volume and porosity of IF apple slices were lower than those of CF samples, however, both the pore volume and porosity decreased with the assistance of ultrasound at the frequency of 28 kHz. However, the pore volume and porosity of IF apple slices increased with ultrasonication at a higher frequency of 40 kHz. In contrast, the bulk density of the USIF samples treated with ultrasound at 28 kHz was higher than that of both CF and IF samples, while the bulk density was lowest in the samples treated with ultrasound at 40 kHz. It would be attributed to more moisture evaporation at the same frying time due to facilitated heat-mass transfer efficiency with the assist of ultrasound 40 kHz, because the pore structures were formed by dehydration of cell structures [32]. Besides, it also can be explained by the results of Gal-A content and pectin structure changes that the cell structure damage was aggravated with higher ultrasonic frequency (40 kHz) during IF.

Effects of ultrasonic infrared frying on macroscopic pore structure parameters and pore distribution of fried apple slices.

For pore distribution characteristics, pores in CF and IF apple slices with diameters between 100 and 250 μm make up the majority of the total pore volume under these test conditions, as a result of internal vapor expansion and micropore integration [33]. With the assist of ultrasonication, the percentage of pore volume with diameters 100–250 μm decreased significantly while the fraction of pore volume with diameters 10–100 μm and 0.02–10 μm increased significantly in USIF samples. Specifically, the pores with diameters in 0.02–10 μm reached the highest volume fraction in USIF-28 kHz samples and pores with diameters in 10–100 μm obtained the greatest volume ratio in USIF-40 kHz samples, respectively. For the number of pores, in comparison to IF and CF samples, the USIF-28 kHz samples exhibited the highest ratio of pore number fraction of 0.02–1 μm, which accounted for over 99 % of the total pore number. Meanwhile, the pore number fractions of 0.02–1 μm were significantly reduced with the assist of ultrasound. It was found that the expansion of micropores (diameter 10–100 μm) and millipores (diameter less than 10 μm) was primarily caused by ultrasound as an auxiliary tool during infrared frying of apple slices. Furthermore, the application of ultrasonic frequency of 40 kHz in IF apple slices was beneficial for the formation of cracks and channels with larger pore size (10–100 μm), while the assistance of 28 kHz ultrasonic tended to form smaller and denser pores (0.02–10 μm). Compared to potato chips, the cellular structure of apple was more pronounced and the crust characteristic of fried apple slices was more dependent on the cellular and intercellular pectin structure [34]. The modifications in the pore distribution of IF apple slices by ultrasonication would be attributed to the structural changes of pectin.

Oil absorption and oil distribution

Fig. 3(a) shows effects of integrated ultrasound infrared frying on the total oil uptake of apple slices with different ultrasound frequency. The total oil uptake in fried apple slices tended to increase with frying time in every frying procedure. Compared with CF apple slices, the total oil absorption was decreased with the integrated ultrasound and infrared frying. The total oil uptake in fried samples by USIF was reduced by 9.2 %-19.2 % compared to the IF samples after 12 min of frying, and by 24.9 %–33.2 % compared to the CF samples. Lowest oil uptake (36.7 g/100 g, db) was obtained in the samples fried by USIF-40 kHz method. Zhang et al. [35] found that the total oil absorption in fried potato chips is closely related to pore properties, with an increase in total pore volume and porosity, but negative correlate to bulk density. In USIF-28 kHz samples, the total oil absorption of the samples was reduced compared to IF and CF samples probable due to diminished pore volume and porosity by the application of ultrasound. However, the lowest oil absorption was achieved in USIF-40 kHz samples with a higher total pore volume and porosity and a lower bulk density. This may be due to the reduction of the pressure gradient between the crust and the interior of slices by ultrasound during the frying process, through the modification of pore properties and morphology, thus reducing the adhesion of surface oil and the penetration of oil into the interior [36].

Fig. 3(b) and Figure S2 show effects of integrated ultrasound infrared frying on the oil fractions of apple slices with different ultrasound frequency. Both the SUOC and STOC in USIF samples were significantly (p < 0.05) reduced compared to that in IF and CF samples throughout the frying process, reducing about 39.2 %-51.3 % and 20.9 %–32.3 % compared to that of CF samples, respectively. The SUOC and STOC obtained the lowest value in USIF-28 kHz and USIF-40 kHz samples, respectively. It suggested that less oil stick to the surface and penetrates into the porous structure of the fried apple slices with the synergistic effect of ultrasonic and infrared radiation. Their layer scanning images are presented in the Supplementary file Figure S3 and Figure S4, respectively. The red fluorescence indicates the presence of oil, and the density of the fluorescence signal represents the amount of oil distribution. The red fluorescence signal became weaker as depth increased, indicating that the oil was mainly present in the crust and moving towards the inner matrix through cracks and channels in a non-uniform manner. In contrast, CLSM images showed a bright and dense fluorescence signal in IF samples, while the fluorescence signal in USIF samples appeared weaker. The information of relative oil content can be obtained through semi-quantitative analysis (RMFI) by computer software. As presented in Fig. 3(c, d), the RMFI of both TOC and STOC in CLSM images of USIF samples were lower than CF and IF samples, which was consistent with the quantification of oil content and oil fractions. According the report of Zhang et al. [35], there was a negative correlation between the oil content and pores in fried potato chips measuring 0.3–10 μm in diameter. Additionally, it was proposed that the percentage of pores with diameters 10–100 μm dominated the penetrated surface oil, while the percentage of pores with diameters 100–250 μm determines the structural oil in fried potato chips. The decreased total oil absorption in USIF apple slices can be explained by the increase in millipores (diameter less than 10 μm) and reduction in micropores (diameter 100–250 μm), which also contribute to a decrease in the internal pressure gradient, and impedes the absorption of structural oil and the penetration of surface oil during the cooling stage [36]. Similar results were observed by Devi et al. [37] that the oil absorption of mushrooms was reduced by up to 20.3 % when ultrasonic was combined in the microwave-assisted vacuum frying process.

Microstructure observation

As observed in the SEM images [Fig. 3 (e)], the CF apple slices displayed wrinkles, warping, and irregular deformation on their surface, as well as the formation of large pores and cracks caused by the ongoing migration and removal of moisture [37]. However, the IF samples displayed a well-organized and smooth surface morphology, with cells appearing collapsed and less deformed. In comparison, the cell structure of the USIF samples was found to be well-preserved and organized, with a denser and smoother surface morphology than the CF and IF samples. Additionally, more micron-sized pores were visible in the crust. Due to the vibration effect and cavitation effect of ultrasound, micro-channels are generated in the cell structure, which intensives the internal water migration, and thus reduces the large voids and surface structure fractures caused by pressure gradient [21]. Furthermore, the synergistic of ultrasound and infrared increased the solubility of pectin and intensified the disruption of pectin structures, which increased micron-sized pores in the crust [38].

Oil deterioration

Peroxide value (POV)

Because of the oxidation-induced production of peroxides, the POV of the oil increased with frying time in each procedure [Fig. 4 (a)] [39]. The POV of oil fried by IF process increased from 2.43 to 20.84 meq/kg after 16 h of repeated frying and showed no significant difference with that of CF. Notably, the increase of POV in circular frying was significantly delayed in USIF, with the oil achieving the lowest POV value in USIF-40 kHz. Lipid oxidation produces peroxide, an intermediate product with high energy, robust activity, and simple decomposition. During the frying process, peroxide forms and breaks down as a dynamic equilibrium process [40]. The reduced POV in USIF would be attributed that the formation of primary oxidation products (hydroperoxides) was hampered by the ultrasonication. Meanwhile, the conversion of secondary oxidation products (such as hydrocarbons, alcohols, ketones, and aldehydes) from very unstable primary oxidation products was facilitated by the synergy of ultrasound. This may be related to the structural destructions of pectin and the release of more phenols and flavonoids with the ultrasonic cavitation, which would act as antioxidant and reduce the oxidation reaction of oil. Sun et al. [41] also observed that the oil's peroxide value during microwave-assisted vacuum frying was considerably lowered by ultrasonic synergism (300 W and 600 W) at the same batch.

Effects of integrated ultrasonic infrared frying on the peroxide value (POV) (a), acid value (AV) (b), carbonyl value (CV) (c), and polar component (PC) (d) of oil during repeated frying duration of 16 h.

Acid value (AV)

In every process, the frying time of the oil samples led to an increase in their AV [Fig. 4 (b)], but when infrared and ultrasound were used, the rate of increase slowed. Furthermore, the AV of the fried oil in the USIF-40 kHz process increased at the slowest rate during 16 h of frying. Repeated frying causes triglycerides to break down into free fatty acids through thermal oxidation and hydrolysis reactions, which raises the oil's acid value [39]. It suggested that the destructions on the triglycerides by thermal oxidation and hydrolysis during repeated frying was hindered by the integrated ultrasonic and infrared radiation [42].

Carbonyl value (CV)

The increase of CV can be an indicator for the formation of carbonyl compounds and the oxidation deterioration of oil, as observed in Fig. 4 (c), the CV of oil increased with the frying time due to continuous thermal oxidation and hydrolysis of lipid during each procedure. However, the increasing rate of CV in the oil sample fried by USIF was significantly (p < 0.05) reduced in comparison to that by CF during 16 h of repeated frying, and the oil in USIF-40 kHz process had the lowest CV. The carbonyl value of oil refers the quantity of carbonyl compounds (such as ketones and aldehydes) produced during the secondary oxidation [40], [39]. Additionally, referencing the result of POV, it can be inferred that the reduction of the peroxidation value of oil by ultrasonication is mainly due to inhibition of the primary stage of oxidation.

Polar component (PC)

As illustrated in Fig. 4 (d), the PC content of oil gradually increased with the frying time, specifically, the increasing rate of PC was significantly delayed in USIF, reaching 16.52 % and 14.92 % in the USIF-28 kHz, and USIF-40 kHz procedures, respectively, whereas 24.73 % and 20.28 % were achieved for CF and IF after 16 h of frying duration. The PC content of oil fried by USIF also obtained its lowest value using ultrasound at a frequency of 40 kHz. The content of polar component measured in the oil mainly came from aldehydes, ketones, epoxides produced by oxidation, and polymers produced by thermal polymerization, which format by the oxidation, polymerization, hydrolysis and decomposition reaction during the frying process and has higher polarity than triglycerides [40]. It indicated that the formation of oxidation products was inhibited by the integrated ultrasonic infrared frying. Similar affections were found by Sun et al. [41] that the deterioration of oil during microwave-assisted vacuum frying was mitigated by the synergy of ultrasonication with varying power levels. It can be concluded from the results of POV, AV, CV, and PC that the oxidation of oil was reduced by the integrated ultrasound in infrared frying and the ultrasound plays an inhibitory role in the primary stage of oil oxidation. Additionally, it was also noticed that the oil fried by USIF had the lowest oxidation parameters when the ultrasound was performed at the frequency of 40 kHz.

Fatty acid composition

The fatty acids concentration and composition of oils after repeated frying and its gas chromatogram were presented in Table 2 and Fig. S5 and S6, respectively. In the fatty acid composition of fresh palm oil, oleic acid (C18:1, 50.7 %) and palmitic acid (C16:0, 36.5 %) occupied the highest proportion, followed by the linoleic acid (C18:2, 6.3 %), which data was consistent with the previous report [41]. After 16 h of repeated frying, each oil sample showed an increase in saturated fatty acids (SFA) while a decrease in the content of unsaturated fatty acids (USFA) due to oxidation and fragmentation of fatty acids [15]. Compared to CF and IF samples, USIF oil samples had a higher proportion of USFA, and the USFA ratio obtained a highest value when the ultrasound 40 kHz was applied in the USIF process. Oppositely, the saturated fatty acid profile of the USIF oil samples was lower than that of the CF and IF oil samples, particularly with regard to palmitic acid C16:0. The ratio of linoleic acid to palmitic acid (C18:2 / C16:0) was reported to be used to describe the degree of thermal oxidation and hydrolysis of oil [43]. This ratio of fresh oil was decreased by 16.02 %, 24.93 %, 11.05 % and 14.11 % after 16 h of repeated frying in CF, IF, USIF-28 kHz and USIF-40 kHz process, respectively. It demonstrated that the integrated ultrasound and infrared radiation decreased the thermal oxidation and hydrolysis of oil during frying of apple slices. Oil becomes more susceptible to oxidation and thermal degradation when water release from fried food, resulting in the destruction in ester linkages of triacylglycerols production of di- and mono-acylglycerols, glycerol, and free fatty acids. [39]. However, flavonoids and polyphenols released from plant tissues could inhibit the lipid oxidation and thermal degradation through eliminate free radicals by supplying peroxide free radicals and eliminating metal ions as catalysts [13]. The synergistic effect of ultrasound and infrared radiation intensifies the structural damage of pectin and releases more polyphenols and flavonoids during the frying of apple slices, thus reducing the oxidation and deterioration of oil at high temperature. The results of the fatty acid composition matched the previously mentioned chemical analysis results of POV, AV, and CV, which further confirming that the deterioration of oil during long duration of frying was retarded by the integrated ultrasonic infrared frying.

Conclusions

The investigations were conducted to determine how integrated ultrasonic infrared frying affected the oil absorption and oil deterioration of fried apple slices. Results indicated that the assistance of ultrasound improved the infrared fried apple slice's heat transfer and moisture migration, which greater attributing to the ‘‘vibration effect” of ultrasound than the “heating effect”. Ultrasonication significantly increased the soluble Gal-A content and esterification degree of pectin, and aggravated the damages in pectin crystal structure and chemical structure in pectin side chain. These damages to pectin were intensified with higher frequency (40 kHz) of ultrasound used in USIF process. Lower retention of phenols was found in fried apple slices by the integrated ultrasonic infrared frying, while the flavonoids content had no significant change compared with the CF samples. Observation of microstructure showed a smoother surface and more microscopic channels and pores was generated in USIF samples. Both the pore volume and porosity of IF samples were reduced by ultrasonication applied with the frequency of 28 kHz, but the USIF-40 kHz process conducted a higher pore volume and porosity. The volume fraction for pores with diameters of 100–250 μm in IF samples was significantly reduced by the ultrasound, and the largest volume fraction of pores was changed to 0.02–10 μm and 10–100 μm in USIF-28 kHz and USIF-40 kHz samples, respectively. The total oil absorption of USIF apple slices was reduced by approximately 9.2 %-19.2 % compared to the IF samples after 12 min of frying, and by 24.9 %–33.2 % compared to the CF samples. The surface and structural oil were also decreased by 39.2 %-51.3 % and 20.9 %–32.3 % compared to CF sample, respectively. Additionally, lower values of peroxide value (POV), acid value (AV), carbonyl value (CV), polar component (PV) and lower ratio of saturated fatty acids (SFA) was obtained in USIF after 16 h of repeated frying duration, which suggesting that the oil deterioration was slowed down by the integrated ultrasound and infrared frying. Based on these results, integrated ultrasonic infrared frying was proved to be an alternative frying method to produced fried fruits and vegetables with reduced oil content and oil deterioration in repeated long-term frying operations.
