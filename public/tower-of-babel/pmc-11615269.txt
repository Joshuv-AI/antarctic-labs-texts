Biological traits and biome features mediate responses of terrestrial bird demography to droughts
Authors: Lyu Bing Zhang, Zilong Ma, Yang Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract Changing drought regimes are a rising threat to biodiversity, yet their impacts on wildlife vary greatly. Acknowledging the factors associated with these consequences brings novel insights into species vulnerability resulting from extreme climatic events and facilitates effective mitigation of climate change risks.Based on 319 observations from 29 peer‐reviewed studies on birds—a well‐monitored taxonomic group—we extract the responses of demographic metrics to droughts for 204 species across eight terrestrial biomes to examine the consequences of droughts. According to relevant studies, we chose the factors potentially moderating bird demography under droughts and compiled the data for these factors from published datasets. A meta‐analysis is performed to determine the drought effect on bird demography at individual and population levels, accounting for the influence of species traits, timescale and severity of droughts, as well as biome features.The results show that droughts have an overall negative effect on bird demography, and the effect is mediated by different factors at each level. For individuals exposed to droughts, declines in demographic rates are found to be related to narrower extents of occurrence of species, and a significant overall reduction in demographic rates is identified for individuals residing in deserts and xeric shrublands. At the population level, declines in abundance or reproductive performance are generally identified for invertivores, frugivores, nectarivores and omnivores; short‐lived species with small clutch sizes also show greater susceptibilities under the impacts of droughts. Our findings additionally suggest that the demographic vulnerability of bird individuals and populations could be affected by the duration and magnitude of drought episodes.Although our results are subject to publication bias, these conclusions advance the assessment of vulnerability to extreme climatic events that used to be based on equally weighted species traits and support bird conservation by prioritizing the declining populations of species with drought‐susceptible traits. Keywords: abundance, birds, droughts, extreme climatic events, reproductive performance, survival rate

INTRODUCTION

As a manifestation of climate change, significant changes resulting from extreme climatic events (ECEs), such as cyclones, droughts and heatwaves, pose risks to natural systems (The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), 2021). Given the increasing extent, frequency, or magnitude of ECEs worldwide, an expanding body of research is crucial for understanding and assessing the biological impacts of these events in recent years (Foden et al., 2019; Sabater et al., 2023). Biological impacts of ECEs encompass physiological and behavioural responses of organisms (Wingfield et al., 2017), as well as consequences for population persistence (Maxwell et al., 2019), community structure (Jenouvrier et al., 2015) and even speciation processes (Lamichhaney et al., 2016; Marrot et al., 2017). Although extant species have coped with climate variability over millennia, it remains uncertain whether they can continue adapting promptly as ECEs intensify compared with past occurrences (Harris et al., 2018). Examining how wildlife has recently responded to these events could help identify factors associated with their vulnerability under evolving ECE regimes, offering novel insights into ecology in the context of climate change and global biodiversity loss.

Since the 1970s, droughts have become more frequent and severe due to globally increasing temperatures and changing precipitation patterns and are predicted to intensify in many regions of the world toward the end of the 21st century (Spinoni et al., 2020; Trenberth et al., 2014). However, the mechanisms by which wildlife endure changing drought regimes are not fully understood, as responses vary substantially across biomes and biological hierarchical levels (Gu et al., 2023; Schwalm et al., 2017). Studies on birds, a taxonomic group closely monitored worldwide, show that the demographic responses of wild populations to droughts can be associated with species' biological traits. In some cases, bird populations were more likely to diminish if their growth rate had been low (Prugh et al., 2018), or if the populations were strongly dependent on drought‐sensitive resources (Butt et al., 2015; Pearce‐Higgins et al., 2015). Populations of species with varying longevity and migratory strategies have also been found to exhibit different demographic patterns during or after droughts (Albright et al., 2010; Martin & Mouton, 2020). However, it remains uncertain whether these findings on species traits generally apply. Moreover, the magnitude and intensity of ECEs and the features of the environment affected by ECEs, such as plant activities, primary productivity and anthropogenic disturbances, can also affect the vulnerability of wildlife populations to these events (Ameca y Juárez et al., 2012).

Recent studies have demonstrated that there can be distinct response patterns in bird occurrence under short‐ and long‐term droughts (Cady et al., 2019; Cohen et al., 2020), indicating a potential influence of time scale on the populations. At the individual level, droughts were found to lower survival or reproduction rates by interfering with energy allocation (Bourne et al., 2020; Cruz‐McDonnell & Wolf, 2016). Yet, evidence has also shown that lower reproductive performance was associated with greater survival rates of willow flycatchers (Empidonax traillii) during extreme droughts, followed by higher reproductive performance in the subsequent non‐drought year (Theimer et al., 2018). The abundance of some bird populations even increased as interspecific competition weakened during droughts (Albright et al., 2010). These disparities indicate that bird demography may respond to droughts through different mechanisms at individual and population levels.

A better understanding of the factors that mediate the demographic responses of birds to droughts would advance our knowledge of their ability to survive and sustain populations in a drying world. Based on a systematic review of relevant research published worldwide, we tested the following hypotheses: (1) the impacts of drought on bird demography are associated with the severity and timescale of droughts, environmental features of the habitat and biological traits of species and (2) bird responses related to demography at the individual level are mediated by different factors compared to those affecting the population level. The aggregation of published data also allowed us to quantify the extent to which droughts can change the metrics related to the demography of wild birds, and to examine how such effects were moderated by species traits, drought characteristics and habitat features at both individual and population levels. By elucidating these responses, we aimed to identify the major sources of drought vulnerability that could potentially contribute to population die‐offs. Our findings provide new insights into the vulnerability of wildlife populations to ECEs and are expected to facilitate risk assessment of the ecological processes dominated by vulnerable populations.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data collection process

Our study was a global review and was based on a meta‐analysis of secondary data rather than primary data. As such, there was no local data collection, and no ethical approval is needed for this study. Aimed at gathering all studies related to birds and drought conditions, we conducted a comprehensive search of peer‐reviewed studies published prior to December 31, 2023 with the Web of Science Core Collection based on the topics of publications (TS) using strings as follows: TS = (bird* AND arid*) OR TS = (avian AND arid*) OR TS = (bird* AND drought*) OR TS = (avian AND drought*) OR TS = (bird* AND “*long* dry*”) OR TS = (avian AND “*long* dry*”). We also conducted a search with Scopus using the strings: TITLE‐ABS (bird* AND arid*) OR TITLE‐ABS (avian AND arid*) OR TITLE‐ABS (bird* AND drought*) OR TITLE‐ABS (avian AND drought*) OR TITLE‐ABS (bird* AND “*long* dry*”) OR TITLE‐ABS (avian AND “*long* dry*”) (see Table S1 for detailed information on the search terms). We selected publications that explicitly reported the responses of birds to droughts, based on parallel screening conducted by three independent examiners. We then screened full‐length papers for eligibility, selecting studies based on field observations of the demography of specific bird species, with numerical parameters provided to calculate effect sizes (see below). This search yielded a set of publications documenting demographic changes in wild birds that occurred during or after (1) one drought event or (2) several droughts of varying severity. Details of publication selection are reported using a PRISMA plot (Page et al., 2021, Figure 1a); the list of publications included in the meta‐analysis is provided in Appendix 1. In the publications that met our criteria, bird responses related to demography were measured using various metrics at individual or population level. We selected metrics justified by the authors as (1) metrics that were of or related to individual fecundity or survival, such as clutch size, fledgling success and survival rate of certain age groups; (2) proxies of abundance or reproductive performance of a population within a sample (e.g. transect and observation point). All metrics are listed with brief descriptions in Table S2.

We selected potential moderators of demographic responses to droughts, including the timescale and severity of droughts, the terrestrial biome of the study location (Olson et al., 2001) and species traits that potentially mediate these responses (Table 1). To quantify the severity of different observations, we used the Standardized Precipitation Evaporation Index (SPEI; Vicente‐Serrano et al., 2010) at the study location over the reported drought period. SPEI is a drought index that quantifies water deficit across multiple timescales, facilitating comparisons across different climates. Our study focused on the short‐term availability of water resources associated with seasonal environmental moisture, and longer‐term water availability affecting streamflow and vegetation. We thus extracted SPEI (0.5 × 0.5°) at 3‐month and 12‐month timescales, respectively (Vicente‐Serrano et al., 2010) from the Global SPEI database (https://spei.csic.es/database.html), given that droughts are pervasive climatic events at the local scale. Drought conditions were identified as SPEI values lower than −0.84 (Vicente‐Serrano et al., 2010), and the magnitude and frequency of these conditions were calculated as measurements of drought severity (Supplementary Methods). We collected data on species traits from published literature and publicly available datasets (Table 1), based on the species names provided in the full‐length papers.

Statistical analysis

From each original study i, we extracted data of demographic metric x of bird species k under drought and non‐drought conditions as an observation. Among the 319 observations, the sample sizes, means and standard deviations of the metrics were obtained for 224 observations from the text, digitized figures, or the supplementary data of the publications, and the effect size was then calculated using a widely used and robust method (Bonett, 2009)—the standardized mean difference in the two groups with Hedges' d for its greater performance on small sample sizes (Hedges et al., 1999):

The effect size of species k (yik) was calculated between the mean of demographic metric documented at drought (x¯ikd) and non‐drought sites (x¯ikc). vikd and vikc represent the standard deviations of x¯ikd and x¯ikc, respectively, with Nikd and Nikc as sample sizes. For four studies where the responses were measured under droughts of different severity, Hedges' d was calculated by comparing the mean of metrics at each severity level (x¯ikd1…x¯ikdn) with the non‐drought level (x¯ikc). To account for the dependence caused by the shared controls, we computed the variance–covariance matrix (Δ) following Lajeunesse (2011) (see Supplementary Methods). For 20 observations where the standard deviations of the metrics were unavailable, values were estimated with Bracken's approach (Sinclair & Bracken, 1992) which imputes standard deviations using the coefficient of variation from all observations with complete means and standard deviations. We additionally extracted the effect sizes provided in 75 observations and transformed them to Hedges' d.

According to our hypothesis that moderators of individual responses differ from those at population levels, we grouped the effect sizes of metrics by the biological organization level. For each level, random‐effect models were adopted to estimate the mean response of the metrics to droughts across the observations, to account for the interdependence among observations within each study in addition to the studies' sampling variances (Borenstein et al., 2009). Moreover, because effect sizes of various power were not thought to contribute equally to the mean, the models weighted the effect size of each observation (yik) with the inverse of its sampling variance (Δ).

A hierarchical structure was built for inverse‐variance‐weighted models by including publication‐level and phylogeny‐associated random effects as nesting factors (Capilla‐Lasheras et al., 2022; Gu et al., 2023; Lundgren et al., 2024), to account for the dependence caused by among‐study differences, phylogenetic differences and the combination of both study and phylogeny. Phylogenetic distances among the 204 bird species (Appendix 2) were used to build a variance–covariance structure into the model (see Supplementary Methods). Using formal Cochran's Q tests, we examined whether variability in the effect sizes at individual and population level under droughts was larger than the expectation based on sampling variability alone.

We examined heterogeneity captured by the moderator of each independent meta‐regression with omnibus tests (Q Moderators , Q M ) and its p‐value. A statistically significant value of Q M indicates that a numeric moderator, or the difference among sub‐groups of a factorial moderator, affects the demography‐related responses to droughts. We also compared the difference in the mean effect sizes among sub‐groups of factorial moderators. Given that a low sample size of studies within some sub‐groups can limit the power of meta‐analyses, we excluded the sub‐groups that sampled by less than five studies (Akande et al., 2023) from the comparison due to the limitation of data availability. The factorial moderators including diet type, habitat dependency, movement type and terrestrial biome, were run without the intercept to obtain the parameter estimates of each sub‐group with more than five studies.

We then conducted multi‐model inference analyses at the individual and population level, respectively, starting from models including all the moderators showing low collinearity (see Supplementary Methods and Table S3 for collinearity diagnosis of the moderators). A set of models was then ranked for the metrics at each level according to model fitness indicated by the Akaike information criterion corrected for small sample size (AICc). The AICc was computed and ranked by running the function that was fit to the dataset in a model formula of mixed‐effects meta‐regression using maximum likelihood estimation (R package ‘glmulti’, Calcagno & de Mazancourt, 2010). The relative importance of moderators was evaluated with the AICc weight (AICcW), which was calculated as the sum of the weights for models in which the variable appeared (Burnham & Anderson, 2010). The smallest subset of models with a cumulative sum of AICcW ≥0.95 was selected as a confidence set of models (Johnson & Omland, 2004).

Publication bias and model sensitivity test

Because studies with statistically significant results may have a higher probability of being published, we examined publication bias for the bird responses included in the meta‐analyses at both individual and population levels, as well as for all moderator sub‐groups within each biological level. We identified publication bias by comparing the effect sizes (Hedges' d) and standard errors from our analyses with the unbiased estimation using funnel plots. Egger's regression test was then applied to quantify the asymmetry of these plots (Koricheva et al., 2013). If a detectable bias was identified, we estimated the fail‐safe number (FSN) to test how readily the result of a meta‐analysis could be overturned as publication bias reduces. FSN is the number of unpublished studies required to bring the meta‐analytic mean effect size down to a statistically insignificant level (Fragkos et al., 2017). We also identified outliers and observations with a strong influence on the mean effect size by calculating a series of leave‐one‐out diagnostics (Viechtbauer, 2010) for each observation, which indicates the influence of deleting one observation at a time on the model fit or the residual values. The random‐effect models were then re‐run without the influential observations to test the sensitivity of the results to these observations. The analysis was performed with the ‘metafor’ package (Viechtbauer, 2010).

RESULTS

Impacts of droughts on demography of bird populations

Data collection process generated a total of 3007 studies for screening titles and abstracts after removing 1287 duplicate publications. From the publications yielded by the literature search, we retained 133 based on title and abstract screening, from which 123 full‐length papers were downloaded to check for eligibility (Figure 1a). This examination resulted in 319 observations from 29 publications that analysed changes in bird demography due to droughts for 204 species across 12 orders. These changes were predominantly observed in xeric shrublands (26.01%), followed by moist broadleaf forests (20.69%) and grasslands, savannas and shrublands (14.73%) in the tropics and subtropics. Additionally, broadleaf and mixed forests, grasslands and shrublands, coniferous forests of the temperate zone, montane grasslands and shrublands and Mediterranean forests, woodlands and scrub together accounted for 38.57% (Figure 1b). Over 70% of the observations recorded bird responses at population level, a majority of which were from species of Passeriformes; individual responses to droughts were more frequently recorded for species of Accipitriformes, Caprimulgiformes, or Charadriiformes (Figure 1c).

According to the drought periods reported in the studies (Appendix 1), over half (65.00%) of the observations of individual responses were recorded in a single drought year. Four observations from two studies reported individual responses to seasonal droughts. Comparatively, we found a large proportion of population‐level responses (77.00%) in situations where the drought lasted for more than one continuous year. Drought magnitude at 3‐month and 12‐month timescale was strongly correlated (r = 0.76). High collinearity was identified between drought magnitude and frequency at 3‐month timescale and the terrestrial biome of the study location at individual and population level (Table S3). The mean demographic responses to droughts were both negative at individual (n = 80, 23 species) and population level (n = 239, 182 species), with −0.72 (95% CI: −1.08 to −0.36) and −0.61 (95% CI: −1.12 to −0.10) as the respective slope estimates (Figure 2). Our results also showed the bird responses to droughts were highly heterogeneous among studies and observations at individual (I 2 = 99.34%, Q t = 1187.85, p < 0.0001) and population level (I 2 = 77.56%, Q t = 959.86, p < 0.05), which we tried to explain with potential moderators. The estimates of random components were reported in Table S4.

Effects of moderators on the responses related to demography

At individual level, the negative effect of droughts on bird demography decreased with the increasing extent of occurrence of species (Figure 3a, Table 2). Droughts caused detectable reductions in bird demographic metrics in deserts and xeric shrublands, while the effect was statistically insignificant in Mediterranean forests, woodlands and scrublands (Figure 4a, Table S5). The mean effect sizes were not examined for the other biomes due to the small sample sizes (Table S5). We did not find that diet type or movement type substantially contributed to the variance of the responses at individual level (Table 2). The negative responses to droughts were detectable for habitat generalists and non‐threatened species (Table S5). For habitat specialists and threatened species, the mean responses were uncertain due to the small sample sizes (Table S5). Multi‐model inference analysis suggested including adult body mass, extent of occurrence, mean clutch size, frequency of droughts at the 12‐month time scale and magnitude of droughts at the 3‐month time scale as moderators in the optimal model set (n = 65), which together explained 18.53% of the total variance of demographic responses (Table S6). Maximum longevity was excluded due to a strong correlation with adult body mass (Table S3).

At population level, the negative impact of droughts on bird demography decreased with increasing mean clutch size (Figure 3b, Table 2). Under drought impacts, the effect sizes of population‐level metrics showed a positive correlation with the extent of occurrence of species (Table 2), but this relationship became statistically insignificant when other moderators were included in the model (Table S7). Overall, the relative abundance or density of populations decreased due to droughts for omnivores and species that fed on invertebrates, fruit, or nectar, while demographic metrics of granivorous species did not change statistically (Figure 4b, Table S5). Both full‐migrant and non‐migrant species exhibited uniformly negative responses to droughts (Table S5). Studies on habitat specialists and threatened species were too few to compare with generalists and non‐threatened species, which were negatively affected by droughts on average (Table S5). The optimal model set recommended by multi‐model inference analysis (n = 183) included mean clutch size, maximum longevity, diet type and frequency of droughts at the 12‐month timescale as important moderators of demographic responses at population level (Tables S6 and S7). Together with the extent of occurrence and threatened status of species, these moderators explained 32.84% of the overall variance in responses.

Sensitivity of the analyses to publication bias and influential cases

Our dataset was subject to the bias that positive responses to drought were underrepresented at both individual and population levels in the publications (Table S8). Publication bias was also detected for the individual responses of species feeding on invertebrates, full‐migrant species and the studies conducted in deserts and xeric shrublands (Table S8). The population‐level responses were biased for species feeding on fruit and nectar, as well as full‐migrant species (Table S8). By estimating the fail‐safe number, we discovered that a disproportionately large number of observations, relative to the sample sizes, were required to overturn the mean negative effect sizes of the groups showing negative mean responses with detectable publication bias (Table S8). Additionally, we diagnosed three observations at individual level and one observation at population level that strongly influenced the mean responses (Figures S1 and S2). By rerunning the meta‐analyses without these observations, we found that the mean responses to drought increased respectively by 18.73% and 1.61% for the individual and population subsets, and both remained negative (Figure S3).

DISCUSSION

Our study demonstrated a general pattern of droughts diminishing metrics related to bird demography at both individual and population levels, which coincided with findings on broader taxonomic groups (Gu et al., 2023; Thakur et al., 2022). To enhance our understanding of bird vulnerability to droughts, we identified species traits that mediated responses at each level and quantified their effects. These traits included adult body mass, extent of species occurrence, major dietary type, mean clutch size and maximum longevity, each contributing differently to the responses at the two biological organizational levels. The responses we examined were further explained by the severity of droughts lasting over a year. Population‐level responses, such as declines in population size or density, were more frequently observed during or after droughts persisting for years than the responses at individual level. However, the insights from our results are subject to publication bias and are limited by the small sample sizes of some moderator sub‐groups (Table S8).

These results indicated that the complexity of bird vulnerability to droughts could be attributed to the multi‐scalar characteristics of drought episodes. Passeriformes, the largest order of birds, had the highest proportion of responses to droughts, many of which exhibited decreases in population abundance. Comparatively, a greater proportion of large‐bodied species, such as eagles, falcons, shorebirds and waterfowls, involved responses at individual level. By quantifying the effect of different traits on the responses to droughts, our study advances the assessment of vulnerability to ECEs that previously relied on equally weighted species traits. The study also revealed that traits affecting responses to drought at local and regional scales (e.g. movement type and habitat dependency) may not always apply generally. Despite of the publication bias in the results, the insights provided by our study help with bird conservation in a drying world by prioritizing the declining populations of species with drought‐susceptible traits and highlighting the necessity of monitoring populations residing in regions predicted to be exposed to intensifying droughts.

Responses to drought at individual level

We identified an overall decrease in demographic rates for bird individuals residing in desert and xeric shrublands. In these arid biomes, many species already live close to their physiological limits and thus individual survival rates and reproductive investment can be more susceptible to drought‐induced dehydration and physiological stress (Albright et al., 2017). Drought‐induced reductions in primary production were greater in arid shrublands (Smith et al., 2024), which may also impose general stresses on the living resources of birds. Among the 16 observations from this biome, half reported a decrease in fecundity and 37.50% recorded declines in nestling survival, indicating that the responses of adults were distinct from those of young birds. Our results showed that an increasing extent of occurrence was associated with a decline in demographic metrics and the effect was particularly strong for species with narrow distributions (Figure 3a). Extent of occurrence is a parameter of current distribution that measures the area encompassing all the known, inferred, or projected sites of present occurrence of a species (International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN), 2021). Given that many fluctuations in environmental conditions are spatially correlated, droughts have a higher probability of affecting individuals restricted to a small area than those spread across a large area and consequently, have a higher probability of triggering negative responses.

For species more likely to be exposed to droughts, individual responses could additionally be mediated by body mass, which was highly correlated with maximum longevity (Tables S6 and S7). Large‐bodied, long‐lived bird species may skip reproducing during drought periods, whereas small‐bodied, short‐lived birds may have to reproduce in every breeding season regardless of drought occurrence (Martin & Mouton, 2020). Our results from multivariable analysis supported this conclusion from tropical evergreen forests. The differences between drought‐coping strategies may drive inconsistent changes in demographic rates between large‐ and small‐bodied species, or between the adult survival rate and fecundity of large‐bodied species. Such heterogeneities will be better explained by comparing the response of different demographic parameters as more observations within each parameter group become available. Drought severity on a 12‐month timescale also showed a weak moderating effect in the multivariable models (Table S7), indicating an association between the responses of bird individuals and long‐term water scarcity. Moreover, the large residual heterogeneity of the optimized model sets (Table S8) suggested that demographic responses were mediated by other factors at individual level. These factors could include availability of nesting sites (Gaston et al., 2005), changes in breeding phenology (Carey, 2009; Lv et al., 2020) and environmental conditions at both breeding and non‐breeding sites for migratory birds (Rockwell et al., 2012; Wegge & Rolstad, 2017).

Responses to drought at population level

Biological traits of species are involved in the mechanisms mediating population dynamics under ECEs by shaping their ability to cope with impacts (Ameca y Juárez et al., 2012). Drought has been shown to alter flower and fruit phenology (Butt et al., 2015; Waser & Price, 2016) and reduce the biomass of invertebrates, including insects (Carnicer et al., 2019; Deacon et al., 2019) and arthropods (Croy et al., 2021). Bird species that rely on these vulnerable food resources are more likely to suffer greater population declines due to pervasive malnutrition during prolonged droughts.

As a phylogenetically constrained trait, the inclusion of mean clutch size in our optimized models confirms the effects of its life‐history correlates, such as lifespan, reproductive rate and environmental seasonality (Wiens & Graham, 2005). Species with large clutch sizes are typically found in highly seasonal environments with frequent climate swings (Jetz et al., 2008; Scholer et al., 2020). Since seasonality of resource conditions limits the number of broods in these areas (Böhning‐Gaese et al., 2000), a large clutch size can compensate for potentially high mortality induced by environmental stressors, such as droughts.

Field observations of multiple taxonomic groups have shown that short‐lived species tend to have more unstable vital rates under climate variability, and their populations are likely to experience stronger impacts than those of longer‐lived species (Morris et al., 2008). Our analysis indicated that such an effect of longevity could also affect bird responses to droughts at population level. Simulated dynamics of age‐structured populations revealed longevity as the dominant trait affecting impacts of environmental disturbances and recovery time, when the disturbance affected only young age classes (White et al., 2022). At individual level, we found that declines in survival rates were more frequently reported for young birds than adults, and these changes could trigger decreases in the population abundance of especially short‐lived species.

A substantially larger proportion of responses at population level were recorded with long‐lasting or recurrent droughts than at individual level, indicating that bird populations might respond to droughts on timescales longer than individuals. Compared to individual level, drought severity at a 12‐month timescale played a more important role in moderating the population‐level responses (Table S7). However, differences in drought severity did not substantially explain the heterogeneity of responses among studies (Table 2). As aboveground production responds differently to the persistence of drought across land biomes (Vicente‐Serrano et al., 2013), the indirect drought impacts on bird populations, such as the declines in critical habitat and food resources, outbreaks of pests and wildfires (Saracco et al., 2018), may have also occurred at different timescales across biomes, which can be one of the sources of heterogeneity among studies. An appropriate study period that captures the major changes is key to measuring these impacts accurately.

Limitations and perspectives

In addition to the publication bias and small sample sizes of sub‐groups, the weights of effect sizes in the meta‐analyses are positively skewed (Table S5), and the causes of the large within‐observation variances remain undetermined. We were not able to quantify drought severity accurately with the globally comparable SPEI, regarding frequency and magnitude, due to a lack of information on the precise timing of the onset and end, and the spatial scale of drought in many studies. The uncertainties in drought quantification can limit the power of our models in explaining the variance among observations. Although time lags between droughts and some ecological responses were identified in field studies (Gorzo et al., 2016; Thakur et al., 2022), such lagged effects are unlikely to be accounted for in studies conducted only in the drought year. Due to geographically biased research effort, the trait data were not available for all the regions covered by this study. We thus used the average of the measurements from available data sources for each trait, which may affect results of the mixed‐effect models. During droughts, wild populations are at a higher risk of decline if they are threatened by other extrinsic disturbances such as habitat loss, exploitation and pollution (Zhang et al., 2019). However, the data from our meta‐analyses cover only 0.68% of the 1464 threatened bird species. Compared with a coverage of 2.13% of non‐threatened species, we consider that threatened species are underrepresented in relevant publications, which prevents a robust estimation of their responses to droughts.

Our study is a crucial step toward understanding the patterns of biological responses to complex climatic events, as we found that the responses of bird demography were associated with the vulnerability of food resources to droughts, and the mechanisms of birds coping with drought impacts possibly vary across the ‘fast‐slow’ spectrum of life‐history strategies at both individual and population levels. For large‐bodied, long‐lived species, young individuals tend to be less affected by droughts, and adults may reduce their energy for reproduction to increase their survival rate. The population abundance of these species is likely to be more resistant but slow in recovery from extreme droughts if the mean clutch size was small.

Comparatively, populations of small‐bodied, short‐lived species may decline more due to higher mortalities of young individuals, but also have faster recovery times if they reproduce quickly with large clutch sizes. Because drought is a multi‐scalar phenomenon with varying intensity, magnitude, duration and spatial extent, we encourage future research to define and measure drought using climatic measurements that are comparable across time and space, such as the SPEI, the Standardized Precipitation Index (Hayes et al., 1999) and the self‐calibrated Palmer Drought Severity Index (Wells et al., 2004). Accurate quantification of droughts will enhance our understanding of the response mechanisms of bird demography. Threatened bird species are in urgent need of relevant research, which is critical for conservation (Ding et al., 2024). We also encourage researchers to explore whether and how droughts affect bird behaviours, morphology and physiology, since responses in these aspects may also contribute to individual and population vulnerability.
