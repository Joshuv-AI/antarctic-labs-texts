Assessment of prevalence, perceptions, and factors associated with reported history of caesarean section among women in the United Arab Emirates: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Alantreesa Siby, Amal Shaji, Fathimath Shahza, Richu Elsa Shaju, Wajiha Ajmal, Nelofar Sami Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCaesarean section (CS) is a life-saving intervention when medically indicated; however, its use has increased substantially worldwide, including in high-access healthcare settings. Emerging evidence suggests that women’s perceptions, prior childbirth experiences, and counselling practices may contribute to persistently elevated CS rates. This study assessed the prevalence of reported history of CS and examined sociodemographic, health-system, and perception-related factors associated with prior CS among women in the United Arab Emirates (UAE).MethodsA cross-sectional questionnaire-based survey was conducted among women aged ≥ 18 years residing in the UAE between February and October 2025. Data were collected using a structured self-administered questionnaire assessing sociodemographic characteristics, obstetric history, delivery-related perceptions, and factors influencing decision-making regarding mode of delivery. Analyses examining CS history were restricted to women with at least one prior delivery, and the outcome was defined as reporting at least one previous CS. Descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, and multivariable binary logistic regression analyses were performed. Separate domain-specific regression models and an additional combined multivariable model were constructed to assess potential confounding between sociodemographic and perception-related variables.ResultsAmong 351 women with previous deliveries, 219 (62.4%) reported a history of at least one CS. The most commonly reported reasons for CS included foetal medical indications (28.3%), healthcare provider recommendation (18.7%), perceived foetal safety (18.7%), and fear of labour pain (16.0%). In the combined multivariable model, age ≥ 31 years (AOR 1.73; 95% CI 1.06–2.81) and undergraduate/postgraduate educational attainment (AOR 2.04; 95% CI 1.13–3.69) were independently associated with prior CS. Perception-related variables associated with prior CS included disagreement that vaginal delivery leads to better maternal recovery (AOR 18.50; 95% CI 2.37–144.51), disagreement that vaginal birth promotes better mother–baby bonding (AOR 2.37; 95% CI 1.28–4.38), and the belief that CS impacts infant immune outcomes (AOR 2.57; 95% CI 1.35–4.90). Women identified improved counselling from healthcare providers as the most important factor perceived to support better delivery-related decision-making.ConclusionsA reported history of CS was high within this UAE study sample. While most sociodemographic and health-system characteristics demonstrated limited independent association with prior CS, selected perception-related and experiential factors remained associated after adjustment. These findings suggest that in high-access healthcare settings, communication- and perception-related influences may contribute meaningfully to delivery-related preferences and experiences. Strengthening balanced, evidence-based, and woman-centred antenatal counselling may support informed decision-making regarding mode of delivery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09397-3.

Introduction

Caesarean section (CS) is a life-saving surgical procedure when medically indicated; however, its use has increased markedly across many regions of the world, raising concern about potential overuse in low-risk pregnancies ([1, 2]). Large national and population-based studies demonstrate substantial variation in CS rates across and within countries, variation that cannot be explained by obstetric risk alone and points to the influence of non-clinical factors [1, 3, 4].

Decisions regarding mode of delivery are shaped by a complex interaction of medical advice, personal experience, perceptions of safety and pain, and the organisation of maternity care [5, 6]. Women’s beliefs about childbirth, particularly fears related to labour pain, bodily harm, recovery, and neonatal safety, have consistently been shown to influence preference for CS, even in the absence of clear medical indications [7–10]. Several studies indicate that many women perceive CS as safer or more predictable for the baby, while simultaneously recognising that it is associated with longer maternal recovery and extended hospital stay [9]. In an Indian hospital-based study, preference for caesarean delivery was primarily driven by perceived foetal safety, whereas preference for vaginal delivery (VD) was commonly linked to expectations of faster maternal recovery [11].

Prior childbirth experience plays a central role in shaping subsequent delivery preferences. Evidence from diverse settings consistently shows that women with a previous CS are substantially more likely to prefer repeat caesarean delivery, whereas women with prior vaginal birth tend to express greater confidence in VD and a lower preference for CS ([3, 12, 13]).

Knowledge and information gaps further shape decision-making related to delivery. Evidence from the Middle East and Asia indicates that many women have a limited understanding of the short- and long-term risks associated with CS, including maternal and reproductive health consequences, and that such gaps persist even among women with higher educational attainment [2, 7, 14, 15]. In these contexts, women’s decisions are strongly shaped by interactions with healthcare providers and by narratives derived from peers’ childbirth experiences, which can reinforce perceptions and preferences regarding mode of delivery [9, 15].

The roles of healthcare providers and the quality of counselling are therefore central to decision-making related to delivery. Qualitative and survey-based evidence from multiple settings shows that clinicians’ recommendations, the way risks and benefits are discussed, and the presence or absence of supportive dialogue influence women’s preferences for caesarean or vaginal birth [6, 16, 17]. Counselling practices that emphasise the perceived safety and predictability of CS, without a comprehensive discussion of alternative modes of birth and recovery, may contribute to perceptions of CS as a reasonable or safer option [6, 16]. Separately, fear of childbirth, inadequate pain control, and negative interactions with healthcare staff have been associated with increased preference for caesarean delivery in subsequent pregnancies [18].

Psychological and experiential factors further influence how childbirth is perceived and remembered. Evidence indicates that negative childbirth experiences, particularly those involving emergency interventions, poor communication, and lack of support, are associated with fear of future childbirth and increased preference for caesarean delivery [13, 18]. Conversely, positive childbirth experiences, supportive maternity care, and improved antenatal education are associated with more positive beliefs about childbirth, lower levels of childbirth-related fear, and greater confidence in VD [3, 10]. Emerging evidence suggests that perceptions related to recovery and infant safety differ according to delivery history and that beliefs surrounding childbirth are not fixed but may be shaped through targeted counselling and antenatal education [3, 10, 11].

In relatively high-access healthcare settings, evidence from the United Arab Emirates (UAE) and Saudi Arabia suggests that variation in CS use is more closely linked to maternal knowledge, perceptions, and counselling practices than to limitations in healthcare access alone [3, 4, 7].

Importantly, unlike obstetric risk factors, perceptions of childbirth, prior experiences, and counselling practices represent potentially modifiable influences on delivery-related decision-making. Understanding how these factors interact with women’s preferences may help explain persistently high CS rates in settings with adequate healthcare access [3, 16].

Against this background, examining CS prevalence alongside women’s perceptions of safety, pain, recovery, bonding, and counselling experiences provides important insight into why CS rates remain high despite comparable obstetric risk profiles. Such evidence is needed to inform balanced, evidence-based, and culturally appropriate counselling strategies that support informed choice and optimise maternal and neonatal outcomes.

Methods

Aim and study design

This study was designed as a cross-sectional, questionnaire-based survey conducted among women residing in the UAE. The primary aim was to assess the prevalence of reported history of CS among women with at least one prior delivery and to examine sociodemographic, health-system, and perception-related factors associated with reported CS history. Given the cross-sectional design and the use of reported history of CS as the outcome variable, observed associations were interpreted as non-causal and potentially bidirectional.

Study setting and participants

The study was conducted in the UAE between February and October 2025. Participants were recruited using a non-probability convenience sampling approach across healthcare and community-based settings, including selected government and private hospitals, maternity and postnatal clinics, outpatient departments, and community outreach platforms. To broaden participation and improve accessibility, the questionnaire was also disseminated online through women’s health-related networks and social media platforms.

Recruitment was conducted in a reasonable manner across available healthcare and community settings, rather than through predefined proportional allocation across hospitals, clinics, and online platforms. Therefore, participant distribution across recruitment sources was not strictly balanced, which may have influenced the sample composition. The mixed in-person and online recruitment strategy was intended to improve accessibility and facilitate participation from women residing in different regions and healthcare settings within the UAE; however, it was not designed to generate a nationally representative sample. Accordingly, the study sample should be interpreted as a convenience sample of women reachable through these recruitment channels.

As the online survey link was disseminated through open recruitment channels, the exact number of individuals who viewed or received the survey invitation could not be reliably determined. Therefore, a precise response rate could not be calculated. Measures were implemented to minimise duplicate responses. The survey platform settings limited participants to a single response, and incomplete responses were excluded during data cleaning.

Women aged 18 years and above who had experienced at least one pregnancy or were currently pregnant at the time of the survey were eligible to participate. Women who had never been pregnant or who declined to provide informed consent were excluded. Analyses examining the reported history of CS were restricted to women who reported at least one prior delivery. Responses with substantial incomplete data were excluded during data cleaning prior to analysis.

Sample size determination and sampling technique

The minimum sample size for this study was calculated using Kish’s (1965) formula for estimating a single population proportion. Assuming a CS prevalence of 39.1% based on previous regional studies, a 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96), and a 5% margin of error, the calculated minimum sample size was 366 participants [3]. To account for potential incomplete or unusable responses, an additional 10% was added, yielding a target sample size of approximately 403 participants, which was achieved.

A non-probability convenience sampling strategy was used because the study aimed to explore the reported history of CS and delivery-related perceptions among women accessible through healthcare and community-based recruitment channels within the available study period. Participants were recruited through both in-person and online approaches to facilitate broader participation across different healthcare and community settings. However, because participation was voluntary and recruitment was not probability-based, the sample may overrepresent women with greater health awareness, internet access, higher educational attainment, or stronger interest in childbirth-related issues.

Data collection tool

Data were collected using a structured, self-administered questionnaire developed following a review of relevant literature and previously published studies examining CS prevalence, delivery-related perceptions, and decision-making factors in maternity care settings [3–5]. Questionnaire items were adapted to the UAE context and modified for clarity, readability, and cultural appropriateness. The questionnaire used for data collection is provided as a supplementary file.

Perception-related items included binary (yes/no) questions and Likert-scale statements. Content validity and the questionnaire’s clarity were reviewed by three faculty members with expertise in obstetrics, public health, and health professions education prior to data collection. Pilot testing was conducted with 10 eligible participants to assess the clarity, comprehensibility, flow, and completion time of the questions. Minor wording modifications were made following pilot feedback to improve readability and clarity, and pilot responses were excluded from the final analysis.

The questionnaire was intentionally designed as a multidomain survey instrument assessing distinct concepts, including perceptions, prior experiences, counselling-related factors, and delivery preferences, rather than a single latent psychometric construct. Overall internal consistency reliability using a single Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was not considered appropriate for this instrument.

Outcome and independent variables

The primary outcome variable was reported history of any CS, categorised as “ever CS” or “never CS” among women with at least one prior delivery. The outcome was defined at the woman-level rather than the delivery-level. Therefore, women who reported both caesarean and vaginal deliveries across different pregnancies were classified in the “ever CS” group. The analysis did not distinguish between primary and repeat CS because the study focused on whether participants had ever experienced a caesarean delivery.

Independent variables included sociodemographic characteristics, obstetric factors, health-system factors (including insurance status and preferred healthcare facility), perception-related variables, and delivery-related decision-making influences. Selected variables were recoded into dichotomous or collapsed categorical groupings where appropriate to improve interpretability, ensure adequate cell sizes, and support stable estimation in multivariable analyses.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 29. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise participant characteristics and study variables and were presented as frequencies and percentages.

Bivariate associations between reported history of CS and explanatory variables were assessed using Pearson’s chi-square test, with row percentages used to compare distributions between groups.

Perception-related Likert-scale items were initially collected using five response categories ranging from strongly agree to strongly disagree. For regression analyses, selected Likert-scale variables were collapsed into three categories (agree, neutral, and disagree) to improve interpretability, reduce sparse cell counts within individual categories, and minimise unstable parameter estimates and excessively wide confidence intervals (CI) in multivariable models. This approach was considered appropriate given the sample size and distribution of responses across categories.

Binary logistic regression analyses were conducted to examine factors associated with a reported history of CS. Initial domain-specific multivariable models were constructed separately for sociodemographic/health-system variables and perception-related variables. Sociodemographic and health-system variables included age, nationality, educational level, monthly household income, health insurance status, and preferred healthcare facility for delivery. These variables were included based on theoretical relevance and prior literature, irrespective of their significance in crude analyses.

A multivariable model for perception-related variables examines their independent associations with reported history of CS. Because perception-related variables and reported CS history were measured simultaneously in the cross-sectional survey, the regression findings were interpreted as associations rather than as causal predictors of delivery decisions.

To address potential confounding across domains, an additional combined multivariable logistic regression model was subsequently constructed to simultaneously include selected sociodemographic, health-system, and perception-related variables. Variables included in the combined model were selected based on theoretical relevance, prior literature, and findings from bivariate analyses.

Multicollinearity among independent variables included in the combined model was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs) and tolerance statistics prior to model interpretation. VIF values below 5 and tolerance values above 0.20 were considered indicative of the absence of problematic multicollinearity. Model fit and stability were additionally evaluated using goodness-of-fit statistics.

Crude odds ratios (ORs) and adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% CI were reported. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Gulf Medical University (approval reference number: Ref. no. IRB-COM-STD-31-Jan-2025). Participation was voluntary, informed consent was obtained electronically prior to questionnaire completion, and all data were collected anonymously. Confidentiality of participant information was strictly maintained throughout the study.

Results

Participant characteristics

Table 1 summarises the sociodemographic, obstetric, and healthcare characteristics of the study participants. A total of 433 questionnaire responses were received during the study period. Of these, 30 responses were excluded due to incomplete data or failure to meet the inclusion criteria, such as participants who had never experienced pregnancy. Consequently, 403 responses were included in the final analysis. The majority were aged 26–35 years (64.3%), most were non-Arab (82.9%), and most had attained undergraduate or postgraduate education (77.7%). Nearly half of the participants were employed (46.9%), while 37.2% were homemakers.

Regarding socioeconomic and healthcare characteristics, 62.8% reported a monthly household income above AED 10,000, and 86.6% had health insurance coverage. Among participants with valid responses, 46.9% reported full insurance coverage for delivery, and 77.2% preferred private healthcare facilities for childbirth. Approximately half of the participants were currently pregnant at the time of the survey (50.6%).

Among women with previous deliveries (n = 351), 54.1% reported having two or more prior deliveries, for a total of 697 deliveries. Of these women, 219 (62.4%) reported a history of at least one CS and were classified as the “ever CS” group, while 132 (37.6%) reported no history of caesarean delivery and were classified as the “never CS” group. This classification reflects woman-level CS history and does not distinguish between primary and repeat caesarean deliveries.

The sample was characterised by a high proportion of non-Arab women, women with higher educational attainment, women with health insurance coverage, and women preferring private healthcare facilities. These characteristics should be considered when interpreting the findings, as the sample may not fully reflect the demographic and healthcare-use diversity of the broader female population in the UAE.

Reasons for caesarean section

Among women with a history of at least one CS (n = 219), the most frequently reported reason for caesarean delivery was foetal medical indication, cited by 28.3% of respondents (Table 2). Provider-related and perception-based factors were also commonly reported, with healthcare provider recommendation and perceived safety for the baby (non-medical) each reported by 18.7% of participants. Fear of labour pain and personal convenience were each reported by 16.0% of women. Less frequently reported reasons included previous traumatic birth experience (7.8%), maternal medical conditions (4.6%), and family or cultural influence (2.7%).

Beliefs and perceptions regarding mode of delivery

Perceptions related to mode of delivery differed between women with and without a history of CS (Table 4). Women without a prior CS were more likely to agree that VD leads to better maternal recovery compared with women with a previous CS (88.5% vs. 67.6%, p < 0.001). Similarly, the proportion agreeing that VD is better for mother–baby bonding was higher among women without a history of CS (42.0% vs. 26.9%, p = 0.010).

Perceived advantages and disadvantages of caesarean section

Detailed distributions of perceived advantages and disadvantages are presented in Supplementary Table S1. Perceived advantages and disadvantages of CS differed according to prior caesarean history. Among perceived advantages, the most frequently reported was that CS is safer for the baby (71.5%), followed by reduction in labour pain (43.4%) and perceived maternal safety (30.7%). Women with a history of CS were more likely than those without prior caesarean delivery to report CS as safer for the baby (79.2% vs. 63.2%, p = 0.004), safer for the mother (34.6% vs. 22.6%, p = 0.037), and as a planned or convenient mode of delivery (28.3% vs. 13.2%, p = 0.004). Perceptions regarding reduction of labour pain showed a borderline difference between groups (47.2% vs. 35.8%, p = 0.068).

Regarding perceived disadvantages, longer recovery time was the most reported concern overall (73.9%) and differed significantly by caesarean history (82.9% among women with prior caesarean delivery vs. 64.6% among those without, p < 0.001). Women without a history of caesarean delivery were more likely to report concerns related to maternal complications (60.8% vs. 38.4%, p < 0.001) and infant complications (31.5% vs. 12.5%, p < 0.001). Perceived higher financial cost did not differ significantly between the two groups (p = 0.840). As multiple responses were permitted, participants frequently reported more than one perceived advantage or disadvantage.

Discussion

This cross-sectional study identified a high reported prevalence of CS among women with prior deliveries in this UAE-based study sample, with nearly two-thirds of participants reporting a history of at least one caesarean birth. Because the outcome was defined at the woman level as “ever CS,” the findings reflect woman-level exposure to caesarean delivery rather than a delivery-level CS rate. The “ever CS” category may therefore include women who experienced a primary CS only, repeat CS, or both caesarean and vaginal deliveries across different pregnancies. This distinction is important because primary and repeat CS may have different underlying determinants. Primary CS may be more closely related to initial obstetric indications, provider recommendations, or maternal preferences, whereas repeat CS may also be influenced by previous caesarean and childbirth experiences, counselling, and institutional practices. Accordingly, the present findings should be interpreted as associations with reported CS history rather than as factors specifically associated with primary or repeat caesarean delivery.

Given the use of convenience sampling and mixed online/in-person recruitment, the observed prevalence should not be interpreted as a nationally representative estimate for the UAE population. The sample composition suggests that selection bias may have influenced the findings. The predominance of non-Arab participants, women with higher educational attainment, insured participants, and women preferring private healthcare facilities may reflect the recruitment channels used, particularly online dissemination and the selection of healthcare settings. Women with lower digital access, lower health literacy, limited engagement with online platforms, or different healthcare-seeking patterns may therefore have been underrepresented. Consequently, the findings are most appropriately interpreted as reflecting associations within this study sample rather than the broader UAE population.

Within this study sample, reported history of caesarean delivery was associated with a combination of medical indications, provider-related factors, women’s perceptions, and prior delivery experiences. However, these associations should be interpreted with caution because the cross-sectional design does not permit the determination of temporal relationships between perceptions and prior CS. Women’s current beliefs regarding recovery, bonding, or infant outcomes may have been shaped by women’s own prior childbirth experiences and counselling exposure.

Educational attainment and age ≥ 31 years emerged as the sociodemographic factors independently associated with a history of caesarean delivery, while income level, health insurance status, and preferred healthcare facility were not. Women’s perceptions of maternal recovery, infant outcomes, and future delivery preferences varied with prior delivery experience, and participants consistently identified improved counselling from healthcare providers as a key factor that supported better decision-making regarding mode of delivery.

Caesarean section prevalence and contributing factors in context

The present study identified a high reported prevalence of CS among women with prior deliveries in the study sample from Ajman, UAE (Table 1), indicating substantial exposure to surgical delivery within this population. Elevated CS rates have similarly been reported within the UAE. A large population-based study conducted in Abu Dhabi reported that 30.2% of deliveries among mothers of children under 2 years of age were by CS, exceeding the World Health Organisation–referenced population threshold of 10–15%, with the majority of procedures performed electively [19]. Although direct comparisons should be interpreted with caution due to methodological and outcome-definition differences between studies, these findings suggest that elevated caesarean utilisation has been well established in UAE obstetric practice for several years.

High CS prevalence has also been documented in other middle- and low-income settings. Hospital-based studies from Bangladesh and Egypt reported caesarean rates exceeding 70%, with fear of labour pain, perceived neonatal safety, and healthcare provider recommendation frequently cited as contributing influences, including a notable proportion of caesarean delivery on maternal request [20, 21]. These findings are broadly consistent with the present study, in which women commonly identified provider recommendation, perceived neonatal safety, and fear-related concerns among reported reasons for caesarean delivery (Table 2).

Fear-based and perception-related influences on caesarean preference have likewise been reported in the Gulf region. In Saudi Arabia, a study among low-risk nulliparous women found that a substantial proportion preferred caesarean delivery in the absence of medical indications, with fear of childbirth commonly reported [22]. Evidence from Central Europe further demonstrated that CS remained associated with maternal characteristics such as age even after adjustment for obstetric and sociodemographic factors, suggesting that non-clinical mechanisms may contribute to delivery-related decision-making [23]. Evidence from Egypt and Northern Ghana additionally suggests that the relative contributions of sociodemographic, clinical, and perception-related influences to caesarean delivery patterns may vary across healthcare systems and cultural contexts [21, 24]. Similarly, the present study observed associations between reported history of caesarean delivery and several perception-related variables, including beliefs regarding maternal recovery, infant outcomes, and mother–baby bonding (Tables 4 and 6, and 7).

Taken together, the available evidence suggests that elevated CS prevalence in this and comparable settings may reflect a combination of medical indications and non-medical influences, including fear of childbirth, perceptions regarding neonatal safety, provider recommendation, and prior childbirth experiences. However, because the present study used a cross-sectional design, these relationships should be interpreted as non-causal associations rather than determinants of delivery decisions.

Factors associated with reported caesarean section history and delivery-related perceptions

Higher educational attainment and age ≥ 31 years remained independently associated with reported history of CS after adjustment for sociodemographic and health-system variables (Tables 5 and 7). Women with undergraduate or postgraduate education and older participants demonstrated higher odds of prior caesarean delivery compared with women with lower educational attainment and younger age groups, respectively. These findings may reflect differences in health information exposure, communication with healthcare providers, delivery expectations, accumulated obstetric experience, and risk perception rather than healthcare access alone. In contrast, household income, insurance status, and preferred healthcare facility were not independently associated with prior CS.

Perception-related factors also appeared to differ according to prior delivery experience. Women with prior CS more frequently reported less favourable perceptions regarding maternal recovery after vaginal delivery, mother–baby bonding associated with vaginal birth, and concerns regarding infant immune outcomes (Tables 4 and 6, and 7). Several of these associations remained evident after adjustment for confounders, suggesting that experiential and perception-related factors may contribute to childbirth-related attitudes and preferences. However, because perceptions and prior delivery history were measured simultaneously, the temporal direction of these relationships cannot be established, and women’s current beliefs may partly reflect their previous childbirth experiences, recovery after delivery, counselling received during pregnancy, or subsequent postpartum experiences. In addition, some perception-related estimates demonstrated relatively wide confidence intervals, particularly for maternal recovery perceptions, indicating statistical imprecision and requiring cautious interpretation.

Prior delivery experience also appeared strongly associated with future delivery preferences. In the present study, 69.9% of women with prior caesarean delivery expressed preference for planned CS in subsequent pregnancies (Table 3). Similar patterns have been reported in Egypt, where women requesting caesarean delivery frequently had a previous caesarean birth [21].

Comparable findings from other settings further support the potential influence of psychological, experiential, and contextual factors on delivery-related preferences and perceptions. In Turkey, traumatic birth perception was associated with reduced confidence in vaginal delivery and increased preference for CS [25], while studies from Saudi Arabia reported associations between fear-related perceptions and preference for caesarean delivery in the absence of medical indications [22]. In Brazil, women commonly described vaginal birth as involving faster physical recovery compared with caesarean delivery [26], while studies from Egypt reported that women without prior caesarean delivery were more likely to perceive vaginal birth as facilitating early mother–infant bonding [21]. Studies from Bangladesh and Malaysia similarly documented concerns regarding potential effects of caesarean delivery on breastfeeding, infant health, and immune outcomes [9, 20]. Comparable concerns regarding infant outcomes associated with CS were also observed in the present study, although these perceptions reflect participant beliefs and experiences rather than definitive clinical evidence. Together, these findings suggest that caesarean delivery patterns may be influenced not only by clinical need, but also by contextual, experiential, and perception-related factors that vary across healthcare systems and cultural settings.

Perceived advantages and disadvantages of caesarean section

In the present study, perceived foetal safety and reduced labour pain were among the most commonly reported perceived advantages of caesarean section (CS), particularly among women with prior caesarean experience (Supplementary Table S1). These findings suggest that prior childbirth experiences and perceptions of delivery may influence women’s attitudes toward delivery mode.

Comparable perceptions have been reported in other settings. In Bangladesh, many women perceived caesarean delivery as safer for the baby and effective in avoiding labour pain [20], while studies from Egypt identified fear of labour pain and fear of episiotomy among commonly reported reasons for caesarean delivery on maternal request [21]. Together, these findings indicate that concerns related to labour pain, childbirth anxiety, and perceived neonatal safety may contribute to favourable perceptions of CS across different cultural and healthcare settings.

At the same time, longer recovery time emerged as the most frequently reported perceived disadvantage of CS in the present study (Supplementary Table S1). Similar concerns regarding prolonged postoperative recovery and discomfort following caesarean delivery have been described in qualitative studies from Brazil [26]. Women without prior caesarean delivery in the present study also demonstrated greater concern regarding potential maternal and infant complications associated with CS, suggesting that perceptions regarding risks and benefits may differ according to previous childbirth experience.

Overall, the findings demonstrate the coexistence of both favourable and unfavourable perceptions regarding caesarean delivery within the study population. These perceptions are likely shaped by a combination of personal experience, counselling exposure, cultural beliefs, and perceived adjustments between labour-related concerns and postoperative recovery.

Decision-making and counselling as potentially modifiable influences on delivery choice

In the present study, women most frequently identified improved counselling from healthcare providers as an important factor for supporting better decision-making regarding mode of delivery (Supplementary Table S3). Family involvement, antenatal education, and access to support groups were also commonly perceived as helpful influences, highlighting the importance of informational and communication-related factors in childbirth-related decision-making.

These findings are consistent with evidence from other settings demonstrating the important role of counselling quality and shared decision-making in women’s delivery experiences. A qualitative evidence synthesis reported that misalignment between women’s concerns and clinicians’ counselling may limit shared decision-making regarding caesarean delivery [16], while provider-based studies suggest that institutional pressures, fear of litigation, and time constraints can influence counselling practices related to non-medically indicated CS [17].

Evidence from Saudi Arabia, Egypt, and Malaysia further indicates that women may have limited awareness regarding postpartum risks and may rely substantially on family input, particularly when antenatal counselling is inconsistent or insufficient [9, 21, 27]. Together, these findings support the importance of balanced, culturally sensitive, and evidence-based antenatal counselling approaches.

Beyond delivery decisions alone, studies from Jordan and Europe suggest that maternal psychological outcomes, including postnatal depressive symptoms and post-traumatic stress, may be influenced more strongly by communication quality, perceived preparedness, and subjective birth experience than by delivery mode itself [28, 29]. Additional evidence from Turkey, India, Iran, and Sweden similarly highlights the influence of counselling quality, supportive care, and shared decision-making on satisfaction with childbirth and attitudes toward future pregnancies [30–33].

Collectively, the available evidence suggests that counselling quality, communication practices, and experiential support may represent potentially modifiable influences on delivery-related perceptions and preferences in settings with high caesarean section prevalence.

Implications for practice

The findings of this study highlight several practice-relevant considerations for maternity care in the UAE and similar high-access healthcare settings with persistently elevated CS prevalence. The high proportion of women reporting prior caesarean delivery, together with the strong preference for planned CS among women with previous caesarean birth (Table 3), underscores the potential importance of early and structured antenatal counselling during both first and subsequent pregnancies. Counselling initiated early in pregnancy may help address women’s expectations, concerns, and informational needs before delivery preferences become strongly shaped by prior experiences.

The observation that educational attainment and older age remained associated with reported history of caesarean delivery, whereas income level, insurance status, and healthcare facility type did not (Tables 5 and 7), suggests that delivery-related decision-making in this setting may be influenced more strongly by informational, experiential, and perception-related factors than by healthcare access alone. These findings highlight the potential importance of counselling approaches that explicitly address women’s beliefs, prior experiences, and informational needs rather than assuming that higher educational attainment necessarily corresponds to an accurate understanding of delivery-related risks and benefits.

Differences in perceptions regarding maternal recovery, infant outcomes, and future delivery preferences between women with and without prior caesarean delivery further support the influence of prior childbirth experiences on subsequent delivery-related attitudes and preferences (Tables 3, 4 and 6, and 7). Importantly, women themselves identified improved counselling as a key factor perceived to support better decision-making regarding mode of delivery (Supplementary Table S3). Together, these findings support the potential value of balanced, woman-centred, and culturally sensitive counselling approaches that address recovery expectations, neonatal outcomes, uncertainty, and balances between delivery modes.

Overall, the findings support a greater emphasis on communication-focused and shared decision-making approaches to optimise delivery-related counselling while maintaining appropriate access to medically indicated caesarean care.

Strengths and limitations

This study provides a comprehensive assessment of reported CS prevalence and associated sociodemographic, health-system, and perception-related factors among women residing in the UAE. By integrating descriptive, bivariate, and multivariable analyses, including both separate and combined regression models, the study offers a multidimensional and woman-centred perspective on delivery-related experiences and decision-making within a high-access healthcare setting.

Several limitations should nevertheless be acknowledged. Because the questionnaire was disseminated in part through open online recruitment channels, an exact response rate could not be calculated. The use of non-probability convenience sampling, including online recruitment, also limits the representativeness and generalisability of the findings. Participants who were more educated, more health-aware, more digitally connected, or more engaged with maternity-related issues may have been more likely to participate. In addition, recruitment through selected healthcare and community channels may have underrepresented women from other emirates, lower socioeconomic groups, women with limited internet access, or those using different healthcare services. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as reflecting associations within the study sample rather than nationally representative estimates for the UAE population.

The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and raises the possibility of reverse causality, particularly for perception-related variables. Because perceptions were measured at the time of the survey, whereas the outcome was reported as a history of CS, women’s current beliefs may have been shaped by their prior delivery experiences rather than influencing the original delivery mode. Therefore, associations between perceptions and reported CS history should be interpreted as potentially bidirectional and non-causal.

Another important limitation is that the outcome definition of “ever CS” combined women with primary and repeat caesarean deliveries. These groups may differ in their underlying determinants, as repeat CS may additionally be influenced by previous caesarean scar, counselling regarding subsequent delivery options, institutional practices, and prior childbirth experiences. Because the available analysis did not stratify primary and repeat CS, the findings should be interpreted as associations with reported CS history rather than as factors specific to primary or repeat caesarean delivery.

Some regression estimates, particularly those related to maternal recovery perceptions, demonstrated wide confidence intervals, indicating statistical imprecision and limited certainty regarding the magnitude of certain associations. This may partly reflect modest subgroup sizes within some response categories and overlap among related perception constructs. Finally, collapsing Likert-scale response categories may have resulted in some loss of response granularity. However, this approach was used to improve interpretability, reduce sparse cell counts, and enhance the stability of multivariable regression estimates.

Despite these limitations, the study provides relevant insights into potentially modifiable experiential, perception-related, and counselling-related influences on CS patterns in settings with high access to maternity care.

Conclusion

This study identified a high proportion of women with prior deliveries reporting a history of at least one CS within this UAE-based study sample and demonstrated associations between reported CS history, women’s perceptions, prior delivery experiences, and counselling-related factors. Because of the cross-sectional design, these relationships should be interpreted as non-causal and potentially bidirectional.

While most sociodemographic and health-system characteristics showed limited independent association with reported caesarean history, higher educational attainment, age above 30 years, and selected perceptions regarding maternal recovery, mother–baby bonding, and infant outcomes remained associated with prior CS after adjustment, although some estimates were substantially imprecise and should be interpreted with caution.

Within the limitations of the convenience sample, the findings suggest that variation in CS use in high-access healthcare settings may be influenced less by structural barriers to care and more by experiential, perception-related, and communication-related influences. Women’s identification of counselling quality as an important influence on delivery-related decision-making further supports the potential value of balanced, evidence-based, and woman-centred antenatal counselling approaches.

Overall, the study adds to the growing evidence that potentially modifiable influences—particularly counselling practices, communication quality, and women’s perceptions—may meaningfully contribute to CS patterns. Strengthening shared decision-making, supportive maternity care, and contextually sensitive counselling may help balance the mode of delivery, preserving access to medically indicated caesarean delivery.
