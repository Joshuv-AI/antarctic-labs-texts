Sustainable water oxidation enabled by a complex-doped cobalt oxide electrode
Authors: Hongsheng Wang, Jia Lei, Jiashun Wu, Yan Shi, Yunze Zhang, Miao Wang, Hao Fei, Siyuan Wang, Ziyi Wang, Ruoqi Liu, Tong Liu, Ting-Shan Chan, Shu-Chih Haw, Cheng-Wei Kao, Aleksandra Wanda Baron-Wiechec, Changfeng Yan, Hui Kong, Zhenbin Wang, Fu-rong Chen, Jian Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAchieving sustainable water oxidation presents significant challenges, particularly employing cobalt-based electrocatalysts. Despite promising activities, many cobalt-based electrocatalysts undergo in-situ partial restructuring into disordered (oxy)hydroxides, as indicated by the Pourbaix diagram. This restructuring typically degrades structural integrity and electronic conductivity, undermining catalytic stability. Here, we propose a complex doping strategy to stabilize LiCoO2, a cobalt oxide that can be sourced from spent lithium-ion batteries, for sustainable water oxidation. Specifically, by co-doping LiCoO2 with Ni, Fe, and Pd, we mitigate the reconstructed extent of the in-situ generated spinel phase during water oxidation reaction and enhance electrochemical stability. Furthermore, complex doping improves the surface conductivity and facilitates gas removal, boosting mechanical robustness. Consequently, the optimized LiCo0.79Ni0.1Fe0.1Pd0.01O2 achieves a competitive water oxidation stability of over 2000 hours. Additionally, in membrane electrolyzer tests, LiCo0.79Ni0.1Fe0.1Pd0.01O2 outperforms the benchmark RuO2, delivering 2.5 A cm−2 at 1.58 V and maintaining stability for over 1400 hours. By elucidating the role of each dopant in LiCo0.79Ni0.1Fe0.1Pd0.01O2, this work offers critical insights for the rational design of sustainable water splitting electrodes.Subject terms: Electrocatalysis, Hydrogen energy, Electrocatalysis, Electrocatalysis

Introduction

Coupling water electrolysis technologies with renewable electricity to produce green hydrogen represents a promising approach for mitigating the intermittency issue of renewable energies and reducing CO2 emissions1. The performance of catalysts in a water electrolyzer significantly impacts the electricity consumption (approximately 70%–90% of the total hydrogen production cost) and operation durability (typically 10,000–100,000 h)2,3. Substantial research has been dedicated to developing low-cost electrocatalysts for water electrolysis, focusing on the anodic oxygen evolution reaction (OER) that poses a significant energy barrier4,5. Despite the considerable achievements in understanding the water-splitting process and developing electrocatalysts, significant challenges remain in transferring the high performance demonstrated by many designed electrocatalysts in rotating disk electrode (RDE) setups to practical electrolyzer applications. For instance, NiFeOxHy, widely accepted as one of the most active OER electrocatalysts in a typical RDE setup, demonstrated considerably poor activity in the membrane electrode assembly (MEA) configuration6–8. Moreover, they can leach Fe in “Fe-free” electrolytes, losing activity and posing threats to ion exchange membranes9. Beyond activity, stability is another big concern. Many OER electrocatalysts undergo alterations in their physicochemical properties due to in-situ reconstruction, a dynamic process that affects structural and catalytic stability10. For instance, cobalt oxides (e.g., Ba0.5Sr0.5Co0.8Fe0.2O3-delta, Co3O4) usually undergo partial reconstructions into layered (oxy)hydroxides, resulting in sacrificed crystallinity and loss of compositing elements11,12. Although this reconstruction can initially enhance activity, it may ultimately impair practical activity and long-term stability due to the less conductive nature of the reconstructed surface13–16. However, previous studies on catalyst reconstructions were usually conducted at ambient temperature and under small current densities17, which limits the applicability of their findings to practical electrolyzer conditions.

Layered LiCoO2 (LCO) is of particular interest among the various cobalt oxides due to the facile tunability of its cobalt redox and associated lithium composition18,19. Also, the abundant availability of LCO from spent lithium-ion batteries makes it an appealing material for recycling and repurposing in other energy applications20. Nevertheless, the intrinsic OER activity of LCO is limited due to the substantial potential barrier that must be overcome to trigger its favorable surface reconstructions18. Several strategies have been employed to enhance its activity, including elemental doping18,21, exfoliation22, cation insertion23, and electrochemical tuning19. While these strategies have shown various improvements, achieving an OER electrode that combines both high performance and long-term durability suitable for practical electrolyzer conditions remains a significant challenge.

Here, we report a complex doping method to improve the practical OER performance of LCO and clarify the critical role of each doping element. By manipulating the catalyst reconstruction extent under elevated electrolysis temperatures, we have developed a high-performance LiCo0.79Ni0.1Fe0.1Pd0.01O2 (LCNFPdO) electrocatalyst with good stability (over 2000 h) and activity that is competitive with benchmark catalysts.

Results

OER activity and stability

A total of 35 doped LCO compositions were experimentally screened, with the selected doping elements and rationale outlined in Fig. S1. After solid-state synthesis, the layered structure (space group: R-3m) of the single LCO phase was confirmed with X-ray diffraction (XRD, Fig. S2). Subsequently, the OER performance was evaluated across a range of temperatures (22–80 °C) in 1 M KOH using cyclic voltammetry (CV) and linear sweep voltammetry (LSV), as illustrated in Figs. S3 and S4. A summary of the typical results is provided in Fig. 1a and Table S1. Among the tested compositions, Single-element-doped samples (e.g., LCFO, LCNO, LCPdO) show the worst OER performance, binary-element-doped samples (e.g., LCNFO, LCNPdO, LCFPdO) show improved performance compared with single-element-doped samples, and the ternary-element-doped LCNFPdO exhibited the most pronounced OER activity, with a small overpotential of 0.351 V at 25 mA cm−2 (22 °C), 0.337 V at 80 mA cm−2 (50 °C), and 0.256 V at 250 mA cm−2 (80 °C). This trend suggests a non-additive, possibly synergistic effect when combining Ni, Fe, and Pd in enhancing the OER performance. LCNFPdO demonstrated better OER performance to other samples, including commercial RuO2 and Co3O4, and significantly outperformed undoped LCO (Fig. S5). Notably, after 10 CV cycles, LCNFPdO displayed an increased current density, indicating activation possibly due to formation of reconstructed species18 (Fig. 1b). In contrast, the undoped LCO showed minimal change in current density throughout the CV process (Fig. S6). Furthermore, the better OER activity of LCNFPdO under different temperatures is corroborated by its smaller Tafel slopes (Fig. S7) and lower polarization resistance (Figs. 1c and S8). The temperature-dependent kinetic current density plots of LCNFPdO and LCO were fitted, confirming a lower activation energy (Ea, 78.6 kJ mol−1) for OER catalysis by LCNFPdO than that of LCO (86.8 kJ mol−1), as illustrated in Fig. 1d. The specific OER activity of LCNFPdO, normalized by the electrochemical active surface area (ECSA, Fig. S9 and Table S2), was at least two orders of magnitude higher than that of RuO2 within the overpotential range of 350 mV (Fig. S10), indicating its considerable potential for catalyzing OER.

Furthermore, LCNFPdO demonstrated an OER stability of over 2000 h. Specifically, at a current density of 10 mA cm−2 (Fig. 1e), its degradation rate of 0.012 mV h−1 was significantly lower than those of LCO (0.086 mV h−1) and RuO2 (1.594 mV h−1). In addition, it could sustain the OER without significant degradation for more than 2250 h under a harsher condition of 50 mA cm−2, further confirming its good stability (Fig. 1f). These results demonstrate that LCNFPdO displays competitive durability among cobalt oxide-based and other OER catalysts (Table S3), maintaining stable performance under high-temperature alkaline conditions, and firmly distinguishing it as a benchmark material for OER.

Structural and compositional evolution

To investigate the good performance of LCNFPdO, we first analyzed the elemental distribution using energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) in conjunction with high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM, Fig. 2a) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Fig. S11). Both techniques revealed homogeneous elemental distribution in its pristine state (designated as “LCNFPdO pristine”) and OER (denoted as “LCNFPdO 22 °C” and “LCNFPdO 50 °C” for samples post-tested at 22 °C and 50 °C, respectively). The Fe, Co, Ni, and Pd concentrations of the pristine LCNFPdO and LCO samples, as determined by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) in Table S4, agreed well with the expected stoichiometric values. In contrast to the well-maintained elements, including Co, Ni, Fe, and Pd, Li concentration decreased after OER (Table S4), particularly at elevated temperatures, indicating cycling-induced Li loss19,24. The LCNFPdO with complex doping retained a higher Li concentration than LCO, indicating better structural integrity.

We further analyzed the crystal structure. The XRD refinement confirmed the single phase of layered LCNFPdO with an expanded c-axis lattice parameter in reference to that of LCO (Fig. S12 and Tables S5 and S6), possibly due to the larger size of the dopant Pd2+ (0.86 Å), Ni2+ (0.69 Å), and Fe3+ (0.55–0.65 Å) than that of Co3+ (0.55–0.61 Å)25. After OER at 50 °C, both LCNFPdO 50 °C and LCO 50 °C retained their layered structures in the bulk (Fig. 2b), which was further confirmed by the XRD refinement (Fig. S13 and Tables S7 and S8). The possible surface reconstruction caused by OER at varying temperatures was investigated using STEM. After OER, a hierarchical configuration comprising a layered bulk and a reconstructed surface was detected in both LCNFPdO (Figs. 2e, g, S15 and S16) and LCO (Figs. 2f, h, S17 and S18), in contrast to the layered surface for the pristine samples (LCNFPdO: Figs. 2c and S14, LCO: Fig. 2d). The corresponding fast Fourier transform (FFT) patterns of LCNFPdO after OER at 22 °C and at 50 °C, as shown in the insets in Fig. 2e and g, detected strong reflections (highlighted with green circles) of spinel structure at the surface, e.g., (002), (2¯20), and (111¯), which can be attributed to the LiCo2O4-type spinel structure due to the stronger intensity of (111) pattern than (200) pattern18. From the STEM results (Fig. 2f and h), the surface of undoped LCO was also reconstructed into LiCo2O4-spinel structure, agreeing well with previous research18. It is worth noting that FFT patterns even detected slight reflections (e.g., (202¯) and (002)) of Co3O4-type spinel structure at the surface of OER-cycled LCO, as indicated by the yellow circles in Fig. 2f and h, suggesting serious Li loss. Moreover, the reconstructed surface layer of OER-cycled LCO was consistently thicker than that of LCNFPdO after OER (Figs. S15–S18), confirming a larger reconstruction extent. The XRD in Fig. 2b also supported less structural change of LCNFPdO than that of LCO after the OER. The above results show that multi-element doping mitigates reconstruction extent. Specifically, the peak intensity of the 104-diffraction pattern, an indicative of structural disorder in layered LCO, increased significantly after the OER, in contrast to the minimum change of LCNFPdO26–28. The catalyst morphology was additionally examined by SEM (Fig. S19), exhibiting minimal alteration before and after the reaction.

Electronic configuration and reconstruction

X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) was employed to gain insights into the metal valence and local atomic structures. From the X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) shown in Fig. 3a, the Co K-edge peak of LCNFPdO pristine exhibited a slightly positive shift compared to that of LCO pristine, indicating a higher Co valence than +3 (Fig. 3a, b), which could be attributed to charge compensation by the introduction of lower-valence dopants such as Ni2+ and Pd2+, as supported by the Bader charge analysis (Table S9 and Supplementary data 1). Quantitative electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) measurements of LCO and LCNFPdO suggest that oxygen vacancies are not a major factor influencing Co valence (Fig. S20). After OER at 22 °C, a notable shift to high energy of the K-edge peak was observed for both LCO and LCNFPdO, indicating Co valence increase29. Such oxidation was even more pronounced after OER at 50 °C, suggesting facilitated reconstruction by high temperature. Notably, the pre-edge peak around 7719 eV, assigned to the 1 s to 4p transition of Co for both LCNFPdO and LCO, disappeared after OER, indicating the existence of high-valence Co30. It is worth noting that the Co valence increase in LCNFPdO was less than that of LCO after OER at all studied temperatures, as shown in Fig. 3b, supporting less oxidation. In addition to bulk-average XAFS, Co L3-edge soft X-ray absorption spectra (sXAS) in Fig. 3c and Co 2p X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) in Figs. 3d and S21 (fitting parameters in Table S10) detected a consistent trend: OER increased surface Co valence, and high temperature promoted this oxidation. By deconvoluting the Co 2p XPS, we found that the increase in surface Co valence was also mitigated for LCNFPdO (former) compared to that of LCO (later): increase of 0.06 vs. 0.13 at 22 °C and 0.15 vs. 0.24 at 50 °C.

The surface Co electronic structure evolution was also characterized by Raman spectra, as shown in Fig. S22. The Raman spectra of both LCNFPdO and LCO pristine showed two characteristic peaks at 487 cm−1 and 595 cm−1, corresponding to the Eg bending and A1g stretching mode31. But the Raman spectrum of pristine LCNFPdO was asymmetric with a shoulder peak in the lower wavenumber region, concurring with its increased surface Co valence state32. After OER at 22 °C and 50 °C, the surface lithium of LCO was deintercalated and cobalt was oxidized, as supported by the negative shift of Raman peaks to lower-wavenumber regions at around 460 cm−1 and 564 cm−1 that can be assigned to the Eg and A1g modes of CoO233. However, the two peaks of LCNFPdO had a smaller shift degree after OER than those of LCO, which was consistent with its higher Li content and lower Co oxidation state. To provide operando insight into surface reconstruction, we performed in-situ Raman spectroscopy at 22 °C (Fig. S23). As the applied potential increased from 1.20 V to 1.55 V vs. reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE), both LCO and LCNFPdO developed two new peaks around 465 cm−1 and 565 cm−1, consistent with vibrations of CoO2 resulting from Li deintercalation. These findings closely match our ex-situ Raman observations. Notably, LCNFPdO exhibits smaller Raman peak shifts than LCO under the same polarization potential, indicating a mitigated structural transformation. Different from Co, Ni, Fe, and Pd on LCNFPdO surface exhibited minimal valence changes after OER, as demonstrated by the XPS and sXAS results in Figs. S24 and S25.

The Fourier-transformed extended X-ray absorption fine structure in Fig. 3e and Fig. S26 showed that LCNFPdO 22 °C/50 °C and LCO 22 °C/50 °C have shorter Co-O bond lengths compared with the pristine samples, supporting a higher cobalt valence after OER and agreeing well with XANES results above34. The curve fitting of the Fourier-transformed EXAFS (Figs. S27 and S28 and Tables S11 and S12) demonstrated increased coordination numbers for both LCNFPdO and LCO after OER, which concurred with their greater Co valence-state increase. From the wavelet transform of k3-weighted Co K-edge EXAFS in Fig. S29, two intensity maxima were observed at around k = 5.9 Å−1 and 7.2 Å−1, corresponding to the Co-O and Co-Co coordination, respectively. The more obvious color change of Co-O coordination in LCO after OER supported its larger Co valence increase than LCNFPdO.

As the major redox center, cobalt oxidation of different extents during OER led to distinct Li loss for charge balance, as supported by the online electrochemical quartz crystal microbalance (EQCM) tests (Fig. 3g, h). During CV cycling at both 22 °C and 50 °C, the weight loss of LCNFPdO was less than that of LCO, suggesting a reduced delithiation degree. ICP-MS (Table S4) corroborated this for LCNFPdO and LCO after OER. Accordingly, ToF-SIMS (Fig. 3f) detected a larger Li concentration on the top surface of LCNFPdO than that of LCO after OER. The loss of Li triggered the transformation of the layered surface into a spinel structure (Fig. 2e). During the OER process, Li+ extraction drives the oxidation of Co3+ in LCO toward Co4+. In Li-deficient Li1-xCoO2, the layered structure is unstable and readily transforms into a LiCo2O4-type spinel phase35,36. Consequently, greater lithium loss promotes deeper reconstruction and the formation of more spinel species. In contrast, LCNFPdO undergoes less Co oxidation (Fig. 3a–d) and lithium loss, resulting in a thinner reconstructed layer than LCO and thus a mitigated reconstruction extent, as illustrated in the scheme in Fig. S30. This modulated reconstruction of LCNFPdO can be reflected from its higher-wavenumber Co–O Raman bands than LCO (Fig. S31) and markedly reduced metal leaching after prolonged operation at 100 mA cm−2 (Table S13). The above results matched previous research that a higher initial Co valence in LiCoO2 can increase the energy barrier for further Co oxidation and led to less Li loss and cobalt oxidation extent during OER18.

Despite less reconstruction extent, the reconstructed surface of LCNFPdO might be more favorable for interacting with the OER intermediates: its oxygen K-edge sXAS (Fig. 3i) detected an obvious rise of the resonance near ~535 eV, which was attributed to the adsorbed hydroxy groups on its surface12,37,38, in contrast to that of LCO. The presence of more adsorbed oxygen species on the surface of LCNFPdO than LCO after the OER was also confirmed by O 1s XPS (Fig. S32) and ToF-SIMS (Fig. S33). Shortly, the complex doping in LCNFPdO effectively mitigated the in-situ Co redox and Li loss, maintaining electrochemical stability and generating more favorable surface species for OER electrocatalysis.

Transport capabilities

The OER overpotential primarily comprises kinetic, ohmic, and mass transfer contributions39. In addition to intrinsic activity, the electronic conductivity and mass transfer properties of the studied catalysts were also examined. To distinguish intrinsic catalytic effects from diffusion limitations, we decomposed the iR-corrected overpotentials using an extended Butler–Volmer/Tafel analysis40, as shown in Fig. S34. The extracted kinetic loss and mass transport loss demonstrate that LCNFPdO outperforms undoped LCO in both metrics, confirming that its good OER performance arises from a balanced enhancement of charge-transfer dynamics and bubble-mediated mass transfer. Contrary to Ni doping, Fe, Pd, or their combinations with other doping elements demonstrated to enhance the electrical conductivity of LCO (Fig. 4a). Notably, LCNFPdO manifested an electrical conductivity of 2.6 mS cm−1, approximately one order of magnitude greater than that of LCO (0.35 mS cm−1) and surpassing conventional Co oxyhydroxides and NiFe oxides (Fig. S35). The high electrical conductivity was favorable to lowering the OER overpotential (Fig. 4b). To evaluate the mass transport (e.g., gas removal) capabilities, we checked the current density limit (Fig. 4c, d) and quantified the number of bubbles (Fig. 4e, f) at high OER currents. The current density limit is defined as the point at which the potential difference between tests without and with rotation (1600 rpm) reaches 10 mV. A high current limit indicates a rapid mass transfer rate between the catalyst surface and electrolyte, enabling the delivery of large currents. Compared with pristine and other element-doped LCO, LCNFPdO achieved the highest current density limit, highlighting its better mass transfer capability. The differential Tafel analysis further corroborates the significantly improved mass transport capability of LCNFPdO (Fig. S36). Fast mass transfer capability might contribute to practical water oxidation performance (Fig. 4d). Furthermore, the number of generated bubbles was counted at various current densities using a homemade transparent cell (Fig. S37). A comparison with multiple control samples revealed that the combination of Fe and Pd doping markedly accelerated the removal of gas bubbles (Fig. 4e, f), which favored to reduce the concentration polarization41. The beneficial effect of Pd in facilitating the rapid diffusion of water molecules has also been reported in other catalytic systems42. The surface of a gas-evolving catalyst typically experiences a competitive adsorption of the aqueous electrolyte and the generated bubbles. The contact angle test (Fig. S38) further demonstrated a lower angle of LCNFPdO (30.1°) than that of LCO (32.3°), implying better hydrophilicity and bubble removal capability. From the above (Fig. 3i), the reconstructed surface of LCNFPdO enabled the adsorption of more oxygen-containing species during the OER, which might further improve its hydrophilicity to effectively remove oxygen bubbles and favor the subsequent OER processes43–45, maintaining thinner bubble layers at large current densities (Fig. S39). Such improved adsorption of OER intermediates was also supported by the decay analysis of the open circuit potential (OCP) in Figs. 4g, h, S40, and S41, which reflected the coverage of reaction intermediates on electrode surfaces46. By comparison, LCNFPdO demonstrated lower decay slopes (absolute value) than LCO at both 22 °C and 50 °C (Figs. 4g and S40), indicating the surface of LCNFPdO had greater accessibility to reaction intermediates47,48. Such improved transport property might be attributed to the synergistic effect of Ni with other doping elements (Fig. 4h), and the complex doping of Ni, Fe, and Pd favored it most, enhancing the kinetics of OER. All the above contributed to reducing the mass transfer overpotential and favoring the maintenance of mechanical stability, particularly during large-current OER electrolysis.

Theoretical calculations

To understand the modulated performance, we performed density functional theory (DFT) calculations to investigate the OER stability and activity. Based on the preceding TEM analysis, the surface of LCO and LCNFPdO reconstructed into a LiCo2O4-type spinel structure. Moreover, the surface Li loss was close to 50% (Fig. 3f), and the ICP-MS data (Table S4) confirmed negligible dissolution of Co, Ni, Fe, or Pd for LCNFPdO after OER. As a result, a calculation model of Li40Co63Ni8Fe8PdO160 (i.e., Li0.5Co0.79Ni0.1Fe0.1Pd0.01O2) (Fig. 5a) was applied, which was identified as the most energetically stable configuration using the special quasi-random structures algorithm. The half-delithiated spinel phase model was consistent with previous reports18,35,36. We observed that the Ni and Fe dopants tended to cluster, while the Pd and Ni dopants remained separated. These chemical arrangement patterns were then used to construct the complex Li0.5Co0.79Ni0.1Fe0.1Pd0.01O2 catalyst surface for subsequent performance investigations (Fig. S42).

Figure 5b shows the calculated dissolution free energies (ΔGdiss) of LiCo2O4 and Li0.5Co0.79Ni0.1Fe0.1Pd0.01O2 (Supplementary data 1) at varying potentials in alkaline media. A higher ΔGdiss value indicates greater catalyst instability under electrochemical working conditions. For LiCo2O4, as the applied potential shifts from 1.23 V to 2.00 V, the calculated ΔGdiss decreases from 0.45 to 0.19 eV/atom. These large ΔGdiss values suggest that LiCo2O4 is unstable under working conditions and tends to decompose into CoOOH(s) and LiOH(aq). This result is in line with our experimental observations. In contrast, doping with Ni, Fe, and Pd considerably reduces the ΔGdiss to 0.21 eV/atom at 1.23 V and 0 eV/atom at 2.00 V. Notably, when the applied potential exceeds 1.55 V, the ΔGdiss becomes 0.03 eV/atom. This value falls well within the stable region where kinetics, driven by solid-solid phase transformation49, play a key role. Specifically, the formation Co3O4(s) and NiPdO3(s) remarkably lowers the ΔGdiss, consequently enhancing the catalyst stability of LCNFPdO under operating conditions. This result aligns well with our experimental observations, as no obvious leaching was detected for LCNFPdO via ICP-MS (Table S4), unlike the Fe-containing hydroxides9. Close examination of the decomposition products reveals that this enhanced catalyst stability primarily arises from the Pd dopant, which facilitates the formation of NiPdO3(s) and Li0.5Co0.79Ni0.1Fe0.1Pd0.01O2(s).

Determining the surface adsorption state under electrochemical working conditions is a crucial first step in studying surface activity. Therefore, we calculated the surface Pourbaix diagram for LCNFPdO, as shown in Fig. 5c. Under typical alkaline OER conditions (e.g., pH = 14, potential = 1.6 V), the catalyst surface is covered by O* at the Ni-Co bridge site. At potentials higher than 1.8 V, O* adsorption becomes accessible at the Co, Ni, and Fe top sites. Coupling two adsorbed O* species at these top sites directly forms O2 gas during DFT structure optimization, a result attributed to the weak binding of O* on the transition metal oxide50. This potential-independent O2 formation indicates that an oxide pathway mechanism is unlikely for LCNFPdO. Furthermore, a surface with oxygen vacancies is only stable at potentials below −0.61 V vs. SHE, which also rules out the lattice oxygen mechanism for OER. In light of these findings, the adsorbate evolution reaction mechanism was applied to investigate the OER activity in this work.

To elucidate the role of each dopant, we calculated the OER activity for LCNFPdO and six other systems for comparison (Table S14 and Supplementary data 1). With the exception of the LCNPdO system, all catalysts reside on the right side of the volcano, as shown in Fig. 5d. This indicates that the formation of O* is the potential-determining step (PDS) due to weak O* binding. For single-element doping, the calculated overpotentials (ηOER) for LCFO (0.90 V) and LCPdO (0.76 V) are higher than that of pristine LCO (0.68 V), suggesting poorer OER activity. In contrast, Ni doping reduces the overpotential to 0.61 V, indicating enhanced activity. Among the binary-doped systems, LCFPdO shows a slight improvement over LCO with a calculated ηOER of 0.67 V. The LCNFO system exhibits significantly enhanced activity with an overpotential of 0.54 V, which can be attributed to electronic optimization between Ni and Fe. In contrast, the LCNPdO system has a higher overpotential of 0.77 V and notably undergoes a PDS shift from O* to OOH* formation, highlighting Pd’s role in electronic restructuring. For the ternary-doped system, LCNFPdO achieves the lowest overpotential of 0.46 V, suggesting that Pd further optimizes the NiFe electronic framework. Taken together, these results indicate that Ni is a pivotal active site that synergizes with Co, and its activity is further amplified by Fe through electronic cooperation. To establish fundamental mechanistic insights, we conducted integrated Bader charge and density of states (DOS) analyses. Bader charge analysis (Table S9) reveals that Ni/Fe co-doping facilitates a substantial charge transfer enhancement of about 0.3e on Co sites. Concurrently, a 0.1e enhancement in charge transfer at Co sites was observed for LCPdO relative to LCO. This demonstrates Pd’s significant contribution to electronic structure modulation. Complementarily, total DOS analysis (Fig. S43 and Supplementary data 1) demonstrates Fe-doping reduces the band gap by 0.4 eV, while LCNFPdO manifests metallic conductivity through further electronic restructuring. The enhanced conductivity is an important indicator of catalysts. Collectively, these synergistic electronic modifications constitute the fundamental mechanism driving enhanced catalytic activity.

Based on our experimental results, we speculate that the mass transport of reactants (H2O) and products (O2) may also influence OER performance. Therefore, the adsorption energies for H2O and O2 were calculated as shown in Fig. 5e. The calculated O2 adsorption energies for LCNFPdO and LCO were 1.10 eV and 0.35 eV, respectively. This result suggests that LCNFPdO possesses weakened O2 adsorption compared to LCO. Such weakened O2 adsorption facilitates the desorption of O2 from the electrode surface. The calculated H2O adsorption energies for LCNFPdO and LCO were −1.07 eV and −0.33 eV, respectively. This result suggests that LCNFPdO has a stronger tendency for H2O adsorption compared with LCO, which is beneficial for enhancing OER efficiency. Comprehensive comparison in Fig. S44 demonstrates Fe doping inhibits H2O/O2 adsorption, whereas Ni and Pd synergistically enhance surface binding. This adsorption dichotomy stems from Co-site electronic modulation: augmented charge transfer elevates valence states and local electronegativity, intensifying adsorbate interactions.

AEMWE performance

To verify the practical application, the catalysts studied were fabricated into the MEA for the AEMWE tests (Fig. 6a). At the same voltage, LCNFPdO achieved a current density much larger than those of RuO2, IrO2, α-Co(OH)2, Co3O4, and β-Co(OH)2 (Fig. 6b). This performance was also accompanied by a lower polarization impedance (Fig. 6c). With iR correction, LCNFPdO delivered 2.5 A cm−2 at only 1.58 V (Fig. 6b), suggesting great application potential. The three-electrode electrolyzer tests confirmed a lower mass transport resistance of LCNFPdO than LCO, as shown in Fig. S45. In addition, the AEMWE cell with LCNFPdO as the anode sustained the current density of 500 mA cm−2 without any degradation for over 1420 h (Fig. 6d). Such a performance is comparable to the highest values reported for cobalt-oxide–based electrocatalysts (Table S15).

Discussion

This work proposed a complex-element doping strategy to yield a sustainable cobalt oxide electrode for highly active and stable OER. The multi-element doping mitigated the reconstructed extent of the in-situ formed spinel surface during the OER, forming a stable surface with significantly enhanced activity at industrial temperatures. The obtained LCNFPdO outperformed RuO2 in OER activity and demonstrated stability of over 2000 h without serious degradation, setting a benchmark for cobalt-based OER electrocatalysts. In addition, the mechanism underlying the complex doping-improved performance was also clarified: (a) The introduction of lower-valence Ni and Pd dopants increased the cobalt valence in pristine LCNFPdO, mitigated its in-situ cobalt oxidation during the OER, thus reducing surface reconstruction extent and improving OER stability. Furthermore, the reconstructed LiCo2O4-type spinel phase of low thickness favored a higher intrinsic OER activity by facilitating the adsorption of oxygen intermediates on the catalyst surface, contributing to higher OER activity. (c) The doping of Fe and Pd also enhanced the electronic conductivity and gas transport properties, resulting in fast reaction kinetics and rapid removal of bubbles. Shortly, these results shed light on the directional designing of active OER catalysts by multiple elements doping and represent a significant advancement in manipulating in situ generated active species to prepare high-performance OER catalysts at industrial temperatures.

Methods

Sample preparation

The layered catalysts were prepared via a solid-state method based on a previous literature51. A stoichiometric amount of specific raw reactants, including Li2CO3 (99.9%, Aladdin), Co3O4 (99.9%, Macklin), Fe2O3 (≥99%, Aladdin), NiO (99%, Aladdin), Pd(NO3)2 (0.216 M, Kojima Chemicals Co., Ltd.), Na2CO3 (≥99.5%, Macklin), K2CO3 (99.5%, Macklin), (MgCO3)4·Mg(OH)2·5H2O (98%, Macklin), MnO2 (99%, Aladdin), RuO2 (Ru 75.2% min, Alfa Aesar), Ce(NO3)3 (≥98%, Wako), Cr2O3 (99%, Aladdin), La2O3 (99.9%, Aladdin), TiO2 (P25, Macklin), MoO2 (99%, Aladdin), ZnO (99%, Dieckmann), Cu(OH)2 (≥96%, Macklin), CoCl2·6H2O (99.99%, Aladdin), CoSO4·7H2O (99%, Aladdin), and LiF (99%, Aladdin), were mixed well and then sintered for 12 h in ambient air with a heating ramping rate of 10 °C min−1. The thermal treatment temperature for catalyst preparation has been optimized, and 700–850 °C is suitable for LCO (Fig. S46), among which 800 °C is chosen as the sintering temperature in this research considering proper kinetics and high-purity phase.

α-Co(OH)2 was synthesized by CoCl2·6H2O (1 mmol, 99.99%, Aladdin) and NaCl (99.5%, Aladdin, 2.5 × g), both of which were dissolved in ultrapure water (150 mL, 18.2 MΩ cm) with stirring (solution A). NH3·H2O (0.133 mL, 25–28%, Aladdin) was mixed with ultrapure water (50 mL) and dropped into solution A under stirring by a peristaltic pump (10 mL h−1). The precipitation was washed by ultrapure water and finally dried.

β-Co(OH)2 was prepared by dropping CoSO4·7H2O (0.1 M, 10 mL, 99%, Aladdin) into a stirring NaOH aqueous solution (0.2 M, 10 mL, 96%, Aladdin). After stirring for one hour, the precipitation was rinsed three times by centrifugation with ultrapure water and finally dried. The XRD of synthesized α-Co(OH)2 and β-Co(OH)2 are presented in Fig. S47.

RuO2 (Alfa Aesar), IrO2 (Aladdin), Co3O4 (99.9%, Macklin), and NiFe2O4 (≥99.5%, Aladdin) were used without further treatment.

Characterizations

XRD measurements were conducted on an X-ray diffractometer (PANalytical X’Pert3 Powder, Cu Kα, λ = 0.15406 nm). The corresponding Rietveld refinements were performed with the General Structure Analysis System (GSAS-II) program. The XAFS and sXAS were collected at the TLS 01C1 and TLS 20A1 beamlines of the National Synchrotron Radiation Research Center, Taiwan. The XANES and EXAFS data were processed and analyzed using the Demeter software package Athena52, and the energy at the maximum value of the first derivative of its XANES was defined as the E0 for energy correction. The EXAFS results of the Co K edge were fitted by Artemis52. Wavelet transform was used to analyze the EXAFS data by a self-developed program53. XPS results were obtained on a Thermo Fisher Nexsa spectrometer using an Al Kα source. For ex-situ XRD and XAS characterizations, catalyst powders were loaded onto carbon paper substrates prior to the OER measurements. The preparation of post-OER sample involved 10 CV cycles (5 mV s−1, 1.02–1.62 V vs. RHE, 1 M KOH, 22 °C or 50 °C) during which the current stabilized. Subsequently, a 30-min chronoamperometry hold at 1.62 V vs. RHE was applied during which no notable current decay was observed. The samples were then rinsed with deionized water and dried under vacuum at 25 °C for 12 h before characterization.

HAADF-STEM images were taken by JEOL-2100F equipped with an energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy detector. A field emission SEM (Zeiss SIGMA 500) mounted with an energy-dispersive X-ray detector was used for morphology and elemental analysis. Raman spectra were collected in a Raman spectrometer (Renishaw inVia Qontor) with the laser at 100 mW (0.05% intensity) and 532 nm. In-situ Raman spectroscopy was performed using a GSL (Gaoss Union) electrochemical cell coupled to the instrument. Measurements employed a 532 nm excitation source with a three-electrode configuration: carbon cloth (Toray Industries) working electrode, Pt wire counter electrode (Gaoss Union), and Ag/AgCl reference electrode (Gaoss Union, 1 M KCl solution) in 1 M KOH electrolyte. The system was stabilized at each potential for 15 min prior to spectral acquisition, which commenced precisely at the 10-min interval. An Iontof 5 spectrometer was used for the ToF-SIMS measurements. The analysis was done with a pulsed 30 keV Bi+ primary ion source and the depth profiling by sputtering with a 1–2 keV Cs+ beam. The pressure was 10−7 Pa. IonSpec software was used for data acquisition and post-processing analysis. Negative ion profiles were recorded due to their sensitivity to fragments originating from oxide matrixes. ICP-MS (NexION 350D Perkin-Elmer) with a mass resolution of 0.3–3.0 a.m.u. was conducted to measure element concentration. The mass variation was characterized by EQCM (10 M Gamry Instruments Inc.) with a working electrode of a gold quartz crystal (5 MHz). EPR spectra were recorded with a Bruker EMXplus X-band spectrometer (≈9.84 GHz) at 25 °C. The spectra were averaged over 5 scans with 1000 points per sweep. Each 2 mg sample was measured under identical settings. The g‑factor was calculated using g = hν/βH. Oxygen vacancy concentrations were quantified by comparing EPR peak areas.

Electrochemical assessment

The OER activity was evaluated in 1 M KOH (≥95%, Macklin) with a three-electrode set-up measured by an electrochemical workstation (Gamry 1010E). The electrolyte was freshly prepared before each experiment using ultrapure (18.2 MΩ·cm) water (150 mL) as the solvent. It was then stored sealed in a chemical-resistant glass reagent bottle and used on the same day. Glassy carbon (Gaoss Union, 3 mm, <50 °C) and carbon paper (Toray Industries, 0.5 cm × 0.5 cm, to avoid catalyst peeling off at the reaction temperature ≥50 °C) were utilized as working electrode, and graphite rod (Gaoss Union), and Ag/AgCl (Gaoss Union, 1 M KCl filling solution) served as counter and reference electrode, respectively. The catalyst ink was prepared by dispersing the mixture of 6 mg of active material with 2 mg of Vulcan XC-72 carbon into a solution made of 0.95 mL ethanol (AR, Uni-Chem) and 0.05 mL of 5 wt% Nafion under ultrasonic treatment for 30 min, and 1 mg cm−2 active material was covered on working electrode by drop casting. CV and LSV were conducted at a scan rate of 5 mV s−1 in the tests. The ECSA was then calculated using ECSA = Cdl/(Cs · m), where m is the loading mass of the catalysts on the electrode and Cs is the reference capacitance per unit area (40 μF cm−255). Notably, the catalyst ink used for ECSA testing was prepared without the addition of XC-72 carbon. The test temperature was controlled by a thermostatic water bath (Sunne Inc., Shanghai, China). EIS was obtained using a Gamry 1010E electrochemical workstation over a frequency range from 1 MHz to 0.1 Hz at 1.65 V vs. RHE with an AC amplitude of 10 mV.

MEA preparation and single-cell tests

The MEAs were fabricated using a hand spray-coating method for both the anode and cathode catalyst layers. The anode catalyst ink was prepared by dispersing the catalyst powder in a mixed solvent of deionized water and isopropanol (mass ratio = 1:4:5). Subsequently, 5 wt% Nafion solution was added, resulting in a Nafion content of 7 wt% in the final ink. The ink was ultrasonicated for 1 h to ensure homogeneous dispersion and then uniformly sprayed onto a nickel foam substrate (Kunshan Shengshijingxin New Material Co., Ltd, China, 1 cm2). The anode catalyst loading was controlled at 10.0 ± 0.5 mg cm−2 (1 mg cm−2 for RuO2 and IrO2), determined by weight difference before and after coating. For the cathode, a 5 wt% Pt/C catalyst (Alfa Aesar) was used. The ink formulation consisted of catalyst, deionized water, and isopropanol in a weight ratio of 1:8:9. After adding 5 wt% Nafion solution (final Nafion content 10 wt%), the ink was ultrasonicated for 1 h and then sprayed onto a carbon paper substrate (TGP-H-120T, Toray). The Pt loading was 0.5 mg cm−2.

A commercial Sustainion® membrane (X37-50, Grade T, Dioxide Materials) was employed as the anion exchange membrane for all single-cell tests. Prior to use, the membrane was pre-treated by soaking in 1 M KOH for 12 h to ensure full ion exchange. The MEA was assembled with an active geometric area of 1.0 cm2 (as shown in Fig. 6a). The single cell hardware was composed of nickel-coated stainless-steel endplates to ensure chemical stability in alkaline media.

All electrochemical measurements were conducted in 1 M KOH electrolyte, supplied to both anode and cathode chambers. Polarization curves were recorded at 80 °C by a Gamry 5000E electrochemical workstation with 30 s hods at each current density ranging from 0.10 A cm−2 to 2.50 A cm−2, followed by a reverse scan. Voltage and current density data were recorded during the downward scan. The durability of the MEA was evaluated by operating the cell at a constant current density of 500 mA cm−2 at 60 °C with a programmable DC power supply (Tommens eTM-K1560SPL).

High-frequency resistance (HFR) was measured via EIS using a Gamry 5000E electrochemical workstation over a frequency range from 1 MHz to 1 Hz at a cell potential of 1.8 V with an AC amplitude of 10 mV. The HFR was obtained from the intercept of the Nyquist plot with the real axis. Three independent measurements were performed, and the mean and standard deviation were subsequently calculated from the replicate data. The voltage data are presented without iR compensation unless specifically indicated.

Three-electrode AEMWE preparation

The anode and cathode used in three-electrode AEMWE are the same with the two-electrode AEMWE introduced above, and the only difference is the membrane. Versogen A40 membrane (thickness 40 μm) was used. Prior to use, the membrane was equilibrated under ambient laboratory conditions for 1 h before removing the protective films. This step allows the bicarbonate form of the membrane to convert to the hydroxide form, which is required to achieve optimal ionic conductivity. To ensure full conversion to the hydroxide form, the membrane was immersed in 0.5 M KOH aqueous solution at 22 °C for 1 h, after which the solution was replaced with freshly prepared 0.5 M KOH and the membrane was immersed for an additional 1 h. Following the two-step soaking process, the membrane was rinsed thoroughly with deionized water until the rinse water reached approximately neutral pH (~7). During handling, exposure of the membrane to ambient air was minimized to prevent CO2 uptake, which would convert the membrane back to the bicarbonate form. Three-electrodes system was established based on our homemade cell (Fig. S45a). The membrane was extended to an extra reference electrode chamber (Fig. S45b) and filled with 0.01 M KOH to ensure good ionic conduction with Ag/AgCl reference electrode. The testing procedure is the same with two-electrode single cell.

Computational methods

All spin-polarized DFT calculations were conducted using the Vienna ab initio simulation package within the projected-augmented wave method58,59. The Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof functional was used to describe the exchange-correlation interaction60. The DFT + U method was utilized to correct the self-interaction error with Hubbard U parameters of 3.32, 5.30, and 6.20 eV for Co, Fe, and Ni, respectively61,62. The convergence criteria of the electronic total energy and structure relaxation were set to 10−5 eV and 0.05 eV Å-1, respectively. A plane wave energy cutoff of 450 eV was applied.

DFT method: The Brillouin zone was sampled using the Gamma scheme with a 1 × 2 × 1 k-point grid for the surface models with 5 atomic layers of LiCo2O463. A vacuum thickness of 16 Å was added along the z direction to avoid periodic interactions, and the dipole correction was applied as well. The (111) surface was selected for exploring the catalytic activity, as it is the most stable surface of the spinel structures64. The doped structure was generated by the special quasi-random structures algorithm implemented in the ATAT package65 and the most energetically favorable one was then selected for the activity and stability studies66. The catalyst stability was assessed by the Strongly Constrained and Appropriately Normed (SCAN) functional calculated bulk Pourbaix diagram68,69. In SCAN calculations, the plane-wave energy cut-off was 520 eV. The electronic energy and structure relaxation were converged to 10−5 eV and 0.02 eV Å−1, respectively. The Hubbard U parameters for Ni, Fe, and Co were empirically calibrated by fitting to experimental redox potentials of their corresponding metal oxides (Table S17)70. This approach ensures the computed U values accurately reproduce redox energetics critical to surface catalytic processes.

Surface model construction: To construct a reasonable surface model, three principles governed surface model construction: (1) We constructed the NiFePd-doped LCO bulk structure by optimizing 128 quasi-random structures and selecting the most stable one. It is observed that the Ni and Fe dopants tend to be located with each other, while the Pt and Ni dopants sit aways from each other. These chemical insights were applied to construct the complex LCFNPdO catalyst surface; (2) Based on previous studies, the (111) facet is the most stable surface in spinel LCO and is therefore used as a surface model; (3) The presence of Pd in the sublayer is more stable than that on the top layer (Formation energy difference: −0.10 eV). Meanwhile, the more active Ni and Fe are often exposed on the topmost layer71,72. The final surface structure model was finally determined by considering all these factors.

All relevant adsorption sites on LCNFPdO were evaluated, including top sites of Co, Fe, and Ni atoms and Ni-Co bridge sites (Table S18). Structural relaxation revealed:(1) Intermediates at Ni/Co top sites consistently migrated to Ni-Co bridge sites, producing identical structures and energies; (2) Fe sites exhibited poor catalytic activity despite electronic modulation effects. The Ni-Co bridge site was consequently identified as the optimal active center.

Magnetic configuration: To address magnetic complexity in transition metal systems, we implemented a rigorous configuration selection protocol. The pristine slab model was first optimized to determine its lowest-energy magnetic moment configuration; Identical NUPDOWN values were subsequently applied to corresponding adsorbate systems; All configurations must satisfy four criteria: absence of antiferromagnetic ordering, adherence to the ∆GOOH-∆GOH scaling relationship (2.8–3.3 V), total magnetization within 4 μB tolerance, and the selection of the lowest-energy state for multiple valid configurations; Systems failing these criteria were recalculated with NUPDOWN adjustments (±1 μB increments) and modified initial moments. Total magnetization data for all configurations are documented in Table S19.
