Decreasing attentional bias for food cues in satiated women with obesity: a pilot study examining feasibility and acceptability
Authors: Nienke C Jonker, Renske W de Jong, Peter J de Jong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAttentional bias to food when in a satiated state may promote dysfunctional food intake, thereby counteracting the wish to reduce weight in people with obesity. Reducing attentional bias when satiated via an attention bias modification (ABM) procedure may thus be a helpful intervention to reduce obesity.ObjectiveAs an important first step, this study examined the feasibility and acceptability of a relatively high-dose ABM using the Bouncing Image Training task (BITT) in individuals with obesity. The intervention required engagement in self-applied ABM sessions while in a satiated state (i.e., after a regular meal).MethodsParticipants were 25 women aged between 18 and 35 with obesity (body mass index > 30) and with an intention to diet. They were instructed to perform the food-based BITT as a daily ABM training for 3 weeks within their home context and following a regular meal. Feasibility and acceptability were assessed qualitatively and quantitatively.ResultsFindings indicated that for the targeted group of women with obesity, it was acceptable and feasible to self-apply the training-sessions within the required satiated state. There was no dropout, and completing > 80% of trials was achieved: 92% of the sessions, 97% of those on the intended day, and 94% in a satiated state. Adding further to the feasibility/acceptability of the current approach, the intervention received a rating between neutral and positive, and there was no evidence for an adverse effect (i.e., no increase) on craving.ConclusionsThe findings support the feasibility of setting the critical next step of testing the BITT’s efficacy to facilitate a decrease in attentional bias to food cues and a decrease in food intake in women with obesity.Teaser textAttention bias modification (ABM) in obesity should be applied when satiated. Feasibility and acceptability of a daily 3-week ABM program were assessed. Introduction

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), “..obesity is one of today’s most blatantly visible – yet most neglected – public health problems” (World Health [28]). Obesity is defined as abnormal or excessive fat accumulation that presents a risk to health and is indexed as having a body mass index (BMI, weight/height2) of 30 or higher. The worldwide prevalence of obesity is increasing, and around 13% of the world’s adult population was obese in 2016 (World Health [29]). Many people attempt to decrease their weight by restricting their food intake [10, 35]. However, they often fail to adhere to their diet [22], or they regain their weight after initially having lost it [11]. Only around 20% of individuals with overweight/obesity are able to obtain lasting weight loss [37]. It is therefore of importance to gain more insight into the factors that play a role in the maintenance of obesity and to develop suitable interventions.

An important starting point for identifying crucial factors is our environment. The increased availability and accessibility of unhealthy foods has been identified as a major driver in the obesity pandemic [24, 33]. Being constantly exposed to food cues makes it difficult not to indulge and, as such, difficult to maintain or obtain a healthy weight. However, not everyone who is exposed to this tempting environment has difficulty maintaining a healthy weight, which might be explained by individual differences in our tendency to attend to food cues in our environment. According to the incentive-sensitization theory [2, 30], signals of food get priority in processing after repeated associations between these signals and the experience of reward (e.g., nice taste or feeling comfortable). As a consequence, signals of food will become signals of reward and thereby grab people’s attention (i.e., attentional bias (AB)) [30]. Individuals with a relatively strong AB for food cues will be tempted much more often, would have a greater chance of seeking out and eating food, and would therefore be more likely to become overweight or obese.

However, systematic reviews showed no evidence for consistent differences between individuals with obesity and those with a healthy weight in their AB to food cues1 [12, 14]. Although meta-analyses show that ABM has the potential to change AB to food and reduce food consumption and food craving, these findings are mostly based on studies in healthy weight individuals [13, 32]. Furthermore, attentional bias modification (ABM) interventions in individuals with obesity by means of adapted visual probe tasks have thus far been found ineffective in influencing eating behavior of [5, 34], even when these interventions were successful in decreasing AB to food [20, 21]. In other words, what would be expected theoretically has not been found (consistently) in empirical research. Although one possible conclusion is that the theory does not apply to eating behavior in individuals with obesity, it could also be that important factors have been overlooked.

One potentially critical but overlooked factor is feeding status. The rewarding value of food is higher for healthy weight individuals when they are hungry than when they are satiated [17]. Furthermore, when individuals are satiated (stuffed), the rewarding value of food seems to have dropped to zero [3, 31]. In line with this, women with a healthy weight were found to have increased attention for food cues when they are hungry but not when they are satiated [8, 18]. However, individuals with obesity have been found to show a strong AB to food cues when they are hungry but also when they are satiated [8, 26]. Taken together, this shows that it is important to take feeding status into account when examining AB to food cues. For the effectiveness of ABM interventions within the context of obesity, it might therefore be critical to apply the ABM training sessions specifically when participants are in a satiated state (e.g., following a regular meal).

All in all, we need to take an approach that requires individuals with obesity to self-apply ABM sessions in their home environment when being in a satiated state over a prolonged time period, to arrive at a training dose that is expected to be sufficiently high to result in a measurable effect of the ABM intervention (e.g., [15]). Before setting the critical and costly step of formally testing the efficacy of ABM when taking these taxing yet critical boundary conditions into consideration, the current study set out to first examine the acceptability and feasibility of such an ABM approach. This is especially relevant within the context of obesity, since treatment of individuals with obesity is generally more challenging because of high attrition rates [25].

In summary, the current study set out to examine the acceptability and feasibility of an ABM intervention for women with obesity that has to be carried out in a satiated state. In this study, we will apply the Bouncing Image Training Task [19, 27] which has advantages over the tasks based on the Visual Probe Paradigm (see, for example, [27]). In the current study, we tested the feasibility of this training program by examining the percentage of training sessions that were completed, completed on the intended day, and completed following the satiety instructions. Acceptability was examined with the Acceptability of Intervention Measure [36], and we examined whether the training did not adversely impact experienced craving right after the sessions. Acceptability and feasibility were also examined with the outcomes of a semi-structured interview.

Materials and methods

Participants

We recruited women aged between 18 and 35 with obesity (BMI > 30) and with an intention to diet. In total, 25 participants were included, based on recommendations for pilot studies suggesting that 10–15 participants should be enough when planning a larger study and 20–25 are recommended when aiming to examine intervention efficacy [16]. Given that differences in AB to food have been found in young adult women (e.g., [8]), and to limit potential noise due to the heterogeneity of the sample, we also focused on this group. Because of the design in which we used a liquid meal, exclusion criteria were a soy and cow milk protein allergy and a vegan diet. Participants were not included if they smoked or if they indicated that they used brain-affecting medication such as anxiety medication, because of its interference with homeostatic hunger. Individuals with a current self-identified eating disorder or other mental disorder were excluded because such diagnoses might interfere with the feasibility and acceptability. These criteria were checked with yes/no questions during the recruitment phase.

Materials

Below we describe the materials that were included as part of the main aim of the study. Additional materials that were used to describe our sample in more detail and to explore the improvement in attentional bias and craving are reported in Supplement A.

Acceptability of the intervention

To assess the acceptability of the intervention, we used the acceptability of intervention measure (AIM; [36]). The AIM is a self-report questionnaire which contains four items (e.g., “The intervention meets my approval”) that are scored on a 5-point Likert scale. The mean of the four items indicates the level of acceptability, with higher scores reflecting a higher acceptability. Internal consistency was high (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.81).

Craving

Food craving was assessed with the question “How much do you crave for food at this moment?” which could be answered on a VAS scale ranging from 0 (no craving at all) to 100 (extreme craving).

Hunger

Objective hunger was assessed with the question “How long has it been since you last ate?” from the Hunger Scale. Scores reflect the number of hours that have passed since the participant last ate, rounded off to quarters of an hour.

Stimuli

The attentional bias modification task relied on two sets of images: one set comprised images of household items (66 images showing objects such as pushpins); one set comprised images of food items (66 images showing high caloric foods such as chocolate or hamburgers). All but 12 of the images were selected from the food-pics database [4], with the remainder drawn from our own database, showing food items familiar to the Dutch population (e.g., croquette). All images were saved at a resolution of 273 × 273 pixels.

Attentional bias modification training task

For modifying AB to food, we used the Bouncing Image Training Task (BITT; [19, 27]) delivered online via Qualtrics (see Fig. 1). In this task, eight square boxes moved continuously across the screen, bouncing off the screen edges and each other. Each box contained an image, and at any point in time, all but one of the boxes displayed a food image, drawn from the stimulus set of food items. Participants were instructed to attend to the single box that contained a non-food image and use the mouse to keep the cursor in the location of this target box. This non-food image was drawn from the stimulus set of household items. Once the cursor was in the correct position, the image turned green for a 500 ms, indicating that the participant was tracking the correct image. At frequent intervals, the images in all boxes changed. Following any such change, the new image in the box previously being tracked by participants could either be another non-food image, in which case this remained the target box and the participant was required to continue attending to and tracking it. Alternatively, following such a change, the new image could be a food item, in which case the participant was required to disengage attention from it, and begin tracking the other box that now contained the single non-food item in the display.

Screenshot of the BITT

Participants could track how much of each block remained by viewing a countdown in the upper right corner of the screen. Every block employed a different random draw of images. To keep participants engaged and motivated, they earned points for keeping the mouse cursor in the location of the target box. Thus, the better they performed the task, the more points they earned. These points were indicated with a green bar across the top of the screen that progressively filled up as points accrued. At the end of each block, the total points earned was shown on the screen. Furthermore, participants were also shown their high score and were told whether their most recent score was their highest score of the day. Across the duration of the study, the BITT was delivered at increasing levels of difficulty (level 3–level 12; cf. [15]) by increasing the speed of the box movement and the frequency with which their image content or category changed (see [19] for detailed information). The aim of this was to ensure that the task remained challenging for participants. Each training session contained four blocks of the same training level of the BITT, and each block took 2.5 min to complete. Each level was used for three consecutive days, after which the level was increased.

Semi-structured interview

We used the format of a semi-structured interview to assess participants’ opinions and experiences of doing the intervention. That is, they were asked specific questions, and these were followed up on until the answers of the participants were clear. The interview consisted of five main topics: experience with the BITT training, trust in the goals and effects of the intervention, motivation, the feasibility of the intervention, and the general satisfaction with the intervention. Most questions were scored by the interviewer (e.g., Were the instructions clear?) on a 7-point scale (e.g., −3 no to 3 yes) (see Table S1 in the supplementary materials). There were additional open questions that were used for qualitative analysis: “If the BITT training would be used, what parts of the training should be kept?”, “… and what should change?” and “Imagine that the training would be added to treatment such as a lifestyle intervention, what would you think about doing the training for a longer period?”. The answers of the interview were transcribed and labeled inductively in ATLAS.ti. The labels were grouped together under a common theme.

Procedure

The procedure was partly based on a previously applied design [19] and partly on outcomes of earlier interviews with individuals with obesity, as well as therapists and dietitians who provide treatment for individuals with obesity (unpublished materials). Outcomes of these interviews were as follows: People expected that doing the training for about 3 weeks would require more of them than just a bit of interest and motivation to help out with research, people thought it would be important to do the training every day at a fixed time also to make it a kind of habit, and most people thought it was important to train a broad range of unhealthy foods since they expected that training only their preferred foods might just change their intake of preferred foods and not their overeating behavior.

This study was approved by the Ethical Committee of the Psychology Department (S-0187). Due to COVID-19 restrictions, the study was performed online, and the two assessment moments were performed online with the researcher present via Google Meets, and questionnaire data was collected via Qualtrics. An overview of the procedure can be found in Fig. 2.

Recruitment and screening

Participants were recruited by a recruitment company, Link2Trials. There was an advertisement on the website which included information specifying that we were searching for women with obesity and a weight loss goal. When participants were interested in the study, they could sign up (n = 42), and they would be asked about our exclusion criteria of soy and cow milk protein allergy (n = 1), following a vegan diet, smoking (n = 3), a current (or in the past 6 months) psychiatric diagnosis, or the use of medication that influences brain function (e.g., anxiety medication, n = 5). These criteria were checked with yes/no questions. Furthermore, they answered the question “I would like to lose weight” to check their current weight loss goal. The question could be answered on a 7-point Likert scale. Individuals scoring a 4 or higher were considered to have a weight loss goal.

When participants fulfilled the inclusion criteria, they received an email with information about the study. Here, it was specified that participants were required to abstain from alcohol 24 h and to fast for 14 h prior to the assessments. A few days after the email, they were contacted by a researcher to clarify the instructions where necessary and to schedule the two assessment moments. Not all women could be reached (n = 3), and some declined to participate at this stage (n = 3). When we reached the intended sample size of 25, the last individuals were informed that the study was full (n = 2). These two appointments were scheduled exactly 3 weeks apart at the same day of the week and time of the day. Participants received a confirmation of their appointments via email. Furthermore, four bottles of standardized liquid meal (Ensure Plus, vanilla from Abbott) were sent to the participant’s home address. They were used during the two assessment moments to keep satiety levels under as much experimental control as possible.

First assessment (baseline)

A few days before the first appointments, participants received a reminder of the restrictions via email, including a link to the Google Meet appointment. During the meeting, participants filled out the informed consent and the Hunger scale. Following this, participants were asked to drink the standardized liquid meal until they felt satiated. Afterwards, they answered several questionnaires—the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI; [7]), Insomnia Severity Index (ISI; [1]), and the Basic Psychological Need Satisfaction and Frustration scale (BPNSF; [9])—which took them approximately 20 min to complete. These questionnaires served to provide distraction while passing time between the meal and the attentional bias measures. Afterwards, subjective hunger was assessed again, and participants performed level 3 of the BITT (cf. [19]). Lastly, the researcher discussed with the participant how they would implement the training into their daily routine for the following weeks.

Training

Participants performed one 10-min training session each day for 3 weeks at home. The level of the training increased over time, starting from level 6 going up to level 12. Every day, participants received an email with a link to the training session of that day. A reminder email was sent an hour after the scheduled time (discussed at the first assessment) if the participant had not finished the training yet. The 10th email contained an additional message to keep the participants motivated: “you are halfway there, keep going.” Before each training session, participants filled out the Hunger scale. Craving was assessed right before and right after each session. Data of all training sessions was saved on Qualtrics, including whether the training was finished and including a date and time stamp.

Second assessment (post-intervention)

The second assessment was almost identical to the first assessment. A few days prior to the assessment, participants were called to remind them about the meeting and the restrictions, and they received an email with the link to the Google Meet’s session. During the meeting, they filled out the Hunger scale, drank the standardized liquid meal, and filled out questionnaires for approximately 20 min. During this session, they answered the EDE-Q, the PSRS, and the BPNSF. Following this, they reported subjective hunger again, and they performed level 3 of the BITT. An addition to this second assessment was the AIM and a semi-structured interview. The structured interview was recorded, and participants provided additional consent for this. After completing the second assessment, participants received 75 € for their participation, independent of the number of training sessions they completed.

Analyses plan

Data were processed with IBM SPSS Statistics version 28. Missing data other than dropout and not performing one or more of the training sessions was not possible because of the use of the forced choice option in Qualtrics.

Feasibility

Feasibility was checked by examining the percentage of completed trials, whether training sessions were performed on the intended days and while satiated (i.e., within 2 h of their last meal), and the input on the questions of the semi-structured interview (see below). The intervention will be considered feasible when the vast majority of participants (> 80%) are retained, the vast majority (> 80%) of the sessions are completed, and when the vast majority of the completed trials (> 80%) are completed on the intended day and while satiated.

Acceptability

Acceptability was checked by examining the AIM scores and the input on the semi-structured interview. Furthermore, if the training (i.e., being exposed to food cues) would lead to increased food craving, this could adversely affect the acceptability and efficacy of the training. Therefore, the impact of the training on experienced craving immediately after the training sessions was examined. The training is considered acceptable if it is not evaluated as negative (i.e., neutral or positive) and if there is no substantial dropout (> 80% of participants are retained).

Interview

The interview questions were processed to get an idea of the experiences of the participants and not used for statistical testing. For the questions that were scored on a 7-point scale, mean scores, standard deviations, median scores, and ranges will be reported. The outcomes of the scored interview questions can be found in Supplement B. The three qualitative questions were transcribed verbatim. Following this, two researchers used an inductive thematic analysis to identify meaningful themes in the data [6]. The researchers started with a session together to facilitate alignment, followed by splitting the interviews, each researcher doing half of them. Afterwards, they went through all interviews together and discussed any uncertainties. There were no disagreements during this process. The themes will be reported alongside some (translated) quotes from participants to provide the context.

Exploration

Furthermore, for exploratory purposes, we also assessed the change in attentional bias and food craving from pre- to post-intervention. The outcome of these assessments is not the main focus of the study and is mainly added because not finding a reduction would cast doubt on the need of a formal test of the efficacy of ABM. Finding an effect does not yet mean that there is a true effect given the small sample size and the absence of a no-intervention control group. Therefore, only a summary of these findings will be reported here, and the detailed analyses will be reported in the supplementary materials.

Training feasibility

There was no dropout, only participants who did not perform all training sessions. Thus, the retainment criteria (> 80%) were fulfilled. Participants performed on average 18.44 (SD = 3.42) out of the 20 training sessions, which corresponds with 92% (SD = 17.08; see Fig. 3). Of these sessions, 97.13% (SD = 6.05%) were performed on the planned day, and 93.5% (SD = 10.02) were performed in a satiated state (i.e., within 2 h of their last meal). Thus, the criteria of > 80% completion rates on the intended day and in a satiated state were fulfilled.

Training acceptability

Participants on average scored 3.28 (SD = 0.72) on the AIM, indicating a score between neutral and positive, meeting the progression criterium of a nonnegative evaluation. The scores ranged from 2 to 5, indicating that there were no participants indicating that the training was really unacceptable for them. Scores on the individual items of the AIM can be found in Supplement B.

Craving ratings before and after the daily training sessions are presented in Table 2. Visual inspection shows that there was no systematic increase in craving following the sessions. In line with this, a paired samples t-test of mean craving before (M = 11.51, SD = 12.03) vs. after the training (M = 11.18, SD = 11.64) provided no evidence for an increase in craving after doing the training in a satiated state (t(24) = 0.41, p = 0.689).

Interview

What should stay? The most heard comment was about the game element (e.g., “for people like me, it is important that you can compete against yourself so that you have that top score”). Many participants also mentioned that just everything of the training should stay (e.g., “I think the task is good and well thought out”). There were also several people who mentioned that doing it daily should stay since it helped them to make it a habit. Other aspects that were mentioned as being relevant aspects of the intervention to them were the breaks between the blocks, the personal contact, the questionnaires during the baseline measure, the pre- and post-assessment, and the 3-week training period.

What should change? Participants indicated that there should be more variation in the images (e.g., “also of fast-food chains, if that is allowed”). They also mentioned that the control images were not always clearly non-food items (e.g., “Some images look way too much like food. One of those images, something with pins in it, looks like a cheese soufflé with pins in it.. that one has to go..”). They indicated that variation in the type of game would be nice (e.g., “perhaps add some memory”), the duration of the daily training should be decreased, music could be added, and the background color should be changed (e.g., “the background no.. brown.. no.. that really is.. that annoyed me, it is such a negative color brown. It should be a more happy one”). Other points were that we should make it work on a phone, instructions should be more clear, they wanted to work on more than just attention in an intervention, have the option to save and resume later, do the task more often on a day, we should give more positive feedback, extend the game element, and that it would be better if they only had to finish some (e.g., 4–5) of the seven training sessions a week.

What if we would add the training to a broader intervention such as lifestyle combination treatment? Mostly, participants thought this would be fine. Others thought this would be okay given a certain precondition (e.g., “If it would get some more variation it would be easier to keep doing it. It would also help to get some more background information”). Some commented that they would be willing to do it for longer, but not a year or that it would be too monotonous.

Exploration

The detailed outcomes of the exploratory analyses can be found in Supplement D. Explorative findings showed that attentional bias as measured with both the training task (BITT) and an additional attention bias task (odd-one-out task) decreased from baseline to post-test, yet no concurrent change (reduction) in food craving was found from baseline to posttest.

Discussion

The current study examined the acceptability and feasibility of an ABM training performed in a satiated state for women with obesity with a goal to restrict their food intake. Findings can be summarized as follows: (1) The training seemed feasible with 92% of the training sessions being completed, 97% of those being completed on the intended day, and 94% being completed in a satiated state, and (2) the training seemed acceptable as it received a rating between neutral and positive, and there was no evidence for an adverse effect on craving. Interviews with participants substantiated these outcomes.

Since differences between obese individuals and individuals with a healthy weight only seem to be found consistently when individuals are in a satiated state [8, 26], it seems important to target AB for food in individuals with obesity when they are in a satiated state. As an important first step, the current study therefore examined the feasibility and acceptability of such adjusted ABM intervention in individuals with obesity. Supporting the feasibility of the Bouncing Image Training Task (BITT) as an ABM intervention in individuals with obesity when systematically applied when these individuals were in a satiated state (i.e., shortly after a meal), the findings showed that individuals with obesity were willing to do this training and comply with instructions to do the training after a meal. Furthermore, our 3-week intervention period during which training had to be done every day was feasible and acceptable. Although no other studies have examined the feasibility of ABM under such specific boundary conditions, a pilot study has tested the feasibility and acceptability of an ABM training using an anti-saccade paradigm at home. Interestingly, the completion rate in that study where participants were not instructed to perform the training while satiated was only 35% [23]. This might suggest that specific instructions about when to perform the training might even facilitate feasibility. However, it should be acknowledged that the training period in that study was also substantially longer (8 vs. 3 weeks). This may indicate that the current approach of providing the training for a more limited time period was also helpful in obtaining sufficient acceptability.

In addition to the quantitative data, qualitative data on participants’ experiences and expectations was collected. These data show that overall participants appreciated the training between neutral and slightly positive, and that the guidance during the intervention was good. Participants understood that the aim was to change their attention, and this was also somewhat believable to them. They were less aware of the aim to change their eating behavior and had also less belief in this aim. In future steps, it could be helpful to integrate this aspect more explicitly in the treatment rationale. Participants furthermore indicated that they were neutral to slightly positive about the obtained effect of the training even though they on average indicated that they did not notice an effect. At the beginning, participants were motivated to do the training, yet this did decrease slightly over the 3-week period. Thus, especially when considering to extend the training period (e.g., to strengthen its effects) in future applications, it would be important to incorporate aspects that could deal with motivation drops. For example, by providing feedback (per session) on improvement in AB to food cues online or in person. Since it is our expectation that ABM will be most beneficial when used as an add-on to other interventions, we also explored participants’ opinion about applying the BITT within the context of a broader (life style) intervention. Many indicated that it would indeed be better to add it to a broader intervention, and they thought it would be feasible to do the training in this context.

Our exploratory analyses showed that the intervention was successful in decreasing ABM to food between baseline and posttest. More specifically, it appeared that attentional engagement to food cues specifically reduced. This seems in contrast with a previous controlled ABM study in unsuccessful dieters where we found that the BITT specifically resulted in increased disengagement from food as indexed with the OOOT, with no effect on attentional engagement [19]. Yet, in that study, feeding status was not taken into account. Moreover, the change in disengagement was only evident when contrasted with the no-intervention control group. The differential effect between the training and control group seemed mainly carried by the pre- to post-test reduction in attentional disengagement within the control group rather than by an increase in disengagement in the training condition. Since the current pilot study did not include a no-intervention control group, it cannot be ruled out that the impact of the BITT on disengagement was obscured by test–retest effects.

There are no unintended effects found in the quantitative data or in the qualitative reports. Nevertheless, the current study has some important limitations to take into account. First, no control condition was included in the design. Since the main focus of the current study was on feasibility and acceptability of performing this ABM training specifically in a state of satiation, there was no need for a control condition. However, the interpretation of outcomes regarding changes in AB would benefit from a no-intervention control condition. Second, we did not examine the impact of our ABM intervention on participants’ BMI, and our study duration was also not long enough to expect measurable changes in BMI. It therefore now seems the most important question whether ABM, as an add-on or as a stand-alone intervention, is also effective in facilitating food restriction and weight loss. Third, this study exclusively focused on women with obesity. Given that most AB and ABM research is done in women only, this makes the field more homogenous and outcomes more comparable. However, this approach clearly hampers the generalization of the current findings to other groups (e.g., men with obesity). It seems therefore recommendable to also include men in future research in this domain.

Conclusion

The current study showed that it is acceptable and feasible for women with obesity who have a dieting goal to do a relatively high dose self-applied ABM training in a satiated state. Our explorative analyses, which can be found in the supplementary materials, also showed that individuals with obesity are willing to refrain from eating for 14 h for the necessary assessment procedures before and after the intervention. Future studies should examine whether the ABM training is effective in decreasing attentional bias to food cues and whether this results in decreased food intake and weight loss in individuals with obesity.
