Early adverse outcomes of neonatal hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy in a resource-constrained setting
Authors: Chigozie Kingsley Onyeje, Blessing Tochukwu Onyeje, Chimezie Udennaka, Emeka Stephen Edokwe, Sylvia Tochukwu Echendu, Somto Celestine Ngonadi, Ann Oluchi Ikeabbah, Ogechukwu Andrea Ezeike-Obi, Ugochukwu Christopher Obi, Samuel Ifeanyi Emmanuel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE) remains a leading cause of neonatal mortality and long-term neurological disability, particularly in resource-limited settings. Understanding factors associated with early adverse outcomes is crucial for optimizing management strategies.ObjectiveTo identify maternal and neonatal factors associated with early adverse outcomes in newborns with HIE in a Nigerian tertiary health institution.MethodsA prospective hospital-based cross-sectional study was conducted at Nnamdi Azikiwe University Teaching Hospital, Nnewi, from September 2023 to May 2024. Seventy term newborns with HIE admitted within 72 h of delivery were enrolled. HIE diagnosis was based on abnormal neurological examination using the Thompson score. Data on demographic characteristics, maternal factors, and biochemical parameters were collected and analyzed using SPSS version 25. Fisher’s exact test and Chi-square analysis was used to determine associations between variables, with p < 0.05 considered significant.ResultsAmong 70 newborns, 45.7% (32/70) experienced short-term adverse outcomes. The case fatality rate was 17.1% (12/70), while 28.6% (20/70) developed complications including seizures (80%), absent nutritive suckling (65%), poor Moro reflex (25%), respiratory distress (20%), and altered consciousness (15%). Place of delivery was significantly associated with adverse outcomes (p = 0.001), with outborn neonates exhibiting a higher risk compared with inborn neonates. Both HIE severity and a 5-minute Apgar score < 7 were significantly associated with adverse outcomes (p < 0.0001 for both). Maternal factors significantly associated with adverse outcomes included mode of delivery (p = 0.006) and maternal occupation (p = 0.026). Random blood glucose levels showed significant association with outcomes (p < 0.0001), while serum magnesium levels did not (p = 0.980).ConclusionHIE severity, a low 5-minute Apgar score, outborn delivery status, and abnormal blood glucose levels were significantly associated with early adverse outcomes in neonatal HIE. These findings emphasize the importance of skilled birth attendance, early recognition, and prompt glucose monitoring in improving outcomes for affected newborns in resource-limited settings.Keywords: Hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy, Neonatal outcomes, Predictors, Resource-limited setting, Nigeria

Introduction

Perinatal asphyxia remains a major cause of neonatal death globally, disproportionately affecting developing regions like Sub-Saharan Africa where survivors often face long-term health challenges [1], including cerebral palsy, seizures, mental disorders, vision and hearing impairment, and cognitive and learning disabilities [2, 3].

Hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE) is a syndrome of neurologic dysfunction which complicates perinatal asphyxia; [4] it is the commonest cause of neonatal encephalopathy [5]. Survival rates in babies with HIE depend not just on severity, but also on factors like place of birth, quality of prenatal care, underlying cause, gestational age, maternal health and age, economic status, and access to timely specialized care [5, 6]. Factors predisposing to HIE can be antenatal [7, 8], , perinatal [9, 10], or a combination of both [11]. During hypoxic–ischaemic injury, excessive glutamate release over-stimulates neuronal glutamate receptors, triggering excitotoxic enzyme cascades that damage cell membranes and intracellular structures, leading to the clinical manifestations of HIE [12]. Using the modified Sarnat and Sarnat staging system, HIE is categorized into three levels of increasing severity [13, 14].

Management outcomes in neonatal HIE depends on several factors including; need for advanced resuscitation and abnormal neurological examination; [15] presence of seizures- clinical or electrophysiological, low APGAR scores and poor electroencephalogram background activity; [16] severity of HIE and the clinical status of the neonate at birth [17]. A hospital-based retrospective study done in Nigeria found that place of delivery, booking status of mother, gestational age at birth, age of infant at presentation and severity of HIE significantly predict in-hospital mortality in babies with HIE [5].

Approximately 20–30% of infants with HIE die in the neonatal period, with about 33–50% of survivors left with permanent neuro-developmental abnormalities [13]. HIE accounts for 6% to 9% of all neonatal deaths and 21% to 23% of deaths in term infants. While HIE affects 1.5–2.5 per 1000 births in developed countries [18], rates increase dramatically to over 26 per 1000 births in resource-limited settings [19], with one hospital-based study in Sagamu, Ogun State Nigeria reporting an even higher prevalence of 35.9% [4]. It is still a scientific puzzle why some asphyxiated newborns develop HIE while others do not. However, some researchers have suggested variation in newborns’ compensatory thresholds and intrinsic resistance of the brain to severe asphyxia; [20] complex relationship between the neonatal brain and the immune system during the perinatal period; [21] developmental maturity of the brain [22] and possibly intrinsic genetic factors of the neonates [23], as plausible explanations.

Identifying neonates with HIE at higher risk of severe neurological impairment or death is a major focus of research, as this will set the tune for determining management modalities including neuro-protective therapies, prognostication and anticipatory counseling of caregivers [24]. We studied the interplay between some identified risk factors and clinical findings at presentation in influencing early adverse outcomes in newborns with HIE in a tertiary health institution in Nigeria. Understanding these factors will not only guide clinicians in predicting management outcomes but also in deciding which interventions are most urgent for affected newborns.

The objectives of the study therefore include: to determine case fatality rate and prevalence of major short-term adverse outcomes among newborns with HIE; evaluate the association of neonatal characteristics (age, gender, place of delivery, sentinel events in labour, 5th minute Apgar score, HIE severity), maternal factors (age, parity, mother’s educational level and occupation and mode of delivery), and serum biochemical parameters (including magnesium and random blood glucose levels) with short-term adverse outcomes among neonates with HIE.

Materials and methods

Study area

This was a hospital-based prospective study conducted at the Special Care Baby unit (SCBU) of the Nnamdi Azikiwe University Teaching Hospital (NAUTH), Nnewi, Anambra State. The facility offers specialized healthcare while serving as a referral center for primary and secondary health facilities in the state and neighboring regions. The SCBU Protocol for management of HIE involves respiratory support with free flow intranasal oxygen or continuous positive airway pressure; anti-seizure drugs; prophylactic antibiotics; dextrose-containing intravenous fluid for caloric support and nasogastric tube feeding when appropriate.

Eligible newborns with HIE received comprehensive evaluation including medical history and clinical examination, then were classified using the Thompson score (mild HIE: 1–10; moderate HIE: 11–14; severe HIE: ≥15).

Exclusion criteria were: all newborns with evidence of septicemia (e.g. temperature instability, abdominal swelling, abnormal bleeding, petechiae rash), intrauterine infection, congenital anomalies, necrotizing enterocolitis; neonates with clinical features of anaemia (including pallor, lethargy, and signs of cardiac failure) and laboratory confirmation by capillary packed cell volume (PCV), as anemia may exacerbate cerebral hypoxia and potentially influence the severity and therefore grading of hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE); newborns of diabetic mothers and those whose received opioids, other depressant medications or general anesthesia.

After obtaining written informed consent from each participating mother in her preferred language and voluntariness assured, eligible newborns were enrolled consecutively into the study and monitored daily until discharge or death. Each diagnosis of HIE was confirmed by two different neonatologists. This sampling technique aimed at reducing selection bias and capturing as many eligible neonates as possible within the survey period.

Research variable

The following information was obtained on admission or at subsequent reviews using a structured researcher administered questionnaire. These include the outcome and predictor variables. Outcome variable – this was defined as the neonatal outcome at the 7th day of admission (or death) among newborns admitted with HIE in the Special Care Baby Unit. Outcomes were categorized into two groups: [1] absence of adverse outcomes; and [2] presence of adverse outcomes, defined as the occurrence of respiratory distress, absent nutritive suckling reflex, altered level of consciousness, seizures, poor Moro reflex, or death. These outcomes are categorized as one since they can be explained by the neurologic manifestations of hypoxic ischemic encephalopathy.

Independent variables – these are variables that are related to newborn management outcomes. They include birth and demographic variables, clinical parameters, laboratory parameters, and HIE scores.

Laboratory parameters include random blood glucose determined using capillary blood samples collected by aseptic heel prick technique and analyzed promptly using a calibrated point-of-care glucometer (Fine test auto-coding premium, OSANG Healthcare Co., Ltd., Korea) to ensure accuracy and minimize pre-analytical variability, normal range 73-109 mg/dl. Serum magnesium- estimated using the xylidyl blue spectrophotometric method, normal range 1.50-2.30 mg/dl.

Affected newborn were followed up by a daily clinical examination (mainly with the Thompson score parameters recorded at presentation, 24 h and 48 h later) and short-term outcomes recorded on day 7 of admission.

Data analysis

The collected data were initially entered into Microsoft Excel, cleaned and subsequently exported and analyzed using statistical package for social science (SPSS) version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA). Descriptive statistics of frequency count, percentages, mean and standard deviation was used to summarize demographic characteristics. Normality was tested for continuous variables (age at presentation, birth weight and maternal age) using graphical methods and the Shapiro–Wilk test. The Fisher’s exact test and Chi-square (χ2) analysis were used to determine association between categorical variables. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 and 95% confidence intervals were reported where applicable.

Ethical consideration

Approval for this study was obtained from the NAUTH Ethics Committee with reference number NAUTH/CS/66/VOL.15/VER.3/100/2022/057. Informed consent was obtained from every mother-newborn dyad following adequate explanation of the research objectives, potential benefits of participation, and assurance that the study posed minimal risk to their newborns. Participation in the study was entirely voluntary and no form of financial inducement was involved. Voluntary withdrawal at any stage of interaction was guaranteed for all subjects without any adverse consequence for the newborns. All information was handled with strict confidentiality.

Results

Among the 603 neonates admitted into the SCBU during the study period, 200 were diagnosed with perinatal asphyxia; however, 100 of them were excluded based on the eligibility criteria (Fig. 1). A further 30 babies were excluded as they did not meet the criteria for diagnosis of HIE or had missing data; thus, a final analysis included 70 newborns. Twenty out of 70 (28.6%) had at least one short-term adverse outcome; while 17.1% (n = 12) died within seven days of admission. The proportion of major short-term adverse outcomes include: seizure- 16/20 (80%), absent nutritive suckling – 13/20 (65%), poor Moro reflex- 5/20 (25%), respiratory distress- 4/20 (20%) and altered consciousness- 3/20 (15%).

Discussion

This study revealed an alarming 17.7% case fatality rate among patients diagnosed with hypoxic ischaemic encephalopathy, highlighting the critical nature of this condition. Comparable high rates have been documented across other developing countries with Ekwochi et al. documenting 18% in Enugu Nigeria [1], and Hafsa et al., reporting 16.4% in Rawalpindi Pakistan [26]. These consistently high fatality rates likely stem from limited access to therapeutic hypothermia; an evidence-based treatment proven effective for moderate to severe HIE [27]. This modality of treatment has been shown to significantly reduce mortality and major disability associated with HIE [28, 29]. However, our findings contrast with several other studies that reported notably lower case fatality rates ranging from 8.5% to 14% [24, 25]. The lower case fatality in the aforementioned studies could be due to use of therapeutic hypothermia as a standard treatment for babies with HIE; also two of the studies had a larger sample size. Furthermore, among survivors of HIE in our study, the most common short-term adverse outcomes included seizures (80%), absence of nutritive suckling (65%), poor Moro reflex (25%), respiratory distress requiring support (20%), and altered consciousness (15%). This finding compares differently to the observation of Teixeira et al., who reported that at discharge of their HIE survivors, 14% were on anti-seizure drugs; 23.3% had no feeding skills; 23.3% had abnormal neurological examination; while 2.1% required oxygen support [30]. Additionally, Grass et al., reported a median seizure prevalence of 32.3% in a large multicentre study [25]. The lower prevalence of short-term adverse outcome noted in the cited studies could be due to use of induced hypothermia as a treatment modality for HIE and probably the availability of better skilled manpower.

Our study also revealed that, adverse outcomes in HIE cases were significantly associated with outborn delivery, severity of encephalopathy, and low fifth-minute Apgar scores. These findings likely reflect inadequate infrastructure for therapeutic hypothermia and high quality neonatal intensive care, poorly planned delivery, inadequate transport facilities in rural areas, a weak referral system and limited neonatal resuscitation expertise; factors that contribute to delayed presentation and increased vulnerability among outborn neonates. These limitations contribute to delays across the care pathway including late recognition of high-risk pregnancies, suboptimal intrapartum monitoring and decision-making, and delayed identification and management of birth asphyxia, thereby increasing the risk and severity of hypoxic injury prior to referral. This result agrees with the findings of several other researchers who noted that adverse outcome in HIE is significantly associated with outborn delivery status, HIE severity, Apgar scores and mode of delivery [1, 24]. The relationship between gender and HIE outcomes remains controversial in literature. One study demonstrated significantly worse outcomes in males; [26] while another reported higher mortality in females [1]. In our study, although males showed a trend toward poorer outcomes, this difference was not statistically significant. The discrepancy in previous findings may be attributed to demographic variations, particularly a higher male-to-female ratio (2:1) and larger sample size in one study; [26] while the increased female mortality in the other study remained unexplained [1].

Furthermore, our study indicated that significant maternal variables associated with early adverse outcomes in newborns with HIE were normal vaginal delivery and maternal occupation. These findings may reflect underlying disparities in access to skilled obstetric care and socioeconomic conditions. A higher proportion of vaginal deliveries occurring outside formal health facilities where skilled birth attendance and supervision may be limited could be associated with poorer neonatal outcomes. Similarly, maternal unemployment and low socioeconomic status may be linked to suboptimal health-seeking behaviours and reduced access to quality care. However, given the cross-sectional design of this study, these relationships should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects. Our findings aligned with previous research showing higher death rates in asphyxiated infants delivered vaginally [1, 31]. Other studies have also demonstrated that low maternal socioeconomic status is significantly associated with adverse outcomes among these neonates [31]. However, Suppiej et al. [24], and Devi et al. [32], reported different results, noting that emergency cesarean deliveries increased the risk of severe outcomes in HIE babies. This contradiction may be explained by the underlying maternal or fetal complications that necessitated the choice of emergency cesarean procedure in the first place and delayed presentation to the hospital.

In addition, our study demonstrated a significant association between abnormal blood glucose levels and poor outcomes in neonates with HIE. These findings align with established pathophysiological mechanisms: hypoglycemia compromises recovery by depriving vulnerable neurons of essential energy, elevating seizure risk, and disrupting cerebral perfusion; while hyperglycemia induces damage through oxidative stress during reperfusion, exacerbates cerebral edema, increases intracranial pressure, and amplifies inflammatory responses. The association between glycemic dysregulation and adverse outcomes in HIE is consistently supported by previous studies, underscoring the critical importance of maintaining glucose homeostasis in the management of these vulnerable neonates [1, 33]. Furthermore, neonates with normal serum magnesium levels had 1.26 times higher odds of developing adverse outcomes compared with those with elevated serum magnesium levels, however, this association was not statistically significant (OR = 1.26, 95% CI: 0.24–6.65, p = 0.783). This finding should be interpreted cautiously, as the lack of statistical significance, a wide confidence interval and the small sample size preclude any inference of a protective effect. Although some studies have reported associations between higher magnesium levels and improved neurological outcomes such as reduced seizure burden and better motor profiles in neonates with HIE; [34] while hypomagnesemia reduced survival rate of the affected neonates; [35] these data are not consistent and are largely derived from observational studies. The apparent trend in our cohort may therefore reflect residual confounding, small sample size, or measurement variability rather than a true biological effect. Therefore, adequately powered prospective studies are required to clarify the role of magnesium in modifying outcomes among neonates with HIE.

Recommendations

Strengthening community-based interventions through training traditional birth attendants, public education, and leveraging mobile health support can improve early recognition, timely referral, and neonatal outcomes. Improving health system capacity through standardized referral pathways, specialized neonatal transport, and point-of-care glucose monitoring can enhance outcomes for infants with perinatal asphyxia. Establishing standardized clinical protocols and strengthening provider training can improve the quality and consistency of neonatal HIE management.

Limitations

This study was conducted in a single facility, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other settings. In addition, the small sample size reduces statistical power, and the potential for selection and measurement bias cannot be fully excluded. Due to resource constraints, we were unable to perform arterial blood gas analysis on cord blood samples- a key method for biochemically confirming asphyxia. Our diagnosis of HIE relied on the Thompson scoring system, which contains subjective elements that may have introduced bias in patient classification. Additionally, as our tertiary care center typically receives complex referrals, our patient population likely included a disproportionate number of severe cases, possibly inflating both the prevalence of severe HIE and the case fatality rate observed in our study. More so, the observational nature of the study limits the ability to establish causal relationships between the identified factors and adverse neonatal outcomes. Lastly, despite the use of binary logistic regression to control for confounding, some residual confounding from factors such as HIE severity, birth weight, and quality of pre-referral resuscitation may still have influenced the observed associations.

Conclusion

Our research demonstrated that birth outside the hospital setting, HIE severity, low Apgar scores at five minutes, blood glucose abnormalities, and specific maternal characteristics were significantly associated with early adverse outcomes in neonatal HIE; all contributing to the high case fatality rate observed in our study.
