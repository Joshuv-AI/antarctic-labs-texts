Comment on “Thrombolytic therapy versus cardiac surgery for left-sided prosthetic heart valve thrombosis: A systematic review and meta-analysis”
Authors: Rachna Rohtagi, Parag Jayant Ratnakar, Deepti M. Sati, Hariharan Srinivasan
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Bagheri et al. synthesized 114 studies comparing thrombolytic therapy with surgery for left-sided prosthetic valve thrombosis and reported similar success but lower in-hospital mortality with thrombolysis [1]. The breadth of the review is valuable; however, its comparative conclusion is not supported by the estimand used. The analysis pools single-arm proportions separately by treatment and then compares subgroup summaries. This approach does not preserve within-study treatment contrasts, permits differences in case mix and treatment era to drive apparent effects, and separates randomized trial arms from the randomization that made them comparable [2]. The reported imbalance in New York Heart Association class IV disease (38% with surgery versus 25% with thrombolysis) illustrates the likely confounding by indication. The mortality difference is therefore a contrast between pooled populations, not an estimated treatment effect.

Comparability is further weakened by the asymmetric definition of treatment success: survival for surgery versus complete or partial improvement in valve function and hemodynamics without subsequent surgery after thrombolysis [1]. These outcomes are neither clinically equivalent nor consistently defined across studies, so the non-significant subgroup comparison cannot establish equivalent efficacy. By contrast, the SAFE-PVT randomized trial applied a common complete clinical response endpoint and demonstrated a more nuanced trade-off: similar response at discharge, more major complications with surgery, and more residual valve dysfunction after fibrinolysis [3].

The slow or very-slow infusion subgroup is also descriptive and lacks a direct head-to-head comparison. Its high success and low mortality cannot, by themselves, establish a dose-duration optimization effect or a treatment hierarchy. Contemporary cohorts such as HATTUSHA support the feasibility of low-dose slow or ultraslow thrombolysis, but treatment selection remains relevant [4]. A more informative synthesis would separate direct comparative studies from single-arm series, preserve within-study contrasts, harmonize outcome definitions, and use adjusted or bias-aware models; single-arm proportions could then be presented as contextual estimates. Until such analyses are available, the review supports thrombolysis as a viable option, but not a causal claim of lower mortality or a first-line treatment hierarchy.
