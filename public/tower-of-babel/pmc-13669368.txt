Structural and mechanistic insights into OCTN1-mediated transport and cancer cell proliferation
Authors: Binghong Xu, Shengjian Liang, Ziyu Wang, Xiaoyu Zhou, Liuying Wang, Qinqin Liang, Xiaoke Liu, Wei Guo, Sai Shi, Cheng Zhu, Sheng Ye, Zhiyong Lou, Yaxin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Novel Organic Cation Transporter 1 (OCTN1) facilitates the uptake of endogenous metabolites and therapeutic drugs across diverse tissues. Recognized as a key transporter for the antioxidant ergothioneine (ET), OCTN1 also mediates the cellular entry and release of various cationic compounds. Dysregulation of OCTN1 has been implicated in cancer progression and drug interaction. In non-neuronal systems, OCTN1-mediated acetylcholine (ACh) transport has been implicated in promoting cancer progression by enhancing tumor cell proliferation and invasion. Despite its physiological and clinical relevance, the structural mechanisms underlying substrate recognition and transport by OCTN1 have remained poorly understood. Here, we present cryo-EM structures of human OCTN1 in both apo and ET-bound states, revealing the binding site of ET and elucidating conformational changes essential for sodium-dependent substrate transport. Furthermore, structure-based virtual screening identified mebendazole (MBZ) as a potent inhibitor targeting OCTN1, effectively suppressing the proliferation of cervical and lung cancer cells. Our findings provide a structural framework for understanding OCTN1 function and inform strategies to target this transporter in cancer.Subject terms: Permeation and transport, Target identification, Cryoelectron microscopy

Introduction

OCTN1, officially designated as solute carrier family 22 member 4 (SLC22A4), belongs to a well-characterized subfamily within the broader human SLC22 transporter superfamily1. Research interest in the intestinal and renal expression of OCTN1 is largely driven by its significant impact on both pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics2. Additionally, OCTN1 is expressed in the context of the nervous system as well3. OCTN1 primarily transports a variety of organic cations and zwitterions. Studies have revealed its involvement in the uptake of diverse substrates such as tetraethylammonium (TEA)4, acetylcholine (ACh)5, choline6, and ergothioneine (ET)7. Among these, the mushroom metabolite ET was identified as a substrate of OCTN1, and it is a naturally occurring food-derived antioxidant8,9. ET was found to be absent in the cells and tissues of OCTN1 knockout animals10, and its effect on the phenotype disappeared11,12. Moreover, OCTN1 is critically involved in drug interaction and delivery13,14. It facilitates the transport of several anti-cancer agents, such as various cytidine analogs15, camptothecin16, mitoxantrone and doxorubicin17.

OCTN1 expression has been observed in various cancer cells, correlating with its involvement in cancer metabolism and disease progression18,19. ACh, a key substrate of OCTN1, not only functions as a neurotransmitter but also acts as a tumor-promoting factor20,21. It contributes to cancer progression by regulating cell proliferation, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and invasion22–25. The function of ACh in human cancers is related to the non-neuronal cholinergic system (NNCS)22,25,26. Unlike in neurons, the plasma membrane transporter OCTN1 plays a crucial role in the NNCS by mediating a slow release of ACh, as demonstrated in lung cancer cell A54927 and cervical cancer cell HeLa2,6. The ability of OCTN1 to transport ACh has also been characterized in the in vitro system of proteoliposomes5,28 and in primary mesothelial cells29. Consequently, OCTN1 represents a promising therapeutic target in aggressive tumors, as its role as the primary mediator of ACh release in cancer cells like A549 and HeLa enables the strategic use of inhibitors to block ACh’s tumor-promoting effects.

With > 30 members, the SLC22 family constitutes one of the largest groups of polyspecific membrane transporters30. This family encompasses a diverse range of substrate specificities, including organic anion transporters (OATs), organic cation transporters (OCTs), and transporters capable of handling both cations and zwitterions (OCTNs)30. Additionally, four members remain functionally uncharacterized and are currently classified as orphans31. The recent determination of three-dimensional structures for several SLC22 transporters, including OCT1-332–35 and the organic anion transporters OAT136, URAT137–39, has markedly advanced our understanding of their substrate specificity and transport mechanisms. Very recently, the cryo-EM structures of human OCTN2, another key member of the OCTN subfamily, were reported in three distinct conformational states, revealing the molecular basis of sodium-dependent carnitine recognition and transport40. Despite this breakthrough, high-resolution structural information for OCTN1 is still lacking, leaving the mechanistic details of its ligand recognition and conformational cycling unresolved.

In this work, we investigate the structures, substrate-binding mechanism and conformational transitions of OCTN1 by cryo-EM and functional experiments. We determine the structures of OCTN1 in both apo and ET-bound states, capturing outward-open and inward-open conformations, respectively. Furthermore, structure-based screening identifies mebendazole (MBZ) as a small-molecule inhibitor that targets OCTN1 to suppress tumor cell proliferation, with an affinity in the nanomolar range. We further validate the anti-tumor efficacy of MBZ in both cellular and mouse models.

Results

Structure determination of human OCTN1

We first set out to determine the structure of human OCTN1 using cryo-EM and single particle analysis. We used full-length OCTN1 over-expressed in HEK293F cells and purified the protein solubilized in detergent lauryl maltose neopentyl glycol (LMNG) using a twin-strep affinity tag and size-exclusion chromatography for the cryo-EM study. We obtained a 3D reconstruction of OCTN1 (apo-state) at 3.11 Å resolution and completed the model (Fig. 1A, B, Supplementary Fig. 1A–E, 3A, Supplementary Table 1). The structure of OCTN1 reveals a classical Major Facilitator Superfamily (MFS) fold, featuring twelve transmembrane helices (TM1-TM12) arranged in an outward-facing conformation (Fig. 1B, C). Additionally, a partially resolved density adjacent to the outward-open pathway was assigned to the extended extracellular loop 1, or ectodomain (ECD) (Fig. 1A-C). The ICHD comprises three intracellular helices that stem from two segments of the protein sequence: the N-terminus (H1) and the linker between TM6 and TM7 (H8-H9) (Fig. 1B, C). The translocation pathway is situated at the interface between two pseudo-symmetrical domains—TM1-6 and TM7-12. A central substrate-binding site is located midway through the membrane, sandwiched by these two domains (Fig. 1D–F).

Ergothioneine (ET) is the key substrate of OCTN17. We incubated purified OCTN1 with saturating concentrations of ET (2 mM) and subsequently determined the structure of OCTN1 in ET-bound state at 3.37 Å resolution (Fig. 1G–J, Supplementary Fig. 2A–E, 3B, Supplementary Table 1). In the structure of ET-bound OCTN1, the TMD is in the inward-facing (IF) conformation, which features a large solvent-accessible cavity in the middle of the TMD from the cytoplasmic side (Fig. 1G, H, K). ET is a thio-histidine betaine that exists as a zwitterion at physiological pH. Its distinctive structure comprises a thioimidazole ring, a quaternary ammonium group and a carboxylic acid moiety (Fig. 1I, J).

Na⁺-dependent ET transport and substrate recognition of human OCTN1

We next performed structural comparison of the inward-facing ET-bound human OCTN1 (PDB: 9WTX) with the inward-facing ipratropium-bound human OCTN2 structure (PDB: 9PFB)40. Structural superposition of the N-terminal domain of the two transporters yielded an RMSD of 0.96 Å over 300 Cα atoms, revealing a highly conserved overall architecture between OCTN1 and OCTN2 (Fig. 2A).

Given that OCTN2 mediates Na⁺-dependent high-affinity carnitine transport via an allosteric Na⁺-binding cavity spatially separated from the substrate pocket, we carefully examined the equivalent region in our OCTN1 structures. However, no distinct Na⁺ density was observed in the corresponding cavity of apo and ET-bound OCTN1 cryo-EM maps. Nevertheless, sequence alignment showed that the key residues lining the Na⁺-coordination site in OCTN2, including S28 (TM1), N32 (TM1), Q180 (TM3) and N210 (TM4), are highly conserved in human OCTN1 (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Fig. 4). To functionally validate the potential role of these conserved residues, we performed alanine substitution mutagenesis and substrate transport assays. Mutations N32A, Q180A and N210A markedly impaired ET transport, reducing the uptake activity to approximately 25% of the wild-type level. In contrast, the S28A mutant only moderately decreased ET transport to ~75% of wild-type activity, consistent with the structural observation that S28 coordinates Na⁺ mainly through its backbone carbonyl group, whose function is less perturbed upon alanine replacement (Fig. 2A, D, E).

We next assessed the dependence of OCTN1-mediated ET transport on extracellular Na⁺ concentration. ET transport activity gradually increased with rising Na⁺ concentration from 10 to 200 mM, whereas a further elevation to 400 mM suppressed ET uptake, indicating that excessively high Na⁺ concentration disrupts optimal transport function (Fig. 2F). Additionally, transport activity remained largely unchanged across a pH range of 5.0 to 9.0, ruling out H⁺-coupled cotransport (Supplementary Fig. 5A). These results suggest that OCTN1 adopts a Na⁺-coupled transport mechanism analogous to OCTN2, even in the absence of resolvable Na⁺ density in our cryo-EM structures. Supporting this notion, AlphaFold 3 (AF3)41 structure prediction further confirmed that Na⁺ can stably occupy the conserved cation-binding cavity in OCTN1 (Supplementary Fig. 5B).

Furthermore, we characterized the substrate-binding pocket of OCTN1 and compared it with that of OCTN2. Both the substrate-binding pocket architecture and key substrate-interacting residues are well conserved between OCTN1 and OCTN2 (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Fig. 4). The ET-binding site in OCTN1 is defined by a constellation of residues that coordinate the substrate through diverse chemical forces (Fig. 2A, C). A triad of aromatic residues (Y211, F239, F441) encapsulates ET, with Y211 (TM4) and F239 (TM5) stabilizing its cationic quaternary ammonium group, and F441 (TM10) packing against the thioimidazole ring. Additional hydrophobic contributions are provided by Q207 (TM4), I208 (TM4), and C236 (TM5). One polar interaction enhances binding specificity and affinity: R469 (TM11) donates a hydrogen bond to the carboxylate oxygen of ET. Q207 (TM4) also contributes some polar interactions, albeit at a somewhat longer distance. Functional alanine mutagenesis confirmed the essential role of these residues, as all mutations impaired transport (Fig. 2E). The R469A substitution was most detrimental, abrogating over 85% of uptake activity, demonstrating the pivotal role of this charge-stabilizing hydrogen bond.

Comparative structural analysis revealed a conserved allosteric coupling circuitry between the Na⁺-binding cavity and substrate-binding pocket within the OCTN subfamily. In OCTN2, a characteristic hydrogen-bonding network composed of conserved polar residues mediates the crosstalk between sodium coordination and carnitine recognition40. A comparable coupling network is also present in OCTN1, though it is subtly remodeled by a slight side-chain deflection of N210 relative to its counterpart in OCTN2. In OCTN1, N32 forms direct hydrogen bonds with N210, and N210 further establishes hydrogen-bonding contacts with the side chain of S28. Meanwhile, N32 also engages in hydrogen bonding with Q207, a key residue residing in the ET substrate-binding pocket (Fig. 2B). This interconnected hydrogen-bonding axis physically links the Na⁺-binding site and the substrate-binding cavity, enabling allosteric communication and supporting the Na⁺-coupled transport mode of OCTN1.

Distinct pocket electrostatic features underlie the zwitterionic substrate recognition of OCTN1

To further elucidate the substrate transport mechanism of OCTN1, we performed a comparative structural analysis with OCT1. Superposition of the outward-open OCTN1-apo (PDB: 9WTY) and apo-OCT1CS (PDB: 8ET6)33 structures based on the NTD (314 Cα atoms, RMSD = 10.22 Å) revealed a moderately expanded substrate-binding cavity in OCTN1 (Fig. 3A–C). This subtle enlargement is predominantly attributed to an outward displacement of TM7, TM9 and TM10 at the extracellular side, while structural rearrangement on the intracellular side remains minimal (Fig. 3B, C). In the inward-facing state, local structural divergence is similarly confined to the cytoplasmic side, where TM7, TM10 and TM11 adopt a slight outward-splayed conformation relative to OCT1 (Supplementary Fig. 6A–C). Such subtle structural remodeling of the transmembrane helices reshapes the microenvironment of the substrate-binding pocket, potentially underlying the divergent substrate preference between OCTN1 and OCT1.

We further compared the substrate-binding pockets of OCTN1 and OCT1 (Fig. 3D, E, Supplementary Fig. 4 and 6D, E). Several key residues in the NTD differ between the two transporters: N32, Q207 and Y211 in OCTN1 correspond to F32, S213 and W217 in OCT1, all of which are involved in substrate transport. In contrast, F239 in OCTN1 is conserved as F244 in OCT1. In the CTD, F441 (OCTN1) corresponds to I446 (OCT1), a residue also critical for transport33. Additionally, Y361 and W354 in OCT1, which are known to participate in cation-π interactions and gating dynamics respectively, are conserved in OCTN1 as Y356 and W34933–35. Mutagenesis studies further confirmed that Y356 and W349 also influence ET transport in OCTN1 (Fig. 3F).

The E381 residue, corresponding to E386, E387, E390 and E383 in OCT1, OCT2, OCT3 and OCTN2 respectively (Supplementary Fig. 4), has been documented as a key residue governing substrate translocation32–35. The E381A mutation was also experimentally confirmed to impair ET transport by OCTN1 (Fig. 3F). A key structural distinction between OCTN1 and OCT1 lies at position 469, which is an arginine (R469) in OCTN1 but an aspartate (D474) in OCT1 (Fig. 3D, E, Supplementary Fig. 4 and 6D, E). In OCT1, D474 forms a salt bridge with K214 (equivalent to I208 in OCTN1), and together with E386, creates a negatively charged binding pocket optimized for cationic substrates33–35. In contrast, the binding pocket of OCTN1 features a complementary charge distribution: E381 provides a negative charge, while R469 contributes a positive charge (Fig. 3G). This configuration enables OCTN1 to accommodate not only cations but also zwitterionic substrates like ET. Consistently, this electrostatic structural feature is conserved in OCTN2, whose substrate-binding pocket also contains the equivalent charged residues E383 and R471. And R471 has been proven to be essential for carnitine substrate recognition, indicating a conserved mechanism for zwitterionic substrate coordination within the OCTN subfamily40. Notably, R469 is evolutionarily conserved within the organic anion transporters (OATs, also members of SLC22 family of transporters), corresponding to R466 in hOAT1, R466 in rOAT1, and R477 in hURAT1, where it plays a critical role in anion recognition36–39 (Supplementary Fig. 4). The electrostatic landscape of the OCTN1 translocation pathway, characterized by a zwitterionic nature (Figs. 1F, 3G, Supplementary Fig. 7A, E), diverges significantly from the negatively charged pores of OCT1 (Supplementary Fig. 7B, F) and the positively charged pores of OATs (Supplementary Fig. 7C, D, G, H).

Conformational changes during ET transport

To better characterize the conformational transition pathway, we structurally aligned OCTN1-apo (outward-open, PDB: 9WTY) and OCTN1-ET (inward-open, PDB: 9WTX) onto the NTD of the occluded OCTN2-carnitine structure (PDB: 9PDQ)40, yielding RMSD values of 1.10 Å and 1.26 Å over 300 Cα atoms, respectively. Structural alignment using the larger NTD as the reference reveals that the CTD undergoes a rigid-body rearrangement relative to the NTD. The extracellular and intracellular segments of CTD transmembrane helices undergo substantial structural rearrangement. This realignment results in the extracellular end of TM7 and TM10 tilting inward and their intracellular ends shifting outward, effectively sealing the central cavity at the extracellular side while opening the cavity toward the cytoplasm, thereby enabling the canonical transition from outward-open to inward-open conformation. The structural rearrangements of TM7 and TM10 are tightly coupled with the adjacent TM9, TM11 and TM12, which form extensive interhelical interactions within the transmembrane domain (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Fig. 8A–C). Notably, the occluded OCTN2-carnitine structure represents a typical intermediate state in the transport cycle. Its transmembrane helices at the cytoplasmic side closely mimic the conformation of outward-open OCTN1-apo, whereas the extracellular region superimposes well with inward-open OCTN1-ET. This distinct arrangement allows the transmembrane helices from both extracellular and cytoplasmic sides to converge toward the central substrate cavity, ultimately adopting a fully closed occluded conformation (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Fig. 8A–C).

Integrating structural insights from OCTN2 and our OCTN1 structures, we propose that OCTN1 follows a canonical MFS-type rocker-switch transport cycle, in which Na⁺ binding within the N-terminal bundle allosterically primes the transporter for substrate recognition. Subsequent ET recruitment is stabilized by key polar and aromatic residues, particularly R469, which is indispensable for coordinating the zwitterionic substrate. Similar with other SLC22 family OCT, OAT and OCTN2 transporters, electrostatic interactions and conserved aromatic gating residues surround the substrate pocket and govern the opening and closure of intracellular and extracellular gates throughout the transport cycle.

In contrast, TM8 displayed only negligible positional rearrangement among all three conformational states (Fig. 4A). Comparative analysis of the NTD and CTD confirmed that the alternating-access transport of OCTN1 is dominated by rigid-body domain rotation, fully consistent with the canonical MFS rocker-switch mechanism (Supplementary Fig. 8D, E). Nevertheless, TM8 does not fully follow this rigid-body movement mode, undergoing a ~ 40° helical tilt during the outward-to-inward conformational transition (Supplementary Fig. 8E, F). The conserved acidic residue E381 located within the substrate-binding pocket is positioned at this structural divergence site (Supplementary Fig. 8F). The molecular mechanism underlying this torsional motion of TM8 remains to be further clarified.

Conformational changes in TM7 and TM10 induce a major rearrangement of the aromatic residues (Y356, F357, F441, Y445) lining the OCTN1 transport pathway (Fig. 4B). Mutagenesis studies confirmed that F357 and Y445 influence ET transport in OCTN1 as well (Supplementary Fig. 8G). These residues shift from an outward-extended configuration to an inward-facing orientation, suggesting that aromatic residues play critical roles in substrate transport by OCTN1. Moreover, during the transition, W349 moves outwardly, contributing to the opening of the intracellular channel (Fig. 4B). In the occluded OCTN2-carnitine structure, residues located on the extracellular side of the substrate pathway, including Y356, F357 and F441, adopt a conformation highly similar with that of inward-open OCTN1-ET. By contrast, the intracellular-side residues W349 and Y445 in the occluded state closely match the configuration observed in outward-open OCTN1-apo (Fig. 4B). These residue conformational preferences are fully consistent with the overall intermediate structural feature of the occluded state. Despite its conserved role in substrate recognition within the SLC22 family, E381 exhibits negligible conformational change in OCTN1. Instead, substantial rearrangements occur at R469, which swings from a horizontal extension in the binding pocket to an inward-facing conformation, and at Q207 on TM4, which reorients from an outward to a horizontal pose, suggesting their active role in the transition mechanism (Fig. 4B).

Integrating our structural and functional data, we propose a refined Na⁺-coupled transport model for zwitterionic substrate translocation by OCTN1 (Fig. 4C). The transport cycle starts with an apo occluded state of OCTN1, which transitions to an outward-open conformation to allow sequential binding of Na⁺ and ET substrate. Following substrate loading, the transporter progresses to a substrate-bound occluded intermediate, accompanied by coordinated local rearrangements of key pocket residues. Subsequent conversion to the inward-open state opens the cytoplasmic pathway, enabling sequential release of ET and Na⁺. Mechanistically, Q207 serves as a critical allosteric residue that physically couples the Na⁺-binding cavity and substrate-binding pocket. The core polar residue R469 is indispensable for recognizing and stabilizing zwitterionic substrates. Meanwhile, the aromatic residue F357, Y445 acts as a dynamic molecular gate, orchestrating substrate entry, occlusion and release throughout the transport process.

Mebendazole represents an inhibitor of OCTN1

We performed a virtual screen of FDA-approved drugs based on their computationally predicted binding affinity (docking score in kcal/mol) to the substrate-binding pocket of OCTN1-ET (PDB: 9WTX) (Fig. 5A). Candidate selection was prioritized according to the Vina scoring function. Among the top candidates, binding affinity was experimentally determined using bio-layer interferometry (BLI), yielding equilibrium dissociation constants (KD). Mebendazole (MBZ) was identified as the top candidate, exhibiting a KD of 2.48 ± 0.31 nM, corresponding to a binding free energy of approximately −8.9 kcal/mol (Fig. 5B–D, Supplementary Fig. 9A). Functional assessment revealed that MBZ potently inhibits OCTN1-mediated substrates transport. At a concentration of 20 nM, it suppressed ET uptake by ~60% (Fig. 5E). Given the physiological relevance of acetylcholine (ACh) transport via OCTN1 in oncogenesis6,27, we evaluated MBZ in this context and observed a ~ 70% inhibition of ACh uptake (Fig. 5F). These findings position MBZ as a potent OCTN1 inhibitor and highlight its potential as a therapeutic agent targeting OCTN1-mediated oncogenic pathways.

To characterize the binding mode of MBZ, we combined AF3 structural prediction and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations (Fig. 5G, H, Supplementary Fig. 10A–D, Supplementary Table 2), with the AF3-derived model adopted to resolve the detailed intermolecular interactions between MBZ and OCTN1 (Fig. 5G, H). Structurally, MBZ consists of three functional moieties: a benzimidazole core, a carbamate group, and a benzoyl group, which collectively engage in extensive cross-domain interactions spanning the NTD and CTD of OCTN1. The benzoyl group primarily interacts with the NTD via hydrophobic contacts with F149, F150, and Q207. The central benzimidazole core bridges the NTD and CTD, forming hydrophobic interactions with F239 and F357, while its nitrogen atom establishes a hydrogen bond with the hydroxyl oxygen of Y356. The carbamate group mainly contributes to CTD binding, with hydrophobic interaction at F441. And the nitrogen atom linking the carbamate group and benzimidazole core forms a hydrogen bond with the side-chain oxygen of Y385 (Fig. 5G, H).

To functionally validate these key interfacial residues, we performed alanine mutagenesis and quantified binding affinity using both BLI and SPR assays. BLI results showed that mutations F149A, F150A, Y211A, and Y385A reduced MBZ binding affinity by approximately two orders of magnitude. The remaining five mutants exhibited undetectable binding signals in preliminary BLI tests, which were further validated by SPR measurement. Consistent with BLI results, SPR assays confirmed affinity attenuation for all tested mutants, with overall WT affinity values approximately three-fold higher than those obtained from BLI. Notably, the F239A mutation caused the greatest impairment, reducing MBZ affinity by nearly 6,000-fold, whereas mutations of Q207, Y211, Y356, F357, and F441 each decreased binding affinity by around 1,000-fold. Collectively, these mutagenesis results verify that the identified residues are critical for MBZ recognition and high-affinity binding to OCTN1 (Fig. 5I, J, Supplementary Fig. 9B–L).

Mebendazole inhibits the proliferation and metastasis of cervical and lung cancer cells through OCTN1

Accumulating evidence indicates that OCTN1 is up-regulated in various cancer types, where it promotes tumor proliferation and migration. To investigate whether MBZ exerts its anti-tumor effect via OCTN1 inhibition, we assessed cancer cell viability using CCK-8 and colony formation assays. CCK-8 analysis demonstrated that MBZ inhibited the viability of HeLa and A549 cells in a concentration-dependent manner, with IC50 values of 79.28 ± 2.04 nM for HeLa and 181.60 ± 16.52 nM for A549 cells (Fig. 6A, B). To verify OCTN1 as a functional target of MBZ, we knocked down its expression in both cell lines using shRNA (Fig. 6C, D). Notably, OCTN1 knockdown alone reduced cell viability (Fig. 6E, F). Moreover, subsequent treatment with 100 nM MBZ (for HeLa) or 200 nM MBZ (for A549) did not further reduce comparable viability in knockdown cells, indicating that the anti-proliferative effect of MBZ was largely abolished upon OCTN1 depletion (Fig. 6E, F). This result supports OCTN1 as a primary target of MBZ. Furthermore, the results of the gene rescue experiment demonstrated that when OCTN1 was reintroduced into the corresponding knockdown cell lines, the phenomenon of MBZ-induced inhibition of cell proliferation reappeared. However, the mutant of OCTN1 (F357A), which is a key residue for MBZ binding to OCTN1, failed to achieve a similar effect. These findings once again provide evidence for the targeting of MBZ towards OCTN1 (Supplementary Fig. 11A–D).

We further evaluated the effect of MBZ on clonogenic capacity using colony formation assays (Fig. 6G–J). Treatment with 50 nM MBZ suppressed colony formation by approximately 75% in both cell lines, while increasing the concentration to 100 nM enhanced inhibition to 90.6% in HeLa and 86.1% in A549 cells, respectively (Fig. 6G–J). Furthermore, we found that MBZ treatment did not affect OCTN1 expression in cancer cells, indicating that this drug merely inhibits the function of the protein without altering its expression level (Supplementary Fig. 11E–H).

Meanwhile, wound healing assays showed that MBZ significantly inhibited the migration of both HeLa and A549 cells (Fig. 7A, C). Statistical analysis confirmed that this inhibitory effect was concentration- and time-dependent, as indicated by decreased migration with increasing drug concentrations (Fig. 7A, C). On this basis, adding 50 nM MBZ did not further inhibit cell migration as sh-NC groups, proving that OCTN1 is the target of MBZ in inhibiting HeLa and A549 cells metastasis (Fig. 7B, D).

Taken together, these results indicate that MBZ regulates the proliferation and migration in HeLa and A549 cells by targeting OCTN1, highlighting its preclinical therapeutic potential for cervical cancer and non-small cell lung cancer.

Mebendazole effectively suppresses the growth of cervical and lung carcinoma in vivo

To evaluate the anti-tumor efficacy of MBZ in vivo, HeLa and A549 tumor xenograft models were established in C57BL/6 J mice. The mice received intraperitoneal administration of MBZ every three days. Treatment with MBZ resulted in significant growth inhibition of both cervical and lung carcinomas (Fig. 8A). Specifically, while tumor volume increased progressively in the control group, the MBZ-treated groups exhibited markedly suppressed tumor growth, with final tumor volumes and weights reduced to approximately one-third of those in the control group (Fig. 8A-C). Importantly, no significant difference in body weight was observed between the treated and control groups (Fig. 8D), indicating minimal systemic toxicity and no adverse impact on normal metabolism. To further elucidate the in vivo pharmacological mechanism, tumor tissues and major organs were subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. The analysis revealed pronounced nuclear dissociation and tissue necrosis in MBZ-treated tumors compared to controls. Furthermore, H&E staining of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) showed no obvious pathological damage, also suggesting a favorable safety profile of MBZ (Fig. 8E). In conclusion, MBZ effectively suppresses the growth of HeLa and A549 tumors in vivo, potentially through specific targeting of OCTN1.

Discussion

OCTN1 mediates the Na⁺-coupled transmembrane transport of organic cations and zwitterions, and participates in a wide range of physiological processes. Herein, we determined cryo-EM structures of OCTN1 in distinct conformational states, including the apo outward-open and ergothioneine (ET)-bound inward-open forms. Combined with the published structures of OCTN2, our results reveal the conserved architecture of the OCTN subfamily, and elaborate on the characteristics of the ligand-binding pocket and substrate permeation pathway as well as the complete Na⁺-dependent alternating-access transport cycle. Furthermore, guided by structural information, we identified mebendazole (MBZ) as a high-affinity OCTN1 inhibitor with nanomolar potency. Functional assays confirmed that MBZ exerts anti-tumor effects by specifically targeting OCTN1 in cell lines and xenograft mouse models, making it a promising candidate for the treatment of cervical and lung cancers.

Our structural analysis of the OCTN1 substrate-binding pocket identifies a cluster of aromatic residues responsible for substrate recognition (Y211, F239, W349, Y356, F357, F441, Y445), which is a conserved feature among the SLC22 superfamily. Different from canonical OCT and OAT transporters, OCTN1 forms a bipolar electrostatic microenvironment via two oppositely charged residues, E381 and R469. Both residues are highly conserved in OCTN2 (E383 and R471), indicating conserved substrate-binding characteristics within the OCTN subfamily, jointly endowing OCTN1/2 with the capacity to transport zwitterionic substrates. Notably, our mutational validation verified that these key residue mutations do not affect OCTN1 expression or plasma membrane localization (Supplementary Fig. 12A, B), confirming that functional defects stem solely from disrupted substrate binding, rather than impaired protein folding, stability or trafficking.

ET is abundantly enriched in daily edible fungi and Actinomycetes42, and can be accumulated in human tissues via endogenous OCTN1-mediated uptake43, making dietary ET intake physiologically accessible to the general population. Emerging animal and mechanistic studies further validate that ET improves lifespan, enhances physical performance, and elevates NAD⁺ levels by regulating CSE-dependent persulfidation, providing molecular evidence for its anti-aging and metabolic benefits44,45. Epidemiological observations also reveal an inverse correlation between circulating ET levels and age-related neurodegenerative disorders45–47, further supporting the population-wide health relevance of adequate dietary ET supplementation. Our structural elucidation of the OCTN1-ET complex clarifies the molecular mechanism of ET recognition and transport, provides mechanistic support for the physiological functions of ET, and offers a structural basis for the rational design of OCTN1-targeted regulators.

OCTN2, another member of the OCTN family, shares 76% sequence identity with OCTN148. OCTN2 has been identified as a Na+-dependent high-affinity carnitine transporter40. Apart from carnitine, it can also transport organic cations in a sodium-independent way49. Evidence from previous studies indicates a lack of ET transport by rat OCTN27. Our functional assays further confirmed that the ET transport capacity of OCTN2 is merely one-third that of OCTN1 (Supplementary Fig. 5C). Despite the distinct substrate preferences, the residues forming the binding sites for Na⁺ and substrates are largely conserved between the two homologs (Fig. 2A). A notable difference lies at position 239: OCTN1 has phenylalanine (F239), while OCTN2 carries tyrosine (Y239). Mutagenesis of Y239 to phenylalanine or alanine failed to enhance ET transport in OCTN2 (Supplementary Fig. 5C), implying additional factors underlie this functional divergence. We also compared L-carnitine transport and found OCTN1 is much less efficient than OCTN2 for this substrate (Supplementary Fig. 5D). Although both ET and L-carnitine are zwitterions, they differ substantially in molecular structure. The molecular mechanism governing distinct substrate selectivity between OCTN1 and OCTN2 remains to be fully elucidated, representing an interesting direction for future investigation.

Members of the SLC22 transporter family often possess genetic polymorphisms, whereas human OCTs are highly polymorphic32,50, the hOAT1 gene exhibits limited variability with only a few nonsynonymous variants36,51. While OCTN1 harbors numerous genetic variants, most are non-pathogenic52. A notable disease-associated mutation is L503F, which has been linked to Crohn’s disease5,53,54. Experimental evidence indicates that the L503F mutation disrupts acetylcholine transport function without altering the substrate-binding site5. Structural analysis reveals the L503 (TM12) residue is situated distal to the substrate-binding center, yet biochemical evidence demonstrates that the L503F mutation significantly compromises ET transport (Supplementary Fig. 13A, B). Notably, the L503F mutation did not affect the protein expression or plasma membrane localization of OCTN1 (Supplementary Fig. 12A, B). Given its peripheral position relative to the substrate-binding region, this mutation may impair overall transport activity through indirect structural effects, such as altered protein folding, conformational flexibility, or domain communication. Further investigations will be required to fully clarify the underlying structural and molecular basis.

Consistent with previous transcript profiling across the NCI-60 cancer cell panel, OCTN1 is broadly overexpressed in multiple cancer types, including melanoma, central nervous system, renal, breast, and lung cancer cell lines19. Although multiple cancer cell lines including HeLa, A549, HCT-15, MCF-7 and HepG2 possess an active non-neuronal cholinergic system (NNCS), HeLa and A549 exhibit markedly higher endogenous OCTN1 expression levels20,22,25,27,43,55. We therefore selected these two cell lines as preferred models to better characterize the biological function of OCTN1 and the MBZ-OCTN1 regulatory axis, while its widespread expression in other tumor types also implies broader therapeutic relevance. To further clarify the molecular basis underlying ACh recognition, we constructed and analyzed the AF3-predicted OCTN1-ACh complex structure, revealing that the cationic ACh is stably accommodated within the substrate-binding pocket of OCTN1 (Supplementary Fig. 14A, B).

Based on the resolved structure, we successfully identified the candidate drug MBZ targeting OCTN1 and validated its antitumor efficacy in both cellular and animal models. Mebendazole (MBZ; methyl 5-benzoylbenzimidazole-2-carbamate), a broad-spectrum anthelmintic benzimidazole widely used in human and veterinary medicine56, has recently gained attention as a potential candidate for repositioning in cancer therapy with a well-established safety profile57–60. In the realm of cancer research, MBZ exerts multi-mechanistic antitumor effects, including suppressing the VEGFR2 signaling pathway60, enhancing the immune system’s response to cancer cells61,62, and reducing cancer cell metastasis63. Notably, to the best of our knowledge, our study provides the first evidence that MBZ binds OCTN1 with nanomolar affinity and efficiently blocks OCTN1-mediated transport of both ET and ACh. Collectively, as a clinically approved therapeutic agent, MBZ is known to act on multiple molecular targets; nevertheless, combined biochemical assays and cancer cell gene knockdown and rescue experiments clearly establish OCTN1 as a target of MBZ. These findings not only reveal an anti-tumor mechanism of MBZ but also lay a structural and experimental foundation for its future clinical repurposing. Notably, MBZ displayed slightly stronger inhibitory effects on HeLa cells compared with A549 cells (Fig. 6). This phenotypic difference may be associated with intrinsic variations in OCTN1 expression, subcellular distribution, as well as distinct metabolic and non-neuronal cholinergic activity between the two cancer cell lines. Moreover, although our in vivo results demonstrated the antitumor effect of MBZ treatment, we did not directly detect the actual concentration of MBZ in mouse plasma and tumor tissues. Further pharmacokinetic measurement of tissue drug exposure will be required to fully validate the in vivo effective dose and tissue distribution of MBZ in future work.

Despite extensive optimization of sample preparation, detergent screening and nanodiscs reconstitution, we failed to resolve the cryo-EM structure of the MBZ-bound OCTN1 complex. Further molecular dynamics simulations revealed that MBZ retains overall binding stability in the pocket but exhibits obvious conformational flexibility at its terminal moiety (Supplementary Fig. 10E). The intrinsic dynamic flexibility of MBZ, together with the multiple conformational states of OCTN1 induced by inhibitor binding, leads to particle heterogeneity and impedes high-resolution structural determination (Supplementary Fig. 10A-E). Such partial binding instability at one terminal region of MBZ also provides a clear structural hint for subsequent rational modification and optimization of this small molecule to develop more potent OCTN1 inhibitors. Nevertheless, integrating molecular dynamics simulations with biochemical and functional assays enables us to establish a reliable binding model and mechanistic explanation for MBZ-mediated inhibition of OCTN1 activity.

Membrane proteins represent one of the most important yet structurally challenging drug target families in translational medicine, as they mediate substance exchange, signal transduction, and pathological progression across diverse diseases. Compared with GPCRs, whose high-resolution structures have greatly facilitated rational drug design, lead optimization, and precise compound screening over the past decades64, the SLC transporter superfamily remains largely underrepresented in structural determination, severely hindering targeted drug development. Nevertheless, SLC transporters have long been recognized as therapeutic targets for a broad spectrum of human diseases65,66. Rational screening and precise design of SLC-targeted small-molecule drugs are highly dependent on high-resolution structural templates. Our structural and mechanistic findings of OCTN1 are expected to provide a valuable reference and tractable framework for future discovery and rational optimization of therapeutic agents targeting the SLC superfamily.

Methods

Ethical Statement

Animal experiments were done in accordance with the authorization of animal operation and in accordance with the China law for animal protection. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethical and Welfare Committee of Tianjin University (Approval No. TJUE2025-A-S-041).

Materials and chemicals

Detergents, Dodecyl Maltoside (DDM), Lauryl Maltose Neopentyl Glycol (LMNG), and Cholesteryl Hemisuccinate (CHS) were purchased from Antrace Inc. The test molecules were purchased from Med Chem Express (MCE) and dissolved in DMSO at concentrations corresponding to their solubility limits.

Cell lines and cell culture

All E. coli strains were cultured at 37 °C using LB medium. For plasmid construction and amplification, strain DH5α was employed to generate vectors encoding OCTN1 and OCTN1 variants, while strain DH10Bac was utilized to produce bacmids.

Sf9 insect cells (ATCC, CRL-1711) were maintained in SIM SF medium (Sino Biological) at 28 °C. Bacmid transfection was performed via X-tremeGENE 9 DNA Transfection Reagent (Roche) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The resulting baculoviruses were further propagated in Sf9 cells under the same culture conditions.

HEK293F cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific, R79007) were cultivated for protein expression in SMM 293-TI medium (Sino Biological) supplemented with 2% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Gibco, USA), at 37 °C with 5% CO₂.

HEK293T, HeLa and A549 cells were grown in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Gibco, USA), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillinand 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Gibco, USA), and maintained under standard cell culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂). For transient transfections, plasmid DNAs were introduced into HeLa or A549 cells using Lipofectamine 3000 Transfection Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following manufacturer’s recommendations.

For the construction of the OCTN1 knockdown cell lines, HeLa or A549 Cells were transduced with sh-NC or sh-OCTN1 at a multiplicity of infection of 10 for 48 h and were subjected to puromycin selection (1-2 μg/μL). After selection, cells were seeded into 96-well plates.

All cell lines used in this study were tested for mycoplasma contamination using a PCR-based method and were confirmed to be negative.

HeLa cells were originated from a female patient with cervical carcinoma; A549 cells were derived from a male patient diagnosed with lung adenocarcinoma. HEK293T and HEK293F cell lines were both isolated from human embryonic kidney tissue of a female aborted fetus. Sf9 cells are ovarian cells derived from Spodoptera frugiperda (fall armyworm), an insect cell line with no mammalian sex characteristics, so sex-based analysis was not applicable for Sf9-related experiments.

Construct design and protein production

The full-length human OCTN1 gene (NCBI accession NM_003059.3) was codon-optimized for mammalian cell expression and chemically synthesized by Tsingke Biological Technology. The synthesized gene, encoding human OCTN1 with a C-terminal twin-Strep tag, was cloned into the pEG-BacMam vector (Invitrogen) for heterologous expression. Site-directed mutagenesis was performed on this parental construct to generate all OCTN1 mutant variants used in this study. All mutagenesis primers were synthesized by Tsingke Biological Technology, and the full oligonucleotide primer sequences used for site-directed mutagenesis are provided as Supplementary Data 1. All newly generated mutant plasmids are available upon reasonable request from the corresponding author.

Protein overexpression was achieved through the BacMam system (Thermo Fisher Scientific): baculovirus was first generated in Sf9 cells and used to infect HEK293F cells at an MOI of 20, with 10 mM sodium butyrate added post-infection to enhance expression. After 48 h of suspension culture at 37 °C, cells were harvested via centrifugation, sonicated in lysis buffer A (25 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, pH 8.0) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (1 μg/mL leupeptin, 1 μg/mL pepstatin A, 1 μg/mL aprotinin) on ice, and membrane proteins solubilized with 0.5% LMNG/0.05% CHS. Supernatants from 4 °C centrifugation (38,000 × g, 40 min) were loaded onto Streptactin Beads 4FF (Smart-Lifesciences), washed with buffer B (buffer A + 0.01% LMNG/0.001% CHS), and eluted with 5 mM D-Desthiobiotin. Final purification involved size-exclusion chromatography on a Superose 6 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) equilibrated with buffer B, yielding a concentrated protein sample (~10.0 mg/mL) for cryo-EM analysis.

To prepare the OCTN1-ergothioneine (OCTN1-ET) complex, the purified apo human OCTN1 sample was incubated on ice overnight with a final concentration of 2 mM ergothioneine (MedChemExpress, CAS: 497-30-3).

Cryo-EM data collection

For cryo-EM sample preparation, the purified human OCTN1-apo and OCTN1-ET proteins at 10 mg/ml were applied to a glow-discharged holey carbon grids (Quantifoil Au R1.2/1.3, 300 mesh), blotted under 100% humidity at 8 °C and plunged into liquid ethane using a Mark IV Vitrobot (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). For OCTN1-apo, movies were acquired on a Titan Krios electron microscope (FEI) operating at 300 kV, equipped with the GIF-Quantum energy filter and a K3 Summit direct electron detector (Gatan). SerialEM software (FEI) was used for automated data collection following standard FEI procedure. Images were recorded at a normal magnification of 105,000 ×, corresponding to a pixel size of 0.851 Å per pixel and with a set defocus range of 1.0 to 1.5 μm. Each micrograph was dose-fractionated to 40 frames, resulting in an accumulated dose of ~54 e-/Å2, a total of 9,779 movies were automatically recorded using EPU. For OCTN1-ET, movies were collected with a 300 kV Titan Krios electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with a Falcon 4 direct electron detector. Images for OCTN1-ET were recorded at 96,000 × magnification and calibrated at a super-resolution pixel size of 0.83 Å/pixel. The exposure time was set to 6.1 s with a total accumulated dose of 50 electrons per Å2. A total of 9,385 movies were automatically recorded using EPU and were collected with a defocus range from −1.0 μm to −2.0 μm.

Image processing and model building

All dose-fractioned images were motion-corrected and dose-weighted by patch motion correction and the contrast transfer function (CTF) of each micrograph was estimated by patch CTF estimation in cryoSPARC67. The initial particles were picked from a few micrographs using blob picker in cryoSPARC and 2D averages were generated. Particles from high-quality 2D classes were then used for training in Topaz. After Topaz extraction and multi-rounds of 2D classification, ab-initio reconstruction and heterogeneous refinement. After three rounds of 2D classification, ab-initio reconstruction, non-uniform refinement and local refinement for reconstructing the density map. All the maps were low-pass filtered to the map-model FSC value. The reported resolutions were based on the FSC = 0.143 criterion. The workflow is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1 and 2. The models were rebuilt using Coot68 with the guidance of the cryo-EM maps and were refined with real-space refinement using Phenix69. The data validation statistics were shown in Supplementary Table 1. Structural figures were generated with Pymol70, UCSF Chimera, and ChimeraX71.

Ergothioneine transport assay

HEK293F cells expressing human OCTN1 (wild-type or variants) or human OCTN2 (wild-type or variants) were harvested 36 h post-viral infection. Cells were pelleted and washed three times with uptake buffer (25 mM HEPES, 125 mM NaCl, 4.8 mM KCl, 1.2 mM MgSO4, 5.6 mM Glucose, 1.2 mM KH2PO4, 1.2 mM CaCl2, pH7.4) to remove residual culture medium. Washed cells were resuspended in fresh uptake buffer at 5 × 106 cells/mL. For ergothioneine (ET) pretreatment, suspensions were incubated with 200 μM ET for 15 min, followed by uptake buffer wash. Cell pellets underwent three freeze-thaw cycles in liquid nitrogen for lysis. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation (14,000 × g, 30 min, 4 °C), and 100 μL supernatant aliquots were transferred to vials for downstream analysis.

Prior to analysis, all samples underwent filtration through 0.22 μm nylon syringe filters. ET quantification was performed using an HPLC system (Waters Arc Series) with isocratic elution at 1 mL/min. The mobile phase consisted of 80% acetonitrile and 20% 1 mM ammonium acetate, maintained over a 30-min run. Detection occurred at 254 nm, with ET concentrations determined by peak area integration against external calibration standards.

To assess the effects of sodium gradient, the Na⁺ concentration in the uptake buffer was adjusted to 0, 25, 50, 125, 200, or 400 mM. In conditions where the Na⁺ concentration was lower than 125 mM, CsCl was added to maintain the total chloride salt concentration at 125 mM40. For Na⁺ concentrations exceeding 125 mM (i.e., 200 and 400 mM), NaCl was simply increased to the desired concentration without CsCl supplementation. For pH gradient experiments, the pH of the uptake buffer was adjusted to values ranging from 5.0 to 9.0 (e.g., pH 5.0, 6.0, 7.4, 8.0, and 9.0) using HCl or NaOH as appropriate, while keeping all other components unchanged.

Acetylcholine transport assay

HEK293F cells expressed human OCTN1 were harvested 36 h after virus infection. To remove residual culture medium, the cells were centrifuged and washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) three times, and then re-suspended in uptake buffer to a final concentration of 5 × 106 cells/mL. The samples were pre-treated by adding 50 μM of the acetylcholinesterase (AChE) inhibitor and incubating for 15 min at ambient temperature. Subsequently, 100 μM acetylcholine (ACh) was introduced, and the mixture was incubated for an additional 15 min. After a single wash with PBS, the pellets were subjected to three freeze–thaw cycles in liquid nitrogen. The resulting lysates were centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C, and 100 µL of the supernatant was transferred to an LC sample vial for subsequent analysis.

Chromatographic separation was performed on a Thermo Scientific Ultimate 3000 UPLC system coupled to a Q Exactive mass spectrometer operated in positive electrospray ionization mode. The column was a ZORBAX SB-AQ (150 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 µm, Agilent) maintained at 25 °C with a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. The mobile phase consisted of acetonitrile (A) and 0.1 % formic acid in water (B) at an isocratic ratio of A:B = 2:98. MS parameters were set as follows: spray voltage 3.2 kV, capillary temperature 300 °C, sheath gas 40 arbitrary units, auxiliary gas 8 arbitrary units, probe heater temperature 300 °C, and maximum spray current 100 µA.

Quantification of L‑carnitine by competitive ELISA

HEK293F cells expressing OCTN1 were harvested, washed, and pretreated with 200 μM L‑carnitine where indicated. After three freeze-thaw lysis cycles and clarification (3,000×g, 20 min, 4°C), L‑carnitine levels in supernatants were measured using a commercial competitive ELISA kit (MM‑92842701) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, with absorbance read at 450 nm and quantification against a standard curve.

Confocal microscopy imaging analysis

To assess the cell surface localization of OCTN1 and its variants, the coding sequences of wild-type and mutant OCTN1 were cloned into the pcDNA3.1 vector with a C-terminal GFP tag. HEK293T cells were seeded onto confocal dishes (NEST) at an appropriate density and cultured for 24 h. Cells were then transiently transfected with 2 μg of plasmid DNA using Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent (Life Technologies) following the manufacturer’s protocol. At 24 h post-transfection, cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, and stained with DAPI for nuclear visualization. Cells were then imaged using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Leica Stellaris 5, Germany) with excitation/emission settings appropriate for GFP and DAPI.

Virtual screening

A structure-based virtual screening was performed against the cryo-EM structure of OCTN1-ET (PDB ID: 9WTX). A total of 2,342 compounds from the FDA-approved drug library were screened using AutoDock Vina. This library was selected due to the clinical translatability of approved drugs and their well-characterized safety profiles.

We first selected compounds with a molecular weight <300 Da to ensure physical compatibility with the binding site. Docking calculations employed a semi-flexible protocol, the substrate-binding pocket was defined based on the ergothioneine (ET) binding site, with a grid box of dimensions 26 × 26 × 26 Å3 centered at the geometric center of ET. Exhaustiveness was set to 50, and binding poses were retained within 5 kcal/mol of the global minimum. The computationally predicted binding affinity, represented by the AutoDock Vina scoring function (kcal/mol), which approximates the free energy of binding and was used for initial ranking of compounds. The experimentally determined binding affinity, measured by BLI as the equilibrium dissociation constant (KD in nM) for selected compounds. Only a subset of compounds with high predicted affinity was subjected to BLI validation.

Bio-Layer Interferometry (BLI) assay

All affinity assays were performed on an Octet® R8 biolayer interferometry instrument (Sartorius, Germany) at 25 °C with shaking at 1,000 rpm. Super Streptavidin (SSA) biosensors (Sartorius, Germany) were hydrated in deionized water for 30 min and then equilibrated in 1 × HBS EP+ buffer (10 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, 3 mM EDTA, 0.05% (v/v) Surfactant P20, pH 7.4; Cytiva, BR100826) for 60 s. Biotinylated OCTN1 variants stock solution was ~1.5 mg/mL, and diluted in the same HBS EP+ buffer to a final loading concentration of ~0.1 mg/mL for sensor immobilization. Biotinylated OCTN1 variants were loaded onto the sensors for 120 s, followed by a baseline equilibration step of 120 s. The analyte mebendazole (MBZ) was two‑fold serially diluted in the same buffer to concentrations ranging from 1.56 nM to 30 μM. This concentration range was designed based on preliminary kinetic estimates of KD values, placing the anticipated affinity near the mid range of the titration series to accommodate variable binding behaviour across different OCTN1 variants. Association was monitored for 120 s and dissociation for 120 s. The sensor agitation speed was set at 1,000 rpm throughout the assay (no continuous fluid flow is applicable for the Octet® R8 system). Data were collected at a data acquisition rate of 5 Hz (instrument default). Negative controls (buffer without MBZ) and solvent controls (buffer containing matched DMSO concentration) were included.

To reduce high‑frequency mechanical noise inherent to the dip‑and‑read format, raw BLI sensorgrams were processed with the instrument’s built‑in smoothing algorithm prior to kinetic fitting. To correct for non‑specific binding and drift, reference surface subtraction (using blank sensors without immobilized protein) and blank injection subtraction (buffer only) were applied as double referencing. All sensorgrams were baseline‑subtracted. Data analysis was performed using the Octet® R8 Data Analysis Software (Sartorius) with a 1:1 binding model and global fitting, where Rmax was set as a global parameter. Kinetic constants (ka, kd) and the affinity constant (KD) were derived from the fitted curves. Fitted curves were overlaid on the processed experimental sensorgrams, and Rmax, χ², and standard errors of fitted parameters are reported (see Source Data files). For steady‑state affinity analysis, the Req versus concentration plot and the full concentration‑series sensorgrams are also provided in the Source Data files.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) assay

SPR experiments were performed on a Polariton S‑Class® high‑throughput SPR instrument (Polariton Life Technologies Ltd., China) at 25 °C. The running buffer was PBS‑T (1 × phosphate‑buffered saline, 0.05% Tween 20, pH 7.4). A CM5 sensor chip (Polariton Life Technologies Ltd., China) was used. The OCTN1 variant was immobilized onto one flow cell via standard amine coupling. Briefly, the chip surface was activated with a 1:1 mixture of 0.4 M EDC and 0.1 M NHS for 7 min, followed by injection of the protein at 5 μg/mL in 10 mM sodium acetate (pH 4.5). The injection time was adjusted to achieve the desired immobilization level; the total immobilization time, including activation, ligand injection, and blocking, was 38 min. Subsequently, the remaining active groups were blocked by injection of 1 M ethanolamine‑HCl (pH 8.5) for 7 min. A reference flow cell was activated and blocked in the same manner but without protein, to correct for bulk refractive index changes and non‑specific binding.

The analyte mebendazole (MBZ) was serially two‑fold diluted in running buffer to concentrations ranging from 0.4 nM to 50 μM. This concentration range was designed based on preliminary kinetic estimates of KD values, placing the anticipated affinity near the mid range of the titration series to accommodate variable binding behaviour across different OCTN1 variants. DMSO solvent correction was performed to compensate for refractive‑index artifacts introduced by DMSO in sample solutions. Matched solvent blanks and negative buffer controls were included as assay controls. Samples were injected over both flow cells at a flow rate of 70 μL/min, with an association time of 120 s and a dissociation time of 120 s (with association and dissociation times varying for some variants, as detailed in the Source Data files). Data were collected at a rate of 1 Hz (instrument default for kinetic analysis). To correct for systematic drift and non‑specific binding, double referencing was applied by subtracting both the reference flow‑cell signal and blank buffer injections (running buffer only). All sensorgrams were baseline‑subtracted before analysis.

Data analysis was performed using the Polariton PL Workstation software (ver. 1.0.0.1). All SPR data were fitted and analyzed with the built‑in 1:1 binding model. Global fitting was applied with Rmax set as a global parameter. The kinetic association (ka) and dissociation (kd) rate constants and the equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) were derived from the fitted curves. Fitted curves were overlaid on the experimental sensorgrams, and Rmax, χ², and standard errors of the fit parameters are reported. For steady‑state affinity analysis, the Req versus concentration plot and the full concentration‑series sensorgrams are provided in the Source Data files.

Structural predictions

The OCTN1/mebendazole complex structure was predicted using locally installed AF3 following the instructions from Google-DeepMind (https://github.com/google-deepmind/alphafold3/blob/main/docs/input.md), with the string of OCTN1 sequences and CCD code of mebendazole (V95) as the sole input. The output of AF3 predictions (ranking scores, confidences, and five models) was provided as supplementary material.

MD simulations

The membrane-protein system containing human OCTN1 and mebendazole (MBZ) was constructed using CHARMM-GUI within a 123 Å × 123 Å × 143 Å periodic box72. CGenFF was employed to generate the topologies and parameters of MBZ. A typical bilayer contained cholesterol and phospholipids (POPC, POPE, POPS, POPI, and DSM)73. The system (~ 20,600 atoms) was solvated with TIP3P water molecules, neutralized with 150 mM NaCl, and then minimized using steepest descent method before equilibration of 10 ns at 310.15 K. Simulations were conducted in CHARMM36 force field using GROMACS 2019.6. The MD system setup data were shown in Supplementary Table 2. Throughout the simulations, velocity-rescale thermostat and constant pressure (1 bar, Parrinello-Rahman NPT ensemble) were applied. The nonbonded interaction cut-off for electrostatics calculations was set as 10 Å and the particle mesh Ewald (PME) method was used for the calculation of long-range electrostatic interactions with h-bonds LINCS constraints. At the equilibration stages, position constraints were applied to the lipids constituting the membrane by using -DPOSRES_FC_LIPID. These constraints were removed during the production run. Three independent replicate simulations were performed for 100 ns of production MD. For analysis, 100 equally spaced snapshots were extracted from the trajectories of each replicate and the gmx cluster tool was used to extract representative conformations.

Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using the EasyPure RNA Kit (#O101011, Transgene) and quantified by spectrophotometric measurement at 260 nm. cDNA synthesis was performed in a thermal cycler under the following conditions: 25 °C for 5 min, 42 °C for 30 min, and 85 °C for 5 min. qPCR reactions were conducted using the Cell Lysis RT-qPCR Kit in a 15 μL reaction volume containing 1 μL diluted cDNA, 0.4 μL forward primer (10 μM), 0.4 μL reverse primer (10 μM), 10 μL SYBR Green master mix, and 3.2 μL nuclease-free water. The thermal cycling protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 58 °C for 40 s, and 72 °C for 40 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. All reactions were performed in triplicate with GAPDH as the endogenous control, and data analysis utilized the ΔΔCT method. Primer sequences were:

OCTN1-F: 5′-CCACCATCGCCAACTTCTCG-3′,

OCTN1-R: 5′-CACCAGATTCCACTCGGTCA-3′;

GAPDH-F: 5′-CCCACTCCTCCACCTTTGACG-3′;

GAPDH-R: 5′-CACCACCCTGTTGCTGTAGCCA-3′.

Western blot assay

HeLa or A549 cells expressing either wild-type OCTN1 or OCTN1 knockdown were washed three times with PBS (#P1022, Solarbio) and lysed with RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. Protein concentration was determined using the BCA protein assay kit (#PC0020, Solarbio). Equal amounts of protein from each sample were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4°C with primary antibodies against OCTN1 (#83192-1-RR, CloneNo. 240064D5, Proteintech; 1:1,000) and GAPDH (#1226, GenuIN; 1:2,000). After washing three times with TBST (Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20), the membranes were incubated with corresponding secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using a Bio-Rad imaging system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

To exclude the possibility that MBZ affects OCTN1 protein expression, HeLa or A549 cells were treated with either DMSO (vehicle control) or MBZ, and cell lysates were subjected to western blot analysis under the same conditions.

To confirm that the tested OCTN1 variants did not alter protein expression levels, HEK293F cells (20 mL culture) expressing wild-type OCTN1 or its variants were harvested. After small-scale membrane protein purification, the eluted fractions were analyzed by western blot.

Uncropped, unprocessed scans of key western blot gels are provided in the Source Data file and Supplementary Information.

CCK-8 assay

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (Solarbio, Beijing, China), a colorimetric assay that measures the metabolic activity of cells based on the reduction of a water-soluble tetrazolium salt (WST-8) to an orange-colored formazan product.

HeLa or A549 Cells were transduced with sh-NC or sh-OCTN1 at a multiplicity of infection of 10 for 48 h and were subjected to puromycin selection (1-2 μg/μl). After selection, cells were seeded into 96-well plates. In the gene rescue experiment, we reintroduced the plasmids of OCTN1 or OCTN1 (F357A) back into the OCTN1 knockdown HeLa or A549 cell lines via transfection. For the assessment of cell viability, HeLa or A549 cells (WT, gene knockdown type or gene rescue type) were plated in 96-well plates and exposed to different concentrations of MBZ for 72 h. Following this incubation period, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and allowed to react for 2-4 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was then read on a Microplate Reader (Tecan, Austria), and cell viability was calculated as the ratio of the absorbance in the test group wells to that in the untreated control group wells.

Colony formation assay

Colony formation assays were performed using HeLa or A549 cells (WT, sh-NC, or sh-OCTN1). Briefly, 500 to 1,000 cells were seeded per well of a six-well plate and allowed to adhere and grow for 24 h. The cells were then treated with different concentrations of MBZ for 10 to 14 days. During the treatment period, the culture medium was refreshed twice a week. At the end of the incubation, cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde (BL539A, Biosharp) and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (#C8470, Solarbio) to visualize colonies. Cloning efficiency was evaluated by comparing the average number of colonies in the treated groups to that in the untreated control group. Colony counting was performed using ImageJ.

Wound healing assay

A standardized wound healing assay was performed to evaluate cell migration. HeLa or A549 cells (WT, sh-NC, or sh-OCTN1) were seeded in 6-well plates and grown to approximately 90% confluence. A uniform wound was then created in each monolayer using a sterile 200 μL pipette tip. Cells were treated with various concentrations of MBZ or with an equal volume of DMSO (Mock), in medium containing 1% FBS to minimize proliferation-dependent closure. Wound images were captured at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h using an inverted microscope. The wound area at each time point was measured with ImageJ, and the relative wound healing percentage was calculated by comparing the remaining wound area to the initial (0 h) wound area. This metric quantitatively reflects cell migration capability under each treatment condition.

Tumor xenografts in mice

Animal experiments were done in accordance with the authorization of animal operation and in accordance with the China law for animal protection. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethical and Welfare Committee of Tianjin University (Approval No. TJUE2025-A-S-041). C57BL/6 J mice aged 4–5 weeks were purchased from Biocytogen Pharmaceuticals (Beijing) Co., Ltd. Mice were housed under a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle at a temperature of 20–26 °C, humidity of 40–70%, with free access to food and water. Animal sex was clearly documented following the Sex and Gender Equity in Research (SAGER) reporting guidelines. C57BL/6 J mice (4-5 weeks old) were subcutaneously inoculated in the right flank with 5 × 10⁶ HeLa or A549 cells suspended in serum-free DMEM (Note: Only female mice were used for the HeLa cell inoculation; equal numbers of male and female mice were included in the A549 xenograft cohorts, with sex balanced evenly across all experimental subgroups. No sex-stratified statistical analyses were performed for A549 tumor and body weight endpoints in this study).

The mice were randomly assigned to two groups (n = 5 per group): (1) Vehicle control (5% DMSO) group, (2) MBZ (5 mg/kg) group. MBZ was first dissolved in DMSO, and then further diluted into a vehicle containing 5% DMSO, 40% PEG300, 5% Tween-80, and 50% saline. The final formulation was verified to be clear and stable by centrifugation before each injection. For a 20 g mouse, each intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection delivered 0.1 mg of MBZ in a total volume of 200 μL, corresponding to a dose of 5 mg/kg. Treatments were administered by intraperitoneal injection every three days for a total of seven doses. Tumor volume and body weight were measured every 3 days, with tumor volume calculated using the formula: volume = (length × width2)/2. Upon completion of the treatment regimen, all mice were euthanized, and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney) along with tumor tissues were harvested for subsequent pathological examination.

The maximum permitted tumor burden for subcutaneous xenograft models, as defined by the Animal Ethical and Welfare Committee of Tianjin University, was 3,000 mm³ for individual tumors. No tumor in our experiments exceeded this approved maximum tumor‑size limit.

Data analysis

The graphical presentation and data analysis were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9. The data are displayed as mean ± standard deviation (S.D.), and the number of replicates is provided in the figure legends. Statistical significance of the differences between group means was evaluated by two‑sided one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using the Tukey honestly significant difference test as a post hoc test; p values ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant (*p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001, ns, not significant).
