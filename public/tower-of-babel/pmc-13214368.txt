Risk factors for hepatic encephalopathy in patients following transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt: a meta-analysis and systematic review
Authors: Weilin Hu, Jinyu Qin, Liming Qiang, Yi Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHepatic encephalopathy (HE) remains a major complication following transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS) placement. The objective of this study was to systematically evaluate and quantify risk factors for HE after TIPS through a meta-analysis.MethodsA comprehensive search of multiple databases was conducted from inception until December 16, 2025 to identify observational studies (cohort or case-control designs) investigating risk factors for HE in adult patients after TIPS. Literature screening, data extraction, and quality assessment using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) were performed independently by two reviewers. Meta-analyses were conducted using Stata software. Odds ratios (ORs) or mean differences (MD) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were pooled for 26 potential risk factors. Clinical trial number: not applicable.ResultsTwenty-seven studies (total 3,934 patients, 862 HE cases) were included. The overall methodological quality was moderate to high. Significant predictors of post-TIPS HE included advanced age (MD = 4.12 years, 95% CI: 2.68 to 5.56), Child-Pugh class C (OR = 2.87, 95% CI: 1.89 to 4.36), prior history of HE (OR = 3.28, 95% CI: 1.92 to 5.61), preoperative hyperammonemia (MD = 8.02 µmol/L, 95% CI: 4.15 to 11.89), hyponatremia (MD = -3.85 mmol/L, 95% CI: -4.72 to -2.98), significant pleural effusion (OR = 2.45, 95% CI: 1.28 to 4.68), and insufficient portal pressure gradient (PPG) reduction (MD = 2.31 mmHg, 95% CI: 1.15 to 3.47). Child-Pugh class A (OR = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.32 to 0.63) and the use of stents with a diameter ≥ 8 mm (OR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.39 to 0.86) were protective factors.ConclusionThis meta-analysis identifies post-TIPS hepatic encephalopathy as a multifactorial condition determined by the interplay of hepatic functional reserve (Child-Pugh score, ammonia), patient characteristics (age, prior HE), electrolyte balance (sodium), hemodynamic response (PPG reduction), and procedural factors (stent diameter). The quantification of 26 distinct factors underscores the necessity of a comprehensive, multidimensional risk assessment that integrates metabolic, neurological, and hemodynamic profiles.Keywords: Hepatic encephalopathy, Transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt, Meta-analysis, Treatment, Clinical

Background

The Transjugular Intrahepatic Portosystemic Shunt (TIPS) procedure is a critical interventional technique for managing complications of cirrhotic portal hypertension, such as refractory ascites or variceal bleeding [1, 2]. By creating an intrahepatic portosystemic shunt, TIPS effectively reduces portal pressure, promptly controls bleeding, and alleviates ascites, significantly improving patients’ short-term survival rates [3–5]. However, the establishment of this shunt also diverts a portion of portal blood flow away from hepatic metabolism directly into the systemic circulation. As a result, gut-derived ammonia and other neurotoxins bypass hepatic detoxification and act directly on the central nervous system, substantially increasing the risk of Hepatic Encephalopathy (HE). Post-TIPS HE not only severely impairs patients’ cognitive function, quality of life, and long-term prognosis but is also a major contributor to rehospitalization, increased healthcare burden, and reduced overall survival [6, 7]. Therefore, identifying and effectively managing HE following TIPS has become a central challenge in clinical practice.

Although numerous studies have investigated the risk of HE after TIPS, reported risk factors exhibit considerable heterogeneity. Variables such as patient age, liver function reserve (e.g., Child-Pugh score, MELD score), shunt diameter, the presence of covert HE prior to the procedure, and biomarkers like serum sodium and ammonia levels have yielded inconsistent conclusions across different studies. These discrepancies likely stem from variations in study design, sample size, population characteristics, and diagnostic criteria, making it difficult for clinicians to derive reliable assessment and prevention strategies from any single study [8, 9]. Systematically synthesizing the existing evidence to identify consistent and clinically actionable risk factors is therefore of paramount importance for enabling precise preoperative risk stratification, facilitating early postoperative monitoring, and guiding the development of individualized preventive measures.

Therefore, this study aims to conduct a meta-analysis to systematically evaluate and synthesize existing observational studies on risk factors for HE following TIPS. The objective is to identify independent and robust predictive factors, thereby providing high-level evidence to support the development of clinical risk prediction models, optimize patient selection, and inform targeted prevention and management strategies.

Methods

Ethical approval and study design

This meta-analysis constitutes a secondary analysis of previously published observational studies (cohort and case-control designs) and involved no direct patient intervention, collection of private information, or access to original medical records. Accordingly, ethical approval was not required. The study was conducted in strict accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [10]. Its objective was to systematically evaluate risk factors for hepatic encephalopathy following transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt placement.

Exclusion Criteria: Case reports, reviews, conference abstracts, animal studies, non-Chinese/English publications, studies with data that cannot be extracted or are duplicate publications, and studies with a sample size smaller than 20.

Exclusion Criteria: Case reports, reviews, conference abstracts, animal studies, non-Chinese/English publications, studies with data that cannot be extracted or are duplicate publications, and studies with a sample size smaller than 20.

Literature search strategy

A combination of computerized and manual supplementary search strategies was employed. Systematic searches were conducted in PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, Web of Science Core Collection, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), WanFang Data, and VIP Chinese Sci-Tech Journals Database (VIP), with the search timeframe spanning from the inception of each database to December 16, 2025. Search terms combined subject headings and free-text terms. Search terms included: “Transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt,” “TIPS,” “hepatic encephalopathy,” “postoperative complications,” “risk factors,” The search strategy was developed with input from an informatics specialist to ensure comprehensive coverage and precision. Furthermore, a backward citation search of all references was performed to identify any missed studies.

Literature screening and data extraction

Literature screening and data extraction were conducted independently and in a blinded manner by two researchers. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion or by consulting a third researcher. The screening process consisted of three steps: first, duplicate records were removed using reference management software; second, titles and abstracts were reviewed for initial screening; finally, full texts were obtained and reviewed for final inclusion determination. A standardized data extraction form was designed to collect information including: first author, publication year, country, study design, sample size, patient baseline characteristics (age, sex, etiology of cirrhosis, Child-Pugh grade, MELD score, etc.), TIPS indication, postoperative follow-up duration, diagnostic criteria and incidence rate of HE, analyzed risk factors and their corresponding effect measures [e.g., hazard ratio, odds ratio, relative risk, along with their 95% confidence intervals], or raw data when available.

Literature quality assessment

Given that all included studies are observational, the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) [11] was used to assess methodological quality. The NOS evaluates cohort studies and case-control studies across three domains: selection of study groups (4 points), comparability of groups (2 points), and assessment of exposure or outcome (3 points), with a maximum score of 9. Studies scoring ≥ 7 were considered high quality, 5–6 as moderate quality, and < 5 as low quality. Two researchers independently performed the assessments, and disagreements were resolved through consensus. The quality assessment results will be used in sensitivity analyses to evaluate the impact of low-quality studies on the pooled results.

Data analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Stata version 16.0 software. For dichotomous risk factor variables, odds ratios (ORs) or risk ratios (RRs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) from individual studies were pooled. If original studies did not provide effect measures, data were calculated from the provided contingency tables where possible. Heterogeneity among the included studies was first assessed using the Q-test and the I² statistic. An I² ≤ 50% and a P-value ≥ 0.10 indicated acceptable heterogeneity, and a fixed-effects model (e.g., Mantel-Haenszel method) was applied. If I² > 50% or P < 0.10, indicating significant heterogeneity, a random-effects model (e.g., DerSimonian-Laird method) was used, and sources of heterogeneity were explored. Subgroup analyses (e.g., by study region, sample size, study quality, TIPS indication, follow-up duration) and meta-regression were conducted to investigate heterogeneity. Potential publication bias was assessed using Egger’s test and visual inspection of funnel plots. All statistical tests were two-sided, with a P-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Literature screening process

The literature screening was conducted in strict accordance with the PRISMA statement, with the detailed flow diagram presented in Fig. 1. A total of 1,464 records were retrieved from database searches, supplemented by 10 records from other sources. After removing duplicates, the titles and abstracts of 1,393 records were screened. Subsequently, 62 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Following full-text review, a total of 27 studies [12–38] were included in this meta-analysis.

Characteristics of included studies

The 27 included studies encompassed a total of 3,934 patients, among whom 862 (21.9%) developed hepatic encephalopathy (HE) after TIPS. The studies were predominantly from China (n = 22), with the remainder originating from the United Kingdom, India, the United States, Italy, and Germany (Table 1). All studies employed a case-control design. The primary indications for TIPS were variceal bleeding and/or refractory ascites. The diagnosis of HE was primarily based on the West Haven criteria. The timing of post-procedural assessment varied from short-term follow-up to several years, reflecting heterogeneity in clinical follow-up strategies.

General patient characteristics and liver function reserve

Advanced age, higher MELD scores, and higher Child-Pugh scores were identified as significant risk factors (all P < 0.001). Specifically, patients with Child-Pugh class C had a 2.87-fold increased risk of developing HE (95% CI: 1.89–4.36) compared to others, whereas Child-Pugh class A was a protective factor (OR = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.32–0.63).

Medical history and preoperative status

A history of prior HE was the strongest predictor of postoperative recurrence (OR = 3.28, 95% CI: 1.92–5.61). The presence of significant pleural effusion also increased the risk (OR = 2.45, 95% CI: 1.28–4.68).

Laboratory parameters

Elevated preoperative blood ammonia (MD = 8.02 µmol/L), decreased serum sodium (MD = -3.85 mmol/L), increased total bilirubin (MD = 2.85 µmol/L), and elevated preoperative blood urea nitrogen (MD = 1.12 mmol/L) were all significant risk factors (all P < 0.01).

Hemodynamic and stent parameters

A smaller reduction in the post-procedural portal pressure gradient (PPG) was a significant risk factor (MD = 2.31 mmHg). Furthermore, the use of stents with a diameter ≥ 8 mm was associated with a significantly lower risk of HE compared to stents < 8 mm in diameter (OR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.39–0.86).

The following factors did not show a statistically significant association with post-TIPS HE in this meta-analysis: Child-Pugh class B, ascites, etiology of cirrhosis (viral hepatitis or alcoholic), preoperative hemoglobin, postoperative INR, MELD-Na score, hepatic venous pressure gradient (HVPG), and stent position (left vs. right branch).

Heterogeneity across studies was assessed using the I² statistic and Cochran’s Q test. As shown in Table 3, substantial heterogeneity was observed for several key risk factors, including age (I² = 58%, P < 0.01), MELD score (I² = 82%, P < 0.01), prior HE history (I² = 71%, P < 0.01), and preoperative sodium (I² = 62%, P < 0.01), warranting the use of random-effects models for these analyses. Moderate heterogeneity was noted for Child-Pugh class C (I² = 55%, P = 0.03) and postoperative PPG (I² = 73%, P < 0.01), which were also analyzed using random-effects models. In contrast, factors such as Child-Pugh class A (I² = 0%, P = 0.71), ascites (I² = 0%, P = 0.48), viral hepatitis (I² = 0%, P = 0.85), and stent diameter (I² = 0%, P = 0.45) demonstrated low heterogeneity and were pooled using fixed-effects models. These heterogeneity assessments informed model selection and support the robustness of the pooled estimates.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive assessment of the risk factors for HE following TIPS through a systematic review and meta-analysis. The findings indicate that advanced age, poor liver function reserve, prior history of HE, elevated preoperative blood ammonia, and an insufficient reduction in portal pressure gradient (PPG) are significantly associated with an increased risk of post-TIPS HE. The clinical implications of our findings merit particular consideration in the context of patients awaiting liver transplantation. For this population, the risk-benefit calculus surrounding TIPS differs fundamentally from that in non-transplant candidates. While advanced age, Child-Pugh class C, and prior HE history—identified as significant risk factors in this meta-analysis—may counsel caution in the general population, they do not necessarily preclude TIPS in transplant-listed patients. In this setting, TIPS serves as a bridge to definitive therapy, offering critical management of refractory ascites or variceal bleeding while the patient awaits organ availability. Post-TIPS HE, though a recognized complication, is generally reversible and amenable to medical management (e.g., lactulose, rifaximin) in the interim period. Conversely, the alternative—uncontrolled complications of portal hypertension—may precipitate clinical deterioration that renders the patient ineligible for transplantation altogether. Therefore, for transplant-listed patients, the identified risk factors should inform, rather than contraindicate, clinical decision-making. Specifically, they should prompt: (1) expedited transplant evaluation and prioritization on the waiting list; (2) more aggressive prophylactic HE management (e.g., early initiation of dual ammonia-lowering therapy); and (3) comprehensive patient counseling regarding the expected post-TIPS course and the temporary nature of HE in the context of eventual transplantation. This nuanced approach acknowledges that in the transplant setting, the therapeutic window for TIPS extends beyond immediate procedural outcomes to encompass the ultimate goal of bridging patients to life-saving surgery.

The occurrence of HE after TIPS results from the interplay of two primary mechanisms: portosystemic shunting and liver functional reserve failure. Our meta-analysis revealed that patients classified as Child-Pugh C face a 2.87-fold increased risk of HE, whereas Child-Pugh A serves as a protective factor. This highlights that the synthetic and detoxification capacity of hepatocytes sets a critical upper limit for blood ammonia fluctuations. While clinical focus often centers on shunt diameter, the “residual metabolic area” of the liver itself – a non-renewable resource – can be overlooked [39]. When the volume of functioning liver parenchyma falls below a critical threshold, even a 6 mm stent can precipitate HE [40]. Therefore, preoperative assessment should treat “Child-Pugh grade” as a non-modifiable variable; for Class C patients, strategies such as using tapered stents or delaying the TIPS procedure should be prioritized over merely optimizing stent diameter [41].

The consistent association of higher MELD scores and advanced age with HE risk underscores that risk arises not only from “static” liver function but also from “dynamic” multi-organ reserve. Advanced age is often accompanied by sarcopenia, which impairs the skeletal muscle pathway for ammonia clearance (via peripheral AST/ALT metabolism and glutamine synthesis). A high MELD score reflects combined hepatorenal dysfunction, leading to increased renal ammonia production and insufficient substrates for the urea cycle [42]. Under this dual burden, blood ammonia can exceed critical thresholds within 24 h. Future risk prediction models should incorporate interaction terms such as “age × MELD” rather than relying solely on MELD cut-off values, to avoid underestimating risk in patients over 60 with high MELD scores [43].

A “prior history of HE” emerged as the strongest single predictor in our analysis (OR = 3.28). The underlying mechanism is likely a permanent reduction in the brain’s ammonia tolerance threshold following the initial insult. Animal studies have shown that a single episode of acute hyperammonemia can trigger phenotypic changes in astrocytes, rendering the brain more susceptible to edema and oxidative stress upon subsequent exposure to similar ammonia levels. Therefore, a history of HE should be viewed as a biomarker of “neuronal vulnerability,” not merely a clinical record. For these patients, preoperative evaluation should routinely include neuropsychological assessment combined with MRI-magnetization transfer imaging to screen for subclinical low-grade cerebral edema [44]. Furthermore, proactive dual-pathway ammonia reduction (e.g., lactulose and rifaximin) should be initiated within the first postoperative week, rather than waiting for the onset of grade 2 symptoms [45].

Regarding hemodynamics, the finding that insufficient PPG reduction (MD = 2.31 mmHg) increases HE risk may appear counterintuitive but reflects a mechanistic cascade: inadequate portal decompression perpetuates intestinal congestion and bacterial translocation, thereby enhancing ammonia production. These findings align with the clinical distinction between de novo stent selection and salvage therapy for established HE. Our meta-analysis suggests that for initial TIPS placement, stents ≥ 8 mm in diameter are protective compared to smaller calibers (OR = 0.58). This counterintuitive finding supports a ‘therapeutic window’ hypothesis: when initial stent diameter falls below 8 mm, higher flow velocities are required to achieve adequate portal decompression, which may paradoxically increase portal flow ‘steal’ from peripheral hepatic branches and reduce sinusoidal perfusion—thereby elevating the proportion of ammonia bypassing hepatic clearance. In contrast, for patients who develop refractory HE post-TIPS, stent diameter reduction or occlusion (via parallel stent-graft placement or occlusion devices) represents an established salvage strategy that directly addresses excessive shunting [46]. These are distinct clinical scenarios: initial selection aims to balance decompression with perfusion, while salvage downsizing addresses established complications of excessive shunting. Accordingly, we recommend intraoperative use of 8–10 mm diameter-controlled expansion stents with pressure-guided dilation (targeting PPG 8–12 mmHg or ≥ 25% reduction) for de novo placement, while reserving diameter reduction for patients with refractory HE who fail medical management.

From a laboratory perspective, hyperammonemia, hyponatremia, and hyperbilirubinemia form a “metabolic triad” pointing to combined dysfunction of hepatocyte mitochondria, bile canaliculi, and sinusoids. Hyponatremia may amplify cerebral ammonia transfer by downregulating blood-brain barrier Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase activity, while hyperbilirubinemia can competitively inhibit UGT1A1, impeding alternative ammonia metabolic pathways. These factors synergistically elevate intracranial ammonia concentration beyond what is reflected by “absolute blood ammonia levels.” These findings suggest that hyponatremia is a quantitatively important risk factor. While our meta-analysis identifies a mean difference of -3.85 mmol/L, translation to a clinically actionable threshold requires integration with established hepatology guidelines. A serum sodium level ≤ 130 mmol/L—recognized by EASL and AASLD guidelines as a marker of advanced decompensation—may serve as a pragmatic trigger for intensified perioperative management. At this threshold, we recommend combined intervention with 3% hypertonic saline and albumin infusion where clinically indicated, aiming to restore the sodium gradient and potentially mitigate the ‘metabolic triad’ cascade [47].

The lack of statistical significance for viral hepatitis and alcoholic cirrhosis as risk factors warrants careful interpretation in light of the study population’s composition. With 81% of studies originating from China—where hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection is the predominant etiology of cirrhosis—the analysis was disproportionately weighted toward HBV-related portal hypertension. This distribution inherently limits the statistical power to detect etiologically specific effects, particularly for alcohol-related liver disease (ALD) and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), which are more prevalent in Western populations. Importantly, the biological mechanisms linking cirrhosis etiology to post-TIPS HE risk may differ fundamentally: ALD is often accompanied by sarcopenia and nutritional deficiencies that impair extrahepatic ammonia clearance via skeletal muscle, while MASLD is associated with systemic inflammation and insulin resistance that may potentiate neurotoxicity. Conversely, well-compensated HBV cirrhosis with preserved hepatocellular function may confer a relatively lower HE risk following TIPS, potentially diluting the observed effect of ‘viral hepatitis’ as a categorical variable. Therefore, the non-significant findings for these etiologies should not be interpreted as evidence of no association, but rather as a reflection of the cohort’s etiologic profile. Future multicenter international registries are essential to disentangle the differential impacts of cirrhosis etiology on HE pathogenesis.

The identification of multiple independent risk factors in this meta-analysis provides a foundation for individualized perioperative management of TIPS patients. For those with high-risk profiles—such as advanced age, Child-Pugh class C, and elevated preoperative ammonia—clinicians should consider preoperative initiation of pharmacologic ammonia reduction (e.g., lactulose and/or rifaximin) [48], coupled with intensified postoperative monitoring of ammonia and electrolytes. The convergence of hyperammonemia, hyponatremia, and hyperbilirubinemia—a “metabolic triad”—may identify patients requiring particularly intensive surveillance, including a lower threshold for ICU admission, early nutritional support, and proactive fluid and electrolyte management. Such patients are also more likely to require “bailout” interventions (e.g., stent reduction or occlusion) for refractory HE, underscoring the importance of optimal initial patient selection and stent sizing. Integrating these measures with appropriate stent diameter selection enables a more comprehensive, personalized treatment strategy [49]. We therefore propose the development of a risk assessment model incorporating key factors—age, liver function reserve, preoperative ammonia, and stent diameter—to quantify individual patient risk. Implementation of such a model would facilitate more precise preoperative patient selection, targeted postoperative preventive measures, and optimized allocation of healthcare resources.

In transplant-listed patients, the risk-benefit assessment for TIPS fundamentally differs from that in non-transplant candidates. For this population, the identified risk factors—including advanced age, Child-Pugh class C, and prior HE history—should inform aggressive prophylactic strategies rather than serve as contraindications. The therapeutic window for TIPS in this setting extends beyond immediate procedural outcomes to encompass the ultimate goal of bridging patients to life-saving transplantation. Uncontrolled complications of portal hypertension (e.g., refractory ascites, variceal bleeding) may precipitate clinical deterioration—including acute kidney injury, spontaneous bacterial peritonitis, or multiorgan failure—that renders patients ineligible for transplantation altogether. Post-TIPS HE, while a recognized complication, is generally reversible and amenable to medical management (e.g., lactulose, rifaximin) in the interim period. Therefore, for transplant-listed patients with high-risk profiles, we recommend: (1) expedited transplant evaluation and prioritization on the waiting list; (2) more aggressive prophylactic HE management, including early initiation of dual ammonia-lowering therapy (lactulose and rifaximin) immediately post-procedure; and (3) comprehensive patient counseling regarding the expected post-TIPS course and the temporary nature of HE in the context of eventual transplantation. This nuanced approach acknowledges that in the transplant setting, the risk of post-TIPS HE must be weighed against the certainty of deterioration without effective portal decompression.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations of this meta-analysis should be acknowledged. First, all included studies employed a case-control design, which precludes establishing causal relationships or temporal sequences between risk factors and HE onset. Second, although Egger’s test did not indicate significant publication bias, 81% of the studies originated from China, where hepatitis B is the predominant etiology of cirrhosis. This geographic and etiologic homogeneity may limit the generalizability of our findings to Western populations with different disease profiles (e.g., alcohol-related or NASH-associated cirrhosis). Third, substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity was observed across studies, including variability in TIPS indications, follow-up durations, HE assessment tools (e.g., West Haven criteria with variable application), and the timing of outcome ascertainment (ranging from hospital discharge to 24 months). This heterogeneity precluded dose-response analyses and limited our ability to account for time-to-event data. Fourth, several clinically relevant parameters could not be analyzed due to inconsistent reporting or insufficient data across studies. These include the presence of spontaneous portosystemic shunts, detailed hemodynamic measurement techniques (e.g., HVPG/PPG methodology, zero-reference positioning, catheter calibration), and pre-TIPS pharmacological interventions (e.g., lactulose, rifaximin). Additionally, while we identified significant associations for key risk factors, the derived effect estimates (e.g., ammonia elevation > 8.02 µmol/L, sodium reduction < -3.85 mmol/L, PPG reduction < 2.31 mmHg) require prospective validation in independent cohorts. The West Haven criteria for HE diagnosis and hemodynamic measurements are subject to interobserver variability, which may have contributed to heterogeneity; future studies should report intraclass correlation coefficients and consider central adjudication of outcomes. Finally, the observational nature of the included studies precludes adjustment for unmeasured confounders, and the absence of individual patient-level data limited our ability to explore interactions between risk factors (e.g., age × MELD). These limitations underscore the need for large-scale, multicenter, prospective registry-based cohort studies with standardized protocols for HE assessment (e.g., West Haven criteria with central adjudication at predefined time points: 1, 3, 6, and 12 months), uniform data collection on hemodynamic techniques and pre-TIPS medications, and systematic reporting of potential confounders. Such studies would enable validation of the identified cutoff values, assessment of interobserver reliability, and development of robust risk prediction models. Concurrently, investigations into novel biomarkers (e.g., inflammatory cytokines, gut microbial markers) and randomized controlled trials of targeted preventive interventions in high-risk patients are warranted to advance evidence-based management of post-TIPS HE.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this meta-analysis demonstrates that post-TIPS hepatic encephalopathy results from a complex multifactorial interplay among hepatic functional reserve, portosystemic shunting, and metabolic disturbances. This meta-analysis quantifies the relative weight of key risk factors—including advanced age, Child-Pugh class C, prior HE, preoperative hyperammonemia, hyponatremia, and insufficient PPG reduction—while identifying Child-Pugh class A and the use of stents ≥ 8 mm as protective. These findings underscore the necessity of moving beyond unidimensional assessments toward a multidimensional risk stratification that integrates age, liver function, ammonia levels, and hemodynamic targets.

For clinical practice, we propose a risk-stratified approach: preoperative pharmacological ammonia reduction in high-risk patients; intraoperative use of 8–10 mm diameter-controlled stents with pressure-guided dilation (targeting a PPG of 8–12 mmHg or a ≥ 25% reduction from baseline); and intensified postoperative monitoring. In patients with a history of prior HE, consideration of neuroimaging and early initiation of dual-pathway therapy may be warranted. Future research should prioritize: (1) validation of risk prediction models using multicenter prospective data, incorporating interaction terms such as age × MELD; (2) investigation of the gut microbiota–inflammation axis in HE pathogenesis; and (3) randomized controlled trials comparing preventive strategies (e.g., combinations of stent diameter selection and pharmacologic prophylaxis) in high-risk populations. Pursuing these research directions will be essential to facilitate the transition from empirical management toward precision medicine in post-TIPS care.
