Inhibition of Candida albicans yeast-to-hypha transition and virulence by cyclic dipeptides derived from probiotic Aeromonas veronii V03
Authors: Jinendiran Sekar, Gokul Parasuraman, Anandakumar Shanmugam, Dhanasekaran Dharumadurai, B S Dileep Kumar, Sivakumar Natesan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCandida albicans, a common opportunistic fungal pathogen, poses serious clinical challenges due to multidrug resistance and limited therapeutic options. In this study, four cyclic dipeptides (diketopiperazines, DKPs), cyclo(l-Pro-l-Leu), cyclo(l-Pro-l-Val), cyclo(d-Pro-l-Phe), and cyclo(l-Pro-d-Tyr) were isolated from the probiotic bacterium Aeromonas veronii strainV03 and evaluated for antimicrobial and antivirulence activities. All DKPs exhibited broad-spectrum antibacterial and antifungal effects, with cyclo(d-Pro-l-Phe) exhibiting particularly potent activity against C. albicans, with minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values lower than the standard antifungal amphotericin B under the tested conditions. The DKPs also significantly inhibited key virulence traits of C. albicans, including yeast-to-hypha transition, secreted hydrolase activities (aspartic proteases and phospholipases), and biofilm formation, in a dose-dependent manner. Molecular docking revealed strong binding affinities of the DKPs to virulence-associated proteins, including ERG1, ERG11, HGC1, HWP1, SAP2, SAP5, and ALS1, consistent with their observed antivirulence effects. These findings highlight DKPs from A. veronii V03 as promising antivirulence agents and potential leads for alternative antifungal therapeutics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48192-0.Keywords: Candida albicans, Diketopiperazines, Antifungal, Antivirulence, Docking analysisSubject terms: Drug discovery, Microbiology

Introduction

Infectious diseases caused by both bacterial and fungal pathogens remain a major global health challenge, contributing to significant morbidity and mortality worldwide1,2. Opportunistic pathogens such as Candida albicans, a WHO critical priority pathogen, alongside bacterial species including Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, Proteus mirabilis, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, are leading causes of nosocomial infections and invasive diseases1–4. These microorganisms are often co-isolated from infection sites, exacerbating disease severity and complicating treatment outcomes1,2,5,6. The increasing prevalence of multidrug resistance among bacterial and fungal pathogens has created an urgent need for alternative therapeutic strategies that go beyond conventional antimicrobial agents2,7. Targeting virulence factors, essential traits for pathogenesis but dispensable for microbial survival, represents a promising approach to suppress infection while minimizing selective pressure for resistance development. Inhibiting virulence can suppress infection while reducing selective pressure for resistance development7,8

Candida albicans exhibits a key virulence trait: the ability to transition from yeast to filamentous hyphal forms. This morphogenetic switch facilitates tissue invasion, adhesion, biofilm formation, and secretion of hydrolytic enzymes7,9–11. Hyphal development is regulated by multiple genes, including HGC1, a hyphal-specific cyclin; adhesion proteins such as HWP1 and ALS1; and secreted aspartyl proteinases such as SAP2 and SAP5, which degrade host proteins to promote tissue invasion. Sterol biosynthesis enzymes, including ERG1 and ERG11, maintain membrane integrity and are common antifungal drug targets2,6,12. Disruption of these genes reduces virulence, making them attractive targets for antivirulence therapies aimed at mitigating pathogenicity without promoting resistance7,8,13–16.

Probiotic bacteria have emerged as a potential strategy to counter infections by shaping host responses, inhibiting pathogens, or producing bioactive compounds17–20. Among microbial metabolites, cyclic dipeptides (diketopiperazines, DKPs) exhibit diverse biological activities, including antibacterial, antifungal, and antitumor effects21,22. For instance, cyclo(L-Pro-D-Arg) exhibits antibacterial and antitumor activity23, while DKPs such as cyclo(L-Leu-L-Tyr) and cyclo(L-Leu-L-Pro) inhibit Staphylococcus epidermidis biofilms24,25. Certain Lactobacillus-derived compounds, including cyclo(L-Phe-L-Pro), cyclo(L-Phe-trans-4-OH-L-Pro), and 1-acetyl-β-carboline, shown to suppress C. albicans growth and morphogenesis8,15,26.

Probiotics are widely recognized as a promising source for disease prevention and health promotion, largely due to their ability to produce antimicrobial peptides. Among them, strains of Lactobacillus have been extensively studied for their antimicrobial properties8,27–29. Beyond Lactobacillus, Aeromonas represents a less-explored genus with potential for producing bioactive peptides. Notably, Gramicidin S, a cyclic decapeptide produced by A. veronii, exhibits antibacterial activity, although its clinical application is largely limited to topical use30,31. Recently, we reported that the culture supernatant of the greenish-yellow pigmented probiotic strain A. veronii V03 inhibits the growth of Aeromonas hydrophila and various Vibrio species32. Moreover, dietary supplementation with A. veronii V03 enhances protection against A. hydrophila in Cyprinus carpio by enhancing host immunity33,34. Despite these promising findings, the production, structural characterization, and therapeutic potential of DKPs from A. veronii V03 remain unexplored. Therefore, in the present study, we aimed to isolate and identify structurally distinct DKPs from A. veronii V03 and evaluate their antimicrobial activities, with a particular focus on their effects on C. albicans virulence factors, including the yeast-to-hypha transition, adhesion, and biofilm formation. To explore potential mechanisms, molecular docking was performed to predict interactions between the DKPs and key C. albicans virulence proteins, complementing experimental observations and supporting their therapeutic relevance.

Results

Purification of bioactive metabolites

We previously reported that the culture supernatant of A. veronii V03 exhibited promising antibacterial activity32. To identify the active metabolite(s), the cell-free supernatant was extracted with ethyl acetate, and the crude extract was evaluated for antimicrobial activity against a broad range of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria as well as fungal pathogens, using the agar well diffusion assay. The ethyl acetate extract exhibited broad-spectrum activity against both bacterial and fungal pathogens (Table S1). Of the 14 bacterial pathogens tested, the ethyl acetate extract showed the largest zones of inhibition against P. mirabilis (24.5 ± 0.5 mm), S. aureus (20 ± 2.5), A. hydrophila (22.5 ± 2 mm), and M. smegmatis (20.5 ± 2) (Table S1; Fig. S1). The extract also exhibited promising anticandidal activity against Candida species (Table S1; Fig. S2). The ethyl acetate extract was subsequently fractionated by column chromatography, and the resulting fractions were evaluated for antimicrobial activity against P. aeruginosa and C. albicans. Among six fractions (F1–F6), fractions F2–F6 exhibited notable antimicrobial activity (Table S2; Fig. S3A, B). Further purification of these bioactive fractions yielded four cyclic dipeptides (DKPs): DKP-1 (eluted with 35% ethyl acetate in hexane), DKP-2 (50% ethyl acetate in hexane), DKP-3 (2% methanol in chloroform: hexane), and DKP-4 (15% methanol in chloroform). Thin-layer chromatography (TLC) of all eluted DKPs on silica gel sheets demonstrated single spots, confirming their purity. Liquid chromatography (LC) further confirmed purity levels: DKP-1 (94.93%), DKP-2 (92.78%), DKP-3 (91.40%), and DKP-4 (90.02%) (Fig. S4A–D).

Structural elucidation of bioactive compounds

The structures of the purified DKPs were elucidated using nuclear magnetic resonance (1H and 13C NMR) spectroscopy and high-resolution mass spectrometry (HR-MS). Based on comprehensive spectral analysis, the isolated cyclic dipeptides were identified as cyclo(l-Pro-l-Leu) (DKP-1), cyclo(l-Pro-l-Val) (DKP-2), cyclo(d-Pro-l-Phe) (DKP-3), and cyclo(l-Pro-d-Tyr) (DKP-4) (Fig. 1). The 1H and 13C NMR spectra showed characteristic signals corresponding to the proline residue present in all DKPs. A triplet signal at δH 4.01 ppm and a corresponding carbon resonance at δC 59.0 ppm were assigned to the α-proton and α-carbon of proline, respectively. Additional multiplet signals at δH 2.2, 1.9, and 3.4 ppm, further supported the presence of the proline moiety (Fig. S5). An amide proton resonance was observed at δH 5.9 ppm in all DKPs containing proline, consistent with the cyclic dipeptide structure. The molecular masses determined by HR-MS were in agreement with the expected molecular formulas of the respective DKPs. The absolute configurations were assigned by comparison of the obtained spectroscopic data and specific optical rotation values with previously reported literature values35–38.

Antimicrobial activity of DKPs against fungal and bacterial pathogens

Disk diffusion assays revealed that all four purified DKPs exhibited antimicrobial activity against the tested bacterial and fungal pathogens (Table 2; Fig. S7A–B). Among the bacterial strains, P. mirabilis was the most susceptible, with all DKPs at 25 μg/mL producing the largest average inhibition zones (24.07 ± 0.84 mm), which were significantly greater than that of the standard antibiotic ciprofloxacin (16.5 ± 2.92 mm) (Table 2; Fig. S7C). Among the fungal pathogens, DKP-3 demonstrated the enhanced antifungal activity, producing the largest inhibition zone against C. albicans (20.8 ± 0.9 mm), compared with amphotericin B (10 ± 0.89 mm) under the tested conditions (Table 2; Fig. S7B–C). These findings indicate the broad-spectrum antimicrobial potential of the cyclic dipeptides, with DKP-3 showing particularly strong antifungal activity.

To further quantify antimicrobial effects, minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) and minimum bactericidal/fungicidal concentrations (MBCs/MFCs) were determined (Tables 3 and 4). Among the bacterial pathogens, DKP-3 exhibited the strongest activity against P. mirabilis, with the lowest MIC (7.85 µg/mL) and MBC (19.41 µg/mL), indicating greater potency than ciprofloxacin under the same experimental conditions. DKP-1 showed notable activity against A. hydrophila (MIC: 20.69 µg/mL; MBC: 87.5 µg/mL), with values comparable to ciprofloxacin. DKP-3 and DKP-4 inhibited S. aureus, while DKP-3 also showed strong activity against P. aeruginosa. Overall, the DKPs were most effective against P. mirabilis among the tested bacterial strains.

Consistent with the antibacterial findings, DKP-3 demonstrated the most potent antifungal activity, with MIC values ranging from 10.62 to 19.28 µg/mL against all tested Candida species (Table 4). DKP-1, DKP-2, and DKP-4 exhibited moderate antifungal activity relative to DKP-3. Notably, the MIC values of DKP-3 were lower than those of amphotericin B under the tested conditions, indicating comparable or enhanced antifungal potency. We further evaluated the fungicidal activity of DKP-3 and amphotericin B against C. albicans and found that DKP-3 exhibited enhanced killing activity, with a marked reduction in CFU/mL at its MIC compared with amphotericin B (Fig. S8).

DKPs inhibit the yeast-to-hyphae transition in C. albicans

The yeast-to-hyphae transition is a key virulence determinant of C. albicans, enabling tissue invasion and disease progression11. To determine whether DKPs interfere with yeast-to-hyphae transition, C. albicans cells were cultured under hypha-inducing conditions in the presence of increasing concentrations of purified DKPs. Under these conditions, untreated cells formed elongated germ tubes (Fig. 2A, B). In contrast, DKP treatment resulted in a marked, dose-dependent suppression of hyphae formation (Fig. 2A, B). Quantitative analysis revealed that mean germ tube length was significantly reduced from 172.56 ± 35.28 in untreated cells to 43.13 ± 15.34 following DKP-3 treatment at 50 μg/mL (p < 0.001), with hyphal formation almost completely abolished at higher concentrations (Fig. 2A, B). Consistently, DKP-3 reduced hyphal formation by 60.67% at 12.5 μg/mL (p < 0.001; Fig. 2B). Microscopic examination further revealed pronounced morphological abnormalities in DKP-treated cells, including cellular shrinkage, membrane disruption and cell death (Fig. 2A). These findings correlate with MIC and MFC results, where DKP-3 exhibited the lowest inhibitory concentrations against C. albicans, highlighting its superior antifungal potency and potential as an antivirulence effect.

DKPs inhibit the enzymatic activity of C. albicans

To assess whether DKPs influence secreted hydrolytic enzyme activities, specifically PLs and SAPs in C. albicans, enzymatic assays were elevated under treatment with DKPs compared to untreated control cultures. Results showed that all four DKPs inhibited both PL and SAP activities in a dose-dependent manner. Notably, DKP-3 reduced phospholipase activity by 75.3% at a concentration of 150 μg/mL (p = 0.0001; Fig. 3A). Consistently, DKP-treated cells exhibited at least a 60% reduction in SAP activity (Fig. 3B; p = 0.0001). The inhibition of these enzymatic activities by DKPs likely contributes to the observed attenuation in pathogenic traits such as hyphal formation and virulence-associated phenotypes.

DKPs inhibit C. albicans biofilm formation

Biofilm formation is a major virulence attribute of C. albicans, contributing to persistent mucosal infections, device-associated colonization, and antifungal resistance40. To assess whether DKPs interfere with this pathogenic process, biofilm formation was quantified under standard biofilm-inducing conditions8,14 in the presence of increasing concentrations of purified DKPs. All four DKPs significantly inhibited biofilm development in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4A, B). At 150 μg/mL, DKP-3 and DKP-1 reduced biofilm formation by 79% and 83%, respectively, relative to untreated controls (p < 0.01) (Fig. 4B). The strong antibiofilm activity of DKP-3 is consistent with its pronounced suppression of hyphal morphogenesis and low MIC/MFC values, suggesting that disruption of morphogenetic switching contributes to impaired biofilm formation.

Molecular docking analysis

To evaluate the potential inhibitory effects of DKPs against key virulence-associated proteins of C. albicans, molecular docking simulations were performed against ERG1, ERG11, SAP2, SAP5, ALS3, HGC1, PLB5, HWP1, ALS1, and PLB1. Binding affinities and detailed amino acid interactions were summarized (Table 5, Table S3; Figs. 5–8). Representative 3D images are also provided (Figs. S9–S12) to visualize the binding poses within the protein pockets. Among the tested compounds, DKP-3 and DKP-4 consistently exhibited the highest binding affinities across multiple targets. For example, DKP-4 showed the strongest interaction with ERG1 ( − 8.5 kcal/mol), forming interactions with key active-site residues ARG337, ASP332, ILE202, and MET345 (Fig. 5D; Table S3). Consistent with MIC data showing DKP-3 as the most potent antifungal, it also exhibited high binding affinity across several targets, particularly ERG11 ( − 7.8 kcal/mol; TYR64, LYS90, PRO230, PHE380), SAP2 ( − 7.5 kcal/mol), and HGC1 ( − 7.1 kcal/mol; Figs. 5G, K, 7K). Both DKP-3 and DKP-4 also bound effectively to SAP5 ( − 7.4 and − 7.5 kcal/mol; Fig. 6C, D) and to adhesins ALS3 ( − 7.1 kcal/mol for both; Fig. 7G, H) and HGC1 ( − 7.1 and − 6.9 kcal/mol; Fig. 7K, L), suggesting potential interference with fungal adhesion and hyphal regulation pathways. In contrast, DKP-1 and DKP-2 exhibited moderate binding affinities, ranging from − 5.0 to − 6.5 kcal/mol across targets (Fig. 8), in agreement with their MIC results. For example, DKP-1 bound ERG11 at − 6.5 kcal/mol (Fig. 5E), while DKP-2 bound ERG1 at − 6.4 kcal/mol (Fig. 5A). Overall, the docking results support the experimental antifungal findings, highlighting DKP-3 and DKP-4 as promising multi-target inhibitors of C. albicans virulence pathways.

Discussion

Bacterial and fungal infections continue to pose a major threat to human health1,2. The rising prevalence of drug-resistant pathogens is associated with prolonged hospital stays, higher treatment costs, and increased morbidity and mortality1,7,12. Therefore, the discovery of novel antimicrobial compounds and alternative therapeutic strategies is urgently needed2,7,13,20. Probiotic-derived metabolites, including antimicrobial peptides and organic acids, have shown considerable potential in controlling microbial infections15,17,18,22. Among these, cyclic dipeptides (DKPs), naturally produced by various microorganisms, are recognized for their immunomodulatory and antimicrobial activities21,22,27. These small molecules have been reported to confer both health-promoting and disease-preventive effects, highlighting their potential as novel therapeutic agents8,27.

In this study, we isolated and identified four cyclic dipeptides (DKP-1-4) from A. veronii V03 and evaluated their antimicrobial and antivirulence activities. The DKPs exhibited broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity, particularly against P. mirabilis, with MIC values lower than the standard antibiotic ciprofloxacin3. DKP-1 and DKP-4 also showed significant inhibitory effects against S. aureus and P. aeruginosa. Notably, DKP-3 demonstrated potent antifungal activity against C. albicans and non-albicans species, more effective than amphotericin B (Table 4), consistent with previous reports that cyclo(L-Pro-L-Tyr) exhibits a lower MIC (32 µg/mL) than amphotericin B (50 µg/mL) against C. albicans39,41. These antimicrobial effects are likely influenced by subtle structural differences among the DKPs. For instance, cyclo(L-Pro-L-Phe) and cyclo(L-Phe-trans-4-OH-L-Pro) from L. plantarum inhibit C. albicans and other fungal pathogens26, whereas cyclo(L-Pro-D-Arg) from B. cereus selectively inhibits P. mirabilis without cytotoxicity to normal monkey kidney cells23. Similarly, cyclo(L-Pro-L-Leu) and cyclo(L-Pro-L-Tyr) suppress C. albicans growth39. Cyclic dipeptides such as cyclo(L-Tyr-L-Pro) and cyclo(L-Tyr-D-Pro) produced by Lactobacillus reuteri RC-14 have been reported to interfere with the staphylococcal quorum-sensing system (agr) and repress TSST-1 expression in S. aureus MN842. Cyclo(L-Tyr-L-Pro) and cyclo(L-Pro-L-Phe) also disrupt biofilm formation and quorum sensing in P. aeruginosa PA014,43, and cyclo(L-Pro-L-Tyr) demonstrates broad-spectrum antibacterial and antifungal activities44. Importantly, our DKPs exhibited no cytotoxic effects toward fibroblast cells or developmental toxicity in zebrafish embryos, even at high exposures up to 96 h38. When combined with their antivirulence (rather than cidal) activity at sub-MIC concentrations, these properties position the compounds as promising leads with a potentially high therapeutic index and a low risk of resistance development. Additionally, administration of live or dietary A. veronii V03 did not induce cytotoxic effects in Artemia salina nauplii or common carp (Cyprinus carpio)32,33.

C. albicans possesses multiple virulence factors, including morphogenesis, secretion of hydrolytic enzymes, adhesion, and biofilm formation, which contribute to its pathogenicity and resistance to antifungal agents7,9,11,45,46. Conventional antifungal therapies often impose selective pressure, leading to the emergence of drug resistance and disruption of the host microbiota7,11,47. In contrast, antivirulence strategies aim to attenuate pathogenic mechanisms without directly affecting microbial viability, thereby reducing the likelihood of resistance development7,8,12. To our knowledge, this study is the first to demonstrate the antivirulence potential of DKPs derived from A. veronii V03 against C. albicans. Specifically, DKPs significantly inhibited hyphal formation and the activity of secreted hydrolytic enzymes, including SAPs and phospholipases, suggesting that these compounds interfere with critical virulence determinants essential for C. albicans pathogenesis.

To further elucidate the molecular basis of these antivirulence effects, we conducted molecular docking simulations of DKP1–4 against major virulence-associated proteins in C. albicans. Among the tested compounds, DKP3 and DKP4 exhibited the highest binding affinities, particularly toward ERG1 and ERG11, enzymes involved in ergosterol biosynthesis8,13. Both DKP3 and DKP4 also showed strong binding to SAP2 and SAP5, enzymes that facilitate tissue invasion and immune evasion7,13,15,40. High-affinity interactions were additionally observed with ALS3 and HGC1, which are implicated in fungal adhesion and the yeast-to-hypha transition. These docking results are consistent with our in vitro observations, where DKP3 and DKP4 most effectively suppressed hyphal development and enzyme secretion. Consistent with our findings, previous studies reported that phytocompounds interact with antifungal proteins such as cytochrome P450 14α-sterol demethylase and N-myristoyl transferase, with 3,7-cyclodecadien-1-one exhibiting strong binding48. Docking studies of 1-ethoxycarbonyl-β-carboline demonstrated inhibition of Yak1, blocking C. albicans biofilm formation8,49, Similarly, cyclic dipeptides were also shown to interact with the quorum-sensing protein LasR, displaying comparable binding efficiency to the autoinducer 3OC12-HSL in P. aeruginosa PAO14,49. Sevadicin interacts with muramyl ligase E from Bacillus pumilus via hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonding, disrupting cell wall synthesis while maintaining a favorable biosafety profile50. Computational analyses have further identified oxidative stress-related proteins, such as HOG1 and TRX1, as potential drug targets in C. albicans, highlighting novel therapeutic opportunities51.

Beyond secreted enzymes and morphogenesis, another critical therapeutic challenge in fungal pathogenesis is biofilm formation. Biofilms contribute to approximately 80% of all microbial infections in clinical settings and are notoriously difficult to treat due to their inherent resistance to antifungal agents11,52. Current strategies, such as coating catheters with antifungal compounds, have shown limited success due to drug leaching and suboptimal concentrations2,9,40,53. In our study, DKPs effectively inhibited C. albicans biofilm formation, highlighting their potential to target multiple virulence pathways. These results were consistent with previous studies showing that peptides such as cecropin-4 derivatives and 1-acetyl-β-carboline from Lactobacillus, and tyrocidines to impair C. albicans biofilms and virulence by targeting pathways including hyphal development, membrane integrity, and cell wall synthesis8,54,55. Additionally, metchnikowin, another antifungal peptide, exerts its effect by targeting β-(1,3)-glucanosyltransferase, thereby impairing cell wall integrity in C. albicans56

In conclusion, four cyclic dipeptides (DKP1–4) were isolated and structurally characterized from A. veronii V03. These compounds demonstrated antimicrobial activity against both bacterial and fungal pathogens. Notably, all DKPs inhibited key virulence traits of C. albicans, including hyphal formation, hydrolytic enzyme secretion, and biofilm formation. Molecular docking analysis indicated that DKP3 and DKP4 bind with relatively high affinity to proteins associated with virulence. Overall, these findings suggest that DKPs may function as antivirulence agents and highlight the potential of targeting fungal virulence mechanisms to reduce pathogenicity.

Study limitations

We acknowledge limitations in the current study. Testing additional clinical bacterial and fungal isolates is needed to enhance the translational relevance. Fluorescence-based competitive binding assays or other biochemical validations would provide direct confirmation of protein–ligand interactions. Additionally, structure–activity relationship (SAR) comparisons and redocking analyses could further validate the computational docking observations. These limitations provide clear directions for future mechanistic and in vivo studies, including exploration of potential synergy with conventional antifungal treatments.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strain and fermentation

The greenish-yellow pigmented probiotic A. veronii strain V03 was previously identified by 16S rRNA gene sequencing and shown to possess desirable probiotic properties32. For the isolation and structural characterization of bioactive compounds, strain V03 was grown in Luria–Bertani (LB) broth (HiMedia, M1245) as described earlier32,33. Briefly, a 50 mL overnight culture was inoculated into 400 mL of sterile LB medium and incubated on a rotary shaker at 120 rpm at 30 °C for 72 h. Cultures were centrifuged at 6500 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the cell-free supernatant was filtered (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and stored at − 4 °C for subsequent metabolite extraction.

Extraction, purification, and structural characterization of bioactive compounds

Cell-free culture supernatants were extracted with an equal volume of ethyl acetate (1:1, v/v), and the organic phase was concentrated using a rotary evaporator (IKA-RV10, Switzerland)38. The resulting dried extract (2.5 g obtained from 15 L of culture) was subjected to silica gel column chromatography (30 cm × 2 cm, 100–200 mesh; HiMedia). Fractions were eluted using a linear gradient of chloroform:methanol (v/v): 98:2, 95:5, 92:8, 85:15, 75:25, 70:30, 50:50, followed by 100% methanol. Fractions were pooled based on TLC profiles (Silica gel 60 F254 plates, Merck) into six major fractions (F1–F6). Bioactive fractions (F2: 265 mg; pooled F3–F4: 320 mg; F5: 631 mg; F6: 533 mg) were subjected to secondary purification using silica gel (200–400 mesh) or C18 column chromatography, employing hexane:ethyl acetate (v/v) or chloroform:methanol gradients. White crystalline cyclic dipeptides (DKPs) were obtained and structurally characterized as cyclo(l-Pro-l-Leu) (DKP-1), cyclo(l-Pro- l-Val) (DKP-2), cyclo(d-Pro-l-Phe) (DKP-3), and cyclo(l-Pro-d-Tyr) (DKP-4). Structural elucidation of the purified DKPs was performed using nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy. 1H and 13C NMR spectra were recorded in CDCl₃ with 0.1% tetramethylsilane (TMS) as the internal standard, using a Bruker DRX-500 MHz spectrometer (Rheinstetten, Germany) equipped with a cryogenically cooled Prodigy broadband inverse detection probe. Chemical shifts (δ) are reported in parts per million (ppm), and coupling constants (J) are expressed in Hertz (Hz). High-resolution mass spectra (HRMS) were acquired using an Orbitrap LC–MS system (Thermo Scientific) with an XBridge BEH C18 column (Waters, Milford, MA). Purity was determined by peak area normalization according to the formula: Purity (%) = Area of DKP peak/Total area of all detected peaks × 100. Optical rotations were measured on a Jasco P-2000 polarimeter at λ = 589 nm38.

Microbial strains and growth conditions

Microbial strains, Bacillus cereus (MTCC1305), Bacillus subtilis (MTCC2756), Staphylococcus epidermidis (MTCC435), Staphylococcus simulans (MTCC3610), Mycobacterium smegmatis (MTCC993), Escherichia coli (MTCC2622), Klebsiella pneumoniae (MTCC109), Proteus mirabilis (MTCC425), Salmonella typhi (MTCC3216), Candida albicans (MTCC277), Candida tropicalis (MTCC184), Candida glabrata (MTCC3019), and Candida parapsilosis (MTCC998) were obtained from the Microbial Type Culture Collection (MTCC, Chandigarh, India). Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC29213), Aeromonas hydrophila (ATCC7966) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (ATCC27853) were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection Centre (ATCC, Rockville, MD, USA). Bacterial strains were grown in Mueller–Hinton broth (MHB), and fungal strains in Sabouraud dextrose broth (SDB) at 30 °C for 18 h. Cultures were stored in 50% glycerol at –80 °C.

Agar well diffusion assay

The antimicrobial activity of crude organic extracts (ethyl acetate) was evaluated using the agar well diffusion method57. Pathogens were grown in MH or SD broth, adjusted to 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL, and uniformly swabbed onto the appropriate agar plates (MHA or SDA). Wells of 6 mm diameter were punched into the agar, and 50 µL of each extract (1 mg/mL) was added. Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was used as a vehicle control. Plates were incubated at 30 °C for 24 h, and inhibition zones were measured in millimeters.

Disk diffusion assay

Purified DKPs were tested against selected bacterial (P. mirabilis, S. aureus, A. hydrophila, P. aeruginosa) and fungal (C. albicans, C. tropicalis, C. glabrata, C. parapsilosis) strains using the disc diffusion method52,58. Log-phase cultures (1 × 10⁶ cells/mL) were plated on MHA or SDA. Sterile discs (6 mm) containing 25 µg of each DKP in DMSO were placed onto the plates. Amphotericin B (20 µg per disk, for fungi) and ciprofloxacin (20 µg per disk, for bacteria) were included as standard positive controls in parallel experiments to provide a reference for antimicrobial activity. Plates were incubated at 30 °C for 24 h, and zones of inhibition were measured in millimeters.

Assessment of MIC, MBC and MFC

MIC, MBC, and MFC of DKPs were determined as described previously23,52,58. Selected bacterial and fungal pathogens (2 × 105 cells/well) were seeded into 96-well plates and treated with DKPs at concentrations ranging from 6.25 to 400 µg/mL. DMSO at a final concentration of 0.25% served as a negative control. The selected DMSO concentration (0.25%) is within the range commonly reported to have negligible effects on microbial growth and cell viability. Amphotericin B (for fungi) and Ciprofloxacin (for bacteria) were included as standard positive controls in parallel experiments to provide a reference for antimicrobial activity. Plates were incubated at 30 °C for 24 h, after which alamarBlue reagent (40 µL/well) was added, and fluorescence was measured. For bacteria, the alamarBlue assay provides a sensitive and rapid measure of metabolic activity, complementing traditional OD₆₀₀ measurements. MIC was defined as the lowest concentration resulting in ≥ 50% growth inhibition. MBC and MFC values were determined by subculturing from wells onto agar plates to confirm the absence of viable bacterial or fungal cells, respectively.

Effects of DKPs on the yeast-hyphae transition of C. albicans

Inhibition of yeast-to-hypha transition in C. albicans was assessed as previously described59. C. albicans (2 × 10⁶ cells/mL) was treated with or without DKPs (12.5–150 µg/mL) and incubated in hyphae-inducing NYP medium (10 mM N-acetylglucosamine, 3.35 g/L yeast nitrogen base, 10 mM proline, 4.5 g/L NaCl, pH 5.4) for 3 h at 30 °C. Morphological changes were visualized using an Olympus CX21 light microscope (20 × magnification) with an HDCE-X3 camera. Hyphal lengths were quantified using ImageJ.

Effects of DKPs on secreted hydrolase of C. albicans

Effects of DKPs on C. albicans phospholipase (PL) and secreted aspartyl proteinase (SAP) activity were assessed using standard methods46,60,61. Cells (2 × 10⁶ cells/mL) were pre-treated with DKPs (12.5–150 µg/mL) for 1 h, washed, and incubated in PL-inducing medium (NaCl 57.3 g/L, peptone 10 g/L, glucose 30 g/L, CaCl₂ 0.55 g/L, egg yolk 8%) or SAP-inducing medium (yeast carbon base 23.4 g/L, yeast extract 2 g/L, BSA 4 g/L, pH 5.0) at 30 °C for 72 h. Enzyme activity was calculated as: % Activity = [(test/control) × 100].

Effects of DKPs on C. albicans biofilm formation

Inhibition of C. albicans biofilm formation was measured as previously described14,62. C. albicans (5 × 105 cells/well) was cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with MOPS in the presence or absence of DKPs (12.5–150 µg/mL) in 96-well plates. After 24 h at 37 °C, non-adherent cells were removed, and wells were washed with PBS. Biofilms were stained with crystal violet, and absorbance was measured to quantify biofilm biomass8,14.

Molecular docking analysis

To determine the potential inhibitory mechanism of DKPs on the yeast-to-hyphae transition and virulence of C. albicans, molecular docking studies were performed to evaluate interactions between selected DKPs and key virulence-associated proteins. The selected targets included SAP2, SAP5, PLB1, PLB5, ERG1, ERG11, ALS1, ALS3, HGC1, and HWP1. High-resolution crystal structures of SAP2 (PDB ID: 1EAG), SAP5 (2QZX), ERG11 (5TZ1), and ALS3 (4LEB) were obtained from the Protein Data Bank. For proteins lacking crystal structures, homology models were generated using the SWISS-MODEL server, guided by AlphaFold DB predictions. The quality of the modeled structures was validated using standard structural assessment tools, including Ramachandran plot analysis, ERRAT, and Verify3D, confirming that the models were within acceptable limits for docking studies. DKP ligand 3D structures (Fig. 1) were retrieved from PubChem and energy-minimized using the Universal Force Field via Open Babel in PyRx (v1.2). Ligand flexibility was ensured by defining all rotatable bonds. Protein and ligand preparations followed standard AutoDock protocols63. Active site pockets were predicted using the CASTp server64, and docking grids were defined to cover the entire predicted binding pockets. Grid box dimensions (x, y, z; Å) were adjusted individually for each protein to ensure complete coverage of the ligand-binding regions. Docking simulations were performed using AutoDock Vina (v1.2.5) with exhaustiveness ≥ 8, while other parameters were kept at default settings63. Protein–ligand complexes were analyzed using Discovery Studio Visualizer (v2.5) to identify interactions such as hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic contacts, π–π stacking, and electrostatic interactions that may contribute to inhibition of virulence functions. Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism statistical software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparison test. Results were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from four independent biological replicates.
