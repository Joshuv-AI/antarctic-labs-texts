A review of practices and policies of international humanitarian organizations in conflict-affected settings: are we ensuring sustainability of surgical care?
Authors: Davy Lau, Irena Zivkovic, Apanuba Puhama, Agnes Zanotto Manoel, Naisan Garraway, Shahrzad Joharifard, Emilie Joos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInternational humanitarian organizations providing surgical care in conflict-affected settings have an ethical obligation to adopt practices that promote sustainable surgical services. Our objective was to evaluate the prevalence of sustainable surgical practices and policies implemented by humanitarian organizations in conflict-afflicted settings, as well as to document instances where surgical services were disrupted or discontinued due to the outbreak of conflict.MethodsA review of primary literature was conducted using Medline. Articles were included if they described sustainable surgical practices or policies in conflict-affected settings. Sustainability was defined according to a previously published modified Delphi consensus framework, which outlined key pillars of sustainability in global surgery partnerships. A grey literature review was also performed to identify sustainable policies published by international humanitarian organizations on their online platforms.ResultsOf 1,057 articles screened, 29 articles met inclusion criteria, describing 54 surgical programs implemented by 17 international humanitarian organizations —most commonly Médecins Sans Frontières (n = 27) and International Committee of the Red Cross (n = 5). These programs were implemented in 36 conflicts between 1946 and 2023, most commonly in Sub-Saharan Africa (n = 20), the Middle East (n = 11), and Southeast Asia (n = 9). The following six sustainability pillars were explicitly described: context-relevant education (94.4%), multidisciplinary involvement (74.1%), outcome measurement (72.2%), stakeholder engagement (66.7%), handover to local stakeholders (18.5%), and multisource funding (13.0%). Escalating violence led to permanent closure of 13.0% of programs and led to service interruptions in 9.3%. The mean duration of programs was 9.5 ± 9.7 years. Publicly available policies were identified for six organizations (35.3%), and all but one organization captured every aforementioned pillar of sustainability.ConclusionsInternational humanitarian organizations have increasingly adopted sustainable practices and policies when delivering surgical care in conflict-affected settings—most notably in the area of context-relevant training. We encourage global actors to continue prioritizing this domain, especially in fragile states where service cessation or interruptions can occur at any time, necessitating sustainable surgical care delivered by trained local physicians. This review highlights exemplary practices documented in the literature and offers insights into how global surgery partners can build on existing efforts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13031-026-00805-1.Keywords: Health Policy; Relief Work; Armed Conflicts; Surgical Procedures, Operative; Delivery of Health Care; Health Care Quality, Access, and Evaluation; Program Evaluation; Sustainable Development

Background

The advancement of peaceful, conflict-free societies is essential to achieving sustainable development, as emphasized in the 2030 United Nations Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 16: Peace, Justice and Strong Institutions [1]. The recent rise in global conflicts has led to widespread displacement and disruptions in access to essential services [2]. Surgical care is a critical component of health services in conflict-affected populations. Even without considering injuries caused by violence and displacement, approximately 30% of the global disease burden is treatable with surgery, indicating a substantial baseline need for surgical services [3]. Conflict imposes an additional burden on already fragile healthcare systems, often prompting international humanitarian organizations to step in and fill these gaps.

Volatile security environments hinder humanitarian organizations from establishing, upgrading, and maintaining the physical and human resources necessary to sustain surgical care. For instance, between 2020 and 2024, Médecins Sans Frontières (MSF) had to withdraw or temporarily suspend activities in parts of Sudan, Haiti, and the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) due to increasing security threats, including attacks on patients, staff, and medical infrastructure [4–6]. Humanitarian surgical interventions must therefore be designed with sustainability in mind, especially given the potential for recurrent instability in conflict-affected regions. Indeed, international humanitarian organizations must collaborate with regional governing bodies and peacekeeping missions not only to deliver care but also to build surgical infrastructure that endures beyond their presence [7].

There is a lack of published data on sustainable practices in international humanitarian surgical programs. One exception was a 2024 scoping review by Pfeiffer-Mundt et al. (2024), which analyzed strategies used by humanitarian organizations to build surgical capacity in conflict and post-conflict settings using a simplified World Health Organization definition of capacity-building [8]. More recently, Binda et al. (2024) developed a broader framework to evaluate sustainability in global surgery [9]. This modified Delphi consensus included fifty experts from 34 countries, with a substantial proportion coming from fragile and conflict-affected regions—44% from lower-income and lower-middle income countries, including 22% from Africa and 8% from the Middle East, with a median global surgery experience of 9.5 (range 1–45) years. This framework is widely used in the Canadian academic context, notably informing all global surgery partnerships at the University of British Columbia and has been cited by several studies over the past 3 years [10–12]. Given the rigorous nature of that Delphi consensus, we used the pillars of sustainability outlined in that framework in this current study, with the aim of comparing and contrasting sustainable practices across various organizations and regions, and identifying gaps in the implementation of sustainability principles in humanitarian surgical care.

This study sought to identify international humanitarian organizations operating in conflict-affected settings, as well as to evaluate the sustainability of their surgical services based on published field experiences and formal organizational policies. This study included two separate reviews. First, we conducted a review of primary literature to identify concrete examples of international organizations demonstrating sustainable surgical practices in conflict and post-conflict settings, and a thematic analysis was performed on these examples. A secondary objective was to identify instances where service delivery by organizations was disrupted due to heightened security risks. Second, we conducted a review of grey literature, specifically reviewing international humanitarian organizations’ websites to identify formally published policies that demonstrate sustainability, followed by a thematic analysis of the documented policies. The purpose of this grey literature review was to examine if these organizations have formal written policies that demonstrate an intent to provide sustainable surgical care for all their projects, irrespective of their ability to implement those policies in actual practice.

Methods

With regards to definitions, international “humanitarian organizations” included non-governmental organizations (NGOs), governmental bodies, nonprofits, and faith-based entities, operating in areas of the world outside of where the organization itself is based. The scope of surgical care was defined as encompassing surgical, obstetric, and anesthesia services. Conflict was defined in this study as per The International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC), which categorizes conflict into three types: international armed conflict, non-international armed conflict, and other forms of violence, such as civil unrest, riots, gang violence, crime, and terrorism [13].

Sustainability was defined as the ongoing support provided by international humanitarian organizations, either through long-term presence or by enabling communities to transition to equivalent services following the organizations’ departure. Our thematic analysis was based on the sustainability framework for global surgery partnerships developed by Binda et al. (2024), which consists of six pillars: stakeholder engagement, multidisciplinary collaboration, context-relevant education and training, multisource funding, outcome measurement, and bilateral authorship [1]. Each pillar comprises a checklist of criteria for application to specific programs. In order to capture as many examples of sustainability as possible, programs were included if they met at least one checklist item within any of the six pillars. In this current study, the pillar of bilateral authorship was replaced with “handover to local stakeholders”: this was done because, while bilateral authorship is important in an academic context, it has no bearing on the day-to-day operations of a surgical program in a conflict-affected setting. Just as a manuscript is the end-product of an academic endeavour, the completed end-product of a humanitarian surgical endeavour is ultimately the handover of surgical care to local healthcare workers and government, and handing over to those partners can best facilitate the long-term sustainability of surgical care in that region. In addition to capturing examples of these six pillars, our secondary objective was to identify examples where service delivery was interrupted due to escalating violence: This objective was selected to support the additional pillar of “handover to local stakeholders”, as any abrupt interruptions in service due to eruption in violence could lead to absence of handover or poor handover of surgical care to local healthcare workers.

Part 1: Primary literature review

This aspect of the review followed the rapid review methodology described by Tricco et al. (2015) and was conducted using the MEDLINE database [14]. This methodology was selected due to the urgency for data surrounding surgical care provision in conflict-affected zones, given the ongoing eruption of conflicts globally. It was felt that a rapid review would better facilitate improved understanding of gaps in sustainability in surgical programs, by being broad in its inclusion to capture as many examples of sustainability as possible, while a more formally structured scoping or systematic review would have been better served for gathering data on a more specific and targeted research question. As well, a rapid review could be more immediately applied to shape currently existing programs in the face of ongoing conflict in the world. The MEDLINE database was chosen as the single database for the rapid review methodology, given that it is known to encompass a substantial proportion of the general health literature.

The search dates were January 1, 1946-December 31, 2023: our intent was to capture examples of sustainability in surgical programs after World War II, a major global conflict, after which the Geneva conventions and additional protocols were implemented, protecting the vulnerable during war. Programs were included if they operated in areas of international armed conflict, non-international armed conflict, “other violence” (as defined by the ICRC), or in “post-conflict zones”—defined as areas recovering from one of the above conflict types [13]. Articles not meeting these criteria or not available in English were excluded.

Each article was screened initially by abstract and title review, and articles that passed the initial screen went through full-text review prior to final inclusion. If an article described multiple organizations or programs, each was analyzed separately. Thematic analysis was conducted using the six aforementioned sustainability pillars [9]. With regards to collecting data on the duration of each surgical program, if the dates were unclear from the included article, additional documents such as reports and press releases were reviewed or the organization was contacted for clarification.

Part 2: Policy review

Organizations identified through the primary literature review were included in the policy analysis. A grey literature search was conducted, focusing on policy documents and reports published online by these organizations outlining formal policies or practices related to sustainable surgical care. Reports by third parties were excluded. Thematic analysis was again guided by the six pillars of sustainability [9].

Results

Part 1: Primary literature review

The initial search strategy yielded 1,057 articles. Following both abstract and full-text review, 29 articles were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). These articles described a total of 54 programs (Appendix A) from 17 international humanitarian organizations, with Médecins Sans Frontières (MSF) (n = 27, 50%) and the International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC) (n = 5, 9%) most commonly represented [15–43].

Search results for review of primary literature

In total, 36 conflicts were documented, ranging from World War II to the Ukraine war (1946–2023). Most surgical programs were started between 2000 and 2010 (Fig. 2). The most commonly cited regions were Sub-Saharan Africa (n = 20), the Middle East (n = 11), Southeast Asia (n = 9), and West Africa (n = 5) (Fig. 3). Types of conflict included non-international armed conflict (n = 40), international armed conflict (n = 21), other forms of violence (n = 3), and post-conflict settings (n = 8). The majority of programs (n = 40, 74%) delivered surgical care within conflict zones, rather than in neighboring or external locations. The mean program duration was 9.5 years (SD 9.7), with a median of 8.0 years (IQR 2.0–15.0) [15–43]. The longest running programs were the ICRC programs in Peshawar, Pakistan (1981–present), Kabul, Afghanistan (1987–present), and Quetta, Pakistan (1987–2012) [35–37, 44, 45]. Surgical care was provided to a broad population including civilians (such as refugees), combatants, war-wounded, and non-war-wounded patients.

Sustainability Pillar Thematic Analysis

With respect to the thematic analysis of sustainability, all six pillars of sustainability were represented among the programs reviewed (Table 1). The most commonly observed pillar was context-relevant education and training, identified in 94% of programs [18–21, 23–37, 40–43]. One key example of this was task-shifting—training local providers to deliver care outside their usual scope of practice. In these cases, non-specialist local healthcare providers, including allied health professionals, were trained to deliver surgical, anesthetic, and obstetric services [19–22, 26, 34]. For instance, Smile Train trains physicians in low-resource countries to perform cleft lip and palate surgeries through video instruction, allowing local providers to carry out the surgeries rather than relying on international teams [41]. In addition, 39% of programs explicitly emphasized tailoring training to local contexts by developing educational materials adapted to the infrastructure and resources available. These programs also ensured that skill development was aligned with the experience level of local staff [19, 20, 22–24, 27, 28, 32, 33, 38–40, 42, 43].

The next most commonly represented pillar was multidisciplinary involvement, defined as mention of inter- or intradisciplinary education, relationship building, and/or knowledge translation plus inclusion of one surgeon, anesthesia provider, and allied health professional in post-surgical care. Nearly three-quarters of programs (n = 40, 74%) demonstrated multidisciplinary involvement by the above definition [15–17, 19–26, 30–33, 35–40, 42, 43].

Outcome measurement (n = 39, 72%) was also commonly represented, with most programs reporting data on short-term outcomes such as perioperative morbidity and mortality [15–22, 25, 29–37, 40, 42]. Only one program—the MSF reconstructive surgery initiative in Amman, Jordan—reported long-term outcomes, specifically rates of non-union and the functional impact of lower limb injuries in war-wounded civilians [17].

Stakeholder engagement was fulfilled in two-thirds of programs (n = 36, 67%), typically through consultation and collaboration with community leaders, local organizations, and government representatives [15–21, 25, 27, 30–35, 38–40, 42, 43]. A quarter (n = 14, 26%) of the programs described a needs assessment that involved engaging local stakeholders [23, 24, 27, 32].

A smaller subset of studies demonstrated adoption of the final two sustainability pillars: handover to local stakeholders (n = 10, 19%) [15, 21-24, 30, 34, 40, 41]and multisource funding (n = 7, 13%) [32, 40, 46]. With respect to handover, ten programs mentioned transitioning services to local stakeholders. In six instances, operational responsibility was transferred to local governments, who continued service delivery [15, 21, 22]. For example, the MSF-supported hospital in Ar Ramtha, Jordan—originally established to serve Syrian refugees—was transferred to the Jordanian Ministry of Health in 2022 following nine years of MSF involvement and infrastructure development [15, 47]. In three other cases, local medical teams assumed control of surgical services. For instance, the provincial hospital in Kompong Chan, Cambodia, supported by MSF during the 1990 civil war, was handed over to local surgeons in 1991, and no expatriate surgeons have provided care there since then [34].

Multisource funding was defined as having contributions from both governmental and non-governmental entities, as well as from both high- and low-resource settings [1]. Only seven programs fully met this criterion, with the primary limiting factor being the inclusion of funding from low-income countries. Funding sources in these cases included national Ministries of Health (from both high- and low-income countries), universities, philanthropic donations, and subsidized user fees paid by local residents [35].

The adoption of sustainability pillars varied across geographic regions (Table 2). Context-relevant education and training was prevalent in Southeast Asia, Sub-Saharan Africa, and West Africa (90–100%) but was less common in the Middle East (73%). Notably, while 74% of all programs were delivered within conflict zones, the majority of Middle East-based programs (8/11, 73%) were implemented outside the conflict area [15–17, 31–33, 35–39, 43]. For example, MSF delivered surgical care to Syrian refugees in Pakistan during the Syrian Civil War [15–17]. As well, Operation Smile shifted its operations at the last minute from Iraq to Amman, Jordan, to serve pediatric patients affected by the Iraq War due to safety concerns [31]. Outcome measurement and stakeholder engagement were reported at similar frequencies across the four regions.

In West Africa, 40% (2/5) programs demonstrated a form of multisource funding [35]. For example, in Liberia and Sierra Leone, organizations such as Marie Stopes International (MSI) and the International Rescue Committee (IRC) implemented subsidized fee systems whereby refugees paid nominal fees, supplemented by philanthropic and governmental funding [32]. One example of multisource funding outside of Africa was the Jungle University program, developed by the Trauma Care Foundation [40, 46]. This initiative trained laypersons in mine-affected regions to deliver first aid, trauma care, and emergency surgical and anesthesia services. The program was funded by the Norwegian Ministry of Foreign Affairs, local authorities, and NGOs in Cambodia, Iraq, Burma, and Angola [46].

The adoption of sustainability pillars varied considerably when stratified by organization (Table 2). Both the ICRC and MSF demonstrated context-relevant education and training (80% vs. 93%), multidisciplinary involvement (80% vs. 81%), outcome measurement (100% vs. 63%), and stakeholder engagement (40% vs. 56%). Handover to local stakeholders was only mentioned in MSF programs (26%). One example of successful handover is the 2006–2011 MSF program at Lubutu General District Hospital in the DRC [26, 48, 49]. During this period, MSF upgraded the facility’s infrastructure with essential equipment, developed a referral network system, and implemented a cost-recovery funding model. This significant investment transformed the hospital into a fully operational referral center, which was handed over to the Ministry of Health after five years [49]. Neither organization reported examples of multisource funding.

Service interruptions due to violence

Five programs (9%) reported temporary suspension of surgical services due to security threats. Three of these were MSF programs, located in Mweso (DRC), Leer (South Sudan), and Cité Soleil (Haiti) [32]. A non-MSF example was the Barsky Unit, a surgical center in Saigon that provided pediatric plastic surgical care during the Vietnam War [23, 24]. The center experienced a three-year service disruption after Saigon was taken by North Vietnamese forces and both local and international staff fled. It was later reclaimed by Vietnamese surgeons in 1978 during the post-conflict period [24].

Seven programs (13%) reported permanent cessation of humanitarian activities. These included MSF operations in Kimbi (DRC), Baraka (DRC), Nasir (South Sudan), Boguila (CAR), Kismayo (Somalia), and Jabal Al-Akrad (Syria), as well as the ICRC program in Quetta (Pakistan) [21, 25, 36, 37]. The MSF hospital in Jabal Al-Akrad, in Northwestern Syria, which was housed on a chicken farm with the operating room set up in a tent, was permanently closed in 2014 following the kidnapping of MSF staff by ISIS militants [25].

Part 2: Policy review

While the primary literature review identified 54 surgical care programs with sustainable practices, none of the included studies explicitly described formal policies outlining the organizations’ approaches to sustainability. However, a subsequent grey literature review revealed that 6 out of 17 of these organizations (35.3%) had publicly available and accessible information about their policies, listed in Table 3. These organizations were: MSF, ICRC, Emergency, International Medical Corps (IMC), Operation Smile, and the World Health Organization (WHO).

Discussion

Sustainability pillars across all programs

Our primary literature review demonstrates that sustainable practices in humanitarian surgical services have been implemented across a wide range of geopolitical contexts and time periods. Over a span of 78 years, we identified 54 surgical programs delivered by 17 different organizations in conflict and post-conflict settings that exhibited one or more pillars of sustainability. Notably, more than 90% of these programs demonstrated the capacity to implement context-relevant education and training—a remarkable finding given the instability typically associated with conflict zones. The Lancet Commission identified the surgical workforce as the key determinant of access to surgical care [62]. Moreover, the Surgeon/Anesthesiologist/Obstetrician (SAO) workforce density has been adopted by the World Bank as a key development metric. Accordingly, strengthening the surgical workforce is directly linked to broader developmental progress. This finding was highlighted by Pfeiffer-Mundt et al. (2024) in their recent scoping review on surgical care in conflict settings; indeed, they describe some form of training (short term/long term/postgraduate) in 14 out of the 15 organizations they studied [8].

Task sharing and shifting (TSS) has emerged as a well-established method for increasing surgical capacity, particularly in low-resource or remote environments where access to specialist care is limited [63, 64]. TSS is quicker, more cost-effective and increases retention of trained providers. This literature review found that 85% of programs employed task shifting as a strategy to maintain continuity of services, underscoring its importance in supporting both sustainability and local healthcare system strengthening. An example of an ongoing TSS initiative within the humanitarian sphere is the Vital Surgery Training Program, built in collaboration with MSF to train non-surgeon physicians in conflict-affected settings in essential surgical skills. The evaluation of the first two cohorts in South Sudan shows that this type of capacity-building is feasible in volatile contexts [65, 66].

In addition to strengthening the healthcare workforce, humanitarian organizations must meet two key minimum standards, both clearly outlined in the Sphere handbook [67]. First, a thorough needs assessment is essential to the success of the program. This was only described in 26% of cases in our literature review, which is lower than the 37.5% reported by Pfeiffer-Mundt [8]. Our findings may be explained by the inclusion of reports prior to 1990, before the first Humanitarian Charter and Minimum Standards in Disaster Response was published [68]. Second, performance indicators must be measured and reviewed regularly. Organizations must collect data metrics to ensure quality of their interventions, even in conflict settings. Our review revealed that 72% of programs reported outcome measurements; however, these indicators were frequently very basic, comprising just documentation of cases and tracking of perioperative morbidity and mortality, with only 1 program (1.9%) documenting long-term outcomes such as functional status. Given the prevalence and upward trend of global conflict, measuring and reporting key outcomes is crucial to the proper deployment of quality surgical programs in conflict zones.

Unsurprisingly, handover of surgical activities to local healthcare teams remains a challenge, with only ten programs (18.5%) describing a successful handover. From our review, it was clear that certain conditions must be met: active engagement with government actors (such as Ministries of Health) and a cadre of trained local medical personnel capable of continuing the surgical care independently. The relatively low frequency of handover in our review may reflect the instability and fragility of conflict-affected regions, where these prerequisites are difficult to achieve. For example, none of the five ICRC surgical programs included in this review—located in Afghanistan, Pakistan, Mali, Nigeria and DRC—reported a transition to local authorities. This may be explained by the fact that many of these regions remain too unstable to facilitate handover; indeed, two of these countries are considered states of extreme fragility, and the other three are classified as states of high fragility [69].

The rise of conflict and states of fragility

Our review aimed to shed light on humanitarian response amidst an alarming global escalation in conflict, which is disproportionally affecting fragile states. According to the Peace Research Institute Oslo, 2023 marked the most violent year since 1946, with more than 50 state-based conflicts occurring annually over the past decade, peaking at 59 in 2023 [2]. These conflicts are disproportionately concentrated in regions characterized by high or extreme fragility. The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) defines fragility as a state’s exposure to risk and its limited capacity to absorb and recover from shocks, across dimensions including economic, political, security, societal, environmental, and human. As of their 2025 report, there were 43 high fragility contexts and 18 extreme fragility contexts [69]. In 2023, 47 of the 59 international and non-international conflicts (80%) occurred in these fragile settings [2, 69].

Our study identified humanitarian surgical programs operating in 13 of the 18 extremely fragile contexts. This underscores the urgent need to continue developing sustainable surgical care, systems, and infrastructure in such regions, along the defined six pillars of sustainability. Specifically, there is a critical need for thorough needs assessments, context-relevant training, and improved transparency in outcome measurements by international humanitarian organizations when implementing surgical services in conflict settings. With concerted effort, sustainability is possible, as demonstrated by the aforementioned MSF program at Lubutu General District Hospital in the DRC. Following infrastructure upgrades, the development of a referral network, and the implementation of a cost-recovery funding model, MSF transformed the hospital into a fully operational referral center, which was handed over to the local Ministry of Health after just five years [49].

Risks to the humanitarian community

The risk of violence against both civilians seeking care and humanitarian workers delivering medical and surgical services remains a considerable challenge in conflict-affected regions. While humanitarian workers are afforded legal protections under the Geneva Conventions and associated protocols, these protections are not always upheld in practice. Over the past two decades, healthcare-focused humanitarian staff have experienced a nine-fold increase in security incidents, compared to a five-fold increase for workers in non-healthcare settings [70]. This concern is reflected in the studies included in our review: 24% of programs were forced to either temporarily suspend or permanently discontinue operations due to safety concerns for healthcare personnel [21, 23, 24, 24–26, 26–29, 35–40]. These findings align with an alarming global trend showing a rise in security incidents targeting humanitarian aid workers [71]. The global community must act to ensure that governments, armed groups and other actors are held accountable to uphold the International Humanitarian Law.

Limitations

One major limitation of this study is that most case examples were drawn from published primary literature and research articles, while the grey literature review focused on evaluating sustainability practices and policies without detailed case analyses. Consequently, organizations that have a research body —such as Epicentre within MSF—may be overrepresented. In fact, this review was limited to capturing the experiences of large, international NGOs, as smaller, local NGOs would likely have lower representation in the academic literature and on public-facing websites. Moreover, organizational transparency presents an additional challenge: published case studies may only represent a small fraction of the surgical programs implemented by these organizations. Similarly, some formal policy and operational procedure documents may exist internally but are not publicly accessible, which may explain the limited availability of such documents found in our review.

Another important limitation is the potential underreporting of sustainability-related activities in the included articles. Many studies prioritized outcomes reporting over detailed descriptions of program operations or organizational planning. For example, some publications focused on clinical management—such as wartime colon injuries in Cambodia or arterial repairs in Pakistan—without discussing the broader structure or sustainability of the surgical programs involved [34, 35]. This selective focus may result in an incomplete depiction of sustainability practices and underestimate the true prevalence of sustainable practices in these programs. At the same time, this selective focus theoretically could have resulted in including programs that described only a single pillar of sustainability: This would thus relatively underestimate the prevalence of the remaining pillars, by inflating the total number of programs included without the programs in question being well-described enough to fulfill more pillars. This was a limitation that we accepted in the methodology, given that the aim of the current study was to seek out any example of sustainable practices, and identify in which pillars those sustainable practices fell under. Therefore, a surgical program that fulfilled even just 1 pillar of sustainability would provide valuable data and be included, as it can help illustrate which pillars across the literature tend to be underrepresented. On a more philosophical note, sustainability exists on a continuum, whereas this current study (and the modified Delphi consensus framework on which this study is based) measures sustainability as a binary outcome: present or absent [9]. For example, the following quote, “In the Democratic Republic of Congo, we have collaborated successfully for many years with the Ministry of Health in Mweso” would be classified in this study as sufficiently fulfilling the pillar of Stakeholder Engagement, despite the actual merits and strength of this collaboration not being interrogated [21]. The reality is that there is no minimum threshold of sustainability, nor has any definition of sustainability in humanitarian settings ever been described in the literature. This gap is ultimately what inspired both the modified Delphi consensus framework and this current study, and we felt it was important to not set arbitrary threshold values on sustainability when none exist in the literature, outside of the binary checklist defined in Binda et al. 2024 [9].

Lastly, the methodology of this review has its own limitation. This was intended to be a rapid review, involving 1 database (Medline) and 1 reviewer, which may have limited the search strategy and the number of articles that were screened eligible and included.

Conclusions

Humanitarian organizations have long delivered surgical services in conflict and post-conflict settings across diverse geopolitical regions. The practices described in this review demonstrate representation across all six defined pillars of sustainability. Formal organizational policies—where available—consistently reflect comprehensive adoption of these pillars. Given the limited availability of published data on sustainable surgical programs, we call on humanitarian organizations and practitioners working in conflict-affected settings to increase transparency by reporting the strategies, policies, and practices that promote sustainability. Doing so will allow for comparative evaluation and continuous improvement in this evolving area of global health, as well as facilitate future research on evaluating whether implementing sustainable practices translates to a truly sustainable impact in conflict-affected zones. In light of the data on sustainability included in this review, we specifically want to highlight the pillars that had the lowest prevalence of being described in this current review. We call on our global surgery partners to ensure that the ultimate goal of handing over surgical programs to local stakeholders is always kept in mind when appropriate, and to utilize multisource funding from both high and low-income countries, from both governmental and non-governmental sources, wherever possible. At the same time, we commend humanitarian organizations for the strong emphasis on training local healthcare workers within these programs, which was the pillar of sustainability with the highest prevalence in this review. Building both technical and non-technical capacity is fundamental to the development of sustainable surgical services in these settings; without such skills transfer, the establishment and growth of a surgical program is not possible. Therefore, we call on organizations to continue sustaining and strengthening efforts in context-relevant education and training, with particular attention to the most fragile states where needs are greatest and long-term impact is most critical.
